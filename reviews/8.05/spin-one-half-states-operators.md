## Arithmetic and answer checks
Recomputed in Python/NumPy:
- Commutators: with $\hat S_i = \frac12\sigma_i$ ($\hbar = 1$), all three of $[\hat S_x,\hat S_y] = i\hat S_z$, $[\hat S_y,\hat S_z] = i\hat S_x$ and $[\hat S_z,\hat S_x] = i\hat S_y$ hold. The page's explicit products $\sigma_1\sigma_2 = \mathrm{diag}(i, -i)$ and $\sigma_2\sigma_1 = \mathrm{diag}(-i, i)$ are correct, and $\frac{\hbar^2}{4}\,\mathrm{diag}(2i, -2i) = i\hbar\hat S_z$. The relabelling $\hat S_x \to -\hat S_y$, $\hat S_y \to \hat S_x$ preserves all three relations (checked).
- Hermitian decomposition: $a\sigma_1 + b\sigma_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & a - ib\\ a + ib & 0\end{pmatrix}$. Correct.
- Eigenvectors: $\sigma_1$ has eigenvectors $(1, \pm1)/\sqrt2$ and $\sigma_2$ has $(1, \pm i)/\sqrt2$, with eigenvalues $\pm1$. Correct. The $y$ states are orthogonal (inner product 0) and $\hat S_y\chi_{y+} = +\frac{\hbar}{2}\chi_{y+}$. $\sigma_i^2 = 1$ for each.
- $\hat S_n$ matrix: for $(\theta, \phi) = (0.7, 1.3)$ and $(2.0, -0.4)$, $n_x\hat S_x + n_y\hat S_y + n_z\hat S_z$ equals $\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta e^{-i\phi}\\ \sin\theta e^{i\phi} & -\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$. $\chi_{n+}$ and the rephased $\chi_{n-}$ are eigenvectors with $+\hbar/2$ and $-\hbar/2$ and are orthogonal. $c_2/c_1 = e^{i\phi}(1 - \cos\theta)/\sin\theta = e^{i\phi}\tan(\theta/2)$. Correct.
- Worked example ($\theta = 60°$, $\phi = 0$): $\cos30° = 0.8660$, $\sin30° = 0.5$. $P(z+) = 0.75$ and $P(z-) = 0.25$. $\langle\hat S_z\rangle = \frac\hbar2(0.5) = 0.25\hbar = \frac\hbar2\cos60°$. $(\chi_{x+}, \chi_{n+}) = 1.3660/\sqrt2 = 0.96593$ and its square is $0.93301 = \cos^2 15°$. The angle from the $x$ axis is $90° - 60° = 30°$, and $|(\chi_{m+}, \chi_{n+})|^2 = \cos^2(\gamma/2)$ in general. All correct. The example is original: neither the lecture nor the notes has a numerical example.
- Quiz 1 (answer 0, $\alpha_1^*\beta_1 + \alpha_2^*\beta_2$): correct (notes (2.12)).
- Quiz 2 (answer 0, $\frac\hbar2\sigma_1$): correct. The explanation is right that $\hbar\sigma_1$ has eigenvalues $\pm\hbar$.
