This is the last lecture of 8.06. Lecture 23 found that symmetric and antisymmetric states, if they exist, form subspaces of the space of $N$ particles; now Zwiebach builds the **projectors** onto those subspaces, proves that they are orthogonal projectors, and then adds the extra law of quantum mechanics that says which states identical particles may have: the **symmetrization postulate** (notes chapter 8, pp. 158–164). He ends with seven comments on the postulate, the last one about **occupation numbers**. (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 8; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The notes put hats on the operators; this page leaves them off. The first minute of the L24.1 recording has audio dropouts.)

## Where Lecture 23 left off

The particles each have the space of states $V$, so $N$ of them live in the tensor product $V\otimes V\otimes\cdots\otimes V$, written $V^{\otimes N}$ (L24.1 transcript pp. 1–2). A symmetric state is unchanged by every permutation operator, $P_\alpha|\psi_S\rangle = |\psi_S\rangle$, and an antisymmetric state picks up the sign of the permutation, $P_\alpha|\psi_A\rangle = \epsilon_\alpha|\psi_A\rangle$, with $\epsilon_\alpha = +1$ for even and $-1$ for odd permutations. A permutation is even or odd according to whether it is built from an even or an odd number of transpositions; the number itself is not fixed, only whether it is even (L24.1 transcript p. 1). Three facts from last time are needed: every $P_\alpha$ is unitary, every transposition is Hermitian as well, and there are as many even permutations as odd ones.

Multiply a symmetric state by a number, or add two of them, and the result is still symmetric; the same holds for antisymmetric states. So they form subspaces (L24.1 transcript p. 2): $\text{Sym}^N V$, the symmetric states, and $\text{Anti}^N V$, the antisymmetric ones. Lecture 23 did not say how to build such states, or what to do with them. A subspace has a projector onto it, so the next step is to find it.

## The symmetrizer and the antisymmetrizer

The candidates are sums over all $N!$ permutation operators (L24.1 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Symmetrizer and antisymmetrizer" tex="S = \frac{1}{N!}\sum_\alpha P_\alpha, \qquad A = \frac{1}{N!}\sum_\alpha \epsilon_\alpha\,P_\alpha" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.34), p. 158" />

$S$ is the **symmetrizer** and $A$ the **antisymmetrizer**. The goal is to show that both are orthogonal projectors: Hermitian, and equal to their own squares (L24.1 transcript p. 2).

## S and A are Hermitian

The claim is (L24.1 transcript p. 2)

<Formula label="S and A are Hermitian" tex="S^\dagger = S, \qquad A^\dagger = A" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.35), p. 159" />

This is not obvious term by term, because a general permutation operator is unitary but not Hermitian (L24.1 transcript pp. 2–3). Zwiebach's argument looks at the whole list instead. Write down all $N!$ permutation operators and take the Hermitian conjugate of each one. The result is the same list in another order. Two different operators have different conjugates, because conjugating twice gives back the operator, so equal conjugates would mean equal operators. And every operator $O$ of the list is the conjugate of one in the list, namely of $O^\dagger$, since $(O^\dagger)^\dagger = O$. So conjugation maps the list one to one and onto itself, and the sum of all of them does not change: $S^\dagger = S$ (L24.1 transcript p. 3).

For $A$ there is one more thing to check: each $P_\alpha$ carries a sign, and after conjugation the sign must still be the right one for the new operator (L24.1 transcript pp. 3–4). It is. A permutation built from an even number of transpositions has as its conjugate the same transpositions in the reverse order (each transposition is Hermitian), so $P_\alpha^\dagger$ is even exactly when $P_\alpha$ is. Hence

<Formula label="Conjugating the antisymmetrizer" tex="A^\dagger = \frac{1}{N!}\sum_\alpha \epsilon_\alpha\,P_\alpha^\dagger = A" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

because $\epsilon_\alpha$ is also the sign of $P_\alpha^\dagger$, and the $P_\alpha^\dagger$ run once over the whole list. In the language of groups (L24.1 transcript p. 4): replacing every element of a group by its inverse gives back all the elements, scrambled, and for unitary operators the Hermitian conjugate is the inverse, $P_\alpha^\dagger = P_\alpha^{-1}$ (notes p. 159).

## Multiplying by one permutation

The next property says how $S$ and $A$ react to a single permutation $P_\beta$, multiplied from either side (L24.2 transcript p. 1; the lecture and the notes call it P with subscript α₀, written β here):

<Formula label="A permutation acting on S and A" tex="P_\beta\,S = S\,P_\beta = S, \qquad P_\beta\,A = A\,P_\beta = \epsilon_\beta\,A" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (8.2.36)–(8.2.37), p. 159" />

The idea is the same as before: multiplying every operator of the list by $P_\beta$ only reorders the list (two different $P_\alpha$ give two different $P_\beta P_\alpha$, and every operator is $P_\beta$ times something in the list). So $P_\beta S = \frac{1}{N!}\sum_\alpha P_\beta P_\alpha$ is again the sum of all permutations, which is $S$ (L24.2 transcript p. 1; notes eq. (8.2.38)).

For $A$ the signs need care (L24.2 transcript pp. 1–2). Insert $1 = \epsilon_\beta\,\epsilon_\beta$, since $\epsilon_\beta^2 = 1$ whichever sign it is, and take one factor out of the sum:

<Formula label="The sign of a product of permutations" tex="P_\beta\,A = \frac{1}{N!}\sum_\alpha \epsilon_\alpha\,P_\beta P_\alpha = \epsilon_\beta\,\frac{1}{N!}\sum_\alpha \left(\epsilon_\beta\,\epsilon_\alpha\right)P_\beta P_\alpha = \epsilon_\beta\,A" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.39), p. 159" />

The last step holds because $\epsilon_\beta\epsilon_\alpha$ is the sign of the product $P_\gamma = P_\beta P_\alpha$: the numbers of transpositions add, so two even permutations give an even one, and one even and one odd give an odd one. The sum is then $\frac{1}{N!}\sum_\gamma \epsilon_\gamma P_\gamma = A$. Zwiebach proves the left multiplication and notes that multiplying from the right is almost the same argument (L24.2 transcript p. 2).

## S and A are orthogonal projectors

Now the main claim (L24.2 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Orthogonal projectors" tex="S^2 = S, \qquad A^2 = A, \qquad AS = SA = 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.40), p. 159" />

With Hermiticity this makes $S$ and $A$ orthogonal projectors, and $AS = 0$ says more: once a state is symmetrized, it has no component along the antisymmetric states (L24.2 transcript p. 2). Each line of the proof writes out the first factor and uses the property just proved on the second (L24.2 transcript p. 3). For $S^2$, each term $P_\alpha S$ is $S$, and there are $N!$ terms:

<Formula label="S squares to itself" tex="S^2 = \frac{1}{N!}\sum_\alpha P_\alpha\,S = S\,\frac{1}{N!}\sum_\alpha 1 = S\,\frac{N!}{N!} = S" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.41), p. 159" />

For $A^2$, each term $\epsilon_\alpha P_\alpha A$ is $\epsilon_\alpha\epsilon_\alpha A = A$, since the square of a sign is $+1$; again $N!$ equal terms, so $A^2 = A$ (notes eq. (8.2.42)). The mixed product is the interesting one:

<Formula label="A and S are orthogonal" tex="AS = \frac{1}{N!}\sum_\alpha \epsilon_\alpha\,P_\alpha\,S = S\,\frac{1}{N!}\sum_\alpha \epsilon_\alpha = 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.43), p. 159" />

The sum of the signs vanishes because the symmetric group has as many even permutations as odd ones, the theorem of Lecture 23 (L24.2 transcript p. 3). The same steps with the factors in the other order give $SA = 0$.

## Where S and A take a state

Take any $|\psi\rangle$ in $V^{\otimes N}$ (L24.2 transcript pp. 3–4). Then $S|\psi\rangle$ is symmetric and $A|\psi\rangle$ antisymmetric, straight from the last section:

<Formula label="S and A produce symmetric and antisymmetric states" tex="P_\alpha\,S|\psi\rangle = S|\psi\rangle, \qquad P_\alpha\,A|\psi\rangle = \epsilon_\alpha\,A|\psi\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (8.2.44)–(8.2.45), p. 159" />

So $S$ maps $V^{\otimes N}$ into $\text{Sym}^N V$ and $A$ maps it into $\text{Anti}^N V$ (notes eq. (8.2.46)). And if $|\psi\rangle$ is already symmetric, every term of $S|\psi\rangle$ is $|\psi\rangle$, so $S|\psi\rangle = |\psi\rangle$; the same goes for $A$ on an antisymmetric state, so the ranges are the whole subspaces. Pictured inside the big space, the two subspaces meet only in the zero vector, which every subspace contains, as a student answers when Zwiebach asks (L24.2 transcript p. 4).

For two particles they fill the space. Then $S = \frac12(1 + P_{21})$ and $A = \frac12(1 - P_{21})$, as in Lecture 23, and $S + A = 1$ (L24.2 transcript p. 4; notes eq. (8.2.15)). [The transcript has the symmetrizer as "1 plus P21", without the ½.] So every vector of two particles is a symmetric part plus an antisymmetric part, $|v\rangle = S|v\rangle + A|v\rangle$: the two subspaces span the space.

## Three particles: S + A is not 1

For three particles there are six permutation operators: the identity $P_{123}$ and the cycles $P_{312}$ and $P_{231}$ are even, the transpositions $P_{132}$, $P_{213}$ and $P_{321}$ are odd (L24.2 transcript p. 5). So

<Formula label="Symmetrizer for three particles" tex="S = \frac16\left(1 + P_{312} + P_{231} + P_{132} + P_{213} + P_{321}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.47), p. 160" />

<Formula label="Antisymmetrizer for three particles" tex="A = \frac16\left(1 + P_{312} + P_{231} - P_{132} - P_{213} - P_{321}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.48), p. 160" />

[In the lecture the even terms of $A$ are read as $1 + P_{312} + P_{213}$; the second cycle is $P_{231}$, as in the notes.] Adding them, the odd permutations cancel:

<Formula label="S + A for three particles" tex="S + A = \frac13\left(1 + P_{312} + P_{231}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.49), p. 160" />

This is not the identity (L24.2 transcript p. 5). A vector of three particles is in general not a symmetric part plus an antisymmetric part: for $N > 2$, $\text{Sym}^N V$ and $\text{Anti}^N V$ together span only part of $V^{\otimes N}$ (notes eq. (8.2.50)). The rest are states of **mixed symmetry**, partly symmetric and partly antisymmetric. Zwiebach points to a more advanced course for them, where they are classified with the **Young tableaux** of the permutation group, and there are further projectors onto them (L24.2 transcript p. 5).

The notes then define a completely symmetric observable as one that commutes with every permutation (eq. (8.2.51), p. 160), the $N$-particle version of the symmetric operators of Lecture 23; the lecture goes straight on to physics.

## The symmetrization postulate

So far everything was a statement about vector spaces (L24.3 transcript p. 1). What connects it to physics is a new postulate, which cannot be derived: Zwiebach calls it an extra axiom of quantum mechanics, perhaps the only way things could be, but more honestly admitted as a postulate. It reads (L24.3 transcript p. 1; notes section 8.3, p. 160):

> **Symmetrization postulate.** In a system of $N$ identical particles, the physical states are not arbitrary states of $V^{\otimes N}$. They are either totally symmetric, in $\text{Sym}^N V$, and the particles are called **bosons**, or totally antisymmetric, in $\text{Anti}^N V$, and the particles are called **fermions**.

This answers the question that started the subject in Lecture 22: for two electrons, one with spin up and one with spin down, the state is neither $|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle$ nor $|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$, and the two cannot be declared equivalent; it is the antisymmetric combination (L24.3 transcript p. 1).

## Comments 1 to 4: dimensions, spin and composite particles

Zwiebach adds seven comments (L24.3 transcript p. 1 to L24.4 transcript p. 5); the first four are in the third video.

1. The postulate is a statement of fact for particles in three space dimensions. In two space dimensions further kinds of statistics are possible (L24.3 transcript pp. 1–2); the notes name the particles **anyons** (p. 160).
2. The postulate is the statistical definition of bosons and fermions: it says how they behave when many are together (L24.3 transcript p. 2).
3. The **spin–statistics theorem** of quantum field theory connects this with spin: particles of integer spin, 0, 1, 2, …, are bosons, and particles of half-integer spin, ½, 3/2, …, are fermions (L24.3 transcript p. 2). Zwiebach calls it a deep connection and a great achievement of quantum field theory. His list of elementary particles: the photon, the gluons and the W and Z are spin 1; the Higgs boson is spin 0; a graviton would be spin 2 (these two names are inaudible in the transcript); the matter particles (quarks and leptons, including neutrinos) are spin ½; a spin-3/2 elementary particle, the gravitino, is hypothetical; higher spins appear in string theory and other proposals, but none is known.
4. The postulate for elementary particles fixes the character of composite ones (L24.3 transcript pp. 2–3; notes pp. 160–161). Take two hydrogen atoms and a wavefunction $\Psi(p_1, e_1; p_2, e_2)$ of the two protons and the two electrons (their positions, say). Exchanging the electrons gives a minus sign, since they are fermions; exchanging the protons gives another. Exchanging the two atoms means exchanging both, and the two minus signs give a plus, $(-1) \times (-1) = +1$:

<Formula label="Exchanging two hydrogen atoms" tex="\Psi(p_2, e_2; p_1, e_1) = +\Psi(p_1, e_1; p_2, e_2)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (8.3.1)–(8.3.3), p. 161" />

So the hydrogen atom is a **boson** (L24.3 transcript p. 3). In general a composite of some bosons and some fermions is a boson or a fermion depending on those numbers. (A sharper form, this page's rather than the lecture's: only the fermions matter, and the composite is a fermion exactly when it contains an odd number of them.)

## Comment 5: exchange degeneracy is gone

The postulate solves the exchange degeneracy problem, and since Lecture 22 made such a fuss about it, Zwiebach checks it carefully (L24.4 transcript p. 1). Take a product state $|u\rangle$ in $V^{\otimes N}$, each particle in one single-particle state (a "mathematical" state, since an arbitrary state is no longer physical) and collect everything that permutations make from it:

<Formula label="The space made by permuting one state" tex="V_u = \text{span}\left\{P_\alpha|u\rangle\right\}" cite="reading5" at="Comment 5, p. 161" />

This was the source of the degeneracy: one state produces many by permutation, and nothing says which to use. Depending on $|u\rangle$, the dimension of $V_u$ runs from 1 up to $N!$ (L24.4 transcript p. 1). For example, for $N = 3$ and $|u\rangle = |a\,a\,b\rangle$ (short for $|a\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$, the first two particles in the same state $a$), permutations give only $|a\,a\,b\rangle$, $|b\,a\,a\rangle$ and $|a\,b\,a\rangle$, so $V_u$ has dimension 3 (L24.4 transcript p. 2).

The claim: up to scale, $V_u$ contains a **single** symmetric state and **at most one** antisymmetric state (L24.4 transcript p. 2). One of each is easy to find: $S|u\rangle$ and $A|u\rangle$. The antisymmetric one can vanish, because the terms of $A|u\rangle$ have both signs and can cancel. They do for $|a\,a\,b\rangle$: three fermions cannot be antisymmetrized when two of them are in the same state. The terms of $S|u\rangle$ all have a plus sign, so for a product state there is no such cancellation. (For a state that is not a product it can happen: $S$ kills $|a\,b\rangle - |b\,a\rangle$, so the claim is about product states, as in the lecture's example.) [Zwiebach first puts the "at most" on the symmetric state and then corrects himself. The notes say a single ket of each (p. 161); the lecture's wording is the careful one.]

Could someone hand you a second symmetric state in $V_u$? Suppose $|\psi\rangle$ is in $V_u$ and symmetric (L24.4 transcript pp. 2–3). Being in $V_u$, it is a combination of the permuted states; being symmetric, it equals $S|\psi\rangle$; and $SP_\alpha = S$ for every term:

<Formula label="Only one symmetric state" tex="|\psi\rangle = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha\,P_\alpha|u\rangle = S|\psi\rangle = \sum_\alpha c_\alpha\,S\,P_\alpha|u\rangle = \left(\sum_\alpha c_\alpha\right)S|u\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (8.3.4)–(8.3.5), pp. 161–162" />

So any symmetric state in $V_u$ is a multiple of $S|u\rangle$, and the same argument with $AP_\alpha = \epsilon_\alpha A$ works for antisymmetric states. Of all the candidates, one physical state survives.

## Comment 6: antisymmetric states are determinants

For fermions there is a famous construction (L24.4 transcript p. 3). Take three different single-particle states and $|u\rangle = |\phi\,\chi\,\omega\rangle$: particle 1 in $\phi$, particle 2 in $\chi$, particle 3 in $\omega$. Then $A|u\rangle$ is $\frac16$ times six terms in which the states are shuffled, three with a plus sign and three with a minus. Those are the six terms of a determinant:

<Formula label="Antisymmetrizing three states" tex="A\,|\phi\,\chi\,\omega\rangle = \frac{1}{3!}\begin{vmatrix} \phi(1) & \phi(2) & \phi(3) \\ \chi(1) & \chi(2) & \chi(3) \\ \omega(1) & \omega(2) & \omega(3) \end{vmatrix}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.3.8), p. 162" />

Here $\phi(2)$ stands for the state $\phi$ given to particle 2, and so on: each row is one state, each column one particle. Each product of the expansion must be put back into the standard order, particle 1 first, before it is read as a ket (notes p. 162). The diagonal gives $|\phi\,\chi\,\omega\rangle$ with a plus sign, the identity permutation; Zwiebach recommends writing out all six terms to check the rest (L24.4 transcript p. 3). If two of the states are equal, say $\phi = \chi$, two rows of the determinant are equal and it vanishes: no antisymmetric state exists. [The lecture makes the analogy with two proportional columns; in the notes' arrangement the equal states are rows.]

The reason is the general formula for a determinant (L24.4 transcript p. 4; the notes give it, as Zwiebach promised):

<Formula label="The determinant as a sum over permutations" tex="\det B = \sum_\alpha \epsilon_\alpha\,B_{\alpha(1),1}\,B_{\alpha(2),2}\cdots B_{\alpha(N),N}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.3.9), p. 162" />

It takes the element in row $\alpha(1)$ from the first column, the element in row $\alpha(2)$ from the second column, and so on, with the sign of the permutation. With row $i$ the state $\omega_i$ and column $j$ particle $j$, the term for $\alpha$ puts the state $\omega_{\alpha(1)}$ on particle 1, $\omega_{\alpha(2)}$ on particle 2, and so on, which is exactly what $P_\alpha$ does to $|\omega_1\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|\omega_N\rangle$. So for any $N$, applying $A$ to a product of $N$ states gives $\frac{1}{N!}$ times the determinant of this matrix of states (notes eqs. (8.3.10)–(8.3.15), pp. 162–163).

## Comment 7: occupation numbers

The last comment (L24.4 transcript pp. 4–5; notes section 8.4, pp. 163–164) describes states of identical particles more economically. Let $u_1, u_2, \ldots$ be an orthonormal basis of $V$, possibly infinite. A basis state of $V^{\otimes N}$ puts each particle in one of these states; what survives symmetrization is only how many particles are in each. So list the **occupation numbers** $n_1, n_2, \ldots$: $n_1$ particles in $u_1$, $n_2$ in $u_2$, and so on. The state is the product with $n_1$ factors $u_1$, then $n_2$ factors $u_2$, and so on, symmetrized for bosons or antisymmetrized for fermions. Symmetrizing is a lot of algebra, Zwiebach says, but the occupation numbers already say everything about the state (L24.4 transcript p. 4). For bosons each $n_k$ can be any whole number; for fermions only 0 or 1, because a fermion state with two particles in the same basis state is killed by $A$. Either way they count the particles:

<Formula label="Occupation numbers" tex="\sum_k n_k = N, \qquad n_k = 0 \text{ or } 1 \text{ for fermions}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (8.4.8)–(8.4.9), p. 164" />

Two basis states with the same occupation numbers are permutations of each other and give the same physical state (up to a sign for fermions); different occupation numbers give different states (notes p. 163). That fermions cannot share a state is the **Pauli exclusion principle**, here a consequence of antisymmetry.

Zwiebach closes the course here, because "occupation numbers is where quantum field theory begins" (L24.4 transcript p. 5): there one builds operators that add and remove particles. The notes go on to normalized occupation-number states, particles with both spatial and spin degrees of freedom, and the counting of states that leads to the Maxwell–Boltzmann, Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein distributions (sections 8.4–8.6, pp. 163–171); the lecture does not cover them.

<WorkedExample title="Symmetrizing, antisymmetrizing and counting states">
<Problem>
A particle has three orthonormal states $|a\rangle$, $|b\rangle$ and $|c\rangle$, so $V$ has dimension 3. (a) For three particles, find $S|a\,a\,b\rangle$ and $A|a\,a\,b\rangle$, and the squared norm of $S|a\,a\,b\rangle$. (b) Find $A|a\,b\,c\rangle$ and its squared norm, and write the normalized antisymmetric state. (c) Count the symmetric and the antisymmetric states of two particles and of three particles, and compare with the dimension of $V^{\otimes N}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A state with two particles in the same state">
Apply the six permutations to $|a\,a\,b\rangle$ (each moves the states, never the particles): $P_{123}$ and $P_{213}$ give $|a\,a\,b\rangle$, $P_{231}$ and $P_{132}$ give $|a\,b\,a\rangle$, and $P_{312}$ and $P_{321}$ give $|b\,a\,a\rangle$. Each of the three different kets appears twice, so $S|a\,a\,b\rangle = \frac26\left(|a\,a\,b\rangle + |a\,b\,a\rangle + |b\,a\,a\rangle\right) = \frac13\left(|a\,a\,b\rangle + |a\,b\,a\rangle + |b\,a\,a\rangle\right)$. In $A$ each ket appears once from an even permutation and once from an odd one ($P_{123}$ and $P_{213}$, $P_{231}$ and $P_{132}$, $P_{312}$ and $P_{321}$), so $A|a\,a\,b\rangle = 0$: two fermions cannot both be in $a$. The three kets in $S|a\,a\,b\rangle$ are orthonormal, so its squared norm is $3 \times \frac19 = \frac13$, and the normalized symmetric state is $\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\left(|a\,a\,b\rangle + |a\,b\,a\rangle + |b\,a\,a\rangle\right)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Three different states">
Now all six kets differ. With the signs of the antisymmetrizer, $A|a\,b\,c\rangle = \frac16\left(|a\,b\,c\rangle + |c\,a\,b\rangle + |b\,c\,a\rangle - |a\,c\,b\rangle - |b\,a\,c\rangle - |c\,b\,a\rangle\right)$, which is also what the determinant with rows $a$, $b$, $c$ gives. Six orthonormal kets with coefficients of size $\frac16$ give a squared norm $6 \times \frac{1}{36} = \frac16$, so the normalized state is $\sqrt6\,A|a\,b\,c\rangle$, the same six kets with coefficients $\pm\frac{1}{\sqrt6}$. A check: $A$ is a projector, so $\langle a\,b\,c|A|a\,b\,c\rangle$ must equal the squared norm of $A|a\,b\,c\rangle$, and only the identity term contributes to it, giving $\frac16$ again.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Counting with occupation numbers">
A state is fixed by occupation numbers $(n_a, n_b, n_c)$ adding up to $N$. For two particles: symmetric states have any occupation numbers, $(2,0,0)$ three ways and $(1,1,0)$ three ways, so $3 \times 4/2 = 6$; antisymmetric ones need numbers 0 or 1, so the three ways to choose 2 of the 3 states. Together $6 + 3 = 9 = 3^2$, the dimension of $V\otimes V$, as $S + A = 1$ requires. For three particles: symmetric states are $(3,0,0)$ three ways, $(2,1,0)$ six ways and $(1,1,1)$ once, 10 in all; the only antisymmetric one is $(1,1,1)$, the state of part (b). Together $10 + 1 = 11$, far fewer than $3^3 = 27$: the other 16 dimensions are states of mixed symmetry, which is $S + A \ne 1$ in numbers.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Fermions and bosons in the early universe">
The difference between occupation numbers 0 or 1 and any whole number shows up directly in cosmology. In 8.286 Guth counts the particle species that fill the hot early universe as black-body radiation: photons are bosons, while neutrinos and electrons are spin-½ fermions and cannot share states, so each fermion species contributes 7/8 as much energy density as a boson species with the same number of spin states, and 3/4 as many particles. That factor sets how fast the universe expanded and cooled in its first seconds. The same exclusion, as degeneracy pressure, holds up white dwarfs.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The symmetrizer $S = \frac{1}{N!}\sum_\alpha P_\alpha$ and the antisymmetrizer $A = \frac{1}{N!}\sum_\alpha \epsilon_\alpha P_\alpha$ are Hermitian: conjugation only reorders the list of permutations and keeps each one's parity.
- Multiplying by one permutation also only reorders the list: $P_\beta S = SP_\beta = S$ and $P_\beta A = AP_\beta = \epsilon_\beta A$. From this, $S^2 = S$, $A^2 = A$ and $AS = SA = 0$ (equal numbers of even and odd permutations), so $S$ and $A$ are the orthogonal projectors onto $\text{Sym}^N V$ and $\text{Anti}^N V$.
- For two particles $S + A = 1$. For three, $S + A = \frac13(1 + P_{312} + P_{231})$: the symmetric and antisymmetric states do not span the space, and states of mixed symmetry remain.
- The symmetrization postulate: physical states of identical particles are totally symmetric (bosons) or totally antisymmetric (fermions). By the spin–statistics theorem, bosons have integer spin and fermions half-integer spin; a composite is a fermion when it contains an odd number of fermions, so hydrogen is a boson.
- The postulate removes exchange degeneracy: the states made by permuting one product state contain one symmetric state and at most one antisymmetric state, $S|u\rangle$ and $A|u\rangle$. The antisymmetric state is $\frac{1}{N!}$ times a determinant, which vanishes when two particles share a state.
- States of identical particles are labelled by occupation numbers adding up to $N$: any whole number for bosons, 0 or 1 for fermions (the Pauli exclusion principle). Occupation numbers are where quantum field theory begins.
