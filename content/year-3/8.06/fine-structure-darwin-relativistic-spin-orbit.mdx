The previous lecture found three small terms to add to hydrogen's Hamiltonian, all of order $\alpha^4mc^2$, about 19,000 times smaller than the levels themselves. This lecture computes each with perturbation theory, the Darwin term first, then the relativistic and spin–orbit terms, and adds them up (L5.1–L5.5 transcripts; notes section 2.4). The real difficulty, Zwiebach stresses, is not the algebra but checking each time that the basis used is a good one. (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in five short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 2; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. Gaussian units, as before.)

## The Darwin term

The Darwin term contains the Laplacian of $V = -e^2/r$ (L5.1 transcript p. 1). Since $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec r)$, the Laplacian of the Coulomb potential is a delta function at the proton, $\nabla^2V = 4\pi e^2\delta^3(\vec r)$, and

<Formula label="Darwin term" tex="\delta H_{\text{Darwin}} = \frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\nabla^2V = \frac{\pi e^2\hbar^2}{2m^2c^2}\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (2.3.19), (2.4.21)" />

(The physicist is Charles Galton Darwin; in the recording his middle name comes out as "Gaston".) A delta function picks out the wavefunctions at the origin, and near the origin they go like $r^l$: **only $l = 0$ states are affected** (L5.1 transcript p. 2). That also removes any worry about degeneracy, because each level has just two S states, spin up and spin down, and the Darwin term does not touch spin. So the first-order shift is a simple expectation value:

<Formula label="Darwin shift as an expectation value" tex="E^{(1)} = \frac{\pi e^2\hbar^2}{2m^2c^2}\,|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2" cite="reading6" at="eq. (2.4.22)" />

The value at the origin needs the correctly normalized wavefunction, which is not easy to get for large $n$. A trick explored in the homework relates it to $\langle 1/r^2\rangle$ and gives (L5.1 transcript p. 3)

<Formula label="S wavefunctions at the origin" tex="|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2 = \frac{1}{\pi n^3a_0^3}, \qquad E^{(1)} = \frac{\alpha^4mc^2}{2n^3}" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (2.4.23), (2.4.24)" />

The Darwin shift is positive: it pushes S states up.

## Why a Darwin term? A smeared-out electron

The relativistic term comes from the kinetic energy, and spin–orbit from the magnetic field the moving electron sees. The Darwin term has a picture too (L5.2 transcript pp. 1–5): it is the correction you would get if the electron were not a point but a small ball of charge. Let $\rho_0(\vec u)$ describe how its charge $-e$ is spread around its centre, with $\int d^3u\,\rho_0 = 1$. With its centre at $\vec r$, the electron's potential energy is the point-particle one, averaged over the ball:

<Formula label="Potential energy of a smeared electron" tex="\tilde V(\vec r) = \int d^3u\;\rho_0(\vec u)\,V(\vec r + \vec u)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (2.4.28)" />

Expand $V(\vec r + \vec u)$ to second order in $\vec u$. For a spherically symmetric ball, the linear term integrates to zero and the quadratic one keeps only its diagonal, $\int d^3u\,\rho_0u_iu_j = \frac13\delta_{ij}\int d^3u\,\rho_0u^2$ (notes eq. (2.4.33)), which turns the second derivatives into a Laplacian:

<Formula label="The correction from smearing" tex="\tilde V = V + \delta V, \qquad \delta V = \frac16\,\nabla^2V\int d^3u\;\rho_0(u)\,u^2" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (2.4.34), (2.4.35)" />

For a uniform ball of radius $u_0$ the integral is $\frac35u_0^2$, so $\delta V = \frac{1}{10}u_0^2\nabla^2V$ (notes eqs. (2.4.36)–(2.4.38)). Take $u_0$ to be the electron's Compton wavelength $\hbar/mc$:

<Formula label="A ball the size of the Compton wavelength" tex="\delta V = \frac{\hbar^2}{10\,m^2c^2}\,\nabla^2V" cite="reading6" at="eq. (2.4.39)" />

One tenth instead of one eighth: a crude model, yet it lands close to the Darwin term. This does not mean the electron is really a ball. The notes draw a different lesson: in a relativistic treatment, the Compton wavelength is the shortest distance over which an electron can be localized (notes section 2.4.1). Zwiebach links this to pair creation: light with a wavelength near $\hbar/mc$ carries energies of the order of $mc^2$, enough (with factors of order one) to create electron–positron pairs, which is why the Compton wavelength matters in quantum field theory (L5.2 transcript p. 5).

## The relativistic correction

Naively, the first-order shift of $|n\,l\,m_l\rangle$ is $-\frac{1}{8m^3c^2}\langle\hat p^4\rangle$ in that state (L5.3 transcript p. 1). But that formula is for non-degenerate levels, and the level $n$ holds $n^2$ orbital states; strictly, an $n^2 \times n^2$ matrix must be checked. The commuting-operator rule of Lecture 3 settles it (L5.3 transcript p. 2). $\hat p^2$ is rotationally invariant, so $\hat p^4$ commutes with every component of $\vec L$, in particular with $\vec L^2$ and $L_z$, and trivially with $S_z$. Degenerate states differ in $l$, $m_l$ or $m_s$, so the uncoupled basis is good and the expectation value is the right answer.

To compute it, move one $\hat p^2$ onto the bra (it is Hermitian), and replace $\hat p^2\psi$ using the Schrödinger equation (L5.3 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Using the Schrödinger equation" tex="E^{(1)} = -\frac{1}{8m^3c^2}\,\langle\hat p^2\psi|\hat p^2\psi\rangle, \qquad \hat p^2\psi = 2m\left(E_n^{(0)} - V\right)\psi" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (2.4.41), (2.4.42)" />

What is left is $(E_n^{(0)})^2 - 2E_n^{(0)}\langle V\rangle + \langle V^2\rangle$, divided by $-2mc^2$. The virial theorem gives $\langle V\rangle = 2E_n^{(0)}$, and the known $\langle 1/r^2\rangle$ gives $\langle V^2\rangle = (E_n^{(0)})^2\,\frac{4n}{l + \frac12}$ (notes eqs. (2.4.44), (2.4.45)), so

<Formula label="Relativistic correction" tex="E^{(1)} = -\frac{(E_n^{(0)})^2}{2mc^2}\left(\frac{4n}{l + \frac12} - 3\right) = -\frac{\alpha^4mc^2}{8}\,\frac{1}{n^4}\left(\frac{4n}{l + \frac12} - 3\right)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (2.4.46)" />

The $l$-degeneracy of each level, the special symmetry of the Coulomb problem, is broken.

**Uncoupled or coupled?** The result depends only on $n$ and $l$, not on $m_l$ or $m_s$ (L5.3 transcript pp. 4–5). Within a fixed $(n, l)$ multiplet the perturbation is therefore a multiple of the identity matrix, and a multiple of the identity looks the same in **every** orthonormal basis. So the coupled states $|n\,l\,j\,m_j\rangle$, which are just other combinations of the same states, get exactly the same shift:

<Formula label="Same shift in the coupled basis" tex="\langle n\,l\,j\,m_j|\,\delta H_{\text{rel}}\,|n\,l\,j\,m_j\rangle = \langle n\,l\,m_l\,m_s|\,\delta H_{\text{rel}}\,|n\,l\,m_l\,m_s\rangle" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (2.4.47), (2.4.48)" />

This matters, because the next correction can only be done in the coupled basis.

## The spin–orbit correction

With $V = -e^2/r$, the spin–orbit term is (L5.4 transcript p. 1)

<Formula label="Spin–orbit term" tex="\delta H_{\text{spin-orbit}} = \frac{e^2}{2m^2c^2}\,\frac{1}{r^3}\,\vec S\cdot\vec L, \qquad \vec S\cdot\vec L = \frac12\left(\vec J^2 - \vec L^2 - \vec S^2\right)" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (2.4.51), (2.4.52)" />

$\vec S\cdot\vec L$ is easy in the coupled basis, where $\vec J^2$, $\vec L^2$ and $\vec S^2$ all have definite values. Is that basis good? The perturbation commutes with $\vec L^2$ (which commutes with every $L_i$, with spin, and with $r$), with $\vec J^2$ and with $J_z$; degenerate coupled states differ in $l$, $j$ or $m_j$, so yes (L5.4 transcript pp. 1–2). Then

<Formula label="Spin–orbit shift" tex="E^{(1)} = \frac{e^2\hbar^2}{4m^2c^2}\left[j(j+1) - l(l+1) - \tfrac34\right]\left\langle\frac{1}{r^3}\right\rangle, \qquad \left\langle\frac{1}{r^3}\right\rangle = \frac{1}{n^3a_0^3\,l\left(l + \frac12\right)(l + 1)}" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (2.4.52), (2.4.53)" />

($\langle 1/r^3\rangle$ is known in the uncoupled basis; it does not depend on $m_l$, so by the same identity-matrix argument it holds in the coupled basis too.) In terms of the unperturbed energy (L5.4 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Spin–orbit correction" tex="E^{(1)} = \frac{(E_n^{(0)})^2}{mc^2}\;n\;\frac{j(j+1) - l(l+1) - \frac34}{l\left(l + \frac12\right)(l + 1)}, \qquad l \neq 0" cite="reading6" at="eq. (2.4.56)" />

For $l = 0$ the operator $\vec L$ gives zero, so physically there is no spin–orbit shift, although the formula is zero over zero. A curiosity (L5.5 transcript p. 1): setting $j = l + \frac12$ and letting $l \to 0$, the formula tends to $\alpha^4mc^2/(2n^3)$, **exactly the Darwin shift** (notes eq. (2.4.58)).

## Adding them up: only j matters

Add the relativistic and spin–orbit results, both in the coupled basis (L5.5 transcript pp. 1–2). For $l = 0$ the sum should really be relativistic plus Darwin, but by the curiosity above the spin–orbit limit gives the same thing, so one formula covers every state:

<Formula label="Relativistic plus spin–orbit" tex="E^{(1)} = \frac{(E_n^{(0)})^2}{2mc^2}\left[3 + 2n\,f(j, l)\right], \qquad f(j, l) = \frac{j(j+1) - 3l(l+1) - \frac34}{l\left(l + \frac12\right)(l + 1)}" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (2.4.59), (2.4.60)" />

A given $j$ can come from $l = j - \frac12$ or from $l = j + \frac12$. Evaluate $f$ in both cases, and something unexpected happens: both give the same,

<Formula label="The l-dependence disappears" tex="f\left(j, j - \tfrac12\right) = f\left(j, j + \tfrac12\right) = -\frac{2}{j + \frac12}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (2.4.61)" />

The $l$-dependence was an illusion. The fine structure of hydrogen, Darwin, relativistic and spin–orbit together, is

<Formula label="Hydrogen's fine structure" tex="E^{(1)} = -\frac{\alpha^4mc^2}{2n^4}\left(\frac{n}{j + \frac12} - \frac34\right)" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (2.4.62), (2.4.63)" />

which Zwiebach calls the most important result of the lecture (L5.5 transcript pp. 2–3). Those who take the Dirac equation seriously would expect it: there the conserved angular momentum is $\vec J = \vec L + \vec S$, not $\vec L$ or $\vec S$ separately, so energy eigenstates are labelled by $j$, and each $j$ multiplet stays whole.

**The new spectrum** (L5.5 transcript pp. 3–4). The bracket is always positive: $j + \frac12$ is at most $l + 1 \le n$, so $n/(j + \frac12) \ge 1 > \frac34$ (notes eq. (2.4.64)). So **every level moves down**, even the ground state, and by less as $n$ grows (the shifts fall like $n^{-3}$). For fixed $n$, a larger $j$ moves down less. In units of $\alpha^4mc^2$ the shifts are

| Multiplets | $n$ | $j$ | Shift ($\alpha^4mc^2$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1S_{1/2}$ | 1 | ½ | $-1/8$ |
| $2S_{1/2}$, $2P_{1/2}$ | 2 | ½ | $-5/128$ |
| $2P_{3/2}$ | 2 | 3/2 | $-1/128$ |
| $3S_{1/2}$, $3P_{1/2}$ | 3 | ½ | $-1/72$ |
| $3P_{3/2}$, $3D_{3/2}$ | 3 | 3/2 | $-1/216$ |
| $3D_{5/2}$ | 3 | 5/2 | $-1/648$ |

Multiplets with the same $n$ and $j$, such as $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$, stay degenerate, and no $j$ multiplet splits, since $m_j$ never appears. (Smaller effects act on top of this, as the notes mention: the Lamb shift, which separates $2S_{1/2}$ from $2P_{1/2}$, and [hyperfine splitting](/courses/8-05/hyperfine-21cm-adding-angular-momenta), covered in 8.05.) Breaking the remaining degeneracy is the job of external fields: the Zeeman and Stark effects.

<WorkedExample title="The 2P doublet in hydrogen">
<Problem>
Using $\alpha = 1/137.036$ and $mc^2 = 511.0$ keV: (a) by how much does the fine structure lower hydrogen's ground state? (b) How far apart are $2P_{1/2}$ and $2P_{3/2}$, in eV, and what frequency and wavelength of radiation would bridge them?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The ground state">
$\alpha^4mc^2 = 511{,}000\ \text{eV}/137.036^4 \approx 1.449 \times 10^{-3}$ eV. For $n = 1$, $j = \frac12$: $E^{(1)} = -\frac{1}{8}\alpha^4mc^2 \approx -1.81 \times 10^{-4}$ eV. Next to 13.6 eV this is about $1.3 \times 10^{-5}$, that is $\alpha^2/4$: a very fine correction indeed.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The 2P doublet">
From the table, $2P_{1/2}$ moves down by $\frac{5}{128}\alpha^4mc^2$ and $2P_{3/2}$ by $\frac{1}{128}\alpha^4mc^2$, so they are $\frac{4}{128} = \frac{1}{32}$ of $\alpha^4mc^2$ apart: about $4.53 \times 10^{-5}$ eV. Dividing by Planck's constant, $4.136 \times 10^{-15}$ eV s, gives a frequency of about 10.95 GHz, and a wavelength $c/f \approx 2.7$ cm: microwaves. In the Lyman-alpha line ($n = 2 \to 1$, at 10.2 eV) this splitting appears as a doublet only about four parts in a million apart.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Darwin: a delta function at the proton, so only S states shift, by $+\alpha^4mc^2/(2n^3)$; it mimics an electron smeared over its Compton wavelength.
- Relativistic: $-\frac{\alpha^4mc^2}{8n^4}\left(\frac{4n}{l + 1/2} - 3\right)$, computed in the uncoupled basis, which is good because $\hat p^4$ commutes with $\vec L^2$ and $L_z$; it is the same in the coupled basis.
- Spin–orbit: computed in the coupled basis, where $\vec S\cdot\vec L$ is diagonal; its $l \to 0$ limit equals the Darwin shift.
- Together: $E^{(1)} = -\frac{\alpha^4mc^2}{2n^4}\left(\frac{n}{j + 1/2} - \frac34\right)$. All levels move down; only $n$ and $j$ matter, so same-$n$, same-$j$ multiplets stay degenerate.
