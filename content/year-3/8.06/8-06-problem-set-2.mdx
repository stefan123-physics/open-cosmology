Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `q^2*Ef^2*a^2/E1`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent, so where two of them are related the problem says which to use: in Problem 2 type the energy `E1`, not $\hbar$ and $m$. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `lam` stands for $\lambda$, `D` for $\Delta$, `w1` and `w2` for $w_1$ and $w_2$, `Ef` for the electric field $\mathcal{E}$, `E1` and `E2` for $E_1$ and $E_2$, `e1` and `e2` for $\epsilon_1$ and $\epsilon_2$, `a0` for the Bohr radius $a_0$ and `e` for the elementary charge.

Give a number answer to 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. Where a box needs more digits, its label says so.

MIT gives the points per problem. The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show or derive something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. The notation is that of the lessons: $H(\lambda) = H^{(0)} + \lambda\,\delta H$, unperturbed states $|k^{(0)}\rangle$ with energies $E_k^{(0)}$, and matrix elements $\delta H_{kl} = \langle k^{(0)}|\,\delta H\,|l^{(0)}\rangle$. Electric charges are in Gaussian units, as in the lessons: the Coulomb energy is $-e^2/r$. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Higher order and normalization in non-degenerate perturbation theory" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

Take a level $n$ of $H^{(0)}$ that is not degenerate. The lesson expands its state and its energy in powers of $\lambda$,

<Formula label="The perturbed state and its energy" tex="|n(\lambda)\rangle = |n^{(0)}\rangle + \lambda|n^{(1)}\rangle + \lambda^2|n^{(2)}\rangle + \dots, \qquad E_n(\lambda) = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda E_n^{(1)} + \lambda^2E_n^{(2)} + \lambda^3E_n^{(3)} + \dots" cite="reading1" at="eq. (1.1.7)" />

with every correction $|n^{(k)}\rangle$ orthogonal to $|n^{(0)}\rangle$, and finds $|n^{(1)}\rangle$, $E_n^{(1)}$ and $E_n^{(2)}$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Go one order further: find the second-order correction to the state, $|n^{(2)}\rangle$, and the third-order correction to the energy, $E_n^{(3)}$, in terms of the matrix elements $\delta H_{kl}$ and the unperturbed energies.

The box is our addition, a test of your formula. A system has two states, with unperturbed energies $0$ and $\Delta$ (with $\Delta$ positive), and

<Formula label="A two-state test system (our addition)" tex="H^{(0)} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \Delta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \delta H = \begin{pmatrix} w_1 & v \\ v & w_2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own test system)" />

with $w_1$, $w_2$ and $v$ real. What is $E^{(3)}$ for the lower state, the one that starts at energy $0$?

<Hint>

Take the lesson's equation of order $\lambda^2$ and project it on $\langle k^{(0)}|$ with $k \neq n$. The left side gives $\left(E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}\right)\langle k^{(0)}|n^{(2)}\rangle$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the right side you need $\langle k^{(0)}|\,\delta H\,|n^{(1)}\rangle$. Insert the lesson's formula for $|n^{(1)}\rangle$, which is a sum over states $l \neq n$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the energy, the lesson has a short formula at every order: $E_n^{(k)} = \langle n^{(0)}|\,\delta H\,|n^{(k-1)}\rangle$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e3" label="$E^{(3)}$ of the lower state $=$" expr="v^2*(w2 - w1)/D^2"
  vars={{ v: [0.5, 2], w1: [0.3, 1.2], w2: [1.5, 3], D: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ w1: "w_1", w2: "w_2", D: "\\Delta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v^2*(w1 - w2)/D^2", hint: "The two terms have their signs swapped. In the double sum both energy differences are −Δ, so their product is positive and δH₂₂ = w₂ enters with a plus sign. The term with δH₁₁ = w₁ has the minus sign." },
    { expr: "v^2*w2/D^2", hint: "The term with δHₙₙ is missing. It comes from the piece with the first-order energy on the right side of the order-λ² equation." },
    { expr: "-v^2/D", hint: "That is the second-order energy. The third-order energy has three factors of δH." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equation of order $\lambda^2$ fixes the components of $|n^{(2)}\rangle$ along every other state, and the energy at order three is a matrix element of $\delta H$ with that state.

<Step title="Projecting the second-order equation">

The equation of order $\lambda^2$ is $\left(H^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}\right)|n^{(2)}\rangle = \left(E_n^{(1)} - \delta H\right)|n^{(1)}\rangle + E_n^{(2)}|n^{(0)}\rangle$ (notes eq. (1.1.13)). Act with $\langle k^{(0)}|$, where $k \neq n$. The last term drops out, and

<Formula label="The second-order equation along another state" tex="\left(E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}\right)\langle k^{(0)}|n^{(2)}\rangle = E_n^{(1)}\,\langle k^{(0)}|n^{(1)}\rangle - \langle k^{(0)}|\,\delta H\,|n^{(1)}\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eq. (1.1.13), projected on a state k ≠ n" />

</Step>

<Step title="Inserting the first-order results">

The lesson gives $E_n^{(1)} = \delta H_{nn}$ and $\langle l^{(0)}|n^{(1)}\rangle = \delta H_{ln}/\left(E_n^{(0)} - E_l^{(0)}\right)$ for $l \neq n$ (notes eqs. (1.1.21) and (1.1.32)). So $\langle k^{(0)}|\,\delta H\,|n^{(1)}\rangle = \sum_{l\neq n}\delta H_{kl}\,\delta H_{ln}/\left(E_n^{(0)} - E_l^{(0)}\right)$. Dividing by $E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}$:

<Formula label="Second-order state" tex="|n^{(2)}\rangle = \sum_{k\neq n}|k^{(0)}\rangle\left[\,\sum_{l\neq n}\frac{\delta H_{kl}\,\delta H_{ln}}{\left(E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}\right)\left(E_n^{(0)} - E_l^{(0)}\right)} - \frac{\delta H_{nn}\,\delta H_{kn}}{\left(E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}\right)^2}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

There is no component along $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ itself: the corrections are chosen orthogonal to it (notes eq. (1.1.14)).

</Step>

<Step title="The third-order energy">

With $E_n^{(3)} = \langle n^{(0)}|\,\delta H\,|n^{(2)}\rangle$ (notes eq. (1.1.26)), each $|k^{(0)}\rangle$ in the sum is replaced by $\delta H_{nk}$:

<Formula label="Third-order energy" tex="E_n^{(3)} = \sum_{k\neq n}\sum_{l\neq n}\frac{\delta H_{nk}\,\delta H_{kl}\,\delta H_{ln}}{\left(E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}\right)\left(E_n^{(0)} - E_l^{(0)}\right)} - \delta H_{nn}\sum_{k\neq n}\frac{\left|\delta H_{kn}\right|^2}{\left(E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The test system">

For the lower state, $n = 1$ and the sums hold only $k = l = 2$, with $E_1^{(0)} - E_2^{(0)} = -\Delta$:

<Formula label="Third-order energy of the lower state of the test system" tex="E^{(3)} = \frac{v\,w_2\,v}{(-\Delta)(-\Delta)} - w_1\,\frac{v^2}{\Delta^2} = \frac{v^2\left(w_2 - w_1\right)}{\Delta^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The test system can be solved exactly. To the orders needed, the lower eigenvalue of $H^{(0)} + \lambda\,\delta H$ obeys $E = \lambda w_1 - \lambda^2v^2/\left(\Delta + \lambda w_2 - E\right)$. Putting $E \approx \lambda w_1$ in the denominator and expanding gives $E = \lambda w_1 - \lambda^2v^2/\Delta + \lambda^3v^2\left(w_2 - w_1\right)/\Delta^2 + \dots$, the same third-order term. It also makes sense: only the difference $w_2 - w_1$ can matter, because adding the same constant to $w_1$ and $w_2$ shifts both levels equally. When the upper state is pushed up more than the lower one ($w_2$ larger than $w_1$), the gap grows and the second-order repulsion $-\lambda^2v^2/\text{gap}$ weakens: a positive correction.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

The state $|n(\lambda)\rangle$ is not normalized. Use it up to its first-order term to find, to order $\lambda^2$, the quantity $Z_n(\lambda)$ defined by

<Formula label="Definition of Z" tex="\frac{1}{Z_n(\lambda)} \equiv \langle n(\lambda)|n(\lambda)\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1, eq. (1)" />

What is the probability that a system in the state $|n(\lambda)\rangle$ is found in the unperturbed state $|n^{(0)}\rangle$?

The first box is our addition: $Z(\lambda)$ to order $\lambda^2$ for the lower state of the test system of part (a).

<Hint>

Write out $\langle n(\lambda)|n(\lambda)\rangle$ to order $\lambda^2$. Which cross terms vanish because the corrections are orthogonal to $|n^{(0)}\rangle$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the probability, first divide the state by its norm. Then take its overlap with $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ and square.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-z" label="$Z(\lambda) =$" expr="1 - lam^2*v^2/D^2"
  vars={{ lam: [0.1, 0.6], v: [0.5, 2], D: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ lam: "\\lambda", D: "\\Delta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1 + lam^2*v^2/D^2", hint: "That is 1/Z, the squared norm of the state, which is larger than 1. Z is its inverse." },
    { expr: "1 - lam^2*v^2/D", hint: "Check the units: Z is a pure number. The first-order state holds v divided by the gap, and the norm squares both." },
    { expr: "1 - 2*lam^2*v^2/D^2", hint: "The norm holds the first-order state once on each side, which gives one term λ²⟨n⁽¹⁾|n⁽¹⁾⟩, not two." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-prob" label="The probability to find $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ is" choices={["1 − Zₙ", "Zₙ", "1/Zₙ", "Zₙ²"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Because every correction is orthogonal to $|n^{(0)}\rangle$, the norm differs from 1 only at order $\lambda^2$, by the norm of the first-order state.

<Step title="The norm to second order">

In $\langle n(\lambda)|n(\lambda)\rangle$ the terms $\lambda\langle n^{(0)}|n^{(1)}\rangle$ and $\lambda^2\langle n^{(0)}|n^{(2)}\rangle$, and their complex conjugates, vanish. What is left to order $\lambda^2$ is $1 + \lambda^2\langle n^{(1)}|n^{(1)}\rangle$. With the lesson's first-order state:

<Formula label="Norm of the perturbed state" tex="\frac{1}{Z_n(\lambda)} = 1 + \lambda^2\sum_{k\neq n}\frac{\left|\delta H_{kn}\right|^2}{\left(E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}\right)^2} + O(\lambda^3) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Z_n(\lambda) = 1 - \lambda^2\sum_{k\neq n}\frac{\left|\delta H_{kn}\right|^2}{\left(E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}\right)^2} + O(\lambda^3)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (1.1.34), used in the norm" />

For the test system the sum has one term, $v^2/\Delta^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The probability">

The normalized state is $\sqrt{Z_n}\,|n(\lambda)\rangle$. Its overlap with $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ is $\sqrt{Z_n}\,\langle n^{(0)}|n(\lambda)\rangle = \sqrt{Z_n}$, because $\langle n^{(0)}|n(\lambda)\rangle = 1$. The probability is the square:

<Formula label="Probability to find the unperturbed state" tex="P = \frac{\left|\langle n^{(0)}|n(\lambda)\rangle\right|^2}{\langle n(\lambda)|n(\lambda)\rangle} = Z_n(\lambda)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$Z_n$ is at most 1, as a probability must be, and it equals 1 without a perturbation. The rest, $1 - Z_n$, is the probability to find one of the other unperturbed states: $\lambda^2\left|\delta H_{kn}\right|^2/\left(E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}\right)^2$ for state $k$, the square of the first-order amplitude.

There is a second route, through the Feynman–Hellmann lemma of Problem 4. Write $H^{(0)} = \sum_k E_k^{(0)}|k^{(0)}\rangle\langle k^{(0)}|$ and treat the number $E_n^{(0)}$ as the parameter. Then $\partial H/\partial E_n^{(0)} = |n^{(0)}\rangle\langle n^{(0)}|$, and its expectation value in the normalized state is the probability above. So $Z_n = \partial E_n/\partial E_n^{(0)}$. Indeed, differentiating the lesson's $E_n(\lambda) = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda\,\delta H_{nn} - \lambda^2\sum_{k\neq n}\left|\delta H_{kn}\right|^2/\left(E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}\right)$ with respect to $E_n^{(0)}$ gives the same $Z_n$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Polarizability of a particle on a ring, revisited" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

A particle of mass $m$ moves in the $xy$ plane on a circular ring of radius $a$. Its only coordinate is the angle $\phi$ around the ring, and its wavefunction $\psi(\phi)$ has period $2\pi$. The energy eigenstates and their energies are

<Formula label="States and energies on the ring" tex="\psi_n(\phi) = \langle\phi|n\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{in\phi}, \qquad E_n = \frac{\hbar^2n^2}{2ma^2} = E_1n^2, \qquad E_1 = \frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2}, \qquad n = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \ldots" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2, eqs. (2) and (3)" />

The ground state $n = 0$ is not degenerate. Every other level holds two states, $|n\rangle$ and $|-n\rangle$. The particle has charge $q$, and a uniform electric field $\mathcal{E}$ points along $x$. That adds the perturbation

<Formula label="The perturbation by the electric field" tex="\delta H = -q\,\mathcal{E}\,a\cos\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

MIT asks for the second-order energies of the degenerate states $|1\rangle$ and $|-1\rangle$ and for the corrected states to zeroth order. Parts (a) to (c) are our way of splitting that question. In the boxes, type `E1` for the energy $E_1$ (not $\hbar$ and $m$) and `Ef` for the field.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the matrix element $\langle n+1|\,\delta H\,|n\rangle$. Then show that the perturbation does not split the level of $|1\rangle$ and $|-1\rangle$ at first order.

<Hint>

Write $\cos\phi = \frac12\left(e^{i\phi} + e^{-i\phi}\right)$. What does $e^{i\phi}$ do to the wavefunction $e^{in\phi}$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-me" label="$\langle n+1|\,\delta H\,|n\rangle =$" expr="-q*Ef*a/2"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], Ef: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ Ef: "\\mathcal{E}" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-q*Ef*a", hint: "The cosine is half the sum of two exponentials. Only one of them takes n to n + 1." },
    { expr: "q*Ef*a/2", hint: "Check the sign of the perturbation: δH = −qℰa cos φ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The cosine moves a state one step up or one step down the ladder of $n$, and nowhere else.

<Step title="The cosine on a state">

Since $e^{\pm i\phi}\,e^{in\phi} = e^{i(n\pm1)\phi}$,

<Formula label="Matrix elements of the perturbation on the ring" tex="\cos\phi\,|n\rangle = \frac12\Big(|n+1\rangle + |n-1\rangle\Big) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle m|\,\delta H\,|n\rangle = -\frac{q\,\mathcal{E}\,a}{2} \ \text{ if } m = n \pm 1, \text{ and zero otherwise}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The level of n = 1 and n = −1 at first order">

Inside the level we need the four elements of $\delta H$ between $|1\rangle$ and $|-1\rangle$. The diagonal ones have $m = n$, and the off-diagonal ones have $m - n = \pm2$. All four vanish. So the matrix of $\delta H$ in the degenerate subspace is zero: both first-order energies are zero, and the degeneracy survives first order (the case of notes eq. (1.2.42), with $E_n^{(1)} = 0$).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The diagonal elements can be seen without any calculation: $|\psi_n|^2 = 1/2\pi$ is uniform around the ring, and the average of $\cos\phi$ over the ring is zero. A state with a uniform density has no dipole moment for the field to act on.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Because the level is still degenerate, the good states are found at second order. Compute the second-order matrix of the lesson in the basis $|1\rangle$, $|-1\rangle$:

<Formula label="The second-order matrix" tex="M^{(2)}_{lk} = -\sum_{p}\frac{\delta H_{l,p}\,\delta H_{p,k}}{E_p - E_1}, \qquad l, k \in \{1, -1\}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (1.2.60)" />

where $p$ runs over all states outside the level. Give its diagonal element $M^{(2)}_{1,1}$ (the same as $M^{(2)}_{-1,-1}$) and its off-diagonal element $M^{(2)}_{1,-1}$.

<Hint>

By part (a), which states $p$ are one step away from $|1\rangle$? Which are one step away from both $|1\rangle$ and $|-1\rangle$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Mind the sign of $E_p - E_1$: one of the states lies below the level.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-m11" label="$M^{(2)}_{1,1} =$" expr="q^2*Ef^2*a^2/(6*E1)"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], Ef: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 2], E1: [0.5, 2] }}

    { expr: "-q^2*Ef^2*a^2/(3*E1)", hint: "The state p = 0 lies below the level, so E_p − E₁ is negative there and its term is positive: levels repel." },
    { expr: "2*q^2*Ef^2*a^2/(3*E1)", hint: "The matrix element is qℰa/2, and it enters squared: a factor 1/4." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-m12" label="$M^{(2)}_{1,-1} =$" expr="q^2*Ef^2*a^2/(4*E1)"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], Ef: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 2], E1: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ Ef: "\\mathcal{E}", E1: "E_1" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "-q^2*Ef^2*a^2/(4*E1)", hint: "The only state in the sum is p = 0, with E₀ − E₁ = −E₁. Together with the minus sign in front of the sum that gives a plus." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At second order the perturbation acts twice: out of the level and back. The only way from $|1\rangle$ to $|-1\rangle$ in two steps goes through the ground state.

<Step title="The diagonal element">

Write $V \equiv q\mathcal{E}a/2$, so that every allowed matrix element is $-V$. From $|1\rangle$ one step leads to $p = 0$, with $E_0 - E_1 = -E_1$, or to $p = 2$, with $E_2 - E_1 = 3E_1$:

<Formula label="Diagonal element of the second-order matrix" tex="M^{(2)}_{1,1} = -\left(\frac{V^2}{-E_1} + \frac{V^2}{3E_1}\right) = \frac{2V^2}{3E_1} = \frac{q^2\mathcal{E}^2a^2}{6E_1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

For $|-1\rangle$ the states are $p = 0$ and $p = -2$, with the same energies, so $M^{(2)}_{-1,-1}$ is the same.

</Step>

<Step title="The off-diagonal element">

A state $p$ contributes only if it is one step from both $|1\rangle$ and $|-1\rangle$. That leaves $p = 0$:

<Formula label="Off-diagonal element of the second-order matrix" tex="M^{(2)}_{1,-1} = -\frac{\delta H_{1,0}\,\delta H_{0,-1}}{E_0 - E_1} = -\frac{V^2}{-E_1} = \frac{V^2}{E_1} = \frac{q^2\mathcal{E}^2a^2}{4E_1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="The unperturbed hydrogen Hamiltonian" tex="H^{(0)} = \frac{\vec p^{\,2}}{2m} - \frac{e^2}{r}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3, eq. (8)" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

A piece of mathematics that makes the rest easier. Let $a$, $b$, $c$ be whole numbers that are not negative, and $f(r)$ a function of $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$ for which the integral below exists. Prove that

<Formula label="An integral that vanishes by symmetry" tex="\int dx\,dy\,dz\;x^a\,y^b\,z^c\,f(r) = 0 \quad \text{unless } a, b \text{ and } c \text{ are all even}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 3, eq. (9)" />

<Hint>

Suppose $a$ is odd. Change the variable of integration from $x$ to $-x$. What happens to $r$, to the range of integration and to the integrand?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A reflection of one coordinate leaves $r$ and the region of integration unchanged, but flips the sign of an odd power.

<Step title="Reflecting one coordinate">

Call the integral $I$ and suppose $a$ is odd. Replace the variable of integration $x$ by $-x$. The integral again runs over the whole line, $r$ is unchanged because it holds $x^2$, and $x^a$ becomes $(-x)^a = -x^a$:

<Formula label="The integral equals minus itself" tex="I = \int dx\,dy\,dz\;(-x)^a\,y^b\,z^c\,f(r) = -\int dx\,dy\,dz\;x^a\,y^b\,z^c\,f(r) = -I \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="The other coordinates">

The same argument with $y \to -y$ works when $b$ is odd, and with $z \to -z$ when $c$ is odd. So the integral can differ from zero only when all three powers are even.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

When all three are even the integrand does not change sign under any reflection, and nothing forces a zero: for $a = b = c = 0$ and a positive $f$ the integral is positive. The statement also holds for a complex $f$, since the argument never used that $f$ is real.

</Step>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The second-order energy is a sum of squared matrix elements over energy denominators. The sum of the squared matrix elements alone is easy, by completeness. Replacing every denominator by the worst one then gives a bound.

<Step title="(i) The sum over all states">

By completeness of the states $\alpha$ (the bound states together with the unbound ones):

<Formula label="The sum of squared matrix elements as an expectation value" tex="\sum_\alpha\left|\langle\alpha|\,z\,|1,0,0\rangle\right|^2 = \sum_\alpha\langle1,0,0|\,z\,|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|\,z\,|1,0,0\rangle = \langle1,0,0|\,z^2\,|1,0,0\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Mean square of z in the ground state" tex="\langle z^2\rangle = \frac13\cdot\frac{4}{a_0^3}\int_0^\infty r^4\,e^{-2r/a_0}\,dr = \frac13\cdot\frac{4}{a_0^3}\cdot 4!\left(\frac{a_0}{2}\right)^5 = \frac13\cdot 3a_0^2 = a_0^2" cite="reading2" at="eq. </Step>

<Step title="(ii) The lowest denominator">

<Formula label="The lowest value of the energy denominator" tex="E^{(0)}_{1,0,0} - E^{(0)}_{2} = -\frac{e^2}{2a_0}\left(1 - \frac14\right) = -\frac{3e^2}{8a_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{E^{(0)}_{1,0,0} - E^{(0)}_\alpha} \geq -\frac{8a_0}{3e^2}" cite="reading2" at="eq. <Step title="(iii) The bound">

<Formula label="Bounding the second-order Stark shift" tex="E^{(2)}_{1,0,0} = \sum_{\alpha\neq(1,0,0)}\frac{e^2\mathcal{E}^2\left|\langle\alpha|z|1,0,0\rangle\right|^2}{E^{(0)}_{1,0,0} - E^{(0)}_\alpha} \;\geq\; -\frac{8a_0}{3e^2}\;e^2\mathcal{E}^2\sum_{\alpha}\left|\langle\alpha|z|1,0,0\rangle\right|^2 = -\frac83\,a_0^3\,\mathcal{E}^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

The term $\alpha = (1,0,0)$ may be added to the last sum because it is zero, by part (b). So $C = \frac83 \approx 2.667$.

</Step>

<Step title="What it means: polarizability">

A system has polarizability $\alpha_p$ if a field induces the dipole moment $\vec p = \alpha_p\vec{\mathcal{E}}$ in it. Building up the field from zero, the energy changes by $-\int p\,d\mathcal{E} = -\frac12\alpha_p\mathcal{E}^2$. (MIT's sheet writes this energy as $-\alpha\mathcal{E}^2$, without the factor $\frac12$. That holds for a dipole of fixed size, not for an induced one.) MIT quotes the exact value, which needs the unbound states as well: $C = \frac94$, a polarizability of $\frac92a_0^3$ (our addition). Polarizability is a step towards the van der Waals force between atoms.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

There is also a bound from the other side (our addition). Every term of the sum is negative, so one term alone already gives a shift that the full sum must lie below. </Step>

The wires give $110\ \text{V}/1.1\ \text{cm} = 100$ V/cm, which is $10^4$ V/m. There the Stark shift of part (d) is $3e\mathcal{E}a_0 = 3 \times 10^4 \times 5.29177 \times 10^{-11}$ eV $= 1.59$ μeV (our addition), about 3.5% of the fine-structure splitting (our addition). The fine structure is far larger. So the atom is not polarized as part (d) says: the good states are set first by the fine structure (states of definite $j$), and the field is a small perturbation of those.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The splitting is of the size the lesson promises for the fine structure: its scale is $\alpha^4mc^2 \approx 1.45 \times 10^{-3}$ eV, here divided by 32. It is tiny next to the distance between the levels $n = 1$ and $n = 2$, which is about 10 eV (both numbers are our additions), so there is a wide range of fields, well above 2852 V/cm, in which part (d) is a good description. One remark on small fields. The lesson's fine structure depends only on $j$, so $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$ stay degenerate, and $\delta H_{\text{Stark}}$ links them. A careful treatment of weak fields therefore diagonalizes the field inside the $j = \frac12$ states. That goes beyond this problem.

</Step>
