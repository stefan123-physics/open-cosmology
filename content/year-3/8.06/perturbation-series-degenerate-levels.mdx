This lecture finishes the discussion of non-degenerate perturbation theory with two general remarks, a test of when the series can be trusted and a classic example, and then begins **degenerate** perturbation theory (L2.1–L2.4 transcripts; notes sections 1.1–1.2). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 1; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Two remarks on the energy formulas

**The ground state, to first order, is too high** (L2.1 transcript pp. 1–2). Take the ground state, $n = 0$. Its energy to first order is the expectation value of the full Hamiltonian in the unperturbed ground state:

<Formula label="First order as an expectation value" tex="E_0^{(0)} + \lambda E_0^{(1)} = \langle 0^{(0)}|H^{(0)}|0^{(0)}\rangle + \lambda\langle 0^{(0)}|\delta H|0^{(0)}\rangle = \langle 0^{(0)}|H(\lambda)|0^{(0)}\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.38)" />

By the variational principle, the expectation value of a Hamiltonian in any normalized state is at least its ground-state energy, so

<Formula label="First order overestimates the ground state" tex="E_0^{(0)} + \lambda E_0^{(1)} \ge E_0(\lambda)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.39)" />

for every $\lambda$. This fits the second-order term: for the ground state every denominator $E_k^{(0)} - E_0^{(0)}$ is positive, so the $\lambda^2$ correction is negative or zero and can only bring the estimate down. (Zwiebach corrects a slip on the board here: the second term must contain $\delta H$.)

**Levels repel** (L2.1 transcript p. 3). For a general level $n$, split the second-order sum into the states above and the states below, flipping the sign of the denominator for the second group:

<Formula label="Level repulsion" tex="E_n^{(2)} = -\sum_{k>n}\frac{|\delta H_{kn}|^2}{E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}} + \sum_{k<n}\frac{|\delta H_{kn}|^2}{E_n^{(0)} - E_k^{(0)}}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.41)" />

Every denominator is now positive. The states above $n$ push its energy down, the states below push it up: the levels, as is usually said, **repel** each other.

## When can the series be trusted?

We said $\delta H$ should be "small" compared with $H^{(0)}$, but both are operators. Small matrix elements are necessary, but not enough (L2.1 transcript pp. 3–6). A two-state example settles what matters. Take a known Hamiltonian, diagonal in its own eigenbasis, and a perturbation with only off-diagonal entries:

<Formula label="Two-state example" tex="H(\lambda) = \begin{pmatrix} E_1^{(0)} & 0 \\ 0 & E_2^{(0)} \end{pmatrix} + \lambda\begin{pmatrix} 0 & V \\ V^* & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (1.1.43), (1.1.44)" />

Its eigenvalues are known exactly:

<Formula label="Exact energies" tex="E_\pm(\lambda) = \frac{E_1^{(0)} + E_2^{(0)}}{2} \pm \frac{E_1^{(0)} - E_2^{(0)}}{2}\sqrt{1 + \frac{\lambda^2|V|^2}{\frac14(E_1^{(0)} - E_2^{(0)})^2}}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.45)" />

Perturbation theory amounts to expanding the square root in powers of $\lambda$. The diagonal entries of the perturbation vanish, so there is no first-order shift, as $\sqrt{1 + \epsilon} \approx 1 + \epsilon/2$ confirms. The series needed is

<Formula label="The square-root series" tex="f(z) = \sqrt{1 + z^2} = 1 + \frac{z^2}{2} - \frac{z^4}{8} + \frac{z^6}{16} - \frac{5z^8}{128} + O(z^{10})" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.46)" />

(Both the lecture and the notes give the $z^6$ coefficient as one sixth, and the notes print the $z^8$ term with a plus sign; the correct values, used here, are one sixteenth and minus five over 128.) As a function of complex $z$, $\sqrt{1 + z^2}$ has branch points at $z = \pm i$, so the series converges only for $|z| < 1$ (L2.1 transcript p. 5). Here $z = 2\lambda|V|/(E_1^{(0)} - E_2^{(0)})$, so convergence requires

<Formula label="Condition for convergence" tex="|\lambda V| < \tfrac12\left|E_1^{(0)} - E_2^{(0)}\right|" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.47)" />

**The perturbation must be small compared with the energy differences**, not just with the energies. In Zwiebach's illustration, two levels at 100 and 101 (in some energy unit) are not gently perturbed by a coupling of 2 or 3, even though 2 or 3 is small next to 100. This is also why degenerate levels, with zero energy difference, need separate treatment.

## The anharmonic oscillator

In a harmonic oscillator all levels are equally spaced; an anharmonic term spoils this (L2.2 transcript pp. 1–2). Here we add an $x^4$ term, which makes the potential grow faster than $x^2$. The oscillator's natural length is

<Formula label="Oscillator length scale" tex="d^2 = \frac{\hbar}{m\omega}, \qquad \frac{\hat x}{d} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (1.1.49), (1.1.50)" />

and a quartic perturbation with units of energy, times the unit-free $\lambda$, is

<Formula label="Quartic perturbation" tex="\lambda\,\delta H = \lambda\,\hbar\omega\,\frac{\hat x^4}{d^4} = \lambda\,\frac{\hbar\omega}{4}\left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)^4" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (1.1.51), (1.1.52)" />

The unperturbed states are the number states $|k\rangle$, with $E_k^{(0)} = \hbar\omega(k + \frac12)$.

**First order** (L2.2 transcript pp. 2–3). Using $[\hat a, \hat a^\dagger] = 1$ one finds $\langle0|(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger)^4|0\rangle = 3$, so

<Formula label="First-order ground-state energy" tex="E_0^{(1)} = \frac34\hbar\omega, \qquad E_0(\lambda) = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\left(1 + \frac32\lambda\right) + O(\lambda^2)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (1.1.54)–(1.1.56)" />

The energy rises, as it should: the $x^4$ term squeezes the ground state.

**Second order** (L2.2 transcript pp. 3–4). Acting with $(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger)^4$ on the ground state is, up to constants, multiplying the ground-state wavefunction by $x^4$, which gives an even function. With at most four raising operators, only $|0\rangle$, $|2\rangle$ and $|4\rangle$ can appear:

<Formula label="The quartic acting on the vacuum" tex="(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger)^4|0\rangle = 3|0\rangle + 6\sqrt2\,|2\rangle + \sqrt{4!}\,|4\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.59)" />

So the sum over $k$ has only two terms, with $\delta H_{20} = \frac{3\sqrt2}{2}\hbar\omega$, $\delta H_{40} = \frac{\sqrt6}{2}\hbar\omega$ and denominators $2\hbar\omega$ and $4\hbar\omega$:

<Formula label="Second-order ground-state energy" tex="E_0^{(2)} = -\frac{|\delta H_{20}|^2}{2\hbar\omega} - \frac{|\delta H_{40}|^2}{4\hbar\omega} = -\frac94\hbar\omega - \frac38\hbar\omega = -\frac{21}{8}\hbar\omega" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.61)" />

**The full series** (L2.2 transcript p. 5). No exact solution of this problem is known, and it has been studied in hundreds of papers. Bender and Wu carried the expansion to very high order in 1969:

<Formula label="Bender and Wu's series" tex="E_0(\lambda) = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\left(1 + \frac32\lambda - \frac{21}{4}\lambda^2 + \frac{333}{8}\lambda^3 - \frac{30885}{64}\lambda^4 + O(\lambda^5)\right)" cite="benderwu" at="as quoted in the 8.06 notes, eq. (1.1.63)" />

(In the recorded lecture the fourth coefficient is said as 30,855/64; the notes give 30,885/64, and the notes' footnote adds that Bender and Wu's own coefficients must be doubled because they normalize the ground-state energy differently.) They also showed that the coefficients grow so fast that **the series converges for no nonzero $\lambda$**: its radius of convergence is zero. It is still useful, as an **asymptotic expansion**. For small $\lambda$ the terms first shrink, then eventually grow; stopping where they are smallest gives a good approximation. Stirling's formula for the factorial and the Feynman-diagram expansion of quantum electrodynamics are asymptotic series too: few series in physics actually converge.

The first-order correction to the state follows from the same matrix elements; Zwiebach leaves it as an exercise, and the notes work it out (notes eqs. (1.1.64)–(1.1.66)).

<WorkedExample title="Where to stop an asymptotic series">
<Problem>
Take $\lambda = 0.1$ in Bender and Wu's series. (a) Compute the terms inside the bracket up to $\lambda^4$. (b) Where should the sum be stopped, and what estimate for $E_0$ does that give?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The terms">
$\frac32\lambda = 0.15$; $\frac{21}{4}\lambda^2 = 0.0525$; $\frac{333}{8}\lambda^3 \approx 0.0416$; $\frac{30885}{64}\lambda^4 \approx 0.0483$. The terms shrink for three steps and then start to grow again; the next one, from the notes' $\lambda^5$ coefficient $916731/128$, is about $0.0716$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Stopping at the smallest term">
The smallest term is the $\lambda^3$ one, so stop there: the bracket is $1 + 0.15 - 0.0525 + 0.0416 \approx 1.139$, and $E_0 \approx 1.139 \times \frac{\hbar\omega}{2} \approx 0.570\,\hbar\omega$. The error is smaller than the first term left out, so a few per cent here. For $\lambda = 0.01$ the terms fall much further before turning, and the series is far more accurate.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Degenerate levels: why the formulas fail

Start with the simplest case (L2.3 transcript pp. 1–2): two states with the same energy and an off-diagonal perturbation,

<Formula label="Degenerate toy model" tex="H(\lambda) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} + \lambda\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \lambda \\ \lambda & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (1.2.1)–(1.2.3)" />

Applying the non-degenerate result $E_n = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda\,\delta H_{nn}$ to the states $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ gives no shift at all, because the diagonal entries of $\delta H$ are zero. But the exact eigenvalues are $1 \pm \lambda$, with eigenvectors $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$ and $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)$ (L2.3 transcript p. 1). The first-order formula failed completely, and the state formula divides $\delta H_{21} = 1$ by an energy difference of zero. (The notes call this result ambiguous, as if the numerator were also zero; it is not, the formula simply diverges.)

It looks as if the eigenvectors jump from $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ to the new ones as soon as $\lambda$ is nonzero, however tiny. They do not (L2.3 transcript p. 2). For a degenerate $H^{(0)}$, **any** combination of the two states is an eigenvector; $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ were only one arbitrary choice. The perturbation breaks the degeneracy and **selects** a particular basis, and that is the basis we should have started from even at $\lambda = 0$. The task is to find it.

## The systematic set-up

Suppose $H^{(0)}$ has $N$ orthonormal states with the same energy $E_n^{(0)}$ (L2.3 transcript pp. 2–3). Label them with two labels, $|n^{(0)};k\rangle$ with $k = 1, \dots, N$, so they cannot be confused with other states:

<Formula label="The degenerate subspace" tex="H^{(0)}|n^{(0)};k\rangle = E_n^{(0)}|n^{(0)};k\rangle, \qquad \langle n^{(0)};p|n^{(0)};l\rangle = \delta_{pl}, \qquad V_N = \text{span}\{|n^{(0)};k\rangle\}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (1.2.12)–(1.2.14)" />

All the other eigenstates, $|p^{(0)}\rangle$ with a single label, span the rest of the state space, $\hat V$, which is orthogonal to $V_N$. This is not an exotic situation: the $n = 2$ level of hydrogen already has four degenerate states (L2.3 transcript p. 3).

Each degenerate state now evolves with its own energy, so the energy corrections carry the label $k$ (L2.3 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Expansion in the degenerate case" tex="|n;k(\lambda)\rangle = |n^{(0)};k\rangle + \lambda|n^{(1)};k\rangle + \dots, \qquad E_{n,k}(\lambda) = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda E_{n,k}^{(1)} + \lambda^2E_{n,k}^{(2)} + \dots" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.17)" />

If the $E_{n,k}^{(1)}$ differ, the states split at first order. As before, each correction $|n^{(p)};k\rangle$ can be chosen orthogonal to its own starting state $|n^{(0)};k\rangle$, but it may still have components along the **other** states of $V_N$, and these will be the hard part. The equations order by order have the same form as before:

<Formula label="The first-order equation" tex="(H^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)})|n^{(1)};k\rangle = (E_{n,k}^{(1)} - \delta H)|n^{(0)};k\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.21)" />

The plan has three steps (L2.4 transcript p. 1): the order-$\lambda$ equation gives the energy corrections, then the part of $|n^{(1)};k\rangle$ in $\hat V$; the part in $V_N$ needs the order-$\lambda^2$ equation. This lecture takes the first step.

## Step one: the good basis

Act on the first-order equation with $\langle n^{(0)};l|$ (L2.4 transcript pp. 1–2). The left side vanishes, since that bra also has energy $E_n^{(0)}$, and orthonormality leaves

<Formula label="The perturbation must be diagonal in the degenerate subspace" tex="\langle n^{(0)};l|\,\delta H\,|n^{(0)};k\rangle = E_{n,k}^{(1)}\,\delta_{lk}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.24)" />

for all $k$ and $l$ from 1 to $N$. This is a remarkable statement: **the basis of the degenerate subspace must be chosen so that $\delta H$ is diagonal there.** Outside $V_N$ it need not be diagonal; inside, it must, or perturbation theory cannot even start. In the toy model the right basis was $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, \pm1)$, the eigenvectors of $\delta H$. Setting $l = k$, the first-order energies are the diagonal entries:

<Formula label="First-order energies, degenerate case" tex="E_{n,k}^{(1)} = \langle n^{(0)};k|\,\delta H\,|n^{(0)};k\rangle \equiv \delta H_{nk,nk}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.25)" />

If these $N$ numbers are all different, the degeneracy is **lifted** at first order, and the basis that diagonalizes $\delta H$ in $V_N$ is the **good basis**: the states that deform continuously as $\lambda$ is turned on. If some of them coincide, the good basis must be found at second order, the subject of the next lecture.

## Summary

- To first order the ground-state energy is an expectation value of $H(\lambda)$, so it is never below the true value; at second order, levels repel.
- The perturbation series converges only if the perturbation is small compared with the **energy differences** it connects.
- Quartic anharmonic oscillator: $E_0 = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}(1 + \frac32\lambda - \frac{21}{4}\lambda^2 + \dots)$, an asymptotic series with zero radius of convergence, to be stopped at its smallest term.
- For a degenerate level the basis of $V_N$ must make $\delta H$ diagonal there; the first-order shifts are its diagonal entries, and if they all differ that basis is the good basis.
