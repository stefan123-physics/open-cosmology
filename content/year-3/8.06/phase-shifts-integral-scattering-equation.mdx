The last lecture showed that a central potential acts on each partial wave only through a phase shift $\delta_l$, and that the phase shifts give the scattering amplitude and the cross section. This one first shows how to compute them for any central potential, then explains why only a few of them usually matter, and finally turns to a method that also works without spherical symmetry: writing the Schrödinger equation as an **integral equation** with a **Green's function** (L21.1–L21.3 transcripts; notes sections 7.2.3 and 7.3, pp. 142–147). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in three short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 7; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The header of the third transcript calls it "L21.2"; it is the third video, L21.3.)

## What we know so far

Zwiebach opens with the results on the board (L21.1 transcript pp. 1–2). Away from the scattering centre, where the potential vanishes, the solution for a central potential is a sum of partial waves $\left(A_l\,j_l(kr) + B_l\,n_l(kr)\right)Y_{l0}(\theta)$, and each phase shift follows from the ratio of the coefficients, $\tan\delta_l = -B_l/A_l$. [Speaking, he says the tangent is set by "the ratio" $B_l/A_l$; the minus sign is part of the definition, as on the previous page.] Far away each wave is a sine shifted by its phase shift, $\sin(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta_l)$, and the phase shifts give $f(\theta)$ and the total cross section. If the potential is not spherically symmetric, the amplitude depends on $\phi$ as well, $f(\theta, \phi)$, and phase shifts are not the right tool; the last part of the lecture gives another method for that case.

## Solving inside the potential

How do you find the phase shifts for a given potential? There is no way around the region $r < a$ where the potential acts, since the phase shifts depend on it (L21.1 transcript p. 2). With $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, one must solve the radial equation there, for each $l$ (L21.1 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The radial equation inside the potential" tex="\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{d^2}{dr^2} + V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2\,l(l+1)}{2mr^2}\right)u_l(r) = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\,u_l(r), \qquad r < a" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The boundary condition at the origin is $u_l \propto r^{l+1}$ as $r \to 0$, and the radial function is $R_l(r) = u_l(r)/r$, multiplied by $Y_{l0}(\theta)$ in the full solution. (Zwiebach adds the wave number as a second label on both functions; this page leaves it out.) This step depends on the potential, and nothing can do it for you (L21.1 transcript pp. 2–3).

## Matching at the edge

For $r > a$ the solution of the same partial wave is $A_l\,j_l(kr) + B_l\,n_l(kr)$, and the two must join smoothly at $r = a$ (L21.1 transcript p. 3; notes p. 142). The functions match:

<Formula label="Matching the functions at r = a" tex="R_l(a) = A_l\,j_l(ka) + B_l\,n_l(ka)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.2.43), p. 142" />

and so do the derivatives. A prime on $R_l$ means $d/dr$; a prime on $j_l$ or $n_l$ means the derivative with respect to the argument, so differentiating $j_l(kr)$ gives a factor $k$. Multiplying both sides by $a$ keeps the units the same as above (L21.1 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Matching the derivatives at r = a" tex="a\,R_{l}'(a) = ka\left(A_l\,j_l'(ka) + B_l\,n_l'(ka)\right)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.2.44), p. 143" />

If the potential has a delta function exactly at $r = a$, as in one of the problem-set exercises, the derivative jumps there instead, and the matching changes accordingly; Zwiebach assumes there is none (L21.1 transcript p. 3).

## The phase shift from the matching

We want only the phase shift, not the normalization, so divide the two equations (L21.1 transcript pp. 3–4). Dividing numerator and denominator by $A_l$ and using $B_l/A_l = -\tan\delta_l$ gives (L21.1 transcript p. 4; notes p. 143)

<Formula label="The ratio of the matching conditions" tex="\beta_l = \frac{a\,R_{l}'(a)}{R_l(a)} = ka\,\frac{j_l'(ka) - \tan\delta_l\;n_l'(ka)}{j_l(ka) - \tan\delta_l\;n_l(ka)}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.2.45), p. 143" />

The notes call this quantity $\beta_l$, the logarithmic derivative of the radial solution (times $a$); the lecture does not name it. [In the notes as printed, eqs. (7.2.45)–(7.2.48), the primes on $j_l$ are missing in the numerators, and the derivative of the Neumann function is printed as an eta with a prime; the equation is as above.] Everything here is known except $\tan\delta_l$: the left side comes from the inside solution, and the spherical Bessel functions are known. So the problem is solved. Solving for $\tan\delta_l$ (L21.1 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The phase shift of any central potential" tex="\tan\delta_l = \frac{j_l'(ka) - \gamma_l\,j_l(ka)}{n_l'(ka) - \gamma_l\,n_l(ka)}, \qquad \gamma_l = \frac{R_{l}'(a)}{k\,R_l(a)} = \frac{\beta_l}{ka}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

(The lecture writes the ratio $R_{l}'(a)/(kR_l(a))$ out in full in both places; the name $\gamma_l$ is this page's shorthand.) It may be messy, but the spherical Bessel functions are only trigonometric functions times powers, much simpler than ordinary Bessel functions. In practice, Zwiebach says, one often computes the first ten or so phase shifts numerically for a given potential and gets a very accurate answer (L21.1 transcript p. 4). He erases the formula in the next video, since the problem set will not need it in this form (L21.2 transcript p. 1).

The notes push the same calculation further instead, to the phase factor itself, an approach not in the lecture (notes eqs. (7.2.46)–(7.2.49), p. 143). Writing $\tan\delta_l$ with exponentials and solving for $e^{2i\delta_l}$ gives

<Formula label="The phase factor from the logarithmic derivative" tex="e^{2i\delta_l} = -\,\frac{j_l - i\,n_l}{j_l + i\,n_l}\cdot\frac{\beta_l - ka\,\frac{j_l' - i\,n_l'}{j_l - i\,n_l}}{\beta_l - ka\,\frac{j_l' + i\,n_l'}{j_l + i\,n_l}}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.2.48), p. 143" />

with every Bessel function at $ka$ (primes restored, as in (7.2.45)). The first factor is exactly the hard-sphere phase factor of the last lecture, $e^{2i\delta_l} = (n_l + i\,j_l)/(n_l - i\,j_l)$, since $n_l + i\,j_l = i\,(j_l - i\,n_l)$ and $n_l - i\,j_l = -i\,(j_l + i\,n_l)$. So any phase shift is the hard-sphere one plus a correction from the bracket, which depends on the inside solution through $\beta_l$ (notes eq. (7.2.49), p. 143).

<Problem>
A particle of mass $m$ meets an attractive spherical well, $V = -V_0$ for $r < a$ and $V = 0$ outside, with $a = 2$ fm. Outside, $k = 0.25$ fm⁻¹; inside, the kinetic energy is larger, and the wave number is $K = \sqrt{k^2 + 2mV_0/\hbar^2} = 0.7$ fm⁻¹. (a) Find $\gamma_0$ from the inside solution. (b) Use the general formula to find $\tan\delta_0$ and $\delta_0$. (c) Find the s-wave cross section and compare it with the unitarity bound. (d) Estimate $\delta_1$ in the same way and check the impact-parameter argument below.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Inside the well">
For $l = 0$ the radial equation inside is $d^2u_0/dr^2 = -K^2u_0$, and $u_0 \propto r$ at the origin selects $u_0 = \sin Kr$, so $R_0 = \sin(Kr)/r$. Then $R_0'(a)/R_0(a) = K\cot Ka - 1/a$, and with $ka = 0.5$ and $Ka = 1.4$, $\gamma_0 = (Ka\cot Ka - 1)/ka = (0.2415 - 1)/0.5$, about $-1.517$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The phase shift">
With $x = ka = 0.5$: $j_0 = \sin x/x = 0.9589$, $n_0 = -\cos x/x = -1.7552$, $j_0' = \cos x/x - \sin x/x^2 = -0.1625$ and $n_0' = \sin x/x + \cos x/x^2 = 4.4692$. The formula gives $\tan\delta_0 = (-0.1625 + 1.517 \times 0.9589)/(4.4692 - 1.517 \times 1.7552) = 1.2921/1.8065$ (with unrounded values), about 0.7153, so $\delta_0$ is about 0.621 rad, or $35.6°$. As a check, the textbook result for this well, $\delta_0 = -ka + \arctan\left(\frac{k}{K}\tan Ka\right)$, gives $-0.5 + \arctan(2.071)$, the same 0.621 rad. The attractive well pulls the wave in: the phase shift is positive, the opposite of the hard sphere's $-ka$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Cross section and bound">
$\sigma_0 = (4\pi/k^2)\sin^2\delta_0$, with $4\pi/k^2 = 201.1$ fm² and $\sin^2\delta_0 = 0.338$, is about 68.0 fm², or 0.680 b. It is a third of the bound $4\pi/k^2$ and larger than the low-energy value $4\pi a^2 = 50.3$ fm² of a hard sphere of the same size; at this $ka = 0.5$ the hard sphere's s wave gives $(4\pi/k^2)\sin^2(0.5)$, about 46.2 fm².
</Step>
<Step title="(d) The p wave">
For $l = 1$ the inside solution is $R_1 = j_1(Kr)$, so $\gamma_1 = (K/k)\,j_1'(Ka)/j_1(Ka)$, about 1.168. The same formula with $j_1$ and $n_1$ at $ka = 0.5$ gives $\tan\delta_1$ of about 0.0056, so $\delta_1$ is only about $0.32°$, and $\sigma_1 = 3(4\pi/k^2)\sin^2\delta_1$ is about 0.019 fm², less than 0.03% of $\sigma_0$. With $ka = 0.5$, the s wave is all that matters, just as the argument of the next section predicts.
</Step>

## Why only a few phase shifts matter

Phase shifts are useful when the first few dominate the cross section; a sum that needs very large $l$ is hard to do (L21.2 transcript p. 1; notes p. 143). This happens when $ka \ll 1$ (in the notes, $ka < 1$), with $a$ the range of the potential: either a **short range** or a **low energy**, since the energy grows as $k^2$. Zwiebach gives three ways to see why.

**The impact parameter.** Picture a classical particle with momentum $p$ coming in along a line that passes the centre at a distance $b$, the **impact parameter** (L21.2 transcript pp. 1–2). Its angular momentum is $L = bp$. For a partial wave, $L$ is about $\hbar l$ and $p = \hbar k$, so (L21.2 transcript p. 2)

<Formula label="Impact parameter of a partial wave" tex="L \simeq bp \quad\to\quad \hbar l \simeq b\,\hbar k \quad\to\quad b \simeq \frac{l}{k}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.2.50), p. 143" />

Increasing $l$ moves the particle further out; $l = 0$ hits the target head on. A particle that passes outside the range, $b > a$, should scatter little or not at all (L21.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Which partial waves scatter" tex="\frac{l}{k} > a \quad\to\quad \text{expect no scattering for } l > ka" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.2.51), p. 144" />

So the phase shifts are small for $l > ka$. For $ka \ll 1$ only $l = 0$ contributes; for $ka$ around 1 or 2, a few; for $ka$ about 5, one can hope that $l$ up to about 5 gives most of it (L21.2 transcript p. 2). [This is a semi-classical rule of thumb, not a theorem: the phase shifts for $l > ka$ fall off quickly but are not exactly zero, especially for a potential with a long tail. The lecture says "little or no scattering"; the notes state it once plainly ("When b > a there is no scattering") and box it as "expect no scattering" for $l > ka$.]

**Where the free wave lives.** The second argument looks at the free partial wave $j_l(kr)\,Y_{l0}(\theta)$ (L21.2 transcript p. 3; notes p. 144). It solves the radial equation with $V = 0$, but that equation still has the centrifugal barrier, an effective potential $\hbar^2l(l+1)/(2mr^2)$, and the wave has energy $\hbar^2k^2/2m$. Inside the turning point, where the barrier is higher than the energy, the wave must be exponentially small. The turning point $r_\star$ is where the two are equal (L21.2 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The turning point of the centrifugal barrier" tex="\frac{\hbar^2\,l(l+1)}{2m\,r_\star^2} = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} \quad\to\quad k^2r_\star^2 = l(l+1) \quad\to\quad kr_\star \simeq l" cite="reading4" at="eqs. (7.2.52)–(7.2.54), p. 144" />

So $j_l(kr)$ is essentially zero for $r < l/k$: the partial wave keeps away from the centre by about its impact parameter, and if $l/k > a$ it never reaches the potential. The two intuitions agree. (The notes plot $\rho^2j_l^2(\rho)$ to show this, Figure 7.2; the graph of $\rho\,j_l(\rho)$ on the [Lecture 19 page](/courses/8-06/scattering-amplitude-cross-sections-partial-waves) shows the same thing, each $j_l$ staying small until $\rho$ is about $l$.)

## Partial-wave unitarity

The third argument starts from the partial cross sections of the last lecture (L21.2 transcript p. 4): $\sigma = \sum_l\sigma_l$, with

<Formula label="Partial cross sections and their bound" tex="\sigma_l = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\,(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l \leq \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\,(2l+1)" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

As last lesson noted in passing, the bound holds simply because $\sin^2\delta_l \leq 1$. Zwiebach presents it as a test: a calculated partial cross section that breaks it is wrong. It is called **partial-wave unitarity**, since unitarity is about the conservation of probability, and a cross section measures the probability to scatter (L21.2 transcript p. 4). A partial wave reaches the bound when its phase shift is $90°$.

## A classical estimate

There is a classical way to see these numbers (L21.2 transcript pp. 4–6). Classical scattering is deterministic, so think of the incoming particles that cross a thin ring, between impact parameters $b$ and $b + \Delta b$, on a cylinder around the beam axis: they all scatter in some definite way, spreading out like the bell of a trumpet, and the ring's area is their contribution to the total cross section. Give each partial wave its own ring, with $b = l/k$, and a thickness equal to the change of $b$ when $l$ changes by one, $\Delta b = 1/k$. Then (L21.2 transcript p. 5)

<Formula label="The classical area of one partial wave" tex="2\pi b\,\Delta b = 2\pi\,\frac{l}{k}\cdot\frac{1}{k} = \frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{k^2}\,(2l)" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

That is about a quarter of the quantum maximum $(4\pi/k^2)(2l + 1)$: a rough classical argument lands within a modest factor of the quantum bound (L21.2 transcript pp. 5–6). [The lecture calls this area "the differential cross section"; it is the ring's contribution to the total cross section, as Zwiebach also says.] Adding the rings up to $l = ka$ gives about $\pi(ka)^2/k^2 = \pi a^2$, the geometric cross section of the target, as a classical picture should. (This sum is not in the lecture.)

## The integral scattering equation

Now a new method, which can find $f(\theta, \phi)$ when phase shifts cannot, because the potential is not spherically symmetric (L21.3 transcript p. 1; notes p. 144). For a spherical problem, phase shifts are still the first thing to try. The starting point is the Schrödinger equation with any potential:

<Formula label="The Schrödinger equation" tex="\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\nabla^2 + V(\vec r)\right)\psi(\vec r) = E\,\psi(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.1), p. 144" />

(The notes write the mass as a capital M.) To clean up the units, set the energy to that of a plane wave and rescale the potential (L21.3 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Energy and rescaled potential" tex="E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}, \qquad V(\vec r) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,U(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.2), p. 145" />

$U$ has units of one over length squared, like $k^2$. The equation becomes (L21.3 transcript p. 1)

<Formula label="The equation in terms of U" tex="\left(-\nabla^2 + U(\vec r)\right)\psi(\vec r) = k^2\,\psi(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.3), p. 145" />

and, moving terms so that the left side is a simple operator and the potential stands on the right (L21.3 transcript p. 1),

<Formula label="The equation to solve" tex="\left(\nabla^2 + k^2\right)\psi(\vec r) = U(\vec r)\,\psi(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.4), p. 145" />

Think of the right side as a source. With no potential it would be zero; with a potential, the solution feeds back into its own source.

## The Green's function

An equation of this kind, a nice operator acting on $\psi$ and something on the right, can be attacked with a **Green's function** (L21.3 transcript p. 2). It is the solution of the same equation with a point source, a delta function at $\vec r'$:

<Formula label="Definition of the Green's function" tex="\left(\nabla^2 + k^2\right)G(\vec r - \vec r') = \delta^3(\vec r - \vec r')" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.5), p. 145" />

It is worth having for two reasons, Zwiebach says: it does not involve the potential, so it is solved once and for all, and once it is known the original equation can be written down at once. The claim (L21.3 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="The integral equation" tex="\psi(\vec r) = \psi_0(\vec r) + \int d^3r'\;G(\vec r - \vec r')\,U(\vec r')\,\psi(\vec r'), \qquad \left(\nabla^2 + k^2\right)\psi_0(\vec r) = 0" cite="reading4" at="eqs. (7.3.6)–(7.3.7), p. 145" />

Any solution of the equation with no source can be added, so $\psi_0$ is still to be chosen. To check the claim, apply $\nabla^2 + k^2$, which acts on $\vec r$ and not on the integration variable $\vec r'$ (L21.3 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Checking the integral equation" tex="\left(\nabla^2 + k^2\right)\psi(\vec r) = \int d^3r'\;\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r')\,U(\vec r')\,\psi(\vec r') = U(\vec r)\,\psi(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.8), p. 145" />

The $\psi_0$ term gives zero, and the delta function sets $\vec r' = \vec r$. This is not yet a solution: the unknown $\psi$ appears both outside and inside the integral, which is why it is called an **integral equation**. It might even look worse than the differential equation. Its power is in the insight it gives and in approximations: one can put a first guess for $\psi$ under the integral and improve it step by step, which leads to the Born approximation (L21.3 transcript p. 3).

To find $G$, set $\vec r' = 0$ and solve for $G(\vec r)$; replacing $\vec r$ by $\vec r - \vec r'$ afterwards gives the rest (L21.3 transcript p. 4). There are several Green's functions, and the problem decides which to use. They can be derived with contour integrals, but here an educated guess is enough. The spherical waves $e^{\pm ikr}/r$ from the start of the scattering chapter satisfy $(\nabla^2 + k^2)(e^{\pm ikr}/r) = 0$ for $r \neq 0$, as the radial Laplacian $\nabla^2 g = \frac{1}{r}\,\frac{d^2}{dr^2}(rg)$ quickly shows. Near $r = 0$ they behave like $1/r$, and from electromagnetism, Poisson's equation for the potential of a point charge, $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r)$. So with a factor $-1/4\pi$ the delta function comes out right (L21.3 transcript pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="The Green's functions" tex="G_\pm(\vec r) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\frac{e^{\pm ikr}}{r}" cite="reading4" at="eqs. (7.3.9)–(7.3.10), p. 145" />

The notes call $G_+$ the **outgoing wave** Green's function and $G_-$ the **ingoing wave** one (notes p. 146). They also check the result directly with vector calculus, which the lecture leaves to Griffiths or to the reader (notes eqs. (7.3.11)–(7.3.14), p. 146). Writing $G_\pm$ as $e^{\pm ikr}$ times $-1/(4\pi r)$, the Laplacian of the product has three terms. With $\nabla^2e^{\pm ikr} = \left(-k^2 \pm 2ik/r\right)e^{\pm ikr}$, the first term gives $-k^2G_\pm$ plus a piece $\mp 2ik\,e^{\pm ikr}/(4\pi r^2)$; the second gives $e^{\pm ikr}\,\delta^3(\vec r)$, which is just $\delta^3(\vec r)$; and the cross term, twice the product of the two gradients, gives $\pm 2ik\,e^{\pm ikr}/(4\pi r^2)$, which cancels the leftover piece. [The second line of the notes' eq. (7.3.14) prints this leftover piece with $4\pi r$ in the denominator; it is $4\pi r^2$, as the line above implies.] So

<Formula label="Direct check of the Green's function" tex="\nabla^2G_\pm(\vec r) = -k^2\,G_\pm(\vec r) + \delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.14), p. 146" />

## Choosing the scattering solution

Now fit the choices to the problem (L21.3 transcript p. 5). The scattering solution should look like $e^{ikz} + f(\theta, \phi)\,e^{ikr}/r$, so take the plane wave as the free solution and the Green's function that makes outgoing waves:

<Formula label="The choices for scattering" tex="\psi_0(\vec r) = e^{ikz}, \qquad G = G_+" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.15), p. 146" />

Any other free solution, or $G_-$, would also give a solution of the Schrödinger equation, just not the one we are after. With these choices (L21.3 transcript pp. 5–6),

<Formula label="The integral equation for scattering" tex="\psi(\vec r) = e^{ikz} + \int d^3r'\;G_+(\vec r - \vec r')\,U(\vec r')\,\psi(\vec r'), \qquad G_+(\vec r - \vec r') = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\frac{e^{ik|\vec r - \vec r'|}}{|\vec r - \vec r'|}" cite="reading4" at="eqs. (7.3.16)–(7.3.17), p. 146" />

This is still exact. The Green's function depends only on the length of its argument, so here it depends only on the distance $|\vec r - \vec r'|$.

## Far from the potential

To recover the scattering amplitude, look far away (L21.3 transcript p. 6; notes p. 146). The integration point $\vec r'$ stays inside the potential, while $\vec r$ is far out, in the direction of the unit vector $\hat n = \vec r/r$. Two approximations, of different accuracy:

<Formula label="Two approximations to the distance" tex="|\vec r - \vec r'| \simeq r \quad \text{(denominator)}, \qquad |\vec r - \vec r'| \simeq r - \hat n\cdot\vec r' \quad \text{(phase)}" cite="reading4" at="eqs. (7.3.18)–(7.3.19), p. 146" />

In the denominator, $r$ is good enough: a hundred times the range away, the error is about 1%, which does no harm. In the phase it is not, because the distance is multiplied by $k$: an error of a fraction of a wavelength changes the phase completely. So there one keeps the next term, the projection of $\vec r'$ on the direction of $\vec r$ (L21.3 transcript p. 6). [Neither the lecture nor the notes state the condition for dropping the term after that: it is about $(r'^2 - (\hat n\cdot\vec r')^2)/2r$, so its effect on the phase is small only if $r \gg k a^2$, not merely $r \gg a$. Far enough away this always holds.] Then (L21.3 transcript p. 7)

<Formula label="The Green's function far away" tex="G_+(\vec r - \vec r') \simeq -\frac{1}{4\pi r}\,e^{ikr}\,e^{-ik\hat n\cdot\vec r'}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.20), p. 147" />

and the integral equation becomes (L21.3 transcript p. 7)

<Formula label="The scattering solution far away" tex="\psi(\vec r) \simeq e^{ikz} + \left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d^3r'\;e^{-ik\hat n\cdot\vec r'}\,U(\vec r')\,\psi(\vec r')\right)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.21), p. 147" />

The bracket depends only on the direction $\hat n$, that is on $\theta$ and $\phi$, and it multiplies the outgoing spherical wave: by definition it is the scattering amplitude (L21.3 transcript p. 7),

<Formula label="The scattering amplitude as an integral" tex="f(\theta, \phi) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d^3r'\;e^{-ik\hat n\cdot\vec r'}\,U(\vec r')\,\psi(\vec r')" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.22), p. 147" />

The integral equation, through the choice of $G_+$, has built in both the Schrödinger equation and the right behaviour far away. But this does not yet determine $f$, since the unknown $\psi$ still sits under the integral. The notes also write the incident and scattered wave vectors, $\vec k_i = k\hat z$ and $\vec k_s = k\hat n$, so that the plane wave is $e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r}$ and the phase under the integral is $e^{-i\vec k_s\cdot\vec r'}$ (notes eq. (7.3.23), p. 147). The lecture stops here; the next one approximates $\psi$ under the integral, which is the Born approximation.

## Summary

- To compute a phase shift, solve the radial equation inside the potential ($u_l \propto r^{l+1}$ at the origin) and match $R_l$ and $R_{l}'$ at $r = a$ to $A_l\,j_l(kr) + B_l\,n_l(kr)$. Dividing the two conditions gives $\tan\delta_l = \frac{j_l'(ka) - \gamma_l\,j_l(ka)}{n_l'(ka) - \gamma_l\,n_l(ka)}$, with $\gamma_l = R_{l}'(a)/(kR_l(a))$.
- Phase shifts are useful when a few of them dominate, which happens for $ka \ll 1$: short range or low energy. A partial wave has impact parameter $b \approx l/k$, and the free wave $j_l(kr)$ is exponentially small inside $r \approx l/k$, so waves with $l > ka$ scatter little.
- **Partial-wave unitarity**: $\sigma_l = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l \leq \frac{4\pi}{k^2}(2l+1)$. A classical ring of impact parameters, $2\pi b\,\Delta b = 2\pi l/k^2$, gives a quarter of that bound.
- With $V = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}U$, the Schrödinger equation is $(\nabla^2 + k^2)\psi = U\psi$. With a Green's function, $(\nabla^2 + k^2)G = \delta^3$, it becomes the **integral equation** $\psi = \psi_0 + \int G\,U\,\psi$.
- $G_\pm(\vec r) = -e^{\pm ikr}/(4\pi r)$. Scattering needs $\psi_0 = e^{ikz}$ and the outgoing $G_+$; far away, $|\vec r - \vec r'| \simeq r$ in the denominator and $r - \hat n\cdot\vec r'$ in the phase, which gives $f(\theta, \phi) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d^3r'\,e^{-ik\hat n\cdot\vec r'}\,U(\vec r')\,\psi(\vec r')$, still with the unknown $\psi$ inside.
