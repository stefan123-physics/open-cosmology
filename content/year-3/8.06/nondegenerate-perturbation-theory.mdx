8.06 begins with the most widely used tool in applied quantum mechanics: **perturbation theory**, a way to find the energies and states of a Hamiltonian that differs a little from one we have already solved (L1.1 transcript pp. 1–6; notes chapter 1, introduction and section 1.1). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos with a transcript each, and his lecture notes, chapter 1; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Why perturbation theory

8.04 and 8.05 solved a few Hamiltonians exactly: the harmonic oscillator, the hydrogen atom, spin in a magnetic field. Real systems are rarely that simple, but many are **almost** that simple (L1.1 transcript pp. 1–2):

- near the minimum of any potential, the potential is quadratic plus small cubic and quartic terms, so a diatomic molecule vibrates as a slightly **anharmonic** oscillator;
- an experimentalist studies hydrogen by putting it in a weak magnetic or electric field, which adds small terms to the hydrogen Hamiltonian;
- two neutral atoms far apart induce dipole moments in each other, a tiny effect that produces the **van der Waals** force.

In each case the Hamiltonian is a known one, $H^{(0)}$, plus an extra piece $\delta H$. All three must be Hermitian. Zwiebach then inserts a unit-free parameter $\lambda$ between $0$ and $1$:

<Formula label="The perturbed Hamiltonian" tex="H(\lambda) = H^{(0)} + \lambda\,\delta H, \qquad 0 \le \lambda \le 1" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.0.2)" />

At $\lambda = 0$ this is the system we know; at $\lambda = 1$ it is the one we want. The parameter does two jobs (L1.1 transcript pp. 2–3). Physically, it is like the strength of an applied field that can be turned up from zero. Mathematically, it organizes the calculation by powers of $\lambda$, and it settles what "small" means: whatever $\delta H$ is, $\lambda\,\delta H$ is small for small enough $\lambda$. (What it means for one operator to be small compared with another turns out to be subtler than comparing matrix elements; the next lecture returns to this.)

## What can happen to the spectrum

Picture the energies of $H(\lambda)$ plotted against $\lambda$ (L1.1 transcript pp. 3–5; notes Figure 1.1). At $\lambda = 0$ we see the spectrum of $H^{(0)}$: perhaps a single ground state, then two states at the same energy, then four. As $\lambda$ grows, every level moves, and the eigenstates change too, since an eigenstate of $H^{(0)}$ is usually not an eigenstate of $H(\lambda)$. A single level simply rises or falls. A degenerate pair usually **splits**, its two energies leaving $\lambda = 0$ with different slopes. A group of four may split into three plus one at first order, with the three separating only at higher order, just as $x^2$, $x^4$ and $x^8$ look alike near zero and part later.

Following one isolated level needs **non-degenerate perturbation theory**, the subject of this page. Following a degenerate group needs **degenerate perturbation theory** (the next two lectures), and the case in which the splitting appears only at higher order needs a more careful version of it.

## The perturbative equations

Suppose $H^{(0)}$ is solved: it has an orthonormal basis of eigenstates $|k^{(0)}\rangle$ with energies $E_k^{(0)}$, ordered so that $E_0^{(0)} \le E_1^{(0)} \le E_2^{(0)} \le \dots$ (the equal signs allow for degeneracy) (L1.1 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="The unperturbed problem" tex="H^{(0)}|k^{(0)}\rangle = E_k^{(0)}|k^{(0)}\rangle, \qquad \langle k^{(0)}|l^{(0)}\rangle = \delta_{kl}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.1)" />

Pick a **non-degenerate** state $|n^{(0)}\rangle$: its energy is strictly above the one below and strictly below the one above (L1.1 transcript pp. 5–6). As $\lambda$ is turned on it becomes an eigenstate $|n(\lambda)\rangle$ of $H(\lambda)$ with energy $E_n(\lambda)$, equal to $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ and $E_n^{(0)}$ at $\lambda = 0$. The key assumption is that both can be written as **power series in $\lambda$** (L1.1 transcript p. 6):

<Formula label="The perturbative ansatz" tex="|n(\lambda)\rangle = |n^{(0)}\rangle + \lambda|n^{(1)}\rangle + \lambda^2|n^{(2)}\rangle + \dots, \qquad E_n(\lambda) = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda E_n^{(1)} + \lambda^2E_n^{(2)} + \dots" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.7)" />

The superscript is the order: $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ and $E_n^{(1)}$ are the first-order corrections, and so on. None of them depends on $\lambda$; all the $\lambda$ dependence is in the explicit powers. These are the unknowns.

Put the series into the Schrödinger equation $(H^{(0)} + \lambda\,\delta H - E_n(\lambda))|n(\lambda)\rangle = 0$. The left side is a series in $\lambda$ that must vanish for every $\lambda$, so the coefficient of each power must vanish separately (L1.2 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Order by order" tex="\begin{aligned} \lambda^0:&\quad (H^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)})|n^{(0)}\rangle = 0 \\ \lambda^1:&\quad (H^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)})|n^{(1)}\rangle = (E_n^{(1)} - \delta H)|n^{(0)}\rangle \\ \lambda^k:&\quad (H^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)})|n^{(k)}\rangle = (E_n^{(1)} - \delta H)|n^{(k-1)}\rangle + E_n^{(2)}|n^{(k-2)}\rangle + \dots + E_n^{(k)}|n^{(0)}\rangle \end{aligned}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.13)" />

The zeroth-order equation says nothing new: $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ is an eigenstate of $H^{(0)}$. Each later equation has two unknowns, one state correction and one energy correction, while everything else in it is already known from lower orders. So the tower can be solved **recursively**, one rung at a time (L1.2 transcript p. 3). A quick check on each equation: the superscripts in every term add up to the power of $\lambda$, counting $\delta H$ as one.

**A useful choice.** We may take every correction orthogonal to the unperturbed state (L1.2 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Corrections orthogonal to the original state" tex="\langle n^{(0)}|n^{(1)}\rangle = \langle n^{(0)}|n^{(2)}\rangle = \dots = 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.14)" />

Zwiebach first gives the intuitive reason (any piece along $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ can be absorbed by rescaling the whole state) and then a sharper one. In the order-$k$ equation the unknown $|n^{(k)}\rangle$ appears only on the left, multiplied by $H^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}$, which annihilates $|n^{(0)}\rangle$. So if $|n^{(k)}\rangle$ is a solution, so is $|n^{(k)}\rangle + c|n^{(0)}\rangle$ for any number $c$, and $c$ can be chosen to cancel the component along $|n^{(0)}\rangle$. The price is that $|n(\lambda)\rangle$ is no longer normalized; it is normalizable, which is all we need.

## The energy corrections

Act with the bra $\langle n^{(0)}|$ on the order-$\lambda$ equation (L1.2 transcript p. 4, L1.3 transcript p. 1). On the left, $\langle n^{(0)}|(H^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}) = 0$, because the bra is also an eigenvector. On the right, $E_n^{(1)}$ is a number and $\langle n^{(0)}|n^{(0)}\rangle = 1$. So:

<Formula label="First-order energy" tex="E_n^{(1)} = \langle n^{(0)}|\,\delta H\,|n^{(0)}\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.21)" />

**The first correction to the energy is the expectation value of the perturbation in the unperturbed state.** We do not need to know how the state changes to know how the energy changes, at this order.

The same move on the order-$k$ equation kills the left side again, and on the right the orthogonality choice kills every term $E_n^{(j)}\langle n^{(0)}|n^{(k-j)}\rangle$ except the last (L1.3 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Energy at order k" tex="E_n^{(k)} = \langle n^{(0)}|\,\delta H\,|n^{(k-1)}\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.26)" />

The energy at any order follows from the state at one order lower. For $k = 1$ this is the formula above.

## The first correction to the state

A vector equation holds when all its components hold. The $\langle n^{(0)}|$ component gave the energy; the rest of the order-$\lambda$ equation is in its components along the other basis states, $\langle k^{(0)}|$ with $k \neq n$ (L1.4 transcript p. 1). On the left, $H^{(0)}$ acts on the bra and gives $E_k^{(0)}$; on the right, $\langle k^{(0)}|n^{(0)}\rangle = 0$ removes the $E_n^{(1)}$ term. Writing the matrix elements of the perturbation in the unperturbed basis as

<Formula label="Matrix elements of the perturbation" tex="\delta H_{kn} \equiv \langle k^{(0)}|\,\delta H\,|n^{(0)}\rangle, \qquad \delta H_{nk} = (\delta H_{kn})^*" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (1.1.30), (1.1.31)" />

(the second relation because $\delta H$ is Hermitian), the components of $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ are

<Formula label="Components of the first-order state" tex="\langle k^{(0)}|n^{(1)}\rangle = -\frac{\delta H_{kn}}{E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}}, \qquad k \neq n" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.32)" />

Here appear the **energy denominators**, which, as Zwiebach puts it, make life both interesting and difficult (L1.4 transcript p. 2). If another state had the same energy as $|n^{(0)}\rangle$, one denominator would vanish and the equation could not be solved: that is exactly why degenerate levels need a different treatment. Degeneracies among the other states do no harm; only degeneracy **with the state we follow** matters.

Inserting the identity $\sum_k|k^{(0)}\rangle\langle k^{(0)}|$ and dropping $k = n$ (by the orthogonality choice):

<Formula label="First-order state" tex="|n^{(1)}\rangle = -\sum_{k\neq n}\frac{\delta H_{kn}}{E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}}\,|k^{(0)}\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.34)" />

The perturbation mixes a little of every other state into $|n^{(0)}\rangle$. The amount of $|k^{(0)}\rangle$ is proportional to the matrix element $\delta H_{kn}$ that connects the two states (zero matrix element, no admixture) and inversely proportional to their energy difference.

## The second-order energy

With $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ known, the order-$k$ energy formula with $k = 2$ gives (L1.4 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Second-order energy" tex="E_n^{(2)} = \langle n^{(0)}|\,\delta H\,|n^{(1)}\rangle = -\sum_{k\neq n}\frac{|\delta H_{kn}|^2}{E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (1.1.35), (1.1.36)" />

(In the board derivation Zwiebach first copies the matrix element with its indices swapped and corrects it on the spot; since $\delta H_{nk} = (\delta H_{kn})^*$, the product is $|\delta H_{kn}|^2$.) Written this way the second-order energy is manifestly real, as an energy must be. Collecting the results:

<Formula label="Results to this order" tex="E_n(\lambda) = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda\,\delta H_{nn} - \lambda^2\sum_{k\neq n}\frac{|\delta H_{kn}|^2}{E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}} + O(\lambda^3)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1.37)" />

For the **ground state** every denominator $E_k^{(0)} - E_0^{(0)}$ is positive, so the second-order correction is never positive: at second order the ground state is never pushed up. The next lecture explains why, and what the formula says about the levels in general.

<WorkedExample title="A charged oscillator in a weak electric field">
<Problem>
A particle of mass $m$ and charge $q$ sits in a harmonic potential of angular frequency $\omega$ and is placed in a weak uniform electric field $\mathcal{E}$ along $x$, so $\delta H = -q\mathcal{E}\hat x$ (take $\lambda = 1$). (a) Find the first- and second-order corrections to the ground-state energy, using $\hat x = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}\,(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger)$. (b) Compare with the exact answer, found by completing the square. (c) For an electron with $\hbar\omega = 1$ eV in a field of $10^7$ V/m, how large is the shift?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The corrections">
First order: $E_0^{(1)} = -q\mathcal{E}\langle0|\hat x|0\rangle = 0$, since the ground state is symmetric. Second order: $\hat x|0\rangle = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}\,|1\rangle$, so only $k = 1$ contributes, with $|\delta H_{10}|^2 = q^2\mathcal{E}^2\hbar/2m\omega$ and denominator $E_1^{(0)} - E_0^{(0)} = \hbar\omega$. Dividing the one by the other, $E_0^{(2)} = -\frac{q^2\mathcal{E}^2}{2m\omega^2}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The exact answer">
$\frac{1}{2}m\omega^2x^2 - q\mathcal{E}x = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2\left(x - \frac{q\mathcal{E}}{m\omega^2}\right)^2 - \frac{q^2\mathcal{E}^2}{2m\omega^2}$: the same oscillator, shifted in position and lowered by exactly $q^2\mathcal{E}^2/2m\omega^2$. Second order is already exact here; all higher energy corrections vanish (the state corrections do not).
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Numbers">
$\omega = 1\text{ eV}/\hbar \approx 1.52 \times 10^{15}$ s⁻¹. With $q = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C, a field of $10^7$ V/m and $m = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg, the shift $q^2\mathcal{E}^2/2m\omega^2$ is about $6.1 \times 10^{-25}$ J, or $3.8 \times 10^{-6}$ eV: a few millionths of the level spacing, so the field is indeed a small perturbation.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Write $H(\lambda) = H^{(0)} + \lambda\,\delta H$, expand the states and energies in powers of $\lambda$, and require each power to vanish: a tower of equations solved one order at a time.
- The corrections $|n^{(k)}\rangle$ may be chosen orthogonal to $|n^{(0)}\rangle$; the state is then not normalized, which is harmless.
- $E_n^{(1)} = \langle n^{(0)}|\delta H|n^{(0)}\rangle$, and in general $E_n^{(k)} = \langle n^{(0)}|\delta H|n^{(k-1)}\rangle$.
- $|n^{(1)}\rangle = -\sum_{k\neq n}\frac{\delta H_{kn}}{E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}}|k^{(0)}\rangle$ and $E_n^{(2)} = -\sum_{k\neq n}\frac{|\delta H_{kn}|^2}{E_k^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}}$.
- Energy denominators require the level to be non-degenerate; degenerate levels need their own method.
