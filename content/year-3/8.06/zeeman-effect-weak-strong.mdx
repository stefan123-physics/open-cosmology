An atom in a magnetic field shows split spectral lines: Pieter Zeeman discovered this in 1896, received the Nobel Prize for it in 1902, and the explanation had to wait for quantum mechanics (L6.1 transcript p. 1). It is still a working tool. Inside a sunspot, where magnetic field lines break out of the Sun, the lines of solar atoms split, and the splitting measures fields of a few thousand gauss. This lecture's first half, the last piece of the hydrogen chapter, is a demanding exercise in choosing the right basis (L6.1–L6.4 transcripts; notes section 2.5). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in five short videos, and his lecture notes; this page covers the first four, on the Zeeman effect, in its own words, with its own worked example. The fifth begins the semiclassical approximation, on the next page. Gaussian units.)

## The Zeeman Hamiltonian

The electron has two magnetic moments, one from its orbital motion and one from its spin, which carries the factor $g = 2$ (L6.1 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Orbital and spin magnetic moments" tex="\vec\mu_l = -\frac{e}{2mc}\,\vec L, \qquad \vec\mu_s = -\frac{e}{mc}\,\vec S" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.5.1)" />

In a constant, uniform external field along $z$, their energy is

<Formula label="Zeeman Hamiltonian" tex="\delta H_Z = -\left(\vec\mu_l + \vec\mu_s\right)\cdot\vec B = \frac{e}{2mc}\left(\vec L + 2\vec S\right)\cdot\vec B = \frac{eB}{2mc}\left(L_z + 2S_z\right)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (2.5.2), (2.5.3)" />

Note $\vec L + 2\vec S$, not $\vec J = \vec L + \vec S$. If this were the only addition to $H^{(0)}$, the problem would be simple. But the fine structure is still there, so the full Hamiltonian is (L6.1 transcript p. 2)

<Formula label="Hydrogen in a magnetic field" tex="H = H^{(0)} + \delta H_{\text{fs}} + \delta H_Z" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.5.4)" />

Which small term should be treated first? The atom has its own internal magnetic field: the field the electron feels as it moves through the proton's electric field, the one responsible for spin–orbit coupling (L6.1 transcript p. 3). Compare the external field with it:

- **Weak field**, $B \ll B_{\text{int}}$: the Zeeman shifts are smaller than the fine structure. $H^{(0)} + \delta H_{\text{fs}}$, solved in Lecture 5, is the known Hamiltonian and $\delta H_Z$ the perturbation (notes eq. (2.5.5)).
- **Strong field**, $B \gg B_{\text{int}}$: now $H^{(0)} + \delta H_Z$ is the known part, and the fine structure is the perturbation (notes eq. (2.5.6)). (In the recording, Zwiebach describes this case once as the external field being "much smaller"; he means much larger.)

In between, neither is small, and one has to diagonalize the sum of both terms; the lecture leaves that case aside (L6.1 transcript pp. 2–3).

## Weak field: the matrix is diagonal

The known states are the coupled states $|n\,l\,j\,m_j\rangle$, with energies that depend on $n$ and $j$ (L6.2 transcript p. 1). Each level is degenerate: within a $j$ multiplet (different $m_j$), and across multiplets with the same $j$ but different $l$, like $2S_{1/2}$ and $2P_{1/2}$. Must we diagonalize $\delta H_Z$ in each degenerate space? No. $L_z + 2S_z$ commutes with $\vec L^2$, which rules out matrix elements between different $l$, and with $J_z = L_z + S_z$, which rules out different $m_j$ (L6.2 transcript pp. 1–2). The matrix is diagonal, and non-degenerate perturbation theory applies:

<Formula label="Weak-field Zeeman shift" tex="E^{(1)} = \frac{e\hbar}{2mc}\,B\;\frac{1}{\hbar}\,\langle n\,l\,j\,m_j|\left(L_z + 2S_z\right)|n\,l\,j\,m_j\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.5.8)" />

The remarkable outcome, still to be shown, is that this matrix element is **proportional to $m_j$**: each multiplet splits into equally spaced levels (L6.2 transcript p. 2). Writing $L_z + 2S_z = J_z + S_z$, the $J_z$ part gives $\hbar m_j$ at once:

<Formula label="Separating the J_z part" tex="\langle n\,l\,j\,m_j|\left(L_z + 2S_z\right)|n\,l\,j\,m_j\rangle = \hbar m_j + \langle n\,l\,j\,m_j|\,S_z\,|n\,l\,j\,m_j\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.5.9)" />

The mystery is why the $S_z$ part should also be proportional to $m_j$. This is a first glimpse of the Wigner–Eckart theorem of graduate quantum mechanics (L6.2 transcript pp. 2–3).

## The projection lemma

$\vec S$ is a **vector operator** under the total angular momentum $\vec J$ (L6.3 transcript p. 1): it commutes with $\vec J$ the way a vector should,

<Formula label="Vector operator" tex="\left[J_i,\ V_j\right] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}V_k" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.5.10)" />

with $\vec V = \vec S$ here. This is almost no calculation: $J_i = L_i + S_i$, $L_i$ does not act on spin, and the components of $\vec S$ obey exactly this algebra among themselves. (Zwiebach recalls that 8.05 may have shown $\vec x$ and $\vec p$ to be vector operators under $\vec L$.) Vector operators obey an identity that is best shown by computation, as the homework does (L6.3 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="A double commutator" tex="\frac{1}{(2i\hbar)^2}\left[\vec J^2,\left[\vec J^2,\vec V\right]\right] = \left(\vec V\cdot\vec J\right)\vec J - \frac12\left(\vec J^2\,\vec V + \vec V\,\vec J^2\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.5.12)" />

Take its expectation value in any state $|j\,m_j\rangle$ that is an eigenstate of $\vec J^2$ (whatever other labels it has). On the left, each term has a $\vec J^2$ next to the bra or the ket, and the two terms cancel: the left side is zero. On the right, $\vec J^2$ gives $\hbar^2j(j+1)$ on either side, so (L6.3 transcript pp. 2–3)

<Formula label="Projection lemma" tex="\langle j\,m_j|\,\vec V\,|j\,m_j\rangle = \frac{\langle j\,m_j|\left(\vec V\cdot\vec J\right)\vec J\,|j\,m_j\rangle}{\hbar^2j(j+1)}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (2.5.15), (2.5.16)" />

It reads like the projection of a vector $\vec v$ along $\vec u$, $(\vec v\cdot\vec u)\,\vec u/u^2$: in these states, a vector operator behaves like its projection on $\vec J$.

**Back to $S_z$** (L6.3 transcript pp. 3–4). With $\vec V = \vec S$ and the $z$ component, $J_z$ on the right gives $\hbar m_j$, and the scalar $\vec S\cdot\vec J$ follows from $\vec L = \vec J - \vec S$, $\vec S\cdot\vec J = \frac12(\vec J^2 + \vec S^2 - \vec L^2)$:

<Formula label="The S_z matrix element" tex="\langle n\,l\,j\,m_j|\,S_z\,|n\,l\,j\,m_j\rangle = \hbar m_j\;\frac{j(j+1) - l(l+1) + \frac34}{2j(j+1)}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (2.5.17)–(2.5.19)" />

No other dependence on $m_j$ appears. Adding $\hbar m_j$, the whole matrix element is $\hbar m_j$ times the **Landé g-factor**:

<Formula label="Landé g-factor" tex="g_J(l) = 1 + \frac{j(j+1) - l(l+1) + \frac34}{2j(j+1)}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (2.5.20), (2.5.21)" />

It acts as if the electron's magnetic moment were rescaled, and it is the same number for all states of a multiplet. The weak-field Zeeman shifts are

<Formula label="Weak-field Zeeman effect" tex="E^{(1)} = \mu_B\,B\;g_J(l)\;m_j, \qquad \mu_B = \frac{e\hbar}{2mc}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.5.22)" />

where $\mu_B$ is the Bohr magneton, about $5.79 \times 10^{-9}$ eV per gauss. (In the recording the value is said as "579 times $10^{-9}$" eV per gauss; it is 5.79 times $10^{-9}$, as the notes give.) Every $j$ multiplet splits into $2j + 1$ equally spaced levels, and multiplets with the same $j$ but different $l$ split differently, because $g_J$ depends on $l$: **the weak field removes every degeneracy** (notes, after eq. (2.5.22)).

## Strong field: the uncoupled states are exact

Now the known Hamiltonian is $H^{(0)}$ plus the Zeeman term (L6.4 transcript p. 1). It looks unsolved, but it is easy. $H^{(0)}$ is rotationally invariant and ignores spin, so

<Formula label="The Zeeman term commutes with hydrogen's Hamiltonian" tex="\left[L_z + 2S_z,\ H^{(0)}\right] = 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.5.24)" />

and better still, the uncoupled states $|n\,l\,m_l\,m_s\rangle$ are eigenstates of $H^{(0)}$, of $L_z$ and of $S_z$. They are therefore **exact** eigenstates, with energies (L6.4 transcript pp. 1–2)

<Formula label="Strong-field energies" tex="E = E_n^{(0)} + \frac{e\hbar}{2mc}\,B\left(m_l + 2m_s\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.5.26)" />

Some degeneracy survives. At $n = 2$ the eight states give $m_l + 2m_s = 2, 1, 1, 0, 0, -1, -1, -2$ (L6.4 transcript pp. 2–3). The pair at 0 is $m_l = \pm1$ with opposite spins, two states of the same $l = 1$ multiplet. The pairs at $\pm1$ each join an S state ($m_l = 0$, $m_s = \pm\frac12$) with a P state ($m_l = 0$, the same $m_s$): degeneracy across $l$ multiplets. Fine structure is now the perturbation, and these are the degenerate subspaces in which one must check whether it is diagonal. Since $\vec L^2$ commutes with the fine-structure terms, states with different $l$ do not mix; the homework settles the rest, and in fact the spin–orbit term turns out to be diagonal in these smaller subspaces (notes, after eq. (2.5.29)).

<WorkedExample title="Hydrogen's n = 2 level in a field of 500 gauss">
<Problem>
Take $\mu_B = 5.788 \times 10^{-9}$ eV/gauss and $B = 500$ gauss. The $2P$ fine-structure gap is $\alpha^4mc^2/32 \approx 4.53 \times 10^{-5}$ eV (from Lecture 5). (a) Find the Landé factors of $2S_{1/2}$, $2P_{1/2}$ and $2P_{3/2}$. (b) Find the Zeeman shifts, and check that the field is weak. (c) Roughly at what field would the Zeeman shifts match the 2P gap?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Landé factors">
$2S_{1/2}$ ($l = 0$, $j = \frac12$): $g_J = 1 + \frac{3/4 + 3/4}{3/2} = 2$, the bare spin value. $2P_{1/2}$ ($l = 1$, $j = \frac12$): $g_J = 1 + \frac{3/4 - 2 + 3/4}{3/2} = \frac23$. $2P_{3/2}$ ($l = 1$, $j = \frac32$): $g_J = \frac43$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The shifts">
$\mu_BB \approx 2.89 \times 10^{-6}$ eV. $2S_{1/2}$: $m_j = \pm\frac12$ gives $\pm2.89 \times 10^{-6}$ eV. $2P_{1/2}$: $\pm\frac13\mu_BB \approx \pm0.96 \times 10^{-6}$ eV. So the pair $2S_{1/2}$, $2P_{1/2}$, degenerate before, now splits differently. $2P_{3/2}$: four levels $\frac43\mu_BB\,m_j$, spaced by $3.86 \times 10^{-6}$ eV (about 0.93 GHz), the outermost at $\pm5.79 \times 10^{-6}$ eV. That largest shift is about 13% of the 2P gap: weak, as assumed.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Where weak ends">
$\mu_BB$ equals $4.53 \times 10^{-5}$ eV at $B \approx 7{,}800$ gauss, about 0.8 tesla. This is only a rough scale: the outermost $2P_{3/2}$ levels, which move by $2\mu_BB$, already reach the gap at half that field. Well below it the weak-field picture holds; only fields many times larger, tens of tesla, are clearly strong for hydrogen's $n = 2$ level, and in between one must diagonalize both terms together.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The Zeeman term is $\frac{eB}{2mc}(L_z + 2S_z)$; weak or strong means compared with the atom's internal spin–orbit field.
- Weak field: the coupled basis is good ($L_z + 2S_z$ commutes with $\vec L^2$ and $J_z$), and by the projection lemma for the vector operator $\vec S$, $E^{(1)} = \mu_B B\,g_J(l)\,m_j$: equally spaced levels, every degeneracy lifted.
- Strong field: the uncoupled states are exact eigenstates of $H^{(0)} + \delta H_Z$, with $E = E_n^{(0)} + \mu_B B(m_l + 2m_s)$; fine structure is then the perturbation, within the degeneracies that remain.
