The last lecture rewrote the Schrödinger equation for scattering as an integral equation, exact but with the unknown wavefunction still under the integral. This lecture shows how to turn it into a series that can be cut off, the **Born series**, and works out its first term, the **first Born approximation**: a famous formula that makes the scattering amplitude the Fourier transform of the potential (L22.1–L22.3 transcripts; notes section 7.3.1, pp. 147–150). It ends with the Yukawa potential, the last example of the scattering chapter; the same lecture then begins identical particles, the course's final subject, on the [next page](/courses/8-06/identical-particles-exchange-degeneracy). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 7; this page follows the first three videos in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Where the integral equation stood

Zwiebach starts with a recap of the last lecture (L22.1 transcript pp. 1–3). With $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ and the potential rescaled as $V = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}U$, the scattering solution obeys $\psi = e^{ikz} + \int d^3r'\,G_+(\vec r - \vec r')\,U(\vec r')\,\psi(\vec r')$, with the outgoing Green's function $G_+(\vec r) = -e^{ikr}/(4\pi r)$. [Speaking, he first says the scaling factor is "h bar squared over m" and reads $G_+$ without its minus sign; the notes and the later parts of the lecture have $U = 2mV/\hbar^2$ and the minus, as here. He also calls the wavefunction "phi" at first.]

Far away, the integration point $\vec r'$ stays inside the potential while $\vec r$ is far out, in the direction of the unit vector $\hat n$. Then $|\vec r - \vec r'| \simeq r$ is good enough in the denominator, but the phase needs the next term, $r - \hat n\cdot\vec r'$, because an error comparable to a wavelength changes the phase completely (L22.1 transcript pp. 1–2). Zwiebach also names two vectors (L22.1 transcript p. 2): the **incident wave vector** $\vec k_i = k\hat z$, so that $e^{ikz} = e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r}$, and the **scattered wave vector** $\vec k_s = k\hat n$, pointing towards the observer, with the same length. Writing the incident direction as a vector also frees it from the $z$ axis. Far away, then (L22.1 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The scattering solution far away" tex="\psi(\vec r) \simeq e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r} + \left(-\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d^3r'\;e^{-i\vec k_s\cdot\vec r'}\,U(\vec r')\,\psi(\vec r')\right)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.23), p. 147" />

The bracket has the form of $f(\theta, \phi)$, but the unknown $\psi$ is still inside it. Have we made progress? The Born approximation shows that we have (L22.1 transcript p. 3).

## Iterating the integral equation

Take the exact integral equation (L22.1 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The integral equation" tex="\psi(\vec r) = e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r} + \int d^3r'\;G_+(\vec r - \vec r')\,U(\vec r')\,\psi(\vec r')" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.24), p. 147" />

and rewrite it with every $\vec r$ renamed $\vec r'$ and every $\vec r'$ renamed $\vec r''$. Nothing has changed except the names (L22.1 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="The same equation, relabelled" tex="\psi(\vec r') = e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r'} + \int d^3r''\;G_+(\vec r' - \vec r'')\,U(\vec r'')\,\psi(\vec r'')" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.25), p. 147" />

The point of the relabelling is that this is an expression for the $\psi(\vec r')$ under the integral of the first equation. Substituting it there gives (L22.1 transcript p. 4)

<Formula label="After one substitution" tex="\psi(\vec r) = e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r} + \int d^3r'\;G_+(\vec r - \vec r')\,U(\vec r')\,e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r'} + \int d^3r'\;G_+(\vec r - \vec r')\,U(\vec r')\int d^3r''\;G_+(\vec r' - \vec r'')\,U(\vec r'')\,\psi(\vec r'')" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.26), p. 147" />

Look at what has happened: the first two terms are known, since the Green's function, the potential and the plane wave are all given. If the last term were dropped, this would no longer be an integral equation but a solution. The unknown has only been pushed to a later term. Repeating the trick replaces $\psi(\vec r'')$ in turn, and so on (L22.1 transcript p. 4; the notes write out the next step in eq. (7.3.27), p. 147).

## The Born series

Doing this forever gives an infinite series. Without the labels, which can always be put back, it reads (L22.1 transcript p. 5)

<Formula label="The Born series" tex="\psi = e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r} + \int G_+U\,e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r} + \int G_+U\int G_+U\,e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r} + \int G_+U\int G_+U\int G_+U\,e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r} + \cdots" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.28), p. 148" />

To put the labels back: each new integral is over a new variable, the Green's function takes the wave from that point to the previous one, and the last one ends at $\vec r$, where we look. Stopping the series and dropping the term that still contains the unknown $\psi$ is the **Born approximation** (L22.1 transcript p. 4). Keeping only the first integral, with $\psi$ replaced by the incident wave, is the **first Born approximation**; the **second Born approximation** keeps the next term as well, again with the incident wave at the end, and so on.

## The first Born approximation

When should the first Born approximation be good? The series splits the wavefunction into a free part and parts caused by the interaction, and the approximation keeps only the first correction (L22.2 transcript p. 1). That works when the free Hamiltonian dominates the perturbation: when particles of high energy meet a potential bump of finite height, the incident plane wave is a good stand-in for $\psi$ under the integral. It is a **high-energy approximation**, better the higher the energy (L22.2 transcript p. 1; notes p. 149).

The far-field work above has already simplified the Green's function, so replacing $\psi(\vec r')$ in the bracket by $e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r'}$ gives (L22.2 transcript p. 1)

<Formula label="The first Born approximation" tex="\psi(\vec r) \simeq e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r} - \frac{1}{4\pi}\left(\int d^3r'\;e^{-i\vec k_s\cdot\vec r'}\,U(\vec r')\,e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r'}\right)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.29), p. 148" />

Now the bracket is known, and it is the scattering amplitude. Both exponentials depend on $\vec r'$, so they combine. Dropping the prime on the integration variable, which is a dummy variable (notes p. 148; in the lecture, Zwiebach drops the primes at L22.3 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The scattering amplitude in the first Born approximation" tex="f(\theta, \phi) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d^3r\;e^{-i\vec K\cdot\vec r}\,U(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.30), p. 148" />

(The notes label the amplitude with the wave number $k$ and the word "Born".) The vector $\vec K$ is the **wave-number transfer** (L22.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The wave-number transfer" tex="|\vec k_i| = |\vec k_s| = k, \qquad \vec K = \vec k_s - \vec k_i" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.31), p. 148" />

It is what must be added to the incident wave vector to get the scattered one; times $\hbar$, it is the momentum transferred to the particle. So the result says: **in the first Born approximation, the scattering amplitude is the Fourier transform of the potential, evaluated at the wave-number transfer** (L22.2 transcript p. 2; notes p. 148). There are not many ways to make something interesting, Zwiebach remarks, and scattering experiments are doing Fourier transforms of the potential.

## Where the angles are

A formula with $\theta$ and $\phi$ on the left and no angle on the right can make one uneasy (L22.2 transcript p. 2). The angles are inside $\vec K$. Put $\vec k_i$ along $z$; then $\vec k_s$ points in the direction $(\theta, \phi)$ of observation, and $\vec K$ depends on both angles. Its length is easy: $\vec k_i$, $\vec k_s$ and $\vec K$ form an isosceles triangle with two sides $k$ and the angle $\theta$ between them, and dropping a perpendicular splits $K$ into two pieces $k\sin(\theta/2)$ (L22.2 transcript p. 3; the sketch beside eq. (7.3.31), notes p. 148; see also Fig. 7.5, p. 150):

<Formula label="The size of the wave-number transfer" tex="K = |\vec K| = 2k\sin\frac{\theta}{2}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.32), p. 148" />

(Equivalently, $K^2 = 2k^2(1 - \cos\theta)$.) Forward scattering, $\theta = 0$, has $K = 0$; backward scattering has the largest transfer, $K = 2k$.

Zwiebach stresses what an approximation buys (L22.2 transcript p. 3). The formula has a clear physical meaning, and it gives answers even when the potential is **not spherically symmetric**, where $f$ depends on $\phi$ and partial waves do not apply. Many such results, he tells the class, are worked out in Griffiths's textbook, which the problem set draws on.

## A picture of the Born series

The Born series has an intuitive picture (L22.3 transcript pp. 1–2; notes pp. 149–150, Figs. 7.3 and 7.4). Think of the Green's function as a **propagator**, which carries a wave from one point to another, and of the potential $U$ as a **medium**, a density of scattering material.

- The zeroth-order term, $e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r}$, is the incident wave arriving directly at $\vec r$.
- The first-order term is a part of the incident wave that reaches a point $\vec r'$ inside the potential, shakes the medium there and makes it a secondary source, whose wave $G_+$ carries to $\vec r$. The strength of the source is the incident wave times $U(\vec r')$, and every $\vec r'$ is added up.
- The second-order term reaches $\vec r''$, is re-emitted there, travels to $\vec r'$, is re-emitted again and only then travels to $\vec r$.

So the observer receives the direct wave, the waves scattered once by the material, those scattered twice, and so on: all the ways the wave can interact with the medium and reach you. Zwiebach compares the pictures with **Feynman diagrams** of field theory, in which, for example, an electron and a photon that scatter are drawn in every way they can interact (L22.3 transcript p. 2).

## Central potentials

For a spherically symmetric potential, $V(\vec r) = V(r)$, the famous formula can be simplified further, because the angular integral can be done (L22.3 transcript p. 2). [The notes say "radial integration" here; it is the angular integral that is done.] Writing $U = 2mV/\hbar^2$ (L22.3 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Born amplitude for a central potential" tex="f(\theta) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\,\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\int d^3r\;e^{-i\vec K\cdot\vec r}\,V(r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.33), p. 148" />

What can this integral depend on? It depends on the vector $\vec K$, but only on its length, Zwiebach argues (L22.3 transcript pp. 3–4). The integration variable is a dummy, and we may choose the $z$ axis of the integral along $\vec K$, whatever its direction. Or: rotating $\vec K$ can be undone by rotating the integration variable, which changes neither $V(r)$ nor the volume element. And since $K$ depends only on $\theta$, so does $f$, as it should for a central potential (notes p. 148).

So take $\vec K$ along the axis of the integral and call $\Theta$ the angle between $\vec K$ and $\vec r$ (the notes use a capital to keep it apart from the scattering angle $\theta$). The $\phi$ integral gives $2\pi$, and the $\cos\Theta$ integral is elementary (L22.3 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Doing the angular integral" tex="f(\theta) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty 2\pi\,r^2\,dr\int_{-1}^{1}d(\cos\Theta)\;e^{-iKr\cos\Theta}\,V(r) = -\frac{m}{\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty r^2\,V(r)\,\frac{2\sin(Kr)}{Kr}\,dr" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.34), p. 149" />

One power of $r$ cancels, and $K$ comes out of the integral (L22.3 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The Born amplitude for a central potential" tex="f(\theta) = -\frac{2m}{K\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty r\,V(r)\sin(Kr)\,dr, \qquad K = 2k\sin\frac{\theta}{2}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.35), p. 149" />

Zwiebach warns not to confuse the capital $K$, the size of the transfer, with the small $k$, the wave number of the particle (L22.3 transcript p. 4). The Fourier transform is now partly done, and for any given potential only a one-dimensional integral is left.

## The Yukawa potential

The standard example is the **Yukawa potential** (L22.3 transcript pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="The Yukawa potential" tex="V(r) = \beta\,\frac{e^{-\mu r}}{r}, \qquad \beta,\ \mu > 0" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.36), p. 149" />

[The transcript has a stray "minus" as Zwiebach begins the formula, before he puts in $\beta$; the notes have it as here, with $\beta > 0$, a repulsive potential. An attractive one has $\beta < 0$, and the formulas below hold for either sign.] Here $1/\mu$ is the range of the force. As $\mu \to 0$ it becomes the Coulomb potential, with $\beta$ the product of the charges (divided by 4πε₀ in SI units): in Zwiebach's words, the electromagnetic potential modified as if the photon had acquired a mass (L22.3 transcript p. 5). Yukawa proposed it in thinking about the forces carried by **pions**, strongly interacting particles that are light compared with a proton: "one fifth of it or even less, maybe one seventh, one eighth", Zwiebach says. [The charged pion has $139.57$ MeV/$c^2$ and the proton $938.27$ MeV/$c^2$ (PDG and CODATA 2022), a ratio of 0.149, about one seventh.] The range is then the pion's reduced Compton wavelength ($\hbar$ divided by the pion's mass times $c$), about 1.41 fm (this page's number).

Putting the potential into the boxed formula, the integral is elementary, $\int_0^\infty e^{-\mu r}\sin(Kr)\,dr = K/(\mu^2 + K^2)$, and (L22.3 transcript p. 5)

<Formula label="The Born amplitude of the Yukawa potential" tex="f(\theta) = -\frac{2m\beta}{K\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty e^{-\mu r}\sin(Kr)\,dr = -\frac{2m\beta}{\hbar^2\left(\mu^2 + K^2\right)}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (7.3.37), p. 149" />

with $K^2 = 4k^2\sin^2(\theta/2)$, so it does depend on the angle. Zwiebach suggests comparing it with the Coulomb case, taking limits and plotting it (L22.3 transcript p. 5). Taking $\mu \to 0$ and using $k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2$:

- the differential cross section becomes $|f|^2 = \frac{\beta^2}{16E^2\sin^4(\theta/2)}$, which is exactly the **Rutherford formula** for Coulomb scattering, even though the Born approximation is not exact (a coincidence of the Coulomb potential);
- it grows without limit as $\theta \to 0$, so the Coulomb potential, which has no range, has an infinite total cross section; a finite $\mu$ cuts this off.

(These limits are this page's, not worked out in the lecture or the notes.) With the Yukawa example, Zwiebach closes the subject of scattering, about three and a half lectures in all (L22.3 transcript p. 5).

<WorkedExample title="A Yukawa potential in the first Born approximation">
<Problem>
A neutron (mass $939.6$ MeV/$c^2$) scatters off a heavy target whose potential is taken to be of Yukawa form, with range $1/\mu$ for $\mu = 0.7$ fm⁻¹ and a weak strength, $2m\beta/\hbar^2 = 0.2$ fm⁻¹. The neutron's wave number is $k = 1$ fm⁻¹. (a) Find its energy and $\beta$, and compare the potential at $r = 1/\mu$ with the energy. (b) Find $f$ and $d\sigma/d\Omega$ at $\theta = 0$, $90°$ and $180°$. (c) Find the total cross section, and compare it with that at $k = 0.5$ fm⁻¹ and at very low energy.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Energy and strength">
With $\hbar c = 197.327$ MeV fm, $E = (\hbar c)^2k^2/(2mc^2) = 38\,938/1879.1$ MeV, about 20.7 MeV. Then $\beta = 0.2$ fm⁻¹ $\times\,\hbar^2/2m = 0.2 \times 20.72$ MeV fm², about 4.14 MeV fm. At $r = 1/\mu = 1.43$ fm the potential is $\beta\mu e^{-1}$, about 1.07 MeV, some twenty times smaller than the energy: the high-energy situation in which the Born approximation is meant to work. (The range is close to the pion's 1.41 fm.)
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The amplitude at three angles">
With $f = -0.2/(0.49 + K^2)$ in fm and $K = 2\sin(\theta/2)$ fm⁻¹: forward, $K = 0$ and $f = -0.408$ fm, so $d\sigma/d\Omega = 0.167$ fm²; at $90°$, $K = \sqrt2 = 1.414$ fm⁻¹, $f = -0.0803$ fm and $d\sigma/d\Omega = 0.00645$ fm²; backward, $K = 2$ fm⁻¹, $f = -0.0445$ fm and $d\sigma/d\Omega = 0.00198$ fm². The forward cross section is 84 times the backward one: the scattering is strongly peaked forward, because $1/\mu$ is longer than the wavelength over $2\pi$, $1/k$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The total cross section">
With $d\Omega = 2\pi\,d(\cos\theta)$ and $K^2 = 2k^2(1 - \cos\theta)$, the solid angle is $d\Omega = \pi\,d(K^2)/k^2$, and the integral of $|f|^2$ over $K^2$ from 0 to $4k^2$ is elementary: $\sigma = \frac{4\pi\,(2m\beta/\hbar^2)^2}{\mu^2(\mu^2 + 4k^2)}$. Here $\sigma = 4\pi \times 0.04/(0.49 \times 4.49)$ fm², about 0.228 fm². At $k = 0.5$ fm⁻¹ (5.18 MeV) it is $4\pi \times 0.04/(0.49 \times 1.49)$, about 0.688 fm², three times more; at very low energy it tends to $4\pi \times 0.04/0.49^2$, about 2.09 fm². In the Born approximation the cross section falls as the energy rises, roughly as $1/k^2$ once $2k \gg \mu$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Dark matter that scatters off itself">
Ferreras's textbook describes **self-interacting dark matter**, in which dark matter particles scatter off each other with a small cross section, as one proposal for the small-scale problems of cold dark matter. In the particle-physics literature (for example Tulin and Yu's 2018 review, not part of the MIT material), a common model lets the particles interact through a light force carrier, which gives exactly a Yukawa potential between them. When the coupling is weak, the cross section is the Born result of this page; Tulin and Yu call this the Born regime. Outside it the cross section must be computed in other ways, for slow particles with partial waves and phase shifts, as in the previous lectures.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Renaming variables and substituting the integral equation into itself gives the **Born series**: $\psi = e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r} + \int G_+U\,e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r} + \int G_+U\int G_+U\,e^{i\vec k_i\cdot\vec r} + \cdots$. Cutting it off after one integral, with the incident wave at the end, is the first Born approximation; after two, the second.
- Pictorially: the direct incident wave, plus waves scattered once, twice, … by the potential, which acts as a medium, with $G_+$ as the propagator; like Feynman diagrams.
- **First Born approximation**: $f(\theta, \phi) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\int d^3r\,e^{-i\vec K\cdot\vec r}\,U(\vec r)$, the Fourier transform of the potential at the wave-number transfer $\vec K = \vec k_s - \vec k_i$, with $K = 2k\sin(\theta/2)$. It is a high-energy approximation and needs no spherical symmetry.
- For a central potential, $f(\theta) = -\frac{2m}{K\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty r\,V(r)\sin(Kr)\,dr$.
- The **Yukawa potential** $V = \beta e^{-\mu r}/r$ gives $f = -\frac{2m\beta}{\hbar^2(\mu^2 + K^2)}$; as $\mu \to 0$ it becomes the Coulomb potential and $|f|^2$ the Rutherford formula.
