With the rates of atomic transitions, time-dependent perturbation theory is complete. The course is organized around approximation methods: time-independent perturbation theory, applied in detail to hydrogen; the WKB approximation, for things that vary slowly in space; and time-dependent perturbation theory, with Fermi's golden rule and atoms in light. The natural next step would be the **adiabatic approximation**, for things that change slowly in time, the time analogue of WKB. Before that, Zwiebach takes a break: for half of this lecture and all of the next, **charged particles in electromagnetic fields**, which leads to Landau levels and to ideas about gauge invariance and why potentials matter. A first taste came with the [Pauli equation](/courses/8-06/hydrogen-fine-structure-pauli-dirac), where the electron's momentum was replaced by $\vec p - q\vec A/c$ (L13.4 transcript p. 1). (MIT's material for this page is the last two of Lecture 13's five videos; the [first page of this lecture](/courses/8-06/thermal-transition-rates-einstein-coefficients) covers the other three. ## Fields or potentials?

The set-up: a particle of mass $m$ and charge $q$ in an electric field $\vec E$ and a magnetic field $\vec B$ (L13.4 transcript p. 1). The usual story from electromagnetism goes like this. $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ are the physical fields, the things that push charges around. The **potentials** are mathematical helpers that make the equations easier to solve, but they are less physical, because they are ambiguous: they can be changed without changing the fields.

Zwiebach calls this view misleading, "put charitably", and in fact wrong: the potentials are **more** important than the fields (L13.4 transcript p. 1). Quantum mechanics couples to the potentials, and only through them, as an afterthought, to the fields; and the modern definition of an electromagnetic field is really a definition of potentials. In open space it makes little difference which one takes as fundamental. But as soon as there is **topology**, a sample living on the surface of a torus, say, or extra dimensions of space, as in many theories, ignoring the potentials can give completely wrong answers (L13.4 transcript pp. 1–2).

## Where the potentials come from

Start with two of Maxwell's equations (L13.4 transcript p. 2). The magnetic field has no divergence, and this is solved by writing it as a curl of a **vector potential** $\vec A$, since the divergence of a curl vanishes:

<Formula label="The vector potential" tex="\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0, \qquad \vec B = \nabla\times\vec A" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Next, Faraday's law. With $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$, and the time derivative commuted past the curl, it says that a certain combination has no curl (L13.4 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Faraday's law with the vector potential" tex="\nabla\times\vec E + \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \nabla\times\left(\vec E + \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t}\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

A field with no curl is a gradient, so the combination in brackets is minus the gradient of a **scalar potential** $\Phi$, and (L13.4 transcript p. 2)

<Formula label="The electric field from the potentials" tex="\vec E = -\nabla\Phi - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

[The transcript first says the combination "is the gradient of phi", then writes the result with $-\nabla\Phi$, which is the right sign.]

## Gauge transformations

The potentials are not unique. A **gauge transformation** changes them without changing the fields (L13.4 transcript p. 2). Since the curl of a gradient vanishes, adding the gradient of any function $\Lambda(\vec r, t)$ to $\vec A$ leaves $\vec B$ alone. It would change $\vec E$, through $\partial\vec A/\partial t$, unless $\Phi$ changes too, and the right change is (L13.4 transcript p. 2)

<Formula label="Gauge transformation of the potentials" tex="\vec A' = \vec A + \nabla\Lambda, \qquad \Phi' = \Phi - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Check: $\nabla\times\vec A' = \nabla\times\vec A$. In $\vec E'$, the gradient of the new $\Phi$ brings an extra $+\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\Lambda$, and the new $\vec A$ brings $-\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\Lambda$; they cancel (L13.4 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="The fields are gauge invariant" tex="\vec E(\Phi', \vec A') = \vec E(\Phi, \vec A), \qquad \vec B(\Phi', \vec A') = \vec B(\Phi, \vec A)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Now this is lifted to a **principle**: potentials related by a gauge transformation are **physically equivalent** (L13.4 transcript p. 3). An electromagnetic field configuration is then, as a mathematician would put it, an **equivalence class** of potentials. If two people describe a field with different $\Phi$ and $\vec A$, they describe the same field exactly when some $\Lambda$ turns one set into the other. This looks like a step backwards, more complicated than simply comparing $\vec E$ and $\vec B$, but Zwiebach insists it is what the physics requires (L13.4 transcript p. 3).

## Two curious effects

The two descriptions, by fields and by classes of potentials, are not the same, and the difference shows in two ways (L13.4 transcript pp. 3–4).

**1. Equal fields, different physics.** If two sets of potentials are gauge equivalent, they give the same $\vec E$ and $\vec B$. The converse can fail. Given two sets with the same fields, one must find a $\Lambda$ relating them by solving a differential equation, and it may have no solution. Then the two are **inequivalent** electromagnetic fields, with possibly different quantum mechanics, even though $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ agree (L13.4 transcript p. 3). This needs a little topology. Take a closed curve that cannot be shrunk to a point, such as a loop around a torus (in an ordinary room every loop can be shrunk), and put along it a constant vector potential tangent to the curve. It gives zero $\vec B$ and zero $\vec E$, yet in general a small constant and a large one are inequivalent: no gauge transformation turns one into the other. (Zwiebach mentions this under the name of **Wilson loops**; strictly, the Wilson loop is the phase built from $\vec A$ along such a curve. Which constants do turn out to be equivalent is a question this lecture leaves open; L13.4 transcript pp. 3–4.)

**2. Fields without potentials.** Suppose $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ satisfy all of Maxwell's equations. Are they allowed? Only if some $\Phi$ and $\vec A$ produce them, since the potentials are what is fundamental and what quantum mechanics needs (L13.4 transcript p. 4). That can fail. A torus can be made from a rectangle of the plane by identifying opposite edges, as the course has made a circle from a line by identifying $x$ with $x + L$. A constant magnetic field on this torus satisfies Maxwell's equations, but for most values of the field no consistent vector potential exists: only particular values are allowed, and the magnetic **flux is quantized** (L13.4 transcript p. 4). The next lecture works this out.

## The Schrödinger equation with potentials

For these reasons the Schrödinger equation of a charged particle is written with the potentials (L13.5 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Schrödinger equation in electromagnetic fields" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \left[\frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\vec A\right)^2 + q\Phi\right]\psi" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The next lecture motivates it. For now, notice the replacement of $\vec p = \frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla$ by $\vec p - \frac{q}{c}\vec A$, where $p^2/2m$ used to be. Is $\vec p$ still $m\vec v$? No (L13.5 transcript p. 1). The term in square brackets is the energy, kinetic plus potential, and the kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = (mv)^2/2m$ must come from the first term. So $\vec p - q\vec A/c$ deserves to be called $m$ times the **velocity operator**, and its Heisenberg equation of motion turns out to look like the Lorentz force law. It is a nicer operator than $\vec p$, for many reasons (L13.5 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Velocity operator" tex="m\,\hat{\vec v} = \vec p - \frac{q}{c}\vec A" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

## Gauge invariance of the Schrödinger equation

Gauge invariance must mean that gauge-equivalent potentials give the same physics. Write the Schrödinger equation twice, once with $\vec A$ and $\Phi$ and once with $\vec A'$ and $\Phi'$ (L13.5 transcript p. 1). Should the same $\psi$ solve both? That would be asking too much, and it does not happen. The wavefunction must be transformed as well, and this sounds dangerous, since changing $\psi$ could change the physics. But the change is only a phase, and simple enough to check that the physics stays the same (L13.5 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Gauge transformation of the wavefunction" tex="\psi' = e^{iq\Lambda/\hbar c}\,\psi" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

The claim is that if $\psi$ solves the Schrödinger equation with $\Phi$ and $\vec A$, then $\psi'$, built with the same $\Lambda$ that transforms the potentials, solves it with $\Phi'$ and $\vec A'$. The phase is not a constant: it depends on position and time, a substantial change (L13.5 transcript p. 2). Two things remain, both for next time: to check the claim, and to check that the physics is unchanged. Some cases are immediate: the expectation value of $x$ is the same, since the phase cancels in $|\psi'|^2 = |\psi|^2$. Others are more subtle, and lead to the notion of **gauge-invariant operators** (L13.5 transcript p. 2).

<WorkedExample title="One magnetic field, two gauges">
<Problem>
A uniform magnetic field $B$ points along $z$. (a) Show that $\vec A = \frac{B}{2}(-y, x, 0)$ and $\vec A' = (-By, 0, 0)$ both give it, and find a gauge function $\Lambda$ with $\vec A' = \vec A + \nabla\Lambda$. (b) An electron ($q = -e$) is described in both gauges. By how much do the phases of $\psi'$ and $\psi$ differ at a point 100 nm from both the $x$ and $y$ axes, for $B = 1$ T ($10^4$ gauss)? (c) Take $\Lambda = -cV_0t$ instead. What does it do to the potentials and to an energy eigenstate? Use $\hbar = 1.0546 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C for the numbers (in SI units the combination $q\Lambda/\hbar c$ reads $q\Lambda/\hbar$, with $\Lambda$ in SI units).
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Two vector potentials">
For $\vec A$, the $z$ component of the curl is $\partial A_y/\partial x - \partial A_x/\partial y = \frac{B}{2} + \frac{B}{2} = B$, and the other components vanish. For $\vec A'$ it is $-\partial(-By)/\partial y = B$. The difference is $\vec A' - \vec A = \left(-\frac{B}{2}y, -\frac{B}{2}x, 0\right)$, the gradient of $\Lambda = -\frac{1}{2}Bxy$. Since $\Lambda$ does not depend on time, $\Phi' = \Phi$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The phase">
With $q = -e$, $\psi' = e^{iq\Lambda/\hbar c}\psi = e^{ieBxy/2\hbar c}\psi$. A convenient length is $\ell = \sqrt{\hbar c/eB}$; in SI units $\ell^2 = \hbar/eB = 1.0546 \times 10^{-34}/(1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 1) \approx 6.58 \times 10^{-16}$ m², so $\ell \approx 25.7$ nm at 1 T. The phase difference is $xy/2\ell^2 = (10^{-7})^2/(2 \times 6.58 \times 10^{-16}) \approx 7.6$ radians. The two wavefunctions look quite different, yet $|\psi'|^2 = |\psi|^2$ everywhere: the electron is equally likely to be found at any point in either description.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A constant shift of the potential">
$\nabla\Lambda = 0$, so $\vec A' = \vec A$, while $\Phi' = \Phi - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial t} = \Phi + V_0$: the scalar potential is shifted by a constant, which changes no field. An energy eigenstate $\psi \propto e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ becomes $\psi' = e^{-iqV_0t/\hbar}\psi \propto e^{-i(E + qV_0)t/\hbar}$, exactly what the Schrödinger equation with $\Phi + V_0$ requires: every energy is shifted by $qV_0$, and energy differences, the measurable ones, do not change. For an electron and a shift of 1 volt (1/299.8 statvolt), $qV_0 = -1$ eV, and the extra phase turns at $1\ \text{eV}/\hbar \approx 1.52 \times 10^{15}$ radians per second.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="From a phase to the forces of nature">
The gauge transformations on this page are where gauge theories begin. In 8.286, Guth presents the same transformations of $\Phi$ and $\vec A$ as the model for the gauge theories of the Standard Model and of grand unified theories (Lecture 21). Lecture 22, taught by a substitute lecturer (Scott Hughes) from Guth's notes, explains that in quantum theory the gauge function becomes a local change of phase of the electron field, which is what it means to call electromagnetism a U(1) gauge theory. The phase $e^{iq\Lambda/\hbar c}$ of this page is that statement for a single particle. Grand unified theories are where Guth's magnetic monopole problem comes from, and that problem is one of his motivations for inflation.
</Callout>

## Summary

- With time-dependent perturbation theory complete, the course turns, for half a lecture and the next one, to charged particles in electromagnetic fields. The notes' chapter on this is not on OpenCourseWare; the page follows the transcripts.
- $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ is solved by $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$, and Faraday's law then gives $\vec E = -\nabla\Phi - \frac{1}{c}\partial\vec A/\partial t$ (Gaussian units).
- A gauge transformation $\vec A' = \vec A + \nabla\Lambda$, $\Phi' = \Phi - \frac{1}{c}\partial\Lambda/\partial t$ leaves $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ unchanged; gauge-equivalent potentials are declared physically equivalent, and a field configuration is a class of equivalent potentials.
- Zwiebach argues the potentials are more fundamental than the fields: with topology, equal fields can come from inequivalent potentials (a constant $\vec A$ along a loop that cannot be shrunk), and fields that obey Maxwell's equations can lack potentials (a constant $\vec B$ on a torus, unless its flux is quantized).
- The Schrödinger equation is $i\hbar\,\partial\psi/\partial t = \left[\frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\vec A\right)^2 + q\Phi\right]\psi$; $\vec p - q\vec A/c$, not $\vec p$, is $m$ times the velocity.
- Gauge invariance: under a gauge transformation the wavefunction must change too, $\psi' = e^{iq\Lambda/\hbar c}\psi$, a phase depending on position and time; the proof and the physical consequences follow next lecture.
