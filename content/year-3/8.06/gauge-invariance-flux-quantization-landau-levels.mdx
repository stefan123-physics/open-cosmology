The previous lesson set up the potentials, their gauge transformations and the Schrödinger equation of a charged particle, and claimed that the wavefunction must change by a phase when the potentials do. This lecture checks the claim. It shows why the Schrödinger equation stays consistent under a gauge transformation, and what that costs: the familiar momentum $\vec p$ stops being an observable. Then come two examples. The previous lesson stated that a constant magnetic field on a torus is allowed only for particular values of its flux; here the calculation is done, and the flux comes in whole units of a **flux quantum**, and a charged particle in a uniform field has evenly spaced, enormously degenerate energy levels, the **Landau levels** (L14.1 transcript p. 1; L14.3 transcript p. 1). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos. The lecture notes for this topic, chapter 5, "Particles in electromagnetic fields", are not available on OCW, so this page relies on the transcripts alone and follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. As in the lecture, all formulas are in **Gaussian units**, where $q\vec A/c$ appears and fields are measured in gauss.)

## The coupling and gauge transformations, recalled

These rules come from the previous lesson, and Zwiebach recalls them from the board at the start of this lecture; the notation here is the same, with $\Phi$ the scalar potential and $\Lambda$ the gauge function. For a particle of mass $m$ and charge $q$, $\vec p$ is replaced by $\vec p - \frac{q}{c}\vec A$ in the kinetic energy and the electric potential energy $q\Phi$ is added (L14.1 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Hamiltonian of a charged particle" tex="H = \frac{1}{2m}\left(\vec p - \frac{q}{c}\,\vec A\right)^2 + q\Phi" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Here $\vec A$ is the vector potential and $\Phi$ the scalar potential. Zwiebach writes $q$ throughout for the charge of the particle, rather than $e$, so that no one has to wonder about the sign: for an electron $q = -e$ (L14.1 transcript pp. 1–2).

Potentials related by a **gauge transformation** with any function $\Lambda(\vec x, t)$, the gauge parameter, describe the same fields and are physically equivalent (L14.1 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Gauge transformation of the potentials" tex="\vec A' = \vec A + \nabla\Lambda, \qquad \Phi' = \Phi - \frac{1}{c}\,\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The Schrödinger equation with the new potentials is not solved by the old wavefunction; the wavefunction must change too, by a phase built from the gauge parameter and the charge (L14.1 transcript p. 1; L14.2 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Gauge transformation of the wavefunction" tex="\psi' = U\psi, \qquad U = e^{iq\Lambda/\hbar c}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

## The Hamiltonian written out

What does the square in $H$ really mean? Expanding it with the order of the operators kept (L14.1 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Expanding the square" tex="H = \frac{1}{2m}\left(p^2 - \frac{q}{c}\,\vec p\cdot\vec A - \frac{q}{c}\,\vec A\cdot\vec p + \frac{q^2}{c^2}\,A^2\right) + q\Phi" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

$p^2$ gives the Laplacian, and $A^2$ is just a function. In $\vec A\cdot\vec p$ the momentum $\vec p = \frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla$ differentiates whatever stands to the right. The term to watch is $\vec p\cdot\vec A$: as an operator, $\vec p$ acts on **everything** to its right, $\vec A$ and the wavefunction behind it. Writing the wavefunction in shows what happens (L14.1 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The ordering of p and A" tex="\vec p\cdot\left(\vec A\,\psi\right) = \frac{\hbar}{i}\,(\nabla\cdot\vec A)\,\psi + \vec A\cdot\vec p\,\psi" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

So it would be wrong to treat the two middle terms as equal, and just as wrong to read $\vec p\cdot\vec A$ as only the divergence of $\vec A$ (L14.1 transcript p. 2). In the **Coulomb gauge**, $\nabla\cdot\vec A = 0$, the two terms do coincide, but in general they do not. Collecting everything (L14.1 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The Hamiltonian in full" tex="H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{i\hbar q}{2mc}\,(\nabla\cdot\vec A) - \frac{q}{mc}\,\vec A\cdot\vec p + \frac{q^2}{2mc^2}\,A^2 + q\Phi" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

In the second term $\nabla\cdot\vec A$ is now just a function, no longer a differential operator. Answering a student, Zwiebach confirms that this is the Hamiltonian of a particle **without spin**; a particle with spin can have an extra term (L14.1 transcript p. 3).

## The gauge covariant derivative

The key to gauge invariance is an identity that Zwiebach calls remarkable (L14.1 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The covariant derivative identity" tex="\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\,\vec A'\right)(U\psi) = U\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\,\vec A\right)\psi" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Moving $U$ from the right of the operator to the left is almost like moving a constant through, except that $\vec A'$ turns into $\vec A$. Because the operator $\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\vec A$ behaves so well under gauge transformations it is called a **gauge covariant derivative** (L14.1 transcript p. 4). The proof takes two lines. The gradient of the exponential $U$ brings down the gradient of its exponent, and $\vec A' = \vec A + \nabla\Lambda$ (L14.1 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Proof of the identity" tex="\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla(U\psi) = \frac{\hbar}{i}\,\frac{iq}{\hbar c}\,(\nabla\Lambda)\,U\psi + U\,\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla\psi, \qquad -\frac{q}{c}\,\vec A'\,U\psi = -\frac{q}{c}\,(\nabla\Lambda)\,U\psi - U\,\frac{q}{c}\,\vec A\,\psi" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

The first term on the left is $\frac{q}{c}(\nabla\Lambda)U\psi$, which cancels the first term on the right. $U$ commutes with $\vec A$, since both are functions of position and time, and what remains is $U$ times the covariant derivative of $\psi$ (L14.1 transcript p. 4). With this identity, proving that $\psi' = U\psi$ solves the transformed Schrödinger equation is a short exercise, which the course sets as homework (L14.1 transcript p. 4).

## The Schrödinger equation made of covariant derivatives

The time derivative fits the same pattern. The scalar and vector potentials together form a four-vector, just as $ct$ and $\vec x$ do (L14.1 transcript p. 5). Moving $q\Phi$ to the left, $i\hbar\,\partial\psi/\partial t - q\Phi\psi = \frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\vec A\right)^2\psi$, and pulling out a factor $-c$ gives (L14.1 transcript p. 5)

<Formula label="The Schrödinger equation with covariant derivatives" tex="-c\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial(ct)} + \frac{q}{c}\,\Phi\right)\psi = \frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\,\vec A\right)^2\psi" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

The bracket on the left has the same shape as the covariant derivative on the right: a derivative times $\frac{\hbar}{i}$, minus $\frac{q}{c}$ times a component of the potential. [With the time coordinate $ct$ and the four-potential $(\Phi, \vec A)$, the time component with a lower index is $-\Phi$, which turns the $+$ into a $-$; the spoken signs in the transcript are hard to follow here.] The whole equation is built from these covariant derivatives (L14.1 transcript p. 5).

This also says something about momentum. The operator $\vec p$ is the **canonical momentum**: it obeys $[x, p_x] = i\hbar$ and generates translations. But, as the previous lesson already noted, in a magnetic field it is **not** mass times velocity. The combination that is, and whose square over $2m$ is the kinetic energy, is (L14.1 transcript p. 5)

<Formula label="Mass times velocity" tex="m\vec v = \vec p - \frac{q}{c}\,\vec A" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

## Gauge-invariant observables

Under a gauge transformation an operator $O$ may change into some $O'$; what must not change is what we measure, its expectation value (L14.2 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Definition of a gauge-invariant observable" tex="\langle\psi'|O'|\psi'\rangle = \langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The canonical momentum fails this test. A gauge transformation changes $\vec A$, $\Phi$ and $\psi$, but nothing tells $\vec p$ to change, so $O' = O = \vec p$. Sandwiched between $\psi' = U\psi$ and its conjugate, $\vec p$ also differentiates the position-dependent phase, and the result does not cancel (L14.2 transcript p. 1). Working it out, which the course leaves for homework:

<Formula label="The canonical momentum is not gauge invariant" tex="\langle\psi'|\vec p|\psi'\rangle = \langle\psi|\vec p|\psi\rangle + \frac{q}{c}\,\langle\psi|\nabla\Lambda|\psi\rangle" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

So $\vec p$ is "not intuitive, not physical, not observable", in Zwiebach's words: once electromagnetic fields are present, the generator of translations loses its privileged place (L14.2 transcript p. 2). The covariant combination, on the other hand, lets the phase pass through by the identity above, and $\vec A'$ in $O'$ becomes $\vec A$ (L14.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The gauge-invariant momentum" tex="\left\langle\psi'\left|\,\vec p - \frac{q}{c}\,\vec A'\,\right|\psi'\right\rangle = \left\langle\psi\left|\,\vec p - \frac{q}{c}\,\vec A\,\right|\psi\right\rangle" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

This is the physical momentum $m\vec v$ of the last section.

## A magnetic field on a torus

A circle is a line with $x$ identified with $x + L$. A **torus** is a plane with two identifications: every point $(x, y)$ is the same point as $(x + L_x, y)$ and as $(x, y + L_y)$ (L14.2 transcript p. 2). Glue the two vertical edges of the rectangle $L_x \times L_y$ to make a cylinder, then glue its two ends together. Now put a constant magnetic field $B_0$ along $z$, through the surface. Maxwell's equations have no objection: $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, there are no currents and no electric field. So any constant $B_0$ seems allowed (L14.2 transcript p. 2).

But suppose quantum mechanics holds and a particle of charge $q$ exists. Its Schrödinger equation needs a vector potential, so the field is consistent only if a vector potential exists on the torus (L14.2 transcript p. 3). With $B_z = \partial A_y/\partial x - \partial A_x/\partial y$, a simple choice is (L14.2 transcript p. 3)

<Formula label="A first vector potential" tex="A_y = B_0\,x, \qquad A_x = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

It does not depend on $y$, so it agrees at $y$ and $y + L_y$. But at $x + L_x$, the same points of the torus, it has a different value, $B_0(x + L_x)$ instead of $B_0x$: as it stands, it does not live on the torus (L14.2 transcript p. 3).

Gauge transformations come to the rescue. A vector potential does not have to agree with itself at the glued points; it is enough that the two values differ by a gauge transformation (L14.2 transcript p. 4). That requires a $\Lambda$ with $A_y(x + L_x, y) = A_y(x, y) + \partial\Lambda/\partial y$ (and $\partial\Lambda/\partial x = 0$ for $A_x$), which works (L14.2 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The gauge parameter that glues the edges" tex="B_0\,(x + L_x) = B_0\,x + \frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial y} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Lambda = B_0\,L_x\,y" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

Now $\Lambda$ itself is not periodic in $y$. But $\Lambda$ never has to be well defined; only $U$ does, the quantity that actually multiplies the wavefunction and from which the change $\nabla\Lambda$ of the potential can be recovered (L14.2 transcript p. 5). $U = e^{iqB_0L_xy/\hbar c}$ is unchanged under $y \rightarrow y + L_y$ only if its exponent changes by a multiple of $2\pi i$ (L14.2 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="U must be single valued" tex="\frac{q\,B_0\,L_x\,L_y}{\hbar c} = 2\pi n, \qquad n = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \ldots" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

Since $B_0L_xL_y$ is the flux of the field through the torus, written $\Phi_B$ here because $\Phi$ is the scalar potential, the flux is **quantized** (L14.2 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="Flux quantization" tex="\Phi_B = B_0\,L_x\,L_y = n\,\frac{2\pi\hbar c}{q} = n\,\Phi_0, \qquad \Phi_0 = \frac{hc}{q}" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

[The transcript garbles this line; the factor $2\pi\hbar$ is $h$.] A field that solves Maxwell's equations is therefore not automatically allowed: to have a well-defined vector potential on the torus, the field had to be quantized (L14.2 transcript p. 5). $\Phi_0$ is the **flux quantum**, the least possible nonzero flux.

For $q = e$ Zwiebach quotes $\Phi_0 \approx 2.068 \times 10^{-15}$ webers, or $2.068 \times 10^{-7}$ maxwells (L14.2 transcript p. 6). A weber is a tesla times a square metre, far too big for this; a maxwell is a gauss times a square centimetre, and 1 Wb is $10^{8}$ Mx, so the two figures agree with each other. For scale, the Earth's magnetic field is about half a gauss (L14.2 transcript p. 6). [The value quoted is $h/2e$, the flux quantum of superconductors, where the charge carriers are pairs of electrons with $q = 2e$. The formula $hc/q$ with $q = e$ gives twice as much: $\Phi_0 = hc/e \approx 4.136 \times 10^{-7}$ G cm², which is $h/e \approx 4.136 \times 10^{-15}$ Wb in SI units. This page uses $hc/e$.]

## Landau levels

The second example is a particle of mass $m$ and charge $q$ in a uniform magnetic field $B$ along $z$, first solved by Lev Landau (L14.3 transcript p. 1). Classically it moves in circles, and all orbits go round at the same angular frequency, the **cyclotron frequency**, which follows from Newton's law with the force $\frac{q}{c}\vec v \times \vec B$ (L14.3 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Cyclotron frequency" tex="\omega_c = \frac{qB}{mc}" cite="reading3" at="p. 1" />

Spin is ignored: with spin there is an extra Zeeman energy, which is easy to add afterwards (L14.3 transcript p. 1). (Here and below take $qB > 0$; for an electron, read $q$ as $e$ and point $B$ the other way.) For the vector potential choose the **Landau gauge**, $\vec A = (-By, 0, 0)$: then $\partial A_y/\partial x - \partial A_x/\partial y = B$ as required (L14.3 transcript p. 1). The Hamiltonian becomes (L14.3 transcript pp. 1–2)

<Formula label="Hamiltonian in the Landau gauge" tex="H = \frac{1}{2m}\left(p_x + \frac{qB}{c}\,y\right)^2 + \frac{p_y^2}{2m} + \frac{p_z^2}{2m}" cite="reading3" at="pp. 1–2" />

Motion along $z$ is just a plane wave and is dropped; the interesting physics is in the plane (L14.3 transcript p. 2). $H$ contains no $x$, so it commutes with $p_x$, and the energy eigenstates can be chosen as eigenstates of $p_x$ too (L14.3 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The form of the eigenstates" tex="\psi(x, y) = e^{ik_xx}\,\chi(y), \qquad p_x = \hbar k_x" cite="reading3" at="p. 2" />

These look odd, plane waves in $x$ when we expected circles. In a more symmetric gauge, with both $A_x$ and $A_y$ nonzero, neither $p_x$ nor $p_y$ would commute with $H$ and this shortcut would be lost; the Landau gauge is simply the easy one to solve (L14.3 transcript p. 2). On these states $p_x$ is the number $\hbar k_x$, and what acts on $\chi(y)$ is, after completing the square (L14.3 transcript pp. 2–3),

<Formula label="A shifted harmonic oscillator" tex="\frac{p_y^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2m}\left(\hbar k_x + \frac{qB}{c}\,y\right)^2 = \frac{p_y^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}\,m\,\omega_c^2\,(y - y_0)^2, \qquad y_0 = -\frac{\hbar k_x c}{qB}" cite="reading3" at="pp. 2–3" />

This is a harmonic oscillator in $y$, centred at $y_0$, and its frequency is exactly the classical cyclotron frequency. The levels are therefore spaced by $\hbar\omega_c$: these are the **Landau levels** (L14.3 transcript p. 3). An oscillator's natural length is $\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}$; with $\omega = \omega_c$ this defines the **magnetic length** $\ell_B$, roughly the width of the ground state, and the centre is set by $k_x$ (L14.3 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Magnetic length and the centre of the orbit" tex="\ell_B = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega_c}} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar c}{qB}}, \qquad y_0 = -k_x\,\ell_B^2" cite="reading3" at="pp. 3–4" />

The units work: $k_x$ is an inverse length and $\ell_B^2$ a length squared (L14.3 transcript p. 4). The energies are those of an oscillator (L14.3 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Landau levels" tex="E = \hbar\omega_c\left(n_y + \frac{1}{2}\right), \qquad n_y = 0, 1, 2, \ldots" cite="reading3" at="p. 4" />

The striking thing is what is missing. The plane wave in $x$ contributes no $\hbar^2k_x^2/2m$: the energy does not depend on $k_x$ at all. So each Landau level, $n_y = 0$ (the lowest Landau level), $n_y = 1$, $n_y = 2$ and so on, is **infinitely degenerate**. Superposing degenerate states with different $k_x$, by a Fourier transform, one can build states localized in $x$ as well (L14.3 transcript p. 4).

## Other gauges and a finite sample

In another gauge, such as a symmetric one with both $A_x$ and $A_y$, the solutions look more like circular orbits, with a little more mathematics. They must describe the same physics, with the same Landau levels and the same degeneracy, but comparing two gauges is not simple: a state in one gauge corresponds to a gauge transformation of some **superposition** of degenerate states in the other (L14.4 transcript p. 1). The course works this out in homework and recitation.

To count the states, take a finite sample of size $L_x \times L_y$ (L14.4 transcript p. 1). For a large sample the boundary conditions hardly matter, so impose periodicity in $x$: $e^{ik_xx}$ must repeat after $L_x$ (L14.4 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Quantized k" tex="k_x\,L_x = 2\pi N_x, \qquad N_x = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \ldots" cite="reading4" at="p. 1" />

The orbit sits at $y_0 = -k_x\ell_B^2$, and it must lie in the sample, $0 \le y_0 \le L_y$, so $N_x$ is negative and runs from 0 down to the value that puts $y_0$ at the far edge, $L_y = 2\pi|N_x|\,\ell_B^2/L_x$ (L14.4 transcript p. 2). The number of allowed values, the degeneracy $N$ of each Landau level, is (L14.4 transcript p. 2)

<Formula label="States per Landau level" tex="N = \frac{L_x\,L_y}{2\pi\,\ell_B^2} = \frac{B\,L_x\,L_y}{2\pi\hbar c/q} = \frac{\Phi_B}{\Phi_0}" cite="reading4" at="p. 2" />

The degeneracy is the number of flux quanta through the sample: the same flux quantum as on the torus. Zwiebach's example is a sample of 1 cm² in a field of 1 gauss, so $\Phi_B = 1$ G cm² (L14.4 transcript p. 2). [He divides by $\Phi_0 \approx 2 \times 10^{-7}$ G cm², the value quoted earlier, and gets about 5 million states. With $\Phi_0 = hc/e \approx 4.136 \times 10^{-7}$ G cm² it is about 2.4 million states.] Either way the numbers are huge. The problem is a classic of condensed matter physics and the first step toward understanding the **quantum Hall effect** (L14.4 transcript p. 2).

<WorkedExample title="Landau levels of an electron at 10 tesla">
<Problem>
An electron moves in a plane in a field $B = 10^{5}$ G (10 T), spin ignored. Use $e = 4.803 \times 10^{-10}$ esu, $m = 9.109 \times 10^{-28}$ g, $c = 2.998 \times 10^{10}$ cm/s, $\hbar = 1.0546 \times 10^{-27}$ erg s, 1 eV $= 1.602 \times 10^{-12}$ erg and $k_B = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/K. (a) Find $\omega_c$ and the spacing $\hbar\omega_c$ of the Landau levels, and compare it with $k_BT$ at 4.2 K, the temperature of liquid helium. (b) Find the magnetic length. (c) How many states does each Landau level hold per square centimetre? (d) If the plane is a torus of 1 μm by 1 μm, how many flux quanta cross it, and in what steps can the field through it change?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Cyclotron frequency and level spacing">
$\omega_c = eB/mc = (4.803 \times 10^{-10} \times 10^{5})/(9.109 \times 10^{-28} \times 2.998 \times 10^{10}) \approx 1.76 \times 10^{12}$ per second, a frequency $\omega_c/2\pi$ of about 280 GHz. Then $\hbar\omega_c \approx 1.855 \times 10^{-15}$ erg, or about 1.16 meV. At 4.2 K, $k_BT \approx 0.362$ meV, so the spacing is only about 3.2 times the thermal energy: the levels can be resolved, but only in a cold sample and a strong field.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Magnetic length">
$\ell_B^2 = \hbar c/eB = (1.0546 \times 10^{-27} \times 2.998 \times 10^{10})/(4.803 \times 10^{-5}) \approx 6.58 \times 10^{-13}$ cm², so $\ell_B \approx 8.11 \times 10^{-7}$ cm, about 8.1 nm. A state of the lowest Landau level is a strip only a few tens of atomic spacings wide.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) States per square centimetre">
With $\Phi_0 = hc/e = 2\pi\hbar c/e \approx 4.136 \times 10^{-7}$ G cm², a square centimetre carries $10^{5}/(4.136 \times 10^{-7}) \approx 2.42 \times 10^{11}$ flux quanta, so each Landau level holds about $2.42 \times 10^{11}$ states per cm². The same number follows from $1/2\pi\ell_B^2$: one state for every $2\pi\ell_B^2$ of area.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) The torus">
A side of 1 μm is $10^{-4}$ cm, so the area is $10^{-8}$ cm², and $\Phi_B = 10^{5} \times 10^{-8} = 10^{-3}$ G cm², about 2,418 flux quanta. On this torus the field can only take the values $n\,\Phi_0$ divided by the area, in steps of about 41.4 G (4.1 mT): tiny next to 10 T, but a real restriction in principle. In fact 10 T itself is not allowed on this torus, since 2,418 is only the nearest whole number; the nearest allowed field, with $n = 2418$, is about 100,010 G.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The flux of a magnetic monopole">
In 8.286 Lecture 22, taught by a substitute lecturer (Scott Hughes) from Guth's notes, grand unified theories are shown to predict magnetic monopoles, and their magnetic charge is quoted from the notes in Gaussian units, $g = e/2\alpha$, where $\alpha = e^2/\hbar c$ is the fine-structure constant. The total flux out of such a monopole is $4\pi g = 2\pi e/\alpha = 2\pi\hbar c/e$: exactly one flux quantum $hc/e$ of this lecture. That is no coincidence, though neither lecture makes the link. Dirac showed in 1931 that if any particle of charge $e$ exists, the same requirement as on the torus, a single-valued phase factor $U$, allows a monopole only if its flux is a whole number of flux quanta. (Dirac's own argument used a string of singular points; in modern language, due to Wu and Yang in 1975, a sphere around the monopole is covered by two patches whose potentials differ by a gauge transformation, and the sphere plays the part of the torus.) This value is the smallest one Dirac's condition permits. The enormous number of such monopoles a conventional Big Bang would make is the monopole problem of 8.286 Lecture 22, which inflation solves in Lecture 23.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A particle of charge $q$ couples to the fields through $H = \frac{1}{2m}(\vec p - \frac{q}{c}\vec A)^2 + q\Phi$ (Gaussian units). Written out, $\vec p\cdot\vec A$ acts on everything to its right, giving $H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{i\hbar q}{2mc}\nabla\cdot\vec A - \frac{q}{mc}\vec A\cdot\vec p + \frac{q^2A^2}{2mc^2} + q\Phi$ for a spinless particle.
- A gauge transformation $\vec A' = \vec A + \nabla\Lambda$, $\Phi' = \Phi - \frac{1}{c}\partial\Lambda/\partial t$ comes with $\psi' = U\psi$, $U = e^{iq\Lambda/\hbar c}$. The covariant derivative $\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\vec A$ lets $U$ pass through while $\vec A'$ becomes $\vec A$, and the whole Schrödinger equation is built from such derivatives.
- Observables need $\langle\psi'|O'|\psi'\rangle = \langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle$. The canonical momentum $\vec p$ fails; $\vec p - \frac{q}{c}\vec A = m\vec v$ passes.
- On a torus a constant field needs a vector potential patched by $\Lambda = B_0L_xy$; a single-valued $U$ then forces $\Phi_B = B_0L_xL_y = n\,hc/q$. For $q = e$, $\Phi_0 = hc/e \approx 4.14 \times 10^{-7}$ G cm² ($h/e$ in SI units; the $2.068 \times 10^{-15}$ Wb quoted in the lecture is $h/2e$).
- In a uniform field the Landau gauge $\vec A = (-By, 0, 0)$ gives states $e^{ik_xx}\chi(y)$ and an oscillator in $y$ at the cyclotron frequency $\omega_c = qB/mc$, centred at $y_0 = -k_x\ell_B^2$ with $\ell_B = \sqrt{\hbar c/qB}$. The Landau levels $E = \hbar\omega_c(n_y + \frac{1}{2})$ do not depend on $k_x$.
- In a sample of area $L_xL_y$ each Landau level holds $N = L_xL_y/2\pi\ell_B^2 = \Phi_B/\Phi_0$ states: for 1 G through 1 cm², about 2.4 million.
