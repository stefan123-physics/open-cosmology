The previous lecture found that in a molecule the electrons move much faster than the nuclei vibrate. This lecture turns that observation into a method, the **Born–Oppenheimer approximation**: solve for the electrons with the nuclei held still, then let the nuclei move in the energy the electrons provide. On the way, Berry's connection reappears in the Hamiltonian of the nuclei. The lecture ends with the simplest molecule, the hydrogen molecule ion H₂⁺, treated with a simple trial function (L18.1–L18.3 transcripts; notes sections 6.1 and 6.3). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in three short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 6; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## The Hamiltonian of a molecule

Zwiebach recalls the energy scales: electronic, vibrational and rotational energies stand in the ratios $1 : \sqrt{m/M} : m/M$, with $m$ the electron mass and $M$ a nuclear mass, so $\sqrt{m/M}$ may be about $10^{-2}$. Because the nuclei vibrate on a time scale much longer than any change of the electron cloud, the electrons should follow the nuclei adiabatically: solve for the electron cloud as a function of the nuclear positions, and use those states as instantaneous eigenstates when the nuclei move (L18.1 transcript p. 1). He warns that one step of the derivation will be skipped in class and left to the notes.

Take a molecule with $N$ nuclei and $n$ electrons. [The transcript has "little m" electrons at the start; the number of electrons is $n$, as the lecture says later and the notes write.] Notation matters in a many-body problem (L18.1 transcript p. 2). Nucleus $\alpha$, with $\alpha = 1, \ldots, N$, has mass $M_\alpha$ and a canonical pair of vector operators $\vec P_\alpha, \vec R_\alpha$; electron $i$, with $i = 1, \ldots, n$, has $\vec p_i, \vec r_i$. Write $\vec R$ for the whole set of nuclear positions and $\vec r$ for the whole set of electron positions. The Hamiltonian is (L18.1 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="The molecular Hamiltonian" tex="H = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\frac{P_\alpha^2}{2M_\alpha} + V_{NN}(\vec R) + \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{p_i^2}{2m} + V_{eN}(\vec R, \vec r) + V_{ee}(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.8), p. 16" />

with $P_\alpha^2 = \vec P_\alpha\cdot\vec P_\alpha$. The first two terms would be all there is if the nuclei lived alone: their kinetic energies and their mutual Coulomb repulsion $V_{NN}$. The rest is the **electronic Hamiltonian**, which gives the electrons their dynamics (L18.1 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The electronic Hamiltonian" tex="H_e = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{p_i^2}{2m} + V_{eN}(\vec R, \vec r) + V_{ee}(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.9), p. 16" />

An electron is affected by the other electrons, through $V_{ee}$, and by the nuclei, through $V_{eN}$, which depends on where the nuclei are. All three potentials are Coulomb potentials. One could dream of putting this Hamiltonian into a computer and reading off the molecule, Zwiebach says, and that is roughly true, but even for a good computer it is difficult; the separation of scales is what makes a simplification possible (L18.1 transcript p. 3).

## Electrons around a frozen skeleton

For large nuclear masses, consider a **fixed nuclear skeleton**: hold every $\vec R_\alpha$ fixed and solve for the electrons alone (L18.1 transcript pp. 3–4). The nuclei are not really classical particles at fixed positions, they vibrate, but to a good approximation they are localized. The electrons then move in a fixed potential, and like any particle in a fixed potential they have many energy eigenstates (L18.1 transcript pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="Electronic states at fixed nuclei" tex="\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nabla_{i}^2 + V_{eN}(\vec R, \vec r) + V_{ee}(\vec r)\right]\phi^{(k)}_{\vec R}(\vec r) = E_e^{(k)}(\vec R)\,\phi^{(k)}_{\vec R}(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.10), p. 17" />

Here $\nabla_{i}$ is the gradient with respect to $\vec r_i$. The wavefunction $\phi^{(k)}_{\vec R}$ is a function of all the electron positions, the argument $\vec r$; the label $k$ counts the states, ground state, next, and so on; and the subscript $\vec R$ records that each skeleton gives different states (L18.1 transcript p. 4). The electronic energies $E_e^{(k)}(\vec R)$ depend on the skeleton but, being eigenvalues, not on $\vec r$. (The notes label the states by $i$; the lecture switches to $k$ to avoid a clash with the electron label, and this page does the same.)

## An exact expansion and a one-term guess

Suppose the electronic problem is solved for every skeleton. The states at each $\vec R$ form a complete set, so the exact molecular wavefunction can be expanded in them, with coefficients that depend on the nuclear positions (L18.1 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="An exact expansion" tex="\psi(\vec R, \vec r) = \sum_{k}\eta^{(k)}(\vec R)\,\phi^{(k)}_{\vec R}(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.11), p. 17" />

This is correct, but the unknowns $\eta^{(k)}$ must be found by substituting into the full Schrödinger equation, $H\psi = E\psi$ (notes eq. (6.12)). Since the $\phi$'s are known, the result is a set of differential equations for the $\eta$'s, all coupled to one another: very difficult (L18.1 transcript p. 5). So keep just one term, with the electronic ground state (L18.1 transcript p. 6):

<Formula label="The Born–Oppenheimer product" tex="\psi(\vec R, \vec r) = \eta(\vec R)\,\phi_{\vec R}(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.13), p. 17" />

This will never be an exact solution, Zwiebach stresses: the Hamiltonian has terms that mix nuclear and electronic variables, so it does not separate, and that is why the $\eta$'s were coupled. But the product is in the spirit of the adiabatic approximation: as the nuclear state changes slowly, the electron cloud adjusts and stays in its ground state, rather than jumping to another (L18.1 transcript p. 6). The question is how to find $\eta$.

## An effective Hamiltonian for the nuclei

Think **variational method** (L18.2 transcript p. 1): for any normalized trial state, the expectation value of the Hamiltonian is at least the ground-state energy, so a good trial state is one that makes it small. Take the product as the trial state, with $\phi_{\vec R}$ known and $\eta$ still to be chosen. The expectation value is an integral over all the nuclear and electron positions, and since $\phi_{\vec R}$ is known, all the $\vec r$ integrals can be done (L18.2 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Integrating out the electrons" tex="\langle H\rangle = \int d\vec R\,d\vec r\;\eta^*(\vec R)\,\phi_{\vec R}^*(\vec r)\,H\,\eta(\vec R)\,\phi_{\vec R}(\vec r) = \int d\vec R\;\eta^*(\vec R)\,H_{eff}\,\eta(\vec R)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.14), p. 17" />

What remains is the expectation value of an **effective Hamiltonian** $H_{eff}$ for the nuclei in the state $\eta$, in which the electrons no longer appear. Minimizing over $\eta$ is now a problem for the nuclei alone. "A lot of physics in that step", Zwiebach says: this is how one finds an effective Hamiltonian for slow degrees of freedom by integrating out fast ones (L18.2 transcript pp. 1–2).

To find $H_{eff}$, split the Hamiltonian into a nuclear part and the electronic Hamiltonian (notes p. 17):

<Formula label="Nuclear and electronic parts" tex="H = H_N + H_e, \qquad H_N = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{N}\frac{P_\alpha^2}{2M_\alpha} + V_{NN}(\vec R)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.15), p. 17" />

The $H_e$ term is easy (L18.2 transcript p. 2). $H_e$ takes no derivatives with respect to the nuclear positions, so $\eta$ is a mere factor for it, and acting on $\phi_{\vec R}$ it gives $E_e(\vec R)\,\phi_{\vec R}$ (the ground-state energy, now written without a label). The remaining $\vec r$ integral is the normalization of $\phi_{\vec R}$, equal to one:

<Formula label="The electronic energy becomes a potential" tex="\langle H_e\rangle = \int d\vec R\,d\vec r\;\eta^*\,\phi_{\vec R}^*\,H_e\,\eta\,\phi_{\vec R} = \int d\vec R\;\eta^*(\vec R)\,E_e(\vec R)\,\eta(\vec R)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.16), p. 18" />

So the whole electron cloud contributes to $H_{eff}$ the term $E_e(\vec R)$: its energy acts as a **potential for the nuclei**. The potential $V_{NN}$ simply copies itself, since it does not act on the electrons.

## The electronic Berry connection

The nuclear kinetic term is the hard one (L18.2 transcript p. 3): $\vec P_\alpha = \frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla_{\alpha}$, with $\nabla_{\alpha}$ the gradient with respect to $\vec R_\alpha$, acts on $\eta$ **and** on $\phi_{\vec R}$, which depends on the nuclear positions. Zwiebach gives the answer rather than the calculation; the notes do the algebra, moving $\phi_{\vec R}^*$ through $\vec P_\alpha$ and doing the $\vec r$ integrals (notes eqs. (6.17)–(6.24), pp. 18–19). The result has a familiar shape (L18.2 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="The effective nuclear Hamiltonian" tex="H_{eff} = \sum_{\alpha}\frac{(\vec P_\alpha - \vec A_\alpha)^2}{2M_\alpha} + U(\vec R)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.25), p. 19" />

The momentum appears shifted, as for a charged particle in a vector potential, although there is no electromagnetic field anywhere. The shift is a **Berry connection**, which shows up because the electronic states depend on the slowly changing $\vec R$ (L18.2 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The electronic Berry connection" tex="\vec A_\alpha(\vec R) = i\hbar\int d\vec r\;\phi_{\vec R}^*(\vec r)\,\nabla_{\alpha}\phi_{\vec R}(\vec r)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.21), p. 18" />

one for each nucleus. **A note on conventions:** the notes and the lecture include a factor $\hbar$ here, so that $\vec A_\alpha$ has units of momentum and can be subtracted from $\vec P_\alpha$. The Berry connection of the previous lecture, $i\langle\psi_n|\nabla_{R}|\psi_n\rangle$ (notes eq. (5.12)), has no $\hbar$. This page keeps the $\hbar$ in $\vec A_\alpha$, so $\vec A_\alpha$ is $\hbar$ times the components of a connection of the earlier kind, for the electronic ground state. The potential is (L18.2 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The potential for the nuclei" tex="U(\vec R) = V_{NN}(\vec R) + E_e(\vec R) + \sum_{\alpha}\frac{\hbar^2}{2M_\alpha}\int d\vec r\,\left|\nabla_{\alpha}\phi_{\vec R}\right|^2 - \sum_{\alpha}\frac{A_\alpha^2}{2M_\alpha}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.26), p. 19" />

the nuclear repulsion and the electronic energy, plus two funny terms, as Zwiebach calls them, built from how fast the electronic state changes with the nuclear positions. This is the Hamiltonian of a molecule in the adiabatic approximation, already much simpler than the real problem: once the electronic wavefunctions are known, the connections can be computed (L18.2 transcript p. 4). The notes go on to write the Schrödinger equation for the nuclei, using $\vec P_\alpha - \vec A_\alpha = \frac{\hbar}{i}\left(\nabla_{\alpha} - \frac{i}{\hbar}\vec A_\alpha\right)$ (notes eqs. (6.27), (6.28), p. 19):

<Formula label="The Schrödinger equation for the nuclei" tex="\left[-\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\hbar^2}{2M_\alpha}\left(\nabla_{\alpha} - \frac{i}{\hbar}\vec A_\alpha\right)^2 + U(\vec R)\right]\eta(\vec R) = E\,\eta(\vec R)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.28), p. 19" />

## Lowest-order Born–Oppenheimer

For a simple enough molecule the electronic wavefunctions $\phi_{\vec R}$ can be chosen **real**. Then, as for any Berry connection, there is none: $\vec A_\alpha = 0$ (L18.2 transcript p. 4). The gradient term $\int|\nabla_{\alpha}\phi_{\vec R}|^2$ can still be there, and it can matter. The lowest-order approximation, the one usually meant by Born–Oppenheimer, drops both the connection and that term (L18.2 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Lowest-order Born–Oppenheimer" tex="H_{eff} = -\sum_{\alpha}\frac{\hbar^2}{2M_\alpha}\nabla_{\alpha}^2 + V_{NN}(\vec R) + E_e(\vec R)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.29), p. 19" />

Keep the nuclear kinetic energy, the nuclear repulsion and the electronic energy; the Berry terms are higher-order corrections. Much has been done with this Hamiltonian (L18.2 transcript p. 4). (In a section the lecture skips, the notes sketch the direct route to the same terms: substitute the expansion in all the electronic states into $H\psi = E\psi$, project onto one state, and drop the couplings to the others; notes section 6.2, pp. 19–20.)

Perhaps the most important lesson, Zwiebach says, is how fast degrees of freedom impose dynamics on slow ones (L18.2 transcript p. 5). Integrating out the fast ones to find an effective Hamiltonian for the slow ones is done all the time in physics, in quantum field theory for example: quarks bind into protons and neutrons, and integrating out their interactions gives the dynamics of the particles that are observed.

## The hydrogen molecule ion

The example is a familiar one: H₂⁺, two protons sharing one electron (L18.3 transcript p. 1). It is a bound state, and its binding energy is the least energy needed to start dissociating it. That does not mean tearing everything apart: removing one proton, leaving a hydrogen atom, already dissociates it. So H₂⁺ must have **lower** energy than a hydrogen atom and a far-away proton, and the H₂ molecule, with a second electron, lower still (L18.3 transcript p. 1).

First fix the skeleton: the protons a distance $R$ apart. Even this step is hard: one proton and one electron, the hydrogen atom, is solvable, but two protons and one electron has no simple analytical solution (L18.3 transcript p. 1). With $r_1$ and $r_2$ the distances from the electron to the two protons, the electronic Hamiltonian is (in Gaussian units, as in the course):

<Formula label="The electronic Hamiltonian of H₂⁺" tex="H_e = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 - e^2\left(\frac{1}{r_1} + \frac{1}{r_2}\right)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.38), p. 20" />

[The lecture corrects its first version of this Hamiltonian to have a Laplacian, as here (L18.3 transcript p. 3).] There is only one electron, and $r_1$ and $r_2$ are not two variables: with the first proton at the origin, $r_1 = |\vec r|$ and $r_2 = |\vec R - \vec r|$, two distances that depend on the single position $\vec r$ (L18.3 transcript p. 2).

## A trial function from two atoms

Since the electronic problem cannot be solved exactly, use the variational method for the electron too (L18.3 transcript p. 2). Modern variational calculations are extremely sophisticated, Zwiebach remarks, with answers converging to fifteen digits; here is a baby version, a constant times the sum of the hydrogen ground states centred on the two protons (L18.3 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The LCAO trial function" tex="\phi_R(\vec r) = C\left[\psi_0(r_1) + \psi_0(r_2)\right], \qquad \psi_0(r) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}}\,e^{-r/a_0}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (6.39), p. 20" />

with $a_0$ the Bohr radius. (The notes call the constant $A$; this page uses $C$, keeping $\vec A$ for the connection.) The technique is called **LCAO**, a linear combination of atomic orbitals: a big name for a simple thing. The trial function has the right symmetry: the two protons are identical, so the Hamiltonian is unchanged when they are exchanged, and so is the trial function, which is symmetric in $r_1$ and $r_2$ (L18.3 transcript pp. 2–3).

Is it better when the protons are far apart or close together (L18.3 transcript p. 3)? Far apart, the electron sits near one proton or the other, and near each it is a hydrogen ground state: excellent. When the protons come together they form a nucleus of charge $2e$, and the true wavefunction should be the ground state for that charge, which has half the size; the trial function, still with $a_0$, decays at the wrong rate. So it is poor at small $R$ (L18.3 transcript p. 3; notes p. 21).

Normalizing already takes some effort. The two terms overlap, and the overlap integral $I = \int d^3r\,\psi_0(r_1)\,\psi_0(r_2)$ enters (L18.3 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The normalization" tex="|C|^2 = \frac{1}{2(1 + I)}, \qquad I = e^{-R/a_0}\left(1 + \frac{R}{a_0} + \frac{1}{3}\,\frac{R^2}{a_0^2}\right)" cite="reading3" at="p. 3" />

[The notes print the exponential as $e^{+R/a}$; the lecture's $e^{-R/a_0}$ is correct: the overlap must fall off as the protons separate, and equal one at $R = 0$.]

## The electronic energy curve

The expectation value of $H_e$ in this state approximates the electronic energy $E_e(R)$ (L18.3 transcript p. 3; notes p. 21). Zwiebach plots it against $x = R/a_0$, in units of the **rydberg**, the ground-state binding energy of hydrogen, about 13.6 eV (L18.3 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="The rydberg" tex="\text{Ry} = \frac{e^2}{2a_0}" cite="reading3" at="p. 4" />

The lecture describes the curve without writing its formula. Working out the three integrals that appear (the overlap $I$; a direct integral $D$, the attraction of an electron in one atom's ground state to the other proton; and an exchange integral $X$) gives the classic result, first obtained in the early days of quantum chemistry:

<Formula label="The LCAO electronic energy" tex="\frac{E_e}{\text{Ry}} = -1 - \frac{2\,(D + X)}{1 + I}, \qquad D = \frac{1}{x} - \left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)e^{-2x}, \qquad X = (1 + x)\,e^{-x}" cite="pauling1928" at="the hydrogen molecule-ion (modern notation)" />

where $D$ and $X$ are the integrals multiplied by $a_0$, so all three are pure numbers. Its behaviour is what the lecture describes (L18.3 transcript p. 4). As $x \to 0$ the energy goes to $-3$ Ry: the trial function is a hydrogen ground state around a charge $2e$, with kinetic energy $+1$ Ry and potential energy $-4$ Ry. (The true ground state for that charge has $-4$ Ry, another sign that the trial function is poor there.) Near $x = 0$ it grows quadratically (L18.3 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Small separations" tex="\frac{E_e}{\text{Ry}} = -3 + x^2 + \cdots" cite="reading3" at="p. 4" />

For large $x$ it rises towards $-1$ Ry, the energy of a hydrogen atom, but slowly, as $-1 - 2/x$: the far proton is still attracted by the atom's electron cloud, with energy $-e^2/R$. [The lecture says the curve approaches its asymptote exponentially (L18.3 transcript p. 4); that holds for the total $E_e + V_{NN}$ below, where the repulsion $+e^2/R$ cancels the $1/x$ term.] Zwiebach's analogy is two distant square wells: the symmetric ground state spends half its time in each, but its energy is that of a single well. Likewise, with the protons far apart, the electron in the symmetric state spends half its time at each proton, with the energy of one hydrogen atom (L18.3 transcript p. 4).

## Adding the repulsion of the protons

The electronic energy alone keeps falling as the protons approach: minimizing it would collapse the system to $R = 0$, certainly not a molecule (L18.3 transcript p. 4). The Born–Oppenheimer Hamiltonian for the nuclei adds the kinetic energy and the repulsion of the protons, which in rydbergs is neat (L18.3 transcript pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="The repulsion of the protons" tex="V_{NN}(R) = \frac{e^2}{R} = \text{Ry}\cdot\frac{2}{x}" cite="reading3" at="pp. 4–5" />

The total potential for the nuclei, $E_e + V_{NN}$, starts high at small $x$, comes down, crosses the line at $-1$ Ry, reaches a rather flat minimum and then rises back towards $-1$ Ry (L18.3 transcript p. 5; notes eq. (6.41), p. 21).

At the minimum (L18.3 transcript p. 5):

[At one point the transcript has "minus 1.297"; a moment later the lecture gives $-1$ Ry $-\ 0.1297$ Ry, which is right.] Happily the minimum is below $-1$ Ry, so there is a bound state. Its binding energy is not the whole $1.1297$ Ry: $-1$ Ry is a hydrogen atom plus a free proton, so the binding is the extra $0.1297$ Ry gained by bringing a proton near the atom, about 1.76 eV (L18.3 transcript p. 5). The equilibrium separation is $2.49 \times 0.529$ Å, about 1.32 Å (L18.3 transcript p. 6).

Experiment gives a separation of 1.06 Å and a binding energy of 2.8 eV (notes p. 21; L18.3 transcript pp. 5–6). [The transcript's "106 angstroms" is 1.06 Å.] The separation comes out about 24% too large, and the binding energy about 63% of the measured value: not great, Zwiebach says, but the right order of magnitude, and all the physics is there (L18.3 transcript pp. 5–6). (Reference data in the NIST Chemistry WebBook show that 2.8 eV is the depth of the well. The real ion has a vibrational wavenumber of 2321.7 cm⁻¹, so its vibrational quantum is about 0.29 eV and its zero-point energy about 0.14 eV. From its lowest vibrational level it needs about 2.65 eV to dissociate, and 2.65 eV plus the zero-point energy is about 2.8 eV.)

The Born–Oppenheimer picture in one sentence: the electronic energy in the fixed skeleton is a potential for the nuclei, which added to their repulsion gives a total potential with a stable minimum (L18.3 transcript p. 6). Expanding it to quadratic order about the minimum gives a harmonic oscillator for the nuclear vibrations, with a frequency and vibrational energies that can be compared with experiment (L18.3 transcript p. 5). Better trial functions give better and better answers, a remarkably powerful tool. This closes the chapter on adiabatic physics; the next lecture begins scattering (L18.3 transcript p. 6).

<Problem>
Near its minimum at $x_0 = 2.493$, the LCAO potential $(E_e + V_{NN})/\text{Ry}$ has second derivative 0.1257 with respect to $x = R/a_0$ (found numerically from the formula above). Take $a_0 = 0.5292$ Å, 1 Ry = 13.606 eV, $\hbar c = 1973.3$ eV Å, $m_pc^2 = 938.27$ MeV and, for the deuteron, $m_dc^2 = 1875.61$ MeV. The relative motion of the two nuclei has the reduced mass $\mu = M/2$. (a) Find the spring constant $k$ of the potential near its minimum. (b) Find the vibrational quantum $\hbar\omega$ of H₂⁺ and its zero-point energy. (c) Repeat (b) for D₂⁺, with two deuterons. (d) Find the energy of the first rotational level of H₂⁺, $\ell = 1$, and compare the three scales with the hierarchy $1 : \sqrt{m/M} : m/M$, using $m_p/m = 1836$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The spring constant">
Near the minimum $U \approx U_0 + \frac{1}{2}k(R - R_0)^2$, and with $R = a_0x$, $k = 0.1257\,\text{Ry}/a_0^2 = 0.1257 \times 13.606/(0.5292)^2$ eV/Å², about 6.107 eV/Å² (about 98 N/m).
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Vibrations of H₂⁺">
$\mu c^2 = 938.27/2 = 469.14$ MeV, and $\hbar\omega = \hbar\sqrt{k/\mu} = \hbar c\sqrt{k/\mu c^2} = 1973.3 \times \sqrt{6.107/(469.14 \times 10^{6})}$ eV, about 0.225 eV. The zero-point energy $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$ is about 0.113 eV, so even in its lowest vibrational state the ion sits 0.11 eV above the bottom of the well: in this model about 1.65 eV, not 1.76 eV, is needed to start dissociating it.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The same potential for D₂⁺">
In the lowest-order Born–Oppenheimer approximation the potential $V_{NN} + E_e$ does not involve the nuclear masses at all: the electron cannot tell protons from deuterons. Only the nuclear kinetic term changes. With $\mu c^2 = 1875.61/2 = 937.81$ MeV, $\hbar\omega = 1973.3 \times \sqrt{6.107/(937.81 \times 10^{6})}$ eV, about 0.159 eV, smaller by about $1/\sqrt 2$ since the deuteron has about twice the proton mass. The zero-point energy is about 0.080 eV.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Rotations and the hierarchy">
With $R_0 = 2.493 \times 0.5292$ Å $= 1.319$ Å, $E_{rot} = \frac{\hbar^2\ell(\ell + 1)}{2\mu R_0^2}$ gives for $\ell = 1$: $(\hbar c)^2/(\mu c^2R_0^2) = 1973.3^2/(469.14 \times 10^{6} \times 1.319^2)$ eV, about 4.77 meV. Taking 1 Ry = 13.606 eV as the electronic scale, the three energies stand as $1 : 0.0165 : 0.00035$, while $\sqrt{m/m_p} \approx 0.023$ and $m/m_p \approx 0.00054$: the same hierarchy, within factors of order one, as the estimates of the previous lecture. (The real ion vibrates faster, with a quantum of about 0.29 eV: its well is deeper and narrower than the LCAO one.)
</Step>

## Summary

- A molecule has the Hamiltonian $H = \sum_\alpha P_\alpha^2/2M_\alpha + V_{NN} + H_e$, where the electronic part $H_e$ depends on the nuclear positions $\vec R$. For a frozen skeleton the electrons have states $\phi^{(k)}_{\vec R}(\vec r)$ with energies $E_e^{(k)}(\vec R)$.
- The exact wavefunction $\sum_k\eta^{(k)}(\vec R)\,\phi^{(k)}_{\vec R}(\vec r)$ leads to coupled equations. The **Born–Oppenheimer** product $\eta(\vec R)\,\phi_{\vec R}(\vec r)$, with the electronic ground state, follows the adiabatic idea: the electron cloud adjusts to the slow nuclei without jumping.
- Integrating out the electrons in $\langle H\rangle$ gives an **effective Hamiltonian** for the nuclei, $\sum_\alpha(\vec P_\alpha - \vec A_\alpha)^2/2M_\alpha + U$, with the electronic Berry connection $\vec A_\alpha = i\hbar\int d\vec r\,\phi_{\vec R}^*\nabla_{\alpha}\phi_{\vec R}$ (with $\hbar$, as in the notes) and $U = V_{NN} + E_e$ plus two small terms.
- For real electronic states $\vec A_\alpha = 0$. At **lowest order** the nuclei move in $V_{NN}(\vec R) + E_e(\vec R)$: the electrons' energy is their potential. Fast degrees of freedom give effective dynamics for slow ones.
- **H₂⁺** with the LCAO trial function $C[\psi_0(r_1) + \psi_0(r_2)]$: $E_e$ runs from $-3$ Ry at $R = 0$ to $-1$ Ry at large $R$; adding $V_{NN} = e^2/R$ gives a minimum at $R = 2.49\,a_0 \approx 1.32$ Å, $0.1297$ Ry $\approx 1.76$ eV below a hydrogen atom and a proton. Experiment: 1.06 Å and 2.8 eV.
