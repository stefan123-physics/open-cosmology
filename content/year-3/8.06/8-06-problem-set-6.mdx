This set practises four lessons: [Fermi's golden rule](/courses/8-06/fermis-golden-rule-constant-perturbations), with its way of counting the states of a continuum; [harmonic perturbations](/courses/8-06/harmonic-perturbations-ionization-of-hydrogen), where light ionizes hydrogen; [Einstein's argument](/courses/8-06/ionization-rate-two-level-atoms-einstein) for spontaneous emission; and [Einstein's A and B coefficients](/courses/8-06/thermal-transition-rates-einstein-coefficients), with lifetimes and selection rules. Problems 1 to 3 follow MIT's Problem Set 6, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. All five problems count for the score (80 points).

A few things differ from MIT's sheet. Its announcements and its reading list are left out: the textbook is not used here. The book was not opened. **Problems 4 and 5 on this page are our own problems** on those two subjects, with our own parts and numbers and with hydrogen wavefunctions that we supply; they keep MIT's 20 points each.

Some boxes and numbers in Problems 1 to 3 are our additions, each marked where it appears: in Problem 1 the box for the dipole matrix element, the choice box about $m_\ell = \pm 1$ and the number of radians of oscillation in one lifetime; in Problem 2 the choice box for the condition, the box for the squared matrix element, the simplification for a fast electron and the choice box about the high-frequency fall; in Problem 3 the choice box about the meaning of the result, the temperature 300 K for "room temperature" and the frequency $5 \times 10^{14}$ Hz for visible light (MIT names neither), and the boxes for the exponent at the visible frequency and for $r$ at the maser frequency, which stand for MIT's question of which process wins. ## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `2*pi*m/hbar^2`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent, so where two of them are related the problem says which to use: in Problem 2 type the wavenumber `k`, not $\omega$ and $V_0$, and in Problems 4 and 5 type `al`, `m`, `c` and `hbar`, not $e$ and $a_0$. Write the symbols as the box names them: `hbar` stands for $\hbar$, `w` for $\omega$, `w0` for $\omega_0$, `E0` for the field amplitude $E_0$, `kB` for $k_B$, `nb` for $\bar n$ and `al` for the fine-structure constant $\alpha$.

Give a number answer to 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. Where a box needs more digits, its label says so.

MIT gives the points per problem. The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons. A harmonic perturbation is written $\delta H(t) = 2H'\cos\omega t$. For two levels, $a$ is the lower one and $b$ the upper one, $\omega_{ba} = (E_b - E_a)/\hbar$, and $\vec d_{ab}$ is the matrix element of the dipole operator $\vec d = q\vec r$ between them. The units are Gaussian, as in the lectures: the Coulomb energy of two charges $e$ a distance $r$ apart is $e^2/r$, and $e^2 = \alpha\hbar c$. Constants (CODATA 2018, rounded): $\alpha = 1/137.036$, the electron's rest energy $mc^2 = 510{,}999$ eV, $\hbar = 6.582120 \times 10^{-16}$ eV s, $h = 6.62607 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K and $c = 2.997925 \times 10^{8}$ m/s. With these the Rydberg energy is $R_y = \frac12\alpha^2mc^2 = 13.6057$ eV and $hc = 1239.84$ eV nm. Problem 1 uses MIT's rounded 13.6 eV. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Decay of the three-dimensional harmonic oscillator" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

A particle of charge $q$ and mass $m$ moves in a three-dimensional isotropic harmonic oscillator of frequency $\omega$. The aim is the lifetime of its first excited states. Write the energy eigenstates as $|n_x, n_y, n_z\rangle$. By analogy with hydrogen, MIT calls the ground state $|0, 0, 0\rangle$ the 1s state, and the three states $|1, 0, 0\rangle$, $|0, 1, 0\rangle$ and $|0, 0, 1\rangle$ the 2p states. Another basis for the 2p states is made of eigenstates of $L_z$:

<Formula label="The 2p states of the oscillator as eigenstates of the z component of angular momentum" tex="|m_\ell = \pm 1\rangle = \frac{|1, 0, 0\rangle \pm i\,|0, 1, 0\rangle}{\sqrt 2}, \qquad |m_\ell = 0\rangle = |0, 0, 1\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Find the rate $\Gamma(2p, m_\ell \rightarrow 1s)$, the probability per unit time that the particle emits light spontaneously and drops to the ground state. Show that the rate is the same for the three values of $m_\ell$, and give $\Gamma(2p \rightarrow 1s)$ in terms of $m$, $\omega$, $q$ and constants of nature. Two of the three boxes are our additions: the squared dipole matrix element for $m_\ell = 0$, and what changes for $m_\ell = \pm 1$.

<Hint>

The rate of spontaneous emission is Einstein's $A$ of the lesson, which needs only the frequency of the transition and the vector $\vec d_{ab} = q\,\langle 1s|\vec r\,|2p\rangle$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each of $x$, $y$ and $z$ is a sum of a lowering and a raising operator of its own one-dimensional oscillator. Which component of $\vec r$ can take $|0, 0, 1\rangle$ to $|0, 0, 0\rangle$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $m_\ell = \pm 1$ the vector $\vec d_{ab}$ has complex components. Its squared length is $\vec d_{ab}^{\,*}\cdot\vec d_{ab}$, the sum of the squared moduli of the components.

</Hint>

  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], w: [0.5, 2], c: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ w: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[

<Solution>

**The idea.** Spontaneous emission needs one thing from the atom: the matrix element of $q\vec r$ between the two states. For an oscillator the ladder operators give it in one line.

<Step title="The rate of spontaneous emission">

The lesson found, from Einstein's argument and the rate in thermal light,

<Formula label="Einstein's A coefficient for the transition from b to a" tex="A = \frac{4}{3}\,\frac{\omega_{ba}^3}{\hbar c^3}\,|\vec d_{ab}|^2, \qquad \vec d_{ab} = q\,\langle a|\vec r\,|b\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eq. (4.5.28)" />

Here $a$ is the 1s state, $b$ one of the 2p states, and $\omega_{ba} = \omega$, because the levels of the oscillator are $\hbar\omega$ apart. So $\Gamma = A$.

</Step>

<Step title="The matrix element for m = 0">

For each direction the position is built from that direction's ladder operators, $x = \sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}\,(a_x + a_x^\dagger)$, and the same for $y$ and $z$. Only $a_z$ takes $|0, 0, 1\rangle$ to $|0, 0, 0\rangle$, with $\langle 0|a_z|1\rangle = 1$. So

<Formula label="Dipole matrix element of the oscillator state with m = 0" tex="\vec d_{ab} = q\,\langle 0, 0, 0|\vec r\,|0, 0, 1\rangle = q\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\;\hat z, \qquad |\vec d_{ab}|^2 = \frac{q^2\hbar}{2m\omega}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The matrix elements for m = +1 and m = −1">

In the same way $\langle 0, 0, 0|\vec r\,|1, 0, 0\rangle = \sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}\;\hat x$ and $\langle 0, 0, 0|\vec r\,|0, 1, 0\rangle = \sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}\;\hat y$. For the two combinations:

<Formula label="Dipole matrix elements of the oscillator states with m = ±1" tex="\vec d_{ab} = q\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\;\frac{\hat x \pm i\,\hat y}{\sqrt 2}, \qquad |\vec d_{ab}|^2 = \vec d_{ab}^{\,*}\cdot\vec d_{ab} = \frac{q^2\hbar}{2m\omega}\cdot\frac{1 + 1}{2} = \frac{q^2\hbar}{2m\omega}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The vector lies in the $xy$ plane and has complex components, but its squared length is the same as for $m_\ell = 0$. So the rate does not depend on $m_\ell$. It could not: the three states are turned into each other by rotations, and the oscillator and the empty space around it look the same in every direction.

</Step>

<Step title="The rate">

<Formula label="Spontaneous decay rate of the 2p level of the oscillator" tex="\Gamma(2p \rightarrow 1s) = \frac{4}{3}\,\frac{\omega^3}{\hbar c^3}\cdot\frac{q^2\hbar}{2m\omega} = \frac{2\,q^2\omega^2}{3\,mc^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The only constant of nature left is $c$. [In SI units, $q^2$ becomes $q^2/4\pi\epsilon_0$.]

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units: $q^2$ is an energy times a length, and $mc^2$ is an energy, so $q^2/(mc^3)$ is a length divided by a speed, a time. Times $\omega^2$ that is one over a time, a rate.

Planck's constant has dropped out, so the result should have a classical meaning, and it does. A classical charge that swings with amplitude $X$ has the energy $E = \frac12m\omega^2X^2$ and the mean squared acceleration $\frac12\omega^4X^2$. Larmor's formula for the radiated power of an accelerated charge, $P = 2q^2\overline{a^2}/(3c^3)$ (a result of electrodynamics, not derived in the lessons of this course), then gives $P/E = 2q^2\omega^2/(3mc^3)$: the classical oscillator loses its energy at exactly the rate $\Gamma$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

What is the lifetime $\tau$ of the 2p level? Treat the oscillator as a model of hydrogen: let the particle be an electron and set $\hbar\omega = \frac34R_y$, where $R_y = 13.6$ eV is the Rydberg energy (the value on MIT's sheet), and give the lifetime in seconds. Then give the number $\omega\tau$, the radians of oscillation in one lifetime (our addition).

<Hint>

The lifetime is one over the rate. To put in numbers, write $q^2 = e^2 = \alpha\hbar c$ and collect the energies $\hbar\omega$ and $mc^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The lifetime then holds only $\alpha$, two energies in electronvolts and $\hbar$ in eV s.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-tau" label="$\tau$ in units of $10^{-10}$ s:" value="6.65" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-tau" />

<Answer id="p1-wtau" label="$\omega\tau$ in units of $10^{7}$:" value="1.03" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-wtau" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With one way to decay, $\tau = 1/\Gamma$. In Gaussian units $e^2 = \alpha\hbar c$ turns the rate into a ratio of energies.

<Step title="The lifetime as a formula">

<Formula label="Lifetime of the 2p level of the oscillator" tex="\tau = \frac{1}{\Gamma} = \frac{3\,mc^3}{2\,e^2\omega^2} = \frac{3\,mc^2\,\hbar}{2\,\alpha\,(\hbar\omega)^2}" cite="reading1" at="text before eq. (4.5.29) for τ = 1/A; the rest is our own solution" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$\hbar\omega = \frac34 \times 13.6\ \text{eV} = 10.2$ eV, so $\omega = 10.2/(6.582120 \times 10^{-16})$ per second $= 1.550 \times 10^{16}$ per second. Then

<Formula label="The lifetime of the oscillator in numbers" tex="\tau = \frac{3 \times 510{,}999\ \text{eV} \times 6.582120 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{eV s} \times 137.036}{2 \times (10.2\ \text{eV})^2} = 6.65 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{s}" cite="codata" at="fine-structure constant, electron mass energy equivalent in MeV, reduced Planck constant in eV s" />

The rate is $\Gamma = 1.505 \times 10^{9}$ per second, and $\omega\tau = 1.550 \times 10^{16} \times 6.645 \times 10^{-10} = 1.03 \times 10^{7}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lifetime is about two thirds of a nanosecond. The electron swings through ten million radians before it has lost its energy: the level is very sharp, and treating the emission as a small perturbation was right. As a formula $\omega\tau = 3mc^2/(2\alpha\hbar\omega)$, and with $\hbar\omega = \frac38\alpha^2mc^2$ this is $4/\alpha^3$ (exactly so with the exact Rydberg; with MIT's rounded 13.6 eV the two differ in the fourth figure): the large number is a power of the inverse fine-structure constant. Problem 4 compares this lifetime with that of the real 2p state of hydrogen.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A one-dimensional model of ionization" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

An electron of mass $m$ sits in the ground state of a deep square well in one dimension:

<Formula label="The square well" tex="V(x) = 0 \ \text{ for } x < 0, \qquad V(x) = -V_0 \ \text{ for } 0 < x < a, \qquad V(x) = 0 \ \text{ for } x > a, \qquad V_0 > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

[MIT's sheet prints the third region as "$x > 0$"; it must be $x > a$.] A very deep well means

<Formula label="The condition for a deep well" tex="V_0 \gg \frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z_0^2 \equiv \frac{2ma^2V_0}{\hbar^2} \gg 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

A light wave with the electric field $E(t) = 2E_0\cos(\omega t)$ along the $x$ axis acts on the electron. It can knock the electron out of the well, a kind of ionization. Two approximations are to be made: take the ground state of the infinitely deep well of the same width as the initial state, and take $-V_0$ as its energy. Write $k$ for the wavenumber of the freed electron: $\hbar^2k^2/2m = \hbar\omega - V_0$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the density of final states $\rho(E)$ in the continuum. Use momentum eigenstates that the well does not change, in a box of length $L$ with periodic boundary conditions, and count the electrons that leave to the right and to the left together. What must hold for $\omega$ if unchanged momentum eigenstates are to be a fair approximation? (The choice box is our way of asking.)

<Hint>

In a box of length $L$ the allowed wavenumbers are $2\pi n/L$ with $n$ any integer, positive or negative. How many of them lie in a small range $dk$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Turn $dk$ into $dE$ with $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$. A given energy belongs to two wavenumbers, $+k$ and $-k$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the condition: the well is a dip of depth $V_0$ in the potential that the free electron crosses. When does a moving particle hardly notice such a dip?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the world in a box, as the lesson does in three dimensions, to make the continuum countable. In one dimension the count is shorter.

<Step title="Counting the states">

The momentum eigenstates in a box of length $L$ with periodic boundary conditions are

<Formula label="Box-normalized momentum eigenstates in one dimension" tex="u_k(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt L}\,e^{ikx}, \qquad kL = 2\pi n \quad\Rightarrow\quad dn = \frac{L}{2\pi}\,dk" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (4.3.4), (4.3.7), written for one dimension" />

With $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, a range $dE$ of energy is a range $dk = m\,dE/(\hbar^2k)$ of wavenumber. There are two such ranges, one around $+k$ (electrons leaving to the right) and one around $-k$ (to the left):

<Formula label="Density of states of a free particle in one dimension, both directions" tex="\rho(E)\,dE = 2\cdot\frac{L}{2\pi}\,dk = \frac{L}{\pi}\,\frac{m}{\hbar^2k}\,dE \quad\Rightarrow\quad \rho(E) = \frac{Lm}{\pi\hbar^2k}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution; the three-dimensional count is eq. (4.3.14) of the notes)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The condition on the frequency">

Energy conservation in the golden rule gives the freed electron the kinetic energy $\hbar^2k^2/2m = \hbar\omega - V_0$ outside the well. Over the well it sees a potential that is lower by $V_0$. A plane wave is a good description if that dip is a small disturbance, which needs a kinetic energy much larger than $V_0$:

<Formula label="Condition for unmodified momentum eigenstates" tex="\hbar\omega - V_0 \gg V_0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \hbar\omega \gg V_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

This is the same reasoning as for hydrogen in the lesson, where the photon energy had to be far above the Rydberg energy. With the deep well it also makes the electron's wavelength much shorter than the well: $k^2a^2 = 2ma^2(\hbar\omega - V_0)/\hbar^2 \gg z_0^2 \gg 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units: $m/(\hbar^2k)$ is one over (energy times length), so $\rho$ is one over an energy, a number of states per unit energy. It grows with $L$, as it must: a larger box has more states. In one dimension $\rho$ falls with $k$, while in three dimensions it grows with $k$. A remark: MIT's field is the same at every $x$, as in the lesson. That is fair as long as the wavelength of the light is much longer than the well, which bounds $\omega$ from above.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

    { expr: "4*pi^2*m*e^2*E0^2/(hbar^3*a*k^4)", hint: "The squared matrix element falls as 1/k⁴ and the density of states brings one more 1/k." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-scale" label="For $\hbar\omega$ far above $V_0$ the rate falls with the frequency as" choices={["ω to the power −1/2", "ω to the power −9/2", "ω to the power −5/2", "ω to the power −3"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Fermi's golden rule for a harmonic perturbation: the rate is $2\pi/\hbar$ times the density of final states times the squared matrix element of $H'$. The matrix element is a Fourier integral over the well.

<Step title="The golden rule and the perturbation">

<Formula label="Fermi's golden rule for absorption" tex="w = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,\rho(E_f)\,\left|\langle f|H'|i\rangle\right|^2, \qquad E_f = E_i + \hbar\omega" cite="reading1" at="eq. (4.3.54)" />

The electron has charge $-e$, so in the field $E(t)$ along $x$ its energy changes by

<Formula label="The perturbation of the electron in the well" tex="\delta H = e\,E(t)\,x = 2\,eE_0\,x\cos\omega t \equiv 2H'\cos\omega t \quad\Rightarrow\quad H' = eE_0\,x" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (4.4.17), (4.4.18), with x in place of z" />

With $E_i = -V_0$ the final energy is $E_f = \hbar\omega - V_0 = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, which is how $k$ was defined.

</Step>

<Step title="The matrix element as an integral">

With $\psi_0 = \sqrt{2/a}\,\sin(\pi x/a)$ and $u_k = e^{ikx}/\sqrt L$:

<Formula label="The matrix element as an integral over the well" tex="\langle u_k|H'|\psi_0\rangle = eE_0\sqrt{\frac{2}{aL}}\;I(k), \qquad I(k) \equiv \int_0^a x\,\sin\left(\frac{\pi x}{a}\right)e^{-ikx}\,dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The integral, exactly">

Without the factor $x$ the integral is elementary: $\int_0^a\sin(\pi x/a)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx = -(\pi/a)\left(1 + e^{-ika}\right)/\left(k^2 - \pi^2/a^2\right)$. Applying $i\,d/dk$ brings down the factor $x$:

<Formula label="The integral over the well, exact" tex="I(k) = -\frac{\pi\,e^{-ika}}{D} + \frac{2\pi i\,k\left(1 + e^{-ika}\right)}{a\,D^2}, \qquad D \equiv k^2 - \frac{\pi^2}{a^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Its squared modulus is

<Formula label="Squared modulus of the integral, exact" tex="|I(k)|^2 = \frac{\pi^2}{D^2} + \frac{4\pi^2k\sin(ka)}{a\,D^3} + \frac{16\pi^2k^2\cos^2(ka/2)}{a^2D^4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="A fast electron">

For $ka \gg 1$, $D \simeq k^2$. The second term is then smaller than the first by a factor of order $4/(ka)$ and the third by $16/(ka)^2$, so

<Formula label="The squared matrix element for a fast electron" tex="|I(k)|^2 \simeq \frac{\pi^2}{k^4} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \left|\langle u_k|H'|\psi_0\rangle\right|^2 \simeq \frac{2\pi^2e^2E_0^2}{a\,L\,k^4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The same follows without the exact integral. Integrating by parts twice, the boundary terms hold the function $f(x) = x\sin(\pi x/a)$ and its slope at the two walls. $f$ vanishes at both walls, and $f'(0) = 0$, but $f'(a) = -\pi$: that one corner gives $I \simeq -\pi e^{-ika}/k^2$. The result does not change when $k$ becomes $-k$ (the integral turns into its complex conjugate), so electrons leave to the left and to the right equally often.

</Step>

<Step title="The rate">

Both directions are counted in $\rho$ of part (a), and they have the same squared matrix element:

<Formula label="Ionization rate of the one-dimensional well" tex="w = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\cdot\frac{Lm}{\pi\hbar^2k}\cdot\frac{2\pi^2e^2E_0^2}{a\,L\,k^4} = \frac{4\pi^2\,m\,e^2E_0^2}{\hbar^3\,a\,k^5}, \qquad \hbar k = \sqrt{2m\left(\hbar\omega - V_0\right)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

For $\hbar\omega \gg V_0$, $k \simeq \sqrt{2m\omega/\hbar}$, and the rate falls as $k^{-5}$, that is as $\omega^{-5/2}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The length $L$ of the box has cancelled, as it must: the box was only a way of counting. The units: $(eE_0)^2$ is (energy/length)², and $m/\hbar^2$ is one over (energy times length²), so $m(eE_0)^2/\hbar^2$ is an energy per length⁴. Dividing by $\hbar$ and by $a\,k^5$, a length⁻⁴, leaves an energy over $\hbar$: a rate. The rate grows as the square of the field, the intensity of the light, as it should at first order. Compare hydrogen in the lesson, where the rate fell faster still, as $k^{-9}$.

One caution about the model. The plane waves $u_k$ are not orthogonal to the bound state $\psi_0$, so the matrix element of $x$ depends on where $x$ is measured from. The result above uses MIT's coordinates, with the well on $0 < x < a$ and $x$ measured from its left wall. Measured from the centre of the well, the leading term would be $\pi^2\sin^2(ka/2)/k^4$ instead of $\pi^2/k^4$. A careful treatment uses continuum states that are orthogonal to the bound state; the plane-wave model is only a rough estimate, as MIT's list of approximations makes clear.

</Step>

</Solution>

The factors $\hbar\omega_0^3/\pi^2c^3$ cancel:

$r$ is a pure number that depends on the atom only through its frequency: the dipole matrix element has cancelled. At low temperature ($k_BT \ll \hbar\omega_0$) it is huge: with almost no photons around, emission is spontaneous. At high temperature $r \simeq \hbar\omega_0/k_BT$ is small and stimulated emission wins.

**The idea.** The mode is an oscillator in contact with a heat bath. Its mean number of quanta is a ratio of two geometric series.

<Step title="The mean photon number">

With $x \equiv \hbar\omega_0/k_BT$, the probability of $n$ photons is $p_n = e^{-nx}/Z$ with $Z = \sum_n e^{-nx} = 1/(1 - e^{-x})$. The mean is

<Formula label="Mean number of photons in one mode" tex="\bar n = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} n\,p_n = -\frac{1}{Z}\,\frac{dZ}{dx} = \frac{e^{-x}}{1 - e^{-x}} = \frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega_0/k_BT} - 1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="The ratio in terms of the photon number">

Comparing with part (a):

<Formula label="The ratio is one over the photon number" tex="r = \frac{1}{\bar n} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad B_{ab}\,U(\omega_0) = \bar n\,A" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The stimulated rate is $\bar n$ times the spontaneous rate. Stimulated emission is in proportion to the number of photons already in the mode, and spontaneous emission goes at exactly the rate that one photon in the mode would stimulate. The total rate of emission is in proportion to $\bar n + 1$, while absorption is in proportion to $\bar n$. This fits the remark of the lesson that spontaneous emission can be seen as emission stimulated by the vacuum, which is always there. The two rates are equal when $\bar n = 1$.

</Step>

For $x \rightarrow 0$ (high temperature) $\bar n \rightarrow k_BT/\hbar\omega_0$: the classical equipartition energy $k_BT$ shared out in quanta of $\hbar\omega_0$. And $\bar n\,\hbar\omega_0$ times the number of modes per unit volume and frequency, $\omega^2/\pi^2c^3$, is Planck's $U(\omega)$ of part (a).

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Decays of the 2s and 2p states of hydrogen (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 4">

MIT's Problem 4 is a pointer to a textbook problem on the decays of the states of hydrogen with $n = 2$. This problem is our own, on the same subject. It goes beyond the worked example of the lesson, which treats the 2p state with $m = 0$.

Ignore spin and fine structure. The wavefunctions needed are these, with the three numbers $n$, $\ell$, $m$ of each state as subscripts and with $a_0$ the Bohr radius:

<Formula label="Hydrogen wavefunctions of the levels n = 1 and n = 2" tex="\psi_{100} = \frac{e^{-r/a_0}}{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}}, \qquad \psi_{200} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{8\pi a_0^3}}\left(1 - \frac{r}{2a_0}\right)e^{-r/2a_0}, \qquad \psi_{210} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{32\pi a_0^3}}\,\frac{r}{a_0}\,e^{-r/2a_0}\cos\theta, \qquad \psi_{21\pm1} = \mp\frac{1}{\sqrt{64\pi a_0^3}}\,\frac{r}{a_0}\,e^{-r/2a_0}\sin\theta\,e^{\pm i\phi}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2 (our own problem; standard wavefunctions, supplied by us)" />

The energies are $E_n = -R_y/n^2$, with $R_y = e^2/2a_0 = \frac12\alpha^2mc^2$ and $a_0 = \hbar/(\alpha mc)$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Which of the four states with $n = 2$ can decay to the ground state by spontaneous emission of one photon, in the electric dipole approximation of the lessons? Show directly from the wavefunctions that $\langle 100|\vec r\,|200\rangle = 0$.

<Hint>

The selection rules of the lesson say what $\ell$ must do. For the direct proof, look at what each factor of the integrand does when $\vec r$ is replaced by $-\vec r$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-which" label="By one electric-dipole photon, the ground state can be reached from" choices={["all four states alike", "the 2s state alone", "the 2p state with m = 0 alone", "the three 2p states, not 2s"]} answer={3} />

<Answer id="p4-why" label="$\langle 100|\vec r\,|200\rangle$ vanishes because" choices={["the photon would carry away no energy", "both wavefunctions are even under r → −r, and r is odd", "the node of the 2s radial function makes the radial integral cancel", "the 2s state has less energy than the 1s state"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The rate of spontaneous emission is in proportion to $|\vec d_{ab}|^2$, so a state decays only if $\vec r$ has a matrix element between it and the lower state.

<Step title="The selection rules">

<Formula label="Dipole selection rules" tex="\Delta m = 0, \pm 1, \qquad \Delta\ell = \pm 1" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (4.5.33), (4.5.34)" />

The ground state has $\ell = 0$. The 2p states have $\ell = 1$ and $m = 1, 0, -1$: both rules hold, so all three can decay. The 2s state has $\ell = 0$: $\Delta\ell = 0$, and the decay is forbidden.

</Step>

<Step title="The 2s state, directly">

$\psi_{100}$ and $\psi_{200}$ depend only on $r$, so both are unchanged when $\vec r \rightarrow -\vec r$. The vector $\vec r$ changes sign. The integrand of $\langle 100|\vec r\,|200\rangle = \int\psi_{100}\,\vec r\,\psi_{200}\,d^3r$ is therefore odd, and the integral over all space vanishes. In spherical coordinates: the $z$ component holds $\int_0^\pi\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = 0$, and the $x$ and $y$ components hold $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\phi\,d\phi = 0$ and $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin\phi\,d\phi = 0$.

It is the angles that give zero, not the radial integral: $\int_0^\infty\psi_{100}\,r\,\psi_{200}\,r^2\,dr$ is not zero (it is about $-0.044\,a_0$).

</Step>

<Step title="What becomes of 2s">

With no dipole matrix element to 1s, and no lower state other than 1s, the 2s state cannot decay at this order. (In this model 2s and 2p have the same energy, so a photon between them would have zero frequency, and the factor $\omega_{ba}^3$ in $A$ makes that rate vanish too.) As the lesson says, 2s lives long and decays by slower processes, such as the emission of two photons, which this course does not treat.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The argument used only that both states are spherically symmetric. So no two s states of any atom are connected by $\vec r$: this is the rule $\Delta\ell = \pm 1$ in its simplest case.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Step title="The pieces">

</Step>

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The rate is the atomic unit of frequency, $\alpha^2mc^2/\hbar$, times $\alpha^3$ and a number: an atom takes very many of its own periods to radiate, because $\alpha$ is small. </Step>

**The idea.** The rate is $\omega^3$ times a squared length. Compare the lengths at the same frequency, then scale both factors with $Z$.

<Formula label="Ratio of the lifetimes of hydrogen and the oscillator" tex="\frac{\tau\ \text{of hydrogen}}{\tau\ \text{of the oscillator}} = \frac{\frac43\,a_0^2}{\left(2^{15}/3^{10}\right)a_0^2} = \frac{3^{9}}{2^{13}} = 2.40" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(d) (our own problem and solution)" />

<Formula label="Lifetime of the 2p states of a one-electron ion" tex="\tau(Z) = \frac{\tau(1)}{Z^4}, \qquad \tau(2) = \frac{1.595\ \text{ns}}{16} = 99.7\ \text{ps}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(d) (our own problem and solution)" />

</Step>

The closed form of part (c) says the same: with $\alpha$ replaced by $Z\alpha$ in the energies and in $a_0$, but one factor $\alpha$ (from $e^2$ of the emitted light) left alone, $\alpha^5$ becomes $Z^4\alpha^5$. </Step>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Decay of the 3s state of hydrogen (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 5">

MIT's Problem 5 is a pointer to a textbook problem on the decays of the 3s state of hydrogen. This problem is our own, on the same subject. It uses the wavefunctions of Problem 4 and

<Formula label="Hydrogen wavefunction of the 3s state" tex="\psi_{300} = \frac{1}{81\sqrt{3\pi a_0^3}}\left(27 - \frac{18\,r}{a_0} + \frac{2\,r^2}{a_0^2}\right)e^{-r/3a_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own problem; standard wavefunction, supplied by us)" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

To which lower states can 3s decay by emitting one electric-dipole photon? What is the wavelength of that photon, in nanometres? Describe the whole route down to the ground state.

<Hint>

The lower states are 1s, 2s and the three 2p states. Apply the selection rule for $\ell$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The photon carries the difference of two levels $-R_y/n^2$. Its wavelength is $hc$ divided by its energy.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-routes" label="From 3s, one electric-dipole photon leads to" choices={["1s, 2s or 2p", "1s or 2p", "2p alone", "2s or 2p"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p5-lambda" label="Wavelength in nanometres:" value="656.1" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p5-lambda" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** An s state can only go to a p state, and the only p states below 3s are the 2p states.

<Step title="The allowed decays">

3s has $\ell = 0$. The rule $\Delta\ell = \pm 1$ forbids 1s and 2s ($\ell = 0$, by the parity argument of Problem 4(a)) and allows 2p ($\ell = 1$). Starting from $m = 0$, the rule $\Delta m = 0, \pm 1$ allows all three 2p states.

</Step>

<Step title="The photon">

<Formula label="Energy and wavelength of the photon from 3s to 2p" tex="\hbar\omega_{ba} = R_y\left(\frac14 - \frac19\right) = \frac{5}{36}\,R_y = 1.890\ \text{eV}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{hc}{\hbar\omega_{ba}} = \frac{1239.84\ \text{eV nm}}{1.890\ \text{eV}} = 656.1\ \text{nm}" cite="codata" at="fine-structure constant, electron mass energy equivalent in MeV, Planck constant, speed of light" />

This is red light: the first line of the Balmer series of hydrogen.

</Step>

<Step title="The route down">

The atom is then in a 2p state, which decays to 1s (Problem 4) with a photon of energy $\frac34R_y = 10.20$ eV, wavelength 121.5 nm, in the ultraviolet. So 3s reaches the ground state in two steps and emits two photons, one red and one ultraviolet: 3s → 2p → 1s. Their energies add up to $E_3 - E_1 = \frac89R_y$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\frac{5}{36} + \frac34 = \frac{5 + 27}{36} = \frac89$, as it must be. The value 656.1 nm is for a nucleus of infinite mass in vacuum: the real line is shifted by about 0.05%, because the proton also moves (the electron's mass is replaced by the reduced mass, smaller by one part in 1836).

</Step>

</Solution>

**The idea.** As in Problem 4: one radial integral, and angles that share the result equally among the three values of $m$.

<Step title="The radial integral">

The integrand holds $r\,e^{-r/2a_0}$ from the 2p state, $r$ from $\vec r$, $r^2$ from the volume element and the polynomial of the 3s state. In units with $a_0 = 1$ the exponent is $-\frac12r - \frac13r = -\frac56r$:

<Formula label="The radial integral for 3s to 2p" tex="\int_0^\infty r^4\left(27 - 18\,r + 2\,r^2\right)e^{-5r/6}\,dr = 27\cdot 4!\left(\frac65\right)^5 - 18\cdot 5!\left(\frac65\right)^6 + 2\cdot 6!\left(\frac65\right)^7 = \frac{27\cdot 24}{5}\left(\frac65\right)^5 = 322.5" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own problem and solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The matrix element of z">

The angles give $\int\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega = 4\pi/3$, and the prefactors are $1/\sqrt{32\pi}$ and $1/(81\sqrt{3\pi})$:

<Formula label="Matrix element of z between 2p with m = 0 and 3s" tex="\langle 210|z|300\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{32\pi}}\cdot\frac{1}{81\sqrt{3\pi}}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}\cdot\frac{27\cdot 24}{5}\left(\frac65\right)^5a_0 = \frac{2^7\,3^3\sqrt 6}{5^6}\,a_0 = 0.542\,a_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own problem and solution)" />
