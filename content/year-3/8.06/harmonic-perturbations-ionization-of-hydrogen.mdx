The last lecture found Fermi's golden rule for a perturbation that is switched on and then stays constant. This lecture lets the perturbation oscillate. Two new features appear: the perturbation can now give energy $\hbar\omega$ to the system or take it away, and so it drives **absorption** and **stimulated emission**. Yet the golden rule ends up looking just like the old one (L11.1 transcript p. 1). The second half of the lecture starts a real application, the **ionization of hydrogen** by a light wave: it sets out when the calculation can be trusted and computes the matrix element the golden rule needs. Zwiebach warns that such calculations take patience, but that the answers, once simplified, are rather nice (L11.1 transcript p. 1). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 4; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Harmonic perturbations

The Hamiltonian is a known one plus a perturbation that oscillates with angular frequency $\omega$ for a time $t_0$ (L11.1 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="A harmonic perturbation" tex="H(t) = H^{(0)} + \delta H(t), \qquad \delta H(t) = 2H'\cos\omega t \ \text{ for } 0 < t < t_0, \qquad \delta H(t) = 0 \ \text{ otherwise}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

(The notes, eqs. (4.3.41), (4.3.42), let it run on for all $t > 0$.) The operator $H'$ does not depend on time; it may depend on positions or anything else. By convention $\omega > 0$; with a cosine its sign would not matter anyway (L11.1 transcript p. 2).

Why the 2? It is pure convention: what one calls $H'$ is a choice. But writing the cosine as exponentials,

<Formula label="The cosine as two exponentials" tex="2\cos\omega t = e^{i\omega t} + e^{-i\omega t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

the 2 cancels the one half, and the golden rule that follows has exactly the same form as for a constant perturbation. Books that leave the 2 out end up with golden rules that look different depending on the case (L11.1 transcript p. 1; notes p. 96).

As in the last lecture, the system starts in a state $i$, so the zeroth-order coefficients are $c_n^{(0)} = \delta_{ni}$, and the amplitude to be found in a state $m$ at time $t_0$ is, to first order (L11.1 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="First-order transition amplitude" tex="c_m^{(1)}(t_0) = \sum_n\int_0^{t_0}dt'\,e^{i\omega_{mn}t'}\,\frac{\delta H_{mn}(t')}{i\hbar}\,c_n^{(0)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The sum collapses to $n = i$, and we call the final state $f$. Only $H'$ has matrix elements; the cosine is just a function of time. Since $H'_{fi} = \langle f|H'|i\rangle$ does not depend on time, it comes out of the integral, and the two exponentials of the cosine give two integrals (L11.1 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="The amplitude for a harmonic perturbation" tex="c_f^{(1)}(t_0) = \frac{H'_{fi}}{i\hbar}\int_0^{t_0}\left(e^{i(\omega_{fi}+\omega)t'} + e^{i(\omega_{fi}-\omega)t'}\right)dt'" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.44)" />

Each is the integral of an exponential, so (L11.1 transcript p. 3)

<Formula label="The two terms of the amplitude" tex="c_f^{(1)}(t_0) = -\frac{H'_{fi}}{\hbar}\left(\frac{e^{i(\omega_{fi}+\omega)t_0} - 1}{\omega_{fi}+\omega} + \frac{e^{i(\omega_{fi}-\omega)t_0} - 1}{\omega_{fi}-\omega}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.44)" />

with $\omega_{fi} = (E_f - E_i)/\hbar$. [The transcript reads "minus $i$" in front; the factor $1/i$ of the formula and the $1/i$ from integrating the exponential multiply to $-1$, as in the notes.]

What does it say? As $t_0 \rightarrow 0$, expanding the exponentials shows that each fraction tends to $it_0$, so the two terms are comparable. But very short times are of no interest: as with constant perturbations, $t_0$ must be large enough for the lobes to be narrow, though not so large that the probability grows too big (L11.1 transcript pp. 3–4). For such times each numerator is bounded: $e^{i\beta} - 1$, for any real $\beta$, has a magnitude between 0 and 2. A term can become large only through its **denominator**, and the interesting cases are those where a denominator nearly vanishes (L11.1 transcript p. 4; notes p. 97).

## Absorption and stimulated emission

There are two cases (L11.2 transcript p. 1; notes p. 97):

<Formula label="The two resonances" tex="\omega_{fi} + \omega \simeq 0: \ E_f \simeq E_i - \hbar\omega \ \text{(stimulated emission)}, \qquad \omega_{fi} - \omega \simeq 0: \ E_f \simeq E_i + \hbar\omega \ \text{(absorption)}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

In **stimulated emission** the system drops to a lower state and releases the energy $\hbar\omega$ to whatever produces the perturbation. It might seem like something the system would do on its own, but here it happens only because the perturbation is there. An excited atom left alone does decay, but that is a different process, **spontaneous emission**, which comes from the atom's coupling to the electromagnetic field, through which it can send out a photon; it is not part of this calculation (L11.2 transcript p. 1). In **absorption** the perturbation supplies the energy $\hbar\omega$ and lifts the system to a higher state. So a harmonic perturbation has two tricks: it can push the system up, or stimulate it to go down (L11.2 transcript p. 1).

Take absorption; stimulated emission goes the same way (L11.2 transcript p. 2). Near $E_f = E_i + \hbar\omega$ the second term is far larger than the first, in principle infinitely larger, since we will integrate over a narrow band of states around the resonance, so we drop the first. Taking half the phase out front turns what is left into a sine:

<Formula label="The absorption amplitude" tex="c_f^{(1)}(t_0) \simeq -\frac{H'_{fi}}{\hbar}\,e^{i(\omega_{fi}-\omega)t_0/2}\;\frac{2i\sin\left((\omega_{fi}-\omega)t_0/2\right)}{\omega_{fi}-\omega}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.45)" />

and its squared magnitude is the transition probability (L11.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Transition probability for absorption" tex="P_{fi}(t_0) = \frac{4\,|H'_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\sin^2\left((\omega_{fi}-\omega)t_0/2\right)}{(\omega_{fi}-\omega)^2}, \qquad E_f \simeq E_i + \hbar\omega" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.46)" />

This is the constant-perturbation result with $V$ replaced by $H'$ and $\omega_{fi}$ by $\omega_{fi} - \omega$ (notes p. 97).

## The golden rule, again

The final step is to sum over a continuum of final states with the density of states $\rho(E_f)$, exactly as before. Over the narrow band that matters, $\rho$ and $H'_{fi}$ are nearly constant and come out of the integral (L11.2 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Summing over the continuum" tex="\sum_f P_{fi}(t_0) = \int\rho(E_f)\,P_{fi}(t_0)\,dE_f = \frac{4\,|H'_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\rho(E_i + \hbar\omega)\int\frac{\sin^2\left((\omega_{fi}-\omega)t_0/2\right)}{(\omega_{fi}-\omega)^2}\,dE_f" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3" />

Zwiebach stresses that $\rho$ is evaluated at the final energy, which for absorption is $E_i + \hbar\omega$. The remaining integrand is the lobed function of the last lecture, now centred at $\omega_{fi} = \omega$ instead of $\omega_{fi} = 0$: the main contribution comes from the band (notes eqs. (4.3.48), (4.3.49))

<Formula label="The band of final states" tex="E_i + \hbar\omega - \frac{2\pi\hbar}{t_0} < E_f < E_i + \hbar\omega + \frac{2\pi\hbar}{t_0}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.49)" />

[The transcript reads that the lobes are separated by "some $\hbar$ divided by $t_0$"; the zeros are $2\pi\hbar/t_0$ apart.] It is the same integral as before, with its zero shifted: with $u = (\omega_{fi} - \omega)t_0/2$ it becomes $(\hbar t_0/2)\int\sin^2u/u^2\,du = \pi\hbar t_0/2$ (notes eqs. (4.3.50)–(4.3.52), which integrate over $\omega_{fi}$ rather than $E_f$). [The transcript reads that the integral gives "$\hbar$ linear in $t_0$ over $2\pi$"; it is $\pi\hbar t_0/2$, as in the last lecture.] One $\hbar$ cancels, and the probability grows linearly in time (L11.2 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Linear growth" tex="\sum_f P_{fi}(t_0) = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,\rho(E_i + \hbar\omega)\,|H'_{fi}|^2\,t_0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.53)" />

Dividing by the elapsed time gives the transition rate, and with it **Fermi's golden rule for harmonic perturbations** (L11.2 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Fermi's golden rule (harmonic perturbation)" tex="w = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,\rho(E_f)\,\left|\langle f|H'|i\rangle\right|^2, \qquad E_f = E_i \pm \hbar\omega" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.54) and the text below it" />

with the plus sign for absorption and the minus sign for stimulated emission. Here $H'$ is defined by $\delta H = 2H'\cos\omega t$, and $\omega$ is the frequency of the perturbation (L11.2 transcript p. 4). [The notes, p. 97 and p. 99, say "spontaneous emission" where they mean stimulated emission.] As before, $w$ is a **probability of transition per unit time**: multiply it by the number of atoms, a billion say, and by the elapsed time, and you know about how many have made the transition (L11.2 transcript p. 4). This is where the atomic transitions of the coming lectures start.

## Ionizing hydrogen: when can we trust the calculation?

Now the example: a hydrogen atom in its ground state, hit by an electromagnetic wave. The wave's oscillating electric field can kick the electron out. One thinks of it in terms of photons of energy $\hbar\omega$, although the treatment here has no photons in it: the field is classical (L11.3 transcript p. 1). Energy conservation fixes the ejected electron's wavenumber $k$: the first $R_y$ of the photon's energy lifts the electron to zero energy, the rest is kinetic (L11.3 transcript p. 1; notes eqs. (4.4.1), (4.4.2)):

<Formula label="Energy of the ejected electron" tex="E_e = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} = \hbar\omega - R_y" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.4.2)" />

Here $R_y$, the Rydberg, about 13.6 eV, is the magnitude of the ground-state energy, the depth of the well. It can be written in several ways, with $a_0$ the Bohr radius and $\alpha$ the fine-structure constant (L11.3 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The Rydberg" tex="2R_y = \frac{e^2}{a_0} = \frac{\hbar^2}{ma_0^2} = \alpha\,\frac{\hbar c}{a_0}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{e^2}{\hbar c}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.4.3); α = e²/ħc on L11.3 transcript p. 2" />

Half the work of such a calculation, Zwiebach says, is understanding when it is valid (L11.3 transcript p. 1). There are two conditions (L11.3 transcript pp. 2–3):

**1. A uniform field across the atom.** A wave's field varies in space. If its wavelength were comparable to the atom, different parts of the atom would feel different fields at the same instant. For the perturbation to be simple, the field must be the same everywhere in the atom, rising and falling in time only, and that needs $\lambda \gg a_0$. In terms of the photon energy (notes eqs. (4.4.4)–(4.4.6)):

<Formula label="Wavelength and photon energy" tex="\frac{\lambda}{a_0} = \frac{2\pi c}{\omega\,a_0} = \frac{2\pi\hbar c}{\hbar\omega\,a_0} = \frac{4\pi}{\alpha}\,\frac{R_y}{\hbar\omega} \gg 1" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.4.5), (4.4.6)" />

using $\hbar c/a_0 = 2R_y/\alpha$. Since $4\pi/\alpha \approx 1722$, the photon energy must satisfy $\hbar\omega \ll 1722\,R_y$, about 23 keV: a photon whose wavelength equals the Bohr radius has about 23 keV, a nice thing to know (L11.3 transcript p. 2; notes eq. (4.4.7)). [The transcript reads "about 1,772"; $4\pi/\alpha$ is 1722, as in the notes, and the 23 keV quoted fits 1722.]

**2. A free electron in the final state.** The photon must also be energetic enough. With just a little more than $R_y$, the electron leaves with almost no energy and feels the proton's Coulomb attraction strongly; the true continuum states of hydrogen are complicated, Coulomb-distorted waves. To use plain momentum eigenstates, the ejected electron's energy must be much larger than $R_y$, so that it hardly notices the proton (L11.3 transcript p. 3; notes eq. (4.4.8)). Together:

<Formula label="The window of validity" tex="R_y \ll \hbar\omega \ll \frac{4\pi}{\alpha}\,R_y" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.4.9)" />

Taking "much less" to mean a factor of 10, the photon energy $\hbar\omega$ should lie roughly between 140 eV and 2.3 keV (10 times 13.6 eV, and a tenth of 23 keV) (L11.3 transcript p. 3; notes eq. (4.4.10)). Doing better is possible, and has been done, but takes much more work. Across this window the electron's wavenumber, from the energy formula above (notes eqs. (4.4.11), (4.4.12)),

<Formula label="The electron's wavenumber" tex="k^2a_0^2 = \frac{\hbar\omega}{R_y} - 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad ka_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega}{R_y} - 1}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.4.12)" />

lies between about 3 and 13 (L11.3 transcript p. 3, left as an exercise; notes eq. (4.4.13)). Finally, the electron is **non-relativistic** even at the top of the window: its energy, at most about 2.3 keV, is far below the electron's rest energy, 511 keV, so there is no need for the Dirac equation (L11.3 transcript p. 4; notes p. 100).

## The perturbation, and the initial and final states

The electron couples to the electric potential $\Phi$: its potential energy is $-e\Phi$. Align the field with the z axis and write its peak value as $2E_0$, by the same convention as the 2 in $2H'$ (L11.3 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The field and its potential" tex="\delta H = -e\Phi, \qquad \vec E(t) = 2E_0\cos\omega t\;\hat z, \qquad \Phi = -E(t)\,z" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.4.14)–(4.4.16)" />

Minus the gradient of $\Phi$ gives back the field. With $z = r\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec r$ and the field (L11.3 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The perturbation for ionization" tex="\delta H = eE(t)\,z = 2eE_0\,r\cos\theta\cos\omega t \equiv 2H'\cos\omega t \quad\Rightarrow\quad H' = eE_0\,r\cos\theta" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.4.17), (4.4.18)" />

The initial state is the hydrogen ground state; the final state is a plane wave normalized in a box of side $L$, as in the last lecture: the box is back. Squared, the plane wave's phase drops out and leaves $1/L^3$, which integrates to 1 over the box (L11.3 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="Initial and final states" tex="\psi_0(r) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}}\,e^{-r/a_0}, \qquad u_k(\vec x) = \frac{1}{L^{3/2}}\,e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.4.19), (4.4.20)" />

## The matrix element

The golden rule needs $\langle f|H'|i\rangle$. First, the geometry: is there a physical angle in the problem? There is one: the angle between the field (the photon's polarization) and the direction in which the electron comes out (L11.4 transcript p. 1). So it pays to put the **electron's momentum** $\vec k$ along the z axis, not the field. Then (L11.4 transcript pp. 1–2; notes Figure on p. 101):

- the field $\vec E$ points along the angles $(\theta, \phi)$, so $\theta$ is now the angle between $\vec E$ and $\vec k$, the one that will survive in the answer;
- the integration variable $\vec r$ has angles $(\theta', \phi')$;
- $\gamma$ is the angle between $\vec E$ and $\vec r$, so that $H' = eE_0\,r\cos\gamma$. (The notes call it $\theta''$.)

Putting the final state first, as a bra, its plane wave is conjugated, and with $\vec k\cdot\vec r = kr\cos\theta'$ (L11.4 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="The matrix element as an integral" tex="\langle f|H'|i\rangle = \frac{eE_0}{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3L^3}}\int r^2dr\,\sin\theta'd\theta'\,d\phi'\;e^{-ikr\cos\theta'}\;r\cos\gamma\;e^{-r/a_0}" cite="reading4" at="pp. 2–3" />

[On L11.4 p. 2 the ground state and the plane wave are written without their square roots; the constant pulled out on p. 3, $eE_0/\sqrt{\pi L^3a_0^3}$, has both. The notes' eqs. (4.4.21) and (4.4.24) show $+ikr\cos\theta'$ in the exponent but continue with $-ikr\cos\theta'$ from eq. (4.4.25), as the conjugated plane wave requires.]

Everything here is simple except $\cos\gamma$, which depends on all four angles; even a numerical integration would find it a headache (L11.4 transcript p. 3). The way out is that $\cos\gamma$ is the dot product of the unit vectors along $\vec E$ and $\vec r$. Writing each as $(\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\theta)$ and collecting terms (L11.4 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="The angle between field and position" tex="\cos\gamma = \sin\theta\sin\theta'\cos(\phi - \phi') + \cos\theta\cos\theta'" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.4.22)" />

Now the nice thing: nothing else in the integrand depends on $\phi'$, and the first term integrates to zero over a full period (L11.4 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The first term drops out" tex="\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi'\,\cos(\phi - \phi') = 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.4.23)" />

So only $\cos\theta\cos\theta'$ survives. $\cos\theta$ is a constant for the integral, the $\phi'$ integral gives $2\pi$, the extra $r$ from $H'$ makes $r^2$ into $r^3$, and $\cos\theta'$ is a good variable of integration (L11.4 transcript pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="The reduced matrix element" tex="\langle f|H'|i\rangle = \frac{eE_0}{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3L^3}}\,2\pi\cos\theta\int_0^\infty r^3e^{-r/a_0}\,dr\int_{-1}^{1}d(\cos\theta')\,\cos\theta'\,e^{-ikr\cos\theta'}" cite="reading4" at="pp. 4–5" />

The hard part, orienting oneself, is done. The lecture only quotes the result and leaves the two integrals to the notes: first the radial one, then the angular one, with $x = \cos\theta'$ (L11.4 transcript p. 5; notes eqs. (4.4.25), (4.4.26)):

<Formula label="The two integrals" tex="\int_0^\infty r^3e^{-r/a_0}e^{-ikrx}\,dr = \frac{6\,a_0^4}{(1 + ika_0x)^4}, \qquad \int_{-1}^{1}\frac{x\,dx}{(1 + ika_0x)^4} = -\frac{8i}{3}\,\frac{ka_0}{(1 + k^2a_0^2)^3}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.4.25), (4.4.26)" />

Multiplying $2\pi \times 6 \times (-8i/3) = -32\pi i$ and tidying up (L11.4 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="The ionization matrix element" tex="\langle f|H'|i\rangle = -32i\sqrt{\pi}\,(eE_0a_0)\,\frac{ka_0^4}{\sqrt{a_0^3L^3}}\,\frac{\cos\theta}{(1 + k^2a_0^2)^3}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.4.27)" />

[The L11.4 transcript reads "$i$ 32 square root of pi", without the minus sign; the notes, and the integrals above, give $-i$. The overall phase does not matter for the rate, which needs only the squared magnitude.] Zwiebach checks the units. $ka_0$ has none, so neither does $1 + k^2a_0^2$; $ka_0^4$ is a length cubed, like $\sqrt{a_0^3L^3}$; and $eE_0a_0$, a charge times a field times a distance, is an energy, as the matrix element of a Hamiltonian between normalized states must be (L11.4 transcript p. 5; notes p. 102). The factor $\cos\theta$ says the electron prefers to leave along the field, and never leaves at right angles to it. What remains, for next time, is to square this, multiply by the density of states, and simplify: the golden rule has already done the integration over final states (L11.4 transcript p. 5).

<WorkedExample title="A 500 eV photon on hydrogen">
<Problem>
Light with photon energy $\hbar\omega = 500$ eV falls on hydrogen atoms in their ground state. Use $R_y = 13.6$ eV, $a_0 = 0.0529$ nm, $\hbar c = 197.3$ eV nm, $1/\alpha = 137.0$ and the electron rest energy $mc^2 = 511$ keV. (a) Is the photon inside the window of validity? (b) Find the kinetic energy of the ejected electron and $ka_0$. (c) Compare the photon's wavelength with $a_0$. (d) Is the electron non-relativistic? (e) If the photon energy is doubled to 1000 eV, by what factor does the $k$-dependent part of $|\langle f|H'|i\rangle|^2$ change? (f) How does the matrix element at $\theta = 60^\circ$ compare with that along the field?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The window">
$\hbar\omega/R_y = 500/13.6 \approx 36.8$: well above 10, and far below $1722/10$. So 500 eV sits comfortably between 140 eV and 2.3 keV.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The electron">
Its kinetic energy is $\hbar\omega - R_y = 500 - 13.6 = 486.4$ eV, and $ka_0 = \sqrt{36.76 - 1} \approx 5.98$, inside the range 3 to 13.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The wavelength">
$\lambda = 2\pi\hbar c/\hbar\omega = 2\pi \times 197.3/500 \approx 2.48$ nm, so $\lambda/a_0 \approx 47$. The same number follows from $(4\pi/\alpha)(R_y/\hbar\omega) = 1722 \times 13.6/500$. Across the atom the field is very nearly uniform.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Non-relativistic">
$v/c = \sqrt{2E_e/mc^2} = \sqrt{2 \times 486.4/511000} \approx 0.044$: the electron moves at about 4.4% of the speed of light.
</Step>
<Step title="(e) A higher photon energy">
The matrix element depends on $k$ through $ka_0/(1 + k^2a_0^2)^3$ (times constants). At 500 eV this is $5.98/36.76^3 \approx 1.20 \times 10^{-4}$. At 1000 eV, $k^2a_0^2 = 1000/13.6 - 1 \approx 72.5$ and $ka_0 \approx 8.52$, giving $8.52/73.53^3 \approx 2.14 \times 10^{-5}$: the matrix element falls by a factor of about 0.178, and its square by about 0.032. For $ka_0 \gg 1$ the factor goes like $(ka_0)^{-5}$, that is like $(\hbar\omega)^{-5/2}$, and $2^{-5/2} \approx 0.177$. Photons far above threshold find the electron hard to eject.
</Step>
<Step title="(f) The angle">
The matrix element is proportional to $\cos\theta$, so at $60^\circ$ it is half its value along the field, and its square a quarter. At $90^\circ$ it vanishes.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Photoionization in the early universe">
The process set up here, a photon ejecting the electron from hydrogen, together with its reverse, recombination, decided when the universe became transparent. In 8.286, Guth explains that the hydrogen stayed ionized until the universe cooled to about 4,000 K, and that the radiation decoupled at about 3,000 K. At 4,000 K, kT is only about 0.34 eV, some 40 times less than the 13.6 eV needed to ionize hydrogen; the photons doing the ionizing are the rare ones in the high-energy tail of the black-body spectrum, and because photons far outnumber atoms, there are enough of them to keep hydrogen ionized down to temperatures that look surprisingly low. (This explanation, and any ionization rate, go beyond what Guth discusses.) Note that those photons are just above threshold, outside this lecture's window $\hbar\omega \gg R_y$: a careful calculation of cosmological recombination needs the Coulomb-distorted final states that this lecture deliberately avoids.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A harmonic perturbation is written $\delta H = 2H'\cos\omega t$, with $\omega > 0$ and $H'$ time independent; the 2 is a convention that keeps the golden rule in its familiar form.
- Its first-order amplitude has two terms, with denominators $\omega_{fi} + \omega$ and $\omega_{fi} - \omega$. For times that are not too short only a nearly vanishing denominator matters: $E_f \simeq E_i - \hbar\omega$ is **stimulated emission**, $E_f \simeq E_i + \hbar\omega$ is **absorption**. Spontaneous emission is a different process, due to the coupling to the electromagnetic field.
- For absorption, $P_{fi} = 4|H'_{fi}|^2\sin^2((\omega_{fi} - \omega)t_0/2)/(\hbar^2(\omega_{fi} - \omega)^2)$; summed over a continuum it grows linearly in time, from final states within about $2\pi\hbar/t_0$ of $E_i + \hbar\omega$.
- **Fermi's golden rule** for harmonic perturbations: $w = (2\pi/\hbar)\rho(E_f)|\langle f|H'|i\rangle|^2$ with $E_f = E_i \pm \hbar\omega$.
- Ionization of hydrogen from the ground state, treated with a uniform field and a plane-wave electron, needs $R_y \ll \hbar\omega \ll (4\pi/\alpha)R_y$: about 140 eV to 2.3 keV, where $ka_0$ runs from about 3 to 13 and the electron is non-relativistic.
- The field $2E_0\cos\omega t\,\hat z$ gives $H' = eE_0r\cos\theta$. With $\vec k$ along z, the $\phi'$ integral removes the awkward part of $\cos\gamma$, and $\langle f|H'|i\rangle = -32i\sqrt{\pi}(eE_0a_0)\,ka_0^4\cos\theta/(\sqrt{a_0^3L^3}(1 + k^2a_0^2)^3)$, an energy, largest for electrons ejected along the field. The rate itself is the next lecture's.
