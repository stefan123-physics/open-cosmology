Hydrogen was solved in 8.04 and 8.05, so why again? Zwiebach's answer: it is the harmonic oscillator of atomic physics, the system on which every approximation method gets tested, and its many degeneracies make it the natural ground for degenerate perturbation theory (L4.1 transcript p. 1). This lecture prepares the ground: it reviews the scales and the spectrum, sets up the two bases that include the electron's spin, and then uses the Pauli and Dirac equations to find which small terms must be added to the familiar Hamiltonian (L4.1–L4.4 transcripts; notes sections 2.1–2.3). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 2; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. As in the notes, formulas are in Gaussian units, where the Coulomb energy is $e^2/r$.)

## Scales

The known, zeroth-order Hamiltonian is (L4.1 transcript p. 1)

<Formula label="Hydrogen's Hamiltonian" tex="H^{(0)} = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} - \frac{e^2}{r}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.1.1)" />

where $m$ is really the reduced mass of the electron and proton, close enough to the electron's mass to use that. For a nucleus with $Z$ protons, $e^2$ becomes $Ze^2$. The length scale is the Bohr radius, built from $\hbar$, $m$ and $e^2$ alone, without the speed of light (L4.1 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Bohr radius" tex="a_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{me^2} \approx 53\ \text{pm}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.1.2)" />

The energies depend only on the principal quantum number $n = 1, 2, \dots$:

<Formula label="Energy levels" tex="E_n = -\frac{e^2}{2a_0}\,\frac{1}{n^2}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.1.3)" />

about $-13.6$ eV for the ground state. Although the speed of light plays no part in $H^{(0)}$, it helps to bring it in (L4.1 transcript pp. 2–3). With the fine-structure constant

<Formula label="Fine-structure constant" tex="\alpha = \frac{e^2}{\hbar c} \approx \frac{1}{137}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.1.4)" />

the energy unit becomes $e^2/a_0 = me^4/\hbar^2 = \alpha^2mc^2$, so

<Formula label="Energy levels in terms of the rest energy" tex="E_n = -\frac12\,\alpha^2mc^2\,\frac{1}{n^2}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (2.1.5), (2.1.6)" />

The binding energies are a factor $\alpha^2 \approx 1/19{,}000$ below the electron's rest energy. (This also warns against reading the $Z$-dependence too quickly: $a_0$ itself contains $e^2$, so the energies go like $e^4$, not $e^2$.) The typical momentum tells the same story:

<Formula label="Typical momentum" tex="p \sim \frac{\hbar}{a_0} = \frac{me^2}{\hbar} = \alpha\,mc" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.1.7)" />

so the electron moves at about $\alpha c$, or $c/137$: slow enough for a non-relativistic start, fast enough that relativity will show up in the details.

## The spectrum and its degeneracy

For each $n$, the orbital angular momentum $l$ runs from 0 to $n - 1$, and one relation organizes everything (L4.1 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Principal quantum number" tex="n = N + l + 1" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.1.9)" />

Here $N \ge 0$ is the degree of the polynomial in $r$ in the wavefunction, which is also its number of radial nodes. Each $l$ multiplet has $2l + 1$ values of $m$, so the level $n$ holds

<Formula label="Degeneracy of level n" tex="\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l + 1) = n^2" cite="reading5" at="after eq. (2.1.9)" />

states, a sum of consecutive odd numbers. The letters S, P, D, F name $l = 0, 1, 2, 3$. The wavefunction shows all three quantum numbers at a glance (L4.1 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="Hydrogen wavefunctions" tex="\psi_{n,l,m}(\vec x) = A\left(\frac{r}{a_0}\right)^{l}\times\left[\text{polynomial in } \frac{r}{a_0} \text{ of degree } N\right]\times e^{-r/(na_0)}\,Y_{l,m}(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.1.11)" />

$n$ in the exponential, $l$ in the leading power and the spherical harmonic, $m$ in the spherical harmonic alone. The normalized ground state is $\psi_{1,0,0} = e^{-r/a_0}/\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}$ (notes eq. (2.1.12)). In a semiclassical picture the states of one level are orbits with the same semi-major axis but different eccentricity, $l = 0$ the most eccentric and $l = n - 1$ the most circular (L4.1 transcript pp. 5–6). Finally, the electron's spin doubles everything: $H^{(0)}$ does not depend on spin, so each level holds $2n^2$ degenerate states (L4.1 transcript p. 6). The corrections of the next lectures, from relativity and from spin, will break much of this degeneracy.

## Two bases that include spin

The **uncoupled basis** lists the orbital and spin labels separately (L4.2 transcript p. 1). Since the electron's spin is always $s = \frac12$, only $m_s = \pm\frac12$ needs recording:

<Formula label="Uncoupled basis" tex="(n,\ l,\ m_l,\ m_s)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.1.14)" />

The **coupled basis** uses the total angular momentum $\vec J = \vec L + \vec S$ (L4.2 transcript pp. 2–3). Tensoring an $l$ multiplet with the spin doublet gives two $j$ multiplets,

<Formula label="Adding spin one-half" tex="l \otimes \tfrac12 = \left(j = l + \tfrac12\right) \oplus \left(j = l - \tfrac12\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.1.17)" />

or only $j = \frac12$ for $l = 0$. All states of the tensor product still have the same $\vec L^2$ and $\vec S^2$ eigenvalues, so $l$ survives as a good quantum number; what is lost is $m_l$ and $m_s$ separately. The coupled labels are therefore

<Formula label="Coupled basis" tex="(n,\ l,\ j,\ m_j)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.1.16)" />

and the multiplets are named $nL_j$ (notes eq. (2.1.20)): the $n = 2$ level, for example, holds $2S_{1/2}$, $2P_{1/2}$ and $2P_{3/2}$, with $2 + 2 + 4 = 8 = 2n^2$ states (L4.2 transcript pp. 3–4). The coupled basis will turn out to be the good basis for the fine-structure perturbations.

## The Pauli equation and g = 2

Classically, a charge $q$ of mass $m$ circulating with angular momentum $\vec L$ has the magnetic moment $\frac{q}{2mc}\vec L$ (notes eq. (2.2.1)). For an elementary particle's spin one inserts a fudge factor $g$, to be measured (L4.3 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Spin magnetic moment" tex="\vec\mu = g\,\frac{q}{2mc}\,\vec S" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.2.2)" />

For the electron, experiment says $g = 2$, so with $q = -e$ and $\vec S = \frac{\hbar}{2}\vec\sigma$, its coupling to a magnetic field is

<Formula label="Electron in a magnetic field" tex="H = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B = \frac{e\hbar}{2mc}\,\vec\sigma\cdot\vec B" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (2.2.3), (2.2.5)" />

Why 2? Pauli's equation comes close to explaining it (L4.3 transcript pp. 2–4). An electron's wavefunction is a two-component **Pauli spinor**, so the free Hamiltonian $\hat p^2/2m$ secretly carries a 2 × 2 identity matrix. The Pauli matrices obey, for any vectors $\vec a$ and $\vec b$,

<Formula label="Pauli's identity" tex="(\vec\sigma\cdot\vec a)(\vec\sigma\cdot\vec b) = (\vec a\cdot\vec b)\,I + i\,\vec\sigma\cdot(\vec a\times\vec b)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.2.9)" />

(the anticommutator of the Pauli matrices gives the first term, the commutator the second). Since $\vec p\times\vec p = 0$, the free Hamiltonian can equally be written

<Formula label="Free Hamiltonian, rewritten" tex="H = \frac{1}{2m}(\vec\sigma\cdot\vec p)(\vec\sigma\cdot\vec p)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.2.11)" />

Nothing has changed yet. But coupling to electromagnetism means replacing the momentum by $\vec\pi = \vec p - q\vec A/c$, where the vector potential depends on position and so is an operator too (L4.3 transcript p. 3). Now the components of $\vec\pi$ do **not** commute: their commutators produce derivatives of $\vec A$, antisymmetrized, which is its curl (L4.3 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The kinetic momentum crossed with itself" tex="\vec\pi\times\vec\pi = i\,\frac{\hbar q}{c}\,\vec B" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.2.19)" />

(reminiscent of $\vec L\times\vec L = i\hbar\vec L$). The cross term of Pauli's identity therefore survives, and with $q = -e$ and the scalar potential $\Phi$ the **Pauli Hamiltonian** is

<Formula label="Pauli Hamiltonian" tex="H = \frac{1}{2m}\left(\vec p + \frac{e}{c}\vec A\right)^2 + \frac{e\hbar}{2mc}\,\vec\sigma\cdot\vec B - e\,\Phi(\vec x)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (2.2.13), (2.2.20)" />

Its second term is exactly the coupling of a magnetic moment with $g = 2$, without ever putting the 2 in by hand.

## The Dirac equation and hydrogen's corrections

Relativity starts from $E^2 - p^2c^2 = m^2c^4$ (L4.4 transcript p. 1). Taking $\sqrt{p^2c^2 + m^2c^4}$ as a Hamiltonian is hopeless as it stands (its Schrödinger equation would contain every power of the momentum), but for slow particles its expansion is instructive:

<Formula label="Expanding the relativistic energy" tex="\sqrt{\hat p^2c^2 + m^2c^4} = mc^2 + \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} - \frac{\hat p^4}{8m^3c^2} + \dots" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.3.4)" />

The rest energy, the familiar kinetic energy, and then a first relativistic correction. Dirac wanted instead a Hamiltonian **linear** in the momentum whose square is $p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$ (L4.4 transcript pp. 1–2). Squaring $c(\alpha_1\hat p_1 + \alpha_2\hat p_2 + \alpha_3\hat p_3) + \beta mc^2$ gives the right answer only if

<Formula label="Dirac's conditions" tex="\alpha_1^2 = \alpha_2^2 = \alpha_3^2 = \beta^2 = 1, \qquad \alpha_i\alpha_j + \alpha_j\alpha_i = 0\ \ (i \neq j), \qquad \alpha_i\beta + \beta\alpha_i = 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.3.6)" />

Numbers that anticommute would have to vanish, so the $\alpha_i$ and $\beta$ must be matrices; the simplest choice is 4 × 4, built from the Pauli matrices (L4.4 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Dirac matrices" tex="\alpha_i = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \sigma_i \\ \sigma_i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \beta = \begin{pmatrix} I & 0 \\ 0 & -I \end{pmatrix}, \qquad H = c\,\alpha_i\hat p_i + \beta mc^2" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (2.3.7), (2.3.8)" />

(summed over $i$). The wavefunction is now a four-component Dirac spinor, made of two Pauli spinors: one behaves like Pauli's, the other is small. Coupling to the proton's potential $V(r) = -e^2/r$ and eliminating the small spinor order by order (a derivation the lecture does not do; Zwiebach points to a textbook and possibly a recitation) gives the Hamiltonian that acts on the Pauli spinor (L4.4 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Hydrogen's Hamiltonian with its fine-structure terms" tex="H = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} + V - \frac{\hat p^4}{8m^3c^2} + \frac{1}{2m^2c^2}\,\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}\,\vec S\cdot\vec L + \frac{\hbar^2}{8m^2c^2}\,\nabla^2V" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.3.14)" />

The first two terms are $H^{(0)}$; the other three are the **relativistic**, **spin–orbit** and **Darwin** corrections. The first is the one the expansion already showed. The second is **spin–orbit coupling**; heuristic arguments for it come out off by a factor of 2, a factor tied to Thomas precession, while the Dirac equation gets it right directly (L4.4 transcript pp. 3–4). (The heuristic value is twice too large.) The third is the **Darwin term**: "not the biologist", as Zwiebach puts it; it is named after the physicist Charles Galton Darwin.

How big are they? With $p \sim \alpha mc$, $r \sim a_0$ and $\vec S\cdot\vec L \sim \hbar^2$, each is of order (notes eqs. (2.3.15)–(2.3.20))

<Formula label="Size of the fine-structure terms" tex="\delta H \sim \alpha^4mc^2" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (2.3.15), (2.3.18), (2.3.20)" />

a factor $\alpha^2 \approx 1/19{,}000$ below the levels themselves. So for hydrogen, $\alpha^2$ plays the part of the perturbation parameter $\lambda$, although nobody can turn it down to zero. That small splitting is the **fine structure**, the subject of the next lecture.

<WorkedExample title="Fine structure grows like Z⁴">
<Problem>
Take a hydrogen-like ion: one electron bound to a nucleus with $Z$ protons, so $e^2 \to Ze^2$ in $H^{(0)}$. Using $\alpha = 1/137.036$ and $mc^2 = 511.0$ keV, (a) find how $a_0$, $E_n$ and the electron's speed scale with $Z$; (b) find $E_2$ and the fine-structure scale for helium's ion He⁺ ($Z = 2$) and compare with hydrogen.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Scaling with Z">
Every $e^2$ becomes $Ze^2$, so $\alpha \to Z\alpha$ wherever $\alpha$ came from $e^2$: the Bohr radius becomes $a_0/Z$, the energies $E_n = -\frac12(Z\alpha)^2mc^2/n^2$ grow like $Z^2$, and the speed $\sim Z\alpha c$ grows like $Z$. Repeating the estimates of the fine-structure terms with $p \sim Z\alpha mc$ and $r \sim a_0/Z$ gives $\delta H \sim (Z\alpha)^4mc^2$: a factor $Z^4$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) He⁺ against hydrogen">
For He⁺, $E_2 = -\frac12(2\alpha)^2mc^2/4 = -\frac12\alpha^2mc^2 \approx -13.6$ eV: its $n = 2$ level sits exactly where hydrogen's ground state does. The fine-structure scale is $(2\alpha)^4mc^2 \approx 0.0232$ eV, against $\alpha^4mc^2 \approx 0.00145$ eV for hydrogen: 16 times larger. Relative to the level it corrects, the fine structure is $(Z\alpha)^2 \approx 2.1 \times 10^{-4}$ for He⁺, four times hydrogen's $5.3 \times 10^{-5}$. The electron moves at about $Z\alpha c \approx 0.015c$. For heavy atoms, with inner electrons near $Z\alpha c$ and $Z$ near 80, relativity is no longer a small correction.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Hydrogen's scale and the cosmic background">
The scale $\frac12\alpha^2mc^2 = 13.6$ eV is hydrogen's binding energy. In the early universe, photons energetic enough to supply it kept the hydrogen ionized. Photons outnumber atoms by more than a billion to one (8.902 takes a baryon-to-photon ratio of about $5 \times 10^{-10}$), so even when the average photon was far too weak, the rare energetic ones were still plentiful enough. Recombination therefore waited until the radiation had cooled to about 4,000 K, in Guth's figure from 8.286, far below the 158,000 K at which $kT$ equals 13.6 eV. Soon after, the universe became transparent, and the cosmic microwave background is the light set free then.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Hydrogen's levels are $E_n = -\frac12\alpha^2mc^2/n^2$, a factor $\alpha^2$ below the electron's rest energy; $a_0 = \hbar^2/(me^2) \approx 53$ pm and the electron moves at about $\alpha c$.
- Level $n$ holds $n^2$ orbital states ($n = N + l + 1$), $2n^2$ with spin. The uncoupled basis is $(n, l, m_l, m_s)$; the coupled basis $(n, l, j, m_j)$, with $j = l \pm \frac12$, names multiplets $nL_j$.
- Writing the kinetic energy as $(\vec\sigma\cdot\vec\pi)^2/2m$ gives the Pauli Hamiltonian, whose $\frac{e\hbar}{2mc}\vec\sigma\cdot\vec B$ term predicts $g = 2$.
- Dirac's linear Hamiltonian $c\,\alpha_i\hat p_i + \beta mc^2$ needs 4 × 4 matrices. Reduced to the Pauli spinor, it adds the relativistic, spin–orbit and Darwin terms to $H^{(0)}$, all of order $\alpha^4mc^2$: the fine structure.
