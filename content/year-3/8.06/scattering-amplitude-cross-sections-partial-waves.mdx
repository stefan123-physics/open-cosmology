With this lecture the course reaches its last approximation method before identical particles: **scattering**. A beam of particles is sent at a target, and what comes out, in which directions and how often, tells us about the forces at work. The lecture sets up elastic scattering with energy eigenstates, defines the **scattering amplitude** and the **cross section**, and begins the method of **partial waves** by writing a plane wave as a sum of spherical waves (L19.1–L19.4 transcripts; notes sections 7.1 and 7.2, pp. 133–138). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 7; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Why scatter particles

Physicists want to know the interactions between particles. Some are well understood, such as the electromagnetic force between the proton and the electron in hydrogen; others, especially at high energies, are complicated and poorly known. Sometimes a potential, say one that traps particles, cannot be measured directly at all. In every case one can send particles in and see how they are deflected: the pattern of scattered particles reveals the potential (L19.1 transcript p. 1). The typical setup is a **beam**, a **target** and **detectors** in all directions, since particles can be deflected at any angle, even backwards.

Collisions can be intricate, because particles can change identity (L19.1 transcript pp. 1–2; notes p. 133). Two protons can produce a neutral pion, p + p → p + p + π⁰, or a proton, a neutron and a positive pion, p + p → p + n + π⁺; an electron and a positron can annihilate into a pair of muons, e⁺ + e⁻ → μ⁺ + μ⁻. Charge is conserved in each, but the number of particles is not, which is one reason such reactions need quantum field theory, Zwiebach notes. In general a collision is a reaction a + b → c + d + e + ⋯. The word **scattering** is kept for collisions in which the particles keep their identity, a + b → a + b (L19.1 transcript p. 2).

## Elastic scattering and its assumptions

Even then much can happen, so the course asks for more: **elastic** scattering, in which any internal states of the particles are unchanged (L19.1 transcript pp. 2–3; notes p. 134). Internal states exist when a particle is itself a bound state, such as an atom. The famous inelastic example is the experiment of Franck and Hertz in 1914: electrons shot through mercury vapour lost energy in fixed, quantized amounts by exciting the mercury atoms, the first direct evidence that atoms have energy levels, a year after Bohr's theory of the atom (1913) (L19.1 transcript p. 3; notes p. 134, footnote 1). [The transcript and the notes spell the name "Frank"; the physicist is James Franck.]

The assumptions for the rest of the chapter (L19.1 transcript pp. 3–4; notes p. 134):

- **No spin.** Spin would not change the ideas, only add degrees of freedom and algebra.
- **Nonrelativistic** motion, as everywhere in the course.
- The interaction depends only on the **difference of positions**, $V(\vec r_1 - \vec r_2)$. As for the hydrogen atom, the centre-of-mass motion then separates, and what remains is **one particle scattering off a fixed potential** $V(\vec r)$, with the reduced mass. For two identical particles of mass $M$ the reduced mass is $M/2$.
- **Energy eigenstates**, not wave packets. Strictly, Zwiebach says, one should use wave packets, since energy eigenstates cannot be normalized and do not describe particles directly; but what wave packets do can mostly be read off from energy eigenstates, as in the one-dimensional barrier problems of 8.04.

## The equation to solve

With $m$ the (reduced) mass, the Hamiltonian, an energy eigenstate and its Schrödinger equation are (L19.2 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Energy eigenstates of the scattering problem" tex="H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V(\vec r), \qquad \Psi(\vec r, t) = \psi(\vec r)\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}, \qquad \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V(\vec r)\right)\psi(\vec r) = E\,\psi(\vec r)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (7.1.1)–(7.1.3), p. 134" />

The potential need not be central yet. (The notes write $M$ for the mass in eqs. (7.1.1)–(7.1.4) and $m$ from eq. (7.1.11) on; the lecture uses $m$ throughout, as here.) The potential is assumed to have **finite range** $a$: it vanishes for $r > a$ (L19.2 transcript pp. 1–2). This is common: the potential of a neutral atom is zero outside it. A potential that falls off fast enough also works, but the Coulomb potential does not fall fast enough (L19.2 transcript p. 1; notes p. 134, figure 7.1). We look at positive energies and write the energy as that of a free particle far away, $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, which turns the equation into (L19.2 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="The Schrödinger equation with E = ħ²k²/2m" tex="\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\nabla^2 + k^2\right) + V(\vec r)\right)\psi(\vec r) = 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.1.4), p. 134" />

This equation has infinitely many solutions for each $k$, and most of them are not what an experiment prepares (L19.2 transcript pp. 1–2). In one dimension the same issue was settled by physics: send in a wave from the left, and the barrier produces a reflected and a transmitted wave; write those with unknown coefficients and solve. The incoming wave is the given input, and the reflected and transmitted waves together are the **scattered wave**. Three dimensions need the same kind of ansatz.

## The incident wave

With no potential, every plane wave $e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}$ with $\vec k\cdot\vec k = k^2$ solves the equation (L19.2 transcript p. 2; notes p. 135). [The transcript at this point says that plane waves are solutions "if the potential is not zero"; the intended statement, as the next sentences and the notes make clear, is that they are solutions where the potential vanishes.] Take the beam along $+\hat z$:

<Formula label="The incident wave" tex="\psi_{in}(\vec r) = e^{ikz}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.1.5), p. 135" />

Zwiebach calls it φ (phi), a different letter from ψ to stress that it is not a full solution; this page writes $\psi_{in}$, keeping $\phi$ for the azimuthal angle. It solves the equation wherever $V = 0$, so for every $r > a$, whatever $z$; inside the range of the potential it does not (L19.2 transcript p. 2). Just as in one dimension, there must be more.

## An outgoing spherical wave

Particles are scattered in all directions, so the extra wave should be a **spherical wave moving away** from the origin (L19.2 transcript p. 3). With the time factor $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, the phase $kr - Et/\hbar$ is constant on spheres that grow, so $e^{ikr}$ moves outwards. But it does not solve the free equation away from the potential: it fails badly (L19.2 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="e^{ikr} is not a solution" tex="\left(\nabla^2 + k^2\right)e^{ikr} \neq 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.1.6), p. 135" />

[The lecture first asks whether the Laplacian of $e^{ikr}$ alone vanishes, then corrects itself: the test to apply is (7.1.6), with the $k^2$, which $e^{ikr}$ fails.]

The reason is physical. As the wave spreads out it must weaken, so that the probability flowing out through a sphere of radius one kilometre also flows out through the sphere of two kilometres; otherwise probability would pile up between them (L19.2 transcript p. 3). The probability through a sphere is $|\psi|^2$ times its area $4\pi r^2$, so the wave must fall off like $1/r$. And indeed (L19.2 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The outgoing spherical wave" tex="\left(\nabla^2 + k^2\right)\frac{e^{ikr}}{r} = 0 \qquad (r \neq 0)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.1.7), p. 135" />

At $r = 0$ the Laplacian produces a delta function, but the origin is not where the outgoing wave is needed. A quick check uses the Laplacian of a function of $r$ alone, which Zwiebach recalls on the board (L19.2 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Laplacian of a radial function" tex="\nabla^2 g(r) = \frac{1}{r}\,\frac{d^2}{dr^2}\big(r\,g(r)\big)" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

For $g = e^{ikr}/r$, $rg = e^{ikr}$, and two derivatives give $-k^2e^{ikr}$, which cancels against $k^2$. For $g = e^{ikr}$ the same rule leaves $2ik\,e^{ikr}/r$ over. The notes put it this way: $e^{ikr}$ is a solution $u_{El}(r)$ of the radial equation (for $l = 0$) where the potential vanishes, and the full radial solution is $u_{El}(r)/r$ (notes p. 135).

The spherical wave need not be the same in every direction: more particles may go one way than another. So it gets an angular factor (L19.2 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The scattered wave" tex="\psi_s(\vec r) = f(\theta, \phi)\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.1.8), p. 135" />

With the angular factor the scattered wave no longer solves the equation exactly, since the Laplacian also acts on $\theta$ and $\phi$, but it is the **leading term** of the solution for $r \gg a$, which is where the detectors are (L19.2 transcript p. 4; notes p. 135). The energy eigenstate we want therefore looks, far away, like

<Formula label="The scattering wavefunction far from the target" tex="\psi(\vec r) = \psi_{in}(\vec r) + \psi_s(\vec r) \simeq e^{ikz} + f(\theta, \phi)\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}, \qquad r \gg a" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.1.9), p. 135" />

This is the most important equation of the setup, Zwiebach says. In one dimension the incident, reflected and transmitted waves were exact solutions to the left and right of the barrier; in three dimensions we can only ask for the leading form far away (L19.2 transcript p. 4). Finding solutions will eventually require the region near the potential too. The function $f(\theta, \phi)$, which depends on $V$ and on the energy (the notes give it a subscript $k$), is the **scattering amplitude**: solving a scattering problem means finding it (L19.2 transcript p. 4; L19.3 transcript p. 1).

## The differential cross section

What does $f$ tell an experimenter? It gives the **cross section** (L19.3 transcript p. 1). A detector at angle $\theta$ covering a small solid angle counts so many particles per second. Ask: how large an area of the incoming beam carries that many particles per second? That area is the **differential cross section** $d\sigma$ for the solid angle $d\Omega$ at $(\theta, \phi)$ (L19.3 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Definition of the differential cross section" tex="d\sigma = \frac{\text{particles scattered per unit time into } d\Omega \text{ about } (\theta, \phi)}{\text{flux of incident particles, in particles per unit area per unit time}}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.1.10), p. 136" />

The units check (L19.3 transcript p. 2): particles are pure numbers, so the numerator is one over time, the denominator one over area times time, and the ratio is an **area**. In the notes' words, $d\sigma$ is the area that removes from the incident beam the particles to be scattered into $d\Omega$ (notes p. 136): it says how big the target looks to the particles coming in. A smaller solid angle collects fewer particles, which is why $d\sigma$ is called differential.

Energy eigenstates cannot be normalized, so "number of particles" needs a convention (L19.3 transcript p. 2). Read $|\psi|^2$ as particles per unit volume: where $|\psi|^2 = 1$, there is one particle in each unit volume. Zwiebach admits this is a little funny, but it gives the right answer. (Any other constant factor in $\psi$ would cancel between numerator and denominator, as part (d) of the worked example below shows.)

## The cross section from the amplitude

The denominator is the probability current of the incident wave (L19.3 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="The incident flux" tex="\vec J_{in} = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\psi_{in}^*\,\nabla\psi_{in}\right) = \frac{\hbar k}{m}\,\hat z" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.1.11), p. 136" />

The gradient of $e^{ikz}$ brings down $ik\,\hat z$, and the imaginary part leaves $k$. Intuitively, it is a density times a velocity: $|e^{ikz}|^2 = 1$ times $v = p/m = \hbar k/m$ (L19.3 transcript p. 3). [In the lecture this gradient is first called a Laplacian, then corrected.]

For the numerator, take a small box at distance $r$ in the direction $(\theta, \phi)$, with cross-section $r^2\,d\Omega$ and thickness $dr$ (L19.3 transcript p. 3). The number of particles in it is the scattered wave's density times its volume (L19.3 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Particles in the little box" tex="dn = |\psi_s|^2\,d^3r = \left|f(\theta, \phi)\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\right|^2 r^2\,d\Omega\,dr = |f(\theta, \phi)|^2\,d\Omega\,dr" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.1.12), p. 136" />

The $r^2$ of the volume cancels the $1/r^2$ of the density, as the flux argument required. Moving at speed $v = \hbar k/m$, all these particles leave the box in the time $dt = dr/v$, so the number scattered into $d\Omega$ per unit time is (L19.3 transcript pp. 3–4; notes p. 136)

<Formula label="Particles per unit time into dΩ" tex="\frac{dn}{dt} = \frac{|f(\theta, \phi)|^2\,d\Omega\,dr}{dr/v} = \frac{\hbar k}{m}\,|f(\theta, \phi)|^2\,d\Omega" cite="reading5" at="p. 136" />

Dividing by the flux $\hbar k/m$, everything cancels except the amplitude (L19.3 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Differential cross section" tex="\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta, \phi)|^2" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.1.13), p. 136" />

Zwiebach prefers to read it as $d\sigma = |f|^2\,d\Omega$, a small area attached to a small solid angle: a ratio of differentials more than a derivative with respect to solid angle (L19.3 transcript p. 4). Integrating over all directions gives the **total cross section** (L19.3 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Total cross section" tex="\sigma = \int d\sigma = \int |f(\theta, \phi)|^2\,d\Omega" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.1.14), p. 136" />

So the amplitude, which captures the effect of the potential, determines a measurable quantity: the cross section measures the probability of interaction (L19.3 transcript p. 4). Designing an accelerator experiment, one needs the cross section to know how many events the detectors will see with the available beam intensity; Zwiebach illustrates this with Higgs bosons at the LHC (L19.3 transcript p. 4). [His figures, a few a day at first and a few hundred later, are rough classroom numbers for events seen; far more Higgs bosons were produced, and only a small fraction is cleanly detected.]

## Central potentials and the free particle

From now on the potential is **central**, $V(\vec r) = V(r)$ (L19.4 transcript p. 1; notes p. 137). The incoming beam still picks out the $z$ axis, so the scattered wave can depend on the angle $\theta$ from that axis, but by symmetry not on $\phi$: the amplitude becomes $f(\theta)$.

The method of partial waves needs the **free particle in spherical coordinates** (L19.4 transcript p. 1). Plane waves are simple, but scattering happens in spherical coordinates, and far from the target the particle is free, so there is no escaping it. For $V = 0$ (L19.4 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Free particle in spherical coordinates" tex="V = 0, \qquad E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}, \qquad \psi(\vec r) = \frac{u_{El}(r)}{r}\,Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.2.1), p. 137" />

The radial function depends on the energy and on $l$, not on $m$. The radial equation has the centrifugal barrier and no potential (L19.4 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The free radial equation" tex="-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{d^2u_{El}}{dr^2} + \frac{\hbar^2\,l(l+1)}{2mr^2}\,u_{El} = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\,u_{El}" cite="reading4" at="p. 2" />

Cancelling $\hbar^2/2m$ (L19.4 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The free radial equation, simplified" tex="\left(-\frac{d^2}{dr^2} + \frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}\right)u_{El}(r) = k^2\,u_{El}(r)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.2.2), p. 137" />

It looks like an eigenvalue problem, so one might expect the energy to be quantized. It is not: with $\rho = kr$ the equation becomes (L19.4 transcript p. 2)

<Formula label="The radial equation in ρ = kr" tex="\left(-\frac{d^2}{d\rho^2} + \frac{l(l+1)}{\rho^2}\right)u_{El}(\rho) = u_{El}(\rho)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.2.3), p. 137" />

and $k$ has disappeared: a solution for one $k$ is a solution for all. That fits a free particle, whose states are not normalizable and whose energies form a continuum (L19.4 transcript p. 2).

## Spherical Bessel and Neumann functions

The two independent solutions are spherical Bessel functions (L19.4 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Free radial solutions" tex="u_{El}(\rho) = A_l\,\rho\,j_l(\rho) + B_l\,\rho\,n_l(\rho)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.2.4), p. 137" />

with $j_l$ the **spherical Bessel function** and $n_l$ the **spherical Neumann function**. Since $\psi$ divides $u$ by $r$, the radial part of $\psi$ is simply $j_l(kr)$ or $n_l(kr)$, with nothing else (L19.4 transcript p. 2). The function $j_l$ is regular at the origin; $n_l$ is singular there. Far away both are well behaved (L19.4 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Far from the origin" tex="\rho\,j_l(\rho) \to \sin\left(\rho - \frac{l\pi}{2}\right), \qquad \rho\,n_l(\rho) \to -\cos\left(\rho - \frac{l\pi}{2}\right) \qquad (\rho \gg 1)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (7.2.5)–(7.2.6), p. 137" />

## The plane wave as a sum of partial waves

Now write the incident wave in spherical waves (L19.4 transcript p. 3). The plane wave $e^{ikz}$ solves the free equation, so it must be a superposition of the free solutions just found, each divided by $r$ and times a spherical harmonic:

<Formula label="A general free solution" tex="\sum_{l,m}\left(a_{lm}\,j_l(kr) + b_{lm}\,n_l(kr)\right)Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading4" at="p. 3" />

That is far more than needed (L19.4 transcript p. 3). The plane wave is finite everywhere, while every $n_l$ diverges at the origin: all $b_{lm} = 0$. And $e^{ikz} = e^{ikr\cos\theta}$ does not change under rotations about the $z$ axis, while every $Y_{lm}$ with $m \neq 0$ depends on $\phi$: only $m = 0$ survives. So (L19.4 transcript p. 3)

<Formula label="Only regular, axially symmetric waves" tex="e^{ikz} = e^{ikr\cos\theta} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}a_l\,j_l(kr)\,Y_{l0}(\theta)" cite="reading4" at="p. 3" />

(The notes write the same sum with Legendre polynomials $P_l(\cos\theta)$, which differ from $Y_{l0}$ by constants; notes eq. (7.2.7), p. 137.) Any choice of the $a_l$ gives an exact free solution, since each term is one; to represent the plane wave they must take particular values (L19.4 transcript pp. 3–4). The notes give the two facts that fix them (notes p. 137):

<Formula label="Two facts that fix the coefficients" tex="Y_{l0}(\theta) = \sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}}\,P_l(\cos\theta), \qquad j_l(x) = \frac{1}{2i^l}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{ixu}\,P_l(u)\,du" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.2.8), p. 137" />

The lecture states the result without proof, as a famous formula perhaps for recitation (L19.4 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Partial-wave expansion of a plane wave" tex="e^{ikz} = \sqrt{4\pi}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{2l+1}\;i^l\,Y_{l0}(\theta)\,j_l(kr)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (7.2.9), p. 138" />

(Using the first fact, $\sqrt{4\pi}\sqrt{2l+1}\,Y_{l0} = (2l+1)P_l(\cos\theta)$, so the coefficients of the notes' sum are $a_l = (2l+1)\,i^l$ when it is written with $P_l$. The integral is then the projection of $e^{ixu}$ onto $P_l$, using $\int_{-1}^{1}P_l^2\,du = 2/(2l+1)$.) The coefficients grow fast, Zwiebach remarks. Each term, a definite $l$, is a **partial wave**, and each is an exact solution when $V = 0$ (notes p. 138). The notes call it an incredible relation: a plane wave built from spherical waves of every angular momentum.

Where are the waves? Far away $j_l(kr)$ is a sine divided by $kr$, and a sine is a difference of exponentials, $\sin x = (e^{ix} - e^{-ix})/2i$ (L19.4 transcript p. 4). So each partial wave contains an **outgoing** spherical wave $e^{ikr}/r$ and an **incoming** one $e^{-ikr}/r$: a plane wave moving along $z$ is a sum of incoming and outgoing spherical waves. The lecture ends here; the next one writes this large-$r$ form out and uses it to compute the scattering amplitude, the incoming waves being the same with or without the potential (L19.4 transcript p. 4; notes eqs. (7.2.10)–(7.2.11), p. 138, and p. 139).

<WorkedExample title="Counting scattered neutrons">
<Problem>
Fast neutrons, of a few MeV, scatter elastically off the nuclei of a thin foil. Suppose that at this energy the scattering amplitude has magnitude $|f(\theta)| = f_0\,(1 + c\cos\theta)$, with $f_0 = 5$ fm and $c = 0.4$, the same for every nucleus. Use 1 barn = 1 b = $10^{-28}$ m² = 100 fm². (a) Find $d\sigma/d\Omega$ at $\theta = 0$, $90°$ and $180°$, in barns per steradian. (b) Find the total cross section, and the fraction of the scattered neutrons that go into the forward hemisphere, $\theta < 90°$. (c) A beam of $10^{8}$ neutrons per second crosses the foil, which has $10^{22}$ nuclei per cm². How many neutrons per second reach a detector covering 0.01 sr at $\theta = 90°$, and what fraction of the beam is scattered at all? (d) The real beam does not have one neutron per unit volume but $|C|^2$, with wavefunction $C\,e^{ikz}$ far ahead of the foil. Does that change the cross sections?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The angular distribution">
$d\sigma/d\Omega = |f|^2 = f_0^2(1 + c\cos\theta)^2$ with $f_0^2 = 25$ fm² = 0.25 b. At $\theta = 0$: $0.25 \times 1.4^2 = 0.49$ b/sr. At $90°$: $0.25$ b/sr. At $180°$: $0.25 \times 0.6^2 = 0.09$ b/sr. Forward scattering is more than five times as likely per unit solid angle as backward scattering.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Total cross section and forward fraction">
With $u = \cos\theta$ and $d\Omega = 2\pi\,du$ (nothing depends on $\phi$), $\sigma = 2\pi f_0^2\int_{-1}^{1}(1 + cu)^2\,du = 2\pi f_0^2\left(2 + \frac{2c^2}{3}\right) = 4\pi f_0^2\left(1 + \frac{c^2}{3}\right)$. Numerically $4\pi \times 25 \times 1.0533$ fm², about 330.9 fm², or 3.31 b. The forward hemisphere is $0 < u < 1$: $2\pi f_0^2\left(1 + c + \frac{c^2}{3}\right)$, and dividing by $\sigma$ gives $1.4533/2.1067$, about 0.690. So 69% of the scattered neutrons go forwards.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Counting rates">
Each nucleus removes from the beam the neutrons crossing its area $d\sigma$, so with $N$ nuclei per unit area the fraction of the beam scattered into $\Delta\Omega$ is $N\,(d\sigma/d\Omega)\,\Delta\Omega$ (for a thin foil, where nuclei do not hide behind one another). With $d\sigma/d\Omega = 25$ fm² $= 2.5 \times 10^{-25}$ cm² at $90°$, the rate is $10^{8} \times 10^{22} \times 2.5 \times 10^{-25} \times 0.01$, which is 2500 neutrons per second. The fraction scattered in any direction is $N\sigma = 10^{22} \times 3.309 \times 10^{-24}$, about 0.033: some $3.3 \times 10^{6}$ neutrons per second, 3.3% of the beam. The foil is indeed thin.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Normalization">
No. The scattered wave is then $C$ times the one used above too, since the Schrödinger equation is linear, so both the incident flux and the number scattered per unit time are multiplied by $|C|^2$, and their ratio $d\sigma$ is unchanged. This is why the "one particle per unit volume" reading of $|e^{ikz}|^2 = 1$ is harmless.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Cross sections in the early universe">
The cross section is the quantity that turns a density of targets into a rate: a particle moving at speed $v$ through targets of number density $n_t$ scatters about $n_t\sigma v$ times per second, and travels about $1/(n_t\sigma)$ between collisions. In 8.286, photons in the early universe kept scattering off free electrons until the electrons were bound into atoms; after that the typical photon never scattered again, and the CMB is the picture of where it last did. The cross section for that process is the Thomson cross section, $\sigma_T \approx 6.65 \times 10^{-29}$ m², about 0.665 b: $8\pi/3$ times the square of the classical electron radius (CODATA). In 8.902, the abundance of dark matter left over from the hot early universe is set by comparing an annihilation rate per particle, the density of partners times $\langle\sigma v\rangle$, with the expansion rate. Thomson scattering involves photons and annihilation needs relativistic quantum field theory, both beyond this chapter's spinless nonrelativistic setting, but the cross section in both is an effective area in exactly the sense defined here.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Elastic scattering**: the particles keep their identity and internal states. With no spin, nonrelativistic motion and an interaction $V(\vec r_1 - \vec r_2)$, it reduces to one particle of reduced mass $m$ scattering off a fixed potential $V(\vec r)$ of finite range $a$, studied with energy eigenstates, $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$.
- Far from the target, $\psi \simeq e^{ikz} + f(\theta, \phi)\,e^{ikr}/r$: an incident plane wave plus an outgoing spherical wave. The $1/r$ makes $e^{ikr}/r$ a free solution away from the origin and keeps the outgoing probability per second the same through every sphere. $f$ is the **scattering amplitude**.
- The **differential cross section** $d\sigma$, particles scattered per unit time into $d\Omega$ divided by the incident flux, is an area. With the incident flux $\hbar k/m$ and $dn/dt = (\hbar k/m)|f|^2\,d\Omega$, $d\sigma/d\Omega = |f|^2$, and the **total cross section** is $\sigma = \int|f|^2\,d\Omega$.
- For a central potential $f = f(\theta)$. The free radial equation, with $\rho = kr$, contains no energy; its solutions are $\rho\,j_l(\rho)$ (regular) and $\rho\,n_l(\rho)$ (singular), with $\rho\,j_l \to \sin(\rho - l\pi/2)$ and $\rho\,n_l \to -\cos(\rho - l\pi/2)$ far away.
- The plane wave is a sum of **partial waves**: $e^{ikz} = \sqrt{4\pi}\sum_l\sqrt{2l+1}\,i^l\,Y_{l0}(\theta)\,j_l(kr)$, only regular functions and only $m = 0$. Far away each partial wave is an incoming plus an outgoing spherical wave, the starting point for phase shifts in the next lecture.
