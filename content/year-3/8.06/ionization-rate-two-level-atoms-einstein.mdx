Last lecture set up the ionization of hydrogen by a light wave and computed the matrix element between the ground state and an outgoing plane wave. This lecture finishes the job: the golden rule turns that matrix element into a **rate**, first per solid angle and then in total, and a little rearranging puts it in the form atomic physicists like (L12.1 transcript pp. 1–3). Then a new subject begins, still within time-dependent perturbation theory: **light and atoms**. A collection of atoms with two levels bathes in thermal radiation; absorption and stimulated emission explain the laser, and **Einstein's argument** shows that a third process, spontaneous emission, must exist, and fixes its rate in terms of the others. The lecture ends by writing the coupling of an atom to a light wave as a **dipole** interaction, ready for next time (L12.2 transcript p. 1; L12.5 transcript pp. 1–3). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in five short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 4, sections 4.4 and 4.5; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## The rate per solid angle

A recap of the set-up (L12.1 transcript p. 1). The atom starts in its ground state and ends with the electron in a plane wave of wavenumber $k$, normalized in a box of side $L$. The field is $2E_0\cos\omega t$, so $E_0$ is half its peak value, and $\theta$ is the angle between the field (the light's polarization) and the electron's momentum $\hbar\vec k$. The photon is energetic enough to make the electron nearly free, $\hbar\omega$ around 10 Rydbergs or more, but its wavelength must stay much longer than the atom, so that the field is uniform across it. [The transcript reads "smaller, or much smaller than the atom"; the condition of the last lecture is $\lambda \gg a_0$, notes eq. (4.4.5).] Last lecture's result, squared (notes eq. (4.4.28)):

<Formula label="The squared matrix element" tex="|H'_{fi}|^2 = 1024\pi\,(eE_0a_0)^2\,\frac{k^2a_0^5}{L^3}\,\frac{\cos^2\theta}{(1 + k^2a_0^2)^6}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.4.28)" />

The other ingredient is the density of states of free particles with energy $E_e$ whose momentum points into a small solid angle $d\Omega$, computed a couple of lectures ago (L12.1 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Density of plane-wave states" tex="\rho(E_e) = \frac{L^3}{8\pi^3}\,\frac{m}{\hbar^2}\,k\,d\Omega" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.4.29)" />

Fermi's golden rule, $w = (2\pi/\hbar)\rho(E_f)|H'_{fi}|^2$, now gives a small rate $dw$ into the solid angle $d\Omega$, because $\rho$ carries the $d\Omega$ (L12.1 transcript p. 2). The box size $L^3$ cancels, and after collecting constants (L12.1 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Ionization rate per solid angle" tex="\frac{dw}{d\Omega} = \frac{256}{\pi}\,\frac{ma_0^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{(eE_0a_0)^2}{\hbar}\,\frac{k^3a_0^3}{(1 + k^2a_0^2)^6}\,\cos^2\theta" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.4.31)" />

Zwiebach grouped the constants so that the units are visible: $eE_0a_0$ is an energy, so $(eE_0a_0)^2/\hbar$ is an energy times a frequency, and the factor in front of it, $ma_0^2/\hbar^2 = 1/(2R_y)$, is one over an energy. So $dw/d\Omega$ is one over a time, as a rate must be (L12.1 transcript p. 2; notes p. 103). The physics is in the $\cos^2\theta$: the electron is ejected **preferentially along the field**, forwards or backwards, and never at right angles to it. It is as if the oscillating field shook the electron out along its own direction (L12.1 transcript p. 2).

## The total rate

The total rate integrates over all directions. Integrating $\cos^2\theta$ over the sphere is quick (L12.1 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Averaging cos² over the sphere" tex="\int\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega = \frac{1}{3}\cdot 4\pi" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.4.32)" />

[The transcript first reads that the integral "is $4\pi$", then that it is "multiplied by 1/3"; the integral is $4\pi/3$.] A way to remember it: on a globe, $\cos^2\theta$ is large only near the poles, a small area, while $\sin^2\theta$ is large over the whole wide equatorial belt, so the average of $\cos^2\theta$ over the sphere is $1/3$ and that of $\sin^2\theta$ is $2/3$ (L12.1 transcript p. 2). With $\hbar^2/ma_0^2 = 2R_y$ the total rate is

<Formula label="Total ionization rate" tex="w = \int d\Omega\,\frac{dw}{d\Omega} = \frac{512}{3}\,\frac{(eE_0a_0)^2}{\hbar R_y}\,\frac{k^3a_0^3}{(1 + k^2a_0^2)^6}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.4.33)" />

A formula can promise more than one should trust. In the window of validity $ka_0$ runs from about 3 to 13, so $(ka_0)^2$ is at least 9, and the 1 next to it is not to be trusted: other approximations give answers without it, and some books show that (L12.1 transcript pp. 2–3; notes p. 103). Dropping it,

<Formula label="Ionization rate for ka₀ much larger than 1" tex="w = \frac{512}{3}\,\frac{(eE_0a_0)^2}{\hbar R_y}\,\frac{1}{(ka_0)^9}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.4.34)" />

a rather high power, which comes from $12 - 3$: the denominator behaves as $(ka_0)^{12}$ and the numerator has $(ka_0)^3$ (L12.1 transcript p. 3).

## The rate in atomic units

Atomic physicists write the same result in a form that shows the scales at once (L12.1 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Ionization rate in atomic units" tex="w = \frac{256}{3}\left(\frac{E_p}{E_*}\right)^2\frac{1}{t_*}\,\frac{1}{(ka_0)^9}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.4.35)" />

Here $E_p = 2E_0$ is the **peak** electric field of the wave. $E_*$ is the **atomic electric field**, the field the proton produces at the distance of the Bohr radius, and $t_*$ is the **atomic time**, the time the electron takes to cross a Bohr radius at its typical speed $v \approx \alpha c$ (in hydrogen the electron's $v/c$ is about the fine-structure constant, $1/137$) (L12.1 transcript p. 3; notes eq. (4.4.36)):

<Formula label="Atomic field and atomic time" tex="E_* = \frac{e}{a_0^2} = \frac{2R_y}{ea_0}, \qquad t_* = \frac{a_0}{\alpha c} = \frac{\hbar}{2R_y}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.4.36); L12.1 transcript p. 3 for ħ/2R_y" />

Numerically $E_* \approx 5.14 \times 10^{11}$ V/m and $t_* \approx 2.42 \times 10^{-17}$ s. [The transcript reads that $t_*$ is "two Rydbergs over $h$ bar"; it is $\hbar$ over two Rydbergs, which is what gives $2.42 \times 10^{-17}$ s.] The ratio $E_p/E_*$ compares the field of your laser with the field that holds the atom together, a meaningful comparison (L12.1 transcript p. 3).

Where did the photon's frequency go? It is hidden in $k$: the photon's energy frees the electron and the rest becomes kinetic energy, so roughly $k \propto \sqrt{\omega}$ (L12.1 transcript p. 3). Zwiebach closes the topic by calling it a precursor of the calculations of quantum field theory: matrix elements and Feynman diagrams take hours of work, yet the final answer often simplifies to something reasonable (L12.1 transcript pp. 3–4).

## Two-level atoms and light

The next subject is still time-dependent perturbation theory, but the light is no longer a controlled wave sent at one atom. Now a **collection of atoms** sits in **thermal radiation** at temperature $T$: black-body photons, unpolarized, coming from all directions (L12.2 transcript p. 1). Each atom is simplified to two levels, $a$ below and $b$ above, with

<Formula label="The transition frequency" tex="\omega_{ba} = \frac{E_b - E_a}{\hbar} > 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

[The notes, p. 104, call the same quantity $\omega_{ab}$ and mix the two names from p. 106 on, with $\omega_{ba}$ in the equations; this page follows the lecture.] Photons of frequency $\omega_{ba}$ can do two things, both known from harmonic perturbations (L12.2 transcript pp. 1–2; notes pp. 104–105):

- **Absorption.** The electron is in $a$; a photon of energy $\hbar\omega_{ba}$ arrives; afterwards the electron is in $b$ and the photon is gone.
- **Stimulated emission.** The electron is in $b$; a photon arrives; afterwards the electron is in $a$ and there are **two** photons.

In first-order perturbation theory the probability to go up equals the probability to come down. Why should an atom need a photon to go down, when going down seems natural? Because an atom in an energy eigenstate stays there forever unless something couples to it; here the radiation itself stimulates the transition, hence the name (L12.2 transcript p. 2).

This is the principle of the **laser**: Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation (L12.2 transcript p. 2). It needs something unnatural. In thermal equilibrium most atoms sit in the lower level, so one must create a **population inversion**, more atoms in $b$ than equilibrium allows. A common way is **optical pumping**: light lifts the atoms from $a$ to a third, higher level, the pump level; from there they drop quickly into $b$, which lives a long time, so they accumulate (L12.2 transcript p. 3). Then one photon stimulates one atom to emit, giving two photons; these stimulate two atoms, giving four; then eight, and so on: amplification. (Zwiebach adds that in good lasers the quick drop from the pump level often gives its energy to vibrations rather than light.) What follows makes this quantitative (L12.2 transcript p. 3).

## Einstein's argument: three facts

Now take the equilibrium seriously. A box holds a few billion atoms, $N_a$ of them in level $a$ and $N_b$ in level $b$, together with photons at temperature $T$; write $\beta = 1/k_BT$ (L12.3 transcript p. 1). Physicists at the start of the twentieth century pictured two processes, absorption and **spontaneous emission**, the decay an excited atom undergoes on its own; stimulated emission was not known, and Zwiebach credits Einstein with discovering it. Our 8.06 instinct goes the other way: we know absorption and stimulated emission and would try those. We will see that they cannot reach equilibrium, and that adding spontaneous emission fixes it. Computing spontaneous emission directly needs the quantized electromagnetic field, about two lectures of work that 8.06 does not do; the beauty of Einstein's argument is that it gives the spontaneous rate in terms of the stimulated one, by thermodynamics (L12.3 transcript pp. 1–2).

**Fact 1: the populations are in equilibrium.** Transitions never stop, but the numbers in each level stay the same (L12.3 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Equilibrium populations" tex="\frac{dN_a}{dt} = \frac{dN_b}{dt} = 0" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.1)" />

**Fact 2: the equilibrium is thermal**, so the populations follow the Boltzmann factor (L12.3 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Boltzmann ratio of the populations" tex="\frac{N_b}{N_a} = \frac{e^{-\beta E_b}}{e^{-\beta E_a}} = e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_{ba}}, \qquad \beta = \frac{1}{k_BT}" cite="reading3" at="p. 2" />

[The notes' eq. (4.5.2) writes $N_a/N_b$ on the left; the right-hand side is $N_b/N_a$, as in the lecture.]

**Fact 3: the radiation is a black body.** Its energy per unit volume in the frequency range $d\omega$, known from Planck's work, is (L12.3 transcript p. 2; L12.4 transcript p. 1 for the constant)

<Formula label="Planck's spectrum" tex="U(\omega)\,d\omega = \frac{\hbar}{\pi^2c^3}\,\frac{\omega^3\,d\omega}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.3)" />

Dividing by $\hbar\omega$, it is essentially a count of the photons of frequency near $\omega$ per unit volume, the ones available to cause transitions (L12.3 transcript p. 2).

## Absorption and stimulated emission are not enough

Each process gets a rate, the number of transitions per unit time in the box (L12.3 transcript p. 3; notes p. 106). Absorption, $a \rightarrow b$, needs atoms in $a$, so its rate is proportional to $N_a$; it needs photons at the right frequency, so it is proportional to $U(\omega_{ba})$; and the remaining factor, a property of the atom that perturbation theory can compute, Einstein called $B_{ab}$. Stimulated emission, $b \rightarrow a$, likewise has a rate proportional to $N_b$, to $U(\omega_{ba})$ and to a coefficient $B_{ba}$:

<Formula label="Rates of absorption and stimulated emission" tex="\text{absorption: } B_{ab}\,U(\omega_{ba})\,N_a, \qquad \text{stimulated emission: } B_{ba}\,U(\omega_{ba})\,N_b" cite="reading3" at="p. 3" />

The population of $b$ grows by absorption and shrinks by stimulated emission (L12.3 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Rate of change of the upper population" tex="\frac{dN_b}{dt} = \text{rate of absorption} - \text{rate of stimulated emission}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.4)" />

Setting it to zero, factoring out $N_a$ and using Fact 2 for $N_b/N_a$ (L12.3 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="The failed equilibrium condition" tex="0 = N_a\left(B_{ab} - B_{ba}\,e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_{ba}}\right)U(\omega_{ba})" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.5)" />

This cannot work. Equilibrium must be possible at every temperature, so the bracket would have to vanish for every $T$. But $B_{ab}$ and $B_{ba}$ do not depend on the temperature: they say how likely a transition is once a photon and an atom are there, and like the matrix element computed for ionization, they depend only on the atom's states and the overlaps of their wavefunctions. The temperature enters through the numbers of photons and atoms, not through the $B$'s. A temperature-dependent exponential cannot be cancelled by constants: **a process is missing** (L12.3 transcript p. 4).

## Spontaneous emission and the A and B coefficients

The missing process is **spontaneous emission**, $b \rightarrow a$ with a photon released. It needs an atom in $b$, so its rate is proportional to $N_b$, but it does not need photons, so no factor $U$: its rate is $AN_b$, with a coefficient Einstein called $A$; hence the name Einstein's $A$ and $B$ coefficients (L12.3 transcript p. 4; notes p. 107). The equilibrium condition becomes (L12.3 transcript p. 5)

<Formula label="Equilibrium with spontaneous emission" tex="0 = \frac{dN_b}{dt} = -A\,N_b - B_{ba}\,U(\omega_{ba})\,N_b + B_{ab}\,U(\omega_{ba})\,N_a" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.6)" />

Fact 3 has not been used yet. This time the equation does not force $U$ to vanish: it can be **solved for** $U$ and compared with Planck (L12.4 transcript p. 1). Dividing by $N_b$, grouping the terms with $U$, factoring out $B_{ab}$ and using $N_a/N_b = e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{ba}}$:

<Formula label="The spectrum that equilibrium requires" tex="U(\omega_{ba}) = \frac{A}{B_{ab}}\,\frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{ba}} - B_{ba}/B_{ab}}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.6)" />

Planck's formula at the same frequency, without the $d\omega$, is (L12.4 transcript p. 1)

<Formula label="Planck's spectrum at the transition frequency" tex="U(\omega_{ba}) = \frac{\hbar\,\omega_{ba}^3}{\pi^2c^3}\,\frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega_{ba}} - 1}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.7)" />

The two must agree at every temperature, and the coefficients are constants, so the terms next to the exponential and the prefactors must match separately (L12.4 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Einstein's relations" tex="B_{ab} = B_{ba}, \qquad \frac{A}{B_{ab}} = \frac{\hbar\,\omega_{ba}^3}{\pi^2c^3}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.8)" />

The first relation is what perturbation theory already told us: going up and coming down have equal rates, so there is just one $B$, as there is one $A$. The second is the prize. The $B$ coefficient belongs to the familiar harmonic transitions and can be calculated, as the next lecture does; $A$ is much harder to compute directly, but with this relation it follows from $B$. Homework problems use exactly this route: to find how fast an excited oscillator decays spontaneously, compute its stimulated rate and multiply by the factor above (L12.4 transcript p. 2).

The rate of spontaneous emission does not depend on how many photons are present. Yet in a full calculation with the quantized field, Zwiebach notes, one can view it as emission **stimulated by the vacuum fluctuations** of the electromagnetic field, which has zero-point energy like any quantum field; in that sense all emission is stimulated, by real photons or by the vacuum. The names stay distinct all the same (L12.4 transcript pp. 2–3; notes p. 107).

Which emission wins depends on the temperature. For an atom in $b$, the ratio of the spontaneous to the stimulated rate is $A/(B_{ab}U(\omega_{ba}))$, and by Einstein's relations and Planck (notes p. 107, unnumbered)

<Formula label="Spontaneous versus stimulated emission" tex="\frac{A}{B_{ab}\,U(\omega_{ba})} = e^{\hbar\omega_{ba}/k_BT} - 1" cite="reading6" at="p. 107" />

At low temperature there are few photons and spontaneous emission dominates; at high temperature the photons are plentiful and stimulated emission takes over (L12.4 transcript p. 3). The two are equal where the exponential is 2, at $k_BT = \hbar\omega_{ba}/\ln 2$.

## Atoms in a light wave: the dipole operator

To compute $B$ one needs the perturbation an atom feels in a light wave (L12.5 transcript p. 1; notes section 4.5.3). Two simplifications:

- **Only the electric field.** Magnetic effects are smaller by about $v/c$, which for atomic electrons is of the order of the fine-structure constant.
- **A uniform field.** Optical light has wavelengths from about 4,000 to 8,000 ångström, while $a_0$ is about 0.5 ångström, so the field barely changes across the atom.

So at the atom the field depends on time only, along a fixed unit vector $\vec n$, the polarization; later $\vec n$ will be averaged over all directions, since thermal radiation comes with every polarization and direction (L12.5 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="The field at the atom" tex="\vec E(t) = E(t)\,\vec n = 2E_0\cos(\omega t)\,\vec n" cite="reading6" at="p. 108" />

A uniform field comes from the potential (L12.5 transcript p. 1)

<Formula label="Potential of a uniform field" tex="\Phi(\vec r, t) = -\vec r\cdot\vec E(t), \qquad \nabla\Phi = -\vec E" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.9)" />

(with time-dependent magnetic fields the field would also get a term from the vector potential, but those are being ignored). For a particle of charge $q$, $q = -e$ for the electron, the perturbation is its potential energy (L12.5 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="The perturbation" tex="\delta H = q\,\Phi(\vec r, t) = -q\,\vec r\cdot\vec E(t)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.10)" />

The **dipole operator** is charge times position, as for any dipole, a sum or integral of charges times position vectors; it is an operator because $\vec r$ is (L12.5 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Dipole form of the perturbation" tex="\vec d = q\,\vec r, \qquad \delta H = -\vec d\cdot\vec E(t) = 2\left(-\vec d\cdot\vec n\,E_0\right)\cos\omega t" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.11)" />

[The transcript has "minus $v$ dot $n$" for $\vec d\cdot\vec n$.] With the convention $\delta H = 2H'\cos\omega t$, everything except the 2 and the cosine is $H'$ (L12.5 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The dipole interaction" tex="H' = -\vec d\cdot\vec n\,E_0" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.12)" />

For a two-level atom, the first-order probability of a transition between $a$ and $b$ is the same in either direction. Call it $P_{ab}(t)$ (the notes write it with an arrow, from $b$ to $a$, and call it the stimulated emission probability). Last lecture's formula with this $H'$ gives (L12.5 transcript pp. 2–3)

<Formula label="Transition probability for the dipole interaction" tex="P_{ab}(t) = \frac{4\,|H'_{ab}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\sin^2\left((\omega_{ba} - \omega)t/2\right)}{(\omega_{ba} - \omega)^2} = \frac{4E_0^2\,|(\vec d\cdot\vec n)_{ab}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\sin^2\left((\omega_{ba} - \omega)t/2\right)}{(\omega_{ba} - \omega)^2}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.5.13)" />

Everything now depends on matrix elements of the dipole operator. This is a probability, not yet a rate: the rate appears when the probability is integrated over all the photons of the thermal spectrum that can contribute, giving an analogue of Fermi's golden rule, next lecture (L12.5 transcript p. 3).

<WorkedExample title="A strong X-ray beam on hydrogen, and two lines in thermal light">
<Problem>
(a) Light with photon energy $\hbar\omega = 500$ eV and peak field $E_p = 1.0 \times 10^{9}$ V/m falls on ground-state hydrogen. Last lecture found $ka_0 \approx 5.98$ for this photon. Use $E_* = 5.14 \times 10^{11}$ V/m and $t_* = 2.42 \times 10^{-17}$ s to find the ionization rate per atom and its inverse, the mean time to ionization. (b) By what factor does keeping the 1 in $(1 + k^2a_0^2)^6$ change the rate? (c) What fraction of the electrons leaves within $30^\circ$ of the field axis (either way), and what fraction of all directions is that? (d) For a transition of energy 10.2 eV (hydrogen's Lyman alpha), compare spontaneous and stimulated emission in radiation at 6,000 K. (e) Do the same for hydrogen's [21 cm line](/courses/8-05/hyperfine-21cm-adding-angular-momenta) (from 8.05), frequency 1420.4 MHz, in radiation at 2.725 K, the temperature of the cosmic microwave background. Use $h = 4.136 \times 10^{-15}$ eV s and $k_B = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/K.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The rate">
$E_p/E_* = 1.0 \times 10^9/5.14 \times 10^{11} \approx 1.95 \times 10^{-3}$, whose square is $3.79 \times 10^{-6}$. Next, $(ka_0)^9 = 5.98^9 \approx 9.78 \times 10^6$. So $w = (256/3) \times 3.79 \times 10^{-6}/(2.42 \times 10^{-17} \times 9.78 \times 10^6)$ per second, about $1.36 \times 10^{6}$ per second, and the mean time to ionization is $1/w \approx 0.73$ μs. Even a field 500 times weaker than the atomic field ionizes an atom within a microsecond.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The 1">
With the 1, the rate has $(ka_0)^3/(1 + k^2a_0^2)^6$ instead of $(ka_0)^{-9}$; the ratio is $\left(k^2a_0^2/(1 + k^2a_0^2)\right)^6 = (35.76/36.76)^6 \approx 0.85$. Keeping it lowers the rate by about 15%, a reminder that this end of the window is only roughly described.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The angles">
The fraction of electrons within $30^\circ$ of either direction of the axis is $2 \times 2\pi\int_0^{\pi/6}\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta$ divided by $4\pi/3$, which is $1 - \cos^3 30^\circ \approx 0.35$. Those two cones cover only $1 - \cos 30^\circ \approx 0.13$ of all directions: 35% of the electrons go into 13% of the sky.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) An ultraviolet line">
$k_BT = 8.617 \times 10^{-5} \times 6000 \approx 0.517$ eV, so $\hbar\omega_{ba}/k_BT \approx 19.7$ and $A/(BU) = e^{19.7} - 1 \approx 3.7 \times 10^{8}$. Spontaneous emission wins by a factor of hundreds of millions: such light is far too thin at 10.2 eV to stimulate much.
</Step>
<Step title="(e) A radio line">
$\hbar\omega_{ba} = h\nu = 4.136 \times 10^{-15} \times 1.4204 \times 10^9 \approx 5.87 \times 10^{-6}$ eV, and $k_BT = 8.617 \times 10^{-5} \times 2.725 \approx 2.35 \times 10^{-4}$ eV. The ratio is $\hbar\omega_{ba}/k_BT \approx 0.0250$, so $A/(BU) = e^{0.0250} - 1 \approx 0.0253$: stimulated emission is about 39 times faster than spontaneous emission. The rates would be equal only at $k_BT = \hbar\omega_{ba}/\ln 2$, that is at about 0.098 K.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Einstein's argument and the cosmic microwave background">
Einstein's argument assumes that radiation in equilibrium has Planck's spectrum. In 8.286 (Lectures 16 and 18), Guth shows that black-body radiation in equilibrium is the same whatever contains it, and how the COBE satellite found in 1990 that the cosmic microwave background fits a Planck spectrum at about 2.7 K extraordinarily well, evidence that the early universe was in thermal equilibrium. The worked example's last part applies this lecture's ratio to that radiation (a calculation that goes beyond Guth's lectures): for a transition as low in energy as hydrogen's 21 cm line, the background is "hot", and stimulated emission outpaces spontaneous emission. For high-energy transitions such as Lyman alpha, spontaneous emission dominates unless the radiation is very hot.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Squaring the matrix element and using the golden rule with $\rho(E_e) = (L^3/8\pi^3)(m/\hbar^2)k\,d\Omega$ gives the ionization rate per solid angle, $dw/d\Omega = (256/\pi)(ma_0^2/\hbar^2)((eE_0a_0)^2/\hbar)\,k^3a_0^3\cos^2\theta/(1 + k^2a_0^2)^6$: electrons leave mostly along the field, never at right angles to it.
- With $\int\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega = 4\pi/3$ and $ka_0 \gg 1$, $w = (512/3)(eE_0a_0)^2/(\hbar R_y)\,(ka_0)^{-9}$; in atomic units $w = (256/3)(E_p/E_*)^2(1/t_*)(ka_0)^{-9}$ with $E_* = e/a_0^2 \approx 5.14 \times 10^{11}$ V/m and $t_* = \hbar/2R_y \approx 2.42 \times 10^{-17}$ s.
- Light on two-level atoms causes absorption and stimulated emission, with equal probabilities in first-order perturbation theory; stimulated emission with a population inversion, made by optical pumping, is the laser.
- **Einstein's argument**: with populations in equilibrium, Boltzmann ratio $N_b/N_a = e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_{ba}}$ and Planck's $U(\omega)$, absorption and stimulated emission alone cannot balance at every temperature. Adding spontaneous emission, $AN_b$, gives $B_{ab} = B_{ba}$ and $A/B_{ab} = \hbar\omega_{ba}^3/\pi^2c^3$, so $A$ follows from $B$. Spontaneous emission dominates when $k_BT < \hbar\omega_{ba}/\ln 2$, stimulated emission above.
- In a uniform field $\vec E(t) = 2E_0\cos(\omega t)\,\vec n$, ignoring magnetic effects of order $v/c$, the perturbation is $\delta H = -\vec d\cdot\vec E(t)$ with the dipole operator $\vec d = q\vec r$, so $H' = -\vec d\cdot\vec n\,E_0$, and transition probabilities depend on $|(\vec d\cdot\vec n)_{ab}|^2$. Integrating over the thermal spectrum, next lecture, gives the rate and $B$.
