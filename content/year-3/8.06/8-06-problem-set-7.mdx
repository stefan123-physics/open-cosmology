## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Gauge invariance and the Schrödinger equation" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

The Schrödinger equation of a charged particle in the potentials $\Phi$ and $\vec A$ is

<Formula label="Schrödinger equation with potentials" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \left[\frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\vec A\right)^2 + q\Phi\right]\psi" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Prove that the Schrödinger equation is gauge invariant: show that if $\psi$ solves it with the potentials $\Phi$ and $\vec A$, then $\psi' = U\psi$ solves it with the transformed potentials $\Phi'$ and $\vec A'$.

<Hint>

Start with the space part. How does $\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\vec A'$ act on the product $U\psi$? The lesson calls the result the covariant derivative identity.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Do the same for the time part: move $q\Phi$ to the left side and look at $\left(i\hbar\,\partial_t - q\Phi'\right)(U\psi)$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each of the two combinations of a derivative and a potential lets the phase $U$ pass through to the left. The equation for $\psi'$ is then $U$ times the equation for $\psi$.

<Step title="The space part">

The gradient of the phase is $\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla U = \frac{q}{c}(\nabla\Lambda)\,U$. That is exactly what the change of the vector potential takes away again:

<Formula label="The covariant derivative identity" tex="\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\vec A'\right)(U\psi) = U\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\vec A\right)\psi" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–4" />

The right side is again $U$ times a function, so the identity can be used a second time:

<Formula label="The identity used twice" tex="\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\vec A'\right)^2(U\psi) = U\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\vec A\right)^2\psi" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The time part">

The time derivative of the phase is $i\hbar\,\partial_tU = -\frac{q}{c}(\partial_t\Lambda)\,U$, and $-q\Phi' = -q\Phi + \frac{q}{c}\,\partial_t\Lambda$. The two terms with $\partial_t\Lambda$ cancel:

<Formula label="The time part lets U through" tex="\left(i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t} - q\Phi'\right)(U\psi) = U\left(i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t} - q\Phi\right)\psi" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Putting them together">

Write the Schrödinger equation for $\psi$ as $\left(i\hbar\,\partial_t - q\Phi\right)\psi - \frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla - \frac{q}{c}\vec A\right)^2\psi = 0$. By the two results, the same expression with primes on $\psi$, $\Phi$ and $\vec A$ equals $U$ times the left side, which is $U \cdot 0 = 0$. So $\psi'$ solves the equation with the transformed potentials.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $\Lambda = \lambda_0$, a constant. The potentials do not change, and $\psi'$ is $\psi$ times a constant phase, which solves the same linear equation: correct. Take $\Lambda = -c\,\epsilon\,t$ with a constant $\epsilon$. Then $\vec A' = \vec A$ and $\Phi' = \Phi + \epsilon$: the energy of the particle is shifted by $q\epsilon$, and $\psi' = e^{-iq\epsilon t/\hbar}\psi$ carries exactly that extra energy in its time dependence.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

An operator $\mathcal{O}$ may change under a gauge transformation into some $\mathcal{O}'$ (it changes when its definition contains the potentials). It is called gauge covariant when

<Formula label="A gauge covariant operator" tex="\mathcal{O}' = U\,\mathcal{O}\,U^{-1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2" />

Physical observables are Hermitian operators with this property. Explain why a gauge covariant operator has a gauge invariant expectation value, $\langle\psi'|\mathcal{O}'|\psi'\rangle = \langle\psi|\mathcal{O}|\psi\rangle$. Then answer, with a short reason each time:

1. Is the sum of two gauge covariant operators gauge covariant?
2. Is their product?
3. Is $\hat x_i$ gauge covariant?
4. Is $\hat p_i$?
5. Is $\hat v_i$?
6. Is the Hamiltonian $H$ gauge covariant under every gauge transformation? If not, for which gauge transformations is it?

<Hint>

$\Lambda$ is real, so $U$ is unitary: $U^\dagger = U^{-1}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For each operator, find two things and compare them: what its definition turns into when the potentials change ($\mathcal{O}'$), and what $U\mathcal{O}U^{-1}$ is. For $\hat p$, let $U\,\hat p\,U^{-1}$ act on a test function.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For question 6, write $H = \frac12m\hat{\vec v}^{\,2} + q\Phi$ and use your answers to questions 1, 2 and 5 for the first term.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-sumprod" label="Sums and products of gauge covariant operators are" choices={["sums: yes; products: no", "sums: yes; products: yes", "sums: no; products: yes", "sums: yes; products: only if they commute"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-x" label="The position $\hat x_i$ is" choices={["gauge covariant: it does not change, and it commutes with U", "not gauge covariant: U depends on position, so U x U⁻¹ differs from x", "gauge covariant only when Λ does not depend on position"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p1-p" label="The canonical momentum $\hat p_i$ is" choices={["gauge covariant: it does not change, and neither does U p U⁻¹", "gauge covariant: it changes to p − (q/c)∇Λ, and so does U p U⁻¹", "not gauge covariant: it does not change, but U p U⁻¹ = p − (q/c)∇Λ", "not gauge covariant: it changes to p + (q/c)∇Λ, but U p U⁻¹ = p stays the same"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p1-v" label="The velocity $\hat v_i$ is" choices={["not gauge covariant, because it contains p", "gauge covariant, because it commutes with U", "not gauge covariant, because it contains the potential A", "gauge covariant: the shift of A cancels that of U p U⁻¹"]} answer={3} />

<Answer id="p1-h" label="$H' = UHU^{-1}$ holds" choices={["for every gauge function Λ", "only when Λ does not depend on time", "only when Λ does not depend on position", "for no gauge function except Λ = 0"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A covariant operator picks up $U$ on its left and $U^{-1}$ on its right, and the transformed states supply the factors that cancel them.

<Step title="Expectation values">

With $|\psi'\rangle = U|\psi\rangle$ and $\langle\psi'| = \langle\psi|U^\dagger$:

<Formula label="Gauge invariant expectation value" tex="\langle\psi'|\mathcal{O}'|\psi'\rangle = \langle\psi|\,U^\dagger\,U\,\mathcal{O}\,U^{-1}\,U\,|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|\mathcal{O}|\psi\rangle" cite="reading3" at="p. 1, with the covariance condition of Problem 1(b)" />

because $U^\dagger U = 1$ for a real $\Lambda$. The same holds for the matrix element between any two states.

</Step>

<Step title="Questions 1 and 2: sums and products">

Both are covariant. The sum of the transformed operators is $U\mathcal{O}_1U^{-1} + U\mathcal{O}_2U^{-1} = U(\mathcal{O}_1 + \mathcal{O}_2)U^{-1}$. For the product, the inner factors cancel: $U\mathcal{O}_1U^{-1}\,U\mathcal{O}_2U^{-1} = U\,\mathcal{O}_1\mathcal{O}_2\,U^{-1}$. (A product of two Hermitian operators is Hermitian only if they commute, but that is a separate matter from covariance.)

</Step>

<Step title="Questions 3 to 5: position, momentum, velocity">

The definition of $\hat x_i$ contains no potential, so $\hat x_i' = \hat x_i$. And $U$ is a function of position, so it commutes with $\hat x_i$: $U\hat x_iU^{-1} = \hat x_i$. The position is covariant.

The definition of $\hat{\vec p} = \frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla$ contains no potential either, so $\hat{\vec p}\,' = \hat{\vec p}$. But $\hat{\vec p}$ does not commute with $U$. On a test function $g$:

<Formula label="The momentum between U and its inverse" tex="U\,\hat{\vec p}\,U^{-1}g = U\,\frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla\left(U^{-1}g\right) = \left(\hat{\vec p} - \frac{q}{c}\nabla\Lambda\right)g" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

This differs from $\hat{\vec p}$ unless $\nabla\Lambda = 0$: the canonical momentum is not covariant, and its expectation value depends on the gauge (the lesson shows the same from the expectation value).

The velocity does contain a potential: $m\hat{\vec v}\,' = \hat{\vec p} - \frac{q}{c}\vec A' = \hat{\vec p} - \frac{q}{c}\nabla\Lambda - \frac{q}{c}\vec A$. By the last formula, and because $\vec A$ commutes with $U$, this is $U\left(\hat{\vec p} - \frac{q}{c}\vec A\right)U^{-1}$. The velocity is covariant.

</Step>

<Step title="Question 6: the Hamiltonian">

The kinetic term $\frac12m\hat{\vec v}^{\,2}$ is a sum of products of covariant operators, so it is covariant. The term $q\Phi$ commutes with $U$, but its definition changes:

<Formula label="The Hamiltonian under a gauge transformation" tex="H' = \frac{m}{2}\,\hat{\vec v}\,'\cdot\hat{\vec v}\,' + q\Phi' = U\,H\,U^{-1} - \frac{q}{c}\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So $H$ is gauge covariant exactly for the gauge transformations with $\partial\Lambda/\partial t = 0$: those with a gauge function that depends on position only. For a time-dependent $\Lambda$ the energy is shifted, as in the check of part (a). What is covariant under every gauge transformation is the combination $i\hbar\,\partial_t - H$, which is what part (a) used.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In a static magnetic field with $\Phi = 0$, two people who use different time-independent vector potentials for the same field are related by a $\Lambda(\vec x)$. They find the same energy levels, since $H' = UHU^{-1}$ has the spectrum of $H$. Problems 3 and 5 rely on this.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Classical motion in a magnetic field" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

A particle of mass $m$ and charge $q$ moves through a constant magnetic field that points along $z$, with $B_z = B$. The motion is classical in this problem, and it stays in the $(x, y)$ plane.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Start from the Lorentz force law,

<Formula label="Lorentz force law" tex="m\frac{d\vec v}{dt} = \frac{q}{c}\,\vec v\times\vec B" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

and show that the general solution is motion on a circle with the angular velocity $\omega_c = qB/mc$. Write the solution with $(X, Y)$ for the centre of the circle. In the boxes, the $x$ coordinate is written as $x(t) = X + r_0\cos(\omega_ct + \theta_0)$, with the radius $r_0$ and a phase $\theta_0$. Give $y(t)$, the radius in terms of the speed $v$, and the sense of the motion (the last two are our additions).

<Hint>

Write out the two components of $\vec v\times\vec B$ for $\vec B = B\hat z$. You get two coupled first-order equations for $v_x$ and $v_y$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiate one of them and use the other: each velocity component obeys the equation of a harmonic oscillator. Then integrate once more to get the position, and call the two constants of that integration $X$ and $Y$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-y" label="$y(t) =$" expr="Y - r0*sin(wc*t + th0)"
  vars={{ Y: [-1, 1], r0: [0.5, 2], wc: [0.5, 2], t: [0.2, 3], th0: [0.1, 1] }}
  names={{ r0: "r_0", wc: "\\omega_c", th0: "\\theta_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Y + r0*sin(wc*t + th0)", hint: "That circle is run through in the opposite sense. Differentiate twice and test it on the x component of the force law, dv_x/dt = ω_c v_y." },
    { expr: "Y + r0*cos(wc*t + th0)", hint: "With a cosine in both coordinates, y − Y equals x − X at all times: that is a straight line, not a circle." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-r0" label="$r_0 =$" expr="v/wc"
  vars={{ v: [0.5, 2], wc: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ wc: "\\omega_c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v*wc", hint: "Check the units: a speed times a frequency is an acceleration, not a length." },
    { expr: "2*pi*v/wc", hint: "That is the length of one turn, the distance covered in the period 2π/ω_c. The radius is smaller by 2π." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-sense" label="For $qB > 0$, seen from the side of positive $z$, the particle circles" choices={["anticlockwise, like the angle φ of polar coordinates", "clockwise, against the angle φ of polar coordinates", "in a sense set by its starting velocity"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The magnetic force is always at right angles to the velocity. It turns the velocity at a constant rate without changing the speed.

<Step title="The equations of motion">

With $\vec B = B\hat z$ the cross product is $\vec v\times\vec B = (v_yB, -v_xB, 0)$. Dividing by $m$:

<Formula label="Equations for the velocity" tex="\frac{dv_x}{dt} = \omega_c\,v_y, \qquad \frac{dv_y}{dt} = -\omega_c\,v_x, \qquad \omega_c = \frac{qB}{mc}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) and eq. (4), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Differentiating the first and using the second gives $d^2v_x/dt^2 = -\omega_c^2v_x$, an oscillation at the angular frequency $\omega_c$. The combination $v_x^2 + v_y^2$ has zero time derivative, so the speed $v$ is constant. The general solution is

<Formula label="The velocity turns at the cyclotron frequency" tex="v_x = -v\sin(\omega_ct + \theta_0), \qquad v_y = -v\cos(\omega_ct + \theta_0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The orbit">

Integrating once more brings in two constants, $X$ and $Y$:

<Formula label="General solution: a circle around (X, Y)" tex="x(t) = X + r_0\cos(\omega_ct + \theta_0), \qquad y(t) = Y - r_0\sin(\omega_ct + \theta_0), \qquad r_0 = \frac{v}{\omega_c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The point $(x, y)$ stays at the distance $r_0$ from $(X, Y)$: a circle. The four constants $X$, $Y$, $r_0$ and $\theta_0$ match the four starting values (two of position, two of velocity), so this is the general solution.

</Step>

<Step title="The sense of the motion">

The direction from the centre to the particle makes the angle $-(\omega_ct + \theta_0)$ with the $x$ axis. For $\omega_c > 0$ this angle decreases: the particle runs clockwise when seen from positive $z$, whatever its starting velocity. For $qB$ negative, $\omega_c$ is negative and the sense is reversed.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At an instant when the velocity is $(0, v)$ with $q$ and $B$ positive, the force $\frac{q}{c}\vec v\times\vec B$ points along $+x$. The centre then lies to the right of a particle moving "up", which is a clockwise turn. Units: $v/\omega_c$ is (m/s)/(1/s), a length. A stronger field makes the circle smaller and the period $2\pi/\omega_c$ shorter, and the period does not depend on the speed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Show that the centre of the orbit can be written with the position and the velocity of the particle at any one time,

<Formula label="Centre of the orbit" tex="X = x + \frac{v_y}{\omega_c}, \qquad Y = y - \frac{v_x}{\omega_c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), eq. (5), p. 2" />

and show by differentiating these expressions that $X$ and $Y$ are constants of the motion.

<Hint>

Compare the velocity components of your solution with $x - X$ and $y - Y$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the second half you need only the two first-order equations for $v_x$ and $v_y$, not their solution.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the circle the velocity is at right angles to the line from the centre, and its size is $\omega_c$ times the radius. So the velocity tells where the centre is.

<Step title="From the solution">

From part (a), $v_y = -r_0\omega_c\cos(\omega_ct + \theta_0) = -\omega_c(x - X)$ and $v_x = -r_0\omega_c\sin(\omega_ct + \theta_0) = \omega_c(y - Y)$. Solving the first for $X$ and the second for $Y$ gives the two expressions.

</Step>

<Step title="Constants of the motion">

Differentiate and use the equations of motion $dv_x/dt = \omega_cv_y$ and $dv_y/dt = -\omega_cv_x$:

<Formula label="The centre does not move" tex="\frac{dX}{dt} = v_x + \frac{1}{\omega_c}\frac{dv_y}{dt} = v_x - v_x = 0, \qquad \frac{dY}{dt} = v_y - \frac{1}{\omega_c}\frac{dv_x}{dt} = v_y - v_y = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A particle at rest has $v_x = v_y = 0$, and then $(X, Y) = (x, y)$: a circle of zero radius around the particle, as it should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Quantum motion in a magnetic field, without choosing a gauge" points={10} mit="Problem 3">

Derive the results of this problem without choosing a gauge. The electric field is zero, the scalar potential is zero, and all motion is in the $(x, y)$ plane. In the boxes of this problem, type `wc` for $\omega_c$ and `lB` for $\ell_B$ where the box offers them.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

Let the magnetic field be arbitrary: $B_z = B(x, y)$ need not be constant. Find the commutator $[\hat v_x, \hat v_y]$ of the velocity operators.

<Hint>

Only the cross terms survive: $[\hat p_x, A_y]$ and $[A_x, \hat p_y]$. The commutator of a momentum with a function of position is a derivative of that function.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-vv" label="$[\hat v_x, \hat v_y] = i\,\times$" expr="hbar*q*B/(m^2*c)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], q: [0.5, 2], B: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], c: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-hbar*q*B/(m^2*c)", hint: "Check the signs: [p_x, A_y] = (ħ/i) ∂A_y/∂x, and each cross term carries the factor −q/c." },
    { expr: "hbar*q*B/(m*c)", hint: "Each velocity operator carries its own factor 1/m, so the commutator has 1/m²." },
    { expr: "hbar*q*B/m^2", hint: "In Gaussian units the potential enters as (q/c)A, so the factor 1/c stays." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two components of $\hat{\vec p} - \frac{q}{c}\vec A$ fail to commute by the curl of $\vec A$, which is the magnetic field.

<Step title="The commutator">

<Formula label="Instantaneous energies of the spin" tex="E_\pm(t) = \mp\,\mu_0\sqrt{B_\perp^2 + \gamma^2t^2}, \qquad E_- - E_+ = 2\mu_0\sqrt{B_\perp^2 + \gamma^2t^2} \;\ge\; 2\mu_0B_\perp" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

The gap is smallest at $t = 0$ and never closes as long as $B_\perp \neq 0$. The adiabatic theorem asks for exactly that, a level that stays separated from the others. Then a spin that starts in $|\psi_+(-T)\rangle$ stays in $|\psi_+(t)\rangle$, up to a phase, if the change is slow enough. If $B_\perp = 0$ the levels cross at $t = 0$, the theorem does not apply, and in fact nothing happens to the spin at all: $H$ is then proportional to $S_z$ at all times, and the spin stays in the state it started in, which after $t = 0$ is the excited state.

</Step>

<Step title="The Landau–Zener form">

In the basis $|1\rangle = |-\rangle$, $|2\rangle = |+\rangle$ (spin down first) the Hamiltonian is

<Formula label="The spin problem as a Landau–Zener problem" tex="H = \begin{pmatrix} \mu_0\gamma t & -\mu_0(B_x + iB_y) \\ -\mu_0(B_x - iB_y) & -\mu_0\gamma t \end{pmatrix} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha = 2\mu_0\gamma, \qquad |H_{12}| = \mu_0B_\perp" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.6), with the entries of this problem" />

The lesson's condition for an adiabatic passage is then

<Formula label="Adiabatic condition for the spin" tex="\frac{|H_{12}|^2}{\hbar\alpha} = \frac{\mu_0B_\perp^2}{2\hbar\gamma} \gg 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \gamma \ll \frac{\mu_0B_\perp^2}{\hbar}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.12)" />

up to a number of order one.

</Step>

<Step title="The same from the coupling between the levels">

The notes' equations for the coefficients couple the two instantaneous eigenstates through $\dot H_{kn}/(E_n - E_k)$. This rate must be small compared with the rate $|E_n - E_k|/\hbar$ at which the relative phase of the two states turns. Here $\dot H = -(2\mu_0\gamma/\hbar)\,S_z$, and its matrix element between the spin states along and against a field that makes the angle $\theta$ with the $z$ axis has the size $\mu_0\gamma\sin\theta$. At $t = 0$, where the gap is smallest and $\sin\theta = 1$:

<Formula label="The coupling compared with the gap" tex="\text{at } t = 0: \qquad \frac{\hbar\,|\dot H_{kn}|}{(E_n - E_k)^2} = \frac{\hbar\,\mu_0\gamma}{4\mu_0^2B_\perp^2} = \frac{\hbar\gamma}{4\mu_0B_\perp^2} \ll 1" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (3.9), (3.10), for the two spin states" />

The same combination, with another number of order one.

</Step>

<Step title="The exact answer">

For $T \to \infty$ the lesson's Landau–Zener formula gives the probability of a non-adiabatic transition, which here means ending in the excited state $|\psi_-(T)\rangle$:

<Formula label="Probability that the spin fails to follow the field" tex="P_{\text{wrong}} = \exp\left(-\frac{2\pi|H_{12}|^2}{\hbar\alpha}\right) = \exp\left(-\frac{\pi\mu_0B_\perp^2}{\hbar\gamma}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.18), with the entries of this problem (our addition)" />

It is small when $\gamma \ll \pi\mu_0B_\perp^2/\hbar$: the "number of order one" is not far from one.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\mu_0B$ is an energy, so $\mu_0B_\perp^2/\hbar$ is a field times (energy/$\hbar$), a field per time, like $\gamma$. A larger field in the plane opens the gap and allows a faster sweep. In words, the condition says that the direction of the field, which near $t = 0$ turns at the rate $\gamma/B_\perp$, must turn slowly compared with the precession of the spin around the field, at the rate $2\mu_0B_\perp/\hbar$.

</Step>

Multiplying by $|H_{12}|^2/\alpha^2$ gives the coefficients $|H_{12}|^2/(\hbar\alpha) = \gamma$ and $|H_{12}|^4/(\hbar\alpha)^2 = \gamma^2$: the stated equation.

</Step>

<Step title="The numerical solution">

Our own solution, started at $\tau = -25$ with $B = 1$ and $dB/d\tau = 0$ (a Runge–Kutta solver with a small error tolerance):

| time $\tau$ | $-25$ | $-5$ | $0$ | $5$ | $10$ | $15$ | $20$ | $25$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| probability | 1 | 0.978 | 0.877 | 0.475 | 0.645 | 0.617 | 0.515 | 0.569 |

$|B|^2$ stays close to 1 until shortly before the crossing, drops steeply through $\tau = 0$ to a lowest value of 0.414 near $\tau = 6.4$, and then oscillates with a slowly shrinking swing. Between $\tau = 15$ and $\tau = 25$ it moves between 0.49 and 0.62, around a mean of 0.55.

</Step>

<Step title="The comparison">

The prediction is $e^{-2\pi \times 0.1} = e^{-0.628} = 0.533$. The curve has not settled by $\tau = 25$, but the value it oscillates around, 0.55, agrees with the prediction within the swing. On a longer interval the agreement improves slowly: started at $\tau = -100$, the same solver gives a mean of 0.538 over the last fifth of the interval up to $\tau = 100$. The first-order result of part (b), $1 - 2\pi \times 0.1 = 0.372$, is well off: $2\pi\gamma = 0.63$ is not small, and the numerical solution sides with the exponential.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Two checks of a numerical solution of this kind. With $\gamma = 0$ it must give $B = 1$ for all $\tau$. And the companion amplitude, $|A|^2 = |dB/d\tau|^2/\gamma^2$ from the first-order equations in the variable $\tau$, must add up with $|B|^2$ to 1 at every $\tau$.

</Step>
