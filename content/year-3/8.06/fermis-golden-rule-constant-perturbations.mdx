The last lecture set up time-dependent perturbation theory and stopped at the first-order transition amplitude. This lecture puts it to work on the most important case: a transition from a discrete state into a **continuum** of states, such as an atom losing an electron. A transition between two discrete states is simple, Zwiebach says; what makes the continuum interesting is that one must integrate over all the final states, and the integral turns out to give a constant transition rate. The result is **Fermi's golden rule** (L10.1 transcript p. 1; notes section 4.3). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 4; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Two kinds of perturbation

Two cases are worth studying (L10.1 transcript p. 1). In the first, a time-independent perturbation $V$ is switched on at $t = 0$ and then left alone:

<Formula label="A constant perturbation" tex="H = H^{(0)} \ \text{ for } t \le 0, \qquad H = H^{(0)} + V \ \text{ for } t > 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.1)" />

There is a little time dependence, the switching on, so this is still a time-dependent problem. Constant perturbations explain **auto-ionization**, in which some atoms ionize all by themselves; that is the application at the end of this lecture (L10.1 transcript p. 2). In the second case the perturbation oscillates:

<Formula label="A harmonic perturbation" tex="\delta H(t) = 2H'\cos\omega t \ \text{ for } 0 < t < t_0, \qquad \delta H(t) = 0 \ \text{ otherwise}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

with $H'$ time independent; the factor 2 is a convention that makes later formulas tidier. (The notes, eq. (4.3.3), let it run on for all $t > 0$.) Harmonic perturbations describe atoms in an electromagnetic wave, and they are the subject of the next lecture. Once the constant case is understood, Zwiebach says, the harmonic one is easier (L10.1 transcript p. 2).

## Counting the continuum: the world in a box

The final states will be free-particle momentum eigenstates, and their momenta take continuous values. One cannot count them, any more than one can count the numbers between 0 and 1. The way out is to add a parameter one wishes one did not need, just as the delta function was smoothed into a pulse in the last lecture: put the whole world in a very large cubic box of side $L$, count the now discrete states, and check at the end that $L$ drops out (L10.1 transcript pp. 2–3). The notes call $L$ a **regulator** (notes p. 88).

In the box a momentum eigenstate is normalized:

<Formula label="Box-normalized momentum eigenstates" tex="\psi(\vec x) = \frac{1}{L^{3/2}}\,e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}, \qquad \int_{\text{box}}|\psi(\vec x)|^2\,d^3x = \frac{1}{L^3}\int_{\text{box}}d^3x = 1" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.3.4), (4.3.5)" />

Zwiebach chooses **periodic boundary conditions**: the wavefunction repeats when $x$, $y$ or $z$ grows by $L$, so each direction is really a circle and space a torus. Hard walls would give the same count in the end (L10.1 transcript p. 3). Periodicity quantizes each component of $\vec k$:

<Formula label="Quantized wavenumbers" tex="k_xL = 2\pi n_x \quad\Rightarrow\quad L\,dk_x = 2\pi\,dn_x" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.3.6), (4.3.7)" />

and the same for $y$ and $z$, with integers $n_x$, $n_y$, $n_z$. A small cell $d^3k = dk_x\,dk_y\,dk_z$ of momentum space therefore holds

<Formula label="Number of states in a cell of momentum space" tex="\Delta N = dn_x\,dn_y\,dn_z = \left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^3 d^3k" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.8)" />

states: a famous formula, "some people know this by heart" (L10.1 transcript pp. 3–4). It does not depend on $\vec k$: the states are spread evenly through momentum space.

What we want is the number of states per unit **energy**. Picture the cell in spherical coordinates: all wavevectors of size between $k$ and $k + dk$ pointing into a small cone of solid angle $d\Omega$, a thin slab of a cone (L10.1 transcript p. 4). Its volume is $d^3k = k^2\,dk\,d\Omega$ (notes eq. (4.3.9)). Differentiating $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ links $dk$ to $dE$:

<Formula label="From wavenumber to energy" tex="E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} \quad\Rightarrow\quad k\,dk = \frac{m}{\hbar^2}\,dE, \qquad d^3k = k\,\frac{m}{\hbar^2}\,dE\,d\Omega" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.3.10), (4.3.11)" />

Writing the number of states as $\Delta N = \rho(E)\,dE$ defines the density of states (L10.1 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="Density of states in the box" tex="\Delta N = \left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^3 k\,\frac{m}{\hbar^2}\,d\Omega\,dE \equiv \rho(E)\,dE, \qquad \rho(E) = \frac{L^3}{8\pi^3}\,\frac{m}{\hbar^2}\,k\,d\Omega" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.3.12)–(4.3.14)" />

Read it carefully: $\rho(E)$ is the number of states per unit energy near $E$ **with momentum pointing into the solid angle $d\Omega$**, with $k$ understood as $\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$. For all directions one integrates over $d\Omega$, but often one wants to keep it: to ask, for instance, how many electrons an atom ejects in a given direction (L10.1 transcript p. 5). With a large box, a sum over states of any quantity $F$ that depends on their momenta becomes an integral:

<Formula label="Sums over states become integrals" tex="\sum_{\text{states}}F \;\rightarrow\; \int F\,\rho(E)\,dE" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.15)" />

## Transitions with a constant perturbation

Now take the perturbation $\delta H = V$, switched on at $t = 0$, and ask for the probability that a system starting in the state $i$ is found in the state $f$ at a time $t_0$ (L10.2 transcript p. 1). The tool is the first-order formula for the coefficients $c_n(t)$ of the interaction-picture state in the basis of eigenstates of $H^{(0)}$, which follows from the perturbation series of the last lecture (Zwiebach mentions that it came up in recitation):

<Formula label="First-order transition amplitude" tex="c_m^{(1)}(t) = \sum_n\int_0^t dt'\,e^{i\omega_{mn}t'}\,\frac{\delta H_{mn}(t')}{i\hbar}\,c_n^{(0)}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.2.33)" />

with $\omega_{mn} = (E_m - E_n)/\hbar$. Starting in the state $i$ means $c_n^{(0)} = \delta_{ni}$, so only the initial state survives the sum; asking about the final state means computing $c_f^{(1)}$. Since $V_{fi} = \langle f|V|i\rangle$ does not depend on time, it comes out of the integral, and only an exponential is left to integrate (L10.2 transcript p. 1; notes eqs. (4.3.17), (4.3.18)):

<Formula label="The amplitude for a constant perturbation" tex="c_f^{(1)}(t_0) = \frac{V_{fi}}{i\hbar}\int_0^{t_0}e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\,dt' = \frac{V_{fi}}{E_f - E_i}\left(1 - e^{i\omega_{fi}t_0}\right) = \frac{V_{fi}\,e^{i\omega_{fi}t_0/2}}{E_f - E_i}\,(-2i)\sin\left(\frac{\omega_{fi}t_0}{2}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.3.18), (4.3.19)" />

The $\hbar$ turned $\omega_{fi}$ into $E_f - E_i$, and taking half the exponent out front made a sine. The transition probability is the squared magnitude (L10.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Transition probability for a constant perturbation" tex="P_{fi}(t_0) = \left|c_f^{(1)}(t_0)\right|^2 = \frac{4\,|V_{fi}|^2\sin^2\left(\omega_{fi}t_0/2\right)}{(E_f - E_i)^2}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.20)" />

Zwiebach writes the labels of $P_{fi}$ final first, so that the initial state appears as a ket and the final one as a bra, as in $\langle f|V|i\rangle$. The result has no units, as a probability must: $|V_{fi}|^2$ and $(E_f - E_i)^2$ are both energy squared.

What does **weak** mean here? A pragmatic answer: the perturbation is weak if this probability is small. A probability of 3 is plainly nonsense; one in a million, an atom in a million ionized, is reasonable (L10.2 transcript p. 2). For a final state at a different energy the condition is

<Formula label="Condition for a weak perturbation" tex="\frac{4\,|V_{fi}|^2}{(E_f - E_i)^2} \ll 1" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.21)" />

Two cases show what the formula says.

**A final state at a different energy, $E_f \neq E_i$.** The probability rises (quadratically at first, as the expansion of the sine shows), reaches $4|V_{fi}|^2/(E_f - E_i)^2$, and returns to zero at $t_0 = 2\pi/|\omega_{fi}|$, over and over (L10.2 transcript pp. 2–3; notes Figure 4.2). If its maximum is small, first order is good for all times. The maximum falls as the energy mismatch grows: transitions that change the energy are disfavoured, because a perturbation that is switched on and then stays constant is not a good supplier of energy. They happen, but suppressed (L10.2 transcript p. 3).

**A different state at the same energy, $E_f = E_i$.** Taking the limit $\omega_{fi} \rightarrow 0$, where the sine of a small angle is the angle itself (L10.2 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Equal energies: quadratic growth" tex="\lim_{E_f \rightarrow E_i}P_{fi}(t_0) = \frac{|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,t_0^2" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.3.22), (4.3.23)" />

This grows without bound, so it can be trusted only while it stays well below 1. It is also the small-$t_0$ behaviour of the first case: near $t_0 = 0$ every curve starts out as this parabola (notes p. 91).

  ]}
  params={{ W: { value: 1.2, min: 1, max: 3, step: 0.1, label: "\\omega_{fi}" } }}
  caption="Computed from P_fi(t₀) = 4|V_fi|² sin²(ω_fi t₀/2)/(E_f − E_i)², in units where |V_fi|²/ħ² = 1 and time in units of a chosen reference (the page's own illustration). Solid: an energy mismatch ω_fi, oscillating with maximum 4/ω_fi² and back to zero at t₀ = 2π/ω_fi. Dashed: equal energies, the parabola t₀². Raise ω_fi and the oscillation becomes smaller and faster." />

Neither case gives what is needed for a **transition rate**. Shine light on a billion atoms and a steady number ionize per second; that needs a total transition probability that grows **linearly** in time, so that probability per unit time is constant. Here one case oscillates and the other grows quadratically. The linear growth appears, Zwiebach promises, by the magic of integration over a continuum of final states whose energies overlap $E_i$; and the transitions that matter will turn out to lie in a narrow band around $E_i$, set by the uncertainty principle: energy-conserving transitions (L10.2 transcript pp. 3–4).

## Summing over the continuum

The total probability to have made a transition into the continuum is the sum over final states, which the box turns into an integral with $\rho(E_f)$ (L10.3 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Summing over the continuum of final states" tex="\sum_f P_{fi}(t_0) = \int P_{fi}(t_0)\,\rho(E_f)\,dE_f = 4\int|V_{fi}|^2\,\frac{\sin^2\left(\omega_{fi}t_0/2\right)}{(E_f - E_i)^2}\,\rho(E_f)\,dE_f" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.24)" />

It looks as if one must stop here, since $\rho$ and $V_{fi}$ depend on the final energy and differ from problem to problem. The great thing about the golden rule is that one can go further (L10.3 transcript p. 1). The integrand is suppressed when $E_f$ is far from $E_i$, so only a narrow band of final energies should contribute. Over a narrow enough band, $|V_{fi}|^2$ and $\rho$ are nearly constant; take them out, evaluated at $E_i$, and check later that this was fair:

<Formula label="Taking the slowly varying factors out" tex="\sum_f P_{fi}(t_0) = \frac{4\,|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\rho(E_i)\,I(t_0), \qquad I(t_0) \equiv \int\frac{\sin^2\left(\omega_{fi}t_0/2\right)}{\omega_{fi}^2}\,dE_f" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.3.25), (4.3.26)" />

As always with an integral like this, plot the integrand first (L10.3 transcript p. 1). As a function of $\omega_{fi}$ (that is, of $E_f$), it has a central lobe of height $t_0^2/4$ at $\omega_{fi} = 0$, zeros at $\omega_{fi} = \pm 2\pi/t_0$, $\pm 4\pi/t_0$ and so on, and smaller side lobes beyond (L10.3 transcript pp. 1–2; notes Figure 4.3).

<Plot variable="w" domain={[-12, 12]} range={[0, 4.4]} xLabel="ω_fi" yLabel="integrand"
  curves={[
    { expr: "abs(w) < 1e-9 ? T^2/4 : sin(w*T/2)^2/w^2" }
  ]}
  params={{ T: { value: 2, min: 1, max: 4, step: 0.1, label: "t_0" } }}
  caption="Computed from the integrand of I(t₀), sin²(ω_fi t₀/2)/ω_fi², with t₀ and ω_fi in matching arbitrary units (the page's own illustration). The central lobe has height t₀²/4 and ends at ω_fi = ±2π/t₀. Doubling t₀ makes the lobe four times taller and half as wide, so its area doubles: the integral grows linearly in t₀." />

Now one can smile, Zwiebach says: height proportional to $t_0^2$ times width proportional to $1/t_0$ makes an area proportional to $t_0$. The quadratic growth and the oscillation have conspired into exactly the linear behaviour a rate needs (L10.3 transcript p. 2). The central lobe holds about 90% of the integral, and with the next lobe on each side about 95% (L10.3 transcript p. 2); ten or more lobes on each side give 99% (notes p. 93). The central lobe covers

<Formula label="The main lobe" tex="-\frac{2\pi}{t_0} < \omega_{fi} < \frac{2\pi}{t_0}, \qquad E_i - \frac{2\pi\hbar}{t_0} < E_f < E_i + \frac{2\pi\hbar}{t_0}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.3.27), (4.3.28)" />

a window of final energies that narrows as $t_0$ grows: for long times the transitions **conserve energy**. If $t_0$ is large enough, even many lobes fit into a band where $\rho$ and $V_{fi}$ do not change, so nothing is lost by extending the limits of the integral to $\pm\infty$ (L10.3 transcript pp. 2–3). [Strictly, $E_f$ cannot run to $-\infty$, since free-particle energies are bounded below; Zwiebach notes it only in passing (L10.3 transcript p. 2), because the far tails contribute nothing.] Then the change of variables

<Formula label="The integral, done" tex="u = \frac{\omega_{fi}t_0}{2}, \quad du = \frac{t_0}{2\hbar}\,dE_f \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad I(t_0) = \frac{\hbar t_0}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2u}{u^2}\,du = \frac{\pi}{2}\,\hbar t_0" cite="reading3" at="p. 3; notes eqs. (4.3.29)–(4.3.31)" />

makes the linear dependence on $t_0$ manifest: the $1/\omega_{fi}^2$ brings in $t_0^2$, the $dE_f$ takes one power away, and the remaining integral is just the number $\pi$ (L10.3 transcript p. 3; the notes change variable through $\omega_{fi}$ instead, with $dE_f = \hbar\,d\omega_{fi}$). This works only because the limits are infinite, so that no $t_0$ hides in them.

## Fermi's golden rule

Putting $I(t_0)$ back, and writing $t$ for $t_0$ now that it is just a time (L10.3 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The transition probability grows linearly" tex="\sum_f P_{fi}(t) = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|V_{fi}|^2\,\rho(E_i)\,t" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.32)" />

The transition rate $w$ is the probability of transition per unit time:

<Formula label="Transition rate" tex="w \equiv \frac{1}{t}\sum_f P_{fi}(t)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.33)" />

and the result is **Fermi's golden rule**, the rate of transitions from a discrete state into a continuum:

<Formula label="Fermi's golden rule (constant perturbation)" tex="w = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|V_{fi}|^2\,\rho(E_f), \qquad E_f = E_i" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.34)" />

The integral over final states has already been done: the matrix element and the density of states are simply evaluated for final states with the initial energy, and nothing more needs integrating (L10.3 transcript p. 4; notes p. 93). The units work: $|V_{fi}|^2$ is energy squared, $\rho$ is one over energy, and dividing by $\hbar$ leaves one over time (L10.3 transcript p. 4). The box size drops out too, as hoped: the final-state wavefunction carries the factor $L^{-3/2}$, so $|V_{fi}|^2 \propto L^{-3}$, which cancels the $L^3$ in $\rho$ (notes eq. (4.3.35), p. 94).

**When does it hold?** Two conditions pull $t_0$ in opposite directions (L10.3 transcript p. 4; notes p. 94). First, $t_0$ must be **long enough** that $\rho$ and $|V_{fi}|^2$ are nearly constant over the window $E_i \pm 2\pi\hbar/t_0$ (or a few lobes more). Second, it must be **short enough** for first order to be valid: since

<Formula label="First order must stay small" tex="\sum_f P_{fi}(t_0) = w\,t_0, \qquad w\,t_0 \ll 1" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.37)" />

the total probability must stay well below 1. Both can be met if the perturbation is weak enough: making $V$ smaller lowers $w$ without changing the window. In physical applications this is the case (L10.3 transcript p. 4). The notes suggest a practical test: compute $w$, choose $t_0$ with $wt_0$ about 0.01, and check that $\rho$ and the matrix element hardly change over an energy range of order $\hbar/t_0$ (notes p. 94).

<WorkedExample title="How continuous is the continuum, and how narrow is the window?">
<Problem>
An atom has a discrete state inside a continuum, and the transition ejects an electron with kinetic energy $E = 27.2$ eV (as in helium below). Suppose the golden rule gives $w = 1.0 \times 10^{12}$ per second (an illustrative value). Put the world in a box of side $L = 1.0$ cm. Use $m = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg, $\hbar = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}$ J s $= 6.582 \times 10^{-16}$ eV s, and 1 eV $= 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ J. (a) Find the electron's wavenumber $k$. (b) Find the density of final states for all directions, and the spacing between neighbouring box states. (c) Following the notes' test, choose $t_0$ with $wt_0 = 0.01$ and find the half-width $2\pi\hbar/t_0$ of the main lobe. Is $\rho$ constant across it? (d) What is $|V_{fi}|^2\rho$, and what happens to its two factors if the box is made twice as large?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The wavenumber">
$E = 27.2 \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ J $= 4.36 \times 10^{-18}$ J, and $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar \approx 2.67 \times 10^{10}$ per metre: a de Broglie wavelength $2\pi/k \approx 0.235$ nm, about the size of an atom.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The density of states">
Integrating $\rho(E)$ over all directions replaces $d\Omega$ by $4\pi$: $\rho = L^3mk/(2\pi^2\hbar^2)$. With $L^3 = 10^{-6}$ cubic metres this is $1.11 \times 10^{41}$ states per joule, or about $1.77 \times 10^{22}$ states per eV. Neighbouring box states are about $5.6 \times 10^{-23}$ eV apart: no instrument could tell this from a true continuum.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The window">
$t_0 = 0.01/w = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ s, ten femtoseconds. The main lobe then reaches $2\pi\hbar/t_0 = 2\pi \times 6.582 \times 10^{-16}/10^{-14} \approx 0.41$ eV on either side of $E$. Since $\rho \propto k \propto \sqrt E$, across the window it changes by only about 0.76% either way (the square root of the ratio of the energies at the edge and at the centre), and the window holds about $1.5 \times 10^{22}$ states. Both conditions of the golden rule are met comfortably. (A rate 100 times larger would force $t_0$ down to 0.1 fs and the half-width up to 41 eV, wider than the 27.2 eV itself: then the golden rule's derivation would fail.)
</Step>
<Step title="(d) The box drops out">
$|V_{fi}|^2\rho = \hbar w/2\pi = 6.582 \times 10^{-16} \times 10^{12}/(2\pi) \approx 1.05 \times 10^{-4}$ eV. Doubling $L$ multiplies $\rho$ by 8 and divides $|V_{fi}|^2$ by 8, since $V_{fi} \propto L^{-3/2}$: the product, and the rate, stay the same.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Auto-ionization of helium

The lecture ends with an application, discussed qualitatively; the rate is not computed (L10.4 transcript p. 1). Its interest is that it shows continuum states where one might not expect them. Take helium: a nucleus with $Z = 2$ and two electrons. Leave out the repulsion between the electrons, and each electron sees the nucleus alone (L10.4 transcript p. 1; notes p. 94):

<Formula label="Helium without the electron repulsion" tex="H^{(0)} = \frac{p_1^2}{2m} - \frac{Ze^2}{r_1} + \frac{p_2^2}{2m} - \frac{Ze^2}{r_2}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.38)" />

[The lecture and the notes write the attraction as $e^2/r_1$, without the $Z$; the energies below include it.] In the lecture, Zwiebach first writes the full Hamiltonian, with the repulsion, and calls it the zeroth-order one; what he then uses, as in the notes, is $H^{(0)}$ without the repulsion, whose eigenstates are hydrogenic. Each electron has its own principal quantum number, and with $Z^2 = 4$ the energies are (L10.4 transcript p. 1)

<Formula label="Hydrogenic energies of helium" tex="E(n_1, n_2) = -(13.6\ \text{eV})\,Z^2\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} + \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right) = -(54.4\ \text{eV})\left(\frac{1}{n_1^2} + \frac{1}{n_2^2}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.39)" />

The spectrum has a surprise (L10.4 transcript pp. 1–2; notes Figure 4.4):

- The ground state $(1,1)$ lies at $-108.8$ eV. [The lecture rounds it to "minus 108".] Above it come $(2,1)$, $(3,1)$, … crowding towards $(\infty,1)$.
- At $(\infty,1)$, one electron is so far out that it is essentially free, and the other is in the 1s state: energy $-54.4$ eV. [The lecture says "54.4" here, without the minus sign.] If the free electron moves, it adds kinetic energy, so at $-54.4$ eV starts a **continuum**, (1s) plus a free electron, extending upwards from there: unlike in hydrogen, it includes states of negative total energy.
- The state $(2,2)$, both electrons in 2s, has $\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2}$ and so lies at $-27.2$ eV: right in the middle of that continuum.
- $(\infty,2)$ at $-13.6$ eV starts a second continuum, (2s) plus a free electron, and so on: infinitely many continua.

So the discrete state (2s)² has the same energy as continuum states in which one electron has dropped to 1s and the other flies off with kinetic energy $-27.2 - (-54.4) = 27.2$ eV:

<Formula label="Auto-ionization of helium" tex="\text{(2s)}^2 \;\rightarrow\; \text{(1s)} + \text{free electron}, \qquad E(2,2) = E(1,\infty) + E_k" cite="reading4" at="p. 2" />

with $E_k$ the free electron's kinetic energy. What drives the transition is the piece left out of $H^{(0)}$, the repulsion between the electrons (notes p. 96):

<Formula label="The perturbation: electron repulsion" tex="H = H^{(0)} + V, \qquad V = \frac{e^2}{|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.3.40)" />

One can picture it this way: by some earlier event both electrons find themselves in 2s; the repulsion then acts as a constant perturbation with good matrix elements to the continuum, and Fermi's golden rule gives a rate for ejecting an electron. These are **Auger** (auto-ionizing) transitions. Decaying by emitting light instead is much less likely (L10.4 transcript p. 3; notes p. 96). The notes add that auto-ionizing states are generally short-lived (notes p. 96).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Reaction rates in the early universe">
Fermi's golden rule is the template for every quantum reaction rate: the squared matrix element of the interaction, times the density of final states. In the early universe such rates decided which reactions kept up with the expansion. In 8.902, the lecture on the thermal history of dark matter compares the mean time between annihilations of dark matter particles with the expansion time of the universe: while annihilations are faster, the particles stay in equilibrium; once they fall behind, their abundance freezes out. The 8.902 notes take the annihilation cross section, the quantity that sets the annihilation rate, as given. Computing it for real particles needs relativistic quantum field theory, which goes beyond this course, but its structure, a squared matrix element times a density of final states, is the one derived here.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Transitions from a discrete state into a continuum are computed for two kinds of perturbation: constant ($V$ switched on at $t = 0$, this lecture) and harmonic ($2H'\cos\omega t$, next lecture).
- A box of side $L$ with periodic boundary conditions makes the continuum countable: $\Delta N = (L/2\pi)^3d^3k$, and $\rho(E) = L^3mk\,d\Omega/(8\pi^3\hbar^2)$ states per unit energy with momentum in $d\Omega$.
- For a constant perturbation, $P_{fi}(t_0) = 4|V_{fi}|^2\sin^2(\omega_{fi}t_0/2)/(E_f - E_i)^2$: it oscillates if $E_f \neq E_i$ (suppressed for large mismatch) and grows as $|V_{fi}|^2t_0^2/\hbar^2$ if $E_f = E_i$.
- Summed over a continuum, the integrand's lobe of height $t_0^2/4$ and width of order $1/t_0$ gives a probability linear in time, from final states within about $2\pi\hbar/t_0$ of $E_i$: energy-conserving transitions.
- Fermi's golden rule: $w = (2\pi/\hbar)|V_{fi}|^2\rho(E_f)$ with $E_f = E_i$, independent of the box size. It needs $t_0$ long enough for a narrow window and short enough that $wt_0 \ll 1$.
- In helium, the doubly excited state (2s)² at $-27.2$ eV lies inside the continuum that starts at $-54.4$ eV, and the electron repulsion makes it auto-ionize, ejecting a 27.2 eV electron.
