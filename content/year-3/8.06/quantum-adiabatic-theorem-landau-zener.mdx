The previous lecture followed a single instantaneous eigenstate of a changing Hamiltonian and found the phases that come close to making it a solution of the Schrödinger equation, but not quite. This lecture states the result properly as the quantum adiabatic theorem, sets up the exact equations in a basis of instantaneous eigenstates to see what the approximation leaves out, and then tests it where it is most at risk: two levels that nearly cross, the Landau–Zener problem (L16.1–L16.5 transcripts; notes sections 3 and 4). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in five short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 6; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## The adiabatic theorem stated

Last time's result was that a state that starts in an instantaneous eigenstate is, at later times, roughly that eigenstate multiplied by two phases, a dynamical one and a geometric one: not an exact solution, but close to one (L16.1 transcript p. 1). To say it precisely, take the whole family of **instantaneous eigenstates** (L16.1 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Instantaneous eigenstates" tex="H(t)\,|\psi_n(t)\rangle = E_n(t)\,|\psi_n(t)\rangle, \qquad n = 1, 2, \ldots" cite="reading6" at="eq. (3.1)" />

where the list may go on forever. They are eigenstates of $H$ at each moment, not solutions of the Schrödinger equation. Start the system in one of them, $|\Psi(0)\rangle = |\psi_k(0)\rangle$, and write $|\Psi(t)\rangle$ for the true state (Zwiebach writes it on the board with a line under the $\psi$, to keep it apart from the instantaneous eigenstates). The level $E_k(t)$ must stay clear of all the others for all times: it must not cross another level, nor touch one (L16.1 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The level must stay separated" tex="E_{k-1}(t) < E_k(t) < E_{k+1}(t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

(The notes ask a little more, that no two levels at all are degenerate, $E_1(t) < E_2(t) < \cdots$, notes p. 7.) Then the **adiabatic theorem** says that if $H(t)$ changes slowly enough, the system stays in that instantaneous eigenstate up to two calculable phases (L16.1 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="The adiabatic theorem" tex="|\Psi(0)\rangle = |\psi_k(0)\rangle \quad\Rightarrow\quad |\Psi(t)\rangle \approx e^{i\theta_k(t)}\,e^{i\gamma_k(t)}\,|\psi_k(t)\rangle" cite="reading6" at="p. 7 and eq. (5.1)" />

<Formula label="The two phases" tex="\theta_k(t) = -\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_0^t E_k(t')\,dt', \qquad \nu_k(t) = i\,\langle\psi_k(t)|\dot\psi_k(t)\rangle, \qquad \gamma_k(t) = \int_0^t \nu_k(t')\,dt'" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (3.12)–(3.14)" />

$\theta_k$ is the **dynamical phase**: for a constant energy it is the familiar $-E_kt/\hbar$ of an energy eigenstate. $\gamma_k$ is the **geometric phase** (notes p. 7); $\nu_k$ is real because $\langle\psi_k|\dot\psi_k\rangle$ is imaginary for a normalized state, as the previous lecture showed. So the theorem says that **the system does not jump to another instantaneous eigenstate**.

How good is "approximately"? That is the hard part of the subject, Zwiebach says, and the lecture motivates the error rather than proving it (L16.1 transcript p. 3). The analysis goes back to Max Born and Vladimir Fock in 1928 and was improved by Tosio Kato [whose [paper](https://doi.org/10.1143/JPSJ.5.435) appeared in 1950; the lecture says "the middle '50s"]. It had a revival when Michael Berry showed that the phase $\gamma_k$ holds real physics and can be observed: the subject of the next lecture (L16.1 transcript p. 3).

**Why phases matter** (L16.1 transcript pp. 3–4). The overall phase of a state is unobservable. But take a superposition of two instantaneous eigenstates,

<Formula label="A superposition" tex="|\Psi(0)\rangle = c_1|\psi_1(0)\rangle + c_2|\psi_2(0)\rangle" cite="reading6" at="eq. (3.2)" />

By linearity each part follows its own instantaneous eigenstate with its own dynamical and geometric phases, and the **relative** phase between the two parts is observable.

## Expanding in instantaneous eigenstates

To see what the approximation neglects, put all the information in (L16.2 transcript p. 1). At each time the instantaneous eigenstates form an orthonormal basis, so any state can be expanded in them:

<Formula label="Expansion of the true state" tex="|\Psi(t)\rangle = \sum_n c_n(t)\,|\psi_n(t)\rangle" cite="reading6" at="eq. (3.3)" />

Both the coefficients and the basis states depend on time, so the Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi\rangle = H|\Psi\rangle$ gives

<Formula label="The Schrödinger equation in this basis" tex="i\hbar\sum_n\left(\dot c_n\,|\psi_n\rangle + c_n\,|\dot\psi_n\rangle\right) = \sum_n c_n\,E_n\,|\psi_n\rangle" cite="reading6" at="eq. (3.4)" />

Act with $\langle\psi_k(t)|$. Orthonormality picks out $\dot c_k$ on the left and $E_kc_k$ on the right, but $\langle\psi_k|\dot\psi_n\rangle$ is not zero for $n \neq k$, so a sum survives. Taking its $n = k$ term apart (L16.2 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="The exact equations for the coefficients" tex="i\hbar\,\dot c_k = \left(E_k - i\hbar\,\langle\psi_k|\dot\psi_k\rangle\right)c_k - i\hbar\sum_{n \neq k}\langle\psi_k|\dot\psi_n\rangle\,c_n" cite="reading6" at="eq. (3.5)" />

If the last sum is dropped, each coefficient evolves on its own, and the first-order equation integrates at once (L16.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Without the coupling" tex="c_k(t) = c_k(0)\;e^{\frac{1}{i\hbar}\int_0^t\left(E_k(t') - i\hbar\langle\psi_k|\dot\psi_k\rangle\right)dt'} = c_k(0)\,e^{i\theta_k(t)}\,e^{i\gamma_k(t)}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (3.11)" />

The $E_k$ part of the exponent gives $e^{i\theta_k}$ and the other part $e^{i\gamma_k}$, since $-\langle\psi_k|\dot\psi_k\rangle = i\nu_k$: exactly the adiabatic result, now seen as the solution with the coupling neglected.

**The coupling does not vanish** (L16.2 transcript p. 3). Start in state $k$: $c_k(0) = 1$ and every other $c_m(0) = 0$. Apply the exact equation to a coefficient $c_m$ with $m \neq k$ at $t = 0$. Its first term vanishes, and of the sum only the $n = k$ term survives:

<Formula label="The other states fill up at once" tex="i\hbar\,\dot c_m(0) = -i\hbar\,\langle\psi_m(0)|\dot\psi_k(0)\rangle, \qquad m \neq k" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

which is not zero in general. The other instantaneous eigenstates start to be populated immediately, so it is not exactly true that the system stays in state $k$. The only hope is that this coupling is small.

## The coupling and the error

To see when it is small, differentiate the defining equation of the instantaneous eigenstates, $H|\psi_n\rangle = E_n|\psi_n\rangle$, which knows how they change in time (L16.3 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Differentiating the eigenvalue equation" tex="\dot H\,|\psi_n\rangle + H\,|\dot\psi_n\rangle = \dot E_n\,|\psi_n\rangle + E_n\,|\dot\psi_n\rangle" cite="reading6" at="eq. (3.7)" />

Act with $\langle\psi_k|$ for $k \neq n$: $H$ acts to the left and gives $E_k$, and the $\dot E_n$ term vanishes by orthogonality, leaving $\langle\psi_k|\dot H|\psi_n\rangle + E_k\langle\psi_k|\dot\psi_n\rangle = E_n\langle\psi_k|\dot\psi_n\rangle$ (notes eq. (3.8)). Hence

<Formula label="The coupling is set by the rate of change of H" tex="\langle\psi_k|\dot\psi_n\rangle = \frac{\langle\psi_k|\dot H|\psi_n\rangle}{E_n - E_k} = \frac{\dot H_{kn}}{E_n - E_k}, \qquad k \neq n" cite="reading6" at="eq. (3.9)" />

A caution from the lecture: $\dot H_{kn}$ is the $kn$ matrix element of the operator $\dot H$, not the time derivative of the matrix element $H_{kn}$, since the basis itself moves (L16.3 transcript pp. 1–2). The equations for the coefficients become

<Formula label="The coefficient equations with the coupling made explicit" tex="i\hbar\,\dot c_k = \left(E_k - i\hbar\,\langle\psi_k|\dot\psi_k\rangle\right)c_k - i\hbar\sum_{n \neq k}\frac{\dot H_{kn}}{E_n - E_k}\,c_n" cite="reading6" at="eq. (3.10)" />

If the sum vanished, $|c_k|$ would stay equal to one: start in $|\psi_k\rangle$ and you stay there (notes p. 8). The form of the coupling explains the conditions of the theorem: it is small when **the Hamiltonian changes slowly** (small $\dot H$) and **the levels are far apart** (large $E_n - E_k$), which is why a level must never come close to another.

**How the error depends on the time of the change** (L16.3 transcript pp. 2–3). Picture a Hamiltonian that is constant before $t = 0$, changes during a time $T$, and is constant again afterwards. A simple model is a straight ramp:

<Formula label="A ramped Hamiltonian" tex="H(t) = H_0 + \frac{t}{T}\,V \quad (0 \le t \le T), \qquad \dot H = \frac{V}{T}, \qquad \dot H_{kn} = \frac{V_{kn}}{T}" cite="reading3" at="p. 2" />

with $H(t) = H_0 + V$ after $T$. The longer the change takes, the smaller $\dot H$, and the coupling term in the equations is proportional to $1/T$. It takes more work to show that integrating the equations does not make the error grow, but that is what the theorem states. With the norm of a state (the square root of its inner product with itself) as the measure of the difference:

<Formula label="The adiabatic theorem as a bound" tex="\Big\|\,|\Psi(t)\rangle - e^{i\theta_k(t)}e^{i\gamma_k(t)}|\psi_k(t)\rangle\,\Big\| \le \frac{C}{T}" cite="reading3" at="p. 3" />

for $0 \le t \le T$, with $C$ a constant that does not depend on $T$; after the change has stopped the error does not grow. [The transcript has "a number of times T" at this point, a slip of the tongue: the sentences around it say the error goes as $1/T$.] So a slow enough change makes the adiabatic state as close as we like to the true one.

## Levels that nearly cross

The best test of the theorem is a case that can be solved: the **Landau–Zener transition**. Lev Landau treated it first and Clarence Zener more carefully (Zwiebach mentions that Zener reportedly found a factor of two error in Landau's work), and Zener's paper remains a fine illustration of the physics (L16.4 transcript p. 1).

Their interest was molecules. Hold the nuclei fixed a distance $R$ apart and solve for the electrons (L16.4 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Electronic states at fixed separation" tex="H(R)\,\psi_1(x;R) = E_1(R)\,\psi_1(x;R)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.1)" />

and the same for a second configuration $\psi_2$, where $x$ stands for the electrons' coordinates. Plotted against $R$, the two energies may come very close near some separation $R_0$ without meeting. Their character swaps there: the configuration that is non-polar on one side becomes polar (a permanent dipole moment) on the other, and the other way round (L16.4 transcript p. 2).

Now let the separation change in time, $R = R(t)$, as in a collision or a reaction. A simple but important point: if the eigenvalue problem has been solved **for every** $R$, it holds at every instant with $R = R(t)$, so $\psi_1(x;R(t))$ and $\psi_2(x;R(t))$ **are** the instantaneous eigenstates, with energies $E_1(R(t))$ and $E_2(R(t))$ (L16.4 transcript p. 2). Plotted against time, the levels look much as they did against $R$. The adiabatic theorem says a slow change keeps the system on its level, $\psi_1$ staying $\psi_1$; but around the time when $R$ passes $R_0$, where the gap is small, a fast change may make it jump to the other level: a **non-adiabatic transition** (L16.4 transcript p. 3; notes p. 9). How likely is the jump?

## A crossing that is not avoided

Start with a toy model, a two-level system with only diagonal entries, $\alpha > 0$ (L16.4 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Two crossing levels" tex="H(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\alpha t}{2} & 0 \\ 0 & -\frac{\alpha t}{2} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad E_1(t) = \frac{\alpha t}{2}, \qquad E_2(t) = -\frac{\alpha t}{2}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.2)" />

The matrix is a fixed matrix times $t$, so its eigenvectors do not change: the instantaneous eigenstates are the same at all times,

<Formula label="Time-independent instantaneous eigenstates" tex="|\psi_1\rangle = |1\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\psi_2\rangle = |2\rangle = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.3)" />

and the two levels cross at $t = 0$. Here the adiabatic states are **exact** solutions, because nothing couples $|1\rangle$ to $|2\rangle$ (L16.4 transcript p. 4). With $\frac{1}{i\hbar}\int_0^t \frac{\alpha t'}{2}\,dt' = -\frac{i\alpha t^2}{4\hbar}$:

<Formula label="Exact solutions" tex="|\Psi_1(t)\rangle = e^{-i\alpha t^2/4\hbar}\,|1\rangle, \qquad |\Psi_2(t)\rangle = e^{+i\alpha t^2/4\hbar}\,|2\rangle" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (4.4), (4.5)" />

Differentiating $|\Psi_1\rangle$ brings down $E_1(t) = \alpha t/2$, which is just what $H$ gives acting on $|1\rangle$, so $i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi_1\rangle = H|\Psi_1\rangle$ holds. A system in $|1\rangle$ at a large negative time stays in $|1\rangle$ forever: it passes straight through the crossing, oblivious of the other level. Crossing levels are special, Zwiebach stresses; they need a Hamiltonian with no coupling between the two states (L16.4 transcript p. 4).

## The avoided crossing

Now add a constant off-diagonal term $H_{12}$ (L16.5 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="The Landau–Zener Hamiltonian" tex="H(t) = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\alpha t}{2} & H_{12} \\ H_{12}^* & -\frac{\alpha t}{2} \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.6)" />

At $t = 0$ only the off-diagonal entries remain, and the eigenvalues are $\pm|H_{12}|$. At large $|t|$ the diagonal entries dominate, so the levels approach the old lines $\pm\alpha t/2$, with eigenstates close to $|1\rangle$ and $|2\rangle$. A short calculation gives the levels at all times:

<Formula label="The instantaneous energies" tex="E_\pm(t) = \pm\sqrt{|H_{12}|^2 + \frac{\alpha^2t^2}{4}}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.8)" />

The two hyperbolas never meet: the **smallest gap**, at $t = 0$, is $E_+ - E_- = 2|H_{12}|$. Following the upper level from $t \to -\infty$ to $t \to +\infty$, its eigenstate turns from $|2\rangle$ (on the line $-\alpha t/2$, which is high at negative $t$) into $|1\rangle$; the lower level does the reverse (L16.5 transcript p. 1). This is the molecule's swap of polar and non-polar character. (The notes also write this Hamiltonian in spin form, as a vector a = (H₁₂, 0, αt/2) dotted into the vector of Pauli matrices σ, for a real H₁₂, so that the instantaneous eigenstates are spin states along a direction that turns from −z to +z as t runs from −∞ to +∞; notes eqs. (4.7), (4.9), (4.10). The lecture does not use this form.)

  curves={[
    { expr: "sqrt(g^2 + t^2/4)", label: "E₊" },
    { expr: "-sqrt(g^2 + t^2/4)", label: "E₋" },

The levels avoid each other with a smallest gap 2|H₁₂| at t = 0; slide |H₁₂| to zero and they become the crossing lines." />

As $H_{12} \to 0$ the hyperbolas collapse onto the crossing lines and we are back to the toy model, where the system goes straight through. So for a very small $H_{12}$ the system is **likely to jump**: it stays on the line it was following, which takes it to the other branch, a non-adiabatic transition. For well separated branches a jump is very unlikely (L16.5 transcript pp. 1–2). The problem can be solved exactly (the solution involves special functions related to the hypergeometric ones), and the course solves it numerically in the homework; many papers have tried to give simple derivations of the answer (L16.5 transcript p. 2).

## When is the passage adiabatic?

Zwiebach compares two time scales (L16.5 transcript pp. 2–3). Draw horizontal tangents to the hyperbolas at $\pm|H_{12}|$ and let them meet the lines $\pm\alpha t/2$, forming a box. The line $\alpha t/2$ reaches the height $|H_{12}|$ at $t = 2\tau_d$, where

<Formula label="The duration of the change" tex="\frac{\alpha}{2}\,(2\tau_d) = |H_{12}| \quad\Rightarrow\quad \tau_d = \frac{|H_{12}|}{\alpha}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.11)" />

(the notes write it with the subscript α instead of d). Inside the box the Hamiltonian turns from its early form into its late form, so $\tau_d$ is the time scale of the change. The second time scale belongs to the system itself. Near $t = 0$ the Hamiltonian is roughly the off-diagonal matrix with entries $H_{12}$ and $H_{12}^*$, and a two-state system with such a Hamiltonian oscillates between $|1\rangle$ and $|2\rangle$, as in Rabi oscillations met in 8.05:

<Formula label="Rabi frequency and period" tex="\omega_{12} = \frac{|H_{12}|}{\hbar}, \qquad T_{12} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_{12}}" cite="reading6" at="p. 11 and eq. (4.13)" />

[The lecture describes the levels at $t = 0$ as separated by $H_{12}$; they sit at $\pm|H_{12}|$, so the gap is $2|H_{12}|$, and $\omega_{12}$ is half the gap divided by $\hbar$. Both the lecture and the notes define $\omega_{12}$ this way.] The passage is **adiabatic** when the change is slow compared with this oscillation, $\tau_d \gg T_{12}$, that is, up to factors of order one (L16.5 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The adiabatic condition" tex="\omega_{12}\,\tau_d \gg 1 \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \frac{|H_{12}|^2}{\hbar\,\alpha} \gg 1" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.12)" />

Both ingredients make sense: widely separated branches (large $|H_{12}|$) are hard to jump, and a gentle slope (small $\alpha$) means a slow change.

The notes add a second way to check this, not done in the lecture (notes p. 12). By analogy with the WKB condition that the local wavelength vary slowly, ask that $\hbar/E_+$, the time scale set by the energy, vary slowly: $|d(\hbar/E_+)/dt| \ll 1$. Differentiating gives $d(\hbar/E_+)/dt = -\hbar\alpha^2t/(4E_+^3)$, whose size is largest at $\alpha^2t^2/4 = |H_{12}|^2/2$, where it equals $\hbar\alpha/(3\sqrt{3}\,|H_{12}|^2)$. So the condition is again $|H_{12}|^2/\hbar\alpha \gg 1$, up to a factor of order one. [The notes' version of this derivative, eq. (4.17), drops the factor $\frac14$; it does not change the conclusion.]

## The Landau–Zener formula

The exact solution gives the probability that the system jumps the gap, ending on the other branch, a **non-adiabatic transition** (L16.5 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Landau–Zener probability of a non-adiabatic transition" tex="P = e^{-2\pi\,\omega_{12}\,\tau_d} = e^{-2\pi|H_{12}|^2/\hbar\alpha}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (4.18)" />

The jump is suppressed exponentially by the adiabatic parameter. A system that starts as $|1\rangle$ at a large negative time (on the lower branch) ends as $|1\rangle$ on the upper branch with probability $P$, having kept its character, and on the lower branch, now $|2\rangle$, with probability $1 - P$, having followed its level as the adiabatic theorem says. Zwiebach leaves the check of this formula to the homework.

<WorkedExample title="Sweeping through an avoided crossing">
<Problem>
A two-level system, such as an electron shared between two neighbouring quantum dots, has a coupling $|H_{12}| = 0.20$ μeV between its two states, and their diagonal energies are swept past each other at the rate $\alpha = 1.0$ μeV/ns. Take $\hbar = 6.582 \times 10^{-16}$ eV s. (a) Find $\tau_d$, $\omega_{12}$, $T_{12}$ and the probability of a non-adiabatic transition. (b) How slow must the sweep be for that probability to be 1%? (c) Compare $\tau_d$ with $T_{12}$ in that case.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A fast sweep">
The smallest gap is $2|H_{12}| = 0.40$ μeV. The duration of the change is $\tau_d = |H_{12}|/\alpha = 0.20$ ns. The Rabi frequency is $\omega_{12} = |H_{12}|/\hbar = 0.20 \times 10^{-6}/(6.582 \times 10^{-16})$, about $3.04 \times 10^{8}$ per second, with period $T_{12} = 2\pi/\omega_{12} \approx 20.7$ ns. The adiabatic parameter is $\omega_{12}\tau_d = |H_{12}|^2/\hbar\alpha \approx 0.061$, far from large, and

$P = e^{-2\pi \times 0.061} \approx 0.68$.

The system jumps the gap more often than not: about two times in three it keeps its character and ends on the other branch.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A slow enough sweep">
$P = 0.01$ needs $2\pi|H_{12}|^2/\hbar\alpha = \ln 100$, so $|H_{12}|^2/\hbar\alpha = \ln 100/2\pi \approx 0.733$ and

$\alpha = 2\pi|H_{12}|^2/(\hbar\ln 100) \approx 0.083$ μeV/ns,

about 12 times slower than in (a).
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The two time scales">
Now $\tau_d = |H_{12}|/\alpha \approx 2.4$ ns, still about 8.6 times shorter than $T_{12} \approx 20.7$ ns, yet the passage is already 99% adiabatic. The condition $\tau_d \gg T_{12}$ is an order-of-magnitude statement: the $2\pi$ in the exponent means that $\omega_{12}\tau_d$ of order one is enough. A sweep slower again by a factor of two, with $\omega_{12}\tau_d \approx 1.47$, would give $P = e^{-2\pi \times 1.47} \approx 10^{-4}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- **Instantaneous eigenstates** satisfy $H(t)|\psi_n(t)\rangle = E_n(t)|\psi_n(t)\rangle$ at each moment; they are not solutions of the Schrödinger equation.
- **The adiabatic theorem:** starting in $|\psi_k(0)\rangle$, with $E_k(t)$ kept apart from every other level and $H$ changing slowly, $|\Psi(t)\rangle \approx e^{i\theta_k}e^{i\gamma_k}|\psi_k(t)\rangle$, with the dynamical phase $\theta_k = -\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_0^t E_k\,dt'$ and the geometric phase $\gamma_k = \int_0^t i\langle\psi_k|\dot\psi_k\rangle\,dt'$. Relative phases of superpositions are observable. Born and Fock (1928) proved it, and Kato (1950) sharpened the proof.
- **Exactly**, with $|\Psi\rangle = \sum c_n|\psi_n\rangle$: $i\hbar\dot c_k = (E_k - i\hbar\langle\psi_k|\dot\psi_k\rangle)c_k - i\hbar\sum_{n \neq k}\frac{\dot H_{kn}}{E_n - E_k}c_n$. The adiabatic result is the solution without the coupling term, which is small when $H$ changes slowly and the levels are far apart.
- For a change lasting a time $T$ the coupling goes as $1/T$, and the theorem bounds the error in the state by a constant over $T$.
- **Landau–Zener:** levels $\pm\alpha t/2$ coupled by $H_{12}$ avoid each other, $E_\pm = \pm\sqrt{|H_{12}|^2 + \alpha^2t^2/4}$, with smallest gap $2|H_{12}|$. With $\tau_d = |H_{12}|/\alpha$ and $\omega_{12} = |H_{12}|/\hbar$, the passage is adiabatic when $\omega_{12}\tau_d = |H_{12}|^2/\hbar\alpha \gg 1$, and the probability of jumping the gap is $P = e^{-2\pi|H_{12}|^2/\hbar\alpha}$.
