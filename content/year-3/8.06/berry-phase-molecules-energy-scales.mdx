The adiabatic theorem left a state that follows its instantaneous eigenstate with two phases: a dynamical one, which runs like a clock, and a geometric one. This lecture shows what is geometric about the second: written in terms of the parameters of the Hamiltonian, it depends only on the path those parameters take, not on how long the journey lasts. That is **Berry's phase**. The lecture then begins a new subject in which adiabatic thinking is essential, molecules, and works out their energy scales (L17.1–L17.4 transcripts; notes sections 5 and 6). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 6; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked examples.)

## The two phases again

Zwiebach first restates the adiabatic theorem in a mathematically careful form (L17.1 transcript p. 1). A Hamiltonian is constant before $t = 0$, changes during a time $T$, and is constant afterwards. If the system starts in the instantaneous eigenstate $|\psi_n(0)\rangle$, then for every time between $0$ and $T$ the true state $|\Psi(t)\rangle$ differs from the instantaneous eigenstate $|\psi_n(t)\rangle$, given a suitable phase, by a state whose norm is of order $1/T$. (The norm of a state is the square root of its inner product with itself.) The suitable phase was found last time (L17.1 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="The adiabatic state" tex="|\Psi(t)\rangle \approx e^{i\theta_n(t)}\,e^{i\gamma_n(t)}\,|\psi_n(t)\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.1), p. 12" />

<Formula label="The dynamical phase" tex="\theta_n(t) = -\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_0^t E_n(t')\,dt'" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.2), p. 12" />

<Formula label="The geometric phase" tex="\nu_n(t) = i\,\langle\psi_n(t)|\dot\psi_n(t)\rangle, \qquad \gamma_n(t) = \int_0^t \nu_n(t')\,dt'" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (5.3), (5.4), p. 12" />

(Last lecture's page used the label $k$ for the starting state; this lecture and the notes use $n$.) For a time-independent Hamiltonian $\theta_n$ is the familiar $-E_nt/\hbar$. $\nu_n$ is real, because $\langle\psi_n|\dot\psi_n\rangle$ is imaginary for a normalized state. The dynamical phase is like a clock: the longer you wait, the more it changes. The geometric phase $\gamma_n$, properly viewed, does **not** depend on how long the change takes, which is very unusual, and is what this lecture sets out to show (L17.1 transcript p. 2).

## Configuration space

Forget the time for a moment and think of a Hamiltonian that depends on a set of parameters, a magnetic field, an electric field, a spring constant, collected into a vector with $N$ components (L17.1 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="The parameters of the Hamiltonian" tex="\vec R = (R_1, R_2, \ldots, R_{N})" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.5), p. 13" />

The space of these parameters is the **configuration space**. Its axes are abstract, not directions in ordinary space: one could be a magnetic field, another a spring constant. Suppose we have learned to solve the Hamiltonian for **every** value of the parameters (L17.1 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Eigenstates for every value of the parameters" tex="H(\vec R)\,|\psi_n(\vec R)\rangle = E_n(\vec R)\,|\psi_n(\vec R)\rangle, \qquad n = 1, 2, \ldots" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.6), p. 13" />

with orthonormal eigenstates. Now let the parameters depend on time, $\vec R = \vec R(t)$. As time runs, the point $\vec R(t)$ moves: the evolution of the system is a **path** in configuration space, parametrized by time, from $\vec R(0)$ to $\vec R(T)$ (L17.1 transcript p. 3). The Hamiltonian $H(\vec R(t))$ becomes time dependent, and because the eigenvalue equation holds for every $\vec R$, it holds in particular at $\vec R(t)$: the states $|\psi_n(\vec R(t))\rangle$ **are** the instantaneous eigenstates (L17.1 transcript p. 4). Knowing the eigenstates everywhere in configuration space gives the instantaneous eigenstates of any motion through it, the same observation that made the molecular states of last lecture's Landau–Zener problem instantaneous eigenstates.

## The geometric phase as a line integral

Now write $\nu_n$ in this language, with the dot as a time derivative (L17.2 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="The rate in terms of the parameters" tex="\nu_n(t) = i\,\langle\psi_n(\vec R(t))|\,\frac{d}{dt}\,|\psi_n(\vec R(t))\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.7), p. 13" />

The ket depends on time only through the $N$ parameters, so the chain rule gives a sum over them, which is the gradient in configuration space, $\nabla_{R}$, dotted with the velocity of the point (L17.2 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="The chain rule" tex="\frac{d}{dt}|\psi_n(\vec R(t))\rangle = \sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{\partial}{\partial R_j}|\psi_n(\vec R(t))\rangle\,\frac{dR_j}{dt} = \nabla_{R}|\psi_n(\vec R(t))\rangle\cdot\frac{d\vec R}{dt}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.8), p. 13" />

The gradient of a ket is a list of $N$ kets; the bra turns them into $N$ numbers, the components of a vector dotted with $d\vec R/dt$. So

<Formula label="The geometric phase as a time integral" tex="\gamma_n(t) = \int_0^t i\,\langle\psi_n(\vec R(t'))|\nabla_{R}|\psi_n(\vec R(t'))\rangle\cdot\frac{d\vec R}{dt'}\,dt'" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (5.9), (5.10), p. 13" />

Here is the step that makes all the difference, Zwiebach says: the $dt'$ cancels (L17.2 transcript p. 2). What is left is an integral along the path in configuration space, from the initial point $\vec R_i = \vec R(0)$ to the final point $\vec R_f$:

<Formula label="Berry's phase" tex="\gamma_n = \int_{\vec R_i}^{\vec R_f} i\,\langle\psi_n(\vec R)|\nabla_{R}|\psi_n(\vec R)\rangle\cdot d\vec R" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.11), p. 13" />

**Time plays no role any more**: take a small step $d\vec R$ along the path, dot it with the vector at that point, and add up. Whether the system took one minute or a billion years to travel the path, the geometric phase is exactly the same (L17.2 transcript p. 2). The dynamical phase, in contrast, grows in proportion to the time taken. The phase is named after Michael Berry, whose [1984 paper](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1984.0023) drew attention to it. Formulas of this kind, Zwiebach remarks, were in many ways known to everybody for a long time; Berry, he suggests, probably clarified the independence of time and emphasized that the phase could be interesting in some cases (L17.2 transcript p. 3).

## Berry's connection

The vector inside the integral gets a name (L17.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The Berry connection" tex="\vec A_n(\vec R) = i\,\langle\psi_n(\vec R)|\nabla_{R}|\psi_n(\vec R)\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.12), p. 13" />

<Formula label="Berry's phase in terms of the connection" tex="\gamma_n = \int_{\vec R_i}^{\vec R_f}\vec A_n(\vec R)\cdot d\vec R" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.13), p. 13" />

The **Berry connection** is a vector with $N$ components living in configuration space: it has a value at every point. There is one for each eigenstate, labelled by $n$ (L17.2 transcript pp. 2–3). Why "connection" rather than "vector"? Because it changes in a special way when the eigenstates are redefined, just as a vector potential does under a gauge transformation.

## Changing the phases of the eigenstates

The instantaneous eigenstates are far from unique. Each can be multiplied by a phase that depends on the parameters, with $\beta(\vec R)$ any real function (L17.2 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Redefined eigenstates" tex="|\psi_n'(\vec R)\rangle = e^{-i\beta(\vec R)}\,|\psi_n(\vec R)\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.14), p. 13" />

These are just as good: $H(\vec R)$ does not act on a function of the parameters (the $R_j$ are numbers, not operators), so the new states are still normalized, orthogonal eigenstates with the same energies. Two people solving a messy problem would most likely choose different phases (L17.2 transcript p. 5). (The notes make the same remark earlier, in the language of time alone: instantaneous eigenstates are ambiguous, since each may be multiplied by an arbitrary phase that depends on time, notes eq. (2.18), p. 7. Here that phase is $\beta(\vec R(t))$, a function of the parameters.) The new connection is (L17.2 transcript p. 4)

<Formula label="The connection transforms like a vector potential" tex="\vec A_n'(\vec R) = i\,\langle\psi_n|e^{i\beta}\,\nabla_{R}\,e^{-i\beta}|\psi_n\rangle = \vec A_n(\vec R) + \nabla_{R}\beta(\vec R)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (5.15), (5.16), p. 14" />

When $\nabla_{R}$ acts on the ket the exponentials cancel and the old connection comes back; when it acts on $e^{-i\beta}$ it brings down $-i\nabla_{R}\beta$, which the $i$ in front turns into $+\nabla_{R}\beta$, times $\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle = 1$. This is exactly how a vector potential changes under a gauge transformation, by the gradient of a function, but in configuration space, not in real space; hence the name connection (L17.2 transcript p. 4).

## Open and closed paths

What happens to Berry's phase? Integrating the new connection along the same path (L17.2 transcript pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="The phase of an open path changes" tex="\gamma'_n = \gamma_n + \int_{\vec R_i}^{\vec R_f}\nabla_{R}\beta\cdot d\vec R = \gamma_n + \beta(\vec R_f) - \beta(\vec R_i)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (5.17), (5.18), p. 14" />

just as the line integral of an electric field that is a gradient is the difference of the potential at the two ends. So for a general path the Berry phase is **not gauge invariant**, and what is not gauge invariant cannot be measured: everyone would get a different answer, depending on their choice of phases (L17.2 transcript p. 5).

There is one way out. If the path in configuration space **ends where it began**, $\vec R_f = \vec R_i$, the two terms cancel. So the observable Berry phase is the one accumulated along a **closed path**, and it is gauge invariant (L17.2 transcript pp. 5–6; notes p. 14). [One refinement that neither the lecture nor the notes spell out: the new eigenstates must be single-valued, so $e^{-i\beta}$ must return to its starting value round the loop, but $\beta$ itself may then change by a multiple of $2\pi$. The closed-path phase is gauge invariant up to a multiple of $2\pi$, which is all that matters for the factor $e^{i\gamma_n}$. The worked example below shows such a case.]

## When there is no Berry phase

Two cases give nothing (L17.3 transcript pp. 1–2).

**Real eigenstates.** If the instantaneous eigenstates can be chosen real all along the path, $\nu_n = 0$. We know $\langle\psi_n|\dot\psi_n\rangle$ is imaginary, but for a real wavefunction it is a real integral, so it must vanish. Directly (L17.3 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="No geometric phase for real eigenstates" tex="\nu_n = i\int dx\,\psi_n(x, t)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi_n(x, t) = \frac{i}{2}\,\frac{d}{dt}\int dx\,\psi_n^2(x, t) = 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.19), p. 14" />

since the wavefunction stays normalized. So with real instantaneous eigenstates, "don't even think of Berry's phase", as Zwiebach puts it.

**One parameter.** If the configuration space is one-dimensional, a closed path has to retrace itself: it goes out and comes back along the same line, and the integral back cancels the integral out (L17.3 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="No Berry phase with one parameter" tex="\oint A_n(R)\,dR = 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.20), p. 14" />

A Berry phase needs at least two parameters.

## Berry's curvature

In a three-dimensional configuration space, with axes $R_1, R_2, R_3$ (the perfect place to confuse yourself, Zwiebach warns, since ordinary space is three-dimensional too), take a closed path $C$ and a surface $S$ whose boundary it is. Stokes' theorem turns the loop integral into a flux (L17.3 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Berry's phase as a flux" tex="\gamma_n[C] = \oint_{C} \vec A_n\cdot d\vec R = \int_S \left(\nabla_{R}\times\vec A_n\right)\cdot d\vec S = \int_S \vec D_n\cdot d\vec S" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.21), p. 14" />

<Formula label="The Berry curvature" tex="\vec D_n = \nabla_{R}\times\vec A_n(\vec R)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.22), p. 14" />

In electromagnetism the loop integral of the vector potential is the magnetic flux through the loop. Here the Berry connection plays the vector potential and the **Berry curvature** $\vec D_n$ plays the magnetic field: the name "curvature" comes from the language in which a magnetic field is the curvature of its connection (L17.3 transcript p. 2). The notes add what the lecture leaves implicit: the curvature does not change when the eigenstates are redefined, since the curl of a gradient vanishes, $\nabla_{R}\times(\vec A_n + \nabla_{R}\beta) = \nabla_{R}\times\vec A_n$ (notes eq. (5.23), p. 15).

## A spin in a turning magnetic field

The classic example, which Zwiebach leaves to recitation as an exercise, is a spin one-half in a magnetic field of fixed size whose direction $\hat n(t)$ is slowly turned (L17.3 transcript pp. 2–3). The lecture does not write the Hamiltonian; the notes take an electron (notes p. 15):

<Formula label="An electron in a field of turning direction" tex="H(t) = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B(t) = \mu_B\,B\,\hat n(t)\cdot\vec\sigma" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.24), p. 15" />

The configuration space is the space of the field $\vec B$, and the path lies on a sphere of radius $B$ in it. The eigenstates have energies $\pm\mu_B B$ (notes eq. (5.25)). **This page's convention**, for the sign: $\chi_{n+}$ is the state with spin **up along** $\hat n$ (eigenvalue $+1$ of $\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma$), $\chi_{n-}$ the state with spin down. For the electron, whose magnetic moment points against its spin, $\chi_{n+}$ is the upper level, $+\mu_B B$; this is the notes' χ₊, and the lecture's "spin up" state. (A Berry phase depends only on the family of states, not on their energies, so the result below holds for spin up along $\hat n$ whatever the sign of the coupling.)

If $\hat n$ turns slowly compared with $\hbar/(\mu_B B)$, the spin follows the field, staying up along $\hat n$, with only a small chance of flipping. When $\hat n$ traces a closed loop $C$ on the sphere of directions, the Berry phase is (L17.3 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Berry's phase of a spin one-half" tex="\gamma_\pm[C] = \mp\frac{\Omega}{2}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (5.27), p. 15" />

where $\Omega$ is the **solid angle** the loop encloses, as seen from the centre of the sphere: minus one half (the spin) times the solid angle for spin up. The phase is set by the geometry of the loop alone. The lecture states the result; the notes state the curvature, $\vec D_\pm = \mp\hat r/(2r^2)$, and integrate its flux, where $r = B$ is the distance from the origin of field space and $\hat r$ the radial unit vector. (In the language of the analogy, not used in the lecture, this is the field of a "magnetic monopole" sitting at $\vec B = 0$, the one point where the two levels meet.) Its flux through the part of the sphere inside the loop is $\mp\Omega/2$ (notes eq. (5.27)). The worked example finds the same answer from the connection directly. (Here $\Omega$ is measured with the loop running anticlockwise as seen from outside the sphere; reversing the loop reverses the sign.)

<WorkedExample title="A spin taken round a cone">
<Problem>
An electron sits in a magnetic field of fixed size $B = 10$ mT. The field direction is turned once round a cone of half-angle $\alpha = 60°$ about the $z$ axis, $\hat n = (\sin\alpha\cos\phi, \sin\alpha\sin\phi, \cos\alpha)$ with $\phi$ running from $0$ to $2\pi$, in a time $T = 1.0$ μs. The spin starts up along $\hat n$. Take $\mu_B = 5.788 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/T and $\hbar = 6.582 \times 10^{-16}$ eV s. (a) Is the motion adiabatic? (b) Find the Berry phase from the connection, using $\chi_{n+} = \cos\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\chi_{z+} + \sin\frac{\alpha}{2}\,e^{i\phi}\,\chi_{z-}$, and compare with $-\Omega/2$. (c) Find the dynamical phase, and what happens to both phases if the turn takes twice as long. (d) Repeat (b) with the eigenstates multiplied by $e^{-i\phi}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The time scales">
The splitting sets the time scale of the spin: $\mu_B B = 5.788 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.010$, about $5.79 \times 10^{-7}$ eV, and $\hbar/(\mu_B B) \approx 1.14$ ns. The turn takes $T = 1.0$ μs, about 880 times longer, so the spin follows the field.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The Berry phase">
Along the path only $\phi$ changes, so the line integral is $\gamma_+ = \int_0^{2\pi} i\langle\chi_{n+}|\frac{d}{d\phi}\chi_{n+}\rangle\,d\phi$. The derivative is $\frac{d}{d\phi}\chi_{n+} = i\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}\,e^{i\phi}\,\chi_{z-}$, and its inner product with $\chi_{n+}$ is $i\sin^2\frac{\alpha}{2}$. So the integrand is $-\sin^2\frac{\alpha}{2}$, a constant, and

$\gamma_+ = -2\pi\sin^2\frac{\alpha}{2} = -\pi(1 - \cos\alpha) = -\pi/2$ for $\alpha = 60°$.

The cap of the sphere inside the cone has solid angle $\Omega = 2\pi(1 - \cos\alpha) = \pi$, so indeed $\gamma_+ = -\Omega/2$. A spin that started down along $\hat n$ would get $+\pi/2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The dynamical phase">
$\theta_+ = -\mu_B BT/\hbar \approx -879$ rad, more than a hundred full turns. If the field is turned twice as slowly, $\theta_+$ doubles to about $-1759$ rad, while $\gamma_+$ stays $-\pi/2$: it depends on the cone, not on the time. For a spin in a superposition of up and down along $\hat n$, each part follows its own state, and their relative phase gains $\gamma_+ - \gamma_- = -\Omega = -\pi$ on top of the dynamical part.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Another choice of phases">
The states $e^{-i\phi}\chi_{n+} = e^{-i\phi}\cos\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\chi_{z+} + \sin\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\chi_{z-}$ are an equally good, single-valued choice, with $\beta = \phi$. Now the derivative is $-i\,e^{-i\phi}\cos\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\chi_{z+}$, the integrand is $\cos^2\frac{\alpha}{2}$, and the phase is $2\pi\cos^2\frac{\alpha}{2} = \pi(1 + \cos\alpha) = 3\pi/2$. That is $-\pi/2 + 2\pi$: since $\beta$ grew by $2\pi$ round the loop, the phase changed by $2\pi$, and $e^{i\gamma_+} = -i$ in both cases, as the gauge argument promised.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Molecules

The second half of the lecture opens the chapter on molecules and the **Born–Oppenheimer approximation** (L17.4 transcript p. 1). Molecules are much harder than atoms. Even atoms with several electrons are hard, because of the repulsion between electrons, but in an atom the nucleus creates a spherically symmetric potential. In a molecule the nuclei sit apart, the spherical symmetry is gone whether there is one electron or many, and the tools of angular momentum help little: the problem must be started anew (L17.4 transcript p. 1; notes p. 15).

What helps is a separation of **mass scales**. The electron mass $m$ is tiny compared with a nuclear mass $M$; Zwiebach and the notes take the ratio $m/M$ to be of order $10^{-4}$ (L17.4 transcript p. 1; notes p. 15). [For a single proton the ratio is $1/1836 \approx 5.4 \times 10^{-4}$, closer to $10^{-3}$; for a carbon nucleus it is about $4.6 \times 10^{-5}$. The lecture's $10^{-4}$ is a typical value, as it says, for nuclei with several nucleons.]

The physical picture (L17.4 transcript pp. 1–2): the positively charged nuclei repel one another, while the electrons, moving around them, attract them all and compensate the repulsion. The result is an equilibrium, but not a classical one, Zwiebach stresses: classically, a negative charge placed between two positive ones gives an unstable equilibrium, and moving it slightly makes the whole arrangement fly apart. Quantum mechanics solves this, as it does for atoms. In the quantum picture the nuclei are fairly well localized, though still quantum particles, while the electrons are completely delocalized. The nuclei vibrate slowly about equilibrium, and one can imagine computing the electron cloud for each fixed separation of the nuclei; as the nuclei move, the electronic state adjusts **adiabatically**, staying in its instantaneous eigenstate.

How remarkable a single electron is (L17.4 transcript pp. 2–3): a proton and an electron make a hydrogen atom, but can one electron bind **two** protons? Yes: that is the hydrogen molecule ion H₂⁺, a hydrogen molecule that has lost one of its electrons, analysed in the next lecture. Asked whether one electron can bind three protons, Zwiebach answers no, mentioning that the mathematical physicist Elliott Lieb has proved theorems of this kind and that the homework may touch on them; this page does not go into them.

## Energy scales

Let the molecule, nuclei and electron cloud, have a size of order $a$. The electrons are confined to that size, which fixes their momentum and energy, the only ingredients being $\hbar$, $a$ and $m$ (L17.4 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Electronic energies" tex="p \sim \frac{\hbar}{a}, \qquad E_{el} \sim \frac{p^2}{m} \sim \frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (6.1), p. 15" />

[The transcript says the momentum is of order $\hbar$ "times" the size of the cloud; it is $\hbar$ divided by the size, as the notes write.]

**Vibrations** (L17.4 transcript p. 3). Treat a nuclear vibration as a harmonic oscillator, with the nuclear mass and a restoring force:

<Formula label="A nuclear vibration" tex="H_{N} = \frac{P^2}{2M} + \frac{1}{2}\,k\,x^2" cite="reading5" at="eq. (6.2), p. 15" />

The restoring force comes from the electron cloud and the Coulomb forces, which adjust as the nuclei separate; it has nothing to do with the nuclear mass. A spring constant is an energy over a length squared, and the only energy and length available are the electronic ones, so

<Formula label="The spring constant" tex="k \sim \frac{E_{el}}{a^2} \sim \frac{\hbar^2}{ma^4}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (6.3), p. 16" />

The frequency is $\omega = \sqrt{k/M}$, with the **nuclear** mass. Multiplying and dividing by $m$ (L17.4 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="The vibrational frequency" tex="M\omega^2 \sim \frac{\hbar^2}{ma^4} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega^2 \sim \frac{m}{M}\,\frac{\hbar^2}{m^2a^4} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \hbar\omega \sim \sqrt{\frac{m}{M}}\;\frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (6.4), p. 16" />

<Formula label="Vibrational energies" tex="E_{vib} \sim \sqrt{\frac{m}{M}}\;E_{el}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (6.5), p. 16" />

With $m/M$ of order $10^{-4}$, the square root is of order $10^{-2}$: vibrational energies are about a hundred times smaller than electronic ones. The same statement in terms of frequencies, with $\omega_{el} = E_{el}/\hbar$ (L17.4 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Nuclear and electronic frequencies" tex="\omega \sim \sqrt{\frac{m}{M}}\;\omega_{el}" cite="reading4" at="p. 4" />

[The transcript has "little m over M" times $\omega_{el}$ at this point; the line before it gives the square root, as here.] The nuclei move much more slowly than the electrons change, which is the reason for adiabatic thinking: as the nuclei slowly vibrate, the electron cloud stays in its instantaneous eigenstate and does not jump (L17.4 transcript p. 4).

**Rotations** (L17.4 transcript p. 4). The whole molecule can also rotate like a solid body, with angular momentum $L$, $L^2 = \hbar^2\ell(\ell + 1)$, and a moment of inertia $I$ of order $Ma^2$:

<Formula label="Rotational energies" tex="E_{rot} = \frac{L^2}{2I} \sim \frac{\hbar^2\,\ell(\ell+1)}{Ma^2} \sim \frac{m}{M}\,\frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E_{rot} \sim \frac{m}{M}\,E_{el}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (6.6), p. 16" />

[The notes print $\hbar$ for $\hbar^2$ in the middle step of this estimate; the result is unchanged.] Rotations hardly distort the molecule, and their energies are smaller still (notes p. 16). Together (L17.4 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The hierarchy of molecular energies" tex="E_{el} : E_{vib} : E_{rot} = 1 : \sqrt{\frac{m}{M}} : \frac{m}{M}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (6.7), p. 16" />

Electronic energies are largest, then vibrations, and the smallest are rotations. The lecture stops here; the next one sets up the Born–Oppenheimer approximation on this basis.

<WorkedExample title="Energy scales of a light molecule">
<Problem>
Estimate the three energy scales for a molecule of size $a = 0.10$ nm whose nuclei are protons, $M = m_p$. Use $\hbar c = 197.33$ eV nm, $mc^2 = 0.5110$ MeV and $m_p/m = 1836$. Find the photon wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi\hbar c/E$ that matches each energy, and compare the periods of electronic and vibrational motion.
</Problem>
<Step title="Electronic energy">
$E_{el} \sim \hbar^2/ma^2 = (\hbar c)^2/(mc^2\,a^2) = (197.33)^2/(0.5110 \times 10^{6} \times 0.010)$, about 7.6 eV. The matching wavelength, $2\pi \times 197.33/7.62$, is about 163 nm, in the ultraviolet.
</Step>
<Step title="Vibrations and rotations">
$\sqrt{m/M} = 1/\sqrt{1836} \approx 0.0233$, so $E_{vib} \sim 0.0233 \times 7.62$, about 0.18 eV, with a wavelength near 7.0 μm, in the infrared. $m/M \approx 5.4 \times 10^{-4}$, so $E_{rot} \sim 4.15$ meV, with a wavelength near 0.30 mm, in the far infrared.
</Step>
<Step title="Periods">
A period is $2\pi\hbar/E$: about $5.4 \times 10^{-16}$ s for the electrons and $2.3 \times 10^{-14}$ s for a vibration, a ratio of $\sqrt{m/M} \approx 0.023$, not $m/M$. The electrons go through about 43 cycles during one vibration, slow enough for them to follow adiabatically. These are order-of-magnitude estimates, with all factors of order one dropped.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Let $H(\vec R)$ depend on parameters $\vec R = (R_1, \ldots, R_{N})$ with eigenstates $|\psi_n(\vec R)\rangle$ known for every $\vec R$. A motion $\vec R(t)$ is a path in **configuration space**, and $|\psi_n(\vec R(t))\rangle$ are its instantaneous eigenstates.
- The geometric phase becomes a line integral of the **Berry connection** $\vec A_n = i\langle\psi_n|\nabla_{R}|\psi_n\rangle$: $\gamma_n = \int \vec A_n\cdot d\vec R$. Time drops out, so it depends only on the path, unlike the dynamical phase $\theta_n = -\frac{1}{\hbar}\int E_n\,dt'$.
- Under $|\psi_n'\rangle = e^{-i\beta(\vec R)}|\psi_n\rangle$, $\vec A_n' = \vec A_n + \nabla_{R}\beta$ and the phase of a path changes by $\beta(\vec R_f) - \beta(\vec R_i)$. Only a **closed path** gives an observable Berry phase (gauge invariant up to multiples of $2\pi$).
- There is no Berry phase for real eigenstates or for one parameter. In three dimensions, $\gamma_n[C] = \int_S \vec D_n\cdot d\vec S$, the flux of the **Berry curvature** $\vec D_n = \nabla_{R}\times\vec A_n$.
- A spin one-half that follows a field whose direction traces a loop of solid angle $\Omega$ picks up $\gamma_\pm = \mp\Omega/2$ (spin up or down along the field).
- **Molecules:** with electron mass $m$, nuclear mass $M$ and size $a$, $E_{el} \sim \hbar^2/ma^2$, and $E_{el} : E_{vib} : E_{rot} = 1 : \sqrt{m/M} : m/M$. The electrons follow the slow nuclei adiabatically.
