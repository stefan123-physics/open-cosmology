So far in this course the perturbation never changed in time. This lecture starts chapter 4 of the notes, where it does: an atom hit by a light wave, a spin in a changing magnetic field. The lecture sets up the tool for such problems, the interaction picture, writes it out in a basis, solves one example exactly, and prepares the perturbation series that later lectures use for Fermi's golden rule, radiation and ionization (L9.1 transcript p. 1; notes section 4.1 and the start of section 4.2). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 4; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## A Hamiltonian that changes in time

As in time-independent perturbation theory, we start from a known Hamiltonian $H^{(0)}$ that does not depend on time, whose eigenstates and energies we know. The new feature is that the perturbation $\delta H(t)$ depends on time (L9.1 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="The time-dependent Hamiltonian" tex="H(t) = H^{(0)} + \delta H(t)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.1.1)" />

This changes everything, Zwiebach stresses: $H(t)$ has **no energy eigenstates** at all. Stationary states came from splitting a solution into a function of position times a phase $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, and that split is impossible when the Hamiltonian itself depends on time (L9.1 transcript pp. 1–2). So the aim is no longer to find energies but to find the state $|\Psi(t)\rangle$ that solves

<Formula label="The Schrödinger equation to be solved" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}|\Psi(t)\rangle = \left(H^{(0)} + \delta H(t)\right)|\Psi(t)\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.1.2)" />

A useful way to picture the problem: until some initial time the Hamiltonian is just $H^{(0)}$; then the perturbation acts for a while; after a final time it is $H^{(0)}$ again (L9.1 transcript p. 2; notes Figure 4.1). Before and after, energy eigenstates of $H^{(0)}$ make sense, and a system sitting in one of them only gathers a phase. So the natural question is: if the system starts in one eigenstate of $H^{(0)}$, and then "the world shakes for a while", in which eigenstates may it be found afterwards, and with what probabilities? A hydrogen atom in its ground state, hit by an electromagnetic wave, is the standard example: it may be excited to a higher level or ionized. Ionization is subtler, because the electron then goes to one of infinitely many continuum states, and later lectures deal with that (L9.1 transcript pp. 2–3).

## The interaction picture

The method combines the Heisenberg and Schrödinger pictures (L9.1 transcript p. 3). Recall the Heisenberg picture: if $U(t)$ is the time-evolution operator, $|\Psi(t)\rangle = U(t)|\Psi(0)\rangle$, then expectation values can be computed with time-dependent operators in the states at time zero (L9.1 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Heisenberg operators" tex="\langle\Psi(t)|A_s|\Psi(t)\rangle = \langle\Psi(0)|U^\dagger(t)\,A_s\,U(t)|\Psi(0)\rangle, \qquad A_H(t) \equiv U^\dagger(t)\,A_s\,U(t)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.1.4), (4.1.5)" />

Here $A_s$ is the Schrödinger operator and $A_H$ its Heisenberg version; the definition holds even when $A_s$ itself depends on time, which is what will let a time-dependent $\delta H(t)$ have a Heisenberg version below (notes p. 74). Since $U^\dagger U = 1$, acting with $U^\dagger(t)$ on $|\Psi(t)\rangle$ gives back $|\Psi(0)\rangle$: $U^\dagger$ **brings states to rest** (L9.1 transcript p. 4; notes eq. (4.1.6)).

For the full $H(t)$ we do not know $U(t)$; that is the whole difficulty. But for $H^{(0)}$ alone we do: $U_0(t) = e^{-iH^{(0)}t/\hbar}$ (notes eq. (4.1.7)). The idea is to let the Heisenberg picture take care of $H^{(0)}$, which we understand, and the Schrödinger picture take care of $\delta H$, which is the hard part (L9.1 transcript p. 4). So we partly bring the state to rest, using only $H^{(0)}$:

<Formula label="The interaction-picture state" tex="|\tilde\Psi(t)\rangle \equiv e^{iH^{(0)}t/\hbar}\,|\Psi(t)\rangle, \qquad |\Psi(t)\rangle = e^{-iH^{(0)}t/\hbar}\,|\tilde\Psi(t)\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.1.8), (4.1.9)" />

If $\delta H$ were zero, $|\tilde\Psi\rangle$ would be constant. Finding $|\tilde\Psi(t)\rangle$ is as good as finding $|\Psi(t)\rangle$: no information is lost (L9.1 transcript pp. 4–5).

Differentiating, $i\hbar\,d/dt$ acting on the exponential brings down $-H^{(0)}$, and acting on $|\Psi(t)\rangle$ brings down the full $H^{(0)} + \delta H$. Writing $|\Psi\rangle$ in terms of $|\tilde\Psi\rangle$, the two $H^{(0)}$ terms cancel, because $H^{(0)}$ commutes with its own exponentials (L9.1 transcript pp. 5–6). What remains is a Schrödinger equation driven by the perturbation alone:

<Formula label="The interaction-picture Schrödinger equation" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}|\tilde\Psi(t)\rangle = \delta\tilde H(t)\,|\tilde\Psi(t)\rangle, \qquad \delta\tilde H(t) \equiv e^{iH^{(0)}t/\hbar}\,\delta H(t)\,e^{-iH^{(0)}t/\hbar}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.1.11), (4.1.12)" />

The operator $\delta\tilde H$ is exactly the Heisenberg version of $\delta H$, built with $H^{(0)}$ instead of the full Hamiltonian; it has picked up extra time dependence. In Zwiebach's summary: what is not interaction is treated à la Heisenberg, and the interaction is treated à la Schrödinger (L9.1 transcript pp. 5–6).

## The equation in a basis

To see what the equation means, use the orthonormal eigenstates of the known Hamiltonian, $H^{(0)}|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$ (notes eq. (4.1.13)). The labels $^{(0)}$ of time-independent perturbation theory are dropped here, because neither these states nor their energies will be corrected: in a time-dependent problem there are no energies to correct (L9.2 transcript p. 1). Expand the unknown state:

<Formula label="Expanding the interaction-picture state" tex="|\tilde\Psi(t)\rangle = \sum_n c_n(t)\,|n\rangle, \qquad |\Psi(t)\rangle = \sum_n c_n(t)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\,|n\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.1.14), (4.1.15)" />

Is this allowed, if the $|n\rangle$ are not eigenstates of $H(t)$? Yes: they are a basis, so at each instant the state can be written in terms of them, with coefficients that change in time (L9.2 transcript p. 1). Without $\delta H$ the $c_n$ are constant, and the second expansion is the familiar evolution of a superposition of stationary states; with $\delta H$ the $c_n$ change, and finding how is the problem (L9.2 transcript p. 2).

Inserting the expansion into the Schrödinger equation, putting a complete set of states $\sum_m|m\rangle\langle m|$ on the right, and comparing the coefficients of each $|m\rangle$ gives $i\hbar\,\dot c_m = \sum_n\delta\tilde H_{mn}\,c_n$, with $\delta\tilde H_{mn} = \langle m|\delta\tilde H|n\rangle$ (L9.2 transcript p. 2; notes eqs. (4.1.16)–(4.1.19)). Since $|m\rangle$ and $|n\rangle$ are eigenstates of $H^{(0)}$, the exponentials in $\delta\tilde H$ just give phases (L9.2 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Matrix elements of the interaction-picture perturbation" tex="\delta\tilde H_{mn}(t) = e^{i\omega_{mn}t}\,\delta H_{mn}(t), \qquad \omega_{mn} \equiv \frac{E_m - E_n}{\hbar}, \qquad \delta H_{mn} \equiv \langle m|\delta H(t)|n\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.1.20)–(4.1.22)" />

and the Schrödinger equation becomes a set of coupled first-order differential equations, one for each coefficient:

<Formula label="Coupled equations for the coefficients" tex="i\hbar\,\dot c_m(t) = \sum_n e^{i\omega_{mn}t}\,\delta H_{mn}(t)\,c_n(t)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.1.23)" />

Nothing has been approximated yet. The problem is now concrete: with a computer one can integrate these equations numerically, and how hard that is depends only on how complicated the time dependence of $\delta H_{mn}$ is (L9.2 transcript p. 3).

## Example: a sudden kick to a two-level system

The example follows Griffiths, problem 9.3 (L9.3 transcript p. 1; notes section 4.1.2). Take two eigenstates of $H^{(0)}$, $|a\rangle$ and $|b\rangle$, with energies $E_a$ and $E_b$, and let $\omega_{ab} = (E_a - E_b)/\hbar$ (Zwiebach calls it $\omega_0$ in the lecture). The perturbation acts only at the instant $t = 0$, and only off the diagonal:

<Formula label="An instantaneous off-diagonal kick" tex="\delta H(t) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \alpha \\ \alpha^* & 0 \end{pmatrix}\delta(t) \equiv U\,\delta(t)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.1.25), (4.1.26)" />

with $\alpha$ a complex number. ($U$ here is just a matrix, not a unitary operator; Zwiebach remarks that the letter is not ideal. Since $\delta(t)$ has units of inverse time, $\alpha$ has the units of $\hbar$.) The system is in $|a\rangle$ before the kick. What is the probability to find it in $|b\rangle$ afterwards?

Think first (L9.3 transcript pp. 1–2). Before $t = 0$, $|a\rangle$ is an energy eigenstate and the system stays there. After the kick there is no perturbation, so whatever superposition of $|a\rangle$ and $|b\rangle$ exists just after $t = 0$ keeps its probabilities forever. So the transition must happen **instantaneously**: just after the kick there must already be some amplitude in $|b\rangle$, presumably proportional to $\alpha$ for a weak kick.

The coupled equations, with only the off-diagonal elements present, read $i\hbar\,\dot c_a = e^{i\omega_{ab}t}\alpha\,\delta(t)\,c_b$ and $i\hbar\,\dot c_b = e^{-i\omega_{ab}t}\alpha^*\delta(t)\,c_a$ (notes eqs. (4.1.33), (4.1.34)). The phases may be set to 1, since the delta function lives at $t = 0$. But replacing $c_b(t)\,\delta(t)$ by $c_b(0)\,\delta(t)$ would be wrong: Zwiebach first writes this and then retracts it, because $c_a$ and $c_b$ **jump** at $t = 0$, and a delta function times a discontinuous function is ambiguous (L9.3 transcript pp. 3–4). The cure is physical: no real signal is a true delta function. Replace $\delta(t)$ by a pulse of height $1/t_0$ lasting from $0$ to $t_0$, which has the same area, solve, and hope the answer does not depend on $t_0$ (L9.3 transcript p. 4). For $t_0$ short enough, $\omega_{ab}t_0$ is tiny, so dropping the phases is still fine. During the pulse,

<Formula label="The regulated equations" tex="i\hbar\,\dot c_a = \frac{\alpha}{t_0}\,c_b, \qquad i\hbar\,\dot c_b = \frac{\alpha^*}{t_0}\,c_a, \qquad 0 \le t \le t_0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.1.37)" />

Differentiating the first and using the second gives $\ddot c_a = -\left(|\alpha|/(\hbar t_0)\right)^2c_a$, an oscillation (notes eq. (4.1.38)). With $c_a(0) = 1$ and $c_b(0) = 0$,

<Formula label="Solution during the pulse" tex="c_a(t) = \cos\left(\frac{|\alpha|\,t}{\hbar\,t_0}\right), \qquad c_b(t) = -i\,\frac{\alpha^*}{|\alpha|}\,\sin\left(\frac{|\alpha|\,t}{\hbar\,t_0}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.1.41), (4.1.42)" />

(The notes write the phase factor as $|\alpha|/\alpha$, which is the same number.) When the pulse ends at $t = t_0$ the coefficients freeze, at values that, happily, do not depend on $t_0$: $c_a = \cos(|\alpha|/\hbar)$ and $c_b = -i(\alpha^*/|\alpha|)\sin(|\alpha|/\hbar)$. The limit $t_0 \rightarrow 0$ is therefore harmless, and after the kick (L9.3 transcript pp. 5–6)

<Formula label="The state after the kick" tex="|\Psi(t)\rangle = \cos\left(\frac{|\alpha|}{\hbar}\right)e^{-iE_at/\hbar}|a\rangle - i\,\frac{\alpha^*}{|\alpha|}\,\sin\left(\frac{|\alpha|}{\hbar}\right)e^{-iE_bt/\hbar}|b\rangle, \qquad t > 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.1.48)" />

<Formula label="Probabilities after the kick" tex="p_b = \left|\langle b|\Psi(t)\rangle\right|^2 = \sin^2\left(\frac{|\alpha|}{\hbar}\right), \qquad p_a = \left|\langle a|\Psi(t)\rangle\right|^2 = \cos^2\left(\frac{|\alpha|}{\hbar}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.1.49)" />

They add up to 1, as they must, and they are constant: the system ends in a superposition of energy eigenstates. As expected, for a weak kick $c_b \approx -i\alpha^*/\hbar$ is proportional to $\alpha^*$, so its size is $|\alpha|/\hbar$. This is an exact result; perturbation theory would give its expansion in powers of $|\alpha|/\hbar$, starting with $p_b \approx |\alpha|^2/\hbar^2$ (L9.3 transcript p. 6; notes p. 80).

<WorkedExample title="How strong is a kick, and when does first order fail?">
<Problem>
A two-level system has $E_a - E_b = 2.0$ eV and starts in $|a\rangle$. It receives an off-diagonal kick as above, delivered as a pulse lasting $t_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{-17}$ s. Use $\hbar = 6.582 \times 10^{-16}$ eV s. (a) Is the pulse short enough for the phases $e^{\pm i\omega_{ab}t}$ to be dropped? (b) For $|\alpha|/\hbar = \pi/6$, find $p_b$ and compare with the first-order estimate, and find the size of the matrix element during the pulse. (c) What happens for $|\alpha|/\hbar = \pi/2$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The phases">
$\omega_{ab} = 2.0/(6.582 \times 10^{-16}) \approx 3.04 \times 10^{15}$ per second, so $\omega_{ab}t_0 \approx 0.030$. During the pulse the phases stay within 0.03 radians of zero, close to 1, so the pulse acts like a delta function for this system. (A pulse longer than about $1/\omega_{ab} \approx 0.33$ fs would not.)
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A moderate kick">
$p_b = \sin^2(\pi/6) = 0.25$ and $p_a = 0.75$. The first-order estimate $|\alpha|^2/\hbar^2 = (\pi/6)^2 \approx 0.274$ is about 10% too high: already noticeable. During the pulse the off-diagonal matrix element is $|\alpha|/t_0 = (\pi/6)\,\hbar/t_0 \approx 34$ eV, much larger than the 2 eV gap: a short kick that transfers a finite amount of probability must be very strong while it lasts.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A strong kick">
$p_b = \sin^2(\pi/2) = 1$: the kick moves the system completely into $|b\rangle$. The first-order estimate $(\pi/2)^2 \approx 2.47$ is not even a possible probability. Perturbation theory is only good when $|\alpha|/\hbar$ is small.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Setting up the perturbation series

For a general $\delta H(t)$ there is no exact solution, and we expand (L9.4 transcript p. 1). As in time-independent perturbation theory, put a unit-free parameter $\lambda$ in front of the perturbation, $H(t) = H^{(0)} + \lambda\,\delta H(t)$, so that $i\hbar\,d|\tilde\Psi\rangle/dt = \lambda\,\delta\tilde H|\tilde\Psi\rangle$, and expand the interaction-picture state in powers of $\lambda$, every term depending on time:

<Formula label="The perturbation series" tex="|\tilde\Psi(t)\rangle = |\tilde\Psi^{(0)}(t)\rangle + \lambda\,|\tilde\Psi^{(1)}(t)\rangle + \lambda^2\,|\tilde\Psi^{(2)}(t)\rangle + O(\lambda^3)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (4.2.1)–(4.2.3)" />

Inserting it and collecting powers of $\lambda$ is easier than in the time-independent case, because $\lambda$ appears only on the right-hand side (L9.4 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Order by order" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}|\tilde\Psi^{(0)}(t)\rangle = 0, \qquad i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}|\tilde\Psi^{(n+1)}(t)\rangle = \delta\tilde H(t)\,|\tilde\Psi^{(n)}(t)\rangle, \quad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots" cite="reading5" at="eq. (4.2.5)" />

The zeroth-order state is constant. Put it into the next equation, and the first-order state follows by a single integral; that one gives the second order, and so on. Zwiebach ends by noting that the first order, sometimes the second, already contains the physics of the applications in the next lectures (L9.4 transcript p. 2).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Transitions in the early universe">
This chapter's theory computes how fast atoms absorb and emit light and how fast light ionizes hydrogen (notes sections 4.4–4.5). In the early universe such processes decided when recombination happened: in 8.286 Guth describes how, as the universe cooled, electrons and protons combined into neutral hydrogen and the photons, soon no longer scattered, were released as the cosmic microwave background. Guth quotes the temperatures at which this happened rather than deriving them; a detailed calculation of recombination needs, among other things, transition rates of exactly the kind this chapter teaches how to compute.
</Callout>

## Summary

- With a time-dependent perturbation, $H(t) = H^{(0)} + \delta H(t)$ has no energy eigenstates; the goal is the state $|\Psi(t)\rangle$, typically to ask which eigenstates of $H^{(0)}$ a system ends up in after the perturbation has acted.
- The interaction picture: $|\tilde\Psi\rangle = e^{iH^{(0)}t/\hbar}|\Psi\rangle$ obeys $i\hbar\,d|\tilde\Psi\rangle/dt = \delta\tilde H|\tilde\Psi\rangle$, with $\delta\tilde H$ the Heisenberg version of $\delta H$ built with $H^{(0)}$.
- In the basis of $H^{(0)}$ eigenstates, $|\tilde\Psi\rangle = \sum_n c_n|n\rangle$ and $i\hbar\,\dot c_m = \sum_n e^{i\omega_{mn}t}\delta H_{mn}c_n$, an exact set of coupled equations.
- A two-level system kicked by $U\delta(t)$, solved by smoothing the delta function, ends with $p_b = \sin^2(|\alpha|/\hbar)$, independent of the pulse length; first order gives only $|\alpha|^2/\hbar^2$.
- With $\delta H \rightarrow \lambda\,\delta H$, the series obeys $i\hbar\,d|\tilde\Psi^{(n+1)}\rangle/dt = \delta\tilde H|\tilde\Psi^{(n)}\rangle$, solved order by order by integrals.
