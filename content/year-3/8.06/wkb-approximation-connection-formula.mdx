The previous lecture set up the language: local momentum, local wavelength, and wavefunctions written as a magnitude times a phase. This lecture does the approximation itself, finds the conditions under which it holds, and shows how it fails at turning points, where connection formulae come to the rescue (L7.1–L7.4 transcripts; notes sections 3.2–3.3). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 3; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## The WKB equation

Write the wavefunction as a single exponential (L7.1 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Wavefunction as a pure exponential" tex="\psi(x) = \exp\!\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}S(x)\right), \qquad S(x) \in \mathbb{C}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.2.1)" />

This loses nothing, because $S$ may be complex: its real part gives the phase and its imaginary part the magnitude. Putting it into $-\hbar^2\psi'' = p^2(x)\psi$ and cancelling the exponential gives (L7.1 transcript pp. 2–3)

<Formula label="The equation for S" tex="\left(S'(x)\right)^2 - i\hbar\,S''(x) = p^2(x)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.2.5)" />

It looks like a step backwards: a linear equation has become a nonlinear one, whose solutions cannot be added. But it shows where the approximation lies. **The term $i\hbar S''$ is small when $V$ varies slowly** (L7.1 transcript pp. 3–4). For a constant potential, the exact solution is a plane wave $e^{ipx/\hbar}$ with constant $p$, so $S = px$ and $S'' = 0$: that term vanishes, and for a potential that varies slowly it should stay small. Treating $\hbar$ as the small parameter, expand

<Formula label="Semiclassical expansion" tex="S(x) = S_0(x) + \hbar\,S_1(x) + \hbar^2S_2(x) + O(\hbar^3)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.2.6)" />

($S_0$ has units of $\hbar$, $S_1$ none, $S_2$ those of $1/\hbar$). As with the small parameter of perturbation theory, each power of $\hbar$ must vanish separately (L7.1 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="Order by order in ħ" tex="\left(S_0'\right)^2 = p^2(x), \qquad 2S_0'S_1' - iS_0'' = 0" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (3.2.8), (3.2.9)" />

## The WKB solutions

The first equation gives $S_0' = \pm p(x)$, the two signs being motion to the right or left; the second then gives an imaginary $S_1$ (L7.2 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="The first two orders" tex="S_0(x) = \pm\int_{x_0}^{x}p(x')\,dx', \qquad S_1' = \frac{i}{2}\,\frac{p'}{p} \quad\Rightarrow\quad iS_1 = -\frac12\ln p(x) + C" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (3.2.10)–(3.2.12)" />

Rebuilding $\psi = e^{iS_0/\hbar}e^{iS_1}$ gives the **basic WKB solution** (L7.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Basic WKB solution" tex="\psi(x) = \frac{A}{\sqrt{p(x)}}\,\exp\!\left(\pm\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{x_0}^{x}p(x')\,dx'\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.2.15)" />

The lesson of the scheme: first get the phase right, then, at the next order, the amplitude. Hardly anyone goes beyond this order, and the solution is not normalized, since its range of validity is not yet clear. In a **classically allowed** region, with $p = \hbar k(x)$, the general solution is a right-moving plus a left-moving wave; in a **forbidden** region, with $p = i\hbar\kappa(x)$, a growing plus a decaying exponential (L7.2 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="General WKB solutions" tex="\psi = \frac{A}{\sqrt{k(x)}}e^{\,i\int_{x_0}^{x}k\,dx'} + \frac{B}{\sqrt{k(x)}}e^{-i\int_{x_0}^{x}k\,dx'}, \qquad \psi = \frac{C}{\sqrt{\kappa(x)}}e^{\int_{x_0}^{x}\kappa\,dx'} + \frac{D}{\sqrt{\kappa(x)}}e^{-\int_{x_0}^{x}\kappa\,dx'}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (3.2.20), (3.2.21)" />

**What the basic solution says** (L7.2 transcript p. 4). Its probability density is

<Formula label="Probability density" tex="\rho = |\psi|^2 = \frac{|A|^2}{p(x)} = \frac{|A|^2}{m\,v(x)}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.2.16)" />

so the particle is most likely to be found where it moves slowly and spends more time, just as for a classical particle. Its phase is $S = \int p\,dx$, so $\nabla S$ is exactly the local momentum, and the current $\rho S'/m$ is

<Formula label="Probability current" tex="J = \frac{|A|^2}{p(x)}\,\frac{p(x)}{m} = \frac{|A|^2}{m}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (3.2.18), (3.2.19)" />

The $p$'s cancel, as they must: in an energy eigenstate $\rho$ does not change in time, so conservation, $\partial\rho/\partial t + dJ/dx = 0$, forces $J$ to be constant.

## When is it valid?

We did not really set $\hbar$ to zero; we made a physical approximation, and the expansion shows which (L7.3 transcript pp. 1–2). Each order-$\hbar$ term must be much smaller than each order-one term, for example $|\hbar S_0''| \ll |S_0'|^2$, that is $\hbar|p'| \ll p^2$. Since $\hbar|p'|/p^2 = |d(\hbar/p)/dx|$:

<Formula label="The local wavelength must vary slowly" tex="\left|\frac{d\lambda}{dx}\right| \ll 1, \qquad \text{or} \qquad \lambda\left|\frac{d\lambda}{dx}\right| \ll \lambda" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (3.2.28), (3.2.29)" />

(dropping factors of $2\pi$, as one may in such estimates). In words: over one wavelength, the wavelength must change by much less than itself. Differentiating $p^2 = 2m(E - V)$ turns this into a condition on the potential:

<Formula label="Slowly varying potential" tex="\lambda(x)\left|\frac{dV}{dx}\right| \ll \frac{p^2}{2m}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.2.32)" />

**over the distance of one local wavelength, the potential may change by only a small fraction of the kinetic energy**. That is what "slowly varying" means in numbers.

**Trouble at turning points** (L7.3 transcript pp. 3–4). Near a turning point $x = a$, any smooth potential is roughly straight: $V - E = g(x - a)$ with $g > 0$. To the left, in the allowed region, $p = \sqrt{2mg(a - x)}$, and

<Formula label="Failure near a turning point" tex="\left|\frac{d\lambda}{dx}\right| = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2mg}}\,\frac{1}{2\,(a - x)^{3/2}}" cite="reading3" at="p. 3" />

which grows without bound as $x \to a$ (here with $\lambda = \hbar/p$; the notes keep the $2\pi$). No choice of $g$ or $m$ saves it: close enough to $a$, WKB fails. This cannot be ignored. Bound states need the forbidden region, where the wavefunction decays, so we must know how a solution on one side of the turning point continues on the other. The way out is to solve the equation exactly near the turning point, where the potential is linear, which brings in Airy functions; Lecture 8 does that. (More elegant methods go around the turning point in the complex $x$ plane, L7.4 transcript p. 1.)

## A connection formula and a quantization rule

For a turning point at $x = a$ with the allowed region to the left and the forbidden region to the right, the results are (L7.4 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="First connection formula: a decaying exponential fixes the oscillation" tex="\text{beyond } a: \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa(x)}}\exp\left(-\int_a^x \kappa\,dx'\right) \quad\rightarrow\quad \text{before } a: \frac{2}{\sqrt{k(x)}}\cos\left(\int_x^a k\,dx' - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.3.1)" />

<Formula label="Second connection formula: an oscillation fixes the growing exponential" tex="\text{before } a: -\frac{1}{\sqrt{k(x)}}\sin\left(\int_x^a k\,dx' - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) \quad\rightarrow\quad \text{beyond } a: \frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa(x)}}\exp\left(\int_a^x \kappa\,dx'\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.3.2)" />

The arrows matter (the notes draw the first one pointing from right to left, since the allowed region is on the left): a pure decaying exponential beyond the turning point fixes the phase-shifted cosine before it, and an oscillation before it fixes the growing exponential beyond it, but neither works in reverse. The odd-looking $-\pi/4$ is what makes the connection work. (The subtleties of using these formulae are the subject of the next lecture.)

**Example: a rising potential with a hard wall** (L7.4 transcript pp. 2–5). Let $V(x)$ have a hard wall at $x = 0$ and rise without bound for $x > 0$. For an energy $E$, still unknown, the turning point $a$ is where $V(a) = E$. Beyond $a$, only the decaying exponential is allowed, since the forbidden region never ends. By the first connection formula, the wavefunction for $0 \le x \ll a$ is then

<Formula label="The connected wavefunction" tex="\psi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{k(x)}}\cos\!\left(\int_x^a k\,dx' - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.3.3)" />

It must vanish at the wall. Splitting $\int_x^a = \int_0^a - \int_0^x$ and expanding the cosine of a difference, the wavefunction becomes $\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\left[\cos\delta\,\cos\int_0^x k + \sin\delta\,\sin\int_0^x k\right]$ with the constant angle $\delta = \int_0^a k\,dx - \frac{\pi}{4}$. Only the sine term vanishes at $x = 0$, so $\cos\delta = 0$: $\delta = \frac{\pi}{2} + n\pi$, and

<Formula label="Quantization condition" tex="\int_0^a k(x)\,dx = \left(n + \frac34\right)\pi, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (3.3.4)–(3.3.6)" />

($n$ cannot be negative, since the integral on the left is positive.) Zwiebach calls it a Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization condition (L7.4 transcript p. 4). With $\hbar k = \sqrt{2m(E - V)}$ and $a$ fixed by $E$, one adjusts $E$ (by hand or by computer) until the integral takes the quantized values, which often gives remarkably accurate energies. The wavefunction is then $\propto \frac{1}{\sqrt k}\sin\int_0^x k\,dx'$, whose phase runs from 0 to a bit more than $n\pi$ by $x = a$: it has the $n$ nodes the $n$-th state must have (L7.4 transcript p. 5).

<WorkedExample title="A particle bouncing on a floor">
<Problem>
A particle of mass $m$ sits above a hard floor at $x = 0$ in a uniform force field: $V(x) = Fx$ for $x > 0$. (a) Use the quantization condition to find its energies. (b) Compare the first three with the exact values, $E_n = \varepsilon\,z_n$ with $\varepsilon = (\hbar^2F^2/2m)^{1/3}$ and $z_n = 2.33811, 4.08795, 5.52056$ (the zeros of the Airy function, magnitudes). (c) For a neutron in the Earth's gravity ($F = mg$, $m = 1.675 \times 10^{-27}$ kg), find the ground-state energy and the height of its turning point.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The WKB energies">
The turning point is $a = E/F$. Then $\int_0^a\sqrt{2m(E - Fx)}\,dx/\hbar = \frac23\sqrt{2m}\,E^{3/2}/(\hbar F)$. Setting it equal to $(n + \frac34)\pi$ gives $E_n = \varepsilon\left[\frac{3\pi}{2}\left(n + \frac34\right)\right]^{2/3}$, with the same scale $\varepsilon$ as the exact result.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) How good is it?">
$\left[\frac{3\pi}{2}(n + \frac34)\right]^{2/3}$ is 2.3203, 4.0818 and 5.5172 for $n = 0, 1, 2$, against the exact 2.3381, 4.0879 and 5.5206: errors of 0.76%, 0.15% and 0.06%. Even the ground state, whose wavefunction holds only $3\pi/4$ of phase (three eighths of a wavelength) and hardly fits the "slowly varying" picture, is within 1%, and the errors shrink as $n$ grows.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A bouncing neutron">
$\varepsilon = (\hbar^2m g^2/2)^{1/3} \approx 9.64 \times 10^{-32}$ J $\approx 0.602$ peV (pico-electronvolts). The ground state is $2.338\,\varepsilon \approx 1.41$ peV (WKB: 1.40 peV), and its turning point lies $E_0/(mg) \approx 13.7$ micrometres above the floor (WKB: 13.6 μm). Quantum effects of gravity on a single particle thus show up on the scale of a fraction of a hair's width.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- With $\psi = e^{iS/\hbar}$, the Schrödinger equation becomes $(S')^2 - i\hbar S'' = p^2$; the $\hbar$ term is small for slowly varying $V$.
- To first order, $\psi \approx \frac{A}{\sqrt{p}}\exp\left(\pm\frac{i}{\hbar}\int p\,dx\right)$: oscillating in allowed regions, growing or decaying in forbidden ones. $\rho \propto 1/v$ and the current is constant.
- Validity: $|d\lambda/dx| \ll 1$, or $\lambda|dV/dx| \ll p^2/2m$; this fails at turning points, which connection formulae bridge.
- A hard wall plus a rising potential gives $\int_0^a k\,dx = (n + \frac34)\pi$, accurate to better than 1% even for the ground state of a particle bouncing on a floor.
