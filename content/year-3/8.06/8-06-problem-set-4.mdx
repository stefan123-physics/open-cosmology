Problem 6 also leans on [degenerate perturbation theory](/courses/8-06/degeneracy-lifted-first-second-order), and Problems 5 and 6 on the two-state picture of [the ammonia molecule](/courses/8-05/ammonia-maser-nmr) from 8.05. The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 4, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. All six problems count for the score (80 points).

Some parts, boxes and numbers are our additions, each marked as such where it appears: Problem 1(c), the linear potential near a turning point; in Problem 2(b) the form of the Airy integral on a turned path, used as a check; in Problem 3 the value $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$, the constants, the height of the neutron's turning point in part (b) and the spacing of the levels in the check of part (c); in Problem 4(c) the full table of relative errors; and in Problem 6 the exact checks for two and three hops, the definition of the rate as one over the crossing time and the largest value of the number $c$ in part (c). ## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `hbar^2/(2*m*L^2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. The letters of a formula box count as independent of each other. Write the symbols as the box names them: `hbar` stands for $\hbar$, `p1` and `p2` for $p'$ and $p''$, `lam` for $\lambda$ (with `l1` and `l2` for $\lambda'$ and $\lambda''$ in Problem 1), `alpha` for $\alpha$, `x0` for $x_0$, `phi` for $\phi$, `omega` for $\omega$, `V0` for $V_0$, `l` for the lattice spacing $\ell$, `eps` for $\epsilon$ and `Delta` for $\Delta$. The natural logarithm is `ln( )`.

Give a number answer to 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. Where a box needs more digits, its label says so.

MIT gives the points per problem. The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons. The local momentum is $p(x)$, with $p^2 = 2m\left(E - V\right)$. In a classically allowed region $p = \hbar k(x)$, in a forbidden region $\hbar\kappa(x) = \sqrt{2m\left(V - E\right)}$. The local de Broglie wavelength is $\lambda(x) = h/p(x) = 2\pi\hbar/p(x)$. A prime is a derivative with respect to $x$. Where a solution says only "notes", it means Zwiebach's lecture notes, chapter 3. Some letters change their meaning from problem to problem, as on MIT's sheet: $g$ is the slope of a linear potential in Problems 1 and 2 and the acceleration of gravity in Problem 3; $k$ is a Fourier variable in Problem 2(b) and the order of perturbation theory in Problem 6; $\lambda$ is a wavelength in Problem 1 and the hopping strength in Problem 6.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The equation satisfied by the approximate WKB solution" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

The Schrödinger equation for an energy eigenstate, written with the local momentum, is

<Formula label="Schrödinger equation with the local momentum" tex="-\hbar^2\,\psi'' = p^2(x)\,\psi" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, eq. (1), p. 1" />

The lesson found an approximate solution of it:

<Formula label="The approximate WKB solution" tex="\psi_a(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{p(x)}}\,\exp\!\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}\int^x p(x')\,dx'\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={5}>

The function $\psi_a$ does not solve the equation above, but it does solve another equation exactly. Find it, and write it as

<Formula label="The equation that the approximate solution obeys" tex="-\hbar^2\,\psi_a'' = \left[\,p^2(x) + X(x)\,\right]\psi_a" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (the name X for the extra terms is ours)" />

where $X(x)$ stands for the extra terms: they are built from $p(x)$ and its derivatives. In the box, type $X$.

<Hint>

Differentiate once and write the result as $\psi_a' = f(x)\,\psi_a$. The function $f$ has two terms: one from the factor $1/\sqrt p$ and one from the exponential.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Then $\psi_a'' = \left(f^2 + f'\right)\psi_a$. Work out $f^2$ and $f'$ and watch what cancels.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-x" label="$X =$" expr="hbar^2*(p2/(2*p) - 3*p1^2/(4*p^2))"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], p: [0.5, 2], p1: [0.5, 2], p2: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", p1: "p'", p2: "p''" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-hbar^2*(p2/(2*p) - 3*p1^2/(4*p^2))", hint: "The sign is turned around. The left side of the equation is −ħ²ψ″, so the terms that come with ψ″/ψ change sign once more." },
    { expr: "hbar^2*(p2/(2*p) - p1^2/(4*p^2))", hint: "The derivative of −p′/(2p) has two terms: −p″/(2p) and +p′²/(2p²). The second one seems to be missing." },
    { expr: "hbar^2*(p2/(2*p) + 3*p1^2/(4*p^2))", hint: "Check the sign of the p′² term: the square of −p′/(2p) and the derivative of −p′/(2p) both add a positive p′² term to ψ″/ψ, and X is minus ħ² times those." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Differentiate twice. The terms of order $1/\hbar^2$ and $1/\hbar$ are the ones the WKB scheme was built to get right. What is left over, of order $\hbar^0$, is the extra piece.

<Step title="The first derivative">

The logarithm of $\psi_a$ is $-\frac12\ln p + \frac{i}{\hbar}\int^x p\,dx'$. Its derivative is $\psi_a'/\psi_a$:

<Formula label="First derivative of the WKB solution" tex="\psi_a' = \left(-\frac{p'}{2p} + \frac{i\,p}{\hbar}\right)\psi_a" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The second derivative">

Call the bracket $f$. Then $\psi_a'' = \left(f^2 + f'\right)\psi_a$ with

<Formula label="The two pieces of the second derivative" tex="f^2 = \frac{p'^2}{4p^2} - \frac{i\,p'}{\hbar} - \frac{p^2}{\hbar^2}, \qquad f' = -\frac{p''}{2p} + \frac{p'^2}{2p^2} + \frac{i\,p'}{\hbar}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The two terms with $i\,p'/\hbar$ cancel. That is no accident: the factor $1/\sqrt p$ was chosen in the lesson to make the terms of order $\hbar$ in the WKB equation vanish. So

<Formula label="Second derivative of the WKB solution" tex="\psi_a'' = \left(-\frac{p^2}{\hbar^2} + \frac{3p'^2}{4p^2} - \frac{p''}{2p}\right)\psi_a" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The exact equation">

Multiply by $-\hbar^2$:

<Formula label="Exact equation for the WKB solution" tex="-\hbar^2\,\psi_a'' = \left[\,p^2 + \hbar^2\left(\frac{p''}{2p} - \frac{3p'^2}{4p^2}\right)\right]\psi_a, \qquad X = \hbar^2\left(\frac{p''}{2p} - \frac{3p'^2}{4p^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $p'/p$ is one over a length, so $X$ has the units of $\hbar^2/\text{length}^2$, a momentum squared, as $p^2$ has. A limit: for a constant potential $p' = p'' = 0$ and $X = 0$. Then $\psi_a$ is a plane wave, which solves the Schrödinger equation exactly. The same $X$ comes out with the other sign in the exponent, and so for the cosine and the sine of the phase: every WKB solution of an allowed region obeys this one equation.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

The WKB solution is good where each of the extra terms is much smaller than $p^2$. Write down these two conditions, and turn them into conditions on the local wavelength $\lambda(x) = 2\pi\hbar/p(x)$ and its derivatives.

In the box, type the ratio $X/p^2$ written with $\lambda$, $\lambda'$ and $\lambda''$ only.

<Hint>

Differentiate $\lambda = 2\pi\hbar/p$ once and twice. The first derivative gives you $p'$ in terms of $\lambda'$. The second derivative holds both $p''$ and $p'^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Solve those two relations for $\hbar\,p'/p^2$ and $\hbar^2p''/p^3$, the two combinations that appear in $X/p^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-ratio" label="$X/p^2 =$" expr="(l1^2 - 2*lam*l2)/(16*pi^2)"
  vars={{ lam: [0.5, 2], l1: [0.5, 2], l2: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ lam: "\\lambda", l1: "\\lambda'", l2: "\\lambda''" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(l1^2 - 2*lam*l2)/4", hint: "That is right for the reduced wavelength ħ/p. On this page λ = h/p = 2πħ/p, so each pair of wavelengths brings a factor 1/(2π)²." },
    { expr: "(-3*l1^2 - 2*lam*l2)/(16*pi^2)", hint: "The second derivative of 1/p holds two terms: one with p″ and one with p′². Solving for p″ therefore brings in a second λ′² term." },
    { expr: "(2*lam*l2 - l1^2)/(16*pi^2)", hint: "The overall sign is turned around. Test it on a case you know: for p² = 2mg(a − x) the extra terms are negative." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each extra term over $p^2$ is a pure number built from $\hbar$, $p$ and derivatives. The wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi\hbar/p$ turns these numbers into statements about how fast the wavelength changes.

<Step title="The two conditions in terms of p">

From part (a), the two extra terms are small next to $p^2$ when

<Formula label="Conditions on the local momentum" tex="\frac{\hbar^2\,p'^2}{p^4} \ll 1, \qquad \frac{\hbar^2\,|p''|}{|p|^3} \ll 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

(numbers of order one, such as $\frac34$ and $\frac12$, are dropped in conditions like these).

</Step>

<Step title="Derivatives of the wavelength">

<Formula label="Derivatives of the local wavelength" tex="\lambda' = -\frac{2\pi\hbar\,p'}{p^2}, \qquad \lambda'' = -\frac{2\pi\hbar\,p''}{p^2} + \frac{4\pi\hbar\,p'^2}{p^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

So $\hbar^2p'^2/p^4 = \lambda'^2/(4\pi^2)$. Multiply the second relation by $\lambda = 2\pi\hbar/p$ to find $\hbar^2p''/p^3 = \left(2\lambda'^2 - \lambda\lambda''\right)/(4\pi^2)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio and the conditions">

<Formula label="The extra terms in units of p squared" tex="\frac{X}{p^2} = \frac{\hbar^2p''}{2p^3} - \frac{3\hbar^2p'^2}{4p^4} = \frac{2\lambda'^2 - \lambda\lambda''}{8\pi^2} - \frac{3\lambda'^2}{16\pi^2} = \frac{\lambda'^2 - 2\lambda\,\lambda''}{16\pi^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The first condition reads $|\lambda'| \ll 1$ (more exactly $\lambda'^2 \ll 4\pi^2$): the wavelength changes by much less than itself over one wavelength. This is the condition the lesson found (notes eq. (3.2.28)). The second condition reads $\left|2\lambda'^2 - \lambda\lambda''\right| \ll 4\pi^2$. Once the first one holds, it adds something new:

<Formula label="Conditions on the local wavelength" tex="\left|\frac{d\lambda}{dx}\right| \ll 1, \qquad \lambda\left|\frac{d^2\lambda}{dx^2}\right| \ll 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution; the first condition is eq. (3.2.28) of the notes)" />

In words: over one wavelength, the rate of change $\lambda'$ must itself change by much less than 1.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $\lambda$ changing at a steady rate, $\lambda = \lambda_0 + c\,x$. Then $\lambda'' = 0$ and $X/p^2 = c^2/(16\pi^2)$: for $c = 1$, a wavelength that grows by its own length in one wavelength, the extra terms are still only $1/(16\pi^2) = 0.0063$ of $p^2$. The large number $16\pi^2 \approx 158$ is one reason why WKB works better than the bare condition suggests.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

This part is our addition. Near a turning point at $x = a$ a smooth potential is close to a straight line, $V - E = g\,(x - a)$ with $g > 0$, as in the lesson. In the allowed region write $s = a - x$ for the distance to the turning point, so that $p^2 = 2mg\,s$. Find the ratio $X/p^2$ as a function of $s$ (write it with $\hbar$, $m$, $g$ and $s$). How far from the turning point, in units of the length $L = \left(\hbar^2/2mg\right)^{1/3}$, must one stay for the extra terms to be less than 1% of $p^2$?

<Hint>

With $p = \sqrt{2mg}\;s^{1/2}$, the ratios $p'/p$ and $p''/p$ hold neither $m$ nor $g$: they are pure numbers divided by $s$ and by $s^2$. A derivative with respect to $x$ is minus a derivative with respect to $s$, which does not matter here: $X$ holds $p''$ and $p'^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-lin" label="$X/p^2 =$" expr="-5*hbar^2/(32*m*g*s^3)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], g: [0.5, 2], s: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-5*hbar^2/(16*s^2)", hint: "That is X itself. The box asks for X divided by p² = 2mgs." },
    { expr: "5*hbar^2/(32*m*g*s^3)", hint: "Check the sign: here p″/p and −p′²/p² are both negative, so X is negative." },
    { expr: "-hbar^2/(32*m*g*s^3)", hint: "The two terms of X add up here: −1/(8s²) from p″/(2p) and −3/(16s²) from the p′² term, together −5/(16s²)." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-dist" label="Distance at which $|X|/p^2 = 0.01$, in units of $L$:" value="3.15" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-dist" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a power law $p \propto s^{1/2}$ both extra terms go like $1/s^2$, while $p^2$ grows like $s$. The ratio falls off like $1/s^3$.

<Step title="The extra terms">

With $p = \sqrt{2mg}\;s^{1/2}$: $p'^2/p^2 = 1/(4s^2)$ and $p''/p = -1/(4s^2)$. So

<Formula label="Extra terms near a turning point" tex="X = \hbar^2\left(-\frac{1}{8s^2} - \frac{3}{16s^2}\right) = -\frac{5\hbar^2}{16\,s^2}, \qquad \frac{X}{p^2} = -\frac{5\hbar^2}{32\,mg\,s^3} = -\frac{5}{16}\left(\frac{L}{s}\right)^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own addition and solution)" />

The last form uses $L^3 = \hbar^2/(2mg)$, the length of Problem 2 and of the lesson on Airy functions.

</Step>

<Step title="One per cent">

$\frac{5}{16}\left(L/s\right)^3 = 0.01$ gives $s/L = 31.25^{1/3} = 3.15$. At the turning point itself the ratio is infinite, and the WKB solution fails, as the lesson said. But a few lengths $L$ away it is already excellent. That is why the Airy function, the exact solution for a straight-line potential, can be joined to WKB solutions on both sides.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The formula of part (b) gives the same. Here $\lambda = 2\pi\hbar/\sqrt{2mg\,s}$, so $\lambda'^2 = \lambda^2/(4s^2)$ and $\lambda\lambda'' = 3\lambda^2/(4s^2)$, and $\left(\lambda'^2 - 2\lambda\lambda''\right)/(16\pi^2) = -5\lambda^2/(64\pi^2s^2) = -5\hbar^2/(32\,mg\,s^3)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Airy functions and bound states in linear potentials" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

A particle of mass $m$ moves in the potential

<Formula label="Linear potential above a hard wall" tex="V(x) = g\,x \quad \text{for } x > 0, \qquad V(x) = \infty \quad \text{for } x \le 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, eq. (1), p. 1" />

with a constant $g > 0$ (a force, in newtons). The wall keeps the particle at $x > 0$, and the wavefunction vanishes at $x = 0$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Take the units out of the Schrödinger equation. First write $x = L\,\tilde u$, where $\tilde u$ is a pure number and $L$ is the length

<Formula label="The length scale" tex="L^3 = \frac{\hbar^2}{2mg}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

Then find a second pure number $u$, which differs from $\tilde u$ by a constant, for which the equation becomes

<Formula label="Airy's equation" tex="\frac{d^2\psi}{du^2} = u\,\psi" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), eq. (2), p. 2" />

In the box, type $u$ in terms of $x$, $L$, $g$ and the energy $E$.

<Hint>

After $x = L\tilde u$, divide the whole equation by $gL$. Which combination of $\hbar$, $m$, $g$ and $L$ then multiplies the second derivative?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The energy is now the only thing left over. A shift of the variable by a constant changes no derivative.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-u" label="$u =$" expr="x/L - E/(g*L)"
  vars={{ x: [0.5, 2], L: [0.5, 2], E: [0.5, 2], g: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x/L", hint: "That is ũ. It leaves the energy in the equation: ψ″ = (ũ − E/gL)ψ. Shift the variable to absorb it." },
    { expr: "x/L + E/(g*L)", hint: "Check the sign of the shift: u must be zero at the classical turning point, where gx = E." },
    { expr: "x/L - E/g", hint: "E/g is a length, the height of the turning point. Divide it by L to make it a pure number." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One length $L$ can be built from $\hbar$, $m$ and $g$. Measured in it, the equation has no constants left except the energy, and a shift of the origin to the turning point removes that too.

<Step title="Scale the length">

For $x > 0$ the Schrödinger equation is $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + g\,x\,\psi = E\,\psi$. With $x = L\tilde u$ and after division by $gL$:

<Formula label="The scaled equation" tex="-\frac{\hbar^2}{2mg\,L^3}\,\frac{d^2\psi}{d\tilde u^2} + \tilde u\,\psi = \frac{E}{gL}\,\psi \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{d^2\psi}{d\tilde u^2} = \left(\tilde u - \frac{E}{gL}\right)\psi" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

since $\hbar^2/(2mgL^3) = 1$ for MIT's choice of $L$.

</Step>

<Step title="Shift the origin">

<Formula label="The pure-number coordinate" tex="u = \tilde u - \frac{E}{gL} = \frac{x}{L} - \frac{E}{gL} = \frac{1}{L}\left(x - \frac{E}{g}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Then $d^2\psi/du^2 = u\,\psi$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$u = 0$ at $x = E/g$, the classical turning point: there $\psi'' = 0$, as it must be where $E = V$. This is the coordinate $u = \eta\,(x - a)$ of the lesson on Airy functions, with $\eta = 1/L$ and $a = E/g$. Units: $gL$ is a force times a length, an energy, so $E/(gL)$ is a pure number.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Forget the wall for a moment and let $u$ run over all real numbers. The equation $\psi'' = u\,\psi$ is then easy to solve in momentum space. With a pure-number momentum $k$, write

<Formula label="Fourier transform and its inverse" tex="\tilde\psi(k) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-iku}\,\psi(u)\,du, \qquad \psi(u) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{iku}\,\tilde\psi(k)\,dk" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2" />

Find the differential equation that $\tilde\psi(k)$ obeys and solve it with $\tilde\psi(0) = 1$. Then write $\psi(u)$ in the form

<Formula label="The form of the answer" tex="\psi(u) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty dk\,\cos\left(\,\cdots\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2" />

where the dots stand for a function of $k$ and $u$. The result is the Airy function, $\psi(u) = \text{Ai}(u)$. MIT gives a number to test your integral with: $\text{Ai}(1) = 0.135292$.

<Hint>

Under the transform, a derivative with respect to $u$ becomes a factor $ik$. What does a factor $u$ become? Differentiate the definition of $\tilde\psi(k)$ with respect to $k$ to see.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The equation for $\tilde\psi$ is of first order: it says that the derivative of $\ln\tilde\psi$ is a simple function of $k$. To get from the integral over all $k$ to an integral over positive $k$, compare the integrand at $k$ and at $-k$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ode" label="The equation in momentum space is" choices={["ψ̃′(k) = −k² ψ̃(k)", "ψ̃″(k) = k ψ̃(k)", "ψ̃′(k) = i k² ψ̃(k)", "ψ̃′(k) = −i k ψ̃(k)"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p2-arg" label="Argument of the cosine $=$" expr="k^3/3 + k*u"
  vars={{ k: [0.5, 2], u: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "k^3/3", hint: "That is the phase of ψ̃(k) alone. The inverse transform multiplies it by e^(iku), which adds to the phase." },
    { expr: "k^3 + k*u", hint: "Integrating k² gives k³/3." },
    { expr: "k^3/3 - k*u", hint: "The inverse transform holds e^(+iku), and ψ̃ = e^(+ik³/3): the two phases add." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In momentum space, "times $u$" is a first derivative and the second derivative is "times $-k^2$". A second-order equation in $u$ becomes a first-order equation in $k$.

<Step title="The equation in momentum space">

Two integrations by parts (the boundary terms vanish for a wavefunction that dies away) give the transform of $\psi''$ as $(ik)^2\tilde\psi = -k^2\tilde\psi$. And

<Formula label="Transform of u times ψ" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-iku}\,u\,\psi(u)\,du = i\,\frac{d}{dk}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-iku}\,\psi(u)\,du = i\,\frac{d\tilde\psi}{dk}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So $\psi'' = u\psi$ becomes $-k^2\tilde\psi = i\,d\tilde\psi/dk$:

<Formula label="Airy's equation in momentum space" tex="\frac{d\tilde\psi}{dk} = i\,k^2\,\tilde\psi \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \tilde\psi(k) = e^{\,ik^3/3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

with $\tilde\psi(0) = 1$. It is a pure phase: its size is 1 for every $k$.

</Step>

<Step title="Back to position space">

<Formula label="Integral for the Airy function" tex="\psi(u) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{\,i\left(\frac{k^3}{3} + ku\right)}\,dk = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty \cos\left(\frac{k^3}{3} + ku\right)dk = \text{Ai}(u)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The phase is odd in $k$, so the integrand at $-k$ is the complex conjugate of the integrand at $k$: the two halves of the line add up to twice the real part of one half. This is the integral of the lesson on Airy functions.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The cosine does not die away at large $k$. It only oscillates faster and faster, so a computer needs help with this integral. One way (our addition): turn the path of integration from the positive real axis to the ray $k = t\,e^{i\pi/6}$, on which $ik^3/3 = -t^3/3$. The integral then converges fast:

<Formula label="The Airy integral on a turned path" tex="\text{Ai}(u) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-\frac{t^3}{3} - \frac{ut}{2}}\,\cos\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\,u\,t + \frac{\pi}{6}\right)dt" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own addition)" />

For $u = 1$ it gives 0.135292, MIT's value. As a second test, the second derivative of this integral at $u = 1.5$ equals 1.5 times the integral, as $\psi'' = u\psi$ demands.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Formula label="Wavefunction above the wall" tex="\psi(x) \propto \text{Ai}\!\left(\frac{x}{L} - \frac{E}{gL}\right), \qquad x > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Use the connection formulae to show that, where the WKB approximation holds,

<Formula label="Quantization with two soft turning points" tex="\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{-x_0}^{x_0}\sqrt{2m\left(E_n - V(x)\right)}\;dx = \left(n + \frac12\right)\pi, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), eq. (1), p. 4" />

<Hint>

Beyond each turning point the wavefunction must be a pure decaying exponential. Each of the two connection formulae for a decaying exponential then gives you the wavefunction inside the well: one written from the left turning point, one from the right.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Both expressions describe one wavefunction, so they must be proportional for every $x$ in the well. Write the phase of one cosine as the phase of the other plus a constant. For which values of that constant are two cosines proportional?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Start outside on each side with the solution that decays. Each side fixes a cosine inside. The two cosines agree only for special values of the total phase across the well.

<Step title="From the right">

For $x > x_0$ the wavefunction is $\frac{A}{\sqrt\kappa}\exp\left(-\int_{x_0}^x\kappa\,dx'\right)$. The connection formula for a forbidden region on the right turns it into

<Formula label="Inside the well, seen from the right" tex="\psi(x) = \frac{2A}{\sqrt{k(x)}}\,\cos\left(\int_x^{x_0}k\,dx' - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)" cite="reading1" at="notes eq. (3.3.1)" />

</Step>

<Step title="From the left">

For $x < -x_0$ the wavefunction is $\frac{B}{\sqrt\kappa}\exp\left(-\int_x^{-x_0}\kappa\,dx'\right)$. The connection formula for a forbidden region on the left gives

<Formula label="Inside the well, seen from the left" tex="\psi(x) = \frac{2B}{\sqrt{k(x)}}\,\cos\left(\int_{-x_0}^{x}k\,dx' - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)" cite="reading1" at="notes eq. (3.4.45)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two must agree">

Call the total phase $\theta = \int_{-x_0}^{x_0}k\,dx'$. Then $\int_x^{x_0}k\,dx' = \theta - \int_{-x_0}^x k\,dx'$, and since the cosine is even the first expression is

<Formula label="The right-hand form rewritten" tex="\psi(x) = \frac{2A}{\sqrt{k(x)}}\,\cos\left(\left[\int_{-x_0}^{x}k\,dx' - \frac{\pi}{4}\right] - \left[\theta - \frac{\pi}{2}\right]\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

The square bracket on the left is the phase of the second expression. Two cosines whose phases differ by a constant are proportional for every $x$ only if that constant is a whole multiple of $\pi$:

<Formula label="The condition on the total phase" tex="\theta - \frac{\pi}{2} = n\pi \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \int_{-x_0}^{x_0}k(x)\,dx = \left(n + \frac12\right)\pi, \qquad B = (-1)^n A" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

Since $\theta > 0$, $n$ cannot be negative. With $\hbar k = \sqrt{2m\left(E - V\right)}$ this is the formula to be shown.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$B = (-1)^nA$ says that the state is even for even $n$ and odd for odd $n$, as it must be in a symmetric potential. ## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Tunnelling from perturbation theory" points={20} mit="Problem 6">

The mark of tunnelling is a rate that is exponentially small, with an exponent that grows with the width of the barrier and with the square root of its height. This problem finds an exponential of that kind in a quite different way: from degenerate perturbation theory at high order.

Picture a particle on a line. The potential is zero at the two ends, and in between there is a barrier of height $V_0$ and width $W$. For an energy far below $V_0$ the WKB probability to cross the barrier is $\exp\left(-2W\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar\right)$.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

First let the particle sit only on the sites $0, 1, \dots, N$. The state $|x\rangle$ has the particle on site $x$. The Hamiltonian has two terms. The "barrier" term is a potential $V_0$ on every site except the two end sites:

<Formula label="Barrier term" tex="H_0 = V_0\sum_{x=1}^{N-1}|x\rangle\langle x|" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), eq. (3), p. 5" />

The "hopping" term moves the particle to a neighbouring site:

<Formula label="Hopping term" tex="\delta H = -\lambda\sum_{x=1}^{N}\Big(|x-1\rangle\langle x| + |x\rangle\langle x-1|\Big)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), eq. (4), p. 5" />

with $0 < \lambda \ll V_0$. (MIT's Figure 1 shows the case $N = 5$: six sites in a row, numbered 0 to 5. The barrier potential is 0 on sites 0 and 5 and $V_0$ on sites 1 to 4, and each of the five links between neighbours carries the hopping strength $-\lambda$.)

Without hopping there are two ground states of energy zero, $|0\rangle$ and $|N\rangle$. More useful are their sum and difference:

<Formula label="Even and odd ground states" tex="|g_+\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle + |N\rangle}{\sqrt2}, \qquad |g_-\rangle = \frac{|0\rangle - |N\rangle}{\sqrt2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 5" />

The other states, $|1\rangle$ to $|N-1\rangle$, all have the energy $V_0$. At a high enough order of perturbation theory the hopping lifts the degeneracy of the two ground states. The correction of order $k$ to the energy of $|g_\pm\rangle$ is

<Formula label="Energy correction of order k" tex="E_{g_\pm}^{(k)} = \sum_{m_1}\cdots\sum_{m_{k-1}}\frac{\delta H_{g_\pm,m_{k-1}}\cdots\delta H_{m_2,m_1}\,\delta H_{m_1,g_\pm}}{\left(E_{g_\pm}^{0} - E_{m_1}^{0}\right)\cdots\left(E_{g_\pm}^{0} - E_{m_{k-1}}^{0}\right)} + \text{other terms}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), eq. (5), p. 6" />

where $m_1, \dots, m_{k-1}$ run over all states outside the degenerate pair, and where no term of this kind couples $|g_+\rangle$ to $|g_-\rangle$. MIT states that the "other terms" start to matter only at higher $k$ and may be left out here.

What is the smallest $k$ for which $E_{g_-}^{(k)} - E_{g_+}^{(k)}$ is not zero? Find the splitting $E_{g_-} - E_{g_+}$ at that order.

<Hint>

Each factor $\delta H$ moves the particle by one site. Read the numerator from right to left as a walk: it starts at an end site, visits barrier sites only, and returns to an end site.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $\langle g_\pm|\cdots|g_\pm\rangle$ out with $|0\rangle$ and $|N\rangle$. The walks from 0 back to 0 and from $N$ back to $N$ give the same for both signs. Only the walks from one end to the other tell $|g_+\rangle$ from $|g_-\rangle$. What is the shortest such walk?

</Hint>

<Hint>

On that shortest walk, count the hops (each a factor $-\lambda$) and the intermediate states (each a factor $1/(0 - V_0)$).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-split" label="$E_{g_-} - E_{g_+} =$" expr="2*lam^N/V0^(N-1)"
  vars={{ lam: [0.1, 0.5], V0: [1, 2], N: [2, 6] }}

  mistakes={[

    { expr: "2*lam^N/V0^N", hint: "A walk of N hops passes N − 1 intermediate states, so there are N − 1 energy denominators, not N." },
    { expr: "-2*lam^N/V0^(N-1)", hint: "Check the signs: N factors of −λ over N − 1 factors of −V₀ leave one minus sign, in the cross term of |g₊⟩. So g₊ moves down and E(g₋) − E(g₊) is positive." },

<Solution>

**The idea.** To tell the sum of $|0\rangle$ and $|N\rangle$ from their difference, the perturbation must carry the particle from one end to the other. Each hop costs a factor $\lambda/V_0$, and there are $N$ of them: an exponential in the width.

<Step title="Which walks count">

With $|g_\pm\rangle = \left(|0\rangle \pm |N\rangle\right)/\sqrt2$, any product $M$ of hops and denominators has

<Formula label="Diagonal and cross terms" tex="\langle g_\pm|M|g_\pm\rangle = \frac12\Big(\langle 0|M|0\rangle + \langle N|M|N\rangle\Big) \pm \frac12\Big(\langle 0|M|N\rangle + \langle N|M|0\rangle\Big)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 6 (our own solution)" />

The first bracket is the same for both states and drops out of the difference. The second needs a walk from one end to the other. Each $\delta H$ moves the particle one site, so the walk needs at least $N$ factors: the first order at which the two energies differ is $k = N$. (At that order the walk goes straight through the sites $1, 2, \dots, N-1$, which all lie outside the degenerate pair, as the formula requires.)

</Step>

<Step title="The straight walk">

The straight walk has $N$ hops, each with the matrix element $-\lambda$, and $N - 1$ intermediate states, each with the denominator $0 - V_0$:

<Formula label="The straight walk" tex="\langle N|M|0\rangle = \langle 0|M|N\rangle = \frac{(-\lambda)^N}{(-V_0)^{N-1}} = -\frac{\lambda^N}{V_0^{N-1}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The splitting">

The cross term is $\pm\frac12\cdot2\cdot\left(-\lambda^N/V_0^{N-1}\right)$:

<Formula label="Splitting of the two ground states" tex="E_{g_\pm}^{(N)} = \mp\frac{\lambda^N}{V_0^{N-1}} + (\text{same for both}), \qquad E_{g_-} - E_{g_+} = \frac{2\lambda^N}{V_0^{N-1}} = 2V_0\left(\frac{\lambda}{V_0}\right)^N" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 6 (our own solution)" />

It falls off exponentially with $N$: $2V_0\,e^{-N\ln(V_0/\lambda)}$. But it depends on $V_0$ through a power and a logarithm, not through a square root in the exponent as in WKB.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The symmetric state $|g_+\rangle$ lies lower, as a ground state should. Two small cases can be solved exactly (our addition). For $N = 2$ the state $|g_-\rangle$ does not couple to the middle site at all, so its energy stays 0, while $|g_+\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ form a two by two matrix with the lower eigenvalue $\frac12\left(V_0 - \sqrt{V_0^2 + 8\lambda^2}\right) \approx -2\lambda^2/V_0$: the splitting is $2\lambda^2/V_0$. For $N = 3$ the even and the odd states see two by two matrices with the barrier energies $V_0 - \lambda$ and $V_0 + \lambda$; their lowest eigenvalues are close to $-\lambda^2/(V_0 - \lambda)$ and $-\lambda^2/(V_0 + \lambda)$, which differ by $2\lambda^3/(V_0^2 - \lambda^2) \approx 2\lambda^3/V_0^2$. Both agree with the formula.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Now suppose the chain of sites came from a particle on a line, with space cut into steps of length $\ell$ and $p^2/2m$ replaced by a finite-difference operator. The operator that stands for $d^2/dx^2$ is

<Formula label="Finite-difference second derivative" tex="D_\ell^2 = \frac{1}{\ell^2}\sum_x\Big(-2\,|x\rangle\langle x| + |x\rangle\langle x+1| + |x\rangle\langle x-1|\Big)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 6" />

Leave out its diagonal part. The kinetic energy $p^2/2m$ is then the hopping term $\delta H$ of part (a). What is the matching value of $\lambda$?

Let the potential be a square barrier of width $W$, and take $\ell = W/N$, so that the barrier is $N$ sites wide. As $\ell$ shrinks, $N = W/\ell$ grows and the splitting of part (a) falls. That is an artefact: the physics should not depend on the step $\ell$, which we would like to send to zero. But $\ell$ cannot be made as small as we like, because $\lambda$ would grow without limit and $\lambda/V_0$ would no longer be small. So fix the small ratio:

<Formula label="The perturbation condition" tex="\frac{\lambda}{V_0} = \epsilon \ll 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 6" />

with a constant $\epsilon$. Check that $\ell^2V_0$ then stays constant as $\ell \to 0$. Remove $\ell$ and estimate the splitting as a function of $\epsilon$, $W$, $V_0$, $m$ and $\hbar$.

<Hint>

$p^2/2m = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$. Replace the second derivative by $D_\ell^2$ and read off the number in front of the hopping terms.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Solve $\lambda = \epsilon V_0$ for $\ell$. Then $N = W/\ell$ is known. Write your splitting of part (a) with the ratio $\lambda/V_0 = \epsilon$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-splitb" label="Splitting $=$" expr="2*V0*eps^(W*sqrt(2*m*eps*V0)/hbar)"
  vars={{ V0: [1, 2], eps: [0.1, 0.5], W: [1, 2], m: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ V0: "V_0", eps: "\\epsilon", hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*V0*eps^(W*sqrt(2*m*V0)/hbar)", hint: "The step is ℓ = ħ/√(2mεV₀), with ε under the root. So N = W/ℓ holds a factor √ε." },
    { expr: "2*V0*eps^(W*sqrt(2*m*V0/eps)/hbar)", hint: "A smaller ε means a larger step ℓ and so fewer sites: N must fall when ε falls." },
    { expr: "2*eps*V0*eps^(W*sqrt(2*m*eps*V0)/hbar)", hint: "2λ^N/V₀^(N−1) is 2V₀(λ/V₀)^N: one factor V₀ in front, and ε only to the power N." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On a lattice the kinetic energy is what lets the particle hop. A finer lattice means stronger hopping, so the demand $\lambda \ll V_0$ sets a smallest allowed step.

<Step title="The hopping strength">

<Formula label="Kinetic energy on the lattice" tex="\frac{p^2}{2m} \rightarrow -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,D_\ell^2 = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m\ell^2}\sum_x\Big(|x\rangle\langle x+1| + |x\rangle\langle x-1|\Big) + \frac{\hbar^2}{m\ell^2}\sum_x|x\rangle\langle x| \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \lambda = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m\ell^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 6 (our own solution)" />

The last sum is the diagonal part that MIT says to leave out (it shifts every site by the same energy).

</Step>

<Step title="The step is fixed">

$\lambda = \epsilon V_0$ means

<Formula label="The lattice step" tex="\ell^2\,V_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m\epsilon} = \text{constant}, \qquad \ell = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2m\epsilon V_0}}, \qquad N = \frac{W}{\ell} = \frac{W\sqrt{2m\epsilon V_0}}{\hbar}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The splitting">

<Formula label="Splitting without the lattice step" tex="E_{g_-} - E_{g_+} = 2V_0\,\epsilon^N = 2V_0\,\epsilon^{\,W\sqrt{2m\epsilon V_0}/\hbar} = 2V_0\,\exp\left(-\sqrt\epsilon\,\ln\frac{1}{\epsilon}\cdot\frac{W\sqrt{2mV_0}}{\hbar}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\sqrt{2mV_0}$ is a momentum, and $W$ times a momentum over $\hbar$ is a pure number, as an exponent must be. The exponent now holds $W\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar$, the very combination of the WKB formula. A plane wave on the lattice gives a test of $\lambda$: the hopping term and the diagonal part together give it the energy $2\lambda\left(1 - \cos q\ell\right)$ for a wavenumber $q$, which is $\lambda\,\ell^2q^2 = \hbar^2q^2/2m$ when $q\ell$ is small.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Parts (a) and (b) gave a splitting of energies, not yet tunnelling. So take the Hamiltonian to be simply

<Formula label="Two-state Hamiltonian" tex="H = E_+\,|g_+\rangle\langle g_+| + E_-\,|g_-\rangle\langle g_-|, \qquad \Delta \equiv E_- - E_+" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 6" />

The particle starts at time 0 in the state $|0\rangle$. After a time $t$ we measure whether it is on site 0 or on site $N$. At what time $t$ is it first found on site $N$ with probability 1?

Take the tunnelling rate to be $1/t$ for that time (this choice is ours; MIT asks for a tunnelling probability per unit time). With the splitting of part (b), how does the rate depend on $W$ and $V_0$? You should find a number times $\exp\left(-c\,W\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar\right)$, where the pure number $c$ depends on $\epsilon$ only. How does that compare with the WKB prediction? The last box is our addition: find the largest value that $c$ can take for $0 < \epsilon < 1$.

<Hint>

Write $|0\rangle$ with $|g_+\rangle$ and $|g_-\rangle$, let each part pick up its phase, and take the inner product with $|N\rangle$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the last box: write $c$ as a function of $\epsilon$ and set its derivative to zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-scale" label="Compared with the 2 in the exponent of the WKB probability, the number $c$ is" choices={["the same: c = 2 for every ε", "always larger than 2", "always smaller than 1", "larger than 2 only for small ε"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p6-c" label="Largest value of $c$:" value="0.736" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The state $|0\rangle$ is an equal mix of $|g_+\rangle$ and $|g_-\rangle$. Their relative phase turns at the rate $\Delta/\hbar$, and when it has turned by $\pi$ the mix has become $|N\rangle$.

<Step title="The time">

$|0\rangle = \left(|g_+\rangle + |g_-\rangle\right)/\sqrt2$ and $\langle N|g_\pm\rangle = \pm1/\sqrt2$. So

<Formula label="Probability to be on the far site" tex="\langle N|\Psi(t)\rangle = \frac12\left(e^{-iE_+t/\hbar} - e^{-iE_-t/\hbar}\right), \qquad P_N(t) = \sin^2\left(\frac{\Delta\,t}{2\hbar}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 6 (our own solution)" />

It first reaches 1 at

<Formula label="Time to cross" tex="t = \frac{\pi\hbar}{\Delta}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The rate">

With the splitting of part (b):

<Formula label="Tunnelling rate from perturbation theory" tex="\frac{1}{t} = \frac{\Delta}{\pi\hbar} = \frac{2V_0}{\pi\hbar}\,\exp\left(-c\,\frac{W\sqrt{2mV_0}}{\hbar}\right), \qquad c = \sqrt\epsilon\,\ln\frac{1}{\epsilon}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), pp. 6 and 7 (our own solution)" />

The exponent grows in step with the width $W$ and with the square root of the height $V_0$: exactly the scaling of the WKB probability $\exp\left(-2W\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar\right)$. Only the number in front differs, $c$ in place of 2 (and the factor in front of the exponential grows with $V_0$).

</Step>

<Step title="The largest c">

$\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\left[\sqrt\epsilon\,\ln(1/\epsilon)\right] = \frac{1}{\sqrt\epsilon}\left[\frac12\ln(1/\epsilon) - 1\right] = 0$ at $\epsilon = e^{-2} = 0.135$, where $c = 2/e = 0.736$. So $c$ is always below 1, and for the small $\epsilon$ that perturbation theory needs it is smaller still.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The fair comparison is with an amplitude, not a probability. This rate is a splitting over $\hbar$, and Problem 5 showed that the splitting in a double well goes like $e^{-\phi}$, with $\phi = W\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar$ for a square barrier and a low energy: the square root of the WKB probability, a number 1 in front of the exponent. The chain gives $c \le 0.736$ in place of that 1. It tunnels too easily, and the reason is the coarse lattice: the condition $\lambda \ll V_0$ forces a step $\ell = \hbar/\sqrt{2m\epsilon V_0}$ that is $1/\sqrt\epsilon$ times longer than the decay length $\hbar/\sqrt{2mV_0}$ of the true wavefunction in the barrier. Perturbation theory gets the form of the answer, an exponential in $W\sqrt{V_0}$, and not its number.

</Step>

</Solution>
