The last video of Lecture 6 opens a new chapter of 8.06: approximations for potentials that vary **slowly in space**, after perturbation theory's small additions to the Hamiltonian; approximations for changes in time come later in the course (L6.5 transcript p. 1). Like the notes, this chapter works in one dimension. (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture and his lecture notes, chapter 3; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## WKB and the semiclassical idea

The method is named after Wentzel, Kramers and Brillouin, who found it in 1926, the year so much of quantum mechanics fell into place. The mathematician Harold Jeffreys had found it three years earlier, in 1923, without much notice; some authors write JWKB, and Zwiebach keeps the usual WKB (L6.5 transcript p. 1). It is also called the **semiclassical approximation**, because classical physics gives the intuition for the quantum wavefunction.

The key quantity is the de Broglie wavelength:

<Formula label="De Broglie wavelength" tex="\lambda = \frac{h}{p}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.1.1)" />

Electrons passing through a slit much wider than their wavelength travel almost like classical particles; when the wavelength is comparable with the slit, they diffract. So, as a first guess, semiclassical means $\lambda$ much smaller than the length scale $L$ of the problem (L6.5 transcript pp. 1–2). Formally this is the limit $\hbar \to 0$. That is odd, since $\hbar$ is a constant of nature, and a constant with units at that: we can only imagine worlds with ever smaller $\hbar$, in which quantum effects set in at ever smaller scales. Unlike perturbation theory's unit-free $\lambda$, a small $\hbar$ is a subtle expansion parameter, and much of the chapter is about what "small" should really mean. The refined answer will be that a suitably defined local wavelength must vary slowly (L6.5 transcript p. 2).

## Local momentum and the Schrödinger equation

Take a particle of energy $E$ in a potential $V(x)$ (L6.5 transcript pp. 2–3). Classically, $E - V(x)$ is its kinetic energy at $x$, which defines a **local momentum** and a **local de Broglie wavelength**:

<Formula label="Local momentum" tex="p^2(x) \equiv 2m\left(E - V(x)\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.1.2)" />

<Formula label="Local de Broglie wavelength" tex="\lambda(x) \equiv \frac{h}{p(x)} = \frac{2\pi\hbar}{p(x)}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.1.3)" />

De Broglie's wavelength was defined for a free particle, whose wavefunction $e^{ipx/\hbar}$ is a wave of exactly that wavelength; how the local version shows up in a real solution is still to be discovered. The Schrödinger equation itself already looks friendly in this language. Moving $2m$ to the left,

<Formula label="Schrödinger equation with the local momentum" tex="-\hbar^2\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = p^2(x)\,\psi, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad \hat p^2\,\psi = p^2(x)\,\psi" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (3.1.4)–(3.1.6)" />

(Zwiebach writes it in three dimensions, with the Laplacian, L6.5 transcript p. 3.) No approximation has been made: this is exact. It looks like an eigenvalue equation for $\hat p^2$, but it is not one, since the right side multiplies $\psi$ by a **function** of position rather than a number (L6.5 transcript p. 3).

Two cases need names (L6.5 transcript p. 4). In a **classically allowed** region, $E > V(x)$, define the local wavenumber $k(x)$; in a **forbidden** region, $V(x) > E$, $p^2$ is negative, and define the local decay constant $\kappa(x)$, like the penetration constant of decaying wavefunctions:

<Formula label="Local wavenumber and decay constant" tex="p^2(x) = 2m\left(E - V(x)\right) = \hbar^2k^2(x), \qquad -p^2(x) = 2m\left(V(x) - E\right) = \hbar^2\kappa^2(x)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (3.1.7), (3.1.8)" />

## The wavefunction in polar form

One more piece of intuition: write the wavefunction like a complex number, as a magnitude times a phase (L6.5 transcript p. 4). With real functions $\rho$ and $S$,

<Formula label="Polar form" tex="\psi(\vec x, t) = \sqrt{\rho(\vec x, t)}\;\exp\!\left(\frac{i}{\hbar}S(\vec x, t)\right), \qquad |\psi|^2 = \rho" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (3.1.9), (3.1.10)" />

$S$ has the units of $\hbar$ (an action, or an angular momentum), and its name is no accident. The WKB solutions will come in exactly this form, so it pays to know what $\rho$ and $S$ mean. $\rho$ is the probability density. For the current, take the gradient and multiply by $\psi^*$ (L6.5 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="The gradient, times ψ*" tex="\psi^*\nabla\psi = \frac12\nabla\rho + \frac{i}{\hbar}\,\rho\,\nabla S" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (3.1.11), (3.1.12)" />

and the probability current is

<Formula label="Probability current in polar form" tex="\vec J = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\psi^*\nabla\psi\right) = \frac{\rho\,\nabla S}{m}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (3.1.13), (3.1.14)" />

The phase alone fixes the direction of flow: the current is perpendicular to the surfaces of constant $S$, the surfaces of constant phase. For a fluid of density $\rho$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ the current is $\rho\vec v$, so this suggests

<Formula label="The phase gradient as a momentum" tex="\vec p(\vec x) \leftrightarrow \nabla S" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.1.15)" />

This is an analogy, not a rule (L6.5 transcript pp. 5–6): in quantum mechanics momentum is an operator whose eigenvalues are numbers, while $\nabla S$ is a function. But for a free particle it is exact. Its wavefunction $e^{i\vec p\cdot\vec x/\hbar - iEt/\hbar}$ has $\rho = 1$ and

<Formula label="Free particle" tex="S = \vec p\cdot\vec x - Et, \qquad \nabla S = \vec p" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.1.16)" />

Its time derivative is $\partial S/\partial t = -E$ (L6.5 transcript p. 6). In advanced classical mechanics, the action viewed as a function of the coordinates has exactly these properties: its gradient is the momentum and its time derivative is minus the energy. That is one more reason for the name semiclassical (L6.5 transcript p. 6). In the WKB solutions, $S$ will depend on $x$ through the local momentum $p(x)$.

<WorkedExample title="An electron climbing a slowly rising potential">
<Problem>
An electron with $E = 10$ eV moves through a region where the potential slowly rises. Use $hc = 1239.84$ eV nm, $\hbar c = 197.33$ eV nm and $mc^2 = 511.0$ keV. (a) Find its local de Broglie wavelength where $V = 0$ and where $V = 7.5$ eV. (b) Where $V = 12$ eV, find $\kappa$ and the decay length $1/\kappa$. (c) If $V$ rises from 0 to 7.5 eV over 10 nm, is a semiclassical treatment reasonable?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The local wavelength">
$\lambda = hc/\sqrt{2mc^2\,(E - V)}$. At $V = 0$: $\sqrt{2 \times 511{,}000 \times 10}$ eV $\approx 3{,}197$ eV, so $\lambda \approx 0.388$ nm. At $V = 7.5$ eV the kinetic energy is 2.5 eV, four times smaller, so $p$ halves and $\lambda \approx 0.776$ nm: the wave stretches as the electron slows.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The forbidden region">
$\hbar\kappa c = \sqrt{2mc^2\,(V - E)} = \sqrt{2 \times 511{,}000 \times 2}$ eV $\approx 1{,}430$ eV, so $\kappa \approx 7.25$ per nm and the wavefunction decays over $1/\kappa \approx 0.14$ nm.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Slowly varying?">
Over 10 nm the wavelength stays between about 0.4 and 0.8 nm, so the potential changes little within one wavelength: the semiclassical picture is reasonable. If the same rise happened over 0.5 nm, about one wavelength, it would not be. (This rough criterion is the one the lecture starts from; the next lecture makes it precise.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- WKB (Wentzel, Kramers, Brillouin 1926; Jeffreys 1923) treats slowly varying potentials, formally as an expansion in small $\hbar$.
- Local momentum $p^2(x) = 2m(E - V(x))$ and local wavelength $\lambda(x) = h/p(x)$; the Schrödinger equation is exactly $\hat p^2\psi = p^2(x)\psi$. Allowed regions: $p = \hbar k(x)$; forbidden: $-p^2 = \hbar^2\kappa^2(x)$.
- In polar form $\psi = \sqrt\rho\,e^{iS/\hbar}$, $\rho$ is the probability density and $\vec J = \rho\nabla S/m$, so $\nabla S$ acts like the momentum: exactly so for a free particle, with $\partial S/\partial t = -E$, as for a classical action.
