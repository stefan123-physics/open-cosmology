Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `2*(a + b)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent, so where two of them are related the problem says which to use: in Problem 1(b) type both `ma` and `mb`, and do not replace one by the other plus or minus 1. Write the symbols as the box names them: `hbar` stands for $\hbar$, `xab` for $|x_{\alpha\beta}|$, `x2a` and `x2b` for $\langle\alpha|x^2|\alpha\rangle$ and $\langle\beta|x^2|\beta\rangle$, `xa` and `xb` for $\langle\alpha|x|\alpha\rangle$ and $\langle\beta|x|\beta\rangle$, `ma` and `mb` for $m_a$ and $m_b$, and `eps` for $\epsilon$.

Give a number answer to 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. Where a box needs more digits, its label says so.

MIT gives the points per problem. The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to write down or explain something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons. Operators carry no hats. A product state $|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle$ has particle 1 in the state $\alpha$ and particle 2 in the state $\beta$, and swapping the particles swaps the two states. For spin $\frac12$ the states are $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$, and $|+-\rangle$ is short for $|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle$. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The exchange force" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

Identical particles in a symmetric state behave as if they attract each other, and in an antisymmetric state as if they repel. No force is involved: the effect comes from the symmetry of the state alone. This problem measures it, first for positions and then for spins.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Let $|\alpha\rangle$ and $|\beta\rangle$ be two orthogonal, normalized states of one particle in one dimension, with wavefunctions $\psi_\alpha(x) = \langle x|\alpha\rangle$ and $\psi_\beta(x) = \langle x|\beta\rangle$. From them build three states of two particles: one as for distinguishable particles, one symmetric and one antisymmetric.

<Formula label="The distinguishable, symmetric and antisymmetric states" tex="|\Psi_D\rangle = |\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle, \qquad |\Psi_S\rangle = \frac{|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle + |\beta\rangle\otimes|\alpha\rangle}{\sqrt2}, \qquad |\Psi_A\rangle = \frac{|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle - |\beta\rangle\otimes|\alpha\rangle}{\sqrt2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), eqs. (1)–(3), p. 1" />

The mean square distance between the particles in each state is

<Formula label="Mean square separation in the three states" tex="D_D = \langle\Psi_D|\left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2|\Psi_D\rangle, \qquad D_S = \langle\Psi_S|\left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2|\Psi_S\rangle, \qquad D_A = \langle\Psi_A|\left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2|\Psi_A\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

First find $D_D$ in terms of the averages of $x$ and of $x^2$ in the two states, $\langle\alpha|x|\alpha\rangle$, $\langle\alpha|x^2|\alpha\rangle$ and the same for $\beta$ (this box is our addition). Then calculate $D_S - D_D$ and $D_A - D_D$. Write them with the size of the matrix element $x_{\alpha\beta} \equiv \langle\alpha|x|\beta\rangle$ (our name), typed `xab`. Explain the signs in a few words.

At the end, an example of our own: an oscillator whose ground state is proportional to $\exp\left(-x^2/2b^2\right)$, with $\alpha$ the ground state and $\beta$ the first excited state. For these, the average of $x$ is zero in both states, $\langle\alpha|x^2|\alpha\rangle = \frac12b^2$, $\langle\beta|x^2|\beta\rangle = \frac32b^2$ and $x_{\alpha\beta} = b/\sqrt2$. Give $D_S$ and $D_A$ in units of $b^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The average in $\Psi_S$ or $\Psi_A$ is the average in the product state plus or minus an exchange term, and only the cross term $-2x_1x_2$ has an exchange term that survives.

<Step title="The product state">

In $|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle$ the average of $x_1^2$ is $\langle\alpha|x^2|\alpha\rangle$, that of $x_2^2$ is $\langle\beta|x^2|\beta\rangle$, and the average of $x_1x_2$ factorizes:

<Formula label="Mean square separation in the product state" tex="D_D = \langle\alpha|x^2|\alpha\rangle + \langle\beta|x^2|\beta\rangle - 2\,\langle\alpha|x|\alpha\rangle\,\langle\beta|x|\beta\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Direct and exchange terms">

Call $O = \left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2$. It is a symmetric operator: it does not change when the labels 1 and 2 are swapped. In the symmetric state,

<Formula label="Average in the symmetric state" tex="\langle\Psi_S|O|\Psi_S\rangle = \tfrac12\Big[\langle\alpha\beta|O|\alpha\beta\rangle + \langle\beta\alpha|O|\beta\alpha\rangle + \langle\alpha\beta|O|\beta\alpha\rangle + \langle\beta\alpha|O|\alpha\beta\rangle\Big]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Here $|\alpha\beta\rangle$ is short for $|\alpha\rangle\otimes|\beta\rangle$. In the antisymmetric state the last two terms, the exchange terms, get a minus sign. The first two terms are both equal to $D_D$, because $O$ is symmetric. The last two are complex conjugates of each other.

</Step>

<Step title="The exchange term">

Take the three pieces of $O$ one at a time. For $x_1^2$ the exchange term is $\langle\alpha|x^2|\beta\rangle\,\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle = 0$, because the two states are orthogonal, and the same holds for $x_2^2$. For the cross term nothing is left over to give an overlap:

<Formula label="The exchange term of the mean square separation" tex="\langle\alpha\beta|\left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2|\beta\alpha\rangle = -2\,\langle\alpha|x|\beta\rangle\,\langle\beta|x|\alpha\rangle = -2\,|x_{\alpha\beta}|^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

It is real, so the two exchange terms are equal, and

<Formula label="The exchange effect on the separation" tex="D_S - D_D = -2\,|x_{\alpha\beta}|^2, \qquad D_A - D_D = +2\,|x_{\alpha\beta}|^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The signs">

Particles in the symmetric state are closer together, on average, than distinguishable particles in the same two states, and particles in the antisymmetric state are farther apart. The reason is in the wavefunctions. $\Psi_A(x_1, x_2) = \left[\psi_\alpha(x_1)\psi_\beta(x_2) - \psi_\beta(x_1)\psi_\alpha(x_2)\right]/\sqrt2$ is zero whenever $x_1 = x_2$: the two particles are never found at the same point. In $\Psi_S$ the two terms are equal at $x_1 = x_2$ and add: the probability to find the particles at the same point is twice that of the product state. The notes show the same thing for two electrons (the "exchange density", eqs. (8.5.14) to (8.5.16), p. 166).

It looks like an attraction and a repulsion, which is why people speak of an exchange force. But the Hamiltonian played no part: nothing in the calculation used it. The effect also disappears when the two wavefunctions do not overlap, because then $x_{\alpha\beta} = \int\psi_\alpha^*\,x\,\psi_\beta\,dx = 0$ (compare notes eqs. (8.5.21) and (8.5.22), p. 167).

</Step>

<Step title="The oscillator example">

With the values given, $D_D = \frac12b^2 + \frac32b^2 - 0 = 2b^2$ and $2|x_{\alpha\beta}|^2 = b^2$, so $D_S = b^2$ and $D_A = 3b^2$: one, two and three times $b^2$ for the symmetric, the product and the antisymmetric state.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two shifts are equal and opposite, as they must be: the average of $D_S$ and $D_A$ is $D_D$, because $|\Psi_D\rangle = \left(|\Psi_S\rangle + |\Psi_A\rangle\right)/\sqrt2$ and a symmetric operator has no matrix element between a symmetric and an antisymmetric state.

The oscillator numbers can be checked another way. There $\Psi_S$ is proportional to $(x_1 + x_2)\exp\left(-(x_1^2 + x_2^2)/2b^2\right)$ and $\Psi_A$ to $(x_1 - x_2)\exp\left(-(x_1^2 + x_2^2)/2b^2\right)$. In terms of $u = (x_1 - x_2)/\sqrt2$, the first is a ground state in $u$, with $\langle u^2\rangle = \frac12b^2$, and the second a first excited state, with $\langle u^2\rangle = \frac32b^2$. Since $\left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2 = 2u^2$, that gives $b^2$ and $3b^2$ again.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in part (a): a direct term, the same for all three states, plus or minus an exchange term. The exchange term exists only when one step of the ladder operators can swap $m_a$ and $m_b$.

<Step title="Reduce to the dot product">

Each spin has $\vec S^{\,2} = \hbar^2s(s+1)$ in every state used here, so

<Formula label="The squared difference of two spins" tex="\left(\vec S_1 - \vec S_2\right)^2 = \vec S_1^{\,2} + \vec S_2^{\,2} - 2\,\vec S_1\cdot\vec S_2 = 2\hbar^2s(s+1) - 2\,\vec S_1\cdot\vec S_2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

and only the average of $\vec S_1\cdot\vec S_2$ is needed. With $S_\pm = S_x \pm iS_y$ for each particle,

<Formula label="The dot product with ladder operators" tex="\vec S_1\cdot\vec S_2 = S_{1z}S_{2z} + \tfrac12\left(S_{1+}S_{2-} + S_{1-}S_{2+}\right)" cite="reading4" at="eq. (2.20)" />

<Formula label="Action of the ladder operators on a spin state" tex="S_\pm|s, m\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{s(s+1) - m(m\pm1)}\;|s, m\pm1\rangle" cite="reading3" at="eq. (2.29)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The product state">

In $|s, m_a\rangle\otimes|s, m_b\rangle$ the term $S_{1z}S_{2z}$ has the value $\hbar^2m_am_b$. The ladder terms change both values of $m$, so they give a state orthogonal to the one we started from, and their average is zero:

<Formula label="Result for the product state of two spins" tex="D_D = 2\hbar^2\left[s(s+1) - m_am_b\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The exchange term">

For $\Psi_S$ and $\Psi_A$ the average of $\vec S_1\cdot\vec S_2$ is the direct term $\hbar^2m_am_b$ plus or minus the exchange term

<Formula label="The exchange term of the dot product" tex="\mathcal{X} = \langle s, m_b|\otimes\langle s, m_a|\ \vec S_1\cdot\vec S_2\ |s, m_a\rangle\otimes|s, m_b\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

$S_{1z}S_{2z}$ gives nothing here, because $m_a \ne m_b$ makes the two product states orthogonal. $S_{1+}S_{2-}$ turns $(m_a, m_b)$ into $(m_a + 1, m_b - 1)$, which is $(m_b, m_a)$ exactly when $m_b = m_a + 1$. Then the two square roots of the ladder formula are equal, because $m_a(m_a + 1) = m_am_b$ and $m_b(m_b - 1) = m_am_b$:

<Formula label="The exchange term for neighbouring values of m" tex="\mathcal{X} = \tfrac12\hbar^2\sqrt{s(s+1) - m_a(m_a+1)}\,\sqrt{s(s+1) - m_b(m_b-1)} = \tfrac12\hbar^2\left[s(s+1) - m_am_b\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

For $m_b = m_a - 1$ the other term, $S_{1-}S_{2+}$, does the same job and gives the same result in terms of $m_am_b$. If $m_a$ and $m_b$ differ by 2 or more, no single step connects the two product states and $\mathcal{X} = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The three results">

The exchange term is real, so the average of $\vec S_1\cdot\vec S_2$ is $\hbar^2m_am_b \pm \mathcal{X}$, with the plus sign for $\Psi_S$, and the mean square difference is $2\hbar^2\left[s(s+1) - m_am_b\right] \mp 2\mathcal{X}$, with the minus sign for $\Psi_S$. For neighbours, $|m_a - m_b| = 1$:

<Formula label="Results for neighbouring values of m" tex="D_S = \hbar^2\left[s(s+1) - m_am_b\right], \qquad D_D = 2\hbar^2\left[s(s+1) - m_am_b\right], \qquad D_A = 3\hbar^2\left[s(s+1) - m_am_b\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

For $|m_a - m_b| \ge 2$ all three are equal to $D_D$. The bracket is positive, since $|m_am_b| \le s^2 < s(s+1)$. So in every case

<Formula label="The order of the three" tex="D_S \le D_D \le D_A" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

with equality when $m_a$ and $m_b$ are not neighbours. The order is the same as in part (a): the symmetric state has the two spins "closer together", the antisymmetric state "farther apart".

</Step>

<Step title="Numbers">

For spin $\frac32$ with $m_a = \frac12$ and $m_b = -\frac12$ the bracket is $\frac{15}{4} + \frac14 = 4$, so $D_S = 4\hbar^2$, $D_D = 8\hbar^2$ and $D_A = 12\hbar^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For spin $\frac12$ the only choice is $m_a = \frac12$, $m_b = -\frac12$. The bracket is $\frac34 + \frac14 = 1$, so $D_S = \hbar^2$, $D_D = 2\hbar^2$ and $D_A = 3\hbar^2$. Now $\Psi_S$ is the triplet state with $m = 0$ and $\Psi_A$ is the singlet, and the total spin $\vec S_{\text{tot}} = \vec S_1 + \vec S_2$ gives an independent check. Since $\left(\vec S_1 - \vec S_2\right)^2 = 2\vec S_1^{\,2} + 2\vec S_2^{\,2} - \vec S_{\text{tot}}^{\,2}$, the triplet has $3\hbar^2 - 2\hbar^2 = \hbar^2$ and the singlet $3\hbar^2 - 0 = 3\hbar^2$. Both agree.

The same identity says what the order means for any $s$: a smaller mean square difference is a larger average of $\vec S_{\text{tot}}^{\,2}$. The symmetric state has the two spins more nearly parallel.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Answer id="p3a-g2" label="Degeneracy of the level $2\hbar\omega$:" value="4" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3a-g2" />

<Answer id="p3a-g3" label="Degeneracy of the level $3\hbar\omega$:" value="8" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3a-g3" />

<Answer id="p3a-up" label="Energy of the state with all spins up, in units of $\hbar\omega$:" value="3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3a-up" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Fermion states are fixed by occupation numbers 0 or 1 of the single-particle states (lesson, Comment 7). Count them level by level.

<Step title="Counting">

The six single-particle states are $\psi_0$, $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$, each with spin up or down. Choosing 3 of the 6 gives $\frac{6\cdot5\cdot4}{3\cdot2\cdot1} = 20$ states in all. Sort them by the numbers $(n_0, n_1, n_2)$ of electrons in the three orbital states. The energy is $E = (n_1 + 2n_2)\hbar\omega$. A doubly occupied orbital state must hold one spin up and one spin down: one way. A singly occupied one holds either spin: two ways.

| $(n_0, n_1, n_2)$ | $E/\hbar\omega$ | Number of states |
|---|---|---|
| (2, 1, 0) | 1 | 2 |
| (2, 0, 1) | 2 | 2 |
| (1, 2, 0) | 2 | 2 |
| (1, 1, 1) | 3 | 8 |
| (1, 0, 2) | 4 | 2 |
| (0, 2, 1) | 4 | 2 |
| (0, 1, 2) | 5 | 2 |

So there are five levels:

<Formula label="Levels and degeneracies for three electrons" tex="E = \hbar\omega,\ 2\hbar\omega,\ 3\hbar\omega,\ 4\hbar\omega,\ 5\hbar\omega \qquad \text{with} \qquad g = 2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 4,\ 2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The lowest energy is $\hbar\omega$, not zero: the Pauli principle forbids three electrons in $\psi_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="All spins up">

The spin state $|+++\rangle$ is symmetric, so the orbital part must be totally antisymmetric. That needs three different orbital states (two equal rows make a determinant vanish), and there are only three: the occupation is $(1, 1, 1)$, there is one such state, and its energy is $3\hbar\omega$. By the lesson's Comment 6 the antisymmetric state is a determinant. Normalized (the lesson's worked example gives the factor):

<Formula label="The state of three electrons with all spins up" tex="\Psi(x_1, x_2, x_3) = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}\begin{vmatrix} \psi_0(x_1) & \psi_0(x_2) & \psi_0(x_3) \\ \psi_1(x_1) & \psi_1(x_2) & \psi_1(x_3) \\ \psi_2(x_1) & \psi_2(x_2) & \psi_2(x_3) \end{vmatrix}\;|+++\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.3.8), p. 162, normalized, for the orbital part" />

Written out, the determinant is $\psi_0(x_1)\psi_1(x_2)\psi_2(x_3) + \psi_1(x_1)\psi_2(x_2)\psi_0(x_3) + \psi_2(x_1)\psi_0(x_2)\psi_1(x_3) - \psi_0(x_1)\psi_2(x_2)\psi_1(x_3) - \psi_1(x_1)\psi_0(x_2)\psi_2(x_3) - \psi_2(x_1)\psi_1(x_2)\psi_0(x_3)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The degeneracies add up: $2 + 4 + 8 + 4 + 2 = 20$. The pattern is symmetric about $3\hbar\omega$, because swapping the roles of $\psi_0$ and $\psi_2$ turns a state of energy $E$ into one of energy $6\hbar\omega - E$. So the average energy of the 20 states is $3\hbar\omega$: $(1\cdot2 + 2\cdot4 + 3\cdot8 + 4\cdot4 + 5\cdot2)/20 = 3$.

The eight states of the middle level are three spins $\frac12$ in three different orbital states: one set of four states with total spin $\frac32$, to which the state above belongs, and two sets of two with total spin $\frac12$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

The three particles are bosons of spin zero. Find the levels and their degeneracies.

<Hint>

For bosons the occupation numbers $(n_0, n_1, n_2)$ can be any whole numbers that add up to 3, and each choice is exactly one state.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3b-total" label="Total number of states:" value="10" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3b-total" />

<Answer id="p3b-levels" label="Number of different energy levels:" value="7" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3b-levels" />

<Answer id="p3b-g3" label="Degeneracy of the level $3\hbar\omega$:" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3b-g3" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One symmetric state for each set of occupation numbers.

<Step title="Counting">

With spin zero the only single-particle states are $\psi_0$, $\psi_1$, $\psi_2$. The symmetric states of three particles are labelled by occupation numbers adding up to 3 (lesson, Comment 7; notes eq. (8.4.8), p. 164):

| $(n_0, n_1, n_2)$ | $E/\hbar\omega$ |
|---|---|
| (3, 0, 0) | 0 |
| (2, 1, 0) | 1 |
| (2, 0, 1) and (1, 2, 0) | 2 |
| (1, 1, 1) and (0, 3, 0) | 3 |
| (1, 0, 2) and (0, 2, 1) | 4 |
| (0, 1, 2) | 5 |
| (0, 0, 3) | 6 |

<Formula label="Levels and degeneracies for three bosons of spin zero" tex="E = 0,\ \hbar\omega,\ 2\hbar\omega,\ 3\hbar\omega,\ 4\hbar\omega,\ 5\hbar\omega,\ 6\hbar\omega \qquad \text{with} \qquad g = 1,\ 1,\ 2,\ 2,\ 2,\ 1,\ 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The total is $1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 10$, the number the lesson's worked example finds for the symmetric states of three particles with three states each. The average energy is again $3\hbar\omega$: $(0 + 1 + 4 + 6 + 8 + 5 + 6)/10 = 3$. Unlike the electrons, the bosons can all sit in $\psi_0$, so the lowest energy is zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

The three particles obey Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics: they have no spin and can be told apart. Find the levels and their degeneracies.

<Hint>

Now every product state, such as $\psi_0(x_1)\,\psi_2(x_2)\,\psi_0(x_3)$, is a state of its own, different from $\psi_2(x_1)\,\psi_0(x_2)\,\psi_0(x_3)$. How many are there?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For each set of occupation numbers, count in how many ways the three particles can be given the three orbital states.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3c-total" label="Total number of states:" value="27" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3c-total" />

<Answer id="p3c-g1" label="Degeneracy of the level $\hbar\omega$:" value="3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3c-g1" />

<Answer id="p3c-g3" label="Degeneracy of the level $3\hbar\omega$:" value="7" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3c-g3" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Without the symmetrization postulate every assignment of an orbital state to each particle counts.

<Step title="Counting">

Each particle has three states, so there are $3^3 = 27$ product states. One set of occupation numbers $(n_0, n_1, n_2)$ now stands for several states, as many as there are ways to hand the orbital states to the particles (the counting of the notes, section 8.6.1, p. 168, with one state per level): 1 way if all three particles are in the same state, 3 ways for two in one state and one in another, 6 ways for three different states.

| $(n_0, n_1, n_2)$ | $E/\hbar\omega$ | Number of states |
|---|---|---|
| (3, 0, 0) | 0 | 1 |
| (2, 1, 0) | 1 | 3 |
| (2, 0, 1) and (1, 2, 0) | 2 | 3 + 3 |
| (1, 1, 1) and (0, 3, 0) | 3 | 6 + 1 |
| (1, 0, 2) and (0, 2, 1) | 4 | 3 + 3 |
| (0, 1, 2) | 5 | 3 |
| (0, 0, 3) | 6 | 1 |

<Formula label="Levels and degeneracies for three distinguishable particles" tex="E = 0,\ \hbar\omega,\ 2\hbar\omega,\ 3\hbar\omega,\ 4\hbar\omega,\ 5\hbar\omega,\ 6\hbar\omega \qquad \text{with} \qquad g = 1,\ 3,\ 6,\ 7,\ 6,\ 3,\ 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The total is $1 + 3 + 6 + 7 + 6 + 3 + 1 = 27$, and the average energy is $3\hbar\omega$ once more: $(0 + 3 + 12 + 21 + 24 + 15 + 6)/27 = 3$. The levels are the same seven as for the bosons. Only the degeneracies differ: the bosons keep one state, the symmetric one, out of each group of product states with the same occupation numbers. Of the 27 states, 10 are symmetric and 1 is antisymmetric (the orbital part of the state of part (a)). The other 16 have mixed symmetry, and no identical particles use them on their own.

</Step>

</Solution>
