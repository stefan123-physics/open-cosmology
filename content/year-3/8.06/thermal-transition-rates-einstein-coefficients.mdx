Last lecture ended with the probability that a single light wave of one frequency drives an atom from one level to the other. Inside a box of thermal radiation, though, the atom is hit by countless waves at once, with every frequency, direction and polarization. This lecture adds up their effects and finds that the probability grows linearly in time, so that there is a **rate**: a result in the style of Fermi's golden rule, now for a transition between two discrete levels. The rate gives Einstein's $B$ coefficient, Einstein's relation from last lecture then gives the spontaneous rate $A$ and with it the lifetimes of excited atoms, and selection rules say which decays can happen at all (L13.1 transcript p. 1; L13.3 transcript pp. 1–3). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in five short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 4, section 4.5.3. This page covers the first three videos; the last two, on charged particles in electromagnetic fields, are on [the second page of this lecture](/courses/8-06/charged-particles-em-fields-gauge-invariance). It follows MIT in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## One wave, recalled

The set-up (L13.1 transcript p. 1). An atom has two levels, $a$ with energy $E_a$ and $b$ with energy $E_b$ above it, and $\omega_{ba} = (E_b - E_a)/\hbar$. A charge $q$ at position $\vec r$ gives the **dipole operator** $\vec d = q\vec r$, an operator because $\vec r$ is. A uniform field $\vec E(t) = 2E_0\cos(\omega t)\,\vec n$, with $E_0$ half the peak amplitude and $\vec n$ the polarization, causes absorption ($a \rightarrow b$) and stimulated emission ($b \rightarrow a$) with exactly the same first-order probability (L13.1 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="The transition probability for one wave" tex="P_{ab}(t) = \frac{4E_0^2\,|(\vec d\cdot\vec n)_{ab}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\sin^2\left((\omega_{ba} - \omega)t/2\right)}{(\omega_{ba} - \omega)^2}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (4.5.13)" />

Here $(\vec d\cdot\vec n)_{ab}$ is the matrix element of $\vec d\cdot\vec n$ between the states $a$ and $b$. In the ionization of hydrogen a golden rule appeared because the final electron belonged to a continuum, and one integrated over it. Here both levels are discrete. What is there to integrate (L13.1 transcript pp. 1–2)?

## Thermal light: many incoherent waves

Black-body radiation is not one wave. Its field is a superposition of many **modes**, each with its own frequency $\omega_i$, its own amplitude $E_0(\omega_i)$ and its own polarization $\vec n(\omega_i)$, and the modes are **incoherent**: their amplitudes are independent of one another, as if random (L13.1 transcript p. 2). For incoherent inputs one adds **probabilities**, not amplitudes. And only the modes with $\omega_i$ near $\omega_{ba}$ matter, because the function of $\omega_{ba} - \omega$ in the formula above becomes tiny away from its peak (L13.1 transcript pp. 2–3). So this time the golden rule comes from integrating over the **stimulus**, the spectrum of the light, rather than over final states.

The $i$-th mode, $2E_0(\omega_i)\cos(\omega_it)\,\vec n(\omega_i)$, contributes the probability (L13.1 transcript p. 3)

<Formula label="The contribution of one mode" tex="P_i(t) = \frac{4E_0^2(\omega_i)\,|(\vec d\cdot\vec n(\omega_i))_{ab}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\sin^2\left((\omega_{ba} - \omega_i)t/2\right)}{(\omega_{ba} - \omega_i)^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

[The transcript calls this the probability "for spontaneous emission" associated with a mode; it is the probability of stimulated emission, or of absorption, caused by the mode. Spontaneous emission needs no mode at all.] The matrix element of $\vec d$ belongs to the atom; the amplitude, frequency and direction belong to the radiation (L13.1 transcript p. 3).

## Amplitude and energy density

Einstein's argument was phrased in terms of $U(\omega)$, the energy per unit volume per unit frequency, because the energy at a frequency counts the photons there, and photons trigger transitions (L13.1 transcript p. 3). So the amplitude $E_0$ must be traded for an energy density. In Gaussian units the energy density of an electric field is $|\vec E|^2/8\pi$, and for our wave (L13.1 transcript pp. 3–4)

<Formula label="Electric energy density of the wave" tex="u_E = \frac{|\vec E(t)|^2}{8\pi} = \frac{4E_0^2}{8\pi}\cos^2\omega t \quad\Rightarrow\quad \langle u_E\rangle = \frac{4E_0^2}{8\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = \frac{E_0^2}{4\pi}" cite="reading4" at="eq. (4.5.14)" />

The oscillation of the energy does not matter, only its time average $\langle\;\rangle$, and the average of $\cos^2$ is $1/2$. In a wave the magnetic energy $u_{M}$ equals the electric one on average, so the total average energy density $\langle u\rangle$ is twice $\langle u_E\rangle$ (L13.1 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Amplitude in terms of energy density" tex="\langle u\rangle = \langle u_E\rangle + \langle u_{M}\rangle = 2\langle u_E\rangle = \frac{E_0^2}{2\pi} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E_0^2 = 2\pi\langle u\rangle" cite="reading4" at="eq. (4.5.15)" />

Zwiebach stresses the many factors of 2: in the energy density, in the time average, in adding the magnetic part. Getting them right is what makes the final formula correct (L13.1 transcript p. 4). Writing $u(\omega_i)$ for the energy density carried by the $i$-th mode, $E_0^2(\omega_i) = 2\pi u(\omega_i)$, and the mode's probability becomes (L13.1 transcript p. 4)

<Formula label="One mode's probability through its energy density" tex="P_i(t) = \frac{4\cdot 2\pi\,u(\omega_i)}{\hbar^2}\,|(\vec d\cdot\vec n(\omega_i))_{ab}|^2\,\frac{\sin^2\left((\omega_{ba} - \omega_i)t/2\right)}{(\omega_{ba} - \omega_i)^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

## From a sum over modes to an integral

The modes are packed densely in frequency. The energy density of all the modes in a range $d\omega$ is $U(\omega)\,d\omega$, so a sum over modes of energy density times any smooth function $f$ of the frequency becomes an integral (L13.2 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Summing incoherent modes" tex="\sum_i E_0^2(\omega_i)\,f(\omega_i) = 2\pi\sum_i u(\omega_i)\,f(\omega_i) \;\rightarrow\; 2\pi\int U(\omega)\,f(\omega)\,d\omega" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

(The notes' eq. (4.5.16) writes the same rule without the function $f$.)

Adding the probabilities of all the modes (L13.2 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="The summed probability" tex="\sum_i P_i(t) = \frac{8\pi}{\hbar^2}\int U(\omega)\,|(\vec d\cdot\vec n)_{ab}|^2\,\frac{\sin^2\left((\omega_{ba} - \omega)t/2\right)}{(\omega_{ba} - \omega)^2}\,d\omega" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

As in Fermi's golden rule, the last factor is sharply peaked at $\omega = \omega_{ba}$ and nothing else varies as fast, so $U(\omega)$ comes out of the integral as $U(\omega_{ba})$ (L13.2 transcript p. 1). The factor $|(\vec d\cdot\vec n)_{ab}|^2$ needs more care. Even at a single frequency, modes arrive from all directions with all polarizations, at random, so it comes out of the integral as an **average over all directions** of $\vec n$, written $\langle\;\rangle$ (L13.2 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Taking the slow factors out" tex="\sum_i P_i(t) = \frac{8\pi}{\hbar^2}\,U(\omega_{ba})\,\langle{|(\vec d\cdot\vec n)_{ab}|^2}\rangle\int\frac{\sin^2\left((\omega_{ba} - \omega)t/2\right)}{(\omega_{ba} - \omega)^2}\,d\omega" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

The remaining integral has been done before: with $x = (\omega_{ba} - \omega)t/2$, a factor $t$ comes out and $\int\sin^2x/x^2\,dx = \pi$ remains (L13.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The frequency integral" tex="\int\frac{\sin^2\left((\omega_{ba} - \omega)t/2\right)}{(\omega_{ba} - \omega)^2}\,d\omega = \frac{t}{2}\int\frac{\sin^2x}{x^2}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{2}\,t" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

[The notes' eq. (4.5.17) drops the factor 4 of (4.5.13), and eq. (4.5.18) evaluates the integral as $2\pi t$ rather than $\pi t/2$. The two slips cancel, and the next result, eq. (4.5.19), agrees with the lecture.]

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-8, 8]} range={[0, 4.4]} xLabel="ω_ba − ω" yLabel="weight" samples={400}
  curves={[
    { expr: "abs(x) < 1e-9 ? T^2/4 : sin(x*T/2)^2/x^2" }

  caption="Computed from sin²((ω_ba − ω)t/2)/(ω_ba − ω)², with ω_ba − ω and t in matching units (for instance 1/ns and ns). As t grows the peak rises as t²/4 and narrows, its first zeros at ±2π/t, so the area under it, πt/2, grows only linearly in t. Only frequencies within a few times 1/t of ω_ba contribute." />

The result is **linear in $t$**, and that matters: the probability grows at a steady pace, so dividing by $t$ gives a constant **transition rate** $w$ (L13.2 transcript p. 2). Combining $8\pi$ with $\pi/2$ gives $4\pi^2$ (L13.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The rate, before averaging" tex="w = \frac{1}{t}\sum_i P_i(t) = \frac{4\pi^2}{\hbar^2}\,\langle{|(\vec d\cdot\vec n)_{ab}|^2}\rangle\,U(\omega_{ba})" cite="reading4" at="eq. (4.5.19)" />

(The notes write the same box without the average brackets, adding the average just after it.)

## Averaging over directions

Only the average is left, and it is simple (L13.2 transcript p. 2). Since $\vec n$ is an ordinary vector of numbers, $(\vec d\cdot\vec n)_{ab} = \vec d_{ab}\cdot\vec n$, where $\vec d_{ab}$ is the vector of the three matrix elements of $x$, $y$ and $z$ (times $q$) between $a$ and $b$. The wavefunctions are complex, so $\vec d_{ab}$ is a **complex vector**, unlike the real field, and the square of a number means the number times its complex conjugate (L13.2 transcript p. 3). Writing each dot product as a sum over components $i$ and $j$:

<Formula label="Expanding the average" tex="\langle{|\vec d_{ab}\cdot\vec n|^2}\rangle = \left\langle\left(\sum_i(\vec d_{ab})_i\,n_i\right)^*\left(\sum_j(\vec d_{ab})_j\,n_j\right)\right\rangle = \sum_{i,j}(\vec d_{ab})_i^*\,(\vec d_{ab})_j\,\langle n_in_j\rangle" cite="reading4" at="eq. (4.5.20)" />

The components of $\vec d_{ab}$ are fixed numbers, so only $\langle n_in_j\rangle$ is averaged. One could parametrize $\vec n$ by its angles and integrate over the sphere, dividing by $4\pi$; a symmetry argument is quicker (L13.2 transcript p. 3). Over the sphere a product like $n_xn_y$ is positive as often as negative and averages to zero. The squares $n_x^2$, $n_y^2$, $n_z^2$ are never negative, and since no axis is special their averages are equal. They add up to the average of $\vec n\cdot\vec n = 1$ (L13.2 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Averages of products of components" tex="\langle n_xn_x\rangle = \langle n_yn_y\rangle = \langle n_zn_z\rangle, \qquad \sum_i\langle n_in_i\rangle = 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \langle n_in_j\rangle = \frac{1}{3}\,\delta_{ij}" cite="reading4" at="eqs. (4.5.21)–(4.5.23)" />

The Kronecker delta $\delta_{ij}$ turns the double sum back into a dot product, of $\vec d_{ab}$ with its complex conjugate (L13.2 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The direction average" tex="\langle{|\vec d_{ab}\cdot\vec n|^2}\rangle = \frac{1}{3}\,\vec d_{ab}^*\cdot\vec d_{ab} \equiv \frac{1}{3}\,|\vec d_{ab}|^2" cite="reading4" at="eq. (4.5.24)" />

Most people write $|\vec d_{ab}|^2$, but it is the vector dotted with its complex conjugate, not with itself (L13.2 transcript p. 4).

## The rate and the B coefficient

The final result, the rate per atom of a transition between $a$ and $b$ (either way) induced by an incoherent bath of radiation (L13.2 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Transition rate in thermal radiation" tex="w = \frac{4\pi^2}{3\hbar^2}\,|\vec d_{ab}|^2\,U(\omega_{ba})" cite="reading4" at="eq. (4.5.25)" />

[The transcript calls it "the rate for spontaneous transition triggered by electromagnetic fields"; it is the rate of induced (stimulated) transitions. Zwiebach also says "spontaneous" and corrects himself to "stimulated" at the start of the lecture, L13.1 transcript p. 1.] A transition between discrete states, combined with an integral over the stimulus, gives a formula in the style of Fermi's golden rule; Griffiths in fact calls it that. He works in SI units, so his version has $\epsilon_0$ and different powers of $\pi$ (L13.2 transcript p. 4). [In SI units, with $u_E = \epsilon_0E^2/2$, the same rate reads $\pi|\vec d_{ab}|^2U(\omega_{ba})/(3\epsilon_0\hbar^2)$.]

Last lecture wrote the absorption rate per atom in level $a$ as $B_{ab}U(\omega_{ba})$, and the stimulated emission rate per atom in level $b$ with the same coefficient. Everything in $w$ except the $U$ is therefore Einstein's $B$ (L13.3 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Einstein's B coefficient" tex="B_{ab} = \frac{4\pi^2}{3\hbar^2}\,|\vec d_{ab}|^2" cite="reading3" at="p. 1; notes eq. (4.5.26)" />

and, since the first-order probabilities are the same both ways, $B_{ba}$ is the same, as Einstein's argument demanded. The $B$ coefficient depends only on the atom, through one matrix element of the dipole operator. Einstein could not compute it, having no quantum theory; with time-dependent perturbation theory we can, from first principles (L13.3 transcript p. 1). [In recalling Einstein's rates, the transcript calls the process with rate $N_bU(\omega_{ba})B_{ab}$ "spontaneous emission"; it is stimulated emission.]

## Spontaneous emission and lifetimes

Einstein's relation from last lecture, $A/B_{ab} = \hbar\omega_{ba}^3/\pi^2c^3$, now gives the rate of **spontaneous** emission from $b$ to $a$, often the rate one really wants (L13.3 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Einstein's A coefficient" tex="A = \frac{\hbar\,\omega_{ba}^3}{\pi^2c^3}\,B_{ab} = \frac{\hbar\,\omega_{ba}^3}{\pi^2c^3}\,\frac{4\pi^2}{3\hbar^2}\,|\vec d_{ab}|^2 = \frac{4}{3}\,\frac{\omega_{ba}^3}{\hbar c^3}\,|\vec d_{ab}|^2" cite="reading3" at="p. 1; notes eqs. (4.5.27), (4.5.28)" />

[The transcript gives the relation as $\hbar\omega_{ba}^3/\pi c^3$ times $B_{ab}$; the denominator is $\pi^2c^3$, as in last lecture and the notes' eq. (4.5.27).] Time-dependent perturbation theory, combined with Einstein's statistical argument, has fixed both rates (L13.3 transcript p. 1). A direct calculation of $A$ would need the quantized electromagnetic field. The factor $\omega_{ba}^3$ is worth noticing: for the same dipole matrix element, spontaneous emission is far faster for ultraviolet lines than for radio lines. [In SI units, $A = \omega_{ba}^3|\vec d_{ab}|^2/(3\pi\epsilon_0\hbar c^3)$.]

To turn this into numbers one needs the idea of a **lifetime** (L13.3 transcript p. 2). $A$ is a probability per unit time for each atom, so in a time $dt$ a population of $N$ atoms in level $b$, with nothing to re-excite them, loses $AN\,dt$ of them, and it decays exponentially with the lifetime $\tau = 1/A$ (L13.3 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Exponential decay" tex="\frac{dN}{dt} = -AN \quad\Rightarrow\quad N(t) = N_0\,e^{-At} = N_0\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{A}" cite="reading3" at="p. 2; notes eq. (4.5.29)" />

A level may decay in several ways, to different lower states. Each **decay channel** removes atoms, so the rates $A_1$, $A_2$, and so on add, and the lifetime is the inverse of the total rate: the inverse of the total lifetime is the sum of the inverses of the partial lifetimes (L13.3 transcript p. 2) [the transcript says the total lifetime itself is that sum]:

<Formula label="Several decay routes" tex="A = A_1 + A_2 + \cdots, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{A}" cite="reading3" at="p. 2; notes eq. (4.5.30)" />

## Selection rules and the decays of hydrogen

With this, the lifetime of an excited state of hydrogen can be calculated, a number that comes out right to within factors of about 2 and can be measured (L13.3 transcript p. 2). The only thing left to compute is the matrix element of the dipole operator, which comes down to matrix elements of $\vec r$ between hydrogen states $|n\ell m\rangle$. When do they vanish? Zwiebach leaves the proof to recitation (it is very readable in Griffiths, he notes) and quotes the result (L13.3 transcript pp. 2–3; notes p. 110): the $z$ component vanishes unless the two values of $m$ agree, and the $x$ and $y$ components vanish unless they differ by one. So a dipole transition needs

<Formula label="Dipole selection rules" tex="\Delta m = m' - m = 0, \pm 1, \qquad \Delta\ell = \ell' - \ell = \pm 1" cite="reading3" at="pp. 2–3; notes eqs. (4.5.33), (4.5.34)" />

(the matrix element can still vanish by accident when these hold). A transition that breaks these rules is **forbidden** at this order. Applied to the lowest levels of hydrogen, 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p and 3d, the rules give a qualitative picture of which states are stable (L13.3 transcript pp. 3–4):

- **1s**, the ground state, has nowhere to go.
- **2s** cannot decay to 1s, since $\ell$ would not change. It is almost stuck: it has a long lifetime and decays only by emitting two photons or by more complicated processes, which 8.06 does not consider.
- **2p** decays to 1s ($\ell$ from 1 to 0).
- **3s** cannot reach 2s or 1s, but it can go to 2p, sideways on an energy diagram ($\ell$ from 0 to 1). [The transcript says "from 1 to 0".]
- **3p** can decay to 2s and all the way down to 1s.
- **3d** can decay only to 2p: reaching 2s or 1s would change $\ell$ by 2.

Computing a couple of these lifetimes is left to the homework (L13.3 transcript p. 4).

<Problem>
A hydrogen atom in the state 2p with $m = 0$ decays to 1s by emitting a photon of energy $\hbar\omega_{ba} = 10.2$ eV (Lyman alpha). The wavefunctions are $\psi_{100} = e^{-r/a_0}/\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}$ and $\psi_{210} = (r/a_0)\,e^{-r/2a_0}\cos\theta/\sqrt{32\pi a_0^3}$. (a) Compute the dipole matrix element. (b) Find $A$ and the lifetime. (c) What fraction of a sample of 2p atoms is still excited after 5 ns? (d) In black-body radiation at 3,000 K, roughly the temperature of decoupling in the early universe, how often is a 2p atom stimulated to emit? Use $\hbar = 6.582 \times 10^{-16}$ eV s, $a_0 = 5.292 \times 10^{-11}$ m, $c = 2.998 \times 10^{8}$ m/s, $\alpha = 1/137.04$ and $k_B = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/K.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The matrix element">
By symmetry only the $z$ component survives: the $x$ and $y$ integrals vanish, as the selection rules say for equal values of $m$. With $z = r\cos\theta$ and $d^3r = r^2\,dr\,d\Omega$, the radial integral is $\int_0^\infty r^4e^{-3r/2a_0}\,dr = 4!\,(2a_0/3)^5$ and the angular one is $\int\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega = 4\pi/3$. Altogether $\langle 100|z|210\rangle = (128\sqrt 2/243)\,a_0 \approx 0.745\,a_0$, so $|\vec d_{ab}|^2 = e^2(0.745\,a_0)^2 \approx 0.555\,e^2a_0^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The decay rate and lifetime">
In Gaussian units $e^2 = \alpha\hbar c$, so $A = \frac{4}{3}\times 0.555\,\alpha\,\omega_{ba}^3a_0^2/c^2$. The frequency is $\omega_{ba} = 10.2/(6.582 \times 10^{-16}) \approx 1.55 \times 10^{16}$ per second. Then $\omega_{ba}^3a_0^2/c^2 \approx 1.16 \times 10^{11}$ per second, and $A \approx 0.740 \times 1.16 \times 10^{11}/137.04 \approx 6.26 \times 10^{8}$ per second. The lifetime is $\tau = 1/A \approx 1.60$ ns. (The states with $m = \pm 1$ give the same answer: a lifetime cannot depend on the orientation of the atom.)
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Decay">
$N/N_0 = e^{-t/\tau} = e^{-5/1.60} \approx 0.044$: after 5 ns only about 4% of the atoms are still in 2p.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Stimulated emission at 3,000 K">
By Einstein's relation and Planck's formula the stimulated rate per atom is $B_{ab}U(\omega_{ba}) = A/(e^{\hbar\omega_{ba}/k_BT} - 1)$. Here $k_BT = 8.617 \times 10^{-5} \times 3000 \approx 0.259$ eV, so $\hbar\omega_{ba}/k_BT \approx 39.5$ and $e^{-39.5} \approx 7.3 \times 10^{-18}$. The rate is $6.26 \times 10^{8} \times 7.3 \times 10^{-18} \approx 4.6 \times 10^{-9}$ per second: once in about $2.2 \times 10^{8}$ s, some 7 years, against a spontaneous decay within nanoseconds. A black body at 3,000 K is very thin at 10.2 eV.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Atoms bathed in black-body radiation">
This lecture's $U(\omega)$ is the black-body spectrum, and in 8.286 (Lecture 16) Guth shows why radiation in equilibrium has this universal form and how its energy density grows as $T^4$. In the early universe, hydrogen was bathed in exactly such radiation: in Lecture 17 Guth dates recombination, when about half the hydrogen became neutral, to about 4,000 K and decoupling to about 3,000 K. The rates on this page are the microscopic side of that story, which Guth's lectures do not compute. Part (d) of the worked example is our own illustration: at 3,000 K the thermal photons almost never stimulate a 2p atom to emit. Going further than 8.286, detailed calculations of recombination must also follow how atoms leave the $n = 2$ level, where the slow, forbidden 2s decay of the selection rules above plays a part.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Thermal radiation is an incoherent superposition of modes with different frequencies, amplitudes and polarizations, so the transition probabilities of the modes add; only modes with $\omega_i$ near $\omega_{ba}$ contribute.
- In Gaussian units a wave $2E_0\cos(\omega t)\,\vec n$ has $\langle u_E\rangle = E_0^2/4\pi$ and, with the equal magnetic part, $\langle u\rangle = E_0^2/2\pi$, so $E_0^2 = 2\pi\langle u\rangle$ and $\sum_iE_0^2(\omega_i)f(\omega_i) \rightarrow 2\pi\int U(\omega)f(\omega)\,d\omega$.
- With $U(\omega_{ba})$ taken out of the peaked integral, which equals $\pi t/2$, the probability grows linearly in $t$, giving the rate $w = (4\pi^2/\hbar^2)\langle{|\vec d_{ab}\cdot\vec n|^2}\rangle U(\omega_{ba})$.
- Averaging over directions, $\langle n_in_j\rangle = \delta_{ij}/3$, so $\langle{|\vec d_{ab}\cdot\vec n|^2}\rangle = |\vec d_{ab}|^2/3$ for the complex vector $\vec d_{ab}$, and $w = 4\pi^2|\vec d_{ab}|^2U(\omega_{ba})/3\hbar^2$ per atom: Einstein's $B_{ab} = B_{ba} = 4\pi^2|\vec d_{ab}|^2/3\hbar^2$.
- Einstein's relation gives $A = 4\omega_{ba}^3|\vec d_{ab}|^2/3\hbar c^3$, and the lifetime is $\tau = 1/A$ (rates of several decay channels add). For hydrogen's 2p state, $\tau \approx 1.6$ ns.
- Dipole transitions need $\Delta m = 0, \pm 1$ and $\Delta\ell = \pm 1$: in hydrogen 2s cannot decay to 1s and lives long, 2p decays to 1s, 3s to 2p, 3p to 2s or 1s, and 3d only to 2p.
