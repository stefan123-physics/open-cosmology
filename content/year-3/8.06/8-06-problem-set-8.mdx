The notation is that of the lessons. The energy is $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, the scattering amplitude is $f(\theta)$, and the phase shift $\delta_l$ of the partial wave $l$ is defined by the radial wave far away, $\sin\left(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta_l\right)$: a positive phase shift pulls the wave in, a negative one pushes it out. A phase shift is fixed only up to a multiple of $\pi$, because nothing that can be measured changes when $\pi$ is added to it. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Which phase?" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

The adiabatic theorem is stated with the instantaneous eigenstates of the Hamiltonian:

<Formula label="Instantaneous eigenstates" tex="H(t)\,|\psi_n(t)\rangle = E_n(t)\,|\psi_n(t)\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, eq. (1), p. 1" />

This equation does not fix $|\psi_n(t)\rangle$, even when no level is degenerate: multiply a solution by a phase that depends on time in any way you like, and it is still a solution. Suppose Alice solves the equation and finds the states $|\psi_n^A(t)\rangle$, and Bob solves it and finds $|\psi_n^B(t)\rangle$. Their states agree at $t = 0$, and later differ by a phase:

<Formula label="Alice's and Bob's eigenstates" tex="|\psi_n^A(0)\rangle = |\psi_n^B(0)\rangle, \qquad |\psi_n^A(t)\rangle = e^{i\alpha_n(t)}\,|\psi_n^B(t)\rangle, \qquad \alpha_n(0) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

At $t = 0$ a system is in the state $|\Psi(0)\rangle = |\psi_n^A(0)\rangle = |\psi_n^B(0)\rangle$. Between $t = 0$ and $t = T$ the Hamiltonian changes adiabatically. Alice and Bob both use the adiabatic theorem to predict the state at time $T$, each with their own eigenstates. Do they predict the same state? The phase counts here: "the same" means equal including the phase.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Write down Alice's prediction $|\Psi^A(T)\rangle$ and Bob's prediction $|\Psi^B(T)\rangle$. Each holds a dynamical phase and a geometric phase $\gamma_n$. Find the difference of the two geometric phases in terms of $\alpha_n(T)$ (type `aT`).

<Hint>

The lesson's statement of the theorem is $|\Psi(t)\rangle \approx e^{i\theta_n(t)}\,e^{i\gamma_n(t)}\,|\psi_n(t)\rangle$. Which of the two phases is built from the eigenstates themselves, and which only from the energies?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put $|\psi_n^A\rangle = e^{i\alpha_n}|\psi_n^B\rangle$ into $\nu_n = i\langle\psi_n|\dot\psi_n\rangle$. The time derivative acts on the phase too.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-gamma" label="$\gamma_n^A(T) - \gamma_n^B(T) =$" expr="-aT"
  vars={{ aT: [0.3, 2] }}
  names={{ aT: "\\alpha_n(T)" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "aT", hint: "Check the sign: the derivative of the phase factor gives iα̇, and ν has one more factor i in front." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "The geometric phase is built from the eigenstates, so it does change when their phases change. Differentiate the phase factor too." },
    { expr: "-2*aT", hint: "The bra gives no extra term: its phase factor cancels against that of the ket. Only the time derivative of the ket's phase is left." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The dynamical phase is the same for both, because it holds only the energy. The geometric phase is built from the eigenstates, so a change of their phases changes it.

<Step title="The two predictions">

The adiabatic theorem gives, for each of them,

<Formula label="The two predictions" tex="|\Psi^A(T)\rangle = e^{i\theta_n(T)}\,e^{i\gamma_n^A(T)}\,|\psi_n^A(T)\rangle, \qquad |\Psi^B(T)\rangle = e^{i\theta_n(T)}\,e^{i\gamma_n^B(T)}\,|\psi_n^B(T)\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eq. (5.1), p. 12, written once for each choice of eigenstates" />

with the two phases of the lesson:

<Formula label="Dynamical and geometric phase" tex="\theta_n(T) = -\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_0^T E_n(t)\,dt, \qquad \gamma_n(T) = \int_0^T \nu_n(t)\,dt, \qquad \nu_n(t) = i\,\langle\psi_n(t)|\dot\psi_n(t)\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (5.2) to (5.4), p. 12" />

The energy $E_n(t)$ is an eigenvalue of $H(t)$ and does not care about the phase of the eigenvector. So $\theta_n(T)$ is the same number for Alice and for Bob.

</Step>

<Step title="The geometric phases">

For Alice's states, $|\dot\psi_n^A\rangle = e^{i\alpha_n}\left(i\dot\alpha_n\,|\psi_n^B\rangle + |\dot\psi_n^B\rangle\right)$. The bra $\langle\psi_n^A| = e^{-i\alpha_n}\langle\psi_n^B|$ removes the phase factor, and the states are normalised:

<Formula label="How the rate of the geometric phase changes" tex="\nu_n^A = i\,\langle\psi_n^A|\dot\psi_n^A\rangle = i\left(i\,\dot\alpha_n + \langle\psi_n^B|\dot\psi_n^B\rangle\right) = \nu_n^B - \dot\alpha_n" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Integrating from $0$ to $T$, with $\alpha_n(0) = 0$:

<Formula label="Difference of the two geometric phases" tex="\gamma_n^A(T) = \gamma_n^B(T) - \alpha_n(T)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson on the geometric phase has the same statement in other words: if the eigenstates are multiplied by $e^{-i\beta}$, the geometric phase changes by $\beta$ at the end of the path minus $\beta$ at its start (notes, chapter 6, eqs. (5.14) and (5.18)). Here $\beta = -\alpha_n$, which gives $-\alpha_n(T) + \alpha_n(0) = -\alpha_n(T)$. As a test with numbers, take the two-component state $\left(1,\ e^{i\omega t}\right)/\sqrt2$ for Bob. It has $\nu_n^B = -\omega/2$, and with any phase $\alpha_n(t)$ in front the rate becomes $-\omega/2 - \dot\alpha_n$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Are the two predicted states the same, or do they differ? Explain.

<Hint>

Put the result of part (a) and the relation between the eigenstates at time $T$ into Alice's prediction.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-same" label="Alice's and Bob's predictions (the choices are ours)" choices={["differ by the phase factor exp(iα(T)), which carries over from the eigenstates", "differ by exp(−iα(T)), because only the geometric phase changes", "are the same state, phase included: the two changes cancel", "are the same only if the path is closed, so that α(T) = 0"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Alice's eigenstate at time $T$ carries the extra factor $e^{i\alpha_n(T)}$, and her geometric phase carries the factor $e^{-i\alpha_n(T)}$. The product is one.

<Step title="Comparing the predictions">

<Formula label="Alice's prediction equals Bob's" tex="|\Psi^A(T)\rangle = e^{i\theta_n(T)}\,e^{i\left(\gamma_n^B(T) - \alpha_n(T)\right)}\,e^{i\alpha_n(T)}\,|\psi_n^B(T)\rangle = e^{i\theta_n(T)}\,e^{i\gamma_n^B(T)}\,|\psi_n^B(T)\rangle = |\Psi^B(T)\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The predictions agree, phase included, for every choice of $\alpha_n(t)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Why it had to be so">

The state of the system obeys the Schrödinger equation, which knows nothing about anyone's choice of eigenvectors. With the same state at $t = 0$ there is one state at time $T$, and the adiabatic theorem approximates that one state. The geometric phase is exactly the piece that makes the answer free of the choice: the factor $e^{i\gamma_n}|\psi_n(T)\rangle$ as a whole does not change when the phases of the instantaneous eigenstates are changed, as long as the starting state is kept. Neither $\gamma_n$ nor $|\psi_n(T)\rangle$ alone has that property.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The agreement does not need a closed path or $\alpha_n(T) = 0$. A closed path matters for a different question: whether $\gamma_n$ by itself has a meaning. It does when $H(T) = H(0)$ and the same eigenvector is used at the start and at the end, which is the Berry phase of the lesson.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Partial waves" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

A certain potential gives the scattering amplitude

<Formula label="The scattering amplitude of this problem" tex="f(\theta) = \frac{1}{k}\left(\frac{\Gamma k}{k_0 - k - ik\Gamma} + 3\,e^{2i\beta k^3}\sin\left(2\beta k^3\right)\cos\theta\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

Here $\Gamma$, $k_0$ and $\beta$ are positive constants that belong to the potential, and $\hbar k$ is the momentum of the particle. ($\Gamma$ is a pure number, $k_0$ a wavenumber, and $\beta$ a volume.)

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Which partial waves take part in the scattering, and what are their phase shifts? Give $\tan\delta_0$ and $\delta_1$. Do the phase shifts behave as they should for $k \to 0$? (The rule of thumb is $\delta_l \sim k^{2l+1}$, which holds for a hard sphere.)

<Hint>

Write the lesson's amplitude with Legendre polynomials: $f(\theta) = \frac1k\sum_l (2l+1)\,e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l\,P_l(\cos\theta)$, with $P_0 = 1$ and $P_1 = \cos\theta$. Compare term by term.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the first term, use $e^{i\delta}\sin\delta = \dfrac{\sin\delta}{\cos\delta - i\sin\delta} = \dfrac{1}{\cot\delta - i}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-waves" label="The waves that scatter are (the choices are ours)" choices={["the s wave only (l = 0)", "the p wave and the d wave (l = 1 and 2)", "every wave up to l = 3, because k³ appears", "the s wave and the p wave (l = 0 and 1)"]} answer={3} />

<Answer id="p2-tan0" label="$\tan\delta_0 =$" expr="G*k/(k0 - k)"
  vars={{ G: [0.2, 1], k: [0.5, 0.9], k0: [2, 3] }}
  names={{ G: "\\Gamma", k0: "k_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(k0 - k)/(G*k)", hint: "That is cot δ₀. Turn it over." },
    { expr: "G/(k0 - k)", hint: "Divide the numerator and the denominator of the first term by Γk and compare with 1/(cot δ − i): a factor k is missing." },
    { expr: "G*k/(k0 - k - G*k)", hint: "The term −ikΓ in the denominator is the −i of 1/(cot δ − i). It does not belong in the real part." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-d1" label="$\delta_1 =$" expr="2*be*k^3"
  vars={{ be: [0.1, 0.5], k: [0.5, 1.2] }}
  names={{ be: "\\beta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "be*k^3", hint: "The amplitude holds exp(iδ) sin δ, not exp(2iδ): the phase shift is the argument of the sine." },
    { expr: "6*be*k^3", hint: "The 3 in front is 2l + 1 for l = 1. It is not part of the phase shift." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the partial-wave sum each $l$ comes with its own Legendre polynomial. A term without $\theta$ is an s wave, a term with $\cos\theta$ a p wave.

<Step title="The amplitude as partial waves">

With $Y_{l0}(\theta) = \sqrt{(2l+1)/4\pi}\,P_l(\cos\theta)$ the amplitude of the lesson reads

<Formula label="The scattering amplitude with Legendre polynomials" tex="f(\theta) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\,e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l\,P_l(\cos\theta)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (7.2.18), p. 139, with Y_l0 from eq. (7.2.8), p. 137" />

The given $f$ has a term without $\theta$ ($P_0 = 1$, $l = 0$) and a term with $\cos\theta$ ($P_1$, $l = 1$), and nothing else. So only the s wave and the p wave scatter: $\delta_l = 0$ for $l \ge 2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The p wave">

The term with $\cos\theta$ must equal $3\,e^{i\delta_1}\sin\delta_1\cos\theta$. It is given as $3\,e^{2i\beta k^3}\sin(2\beta k^3)\cos\theta$, which has this form at once:

<Formula label="The p-wave phase shift" tex="\delta_1 = 2\beta k^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The s wave">

The term without $\theta$ must equal $e^{i\delta_0}\sin\delta_0 = 1/(\cot\delta_0 - i)$. Divide the numerator and the denominator of the given term by $\Gamma k$:

<Formula label="The s-wave term" tex="\frac{\Gamma k}{k_0 - k - ik\Gamma} = \frac{1}{\dfrac{k_0 - k}{\Gamma k} - i} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cot\delta_0 = \frac{k_0 - k}{\Gamma k}, \qquad \tan\delta_0 = \frac{\Gamma k}{k_0 - k}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

That the term can be written this way at all is not a matter of course: a single partial wave must have the form $e^{i\delta}\sin\delta$ with a real $\delta$, and the $-ik\Gamma$ in the denominator is exactly what makes it so.

</Step>

<Step title="Small k">

For $k \to 0$, $\tan\delta_0 \to \Gamma k/k_0$, so $\delta_0 \approx \Gamma k/k_0 \sim k^1$. And $\delta_1 = 2\beta k^3 \sim k^3$ exactly. Both follow the rule $\delta_l \sim k^{2l+1}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The real and imaginary parts of the s-wave term are $\Gamma k(k_0 - k)/D$ and $\Gamma^2k^2/D$, with $D = (k_0 - k)^2 + \Gamma^2k^2$. They must be $\sin\delta_0\cos\delta_0$ and $\sin^2\delta_0$: their ratio, imaginary over real, is $\Gamma k/(k_0 - k) = \tan\delta_0$, as found.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

With $D = (k_0 - k)^2 + \Gamma^2k^2$, part (a) gives $\sin^2\delta_0 = \Gamma^2k^2/D$ and $\sin\delta_0\cos\delta_0 = \Gamma k(k_0 - k)/D$. Writing $\sin\delta_0\cos(\delta_0 - \delta_1) = \sin\delta_0\cos\delta_0\cos\delta_1 + \sin^2\delta_0\sin\delta_1$:

- $\gamma b = 0$: no well, $u = r$, and $a = 0$.
- $\gamma b = \pi/4$: the sine has bent the wave towards the axis. The line outside has the slope $\cos(\pi/4)$ and points back to a crossing at negative $r$: $a/b = 1 - \tan(\pi/4)/(\pi/4) = 1 - 4/\pi = -0.273$.

- $\gamma b = \pi$: the sine is back at zero at the edge, so the line crosses the axis there: $a = b$, and the slope outside is negative. The wave has one zero outside the origin, the mark of the one bound state that the well holds by now.

</Step>

<Step title="The bound state fixes the logarithmic derivative">

<Formula label="The logarithmic derivative at the edge, at low energy" tex="q\cot(qb) = -\kappa - \frac{b}{2}\left(k^2 + \kappa^2\right) + \ldots \approx -\kappa" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(e), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The scattering condition is then $k\cot(kb + \delta_0) \approx -\kappa$. For $kb \ll 1$ the shift by $kb$ can be dropped:

<Formula label="Phase shift near a threshold bound state" tex="k\cot\delta_0 \approx -\kappa \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sin^2\delta_0 = \frac{1}{1 + \cot^2\delta_0} \approx \frac{k^2}{k^2 + \kappa^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(e), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Just below kR = nπ: a quick rise by π">

Write $x = n\pi + \varepsilon$ with $|\varepsilon| \ll 1$. Then $\sin x \approx (-1)^n\varepsilon$ and $\cos x \approx (-1)^n$, and the signs cancel:

<Formula label="The tangent near kR = nπ" tex="\tan\left(x + \delta_0\right) \approx \frac{n\pi\,\varepsilon}{n\pi + \lambda\,\varepsilon} = \frac{n\pi}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon - \varepsilon_r}, \qquad \varepsilon_r = -\frac{n\pi}{\lambda}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Where the phase shift of the shell rises" tex="\tan x = -\frac{x}{\lambda} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad kR \approx n\pi\left(1 - \frac{1}{\lambda}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="What is special about kR = nπ">

<Formula label="Born total cross section of the Yukawa potential" tex="\sigma = \frac{4m^2\beta^2}{\hbar^4}\cdot\frac{\pi}{k^2}\int_0^{4k^2}\frac{d(K^2)}{\left(\mu^2 + K^2\right)^2} = \frac{4\pi m^2\beta^2}{\hbar^4k^2}\left(\frac{1}{\mu^2} - \frac{1}{\mu^2 + 4k^2}\right) = \frac{16\pi m^2\beta^2}{\hbar^4\mu^2\left(\mu^2 + 4k^2\right)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

With $k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2$:

<Formula label="Born total cross section of the Yukawa potential as a function of the energy" tex="\sigma(E) = \frac{16\pi m^2\beta^2}{\hbar^2\mu^2\left(\hbar^2\mu^2 + 8mE\right)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units: $\beta$ is an energy times a length, so $m\beta/\hbar^2$ is one over a length, and $1/\mu^2$ is a length squared: $f$ is a length. At low energy $f = -2m\beta/\hbar^2\mu^2$ does not depend on the angle and $\sigma = 4\pi f^2$. At high energy $\sigma \approx 2\pi m\beta^2/(\hbar^2\mu^2E)$ falls as $1/E$. The sign of $\beta$ does not matter for the cross section: in the first Born approximation attraction and repulsion scatter alike.

</Step>

</Solution>

Those orbits are hyperbolas, on which the impact parameter $b_i$ and the angle are related by $b_i = (Q_1Q_2/2E)\cot(\theta/2)$, and $d\sigma/d\Omega = \left|b_i\,db_i/d\theta\right|/\sin\theta$ gives the same expression. </Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$Q_1Q_2/E$ is a length in Gaussian units: at a head-on approach the particle stops at the distance $Q_1Q_2/E$. <Formula label="Counting rate for a thin target" tex="\frac{d^2N^{\text{out}}}{dt\,d\Omega} = \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\cdot\frac{\dot N_{\text{inc}}}{A}\cdot n\,A\,T = \frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\;n\,T\;\dot N_{\text{inc}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

<Exercise id="p6" title="The Born approximation in one dimension" points={15} mit="Problem 6">

Consider the Schrödinger equation in one dimension for a particle of mass $m$ in a potential $V(x)$, with the energy $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$. As in the lesson, define the rescaled potential $U(x)$ by

<Formula label="The rescaled potential in one dimension" tex="V(x) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,U(x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the Green's function $G(x)$ with which the Schrödinger equation can be written as an integral equation,

<Formula label="The integral equation in one dimension" tex="\psi(x) = \psi_0(x) + \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx'\;G(x - x')\,U(x')\,\psi(x')" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), pp. 4 and 5" />

where $\psi_0(x)$ solves the equation without a potential. Choose the outgoing Green's function, as in three dimensions.

<Hint>

The equation is $\psi'' + k^2\psi = U\psi$. So $G$ must obey $\left(d^2/dx^2 + k^2\right)G = \delta(x)$: away from $x = 0$ it is a free wave, and at $x = 0$ its slope jumps by one.

</Hint>

<Hint>

"Outgoing" means: waves that travel away from the source at $x = 0$, to the right on the right-hand side and to the left on the left-hand side. With the time factor $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, which function of $|x|$ does that?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-g" label="The outgoing Green's function is $G(x) =$ (the choices are ours)" choices={["sin(k|x|)/(2k)", "e^(ikx)/(2ik)", "−e^(ik|x|)/(4π|x|)", "e^(ik|x|)/(2ik)", "−e^(−ik|x|)/(2ik)"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The Green's function is the wave sent out by a point source. In one dimension it is a pair of plane waves that leave the source, joined with a kink.

<Step title="The equation for G">

The Schrödinger equation is $\psi'' + k^2\psi = U\psi$. If $G$ obeys

<Formula label="Definition of the Green's function in one dimension" tex="\left(\frac{d^2}{dx^2} + k^2\right)G(x) = \delta(x)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (7.3.5), p. 145, in one dimension" />

then applying $d^2/dx^2 + k^2$ to the integral equation gives $0 + \int\delta(x - x')U(x')\psi(x')\,dx' = U\psi$, as needed.

</Step>

<Step title="Solving it">

Away from the origin $G$ is a combination of $e^{\pm ikx}$. Outgoing waves are $e^{ikx}$ for $x > 0$ and $e^{-ikx}$ for $x < 0$, that is $G = C\,e^{ik|x|}$, which is continuous. Integrating the equation across $x = 0$ gives $G'(0^+) - G'(0^-) = 1$, so $2ikC = 1$:

<Formula label="The outgoing Green's function in one dimension" tex="G(x) = \frac{e^{ik|x|}}{2ik} = -\frac{i}{2k}\,e^{ik|x|}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Three of the other choices fail in different ways. $\sin(k|x|)/2k$ and $-e^{-ik|x|}/2ik$ do obey the equation for $G$, kink included, but the first is a standing wave and the second a wave that runs towards the source. $e^{ikx}/2ik$ has no kink, so it solves the free equation, without the delta function. And $-e^{ik|x|}/4\pi|x|$ is the Green's function of three dimensions. Compare the two outgoing functions: in three dimensions the wave thins out as $1/r$, in one dimension it has nowhere to spread and keeps its size.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Let the potential vanish outside $-x_0 \le x \le x_0$, for some positive $x_0$, and let $\psi_0$ be a wave that comes in from the left, $\psi_0(x) = A\,e^{ikx}$. Show that in the first Born approximation the reflection coefficient is

<Formula label="Reflection coefficient in the first Born approximation" tex="R \simeq \left(\frac{m}{\hbar^2k}\right)^2\left|\int_{-x_0}^{x_0}dx'\;e^{2ikx'}\,V(x')\right|^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 5" />

</Hint>

**The idea.** To first order the potential is hit by the free wave only. Far to the left the result is a wave $e^{-ikx}$, and its size relative to the incoming wave is the reflection amplitude.

<Step title="The first Born approximation">

<Formula label="The wave in the first Born approximation, one dimension" tex="\psi(x) \simeq A\,e^{ikx} + \frac{A}{2ik}\int_{-x_0}^{x_0}dx'\;e^{ik|x - x'|}\,U(x')\,e^{ikx'}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (7.3.29), p. 148, in one dimension with the Green's function of part (a)" />

</Step>

<Step title="To the left of the potential">

For $x < -x_0$, $e^{ik|x - x'|} = e^{ik(x' - x)}$, and the factor $e^{-ikx}$ comes out of the integral:

<Formula label="The reflected wave in the first Born approximation" tex="\psi(x) \simeq A\,e^{ikx} + r\,A\,e^{-ikx}, \qquad r = \frac{1}{2ik}\int_{-x_0}^{x_0}dx'\;e^{2ikx'}\,U(x') = \frac{m}{i\hbar^2k}\int_{-x_0}^{x_0}dx'\;e^{2ikx'}\,V(x')" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

The second term is a wave that travels to the left: the reflected wave. The reflection coefficient is the ratio of the reflected to the incoming probability current, and both waves have the same speed, so $R = |r|^2$, which is the formula to be shown.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The integral is the Fourier transform of the potential at the wavenumber $2k$. That is the one-dimensional form of the lesson's result: the wave-number transfer for scattering straight back is $K = 2k\sin(\pi/2) = 2k$. To the right of the potential the same steps give $e^{ik|x - x'|}e^{ikx'} = e^{ikx}$: a transmitted wave $A\,e^{ikx}\left(1 + \frac{1}{2ik}\int U\,dx'\right)$. The units: $mV/\hbar^2k$ is one over a length, and the integral over $x'$ brings a length.

</Step>

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a delta function the Born integral is trivial, and the exact problem is two matching conditions. The comparison shows what "high energy" means for the Born approximation.

<Step title="The Born value">

The integral is $\int e^{2ikx'}(-\alpha)\,\delta(x')\,dx' = -\alpha$:

<Formula label="Born reflection coefficient of the delta-function potential" tex="R_{\text{B}} = \left(\frac{m\alpha}{\hbar^2k}\right)^2 = \frac{m\alpha^2}{2\hbar^2E}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="The exact value (our derivation)">

With $\psi = e^{ikx} + r\,e^{-ikx}$ for $x < 0$ and $\psi = t\,e^{ikx}$ for $x > 0$: continuity gives $1 + r = t$, and the jump of the slope gives $ikt - ik(1 - r) = -(2m\alpha/\hbar^2)\,t$. With $\kappa_0 \equiv m\alpha/\hbar^2$ these give $2ikr = -2\kappa_0(1 + r)$, so

<Formula label="Exact reflection by the delta-function potential" tex="r = -\frac{\kappa_0}{\kappa_0 + ik}, \qquad R = |r|^2 = \frac{\kappa_0^2}{\kappa_0^2 + k^2} = \frac{m\alpha^2}{m\alpha^2 + 2\hbar^2E} = \frac{1}{1 + 2\hbar^2E/m\alpha^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 5 (our own solution, in place of the textbook formula the sheet points to)" />

The energy scale $m\alpha^2/2\hbar^2$ is the binding energy of the one bound state of the attractive delta function (8.04): the Born approximation works when the particle's energy is far above it. The sign of $\alpha$ drops out of both results, so a repulsive delta function reflects exactly as much. The amplitudes show how the Born series is built: the Born amplitude of part (b) is $r_{\text{B}} = -\kappa_0/ik$, and the exact one is $r = r_{\text{B}}/(1 + \kappa_0/ik) = r_{\text{B}}\left(1 - \kappa_0/ik + \ldots\right)$, a geometric series in the small number $\kappa_0/k$, whose first term is the first Born approximation. For $E \to \infty$ both coefficients go to zero, and for $E \to 0$ the exact one goes to 1.
