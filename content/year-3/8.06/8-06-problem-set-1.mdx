## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `hbar*omega*(n + 1/2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Where a problem asks for a series up to a given term, type exactly those terms: the box compares values, so the exact expression is not accepted in place of its expansion. Write the symbols as the box names them: `hbar` stands for $\hbar$, `omega` for $\omega$, `lam` for $\lambda$, `alpha` and `beta` for $\alpha$ and $\beta$, `eps` for the field $\varepsilon$, `a0`, `a1`, `a2`, `a3` for $a_0$ to $a_3$, and `n1`, `n2`, `n3` for $n_1$ to $n_3$.

Give a number answer to 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. Where a box needs more digits, its label says so.

MIT gives the points per problem. The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons: $H^{(0)}$ or $H_0$ is the solved Hamiltonian, $\delta H$ the perturbation, $E_n^{(1)}$ and $E_n^{(2)}$ the first and second corrections to the energy, and $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ the first correction to the state. Problem 5 uses Gaussian units, as MIT's sheet does: the potential energy of two charges $e$ a distance $r$ apart is $e^2/r$. Where a solution says only "notes", it means Zwiebach's lecture notes, chapter 1.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The joy of two by two Hermitian matrices" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

The three Pauli matrices $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$ obey

<Formula label="Product of two Pauli matrices" tex="\sigma_i\,\sigma_j = \delta_{ij}\,I + \sum_{k=1}^{3} i\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,\sigma_k" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

where $I$ is the two by two identity matrix. For a vector $\vec a = (a_1, a_2, a_3)$ of three real numbers, $\vec a\cdot\vec\sigma$ means $a_1\sigma_1 + a_2\sigma_2 + a_3\sigma_3$. Every Hermitian two by two matrix can be written as $a_0 I + \vec a\cdot\vec\sigma$ with real $a_0$ and $\vec a$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Work out $\left(\vec a\cdot\vec\sigma\right)^2$ with the product formula, and write down its eigenvalues. Each Pauli matrix has trace zero, so $\text{tr}\left(\vec a\cdot\vec\sigma\right) = 0$. What does that tell you about the eigenvalues of $\vec a\cdot\vec\sigma$ itself? And what are the eigenvalues of $a_0 I + \vec a\cdot\vec\sigma$?

In the box, type the larger of the two eigenvalues of $a_0 I + \vec a\cdot\vec\sigma$.

<Hint>

Write $\left(\vec a\cdot\vec\sigma\right)^2 = \sum_{i,j} a_i a_j\,\sigma_i\sigma_j$. The product $a_i a_j$ does not change when $i$ and $j$ are swapped. What does $\epsilon_{ijk}$ do?

</Hint>

<Hint>

If a matrix $M$ has eigenvalue $\mu$, then $M^2$ has eigenvalue $\mu^2$. The trace of a matrix is the sum of its eigenvalues.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-top" label="Larger eigenvalue $=$" expr="a0 + sqrt(a1^2 + a2^2 + a3^2)"
  vars={{ a0: [-2, 2], a1: [0.5, 2], a2: [0.5, 2], a3: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ a0: "a_0", a1: "a_1", a2: "a_2", a3: "a_3" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(a1^2 + a2^2 + a3^2)", hint: "That is the larger eigenvalue of a·σ alone. Adding a₀I shifts every eigenvalue by a₀." },
    { expr: "a0 + a1 + a2 + a3", hint: "The square of a·σ is the squared length of the vector a times the identity, so the eigenvalues hold the length of a, not the sum of its components." },
    { expr: "a0 + a1^2 + a2^2 + a3^2", hint: "The squared length of a is the eigenvalue of (a·σ)². The eigenvalue of a·σ is its square root." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** The square of $\vec a\cdot\vec\sigma$ is a multiple of the identity. That fixes each eigenvalue up to a sign, and the trace fixes the signs.

<Step title="The square">

Insert the product formula:

<Formula label="Square of a combination of Pauli matrices" tex="\left(\vec a\cdot\vec\sigma\right)^2 = \sum_{i,j} a_i a_j\left(\delta_{ij}\,I + \sum_k i\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,\sigma_k\right) = \left(a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2\right)I = |\vec a|^2\,I" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The second term vanishes because $a_i a_j$ is symmetric in $i$ and $j$ while $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is antisymmetric: every term in the sum is cancelled by the term with $i$ and $j$ swapped. A multiple of the identity has one eigenvalue, twice: here $|\vec a|^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The eigenvalues">

If $\mu$ is an eigenvalue of $\vec a\cdot\vec\sigma$, then $\mu^2$ is an eigenvalue of its square, so $\mu^2 = |\vec a|^2$ and $\mu = +|\vec a|$ or $-|\vec a|$. The two eigenvalues add up to the trace, which is zero. So there is one of each:

<Formula label="Eigenvalues of a two by two Hermitian matrix" tex="\vec a\cdot\vec\sigma:\quad \pm|\vec a|, \qquad\qquad a_0 I + \vec a\cdot\vec\sigma:\quad a_0 \pm |\vec a|" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Adding $a_0 I$ changes no eigenvector and adds $a_0$ to each eigenvalue.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Written out, the matrix $a_0 I + \vec a\cdot\vec\sigma$ has the entries $a_0 + a_3$ and $a_0 - a_3$ on the diagonal and $a_1 - ia_2$ and $a_1 + ia_2$ off it. Its characteristic equation is $(a_0 - \mu)^2 - a_3^2 - \left(a_1^2 + a_2^2\right) = 0$, with the roots $\mu = a_0 \pm |\vec a|$: the same result. For $\vec a = (0, 0, 1)$ and $a_0 = 0$ the matrix is $\sigma_3$, with eigenvalues $\pm 1$, as it should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Let $\vec a = (\alpha, 0, \beta)$. Write down the exact eigenvalues of $\vec a\cdot\vec\sigma$. Then expand them, keeping the leading term and the first two corrections, for $|\alpha| \ll |\beta|$ and for $|\alpha| \gg |\beta|$. Compare with what second-order perturbation theory gives.

For the boxes take $\alpha$ and $\beta$ positive. First box: the upper eigenvalue for $\alpha \ll \beta$, up to and including the second correction. Second box: the second-order energy shift $E^{(2)}$ of the upper level in the other case, $\beta \ll \alpha$, from perturbation theory. The third box is our addition.

<Hint>

Part (a) gives the exact eigenvalues at once. To expand, pull the larger of the two numbers out of the square root and use the series of $\sqrt{1 + z}$ for small $z$, which the lesson on the perturbation series quotes (there with $z^2$ in place of $z$).

</Hint>

<Hint>

For perturbation theory with $|\alpha| \ll |\beta|$, split the matrix as $H^{(0)} = \beta\sigma_3$ and $\delta H = \alpha\sigma_1$. What are the diagonal and the off-diagonal elements of $\sigma_1$ between the eigenstates of $\sigma_3$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $|\alpha| \gg |\beta|$ the roles swap: $H^{(0)} = \alpha\sigma_1$ and $\delta H = \beta\sigma_3$. Between the two eigenstates of $\sigma_1$, the matrix $\sigma_3$ has no diagonal elements and off-diagonal elements 1.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-pt" label="Upper level, $\beta \ll \alpha$: $E^{(2)} =$" expr="beta^2/(2*alpha)"

<Answer id="p1-order" label="The second correction, $-\alpha^4/(8\beta^3)$, first appears in perturbation theory at order" choices={["two", "three", "four", "none: perturbation theory never finds it"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The exact eigenvalues are a square root. Its series in the small ratio is the perturbation series, term by term.

<Step title="Exact eigenvalues and their expansions">

From part (a), with $|\vec a|^2 = \alpha^2 + \beta^2$:

<Formula label="Exact eigenvalues" tex="\mu_\pm = \pm\sqrt{\alpha^2 + \beta^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

With $\sqrt{1 + z} = 1 + \frac{z}{2} - \frac{z^2}{8} + \dots$ for small $z$:

<Formula label="The two expansions" tex="\mu_\pm \approx \pm|\beta|\left(1 + \frac{\alpha^2}{2\beta^2} - \frac{\alpha^4}{8\beta^4}\right)\ \ \text{for } |\alpha| \ll |\beta|, \qquad \mu_\pm \approx \pm|\alpha|\left(1 + \frac{\beta^2}{2\alpha^2} - \frac{\beta^4}{8\alpha^4}\right)\ \ \text{for } |\alpha| \gg |\beta|" cite="reading1" at="notes eq. (1.1.46), the series of the square root, with z² replaced by the small ratio squared" />

</Step>

<Step title="Perturbation theory for small α">

Take $H^{(0)} = \beta\sigma_3$, with eigenstates "up" and "down" along the third axis and energies $+\beta$ and $-\beta$, and $\delta H = \alpha\sigma_1$. The matrix $\sigma_1$ has zeros on its diagonal, so the first-order shifts vanish. Its off-diagonal elements are 1, so $|\delta H_{kn}|^2 = \alpha^2$, and the second-order formula gives for the level $+\beta$

<Formula label="Second-order shift for small α" tex="E_+^{(2)} = -\frac{|\delta H_{-+}|^2}{E_-^{(0)} - E_+^{(0)}} = -\frac{\alpha^2}{(-\beta) - \beta} = \frac{\alpha^2}{2\beta}, \qquad E_-^{(2)} = -\frac{\alpha^2}{2\beta}" cite="reading1" at="notes eq. (1.1.36), for two states" />

This is the first correction of the exact series. (For negative $\beta$ the upper level is $-\beta = |\beta|$ and the same steps give $\alpha^2/(2|\beta|)$.)

</Step>

<Step title="Perturbation theory for small β">

Now $H^{(0)} = \alpha\sigma_1$ and $\delta H = \beta\sigma_3$. The eigenstates of $\sigma_1$ are the sum and the difference of "up" and "down", divided by $\sqrt2$, with energies $+\alpha$ and $-\alpha$. Between them $\sigma_3$ has diagonal elements $\frac12(1 - 1) = 0$ and off-diagonal elements $\frac12(1 + 1) = 1$. So again the first order vanishes and

<Formula label="Second-order shift for small β" tex="E_\pm^{(2)} = \pm\frac{\beta^2}{2\alpha}" cite="reading1" at="notes eq. (1.1.36), for two states" />

the first correction of the second series, for positive $\alpha$.

</Step>

<Step title="What second order cannot see">

Second-order perturbation theory gives the leading term and the first correction, and nothing more. The second correction, $-\alpha^4/(8\beta^3)$, holds four powers of the small number $\alpha$: it is the fourth-order term. The third-order term is zero, as every odd order is here, because the exact eigenvalues do not change when $\alpha$ changes sign.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $\alpha = 0.1$ and $\beta = 1$. The exact upper eigenvalue is $\sqrt{1.01} = 1.0049876$. The three terms give $1 + 0.005 - 0.0000125 = 1.0049875$. Two terms give 1.005: the error, about $1.2\times 10^{-5}$, is the size of the fourth-order term. The series converges only while the small ratio stays below 1, in line with the lesson's condition $|\lambda V| < \frac12\left|E_1^{(0)} - E_2^{(0)}\right|$, which here reads $|\alpha| < |\beta|$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Define an inner product of two matrices by $\langle A, B\rangle \equiv \text{tr}\left[A^\dagger B\right]$. Let

<Formula label="The matrix to take apart" tex="A = \frac{a_0 I + \vec a\cdot\vec\sigma}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

Find four Hermitian matrices $Q_0, Q_1, Q_2, Q_3$ with $a_i = \langle A, Q_i\rangle$ for $i = 0, 1, 2, 3$.

<Hint>

Take the trace of the product formula of part (a). What are $\text{tr}\,I$, $\text{tr}\,\sigma_k$ and so $\text{tr}\left[\sigma_i\sigma_j\right]$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-q" label="The matrices are" choices={["Q₀ = I/2 and Qᵢ = σᵢ/2", "Q₀ = I and Qᵢ = σᵢ", "Q₀ = 2I and Qᵢ = 2σᵢ", "Q₀ = I and Qᵢ = iσᵢ"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Under this inner product the identity and the three Pauli matrices are orthogonal to each other. An inner product with one of them picks out one coefficient.

<Step title="Traces">

The identity has trace 2 and each Pauli matrix has trace 0. The trace of the product formula is then

<Formula label="Traces of products of Pauli matrices" tex="\text{tr}\left[\sigma_i\sigma_j\right] = 2\,\delta_{ij}, \qquad \text{tr}\left[I\,\sigma_j\right] = 0, \qquad \text{tr}\left[I\,I\right] = 2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The coefficients">

For real $a_0$ and $\vec a$ the matrix $A$ is Hermitian, $A^\dagger = A$. Then

<Formula label="Picking out the coefficients" tex="\langle A, I\rangle = \tfrac12\,\text{tr}\left[a_0 I + \vec a\cdot\vec\sigma\right] = a_0, \qquad \langle A, \sigma_j\rangle = \tfrac12\,\text{tr}\left[a_0\sigma_j + \sum_i a_i\,\sigma_i\sigma_j\right] = a_j" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

So $Q_0 = I$ and $Q_i = \sigma_i$: all four Hermitian. The factor $\frac12$ in $A$ makes up for the "length squared" 2 of each of the four matrices.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $j = 3$. On the diagonal of $A$ stand $\frac12(a_0 + a_3)$ and $\frac12(a_0 - a_3)$, and $\sigma_3$ has $+1$ and $-1$ on its diagonal, so $\text{tr}\left[A\sigma_3\right] = \frac12(a_0 + a_3) - \frac12(a_0 - a_3) = a_3$. If the coefficients were complex, $A^\dagger$ would hold their complex conjugates, and the formula would read $a_i = \langle Q_i, A\rangle$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Anharmonic oscillator" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

An oscillator has a cubic term added to its potential:

<Formula label="Oscillator with a cubic term" tex="H = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} + \frac12 m\omega^2\hat x^2 + \lambda\,\sqrt2\,\hbar\omega\,\frac{\hat x^3}{d^3}, \qquad d^2 = \frac{\hbar}{m\omega}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

Here $\lambda$ is a small pure number and $d$ is the oscillator's length, as in the lesson. Treat the cubic term as the perturbation. The states $|n\rangle$ are the energy eigenstates of the oscillator without it.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Show that the ground state energy does not change at first order in $\lambda$. Then find the change at order $\lambda^2$. In the box, type the ground state energy up to and including order $\lambda^2$.

<Hint>

The lesson writes $\hat x/d = \left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)/\sqrt2$. Write the cubic term with $\left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)^3$ and collect the numbers in front.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Apply $\hat a + \hat a^\dagger$ to $|0\rangle$ three times, one factor at a time, with $\hat a|n\rangle = \sqrt n\,|n-1\rangle$ and $\hat a^\dagger|n\rangle = \sqrt{n+1}\,|n+1\rangle$. Which states appear?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the second-order sum each state that appeared counts with the square of its coefficient, divided by its distance in energy from the ground state.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-e" label="$E_0 \approx$" expr="hbar*omega*(1/2 - 11*lam^2/4)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], omega: [0.5, 2], lam: [0.2, 1] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", omega: "\\omega", lam: "\\lambda" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "hbar*omega*(1/2 - 9*lam^2/4)", hint: "That counts only the state |1⟩. The cubic term also connects the ground state to |3⟩." },
    { expr: "hbar*omega*(1/2 - 15*lam^2/4)", hint: "The energy denominators are missing: |1⟩ lies ħω above the ground state and |3⟩ lies 3ħω above it." },
    { expr: "hbar*omega*(1/2 + 11*lam^2/4)", hint: "The second-order shift of a ground state is never positive: every denominator has the same sign." },
    { expr: "-11*hbar*omega*lam^2/4", hint: "That is the shift alone. The box asks for the energy: add the unperturbed ground state energy." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The cubic term is odd in $x$, so its expectation value in the even ground state vanishes. At second order it reaches the states $|1\rangle$ and $|3\rangle$.

<Step title="The perturbation with ladder operators">

With $\hat x/d = \left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)/\sqrt2$ (notes eq. (1.1.50)), $\hat x^3/d^3 = \left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)^3/(2\sqrt2)$ and

<Formula label="The cubic perturbation" tex="\lambda\,\delta H = \lambda\,\sqrt2\,\hbar\omega\,\frac{\hat x^3}{d^3} = \lambda\,\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Acting on the ground state">

One factor at a time: $\left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)|0\rangle = |1\rangle$, then $\left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)|1\rangle = |0\rangle + \sqrt2\,|2\rangle$, then

<Formula label="The cubic acting on the ground state" tex="\left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)^3|0\rangle = \left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)\left(|0\rangle + \sqrt2\,|2\rangle\right) = 3\,|1\rangle + \sqrt6\,|3\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So the only non-zero matrix elements from the ground state are $\delta H_{10} = \frac32\hbar\omega$ and $\delta H_{30} = \frac{\sqrt6}{2}\hbar\omega$.

</Step>

<Step title="First order">

$E_0^{(1)} = \langle 0|\delta H|0\rangle = 0$: the state $\delta H|0\rangle$ has no part along $|0\rangle$. In words: $x^3$ is odd and $|\psi_0(x)|^2$ is even.

</Step>

<Step title="Second order">

The states $|1\rangle$ and $|3\rangle$ lie $\hbar\omega$ and $3\hbar\omega$ above the ground state:

<Formula label="Second-order shift of the ground state" tex="E_0^{(2)} = -\frac{|\delta H_{10}|^2}{\hbar\omega} - \frac{|\delta H_{30}|^2}{3\hbar\omega} = -\frac94\hbar\omega - \frac12\hbar\omega = -\frac{11}{4}\hbar\omega" cite="reading1" at="notes eq. (1.1.36), with the two matrix elements above" />

<Formula label="Ground state energy to second order" tex="E_0(\lambda) = \hbar\omega\left(\frac12 - \frac{11}{4}\lambda^2\right) + O(\lambda^4)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A sum rule tests the matrix elements. The squares of all matrix elements from $|0\rangle$ must add up to $\langle 0|\delta H^2|0\rangle = 2(\hbar\omega)^2\langle x^6\rangle/d^6$. For the Gaussian ground state $\langle x^6\rangle = \frac{15}{8}d^6$, which gives $\frac{15}{4}(\hbar\omega)^2$. And indeed $\frac94 + \frac64 = \frac{15}{4}$: no state other than $|1\rangle$ and $|3\rangle$ is reached. The shift is negative, as it must be for a ground state, and the next term is of order $\lambda^4$ because the energy cannot depend on the sign of $\lambda$ (changing the sign of $\lambda$ is the same as mirroring $x$).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Step title="The shape of the potential">

<Formula label="Top of the barrier" tex="x_b = -\frac{d}{3\sqrt2\,\lambda}, \qquad V(x_b) = \hbar\omega\left(\frac{1}{36\lambda^2} - \frac{1}{54\lambda^2}\right) = \frac{\hbar\omega}{108\,\lambda^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2 (our own solution and our own addition)" />

Beyond the barrier the potential comes back to zero at $x = -d/(2\sqrt2\,\lambda)$ and then falls to $-\infty$. For small $\lambda$ the barrier is far away and high: for $\lambda = 0.1$ (our example) its top is at $x_b = -2.36\,d$ and its height is $0.926\,\hbar\omega$, to be compared with the ground state energy of about $\frac12\hbar\omega$.

<Step title="Is the state of part (b) the true ground state?">

Strictly, there is no ground state. The potential has no lowest value, so the energies are not bounded from below, and no normalizable state stays in the well for ever. A particle placed in the well will leak out through the barrier by tunnelling and run off to large negative $x$.

For small $\lambda$ the leak is very slow. The state of part (b) then describes well what the particle does for a long time: it sits in the well, a little lower in energy and leaning to the left, in a state that is almost stationary. So the perturbative state is like a long-lived "almost bound" state, not like a true ground state.

</Step>

<Step title="What perturbation theory missed">

It missed the tunnelling. The reason is the way the tunnelling rate depends on $\lambda$. The barrier has a width of order $d/\lambda$ and a height of order $\hbar\omega/\lambda^2$, so the rate falls like $e^{-c/\lambda^2}$ with a positive number $c$. A simple estimate (our addition, with the method of the later lesson on [the WKB approximation](/courses/8-06/wkb-approximation-connection-formula), for a particle of zero energy) gives $c = \frac{1}{15}$. The function $e^{-c/\lambda^2}$ and all its derivatives vanish at $\lambda = 0$: its power series in $\lambda$ is zero, term by term. A series in powers of $\lambda$ cannot show such an effect at any order.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $x_b$ is a length and $V(x_b)$ an energy. The barrier moves away and grows as $\lambda \to 0$, so the pure oscillator comes back, as it should. For negative $\lambda$ the picture is mirrored: the barrier is on the positive side.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Perturbation of the three-dimensional harmonic oscillator" points={25} mit="Problem 3">

The levels of the three-dimensional harmonic oscillator are highly degenerate. This problem shows how a perturbation removes part of that degeneracy. It can be worked through with little more than matrices: it is a first meeting with the ideas of the lessons on degenerate levels.

The Hamiltonian is $H = H_0 + \delta H$ with

<Formula label="Three-dimensional oscillator with a rotation term" tex="H_0 = \frac{1}{2m}\,\vec p^{\,2} + \frac12 m\omega^2\,\vec x^{\,2}, \qquad \delta H = \lambda\,\omega\,L_2, \qquad L_2 = x_3\,p_1 - x_1\,p_3" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2, eqs. (1) to (3)" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Three independent oscillators: the energies add.

<Step title="Operators">

For each direction $j = 1, 2, 3$ define, as for one oscillator,

<Formula label="Ladder operators for each direction" tex="\hat a_j = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\frac{x_j}{d} + \frac{i\,d\,p_j}{\hbar}\right), \qquad \hat a_j^\dagger = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\frac{x_j}{d} - \frac{i\,d\,p_j}{\hbar}\right), \qquad N_j = \hat a_j^\dagger\hat a_j, \qquad d^2 = \frac{\hbar}{m\omega}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

with $\left[\hat a_j, \hat a_k^\dagger\right] = \delta_{jk}$. Then $H_0 = \hbar\omega\left(N_1 + N_2 + N_3 + \frac32\right)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Energies and the degenerate level">

<Formula label="Energies of the three-dimensional oscillator" tex="H_0\,|n_1, n_2, n_3\rangle = \hbar\omega\left(n_1 + n_2 + n_3 + \frac32\right)|n_1, n_2, n_3\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The energy $\frac52\hbar\omega$ needs $n_1 + n_2 + n_3 = 1$: one quantum, in one of three directions. The level holds the three states $|1,0,0\rangle$, $|0,1,0\rangle$ and $|0,0,1\rangle$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The 8.05 lesson counts $(N+1)(N+2)/2$ states at the level with $N$ quanta. For $N = 1$ that is 3. The ground state, $N = 0$, is a single state with energy $\frac32\hbar\omega$.

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Write the perturbation $\delta H$ with creation and annihilation operators.

<Hint>

**The idea.** $L_2$ is a product of positions and momenta of two different directions. In ladder operators it moves a quantum from one of these directions to the other.

<Step title="Positions and momenta">

<Formula label="Position and momentum with ladder operators" tex="x_j = \frac{d}{\sqrt2}\left(\hat a_j + \hat a_j^\dagger\right), \qquad p_j = \frac{i\hbar}{\sqrt2\,d}\left(\hat a_j^\dagger - \hat a_j\right)" cite="reading1" at="notes eq. (1.1.50), with the matching formula for the momentum" />

<Step title="The angular momentum">

<Formula label="Angular momentum along the second axis" tex="L_2 = x_3p_1 - x_1p_3 = \frac{i\hbar}{2}\left[\left(\hat a_3 + \hat a_3^\dagger\right)\left(\hat a_1^\dagger - \hat a_1\right) - \left(\hat a_1 + \hat a_1^\dagger\right)\left(\hat a_3^\dagger - \hat a_3\right)\right] = i\hbar\left(\hat a_1^\dagger\hat a_3 - \hat a_3^\dagger\hat a_1\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Multiplying out gives eight terms. The terms $\hat a_3\hat a_1$ and $\hat a_3^\dagger\hat a_1^\dagger$ appear once in each product with the same sign and cancel in the difference. The terms $\hat a_1^\dagger\hat a_3$ and $\hat a_3^\dagger\hat a_1$ appear twice each.

<Formula label="The perturbation with ladder operators" tex="\delta H = \lambda\,\omega\,L_2 = i\,\lambda\,\hbar\omega\left(\hat a_1^\dagger\hat a_3 - \hat a_3^\dagger\hat a_1\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is Hermitian: the adjoint of $i\,\hat a_1^\dagger\hat a_3$ is $-i\,\hat a_3^\dagger\hat a_1$, which is the second term. Every term removes one quantum and adds one, so $\delta H$ never changes the total number of quanta. Part (f) rests on this.

</Step>

<Part label="f" points={5}>

<Hint>

**The idea.** $\delta H$ moves quanta between directions but never changes how many there are, and $H_0$ depends only on that number. So the two operators commute, and an operator that commutes with $H_0$ cannot connect states of different energy.

<Step title="The commutator">

With $N = N_1 + N_2 + N_3$ we have $H_0 = \hbar\omega\left(N + \frac32\right)$. For the first term of $\delta H$:

<Formula label="The perturbation keeps the number of quanta" tex="\left[N, \hat a_1^\dagger\hat a_3\right] = \left[N_1, \hat a_1^\dagger\right]\hat a_3 + \hat a_1^\dagger\left[N_3, \hat a_3\right] = \hat a_1^\dagger\hat a_3 - \hat a_1^\dagger\hat a_3 = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(f), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

and the same holds for $\hat a_3^\dagger\hat a_1$. So $\left[H_0, \delta H\right] = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The matrix element">

Let $H_0|\psi\rangle = E_\psi|\psi\rangle$ and $H_0|\phi\rangle = E_\phi|\phi\rangle$. Then

<Formula label="Matrix elements between different levels vanish" tex="0 = \langle\phi|\left[H_0, \delta H\right]|\psi\rangle = \left(E_\phi - E_\psi\right)\langle\phi|\,\delta H\,|\psi\rangle \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle\phi|\,\delta H\,|\psi\rangle = 0 \ \text{ when } E_\phi \neq E_\psi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(f), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

This is the argument of the lesson's "shortcut" (notes eq. (1.2.29)), with the roles of the operators exchanged.

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

There is a reason for the commutator that needs no ladder operators: $H_0$ does not change under rotations, and $L_2$ generates rotations about the second axis.

Because $\delta H$ never connects different levels, every second-order sum of the lessons vanishes, and so does every higher order. Treating one level at a time, as parts (a) to (e) did, is then the complete solution, and it is exact for every $\lambda$, large or small. MIT's sheet calls such a perturbation "non-generic". A general perturbation does connect different levels, and then the one-level treatment is only the first step, good for small $\lambda$.

One consequence (our addition): since the levels do not influence each other, they can cross. The top state of this level, with energy $\hbar\omega\left(\frac52 + \lambda\right)$, meets the lowest state of the level with two quanta, which has energy $\hbar\omega\left(\frac72 - 2\lambda\right)$, at $\lambda = \frac13$. For larger $\lambda$ the order of the levels is no longer that of $H_0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

MIT's sheet (2018) quotes as the most precise measurement of the proton radius the one made at the Paul Scherrer Institute (PSI), $R = 0.84184(67)\times 10^{-13}$ cm (R. Pohl and others, Nature, 2010), and calls it controversial because it lies 4% below the CODATA world average of the time, $R = 0.8768(69)\times 10^{-13}$ cm. Leaving the controversy aside: explain why the PSI experiment could reach such accuracy by using muonic hydrogen, a bound state of a muon and a proton, in place of ordinary hydrogen. MIT's hint: the muon has much the same properties as the electron, except that it is 206.8 times heavier.

The box is our addition. By which factor is the shift of part (b), taken as a fraction of the binding energy, larger in muonic hydrogen than in hydrogen? Use the hint as it stands: treat the proton as infinitely heavy in both atoms.

<Hint>

How does the Bohr radius depend on the mass of the orbiting particle? Put the muon's mass in place of the electron's.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Take your fraction from part (b). How does it depend on $a_0$, and how does $a_0$ depend on the mass? The proton has the same $R$ in both atoms.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-mu" label="Factor, in units of $10^4$:" value="4.28" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-mu" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A heavier particle orbits closer in. The muon spends far more of its time inside the proton than the electron does, so the size of the proton changes its energy levels far more.

<Step title="The muonic atom is small">

The Bohr radius $a_0 = \hbar^2/(me^2)$ is inversely proportional to the mass. With a muon it is 206.8 times smaller, and the density at the proton, $1/(\pi a_0^3)$, is $206.8^3 \approx 8.8\times 10^6$ times larger. The shift $\frac25e^2R^2/a_0^3$ grows by that same factor.

</Step>

<Step title="As a fraction of the binding energy">

The binding energy $e^2/(2a_0)$ grows by one factor 206.8, so the fraction $\frac45\left(R/a_0\right)^2$ grows by two:

<Formula label="Gain from using a muon" tex="\frac{\left(\Delta E/E_\text{binding}\right)_{\mu p}}{\left(\Delta E/E_\text{binding}\right)_{ep}} = \left(\frac{m_\mu}{m_e}\right)^2 = 206.8^2 = 4.28\times 10^4" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 5 (our own solution, with MIT's mass ratio)" />

In hydrogen the size of the proton moves the ground state by $2.86\times 10^{-10}$ of the binding energy. In muonic hydrogen the same formula gives about $1.2\times 10^{-5}$: a part in a hundred thousand instead of a few parts in ten billion. A shift of that size can be measured well with laser spectroscopy of the muonic atom, and since the shift goes like $R^2$, a good measurement of it gives a precise radius. In ordinary hydrogen the same information is buried forty thousand times deeper.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The proton is not infinitely heavy, and for the muon that matters. With the reduced mass in place of the mass (our addition, with the mass ratios $m_\mu/m_e = 206.768$ and $m_p/m_e = 1836.15$), the muon's effective mass is 185.8 electron masses and the factor becomes about $3.5\times 10^4$. The conclusion is the same.

The two radii on MIT's sheet differ by $(0.8768 - 0.84184)/0.8768 = 0.040$, which is 4.0%, as it says. (A later development: the CODATA adjustment of 2018 moved its recommended value to $0.8414(19)\times 10^{-13}$ cm, in line with the muonic result.)

</Step>
