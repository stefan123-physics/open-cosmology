The last lecture ended with a problem: for identical particles, many different kets seem to describe the same physical situation, and declaring them all equivalent leads to a contradiction. Zwiebach now builds the tools that resolve it, **permutation operators**, which rearrange the states of particles, and with them "a bit of group theory" (L23.1 transcript p. 1). This page covers the whole of Lecture 23: two particles first, then $N$ particles, the symmetric group, even and odd permutations, and what symmetric and antisymmetric states must satisfy (notes chapter 8, section 8.2.2, pp. 154–158). The projectors onto those states, and the postulate that settles exchange degeneracy, come in the next lecture. (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 8; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The notes put hats on the operators; this page leaves them off.)

## Two particles and the operator P₂₁

Start with two particles, each with the same space of states $V$, so that the pair lives in $V\otimes V$ (L23.1 transcript pp. 1–2). Whether the particles are identical plays no role in this lecture: everything here is a statement about operators on $V\otimes V$. If the $|u_i\rangle$ form an orthonormal basis of $V$, the kets $|u_i\rangle\otimes|u_j\rangle$, for all $i$ and $j$, form a basis of $V\otimes V$. As before, the first ket is particle 1's state and the second particle 2's; the notes write the particle's number under each ket, and Zwiebach admits to sometimes getting lazy and writing just $u_i u_j$ (L23.1 transcript p. 2).

A linear operator is safely defined by its action on a basis: then linearity gives its action on every sum of basis vectors (L23.1 transcript p. 2). The **permutation operator** $P_{21}$ is defined by

<Formula label="The permutation operator P₂₁" tex="P_{21}\,|u_i\rangle\otimes|u_j\rangle = |u_j\rangle\otimes|u_i\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.7), p. 154" />

Read the label as an instruction: the state that was in position 2 goes to position 1, and the state that was in position 1 goes to position 2 (L23.1 transcript p. 2). The particles themselves are never reordered (particle 1 is always written first); it is the states that move. For distinguishable particles, the notes stress, the two kets in this equation are different states (eq. (8.2.6), p. 154).

Acting twice swaps the states and swaps them back (L23.1 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="P₂₁ is its own inverse" tex="P_{21}\,P_{21} = 1" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.8), p. 154" />

## P₂₁ is Hermitian and unitary

Less obvious is that $P_{21}$ is **Hermitian** (L23.1 transcript p. 3). An operator $M$ is Hermitian when it can be moved from one side of an inner product to the other unchanged. Check it on basis states: with $P_{21}$ acting on the ket, the states $u_j$ and $u_i$ end up opposite $u_k$ and $u_l$; with $P_{21}$ acting on the bra instead, $u_l$ and $u_k$ end up opposite $u_i$ and $u_j$. Both ways give the same pair of Kronecker deltas (in the short notation, where $|u_i\,u_j\rangle$ stands for $|u_i\rangle\otimes|u_j\rangle$):

<Formula label="Matrix elements of P₂₁" tex="\langle u_k\,u_l|\,P_{21}\,|u_i\,u_j\rangle = \delta_{kj}\,\delta_{li}" cite="reading5" at="p. 154, between eqs. (8.2.10) and (8.2.11)" />

Swapping the states in the ket gives the same inner product as swapping them in the bra, which is the intuition behind the result. So

<Formula label="P₂₁ is Hermitian" tex="P_{21}^\dagger = P_{21}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (8.2.9), (8.2.11), p. 154" />

and, combined with $P_{21}P_{21} = 1$, it is also **unitary** (L23.1 transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="P₂₁ is unitary" tex="P_{21}^\dagger\,P_{21} = P_{21}\,P_{21}^\dagger = 1" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.12), p. 154" />

A unitary operator preserves the norm of states, so it could be a symmetry of a quantum system, Zwiebach notes (L23.1 transcript p. 4).

## The projectors S and A

With $P_{21}$ define two new operators (L23.1 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Symmetric and antisymmetric projectors for two particles" tex="S = \frac12\left(1 + P_{21}\right), \qquad A = \frac12\left(1 - P_{21}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.13), p. 155" />

The claim is that both are **orthogonal projectors**. Recall what that means (L23.1 transcript p. 4; notes footnote 2, p. 155). A projector maps the space onto its **range**, and it has a **null space**, the vectors it kills. The whole space splits into these two parts, and the projector is orthogonal when every vector of the range is orthogonal to every vector of the null space. In practice there is a simple test: a Hermitian operator $P$ with $P^2 = P$ is an orthogonal projector (L23.1 transcript p. 4).

Zwiebach proves this (L23.1 transcript p. 5). Any vector can be split as

<Formula label="Splitting a vector with a projector" tex="|v\rangle = P\,|v\rangle + \left(1 - P\right)|v\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.14), p. 155" />

The first part is in the range of $P$. The second is in its null space, since $P(1 - P) = P - P^2 = 0$. For orthogonality, take something in the range, $P|v\rangle$, and something in the null space, $(1 - P)|w\rangle$. Moving $P$ across the inner product turns it into $P^\dagger$, which is $P$ because $P$ is Hermitian:

<Formula label="Range and null space are orthogonal" tex="\langle v|\,P^\dagger\left(1 - P\right)|w\rangle = \langle v|\,P\left(1 - P\right)|w\rangle = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

$P^2 = P$ alone gives a projector; it takes Hermiticity to make it an orthogonal one.

Now check $S$ and $A$ (L23.1 transcript pp. 5–6). Both are Hermitian, because $1$ and $P_{21}$ are. Both square to themselves, which can be shown at once with the upper sign for $S$ and the lower one for $A$, using $P_{21}P_{21} = 1$:

<Formula label="S and A square to themselves" tex="\frac14\left(1 \pm P_{21}\right)\left(1 \pm P_{21}\right) = \frac14\left(1 \pm 2P_{21} + P_{21}P_{21}\right) = \frac12\left(1 \pm P_{21}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

So $S^2 = S$ and $A^2 = A$. The notes add (eq. (8.2.15) and the lines before it, p. 155) that $S + A = 1$ and $SA = AS = 0$, and that $P_{21}S = S$ and $P_{21}A = -A$: for any state $|\psi\rangle$, $S|\psi\rangle$ is unchanged by $P_{21}$ (symmetric) and $A|\psi\rangle$ changes sign (antisymmetric). Lecture 24 builds the $N$-particle versions of these projectors and returns to $S + A = 1$ for two particles.

## Permutation operators acting on operators

Permutation operators also act on operators, and that matters for Hamiltonians (L23.2 transcript p. 1). Take an operator $B$ on the single-particle space $V$. On $V\otimes V$ it gives two operators, $B_1$, which acts on the first particle only, and $B_2$, which acts on the second (the notes write them with the particle number in brackets, p. 155):

<Formula label="An operator on particle 1 or particle 2" tex="B_1\,|u_i\rangle\otimes|u_j\rangle = \left(B\,|u_i\rangle\right)\otimes|u_j\rangle, \qquad B_2\,|u_i\rangle\otimes|u_j\rangle = |u_i\rangle\otimes\left(B\,|u_j\rangle\right)" cite="reading5" at="p. 155" />

An operator acts on an operator from the left and from the right: if states change with a unitary $U$, operators change by $U$ on one side and $U^\dagger$ on the other (L23.2 transcript p. 1). So the natural question is not what $P_{21}B_1$ is, but what $P_{21}B_1P_{21}^\dagger$ is. Apply it to a basis state: $P_{21}^\dagger = P_{21}$ swaps the states, $B_1$ acts on $u_j$, which is now in position 1, and the last $P_{21}$ swaps back, leaving $B$ acting on $u_j$ in position 2. That is exactly $B_2$ (L23.2 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="P₂₁ moves an operator to the other particle" tex="P_{21}\,B_1\,P_{21}^\dagger = B_2" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.17), p. 156" />

Multiplying by $P_{21}$ on the left and $P_{21}^\dagger$ on the right gives the reverse without a new calculation (L23.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="And back" tex="P_{21}\,B_2\,P_{21}^\dagger = B_1" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.18), p. 156" />

Now let $O(1,2)$ be any operator built from operators acting on particle 1 and on particle 2, products and sums of them in any order (L23.2 transcript p. 2). Conjugating a product with a unitary operator is the same as conjugating each factor, since $P^\dagger P = 1$ can be slipped in between any two factors. So every label 1 becomes a 2 and every 2 a 1:

<Formula label="Conjugation exchanges the labels" tex="P_{21}\,O(1,2)\,P_{21}^\dagger = O(2,1)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.19), p. 156" />

An operator is **symmetric** if $O(2,1) = O(1,2)$. Then the left side above equals $O(1,2)$; multiplying on the right by $P_{21}$ gives $P_{21}\,O(1,2) = O(1,2)\,P_{21}$ (L23.2 transcript pp. 2–3). Reading the steps backwards gives the converse:

<Formula label="Symmetric operators commute with P₂₁" tex="\left[P_{21},\,O(1,2)\right] = 0" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (8.2.20)–(8.2.21), p. 156" />

holds exactly when $O(1,2)$ is symmetric. (This page's remark: a Hamiltonian of two identical particles is symmetric in this sense, which is where the result will matter; the lecture only says it helps to understand what a symmetric Hamiltonian means.)

## N particles: the symmetric group

Two particles are a little too special to show the structure (L23.3 transcript p. 1). For $N$ particles a permutation is a reordering of $N$ objects: there are $N$ choices for the new first, $N - 1$ for the second, and so on, so there are $N!$ **permutation operators**. The permutations of $N$ objects form a group, the **symmetric group** $S_N$, with $N!$ elements. Unlike the groups of $2\times2$ unitary matrices or of rotations in space, it is a finite, discrete group (L23.3 transcript p. 1).

The notation is best learned from an example, with four particles in states $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ (L23.3 transcript pp. 1–2). The label of $P_{3142}$ is read position by position: put the third state in position 1, the first in position 2, the fourth in position 3, the second in position 4:

<Formula label="A permutation of four particles" tex="P_{3142}\,|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\otimes|c\rangle\otimes|d\rangle = |c\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\otimes|d\rangle\otimes|b\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.28), p. 157" />

Other books define permutations the other way round, so it pays to check which convention is meant (L23.3 transcript pp. 1–2). With this one, the group structure is easy to work with. Every permutation has an inverse, since what was rearranged can be rearranged back. To undo $P_{3142}$, put the second state of $|c\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\otimes|d\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$ back in position 1, the fourth in position 2, the first in position 3 and the third in position 4. Applying both to $a, b, c, d$ confirms it (L23.3 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The inverse of P₃₁₄₂" tex="P_{2413}\,P_{3142} = 1" cite="reading3" at="p. 2" />

[The transcript once has "p213" for the inverse at this point; the operator Zwiebach writes and checks is $P_{2413}$.] The notes give a three-particle example, $P_{231}P_{312} = 1$ (eq. (8.2.23), p. 156).

In general a permutation is a rule $\alpha$ that rearranges the integers $1, 2, \ldots, N$ into $\alpha(1), \alpha(2), \ldots, \alpha(N)$, and $P_\alpha$ is the operator whose label is the list $\alpha(1)\alpha(2)\cdots\alpha(N)$ (L23.3 transcript p. 2). Acting on a product of states, it puts the state of particle $\alpha(1)$ first, and so on:

<Formula label="The general permutation operator" tex="P_\alpha\,|u_1\rangle\otimes|u_2\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|u_N\rangle = |u_{\alpha(1)}\rangle\otimes|u_{\alpha(2)}\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|u_{\alpha(N)}\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (8.2.24)–(8.2.26), p. 156" />

If $\alpha(1) = 3$, say, the state $u_3$ is pulled into the first position (L23.3 transcript p. 3).

## Three particles and transpositions

For $N = 3$ there are $3! = 6$ permutation operators (L23.3 transcript p. 3; notes p. 157). $P_{123}$ changes nothing: it is the identity. Cycling its label gives $P_{312}$ and $P_{231}$. Exchanging the last two labels gives $P_{132}$, and cycling that gives $P_{213}$ and $P_{321}$. (In the lecture the first list goes astray on the board and a student corrects it: the cycles are $P_{312}$ and $P_{231}$.)

The last three are special. $P_{132}$ leaves the first state in place and exchanges the other two; $P_{321}$ leaves the second in place and exchanges the first and third; $P_{213}$ exchanges the first two. Each is a **transposition**, a permutation that exchanges just two states and leaves the rest alone, and is often written by naming the pair (L23.3 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The transpositions of three particles" tex="(12) = P_{213}, \qquad (13) = P_{321}, \qquad (23) = P_{132}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.29), p. 157" />

A transposition works just like $P_{21}$ for two particles, so the same proof shows that every transposition is **Hermitian and unitary** (L23.3 transcript p. 3; notes p. 157).

Then comes the first remarkable fact about the group: **every permutation is a product of transpositions** (L23.3 transcript pp. 3–4). Exchanging two states at a time, you can reach any order. Since every transposition is unitary, so is every product of them: **all permutation operators are unitary**. They are not all Hermitian, because a product of Hermitian operators that do not commute is not Hermitian in general, just as $x$ and $p$ are Hermitian but their product is not (L23.3 transcript p. 4). The notes add that the adjoint of a permutation is its inverse (eqs. (8.2.30)–(8.2.31), p. 157).

## Even and odd permutations

The product of transpositions that gives a permutation is not unique: different people may reach the same order by different exchanges (L23.3 transcript p. 4). What is unique is whether the number of transpositions is **even or odd**. Zwiebach finds this plausible: adding two transpositions, exchanging two states and exchanging them back, changes nothing, so the number can grow by two without changing the permutation. That it can never change by an odd number is a theorem, which the lecture and the notes state without proof. One way to see it: count the pairs of states that stand in the wrong order (the inversions). Exchanging the states in two positions changes that count by an odd number, so the parity of the number of transpositions equals the parity of the inversion count of the final order, which the permutation alone fixes. (The same fact makes the sign of a determinant well defined; see [Properties of determinants](/courses/18-06/properties-of-determinants).) A permutation is **even** if it is a product of an even number of transpositions and **odd** otherwise (L23.3 transcript p. 4; notes p. 157).

In $S_3$, the identity (zero transpositions) and the two cycles (two each) are even; the three transpositions are odd (L23.3 transcript p. 5). Zwiebach suggests working out the group's multiplication table; the notes give it (Table 8.1, p. 158). Three even and three odd is no coincidence:

**Theorem.** In $S_N$, for $N \ge 2$, there are as many even permutations as odd ones (L23.3 transcript pp. 5–6; notes Theorem 8.2.1, p. 158).

The proof uses the transposition of the first two states, called $P_{21}$ again in the lecture, with the states of particles 3 to $N$ left alone (the notes write it $(12)$). Multiplying an even permutation by it adds one transposition, so it maps every even permutation to an odd one. The map is **one to one**: two different even permutations go to two different odd ones, because $P_{21}$ has an inverse, itself:

<Formula label="The map is one to one" tex="P_{21}\,P_\alpha = P_{21}\,P_\beta \quad \text{implies} \quad P_\alpha = P_\beta" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.32), p. 158" />

(Zwiebach jokes that "one to one" should really be called "two to two" (L23.3 transcript p. 6).) It is also **onto**: every odd permutation is reached, because any odd $P_\gamma$ can be written as

<Formula label="The map is onto" tex="P_\gamma = P_{21}\left(P_{21}\,P_\gamma\right)" cite="reading5" at="p. 158" />

and $P_{21}P_\gamma$ is even (L23.3 transcript p. 6). So the even and the odd permutations are equal in number, $N!/2$ each.

## Symmetric and antisymmetric states

Now the goal: states that will turn out to be the physical ones (L23.4 transcript p. 1). Permutation operators do not commute, and many are not even Hermitian, so there is no hope of a basis of states that are eigenstates of all of them. That does not rule out **some** states being eigenstates of every one: operators that do not commute cannot share a complete basis of eigenstates, but they can share a single eigenstate (L23.4 transcript p. 1).

With $N$ particles each in $V$, the states live in the $N$-fold tensor product $V\otimes V\otimes\cdots\otimes V$. Postulate **symmetric states** $|\psi_S\rangle$, unchanged by every permutation (L23.4 transcript pp. 1–2; notes p. 158):

<Formula label="Symmetric states" tex="P_\alpha\,|\psi_S\rangle = |\psi_S\rangle \quad \text{for all } \alpha" cite="reading5" at="p. 158" />

Each is a simultaneous eigenvector of all permutation operators, with eigenvalue 1. Since these operators do not commute, such states cannot form a basis of the whole space: they make up a smaller space, to be reached later by a projector (L23.4 transcript p. 2).

For **antisymmetric states** the first guess, $P_\alpha|\psi_A\rangle = -|\psi_A\rangle$ for every $\alpha$, is untenable (L23.4 transcript p. 2). The identity is a permutation, and it cannot change the sign. Worse, if each transposition gives a minus sign, a product of two transpositions, itself a permutation, gives a plus. The only consistent choice uses the parity of the permutation:

<Formula label="Antisymmetric states" tex="P_\alpha\,|\psi_A\rangle = \epsilon_\alpha\,|\psi_A\rangle, \qquad \epsilon_\alpha = +1 \text{ if } P_\alpha \text{ is even}, \quad \epsilon_\alpha = -1 \text{ if } P_\alpha \text{ is odd}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (8.2.33), p. 158" />

The identity is even and gets $+1$; a single transposition gets $-1$; two transpositions get $+1$, as they must (L23.4 transcript pp. 2–3).

The symmetric states form a subspace of this $N$-particle space called $\text{Sym}^N V$, and the antisymmetric states a subspace called $\text{Anti}^N V$ (L23.4 transcript p. 3; notes p. 158). The next lecture constructs the projectors onto them, studies these spaces, and shows how they solve the problem of exchange degeneracy, which takes one more postulate of quantum mechanics (L23.4 transcript p. 3).

<Step title="(a) Two products in S₃">
The operator on the right acts first. $P_{213}$ exchanges the first two states: $|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\otimes|c\rangle$. Then $P_{231}$ puts the second state, $a$, first, the third, $c$, second, and the first, $b$, third: $|a\rangle\otimes|c\rangle\otimes|b\rangle$. So $P_{231}P_{213} = P_{132}$. In the other order, $P_{231}$ gives $|b\rangle\otimes|c\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$, and $P_{213}$ then gives $|c\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$, so $P_{213}P_{231} = P_{321}$. The two products differ: these operators do not commute. Both results are transpositions, odd, as they must be for an even permutation times an odd one; they agree with Table 8.1 of the notes.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) An inverse and a parity in S₄">
$P_{2341}$ turns $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\otimes|c\rangle\otimes|d\rangle$ into $|b\rangle\otimes|c\rangle\otimes|d\rangle\otimes|a\rangle$. To undo it, the state now in position 4, $a$, goes back to position 1, then the first, $b$, to position 2, the second, $c$, to position 3 and the third, $d$, to position 4: the inverse is $P_{4123}$. The same order is reached by three exchanges of neighbouring positions (1 and 2, then 2 and 3, then 3 and 4), so $P_{2341}$ is odd, and so is its inverse, which undoes those three exchanges in reverse order. Of the $4! = 24$ permutations of four particles, half, 12, are even.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Symmetric and antisymmetric parts">
$P_{21}$ swaps the states in each term: $P_{21}|\psi\rangle = 0.6\,|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle + 0.8\,|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$. Then $S|\psi\rangle = \frac12\left(|\psi\rangle + P_{21}|\psi\rangle\right) = 0.7\left(|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle + |-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle\right)$ and $A|\psi\rangle = \frac12\left(|\psi\rangle - P_{21}|\psi\rangle\right) = 0.1\left(|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle - |-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle\right)$. Their sum is $|\psi\rangle$, as $S + A = 1$ requires. Their inner product is $0.7 \times 0.1 - 0.7 \times 0.1 = 0$: range and null space of $S$ are orthogonal. The squared norms are $2 \times 0.7^2 = 0.98$ and $2 \times 0.1^2 = 0.02$, which add up to 1, the squared norm of $|\psi\rangle$. And $P_{21}$ leaves $S|\psi\rangle$ unchanged and flips the sign of $A|\psi\rangle$.
</Step>

## Summary

- For two particles in $V\otimes V$, the permutation operator $P_{21}$ swaps the states: $P_{21}\,|u_i\rangle\otimes|u_j\rangle = |u_j\rangle\otimes|u_i\rangle$. It is its own inverse, Hermitian and unitary.
- $S = \frac12(1 + P_{21})$ and $A = \frac12(1 - P_{21})$ are Hermitian and square to themselves, so they are orthogonal projectors: a Hermitian $P$ with $P^2 = P$ splits the space into a range and a null space that are orthogonal.
- On operators, $P_{21}B_1P_{21}^\dagger = B_2$, and in general $P_{21}\,O(1,2)\,P_{21}^\dagger = O(2,1)$; an operator is symmetric exactly when it commutes with $P_{21}$.
- For $N$ particles there are $N!$ permutation operators $P_\alpha$, forming the symmetric group $S_N$; $P_\alpha$ puts the state of particle $\alpha(k)$ in position $k$. All are unitary; the transpositions are also Hermitian. Every permutation is a product of transpositions and is either even or odd, and there are as many even as odd ones.
- Symmetric states obey $P_\alpha|\psi_S\rangle = |\psi_S\rangle$ and antisymmetric states $P_\alpha|\psi_A\rangle = \epsilon_\alpha|\psi_A\rangle$, with $\epsilon_\alpha = \pm1$ for even and odd permutations. They form the subspaces $\text{Sym}^N V$ and $\text{Anti}^N V$; the projectors onto them come next.
