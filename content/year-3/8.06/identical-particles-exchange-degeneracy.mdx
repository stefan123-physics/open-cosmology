With scattering done, Zwiebach turns to the last subject of the course, **identical particles**, which he finds a subject with lots to think about and a fitting end to the course (L22.4 transcript p. 1; notes chapter 8, pp. 151–153). This page covers the last video of Lecture 22: what identical particles are, why classical mechanics has no trouble with them, and the problem they pose in quantum mechanics, **exchange degeneracy**. The solution comes in the next two lectures. (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture and his lecture notes, chapter 8; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The OCW resource page of the notes file carries the title "Chapter 8: Summary of connection formulas", but the file is the chapter on identical particles. The [first page of this lecture](/courses/8-06/born-approximation) covers the Born approximation.)

## What makes particles identical

Two particles are **identical** if all their intrinsic properties are the same: mass, spin, charge, magnetic moment and so on (L22.4 transcript p. 1; notes p. 151). All electrons are identical in this sense. An electron at rest and an electron moving fast do not look the same, but that is a difference of **state**, not of particle: one can have one momentum and the other another, one spin up and the other spin down.

Identical also means **indistinguishable**. Suppose two people each hand Zwiebach an electron with spin up in the same momentum state; he plays with them for a minute and hands them back. There is no experiment by which either person can tell whether they got their own electron or their friend's (L22.4 transcript p. 1).

The idea is not limited to elementary particles (L22.4 transcript p. 2). A proton is made of quarks, but two protons have the same spin, mass and magnetic moment, and prepared in the same state they cannot be told apart. The same holds for neutrons. Quantum mechanics even treats hydrogen atoms as identical particles. It is no objection that one atom may be in its ground state and another excited: those are states of the hydrogen atom, just as spin up and spin down are states of an electron (notes p. 151).

## An aside: isospin

Such statements can be subtler than they look (L22.4 transcript pp. 2–3). Physicists used to treat the proton and the neutron as the same particle. Their masses differ little, and the strong interactions in a nucleus hardly care which is which. So they were described as two states of one particle, the **nucleon**, with a new spin-½ quantum number, **isospin**: isospin up for the proton, isospin down for the neutron, a doublet, much as an electron has spin up and spin down. In that description all nucleons are identical. Isospin is a famous approximate symmetry of the strong interactions; the point is that the formalism of quantum mechanics lets you treat particles as identical, in different states, whenever your experiments cannot tell them apart.

## Identical particles in classical mechanics

In classical mechanics identical particles are **distinguishable** (L22.4 transcript p. 3). You can follow them: this one is particle 1, that one particle 2, and even if they come together, go round each other and separate, their trajectories tell you which is which from beginning to end. You can also **tag** them: put a red dot on one billiard ball and a black dot on the other, which does not change how they collide, and at the end check where the red ball went (L22.4 transcript pp. 3–4).

One feature of identical particles survives even classically: the Hamiltonian is unchanged if the particles are exchanged (L22.4 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The classical Hamiltonian is symmetric" tex="H(\vec r_1, \vec p_1; \vec r_2, \vec p_2) = H(\vec r_2, \vec p_2; \vec r_1, \vec p_1)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (8.1.1), p. 151" />

(The lecture states it at L22.4 transcript p. 4.) The notes spell out why that makes labelling harmless (notes pp. 151–152, Fig. 8.1). Give the particle that starts at one chosen point the label 1, the other the label 2, and solve for both trajectories. Then swap the labels and solve again: since $H$ is symmetric, the equations are the same, and the particle that started at a given point still ends at the same place. The two descriptions are equivalent, so you choose one labelling, as if the particles were different, and follow it through.

## The problem in quantum mechanics

In quantum mechanics both tools fail (L22.4 transcript pp. 3–4). There are no trajectories, only waves, which overlap, do things and separate again, and you cannot tell which went where. And nobody has found a way to tag a particle without drastically changing how it interacts. Classical mechanics applies, in a sense, while the particles stay far apart; when they come close, you lose track of which is which.

So picture two identical particles that collide, with two detectors that click (notes Fig. 8.2, p. 153; L22.4 transcript p. 4). Particle 1 may have been deflected into the first detector and particle 2 into the second, or the other way round, and quantum mechanics offers no way to find out which of the two took place.

## How to write the state?

For **distinguishable** particles we know what to do (L22.4 transcript p. 4). The state of $N$ particles is a tensor product, in which particle 1 is in the state $|\psi_1\rangle$, particle 2 in $|\psi_2\rangle$, and so on:

<Formula label="A state of distinguishable particles" tex="|\psi_1\rangle\otimes|\psi_2\rangle\otimes\cdots\otimes|\psi_N\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

(Zwiebach writes the states with double subscripts, i₁, i₂, …, to stress that each is one of many possible states.)

The simplest case shows the trouble with identical ones (L22.4 transcript pp. 4–5; notes p. 152). Take two spin-½ particles and look only at their spins: one is up, $|+\rangle$, and the other down, $|-\rangle$. (Think of them, the notes say, as static and very close to each other, even on top of each other.) The first ket of a tensor product is particle 1's and the second particle 2's (the notes also write the particle's number under each ket). Should the state be

<Formula label="Two candidate states" tex="|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle \qquad \text{or} \qquad |-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle\;?" cite="reading2" at="eq. (8.2.1), p. 152" />

A priori either one: they look equally plausible. Yet, the notes point out, with our inner product these two states are **orthogonal**.

## Can we declare them equivalent?

If you were inventing quantum mechanics, a tempting hypothesis would be: since the particles are identical, the two kets describe the same physical state, and we may simply declare them equivalent (L22.4 transcript p. 5). Unfortunately, that does not work.

If the two kets were equivalent, then so would be every normalized superposition of them, since a superposition of equivalent states would describe the same state too (L22.4 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="Superpositions of the two candidates" tex="|\psi\rangle = \alpha\,|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle + \beta\,|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle, \qquad |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1" cite="reading2" at="eq. (8.2.2), p. 152" />

The notes give this ambiguity, one physical situation with many candidate kets, a name: **exchange degeneracy** (notes p. 153). To test the hypothesis, ask for the probability that the two particles are found with both spins along $+x$. That state is unambiguous: whatever the hypothesis, "plus and plus" can be written only one way. With $|+, x\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|+\rangle + |-\rangle\right)$ for each particle (L22.4 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="Both spins along +x" tex="|\psi_0\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|+\rangle + |-\rangle\right)\otimes\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|+\rangle + |-\rangle\right) = \frac12\left(|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle + |+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle + |-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle + |-\rangle\otimes|-\rangle\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (8.2.3), p. 153" />

In the inner product with $|\psi\rangle$ only the two mixed terms survive, each with coefficient $\frac12$, so $\langle\psi_0|\psi\rangle = \frac12(\alpha + \beta)$, and the probability is (L22.4 transcript pp. 5–6)

<Formula label="The probability depends on α and β" tex="\left|\langle\psi_0|\psi\rangle\right|^2 = \left|\frac12\left(\alpha + \beta\right)\right|^2" cite="reading2" at="eq. (8.2.4), p. 153" />

Expanding, $|\alpha + \beta|^2 = |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 + \alpha\beta^* + \alpha^*\beta = 1 + 2\,(\text{real part of } \alpha\beta^*)$, so the probability is $\frac14$ plus half the real part of $\alpha\beta^*$. [The lecture squares only the $\alpha + \beta$ and not the $\frac12$, and gets $\frac12$ plus the real part of $\alpha\beta^*$ (L22.4 transcript p. 5); the notes have the probability as above. The conclusion is the same.] So if all these states were equivalent, one physical state would have a probability that depends on which $\alpha$ and $\beta$ we happened to pick: from 0, for $\beta = -\alpha$, to $\frac12$, for $\beta = \alpha$. That is a contradiction (L22.4 transcript p. 6), "a tremendous ambiguity", as the notes put it (p. 153).

So exchange degeneracy cannot be removed by declaring all the candidate states the same. Another way is needed, and that is the subject of the next lecture (L22.4 transcript p. 6).

## More particles

The notes add how the degeneracy grows with the number of particles, which the lecture does not discuss (notes p. 153). Three identical particles in three different states $|a\rangle$, $|b\rangle$ and $|c\rangle$ can be written in six ways, one for each assignment of states to particles:

<Formula label="Three particles in three different states" tex="|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\otimes|c\rangle, \quad |a\rangle\otimes|c\rangle\otimes|b\rangle, \quad |b\rangle\otimes|c\rangle\otimes|a\rangle, \quad |b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\otimes|c\rangle, \quad |c\rangle\otimes|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle, \quad |c\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\otimes|a\rangle" cite="reading2" at="p. 153" />

and any superposition of them is a candidate too. In general $N$ particles in $N$ different states give $N!$ such kets.

<WorkedExample title="How much the choice matters, and how many kets there are">
<Problem>
(a) For the two spin-½ particles above, find the probability of finding both spins along $+x$ for $(\alpha, \beta) = (0.6, 0.8)$, $(0.6, -0.8)$, $(0.6, 0.8i)$ and $(1/\sqrt2, 1/\sqrt2)$. (b) Find the probability of finding both spins along $+z$ instead, for any $\alpha$ and $\beta$. (c) How many product kets describe four identical particles in four different states? And three spin-½ particles, two up and one down?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Along +x">
The probability is $\frac14|\alpha + \beta|^2$. For $(0.6, 0.8)$: $\frac14(1.4)^2 = 0.49$. For $(0.6, -0.8)$: $\frac14(0.2)^2 = 0.01$. For $(0.6, 0.8i)$: $|0.6 + 0.8i|^2 = 0.36 + 0.64 = 1$, so 0.25; here $\alpha\beta^*$ is purely imaginary. For $\alpha = \beta = 1/\sqrt2$: $\frac14 \times 2 = 0.5$, the largest possible value. The same pair of spins, one up and one down, gives anything from 0.01 to 0.5 depending on how we wrote it.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Along +z">
The state $|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$ is orthogonal to both $|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle$ and $|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$, so the probability is 0 for every choice. Some predictions do not depend on $\alpha$ and $\beta$, which is why the problem is easy to miss; one that does is enough to rule the hypothesis out.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Counting">
Four particles in four different states: $4! = 24$ kets, from $4 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1$ choices of state for particles 1 to 4. For two spins up and one down, only the position of the down spin matters: $|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle$, $|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$, three kets, which is $3!/2!$: swapping the two particles that are both up gives nothing new.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Particles are **identical** when all their intrinsic properties (mass, spin, charge, magnetic moment, …) agree, so that no experiment can tell them apart. They can be in different states. Electrons, protons, neutrons, even hydrogen atoms are examples; isospin treats protons and neutrons as two states of one nucleon, as an approximation.
- Classically, identical particles are distinguishable: their trajectories can be followed, or they can be tagged. The Hamiltonian is symmetric under exchange, $H(\vec r_1, \vec p_1; \vec r_2, \vec p_2) = H(\vec r_2, \vec p_2; \vec r_1, \vec p_1)$, so any labelling gives the same physics.
- Quantum particles have no trajectories and cannot be tagged without changing their interactions. Distinguishable particles are described by tensor products; for identical ones, $|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle$, $|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$ and every normalized $\alpha\,|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle + \beta\,|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$ seem equally good: **exchange degeneracy**.
- Declaring them all equivalent fails: the probability of finding both spins along $+x$ is $\left|\frac12(\alpha + \beta)\right|^2$, which ranges from 0 to $\frac12$. For $N$ particles in $N$ different states there are $N!$ candidate kets. The resolution, through permutation operators, comes next.
