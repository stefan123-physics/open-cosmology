The chapter on time-dependent perturbation theory dealt with fields switched on suddenly or oscillating at a definite frequency. This lecture starts a new chapter, on the **adiabatic approximation**: what happens when some parameter of a system changes **slowly** (L15.1 transcript p. 1). That covers a parameter that really drifts in time, such as a magnetic field around a spin that is slowly turned, and also a particle moving slowly through a field that varies in space: the particle sees a field that changes slowly in time, though the field itself does not. One expects everything in the system to adjust slowly when a parameter changes slowly, and it does. The surprise is that some quantities change **very** slowly, much more slowly than one would have guessed; those are the adiabatic ones (L15.1 transcript p. 1). Zwiebach finds the first example in classical mechanics, then turns to quantum mechanics. (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 6, "Adiabatic approximation", pp. 1–6. This page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## An oscillator with a changing frequency

Picture a pendulum held in your hand, and raise and lower your hand so that the length of the string, and with it the frequency, changes (L15.1 transcript p. 1). The model is a harmonic oscillator whose frequency $\omega(t)$ is a prescribed function of time, with $x(t)$ and $p(t)$ following the classical motion (L15.1 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Oscillator with a time-dependent frequency" tex="H(x, p, t) = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}\,m\,\omega^2(t)\,x^2" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.1), p. 1" />

With a time-dependent Hamiltonian there is no conservation of energy: whoever moves the hand does work on the system (L15.1 transcript p. 2). Along the motion, $H$ changes because $x$ changes, because $p$ changes and because $\omega$ changes (L15.1 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Rate of change of the energy" tex="\frac{dH}{dt} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial x}\,\dot x + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\,\dot p + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2), p. 1" />

The motion obeys **Hamilton's equations** (L15.1 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Hamilton's equations" tex="\frac{\partial H}{\partial p} = \dot x, \qquad \frac{\partial H}{\partial x} = -\dot p" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.3), p. 1" />

If these are less familiar than their quantum versions, Zwiebach recalls those too. By Ehrenfest's theorem, and with $x$ acting like $i\hbar\,\partial/\partial p$ just as $p$ acts like $\frac{\hbar}{i}\,\partial/\partial x$, the commutators give (L15.1 transcript pp. 2–3)

<Formula label="The quantum analogues" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \langle[x, H]\rangle = \left\langle i\hbar\,\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\right\rangle, \qquad i\hbar\,\frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = \langle[p, H]\rangle = \left\langle -i\hbar\,\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}\right\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.4), p. 2" />

With Hamilton's equations the first two terms of $dH/dt$ are $-\dot p\,\dot x$ and $+\dot x\,\dot p$, which cancel. Only the explicit time dependence is left, as it should be: with none, the energy would be conserved (L15.1 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The energy changes through ω only" tex="\frac{dH}{dt} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = m\,\omega\,\dot\omega\,x^2" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.5), p. 2" />

So the energy changes, and slowly if $\omega$ changes slowly. True, but, as Zwiebach says, not very interesting (L15.1 transcript pp. 2–3).

## What "slowly" means

An **adiabatic** change is one whose time scale $\tau$ is much longer than the period of the oscillation, $T = 2\pi/\omega(t)$ (notes p. 2). Picture $\omega(t)$ constant, then rising gently over a time $\tau$ to a new constant value; adiabatic means $\tau \gg T$. With a period of about a second, a change spread over a year would certainly qualify (L15.1 transcript p. 3).

To make this precise, Zwiebach copies what was done for WKB, where the de Broglie wavelength had to change little over one wavelength (L15.1 transcript p. 4). Here the change of $\omega$ over one period, roughly $\dot\omega\,T$, must be much smaller than $\omega$ itself. Dropping the $2\pi$, that gives two equivalent forms, the second because the derivative of $1/\omega$ is $-\dot\omega/\omega^2$ (L15.1 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Condition for an adiabatic change" tex="\frac{2\pi}{\omega^2}\left|\frac{d\omega}{dt}\right| \ll 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{\dot\omega}{\omega^2} \ll 1, \qquad \left|\frac{dT}{dt}\right| \ll 1" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

The last form says the period itself changes slowly, the analogue of the WKB condition $|d\lambda/dx| \ll 1$ (L15.1 transcript p. 4). (These two precise forms are from the lecture; the notes state only $\tau \gg T$.)

## The adiabatic invariant E/ω

Is there something that changes even more slowly than the energy, something that hardly changes at all? Two quantities that both drift slowly could drift together, so that their ratio stays almost fixed. This was an important discovery of classical mechanics, and the claim is that the ratio is energy over frequency (L15.2 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="The adiabatic invariant" tex="I(t) = \frac{H(t)}{\omega(t)}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.6), p. 2" />

$I$ is almost constant in adiabatic changes and is called the **adiabatic invariant**; it has units of energy times time, like $\hbar$ (L15.2 transcript p. 1). That is a hint. For a quantum oscillator $E = \hbar\omega\,(n + \frac{1}{2})$, so $E/\omega$ is essentially a quantum number, and a whole number cannot change slowly: if it changes at all, it jumps. The adiabatic invariant reflects, in classical physics, a system's resistance to changing its quantum level (L15.2 transcript p. 1). Zwiebach does not prove that $I$ is constant (it is not, exactly), but shows why it changes so little.

Differentiate the ratio and use the rate of change of $H$ found above (L15.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Rate of change of the invariant" tex="\frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{1}{\omega^2}\left(\omega\,\frac{dH}{dt} - H\,\dot\omega\right) = \frac{\dot\omega}{\omega^2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\,m\,\omega^2x^2 - \frac{p^2}{2m}\right) = \frac{\dot\omega}{\omega^2}\,(V - K)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.7), p. 2" />

The $x^2$ terms do not cancel completely, because of their factors of 2: $m\omega^2x^2 - \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2x^2$ leaves half. What remains is the Hamiltonian with the sign of the kinetic term flipped: the potential energy $V$ minus the kinetic energy $K$ (L15.2 transcript p. 2). The factor $\dot\omega/\omega^2$ is small and slowly varying. The bracket is neither: $V$ is largest at the turning points, where $K$ vanishes, and $K$ is largest at the centre. For a fixed frequency and the motion $x = A\sin\omega t$, $p = Am\omega\cos\omega t$ (L15.2 transcript pp. 2–3; notes p. 2),

<Formula label="V − K oscillates at twice the frequency" tex="V - K = \frac{1}{2}\,m\,\omega^2A^2\left(\sin^2\omega t - \cos^2\omega t\right) = -\frac{1}{2}\,m\,\omega^2A^2\cos 2\omega t" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.8), p. 2" />

[The notes drop the factor $A^2$, and the lecture says only that $V - K$ goes like $\omega^2\cos 2\omega t$; the full expression is written here.] This oscillates quickly and averages to zero. Over one period (L15.2 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Change of I over one period" tex="I(t + T) - I(t) = \int_t^{t + T}\frac{\dot\omega}{\omega^2}(t')\,\bigl(V(t') - K(t')\bigr)\,dt'" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.9), p. 3" />

Since $\dot\omega/\omega^2$ barely changes in one period, it comes out of the integral (L15.2 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Taking the slow factor out" tex="I(t + T) - I(t) \approx \frac{\dot\omega}{\omega^2}(t)\int_t^{t + T}\bigl(V(t') - K(t')\bigr)\,dt' \approx 0" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (1.10), (1.11), p. 3" />

For a fixed frequency the potential and kinetic energies of an oscillator have exactly the same average over a period, and the integral vanishes; for a slowly changing frequency it nearly vanishes. That is an **extra suppression**: $I$ already changes slowly because everything does, and on top of that its rate of change averages out. When the energy changes slowly, $E/\omega$ changes very much more slowly, and that is what earns it the name adiabatic invariant (L15.2 transcript p. 3).

## The invariant as an area in phase space

There is a geometric picture (L15.3 transcript p. 1). Plot the motion in the phase plane, $x$ across and $p$ up. At fixed $\omega$ the energy is conserved, and

<Formula label="An orbit in phase space" tex="\frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}\,m\,\omega^2x^2 = E" cite="reading5" at="p. 3" />

is an ellipse: the particle goes round it once per period. Its semi-axes are the value of $x$ where $p = 0$ and the value of $p$ where $x = 0$ (L15.3 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Semi-axes of the ellipse" tex="a = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m\,\omega^2}}, \qquad b = \sqrt{2mE}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.12), p. 3" />

[The transcript has "2 square root of 2E over m omega squared" for $a$; the notes have no factor 2 in front, and only without it does the area below come out right.] Which axis is the longer depends on the units, and does not matter. The area of an ellipse is $\pi ab$; the masses cancel and (L15.3 transcript p. 1)

<Formula label="Area of the orbit" tex="\pi ab = \frac{2\pi E}{\omega}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.13), p. 3" />

The adiabatic invariant is the area of the orbit, divided by $2\pi$. As $\omega$ changes slowly, the ellipse changes shape, but its area stays almost fixed (L15.3 transcript p. 1).

The area can also be written as an integral. Going round the orbit clockwise, from left to right along the top half, $\int p\,dx$ is the area between the top half and the $x$ axis; coming back along the bottom, $dx$ and $p$ are both negative and the integral adds the area of the bottom half (L15.3 transcript p. 2). So the integral round the closed orbit is the whole area (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="The invariant as a contour integral" tex="\oint p\,dx = \frac{2\pi E}{\omega} = 2\pi I" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.14), p. 4" />

This form is the general one: in classical mechanics, for other periodic systems too, $\oint p\,dx$ over an orbit is an adiabatic invariant, and adiabatic invariants are found as such integrals over phase space (L15.3 transcript p. 2; notes p. 4).

## Quantum numbers do not like to change

In quantum mechanics the oscillator makes the link exact (L15.3 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The oscillator's invariant is a quantum number" tex="\frac{E}{\omega} = \frac{\hbar\omega\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)}{\omega} = \hbar\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.15), p. 4" />

The adiabatic invariant becomes a quantum number. The quantum adiabatic theorem will say, in essence, that a system that starts in a state with definite quantum numbers and is changed slowly almost certainly stays in the corresponding state: quantum numbers do not change under adiabatic changes (L15.3 transcript p. 2). That sounds odd after a whole chapter of time-dependent perturbation theory, which was about nothing but changing quantum numbers (L15.3 transcript p. 3).

WKB tells the same story. For a potential with two turning points $a$ and $b$ (here $a$ and $b$ are positions, not the semi-axes), the Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization condition, which gives the oscillator exactly, is (L15.3 transcript p. 3)

<Formula label="Bohr–Sommerfeld quantization" tex="\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_a^b p(x)\,dx = \left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)\pi \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \oint p(x)\,dx = 2\pi\hbar\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.16), p. 4" />

Twice the integral from $a$ to $b$ is the integral there and back, round the orbit. So the classical adiabatic invariant $\oint p\,dx$ is, semiclassically, a quantum number, in any potential, not only the oscillator: one more reason to expect quantum numbers to survive slow changes (L15.3 transcript p. 3).

Transitions point the same way (L15.3 transcript pp. 3–4). To first order, a perturbation $\delta H(t)$ takes a system from $|i\rangle$ to $|f\rangle$ with probability

<Formula label="First-order transition probability" tex="P_{fi}(t) = \left|\int_0^t e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\,\frac{\delta H_{fi}(t')}{i\hbar}\,dt'\right|^2" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.17), p. 4" />

For a constant perturbation the matrix element comes out of the integral, and (L15.3 transcript p. 3)

<Formula label="Constant perturbation" tex="P_{fi}(t) = \frac{|\delta H_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\left|e^{i\omega_{fi}t} - 1\right|^2}{\omega_{fi}^2}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.18), p. 4" />

This stays bounded however long one waits, and it is suppressed by $1/\omega_{fi}^2$: a big energy gap between discrete levels is hard to jump (L15.3 transcript p. 4; notes p. 4). The suppression survives if the perturbation varies slowly compared with $\omega_{fi}$, since over a short stretch it is almost constant. Earlier lectures escaped it in two ways: a continuum of final states, where no big energy change is needed (Fermi's golden rule), or a perturbation oscillating at the right frequency. A slowly varying Hamiltonian finds it hard to cause transitions (L15.3 transcript p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="Shortening a pendulum slowly">
<Problem>
A pendulum bob of mass $m = 0.50$ kg hangs on a string of length 1.00 m and swings with a small amplitude, $A_1 = 5.0$ cm measured sideways. Over 10 minutes the string is pulled up steadily through a small ring until it is 0.25 m long. For small swings the bob is an oscillator in $x$, its sideways displacement, with $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$, $g = 9.81$ m/s². (a) Find the frequency and period at the start and at the end. (b) Check that the change is adiabatic. (c) Find the energy at the start and, from the adiabatic invariant, at the end, and the work done by whoever pulls the string. (d) Find the final amplitude, as a length and as an angle. (e) How large is $I/\hbar$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Frequencies and periods">
At the start $\omega_1 = \sqrt{9.81/1.00} \approx 3.132$ rad/s, a period of $2\pi/\omega_1 \approx 2.006$ s. At the end $\omega_2 = \sqrt{9.81/0.25} \approx 6.264$ rad/s, twice as fast, and the period is about 1.003 s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Is it slow?">
The string shortens at $0.75/600 = 1.25 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s. With $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$, $\dot\omega/\omega^2 = |\dot L|/(2\sqrt{gL})$, which grows as the string shortens: about $2.0 \times 10^{-4}$ at the start and $4.0 \times 10^{-4}$ at the end. Both are far below 1, so the change is adiabatic. (The period changes by $|dT/dt| = 2\pi\,\dot\omega/\omega^2 \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-3}$ second per second at most.)
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Energies and work">
$E_1 = \frac{1}{2}m\omega_1^2A_1^2 = 0.5 \times 0.50 \times 9.81 \times 0.050^2 \approx 6.13 \times 10^{-3}$ J, so $I = E_1/\omega_1 \approx 1.96 \times 10^{-3}$ J s. The invariant stays fixed while the frequency doubles, so $E_2 = I\,\omega_2 = 2E_1 \approx 1.23 \times 10^{-2}$ J. The person pulling the string has done about $6.1 \times 10^{-3}$ J of work on the swing, on top of the work of lifting the bob.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Final amplitude">
From $E = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2A^2$ and $E/\omega$ fixed, $A^2 \propto 1/\omega$, so $A_2 = A_1\sqrt{\omega_1/\omega_2} = 5.0/\sqrt 2 \approx 3.5$ cm. As an angle, the swing grows: from $0.050/1.00 = 0.050$ rad (about 2.9°) to $0.0354/0.25 \approx 0.141$ rad (about 8.1°), still a small swing, so the oscillator model holds throughout.
</Step>
<Step title="(e) The quantum number">
With $\hbar = 1.0546 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $I/\hbar \approx 1.86 \times 10^{31}$. If the pendulum were a quantum oscillator, it would sit in a state with $n + \frac{1}{2}$ near $1.86 \times 10^{31}$, and the slow pull would keep that number, while the energy of each quantum, $\hbar\omega$, doubles.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Instantaneous eigenstates

Now for the quantum calculation (L15.4 transcript p. 1). The message, stated in advance: a system that begins in some quantum state stays in that state as the state itself changes, apart from a **phase**. The phase has two parts: a familiar **dynamical phase**, and a less familiar one called **Berry's phase**. The system does not jump to other states. The next lecture then turns to Landau–Zener transitions, an example where jumps do happen, and how strongly they are suppressed (L15.4 transcript p. 1).

The tool is a strange-looking equation. Given $H(t)$, look for states that obey (L15.4 transcript p. 1)

<Formula label="Instantaneous eigenstates" tex="H(t)\,|\psi(t)\rangle = E(t)\,|\psi(t)\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.1), p. 5" />

At each moment $t$, $|\psi(t)\rangle$ is an eigenstate of the Hamiltonian **at that moment**, with energy $E(t)$: an **instantaneous eigenstate**. One can build them by solving the eigenvalue problem at time 0, at a slightly later time, and so on, and joining each eigenstate to the one nearest it a moment later; if $H$ changes slowly, it is clear which goes with which (L15.4 transcript pp. 1–2). Two complications: if two levels cross, it is not clear which way to follow, and Zwiebach hopes for now that this does not happen; and the states are not unique, since multiplying $|\psi(t)\rangle$ by any time-dependent phase $e^{i\chi(t)}$ still solves the equation (L15.4 transcript p. 2). The states are taken normalized at every moment, $\langle\psi(t)|\psi(t)\rangle = 1$ (L15.4 transcript p. 4).

The instantaneous eigenstates do **not** solve the Schrödinger equation (L15.4 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The Schrödinger equation" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi(t)\rangle = H(t)\,|\Psi(t)\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.2), p. 5" />

Put $|\psi(t)\rangle$ in, and the left side has a time derivative $|\dot\psi\rangle$ that nothing on the right matches. They are auxiliary states, to be used to build solutions (L15.4 transcript p. 2).

## A first attempt at the solution

For a time-independent Hamiltonian, an energy eigenstate evolves with the factor $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$. With a changing energy, the natural guess is the exponential of the integral of the energy, whose time derivative is the energy itself; and in case that is not enough, a factor $c(t)$ to be determined (L15.4 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="The ansatz" tex="|\Psi(t)\rangle = c(t)\,e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t E(t')\,dt'}\,|\psi(t)\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.3), p. 5" />

Substituting, the left side of the Schrödinger equation has three terms, from differentiating $c$, the exponential and the state (L15.4 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Left side" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi(t)\rangle = e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t E\,dt'}\left(i\hbar\,\dot c\,|\psi(t)\rangle + c\,E(t)\,|\psi(t)\rangle + i\hbar\,c\,|\dot\psi(t)\rangle\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.4), p. 5" />

On the right, $H(t)$ passes through $c(t)$ and the exponential and acts on the instantaneous eigenstate, giving $E(t)$ (L15.4 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Right side" tex="H(t)\,|\Psi(t)\rangle = c\,e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t E\,dt'}\,E(t)\,|\psi(t)\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.5), p. 5" />

The energy terms cancel, as the guess intended. What is left must vanish, and after cancelling the exponential and $i\hbar$ (L15.4 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The condition on c" tex="\dot c(t)\,|\psi(t)\rangle = -c(t)\,|\dot\psi(t)\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.6), p. 5" />

Take the inner product with $\langle\psi(t)|$. Since $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = 1$, this gives an ordinary differential equation, solved by integration, with $c(0) = 1$ (L15.4 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Solving for c" tex="\dot c(t) = -c(t)\,\langle\psi(t)|\dot\psi(t)\rangle \quad\Rightarrow\quad c(t) = e^{-\int_0^t\langle\psi(t')|\dot\psi(t')\rangle\,dt'}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (2.7), (2.8), p. 5" />

This looks like a decaying factor, but it is a phase: $\langle\psi|\dot\psi\rangle$ is **purely imaginary** (L15.4 transcript p. 4). Write the inner product as an integral over space and move the time derivative, as in an integration by parts (L15.4 transcript pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="The overlap with the time derivative" tex="\langle\psi|\dot\psi\rangle = \int dx\,\psi^*\,\frac{d\psi}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\int dx\,\psi^*\psi - \int dx\,\frac{d\psi^*}{dt}\,\psi = 0 - \langle\dot\psi|\psi\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.9), p. 6" />

The first term is the time derivative of the norm, which is 1, so it vanishes; the second is the complex conjugate of the overlap itself (L15.4 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="The overlap is imaginary" tex="\langle\psi|\dot\psi\rangle = -\langle\dot\psi|\psi\rangle = -\langle\psi|\dot\psi\rangle^*" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.10), p. 6" />

A number equal to minus its complex conjugate is imaginary. To show this in the notation, write $i\langle\psi|\dot\psi\rangle$, which is real, and put another $i$ outside (L15.4 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="c is a pure phase" tex="c(t) = e^{\,i\int_0^t i\langle\psi(t')|\dot\psi(t')\rangle\,dt'}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.11), p. 6" />

Back in the ansatz, with a constant $c(0)$ allowed in front (L15.4 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="The adiabatic result (approximate)" tex="|\Psi(t)\rangle \approx c(0)\,e^{\,i\int_0^t i\langle\psi(t')|\dot\psi(t')\rangle\,dt'}\,e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t E(t')\,dt'}\,|\psi(t)\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.12), p. 6" />

The second exponential is the dynamical phase; the first will become Berry's phase.

## Where the calculation went wrong

Zwiebach then asks the class to find a **serious mistake** (L15.4 transcript p. 5). If the calculation were right, it would be an unbelievable result: an exact solution of the Schrödinger equation built from instantaneous eigenstates, saying that a system always stays in its instantaneous eigenstate, and nowhere was it used that $H$ changes slowly. That cannot be right.

The mistake: equation (2.6) is a **vector** equation, and taking its inner product with $\langle\psi(t)|$ checks only one component of it, as if one had solved only the $x$ component of a vector equation (L15.4 transcript p. 6). As in perturbation theory, where one projected both onto the original subspace and outside it, the equation must be checked against every state of a basis. Since $|\psi(t)\rangle$ is known, (2.6) can hold only if $|\dot\psi\rangle$ is parallel to $|\psi\rangle$, and in general it is not (notes p. 6). Concretely, at $t = 0$ build an orthonormal basis by Gram–Schmidt, with $|\psi(0)\rangle$ as its first vector (notes p. 6):

<Formula label="A basis starting from the eigenstate" tex="\bigl\{\,|1\rangle = |\psi(0)\rangle,\ |2\rangle,\ |3\rangle,\ \ldots\,\bigr\}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.14), p. 6" />

The other components of (2.6) at $t = 0$ then demand (notes p. 6)

<Formula label="What the other components require" tex="\langle n|\dot\psi(0)\rangle = 0, \qquad n = 2, 3, \ldots" cite="reading5" at="eq. (2.15), p. 6" />

which will not hold in general. So the ansatz is not good enough, and (2.12) carries an approximate sign. It is still a useful mistake: (2.12) is a good approximation when the Hamiltonian changes adiabatically, and that statement is the **adiabatic theorem**: the system follows its instantaneous eigenstate, up to a dynamical phase and a Berry phase, but in some cases makes transitions between instantaneous eigenstates (L15.4 transcript p. 6). The next lecture justifies it.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Photons in a slowly expanding universe">
In 8.286 Lecture 15, Guth shows that each photon's frequency, and so its energy $h\nu$, falls as $1/a(t)$ as the universe expands, so that radiation's energy density falls as $1/a^4$; in Lecture 17 he finds from conservation of entropy that, as long as the number of particle species stays fixed, the temperature of the cosmic radiation falls as $1/a$. Neither lecture uses adiabatic invariants, but they give a neat way to see both results; the argument below is a standard one, not taken from either course. Picture the radiation in a large box that grows with the universe. Each mode of the box is an oscillator whose frequency is proportional to $1/a$, so the expansion slowly changes its frequency. How slowly? The relevant ratio is $|\dot\omega|/\omega^2 = H/\omega$, with $H = \dot a/a$ the Hubble expansion rate. With $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc, about $2.3 \times 10^{-18}$ per second, and a microwave photon of 100 GHz, $\omega \approx 6.3 \times 10^{11}$ per second, the ratio is about $4 \times 10^{-30}$: about as adiabatic as anything can be. (For free radiation the result $\omega \propto 1/a$ is in fact exact, from Maxwell's equations, so the slowness is sufficient here but not needed.) So $E/\omega$ of each mode stays fixed. Quantum mechanically, that is the number of photons in the mode, which does not change; classically, the energy in the mode falls just as its frequency does, which is the redshift. And because every mode keeps its photon number while all frequencies scale by the same factor $1/a$, a black-body spectrum stays a black-body spectrum, with a temperature that falls as $1/a$, as Guth finds by a different route. This matters most after decoupling, when the photons no longer interact and nothing but the stretching could keep the shape: the CMB spectrum of 8.286 Lecture 18 is still a near-perfect black body.
</Callout>

## Summary

- An adiabatic change is slow compared with the system's own period: $\tau \gg T = 2\pi/\omega$, or more precisely $\dot\omega/\omega^2 \ll 1$, equivalently $|dT/dt| \ll 1$, like WKB's $|d\lambda/dx| \ll 1$.
- For an oscillator with frequency $\omega(t)$, Hamilton's equations give $dH/dt = \partial H/\partial t = m\omega\dot\omega x^2$: the energy changes, slowly.
- The adiabatic invariant $I = E/\omega$ changes much more slowly: $dI/dt = (\dot\omega/\omega^2)(V - K)$, and $V - K$, oscillating as $\cos 2\omega t$, averages to almost zero over a period.
- $2\pi I$ is the area of the orbit in phase space, $\pi ab = 2\pi E/\omega = \oint p\,dx$; in general $\oint p\,dx$ is an adiabatic invariant.
- Quantum mechanically $E/\omega = \hbar(n + \frac{1}{2})$ and, by Bohr–Sommerfeld, $\oint p\,dx = 2\pi\hbar(n + \frac{1}{2})$: the invariant is a quantum number, which should not change under slow changes. First-order transitions under constant or slow perturbations are suppressed by $1/\omega_{fi}^2$, pointing the same way.
- Instantaneous eigenstates obey $H(t)|\psi(t)\rangle = E(t)|\psi(t)\rangle$ but do not solve the Schrödinger equation. The ansatz $c(t)e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int E\,dt'}|\psi(t)\rangle$ gives $c = e^{i\int i\langle\psi|\dot\psi\rangle dt'}$, a pure phase because $\langle\psi|\dot\psi\rangle$ is imaginary, but only by projecting a vector equation onto one state. The result holds only approximately, for slow changes: the adiabatic theorem, with a dynamical phase and a Berry phase.
