The previous lecture stated the connection formulae and used one of them. This lecture derives them: it solves the Schrödinger equation exactly for a linear potential, which brings in Airy functions, learns how those functions behave far from the turning point, and reads off how WKB solutions on either side must be joined (L8.1–L8.4 transcripts; notes section 3.4). Along the way it explains why the formulae work in one direction only. (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 3; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The last section, on tunnelling, comes from the notes, section 3.5, which Zwiebach points to at the end of the lecture.)

## Airy's equation and its solutions

The functions are named after George Biddell Airy, seventh Astronomer Royal from 1835 to 1881, who worked at Greenwich, where the Prime Meridian was established, and who met these functions in optics (L8.1 transcript p. 1). The equation is deceptively simple:

<Formula label="Airy's equation" tex="\frac{d^2\psi}{du^2} = u\,\psi" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.4.19)" />

A Fourier transform turns it into a first-order equation (multiplication by $u$ becomes a derivative in $k$), which yields one solution as an integral (L8.1 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Integral representation" tex="\psi(u) = C\int_\Gamma dk\;e^{\,i\left(\frac{k^3}{3} + ku\right)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Along the real axis the integrand has modulus 1 and merely oscillates faster and faster. It decays where $\text{Im}(k^3) > 0$: in the sector of directions between 0 and $\pi/3$ from the positive real axis, and in the two copies of that sector rotated by $2\pi/3$ (L8.1 transcript pp. 2–3). Since the integrand has no singularities, the contour can be deformed freely as long as its ends go off to infinity inside these sectors. That also makes the derivation rigorous: the integration by parts behind the Fourier argument leaves no boundary terms when the ends of $\Gamma$ lie in decaying sectors (L8.1 transcript pp. 4–5). With $\Gamma$ the real axis and $C = 1/2\pi$, the integral is the Airy function $\text{Ai}(u)$. A second contour, which comes in parallel to the real axis and then goes down into another decaying sector, gives a second solution; the standard second Airy function $\text{Bi}$ combines it with the first (L8.2 transcript p. 1). In real form:

<Formula label="The two Airy functions" tex="\text{Ai}(u) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty\cos\!\left(\frac{k^3}{3} + ku\right)dk, \qquad \text{Bi}(u) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\infty\left[e^{-\frac{k^3}{3} + ku} + \sin\!\left(\frac{k^3}{3} + ku\right)\right]dk" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

## Their behaviour far from the turning point

Unlike $e^z$, whose Taylor series is the same in every direction, the Airy functions have different asymptotic forms in different directions, a Stokes phenomenon (L8.2 transcript p. 2). They follow from **stationary phase**: the integral is dominated by points where the phase $\phi(k) = k^3/3 + ku$ is stationary, $\phi'(k) = k^2 + u = 0$. For $u > 0$ these are $k = \pm i\sqrt u$; lifting the contour through $k = i\sqrt u$, the phase there is $\frac23iu^{3/2}$ plus a quadratic term, which gives a narrow Gaussian (L8.2 transcript pp. 2–3). For $u < 0$ the stationary points are real and the result oscillates (L8.2 transcript p. 4). With $\xi = \frac23|u|^{3/2}$:

<Formula label="Asymptotic forms of Ai" tex="\text{Ai}(u) \simeq \frac{1}{2\sqrt\pi}\,|u|^{-1/4}e^{-\xi} \quad (u \gg 1), \qquad \text{Ai}(u) \simeq \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\,|u|^{-1/4}\cos\!\left(\xi - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) \quad (u \ll -1)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (3.4.21), (3.4.23)" />

<Formula label="Asymptotic forms of Bi" tex="\text{Bi}(u) \simeq \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\,|u|^{-1/4}e^{\xi} \quad (u \gg 1), \qquad \text{Bi}(u) \simeq -\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\,|u|^{-1/4}\sin\!\left(\xi - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) \quad (u \ll -1)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.4.22)" />

Ai decays on one side and oscillates on the other; Bi grows instead of decaying, and oscillates with a sine. Both forms fail near $u = 0$, as they must (L8.2 transcript p. 4).

## Deriving the connection formulae

Take a turning point at $x = a$ with the allowed region to the left, and treat the potential as exactly linear, $V - E = g(x - a)$ with $g > 0$ (L8.3 transcript p. 1). Away from $a$, on both sides, the WKB solutions are

<Formula label="WKB solutions on the two sides" tex="\text{for } x > a: \quad \psi = \frac{A}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\,e^{-\int_a^x\kappa\,dx'} + \frac{B}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\,e^{\int_a^x\kappa\,dx'}, \qquad \text{for } x < a: \quad \psi = \frac{C}{\sqrt{k}}\cos\!\left(\int_x^a k\,dx' - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) + \frac{D}{\sqrt{k}}\sin\!\left(\int_x^a k\,dx' - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)" cite="reading3" at="pp. 1–3" />

The shifts by $-\pi/4$ on the left are allowed (cosine and sine shifted by the same amount still form a general solution) and convenient. Near $a$, but not so far that the potential stops being linear, the exact Schrödinger equation $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + g(x - a)\psi = 0$ becomes Airy's equation in the unit-free variable (L8.3 transcript pp. 2–4)

<Formula label="The scaled coordinate" tex="u = \eta\,(x - a), \qquad \eta = \left(\frac{2mg}{\hbar^2}\right)^{1/3}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.4.18)" />

so its exact solutions are $\psi = \alpha\,\text{Ai}(u) + \beta\,\text{Bi}(u)$ (notes eq. (3.4.20)). In this variable the WKB integrals are simply $\int_a^x\kappa\,dx' = \int_x^ak\,dx' = \frac23|u|^{3/2} = \xi$, and $\sqrt\kappa$, $\sqrt k$ are proportional to $|u|^{1/4}$ (notes eqs. (3.4.30)–(3.4.32)): the asymptotic forms of the Airy functions **are** WKB solutions.

Now compare (L8.3 transcript pp. 4–5; L8.4 transcript p. 1). The exact solution Ai has, on the right, only the decaying exponential, with a factor $\frac12$, and on the left the cosine. Matching, $C = 2A$: this ½ is the origin of the 2 in the first connection formula. The exact solution Bi has the growing exponential on the right and $-\sin$ on the left, so $D = -B$. Together:

<Formula label="The general connection" tex="\frac{A}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\,e^{-\int_a^x\kappa\,dx'} + \frac{B}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\,e^{\int_a^x\kappa\,dx'} \quad\text{connects to}\quad \frac{2A}{\sqrt{k}}\cos\!\left(\int_x^a k\,dx' - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) - \frac{B}{\sqrt{k}}\sin\!\left(\int_x^a k\,dx' - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.4.44)" />

With $A = 1$, $B = 0$ and with $A = 0$, $B = 1$, these are the two connection formulae of the previous lecture. The notes also check how good the asymptotic forms are: $u^{-1/4}e^{-\xi}$ solves not Airy's equation but $\psi'' = \left(u + \frac{5}{16u^2}\right)\psi$, and the WKB condition $|k'| \ll k^2$ becomes $|u| \gg (1/2)^{2/3} \approx 0.63$ (notes eqs. (3.4.24)–(3.4.29)). The turning point's "bad region" is only a few units of $u$ wide.

## Why the arrows

Can the formulae be used both ways? No (L8.4 transcript pp. 1–3). A real potential is only approximately linear, so the matched forms are only the leading behaviour, and small errors are always present. Suppose the solution on the right is known to be a pure decaying exponential, as for a barrier that goes on forever. Then no growing piece is present, and the cosine on the left follows reliably. The reverse fails: an oscillation on the left might contain a tiny unseen admixture of the sine, and that turns into a growing exponential on the right which eventually overwhelms the decaying one. Similarly, an oscillation on the left reliably predicts a growing exponential on the right, since any error only adds a decaying piece that does not matter; but a growing exponential on the right says nothing reliable about the left, because a decaying piece invisible on the right would produce an oscillation as large as the sine. In short:

<Formula label="Rule for the connection formulae" tex="\text{decaying exponential} \rightarrow \text{oscillation}, \qquad \text{oscillation} \rightarrow \text{growing exponential}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (3.4.47)" />

that is, one can connect away from a decaying exponential and into a growing one. For a turning point $x = b$ with the forbidden region on the **left**, the same reasoning gives the mirror-image formulae (L8.4 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Connection formulae, forbidden region on the left" tex="\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\,e^{-\int_x^b\kappa\,dx'} \rightarrow \frac{2}{\sqrt{k}}\cos\!\left(\int_b^x k\,dx' - \frac{\pi}{4}\right), \qquad \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\sin\!\left(\int_b^x k\,dx' - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) \rightarrow -\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\,e^{\int_x^b\kappa\,dx'}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (3.4.45), (3.4.46)" />

(Zwiebach notes that textbooks such as Griffiths do not list all of these.)

## Tunnelling through a barrier

With both kinds of turning point, the notes treat tunnelling through a wide, smooth barrier between turning points $a$ and $b$, an exercise Zwiebach recommends (L8.4 transcript p. 4; notes section 3.5). Start with a transmitted wave beyond $b$; its sine part connects, by the arrow rule, into an exponential that grows toward the left, which is a decaying exponential seen from $a$; that connects to the incident and reflected waves on the left. Comparing the currents of the transmitted and incident waves,

<Formula label="WKB tunnelling probability" tex="T \simeq \exp\!\left(-2\int_a^b\kappa(x)\,dx\right) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{2}{\hbar}\int_a^b\sqrt{2m\left(V(x) - E\right)}\,dx\right)" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (3.5.10), (3.5.11)" />

This captures the exponential suppression, set by the height and width of the barrier; a prefactor in front of it is beyond this approximation. The notes go on to compare this with the exact result for a rectangular barrier, whose prefactor $16(E/V_0)(1 - E/V_0)$ WKB misses, and to use $T$ to estimate the lifetime of a particle trapped behind a barrier; those parts are left to the notes.

<WorkedExample title="An electron near a turning point and through a barrier">
<Problem>
Use $mc^2 = 511.0$ keV and $\hbar c = 197.33$ eV nm. (a) An electron moves in a uniform electric field that changes its potential energy by 1 eV per nm, so $g = 1$ eV/nm. How far from its turning point must it be for WKB to hold, $|u| \gg 0.63$? (b) An electron with $E = 1$ eV meets a barrier of constant height 2 eV. Estimate $T$ for widths 0.5 nm and 1 nm.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The size of the bad region">
$\eta^3 = 2mg/\hbar^2 = 2mc^2g/(\hbar c)^2 = 2 \times 511{,}000 \times 1/197.33^2 \approx 26.2$ nm⁻³, so $\eta \approx 2.97$ per nm. $|u| = 0.63$ corresponds to $|x - a| = 0.63/\eta \approx 0.21$ nm. So WKB needs to stay well beyond a fraction of a nanometre of the turning point; closer than that, the Airy functions are needed.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Tunnelling">
Inside the barrier $\hbar c\,\kappa = \sqrt{2mc^2 \times 1\ \text{eV}}$, so $\kappa \approx 5.12$ per nm, constant. For width $w$, $T \approx e^{-2\kappa w}$: $e^{-5.12} \approx 6.0 \times 10^{-3}$ for 0.5 nm and $e^{-10.25} \approx 3.5 \times 10^{-5}$ for 1 nm. Doubling the width squares $T$. (A rectangular barrier has sharp walls, so WKB is only a rough guide here; its exponent is right, its prefactor is not.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Tunnelling out of a false vacuum">
In Guth's original model of inflation, the universe sat in a false vacuum: a field held at a local minimum of its potential energy, classically stable, but able to tunnel through the barrier to the true vacuum, forming bubbles. As Sidney Coleman showed, its decay rate is set by an exponential of a barrier integral, the field-theory cousin of $\exp(-2\int\kappa\,dx)$. Inflation was to end this way, but, as Guth explains in 8.286, the bubbles collided into an extremely inhomogeneous universe instead of a smooth one, which is why that first model had to be replaced.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Near a turning point, the Schrödinger equation becomes Airy's equation $\psi'' = u\psi$ with $u = (2mg/\hbar^2)^{1/3}(x - a)$; its solutions Ai and Bi are contour integrals of $e^{i(k^3/3 + ku)}$.
- Stationary phase gives their asymptotic forms, which are WKB solutions: Ai $\simeq \frac{1}{2\sqrt\pi}|u|^{-1/4}e^{-\xi}$ and $\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}|u|^{-1/4}\cos(\xi - \frac{\pi}{4})$; Bi grows, and oscillates with $-\sin$.
- Matching gives the connection formulae, with the factor 2 from Ai's ½. They work only away from a decaying exponential or into a growing one.
- For a barrier, $T \approx \exp(-2\int_a^b\kappa\,dx)$.
