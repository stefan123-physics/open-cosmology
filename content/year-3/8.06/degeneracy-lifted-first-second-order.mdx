The previous lecture found the first and most important result of degenerate perturbation theory: in the degenerate subspace the perturbation must be diagonal. This lecture finishes the job (L3.1–L3.4 transcripts; notes section 1.2). It first finds the corrected states and the second-order energies when the first-order shifts are all different, then shows what to do when they are all equal. (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in four short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 1; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Where we left off

The unperturbed Hamiltonian $H^{(0)}$ has a degenerate subspace $V_N$, spanned by $N$ orthonormal states $|n^{(0)};k\rangle$ with the same energy $E_n^{(0)}$; the rest of the state space, $\hat V$, is spanned by states $|p^{(0)}\rangle$ with a single label (L3.1 transcript p. 1). Other degeneracies in $\hat V$ do not matter: degenerate perturbation theory looks at one degenerate level at a time and treats the rest as the rest, just as non-degenerate theory looks at one non-degenerate level in a spectrum that may well contain degeneracies elsewhere. (In the recording Zwiebach says "non-degenerate space" at this point; he means the degenerate subspace.)

The states and energies are expanded in powers of $\lambda$, and the Schrödinger equation gives, order by order (L3.1 transcript pp. 1–2),

<Formula label="The order-λ and order-λ² equations" tex="\left(H^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}\right)|n^{(1)};k\rangle = \left(E_{n,k}^{(1)} - \delta H\right)|n^{(0)};k\rangle, \qquad \left(H^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}\right)|n^{(2)};k\rangle = \left(E_{n,k}^{(1)} - \delta H\right)|n^{(1)};k\rangle + E_{n,k}^{(2)}|n^{(0)};k\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (1.2.21), (1.2.22)" />

As usual, each correction $|n^{(p)};k\rangle$ is chosen orthogonal to $|n^{(0)};k\rangle$. Projecting the order-$\lambda$ equation on $\langle n^{(0)};l|$ showed (L3.1 transcript p. 2) that the chosen basis must make $\delta H$ diagonal in $V_N$, with the first-order shifts on the diagonal:

<Formula label="The perturbation is diagonal in the degenerate subspace" tex="\langle n^{(0)};l|\,\delta H\,|n^{(0)};k\rangle = E_{n,k}^{(1)}\,\delta_{lk}, \qquad E_{n,k}^{(1)} = \delta H_{nk,nk}" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (1.2.24), (1.2.25)" />

Either you start with a basis that already diagonalizes $\delta H$ in $V_N$, or you compute the $N \times N$ matrix of $\delta H$ there and diagonalize it.

## Lifting the degeneracy

If the $N$ first-order shifts are all different,

<Formula label="Degeneracy lifted at first order" tex="E_{n,k}^{(1)} \neq E_{n,l}^{(1)} \quad \text{whenever } k \neq l" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.27)" />

the degeneracy is **lifted**: as $\lambda$ grows from zero, the $N$ levels fan out with different slopes (L3.1 transcript pp. 2–3). The basis that diagonalizes $\delta H$ in $V_N$ is then called a **good basis**: its states are the ones that deform continuously as the perturbation is switched on. The first-order shifts are correct even when they are not all different; what fails then is only the identification of the good basis. Until the last two sections, this page assumes the degeneracy is lifted.

## A shortcut: an operator that commutes with δH

Often you have a favourite basis of $V_N$ and want to know whether $\delta H$ is diagonal in it without computing all the off-diagonal elements (L3.1 transcript pp. 3–5). Here is the rule. **If the basis states are eigenstates of a Hermitian operator $K$, all with different eigenvalues, and $K$ commutes with $\delta H$, the basis is good** (the off-diagonal elements of $\delta H$ vanish). The proof takes one line. For two basis states with $K$ eigenvalues $\kappa_p \neq \kappa_q$, sandwich the commutator, which is zero, and let $K$ act to the left and to the right:

<Formula label="Why a commuting operator diagonalizes δH" tex="0 = \langle n^{(0)};p|[\delta H, K]|n^{(0)};q\rangle = (\kappa_q - \kappa_p)\,\langle n^{(0)};p|\,\delta H\,|n^{(0)};q\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.29)" />

Since $\kappa_q - \kappa_p \neq 0$, every off-diagonal element is zero. (On the board, and in the notes, these eigenvalues are written with the letter lambda; this page uses $\kappa$ so they cannot be confused with the perturbation parameter $\lambda$.) In practice $K$ is often an angular momentum, such as $L_z$: a perturbation that commutes with it cannot connect states of different $m$. This will be the working tool for the hydrogen atom.

## Step two: the part of the state outside V_N

The first-order correction $|n^{(1)};k\rangle$ is orthogonal to $|n^{(0)};k\rangle$, but it may have components along the **other** degenerate states as well as along $\hat V$ (L3.2 transcript p. 1). Projecting the order-$\lambda$ equation on $\langle p^{(0)}|$, the term with $E_{n,k}^{(1)}$ drops out because $|p^{(0)}\rangle$ is orthogonal to $V_N$, and what remains is

<Formula label="Components outside the degenerate subspace" tex="\left(E_p^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}\right)\langle p^{(0)}|n^{(1)};k\rangle = -\,\delta H_{p,nk}, \qquad \delta H_{p,nk} \equiv \langle p^{(0)}|\,\delta H\,|n^{(0)};k\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.31)" />

The energy difference is never zero, because every state of $\hat V$ has an energy different from $E_n^{(0)}$. So (L3.2 transcript p. 2)

<Formula label="The first-order state, so far" tex="|n^{(1)};k\rangle = -\sum_p \frac{\delta H_{p,nk}}{E_p^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}}\,|p^{(0)}\rangle + \big(\text{a piece in } V_N\big)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.32)" />

The last term is still unknown. Writing the sum alone, as if it were the whole answer, is a tempting mistake that Zwiebach admits he once made himself: the order-$\lambda$ equation has now been used completely (projected on $V_N$ it gave the energies, on $\hat V$ this piece), and it simply contains no information about the rest (L3.2 transcript pp. 2–3). The missing piece will come from the order-$\lambda^2$ equation, which is why degenerate perturbation theory has a reputation for being laborious.

## Step three: the second-order equation

Project the order-$\lambda^2$ equation on $\langle n^{(0)};l|$ (L3.2 transcript pp. 3–5). Its left side vanishes, and one term needs care: $\delta H$ acting between $\langle n^{(0)};l|$ and the unknown piece in $V_N$. Although $\delta H$ is only diagonal **within** $V_N$, a degenerate basis state is almost an eigenstate of it: inserting a resolution of the identity,

<Formula label="δH on a degenerate basis state" tex="\delta H\,|n^{(0)};l\rangle = E_{n,l}^{(1)}\,|n^{(0)};l\rangle + \sum_p |p^{(0)}\rangle\langle p^{(0)}|\,\delta H\,|n^{(0)};l\rangle" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.28)" />

The extra piece lies in $\hat V$ and drops out of any inner product with a vector in $V_N$; used on the bra $\langle n^{(0)};l|$, this lets $\delta H$ act like the number $E_{n,l}^{(1)}$ (notes eqs. (1.2.34), (1.2.35)). With this, the projected equation becomes

<Formula label="The master equation" tex="\sum_p \frac{\delta H_{nl,p}\,\delta H_{p,nk}}{E_p^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}} + \left(E_{n,k}^{(1)} - E_{n,l}^{(1)}\right)\langle n^{(0)};l|n^{(1)};k\rangle + E_{n,k}^{(2)}\,\delta_{lk} = 0" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.36)" />

**For $l = k$** the unknown overlap drops out and the second-order energy follows (L3.2 transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="Second-order energy, degeneracy lifted" tex="E_{n,k}^{(2)} = -\sum_p \frac{|\delta H_{p,nk}|^2}{E_p^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.37)" />

It looks exactly like the non-degenerate formula, except that the sum runs only over states **outside** the degenerate subspace.

**For $l \neq k$** the equation shows that the piece in $V_N$ cannot be zero. Without it, the equation would claim that the first sum vanishes, and there is no reason why it should (L3.2 transcript p. 5). Since the shifts differ, we can divide (L3.2 transcript p. 6):

<Formula label="The piece of the first-order state inside V_N" tex="\langle n^{(0)};l|n^{(1)};k\rangle = -\frac{1}{E_{n,k}^{(1)} - E_{n,l}^{(1)}}\sum_p \frac{\delta H_{nl,p}\,\delta H_{p,nk}}{E_p^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}}, \qquad l \neq k" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.39)" />

Here the lifted degeneracy was essential: an equal pair of shifts would put a zero in the denominator. The formula also seems to count orders wrongly, since its numerator has two powers of $\delta H$. But the denominator is a difference of first-order shifts and carries one power of $\delta H$ too, so the ratio is properly first order (L3.2 transcript p. 6). Putting everything together:

<Formula label="Degenerate perturbation theory with the degeneracy lifted at first order" tex="E_{n,k}(\lambda) = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda\,\delta H_{nk,nk} - \lambda^2\sum_p \frac{|\delta H_{p,nk}|^2}{E_p^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}} + O(\lambda^3)" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.41)" />

with the state $|n^{(0)};k\rangle$ corrected at order $\lambda$ by the two pieces above.

## When the degeneracy survives first order

Now suppose every first-order shift is the same (L3.3 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Degeneracy not lifted at first order" tex="\langle n^{(0)};l|\,\delta H\,|n^{(0)};k\rangle = E_n^{(1)}\,\delta_{lk}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.42)" />

The levels leave $E_n^{(0)}$ with a common slope and, we assume, separate only at order $\lambda^2$. This happens often in practice, and the homework has cases of it. Zwiebach remarks that he knows no textbook that treats this case to the end (L3.3 transcript p. 1). The difficulty is that $\delta H$ is now diagonal in **every** basis of $V_N$, so it no longer picks out the good basis, the one whose states change continuously. We must keep the zeroth-order states unknown and let higher orders decide them. Write the good states as unknown combinations,

<Formula label="Unknown good states" tex="|\psi_I^{(0)}\rangle = \sum_{k=1}^{N} a_{Ik}^{(0)}\,|n^{(0)};k\rangle, \qquad I = 1, \dots, N" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.44)" />

where each column vector $a_I^{(0)}$ describes one good state, and expand as before, with an energy that gains its label $I$ only at second order:

<Formula label="Expansion when the degeneracy survives first order" tex="E_{nI}(\lambda) = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda E_n^{(1)} + \lambda^2E_{nI}^{(2)} + \lambda^3E_{nI}^{(3)} + \dots" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.46)" />

The order-$\lambda$ equation projected on $\langle p^{(0)}|$ gives the part of $|\psi_I^{(1)}\rangle$ in $\hat V$, but in terms of the unknown coefficients (L3.3 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="First-order state outside V_N, in terms of the unknown coefficients" tex="\langle p^{(0)}|\psi_I^{(1)}\rangle = -\frac{1}{E_p^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\delta H_{p,nk}\,a_{Ik}^{(0)}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.54)" />

The order-$\lambda$ equation then has nothing more to say. Projecting the order-$\lambda^2$ equation on $\langle n^{(0)};l|$ (L3.4 transcript pp. 1–2), the term with the unknown piece of $|\psi_I^{(1)}\rangle$ in $V_N$ vanishes this time, because all first-order shifts are equal; inserting the result above leaves an equation for the coefficients alone. Define the $N \times N$ Hermitian matrix

<Formula label="The second-order matrix" tex="M_{lk}^{(2)} \equiv -\sum_p \frac{\delta H_{nl,p}\,\delta H_{p,nk}}{E_p^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)}}" cite="reading5" at="eq. (1.2.60)" />

Then the equation is an eigenvalue problem:

<Formula label="The good basis at second order" tex="\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(M_{lk}^{(2)} - E_{nI}^{(2)}\,\delta_{lk}\right)a_{Ik}^{(0)} = 0" cite="reading5" at="eqs. (1.2.61), (1.2.62)" />

**The second-order energies are the eigenvalues of $M^{(2)}$, and its normalized eigenvectors give the good basis.** Once that basis is known, the perturbation theory can be restarted from it, and if the eigenvalues of $M^{(2)}$ all differ, the remaining pieces of the states follow (L3.4 transcript pp. 2–3). Doing so needs the order-$\lambda^3$ equation; the notes quote the results, including the third-order energies (notes eqs. (1.2.63)–(1.2.65)), and leave the details to the exercises.

<WorkedExample title="A pair that splits only at second order">
<Problem>
Two states $|1\rangle$ and $|2\rangle$ have energy 0, and a third state $|3\rangle$ has energy $\Delta = 1$ eV. The perturbation $\lambda\,\delta H$ connects only $|3\rangle$ with the pair: $\langle 3|\lambda\,\delta H|1\rangle = 0.03$ eV and $\langle 3|\lambda\,\delta H|2\rangle = 0.04$ eV (both real), and all its other matrix elements vanish. (a) Is the degeneracy lifted at first order? (b) Find the second-order energies and the good basis. (c) Compare with the exact energies.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) First order">
Inside the pair, $\delta H$ is the zero matrix, so both first-order shifts are zero: the degeneracy is not lifted, and every basis of the pair is equally "diagonal". First order cannot tell us which combinations are good.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The matrix M⁽²⁾">
There is one outside state, $p = 3$, with $E_3^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)} = 1$ eV. In eV, $\lambda^2M^{(2)}$ has entries $-(0.03)^2 = -0.0009$, $-(0.03)(0.04) = -0.0012$ (both off-diagonal) and $-(0.04)^2 = -0.0016$. Its eigenvalues are $0$ and $-0.0025$ eV, with normalized eigenvectors $(4, -3)/5$ and $(3, 4)/5$. So the good basis is $\frac15(4|1\rangle - 3|2\rangle)$, which does not shift at all at second order, and $\frac15(3|1\rangle + 4|2\rangle)$, which moves down by 2.5 meV. The first combination does not couple to $|3\rangle$ at all ($0.03 \times 4 - 0.04 \times 3 = 0$); the second carries the whole coupling, $0.03 \times 0.6 + 0.04 \times 0.8 = 0.05$ eV, and picks up a first-order admixture of about $-0.05\,|3\rangle$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The exact answer">
The uncoupled combination is an exact eigenstate with energy 0. The coupled one and $|3\rangle$ form a two-state problem with off-diagonal element 0.05 eV, whose lower energy is $\frac12\left(1 - \sqrt{1 + 4 \times 0.0025}\right)$ eV $\approx -0.00249$ eV. Second order gave $-0.0025$ eV: agreement to about 0.25%, as expected for a coupling twenty times smaller than the gap.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- If the first-order shifts all differ, the degeneracy is lifted and the basis that diagonalizes $\delta H$ in $V_N$ is the **good basis**. A Hermitian operator that commutes with $\delta H$, and whose eigenvalues distinguish the basis states, certifies a good basis without diagonalizing.
- The first-order state has a piece in $\hat V$, from the order-$\lambda$ equation, and a piece in $V_N$ that only the order-$\lambda^2$ equation fixes, with a first-order energy difference in its denominator.
- The second-order energies, $-\sum_p |\delta H_{p,nk}|^2/(E_p^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)})$, have the non-degenerate form, summed only outside $V_N$.
- If the degeneracy survives first order, the good basis and the second-order energies come from diagonalizing $M^{(2)}$.
