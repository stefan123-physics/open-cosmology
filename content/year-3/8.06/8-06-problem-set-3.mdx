The notation is that of the lessons. The electron has mass $m$ and charge $-e$, units are Gaussian, $a_0 = \hbar^2/(me^2)$ is the Bohr radius and $\alpha = e^2/(\hbar c)$ the fine-structure constant. Hydrogen's states without spin are $\psi_{nlm}$. With spin they are the uncoupled states $|n\,l\,m_l\,m_s\rangle$ or the coupled states $|n\,l\,j\,m_j\rangle$. Vectors carry an arrow and operators a hat only where it helps.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Are p² and p⁴ Hermitian on S states?" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

The relativistic correction of the lesson moved one factor $\hat p^2$ from the ket to the bra. This problem looks at when that is allowed. Take states of hydrogen with $l = 0$. Their wavefunctions depend only on $r$, and on such functions

<Formula label="The operator p² on functions of r alone" tex="\hat p^2 = -\hbar^2\,\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{d}{dr}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

Throughout this problem $f(r)$ and $g(r)$ are real functions of $r$ alone, and $\langle f|g\rangle = \int_0^\infty 4\pi r^2\,f\,g\,dr$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Integrate by parts to show that $\hat p^2$ is Hermitian up to a boundary term:

<Formula label="Moving p² to the other side leaves a boundary term" tex="\langle f|\hat p^2g\rangle = -4\pi\hbar^2\left[r^2f\,\frac{dg}{dr} - r^2g\,\frac{df}{dr}\right]_0^\infty + \langle \hat p^2f|g\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

The S states of hydrogen have the form

<Formula label="Shape of hydrogen's S wavefunctions" tex="\psi_{n00}(r) = N\left(1 + c_1r + \dots + c_{n-1}r^{n-1}\right)\exp\left(-\frac{r}{na_0}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

with constants $N$ and $c_i$. Check that the boundary term vanishes for $g = \psi_{n00}$ and $f = \psi_{n'00}$.

As a test with numbers (our addition), take $f = e^{-k'r}$ and $g = e^{-kr}$ with $k, k' > 0$, and compute $\langle f|\hat p^2g\rangle$. In the box type `q` for $k'$.

<Hint>

Write $\langle f|\hat p^2g\rangle$ as an integral over $r$. The factor $r^2$ of the volume element cancels the $1/r^2$ of the operator, and what is left is $f$ times a total derivative.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate by parts once in $\langle f|\hat p^2g\rangle$ and once in $\langle\hat p^2f|g\rangle$. Both leave the same integral, of $r^2f'g'$. Subtract.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the box: $\hat p^2e^{-kr} = -\hbar^2\left(k^2 - 2k/r\right)e^{-kr}$, and $\int_0^\infty r^ne^{-Kr}\,dr = n!/K^{n+1}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-p2" label="$\langle e^{-k'r}|\hat p^2e^{-kr}\rangle =$" expr="8*pi*hbar^2*k*q/(k+q)^3"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2], q: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", q: "k'" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*hbar^2*k*q/(k+q)^3", hint: "The volume element of a function of r alone is 4π r² dr. The factor 4π is missing." },
    { expr: "-8*pi*hbar^2*k*q/(k+q)^3", hint: "Your result is negative. For k′ = k it is the mean of p² in an unnormalized state, which cannot be negative. Check the sign in p² = −ħ²∇²." },
    { expr: "8*pi*hbar^2*k*(2*k+q)/(k+q)^3", hint: "This is not the same when k and k′ are swapped. The term 2k/r in p² e^(−kr) has a sign opposite to the term k²." },
    { expr: "-8*pi*hbar^2*k*(2*k+q)/(k+q)^3", hint: "This is not the same when k and k′ are swapped. The term 2k/r in p² e^(−kr) has a sign opposite to the term k²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One integration by parts moves a derivative from $g$ to $f$. Doing it from both sides leaves the same integral, so the two inner products differ only by what the integrations leave at $r = 0$ and $r = \infty$.

<Step title="Integrate by parts from both sides">

<Formula label="The inner product with p² on the right" tex="\langle f|\hat p^2g\rangle = -4\pi\hbar^2\int_0^\infty f\,\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dg}{dr}\right)dr = -4\pi\hbar^2\left[r^2f\,\frac{dg}{dr}\right]_0^\infty + 4\pi\hbar^2\int_0^\infty r^2\,\frac{df}{dr}\frac{dg}{dr}\,dr" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The same steps with $f$ and $g$ exchanged give $\langle\hat p^2f|g\rangle = -4\pi\hbar^2\left[r^2g\,f'\right]_0^\infty + 4\pi\hbar^2\int_0^\infty r^2f'g'\,dr$. The integrals are equal. Subtracting gives the relation asked for.

</Step>

<Step title="The boundary term for hydrogen's S states">

With $f = \psi_{n'00}$ and $g = \psi_{n00}$, the product $f\,g'$ is a polynomial times $\exp\left(-r/(n'a_0) - r/(na_0)\right)$. At $r \to \infty$ the exponential beats $r^2$ times any polynomial, so both terms vanish. At $r = 0$ the functions and their first derivatives are finite, so $r^2fg'$ and $r^2gf'$ vanish because of the factor $r^2$. The boundary term is zero and $\langle f|\hat p^2g\rangle = \langle\hat p^2f|g\rangle$.

</Step>

<Step title="The two exponentials (our addition)">

With $K = k + k'$:

<Formula label="Matrix element of p² between two exponentials" tex="\langle e^{-k'r}|\hat p^2e^{-kr}\rangle = -4\pi\hbar^2\int_0^\infty\left(k^2r^2 - 2kr\right)e^{-Kr}\,dr = -4\pi\hbar^2\left(\frac{2k^2}{K^3} - \frac{2k}{K^2}\right) = \frac{8\pi\hbar^2\,kk'}{(k + k')^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result does not change when $k$ and $k'$ are exchanged, as Hermiticity demands for real functions. It also equals the integral that both sides share, $4\pi\hbar^2\int r^2f'g'\,dr = 4\pi\hbar^2kk'\cdot 2/K^3$. For $k' = k$ it is positive, as the mean of $\hat p^2$ must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={9}>

For $\hat p^4$ more care is needed, because the Laplacian of $1/r$ holds a delta function: $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r)$. Show that

<Formula label="The Laplacian applied twice to an exponential" tex="\nabla^2\nabla^2e^{-kr} = \left(-\frac{4k^3}{r} + k^4\right)e^{-kr} + 8\pi k\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

Then use it to compute $\langle e^{-k'r}|\hat p^4e^{-kr}\rangle$ and verify that $\hat p^4$ is Hermitian in this inner product: the result must equal $\langle\hat p^4e^{-k'r}|e^{-kr}\rangle$. In the box type `q` for $k'$.

Last (our addition), use your result for the ground state of hydrogen, $\psi_{100} = e^{-r/a_0}/\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}$: find $\langle\hat p^4\rangle$ in units of $\hbar^4/a_0^4$.

<Hint>

Apply the Laplacian once: $\nabla^2e^{-kr} = \left(k^2 - 2k/r\right)e^{-kr}$. In the second step, split $e^{-kr}/r$ as $1/r$ plus $\left(e^{-kr} - 1\right)/r$. The second piece is finite at the origin, so the formula for functions of $r$ can be used on it without worry.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the inner product the delta function picks out the value of $e^{-k'r}$ at the origin, which is 1. The other terms are integrals of the kind $\int_0^\infty r^ne^{-Kr}\,dr = n!/K^{n+1}$ with $K = k + k'$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For real functions, Hermitian means: the result is the same when $k$ and $k'$ are exchanged. Bring everything over the denominator $(k + k')^3$ and look.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-p4" label="$\langle e^{-k'r}|\hat p^4e^{-kr}\rangle =$" expr="8*pi*hbar^4*k*q*(k^2+3*k*q+q^2)/(k+q)^3"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2], q: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", q: "k'" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-8*pi*hbar^4*k^3*(k+2*q)/(k+q)^3", hint: "This is the result without the delta function. It changes when k and k′ are swapped, so it cannot be right for a Hermitian operator." },
    { expr: "2*hbar^4*k*q*(k^2+3*k*q+q^2)/(k+q)^3", hint: "The volume element is 4π r² dr, and the delta function term carries 8π k. A factor 4π is missing overall." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-1s" label="In $\psi_{100}$: $\langle\hat p^4\rangle\,a_0^4/\hbar^4 =$" value="5" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-1s" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The first Laplacian produces a term $1/r$. The second Laplacian acts on it and gives a delta function at the origin. In an inner product that delta function adds exactly the piece that makes the result symmetric.

<Step title="The first Laplacian">

For a function $h$ of $r$ alone, $\nabla^2h = \frac{d^2h}{dr^2} + \frac{2}{r}\frac{dh}{dr}$. With $h = e^{-kr}$:

<Formula label="The Laplacian of an exponential" tex="\nabla^2e^{-kr} = \left(k^2 - \frac{2k}{r}\right)e^{-kr}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

No delta function appears here, because $e^{-kr}$ is finite at the origin.

</Step>

<Step title="The second Laplacian">

The term $k^2e^{-kr}$ gives $k^2\left(k^2 - 2k/r\right)e^{-kr}$. For the other term write $e^{-kr}/r = 1/r + \left(e^{-kr} - 1\right)/r$. The first piece gives $-4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r)$. The second piece is finite at the origin, and the same formula gives $k^2e^{-kr}/r$ for it. So

<Formula label="The Laplacian of the screened 1/r" tex="\nabla^2\left(\frac{e^{-kr}}{r}\right) = \frac{k^2e^{-kr}}{r} - 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Adding the two terms, with the factor $-2k$ in front of the second:

<Formula label="Adding the two pieces" tex="\nabla^2\nabla^2e^{-kr} = \left(k^4 - \frac{2k^3}{r}\right)e^{-kr} - \frac{2k^3}{r}\,e^{-kr} + 8\pi k\,\delta^3(\vec r) = \left(-\frac{4k^3}{r} + k^4\right)e^{-kr} + 8\pi k\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The inner product">

$\hat p^4 = \hbar^4\nabla^2\nabla^2$. With $K = k + k'$, and the delta function giving $8\pi k\,e^{0} = 8\pi k$:

<Formula label="Matrix element of p⁴ between two exponentials" tex="\langle e^{-k'r}|\hat p^4e^{-kr}\rangle = \hbar^4\left[4\pi\left(\frac{2k^4}{K^3} - \frac{4k^3}{K^2}\right) + 8\pi k\right] = 8\pi\hbar^4\,\frac{k^4 - 2k^3K + kK^3}{K^3} = 8\pi\hbar^4\,\frac{kk'\left(k^2 + 3kk' + k'^2\right)}{(k + k')^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The last form is the same when $k$ and $k'$ are exchanged. Exchanging them is the same as letting $\hat p^4$ act on $e^{-k'r}$ instead, so $\langle e^{-k'r}|\hat p^4e^{-kr}\rangle = \langle\hat p^4e^{-k'r}|e^{-kr}\rangle$: Hermitian.

</Step>

<Step title="What the delta function repairs">

Without the delta function the result would be $-8\pi\hbar^4k^3(k + 2k')/K^3$, which is not symmetric. It differs from the right result by exactly $-8\pi k\hbar^4$. This is the boundary term of part (a), now for the functions $f = e^{-k'r}$ and $\hat p^2g$: because $\hat p^2g$ behaves like $1/r$ near the origin, $r^2f\,d(\hat p^2g)/dr$ does not vanish there. Keeping the delta function and dropping the boundary term are two ways of doing the same bookkeeping.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result, and the 1S state (our addition)">

A second route: $\hat p^2$ is Hermitian by part (a), so the inner product must equal $\langle\hat p^2e^{-k'r}|\hat p^2e^{-kr}\rangle = 4\pi\hbar^4\int_0^\infty r^2\left(k'^2 - 2k'/r\right)\left(k^2 - 2k/r\right)e^{-Kr}\,dr$. The three integrals give $4\pi\hbar^4kk'\left(2kk' + 2K^2\right)/K^3$, the same result. This is the form the lesson used for the relativistic correction.

For the ground state set $k = k' = 1/a_0$ and multiply by the square of the normalization, $1/(\pi a_0^3)$:

<Formula label="Mean of p⁴ in the ground state" tex="\langle\psi_{100}|\hat p^4|\psi_{100}\rangle = \frac{1}{\pi a_0^3}\cdot 8\pi\hbar^4\,\frac{5}{8a_0} = \frac{5\hbar^4}{a_0^4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The relativistic shift is then $-\langle\hat p^4\rangle/(8m^3c^2) = -\frac58\,\alpha^4mc^2$, because $\hbar/a_0 = \alpha mc$. The lesson's formula gives the same for $n = 1$, $l = 0$: $-\frac18\alpha^4mc^2\left(8 - 3\right)$. Without the delta function the mean of $\hat p^4$ would come out as $-3\hbar^4/a_0^4$: negative, which is impossible for the square of a Hermitian operator.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The wavefunction at the origin of spherically symmetric bound states" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

A particle of mass $m$ is in a bound state with zero angular momentum ($l = 0$) of a central potential $V(r)$. There is a surprising link between the value of the wavefunction at the origin and the mean slope of the potential:

<Formula label="A relation to be made exact" tex="|\psi(0)|^2 = C\left\langle\frac{dV}{dr}\right\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

Conventions for this problem: the state is normalized and its wavefunction is $\psi(r) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}\,\frac{u(r)}{r}$ with $u$ real, $u(0) = 0$ and $\int_0^\infty u^2\,dr = 1$. The radial equation is $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u'' + V u = E u$. Assume that $r^2V(r) \to 0$ for $r \to 0$, as holds for the Coulomb potential.

<Part label="a" points={9}>

Derive the relation and find the constant $C$. MIT's advice: multiply the radial equation by $u'(r)$ and integrate from $r = 0$ to $r = \infty$.

<Hint>

After multiplying by $u'$, two of the three terms are total derivatives: $u'u'' = \frac12\left(u'^2\right)'$ and $u\,u' = \frac12\left(u^2\right)'$. The term with $V$ needs one integration by parts.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Collect what survives at the ends. At infinity $u$ and $u'$ vanish for a bound state. At the origin $u = 0$ but $u'(0)$ is not zero. How is $u'(0)$ related to $\psi(0)$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$C =$" expr="m/(2*pi*hbar^2)"
  vars={{ m: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*m/hbar^2", hint: "That is the constant for u′(0)². The wavefunction is ψ = u/(r√(4π)), so |ψ(0)|² = u′(0)²/(4π)." },
    { expr: "m/(pi*hbar^2)", hint: "A factor 2 is off. Both u′u″ and u u′ are half of a total derivative: ½(u′²)′ and ½(u²)′." },
    { expr: "m/(4*pi*hbar^2)", hint: "A factor 2 is off. Both u′u″ and u u′ are half of a total derivative: ½(u′²)′ and ½(u²)′." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Multiplying by $u'$ turns the radial equation into total derivatives plus one term with $V'$. The total derivatives leave only $u'(0)^2$, which is $4\pi|\psi(0)|^2$.

<Step title="Multiply by u′ and integrate">

<Formula label="The radial equation times u′, integrated" tex="-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int_0^\infty u'u''\,dr + \int_0^\infty\left(V - E\right)u\,u'\,dr = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The first integral is $\frac12\left[u'^2\right]_0^\infty = -\frac12u'(0)^2$. The second, by parts, is $\frac12\left[(V - E)u^2\right]_0^\infty - \frac12\int_0^\infty V'u^2\,dr$. Its boundary term vanishes: at infinity $u \to 0$, and near the origin $u \approx u'(0)\,r$, so $V u^2$ behaves like $r^2V$, which goes to zero by assumption.

</Step>

<Step title="Read off the relation">

What is left is $\frac{\hbar^2}{4m}u'(0)^2 = \frac12\int_0^\infty V'u^2\,dr$. For an $l = 0$ state the mean of a function of $r$ is $\int_0^\infty(\dots)\,u^2\,dr$, so the integral is $\langle dV/dr\rangle$. Near the origin $u/r \to u'(0)$, so $\psi(0) = u'(0)/\sqrt{4\pi}$ and

<Formula label="The wavefunction at the origin from the slope of the potential" tex="|\psi(0)|^2 = \frac{u'(0)^2}{4\pi} = \frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\left\langle\frac{dV}{dr}\right\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $dV/dr$ is an energy per length, and $m/\hbar^2$ is 1/(energy × length²), so the right side is 1/length³, a probability density. A test on another potential (our addition): the ground state of the three-dimensional oscillator $V = \frac12m\omega^2r^2$ is $\psi = \left(m\omega/\pi\hbar\right)^{3/4}e^{-m\omega r^2/2\hbar}$, with $\langle r\rangle = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}$. The relation gives $\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\,m\omega^2\langle r\rangle = \left(m\omega/\pi\hbar\right)^{3/2}$, which is indeed $|\psi(0)|^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Use the relation to find $|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2$ for the $nS$ states of hydrogen, where $V = -e^2/r$. You may use $\langle 1/r^2\rangle = 1/\left(n^3a_0^2\left(l + \frac12\right)\right)$, which is the lesson's $\langle V^2\rangle$ divided by $e^4$. Give the result in units of $1/a_0^3$, and verify it for $n = 1$ with $\psi_{100} = e^{-r/a_0}/\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}$.

<Hint>

What is $dV/dr$ for the Coulomb potential? Its mean needs only the quoted $\langle 1/r^2\rangle$, with $l = 0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

To clean up, use $a_0 = \hbar^2/(me^2)$: the combination $me^2/\hbar^2$ is $1/a_0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-n" label="$a_0^3\,|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2 =$" expr="1/(pi*n^3)"
  vars={{ n: [1, 6] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/(2*pi*n^3)", hint: "Check ⟨1/r²⟩: for l = 0 the factor 1/(l + ½) is 2, not 1." },
    { expr: "2/(pi*n^3)", hint: "A factor 2 too many. The constant of part (a) has 2π in its denominator, and ⟨1/r²⟩ brings one factor 2 for l = 0." },
    { expr: "1/(pi*n^2)", hint: "⟨1/r²⟩ falls off as 1/n³, not as 1/n²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For the Coulomb potential the slope is $e^2/r^2$, so the relation needs only $\langle 1/r^2\rangle$.

<Step title="Apply the relation">

$dV/dr = e^2/r^2$ and, for $l = 0$, $\langle 1/r^2\rangle = 2/(n^3a_0^2)$ (the lesson's $\langle V^2\rangle$, notes eq. (2.4.45), divided by $e^4$). Then

<Formula label="Hydrogen's S wavefunctions at the origin" tex="|\psi_{n00}(0)|^2 = \frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\cdot\frac{2e^2}{n^3a_0^2} = \frac{me^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{1}{\pi n^3a_0^2} = \frac{1}{\pi n^3a_0^3}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.4.23), here derived from the relation of part (a)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $n = 1$ this is $1/(\pi a_0^3)$, and the ground state $e^{-r/a_0}/\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}$ has exactly this square at $r = 0$. The result is the one the lesson used for the Darwin term. It falls as $1/n^3$: a higher S state is spread over a larger volume, so less of it sits at the proton.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Numerical estimates" points={5} mit="Problem 3">

**The idea.** A set is a CSCO if its operators commute and its eigenvalues fix the eigenvalues of a known CSCO (or simply relabel its states).

<Step title="Set (i): a CSCO">

$\vec L\cdot\vec S = \frac12\left(\vec J^2 - \vec L^2 - \frac34\hbar^2\right)$ is built from operators of the coupled set, so everything commutes. On $|n\,l\,j\,m_j\rangle$ its eigenvalue is

<Formula label="Eigenvalues of L·S" tex="\vec L\cdot\vec S = \frac{\hbar^2}{2}\left[j(j + 1) - l(l + 1) - \tfrac34\right] = \begin{cases}\ \ \frac{\hbar^2}{2}\,l & j = l + \frac12\\[2pt] -\frac{\hbar^2}{2}\,(l + 1) & j = l - \frac12\end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="eq. For a given $l$ the two values differ (and for $l = 0$ only $j = \frac12$ exists). So $l$ and the eigenvalue of $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ fix $j$: the set carries the same information as $\{H_0, \vec L^2, \vec J^2, J_z\}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Set (ii): a CSCO">

$S_x$ acts on the spin only, so it commutes with $H_0$, $\vec L^2$ and $L_z$. The common eigenstates are $|n\,l\,m_l\rangle$ times an eigenstate of $S_x$, one state for every list of eigenvalues. Nothing singles out the $z$ axis for the spin: this is the uncoupled set with the spin measured along $x$.

</Step>

<Step title="Set (iii): a CSCO">

$J_z = L_z + S_z$ commutes with $S_z$ and with the rest. The eigenvalues of $J_z$ and $S_z$ give that of $L_z = J_z - S_z$, so the set carries the same information as $\{H_0, \vec L^2, L_z, S_z\}$. The states are the uncoupled ones, labelled by $m_j = m_l + m_s$ and $m_s$.

</Step>

<Step title="Set (iv): not a CSCO">

$S_z$ does not commute with $\vec J^2 = \vec L^2 + \vec S^2 + 2\vec L\cdot\vec S$:

<Formula label="The spin component does not commute with J²" tex="\left[\vec J^2,\ S_z\right] = 2\left[L_xS_x + L_yS_y,\ S_z\right] = 2i\hbar\left(L_yS_x - L_xS_y\right) \neq 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Set (v): a CSCO">

</Step>

<Step title="Step 1: the mean of J²">

<Formula label="Mean of J² in an uncoupled state" tex="\langle\vec J^2\rangle = \hbar^2\left[l(l + 1) + \tfrac34 + 2m_lm_s\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b)(i), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a degenerate level the expectation values are the right shifts if the basis is "good": the perturbation is diagonal inside every degenerate subspace. The uncoupled basis is good here, because $\vec L^2$ and $J_z$ commute with the fine structure and tell all degenerate states apart.

<Step title="The degeneracies of n = 2">

The values of $m_l + 2m_s$ are: for $2S$ ($m_l = 0$), $+1$ and $-1$; for $2P$, with $m_s = +\frac12$: $2, 1, 0$ and with $m_s = -\frac12$: $0, -1, -2$. So the eight states have five energies, $m_l + 2m_s = 2, 1, 0, -1, -2$, with $1, 2, 2, 2, 1$ states. The degenerate pairs are:

- at $+1$: $2S$ with $m_s = \frac12$, and $2P$ with $m_l = 0$, $m_s = \frac12$ (different $l$);
- at $-1$: the same two with $m_s = -\frac12$ (different $l$);
- at $0$: $2P$ with $m_l = 1$, $m_s = -\frac12$, and $2P$ with $m_l = -1$, $m_s = \frac12$ (same $l$, but $m_j = +\frac12$ and $-\frac12$).

</Step>

<Step title="Why the uncoupled basis is good">

The fine structure commutes with $\vec L^2$ and with $J_z$: the form $H_\text{fs}$ above is a function of $\vec J^2$, and the true terms (relativistic, spin–orbit, Darwin) commute with $\vec L^2$ and with every component of $\vec J$, as the lesson found. Every uncoupled state is an eigenstate of $\vec L^2$ and of $J_z = L_z + S_z$, with $m_j = m_l + m_s$. If an operator $A$ commutes with $H_\text{fs}$ and two states have different eigenvalues $a_1 \neq a_2$ of $A$, then

<Formula label="No mixing between states that a commuting operator tells apart" tex="0 = \langle 1|\left[A,\ H_\text{fs}\right]|2\rangle = \left(a_1 - a_2\right)\langle 1|H_\text{fs}|2\rangle \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle 1|H_\text{fs}|2\rangle = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b)(ii), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Now take two different uncoupled states with the same $n$ and the same $m_l + 2m_s$. If they also had the same $l$ and the same $m_l + m_s$, then $m_s$ (the difference of the two sums) and $m_l$ would be equal too, and the states would be the same. So two different degenerate states always differ in $l$ or in $m_j$, and $H_\text{fs}$ has no matrix element between them.

</Step>

<Step title="Conclusion and check">

Inside every degenerate subspace the matrix of $H_\text{fs}$ is diagonal in the uncoupled basis. Its diagonal entries, the expectation values of part (i), are therefore the first-order shifts, and the uncoupled states are the right zeroth-order states. For $n = 2$ this can be seen directly in the list above: the pairs at $\pm1$ differ in $l$, the pair at $0$ in $m_j$. Note that part (i) itself was not affected: the probabilities $P_+$ and $P_-$ are properties of one state and need no perturbation theory.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Identities with vector operators" points={20} mit="Problem 5">

Let $\hat J_i$ ($i = 1, 2, 3$) be the components of an angular momentum $\vec J$. Three operators $\hat W_i$ form a vector operator $\vec W$ under $\vec J$ if

<Formula label="Definition of a vector operator" tex="\left[\hat J_i,\ \hat W_j\right] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,\hat W_k" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3" />

with a sum over the repeated index $k$. The angular momentum $\vec J$ is itself a vector operator. These identities are the ones behind the projection lemma of the lesson. Hats are dropped below.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Take $\vec J$ to be the orbital angular momentum $\vec L = \vec x\times\vec p$. Show that the position $\vec x$ is a vector operator.

<Hint>

Write $L_i = \epsilon_{iab}\,x_ap_b$ and use $[x_a, x_j] = 0$ and $[p_b, x_j] = -i\hbar\,\delta_{bj}$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only the momentum inside $\vec L$ fails to commute with $x_j$, and it leaves a delta that removes one index.

<Step title="The commutator">

<Formula label="Position is a vector operator under L" tex="\left[L_i,\ x_j\right] = \epsilon_{iab}\,x_a\left[p_b,\ x_j\right] = -i\hbar\,\epsilon_{iaj}\,x_a = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ija}\,x_a" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The last step exchanges two indices of $\epsilon$, which changes the sign. This is the defining relation with $W = x$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

One case by hand: $[L_z, x] = [xp_y - yp_x,\ x] = -y\,[p_x, x] = i\hbar\,y$. The formula gives the same: for $i = z$ and $j = x$ the only term is $k = y$, and the Levi-Civita symbol is $+1$ for the cyclic order $z, x, y$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Show that if $\vec U$ and $\vec V$ are vector operators, then so is their cross product $\vec U\times\vec V$, with components $\left(\vec U\times\vec V\right)_j = \epsilon_{jab}\,U_aV_b$. The components of $\vec U$ and $\vec V$ need not commute with each other, so keep their order.

<Hint>

Use $[J_i, U_aV_b] = [J_i, U_a]V_b + U_a[J_i, V_b]$. Each term then holds a product of two $\epsilon$ symbols.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\epsilon_{abc}\,\epsilon_{ade} = \delta_{bd}\,\delta_{ce} - \delta_{be}\,\delta_{cd}$. Work out both sides of the relation you want and compare.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compute $[J_i, (\vec U\times\vec V)_j]$ with the product rule, and show it equals $i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}(\vec U\times\vec V)_k$. Both reduce to $i\hbar\left(U_iV_j - U_jV_i\right)$.

<Step title="The left side">

<Formula label="Commutator of J with a cross product" tex="\left[J_i,\ \epsilon_{jab}U_aV_b\right] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{jab}\left(\epsilon_{iac}\,U_cV_b + \epsilon_{ibc}\,U_aV_c\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

In the first term $\epsilon_{jab}\,\epsilon_{iac} = \delta_{ji}\,\delta_{bc} - \delta_{jc}\,\delta_{bi}$, which gives $\delta_{ij}\,\vec U\cdot\vec V - U_jV_i$. In the second $\epsilon_{jab}\,\epsilon_{ibc} = \delta_{jc}\,\delta_{ai} - \delta_{ji}\,\delta_{ac}$, which gives $U_iV_j - \delta_{ij}\,\vec U\cdot\vec V$. The terms with $\vec U\cdot\vec V$ cancel, and the left side is $i\hbar\left(U_iV_j - U_jV_i\right)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The right side">

<Formula label="What a vector operator must give" tex="i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\left(\vec U\times\vec V\right)_k = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{kab}\,U_aV_b = i\hbar\left(\delta_{ia}\,\delta_{jb} - \delta_{ib}\,\delta_{ja}\right)U_aV_b = i\hbar\left(U_iV_j - U_jV_i\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The two sides agree, so $\vec U\times\vec V$ is a vector operator. The order of $U$ and $V$ was never changed.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

An example: $\vec x$ and $\vec p$ are vector operators under $\vec L$, so $\vec L = \vec x\times\vec p$ must be one too. It is: $[L_i, L_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}L_k$ is the algebra of angular momentum. The same steps show that $\vec U\cdot\vec V$ commutes with every $J_i$: it is a scalar.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Show that for a vector operator $\vec W$

<Formula label="Commutator of J² with a vector operator" tex="\left[\vec J^2,\ \vec W\right] = 2i\hbar\left(\vec W\times\vec J - i\hbar\,\vec W\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3" />

and check the formula for $\vec W = \vec J$. For that check you need $\vec J\times\vec J$ (the choice box is our addition).

<Hint>

$\vec J^2 = J_iJ_i$, and $[J_iJ_i, W_j] = J_i[J_i, W_j] + [J_i, W_j]J_i$. This gives $W$ on both sides of $J$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Move every $J$ to the right of $W$ with the defining commutator. The extra term holds two $\epsilon$ symbols with two indices summed: $\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{ikl} = -2\,\delta_{jl}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-jj" label="$\vec J\times\vec J =$" choices={["0, as for any vector crossed with itself", "−iħ J", "iħ J", "2iħ J"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Expand the commutator with $J_iJ_i$, then put all factors in the order "W, then J".

<Step title="Expand">

<Formula label="First step of the commutator with J²" tex="\left[J_iJ_i,\ W_j\right] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\left(J_iW_k + W_kJ_i\right) = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\left(2W_kJ_i + i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ikl}W_l\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

using $J_iW_k = W_kJ_i + i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ikl}W_l$.

</Step>

<Step title="Recognize the pieces">

$\epsilon_{ijk}W_kJ_i = \epsilon_{jki}W_kJ_i = \left(\vec W\times\vec J\right)_j$, and $\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{ikl} = -2\,\delta_{jl}$. So

<Formula label="The commutator, assembled" tex="\left[\vec J^2,\ W_j\right] = 2i\hbar\left(\vec W\times\vec J\right)_j + (i\hbar)^2(-2)\,W_j = 2i\hbar\left(\vec W\times\vec J - i\hbar\,\vec W\right)_j" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check with W = J">

$\left(\vec J\times\vec J\right)_k = \epsilon_{kab}J_aJ_b = \frac12\epsilon_{kab}[J_a, J_b] = \frac12\,i\hbar\,\epsilon_{kab}\,\epsilon_{abc}J_c = i\hbar\,J_k$. Operators that do not commute can have a nonzero cross product with themselves. The bracket is then $i\hbar\vec J - i\hbar\vec J = 0$, in agreement with $[\vec J^2, \vec J] = 0$. A second test: $\vec W = \vec x$ under $\vec L$, acting on a function $f(r)$ of $r$ alone, for which $\vec Lf = 0$. The left side gives $\vec L^2\left(zf\right) = 2\hbar^2zf$, because $zf$ has $l = 1$. The right side gives $2i\hbar(0 - i\hbar\,z)f = 2\hbar^2zf$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={6}>

Show that for a vector operator $\vec V$

<Formula label="The double commutator" tex="\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\vec J^2,\left[\vec J^2,\ \vec V\right]\right] = \left(\vec V\cdot\vec J\right)\vec J - \frac12\left(\vec J^2\,\vec V + \vec V\,\vec J^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 4" />

and find the constant. In this part only, $\alpha$ is that constant and not the fine-structure constant.

Then put the identity to work (our addition). Between states with the same $j$ its left side has no expectation value, which is the projection lemma of the lesson. Use the lemma's result for $\vec V = \vec S$ to find $\langle S_z\rangle/\hbar$ in the state $2P_{1/2}$ with $m_j = +\frac12$.

<Hint>

Use part (c) twice. After the first use you need $[\vec J^2, \vec V\times\vec J]$, and by part (b) $\vec V\times\vec J$ is again a vector operator.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You will meet $\left(\vec V\times\vec J\right)\times\vec J$. In components it is $V_kJ_iJ_k - V_iJ^2$. Move $J_k$ next to $V_k$ with $[J_i, J_k]$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

What is left over, $i\hbar\,\vec V\times\vec J + \hbar^2\vec V$, can be traded for $\frac12\left(\vec J^2\vec V - \vec V\vec J^2\right)$ with part (c).

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the number: the lesson's result is $\langle S_z\rangle = \hbar m_j\,\dfrac{j(j + 1) - l(l + 1) + \frac34}{2j(j + 1)}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-alpha" label="$\alpha =$" expr="-4*hbar^2"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "4*hbar^2", hint: "Each use of part (c) brings a factor 2iħ, and i² = −1." },
    { expr: "-2*hbar^2", hint: "Part (c) is used twice, and each time it brings a factor 2iħ." },
    { expr: "-hbar^2", hint: "Part (c) is used twice, and each time it brings a factor 2iħ, not iħ." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p5-sz" label="$2P_{1/2}$, $m_j = +\tfrac12$: $\langle S_z\rangle/\hbar =$" value="-0.1667" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-sz" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Part (c) turns each commutator with $\vec J^2$ into a cross product with $\vec J$. Two cross products with $\vec J$ give "the part of $\vec V$ along $\vec J$" minus $\vec V\,J^2$, up to terms that part (c) removes.

<Step title="Use part (c) twice">

With $\left[\vec J^2, \vec V\right] = 2i\hbar\left(\vec V\times\vec J - i\hbar\vec V\right)$, and the same formula for the vector operator $\vec V\times\vec J$:

<Formula label="The double commutator after two uses of part (c)" tex="\left[\vec J^2,\left[\vec J^2,\ \vec V\right]\right] = (2i\hbar)^2\left[\left(\vec V\times\vec J\right)\times\vec J - 2i\hbar\,\vec V\times\vec J - \hbar^2\,\vec V\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The double cross product">

$\left[\left(\vec V\times\vec J\right)\times\vec J\right]_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{jab}\,V_aJ_bJ_k = V_kJ_iJ_k - V_iJ^2$. With $J_iJ_k = J_kJ_i + i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ikl}J_l$:

<Formula label="The double cross product" tex="\left(\vec V\times\vec J\right)\times\vec J = \left(\vec V\cdot\vec J\right)\vec J + i\hbar\,\vec V\times\vec J - \vec V\,\vec J^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Assemble">

The square bracket becomes $\left(\vec V\cdot\vec J\right)\vec J - \vec V\vec J^2 - \left(i\hbar\,\vec V\times\vec J + \hbar^2\vec V\right)$. By part (c), $i\hbar\,\vec V\times\vec J + \hbar^2\vec V = \frac12\left[\vec J^2, \vec V\right] = \frac12\left(\vec J^2\vec V - \vec V\vec J^2\right)$. So

<Formula label="The double commutator, with its constant" tex="\left[\vec J^2,\left[\vec J^2,\ \vec V\right]\right] = (2i\hbar)^2\left[\left(\vec V\cdot\vec J\right)\vec J - \frac12\left(\vec J^2\,\vec V + \vec V\,\vec J^2\right)\right], \qquad \alpha = (2i\hbar)^2 = -4\hbar^2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.5.12), here derived" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the constant">

Take matrix elements between states of different $j$ and $j'$ where $\vec V$ has a nonzero element $v$. On the left each commutator gives a factor $\hbar^2\left[j(j + 1) - j'(j' + 1)\right]$. On the right the first term drops out, because $\vec V\cdot\vec J$ is a scalar and cannot change $j$, and the rest gives $-\frac12\hbar^2\left[j(j + 1) + j'(j' + 1)\right]v$. For $j' = j + 1$ the difference is $-2(j + 1)$ and the sum is $2(j + 1)^2$, so $\hbar^4\cdot 4(j + 1)^2 = -\alpha\,\hbar^2(j + 1)^2$: again $\alpha = -4\hbar^2$, for every $j$.

</Step>

<Step title="The projection lemma at work (our addition)">

In a state $|j\,m_j\rangle$ the left side has zero expectation value ($\vec J^2$ acts as the same number on both sides), so $\langle\vec V\rangle = \langle(\vec V\cdot\vec J)\vec J\rangle/\left(\hbar^2j(j + 1)\right)$. For $\vec V = \vec S$ this is the lesson's formula (notes eqs. (2.5.17) to (2.5.19)). With $l = 1$, $j = \frac12$, $m_j = \frac12$:

<Formula label="Mean spin component in the state 2P½ with m_j = +½" tex="\frac{\langle S_z\rangle}{\hbar} = m_j\,\frac{j(j + 1) - l(l + 1) + \frac34}{2j(j + 1)} = \frac12\cdot\frac{\frac34 - 2 + \frac34}{\frac32} = -\frac16 = -0.1667" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (2.5.17)–(2.5.19), evaluated for l = 1, j = 1/2" />

The spin points mostly against the total angular momentum. This agrees with the probabilities of Problem 4: the state is $\frac23$ of $|m_l = 1, m_s = -\frac12\rangle$ and $\frac13$ of $|m_l = 0, m_s = +\frac12\rangle$, and $\frac23\left(-\frac12\right) + \frac13\left(\frac12\right) = -\frac16$.

</Step>

</Solution>
