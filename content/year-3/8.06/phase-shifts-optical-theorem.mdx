The last lecture wrote the incident plane wave as a sum of partial waves, one for each angular momentum $l$. This one shows what a central potential does to each of them: far away it can only shift the phase of the outgoing part. These **phase shifts** $\delta_l$ give the scattering amplitude and the total cross section in a few lines, and with them comes, almost for free, the **optical theorem**. The lecture ends by finding every phase shift of a hard sphere (L20.1–L20.5 transcripts; notes section 7.2, pp. 138–142). (MIT's material is Zwiebach's recorded lecture, in five short videos, and his lecture notes, chapter 7; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Where the last lecture left off

Zwiebach starts with a recap (L20.1 transcript pp. 1–3). Far from the target, at distances $r \gg a$ beyond the range $a$ of the potential, the scattering energy eigenstate with $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ is an incident plane wave plus an outgoing spherical wave, $e^{ikz} + f(\theta)\,e^{ikr}/r$. It is only the leading form far away, not an exact solution, and not valid near the target. Its amplitude gives the differential cross section, $d\sigma = |f(\theta)|^2\,d\Omega$, which is what detectors measure, so $f$ is what we are after. (The notes give $f$ a subscript $k$ for the wavenumber; this page leaves it out, as in the last lecture.) For a central potential nothing depends on the azimuthal angle, since the incoming beam picks out the $z$ axis and the target looks the same from every side of it.

Where the potential vanishes, $r > a$, the general solution with no dependence on the azimuthal angle is a sum of terms $\left(A_l\,j_l(kr) + B_l\,n_l(kr)\right)Y_{l0}(\theta)$ (L20.1 transcript p. 2). It cannot hold down to $r = 0$, since $n_l$ diverges there. The plane wave itself contains only the regular functions, $e^{ikz} = \sqrt{4\pi}\sum_l\sqrt{2l+1}\,i^l\,Y_{l0}(\theta)\,j_l(kr)$, and for large argument $j_l$ and $n_l$ fall off as one over the argument times a sine or a cosine shifted by $l\pi/2$. With these ingredients the task is to compute $f$ (L20.1 transcript pp. 2–3).

## Partial waves far away

Expanding each $j_l$ for large $kr$ shows the waves inside the plane wave (L20.2 transcript p. 1; notes p. 138):

<Formula label="Spherical Bessel function far away" tex="j_l(kr) \simeq \frac{1}{kr}\,\sin\left(kr - \frac{l\pi}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{2ik}\left(\frac{e^{i(kr - l\pi/2)}}{r} - \frac{e^{-i(kr - l\pi/2)}}{r}\right)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.10), p. 138" />

So, for $r \gg a$ (L20.2 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="The plane wave far away, as partial waves" tex="e^{ikz} \simeq \frac{\sqrt{4\pi}}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{2l+1}\;i^l\,Y_{l0}(\theta)\,\frac{1}{2i}\left(\frac{e^{i(kr - l\pi/2)}}{r} - \frac{e^{-i(kr - l\pi/2)}}{r}\right)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.11), p. 138" />

The $1/k$ in front comes from the $1/kr$ of the expansion. Each exact expansion has become an approximate one, valid far away. With the time factor $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, the first wave moves outwards and the second inwards: every partial wave is an **outgoing** minus an **incoming** spherical wave (L20.2 transcript p. 1).

Zwiebach stresses a point that matters later (L20.2 transcript pp. 1–2). The sum is not an approximate solution because many values of $l$ conspire: each term on its own is one. Keep only the term with $l = 50$, say: on its own it is an exact solution, and far away it looks like its own term in the sum. More than that, the Schrödinger equation does not mix incoming and outgoing waves far away, so the outgoing wave of a term alone, or the incoming wave alone, is also an approximate solution there. That independence of the different $l$ is why they are called **partial waves**.

## A one-dimensional analogy

To see what the potential must do, Zwiebach goes back to scattering in one dimension, as done in 8.04 (L20.2 transcript pp. 2–3). Put a hard wall at $x = 0$ and a potential that vanishes beyond $x = a$. Without the potential the energy eigenstate must vanish at the wall, so it is a sine (L20.2 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="One dimension, no potential" tex="\phi(x) = \sin kx = \frac{1}{2i}\left(e^{ikx} - e^{-ikx}\right)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.12), p. 138" />

Here $x > 0$ plays the role of $r$: $e^{-ikx}$ moves towards the wall, so it is the incoming wave, and $e^{ikx}$ the outgoing one. This $\phi$ is the analogue of $e^{ikz}$, a solution with no potential that contains both.

With the potential, the scattering solution must have the **same incoming wave**, since that is what we send in (L20.2 transcript p. 3). What comes out is not under our control, and finding it is the scattering problem. It must have the same energy, and everything sent in comes back off the wall, so the outgoing wave must carry the same probability current as the incoming one: the same magnitude. The only freedom left is a phase, written by convention as $e^{2i\delta}$, with $\delta$ the **phase shift**, which depends on $k$ (L20.2 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="One dimension, with the potential" tex="\psi(x) = \frac{1}{2i}\left(e^{ikx}\,e^{2i\delta} - e^{-ikx}\right), \qquad x > a" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.13), p. 138" />

This holds only outside the potential, $x > a$; inside, the solution depends on the details. Defining the scattered wave as what the potential adds (L20.2 transcript p. 3),

<Formula label="Full wave = reference wave + scattered wave" tex="\psi(x) = \phi(x) + \psi_s(x)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.14), p. 138" />

the incoming waves cancel in the difference, and the scattered wave is purely outgoing (L20.2 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The one-dimensional scattered wave" tex="\psi_s(x) = \frac{1}{2i}\left(e^{2i\delta} - 1\right)e^{ikx} = e^{i\delta}\sin\delta\;e^{ikx}" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

The last step factors out $e^{i\delta}$, leaving $(e^{i\delta} - e^{-i\delta})/2i = \sin\delta$. This is exactly the structure of the three-dimensional problem: $e^{ikz}$, like $\phi$, contains incoming and outgoing waves, and the scattered wave $f\,e^{ikr}/r$ is purely outgoing (L20.2 transcript p. 3).

## Phase shifts in three dimensions

Now repeat the argument for $\psi$ (L20.3 transcript p. 1). Far away (L20.3 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="The scattering solution far away" tex="\psi(\vec r) \simeq e^{ikz} + f(\theta)\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}, \qquad r \gg a" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.15), p. 138" />

Put the partial-wave form of $e^{ikz}$ on the right. The only incoming waves on the right are those of $e^{ikz}$, since the scattered wave is outgoing, so the full solution must contain exactly those incoming partial waves. For the outgoing ones Zwiebach uses the intuition from one dimension (L20.3 transcript pp. 1–2): each $l$ is a separate scattering problem with one wave in and one wave out; a central potential does not mix different $l$; and the outgoing wave must have the same amplitude as the incoming one, or probability would pile up. So it can differ only by a phase, one for each $l$:

<Formula label="The phase-shift ansatz" tex="\psi(\vec r) \simeq \frac{\sqrt{4\pi}}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{2l+1}\;i^l\,Y_{l0}(\theta)\,\frac{1}{2i}\left(\frac{e^{i(kr - l\pi/2)}\,e^{2i\delta_l}}{r} - \frac{e^{-i(kr - l\pi/2)}}{r}\right)" cite="reading6" at="p. 139" />

[In the lecture the factor $1/2i$ is first written as one over $2\pi$, and a student corrects it (L20.3 transcript pp. 1–2).] The picture changes: instead of a plane wave going in and a spherical wave coming out, think of infinitely many partial waves, $l = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$, each scattering on its own (L20.3 transcript p. 2). We have parametrised our ignorance physically, Zwiebach says: everything about the scattering is in the real numbers $\delta_l(k)$. One dimension needed a single phase shift, because at each energy there were only two plane waves, in and out; in three dimensions each energy is infinitely degenerate, and there is one phase shift for each $l$.

## The scattering amplitude

Set the ansatz equal to $e^{ikz} + f\,e^{ikr}/r$ (L20.3 transcript p. 3). The incoming waves cancel, and moving the outgoing waves of $e^{ikz}$ to the other side leaves

<Formula label="The scattered wave in partial waves" tex="f(\theta)\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{r} = \frac{\sqrt{4\pi}}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{2l+1}\;i^l\,Y_{l0}(\theta)\,\frac{e^{2i\delta_l} - 1}{2i}\,e^{-il\pi/2}\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.16), p. 139" />

Two small facts simplify it (L20.3 transcript p. 3; notes p. 139):

<Formula label="Two simplifications" tex="\frac{e^{2i\delta_l} - 1}{2i} = e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l, \qquad e^{-il\pi/2} = (-i)^l, \qquad i^l\,(-i)^l = 1" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (7.2.16)–(7.2.17), p. 139" />

The first is the one-dimensional step again. After that the factor $e^{ikr}/r$ is the same on both sides and cancels, and the scattering amplitude is (L20.3 transcript p. 3)

<Formula label="The scattering amplitude from phase shifts" tex="f(\theta) = \frac{\sqrt{4\pi}}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{2l+1}\;Y_{l0}(\theta)\,e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.18), p. 139" />

To know $f$, and hence the cross section, one must know the phase shifts (L20.3 transcript p. 3). Since $\sqrt{4\pi}\sqrt{2l+1}\,Y_{l0} = (2l+1)P_l(\cos\theta)$, the same formula reads $f(\theta) = \frac1k\sum_l(2l+1)\,e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l\,P_l(\cos\theta)$, which is handy for numbers.

## The total cross section

The total cross section integrates $|f|^2 = f^*f$ over directions (L20.4 transcript p. 1; notes p. 139). Two sums appear, over $l$ and $l'$:

<Formula label="The total cross section as a double sum" tex="\sigma = \int |f(\theta)|^2\,d\Omega = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_{l,l'}\sqrt{2l+1}\,\sqrt{2l'+1}\;e^{-i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l\;e^{i\delta_{l'}}\sin\delta_{l'}\int Y_{l0}^*\,Y_{l'0}\,d\Omega" cite="reading6" at="p. 139" />

The spherical harmonics are orthonormal, so the integral is 1 for $l' = l$ and 0 otherwise. Then the phases cancel, the square roots multiply to $2l+1$, and (L20.4 transcript p. 1)

<Formula label="Total cross section from phase shifts" tex="\sigma = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.20), p. 139" />

A very nice formula for an observable, Zwiebach says. The total cross section is a sum of **partial cross sections**, one from each partial wave, $\sigma_l = (4\pi/k^2)(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$ (L20.4 transcript p. 1). The differential cross section is different: without the integral, the products of different $l$ remain, and the partial waves **interfere** in $d\sigma/d\Omega$ but not in $\sigma$. Phases are often said not to matter; relative phases like these matter a lot. (Since $\sin^2\delta_l \leq 1$, each partial wave can contribute at most $(4\pi/k^2)(2l+1)$, a bound the lecture does not discuss.)

## The optical theorem

There is a famous constraint on the scattering amplitude, the **optical theorem**, which Zwiebach introduces with a picture from optics (L20.4 transcript pp. 1–2). Shine light on a sphere: behind it is a shadow. The light missing from the shadow is the light the sphere scattered. So what happens in the forward direction knows how much was scattered in all directions. The details are subtler, because in the forward direction the incident wave and the scattered wave overlap and interfere. The usual proof uses conservation of probability flux; Zwiebach mentions it, perhaps for a problem, but in this lecture the theorem simply falls out of the phase-shift formulas.

For the forward amplitude, $\theta = 0$, we need the spherical harmonics there (L20.4 transcript p. 2; notes p. 139):

<Formula label="Spherical harmonics in the forward direction" tex="Y_{l0}(\theta) = \sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}}\,P_l(\cos\theta), \qquad P_l(1) = 1, \qquad Y_{l0}(0) = \sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}}" cite="reading6" at="p. 139" />

Every Legendre polynomial is defined to equal 1 at argument 1. The square roots in $f$ then combine (L20.4 transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="The forward scattering amplitude" tex="f(0) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\,e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l" cite="reading6" at="p. 140" />

Its imaginary part replaces $e^{i\delta_l}$ by $\sin\delta_l$, and the sum is the one in $\sigma$ (L20.4 transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Imaginary part of the forward amplitude" tex="\text{Im}\,f(0) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l = \frac{k}{4\pi}\,\sigma" cite="reading4" at="p. 3" />

[The notes, eq. (7.2.21), p. 140, keep a stray factor $e^{i\delta_l}$ in the middle expression; the imaginary part has none, as the lecture says.] That is the theorem (L20.4 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The optical theorem" tex="\sigma = \frac{4\pi}{k}\,\text{Im}\,f(0)" cite="reading6" at="p. 140" />

The total cross section is fixed by the imaginary part of the forward amplitude. Zwiebach notes that it was first found in optics, from the physics of shadows (L20.4 transcript p. 3): the forward direction carries the information about everything lost from the beam. Why the imaginary part, and why these factors, comes from how the incoming and the scattered waves interfere in the forward direction, which needs a careful look, but the intuition is simple (L20.4 transcript p. 3).

## Three ways to see a phase shift

Phase shifts are useful because they can be calculated (L20.5 transcript p. 1). The general method takes two steps and is finished in the next lecture; here is how to recognise a phase shift. Take a single partial wave outside the potential (L20.5 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="One partial wave outside the potential" tex="\psi_l(\vec r) = \left(A_l\,j_l(kr) + B_l\,n_l(kr)\right)Y_{l0}(\theta), \qquad r > a" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.22), p. 140" />

The term with $B_l$ is the **signal of scattering**. With no potential the solution holds down to $r = 0$, where $n_l$ diverges, so $B_l = 0$; a nonzero $B_l$ says that the solution cannot be continued inwards, because something happens there (L20.5 transcript p. 1; notes p. 140). For large $kr$ (L20.5 transcript p. 1),

<Formula label="The partial wave far away" tex="\psi_l \simeq \left(\frac{A_l}{kr}\sin\left(kr - \frac{l\pi}{2}\right) - \frac{B_l}{kr}\cos\left(kr - \frac{l\pi}{2}\right)\right)Y_{l0}(\theta)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.23), p. 140" />

and Zwiebach's claim is that the phase shift is simply (L20.5 transcript p. 1)

<Formula label="The phase shift from the coefficients" tex="\tan\delta_l = -\frac{B_l}{A_l}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.24), p. 140" />

To check it, take out $A_l$, write $\tan\delta_l = \sin\delta_l/\cos\delta_l$, and use the addition formula for the sine (L20.5 transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="A single shifted sine" tex="\psi_l \simeq \frac{A_l}{kr}\left(\sin\left(kr - \frac{l\pi}{2}\right) + \tan\delta_l\cos\left(kr - \frac{l\pi}{2}\right)\right)Y_{l0}(\theta) = \frac{A_l}{kr\cos\delta_l}\,\sin\left(kr - \frac{l\pi}{2} + \delta_l\right)Y_{l0}(\theta)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.25), p. 140" />

Writing that sine as exponentials and taking out a factor $e^{-i\delta_l}$ (L20.5 transcript pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Back to the in and out waves" tex="\sin\left(kr - \frac{l\pi}{2} + \delta_l\right) = \frac{e^{-i\delta_l}}{2i}\left(e^{i(kr - l\pi/2 + 2\delta_l)} - e^{-i(kr - l\pi/2)}\right)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.26), p. 140" />

Up to a constant these are the waves of the ansatz: the outgoing wave carries $e^{2i\delta_l}$, the incoming one nothing. So the $\delta_l$ defined by the coefficients is the same phase shift. There are now **three ways** to recognise it (L20.5 transcript p. 2):

1. in the partial-wave expansion, as the factor $e^{2i\delta_l}$ on the outgoing wave;
2. in the wave far away, which goes like $\sin(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta_l)$, a sine shifted by $\delta_l$ from the free one (notes eq. (7.2.27), p. 141);
3. in the solution outside the potential, as $\tan\delta_l = -B_l/A_l$.

The notes add an aside not in the lecture (notes eqs. (7.2.28)–(7.2.29), p. 141): choosing $A_l = -iB_l$ gives a purely outgoing partial wave, and $A_l = iB_l$ a purely incoming one. Each is an acceptable solution far away, but neither is a scattering solution, which needs both.

## The hard sphere

An example shows that the method really works (L20.5 transcript p. 2). Take a **hard sphere** of radius $a$:

<Formula label="Hard-sphere potential" tex="V(r) = \infty \quad (r \leq a), \qquad V(r) = 0 \quad (r > a)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.30), p. 141" />

It may look hard; it is easy (L20.5 transcript pp. 2–3). Outside, every radial solution is a combination of the free ones, and the full wavefunction a sum of them over $l$, with $P_l(\cos\theta)$ in place of $Y_{l0}$, which differs only by a constant:

<Formula label="The solution outside the sphere" tex="R_l(r) = A_l\,j_l(kr) + B_l\,n_l(kr), \qquad \psi(r, \theta) = \sum_l R_l(r)\,P_l(\cos\theta)" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.31), p. 141; L20.5 transcript pp. 2–3" />

The sphere is infinitely hard, so the wavefunction must vanish on it: $\psi(a, \theta) = 0$ for every $\theta$ (L20.5 transcript p. 3). That is one equation for infinitely many unknowns, but the $P_l$ are linearly independent (complete), so a sum of numbers times $P_l$ vanishes only if every number does:

<Formula label="The boundary condition, wave by wave" tex="A_l\,j_l(ka) + B_l\,n_l(ka) = 0 \qquad \text{for every } l" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.33), p. 141" />

and therefore (L20.5 transcript p. 3)

<Formula label="Hard-sphere phase shifts" tex="\tan\delta_l = -\frac{B_l}{A_l} = \frac{j_l(ka)}{n_l(ka)}" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (7.2.34)–(7.2.35), p. 141" />

"Done", in Zwiebach's word: all the phase shifts are known, since the Bessel functions can be computed to any accuracy, and with them the differential and total cross sections. [In the transcript the minus sign is dropped once when this is read out; the result is as written.] For $l = 0$, with $j_0(x) = \sin x/x$ and $n_0(x) = -\cos x/x$, this gives $\tan\delta_0 = -\tan ka$, so $\delta_0 = -ka$: far away the s wave is $\sin(kr - ka) = \sin(k(r - a))$, the free wave pushed out so that it vanishes at the surface. (This check is not in the lecture.)

For the cross section we need $\sin^2\delta_l$ (L20.5 transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="sin²δ from tan δ" tex="\sin^2\delta_l = \frac{\tan^2\delta_l}{1 + \tan^2\delta_l} = \frac{j_l^2(ka)}{j_l^2(ka) + n_l^2(ka)}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.36), p. 142" />

so that (L20.5 transcript pp. 3–4)

<Formula label="Hard-sphere cross section" tex="\sigma = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}(2l+1)\,\frac{j_l^2(ka)}{j_l^2(ka) + n_l^2(ka)}" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.37), p. 142" />

[When the lecture reads this out, the prefactor is said as $4\pi/k$; it is $4\pi/k^2$, as written on the board earlier.] This formula is interesting at every energy, and so is its angular dependence. At low energy, $ka \ll 1$ (long wavelength), $j_l$ stays finite while $n_l$ blows up and dominates the denominator, and expanding both gives (L20.5 transcript p. 4)

<Formula label="Hard sphere at low energy" tex="\sigma \simeq \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2l+1}\left(\frac{2^l\,l!}{(2l)!}\right)^4(ka)^{4l+2}, \qquad ka \ll 1" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.38), p. 142" />

[The notes attribute this expansion to Griffiths. Spoken in the lecture, the fourth power of the bracket is lost, but it is right. For small $ka$, $j_l$ is $(ka)^l$ divided by the product of the odd numbers up to $2l+1$, and $n_l$ is minus the product of the odd numbers up to $2l-1$ divided by $(ka)^{l+1}$. So $\sin^2\delta_l \simeq \tan^2\delta_l$ is $(ka)^{4l+2}$ divided by the square of the two products, and since the product of the odd numbers up to $2l-1$ equals $(2l)!/(2^l\,l!)$, the factor $2l+1$ in $\sigma$ turns this into $\frac{1}{2l+1}$ times the fourth power of the bracket.] The powers grow quickly with $l$, so for $ka \ll 1$ the series converges fast and $l = 0$ dominates, with all the factorials equal to 1 (L20.5 transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="The low-energy limit" tex="\sigma \simeq \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\,(ka)^2 = 4\pi a^2" cite="reading6" at="eq. (7.2.39), p. 142" />

A cute answer, Zwiebach says: not the geometric cross section $\pi a^2$ that the sphere shows to the beam, but four times that, the **whole surface area** of the sphere. [In the transcript the apparent cross section is called "the diameter"; it is the area $\pi a^2$.] One tries to picture it as a wave so long that it wraps around the sphere. More on this comes in the next lecture.

<Formula label="The phase factor of the hard sphere" tex="e^{2i\delta} = \frac{1 + i\tan\delta}{1 - i\tan\delta}, \qquad e^{2i\delta_l} = \frac{n_l(ka) + i\,j_l(ka)}{n_l(ka) - i\,j_l(ka)}" cite="reading6" at="eqs. (7.2.40)–(7.2.41), p. 142" />

<WorkedExample title="Two partial waves">
<Problem>
At some energy a short-range central potential has phase shifts $\delta_0 = 40°$ and $\delta_1 = 15°$, with all higher ones negligible, at $k = 0.5$ fm⁻¹. (a) Find the partial cross sections and the total cross section. (b) Find the forward amplitude $f(0)$ and check the optical theorem. (c) Find $d\sigma/d\Omega$ at $\theta = 0$, $90°$ and $180°$, and compare with what one would get by adding the two partial waves' contributions without interference. (d) Show that the interference does not change $\sigma$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Partial cross sections">
The prefactor is $4\pi/k^2 = 4\pi/0.25$ fm², about 50.27 fm². With $\sin^2 40° = 0.4132$, the s wave gives $\sigma_0$ of about 20.77 fm². With $\sin^2 15° = 0.06699$ and the factor $2l + 1 = 3$, the p wave gives $\sigma_1$ of about 10.10 fm². The total is about 30.87 fm², or 0.309 b.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Forward amplitude and the optical theorem">
With $P_l$, $f(\theta) = \frac1k\left(e^{i\delta_0}\sin\delta_0 + 3\,e^{i\delta_1}\sin\delta_1\cos\theta\right)$. Here $e^{i\delta_0}\sin\delta_0 = \sin\delta_0\cos\delta_0 + i\sin^2\delta_0 = 0.4924 + 0.4132i$ and $3\,e^{i\delta_1}\sin\delta_1 = 0.75 + 0.2010i$. At $\theta = 0$ they add, and dividing by $k$ doubles them: $f(0) = (2.485 + 1.228i)$ fm. Then $(4\pi/k)\,\text{Im}\,f(0) = 25.13$ fm $\times\ 1.228$ fm, which is again 30.87 fm², the total cross section from (a).
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Interference in the angular distribution">
At $\theta = 0$, $|f(0)|^2$ is about 7.68 fm² per steradian. At $90°$ only the s wave is left: $\sin^2\delta_0/k^2 = 1.65$ fm² per steradian. At $180°$ the p wave changes sign, $f = (-0.515 + 0.424i)$ fm, and $|f|^2$ is only about 0.446 fm² per steradian. Forward scattering is about 17 times as likely per unit solid angle as backward scattering. Without interference, $(\sin^2\delta_0 + 9\sin^2\delta_1\cos^2\theta)/k^2$ would give about 4.06 fm² per steradian at both $0$ and $180°$: the forward–backward difference is entirely interference.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) No interference in σ">
With $u = \cos\theta$, $|f|^2$ contains the cross term $\frac{2}{k^2}\,\text{Re}\left(e^{-i\delta_0}\sin\delta_0 \cdot 3\,e^{i\delta_1}\sin\delta_1\right)u$, which is odd in $u$. Over the whole sphere, $d\Omega = 2\pi\,du$ from $u = -1$ to 1, it integrates to zero, and only the squares survive: $2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}(\sin^2\delta_0 + 9\sin^2\delta_1\,u^2)\,du/k^2 = (4\pi/k^2)(\sin^2\delta_0 + 3\sin^2\delta_1)$, the result of (a).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Slow collisions of dark matter">
Ferreras's textbook describes **self-interacting dark matter**, in which dark matter particles have a small cross section for scattering off each other, one proposal for the small-scale problems of cold dark matter. When the particles collide slowly ($ka \ll 1$) and the force is short-range and contact-like, away from a resonance, the s wave dominates, as for the hard sphere, and the cross section tends to a constant, independent of the speed. Models in which the force is carried by a light particle give instead a cross section that depends on the speed. Partial waves and phase shifts, as in this lecture, are a standard way to compute such cross sections.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Far away, each partial wave of $e^{ikz}$ is an outgoing minus an incoming spherical wave, and each term, even each in or out wave on its own, is an approximate solution there: the partial waves scatter independently.
- In one dimension, with a hard wall, the potential can only multiply the outgoing wave by $e^{2i\delta}$, so the scattered wave is $e^{i\delta}\sin\delta\,e^{ikx}$. In three dimensions each $l$ behaves the same way: same incoming wave, outgoing wave times $e^{2i\delta_l}$, one real **phase shift** per $l$.
- Matching to $e^{ikz} + f\,e^{ikr}/r$ gives $f(\theta) = \frac{\sqrt{4\pi}}{k}\sum_l\sqrt{2l+1}\,Y_{l0}(\theta)\,e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l$, and the total cross section $\sigma = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_l(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$: partial waves interfere in $d\sigma/d\Omega$ but not in $\sigma$.
- With $Y_{l0}(0) = \sqrt{(2l+1)/4\pi}$, $\text{Im}\,f(0) = \frac1k\sum_l(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$, which gives the **optical theorem**, $\sigma = \frac{4\pi}{k}\,\text{Im}\,f(0)$.
- A phase shift shows up three ways: $e^{2i\delta_l}$ on the outgoing wave, a far-away $\sin(kr - l\pi/2 + \delta_l)$, and $\tan\delta_l = -B_l/A_l$ for the solution $A_l\,j_l + B_l\,n_l$ outside the potential.
- **Hard sphere**: $\psi = 0$ at $r = a$ gives $\tan\delta_l = j_l(ka)/n_l(ka)$, so $\delta_0 = -ka$; at low energy $\sigma \simeq 4\pi a^2$, four times the geometric cross section.
