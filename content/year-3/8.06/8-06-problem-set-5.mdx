## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Tunnelling and the Stark effect" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

The Stark effect is the physics of an atom in an electric field. A field does more than shift the levels: it lets the electron of a bound state tunnel out, so the state is no longer stable. This problem studies that in one dimension. An electron of mass $m$ sits in a square well of depth $V_0$ and width $d$:

<Formula label="The square well" tex="V(x) = -V_0 \ \text{ for } |x| < \tfrac{d}{2}, \qquad V(x) = 0 \ \text{ for } |x| \ge \tfrac{d}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

A weak, constant electric field of strength $\mathcal{E}$ along $x$ is then switched on. It changes the potential energy to

<Formula label="The well in the field" tex="V(x) \;\rightarrow\; V(x) - e\,\mathcal{E}\,x" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Take $e\mathcal{E}$ positive, so that the potential falls towards positive $x$. Throughout, assume

<Formula label="The two assumptions" tex="e\,\mathcal{E}\,d \;\ll\; \frac{\hbar^2}{2md^2} \;\ll\; V_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Two names are used below. $E_1$ is the energy of the ground state above the bottom of the well, which part (a) estimates. $W \equiv V_0 - E_1$ is the binding energy (our notation), the energy needed to free the electron: measured from the top of the well, the zero of $V$, the state lies at $E = -W$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

In this part there is no field. Estimate the energy of the ground state above the bottom of the well, $E_1$, by pretending that the well is infinitely deep (the second assumption makes that a fair estimate). Use this estimate in the rest of the problem. The true ground state energy is lower than the estimate: why?

<Hint>

In an infinitely deep well of width $d$ the ground state is half a wavelength of a sine. What is its wavenumber?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the last question: where is the true wavefunction at the walls of a well of finite depth, and what does that do to its wavelength inside?

</Hint>

  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], d: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[

<Answer id="p1-lower" label="The true energy is lower because the true wavefunction" choices={["has a node in the middle of the well, which the estimate leaves out", "reaches into the walls, so its wavelength inside is longer", "is pushed to one side of the well by the walls", "has a larger potential energy and a smaller kinetic energy inside the well"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With walls of infinite height the wavefunction must vanish at the walls, which fixes its wavelength.

<Step title="The infinite well">

The ground state is $\cos(\pi x/d)$, with wavenumber $k = \pi/d$ and zeros at $x = \pm d/2$. Its kinetic energy is the energy above the bottom:

<Formula label="Estimate of the ground state energy above the bottom of the well" tex="E_1 = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2md^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Measured from the top of the well, the energy of the state is $E = -V_0 + E_1 = -W$.

</Step>

<Step title="Why the true energy is lower">

In a well of finite depth the wavefunction does not vanish at the walls. It leaks into the forbidden regions as a decaying exponential, and joins smoothly to it. So less than half a wavelength fits between the walls: the wavelength inside is longer, the wavenumber smaller than $\pi/d$, and the kinetic energy smaller. In other words, the electron has more room, and more room means less energy.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units: $\hbar^2/(md^2)$ is (J s)² / (kg m²) = J. The estimate is $\pi^2$ times the scale $\hbar^2/2md^2$ of the assumptions, so "$\hbar^2/2md^2 \ll V_0$" must hold with room to spare for the estimate to be good: see part (e), where $E_1$ comes out near half of $V_0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Sketch the potential with the field on, and explain why the ground state of the well without the field is no longer stable.

<Hint>

The field adds a straight line with a negative slope to the well. Draw the level $E = -W$ across the picture. Where is the electron allowed, classically?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-escape" label="With the field on, the electron can leave the well" choices={["to the left, where the potential rises without limit", "over the top of the right wall, which the field has lowered below the level", "to the right, by tunnelling through a barrier of finite width", "in neither direction: it only shifts a little to the right"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Far to the right the tilted potential drops below the energy of the state. Between the well and that region lies a barrier of finite width, and a quantum particle can tunnel through such a barrier.

<Step title="The sketch">

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-3, 7]} range={[-1.5, 0.6]} xLabel="x / d" yLabel="V / V₀"
  curves={[
    { expr: "(abs(x) < 0.5 ? -1 : 0) - 0.1*x", label: "V" },
    { expr: "-0.5", label: "level", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="Computed from the formula V(x) − eℰx above, in units of the well's depth and width, with the slope eℰd = 0.1 V₀ (far steeper than the problem allows, so that the tilt can be seen) and a level at −0.5 V₀ (dashed). The level crosses the falling line at x = 5 d: beyond that point the electron is free." />

To the left of the well the potential rises for ever, so nothing escapes that way. To the right of the well the potential is $-e\mathcal{E}x$, a line that falls. It crosses the level $E = -W$ at the point

<Formula label="The outer turning point" tex="-e\,\mathcal{E}\,x_{\text{out}} = -W \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_{\text{out}} = \frac{W}{e\,\mathcal{E}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Why the state is unstable">

Between $x = d/2$ and $x_{\text{out}}$ the potential is above the level: a classically forbidden region, a barrier in the shape of a triangle. Beyond $x_{\text{out}}$ the electron is allowed again, with a kinetic energy that grows without limit as it runs off to the right. Without the field the barrier is infinitely wide and the state is a true bound state. With the field the barrier has a finite width, so the wavefunction has a small tail beyond it, and the electron leaks out by tunnelling. The potential now has no lowest energy at all, and the old ground state is at best a long-lived state.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

When the field goes to zero, $x_{\text{out}} = W/e\mathcal{E}$ goes to infinity: the barrier becomes infinitely wide and the state stable again, as it should.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Use the semiclassical approximation to find the barrier penetration factor (the tunnelling probability) $T$ for the ground state. Use $e\mathcal{E}d \ll \hbar^2/2md^2$ to simplify the result. In the box, type the binding energy as `W` and give $-\ln T$ to leading order in the small field.

<Hint>

The lesson's formula is $T \simeq \exp\left(-2\int\kappa\,dx\right)$ over the forbidden region, with $\hbar\kappa(x) = \sqrt{2m\left(V(x) - E\right)}$. Here the region runs from the right wall of the well to the outer turning point of part (b).

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the barrier $V(x) - E = W - e\mathcal{E}x$. Substitute $u = W - e\mathcal{E}x$ to do the integral.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The lower limit gives $W - e\mathcal{E}d/2$. Compare $e\mathcal{E}d$ with $W$, using both assumptions.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-lnt" label="$-\ln T =$" expr="4*sqrt(2*m)*W^(3/2)/(3*hbar*e*Ef)"
  vars={{ m: [0.5, 2], W: [1, 3], hbar: [0.5, 2], e: [0.5, 2], Ef: [0.1, 0.4], d: [0.5, 1] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", Ef: "\\mathcal{E}" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*sqrt(2*m)*W^(3/2)/(3*hbar*e*Ef)", hint: "That is the integral of κ itself. The probability is the square of the amplitude: T = exp(−2∫κ dx)." },
    { expr: "4*sqrt(2*m)*(W - e*Ef*d/2)^(3/2)/(3*hbar*e*Ef)", hint: "That is the integral before the simplification. The problem asks you to use the weak field: eℰd/2 is tiny next to W, so drop it." },
    { expr: "2*sqrt(2*m)*W^(3/2)/(hbar*e*Ef)", hint: "That treats the barrier as a rectangle of height W. The barrier is a triangle: its height falls in a straight line to zero, which gives a factor 2/3." },
    { expr: "4*sqrt(2*m*W)/(3*hbar*e*Ef)", hint: "Check the units: −ln T is a pure number. The integral of the root of u is (2/3)u^(3/2)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The tunnelling probability is the exponential of minus twice the integral of $\kappa$ through the barrier, and the barrier here is a triangle.

<Step title="The integral through the barrier">

With the lesson's formula and $V(x) - E = W - e\mathcal{E}x$ between $x = d/2$ and $x_{\text{out}} = W/e\mathcal{E}$:

<Formula label="WKB tunnelling probability for the well in the field" tex="T \simeq \exp\left(-\frac{2}{\hbar}\int_{d/2}^{W/e\mathcal{E}}\sqrt{2m\left(W - e\,\mathcal{E}\,x\right)}\,dx\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (3.5.11), for the triangular barrier" />

With $u = W - e\mathcal{E}x$ the integral is $\frac{1}{e\mathcal{E}}\int_0^{W - e\mathcal{E}d/2}\sqrt{2mu}\,du$:

<Formula label="The exponent before the simplification" tex="-\ln T = \frac{4\sqrt{2m}}{3\,\hbar\,e\,\mathcal{E}}\left(W - \frac{e\,\mathcal{E}\,d}{2}\right)^{3/2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Using the weak field">

The two assumptions together give $e\mathcal{E}d \ll V_0$, and $W$ is of the size of $V_0$. So $e\mathcal{E}d/2$ can be dropped next to $W$:

<Formula label="Barrier penetration factor" tex="T \simeq \exp\left(-\frac{4\sqrt{2m}\;W^{3/2}}{3\,\hbar\,e\,\mathcal{E}}\right), \qquad W = V_0 - \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2md^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The same assumption has been used twice more without saying so. The field changes the potential across the well by only $e\mathcal{E}d$, far less than the spacing of the levels, so the energy of the state is still $-W$ (its shift vanishes at first order, because the ground state is even and $x$ is odd). And the wavefunction inside the well is still that of part (a).

</Step>

<Step title="How good is the simplification?">

Expanding the bracket, $\left(W - e\mathcal{E}d/2\right)^{3/2} \approx W^{3/2} - \frac34W^{1/2}e\mathcal{E}d$. The second term changes $-\ln T$ by $-d\sqrt{2mW}/\hbar$, which does not depend on the field. Relative to the exponent itself that is tiny (of the size $e\mathcal{E}d/W$). In $T$ it is a constant factor $\exp\left(d\sqrt{2mW}/\hbar\right)$ (our addition), about 28 for the numbers of part (e). The WKB formula does not give the factor in front of the exponential anyway (the lesson says so), so a constant factor of this kind is beyond the accuracy of the estimate.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units: $\sqrt{2mW}$ is a momentum and $W/e\mathcal{E}$ a length, so $\sqrt{m}\,W^{3/2}/(\hbar e\mathcal{E})$ is a momentum times a length divided by $\hbar$, a pure number. The exponent is $\frac23$ of the value $2\sqrt{2mW}\,x_{\text{out}}/\hbar$ for a rectangular barrier of height $W$ and width $x_{\text{out}}$, as it must be for a barrier that falls from $W$ to zero. A weaker field makes the barrier wider and $T$ smaller.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Use classical arguments to turn the barrier penetration factor into an estimate of the lifetime $\tau$ of the bound state. (The lesson leaves this step to section 3.5 of the notes: the first hint gives the idea.) In the box, give the time $\Delta t$ between two hits of the electron on the right wall.

<Hint>

Picture the electron as a classical particle that bounces between the walls. Each time it hits the right wall it has the chance $T$ to get out. How many hits per second?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Inside the well the kinetic energy is $E_1$ of part (a). That gives the speed.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-dt" label="$\Delta t =$" expr="2*m*d^2/(pi*hbar)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], d: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*d^2/(pi*hbar)", hint: "That is the time to cross the well once. Between two hits on the right wall the electron goes to the left wall and back." },
    { expr: "2*m*d^2/hbar", hint: "The speed follows from the kinetic energy E₁ of part (a), which holds a factor π²: the speed is πħ/(md)." },
    { expr: "4*m*d^2/(pi*hbar)", hint: "The well has width d, so the way to the other wall and back is 2d, not 4d." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The lifetime is the time between two hits on the barrier, divided by the chance to get through at each hit.

<Step title="Rate and lifetime">

If the electron escapes at a constant rate $B$ per unit time, the probability that it is still in the well falls as $e^{-Bt}$, so the lifetime is $\tau = 1/B$. The rate is the number of hits on the barrier per unit time, multiplied by $T$:

<Formula label="Lifetime from the tunnelling probability" tex="\tau = \frac{1}{B}, \qquad B = \frac{T}{\Delta t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tau = \frac{\Delta t}{T}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (3.5.24), (3.5.25), (3.5.27)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The bounce time">

Inside the well the kinetic energy is $E_1 = \frac12mv^2$, so the speed is $v = \sqrt{2E_1/m} = \pi\hbar/(md)$. The way from the right wall to the left wall and back is $2d$:

<Formula label="Time between two hits on the right wall" tex="\Delta t = \frac{2d}{v} = \frac{2md^2}{\pi\hbar}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (3.5.26), with a constant speed inside the well" />

</Step>

<Step title="The lifetime">

<Formula label="Lifetime of the bound state in the field" tex="\tau \simeq \frac{2md^2}{\pi\hbar}\,\exp\left(\frac{4\sqrt{2m}\;W^{3/2}}{3\,\hbar\,e\,\mathcal{E}}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units are carried by $\Delta t$: $md^2/\hbar$ is kg m² / (J s) = s. In other words, $\Delta t = \pi\hbar/E_1$: the bounce time is half the period $2\pi\hbar/E_1$ that belongs to the energy $E_1$. A weaker field gives a longer lifetime, and without a field the lifetime is infinite.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="f" points={2}>

Show that the lifetime goes like $\exp(1/\mathcal{E})$. Explain why this means that the instability could not be found at any finite order of perturbation theory, with the field as a perturbation of the Hamiltonian.

<Hint>

Perturbation theory gives a series in powers of $\mathcal{E}$. Take the decay rate $1/\tau \propto \exp(-c/\mathcal{E})$ and ask for its Taylor series around $\mathcal{E} = 0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-series" label="Every coefficient of the Taylor series of $\exp(-c/\mathcal{E})$ around $\mathcal{E} = 0$ (approached from positive fields) is" choices={["infinite", "equal to 1", "zero", "a power of c"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A function such as $e^{-c/\mathcal{E}}$ goes to zero faster than every power of $\mathcal{E}$. A power series cannot see it.

<Step title="The dependence on the field">

In the result of part (d) the field appears only in the exponent:

<Formula label="The lifetime as a function of the field" tex="\tau \simeq \Delta t\,\exp\left(\frac{c}{\mathcal{E}}\right), \qquad c = \frac{4\sqrt{2m}\;W^{3/2}}{3\,\hbar\,e}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(f), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

with $\Delta t$ and $c$ independent of the field. The decay rate is $1/\tau \propto e^{-c/\mathcal{E}}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Why perturbation theory misses it">

Perturbation theory in the field gives the energy as a power series, $E(\mathcal{E}) = E^{(0)} + \mathcal{E}E^{(1)} + \mathcal{E}^2E^{(2)} + \dots$, with real coefficients: at every order the state is still a stationary state with a real energy, shifted and polarized but stable. The decay rate would have to appear as a term in such a series. But every derivative of $e^{-c/\mathcal{E}}$ is a polynomial in $1/\mathcal{E}$ times $e^{-c/\mathcal{E}}$, and the exponential wins: all derivatives go to zero as $\mathcal{E} \to 0$ from above. So the Taylor series of the rate around $\mathcal{E} = 0$ is zero term by term. The instability is invisible at every finite order. It is a non-perturbative effect.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

There is a second sign that the series cannot be the whole story. For a field of the other sign, $-\mathcal{E}$, the same physics holds (the electron leaves to the left), yet $e^{-c/\mathcal{E}}$ would blow up: the function is not analytic at $\mathcal{E} = 0$. And however weak the field, far away the term $-e\mathcal{E}x$ is not small, so the perturbation is never small everywhere.

</Step>

</Solution>

The growth with $t^2$ is the lesson's result for a transition between two discrete states of equal energy. A rate, with a probability that grows like $t$, appears only after a sum over a continuum of final states.

</Step>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A Gaussian pulse" points={10} mit="Problem 4">

A Hamiltonian $H_0$ has the states and energies $H_0|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$ for $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$. The perturbation is a pulse with the shape of a Gaussian:

<Formula label="The Gaussian pulse" tex="\delta H(t) = \frac{\exp\left(-\frac{t^2}{2\tau^2}\right)}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau^2}}\;\hat V" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 3" />

<Part label="a" points={6}>

The system starts in the state $|0\rangle$ at $t = -\infty$. With first-order time-dependent perturbation theory, find the probability that it is in the state $|n\rangle$ (with $n \neq 0$) at $t = +\infty$. In the box, type `Vn0` for $|V_{n0}|$ and `w` for $\omega_{n0} = (E_n - E_0)/\hbar$.

**The idea.** The amplitude is the Fourier transform of the pulse at the frequency of the transition, and the Fourier transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian.

<Step title="The amplitude">

With $c_0(-\infty) = 1$ and $\delta H_{n0}(t) = V_{n0}\,e^{-t^2/2\tau^2}/\sqrt{2\pi\tau^2}$:

<Formula label="First-order amplitude for the Gaussian pulse" tex="c_n^{(1)}(+\infty) = \frac{V_{n0}}{i\hbar}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-t^2/2\tau^2}}{\sqrt{2\pi\tau^2}}\,e^{i\omega_{n0}t}\,dt = \frac{V_{n0}}{i\hbar}\,e^{-\omega_{n0}^2\tau^2/2}" cite="reading2" at="eq. The integral follows from completing the square, as in the second hint. Without the phase it is 1.

</Step>

The units: the pulse shape has the units of 1/time, so $\hat V$ is an energy times a time, and $|V_{n0}|/\hbar$ is a pure number, as a probability needs. </Step>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

What happens in the limits $\tau \to 0$ and $\tau \to \infty$? Take a state $|n\rangle$ with $E_n \neq E_0$.

<Hint>

What does the pulse look like in each limit? Its area is 1 for every $\tau$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-fast" label="For $\tau \to 0$ the probability" choices={["goes to zero for every state, because the pulse has no time to act", "becomes |Vₙ₀|²/ħ², whatever the energy of the state", "grows without limit", "becomes |Vₙ₀|²/(Eₙ − E₀)²"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p4-slow" label="For $\tau \to \infty$ the probability" choices={["goes to zero very fast", "becomes |Vₙ₀|²/ħ²", "goes to one half", "falls slowly, as 1/τ²"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A pulse much shorter than the periods of the system acts as a sudden kick that reaches all states. A pulse much longer than those periods changes the system so gently that it makes no jumps.

<Step title="A short pulse">

For $\tau \to 0$ the exponential $\exp(-\omega_{n0}^2\tau^2)$ goes to 1:

<Formula label="The limit of a sudden kick" tex="\tau \to 0: \qquad \delta H(t) \to \delta(t)\,\hat V, \qquad P_n \to \frac{|V_{n0}|^2}{\hbar^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The pulse becomes a delta function, which holds all frequencies equally. So the energy of the final state does not matter: every state that $\hat V$ links to $|0\rangle$ is reached, however far away in energy.

</Step>

<Step title="A long pulse">

For $\tau \to \infty$ and $E_n \neq E_0$:

<Formula label="The limit of a slow pulse" tex="\tau \to \infty: \qquad P_n = \frac{|V_{n0}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,e^{-\omega_{n0}^2\tau^2} \to 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The pulse is switched on and off so slowly that it holds almost no frequencies as high as $\omega_{n0}$. The system follows the slow change and comes back to $|0\rangle$: no transitions. The fall is faster than any power of $1/\tau$. Only a state with the same energy as $|0\rangle$ ($\omega_{n0} = 0$) keeps the probability $|V_{n0}|^2/\hbar^2$ for every $\tau$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The border between the two limits is $\omega_{n0}\tau \approx 1$: the width of the pulse against the period of the transition. A pulse of width $\tau$ holds frequencies up to about $1/\tau$, in line with the uncertainty relation between time and energy: it can cause transitions with $|E_n - E_0|$ up to about $\hbar/\tau$. The short limit agrees with Problem 2(b): for a delta kick on two states the exact probability is $\sin^2(|V_{n0}|/\hbar)$, and its first term is $|V_{n0}|^2/\hbar^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Answer id="p5-mode" label="Light is absorbed by" choices={["the first mode only, at the frequency ω₁", "the second mode only, at the frequency ω₂", "both modes, each at its own frequency", "neither mode: the molecule has no net charge"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The dipole moment is a multiple of one normal mode displacement. Only the mode that moves the charges against each other couples to a uniform field.

<Step title="The dipole in normal modes">

The combination $-x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3$ is $-\sqrt6$ times $y_1 = (x_1 - 2x_2 + x_3)/\sqrt6$:

<Formula label="The dipole moment as a normal mode displacement" tex="\vec d = -\sqrt6\,q\,y_1\,\hat x" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(e), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="With ladder operators">

Adding the two operators of part (d), $a_1 + a_1^\dagger = \sqrt{2m\omega_1/\hbar}\;y_1$, so $y_1 = \sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega_1)}\left(a_1 + a_1^\dagger\right)$:

<Formula label="The dipole moment with ladder operators" tex="\vec d = -q\,\sqrt{\frac{3\hbar}{m\omega_1}}\left(a_1 + a_1^\dagger\right)\hat x, \qquad \omega_1 = \sqrt{\frac{3k}{m}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(e), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

The operators $a_2$, $a_2^\dagger$ and $\pi_3$ do not appear.

</Step>

<Step title="Which mode absorbs light">

A field along $x$ that oscillates in time adds the perturbation $\delta H(t) = -\vec d\cdot\vec{\mathcal{E}}(t)$, a harmonic perturbation in the sense of the lesson. Its matrix elements between the states $|n_1, n_2, \pi_3\rangle$ are those of $a_1 + a_1^\dagger$: they change $n_1$ by one and leave $n_2$ and $\pi_3$ alone. So only the first mode, the asymmetric stretch, absorbs light: one quantum $\hbar\omega_1$ at a time, from light of frequency near $\omega_1$. The second mode, the symmetric stretch, does not couple to the field at all.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The reason can be seen in the motion. In the second mode the two oxygen atoms move by equal amounts in opposite directions and the carbon atom rests: the charges $-q$ shift by $+s$ and $-s$, and the dipole moment does not change. In the first mode the positive carbon atom moves against the two negative oxygen atoms, so the dipole moment swings back and forth. A shift of the whole molecule ($y_3$) does not change the dipole moment either, because the total charge $-q + 2q - q$ is zero: the light does not push the molecule as a whole. The units: $\sqrt{\hbar/(m\omega_1)}$ is a length, so $D$ is a charge times a length. One remark (ours): the model is a line, so it has no bending. The real molecule can also bend, and that motion changes the dipole moment across the axis.

</Step>
