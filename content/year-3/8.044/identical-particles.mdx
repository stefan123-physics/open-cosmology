Up to now the course has treated material particles classically, or as collections of distinguishable units (only the radiation modes of Lecture 15 were quantum). This lecture sets out what quantum mechanics demands of **identical** particles, and why it forces a new kind of statistics (slides 1–19). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## One particle

A particle's Hamiltonian depends on its position $\vec r$, momentum $\vec p$ and spin $\vec s$, and its energy eigenfunctions $\psi_n(\vec r, \vec s)$ carry a state index $n$ that may have several parts; for the electron in hydrogen, the index stands for the four quantum numbers $n$, $l$, $m_l$ and $m_s$ (slide 1). Often $\psi_n$ factors into a spatial part times a spin part (slide 2). Two spatial parts met before: a one-dimensional oscillator's, a Gaussian times a Hermite polynomial, and a free particle's plane wave $e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}$.

Spin is an angular momentum: for spin magnitude $S$ there are $2S + 1$ values of $m_S$. For $S = \frac12$ the two eigenfunctions of the $z$ component have eigenvalues $\pm\hbar/2$ (slide 3). A spin state need not be one of these (it can be a combination with coefficients $1/\sqrt2$), and a wavefunction need not factor at all: $f(x)$ times spin up plus $g(x)$ times spin down is a perfectly good state (slide 4).

## Many distinguishable particles

Label particle $i$'s space and spin variables together as $i$. For $N$ non-interacting particles in the same potential, the Hamiltonian is a sum of copies of one single-particle Hamiltonian, each with its own particle's variables (slide 5). Every particle has the same single-particle states available, each may be in a different one, and the system's eigenfunctions are products (slide 6; here $a, b, \dots, z$ stand for the single-particle states that particles $1, 2, \dots, N$ occupy, which the slides write as $n_1, n_2, \dots, n_N$):

<Formula label="Product states" tex="\Psi(1, 2, \dots, N) = \psi_a(1)\,\psi_b(2)\cdots\psi_z(N), \qquad E = E_a + E_b + \dots + E_z" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

With pairwise interactions added, these products are no longer eigenfunctions, but the true eigenfunctions can still be written as combinations of them (slide 7).

## Identical particles

Define the exchange operator $P_{ij}$, which swaps the variables of particles $i$ and $j$ (slide 8). Swapping twice restores everything, $P_{ij}^2 = 1$, so its eigenvalues are $+1$ and $-1$, with symmetric and antisymmetric eigenfunctions.

**Identical** means that no physical operation can tell particle $i$ from particle $j$: every physical operator commutes with $P_{ij}$ (slide 9). So the energy eigenfunctions must also be eigenfunctions of exchange, either symmetric or antisymmetric, and two states that differ only by swapping two particles' coordinates are the same state (slide 10). Relativistic quantum mechanics settles which:

- **integer spin**: symmetric, **bosons**;
- **half-integer spin**: antisymmetric, **fermions**.

**Composite particles** (slides 11–13). A nucleus or atom built of electrons, protons and neutrons, each with spin $\frac12$, behaves as a single particle as long as its internal states are not excited. Exchanging two such composites exchanges all their constituents, one sign per constituent: an even number of constituents gives a boson (and integer spin), an odd number a fermion (half-integer spin). So hydrogen (one proton, one electron) is a boson, deuterium a fermion, helium-4 a boson but helium-3 a fermion, lithium-7 a boson and lithium-6 a fermion; diatomic molecules of identical atoms such as H₂ are bosons.

## Building symmetric and antisymmetric states

A simple product $\psi_\alpha(1)\psi_\beta(2)$ has no exchange symmetry. Instead, sum over all permutations (slide 14):

<Formula label="Symmetric state of two particles" tex="\Psi^{(+)}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\psi_\alpha(1)\psi_\beta(2) + \psi_\beta(1)\psi_\alpha(2)\right]" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

(normalized as written when $\alpha \ne \beta$), and for $N$ bosons, the sum of all $N!$ permutations times $1/\sqrt{N!}$ and, for each state $\alpha$ occupied $n_\alpha$ times, one over the square root of $n_\alpha$ factorial. (The slides write the single-particle states as $\alpha(1)$; this page writes $\psi_\alpha(1)$.) The antisymmetric version changes the sign of every odd permutation, which is exactly the rule for a **determinant** (slide 15):

<Formula label="Antisymmetric state of two particles" tex="\Psi^{(-)}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\psi_\alpha(1)\psi_\beta(2) - \psi_\beta(1)\psi_\alpha(2)\right]" cite="mit" at="slide 15" />

For $N$ fermions, $\Psi^{(-)}_N$ is $1/\sqrt{N!}$ times the determinant whose rows are the particles and whose columns are the occupied single-particle states. Two facts follow at once (slide 16):

- If two particles are put in the same single-particle state, two columns are equal and $\Psi^{(-)}_N = 0$: the **Pauli principle**.
- If two particles have the same position and spin, two rows are equal and $\Psi^{(-)}_N = 0$: identical fermions avoid each other.

## Occupation numbers

Since swapping particles changes nothing, a many-particle state is specified completely by saying how many particles occupy each single-particle state $\alpha$: the **occupation numbers** $\{n_\alpha\}$, each between $0$ and $N$, with $\sum n_\alpha = N$ (slides 16–17). For fermions each $n_\alpha$ is 0 or 1, as in $(1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, \dots)$; for bosons any number is allowed, as in $(2, 0, 1, 3, 6, 1, \dots)$. The energy is $E = \sum_\alpha\epsilon_\alpha n_\alpha$. Atomic configurations are written this way: neon is $(1S)^2(2S)^2(2P)^6$, sodium adds $(3S)^1$, and excited helium is $(1S)^1(2S)^1$.

## Trying the canonical ensemble

With occupation numbers the partition function is (slide 18)

<Formula label="Canonical sum over occupation numbers" tex="Z(N, V, T) = \sum_{\text{states}}e^{-E(\text{state})/kT} = \sum_{\{n_\alpha\}}\exp\left(-\frac{\sum_\alpha\epsilon_\alpha n_\alpha}{kT}\right)" cite="mit" at="slide 18" />

where the sum runs only over sets with $\sum n_\alpha = N$ (the slides mark this restriction with a prime). The exponential is a product of one factor per state, but because the occupation numbers are tied together the sum over them cannot be swapped with that product, so the sum **cannot be carried out** state by state. The cure, in the next lecture, is to let $N$ vary too.

**At zero temperature** (slide 19) the system is in its lowest total energy. Bosons put all $N$ particles into the lowest single-particle state. Fermions fill the lowest $N$ single-particle states once each; the highest filled energy is the **Fermi energy** $\epsilon_F$, so $n(\epsilon) = 1$ below $\epsilon_F$ and $0$ above.

</Step>
<Step title="(b) Lithium-7">

## Summary

- Distinguishable non-interacting particles: product eigenfunctions, energies add.
- Identical particles: exchange commutes with everything, so states are symmetric (bosons, integer spin) or antisymmetric (fermions, half-integer spin); composites follow from counting spin-½ constituents.
- Symmetrize by summing permutations; antisymmetric states are determinants, giving the Pauli principle.
- States are labelled by occupation numbers $n_\alpha$ with $\sum n_\alpha = N$; the constraint blocks factorizing the canonical sum.
- At $T = 0$: bosons all in the lowest state; fermions fill up to the Fermi energy.
