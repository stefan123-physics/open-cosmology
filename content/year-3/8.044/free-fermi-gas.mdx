The grand canonical ensemble gave the occupation of each single-particle state. To use it for a gas, one needs to know how many states there are at each energy. This lecture counts them and applies the result to the most important Fermi system, the electrons in a metal (slides 1–24, numbered as printed, with 21a). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Counting states

**Particle in a box** (slide 1). In a box $L_x \times L_y \times L_z$, standing waves $\sin k_xx\,\sin k_yy\,\sin k_zz$ must vanish at the walls, so $k_x = n_x\pi/L_x$ with $n_x = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, and similarly for $y$ and $z$. The allowed wave vectors form a grid in the positive octant, one per volume $\pi^3/V$ of $k$-space, and with $2S + 1$ spin states each:

<Formula label="Density of states in k-space, box" tex="D_{\text{states}}(\vec k) = (2S + 1)\frac{V}{\pi^3}" cite="mit" at="slide 1" />

**Plane waves with periodic boundary conditions** (slide 2). Requiring $\psi$ to repeat after $L_x$, $L_y$, $L_z$ gives $k_x = 2\pi n_x/L_x$ with $n_x = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots$: a grid over all of $k$-space, one point per $(2\pi)^3/V$:

<Formula label="Density of states in k-space, periodic" tex="D_{\text{states}}(\vec k) = (2S + 1)\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}" cite="mit" at="slide 2" />

Either way the number of wave vectors with $|\vec k| < k$ is the same (slide 3), $\frac43\pi k^3V/(2\pi)^3$: one eighth as dense, but over eight times the volume. For free particles, $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, so the number below $E$ and its derivative are (slides 3–4)

<Formula label="Density of states in energy" tex="D_{\text{states}}(E) = (2S + 1)\frac{V}{4\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}E^{1/2}" cite="mit" at="slide 4" />

## The Fermi gas at zero temperature

Take non-interacting, free, spin-½ fermions at $T = 0$ (slide 5). They fill the lowest states: in $k$-space, a sphere, the **Fermi sea**, of radius the **Fermi wave vector** $k_F$; its surface is the **Fermi surface**, at the **Fermi energy** $E_F = \hbar^2k_F^2/2m$ (written $\epsilon_F$ in the formulas for $N$ and $U$, as on the slides). Counting two spin states per wave vector inside the sphere (slide 6),

<Formula label="Fermi wave vector and Fermi energy" tex="N = 2\cdot\frac43\pi k_F^3\frac{V}{(2\pi)^3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k_F = \left(3\pi^2\frac NV\right)^{1/3}, \qquad E_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(3\pi^2\frac NV\right)^{2/3}" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

Writing $D(\epsilon) = a\epsilon^{1/2}$ (slide 7):

<Formula label="Number and energy at T = 0" tex="N = \int_0^{\epsilon_F}D(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon = \frac23a\epsilon_F^{3/2}, \qquad U = \int_0^{\epsilon_F}\epsilon D(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon = \frac25a\epsilon_F^{5/2} = \frac35N\epsilon_F" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

so $U \propto N(N/V)^{2/3}$.

**Motion at zero temperature** (slide 8). The electrons at the Fermi surface have momentum $p_F = \hbar k_F$ and speed $v_F = \hbar k_F/m$. For copper, with one conduction electron per atom and $N/V = 8.45 \times 10^{22}$ cm⁻³, $k_F = 1.36 \times 10^8$ cm⁻¹, $v_F = 1.57 \times 10^8$ cm/s, $p_F = 1.43 \times 10^{-19}$ g cm/s and $E_F/k \approx 81\,000$ K: even at absolute zero the electrons move at about half a percent of the speed of light.

**Pressure at zero temperature** (slides 9–10). At $T = 0$, $S = 0$, so $P = -(\partial U/\partial V)_{N,S}$; with $U = \frac35NE_F$ and $E_F \propto V^{-2/3}$:

<Formula label="Pressure and compressibility of the Fermi gas" tex="P = \frac25\frac NVE_F \propto \left(\frac NV\right)^{5/3}, \qquad K_T = -\frac1V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_{N,T} = \frac{3}{5P} = \frac{3}{2(N/V)E_F}" cite="mit" at="slides 9–10" />

For potassium, with $E_F = 3.39 \times 10^{-12}$ erg ($E_F/k = 2.46 \times 10^4$ K) and $1.40 \times 10^{22}$ conduction electrons per cm³, this gives $K_T \approx 31.6 \times 10^{-12}$ cm³/erg, against a measured value of about $31 \times 10^{-12}$, a striking agreement that the slide marks with an exclamation mark: the free electron gas is a large part of what makes the metal hard to squeeze.

## Pauli paramagnetism

In a field $H$ an electron's energy shifts by $\pm\mu_eH$ depending on its spin, so the two spin populations, each with density of states $\frac12D(\epsilon)$, are displaced against each other (slide 11). Only electrons near the Fermi surface can move from one to the other (slide 12):

<Formula label="Pauli susceptibility" tex="N_{\text{up}} - N_{\text{down}} = \mu_eHD(E_F), \qquad M = \mu_e^2HD(E_F), \qquad \chi = \frac MH = \mu_e^2D(E_F)" cite="mit" at="slide 12" />

This holds while $kT \ll E_F$, so $\chi$ is **independent of temperature**: not Curie's law but **Pauli paramagnetism**.

## Low but non-zero temperature

The occupation $\langle n\rangle$ is still a function of energy alone (slide 13), now $1/(e^{(\epsilon - \mu)/kT} + 1)$: the sharp step at $T = 0$ is rounded over a width of a few $kT$ around $\mu$.

**Heat capacity, estimated** (slide 14). Classically all $N$ electrons take part, $U = \frac32NkT$ and $C_V = \frac32Nk$. Quantum mechanically only about $N(kT/E_F)$ of them, those within $kT$ of the Fermi surface, can be excited, each by about $kT$, so $U$ rises by about $N(kT)^2/E_F$ and $C_V \sim 2Nk(kT/E_F)$. The exact result is

<Formula label="Electronic heat capacity" tex="C_V = \frac{\pi^2}{2}Nk\frac{kT}{E_F}" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

**The chemical potential** (slides 15–17). $N = \int\langle n(\epsilon, \mu, T)\rangle D(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon$ fixes $\mu(T)$ implicitly, and a careful $C_V$ must include its temperature dependence. The rounding of the step is symmetric about $\mu$: $\langle n\rangle - \frac12$ at $\mu + \delta$ is minus its value at $\mu - \delta$. Because $D(\epsilon)$ increases with $\epsilon$, more states sit just above $\mu$ than just below, so to keep $N$ fixed $\mu$ must **decrease** slightly as $T$ rises.

## Beyond free particles

**Elementary excitations** (slides 18–19). In interacting many-body systems the low-lying excitations are not single particles but either **collective modes**, behaving like harmonic oscillators (plasma oscillations, or plasmons, in an electron gas; phonons in solids; spin waves in ferromagnets; ripplons on surfaces), or **quasi-particles**, "dressed" particles with a modified mass (electrons in a metal, with an effective mass $m^*$ in $\epsilon = \hbar^2k^2/2m^*$; polarons, an electron with its lattice distortion, in ionic crystals). Much of the free-particle picture survives with these as the "particles".

**Electrons in solids** (slide 20). The density of states of a solid has bands and gaps. In a metal $E_F$ lies inside a band; in an insulator it lies in a wide gap; in an intrinsic semiconductor, in a narrow gap; doping adds states inside the gap.

**An intrinsic semiconductor** (slides 21–24). Put the top of the filled band at $\epsilon = 0$ and the bottom of the empty band at $\epsilon_G$, with $\epsilon_G \gg kT$. Assume (and check at the end) that $\mu$ lies far from both band edges; then the Fermi function reduces to Boltzmann tails (slide 21a): $\langle n_e\rangle \approx e^{-(\epsilon - \mu)/kT}$ for electrons above the gap, and $\langle n_h\rangle = 1 - \langle n\rangle \approx e^{-(\mu - \epsilon)/kT}$ for holes below it. With free-particle densities of states measured from each band edge, with effective masses $m_e$ and $m_h$ (slides 22–23):

<Formula label="Electrons and holes" tex="N_e = \frac V4\left(\frac{2m_ekT}{\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}e^{-(\epsilon_G - \mu)/kT}, \qquad N_h = \frac V4\left(\frac{2m_hkT}{\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}e^{-\mu/kT}" cite="mit" at="slides 22–23" />

Every electron in the upper band leaves a hole behind, so $N_e = N_h$, which gives (slide 24)

<Formula label="Chemical potential of an intrinsic semiconductor" tex="\mu = \frac{\epsilon_G}{2} - \frac34kT\ln\frac{m_e}{m_h}" cite="mit" at="slide 24" />

near the middle of the gap, shifting slightly towards the band with the lighter carriers.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Counting states in the early universe">
The rule used here, $(2S + 1)V/(2\pi)^3$ states per unit volume of wave-vector space, is the same one 8.286 uses (Lecture 17) to count the photons, neutrinos and electrons that filled the early universe. There the particles are relativistic and the chemical potential is near zero, but the states are counted in exactly the same way.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="The electrons in sodium">
<Problem>
Sodium has one conduction electron per atom, $N/V = 2.65 \times 10^{28}$ m⁻³. Use $\hbar = 1.054572 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $m = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg, $k = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K and $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602177 \times 10^{-19}$ J. (a) Find $k_F$, $E_F$ (in eV) and $E_F/k$. (b) Find the Fermi speed. (c) At 300 K, compare the electronic heat capacity with the classical $\frac32Nk$. (d) Find the pressure of the electron gas at $T = 0$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Fermi wave vector and energy">
$k_F = (3\pi^2 \times 2.65 \times 10^{28})^{1/3} \approx 9.22 \times 10^9$ m⁻¹, so $E_F = \hbar^2k_F^2/2m \approx 5.19 \times 10^{-19}$ J $\approx 3.24$ eV, and $E_F/k \approx 37\,600$ K.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Fermi speed">
$v_F = \hbar k_F/m \approx 1.07 \times 10^6$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Heat capacity">
$\frac{\pi^2}{2}(kT/E_F)/\frac32 = \frac{\pi^2}{3}(300/37\,600) \approx 0.026$: the electrons carry under 3% of the classical heat capacity, which is why metals' heat capacities are dominated by lattice vibrations at room temperature.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Pressure">
$P = \frac25(N/V)E_F \approx 5.5 \times 10^9$ Pa, about fifty thousand atmospheres, balanced by the attraction of the ions.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- States in $k$-space: $(2S + 1)V/(2\pi)^3$ per unit volume (periodic conditions); for free particles $D(E) = (2S + 1)\frac{V}{4\pi^2}(2m/\hbar^2)^{3/2}E^{1/2}$.
- $T = 0$ Fermi gas: $k_F = (3\pi^2N/V)^{1/3}$, $E_F = \hbar^2k_F^2/2m$, $U = \frac35NE_F$, $P = \frac25(N/V)E_F$, $K_T = 3/[2(N/V)E_F]$ (agreeing with potassium).
- Pauli paramagnetism $\chi = \mu_e^2D(E_F)$, independent of $T$; $C_V = \frac{\pi^2}{2}Nk(kT/E_F)$; $\mu$ falls slightly with $T$.
- Interacting systems: collective modes and quasi-particles. Intrinsic semiconductor: $\mu = \frac{\epsilon_G}{2} - \frac34kT\ln(m_e/m_h)$.
