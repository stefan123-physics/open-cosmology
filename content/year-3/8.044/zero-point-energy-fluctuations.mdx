This lecture collects three results that statistical mechanics makes possible: evidence that the zero-point energy of quantum oscillators is real, a formula for the size of thermodynamic fluctuations, and a set of counter-intuitive behaviours (slides 1–15, numbered as printed, with 3a–3e and 4a–4c). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The slides also link to two videos of critical opalescence, which are not reproduced here.)

## Phonons, briefly

The test of the zero-point energy is the difference between the low-temperature lattice spacings of lithium-6 and lithium-7 (slide 2). It needs a short review of **phonons**, the elementary excitations of a crystal's vibrations.

**Wave vectors on a lattice** (slides 3a–3e). On a lattice of spacing $a$, a wave is only sampled at the atoms. Adding $2\pi/a$ (or any multiple) to $k_x$ gives exactly the same displacements at every atom: on a chain of length $L$ with $a = L/10$, the waves with $k = 4\pi/L$, $24\pi/L$ and $44\pi/L$ all move the atoms identically. So only wave vectors with $-\pi/a < k_x \le \pi/a$ give distinct states, and their number equals the number of lattice points, that is, of unit cells, in one, two or three dimensions.

**Branches and density of states** (slides 4a–4b). For each allowed wave vector there are $3J$ phonon frequencies, where $J$ is the number of atoms in the primitive cell. The density of states is large where $d\omega/dk = 0$.

**The Grüneisen rule** (slide 4c). The crystal's phonons are $3JN$ harmonic oscillators with many frequencies $\omega_i$ (this page labels the modes by a single index $i$, which stands for the wave vector and branch, written $\vec k, j$ on the slides). Experimentally, to a good approximation, all of them respond to a change in the crystal's volume in the same proportion (the Grüneisen rule, a name not used on the slide):

<Formula label="Volume dependence of the phonon frequencies" tex="\frac{d\omega}{\omega} = -\gamma\frac{dV}{V}" cite="mit" at="slide 4c" />

with one constant $\gamma$: squeezing the crystal stiffens every mode.

## The zero-point energy is real

At constant $T$ and $V$, a spontaneous process lowers the **Helmholtz free energy** (slide 5): combining $dQ \le T\,dS$ with $dF = dU - T\,dS - S\,dT$ and $dU = dQ - P\,dV$,

<Formula label="Free energy at constant T and V" tex="dF \le -S\,dT - P\,dV" cite="mit" at="slide 5" />

so the crystal settles at the volume that minimizes $F$. Write $F = \phi(V) + F_{\text{phonons}}(T, V)$, where $\phi(V)$ is the energy of the atoms sitting still at their sites and the phonon part is a sum of $-kT\ln Z_i$ over all modes (slide 6). For one oscillator, $Z_1 = \sum_ne^{-(n + \frac12)\hbar\omega/kT}$; differentiating with respect to $V$ through $\omega(V)$, using the Grüneisen rule (slide 7),

<Formula label="Volume derivative of one oscillator's partition function" tex="\frac{1}{Z_1}\left(\frac{\partial Z_1}{\partial V}\right)_T = \frac{\gamma}{V}\frac{\langle E\rangle}{kT}" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

where $\langle E\rangle$ includes the zero-point energy $\frac12\hbar\omega$. The minimum of $F$ is therefore where (slide 8)

<Formula label="Equilibrium volume of a crystal" tex="\left(\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial V}\right)_T = kT\sum_i\frac{1}{Z_i}\left(\frac{\partial Z_i}{\partial V}\right)_T = \frac{\gamma}{V}\sum_i\langle\epsilon_i\rangle = \gamma\frac{U_{\text{phonons}}}{V}" cite="mit" at="slides 6–8" />

The static energy $\phi(V)$ has a minimum; the phonons push the equilibrium slightly beyond it, to larger volume, and the more vibrational energy there is, the further. Even at $T = 0$ the zero-point energy pushes.

**Lithium-6 and lithium-7** (slide 9). At low temperature $U_{\text{phonons}} = U_{\text{zp}} + aT^4$, with $U_{\text{zp}} = N \times \frac12\hbar\langle\omega\rangle$. As for a mass on a spring, every mode frequency scales as $1/\sqrt{m_{\text{atom}}}$, so

<Formula label="Zero-point energies of the two isotopes" tex="\frac{U_{\text{zp}}(^6\text{Li})}{U_{\text{zp}}(^7\text{Li})} = \sqrt{\frac76} = 1.08" cite="mit" at="slide 9" />

and the measurements agree: near absolute zero, lithium-6 crystals have lattice spacings about 0.07% larger than lithium-7 crystals. The two isotopes are chemically identical; only the zero-point motion differs.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Zero-point energy on a cosmic scale">
The lithium measurement shows that zero-point energy has real, measurable effects. Quantum fields are collections of oscillators too, one for every mode, and each carries its own zero-point energy. Added up, these suggest that empty space itself has an energy density, a vacuum energy that would act like Einstein's cosmological constant. 8.286 takes this up in Lecture 20, where a naive sum overshoots the observed value enormously.
</Callout>

## Fluctuations

Fluctuations have so far been dismissed as negligible, and for most systems they are; the exceptions, where they grow until they control what the system does, are the interesting part (slide 10). Consider the number of particles in a volume $V$, described grand canonically.

Differentiating the grand partition function $\Xi$ (a script Z on the slides) once with respect to $\mu$ gives $\langle N\rangle$ (Lecture 18); differentiating twice brings down $N^2$ (slide 11), and combining the two (slide 12):

<Formula label="Number fluctuations from the grand partition function" tex="\langle N^2\rangle - \langle N\rangle^2 = kT\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\left(kT\frac{\partial\ln\Xi}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T,V} = -kT\left(\frac{\partial^2\Phi}{\partial\mu^2}\right)_{T,V}" cite="mit" at="slides 12–13" />

with $\Phi = -kT\ln\Xi$ the grand potential (written $\Phi_G$ on the Lecture 18 page). For a hydrostatic system one can show that $(\partial^2\Phi/\partial\mu^2)_{T,V} = -\langle N\rangle^2\kappa_T/V$, with $\kappa_T = -\frac1V(\partial V/\partial P)_T$ the isothermal compressibility (slides 13–14). So the relative fluctuations of the number, and of the density $n = N/V$, are

<Formula label="Relative fluctuation of particle number" tex="\frac{\langle N^2\rangle - \langle N\rangle^2}{\langle N\rangle^2} = \frac{\langle n^2\rangle - \langle n\rangle^2}{\langle n\rangle^2} = \frac{kT}{V}\kappa_T" cite="mit" at="slide 13" />

For an ideal gas $\kappa_T = 1/P$ and this is $1/\langle N\rangle$, very small for any macroscopic volume. But near the **critical point** of a liquid–gas transition, where the distinction between liquid and gas vanishes, $\kappa_T$ grows without limit. The density fluctuations then become large and extend over regions as big as the wavelength of light, scatter it strongly, and the clear fluid turns milky: **critical opalescence**.

## Counter-intuitive results

The lecture ends with questions (slide 15). Does increasing the force or pressure always make the length or volume decrease? Does raising the temperature always make it increase? Do liquids always shrink when they freeze? Does a solid always melt when heated and a liquid always solidify when cooled? (The answers below are this page's own.) Only the first is guaranteed, in its proper form: squeezing harder always reduces a fluid's volume, since a negative compressibility would make the fluctuations above negative, which is impossible, so stability requires $\kappa_T > 0$; likewise pulling harder on a wire always lengthens it. The others can fail: a stretched rubber band contracts when heated (Lecture 11), water expands when it freezes, and helium-3 under about 30 atmospheres and below about 0.3 K solidifies when heated (the Pomeranchuk effect), because there its solid has more entropy than its liquid.

<WorkedExample title="How big are fluctuations?">
<Problem>
Air at 273.15 K and 101 325 Pa, treated as an ideal gas ($k = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K). (a) How many molecules are in a cube 1 μm on a side, and what is the root-mean-square fractional fluctuation of that number? (b) The same for a cube 1 cm on a side. (c) By what factor would $\kappa_T$ have to exceed its ideal-gas value for the 1 μm cube to show 1% fluctuations?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) One cubic micrometre">
$N = PV/kT = 101\,325 \times 10^{-18}/(1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 273.15) \approx 2.69 \times 10^7$. With $\kappa_T = 1/P$ the relative variance is $1/N$, so the rms fraction is $1/\sqrt N \approx 1.93 \times 10^{-4}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) One cubic centimetre">
$N \approx 2.69 \times 10^{19}$ and $1/\sqrt N \approx 1.93 \times 10^{-10}$: invisible.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Near a critical point">
The rms fraction grows as $\sqrt{\kappa_T}$, so reaching $10^{-2}$ needs $\kappa_T$ about $(10^{-2}/1.93 \times 10^{-4})^2 \approx 2.7 \times 10^3$ times its ideal-gas value, which a fluid close to its critical point can exceed.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- On a lattice only wave vectors in $-\pi/a < k \le \pi/a$ are distinct, one per unit cell; there are $3J$ branches, and all frequencies scale with volume as $d\omega/\omega = -\gamma\,dV/V$.
- At fixed $T$ and $V$, $F$ is minimized; the phonons push the lattice outwards with $\gamma U_{\text{phonons}}/V$, zero-point energy included, so lithium-6 (zero-point energy $\sqrt{7/6}$ times larger) has a lattice spacing about 0.07% larger than lithium-7.
- Number fluctuations: $(\langle N^2\rangle - \langle N\rangle^2)/\langle N\rangle^2 = kT\kappa_T/V$, tiny normally, large near a critical point (critical opalescence).
- Stability requires $\kappa_T > 0$; other "obvious" behaviours (thermal expansion, shrinking on freezing, melting on heating) have exceptions.
