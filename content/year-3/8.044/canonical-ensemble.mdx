This lecture finishes thermodynamics with refrigerators and the third law, and then returns to statistical mechanics to derive its most useful tool, the **canonical ensemble** (slides 1–15, numbered as printed; notes pp. 1–4). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides and his "Notes on the Canonical Ensemble"; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The slide set also includes two slides from Lecture 7 and a worked solution on oscillators, between slides 7 and 8, as reminders.)

## Refrigerators and heat pumps

**A refrigerator** runs an engine cycle backwards: it extracts heat $|Q_C|$ at the cold end and dumps $|Q_H|$ at the hot end, with work $W = |Q_H| - |Q_C|$ done on the substance (slide 1). Its figure of merit is the heat extracted per unit work; for a quasi-static Carnot cycle, where $|Q_C|/|Q_H| = T_C/T_H$,

<Formula label="Refrigerator performance" tex="\frac{|Q_C|}{W} = \frac{T_C}{T_H - T_C}" cite="mit" at="slide 1" />

As with engines, the Carnot cycle is the best possible (slide 2). In practice it gets harder and harder to approach $T = 0$, since the performance falls towards zero as $T_C$ does; and, as a matter of principle, absolute zero is where a body has no heat left to give up.

**A heat pump** is the same reversed cycle, but used for the heat dumped at the hot end (slide 3):

<Formula label="Heat pump performance" tex="\frac{|Q_H|}{W} = \frac{T_H}{T_H - T_C}" cite="mit" at="slide 3" />

Slide 4's example: the ground below the surface at 286 K (55 °F, typical at 40° latitude) heating a room to 294 K (70 °F) gives an ideal $|Q_H|/W = 294/8 \approx 37$: each joule of work delivers dozens of joules of heat.

## The third law

<Formula label="Third law" tex="\lim_{T \to 0} S = S_0" cite="mit" at="slide 5" />

As $T \to 0$ the entropy of a substance approaches a constant, independent of the other thermodynamic variables (slide 5). Originally a hypothesis, it is now understood from quantum mechanics: at $T = 0$ the system is in its ground state, with degeneracy $g$ (usually 1), so $S_0 = k\ln g$, usually zero.

**Consequences** (slide 6). Since $S_0$ does not depend on any other variable $x$, $(\partial S/\partial x)_T \to 0$ as $T \to 0$. For a hydrostatic system, the Gibbs Maxwell relation turns the expansion coefficient into an entropy derivative:

<Formula label="Expansion coefficient at low temperature" tex="\alpha = \frac1V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = -\frac1V\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T \to 0" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

so $C_P - C_V = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T \to 0$ too. And since $S(T) - S(0) = \int_0^T C_V(T')/T'\,dT'$ must be finite, $C_V(T) \to 0$ as $T \to 0$.

## Ensembles

Three ensembles cover statistical mechanics (slide 7):

- **Microcanonical**: $E$ and $N$ fixed. The starting point for everything, but hard to use for specific systems.
- **Canonical**: $N$ fixed and $T$ specified, so $E$ varies. The workhorse.
- **Grand canonical**: $T$ and the chemical potential specified, so $E$ and $N$ vary. Used when the particle number is not fixed.

## Deriving the canonical ensemble

Take a large isolated system, described microcanonically, and divide it into a **subsystem 1**, the system of interest, and a much larger **remainder 2**, the bath; energy flows between them (slide 8; notes p. 1). Subsystem 1 may be anything from a single degree of freedom to a macroscopic body. Each part has its own phase space, and phase volumes $\Omega_1$ and $\Omega_2$ can be defined for them even though their energies are not fixed.

The whole is microcanonical, so the probability of any condition is a ratio of phase volumes. For subsystem 1 at one particular point $\{p_1, q_1\}$, with the remainder in any state (slide 9; notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Probability of a state of the subsystem" tex="p(\{p_1, q_1\}) = \frac{\Omega_1\,\Omega_2(E - E_1)}{\Omega(E)}, \qquad \Omega_1 = \Omega_1(\{p_1, q_1\})" cite="mit" at="slide 9" />

Fixing every variable of 1 pins it to a single point of its phase space, so $\Omega_1 = 1$ and $k\ln\Omega_1 = 0$. Taking $k\ln$ of the rest turns the phase volumes into entropies: $k\ln p = S_2(E - E_1) - S(E)$. Expand $S_2$ about $E$ (only a mathematical step, as the notes stress on p. 2; no energy is really moved) (slide 10):

<Formula label="Expanding the bath's entropy" tex="S_2(E - E_1) \approx S_2(E) - \left(\frac{\partial S_2}{\partial E_2}\right)_{E_2 = E}E_1" cite="mit" at="slide 10" />

The derivative is $1/T$, the bath's temperature; the bath is so large (notes p. 2) that it hardly matters whether it is evaluated at $E$ or at the bath's average energy. With $E_1 = H_1(\{p_1, q_1\})$, the subsystem's Hamiltonian (slide 11):

<Formula label="Log of the probability" tex="k\ln p(\{p_1, q_1\}) = -\frac{H_1}{T} + S_2(E) - S(E)" cite="mit" at="slide 11" />

Only the first term depends on which state the subsystem is in; the rest depend on the bath and on the subsystem's average properties.

**Always** (slide 12), even when the subsystem is too small for thermodynamics, this gives the probability density up to normalization:

<Formula label="Canonical probability density" tex="p(\{p_1, q_1\}) = \frac{1}{Z_1}\exp\left[-\frac{H_1}{kT}\right], \qquad Z_1 = \int\exp\left[-\frac{H_1}{kT}\right]\{dp_1, dq_1\}" cite="mit" at="slide 12" />

Here $H_1$ is evaluated at $\{p_1, q_1\}$ (this page writes it without the argument inside fractions) and $T$ is the bath's temperature, which exists even if the subsystem's does not.

**When thermodynamics applies** to the subsystem (slides 13–14; notes p. 3), the normalizing constant has a meaning. Entropy is additive, $S(E) = S_1(\langle E_1\rangle) + S_2(\langle E_2\rangle)$, with $\langle E_1\rangle + \langle E_2\rangle = E$, so $S_2(E) - S(E) = [S_2(E) - S_2(\langle E_2\rangle)] - S_1 \approx \langle E_1\rangle/T - S_1$. Then

<Formula label="Normalization and the free energy" tex="p(\{p_1, q_1\}) = \exp\left[\frac{\langle E_1\rangle - TS_1}{kT}\right]\exp\left[-\frac{H_1}{kT}\right], \qquad \langle E_1\rangle - TS_1 = U_1 - T_1S_1 = F_1" cite="mit" at="slides 13–14" />

since $\langle E_1\rangle$ is the internal energy and $T_1 = T$ in equilibrium: the prefactor is $e^{F/kT}$, with $F$ the Helmholtz free energy. Dropping the subscripts, $p(\{p, q\}) = Z^{-1}e^{-H/kT}$ with the **partition function** ($H$ evaluated at $\{p, q\}$)

<Formula label="Partition function" tex="Z_N(T, V) = \int\exp\left[-\frac{H}{kT}\right]\{dp, dq\} = \exp\left[-\frac{F(T, V, N)}{kT}\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

(The slides divide the phase-space integral by a power of Planck's constant to make $Z$ a pure number.) In the canonical ensemble **the partition function is the source of thermodynamic information** (slide 15):

<Formula label="Thermodynamics from Z" tex="F(T, V, N) = -kT\ln Z_N(T, V), \qquad S = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_{V,N}, \qquad P = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\right)_{T,N}" cite="mit" at="slide 15" />

The notes add a warning (p. 4): this assumes the internal energy equals the average of the Hamiltonian, $E = \langle H\rangle$. That is true for most systems but not, for example, for a simple paramagnet, which the course returns to later.

<WorkedExample title="Cold rooms and a single two-level member">
<Problem>
(a) A freezer holds 255 K in a 300 K kitchen. What is its best possible $|Q_C|/W$? (b) A heat pump keeps a house at 293 K when it is 270 K outside. What is its best possible $|Q_H|/W$? (c) A single member of a two-level system (energies $0$ and $\epsilon$) is in contact with a bath with $kT = \epsilon$. Using the canonical ensemble, find $Z$, the probability of the upper level, and $F/kT$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Freezer">
$T_C/(T_H - T_C) = 255/45 \approx 5.67$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Heat pump">
$T_H/(T_H - T_C) = 293/23 \approx 12.7$. The smaller the temperature lift, the better a heat pump can do.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) One member, canonically">
The "phase space" is just two states, so the integral becomes a sum: $Z = 1 + e^{-\epsilon/kT} = 1 + e^{-1} \approx 1.368$. The upper level has probability $e^{-1}/Z = 1/(e + 1) \approx 0.269$, exactly what Lecture 9 found by counting states of the whole system microcanonically. And $F/kT = -\ln Z \approx -0.313$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Refrigerator: $|Q_C|/W \le T_C/(T_H - T_C)$; heat pump: $|Q_H|/W \le T_H/(T_H - T_C)$.
- Third law: $S \to S_0 = k\ln g$ as $T \to 0$; expansion coefficients, $C_P - C_V$ and heat capacities vanish there.
- Canonical ensemble (fixed $N$, bath at $T$): $p(\{p, q\}) = Z^{-1}e^{-H/kT}$, derived by expanding the bath's entropy.
- $Z = \int e^{-H/kT}\{dp, dq\} = e^{-F/kT}$; $F = -kT\ln Z$, $S = -\partial F/\partial T$, $P = -\partial F/\partial V$.
