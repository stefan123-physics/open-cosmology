## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. Unless a box asks for more digits, give 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `k*T*log(Z1)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent. Type `exp(...)` for an exponential, `sqrt(...)` for a square root, `log(...)` for the natural logarithm and `pi` for $\pi$. Write the symbols as the box names them: `I1`, `I2`, `I3` for $I_1$, $I_2$, $I_3$, `L1`, `L2`, `L3` for $L_1$, $L_2$, $L_3$, `Z1` for $Z_1$, `D` for $\Delta$, `T0` and `a0` for $T_0$ and $a_0$, `hbar` for $\hbar$, and `E` for $E_{\min}$ in Problem 5.

The points per problem and per part are ours. Parts that ask for an argument have a choice box or none: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons: $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, $Z_1$ is the partition function of one grain, atom or molecule, and $Z$ that of all $N$ of them. In Problems 1 to 4 the energy of the whole system is $E$ and its heat capacity is $C$; in Problem 5, $E$ is the kinetic energy of one particle. The letter $m$ is a mass in Problems 3 and 5 and a quantum number in Problem 4. The letter $a$ in Problem 3 is the strength of the trap, as on MIT's sheet.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Dust grains in space" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

The space between the stars holds clouds of dust grains. Their material may be silicates (as in sand) or compounds of carbon (graphite, silicon carbide), and there is evidence that many grains are shaped like needles.

The rotation of a grain is described with its three principal axes, numbered 1, 2 and 3. The coordinates are three angles $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$, $\theta_3$, and the momenta that belong to them are the angular momenta $L_1$, $L_2$, $L_3$ about the three axes. The classical Hamiltonian of one grain is

<Formula label="Rotational Hamiltonian of a dust grain" tex="H = \frac{L_1^2}{2I_1} + \frac{L_2^2}{2I_2} + \frac{L_3^2}{2I_3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

where $I_i$ is the moment of inertia about axis $i$. The cloud is in thermal equilibrium at the temperature $T$. Following MIT's solution, take each angle to run over a range of $2\pi$, independently of the others.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the joint probability density $p(\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3, L_1, L_2, L_3)$ of the six canonical variables of one grain, as a formula without integrals.

<Hint>

In the canonical ensemble the density is $e^{-H/kT}$ divided by its integral over all six variables.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The Hamiltonian is a sum of three terms, each with one variable. So the density is a product, and the three integrals over the angular momenta are Gaussian integrals. What does the integral over each angle give, when $H$ does not contain the angles?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-joint" label="$p(\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3, L_1, L_2, L_3) =$" expr="exp(-L1^2/(2*I1*k*T) - L2^2/(2*I2*k*T) - L3^2/(2*I3*k*T))/((2*pi)^3*(2*pi*k*T)^(3/2)*sqrt(I1*I2*I3))"
  vars={{ L1: [-1, 1], L2: [-1, 1], L3: [-1, 1], I1: [1, 2], I2: [1, 2], I3: [1, 2], k: [1, 2], T: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ L1: "L_1", L2: "L_2", L3: "L_3", I1: "I_1", I2: "I_2", I3: "I_3" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-L1^2/(2*I1*k*T) - L2^2/(2*I2*k*T) - L3^2/(2*I3*k*T))/((2*pi*k*T)^(3/2)*sqrt(I1*I2*I3))", hint: "That is the density of the three angular momenta alone. The three angles are random variables too: each is uniform over its range of 2π, which gives a factor 1/2π each." },
    { expr: "exp(-L1^2/(2*I1*k*T) - L2^2/(2*I2*k*T) - L3^2/(2*I3*k*T))", hint: "That is the Boltzmann factor alone. A density must integrate to 1: divide by the integral of this factor over all six variables." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The canonical density is the Boltzmann factor $e^{-H/kT}$, normalized. Because $H$ is a sum of terms with one variable each, the density is a product of six densities of one variable each.

<Step title="Six independent variables">

The Hamiltonian does not contain the angles, so each angle is uniform over its range: its density is $1/2\pi$. Each angular momentum appears in one term, $L_i^2/2I_i$, so its density is a Gaussian with zero mean and variance $I_ikT$. The six variables are statistically independent (MIT's solutions p. 1), and

<Formula label="Joint density of the angles and angular momenta of a grain" tex="p(\theta_1, \theta_2, \theta_3, L_1, L_2, L_3) = \left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\right)^3\frac{e^{-L_1^2/2I_1kT}}{\sqrt{2\pi I_1kT}}\;\frac{e^{-L_2^2/2I_2kT}}{\sqrt{2\pi I_2kT}}\;\frac{e^{-L_3^2/2I_3kT}}{\sqrt{2\pi I_3kT}}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Each of the six factors integrates to 1 over its own variable, so the product integrates to 1 over all six. The unit is right too: $I_ikT$ is the square of an angular momentum, so the density is one over the cube of an angular momentum (the angles have no unit).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Let axis 3 lie along the needle, so that $I_3$ is much smaller than $I_1 = I_2$. Is the angular momentum of a grain more likely to be parallel or perpendicular to the long axis? Support your answer with a number: find the mean square $\langle L_1^2\rangle$.

The third box is our addition. For a grain with $I_3 = I_1/400$ (our example), find the ratio of the root mean square of $L_3$ to the root mean square of the part of the angular momentum that is perpendicular to the needle, $\sqrt{\langle L_1^2 + L_2^2\rangle}$.

<Hint>

Read the mean squares off the density of part (a): each $L_i$ is Gaussian. What is its variance?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The same result follows from equipartition: each term $L_i^2/2I_i$ of the Hamiltonian has the mean $\frac12kT$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-l1" label="$\langle L_1^2\rangle =$" expr="I1*k*T"
  vars={{ I1: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ I1: "I_1" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "I1*k*T/2", hint: "The mean of the energy L₁²/2I₁ is kT/2. Solve that for the mean of L₁²: the 2 below I₁ cancels the 2 below kT." },
    { expr: "k*T/2", hint: "That is the mean energy of the rotation about axis 1, the mean of L₁²/2I₁. The question asks for the mean of L₁² itself." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-axis" label="The angular momentum is most likely" choices={["nearly parallel to the long axis: the grain spins most easily about the axis with the smallest moment of inertia", "nearly perpendicular to the long axis: the mean squares of L₁ and L₂ are far larger than that of L₃", "in no special direction: equipartition gives each of the three axes the same share"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-ratio" label="$I_3 = I_1/400$: ratio of the root mean squares $=$" value="0.0354" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each component of the angular momentum is Gaussian with variance $I_ikT$. A small moment of inertia means a small spread of that component.

<Step title="The mean squares">

<Formula label="Mean squares of the angular momentum components" tex="\langle L_1^2\rangle = \langle L_2^2\rangle = I_1kT \;\gg\; \langle L_3^2\rangle = I_3kT" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

So the angular momentum vector is almost perpendicular to axis 3, the long axis: the needle tumbles end over end, it does not spin like a drill.

</Step>

<Step title="The example (our addition)">

The perpendicular part has $\langle L_1^2 + L_2^2\rangle = 2I_1kT$. The ratio of the root mean squares is $\sqrt{I_3/2I_1} = \sqrt{1/800} = 0.0354$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Equipartition gives every axis the same mean energy, $\frac12kT$, not the same angular momentum. The same energy with a smaller moment of inertia means a faster spin but a smaller angular momentum: $L_3^2 = 2I_3\times$ energy.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Find the contribution of the rotation to the entropy of a cloud of $N$ grains. First find the partition function $Z_1$ of the rotation of one grain, with a factor $1/h$ for each pair of an angle and its angular momentum, as in the lesson. Then give the entropy in terms of $N$, $k$ and $Z_1$.

<Hint>

$Z_1$ is the integral of $e^{-H/kT}$ over the six variables, divided by $h^3$. You did these integrals in part (a).

</Hint>

<Hint>

The $N$ grains rotate independently, so the rotational part is $Z = Z_1^N$ and $F = -NkT\ln Z_1$. The entropy is $S = -\left(\partial F/\partial T\right)_N$. How does $Z_1$ depend on $T$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-z" label="$Z_1 =$" expr="(2*pi)^(9/2)*sqrt(I1*I2*I3)*(k*T)^(3/2)/h^3"
  vars={{ I1: [0.5, 2], I2: [0.5, 2], I3: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2], h: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ I1: "I_1", I2: "I_2", I3: "I_3" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(2*pi)^(9/2)*sqrt(I1*I2*I3)*(k*T)^(3/2)", hint: "That is the integral of the Boltzmann factor, as MIT's solution prints it. It has the unit of an angular momentum cubed: divide by h³, one h for each pair of an angle and its angular momentum, to get a pure number." },
    { expr: "(2*pi)^(3/2)*sqrt(I1*I2*I3)*(k*T)^(3/2)/h^3", hint: "The integrals over the three angles are missing: each gives a factor 2π." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-s" label="$S =$" expr="N*k*log(Z1) + 3*N*k/2"
  vars={{ N: [1, 5], k: [0.5, 2], Z1: [2, 50] }}
  names={{ Z1: "Z_1" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "N*k*log(Z1)", hint: "That is −F/T. The derivative of F with respect to T also acts on the T inside Z₁, and that adds a constant term." },
    { expr: "N*k*log(Z1) + 3*N*k", hint: "Z₁ grows as T to the power 3/2, not 3: each of the three Gaussian integrals gives one factor √T." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The partition function of one grain is the normalizing integral of part (a). The entropy is the temperature derivative of the free energy.

<Step title="The partition function of one grain">

The three angles give $(2\pi)^3$ and the three Gaussian integrals give $\sqrt{2\pi I_ikT}$ each:

<Formula label="Rotational partition function of one grain" tex="Z_1 = \frac{1}{h^3}\int e^{-H/kT}\,d\theta_1\,d\theta_2\,d\theta_3\,dL_1\,dL_2\,dL_3 = \frac{(2\pi)^{9/2}\sqrt{I_1I_2I_3}\,(kT)^{3/2}}{h^3}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

(MIT's solutions print this without the $h^3$. That changes the entropy by a constant only, and nothing below depends on it.)

</Step>

<Step title="The entropy">

With $F = -NkT\ln Z_1$ and $Z_1 \propto T^{3/2}$, so that $\partial Z_1/\partial T = \frac32Z_1/T$:

<Formula label="Rotational entropy of the dust cloud" tex="S = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_N = Nk\ln Z_1 + NkT\,\frac{1}{Z_1}\,\frac32\,\frac{Z_1}{T} = Nk\ln Z_1 + \frac32Nk" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

No factor $1/N!$ is used here: the correct Boltzmann counting for identical grains is done once, in the part of the partition function that belongs to the motion of the centres of mass.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The energy is $E = F + TS = \frac32NkT$: half a $kT$ for each of the three quadratic terms of the Hamiltonian, as equipartition says. The heat capacity of the rotation is $\frac32Nk$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Does this model of the rotation obey the third law of thermodynamics? Give your reasoning.

<Hint>

The third law of the lesson says that the entropy tends to a constant as $T \to 0$. What does the result of part (c) do there?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-third" label="Third law:" choices={["obeyed: the entropy falls to zero at T = 0", "obeyed: the heat capacity stays constant, which the third law allows", "broken: the entropy falls without bound as T goes to 0, and the heat capacity does not go to zero"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow the entropy of part (c) down to $T = 0$.

<Step title="The limit">

$Z_1$ is proportional to $T^{3/2}$, so it goes to zero with $T$ and its logarithm goes to minus infinity:

<Formula label="Entropy of the classical rotation at low temperature" tex="\lim_{T \to 0}S = \lim_{T \to 0}\left(Nk\ln Z_1 + \frac32Nk\right) = -\infty" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(d), p. 1" />

The third law asks for a finite constant. It is broken. The heat capacity shows the same thing: it stays at $\frac32Nk$, while the third law makes every heat capacity vanish as $T \to 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="What is wrong">

The model is classical. At very low temperature the rotation must be treated with quantum mechanics, and that treatment gives a result that agrees with the third law (MIT's solutions p. 1).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

Should this model of the rotation show energy gap behaviour? Why?

<Hint>

Energy gap behaviour is the exponentially small heat capacity of a system whose first excited state lies a finite energy above the ground state. Which rotational energies does the classical Hamiltonian allow?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-gap" label="Energy gap behaviour:" choices={["no: the classical energies run on from zero without a gap, and the heat capacity keeps its full value at every temperature", "yes: at low temperature the rotation about the long axis freezes out", "yes: every heat capacity is exponentially small at low temperature"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A gap needs a lowest excited state at a finite energy above the ground state.

<Step title="No gap">

Classically every value of $L_1$, $L_2$ and $L_3$ is allowed, so the rotational energy can be as small as one likes. There is no gap, and no energy gap behaviour: the heat capacity is $\frac32Nk$ at every temperature. The quantum treatment has discrete levels, and its result will show a gap (MIT's solutions p. 1). Problem 4 is an example.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Adsorption on a stepped surface" points={25} mit="Problem 2">

A perfect crystal cut exactly along a symmetry direction can show one flat plane of atoms. Cut at a small angle to that direction, its surface becomes a staircase: flat terraces of a fixed width, each one atomic layer higher than the next. MIT's sheet shows this in two drawings. The first is a cut through the crystal, at right angles to the surface, with one step between two terraces. The second looks down on the surface: the edge of a step is not a straight line but has kinks. An impurity atom can then sit in three kinds of place: on the open face of a terrace, against the edge of a step, or in the corner at a kink, where it touches the most neighbours.

$N$ identical xenon atoms are adsorbed on a silicon surface with $M$ possible sites.

- One percent of the sites are **corner** sites. Their energy is the zero of the energy scale.
- Fourteen percent are **edge** sites, with the energy $\Delta$ above a corner site.
- The other 85 percent are **face** sites, with the energy $1.5\Delta$ above a corner site.

$M$ is so much larger than $N$ that the atoms never compete for a site: treat them as completely independent. Neglect their kinetic energy.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the partition function. In the box give $Z_1$, the partition function of one atom, in terms of $M$, $\Delta$, $k$ and $T$. As MIT's solution does, take the partition function of all the atoms as $Z = Z_1^N$.

<Hint>

For discrete states the partition function is a sum of $e^{-\epsilon/kT}$ over the states. One state of an atom is one site. How many sites have each energy?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-z" label="$Z_1 =$" expr="M*(0.01 + 0.14*exp(-D/(k*T)) + 0.85*exp(-1.5*D/(k*T)))"
  vars={{ M: [100, 1000], D: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ D: "\\Delta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1 + exp(-D/(k*T)) + exp(-1.5*D/(k*T))", hint: "That sums over the three energies. The sum is over states, and every site is a state: each energy must be counted as often as there are sites with it." },
    { expr: "0.01 + 0.14*exp(-D/(k*T)) + 0.85*exp(-1.5*D/(k*T))", hint: "The numbers of sites are 0.01M, 0.14M and 0.85M: the factor M is missing. It cancels in every probability, but it matters for the entropy in part (e)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Sum the Boltzmann factor over the $M$ sites. Sites with the same energy give equal terms.

<Step title="The sum over sites">

<Formula label="Partition function of one adsorbed atom" tex="Z_1 = \sum_{\text{states}}e^{-\epsilon_{\text{state}}/kT} = 0.01M + 0.14M\,e^{-\Delta/kT} + 0.85M\,e^{-1.5\Delta/kT}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

and $Z = Z_1^N$ for the $N$ independent atoms.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At high temperature every exponential is 1 and $Z_1 \to M$, the number of states. At low temperature $Z_1 \to 0.01M$, the number of states of lowest energy.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the ratio of the number of xenon atoms on face sites to the number on corner sites.

The second box is our addition: at which temperature are the two numbers equal? Give $kT/\Delta$.

<Hint>

The probability that an atom is on one given site is $e^{-\epsilon/kT}/Z_1$. The probability that it is on any site of one kind is that, times the number of such sites.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ratio" label="$n_{\text{face}}/n_{\text{corner}} =$" expr="85*exp(-1.5*D/(k*T))"
  vars={{ D: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ D: "\\Delta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-1.5*D/(k*T))", hint: "That is the ratio for one face site and one corner site. There are 85 times as many face sites as corner sites." },
    { expr: "85*exp(1.5*D/(k*T))", hint: "Check the sign in the exponent: the face sites have the higher energy, so each one is less likely than a corner site. With your sign the faces would be favoured most at low temperature." },
    { expr: "0.85*exp(-1.5*D/(k*T))", hint: "The share of the corner sites, 0.01, is missing below: the ratio of the numbers of sites is 0.85/0.01." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-equal" label="Equal numbers at $kT/\Delta =$" value="0.338" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-equal" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The number of atoms on a kind of site is $N$ times the probability of that kind. In the ratio, $N$ and $Z_1$ cancel.

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Ratio of atoms on face sites to atoms on corner sites" tex="\frac{n_{\text{face}}}{n_{\text{corner}}} = \frac{p_{\text{face}}}{p_{\text{corner}}} = \frac{0.85M\,e^{-1.5\Delta/kT}}{0.01M} = 85\,e^{-1.5\Delta/kT}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Equal numbers (our addition)">

The ratio is 1 when $1.5\Delta/kT = \ln 85$, so at $kT/\Delta = 1.5/\ln 85 = 0.338$. Below that temperature the few corner sites hold more atoms than all the faces together.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At high temperature the ratio tends to 85, the ratio of the numbers of sites. At low temperature it tends to zero: all the atoms settle in corners.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Find the heat capacity $C$ of the adsorbed xenon atoms in the limit $kT \ll \Delta$. Give the leading term: the one with the lowest power of $e^{-\Delta/kT}$.

<Hint>

At low temperature almost every atom is in a corner, a few are on edges, and far fewer on faces. Keep only the two lowest levels.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The energy is $N$ times the mean energy of one atom: $\Delta$ times the probability of an edge site. In the limit, what is the larger term in the denominator of that probability?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiate the energy with respect to $T$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$C \approx$" expr="14*N*k*(D/(k*T))^2*exp(-D/(k*T))"
  vars={{ N: [1, 5], D: [1, 2], k: [1, 2], T: [0.3, 1] }}
  names={{ D: "\\Delta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "14*N*D*exp(-D/(k*T))", hint: "That is the energy at low temperature. The heat capacity is its derivative with respect to T." },
    { expr: "0.14*N*k*(D/(k*T))^2*exp(-D/(k*T))", hint: "The edge sites must be compared with the corner sites, which hold nearly all the atoms: the factor in front is 0.14/0.01, not 0.14." },
    { expr: "14*N*k*(D/(k*T))*exp(-D/(k*T))", hint: "Check the derivative of exp(−Δ/kT) with respect to T: it is Δ/kT² times the exponential. Together with the Δ in front that makes k(Δ/kT)²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For $kT \ll \Delta$ the face sites can be left out, and the corner term dominates the partition function.

<Step title="The energy">

With the two lowest levels only (MIT's solutions p. 2):

<Formula label="Energy of the adsorbed atoms at low temperature" tex="E = N\langle\epsilon\rangle = N\,\Delta\,\frac{0.14M\,e^{-\Delta/kT}}{0.01M + 0.14M\,e^{-\Delta/kT}} \approx 14N\Delta\,e^{-\Delta/kT}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The heat capacity">

<Formula label="Heat capacity of the adsorbed atoms at low temperature" tex="C = \frac{\partial E}{\partial T} = 14N\Delta\,\frac{\Delta}{kT^2}\,e^{-\Delta/kT} = 14Nk\left(\frac{\Delta}{kT}\right)^2e^{-\Delta/kT}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2" />

(MIT's solutions write the derivative at constant area. The model has no other variable, so the plain derivative is meant.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The graph compares this with the heat capacity computed from the full $Z_1$ of part (a).

<Plot variable="t" domain={[0.03, 1.2]} range={[0, 6.2]} xLabel="kT / Δ" yLabel="C / Nk"

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Why stars shine" points={20} mit="Problem 5">

MIT's sheet opens with some history, retold here in our words. Relativity is usually linked with high energies and great distances, and quantum mechanics with small distances or low temperatures. Both links are too narrow. This problem shows that quantum mechanics is needed in an early stage of the life of a star: without it, stars could not shine.

In 1926 Arthur Eddington collected what was known about the inside of stars in his book *The Internal Constitution of the Stars*. The equivalence of mass and energy was well understood by then, and he concluded that the only possible source of the huge energy output of stars was the fusion of hydrogen nuclei into helium nuclei. Other physicists objected. Whatever the chain of reactions from hydrogen to helium, two nuclei must first come close together against the repulsion of their positive charges, and the temperature inside the Sun, taken as 40 million kelvin, seemed far too low for that. The four parts below work out how strong the objection was.

Use these numbers, in the units of MIT's sheet (centimetres, grams, seconds, with charges in esu, so that two charges $e$ at the distance $r$ have the potential energy $e^2/r$ in erg):

- the charge of a proton, $|e| = 4.8 \times 10^{-10}$ esu, and its radius, $R = 1.2 \times 10^{-13}$ cm;
- Boltzmann's constant, $k = 1.381 \times 10^{-16}$ erg/K, and the mass of a proton, $m = 1.67 \times 10^{-24}$ g (the values MIT's solutions use);
- the temperature $T = 4 \times 10^7$ K and the mass density 100 g/cm³ at the centre of the Sun.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Take the charge of a proton to be spread uniformly through a sphere of radius $R$. Find the smallest energy $E_{\min}$, in erg, that a second proton (taken as a point) must have to come within the distance $R$ of the centre of the first.

<Hint>

Outside a sphere with a charge spread evenly through it, the potential is the same as that of a point charge at its centre.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-emin" label="$E_{\min} =$" value="1.92e-6" unit="erg" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-emin" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The second proton must climb the Coulomb hill of the first, up to the distance $R$.

<Step title="The potential energy">

For $r \ge R$ the potential of the charged sphere is $|e|/r$, so the potential energy of the second proton is $e^2/r$. To reach $r = R$ it needs at least

<Formula label="Height of the Coulomb barrier between two protons, in erg" tex="E_{\min} = V(R) = \frac{e^2}{R} = \frac{\left(4.8 \times 10^{-10}\right)^2}{1.2 \times 10^{-13}} = 1.92 \times 10^{-6}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(a), p. 6" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

An esu squared divided by a centimetre is an erg. In the units of nuclear physics (our conversion) this is about 1.2 MeV, while $kT$ at $4 \times 10^7$ K is $5.52 \times 10^{-9}$ erg, about 3.4 keV: the barrier is some 350 times higher than $kT$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find the probability $p_+$ that the kinetic energy of a particle in an ideal gas is larger than $E_{\min}$, when $E_{\min}$ is much larger than $kT$. Use the density of the energy from Problem 4(a) of [Problem Set 2](/courses/8-044/8-044-problem-set-2#p4), and MIT's note:

<Formula label="An integral for a large lower limit" tex="\int_a^\infty\sqrt{y}\,e^{-y}\,dy \approx \sqrt{a}\,e^{-a} \qquad\text{when } a \gg 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 5" />

(In this formula $a$ is any large number, not the trap strength of Problem 3.) In the first box type `E` for $E_{\min}$.

Then put in the numbers. The result is far too small for a calculator, so give its logarithm to base 10.

<Hint>

$p_+$ is the integral of the density $p(E)$ from $E_{\min}$ to infinity. Substitute $y = E/kT$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the number, take the logarithm of each factor separately: $\log_{10}e^{-y} = -0.4343\,y$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-pplus" label="$p_+ \approx$" expr="2/sqrt(pi)*sqrt(E/(k*T))*exp(-E/(k*T))"
  vars={{ E: [3, 8], k: [1, 2], T: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ E: "E_{\\min}" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "2/sqrt(pi)*sqrt(E/(k*T))*exp(-E/(k*T))/(k*T)", hint: "That is the density p(E) at the energy E_min, which has the unit of one over an energy. A probability is its integral from E_min upwards: with y = E/kT the factor 1/kT is used up by dE." },

<Answer id="p5-logp" label="$\log_{10}p_+ =$ (1 decimal place)" value="-149.63" tolerance="0.1" calc="p5-logp" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Integrate the tail of the density of the energy. Far out in the tail the exponential falls so fast that the integral is the integrand at its lower limit.

<Step title="The tail">

From Problem Set 2 the density of the kinetic energy of a particle in a classical gas in three dimensions is $p(E) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\frac{1}{kT}\sqrt{E/kT}\;e^{-E/kT}$. With $y = E/kT$ and $y_{\min} = E_{\min}/kT$:

<Formula label="Probability of a kinetic energy above the barrier" tex="p_+ = \int_{E_{\min}}^\infty p(E)\,dE = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{y_{\min}}^\infty\sqrt{y}\,e^{-y}\,dy \approx \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\sqrt{\frac{E_{\min}}{kT}}\;e^{-E_{\min}/kT}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(b), p. 6" />

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

$E_{\min}/kT = 1.92 \times 10^{-6}/\left(1.381 \times 10^{-16} \times 4 \times 10^7\right) = 347.57$. The factor in front is $\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\sqrt{347.57} = 21.04$, with the logarithm 1.323. The exponential has the logarithm $-0.4343 \times 347.57 = -150.95$. Together:

<Formula label="Logarithm of the probability of a kinetic energy above the barrier" tex="\log_{10}p_+ = 1.323 - 150.95 = -149.63, \qquad p_+ \approx 2 \times 10^{-150}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(b), p. 6" />

MIT's solutions round this to $\log_{10}p_+ = -149.6$ and $p_+ = 0.2 \times 10^{-149}$. (MIT's sheet, in part (c), quotes the probability as $10^{-148}$: a rougher figure than its own solutions. On this scale the difference does not matter.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The integral can also be done exactly with the complementary error function: $p_+ = \text{erfc}\left(\sqrt{y_{\min}}\right) + \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\sqrt{y_{\min}}\;e^{-y_{\min}}$. For $y_{\min} = 347.6$ this is larger than the approximation by a factor 1.0014, close to $1 + 1/2y_{\min}$: the note on MIT's sheet is very good here.

</Step>

</Solution>

MIT's sheet remarks that this probability does seem small, but that its consequences should be checked to be sure. In a gas the mean speed $\langle v\rangle$, the mean free path $L$ and the mean free time $\tau$ are

<Solution>

**The idea.** Three formulas, used in turn.

<Step title="The numbers">

The mean speed is $\langle v\rangle = \sqrt{8 \times 1.381 \times 10^{-16} \times 4 \times 10^7/\left(\pi \times 1.67 \times 10^{-24}\right)} = 9.18 \times 10^7$ cm/s.

The cross-section is $\sigma = \pi\left(2.4 \times 10^{-13}\right)^2 = 1.81 \times 10^{-25}$ cm², and the number density is $n = 100/\left(1.67 \times 10^{-24}\right) = 5.99 \times 10^{25}$ protons per cm³. So

<Formula label="Mean free path and mean free time at the centre of the Sun" tex="L = \frac{1}{n\sigma} = 0.0923\ \text{cm}, \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{\langle v\rangle} = 1.01 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{s}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(c), p. 7" />

(MIT's solutions print $L = 9.22 \times 10^{-2}$ cm, from rounded factors.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A proton at the centre of the Sun moves at about 0.3% of the speed of light, so the formulas without relativity are good enough. It collides about a thousand million times each second.

<Part label="d" points={5}>

Now assume that in each collision two protons fuse with the probability $p_+$, whatever happened before. What is the mean time until a given proton fuses? Give the logarithm to base 10 of that time in seconds, and compare the time with the age of the universe, which MIT's sheet takes as $15 \times 10^9$ years.

The mass of the Sun is $2 \times 10^{33}$ g. If all of it were protons at the density of the centre, how many fusions would take place in the Sun each second? (Count, as MIT's solution does, the number of protons divided by the mean time to fusion of one proton.) Give the logarithm to base 10 of that number.

<Hint>

A rate is one over the mean time between events. If only a fraction $p_+$ of the collisions leads to fusion, how does the mean time to a fusion follow from the mean time between collisions?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The number of fusions per second in the whole Sun is the number of protons divided by the mean time to fusion of one proton (MIT's counting).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-logt" label="$\log_{10}$ of the mean time to fusion in seconds $=$ (1 decimal place)" value="140.63" tolerance="0.1" calc="p5-logt" />

<Answer id="p5-lograte" label="$\log_{10}$ of the number of fusions per second $=$" value="-83.55" tolerance="0.2" calc="p5-lograte" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The rate of fusion for one proton is its rate of collisions times $p_+$.

<Step title="The mean time to fusion">

<Formula label="Mean time until a proton fuses without tunnelling" tex="\tau_{\text{fusion}} = \frac{\tau}{p_+}, \qquad \log_{10}\tau_{\text{fusion}} = \log_{10}\left(1.01 \times 10^{-9}\right) + 149.63 = 140.63, \qquad \tau_{\text{fusion}} \approx 4 \times 10^{140}\ \text{s}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(d), p. 7" />

(MIT's solutions get $5 \times 10^{140}$ s, because they use $p_+$ rounded to $0.2 \times 10^{-149}$. Both values pass the box.)

<Step title="The age of the universe">

$15 \times 10^9$ years is $15 \times 10^9 \times 365 \times 24 \times 3600 = 4.7 \times 10^{17}$ s. The mean time to fusion is longer by a factor of about $10^{123}$. (The measured age of the universe is 13.8 billion years, which changes nothing here.)

</Step>

<Step title="The whole Sun">

The number of protons is $2 \times 10^{33}/\left(1.67 \times 10^{-24}\right) = 1.2 \times 10^{57}$. Each fuses once in the time $\tau_{\text{fusion}}$ on average, so the number of fusions per second is

<Formula label="Fusions per second in the Sun without tunnelling" tex="\frac{N_{\text{protons}}}{\tau_{\text{fusion}}}, \qquad \log_{10}\frac{N_{\text{protons}}}{\tau_{\text{fusion}}} = 57.08 - 140.63 = -83.55" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(d), p. 7" />

about $3 \times 10^{-84}$ per second (MIT's solutions: $2 \times 10^{-84}$, with the rounded $p_+$). A remark of ours: each fusion uses up two protons, so counting events would halve this. On this scale it does not matter.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In the whole age of the universe the Sun would have about $10^{-84} \times 10^{18} = 10^{-66}$ fusions: none at all. Classical physics leaves the Sun dark, and the objection to Eddington was a strong one.

</Step>

The rest of the story, as MIT's sheet tells it (pp. 6 and 7). Eddington knew these arguments and still held that fusion must be the source. In his book he answered the critic who finds the stars not hot enough: "we tell him to go and find a hotter place" (*The Internal Constitution of the Stars*, 1926, p. 301). The way out came from quantum mechanics. In 1928 Gamow showed that tunnelling through the Coulomb barrier explains the radioactive decay of nuclei, and in 1929 Atkinson and Houtermans showed that tunnelling in the other direction, from outside the barrier, lets fusion happen at the temperatures calculated for the inside of stars. Tunnelling through the Coulomb barrier is usually worked out with the WKB approximation, and MIT does not do that here.

MIT's sheet adds that the probability of tunnelling, put into the calculation of this problem, gives a fusion rate for the Sun that is too high. A realistic rate has three more factors, each of which lowers it. A proton that has entered a light nucleus is still far more likely to tunnel out again than to fuse. A full cycle needs several fusion events (five for the proton-proton chain, four for the carbon cycle). And only the protons in the central region of the Sun are hot and dense enough to take part. The sheet also notes, as a surprise, that Eddington's note to the 1930 printing of his book said that the advances since 1926 hardly called for a revision. The fusion cycles themselves were worked out by Hans Bethe from 1938, work for which, with his other contributions to the theory of nuclear reactions, he received the Nobel Prize in 1967.
