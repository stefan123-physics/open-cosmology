The microcanonical ensemble gave entropy, temperature and the second law. This lecture shows how the entropy, written as a function of the constraints, holds **all** of a system's thermodynamics, and works through a system small enough to count by hand: the two-level system (slides 1–19, numbered as printed, with 8a and 8b; notes pp. 7–9). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides, section 7 of his "Notes on the Microcanonical Ensemble" and his note on interstitial atoms; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## The combined first and second laws

Work on a system takes many forms (slide 1): $-P\,dV$ for a gas, surface tension times $dA$ for a surface film, $H\,dM$ for a magnet, $F\,dL$ for a wire, and so on. In general each is a **generalized force** $X_i$ times a change in an external parameter $x_i$:

<Formula label="Work in general" tex="\delta W = \sum_i X_i\,dx_i" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

In the microcanonical ensemble the internal energy $U$ is the fixed energy $E$, so the first law reads $dE = \delta Q + \delta W$. The constraints used to count $\Omega$, the energy and the extensive variables $V$, $M$, …, are a complete set of independent thermodynamic variables, so $S = k\ln\Omega = S(E, \{x_i\})$, which can be inverted to $E(S, \{x_i\})$ (slide 2). With no work, the first law gives $dE = \delta Q$ and the second law $\delta Q \le T\,dS$. Adding the work back (slide 3):

<Formula label="Combined first and second laws" tex="dE \le T\,dS + \delta W = T\,dS - P\,dV + \dots" cite="mit" at="slide 3" />

with equality for equilibrium (quasi-static, reversible) changes. Solving for $dS$ (slide 4):

<Formula label="The differential of the entropy" tex="dS = \frac1T\,dE + \frac PT\,dV - \frac HT\,dM - \dots, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial x_j}\right)_{E} = -\frac{X_j}{T}" cite="mit" at="slide 4" />

where the derivative with respect to $x_j$ holds $E$ and all the other $x_i$ fixed.

Two results follow (notes pp. 8–9). An isolated system ($dE = 0$ and every $dx_i = 0$) can only change in ways that raise its entropy or leave it unchanged, a restatement of the second law. And the bridge from statistical mechanics to thermodynamics is the entropy: knowing $S(E, \{x_i\})$, or equivalently $\Omega$, each derivative gives a generalized force, that is, an **equation of state**.

## Using S

Geometrically, $S(E, V)$ is a surface whose slopes along $E$ and $V$ are $1/T$ and $P/T$ (slide 5). In practice (slide 6):

- $\partial S/\partial E = 1/T$ gives $T(E, V, N)$, which inverted is the **energy** $E(T, V, N)$;
- $\partial S/\partial V = P/T$ gives $P(E, T, V, N)$, and with the energy, the **equation of state** $P(T, V, N)$.

**The ideal gas** (slide 7). With $S = kN\ln[V(4\pi emE/3N)^{3/2}]$ from the previous lecture,

<Formula label="Ideal gas law from the entropy" tex="\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_{E,N} = \frac{kN}{V} = \frac PT \quad\Longrightarrow\quad PV = NkT" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

## Counting

Two facts of combinatorics (slide 8a). $K$ distinguishable objects can be ordered in $K!$ ways. Choosing $L$ of them can be done in $K!/J!$ ways if the order matters and $K!/(L!\,J!)$ ways if it does not, where $J = K - L$ is the number left over. (Slide 8b illustrates with five chairs at a table: $5! = 120$ seatings of five people, $5!/2! = 60$ ways to seat three people, and $5!/(3!\,2!) = 10$ ways to choose the chairs for three place settings.)

## The two-level system

$N$ independent members, each in its ground state (energy $0$) or an excited state (energy $\epsilon$); $N_0$ in the lower and $N_1$ in the upper, with $N = N_0 + N_1$ and $E = \epsilon N_1$ (slide 9). Examples are molecules adsorbed on a surface, ions in a crystal that can sit in either of two wells, and systems in which only the two lowest-lying states matter. The levels are fixed, so **no work is possible**: only heat flows (slide 10).

The energy fixes $N_1$, and the states with that energy are the ways of choosing which members are excited: the unordered count of slide 8a with $K = N$, $L = N_1$ and $J = N_0$ (slide 11; the slide writes the factorials of $N_1$ and $N - N_1$ directly, which this page spells with $L$ and $J$ so that read-aloud can say them):

<Formula label="Number of states" tex="\Omega(E) = \frac{N!}{L!\,J!}, \quad L = N_1, \quad J = N_0; \qquad S(E) = k\ln\Omega(E)" cite="mit" at="slide 11" />

$\Omega$ is 1 when $N_1 = 0$ or $N$ and largest at $N_1 = N/2$. So $S(E)$ rises from zero, peaks at $E = \epsilon N/2$, and falls back to zero at $E = \epsilon N$. Where it rises, $1/T = \partial S/\partial E > 0$; at the peak $T = \pm\infty$; beyond it, $T < 0$.

With Stirling's approximation and $\partial N_1/\partial E = 1/\epsilon$ (slide 12),

<Formula label="Temperature of the two-level system" tex="\frac1T = \frac{\partial S}{\partial E} = \frac{k}{\epsilon}\ln\frac{N_0}{N_1} = \frac k\epsilon\ln\left(\frac N{N_1} - 1\right)" cite="mit" at="slide 12" />

and inverting (slide 13),

<Formula label="Occupation and energy" tex="N_1 = \frac{N}{e^{\epsilon/kT} + 1}, \qquad E = \epsilon N_1 = \frac{\epsilon N}{e^{\epsilon/kT} + 1}" cite="mit" at="slide 13" />

At low temperature $N_1/N \approx e^{-\epsilon/kT}$; at high temperature it approaches $\frac12$ from below. Positive temperatures never put more than half the members in the upper level. The heat capacity (slide 14):

<Formula label="Heat capacity of the two-level system" tex="C \equiv \frac{\partial E}{\partial T} = Nk\left(\frac{\epsilon}{kT}\right)^2\frac{e^{\epsilon/kT}}{\left(e^{\epsilon/kT} + 1\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

going as $Nk(\epsilon/kT)^2e^{-\epsilon/kT}$ at low $T$ and $\frac{Nk}4(\epsilon/kT)^2$ at high $T$: zero at both ends with a peak between, because once the levels are about equally filled extra heat has nowhere to go.

  caption="Computed from the formulas above: the fraction in the upper level rises towards one half, and the heat capacity per member peaks at about 0.44 near kT ≈ 0.42ε before falling away." />

**One member** (slides 15–17). The probability that one chosen member is in state $n$ ($n = 0$ or $1$) is the share of the system's states with that member in state $n$: remove it, so $N \to N - 1$ and $N_1 \to N_1 - n$, count, and divide by $\Omega$. The result is

<Formula label="Probabilities for one member" tex="p(0) = 1 - \frac{N_1}{N}, \qquad p(1) = \frac{N_1}{N} = \frac{1}{e^{\epsilon/kT} + 1}" cite="mit" at="slide 16" />

so $p(0) + p(1) = 1$ and $E = N[0 \cdot p(0) + \epsilon\,p(1)]$, which is the energy found before. Knowing $E$, one could have worked backwards to $p(1)$ (slide 17).

## Interstitial atoms

Greytak's notes add a second example (reading 2, pp. 1–3). A crystal has $N$ atoms on $N$ lattice sites and $M$ identical interstitial sites between them; moving an atom onto an interstitial site costs energy $\epsilon$, so $E = n\epsilon$ with $n$ displaced atoms. $\Omega$ is the number of ways to choose the $n$ vacated lattice sites times the number of ways to choose the $n$ occupied interstitial sites. Taking $\partial S/\partial E$ through $n$, as for the two-level system, and using $n \ll N, M$:

<Formula label="Number of interstitial atoms" tex="\frac{n^2}{(N - n)(M - n)} = e^{-\epsilon/kT} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n \approx \sqrt{NM}\,e^{-\epsilon/2kT}" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

The number falls exponentially with $\epsilon$, rises with $T$, and grows with $M$ at fixed $\epsilon$ and $T$: more interstitial sites mean more ways to arrange the atoms, an **entropy** effect that pulls atoms off their sites.

## The microcanonical scheme

Model the system, count $\Omega(E, N, V, \dots)$, then take $S = k\ln\Omega$ and its derivatives for the thermodynamics, and ratios of phase volumes for the microscopic probabilities (slide 18). This ensemble is the foundation, but it will no longer be used to solve problems: the course turns next to the second law and then derives the **canonical ensemble**, the real workhorse of statistical mechanics (slide 19).

<WorkedExample title="A two-level system and a crystal">
<Problem>
A two-level system has $\epsilon/k = 100$ K. (a) What fraction of members is excited at 100 K and at 50 K? (b) What is $C/Nk$ at 100 K? (c) The system is prepared with three quarters of its members excited. What is its temperature? (d) Separately, in a crystal with $M = N$ and $\epsilon = 1$ eV, what fraction of atoms sits on interstitial sites at 1000 K ($k = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/K)?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Occupations">
At 100 K, $\epsilon/kT = 1$ and $N_1/N = 1/(e + 1) \approx 0.269$. At 50 K, $\epsilon/kT = 2$ and $N_1/N = 1/(e^2 + 1) \approx 0.119$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Heat capacity">
$C/Nk = 1^2 \times e/(e + 1)^2 \approx 2.718/13.83 \approx 0.197$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Negative temperature">
$N/N_1 - 1 = \frac43 - 1 = \frac13$, so $\epsilon/kT = \ln\frac13 \approx -1.099$ and $T \approx 100/(-1.099) \approx -91.0$ K. Such a system is hotter than any positive temperature: in contact with one, it gives up energy.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Interstitials">
$kT = 0.08617$ eV, so $\epsilon/2kT \approx 5.80$ and $n/N \approx e^{-5.80} \approx 3.0 \times 10^{-3}$, small enough for the approximation $n \ll N$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Combined laws: $dE \le T\,dS + \delta W$; solving for $dS$, every derivative of $S(E, \{x_i\})$ is a thermodynamic variable: $\partial S/\partial E = 1/T$, $\partial S/\partial V = P/T$, $\partial S/\partial x_j = -X_j/T$.
- From $S$ alone come the energy $E(T, V, N)$ and the equation of state; for the ideal gas, $PV = NkT$.
- Two-level system: $\Omega = N!/(L!\,J!)$ with $L = N_1$ and $J = N_0$, $N_1 = N/(e^{\epsilon/kT} + 1)$, a heat-capacity peak, and $T < 0$ when more than half are excited.
- Interstitial atoms: $n \approx \sqrt{NM}\,e^{-\epsilon/2kT}$, with an entropy effect from the number of sites.
- The microcanonical ensemble is the foundation; the canonical ensemble will do the work from here.
