MIT's sheet gives no points. The points here (30 in all) and their split between parts are ours. Most of this problem is "show that" and "explain why": for those, open the solution, compare it with your own work and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons: $\Phi(E, V, N)$ is the cumulative volume in phase space (the volume of all states with energy below $E$), $S$ the entropy, $k$ Boltzmann's constant and $m$ the mass of one atom. The letter $e$ inside the logarithms is the number 2.718…, left there by Stirling's approximation. The first law is written as in the lesson on [the first law](/courses/8-044/first-law), $\Delta E = \Delta Q + \Delta W$, with $\Delta W$ the work done on the gas. For the two number boxes you need Boltzmann's constant, $k = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K, and the number of atoms in one mole, $6.02214 \times 10^{23}$.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Correct Boltzmann counting" points={30} mit="Problem 1">

The way the lessons have counted the accessible volume in phase space of a classical system so far has a flaw. In parts (a) and (b) you find two wrong results that follow from it. In part (c) you repair it.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

A state variable $F$ is called **extensive** if the following test works. Multiply every extensive variable in the expression for $F$ by a scale factor $\lambda$ and leave every intensive variable as it is. Then $F$ must become $\lambda F$. (The lesson on [thermodynamic variables](/courses/8-044/thermodynamic-variables) says the same thing in words: an extensive variable is proportional to the amount of material.)

For the ideal monatomic gas the microcanonical ensemble gave

<Formula label="Cumulative phase-space volume found so far" tex="\Phi(E, V, N) = V^N\left(\frac{4\pi emE}{3N}\right)^{3N/2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

Use $S = k\ln\Phi$ and the result $E = \frac{3}{2}NkT$ to write the entropy as a function of $N$, $V$ and $T$. Entropy should be extensive on physical grounds: two equal samples of gas side by side have twice the entropy of one. Show that this $S(N, V, T)$ fails the test.

As a check of your work (our addition), give the amount by which it fails, $S(\lambda N, \lambda V, T) - \lambda\,S(N, V, T)$, in terms of `lam`, `N` and `k`. Then put in numbers: one mole of atoms in a volume $V$ is compared with two moles in $2V$ at the same temperature. By how many joules per kelvin does the entropy of the larger sample exceed twice that of the smaller one?

<Hint>

Replace $E$ first. What is left inside the bracket once $E = \frac{3}{2}NkT$ is put in? Does it still contain $N$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The logarithm of a product is a sum of logarithms, and the logarithm of an $N$-th power is $N$ times the logarithm. You get one term with $\ln V$ and one with the logarithm of the bracket.

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write $S$ as a sum of terms and scale each one. A term passes the test if it is $N$ times something intensive. A bare $\ln V$ is not intensive.

<Step title="Put in the energy">

With $E = \frac{3}{2}NkT$ the bracket loses its $N$: $4\pi emE/3N = 2\pi emkT$. So

<Formula label="Phase-space volume in terms of the temperature" tex="\Phi = V^N\left[2\pi emkT\right]^{3N/2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Take the logarithm">

<Formula label="Entropy found so far, as a function of N, V and T" tex="S(N, V, T) = k\ln\Phi = Nk\ln V + \frac{3}{2}Nk\ln\left[2\pi emkT\right]" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Scale the extensive variables">

$N$ and $V$ are extensive, $T$ is intensive. So let $N \to \lambda N$ and $V \to \lambda V$ and keep $T$:

<Formula label="The scaled entropy" tex="S(\lambda N, \lambda V, T) = \lambda Nk\ln(\lambda V) + \lambda\,\frac{3}{2}Nk\ln\left[2\pi emkT\right]" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

The second term is $\lambda$ times what it was. The first is not, because $\lambda Nk\ln(\lambda V)$ differs from $\lambda Nk\ln V$. So $S$ does not become $\lambda S$, and the failure sits in the volume term. This is where MIT's solution stops.

</Step>

<Step title="How large is the failure (our addition)">

Writing $\ln(\lambda V) = \ln\lambda + \ln V$ in the first term shows what is left over:

<Formula label="Amount by which the old entropy fails the test" tex="S(\lambda N, \lambda V, T) - \lambda\,S(N, V, T) = \lambda Nk\ln\lambda" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (MIT shows that the first term fails; the difference is our addition)" />

For one mole, $Nk = 6.02214 \times 10^{23} \times 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K $= 8.314$ J/K. With $\lambda = 2$ the excess is $2 \times 8.314 \times \ln 2 = 11.53$ J/K.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\lambda = 1$ nothing is scaled and the difference is $1 \cdot Nk\ln 1 = 0$, as it must be. The difference does not contain $V$, $T$ or $m$: it depends only on how many atoms there are and on the scale factor. And $Nk$ has the units of $k$, joules per kelvin, as an entropy should. Keep the number 11.53 J/K in mind: the same amount comes back in part (b) as the entropy of mixing of two moles.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={12}>

Now a mixing experiment with two ideal gases, called 1 and 2. A box of volume $V$ is divided by a movable partition into $V_1 = \alpha V$ and $V_2 = (1 - \alpha)V$, where $\alpha$ lies between 0 and 1. The $N_1$ atoms of gas 1 are in $V_1$ and the $N_2$ atoms of gas 2 are in $V_2$. (MIT's sketch shows the box before, with the two gases side by side at temperature $T$, and after, with both gases spread over all of $V$, still at $T$ because the gases are ideal.)

**First**, show that if the temperature and the pressure are the same on both sides, the ideal gas law demands $N_1 = \alpha N$ and $N_2 = (1 - \alpha)N$, where $N = N_1 + N_2$.

**Second**, pull the partition out. If the gases are different they mix, and this cannot be undone by putting the partition back: the mixing is irreversible. Entropy is a state function, though, so the change $\Delta S_i \equiv S_i(\text{final}) - S_i(\text{initial})$ of each gas can be found from the expression of part (a). Show that

<Formula label="Entropy of mixing to be shown" tex="\Delta S_1 = \alpha Nk\ln\frac{1}{\alpha}, \qquad \Delta S_T \equiv \Delta S_1 + \Delta S_2 = Nk\left[\alpha\ln\frac{1}{\alpha} + (1 - \alpha)\ln\frac{1}{1 - \alpha}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

This **entropy of mixing** is never negative, as the picture of entropy as disorder leads us to expect. As a check of your work (our addition), give $\Delta S_2$ in terms of `alpha`, `N` and `k`, and the value of $\Delta S_T$ in joules per kelvin for one mole of atoms in all ($N = 6.02214 \times 10^{23}$) with $\alpha = 0.25$.

**Third**, let the two gases be the same. Should the total entropy rise when the partition is slid out? Intuition does not decide this easily, because the partition does limit where each atom can go. Thermodynamics does decide it: $\Delta S_T$ must be zero. To see why, explain why the internal energy does not change, $\Delta E = 0$, and why no work is done, $\Delta W = 0$. (MIT's second sketch shows the partition pulled straight out of the box along its own plane, and pushed back in the same way.) The first law then gives $\Delta Q = 0$. With the same gas on both sides, sliding the partition out and in again is a reversible process, so $\Delta S = \Delta Q/T = 0$. The calculation above gave a $\Delta S$ that is not zero, and it never used the fact that the gases were different. This contradiction is known as **Gibbs' paradox**.

<Hint>

For the first task, write $P = NkT/V$ for each side and set the two pressures equal. The temperatures are equal too, so they cancel.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the second task, look at what changes for gas 1 alone: its atoms, $N_1$ of them, go from the volume $V_1$ to the volume $V$ at the same temperature. Which term of $S(N, V, T)$ notices that?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the third task, ask what the energy of an ideal gas depends on, and whether any force opposes the partition as it is pulled out.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-work" label="Sliding the partition out does no work on the gas because"
  choices={["the temperature stays the same, and work on a gas always changes its temperature", "an ideal gas exerts no force on a wall", "the pressure is the same on both of its faces and it moves in its own plane, so no force opposes the motion and no volume is swept", "the energy does not change, and without a change of energy there can be no work"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each ideal gas ignores the other. Removing the partition is, for each gas on its own, an expansion into a larger volume at the same temperature, and only the volume term of its entropy changes.

<Step title="The numbers of atoms">

The pressure is the same on both sides, and so is the temperature:

<Formula label="Equal pressures on the two sides" tex="P = \frac{N_1kT}{\alpha V} = \frac{N_2kT}{(1 - \alpha)V} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_2 = \frac{1 - \alpha}{\alpha}\,N_1" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

Then $N = N_1 + N_2 = N_1\left(1 + \frac{1 - \alpha}{\alpha}\right) = N_1/\alpha$, which gives

<Formula label="Atoms on each side" tex="N_1 = \alpha N, \qquad N_2 = (1 - \alpha)N" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

Equal pressure and temperature mean equal number density on the two sides, so each side holds atoms in proportion to its volume.

</Step>

<Step title="The entropy change of each gas">

The gases are ideal: their atoms do not interact, the energy of each gas stays the same, and so does the temperature. The term with $\ln\left[2\pi emkT\right]$ in the entropy of each gas is unchanged, and only the volume term counts:

<Formula label="Entropy changes of the two gases" tex="\Delta S_1 = N_1k\ln V - N_1k\ln(\alpha V) = \alpha Nk\ln\frac{1}{\alpha}, \qquad \Delta S_2 = N_2k\ln V - N_2k\ln\left[(1 - \alpha)V\right] = (1 - \alpha)Nk\ln\frac{1}{1 - \alpha}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The entropy of mixing">

<Formula label="Entropy of mixing" tex="\Delta S_T = \Delta S_1 + \Delta S_2 = Nk\left[\alpha\ln\frac{1}{\alpha} + (1 - \alpha)\ln\frac{1}{1 - \alpha}\right] > 0" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2" />

Every factor is positive when $\alpha$ lies strictly between 0 and 1: $\alpha$ and $1 - \alpha$ are positive, and $1/\alpha$ and $1/(1 - \alpha)$ are larger than 1, so their logarithms are positive. For two different gases this is the right answer.

For one mole in all and $\alpha = 0.25$ (our numbers), the bracket is $0.25\ln 4 + 0.75\ln\frac{4}{3} = 0.5623$ and $\Delta S_T = 8.314 \times 0.5623 = 4.676$ J/K. The graph (ours) shows the bracket for every $\alpha$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 1]} range={[0, 0.8]} xLabel="α" yLabel="ΔS_T / Nk"
  curves={[
    { expr: "x <= 0 ? 0 : (x >= 1 ? 0 : -(x*log(x) + (1 - x)*log(1 - x)))", label: "entropy of mixing" }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 0.5, y: 0.6931, label: "ln 2" }, { x: 0.25, y: 0.5623, label: "0.5623" }]}
  caption="The entropy of mixing in units of Nk as a function of α, the share of the volume held by gas 1, computed from the formula above. It is zero when one of the gases is absent (α = 0 or 1) and largest, ln 2 = 0.6931, when the two sides are equal. The second point is the example of the text, α = 0.25." />

</Step>

<Step title="The same gas on both sides">

Nothing in the calculation used that the gases differ, so it gives the same positive $\Delta S_T$ for one gas. Thermodynamics says otherwise.

- $\Delta E = 0$. The energy of an ideal gas depends on its temperature and the number of atoms, not on its volume, $E = \frac{3}{2}NkT$. The temperature is the same before and after, and so is the number of atoms.
- $\Delta W = 0$. Without friction nothing resists the partition as it slides in and out. The gas pushes equally on its two faces, and the partition moves along its own plane, so the volume of the box does not change.
- The first law, $\Delta E = \Delta Q + \Delta W$, then gives $\Delta Q = 0$.
- With the same gas on both sides the partition can be put back, and the gas is in the state it started in: the process is reversible. For a reversible process $\Delta S = \Delta Q/T$, so $\Delta S = 0$.

The count of states gives $\Delta S_T > 0$, thermodynamics gives $\Delta S_T = 0$. That is Gibbs' paradox.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The bracket is unchanged when $\alpha$ and $1 - \alpha$ are swapped, as it should be: it cannot matter which gas is called 1. It vanishes for $\alpha \to 0$ and $\alpha \to 1$, when there is only one gas and nothing to mix. For $\alpha = \frac{1}{2}$ it is $\ln 2$: each atom has twice the room it had, and $\Delta S_T = Nk\ln 2$. For two moles in all that is $2 \times 8.314 \times \ln 2 = 11.53$ J/K, exactly the excess found in part (a). The two faults are one fault: the old entropy of two moles in $2V$ is larger than that of two separate moles by the entropy of mixing them.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={10}>

One might blame the trouble on the use of classical instead of quantum mechanics. But at high temperature and low density $N/V$ a classical calculation should be right, and this one is not.

What is missing is an idea that is central in quantum mechanics without being a result of it: identical particles cannot be told apart. One argon atom is the same as any other argon atom of the same isotope. To specify the state of $N$ atoms it does not matter *which* atom has the momentum $\vec p$ at the place $\vec q$, only that *some* atom does. Our count took every relabelling of the atoms as a new state, so it counted each meaningful state $N!$ times, where $N$ is the number of identical particles. The cure is to divide the phase-space volumes found so far ($\Omega$, and with it $\Phi$ and the density of states) by $N!$. This is called **correct Boltzmann counting**.

Show by direct calculation that it removes the faults found in parts (a) and (b). Take $N$ so large that terms of order $N$ can be dropped next to terms of order $N\ln N$.

As a check of your work (our addition): the new entropy has the form $S = [\ \dots\ ] + \frac{3}{2}Nk\ln\left[2\pi emkT\right]$. Give the term in the bracket, in terms of `N`, `V` and `k`. Follow MIT here and drop the term $kN$ that Stirling's approximation leaves behind.

<Hint>

Dividing $\Phi$ by $N!$ subtracts $k\ln N!$ from the entropy. Stirling's approximation, quoted in the lesson on the microcanonical ensemble, turns $\ln N!$ into something you can combine with $N\ln V$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For part (a): after the change, is the argument of the logarithm extensive or intensive?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For part (b) with one gas: before the partition is removed there are two separate samples, each with its own number of atoms and its own volume. Write the volume term of the new entropy for each and add them. Then write it for the single sample of $N$ atoms in $V$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-diff" label="With correct counting, mixing two different gases gives"
  choices={["no entropy of mixing at all", "the same entropy of mixing as in part (b)", "half the entropy of mixing of part (b)", "the entropy of mixing of part (b) minus Nk ln N"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The factorial puts $N$ under the volume inside the logarithm. The entropy then depends on the volume per atom, $V/N$, which is intensive, and both faults disappear together.

<Step title="The new entropy">

<Formula label="Phase-space volume with correct Boltzmann counting" tex="\Phi = \frac{V^N}{N!}\left[2\pi emkT\right]^{3N/2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2" />

With Stirling's approximation, $\ln N! \approx N\ln N - N$:

<Formula label="Entropy with correct Boltzmann counting" tex="S(N, V, T) = Nk\ln V - Nk\ln N + kN + \frac{3}{2}Nk\ln\left[2\pi emkT\right] \approx Nk\ln\frac{V}{N} + \frac{3}{2}Nk\ln\left[2\pi emkT\right]" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2" />

MIT drops the term $kN$ because it is small next to $Nk\ln N$. (Keeping it would change nothing below: $kN$ is itself $N$ times a constant, so it is extensive, and it cancels in every difference taken at a fixed number of atoms.)

</Step>

<Step title="The entropy is now extensive">

Let $N \to \lambda N$ and $V \to \lambda V$ and keep $T$. The ratio $V/N$ does not change, so

<Formula label="Scaling the new entropy" tex="S(\lambda N, \lambda V, T) = \lambda Nk\ln\frac{V}{N} + \lambda\,\frac{3}{2}Nk\ln\left[2\pi emkT\right] = \lambda\,S(N, V, T)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2" />

The fault of part (a) is gone.

</Step>

<Step title="The same gas no longer mixes with itself">

Only the volume term matters, since the temperature does not change. With the partition in place there are two samples of the same gas, $\alpha N$ atoms in $\alpha V$ and $(1 - \alpha)N$ atoms in $(1 - \alpha)V$. Without it there is one sample, $N$ atoms in $V$. MIT sets the old and the new counting side by side:

| Volume term of the entropy | With the partition | Without the partition |
|---|---|---|
| Old counting | $\alpha Nk\ln(\alpha V) + (1 - \alpha)Nk\ln\left[(1 - \alpha)V\right]$, which is $Nk\left[\alpha\ln\alpha + (1 - \alpha)\ln(1 - \alpha) + \ln V\right]$ | $Nk\ln V$ |
| New counting | $\alpha Nk\ln\dfrac{\alpha V}{\alpha N} + (1 - \alpha)Nk\ln\dfrac{(1 - \alpha)V}{(1 - \alpha)N}$, which is $Nk\ln\dfrac{V}{N}$ | $Nk\ln\dfrac{V}{N}$ |

In the old counting the two columns differ by the entropy of mixing of part (b), because $\alpha\ln\alpha + (1 - \alpha)\ln(1 - \alpha)$ is not zero. In the new counting they are equal: $\Delta S_T = 0$, as thermodynamics demands. (Two slips in MIT's table, p. 3: a brace marks $\alpha\ln\alpha + (1 - \alpha)\ln(1 - \alpha)$ with a plus sign, although both logarithms are negative and so is the sum; and the first line of the new counting prints the second term without its factor $Nk$. Neither changes MIT's conclusion.)

This is the same fact as in the previous step, seen again. The two samples have the same $V/N$, and an extensive entropy of two samples with the same intensive variables is the sum of the two.

</Step>

<Step title="Two different gases still mix (our addition)">

MIT's solution stops at one gas. The cure should not remove the entropy of mixing where it belongs, so check two different gases. Atoms of gas 1 can be told from atoms of gas 2, so the count is divided by the factorial of $N_1$ and by the factorial of $N_2$, both before and after the partition is removed. The volume term of gas 1 goes from $N_1k\ln(V_1/N_1)$ to $N_1k\ln(V/N_1)$: its $N_1$ atoms now have the volume $V$. The $\ln N_1$ is the same before and after and cancels:

<Formula label="Mixing of two different gases with correct counting" tex="\Delta S_1 = N_1k\ln\frac{V}{N_1} - N_1k\ln\frac{V_1}{N_1} = \alpha Nk\ln\frac{1}{\alpha}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b) and (c), p. 2 (MIT's formulas; applying the new counting to two different gases is our addition)" />

The same holds for gas 2, so $\Delta S_T$ is that of part (b). The difference between the two cases is the factorial: one for each kind of atom when there are two kinds, the single $N!$ when there is one.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with exact factorials (our addition)">

Stirling's approximation hides how good the cancellation is. For the same gas with $\alpha = \frac{1}{2}$ the temperature terms cancel, and with exact factorials the phase-space volumes give

<Formula label="Same gas, equal halves, exact factorials" tex="\frac{\Delta S_T}{k} = \ln\frac{V^N}{N!} - 2\ln\frac{(V/2)^{N/2}}{(N/2)!} = \ln\frac{2^N\left[(N/2)!\right]^2}{N!} \approx \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{\pi N}{2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own check, from MIT's phase-space volume)" />

For $N = 100$ the exact value is $\ln 12.56 = 2.531$ and the approximation gives $2.528$, while the old counting gives $N\ln 2 = 69.31$. For one mole the remainder is $\frac{1}{2}\ln(\pi N/2) = 27.6$ in units of $k$, which is $3.8 \times 10^{-22}$ J/K, next to the $5.763$ J/K ($Nk\ln 2$) of the old counting. So $\Delta S_T$ is not exactly zero but of order $k\ln N$, far below the terms of order $N$ that were dropped. The remainder has a meaning: the ratio $N!/(2^N[(N/2)!]^2)$ is the probability that the atoms of the open box are split exactly half and half, and closing the partition fixes that split.
