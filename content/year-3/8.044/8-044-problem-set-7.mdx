## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `2*K*exp(-A/(k*T))`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Type `sqrt(...)` for a square root and `exp(...)` for an exponential. Write the symbols as the box names them: `Ti` for $T_i$, `V0` for $V_0$, `TH` and `TC` for $T_H$ and $T_C$, `C0` for $C_0$. Capital and small letters are different symbols: in Problem 4, `k` is Boltzmann's constant and `K` is the stiffness of the wire.

The points per problem and per part are ours. Parts that ask for an argument have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons: $E$ is the internal energy, $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, and a small amount of heat is written $\delta Q$. Two letters change their meaning between problems, as on MIT's sheet: $a$ and $b$ are the constants of the gas in Problem 1 and the constants of the two heat capacities in Problem 3. MIT's sheet calls the kinetic energy of the pendulum $T$, the same letter as the temperature; here $T$ is always the temperature and the kinetic energy is written out. The final temperature of Problem 1 is written $T_{\text{end}}$ here, where MIT's solutions write T with a small f, close to the capital F of the final temperature in Problem 2, to keep the two apart when the page is read aloud.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Free expansion of a gas" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

A classical gas of $N$ atoms (single atoms, not molecules) is not ideal. Its equation of state is

<Formula label="Equation of state of the gas" tex="P(T, V) = \frac{NkT}{V - bN} - a\left(\frac{N}{V}\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

where $a$ and $b$ are positive constants. The term $-bN$ takes away the volume that the other atoms occupy. The term $-a(N/V)^2$ lowers the pressure because the atoms attract each other at long range (the van der Waals attraction). Neither term changes the heat capacity at constant volume, which stays that of an ideal gas of single atoms, $C_V = \frac32Nk$.

The gas is in a container of negligible mass that is isolated from its surroundings. A partition keeps the gas in one third of the container's volume $V_0$, where it is in equilibrium at the temperature $T_i$. Then a hole is opened in the partition. The gas streams into the empty part, an irreversible process, and in the end fills the whole volume $V_0$. (MIT's sheet shows the container before, with the gas in the left third, and after, with the gas everywhere.)

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Which thermodynamic quantities are conserved in this process? In the box, choose the quantity of the gas (other than the number of atoms) that is the same before and after.

<Hint>

Use the first law. Does heat enter the gas? Is work done on it? Remember that the gas pushes against nothing when it flows into the empty part.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-conserved" label="Conserved in the expansion:" choices={["the temperature: an expansion into a vacuum never changes it", "the entropy: no heat flows into the gas", "the internal energy: no heat flows in and no work is done", "the pressure times the volume: the gas has no time to exchange heat"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The first law keeps the books: the energy of the gas changes only by heat and by work.

<Step title="No heat, no work">

The container is isolated, so no heat enters: $Q = 0$. The walls do not move and the gas expands into empty space, so nothing does work on the gas: $W = 0$. Then

<Formula label="First law for the free expansion" tex="\Delta E = Q + W = 0" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

The internal energy is conserved. So is the number of atoms.

</Step>

<Step title="Check: what is not conserved">

The entropy is not conserved, although no heat flows: the process is irreversible, and $dS = \delta Q/T$ holds only for slow, reversible changes. The entropy of the isolated gas rises. Whether the temperature changes is the question of part (b): for an ideal gas it does not (the lesson on the [first law](/courses/8-044/first-law)), for this gas it does.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={15}>

Find the temperature $T_{\text{end}}$ of the gas once it has come to equilibrium again in the volume $V_0$.

The first box is our addition, a step on the way: how the energy of this gas changes with its volume at fixed temperature. In the second box use the letters $T_i$, $a$, $N$, $k$ and $V_0$.

<Hint>

The energy is a function of state, so compare $E(T, V)$ in the two equilibrium states: before and after. You need to know how $E$ depends on both $T$ and $V$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Start from $dE = T\,dS - P\,dV$ and write $dS$ in terms of $dT$ and $dV$. The coefficient of $dT$ is the heat capacity $C_V$. The coefficient of $dV$ holds the derivative of $S$ with respect to $V$ at fixed $T$, which a Maxwell relation turns into a derivative of the pressure.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate $dE$ to get $E(T, V)$ up to a constant, then set the energy at $(T_i, V_0/3)$ equal to the energy at $(T_{\text{end}}, V_0)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-dedv" label="$\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial V}\right)_T =$" expr="a*N^2/V^2"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 1.5], N: [1, 2], V: [3, 6] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "0", hint: "That is true of an ideal gas. Here the pressure has a second term, which T(∂P/∂T) − P does not cancel." },
    { expr: "-a*N^2/V^2", hint: "Check the sign of T(∂P/∂T) − P: the pressure holds −a(N/V)², and it is subtracted. The energy must rise with the volume, because pulling attracting atoms apart costs energy." },
    { expr: "a*N/V^2", hint: "Check the power of N: the attraction term of the pressure is a(N/V)², with N squared." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-tf" label="$T_{\text{end}} =$" expr="Ti - 4*a*N/(3*k*V0)"
  vars={{ Ti: [3, 5], a: [0.5, 1.5], N: [1, 2], k: [0.5, 1.5], V0: [3, 6] }}
  names={{ Ti: "T_i", V0: "V_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Ti", hint: "That is the result for an ideal gas, whose energy depends on the temperature alone. The energy of this gas also depends on its volume." },
    { expr: "Ti + 4*a*N/(3*k*V0)", hint: "Check the sign. The atoms attract each other, so moving them apart raises their potential energy, and with the total energy fixed the gas must cool." },
    { expr: "Ti - 2*a*N/(k*V0)", hint: "You divided the change of the potential energy by Nk. The heat capacity of the gas is (3/2)Nk." },
    { expr: "Ti - 2*a*N/(3*k*V0)", hint: "Check the volume at the start: it is V₀/3, so the term aN²/V is three times its final size there, and the difference is 2aN²/V₀." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The energy is the same before and after, and it is a function of state. Find $E(T, V)$ from the equation of state and the heat capacity, then solve for the final temperature.

<Step title="The differential of the energy">

Write the entropy as a function of $T$ and $V$ in the combined first and second laws:

<Formula label="Energy in terms of temperature and volume" tex="dE = T\,dS - P\,dV = T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_V dT + \left[T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T - P\right]dV" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

The coefficient of $dT$ is the heat capacity at constant volume, $C_V = \frac32Nk$. For the coefficient of $dV$, the Maxwell relation that comes from the Helmholtz free energy (see the [lesson](/courses/8-044/maxwell-relations)) gives

<Formula label="Maxwell relation applied to the gas" tex="\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V = \frac{Nk}{V - bN}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

Multiplied by $T$ this is the first term of the pressure. Subtracting $P$ leaves only the attraction term, with its sign reversed:

<Formula label="How the energy of the gas changes" tex="\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial V}\right)_T = T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V - P = a\left(\frac NV\right)^2, \qquad dE = \frac32Nk\,dT + a\left(\frac NV\right)^2dV" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

This is the first box. The constant $b$ has dropped out: taking volume away from the atoms does not change the energy.

</Step>

<Step title="The energy function">

Each term depends on one variable only, so the integration is direct:

<Formula label="Energy of the gas" tex="E(T, V) = \frac32NkT - \frac{aN^2}{V} + \text{constant}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

The first term is the kinetic energy of the atoms. The second is the potential energy of their attraction: it is negative, and it is larger in size when the gas is dense.

</Step>

<Step title="Before and after">

Set the energy at $(T_i, V_0/3)$ equal to the energy at $(T_{\text{end}}, V_0)$. The constant cancels.

<Formula label="Energy balance of the free expansion" tex="\frac32NkT_i - \frac{aN^2}{V_0/3} = \frac32NkT_{\text{end}} - \frac{aN^2}{V_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac32Nk\left(T_{\text{end}} - T_i\right) = -\frac{2aN^2}{V_0}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

<Formula label="Final temperature of the gas" tex="T_{\text{end}} = T_i - \frac43\,\frac ak\,\frac{N}{V_0}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

The gas cools. The atoms move apart against their attraction, the potential energy rises by $2aN^2/V_0$, and the kinetic energy pays for it.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $aN^2/V$ is an energy (it is a term of $E$), so $aN/(kV_0)$ is an energy per atom divided by $k$, a temperature. Limit: with $a = 0$ the temperature does not change, as for the ideal gas. The result holds while the drop is small compared with $T_i$: the equation of state is a model for a gas, and it does not describe a gas cooled until it turns liquid.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Use of a Carnot cycle" points={20} mit="Problem 2">

Two identical bodies have the same heat capacity $C_0$, which does not depend on temperature. At the start they are at different temperatures, $T_H$ and $T_C$ (with $T_H > T_C$). A Carnot engine is run between them, in a great many very small cycles, until both bodies have the same temperature $T_F$.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Find $T_F$ in terms of $T_H$ and $T_C$.

<Hint>

The two bodies are not heat baths: each small cycle takes a little heat from the hot body, which cools a little, and gives a little heat to the cold body, which warms a little. During one small cycle the two temperatures are nearly fixed.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A Carnot cycle is reversible. What does that say about the entropy lost by the hot body and the entropy gained by the cold body in one small cycle? Write each as $C_0\,dT/T$ and add up from the start to the end.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-tf" label="$T_F =$" expr="sqrt(TH*TC)"
  vars={{ TH: [300, 600], TC: [100, 250] }}
  names={{ TH: "T_H", TC: "T_C" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(TH + TC)/2", hint: "That is the final temperature when the bodies are simply put in contact and all the energy stays in them. Here some energy leaves as work. What stays the same is the total entropy." },
    { expr: "TH*TC", hint: "Check the units: that is a temperature squared." },
    { expr: "2*TH*TC/(TH + TC)", hint: "Add up dS = C₀ dT/T for each body: the integrals are logarithms, and the sum of the two logarithms must be zero." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The engine is reversible, so the total entropy of the two bodies does not change. That one condition fixes the final temperature.

<Step title="One small cycle">

In a small Carnot cycle the entropy that leaves the hot body equals the entropy that enters the cold body: $dS_H = -dS_C$. A body with heat capacity $C_0$ whose temperature changes by $dT$ takes in the heat $\delta Q = C_0\,dT$, so its entropy changes by $C_0\,dT/T$.

</Step>

<Step title="Adding up">

The hot body goes from $T_H$ down to $T_F$, the cold body from $T_C$ up to $T_F$:

<Formula label="Entropy balance of the two bodies" tex="\int_{T_H}^{T_F}\frac{C_0\,dT}{T} = -\int_{T_C}^{T_F}\frac{C_0\,dT}{T} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln\frac{T_F}{T_H} = -\ln\frac{T_F}{T_C} = \ln\frac{T_C}{T_F}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

<Formula label="Common final temperature" tex="\frac{T_F}{T_H} = \frac{T_C}{T_F} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_F = \sqrt{T_HT_C}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

The final temperature is the geometric mean of the two starting temperatures.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is a temperature, it lies between $T_C$ and $T_H$, and for $T_H = T_C$ it gives that same temperature. A number: bodies at 400 K and 100 K end at $\sqrt{400 \times 100}$ K $= 200$ K. Put in contact with no engine they would end at the ordinary mean, 250 K (our comparison). The engine leaves them colder, because it carries energy away as work.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Find the total work that the engine does on the outside world in this process. Is it positive or negative?

<Hint>

Over a whole number of cycles the engine itself returns to its starting state. So the work it delivers is the heat it took from the hot body minus the heat it gave to the cold body.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The heat that a body with a constant heat capacity gives up or takes in is $C_0$ times its change of temperature. Use your $T_F$ and look for a perfect square.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-w" label="$W_{\text{out}} =$" expr="C0*(sqrt(TH) - sqrt(TC))^2"
  vars={{ C0: [1, 3], TH: [300, 600], TC: [100, 250] }}
  names={{ C0: "C_0", TH: "T_H", TC: "T_C" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "C0*(TH - TC)", hint: "That is the heat the hot body would give up if it cooled all the way to T_C. It stops at T_F, and part of the heat it gives up goes into the cold body." },
    { expr: "C0*(TH + TC - sqrt(TH*TC))", hint: "Both bodies end at T_F, so T_F comes in twice: once for the heat out of the hot body and once for the heat into the cold body." },
    { expr: "-C0*(sqrt(TH) - sqrt(TC))^2", hint: "That is the work done on the engine. The question asks for the work the engine does on the outside world." },
    { expr: "C0*(sqrt(TH) + sqrt(TC))^2", hint: "Check the sign of the middle term: T_F is subtracted twice. For equal starting temperatures the work must be zero." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-sign" label="The work done on the outside world is" choices={["negative: work must be supplied to bring two bodies to the same temperature", "positive: the hot body gives up more heat than the cold body takes in", "zero: a reversible engine wastes nothing, so nothing is left over"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Energy is conserved: what the hot body loses and the cold body does not gain has left as work.

<Step title="Heat out minus heat in">

<Formula label="Work delivered by the engine" tex="W_{\text{out}} = |Q_H| - |Q_C| = \int_{T_F}^{T_H}C_0\,dT - \int_{T_C}^{T_F}C_0\,dT = C_0\left[\left(T_H - T_F\right) - \left(T_F - T_C\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="A perfect square">

With $T_F = \sqrt{T_HT_C}$:

<Formula label="Total work in terms of the starting temperatures" tex="W_{\text{out}} = C_0\left(T_H - 2\sqrt{T_HT_C} + T_C\right) = C_0\left(\sqrt{T_H} - \sqrt{T_C}\right)^2 > 0" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2" />

A square cannot be negative, and it is zero only when the two bodies start at the same temperature. The engine does positive work on the outside world.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: a heat capacity times a temperature is an energy. The number of part (a): for 400 K and 100 K the work is $C_0\,(20 - 10)^2$ K $= 100\ \text{K} \times C_0$. Our comparison agrees: with no engine both bodies would end at 250 K, with the engine at 200 K, and the energy missing from the two bodies is $2C_0\,(250 - 200)$ K $= 100\ \text{K} \times C_0$. No other way of bringing the two bodies to a common temperature delivers more work: the total entropy cannot fall, so $C_0\ln(T_F^2/T_HT_C) \ge 0$ and $T_F \ge \sqrt{T_HT_C}$, and the work $C_0(T_H + T_C - 2T_F)$ is largest for the reversible engine, which reaches that lower bound (the lesson on [heat engines](/courses/8-044/heat-engines-carnot)).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Cooling liquid helium" points={20} mit="Problem 3">

In an experiment, liquid helium at 1.0 K is to be cooled further. It is brought into contact with a paramagnetic salt that starts at a lower temperature $T_0$. The heat capacity of the liquid is $C_L = aT^3$ with $a = 128 \times 10^4$ erg K⁻⁴. The heat capacity of the salt is $C_S = bT^{-2}$ with $b = 15 \times 10^4$ erg K. (The erg is the energy unit of MIT's sheet: 1 erg $= 10^{-7}$ J.) Helium and salt together are isolated from the rest of the universe, thermally and mechanically, and no work is done.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

What must the starting temperature $T_0$ of the salt be, if helium and salt are to end together at 0.5 K? Give it in kelvin.

<Hint>

Nothing else takes part, and no work is done. So the heat that the salt takes in is the heat that the helium gives up.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The heat that a body takes in as its temperature changes is the integral of its heat capacity over temperature. The heat capacities change with temperature, so they must stay inside the integrals.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-t0" label="$T_0 =$" value="0.25" unit="K" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-t0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Energy balance: heat into the salt plus heat into the helium is zero.

<Step title="The two heats">

<Formula label="Heat balance of salt and helium" tex="Q_S = -Q_L: \qquad \int_{T_0}^{1/2}bT^{-2}\,dT = -\int_1^{1/2}aT^3\,dT" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(a), p. 3" />

Here the temperatures are written as plain numbers of kelvin. The integrals are

<Formula label="The two heats, integrated" tex="b\left(\frac{1}{T_0} - 2\right) = \frac a4\left(1 - \frac1{16}\right) = \frac{15}{64}\,a" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(a), p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solving for the temperature">

<Formula label="Starting temperature of the salt" tex="\frac{1}{T_0} = 2 + \frac{15}{64}\,\frac ab = 2 + \frac{15}{64} \times \frac{128}{15} = 4, \qquad T_0 = 0.25\ \text{K}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(a), p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The salt starts colder than the final 0.5 K, as it must. The heat itself: the helium gives up $\frac{15}{64}a = 30 \times 10^4$ erg, and the salt, warming from 0.25 K to 0.5 K, takes in $b\,(4 - 2) = 30 \times 10^4$ erg. That is 0.030 J.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Find the net change of the entropy of the universe in this process. MIT asks for the answer in terms of the parameters $a$ and $b$: write every temperature as a plain number of kelvin (1, $\frac12$ and your $T_0$), so that the result is a number times $b$ plus a number times $a$.

The second box is our addition: the same entropy change as a number, in units of $10^4$ erg/K.

<Hint>

The real process is irreversible, but entropy is a function of state. For each body alone, imagine its temperature changed slowly from its starting value to 0.5 K, and add up $dS = \delta Q/T$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For each body $\delta Q = C\,dT$, so $dS = C\,dT/T$. Mind the signs: one body warms and one cools.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-ds" label="$\Delta S =$" expr="6*b - 7*a/24"
  vars={{ a: [50, 200], b: [10, 30] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "6*b + 7*a/24", hint: "The helium cools, so its entropy falls: its term must be negative." },
    { expr: "2*b - 15*a/64", hint: "You integrated C dT, which gives the heats. The entropy needs C dT/T: one more power of T in the denominator." },
    { expr: "7*a/24 - 6*b", hint: "Check the limits: each integral runs from the body's starting temperature to 0.5 K. The salt warms and gains entropy, the helium cools and loses it." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-ds-num" label="$\Delta S =$" value="52.7" unit="× 10⁴ erg/K" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-ds-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The entropy change of each body depends only on its first and last state. Compute it along a slow path, where $dS = \delta Q/T = C\,dT/T$, and add the two.

<Step title="Salt and helium">

<Formula label="Entropy change of salt and helium" tex="\Delta S = \Delta S_S + \Delta S_L = \int_{1/4}^{1/2}bT^{-3}\,dT + \int_1^{1/2}aT^2\,dT = \frac b2\left(16 - 4\right) + \frac a3\left(\frac18 - 1\right)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(b), p. 3" />

<Formula label="Net entropy change" tex="\Delta S = 6b - \frac{7}{24}\,a" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(b), p. 3" />

With the units put back, the first term is $6b$ divided by K² and the second is $\frac7{24}a$ times K³: both are an energy per kelvin.

</Step>

<Step title="The number (our addition)">

With MIT's values, $6b = 90 \times 10^4$ erg/K and $\frac{7}{24}a = 37.3 \times 10^4$ erg/K, so

<Formula label="Net entropy change as a number" tex="\Delta S = \left(90 - 37.3\right) \times 10^4 = 52.7 \times 10^4 \quad \text{(in erg/K)}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(b), p. 3 (MIT stops at the formula; the number is ours)" />

which is 0.0527 J/K.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The helium loses entropy and the salt gains more than the helium loses, so the total rises, as the second law demands of heat flowing from a warmer body to a colder one. The same heat, $30 \times 10^4$ erg, leaves the helium at temperatures between 1 K and 0.5 K and enters the salt at temperatures between 0.25 K and 0.5 K: each bit of heat enters at a lower temperature than it left, so $\delta Q/T$ is larger on the way in.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Torsional pendulum" points={20} mit="Problem 4">

A torsional pendulum is a disc that hangs on a wire and turns about the wire, twisting it. Its position is the angle $\theta$ through which it has turned. (MIT's sheet shows the disc on its wire, with $\theta$ marked as the angle of a line on the disc.) To measure a certain physical quantity one must know the angle $\theta_0$ at which the pendulum is in equilibrium, which is the angle where its potential energy is lowest:

<Formula label="Energies of the torsional pendulum" tex="V(\theta) = \frac12K\left(\theta - \theta_0\right)^2, \qquad \text{kinetic energy} = \frac12I\dot\theta^2, \qquad \dot\theta \equiv \frac{d\theta}{dt}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2" />

Here $K$ is the stiffness of the wire and $I$ the moment of inertia of the disc. The pendulum is in thermal equilibrium with its surroundings at the temperature $T$, and that limits how precisely $\theta_0$ can be found.

<Part label="a" points={12}>

Find the root-mean-square uncertainty $\left\langle\left(\theta - \theta_0\right)^2\right\rangle^{1/2}$ of a measurement of $\theta$ that comes from classical thermal noise.

The second box is our addition: the size of this uncertainty for a wire of stiffness $K = 4.0 \times 10^{-9}$ N m per radian at $T = 300$ K, in microradians. Use $k = 1.381 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K.

<Hint>

The pendulum is a small system in contact with a heat bath: use the canonical ensemble. Write down its energy as a function of $\theta$ and $\dot\theta$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The probability density is proportional to $e^{-H/kT}$. Look at how it depends on $\theta$: compare with the Gaussian density of the probability lessons and read off the variance.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-rms" label="$\left\langle\left(\theta - \theta_0\right)^2\right\rangle^{1/2} =$" expr="sqrt(k*T/K)"
  vars={{ k: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2], K: [0.5, 2] }}

    { expr: "sqrt(2*k*T/K)", hint: "Compare the exponent with a Gaussian, exp(−x²/2σ²): the factor ½ of the potential energy matches the 2 of the Gaussian. The mean potential energy is ½kT, not kT." },

<Answer id="p4-rms-num" label="rms uncertainty $=$" value="1.02" unit="μrad" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-rms-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the canonical ensemble the probability density is $e^{-H/kT}$ up to a constant. For this pendulum the exponent is quadratic, so the density is Gaussian and the answer can be read off.

<Step title="The energy function">

<Formula label="Energy function of the pendulum" tex="H = \frac12I\dot\theta^2 + \frac12K\left(\theta - \theta_0\right)^2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4(a), p. 4" />

</Step>

<Step title="The canonical density">

<Formula label="Probability density of angle and angular velocity" tex="p(\theta, \dot\theta) \propto \exp\left[-\frac{H}{kT}\right] = \exp\left[-\frac{\dot\theta^2}{2\left(kT/I\right)}\right]\exp\left[-\frac{\left(\theta - \theta_0\right)^2}{2\left(kT/K\right)}\right]" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4(a), p. 4" />

(The canonical density of the [lesson](/courses/8-044/canonical-ensemble) is a density in the coordinate and its momentum, here $\theta$ and $I\dot\theta$. The momentum is $\dot\theta$ times a constant, so the density in $\theta$ and $\dot\theta$ has the same form. This remark is ours.)

Two things stand out, as MIT's solutions note. The density is a product of a function of $\dot\theta$ and a function of $\theta$, so the two are statistically independent. And each factor is a Gaussian. The factor in $\theta$ has mean $\theta_0$ and variance $\sigma^2 = kT/K$, so

<Formula label="Thermal uncertainty of the angle" tex="\left\langle\left(\theta - \theta_0\right)^2\right\rangle^{1/2} = \sqrt{\frac{kT}{K}}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4(a), p. 4" />

</Step>

<Step title="The number (our addition)">

<Formula label="Thermal uncertainty for the example wire" tex="\sqrt{\frac{kT}{K}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.381 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K} \times 300\ \text{K}}{4.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{N m}}} = 1.02 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{rad}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4(a), p. 4 (our own numbers)" />

About one microradian. A single reading of the angle cannot place $\theta_0$ better than this, however good the instrument.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $kT$ is an energy and $K$ is an energy per radian squared, so the ratio is an angle squared. The result says that the mean potential energy is $\frac12K\left\langle\left(\theta - \theta_0\right)^2\right\rangle = \frac12kT$, the same share that each quadratic term of the energy of the classical oscillators gets in the lesson on [examples](/courses/8-044/canonical-examples). A stiffer wire or a colder room gives a sharper angle. The moment of inertia does not appear.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

Find $\left\langle\theta\dot\theta\right\rangle$, the mean of the product of the angle and the angular velocity.

<Hint>

Look at the density of part (a) again. What does it say about how $\theta$ and $\dot\theta$ are related? What is the mean of a product of two such variables?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Find the mean of $\dot\theta$ from its own factor of the density, which is symmetric about a certain value.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-mean" label="$\left\langle\theta\dot\theta\right\rangle$ is" choices={["zero: the angle and the angular velocity are independent, and the mean angular velocity is zero", "θ₀ √(kT/I): the mean angle times the rms angular velocity", "kT/√(IK): the rms angle times the rms angular velocity"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For independent variables the mean of a product is the product of the means.

<Step title="Product of the means">

The density of part (a) is a product, so $\theta$ and $\dot\theta$ are statistically independent and

<Formula label="Mean of the product" tex="\left\langle\theta\dot\theta\right\rangle = \left\langle\theta\right\rangle\left\langle\dot\theta\right\rangle = \theta_0 \times 0 = 0" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4(b), p. 4" />

The factor in $\dot\theta$ is a Gaussian centred on zero: the disc is as likely to be turning one way as the other.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result (our addition)">

A second argument gives the same answer. The product $\theta\dot\theta$ is the time derivative of $\frac12\theta^2$. In equilibrium the mean of $\theta^2$ does not change with time, so the mean of its rate of change is zero. Note that the rms values of the two factors are not zero: the mean of a product of rms size at least $kT/\sqrt{IK}$ vanishes because positive and negative products are equally likely.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="The hydrogen atom" points={20} mit="Problem 5">

In the simplest model of hydrogen, the bound states of the electron are labelled by three quantum numbers: $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$; $l = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1$; and $m_l = -l, \dots, l$. The energy $\epsilon_n$ of a state depends on $n$ only:

<Formula label="Energies of the bound states of hydrogen" tex="\epsilon_n = -\frac{A}{n^2}, \qquad A = 13.6\ \text{eV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3" />

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Find the ratio of the number of atoms in the first excited energy level to the number in the lowest energy level, at the temperature $T$. (MIT's hint: mind the difference between energy levels and states of the system.) Then evaluate it for $T = 300$ K and for $T = 1000$ K.

The ratios are far too small for an ordinary number box. Write each as $10^{-x}$ and give $x$. Use $k = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/K.

<Hint>

In the canonical ensemble each state has the probability $e^{-E_{\text{state}}/kT}/Z$. A level is all the states with the same energy: its probability is the probability of one of its states times the number of them.

</Hint>

<Hint>

List the states with $n = 2$: every allowed pair of $l$ and $m_l$. In the ratio of the two levels the partition function cancels.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the numbers: the logarithm to base 10 of $e^{-u}$ is $-u/\ln 10$. Do not forget the factor in front of the exponential.

<Answer id="p5-ratio" label="$\frac{N(n = 2)}{N(n = 1)} =$" expr="4*exp(-3*A/(4*k*T))"

<Solution>

**The idea.** Boltzmann factors belong to states. To compare levels, multiply each Boltzmann factor by the number of states in the level.

<Step title="Counting states">

The lowest level, $n = 1$, has the energy $-A$ and a single state: $l = 0$, $m_l = 0$. The first excited level, $n = 2$, has the energy $-A/4$ and four states: one with $l = 0$, and three with $l = 1$ ($m_l = 1, 0, -1$).

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Ratio of the populations of the two lowest levels" tex="\frac{N(-A/4)}{N(-A)} = \frac{4\exp\left[A/4kT\right]}{\exp\left[A/kT\right]} = 4\exp\left[-\frac34\,\frac{A}{kT}\right]" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(a), p. 5" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

In temperature units $A/k = 13.6\ \text{eV}/(8.617 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{eV/K}) = 1.578 \times 10^5$ K. At 300 K the exponent is $\frac34 \times 1.578 \times 10^5/300 = 394.6$, and at 1000 K it is $118.4$:

<Formula label="The ratio at the two temperatures" tex="4e^{-394.6} = 10^{-170.76} \approx 1.75 \times 10^{-171}\ \ (300\ \text{K}), \qquad 4e^{-118.4} = 10^{-50.81} \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-51}\ \ (1000\ \text{K})" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(a), p. 5" />

(MIT's solutions print $4.8 \times 10^{-170}$ at 300 K. With MIT's own conversion, 1 meV for 11.6 K, the formula gives $2.1 \times 10^{-171}$, about 23 times smaller, so the printed value looks like a slip. The value at 1000 K agrees with MIT's.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At high temperature the exponential tends to 1 and the ratio to 4, the ratio of the numbers of states: when $kT$ is far above the gap, every state is equally likely. At these two temperatures $kT$ is more than a hundred times smaller than the gap of 10.2 eV, and the ratio is so small that in any laboratory sample of gas in thermal equilibrium not one atom would be found in the excited level. The numbers are very sensitive: rounding $A/k$ to three figures, $1.58 \times 10^5$ K, makes the ratio at 300 K smaller by about a third, which is why the box asks for the exponent $x$.

</Step>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

To find the actual fraction of the atoms in any given state one needs the partition function. Show that the partition function diverges, even when the unbound states are left out.

The box is our addition, a step on the way: the number of states in the level with quantum number $n$.

<Hint>

For a given $l$ there are $2l + 1$ values of $m_l$. Add them up for $l = 0$ to $n - 1$.

<Hint>

<Answer id="p5-g" label="States in level $n$:" expr="n^2"
  vars={{ n: [2, 7] }}
  mistakes={[

<Solution>

**The idea.** The energies crowd towards zero from below, so the Boltzmann factors of the high levels do not die away, while the number of states per level keeps growing.

<Step title="States per level">

<Formula label="Number of states in level n" tex="\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}\left(2l + 1\right) = 1 + 3 + 5 + \dots + \left(2n - 1\right) = n^2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(b), p. 5" />

</Step>

<Step title="The partition function">

For one atom, with the unbound states left out,

<Formula label="Partition function of the bound states" tex="Z = \sum_{\text{states}}\exp\left[-\frac{E_{\text{state}}}{kT}\right] = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^2\exp\left[\frac{\alpha}{n^2}\right], \qquad \alpha \equiv \frac{A}{kT}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(b), p. 5" />

Since $\alpha > 0$, every exponential is larger than 1. So

<Formula label="Lower bound on the partition function" tex="Z > \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}n^2, \qquad \text{which diverges}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(b), p. 5" />

A sum that is larger than a divergent sum of positive terms diverges. The partition function is infinite at every temperature.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The number of states: $n = 1$ gives 1 and $n = 2$ gives 4, as counted in part (a). The divergence comes from the upper end of the sum: the terms themselves grow as $n^2$, so they do not even tend to zero.

</Step>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Statistical mechanics seems to fail for one of the simplest systems in nature. Any ideas why? Write down your thoughts, then open the solution and compare.

<Hint>

Which states cause the divergence? Think about how large an atom in such a state would be, and what else there is in a real gas.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The trouble lies in the model, an electron and a proton alone in infinite empty space, and not in statistical mechanics.

<Step title="MIT's answer">

MIT's solutions make three points. The Coulomb potential is unusual in that it has infinitely many bound states below zero energy. In the real world this is changed by walls (think of the levels of a particle in a box) or by other atoms nearby. And for hydrogen atoms in the space between the stars, where neighbours are far away, the reason they are found in low states probably has more to do with a lack of ways to excite them, so that they are not in thermal equilibrium, than with neighbouring atoms.

</Step>

<Step title="How large the troublesome states are (our addition)">

An estimate shows how harmless the divergence is in practice. Cut the sum off at a largest quantum number $n_{\text{max}}$. The lowest state contributes $e^{\alpha}$, and the high levels contribute about $\sum n^2 \approx n_{\text{max}}^3/3$. At 300 K, $\alpha = A/kT \approx 526$. The high levels match the single lowest state only when $n_{\text{max}}^3/3 \approx e^{526}$, that is for $n_{\text{max}}$ of about $10^{76}$. The size of a state grows roughly as $n^2$ times the Bohr radius (as shown in [8.04 Problem Set 10](/courses/8-04/8-04-problem-set-10#p6), Problem 6(d)), and the Bohr radius is $0.529 \times 10^{-10}$ m (lesson on the [hydrogen atom](/courses/8-04/hydrogen-atom)), so such an atom would be about $10^{142}$ m across. Long before that, the electron meets the wall of the container or another atom, and the model of one electron around one proton no longer applies. With any reasonable cut-off, practically every atom is in the lowest state at room temperature, as part (a) found.

</Step>

</Solution>
