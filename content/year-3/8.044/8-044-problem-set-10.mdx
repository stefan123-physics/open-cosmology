## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `exp(-e2/(k*T))`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent. Type `exp(...)` for an exponential, `sqrt(...)` for a square root, `log(...)` for the natural logarithm and `pi` for $\pi$. Write the symbols as the box names them: `e0`, `e2`, `e3` for $\epsilon_0$, $\epsilon_2$, $\epsilon_3$, `D` for $\Delta$, `Theta` for $\Theta_R$, `lam` for the thermal wavelength $\lambda$, `a` for $\alpha$, `mu` for $\mu$ and `Phi` for $\Phi_G$.

The points per problem and per part are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box for the proof: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons: $k$ is Boltzmann's constant (MIT's sheet writes it with a subscript B in Problems 4 and 5), $Z$ is the canonical partition function, $\Xi$ the grand partition function and $\Phi_G$ the grand potential (MIT's sheet writes a script $Z$ and $\Phi$ for these two). ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Two identical particles" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

Two identical particles do not interact and have no spin variables at all. Each can be in one of only three single-particle states, numbered 1, 2 and 3, with the energies $\epsilon_1 = 0 < \epsilon_2 < \epsilon_3$. A state of the pair is written with its occupation numbers, $(n_1, n_2, n_3)$: the number of particles in each single-particle state.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Let the particles be fermions. List all the two-particle states with their energies. Which state is occupied at $T = 0$?

<Hint>

No two identical fermions can be in the same single-particle state, so each occupation number is 0 or 1. The three numbers add up to 2.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-nf" label="Number of two-fermion states $=$" value="3" tolerance="0.1" calc="p1-nf" />

<Answer id="p1-f0" label="Fermions at $T = 0$:" choices={["(2, 0, 0)", "(1, 0, 1)", "(1, 1, 0)", "(0, 1, 1)"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A state of identical particles is fixed by its occupation numbers. For fermions each is 0 or 1.

<Step title="The list">

Two of the three single-particle states hold one particle each, and one is empty. There are three ways to choose the empty one (MIT's solutions p. 1):

| State $(n_1, n_2, n_3)$ | Energy |
|---|---|
| (1, 1, 0) | $\epsilon_2$ |
| (1, 0, 1) | $\epsilon_3$ |
| (0, 1, 1) | $\epsilon_2 + \epsilon_3$ |

At $T = 0$ the pair is in the state of lowest energy, (1, 1, 0). Its energy is $\epsilon_2$, not zero: the second fermion cannot join the first in state 1.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The number of ways to choose 2 different states out of 3 is $3 \times 2/2 = 3$, as in the worked example of the lesson.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Repeat part (a) for bosons.

<Hint>

For bosons an occupation number can be 0, 1 or 2. The three numbers still add up to 2.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-nb" label="Number of two-boson states $=$" value="6" tolerance="0.1" calc="p1-nb" />

<Answer id="p1-b0" label="Bosons at $T = 0$:" choices={["(1, 1, 0)", "(2, 0, 0)", "(0, 0, 2)", "(0, 2, 0)"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Bosons may share a single-particle state, so three more states appear: the ones with both particles in the same state.

<Step title="The list">

(MIT's solutions p. 1.)

| State $(n_1, n_2, n_3)$ | Energy |
|---|---|
| (2, 0, 0) | $0$ |
| (1, 1, 0) | $\epsilon_2$ |
| (1, 0, 1) | $\epsilon_3$ |
| (0, 2, 0) | $2\epsilon_2$ |
| (0, 1, 1) | $\epsilon_2 + \epsilon_3$ |
| (0, 0, 2) | $2\epsilon_3$ |

At $T = 0$ both bosons are in state 1: the state (2, 0, 0), with energy zero. The table is in the order of MIT's solutions. Whether $\epsilon_3$ or $2\epsilon_2$ is the higher energy depends on the values: the order of the first two rows does not.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The three states of part (a) plus three states with a double occupation make 6. Distinguishable particles would have $3 \times 3 = 9$ states.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Hint>

The partition function is a sum of $e^{-E/kT}$ over the states of the whole system: one term for each row of your lists.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** One Boltzmann factor for each state of the pair.

<Step title="The two sums">

<Formula label="Partition function of two fermions on three levels" tex="Z_F = e^{-\epsilon_2/kT} + e^{-\epsilon_3/kT} + e^{-(\epsilon_2 + \epsilon_3)/kT}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

<Formula label="Partition function of two bosons on three levels" tex="Z_B = 1 + e^{-\epsilon_2/kT} + e^{-\epsilon_3/kT} + e^{-2\epsilon_2/kT} + e^{-(\epsilon_2 + \epsilon_3)/kT} + e^{-2\epsilon_3/kT}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

(MIT's solutions write these with the letter β for $1/kT$.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At very high temperature every exponential tends to 1, and each partition function tends to its number of states: $Z_F \to 3$ and $Z_B \to 6$. Neither is the square of the partition function of one particle, which has 9 terms, nor half of that square. At very low temperature $Z_B \to 1$, the one ground state, and $Z_F \to 0$: every state of the fermions lies above the zero of energy.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

Keep only the two leading terms of each partition function: the two that are largest at low temperature. Find the internal energy $U$ from them in each case. In the first two boxes give the energy exactly as the two terms give it, without a further expansion. Then compare the two cases near $T = 0$: what is the energy at $T = 0$, and what is the energy gap $\Delta$ that controls the approach to it?

<Hint>

The leading terms are the ones with the lowest energies: $\epsilon_2$ and $\epsilon_3$ for the fermions, $0$ and $\epsilon_2$ for the bosons.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With two states only, the energy is the sum of each energy times its probability. The probability of a state is its Boltzmann factor divided by the sum of the two Boltzmann factors.

</Hint>

<Hint>

To see the gap, divide above and below by the larger of the two exponentials. The energy is then the energy at $T = 0$ plus a term with one small exponential, $e^{-\Delta/kT}$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** At low temperature only the two lowest states matter. Two states give the energy of a two-level system, measured from the lower state.

<Step title="Fermions">

The two leading terms are $Z_F \approx e^{-\epsilon_2/kT} + e^{-\epsilon_3/kT}$. Then

<Formula label="Energy of two fermions at low temperature" tex="U_F \approx \frac{\epsilon_2e^{-\epsilon_2/kT} + \epsilon_3e^{-\epsilon_3/kT}}{e^{-\epsilon_2/kT} + e^{-\epsilon_3/kT}} = \frac{\epsilon_2 + \epsilon_3e^{-(\epsilon_3 - \epsilon_2)/kT}}{1 + e^{-(\epsilon_3 - \epsilon_2)/kT}} \approx \epsilon_2 + (\epsilon_3 - \epsilon_2)\,e^{-(\epsilon_3 - \epsilon_2)/kT}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(d), p. 1" />

The last step keeps the first order in the small exponential. The energy at $T = 0$ is $\epsilon_2$, a finite energy, and the approach to it is energy gap behaviour with the gap $\Delta = \epsilon_3 - \epsilon_2$ (MIT's solutions p. 2).

</Step>

<Step title="Bosons">

The two leading terms are $Z_B \approx 1 + e^{-\epsilon_2/kT}$. Then

<Formula label="Energy of two bosons at low temperature" tex="U_B \approx \frac{\epsilon_2e^{-\epsilon_2/kT}}{1 + e^{-\epsilon_2/kT}} \approx \epsilon_2\,e^{-\epsilon_2/kT}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2" />

The energy at $T = 0$ is zero, and the gap is $\Delta = \epsilon_2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The contrast">

Both systems show energy gap behaviour, as every system with discrete levels does. They differ in two ways. The fermions keep an energy at $T = 0$, because the Pauli principle holds one of them in state 2. And the gaps differ: the cheapest excitation of the fermions moves a particle from state 2 to state 3, that of the bosons from state 1 to state 2.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Our numbers, for $\epsilon_3 = 2\epsilon_2$ and $kT = 0.2\,\epsilon_2$. Fermions: all three states give $U_F = 1.006783\,\epsilon_2$, the two leading terms give $1.006693\,\epsilon_2$ and the expanded form $1.006738\,\epsilon_2$. Bosons: all six states give $U_B = 0.006874\,\epsilon_2$, the two leading terms give $0.006693\,\epsilon_2$ and the expanded form $0.006738\,\epsilon_2$. With these values the two gaps are equal, so the two leading terms give the same rise above the energy at $T = 0$ in both cases. The next states differ, and so do the full results.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A number of two-state particles" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

$N$ identical bosons do not interact and have no spin. Each has only two single-particle energy eigenstates: state 0 with the energy $0$ and state 1 with the energy $\Delta$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

How would you label the energy eigenstates of the $N$ bosons in the occupation number representation? Let $n$ be the number of particles in the excited state 1. Give the energy $E_n$ of the state with that $n$, and the number of $N$-body states in all.

<Hint>

The two occupation numbers add up to $N$, so one of them fixes the other.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-e" label="$E_n =$" expr="n*D"
  vars={{ n: [0, 5], D: [0.5, 2], N: [5, 9] }}
  names={{ D: "\\Delta" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "N*D", hint: "That is the energy of the one state with every particle excited. Only n of the particles are in state 1." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-count" label="Number of $N$-body states $=$" expr="N + 1"
  vars={{ N: [1, 9] }}
  mistakes={[

<Solution>

**The idea.** With two levels, a state of the $N$ bosons is fixed by one number.

<Step title="The states">

A state has $N - n$ particles in state 0 and $n$ in state 1, written $(N - n, n)$, with $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots, N$:

<Formula label="Energies of N bosons on two levels" tex="E_n = n\Delta, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots, N" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

That makes $N + 1$ states, each with a different energy (MIT's solutions p. 2, where the number is called $n_1$ and a small drawing shows the two levels).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $N = 2$: (2, 0), (1, 1) and (0, 2), three states. Problem 1(b) with only two levels gives the same three.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the partition function $Z(N, T)$ in the canonical ensemble, in closed form (without a sum).

<Hint>

The sum over states is a sum over $n$ from 0 to $N$ of $e^{-n\Delta/kT}$: a geometric series with a finite number of terms.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A finite geometric series is the difference of two infinite ones: the full series, minus the series that starts at $n = N + 1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-z" label="$Z(N, T) =$" expr="(1 - exp(-(N + 1)*D/(k*T)))/(1 - exp(-D/(k*T)))"
  vars={{ N: [1, 6], D: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ D: "\\Delta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/(1 - exp(-D/(k*T)))", hint: "That is the series carried on to infinity: the partition function of a harmonic oscillator, or of an unlimited supply of particles. Here n stops at N." },
    { expr: "(1 + exp(-D/(k*T)))^N", hint: "That is the result for N distinguishable particles, part (d). For identical bosons each value of n is one state, not many." },
    { expr: "(1 - exp(-N*D/(k*T)))/(1 - exp(-D/(k*T)))", hint: "That is a series of N terms. The sum runs from n = 0 to n = N, which is N + 1 terms." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Sum the Boltzmann factor over the $N + 1$ states. The terms form a geometric series.

<Step title="The sum">

Write the finite sum as the infinite series minus its tail. The tail is the full series again, times its first term $e^{-(N + 1)\Delta/kT}$:

<Formula label="Partition function of N bosons on two levels" tex="Z(N, T) = \sum_{n = 0}^{N}e^{-n\Delta/kT} = \frac{1}{1 - e^{-\Delta/kT}} - \frac{e^{-(N + 1)\Delta/kT}}{1 - e^{-\Delta/kT}} = \frac{1 - e^{-(N + 1)\Delta/kT}}{1 - e^{-\Delta/kT}}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $N = 1$ the result is $\left(1 - x^2\right)/(1 - x) = 1 + x$ with $x = e^{-\Delta/kT}$: one particle with two states. At high temperature $x \to 1$ and $Z \to N + 1$, the number of states. At low temperature $Z \to 1$: only the ground state.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

What is the probability $p(n)$ that $n$ particles are found in the excited state 1?

The second box is our addition. For $N = 3$ at the temperature where $e^{-\Delta/kT} = \frac12$, find the probability that no particle is excited.

<Hint>

Each $n$ is one state of the system. The probability of a state in the canonical ensemble is its Boltzmann factor divided by $Z$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-p" label="$p(n) =$" expr="exp(-n*D/(k*T))*(1 - exp(-D/(k*T)))/(1 - exp(-(N + 1)*D/(k*T)))"
  vars={{ n: [0, 4], N: [4, 8], D: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ D: "\\Delta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-n*D/(k*T))", hint: "That is the Boltzmann factor alone. The probabilities of the N + 1 states must add up to 1: divide by Z." },
    { expr: "exp(-n*D/(k*T))*(1 - exp(-D/(k*T)))", hint: "That is normalized for n running on to infinity. Here n stops at N, so the sum of your probabilities is a little less than 1." },
    { expr: "exp(-n*D/(k*T))*(1 - exp(-(N + 1)*D/(k*T)))/(1 - exp(-D/(k*T)))", hint: "That multiplies the Boltzmann factor by Z. A probability is the Boltzmann factor divided by Z." }

<Answer id="p2-p0" label="$N = 3$, $e^{-\Delta/kT} = \frac12$: $p(0) =$" value="0.533" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-p0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One state for each $n$, so $p(n) = e^{-E_n/kT}/Z$.

<Step title="The probability">

<Formula label="Probability of n excited bosons" tex="p(n) = \frac{e^{-n\Delta/kT}}{Z} = \frac{1 - e^{-\Delta/kT}}{1 - e^{-(N + 1)\Delta/kT}}\;e^{-n\Delta/kT}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(c), p. 3" />

It falls by the same factor $e^{-\Delta/kT}$ at every step in $n$: a geometric distribution, cut off at $n = N$.

</Step>

<Step title="The example (our addition)">

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Identical particle effects in rotational Raman scattering" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

Chlorine has two stable isotopes: chlorine-35 (76% of the atoms) and chlorine-37 (24%). Both nuclei have the spin $\frac32$.

What the problem needs from MIT's notes, in our words. The rotational Raman spectrum of a gas of diatomic molecules is a series of evenly spaced lines, one for each rotational level $L = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ that a molecule can start from. The strength of a line follows the number of molecules in its starting level. When a two-body wavefunction has to be antisymmetric, either its spin part or its space part can carry the antisymmetry. For a molecule made of two identical nuclei the space part is the rotation: swapping the two nuclei leaves a rotational state with even $L$ unchanged and changes the sign of one with odd $L$ (MIT's solutions p. 3).

<Hint>

A nucleus with half-integer spin is a fermion. The whole wavefunction of the two nuclei, rotation times spin, must change sign when they are swapped.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Two spins of $\frac32$ have $4 \times 4 = 16$ states. They combine to a total spin of 3, 2, 1 or 0. The states with the highest total spin are symmetric, and the symmetry alternates as the total spin goes down by one.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A rotational level with even $L$ can only go with the antisymmetric spin states, one with odd $L$ only with the symmetric ones. The number of spin states that a level may use is an extra degeneracy of that level.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-sym" label="Symmetric spin states $=$" value="10" tolerance="0.1" calc="p3-sym" />

<Answer id="p3-ratio" label="Weaker series : stronger series $=$" value="0.6" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-ratio" />

<Answer id="p3-which" label="The stronger lines start from levels with" choices={["odd L, which go with the symmetric spin states", "even L, which go with the symmetric spin states", "even L, which go with the antisymmetric spin states", "odd L, which go with the antisymmetric spin states"]} answer={0} />

**The idea.** The two nuclei are identical fermions, so the product of the rotational state and the nuclear spin state must be antisymmetric. Each rotational level can use only part of the 16 spin states, and that part alternates with $L$.

<Step title="Counting the spin states">

Two spins of $\frac32$ add to a total spin $I_T = 3, 2, 1$ or $0$, with $2I_T + 1 = 7, 5, 3$ and $1$ states. The states with $I_T = 3$ are symmetric, and the symmetry alternates on the way down (MIT's solutions p. 3):

<Formula label="Symmetric and antisymmetric states of two spins 3/2" tex="(2 \cdot 3 + 1) + (2 \cdot 1 + 1) = 10 \;\text{(symmetric)}, \qquad (2 \cdot 2 + 1) + (2 \cdot 0 + 1) = 6 \;\text{(antisymmetric)}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(a), p. 3" />

<Step title="Pairing with the rotation">

Even $L$ is symmetric in space, so it needs an antisymmetric spin state: 6 choices. Odd $L$ is antisymmetric in space, so it needs a symmetric spin state: 10 choices. The lines that start from odd $L$ are the stronger ones, and the two series lie on two envelopes of the same shape with the ratio

<Formula label="Ratio of the two series of Raman lines of chlorine" tex="\frac{\text{weaker (even } L)}{\text{stronger (odd } L)} = \frac{6}{10} = \frac35 = 0.60" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(a), p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Count in another way (ours). Each nucleus has 4 spin states. The symmetric combinations of two objects with 4 states each number $4 \times 5/2 = 10$, the antisymmetric ones $4 \times 3/2 = 6$, and $10 + 6 = 16$. For a nuclear spin $I$ in general the ratio is $I/(I + 1)$, which is $\frac35$ for $I = \frac32$.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="The information theory approach to statistical mechanics" points={20} mit="Problem 5">

Information theory measures how much is unknown about a signal before it is read. If item $i$ occurs with the probability $P_i$, the information in that item is measured by $\ln P_i$, and the Shannon entropy of the signal is the average over the items, weighted by how often each occurs:

<Formula label="Shannon entropy" tex="S_I \equiv -k\sum_iP_i\ln P_i" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3" />

The information theory approach to statistical mechanics rests on two assumptions (MIT's sheet pp. 3 and 4, in our words).

**Assumption 1.** The thermodynamic entropy of a system has the form of the Shannon entropy: $S_I = -k\sum p_{\text{state}}\ln p_{\text{state}}$, where $p_{\text{state}}$ is the probability of a microscopic state and the sum runs over all the states of the system.

**Assumption 2.** The probabilities in equilibrium are the ones that make $S_I$ as large as possible, given the constraints that are imposed on the system.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Show that Assumption 1 gives the correct entropy for the microcanonical ensemble, where every one of the $\Omega$ states has the same probability, $p_{\text{state}} = 1/\Omega$.

The box is our addition. A system has three states with the probabilities $\frac12$, $\frac14$ and $\frac14$. Find $S_I/k$.

<Hint>

Every term of the sum is the same. How many terms are there?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-three" label="$S_I/k =$" value="1.04" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-three" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put $p_{\text{state}} = 1/\Omega$ into the sum. The sum of 1 over the states is $\Omega$.

<Step title="The microcanonical ensemble">

<Formula label="Shannon entropy of the microcanonical ensemble" tex="S_I = -k\sum_{\text{states}}\frac{1}{\Omega}\ln\frac{1}{\Omega} = k\,\frac{\ln\Omega}{\Omega}\sum_{\text{states}}1 = k\ln\Omega" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(a), p. 6" />

That is the entropy of the microcanonical ensemble.

</Step>

<Step title="Three states (our addition)">

$S_I/k = \frac12\ln 2 + 2 \times \frac14\ln 4 = \frac32\ln 2 = 1.04$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Three equally likely states would give $\ln 3 = 1.10$, which is more. That is Assumption 2 at work: with no constraint but normalization, the largest $S_I$ belongs to equal probabilities.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

In the box give $S_I$ in terms of the internal energy $U$, the temperature and $Z$, as it comes out of the sum.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-canon" label="$S_I =$" expr="U/T + k*log(Z)"
  vars={{ U: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2], Z: [2, 50] }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "U/(k*T) + log(Z)", hint: "That is the entropy divided by k: the factor k in front of the sum is missing." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Take the logarithm of the canonical probability and average it.

<Step title="The sum">

<Formula label="Shannon entropy of the canonical ensemble" tex="S_I = -k\sum_{\text{states}}p_{\text{state}}\left(-\frac{E_{\text{state}}}{kT} - \ln Z\right) = \frac{1}{T}\langle E\rangle + k\ln Z\sum_{\text{states}}p_{\text{state}} = \frac{U}{T} + k\ln Z" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(b), p. 6" />

</Step>

<Step title="Thermodynamics">

With $k\ln Z = -F/T$ and $F = U - TS$:

<Formula label="Shannon entropy equals the thermodynamic entropy" tex="S_I = \frac{1}{T}(U - F) = \frac{1}{T}\left[U - (U - TS)\right] = S" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(b), p. 6" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A two-level example of ours: energies $0$ and $\Delta$ at $kT = \Delta$. The probabilities are 0.7311 and 0.2689, and the sum $-\sum p\ln p$ is 0.5822. The other route: $U/kT = 0.2689$ and $\ln Z = \ln\left(1 + e^{-1}\right) = 0.3133$, together 0.5822.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Now use Assumption 2 to find the probabilities themselves, for the grand canonical ensemble. There the number of particles $N$ varies, so a state is labelled by $N$ and by $\nu$, which stands for all the other microscopic variables. Its probability is $p_{N,\nu}$ and its energy $E_{N,\nu}$. Three constraints are imposed: normalization, the mean energy (taken to be the internal energy $U$) and the mean number of particles $\langle N\rangle$:

<Formula label="Constraints on the grand canonical probabilities" tex="\sum_{N,\nu}p_{N,\nu} = 1, \qquad \sum_{N,\nu}E_{N,\nu}\,p_{N,\nu} = U, \qquad \sum_{N,\nu}N\,p_{N,\nu} = \langle N\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4" />

By the method of Lagrange multipliers, $S_I$ is largest under the three constraints where the derivative of

<Formula label="Form of the probabilities that maximize the Shannon entropy" tex="\ln p_{N,\nu} = \frac{\lambda_1}{k} - 1 + \frac{\lambda_U}{k}\,E_{N,\nu} + \frac{\lambda_N}{k}\,N" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 5" />

</Hint>

<Hint>

**The idea.** Differentiate the function with respect to one particular $p_{N,\nu}$. Only the terms that contain that probability survive.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Maxwell relations" points={10} mit="Problem 6">

Express each of the following four partial derivatives by another partial derivative that does not contain the chemical potential $\mu$. The system has one kind of particle, with $dU = T\,dS - P\,dV + \mu\,dN$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

The derivative $\left(\partial S/\partial\mu\right)_{V,N}$.

<Hint>

A Maxwell relation equates the two mixed second derivatives of a state function. Which function has $S$, $V$ and $N$ as its natural variables?

</Hint>

<Hint>

At constant $V$, the cross derivatives of $U$ relate $\left(\partial\mu/\partial S\right)_{V,N}$ to a derivative of $T$. The derivative you need is the inverse of that one.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The internal energy is a state function of $S$, $V$ and $N$, so its mixed second derivatives are equal.

<Step title="Cross derivatives of U">

<Formula label="Maxwell relation from the internal energy" tex="\left(\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial S}\right)_{V,N} = \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial N}\right)_{S,V}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6, p. 8" />

<Step title="Invert both sides">

Each side is a derivative with the same variables held fixed on its own side, so each can be turned upside down:

<Formula label="Derivative of the entropy with respect to the chemical potential" tex="\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial\mu}\right)_{V,N} = \left(\frac{\partial N}{\partial T}\right)_{S,V}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6, p. 8" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both sides have the unit of one over a temperature: an entropy divided by an energy on the left, a number divided by a temperature on the right. A check with the monatomic ideal gas is in part (d).

The derivative $\left(\partial\mu/\partial V\right)_{T,N}$.
