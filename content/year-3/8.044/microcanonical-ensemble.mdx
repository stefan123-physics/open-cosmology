Statistical mechanics starts from the microscopic description of a system and derives both the probabilities of its microscopic variables and its macroscopic equations of state. Its simplest setting is the **microcanonical ensemble**: an isolated system with fixed energy. This lecture sets it up and applies it to the ideal gas (slides 1–17; notes pp. 1–3). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides and the first sections of his "Notes on the Microcanonical Ensemble"; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Liouville's theorem

A classical system of $N$ particles is a point in **phase space**, with $3N$ coordinates $q_i$ and $3N$ momenta $p_i$. A probability density $\rho(\{p, q\})$ over phase space flows, under Hamilton's equations, like an **incompressible fluid** (slide 1):

<Formula label="Liouville's theorem" tex="\frac{d\rho}{dt} = \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \sum_{i=1}^{3N}\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p_i}\frac{dp_i}{dt} + \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial q_i}\frac{dq_i}{dt}\right) = 0" cite="mit" at="slide 1" />

and with $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q_i$ and $\dot q_i = \partial H/\partial p_i$ this says $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \sum_i\left(\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} - \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\right)$. If $\rho$ depends on $\{p, q\}$ only through the energy, $\rho = \rho(H)$, the two terms cancel and $\partial\rho/\partial t = 0$: a density that depends only on the energy is a valid **equilibrium** density (slide 2).

## The ensemble

**The system** (notes p. 1; slide 3). $N$ particles in a volume $V$, with enough constraints (a magnetization or magnetic field, an electric polarization or electric field, and so on) to fix the thermodynamic state once the energy is also fixed, to within a small $\Delta \ll E$:

<Formula label="Energy shell" tex="E < \text{energy} \le E + \Delta" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

How many quantities must be fixed, $E$ among them, depends on how many independent macroscopic variables the system has; the notes work with three: $E$, $V$ and $N$. Many microscopic states satisfy the constraints.

**The probability density** (notes pp. 1–2; slides 4–5). The simplest choice: **every accessible microscopic state is equally probable**, the **postulate of equal a priori probabilities** on which all of statistical mechanics rests. Classically,

<Formula label="Microcanonical density" tex="p(\{p, q\}) = \begin{cases} 1/\Omega & E < H(\{p, q\}) \le E + \Delta \\ 0 & \text{elsewhere} \end{cases}, \qquad \Omega(E, V, N) = \int_{\text{accessible}}\{dp, dq\}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

so $\Omega$ is the accessible volume of phase space; in quantum mechanics, $\Omega$ is the number of accessible energy eigenstates $k$, those whose energy $E_k$ satisfies $E < E_k \le E + \Delta$, a pure number (notes p. 2). (The classical volume can be made unit-free by dividing by a power of Planck's constant, as the notes mention.)

**Microscopic information** comes from integrating out unwanted variables (notes pp. 2–3; slide 6). For any condition $X$ on a subset of the variables,

<Formula label="Probability of a microscopic condition" tex="p(X) = \frac{\text{volume of accessible phase space consistent with } X}{\text{total volume of accessible phase space}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The thermodynamic information will come from how $\Omega$ depends on $E$, $V$ and $N$.

**Related quantities** (notes p. 3; slide 7):

<Formula label="Cumulative volume, density of states and Ω" tex="\Phi(E, V, N) \equiv \int_{H(\{p, q\}) < E}\{dp, dq\}, \qquad \rho(E, V, N) \equiv \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial E}, \qquad \Omega(E, V, N) = \rho(E, V, N)\,\Delta" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

the cumulative phase-space volume, the **density of states**, and the volume of the energy shell.

## The ideal monatomic gas

$N$ atoms of mass $m$ in a box $V = L_xL_yL_z$, with $H = \sum_{i=1}^{3N}p_i^2/(2m)$ and no potential energy inside the box (slide 8). The position integrals give $V^N$ (slide 9). The momenta with $H < E$ fill the inside of a $3N$-dimensional sphere $\sum p_i^2 = 2mE$, of radius $R = \sqrt{2mE}$ (slide 10). Two pieces of mathematics (slide 11):

<Formula label="Volume of a sphere in α dimensions, and Stirling's approximation" tex="V_\alpha(R) = \frac{\pi^nR^{2n}}{n!}, \quad n = \frac{\alpha}{2}; \qquad \ln M! \approx M\ln M - M \quad (M \gg 1)" cite="mit" at="slide 11" />

So (slide 12)

<Formula label="Phase-space volume of the ideal gas" tex="\Phi(E, V, N) = V^N\,\frac{(2\pi mE)^M}{M!}, \quad M = \frac{3N}{2}; \qquad \Phi \approx V^N\left(\frac{4\pi emE}{3N}\right)^{3N/2}, \qquad \rho = \frac{3N}{2}\frac{\Phi}{E}, \qquad \Omega = \frac{3N}{2}\frac{\Delta}{E}\Phi" cite="mit" at="slide 12" />

the middle form using Stirling's approximation. With $N$ near $10^{23}$, the dependence on $E$ is enormously steep: nearly all of the sphere's volume lies just under its surface.

## Microscopic distributions of the gas

**Positions** (slide 13). Integrating out everything but one coordinate leaves a factor $V^{N-1}L_yL_z$ out of $V^N$, so $p(x_i) = 1/L_x$, uniform in the box. Two coordinates give $p(x_i, y_j) = 1/(L_xL_y) = p(x_i)\,p(y_j)$: statistically independent.

**One momentum component** (slides 13–17). Fix $p_x$ of one atom, with energy $\epsilon = p_x^2/(2m)$ (a script E on the slides); the remaining $3N - 1$ momenta share $E - \epsilon$ on a sphere of one dimension less. The ratio of phase volumes splits into factors that, for large $N$, simplify with $\lim(1 + x/n)^n = e^x$, using $\langle\epsilon\rangle = E/3N$, the average energy per momentum component (slides 14–16). The result (slide 17):

<Formula label="Distribution of one momentum component" tex="p(p_x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\langle p_x^2\rangle}}\exp\left(-\frac{p_x^2}{2\langle p_x^2\rangle}\right), \qquad \langle p_x^2\rangle = 2m\langle\epsilon\rangle" cite="mit" at="slide 17" />

a Gaussian: equal probability for all states of the whole gas becomes, for one atom, the Maxwell distribution of a velocity component.

## Summary

- Liouville's theorem: phase-space density flows incompressibly, so any density depending only on $H$ is an equilibrium density.
- Microcanonical ensemble: fixed $E$ (within $\Delta$), $V$, $N$; all accessible microstates equally probable, $p = 1/\Omega$.
- $\Phi$ is the phase volume below $E$, $\rho = \partial\Phi/\partial E$ the density of states, $\Omega = \rho\Delta$; probabilities of microscopic conditions are ratios of phase volumes.
- Ideal gas: $\Phi = V^N(2\pi mE)^M/M!$ with $M = 3N/2$; positions are uniform and independent, and one momentum component is Gaussian with $\langle p_x^2\rangle = 2mE/3N$.
