A gas of molecules is still a set of non-interacting, identical particles, so $Z = Z_1^N/N!$ and the only task is $Z_1$ for one molecule (slide 1). That task is made easy by splitting the molecule's motion into independent parts; the new feature is that some of those parts must be treated quantum mechanically (slides 1–22, numbered as printed, with 9a, 9b, 18a and 19a). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Counting the motions

A molecule of $n_a$ atoms has $3n_a$ position coordinates (the slides write # for the number of atoms), divided among centre-of-mass motion, rotation ($n_r$) and vibration ($n_v$) (slide 1):

<Formula label="Degrees of freedom" tex="3n_a = 3 + n_r + n_v" cite="mit" at="slide 1" />

A single atom cannot rotate; a linear molecule has two rotations (none about its own axis); any other has three (slide 2):

| | Monatomic (Xe) | Diatomic (HS) | Linear triatomic (CO₂) | Non-linear triatomic (H₂O) |
|:-|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| Centre of mass | 3 | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| Rotation $n_r$ | 0 | 2 | 2 | 3 |
| Vibration $n_v$ | 0 | 1 | 4 | 3 |
| Total $3n_a$ | 3 | 6 | 9 | 9 |

**Classical or quantum?** (slide 3). Centre-of-mass motion is a particle in a box whose level spacings are far below $kT$: classical. But for hydrogen, the rotational frequency $3.65 \times 10^{12}$ Hz corresponds to $h\nu/k \approx 175$ K (the gap from $l = 0$ to $l = 1$, which is $2\Theta_R$ in the notation below), and the vibrational $1.32 \times 10^{14}$ Hz to about 6320 K (6335 K by direct calculation), so rotation and vibration need quantum mechanics. Because $H = H_{\text{CM}} + H_{\text{vib}} + H_{\text{rot}}$, the problem separates: $Z_1$ is a product, and the heat capacities add.

## Vibration

Each vibrational mode is a one-dimensional harmonic oscillator, so treat it quantum mechanically (slide 4): non-degenerate levels

<Formula label="Oscillator levels" tex="\epsilon_n = \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots" cite="mit" at="slide 4" />

The canonical probabilities form a geometric series. Writing $b = e^{-\hbar\omega/kT}$ (slides 5–6),

<Formula label="Distribution of quanta" tex="p(n) = (1 - b)\,b^n, \qquad \langle n\rangle = \frac{b}{1 - b} = \frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1}" cite="mit" at="slides 5–6" />

a **geometric**, or **Bose–Einstein**, distribution. When $kT \ll \hbar\omega$, $\langle n\rangle \approx e^{-\hbar\omega/kT}$; when $kT \gg \hbar\omega$, expanding the exponential gives $\langle n\rangle \approx kT/\hbar\omega - \frac12$, so $\langle\epsilon\rangle = (\langle n\rangle + \frac12)\hbar\omega \approx kT$, the classical result, while at low $T$ the energy settles at the ground-state $\frac12\hbar\omega$ (slides 7–8).

The heat capacity of $N$ oscillators (slide 9a):

<Formula label="Vibrational heat capacity" tex="C_V = N\frac{\partial\langle\epsilon\rangle}{\partial T} = Nk\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{kT}\right)^2\frac{e^{\hbar\omega/kT}}{\left(e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="slide 9a" />

At low $T$ it is $Nk(\hbar\omega/kT)^2e^{-\hbar\omega/kT}$, the **energy-gap** behaviour of any system with a gap above its ground state; at high $T$ it tends to $Nk$. Unlike a two-level system, whose heat capacity peaks and falls because its levels saturate, the oscillator's ladder never runs out (slide 9b).

<Plot variable="t" domain={[0.05, 3]} range={[0, 1.15]} xLabel="kT/ħω" yLabel="C/Nk"
  samples={300}

  caption="Computed from the formulas: the oscillator's heat capacity rises from an energy-gap start to the classical value 1 (in units of Nk); a two-level system with the same gap ħω rises the same way at first, then falls because it saturates." />

**Limits without the full solution** (slides 10–12). At high temperature many levels lie within $kT$, so the sum for $Z_1$ becomes an integral: $Z_1 \approx kT/\hbar\omega = (\beta\hbar\omega)^{-1}$ with $\beta = 1/kT$, giving $Z_{\text{vib}} \propto \beta^{-N}$, $U_{\text{vib}} = NkT$ and $C_{\text{vib}} = Nk$. At low temperature only $n = 0$ and $n = 1$ matter: $p(1) \approx e^{-\hbar\omega/kT}$, $\langle E\rangle \approx \frac12N\hbar\omega + N\hbar\omega e^{-\hbar\omega/kT}$, and $C_V \approx Nk(\hbar\omega/kT)^2e^{-\hbar\omega/kT}$.

## Rotation

**Angular momentum** (slides 13–14). Classically it takes three numbers; quantum mechanically, only its magnitude and one component can be specified together: $L^2$ has values $l(l + 1)\hbar^2$ with $l = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, and $L_z$ has values $m\hbar$ with $m = l, l - 1, \dots, -l$, which is $2l + 1$ values.

For a **linear** molecule there is no rotation about its own axis, and the two perpendicular moments of inertia are equal, $I_\perp$, so $H_{\text{rot}} = L^2/2I_\perp$ (slides 14–15):

<Formula label="Rotational levels" tex="\epsilon_l = \frac{\hbar^2}{2I_\perp}l(l + 1) = k\Theta_R\,l(l + 1), \qquad \Theta_R \equiv \frac{\hbar^2}{2I_\perp k}" cite="mit" at="slide 15" />

each $(2l + 1)$-fold degenerate; $\Theta_R$ is the **rotational temperature**. Then (slide 16)

<Formula label="Rotational partition function" tex="Z_R = \sum_l(2l + 1)\,e^{-l(l + 1)\Theta_R/T}" cite="mit" at="slide 16" />

**Low temperature**, $T \ll \Theta_R$ (slides 16–17): keep $l = 0$ and $l = 1$, $Z_R \approx 1 + 3e^{-2\Theta_R/T}$, $\langle\epsilon\rangle \approx 6k\Theta_Re^{-2\Theta_R/T}$, and

<Formula label="Rotational heat capacity at low temperature" tex="C_{\text{rot}} \approx 3Nk\left(\frac{2\Theta_R}{T}\right)^2e^{-2\Theta_R/T}" cite="mit" at="slide 17" />

energy-gap behaviour again. **High temperature**, $T \gg \Theta_R$ (slides 17–18a): the sum becomes an integral; with $x = l(l + 1)\Theta_R/T$, $dx = (2l + 1)(\Theta_R/T)\,dl$, so $Z_R \approx T/\Theta_R$, $\langle\epsilon\rangle = kT$ and $C_{\text{rot}} = Nk$, the classical result.

**Altogether** (slide 19a; the slides write these as $C_V$ followed by a vertical evaluation bar with the subscript), $C_V = C_{\text{CM}} + C_{\text{rot}} + C_{\text{vib}}$: the centre-of-mass part ($\frac32Nk$) is there at all temperatures, rotation switches on at modest temperatures, and vibration only at the highest.

## Raman scattering

Light scattered by a molecule can leave it in a different level, and the scattered light's frequency shifts by the energy difference divided by $h$ (slide 20); which changes occur is set by selection rules from symmetry and quantum mechanics. For rotation the rule is $\Delta l = \pm2$ (slide 21), so the line from level $l$ going up to $l + 2$ is shifted by

<Formula label="Rotational Raman shift" tex="\Delta\nu = -\frac{k\Theta_R}{h}\left[(l + 2)(l + 3) - l(l + 1)\right] = -(4l + 6)\frac{k\Theta_R}{h}" cite="mit" at="slide 21" />

The lines are **uniformly spaced** by $4k\Theta_R/h$, and each line's intensity follows the number of molecules starting in level $l$, proportional to $(2l + 1)e^{-l(l + 1)\Theta_R/T}$: the degeneracy pushes it up, the Boltzmann factor down, so the spectrum rises and then falls along the series (slides 21–22).

<WorkedExample title="A diatomic gas at room temperature">
<Problem>
A diatomic molecule has $\Theta_R = 15.0$ K and $\hbar\omega/k = 4000$ K (close to hydrogen chloride). At 300 K: (a) find $\langle n\rangle$ for its vibration; (b) find $C_{\text{vib}}/Nk$; (c) find the molar $C_V$ ($R = 8.314$ J/K per mole); (d) find the rotational Raman line spacing and the most populated starting level $l$ ($k = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K, $h = 6.62607 \times 10^{-34}$ J s).
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Vibrational quanta">
$\hbar\omega/kT = 13.33$, so $\langle n\rangle = 1/(e^{13.33} - 1) \approx 1.62 \times 10^{-6}$: the vibration is frozen out.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Vibrational heat capacity">
$13.33^2e^{13.33}/(e^{13.33} - 1)^2 \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-4}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Total">
$T/\Theta_R = 20$, so rotation is classical: $C_V \approx (\frac32 + 1)R \approx 20.8$ J/K per mole, the familiar $\frac52R$ of a diatomic gas at room temperature.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Raman lines">
Spacing $4k\Theta_R/h \approx 1.250 \times 10^{12}$ Hz. Weights $(2l + 1)e^{-l(l + 1)/20}$: $l = 2$ gives 3.70, $l = 3$ gives 3.84, $l = 4$ gives 3.31, so the strongest line starts from $l = 3$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $3n_a = 3 + n_r + n_v$; $H = H_{\text{CM}} + H_{\text{rot}} + H_{\text{vib}}$, so $Z_1$ factorizes and heat capacities add.
- Translation is classical; rotation ($\sim$100 K) and vibration ($\sim$1000s of K) are quantized.
- Oscillator: $p(n) = (1 - b)b^n$, $\langle n\rangle = 1/(e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1)$; $C$ goes from energy-gap behaviour to $Nk$.
- Linear rotor: $\epsilon_l = k\Theta_Rl(l + 1)$, $(2l + 1)$-fold; $C_{\text{rot}}$ from $3Nk(2\Theta_R/T)^2e^{-2\Theta_R/T}$ to $Nk$.
- Rotational Raman lines: spacing $4k\Theta_R/h$, intensities $\propto (2l + 1)e^{-l(l + 1)\Theta_R/T}$.
