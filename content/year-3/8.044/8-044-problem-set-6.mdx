## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box wants a decimal number and accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `2.5*a*T^1.5`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent, so each part says which letters to use. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `alpha` for $\alpha$, `kappa` for $\kappa_T$, `c` for $C_V/V_0$ in Problem 1, `T0` for $T_0$, `A1` and `A2` for $A_1$ and $A_2$. Write powers with `^` (`T^2.5` for $T^{5/2}$) and type `log( )` for the natural logarithm.

MIT's sheet gives no points. The points here (100 in all) and their split between parts are ours. Parts that say "show that" have no box for the proof itself: open the solution, compare it with your own work and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons and of MIT's sheet. $S$ is the entropy, $F = E - TS$ the Helmholtz free energy and $k$ Boltzmann's constant. The energy is $U$ in Problem 1 and $E$ in the other problems, as on MIT's sheet. The surface tension of Problem 2 is written $\mathcal{S}$, to keep it apart from the entropy. No constants of nature are needed.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Sound waves in a solid" points={25} mit="Problem 1">

A sound wave is usually described as a wave of pressure. A small wave of temperature travels with it, and it matters: heat that leaks from the warm crests to the cool troughs is one of the ways a sound wave is damped.

A sound wave moves with the speed $v$ through a solid that is the same everywhere and in every direction. The small departures of the pressure and the temperature from their undisturbed values are

<Formula label="Pressure and temperature in the sound wave" tex="\Delta P(x, t) = \Delta P\cos(x - vt), \qquad \Delta T(x, t) = \Delta T\cos(x - vt)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

(The sheet writes the phase as $x - vt$, so $x$ and $vt$ are measured in a unit of length that makes it a pure number.) On the right, $\Delta P$ and $\Delta T$ are the two amplitudes. At the high frequencies that are usual for sound there is no time for heat to flow, and the process may be treated as adiabatic.

The equation of state of the solid is, to a good approximation,

<Formula label="Equation of state of the solid" tex="V = V_0\left(1 + \alpha T - \kappa_T P\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

with constant $V_0$, $\alpha$ and $\kappa_T$. The solid is a hydrostatic system (its work is $-P\,dV$), so the energy equation of the lesson holds:

<Formula label="Energy equation" tex="\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T = T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V - P" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

The aim is the ratio $\Delta T/\Delta P$ of the two amplitudes, in terms of $\alpha$, $\kappa_T$, $T$ and the heat capacity at constant volume per unit volume, $C_V/V_0$. MIT's sheet says the answer can be brought to the form of one term divided by the sum of two terms.

<Part label="a" points={9}>

Show that in an adiabatic change the solid obeys

<Formula label="Adiabatic condition for the solid" tex="C_V\,dT + \frac{\alpha T}{\kappa_T}\,dV = 0" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

On the way you need $(\partial P/\partial T)_V$ for this solid. Give it in terms of `alpha` and `kappa`.

<Hint>

Adiabatic means that no heat flows. Write the first law with $\delta Q = 0$ and with the work $-P\,dV$, then write $dU$ with $T$ and $V$ as the independent variables.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The coefficient of $dT$ is $C_V$ by definition. The coefficient of $dV$ is $(\partial U/\partial V)_T + P$: the energy equation turns it into a derivative of the equation of state. To get $(\partial P/\partial T)_V$, either solve the equation of state for $P$ or use the chain rule for three variables tied by one relation.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-dpdt" label="$\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V =$" expr="alpha/kappa"
  vars={{ alpha: [0.5, 2], kappa: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ alpha: "\\alpha", kappa: "\\kappa_T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-alpha/kappa", hint: "Check the sign. The three-variable chain rule has a minus sign, and so has the slope of V against P at fixed T: the two cancel. A solid that expands on heating pushes harder when it is heated at fixed volume." },
    { expr: "kappa/alpha", hint: "That is upside down: it is the slope of T against P at fixed V. A stiffer solid (smaller compressibility) must give a larger pressure rise per kelvin." },
    { expr: "alpha", hint: "That is the fractional expansion per kelvin at fixed pressure. To hold the volume fixed, the pressure must rise by enough to undo that expansion, which brings in the compressibility." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow MIT's three steps: the first law with no heat, the energy equation for the coefficient of $dV$, and the chain rule for the derivative of the equation of state.

<Step title="The first law with no heat">

With $\delta Q = 0$ and the work $\delta W = -P\,dV$, the first law says $dU + P\,dV = 0$. Write $dU$ in terms of $dT$ and $dV$:

<Formula label="First law for an adiabatic change" tex="0 = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V dT + \left[\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T + P\right]dV" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

The first bracket is $C_V$. By the energy equation the second is $T(\partial P/\partial T)_V$.

</Step>

<Step title="The derivative of the equation of state">

MIT uses the chain rule for three variables with one relation between them, $(\partial P/\partial T)_V = -(\partial V/\partial T)_P\,/\,(\partial V/\partial P)_T$. The equation of state gives $(\partial V/\partial T)_P = \alpha V_0$ and $(\partial V/\partial P)_T = -\kappa_T V_0$, so

<Formula label="Pressure against temperature at fixed volume" tex="\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V = -\frac{\alpha V_0}{-\kappa_T V_0} = \frac{\alpha}{\kappa_T}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Putting this into the first law gives $C_V\,dT + (\alpha T/\kappa_T)\,dV = 0$, which was to be shown.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The equation of state can also be solved for the pressure (our check): $P = (1 + \alpha T - V/V_0)/\kappa_T$. Its derivative with respect to $T$ at fixed $V$ is $\alpha/\kappa_T$, the same. In this equation of state $\alpha$ and $\kappa_T$ are the lesson's expansion coefficient and compressibility with $V_0$ in place of $V$, which makes almost no difference for the small changes of volume in a solid.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Find the ratio $\Delta T/\Delta P$ of the amplitudes, in terms of `alpha`, `kappa`, `T` and `c`, where `c` stands for $C_V/V_0$. Then say how the two waves lie with respect to each other.

<Hint>

The wave is described by its pressure and its temperature, but the condition of part (a) has $dT$ and $dV$. Which relation turns a change of volume into changes of temperature and pressure?

</Hint>

<Hint>

From the equation of state, $dV = \alpha V_0\,dT - \kappa_T V_0\,dP$. Put it into the condition of part (a) and collect the terms with $dT$. The small departures in the wave are such changes: $dT$ is $\Delta T$ and $dP$ is $\Delta P$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-ratio" label="$\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta P} =$" expr="alpha*T/(c + alpha^2*T/kappa)"
  vars={{ alpha: [0.5, 1.2], T: [0.5, 2], kappa: [1, 2], c: [3, 6] }}
  names={{ alpha: "\\alpha", kappa: "\\kappa_T", T: "T", c: "C_V/V_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "alpha*T/c", hint: "That keeps only one term of dV. A change of temperature changes the volume too, through the thermal expansion, and that adds a second term to the denominator." },
    { expr: "alpha*T/(c - alpha^2*T/kappa)", hint: "Check the sign in the denominator. Both terms that multiply dT come with the same sign: the heat capacity, and the expansion term, which holds the square of alpha." },
    { expr: "alpha*T/kappa/(c + alpha^2*T/kappa)", hint: "The compressibility in the term with dP cancels: the coefficient of dV is alpha T over kappa, and dV holds minus kappa times V0 times dP." },
    { expr: "alpha/(c + alpha^2/kappa)", hint: "A factor T is missing in two places. The coefficient of dV in the adiabatic condition is T times the slope of P against T." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-phase" label="For $\alpha > 0$, at the places where the pressure is highest the temperature is"
  choices={["lowest", "at its undisturbed value", "highest too", "not fixed by this argument: it depends on the speed of the wave"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Trade $dV$ for $dT$ and $dP$ with the equation of state. What is left is one linear relation between the small changes of temperature and pressure along an adiabat.

<Step title="From dV to dT and dP">

The equation of state gives

<Formula label="Change of volume" tex="dV = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P dT + \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T dP = \alpha V_0\,dT - \kappa_T V_0\,dP" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

In the adiabatic condition this becomes

<Formula label="Adiabatic condition with dT and dP" tex="0 = C_V\,dT + \frac{\alpha T}{\kappa_T}\left(\alpha V_0\,dT - \kappa_T V_0\,dP\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha T V_0\,dP = \left(C_V + \frac{\alpha^2 T V_0}{\kappa_T}\right)dT" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

The departures in the wave are small adiabatic changes, so $\Delta T/\Delta P$ is the ratio of $dT$ to $dP$ in this relation. Divide by $V_0$:

<Formula label="Temperature swing over pressure swing" tex="\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta P} = \frac{\alpha T}{\dfrac{C_V}{V_0} + \dfrac{\alpha^2 T}{\kappa_T}}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

This has the form MIT's sheet announces: one term over the sum of two. For $\alpha > 0$ the ratio is positive, so $\Delta T$ and $\Delta P$ have the same sign: the solid is warmest where it is squeezed hardest, and the two waves travel crest on crest. With $\alpha < 0$ the crests of one would lie on the troughs of the other.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\alpha T$ is a pure number and $C_V/V_0$ is in J m⁻³ K⁻¹, so the ratio is in K m³ J⁻¹, which is K Pa⁻¹, as it must be. The second term of the denominator, $\alpha^2T/\kappa_T$, is in Pa K⁻¹, the same unit as the first. With $\alpha = 0$ the volume does not care about the temperature, and the ratio is zero: no temperature wave.

A second check (ours) uses the lesson's result $C_P - C_V = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T$. With $V \approx V_0$ the denominator is $C_P/V_0$, so $\Delta T/\Delta P = \alpha T V_0/C_P$. The same follows in one line from the enthalpy's Maxwell relation $(\partial T/\partial P)_S = (\partial V/\partial S)_P$ together with $(\partial S/\partial T)_P = C_P/T$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

This part is our addition, with made-up round numbers. A solid has $\alpha = 5.0 \times 10^{-5}$ K⁻¹, $\kappa_T = 8.0 \times 10^{-12}$ Pa⁻¹ and $C_V/V_0 = 3.0 \times 10^{6}$ J m⁻³ K⁻¹, at $T = 300$ K. A sound wave in it has the pressure amplitude $\Delta P = 1.0 \times 10^{5}$ Pa. Find its temperature amplitude $\Delta T$ in millikelvin (1 mK is $10^{-3}$ K).

<Hint>

Work out the two terms of the denominator one at a time, in J m⁻³ K⁻¹, and see which one is larger.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The ratio of part (b) comes out in kelvin per pascal. Multiply it by the pressure amplitude, then change kelvin to millikelvin.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-dt" label="$\Delta T$ in mK $=$" value="0.485" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-dt" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into the ratio of part (b).

<Step title="The denominator">

The first term is $C_V/V_0 = 3.0 \times 10^6$ J m⁻³ K⁻¹. The second is

<Formula label="Expansion term of the denominator" tex="\frac{\alpha^2 T}{\kappa_T} = \frac{\left(5.0 \times 10^{-5}\right)^2 \times 300}{8.0 \times 10^{-12}}\ \text{J m}^{-3}\,\text{K}^{-1} = 9.4 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J m}^{-3}\,\text{K}^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own numbers)" />

It is 3.1% of the first term: by the second check of part (b), $C_P$ is 3.1% larger than $C_V$ for these numbers.

</Step>

<Step title="The temperature amplitude">

<Formula label="Temperature amplitude of the example" tex="\frac{\Delta T}{\Delta P} = \frac{5.0 \times 10^{-5} \times 300}{3.0 \times 10^{6} + 9.4 \times 10^{4}}\ \text{K Pa}^{-1} = 4.85 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{K Pa}^{-1}, \qquad \Delta T = 4.85 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{K} = 0.485\ \text{mK}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own numbers)" />

<Step title="Check the result">

Leaving out the small second term would give 0.500 mK: the full answer must be a little below that, and it is. A pressure swing of about one atmosphere moves the temperature of this solid by only half a millikelvin.

## Problem 2

MIT's sheet notes that each of Problems 2, 3 and 4 needs a Maxwell relation. You may derive it from the equality of cross derivatives, or read it from the square diagram of the lesson (MIT's "magic square").

<Exercise id="p2" title="Energy of a film" points={20} mit="Problem 2">

A surface film has the area $A$ and the surface tension

<Formula label="Surface tension of the film" tex="\mathcal{S}(T, A) = -\frac{NkT}{A - b}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

where $b$ is a constant with the units of an area. The work done on the film when its area changes is $\mathcal{S}\,dA$, so that $dE = T\,dS + \mathcal{S}\,dA$.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Show that the heat capacity at constant area, $C_A$, does not depend on the area at fixed temperature. On the way you need $(\partial S/\partial A)_T$, the slope of the entropy against the area. Give it in terms of `N`, `k`, `A` and `b`.

<Hint>

Write $C_A$ as $T$ times a derivative of the entropy. Its derivative with respect to $A$ is then a mixed second derivative of $S$, and the order of the two derivatives may be swapped.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You need $(\partial S/\partial A)_T$. The potential with the natural variables $T$ and $A$ is the free energy $F = E - TS$. Write $dF$ and set its cross derivatives equal.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-dsda" label="$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial A}\right)_T =$" expr="N*k/(A - b)"
  vars={{ N: [1, 3], k: [0.5, 2], A: [3, 6], b: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ N: "N", k: "k", A: "A", b: "b", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-N*k/(A - b)", hint: "Check the sign. The Maxwell relation from dF has a minus sign, and this surface tension is negative: the two signs cancel. The entropy of this film grows with its area." },
    { expr: "N*k*T/(A - b)", hint: "That is minus the surface tension itself. The Maxwell relation needs its derivative with respect to T at fixed area." },
    { expr: "N*k/(A - b)^2", hint: "That differentiates with respect to the area. The Maxwell relation from dF turns the slope of S against A into the slope of the surface tension against T." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find a general expression for $(\partial C_A/\partial A)_T$ and show that it is zero for this film. The expression needs a Maxwell relation.

<Step title="The heat capacity as a derivative of the entropy">

For a reversible change at constant area the heat is $T\,dS$ (the second law), so

<Formula label="Heat capacity at constant area and its slope" tex="C_A = T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_A, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial C_A}{\partial A}\right)_T = T\,\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial A\,\partial T} = T\,\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\left[\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial A}\right)_T\right]_A" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

The last step swaps the order of the two derivatives.

</Step>

<Step title="The Maxwell relation">

MIT derives the relation here instead of reading it from the square. From $dE = T\,dS + \mathcal{S}\,dA$ the free energy has $dF = dE - d(TS) = -S\,dT + \mathcal{S}\,dA$. Because $F$ is a state function, its cross derivatives are equal:

<Formula label="Maxwell relation for the film" tex="-\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial A}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{S}}{\partial T}\right)_A = -\frac{Nk}{A - b} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial A}\right)_T = \frac{Nk}{A - b}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The slope of the heat capacity">

<Formula label="The heat capacity does not depend on the area" tex="\left(\frac{\partial C_A}{\partial A}\right)_T = T\,\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\left[\frac{Nk}{A - b}\right]_A = 0" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

So $C_A(T, A) = C_A(T)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Put together, the steps say that $(\partial C_A/\partial A)_T$ is $-T$ times the second derivative of $\mathcal{S}$ with respect to $T$ at fixed area. It is zero whenever the force is a straight-line function of $T$. Here $\mathcal{S}$ is proportional to $T$, so the result is as expected.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

Show that the energy of the film depends on the temperature alone, and write the energy in terms of the heat capacity.

<Hint>

Write $dE = T\,dS + \mathcal{S}\,dA$ with $T$ and $A$ as the independent variables: expand $dS$ in $dT$ and $dA$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The coefficient of $dA$ is $T(\partial S/\partial A)_T + \mathcal{S}$. Part (a) gave the first term. What do the two terms add up to for this film?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-stretch" label="When this film is stretched slowly at fixed temperature, its energy"
  choices={["falls: the film does work and no heat flows", "stays the same: the heat that flows in equals the work the film does", "rises: heat flows in and no work is done", "rises: stretching a film always takes work"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the exact differential of $E(T, A)$. If the coefficient of $dA$ is zero, $E$ depends on $T$ alone, and integrating the coefficient of $dT$ gives the energy.

<Step title="The differential of the energy">

<Formula label="Differential of the energy of the film" tex="dE = T\,dS + \mathcal{S}\,dA = T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_A dT + \left[T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial A}\right)_T + \mathcal{S}\right]dA" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3" />

The coefficient of $dT$ is $C_A(T)$. With part (a), the coefficient of $dA$ is

<Formula label="Energy against area at fixed temperature" tex="\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial A}\right)_T = \frac{NkT}{A - b} - \frac{NkT}{A - b} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="The energy">

So $E(T, A) = E(T)$, and integrating $dE = C_A(T)\,dT$ from zero temperature gives

<Formula label="Energy of the film" tex="E(T) = \int_0^T C_A(T')\,dT' + E(T = 0)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The film is the two-dimensional copy of a gas with $P = NkT/(V - b)$: put $-\mathcal{S}$ for $P$ and $A$ for $V$. With $b = 0$ that is the ideal gas, whose energy the lesson also found to depend on $T$ alone. In an isothermal stretch the energy stays fixed, so the first law gives $\delta Q = -\delta W = -\mathcal{S}\,dA$, which is positive for $dA > 0$ because $\mathcal{S}$ is negative: heat flows in, and the film does that much work on its surroundings.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

This part is our addition. The film is stretched at fixed temperature from the area $A_1$ to the area $A_2$. Find the change of its entropy, $S(T, A_2) - S(T, A_1)$, in terms of `N`, `k`, `A1`, `A2` and `b`.

<Hint>

Part (a) gave the slope of the entropy against the area at fixed temperature. Integrate it from $A_1$ to $A_2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ds" label="$S(T, A_2) - S(T, A_1) =$" expr="N*k*log((A2 - b)/(A1 - b))"
  vars={{ N: [1, 3], k: [0.5, 2], A1: [3, 5], A2: [6, 9], b: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ N: "N", k: "k", A1: "A_1", A2: "A_2", b: "b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "N*k*log(A2/A1)", hint: "That is right only for b = 0. The slope of the entropy has A minus b in its denominator, so the logarithm holds A minus b." },
    { expr: "N*k*log((A1 - b)/(A2 - b))", hint: "Check the sign: this is negative for a stretch. The slope of the entropy against the area is positive, so the entropy grows when the area grows." },
    { expr: "N*k*(1/(A1 - b) - 1/(A2 - b))", hint: "That is the integral of one over the square of A minus b. The slope of the entropy holds one over A minus b itself, and its integral is a logarithm." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Integrate the Maxwell relation of part (a) along the isotherm.

<Step title="The integral">

<Formula label="Entropy change of the film in an isothermal stretch" tex="S(T, A_2) - S(T, A_1) = \int_{A_1}^{A_2}\frac{Nk}{A - b}\,dA = Nk\ln\frac{A_2 - b}{A_1 - b}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (the Maxwell relation; the integral is our addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $b = 0$ this is $Nk\ln(A_2/A_1)$, the two-dimensional copy of the term $Nk\ln(V/V_0)$ in the lesson's entropy of an ideal gas. Times $T$ it is the heat that flows in during the stretch, and that equals the work the film does, $-\int\mathcal{S}\,dA$, as part (b) requires.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Bose-Einstein gas" points={30} mit="Problem 3">

A gas of atoms that obey Bose-Einstein statistics goes, at a very low temperature, through a phase transition to another gas phase with quite different properties. The course comes to it at its end, in [Bose-Einstein condensation](/courses/8-044/bose-einstein-condensation). MIT's sheet adds that the transition was first seen in the laboratory in 1995, and that Wolfgang Ketterle of MIT shared the 2001 Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on it.

Below the transition temperature the equation of state and the heat capacity of the gas are

<Formula label="Equation of state and heat capacity of the Bose gas" tex="P(T, V) = aT^{5/2} + bT^3 + cV^{-2}, \qquad C_V(T, V) = dT^{3/2}V + eT^2V + fT^{1/2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

where $a$ to $f$ are constants that depend on neither $T$ nor $V$.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Find the differential of the energy, $dE(T, V)$, in terms of $dT$ and $dV$. Type the coefficient of $dV$, which is $(\partial E/\partial V)_T$, in terms of `a`, `b`, `c`, `T` and `V`.

<Hint>

Start from $dE = T\,dS - P\,dV$ and expand $dS$ in $dT$ and $dV$. The coefficient of $dT$ is the heat capacity, which is given.

</Hint>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Paramagnet" points={25} mit="Problem 4">

In a paramagnetic material the magnetization $M$ and the applied magnetic field $H$ are tied by the equation of state

<Formula label="Equation of state of the paramagnet" tex="M = \frac{AH}{T - T_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 3" />

where $A$ and $T_0$ are constants. The expression holds only for $T > T_0$. The work done on the material is $H\,dM$, so that $dE = T\,dS + H\,dM$.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Show that the heat capacity at constant magnetization, $C_M$, does not depend on $M$ at fixed $T$. On the way you need $(\partial S/\partial M)_T$. Give it in terms of `M` and `A`.

<Hint>

This runs like Problem 2(a), with $M$ in place of the area and $H$ in place of the surface tension. First solve the equation of state for the field, $H(T, M)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The free energy has $dF = -S\,dT + H\,dM$. Its cross derivatives tie $(\partial S/\partial M)_T$ to $(\partial H/\partial T)_M$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in Problem 2(a): write $(\partial C_M/\partial M)_T$ as $T$ times a derivative of $(\partial S/\partial M)_T$, and find that slope from a Maxwell relation.

<Step title="The heat capacity and its slope">

<Formula label="Heat capacity at constant magnetization and its slope" tex="C_M = T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_M, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial C_M}{\partial M}\right)_T = T\,\frac{\partial^2 S}{\partial M\,\partial T} = T\,\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\left[\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial M}\right)_T\right]_M" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4(a), p. 5" />

<Step title="The Maxwell relation">

Solved for the field, the equation of state is $H = M(T - T_0)/A$. From $dE = T\,dS + H\,dM$ the free energy has $dF = -S\,dT + H\,dM$, and its cross derivatives give

<Formula label="Maxwell relation for the paramagnet" tex="-\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial M}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial T}\right)_M = \frac{M}{A}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4(a), p. 5" />

</Step>

<Step title="The slope of the heat capacity">

<Formula label="The heat capacity does not depend on the magnetization" tex="\left(\frac{\partial C_M}{\partial M}\right)_T = T\,\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\left[-\frac{M}{A}\right]_M = 0" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4(a), p. 6" />

So $C_M(T, M) = C_M(T)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

As in Problem 2, the force (here $H$) is a straight-line function of $T$ at fixed $M$, so its second derivative with respect to $T$ is zero, and that second derivative is what $(\partial C_M/\partial M)_T$ is made of.

</Step>
