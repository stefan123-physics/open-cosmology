The second law made entropy a **state function**. This lecture puts that to work: because $S$ (and, below, the energy and its relatives) has a well-defined value in every equilibrium state, its mixed second derivatives are equal, and that single fact generates relations between measurable quantities that do not mention $S$ at all (slides 1–15; notes pp. 1–5). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides and his note "Maxwell Relations: A Wealth of Partial Derivatives"; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Entropy as a state function

The second law (slide 1): $\Delta S \ge 0$ for an isolated system, and $dS_1 \ge \delta Q_1/T_{\text{bath}}$ for a system exchanging heat with a bath. For a quasi-static change $dS = \delta Q/T$, so a quasi-static **adiabatic** change ($\delta Q = 0$) keeps $S$ constant (slide 2). That is why the adiabatic compressibility carries the subscript $S$:

<Formula label="Isothermal and adiabatic compressibilities" tex="\kappa_T \equiv -\frac1V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T, \qquad \kappa_S \equiv -\frac1V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_S" cite="mit" at="slide 2" />

## A relation without entropy

Take a hydrostatic system (a gas or liquid whose only work is $-P\,dV$), with $T$ and $V$ as the independent variables (slides 3–4). From $dU = T\,dS - P\,dV$,

<Formula label="dS in terms of T and V" tex="dS = \frac1T\,dU + \frac PT\,dV = \frac1T\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V dT + \frac1T\left[\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T + P\right]dV" cite="mit" at="slide 3" />

The coefficients are $(\partial S/\partial T)_V$ and $(\partial S/\partial V)_T$, and since $S$ is a state function the derivative of the first with respect to $V$ equals the derivative of the second with respect to $T$. The terms with $\partial^2U/\partial T\,\partial V$ cancel, leaving

<Formula label="Energy equation" tex="\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T = T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V - P" cite="mit" at="slide 4" />

**New information that does not contain $S$**: how the energy changes with volume follows from the equation of state alone.

## Consequences

**(a) The difference of the heat capacities** (slides 5–6). With $\delta Q = dU + P\,dV$ expanded in $T$ and $V$, holding $P$ fixed gives $C_P = C_V + [(\partial U/\partial V)_T + P](\partial V/\partial T)_P$, and the energy equation replaces the bracket by $T(\partial P/\partial T)_V$. Writing $\alpha \equiv \frac1V(\partial V/\partial T)_P$ for the expansion coefficient, and using $(\partial P/\partial T)_V = \alpha/\kappa_T$ (a chain-rule identity),

<Formula label="Difference of the heat capacities" tex="C_P - C_V = \frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T}" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

which is never negative, since $\kappa_T > 0$ for any stable system. Dividing by $C_V$ gives the ratio of the heat capacities, $\gamma \equiv C_P/C_V$:

<Formula label="Ratio of the heat capacities" tex="\gamma - 1 = \frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_TC_V}" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

For an ideal gas $\alpha = 1/T$ and $\kappa_T = 1/P$, so $C_P - C_V = PV/T = Nk$, for polyatomic as well as monatomic gases (slide 6).

**(b) The ideal gas's energy** (slide 7). With $P = NkT/V$, $T(\partial P/\partial T)_V - P = P - P = 0$: the energy of an ideal gas does not depend on its volume. So $dU = C_V\,dT$, $C_V$ can depend only on $T$, and

<Formula label="Energy of an ideal gas" tex="U = \int_0^T C_V(T')\,dT' + \text{constant}" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

**(c) The ideal gas's entropy** (slides 8–10). Now $(\partial S/\partial T)_V = C_V/T$ and $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = \frac1T[(\partial U/\partial V)_T + P] = Nk/V$, so $dS = \frac{C_V(T)}{T}dT + \frac{Nk}{V}dV$ and

<Formula label="Entropy of an ideal gas" tex="S(T, V) = \int_{T_0}^T\frac{C_V(T')}{T'}\,dT' + Nk\ln\frac{V}{V_0} + S(T_0, V_0)" cite="mit" at="slide 9" />

For a monatomic gas, $C_V = \frac32Nk$, and $S(T, V) - S(T_0, V_0) = Nk\ln[(V/V_0)(T/T_0)^{3/2}]$: the same as the microcanonical result, now built from measured quantities. Isentropic (adiabatic) changes keep $VT^{3/2}$, and so $V^{5/3}P$, constant (slide 10).

## Maxwell relations

**From the energy** (slide 11; notes p. 1). Regard $E$ (the internal energy; the notes use $E$ here, as Reif does) as a function of its **natural variables** $S$ and $V$. Its differential, $dE = T\,dS - P\,dV$, has the conjugate variables as coefficients: $(\partial E/\partial S)_V = T$ and $(\partial E/\partial V)_S = -P$. Equal cross derivatives give

<Formula label="Maxwell relation from E(S, V)" tex="\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_S = -\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial S}\right)_V" cite="mit" at="slide 11" />

The same works for other systems (slide 12): a wire, with $dE = T\,dS + F\,dL$, gives $(\partial T/\partial L)_S = (\partial F/\partial S)_L$; a magnet, with $dE = T\,dS + H\,dM$, gives $(\partial T/\partial M)_S = (\partial H/\partial S)_M$.

**Other potentials** (slides 12–14; notes pp. 2–3). The trick needs a state function whose differential has thermodynamic variables as coefficients. Using $d(TS) = T\,dS + S\,dT$ and $d(PV) = P\,dV + V\,dP$ to swap a variable for its conjugate gives three more, each with units of energy:

<Formula label="Helmholtz free energy" tex="F \equiv E - TS, \qquad dF = -S\,dT - P\,dV, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V" cite="mit" at="slide 13" />

<Formula label="Enthalpy" tex="H \equiv E + PV, \qquad dH = T\,dS + V\,dP, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_S = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_P" cite="mit" at="slide 13" />

<Formula label="Gibbs free energy" tex="G \equiv E + PV - TS, \qquad dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

$E$, $F$, $H$ and $G$ are the **thermodynamic potentials**; their natural variables are $(S, V)$, $(T, V)$, $(S, P)$ and $(T, P)$. Their physical meaning comes later; for now they are machines for derivative relations. (Here $F$ and $H$ are the free energy and enthalpy, not the wire's tension or the magnetic field of slide 12.)

**The magic square** (slide 15; notes pp. 4–5). A diagram holds all of this. The four potentials go at the corners, in alphabetical order going clockwise from the top left. Each side carries the natural variable that its two corners share, with a minus sign where that variable's term in their differentials is negative:

| | | |
|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| $E$ | $-V$ | $F$ |
| $S$ | | $-T$ |
| $H$ | $P$ | $G$ |

Read off any corner: $E$ lies between $S$ and $-V$, so $dE = T\,dS - P\,dV$, each variable paired with its conjugate from the opposite side. Conjugate variables ($S$ and $T$, $V$ and $P$) face each other with opposite signs.

To get a Maxwell relation, walk round all four sides in order (either direction), then turn back along two of them. The six variables you pass fill the six places in the two derivatives, and the signs you pass go with them. Starting on the left side and going down and round, the sides passed are $S$, $P$, $-T$, $-V$; turning back adds $-T$ and $P$. The first three fill $(\partial S/\partial P)_T$ with a sign $-1$ from $-T$, the last three fill $(\partial V/\partial T)_P$ with signs from $-V$ and $-T$, giving slide 15's example, $(-1)(\partial S/\partial P)_T = (-1)(-1)(\partial V/\partial T)_P$, which is the Gibbs relation above. For a general work term $X\,dx$ the same square applies with $x$ on the top side in place of $V$ (and $-X$ on the bottom side in place of $P$).

<WorkedExample title="Using the relations">
<Problem>
(a) One mole of a van der Waals gas obeys $P = RT/(V - b) - a/V^2$, with $a = 0.137$ Pa m⁶ per mole squared for nitrogen. How much does its energy change if it expands at constant temperature from 0.0224 m³ to 0.0448 m³? (b) Water at 293 K has $\alpha \approx 2.07 \times 10^{-4}$ K⁻¹, $\kappa_T \approx 4.59 \times 10^{-10}$ Pa⁻¹ and molar volume $1.805 \times 10^{-5}$ m³. Find $C_P - C_V$ per mole. (c) One mole of a monatomic ideal gas is heated from 300 K to 600 K while its volume doubles ($R = 8.314$ J/K per mole). Find its entropy change.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Energy of a non-ideal gas">
$T(\partial P/\partial T)_V - P = RT/(V - b) - P = a/V^2$, so $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = a/V^2$ and $\Delta U = a(1/V_1 - 1/V_2) = 0.137 \times (44.64 - 22.32) \approx 3.06$ J. Attractions between molecules store energy that changes with spacing; an ideal gas would give zero.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Water">
$TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T = 293 \times 1.805 \times 10^{-5} \times (2.07 \times 10^{-4})^2/(4.59 \times 10^{-10}) \approx 0.494$ J/K per mole, under 1% of water's $C_P$ of about 75 J/K per mole: for liquids and solids the two heat capacities are nearly equal.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Entropy change">
$\Delta S = \frac32R\ln 2 + R\ln 2 = \frac52R\ln 2 \approx 14.41$ J/K.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Entropy is a state function; quasi-static adiabatic changes keep it constant.
- Equal mixed derivatives of $S(T, V)$ give $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = T(\partial P/\partial T)_V - P$, without $S$; hence $C_P - C_V = TV\alpha^2/\kappa_T$ ($Nk$ for an ideal gas), an ideal-gas energy depending on $T$ alone, and the ideal gas's $S(T, V)$.
- Thermodynamic potentials $E(S, V)$, $F = E - TS$ $(T, V)$, $H = E + PV$ $(S, P)$ and $G = E + PV - TS$ $(T, P)$; equal cross derivatives give the four Maxwell relations, remembered with the square diagram.
