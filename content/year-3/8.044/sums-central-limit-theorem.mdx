Many quantities in physics are sums: the energy of a gas is a sum over its molecules, the displacement after a random walk a sum of steps. This lecture finds how sums of random variables behave, from their means and variances to their full densities, and ends with the central limit theorem, the reason the Gaussian is so common (slides 1–19; notes pp. 47–66). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides and his probability notes, Chapter 4; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The notes' figures are not reproduced. In the formulas for the density of a sum, this page writes $\zeta$ for the value of the sum and $\eta$ for the integration variable; the notes use other letters.)

## Means and variances add

Let $S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n x_i$ for $n$ statistically independent variables, with means $E_i$ and variances $\sigma_i^2$; their densities need not be alike (notes p. 47). The mean of the sum is the sum of the means. For the variance, expanding $\langle(S_n - \langle S_n\rangle)^2\rangle$ gives terms $\langle(x_i - E_i)(x_j - E_j)\rangle$, which vanish for $i \ne j$ by independence (notes pp. 47–48):

<Formula label="Mean and variance of a sum" tex="\langle S_n\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^nE_i, \qquad \text{Var}(S_n) = \sum_{i=1}^n\sigma_i^2" cite="reading1" at="pp. 47–48" />

These hold for any $n$, for continuous and discrete variables alike, even when one term dominates (slides 1–2). For identical variables, $\langle S_n\rangle = nE$ and $\text{Var}(S_n) = n\sigma^2$: the mean grows like $n$ but the width only like $\sqrt n$, so the density becomes sharply peaked about its mean as $n$ grows (notes p. 48; slide 3). This is why the thermodynamic properties of large systems are so well defined.

## The density of a sum: convolution

For the whole density of $s = x + y$, the three-step method of the last lecture works: $x + y < \zeta$ below the line $y = \zeta - x$, so (notes pp. 48–49; slides 4–5)

<Formula label="Density of a sum" tex="p_s(\zeta) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\eta\;p_{x,y}\left(\eta, \zeta - \eta\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 49" />

valid even when $x$ and $y$ are dependent; for the jointly Gaussian pair of Lecture 2 it gives a Gaussian of zero mean and variance $2\sigma^2(1 + \rho)$ (slide 5). For independent variables it is a **convolution** (notes p. 49; slide 6):

<Formula label="Convolution" tex="p_s(\zeta) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_x(\eta)\,p_y(\zeta - \eta)\,d\eta" cite="reading1" at="p. 49" />

In general the convolution of two functions $p$ and $q$ (written p ⊗ q in the notes) is $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(z)\,q(x - z)\,dz$. Convolution is commutative, distributive and associative. To picture it: flip $q$ about the origin, shift it by $x$, multiply by $p$, integrate, and repeat for each $x$ (notes pp. 49–51).

**Examples** (notes pp. 51–55; slides 7–13). Two independent uniform variables, on $|x| \le a$ and $|y| \le b$ with $b < a$, sum to a trapezoid, flat for $|S| < a - b$ and falling linearly to zero at $a + b$; equal widths give a triangle. Unpolarized thermal light is two independent, equally bright polarizations, each with the intensity density of Lecture 3; their convolution is a pure exponential, $p(I_T) = \frac{1}{2\sigma}e^{-I_T/(2\sigma)}$, where $\sigma$ is now the mean intensity of one polarization (the notes reuse the letter this way; on the previous page $\sigma^2$ was the variance of the field). In the slides, two members of the family $\frac{1}{n!\,a}(z/a)^ne^{-z/a}$ convolve into a member of the same family; and the total time an atom of a hydrogen maser spends on the wall of its storage flask, after $n$ independent exponential stays of mean $\tau$, has the density $\frac{1}{k!\,\tau}(t/\tau)^ke^{-t/\tau}$ with $k = n - 1$.

**Forms that survive** (notes pp. 55–59; slides 14–15). Convolution usually changes the shape, but not always. The sum of independent **Gaussians** is Gaussian, with means and variances adding; the sum of independent **Poisson** variables is Poisson; and the sum of independent **Lorentzians** (Cauchy densities)

<Formula label="Lorentzian density" tex="p(x) = \frac{1}{\pi}\,\frac{\Gamma}{(x - m)^2 + \Gamma^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 56" />

is Lorentzian with the half-widths $\Gamma$ adding, not their squares: the Lorentzian's variance is infinite, so the rule for variances does not apply (notes pp. 56 and 60). The easy way to see all three is with Fourier transforms: the transform of a convolution is the product of the transforms, and these three transforms keep their form when multiplied (notes pp. 58–59).

## The central limit theorem

When all $n$ variables are independent, identically distributed, with finite variance, and $n$ is large (notes p. 60; slides 16–18):

<Formula label="Central limit theorem" tex="p(S_n) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi n\sigma_x^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(S_n - nE_x\right)^2}{2n\sigma_x^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 60" />

The finite variance excludes cases like the Lorentzian. "For large $n$" is often satisfied quickly. And "well represented" is deliberately loose: a sum of discrete variables stays discrete, a comb of delta functions whose envelope approaches the Gaussian (notes pp. 60–61; slide 18). Useful non-rigorous extensions: the Gaussian is often a good approximation for modest $n$; the theorem may work even when the variables are not identical, provided none dominates the sum; and even independence can sometimes be relaxed when $n$ is very large (notes p. 61; slide 19). Knowingly or not, the theorem is often behind the assumption that a physical quantity is Gaussian.

**How fast?** (notes pp. 62–66). For four uniform variables on $(-a, a)$, the exact density, a piecewise cubic, is already close to the Gaussian of variance $4a^2/3$. A one-sided exponential, discontinuous and lopsided, needs more terms: the sum of $n$ exponentials of mean $a$ has the density $\frac{S^ke^{-S/a}}{k!\,a^{k+1}}$ with $k = n - 1$, which approaches the Gaussian only slowly. A Poisson density of large mean, itself a sum of Poissons, approaches a Gaussian envelope too.

<WorkedExample title="Dice, light and a maser">
<Problem>
(a) Ten fair dice are rolled and added. Find the mean and standard deviation of the total, and estimate with the central limit theorem the probability that the total is 45 or more. (b) For unpolarized thermal light with mean total intensity $\langle I_T\rangle$, what fraction of the time is the intensity above twice its mean? (c) An atom makes 25 independent stays on a wall, each exponential with mean $\tau$. Find the mean and standard deviation of the total time.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Dice">
One die has mean 3.5 and variance $\frac{35}{12} \approx 2.92$. Ten dice: mean 35, variance about 29.2, standard deviation about 5.40. A total of 45 or more, with the half-step correction for a discrete sum, starts at 44.5, which is $9.5/5.40 \approx 1.76$ standard deviations above the mean; the Gaussian gives about 0.039.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Bright moments">
$p(I_T) = \frac{1}{\langle I_T\rangle}e^{-I_T/\langle I_T\rangle}$, so $\text{prob}(I_T > 2\langle I_T\rangle) = e^{-2} \approx 0.135$: thermal light is bright in bursts.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Time on the wall">
Means and variances add: $25\tau$ and $25\tau^2$, so the standard deviation is $5\tau$, a fifth of the mean, while for a single stay it equals the mean.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- For independent variables means add and variances add; a sum of $n$ similar terms has relative width $\propto 1/\sqrt n$.
- The density of a sum is $\int p_{x,y}(\eta, \zeta - \eta)\,d\eta$, the convolution of $p_x$ and $p_y$ when they are independent.
- Gaussians, Poissons and Lorentzians keep their form under addition; Fourier transforms make this easy to see.
- Central limit theorem: a sum of many independent, identical variables of finite variance is close to Gaussian, with mean $nE$ and variance $n\sigma^2$.
