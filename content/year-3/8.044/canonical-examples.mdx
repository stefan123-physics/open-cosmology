The canonical ensemble now does real work. This lecture explains why it is easier than the microcanonical one and applies it to three systems: the ideal gas, harmonic oscillators and a two-level impurity (slides 1–23). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Why the canonical ensemble is easier

The probability density of a microstate is $p(\{p, q\}) \propto e^{-H(\{p, q\})/kT}$, but the probability density of the **energy** is not simply $e^{-E/kT}$ (slide 1): it also contains the density of states. (The page's own explanation: for a system with many degrees of freedom the density of states grows rapidly with $E$, so $p(E)$ rises and then falls; for a single one-dimensional oscillator it happens to be constant, and $p(E)$ is exponential after all.)

**One integrates over all of phase space** (slide 2), instead of over the thin shell of constant energy that the microcanonical ensemble requires.

**Separation** (slides 3–4). If the Hamiltonian splits into independent parts, $H = H_a + H_b$, then $e^{-H/kT} = e^{-H_a/kT}e^{-H_b/kT}$: the parts are statistically independent, and

<Formula label="Separation" tex="Z = Z_aZ_b, \qquad F = F_a + F_b, \qquad S = S_a + S_b" cite="mit" at="slide 3" />

For $N$ similar, non-interacting systems, $Z = Z_1^N$, $F = NF_1$, $S = NS_1$. For $N$ **indistinguishable** particles, swapping two of them gives no new state, so (slide 4)

<Formula label="Correct Boltzmann counting" tex="Z = \frac{Z_1^N}{N!}" cite="mit" at="slide 4" />

## The classical ideal gas

$H = \sum_{i=1}^N\vec p_i\cdot\vec p_i/2m$, a sum of one-atom terms (slide 5). One atom's probability density is a Gaussian in each momentum component, with $\langle p^2\rangle = \langle p_x^2 + p_y^2 + p_z^2\rangle = 3mkT$, so $\langle H_1\rangle = \frac32kT$. Its partition function (slide 6), dividing the phase-space integral by $h^3$:

<Formula label="One atom" tex="Z_1 = \int e^{-(p_x^2 + p_y^2 + p_z^2)/2mkT}\,\frac{dp_x\,dp_y\,dp_z\,dx\,dy\,dz}{h^3} = \frac{V}{\lambda^3}, \qquad \lambda \equiv \frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi mkT}}" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

where $\lambda(T)$ is the **thermal de Broglie wavelength**, and so $Z(T, V, N) = \frac1{N!}(V/\lambda^3)^N$. With Stirling's approximation (slide 7),

<Formula label="Free energy of the ideal gas" tex="F = -kT\ln Z = -NkT\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} - NkT" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

and, since $\lambda^3 \propto T^{-3/2}$ (slide 7),

<Formula label="Pressure, entropy and energy of the ideal gas" tex="P = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial V}\right)_{T,N} = \frac{NkT}{V}, \qquad S = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_{V,N} = Nk\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} + \frac52Nk, \qquad E = F + TS = \frac32NkT" cite="mit" at="slide 8" />

The entropy, the **Sackur–Tetrode** formula (the name is standard, not on the slide), is extensive because of the $N!$ (a remark of this page). On an adiabat $S$ is constant, so $V/N\lambda^3$ is constant: $VT^{3/2}$ is constant, as before (slide 9; one step on the slide writes $V/T^{3/2}$, a slip, since its final line gives $V/V_0 = (T/T_0)^{-3/2}$).

## Classical harmonic oscillators

One oscillator has $H_1 = p^2/2m + \frac12Kx^2$ (slide 10). Its probability density factorizes into two Gaussians, with variances $mkT$ for $p$ and $kT/K$ for $x$, so $p$ and $x$ are statistically independent, and

<Formula label="One oscillator" tex="Z_1 = \frac{2\pi}{h}\sqrt{\frac mK}\,kT" cite="mit" at="slide 10" />

For $N$ similar stationary oscillators, $Z = Z_1^N$ (no $N!$: fixed in place, they are distinguishable) (slides 11–12):

<Formula label="Oscillators: thermodynamics" tex="F = -NkT\ln Z_1, \qquad S = Nk\ln Z_1 + Nk, \qquad E = F + TS = NkT" cite="mit" at="slides 11–12" />

Since $Z_1$ depends only on $T$, an adiabat of classical oscillators is a path of **constant temperature**. And the heat capacity is $C = Nk$, independent of temperature, and that stays true when the oscillators do not all share one frequency (slide 12).

## Classical and quantum

The same ensemble in both languages (slide 13): classically $p(\{p, q\}) = e^{-H/kT}/Z$ with $Z = \int e^{-H/kT}\{dp, dq\}$ divided by a power of $h$ set by the dimension of the phase space; quantum mechanically

<Formula label="Quantum canonical ensemble" tex="p(\text{state}) = \frac{e^{-E_{\text{state}}/kT}}{Z}, \qquad Z = \sum_{\text{states}}e^{-E_{\text{state}}/kT}" cite="mit" at="slide 13" />

## A two-level impurity

An impurity in a solid has a ground state at energy $0$ and an excited level at $\epsilon$ that is **$g$-fold degenerate** (slide 14): it might sit in one of $g$ equivalent positions, or have an internal excitation. Label the ground state $0$ and the excited states $1$ to $g$ (kets on slide 15):

<Formula label="Two-level impurity" tex="Z_1 = \sum_{\text{states}}e^{-E_{\text{state}}/kT} = 1 + ge^{-\epsilon/kT}, \qquad p(0) = \frac{1}{1 + ge^{-\epsilon/kT}}, \qquad p(i) = \frac{e^{-\epsilon/kT}}{1 + ge^{-\epsilon/kT}}" cite="mit" at="slide 15" />

for each excited state $i = 1, \dots, g$. At high temperature every state is equally likely: the ground state has probability $1/(1 + g)$ and the excited level as a whole $g/(1 + g)$ (slide 16).

For $N$ impurities (slides 18–19), $Z = Z_1^N$ and $F = -NkT\ln Z_1$, giving

<Formula label="Impurities: entropy and energy" tex="S = Nk\ln\left(1 + ge^{-\epsilon/kT}\right) + Nk\,\frac{\epsilon}{kT}\,\frac{ge^{-\epsilon/kT}}{1 + ge^{-\epsilon/kT}}, \qquad U = F + TS = N\epsilon\,\frac{ge^{-\epsilon/kT}}{1 + ge^{-\epsilon/kT}}" cite="mit" at="slide 19" />

that is, $U = N\epsilon\,p(E = \epsilon)$. The heat capacity $C = dU/dT$ peaks at $kT$ between about $0.32\epsilon$ ($g = 5$) and $0.42\epsilon$ ($g = 1$), and a larger $g$ gives a taller peak (slide 17):

<Plot variable="t" domain={[0.05, 2.8]} range={[0, 1.6]} xLabel="kT/ε" yLabel="C/Nk"
  samples={300}
  curves={[
    { expr: "g * (1/t)^2 * exp(-1/t) / (1 + g * exp(-1/t))^2", label: "C/Nk" }

  caption="Computed from C = dU/dT for the impurity energy above: C/Nk = g(ε/kT)² e^(−ε/kT)/(1 + g e^(−ε/kT))². Move g from 1 to 5: more excited states give more entropy to gain, so a taller peak that moves to lower temperature." />

**Pressure from a level that moves** (slides 18–19). If squeezing the solid shifts the level as $\epsilon = \epsilon_0(V/V_0)^{-\gamma}$, then $F$ depends on $V$ through $\epsilon$, and

<Formula label="Pressure of the impurities" tex="P = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\epsilon}\right)_T\frac{\partial\epsilon}{\partial V} = \gamma\,\frac UV" cite="mit" at="slide 19" />

## A shortcut to the energy

Usually, but not always, $U = \langle H\rangle$ (slide 20). Write $\beta \equiv 1/kT$, so $Z = c\int e^{-\beta H}\{dp, dq\}$ with $c$ the constant factor. Differentiating with respect to $\beta$ brings down $-H$, and dividing by $Z$ turns the integral into an average with the canonical weights (slide 21):

<Formula label="Energy from Z" tex="U = -\frac1Z\left(\frac{\partial Z}{\partial\beta}\right)_{N,V} = -\left(\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}\right)_{N,V}" cite="mit" at="slide 21" />

For the ideal gas $Z \propto \beta^{-3N/2}$, so $U = \frac{3N}{2\beta} = \frac32NkT$ (slide 22). For the impurities $Z = (1 + ge^{-\beta\epsilon})^N$, and the derivative gives back $U = gN\epsilon e^{-\epsilon/kT}/(1 + ge^{-\epsilon/kT})$ (slide 23).

<WorkedExample title="Helium in a box, and an impurity">
<Problem>
Helium atoms ($m = 6.6465 \times 10^{-27}$ kg) at 300 K and 101 325 Pa. Use $h = 6.62607 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and $k = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K. (a) Find the thermal de Broglie wavelength. (b) Find the number density $n = N/V$ and $n\lambda^3$. (c) Find the entropy per mole from the Sackur–Tetrode formula ($R = 8.314$ J/K per mole). (d) Separately, an impurity with $g = 2$ is at $kT = \epsilon$. What is the probability that it is excited?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Wavelength">
$2\pi mkT \approx 1.730 \times 10^{-46}$, so $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mkT} \approx 5.04 \times 10^{-11}$ m, about a twentieth of a nanometre.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) How classical is it?">
$n = P/kT \approx 2.446 \times 10^{25}$ m⁻³, and $n\lambda^3 \approx 3.13 \times 10^{-6}$. The atoms are much farther apart than $\lambda$, which is why the classical treatment (and the $1/N!$) works.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Entropy">
$S/Nk = \ln(1/n\lambda^3) + \frac52 \approx 12.68 + 2.50 = 15.18$, so $S \approx 15.18 \times 8.314 \approx 126.2$ J/K per mole, very close to the value measured for helium gas at room temperature.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Impurity">
$2e^{-1}/(1 + 2e^{-1}) \approx 0.736/1.736 \approx 0.424$: the two excited states together are occupied 42% of the time, against 27% for $g = 1$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Canonical: $p(\{p, q\}) \propto e^{-H/kT}$ (but $p(E)$ includes the density of states); integrate over all phase space; independent parts multiply $Z$ and add $F$ and $S$.
- $Z = Z_1^N$ for distinguishable systems, $Z_1^N/N!$ for identical particles.
- Ideal gas: $Z_1 = V/\lambda^3$ with $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mkT}$; $PV = NkT$, $E = \frac32NkT$, $S = Nk[\ln(V/N\lambda^3) + \frac52]$.
- Classical oscillators: $Z_1 \propto kT$, $E = NkT$, $C = Nk$; adiabats are isotherms.
- Two-level impurity: $Z_1 = 1 + ge^{-\epsilon/kT}$; heat-capacity peak grows with $g$; $P = \gamma U/V$ if $\epsilon \propto V^{-\gamma}$.
- $U = -\partial\ln Z/\partial\beta$ with $\beta = 1/kT$.
