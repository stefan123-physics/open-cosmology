## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it; type a very small number as `1.2e-7`. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `3*a^2*exp(-d/a)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent, so where letters are related (as $V_2T_2^3 = V_1T_1^3$ in Problem 5) the problem says which of them to use. Type `exp(...)` for an exponential, `sqrt(...)` for a square root and `pi` for $\pi$. Write the symbols as the box names them: `P1`, `T1`, `V1` for $P_1$, $T_1$, $V_1$, `kappa` for $\kappa_T$, `alpha` for $\alpha$, `sigma` for $\sigma$, `Theta` for $\Theta$, `Hp`, `Mp`, `Cp` for $H'$, $M'$, $C'$, `M0` for $M_0$ and `Tc` for $T_c$.

The points per problem and per part are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem or part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Clearing impurities" points={12} mit="Problem 1">

To clear impurities out of a nano-wire, a laser beam is swept along the wire again and again while an electric field points along the wire. One sweep moves an impurity that starts at $x = 0$ to a new position $x$, with the probability density

<Formula label="Density of the step in one sweep" tex="p(x) = \frac13\,\delta(x) + \frac{2}{3a}\exp\left(-\frac{x}{a}\right) \quad\text{for}\quad x \ge 0, \qquad p(x) = 0 \quad\text{for}\quad x < 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

where $a$ is a fixed length. In words: with probability $\frac13$ the impurity stays where it is (the delta function), and otherwise it moves forward by a distance that is spread exponentially.

Give an approximate probability density for the total distance $d$ the impurity has moved along the wire after 36 sweeps.

The first two boxes are our addition, as steps on the way: the mean of $d$ and the variance of $d$.

<Hint>

The total distance is a sum of 36 steps. Each sweep is independent of the others and has the same density. Which theorem of the lesson gives the density of such a sum, and which two numbers does it need?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Find the mean $\langle x\rangle$ and the mean square $\langle x^2\rangle$ of one step. The delta function sits at $x = 0$, so it adds nothing to either. You need $\int_0^\infty u\,e^{-u}\,du = 1$ and $\int_0^\infty u^2e^{-u}\,du = 2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For independent steps the mean of the sum is the sum of the means, and the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances. Put both into a Gaussian.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-mean" label="$\langle d\rangle =$" expr="24*a"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*a/3", hint: "That is the mean of one step. The total distance is the sum of 36 of them." },
    { expr: "36*a", hint: "That would be right if every sweep moved the impurity. One sweep in three leaves it in place, and the exponential part carries only the weight 2/3." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-var" label="$\text{Var}(d) =$" expr="32*a^2"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "48*a^2", hint: "That is 36 times the mean square of one step. Subtract the square of the mean of one step before you multiply by 36." },
    { expr: "8*a^2/9", hint: "That is the variance of one step. The variances of the 36 independent steps add." },
    { expr: "1152*a^2", hint: "Variances add, they do not multiply by 36 squared: that would hold only if all 36 steps were the same step." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-density" label="$p(d) \approx$" expr="exp(-(d - 24*a)^2/(64*a^2))/(8*a*sqrt(pi))"
  vars={{ d: [15, 33], a: [0.9, 1.2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-(d - 24*a)^2/(32*a^2))/(8*a*sqrt(pi))", hint: "The exponent of a Gaussian holds twice the variance below the line, and the variance is 32a²." },
    { expr: "exp(-(d - 24*a)^2/(64*a^2))/(8*sqrt(pi))", hint: "A density of a length has the unit of one over a length. Yours is a pure number: check the factor in front." },
    { expr: "2/(3*a)*exp(-d/a)", hint: "That is the moving part of a single step. After 36 steps the sum is close to a Gaussian." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The total distance is the sum of 36 independent steps with the same density. By the central limit theorem such a sum is close to a Gaussian, whatever the density of one step looks like, and the Gaussian is fixed by the mean and the variance of the sum.

<Step title="One step">

The delta function contributes nothing to $\langle x\rangle$ or $\langle x^2\rangle$, because it sits at $x = 0$. With $u = x/a$:

<Formula label="Mean and mean square of one step" tex="\langle x\rangle = \int_0^\infty x\,\frac{2}{3a}\,e^{-x/a}\,dx = \frac23\,a\int_0^\infty u\,e^{-u}\,du = \frac23\,a, \qquad \langle x^2\rangle = \frac23\,a^2\int_0^\infty u^2e^{-u}\,du = \frac43\,a^2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

<Formula label="Variance of one step" tex="\text{Var}(x) = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2 = \left(\frac43 - \frac49\right)a^2 = \frac89\,a^2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Thirty-six steps">

Means add, and for independent steps variances add:

<Formula label="Mean and variance of the total distance" tex="\langle d\rangle = 36\,\langle x\rangle = 24\,a, \qquad \sigma^2 = \text{Var}(d) = 36\,\text{Var}(x) = 32\,a^2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The Gaussian">

<Formula label="Approximate density of the total distance" tex="p(d) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(d - \langle d\rangle\right)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) = \frac{1}{8\sqrt\pi\,a}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(d - 24a\right)^2}{64\,a^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

<Plot variable="d" domain={[0, 50]} range={[0, 0.08]} xLabel="d / a" yLabel="a p(d)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "exp(-(d - 24)^2/64)/(8*sqrt(pi))", label: "p(d)" }
  ]}
  caption="The approximate density of the total distance after 36 sweeps, computed from the Gaussian above (MIT's solutions show the same sketch). It peaks at d = 24a with the height 0.0705/a." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The density has the unit of one over a length. Its peak height is $1/\left(8\sqrt\pi\,a\right) = 0.0705/a$, and its standard deviation is $\sqrt{32}\,a = 5.66\,a$. The mean and the variance are exact: only the Gaussian shape is an approximation. One sign of that: the true density is zero for $d < 0$, because every step is forward, while a Gaussian never quite reaches zero. Here $d = 0$ lies $24/\sqrt{32} = 4.24$ standard deviations below the mean, so the Gaussian puts almost nothing there.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Probability densities of macroscopic and of microscopic variables" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

One cubic centimetre holds a dilute gas of atoms of mass $M$, in thermal equilibrium at the temperature $T = 0$ °C and at atmospheric pressure. At that temperature and pressure one cubic metre of an ideal gas holds $2.69 \times 10^{25}$ atoms or molecules (Loschmidt's number, which MIT's sheet spells "Lochmidt").

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean for the kinetic energy $E_1$ of a single atom, as a number. You may use the density found in [Problem 4 of Problem Set 2](/courses/8-044/8-044-problem-set-2#p4):

<Formula label="Density of the kinetic energy of one atom" tex="p(E_1) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\,\frac{1}{kT}\sqrt{\frac{E_1}{kT}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{E_1}{kT}\right) \quad\text{for}\quad E_1 \ge 0" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

<Hint>

You need $\langle E_1\rangle$ and $\langle E_1^2\rangle$. Substitute $u = E_1/kT$ in both integrals: every factor of $kT$ then comes out in front.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The integrals left are $\int_0^\infty u^{3/2}e^{-u}\,du = \frac34\sqrt\pi$ and $\int_0^\infty u^{5/2}e^{-u}\,du = \frac{15}{8}\sqrt\pi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-single" label="Standard deviation / mean for one atom $=$" value="0.816" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-single" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compute the first two moments of the given density. The temperature drops out of the ratio.

<Step title="The mean">

<Formula label="Mean kinetic energy of one atom" tex="\langle E_1\rangle = \int_0^\infty E_1\,p(E_1)\,dE_1 = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\,kT\int_0^\infty u^{3/2}e^{-u}\,du = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\cdot\frac34\sqrt\pi\;kT = \frac32\,kT" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The mean square and the variance">

<Formula label="Mean square of the kinetic energy of one atom" tex="\langle E_1^2\rangle = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\left(kT\right)^2\int_0^\infty u^{5/2}e^{-u}\,du = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\cdot\frac{15}{8}\sqrt\pi\left(kT\right)^2 = \frac{15}{4}\left(kT\right)^2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

<Formula label="Variance of the kinetic energy of one atom" tex="\text{Var}(E_1) = \langle E_1^2\rangle - \langle E_1\rangle^2 = \left(\frac{15}{4} - \frac94\right)\left(kT\right)^2 = \frac32\left(kT\right)^2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Relative width of the energy of one atom" tex="\frac{\sqrt{\text{Var}(E_1)}}{\langle E_1\rangle} = \frac{\sqrt{3/2}\;kT}{(3/2)\,kT} = \sqrt{\frac23} = 0.816" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ratio is a pure number: $kT$ and the mass of the atom have dropped out. The mean $\frac32kT$ is the familiar mean kinetic energy of an atom in a gas. A ratio close to 1 says that the energy of a single atom is very uncertain: its spread is nearly as large as its mean.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the same ratio for the total energy $E_N$ of the gas in the cubic centimetre. Take the energy to be all kinetic.

<Hint>

The total energy is the sum of the kinetic energies of the $N$ atoms, and in a dilute gas these are statistically independent. How do the mean and the variance of a sum grow with $N$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Find $N$ for one cubic centimetre from the number for one cubic metre.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-gas" label="Standard deviation / mean for the gas $=$" value="1.57e-10" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-gas" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The mean of a sum of $N$ independent terms grows like $N$, its standard deviation only like $\sqrt N$. The ratio shrinks like $1/\sqrt N$.

<Step title="The number of atoms">

One cubic centimetre is $10^{-6}$ cubic metres, so $N = 2.69 \times 10^{25} \times 10^{-6} = 2.69 \times 10^{19}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Relative width of the total energy of the gas" tex="\frac{\sqrt{\text{Var}(E_N)}}{\langle E_N\rangle} = \frac{\sqrt{N\cdot\frac32\left(kT\right)^2}}{N\cdot\frac32\,kT} = \frac{1}{\sqrt N}\sqrt{\frac23} = \frac{0.816}{\sqrt{2.69 \times 10^{19}}} = 1.57 \times 10^{-10}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2" />

MIT's solutions round this to $1.6 \times 10^{-10}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The number of atoms agrees with the ideal gas law: $P/kT$ at $101\,325$ Pa and $273.15$ K is $2.687 \times 10^{25}$ per cubic metre. The lesson of the problem is in the two numbers side by side. The energy of one atom (a microscopic variable) is spread over a range as wide as its mean. The energy of the gas (a macroscopic variable) is sharp to about one part in ten thousand million, even for a single cubic centimetre. That is why thermodynamics can treat the energy of a gas as a definite number.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Temperature" points={12} mit="Problem 3">

Systems A and B are paramagnetic salts. The state of A is given by the field $H$ and the magnetization $M$, the state of B by $H'$ and $M'$. System C is a gas with the pressure $P$ and the volume $V$. When A and C are in thermal equilibrium with each other, their variables are found to obey

<Formula label="Equilibrium of salt A with the gas" tex="nRCH - MPV = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

When B and C are in thermal equilibrium with each other, they obey

<Formula label="Equilibrium of salt B with the gas" tex="nR\Theta M' + nRC'H' - M'PV = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

Here $n$, $R$, $C$, $C'$ and $\Theta$ are constants.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Three functions, one of the variables of each system, are equal to one another in thermal equilibrium. Find them. Write them so that the function of the gas is $PV/(nR)$; the boxes ask for the functions of A and of B.

<Hint>

The lesson's argument for the zeroth law: solve each equilibrium condition for the same variable of the gas, then set the two results equal.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Better still, solve each equation for the combination $PV/(nR)$. Then the variables of each system stand alone on one side.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-ha" label="Function of A $=$" expr="C*H/M"
  vars={{ C: [0.5, 2], H: [0.5, 2], M: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "M/(C*H)", hint: "That is one over the function asked for. It is also equal for systems in equilibrium, but the box wants the form that equals PV/(nR)." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-hb" label="Function of B $=$" expr="Theta + Cp*Hp/Mp"
  vars={{ Theta: [0.5, 2], Cp: [0.5, 2], Hp: [0.5, 2], Mp: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ Theta: "\\Theta", Cp: "C'", Hp: "H'", Mp: "M'" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Cp*Hp/Mp", hint: "The term with Θ is missing. Divide every term of the second equation by nRM′." },
    { expr: "Theta*Mp + Cp*Hp", hint: "That equals M′PV/(nR), which still mixes B with the gas. Divide by M′ as well." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** By the zeroth law, A and B are in equilibrium with each other when each is in equilibrium with C. That condition cannot contain the variables of C, so each equilibrium condition must separate into a function of one system equal to a function of the other.

<Step title="Separate the variables">

MIT's solutions solve both equations for $V$ and then cancel the common factor $nR/P$. The same in one step: divide the first equation by $nRM$ and the second by $nRM'$.

<Formula label="The three functions that are equal in equilibrium" tex="\frac{PV}{nR} = \frac{CH}{M} = \Theta + \frac{C'H'}{M'}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(a), p. 3" />

The first equality is the condition for A with C, the second (with $PV/(nR)$ on the left) the condition for B with C. So the three functions are $PV/(nR)$ for the gas, $CH/M$ for salt A and $\Theta + C'H'/M'$ for salt B. MIT calls their common value $h$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

When A and B are each in equilibrium with C, the chain gives $CH/M = \Theta + C'H'/M'$: a condition between A and B alone, without $P$ or $V$, as the zeroth law demands. The choice is not unique: as the lesson says, any function of $h$ that rises steadily with it is also equal in the three systems. That is why the problem fixed the form for the gas.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Set each of the three functions equal to the ideal gas temperature $T$. Write the resulting equations of state of the two salts as $M(H, T)$ and $M'(H', T)$. Do you recognise any of the three equations of state?

The choice box is our wording of MIT's open question.

<Hint>

For the gas you get an equation you know well. For each salt, solve for the magnetization.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-ma" label="$M =$" expr="C*H/T"
  vars={{ C: [0.5, 2], H: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "C*H*T", hint: "With this, the magnetization at a fixed field would grow with temperature. Start from CH/M = T and solve for M." },
    { expr: "T/(C*H)", hint: "That is 1/M: turn the fraction over." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-mb" label="$M' =$" expr="Cp*Hp/(T - Theta)"
  vars={{ Cp: [0.5, 2], Hp: [0.5, 2], T: [3, 5], Theta: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ Theta: "\\Theta", Cp: "C'", Hp: "H'" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Cp*Hp/(T + Theta)", hint: "Check the sign: Θ + C′H′/M′ = T gives C′H′/M′ = T − Θ." },
    { expr: "Cp*Hp/T", hint: "That is the law of salt A again: the constant Θ has been dropped." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-name" label="The equation of state of salt A is" choices={["the law of a ferromagnet: the magnetization stays finite when the field goes to zero", "a law in which the magnetization at a fixed field does not depend on temperature", "the Curie law of a simple paramagnet: at a fixed field the magnetization falls as 1/T", "a law in which the magnetization becomes infinite at a temperature above absolute zero"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The common value of the three functions is an empirical temperature. Choosing it equal to $T$ turns each function into an equation of state.

<Step title="The three equations of state">

<Formula label="Equations of state of the gas and the two salts" tex="PV = nRT, \qquad M = \frac{CH}{T}, \qquad M' = \frac{C'H'}{T - \Theta}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(b), p. 3" />

The first is the ideal gas law. The second is the Curie law of a simple paramagnet: at a fixed field the magnetization is proportional to $1/T$. The third describes, in the words of MIT's solutions, a paramagnet with an ordering phase transition to a ferromagnet at $T = \Theta$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The third equation makes sense only for $T > \Theta$. As $T$ falls towards $\Theta$, the magnetization produced by a given field grows without limit: the salt is about to order by itself. It has the form of the lesson's own third example of an equation of state, the one for a simple paramagnet. Setting $\Theta = 0$ gives back the Curie law of salt A.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Work in a simple solid" points={18} mit="Problem 4">

In the simplest model of an elastic solid, small changes of the pressure and the temperature change the volume by

<Formula label="Volume change of a simple solid" tex="dV = -V\kappa_T\,dP + V\alpha\,dT" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2" />

where $\kappa_T$ is the isothermal compressibility and $\alpha$ the thermal expansion coefficient, both constants. The solid is taken from the state $(P_1, T_1)$ to the state $(P_2, T_2)$, with $P_2 > P_1$ and $T_2 > T_1$, along one of three paths in the plane of $P$ and $T$:

- **path a**: first heat it from $T_1$ to $T_2$ at the constant pressure $P_1$, then raise the pressure from $P_1$ to $P_2$ at the constant temperature $T_2$;
- **path b**: first raise the pressure from $P_1$ to $P_2$ at the constant temperature $T_1$, then heat it from $T_1$ to $T_2$ at the constant pressure $P_2$;
- **path c**: go along the straight line from $(P_1, T_1)$ to $(P_2, T_2)$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0.5, 2.5]} range={[0.5, 2.5]} xLabel="T / T₁" yLabel="P / P₁"
  curves={[
    { x: "1 + s", y: "1", range: [0, 1], label: "a" },
    { x: "2", y: "1 + s", range: [0, 1] },
    { x: "1", y: "1 + s", range: [0, 1], label: "b" },
    { x: "1 + s", y: "2", range: [0, 1] },
    { x: "1 + s", y: "1 + s", range: [0, 1], label: "c", dash: true }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 1, y: 1, label: "1" }, { x: 2, y: 2, label: "2" }]}
  caption="The three paths, redrawn from the sketch on MIT's sheet, here for T₂ = 2T₁ and P₂ = 2P₁. Path a runs along the bottom and up the right side, path b up the left side and along the top, path c (dashed) straight across." />

Find the work done **on** the solid along each path. The fractional change of the volume is small, so wherever $V$ appears as a factor in the expression for $dV$ you may replace it by the constant $V_1 = V(P_1, T_1)$.

In the boxes use $\kappa_T$, $\alpha$, $V_1$, $P_1$, $P_2$, $T_1$ and $T_2$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the work done on the solid along path a.

<Hint>

The work done on the solid in a small step is $\delta W = -P\,dV$. Put the model's $dV$ into it, with $V_1$ for $V$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On each leg of the path one of $dP$ and $dT$ is zero. On the leg where the temperature changes, what is the pressure?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="Path a: $W =$" expr="kappa*V1*(P2^2 - P1^2)/2 - alpha*V1*P1*(T2 - T1)"
  vars={{ kappa: [0.5, 1.5], alpha: [0.5, 1.5], V1: [0.5, 1.5], P1: [1, 2], P2: [3, 4], T1: [1, 2], T2: [3, 4] }}
  names={{ kappa: "\\kappa_T", alpha: "\\alpha", V1: "V_1", P1: "P_1", P2: "P_2", T1: "T_1", T2: "T_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "kappa*V1*(P2^2 - P1^2)/2 - alpha*V1*P2*(T2 - T1)", hint: "That has the solid heated at the pressure P₂, which is path b. On path a the heating comes first, while the pressure is still P₁." },
    { expr: "-kappa*V1*(P2^2 - P1^2)/2 + alpha*V1*P1*(T2 - T1)", hint: "That is the work done by the solid. The work done on it is −P dV: squeezing (dP > 0 at fixed T) must count as positive." },
    { expr: "kappa*V1*(P2^2 - P1^2) - alpha*V1*P1*(T2 - T1)", hint: "The integral of P dP is half of P², so the compression term needs a factor 1/2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write the work in terms of $dP$ and $dT$, then add up the two legs. On each leg only one of the two terms is alive.

<Step title="The work in a small step">

<Formula label="Work done on the simple solid in a small step" tex="\delta W = -P\,dV = \kappa_TPV_1\,dP - \alpha PV_1\,dT" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4, p. 3" />

</Step>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Along a line the two differentials are not independent. Express one by the other and integrate once.

<Step title="The relation along the line">

<Formula label="Temperature and pressure steps along the straight path" tex="dT = \frac{T_2 - T_1}{P_2 - P_1}\,dP" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4, p. 4" />

<Formula label="Work in a small step along the straight path" tex="\delta W = \left[\kappa_TV_1 - \alpha V_1\,\frac{T_2 - T_1}{P_2 - P_1}\right]P\,dP" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4, p. 4" />

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

With $\int_{P_1}^{P_2}P\,dP = \frac12\left(P_2^2 - P_1^2\right) = \frac12\left(P_1 + P_2\right)\left(P_2 - P_1\right)$:

<Formula label="Work on the solid along path c" tex="W = \frac12\,\kappa_TV_1\left(P_2^2 - P_1^2\right) - \frac12\,\alpha V_1\left(P_1 + P_2\right)\left(T_2 - T_1\right)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4, p. 4" />

</Step>

<Step title="Which path takes the least work">

The three results differ only in the thermal expansion term, which holds $P_1$ on path a, $P_2$ on path b and their average on path c. With $\alpha > 0$, $T_2 > T_1$ and $P_2 > P_1$ that term is most negative on path b. So path b takes the least work and path a the most (MIT's solutions p. 4), with path c in between. The work is not a state function: the same two end states, three different amounts of work.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The work along path c is exactly the average of the work along paths a and b, as it should be for a quantity that is linear in the pressure during heating. If $\alpha$ were zero, all three paths would give the same work, $\frac12\kappa_TV_1\left(P_2^2 - P_1^2\right)$: then $\delta W$ would depend on $P$ alone and would be an exact differential.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Integrate the exact differential the way the lesson describes: integrate one coefficient, leave an unknown function of the other variable, and fix that function with the second coefficient.

<Step title="Integrate over the temperature">

With $f = aH$:

<Formula label="Magnetization up to a function of the field" tex="M(H, T) = \int\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial T}\right)_HdT + g(H) = \frac{aH}{1 - T/T_c} + M_0\left(1 - T/T_c\right)^{1/2} + g(H)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(b), p. 6" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compare with the susceptibility">

<Formula label="Condition on the function of the field" tex="\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial H}\right)_T = \frac{a}{1 - T/T_c} + \frac{dg}{dH} = \frac{a}{1 - T/T_c} + 3bH^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g(H) = bH^3 + K" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(b), p. 6" />

At $H = 0$ the result so far is $M = M_0\left(1 - T/T_c\right)^{1/2} + K$. The spontaneous magnetization must be zero at $T = T_c$, so $K = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The equation of state">

<Formula label="Equation of state of the model ferromagnet" tex="M(H, T) = M_0\left(1 - \frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{1/2} + \frac{aH}{1 - T/T_c} + bH^3" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(b), p. 6" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiating gives back both model expressions. At $H = 0$ the magnetization is $M_0\left(1 - T/T_c\right)^{1/2}$: it goes to zero at $T_c$ with an infinitely steep slope, like the plunge at the end of the curve on MIT's sheet. In a small field the response $aH/\left(1 - T/T_c\right)$ grows without limit as $T$ approaches $T_c$: near its critical point the magnet is extremely easy to magnetize. The same blow-up appeared from the other side in salt B of Problem 3.
