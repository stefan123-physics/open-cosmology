## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it; a large number can be typed as `1.2e5`. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `3*N*k*T/2`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent, so each part says which letters to use. Type `exp(...)` for an exponential, `sqrt(...)` for a square root and `pi` for $\pi$. Write the symbols as the box names them: `hbar` for $\hbar$, `omega` for $\omega$, `x` for the ratio $\hbar\omega/kT$ in Problem 2, `v` for the sound velocity $\langle v\rangle$ in Problem 2(d) and for the voltage in Problem 3, `i` for the current, and `vN2` for the mean square noise voltage $\langle v_N^2(\omega)\rangle$ in Problem 5.

The points per problem and per part are ours. Parts that ask for an argument have a choice box or none: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons: $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, $\hbar$ is Planck's constant divided by $2\pi$, and $\omega$ is a radian frequency. Some letters change their meaning from one problem to the next, as they do on MIT's sheet. The letter $V$ is a volume. $C_V$ is a heat capacity, but $C$ alone is the capacitance of a capacitor. $L$ is an inductance in Problem 3 and the length of a transmission line in Problem 4. $N$ is the number of unit cells of a crystal in Problem 2. $D(\omega)$ is a density of modes: $D(\omega)\,d\omega$ is the number of modes with frequencies between $\omega$ and $\omega + d\omega$. For numbers use $k = 1.381 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The Big Bang" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

When the universe was young it was much smaller and very hot, and matter and radiation were in thermal equilibrium with each other. When the temperature had fallen to about 3000 K, the two decoupled: from then on the radiation moved freely and no longer exchanged energy with matter. This radiation still fills the universe as black-body radiation, the cosmic background radiation. Its temperature today has been measured: MIT's sheet rounds it to 3 K.

Assume that the radiation has expanded adiabatically ever since. The lesson on black-body radiation derived the Helmholtz free energy of thermal radiation in a volume $V$:

<Formula label="Free energy of thermal radiation" tex="F(T, V) = -\frac{1}{45}\,\frac{\pi^2}{c^3\hbar^3}\,(kT)^4\,V" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

(MIT's sheet adds a note: this background radiation is not the same thing as the radiation near a star, a nebula or a planet, which can well be in equilibrium with the matter around it.)

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the entropy $S(T, V)$ of the radiation, with the letters $k$, $T$, $V$, $\hbar$ and $c$. Which combination of $T$ and $V$ stays constant in an adiabatic expansion?

<Hint>

The free energy has $dF = -S\,dT - P\,dV$. Which derivative of $F$ gives the entropy?

</Hint>

<Hint>

A slow adiabatic expansion keeps the entropy constant. Look at how $T$ and $V$ appear in your entropy.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-s" label="$S(T, V) =$" expr="4*pi^2*k^4*T^3*V/(45*hbar^3*c^3)"
  vars={{ k: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2], V: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 2], c: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi^2*k^4*T^3*V/(45*hbar^3*c^3)", hint: "That is −F/T. The entropy is the derivative of −F with respect to T, and the derivative of T⁴ brings down a factor 4." },
    { expr: "-4*pi^2*k^4*T^3*V/(45*hbar^3*c^3)", hint: "Check the sign: S = −(∂F/∂T) at constant V, and F itself is negative, so the entropy comes out positive." },
    { expr: "pi^2*k^4*T^4/(45*hbar^3*c^3)", hint: "That is −(∂F/∂V) at constant T, the pressure of the radiation. The entropy is the derivative with respect to T." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The entropy is minus the temperature derivative of the free energy. In a reversible adiabatic change the entropy does not change.

<Step title="The entropy">

<Formula label="Entropy of thermal radiation" tex="S = -\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_V = \frac{4\pi^2}{45}\,\frac{k^4T^3}{c^3\hbar^3}\,V" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The adiabat">

The entropy contains $T$ and $V$ only in the product $T^3V$. So an adiabatic expansion keeps $T^3V$ constant (MIT's solutions p. 1): when the volume grows, the temperature falls as $V^{-1/3}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson on black-body radiation has the same entropy and the same adiabat. The unit is right: $kT/\hbar c$ is one over a length, so $(kT/\hbar c)^3V$ is a pure number and $S$ is a number times $k$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

By what factor has the volume of the universe grown since the radiation decoupled from matter at 3000 K, if the radiation is at 3 K now? (MIT's sheet asks "by what fraction": the answer is the ratio of the volume now to the volume then.)

The second and third box are our additions. In 2013 the measured temperature used in 8.286 was $T_0 = 2.725$ K. With this value and decoupling at 3000 K, find the factor by which the volume has grown, and the factor by which every length has grown.

<Hint>

Write the constant of part (a) at the two times, then and now, and solve for the ratio of the volumes.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A volume is the cube of a length. So the factor for lengths is the cube root of the factor for volumes.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-volume" label="3000 K to 3 K: $V_{\text{now}}/V_{\text{then}} =$" value="1e9" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-volume" />

<Answer id="p1-volume-cmb" label="3000 K to 2.725 K: $V_{\text{now}}/V_{\text{then}} =$" value="1.334e9" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-volume-cmb" />

<Answer id="p1-length" label="3000 K to 2.725 K: factor for lengths $=$" value="1101" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-length" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $T^3V$ is the same then and now.

<Step title="MIT's numbers">

<Formula label="Growth of the volume since decoupling" tex="\frac{V(3\ \text{K})}{V(3000\ \text{K})} = \frac{3000^3}{3^3} = 10^9" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

The universe is a billion times larger in volume than it was when the radiation was set free.

</Step>

<Step title="The measured temperature (our addition)">

With $T_0 = 2.725$ K the ratio of the temperatures is $3000/2.725 = 1101$. Lengths have grown by this factor, 1101, and volumes by its cube, $1.334 \times 10^9$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson of 8.286 on [the temperature of the early universe and decoupling](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling) finds the same law in another way: the entropy density thins out as one over the cube of the scale factor $a$, so $T \propto 1/a$. A growth of all lengths by a factor of about 1100 is the redshift of the background radiation: each wavelength has been stretched by that factor, which moved the radiation from visible and near-infrared light to microwaves. Its [measured spectrum](/courses/8-286/cmb-spectrum) is still that of a black body, as it must be if every wavelength is stretched by the same factor.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Lattice heat capacity of solids" points={30} mit="Problem 2">

This problem is about the part of the heat capacity of a solid that comes from the vibrations of its atoms, the lattice. (A solid may have other parts too, for example from the mobile electrons of a metal or from magnetic moments.)

A crystal is built of $N$ primitive unit cells with $J$ atoms each. A primitive unit cell is the smallest piece of the solid that rebuilds the whole crystal when it is shifted, without turning, again and again. The atoms in a cell may be equal or different: diamond has two carbon atoms in its cell, sodium chloride one sodium and one chlorine atom. Aluminium has $J = 1$; the crystal of a large biological molecule can have a $J$ of tens of thousands.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

**The classical model.** Assume that every atom is statistically independent of all the others, and that it vibrates about its rest position as a harmonic oscillator in each of three perpendicular directions. (Such a model could have up to $3J$ different frequencies. Symmetry usually makes some of them equal, which changes the number of frequencies but not the number of modes.) Use classical mechanics to find the heat capacity at constant volume, $C_V$, with the letters $J$, $N$ and $k$.

<Hint>

The lesson on examples using the canonical ensemble found the heat capacity of one classical harmonic oscillator. Does it depend on the frequency?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Count the oscillators: directions, atoms in a cell, cells.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-classical" label="$C_V =$" expr="3*J*N*k"
  vars={{ J: [1, 5], N: [1, 5], k: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*N*k", hint: "That counts one atom in each unit cell. Each of the N cells has J atoms." },
    { expr: "3*J*N*k/2", hint: "That gives each oscillator kT/2. An oscillator has two quadratic terms in its energy, kinetic and potential, with kT/2 each." },
    { expr: "J*N*k", hint: "That counts one direction of vibration for each atom. Each atom vibrates in three directions." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each classical harmonic oscillator has the mean energy $kT$, whatever its frequency, so its heat capacity is $k$. Then count.

<Step title="Count the oscillators">

There are 3 directions for each of the $J$ atoms in each of the $N$ cells: $3JN$ oscillators.

<Formula label="Classical heat capacity of a lattice" tex="C_V = 3JNk" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A remark of ours: for one mole of atoms ($JN$ equal to Avogadro's number) this is three times the gas constant, about 25 J/K, the same for every solid. That is the law of Dulong and Petit, and it holds for many solids at room temperature.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

**The Einstein model.** The classical result does not agree with measurements: the heat capacity of a lattice changes with the temperature and goes to zero at $T = 0$. Keep the independent atoms and their harmonic motion, but now use quantum mechanics. To keep it simple, give all $3JN$ oscillators the same frequency $\omega$.

Find $C_V$, with the letters $J$, $N$, $k$ and the ratio $x = \hbar\omega/kT$. Then find the form that $C_V$ takes for $kT \ll \hbar\omega$ (with the same letters), and state what it becomes for $kT \gg \hbar\omega$.

The third box is our addition: the value of $C_V/3JNk$ at the temperature where $kT = \hbar\omega$.

<Hint>

The lesson on polyatomic gases has the mean energy of a quantum oscillator: $\hbar\omega\left(\langle n\rangle + \frac12\right)$ with $\langle n\rangle = 1/\left(e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1\right)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiate the mean energy with respect to $T$. The temperature sits in the exponent, so the chain rule gives a factor $\hbar\omega/kT^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the limits: when $x$ is large, $e^x - 1$ is nearly $e^x$. When $x$ is small, $e^x - 1$ is nearly $x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-einstein" label="$C_V =$" expr="3*J*N*k*x^2*exp(x)/(exp(x) - 1)^2"

<Solution>

**The idea.** Replace the classical mean energy $kT$ of each oscillator with the quantum one, and differentiate.

<Step title="The mean energy of one oscillator">

<Formula label="Mean energy of a quantum oscillator" tex="\langle\epsilon\rangle = \hbar\omega\left(\langle n\rangle + \frac12\right), \qquad \langle n\rangle = \frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The heat capacity">

All $3JN$ oscillators are alike, so $C_V = 3JN\,\partial\langle\epsilon\rangle/\partial T$. Only $\langle n\rangle$ depends on $T$:

<Formula label="Heat capacity of the Einstein model" tex="C_V = 3JN\,\hbar\omega\,\frac{\partial\langle n\rangle}{\partial T} = 3JNk\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{kT}\right)^2\frac{e^{\hbar\omega/kT}}{\left(e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1\right)^2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), pp. 1 and 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two limits">

<Formula label="Limits of the Einstein heat capacity" tex="kT \ll \hbar\omega:\quad C_V \approx 3JNk\left(\frac{\hbar\omega}{kT}\right)^2e^{-\hbar\omega/kT}; \qquad kT \gg \hbar\omega:\quad C_V \to 3JNk" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2" />

The first is the energy-gap behaviour of the lesson on polyatomic gases: an oscillator needs the energy $\hbar\omega$ to leave its ground state, and at low temperature few get it. The second is the classical result of part (a).

</Step>

<Step title="One number (our addition) and a check">

At $kT = \hbar\omega$, $x = 1$ and $C_V/3JNk = e/(e - 1)^2 = 0.921$: the crystal is already within 8% of its classical heat capacity. The zero-point energy $\frac12\hbar\omega$ of each oscillator does not depend on $T$, so it does not appear in $C_V$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

**Phonons, high temperature** (MIT's part (c)(i)). The Einstein model is closer to the measurements, but still not right. The measured lattice heat capacity goes to zero as $T^3$, more slowly than the exponential of the Einstein model, and using $3J$ different frequencies does not repair that. The flaw is that the atoms do not move independently: pluck one atom and its energy soon spreads through the whole crystal. What the crystal really has is $3JN$ normal modes of vibration, called phonons, and each of them involves all the atoms. The amplitude of a normal mode behaves as a harmonic oscillator: a phonon of radian frequency $\omega$ is a quantum harmonic oscillator of that frequency. The frequencies of the modes cover a wide range, from almost zero up to about the frequency of one atom that vibrates between fixed neighbours.

The density of frequencies $D(\omega)$ counts the modes: $D(\omega)\,d\omega$ is the number of modes with frequencies between $\omega$ and $\omega + d\omega$. Its normalization and the internal energy of the lattice are

<Formula label="Normalization of the density of modes and energy of the lattice" tex="\int_0^\infty D(\omega)\,d\omega = 3JN, \qquad E(T) = \int_0^\infty\langle\epsilon(\omega, T)\rangle\,D(\omega)\,d\omega" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2" />

where $\langle\epsilon(\omega, T)\rangle$ is the mean energy of a quantum oscillator of radian frequency $\omega$ at the temperature $T$.

Write $E(T)$ out as an integral in full. Then evaluate it for $kT \gg \hbar\omega_{\max}$, where $\omega_{\max}$ is the highest frequency of a phonon in the solid, with the letters $J$, $N$, $k$ and $T$. What is the heat capacity in this limit?

<Hint>

Put the mean energy of part (b) into the integral.

</Hint>

<Hint>

When $kT$ is far above $\hbar\omega$ for every mode in the crystal, what does the mean energy of each oscillator become? Does it still depend on $\omega$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-high" label="For $kT \gg \hbar\omega_{\max}$: $E(T) \approx$" expr="3*J*N*k*T"
  vars={{ J: [1, 5], N: [1, 5], k: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*J*N*k", hint: "That is the heat capacity in this limit. The box asks for the energy." },
    { expr: "3*J*N*k*T/2", hint: "Each mode is an oscillator with a kinetic and a potential term, kT/2 each, so its mean energy at high temperature is kT." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At high temperature every mode has the classical mean energy $kT$, which can be taken out of the integral. What is left is the number of modes.

<Step title="The full integral">

<Formula label="Energy of the phonons" tex="E(T) = \int_0^\infty D(\omega)\,\hbar\omega\left(\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1} + \frac12\right)d\omega" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(c)(i), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="High temperature">

For $kT \gg \hbar\omega$ the mean energy of an oscillator tends to $kT$, independent of $\omega$. If this holds up to $\omega_{\max}$, it holds wherever $D(\omega)$ is not zero:

<Formula label="Energy and heat capacity of the phonons at high temperature" tex="E(T) \approx kT\int_0^\infty D(\omega)\,d\omega = 3JNkT, \qquad C_V = 3JNk" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(c)(i), p. 2" />

the classical result of part (a).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A remark of ours on how good the limit is. With $x = \hbar\omega/kT$, the mean energy is $kT\left(x/(e^x - 1) + x/2\right) = kT\left(1 + x^2/12 - \dots\right)$: the zero-point energy cancels the term $-x/2$ of the first fraction, so the correction is of second order in $x$. At $x = 0.5$ the mean energy is 1.021 times $kT$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={10}>

**Phonons, low temperature** (MIT's part (c)(ii)). It can be shown that near $\omega = 0$ the density of modes is quadratic:

<Formula label="Density of phonon modes at low frequency" tex="D(\omega) \to \frac{3V}{2\pi^2\langle v\rangle^3}\,\omega^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c)(ii), p. 3" />

where $V$ is the volume of the crystal and $\langle v\rangle$ is an average velocity of sound in the solid. Find the heat capacity of the lattice at temperatures so low that only phonons in this quadratic region are excited, with the letters $V$, $k$, $T$, $\hbar$ and $v$ (for $\langle v\rangle$). Use

<Formula label="An integral for the phonon energy" tex="\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3}{e^x - 1}\,dx = \frac{\pi^4}{15}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c)(ii), p. 3" />

The Einstein model gave an exponential at low temperature. Why does the phonon model give a power of $T$? (Our addition: MIT's sheet does not ask this; its solutions answer it in a note on p. 3.)

<Hint>

Split the integral of part (c) into the zero-point part, which does not depend on $T$, and the thermal part. Only the thermal part matters for $C_V$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the thermal part, the factor $1/\left(e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1\right)$ cuts the integrand off above $\hbar\omega \approx kT$. So at low $T$ you may use the quadratic $D(\omega)$ for all $\omega$ and keep the upper limit at infinity.

</Hint>

<Hint>

  vars={{ V: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 2], v: [0.5, 2] }}

    { expr: "4*pi^2*V*k*(k*T/(hbar*v))^3/15", hint: "That is the result for two polarizations, as for light with v in place of c. The 3 in D(ω) stands for three: sound has two transverse waves and one longitudinal wave." }

<Answer id="p2-gap" label="A power of $T$, not an exponential, because" choices={["the zero-point energy of the modes, which the Einstein model leaves out, adds a term that grows as a power of T", "there are modes with frequencies all the way down to zero, so at any temperature some have ħω below kT", "the atoms of a real crystal are not harmonic oscillators at low temperature", "phonons are classical waves, so equipartition holds for them at every temperature"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The thermal energy is an integral over the modes that are excited, those with $\hbar\omega$ up to a few $kT$. At low temperature all of them lie in the quadratic region, and the integral becomes a pure number times a power of $T$.

<Step title="Split off the zero-point energy">

<Formula label="Zero-point and thermal energy of the phonons" tex="E(T) = E_0 + \int_0^\infty\frac{D(\omega)\,\hbar\omega}{e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1}\,d\omega, \qquad E_0 = \int_0^\infty D(\omega)\,\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\,d\omega" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(c)(ii), p. 2" />

The zero-point energy $E_0$ does not depend on $T$.

</Step>

<Step title="The thermal part">

Put in the quadratic $D(\omega)$ and change to $x = \hbar\omega/kT$, so that $\omega^3\,d\omega = (kT/\hbar)^4x^3\,dx$:

<Formula label="Thermal energy of the phonons at low temperature" tex="E(T) = E_0 + \frac{3V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{kT}{\hbar\langle v\rangle}\right)^3kT\int_0^\infty\frac{x^3}{e^x - 1}\,dx = E_0 + \frac{\pi^2}{10}\,V\left(\frac{kT}{\hbar\langle v\rangle}\right)^3kT" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(c)(ii), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The heat capacity">

The thermal energy grows as $T^4$, so

<Formula label="Lattice heat capacity at low temperature" tex="C_V = \frac{2\pi^2}{5}\,Vk\left(\frac{kT}{\hbar\langle v\rangle}\right)^3 \propto T^3" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(c)(ii), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Why no exponential">

Each single mode still has an energy gap $\hbar\omega$. But the gaps are spread over all values down to zero, so at any temperature some modes have $\hbar\omega$ below $kT$ and are fully excited. The spread of gaps washes out the exponential (MIT's solutions p. 3). As $T$ falls, the number of excited modes falls as $T^3$, and that is the $T^3$ of the heat capacity.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A check of ours with the lesson on black-body radiation. Light in a cavity has the energy $\frac{\pi^2}{15}V(kT)^4/(\hbar c)^3$, so its heat capacity is $\frac{4\pi^2}{15}Vk(kT/\hbar c)^3$. Sound has three polarizations where light has two. Multiplying by $\frac32$ and replacing $c$ with $\langle v\rangle$ gives $\frac{2\pi^2}{5}Vk(kT/\hbar\langle v\rangle)^3$, the result above. Phonons at low temperature are black-body radiation of sound. The unit is right too: $kT/\hbar\langle v\rangle$ is one over a length, so $C_V$ is a pure number times $k$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 4

It is made of parts with many different frequencies. The noise power in a unit interval of frequency around the radian frequency $\omega$ is written $S_v(\omega)$ and called the power spectrum of the voltage. The method of Problem 3 gives mean squares in components without loss at once, without looking at the rest of the circuit. But it cannot give the spectrum. (MIT's sheet calls this "Problem 2": some of the numbers by which its three circuit problems refer to each other are one too low.)

<Answer id="p5-compare" label="Compared with Problem 3(a), the mean square voltage is" choices={["the same: the resistor sets how the noise is spread over frequency, but not its total", "larger: the resistor adds its Johnson noise to the thermal noise of the capacitor", "the same only for one value of R: a larger resistor gives a larger noise voltage on the capacitor"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The noise source of the resistor is the input of the filter. The filter formula gives the spectrum on the capacitor, and the integral over the spectrum gives the mean square voltage.

<Step title="The spectrum">

Take the $R$ of the filter to be the ideal resistor of the model and $v_o(\omega)$ to be its noise source $v_N(\omega)$:

<Formula label="Noise spectrum of the voltage on the capacitor" tex="S_{v_c}(\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + (\omega RC)^2}\,\langle v_N^2(\omega)\rangle = \frac{2kT}{\pi}\,\frac{R}{1 + (\omega RC)^2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(c), p. 6" />

</Step>

<Step title="The sketch">

The spectrum is flat at low frequency, with the value $2RkT/\pi$ of the source. It falls to half of that at $\omega = 1/RC$ and decreases as $1/\omega^2$ far above it.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 6]} range={[0, 1.1]} xLabel="ωRC" yLabel="S / (2RkT/π)"
  samples={200}
  curves={[
    { expr: "1/(1 + x^2)", label: "spectrum" }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 1, y: 0.5, label: "half, at ω = 1/RC" }]}
  caption="The noise spectrum of the voltage on the capacitor, computed from the formula, in units of its value at zero frequency. The frequency is in units of 1/RC. A larger resistor raises the flat part and narrows the spectrum by the same factor, so the area under the curve stays the same." />

</Step>

<Step title="The mean square voltage">

With $x = \omega RC$, $d\omega = dx/RC$:

<Formula label="Mean square noise voltage on the capacitor from the spectrum" tex="\langle v_c^2\rangle = \frac{2kT}{\pi}\int_0^\infty\frac{R\,d\omega}{1 + (\omega RC)^2} = \frac{2}{\pi}\,\frac{kT}{C}\int_0^\infty\frac{dx}{1 + x^2} = \frac{2}{\pi}\,\frac{kT}{C}\,\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{kT}{C}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(c), p. 6" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is the result of Problem 3(a), found there from the canonical ensemble for the capacitor alone, without any resistor. The resistance has dropped out: the height of the spectrum grows with $R$ and its width $1/RC$ shrinks with $R$. MIT's solutions draw the conclusion (p. 6): the thermal noise in a circuit can be thought of, and calculated, as coming from the elements that dissipate.

</Step>
