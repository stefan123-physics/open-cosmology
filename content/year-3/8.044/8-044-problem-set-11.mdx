## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `A*kB*T/b`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent. Type `exp(...)` for an exponential, `sqrt(...)` for a square root, `log(...)` for the natural logarithm and `pi` for $\pi$. Write the symbols as the box names them: `kB` for $k_B$, `E` for the energy $\epsilon$ and `EF` for $\epsilon_F$, `g` for $\gamma$, `D` for $\Delta$, `ND` for $N_D$, `alpha` for $\alpha$, `mu0` for $\mu_0$, `hbar` for $\hbar$ and `v0` for $v_0$.

The points per problem and per part are ours. Parts that ask for an argument have a choice box or none: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of MIT's sheet. Boltzmann's constant is written $k_B$ on this page, because $k$ is the length of a wavevector $\vec k$. The energy of one state of one particle (or of one quantum of a wave) is $\epsilon$, and $D(\epsilon)$ is the density of states: $D(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon$ is the number of states with an energy between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon + d\epsilon$. The mean number of particles in one state is $\langle n\rangle$. The energy of the whole system is $U$. The letter $n$ is also a number density in Problems 4 and 5, as on MIT's sheet.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Ripplons" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

The vibrations of a solid or a liquid are a set of normal modes, each with the Hamiltonian of a harmonic oscillator: the phonons of the lesson. The waves on the surface between a liquid and its vapour can be described in the same way. Their normal modes are called **ripplons**. They live in two dimensions, and each has a single polarization, at right angles to the surface. Their dispersion relation is the same in every direction (MIT's sheet has a sketch of it, a curve that rises ever more steeply):

<Formula label="Dispersion relation of ripplons" tex="\epsilon(\vec k) = b\,k^{3/2}, \qquad k = \sqrt{k_x^2 + k_y^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

with a constant $b$. For a rectangular surface with the sides $L_x$ and $L_y$ and periodic boundary conditions, the allowed wavevectors are $k_x = 2\pi m/L_x$ and $k_y = 2\pi n/L_y$, where $m$ and $n$ run over all the integers, positive and negative. The area of the surface is $A = L_xL_y$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the density of allowed wavevectors $D(\vec k)$: the number of allowed wavevectors in the small area $dk_x\,dk_y$ around the point $\vec k$ is $D(\vec k)\,dk_x\,dk_y$.

<Hint>

The allowed wavevectors form a grid in the plane of $k_x$ and $k_y$. How much area of that plane belongs to one grid point?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-dk" label="$D(\vec k) =$" expr="A/(2*pi)^2"
  vars={{ A: [1, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(2*pi)^2/A", hint: "That is the area of the k-plane that belongs to one allowed wavevector. The density is one over it." },
    { expr: "A/pi^2", hint: "That is the density for waves that must vanish at the edges, where the spacing is π/L and only positive values count. With periodic boundary conditions the spacing is 2π/L in each direction." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A uniform grid of points has a density of one over the area per point.

<Step title="The grid">

Neighbouring allowed values of $k_x$ are $2\pi/L_x$ apart, and those of $k_y$ are $2\pi/L_y$ apart. Each allowed wavevector owns a rectangle of the area $(2\pi)^2/L_xL_y$, so

<Formula label="Density of allowed wavevectors on a surface" tex="D(\vec k) = \frac{L_xL_y}{(2\pi)^2} = \frac{A}{(2\pi)^2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The density is the same at every $\vec k$ and grows with the area of the sample, as the density in the lesson grows with the volume. It has the unit of an area, which fits: $dk_x\,dk_y$ is one over an area.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the density of states as a function of energy, $D(\epsilon)$, of the ripplons, in terms of $b$ and $A$. Sketch it.

<Hint>

Count first: how many allowed wavevectors have an energy below $\epsilon$? They fill a circle in the plane of $k_x$ and $k_y$. What is its radius?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The density of states is the derivative of that number with respect to $\epsilon$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-de" label="$D(\epsilon) =$" expr="A*E^(1/3)/(3*pi*b^(4/3))"
  vars={{ A: [1, 10], E: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ E: "\\epsilon" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "A*E^(4/3)/(4*pi*b^(4/3))", hint: "That is the number of states with an energy below ε. The density of states is its derivative with respect to ε." },
    { expr: "2*A*E^(1/3)/(3*pi*b^(4/3))", hint: "That counts two states for each wavevector. A ripplon has one polarization only, so one state." },
    { expr: "A*E^(1/3)/(3*pi*b)", hint: "Check the power of b: the radius of the circle is (ε/b) to the power 2/3, and the area of the circle has its square." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The states below the energy $\epsilon$ are the allowed wavevectors inside the circle on which the energy is $\epsilon$.

<Step title="The number of states below an energy">

From $\epsilon = bk^{3/2}$ the radius of that circle is $k(\epsilon) = (\epsilon/b)^{2/3}$. With one state for each wavevector, the number of states below $\epsilon$, written $G(\epsilon)$ here (MIT writes it with the sign #), is the area of the circle times the density of part (a):

<Formula label="Number of ripplon states below an energy" tex="G(\epsilon) = \pi k^2(\epsilon)\,D(\vec k) = \pi\left(\frac{\epsilon}{b}\right)^{4/3}\frac{A}{(2\pi)^2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The density of states">

<Formula label="Density of states of ripplons" tex="D(\epsilon) = \frac{dG}{d\epsilon} = \frac43\,\frac{A}{(2\pi)^2}\,\pi\,\frac{\epsilon^{1/3}}{b^{4/3}} = \frac{A}{3\pi b^{4/3}}\,\epsilon^{1/3}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

(MIT's solutions print a stray letter $m$ in the middle expression. The result is as here.)

</Step>

<Step title="The sketch">

<Plot variable="u" domain={[0, 3]} range={[0, 1.6]} xLabel="ε (any unit)" yLabel="D(ε)"
  samples={300}
  curves={[
    { expr: "u^(1/3)" }
  ]}
  caption="The density of states of ripplons, computed from the cube root of the energy (the unit of the vertical axis is the value at ε = 1). The curve starts at zero with a vertical tangent and keeps rising, ever more slowly." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Integrated from zero to $\epsilon$, the density of states gives back the number of wavevectors in the circle, $Ak^2/4\pi$. The unit is right as well: $b^{4/3}$ is an energy to the power $4/3$ times an area, so $D(\epsilon)$ is one over an energy.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Find the contribution of the ripplons to the heat capacity at constant area, $C_A(T)$. Leave a dimensionless integral in your result, and write it with

<Formula label="Dimensionless integral in the heat capacity of ripplons" tex="I = \int_0^\infty\frac{x^{7/3}e^x}{\left(e^x - 1\right)^2}\,dx" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

How does $C_A$ depend on $T$? Sketch it.

Two boxes are our additions: the power of $T$ in $C_A$, as a decimal number, and the value of $I$, found by any numerical method you like.

<Hint>

Each mode is a harmonic oscillator with the mean energy $\epsilon\left(\langle n\rangle + \frac12\right)$, where $\langle n\rangle = 1/\left(e^{\epsilon/k_BT} - 1\right)$. Add up the modes with the density of states of part (b).

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiate with respect to $T$ under the integral. Only $\langle n\rangle$ depends on $T$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Substitute $x = \epsilon/k_BT$ and collect the powers of $k_BT$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$C_A =$" expr="A*kB*(kB*T)^(4/3)*I/(3*pi*b^(4/3))"
  vars={{ A: [1, 10], kB: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2], I: [1, 5] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "7*A*kB*(kB*T)^(4/3)*I/(9*pi*b^(4/3))", hint: "The factor 7/9 belongs to the other form of the result, with the integral of x^(4/3)/(e^x − 1). With the I of this box the factor is 1/3." },
    { expr: "A*(kB*T)^(7/3)*I/(3*pi*b^(4/3))", hint: "That has the unit of an energy, and the power of T of the thermal energy U. The heat capacity is the derivative of U with respect to T." },
    { expr: "A*kB*(kB*T)^(1/3)*I/(3*pi*b^(4/3))", hint: "Count the powers of kT again: the integrand has ε² from the two factors ε/kT, ε^(1/3) from the density of states and dε, which makes (kT) to the power 10/3, divided by kT²." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-power" label="Power of $T$ in $C_A$ $=$" value="1.333" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-power" />

<Answer id="p1-int" label="$I =$" value="3.93" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-int" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The ripplons are independent harmonic oscillators. Their energy is the mean energy of one oscillator, summed over the modes with the density of states.

<Step title="The energy">

<Formula label="Energy of the ripplons" tex="U = \int_0^\infty\epsilon\left(\langle n\rangle + \frac12\right)D(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon = \int_0^\infty\epsilon\left(\frac{1}{e^{\epsilon/k_BT} - 1} + \frac12\right)D(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

The term with $\frac12$ is the zero-point energy. It does not depend on the temperature, so it adds nothing to the heat capacity.

</Step>

<Step title="The heat capacity">

The derivative of $\langle n\rangle$ with respect to $T$ is $\left(\epsilon/k_BT^2\right)e^{\epsilon/k_BT}/\left(e^{\epsilon/k_BT} - 1\right)^2$. With $D(\epsilon)$ of part (b) and $x = \epsilon/k_BT$:

<Formula label="Heat capacity of ripplons at constant area" tex="C_A = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_A = \int_0^\infty\epsilon\,\frac{\left(\epsilon/k_BT^2\right)e^{\epsilon/k_BT}}{\left(e^{\epsilon/k_BT} - 1\right)^2}\,\frac{A}{3\pi b^{4/3}}\,\epsilon^{1/3}\,d\epsilon = \frac{Ak_B}{3\pi b^{4/3}}\left(k_BT\right)^{4/3}I \;\propto\; T^{4/3}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

(MIT's solutions keep the $\frac12$ inside the derivative in the middle expression: a slip in print, since the derivative of a constant is zero. Their last line is as here.)

</Step>

<Step title="A second way">

MIT's solutions (p. 2) also start from the energy itself. Without the constant zero-point term, $U = A\left(k_BT\right)^{7/3}/\left(3\pi b^{4/3}\right)$ times the integral of $x^{4/3}/\left(e^x - 1\right)$, and one derivative gives

<Formula label="Heat capacity of ripplons, second form" tex="C_A = \frac{7Ak_B}{9\pi b^{4/3}}\left(k_BT\right)^{4/3}\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{4/3}}{e^x - 1}\,dx" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2" />

The two forms agree: an integration by parts turns $I$ into $\frac73$ times this second integral.

</Step>

<Step title="The power and the number (our additions)">

The power is $\frac43 = 1.333$. A numerical integration gives $I = 3.93$ (the second integral is 1.685, and $\frac73 \times 1.685 = 3.93$). The sketch is a curve through the origin that bends upwards, a little more steeply than a straight line.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The powers can be counted without any integral. At the temperature $T$ the modes with $\epsilon$ up to about $k_BT$ are excited. Their number is $G(k_BT) \propto T^{4/3}$, and each holds about $k_BT$, so $U \propto T^{7/3}$ and $C_A \propto T^{4/3}$. For phonons in three dimensions the same count gives the familiar $T^3$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Does the system show energy gap behaviour? Give your reasoning.

<Hint>

Energy gap behaviour is a heat capacity that is exponentially small at low temperature. It needs a lowest excitation at a finite energy. Is there one here?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-gap" label="Energy gap behaviour:" choices={["yes: every mode is a harmonic oscillator, and an oscillator has a gap between its levels", "no: there are modes with energies as close to zero as one likes", "no: ripplons live in two dimensions only"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One oscillator has a gap, its level spacing. A set of oscillators has a gap only if the smallest spacing is finite.

<Step title="No gap">

The energy $bk^{3/2}$ of a mode goes to zero with $k$, and the density of states of part (b) is not zero just above $\epsilon = 0$. So at any temperature there are modes with an energy below $k_BT$ that can be excited (MIT's solutions p. 2). There is no gap, and no energy gap behaviour: the heat capacity falls as the power $T^{4/3}$, not exponentially.

</Step>

<Step title="Mark yourself">

Give yourself the points if you said that the energies of the modes reach down to zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A two-dimensional metal" points={20} mit="Problem 2">

The electrons of the lesson moved in a box with zero potential energy. A simple model of the conduction electrons in a crystal puts a periodic potential in the box. The states of one electron can still be labelled by a wavevector $\vec k$, but the energy $\epsilon(\vec k)$ need not be quadratic in $k$, and need not be the same in every direction.

MIT's sheet shows an approximate dispersion relation for one two-dimensional metal as a drawing: a square pyramid standing on its tip at $\vec k = 0$, with four-fold rotational symmetry. Along the $k_x$ axis the energy is $\gamma\left|k_x\right|$, with a constant $\gamma$. A second drawing shows a contour of constant energy in the plane of $k_x$ and $k_y$: a square with its centre at the origin and its sides parallel to the axes. In a formula (ours, read from the drawings):

<Formula label="Dispersion relation of the two-dimensional metal" tex="\epsilon(\vec k) = \gamma\,\max\left(\left|k_x\right|, \left|k_y\right|\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

(MIT's sheet remarks that planes of conduction electrons are real: they matter in semiconductor electronics and in high-temperature superconductors.)

<Part label="a" points={2}>

The electron wavefunctions obey periodic boundary conditions in a square sample of side $L$. What are the allowed wavevectors? In the box give the distance between neighbouring allowed values of $k_x$.

<Hint>

A plane wave $e^{ik_xx}$ must have the same value at $x$ and at $x + L$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-spacing" label="Spacing of the allowed $k_x$ $=$" expr="2*pi/L"
  vars={{ L: [1, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi/L", hint: "That is the spacing for waves that vanish at the walls. A periodic wave must fit a whole number of wavelengths into L, not of half wavelengths." },
    { expr: "L/(2*pi)", hint: "That is one over the spacing: the number of allowed values per unit of k_x." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Periodicity over the length $L$ fixes the phase $k_xL$ to a multiple of $2\pi$.

<Step title="The allowed wavevectors">

<Formula label="Periodic boundary condition on a plane wave" tex="e^{ik_x(x + L)} = e^{ik_xx}\,e^{ik_xL} = e^{ik_xx} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k_x = \frac{2\pi}{L}\,n_x" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

with an integer $n_x$, and the same for $k_y$ with an integer $n_y$. The allowed wavevectors form a square grid with the spacing $2\pi/L$. (MIT's solutions list the integers as $\pm1, \pm2, \dots$ The value 0 is allowed too: a constant is periodic. One point does not change any density.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Find $D(\vec k)$, the density of allowed wavevectors. Count wavevectors only: the spin comes in part (c).

<Hint>

As in Problem 1(a): one over the area of the $k$-plane that belongs to one grid point.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-dk" label="$D(\vec k) =$" expr="L^2/(2*pi)^2"
  vars={{ L: [1, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*L^2/(2*pi)^2", hint: "That already counts the two spin states of each wavevector. This box asks for the wavevectors alone; the spin comes in part (c)." },
    { expr: "L/(2*pi)", hint: "That is the density along one axis. The wavevector has two components, and each brings a factor L/2π." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A square grid with the spacing $2\pi/L$ has one point for each area $(2\pi/L)^2$.

<Step title="The density">

<Formula label="Density of allowed wavevectors in the two-dimensional metal" tex="D(\vec k) = \left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^2 \quad\text{for all } \vec k" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2" />

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find $D(\epsilon)$, the density of the states of one electron as a function of the energy. Make a sketch with labelled axes.

<Hint>

Which wavevectors have an energy below $\epsilon$? They fill the inside of the contour. What is its area?

</Hint>

<Hint>

An electron has spin $\frac12$: two states for each wavevector.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-de" label="$D(\epsilon) =$" expr="4*L^2*E/(pi^2*g^2)"
  vars={{ L: [1, 10], E: [0.5, 2], g: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ E: "\\epsilon", g: "\\gamma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*L^2*E/(pi^2*g^2)", hint: "That counts one state for each wavevector. An electron has two spin states." },
    { expr: "2*L^2*E^2/(pi^2*g^2)", hint: "That is the number of states with an energy below ε. The density of states is its derivative with respect to ε." },
    { expr: "L^2*E/(pi*g^2)", hint: "That takes the contour to be a circle of radius ε/γ. For this metal it is a square of side 2ε/γ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count the states inside the contour of the energy $\epsilon$, then differentiate.

<Step title="The number of states below an energy">

The contour is a square that cuts the axes at $\pm\epsilon/\gamma$, so its side is $2\epsilon/\gamma$. With two spin states for each wavevector, the number $G(\epsilon)$ of states below $\epsilon$ is

<Formula label="Number of electron states below an energy in the two-dimensional metal" tex="G(\epsilon) = 2\,D(\vec k)\left(\frac{2\epsilon}{\gamma}\right)^2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The density of states">

<Formula label="Density of states of the two-dimensional metal" tex="D(\epsilon) = \frac{dG}{d\epsilon} = 2\left(\frac{L}{2\pi}\right)^2\frac{8}{\gamma^2}\,\epsilon = 4\left(\frac{L}{\pi\gamma}\right)^2\epsilon" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2" />

The sketch (MIT's solutions p. 3) is a straight line through the origin: energy $\epsilon$ on the horizontal axis, $D(\epsilon)$ on the vertical axis, with the slope $4L^2/\pi^2\gamma^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For free electrons in two dimensions the energy grows as $k^2$ and the density of states is a constant. Here the energy grows only as $k$, so equal steps in energy take in ever wider rings of the $k$-plane, and $D(\epsilon)$ grows with $\epsilon$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

The metal has $N$ conduction electrons. Find the Fermi energy $\epsilon_F$, the energy of the last one-electron state that is filled at $T = 0$.

<Hint>

At $T = 0$ the $N$ electrons fill the $N$ states of lowest energy: $G(\epsilon_F) = N$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ef" label="$\epsilon_F =$" expr="pi*g*sqrt(N/2)/L"
  vars={{ g: [0.5, 2], N: [10, 1000], L: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ g: "\\gamma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi*g*sqrt(N)/L", hint: "Each wavevector holds two electrons, one for each spin state. That halves the number of wavevectors needed." },
    { expr: "pi^2*g^2*N/(2*L^2)", hint: "That is the square of the Fermi energy: the number of states below ε grows as ε², so a square root is still to be taken." },
    { expr: "pi*g*sqrt(N/2)", hint: "The side L of the sample is missing. The Fermi energy depends on the number of electrons per area, N/L²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Fill the states from the bottom until all $N$ electrons are placed.

<Step title="The Fermi energy">

With $A = L^2$ for the area of the sample:

<Formula label="Fermi energy of the two-dimensional metal" tex="N = G(\epsilon_F) = \frac{8L^2}{(2\pi)^2}\left(\frac{\epsilon_F}{\gamma}\right)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \epsilon_F = \left(\frac12\pi^2\gamma^2\,\frac{N}{A}\right)^{1/2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(d), p. 3" />

(MIT's solutions print the first equation without the square on the last bracket: a slip in print. Their $\epsilon_F$ is as here.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The Fermi energy depends on $N$ and $L$ only through the number of electrons per area, as an intensive quantity should. The filled states are a square in the $k$-plane with the corners at $k_x = \pm\epsilon_F/\gamma$, $k_y = \pm\epsilon_F/\gamma$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

Find the total energy $U$ of the electrons at $T = 0$, in terms of $N$ and $\epsilon_F$.

<Hint>

Write $D(\epsilon) = a\epsilon$ with a constant $a$. Both $N$ and $U$ are integrals up to $\epsilon_F$. Divide one by the other and $a$ drops out.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-u" label="$U =$" expr="2*N*EF/3"
  vars={{ N: [10, 1000], EF: [0.5, 5] }}
  names={{ EF: "\\epsilon_F" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*N*EF/5", hint: "That is the result for free electrons in three dimensions, where the density of states grows as the square root of ε. Here it grows as ε." },
    { expr: "N*EF/2", hint: "That would hold for a constant density of states. Here the density of states grows with ε, so more of the electrons sit near the top." },
    { expr: "N*EF", hint: "That gives every electron the Fermi energy. Only the last electron has it: the others fill the states below." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The mean energy of an electron is the mean of $\epsilon$ over the filled part of the density of states.

<Step title="The two integrals">

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Donor impurity states in a semiconductor" points={25} mit="Problem 3">

The lesson treated a pure (intrinsic) semiconductor: the number of electrons in the conduction band and the chemical potential, both as functions of the temperature. In a pure semiconductor the number of carriers depends very strongly on the temperature, which makes it of little use for electronics. One would like a number of carriers that hardly changes with the temperature, but that can be steered by a voltage, through the position of the conduction band relative to the chemical potential. That is done by **doping**: adding impurity atoms that easily give up an outer electron to the conduction band. Such impurities are called donors.

MIT's sheet sketches the density of states of a doped semiconductor. On the left is the top of the valence band, whose states are all filled at $T = 0$. Inside the gap of the pure material stands a delta function: the $N_D$ donor states, one electron for each donor atom, also all filled at $T = 0$. On the right is the bottom of the conduction band, empty at $T = 0$. Because the mean occupation number $\langle n\rangle$ is a step at $T = 0$, the Fermi energy $\epsilon_F$ (the chemical potential at $T = 0$) must lie above the donor energy and below the bottom of the conduction band.

When the temperature rises from zero, electrons are first lifted from the donors into the conduction band. Only at a much higher temperature, when the donors have lost almost all their electrons, do electrons from the valence band follow. (MIT's sheet says "valance band" for the first of these two steps as well: a slip in print.) The difficulty is that the chemical potential falls through the donor energy on the way. The approximate forms of $\langle n\rangle$ may be good in the band, but at the donor energy the full form is needed.

The model of this problem is simpler: leave out the valence band. Put $\epsilon = 0$ at the donor states, and let $\Delta$ be the energy of the bottom of the conduction band. The density of states is then

<Formula label="Density of states of the donor model" tex="D(\epsilon) = N_D\,\delta(\epsilon) + \frac{V}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m_e}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\left(\epsilon - \Delta\right)^{1/2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 4" />

where the second term is there for $\epsilon > \Delta$ only, $V$ is the volume and $m_e$ the mass of an electron in the band. The Fermi energy lies somewhere between 0 and $\Delta$, and the chemical potential $\mu$ moves with the temperature. Assume $\Delta \gg k_BT$ throughout, as holds in practice.

<Formula label="Densities of states of the two spin states in a field" tex="D_+(\epsilon) = \frac12D_0\left(\epsilon + \mu_0H\right), \qquad D_-(\epsilon) = \frac12D_0\left(\epsilon - \mu_0H\right), \qquad D(\epsilon) = \frac12\left[D_0\left(\epsilon + \mu_0H\right) + D_0\left(\epsilon - \mu_0H\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4(a), p. 7" />

</Step>

<Step title="The sketch">

<Step title="Check the result">

For $H = 0$ the two halves lie on top of each other and add up to $D_0(\epsilon)$. Far above $\mu_0H$ the shift matters little and $D(\epsilon)$ comes back to $D_0(\epsilon)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

For $N$ particles in the volume $V$ at $T = 0$, find the smallest magnetic field $H_0$ that polarizes the spins completely: no particle is left in the spin state of higher energy.

<Hint>

At $T = 0$ the states are filled from the bottom, which is at $\epsilon = -\mu_0H$. Up to which energy may they be filled if no particle is to have $m_s = -\frac12$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

At the field $H_0$ the $N$ particles exactly fill the states of $D_+$ between $-\mu_0H_0$ and $+\mu_0H_0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-h0" label="$H_0 =$" expr="hbar^2*(6*pi^2*N/V)^(2/3)/(4*m*mu0)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], N: [1, 10], V: [1, 10], m: [0.5, 2], mu0: [0.5, 2] }}

**The idea.** The filling at $T = 0$ must stop just before $\epsilon = \mu_0H$, where the states with $m_s = -\frac12$ begin.

<Step title="Filling one spin state">

Write $D_0(\epsilon) = a\,\epsilon^{1/2}$ with $a = \left(V/2\pi^2\right)\left(2m/\hbar^2\right)^{3/2}$. At the smallest field that polarizes the gas,

<Formula label="Number of particles in the fully polarized gas" tex="N = \int_{-\mu_0H}^{\mu_0H}D(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon = \int_0^{2\mu_0H}\frac12D_0(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon = \frac a2\,\frac23\left(2\mu_0H\right)^{3/2} = \frac{V}{6\pi^2}\left(\frac{4m\mu_0H}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4(b), p. 7" />

</Step>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="How much helium-3 dissolves in helium-4 at T = 0" points={10} mit="Problem 5">

Helium has two stable isotopes. The common one, helium-4, has nuclear spin 0 and is a composite boson. The rare, lighter one, helium-3, has nuclear spin $\frac12$ and is a composite fermion. Helium atoms attract each other in pairs by the van der Waals interaction, which is the same for both isotopes.

At very low temperature a liquid mixture of the two separates into a phase rich in helium-3 and a phase rich in helium-4. MIT's sheet shows the phase diagram: the temperature, from 0 to about 0.5 K, against the fraction $x$ of helium-3, with a region in the middle that has no stable mixture. At $T = 0$ the phase rich in helium-3 becomes pure helium-3. The phase rich in helium-4 does not become pure: it can hold helium-3 up to a critical concentration

<Formula label="Critical concentration of helium-3 in helium-4" tex="x_C = \frac{N_3}{N_3 + N_4} = \frac{n_3}{n_3 + n_4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 6" />

where $n_3$ and $n_4$ are the number densities $N/V$ of the two isotopes in that phase.

A simple model explains this. The lighter isotope has the larger zero-point motion, so the atoms in pure liquid helium-3 are farther apart than those in liquid helium-4. A single helium-3 atom dissolved in helium-4 therefore has neighbours that are closer, and its energy is lower than it would be in pure helium-3. MIT's sheet draws this as a step in an effective potential energy at the boundary of the two phases: the potential energy of a helium-3 atom is lower by $W$ in the phase rich in helium-4.

As more helium-3 atoms dissolve, they behave as free particles with an effective mass $m^*$, which comes from their motion through the liquid around them. A dissolved atom with the momentum $\hbar k$ has the kinetic energy $\epsilon(k) = \hbar^2k^2/2m^*$. Helium-3 goes on dissolving until the total energy needed to add one more atom, potential plus kinetic, is larger than the energy of adding it to the pure helium-3.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the largest number density of helium-3 atoms that can be dissolved in helium-4 at $T = 0$.

<Hint>

The dissolved atoms are fermions with spin $\frac12$ at $T = 0$. Each new atom must go into the lowest free state. What is its kinetic energy when $n_3$ atoms per volume are already there?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Dissolving stops when that kinetic energy uses up the whole gain $W$ in potential energy.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-n" label="Largest $n_3 =$" expr="(2*m*W/hbar^2)^(3/2)/(3*pi^2)"
  vars={{ m: [0.5, 2], W: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ m: "m^*", hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(2*m*W/hbar^2)^(3/2)/(6*pi^2)", hint: "That allows one atom for each wavevector. Helium-3 has spin 1/2, so each wavevector holds two atoms." },
    { expr: "(m*W/hbar^2)^(3/2)/(3*pi^2)", hint: "The kinetic energy is ħ²k²/2m*: solving it for the Fermi wavevector gives 2m*W under the power, not m*W." },
    { expr: "(2*m*W/hbar^2)^(2/3)/(3*pi^2)", hint: "The power is upside down: the Fermi energy grows as the density to the power 2/3, so the density grows as the energy to the power 3/2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The dissolved atoms form a Fermi gas. The next atom needs the Fermi energy as kinetic energy, and it gains $W$ in potential energy.

<Step title="The Fermi energy of the dissolved atoms">

For fermions with spin $\frac12$ and the mass $m^*$ in three dimensions (the lesson):

<Formula label="Fermi energy of the dissolved helium-3" tex="k_F = \left(3\pi^2n_3\right)^{1/3}, \qquad \epsilon_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*}\left(3\pi^2n_3\right)^{2/3}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(a), p. 8" />

</Step>

<Step title="The limit">

When the Fermi energy reaches $W$, a further atom is better off in the pure helium-3, where its kinetic energy can start from zero:

<Formula label="Largest density of helium-3 dissolved in helium-4 at T = 0" tex="W = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m^*}\left(3\pi^2n_3\right)^{2/3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n_3 = \frac{1}{3\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m^*W}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5(a), p. 8" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A deeper step $W$ or a heavier effective mass lets more atoms in, as expected: a heavier atom has less kinetic energy for the same wavevector. In the language of the lesson, the chemical potentials of helium-3 in the two phases are equal at the limit.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Check that the result of part (a) is reasonable. Estimate $x_C$ with MIT's round numbers: $\hbar \approx 1 \times 10^{-27}$ erg s, $k_B \approx 1 \times 10^{-16}$ erg K⁻¹, $m^* \approx 10 \times 10^{-24}$ g, $W \approx 0.5$ K (a temperature: the energy is $k_B$ times it) and $n_4 \approx 2 \times 10^{22}$ cm⁻³.

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="The melting curve of helium-3" points={15} mit="Problem 6">

Helium-3 is a composite fermion with nuclear spin $\frac12$. Under its own vapour pressure it stays liquid down to $T = 0$. Compressed at low temperature it becomes solid at pressures of the order of 30 atmospheres. MIT's sheet shows part of the phase diagram, pressure against temperature, with the solid at high pressure and the liquid below it. The temperature axis is divided into three regions: I at the lowest temperatures, II above it, and III above about 0.3 K. A question mark stands where the melting curve belongs.

In region III the temperature is too high for simple models. In region II the liquid can be treated as a degenerate Fermi gas with spin $\frac12$, at a temperature so low that it may be taken to be $T = 0$. The solid can be treated as a set of spin-$\frac12$ paramagnets that do not interact and that sit on fixed lattice sites, so that the atoms in the solid are distinguishable. There is no magnetic field.

In region I the liquid is still a degenerate Fermi gas. The nuclear spins in the solid are now ordered, in an antiferromagnetic state: each nuclear spin behaves as if it were in an effective magnetic field $H_{\text{eff}}$, made by the alignment of all the other moments. In region I the energy of a moment in this field is much larger than $k_BT$.

The slope of the melting curve $P_M(T)$ is

<Formula label="Slope of the melting curve" tex="\frac{dP_M}{dT} = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta V}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 8" />

where $\Delta S$ is the entropy of the liquid minus that of the solid, and $\Delta V$ is the volume of the liquid minus that of the solid. In helium-3, as in most substances, $\Delta V$ is positive. Take it to be $Nv_0$ with a constant $v_0$, at every temperature.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the entropy of $N$ atoms of the liquid in region II?

<Hint>

In how many ways can $N$ fermions be put into single-particle states at $T = 0$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-liquid" label="Entropy of the liquid in region II:" choices={["N k_B ln 2, from the two spin states that each atom can choose between", "(3/2) N k_B, as for any gas of atoms", "zero: the filled Fermi sea is a single state"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The model says: a Fermi gas at $T = 0$.

<Step title="One state">

At $T = 0$ the fermions fill the $N$ lowest single-particle states, two atoms of opposite spin for each wavevector. That is one state of the whole gas, and its entropy is zero (MIT's solutions p. 9). The spins give no freedom: both spin states of every filled wavevector are taken.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

What is the entropy of $N$ atoms of the solid in region II?

<Hint>

Each atom sits on its own site, and without a field its two spin states have the same energy.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-solid" label="Entropy of the solid in region II $=$" expr="N*kB*log(2)"
  vars={{ N: [1, 10], kB: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "kB*log(2)", hint: "That is the entropy of one spin. The N spins are independent, so their entropies add." },
    { expr: "kB*log(2*N)", hint: "N independent spins with two states each have 2 to the power N states together, not 2N." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count the states of $N$ distinguishable spins that are free to point either way.

<Step title="Counting">

Each spin has two states of equal energy, so the solid has $2^N$ equally likely spin states:

<Formula label="Entropy of the solid helium-3 in region II" tex="S = k_B\ln 2^N = Nk_B\ln 2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(b), p. 9" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is the high-temperature limit of the paramagnet of the lesson with two levels, here reached at any temperature because the field is zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

What is the entropy of the solid in region I (in effect at $T = 0$)?

<Hint>

In a field that is strong compared with $k_BT$, how many of its two states does a spin really use?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-solid1" label="Entropy of the solid in region I:" choices={["zero: every moment is fixed by the effective field", "N k_B ln 2, as in region II", "(1/2) N k_B ln 2: only half of the spins are still free to turn"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A strong field leaves each spin one state.

<Step title="The ordered solid">

With the energy in the effective field much larger than $k_BT$, every moment takes its state of lowest energy in that field. The solid has one spin state, and its entropy goes to $Nk_B\ln 1 = 0$ (MIT's solutions p. 9).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

What is the slope of the melting curve in region II?

<Hint>

Use the entropies of parts (a) and (b). Mind the order: liquid minus solid.

</Hint>

  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 2], v0: [0.5, 2], N: [2, 10] }}

  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the two entropies into the formula for the slope.

<Step title="The slope">

<Formula label="Slope of the melting curve of helium-3 in region II" tex="\frac{dP_M}{dT} = \frac{0 - Nk_B\ln 2}{Nv_0} = -\frac{k_B\ln 2}{v_0}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(d), p. 9" />

It is negative: the melting pressure falls as the temperature rises. That is unusual. In most substances the liquid has the larger entropy, and the melting curve rises.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The slope is a constant in this model, so in region II the melting curve is a straight line that falls to the right.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

What is the slope of the melting curve in region I?

<Hint>

Use the entropies of parts (a) and (c).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-slope1" label="Slope in region I:" choices={["negative, as in region II", "zero: liquid and solid both have no entropy", "positive: the ordered solid now has less entropy than the liquid"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both phases have gone to their single lowest state.

<Step title="The slope">

$\Delta S = 0 - 0$, so the slope of the melting curve goes to zero (MIT's solutions p. 9): the curve becomes flat as $T \to 0$. The third law asks for exactly this, since all entropy differences vanish at $T = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The whole curve (a remark of ours)">

Put together: coming from $T = 0$ the melting curve starts flat (region I) and then falls along a straight line (region II). In region III, which the models do not reach, the liquid gains entropy until it has more than the solid, so the curve must turn upwards again. The melting pressure has a minimum there.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="f" points={3}>

The system is on the melting curve in region II: liquid and solid are both present, in equilibrium. Heat is added. Does some of the solid melt, or does some of the liquid become solid? Why?

<Hint>

Adding heat reversibly raises the entropy. Which of the two phases has the larger entropy for each atom here?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-heat" label="When heat is added," choices={["some solid melts, as ice does when it is heated", "nothing changes until the temperature has left the melting curve", "some liquid becomes solid, the phase with more entropy"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Heat taken in raises the entropy, and on the melting curve the only way to change the entropy is to move atoms from one phase to the other.

<Step title="Freezing by heating">

In region II the solid has the entropy $k_B\ln 2$ for each atom and the liquid has none. So the entropy can rise only if liquid turns into solid: the system freezes when it is heated and melts when it is cooled (MIT's solutions p. 9).

</Step>

<Step title="A remark of ours">

Read the other way round, this is a way of cooling: squeezing a mixture of liquid and solid helium-3 turns liquid into solid, which takes up entropy, and with no heat coming in the temperature falls. The method is known as Pomeranchuk cooling.

</Step>

</Step>

</Solution>
