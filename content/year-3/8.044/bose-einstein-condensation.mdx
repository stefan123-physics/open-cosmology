The last lecture of the course takes the Bose–Einstein occupation of Lecture 18 to its most dramatic consequence. For bosons there is no exclusion principle, and at low enough temperature a finite fraction of all the particles piles into one single quantum state (slides 1–9, and an illustrated history after them). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The history part shows photographs and figures from other sources, which are described here in words only.)

## The chemical potential of a Bose gas

The two occupations (slide 1), as functions of $(\epsilon - \mu)/kT$: Fermi–Dirac never exceeds 1, but Bose–Einstein, $1/(e^{(\epsilon - \mu)/kT} - 1)$, grows without limit as $\epsilon$ approaches $\mu$ from above. So for bosons $\mu$ must stay **below** the lowest single-particle energy, here $\epsilon = 0$.

Fix the number of particles $N$ and the volume, and lower the temperature (slides 1–6; the occupation's divergence at $\epsilon = \mu$ is why $\mu$ cannot enter the boson spectrum). The number in the gas is $\int\langle n(\epsilon)\rangle D(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon$, with $D(\epsilon) \propto \epsilon^{1/2}$ for free particles (Lecture 19; slide 2 contrasts this with a Fermi gas, whose $\mu$ can sit inside the spectrum). As $T$ falls, each occupation shrinks for given $\mu$, so to keep $N$ fixed $\mu$ must rise towards $0$, making the occupations near the bottom larger. At some temperature $T_c$ it arrives: $\mu(T_c) = 0$.

## The critical temperature

With $\mu = 0$ the integral gives the largest number of particles the excited states can hold (slide 7):

<Formula label="Particles in the excited states at μ = 0" tex="N = \int_0^\infty\frac{(2S + 1)V}{4\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\frac{E^{1/2}}{e^{E/kT} - 1}\,dE = (2S + 1)\,\zeta(3/2)\,\frac{V}{\lambda^3(T)}" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

using $\int_0^\infty x^{1/2}/(e^x - 1)\,dx = \frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\zeta(3/2)$, where $\zeta(3/2) = 2.612\ldots$ is a value of the Riemann zeta function and $\lambda(T) = h/\sqrt{2\pi mkT}$ is the thermal de Broglie wavelength (slide 8). For spin-0 bosons at fixed number density $n = N/V$, this fixes the **critical temperature**; equivalently, at fixed temperature, a **critical density**:

<Formula label="Critical temperature and density" tex="T_c = \frac{h^2}{2\pi mk}\left(\frac{n}{\zeta(3/2)}\right)^{2/3} \propto n^{2/3}, \qquad n_c = \zeta(3/2)\,\lambda^{-3}(T) \propto T^{3/2}" cite="mit" at="slide 8" />

The condition $n\lambda^3 = \zeta(3/2)$ says that condensation sets in when the particles' thermal wavelength becomes comparable to the distance between them.

## The condensate

Below $T_c$, $\mu$ stays at $0$ (it cannot go higher), and the excited states can hold only $N(T/T_c)^{3/2}$ particles. The rest have nowhere to go but the lowest state, $\epsilon = 0$ (slide 9):

<Formula label="Condensate fraction" tex="N_0 = N - \int_0^\infty\langle n(\epsilon)\rangle D(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon = N\left[1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{3/2}\right]" cite="mit" at="slide 9" />

A macroscopic number of particles in one single-particle state: **Bose–Einstein condensation**. (The smooth density of states misses this state entirely, which is why it has to be added by hand.)

## How it was found

The rest of the lecture tells the story of how this prediction was finally seen, as a timeline running down in temperature from 300 K to nanokelvin (history slides after slide 9). In summary, in this page's own words:

- **Waves, not points.** For atoms at room temperature the thermal wavelength is smaller than the atoms themselves, so they behave like classical particles. Quantum mechanics still matters: it stabilizes electrons in atoms, lets protons get close enough to fuse in the Sun, and lets molecules stick to surfaces (as in Lecture 18).
- **Bose and Einstein**, in 1924–25, worked out the statistics of identical bosons and used it on the simplest possible system, an ideal gas of atoms. Cooling makes each atom's thermal wavelength longer; once it rivals the distance between neighbours, the gas changes phase, and a share of the atoms stops behaving as separate particles, becoming one coherent matter wave across the whole container. Roughly half of all kinds of atoms are bosons and half fermions; at low temperature fermions fill a Fermi sea while bosons condense.
- **Real atoms interact**, and liquefy and freeze first: oxygen liquefies near 90 K, hydrogen near 20 K and freezes near 14 K, helium liquefies near 4 K, and pumping on liquid helium-4 reaches lower still. Along this path came **superconductivity** (Kamerlingh Onnes, 1911), **superfluid helium-4** (1930s, near 2 K, a strongly interacting relative of condensation), and the two **superfluid phases of helium-3**, a Fermi liquid, found as glitches on its melting curve (Nobel Prize 1996).
- **A gas that stays a gas.** A 1976 paper argued that hydrogen atoms, if a field keeps their spins lined up, can never pair into molecules and so should stay gaseous at any temperature, and several groups, MIT among them, set out to condense it. Contact with a container flipped spins and let pairs form molecules, so magnetic fields were used to hold the atoms away from any surface; in 1986 Harald Hess, a postdoc in the MIT hydrogen group, proposed cooling the trapped gas further by **evaporation**, letting the most energetic atoms escape.
- **Lasers and evaporation.** From the 1980s, laser cooling took certain atoms, such as lithium, sodium and rubidium, into the microkelvin range (Nobel Prize 1997), but not far enough; evaporative cooling in magnetic traps did the rest. In 1995 (a date read from the lecture's timeline) groups led by Cornell and Wieman (rubidium) and Ketterle (sodium) produced Bose–Einstein condensates at temperatures from about a hundred nanokelvin to a few microkelvin; Hulet's group worked with lithium.
- **Seeing it.** The proof came from letting the cloud go: the uncondensed atoms, which move with thermal speeds, spread out quickly, while the nearly motionless condensate stays compact, so a sharp central peak stands out in an image taken a little later. Overlapping condensates also show interference fringes, as waves should (Nobel Prize 2001 to Cornell, Ketterle and Wieman).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Photons are bosons too">
Black-body radiation is a gas of bosons whose number is not conserved, so its chemical potential is zero, the same value a Bose gas reaches at $T_c$. The integrals that gave $N$ here, with $\epsilon^2$ in place of $\epsilon^{1/2}$ because photons are relativistic, give the number of photons per unit volume, proportional to $\zeta(3)T^3$. 8.286 uses exactly this to count the photons left over from the early universe (Lecture 16).
</Callout>

Find $T_c$, treating it as an ideal, uniform gas. (b) What fraction is condensed at 100 nK? (c) Compare the thermal wavelength at $T_c$ with the spacing $n^{-1/3}$. (d) Liquid helium-4 ($m = 6.6465 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, mass density 145 kg/m³): what $T_c$ would the ideal-gas formula give?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Rubidium">
$h^2/(2\pi mk) \approx 3.51 \times 10^{-20}$ K m², and $(n/2.612)^{2/3} \approx 1.14 \times 10^{13}$ m⁻², so $T_c \approx 3.98 \times 10^{-7}$ K, about 400 nK.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Condensate at 100 nK">
$1 - (100/398)^{3/2} \approx 0.87$: most of the atoms are in the lowest state.
</Step>

</Step>

</Step>

## Summary

- Bose–Einstein occupations require $\mu$ below the lowest level; at fixed density, $\mu$ rises towards $0$ as $T$ falls.
- At $\mu = 0$ the excited states hold at most $(2S + 1)\zeta(3/2)V/\lambda^3$, which defines $T_c = \frac{h^2}{2\pi mk}(n/\zeta(3/2))^{2/3}$ (spin 0), or $n_c = \zeta(3/2)/\lambda^3$.
- Below $T_c$: $N_0 = N[1 - (T/T_c)^{3/2}]$ in the single lowest state.
- Observed in 1995 in dilute alkali gases, laser-cooled and then evaporatively cooled in magnetic traps to between about a hundred nanokelvin and a few microkelvin.
