This set practises the lesson on [one random variable](/courses/8-044/one-random-variable): probability densities, cumulative functions, delta functions for discrete and mixed variables, and averages. The six problems follow MIT's Problem Set 1, in our own words. MIT publishes its solutions on OpenCourseWare. The hints here are ours. The solutions are rewritten from MIT's: they follow MIT's method in our own words, cite MIT's solutions at the printed page, and were checked again by an independent route.

A few things differ from MIT's material. The due date is left out. MIT's sketches are redrawn as graphs computed from formulas. MIT's solutions have two slips, each noted in brackets where it matters: a sign in the last line of Problem 1(c), and the label of Problem 6(b). MIT's solutions to Problem 3 end with a figure of a quantum oscillator in a high energy state: it is not reproduced, only described. Some boxes, checks and remarks are our additions, each marked as such: the formula for $P(x)$ beyond the grain boundary in Problem 1(a) and the second route to the variance in Problem 1(c); the remark on the order of the limits in Problem 2(c) and the cut-off integral in Problem 2(e); the second route to the constant in Problem 3(b) and the probability in Problem 3(c); the variance and the value of the cumulative function in Problem 4; the peak height in Problem 5(c); and, in Problem 6, the numbers for $a = 0.75$, the box for the envelope, the value of $\phi$ for $\langle n\rangle = 10$ and the correction $\frac{1}{2}$. Both PDFs have printed page numbers, which are the ones cited below: "p. 1" to "p. 5" for the sheet, "p. 1" to "p. 10" for the solutions.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box wants a decimal number, not a fraction, and accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `0.5*l + 2*d`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent, so each problem says which letters to use. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `l`, `d`, `s`, `b`, `L`, `x`, `x0` for $x_0$, `omega` for $\omega$, `alpha` for $\alpha$, `t`, `a`, and `N` for the mean $\langle n\rangle$ in Problem 6. Type `pi` for $\pi$, `exp(-x/l)` for $e^{-x/l}$, `sqrt( )` for a square root, `atan( )` for the arctangent and `log( )` for the natural logarithm.

MIT's sheet gives no points. The points here (100 in all) and their split between parts and boxes are ours. Parts that ask for a sketch or an explanation have no box, or a box for only a piece of them: open the solution, compare it with your own work and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lesson: $p(x)$ is the probability density, $P(x)$ the cumulative function, $\langle x\rangle$ the mean and $\text{Var}(x)$ the variance. No constants of nature are needed: every answer is a formula or a pure number.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Doping a semiconductor" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

Impurity atoms have been diffused into a semiconductor from its surface. Let $x$ be the depth of one impurity atom below the surface. Its probability density is zero for $x < 0$, and for $x \ge 0$ it is

<Formula label="Density of the depth of an impurity" tex="p(x) = \frac{0.8}{l}\,e^{-x/l} + 0.2\,\delta(x - d)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Here $l$ and $d$ are two lengths. The delta function is there because some of the impurities were caught on a grain boundary (a flaw in the crystal that was not planned) at the depth $d$. The graph shows this density for $d = 1.5\,l$.

Its height on the graph means nothing: a delta function is infinitely high and infinitely narrow, and what counts is its weight, 0.2." />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Sketch the cumulative function $P(x)$ and label its important features. MIT does not ask for a formula for $P(x)$. As a check of your sketch (our addition), give $P(x)$ for depths beyond the grain boundary, $x > d$, in terms of `x` and `l`.

<Hint>

$P(x)$ is the area under $p$ to the left of $x$. How much area does a delta function add once $x$ has passed it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Before the boundary only the exponential part counts. Its area up to $x$ is an integral you can do. At large $x$ all the probability must have been collected.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-jump" label="At $x = d$ the cumulative function"
  choices={["jumps up by 0.2 and stays up", "has a spike of height 0.2 and comes back down", "has a kink but no jump", "jumps up to the value 0.2"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p1-cum" label="For $x > d$: $P(x) =$" expr="1 - 0.8*exp(-x/l)"
  vars={{ x: [0.5, 4], l: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ x: "x", l: "l" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "0.8*(1 - exp(-x/l))", hint: "That is the exponential part alone. It tends to 0.8, not to 1, at great depth: beyond d the impurities on the grain boundary count as well." },
    { expr: "1 - exp(-x/l)", hint: "That would be the cumulative function if every impurity followed the exponential. Only a fraction 0.8 of them does." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Integrate the density in your head from the left. The exponential part adds area smoothly, ever more slowly. The delta function adds its whole weight at one point.

<Step title="The shape">

$P(x)$ is zero at the surface. It first rises as the area under the exponential part, $0.8\left(1 - e^{-x/l}\right)$, with a slope that keeps falling. At $x = d$ it jumps by 0.2, the weight of the delta function. Beyond $d$ it keeps rising, more and more slowly, towards 1:

<Formula label="Cumulative function of the depth" tex="P(x) = 0.8\left(1 - e^{-x/l}\right) \quad (0 \le x < d), \qquad P(x) = 0.8\left(1 - e^{-x/l}\right) + 0.2 = 1 - 0.8\,e^{-x/l} \quad (x \ge d)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (MIT gives the sketch and its description; the formula is our addition)" />

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 6]} range={[0, 1.05]} xLabel="x / l" yLabel="P(x)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "0.8*(1 - exp(-x)) + (x >= 1.5 ? 0.2 : 0)", label: "P(x)" },
    { expr: "1", dash: true }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 1.5, y: 0.6215, label: "below the jump" }, { x: 1.5, y: 0.8215, label: "above the jump" }]}
  caption="The cumulative function for d = 1.5 l, computed from the formula above. It jumps by 0.2 at x = d, here from 0.6215 to 0.8215, and tends to 1 (dashed line) at great depth." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$P(0) = 0$ and $P \to 1$ as $x \to \infty$, as for every cumulative function. The slope of $P$ away from $d$ is the exponential part of $p$, and the jump of 0.2 is the weight of the delta function: differentiating $P$ gives $p$ back.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the mean depth $\langle x\rangle$, in terms of `l` and `d`.

<Hint>

The mean is the integral of $x\,p(x)$. Take the two terms of $p$ one at a time.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Under an integral, a delta function picks out the value of everything else at the point where it sits (the sifting property of the lesson). The integral with the exponential can be done by parts.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-mean" label="$\langle x\rangle =$" expr="0.8*l + 0.2*d"
  vars={{ l: [0.5, 2], d: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ l: "l", d: "d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "l + 0.2*d", hint: "The exponential part holds only a fraction 0.8 of the impurities, so its mean depth l counts with the weight 0.8." },
    { expr: "0.8*l", hint: "That leaves out the impurities on the grain boundary. They sit at the depth d and make up a fraction 0.2 of all." },
    { expr: "0.8*l + d", hint: "The delta function has the weight 0.2, so the depth d counts with the weight 0.2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** An average is an integral over the density. The delta function turns its integral into the value of the integrand at $x = d$.

<Step title="The two integrals">

Integrating by parts, $\int_0^\infty x\,e^{-x/l}\,dx = l^2$. The delta function gives $\int_0^\infty x\,\delta(x - d)\,dx = d$. So

<Formula label="Mean depth" tex="\langle x\rangle = \frac{0.8}{l}\int_0^\infty x\,e^{-x/l}\,dx + 0.2\int_0^\infty x\,\delta(x - d)\,dx = 0.8\,l + 0.2\,d" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both terms are lengths. The result is a weighted average: the impurities that follow the exponential have the mean depth $l$ and the weight 0.8, and those on the boundary have the depth $d$ and the weight 0.2. If $d = l$, the mean is $l$, as it must be when both groups have the same mean.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Find the variance of the depth, $\text{Var}(x) \equiv \left\langle\left(x - \langle x\rangle\right)^2\right\rangle$, in terms of `l` and `d`.

<Hint>

The lesson gives a shorter form: $\text{Var}(x) = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2$. So find $\langle x^2\rangle$ first, in the same way as the mean.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrating by parts twice, $\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x/l}\,dx = 2l^3$. When you square the mean of part (b), do not lose the cross term.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-var" label="$\text{Var}(x) =$" expr="0.96*l^2 - 0.32*l*d + 0.16*d^2"
  vars={{ l: [0.5, 2], d: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ l: "l", d: "d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1.6*l^2 + 0.2*d^2", hint: "That is the mean square. The variance is the mean square minus the square of the mean." },
    { expr: "0.96*l^2 - 0.32*l*d - 0.16*d^2", hint: "Check the sign of the last term: 0.2 d² minus 0.04 d². With a minus sign the result would be negative for small l, and a variance is never negative." },
    { expr: "0.96*l^2 + 0.16*d^2", hint: "The square of the mean, (0.8 l + 0.2 d)², has a cross term, 0.32 l d." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the mean square as in part (b), then subtract the square of the mean.

<Step title="The mean square">

With $\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x/l}\,dx = 2l^3$ and $\int_0^\infty x^2\,\delta(x - d)\,dx = d^2$:

<Formula label="Mean square of the depth" tex="\langle x^2\rangle = \frac{0.8}{l}\,2l^3 + 0.2\,d^2 = 1.6\,l^2 + 0.2\,d^2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The variance">

<Formula label="Variance of the depth" tex="\text{Var}(x) = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2 = \left(1.6\,l^2 + 0.2\,d^2\right) - \left(0.64\,l^2 + 0.32\,l\,d + 0.04\,d^2\right) = 0.96\,l^2 - 0.32\,l\,d + 0.16\,d^2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

(The last line of MIT's solution prints the final term as $-0.16\,d^2$. That is a sign slip: the line above it in MIT's solution, the same as the middle step here, gives $0.2\,d^2 - 0.04\,d^2 = +0.16\,d^2$.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result (a second route, our addition)">

Treat the impurities as two groups. The exponential group (weight 0.8) has the mean $l$ and the variance $2l^2 - l^2 = l^2$. The group on the boundary (weight 0.2) has the mean $d$ and no spread. The variance of the whole is the average of the variances inside the groups, plus the spread of the two group means about the overall mean, which for two groups is the product of the weights times the square of the distance between the means:

<Formula label="Variance from the two groups" tex="\text{Var}(x) = 0.8\,l^2 + 0.8 \times 0.2\,(l - d)^2 = 0.96\,l^2 - 0.32\,l\,d + 0.16\,d^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own check)" />

This is the same result, and in this form it is clearly never negative. In the limit $l \to 0$ the exponential part shrinks to a spike at the surface, and the variance becomes $0.16\,d^2$: that of a variable that is $0$ with probability 0.8 and $d$ with probability 0.2.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={6}>

An impurity adds to the microwave surface impedance of the sample, and its contribution falls off with its depth as $e^{-x/s}$. The length $s$ is called the skin depth. Find the average $\left\langle e^{-x/s}\right\rangle$, in terms of `l`, `d` and `s`.

<Hint>

The average of any function $f(x)$ is $\int f(x)\,p(x)\,dx$. Here $f(x) = e^{-x/s}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The product of two exponentials is one exponential, with the decay rate $\frac{1}{s} + \frac{1}{l}$. The delta function again picks out the value at $x = d$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-imp" label="$\left\langle e^{-x/s}\right\rangle =$" expr="0.8/(1 + l/s) + 0.2*exp(-d/s)"
  vars={{ l: [0.5, 2], d: [0.5, 3], s: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ l: "l", d: "d", s: "s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "0.8/(1 + l/s)", hint: "That leaves out the impurities on the grain boundary. Each of them contributes exp(−d/s), and they make up a fraction 0.2." },
    { expr: "0.8*l*s/(l + s) + 0.2*exp(-d/s)", hint: "The first term is a length here, and the average of exp(−x/s) is a pure number. Do not forget the factor 1/l in the density." },
    { expr: "0.8/(1 + s/l) + 0.2*exp(-d/s)", hint: "Check the limit of a very large skin depth s: then exp(−x/s) is 1 for every impurity and the average must be 1. This form gives 0.2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same two integrals as before, now with $e^{-x/s}$ in place of $x$.

<Step title="The two integrals">

The exponential part gives $\int_0^\infty e^{-x/s}e^{-x/l}\,dx = \left(\frac{1}{s} + \frac{1}{l}\right)^{-1}$. The delta function gives $e^{-d/s}$. So

<Formula label="Average contribution to the surface impedance" tex="\left\langle e^{-x/s}\right\rangle = \frac{0.8}{l}\left(\frac{1}{s} + \frac{1}{l}\right)^{-1} + 0.2\,e^{-d/s} = \frac{0.8}{1 + l/s} + 0.2\,e^{-d/s}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is a pure number between 0 and 1, as an average of $e^{-x/s}$ must be. For a very large skin depth every impurity counts fully and the average tends to $0.8 + 0.2 = 1$. MIT's solution points out two other limits. If $s$ is much smaller than $d$, the second term dies out: the impurities on the grain boundary lie too deep to matter. If $s$ is much smaller than $l$, the first term becomes about $0.8\,s/l$, which is small: only the impurities within about one skin depth of the surface contribute.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A peculiar probability density" points={20} mit="Problem 2">

A random variable $x$ can take any value from $-\infty$ to $\infty$, with the density

<Formula label="Lorentzian density" tex="p(x) = \frac{a}{b^2 + x^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

where $b$ is a positive constant. This shape is called a Lorentzian or a Cauchy density. It is a single symmetric peak with its maximum, $a/b^2$, at $x = 0$. In physics it describes many spectral lines that come from a resonance, with a frequency in the place of $x$.

From part (b) on, give your answers in terms of `b` (and `x` or `L` where needed), not $a$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Use normalization to find $a$ in terms of `b`.

<Hint>

The total area under a density is 1.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Substitute $x = b$ times a new variable. The integral of 1 over (1 plus the square of the variable), over the whole line, is $\pi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$a =$" expr="b/pi"
  vars={{ b: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ b: "b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "b/(2*pi)", hint: "With that value the area under p is one half. The integral runs over negative x as well." },
    { expr: "1/(pi*b)", hint: "Check the dimensions: p is 1 over a length and b² + x² is a length squared, so a must be a length." },
    { expr: "pi/b", hint: "Check the dimensions: p is 1 over a length and b² + x² is a length squared, so a must be a length." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Set the area under $p$ equal to 1.

<Step title="The area">

The density is even, so the area is twice the integral from 0 to $\infty$. With $x = b\,\xi$:

<Formula label="Normalization of the Lorentzian" tex="1 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(x)\,dx = \frac{2a}{b}\int_0^\infty\frac{d\xi}{1 + \xi^2} = \frac{2a}{b}\,\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi a}{b} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = \frac{b}{\pi}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$a$ is a length, so $p = a/(b^2 + x^2)$ is 1 over a length, as a density of $x$ must be. The maximum of the density is $a/b^2 = 1/(\pi b)$: a narrower peak is a higher one.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the cumulative function $P(x)$, in terms of `x` and `b`, and sketch it.

<Hint>

$P(x)$ is the integral of the density from $-\infty$ up to $x$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The antiderivative of $1/(b^2 + x^2)$ is an arctangent. Take care with its value at the lower limit, $-\infty$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-cum" label="$P(x) =$" expr="1/2 + atan(x/b)/pi"
  vars={{ x: [-3, 3], b: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ x: "x", b: "b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "atan(x/b)/pi", hint: "That tends to −1/2 as x goes to −∞, and a cumulative function starts at 0. The lower limit of the integral contributes too." },
    { expr: "1/2 + atan(x/b)", hint: "That tends to 1/2 + π/2 at large x, and a cumulative function ends at 1. The density holds the factor a = b/π." },
    { expr: "1/2 + atan(x)/pi", hint: "The argument of the arctangent must be a pure number: x must be divided by a length." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Integrate the density from $-\infty$ to $x$.

<Step title="The integral">

Call the variable of integration $x'$. Since $\frac{d}{dx'}\arctan(x'/b) = \frac{b}{b^2 + x'^2}$ and $\arctan(-\infty) = -\frac{\pi}{2}$:

<Formula label="Cumulative function of the Lorentzian" tex="P(x) = \frac{b}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{x}\frac{dx'}{b^2 + x'^2} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[\arctan\frac{x}{b} + \frac{\pi}{2}\right] = \frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\frac{x}{b} + \frac{1}{2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3" />

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-6, 6]} range={[0, 1.05]} xLabel="x / b" yLabel="P(x)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "0.5 + atan(x)/pi", label: "P(x)" },
    { expr: "1", dash: true }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 0, y: 0.5, label: "1/2 at x = 0" }]}
  caption="The cumulative function of the Lorentzian, computed from the formula above, with x in units of b. It passes through 1/2 at x = 0 and creeps towards 0 and 1 (dashed line) very slowly: at x = 2b it has reached only 0.85." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$P \to 0$ as $x \to -\infty$ and $P \to 1$ as $x \to \infty$. $P(0) = \frac{1}{2}$: half of the probability lies on each side of the centre, as the symmetry demands. The slow approach to 0 and 1 shows how much probability sits in the far wings: at $x = 2b$ the cumulative function is $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{\pi}\arctan 2 = 0.85$, so 15 percent of the probability still lies beyond.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Find the mean $\langle x\rangle$.

<Hint>

Look at the symmetry of $x\,p(x)$ before you integrate anything.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-mean" label="$\langle x\rangle =$" choices={["0", "b", "b/π", "a/b²"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The density is even and $x$ is odd.

<Step title="Symmetry">

$p(-x) = p(x)$, so the product $x\,p(x)$ is odd: its value at $-x$ cancels its value at $x$. MIT's solution concludes

<Formula label="Mean of the Lorentzian" tex="\langle x\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,p(x)\,dx = 0" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(c), p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="A remark on this integral (our addition)">

This mean is more delicate than it looks. At large $|x|$ the integrand $x\,p(x)$ falls off only as $\frac{b}{\pi x}$, so the integral over each half of the line alone grows without limit, like a logarithm. The value 0 comes out when the two limits go to infinity together, from $-L$ to $L$. With the limits $-L$ and $2L$ the same integral tends to $\frac{b}{\pi}\ln 2$ instead. The centre of the density is at $x = 0$ beyond any doubt (it is the median and the most probable value), but the mean is 0 only in this symmetric sense. Part (e) shows that the next average is worse.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Find the half-width at half-height: the distance from the centre at which the density has fallen to half of its maximum. Give it in terms of `b`.

<Hint>

Set $p(x)$ equal to half of $p(0)$. The constant $a$ drops out.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-half" label="half-width at half-height $=$" expr="b"
  vars={{ b: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ b: "b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*b", hint: "That is the full width at half-height, from one side of the peak to the other. The half-width is measured from the centre." },
    { expr: "b/2", hint: "At x = b/2 the density is a/(b² + b²/4), which is four fifths of its maximum, not one half." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2)*b", hint: "At x = √2 b the density is a/(3b²), one third of its maximum. Solve b² + x² = 2b²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The density is halved when its denominator is doubled.

<Step title="Half of the maximum">

$p(x) = \frac{1}{2}p(0)$ means $b^2 + x^2 = 2b^2$, so

<Formula label="Half-width at half-height of the Lorentzian" tex="x = \pm b" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(d), p. 3" />

The constant $b$ is the half-width at half-height.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-5, 5]} range={[0, 0.36]} xLabel="x / b" yLabel="p(x) · b"
  curves={[
    { expr: "1/(pi*(1 + x^2))", label: "p(x)" },
    { expr: "abs(x) <= 1 ? 1/(2*pi) : NaN", dash: true }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: -1, y: 0.1592, label: "x = −b" }, { x: 1, y: 0.1592, label: "x = b" }]}
  caption="The Lorentzian density computed from its formula with a = b/π, with x in units of b. Its maximum is 1/(πb), about 0.318/b. The dashed line at half of the maximum, about 0.159/b, meets the curve at x = −b and x = b." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $x = b$ the density is $a/(2b^2)$, half of $a/b^2$. For a spectral line, $b$ is the half-width of the line in frequency.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={5}>

<Answer id="p2-cut" label="$\int_{-L}^{L}x^2\,p(x)\,dx =$" expr="(2*b/pi)*(L - b*atan(L/b))"
  vars={{ b: [0.5, 2], L: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ b: "b", L: "L" }}
  mistakes={[

<Solution>

**The idea.** Far from the centre $p(x)$ falls off as $1/x^2$, so $x^2p(x)$ does not fall off at all.

</Step>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Bose-Einstein statistics" points={15} mit="Problem 6">

The electromagnetic field in a cavity can be taken apart into a countable, infinite set of modes, each with its own wavevector and direction of polarization (a Fourier series in three dimensions, as in 8.03). Quantum mechanics says that the energy of a mode of angular frequency $\omega$ comes in units of $\hbar\omega$. One unit is called a photon, and a mode with $n$ units is said to hold $n$ photons. Later the course derives that, in thermal equilibrium, the probability that a given mode holds $n$ photons is

<Formula label="Bose-Einstein density" tex="p(n) = (1 - a)\,a^n, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4" />

Here $a$ is a pure number between 0 and 1 that depends on $\omega$ and on the temperature. Physicists call this a Bose-Einstein density. Mathematicians, who meet it in other places too, call it a geometric density.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the mean number of photons $\langle n\rangle$, in terms of `a`. MIT's hint: differentiate the normalization sum, $\sum a^n$, with respect to $a$.

<Hint>

Normalization tells you the value of $\sum_n a^n$: it is a geometric series.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiating $a^n$ with respect to $a$ brings down a factor $n$, which is what the mean needs. Differentiate both sides of the geometric series, then multiply by $a$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-mean" label="$\langle n\rangle =$" expr="a/(1 - a)"
  vars={{ a: [0.1, 0.9] }}
  names={{ a: "a" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "a/(1 - a)^2", hint: "That is the sum of n aⁿ. The mean also holds the factor (1 − a) of p(n)." },
    { expr: "1/(1 - a)", hint: "That is the geometric series itself, the sum of aⁿ. The mean needs a factor n in each term, and the factor (1 − a) of p(n)." },
    { expr: "1/(1 - a)^2", hint: "That is the sum of n aⁿ⁻¹. Multiply by a to get the sum of n aⁿ, and by (1 − a) for the mean." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The mean is a sum with a factor $n$ in each term. Differentiating the geometric series with respect to $a$ produces that factor.

<Step title="The geometric series">

Normalization, $\sum_n(1 - a)a^n = 1$, is the statement that

<Formula label="Geometric series" tex="\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a^n = \frac{1}{1 - a}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(a), p. 8" />

</Step>

<Step title="Differentiating it">

Differentiate both sides with respect to $a$, then multiply by $a$:

<Formula label="Sum for the mean" tex="\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\,a^{n-1} = \frac{1}{(1 - a)^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\,a^n = \frac{a}{(1 - a)^2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(a), p. 8" />

</Step>

<Step title="The mean">

<Formula label="Mean number of photons in a mode" tex="\langle n\rangle = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\,p(n) = (1 - a)\,\frac{a}{(1 - a)^2} = \frac{a}{1 - a}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(a), p. 8" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result (the numbers are our addition)">

For small $a$ the mode is nearly always empty and $\langle n\rangle \approx a$, the probability of one photon. As $a \to 1$ the mean grows without limit. For $a = 0.75$ the mean is $0.75/0.25 = 3$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find the variance of $n$ and write it in terms of $\langle n\rangle$ alone: in the boxes type `N` for $\langle n\rangle$. MIT's hint: now differentiate the sum that gave the mean, $\sum n\,a^n$. For a given mean, the variance of the Bose-Einstein density is larger than that of a Poisson density by a factor. What is that factor?

<Hint>

Differentiating $\sum_n n\,a^n$ with respect to $a$ brings down a second factor $n$. That leads to $\langle n^2\rangle$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $\langle n^2\rangle$ with the combination $\frac{a}{1 - a}$, which is $\langle n\rangle$. For the last question, the lesson gives the variance of a Poisson density in terms of its mean.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-var" label="$\text{Var}(n) =$" expr="N*(1 + N)"
  vars={{ N: [0.5, 20] }}
  names={{ N: "\\langle n\\rangle" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*N^2 + N", hint: "That is the mean square. Subtract the square of the mean." },
    { expr: "N", hint: "That is the variance of a Poisson density. The Bose-Einstein density is wider." },
    { expr: "N^2", hint: "That is close only for a large mean. A term is missing: for a small mean the variance must tend to the mean itself." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p6-factor" label="factor $=$" expr="1 + N"
  vars={{ N: [0.5, 20] }}
  names={{ N: "\\langle n\\rangle" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "N*(1 + N)", hint: "That is the variance itself. Divide it by the variance of a Poisson density with the same mean." },
    { expr: "N", hint: "The ratio of the two variances can never be below 1: for a small mean the two densities have nearly the same variance." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same trick once more, on the sum that gave the mean.

<Step title="The sum for the mean square">

Differentiate $\sum_n n\,a^n = \frac{a}{(1 - a)^2}$ with respect to $a$, then multiply by $a$:

<Formula label="Sum for the mean square" tex="\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^2a^{n-1} = \frac{1}{(1 - a)^2} + \frac{2a}{(1 - a)^3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^2a^n = \frac{a}{(1 - a)^2} + \frac{2a^2}{(1 - a)^3}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(b), p. 9 (MIT's solutions label this part c)" />

(MIT's solutions print the label "c)" at this part, on p. 8. It is part (b): the real part (c) follows on p. 9.)

</Step>

<Step title="The mean square and the variance">

<Formula label="Mean square of the number of photons" tex="\langle n^2\rangle = (1 - a)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n^2a^n = \frac{a}{1 - a} + 2\left(\frac{a}{1 - a}\right)^2 = \langle n\rangle + 2\langle n\rangle^2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(b), p. 9" />

<Formula label="Variance of the Bose-Einstein density" tex="\text{Var}(n) = \langle n^2\rangle - \langle n\rangle^2 = \langle n\rangle^2 + \langle n\rangle = \langle n\rangle\left(1 + \langle n\rangle\right)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(b), p. 9" />

</Step>

<Step title="The comparison with a Poisson density">

A Poisson density has $\text{Var}(n) = \langle n\rangle$ (see the lesson). So for the same mean the Bose-Einstein variance is larger by the factor $1 + \langle n\rangle$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result (the numbers are our addition)">

For a small mean the factor tends to 1: a mode that is nearly always empty cannot tell the two densities apart. For $a = 0.75$, with the mean 3, the variance is $3 \times 4 = 12$ and the standard deviation is 3.46, against 1.73 for a Poisson density of the mean 3. The standard deviation of a Bose-Einstein density, $\sqrt{\langle n\rangle^2 + \langle n\rangle}$, is always larger than its mean: the number of photons in one mode of a cavity in thermal equilibrium is very uncertain.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Now treat the number of photons as a continuous variable $x$ with a density $p(x)$. Write $p(x)$ as an envelope function $f(x)$ times a train of delta functions of unit area at the integers $0, 1, 2, \dots$ Show that the envelope falls off exponentially, as $e^{-x/\phi}$. Express $\phi$ in terms of $\langle n\rangle$ (type `N`), and show that $\phi \to \langle n\rangle$ when $\langle n\rangle$ is large.

Two boxes are our additions: the envelope $f(x)$ in terms of `a` and `x`, and the value of $\phi$ for $\langle n\rangle = 10$ as a decimal number.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put a delta function of weight $p(n)$ at each integer. An exponential that passes through all the weights can then be taken out as a common envelope.

<Step title="A train of delta functions">

<Formula label="Bose-Einstein density as a train of delta functions" tex="p(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(1 - a)\,a^n\,\delta(x - n) = f(x)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\delta(x - n)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(c), p. 9" />

The second form is right for any function $f$ with $f(n) = (1 - a)a^n$ at every integer $n \ge 0$, because next to $\delta(x - n)$ only the value at $x = n$ counts.

</Step>

<Step title="The envelope">

Try the exponential $f(x) = (1 - a)\,e^{-x/\phi}$. At $x = n$ it is $(1 - a)\left(e^{-1/\phi}\right)^n$, which equals $(1 - a)a^n$ for every $n$ when

<Formula label="Envelope of the Bose-Einstein density" tex="e^{-1/\phi} = a, \qquad f(x) = (1 - a)\,e^{-x/\phi} = (1 - a)\,a^x" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(c), p. 9" />

So the envelope falls off exponentially, with the decay length $\phi = -1/\ln a$.

</Step>

<Step title="In terms of the mean">

Part (a) turned around gives $a = \frac{\langle n\rangle}{1 + \langle n\rangle}$. Then

<Formula label="Decay length of the envelope" tex="\frac{1}{\phi} = -\ln a = \ln\frac{1 + \langle n\rangle}{\langle n\rangle} = \ln\left(1 + \frac{1}{\langle n\rangle}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(c), p. 9" />

</Step>

<Step title="A large mean">

For a small number $\epsilon$, $\ln(1 + \epsilon) = \epsilon - \frac{1}{2}\epsilon^2 + \dots$ With $\epsilon = 1/\langle n\rangle$ and $\langle n\rangle$ large, the first term is enough:

<Formula label="Decay length for a large mean" tex="\frac{1}{\phi} \to \frac{1}{\langle n\rangle}, \qquad \phi \to \langle n\rangle" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(c), p. 10" />

Keeping the second term as well (our addition) gives $\phi \approx \langle n\rangle + \frac{1}{2}$. For $\langle n\rangle = 10$ the exact value is $1/\ln 1.1 = 10.49$.

</Step>
