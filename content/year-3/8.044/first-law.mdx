Thermodynamics is built on a few facts and a lot of careful mathematics. This lecture shows how measured derivatives give an equation of state, defines the internal energy and heat, states the first law, and applies it to heat capacities and to the ideal gas, including its adiabatic paths (slides 1–24). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides; there are no lecture notes for it. This page follows the slides in its own words, with its own worked example; the slides' sketches are not reproduced.)

## Response functions

Thermodynamics focuses on state functions such as $P$, $V$, $M$ and $S$, but experiments usually measure **response functions**, derivatives of one with respect to another (slide 1):

<Formula label="Response functions" tex="\alpha \equiv \frac1V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P, \qquad \kappa_T \equiv -\frac1V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T, \qquad \chi_T \equiv \left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial H}\right)_T, \qquad \kappa_S \equiv -\frac1V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_{\text{adiabatic}}" cite="mit" at="slide 1" />

the expansion coefficient, the isothermal compressibility, the magnetic susceptibility and the adiabatic compressibility.

**Rebuilding an equation of state** (slides 2–4). Suppose a non-ideal gas is known to approach an ideal gas at large $T$ and $V$, and two of its derivatives are measured:

<Formula label="Measured derivatives of a non-ideal gas" tex="\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V = \frac{Nk}{V - Nb}, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_T = -\frac{NkT}{(V - Nb)^2} + \frac{2aN^2}{V^3}" cite="mit" at="slide 2" />

Integrating the first at fixed $V$ gives $P = \frac{NkT}{V - Nb} + f(V)$; differentiating with respect to $V$ and comparing with the second gives $f'(V) = 2aN^2/V^3$, so $f = -aN^2/V^2 + c$, and the ideal-gas limit sets $c = 0$ (slides 3–4):

<Formula label="Reconstructed equation of state" tex="P = \frac{NkT}{V - Nb} - \frac{aN^2}{V^2}" cite="mit" at="slide 4" />

the van der Waals equation (the name, and the reading of $b$ as the volume each molecule occupies and of $a$ as the attraction between molecules, are the page's additions).

## Internal energy, heat and the first law

**Internal energy** (slides 5–6). The observational fact: if an isolated system is taken from one state to another by doing work $W$ on it through adiabatic walls, the final state, and the work required, do not depend on how the work is applied or which adiabatic path is followed. So there is a state function $U$ with

<Formula label="Internal energy from adiabatic work" tex="\Delta U = W_{\text{adiabatic}}" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

a function of the independent variables, for a simple fluid $U(T, V)$, $U(T, P)$ or $U(P, V)$.

**Heat** (slide 7). On a path that is not adiabatic, $dU \ne \delta W$; the difference is the **heat** added to the system, which behaves in every way as heat is expected to (the slides write these with a barred d; this page writes $\delta Q$ and $\delta W$ for small amounts that are not changes of any state function):

<Formula label="Heat" tex="\delta Q \equiv dU - \delta W" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

> **First law of thermodynamics** (slide 8): $dU = \delta Q + \delta W$. $U$ is a state function; heat is a flow of energy; energy is conserved.

With no work, heat flows from the higher temperature to the lower, which orders temperatures (slide 9).

## Heat capacities of a fluid

For a gas, liquid or simple solid at fixed $N$, two of $P$, $V$, $T$, $U$ are independent. The heat capacities are $C_V \equiv (\delta Q/dT)_V$ and $C_P \equiv (\delta Q/dT)_P$ (slide 10). With $\delta Q = dU + P\,dV$ and $dU = (\partial U/\partial T)_VdT + (\partial U/\partial V)_TdV$ (slides 11–12):

<Formula label="Heat capacities" tex="C_V = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_V, \qquad C_P - C_V = \left[\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T + P\right]\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P" cite="mit" at="slides 12–13" />

and $C_P$ is not $(\partial U/\partial T)_P$; the second law will simplify the difference further (slide 13).

**Paths are experiments** (slides 14–15). Holding a variable fixed is a physical arrangement: a sealed container keeps $V$ constant, a floating piston keeps $P$ constant, a bath keeps $T$ constant, insulation keeps $Q = 0$. Sometimes $Q = 0$ comes from time instead: in a sound wave, compressions and rarefactions alternate too fast for heat to flow between them, so the speed of sound involves the adiabatic compressibility $\kappa_S$ (slide 15).

## The ideal gas

**$U$ depends only on $T$** (slides 16–17). In a free expansion (Joule's experiment, a name the page adds), a gas in a container placed in a bath expands into vacuum: no work is done and, the bath showing no change of temperature, no heat flows, so $\Delta U = 0$ while $V$ changes. Hence $(\partial U/\partial V)_T = 0$, a result then used for quasistatic changes too. Physics explains it (no interactions, only single-particle energies), and thermodynamics will later derive it from the second law and $PV = NkT$. Then (slides 18–19)

<Formula label="Ideal gas energy and heat capacities" tex="U = \int_0^TC_V(T')\,dT', \qquad C_P - C_V = P\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = Nk" cite="mit" at="slides 18–19" />

For a monatomic gas, $C_V = \frac32Nk$ is observed, so, setting the integration constant to zero, $U = \frac32NkT$, $C_P = \frac52Nk$ and $\gamma \equiv C_P/C_V = \frac53$ (slides 18–19).

**Adiabatic paths** (slides 20–22). Setting $\delta Q = C_VdT + \left[(\partial U/\partial V)_T + P\right]dV = 0$ gives the slope of an adiabat:

<Formula label="Slope of an adiabat" tex="\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_{Q=0} = -\frac{C_P - C_V}{C_V\,V\alpha} = -\frac{\gamma - 1}{V\alpha}" cite="mit" at="slide 20" />

For an ideal gas $\alpha = 1/T$, so $dT/T = -(\gamma - 1)dV/V$ and (slides 21–22)

<Formula label="Adiabats of an ideal gas" tex="TV^{\gamma - 1} = \text{constant}, \qquad PV^\gamma = \text{constant}" cite="mit" at="slides 21–22" />

Adiabats are steeper than isotherms: $dP/dV = -\gamma P/V$ against $-P/V$.

<Plot variable="V" domain={[0.4, 2]} range={[0, 4]} xLabel="V / V₀" yLabel="P / P₀"

**Two expansions** (slide 23). A gas rushing through a ruptured diaphragm into vacuum is adiabatic but not quasistatic: $W = 0$, $\Delta U = 0$, and an ideal gas's temperature does not change. Expanded slowly against a piston, it does negative work, $\Delta U < 0$, and it cools along the adiabat.

In short (slide 24): a few facts, the first law, the forms of $\delta W$ and the mathematics of state functions give relations between thermodynamic quantities, a general $dU$ and the adiabatic constraint; adding an equation of state and a heat capacity gives $U$ and the adiabatic path.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The universe expands adiabatically">
The expanding universe has no outside to exchange heat with, so its contents expand adiabatically. A monatomic gas cools as $T \propto V^{-2/3}$, that is as $1/a^2$ for a volume growing as $a^3$; black-body radiation follows $PV^{4/3} = \text{constant}$ (an adiabatic exponent of $\frac43$, though not a ratio of heat capacities) and cools as $V^{-1/3}$, that is as $1/a$. So once the gas of atoms stopped exchanging energy with the photons, which happened somewhat after the photons decoupled because leftover free electrons kept the two in contact, it cooled faster than the cosmic microwave background (this comparison is the page's own). [8.286 follows the temperature through the early universe](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling).
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="A mole of helium">
<Problem>
One mole of helium ($Nk = R = 8.314$ J/K) is a monatomic ideal gas at 300 K. (a) Find $C_V$, $C_P$ and $U$. (b) It is compressed adiabatically to half its volume. Find the final temperature and the pressure ratio, and the work done on it. (c) Find its expansion coefficient $\alpha$ at 300 K.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Heat capacities and energy">
$C_V = \frac32R \approx 12.5$ J/K, $C_P = \frac52R \approx 20.8$ J/K, and $U = \frac32RT \approx 3.74$ kJ.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Adiabatic compression">
$T_2 = 300 \times 2^{2/3} \approx 476$ K and $P_2/P_1 = 2^{5/3} \approx 3.17$. With no heat, the work done equals the rise in $U$: $W = C_V(T_2 - T_1) \approx 12.47 \times 176.2 \approx 2.20$ kJ.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Expansion coefficient">
$\alpha = \frac1V\frac{Nk}{P} = \frac1T \approx 3.33 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Response functions ($\alpha$, $\kappa_T$, $\chi_T$, $\kappa_S$) are what experiments measure; two of them can rebuild an equation of state, such as the van der Waals equation.
- Adiabatic work defines the internal energy; heat is $\delta Q = dU - \delta W$; first law: $dU = \delta Q + \delta W$.
- $C_V = (\partial U/\partial T)_V$ and $C_P - C_V = [(\partial U/\partial V)_T + P](\partial V/\partial T)_P$; for an ideal gas $U = U(T)$ and $C_P - C_V = Nk$.
- Ideal-gas adiabats: $TV^{\gamma - 1}$ and $PV^\gamma$ constant, $\gamma = \frac53$ for a monatomic gas; free expansion leaves $T$ unchanged, slow adiabatic expansion cools.
