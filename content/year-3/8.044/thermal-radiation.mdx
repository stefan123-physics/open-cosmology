Radiation in a closed, heated cavity comes to equilibrium with the walls. This lecture finds its spectrum in two ways: first with thermodynamics, which fixes how its energy depends on temperature but not the constant, and then with statistical mechanics, which supplies everything, and shows why classical physics fails (slides 1–26). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Thermal radiation

Radiation is a superposition of plane waves, each with $\vec E = \vec E_0e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r - \omega t)}$, $\vec B_0 = \hat k \times \vec E_0/c$ and $\omega = c|\vec k|$ (slide 1). Averaged over time, a plane wave has energy density $u = \frac12\epsilon_0|\vec E_0|^2$, carries energy flux $cu$ along $\hat k$, and presses on a perpendicular absorber with pressure $u$ (slide 2). Thermal radiation has a continuous spread of frequencies, with spectral energy density $u(\omega, T)$ (this page uses the angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi\nu$ throughout, where slides 2–15 use $\nu$) that peaks near $h\nu \approx 3kT$ (the table on slide 3 uses $h\nu = 2.82kT$); since $h/k \approx 5 \times 10^{-11}$ K s, each part of the spectrum has its own temperature (slide 3): microwaves at $10^{10}$ Hz go with about 0.17 K (the slide marks this region as where the cosmic background radiation lies), infrared at $10^{13}$ Hz with about 170 K (room-temperature objects), visible light with the Sun's surface, X-rays with about $10^7$ K.

## Properties from equilibrium alone

Two quantities describe a surface (slide 4): its **absorptivity** $\alpha(\omega, T)$, the fraction of incident energy it absorbs, and its **emissive power** $e(\omega, T)$, the energy it emits per unit area and time.

Connect two cavities at the same temperature through a filter that passes only some frequencies or one polarization (slide 5). If the two spectra differed in any way, energy would flow from one to the other at equal temperatures, which the second law forbids. Hence (slide 6) $u(\omega, T)$ is **independent of the cavity's shape and wall material**, **isotropic** and **unpolarized**.

**Energy flux onto a surface** (slides 7–8). Radiation reaching an area $dA$ from direction $\theta$ in time $\Delta t$ comes from a slanted cylinder of volume $\cos\theta\,c\,\Delta t$ per unit of $dA$. Directions are weighted evenly over the whole sphere, and only the half heading towards $dA$ contributes; the $\cos\theta$ then makes the average $\frac14$:

<Formula label="Energy flux onto a surface" tex="\text{energy flux onto } dA = \frac14\,c\,u(\omega, T)" cite="mit" at="slide 8" />

**Pressure** (slides 9–10). A plane wave has momentum density $(u/c)\hat k$, and reflection reverses its normal component, transferring twice the incoming momentum. Taking the normal component brings a second factor of $\cos\theta$, which on its own would give $\frac16$; the factor 2 from reflection makes it $\frac13$:

<Formula label="Radiation pressure" tex="P(T) = \frac13\int_0^\infty u(\omega, T)\,d\omega" cite="mit" at="slide 10" />

**Kirchhoff's law** (slide 11). An object inside the cavity, in equilibrium, must emit as much as it absorbs at each frequency (detailed balance): $e\,dA = \alpha\left(\frac14cu\right)dA$, so

<Formula label="Kirchhoff's law" tex="\frac{e(\omega, T)}{\alpha(\omega, T)} = \frac14\,c\,u(\omega, T)" cite="mit" at="slide 11" />

the same for every material. A **black body** has $\alpha = 1$, so its emission is $\frac14cu(\omega, T)$: measuring the light from a small hole in an oven measures $u(\omega, T)$ (slide 12).

## Thermodynamics: the T⁴ law

Define the total energy density $u(T) = \int_0^\infty u(\omega, T)\,d\omega$; then $E(T, V) = u(T)V$ and $P = \frac13u(T)$ (slide 13). That is enough. The energy equation from Lecture 10, $(\partial E/\partial V)_T = T(\partial P/\partial T)_V - P$, gives (slide 14)

<Formula label="Thermodynamic derivation" tex="u(T) = \frac13Tu'(T) - \frac13u(T) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u'(T) = \frac{4u(T)}{T} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u(T) = AT^4" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

A black body therefore emits (slide 15)

<Formula label="Stefan–Boltzmann law" tex="e(T) = \frac14cu(T) = \frac14AcT^4 \equiv \sigma T^4, \qquad \sigma = 56.7 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\,\text{K}^{-4}" cite="mit" at="slide 15" />

Thermodynamics cannot give the constant $A$, nor the spectrum.

## Statistical mechanics: modes as oscillators

Take one standing-wave mode of a conducting cavity, $E_x = f(t)\sin(n\pi z/L)$ (slide 16; the slides call the amplitude $E(t)$, which this page renames $f(t)$ to keep $E$ for the energy). Its energy, integrated over the cavity, is a sum of two squares, one in the field amplitude and one in its rate of change (slide 17): **each mode is a harmonic oscillator**.

**Counting modes** (slides 18–20). In a cubic cavity of side $L$ the modes have $\omega^2 = (\pi c/L)^2(n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2)$ with $n_i = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ and two polarizations. The modes below $\omega$ fill one octant of a sphere of radius $R = L\omega/\pi c$ in $n$-space, so their number is $2 \times \frac18 \times \frac43\pi R^3 = L^3\omega^3/3\pi^2c^3$, and the **density of modes**, the number per unit $\omega$, is

<Formula label="Density of modes" tex="D(\omega) = \frac{V\omega^2}{\pi^2c^3}" cite="mit" at="slide 20" />

**Classically** (slide 21) each oscillator has mean energy $kT$, so $u(\omega, T) = \omega^2kT/\pi^2c^3$: it agrees with experiment at low frequency but grows without limit, and $\int_0^\infty u\,d\omega$ is infinite.

**Quantum mechanically** (slide 22) an oscillator has mean energy $\hbar\omega/(e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1) + \hbar\omega/2$, as in Lecture 14. Dropping the zero-point term,

<Formula label="Planck spectrum" tex="u(\omega, T) = \frac{\langle E(\omega)\rangle D(\omega)}{V} = \frac{\hbar\omega^3}{\pi^2c^3}\,\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/kT} - 1}" cite="mit" at="slide 22" />

The high-frequency modes, with $\hbar\omega \gg kT$, are frozen out, which removes the divergence. Setting $du/d\omega = 0$ puts the maximum at $\hbar\omega/kT \approx 2.82$.

**The partition function** (slides 23–25). The modes are independent, so $Z$ is the product of one factor $Z_i = e^{-\hbar\omega_i/2kT}(1 - e^{-\hbar\omega_i/kT})^{-1}$ per mode $i$, the first part coming from the zero-point energy. Then $F = -kT\sum\ln Z_i$; turning the sum into $\int D(\omega)\,d\omega$, dropping the zero-point part and using $\int_0^\infty x^2\ln(1 - e^{-x})\,dx = -\pi^4/45$:

<Formula label="Free energy of black-body radiation" tex="F(V, T) = \frac{kTV}{\pi^2c^3}\int_0^\infty\omega^2\ln\left(1 - e^{-\hbar\omega/kT}\right)d\omega = -\frac{\pi^2}{45}\frac{(kT)^4}{(\hbar c)^3}V" cite="mit" at="slide 24" />

<Formula label="Pressure, entropy and energy" tex="P = \frac{\pi^2}{45}\frac{(kT)^4}{(\hbar c)^3}, \qquad S = \frac{4\pi^2}{45}\frac{k^4T^3}{(\hbar c)^3}V, \qquad E = F + TS = \frac{\pi^2}{15}\frac{(kT)^4}{(\hbar c)^3}V" cite="mit" at="slide 25" />

So $P = \frac13E/V = \frac13u(T)$, independent of $V$, as thermodynamics required, and now $A = \pi^2k^4/15(\hbar c)^3$. Since $S \propto T^3V$, an adiabatic change keeps $T^3V$ constant: $T/T_0 = (V/V_0)^{-1/3}$ (slide 26).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The universe as a cavity">
The cosmic microwave background is black-body radiation filling the whole universe, now at about $T = 2.725$ K. As space expands, the radiation expands adiabatically, so $T^3V$ stays constant and $T \propto 1/a$, the scale factor: the same conserved-entropy argument used in 8.286 (the entropy density, proportional to $T^3$, thins out as $1/a^3$). The lecture also shows the COBE satellite's measurement of the background spectrum, which follows a black-body curve almost perfectly. Its energy density $u = \frac{\pi^2}{15}(kT)^4/(\hbar c)^3$ is about $4.17 \times 10^{-14}$ J/m³ today, and the $T^4$ law, together with $T \propto 1/a$, makes the radiation energy density grow as $1/a^4$ going back in time, faster than matter's $1/a^3$, so radiation dominated the early universe.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Cavity radiation: $u(\omega, T)$ independent of walls, isotropic, unpolarized; flux onto a surface $\frac14cu$; pressure $\frac13u$; Kirchhoff: $e/\alpha = \frac14cu$.
- Thermodynamics with $E = uV$, $P = \frac13u$ gives $u = AT^4$ and the Stefan–Boltzmann law $e = \sigma T^4$.
- Modes are oscillators, $D(\omega) = V\omega^2/\pi^2c^3$; classical equipartition diverges, quantum oscillators give Planck's spectrum, peaking at $\hbar\omega \approx 2.82kT$.
- From $Z$: $F = -\frac{\pi^2}{45}\frac{(kT)^4}{(\hbar c)^3}V$, $E = -3F$, $P = E/3V$, $S \propto T^3V$; adiabats keep $T^3V$ constant.
