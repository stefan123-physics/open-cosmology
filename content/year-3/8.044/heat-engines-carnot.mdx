This lecture has two parts: a full worked example of the Maxwell relations, an elastic rod (and the curious thermodynamics of rubber), and then **heat engines**, where the second law sets a hard limit on efficiency (slides 1–15, numbered as printed, with 11a and 11b). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Example: an elastic rod

A rod (or band) of length $L$ under tension $F$, where work on it is $+F\,dL$ (slide 1). (The slides write the tension as a script F and the heat-capacity constant as $e$; this page uses $F$ and $A$.) Suppose experiments give

<Formula label="Elastic rod: tension and heat capacity" tex="F = (a + bT)(L - L_0), \qquad C_L = AT^3" cite="mit" at="slide 1" />

with $a + bT > 0$ for stability (the rod must pull back when stretched). Find $E(T, L)$ and $S(T, L)$.

**The energy** (slides 1–3). Expanding $dS$ in $T$ and $L$, $dE = T\,dS + F\,dL = T(\partial S/\partial T)_L\,dT + [T(\partial S/\partial L)_T + F]\,dL$, and the first coefficient is $C_L$. For the second, use the Maxwell relation from $E - TS$, whose differential is $-S\,dT + F\,dL$ (slide 2):

<Formula label="Maxwell relation for the rod" tex="-\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial L}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial T}\right)_L = b(L - L_0)" cite="mit" at="slide 2" />

so $T(\partial S/\partial L)_T + F = -bT(L - L_0) + (a + bT)(L - L_0) = a(L - L_0)$, and $dE = AT^3\,dT + a(L - L_0)\,dL$. Integrating the first term and matching the second (slide 3),

<Formula label="Energy of the rod" tex="E(T, L) = \frac A4T^4 + \frac a2(L - L_0)^2 + c_1" cite="mit" at="slide 3" />

**The entropy** (slide 4). $(\partial S/\partial T)_L = C_L/T = AT^2$ and $(\partial S/\partial L)_T = -b(L - L_0)$, so

<Formula label="Entropy of the rod" tex="S(T, L) = \frac A3T^3 - \frac b2(L - L_0)^2 + c_2" cite="mit" at="slide 4" />

(A related homework problem, slide 5, models a chain of $N$ links of length $l$ that point either way along the force, giving $L = Nl\tanh(lF/kT)$; for small extensions its expansion coefficient at fixed tension is $-1/T$.)

**Rubber** (slides 6–7). Setting $dF = (a + bT)\,dL + b(L - L_0)\,dT = 0$ gives the expansion coefficient at fixed tension:

<Formula label="Thermal expansion at fixed tension" tex="\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial T}\right)_F = -\frac{b(L - L_0)}{a + bT}" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

A stretched rubber band ($L > L_0$) **contracts when heated**, a negative expansion coefficient; with the denominator positive for stability, this means $b > 0$. Then the entropy above falls as the band is stretched. A rapid stretch has no time to exchange heat, so $S$ stays about constant, and the larger $(L - L_0)^2$ must be paid for by a larger $T^3$: **a rubber band warms when stretched quickly** (slide 7), as you can feel by stretching one against your lip.

## Heat engines

A **heat engine** takes a substance around a closed cycle; heat goes in and comes out, and work is taken out (slide 8). Its **efficiency** is

<Formula label="Efficiency" tex="\eta \equiv \frac{\text{work out}}{\text{heat in}}" cite="mit" at="slide 8" />

Round a closed cycle the state function $U$ returns to its start, so $\Delta U = Q + W = 0$ and $Q = -W$ (slide 9). Heat $|Q_H|$ comes in on part of the cycle (from a hot reservoir) and $|Q_C|$ leaves on the rest (to a cold one), so in the most general case (slide 10)

<Formula label="Efficiency of any cycle" tex="W_{\text{out}} = -W = Q = |Q_H| - |Q_C|, \qquad \eta = \frac{W_{\text{out}}}{|Q_H|} = 1 - \frac{|Q_C|}{|Q_H|}" cite="mit" at="slide 10" />

## The Carnot cycle

A very special case (slides 10–11b): **any** working substance, taken around two isotherms (at $T_H$ and $T_C$) joined by two adiabats. In the $T$–$S$ plane it is a rectangle. For an ideal gas in the $P$–$V$ plane it looks like the figure below, drawn as on slide 11b with $T_H = 1.5T_C$ and an entropy step of $\frac32Nk\ln 2$ between the adiabats.

**Its efficiency** (slide 12). Use the second law, $\delta Q \le T\,dS$. Let $\Delta S$ be the entropy the substance gains along the hot isotherm. Then $|Q_H| \le T_H\Delta S$. The reversible adiabats leave $S$ unchanged and $S$ returns to its start around the cycle, so the cold isotherm takes away the same $\Delta S$: there $-|Q_C| \le -T_C\Delta S$, that is, $|Q_C| \ge T_C\Delta S$. (The slides label the ends of the isotherms as points 1, 1′, 2, 2′ on slides 11a and 11b and write these as integrals of $dS$ between them.) Dividing,

<Formula label="Carnot efficiency" tex="\frac{|Q_C|}{|Q_H|} \ge \frac{T_C}{T_H}, \qquad \eta = 1 - \frac{|Q_C|}{|Q_H|} \le 1 - \frac{T_C}{T_H}" cite="mit" at="slide 12" />

with equality for a reversible (quasi-static) cycle.

## Any cycle

For an arbitrary cycle (slides 13–14), $\delta Q \le T\,dS$ still holds for each element. On the part of the path from 1 to 2 where heat comes in,

<Formula label="Heat in, bounded" tex="|Q_H| = \int_1^2\delta Q \le \int_1^2 T\,dS \le T_{\text{max}}\int_1^2 dS" cite="mit" at="slide 13" />

and on the return, where both sides are negative, $|Q_C| \ge T_{\text{min}}\left|\int_2^1 dS\right| = T_{\text{min}}\int_1^2 dS$, since $\oint dS = 0$. Hence

<Formula label="Efficiency of any engine" tex="\frac{|Q_C|}{|Q_H|} \ge \frac{T_{\text{min}}}{T_{\text{max}}}, \qquad \eta \le 1 - \frac{T_{\text{min}}}{T_{\text{max}}}" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

No engine working between those extremes beats a Carnot engine between them.

**In pure thermodynamics** (slide 15), the logic runs the other way: without statistical mechanics, the Carnot cycle is used to *define* the temperature scale, through $\eta = 1 - |Q_C|/|Q_H| \equiv 1 - T_C/T_H$, and to *define* the entropy, since $\oint\delta Q/T \le 0$ makes $\delta Q/T$, for reversible changes, an exact differential $dS$.

<WorkedExample title="A power station and a gas engine">
<Problem>
(a) A power station's steam reaches 800 K and its condenser runs at 300 K. What is the largest possible efficiency? (b) The station actually delivers 1.0 GW of work at 40% efficiency. How much heat does it take in and reject each second? (c) By how much does the entropy of the two reservoirs grow each second? (d) One mole of monatomic ideal gas runs the Carnot cycle in the figure with $T_C = 300$ K and $T_H = 450$ K. Find the heat in and the work out per cycle ($R = 8.314$ J/K per mole).
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Carnot limit">
$1 - 300/800 = 0.625$, so at most 62.5%.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Heat flows">
$|Q_H| = 1.0/0.40 = 2.5$ GW in, and $|Q_C| = 2.5 - 1.0 = 1.5$ GW out.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Entropy">
The hot reservoir loses $2.5 \times 10^9/800 = 3.125 \times 10^6$ W/K and the cold gains $1.5 \times 10^9/300 = 5.0 \times 10^6$ W/K: a net growth of about $1.875 \times 10^6$ W/K. A reversible engine would make this zero.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Gas engine">
$\Delta S = \frac32R\ln 2 \approx 8.644$ J/K, so $|Q_H| = T_H\Delta S \approx 3890$ J and $W_{\text{out}} = (T_H - T_C)\Delta S \approx 1297$ J: an efficiency of exactly $1/3 = 1 - 300/450$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Elastic rod: from $F = (a + bT)(L - L_0)$, $C_L = AT^3$ and the Maxwell relation $-(\partial S/\partial L)_T = (\partial F/\partial T)_L$ follow $E(T, L)$ and $S(T, L)$; rubber ($b > 0$) contracts when heated and warms when stretched quickly.
- Heat engine: closed cycle, $W_{\text{out}} = |Q_H| - |Q_C|$, $\eta = 1 - |Q_C|/|Q_H|$.
- With $\delta Q \le T\,dS$: Carnot, $\eta \le 1 - T_C/T_H$; any cycle, $\eta \le 1 - T_{\text{min}}/T_{\text{max}}$.
- In pure thermodynamics the Carnot cycle defines temperature and entropy.
