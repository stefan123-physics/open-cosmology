So far $N$ has been fixed. But particles can change phase (gas to liquid to solid, paramagnet to ferromagnet, normal fluid to superfluid), take part in chemical reactions, or move between places (gas sticking to a surface, charge carriers in a semiconductor). This lecture introduces the variable that governs those changes, the **chemical potential**, and the ensemble built on it (slides 1–27). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. Its slides 22–24 repeat slides 16–18 of Lecture 17.)

## The chemical potential

With particle number as a variable, the combined first and second laws gain a term (slide 1):

<Formula label="First and second laws with variable N" tex="dU = T\,dS - P\,dV + \mu\,dN" cite="mit" at="slide 1" />

So $\mu = (\partial U/\partial N)_{S,V}$, at fixed **entropy**, not fixed temperature, a frequent source of misunderstanding. With $F = U - TS$, $dF = -S\,dT - P\,dV + \mu\,dN$, so (slide 2)

<Formula label="Chemical potential from the free energy" tex="\mu = \left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial N}\right)_{T,V}" cite="mit" at="slide 2" />

**Which way particles flow** (slides 3–4). Let two systems exchange energy and particles but not volume. Since $dS = dU/T + (P/T)dV - (\mu/T)dN$ for each, and $dU_1 = -dU_2$, $dN_1 = -dN_2$,

<Formula label="Entropy change on exchange" tex="dS = \left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)dU_2 + \left(\frac{\mu_1}{T_1} - \frac{\mu_2}{T_2}\right)dN_2 \ge 0" cite="mit" at="slide 3" />

If $T_1 > T_2$, energy flows to system 2. At equal temperatures, if $\mu_1 > \mu_2$, particles flow to system 2. Only with $T_1 = T_2$ **and** $\mu_1 = \mu_2$ does nothing flow: that is equilibrium.

## Example: adsorption on a surface

A gas in a container is in contact with a surface where an atom can stick, with binding energy $\epsilon_0$, and move freely as a two-dimensional gas (slide 5). Measure energies from rest in the bulk.

**The bulk gas** (slide 6). With $Z_1 = V/\lambda^3$, where $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mkT}$ is the thermal de Broglie wavelength, and $Z = Z_1^N/N!$:

<Formula label="Chemical potential of an ideal gas" tex="\mu = \left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial N}\right)_{V,T} = -kT\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3} = kT\ln\left(n\lambda^3\right)" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

with $n = N/V$ the number density. For a classical gas $n\lambda^3 \ll 1$, so $\mu$ is large and negative.

**The surface gas** (slide 7). On area $A$, with $N$ atoms on the area, each atom's partition function carries a factor $e^{\epsilon_0/kT}$ from the binding and only two momentum integrals: $Z_1 = e^{\epsilon_0/kT}A/\lambda^2$, and

<Formula label="Chemical potential of the surface gas" tex="\mu = -\epsilon_0 + kT\ln\left(\sigma\lambda^2\right)" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

with $\sigma = N/A$ the surface density.

**Equilibrium** (slides 8–9) requires $\mu_{\text{surface}} = \mu_{\text{bulk}}$:

<Formula label="Adsorbed density" tex="\sigma = \lambda\,n\,e^{\epsilon_0/kT} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi mkT}}\,e^{\epsilon_0/kT}\,n" cite="mit" at="slides 8–9" />

The surface density is proportional to the bulk density, and grows steeply as the temperature falls.

## The grand canonical ensemble

The three ensembles (slide 10): microcanonical ($E$ and $N$ fixed), canonical ($N$ fixed, $T$ given), and **grand canonical** ($T$ and $\mu$ given, both $E$ and $N$ free), for when the particle number is not fixed.

**Derivation** (slides 11–17), as for the canonical ensemble. A subsystem 1 exchanges both energy and particles with a much larger remainder 2; together they are isolated and microcanonical. The probability of finding the subsystem in a particular state $\{p_1, q_1\}$ with $N_1$ particles is the share of phase space left to the remainder, $\Omega_2(E - E_1, N - N_1)/\Omega(E, N)$. Expanding the remainder's entropy to first order in both $E_1$ and $N_1$, with $\partial S_2/\partial E_2 = 1/T$ and $\partial S_2/\partial N_2 = -\mu/T$ (slide 14):

<Formula label="Log of the probability" tex="k\ln p(\{p_1, q_1, N_1\}) = -\frac{H_1}{T} + \frac{\mu N_1}{T} + S_2(E, N) - S(E, N)" cite="mit" at="slide 15" />

with $H_1$ the subsystem's Hamiltonian at that state. Using the additivity of entropy, the state-independent part is $(F_1 - \mu\langle N_1\rangle)/T$ (slide 16). Dropping subscripts (slides 17–18), the probability density and its normalization are

<Formula label="Grand canonical density" tex="p(\{p, q\}, N) = \frac{e^{(\mu N - H)/kT}}{\Xi}, \qquad \Xi(T, V, \mu) = \sum_{N=1}^{\infty}e^{\mu N/kT}Z(T, V, N) = e^{-(F - \mu\langle N\rangle)/kT}" cite="mit" at="slide 18" />

where $\Xi$ is the **grand partition function** (a script Z on the slides; the sum over $N$ starts at 1 there, though the empty state $N = 0$, contributing 1, is normally included, as it is automatically in the sum over occupation numbers below). Differentiating with respect to $\mu$ brings down $N$ and averages it (slide 19):

<Formula label="Mean particle number" tex="\langle N\rangle = kT\left(\frac{\partial\ln\Xi}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T,V}" cite="mit" at="slide 19" />

**The grand potential** (slides 20–21). Define $\Phi_G \equiv F - \mu\langle N\rangle = U - TS - \mu\langle N\rangle$ (as on the slides); comparing with slide 18, $\Phi_G = -kT\ln\Xi$. (Most slides write the mean particle number as $\bar N$; this page writes $\langle N\rangle$, as slides 19 and 27 do.) Its differential and its derivatives connect the ensemble to thermodynamics:

<Formula label="Grand potential" tex="d\Phi_G = -S\,dT - P\,dV - \langle N\rangle\,d\mu, \qquad S = -\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_G}{\partial T}\right)_{V,\mu}, \qquad P = -\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_G}{\partial V}\right)_{T,\mu}, \qquad \langle N\rangle = -\left(\frac{\partial\Phi_G}{\partial\mu}\right)_{T,V}" cite="mit" at="slides 20–21" />

## Identical particles, solved

Lecture 17 left a canonical sum over occupation numbers that could not be done, because of the constraint $\sum n_\alpha = N$ (slides 22–24). The grand canonical ensemble sums over every $N$ as well, which removes the constraint. With $N = \sum n_\alpha$ and $E = \sum\epsilon_\alpha n_\alpha$, the exponential splits into one factor per single-particle state $\alpha$, and the sum over each $n_\alpha$ can be done separately (slide 25):

<Formula label="Grand sum over occupation numbers" tex="\Xi = \sum_{\{n_\alpha\}}\exp\left(\frac{\sum_\alpha(\mu - \epsilon_\alpha)n_\alpha}{kT}\right), \qquad \ln\Xi = \sum_\alpha\ln\left(\sum_{n_\alpha}e^{(\mu - \epsilon_\alpha)n_\alpha/kT}\right)" cite="mit" at="slide 25" />

For **fermions** $n_\alpha$ is 0 or 1; for **bosons** it runs over $0, 1, 2, \dots$, a geometric series (slide 26):

<Formula label="Fermions and bosons" tex="\ln\Xi_{\text{FD}} = \sum_\alpha\ln\left(1 + e^{(\mu - \epsilon_\alpha)/kT}\right), \qquad \ln\Xi_{\text{BE}} = -\sum_\alpha\ln\left(1 - e^{(\mu - \epsilon_\alpha)/kT}\right)" cite="mit" at="slide 26" />

Then $\langle N\rangle = \sum\langle n_\alpha\rangle$, and differentiating with respect to $\mu$ gives the mean occupation of each state (slide 27):

<Formula label="Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein occupations" tex="\langle n_\alpha\rangle = \frac{1}{e^{(\epsilon_\alpha - \mu)/kT} \pm 1}" cite="mit" at="slide 27" />

with $+$ for fermions (Fermi–Dirac) and $-$ for bosons (Bose–Einstein). For bosons $\mu$ must lie below every $\epsilon_\alpha$, or the occupation would turn negative.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Occupations in the early universe">
In the hot early universe, particles were created and destroyed freely, so their numbers were not fixed and the grand canonical occupations apply directly. With the chemical potentials essentially zero, photons follow $1/(e^{\epsilon/kT} - 1)$ and neutrinos and electrons $1/(e^{\epsilon/kT} + 1)$. Integrating these over states gives the factor $\frac78$ by which each fermion species counts less than a boson species, which 8.286 uses to count the particles that made up the radiation (Lecture 17).
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Molecules on a surface, and three kinds of occupation">
<Problem>
(a) A gas of molecules of mass $4.65 \times 10^{-26}$ kg is at 300 K and 100 Pa above a surface where they bind with $\epsilon_0 = 0.1$ eV. Find $kT$ in eV, $\lambda$, the bulk density $n$, and the surface density $\sigma$ ($k = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K, $h = 6.62607 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602177 \times 10^{-19}$ J). (b) For a state with $\epsilon - \mu = kT$, compare the Fermi–Dirac, Bose–Einstein and classical occupations.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Adsorption">
$kT \approx 0.02585$ eV, so $\epsilon_0/kT \approx 3.868$ and $e^{\epsilon_0/kT} \approx 47.9$. Then $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mkT} \approx 1.905 \times 10^{-11}$ m and $n = P/kT \approx 2.414 \times 10^{22}$ m⁻³, so $\sigma = \lambda ne^{\epsilon_0/kT} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{13}$ m⁻². A full layer of molecules would be around $10^{19}$ m⁻², so the surface is still almost bare: the model's assumption of a dilute two-dimensional gas holds.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Occupations">
Fermi–Dirac $1/(e + 1) \approx 0.269$; Bose–Einstein $1/(e - 1) \approx 0.582$; classical $e^{-1} \approx 0.368$. Fermions are pushed below, and bosons pulled above, the classical value.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $dU = T\,dS - P\,dV + \mu\,dN$; $\mu = (\partial U/\partial N)_{S,V} = (\partial F/\partial N)_{T,V}$; particles flow from high $\mu$ to low, and equilibrium needs equal $T$ and $\mu$.
- Ideal gas: $\mu = kT\ln(n\lambda^3)$; adsorbed layer in equilibrium: $\sigma = \lambda ne^{\epsilon_0/kT}$.
- Grand canonical: $p \propto e^{(\mu N - H)/kT}$, $\Xi = \sum_N e^{\mu N/kT}Z_N$; $\Phi_G = -kT\ln\Xi$, $d\Phi_G = -S\,dT - P\,dV - \langle N\rangle d\mu$.
- For identical particles the grand sum factorizes: $\langle n_\alpha\rangle = 1/(e^{(\epsilon_\alpha - \mu)/kT} \pm 1)$, Fermi–Dirac ($+$) and Bose–Einstein ($-$).
