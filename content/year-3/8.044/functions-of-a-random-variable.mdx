If $x$ is a random variable, any function of it is one too. Knowing the density of an atom's speed, say, one may want the density of its kinetic energy, not just its average. This lecture gives one method for finding the density of a function, a method that always works and is the most useful in physics, and applies it to several examples (slides 1–17; notes pp. 35–46). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides and his probability notes, Chapter 3; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The notes' figures are not reproduced.)

## The method

Given $p_x(\zeta)$ and a function $f(x)$, find $p_f(\eta)$ in three steps (notes p. 35; slide 2):

- **A.** Sketch $f(x)$ and find the region of $x$ where $f(x) < \eta$.
- **B.** Integrate $p_x$ over that region: this is the cumulative distribution $P_f(\eta)$.
- **C.** Differentiate $P_f(\eta)$ to get $p_f(\eta)$.

The integral in step B rarely needs to be done, because of a rule for differentiating an integral whose limits and integrand depend on the variable (notes p. 35; slide 4):

<Formula label="Derivative of an integral" tex="\frac{d}{dy}\int_{a(y)}^{b(y)}g(y, x)\,dx = g\left(y, b(y)\right)\frac{db}{dy} - g\left(y, a(y)\right)\frac{da}{dy} + \int_{a(y)}^{b(y)}\frac{\partial g(y, x)}{\partial y}\,dx" cite="reading1" at="p. 35" />

## The intensity of thermal light

The instantaneous intensity of a linearly polarized wave is $I = aE^2$; the same square law appears for power in electrical and acoustic systems (notes p. 36). For thermal radiation, the field has a Gaussian density with zero mean and variance $\sigma^2$. The mean intensity is easy, $\langle I\rangle = a\sigma^2$, but the density needs the method (notes pp. 36–38; slides 3–5). $I < \eta$ when $|E| < \sqrt{\eta/a}$, so $P_I(\eta) = \int_{-\sqrt{\eta/a}}^{\sqrt{\eta/a}}p_E(\zeta)\,d\zeta$, and differentiating the limits:

<Formula label="Density of the intensity" tex="p_I(\eta) = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{a\eta}}\left(p_E\left(\sqrt{\eta/a}\right) + p_E\left(-\sqrt{\eta/a}\right)\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi a\sigma^2I}}\exp\left(-\frac{I}{2a\sigma^2}\right), \quad I > 0" cite="reading1" at="pp. 37–38" />

The first form holds for any density of $E$; the second for the Gaussian. The density diverges at $I = 0$, though integrably, for two reasons: the square law crowds small fields into small intensities, and the Gaussian gives small fields a finite weight (notes pp. 38–39). Such crowding returns later as the density of states.

## Harmonic motion with a random phase

Let $x = x_0\sin\phi$ with $\phi$ uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$: an oscillator of fixed energy observed at a random time, or a binary star seen edge-on at a random moment (notes p. 39). For $0 < \eta < x_0$, $x < \eta$ except for $\phi$ between $\arcsin(\eta/x_0)$ and $\pi - \arcsin(\eta/x_0)$, so $P_x(\eta) = \frac12 + \frac1\pi\arcsin(\eta/x_0)$; the case $x < 0$ follows the same way or by symmetry. Differentiating (notes pp. 40–42):

<Formula label="Position of an oscillator with random phase" tex="p_x(\eta) = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{x_0^2 - \eta^2}}, \qquad -x_0 < \eta < x_0" cite="reading1" at="pp. 41–42" />

It diverges at the turning points $\pm x_0$, where the oscillator moves slowest.

## Functions of two variables, and changes of variable

The same three steps work for a function of two variables (notes pp. 42–44). For the product $z = xy$, the region where $z < \eta$ is bounded by the hyperbolas $y = \eta/x$; differentiating gives, for either sign of $\eta$ (the notes' closing line says "for all z < 0", a slip for all z),

<Formula label="Density of a product" tex="p_z(\eta) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\zeta}{|\zeta|}\,p_{x,y}\left(\zeta, \frac{\eta}{\zeta}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 44" />

A common use is a **change of variables** (notes pp. 45–46). For points uniform in a unit disk, the fraction with radius below $r$ and angle below $\theta$ is $r^2\theta/(2\pi)$, so $p(r, \theta) = r/\pi$, and then $p(r) = 2r$ for $r < 1$ and $p(\theta) = 1/(2\pi)$. Now $p(r)p(\theta) = p(r, \theta)$: in polar coordinates the two variables are independent, although $x$ and $y$ were not.

## The slides' examples

**Black-body photons** (slides 6–9). The frequency of a photon in black-body radiation at temperature $T$ has the density $p(\nu) = \frac{1}{2.404}\frac{1}{\nu_0}\frac{(\nu/\nu_0)^2}{\exp(\nu/\nu_0) - 1}$, with $\nu_0 = kT/h$ (the slide writes the constant as $2\zeta(3) = 2.404$; slide 6). With $\lambda = c/\nu$, step B gives $P(\lambda) = \int_{c/\lambda}^{\infty}p(\nu)\,d\nu$, and step C (slide 8):

<Formula label="Black-body photons per wavelength" tex="p(\lambda) = \frac{c}{\lambda^2}\,p_\nu\left(\frac{c}{\lambda}\right) = \frac{1}{2.404}\,\frac{1}{\lambda_0}\,\frac{(\lambda/\lambda_0)^{-4}}{\exp\left((\lambda/\lambda_0)^{-1}\right) - 1}, \qquad \lambda_0 = \frac{c}{\nu_0}" cite="mit" at="slide 8" />

At long wavelengths it falls as $(\lambda/\lambda_0)^{-3}$; at short ones as $(\lambda/\lambda_0)^{-4}e^{-\lambda_0/\lambda}$ (slide 9).

**The larger of two random numbers** (slides 10–12). If $x$ and $y$ are independent and uniform on $(0, 1)$, $z = \max(x, y)$ is below $\eta$ only when both are, so $P_z(\eta) = \eta^2$, $p_z = 2\eta$, $\langle z\rangle = \frac23$, $\langle z^2\rangle = \frac12$, $\text{Var}(z) = \frac1{18}$ and the standard deviation is about 0.24.

**A desorbing atom** (slides 13–17). An atom leaving a surface has independent speed, polar angle and azimuth, with $p(v) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^4}v^3e^{-v^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ and $p(\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$. Its velocity along the normal, $v_z = v\cos\theta$, comes out after a substitution in the angle integral as (slides 15–17)

<Formula label="Normal velocity of a desorbing atom" tex="p(v_z) = \frac{v_z}{\sigma^2}\exp\left(-\frac{v_z^2}{2\sigma^2}\right), \qquad v_z > 0" cite="mit" at="slides 16–17" />

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="One spectrum, two shapes">
The cosmic microwave background is black-body radiation at 2.7 K. Plotted per unit frequency or per unit wavelength its spectrum peaks at different places, because changing variables changes the density, exactly as in the black-body example above: $p(\lambda) = \frac{c}{\lambda^2}p_\nu(c/\lambda)$. [8.286 discusses the CMB spectrum](/courses/8-286/cmb-spectrum).
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Three transformations">
<Problem>
(a) A gas atom's velocity component $v$ is Gaussian with zero mean and variance $\sigma^2 = kT/m$. Find the density of its kinetic energy $K = \frac12mv^2$ and check that $\langle K\rangle = \frac12kT$. (b) A pointer spins to a uniformly random angle $\phi$; let $y = \cos\phi$. What fraction of spins give $|y| > 0.9$? (c) For two independent uniform numbers on $(0, 1)$, find the density and mean of the smaller one.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Kinetic energy">
This is the intensity example with $a = m/2$: $p(K) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi kTK}}e^{-K/(kT)}$ for $K > 0$. Its mean is $a\sigma^2 = \frac m2\cdot\frac{kT}{m} = \frac12kT$, one half $kT$ per quadratic term, a result met again in the canonical ensemble.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A cosine">
As for the oscillator, $p(y) = \frac{1}{\pi\sqrt{1 - y^2}}$. Then $\text{prob}(|y| > 0.9) = 1 - \frac2\pi\arcsin 0.9 \approx 1 - 0.713 = 0.287$: more than a quarter of the spins land in the outer tenth of the range.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The smaller number">
The smaller one, $w$, exceeds $\eta$ only if both do, so $1 - P_w(\eta) = (1 - \eta)^2$, $p_w = 2(1 - \eta)$ and $\langle w\rangle = \int_0^12\eta(1 - \eta)\,d\eta = \frac13$; with the larger one's $\frac23$, the two average to $\frac12$, as they must.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The density of $f(x)$ follows from three steps: find where $f < \eta$, integrate $p_x$ there, differentiate; the rule for differentiating an integral avoids doing it.
- Thermal light: $p(I) \propto I^{-1/2}e^{-I/(2a\sigma^2)}$, divergent but integrable at $I = 0$; an oscillator of random phase: $p(x) = 1/(\pi\sqrt{x_0^2 - x^2})$.
- The method extends to functions of two variables, such as a product, and to changes of variables, like polar coordinates in a disk.
- Changing from frequency to wavelength reshapes the black-body density: $p(\lambda) = (c/\lambda^2)p_\nu(c/\lambda)$.
