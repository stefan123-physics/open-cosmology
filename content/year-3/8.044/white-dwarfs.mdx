Statistical physics decides the fate of stars. A star is self-gravitating, so its structure must be found self-consistently, and what matters is which physical process resists the collapse (slides 1–19). This lecture follows that question from planets to neutron stars, with the free Fermi gas of Lecture 19 as the hero. (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides, which also show astronomical photographs from other sources; this page uses only the physics, in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## From planets to dead stars

**Planets** (slide 2): their mass is small, so gravity is weak, and ordinary atomic forces, the stiffness of solids and liquids, hold them up.

**Normal stars** (slide 3): gravitational contraction heats the gas until fusion starts and supplies energy. The star is a plasma of electrons and nuclei, held up by kinetic pressure $P = nkT$ and by radiation pressure $P = \frac13u(T)$ from Lecture 15, which the slide says helps, and dominates in stars above about ten solar masses.

When fusion ends, a star has four possible fates, in order of increasing mass (slide 4): it disperses as gas, or becomes a **white dwarf**, a **neutron star**, or a **black hole**.

## The white dwarf

In a white dwarf (slide 5) fusion has stopped and the star has collapsed until its nuclei are about a hundredth as far apart as in a solid. It is supported by the **degeneracy pressure** of its electrons, the zero-temperature Fermi pressure of Lecture 19, $P \propto n^{5/3}/m_e$. It glows white while it still has heat to radiate, then fades through grey to brown: dead and cold.

**A simple model** (slides 6–7). Take a uniform sphere of helium nuclei (alpha particles, mass $m_\alpha$) and electrons, of radius $R$ and volume $V = \frac43\pi R^3$. The mass is in the nuclei, two electrons per nucleus, so $N_e = 2M/m_\alpha$. The nuclei's kinetic energy is small; the electrons' is that of a Fermi gas at $T = 0$ (the slide's last line writes $(M/m_\alpha)^{2/3}$ for the final mass factor, a slip; the power is $5/3$):

<Formula label="Kinetic energy of the electrons" tex="E_K = \frac35N_e\epsilon_F = \frac35N_e\frac{\hbar^2}{2m_e}\left(3\pi^2\frac{N_e}{V}\right)^{2/3} = \frac35\left(\frac{9\pi}{2}\right)^{2/3}\frac{\hbar^2}{m_em_\alpha^{5/3}}\frac{M^{5/3}}{R^2}" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

The gravitational energy of a uniform sphere is

<Formula label="Gravitational energy" tex="E_P = -\frac35\frac{GM^2}{R}" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

The total $E_K + E_P$ has a minimum where its derivative with respect to $R$ vanishes (slide 7):

<Formula label="Radius of a white dwarf" tex="MR_0^3 = 8\left(\frac{9\pi}{2}\right)^2\frac{\hbar^6}{G^3m_e^3m_\alpha^5} \approx 0.74 \times 10^{51}\ \text{kg m}^3" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

So $R_0 \propto M^{-1/3}$: **heavier white dwarfs are smaller**, and in this model a stable size exists for any mass.

**Sirius B** (slides 8–9). With $M = 2.1 \times 10^{30}$ kg the model gives $R_0 \approx 7.1 \times 10^6$ m, against an observed $5.6 \times 10^6$ m: good for so crude a model. But a better model gives $8.6 \times 10^6$ m, further off, which signals a problem. The slide's figures for the electrons, $n_e \approx 8.6 \times 10^{29}$ cm⁻³ and $\epsilon_F \approx 4.7 \times 10^{-7}$ erg, a Fermi temperature of about $3.4 \times 10^9$ K, show the problem. (That density matches the observed radius; at the model radius it would be about half as large, with $\epsilon_F$ about $3.3 \times 10^{-7}$ erg, and the conclusion is the same.) The Fermi temperature is far above the slide's comparison temperature of $2 \times 10^7$ K (labelled the surface temperature there, though Sirius B's surface is nearer $25\,000$ K; either way $T \approx 0$ is justified), and $\epsilon_F$ is comparable with $m_ec^2 = 8.2 \times 10^{-7}$ erg. **Relativity is needed.**

## The relativistic Fermi gas

For highly relativistic electrons $\epsilon = c\hbar|\vec k|$ (slide 10). The counting of states is unchanged, so $k_F = (3\pi^2N/V)^{1/3}$ and

<Formula label="Relativistic Fermi energy" tex="\epsilon_F = c\hbar\left(3\pi^2\frac NV\right)^{1/3}, \qquad D(\epsilon) = \frac{V}{\pi^2}\frac{\epsilon^2}{(c\hbar)^3}" cite="mit" at="slides 10–11" />

With $D(\epsilon) = a\epsilon^2$ (slides 12–13), $N = \frac13a\epsilon_F^3$ and $E = \frac14a\epsilon_F^4 = \frac34N\epsilon_F$, and at $T = 0$ (where $dS = 0$)

<Formula label="Relativistic degeneracy pressure" tex="P = -\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial V}\right)_{N,S} = \frac14\frac NV\epsilon_F \propto \left(\frac NV\right)^{4/3}" cite="mit" at="slide 13" />

which rises **less steeply** with density than the non-relativistic $(N/V)^{5/3}$.

## The Chandrasekhar limit

Redo the energy balance with relativistic electrons (slide 14):

<Formula label="Relativistic kinetic energy of a white dwarf" tex="E_K = \frac34N_e\epsilon_F = \frac32\left(\frac{9\pi}{2}\right)^{1/3}\frac{c\hbar}{m_\alpha^{4/3}}\frac{M^{4/3}}{R}" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

Now both energies go as $1/R$: $E_K = a/R$, $E_P = -b/R$, and $E_K + E_P = (a - b)/R$ has **no minimum** (slide 15). If $a > b$ the star expands until the electrons are no longer relativistic; if $b > a$ it collapses without limit. The dividing line $a = b$, comparing $c\hbar(M/m_\alpha)^{4/3}$ with $GM^2$, sets a critical mass (slide 16):

<Formula label="Critical mass, order of magnitude" tex="M \sim \left(\frac{c\hbar}{Gm_\alpha^2}\right)^{3/2}m_\alpha" cite="mit" at="slide 16" />

The full calculation (whose details the slide does not give; it replaces the uniform density with the star's actual profile) gives the **Chandrasekhar limit** (slide 17):

<Formula label="Chandrasekhar limit" tex="M_{\text{Ch}} = 0.20\left(\frac ZA\right)^2\left(\frac{ch}{Gm_p^2}\right)^{3/2}m_p" cite="mit" at="slide 17" />

where $Z/A$ is the average ratio of atomic number to mass number and $m_p$ the proton mass: the same form as the estimate. For $Z/A = 0.5$ (helium, carbon or oxygen) it is about $1.4M_\odot$, 1.4 solar masses. No white dwarf can be heavier.

## Neutron stars and beyond

A heavier dead star collapses further (slide 18). Protons capture electrons, $p^+ + e^- \to n$, lowering the Coulomb energy, and the star becomes a ball of neutrons held up by **neutron** degeneracy pressure. The same estimate with $m_n$ in place of both masses gives $MR_0^3 \propto \hbar^6/G^3m_n^8$, so $R_0 \approx 15$ km for $1.4M_\odot$ according to the slide (the uniform-density model alone gives somewhat less). Nuclear forces also contribute to the pressure. Rotating neutron stars are seen as **pulsars**. They too have a maximum mass, of order $2M_\odot$; beyond it, nothing known stops the collapse to a **black hole**.

In summary (slide 19): normal stars, about 1 g/cm³, held by gas (and radiation) pressure; white dwarfs, about $10^6$ g/cm³, by electron degeneracy; neutron stars, about $10^{14}$ g/cm³, by neutron degeneracy and nuclear forces.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A standard candle from a mass limit">
A white dwarf in a close binary can pull matter from its companion until it nears the Chandrasekhar mass, and is then thought to explode as a type Ia supernova. Exploding near a common mass is one reason to expect these supernovae to be similar, but as 8.286 notes there is no complete theory of their brightness: in practice astronomers calibrate it empirically, using the shape of each supernova's light curve, which is what made them usable as standard candles. In 1998 they showed that distant supernovae are dimmer than expected: the expansion of the universe is accelerating, as 8.286 discusses in Lecture 20.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Sizing up white dwarfs">
<Problem>
Use $MR_0^3 = 0.74 \times 10^{51}$ kg m³, $M_\odot = 1.989 \times 10^{30}$ kg and $m_\alpha = 6.645 \times 10^{-27}$ kg. (a) Find $R_0$ for $M = 1.0M_\odot$ and compare with the Earth's radius, 6371 km. (b) Find the mean density. (c) Find the electron density $n_e = 2M/(m_\alpha V)$. (d) How much smaller is a dwarf of twice the mass?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Radius">
$R_0 = (0.74 \times 10^{51}/1.989 \times 10^{30})^{1/3} \approx 7.19 \times 10^6$ m, about 7200 km: a star the size of the Earth.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Density">
$\rho = M/\frac43\pi R_0^3 \approx 1.28 \times 10^9$ kg/m³, about $1.3 \times 10^6$ g/cm³, matching slide 19's $10^6$ g/cm³: a teaspoon would weigh several tonnes.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Electrons">
$n_e = 2\rho/m_\alpha \approx 3.84 \times 10^{35}$ m⁻³, that is $3.8 \times 10^{29}$ cm⁻³.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Twice the mass">
$R_0 \propto M^{-1/3}$, so $R_0$ shrinks by a factor $2^{1/3} \approx 1.26$. Squeezing the electrons further makes them more relativistic, which is exactly how the model approaches its limit.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Stars resist gravity with atomic forces (planets), gas and radiation pressure (normal stars), electron degeneracy (white dwarfs) or neutron degeneracy and nuclear forces (neutron stars).
- Non-relativistic white dwarf: $E_K \propto M^{5/3}/R^2$, $E_P = -\frac35GM^2/R$, so $MR_0^3 \approx 0.74 \times 10^{51}$ kg m³ and $R_0 \propto M^{-1/3}$.
- Sirius B's electrons are relativistic. Relativistic Fermi gas: $\epsilon_F = c\hbar(3\pi^2N/V)^{1/3}$, $P = \frac14(N/V)\epsilon_F \propto n^{4/3}$.
- Then $E_K$ and $E_P$ both go as $1/R$: above $M_{\text{Ch}} = 0.20(Z/A)^2(hc/Gm_p^2)^{3/2}m_p \approx 1.4M_\odot$ collapse is unavoidable.
