After four lectures of probability, 8.044 turns to thermodynamics: the description of large systems by a handful of measurable quantities. This lecture defines the vocabulary, derives temperature from the zeroth law, introduces work, and collects the mathematics of partial derivatives that thermodynamics relies on (slides 1–25; notes pp. 1–3). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides and his "Notes on Thermodynamic Systems"; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The figures are not reproduced.)

## The vocabulary

A **microscopic variable** belongs to individual atoms or molecules: a velocity component, the orientation of a molecule, a vibrational quantum number, the magnetic moment of an ion. A **macroscopic** (thermodynamic) variable is a measurable property of the whole system, depending collectively on all its particles (notes p. 1). If the system contains some fixed number $N$ of molecules, **extensive** variables are proportional to $N$, **intensive** ones independent of it (notes p. 1; slide 2):

| Extensive | Intensive |
|---|---|
| volume $V$, area $A$, length $L$ | pressure $P$, surface tension $S$, tension $F$ |
| polarization, magnetization $M$ | electric field $E$, magnetic field $H$ |
| internal energy $U$ | temperature $T$ |

The last row is new and needs care later (notes p. 1).

**Walls and states** (notes p. 2; slide 3). **Adiabatic** walls let no heat through; **diathermic** walls do. **Equilibrium** is reached by a system inside adiabatic walls once all transients have died out: its macroscopic variables, and the probability densities of its microscopic ones, no longer change, although the atoms keep moving, and many microscopic states correspond to the same macroscopic one. A **steady state** also has unchanging macroscopic variables but is driven: heat, current or matter flows through it. Equilibrium can take long to reach, as in alloys or glassy solids.

**Complete specification** (notes p. 2). For each system a fixed number of macroscopic variables, the independent ones, determines all the others, though which ones are chosen is free: two for a simple fluid ($P$ and $V$, or $T$ and $P$, or $U$ and $V$), three for a binary mixture, two for a rigid lattice of magnetic dipoles, three for a gas of electric dipoles. An **equation of state** relates the main variables (notes p. 2; slide 4), for example

<Formula label="Equations of state" tex="PV = NkT, \qquad V = V_0\left(1 + \alpha T - \kappa_TP\right), \qquad M = \frac{cH}{T - T_0}\ \ (T > T_0)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

for an ideal gas, a simple elastic solid and a simple paramagnet. Two systems are **in equilibrium with each other** if, brought into contact through a diathermic wall, neither changes (notes p. 3). **Thermodynamics** studies the relations between macroscopic variables, work and heat, taking the equation of state as input; **statistical mechanics** starts from the microscopic description, usually the Hamiltonian, and aims both at the probability densities of the microscopic variables and at the equations of state (notes p. 3).

## The zeroth law and temperature

The observational fact (slide 5):

> **Zeroth law.** If A is in equilibrium with C, and B is in equilibrium with C, then A is in equilibrium with B.

And a further observation, which the slide heads "Law 0.5?": many macroscopic states of B can be in equilibrium with a given state of A, forming a curve in B's state space (slide 6).

**A predictor of equilibrium** (slides 7–11). From these follows a theorem: there is a function $h(X, Y)$ of each system's state variables, a state function, defined only in equilibrium, taking the same value on many states and with a different form for each system, whose values are equal for any two systems in equilibrium with each other. The argument: equilibrium of A with C is one equation linking their variables, $F_1(X_C, Y_C, X_A, Y_A) = 0$, which can be solved for $X_C = f_1(Y_C, X_A, Y_A)$; likewise B with C gives $X_C = f_2(Y_C, X_B, Y_B)$. Setting these equal gives a condition linking A and B. But by the zeroth law, A and B are then in equilibrium with each other, a condition that cannot involve $C$'s variable $Y_C$; so $Y_C$ must drop out, and the condition must take the form $h(X_A, Y_A) = h(X_B, Y_B)$. For dilute gases, the slides illustrate it: equilibrium means equal values of $PV$ (slides 8–11).

That common value is an **empirical temperature** $t$. Curves of constant $t$ in each system's state space are its **isotherms** (slide 12). For a low-density gas, $PV/N$ is constant along an isotherm, so one may define $t \propto PV/N$, use the gas to find isotherms of other systems, and find, for instance, that a simple paramagnet has $t \propto (M/H)^{-1}$ (slides 13–14a). But any monotonic function of $PV/N$ would serve as well: the slides compare $t = PV/Nk$ with its square and its square root, and conclude that there are many possible choices (slides 14a–14d).

## Work

$dW$ denotes the work done **on** the system in a small change, minus the work done by it (slide 15). For a fluid pushed by a piston, $dW = F\,dx = (PA)(-dV/A)$ (slide 15). The general pattern (slides 15–17):

<Formula label="Work done on a system" tex="dW = -P\,dV \ \text{(fluid)}, \quad F\,dL \ \text{(wire)}, \quad S\,dA \ \text{(film)}, \quad \varepsilon\,dZ \ \text{(cell)}, \quad E\,dP_e \ \text{(dielectric)}, \quad H\,dM \ \text{(magnet)}" cite="mit" at="slides 15–17" />

Here $\varepsilon$ is a cell's electromotive force and $Z$ the charge it delivers, and $P_e$ the total electric polarization. In each, an intensive variable multiplies the change of an extensive one, and only $-P\,dV$ carries a minus sign, because pressure pushes while tension pulls. In the magnetic and electric forms, $H$ and $E$ are the fields set up by the external sources, and the work counts only what is done on the matter, not the energy the field would store without it (slide 17). These hold only for **quasistatic** processes, slow enough that the system stays close to equilibrium (slide 18).

**Work depends on the path** (slides 18–20). $W = \int_a^bY\,dX$ depends on how $Y$ varies along the way, so $W$ is **not a state function**. For a dilute gas taken from $(P_1, V_1)$ to $(P_2, V_2)$ on the same isotherm, with $V_2 < V_1$: compressing first at $P_1$ and then raising the pressure gives $W = P_1(V_1 - V_2)$; raising the pressure first and then compressing at $P_2$ gives $P_2(V_1 - V_2)$; and following the isotherm gives (slide 20)

<Formula label="Isothermal work on a dilute gas" tex="W_{12} = -\int_{V_1}^{V_2}\frac{NkT}{V}\,dV = NkT\ln\frac{V_1}{V_2} = P_1V_1\ln\frac{V_1}{V_2}" cite="mit" at="slide 20" />

## The mathematics of thermodynamics

**Three variables, two independent** (slides 21–21a). If $F(x, y, z) = 0$, each variable is a function of the other two, and

<Formula label="Reciprocal and triple product rules" tex="\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_z = \frac{1}{\left(\partial y/\partial x\right)_z}, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_z\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial z}\right)_x\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)_y = -1" cite="mit" at="slide 21" />

If a further function $w(x, y, z)$ (the slides' $W$) is held fixed along a path, then $\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_w\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial z}\right)_w\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)_w = +1$ (slide 21a), and the chain rule $\left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_w = \left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial z}\right)_w\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\right)_w$ holds (slide 22).

**Exact differentials** (slides 22–23). A state function $S(x, y)$ of two independent variables has the differential $dS = A\,dx + B\,dy$ with $A = (\partial S/\partial x)_y$ and $B = (\partial S/\partial y)_x$. Equality of the mixed second derivatives gives a condition that is necessary and also sufficient:

<Formula label="Test for an exact differential" tex="A\,dx + B\,dy \text{ is exact } \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial y}\right)_x = \left(\frac{\partial B}{\partial x}\right)_y" cite="mit" at="slide 23" />

Then $\int_1^2dS = S(x_2, y_2) - S(x_1, y_1)$ is independent of the path. Work, by contrast, is an inexact differential.

**Integrating an exact differential** (slides 24–25). Integrate $A$ with respect to $x$ at fixed $y$, adding an unknown $f(y)$; differentiate the result with respect to $y$ and set it equal to $B$, which gives $df/dy$; integrate that to find $f$.

<WorkedExample title="Paths, rules and a state function">
<Problem>
(a) A dilute (ideal) gas at $P_1 = 1.00$ atm $= 1.013 \times 10^5$ Pa fills $V_1 = 1.00$ L and is compressed to $0.50$ L at constant temperature. Find the work done on it, and compare with the two two-step paths. (b) Check the triple product rule for an ideal gas. (c) Is $y\,dx - x\,dy$ exact? Is $y\,dx + x\,dy$? Integrate the one that is.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Three paths">
$P_1V_1 = 101.3$ J. Along the isotherm, $W = 101.3\ln 2 \approx 70.2$ J. At the lower pressure first, $W = P_1(V_1 - V_2) \approx 50.7$ J; at the higher pressure $P_2 = 2P_1$ first, $W \approx 101.3$ J. Same end states, three different amounts of work.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Triple product">
With $P = NkT/V$: $\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_T = -\frac{NkT}{V^2}$, $\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = \frac{Nk}{P}$, $\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_V = \frac{V}{Nk}$. Their product is $-\frac{NkT}{V^2}\cdot\frac{Nk}{P}\cdot\frac{V}{Nk} = -\frac{NkT}{PV} = -1$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Exactness">
For $y\,dx - x\,dy$: $\partial A/\partial y = 1$ but $\partial B/\partial x = -1$, not exact. For $y\,dx + x\,dy$ both are 1, so it is exact: integrating $A = y$ in $x$ gives $xy + f(y)$; its $y$ derivative $x + f'(y)$ must equal $B = x$, so $f$ is a constant and $S = xy$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Macroscopic variables are extensive (scaling with $N$) or intensive; a fixed number of independent ones specifies an equilibrium state, and an equation of state links them.
- Zeroth law: equilibrium with a common third system implies mutual equilibrium; hence an empirical temperature, a state function equal in systems in equilibrium.
- Work done on a system is an intensive variable times the change of an extensive one ($-P\,dV$, $F\,dL$, $H\,dM$, …), valid for quasistatic processes, and depends on the path.
- $A\,dx + B\,dy$ is exact if and only if $\partial A/\partial y = \partial B/\partial x$; exact differentials integrate to state functions, independent of the path.
