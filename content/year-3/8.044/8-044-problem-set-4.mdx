This set practises four lessons. Problems 1 to 3 use [the first law and paths](/courses/8-044/first-law): heat, work, heat capacities and adiabatic changes, for a fluid and then for a magnet. Problems 4 to 6 use [the microcanonical ensemble](/courses/8-044/microcanonical-ensemble), [entropy and temperature](/courses/8-044/entropy-temperature-second-law) and [entropy as a thermodynamic variable](/courses/8-044/entropy-thermodynamic-variable): count the accessible states, take the logarithm, and read the temperature and the equation of state from its derivatives. The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 4, in our own words.

MIT publishes its solutions to this set. The hints and solutions here are rewritten from MIT's: they follow MIT's method in our own words, cite MIT's solutions at the printed page, and were checked again by an independent calculation. Where MIT's solutions have a slip, the solution here says so in brackets.

A few things differ from MIT's sheet. The due date is left out. MIT's sheet gives no points: all the points here are ours. MIT's sketches (the paths of Problem 2, the magnetization curves of Problem 3, the chain of Problem 5, and the sketches in its solutions) are redrawn as graphs computed from the formulas, or described in words. Some boxes are our additions, each marked as such: the question about a negative expansion coefficient in Problem 1, the box for $C_P$ in Problem 2, the factor per oscillator in Problem 6(a) and the question about independence in Problem 6(d). Where MIT asks an open question ("is there some condition...", "see if the weight goes up or down") the choices in the box are our wording. Some checks and remarks in the solutions are ours and say so: the test for an exact differential in Problem 3(d), the entropy check in Problem 3(f), where the Gaussian volume of Problem 4 comes from, the numerical test of Stirling's approximation in Problem 5(a), the expansion coefficient of the chain at any temperature in Problem 5(d), and the exact density for a finite number of oscillators in Problem 6(d). In Problem 3 the constants $a$ and $b$ are taken positive, which MIT's sheet leaves unsaid.

If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons. A small amount of heat is written $\delta Q$, where MIT's sheet writes a d with a bar through it. Heat $Q$ is counted positive when it flows into the system, and work $W$ positive when it is done on the system, so the first law reads $dU = \delta Q + \delta W$. The internal energy is $U$ in the thermodynamic problems and $E$ in the counting problems, as on MIT's sheet. $k$ is Boltzmann's constant.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Heat capacity at constant pressure in a simple fluid" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A simple fluid has two independent variables. Its thermal expansion coefficient is $\alpha = \frac1V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P$. Show that its heat capacity at constant pressure is

<Formula label="Heat capacity at constant pressure" tex="C_P = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_P + \alpha VP" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

The expansion coefficient can have either sign, so $C_P$ can be larger or smaller than $\left(\partial U/\partial T\right)_P$.

The box is our addition. Liquid water just above its freezing point has a negative expansion coefficient. What does the result say about it?

<Hint>

Start from the first law with the work done on a fluid, $\delta W = -P\,dV$, and solve it for $\delta Q$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The derivative you are after is taken at constant $P$, so choose $T$ and $P$ as the independent variables. Write both $dU$ and $dV$ as a term with $dT$ plus a term with $dP$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A heat capacity is $\delta Q$ divided by $dT$ along the chosen path. Along a path of constant pressure, $dP = 0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-sign" label="For a fluid with $\alpha$ negative, $C_P$ is" choices={["larger than (∂U/∂T) at constant P: a heat capacity at constant pressure always includes extra heat for expansion", "equal to (∂U/∂T) at constant P: the sign of α does not enter", "smaller than (∂U/∂T) at constant P: the surroundings do work on a fluid that shrinks as it warms", "smaller than (∂U/∂T) at constant P, because the internal energy of such a fluid falls when it is warmed"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The first law gives $\delta Q$. Written out in the variables $T$ and $P$, the part of it that multiplies $dT$ is the heat capacity at constant pressure.

<Step title="The first law for a fluid">

<Formula label="Heat from the first law" tex="\delta Q = dU - \delta W = dU + P\,dV" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Expand in T and P">

Both $U$ and $V$ are state functions, so each has an exact differential in the variables $T$ and $P$:

<Formula label="Differentials of the energy and the volume" tex="dU = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_P dT + \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial P}\right)_T dP, \qquad dV = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P dT + \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T dP" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Put both into the first law and collect the terms:

<Formula label="Heat in the variables T and P" tex="\delta Q = \left[\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_P + P\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P\right]dT + \left[\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial P}\right)_T + P\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T\right]dP" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Hold the pressure fixed">

At constant pressure $dP = 0$ and the second bracket drops out. The heat per unit rise of temperature is the first bracket, and $\left(\partial V/\partial T\right)_P = \alpha V$ by the definition of $\alpha$:

<Formula label="The result" tex="C_P \equiv \left(\frac{\delta Q}{dT}\right)_P = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_P + P\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_P + \alpha VP" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The sign, and a check">

The term $\alpha VP$ is the work the fluid does on its surroundings per unit rise of temperature. If $\alpha$ is positive the fluid expands as it warms, and the heat must pay for that work as well as for the rise of $U$. If $\alpha$ is negative the fluid shrinks, the surroundings do work on it, and less heat is needed: $C_P$ is then smaller than $\left(\partial U/\partial T\right)_P$.

Check with a monatomic ideal gas, for which $U = \frac32NkT$ and $V = NkT/P$. Then $\left(\partial U/\partial T\right)_P = \frac32Nk$, $\alpha = 1/T$ and $\alpha VP = Nk$, so the formula gives $C_P = \frac52Nk$, which is $C_V + Nk$ as in the lesson.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Heat depends on the path. On the legs at fixed $V$ or fixed $P$ it is a heat capacity times a rise of temperature. On the straight line it follows from the first law, because the change of $U$ is the same on every path.

<Step title="The heat capacity at constant pressure">

With $\left(\partial U/\partial V\right)_T = 0$ and $V = NkT/P$, the lesson's relation gives

<Formula label="Heat capacity at constant pressure of this gas" tex="C_P = C_V + \left[\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_T + P\right]\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P = \frac52Nk + P\,\frac{Nk}{P} = \frac72Nk" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2, pp. 1–2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The four corners">

From $PV = NkT$, with $P_1V_1 = NkT_1$: the temperature is $T_1$ at $a$, $2T_1$ at $c$ (twice the pressure), $2T_1$ at $d$ (twice the volume) and $4T_1$ at $b$ (twice both).

</Step>

<Step title="The two paths with corners">

<Formula label="Heat along the paths through c and through d" tex="Q(acb) = C_V\left(2T_1 - T_1\right) + C_P\left(4T_1 - 2T_1\right) = \frac{19}{2}NkT_1, \qquad Q(adb) = C_P\left(2T_1 - T_1\right) + C_V\left(4T_1 - 2T_1\right) = \frac{17}{2}NkT_1" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

(MIT's solutions write the same sums as integrals over $P$ and over $V$.)

</Step>

<Step title="The straight path">

The work done on the gas along $adb$ is all on the first leg: $W(adb) = -P_1\left(V_2 - V_1\right) = -NkT_1$. So the change of the internal energy, which is the same for every path from $a$ to $b$, is

<Formula label="Change of the internal energy from a to b" tex="\Delta U = Q(adb) + W(adb) = \left(\frac{17}{2} - 1\right)NkT_1 = \frac{15}{2}NkT_1" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

On the straight line $P = \left(P_1/V_1\right)V$, so

<Formula label="Work and heat along the straight line" tex="W(ab) = -\int_{V_1}^{V_2}\frac{P_1}{V_1}\,V\,dV = -\frac{P_1}{2V_1}\left(V_2^2 - V_1^2\right) = -\frac32NkT_1, \qquad Q(ab) = \Delta U - W(ab) = 9NkT_1" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), pp. 2–3" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\Delta U$ is also $C_V$ times the rise of temperature, $\frac52Nk \times 3T_1 = \frac{15}{2}NkT_1$, as found. The three heats differ only by the work, and they are in the order of the areas under the paths: $acb$ lies highest in the diagram and needs the most heat, $adb$ lies lowest and needs the least, and the straight line is in between.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

**The idea.** A heat capacity can be defined for any path: it is $\delta Q/dT$ along it. The path fixes how fast the volume grows with temperature.

<Step title="Heat along a general path">

<Formula label="Heat capacity along the path ab" tex="\delta Q = dU + P\,dV = C_V\,dT + P\,dV \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_{ab} \equiv \left(\frac{\delta Q}{dT}\right)_{ab} = C_V + P\left(\frac{dV}{dT}\right)_{ab}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3" />

The term with $\left(\partial U/\partial V\right)_T$ is absent because that derivative is zero.

</Step>

On the line $P = \left(P_1/V_1\right)V$. With $P = NkT/V$ this gives $V^2 = \left(V_1/P_1\right)NkT$, and its differential is $2V\,dV = \left(V_1/P_1\right)Nk\,dT$. So

<Formula label="The result" tex="C_{ab} = C_V + \frac{P_1}{V_1}V \cdot \frac{V_1}{P_1}\,\frac{Nk}{2V} = \frac52Nk + \frac12Nk = 3Nk" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3" />

</Step>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Thermodynamics of a Curie law paramagnet" points={30} mit="Problem 3">

A simple magnetic system has two independent variables. Its state variables include the magnetic field $H$, the magnetization $M$, the temperature $T$ and the internal energy $U$. The work done on it is $\delta W = H\,dM$. Four quantities are often measured:

<Formula label="Four measured quantities of a magnet" tex="\chi_T \equiv \left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial H}\right)_T, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial T}\right)_H, \qquad C_M \equiv \left(\frac{\delta Q}{dT}\right)_M, \qquad C_H \equiv \left(\frac{\delta Q}{dT}\right)_H" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

They are the isothermal susceptibility, the temperature coefficient, and the heat capacities at constant $M$ and at constant $H$.

A particular magnet is the **Curie law paramagnet**. Its equation of state is $M = aH/T$ with $a$ a positive constant: at a fixed temperature $M$ is proportional to $H$, more steeply the colder it is, and at a fixed field $M$ falls as $1/T$ (MIT's sheet sketches both). For this magnet one can show that $\left(\partial U/\partial M\right)_T = 0$, and you may assume $C_M = bT$ with $b$ a positive constant.

Parts (a), (c) and (e) are about any simple magnetic system. In their boxes type `UM` for $\left(\partial U/\partial M\right)_T$, `MT` for the temperature coefficient $\left(\partial M/\partial T\right)_H$, `chi` for $\chi_T$, and `CM`, `CH` for the heat capacities.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Take $T$ and $M$ as the independent variables, for any simple magnetic system. Write $C_M$ as a derivative of the internal energy. Find $C_H - C_M$ in terms of a derivative of the internal energy, $H$ and the temperature coefficient. Then write $dU(T, M)$ with the coefficients of $dT$ and $dM$ expressed through measured quantities and $H(T, M)$.

<Hint>

This is the fluid calculation of the lesson with $-P\,dV$ replaced by $H\,dM$: write $dU$ in the variables $T$ and $M$, then $\delta Q = dU - H\,dM$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $C_H$, divide $\delta Q$ by $dT$ along a path of constant $H$. On such a path $dM/dT$ is the temperature coefficient.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Solve your result for $C_H - C_M$ for the derivative $\left(\partial U/\partial M\right)_T$ and put it back into $dU$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-diff" label="$C_H - C_M =$" expr="(UM - H)*MT"

    { expr: "(UM - H)/MT", hint: "Dividing δQ by dT along a path of constant H turns dM into dM/dT, the temperature coefficient: it multiplies the bracket." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-dudm" label="Coefficient of $dM$ in $dU(T, M)$ $=$" expr="(CH - CM)/MT + H"
  vars={{ CH: [3, 5], CM: [1, 2], MT: [-2, -0.5], H: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ CH: "C_H", CM: "C_M", MT: "\\left(\\partial M/\\partial T\\right)_H" }}

    { expr: "(CH - CM)/MT", hint: "That is (∂U/∂M) − H. The derivative of U itself has the field added back." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same steps as for a fluid in the lesson, with the work $H\,dM$.

<Step title="Heat in the variables T and M">

<Formula label="First law for a magnet" tex="dU = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_M dT + \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial M}\right)_T dM, \qquad \delta Q = dU - H\,dM = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_M dT + \left[\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial M}\right)_T - H\right]dM" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(a), p. 4" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two heat capacities">

At constant $M$ only the first term is left. At constant $H$, $dM = \left(\partial M/\partial T\right)_H dT$:

<Formula label="Heat capacities of a magnet" tex="C_M = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_M, \qquad C_H = \left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right)_M + \left[\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial M}\right)_T - H\right]\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial T}\right)_H" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(a), p. 4" />

<Formula label="Difference of the heat capacities" tex="C_H - C_M = \left[\left(\frac{\partial U}{\partial M}\right)_T - H\right]\left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial T}\right)_H" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(a), p. 4" />

</Step>

<Answer id="p6-c" label="$c =$" expr="2*pi/omega"
  vars={{ omega: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi", hint: "That gives the volume in the space of the x. Convert it to the space of the p and q." },
    { expr: "2*pi*omega", hint: "Check the units: cE must be an action (an energy times a time), like the product of a p and a q. E/ω is one; Eω is not." },
    { expr: "2/omega", hint: "The π of the sphere's volume is missing: the sphere contributes π^N E^N/N!." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** Rescale the axes so that the energy surface becomes a sphere, use the known volume of a sphere, and undo the scaling.

<Step title="The sphere">

In the new variables the surface says that the squares of all $2N$ variables $x$ add up to $E$: a sphere in $2N$ dimensions of radius $\sqrt E$. By the lesson's formula with $n = N$ its volume is $\pi^NE^N/N!$.

</Step>

<Step title="Back to p and q">

Each $dp_i = \sqrt{2m}\,dx$ and each $dq_i = \sqrt{2/(m\omega^2)}\,dx$, so one pair gives the factor $\sqrt{2m}\sqrt{2/(m\omega^2)} = 2/\omega$, and there are $N$ pairs:

<Formula label="Phase-space volume of the oscillators" tex="\Phi(E, N) = \frac{\pi^N}{N!}\left(\sqrt{2m}\right)^N\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{m\omega^2}}\right)^N E^N = \left(\frac{2\pi}{\omega}\right)^N\frac{1}{N!}\,E^N" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(a), p. 14" />

So $c = 2\pi/\omega$. (MIT's solutions call this volume $\Omega$; the sheet calls it $\Phi$, as the lessons do for the volume enclosed by the energy surface.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For one oscillator, $N = 1$, the surface is an ellipse in the plane of $q$ and $p$ with half-axes $\sqrt{2E/(m\omega^2)}$ and $\sqrt{2mE}$. Its area is $\pi$ times their product, $2\pi E/\omega$, as the formula says. The mass has dropped out.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Formula label="Entropy of the oscillators" tex="S(E, N) = k\ln\Phi(E, N) = k\ln\left\{\left(\frac{2\pi}{\omega}\right)^N\frac{1}{N!}\,E^N\right\} \approx Nk\left[\ln\left(\frac{2\pi E}{\omega N}\right) + 1\right]" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(b), p. 14 (MIT stops before Stirling's approximation)" />

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Find $T$, and express $E$ in terms of $N$ and $T$.

<Hint>

The statistical definition of temperature: $1/T = \left(\partial S/\partial E\right)_N$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-e" label="$E =$" expr="N*k*T"
  vars={{ N: [50, 100], k: [0.5, 2], T: [1, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3/2*N*k*T", hint: "That is the monatomic ideal gas, whose volume grows as E to the power 3N/2. Here Φ grows as E to the power N." },
    { expr: "N*k*T/2", hint: "Each oscillator has two squared terms in its energy, one with p and one with q. Together they give kT, not kT/2." },
    { expr: "k*T", hint: "That is the mean energy of one oscillator. E is the energy of all N." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only the factor $E^N$ of $\Phi$ depends on the energy.

<Step title="Temperature and energy">

<Formula label="Temperature of the oscillators" tex="\frac1T = \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}\right)_N = \frac{Nk}{E} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E = NkT, \qquad C_N = Nk" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(c), p. 14" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ideal monatomic gas of the lesson has $\Phi \propto E^{3N/2}$ and $E = \frac32NkT$: there are $3N$ squared terms in its energy and each carries $\frac12kT$. The oscillators have $2N$ squared terms, $N$ with a $p$ and $N$ with a $q$, and $E = 2N \times \frac12kT$. The heat capacity $Nk$ does not depend on the mass or on the frequency.

</Step>

<Part label="d" points={8}>

Find the joint probability density for the position $q_i$ and the momentum $p_i$ of one of the oscillators, in terms of $T$. Sketch it. In the box type `p` and `q` for $p_i$ and $q_i$.

The second box is our addition: are $p_i$ and $q_i$ statistically independent?

<Hint>

As in Problem 4: the density is $\Phi'/\Phi$, where $\Phi'$ is the volume for the other $N - 1$ oscillators when this one has the energy $\epsilon = p_i^2/(2m) + m\omega^2q_i^2/2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The others then share the energy $E - \epsilon$. Write the ratio with the factor $\left(1 - \epsilon/E\right)$ raised to a large power.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For large $N$, $\left(1 - x/N\right)^N \to e^{-x}$. Use $E = NkT$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-pq" label="$p(p_i, q_i) =$" expr="omega/(2*pi*k*T)*exp(-p^2/(2*m*k*T) - m*omega^2*q^2/(2*k*T))"
  vars={{ omega: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2], T: [1, 3], m: [0.5, 2], p: [0.3, 1.5], q: [0.3, 1.5] }}
  names={{ omega: "\\omega", p: "p_i", q: "q_i" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-p^2/(2*m*k*T) - m*omega^2*q^2/(2*k*T))/(k*T)", hint: "That is the density of the energy ε of one oscillator. A density in p and q counts area in their plane, and the ring between ε and ε + dε has the area (2π/ω) dε." },
    { expr: "omega/(2*pi*k*T)*exp(-p^2/(2*m*k*T))", hint: "The energy of the oscillator has a second part, mω²q²/2. As it stands your density does not fall off with q and cannot be normalized." },

  ]} />

<Answer id="p6-indep" label="$p_i$ and $q_i$ are" choices={["not independent: both are tied together by the energy of the oscillator", "independent: the joint density is a function of p times a function of q", "not independent: a large momentum makes a large displacement more likely", "independent only at high temperature"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One oscillator is a small part of a large system. The volume left for the rest falls off exponentially with the energy the one oscillator takes.

<Step title="The ratio of volumes">

Let $\Phi'$ be the volume for $N - 1$ oscillators with the total energy $E - \epsilon$, where $\epsilon = p_i^2/(2m) + m\omega^2q_i^2/2$:

<Formula label="Density as a ratio of volumes" tex="p(p_i, q_i) = \frac{\Phi'}{\Phi} = \left(\frac{2\pi}{\omega}\right)^{-1}\frac{N!}{\left(N - 1\right)!}\,\frac{\left(E - \epsilon\right)^{N-1}}{E^N} = \frac{\omega}{2\pi}\,\frac NE\left(1 - \frac{\epsilon}{E}\right)^{N-1}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(d), p. 15" />

</Step>

<Step title="Large N">

All the oscillators are alike, so the mean energy of one is $\langle\epsilon\rangle = E/N = kT$. With $E = NkT$ and $N$ large, $\left(1 - \epsilon/(NkT)\right)^{N-1} \to e^{-\epsilon/kT}$:

<Formula label="Joint density of the momentum and position of one oscillator" tex="p(p_i, q_i) = \frac{\omega}{2\pi kT}\,e^{-\epsilon/kT} = \frac{\omega}{2\pi kT}\exp\left[-\frac{p_i^2}{2mkT}\right]\exp\left[-\frac{m\omega^2q_i^2}{2kT}\right]" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(d), p. 15" />

</Step>

<Step title="Two Gaussians">

The density is a product of a Gaussian in $p_i$ with variance $mkT$ and a Gaussian in $q_i$ with variance $kT/(m\omega^2)$:

<Formula label="The density as a product" tex="p(p_i, q_i) = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi mkT}}\exp\left[-\frac{p_i^2}{2mkT}\right]\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi kT/(m\omega^2)}}\exp\left[-\frac{q_i^2}{2kT/(m\omega^2)}\right]\right) = p(p_i)\,p(q_i)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6(d), p. 15" />

So $p_i$ and $q_i$ are statistically independent. The density is highest at the origin, and its contours of constant probability are the ellipses of constant $\epsilon$, centred on the origin with their axes along $q_i$ and $p_i$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-3, 3]} range={[-3, 3]} xLabel="q / σ_q" yLabel="p / σ_p"
  curves={[
    { x: "cos(s)", y: "sin(s)", range: [0, 6.2832], label: "0.61 of peak" },
    { x: "2*cos(s)", y: "2*sin(s)", range: [0, 6.2832], label: "0.14 of peak", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="Contours of constant probability density, computed from the formula, with each axis in units of its own standard deviation: σ_q² = kT/(mω²) and σ_p² = mkT. In these units the contours are circles; in plain p and q they are ellipses. On the inner one the density is 0.61 of its peak value, on the outer one 0.14." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The density is normalized: each Gaussian factor integrates to 1. The mean energy it gives is $\langle p_i^2\rangle/(2m) + m\omega^2\langle q_i^2\rangle/2 = \frac12kT + \frac12kT = kT$, in agreement with part (c).

A remark of ours on the first step. Strictly, the accessible volume $\Omega$ of the shell is proportional to $\partial\Phi/\partial E$, not to $\Phi$. Using it for both volumes gives $\frac{\omega}{2\pi}\frac{N - 1}{E}\left(1 - \epsilon/E\right)^{N-2}$, which is normalized exactly for every $N$ and has the same limit for large $N$.

</Step>

</Solution>
