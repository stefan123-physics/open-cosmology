Statistical physics deals with systems of enormous numbers of particles, whose individual behaviour cannot be followed. Its language is **probability**. 8.044 begins with a short course in probability, from one random variable to sums of many, and this first lecture covers a single variable (slides 1–14, of which the opening overview and the closing Poisson plots are only described; notes pp. 1–18). (MIT's material for this lecture is Thomas Greytak's lecture slides and his lecture notes on probability, Chapter 1; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The notes' figures are not reproduced.)

## Random variables

A **random variable** is a quantity whose value cannot be predicted from the information at hand, although its behaviour follows the rules of probability (notes p. 1). The uncertainty has two sources: **ignorance**, as for the velocity of an atom picked at random from a gas whose individual atoms we have not followed, and **quantum mechanics**, as for the distance of a hydrogen atom's electron from its proton, random even when the atom's state is completely known (notes p. 1; slide 2). Random variables can be **continuous** (a velocity), **discrete** (the number of atoms in a small volume) or **mixed** (the energy of an electron in a hydrogen discharge, discrete for bound states and continuous above) (notes pp. 1–2).

Probability rests on an **ensemble**: a very large number $M$ of *similarly* prepared systems, everything specified about the system set alike in each, the rest left to chance (notes p. 2). Building a histogram of the values $x_n$ they produce and letting $M \to \infty$ defines the **probability density** (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Probability density" tex="p_x(\zeta) \equiv \lim_{M \to \infty,\ d\zeta \to 0}\frac{\text{number of systems with } \zeta \le x < \zeta + d\zeta}{M\,d\zeta}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

So $p_x(\zeta)d\zeta$ is the probability that one such system gives a value between $\zeta$ and $\zeta + d\zeta$. (The subscript names the variable and $\zeta$ is a dummy argument; when nothing is ambiguous one writes simply $p(x)$.) From the histogram picture (notes p. 3; slide 3):

<Formula label="Properties of a density" tex="p_x(\zeta) \ge 0, \qquad \text{probability}(a < x \le b) = \int_a^bp_x(\zeta)\,d\zeta, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_x(\zeta)\,d\zeta = 1" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The **cumulative distribution** (or probability distribution function) is the probability that $x$ does not exceed $\zeta$ (notes pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Cumulative distribution" tex="P_x(\zeta) \equiv \text{probability}(x \le \zeta) = \int_{-\infty}^{\zeta}p_x(\eta)\,d\eta, \qquad p_x(\zeta) = \frac{d}{d\zeta}P_x(\zeta)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–4" />

Either one specifies the random variable completely: $p_x$ contains all that can be known about $x$ for the given system (notes p. 4). The practical questions are how to find $p_x$, which is the subject of the rest of the course, and what can be extracted from it once known, the subject of these notes.

**Waiting for a decay** (notes pp. 4–6). A radioactive source behind a shutter gives detections at a mean rate $1/\tau$. Opening the shutter at $t = 0$, the waiting time to the first detection has the **exponential** density $p(t) = \tau^{-1}e^{-t/\tau}$ for $t \ge 0$ (and zero before), which is normalized and has the cumulative $P(t) = 1 - e^{-t/\tau}$.

## Delta functions: discrete and mixed variables

To treat continuous, discrete and mixed variables with one formalism, use the **delta function** $\delta(x)$: zero for $x \ne 0$, undefined at $x = 0$, with unit area (notes pp. 6–7). It is the limit of narrow normalized functions, such as a rectangle of width $w$ and height $1/w$, or a Gaussian of width $\sigma$, as the width goes to zero. Its key property is (notes p. 7)

<Formula label="Sifting property of the delta function" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,b\,\delta(x - a)\,dx = b\,f(a)" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

and its integral is a unit step at $x = a$ (notes p. 8). On a graph, a delta function is an arrow labelled with its weight.

A discrete variable is then a sum of weighted delta functions. The notes' example is the number of stars in a closely bound group, with illustrative probabilities $\frac13$, $\frac12$, $\frac16$ for single, binary and triple stars: $p(x)$ is three delta functions, and $P(x)$ is a staircase whose steps, $\frac13$, $\frac12$ and $\frac16$, are their weights; differentiating the staircase gives the delta functions back (notes pp. 8–9). A **mixed** variable has both: in a forest where one tree in six has been cut to a 4-foot stump, the height has a delta function at 4 feet plus a continuous part for the uncut trees (notes pp. 9–10). Light from a xenon discharge mixes sharp lines, from transitions between bound electron states, with a continuum, from transitions involving free electrons; its wavelength, as a random variable, has a mixed density too. Since photon energy is inversely proportional to wavelength, turning that density into one for energy needs the method of functions of a random variable, two lectures on (notes pp. 10–12).

## The slides' examples

The slides illustrate these ideas with gases and surfaces (slides 4–9). An atom leaving a surface after physical adsorption flies off at an angle $\theta$ from the normal with density $p(\theta) = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$ on $0 \le \theta \le \pi/2$, so most atoms leave neither straight up nor grazing the surface; per unit solid angle $d\Omega = \sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$, the probability goes as $\cos\theta/\pi$ (slides 4–6). An atom bouncing around inside a cavity with a small hole escapes at each wall encounter with probability $A_H/A_T$, the hole's share of the wall area, so the number $n$ of encounters before it escapes has the **geometric** (or Bose–Einstein) density

<Formula label="Geometric density" tex="p(n) = \frac{A_H}{A_T}\left(1 - \frac{A_H}{A_T}\right)^n, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots" cite="mit" at="slides 7–8" />

a discrete variable written as delta functions at the integers (slides 7–8). And an atom that may be either physically or chemically adsorbed has a mixed density whose discrete part shrinks with time as $e^{-t/\tau}$ (slide 9).

## Averages

Often less than the full density is needed: an ensemble **average** of some function of $x$ (notes p. 12; slide 10):

<Formula label="Ensemble average" tex="\langle f(x)\rangle \equiv \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\zeta)\,p_x(\zeta)\,d\zeta" cite="reading1" at="p. 12" />

The **mean** $\langle x\rangle$ locates the density; the **mean square** $\langle x^2\rangle$ is often tied to energy or power; and the **variance** measures the spread about the mean (notes pp. 12–13):

<Formula label="Variance" tex="\text{Var}(x) \equiv \left\langle\left(x - \langle x\rangle\right)^2\right\rangle = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

Its square root, the **standard deviation**, measures the width of the density.

**The Gaussian** (notes pp. 13–15; slide 11). The density

<Formula label="Gaussian density" tex="p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x - a)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

appears whenever a variable contributes quadratically to the energy in thermal equilibrium (a velocity component of a gas atom), or is the sum of many small independent contributions (a random walk). It has $\langle x\rangle = a$ and $\text{Var}(x) = \sigma^2$: two separate parameters set its position and its width (notes p. 14). Its cumulative distribution has no closed form; it is the tabulated error function (notes p. 15). By contrast, the exponential density has mean $\tau$ and variance $\tau^2$, both set by the same parameter (slide 11). Averages over the angle density above give, for instance, the mean path $\langle L\rangle$ of an atom crossing a layer of thickness $d$ in a straight line from its surface: $L = d/\cos\theta$, and $\langle L\rangle = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d}{\cos\theta}2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,d\theta = 2d$ (slide 12).

**The Poisson density** (notes pp. 15–18; slide 13). Suppose events occur along a line so that, in a short stretch $\Delta X$, exactly one event happens with probability $r\Delta X$, independently of all other stretches. Then the number of events in a length $L$ follows (notes p. 15)

<Formula label="Poisson density" tex="p(n) = \frac{1}{n!}\left(rL\right)^ne^{-rL}, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots" cite="reading1" at="p. 15" />

(derived in the next lecture, with the tools for two random variables). Examples: the number of decays from a radioactive sample in a time short compared with its mean life; the number of helium-3 atoms in a small patch of a monolayer of natural helium; the number of atoms within some distance of a given atom in a dilute gas (notes p. 16). Summing the series, with a derivative trick for the sums weighted by $n$ and $n^2$, gives normalization, $\langle n\rangle = rL$ (rate times interval) and $\langle n^2\rangle = \langle n\rangle + \langle n\rangle^2$, so (notes pp. 16–18)

<Formula label="Poisson mean and variance" tex="\langle n\rangle = rL, \qquad \text{Var}(n) = \langle n\rangle, \qquad p(n) = \frac{1}{n!}\langle n\rangle^ne^{-\langle n\rangle}" cite="reading1" at="p. 18" />

A single parameter, the mean, specifies everything.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Random variables on the largest scales">
The tiny density differences in the early universe, from which galaxies grew, are treated as a Gaussian random field: at each place the density contrast is a Gaussian random variable with zero mean. The Press–Schechter argument counts how many regions exceed the threshold for collapse, using the Gaussian's cumulative distribution, to predict how many dark matter halos of each mass form. [8.902 develops the Press–Schechter mass function](/courses/8-902/press-schechter).
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Three densities at work">
<Problem>
(a) A detector registers events at a mean rate of 0.5 per second. What is the probability that the first event comes within 1 s of opening the shutter, and what are the mean and standard deviation of the waiting time? (b) In a fixed time the number of counts is Poisson with mean 2.5. Find the probabilities of no counts, exactly two counts, and at least one count. (c) For a Gaussian, what fraction of values lies within one standard deviation of the mean?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Waiting time">
$\tau = 2$ s, so $P(1\ \text{s}) = 1 - e^{-0.5} \approx 0.393$. The mean is $\tau = 2$ s and the standard deviation $\sqrt{\tau^2} = 2$ s: for an exponential, the spread is as large as the mean.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Counting">
$p(0) = e^{-2.5} \approx 0.082$; $p(2) = \frac{2.5^2}{2}e^{-2.5} \approx 0.257$; and $1 - p(0) \approx 0.918$. The variance is 2.5, so the standard deviation is about 1.6 counts.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Within one sigma">
$\int_{a-\sigma}^{a+\sigma}p(x)\,dx = 1 - \text{erfc}(1/\sqrt2) \approx 0.683$: about two thirds of the values lie within one standard deviation.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A random variable reflects ignorance or quantum mechanics; it is specified completely by its density $p(x)$ or its cumulative distribution $P(x)$, with $p = dP/dx$.
- Delta functions let discrete and mixed variables use the same formalism.
- Averages: $\langle f\rangle = \int f\,p\,dx$; the mean, the mean square and the variance $\langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2$.
- Key densities: the Gaussian (mean and variance set separately), the exponential (both set by $\tau$) and the Poisson ($\text{Var}(n) = \langle n\rangle = rL$).
