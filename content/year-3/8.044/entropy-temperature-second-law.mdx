The previous lecture counted the accessible phase-space volume $\Omega$. This one turns that count into **entropy**, uses it to define **temperature** statistically, and derives the **second law** (slides 1–13; notes pp. 4–7). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides and sections 4–6 of his "Notes on the Microcanonical Ensemble"; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Entropy

$\Omega(E, V, N)$ grows exponentially with the size of the system. An **extensive** measure, one that adds when two systems are put together, is its logarithm (slide 2; notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Entropy" tex="S(E, V, N) \equiv k\ln\Omega(E, V, N) \approx k\ln\rho(E, V, N) \approx k\ln\Phi(E, V, N)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

The three forms differ only by terms of order $\ln N$, while each is proportional to $N$, so for a large system they agree. The entropy is a **state function**; it is **extensive**; it is a logarithmic measure of how many microscopic states belong to one macroscopic state; and $k$, Boltzmann's constant, has units of energy per kelvin.

## Temperature

Take two systems, 1 and 2, each with its own microcanonical ensemble. Isolated from everything else, they may exchange **heat** ($\delta Q_1 = -\delta Q_2$) but not work ($\delta W_1 = \delta W_2 = 0$), and the coupling is weak enough that each keeps its own identity (slide 3; notes p. 4). The combined system is also microcanonical, and its phase volume counts every way of dividing the total energy $E$:

<Formula label="Phase volume of the combined system" tex="\Omega(E) = \int_0^E \Omega_1(E')\,\Omega_2(E - E')\,dE'" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

What is the most probable energy of system 1? Its probability density is the share of the combined phase volume with system 1 at $E_1$ (slide 4; notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Probability of the energy division" tex="p(E_1) = \frac{\Omega_1(E_1)\,\Omega_2(E - E_1)}{\Omega(E)}, \qquad \ln p(E_1) = \frac1k\left[S_1(E_1) + S_2(E - E_1) - S(E)\right]" cite="mit" at="slide 4" />

which integrates to one over $0 \le E_1 \le E$. Setting the derivative with respect to $E_1$ to zero, and noting that $dE_2 = -dE_1$, the maximum lies where (slide 5; notes p. 5)

<Formula label="Condition for the most probable energy" tex="\left(\frac{\partial S_1}{\partial E_1}\right)_{\delta W_1 = 0} = \left(\frac{\partial S_2}{\partial E_2}\right)_{\delta W_2 = 0}" cite="mit" at="slide 5" />

The left side depends only on system 1 and the right only on system 2. It fixes the most probable $E_1$, but it also says something deeper: it is the **condition for thermal equilibrium**. So $\partial S/\partial E$ must be some function of the temperature alone, and the choice that matches both the ideal-gas temperature and the thermodynamic (Carnot) temperature is (notes pp. 5–6)

<Formula label="Statistical definition of temperature" tex="\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}\right)_{\delta W = 0} = \frac1T" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

For large systems $p(E_1)$ is extremely sharp (a remark of this page, illustrated below), so the most probable energy is, for all purposes, the energy.

## The second law

**An isolated system** (slides 6–7; notes p. 6). Now let the two systems start at different temperatures: system 1 has energy $E_1$ just before contact and $\bar E_1$ once equilibrium is reached (the slides and notes mark it with a star). Since $\bar E_1$ is the most probable value, $p(E_1) \le p(\bar E_1)$, with equality only if they were already in equilibrium. In terms of entropy this reads

<Formula label="Entropy cannot decrease" tex="0 \le \left[S_1(\bar E_1) - S_1(E_1)\right] + \left[S_2(E - \bar E_1) - S_2(E - E_1)\right] = \Delta S_1 + \Delta S_2 = \Delta S" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

> Left to itself, an isolated system can only gain entropy; once it is in equilibrium its entropy no longer changes: $\Delta S \ge 0$.

**Contact with a bath** (slide 8; notes pp. 6–7). Now make system 2 enormous, so that no amount of heat from system 1 shifts its temperature. Such a system is a **bath** (reservoir), at temperature $T_{\text{bath}}$. No work is done, so by the definition of temperature $dS_2 = dE_2/T_2 = \delta Q_2/T_2 = -\delta Q_1/T_{\text{bath}}$, and $dS_1 + dS_2 \ge 0$ becomes

<Formula label="Second law with a heat bath" tex="dS_1 \ge \frac{\delta Q_1}{T_{\text{bath}}}" cite="mit" at="slide 8" />

with equality for a system that stays in equilibrium with the bath, where $dS = \delta Q/T$. The two inequalities together are the **second law of thermodynamics** (notes p. 7). Equality needs a **quasi-static** process, one kept arbitrarily close to equilibrium, which is also what the work formulas of earlier lectures assume (slide 13).

## The ideal monatomic gas

From the previous lecture, $\Phi = V^N(4\pi emE/3N)^{3N/2}$ for large $N$ (slide 9):

<Formula label="Entropy of the ideal monatomic gas" tex="S(E, V, N) = kN\ln\left[V\left(\frac{4\pi emE}{3N}\right)^{3/2}\right]" cite="mit" at="slide 9" />

(Using $\Omega$ instead adds $k\ln(3N\Delta/2E)$, of order $\ln N$ and negligible; the slide leaves out the factor $k$ on this term.)

**The energy relation** (slide 10). Holding $N$ and $V$ fixed, no work is done, so

<Formula label="Energy of the ideal gas" tex="\frac1T = \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}\right)_{N,V} = \frac{\frac32Nk}{E} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E = \frac32NkT, \qquad C_V = \frac32Nk" cite="mit" at="slide 10" />

since here the internal energy $U$ is $E$. The temperature defined from the entropy agrees with the ideal-gas temperature.

**The adiabatic condition** (slide 11). A quasi-static adiabatic process has $\delta Q = 0$, hence $\Delta S = 0$. Eliminating $E$ with the energy relation, $S = kN\ln[V(2\pi emkT)^{3/2}]$, so at fixed $N$

<Formula label="Adiabat of the ideal monatomic gas" tex="VT^{3/2} = \text{constant}" cite="mit" at="slide 11" />

the same as $PV^{5/3}$ = constant from Lecture 6, now derived from counting states.

**The logic** (slide 12). The microcanonical ensemble (fixed $E$, equal a priori probabilities) gives the microscopic probability densities; adding the definition of entropy gives the temperature scale and the second-law inequalities.

<WorkedExample title="Sharing energy between two gases">
<Problem>
One mole of helium (system 1) and two moles of helium (system 2), each at fixed volume, share a total energy of 3 kJ. At first system 1 has $E_1 = 2$ kJ and system 2 has 1 kJ. Use $R = N_Ak = 8.314$ J/K per mole. (a) Find the initial temperatures. (b) Find the equilibrium energies and temperature. (c) Find the entropy change. (d) Separately, one mole of helium at 300 K is compressed quasi-statically and adiabatically to one eighth of its volume. Find its final temperature.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Before contact">
$T = 2E/(3nR)$ for $n$ moles: $T_1 = 2 \times 2000/(3 \times 8.314) \approx 160.4$ K and $T_2 = 2 \times 1000/(3 \times 2 \times 8.314) \approx 40.1$ K.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Equilibrium">
Equal $\partial S/\partial E = \frac32Nk/E$ means equal energy per particle, so $\bar E_1 = 3 \times \frac13 = 1$ kJ and $\bar E_2 = 2$ kJ, at $T = 2 \times 1000/(3 \times 8.314) \approx 80.2$ K.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Entropy change">
At fixed $V$ and $N$, $\Delta S = \frac32Nk\ln(E_{\text{final}}/E_{\text{initial}})$ for each gas. System 1: $12.47 \ln\frac12 \approx -8.64$ J/K. System 2: $24.94\ln 2 \approx +17.29$ J/K. Total $\Delta S \approx +8.64$ J/K, positive as the second law demands: the cold gas gains more entropy than the hot gas loses.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Adiabatic compression">
$VT^{3/2}$ constant gives $T_2 = 300 \times 8^{2/3} = 1200$ K.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Entropy: $S = k\ln\Omega$ ($\approx k\ln\rho \approx k\ln\Phi$); a state function, extensive, a logarithmic count of microstates.
- Two systems sharing energy: the most probable division has equal $\partial S/\partial E$; this is thermal equilibrium, and $1/T = (\partial S/\partial E)$ with no work defines temperature.
- Second law: $\Delta S \ge 0$ for an isolated system; $dS \ge \delta Q/T_{\text{bath}}$ in contact with a bath; equality only for quasi-static processes.
- Ideal monatomic gas: $S = kN\ln[V(4\pi emE/3N)^{3/2}]$ gives $E = \frac32NkT$, $C_V = \frac32Nk$ and $VT^{3/2}$ = constant on an adiabat.
