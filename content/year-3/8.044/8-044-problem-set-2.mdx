## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `3*x^2*exp(-x/x0)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent, so where letters are related (as $\sigma^2 = kT/m$ in Problem 4) the problem says which of them to use. Type `exp(...)` for an exponential, `sqrt(...)` for a square root and `pi` for $\pi$. Write the symbols as the box names them: `x0`, `x1`, `x2` for $x_0$, $x_1$, $x_2$, `rho` for $\rho$, `pr` for the value $p_r\left(\sqrt{GM/a}\right)$ in Problem 3(b), `sigma` for $\sigma$, `eta` for $\eta$, and `r` for $r_\perp$ in Problem 6.

The points per problem and per part are ours. Parts that ask for a sketch or for an argument have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons. A conditional density is written $p(x \text{ given } y)$, where MIT's sheet writes the same thing with a vertical bar between the two letters. Where two densities in one problem could be confused, a subscript says whose density it is: $p_r$ is the density of $r$ and $p_a$ that of $a$. Capital $P$ is a cumulative distribution.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Two quantum particles" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

Two particles, 1 and 2, move along a line. A possible wavefunction for the pair is

<Formula label="Wavefunction of the two particles" tex="\psi(x_1, x_2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}\,x_0^2}\left(x_2 - x_1\right)\exp\left(-\frac{x_1^2 + x_2^2}{2x_0^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

where $x_0$ is a fixed length. The joint probability density for finding particle 1 at $x_1$ and particle 2 at $x_2$ is the square of the size of the wavefunction, $|\psi|^2$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the joint density $p(x_1, x_2)$. Sketch the plane of $x_1$ and $x_2$ and mark where the density is highest and where it is lowest.

The second box is our addition: the density has two equal maxima, at $x_1 = -x_2 = \pm\,c\,x_0$. Find the number $c$.

<Hint>

The wavefunction is real, so $|\psi|^2$ is its square. Squaring an exponential doubles its exponent.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the sketch: where is the factor $\left(x_2 - x_1\right)^2$ zero? The exponential depends only on the distance from the origin, so on a circle around the origin only that factor changes. Where on the circle is it largest?

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the line $x_2 = -x_1$ the density is a function of $x_1$ alone. Set its derivative to zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-max" label="$c =$" value="0.707" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-max" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The density is the square of the wavefunction. Its shape follows from its two factors: one vanishes on a line, the other falls off in every direction.

<Step title="The joint density">

<Formula label="Joint density of the two positions" tex="p(x_1, x_2) = \left|\psi(x_1, x_2)\right|^2 = \frac{1}{\pi x_0^2}\left(\frac{x_1 - x_2}{x_0}\right)^2\exp\left(-\frac{x_1^2 + x_2^2}{x_0^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Where it is lowest and highest">

The density is never negative, and it is zero on the whole line $x_1 = x_2$: the two particles are never found at the same place. That line is where the density is lowest. It also dies away far from the origin in every direction.

The exponential depends only on $x_1^2 + x_2^2$, the squared distance from the origin. On a circle around the origin it is constant, and the other factor, $\left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2$, is largest where the circle crosses the line $x_2 = -x_1$. So the maxima lie on that line. There the density is

<Formula label="Density along the line of the maxima" tex="p(x_1, -x_1) = \frac{4x_1^2}{\pi x_0^4}\exp\left(-\frac{2x_1^2}{x_0^2}\right), \qquad \frac{d}{dx_1}\,p(x_1, -x_1) = 0 \quad\text{at}\quad x_1^2 = \frac{x_0^2}{2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (MIT gives the sketch; the calculation of the positions is ours)" />

The two maxima are at $(x_1, x_2) = \left(x_0/\sqrt2, -x_0/\sqrt2\right)$ and $\left(-x_0/\sqrt2, x_0/\sqrt2\right)$, so $c = 1/\sqrt2 = 0.707$: one particle on each side of the origin.

  curves={[

    { x: "(s - sqrt(max(log(2*s^2) + 1 - s^2, 0)))/sqrt(2)", y: "(-sqrt(max(log(2*s^2) + 1 - s^2, 0)) - s)/sqrt(2)", range: [0.48163, 1.63656] },
    { x: "(-s + sqrt(max(log(2*s^2) + 1 - s^2, 0)))/sqrt(2)", y: "(sqrt(max(log(2*s^2) + 1 - s^2, 0)) + s)/sqrt(2)", range: [0.48163, 1.63656] },
    { x: "(-s - sqrt(max(log(2*s^2) + 1 - s^2, 0)))/sqrt(2)", y: "(-sqrt(max(log(2*s^2) + 1 - s^2, 0)) + s)/sqrt(2)", range: [0.48163, 1.63656] }

  points={[{ x: 0.7071, y: -0.7071, label: "max", offCurve: true }, { x: -0.7071, y: 0.7071, label: "max", offCurve: true }]}
  caption="The plane of the two positions, computed from the joint density. On the dashed line the density is zero. The two closed curves are where the density has half its greatest value. The marked points are the two maxima." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The density has the unit of one over a length squared, as a density in two positions must. Integrated over the whole plane it gives 1: the average of $\left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2 = x_1^2 - 2x_1x_2 + x_2^2$ over the Gaussian factor is $\frac12x_0^2 - 0 + \frac12x_0^2 = x_0^2$, which cancels the $x_0^2$ below it.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

Find the probability density for finding particle 1 at the position $x$ along the line, wherever particle 2 is. Do the same for particle 2. Are the two positions statistically independent?

<Hint>

The density of one variable alone is the joint density integrated over all values of the other.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Expand $\left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2$ into three terms before you integrate over $x_2$. You need $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-u^2/x_0^2}\,du = \sqrt\pi\,x_0$, and the same integral with $u$ or with $u^2$ in front. One of them vanishes by symmetry.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For independence, compare the joint density with the product of the two single densities. Look at the line $x_1 = x_2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-marg" label="$p(x) =$" expr="(x^2/x0^2 + 1/2)/(sqrt(pi)*x0)*exp(-x^2/x0^2)"
  vars={{ x: [-1.5, 1.5], x0: [0.7, 1.5] }}
  names={{ x0: "x_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-x^2/x0^2)/(sqrt(pi)*x0)", hint: "That treats the factor (x₁ − x₂)² as if it did not contain x₁. Expand it first: one of its three terms is x₁², which stays outside the integral over x₂." },
    { expr: "(x^2/x0^2 + 1)/(sqrt(pi)*x0)*exp(-x^2/x0^2)", hint: "Check the integral with x₂² in front of the Gaussian: it is half of x₀² times the plain Gaussian integral. As it stands your density integrates to 3/2, not 1." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-indep" label="The two positions are" choices={["independent: the two single densities have the same form", "not independent: the joint density is not the product of the two single densities", "independent: the particles do not interact", "not independent, but only because the particles repel each other with a force"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Integrate the joint density over the position that is not asked for. Then test whether the joint density factors.

<Step title="Integrating out particle 2">

<Formula label="Reduction to the position of particle 1" tex="p(x_1) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(x_1, x_2)\,dx_2 = \frac{1}{\pi x_0^4}\,e^{-x_1^2/x_0^2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(x_1^2 - 2x_1x_2 + x_2^2\right)e^{-x_2^2/x_0^2}\,dx_2" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

The three Gaussian integrals are $\sqrt\pi\,x_0$ for the term without $x_2$, zero for the term with one factor $x_2$ (it is odd) and $\frac12x_0^2\sqrt\pi\,x_0$ for the term with $x_2^2$. So

<Formula label="Density of the position of one particle" tex="p(x_1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi\,x_0}\left(\frac{x_1^2}{x_0^2} + \frac12\right)\exp\left(-\frac{x_1^2}{x_0^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

The joint density does not change when $x_1$ and $x_2$ are swapped, so particle 2 has the same density, with $x_2$ in place of $x_1$ (MIT's solutions p. 2). The box takes either, with $x$ for the position.

</Step>

<Step title="Independence">

Two variables are independent when the joint density is the product of the single densities. Here $p(x_1)\,p(x_2)$ is positive everywhere, while $p(x_1, x_2)$ is zero on the line $x_1 = x_2$. They are not equal, so the positions are **not** statistically independent. This has nothing to do with a force: no interaction between the particles was assumed anywhere.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The single density integrates to 1: the term with $x_1^2/x_0^2$ gives $\frac12$ and the term with $\frac12$ gives $\frac12$. It is not a plain Gaussian: the extra term $x_1^2/x_0^2$ pushes probability away from the centre.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={7}>

Find the conditional density $p(x_1 \text{ given } x_2)$ that particle 1 is at $x_1$ when particle 2 is known to be at $x_2$. Sketch it.

The second box is our addition: find the mean position of particle 1 when particle 2 is known to be at $x_2$.

<Hint>

Bayes' theorem of the lesson: the conditional density is the joint density divided by the density of the variable that is known.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the sketch, think of $x_2$ as a fixed number, a little to the right of the origin. Where is the conditional density zero? On which side of that point does the Gaussian leave more room?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the mean, multiply the conditional density by $x_1$ and integrate over $x_1$. Expand $\left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2$ again. Only one of the three terms survives.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-cond" label="$p(x_1 \text{ given } x_2) =$" expr="2*(x1 - x2)^2/(sqrt(pi)*x0^3*(1 + 2*x2^2/x0^2))*exp(-x1^2/x0^2)"
  vars={{ x1: [-1.5, 1.5], x2: [-1.5, 1.5], x0: [0.7, 1.5] }}
  names={{ x1: "x_1", x2: "x_2", x0: "x_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*(x1 - x2)^2/(sqrt(pi)*x0^3*(1 + 2*x1^2/x0^2))*exp(-x2^2/x0^2)", hint: "That is the density of x₂ given x₁. Divide by the density of the position that is known, which is x₂." },
    { expr: "(x1 - x2)^2/(pi*x0^4)*exp(-(x1^2 + x2^2)/x0^2)", hint: "That is still the joint density. A slice of it at fixed x₂ does not integrate to 1 over x₁: divide by the density of x₂." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-mean" label="$\langle x_1\rangle$ given $x_2$ $=$" expr="-2*x2*x0^2/(x0^2 + 2*x2^2)"
  vars={{ x2: [0.3, 1.5], x0: [0.7, 1.5] }}
  names={{ x2: "x_2", x0: "x_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*x2*x0^2/(x0^2 + 2*x2^2)", hint: "Check the sign of the cross term: (x₁ − x₂)² holds −2x₁x₂. The mean of particle 1 lies on the other side of the origin from particle 2." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "That is the mean of particle 1 when nothing is known about particle 2. Knowing x₂ shifts it, because the positions are not independent." },
    { expr: "x2", hint: "Particle 1 is never found at x₂: the conditional density is zero there." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Slice the joint density at the known value of $x_2$ and divide by $p(x_2)$, which makes the slice integrate to 1.

<Step title="The conditional density">

<Formula label="Conditional density of the position of particle 1" tex="p(x_1 \text{ given } x_2) = \frac{p(x_1, x_2)}{p(x_2)} = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi\,x_0}\;\frac{1}{1 + 2\left(x_2/x_0\right)^2}\left(\frac{x_1 - x_2}{x_0}\right)^2\exp\left(-\frac{x_1^2}{x_0^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2" />

The factor $e^{-x_2^2/x_0^2}$ has cancelled. As a function of $x_1$ this is a Gaussian centred on the origin, times a parabola that is zero at $x_1 = x_2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The sketch">

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-3.5, 3.5]} range={[0, 0.75]} xLabel="x₁ / x₀" yLabel="x₀ p(x₁ given x₂)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "2*(x - b)^2/(sqrt(pi)*(1 + 2*b^2))*exp(-x^2)", label: "p" }
  ]}
  params={{ b: { value: 0.5, min: -2, max: 2, step: 0.1, label: "x_2/x_0" } }}
  caption="The conditional density of particle 1, computed from the formula above, for a chosen position of particle 2 (the slider). It is zero where particle 2 sits. With particle 2 to the right of the origin, most of the probability for particle 1 is to the left, and the other way round." />

For $x_2 > 0$ there is a large hump on the left and a small one on the right of $x_2$, as in MIT's sketch. For $x_2 = 0$ the two humps are equal. MIT's solutions put it this way: the particles avoid each other.

</Step>

<Step title="The mean position (our addition)">

Multiply by $x_1$ and integrate. Of $x_1\left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2 = x_1^3 - 2x_2x_1^2 + x_2^2x_1$ only the middle term is even in $x_1$, and its Gaussian integral is $-2x_2\cdot\frac12x_0^2\sqrt\pi\,x_0$:

<Formula label="Mean position of particle 1 when particle 2 is known" tex="\langle x_1\rangle \text{ given } x_2 = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi\,x_0^3}\;\frac{-x_2\,x_0^3\sqrt\pi}{1 + 2\left(x_2/x_0\right)^2} = -\frac{2x_2\,x_0^2}{x_0^2 + 2x_2^2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (the density is MIT's; the mean is our addition)" />

The mean has the opposite sign to $x_2$: the number behind "they avoid each other". For $|x_2|$ much larger than $x_0$ it tends to $-x_0^2/x_2$, which goes to zero: a far-away particle 2 hardly matters.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The conditional density integrates to 1 over $x_1$: the average of $\left(x_1 - x_2\right)^2$ over the Gaussian is $\frac12x_0^2 + x_2^2$, and $\frac{2}{x_0^2}\left(\frac12x_0^2 + x_2^2\right) = 1 + 2\left(x_2/x_0\right)^2$ cancels the denominator.

MIT's solutions (p. 2) add where such a wavefunction comes from. It describes two identical particles without spin that obey Fermi–Dirac statistics and do not interact, in a harmonic oscillator potential: one in the ground state and one in the first excited state. The wavefunction changes sign when the two particles are swapped, and that alone keeps them apart. For particles that obey Bose–Einstein statistics the factor $x_2 - x_1$ would be replaced by $x_1 + x_2$, and the two could well be found close together. A later lesson, [quantum states of many identical particles](/courses/8-044/identical-particles), takes this up.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A pyramidal density" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

Two random variables $x$ and $y$ have the joint density

<Formula label="The pyramidal joint density" tex="p(x, y) = 6\left(1 - x - y\right) \quad\text{if}\quad x \ge 0,\ y \ge 0 \text{ and } x + y \le 1, \qquad p(x, y) = 0 \quad\text{otherwise}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

Drawn as a height above the plane of $x$ and $y$, it is a pyramid: its base is the triangle with corners $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, and its top, of height 6, is above the origin.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the density $p(x)$ of $x$ alone, for $0 \le x \le 1$. Sketch it.

<Hint>

Integrate the joint density over $y$. The work is in the limits.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Draw the triangle and a thin vertical strip at a fixed $x$. Between which two values of $y$ does the strip lie inside the triangle?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-px" label="$p(x) =$" expr="3*(1 - x)^2"
  vars={{ x: [0.05, 0.95] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3 - 6*x", hint: "That integrates y from 0 to 1. But the density is zero above the line y = 1 − x, and the formula 6(1 − x − y) is even negative there." },
    { expr: "6*(1 - x)^2", hint: "Put the upper limit into both terms: (1 − x)y gives (1 − x)², and y²/2 takes half of that away." },
    { expr: "6*(1 - x)", hint: "That is the joint density on the edge y = 0, not its integral over y." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The density of $x$ alone is the area of the cut through the pyramid at that $x$.

<Step title="The limits">

At a fixed $x$ between 0 and 1 the joint density differs from zero only for $0 \le y \le 1 - x$. MIT's solutions (p. 3) draw the triangle with a vertical strip to make this clear.

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

<Formula label="Density of x alone for the pyramid" tex="p(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p(x, y)\,dy = 6\int_0^{1-x}\left(1 - x - y\right)dy = 6\left[(1 - x)^2 - \frac12(1 - x)^2\right] = 3\left(1 - x\right)^2, \qquad 0 \le x \le 1" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(a), p. 3" />

Outside that range $p(x) = 0$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 1]} range={[0, 3.2]} xLabel="x" yLabel="p(x)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "3*(1 - x)^2", label: "p(x)" }
  ]}
  caption="The density of x alone, computed from the formula above: a parabola that falls from 3 at x = 0 to zero, with zero slope, at x = 1." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\int_0^13\left(1 - x\right)^2dx = 1$, as it must be. The cut at fixed $x$ is a triangle of base $1 - x$ and height $6(1 - x)$, with area $\frac12\cdot(1 - x)\cdot6(1 - x) = 3(1 - x)^2$: the same result without an integral.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the conditional density $p(y \text{ given } x)$, for $0 \le y \le 1 - x$. Sketch it.

The second box is our addition: are $x$ and $y$ statistically independent?

<Hint>

Divide the joint density by the result of part (a).

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the sketch, $x$ is a fixed number. What kind of function of $y$ is left, and where does it start and end?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-cond" label="$p(y \text{ given } x) =$" expr="2*(1 - x - y)/(1 - x)^2"
  vars={{ x: [0.05, 0.5], y: [0.05, 0.45] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*(1 - x - y)/(1 - y)^2", hint: "That is the density of x given y. Here x is the known variable, so divide by p(x)." },
    { expr: "6*(1 - x - y)", hint: "That is the joint density. Integrated over y at fixed x it gives p(x), not 1: divide by p(x)." }

<Answer id="p2-indep" label="$x$ and $y$ are" choices={["independent: the joint density treats x and y in the same way", "independent: p(y given x) is a straight line in y for every x", "not independent: the conditional density of y changes with x"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The conditional density is the cut through the pyramid at the known $x$, scaled to area 1.

<Step title="The conditional density">

<Formula label="Conditional density of y for the pyramid" tex="p(y \text{ given } x) = \frac{p(x, y)}{p(x)} = \frac{6\left(1 - x - y\right)}{3\left(1 - x\right)^2} = \frac{2}{\left(1 - x\right)^2}\left(1 - x - y\right), \qquad 0 \le y \le 1 - x" cite="reading1" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3" />

It is a straight line in $y$, falling from $2/(1 - x)$ at $y = 0$ to zero at $y = 1 - x$.

<Solution>

**The idea.** The nearest neighbour is between $r$ and $r + dr$ when the sphere of radius $r$ is empty and the thin shell around it holds a star. For stars placed at random these two events are independent.

<Step title="An empty sphere">

Stars spread at random are a Poisson process in three dimensions: the number $n$ in a volume $V$ has the Poisson density with mean $\langle n\rangle = \rho V$. For a sphere of radius $r$, $\langle n\rangle = \frac43\pi\rho r^3$, and

<Formula label="Probability of no star within a distance" tex="p(\text{no star within } r) = p(n = 0) = \exp\left(-\frac43\pi\rho r^3\right)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(a), p. 4" />

</Step>

<Step title="A star in the shell">

The shell between $r$ and $r + dr$ has the volume $4\pi r^2dr$, so small that the mean number of stars in it, $4\pi\rho r^2dr$, is far below one. Then the probability of one star there is that mean number itself, and two or more stars can be neglected (MIT's solutions p. 4).

</Step>

<Step title="The product">

<Formula label="Density of the distance to the nearest star" tex="p_r(r)\,dr = \exp\left(-\frac43\pi\rho r^3\right)\times4\pi\rho r^2\,dr \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p_r(r) = 4\pi\rho\,r^2\exp\left(-\frac43\pi\rho r^3\right), \qquad r \ge 0" cite="reading1" at="Problem 3(a), p. 4" />

</Step>

<Step title="The most likely distance (our addition)">

The derivative of $p_r$ is zero where $2r - 4\pi\rho r^4 = 0$, so at $r = \left(2\pi\rho\right)^{-1/3}$. For $\rho = 0.004$ per cubic light year this is $\left(0.02513\right)^{-1/3} = 3.41$ light years.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The cumulative distribution is $P_r(r) = 1 - \exp\left(-\frac43\pi\rho r^3\right)$, the probability that the sphere is not empty. It rises from 0 to 1, so $p_r$ is normalized, and its derivative is the $p_r$ above. The unit of $p_r$ is that of $\rho r^2$: one over a length.

<Formula label="Cumulative distribution of the energy in three dimensions" tex="P(E) = \frac{4\pi}{\left(2\pi\sigma^2\right)^{3/2}}\int_0^{\sqrt{2E/m}}v^2\,e^{-v^2/2\sigma^2}\,dv" cite="reading1" at="Problem 4(a), p. 6" />

The second and third boxes are our additions. At which speed is $p_{v_x}$ largest? At which position $s$ is $p_s$ largest?

<Hint>

The speed is a function of the random variable $s$: $v_x = \sqrt{A/s}$. It falls as $s$ grows. Which positions $s$ belong to speeds below a chosen value $\eta$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

$P_{v_x}(\eta)$ is the integral of $p_s(\zeta)$ from $\zeta = A/\eta^2$ to infinity. Do not try to do the integral: differentiate it with respect to $\eta$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For a peak, set the derivative to zero. It is easier to differentiate the logarithm of the density.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-pv" label="$p_{v_x}(\eta) =$" expr="2*eta^2/(sqrt(2*pi)*sigma^3)*exp(-eta^2/(2*sigma^2))"
  vars={{ eta: [0.3, 3], sigma: [0.5, 2], A: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ eta: "\\eta", sigma: "\\sigma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "eta^5/(sqrt(2*pi)*A*sigma^3)*exp(-eta^2/(2*sigma^2))", hint: "That is p_s with ζ = A/η² put in. The derivative of the limit A/η² with respect to η is missing. The result should not contain A." },
    { expr: "eta^2/(sqrt(2*pi)*sigma^3)*exp(-eta^2/(2*sigma^2))", hint: "A factor 2 is lost: the derivative of A/η² is −2A/η³. As it stands your density integrates to one half." },
    { expr: "-2*eta^2/(sqrt(2*pi)*sigma^3)*exp(-eta^2/(2*sigma^2))", hint: "A density is never negative. A/η² is the lower limit of the integral, and that minus sign cancels the one from its derivative." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p5-vpeak" label="$p_{v_x}$ is largest at $\eta =$" expr="sqrt(2)*sigma"
  vars={{ sigma: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ sigma: "\\sigma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sigma", hint: "That is where η exp(−η²/2σ²) peaks. Here the factor in front is η²." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "A Gaussian alone peaks at zero, but the factor η² makes this density vanish there." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p5-speak" label="$p_s$ is largest at $s =$" expr="A/(5*sigma^2)"
  vars={{ sigma: [0.5, 2], A: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ sigma: "\\sigma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "A/(2*sigma^2)", hint: "That is how far an atom with the most likely speed falls. The peak of p_s is somewhere else: the factor between the two densities changes with s and shifts the peak." },
    { expr: "A/sigma^2", hint: "That is how far an atom with the speed σ falls. To find the peak, differentiate p_s itself, or its logarithm." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Fast atoms land high and slow atoms land low. So "speed below $\eta$" means "position beyond $A/\eta^2$", and the density of the speed follows by differentiating that statement.

<Step title="The cumulative distribution">

Speeds are never negative here. The speed is at most $\eta$ when $s \ge A/\eta^2$:

<Formula label="Cumulative distribution of the speed at the source" tex="P_{v_x}(\eta) = \frac{A^{3/2}}{\sigma^2\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\int_{A/\eta^2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\zeta^{5/2}}\,e^{-A/(2\sigma^2\zeta)}\,d\zeta" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5, p. 9" />

</Step>

<Step title="The derivative">

Only the lower limit depends on $\eta$. At $\zeta = A/\eta^2$ the integrand is $\left(\eta^2/A\right)^{5/2}e^{-\eta^2/2\sigma^2}$, and the limit has the derivative $-2A/\eta^3$:

<Formula label="Density of the speed at the source" tex="p_{v_x}(\eta) = -\frac{A^{3/2}}{\sigma^2\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\left(\frac{\eta^2}{A}\right)^{5/2}e^{-\eta^2/2\sigma^2}\,\frac{d}{d\eta}\frac{A}{\eta^2} = \frac{2\eta^2}{\sigma^2\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\;e^{-\eta^2/2\sigma^2}, \qquad \eta \ge 0" cite="reading1" at="Problem 5, p. 9" />

The constant $A$ has dropped out: the speeds at the source cannot depend on where the target stands.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 5]} range={[0, 0.65]} xLabel="vₓ / σ" yLabel="σ p(vₓ)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "2*x^2/sqrt(2*pi)*exp(-x^2/2)", label: "p" }
  ]}
  caption="The density of the speed at the source, computed from the formula above. It starts at zero as the square of the speed and falls off as a Gaussian." />

</Step>

<Step title="The two peaks (our addition)">

The logarithm of $p_{v_x}$ is $2\ln\eta - \eta^2/2\sigma^2$ plus a constant. Its derivative, $2/\eta - \eta/\sigma^2$, is zero at $\eta = \sqrt2\,\sigma$.

The logarithm of $p_s$ is $-\frac52\ln\zeta - A/(2\sigma^2\zeta)$ plus a constant. Its derivative, $-\frac{5}{2\zeta} + \frac{A}{2\sigma^2\zeta^2}$, is zero at $\zeta = A/(5\sigma^2)$.

An atom with the most likely speed falls $A/(2\sigma^2)$, which is not where most atoms land per unit of $s$. The two peaks do not belong to each other, because the factor between the two densities, $|ds/dv_x|$, is not constant.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0.01, 1.5]} range={[0, 2]} xLabel="s σ² / A" yLabel="(A / σ²) p(s)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "x^(-5/2)*exp(-1/(2*x))/sqrt(2*pi)", label: "p" }
  ]}
  caption="The fitted density of the position on the target, computed from the formula in the problem. It is almost exactly zero near s = 0 (very fast atoms are rare), peaks at one fifth of A/σ², and has a long tail of slow atoms." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$p_{v_x}$ integrates to 1: $\int_0^\infty\eta^2e^{-\eta^2/2\sigma^2}d\eta = \frac12\sigma^2\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}$. Its unit is one over a speed, since $\sigma$ is a speed. The shape of the second graph, a steep rise and a long tail, is that of the measured curve on MIT's sheet, whose peak is near $s = 0.1$ mm.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Planetary nebulae" points={15} mit="Problem 6">

Late in its life a star of low mass can blow off a large part of its mass as a spherical shell of hot gas that expands. Radiation from the hot star in the middle keeps the shell glowing. Astronomers call it a planetary nebula (a name with a history: it has nothing to do with planets). In a photograph it often shows as a bright ring. Why does a shell look like a ring? MIT puts the same question another way: what shadow does a half-transparent balloon cast?

A photograph records how much matter lies at each distance $r_\perp$ from the line of sight, the line from the observer through the central star. Assume the atoms of the gas are spread uniformly over a spherical shell of radius $R$ around the star. Find the probability density $p(r_\perp)$ that a given atom lies at the perpendicular distance $r_\perp$ from the line of sight, for $0 \le r_\perp \le R$.

MIT's hint: use spherical coordinates with the angle $\theta$ measured from the line of sight. Then $r_\perp = R\sin\theta$ for an atom on the shell, and $P(r_\perp)$ is the fraction of the sphere on which $R\sin\theta$ is below $r_\perp$.

The second box is our addition: which fraction of the atoms is seen in the outer rim, at $r_\perp > 0.9R$?

<Hint>

Uniform over the sphere means: the probability of a patch is its area divided by $4\pi R^2$. A band between $\theta$ and $\theta + d\theta$ has the area $2\pi R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta$. The angle $\theta$ itself is not uniform.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Where on the sphere is $R\sin\theta$ below a chosen value? Near the point facing the observer, and also near the point on the far side: two caps.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Add the areas of the two caps, divide by the area of the sphere, and differentiate with respect to $r_\perp$. For the rim you do not need the density: $P(r_\perp)$ is enough.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-pr" label="$p(r_\perp) =$" expr="r/(R*sqrt(R^2 - r^2))"
  vars={{ r: [0.1, 0.9], R: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ r: "r_\\perp" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2/(pi*sqrt(R^2 - r^2))", hint: "That takes the angle θ as uniform between 0 and π. Uniform over the sphere means uniform in area, and a band at θ has an area proportional to sin θ." },
    { expr: "r/(2*R*sqrt(R^2 - r^2))", hint: "That counts one cap only. The far side of the shell is seen too: atoms near θ = π are as close to the line of sight as those near θ = 0." },
    { expr: "2*r/R^2", hint: "That is a filled disk, uniform on the sky. A shell seen from the side puts far more matter per unit of sky near its edge." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p6-rim" label="Fraction at $r_\perp > 0.9R$ $=$" value="0.436" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-rim" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $r_\perp$ is a function of the random position of an atom on the sphere. It is small on two caps, one facing the observer and one on the far side. The areas of the caps give $P(r_\perp)$.

<Step title="The density on the sphere">

An atom on the shell is located by the angles $\theta$ and $\phi$. Uniform over the sphere means a probability $\frac{1}{4\pi}\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$ for the patch $d\theta\,d\phi$ (MIT's solutions p. 10 write this as a density $1/4\pi$ per unit of solid angle).

</Step>

<Step title="The cumulative distribution">

$R\sin\theta \le r_\perp$ holds for $0 \le \theta \le \sin^{-1}(r_\perp/R)$ and for $\pi - \sin^{-1}(r_\perp/R) \le \theta \le \pi$. The two caps are equal, and $\cos\left(\sin^{-1}(r_\perp/R)\right) = \sqrt{1 - r_\perp^2/R^2}$:

<Formula label="Cumulative distribution of the perpendicular distance" tex="P(r_\perp) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\left(\int_0^{\sin^{-1}(r_\perp/R)}\sin\theta\,d\theta + \int_{\pi - \sin^{-1}(r_\perp/R)}^{\pi}\sin\theta\,d\theta\right) = 1 - \sqrt{1 - \frac{r_\perp^2}{R^2}}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6, p. 10" />

(MIT's solutions print the second cap as running from $\sin^{-1}(r_\perp/R)$ to $\pi$, in the text and in the integral: a slip. With that limit the bracket would give 2 for every $r_\perp$, so $P = 1$. The result printed there is the right one.)

</Step>

<Step title="The density">

<Formula label="Density of the perpendicular distance for a shell" tex="p(r_\perp) = \frac{dP(r_\perp)}{dr_\perp} = \frac{r_\perp}{R\sqrt{R^2 - r_\perp^2}} \quad\text{for } 0 \le r_\perp \le R, \qquad p(r_\perp) = 0 \quad\text{otherwise}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 6, p. 10" />

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 0.999]} range={[0, 6]} xLabel="r⊥ / R" yLabel="R p(r⊥)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "x/sqrt(1 - x^2) <= 6 ? x/sqrt(1 - x^2) : NaN", label: "p" }
  ]}
  caption="The density of the perpendicular distance, computed from the formula above. It rises slowly from zero at the centre and without bound at the edge of the shell: the bright ring." />

The density grows without bound at $r_\perp = R$, where the line of sight runs along the shell and passes through much more gas. That is the ring.

</Step>

<Step title="The rim (our addition)">

The fraction of the atoms at $r_\perp > 0.9R$ is $1 - P(0.9R) = \sqrt{1 - 0.81} = 0.436$. For a disk filled uniformly on the sky it would be $1 - 0.81 = 0.19$: the shell puts more than twice as much in its outer rim.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$P$ rises from 0 at the centre to 1 at $r_\perp = R$, so the density is normalized although it is infinite at the edge. A second route: by a theorem of Archimedes, the area of a sphere between two planes perpendicular to an axis depends only on the distance between the planes. So the coordinate $z = R\cos\theta$ of an atom along the line of sight is uniform between $-R$ and $R$. With $r_\perp = \sqrt{R^2 - z^2}$, the distance is below $r_\perp$ when $|z| \ge \sqrt{R^2 - r_\perp^2}$, which has the probability $1 - \sqrt{R^2 - r_\perp^2}/R$: the same $P(r_\perp)$.

MIT's sheet adds that not every planetary nebula is as tidy as the two it shows: most have far less regular shapes.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>
