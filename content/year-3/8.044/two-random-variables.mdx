Physical questions often involve several random variables at once: the velocity components of an atom, or a noise voltage measured at two times. This lecture extends the one-variable tools to two variables and introduces three new ideas: reducing to one variable, conditional probability and statistical independence (slides 1–17; the slides' closing exam problem on collision products is left for the course's problem pages; notes pp. 19–34). (MIT's material for this lecture is Greytak's slides and his probability notes, Chapter 2; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The notes' figures are not reproduced. The notes write a conditional density with a vertical bar, p(x|y); this page writes it as p(x given y), which read-aloud can say.)

## Joint densities

Much carries over directly from one variable (notes p. 19; slides 2–3):

<Formula label="Joint density and distribution" tex="p_{x,y}(\zeta, \eta)\,d\zeta\,d\eta \equiv \text{prob}\left(\zeta < x \le \zeta + d\zeta \text{ and } \eta < y \le \eta + d\eta\right), \qquad P_{x,y}(\zeta, \eta) = \int_{-\infty}^{\zeta}\int_{-\infty}^{\eta}p_{x,y}(\zeta', \eta')\,d\zeta'\,d\eta'" cite="reading1" at="p. 19" />

with $p_{x,y} = \partial^2P_{x,y}/\partial\zeta\,\partial\eta$, and averages $\langle f(x, y)\rangle = \int\int f(\zeta, \eta)\,p_{x,y}(\zeta, \eta)\,d\zeta\,d\eta$.

**Reduction to a single variable** (notes p. 19). The density of $x$ regardless of $y$ comes from integrating over all $y$:

<Formula label="Reduction to one variable" tex="p_x(\zeta) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{x,y}(\zeta, \eta)\,d\eta" cite="reading1" at="p. 19" />

## Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

At the other extreme, suppose $y$ is known. The **conditional density** $p_x(\zeta \text{ given } y)$ is the density of $x$ given that $y$ has that value; in it $\zeta$ is the variable and $y$ only a parameter (notes pp. 19–20). Knowing $y$ restricts the possibilities to the line $\eta = y$ in the plane, so $p_x(\zeta \text{ given } y)$ must be proportional to the slice $p_{x,y}(\zeta, y)$. Integrating over $\zeta$ fixes the constant as $p_y(y)$ (notes p. 20), which gives **Bayes' theorem**, the fundamental law of conditional probability (slides 4–6):

<Formula label="Bayes' theorem" tex="p(x, y) = p(x \text{ given } y)\,p(y) = p(y \text{ given } x)\,p(x), \qquad p(x \text{ given } y) = \frac{p(x, y)}{p(y)}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 20–21" />

Read one way, it finds a conditional density by slicing the joint density and renormalizing; read the other way, it rebuilds the joint density by stacking the conditional slices, weighted by the density of the conditioning variable, much as a loaf can be reassembled from its slices, in the notes' image (notes p. 21).

**Statistical independence** (notes p. 22; slide 6). Two variables are **statistically independent** (S.I.) if

<Formula label="Statistical independence" tex="p_{x,y}(\zeta, \eta) = p_x(\zeta)\,p_y(\eta), \qquad \text{equivalently} \quad p(x \text{ given } y) = p(x)" cite="reading1" at="p. 22" />

Knowing one then tells nothing more about the other than its own density already did.

**A uniform disk** (notes pp. 22–24; slides 7–8). For points spread uniformly over a disk (the notes take radius 1, density $1/\pi$; the slides radius $a$), integrating across the disk gives $p(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{1 - x^2}$ for $|x| \le 1$, and the same form for $y$. Their product is not $1/\pi$, so $x$ and $y$ are **not** independent. The conditional density is $p(x \text{ given } y) = 1/\left(2\sqrt{1 - y^2}\right)$ for $|x| \le \sqrt{1 - y^2}$: uniform along the chord at height $y$, as one would expect, but over a range that depends on $y$.

## Deriving the Poisson density

The tools are enough to derive the Poisson density quoted in Lecture 1 (notes pp. 24–27; slides 10–12). Events occur along a line with probability $r\,\Delta X$ for one event in a short stretch $\Delta X$, independently in different stretches.

**No events** (notes p. 25). Cut $L$ into pieces $dL$ so short that $p(0) \gg p(1) \gg p(n > 1)$, so $p(0) \approx 1 - r\,dL$. The pieces are independent, so the probability of no event anywhere is the product over the $L/dL$ pieces; taking the logarithm, $\ln p(0; L) \approx \sum(-r\,dL) = -rL$:

<Formula label="Probability of no events" tex="p(n = 0; L) = e^{-rL}" cite="reading1" at="p. 25" />

(This is not a density for $L$: it does not integrate to 1 over $L$. It is one entry of the density for $n$, with $L$ as a parameter.)

**A recursion** (notes p. 26). Extend the interval from $L$ to $L + \Delta L$. Having $n$ events in all means either $n$ in $L$ and none in $\Delta L$, or $n - 1$ in $L$ and one in $\Delta L$; so $p(n; L + \Delta L) = p(n; L)(1 - r\Delta L) + p(n - 1; L)\,r\Delta L$, and in the limit

<Formula label="Recursion for the Poisson probabilities" tex="\frac{d}{dL}p(n; L) + r\,p(n; L) = r\,p(n - 1; L)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 24 and 26" />

**The solution** (notes p. 27). Differentiating $p(n; L) = \frac{1}{n!}(rL)^ne^{-rL}$ gives exactly $r\,p(n - 1; L) - r\,p(n; L)$, and with the boundary conditions $p(0; L) = e^{-rL}$ and $p(n; 0) = 0$ for $n \ne 0$ the solution is unique. The slides reach the same result another way: put $n$ identical events into $N$ boxes of length $L/N$, count the arrangements (the binomial coefficient, N choose n), and let $N \to \infty$ (slides 11–12).

## Jointly Gaussian variables

A circuit with all sources off still shows a fluctuating voltage at high gain: **thermal** (Johnson) noise from the random motion of electrons, distinct from the shot noise of discrete charges (notes pp. 27–28). It has zero mean; a fairly well defined typical size; statistics that do not change in time; and a **correlation time** $\tau_c$, over which it changes little, with values much further apart statistically independent. Such a fluctuating signal is a **random process** (other examples: pressure or temperature fluctuations, thermal radiation fields, seismic background; slide 13). Sampling it at two times gives two random variables, often described by the **jointly Gaussian** density (notes p. 29; slide 14):

<Formula label="Jointly Gaussian density" tex="p(v_1, v_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{v_1^2 - 2\rho v_1v_2 + v_2^2}{2\sigma^2\left(1 - \rho^2\right)}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 29" />

$\sigma$ sets the size; the **correlation coefficient** $\rho$, with $|\rho| \le 1$, depends on the time separation $\tau = |t_2 - t_1|$, close to 1 for short separations and falling to 0 for long ones. Contours of constant probability are ellipses with their long axis along $v_1 = v_2$ for $\rho > 0$ (along $v_1 = -v_2$ for $\rho < 0$), long and narrow as $|\rho| \to 1$ and circles at $\rho = 0$ (notes p. 29).

From it (notes pp. 31–33): each voltage alone is a zero-mean Gaussian of variance $\sigma^2$, the same at all times; the two are independent only when $\rho = 0$; and

<Formula label="Conditional density of the second voltage" tex="p(v_2 \text{ given } v_1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2\left(1 - \rho^2\right)}}\exp\left(-\frac{\left(v_2 - \rho v_1\right)^2}{2\sigma^2\left(1 - \rho^2\right)}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 31" />

a Gaussian with mean $\rho v_1$ and variance $\sigma^2(1 - \rho^2)$. Integrating $v_2$ against this, and then $v_1$, gives the **correlation function** (notes p. 33):

<Formula label="Correlation function" tex="R(\tau) \equiv \langle v(t)\,v(t + \tau)\rangle = \sigma^2\rho(\tau)" cite="reading1" at="p. 33" />

so $\rho$ is the normalized correlation function. It need not stay positive: for noise from a circuit resonant near one frequency, say 500 Hz, a positive value tends to be followed by a negative one half a period (1 ms) later, and $\rho(\tau)$ oscillates as it decays (notes pp. 33–34).

<WorkedExample title="A triangle of probability">
<Problem>
(a) Points are spread uniformly over the triangle $0 < y < x < 1$, so $p(x, y) = 2$ there. Find $p(x)$, $p(y)$ and $p(x \text{ given } y)$. Are $x$ and $y$ independent? (b) Find $\langle x\rangle$, $\langle y\rangle$ and $\langle xy\rangle - \langle x\rangle\langle y\rangle$. (c) Two noise samples are jointly Gaussian with $\sigma = 1.0$ mV and $\rho = 0.8$; the first reads 1.5 mV. Find the mean and standard deviation of the second.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Densities">
$p(x) = \int_0^x2\,dy = 2x$ and $p(y) = \int_y^12\,dx = 2(1 - y)$, both on $(0, 1)$. Then $p(x \text{ given } y) = \frac{2}{2(1 - y)} = \frac{1}{1 - y}$ for $y < x < 1$: uniform, over a range set by $y$. Since $2x \cdot 2(1 - y) \ne 2$, they are not independent.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Averages">
$\langle x\rangle = \int_0^12x^2dx = \frac23$, $\langle y\rangle = \int_0^12y(1 - y)dy = \frac13$, and $\langle xy\rangle = \int_0^1\int_0^x2xy\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1x^3dx = \frac14$. So $\langle xy\rangle - \langle x\rangle\langle y\rangle = \frac14 - \frac29 = \frac{1}{36}$: positive, since a large $y$ forces a large $x$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Correlated noise">
The second reading has mean $\rho v_1 = 0.8 \times 1.5 = 1.2$ mV and standard deviation $\sigma\sqrt{1 - \rho^2} = 0.6$ mV, compared with 0 and 1.0 mV without the first reading.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A joint density $p(x, y)$ gives probabilities for pairs; integrating out $y$ gives $p(x)$.
- Bayes' theorem: $p(x, y) = p(x \text{ given } y)\,p(y)$; the conditional density is a renormalized slice.
- Statistical independence means $p(x, y) = p(x)p(y)$; points uniform in a disk are not independent in $x$ and $y$.
- Independence of short intervals yields the Poisson density through $\frac{dp(n)}{dL} = r\,p(n - 1) - r\,p(n)$; jointly Gaussian noise samples have conditional mean $\rho v_1$, variance $\sigma^2(1 - \rho^2)$, and $\langle v(t)v(t + \tau)\rangle = \sigma^2\rho(\tau)$.
