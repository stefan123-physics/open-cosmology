This lecture states the general facts about energy eigenstates in one dimension, then introduces the **variational principle**, a way to bound the ground-state energy without solving the Schrödinger equation (transcript pp. 1–23; notes sections 3–5). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's recorded lecture and his notes "Wave Mechanics"; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. Several results were met in 8.04, on the pages on [the shape of wavefunctions](/courses/8-04/shape-of-wavefunctions) and [the node theorem](/courses/8-04/delta-well-node-theorem-oscillator).)

## Bound states and four theorems

An energy eigenstate is a **bound state** if $\psi(x) \to 0$ as $|x| \to \infty$: it is not spread over all space (transcript p. 1). To tidy the constants, scale energy and potential by $2m/\hbar^2$ (written in calligraphic letters in the notes, here with a tilde) (transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Rescaled Schrödinger equation" tex="\psi'' + \left(\tilde E - \tilde V(x)\right)\psi = 0, \qquad \tilde E = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}E, \qquad \tilde V(x) = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}V(x)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (3.2), (3.3)" />

**Theorem 1: there is no degeneracy for bound states in one dimension** (transcript pp. 2–3). Strategy: suppose $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ are bound states with the same energy. Multiplying each equation by the other function and subtracting gives $(\psi_1\psi_2' - \psi_2\psi_1')' = 0$; the bracket is constant and vanishes at infinity, so $\psi_1 = c\psi_2$: the same state.

**Theorem 2: energy eigenstates can be chosen real** (transcript pp. 3–4). If $\psi$ is a complex solution, so is $\psi^*$ with the same energy, and so are the real combinations

<Formula label="Real and imaginary parts" tex="\psi_r = \frac12(\psi + \psi^*), \qquad \psi_{im} = \frac{1}{2i}(\psi - \psi^*)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.12)" />

**Corollary.** For bound states, Theorem 1 forces $\psi_{im} = c\,\psi_r$ with $c$ real, so $\psi = (1 + ic)\psi_r$: **any bound state is, up to a constant phase, a real function** (transcript pp. 4–5).

**Theorem 3: if $V(-x) = V(x)$, eigenstates can be chosen even or odd** (transcript pp. 5–6). If $\psi(x)$ solves the equation, so does $\psi(-x)$, with the same energy, and so do their even and odd combinations

<Formula label="Even and odd combinations" tex="\psi_s(x) = \frac12\left(\psi(x) + \psi(-x)\right), \qquad \psi_a(x) = \frac12\left(\psi(x) - \psi(-x)\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.19)" />

**Corollary.** For bound states the word "chosen" can be dropped. With no degeneracy, $\psi(-x) = c\,\psi(x)$, with $c$ real once $\psi$ is taken real (Theorem 2); applying it twice gives $c^2 = 1$, so **every bound state of an even potential is either even or odd** (transcript pp. 6–7). (Zwiebach recalls a qualifying-exam problem whose author expected a bound state that was neither.)

A state that is not bound, such as an oscillating wave that does not decay at infinity, cannot be normalized; it is used through superpositions that are localized (transcript p. 7).

## Continuity of ψ and ψ′

Write the equation as $\psi'' = (\tilde V - \tilde E)\psi$ and take $\psi$ continuous, so that potentials need nothing worse than delta functions (transcript pp. 8–9). If $V$ is continuous, so are $\psi''$ and $\psi'$. If $V$ has finite jumps, $\psi''$ has finite jumps and $\psi'$ is still continuous, with at most a corner. If $V$ contains a delta function, $\psi''$ does too and $\psi'$ jumps; and at a hard wall, $\psi$ vanishes while $\psi'$ does not, so $\psi'$ effectively jumps to zero.

In short (notes eq. (4.2)): **apart from delta functions and hard walls in the potential, both $\psi$ and its slope are continuous; at those two kinds of point the slope may jump by a finite amount.**

## Why the spectrum is discrete

Take a potential well whose value at large $|x|$ on both sides is above the energy $E$ (transcript pp. 10–12). Outside, the solution decays, $\alpha e^{\kappa x}$ on the left and $\beta e^{-\kappa x}$ on the right; inside, it oscillates, needing two coefficients. That is four coefficients, but one is only an overall normalization, which does not affect whether a solution exists: three to fix. Continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at the two edges imposes four conditions. More conditions than unknowns, unless the energy, which sets $\kappa$ and the oscillation, is counted as a fourth unknown: solutions exist only at **particular energies**, one solution each time, a **discrete, non-degenerate** spectrum. The same counting for an energy above the potential on one side but below it on the other gives a continuous, non-degenerate spectrum, and above the potential on both sides a continuous, doubly degenerate one.

**Theorem 4: nodes** (transcript pp. 12–13). For the discrete bound states $E_1 < E_2 < E_3 < \dots$, the wavefunction $\psi_n$ has $n - 1$ nodes: the ground state has none. A physicist's argument grows an infinite square well, whose ground state has no node, into the real potential while checking that no node can appear; it is not rigorous. A partial result, that $\psi_{n+1}$ has at least one more node than $\psi_n$, can be proved and is left as homework.

## The variational principle

The **calculus of variations** looks not for the point where a function is extreme but for the **function** that makes some quantity extreme (transcript pp. 14–16). Its history runs through Newton's problem of the shape of least resistance in a fluid and Johann Bernoulli's 1696 challenge, the **brachistochrone**, the curve of fastest descent between two points, solved by, among others, Leibniz, Newton (overnight, it is said) and Jacob Bernoulli. For quantum mechanics the question becomes: what do the energy eigenstates extremize?

**The ground-state bound** (transcript pp. 16–18). For any normalized function $\psi$, not a solution of anything, just a **trial wavefunction**:

<Formula label="Variational bound" tex="E_{\text{gs}} \le \int\psi^*(\vec x)\,\hat H\,\psi(\vec x)\,d^3x = \langle\hat H\rangle" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.13)" />

Proof: expand $\psi = \sum b_n\psi_n$ in eigenstates, with $\sum|b_n|^2 = 1$; then $\langle\hat H\rangle = \sum|b_n|^2E_n \ge E_1\sum|b_n|^2 = E_1$, since every $E_n \ge E_1$. (The proof assumes, for simplicity of notation, no continuous spectrum, and degeneracy does no harm.)

Without normalizing (transcript pp. 18–19):

<Formula label="The energy functional" tex="E_{\text{gs}} \le F[\psi] \equiv \frac{\int\psi^*\hat H\psi\,d^3x}{\int\psi^*\psi\,d^3x}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.20)" />

$F$ is a **functional**: it takes a function and returns a number, as the travel time of a bead does for the brachistochrone. The ground state is its minimum, a critical point in a space with infinitely many directions; every other eigenstate is also a critical point, but a saddle, with directions in which $F$ decreases (to be shown in homework) (transcript pp. 19–20).

**Example: the delta-function well** (transcript pp. 21–23; the notes credit it to Griffiths). For $V(x) = -\alpha\delta(x)$ with $\alpha > 0$, the exact ground-state energy is $-m\alpha^2/2\hbar^2$. Take the trial function $\psi = e^{-\beta^2x^2/2}$, keeping the width $\beta$ as a parameter, with $\int\psi^2dx = \sqrt\pi/\beta$. The delta function picks out $\psi(0)$; integrating the kinetic term by parts gives $\int(\psi')^2$, and

<Formula label="Delta well: Gaussian trial" tex="F(\beta) = \frac{\beta^2\hbar^2}{4m} - \frac{\beta\alpha}{\sqrt\pi}, \qquad E_{\text{gs}} \le F_{\text{min}} = -\frac{m\alpha^2}{\pi\hbar^2} = \frac{2}{\pi}\left(-\frac{m\alpha^2}{2\hbar^2}\right)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (5.24)–(5.26)" />

where $F_{\text{min}}$ is the smallest value of $F(\beta)$, at $\beta = 2m\alpha/(\sqrt\pi\hbar^2)$. The bound, about $0.64E_{\text{gs}}$, lies above the true (negative) energy, as it must. Here $m\alpha^2/\hbar^2$ is the only energy scale, so by dimensional analysis any such bound is a number times the true value. The principle also helps with the first excited state of symmetric potentials and proves that any attractive potential in one dimension has a bound state.

<WorkedExample title="A parabola in a box">
<Problem>
For an infinite square well on $0 < x < a$, use the trial function $\psi = x(a - x)$, which vanishes at both walls. (a) Find $F[\psi]$ and compare it with the exact $E_1 = \pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2$. (b) For an electron in a 1 nm well, what are the bound and the exact energy, given $\hbar^2/(ma^2) \approx 7.62 \times 10^{-2}$ eV?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The functional">
Inside the well $\hat H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$; integrating by parts, the numerator is $\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int_0^a(\psi')^2dx = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\cdot\frac{a^3}{3}$, and the denominator is $\int_0^a\psi^2dx = \frac{a^5}{30}$. So $F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\cdot\frac{10}{a^2} = 5\frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2}$, against the exact $\frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2} \approx 4.935\frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2}$: only 1.3% above.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) An electron">
$F \approx 5 \times 0.0762 \approx 0.381$ eV, against the exact $4.935 \times 0.0762 \approx 0.376$ eV. A simple guess that respects the boundary conditions does very well.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- One-dimensional bound states: no degeneracy; real up to a phase; even or odd when $V$ is even; $\psi_n$ has $n - 1$ nodes.
- $\psi$ and $\psi'$ are continuous except where $V$ has delta functions or hard walls ($\psi'$ jumps).
- Counting conditions against parameters explains discrete, non-degenerate bound-state energies.
- Variational principle: $E_{\text{gs}} \le F[\psi] = \int\psi^*\hat H\psi/\int\psi^*\psi$ for any trial function; the delta well with a Gaussian gives $\frac2\pi E_{\text{gs}}$.
