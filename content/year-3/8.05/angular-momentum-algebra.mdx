The last lecture began angular momentum with vectors whose components are operators. This one uses them to get the commutation relations of angular momentum almost for free, relates $\vec L^2$ to the Laplacian, and then derives the whole spectrum of angular momentum from its algebra alone. It ends with a surprise: an angular momentum hidden in the two-dimensional oscillator (transcript pp. 1–27; notes chapter 9, sections 1.2, 1.3 and 2). (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Vectors under rotations

A triplet of operators $\vec u$ is called a **vector under rotations** if it commutes with the angular momentum as follows (transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Vector under rotations" tex="\left[L_i, u_j\right] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,u_k" cite="reading2" at="eq. (1.44)" />

The homework showed that $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ are vectors in this sense. The key theorem, also proved as an exercise, is that if $\vec u$ and $\vec v$ are vectors under rotations, then $\vec u\cdot\vec v$ is a **scalar**, commuting with every $L_i$, and $\vec u\times\vec v$ is again a vector (transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Dot products are scalars, cross products vectors" tex="\left[L_i, \vec u\cdot\vec v\right] = 0, \qquad \left[L_i, \left(\vec u\times\vec v\right)_j\right] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\left(\vec u\times\vec v\right)_k" cite="reading2" at="eq. (1.46)" />

So $L_i$ commutes with $\vec r^{\,2}$, $\vec p^{\,2}$ and $\vec r\cdot\vec p$ without further work. Taking $\vec u = \vec r$ and $\vec v = \vec p$ makes $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p$ a vector under rotations, which is the celebrated algebra of angular momentum, usually a tedious calculation with $x$'s and $p$'s (transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Algebra of angular momentum" tex="\left[L_i, L_j\right] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,L_k, \qquad \left[L_i, \vec L^2\right] = 0" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (1.48)–(1.51)" />

Since $\vec L$ is a vector, $\vec L^2 = \vec L\cdot\vec L$ is a scalar. That second result needs only the algebra, not how $\vec L$ is built from $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ (transcript p. 3; notes p. 8). The spin operators obey the same algebra, and indeed $\vec S^2 = \frac{3}{4}\hbar^2\,1$ commutes with each $S_i$ (transcript p. 3). So from now on $J$ is the generic name for any angular momentum, orbital, spin or total, obeying $[J_i, J_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$; Zwiebach urged everyone to check $[J_x, \vec J^2] = 0$ directly, in a couple of lines (transcript pp. 3–4). In vector form the algebra reads $\vec J\times\vec J = i\hbar\vec J$, and for any vector under rotations $\vec J\times\vec u + \vec u\times\vec J = 2i\hbar\,\vec u$ (transcript p. 4; notes eqs. (1.52) and (1.57)).

## L² and central potentials

For operators, $(\vec a\times\vec b)^2$ differs from the classical $\vec a^{\,2}\vec b^{\,2} - (\vec a\cdot\vec b)^2$ by extra terms; for $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ there is one (transcript pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="L squared" tex="\vec L^2 = \vec r^{\,2}\,\vec p^{\,2} - \left(\vec r\cdot\vec p\right)^2 + i\hbar\,\vec r\cdot\vec p" cite="reading2" at="eq. (1.62)" />

Solve for $\vec p^{\,2}$, applying $1/r^2$ from the left since the order matters. With $\vec p = \frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla$, $\vec r\cdot\vec p = \frac{\hbar}{i}r\frac{\partial}{\partial r}$, and the radial terms collapse into a compact form in which the $r$ on the right acts on whatever follows (transcript pp. 5–6; notes eqs. (1.63)–(1.67)):

<Formula label="p squared" tex="\vec p^{\,2} = -\hbar^2\,\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2}\,r + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\vec L^2" cite="reading2" at="eq. (1.67)" />

Comparing with $\vec p^{\,2} = -\hbar^2\nabla^2$ in spherical coordinates shows that the angular part of the Laplacian is $-\vec L^2/\hbar^2r^2$ (transcript p. 6):

<Formula label="L squared as an angular operator" tex="\vec L^2 = -\hbar^2\left(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (1.68)" />

It acts only on angles, as it should for the generator of rotations, and the direct route through $L_x^2 + L_y^2 + L_z^2$ would be painful (transcript pp. 6–7). For a **central potential** $V(r)$ the Hamiltonian becomes (transcript p. 7)

<Formula label="Central potential Hamiltonian" tex="H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2}\,r + \frac{1}{2mr^2}\,\vec L^2 + V(r)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (1.69)" />

Which operators can join $H$ in a set of commuting observables? Not the $x_i$, which fail to commute with the kinetic term, nor the $p_i$, which fail with $V$, nor $\vec r^{\,2}$, $\vec p^{\,2}$ or $\vec r\cdot\vec p$ (transcript pp. 7–8). But every $L_i$ commutes with $H$: with $\vec p^{\,2}$ because it is a scalar, and with $V(r)$ because it is a function of $\vec r^{\,2}$. That is conservation of angular momentum (transcript pp. 8–9; notes eqs. (1.70)–(1.71)). The $L_i$ do not commute with each other, so only one can be kept, by convention $L_z$, and $\vec L^2$ commutes with all of them (transcript p. 9):

<Formula label="Commuting observables for a central potential" tex="H, \quad L_z, \quad \vec L^2" cite="reading2" at="eq. (1.74)" />

$\vec L^2$ is needed because states with the same $L_z$ can have different total angular momentum. (Zwiebach drew such states as arrows with equal $z$ components, warning that arrows mislead, since the three components cannot be measured together.) That no further label is needed for bound states without spin follows later, once the problem reduces to a one-dimensional radial equation, whose bound states are not degenerate (transcript pp. 9–10).

## Ladder operators

Now forget where angular momentum comes from and use only the algebra and the Hermiticity of the $J_i$ (transcript pp. 10–11; notes eq. (2.1)). Define (transcript p. 11)

<Formula label="Raising and lowering operators" tex="J_\pm = J_x \pm iJ_y, \qquad \left(J_+\right)^\dagger = J_-" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.4)–(2.5)" />

Multiplying out and using $[J_x, J_y] = i\hbar J_z$ gives $J_\pm J_\mp = J_x^2 + J_y^2 \pm \hbar J_z$, hence (transcript pp. 11–12; notes eqs. (2.6)–(2.10))

<Formula label="Ladder algebra" tex="\left[J_+, J_-\right] = 2\hbar J_z, \qquad \vec J^2 = J_+J_- + J_z^2 - \hbar J_z = J_-J_+ + J_z^2 + \hbar J_z" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.8) and (2.10)" />

and, from the cyclic commutators (transcript p. 12; notes eq. (2.13)),

<Formula label="J_z with the ladder operators" tex="\left[J_z, J_\pm\right] = \pm\hbar\,J_\pm" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.13)" />

just like $[N, a^\dagger] = a^\dagger$ and $[N, a] = -a$ for the oscillator: $J_+$ will raise the $J_z$ eigenvalue and $J_-$ lower it (transcript p. 13). (The board used $J_1, J_2, J_3$ and the notes $J_x, J_y, J_z$; Zwiebach apologised for switching, transcript pp. 12 and 14.) There is a striking difference. The oscillator algebra, which looks simpler, only has infinite-dimensional representations; the angular momentum algebra, which mathematicians would call the simpler one, has finite ones (transcript pp. 13–14).

## The states |j, m⟩

$\vec J^2$ and $J_z$ are Hermitian and commute, so they have a common orthonormal basis of eigenstates. Label them (transcript pp. 14–15)

<Formula label="Eigenstates" tex="\vec J^2|j, m\rangle = \hbar^2\,j(j+1)\,|j, m\rangle, \qquad J_z|j, m\rangle = \hbar m\,|j, m\rangle, \qquad j, m \in \mathbb{R}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.14)" />

The eigenvalue of $\vec J^2$ is written $\hbar^2j(j+1)$ rather than $\hbar^2j^2$ for convenience that pays off below (transcript p. 15). It cannot be negative, since $\langle j, m|\vec J^2|j, m\rangle$ is a sum of squared norms $\|J_i|j, m\rangle\|^2$ (transcript pp. 15–16; notes eq. (2.16)). So $j(j+1) \ge 0$, satisfied by $j \ge 0$ or $j \le -1$; each allowed value of $j(j+1)$ is reached by exactly one $j \ge 0$, so states are labelled with $j \ge 0$ (transcript p. 16; notes eq. (2.18)).

$J_\pm$ commute with $\vec J^2$, so they do not change $j$; and $J_zJ_\pm|j, m\rangle = ([J_z, J_\pm] + J_\pm J_z)|j, m\rangle = \hbar(m \pm 1)J_\pm|j, m\rangle$, so they change $m$ by one unit (transcript pp. 16–17). Write $J_\pm|j, m\rangle = C_\pm(j, m)\,|j, m\pm 1\rangle$. The squared norm of the left side is $\langle j, m|J_\mp J_\pm|j, m\rangle$, and with $J_\mp J_\pm = \vec J^2 - J_z^2 \mp \hbar J_z$ (transcript pp. 18–19):

<Formula label="Action of the ladder operators" tex="J_\pm|j, m\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{j(j+1) - m(m\pm 1)}\;|j, m\pm 1\rangle" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.26)–(2.29)" />

This is where $j(j+1)$ pays off: it compares neatly with $m(m \pm 1)$ (transcript p. 19).

## Why j comes in halves

The squared norms under the square root cannot be negative (transcript pp. 19–20):

- Raising: $m(m+1) \le j(j+1)$, which means $-j-1 \le m \le j$ (notes eqs. (2.30)–(2.31)).
- Lowering: $m(m-1) \le j(j+1)$, which means $-j \le m \le j+1$ (notes eqs. (2.33)–(2.34)).

Both must hold, so $-j \le m \le j$ (transcript p. 20). Now raise repeatedly. Unless the sequence lands exactly on $m = j$, where $C_+(j, j) = 0$ and the next state is the zero vector, one more step would produce a state with $m > j$ and then one of negative squared norm. So the raising must stop at $m = j$, and likewise the lowering at $m = -j$ (transcript pp. 20–22; notes eqs. (2.32) and (2.35)). Since $m$ moves in whole steps, the distance $2j$ between the ends is an integer (transcript p. 22):

<Formula label="Quantization of angular momentum" tex="2j \in \mathbb{Z} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad j = 0, \tfrac{1}{2}, 1, \tfrac{3}{2}, 2, \dots, \qquad m = j, j-1, \dots, -j" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.36)–(2.37)" />

Each multiplet has $2j + 1$ states: one for $j = 0$, two for $j = \frac{1}{2}$ (the spin up and down states), three for $j = 1$, and so on (transcript p. 22; notes eqs. (2.38)–(2.41)). This is the main result of the theory of angular momentum. The vector space, typically infinite-dimensional, breaks up into such multiplets; which values of $j$ appear depends on the system. Orbital angular momentum will give whole numbers, spin gives $\frac{1}{2}$ (transcript pp. 22–23). (The lecture lists "0, 1, 2, 4" for central potentials; every whole number occurs.)

## A hidden angular momentum in the 2D oscillator

The isotropic oscillator in two dimensions has a ground state, then 2 degenerate states ($a_x^\dagger|0\rangle$, $a_y^\dagger|0\rangle$), then 3, and at level $n$ there are $n + 1$ states. The counts 1, 2, 3, 4, … match the sizes of angular momentum multiplets, and that is no accident (transcript p. 23). Use the circular operators $a_R = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_x - ia_y)$ and $a_L = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_x + ia_y)$, with number operators $N_R$ and $N_L$. (In the lecture the signs were first given the other way round and then corrected, transcript pp. 23–24.) Level $n$ is spanned by $(a_R^\dagger)^{n_R}(a_L^\dagger)^{n_L}|0\rangle$ with $n_R + n_L = n$, and the orbital angular momentum is $L_z = xp_y - yp_x = \hbar(N_R - N_L)$ (transcript p. 24).

The $L_z$ values do not fit: at level 1 they are $\pm\hbar$, not $\pm\frac{\hbar}{2}$; at level 2 they are $2\hbar, 0, -2\hbar$ rather than $\hbar, 0, -\hbar$. Yet $a_R^\dagger a_L$ kills the top state of each level and $a_L^\dagger a_R$ the bottom one, just as $J_+$ and $J_-$ should (transcript pp. 24–25). The motion is in a plane, so there is no real three-dimensional angular momentum, but there is an **abstract** one (transcript p. 25). Define

<Formula label="Hidden angular momentum" tex="J_z = \frac{\hbar}{2}\left(N_R - N_L\right) = \frac{1}{2}L_z, \qquad J_+ = \hbar\,a_R^\dagger a_L, \qquad J_- = \hbar\,a_L^\dagger a_R" cite="reading1" at="p. 25" />

The lecture fixed the $\frac{1}{2}$ in $J_z$ and left the constant in front of $J_\pm$ for the students to find by demanding the algebra (transcript pp. 25–26). Working it out (this page's own step): $[J_+, J_-] = \hbar^2(N_R - N_L)$ must equal $2\hbar J_z$, which requires the factor $\hbar$ written above. They commute with $H = \hbar\omega(N_R + N_L + 1)$, so they act within each energy level, which must therefore be a multiplet: level $n$ is the multiplet $j = n/2$ (the lecture's list of multiplets ends "J equals n"; level $n$ itself carries $j = n/2$), and together the levels contain every $j = 0, \frac{1}{2}, 1, \frac{3}{2}, \dots$ once (transcript p. 26). This is a first example of a **hidden symmetry**; Zwiebach credited its discovery to a famous paper by Julian Schwinger: degeneracies that might have looked accidental are forced by an angular momentum no one expected (transcript pp. 26–27).

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="A spin-3/2 ladder, the length of a j = 2 vector, and an oscillator level">
<Problem>
(a) For $j = \frac{3}{2}$, find the constants in $J_+|j, m\rangle = C_+\,|j, m+1\rangle$ for $m = -\frac{3}{2}, -\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{3}{2}$. (b) For $j = 2$, compare the largest $J_z$ with $\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)}$; at what angle to the $z$ axis would a classical vector of that length with that $z$ component point? (c) For the 2D oscillator level $n = 4$, give its energy, $j$, and the $L_z$ values of its states.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The j = 3/2 ladder">
$j(j+1) = \frac{15}{4}$. For $m = -\frac{3}{2}$: $\frac{15}{4} - \frac{3}{4} = 3$, so $C_+ = \sqrt{3}\,\hbar$. For $m = -\frac{1}{2}$: $\frac{15}{4} + \frac{1}{4} = 4$, so $C_+ = 2\hbar$. For $m = \frac{1}{2}$: $\frac{15}{4} - \frac{3}{4} = 3$, so $C_+ = \sqrt{3}\,\hbar$. For $m = \frac{3}{2}$: $\frac{15}{4} - \frac{15}{4} = 0$, the ladder stops.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Never along the axis">
$\hbar\sqrt{6} \approx 2.449\,\hbar$ against $J_z = 2\hbar$ at most, so $\cos\alpha = 2/\sqrt{6} \approx 0.816$ and $\alpha \approx 35.3°$. Even in the top state the angular momentum is never fully along $z$; for large $j$, $\sqrt{j(j+1)} \approx j + \frac{1}{2}$ and the angle shrinks.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Level n = 4">
$E = \hbar\omega(4 + 1) = 5\hbar\omega$, with $5$ states, so $j = 2$. With $n_R = 4, 3, 2, 1, 0$, $m = \frac{1}{2}(n_R - n_L) = 2, 1, 0, -1, -2$, and $L_z = 2\hbar m = 4\hbar, 2\hbar, 0, -2\hbar, -4\hbar$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A vector under rotations obeys $[L_i, u_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}u_k$; dot products of such vectors are scalars and cross products vectors, so $[L_i, L_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}L_k$ and $[L_i, \vec L^2] = 0$.
- $\vec L^2 = \vec r^{\,2}\vec p^{\,2} - (\vec r\cdot\vec p)^2 + i\hbar\,\vec r\cdot\vec p$ is the angular part of $-\hbar^2\nabla^2$; for a central potential, $H$, $L_z$ and $\vec L^2$ commute.
- From the algebra and Hermiticity: $J_\pm = J_x \pm iJ_y$, $[J_z, J_\pm] = \pm\hbar J_\pm$, $J_\pm|j, m\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{j(j+1) - m(m\pm 1)}\,|j, m\pm 1\rangle$; $2j$ is an integer and each multiplet has $2j + 1$ states.
- The 2D oscillator hides an angular momentum $J_z = \frac{1}{2}L_z$, $J_+ = \hbar a_R^\dagger a_L$, $J_- = \hbar a_L^\dagger a_R$: level $n$ is the multiplet $j = n/2$ (Schwinger).
