## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `3*hbar^2*alpha/m`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent, so each problem says which letters to use. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `hbar` stands for $\hbar$, `alpha` for $\alpha$, `pi` for $\pi$, `V0` for $V_0$, `eta` for $\eta$, `z0` for $z_0$, `phi` for $\phi(x)$, `da` for $\alpha'(x)$, `A2` for $|A|^2$ and `B2` for $|B|^2$.

MIT gives the points per problem. The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons: $\hat p = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d}{dx}$, a prime is a derivative with respect to $x$, and expectation values are written $\langle\hat x\rangle$. The letter $\alpha$ is MIT's in three places and means something else each time: a positive constant with units of one over length squared in Problem 1, a constant phase in Problem 5, and a phase that depends on $x$ in Problem 6. The letters $A$, $B$ and $C$ are constants of the wavefunction at hand. No constants of nature are needed: every answer is a formula, a pure number or a choice. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Properties of a wavefunction" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A particle of mass $m$ moves in one dimension in a potential $V(x)$. Its wavefunction is

<Formula label="The wavefunction of Problem 1" tex="\psi(x) = N\,x\,\exp\left(-\tfrac12\,\alpha\,x^2\right), \qquad \alpha > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Normalize $\psi$ to find the constant $N$ (take it real and positive). Then find $\langle\hat x\rangle$ and $\langle\hat x^2\rangle$.

<Hint>

All three need integrals of the form $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^{2j}e^{-\alpha x^2}dx$. The first is $\sqrt{\pi/\alpha}$. Each derivative with respect to $\alpha$ brings down a factor $-x^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Before you integrate for $\langle\hat x\rangle$: is $x\,|\psi(x)|^2$ an even or an odd function of $x$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-n" label="$N =$" expr="sqrt(2)*alpha^(3/4)/pi^(1/4)"
  vars={{ alpha: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*alpha^(3/2)/sqrt(pi)", hint: "That is N squared. Take the square root." },
    { expr: "(alpha/pi)^(1/4)", hint: "That normalizes the plain Gaussian exp(−αx²/2). Here the wavefunction has an extra factor x, so the integral is that of x² exp(−αx²)." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-x" label="$\langle\hat x\rangle =$" value="0" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-x" />

<Answer id="p1-x2" label="$\langle\hat x^2\rangle =$" expr="3/(2*alpha)"
  vars={{ alpha: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/(2*alpha)", hint: "That is the value for the plain Gaussian exp(−αx²/2). The factor x pushes the probability outward, so the result here is larger." },
    { expr: "3*sqrt(pi)/(4*alpha^(5/2))", hint: "That is the bare integral of x⁴ exp(−αx²). Multiply it by N²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Everything follows from three Gaussian integrals. The symmetry of $\psi$ settles $\langle\hat x\rangle$ without any integral.

<Step title="The Gaussian integrals">

Differentiating $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x^2}dx = \sqrt{\pi/\alpha}$ with respect to $\alpha$, once and twice:

<Formula label="Three Gaussian integrals" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\alpha x^2}dx = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^2e^{-\alpha x^2}dx = \frac{1}{2\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^4e^{-\alpha x^2}dx = \frac{3}{4\alpha^2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The normalization">

<Formula label="Normalization of the wavefunction" tex="1 = N^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^2e^{-\alpha x^2}dx = N^2\,\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2\,\alpha^{3/2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N^2 = \frac{2\,\alpha^{3/2}}{\sqrt\pi}, \qquad N = \frac{\sqrt2\,\alpha^{3/4}}{\pi^{1/4}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (1.6), with the wavefunction of Problem 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two expectation values">

The density $|\psi|^2 = N^2x^2e^{-\alpha x^2}$ is even, so $x|\psi|^2$ is odd and its integral over all $x$ vanishes: $\langle\hat x\rangle = 0$. For the square:

<Formula label="Mean square position" tex="\langle\hat x^2\rangle = N^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x^4e^{-\alpha x^2}dx = \frac{2\,\alpha^{3/2}}{\sqrt\pi}\cdot\frac{3\sqrt\pi}{4\,\alpha^{5/2}} = \frac{3}{2\alpha}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.26), with the wavefunction of Problem 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\alpha x^2$ is a pure number, so $\alpha$ is one over a length squared. Then $N^2 \propto \alpha^{3/2}$ is one over a length cubed, which makes $N^2x^2$ a density per length, and $3/(2\alpha)$ is a length squared. The plain Gaussian $e^{-\alpha x^2/2}$ has $\langle\hat x^2\rangle = 1/(2\alpha)$: the factor $x$ removes the probability from the middle and triples the mean square.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Find $\langle\hat p\rangle$ and $\langle\hat p^2\rangle$.

<Hint>

$\psi$ is real. What does that say about $\int\psi\,\frac{\hbar}{i}\psi'\,dx$, which must be a real number?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $\langle\hat p^2\rangle$ you can let $\hat p^2 = -\hbar^2\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$ act on $\psi$, or integrate by parts once and compute $\hbar^2\int\left(\psi'\right)^2dx$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-p" label="$\langle\hat p\rangle =$" value="0" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-p" />

<Answer id="p1-p2" label="$\langle\hat p^2\rangle =$" expr="3*hbar^2*alpha/2"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], alpha: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "hbar^2*alpha/2", hint: "That is the value for the plain Gaussian exp(−αx²/2). This wavefunction has a node and wiggles more." },
    { expr: "-3*hbar^2*alpha/2", hint: "The square of a Hermitian operator has a positive expectation value. Check the sign: the operator is −ħ² d²/dx²." },
    { expr: "3*hbar*alpha/2", hint: "Check the units: a momentum squared needs ħ², because ħ√α is a momentum." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A real wavefunction has no mean momentum. For the square, one integration by parts turns the second derivative into the square of the first.

<Step title="The mean momentum">

$\langle\hat p\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{i}\int\psi\,\psi'\,dx = \frac{\hbar}{2i}\int\left(\psi^2\right)'dx = \frac{\hbar}{2i}\Big[\psi^2\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = 0$, because $\psi$ vanishes at both ends. (Problem 5(a) is this argument in general.)

</Step>

<Step title="The mean square momentum">

The slope is $\psi' = N\left(1 - \alpha x^2\right)e^{-\alpha x^2/2}$. Integrating by parts, with no boundary term:

<Formula label="Mean square momentum" tex="\langle\hat p^2\rangle = -\hbar^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi\,\psi''\,dx = \hbar^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\psi'\right)^2dx = \hbar^2N^2\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(1 - 2\alpha x^2 + \alpha^2x^4\right)e^{-\alpha x^2}dx" cite="reading1" at="eq. (6.36), used twice" />

With the three integrals of part (a) the bracket gives $\sqrt{\pi/\alpha}\left(1 - 1 + \frac34\right)$, so

<Formula label="Value of the mean square momentum" tex="\langle\hat p^2\rangle = \hbar^2\cdot\frac{2\,\alpha^{3/2}}{\sqrt\pi}\cdot\frac34\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}} = \frac32\,\hbar^2\alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The product of the uncertainties is $\Delta x\,\Delta p = \sqrt{\frac{3}{2\alpha}}\sqrt{\frac32\hbar^2\alpha} = \frac32\hbar$, above the least possible value $\frac12\hbar$, as it must be. It is also what the first excited state of a harmonic oscillator has, $\left(n + \frac12\right)\hbar$ with $n = 1$: part (e) shows that this is no accident.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

Is $\psi$ an eigenstate of position? Is it an eigenstate of momentum? Explain. (The choice box is our addition.)

<Hint>

An eigenstate of an operator is a function that the operator turns into a number times the same function. Apply $\hat x$ and $\hat p$ to $\psi$ and look at what comes out.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-eig" label="$\psi$ is" choices={["an eigenstate of position with eigenvalue 0, because ⟨x⟩ = 0", "an eigenstate of momentum with eigenvalue 0, because ⟨p⟩ = 0", "an eigenstate of neither operator", "an eigenstate of both, because ⟨x⟩ and ⟨p⟩ both vanish"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A vanishing expectation value is an average. An eigenstate needs more: the operator must give back the same function.

<Step title="Position and momentum acting on ψ">

$\hat x\,\psi = x\,\psi$ is not a constant times $\psi$: the factor $x$ changes from point to point. And

<Formula label="The momentum operator on the wavefunction" tex="\hat p\,\psi = \frac{\hbar}{i}\,\psi' = \frac{\hbar}{i}\left(\frac1x - \alpha x\right)\psi" cite="reading1" at="eq. (6.36), with the wavefunction of Problem 1" />

where the bracket again depends on $x$. So $\psi$ is an eigenstate of neither operator.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson's eigenstates look quite different: a delta function $\delta(x - x_0)$ for position and a plane wave $e^{ipx/\hbar}$ for momentum (8.05 notes, eqs. (6.33) and (6.38)), and neither can be normalized. An eigenstate has no spread in its eigenvalue, but here $\Delta x = \sqrt{3/(2\alpha)}$ and $\Delta p = \hbar\sqrt{3\alpha/2}$ are both finite and not zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Step title="The mean energy">

<Part label="e" points={3}>

Now suppose that nothing is known about $V(x)$, except that $\psi$ is an energy eigenstate and that $V(0) = 0$. Find the potential $V(x)$ and the energy eigenvalue $E$. Could $\psi$ be the ground state of the particle?

<Hint>

Put $\psi$ into the time-independent Schrödinger equation and solve it for $V(x)$: $V(x) = E + \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\psi''}{\psi}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$V(x) - E$ comes out as a term with $x^2$ and a constant. The condition $V(0) = 0$ fixes $E$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the last question, count the nodes of $\psi$ and recall the node theorem of the lesson.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-v" label="$V(x) =$" expr="hbar^2*alpha^2*x^2/(2*m)"

<Answer id="p1-e" label="$E =$" expr="3*hbar^2*alpha/(2*m)"

  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[

<Answer id="p1-gs" label="Could $\psi$ be the ground state?" choices={["Yes: it is normalizable and it solves the Schrödinger equation", "Yes: its energy is the lowest that this potential allows", "No: it is not an eigenstate of momentum", "No: it has a node at x = 0, and a ground state has none"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A wavefunction without zeros fixes $V - E$ through $\psi''/\psi$. Here $\psi$ has a zero, but $\psi''$ vanishes there too, so the ratio is still well defined.

<Step title="The second derivative">

From $\psi' = N\left(1 - \alpha x^2\right)e^{-\alpha x^2/2}$:

<Formula label="Second derivative of the wavefunction" tex="\psi'' = N\left(-2\alpha x - \alpha x\left(1 - \alpha x^2\right)\right)e^{-\alpha x^2/2} = \left(\alpha^2x^2 - 3\alpha\right)\psi" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Potential and energy">

The time-independent Schrödinger equation, $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + V\psi = E\psi$, then reads

<Formula label="The Schrödinger equation for the given wavefunction" tex="V(x) - E = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\psi''}{\psi} = \frac{\hbar^2\alpha^2}{2m}\,x^2 - \frac{3\hbar^2\alpha}{2m}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.10), solved for V − E" />

At $x = 0$, with $V(0) = 0$, this gives the energy, and what is left is the potential:

<Formula label="Energy and potential" tex="E = \frac{3\hbar^2\alpha}{2m}, \qquad V(x) = \frac{\hbar^2\alpha^2}{2m}\,x^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Not the ground state">

$\psi$ vanishes at $x = 0$ and changes sign there: one node. By the node theorem the ground state of a one-dimensional potential has no node, and the state with one node is the first excited state. So $\psi$ cannot be the ground state. The ground state of this potential is the Gaussian $e^{-\alpha x^2/2}$, with energy $\hbar^2\alpha/(2m)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The potential is that of a harmonic oscillator, $\frac12m\omega^2x^2$ with $\omega = \hbar\alpha/m$. Its levels are $\hbar\omega\left(n + \frac12\right)$, and $n = 1$ gives $\frac32\hbar\omega = 3\hbar^2\alpha/(2m)$, the energy found. The energy is twice the kinetic part $3\hbar^2\alpha/(4m)$ of part (d): the potential part is $\frac12m\omega^2\langle\hat x^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2\alpha^2}{2m}\cdot\frac{3}{2\alpha}$, the same amount, as always in an oscillator eigenstate.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The energy must exceed the minimum of the potential" points={5} mit="Problem 2">

Take the time-independent Schrödinger equation for a particle of energy $E$ in a potential $V(x)$, on the whole line $-\infty < x < \infty$:

<Formula label="The time-independent Schrödinger equation, solved for the second derivative" tex="\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\left[V(x) - E\right]\psi(x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1, eq. (1)" />

The wavefunction can be taken real without loss of generality. Suppose that the potential is bounded below,

<Formula label="A potential that is bounded below" tex="V(x) \ge V_\text{min} \quad \text{for all } x" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

with $V_\text{min}$ the lowest value of the potential. Prove that normalizable solutions exist only if $E > V_\text{min}$. To do so, assume $E \le V_\text{min}$ and use the equation and an integration to reach a contradiction.

<Hint>

If $E \le V_\text{min}$, what is the sign of $V(x) - E$? And then the sign of the product $\psi\,\psi''$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Look at the function $\psi^2$. Its second derivative is $2\left(\psi'\right)^2 + 2\psi\,\psi''$. What does the sign of that say about its first derivative?

</Hint>

<Hint>

A function whose slope never decreases, and whose slope is positive somewhere, grows at least like a straight line from there on. Can its integral be finite?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** If the energy lies at or below the bottom of the potential, the equation makes $\psi$ curve away from the axis everywhere. A function that does that cannot come down to zero on both sides.

<Step title="The sign of the curvature">

Assume $E \le V_\text{min}$. Then $V(x) - E \ge 0$ for every $x$, and multiplying the equation by $\psi$ gives

<Formula label="The wavefunction curves away from the axis" tex="\psi\,\psi'' = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\left[V(x) - E\right]\psi^2 \ge 0 \quad \text{for all } x" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, pp. 1–2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The density has a slope that never decreases">

Consider $\psi^2$. Its second derivative is a sum of two terms that are never negative:

<Formula label="Second derivative of the density" tex="\left(\psi^2\right)'' = 2\left(\psi'\right)^2 + 2\,\psi\,\psi'' \ge 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

So the slope $\left(\psi^2\right)'$ never decreases as $x$ grows.

</Step>

<Step title="Integrate once">

Suppose the slope of $\psi^2$ is positive at some point $x_1$, with value $s > 0$. Integrating the inequality from $x_1$ to $x$ shows that the slope stays at least $s$ for all $x > x_1$, and one more integration gives

<Formula label="The density grows at least linearly" tex="\psi^2(x) \ge \psi^2(x_1) + s\left(x - x_1\right) \quad \text{for } x > x_1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

Then $\int\psi^2dx$ is infinite and $\psi$ is not normalizable. If the slope is negative at some point, the same argument run to the left gives the same result. The only escape is that the slope of $\psi^2$ is zero everywhere. Then $\psi^2$ is a constant, and a constant has a finite integral over the whole line only if it is zero. So for $E \le V_\text{min}$ there is no normalizable solution other than $\psi = 0$: a normalizable solution needs $E > V_\text{min}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A shorter route, for a wavefunction that goes to zero at both ends with a bounded slope (the lesson's conditions on bound states): multiply the equation by $\psi$ and integrate over all $x$. Integration by parts gives

<Formula label="The same result from one integration by parts" tex="-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\psi'\right)^2dx = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[V(x) - E\right]\psi^2\,dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

The left side is negative unless $\psi$ is constant. The right side is not negative when $E \le V_\text{min}$. That is the contradiction. Read the other way, the formula says $E = \langle\hat K\rangle + \langle V\rangle$ with a kinetic energy $\langle\hat K\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int\left(\psi'\right)^2dx > 0$ and $\langle V\rangle \ge V_\text{min}$: the energy exceeds the lowest potential energy by at least the kinetic energy that confinement costs. Examples: the harmonic oscillator of Problem 1 has $V_\text{min} = 0$ and its lowest level is $\frac12\hbar\omega > 0$, and every bound state of the square well of Problem 4 has $E > -V_0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Three delta functions" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

A particle of mass $m$ moves in one dimension in a potential made of three attractive delta functions, evenly spaced:

<Formula label="Three delta-function wells" tex="V(x) = -V_0\,a\sum_{n=-1}^{1}\delta(x - na), \qquad V_0 > 0, \quad a > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

Here $V_0$ is an energy and $a$ a length, so each delta function has the strength $V_0a$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the discontinuity of the first derivative of the wavefunction at $x = -a$, $0$ and $a$. The jump at $x = na$ is proportional to $\psi(na)$: in the box give the ratio (the form of the answer is our addition).

<Hint>

Integrate the time-independent Schrödinger equation over a short interval around $x = na$ and let the interval shrink. The lesson on the delta-function well does this for one delta function.

In the first box give the function $f(\alpha)$ in $\langle\hat p\rangle = f(\alpha)\,\hbar\int\phi_1\frac{d\phi_2}{dx}dx$ (this form of the answer, and the choice box, are our additions).

<Hint>

Multiply out $\psi^*\,\frac{\hbar}{i}\,\psi'$. There are four terms. Two of them are of the kind met in part (a).

</Hint>

<Hint>

The two cross terms hold $\int\phi_1\phi_2'\,dx$ and $\int\phi_2\phi_1'\,dx$. One integration by parts relates them.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-f" label="$f(\alpha) =$" expr="2*sin(alpha)"
  vars={{ alpha: [0.3, 2.8] }}
  names={{ alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-2*sin(alpha)", hint: "That is the factor in front of the other integral, the one with φ₂ and dφ₁/dx. The two integrals differ by a sign." },
    { expr: "2*cos(alpha)", hint: "For α = 0 the wavefunction is real, and part (a) says that ⟨p⟩ must then vanish. This function does not." },
    { expr: "sin(alpha)", hint: "Both cross terms contribute, and after the integration by parts they are equal." }

<Answer id="p5-zero" label="$\langle\hat p\rangle$ is sure to vanish for" choices={["α equal to any odd multiple of π/2, where cos α vanishes", "α equal to any whole multiple of π", "α = 0 only", "no value of α"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each function alone carries no momentum. The momentum comes from the interference of the two, and it needs a relative phase that is not real.

<Step title="The four terms">

<Formula label="The four terms of the mean momentum" tex="\langle\hat p\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{i}\int\left(\phi_1 + e^{-i\alpha}\phi_2\right)\left(\phi_1' + e^{i\alpha}\phi_2'\right)dx = \frac{\hbar}{i}\int\left(\phi_1\phi_1' + \phi_2\phi_2' + e^{i\alpha}\phi_1\phi_2' + e^{-i\alpha}\phi_2\phi_1'\right)dx" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (2.26) and (6.36), with the wavefunction of Problem 5(b)" />

The first two terms vanish as in part (a).

</Step>

<Step title="The cross terms">

Integrating by parts, $\int\phi_2\phi_1'\,dx = -\int\phi_1\phi_2'\,dx$, with no boundary term. So

<Formula label="Mean momentum from the interference of two real functions" tex="\langle\hat p\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{i}\left(e^{i\alpha} - e^{-i\alpha}\right)\int\phi_1\,\phi_2'\,dx = 2\hbar\,\sin\alpha\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi_1\,\frac{d\phi_2}{dx}\,dx = -2\hbar\,\sin\alpha\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi_2\,\frac{d\phi_1}{dx}\,dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="When it vanishes">

Without knowing the two functions, $\langle\hat p\rangle = 0$ is certain only when $\sin\alpha = 0$, that is for $\alpha = j\pi$ with $j$ a whole number. Then $e^{i\alpha} = \pm1$ and $\psi = \phi_1 \pm \phi_2$ is real: the case of part (a). For other values of $\alpha$ it can still vanish, but only if the integral happens to be zero (for instance when $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ are both even).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is real, as an expectation value must be. If $\psi$ is not normalized, divide by $\int|\psi|^2dx = \int\left(\phi_1^2 + \phi_2^2 + 2\cos\alpha\,\phi_1\phi_2\right)dx$: the answer is then no longer a function of $\alpha$ times one integral, which is why the normalization is assumed. The largest effect is at $\alpha = \pm\frac{\pi}{2}$, where $\psi = \phi_1 \pm i\phi_2$ has $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ as its real and imaginary parts.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

This time take

<Formula label="A real wavefunction times a plane wave" tex="\psi(x) = e^{ikx}\,\phi(x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3" />

with $k$ a real constant and $\phi(x)$ real, and $\psi$ normalized. Calculate $\langle\hat p\rangle$.

<Hint>

The derivative of $\psi$ has two terms. One of them leads back to part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-k" label="$\langle\hat p\rangle =$" expr="hbar*k"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2] }}

With $\psi' = \left(ik\phi + \phi'\right)e^{ikx}$:

The first integral is 1 by normalization and the second vanishes as in part (a).

For $k = 0$ this is part (a). The result does not depend on the shape of $\phi$: a real wave packet of any form, multiplied by $e^{ikx}$, moves with the mean momentum $\hbar k$ of the plane wave. Problem 6(c) shows the same thing point by point.

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Conserved probability current" points={10} mit="Problem 6">

Let $\Psi(x, t)$ obey the Schrödinger equation in one dimension, with a real potential $V(x)$ (the sheet does not say that it is real, but the result needs it):

<Formula label="The Schrödinger equation in one dimension" tex="-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\Psi(x, t) + V(x)\,\Psi(x, t) = i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi(x, t)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4, eq. (2)" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Derive the conservation law for probability,

<Formula label="Conservation of probability" tex="\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial J}{\partial x} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 4, eq. (3)" />

where $\rho$ is the probability density and $J$ the probability current density:

<Formula label="Probability density and probability current" tex="\rho(x, t) = \Psi^*\Psi, \qquad J(x, t) = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 4, eq. (4)" />

What are the units of $\rho$ and of $J$?

<Hint>

Differentiate $\Psi^*\Psi$ with respect to time, and replace each time derivative with the help of the Schrödinger equation and its complex conjugate.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The terms with $V$ cancel. What is left, $\Psi^*\Psi'' - \Psi\,\frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2}$, is the derivative with respect to $x$ of $\Psi^*\Psi' - \Psi\,\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the units: the integral of $\rho$ over $x$ is a probability, a pure number.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-units" label="Units of $\rho$ and $J$:" choices={["ρ: one over length; J: length over time", "ρ: a pure number; J: one over time", "ρ: one over length cubed; J: one over (length squared times time)", "ρ: one over length; J: one over time"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The Schrödinger equation gives the rate of change of $\Psi$. Because the potential is real, the rate of change of $|\Psi|^2$ comes out as a total derivative with respect to $x$.

<Step title="The time derivative of the density">

From the equation, $\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\Psi'' - \frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi$, and the complex conjugate equation gives $\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t} = -\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2} + \frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi^*$, where a prime is a derivative with respect to $x$. So

<Formula label="Rate of change of the probability density" tex="\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t}\Psi = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi^*\Psi'' - \Psi\,\frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2}\right) = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\Psi^*\Psi' - \Psi\,\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

The terms with $V$ have cancelled, which needs $V$ real.

</Step>

<Step title="The current appears">

The bracket is a number minus its complex conjugate: $\Psi^*\Psi' - \Psi\,\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x} = 2i\,\text{Im}\left(\Psi^*\Psi'\right)$. Therefore

<Formula label="The conservation law derived" tex="\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\cdot2i\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\text{Im}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right)\right] = -\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (1.9) and (1.12)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The units">

$\int\rho\,dx$ is a probability, so $\rho$ has units of one over length, and $\Psi$ of one over the square root of a length. $\hbar/m$ is an energy times a time over a mass, a length squared over a time. In $J$ it multiplies $\Psi^*\Psi'$, which is one over a length squared. So $J$ has units of one over time: a probability per unit time.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two units fit the law: $\partial\rho/\partial t$ and $\partial J/\partial x$ are both one over (length times time). In three dimensions the density would be per volume and the current per area and time. In one dimension the "area" through which probability flows is a point, so the current is just a rate.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Explain why this is a conservation law for probability. To do so, define

<Formula label="Probability to be found between a and b" tex="P_{ab}(t) \equiv \int_a^b\rho(x, t)\,dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 4" />

find $\frac{dP_{ab}}{dt}$ in terms of currents, and interpret the result. Then show that a wavefunction that is normalized at one time stays normalized at later times.

<Hint>

Differentiate under the integral and use the conservation law. The integral of $\partial J/\partial x$ can be done.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the normalization let $a \to -\infty$ and $b \to \infty$. What does the current do where $\Psi$ goes to zero?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-dp" label="$\dfrac{dP_{ab}}{dt} =$" choices={["J(b, t) − J(a, t)", "J(a, t) + J(b, t)", "J(a, t) − J(b, t)", "zero, for every a and b"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The probability in an interval can change only by flowing through its two ends.

<Step title="The rate of change">

<Formula label="Rate of change of the probability in an interval" tex="\frac{dP_{ab}}{dt} = \int_a^b\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\,dx = -\int_a^b\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}\,dx = J(a, t) - J(b, t)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (1.14)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Interpretation">

A positive $J$ is a flow of probability towards larger $x$. So $J(a, t)$ is the rate at which probability enters the interval through its left end, and $J(b, t)$ the rate at which it leaves through the right end. Probability is not created or destroyed anywhere inside: it only moves, as electric charge does. That is what a local conservation law means, and it says more than the constancy of the total.

</Step>

<Step title="Normalization is kept">

Let $a \to -\infty$ and $b \to \infty$. A normalizable $\Psi$ goes to zero at both ends while its slope stays bounded, so $J = \frac{\hbar}{m}\text{Im}\left(\Psi^*\Psi'\right)$ goes to zero there. Then

<Formula label="The total probability does not change" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi(x, t)|^2\,dx = J(-\infty, t) - J(\infty, t) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

If the integral is 1 at one time, it is 1 at all times.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A test with numbers (ours): take the equal sum of the two lowest states of a harmonic oscillator, in units with $\hbar = m = \omega = 1$. The density sloshes from side to side with the period $2\pi$. For the interval from 0.3 to 1.2 at the time 0.7, a numerical derivative of the integral of $\rho$ gives $-0.1740$, and $J(0.3) - J(1.2)$ computed from the wavefunction gives the same number.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

From here on take stationary states, with wavefunctions $\psi(x)$. Compute the probability current for

<Formula label="A wavefunction with a phase that depends on position" tex="\psi(x) = e^{i\alpha(x)}\,\phi(x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 4" />

where $\alpha(x)$ and $\phi(x)$ are real, and show that

<Formula label="The current over the density" tex="\frac{J(x)}{\rho(x)} = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\alpha'(x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 4" />

Explain why the ratio $J/\rho$ can be seen as the local velocity of the particle. In the box give $J$, typing `phi` for $\phi(x)$ and `da` for $\alpha'(x)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-j" label="$J(x) =$" expr="hbar*phi^2*da/m"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], phi: [0.5, 2], da: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", phi: "\\phi", da: "\\alpha'" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "hbar*da/m", hint: "That is J/ρ. The box asks for J itself: multiply by the density." },

<Solution>

**The idea.** The size of the wavefunction gives the density, and the slope of its phase gives the flow.

<Step title="The current">

$\psi' = \left(i\alpha'\phi + \phi'\right)e^{i\alpha}$, so $\psi^*\psi' = i\alpha'\phi^2 + \phi\,\phi'$. Only the first term is imaginary:

<Formula label="Current of a wavefunction with a position-dependent phase" tex="J = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(i\,\alpha'\phi^2 + \phi\,\phi'\right) = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\alpha'(x)\,\phi^2(x), \qquad \rho = \phi^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{J}{\rho} = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\alpha'(x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="A local velocity">

For a stream of classical particles, or of charge, the current is the density times the velocity of the flow: $J = \rho\,v$. So $v = J/\rho$ is the velocity with which probability moves at the point $x$. The result agrees with de Broglie. Near a point $x_0$ the phase is $\alpha(x_0) + \alpha'(x_0)(x - x_0)$, so the wavefunction looks there like a plane wave of wavenumber $\alpha'(x_0)$. Such a wave has the momentum $\hbar\alpha'(x_0)$ and the velocity $\hbar\alpha'(x_0)/m$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\alpha$ is a pure number, $\alpha'$ one over a length, and $\hbar/m$ a length squared over a time, so $J/\rho$ is a velocity. For a constant phase, $J = 0$ everywhere: that is Problem 5(a), and bound states carry no current. For $\alpha(x) = kx$ the velocity is $\hbar k/m$ at every point, and $m\int J\,dx = \hbar k\int\phi^2dx = \hbar k$ is the mean momentum of Problem 5(c). (In general $\langle\hat p\rangle = m\int J\,dx$: the mean momentum is the mass times the total flow.)

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Take $\psi(x) = Ae^{ipx/\hbar} + Be^{-ipx/\hbar}$, with $A$ and $B$ complex constants. Calculate $J(x)$. Are there cross terms in $J$ between the right-moving and the left-moving part of $\psi$?

In the box type `A2` for $|A|^2$ and `B2` for $|B|^2$. (The choice box is our addition.)

<Hint>

Multiply out $\psi^*\psi'$. Two of the four terms hold $|A|^2$ and $|B|^2$. The other two hold $A^*B$ and $AB^*$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The two cross terms together have the form $z^* - z$ for a complex number $z$. Is that real or imaginary? Do not forget the factor $i$ in front.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-jd" label="$J =$" expr="p*(A2 - B2)/m"
  vars={{ p: [0.5, 2], A2: [0.5, 2], B2: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ A2: "|A|^2", B2: "|B|^2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "p*(A2 + B2)/m", hint: "The wave exp(−ipx/ħ) moves to the left: its current is negative." },

    { expr: "p*(A2 - B2)", hint: "Check the units: |A|² is one over a length and J is one over a time. Divide the momentum by m to get a velocity." }

<Answer id="p6-cross" label="Cross terms in $J$?" choices={["No: they cancel, and J is the same at every x", "Yes: J has a part that oscillates as cos(2px/ħ)", "No: because A and B can always be chosen real", "Yes, but only if A and B have different phases"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each plane wave carries its own current. The interference between them shows up in the density, but not in the current.

<Step title="The product">

With $\psi' = \frac{ip}{\hbar}\left(Ae^{ipx/\hbar} - Be^{-ipx/\hbar}\right)$:

<Formula label="The product that enters the current" tex="\psi^*\psi' = \frac{ip}{\hbar}\left(|A|^2 - |B|^2 + AB^*e^{2ipx/\hbar} - A^*Be^{-2ipx/\hbar}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The current">

The last two terms in the bracket are $z - z^*$ with $z = AB^*e^{2ipx/\hbar}$, an imaginary number. Times $i$ it is real and drops out of the imaginary part. So

<Formula label="Current of two opposite plane waves" tex="J = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\psi^*\psi'\right) = \frac{p}{m}\left(|A|^2 - |B|^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

There are no cross terms: the current is the current of the right-moving wave minus that of the left-moving wave, each a density times the velocity $p/m$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The cross terms had to cancel. For a stationary state the density does not change in time, so the conservation law gives $\partial J/\partial x = 0$: the current must be the same at every $x$, and a cross term would oscillate as $e^{\pm2ipx/\hbar}$. The density does have such a term, $\rho = |A|^2 + |B|^2 + 2\,\text{Re}\left(A^*Be^{-2ipx/\hbar}\right)$: a standing pattern that does not move. For $|A| = |B|$ the current vanishes, as for the real wavefunctions $\cos(px/\hbar)$ and $\sin(px/\hbar)$. This formula is what defines reflection and transmission in scattering: the incoming, reflected and transmitted currents can be told apart because they do not mix.

</Step>

</Solution>
