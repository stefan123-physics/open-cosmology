Problem 1 uses the position eigenstates that close Lecture 15. Problem 2 goes through the coherent states of Lecture 14 once more, from their definition to their motion in time (Problem Set 7 already worked out their wavefunction). Problem 3 squeezes and then translates the ground state. Problem 4 is about the single mode of light of Lecture 16. Problems 5 and 6 are two-state systems: a particle that tunnels through a wall, as the nitrogen atom of ammonia does, and a system seen as a spin that precesses. The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 8, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

MIT's Problems 4 and 6 have no parts: Problem 4 is split here into (a) and (b), and Problem 6 has a part (b) of our own with numbers. Most of MIT's parts ask for a proof or a general formula. So that there is something to check, some boxes are our additions, each marked as such in its part: in Problem 2(b) the box takes the level $n = 2$ (a factorial cannot be typed in a box); the choice box of Problem 4(a); the time box of Problem 5(c); and the two number boxes of Problem 6(b). ## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `sqrt(hbar/(2*m*w))`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `hbar` stands for $\hbar$, `pi` for $\pi$, `w` for $\omega$, `r` for $|\alpha|$, `g` for $\gamma$, `th` for $\theta$, `D` for $\Delta$, `E0` for $E_0$, `a0` for $\alpha_0$, `a1` and `a2` for the real and the imaginary part of $\alpha$, `d` for $|\alpha - \beta|$ and `pm` for $p_{\text{min}}$. The hyperbolic functions are typed `sinh(g)`, `cosh(g)` and `tanh(g)`.

MIT gives the points per problem: 10 each for Problems 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6, and 15 for Problem 3, 65 points in all. The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is MIT's. The oscillator has mass $m$ and angular frequency $\omega$, its energy eigenstates are $|n\rangle$ with $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, and $\hat N = \hat a^\dagger\hat a$ is the number operator. Throughout,

<Formula label="Position and momentum in terms of the ladder operators" tex="\hat x = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right), \qquad \hat p = i\sqrt{\frac{m\omega\hbar}{2}}\left(\hat a^\dagger - \hat a\right), \qquad [\hat a, \hat a^\dagger] = 1" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (1.16) and (1.18)" />

and the ground state $|0\rangle$, with $\hat a|0\rangle = 0$, has the wavefunction

<Formula label="The ground state wavefunction" tex="\psi_0(x) = \langle x|0\rangle = \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}\exp\left(-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}\,x^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (1.42)" />

A rule that is used again and again: if the commutator $[A, B]$ commutes with $B$, then $[A, e^B] = [A, B]\,e^B$ (the lecture notes use it at their eq. (5.17)).

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Position and momentum eigenstates in the harmonic oscillator" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

Lecture 15 ended with a surprise: the position eigenstates $|x\rangle$ can be built from the ground state of the oscillator with raising operators alone, as squeezed states pushed to the limit:

<Formula label="The position eigenstate from the oscillator ground state" tex="|x\rangle = \psi_0(x)\,\exp\left(\sqrt{\frac{2m\omega}{\hbar}}\;x\,\hat a^\dagger - \frac{1}{2}\,\hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger\right)|0\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eq. (5.36)" />

Here $\psi_0(x)$ is the ground state wavefunction given above: it is an ordinary number that depends on the label $x$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Use this expression for $|x\rangle$ to find the wavefunction $\psi_2(x) = \langle x|2\rangle$ of the second excited state,

<Formula label="The second excited state" tex="|2\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\,\hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger|0\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

Give the answer in the box as the ratio $\psi_2(x)/\psi_0(x)$.

<Hint>

Take the adjoint of the expression for $|x\rangle$ to get the bra $\langle x|$: every $\hat a^\dagger$ becomes an $\hat a$, and the numbers in it are real.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Expand the exponential in the bra as a power series. Between $\langle 0|$ and $\hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger|0\rangle$ only the terms with exactly two lowering operators survive. There are two such terms.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-psi2" label="$\psi_2(x)/\psi_0(x) =$" expr="(2*m*w*x^2/hbar - 1)/sqrt(2)"
  vars={{ m: [0.5, 2], w: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 2], x: [0.3, 1.5] }}
  names={{ w: "\\omega", hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*m*w*x^2/hbar - 1", hint: "That is ⟨x|â†â†|0⟩ divided by ψ₀(x). The state |2⟩ carries a further factor 1/√2." },
    { expr: "(m*w*x^2/hbar - 1)/sqrt(2)", hint: "This function is zero at x = ±√(ħ/(mω)). Check the term in x²: it comes from the second-order term of the exponential, and ⟨0|ââ â†â†|0⟩ is 2, not 1." },
    { expr: "(1 - 2*m*w*x^2/hbar)/sqrt(2)", hint: "This is minus the right function. Both give the same probabilities, but the definition |2⟩ = â†â†|0⟩/√2 fixes the sign: ψ₂ is positive for large x." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The bra $\langle x|$ is $\langle 0|$ followed by an exponential of lowering operators. Acting on a state with two quanta, only the terms of the series that remove exactly two quanta give something.

<Step title="The bra">

Write $\beta \equiv \sqrt{2m\omega/\hbar}\;x$, a real number. The adjoint of the expression for $|x\rangle$ is

<Formula label="The position bra" tex="\langle x| = \psi_0(x)\,\langle 0|\exp\left(\beta\,\hat a - \frac{1}{2}\,\hat a\hat a\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The terms that survive">

The series of the exponential is $1 + \left(\beta\hat a - \frac{1}{2}\hat a\hat a\right) + \frac{1}{2}\left(\beta\hat a - \frac{1}{2}\hat a\hat a\right)^2 + \dots$ A term with $k$ lowering operators, acting on $\hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger|0\rangle$, gives zero for $k > 2$, and for $k < 2$ it leaves a state with quanta, which is orthogonal to $\langle 0|$. The terms with $k = 2$ are $-\frac{1}{2}\hat a\hat a$ from the first order and $\frac{1}{2}\beta^2\,\hat a\hat a$ from the second. With $\langle 0|\hat a\hat a\,\hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger|0\rangle = 2$:

<Formula label="The matrix element" tex="\langle x|\hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger|0\rangle = \psi_0(x)\left(\frac{\beta^2}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\right)\cdot 2 = \psi_0(x)\left(\beta^2 - 1\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The wavefunction">

<Formula label="The wavefunction of the second excited state" tex="\psi_2(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\frac{2m\omega}{\hbar}\,x^2 - 1\right)\psi_0(x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ratio is a pure number, since $m\omega x^2/\hbar$ has no units. The function is even, with two nodes, at $x = \pm\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)}$, as the second excited state should have. It is orthogonal to $\psi_0$ and normalized: with $u = x\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}$ the integrals of $u^2e^{-u^2}$ and $u^4e^{-u^2}$ are $\frac{1}{2}\sqrt\pi$ and $\frac{3}{4}\sqrt\pi$, so the integral of $(2u^2 - 1)\,e^{-u^2}$ is zero and that of $\frac{1}{2}(2u^2 - 1)^2e^{-u^2}$ is $\sqrt\pi$, which the factor $\sqrt{m\omega/(\pi\hbar)}$ of $\psi_0^2$ turns into one. Applying $\hat a^\dagger$ twice as a differential operator gives the same function.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Build the momentum eigenstates $|p\rangle$ of the oscillator in the same way: as a number times an exponential of raising operators acting on $|0\rangle$. They must obey

<Formula label="The momentum eigenstates" tex="\hat p\,|p\rangle = p\,|p\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

Write your state as

<Formula label="The form of the answer" tex="|p\rangle = \mathcal{N}(p)\,\exp\left(i\,\mu\,\hat a^\dagger + \nu\,\hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger\right)|0\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own notation)" />

with real numbers $\mu$ and $\nu$, and fix $\mathcal{N}(p)$ so that $\langle x|p\rangle = e^{ipx/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$, the usual normalization. The boxes ask for $\mu$, $\nu$ and $\mathcal{N}(p)$.

<Hint>

Act with $\hat a$ on the exponential, using the rule for $[A, e^B]$ from "How to answer". The result is the same state times a combination of a number and $\hat a^\dagger$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In $\hat p \propto \hat a^\dagger - \hat a$ the terms with $\hat a^\dagger$ must cancel: that fixes $\nu$. What is left fixes $\mu$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $\mathcal{N}(p)$ take the inner product with $\langle 0|$: every $\hat a^\dagger$ acting to the left on $\langle 0|$ gives zero. So $\mathcal{N}(p) = \langle 0|p\rangle$, which is the complex conjugate of the ground state wavefunction in momentum space.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-coef" label="$\mu =$" expr="p*sqrt(2/(m*w*hbar))"

<Answer id="p1-sign" label="$\nu =$" choices={["−1/2, as in the position state", "+1/2", "−1", "+1"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-norm" label="$\mathcal{N}(p) =$" expr="(pi*m*w*hbar)^(-1/4)*exp(-p^2/(2*m*w*hbar))"

<Solution>

**The idea.** On an exponential of raising operators, $\hat a$ acts like a derivative and brings down a number plus a multiple of $\hat a^\dagger$. The state is an eigenstate of $\hat a^\dagger - \hat a$ when the multiples of $\hat a^\dagger$ cancel.

<Step title="The lowering operator on the exponential">

Let $B = i\mu\,\hat a^\dagger + \nu\,\hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger$. Then $[\hat a, B] = i\mu + 2\nu\,\hat a^\dagger$, which commutes with $B$. Since $\hat a|0\rangle = 0$:

<Formula label="The lowering operator on the trial state" tex="\hat a\,e^{B}|0\rangle = [\hat a, e^{B}]\,|0\rangle = \left(i\mu + 2\nu\,\hat a^\dagger\right)e^{B}|0\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The eigenvalue equation">

<Formula label="The momentum operator on the trial state" tex="\hat p\,e^{B}|0\rangle = i\sqrt{\frac{m\omega\hbar}{2}}\left(\left(1 - 2\nu\right)\hat a^\dagger - i\mu\right)e^{B}|0\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is a number times the state only if $\nu = \frac{1}{2}$. The number is then $\sqrt{m\omega\hbar/2}\;\mu$, and it must equal $p$:

<Formula label="The two numbers in the exponent" tex="\nu = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad \mu = \sqrt{\frac{2}{m\omega\hbar}}\;p" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The normalization">

Taking the inner product with $\langle 0|$, every term of the series with an $\hat a^\dagger$ drops out, so $\langle 0|p\rangle = \mathcal{N}(p)$. With $\langle x|p\rangle = e^{ipx/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$ this number is

<Formula label="The normalization constant" tex="\mathcal{N}(p) = \langle 0|p\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_0(x)\,\frac{e^{ipx/\hbar}}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\,dx = \left(\frac{1}{\pi m\omega\hbar}\right)^{1/4}\exp\left(-\frac{p^2}{2m\omega\hbar}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The momentum eigenstate for a given $p$ is unique up to a factor, so a state that obeys the eigenvalue equation and has the right overlap with $|0\rangle$ is the usual $|p\rangle$. All in all

<Formula label="The momentum eigenstate from the oscillator ground state" tex="|p\rangle = \left(\frac{1}{\pi m\omega\hbar}\right)^{1/4}\exp\left(-\frac{p^2}{2m\omega\hbar}\right)\exp\left(i\sqrt{\frac{2}{m\omega\hbar}}\;p\,\hat a^\dagger + \frac{1}{2}\,\hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger\right)|0\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The exponent is a pure number: $\sqrt{m\omega\hbar}$ is a momentum. For $p = 0$ the state is $\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger\right)|0\rangle$ up to a factor, which is the squeezed vacuum with $\gamma \to -\infty$ that the lecture notes identify with a state of zero momentum (their eq. (5.32)). The overlap with the first excited state is $\langle 1|p\rangle = \langle 0|\hat a|p\rangle = i\mu\,\mathcal{N}(p)$, and this is indeed the complex conjugate of the momentum-space wavefunction of $|1\rangle$, which is $-i\mu$ times that of the ground state. The exponent of the momentum state follows from that of the position state by the replacements $x\sqrt{m\omega/\hbar} \to p/\sqrt{m\omega\hbar}$ and $\hat a^\dagger \to i\hat a^\dagger$: a quarter turn in phase space. A quarter of a period of the oscillator turns position states into momentum states.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Coherent states of the harmonic oscillator" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

This problem goes through the coherent states once more. Some of its questions are answered in the lecture notes and in the lesson of Lecture 14: MIT's advice is to use them as little as you can. For any complex number $\alpha$ the coherent state $|\alpha\rangle$ is defined by

<Formula label="The coherent state" tex="|\alpha\rangle \equiv e^{\alpha\hat a^\dagger - \alpha^*\hat a}\,|0\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

The exponent is anti-Hermitian, so the exponential is unitary and $|\alpha\rangle$ is normalized. The Hamiltonian is $\hat H = \hbar\omega\left(\hat N + \frac{1}{2}\right)$.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

Show that the coherent state is an eigenstate of the lowering operator:

<Formula label="Eigenstate of the lowering operator" tex="\hat a\,|\alpha\rangle = \alpha\,|\alpha\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1" />

<Hint>

Since $\hat a|0\rangle = 0$, you may replace $\hat a\,e^{B}|0\rangle$ by the commutator $[\hat a, e^{B}]\,|0\rangle$, with $B$ the exponent.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The commutator of $\hat a$ with the exponent is the number $\alpha$, so $\hat a$ passes through the exponential at the cost of a factor $\alpha$.

<Step title="The commutator">

With $B = \alpha\hat a^\dagger - \alpha^*\hat a$, $[\hat a, B] = \alpha\,[\hat a, \hat a^\dagger] = \alpha$, a number, which commutes with $B$. So $[\hat a, e^{B}] = \alpha\,e^{B}$ and

<Formula label="The lowering operator on a coherent state" tex="\hat a\,|\alpha\rangle = \hat a\,e^{B}|0\rangle = [\hat a, e^{B}]\,|0\rangle = \alpha\,e^{B}|0\rangle = \alpha\,|\alpha\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eq. (4.76)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\alpha = 0$ the state is $|0\rangle$ and the equation reads $\hat a|0\rangle = 0$. The eigenvalue may be complex because $\hat a$ is not Hermitian.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Write $|\alpha\rangle$ as a sum over the energy eigenstates $|n\rangle$. What is the probability that the oscillator in the state $|\alpha\rangle$ is found with the energy $E_n = \hbar\omega\left(n + \frac{1}{2}\right)$?

The box (in this form our addition) asks for the probability of the level $n = 2$, written with $r = |\alpha|$.

<Hint>

Split the exponential into a factor with $\hat a^\dagger$ to the left of a factor with $\hat a$, with the identity $e^{X + Y} = e^Xe^Ye^{-\frac{1}{2}[X, Y]}$, which holds when $[X, Y]$ commutes with $X$ and with $Y$. The factor with $\hat a$ does nothing to $|0\rangle$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Then expand $e^{\alpha\hat a^\dagger}$ and use $(\hat a^\dagger)^n|0\rangle = \sqrt{n!}\,|n\rangle$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-prob" label="Probability of the level $n = 2$:" expr="exp(-r^2)*r^4/2"
  vars={{ r: [0.4, 2] }}
  names={{ r: "|\\alpha|" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-r^2/2)*r^2/sqrt(2)", hint: "That is the size of the amplitude ⟨2|α⟩. The probability is its square." },
    { expr: "exp(-r^2)*r^4", hint: "Check the factorial: the amplitude has 1/√(n!), so the probability has 1/n!, which is 1/2 for n = 2." },
    { expr: "exp(-r^2)*r^2/2", hint: "The amplitude of level n has αⁿ, so the probability has |α| to the power 2n: the fourth power for n = 2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Once the exponential is ordered with the raising operators to the left, the lowering operators act on $|0\rangle$ and disappear. What is left is a power series in $\hat a^\dagger$.

<Step title="Ordering the exponential">

Take $X = \alpha\hat a^\dagger$ and $Y = -\alpha^*\hat a$. Then $[X, Y] = -|\alpha|^2\,[\hat a^\dagger, \hat a] = |\alpha|^2$, a number. The identity

<Formula label="The identity for the exponential of a sum" tex="e^{X + Y} = e^{X}\,e^{Y}\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}[X, Y]} \quad\text{if } [X, Y] \text{ commutes with } X \text{ and with } Y" cite="reading1" at="eq. (4.49)" />

and $e^{-\alpha^*\hat a}|0\rangle = |0\rangle$ give

<Formula label="The coherent state with raising operators only" tex="|\alpha\rangle = e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^2}\,e^{\alpha\hat a^\dagger}|0\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eq. (4.78)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The sum over energy eigenstates">

Expanding the exponential, with $(\hat a^\dagger)^n|0\rangle = \sqrt{n!}\,|n\rangle$:

<Formula label="The coherent state in the energy basis" tex="|\alpha\rangle = e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}\,|n\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eq. (4.94)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The probabilities">

The probability of the energy $E_n$ is the absolute value squared of the coefficient of $|n\rangle$:

<Formula label="The probability of the level n" tex="P_n = \left|\langle n|\alpha\rangle\right|^2 = e^{-|\alpha|^2}\,\frac{|\alpha|^{2n}}{n!}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

For $n = 2$ this is $\frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^4e^{-|\alpha|^2}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The probabilities add up to one, because $\sum_n |\alpha|^{2n}/n! = e^{|\alpha|^2}$. They form a Poisson distribution with the mean $|\alpha|^2$, which is $\langle\hat N\rangle$. For $\alpha = 0$ only $P_0 = 1$ is left.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

Show that two different coherent states $|\alpha\rangle$ and $|\beta\rangle$ are never orthogonal, by working out $\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle$.

In the box give $\left|\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle\right|^2$ in terms of the distance $d = |\alpha - \beta|$ between the two labels in the complex plane.

<Hint>

Use the sum of part (b) for both states. The inner products $\langle m|n\rangle$ leave a single sum, which is an exponential series.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-overlap" label="$\left|\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle\right|^2 =$" expr="exp(-d^2)"
  vars={{ d: [0.3, 2] }}
  names={{ d: "|\\alpha - \\beta|" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-d^2/2)", hint: "That is |⟨β|α⟩|, the size of the overlap itself. The box asks for its square." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "That would hold for eigenstates of a Hermitian operator with different eigenvalues. The lowering operator is not Hermitian." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With both states written in the energy basis, the overlap is an exponential series in $\beta^*\alpha$.

<Step title="The overlap">

<Formula label="The overlap of two coherent states" tex="\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle = e^{-\frac{1}{2}|\beta|^2 - \frac{1}{2}|\alpha|^2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(\beta^*\alpha)^n}{n!} = \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2\right) + \beta^*\alpha\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (4.79)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Its size">

The absolute value squared multiplies this by its complex conjugate, and $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 - \beta^*\alpha - \beta\alpha^* = |\alpha - \beta|^2$:

<Formula label="The squared overlap" tex="\left|\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle\right|^2 = e^{-|\alpha - \beta|^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

An exponential of a real number is never zero, so no two coherent states are orthogonal.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\beta = \alpha$ the overlap is one, as it must be for a normalized state. For labels far apart it is tiny, so such states are nearly orthogonal. For real labels the result can be checked with wavefunctions: the two states are the ground state Gaussian shifted to two centres, and the overlap integral of two such Gaussians gives the same exponential.

</Step>

</Solution>

The normalized squeezed vacuum $|0_\gamma\rangle$, with $\gamma$ a real number, is made by a unitary operator $S(\gamma)$ acting on the ground state:

<Formula label="The squeezed vacuum and the squeezing operator" tex="|0_\gamma\rangle \equiv S(\gamma)\,|0\rangle, \qquad S(\gamma) = \exp\left(-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(\hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger - \hat a\hat a\right)\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

<Exercise id="p4" title="Maxwell's equations and photon states" points={10} mit="Problem 4">

<Formula label="The fields of one mode in the cavity" tex="E_x(z, t) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{V\epsilon_0}}\;\omega\,q(t)\sin kz, \qquad cB_y(z, t) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{V\epsilon_0}}\;p(t)\cos kz" cite="reading1" at="eq. (5.38)" />

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

<Step title="Maxwell's equations for E and cB">

Without charges and currents, and with $c\vec B$ as the magnetic variable:

<Formula label="Maxwell's equations in empty space" tex="\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0, \qquad \nabla\cdot\left(c\vec B\right) = 0, \qquad \nabla\times\vec E = -\frac{1}{c}\,\frac{\partial\left(c\vec B\right)}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla\times\left(c\vec B\right) = \frac{1}{c}\,\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

In this form the two curl equations look alike, up to a sign: each field has the same units and the same factor $1/c$ with the time derivative.

</Step>

<Step title="The two divergences">

$\vec E$ has only an $x$ component, which does not depend on $x$, so $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \partial E_x/\partial x = 0$. In the same way $\nabla\cdot(c\vec B) = \partial(cB_y)/\partial y = 0$. The two Gauss laws give no condition.

</Step>

<Step title="Faraday's law">

The curl of $\vec E$ points along $y$ and equals $\partial E_x/\partial z$:

<Formula label="Faraday's law for the mode" tex="\sqrt{\frac{2}{V\epsilon_0}}\;\omega\,q\,k\cos kz = -\frac{1}{c}\sqrt{\frac{2}{V\epsilon_0}}\;\frac{dp}{dt}\,\cos kz \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dp}{dt} = -ck\,\omega\,q = -\omega^2q" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The Ampère-Maxwell law">

<Formula label="The Ampère-Maxwell law for the mode" tex="\sqrt{\frac{2}{V\epsilon_0}}\;p\,k\sin kz = \frac{1}{c}\sqrt{\frac{2}{V\epsilon_0}}\;\omega\,\frac{dq}{dt}\,\sin kz \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{ck}{\omega}\,p = p" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

A solution is $q = \cos\omega t$, $p = -\omega\sin\omega t$: the electric and the magnetic field are a quarter period out of step, as in any standing wave.

</Step>

</Solution>

At $t = 0$ the particle is on the right side with certainty. What is the probability $P_L(t)$ of finding it on the left side at the time $t$?

The second box is our addition: the first time at which the particle is certain to be on the left.

<Hint>

Put $c_L = 0$ and $c_R = 1$ in part (b). The probability is the absolute square of the coefficient of $|L\rangle$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-pl" label="$P_L(t) =$" expr="sin(D*t/hbar)^2"
  vars={{ D: [0.5, 2], t: [0.2, 0.7], hbar: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ D: "\\Delta", hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "cos(D*t/hbar)^2", hint: "That is the probability of finding the particle still on the right: it is 1 at t = 0." },
    { expr: "sin(2*D*t/hbar)^2", hint: "The energies are ±Δ, so the amplitude is sin(Δt/ħ). Its square, (1 − cos(2Δt/ħ))/2, already swings at the frequency 2Δ/ħ of the energy gap." },
    { expr: "sin(D*t/(2*hbar))^2", hint: "That would fit a gap of Δ between the two levels. Here the gap is 2Δ." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p5-time" label="First time with $P_L = 1$:" expr="pi*hbar/(2*D)"
  vars={{ D: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ D: "\\Delta", hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi*hbar/D", hint: "At that time the particle is back on the right: it is the full period of the probability." },
    { expr: "pi*hbar/(4*D)", hint: "At that time the probability on the left is one half only." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A state that starts on one side is an equal mix of the two energy eigenstates. Their relative phase grows in time and carries the particle to the other side and back.

<Step title="The probability">

With $c_L = 0$ and $c_R = 1$ the coefficient of $|L\rangle$ is $-i\sin(\Delta t/\hbar)$:

<Formula label="The probability on the left" tex="P_L(t) = \left|\langle L|\psi, t\rangle\right|^2 = \sin^2\frac{\Delta t}{\hbar}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The first full crossing">

$P_L = 1$ first when $\Delta t/\hbar = \pi/2$, at $t = \frac{\pi\hbar}{2\Delta}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$P_L(0) = 0$, and the probability on the right is $\cos^2(\Delta t/\hbar)$, so the two add up to one. Written as $\frac{1}{2}\left(1 - \cos(2\Delta t/\hbar)\right)$ the probability oscillates at the angular frequency $2\Delta/\hbar$, the energy gap over $\hbar$. This is the flipping of the nitrogen atom in ammonia (the notes, chapter 7, eq. (4.51)). The time $\frac{\pi\hbar}{2\Delta}$ is also the shortest time that Problem Set 6 allows for reaching an orthogonal state, $\frac{h}{4\,\Delta H}$: here $\Delta H = \Delta$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Suppose someone makes a mistake and writes the Hamiltonian as

<Formula label="The faulty Hamiltonian" tex="\hat H = \Delta\,|L\rangle\langle R|" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 3" />

What is wrong with it? Evolve a state with $e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}$, as in part (b), and show that the total probability is not conserved.

In the second box give the total probability at the time $t$ (the probability on the left plus the probability on the right) for a particle that starts on the right.

<Hint>

Compare $\hat H$ with its adjoint. Then work out $\hat H^2$: the series of the exponential stops early.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-wrong" label="What is wrong with this $\hat H$?" choices={["Its eigenvalues are complex", "It is not linear", "It has the wrong units", "It is not Hermitian"]} answer={3} />

<Answer id="p5-norm" label="Total probability at time $t$:" expr="1 + (D*t/hbar)^2"
  vars={{ D: [0.5, 2], t: [0.2, 1.2], hbar: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ D: "\\Delta", hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1", hint: "That holds when the Hamiltonian is Hermitian. Here the exponential is not unitary: work it out with Ĥ² = 0." },
    { expr: "1 - (D*t/hbar)^2", hint: "The amplitude on the left is −iΔt/ħ. Its absolute square is added to the probability, not subtracted." },
    { expr: "(D*t/hbar)^2", hint: "That is the probability on the left alone. The amplitude on the right stays 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** This operator moves the particle from right to left but never back. It is not Hermitian, so the operator $e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}$ is not unitary and the norm of the state is not kept.

<Step title="Not Hermitian">

The adjoint of $|L\rangle\langle R|$ is $|R\rangle\langle L|$, so $\hat H^\dagger = \Delta\,|R\rangle\langle L| \ne \hat H$. An observable such as the energy needs a Hermitian operator, and only a Hermitian $\hat H$ makes the time evolution unitary.

</Step>

<Step title="The time evolution">

$\hat H^2 = \Delta^2\,|L\rangle\langle R|L\rangle\langle R| = 0$, because $\langle R|L\rangle = 0$. The series of the exponential stops after two terms:

<Formula label="Time evolution with the faulty Hamiltonian" tex="e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar} = 1 - \frac{i\Delta t}{\hbar}\,|L\rangle\langle R|, \qquad |\psi, t\rangle = \left(c_L - \frac{i\Delta t}{\hbar}\,c_R\right)|L\rangle + c_R\,|R\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The total probability">

<Formula label="The norm is not kept" tex="\langle\psi, t|\psi, t\rangle = \left|c_L - \frac{i\Delta t}{\hbar}\,c_R\right|^2 + |c_R|^2 = 1 + \frac{2\Delta t}{\hbar}\,\text{Im}\left(c_L^*c_R\right) + \frac{\Delta^2t^2}{\hbar^2}\,|c_R|^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

for a state that is normalized at $t = 0$. For a particle that starts on the right, $c_L = 0$ and $c_R = 1$, this is $1 + \Delta^2t^2/\hbar^2$: the total probability grows without limit.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Only a state with $c_R = 0$ keeps its norm: the particle is then on the left, where this $\hat H$ does nothing. The eigenvalues of the faulty matrix are both zero, so they are real, and the units are those of an energy: the one thing wrong is that the matrix is not Hermitian. The rate of change of the norm, $\frac{d}{dt}\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle\psi|\left(\hat H^\dagger - \hat H\right)|\psi\rangle$, vanishes for every state only if $\hat H^\dagger = \hat H$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Altering an oscillation" points={10} mit="Problem 6">

A two-state system has the basis states $|1\rangle$ and $|2\rangle$ and the Hamiltonian

<Formula label="The Hamiltonian of the oscillating system" tex="H = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -\Delta\\ -\Delta & 0\end{pmatrix} = -\Delta\,\sigma_1, \qquad \Delta > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4" />

If the system starts in the state $|1\rangle$, the probability of finding it in $|1\rangle$ swings between one and zero, as in Problem 5 and as for the ammonia molecule.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Add to $H$ a term along $\sigma_3$ that does not depend on time, $\lambda\,\sigma_3$, so that the probability of finding the system in $|1\rangle$ now swings between one and a smallest value $p_{\text{min}} > 0$. Find the new term in terms of $\Delta$ and $p_{\text{min}}$.

MIT's hint: write the Hamiltonian as the dot product of a vector with the spin operator and think of the time evolution as precession. In the box give the size $|\lambda|$ of the coefficient.

<Hint>

Lecture 16: with $H = \vec g\cdot\vec\sigma$ the two-state system behaves like a spin that precesses around the direction of $\vec g$. The state $|1\rangle$ is the spin along $+z$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A spin that starts along $z$ and turns around an axis at the angle $\theta$ from $z$ moves on a cone. How far from the $z$ axis does it get? The probability of finding a spin "up along $z$" when it points at the angle $\phi$ from $z$ is $\cos^2(\phi/2)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-eps" label="$|\lambda| =$" expr="D*sqrt(pm/(1 - pm))"
  vars={{ D: [0.5, 2], pm: [0.1, 0.8] }}
  names={{ D: "\\Delta", pm: "p_{\\text{min}}" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "D*sqrt((1 - pm)/pm)", hint: "This is the inverse relation: it would make the smallest probability 1 − p_min. A larger p_min needs a larger term along σ₃, which holds the spin closer to the z axis." },
    { expr: "D*pm/(1 - pm)", hint: "That is λ²/Δ. The smallest probability depends on λ², so a square root is needed." },
    { expr: "D*sqrt(pm)", hint: "That sets λ²/Δ² equal to p_min. But the smallest probability is λ²/(Δ² + λ²): the axis of precession is fixed by both terms." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The new term tilts the axis of precession away from the $x$ axis towards the $z$ axis. A spin that starts along $z$ then no longer reaches $-z$: the cone it moves on is narrower, and the smallest probability is fixed by the angle of the cone.

<Step title="The Hamiltonian as a vector">

The new Hamiltonian is

<Formula label="The Hamiltonian with the new term" tex="H = -\Delta\,\sigma_1 + \lambda\,\sigma_3 = \vec g\cdot\vec\sigma, \qquad \vec g = \left(-\Delta,\ 0,\ \lambda\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.29)" />

By Lecture 16 the states precess, as spin states, around the direction of $\vec g$:

<Formula label="Precession of a general two-state system" tex="\vec\omega_L = \frac{2\,\vec g}{\hbar}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.36)" />

The axis lies in the $xz$ plane, at the angle $\theta$ from the $z$ axis with $\cos\theta = \lambda/\sqrt{\Delta^2 + \lambda^2}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The cone">

The state $|1\rangle$ is the spin along $+z$. Precession keeps the angle between the spin and the axis, so the spin moves on a cone of half-angle $\theta$ around $\vec g$. It starts along $z$ and, half a turn later, it is as far from $z$ as it gets: at the angle $2\theta$ from the $z$ axis, on the other side of the axis.

</Step>

<Step title="The smallest probability">

The probability of finding $|1\rangle$ for a spin at the angle $\phi$ from $z$ is $\cos^2(\phi/2)$. It is smallest at $\phi = 2\theta$:

<Formula label="The smallest probability" tex="p_{\text{min}} = \cos^2\theta = \frac{\lambda^2}{\Delta^2 + \lambda^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The new term">

Solving for $\lambda$:

<Formula label="The term that must be added" tex="\lambda\,\sigma_3 \quad\text{with}\quad \lambda = \pm\Delta\sqrt{\frac{p_{\text{min}}}{1 - p_{\text{min}}}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

Either sign will do: the cone has the same angle whether the axis is tilted towards $+z$ or towards $-z$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $p_{\text{min}} \to 0$ no term is needed, and for $p_{\text{min}} \to 1$ the term must be huge: the axis then lies along $z$ and the state $|1\rangle$ is nearly an energy eigenstate. The result agrees with solving the Schrödinger equation directly. With $E = \sqrt{\Delta^2 + \lambda^2}$ the amplitudes are $c_1(t) = \cos\frac{Et}{\hbar} - i\,\frac{\lambda}{E}\sin\frac{Et}{\hbar}$ and $c_2(t) = i\,\frac{\Delta}{E}\sin\frac{Et}{\hbar}$, which obey the Schrödinger equation, $i\hbar\,\frac{dc_1}{dt} = \lambda c_1 - \Delta c_2$ and $i\hbar\,\frac{dc_2}{dt} = -\Delta c_1 - \lambda c_2$. So

<Formula label="The probability in time" tex="|c_1(t)|^2 = 1 - \frac{\Delta^2}{\Delta^2 + \lambda^2}\,\sin^2\frac{\sqrt{\Delta^2 + \lambda^2}\;t}{\hbar}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

whose smallest value is $\frac{\lambda^2}{\Delta^2 + \lambda^2}$. This is the Hamiltonian of ammonia in a constant electric field (Lecture 17), where the field plays the part of $\lambda$: a strong field stops the nitrogen atom from flipping.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

This part is our addition. Take $p_{\text{min}} = 0.25$. Give the ratio $|\lambda|/\Delta$. Then give the period of the probability with the new term, as a fraction of the period without it.

<Hint>

The period of the probability is the time of one full turn of the spin around the axis. The angular velocity of the precession is $2|\vec g|/\hbar$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-num" label="$|\lambda|/\Delta$ for $p_{\text{min}} = 0.25$:" value="0.577" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-num" />

<Answer id="p6-period" label="New period divided by old period:" value="0.866" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-period" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The size of $\vec g$ sets the speed of the precession. The new term makes $\vec g$ longer, so the oscillation becomes faster as well as less deep.

<Step title="The coefficient">

<Formula label="The coefficient for a smallest probability of one quarter" tex="\frac{|\lambda|}{\Delta} = \sqrt{\frac{0.25}{1 - 0.25}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt3} = 0.577" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The period">

One full turn of the spin takes the time $2\pi/\omega_L$ with $\omega_L = 2|\vec g|/\hbar$. Without the new term $|\vec g| = \Delta$, and with it $|\vec g| = \sqrt{\Delta^2 + \lambda^2}$. The ratio of the periods is

<Formula label="The ratio of the periods" tex="\frac{\text{new period}}{\text{old period}} = \frac{\Delta}{\sqrt{\Delta^2 + \lambda^2}} = \sqrt{1 - p_{\text{min}}} = \sqrt{0.75} = 0.866" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The axis is then at the angle $\theta$ from $z$ with $\cos^2\theta = 0.25$, so $\theta = 60^\circ$, and the spin swings out to $120^\circ$ from the $z$ axis, where $\cos^2 60^\circ = 0.25$. The periods agree with the formula for $|c_1(t)|^2$ in part (a): the probability repeats when $\sqrt{\Delta^2 + \lambda^2}\,t/\hbar$ grows by $\pi$.

</Step>

</Solution>
