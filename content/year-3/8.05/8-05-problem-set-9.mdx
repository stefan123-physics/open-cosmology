Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `hbar` stands for $\hbar$, `pi` for $\pi$, `th` for the polar angle ($\theta_0$ in Problem 1, $\theta$ later), `ph` for $\phi_0$, `mu` for $\mu$, `F` for the electric field, `D` for $\Delta$, `EO` and `EC` for $E_O$ and $E_C$, `g` for $\gamma$, `B0` and `B1` for $B_0$ and $B_1$.

Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of MIT's lecture notes. A direction in space is a unit vector with polar angle $\theta$ and azimuthal angle $\phi$, and the spin state that points along it is

<Formula label="A direction and its spin states" tex="\vec n = (\sin\theta\cos\phi,\ \sin\theta\sin\phi,\ \cos\theta), \qquad |\vec n; +\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,|+\rangle + \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{i\phi}\,|-\rangle, \qquad |\vec n; -\rangle = \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,|+\rangle - \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{i\phi}\,|-\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (4.67), (4.81) and (4.82)" />

Here $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ are the states with spin up and down along $z$. The state $|\vec n; +\rangle$ has spin $+\hbar/2$ along $\vec n$ and $|\vec n; -\rangle$ has $-\hbar/2$. Every normalized state of a spin one-half is, up to a phase, $|\vec n; +\rangle$ for some direction $\vec n$. The spin operators are $S_i = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_i$ with the Pauli matrices $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$, $\sigma_3$, and the spin along $\vec n$ is $S_n = \vec n\cdot\vec S$.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Arbitrary spin state" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A spin is in the state $|\vec n; +\rangle$, where the unit vector $\vec n = (n_x, n_y, n_z)$ has the polar angle $\theta_0$ and the azimuthal angle $\phi_0$. The three spin operators are collected into one vector:

<Formula label="The spin vector operator" tex="\vec S \equiv S_x\,\hat x + S_y\,\hat y + S_z\,\hat z" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

The unit vectors $\hat x$, $\hat y$ and $\hat z$ along the axes are ordinary vectors, not operators on spin states, so they can be moved past any operator.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Work out the vector of expectation values

<Formula label="The expectation value of the spin vector" tex="\langle\vec S\rangle_n \equiv \langle S_x\rangle_n\,\hat x + \langle S_y\rangle_n\,\hat y + \langle S_z\rangle_n\,\hat z" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

where $\langle\dots\rangle_n$ is the expectation value in the state $|\vec n; +\rangle$. Give the components in terms of $\theta_0$ and $\phi_0$. Then say how the vector $\langle\vec S\rangle_n$ is related to $\vec n$.

<Hint>

Write the state as a column with the entries $\cos\frac{\theta_0}{2}$ and $\sin\frac{\theta_0}{2}e^{i\phi_0}$, and each $S_i$ as $\frac{\hbar}{2}$ times a Pauli matrix. An expectation value is row times matrix times column.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The double-angle formulas turn products of $\cos\frac{\theta_0}{2}$ and $\sin\frac{\theta_0}{2}$ into functions of $\theta_0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-rel" label="The vector $\langle\vec S\rangle_n$" choices={["is perpendicular to n and has the length ħ/2", "equals ħ times n", "equals ħ/2 times n", "points along n, with a length that depends on θ₀"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each component is the expectation value of a $2\times 2$ matrix in a known column vector. The three results turn out to be $\frac{\hbar}{2}$ times the three components of $\vec n$.

<Step title="The x component">

Write $c = \cos\frac{\theta_0}{2}$ and $s = \sin\frac{\theta_0}{2}$. The state is the column $(c,\ s\,e^{i\phi_0})$ and the matrix $\sigma_1$ swaps the two entries, so

<Formula label="The expectation value of the x component" tex="\langle S_x\rangle_n = \frac{\hbar}{2}\left(c\cdot s\,e^{i\phi_0} + s\,e^{-i\phi_0}\cdot c\right) = \frac{\hbar}{2}\,2cs\,\cos\phi_0 = \frac{\hbar}{2}\,\sin\theta_0\cos\phi_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The y and z components">

The matrix $\sigma_2$ swaps the entries too, with the factors $-i$ and $i$:

<Formula label="The expectation value of the y component" tex="\langle S_y\rangle_n = \frac{\hbar}{2}\left(-i\,c\,s\,e^{i\phi_0} + i\,s\,e^{-i\phi_0}\,c\right) = \frac{\hbar}{2}\,2cs\,\sin\phi_0 = \frac{\hbar}{2}\,\sin\theta_0\sin\phi_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The matrix $\sigma_3$ is diagonal with the entries $1$ and $-1$:

<Formula label="The expectation value of the z component" tex="\langle S_z\rangle_n = \frac{\hbar}{2}\left(c^2 - s^2\right) = \frac{\hbar}{2}\,\cos\theta_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The vector">

The three results are $\frac{\hbar}{2}$ times the components of $\vec n$ at the start of this page:

<Formula label="The spin vector points along the direction of the state" tex="\langle\vec S\rangle_n = \frac{\hbar}{2}\,\vec n" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\theta_0 = 0$ the state is $|+\rangle$ and the vector is $\frac{\hbar}{2}\hat z$, as it must be. The length of the vector is $\frac{\hbar}{2}$ for every direction. This is what "the spin points along $\vec n$" means: the state is an eigenstate of $S_n$ only, but the averages of all three components line up along $\vec n$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Let $\vec n'$ be another unit vector. What is the value of $\langle\vec S\cdot\vec n'\rangle_n$?

The number box is our addition. Take $\vec n$ with $\theta_0 = 50°$ and $\phi_0 = 20°$, and $\vec n'$ with the polar angle $80°$ and the azimuthal angle $80°$. Give $\langle\vec S\cdot\vec n'\rangle_n$ in units of $\hbar$.

<Hint>

The components of $\vec n'$ are numbers, so they come out of the expectation value. What is left is the vector of part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-dot" label="$\langle\vec S\cdot\vec n'\rangle_n$ equals" choices={["ħ/2, whatever the two directions are", "ħ/2 times cos of the angle between n and n′", "ħ/2 times the sine of the angle between n and n′", "ħ/2 times cos² of half the angle between n and n′"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-num" label="$\langle\vec S\cdot\vec n'\rangle_n$ for these two directions, in units of $\hbar$:" value="0.244" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The operator $\vec S\cdot\vec n'$ is a sum of the three spin operators with numbers in front, so its expectation value is a dot product of two ordinary vectors.

<Step title="The dot product">

<Formula label="The spin along another direction" tex="\langle\vec S\cdot\vec n'\rangle_n = n'_x\langle S_x\rangle_n + n'_y\langle S_y\rangle_n + n'_z\langle S_z\rangle_n = \langle\vec S\rangle_n\cdot\vec n' = \frac{\hbar}{2}\,\vec n\cdot\vec n'" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Since both are unit vectors, $\vec n\cdot\vec n'$ is the cosine of the angle between them.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

With the components from the start of the page, $\vec n = (0.720,\ 0.262,\ 0.643)$ and $\vec n' = (0.171,\ 0.970,\ 0.174)$, so $\vec n\cdot\vec n' = 0.123 + 0.254 + 0.112 = 0.489$ and $\langle\vec S\cdot\vec n'\rangle_n = 0.244\,\hbar$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\vec n' = \vec n$ the result is $\frac{\hbar}{2}$, the eigenvalue. For $\vec n' = -\vec n$ it is $-\frac{\hbar}{2}$. For $\vec n'$ at right angles to $\vec n$ it is zero: a measurement gives $+\frac{\hbar}{2}$ and $-\frac{\hbar}{2}$ equally often. This agrees with the probability $\cos^2$ of half the angle for the result $+\frac{\hbar}{2}$: the average is $\frac{\hbar}{2}\left(\cos^2\frac{\gamma}{2} - \sin^2\frac{\gamma}{2}\right) = \frac{\hbar}{2}\cos\gamma$ for two directions an angle $\gamma$ apart.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Rotating spin operators and spin states" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

Problem Set 3 introduced an operator built from a unit vector $\vec n$ and an angle $\alpha$:

<Formula label="The rotation operator" tex="R_\alpha(\vec n) \equiv \exp\left(-\frac{i\alpha\,S_n}{\hbar}\right) = \exp\left(-i\,\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

The claim was that it turns the spin state along a direction $\vec n'$ into the spin state along a new direction: $\vec n'$ rotated through the angle $\alpha$ about the axis $\vec n$. This problem shows why.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Take a very small angle, $\alpha = \epsilon$. Work out how the rotation operator acts on the spin vector operator $\vec S$ of Problem 1, by finding the term of order $\epsilon$ in

<Formula label="The equation to complete" tex="R_\epsilon^\dagger(\vec n)\,\vec S\,R_\epsilon(\vec n) = \vec S + \epsilon\,(\dots) + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), eq. (1), p. 1" />

Terms of order $\epsilon^2$ can be dropped. The second box is our addition: the same equation for the $x$ component alone, when the axis is $\vec n = \hat z$.

<Hint>

Expand the exponential to first order in $\epsilon$, for $R_\epsilon$ and for its adjoint. The term of order $\epsilon$ is a commutator of $S_n$ with $\vec S$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Work with one component at a time, say $S_x$, and write $S_n = n_xS_x + n_yS_y + n_zS_z$. The commutator of two different spin operators is the third one, with a sign that depends on their order: compare the result with the components of a cross product.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-term" label="The term of order $\epsilon$ is $\epsilon$ times" choices={["−n × S", "(n · S) n", "n × S", "i n × S"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p2-z" label="For $\vec n = \hat z$: $R_\epsilon^\dagger S_x R_\epsilon = S_x + \epsilon\,(\dots)$, with $(\dots) =$" choices={["+S_y", "−S_y", "+S_z", "0"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** To first order the rotation operator is one plus a small multiple of $S_n$, so the change of any operator is a commutator with $S_n$. The commutators of the spin operators have the same structure as a cross product.

<Step title="First order">

To first order, $R_\epsilon = 1 - \frac{i\epsilon}{\hbar}S_n$ and, because $S_n$ is Hermitian, $R_\epsilon^\dagger = 1 + \frac{i\epsilon}{\hbar}S_n$. For one component, say $S_x$:

<Formula label="The change is a commutator" tex="R_\epsilon^\dagger\,S_x\,R_\epsilon = S_x + \frac{i\epsilon}{\hbar}\left[S_n, S_x\right] + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The commutator">

The spin operators obey

<Formula label="The algebra of the spin operators" tex="\left[S_x, S_y\right] = i\hbar\,S_z, \qquad \left[S_y, S_z\right] = i\hbar\,S_x, \qquad \left[S_z, S_x\right] = i\hbar\,S_y" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.17)" />

With $S_n = n_xS_x + n_yS_y + n_zS_z$, and since $S_x$ commutes with itself:

<Formula label="The commutator with the spin along n" tex="\left[S_n, S_x\right] = n_y\left[S_y, S_x\right] + n_z\left[S_z, S_x\right] = -i\hbar\left(n_yS_z - n_zS_y\right) = -i\hbar\,(\vec n\times\vec S)_x" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The bracket $n_yS_z - n_zS_y$ is the $x$ component of the cross product of $\vec n$ and $\vec S$. The $y$ and $z$ components follow in the same way, by moving each of $x$, $y$, $z$ one place on.

</Step>

<Step title="The result">

The factor $\frac{i\epsilon}{\hbar}$ times $-i\hbar$ is $+\epsilon$, for every component:

<Formula label="An infinitesimal rotation of the spin operator" tex="R_\epsilon^\dagger(\vec n)\,\vec S\,R_\epsilon(\vec n) = \vec S + \epsilon\,\vec n\times\vec S + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), eq. (1), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

For $\vec n = \hat z$ the cross product is $\hat z\times\vec S = -S_y\,\hat x + S_x\,\hat y$, so the $x$ component is $S_x - \epsilon\,S_y$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

An ordinary vector that is turned through the small angle $\epsilon$ about $\vec n$ changes by $\epsilon$ times the cross product of $\vec n$ with it (notes chapter 7, eq. (2.21), with the angular velocity times the time equal to $\epsilon\,\vec n$). So the three operators $S_x$, $S_y$, $S_z$ change among themselves just as the components of a rotated vector do. For the axis $\hat z$ this can be compared with the exact result for any angle, $S_x\cos\alpha - S_y\sin\alpha$, which follows from the same commutators: its first-order part is $S_x - \alpha\,S_y$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Take the expectation value of the equation of part (a) in the spin state $|\vec n'; +\rangle$, and use Problem 1 to argue that $R_\epsilon(\vec n)$ rotates spin states as claimed.

The number box is our addition. A spin is in the state with spin up along $x$. The operator $R_\epsilon(\hat z)$ with $\epsilon = 0.0200$ radians acts on it. Give $\langle S_y\rangle$ in the new state, in units of $\hbar$, to first order in $\epsilon$.

<Hint>

The expectation value of $R_\epsilon^\dagger\,\vec S\,R_\epsilon$ in the state $|\vec n'; +\rangle$ is the expectation value of $\vec S$ in the state $R_\epsilon|\vec n'; +\rangle$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The new state is a normalized spin state, so it is the state $|\vec m; +\rangle$ for some direction $\vec m$, up to a phase. Problem 1 tells you its vector of expectation values in terms of $\vec m$. Compare.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-sy" label="$\langle S_y\rangle$ in the new state, in units of $\hbar$:" value="0.0100" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-sy" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** By Problem 1 a spin state is known, up to a phase, once the direction of its vector $\langle\vec S\rangle$ is known. Part (a) says how that vector changes.

<Step title="The spin vector of the new state">

Let $|\psi\rangle = R_\epsilon(\vec n)\,|\vec n'; +\rangle$. Then, by part (a) and Problem 1,

<Formula label="The spin vector after the rotation operator has acted" tex="\langle\psi|\,\vec S\,|\psi\rangle = \langle\vec S\rangle_{n'} + \epsilon\,\vec n\times\langle\vec S\rangle_{n'} = \frac{\hbar}{2}\left(\vec n' + \epsilon\,\vec n\times\vec n'\right) + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The components of $\vec n$ are numbers and come out of the expectation value, which is why the cross product can be taken after it.

</Step>

<Step title="The direction of the new state">

The operator $R_\epsilon$ is unitary, so $|\psi\rangle$ is normalized. Every normalized spin state is $|\vec m; +\rangle$ for some unit vector $\vec m$, up to a phase, and by Problem 1 its spin vector is then $\frac{\hbar}{2}\vec m$. Comparing with the last formula,

<Formula label="The direction of the rotated state" tex="\vec m = \vec n' + \epsilon\,\vec n\times\vec n' + \mathcal{O}(\epsilon^2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is $\vec n'$ turned through the angle $\epsilon$ about $\vec n$: the change is at right angles to both $\vec n$ and $\vec n'$, and its size is $\epsilon$ times the distance of the tip of $\vec n'$ from the axis. So $R_\epsilon(\vec n)\,|\vec n'; +\rangle$ is the spin state along the rotated direction.

</Step>

<Step title="A finite angle">

For a finite angle, split $\alpha$ into a large number of equal small angles. $R_\alpha(\vec n)$ is the product of that many rotation operators, each with the small angle, because all the exponents commute. Each factor turns the direction through its small angle about $\vec n$, with an error proportional to the square of that angle. The errors add up to something proportional to the small angle itself, which vanishes as the pieces are made smaller. So $R_\alpha(\vec n)$ turns the direction through the full angle $\alpha$.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

Here $\vec n = \hat z$ and $\vec n' = \hat x$, and $\hat z\times\hat x = \hat y$. The new spin vector is $\frac{\hbar}{2}\left(\hat x + \epsilon\,\hat y\right)$, so $\langle S_y\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2}\times 0.0200 = 0.0100\,\hbar$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The exact rotation of $\hat x$ through the angle $\epsilon$ about $\hat z$ has the $y$ component $\sin\epsilon$, which gives $\langle S_y\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sin(0.0200) = 0.0100\,\hbar$ to three figures. The sign is right: a turn about $\hat z$ in the positive sense carries $\hat x$ towards $\hat y$. This result is what Lecture 16 used: the time evolution operator of a spin in a magnetic field has the form of $R_\alpha(\vec n)$ with an angle that grows in time, so the spin precesses.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The ammonia molecule" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

An ammonia molecule sits in an electric field $F$. With the average energy set to zero, its Hamiltonian is

<Formula label="Ammonia in an electric field" tex="H = \begin{pmatrix}\mu F & -\Delta\\ -\Delta & -\mu F\end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

Here $\mu$ is the electric dipole moment of the molecule and $\Delta > 0$. Take $F \ge 0$. The basis states are $|1\rangle = |{\uparrow}\rangle$, with the nitrogen atom above the plane of the three hydrogen atoms, and $|2\rangle = |{\downarrow}\rangle$, with the nitrogen below it. They are not energy eigenstates. In the language of spins, $|{\uparrow}\rangle$ plays the part of $|+\rangle$ and $|{\downarrow}\rangle$ that of $|-\rangle$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Show that the Hamiltonian can be written as

<Formula label="The Hamiltonian as a spin in a field" tex="H = -K\,\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2" />

with a constant $K > 0$ and a unit vector $\vec n$ with the polar angles $(\theta, \phi)$. Give $K$, $\cos\theta$ and $\phi$ in terms of $\mu F$ and $\Delta$.

<Hint>

Write the matrix $\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma = n_x\sigma_1 + n_y\sigma_2 + n_z\sigma_3$ in full and compare it, entry by entry, with $H$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The vector $-K\vec n$ has the components you read off. $K$ is its length, because $\vec n$ is a unit vector. Mind the signs: $K$ is positive, so the signs sit in $\vec n$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-k" label="$K =$" expr="sqrt(D^2 + (mu*F)^2)"
  vars={{ D: [1.2, 2], mu: [0.5, 1.5], F: [0.3, 0.8] }}
  names={{ D: "\\Delta", mu: "\\mu" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "D + mu*F", hint: "Δ and μF multiply different Pauli matrices, so they are components of one vector at right angles to each other. The length of a vector is not the sum of its components." },
    { expr: "sqrt(D^2 - (mu*F)^2)", hint: "This would be zero for μF = Δ, and the matrix H is not zero then. The squares of the components add." },
    { expr: "D^2 + (mu*F)^2", hint: "That is K², with the units of an energy squared. K multiplies a matrix of pure numbers and must be an energy." }
  ]} />

<Formula label="The probability is frozen by the strong field" tex="P_{\text{up}}(t) \approx \sin^2\frac{\Delta t_1}{\hbar} \quad\text{for}\quad t > t_1" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(e), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Spin states for two particles" points={10} mit="Problem 6">

<Answer id="p6-why" label="The reason is that" choices={["R is Hermitian, so its eigenvalues are real", "R is unitary, so it keeps states orthogonal", "R commutes with S_z, so it leaves |−⟩ alone", "R squares to one, so it swaps the two states"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A rotation operator is unitary, so it keeps orthogonal states orthogonal, and in two dimensions there is only one direction orthogonal to a given state.

<Step title="Orthogonality is kept">

$R = \exp\left(-i\alpha S_n/\hbar\right)$ with a Hermitian $S_n$ is unitary: $R^\dagger R = 1$. So

<Formula label="The rotated states stay orthogonal" tex="\left(R|-\rangle\right)^\dagger\left(R|+\rangle\right) = \langle -|\,R^\dagger R\,|+\rangle = \langle -|+\rangle = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

and $R|-\rangle$ has length one, because $|-\rangle$ has.

</Step>

<Step title="Only one state is left">

So $R|-\rangle$ is a normalized state orthogonal to $R|+\rangle = |\vec n; +\rangle$. The states $|\vec n; +\rangle$ and $|\vec n; -\rangle$ are an orthonormal basis of the two-dimensional space (notes chapter 2, eq. (4.84)). A state with no part along $|\vec n; +\rangle$ is a number times $|\vec n; -\rangle$, and a number of size one is a phase:

<Formula label="The rotated spin-down state" tex="R\,|-\rangle = e^{i\delta}\,|\vec n; -\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In pictures: a rotation that carries $\hat z$ to $\vec n$ carries $-\hat z$ to $-\vec n$. The state $|-\rangle$ is the spin along $-\hat z$, and $|\vec n; -\rangle$ is the spin along $-\vec n$. An example is the rotation by $90°$ about the $y$ axis used in part (b).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Step>

<Step title="Multiplying out">

Write $c = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}$ and $s = \sin\frac{\theta}{2}$, so that $|\vec n; +\rangle = c\,|+\rangle + s\,e^{i\phi}|-\rangle$ and $|\vec n; -\rangle = s\,|+\rangle - c\,e^{i\phi}|-\rangle$. In the difference of the two products the coefficients are:

- of $|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$: $cs - sc = 0$;
- of $|-\rangle\otimes|-\rangle$: $-sc\,e^{2i\phi} + cs\,e^{2i\phi} = 0$;
- of $|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle$: $-c^2e^{i\phi} - s^2e^{i\phi} = -e^{i\phi}$;
- of $|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$: $s^2e^{i\phi} + c^2e^{i\phi} = +e^{i\phi}$.

So

<Formula label="The antisymmetric combination in another basis" tex="|\vec n; +\rangle\otimes|\vec n; -\rangle - |\vec n; -\rangle\otimes|\vec n; +\rangle = -e^{i\phi}\left(|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle - |-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The result">

<Formula label="The singlet is invariant under rotations" tex="(R\otimes R)\,|\Psi\rangle = -e^{i(\delta + \phi)}\,|\Psi\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The state comes back multiplied by a phase, which does not change the physical state. This is the statement of the notes that the singlet has the same form for every choice of direction (notes chapter 8, eq. (5.3)).

</Step>

<Step title="The example">

For the rotation by $90°$ about $y$, $R = \cos\frac{\pi}{4} - i\sigma_2\sin\frac{\pi}{4}$, and $-i\sigma_2$ sends $|+\rangle$ to $|-\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ to $-|+\rangle$. So

<Formula label="A rotation by 90 degrees about the y axis" tex="R\,|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|+\rangle + |-\rangle\right), \qquad R\,|-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(-|+\rangle + |-\rangle\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The first is the spin state along $x$ ($\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$, $\phi = 0$), and the second is $-|\vec n; -\rangle$ for that direction, so $e^{i\delta} = -1$ and the phase is $-e^{i(\delta + \phi)} = +1$. The state comes back exactly: $\langle\Psi|(R\otimes R)|\Psi\rangle = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Multiply the example out directly. $R|+\rangle\otimes R|-\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\left(-|{+}{+}\rangle + |{+}{-}\rangle - |{-}{+}\rangle + |{-}{-}\rangle\right)$ and $R|-\rangle\otimes R|+\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\left(-|{+}{+}\rangle - |{+}{-}\rangle + |{-}{+}\rangle + |{-}{-}\rangle\right)$, writing $|{+}{-}\rangle$ for $|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle$. The difference is $|{+}{-}\rangle - |{-}{+}\rangle$: the singlet again, with the factor one. There is a reason behind it. For any $2\times 2$ matrix $R$ the antisymmetric combination comes back multiplied by the determinant of $R$, and a rotation operator $\exp\left(-i\frac{\alpha}{2}\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma\right)$ has the determinant one, since its two eigenvalues are $e^{-i\alpha/2}$ and $e^{i\alpha/2}$. So for rotation operators the phase is always one. Lecture 20 uses this invariance: in the singlet the two spins are opposite along every direction, whichever axis is measured.

</Step>
