The Stern–Gerlach experiments suggested that spin one-half lives in a two-dimensional complex vector space. This lecture builds that space: states as column vectors, the inner product, and the spin operators as matrices, ending with the spin state that points in any direction (transcript pp. 1–23; notes chapter 2, sections 2 and 4). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's recorded lecture and his notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. Zwiebach writes states as kets and bras, which this site's read-aloud cannot yet say, so this page names them $\chi_{z+}$, $\chi_{z-}$ and so on, and writes the inner product of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ as $(\alpha, \beta)$.)

## The state space

The two basis states $\chi_{z+}$ and $\chi_{z-}$ have $S_z = +\hbar/2$ and $-\hbar/2$: they are eigenstates of the operator $\hat S_z$ (transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Basis states as eigenstates of Sz" tex="\hat S_z\,\chi_{z\pm} = \pm\frac{\hbar}{2}\,\chi_{z\pm}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.2)" />

(They stand for Zwiebach's kets for $z$ up and down, and the notes also call them states 1 and 2, here $\chi_1$ and $\chi_2$.) The physical assumption is that these two suffice: every spin state, including those that point along $x$ or $y$, is a superposition (transcript pp. 2–3),

<Formula label="General spin state" tex="\Psi = c_1\,\chi_{z+} + c_2\,\chi_{z-}, \qquad c_1, c_2 \text{ complex}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.3)" />

so the states form a **two-dimensional complex vector space**: complex because the coefficients are complex numbers, two-dimensional because there are two basis vectors.

**A representation** (transcript pp. 4–5). To make this concrete, represent the basis states by column vectors, $\chi_{z+} \leftrightarrow (1, 0)^T$ and $\chi_{z-} \leftrightarrow (0, 1)^T$, so that $\Psi \leftrightarrow (c_1, c_2)^T$. (The notes also call the basis states 1 and 2.)

**Orthonormality** (transcript pp. 5–6). The experiment with two $\hat z$ filters showed that $z+$ states have no overlap with $z-$ states and pass completely as $z+$: the basis is orthonormal, $(\chi_i, \chi_j) = \delta_{ij}$. To compute overlaps, associate with each state a **bra**, the row vector of **complex conjugated** components (transcript pp. 6–7):

<Formula label="Inner product" tex="(\alpha, \beta) = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1^* & \alpha_2^*\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_1\\ \beta_2\end{pmatrix} = \alpha_1^*\beta_1 + \alpha_2^*\beta_2" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.11), (2.12)" />

The conjugation makes $(\alpha, \alpha) = |\alpha_1|^2 + |\alpha_2|^2$ a positive length squared.

**The operator $\hat S_z$ as a matrix** (transcript pp. 7–8). Operators act on two-component columns, so they are $2 \times 2$ matrices. A matrix that gives each basis vector back with the right eigenvalue does the right thing on every vector:

<Formula label="Sz as a matrix" tex="\hat S_z = \frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.13)" />

## Finding Ŝx and Ŝy

What are the states along $x$, and the operators $\hat S_x$ and $\hat S_y$? Rather than follow Feynman's analysis of rotated Stern–Gerlach machines, Zwiebach borrows from orbital angular momentum (transcript pp. 8–11). There $\hat L_x$, $\hat L_y$, $\hat L_z$ are Hermitian and satisfy the **algebra of angular momentum**; declare that spin obeys the same:

<Formula label="Spin algebra" tex="[\hat S_i, \hat S_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,\hat S_k, \qquad [\hat S_x, \hat S_y] = i\hbar\hat S_z,\ \ [\hat S_y, \hat S_z] = i\hbar\hat S_x,\ \ [\hat S_z, \hat S_x] = i\hbar\hat S_y" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.17), (2.18)" />

with $\epsilon_{ijk}$ totally antisymmetric and $\epsilon_{123} = 1$, repeated indices summed. $\hat S_z$ is indeed Hermitian: equal to its own conjugate transpose. (Spin differs from orbital angular momentum in its eigenvalues, $\pm\hbar/2$ rather than integer multiples of $\hbar$, but this algebra is assumed to hold.)

**Hermitian $2 \times 2$ matrices** (transcript pp. 11–13). The general one has real numbers on the diagonal and complex conjugates off it. Any part proportional to the identity commutes with everything and cannot help with commutators, and any part proportional to $\hat S_z$ should be removed, since $\hat S_x$ and $\hat S_y$ must be independent of it. What remains is

<Formula label="What remains" tex="\begin{pmatrix}0 & a - ib\\ a + ib & 0\end{pmatrix} = a\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix} + b\begin{pmatrix}0 & -i\\ i & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad a, b \text{ real}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.20)–(2.22)" />

The Hermitian $2 \times 2$ matrices form a **real** vector space with four basis matrices: these two, the diagonal one of $\hat S_z$, and the identity. The scale is fixed by physics: measured along $x$ or $y$, the spin must also give $\pm\hbar/2$. Both matrices have eigenvalues $\pm1$, with normalized eigenvectors $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, \pm1)^T$ and $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, \pm i)^T$ (transcript pp. 14–15). So try

<Formula label="The spin operators" tex="\hat S_x = \frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat S_y = \frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0 & -i\\ i & 0\end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.26)" />

and check the algebra: $[\hat S_x, \hat S_y] = \frac{\hbar^2}{4}\left[\begin{pmatrix}i & 0\\ 0 & -i\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}-i & 0\\ 0 & i\end{pmatrix}\right] = i\hbar\hat S_z$, and the other two work too (transcript p. 15). The answer is not unique (for instance, replacing $\hat S_x \to -\hat S_y$ and $\hat S_y \to \hat S_x$ leaves the algebra unchanged), but any consistent choice is equivalent, and everyone uses this one (transcript pp. 13–14). In terms of the **Pauli matrices**:

<Formula label="Pauli matrices" tex="\hat S_i = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_i, \qquad \sigma_1 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -i\\ i & 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_3 = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.29), (2.30)" />

Each squares to the identity, consistent with eigenvalues $\pm1$ (transcript p. 16).

## States along x and y

The eigenvectors of $\hat S_x$ and $\hat S_y$ are the spin states along $x$ and $y$, not new states but superpositions of the old (transcript pp. 16–18):

<Formula label="States along x and y" tex="\chi_{x\pm} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\chi_{z+} \pm \chi_{z-}\right), \qquad \chi_{y\pm} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\chi_{z+} \pm i\,\chi_{z-}\right)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.31)–(2.36)" />

Inverting, $\chi_{z+} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\chi_{x+} + \chi_{x-})$, so the amplitude for $z+$ to pass an $x$ filter as $x+$ is $(\chi_{x+}, \chi_{z+}) = 1/\sqrt2$, and as $x-$ also $1/\sqrt2$: probabilities $\frac12$ each, as the experiment found. The $x$ pair already uses up the real combinations of equal weight; the factor $i$ is what allows a second such pair, the $y$ states, orthogonal to each other and with the same probabilities one half against $z$.

## Spin along any direction

Take a unit vector $\vec n = (\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\theta)$ and define the **spin operator along $\vec n$** (transcript pp. 18–20). The triplet $\hat{\vec S} = (\hat S_x, \hat S_y, \hat S_z)$ is only a bookkeeping device (its unit vectors have nothing to do with the space the matrices act on), but its dot product with $\vec n$ is a single operator:

<Formula label="Spin along n" tex="\hat S_n \equiv \vec n\cdot\hat{\vec S} = n_x\hat S_x + n_y\hat S_y + n_z\hat S_z = \frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta\,e^{-i\phi}\\ \sin\theta\,e^{i\phi} & -\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (4.67)–(4.73)" />

Setting the determinant of $\hat S_n - \lambda\cdot 1$ to zero gives $\lambda = \pm\hbar/2$ (transcript p. 20). For the eigenvector with $+\hbar/2$, write $\chi_{n+} = c_1\chi_{z+} + c_2\chi_{z-}$; the two equations agree and give $c_2 = e^{i\phi}\frac{1 - \cos\theta}{\sin\theta}c_1 = e^{i\phi}\tan\frac{\theta}{2}\,c_1$, by the half-angle identities. Normalizing, $|c_1| = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}$, and an overall phase can be dropped (transcript pp. 20–22):

<Formula label="Spin state along n" tex="\chi_{n+} = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,\chi_{z+} + \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{i\phi}\,\chi_{z-}, \qquad \chi_{n-} = -\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{-i\phi}\,\chi_{z+} + \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,\chi_{z-}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (4.81), (4.83)" />

At $\theta = 0$ these give $\chi_{z+}$ and $\chi_{z-}$, whatever $\phi$ (which is undefined at the pole). For $\chi_{n-}$ the most direct solution, $\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\chi_{z+} - \cos\frac{\theta}{2}e^{i\phi}\chi_{z-}$, would give $-e^{i\phi}\chi_{z-}$ at $\theta = 0$, with an ill-defined phase; multiplying it by $-e^{-i\phi}$ gives the form above, well behaved at the north pole (transcript pp. 22–23). The two states are orthonormal (notes eq. (4.84)). Along $x$ ($\theta = \pi/2$, $\phi = 0$) they give $\chi_{x+}$ and $-\chi_{x-}$, and along $y$ ($\phi = \pi/2$) $\chi_{y+}$ and $i\chi_{y-}$: the same states up to phases, which carry no physics (notes eqs. (4.85)–(4.87)). The rephasing does not remove every ambiguity: at the south pole, $\theta = \pi$, the phase $\phi$ becomes ill-defined again for $\chi_{n+}$, and there a different phase choice works better (notes footnote 3). So every spin direction is a state of the two-dimensional space: the model is complete.

<WorkedExample title="A spin tilted by 60°">
<Problem>
A spin is prepared along $\vec n$ with $\theta = 60°$, $\phi = 0$ (in the $xz$ plane). (a) Write $\chi_{n+}$. (b) It enters a $\hat z$ filter. What are the probabilities of $z+$ and $z-$? (c) What is the expectation value of $\hat S_z$? (d) What is the probability that it passes an $x$ filter as $x+$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The state">
$\chi_{n+} = \cos30°\,\chi_{z+} + \sin30°\,\chi_{z-} \approx 0.866\,\chi_{z+} + 0.5\,\chi_{z-}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A z filter">
$P(z+) = \cos^230° = 0.75$ and $P(z-) = \sin^230° = 0.25$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Mean Sz">
$\langle\hat S_z\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2}(0.75 - 0.25) = 0.25\hbar = \frac{\hbar}{2}\cos60°$: the classical projection of a spin $\hbar/2$ on the $z$ axis, recovered only on average.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) An x filter">
$(\chi_{x+}, \chi_{n+}) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(0.866 + 0.5) \approx 0.966$, so $P(x+) \approx 0.933 = \cos^2 15°$: the spin is $30°$ from the $x$ axis, and the probability is the cosine squared of half that angle.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Spin one-half: a two-dimensional complex vector space with orthonormal basis $\chi_{z\pm}$, represented by $(1, 0)^T$ and $(0, 1)^T$; inner product $(\alpha, \beta) = \alpha_1^*\beta_1 + \alpha_2^*\beta_2$.
- Hermiticity, the angular-momentum algebra and eigenvalues $\pm\hbar/2$ give $\hat S_i = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_i$ with the Pauli matrices.
- $\chi_{x\pm} = (\chi_{z+} \pm \chi_{z-})/\sqrt2$, $\chi_{y\pm} = (\chi_{z+} \pm i\chi_{z-})/\sqrt2$, reproducing the Stern–Gerlach probabilities of one half.
- Along any $\vec n$: $\hat S_n = \vec n\cdot\hat{\vec S}$ has eigenvalues $\pm\hbar/2$ and $\chi_{n+} = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\chi_{z+} + \sin\frac{\theta}{2}e^{i\phi}\chi_{z-}$.
