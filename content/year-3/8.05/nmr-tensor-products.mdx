This lecture finishes nuclear magnetic resonance, the last example of two-state dynamics, and then starts a new subject: states of several particles and the **tensor product** of vector spaces (transcript pp. 1–24; notes chapter 7, section 6, and chapter 8, section 1). Zwiebach introduced the tensor product here, a couple of weeks before adding angular momenta, so that the mathematics is familiar by then (transcript p. 1). (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## The rotating frame

The set-up from last time: a large static field $B_0$ along $z$ and a much smaller field $B_1$ rotating clockwise in the $xy$ plane at angular frequency $\omega$ (transcript p. 1). The spin Hamiltonian is (transcript p. 2)

<Formula label="NMR Hamiltonian" tex="H_S(t) = -\gamma\vec B(t)\cdot\vec S = -\gamma\left(B_0S_z + B_1\left(\cos\omega t\,S_x - \sin\omega t\,S_y\right)\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (6.24)" />

It depends on time and does not commute with itself at different times, so the only general tool is the time-ordered exponential, an endless series that is rarely of practical help (transcript p. 2). Instead, move to a frame rotating with the field and hope that there the Hamiltonian is constant. In a frame rotating at $\omega$ about $z$, static spins appear to precess, as they would under the Hamiltonian $H_U = \omega S_z$ (transcript pp. 2–3). So define

<Formula label="Rotating-frame state" tex="\psi_R(t) = U(t)\,\psi(t), \qquad U(t) = \exp\left(-\frac{i\omega t\,S_z}{\hbar}\right)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (6.25)–(6.26)" />

This time Zwiebach found the new Hamiltonian in a quicker way than in the last lecture. Write $\psi_R(t) = U(t)\,U_S(t)\,\psi(0)$, where $U_S$ is the (unknown) time evolution under $H_S$. For any time-evolution operator the Hamiltonian is $i\hbar\,(dU/dt)\,U^\dagger$; applying that to the product $UU_S$, the derivative of $U$ gives one term and the derivative of $U_S$ gives $H_S$ sandwiched between $U$ and $U^\dagger$ (transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Hamiltonian in the rotating frame" tex="H_R = i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}\left(UU_S\right)\left(UU_S\right)^\dagger = H_U + U H_S U^\dagger" cite="reading2" at="eq. (6.28)" />

(A student pointed out that the Hamiltonian of $U$ alone should not be called $H_R$; Zwiebach agreed and renamed it $H_U$, transcript p. 5.) The new Hamiltonian is the transformed old one plus the Hamiltonian of the transformation.

## The time dependence disappears

Since $S_z$ commutes with $U$, the $B_0$ term passes through unchanged and joins $\omega S_z$ (transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="The remaining operator" tex="H_R = \left(\omega - \gamma B_0\right)S_z - \gamma B_1 M(t), \qquad M(t) = e^{-i\omega tS_z/\hbar}\left(\cos\omega t\,S_x - \sin\omega t\,S_y\right)e^{i\omega tS_z/\hbar}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (6.29)" />

Rather than expanding the exponentials, Zwiebach suggested a trick worth remembering: when an operator looks complicated, take its time derivative (transcript p. 6). Differentiating the outer exponentials gives a commutator with $S_z$, using $\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{tA}Be^{-tA}\right) = e^{tA}[A, B]e^{-tA}$ (transcript p. 9), and differentiating the middle gives $-\omega\sin\omega t\,S_x - \omega\cos\omega t\,S_y$. With $[S_z, S_x] = i\hbar S_y$ and $[S_z, S_y] = -i\hbar S_x$, the commutator term is $\omega\cos\omega t\,S_y + \omega\sin\omega t\,S_x$, which cancels the rest exactly (transcript pp. 6–7):

<Formula label="M does not change" tex="\frac{dM}{dt} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M(t) = M(0) = S_x" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (6.30)–(6.31)" />

In the rotating frame the rotating field simply stays along $x$, where it was at $t = 0$ (transcript pp. 7–8). With the Larmor frequency $\omega_0 = \gamma B_0$ of the static field, the Hamiltonian becomes that of a spin in a constant effective field (transcript pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="Effective field" tex="H_R = -\gamma\vec B_R\cdot\vec S, \qquad \vec B_R = B_1\,\hat x + B_0\left(1 - \frac{\omega}{\omega_0}\right)\hat z" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (6.32)–(6.33)" />

Because $H_R$ does not depend on time, $\psi_R$ evolves with $e^{-iH_Rt/\hbar}$, and $\psi_R(0) = \psi(0)$ since $U(0) = 1$. Undoing the rotation gives the complete solution (transcript p. 8):

<Formula label="Complete solution" tex="\psi(t) = \exp\left(\frac{i\omega t\,S_z}{\hbar}\right)\exp\left(\frac{i\gamma\,\vec B_R\cdot\vec S\;t}{\hbar}\right)\psi(0)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (6.36)" />

(On the board the effective field was first written with its unit vectors swapped; a student caught it, transcript p. 9.) With $B_1 = 0$ the two exponentials combine into $e^{i\omega_0tS_z/\hbar}$, plain precession about $z$ at the Larmor frequency, as it should (notes p. 19).

## What the spin does

In practice $B_1 \ll B_0$. Take a spin that starts along $+z$ (transcript p. 10).

- **Far from resonance, $\omega \ll \omega_0$.** Then $\vec B_R \approx B_0\hat z + B_1\hat x$, a field mostly along $z$, tipped slightly towards $x$. The right-hand exponential makes the spin precess quickly, at about $\omega_0$, on a narrow cone around $\vec B_R$; the left-hand one slowly carries the whole cone round the $z$ axis at $\omega$ (transcript pp. 10–11; notes eq. (6.38)). The spin never strays far from $z$.
- **At resonance, $\omega = \omega_0$.** The $z$ part of the effective field vanishes and $\vec B_R = B_1\hat x$. In the rotating frame the spin now turns about the $x$ axis at $\omega_1 = \gamma B_1$ (in the negative sense, about $-\hat x$, because of the minus sign in $-\gamma\vec B\cdot\vec S$), moving from $z$ towards $y$, while the left-hand exponential spins it rapidly about $z$ at $\omega_0$. The tip of the spin traces a spiral down the sphere (transcript pp. 11–12; notes eq. (6.39) and p. 20).

Physicists know $\omega_0$ and tune the radio frequency to it (transcript p. 11). Leaving the field on for a time $T$ with (transcript p. 12)

<Formula label="The 90° pulse" tex="\omega_1T = \frac{\pi}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = \frac{\pi}{2\gamma B_1}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (6.40)" />

brings the spin down to the equator. Then $B_1$ is switched off, and the spin keeps rotating in the $xy$ plane about the static field. (Zwiebach first called it a "half pulse" before settling on the usual name, the 90° pulse.) Finding the equation of the spiral was left as a homework exercise (transcript p. 13).

## Magnetic resonance imaging

This is the technique behind MRI, one of the great technological uses of quantum mechanics, which lets doctors tell soft tissues apart in a way X-rays cannot (transcript p. 13; notes p. 20). Edward Purcell and Felix Bloch shared the 1952 Nobel Prize for nuclear magnetic resonance, and the first human studies by MRI came in 1977 (notes p. 20).

- **The magnet.** A typical scanner has $B_0$ of about 2 tesla (20,000 gauss), from a superconducting solenoid cooled with liquid helium. (The lecture says 3 kelvin; liquid helium boils at about 4.2 kelvin at atmospheric pressure.) "Open" scanners for claustrophobic patients use weaker fields (transcript p. 13; notes p. 21).
- **The signal.** The protons of the hydrogen in water carry magnetic moments, and at body temperature only a tiny excess of them, of order one in a million in Zwiebach's estimate, lines up with $B_0$, which is enough (transcript p. 14). A 90° pulse sets them rotating in the plane at $\omega_0$; a rotating magnetic dipole radiates, and receivers pick up a signal proportional to the proton density, which already distinguishes tissues (transcript p. 14; notes p. 20).
- **Two relaxation times.** The rotation dies away with a time $T_2$, typically well under a second, through interactions between spins; the alignment with $B_0$ returns with a time $T_1$, of order a second, through interactions with the surrounding atoms (transcript p. 14; notes pp. 20–21). These are not Schrödinger evolution of a single spin and are measured, not computed; tables of ($T_1$, $T_2$) for known substances help identify what is seen (transcript pp. 14–16). (In the lecture Zwiebach said $T_2$ alone separates white matter, grey matter and fluid in the brain; the notes credit $T_1$ for this, since those tissues have about the same proton density.)
- **Where the signal comes from.** Gradient magnets, the source of the loud knocking, make $B_0$ and hence $\omega_0$ vary with position, so the frequency of the signal tells where it came from. Resolutions of about half a millimetre are reached (transcript p. 15; notes p. 21).

## States of two particles

Now a new subject. Particle 1 is described by a complex vector space $V$, with its operators (position, momentum, spin, Hamiltonian …), and particle 2 by a complex vector space $W$ with its own (transcript p. 16). A first guess would describe the pair by listing a state $v$ of the first and a state $w$ of the second. That is a possible state, but not the general one. Write it instead as $v \otimes w$, an element of a new complex vector space, the **tensor product** $V \otimes W$; nothing is multiplied, the symbol just places the two vectors side by side (transcript p. 17; notes eq. (1.1)). Two rules make it physical (transcript pp. 17–19):

<Formula label="Scaling" tex="(av)\otimes w = v\otimes(aw) = a\,(v\otimes w)" cite="reading3" at="eq. (1.2)" />

Doubling the amplitude for one particle doubles the amplitude for the pair, and the number comes out without complex conjugation. Superpositions of one particle give superpositions of the pair:

<Formula label="Distributive rules" tex="(v_1 + v_2)\otimes w = v_1\otimes w + v_2\otimes w, \qquad v\otimes(w_1 + w_2) = v\otimes w_1 + v\otimes w_2" cite="reading3" at="eq. (1.3)" />

Without these rules, $\alpha v \otimes w$ and $v \otimes w$ would be unrelated vectors and the space would be far too large. (Zwiebach noted that some textbooks call the result a "direct product", which is not the mathematicians' name for it, transcript p. 18.) Since $V \otimes W$ is a vector space, it also contains sums such as $\alpha_1\,v_1 \otimes w_1 + \alpha_2\,v_2 \otimes w_2$: particle 1 doing one thing while particle 2 does another, **plus** particle 1 doing something else while particle 2 does something else. Such a state cannot be described by giving a state for each particle. This is the origin of **entanglement**, the subject of the next lecture (transcript pp. 18–19; notes eq. (1.4)).

## Basis and dimension

If $e_1, \dots, e_n$ is a basis of $V$ and $f_1, \dots, f_m$ a basis of $W$, then expanding $v = \sum_i v_ie_i$ and $w = \sum_j w_jf_j$ with the rules above gives $v \otimes w = \sum_{i,j} v_iw_j\,e_i \otimes f_j$. So the $nm$ products form a basis (transcript p. 20; notes eq. (1.5)):

<Formula label="Dimensions multiply" tex="\dim\left(V\otimes W\right) = \dim V\cdot\dim W" cite="reading3" at="eq. (1.6)" />

A 4-dimensional space and a 10-dimensional one give a 40-dimensional tensor product, not 14: dimensions multiply, they do not add (transcript p. 20). Both factors, and the product, are complex vector spaces (transcript p. 20).

## Operators on the tensor product

If $T$ acts on $V$ and $S$ on $W$, the natural definition lets each act on its own vector (transcript p. 21):

<Formula label="Product operator" tex="\left(T\otimes S\right)\left(v\otimes w\right) = Tv\otimes Sw" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (1.7)–(1.8)" />

An operator of one particle alone is **upgraded** by pairing it with the identity on the other factor: $T \to T \otimes 1$ and $S \to 1 \otimes S$ (transcript pp. 21–22; notes eq. (1.9)). Acting on $v \otimes w$ in either order gives $Tv \otimes Sw$, so (transcript p. 22)

<Formula label="Operators of different particles commute" tex="\left[\,T\otimes 1,\; 1\otimes S\,\right] = 0" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (1.10)–(1.11)" />

This is the basis of most of our intuition about two particles: they "do not know about each other". For two particles that do not interact, the total Hamiltonian is $H_1 \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes H_2$ (transcript p. 22; notes eq. (1.12)).

## Two spins

For two spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ particles, each with basis $|+\rangle$, $|-\rangle$, the tensor product has the four basis states $|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$, $|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle$, $|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle\otimes|-\rangle$: two spins form a 4-dimensional complex vector space (transcript pp. 22–23; notes eq. (1.14)). The first ket always belongs to particle 1 and the second to particle 2. The general state is (transcript p. 23)

<Formula label="General state of two spins" tex="\psi = \alpha_1|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle + \alpha_2|+\rangle\otimes|-\rangle + \alpha_3|-\rangle\otimes|+\rangle + \alpha_4|-\rangle\otimes|-\rangle" cite="reading3" at="eq. (1.15)" />

The total $z$ component of spin is not simply "$S_z$ of one plus $S_z$ of the other": it is a new operator on the tensor product, built from the upgraded ones (transcript p. 23):

<Formula label="Total S_z" tex="S_z^{T} = S_z^{(1)}\otimes 1 + 1\otimes S_z^{(2)}" cite="reading3" at="eq. (1.16)" />

The first term multiplies each component by $\pm\frac{\hbar}{2}$ according to the first spin, the second according to the second spin. Adding them, the mixed terms cancel (transcript p. 24):

<Formula label="Total S_z on the general state" tex="S_z^{T}\,\psi = \hbar\left(\alpha_1|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle - \alpha_4|-\rangle\otimes|-\rangle\right)" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (1.17)–(1.18)" />

So a state with total $S_z = 0$ needs $\alpha_1 = \alpha_4 = 0$. Computing the total $S_x$ and $S_y$ as well was left for recitation (transcript p. 24); the notes do it and find that only the combination $|+\rangle|-\rangle - |-\rangle|+\rangle$ has zero total spin along every axis (notes eqs. (1.20)–(1.25)).

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="A 90° pulse in a hospital scanner, and three spins">
<Problem>
(a) Protons have $\gamma = 2.675 \times 10^{8}$ rad s⁻¹ T⁻¹. In a scanner with $B_0 = 2.0$ T, find $\omega_0$ and the resonance frequency $f_0 = \omega_0/2\pi$. (b) With a rotating field $B_1 = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}$ T, how long is the 90° pulse, and about how many turns about $z$ does the spin make while it spirals down? (c) A gradient of 10 mT per metre is added along $z$. By how much does $f_0$ differ between two slices 0.5 mm apart? (d) What is the dimension of the state space of three spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ particles?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Larmor frequency">
$\omega_0 = \gamma B_0 = 2.675 \times 10^{8} \times 2.0 = 5.350 \times 10^{8}$ rad/s, so $f_0 = 5.350 \times 10^{8}/2\pi \approx 85.1$ MHz, a radio frequency.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Pulse length and turns">
$\omega_1 = \gamma B_1 = 2.675 \times 10^{8} \times 10^{-5} = 2675$ rad/s, so $T = \pi/(2\omega_1) = \pi/5350 \approx 5.87 \times 10^{-4}$ s, about 0.6 ms. In that time the spin turns about $z$ some $f_0T \approx 85.1 \times 10^{6} \times 5.87 \times 10^{-4} \approx 5.0 \times 10^{4}$ times: the spiral has about fifty thousand windings.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Spatial resolution">
The field differs by $0.010 \times 0.0005 = 5 \times 10^{-6}$ T between the slices, so $f_0$ differs by $\gamma\,\Delta B/2\pi = 2.675 \times 10^{8} \times 5 \times 10^{-6}/2\pi \approx 213$ Hz, so a receiver that tells apart signals a couple of hundred hertz apart separates the two slices.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Three spins">
Each spin has a 2-dimensional space, and dimensions multiply: $2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8$. A list of three separate spin states would need only $2 + 2 + 2 = 6$ components; the extra room holds the entangled states.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- In the frame rotating with the radio-frequency field, $H_R = H_U + UH_SU^\dagger$ is time-independent: $H_R = -\gamma\vec B_R\cdot\vec S$ with $\vec B_R = B_1\hat x + B_0(1 - \omega/\omega_0)\hat z$, and $\psi(t) = e^{i\omega tS_z/\hbar}e^{i\gamma\vec B_R\cdot\vec S\,t/\hbar}\psi(0)$.
- At resonance the spin spirals from the $z$ axis to the plane; a 90° pulse lasts $\pi/(2\gamma B_1)$. MRI reads the signal of the rotating proton spins, the relaxation times $T_1$ and $T_2$, and uses field gradients to locate it.
- Two particles live in $V \otimes W$: linear combinations of $v \otimes w$, with numbers factoring out and distributive rules. Its basis is $e_i \otimes f_j$, so dimensions multiply; superpositions of products allow entanglement.
- Operators act as $(T \otimes S)(v \otimes w) = Tv \otimes Sw$; one particle's operators are upgraded to $T \otimes 1$ or $1 \otimes S$, and these commute. The total $S_z$ of two spins is $S_z^{(1)} \otimes 1 + 1 \otimes S_z^{(2)}$.
