The notation is MIT's. The lessons write the spin states as columns $\chi_{z\pm}$ and $\chi_{n\pm}$; here they are kets, $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ for spin up and down along $z$, and $|\vec n; \pm\rangle$ for spin up and down along the unit vector $\vec n$. The inner product that the lessons write $(a, b)$ is written $\langle a|b\rangle$. The direction $\vec n$ has the polar angle $\theta$ (measured from the $z$ axis) and the azimuthal angle $\phi$ (measured from the $x$ axis in the $xy$-plane), and its spin states are

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Spin one-half states along an arbitrary axis" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

<Part label="a" points={4}>

A spin state, not normalized, is

<Formula label="The state of Problem 1(a)" tex="|\psi\rangle = (1 + i)\,|+\rangle - \left(1 + i\sqrt3\right)|-\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

In which direction does this spin point? Give the polar angle $\theta$ and the azimuthal angle $\phi$ of the direction $\vec n$ for which $|\psi\rangle$ is the state $|\vec n; +\rangle$ up to a constant factor. Give both in degrees, with $\phi$ between $0°$ and $360°$.

<Hint>

A constant factor in front of a state does not change the direction of the spin. Divide the state by the coefficient of $|+\rangle$ and compare what is left with $|\vec n; +\rangle$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The ratio of the two coefficients must be $\tan\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{i\phi}$. Write each complex number in polar form: its modulus gives $\theta$ and its phase gives $\phi$. Do not forget the minus sign.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-theta" label="$\theta$ in degrees:" value="109.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-theta" />

<Answer id="p1-phi" label="$\phi$ in degrees:" value="195" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-phi" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Every spin state is the "up" state along some direction. A common factor of the two coefficients does not matter, so the direction sits in their ratio alone.

<Step title="The ratio of the coefficients">

In $|\vec n; +\rangle$ the coefficient of $|-\rangle$ divided by the coefficient of $|+\rangle$ is $\tan\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{i\phi}$. For the given state, with $1 + i = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}$, $1 + i\sqrt3 = 2\,e^{i\pi/3}$ and $-1 = e^{i\pi}$,

<Formula label="The ratio in polar form" tex="\frac{-(1 + i\sqrt3)}{1 + i} = \frac{2}{\sqrt2}\,e^{i(\pi + \pi/3 - \pi/4)} = \sqrt2\,e^{13\pi i/12}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two angles">

The modulus gives the polar angle and the phase gives the azimuthal angle:

<Formula label="The direction of the spin" tex="\tan\frac{\theta}{2} = \sqrt2 \ \Rightarrow\ \theta = 2\arctan\sqrt2 \approx 109.5°, \qquad \phi = \frac{13\pi}{12} = 195°" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Since $\cos\theta = \frac{1 - \tan^2(\theta/2)}{1 + \tan^2(\theta/2)} = -\frac13$, the direction is $\vec n \approx (-0.911, -0.244, -0.333)$: below the $xy$-plane, close to the negative $x$ axis.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The direction is also the expectation value of the Pauli matrices. With $c_1 = 1 + i$ and $c_2 = -(1 + i\sqrt3)$ the norm squared is $|c_1|^2 + |c_2|^2 = 2 + 4 = 6$, and

<Formula label="The direction from expectation values" tex="n_z = \frac{|c_1|^2 - |c_2|^2}{6} = -\frac13, \qquad n_x + i\,n_y = \frac{2\,c_1^*c_2}{6} = -\frac{1 + \sqrt3}{3} - i\,\frac{\sqrt3 - 1}{3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

That is $(-0.911, -0.244, -0.333)$ again, and its length is 1.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Three Stern-Gerlach magnets stand in a row.

1. The first is oriented along $z$ and keeps only the atoms with $\hat S_z = +\hbar/2$. This prepares a beam of spin one-half atoms in the state $|+\rangle$.
2. The second measures $\hat S_n$ for a direction $\vec n$ in the $xz$-plane: $\phi = 0$, and $\theta$ is not zero. Only the atoms with eigenvalue $+\hbar/2$ are kept.
3. The third is oriented along $z$, like the first, and acts on the atoms that remain.

Of all the atoms that enter the second magnet, which fraction does the third magnet find in $|+\rangle$? Which fraction does it find in $|-\rangle$? Which fraction never reaches the third magnet? Give the three fractions as functions of `theta`. Then argue that they make sense for $\theta = 0$ ($\vec n$ along $z$), $\theta = \pi/2$ ($\vec n$ along $x$) and $\theta = \pi$ ($\vec n$ along $-z$).

<Hint>

Each magnet is a measurement. The chance of an outcome is the squared modulus of the overlap of the state that enters with the state that is kept, and after the magnet the atom is in the state that was kept.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $\phi = 0$ the state kept by the second magnet is $\cos\frac{\theta}{2}|+\rangle + \sin\frac{\theta}{2}|-\rangle$. Use it twice: once for the atoms that enter the second magnet, once for the atoms that leave it. Multiply the chances of the steps of one path.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-minus" label="Found in $|-\rangle$:" expr="cos(theta/2)^2*sin(theta/2)^2"

<Answer id="p1-lost" label="Never reaches the third magnet:" expr="sin(theta/2)^2"
  vars={{ theta: [0.3, 2.8] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "cos(theta/2)^2", hint: "That is the fraction that passes the second magnet. The question is about the rest." },

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow one atom. At each magnet the chance of an outcome is the squared overlap of its state with the state for that outcome, and the atom leaves in that state. The fraction for a path is the product of the chances along it.

<Step title="The second magnet">

For $\phi = 0$ the state with $\hat S_n = +\hbar/2$ is $|\vec n; +\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}|+\rangle + \sin\frac{\theta}{2}|-\rangle$. The atoms arrive in $|+\rangle$, so

<Formula label="Passing the second magnet" tex="P_\text{pass} = \left|\langle\vec n; +|+\rangle\right|^2 = \cos^2\frac{\theta}{2}, \qquad P_\text{lost} = 1 - \cos^2\frac{\theta}{2} = \sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The atoms that pass are now in the state $|\vec n; +\rangle$. They have forgotten that they were up along $z$.

</Step>

<Step title="The third magnet">

An atom in $|\vec n; +\rangle$ is found up along $z$ with chance $|\langle +|\vec n; +\rangle|^2 = \cos^2\frac{\theta}{2}$ and down with chance $|\langle -|\vec n; +\rangle|^2 = \sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}$. As fractions of the atoms that entered the second magnet:

<Formula label="The three fractions" tex="P_+ = \cos^4\frac{\theta}{2}, \qquad P_- = \cos^2\frac{\theta}{2}\,\sin^2\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac14\sin^2\theta, \qquad P_\text{lost} = \sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

They add up to 1: $\cos^2\frac{\theta}{2}\left(\cos^2\frac{\theta}{2} + \sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}\right) + \sin^2\frac{\theta}{2} = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the three special angles">

For $\theta = 0$ the second magnet repeats the first. Every atom passes and is still in $|+\rangle$: the fractions are $1$, $0$ and $0$.

For $\theta = \pi/2$ the second magnet measures $\hat S_x$. A state up along $z$ is up or down along $x$ with equal chances, so half the atoms are lost. Those that pass are up along $x$, which is up or down along $z$ with equal chances: the fractions are $\frac14$, $\frac14$ and $\frac12$. A quarter of the atoms now come out down along $z$, although every atom started up: the measurement in between has changed the state.

For $\theta = \pi$ the second magnet keeps the atoms that are up along $-z$, which means down along $z$. The beam has none: the fractions are $0$, $0$ and $1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Overlap of two spin one-half states" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

Take the spin state $|\vec n; +\rangle$, where the unit vector $\vec n$ has the polar and azimuthal angles $\theta$ and $\phi$, and the spin state $|\vec n'; +\rangle$, where the unit vector $\vec n'$ has the angles $\theta'$ and $\phi'$. Call $\gamma$ the angle between the two unit vectors: $\vec n\cdot\vec n' = \cos\gamma$. Show by direct computation that the overlap of the two spin states is set by half of that angle:

<Formula label="Overlap of two spin states" tex="\left|\langle\vec n'; +|\vec n; +\rangle\right|^2 = \cos^2\frac{\gamma}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

Two boxes, both our additions, go with the proof. The first asks for the step in the middle: the left-hand side written with the angles. Type it with `t` for $\theta$, `tp` for $\theta'$ and `d` for the difference $\phi - \phi'$. The second is an example with numbers: $\vec n$ has $\theta = 60°$ and $\phi = 0°$, and $\vec n'$ has $\theta' = 120°$ and $\phi' = 90°$. Then open the solution and mark yourself.

<Hint>

Write both states with the formula for $|\vec n; +\rangle$. The bra of a state has the complex conjugates of its coefficients. The overlap is then a sum of two terms, one from $|+\rangle$ and one from $|-\rangle$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The squared modulus of a sum of two complex numbers has a cross term, and here it carries $\cos(\phi - \phi')$. To compare with $\vec n\cdot\vec n'$, turn the half angles into whole ones: $\cos^2\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac12(1 + \cos\theta)$, $\sin^2\frac{\theta}{2} = \frac12(1 - \cos\theta)$ and $2\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\frac{\theta}{2} = \sin\theta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-angles" label="$\left|\langle\vec n'; +|\vec n; +\rangle\right|^2 =$" expr="(1 + cos(t)*cos(tp) + sin(t)*sin(tp)*cos(d))/2"
  vars={{ t: [0.3, 2.8], tp: [0.3, 2.8], d: [0.3, 2.8] }}
  names={{ t: "\\theta", tp: "\\theta'", d: "(\\phi - \\phi')" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "cos((t - tp)/2)^2", hint: "That is the result when the two directions have the same azimuthal angle. In general the cross term carries cos(φ − φ′)." },
    { expr: "cos(t/2)^2*cos(tp/2)^2 + sin(t/2)^2*sin(tp/2)^2", hint: "This adds the squares of the two terms of the overlap. The overlap is a sum of two complex numbers: the square of its modulus has a cross term too." },
    { expr: "cos(t)*cos(tp) + sin(t)*sin(tp)*cos(d)", hint: "That is cos γ itself, the dot product of the two unit vectors. It can be negative, and a squared modulus cannot." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-example" label="Example: $\left|\langle\vec n'; +|\vec n; +\rangle\right|^2 =$" value="0.375" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-example" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compute the overlap from the two kets, square its modulus, and turn every half angle into a whole angle. What appears is the dot product of the two unit vectors.

<Step title="The overlap">

The bra of $|\vec n'; +\rangle$ is $\cos\frac{\theta'}{2}\langle +| + \sin\frac{\theta'}{2}\,e^{-i\phi'}\langle -|$. Since $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ are orthonormal,

<Formula label="The overlap of the two states" tex="\langle\vec n'; +|\vec n; +\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta'}{2}\cos\frac{\theta}{2} + \sin\frac{\theta'}{2}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{i(\phi - \phi')}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Its squared modulus">

The squared modulus of a sum of two complex numbers is the sum of their squared moduli plus twice the real part of the first times the complex conjugate of the second. Here the two moduli are the real products of cosines and of sines, and the phase between the terms is $\phi - \phi'$. So

<Formula label="The squared modulus with half angles" tex="\left|\langle\vec n'; +|\vec n; +\rangle\right|^2 = \cos^2\frac{\theta'}{2}\cos^2\frac{\theta}{2} + \sin^2\frac{\theta'}{2}\sin^2\frac{\theta}{2} + 2\cos\frac{\theta'}{2}\sin\frac{\theta'}{2}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\cos(\phi - \phi')" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Whole angles">

The first two terms are $\frac14(1 + \cos\theta')(1 + \cos\theta) + \frac14(1 - \cos\theta')(1 - \cos\theta) = \frac12(1 + \cos\theta\cos\theta')$. The last term is $\frac12\sin\theta\sin\theta'\cos(\phi - \phi')$. Together:

<Formula label="The squared modulus with whole angles" tex="\left|\langle\vec n'; +|\vec n; +\rangle\right|^2 = \frac12\left[1 + \cos\theta\cos\theta' + \sin\theta\sin\theta'\cos(\phi - \phi')\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The dot product">

From the components of the two unit vectors,

<Formula label="The angle between the two directions" tex="\cos\gamma = \vec n\cdot\vec n' = \sin\theta\sin\theta'\left(\cos\phi\cos\phi' + \sin\phi\sin\phi'\right) + \cos\theta\cos\theta' = \sin\theta\sin\theta'\cos(\phi - \phi') + \cos\theta\cos\theta'" cite="reading1" at="eq. (4.67), used for both directions" />

This is the bracket of the step before, without the 1. Therefore

<Formula label="The result of Problem 2" tex="\left|\langle\vec n'; +|\vec n; +\rangle\right|^2 = \frac12\left(1 + \cos\gamma\right) = \cos^2\frac{\gamma}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result, and the example">

For $\vec n' = \vec n$ the angle is $\gamma = 0$ and the overlap is 1. For $\vec n' = -\vec n$ it is $\gamma = \pi$ and the overlap is 0: spin states along opposite directions are orthogonal, although the two arrows are not at right angles. Problem 1(b) is a third case: there the two directions were $z$ and $\vec n$, with $\gamma = \theta$, and the chance was $\cos^2\frac{\theta}{2}$.

In the example, $\cos\gamma = \sin 60°\sin 120°\cos(-90°) + \cos 60°\cos 120° = 0 - 0.25$, so $\gamma \approx 104.5°$ and the overlap squared is $\frac12(1 - 0.25) = 0.375$. Directly: the overlap is $\cos 60°\cos 30° + \sin 60°\sin 30°\,e^{-i\pi/2} = 0.433\,(1 - i)$, with squared modulus $0.375$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Rotation of spin states" points={10} mit="Problem 3">

For a real number $\alpha$ and a unit vector $\vec n$, define the operator

<Formula label="The rotation operator" tex="\hat R_{\vec n}(\alpha) \equiv \exp\left(-\frac{i\alpha\,\hat S_n}{\hbar}\right) = \exp\left(-i\,\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma\right), \qquad \hat S_n = \frac{\hbar}{2}\,\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Use the definition of the exponential function as a power series, and the properties of the Pauli matrices, to show that

<Formula label="The rotation operator in closed form" tex="\hat R_{\vec n}(\alpha) = I\cos\frac{\alpha}{2} - i\,\vec\sigma\cdot\vec n\,\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2" />

Then verify by direct computation that $\hat R_{\vec n}(\alpha)$ is unitary.

The choice box, our addition, checks the key step. Then open the solution and mark yourself.

<Hint>

The exponential of a matrix $M$ is $I + M + \frac{1}{2!}M^2 + \frac{1}{3!}M^3 + \dots$ Work out the first few powers of $\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Sort the series into even and odd powers and compare each part with the series of the cosine and of the sine. For the second question: unitary means $\hat R^\dagger\hat R = I$. The Pauli matrices are Hermitian, and $\alpha$ and $\vec n$ are real.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-series" label="The series can be summed because" choices={["n·σ has trace zero, so all of its even powers vanish", "n·σ squares to the identity matrix", "n·σ is Hermitian, so each power equals n·σ itself", "n·σ commutes with each of the three Pauli matrices"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The matrix $\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma$ squares to the identity. Its powers are therefore $I$ and $\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma$ in turn, and the exponential series splits into a cosine series and a sine series.

<Step title="Powers of the matrix">

For a unit vector, the product identity of the Pauli matrices gives

<Formula label="The square of the spin matrix along n" tex="(\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma)^2 = (\vec n\cdot\vec n)\,I + i\,(\vec n\times\vec n)\cdot\vec\sigma = I" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (3.60) and (3.62)" />

So $(\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma)^{2k} = I$ and $(\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma)^{2k+1} = \vec n\cdot\vec\sigma$ for every whole number $k \ge 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Summing the series">

Split the series into even and odd powers, and use $(-i)^{2k} = (-1)^k$ and $(-i)^{2k+1} = -i\,(-1)^k$:

<Formula label="The exponential series, even and odd powers" tex="\exp\left(-i\,\frac{\alpha}{2}\,\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma\right) = I\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k)!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{2k} - i\,\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^k}{(2k+1)!}\left(\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)^{2k+1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The first sum is the series of $\cos\frac{\alpha}{2}$ and the second that of $\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}$. This is the closed form asked for.

</Step>

<Step title="Unitary">

The Pauli matrices are Hermitian and the components of $\vec n$ are real, so $(\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma)^\dagger = \vec n\cdot\vec\sigma$. Taking the adjoint only turns $-i$ into $+i$:

<Formula label="The adjoint times the operator" tex="\hat R_{\vec n}^\dagger\,\hat R_{\vec n} = \left(I\cos\frac{\alpha}{2} + i\,\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)\left(I\cos\frac{\alpha}{2} - i\,\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma\sin\frac{\alpha}{2}\right) = I\cos^2\frac{\alpha}{2} + (\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma)^2\sin^2\frac{\alpha}{2} = I" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The two cross terms cancel. The product in the other order is the same computation, so $\hat R_{\vec n}\hat R_{\vec n}^\dagger = I$ as well.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The adjoint is the same operator with $-\alpha$: $\hat R_{\vec n}(\alpha)^\dagger = \hat R_{\vec n}(-\alpha)$, the rotation that undoes the first one. For $\alpha = 0$ the operator is $I$. For $\alpha = 2\pi$ it is $-I$, not $I$: a full turn changes the sign of every spin one-half state, which no measurement on one spin alone can see, since an overall factor drops out of every probability.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Write $\hat R_y(\alpha)$ for the operator with $\vec n$ along the $y$ axis. Evaluate

<Formula label="The operator to evaluate" tex="\hat R_y(\alpha)\,\hat S_z\,\hat R_y(\alpha)^\dagger" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 2" />

in terms of $\hat S_x$, $\hat S_y$ and $\hat S_z$. Type the result with `Sx`, `Sy`, `Sz` and `alpha`.

<Hint>

Use the closed form of part (a) with $\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma = \sigma_2$, and $\hat S_z = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_3$. Multiply out the three factors: four terms.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You need the products $\sigma_2\sigma_3 = i\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_3\sigma_2 = -i\sigma_1$, and $\sigma_2\sigma_3\sigma_2 = -\sigma_3$. At the end the half angles combine into whole ones.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-rot" label="$\hat R_y(\alpha)\,\hat S_z\,\hat R_y(\alpha)^\dagger =$" expr="Sz*cos(alpha) + Sx*sin(alpha)"
  vars={{ Sx: [0.5, 2], Sy: [0.5, 2], Sz: [0.5, 2], alpha: [0.3, 2.8] }}
  names={{ Sx: "\\hat S_x", Sy: "\\hat S_y", Sz: "\\hat S_z", alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Sz*cos(alpha) - Sx*sin(alpha)", hint: "That is the product in the other order, with the adjoint on the left. Check the signs of the two cross terms: they add." },
    { expr: "Sz*cos(alpha/2) + Sx*sin(alpha/2)", hint: "The half angle belongs to each factor R. In the product of the two factors the half angles combine: cos² − sin² and 2 sin cos of α/2." },
    { expr: "Sz*cos(alpha) + Sy*sin(alpha)", hint: "A rotation about the y axis tilts the z axis in the xz-plane. It cannot bring in the y component." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Insert the closed form of part (a) on both sides of $\sigma_3$ and multiply out with the product rule of the Pauli matrices.

<Step title="Multiplying out">

Write $c = \cos\frac{\alpha}{2}$ and $s = \sin\frac{\alpha}{2}$. Then

**The idea.** A length squared is never negative. Choosing $\lambda$ so that $\vec a - \lambda\vec b$ is as short as possible gives the sharpest statement, and that statement is the Schwarz inequality.

<Step title="The lowest point">

If $\vec b = 0$ both sides of the inequality are zero and there is nothing to prove. Take $\vec b \ne 0$. The dot product is symmetric and linear, so

<Formula label="The quadratic function" tex="f(\lambda) = \vec a\cdot\vec a - 2\lambda\,\vec a\cdot\vec b + \lambda^2\,\vec b\cdot\vec b, \qquad f'(\lambda) = -2\,\vec a\cdot\vec b + 2\lambda\,\vec b\cdot\vec b = 0 \ \Rightarrow\ \lambda_\text{best} = \frac{\vec a\cdot\vec b}{\vec b\cdot\vec b}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="The inequality">

At the lowest point

<Formula label="The smallest value" tex="f(\lambda_\text{best}) = \vec a\cdot\vec a - \frac{(\vec a\cdot\vec b)^2}{\vec b\cdot\vec b} \ge 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Multiply by the positive number $\vec b\cdot\vec b$: $(\vec a\cdot\vec b)^2 \le (\vec a\cdot\vec a)(\vec b\cdot\vec b)$. Taking the square root gives the second form.

<Step title="When it is saturated">

So for $\vec b \ne 0$ equality holds exactly when $\vec a$ is a multiple of $\vec b$: the two vectors are parallel or antiparallel, or $\vec a = 0$. With the trivial case $\vec b = 0$: equality holds exactly when one of the vectors is a multiple of the other.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The vector $\vec a - \lambda_\text{best}\vec b$ is $\vec a$ with its part along $\vec b$ taken away: its dot product with $\vec b$ is $\vec a\cdot\vec b - \lambda_\text{best}\,\vec b\cdot\vec b = 0$. and this is the zero sequence only if $a_1 = a_2 = \dots = a_n = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The contradiction">

Suppose $\mathbb{F}^\infty$ were finite-dimensional. Then some list of vectors spans it; call its length $m$. By the result quoted from the notes (chapter 3, p. 4, below eq. (1.8)), every linearly independent list then has length at most $m$. But the list of the first $m + 1$ of the sequences $e_k$ is linearly independent and has length $m + 1$. This is a contradiction, so no list spans $\mathbb{F}^\infty$: it is infinite-dimensional.

That it is a vector space at all is the statement of the notes: sums and multiples of sequences are sequences, and the rules of a vector space hold entry by entry because they hold for numbers.

</Step>

<Step title="Why the shortcut fails">

A list has a finite length by definition, and a linear combination is a finite sum. A finite sum of the $e_k$ is a sequence that ends in zeros. The sequence $(1, 1, 1, \dots)$ is not of that kind, so the $e_k$ together do not span the space, and they are not a basis of it in the sense of the notes. The proof above never needs a basis: it only needs independent lists that are longer than any given number.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

A linear operator $T$ on a vector space $V$ is injective if $Tu = Tv$ implies $u = v$. Show that $T$ is injective if and only if $\text{null}\,T = \{0\}$.

The choice box, our addition, applies the result to the left shift $L(x_1, x_2, x_3, \dots) = (x_2, x_3, \dots)$ on $\mathbb{F}^\infty$. Then open the solution and mark yourself.

<Hint>

There are two directions to prove. For the first you need to know what $T$ does to the zero vector.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the second direction: by linearity, $Tu = Tv$ is the same statement as $T(u - v) = 0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-shift" label="Is the left shift $L$ injective?" choices={["No: it sends (1, 0, 0, …) to zero, so its null space is not {0}", "Yes: its null space holds the zero sequence only", "No: no sequence at all is sent to (1, 0, 0, …)", "Yes: every sequence is L of some sequence, which is all that injective asks"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Linearity turns a statement about two vectors with the same image into a statement about one vector with image zero.

<Step title="Injective implies a trivial null space">

A linear operator sends the zero vector to itself: $T(0) = T(0 + 0) = T(0) + T(0)$, and taking away $T(0)$ on both sides leaves $T(0) = 0$ (notes, chapter 3, p. 7). Now let $T$ be injective and let $v$ be in $\text{null}\,T$. Then $Tv = 0 = T(0)$, and injectivity gives $v = 0$. So $\text{null}\,T = \{0\}$.

</Step>

<Step title="A trivial null space implies injective">

Let $\text{null}\,T = \{0\}$ and suppose $Tu = Tv$. By linearity

<Formula label="The difference of two vectors with the same image" tex="T(u - v) = Tu - Tv = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

so $u - v$ is in $\text{null}\,T$, and therefore $u - v = 0$: $u = v$. So $T$ is injective.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with an example">

The left shift sends $(1, 0, 0, \dots)$ to $(0, 0, \dots)$. Its null space is not $\{0\}$, so it is not injective: indeed $(1, 0, 0, \dots)$ and the zero sequence have the same image. The right shift $(x_1, x_2, \dots) \to (0, x_1, x_2, \dots)$ gives zero only for the zero sequence, so it is injective (notes, chapter 3, p. 8).

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Basis independent quantities" points={10} mit="Problem 6">

A vector space $V$ has two bases, $(v_1, \dots, v_n)$ and $(u_1, \dots, u_n)$. The linear operator $A$ takes the first to the second: $A v_k = u_k$ for $k = 1, \dots, n$. It is invertible. With $B$ the operator that has $B u_k = v_k$, the product $BA$ takes each $v_k$ to itself and $AB$ takes each $u_k$ to itself, so $BA = I$ and $AB = I$: $B$ is the inverse of $A$.

Sums over indices are written out on this page. (The notes leave the sum signs out and sum over every index that appears twice.) The matrix of an operator $T$ in the basis $(v_1, \dots, v_n)$ has the entries $T_{ij}(\{v\})$ defined by $T v_j = \sum_i T_{ij}(\{v\})\,v_i$, and in the same way for the basis of the $u$'s.

The examples with numbers are our additions. They use a real space with $n = 2$ and

<Formula label="The example of Problem 6" tex="u_1 = v_1 + 2v_2, \qquad u_2 = v_1 + 3v_2, \qquad T(\{v\}) = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4 (our own example)" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Take the two equations

<Formula label="The two mapping equations" tex="u_k = A\,v_k \qquad\text{and}\qquad v_k = B\,u_k" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 4" />

and write them out with the matrix of $A$ in the basis of the $v$'s and the matrix of $B$ in the basis of the $u$'s. Show that these two matrices are inverses of each other.

In the example, find the entry $B_{21}$ of the matrix of $B$ in the basis of the $u$'s.

<Hint>

By the definition of a matrix, $A v_k = \sum_j A_{jk}\,v_j$: the $k$-th column of the matrix holds the components of the image of the $k$-th basis vector. Write the same for $B$ with the $u$'s.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put the second equation into the first. You get each $u_k$ as a combination of the $u$'s. A basis is linearly independent, so that combination must be $u_k$ itself.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-b21" label="Example: $B_{21} =$" value="-2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p6-b21" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each equation expresses one basis in the other. Substituting one into the other gives a basis in terms of itself, and that pins down the product of the two matrices.

<Step title="The equations with matrices">

<Formula label="The mapping equations in components" tex="u_k = \sum_j A_{jk}\,v_j, \qquad v_k = \sum_j B_{jk}\,u_j" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.38)" />

Here $A_{jk}$ is the matrix of $A$ in the basis of the $v$'s, and $B_{jk}$ the matrix of $B$ in the basis of the $u$'s.

</Step>

<Step title="Substituting">

Put the second equation, written for $v_j$, into the first:

<Formula label="One basis in terms of itself" tex="u_k = \sum_j A_{jk}\sum_i B_{ij}\,u_i = \sum_i\Big(\sum_j B_{ij}A_{jk}\Big)u_i" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.39)" />

The $u$'s are linearly independent, so the only way to write $u_k$ as a combination of them is with the coefficient 1 for $u_k$ and 0 for the others. In the same way, putting the first equation into the second gives $v_k = \sum_p\left(\sum_j A_{pj}B_{jk}\right)v_p$. Therefore

<Formula label="The two matrices are inverses" tex="\sum_j B_{ij}A_{jk} = \delta_{ik}, \qquad \sum_j A_{pj}B_{jk} = \delta_{pk}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.40)" />

In matrix language: the matrix $B$ times the matrix $A$ is the identity matrix, in both orders. So $v_k = \sum_j (A^{-1})_{jk}\,u_j$.

</Step>

<Step title="The example">

The columns of the matrix of $A$ are the components of $u_1$ and $u_2$ in the basis of the $v$'s:

<Formula label="The matrices of the example" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 2 & 3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}3 & -1\\ -2 & 1\end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 4 (our own example)" />

The determinant of $A$ is $3 - 2 = 1$. The first column of $B$ says $v_1 = 3u_1 - 2u_2$, so $B_{21} = -2$. Check: $3(v_1 + 2v_2) - 2(v_1 + 3v_2) = v_1$. The second column says $v_2 = -u_1 + u_2$, which also checks.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Now let $T$ be any linear operator on $V$. Find the relation between its matrix $T(\{u\})$ in the basis of the $u$'s and its matrix $T(\{v\})$ in the basis of the $v$'s, written with the matrix of $A$ and its inverse.

In the example, find the entries $T_{11}(\{u\})$ and $T_{21}(\{u\})$.

<Hint>

Start from the definition $T u_k = \sum_i T_{ik}(\{u\})\,u_i$. On the left, write $u_k$ in terms of the $v$'s, let $T$ act, and then go back from the $v$'s to the $u$'s.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Three matrices appear in a row. Keep track of which index is summed with which: that fixes the order of the matrix product.

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compute $T u_k$ in two ways: from the definition of the matrix in the basis of the $u$'s, and by passing through the basis of the $v$'s, where the matrix of $T$ is known.

<Step title="Through the other basis">

With $u_k = \sum_j A_{jk}v_j$, then $T v_j = \sum_p T_{pj}(\{v\})\,v_p$, then $v_p = \sum_i (A^{-1})_{ip}\,u_i$:

<Formula label="The operator acting on the new basis" tex="T u_k = \sum_j A_{jk}\,T v_j = \sum_{j,p} A_{jk}\,T_{pj}(\{v\})\,v_p = \sum_i\Big(\sum_{p,j}(A^{-1})_{ip}\,T_{pj}(\{v\})\,A_{jk}\Big)u_i" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.44) and (2.45)" />

<Step title="Reading off the matrix">

Compare with $T u_k = \sum_i T_{ik}(\{u\})\,u_i$. The $u$'s are a basis, so the coefficients agree:

<Formula label="Change of basis for the matrix of an operator" tex="T_{ik}(\{u\}) = \sum_{p,j}(A^{-1})_{ip}\,T_{pj}(\{v\})\,A_{jk}, \qquad T(\{u\}) = A^{-1}\,T(\{v\})\,A" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.46)" />

<Step title="The example">

<Formula label="The matrix of the example in the new basis" tex="T(\{u\}) = \begin{pmatrix}3 & -1\\ -2 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\ 0 & 3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 2 & 3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}3 & -1\\ -2 & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}4 & 5\\ 6 & 9\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}6 & 6\\ -2 & -1\end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 4 (our own example)" />

So $T_{11}(\{u\}) = 6$ and $T_{21}(\{u\}) = -2$. Check without matrices: $T u_1 = T v_1 + 2\,T v_2 = 2v_1 + 2(v_1 + 3v_2) = 4v_1 + 6v_2$, and $6u_1 - 2u_2 = 6(v_1 + 2v_2) - 2(v_1 + 3v_2) = 4v_1 + 6v_2$ as well.

</Step>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Show that the determinant of the matrix of $T$ does not depend on the basis.

In the example, suppose you know only $T_{11}(\{u\}) = 6$, $T_{21}(\{u\}) = -2$ and $T_{22}(\{u\}) = -1$. Find $T_{12}(\{u\})$ from the determinant.

<Hint>

The determinant of a product of matrices is the product of their determinants. What does that say about the determinants of $A$ and of $A^{-1}$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-t12" label="Example: $T_{12}(\{u\}) =$" value="6" tolerance="0.01" calc="p6-t12" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A determinant is a number, and numbers commute: the determinants of $A^{-1}$ and of $A$ meet and cancel.

<Step title="The determinant in the new basis">

From $\det(XY) = \det X\,\det Y$ and $A^{-1}A = I$ it follows that $\det(A^{-1})\det A = 1$. Then

<Formula label="The determinant does not depend on the basis" tex="\det T(\{u\}) = \det(A^{-1})\,\det T(\{v\})\,\det A = \det T(\{v\})" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.48)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The example">

The determinant of $T(\{v\})$ is $2\times3 - 1\times0 = 6$. In the new basis it is $6\times(-1) - T_{12}(\{u\})\times(-2) = -6 + 2\,T_{12}(\{u\})$. Setting this equal to 6 gives $T_{12}(\{u\}) = 6$, as in part (b).

Because they do not depend on the basis, the trace and the determinant belong to the operator itself. In a basis of eigenvectors, when there is one, the matrix is diagonal: the trace is then the sum of the eigenvalues and the determinant their product. For this $T$ the eigenvalues are 2 and 3, with sum 5 and product 6.

</Step>
