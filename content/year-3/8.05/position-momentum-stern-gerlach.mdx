This lecture completes the review of wave mechanics with position and momentum, then begins the study of **spin**, starting from the experiment that revealed it (transcript pp. 1–24; notes chapter 1, section 6, and chapter 2, section 1). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's recorded lecture and his notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. The momentum representation was introduced in 8.04, on the page on [momentum space and expectation values](/courses/8-04/momentum-space-expectation-values).)

## Position

Position and momentum are operators that do not commute (transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Canonical commutator" tex="[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar" cite="reading2" at="eq. (6.27)" />

Time plays no role here, so take a wavefunction $\psi(x)$ at one moment. In the **position representation**, $\hat x$ acts by multiplication, $\hat x\psi(x) = x\psi(x)$ (transcript p. 2).

**Wavefunctions as vectors** (transcript pp. 2–4). On $0 \le x \le a$, split the interval into $N$ steps of size $\epsilon = a/N$ and list the values $\psi(0), \psi(\epsilon), \dots, \psi(N\epsilon)$: a column vector with $N + 1$ entries, approximating the function as finely as one likes. Then $\hat x$ is the $(N + 1) \times (N + 1)$ diagonal matrix with entries $0, \epsilon, 2\epsilon, \dots, N\epsilon$, and multiplying gives the vector of values of $x\psi(x)$, as it should. The expectation value is $\langle\hat x\rangle = \int\psi^*(x)\,x\,\psi(x)\,dx$.

**Eigenstates of position** (transcript pp. 4–5) are delta functions:

<Formula label="Position eigenstates" tex="\psi_{x_0}(x) = \delta(x - x_0), \qquad \hat x\,\psi_{x_0}(x) = x\,\delta(x - x_0) = x_0\,\psi_{x_0}(x)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (6.33), (6.34)" />

with eigenvalue $x_0$. They cannot be normalized, so they do not represent a particle, but they are useful.

## Momentum

In the position representation (transcript p. 5),

<Formula label="Momentum operator" tex="\hat p\,\psi(x) = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d\psi}{dx}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (6.36)" />

the form suggested by de Broglie's link between wavelength and momentum. It must reproduce the commutator. Acting on any $\psi$, $\hat x\hat p\psi = \frac{\hbar}{i}x\psi'$ while $\hat p\hat x\psi = \frac{\hbar}{i}(\psi + x\psi')$, so $[\hat x, \hat p]\psi = i\hbar\psi$; an operator identity holds if it holds on every function (transcript pp. 6–7). (A finite-difference matrix for $\hat p$ can be built too, and checking its commutator with $\hat x$ turns up surprises the course returns to later.)

**Eigenstates of momentum** (transcript p. 7) are plane waves, with a convenient normalization:

<Formula label="Momentum eigenstates" tex="\psi_p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}e^{ipx/\hbar}, \qquad \hat p\,\psi_p(x) = p\,\psi_p(x)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (6.38), (6.39)" />

**The momentum representation** (transcript pp. 8–11). Fourier's theorem writes any wavefunction as a superposition of momentum eigenstates, and inverts:

<Formula label="Fourier transform" tex="\psi(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dp}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}e^{ipx/\hbar}\tilde\psi(p), \qquad \tilde\psi(p) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}e^{-ipx/\hbar}\psi(x)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (6.41), (6.40)" />

$\psi(x)$ and $\tilde\psi(p)$ carry the same information in two ways: two huge vectors, one indexed by position and one by momentum, related by an infinite "matrix" of phases $e^{-ipx/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$. Differentiating under the integral shows that $\frac{\hbar}{i}\psi'(x)$ corresponds to $p\tilde\psi(p)$: in momentum space $\hat p$ simply multiplies, while $\hat x$ becomes a derivative (an exercise in the lecture and notes),

<Formula label="Operators in the momentum representation" tex="\hat p\,\tilde\psi(p) = p\,\tilde\psi(p), \qquad \hat x = i\hbar\frac{d}{dp}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (6.45), (6.46)" />

The operator is the same; only the way the data are given changes.

## Magnetic moments and spin

**A short history** (transcript pp. 12–13). Stern and Gerlach's experiment was done in Frankfurt in 1922, and for years nobody understood it. Pauli suspected the electron had a two-valued degree of freedom. Kronig proposed that the electron rotates, but Pauli objected that a spinning ball of charge would need its surface to move faster than light, and Kronig did not publish. In 1925 Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit had the same idea; their adviser Ehrenfest thought it doubtful but told them to publish, and they get the credit for the electron's spin. By 1927 the experiment was understood. Stern and Gerlach themselves had set out to detect the orbital angular momentum of Bohr's electron orbits, but silver's outer electron has none: they saw spin. Their name for the effect, "space quantization", reflects the confusion.

**Magnetic moments** (transcript pp. 13–14). Spin is never seen directly, only through a **magnetic moment** $\vec\mu$, the magnetic analogue of an electric dipole: for a current loop, $\vec\mu = I\vec A$, current times oriented area. Since $\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$ is an energy, $\mu$ has units of joules per tesla.

**A rotating ring** (transcript pp. 14–15). A ring of radius $R$, charge $Q$ and mass $M$, uniformly spread and turning with speed $v$, carries current $I = Qv/2\pi R$, so $\mu = I\pi R^2 = \frac12QvR$; its angular momentum is $L = MvR$. Hence

<Formula label="Classical gyromagnetic ratio" tex="\vec\mu = \frac{Q}{2M}\vec L" cite="reading3" at="eq. (1.7)" />

The radius and speed have dropped out, so the same ratio holds for any axially symmetric rotating body built from such rings, provided its charge and mass are distributed alike: a universal relation.

**The electron** (transcript pp. 15–18). For a particle with spin $\vec S$, write the moment with $\hbar$ pulled out and a dimensionless **g-factor** in front:

<Formula label="Electron magnetic moment" tex="\vec\mu = -g\,\mu_B\frac{\vec S}{\hbar}, \qquad g = 2, \qquad \mu_B = \frac{e\hbar}{2m_e} \approx 9.27 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{J/T}" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (1.10)–(1.13)" />

The minus sign is because the electron is negative; $\mu_B$ is the **Bohr magneton**. The value $g = 2$ (measured as about 2.002), twice the classical ratio, is predicted by Dirac's relativistic equation for the electron. The classical picture is wrong anyway: the electron is a point particle, with spin but no size and nothing rotating. Protons and neutrons, built of quarks, have more complicated moments (the neutron has one despite having no charge), and because the mass appears in the denominator their moments are far smaller than the electron's.

**The force on a moment** (transcript pp. 18–19), derived in electromagnetism:

<Formula label="Force on a magnetic moment" tex="\vec F = \nabla\left(\vec\mu\cdot\vec B\right)" cite="reading3" at="eq. (1.14)" />

pushing the moment towards where $\vec\mu\cdot\vec B$ grows fastest. Where the field lines spread out, a loop whose moment points along the field is pushed towards the stronger field, and one pointing against it towards the weaker field: that is why the two spin states are deflected in opposite directions.

## The Stern–Gerlach experiment

Silver atoms (transcript pp. 19–21) have 47 electrons: 46 fill closed shells and carry no net angular momentum, and the 47th, a 5s electron, has no orbital angular momentum. Sending silver atoms through the apparatus is like sending free spins, that is, magnetic moments. An oven releases atoms, a slit collimates them, and they pass between magnet poles shaped so that $B$ points mostly along $z$ and grows along $z$. Then

<Formula label="Force in the Stern–Gerlach magnet" tex="F_z \approx \mu_z\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial z}" cite="reading3" at="eq. (1.15)" />

the small sideways gradients averaging out.

Classically, atoms leave the oven with moments in random directions, so a continuous smear was expected on the screen. Instead there were **two spots** with nothing between. In their apparatus, with fields of about 0.1 T, the spots were about a fifth of a millimetre apart. From $\mu_z = -2\mu_BS_z/\hbar$ and the measured deflections:

<Formula label="Spin one-half" tex="\frac{S_z}{\hbar} = \pm\frac12" cite="reading3" at="eq. (1.17)" />

a **spin-½** particle. In today's interpretation each atom is in a superposition of up and down; the magnet splits the beam, each atom travels both ways, and only at the screen is one outcome registered (transcript p. 21).

## Lessons from sequences of filters

Draw a Stern–Gerlach apparatus along $\hat z$ as a box with one beam in and two out, $S_z = +\hbar/2$ above and $-\hbar/2$ below (transcript pp. 21–24).

1. **$\hat z$ then $\hat z$**: block the lower beam of a first $\hat z$ box and send the upper one into a second. Everything comes out up, nothing down. So the spin states $z+$ and $z-$ are **orthogonal**, with overlap zero, and $z+$ with itself has overlap one: an orthonormal basis of a **two-dimensional complex vector space**, in which every spin state is a combination. That every spin state is such a combination is, as Zwiebach stresses, a bold physical assumption. This is confusing at first: the arrows "up" and "down" point in opposite directions, yet the states are orthogonal, not opposite.
2. **$\hat z$ then $\hat x$**: send $z+$ into an $\hat x$ box. Half come out with $S_x = +\hbar/2$ and half with $-\hbar/2$. States along $z$ and along $x$, at right angles as arrows, are **not** orthogonal as states.
3. **$\hat z$, $\hat x$, then $\hat z$**: keep only the $S_x = -\hbar/2$ beam and send it into a $\hat z$ box. Again half up, half down: the earlier $S_z = +\hbar/2$ has been forgotten. No state has both definite $S_z$ and definite $S_x$.

<WorkedExample title="How far do the atoms split?">
<Problem>
A silver atom ($m = 1.79 \times 10^{-25}$ kg) with moment $\mu_z = \pm\mu_B$ ($\mu_B = 9.274 \times 10^{-24}$ J/T) crosses a magnet 3.5 cm long at 550 m/s, in a field gradient of 1000 T/m. (a) Find the force and acceleration. (b) Find the deflection at the end of the magnet, and the separation of the two beams there. (c) By what factor is a proton's magnetic moment scale, $e\hbar/2m_p$, smaller than $\mu_B$ ($m_p/m_e = 1836$)?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Force">
$F = \mu_B\,\partial B_z/\partial z \approx 9.27 \times 10^{-21}$ N, so $a = F/m \approx 5.18 \times 10^4$ m/s², about five thousand times gravity.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Deflection">
The time in the magnet is $0.035/550 \approx 6.36 \times 10^{-5}$ s, so each beam moves $\frac12at^2 \approx 1.05 \times 10^{-4}$ m, and the two are about 0.21 mm apart: the fifth of a millimetre Stern and Gerlach saw.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Nuclear moments">
The scale goes as $1/m$, so it is 1836 times smaller for the proton: nuclear moments barely deflect atoms, which is why the electron's spin dominates.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$. Position representation: $\hat x$ multiplies, $\hat p = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d}{dx}$; on a grid, $\psi$ is a column vector and $\hat x$ a diagonal matrix.
- Eigenstates: $\delta(x - x_0)$ for position, $e^{ipx/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$ for momentum; the Fourier transform changes representation, with $\hat p = p$ and $\hat x = i\hbar\,d/dp$ in momentum space.
- Classically $\mu = \frac{Q}{2M}L$; for the electron $\vec\mu = -2\mu_B\vec S/\hbar$; the force is $\nabla(\vec\mu\cdot\vec B)$.
- Stern–Gerlach (1922): two spots, $S_z = \pm\hbar/2$. Filter sequences show a two-dimensional state space in which up and down are orthogonal and $z$ and $x$ states overlap.
