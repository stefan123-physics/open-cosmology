Quantum Physics II begins by restating the wave mechanics of 8.04 with care: what the Schrödinger equation does and does not say, which wavefunctions and potentials are allowed, and the two properties of energy eigenstates, orthonormality and completeness, on which everything else rests (transcript pp. 1–22; notes sections 1–2). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's recorded lecture and his notes "Wave Mechanics"; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. Much of it reviews the 8.04 pages on the [Schrödinger equation](/courses/8-04/schrodinger-equation), the [probability current](/courses/8-04/probability-current) and [stationary states](/courses/8-04/stationary-states).)

## The Schrödinger equation

For a non-relativistic particle in one dimension, in a potential that may depend on time (transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Schrödinger equation" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}(x, t) = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x, t)\right)\Psi(x, t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

**$\Psi$ must be complex.** With a real potential, a real $\Psi$ would make the right side real and the left side imaginary. This is different from electromagnetism, where complex fields such as a circularly polarized wave are only a convenience and one always means the real part: here the $i$ is essential (transcript pp. 1–2).

Three basic properties (transcript pp. 2–4):

- **First order in time**: knowing $\Psi(x, t_0)$ for all $x$ determines $\Psi$ at all later times.
- **Linear**: if $\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2$ are solutions, so is $a_1\Psi_1 + a_2\Psi_2$ for any complex $a_1$, $a_2$.
- **Probability** (the notes write the density as a script P, also denoted $\rho$, which this page uses): $\rho(x, t)\,dx = |\Psi(x, t)|^2dx$ is the probability of finding the particle between $x$ and $x + dx$, so a single particle needs $\int|\Psi|^2dx = 1$ at all times, and $\Psi$ has units of $1/\sqrt{\text{length}}$.

<Formula label="Probability density and normalization" tex="\rho(x, t) = \Psi^*(x, t)\Psi(x, t) = |\Psi(x, t)|^2, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi(x, t)|^2\,dx = 1" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (1.4), (1.6)" />

Is the normalization kept as time goes on? Zwiebach leaves it as a quick calculation: show $\frac{d}{dt}\int|\Psi|^2dx = 0$ using the equation and its complex conjugate. The integration by parts drops boundary terms, which needs $\Psi \to 0$ at infinity and $\partial\Psi/\partial x$ bounded (transcript pp. 4–6).

**The probability current** (transcript pp. 6–7):

<Formula label="Probability current and conservation" tex="J(x, t) = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right), \qquad \frac{\partial J}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = 0, \qquad \frac{dP_{ab}}{dt} = J(a, t) - J(b, t)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (1.9), (1.12), (1.14)" />

the analogue of charge conservation: the probability $P_{ab}$ of finding the particle between $a$ and $b$ changes only by flow through the ends.

**Equivalent wavefunctions** (transcript p. 8). $\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2$ describe the same physics if $\Psi_1 = c\Psi_2$ for a complex constant $c$; if both are normalized, $c$ is a phase. Every observable must give the same answer for both.

## Stationary states

Take $V$ independent of time and write the equation as $i\hbar\,\partial\Psi/\partial t = \hat H\Psi$ with the Hamiltonian operator $\hat H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x)$. A **stationary state** of energy $E$ (a real number) has the form (transcript p. 9)

<Formula label="Stationary state" tex="\Psi(x, t) = e^{-iEt/\hbar}\,\psi(x)" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

Its probability density and current carry no time dependence, and nor does the expectation value of any time-independent operator. Substituting into the equation gives the **time-independent Schrödinger equation** (transcript p. 10):

<Formula label="Time-independent Schrödinger equation" tex="\hat H\psi(x) = E\psi(x), \qquad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V(x)\psi(x) = E\psi(x)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.9), (2.10)" />

Here $\psi$ can be real. Being a second-order equation, it is solved once $\psi(x_0)$ and $\psi'(x_0)$ are given at one point, for any reasonable $V$ (transcript p. 11). In particular, if $\psi(x_0) = \psi'(x_0) = 0$ then $\psi = 0$ everywhere (transcript pp. 11–12): a nonzero solution can only reach zero with a nonzero slope.

A solution is an **energy eigenstate**, the set of allowed $E$ is the **spectrum**, and an energy with more than one solution is **degenerate** (transcript p. 12). Normalizability often allows only particular values of $E$.

**What is allowed** (transcript pp. 12–13). Potentials may be discontinuous at isolated points (the finite square well), unbounded (the oscillator), and may contain delta functions, but not their derivatives or powers; the notes also allow hard walls, as in the infinite square well. Wavefunctions are taken to satisfy

<Formula label="Conditions on wavefunctions" tex="\psi(x)\ \text{continuous and bounded}, \qquad \psi'(x)\ \text{bounded}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.12)" />

Normalizability is not demanded in general, because momentum eigenstates $e^{ipx/\hbar}$, which cannot be normalized, are too useful to exclude.

## Orthonormality and completeness

List the eigenstates by energy, $E_0 \le E_1 \le E_2 \le \dots$ (counting from 0, as in the lecture; the notes start at 1), with $\hat H\psi_n = E_n\psi_n$ (transcript p. 14). Think of functions as vectors with infinitely many components, one per point; their dot product is an integral, and the eigenstates can be chosen **orthonormal** (transcript p. 15):

<Formula label="Orthonormality" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_m^*(x)\,\psi_n(x)\,dx = \delta_{mn}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.16)" />

They are also **complete**: any reasonable function can be expanded in them, $\psi(x) = \sum_nb_n\psi_n(x)$ with complex $b_n$, a result mathematicians prove for this kind of (Sturm–Liouville) equation (transcript p. 16).

**Time evolution solved** (transcript pp. 16–17). If the state at $t = 0$ is $\sum_nb_n\psi_n(x)$, attach to each term its own phase:

<Formula label="Time evolution by expansion" tex="\Psi(x, t) = \sum_n b_n\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\,\psi_n(x), \qquad b_n = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_n^*(x)\,\Psi(x, 0)\,dx" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.20), (2.21)" />

Each term solves the Schrödinger equation, so the sum does; it matches the initial state; and since the equation is first order in time, it is **the** solution. The coefficients follow from orthonormality.

**The completeness relation** (transcript pp. 18–19). Substituting $b_n$ back into the expansion gives $\psi(x) = \int dx'\,K(x, x')\psi(x')$ with $K(x, x') = \sum_n\psi_n^*(x')\psi_n(x)$; a kernel that returns any function unchanged must be a delta function:

<Formula label="Completeness relation" tex="\sum_n\psi_n^*(x')\,\psi_n(x) = \delta(x - x')" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.25)" />

Compare it with orthonormality. There one sums over space with the labels fixed and gets a unit matrix in the labels; here one sums over the labels with the positions fixed and gets a "unit matrix" in position, the delta function. The two are dual statements (transcript pp. 19–20).

## Expectation values

For a time-independent operator $\hat A$ and a normalized state (transcript p. 20):

<Formula label="Expectation value" tex="\langle\hat A\rangle(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi^*(x, t)\,\hat A\,\Psi(x, t)\,dx" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.26)" />

which depends on time unless the state is stationary; a superposition of stationary states with different energies is not stationary. The Hamiltonian is the exception (transcript p. 21):

<Formula label="Expectation value of the energy" tex="\langle\hat H\rangle = \sum_n|b_n|^2E_n" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.28)" />

a time-independent weighted average: energy is conserved. Finally, an unnormalized $\Psi$ can be used directly by dividing by its norm (transcript pp. 21–22):

<Formula label="Expectation value with an unnormalized state" tex="\langle\hat A\rangle = \frac{\int\Psi^*\hat A\Psi\,dx}{\int\Psi^*\Psi\,dx}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.30)" />

<WorkedExample title="Two levels of a box">
<Problem>
An electron ($m = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg) is in an infinite square well of width $a = 1$ nm, with $E_n = n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2$ for $n = 1, 2, \dots$ ($\hbar = 1.054572 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $1$ eV $= 1.602177 \times 10^{-19}$ J, $h = 4.135667 \times 10^{-15}$ eV s). (a) Find $E_1$ and $E_2$. (b) The state at $t = 0$ is $(\psi_1 + \psi_2)/\sqrt2$. Find $\langle\hat H\rangle$, and the period with which the probability density sloshes back and forth. (c) For the unnormalized state $3\psi_1 + 4i\psi_2$, find $\langle\hat H\rangle$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Energies">
$E_1 = \pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2 \approx 6.025 \times 10^{-20}$ J $\approx 0.376$ eV, and $E_2 = 4E_1 \approx 1.504$ eV.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) An equal mixture">
$\langle\hat H\rangle = \frac12E_1 + \frac12E_2 \approx 0.940$ eV. In $|\Psi|^2$ the cross term carries $e^{-i(E_2 - E_1)t/\hbar}$, so the density repeats with period $h/(E_2 - E_1) \approx 4.136 \times 10^{-15}/1.128 \approx 3.67 \times 10^{-15}$ s: a few femtoseconds.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Without normalizing">
The norm is $9 + 16 = 25$, so $\langle\hat H\rangle = (9E_1 + 16E_2)/25 = (9 + 64)E_1/25 = 2.92E_1 \approx 1.098$ eV. Normalizing first, with $b_1 = 3/5$ and $b_2 = 4i/5$, gives the same.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The wavefunction is necessarily complex; the Schrödinger equation is first order in time and linear; $|\Psi|^2$ is a probability density, conserved, with current $J = \frac{\hbar}{m}\text{Im}(\Psi^*\,\partial\Psi/\partial x)$; $\Psi$ and $c\Psi$ are the same state.
- Stationary states $e^{-iEt/\hbar}\psi(x)$ give $\hat H\psi = E\psi$, a second-order equation fixed by $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at one point.
- Allowed potentials: piecewise continuous, possibly unbounded, with delta functions; wavefunctions: $\psi$ continuous and bounded, $\psi'$ bounded.
- Eigenstates are orthonormal and complete ($\sum_n\psi_n^*(x')\psi_n(x) = \delta(x - x')$); expanding the initial state solves time evolution; $\langle\hat H\rangle = \sum|b_n|^2E_n$.
