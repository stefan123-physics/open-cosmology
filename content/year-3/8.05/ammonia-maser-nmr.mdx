Two-state systems in action: the ammonia molecule, which leads to the **maser** (microwave amplification by stimulated emission of radiation, the microwave version of the laser), and the start of **nuclear magnetic resonance** (transcript pp. 1–24; notes chapter 7, sections 4–6). The last lecture's result is the tool throughout: $H = g_0I + \vec g\cdot\vec\sigma$ has energies $g_0 \pm g$ with eigenstates along $\pm\vec g$, and states precess about $\vec g$ at $2g/\hbar$. The first basis state plays the role of spin up and the second of spin down, whatever they physically are (transcript pp. 1–2). Electric field strengths are written $F$ here, so as not to clash with the energy $E_0$. (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Ammonia as a two-state system

Ammonia, NH₃, is a flattened tetrahedron: the nitrogen sits above the plane of the three hydrogens, with an angle of about $108°$ between the nitrogen–hydrogen bonds (a flat molecule would have $120°$) (transcript pp. 2–3). The nitrogen could equally sit below the plane; both are stable, separated by a barrier, like a particle in a double-well potential. Take the two basis states "nitrogen up" and "nitrogen down" (notes eq. (4.37)). A Hamiltonian $E_0I$ would make them degenerate, but in a double well the ground state is symmetric and the first excited state antisymmetric, at slightly different energies. Add an off-diagonal term (transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Ammonia Hamiltonian" tex="H = \begin{pmatrix}E_0 & -\Delta\\ -\Delta & E_0\end{pmatrix} = E_0I - \Delta\sigma_1, \qquad \Delta > 0" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (4.41)–(4.42)" />

The sign of $\Delta$ is a choice of basis, since flipping the sign of one basis vector flips it. Asked why $\Delta$ is real, Zwiebach answered that one tries the simplest thing that works (transcript p. 5). Comparing with $\vec g\cdot\vec\sigma$: $\vec g = -\Delta\hat x$ and $g = \Delta$. So the energies are $E_0 \pm \Delta$, with eigenstates (transcript pp. 5–6)

<Formula label="Ground and excited states" tex="\psi_G = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\text{up} + \text{down}\right), \quad E_G = E_0 - \Delta; \qquad \psi_E = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\text{up} - \text{down}\right), \quad E_E = E_0 + \Delta" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (4.43)–(4.44)" />

In the spin picture the excited state points along $\vec g$, that is along $-x$, and the ground state along $+x$ (transcript p. 6). The gap $2\Delta$ is (transcript p. 7)

<Formula label="Ammonia splitting" tex="2\Delta = 0.9872 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{eV}, \qquad \nu = 23.870\ \text{GHz}, \qquad \lambda = 1.2559\ \text{cm}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (4.45)–(4.47)" />

A molecule that starts with the nitrogen up is not in a stationary state. Expanding in $\psi_G$ and $\psi_E$ and evolving (transcript pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="Nitrogen flipping" tex="\psi(t) = e^{-iE_0t/\hbar}\left(\cos\frac{\Delta t}{\hbar}\;\text{up} + i\sin\frac{\Delta t}{\hbar}\;\text{down}\right), \qquad P_{\text{up}}(t) = \cos^2\frac{\Delta t}{\hbar}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (4.49)–(4.51)" />

The nitrogen flips up and down about 23 billion times a second with no outside influence. In the spin picture the state precesses about $x$ at $2\Delta/\hbar$, twice the rate in the cosine, because spin states depend on half the angle (transcript p. 8).

## Ammonia in an electric field

The hydrogens' electrons cluster near the nitrogen, so the molecule has an electric dipole moment, pointing down when the nitrogen is up. Following Feynman's lectures, call it $\mu$ (transcript pp. 8–9). In a field $F$ along $z$, the energy $-\vec\mu\cdot\vec F$ is $+\mu F$ for nitrogen up and $-\mu F$ for nitrogen down (transcript p. 9):

<Formula label="Ammonia in an electric field" tex="H = \begin{pmatrix}E_0 + \mu F & -\Delta\\ -\Delta & E_0 - \mu F\end{pmatrix} = E_0I - \Delta\sigma_1 + \mu F\sigma_3" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.2)" />

Now $\vec g = (-\Delta, 0, \mu F)$, so (transcript p. 10)

<Formula label="Energies in a field" tex="E_E, E_G = E_0 \pm \sqrt{\Delta^2 + \mu^2F^2} \approx E_0 \pm \left(\Delta + \frac{\mu^2F^2}{2\Delta}\right) \quad \text{for } \mu F \ll \Delta" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (5.3)–(5.4)" />

For laboratory fields $\mu F$ is much smaller than $\Delta$. The excited state's energy rises with the field, so in a field gradient it moves towards weak fields, while the ground state moves towards strong fields. Like a Stern–Gerlach apparatus, this separates a beam into excited and ground molecules (transcript pp. 10–11).

## The maser

Send the excited molecules through a cavity tuned to 23.87 GHz. A molecule that enters excited and leaves in the ground state has lost $2\Delta$, which can become one photon in the cavity (transcript p. 11). But an energy eigenstate is stationary: it would stay excited forever. The transition happens only because the cavity already holds an oscillating field, which makes the excited state no longer an eigenstate. The process starts from small fluctuations, a photon or two, and builds up into a self-consistent field (transcript pp. 11–12).

In the basis of $\psi_E$ and $\psi_G$ the Hamiltonian is $\begin{pmatrix}E_0 + \Delta & \mu F\\ \mu F & E_0 - \Delta\end{pmatrix}$ (transcript pp. 12–13; notes eq. (5.9)). Drop $E_0$, which only adds a phase. Writing $C_E = e^{-i\Delta t/\hbar}\beta_E$ and $C_G = e^{i\Delta t/\hbar}\beta_G$ removes the fast phases, and with the cavity field $F(t) = 2F_0\cos\omega_0t$, where $\hbar\omega_0 = 2\Delta$ (transcript pp. 14–15):

<Formula label="Equation for the slow amplitude" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{d\beta_E}{dt} = \mu F_0\left(1 + e^{2i\omega_0t}\right)\beta_G" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (5.13)–(5.16)" />

and similarly for $\beta_G$. Since $\mu F_0 \ll \hbar\omega_0$, the betas change slowly, and the rapidly oscillating $e^{2i\omega_0t}$ averages to zero over their time scale. Zwiebach checked this numerically and drops it (transcript pp. 15–16). Then $i\dot\beta_E = (\mu F_0/\hbar)\beta_G$ and $i\dot\beta_G = (\mu F_0/\hbar)\beta_E$, so $\ddot\beta_E = -(\mu F_0/\hbar)^2\beta_E$, and for a molecule entering excited (transcript pp. 16–17):

<Formula label="Probability to stay excited" tex="P_E(t) = \cos^2\left(\frac{\mu F_0\,t}{\hbar}\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.20)" />

(The transcript drops the square on $\mu F_0/\hbar$ in the equation for $\ddot\beta_E$ on p. 16; the solution uses it correctly.) The molecules must spend a time $T$ in the cavity with $\mu F_0T/\hbar = \pi/2$ (or $3\pi/2$, …), so their speed, set by the temperature, must match the cavity field (notes eq. (5.21)). Each molecule then leaves in the ground state, having added a photon. A cavity twice too long would let the molecule absorb the photon again (transcript p. 19).

Charles Townes, James Gordon and Herbert Zeiger built the ammonia maser at Columbia in 1953, and Townes shared the 1964 Nobel Prize. Because the emitting molecules are neutral, they do not disturb the field as charged electrons would; in his 1964 Nobel lecture Townes claimed that masers give the most perfect amplification the uncertainty principle allows (transcript p. 18; notes p. 16). The light is a coherent state: with $\Delta N = \sqrt{N}$ and $\Delta N\,\Delta\phi$ of order $\frac{1}{2}$, the phase is uncertain by only about $1/(2\sqrt{N})$, tiny for the $10^{12}$ to $10^{15}$ photons a cavity can easily hold (transcript p. 18; notes eq. (5.22)).

## Nuclear magnetic resonance: the set-up

A spin sits in a large static field along $z$ plus a smaller field rotating clockwise in the $xy$ plane (transcript p. 20):

<Formula label="NMR magnetic field" tex="\vec B(t) = B_0\hat z + B_1\left(\cos\omega t\,\hat x - \sin\omega t\,\hat y\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (6.23)" />

The Hamiltonian $-\gamma\vec B\cdot\vec S$ depends on time, and at different times it does **not** commute with itself (it contains $S_x$ at one time and $S_y$ a quarter period later), so none of the recipes for $U$ apply (transcript pp. 20–21). The idea is to change to a frame rotating with the field. Even if $H_S = 0$, a static spin seen from a frame rotating at $\omega$ about $z$ precesses, so the rotating frame needs a Hamiltonian $\omega S_z$ (transcript p. 22). In general, define $\psi_R = U(t)\psi$. Differentiating, one term comes from $U$ and one from the Schrödinger equation for $\psi$ (transcript p. 23):

<Formula label="Hamiltonian in a moving frame" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{d\psi_R}{dt} = H_R\,\psi_R, \qquad H_R = UH_SU^\dagger + i\hbar\,\frac{dU}{dt}\,U^\dagger" cite="reading2" at="eq. (6.28)" />

Requiring the second term to be $\omega S_z$ gives $U = e^{-i\omega tS_z/\hbar}$, so $\psi(t) = e^{i\omega tS_z/\hbar}\psi_R(t)$ (transcript pp. 23–24; notes eqs. (6.25)–(6.26)). Whether $H_R$ is then time-independent, which would solve the problem, is left for the next lecture.

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="Stark shift and maser timing">
<Problem>
(a) Ammonia has $\Delta = 0.4936 \times 10^{-4}$ eV. In a static field with $\mu F = 1.0 \times 10^{-5}$ eV, find the exact rise of the excited level, $\sqrt{\Delta^2 + \mu^2F^2} - \Delta$, and compare with the approximation $(\mu F)^2/2\Delta$. (b) In a maser with $\mu F_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{-11}$ eV, how long must a molecule stay in the cavity to leave in the ground state, and how long is the cavity for molecules moving at 400 m/s? Use $\hbar = 6.582 \times 10^{-16}$ eV s.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Exact and approximate shifts">
$\sqrt{(4.936)^2 + 1^2} \times 10^{-5} = 5.036 \times 10^{-5}$ eV, so the rise is $(5.036 - 4.936) \times 10^{-5} \approx 1.00 \times 10^{-6}$ eV. The approximation gives $(10^{-5})^2/(9.872 \times 10^{-5}) \approx 1.01 \times 10^{-6}$ eV, within about 1%, since $\mu F$ is only a fifth of $\Delta$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Time in the cavity">
$\mu F_0T/\hbar = \pi/2$ gives $T = \pi\hbar/(2\mu F_0) = \pi \times 6.582 \times 10^{-16}/(2.0 \times 10^{-11}) \approx 1.03 \times 10^{-4}$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Length">
At 400 m/s the molecules cross $400 \times 1.03 \times 10^{-4} \approx 0.041$ m, a cavity about 4 cm long. A stronger field would need a shorter cavity, and a cavity twice as long would return the molecules to the excited state.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Ammonia: $H = E_0I - \Delta\sigma_1$ in the nitrogen up/down basis; ground state (up + down)/√2 at $E_0 - \Delta$, excited state (up − down)/√2 at $E_0 + \Delta$, a 23.87 GHz (1.26 cm) transition; nitrogen up oscillates as $\cos^2(\Delta t/\hbar)$.
- In an electric field $H$ gains $\mu F\sigma_3$ and the levels become $E_0 \pm \sqrt{\Delta^2 + \mu^2F^2}$, so a field gradient separates excited from ground molecules.
- In a cavity field $2F_0\cos\omega_0t$, dropping fast terms gives $P_E = \cos^2(\mu F_0t/\hbar)$; molecules timed to leave in the ground state each add a photon to a coherent field: the maser (Townes, Gordon and Zeiger, 1953).
- NMR uses $B_0\hat z$ plus a rotating $B_1$; in a frame changed by $U(t)$, $H_R = UH_SU^\dagger + i\hbar\,\frac{dU}{dt}U^\dagger$, and $U = e^{-i\omega tS_z/\hbar}$ adds $\omega S_z$.
