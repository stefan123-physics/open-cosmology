This lecture was given by a guest lecturer, with Professor Zwiebach away (transcript p. 1). It continues hydrogen's small corrections with the **hyperfine** coupling between the electron's and the proton's spins, whose splitting of the ground state produces the astronomers' 21 cm line, and then treats the addition of two arbitrary angular momenta in general (transcript pp. 1–22; notes chapter 10, sections 2 and 6; the notes' section on hyperfine splitting has only its heading, so that part follows the lecture alone). Charges are in Gaussian units. (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Recap and the proton's spin

With spin–orbit coupling, $L_z$ and $S_z$ are no longer good quantum numbers; $\vec J^2$ and $J_z$ replace them, and the six $n = 2$, $l = 1$ states split into a $j = \frac{3}{2}$ quartet, up by $\frac{\hbar^2}{2}$ times the radial factor, and a $j = \frac{1}{2}$ doublet, down by $\hbar^2$ times it: the **fine structure** (transcript pp. 1–2). The proton also has spin $\frac{1}{2}$, which brings a smaller effect, the **hyperfine structure**. Now only the total $\vec J = \vec L + \vec S_e + \vec S_p$ is conserved, and states are labelled by $H$, $\vec L^2$, $\vec S_e^2$, $\vec S_p^2$, $\vec J^2$ and $J_z$ (transcript pp. 2–3). Of the possible couplings, the lecture treats the one between the two spins (transcript p. 3).

Both spins carry magnetic moments (transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="Magnetic moments" tex="\vec\mu_e = -\frac{e}{m_ec}\,\vec S_e, \qquad \vec\mu_p = \frac{g_pe}{2m_pc}\,\vec S_p, \qquad g_p \approx 5.6" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

For the electron, a fundamental particle, $g$ is very close to 2 (here $\frac{g}{2} = 1$). The proton's $g_p \approx 5.6$ betrays a composite particle, made of quarks and gluons (transcript p. 3).

## The hyperfine Hamiltonian

The electron sits in the magnetic field of the proton's dipole, $H_{\text{HF}} = -\vec\mu_e\cdot\vec B_p$ (transcript p. 3). The field of a point dipole has the familiar $1/r^3$ part plus a term at the origin, needed to get the field inside a spinning charge right as its size shrinks to zero; Griffiths derives it (transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Field of a point magnetic dipole" tex="\vec B_p = \frac{1}{r^3}\left(3\left(\vec\mu_p\cdot\hat r\right)\hat r - \vec\mu_p\right) + \frac{8\pi}{3}\,\vec\mu_p\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

(The transcript has a $c^2$ in the contact term; in Gaussian units as written here there is none.) Inserting the moments gives a coupling of the form $S_{e,i}S_{p,j}$ times $\frac{1}{r^3}(3\hat r_i\hat r_j - \delta_{ij})$ plus a delta-function term (transcript p. 4). For the ground state, take its expectation value in the spatial wavefunction $\psi_{100}$, which depends only on $r$ (transcript p. 5). Symmetry does the work: $\int d^3r\,r_i f(r^2)$ must vanish, since it carries one index and there is no vector to supply it, and $\int d^3r\,r_ir_jf(r^2)$ can only be a number times $\delta_{ij}$, the number making $\hat r_i\hat r_j$ average to $\frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}$ (transcript pp. 5–6). So the $1/r^3$ part cancels and only the contact term survives, weighted by $|\psi_{100}(0)|^2 = 1/\pi a_0^3$ (transcript p. 6):

<Formula label="Hyperfine coupling in the ground state" tex="\left\langle H_{\text{HF}}\right\rangle_{100} = \frac{\Delta E_{\text{HF}}}{\hbar^2}\,\vec S_e\cdot\vec S_p, \qquad \Delta E_{\text{HF}} = \frac{4}{3}\,g_p\,\frac{m_e}{m_p}\,\alpha^4\,m_ec^2" cite="reading1" at="pp. 6–7, 10 and 12" />

(The closed form for $\Delta E_{\text{HF}}$ is this page's own working: the lecture gave its pieces, $\frac{4}{3}g_p$ and $m_e/m_p$ on pp. 6–7 and the scaling $\alpha^4m_ec^2\,m_e/m_p$ on p. 12. With the contact term, $\Delta E_{\text{HF}} = \hbar^2\cdot\frac{8\pi}{3}\frac{g_pe^2}{2m_em_pc^2}\cdot\frac{1}{\pi a_0^3}$, and $a_0 = \hbar^2/m_ee^2$ turns this into the form above. The lecture also added a missing $\hbar^2$ when a student asked, transcript p. 10.) This is still an operator, acting on the spins: the coupling that the homework had simply assumed (transcript p. 6).

## Two spins one-half

The $z$ components of the two spins are no longer good quantum numbers, only $J_z$ (transcript p. 7). Combine $j_1 = j_2 = \frac{1}{2}$ with $m = m_1 + m_2$. The state with both spins up has $m = 1$ and both down $m = -1$; these must belong to a $j = 1$ multiplet, and the two $m = 0$ states give one more $j = 1$ state and a $j = 0$ state (transcript p. 7). Lowering $|1, 1\rangle = |\uparrow\uparrow\rangle$ with $J_- = J_-^{(1)} + J_-^{(2)}$ gives $\sqrt{2}\,\hbar|1, 0\rangle = \hbar(|\downarrow\uparrow\rangle + |\uparrow\downarrow\rangle)$, and the orthogonal combination is the singlet (transcript pp. 8–9; notes eqs. (2.15)–(2.18)):

<Formula label="Triplet and singlet" tex="j = 1:\ \ |\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,\ \ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle + |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle\right),\ \ |\downarrow\downarrow\rangle; \qquad j = 0:\ \ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|\uparrow\downarrow\rangle - |\downarrow\uparrow\rangle\right)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.11)–(2.18)" />

The first arrow always refers to the first spin (transcript p. 9). In the ground state $l = 0$, so $\vec J = \vec S_e + \vec S_p$ and $\vec S_e\cdot\vec S_p = \frac{1}{2}(\vec J^2 - \vec S_e^2 - \vec S_p^2)$, with $\vec S_e^2 = \vec S_p^2 = \frac{3}{4}\hbar^2$ (transcript pp. 9–10):

<Formula label="Hyperfine splitting" tex="\frac{\Delta E_{\text{HF}}}{\hbar^2}\,\vec S_e\cdot\vec S_p = \frac{\Delta E_{\text{HF}}}{2\hbar^2}\left(\vec J^2 - \frac{3}{2}\hbar^2\right) = \begin{cases}\ \ \frac{1}{4}\,\Delta E_{\text{HF}}, & j = 1\\[2pt] -\frac{3}{4}\,\Delta E_{\text{HF}}, & j = 0\end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11" />

The four ground states split into a triplet above and a singlet below, separated by $\Delta E_{\text{HF}}$ (transcript p. 11).

## A ladder of small corrections

Numerically $\Delta E_{\text{HF}} \approx 5.9 \times 10^{-6}$ eV, against 13.6 eV for the binding (transcript p. 11). The corrections form a ladder (transcript pp. 11–12):

- the Bohr energies go like $\alpha^2m_ec^2$;
- the fine structure like $\alpha^4m_ec^2$, smaller by $\alpha^2 \approx 1/19{,}000$;
- the hyperfine structure like $\alpha^4m_ec^2\,(m_e/m_p)$, smaller again by a factor of about 2000 (the proton mass is about 1836 electron masses);
- the Lamb shift goes like $\alpha^5m_ec^2$, and higher corrections follow; these energy levels are calculated to very high precision.

## The 21 cm line

An atom in the triplet can drop to the singlet by emitting a photon with energy $\Delta E_{\text{HF}}$ (transcript p. 12):

<Formula label="The 21 cm line" tex="\lambda = \frac{hc}{\Delta E_{\text{HF}}} \approx 21.1\ \text{cm}, \qquad \nu \approx 1420\ \text{MHz}" cite="reading1" at="p. 12" />

These are radio waves at about 1.4 GHz, far above FM radio at 88 to 108 MHz. (The lecture placed them at the end of the FM band, transcript p. 12.) Their wavelength is long compared with interstellar dust grains, so they pass straight through the dust (transcript p. 12). The cosmic microwave background, at 2.7 K, has photons of energy $kT \approx 0.2$ meV, far more than the splitting, so it can excite hydrogen, which then emits the 21 cm line (transcript pp. 12–13). The transition is extremely slow, a lifetime of about $10^7$ years, impossible to see in a laboratory, but hydrogen in space drifts undisturbed for that long and there is a great deal of it (transcript pp. 13–14). The long lifetime also makes the line very narrow (transcript p. 14). The line was first observed around 1951; in the lecturer's account it was the first way the spiral arms of our Galaxy were seen, and it did give the first map of them across the whole disc (transcript p. 13).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Weighing galaxies with hydrogen">
Gas on the two sides of a rotating galaxy moves towards us on one side and away on the other, so its 21 cm line is Doppler shifted up on one side and down on the other. The shift measures how fast the galaxy spins. The hydrogen moves faster than the visible matter could hold together: without extra, unseen mass the galaxy would fly apart. This is the evidence for **dark matter** that does not interact with light (transcript pp. 13–14). The rotation curves themselves are the subject of the dark matter course.
</Callout>

## Coupled and uncoupled bases

Now in general (transcript pp. 14–15). Let the first system have angular momentum $j_1$ with states $|j_1, m_1\rangle$, $m_1 = -j_1, \dots, j_1$, and the second $j_2$; the full space of, say, an electron in an atom is a sum of such fixed-$j$ spaces over all $j$, but it is enough to study one pair $(j_1, j_2)$ at a time (transcript p. 15). The total is $\vec J = \vec J_1\otimes 1 + 1\otimes\vec J_2$ (transcript p. 15). Two complete sets of commuting observables are available:

- **Uncoupled:** $\vec J_1^2$, $J_{1z}$, $\vec J_2^2$, $J_{2z}$, with basis $|j_1, j_2; m_1, m_2\rangle$, the products (transcript p. 15).
- **Coupled:** $\vec J_1^2$, $\vec J_2^2$, $\vec J^2$, $J_z$, with basis $|j_1, j_2; j, m\rangle$. Since $\vec J^2 = \vec J_1^2 + \vec J_2^2 + 2\sum_kJ_{1k}\otimes J_{2k}$ fails to commute with $J_{1z}$ and $J_{2z}$, those two must be dropped (transcript p. 16).

Both are orthonormal bases of the same space, so one can be expanded in the other, using the identity on the fixed-$(j_1, j_2)$ space (transcript pp. 16–17):

<Formula label="Clebsch–Gordan expansion" tex="|j_1, j_2; j, m\rangle = \sum_{m_1, m_2}|j_1, j_2; m_1, m_2\rangle\,\langle j_1, j_2; m_1, m_2|j_1, j_2; j, m\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 17" />

The numbers $\langle j_1, j_2; m_1, m_2|j_1, j_2; j, m\rangle$ are the **Clebsch–Gordan coefficients**, also written with six indices in other notations (transcript p. 17). They vanish unless $m = m_1 + m_2$: the operator $J_z - J_{1z} - J_{2z}$ is zero, and sandwiching it between the coupled state and the product state gives $\hbar(m - m_1 - m_2)$ times the coefficient (transcript pp. 17–18). They can be chosen real, obey a recursion relation but have no simple closed form, and are tabulated in many places (transcript p. 22).

## Which j occur

The total angular momenta that occur are (transcript p. 18; notes eq. (6.13))

<Formula label="Addition of angular momentum" tex="j_1\otimes j_2 = \left(j_1 + j_2\right)\oplus\left(j_1 + j_2 - 1\right)\oplus\dots\oplus\left|j_1 - j_2\right|" cite="reading2" at="eq. (6.13)" />

each exactly once. Picture adding a vector of length $j_2$ to one of length $j_1$ in any direction, but landing only on allowed lengths, from $j_1 + j_2$ down to $|j_1 - j_2|$ (transcript p. 18). Three checks:

- **Counting.** For $j_1 \ge j_2$, $\sum_{j = j_1 - j_2}^{j_1 + j_2}(2j + 1)$, written as the difference of two sums of $2j + 1$ from 0, equals $(2j_1 + 1)(2j_2 + 1)$, the number of product states (transcript p. 18).
- **The top state.** The only state with $m = j_1 + j_2$ is $|j_1, m_1 = j_1\rangle|j_2, m_2 = j_2\rangle$. Writing $\vec J^2 = \vec J_1^2 + \vec J_2^2 + 2J_{1z}J_{2z} + J_{1+}J_{2-} + J_{1-}J_{2+}$, the last two terms kill it, since both raising operators annihilate a top state, leaving $\hbar^2(j_1(j_1 + 1) + j_2(j_2 + 1) + 2j_1j_2) = \hbar^2(j_1 + j_2)(j_1 + j_2 + 1)$, as for $j = j_1 + j_2$ (transcript pp. 19–20).
- **Counting by $m$.** There is 1 product state with $m = j_1 + j_2$, 2 with one less, and so on, up to $2j_2 + 1$ states, a number that then stays constant down to $m = -(j_1 - j_2)$ before shrinking again. Each new state at a lower $m$ starts a new multiplet, one unit lower in $j$ (transcript pp. 20–22). (In the lecture the largest count was first given as "$2j_2 + 2$"; it is $2j_2 + 1$.)

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="Hydrogen's radio line from first principles, a Doppler shift, and 2 ⊗ 3/2">
<Problem>
(a) Using $g_p = 5.586$, $m_e/m_p = 1/1836.15$, $\alpha = 1/137.036$ and $m_ec^2 = 510{,}999$ eV, compute $\Delta E_{\text{HF}}$, the wavelength $hc/\Delta E_{\text{HF}}$ (with $hc = 1.23984 \times 10^{-6}$ eV m) and the frequency. (b) Hydrogen at the edge of a galaxy moves towards us at 200 km/s. By how much is its 1420.4 MHz line shifted? (c) Which multiplets make up $2\otimes\frac{3}{2}$, and do the states add up?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The splitting">
$\alpha^4m_ec^2 = 510{,}999/137.036^4 \approx 1.449 \times 10^{-3}$ eV. Times $\frac{4}{3} \times 5.586/1836.15 = 4.056 \times 10^{-3}$ gives $\Delta E_{\text{HF}} \approx 5.88 \times 10^{-6}$ eV. Then $\lambda = 1.23984 \times 10^{-6}/5.88 \times 10^{-6} \approx 0.211$ m and $\nu = c/\lambda \approx 1.42$ GHz. This simple calculation lands within 0.1% of the measured 1420.4 MHz.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The Doppler shift">
$\Delta\nu = \nu\,v/c = 1420.4 \times 200/299{,}792 \approx 0.948$ MHz, upwards for gas approaching us. Gas on the far side, receding at the same speed, is shifted down by as much, so the two sides differ by about 1.9 MHz.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) 2 ⊗ 3/2">
$j$ runs from $2 + \frac{3}{2} = \frac{7}{2}$ down to $2 - \frac{3}{2} = \frac{1}{2}$: $\frac{7}{2}\oplus\frac{5}{2}\oplus\frac{3}{2}\oplus\frac{1}{2}$, with $8 + 6 + 4 + 2 = 20$ states, equal to $5 \times 4 = 20$ product states.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The proton's dipole field has a contact term $\frac{8\pi}{3}\vec\mu_p\delta^3(\vec r)$; in the spherically symmetric ground state only it survives, giving $H = \frac{\Delta E_{\text{HF}}}{\hbar^2}\vec S_e\cdot\vec S_p$ with $\Delta E_{\text{HF}} = \frac{4}{3}g_p\frac{m_e}{m_p}\alpha^4m_ec^2 \approx 5.9 \times 10^{-6}$ eV.
- $\frac{1}{2}\otimes\frac{1}{2} = 1\oplus 0$: the triplet moves up by $\frac{1}{4}\Delta E_{\text{HF}}$, the singlet down by $\frac{3}{4}\Delta E_{\text{HF}}$.
- The 21 cm (1420 MHz) line passes through dust, lives about $10^7$ years, and through its Doppler shifts measures galaxy rotation, pointing to dark matter.
- $j_1\otimes j_2 = (j_1 + j_2)\oplus\dots\oplus|j_1 - j_2|$, each once; coupled and uncoupled bases are related by Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, which vanish unless $m = m_1 + m_2$.
