The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 7, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

A few things differ from MIT's sheet. Its due date is left out. MIT's Problems 2 and 7 have no parts: Problem 2 is kept whole, and the labels (a) and (b) in Problem 7 are ours, with part (a) a step that the lecture notes leave to this problem set.

Several of MIT's parts ask for a derivation or an explanation, which no box can check. So that there is something to check, some boxes are our additions, each marked as such in its part: the last box of Problem 1(d) (a field that changes too fast); the choice box of Problem 3(a) and the box of Problem 3(c); all four boxes of Problem 4; the last box of Problem 5(c) (an electron); and the second box of Problem 7(b). Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is MIT's. The lessons write the spin states as columns; here they are kets, $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ for spin up and down along $z$, and $|x; \pm\rangle$ for spin up and down along $x$. Position, momentum and the oscillator's lowering operator carry hats ($\hat x$, $\hat p$, $\hat a$); the spin operators $S_x$, $S_y$, $S_z$ and the Hamiltonian $H$ do not. A Heisenberg operator has the subscript $H$ and a time, as in $\hat x_H(t)$; for the spin MIT writes it with a hat, $\hat S_x(t)$. The oscillator length that the lesson on coherent states calls $d_0$ is $d$ here, as on MIT's sheet. The letter $\lambda$ is MIT's in two places: the constant that ties the magnetic moment to the spin in Problems 1 and 2 (the lesson of Lecture 16 calls it $\gamma$), and the coefficient of $t^2$ in Problem 5. Where a solution says "notes" it means Zwiebach's notes *Quantum Dynamics* of this course. Two problems use constants of nature (CODATA 2018, rounded): the electron's mass $9.109\times 10^{-31}$ kg and $\hbar = 1.0546\times 10^{-34}$ J s in Problem 5, and the energy $-13.6$ eV of the ground state of hydrogen in Problem 4.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Spin in a time-varying magnetic field" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A spin one-half sits in a magnetic field that is the same everywhere and points along $z$, but whose strength oscillates:

<Formula label="The oscillating field" tex="\vec B = B_0\cos(\omega t)\,\vec e_z" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

As in Problem 2, the magnetic moment is $\lambda$ times the spin, so that the Hamiltonian is $H(t) = -\lambda\,\vec B\cdot\vec S = -\lambda B_0\cos(\omega t)\,S_z$. At $t = 0$ the spin is in the eigenstate of $S_x$ with eigenvalue $+\hbar/2$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the unitary operator $\mathcal U(t)$ that takes the state at time 0 to the state at time $t$. The Hamiltonian depends on time, but its values at two different times commute: $[H(t), H(t')] = 0$. Write your result as

<Formula label="The form of the answer" tex="\mathcal U(t) = \exp\left(\frac{i\,\beta(t)\,S_z}{\hbar}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our way of writing the answer)" />

and give the angle $\beta(t)$.

<Hint>

When the Hamiltonians at different times commute, the lesson on the Heisenberg picture gives the time-evolution operator as an exponential of an integral. Which integral?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The exponent is $-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t H(t')\,dt'$. Only the cosine depends on time.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-beta" label="$\beta(t) =$" expr="lam*B0*sin(omega*t)/omega"
  vars={{ lam: [0.5, 2], B0: [0.5, 2], omega: [0.5, 2], t: [0.2, 1.2] }}
  names={{ lam: "\\lambda", B0: "B_0", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "lam*B0*cos(omega*t)*t", hint: "That is the Hamiltonian at time t, multiplied by t. The exponent holds the integral of H from 0 to t, not H(t) times t." },
    { expr: "-lam*B0*sin(omega*t)/omega", hint: "Count the minus signs: one in H = −λ B S_z and one in the exponent −(i/ħ)∫H dt. Together they give a plus." },
    { expr: "lam*B0*sin(omega*t)", hint: "The integral of cos(ωt′) from 0 to t is sin(ωt)/ω. Without the 1/ω the exponent would not be a pure number." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At every time the Hamiltonian is a number times the same operator $S_z$, so the Hamiltonians at different times commute. Then the time-evolution operator is the exponential of the integral of the Hamiltonian.

<Step title="The rule for commuting Hamiltonians">

<Formula label="Time evolution when the Hamiltonians commute" tex="\mathcal U(t, 0) = \exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t dt'\,H(t')\right]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.46)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

<Formula label="The exponent for the oscillating field" tex="-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^t dt'\,\bigl(-\lambda B_0\cos\omega t'\bigr)S_z = \frac{i}{\hbar}\,\frac{\lambda B_0}{\omega}\,\sin(\omega t)\,S_z, \qquad \beta(t) = \frac{\lambda B_0}{\omega}\,\sin\omega t" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$S_z$ has the units of $\hbar$ and $H$ is an energy, so $\lambda B_0$ is one over a time and $\beta$ is a pure number, as an exponent must be. For times much shorter than $1/\omega$ the field has not changed yet, and $\beta \approx \lambda B_0 t$: that is $\mathcal U = e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ for the constant Hamiltonian $-\lambda B_0 S_z$. And $\mathcal U(0)$ is the identity.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find the state at time $t$. A spin state that points along the direction with polar angle $\theta$ and azimuthal angle $\phi$ is, up to a phase, $\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,|+\rangle + \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{i\phi}\,|-\rangle$. Give the angles $\theta(t)$ and $\phi(t)$ of the spin, with $\phi(0) = 0$ and $\phi$ continuous in time.

<Hint>

Write the starting state in the basis $|+\rangle$, $|-\rangle$. On each of the two, $S_z$ in the exponent is just a number.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Take a common phase out of the state so that the coefficient of $|+\rangle$ is real and positive. The phase left on $|-\rangle$ is $e^{i\phi}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-theta" label="$\theta(t)$:" choices={["θ = ωt", "θ = π/2 − β(t)", "θ = π/2 at all times", "θ swings between 0 and π"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p1-phi" label="$\phi(t) =$" expr="-lam*B0*sin(omega*t)/omega"
  vars={{ lam: [0.5, 2], B0: [0.5, 2], omega: [0.5, 2], t: [0.2, 1.2] }}
  names={{ lam: "\\lambda", B0: "B_0", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "lam*B0*sin(omega*t)/omega", hint: "Check the direction. The state |+⟩ gets the phase e^(iβ/2) and |−⟩ gets e^(−iβ/2), so the relative phase of |−⟩ is e^(−iβ)." },
    { expr: "-lam*B0*sin(omega*t)/(2*omega)", hint: "Each state gets half of β, with opposite signs. The relative phase is the difference of the two, which is the whole of β." },
    { expr: "-lam*B0*t", hint: "That is the angle for a constant field B₀. Here the field oscillates, so the angle is the integral of −λB(t)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the basis of $S_z$ the operator $\mathcal U$ only multiplies each basis state by a phase. The two phases are opposite, and their difference turns the spin about the $z$ axis.

<Step title="The starting state">

<Formula label="Spin up along x" tex="|x; +\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\bigl(|+\rangle + |-\rangle\bigr)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (4.86)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Acting with the time-evolution operator">

On $|\pm\rangle$ the operator $S_z$ is $\pm\hbar/2$, so $\mathcal U$ multiplies them by $e^{\pm i\beta/2}$:

<Formula label="The state at time t" tex="|\psi, t\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\Bigl(e^{i\beta/2}\,|+\rangle + e^{-i\beta/2}\,|-\rangle\Bigr) = \frac{e^{i\beta/2}}{\sqrt 2}\Bigl(|+\rangle + e^{-i\beta(t)}\,|-\rangle\Bigr)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Reading off the angles">

Compare with the spin state along a direction,

<Formula label="Spin state along the direction with angles θ and φ" tex="|\vec n; +\rangle = \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\,|+\rangle + \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\,e^{i\phi}\,|-\rangle" cite="reading2" at="eq. (4.83)" />

The two coefficients have the same size, so $\cos\frac{\theta}{2} = \sin\frac{\theta}{2}$ and $\theta = \pi/2$ at all times. The relative phase gives

<Formula label="The angles of the spin" tex="\theta(t) = \frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad \phi(t) = -\beta(t) = -\frac{\lambda B_0}{\omega}\,\sin\omega t" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The spin stays in the $xy$-plane and swings to and fro about the $z$ axis, never further than the angle $\lambda B_0/\omega$ from the $x$ axis.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The rate of turning is $d\phi/dt = -\lambda B_0\cos\omega t = -\lambda B(t)$. This is the precession of [Lecture 16](/courses/8-05/photon-states-spin-precession), where a spin in a constant field $B$ turns about the field at the rate $-\lambda B$ (there written $-\gamma B$): here the same holds at each moment, with the field of that moment. When the field reverses, the spin turns back.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Find the probability, as a function of time, that a measurement of $S_x$ gives $-\hbar/2$.

<Hint>

The probability is $|\langle x; -|\psi, t\rangle|^2$, with $|x; -\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}(|+\rangle - |-\rangle)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-prob" label="$P(S_x = -\hbar/2) =$" expr="sin(lam*B0*sin(omega*t)/(2*omega))^2"
  vars={{ lam: [0.5, 2], B0: [0.5, 2], omega: [0.5, 2], t: [0.2, 1.2] }}
  names={{ lam: "\\lambda", B0: "B_0", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sin(lam*B0*sin(omega*t)/omega)^2", hint: "The amplitude is (e^(iβ/2) − e^(−iβ/2))/2 = i sin(β/2): the half angle stays." },
    { expr: "cos(lam*B0*sin(omega*t)/(2*omega))^2", hint: "That is the probability of finding +ħ/2: it is 1 at t = 0. The probability asked for starts at 0." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Project the state of part (b) on the state with spin down along $x$.

<Step title="The amplitude">

<Formula label="Amplitude for spin down along x" tex="\langle x; -|\psi, t\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\Bigl(e^{i\beta/2} - e^{-i\beta/2}\Bigr) = i\,\sin\frac{\beta}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The probability">

<Formula label="Probability of spin down along x" tex="P\bigl(S_x = -\frac{\hbar}{2}\bigr) = \sin^2\frac{\beta(t)}{2} = \sin^2\left(\frac{\lambda B_0}{2\omega}\,\sin\omega t\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ the probability is 0, as it must be for a spin prepared along $+x$. A spin along a direction that makes the angle $\phi$ with the $x$ axis is found along $-x$ with probability $\sin^2(\phi/2)$, and with $\phi = -\beta$ this is the same result. The probabilities for $+\hbar/2$ and $-\hbar/2$ add to 1: $\cos^2\frac{\beta}{2} + \sin^2\frac{\beta}{2} = 1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

For which values of $\omega$ does the spin, at some moment, point along $-x$ with certainty (a full flip of $S_x$)? Give the largest such $\omega$.

Then (our addition) let the field oscillate faster than that, with $\omega = \lambda B_0/2$. What is the largest probability of finding $S_x = -\hbar/2$ that is ever reached?

<Hint>

A full flip needs $\sin^2(\beta/2) = 1$ at some time. What is the largest value that $\beta(t)$ reaches?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The largest $\beta$ is $\lambda B_0/\omega$, reached when $\sin\omega t = 1$. How large must it be for $\sin^2(\beta/2)$ to reach 1?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-wmax" label="Largest $\omega$ with a full flip:" expr="lam*B0/pi"
  vars={{ lam: [0.5, 2], B0: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ lam: "\\lambda", B0: "B_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*lam*B0/pi", hint: "That makes the largest β equal to π/2, a quarter turn: the probability then reaches only sin²(π/4) = 1/2. A full flip needs β = π." },
    { expr: "lam*B0/(2*pi)", hint: "That makes the largest β equal to 2π. The spin then flips on the way, but the condition for a flip is already met when the largest β is π, at a larger ω." },
    { expr: "lam*B0", hint: "That makes the largest β equal to 1 radian. A full flip needs half a turn, β = π." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-half" label="For $\omega = \lambda B_0/2$, the largest probability:" value="0.708" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-half" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The spin turns away from $+x$ by the angle $|\beta|$, which is never larger than $\lambda B_0/\omega$. A flip needs half a turn.

<Step title="The condition">

The probability of part (c) is 1 when $\beta/2 = \pm\pi/2$, that is when $|\beta| = \pi$ (or $3\pi$, $5\pi$, …). Since $|\beta(t)| \le \lambda B_0/\omega$, some time with $|\beta| = \pi$ exists exactly when

<Formula label="Condition for a full flip" tex="\frac{\lambda B_0}{\omega} \ge \pi \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \omega \le \frac{\lambda B_0}{\pi}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

At the largest of these, $\omega = \lambda B_0/\pi$, the flip happens when $\sin\omega t = 1$, first at $t = \pi/(2\omega)$, a quarter of the field's period. For slower fields the spin passes $-x$ earlier and turns on.

</Step>

<Step title="A field that changes too fast">

For $\omega = \lambda B_0/2$ the largest angle is $\lambda B_0/\omega = 2$ radians, less than $\pi$. The largest probability is $\sin^2(2/2) = \sin^2 1 \approx 0.708$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The meaning is simple. Starting at $t = 0$ the field keeps its sign for a quarter of its period, $\pi/(2\omega)$, and in that time it turns the spin by the angle $\lambda B_0/\omega$. Then the field reverses and turns the spin back. If the field reverses too soon, the spin has not gone half way round. In the limit $\omega \to \infty$ the angle goes to zero and the spin does not move at all.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Heisenberg operators for spin" points={5} mit="Problem 2">

A spin sits in a magnetic field that is constant in time and the same everywhere, of size $B$ along $z$. Its Hamiltonian in the Schrödinger picture does not depend on time:

<Formula label="Spin in a constant field" tex="H = -\lambda B\,S_z" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

Here $\lambda$ is the real constant that ties the magnetic moment to the spin. Find the Heisenberg operators $\hat S_x(t)$, $\hat S_y(t)$ and $\hat S_z(t)$ that belong to the Schrödinger operators $S_x$, $S_y$ and $S_z$, written with $S_x$, $S_y$, $S_z$ and the time. In the boxes, type `Sx` and `Sy` for the operators $S_x$ and $S_y$.

<Hint>

Use the Heisenberg equation of motion, $i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}\hat A_H = [\hat A_H, H_H]$. Here $H_H = H$, and the commutators are those of the spin operators.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You get two coupled equations: the rate of change of $\hat S_x(t)$ is a number times $\hat S_y(t)$, and the other way round. Differentiate one of them once more, as for the oscillator in the lesson.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-sx" label="$\hat S_x(t) =$" expr="Sx*cos(lam*B*t) + Sy*sin(lam*B*t)"
  vars={{ Sx: [0.5, 2], Sy: [0.5, 2], lam: [0.5, 2], B: [0.5, 2], t: [0.2, 1.2] }}
  names={{ Sx: "S_x", Sy: "S_y", lam: "\\lambda" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Sx*cos(lam*B*t) - Sy*sin(lam*B*t)", hint: "The turning goes the other way. Check the Heisenberg equation at t = 0: [S_x, S_z] = −iħ S_y gives dŜ_x/dt = +λB S_y." },
    { expr: "Sx*cos(lam*B*t/2) + Sy*sin(lam*B*t/2)", hint: "Half angles belong to the spin states. The operators turn like an ordinary vector, by the full angle λBt." },
    { expr: "Sx", hint: "S_x does not commute with the Hamiltonian, so its Heisenberg operator changes in time. Only S_z stays as it is." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-sy" label="$\hat S_y(t) =$" expr="Sy*cos(lam*B*t) - Sx*sin(lam*B*t)"
  vars={{ Sx: [0.5, 2], Sy: [0.5, 2], lam: [0.5, 2], B: [0.5, 2], t: [0.2, 1.2] }}
  names={{ Sx: "S_x", Sy: "S_y", lam: "\\lambda" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Sy*cos(lam*B*t) + Sx*sin(lam*B*t)", hint: "Check the Heisenberg equation at t = 0: [S_y, S_z] = iħ S_x gives dŜ_y/dt = −λB S_x. One of the two operators must get a minus sign, or their commutator would not stay iħ S_z." },
    { expr: "Sy*cos(lam*B*t/2) - Sx*sin(lam*B*t/2)", hint: "Half angles belong to the spin states. The operators turn like an ordinary vector, by the full angle λBt." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-sz" label="$\hat S_z(t) =$" choices={["S_z cos(λBt)", "S_z", "S_z − λBt", "S_z cos(λBt) + S_x sin(λBt)"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The Heisenberg equation turns the commutators of the spin operators into equations of motion. They are the equations of a vector that turns about the $z$ axis at a constant rate.

<Step title="The equations of motion">

The Hamiltonian does not depend on time, so $H_H(t) = H$, and $\hat S_z(t)$ commutes with it: $\hat S_z(t) = S_z$. For the other two use

<Formula label="Heisenberg equation of motion" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{d\hat A_H(t)}{dt} = \bigl[\hat A_H(t), H_H(t)\bigr]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (3.23)" />

and the commutators of the spin operators, which keep their form in the Heisenberg picture,

<Formula label="Commutators of the spin operators" tex="[S_x, S_y] = i\hbar\,S_z, \qquad [S_y, S_z] = i\hbar\,S_x, \qquad [S_z, S_x] = i\hbar\,S_y" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.17)" />

Then

<Formula label="Equations of motion for the spin" tex="\frac{d\hat S_x}{dt} = \frac{-\lambda B}{i\hbar}\,[\hat S_x, \hat S_z] = \lambda B\,\hat S_y, \qquad \frac{d\hat S_y}{dt} = \frac{-\lambda B}{i\hbar}\,[\hat S_y, \hat S_z] = -\lambda B\,\hat S_x" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solving them">

Differentiating the first equation and using the second: $\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\hat S_x = -(\lambda B)^2\,\hat S_x$. So $\hat S_x(t)$ is a combination of $\cos\lambda Bt$ and $\sin\lambda Bt$ with constant operators in front. At $t = 0$ it must be $S_x$ and its rate of change must be $\lambda B\,S_y$:

<Formula label="Heisenberg operators of the spin" tex="\hat S_x(t) = S_x\cos\lambda Bt + S_y\sin\lambda Bt, \qquad \hat S_y(t) = S_y\cos\lambda Bt - S_x\sin\lambda Bt, \qquad \hat S_z(t) = S_z" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The second follows from $\hat S_y = \frac{1}{\lambda B}\frac{d\hat S_x}{dt}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The commutator keeps its form: $[\hat S_x(t), \hat S_y(t)] = (\cos^2\lambda Bt + \sin^2\lambda Bt)\,[S_x, S_y] = i\hbar\,\hat S_z(t)$. As a check on the signs, take the spin of Problem 1 with a constant field: in the state $|x; +\rangle$ the expectation values are $\langle S_x\rangle = \hbar/2$ and $\langle S_y\rangle = 0$, so $\langle\hat S_x(t)\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2}\cos\lambda Bt$ and $\langle\hat S_y(t)\rangle = -\frac{\hbar}{2}\sin\lambda Bt$. The spin points along the azimuthal angle $\phi = -\lambda Bt$, which is Problem 1(b) for times much shorter than $1/\omega$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The Heisenberg picture and Newton's laws" points={10} mit="Problem 3">

<Part label="a" points={4}>

A particle on a line has the Hamiltonian $H = \hat p^2/(2m) + V(\hat x)$. Derive the Heisenberg equations of motion for $\hat x_H(t)$ and $\hat p_H(t)$, and from them Ehrenfest's theorem:

<Formula label="Ehrenfest's theorem" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat x\rangle = \frac{\langle\hat p\rangle}{m}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat p\rangle = -\langle V'(\hat x)\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), eq. (1), p. 1" />

Here $\langle\hat x\rangle = \langle\psi, 0|\hat x_H(t)|\psi, 0\rangle = \langle\psi, t|\hat x|\psi, t\rangle$, and the same for the other operators; $V'$ is the derivative of $V$. Combine the two equations into one for $\frac{d^2}{dt^2}\langle\hat x\rangle$, and say what the potential must be like for this equation to be Newton's law of classical mechanics for the mean position.

<Hint>

In the Heisenberg picture the Hamiltonian is the same function of $\hat x_H$ and $\hat p_H$, and $[\hat x_H(t), \hat p_H(t)] = i\hbar$ at every time. You need $[\hat x, \hat p^2]$ and $[\hat p, V(\hat x)]$.

<Step title="The Heisenberg equations">

With $H_H = \hat p_H^2/(2m) + V(\hat x_H)$ and $[\hat x_H, \hat p_H] = i\hbar$:

<Formula label="Heisenberg equations for position and momentum" tex="\frac{d\hat x_H}{dt} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\Bigl[\hat x_H, \frac{\hat p_H^2}{2m}\Bigr] = \frac{\hat p_H}{m}, \qquad \frac{d\hat p_H}{dt} = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\bigl[\hat p_H, V(\hat x_H)\bigr] = -V'(\hat x_H)" cite="reading1" at="eqs. </Step>

<Step title="Ehrenfest's theorem">

Take the expectation value of both equations in the state $|\psi, 0\rangle$. This state does not depend on time, so the time derivative can be taken outside. The two agree when $\langle V'(\hat x)\rangle = V'(\langle\hat x\rangle)$. So the potential must be at most quadratic: no force, a constant force, or a force that grows in proportion to $x$ (the oscillator). </Step>

<Formula label="Heisenberg equations for a constant force" tex="\frac{d\hat x_H}{dt} = \frac{\hat p_H}{m}, \qquad \frac{d\hat p_H}{dt} = -g \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m\,\frac{d^2\hat x_H}{dt^2} = -g = F" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Integrating">

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ these are $\hat x$ and $\hat p$. The commutator is kept: the terms with $g$ are numbers, so $[\hat x_H(t), \hat p_H(t)] = [\hat x + \hat p\,t/m, \hat p] = i\hbar$. With $g = 0$ the result is the free particle.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The force only adds a number to $\hat x_H(t)$, and a number does not change an uncertainty. What is left is the free-particle operator $\hat x + \hat p\,t/m$, whose uncertainty grows because the packet holds a spread of momenta.

<Step title="Dropping the number">

$\hat x_H(t) = \hat X(t) - \frac{g}{2m}t^2$ with $\hat X(t) = \hat x + \hat p\,t/m$. The uncertainty of $\hat x_H(t)$ is that of $\hat X(t)$, and by part (b) $\langle\hat X(t)\rangle = 0$. So

<Formula label="The squared uncertainty in three terms" tex="(\Delta x(t))^2 = \langle\hat X(t)^2\rangle = \langle\hat x^2\rangle + \frac{t}{m}\,\langle\hat x\hat p + \hat p\hat x\rangle + \frac{t^2}{m^2}\,\langle\hat p^2\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="The three expectation values">

For the normalised Gaussian, $\psi^2 \propto e^{-x^2/\Delta^2}$, so

<Formula label="Expectation values in the Gaussian" tex="\langle\hat x^2\rangle = \frac{\Delta^2}{2}, \qquad \langle\hat p^2\rangle = \hbar^2\int(\psi')^2\,dx = \frac{\hbar^2}{\Delta^4}\,\langle\hat x^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\Delta^2}, \qquad \langle\hat x\hat p + \hat p\hat x\rangle = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The second uses $\psi' = -x\,\psi/\Delta^2$. For the third: $\langle\hat x\hat p\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{i}\int\psi\,x\,\psi'\,dx = \frac{\hbar}{2i}\int x\,(\psi^2)'\,dx = -\frac{\hbar}{2i} = \frac{i\hbar}{2}$, and $\langle\hat p\hat x\rangle$ is its complex conjugate, so the sum is zero.

</Step>

<Step title="The coefficient">

<Formula label="The squared uncertainty of the position" tex="(\Delta x(t))^2 = \frac{\Delta^2}{2} + \frac{\hbar^2}{2m^2\Delta^2}\,t^2, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m^2\Delta^2} = \frac{(\Delta p)^2}{m^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The force $g$ is not in it: the packet spreads exactly as a free packet does, while its centre falls. A constant force pushes every part of the packet in the same way.

</Step>

<Step title="The electron">

The squared uncertainty has doubled when $\lambda t^2 = \Delta^2/2$, that is at $t = m\Delta^2/\hbar$. For an electron with $\Delta = 1.0$ nm:

<Formula label="Doubling time for the electron" tex="t = \frac{m\Delta^2}{\hbar} = \frac{9.109\times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}\times(1.0\times 10^{-9}\ \text{m})^2}{1.0546\times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}} \approx 8.64\times 10^{-15}\ \text{s} = 8.64\ \text{fs}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\lambda t^2$ is a length squared: $\hbar/(m\Delta)$ is a velocity. In words, $\sqrt\lambda = \Delta p/m$ is the spread of velocities in the packet, and at late times the width grows at that speed. A narrower packet (smaller $\Delta$) has a larger spread of momenta and spreads faster. At $t = 0$ the product of the uncertainties is $\frac{\Delta}{\sqrt 2}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt 2\,\Delta} = \frac{\hbar}{2}$, the smallest value allowed, as it should be for a Gaussian.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="The shifted harmonic oscillator" points={10} mit="Problem 6">

A harmonic oscillator is pushed by a constant force of size $F$ in the positive $x$ direction. Its Hamiltonian is

<Formula label="Oscillator with a constant force" tex="H = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}\,m\omega^2\hat x^2 - F\,\hat x" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 3" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A constant force only moves the resting point of an oscillator and lowers its energy by a constant. In the shifted position variable the Hamiltonian is the usual oscillator.

<Step title="Completing the square">

<Formula label="The Hamiltonian in the shifted variable" tex="H = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}\,m\omega^2\,\hat y^2 - \frac{F^2}{2m\omega^2}, \qquad \hat y = \hat x - x_0, \qquad x_0 = \frac{F}{m\omega^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Multiplying out $\frac{1}{2} m\omega^2(\hat x - x_0)^2$ gives $\frac{1}{2} m\omega^2\hat x^2 - m\omega^2x_0\,\hat x + \frac{1}{2} m\omega^2x_0^2$: the middle term is $-F\hat x$, and the last term, $F^2/(2m\omega^2)$, must be taken off again.

</Step>

<Step title="The ground state">

$\hat y$ and $\hat p$ obey $[\hat y, \hat p] = i\hbar$, so the first two terms are a harmonic oscillator with the same $m$ and $\omega$, and energies $\hbar\omega(n + \frac{1}{2})$. The constant shifts them all:

<Formula label="Ground-state energy and mean position" tex="E_0 = \frac{1}{2}\,\hbar\omega - \frac{F^2}{2m\omega^2}, \qquad \langle\hat x\rangle = x_0 + \langle\hat y\rangle = \frac{F}{m\omega^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$x_0$ is where the spring force $m\omega^2x_0$ balances $F$: the classical resting point. The energy shift is the classical one too: at $x_0$ the potential is $\frac{1}{2} m\omega^2x_0^2 - Fx_0 = -F^2/(2m\omega^2)$. With $F = 0$ everything returns to the plain oscillator.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

<Formula label="The new ground state from the old one" tex="|0'\rangle = N\,e^{\alpha\hat a^\dagger}\,|0\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="The wavefunction">

Act with $\langle x|$. On this bra $\hat x$ is the number $x$. The last exponential is the translation operator $T_{x_0}$, which moves a wavefunction by $x_0$:

<Formula label="Translating a wavefunction" tex="\langle x|\,T_{x_0}\,|\psi\rangle = \psi(x - x_0), \qquad T_{x_0} = e^{-i\hat p\,x_0/\hbar}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (4.1) and (4.13)" />

So

<Formula label="Wavefunction of the coherent state" tex="\psi_\alpha(x) = \exp\left[\frac{i\,p_0}{\hbar}\Bigl(x - \frac{x_0}{2}\Bigr)\right]\psi_0(x - x_0), \qquad \psi_0(x) = \frac{1}{\pi^{1/4}\sqrt d}\,e^{-x^2/(2d^2)}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (1.42) for the ground state; our own solution" />

that is $\eta(x) = \frac{p_0}{\hbar}\left(x - \frac{x_0}{2}\right)$. The ground-state wavefunction of the notes, $\left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}e^{-m\omega x^2/(2\hbar)}$, is written here with $d$.

</Step>

<Step title="The probability density">

The phase has size 1, so

<Formula label="Probability density of the coherent state" tex="|\psi_\alpha(x)|^2 = \psi_0(x - x_0)^2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt\pi\,d}\,e^{-(x - x_0)^2/d^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 3 (our own addition)" />

The wavefunction is normalised at sight: it is a phase times the normalised ground state, moved over.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The density is the ground-state Gaussian centred on $x_0$, so $\langle\hat x\rangle = x_0$. The phase winds at the rate $p_0/\hbar$ along $x$, the mark of the mean momentum $p_0$. Both agree with the lesson: $\langle\hat x\rangle = \sqrt 2\,d\,\text{Re}\,\alpha$ and $\langle\hat p\rangle = \frac{\sqrt 2\,\hbar}{d}\,\text{Im}\,\alpha$. The other order of the two exponentials gives the same wavefunction: $e^Y e^X e^{+\frac{1}{2}[X, Y]}$ first multiplies $\psi_0(x)$ by $e^{ip_0x/\hbar}$, then moves everything by $x_0$ to $e^{ip_0(x - x_0)/\hbar}\psi_0(x - x_0)$, and the constant $e^{+ip_0x_0/(2\hbar)}$ brings the phase back to $\frac{p_0}{\hbar}(x - \frac{x_0}{2})$. The constant part of the phase has a meaning too: with it, the overlap $\langle 0|\alpha\rangle = \int\psi_0\,\psi_\alpha\,dx$ is the real, positive number $e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}$ of the notes (eq. (4.78)).

</Step>
