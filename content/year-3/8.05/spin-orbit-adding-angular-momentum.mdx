This lecture begins the addition of angular momentum, motivated by a physical problem: the coupling between an electron's spin and its orbital motion in hydrogen. Three steps are needed: a simple result from perturbation theory, the theorem that two angular momenta add to a third, and the decomposition of a product of multiplets into multiplets of the total (transcript pp. 1–24; notes chapter 10, sections 1 and 3; the notes' section on spin–orbit coupling has only its heading, so that part follows the lecture alone). Charges are in Gaussian units. (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## The Feynman–Hellmann lemma

Let $H(\lambda)$ depend on a parameter, with normalised eigenstates $\psi_n(\lambda)$ and energies $E_n(\lambda)$ (transcript p. 1). Start from $E_n = \langle\psi_n|H|\psi_n\rangle$ and differentiate. The two terms in which the state is differentiated combine, since $H$ is Hermitian, into $E_n\frac{d}{d\lambda}\langle\psi_n|\psi_n\rangle$, which vanishes because the state stays normalised (transcript pp. 2–3; notes eqs. (3.3)–(3.6)):

<Formula label="Feynman–Hellmann lemma" tex="\frac{dE_n}{d\lambda} = \left\langle\psi_n(\lambda)\right|\frac{dH}{d\lambda}\left|\psi_n(\lambda)\right\rangle" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.1)" />

Zwiebach called it more a lemma than a theorem, and said he thought Feynman had found it as an undergraduate (transcript p. 3). Its use: for $H = H^{(0)} + \lambda H_1 = H^{(0)} + \Delta H$, expand $E_n(\lambda)$ about $\lambda = 0$ (transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="First-order energy shift" tex="E_n = E_n^{(0)} + \left\langle\psi_n^{(0)}\right|\Delta H\left|\psi_n^{(0)}\right\rangle + O\!\left(\Delta H^2\right)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (3.7)–(3.12)" />

Knowing the unperturbed states, the first correction to an energy is the expectation value of the extra term; correcting the states themselves is the business of perturbation theory in 8.06 (transcript p. 4). There is a catch: the derivation assumes the state evolves smoothly into an eigenstate of the new Hamiltonian, which fails for degenerate levels unless the right combinations are chosen first (transcript p. 5; notes p. 8).

## The sum of two angular momenta

Let $J_i^{(1)}$ obey the angular momentum algebra on a space $V_1$ and $J_i^{(2)}$ on $V_2$. Operators on different spaces can only be added on the tensor product, where each is upgraded (transcript pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="Total angular momentum" tex="J_i \equiv J_i^{(1)}\otimes 1 + 1\otimes J_i^{(2)}, \qquad \left[J_i, J_j\right] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,J_k \ \text{ on } V_1\otimes V_2" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (1.3) and (1.7)" />

The proof is short. Cross terms vanish, because operators of different spaces commute; in the rest, the identity is a spectator, leaving $[J_i^{(1)}, J_j^{(1)}]\otimes 1 + 1\otimes[J_i^{(2)}, J_j^{(2)}] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}(J_k^{(1)}\otimes 1 + 1\otimes J_k^{(2)})$ (transcript pp. 6–8; notes eqs. (1.4)–(1.7)). The sum is essentially the only combination that works: a minus sign, a factor 2 or a factor $\frac{1}{2}$ in front of either term would spoil the algebra (transcript p. 6; notes eqs. (1.8)–(1.9)). The notes stress that adding, say, spin to orbital angular momentum is not adding apples to oranges: all angular momenta are apples (notes pp. 1–2).

## Spin–orbit coupling: the physics

Hydrogen has no external magnetic field, yet the electron's magnetic moment $\vec\mu = -\frac{ge}{2mc}\vec S$ (with $g \approx 2$) feels one. Seen from the electron, the proton circles it: a current, producing a magnetic field along the orbital angular momentum (transcript pp. 8–10). More precisely, a charge moving through the proton's electric field sees, to first order in $v/c$, a magnetic field $\vec B' = \frac{1}{c}\vec E\times\vec v$. The electric field is radial with $eE = dV/dr$, where $V = -e^2/r$ is the potential energy, and $\vec r\times\vec v = \vec L/m$ (transcript pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="Magnetic field seen by the electron" tex="\vec B' = \frac{1}{ecm}\,\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}\,\vec L" cite="reading1" at="p. 11" />

Then $\Delta H = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B'$ gives a coupling of spin to orbital angular momentum (transcript pp. 11–13):

<Formula label="Spin–orbit coupling" tex="\Delta H = \frac{g - 1}{2m^2c^2}\,\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}\,\vec S\cdot\vec L" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–14" />

The naive calculation gives $g$ where $g - 1$ stands: the electron's rest frame is not inertial, since it moves on a curve, and a relativistic correction to precession rates, the **Thomas precession**, replaces $g$ by $g - 1$, halving the result for $g \approx 2$ (transcript pp. 11–12). Doing it properly would take an hour of special relativity, so the lecture, like Griffiths, only quotes it (transcript p. 12). (In the lecture the factor $1/ecm$ was at first left out and then restored, transcript pp. 12–13.) In SI units, as in Griffiths, $\Delta H = \frac{e^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{1}{m^2c^2r^3}\vec S\cdot\vec L$, already corrected (transcript p. 13).

**Size.** With $\vec S\cdot\vec L \sim \hbar^2$, $r \sim a_0 = \hbar^2/me^2$ and $\frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr} = e^2/r^3$, the ratio to the binding energy $e^2/2a_0$ is about $\hbar^2/m^2c^2a_0^2$ (transcript pp. 12–13):

<Formula label="Size of the fine structure" tex="\frac{\Delta H}{e^2/a_0} \sim \frac{\hbar^2}{m^2c^2a_0^2} = \left(\frac{e^2}{\hbar c}\right)^2 = \alpha^2 \approx \frac{1}{137^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

about one part in 19,000: the **fine structure** of hydrogen, invisible on a diagram scaled to 13.6 eV but very important (transcript p. 13). (The transcript says "1 over alpha squared" in one sentence; the ratio is $\alpha^2$.)

## Six degenerate states

Take the hydrogen level $n = 2$, $l = 1$ (transcript p. 14). Its orbital part has three states $|1, m_l\rangle$, and the electron's spin, a different vector space for the same particle, has two $|\frac{1}{2}, m_s\rangle$; the level is the tensor product, six states $|1, m_l\rangle\otimes|\frac{1}{2}, m_s\rangle$, the **uncoupled basis** (transcript pp. 14–15). These are degenerate and are not eigenstates of $\Delta H$, so the Feynman–Hellmann result cannot be applied to them directly: first the right combinations must be found (transcript p. 15). The operator is $\vec L\cdot\vec S = \sum_i L_i\otimes S_i$, which acts on the tensor product (transcript pp. 15–16).

The way out is the total angular momentum $\vec J = \vec L + \vec S$, meaning $\vec L\otimes 1 + 1\otimes\vec S$ (transcript p. 16). Squaring it, the mixed terms give $2\sum_iL_i\otimes S_i$, so (transcript p. 16)

<Formula label="L · S in terms of J" tex="\vec L\cdot\vec S = \frac{1}{2}\left(\vec J^2 - \vec L^2 - \vec S^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 16" />

For the hydrogen Hamiltonian with spin, $H$, $\vec L^2$, $L_z$, $\vec S^2$, $S_z$ commute. With $\Delta H$ added, $\vec L^2$ and $\vec S^2$ survive, since they commute with every $L_i$ and $S_i$; $L_z$ does not, because $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ contains $L_xS_x + L_yS_y$; but $\vec J^2$ and $J_z$ do, since $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ is built from $\vec J^2$, $\vec L^2$, $\vec S^2$ (transcript pp. 17–18). So the new commuting observables are $H$, $\vec L^2$, $\vec S^2$, $\vec J^2$, $J_z$: the states must be organised by total angular momentum (transcript p. 18).

## Spin 1 times spin ½

Arrange the six uncoupled states by $J_z = L_z + S_z$ (transcript p. 19). $|1, 1\rangle|\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\rangle$ alone has $J_z = \frac{3}{2}\hbar$ and $|1, -1\rangle|\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{1}{2}\rangle$ alone has $-\frac{3}{2}\hbar$; two states each have $J_z = \pm\frac{1}{2}\hbar$. The states must form multiplets of $\vec J$. The top value $\frac{3}{2}$ requires a $j = \frac{3}{2}$ multiplet, using four states, one combination at each $J_z$; the two left over make a $j = \frac{1}{2}$ multiplet (transcript pp. 19–20). (The transcript lists the $J_z$ values of the quartet as "3/2, 1/2, minus 1/2, and 0"; the last is $-\frac{3}{2}$, transcript p. 20.)

<Formula label="Spin 1 times spin ½" tex="1\otimes\frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{2}\oplus\frac{1}{2}, \qquad 3\times 2 = 4 + 2" cite="reading1" at="p. 20" />

The states themselves follow from the ladder operators. The top state is $|\frac{3}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\rangle = |1, 1\rangle|\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\rangle$. Apply $J_- = L_- + S_-$: on the left, $J_-|\frac{3}{2}, \frac{3}{2}\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{3}\,|\frac{3}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\rangle$; on the right, $L_-$ gives $\hbar\sqrt{2}\,|1, 0\rangle|\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\rangle$ and $S_-$ gives $\hbar\,|1, 1\rangle|\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{1}{2}\rangle$. Dividing (transcript pp. 20–22):

<Formula label="The j = 3/2 states with m = ±1/2" tex="\left|\tfrac{3}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}\,|1, 0\rangle\left|\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle + \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{3}}\,|1, 1\rangle\left|\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle, \qquad \left|\tfrac{3}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle = \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}\,|1, 0\rangle\left|\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle + \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{3}}\,|1, -1\rangle\left|\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 22" />

and $|\frac{3}{2}, -\frac{3}{2}\rangle = |1, -1\rangle|\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{1}{2}\rangle$. (In the lecture some coefficients were read out as "2 over 3" and "2 over square root of 3" where $\sqrt{2/3}$ is meant, and one was corrected on the spot, transcript pp. 22–23.) The $j = \frac{1}{2}$ states are the combinations orthogonal to these at the same $J_z$ (transcript pp. 22–23):

<Formula label="The j = 1/2 states" tex="\left|\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle = -\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{3}}\,|1, 0\rangle\left|\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle + \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}\,|1, 1\rangle\left|\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle, \qquad \left|\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{3}}\,|1, 0\rangle\left|\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle - \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}\,|1, -1\rangle\left|\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}\right\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 23" />

(Orthogonality fixes each $j = \frac{1}{2}$ state only up to an overall sign. The signs here follow the usual (Condon–Shortley) convention, in which $J_-$ takes $|\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\rangle$ to $\hbar|\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{1}{2}\rangle$.)

## The splitting

In these states $\vec J^2$, $\vec L^2$ and $\vec S^2$ all have definite values, with $l = 1$ and $s = \frac{1}{2}$ (transcript p. 23):

<Formula label="Eigenvalues of L · S" tex="\vec L\cdot\vec S = \frac{\hbar^2}{2}\left(j(j+1) - l(l+1) - s(s+1)\right) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2}\left(j(j+1) - 2 - \frac{3}{4}\right) = \begin{cases}\ \ \frac{\hbar^2}{2}, & j = \frac{3}{2}\\[2pt] -\hbar^2, & j = \frac{1}{2}\end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="p. 23" />

So the six states split: four ($j = \frac{3}{2}$) move up by $\frac{\hbar^2}{2}$ times the radial factor of $\Delta H$, two ($j = \frac{1}{2}$) move down by $\hbar^2$ times it (transcript pp. 23–24). All the work was needed because $\Delta H$ is simple in $\vec J^2$: once one knows that $1\otimes\frac{1}{2}$ gives a quartet and a doublet, the pattern of the splitting follows (transcript p. 24).

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="A d electron, the fine-structure scale, and a check of the states">
<Problem>
(a) For an electron with $l = 2$ (spin $\frac{1}{2}$), which values of $j$ occur, with how many states each, and what is $\vec L\cdot\vec S$ in units of $\hbar^2$ for each? Check that the shifts, weighted by the number of states, add up to zero. (b) Using $\alpha = 1/137.036$, estimate the fine-structure energy scale $\alpha^2 \times 13.6$ eV. (c) In the state $|\frac{3}{2}, \frac{1}{2}\rangle$ found above, what is the expectation value of $S_z$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A d electron">
The top state has $J_z = (2 + \frac{1}{2})\hbar$, so $j = \frac{5}{2}$ (6 states), and the rest form $j = \frac{3}{2}$ (4 states): $5 \times 2 = 6 + 4$. With $l(l+1) + s(s+1) = 6 + \frac{3}{4} = \frac{27}{4}$: for $j = \frac{5}{2}$, $\frac{1}{2}(\frac{35}{4} - \frac{27}{4}) = 1$; for $j = \frac{3}{2}$, $\frac{1}{2}(\frac{15}{4} - \frac{27}{4}) = -\frac{3}{2}$. Weighted: $6 \times 1 + 4 \times (-\frac{3}{2}) = 0$, as it must be, since $\vec L\cdot\vec S = \sum_i L_i\otimes S_i$ has zero trace.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The scale">
$\alpha^2 = 1/18{,}779 \approx 5.33 \times 10^{-5}$, so $\alpha^2 \times 13.6$ eV $\approx 7.2 \times 10^{-4}$ eV, less than a thousandth of an electronvolt.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Spin along z">
$\langle S_z\rangle = \frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{2} + \frac{1}{3}\cdot\left(-\frac{\hbar}{2}\right) = \frac{\hbar}{6}$, and $\langle L_z\rangle = \frac{2}{3}\cdot 0 + \frac{1}{3}\cdot\hbar = \frac{\hbar}{3}$: together $\frac{\hbar}{2}$, the $m = \frac{1}{2}$ of the state, though neither part alone is definite.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Feynman–Hellmann: $\frac{dE_n}{d\lambda} = \langle\psi_n|\frac{dH}{d\lambda}|\psi_n\rangle$; to first order a small $\Delta H$ shifts a non-degenerate level by $\langle\psi_n^{(0)}|\Delta H|\psi_n^{(0)}\rangle$.
- Two angular momenta add to $J_i = J_i^{(1)}\otimes 1 + 1\otimes J_i^{(2)}$, an angular momentum on the tensor product.
- Spin–orbit coupling: the electron sees a magnetic field $\propto\vec L$; $\Delta H \propto \frac{1}{r}\frac{dV}{dr}\vec S\cdot\vec L$ with the Thomas factor $g - 1$; its size is about $\alpha^2$ of the binding energy.
- $\vec L\cdot\vec S = \frac{1}{2}(\vec J^2 - \vec L^2 - \vec S^2)$ is diagonal in states of definite $j$; $1\otimes\frac{1}{2} = \frac{3}{2}\oplus\frac{1}{2}$, the states follow by lowering and orthogonality, and the $n = 2$, $l = 1$ level splits into four states (up by $\frac{\hbar^2}{2}$) and two (down by $\hbar^2$), times the radial factor.
