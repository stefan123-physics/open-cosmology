The lecture is called "Dirac's bra and ket notation", but it covers more: two short theorems about Hermitian operators, unitary operators, the position and momentum bases where Dirac notation is at its best, and the definition of uncertainty (transcript pp. 1–22; notes chapter 3, eqs. (7.31)–(7.45); chapter 4, section 3; chapter 5, section 1). Zwiebach postpones diagonalisation to a later lecture to spread out the mathematics. In homework, students check the theorem of the last lecture with $2 \times 2$ matrices: if $(v, Tv) = 0$ for every $v$, then $T = 0$ on a complex space but not on a real one (transcript p. 1). (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example. Dirac's brackets are written here in prose, and formulas use the inner product $(u, v)$ of the notes, so that read-aloud can say them.)

## Hermitian operators: real eigenvalues

A Hermitian operator ($T^\dagger = T$) has real expectation values, $(v, Tv)$, which Dirac writes ⟨v|T|v⟩ (transcript pp. 1–2). Its eigenvalues are real too. Let $Tv = \lambda v$ with $v \ne 0$, and evaluate $(v, Tv)$ in two ways: once with the eigenvalue equation in the second entry, once by moving $T$ to the first entry, where the number comes out conjugated (transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator are real" tex="(v, Tv) = \lambda\,(v, v), \qquad (v, Tv) = (Tv, v) = \lambda^*(v, v) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (\lambda - \lambda^*)(v, v) = 0" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (7.31)–(7.32)" />

Since $(v, v) \ne 0$, $\lambda = \lambda^*$.

## Hermitian operators: orthogonal eigenvectors

If $Tv_1 = \lambda_1v_1$ and $Tv_2 = \lambda_2v_2$ with $\lambda_1 \ne \lambda_2$, then $v_1$ and $v_2$ are orthogonal (transcript pp. 3–4). An eigenvalue may have a whole subspace of eigenvectors (**degeneracy**, which "makes life interesting" in quantum mechanics); the theorem then says that every eigenvector of one eigenvalue is orthogonal to every eigenvector of the other. The proof evaluates $(v_2, Tv_1)$ in two ways, using that the eigenvalues are already known to be real (transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Orthogonality of eigenvectors" tex="(v_2, Tv_1) = \lambda_1(v_2, v_1), \qquad (v_2, Tv_1) = (Tv_2, v_1) = \lambda_2(v_2, v_1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)(v_1, v_2) = 0" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (7.34)–(7.36)" />

## Unitary operators

An operator $S$ is **unitary** if it does not change the length of any vector (mathematicians call this an isometry; the notes also require it to be surjective, so that the definition works in infinite dimensions) (transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="Unitary operator" tex="|Su| = |u| \quad \text{for all } u" cite="reading2" at="eq. (7.37)" />

A simple example is $\lambda I$ with $|\lambda| = 1$, a pure phase. A unitary operator kills only the zero vector, since every other vector has a length to keep, so it is invertible (transcript pp. 5–6). Squaring the definition and moving one $S$ across gives $(u, (S^\dagger S - I)u) = 0$ for every $u$, and the theorem of the last lecture turns this into (transcript p. 6)

<Formula label="The adjoint is the inverse" tex="S^\dagger S = I" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (7.39)–(7.40)" />

(Zwiebach assumes, as holds in the course's examples, that the left inverse is also a right inverse in infinite dimensions.) Unitary operators preserve not just lengths but all inner products, $(Su, Sv) = (u, S^\dagger Sv) = (u, v)$ (transcript p. 7). So if $e_1, \dots, e_n$ is an orthonormal basis and $U$ is unitary (Zwiebach switches here to the usual letter $U$, transcript p. 6), $f_i = Ue_i$ is another one (transcript p. 7):

<Formula label="A unitary operator maps orthonormal bases to orthonormal bases" tex="(f_i, f_j) = (Ue_i, Ue_j) = (e_i, e_j) = \delta_{ij}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (7.43)" />

A curiosity: the matrix elements of $U$ in the new basis equal those in the old one, since $(f_k, Uf_i) = (Ue_k, Uf_i) = (e_k, f_i) = (e_k, Ue_i)$; Zwiebach leaves the reason as a puzzle (transcript p. 7; notes eq. (7.45)). A student asked whether any two orthonormal bases are related by a unitary operator; the answer is yes (transcript p. 8).

## Position states

Dirac notation is used by everyone for position and momentum, because there it prevents a real confusion (transcript p. 8). For each point $x$ of the line there is a **position state** |x⟩, a particle at $x$. The label $x$ is a real number, but the ket is a vector in an infinite-dimensional complex vector space. So |ax⟩, a particle at the point $ax$, has nothing to do with $a$|x⟩, a particle at $x$ with a different amplitude, and |−x⟩ is not −|x⟩ (transcript pp. 8–9; notes chapter 4, after eq. (3.87)). In three dimensions the label is an ordinary vector of space, and the ket is a vector of the state space. The basis is **non-denumerable**: its states cannot be listed one by one. The inner product is (transcript pp. 9–10)

<Formula label="Inner product of position states" tex="(x, y) = \delta(x - y)" cite="reading3" at="eq. (3.88)" />

so different positions are orthogonal, and position states cannot be normalised: they are idealisations. The identity is the integral over $x$ of |x⟩⟨x|, with no extra factor, as acting on a state |y⟩ confirms (transcript p. 10; notes eqs. (3.89)–(3.90)). The position operator is defined by $\hat x$|x⟩ = $x$|x⟩, so position states are its eigenstates (transcript p. 10; notes eq. (3.91)).

A state $\psi$ has the **wavefunction** (transcript pp. 10–11)

<Formula label="The wavefunction as a component" tex="\psi(x) = (x, \psi)" cite="reading3" at="eq. (3.94)" />

Inserting the identity shows that the state is the superposition of position states with weights $\psi(x)$: the wavefunction is the component of the state along each basis state (transcript p. 11; notes eq. (3.95)). Inserting it again gives the familiar overlap and matrix element (transcript pp. 11–12):

<Formula label="Overlap and position matrix element" tex="(\phi, \psi) = \int dx\,\phi^*(x)\,\psi(x), \qquad (\phi, \hat x\psi) = \int dx\,\phi^*(x)\,x\,\psi(x)" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (3.96)–(3.97)" />

## Momentum states

Momentum states |p⟩ work in exactly the same way: $(p', p) = \delta(p - p')$, the identity is the integral over $p$ of |p⟩⟨p|, and $\hat p$|p⟩ = $p$|p⟩ (transcript p. 12; notes eq. (3.98)). The physics enters only in how the two bases are related. A particle with momentum $p$ has a plane-wave wavefunction (transcript p. 12):

<Formula label="Position-momentum overlap" tex="(x, p) = \frac{e^{ipx/\hbar}}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}" cite="reading3" at="eq. (3.100)" />

with the normalisation chosen to match the delta functions. The component of a state along |p⟩ is then the Fourier transform of the wavefunction, the **momentum-space wavefunction** (transcript pp. 12–13):

<Formula label="Momentum-space wavefunction" tex="\tilde\psi(p) = (p, \psi) = \int dx\,\frac{e^{-ipx/\hbar}}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\,\psi(x)" cite="reading3" at="eq. (3.107)" />

Finally, what is $(x, \hat p\psi)$? Insert momentum states so that $\hat p$ acts on its eigenstates, note that $p$ times $(x, p)$ equals $(\hbar/i)\,d/dx$ of $(x, p)$, take the derivative outside the $p$ integral (nothing else there depends on $x$), and remove the identity (transcript pp. 13–14):

<Formula label="Momentum in the position representation" tex="(x, \hat p\psi) = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d}{dx}\,\psi(x)" cite="reading3" at="eq. (3.112)" />

This derives, rather than assumes, the rule $\hat p = (\hbar/i)\,d/dx$. The reverse exercise gives $i\hbar\,d\tilde\psi/dp$ for $\hat x$ in momentum space (notes eq. (3.113)). Asked what the basis vectors "look like", Zwiebach answered that they are delta functions; mathematicians who want to avoid them put the system on a large circle, where integrals become Fourier sums. Position and momentum are not bounded operators in the mathematicians' sense, but physicists know how to use them safely (transcript pp. 14–15).

## Uncertainty

When a state is an eigenstate of an observable, a measurement gives the eigenvalue with certainty. Otherwise the result is uncertain, and the uncertainty must refer to a state (transcript p. 16). For a Hermitian operator $A$ and a normalised state $\psi$, the expectation value is $\langle A\rangle = (\psi, A\psi)$, which is real. The uncertainty should be real, never negative, and zero exactly for eigenstates. A norm does all of this (transcript p. 17):

<Formula label="Definition of uncertainty" tex="\Delta A(\psi) = \left|\left(A - \langle A\rangle I\right)\psi\right|" cite="reading4" at="eq. (1.2)" />

If $\Delta A = 0$, the vector vanishes and $A\psi = \langle A\rangle\psi$: $\psi$ is an eigenstate, with eigenvalue $\langle A\rangle$. Conversely, in an eigenstate $A\psi = \lambda\psi$, the inner product with $\psi$ gives $\lambda = \langle A\rangle$, so the vector vanishes. The uncertainty is zero **if and only if** the state is an eigenstate; the step from zero uncertainty to an eigenstate uses that only the zero vector has zero length (transcript pp. 17–18). (The state must be normalised: rescaling it would rescale the uncertainty.)

Squaring, moving the Hermitian operator $A - \langle A\rangle I$ across and expanding gives the more familiar form (transcript pp. 18–19):

<Formula label="Uncertainty squared" tex="(\Delta A)^2 = \langle A^2\rangle - \langle A\rangle^2" cite="reading4" at="eq. (1.9)" />

Because the left side is not negative, $\langle A^2\rangle \ge \langle A\rangle^2$ always; the formula is a standard deviation, as in statistics (transcript p. 20).

**A picture** (transcript pp. 20–21). Unless $\psi$ is an eigenstate, $A\psi$ points in a different direction. Project $A\psi$ orthogonally onto the line through $\psi$, using the projector |ψ⟩⟨ψ|: the projection is $\langle A\rangle\psi$. What is left is orthogonal to $\psi$, as taking its inner product with $\psi$ confirms (notes eqs. (1.11)–(1.12), and the text after (1.13); transcript p. 21):

<Formula label="The orthogonal part" tex="A\psi - \langle A\rangle\psi = \psi_\perp, \qquad (\psi, \psi_\perp) = \langle A\rangle - \langle A\rangle = 0" cite="reading4" at="eq. (1.13)" />

and its length is exactly the uncertainty. To make the uncertainty vanish, $A\psi$ must lie along $\psi$. Zwiebach finds this picture suggestive, though he suspects it is not widely known.

The lecture ends by pointing at the uncertainty principle for two Hermitian operators $A$ and $B$. Uncertainties are norms, and the only inequality relating norms to an inner product is the Schwarz inequality; applying it to $f = (A - \langle A\rangle)\psi$ and $g = (B - \langle B\rangle)\psi$ is the next lecture (transcript p. 22).

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="Uncertainty of a two-state observable">
<Problem>
Let $A = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\psi = (3/5, 4/5)$. Find $\langle A\rangle$, $\Delta A$ from $\langle A^2\rangle - \langle A\rangle^2$, and check the result with the orthogonal part $\psi_\perp$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Normalisation and expectation value">
$(\psi, \psi) = 9/25 + 16/25 = 1$. Then $A\psi = (3/5, -4/5)$ and $\langle A\rangle = 9/25 - 16/25 = -7/25$.
</Step>
<Step title="Uncertainty from the formula">
$A^2 = I$, so $\langle A^2\rangle = 1$, and $(\Delta A)^2 = 1 - 49/625 = 576/625$: $\Delta A = 24/25$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with the picture">
$\langle A\rangle\psi = (-21/125, -28/125)$, so $\psi_\perp = A\psi - \langle A\rangle\psi = (96/125, -72/125)$. It is orthogonal to $\psi$: $(3 \times 96 - 4 \times 72)/625 = 0$. Its squared length is $(9216 + 5184)/15625 = 14400/15625$, and $\sqrt{14400}/125 = 120/125 = 24/25$, the same uncertainty.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Hermitian operators have real eigenvalues, and eigenvectors with different eigenvalues are orthogonal; degenerate eigenvalues have whole subspaces of eigenvectors.
- Unitary operators preserve lengths; then $S^\dagger S = I$, they preserve inner products and map orthonormal bases to orthonormal bases.
- Position states form a non-denumerable basis with $(x, y) = \delta(x - y)$; the label is a number, not a vector of the state space; $\psi(x) = (x, \psi)$ is the component of the state along |x⟩.
- With $(x, p) = e^{ipx/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$, the momentum-space wavefunction is the Fourier transform, and $(x, \hat p\psi) = (\hbar/i)\,d\psi/dx$ follows by inserting complete sets of states.
- The uncertainty $\Delta A(\psi) = |(A - \langle A\rangle I)\psi|$ vanishes exactly for eigenstates, satisfies $(\Delta A)^2 = \langle A^2\rangle - \langle A\rangle^2$, and is the length of the part of $A\psi$ orthogonal to $\psi$.
