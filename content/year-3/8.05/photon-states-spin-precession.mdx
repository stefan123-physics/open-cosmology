Half of this lecture applies coherent states to light, a first taste of quantum field theory; the other half starts the systematic study of two-state systems (transcript pp. 1–22; notes chapter 6, section 5.3; chapter 7, sections 2–3). Spin states are written $\chi_{z\pm}$ and $\chi_{n\pm}$ as on the spin one-half page. (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## One mode of the electromagnetic field

A photon is a quantum of the electromagnetic field, so describing photon states is doing quantum field theory, and the harmonic oscillator turns out to be central (transcript p. 1). Start from the field energy (transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Energy of the electromagnetic field" tex="E = \frac{1}{2}\int dV\,\epsilon_0\left(\vec E^2 + c^2\vec B^2\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.37)" />

Keep a single mode in a box of volume $V$, with $k = \omega/c$ and two functions of time $q(t)$ and $p(t)$ (transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="One field mode" tex="E_x(z, t) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\epsilon_0V}}\,\omega\,q(t)\sin kz, \qquad cB_y(z, t) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\epsilon_0V}}\,p(t)\cos kz" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.38)" />

The factor $\omega$ has no deep meaning; it is chosen so that, averaging $\sin^2$ and $\cos^2$ over the box to $\frac{1}{2}$, the energy comes out as (transcript pp. 3–4)

<Formula label="Energy of the mode" tex="E = \frac{1}{2}\left(p^2 + \omega^2q^2\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.39)" />

This is an oscillator **without a mass**, as it must be, since photons are massless. $p$ has units of $\sqrt{\text{energy}}$ and $q$ of time times $\sqrt{\text{energy}}$, so $pq$ has the units of $\hbar$ (transcript pp. 4–5). The bold step is to declare $\hat q$ and $\hat p$ operators with $[\hat q, \hat p] = i\hbar$ and this energy the Hamiltonian. A check, done in the homework: Maxwell's equations for $q(t)$ and $p(t)$ are the same as the Heisenberg equations of this Hamiltonian (transcript p. 5).

All the oscillator formulas then hold with $m = 1$ (transcript p. 6):

<Formula label="Photons as oscillator quanta" tex="\hat q = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2\omega}}\left(a + a^\dagger\right), \qquad H = \hbar\omega\left(\hat N + \frac{1}{2}\right)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (5.43)–(5.44)" />

A state with number $N$ has energy $N\hbar\omega$ above the ground state: $N$ photons. It took physicists about 30 years to understand this step (transcript pp. 6–7). In ordinary quantum mechanics $x$ and $p$ become operators; here $\hat q$ and $\hat p$ have nothing to do with position and momentum, being really the electric and magnetic fields: **in quantum field theory the fields become operators** (transcript p. 7). With the Heisenberg operators $a\,e^{-i\omega t}$ and $a^\dagger e^{i\omega t}$, the electric field becomes (transcript p. 7)

<Formula label="Electric field operator" tex="\hat E_x(z, t) = E_0\left(a\,e^{-i\omega t} + a^\dagger e^{i\omega t}\right)\sin kz, \qquad E_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar\omega}{\epsilon_0V}}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.46)" />

## Which states look like classical waves?

In a state with exactly $n$ photons, the expectation value of $\hat E_x$ is **zero**: $a$ and $a^\dagger$ change the number, so their expectation values in a number state vanish (transcript p. 8). Energy eigenstates are unintuitive and nothing in them changes in time. In a coherent state, $a\psi_\alpha = \alpha\psi_\alpha$ gives, with $\alpha = |\alpha|e^{i\theta}$ (transcript pp. 8–9),

<Formula label="Mean field in a coherent state" tex="\langle\hat E_x\rangle = 2E_0\,|\alpha|\cos(\omega t - \theta)\,\sin kz" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.50)" />

the standing wave of classical electromagnetism. So every classical wave met in earlier courses is, quantum mechanically, a coherent state of the field, and so is the light of a laser, with the strong number–phase uncertainty of the last lecture (transcript p. 9). Its energy is $\hbar\omega(|\alpha|^2 + \frac{1}{2})$, so the coherent state contains $|\alpha|^2$ photons on average (transcript p. 10; notes eq. (5.51)). Many modes, their commutators and so on belong to a quantum field theory course.

**Zero-point energy.** As $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ cannot both vanish, the electric and magnetic fields of a mode cannot both be zero, so even the vacuum has $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$ in every mode (transcript pp. 10–11). Summed over all modes this is infinite. The infinity is not physical and can be handled; what remains can depend on the geometry, as in **Casimir energies**, studied at MIT by Kardar and Jaffe. Zero-point energies cause no trouble unless gravity is involved, because gravity responds to every bit of energy (transcript p. 11). Asked about uncountably many modes, Zwiebach answered that one works with integrals, the states being delta-function normalised like position states, and that to avoid conceptual trouble one can start in a big box and let it grow (transcript pp. 11–12).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Zero-point energy and the cosmological constant">
Zwiebach's remark that only gravity notices zero-point energies is the starting point of one of the deepest puzzles in cosmology. The vacuum of a quantum field is not empty: every mode keeps its $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$. Energy of the vacuum itself acts in Einstein's equations like a cosmological constant, 8.286 Lecture 18 discusses vacuum energy and why quantum field theory gives no reason for it to vanish, and Lecture 20 shows that summing the zero-point energies up to a natural cutoff gives a value enormously larger than the one observed.
</Callout>

## Spin precession

The rest of the lecture begins two-state systems, starting with spin, the system already familiar (transcript p. 12). Any two-state system can be thought of mathematically as a spin in a magnetic field, whether it describes an electron shared between two atoms or the ammonia molecule (transcript pp. 12–13). A charged spinning object has a magnetic moment; classically $\vec\mu = \frac{q}{2m}\vec S$, and quantum mechanically there is an extra **g factor**, with $\vec S$ an operator (transcript p. 13; notes eqs. (2.1)–(2.2)). For the electron $g = 2$, predicted by Dirac's equation. Even the neutral neutron has a magnetic moment, from the charges of its three quarks (transcript pp. 13–14). Everything is collected into one constant $\gamma$ (transcript p. 14):

<Formula label="Spin in a magnetic field" tex="\vec\mu = \gamma\vec S, \qquad H_S = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B = -\gamma\,\vec B\cdot\vec S" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (2.7)–(2.8)" />

For $\vec B = B\hat z$, $U(t, 0) = \exp\left(-\frac{i}{\hbar}(-\gamma Bt)S_z\right)$. The rotation operator $\exp(-i\alpha S_n/\hbar)$, met in the homework, rotates spin states by the angle $\alpha$ about $\hat n$; so $U$ should rotate them by $-\gamma Bt$ about $z$ (transcript pp. 14–15; notes eqs. (2.10)–(2.11)). Check it on a general spin state with polar angles $\theta_0$ and $\phi_0$ (transcript pp. 16–17):

<Formula label="Precession of a spin state" tex="\psi(t) = e^{i\gamma Bt/2}\left(\cos\frac{\theta_0}{2}\,\chi_{z+} + \sin\frac{\theta_0}{2}\,e^{i(\phi_0 - \gamma Bt)}\chi_{z-}\right)" cite="reading3" at="eq. (2.15)" />

Up to an irrelevant phase this is the spin state with $\theta = \theta_0$ and $\phi = \phi_0 - \gamma Bt$: the spin **precesses** about the field (transcript p. 17).

Classically the same happens (transcript pp. 17–18). A magnetic moment in a field feels the torque $\vec\mu\times\vec B$, which changes the angular momentum:

<Formula label="Classical precession" tex="\frac{d\vec S}{dt} = \vec\mu\times\vec B = -\gamma\vec B\times\vec S" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (2.17)–(2.19)" />

This has the form $d\vec x/dt = \vec\omega\times\vec x$ of a vector rotating about $\vec\omega$ with angular speed $|\vec\omega|$, so the **Larmor frequency** is (transcript pp. 18–19)

<Formula label="Larmor frequency" tex="\vec\omega_L = -\gamma\vec B, \qquad H_S = \vec\omega_L\cdot\vec S" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (2.20)–(2.22)" />

Quantum and classical derivations agree, which is no coincidence: classical variables became operators without changing the physics. Whenever a spin Hamiltonian is some vector dotted into $\vec S$, that vector is the angular velocity of precession (transcript pp. 18–19; notes eq. (2.26)).

## The general two-state system

A two-state system has two **basis** states, so its Hamiltonian is a $2 \times 2$ Hermitian matrix, described by four real numbers (transcript pp. 19–20):

<Formula label="General two-state Hamiltonian" tex="H = \begin{pmatrix}g_0 + g_3 & g_1 - ig_2\\ g_1 + ig_2 & g_0 - g_3\end{pmatrix} = g_0I + g_1\sigma_1 + g_2\sigma_2 + g_3\sigma_3 = g_0I + \vec g\cdot\vec\sigma" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (3.27)–(3.29)" />

because the identity and the Pauli matrices form a basis of the Hermitian $2 \times 2$ matrices. Write $\vec g = g\hat n$ with $g = |\vec g|$. The eigenvectors of $\hat n\cdot\vec\sigma$ are already known, the spin states $\chi_{n\pm}$ with eigenvalues $\pm 1$, so no diagonalisation is needed (transcript pp. 20–21):

<Formula label="Spectrum" tex="H\chi_{n\pm} = (g_0 \pm g)\,\chi_{n\pm}" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (3.33)–(3.34)" />

The splitting between the two levels is $2g$. For time evolution, $\vec\sigma = 2\vec S/\hbar$ turns $\vec g\cdot\vec\sigma$ into $\frac{2}{\hbar}\vec g\cdot\vec S$, so (transcript pp. 21–22; notes eq. (3.35))

<Formula label="Precession of a general two-state system" tex="\vec\omega_L = \frac{2\vec g}{\hbar}" cite="reading3" at="eq. (3.36)" />

The $g_0$ term only multiplies every state by the same phase $e^{-ig_0t/\hbar}$ and does nothing physical. So the dynamics of any two-state system is **precession**: picture the state as a spin direction and let it rotate about $\vec g$ at angular speed $2g/\hbar$. The ammonia molecule and NMR come next (transcript p. 22).

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="A two-state Hamiltonian, and a laser mode">
<Problem>
(a) In some unit of energy $\varepsilon$, $H = \varepsilon\begin{pmatrix}3 & 1 - i\\ 1 + i & 1\end{pmatrix}$. Find $g_0$, $\vec g$, the two energies, the splitting and the precession axis and rate. (b) A mode in a coherent state has a mean electric field of amplitude $20\,E_0$. How many photons does it contain on average, and what is the spread?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Read off the g's">
$g_0 \pm g_3$ are the diagonal entries, so $g_0 = 2\varepsilon$ and $g_3 = \varepsilon$. The off-diagonal entry $g_1 - ig_2 = (1 - i)\varepsilon$ gives $g_1 = g_2 = \varepsilon$. So $\vec g = \varepsilon(1, 1, 1)$, $g = \sqrt{3}\,\varepsilon$, and $\hat n = (1, 1, 1)/\sqrt{3}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Energies and precession">
The energies are $g_0 \pm g = (2 \pm \sqrt{3})\varepsilon$, about $3.73\varepsilon$ and $0.27\varepsilon$, with splitting $2\sqrt{3}\,\varepsilon \approx 3.46\varepsilon$. States precess about the diagonal direction $(1, 1, 1)/\sqrt{3}$ with angular speed $2\sqrt{3}\,\varepsilon/\hbar$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Photon number">
The amplitude is $2E_0|\alpha| = 20\,E_0$, so $|\alpha| = 10$: on average $|\alpha|^2 = 100$ photons, with spread $\Delta N = |\alpha| = 10$ (notes chapter 6, eq. (4.99)), a 10% uncertainty in the number.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- One field mode has energy $\frac{1}{2}(p^2 + \omega^2q^2)$, an oscillator with $m = 1$ whose "position" and "momentum" are the electric and magnetic field amplitudes; quantised, $H = \hbar\omega(\hat N + \frac{1}{2})$ and $\hat N$ counts photons.
- The field operator has zero mean in number states; coherent states give classical standing waves, $\langle\hat E_x\rangle = 2E_0|\alpha|\cos(\omega t - \theta)\sin kz$, with $|\alpha|^2$ photons on average.
- Every mode keeps a zero-point energy $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$, harmless except to gravity.
- A spin in a magnetic field, $H = -\gamma\vec B\cdot\vec S = \vec\omega_L\cdot\vec S$, precesses with $\vec\omega_L = -\gamma\vec B$, as classical torque also predicts.
- Any two-state Hamiltonian is $g_0I + \vec g\cdot\vec\sigma$: energies $g_0 \pm |\vec g|$ with eigenstates $\chi_{n\pm}$, and states precess about $\vec g$ with $\vec\omega_L = 2\vec g/\hbar$.
