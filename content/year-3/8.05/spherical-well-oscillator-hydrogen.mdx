The last lecture drew the spectral diagram of a central potential, with a column of single lines above each $l$. This one fills it in for three problems and finds three very different patterns: none at all for the spherical well, regular coincidences for the 3D oscillator, and an even larger degeneracy for hydrogen, which leads to the Runge–Lenz vector (transcript pp. 1–25; notes chapter 9, sections 5.2, 6 and 7). Electric charges are in Gaussian units, so $e^2$ stands for $e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0$. (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Why some wells have no bound states

Before the main examples, a remark answered in the homework: any attractive potential in one dimension has a bound state, but a finite spherical well in three dimensions may have none (transcript p. 3). The reason is that $u$ must vanish at the origin even for $l = 0$. The radial problem is like a one-dimensional well with an infinite wall at $r = 0$, and a wavefunction that starts at zero needs enough room inside the well to turn over and decay; a shallow or narrow well does not give it (transcript p. 4). A delta-function potential in three dimensions is rather singular: it has infinitely many bound states, falling all the way to minus infinite energy (transcript p. 4).

## The infinite spherical well

Take $V = 0$ for $r < a$ and $V = \infty$ beyond, a hollow cavity in which a particle moves freely (transcript p. 3). Inside, with $\rho = kr$ and $k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2$, the radial equation is the free one (transcript p. 5). For $l = 0$ it is just $u'' = -u$, so $u = A\sin\rho + B\cos\rho$; $u \propto r$ near the origin removes the cosine, and $u(a) = 0$ quantises $k$ (transcript pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="Spherical well, l = 0" tex="u_{n,0}(r) = \sin\frac{n\pi r}{a}, \qquad k_na = n\pi, \qquad E_{n,0} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\left(n\pi\right)^2" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (5.41)–(5.44)" />

$\hbar^2/2ma^2$ is the natural energy of a box of size $a$, so define unit-free energies $\epsilon_{n,l} = E_{n,l}/(\hbar^2/2ma^2)$: then $\epsilon_{1,0} \approx 9.870$, $\epsilon_{2,0} \approx 39.48$, $\epsilon_{3,0} \approx 88.83$ (transcript p. 6; notes eq. (5.45)). For general $l$, $u = rj_l(kr)$ and the wall requires $j_l(ka) = 0$. With $z_{n,l}$ the $n$-th non-trivial zero of $j_l$ (the zero at the origin is not counted), $k_{n,l}a = z_{n,l}$ and (transcript pp. 6–7)

<Formula label="Spherical well, any l" tex="E_{n,l} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\,z_{n,l}^2, \qquad \epsilon_{n,l} = z_{n,l}^2, \qquad j_l\left(z_{n,l}\right) = 0" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (5.47)–(5.49)" />

For $l = 1$, $j_1(\rho) = \frac{\sin\rho}{\rho^2} - \frac{\cos\rho}{\rho}$ vanishes where $\tan\rho = \rho$, which must be solved numerically; tables of zeros exist for higher $l$ (transcript p. 6; notes eq. (5.46)). The values (transcript pp. 7–8; notes eqs. (5.50)–(5.51)):

| $l$ | $z_{1,l}$ | $z_{2,l}$ | $z_{3,l}$ | $\epsilon_{1,l}$ | $\epsilon_{2,l}$ | $\epsilon_{3,l}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 3.1416 | 6.2832 | 9.4248 | 9.870 | 39.48 | 88.83 |
| 1 | 4.4934 | 7.7252 | 10.904 | 20.19 | 59.68 | 118.9 |
| 2 | 5.7634 | 9.095 | | 33.22 | 82.72 | |
| 3 | 6.9879 | 10.417 | | 48.83 | 108.5 | |

Placed on the diagram, the levels show no pattern whatsoever: the lowest line rises with $l$ as it must, but no line in one column ever matches one in another (transcript p. 8; notes Figure 5). Zwiebach expected, without proof, that no two zeros of different $j_l$ ever coincide. That is fine: a round box has no symmetry beyond rotations, so there is nothing that could explain a coincidence (transcript pp. 8–9).

## The 3D oscillator: one particle, a tensor product

The isotropic oscillator has $V = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2(x^2 + y^2 + z^2) = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2r^2$ and (transcript p. 9)

<Formula label="3D oscillator" tex="H = \hbar\omega\left(N_1 + N_2 + N_3 + \frac{3}{2}\right) = \hbar\omega\left(N + \frac{3}{2}\right)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (6.52)–(6.53)" />

Its states are built by piling $a_x^\dagger$, $a_y^\dagger$, $a_z^\dagger$ on the vacuum, so the state space is a tensor product, $\mathcal{H}_{3D} = \mathcal{H}_x\otimes\mathcal{H}_y\otimes\mathcal{H}_z$, with basis states $(a_x^\dagger)^{n_x}(a_y^\dagger)^{n_y}(a_z^\dagger)^{n_z}|0\rangle$ (transcript pp. 9–10; notes eqs. (6.54)–(6.58)). There is only **one** particle: the tensor product describes what it does in three independent directions, and tensor products appear whenever a system has several independent sets of attributes. A single basis state has a wavefunction that factorises into $x$, $y$ and $z$ parts; a superposition of them generally does not, the analogue of entanglement (transcript pp. 10–11; notes p. 29).

## The 3D oscillator: the diagram

The states at each level must organise into angular momentum multiplets, using two rules: no $l$ can appear twice at one energy (the radial spectrum is non-degenerate), and the lowest line rises with $l$ (transcript pp. 11–15).

- $N = 0$, $E = \frac{3}{2}\hbar\omega$: one state, so $l = 0$. A student's explicit argument also works, but the count alone settles it: $l = 1$ would need three states (transcript pp. 11–12).
- $N = 1$, $E = \frac{5}{2}\hbar\omega$: three states, $a_x^\dagger|0\rangle$, $a_y^\dagger|0\rangle$, $a_z^\dagger|0\rangle$. Three $l = 0$ lines at one energy are forbidden, so this is one $l = 1$ multiplet (transcript p. 12).
- $N = 2$, $E = \frac{7}{2}\hbar\omega$: six states. Two $l = 1$ multiplets are forbidden, so $l = 2$ plus $l = 0$ (transcript p. 13). For the first time lines in different columns match: $l = 0$ and $l = 2$ at the same energy, which needs an explanation (transcript pp. 13–14).
- $N = 3$, $E = \frac{9}{2}\hbar\omega$: ten states, either $l = 4$ plus $l = 0$ or $l = 3$ plus $l = 1$. The first would put the lowest $l = 4$ line below the lowest $l = 3$ one, so it is $l = 3$ plus $l = 1$ (transcript pp. 14–15; notes eq. (6.65)).

The six states at $N = 2$ make a six-dimensional space that is the **direct sum** of a five-dimensional and a one-dimensional one, not a tensor product, which would have $5 \times 1 = 5$ dimensions (transcript p. 14; notes eq. (6.63)). Counting the solutions of $n_x + n_y + n_z = N$ row by row on a triangle of lattice points gives the number of states $D(N)$ (the notes write #(N)) (transcript p. 15)

<Formula label="Number of states at level N" tex="D(N) = 1 + 2 + \dots + (N + 1) = \frac{(N+1)(N+2)}{2}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (6.67)" />

So $N = 4$ has 15 states, which must be $l = 4, 2, 0$, and $N = 5$ has 21, which are $l = 5, 3, 1$: in general $l = N, N - 2, \dots$, jumping by two (transcript pp. 15–16; notes eq. (6.68) and Figure 7).

## Why l jumps by two

With $a_R = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_x - ia_y)$ and $a_L = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(a_x + ia_y)$ as for the 2D oscillator, $L_z = \hbar(N_R - N_L)$, and $a_z$ carries no $L_z$. This time it is the real angular momentum of the particle (transcript p. 16; notes eqs. (6.69)–(6.70)). At $N = 1$ the states $a_R^\dagger|0\rangle$, $a_z^\dagger|0\rangle$, $a_L^\dagger|0\rangle$ have $L_z = \hbar, 0, -\hbar$: the $l = 1$ multiplet in full. At level $N$, $(a_R^\dagger)^N|0\rangle$ has the largest $L_z$, $N\hbar$, so the top multiplet is $l = N$ (transcript p. 17). One unit lower there is a single state, $(a_R^\dagger)^{N-1}a_z^\dagger|0\rangle$; two units lower there are two (transcript pp. 17–18):

<Formula label="States below the top" tex="L_z = (N-2)\hbar:\quad (a_R^\dagger)^{N-2}(a_z^\dagger)^2|0\rangle, \quad (a_R^\dagger)^{N-1}a_L^\dagger|0\rangle" cite="reading2" at="eq. (6.72)" />

One combination continues the $l = N$ multiplet; the other must start a new multiplet, with $l = N - 2$. That is the jump of two (transcript p. 18). The coincidences call for operators that commute with $H$ but change $l$. Operators like $a_x^\dagger a_y$, which remove one quantum and add another, cannot change the energy; there are many such operators, and they generate a hidden symmetry, left as an exercise (transcript p. 19). The notes name one of them, $K = a_R^\dagger a_L$, which takes the top state of the $l = N - 2$ multiplet to the top state of the $l = N$ one (notes eq. (6.74)).

## Hydrogen: scales and spectrum

For hydrogen, $H = \frac{p^2}{2m} - \frac{e^2}{r}$, with $m$ the reduced mass, close to the electron mass (transcript p. 19). Its length scale follows from units alone: with $p \sim \hbar/a_0$, set the kinetic energy equal to the potential energy (transcript pp. 19–20):

<Formula label="Bohr radius" tex="\frac{\hbar^2}{ma_0^2} = \frac{e^2}{a_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{me^2} \approx 0.529\ \text{Å}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (7.76)" />

A weaker attraction, smaller $e^2$, gives a larger atom, as it should (transcript p. 20). The energy scale is $e^2/a_0$, and $e^2/2a_0 \approx 13.6$ eV is the binding energy of the ground state (transcript p. 20; notes p. 34). The ground state can be found elegantly by factorising the Hamiltonian, as a constant plus $\frac{1}{2m}\sum_k(p_k + i\beta x_k/r)(p_k - i\beta x_k/r)$ for suitable constants; Zwiebach said a colleague, Professor Jackiw, had shown him this and left it for the homework (transcript pp. 20–21; notes eqs. (7.79)–(7.80)).

The spectrum is highly degenerate. Label the solutions for each $l$ by $\nu = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ and define $n = \nu + l + 1$. Then (transcript p. 21)

<Formula label="Hydrogen spectrum" tex="E = -\frac{e^2}{2a_0}\,\frac{1}{n^2}, \qquad n = \nu + l + 1, \qquad l = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1" cite="reading1" at="p. 21; notes eqs. (7.81)–(7.82) with ν counted from 1" />

The first $l = 1$ line sits level with the second $l = 0$ line, and so on: every level $n$ holds all $l$ from 0 to $n - 1$ (transcript p. 21; notes Figure 8). (The notes count $\nu$ from 1 and define $n = \nu + l$; the lecture counts from 0, which gives the same $n$.) Three diagrams, three different patterns; the oscillator's is not hard to understand, but hydrogen's needs a new conserved quantity (transcript pp. 21–22).

## The Runge–Lenz vector

Classically, for $H = \frac{p^2}{2m} + V(r)$, the force is $-V'(r)\,\vec r/r$, and the angular momentum is conserved, since a central force exerts no torque (transcript p. 22; notes eqs. (7.84)–(7.86)). (In the lecture "the angular momentum is 0" was said where its rate of change is meant.) Something more is conserved. A few lines of algebra with $d\vec p/dt$ and the constant $\vec L$ give (transcript p. 22; notes eqs. (7.87)–(7.89))

<Formula label="Time derivative of p × L" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\vec p\times\vec L\right) = mV'(r)\,r^2\,\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\vec r}{r}\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (7.89)" />

This is a total derivative only if $V'(r)r^2$ is constant, that is $V = -e^2/r$ (up to a constant): exactly the inverse-square force of hydrogen and of planetary orbits (transcript p. 23; notes eqs. (7.90)–(7.93)). Then $\vec p\times\vec L - me^2\vec r/r$ is conserved, and dividing by $me^2$ gives a vector without units (transcript p. 23):

<Formula label="Runge–Lenz vector" tex="\vec R = \frac{1}{me^2}\,\vec p\times\vec L - \frac{\vec r}{r}, \qquad \frac{d\vec R}{dt} = 0" cite="reading2" at="eq. (7.95)" />

It was discovered and forgotten two or three times before its role was appreciated (transcript p. 23). For a **circular** orbit, $\vec p\times\vec L$ points outward and, since $mv^2/r = e^2/r^2$, has length $me^2$, so the two terms cancel and $\vec R = 0$: a rotating radial vector could not have been conserved anyway (transcript pp. 23–24; notes eq. (7.96)). For an **ellipse** it is not zero. At the point farthest from the focus, both terms are along the major axis, so $\vec R$ must lie along it; at the point above the focus, $\vec p$ is no longer perpendicular to the radius and $\vec p\times\vec L$ tilts away from the far end, towards the near end of the orbit, which fixes the direction: $\vec R$ lies along the major axis, pointing from the far end towards the focus (transcript p. 24; notes p. 37). Its length is the **eccentricity** of the orbit (transcript pp. 24–25; notes eqs. (7.97)–(7.99)). In general relativity the potential is not exactly $1/r$, orbits precess, and $\vec R$ is not conserved (transcript p. 24). Quantum mechanically a Hermitian version of $\vec R$ commutes with the hydrogen Hamiltonian, a homework result that will explain the degeneracy (transcript p. 23; notes eqs. (7.100)–(7.101)).

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="A nanometre cavity, an oscillator level and hydrogen's third shell">
<Problem>
(a) An electron is trapped in a spherical cavity of radius $a = 1$ nm. Using $\hbar^2/2m_e = 3.81$ eV Å², find its two lowest energy levels and their degeneracies. (b) How many states does the 3D oscillator have at $N = 6$, and which multiplets are they? (c) For hydrogen at $n = 3$, find the energy, the allowed $l$ with their $\nu$, and the number of states.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The cavity">
$\hbar^2/2m_ea^2 = 3.81/100 = 0.0381$ eV. The lowest level is $\epsilon_{1,0} = 9.870$: $E = 0.376$ eV, a single state. Next comes $\epsilon_{1,1} = 20.19$: $E = 0.769$ eV, an $l = 1$ multiplet of 3 states.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) N = 6">
$D(6) = 7 \times 8/2 = 28$ states. With $l = 6, 4, 2, 0$: $13 + 9 + 5 + 1 = 28$. The energy is $\hbar\omega(6 + \frac{3}{2}) = 7.5\,\hbar\omega$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Hydrogen, n = 3">
$E = -13.6/9 \approx -1.51$ eV. $l = 0$ with $\nu = 2$, $l = 1$ with $\nu = 1$, $l = 2$ with $\nu = 0$; states $1 + 3 + 5 = 9 = n^2$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Infinite spherical well: $E_{n,l} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2}z_{n,l}^2$ with $z_{n,l}$ the zeros of $j_l$; no coincidences between columns.
- 3D isotropic oscillator: the state space is $\mathcal{H}_x\otimes\mathcal{H}_y\otimes\mathcal{H}_z$ for one particle; level $N$ has $\frac{(N+1)(N+2)}{2}$ states in multiplets $l = N, N - 2, \dots$; the jump of two follows from counting $L_z$ states with $a_R$, $a_L$, $a_z$.
- Hydrogen: $a_0 = \hbar^2/me^2 \approx 0.529$ Å, $E = -\frac{e^2}{2a_0}\frac{1}{n^2}$ with $n = \nu + l + 1$, $l \le n - 1$: $n^2$ states per level.
- The Runge–Lenz vector $\vec R = \vec p\times\vec L/me^2 - \vec r/r$ is conserved only for $1/r$ potentials; it points along the major axis with length equal to the eccentricity, and its quantum version explains hydrogen's degeneracy.
