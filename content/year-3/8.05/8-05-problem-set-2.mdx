## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Square well with a delta function" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A particle of mass $m$ is in the infinite square well $0 \le x \le a$. In the middle of the well stands a repulsive delta function:

<Formula label="The barrier in the middle of the well" tex="V(x) = V_0\,a\,\delta\left(x - \frac{a}{2}\right), \qquad V_0 > 0, \qquad \gamma \equiv \frac{V_0}{\hbar^2/(ma^2)} \gg 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1, eqs. (1) and (2)" />

Here $V_0$ is an energy, and it is large compared with the natural energy $\hbar^2/(ma^2)$ of the well: the pure number $\gamma$ is large. The delta function is a barrier between the left half and the right half. As $V_0$ grows without limit, something singular can happen.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Inside each half the particle is free, so write $\psi = A\sin kx$ on the left, with $E = \hbar^2k^2/(2m)$. Show that the energy eigenstates are of two kinds: those that vanish at the middle, which do not feel the barrier at all, and those that do not vanish there, for which $k$ obeys a condition of the form $\tan\frac{ka}{2} = \dots$ Find the right side (our box). Use the letters `k`, `a` and `gam`.

<Hint>

On the right write $\psi = B\sin k(a - x)$, which vanishes at the wall $x = a$. The wavefunction is continuous at $x = a/2$. What does that say about $A$ and $B$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The slope is not continuous. Integrate the Schrödinger equation from just left of $a/2$ to just right of it: the delta function leaves $\psi'(a/2^+) - \psi'(a/2^-) = \frac{2mV_0a}{\hbar^2}\psi(a/2)$. Write the factor with $\gamma$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-cond" label="$\tan\dfrac{ka}{2} =$" expr="-k*a/gam"
  vars={{ k: [0.5, 3], a: [0.5, 2], gam: [5, 60] }}
  names={{ gam: "\\gamma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "k*a/gam", hint: "This is the condition for an attractive delta function, with −V₀ in the place of V₀. At a repulsive one the slope jumps the other way." },
    { expr: "-k*a/(2*gam)", hint: "This is what follows if the slope changes by k cos(ka/2). It changes from +k cos(ka/2) to −k cos(ka/2): twice as much." },
    { expr: "-2*k*a/gam", hint: "This follows from a jump of the slope of (γ/a)ψ. Integrating the Schrödinger equation across the delta function gives (2γ/a)ψ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Away from the middle the particle is free. The delta function only fixes how the two halves are joined: $\psi$ is continuous, and its slope jumps by an amount set by $\psi(a/2)$.

<Step title="The two halves">

With $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$, the solutions that vanish at the walls are

<Formula label="The wavefunction in the two halves" tex="\psi(x) = A\sin kx \quad \left(0 \le x \le \tfrac{a}{2}\right), \qquad \psi(x) = B\sin k(a - x) \quad \left(\tfrac{a}{2} \le x \le a\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Continuity at the middle demands $(A - B)\sin\frac{ka}{2} = 0$. So either $\sin\frac{ka}{2} = 0$ or $A = B$.

</Step>

<Step title="The jump of the slope">

Integrate $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + V_0a\,\delta\left(x - \frac{a}{2}\right)\psi = E\psi$ over a small interval around $a/2$. The right side gives nothing as the interval shrinks, because $\psi$ is bounded, and

<Formula label="Jump of the slope at the barrier" tex="\psi'\left(\tfrac{a}{2}^+\right) - \psi'\left(\tfrac{a}{2}^-\right) = \frac{2mV_0a}{\hbar^2}\,\psi\left(\tfrac{a}{2}\right) = \frac{2\gamma}{a}\,\psi\left(\tfrac{a}{2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is the rule of the lesson: at a delta function the slope may jump (notes eq. (4.2)).

</Step>

<Step title="States that vanish at the middle">

If $\sin\frac{ka}{2} = 0$, then $\psi(a/2) = 0$ and the slope does not jump: $Ak\cos\frac{ka}{2} = -Bk\cos\frac{ka}{2}$, so $B = -A$ and $\psi = A\sin kx$ in the whole well, with $ka = 2\pi, 4\pi, \dots$ These are the states of the plain well with a node at the middle. A delta function at a node does nothing, whatever its strength.

</Step>

<Step title="States that do not vanish at the middle">

If $A = B$, the wavefunction is symmetric about the middle. Its slope is $Ak\cos\frac{ka}{2}$ just left of the middle and $-Ak\cos\frac{ka}{2}$ just right of it, so the jump condition reads $-2Ak\cos\frac{ka}{2} = \frac{2\gamma}{a}A\sin\frac{ka}{2}$, or

<Formula label="Condition for the states that feel the barrier" tex="\tan\frac{ka}{2} = -\frac{ka}{\gamma}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Without a barrier ($\gamma \to 0$) the right side is infinite in size, so $\frac{ka}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}, \dots$: the symmetric states $\sin\frac{\pi x}{a}, \sin\frac{3\pi x}{a}, \dots$ of the plain well. With a barrier that cannot be crossed ($\gamma \to \infty$) the right side vanishes, so $\frac{ka}{2} = \pi, 2\pi, \dots$: each half holds the states of a well of width $a/2$. Both limits are as they must be. The division into two kinds of state is the lesson's theorem for an even potential, with the middle of the well as the origin.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find the energy $E_1$ of the ground state for large $\gamma$: keep the correction of order $1/\gamma$ and drop higher orders. Type it as a multiple of $\frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2}$ times a bracket of the form $(1 + \text{number}/\gamma)$, with the letters `hbar`, `m`, `a` and `gam`.

<Hint>

The ground state has no node, so it is one of the states that do not vanish at the middle: the one with the smallest $k$. Where does the first solution of the condition of part (a) lie when the right side is small and negative?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $\frac{ka}{2} = \pi - s$ with $s$ small. Then $\tan\frac{ka}{2} = -\tan s \approx -s$. Solve for $s$ to first order in $1/\gamma$, then square $k$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-gs" label="$E_1 \approx$" expr="2*pi^2*hbar^2/(m*a^2)*(1 - 4/gam)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 2], gam: [20, 200] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", gam: "\\gamma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*pi^2*hbar^2/(m*a^2)*(1 - 2/gam)", hint: "The factor (1 − 2/γ) is the change of k. The energy goes as k squared." },
    { expr: "2*pi^2*hbar^2/(m*a^2)*(1 + 4/gam)", hint: "This lies above the energy of a well of width a/2. A barrier that lets the wavefunction leak a little into the other half gives the particle more room, which lowers the energy." },
    { expr: "pi^2*hbar^2/(2*m*a^2)", hint: "That is the ground state of the well without a barrier. A strong barrier pushes the wavefunction out of the middle: the state is close to the lowest state of a well of width a/2." },
    { expr: "2*pi^2*hbar^2/(m*a^2)", hint: "That is the limit of an infinite barrier. Keep the first correction, of order 1/γ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For large $\gamma$ the right side of the condition is small, so $\frac{ka}{2}$ sits just below a zero of the tangent. The lowest such state is the ground state.

<Step title="Which solution is the ground state">

Write $\xi \equiv \frac{ka}{2}$, so that the condition is $\tan\xi = -2\xi/\gamma$. The right side is negative. The tangent is positive for $0 < \xi < \frac{\pi}{2}$ and negative for $\frac{\pi}{2} < \xi < \pi$, so the first solution lies between $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and $\pi$. It is below the first state of the other kind, which has $\xi = \pi$. It is the ground state, and it has no node, as the node theorem demands.

</Step>

<Step title="Expand near the zero of the tangent">

Put $\xi = \pi - s$ with $s$ small. Then $\tan\xi = -\tan s = -s + \dots$, and the condition becomes $s = 2(\pi - s)/\gamma$. To first order in $1/\gamma$ this is $s = 2\pi/\gamma$:

<Formula label="Wavenumber of the ground state" tex="\frac{ka}{2} = \pi\left(1 - \frac{2}{\gamma}\right) + \dots" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The energy">

<Formula label="Ground-state energy for a strong barrier" tex="E_1 = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} = \frac{2\pi^2\hbar^2}{ma^2}\left(1 - \frac{2}{\gamma}\right)^2 + \dots = \frac{2\pi^2\hbar^2}{ma^2}\left(1 - \frac{4}{\gamma}\right) + \dots" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result with numbers">

Take $\gamma = 50$ (our choice). Solving $\tan\frac{ka}{2} = -\frac{ka}{50}$ numerically gives $ka = 6.0426$, so the exact energy is $18.257\,\frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2}$. The formula gives $2\pi^2(1 - 4/50) = 18.160$ in the same unit: the two differ by half a percent, a difference of order $1/\gamma^2$. Both lie below the limit $2\pi^2 = 19.739$ of a well of width $a/2$, and far above the $\frac{\pi^2}{2} = 4.935$ of the well without a barrier.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the energy $E_2$ of the first excited state, and the difference $E_2 - E_1$ to the lowest order in $1/\gamma$ (letters `hbar`, `m`, `a`, `gam`). What happens to the difference as the barrier becomes infinitely strong?

<Hint>

The first excited state has one node. By the symmetry of the well the node is at the middle. What does part (a) say about such states?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e2" label="$E_2 =$" expr="2*pi^2*hbar^2/(m*a^2)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[

  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-de" label="$E_2 - E_1 \approx$" expr="8*pi^2*hbar^2/(gam*m*a^2)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 2], gam: [20, 200] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", gam: "\\gamma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "4*pi^2*hbar^2/(gam*m*a^2)", hint: "This is the difference if the energy changed by the factor (1 − 2/γ). That factor belongs to k, and the energy goes as k squared." },
    { expr: "-8*pi^2*hbar^2/(gam*m*a^2)", hint: "This is E₁ − E₂. The first excited state lies above the ground state." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-limit" label="As $\gamma \to \infty$ the difference" choices={["stays fixed, while both energies grow without limit", "shrinks as 1/γ, but the two levels can never meet, even for an infinite barrier, because bound states in one dimension are never degenerate under any circumstances", "shrinks as 1/γ and vanishes for an infinite barrier: the well is then two separate wells, and the theorem on degeneracy does not apply", "grows as γ, because the barrier raises the ground state and leaves the first excited state alone"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The first excited state has a node at the barrier and does not feel it. The ground state is pushed up towards it from below.

<Step title="The first excited state">

The lowest state of the first kind in part (a) has $ka = 2\pi$: it is $\sin\frac{2\pi x}{a}$, the second level of the plain well, with one node. No state of the second kind lies between the ground state and it, because the second solution of $\tan\xi = -2\xi/\gamma$ lies between $\frac{3\pi}{2}$ and $2\pi$. So

<Formula label="Energy of the first excited state" tex="E_2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{2\pi}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{2\pi^2\hbar^2}{ma^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

exactly, for every $\gamma$.

</Step>

<Step title="The difference">

<Formula label="Splitting of the two lowest levels" tex="E_2 - E_1 = \frac{2\pi^2\hbar^2}{ma^2}\cdot\frac{4}{\gamma} + \dots = \frac{8\pi^2\hbar^2}{\gamma\,ma^2} + \dots = \frac{8\pi^2\hbar^4}{m^2a^4\,V_0} + \dots" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

It falls as $1/\gamma$ and vanishes for an infinite barrier: the two lowest levels become degenerate.

</Step>

<Step title="Why this does not contradict the theorem">

The lesson's Theorem 1 says that bound states in one dimension are not degenerate. Its proof shows that two states of the same energy obey $\psi_1\psi_2' - \psi_2\psi_1' = 0$, and concludes that they are proportional. That last step divides by the wavefunctions. With an infinite barrier both wavefunctions vanish at the middle, and the constant of proportionality can differ on the two sides: in the limit the two states are equal in the left half and opposite in the right half. Physically the well has become two wells of width $a/2$ with no connection, each with its own state of energy $\frac{2\pi^2\hbar^2}{ma^2}$. This is the singular situation that the problem announces.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result with a graph">

For $\gamma = 50$ the two wavefunctions are drawn below. On the left half they are almost equal. On the right half they are almost opposite. The ground state has a kink at the barrier and no node. The first excited state passes through zero there.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 1]} range={[-1.25, 1.25]} xLabel="x / a" yLabel="ψ"
  curves={[
    { expr: "sin(6.0426*(x < 0.5 ? x : 1 - x))", label: "ground" },
    { expr: "sin(2*pi*x)", label: "first excited", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="Computed from the solutions of part (a) for γ = 50: sin(kx) on the left and sin(k(a − x)) on the right with ka = 6.0426 for the ground state, and sin(2πx/a) for the first excited state. Neither is normalized. At the barrier (x = a/2) the ground state is small but not zero, and its slope jumps." />

The sum and the difference of the two are states that sit almost entirely in one half. With a finite barrier they are not energy eigenstates: a particle put in the left half moves to the right half and back, at a rate set by $E_2 - E_1$, which goes to zero as the barrier closes.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Nodes in wavefunctions" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

Write the time-independent Schrödinger equation as in the lesson, with the energy and the potential multiplied by $2m/\hbar^2$:

<Formula label="The rescaled Schrödinger equation" tex="\psi'' + \left(\tilde E - \tilde V(x)\right)\psi = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

(MIT's sheet writes a script E and $U(x)$ for the lesson's $\tilde E$ and $\tilde V(x)$.) Let $\psi_k$ be the real energy eigenstate with energy $\tilde E_k$, and $\psi_{k+1}$ the real eigenstate with the next energy, $\tilde E_{k+1} > \tilde E_k$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Show that for any two points $a$ and $b$

<Formula label="The identity to be shown" tex="\left[\psi_{k+1}\,\psi_k' - \psi_k\,\psi_{k+1}'\right]_a^b = \left(\tilde E_{k+1} - \tilde E_k\right)\int_a^b\psi_k\,\psi_{k+1}\,dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2, eq. (1)" />

Then test it (our addition). Take the infinite well $0 \le x \le 1$ with $\tilde V = 0$ inside, the states $\psi_k = \sin\pi x$ and $\psi_{k+1} = \sin 2\pi x$ with $\tilde E_k = \pi^2$ and $\tilde E_{k+1} = 4\pi^2$, and the points $a = 0$, $b = \frac12$. What is the common value of the two sides?

<Hint>

Differentiate the bracket on the left. Two of the four terms cancel.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Replace each second derivative by means of the Schrödinger equation, $\psi_k'' = \left(\tilde V - \tilde E_k\right)\psi_k$, and integrate the result from $a$ to $b$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-test" label="Both sides $=$" value="6.283" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p2-test" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The derivative of the bracket contains only second derivatives, and the Schrödinger equation turns those into the wavefunctions themselves. The potential drops out.

<Step title="Differentiate the bracket">

<Formula label="Derivative of the bracket" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\left(\psi_{k+1}\,\psi_k' - \psi_k\,\psi_{k+1}'\right) = \psi_{k+1}\,\psi_k'' - \psi_k\,\psi_{k+1}''" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The two terms $\psi_{k+1}'\psi_k'$ cancel.

</Step>

<Step title="Use the Schrödinger equation">

With $\psi_k'' = \left(\tilde V - \tilde E_k\right)\psi_k$ and $\psi_{k+1}'' = \left(\tilde V - \tilde E_{k+1}\right)\psi_{k+1}$:

<Formula label="The potential drops out" tex="\psi_{k+1}\,\psi_k'' - \psi_k\,\psi_{k+1}'' = \left(\tilde V - \tilde E_k\right)\psi_k\psi_{k+1} - \left(\tilde V - \tilde E_{k+1}\right)\psi_k\psi_{k+1} = \left(\tilde E_{k+1} - \tilde E_k\right)\psi_k\,\psi_{k+1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Integrating from $a$ to $b$ gives the identity. The same steps with equal energies give the lesson's proof that bound states are not degenerate.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result on the square well">

On the left, the bracket is $\pi\sin 2\pi x\cos\pi x - 2\pi\sin\pi x\cos 2\pi x$. At $x = \frac12$ it is $0 - 2\pi\cdot 1\cdot(-1) = 2\pi$, and at $x = 0$ it is zero: the left side is $2\pi$. On the right, $\sin\pi x\sin 2\pi x = 2\sin^2\pi x\cos\pi x$ integrates to $\frac{2}{3\pi}\sin^3\pi x$, which is $\frac{2}{3\pi}$ between $0$ and $\frac12$. Times $4\pi^2 - \pi^2 = 3\pi^2$ this is $2\pi$ again. Both sides are $2\pi = 6.283$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Now let $a < b$ be two successive zeros of $\psi_k$, and choose the sign of $\psi_k$ so that $\psi_k(x) > 0$ for $a < x < b$. Use the identity to show that $\psi_{k+1}$ must change sign in the interval $(a, b)$: it has at least one zero between each pair of zeros of $\psi_k$. MIT's hint: suppose that $\psi_{k+1}$ does not change sign in $(a, b)$, and look at the sign of each side. The box (ours) asks for those signs.

<Hint>

At $a$ and $b$ the terms with $\psi_k$ vanish. What is left on the left side holds the slopes $\psi_k'(a)$ and $\psi_k'(b)$. What are their signs, given that $\psi_k$ is positive in between?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Can $\psi_k'(a)$ be zero? Remember from the lesson of Lecture 1 what follows when a solution and its slope both vanish at one point.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-signs" label="If $\psi_{k+1} > 0$ throughout $(a, b)$, then" choices={["the left side is negative or zero and the right side is positive", "the left side is positive and the right side is negative or zero", "both sides are positive, so more information on the potential is needed before any conclusion can be drawn", "both sides vanish"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $\psi_k$ positive between its zeros, its slope is positive at the left zero and negative at the right one. If $\psi_{k+1}$ kept one sign, the two sides of the identity would have opposite signs.

<Step title="The slopes of the lower state at its zeros">

Because $\psi_k > 0$ just right of $a$ and $\psi_k(a) = 0$, the slope there cannot be negative: $\psi_k'(a) \ge 0$. It cannot be zero either. A solution of the Schrödinger equation with $\psi = 0$ and $\psi' = 0$ at one point vanishes everywhere (the lesson of Lecture 1). So $\psi_k'(a) > 0$, and in the same way $\psi_k'(b) < 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The left side">

At $a$ and $b$ the terms with $\psi_k$ vanish:

<Formula label="The left side at two zeros of the lower state" tex="\left[\psi_{k+1}\,\psi_k' - \psi_k\,\psi_{k+1}'\right]_a^b = \psi_{k+1}(b)\,\psi_k'(b) - \psi_{k+1}(a)\,\psi_k'(a)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Suppose $\psi_{k+1}$ does not change sign in $(a, b)$, and say it is positive there. Then $\psi_{k+1}(a) \ge 0$ and $\psi_{k+1}(b) \ge 0$ by continuity. The first term is a number that is not negative times a negative slope, and the second is subtracted and is a number that is not negative times a positive slope. The left side is negative or zero.

</Step>

<Step title="The right side">

The factor $\tilde E_{k+1} - \tilde E_k$ is positive, and the integrand $\psi_k\psi_{k+1}$ is positive throughout the interval. The right side is positive. This contradicts the left side. If $\psi_{k+1}$ is negative throughout instead, both sides change sign and the contradiction is the same. So $\psi_{k+1}$ changes sign in $(a, b)$, and being continuous it has a zero there.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result, and count">

In the test of part (a) the lower state $\sin\pi x$ has the successive zeros $0$ and $1$, and $\sin 2\pi x$ indeed has a zero between them, at $\frac12$. The argument also works when $a$ or $b$ is at infinity for a bound state, or at a hard wall: the terms at that end vanish because the wavefunctions do. A bound state $\psi_k$ with $j$ nodes cuts the line into $j + 1$ intervals, and $\psi_{k+1}$ has a zero inside each: at least $j + 1$ nodes, one more than $\psi_k$. This is the partial result that the lesson quotes on the way to the node theorem.

</Step>

Since the unit-free equation holds no constants, the energies are pure numbers $e$ times $\left(\hbar^4\alpha/m^2\right)^{1/3}$: they grow only as the cube root of the strength $\alpha$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equation fixes a solution once $\psi$ and $\psi'$ are given at one point. Symmetry gives those two numbers at the origin, up to the size of $\psi$. Then $e$ is the only unknown, and the demand that $\psi$ stays finite picks its values.

<Step title="The starting values">

The potential $u^4$ is even, so each bound state is even or odd (the lesson's Theorem 3 with its corollary). The ground state has no node. An odd function vanishes at the origin, so the ground state is even. The derivative of an even function is odd, and the slope is continuous here, so $\psi'(0) = 0$. The size of $\psi$ is free, because the equation is linear: $\psi(0) = 1$ is as good as any other choice. A solution with these starting values is even, so integrating towards positive $u$ is enough.

</Step>

<Step title="Shooting">

Write the equation as $\psi'' = 2\left(u^4 - e\right)\psi$. For $u^4 > e$ the wavefunction curves away from the axis, and a general solution runs off to plus or minus infinity. Integrating with a standard method of high accuracy up to $u = 4$ shows this: for $e = 0.66$ the solution runs off to plus infinity, and for $e = 0.67$ to minus infinity. In between there is a value for which it does neither and decays: an energy eigenstate. Halving the interval, each time keeping the half over which the sign changes, gives

<Formula label="Unit-free ground-state energy of the quartic potential" tex="e_1 = 0.667986, \qquad E_1 = 0.667986\left(\frac{\hbar^4\alpha}{m^2}\right)^{1/3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 4 (our own computation)" />

(the value does not change in its first nine digits, 0.667986259, when the end point is moved from $u = 4$ to $u = 5$). Values of $e$ below this one give solutions that never cross the axis and grow: there is no eigenstate below, so this is the ground state.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result with the variational principle">

A check of our own, by the lesson's method. The Gaussian $e^{-bu^2/2}$ has, in the unit-free equation, the kinetic part $\frac{b}{4}$ (Problem 4(a) with $\hbar = m = 1$) and the potential part $\frac{3}{4b^2}$, the mean of $u^4$. </Step>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="A property of matrices" points={5} mit="Problem 6">

A function of a matrix is defined by its power series: if $f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_nz^n$, then $f(\mathbf{M}) \equiv \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}f_n\mathbf{M}^n$. Let $\mathbf{M}$ be the matrix

<Formula label="The matrix of Problem 6" tex="\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -i\\ i & 0\end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4" />

(the lesson's Pauli matrix $\sigma_2$). Show that, for a real number $\theta$,

<Formula label="The form of the exponential" tex="e^{i\mathbf{M}\theta} = A(\theta)\,\mathbf{1} + B(\theta)\,\mathbf{M}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4, eq. (4)" />

where $\mathbf{1}$ is the $2 \times 2$ identity matrix, and find the functions $A$ and $B$. Which algebraic property of a matrix $\mathbf{M}$ of any size leads to this result? In the second box type $B$ without its factor $i$. Use the letter `theta`.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-prop" label="The property that is needed:" choices={["M is Hermitian", "M has trace zero", "M is antisymmetric and purely imaginary, so that the result holds only for matrices built exactly like this one and for no others", "M squared is the identity matrix"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The square of this matrix is the identity, so its powers take only two values: the identity and the matrix itself. The series then splits into two ordinary series.

<Step title="The powers of the matrix">

<Formula label="The square of the matrix" tex="\mathbf{M}^2 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -i\\ i & 0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0 & -i\\ i & 0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}(-i)(i) & 0\\ 0 & (i)(-i)\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{1}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.29), (2.30): the matrix is the Pauli matrix with index 2" />

So $\mathbf{M}^{2n} = \mathbf{1}$ and $\mathbf{M}^{2n+1} = \mathbf{M}$ for every whole number $n \ge 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Split the series">

<Formula label="The exponential, summed" tex="e^{i\mathbf{M}\theta} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\theta)^{2n}}{(2n)!}\,\mathbf{1} + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(i\theta)^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}\,\mathbf{M} = \cos\theta\;\mathbf{1} + i\sin\theta\;\mathbf{M}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

because $(i\theta)^{2n} = (-1)^n\theta^{2n}$ and $(i\theta)^{2n+1} = i\,(-1)^n\theta^{2n+1}$. So $A(\theta) = \cos\theta$ and $B(\theta) = i\sin\theta$.

</Step>

<Step title="The property">

Only $\mathbf{M}^2 = \mathbf{1}$ was used. Any square matrix of any size whose square is the identity obeys the same formula. It is Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$ with $\mathbf{M}$ in the place of the number 1. All three Pauli matrices have this property, and so does every combination $n_x\sigma_1 + n_y\sigma_2 + n_z\sigma_3$ with a unit vector $(n_x, n_y, n_z)$, which is why this formula returns later in the course for the rotation of spin states.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Written out for this matrix,

<Formula label="The exponential as a matrix" tex="e^{i\mathbf{M}\theta} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta\\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

a rotation of the plane by the angle $\theta$. It is the identity at $\theta = 0$, as $e^0$ must be. Its determinant is $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$, and its derivative at $\theta = 0$ is $i\mathbf{M}$, as the series demands. Two such rotations multiply to a rotation by the sum of the angles, as exponentials of the same matrix must.

</Step>

</Solution>
