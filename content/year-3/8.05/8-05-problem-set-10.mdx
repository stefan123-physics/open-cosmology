A formula box takes plain typing, for example `2*cos(th) - 1`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `th` stands for $\theta$, `a0`, `a1`, `b0` and `b1` for the angles $\alpha_0$, $\alpha_1$, $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ of Problem 2, `nR` and `nL` for $n_R$ and $n_L$, `hbar` for $\hbar$, `w` for $\omega$ and `pi` for $\pi$.

Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of MIT's lecture notes. The states $|+\rangle$ and $|-\rangle$ have spin up and down along $z$. For a unit vector $\vec n$, the states $|\vec n; +\rangle$ and $|\vec n; -\rangle$ have spin $+\hbar/2$ and $-\hbar/2$ along $\vec n$, and the spin operator along $\vec n$ is $S_n = \vec n\cdot\vec S = \frac{\hbar}{2}\,\vec n\cdot\vec\sigma$, with the three Pauli matrices collected in $\vec\sigma$. For two particles, a superscript in brackets says which particle an operator acts on: $S_a^{(1)}$ is the spin of particle one along $\vec a$. The singlet state of two spins has the same form along every direction (in a product $\otimes$ the first state belongs to particle one and the second to particle two):

<Formula label="The singlet state" tex="|\Psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\Big(|\vec n; +\rangle\otimes|\vec n; -\rangle - |\vec n; -\rangle\otimes|\vec n; +\rangle\Big) \quad\text{for every unit vector } \vec n" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (5.2) and (5.3)" />

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Another view on Bell's inequalities" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

Many pairs of spins are prepared, each pair in the singlet state. On each pair the spin of particle one is measured along a unit vector $\vec a$ and the spin of particle two along a unit vector $\vec b$. Following the reasoning of Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen (EPR), define the correlation coefficient as the average, over all the pairs, of the product of the two results:

<Formula label="The correlation coefficient" tex="C(\vec a, \vec b) \equiv \left[\frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_a^{(1)}S_b^{(2)}\right]_{\text{av}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Each result is $+\hbar/2$ or $-\hbar/2$, so each product in the bracket is $+1$ or $-1$ and the average lies between $-1$ and $+1$.

Now take three directions $\vec a$, $\vec b$ and $\vec c$, and the quantity

<Formula label="The quantity g" tex="g(\vec a, \vec b, \vec c) \equiv -\frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_a^{(1)}S_b^{(1)}\left(1 - \frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_b^{(1)}S_c^{(1)}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Every spin in $g$ belongs to particle one. In EPR's view the symbols $S_n^{(i)}$ are not operators here: each stands for a value that the particle carries, the result that a measurement along $\vec n$ would give. So for every unit vector $\vec n$ the square $S_n^{(1)}S_n^{(1)}$ is $\hbar^2/4$, and because the two spins of a singlet are always found opposite along the same direction,

<Formula label="The anticorrelation of the singlet, in EPR's view" tex="S_n^{(1)} = -S_n^{(2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Show that the average of $g$ over the pairs is a difference of two correlation coefficients:

<Formula label="The average of g" tex="\big[g(\vec a, \vec b, \vec c)\big]_{\text{av}} = C(\vec a, \vec b) - C(\vec a, \vec c)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2" />

<Hint>

Multiply out the bracket in $g$. One of the two terms contains $S_b^{(1)}$ twice.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In each term, one of the two spins of particle one can be traded for the spin of particle two along the same direction, at the cost of a sign. Then compare with the definition of $C$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** For values (not operators) the square of a spin value is $\hbar^2/4$, which removes $S_b^{(1)}$ from the second term. Trading one spin of particle one for the opposite spin of particle two then turns each term into a product that can really be measured on a pair.

<Step title="Multiply out">

<Formula label="The quantity g multiplied out" tex="g = -\frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_a^{(1)}S_b^{(1)} + \frac{16}{\hbar^4}\,S_a^{(1)}\,S_b^{(1)}S_b^{(1)}\,S_c^{(1)} = -\frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_a^{(1)}S_b^{(1)} + \frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_a^{(1)}S_c^{(1)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The second step used $S_b^{(1)}S_b^{(1)} = \hbar^2/4$.

</Step>

<Step title="Move one spin to particle two">

Put $S_b^{(1)} = -S_b^{(2)}$ in the first term and $S_c^{(1)} = -S_c^{(2)}$ in the second:

<Formula label="The quantity g with one spin on each particle" tex="g = \frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_a^{(1)}S_b^{(2)} - \frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_a^{(1)}S_c^{(2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The average of a difference is the difference of the averages, and each average is a correlation coefficient by its definition: $[g]_{\text{av}} = C(\vec a, \vec b) - C(\vec a, \vec c)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $\vec c = \vec b$. Then the bracket in $g$ is $1 - 1 = 0$ for every pair, so $g = 0$, and the right side is $C(\vec a, \vec b) - C(\vec a, \vec b) = 0$ as well.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

For any quantity, the absolute value of the average is at most the average of the absolute value:

<Formula label="The inequality to use" tex="\big|[g]_{\text{av}}\big| \le \big[\,|g|\,\big]_{\text{av}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2" />

Use it to show that

<Formula label="Bell's inequality for the correlation coefficients" tex="\big|C(\vec a, \vec b) - C(\vec a, \vec c)\big| - C(\vec b, \vec c) \le 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), eq. (1), p. 2" />

Any theory of the kind EPR had in mind must obey this inequality. The choice box is our addition.

<Hint>

$g$ is a product of two factors, so $|g|$ is the product of their absolute values. What values can each factor take for one pair?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The first factor is $+1$ or $-1$. The second factor, the bracket, is never negative, so its absolute value is the bracket itself. Average it, after moving one spin to particle two.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-absg" label="For one pair, $|g|$ equals" choices={["1, whatever the three values are", "1 + (4/ħ²) S_b S_c, with both values of particle one", "1 − (4/ħ²) S_b S_c, with both values of particle one", "the absolute value of (4/ħ²) S_b S_c, with both values of particle one"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The absolute value of $g$ is simple for values: the first factor has the size 1 and the bracket is 0 or 2, never negative. Its average is one more correlation coefficient.

<Step title="The absolute value for one pair">

The product $\frac{4}{\hbar^2}S_a^{(1)}S_b^{(1)}$ is $+1$ or $-1$, and so is $\frac{4}{\hbar^2}S_b^{(1)}S_c^{(1)}$. So the bracket $1 - \frac{4}{\hbar^2}S_b^{(1)}S_c^{(1)}$ is $0$ or $2$, and

<Formula label="The absolute value of g" tex="|g| = \left|\frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_a^{(1)}S_b^{(1)}\right|\,\left|1 - \frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_b^{(1)}S_c^{(1)}\right| = 1 - \frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_b^{(1)}S_c^{(1)} = 1 + \frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_b^{(1)}S_c^{(2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The last step used $S_c^{(1)} = -S_c^{(2)}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Average">

The average of the last expression is $1 + C(\vec b, \vec c)$. With part (a) for the left side, the inequality $|[g]_{\text{av}}| \le [\,|g|\,]_{\text{av}}$ reads

<Formula label="The inequality, before rearranging" tex="\big|C(\vec a, \vec b) - C(\vec a, \vec c)\big| \le 1 + C(\vec b, \vec c)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Moving $C(\vec b, \vec c)$ to the left gives the result.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $\vec c = \vec b$. EPR's values give $C(\vec b, \vec b) = -1$, since the two spins are always opposite along one direction. The inequality then reads $0 + 1 \le 1$: it holds, with nothing to spare.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Now turn to quantum mechanics. There $S_a^{(1)}$ and $S_b^{(2)}$ are operators, the spin of the first particle along $\vec a$ and of the second along $\vec b$, and the correlation coefficient is the expectation value of their product in the singlet state:

<Formula label="The correlation coefficient in quantum mechanics" tex="C(\vec a, \vec b) \equiv \left\langle\frac{4}{\hbar^2}\,S_a^{(1)}S_b^{(2)}\right\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2" />

Find $C(\vec a, \vec b)$ in terms of the angle $\theta_{ab}$ between $\vec a$ and $\vec b$, and prove your result.

The number box is our addition. Let $\vec a$ and $\vec b$ be $60°$ apart. For what fraction of the pairs are the two results equal (both $+\hbar/2$ or both $-\hbar/2$)?

<Hint>

Write the singlet in the basis along $\vec b$ and let $S_b^{(2)}$ act on it. Compare with what $S_b^{(1)}$ does to the same state.

</Hint>

<Hint>

After that step both operators act on particle one. The product of two Pauli matrices along $\vec a$ and $\vec b$ is $\vec a\cdot\vec b$ times the unit matrix, plus a term that is linear in the Pauli matrices. What is the expectation value of a single spin component of particle one in the singlet?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$C(\vec a, \vec b) =$" expr="-cos(th)"
  vars={{ th: [0.3, 2.8] }}
  names={{ th: "\\theta_{ab}" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "cos(th)", hint: "Check the sign with θ_ab = 0: along one direction the two spins of a singlet are always opposite, so every product is −1." },
    { expr: "-cos(th/2)", hint: "Half angles belong to amplitudes. An expectation value has two amplitudes in each term, which brings the full angle. For θ_ab = π/2 the result must be 0." },
    { expr: "sin(th/2)^2", hint: "That is the fraction of pairs with equal results. The pairs with opposite results count too, with the product −1." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-eq" label="Fraction of pairs with equal results at $60°$:" value="0.25" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-eq" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the singlet, the spin of particle two along any direction acts as minus the spin of particle one along that direction. That is the quantum form of EPR's rule, and it moves both operators to particle one, where the product of two Pauli matrices is known.

<Step title="Move the second operator to particle one">

Write $\sigma_b = \vec b\cdot\vec\sigma$, so that $S_b = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_b$. Use the singlet in the basis along $\vec b$. The operator $\sigma_b^{(2)}$ multiplies each term by the sign of the second spin, and $\sigma_b^{(1)}$ by the sign of the first, which is the opposite one in both terms:

<Formula label="The two spins along one direction, acting on the singlet" tex="\sigma_b^{(2)}|\Psi\rangle = -\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\Big(|\vec b; +\rangle\otimes|\vec b; -\rangle + |\vec b; -\rangle\otimes|\vec b; +\rangle\Big) = -\sigma_b^{(1)}|\Psi\rangle" cite="reading1" at="eq. (5.3) (our own step)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The product of two Pauli matrices">

Now $C = \langle\Psi|\sigma_a^{(1)}\sigma_b^{(2)}|\Psi\rangle = -\langle\Psi|\sigma_a^{(1)}\sigma_b^{(1)}|\Psi\rangle$. For one spin,

<Formula label="The product of two Pauli matrices along two directions" tex="(\vec a\cdot\vec\sigma)(\vec b\cdot\vec\sigma) = \vec a\cdot\vec b + i\,(\vec a\times\vec b)\cdot\vec\sigma" cite="reading3" at="eq. (3.60)" />

The first term on the right multiplies the unit matrix. The last term is a number times the Pauli matrix along the direction of $\vec a\times\vec b$. In the singlet a single spin component of particle one has the expectation value zero: written along that direction, the state has the two results $+1$ and $-1$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. Only the first term is left:

<Formula label="The quantum correlation coefficient" tex="C(\vec a, \vec b) = -\,\vec a\cdot\vec b = -\cos\theta_{ab}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result with probabilities">

Lecture 20 found the probability that both spins are up, particle one along $\vec a$ and particle two along $\vec b$: it is $\frac{1}{2}\sin^2\frac{\theta_{ab}}{2}$ (notes chapter 8, eq. (5.8)). Both down has the same probability. "Up along $\vec a$ and down along $\vec b$" is "up along $\vec a$ and up along $-\vec b$", with the angle $\pi - \theta_{ab}$, so its probability is $\frac{1}{2}\cos^2\frac{\theta_{ab}}{2}$, and the same for "down and up". The average of the product is

<Formula label="The correlation coefficient from the four probabilities" tex="C = (+1)\,\sin^2\frac{\theta_{ab}}{2} + (-1)\,\cos^2\frac{\theta_{ab}}{2} = -\cos\theta_{ab}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (5.8) (our own check)" />

The same count answers the number box: the results are equal for the fraction $\sin^2\frac{\theta_{ab}}{2}$ of the pairs, which is $\sin^2 30° = 0.25$ at $60°$. Then $C = 0.25 - 0.75 = -0.5 = -\cos 60°$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Let $\vec a$, $\vec b$ and $\vec c$ lie in one plane, with $\theta_{ab} = \theta_{bc} = \theta$ and $\theta_{ac} = 2\theta$. Work out the left side of Bell's inequality of part (b) with the quantum result of part (c), plot it as a function of $\theta$, and find the angles at which the inequality is broken.

In the formula box give the left side for $0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$. The two number boxes are our addition: the largest value that the left side reaches, and the angle $\theta$, in degrees, at which it does so.

MIT writes that the inequality is violated for $\theta \le \pi/2$. At the two ends of that range, $\theta = 0$ and $\theta = \pi/2$, the left side equals 1 exactly: the inequality is broken strictly between them.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** With the quantum correlation $-\cos\theta$ the left side is a simple function of one angle. It exceeds 1 for every angle between $0$ and $\pi/2$.

<Step title="The left side">

<Formula label="The left side with the quantum correlations" tex="\big|C(\vec a, \vec b) - C(\vec a, \vec c)\big| - C(\vec b, \vec c) = \big|\cos 2\theta - \cos\theta\big| + \cos\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

For $0 \le \theta \le \frac{2\pi}{3}$ the cosine of the larger angle $2\theta$ is the smaller one, so the absolute value is $\cos\theta - \cos 2\theta$ and the left side is $2\cos\theta - \cos 2\theta$. That range contains $0 \le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$, the range of the formula box.

</Step>

<Step title="Where it exceeds 1">

With $\cos 2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$,

<Formula label="The left side minus one, factorised" tex="2\cos\theta - \cos 2\theta = 1 + 2\cos\theta\,(1 - \cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The factor $1 - \cos\theta$ is positive for $\theta > 0$, so the left side is larger than 1 exactly when $\cos\theta > 0$: for $0 < \theta < \frac{\pi}{2}$. The product $\cos\theta\,(1 - \cos\theta)$ is largest when $\cos\theta = \frac{1}{2}$, at $\theta = 60°$, where the left side is $1 + 2\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} = 1.5$.

</Step>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Beating the odds using entangled states" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

Two spins are in the entangled state

<Formula label="The shared entangled state" tex="|\Psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\Big(|+\rangle\otimes|+\rangle + |-\rangle\otimes|-\rangle\Big)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, eq. (1), p. 2" />

(This is not the singlet of Problem 1: the letter $\Psi$ has a new meaning in this problem.) For a spin state with the amplitudes $v_+$ and $v_-$, a bar denotes the state with the complex conjugate amplitudes:

<Formula label="The barred state" tex="|v\rangle = v_+|+\rangle + v_-|-\rangle \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |\bar v\rangle \equiv v_+^*|+\rangle + v_-^*|-\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Let $|v_a\rangle$ be a normalized spin state of the first particle and $|w_b\rangle$ one of the second. Show that the probability of finding the first particle in $|v_a\rangle$ and the second in $|w_b\rangle$, in a measurement on the entangled state, is

<Formula label="The probability of a pair of outcomes" tex="P(v_a, w_b) = \frac{1}{2}\,\big|\langle\bar v_a|w_b\rangle\big|^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), eq. (2), p. 2" />

The number box is our addition. Take $|v_a\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\big(|+\rangle + i|-\rangle\big)$ and $|w_b\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\big(|+\rangle - i|-\rangle\big)$. What is $P(v_a, w_b)$?

<Hint>

The amplitude is the inner product of the product state $|v_a\rangle\otimes|w_b\rangle$ with $|\Psi\rangle$. Inner products of product states are taken particle by particle.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write both states with amplitudes, $|v_a\rangle = v_+|+\rangle + v_-|-\rangle$ and the same for $|w_b\rangle$. A bra carries the complex conjugates of the amplitudes of its ket. What are the amplitudes in the bra of the barred state?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-bar" label="$P(v_a, w_b) =$" value="0.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-bar" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The amplitude for the pair of outcomes is the overlap of a product state with $|\Psi\rangle$. Both bras bring complex conjugates, and the bar is the bookkeeping that turns the result into an overlap of two single-spin states.

<Step title="The amplitude">

With $|v_a\rangle = v_+|+\rangle + v_-|-\rangle$ and $|w_b\rangle = w_+|+\rangle + w_-|-\rangle$,

<Formula label="The amplitude for the pair of outcomes" tex="\big(\langle v_a|\otimes\langle w_b|\big)|\Psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\Big(\langle v_a|+\rangle\langle w_b|+\rangle + \langle v_a|-\rangle\langle w_b|-\rangle\Big) = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\big(v_+^*w_+^* + v_-^*w_-^*\big)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The barred state">

The ket $|\bar v_a\rangle$ has the amplitudes $v_+^*$ and $v_-^*$, so its bra has the amplitudes $v_+$ and $v_-$:

<Formula label="The overlap with the barred state" tex="\langle\bar v_a|w_b\rangle = v_+w_+ + v_-w_-" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The amplitude above is $\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}$ times the complex conjugate of this overlap. A number and its conjugate have the same size, so the probability is $\frac{1}{2}\big|\langle\bar v_a|w_b\rangle\big|^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

For the two given states $v_+ = w_+ = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}$, $v_- = \frac{i}{\sqrt 2}$ and $w_- = -\frac{i}{\sqrt 2}$. Then $v_+w_+ + v_-w_- = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2} = 1$ and $P = \frac{1}{2}$. Here $|w_b\rangle$ is exactly $|\bar v_a\rangle$. Without the bar the overlap would be $\langle v_a|w_b\rangle = \frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{2} = 0$: the two states are orthogonal, and yet the pair of outcomes is as likely as it can be.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Fix $|v_a\rangle$ and add the probabilities for $|w_b\rangle$ and for the state orthogonal to it: the two squared overlaps with the normalized state $|\bar v_a\rangle$ add up to 1, so the sum is $\frac{1}{2}$. Each outcome of the first particle alone has the probability $\frac{1}{2}$, whatever basis is used, as it should for half of a maximally entangled pair. The formula also says what the first measurement does: when the first particle is found in $|v_a\rangle$, the second is left in $|\bar v_a\rangle$.

</Step>

</Solution>

The rest of the problem is a game. Alice and Bob are far apart and cannot send each other anything. They play as a team against Charlie. In each round Charlie gives Alice a bit $x$ and Bob a bit $y$; a bit is a variable with the two values 0 and 1. Neither player knows the other's bit. Alice answers with a bit $a$, which may depend on $x$, and Bob with a bit $b$, which may depend on $y$. They win the round if

<Formula label="The condition for winning a round" tex="a + b \equiv x\,y \ (\text{mod } 2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 3" />

So when $x = y = 1$ the right side is 1 and they win if their answers differ. When $x$ or $y$ is 0 the right side is 0 and they win if their answers are equal. A simple plan is to answer $a = b = 0$ always (or $a = b = 1$ always): it wins unless $x = y = 1$. Each of the four pairs of bits is taken to be equally likely (MIT's sheet does not say so in words, but its probabilities assume it), so this plan wins with the probability $\frac{3}{4}$.

<Part label="b" points={3}>

In a classical strategy Alice fixes a function $a(x)$ beforehand, and Bob a function $b(y)$.

1. Explain why Alice has exactly four choices, which can be written as the functions $0$, $1$, $x$ and $1 - x$. Bob likewise has $0$, $1$, $y$ and $1 - y$.
2. For every choice the sum can be written as

<Formula label="The sum of the two answers in a classical strategy" tex="a(x) + b(y) \equiv c_0 + c_1x + c_2y \ (\text{mod } 2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3" />

with constants $c_0$, $c_1$ and $c_2$ that are each 0 or 1. Show that for no choice of the constants the right side equals $x\,y$ modulo 2 for all four pairs of bits: no classical strategy wins always.

3. Explain why it follows that no classical strategy wins with a probability larger than $\frac{3}{4}$.

The number box is our addition. There are $4\times 4 = 16$ pairs of functions $\big(a(x), b(y)\big)$. How many of them win for exactly three of the four pairs of bits?

<Hint>

A function of one bit is fixed by its two values, $a(0)$ and $a(1)$. List the possibilities.

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the last point: a strategy that fails for at least one of four equally likely inputs wins at most three times in four. Players may also toss coins or share random numbers made beforehand. Such a strategy is a mixture of fixed ones: what does that do to the winning probability?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-count" label="Number of pairs of functions that win for exactly three of the four inputs:" value="8" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-count" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A sum of a function of $x$ alone and a function of $y$ alone has no term $x\,y$. Fixing the function on the three inputs that contain a zero forces the wrong answer on the fourth.

<Step title="Four functions of one bit">

A function $a(x)$ is fixed by the two values $a(0)$ and $a(1)$, each 0 or 1: four functions in all. With the values written as $\big(a(0), a(1)\big)$ they are $(0, 0)$, the function $0$; $(1, 1)$, the function $1$; $(0, 1)$, the function $x$; and $(1, 0)$, the function $1 - x$. The same holds for Bob.

</Step>

<Step title="The sum has no product term">

Modulo 2, $1 - x \equiv 1 + x$. So each of Alice's functions is a constant plus $c_1x$ and each of Bob's is a constant plus $c_2y$, with all four numbers 0 or 1, and the sum is $c_0 + c_1x + c_2y$, where $c_0$ is the sum of the two constants modulo 2. Suppose it equals $x\,y$ for every input. Then

<Formula label="The three inputs with a zero fix the constants" tex="(x, y) = (0, 0):\ c_0 \equiv 0, \qquad (1, 0):\ c_0 + c_1 \equiv 0, \qquad (0, 1):\ c_0 + c_2 \equiv 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

so $c_0 = c_1 = c_2 = 0$. But then for $x = y = 1$ the sum is $0$, while $x\,y = 1$. So no choice of constants works for all four inputs.

</Step>

<Step title="The bound of three quarters">

Every pair of fixed functions therefore loses for at least one of the four equally likely inputs, and wins with a probability of at most $\frac{3}{4}$. Tossing coins or using shared random numbers does not help. Such a strategy picks one of the 16 pairs of fixed functions with some probability, without knowing the other player's bit, and its winning probability is the average of theirs. An average of numbers that are at most $\frac{3}{4}$ is at most $\frac{3}{4}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Count the strategies">

Add up $c_0 + c_1x + c_2y$ over the four inputs: the result is $4c_0 + 2c_1 + 2c_2$, an even number. The sum of $x\,y$ over the four inputs is 1, an odd number. So the two functions differ on an odd number of inputs, 1 or 3: a fixed strategy wins exactly three inputs or exactly one. The sums that differ from $x\,y$ on one input only are $0$ (wrong for $(1, 1)$), $x$ (wrong for $(1, 0)$), $y$ (wrong for $(0, 1)$) and $1 + x + y$ (wrong for $(0, 0)$). Each of these four sums comes from two pairs of functions, since the constant $c_0$ can be split between Alice and Bob in two ways (for $c_0 = 0$ their constants are both 0 or both 1, for $c_0 = 1$ they differ). That gives $4\times 2 = 8$ pairs of functions that win three times in four. The other 8 win only once in four.

</Step>

</Solution>

Now Alice and Bob use quantum mechanics. Before the game they share the entangled pair $|\Psi\rangle$ above: Alice holds the first spin and Bob the second. Alice has two bases, one for each value of her bit $x$, built with two angles $\alpha_0$ and $\alpha_1$:

<Formula label="Alice's basis for the bit x" tex="|v_0^x\rangle \equiv \cos\frac{\alpha_x}{2}\,|+\rangle + \sin\frac{\alpha_x}{2}\,|-\rangle, \qquad |v_1^x\rangle \equiv -\sin\frac{\alpha_x}{2}\,|+\rangle + \cos\frac{\alpha_x}{2}\,|-\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 3" />

<Formula label="Bob's basis for the bit y" tex="|w_0^y\rangle \equiv \cos\frac{\beta_y}{2}\,|+\rangle + \sin\frac{\beta_y}{2}\,|-\rangle, \qquad |w_1^y\rangle \equiv -\sin\frac{\beta_y}{2}\,|+\rangle + \cos\frac{\beta_y}{2}\,|-\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 3" />

If she finds the first basis state she answers $a = 0$, and if she finds the second she answers $a = 1$. Bob does the same with his bit $y$, his spin and the basis $\big(|w_0^y\rangle, |w_1^y\rangle\big)$ to get $b$. The four angles are theirs to choose.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Part (a) gives the probability of each pair of outcomes as half a squared overlap. For real states the overlap of Alice's state with Bob's depends only on the difference of their angles.

<Step title="The two overlaps">

The basis states have real amplitudes, so $|\bar v\rangle = |v\rangle$. Then

<Formula label="The overlaps of the matching basis states" tex="\langle v_0^x|w_0^y\rangle = \cos\frac{\alpha_x}{2}\cos\frac{\beta_y}{2} + \sin\frac{\alpha_x}{2}\sin\frac{\beta_y}{2} = \cos\frac{\alpha_x - \beta_y}{2} = \langle v_1^x|w_1^y\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="Check the result">

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Properties of the angular momentum operators" points={20} mit="Problem 3">

MIT's sheet asks for Exercises 1 to 7 of the lecture notes on angular momentum (chapter 9). They are parts (a) to (g) here. What they build on is collected first.

A vector operator is a triplet of operators, such as the position $\vec r$ with the components $x_i$, the momentum $\vec p$ with the components $p_i$ and the angular momentum $\vec L$ with the components $L_i$. (The notes put hats on these operators and print the vectors in bold.) Repeated indices are summed over 1, 2, 3. For two vector operators $\vec a$ and $\vec b$, whose components need not commute, the products are defined with the operators in this order:

<Formula label="Dot and cross products of vector operators" tex="\vec a\cdot\vec b \equiv a_ib_i, \qquad (\vec a\times\vec b)_i \equiv \epsilon_{ijk}\,a_jb_k, \qquad \vec a^{\,2} \equiv \vec a\cdot\vec a" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (1.11) and (1.12)" />

Because the order matters, the cross product is not simply antisymmetric:

<Formula label="Swapping the factors of a cross product" tex="(\vec a\times\vec b)_i = -(\vec b\times\vec a)_i + \epsilon_{ijk}\,[a_j, b_k]" cite="reading2" at="eq. (1.18)" />

Two identities for the $\epsilon$ symbol are needed:

<Formula label="Products of two epsilon symbols" tex="\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{ipq} = \delta_{jp}\delta_{kq} - \delta_{jq}\delta_{kp}, \qquad \epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{ijq} = 2\,\delta_{kq}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (1.30) and (1.31)" />

The orbital angular momentum is $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p$, that is $L_i = \epsilon_{ijk}x_jp_k$, with $[x_i, p_j] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}$. Its commutators with position and momentum are

<Formula label="Position and momentum are vectors under rotations" tex="[L_i, x_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,x_k, \qquad [L_i, p_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,p_k" cite="reading2" at="eq. (1.40)" />

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Exercise 1. Prove the two rules for the Hermitian conjugate of a product of vector operators:

<Formula label="Hermitian conjugates of the dot and the cross product" tex="(\vec a\cdot\vec b)^\dagger = \vec b^{\,\dagger}\cdot\vec a^{\,\dagger}, \qquad (\vec a\times\vec b)^\dagger = -\,\vec b^{\,\dagger}\times\vec a^{\,\dagger}" cite="reading2" at="Exercise 1, eq. (1.22), p. 4" />

<Hint>

Write each product in components. The adjoint of a product of two operators is the product of the adjoints in the opposite order. The $\epsilon$ symbol is a real number.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-herm" label="For Hermitian $\vec a$ and $\vec b$, the operator $\vec a\cdot\vec b$ is Hermitian" choices={["always: a sum of products of Hermitian operators is Hermitian", "exactly when the sum of the commutators [a_i, b_i] is zero", "exactly when the cross product a × b is Hermitian as well", "never, unless one of the two vectors is zero"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Taking the adjoint reverses the order of the two operators in each term. For the dot product that is all. For the cross product the reversed order costs a sign, from the $\epsilon$ symbol.

<Step title="The dot product">

<Formula label="The adjoint of the dot product" tex="(\vec a\cdot\vec b)^\dagger = (a_ib_i)^\dagger = b_i^\dagger a_i^\dagger = \vec b^{\,\dagger}\cdot\vec a^{\,\dagger}" cite="reading2" at="Exercise 1, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="The cross product">

<Formula label="The adjoint of the cross product" tex="\big((\vec a\times\vec b)_i\big)^\dagger = \epsilon_{ijk}\,(a_jb_k)^\dagger = \epsilon_{ijk}\,b_k^\dagger a_j^\dagger = -\epsilon_{ikj}\,b_k^\dagger a_j^\dagger = -(\vec b^{\,\dagger}\times\vec a^{\,\dagger})_i" cite="reading2" at="Exercise 1, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

In the last step the first operator carries the second index of the $\epsilon$ symbol and the second operator the third, which is the definition of the cross product.

<Step title="The box, and a check">

For Hermitian vectors the first rule says $(\vec a\cdot\vec b)^\dagger = \vec b\cdot\vec a$. Since $\vec a\cdot\vec b = \vec b\cdot\vec a + [a_i, b_i]$ (notes eq. (1.14)), the dot product is Hermitian exactly when the sum of commutators $[a_i, b_i]$ is zero. Example: $\vec r\cdot\vec p$ is not Hermitian, because $(\vec r\cdot\vec p)^\dagger = \vec p\cdot\vec r = \vec r\cdot\vec p - 3i\hbar$ (notes eq. (1.16)). So $\vec r\times\vec p$ is Hermitian while $\vec r\cdot\vec p$ is not, which rules out the third choice.

<Step title="Two terms">

<Formula label="The four extra terms in the special case" tex="-a_j\,\gamma\,\delta_{jk}\,b_k + a_j\,(3\gamma)\,b_j - a_j\,\gamma\,\delta_{kj}\,b_k - 0 = (-1 + 3 - 1)\,\gamma\,\vec a\cdot\vec b = \gamma\,\vec a\cdot\vec b" cite="reading2" at="Exercise 2, p. 6 (our own solution)" />

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A three-dimensional angular momentum in the two-dimensional oscillator" points={15} mit="Problem 5">

Problem 6 of [Problem Set 6](/courses/8-05/8-05-problem-set-6) treated the isotropic harmonic oscillator in two dimensions, with the ladder operators $(a_x, a_x^\dagger)$ and $(a_y, a_y^\dagger)$. The combinations

<Formula label="Left and right ladder operators" tex="a_L = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\big(a_x + i\,a_y\big), \qquad a_R = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\big(a_x - i\,a_y\big), \qquad N_L = a_L^\dagger a_L, \qquad N_R = a_R^\dagger a_R" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4" />

are two independent sets of ladder operators: $[a_L, a_L^\dagger] = [a_R, a_R^\dagger] = 1$, and every operator with the label $L$ commutes with every operator with the label $R$. With them the Hamiltonian and the angular momentum $L_z = x\,p_y - y\,p_x$ are

<Formula label="The Hamiltonian and the angular momentum of the two-dimensional oscillator" tex="H = \hbar\omega\,\big(N_R + N_L + 1\big), \qquad L_z = \hbar\,\big(N_R - N_L\big)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4" />

The ground state $|0\rangle$ is killed by $a_L$ and by $a_R$. The states $(a_R^\dagger)^{n_R}(a_L^\dagger)^{n_L}|0\rangle$ are a basis, with $n_R$ and $n_L$ the eigenvalues of $N_R$ and $N_L$.

This problem shows that the oscillator hides a three-dimensional algebra of angular momentum. Its operators are

<Formula label="The operators of the hidden angular momentum" tex="J_z = \alpha\,L_z = \alpha\,\hbar\,\big(N_R - N_L\big), \qquad J_+ = \beta\,\hbar\,a_R^\dagger a_L, \qquad J_- = \beta\,\hbar\,a_L^\dagger a_R" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4" />

with two real constants $\alpha$ and $\beta$ to be found. (They have nothing to do with the angles of Problem 2.) As it must be, $J_+^\dagger = J_-$. As always, $J_\pm = J_x \pm iJ_y$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the constants $\alpha$ and $\beta$ from the condition that the three operators obey the algebra of angular momentum. The algebra fixes $\beta$ up to its sign, so the second box asks for $\beta^2$.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-alpha" label="$\alpha =$" value="0.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-alpha" />

<Answer id="p5-beta2" label="$\beta^2 =$" value="1" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-beta2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The commutator with $J_z$ counts by how much $J_+$ changes $N_R - N_L$: that fixes $\alpha$. The commutator of $J_+$ with $J_-$ is quadratic in $\beta$ and fixes $\beta^2$.

<Step title="The commutator with J_z">

From $[N_R, a_R^\dagger] = a_R^\dagger$ and $[N_L, a_L] = -a_L$,

<Formula label="The commutator of J_z with J_+" tex="[J_z, J_+] = \alpha\beta\hbar^2\,\big[N_R - N_L,\ a_R^\dagger a_L\big] = \alpha\beta\hbar^2\,\big(a_R^\dagger a_L + a_R^\dagger a_L\big) = 2\alpha\,\hbar\,J_+" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

The algebra demands $[J_z, J_+] = \hbar\,J_+$ (notes chapter 9, eq. (2.13)), so $\alpha = \frac{1}{2}$. The adjoint of this equation is $[J_z, J_-] = -\hbar\,J_-$, so nothing new comes from $J_-$.

</Step>

<Step title="The commutator of J_+ with J_-">

Since the $L$ and $R$ operators commute, $[a_R^\dagger a_L, a_L^\dagger a_R] = a_R^\dagger a_R\,a_La_L^\dagger - a_L^\dagger a_L\,a_Ra_R^\dagger = N_R(N_L + 1) - N_L(N_R + 1) = N_R - N_L$. So

<Formula label="The commutator of J_+ with J_-" tex="[J_+, J_-] = \beta^2\hbar^2\,\big(N_R - N_L\big) = \frac{\beta^2}{\alpha}\,\hbar\,J_z" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

The algebra demands $[J_+, J_-] = 2\hbar\,J_z$ (notes eq. (2.8)), so $\beta^2 = 2\alpha = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="The result">

<Formula label="The hidden angular momentum" tex="J_z = \frac{1}{2}L_z = \frac{\hbar}{2}\big(N_R - N_L\big), \qquad J_+ = \hbar\,a_R^\dagger a_L, \qquad J_- = \hbar\,a_L^\dagger a_R" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

with the choice $\beta = 1$. The choice $\beta = -1$ obeys the algebra just as well: it is the same construction with $a_L$ replaced by $-a_L$, which changes nothing else.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two commutators above are the whole algebra: with $J_x = \frac{1}{2}(J_+ + J_-)$ and $J_y = \frac{1}{2i}(J_+ - J_-)$ they are equivalent to $[J_i, J_j] = i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$. In terms of the original operators the two new components are $J_x = \frac{\hbar}{2}\big(a_x^\dagger a_x - a_y^\dagger a_y\big)$ and $J_y = \frac{\hbar}{2}\big(a_x^\dagger a_y + a_y^\dagger a_x\big)$, found by multiplying out $a_R^\dagger a_L$: $J_x$ compares the numbers of quanta along $x$ and along $y$. Both are Hermitian, as components of an angular momentum must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Solution>

**The idea.** $J_-$ turns quanta of the kind $R$ into quanta of the kind $L$, one at a time. Starting from the top state of part (c) it runs through all the states with the same total number of quanta: one energy level is exactly one multiplet.

<Step title="Lowering from the top state">

Apply $J_- = \hbar\,a_L^\dagger a_R$ to $(a_R^\dagger)^N|0\rangle$ repeatedly. Each step removes one quantum of the kind $R$ and adds one of the kind $L$, so after $k$ steps the state is a multiple of $(a_R^\dagger)^{N-k}(a_L^\dagger)^k|0\rangle$. It has $m = \frac{1}{2}(N - 2k) = \frac{N}{2} - k$, and the same $j = \frac{N}{2}$, since $J_-$ commutes with $\vec J^{\,2}$. After $N$ steps no quantum of the kind $R$ is left and the next $J_-$ gives zero. The values of $m$ are $\frac{N}{2}, \frac{N}{2} - 1, \dots, -\frac{N}{2}$: these are $N + 1 = 2j + 1$ states.

</Step>

<Step title="One level, one multiplet">

The states with $n_R + n_L = N$ are exactly these $N + 1$ states, and they all have the energy $\hbar\omega(N + 1)$. So in general

<Formula label="The labels and the energy of the oscillator states" tex="j = \frac{n_R + n_L}{2}, \qquad m = \frac{n_R - n_L}{2}, \qquad E = \hbar\omega\,(2j + 1)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

The spectrum of the oscillator is one multiplet for each $j = 0, \frac{1}{2}, 1, \frac{3}{2}, \dots$, each appearing once: the singlet $j = 0$ is the ground state with the energy $\hbar\omega$, the doublet $j = \frac{1}{2}$ is the first excited level with $2\hbar\omega$, the triplet $j = 1$ has $3\hbar\omega$, and so on. The degeneracies 1, 2, 3, … of the levels, which looked like an accident, are the sizes $2j + 1$ of the multiplets. Since the $J_i$ commute with $H$ (part (b)), all the states of a multiplet must have one energy.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The eigenvalue of $\vec J^{\,2}$ can be found for all states at once. With $J_-J_+ = \hbar^2\,a_L^\dagger a_L\,a_Ra_R^\dagger = \hbar^2\,N_L(N_R + 1)$,

<Formula label="The square of the hidden angular momentum" tex="\vec J^{\,2} = \hbar^2\left[N_L(N_R + 1) + \frac{(N_R - N_L)^2}{4} + \frac{N_R - N_L}{2}\right] = \hbar^2\,\frac{N_R + N_L}{2}\left(\frac{N_R + N_L}{2} + 1\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

which is $\hbar^2j(j+1)$ with $j = \frac{1}{2}(n_R + n_L)$ on every basis state. In this system $\vec J^{\,2}$ is a function of the Hamiltonian: $\vec J^{\,2} = \frac{\hbar^2}{4}\left[\left(\frac{H}{\hbar\omega}\right)^2 - 1\right]$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

Explain why, looking back, the constant $\alpha$ could not have been 1: the operator $L_z$ itself cannot be the $J_z$ of an angular momentum algebra that acts within the energy levels. This is why the hidden angular momentum is so well hidden.

The choice box is our addition.

<Hint>

List the eigenvalues of $L_z$ in the level with one quantum, and in the level with two. Compare with the values of $\hbar m$ in a multiplet with as many states.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-why" label="Why can $L_z$ not be the $J_z$ of the hidden algebra?" choices={["L_z does not commute with the Hamiltonian of the oscillator, so it is not conserved", "J₊ changes L_z by 2ħ, but a raising operator changes J_z by ħ", "the eigenvalues of L_z are not whole multiples of ħ, as those of a J_z must be", "L_z is not a Hermitian operator"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Within a multiplet the eigenvalues of $J_z$ go down in steps of $\hbar$, from $\hbar j$ to $-\hbar j$. Within an energy level of the oscillator the eigenvalues of $L_z$ go in steps of $2\hbar$.

<Step title="Compare the eigenvalues">

The level with $N$ quanta has $N + 1$ states. $L_z = \hbar(n_R - n_L)$ takes the values $N\hbar, (N-2)\hbar, \dots, -N\hbar$ there: moving one quantum from $L$ to $R$ changes $n_R - n_L$ by 2. A multiplet with $N + 1$ states has $j = \frac{N}{2}$ and needs the values $\frac{N}{2}\hbar, \left(\frac{N}{2} - 1\right)\hbar, \dots, -\frac{N}{2}\hbar$, in steps of $\hbar$. The first excited level shows it most clearly: its two states have $L_z = \pm\hbar$, but a doublet needs $\pm\frac{\hbar}{2}$. Only $\frac{1}{2}L_z$ fits, so $\alpha = \frac{1}{2}$.

</Step>

<Step title="What this means">

$L_z$ is the true angular momentum of the motion in the plane: it turns the $x$ and $y$ axes into each other, and its eigenvalues are whole multiples of $\hbar$. The hidden $\vec J$ is not that rotation and not a rotation of space at all. Its component $J_z$ is only half of $L_z$, its multiplets have half-integer $j$ for every odd $N$, and its other two components mix or compare the two oscillators (part (a)). Nobody looking for a rotation of the plane would have found it. What the three $J_i$ share with an angular momentum is only the algebra, and the algebra alone was enough to fix the sizes of the multiplets in Lecture 21.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the other choices">

$L_z$ is Hermitian and commutes with $H$ (both are built from $N_R$ and $N_L$), and its eigenvalues are whole multiples of $\hbar$. The eigenvalues of a $J_z$ need not be: half-integer multiples occur, as for spin one-half. What fails is the step: $[L_z, J_+] = 2\hbar\,J_+$, while the algebra demands $[J_z, J_+] = \hbar\,J_+$.

</Step>

</Solution>
