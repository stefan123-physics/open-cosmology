This lecture closes the chapter on spins with one of the deepest results in physics. It looks at the singlet state of two spins, states the claim of Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen (EPR) that physics must obey **local realism**, and shows with **Bell's inequality** that quantum mechanics, and nature, do not. It then begins angular momentum, with the algebra of vectors whose components are operators (transcript pp. 1–23; notes chapter 8, section 5, and chapter 9, section 1.1). (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## The singlet and its correlations

The **singlet** is the two-spin state with no total angular momentum (transcript p. 1):

<Formula label="Singlet state" tex="\psi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|+\rangle|-\rangle - |-\rangle|+\rangle\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.2)" />

Such states are easy to make. The neutral eta meson, a spinless particle (rest mass 547 MeV), sometimes decays into a muon and an antimuon, two spin-$\frac{1}{2}$ particles. If they fly apart with no orbital angular momentum, conservation of angular momentum leaves their spins in the singlet (transcript pp. 1–2; notes eq. (5.1)). Because the singlet is unchanged by rotations, it has the same form in the basis of spin up and down along **any** unit vector $\vec n$ (transcript p. 2; notes eq. (5.3)).

Let $P(\vec a, \vec b)$ be the probability of finding particle 1 up along $\vec a$ and particle 2 up along $\vec b$. Writing the singlet in the basis along $\vec a$ makes this easy: only the term with particle 1 up along $\vec a$ survives, leaving $\frac{1}{2}$ times the squared overlap of "up along $\vec b$" with "down along $\vec a$" (transcript pp. 2–3; notes eqs. (5.4)–(5.6)). The squared overlap of two spin states is the cosine squared of half the angle between their directions, and the angle between $\vec b$ and $-\vec a$ is $\pi - \theta_{ab}$ (transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Singlet correlation" tex="P(\vec a, \vec b) = \frac{1}{2}\cos^2\frac{\pi - \theta_{ab}}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\sin^2\frac{\theta_{ab}}{2}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (5.7)–(5.8)" />

Checks: for $\vec b = -\vec a$, $\theta_{ab} = \pi$ and $P = \frac{1}{2}$, since in half the pairs particle 1 is up along $\vec a$ and then particle 2 is certainly down along it. For $\hat z$ and $\hat x$, $P = \frac{1}{2}\sin^2 45° = \frac{1}{4}$ (transcript p. 4; notes eq. (5.9)). The two particles may be far apart, and each probability can be measured.

## Local realism

EPR's paper rested on two assumptions about measurement, together called local realism (transcript pp. 5–6; notes p. 17):

1. **Realism.** A measurement result corresponds to some element of reality: the value was a property the system already had. If a spin comes out up in a Stern–Gerlach apparatus, it was up all along; we just did not know.
2. **Locality.** A measurement here cannot depend on what is done at the same time at a distant place, the Moon say, since no signal could connect them.

Both sound reasonable, and Einstein insisted that physics must satisfy them. Zwiebach found it sad that the person who saw so deeply into nature became convinced of this, because both are wrong (transcript p. 6). Quantum mechanics has already given up the first: a particle in a superposition of up and down has no definite value before measurement. Attempts to restore it add **hidden variables**, unknown properties that would fix every result, and the surprise is that this can be tested (transcript pp. 6–7). Giving up the second does not let anyone send information faster than light (transcript p. 7).

## EPR's explanation, and where it works

For the singlet, if Alice measures along $z$ and finds up, Bob finds down along $z$, and the other way round. EPR would say there is nothing mysterious: half the pairs are made with particle 1 up and particle 2 down, half the other way, each with definite spins. Every probability for measurements along $z$ comes out right (transcript p. 8; notes p. 17).

Let each observer measure, once per particle, along either $z$ or $x$. EPR now give each particle a definite answer for both axes: a particle of type ($z+$, $x-$) gives up if measured along $z$ and down if measured along $x$. Take four kinds of pairs, 25% each, with particle 2 always opposite to particle 1 on both axes (transcript pp. 9–10; notes p. 18). Then $P(z+, z-) = \frac{1}{2}$ and $P(z+, x+) = \frac{1}{4}$, exactly the quantum answers (transcript pp. 10–11). With one or two axes, local realism cannot be told apart from quantum mechanics, and for a long time people thought the question was undecidable, a matter of philosophy (transcript p. 11).

## Bell's inequality

John Bell tried **three** directions, $\vec a$, $\vec b$, $\vec c$ (transcript p. 11). Now each particle has a definite answer for all three, giving eight types for particle 1, with particle 2 always opposite in every direction. Rather than fix the fractions, let $N_1, \dots, N_8$ be the numbers of pairs of each type, with $N = N_1 + \dots + N_8$ (transcript pp. 11–12; notes p. 18). In the order of the notes' table, particle 1 has the types

| Pairs | Particle 1 along $a$, $b$, $c$ | Pairs | Particle 1 along $a$, $b$, $c$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $N_1$ | $+$, $+$, $+$ | $N_5$ | $-$, $+$, $+$ |
| $N_2$ | $+$, $+$, $-$ | $N_6$ | $-$, $+$, $-$ |
| $N_3$ | $+$, $-$, $+$ | $N_7$ | $-$, $-$, $+$ |
| $N_4$ | $+$, $-$, $-$ | $N_8$ | $-$, $-$, $-$ |

and particle 2 the opposite signs. "Particle 2 up along $\vec b$" therefore means "particle 1 down along $\vec b$". For example, $P(\vec a, \vec b)$ counts the pairs with particle 1 up along $a$ and down along $b$: types 3 and 4. In the same way (transcript pp. 12–13):

<Formula label="Probabilities in a local realist model" tex="P(\vec a, \vec b) = \frac{N_3 + N_4}{N}, \qquad P(\vec a, \vec c) = \frac{N_2 + N_4}{N}, \qquad P(\vec c, \vec b) = \frac{N_3 + N_7}{N}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.10)" />

Since $N_3 + N_4 \le N_3 + N_4 + N_2 + N_7$ for any populations whatever, dividing by $N$ gives (transcript p. 13; notes eq. (5.11))

<Formula label="Bell's inequality" tex="P(\vec a, \vec b) \le P(\vec a, \vec c) + P(\vec c, \vec b)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.12)" />

Bell's achievement was to turn the philosophical assumption into an inequality between measurable probabilities, which quantum mechanics can be tested against (transcript p. 13). Inserting the singlet result (transcript pp. 13–14):

<Formula label="Quantum prediction in Bell's inequality" tex="\frac{1}{2}\sin^2\frac{\theta_{ab}}{2} \le \frac{1}{2}\sin^2\frac{\theta_{ac}}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\sin^2\frac{\theta_{cb}}{2}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.13)" />

Choose three directions in a plane with $\vec c$ between $\vec a$ and $\vec b$: $\theta_{ab} = 2\theta$ and $\theta_{ac} = \theta_{cb} = \theta$ (transcript p. 14; notes eq. (5.14)). The inequality becomes

<Formula label="The inequality for the planar choice" tex="\frac{1}{2}\sin^2\theta \le \sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.15)" />

For small $\theta$ the left side is about $\frac{1}{2}\theta^2$ and the right about $\frac{1}{4}\theta^2$: false. Writing $\sin^2\theta = 4\sin^2\frac{\theta}{2}\cos^2\frac{\theta}{2}$, the inequality is $2\cos^2\frac{\theta}{2} \le 1$, so it fails for every $0 < \theta < \pi/2$ and is an equality at $\pi/2$ (and trivially at $\theta = 0$) (transcript p. 14; notes p. 19).

So quantum mechanics contradicts local realism: no theory with definite hidden values and no influence at a distance can reproduce it (transcript p. 15). The decisive experiments, by Alain Aspect and others around 1980 to 1982, measured such correlations with entangled pairs and found the inequality violated, as quantum mechanics predicts (transcript p. 15). (Asked about David Bohm's hidden-variable theory, Zwiebach said that in his view no hidden-variable theory works at present; a student added that Bohm's is non-local, so it gives up the second assumption rather than the first, transcript pp. 15–16. Bell's theorem rules out only local hidden variables: Bohm's non-local theory does reproduce non-relativistic quantum mechanics, though many physicists find it unnatural, as Zwiebach did.) Other forms of Bell's inequality use expectation values or correlation functions, or games that an entangled pair can win more often than any classical strategy (transcript p. 17).

## Vectors whose components are operators

The rest of the course is about angular momentum. Orbital angular momentum is $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p$, with components such as $L_x = yp_z - zp_y$ (transcript p. 17; notes eq. (1.6)). (In the lecture $L_z$ was given as $xp_y - yp_z$; it is $xp_y - yp_x$.) Numbering the coordinates $x_1, x_2, x_3$ and momenta $p_1, p_2, p_3$, the canonical commutators are (transcript pp. 17–18)

<Formula label="Canonical commutators" tex="\left[x_i, p_j\right] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}, \qquad \left[x_i, x_j\right] = \left[p_i, p_j\right] = 0" cite="reading3" at="eq. (1.5)" />

Write $\vec r = x_1\vec e_1 + x_2\vec e_2 + x_3\vec e_3$, and similarly $\vec p$ and $\vec L$: vectors whose components are operators, with basis vectors that commute with everything (transcript p. 18; notes eq. (1.9)). Since components need not commute, the order is fixed once and for all by the definitions (transcript pp. 18–19):

<Formula label="Dot and cross products" tex="\vec a\cdot\vec b \equiv a_ib_i, \qquad \left(\vec a\times\vec b\right)_i \equiv \epsilon_{ijk}\,a_jb_k, \qquad \vec a^{\,2} \equiv \vec a\cdot\vec a" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (1.11)–(1.12)" />

with repeated indices summed. Familiar rules now get correction terms. Writing $a_ib_i = [a_i, b_i] + b_ia_i$ (transcript p. 19):

<Formula label="The dot product is not symmetric" tex="\vec a\cdot\vec b = \vec b\cdot\vec a + \left[a_i, b_i\right], \qquad \vec r\cdot\vec p = \vec p\cdot\vec r + 3i\hbar" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (1.14)–(1.16)" />

The $3$ comes from summing $[x_i, p_i] = i\hbar$ over the three directions (transcript pp. 19–20). For the cross product, flipping the indices $j$ and $k$ in the reordered term gives (transcript p. 20)

<Formula label="The cross product is not antisymmetric" tex="\left(\vec a\times\vec b\right)_i = -\left(\vec b\times\vec a\right)_i + \epsilon_{ijk}\left[a_j, b_k\right]" cite="reading3" at="eq. (1.18)" />

So $\vec r\times\vec r = 0$ and $\vec p\times\vec p = 0$, since their components commute; but $\vec L\times\vec L$ is not zero, because the components of $\vec L$ do not commute with each other (it turns out to be proportional to $\hbar\vec L$, a result for later) (transcript pp. 20–21). For $\vec r\times\vec p$ the extra term is $\epsilon_{ijk}\,i\hbar\,\delta_{jk}$, an antisymmetric symbol summed against a symmetric one, which vanishes: relabelling $j \leftrightarrow k$ shows it equals minus itself (transcript p. 21):

<Formula label="Angular momentum both ways" tex="\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p = -\vec p\times\vec r, \qquad L_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\,x_jp_k" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (1.20)–(1.24)" />

## L is perpendicular to r and p

Classically $\vec L$ is perpendicular to both $\vec r$ and $\vec p$. Quantum mechanically, $\vec r\cdot\vec L = \epsilon_{ijk}\,x_ix_jp_k$, and since $x_i$ and $x_j$ commute this is symmetric in $i, j$ against an antisymmetric $\epsilon$: zero (transcript p. 22; notes eq. (1.27)). For $\vec p\cdot\vec L = \epsilon_{ijk}\,p_ix_jp_k$ it is tempting to argue the same way in $i, k$, but an $x_j$ sits between the two momenta. It can be moved, because $[p_i, x_j] = -i\hbar\delta_{ij}$ is killed by the $\epsilon$; once the momenta are next to each other the symmetry argument works. Using $\vec L = -\vec p\times\vec r$ from the start puts the momenta together at once (transcript pp. 22–23; notes eq. (1.28)):

<Formula label="L is perpendicular to r and p" tex="\vec r\cdot\vec L = \vec p\cdot\vec L = 0" cite="reading3" at="eq. (1.29)" />

and in the same way $\vec L\cdot\vec r$ and $\vec L\cdot\vec p$ also vanish (transcript p. 23).

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="Correlations at 60°, and a Bell test at 30°">
<Problem>
(a) In the singlet, what is the probability that particle 1 is up along $\vec a$ and particle 2 up along $\vec b$ when the two directions are 60° apart? (b) In the planar configuration, evaluate both sides of Bell's inequality for $\theta = 30°$ and for $\theta = 120°$. (c) In a local realist model in which all eight types of pairs are equally common, what is $P(\vec a, \vec b)$, whatever the angles?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The 60° correlation">
$P = \frac{1}{2}\sin^2 30° = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{8} = 0.125$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) θ = 30°">
The left side is $\frac{1}{2}\sin^2 30° = 0.125$; the right side is $\sin^2 15° \approx 0.0670$. Since $0.125 > 0.0670$, the inequality is violated: quantum mechanics predicts nearly twice what local realism allows.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) θ = 120°">
The left side is $\frac{1}{2}\sin^2 120° = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{3}{4} = 0.375$; the right side is $\sin^2 60° = 0.75$. Here $0.375 \le 0.75$: no violation, as expected for $\theta > \pi/2$. (Here $\theta_{ab} = 240°$, which gives the same $\sin^2(\theta_{ab}/2)$ as the angle $120°$ between the two directions.)
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Equal populations">
With $N_i = N/8$, $P(\vec a, \vec b) = (N_3 + N_4)/N = \frac{2}{8} = 0.25$ for every pair of directions. Quantum mechanics instead makes the probability depend on the angle, from 0 for parallel axes to $\frac{1}{2}$ for opposite ones.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- In the singlet, $P(\vec a, \vec b) = \frac{1}{2}\sin^2(\theta_{ab}/2)$; it is $\frac{1}{2}$ for opposite axes and $\frac{1}{4}$ for perpendicular ones.
- EPR's local realism: results reveal pre-existing values, and distant actions cannot affect them. Models of that kind match the singlet for one or two measurement axes.
- With three axes, local realism implies Bell's inequality $P(\vec a, \vec b) \le P(\vec a, \vec c) + P(\vec c, \vec b)$; quantum mechanics violates it (for the planar choice, whenever $0 < \theta < \pi/2$), and so do experiments.
- For vectors of operators, $\vec a\cdot\vec b = a_ib_i$ and $(\vec a\times\vec b)_i = \epsilon_{ijk}a_jb_k$ in that order; then $\vec r\cdot\vec p = \vec p\cdot\vec r + 3i\hbar$, $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p = -\vec p\times\vec r$, and $\vec r\cdot\vec L = \vec p\cdot\vec L = 0$.
