After a closer look at coherent states, this lecture introduces **squeezed states**: Gaussians narrower in position (or in momentum) than the oscillator's ground state; photon states are left for the next lecture (transcript pp. 1–24; notes chapter 6, end of section 4 and sections 5.1–5.2). States are written as $\psi$ with labels instead of kets: $\psi_0$ is the ground state, $\psi_\alpha$ a coherent state. (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Coherent states as blobs

Recall $\psi_\alpha = e^{\alpha a^\dagger - \alpha^*a}\psi_0$, with $a\psi_\alpha = \alpha\psi_\alpha$, real part of $\alpha$ the mean position divided by $\sqrt{2}\,d_0$ and imaginary part the mean momentum times $d_0/\sqrt{2}\hbar$, and $\alpha$ rotating as $\alpha e^{-i\omega t}$ (transcript pp. 1–3). A coherent state is not an eigenstate of position, momentum or energy, so people draw it in the $\alpha$ plane as a small **blob** around the point $\alpha$, a rough picture of where measured values would fall; Wigner distributions make it precise, though not much clearer (transcript pp. 3, 7–9).

A rough argument from waves (transcript p. 4): a light wave $A\cos\omega t$ has phase $\phi = \omega t$ and energy $E = N\hbar\omega$ for $N$ photons; putting $\Delta t = \Delta\phi/\omega$ and $\Delta E = \hbar\omega\,\Delta N$ into $\Delta E\,\Delta t \sim \hbar/2$ gives $\Delta N\,\Delta\phi$ of order 1. Zwiebach calls this derivation pretty bad, since $\Delta t$ is not defined, and makes it more concrete with the blob. First, the number uncertainty (transcript pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="Number and its uncertainty in a coherent state" tex="\langle\hat N\rangle = |\alpha|^2, \qquad \langle\hat N^2\rangle = |\alpha|^2\left(1 + |\alpha|^2\right), \qquad \Delta N = |\alpha|" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (4.96)–(4.99)" />

So the length of $\alpha$ is $\Delta N$, while the mean number is its square. The position and momentum uncertainties are those of the ground state, $\Delta x = d_0/\sqrt{2}$ and $\Delta p = \hbar/(\sqrt{2}\,d_0)$, which in the scaled units of the $\alpha$ plane give a blob of size about $\frac{1}{2}$ to 1 in each direction (transcript pp. 6–8; notes eqs. (4.101)–(4.105)). Seen from the origin, a blob of size about 1 at distance $|\alpha|$ spans an angle $\Delta\phi \approx 1/|\alpha|$, so (transcript p. 7)

<Formula label="Number–phase uncertainty" tex="\Delta N\,\Delta\phi \approx 1" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (4.106)–(4.107)" />

This rough relation is taken seriously in quantum optics: a beam with a well-defined photon number has a poorly defined phase, and the reverse.

## Squeezing by a sudden change

Squeezed states have many modern applications, and by 2013 experimenters had become increasingly able to make them (transcript p. 9). Here is how one arises. An oscillator with mass $m_1$ and frequency $\omega_1$ sits in its ground state, with $\Delta x = \sqrt{\hbar/2m_1\omega_1}$ and $\Delta p = \sqrt{\hbar m_1\omega_1/2}$. At $t = 0$ the mass or spring changes suddenly to $m_2$, $\omega_2$ (transcript pp. 9–10; notes eqs. (5.1)–(5.4)). The wavefunction does not change at that instant, but it is no longer the ground state of the new Hamiltonian. Rewriting the same uncertainties in the new variables (transcript pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="Uncertainties just after the change" tex="\Delta x = e^{-\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m_2\omega_2}}, \qquad \Delta p = e^{\gamma}\sqrt{\frac{\hbar m_2\omega_2}{2}}, \qquad e^{\gamma} = \sqrt{\frac{m_1\omega_1}{m_2\omega_2}}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (5.5)–(5.6)" />

Their product is still $\hbar/2$, but for $\gamma > 0$ the position uncertainty is **squeezed** below the new ground state's value and the momentum uncertainty stretched above it. From the second Hamiltonian's point of view, the first ground state is a squeezed state (transcript p. 11). These are the values just after the change; the state then evolves under the new Hamiltonian (transcript p. 12).

## The Bogoliubov transformation

The particle is the same, so $\hat x$ and $\hat p$ are the same operators, written either with the old oscillators or the new ones. Zwiebach calls this a very strong physical assumption, to be judged by its results (transcript p. 13; notes eq. (5.7)). Equating the two expressions and solving (transcript pp. 13–14):

<Formula label="Bogoliubov transformation" tex="a_1 = a_2\cosh\gamma + a_2^\dagger\sinh\gamma, \qquad a_1^\dagger = a_2^\dagger\cosh\gamma + a_2\sinh\gamma" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.9)" />

The old annihilation operator is a mixture of the new annihilation **and creation** operators. This **Bogoliubov transformation** appears all over physics, for instance between the fields near and far from a black hole in the analysis of black-hole radiation (transcript p. 14). The inverse follows by $\gamma \to -\gamma$ (notes eq. (5.10)).

## The squeezed vacuum

The old ground state $\psi_0^{(1)}$ is killed by $a_1$, so $(a_2\cosh\gamma + a_2^\dagger\sinh\gamma)\psi_0^{(1)} = 0$ (transcript p. 15; notes eq. (5.12)). It is an even function, so it should contain only even states of the new oscillator, created in pairs. Try a Gaussian-like ansatz $N(\gamma)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}f(\gamma)\,a_2^\dagger a_2^\dagger\right)\psi_0^{(2)}$ (transcript pp. 15–16; notes eq. (5.14)). The $a_2^\dagger$ term commutes with the exponential. The $a_2$ term can be replaced by its commutator with the exponential, since $a_2$ kills the vacuum; that commutator is $-f\,a_2^\dagger$ times the exponential, because $[a_2, a_2^\dagger a_2^\dagger] = 2a_2^\dagger$ commutes with the exponent (transcript pp. 16–17). The two terms cancel when $f = \tanh\gamma$ (notes eq. (5.19)).

For the normalisation, the norm itself is hard to compute directly, but the overlap with the new vacuum is easy: every $a_2^\dagger$ meets the vacuum bra and gives zero, so $(\psi_0^{(2)}, \psi_0^{(1)}) = N(\gamma)$. Inserting position states turns this into an integral of the two Gaussians, which gives $1/\sqrt{\cosh\gamma}$ (transcript pp. 18–19; notes eqs. (5.21)–(5.24)). Forgetting where it came from, one oscillator then has the **squeezed vacuum** (transcript p. 20):

<Formula label="Squeezed vacuum" tex="\psi_\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\cosh\gamma}}\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\tanh\gamma\;a^\dagger a^\dagger\right)\psi_0" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.26)" />

with $\Delta x = e^{-\gamma}\sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}$ and $\Delta p = e^{\gamma}\sqrt{\hbar m\omega/2}$ (notes eqs. (5.5) and (5.28)). Being normalised, it must come from a unitary operator acting on the vacuum, and remarkably that operator is simple (transcript p. 21):

<Formula label="Squeezing operator" tex="\psi_\gamma = S(\gamma)\,\psi_0, \qquad S(\gamma) = \exp\left(-\frac{\gamma}{2}\left(a^\dagger a^\dagger - aa\right)\right)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (5.29)–(5.30)" />

Reordering $S(\gamma)$ to reach the form above is an interesting calculation that the course does not do. Squeezing and then displacing with $D(\alpha)$ gives the most general states used in quantum optics: in the $\alpha$ plane, the round blob is first flattened into an ellipse and then moved off, after which it rotates (transcript pp. 21–22).

<Callout kind="note" title="Squeezed light in gravitational-wave detectors">
Zwiebach mentions that Nergis Mavalvala at MIT works on this: injecting a squeezed vacuum into a gravitational-wave detector reduces the quantum uncertainty in the measured position of its mirrors (transcript p. 22). (Squeezed light was already used in the GEO600 detector from 2010, and after the lecture it became part of the LIGO detectors, from their 2019 observing run.)
</Callout>

## Infinite squeezing: position and momentum states

Let $\gamma \to \infty$ (transcript pp. 22–23). The normalisation goes to zero and $\tanh\gamma \to 1$, leaving $e^{-\frac{1}{2}a^\dagger a^\dagger}\psi_0$, which cannot be normalised. It is annihilated by $a + a^\dagger$, and so by $\hat x$: it is the position eigenstate at $x = 0$ (notes eqs. (5.31)–(5.32)). For $\gamma \to -\infty$ the sign flips and the state is annihilated by $a - a^\dagger$, hence by $\hat p$: the momentum eigenstate at $p = 0$. Coefficients beyond $\pm 1$ in front of $\frac{1}{2}a^\dagger a^\dagger$ would give states worse than any delta function. Adding a coherent displacement gives every position eigenstate explicitly (transcript p. 24):

<Formula label="Position eigenstate from oscillators" tex="\psi_x = \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}e^{-m\omega x^2/2\hbar}\,\exp\left(\sqrt{\frac{2m\omega}{\hbar}}\,x\,a^\dagger - \frac{1}{2}a^\dagger a^\dagger\right)\psi_0" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.36)" />

which satisfies $\hat x\psi_x = x\,\psi_x$ (notes eq. (5.34)): the oscillator's creation operators are flexible enough to build position eigenstates concretely.

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="Weakening a spring suddenly">
<Problem>
An oscillator in its ground state has its spring suddenly weakened so that, with the same mass, the frequency drops to a quarter, $\omega_2 = \omega_1/4$. Find $\gamma$, the squeeze in $\Delta x$, and the probabilities of finding the new oscillator in its levels 0, 2 and 4.
</Problem>
<Step title="Squeezing parameter">
$e^{\gamma} = \sqrt{m\omega_1/m\omega_2} = \sqrt{4} = 2$, so $\gamma = \ln 2$. Then $\Delta x$ is half the new ground state's value and $\Delta p$ twice its value. Also $\cosh\gamma = (2 + \frac{1}{2})/2 = \frac{5}{4}$ and $\tanh\gamma = (2 - \frac{1}{2})/(2 + \frac{1}{2}) = \frac{3}{5}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Level 0">
The amplitude is the normalisation $1/\sqrt{\cosh\gamma} = 2/\sqrt{5}$, so the probability of the new ground state is $\frac{4}{5} = 0.8$.
</Step>
<Step title="Levels 2 and 4">
Expanding the exponential, the next term is $-\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{3}{5}\,a^\dagger a^\dagger\psi_0 = -\frac{3}{10}\sqrt{2}\,\psi_2$, since $(a^\dagger)^2\psi_0 = \sqrt{2}\,\psi_2$. Its probability is $\frac{4}{5} \times \frac{18}{100} = 0.144$. The following term is $\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{3}{10}\right)^2(a^\dagger)^4\psi_0 = \frac{9}{200}\sqrt{24}\,\psi_4$, with probability $\frac{4}{5} \times \frac{81 \times 24}{40000} \approx 0.039$. Together these already account for about 98% of the probability, and only even levels appear.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A coherent state is a blob in the $\alpha$ plane; $\langle\hat N\rangle = |\alpha|^2$ and $\Delta N = |\alpha|$, giving the rough number–phase relation $\Delta N\,\Delta\phi \approx 1$.
- Suddenly changing an oscillator's mass or frequency leaves its old ground state with $\Delta x = e^{-\gamma}\sqrt{\hbar/2m_2\omega_2}$, $\Delta p = e^{\gamma}\sqrt{\hbar m_2\omega_2/2}$: a squeezed state, with $e^\gamma = \sqrt{m_1\omega_1/m_2\omega_2}$.
- Old and new oscillators are related by a Bogoliubov transformation, $a_1 = a_2\cosh\gamma + a_2^\dagger\sinh\gamma$.
- The squeezed vacuum is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{\cosh\gamma}}\exp(-\frac{1}{2}\tanh\gamma\,a^\dagger a^\dagger)\psi_0 = \exp(-\frac{\gamma}{2}(a^\dagger a^\dagger - aa))\psi_0$, containing only even levels.
- Squeezed light improves gravitational-wave detectors; infinite squeezing gives position or momentum eigenstates.
