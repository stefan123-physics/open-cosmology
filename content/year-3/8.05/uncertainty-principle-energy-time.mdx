This lecture proves the uncertainty principle exactly, with the uncertainty defined last time, finds when it becomes an equality, and then gives the subtler energy–time relation a precise meaning (transcript pp. 1–23; notes chapter 5, sections 2–3). (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## A quick example

Recall $\Delta A(\psi) = |(A - \langle A\rangle I)\psi|$, with $(\Delta A)^2 = \langle A^2\rangle - \langle A\rangle^2$. Take a spin in the state z+ and ask for the uncertainty of $S_x$ (transcript pp. 1–2). In the basis of z+ and z−, $S_x$ is $\hbar/2$ times the matrix that swaps the two components, so it turns z+ into $(\hbar/2)$ z−, which is orthogonal to z+: $\langle S_x\rangle = 0$. But $S_x^2 = (\hbar/2)^2I$, whose expectation value in any normalised state is $(\hbar/2)^2$. So (transcript p. 2)

<Formula label="Uncertainty of S_x in the state z+" tex="(\Delta S_x)^2 = \left(\frac{\hbar}{2}\right)^2 - 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta S_x = \frac{\hbar}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

## The uncertainty principle

For two Hermitian operators $A$ and $B$ and a normalised state $\psi$ (transcript p. 3):

<Formula label="The uncertainty principle" tex="(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2 \ge \left(\left\langle\frac{1}{2i}[A, B]\right\rangle\right)^2" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.14)" />

The uncertainties always refer to the state, even when $\psi$ is not written. The left side is real and not negative, so the expectation value on the right must be real. It is, because the operator $[A, B]/2i$ is Hermitian, even though $[A, B]$ is not: the adjoint of $AB - BA$ is $BA - AB$, and the conjugated $1/i$ supplies a second minus sign (transcript pp. 3–4; notes eq. (2.16)). The commutator $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$ shows the pattern: Hermitian operators, but a commutator that is $i$ times a real number. Taking square roots (transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="The uncertainty principle, square-root form" tex="\Delta A\,\Delta B \ge \left|\left\langle\frac{1}{2i}[A, B]\right\rangle\right|" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.17)" />

where the bars are an absolute value of a real number. This is precise, unlike the rough "approximately equal" statements met first. Why prove it? Because the proof tells when the inequality becomes an **equality**: which states have minimum uncertainty. If $A$ and $B$ commute, the bound is zero, which allows but does not guarantee zero uncertainties (transcript p. 5).

For $A = \hat x$ and $B = \hat p$, $[\hat x, \hat p]/2i = \hbar/2$ times the identity, whose expectation value in a normalised state is $\hbar/2$ (transcript p. 6):

<Formula label="Position and momentum" tex="\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.19)" />

A perfectly localised particle must then have infinite momentum uncertainty, and a momentum eigenstate is spread over all space. Asked whether a product of zero and infinity makes sense, Zwiebach suggests thinking of sequences of wavefunctions in which $\Delta x$ shrinks and $\Delta p$ grows (transcript p. 12).

## The proof

Define the two vectors whose lengths are the uncertainties (transcript p. 6):

<Formula label="The two vectors" tex="f = (A - \langle A\rangle I)\,\psi, \qquad g = (B - \langle B\rangle I)\,\psi, \qquad (\Delta A)^2 = (f, f), \quad (\Delta B)^2 = (g, g)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.20)–(2.21)" />

The Schwarz inequality $(f, f)(g, g) \ge |(f, g)|^2$ holds, with equality only if $f$ and $g$ are parallel. Splitting the complex number $(f, g)$ into its real and imaginary parts (transcript p. 7):

<Formula label="Schwarz applied" tex="(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2 \ge |(f, g)|^2 = \left(\text{Re}\,(f, g)\right)^2 + \left(\text{Im}\,(f, g)\right)^2" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.23)" />

Expanding, with the Hermitian operator $A - \langle A\rangle I$ moved across, the cross terms combine into a single product of expectation values (transcript pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="The inner product of f and g" tex="(f, g) = \langle AB\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle, \qquad (g, f) = \langle BA\rangle - \langle B\rangle\langle A\rangle" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (2.24)–(2.25)" />

Since $(g, f)$ is the conjugate of $(f, g)$, the imaginary part is half their difference divided by $i$, and the products of expectation values cancel (transcript p. 8):

<Formula label="The imaginary part" tex="\text{Im}\,(f, g) = \frac{1}{2i}\left((f, g) - (g, f)\right) = \left\langle\frac{1}{2i}[A, B]\right\rangle" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.26)" />

The real part is half the expectation value of the **anticommutator** $\{A, B\} = AB + BA$ of the shifted operators $A - \langle A\rangle I$ and $B - \langle B\rangle I$ (transcript p. 9; notes eq. (2.27)). Keeping both terms gives what some books call the generalised uncertainty principle (notes eq. (2.28)); Zwiebach knows of no case where the anticommutator term is useful. It is a square of a real number, so dropping it keeps the inequality, and that is the uncertainty principle (transcript p. 9).

## Minimum uncertainty

Equality needs two things (transcript pp. 10–11). First, Schwarz must be saturated: $g = \beta f$ for some complex number $\beta$. Second, the dropped term must vanish: $(f, g) + (g, f) = (\beta + \beta^*)(f, f) = 0$, so $\beta$ is purely imaginary, $\beta = i\lambda$ with $\lambda$ real (notes eqs. (2.29)–(2.30)). The condition on the state is (transcript p. 11)

<Formula label="Saturation condition" tex="(B - \langle B\rangle I)\,\psi = i\lambda\,(A - \langle A\rangle I)\,\psi, \qquad \lambda \text{ real}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.31)" />

For $\hat x$ and $\hat p$ in the position representation this becomes a differential equation for the wavefunction; finding its solutions, the minimum-uncertainty packets, was left to recitation. The constant is not free: taking the length of both sides gives $|\lambda| = \Delta B/\Delta A$ (transcript pp. 11–12; notes eq. (2.32)).

## Energy and time: the rough argument

Energy–time uncertainty is widely misunderstood, though (as Zwiebach quotes Griffiths) rarely in a way that leads to serious mistakes (transcript p. 13). A classical start: a wave pulse lasting a time $T$, with angular frequency $\omega$, contains $N = \omega T/2\pi$ full waves. Its ends cannot be read to better than about one wave, $\Delta N \approx 1$, so $\Delta\omega\,T \approx 2\pi$ (transcript pp. 13–14; notes eqs. (3.33)–(3.34)). For a photon, $E = \hbar\omega$, which turns this into $\Delta E\,T \approx 2\pi\hbar = h$, with $T$ the time the pulse takes to pass the detector (transcript p. 14; notes eq. (3.35)). The weak point is $T$: there is no Hermitian operator whose eigenvalues are times, so time cannot have an uncertainty in the sense defined last lecture (transcript p. 14).

## Energy and time: the precise version

Use the Hamiltonian $H$ as one operator and any observable $Q$ **with no explicit time dependence** as the other (transcript p. 15). The expectation value of $Q$ can still change, because the state changes. Differentiating $(\psi, Q\psi)$ and using the Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\partial\psi/\partial t = H\psi$ for both time derivatives (the one in the first entry comes out conjugated) gives (transcript pp. 15–17)

<Formula label="Rate of change of an expectation value" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\langle Q\rangle = \frac{i}{\hbar}\left\langle[H, Q]\right\rangle" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.39)" />

a result that should "stick in your mind", and which returns later in the Heisenberg picture. Putting $\langle[H, Q]\rangle = (\hbar/i)\,d\langle Q\rangle/dt$ into the uncertainty principle (transcript p. 17):

<Formula label="Energy uncertainty and rate of change" tex="\Delta H\,\Delta Q \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}\left|\frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt}\right|" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.41)" />

The ratio of $\Delta Q$ to the rate of change has the units of time: it is roughly the time for $\langle Q\rangle$ to change by one uncertainty, like a distance divided by a speed. Calling it $\Delta t$ (transcript p. 18):

<Formula label="Energy–time uncertainty" tex="\Delta t = \frac{\Delta Q}{\left|d\langle Q\rangle/dt\right|}, \qquad \Delta H\,\Delta t \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (3.42)–(3.43)" />

It is a precise statement, which the notes credit to Mandelstam and Tamm, though $\Delta t$ depends on which observable $Q$ is chosen, and there is no rule for the best one. A different precise version, met in the homework, takes $\Delta t$ to be the time for a state to become **orthogonal** to its initial self, the largest possible change; it gives $\Delta H\,\Delta t \ge h/4$ (notes eq. (3.44); the transcript on p. 19 reads "h bar over 4", but the bound is $h/4 = \pi\hbar/2$). A stationary state has $\Delta H = 0$ and never changes, consistent with an infinite $\Delta t$ (transcript pp. 18–19).

## Conservation of energy uncertainty

For an isolated system with a time-independent Hamiltonian, put $Q = H$ and then $Q = H^2$: both commutators vanish, since $H$ commutes with itself (transcript p. 20; notes eqs. (3.45)–(3.46)). So

<Formula label="Energy uncertainty is conserved" tex="\frac{d}{dt}(\Delta H)^2 = \frac{d}{dt}\langle H^2\rangle - \frac{d}{dt}\langle H\rangle^2 = 0" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (3.45)–(3.47)" />

This organises the picture of a **decay** (transcript pp. 20–22). An excited atomic state that decays cannot be a stationary state, so it has an energy spread $\Delta E$, related to its lifetime $\tau$ by roughly $\Delta E\,\tau \sim \hbar/2$, because some observable changes a lot during $\tau$. After the decay the atom is in its sharp ground state, so by conservation the emitted photon must carry the uncertainty: its frequency is spread by $\Delta\omega = \Delta E/\hbar$.

The best-known case is the **hyperfine transition** of hydrogen. The magnetic interaction between the proton and electron spins splits the ground state by about $5.88 \times 10^{-6}$ eV, and the transition emits a photon of wavelength 21.1 cm and frequency about 1420 MHz (transcript p. 23; notes p. 8). The upper level lives about 11 million years, about $3.5 \times 10^{14}$ s (a year is close to $\pi \times 10^7$ s; the notes round this to $3.4 \times 10^{14}$ s). Its energy spread is then only about $\hbar/\tau \approx 2 \times 10^{-30}$ eV (the notes use $\hbar/\tau$ for this order-of-magnitude estimate; a factor 2 does not matter here), a fraction of about $3 \times 10^{-25}$ of the photon energy: the line has essentially no natural broadening (notes pp. 8–9). (In the lecture Zwiebach quoted the lifetime as roughly 10 million years, and the transcript's figure for the relative width, of order $10^{-8}$, appears garbled; this page uses the notes' figures.)

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Why the 21 cm line is so useful">
Zwiebach calls the long hyperfine lifetime "a great break for astronomy and cosmology" (transcript p. 22). Because the 21 cm line is so sharp, any shift or width it shows comes from the motion of the gas, not from the atom. Radio astronomers use it to weigh the atomic hydrogen in galaxies and to measure how fast that gas orbits far beyond the visible stars, one of the main pieces of evidence for dark matter.
</Callout>

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="Saturating the bound with spin, and a natural line width">
<Problem>
(a) In the spin state z+, check the uncertainty principle for $S_x$ and $S_y$, using $[S_x, S_y] = i\hbar S_z$ and $\Delta S_y = \hbar/2$ (by the same argument as for $S_x$). Is it saturated? (b) An excited atomic level decays with lifetime $\tau = 2.0$ ns. Estimate its energy spread from $\Delta E \approx \hbar/(2\tau)$, with $\hbar = 6.58 \times 10^{-16}$ eV s, and compare it with a 2.0 eV transition.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The right-hand side">
$\frac{1}{2i}[S_x, S_y] = \frac{\hbar}{2}S_z$, and in z+, $\langle S_z\rangle = \hbar/2$. So the bound is $\frac{\hbar}{2} \cdot \frac{\hbar}{2} = \hbar^2/4$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) The left-hand side">
$\Delta S_x\,\Delta S_y = \frac{\hbar}{2} \cdot \frac{\hbar}{2} = \hbar^2/4$: the inequality holds as an **equality**. The state z+ is a minimum-uncertainty state for this pair.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Line width">
$\Delta E \approx 6.58 \times 10^{-16}/(2 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-9}) \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-7}$ eV, a fraction $1.6 \times 10^{-7}/2.0 \approx 8 \times 10^{-8}$ of the transition energy: small, but about $10^{17}$ times the relative width of the 21 cm line.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The uncertainty principle: $\Delta A\,\Delta B \ge |\langle[A, B]/2i\rangle|$, with $[A, B]/2i$ Hermitian; for position and momentum $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2$.
- Proof: Schwarz for $f = (A - \langle A\rangle)\psi$ and $g = (B - \langle B\rangle)\psi$; the imaginary part of $(f, g)$ gives the commutator, the real part (an anticommutator) is dropped.
- Equality requires $(B - \langle B\rangle)\psi = i\lambda(A - \langle A\rangle)\psi$ with real $\lambda$, and then $|\lambda| = \Delta B/\Delta A$.
- With no time operator, energy–time uncertainty comes from $d\langle Q\rangle/dt = (i/\hbar)\langle[H, Q]\rangle$: $\Delta H\,\Delta t \ge \hbar/2$ with $\Delta t = \Delta Q/|d\langle Q\rangle/dt|$.
- For a time-independent Hamiltonian $\Delta H$ is constant, so a decaying state hands its energy spread to the photon; hydrogen's 21 cm line, with an 11-million-year lifetime, is extremely sharp.
