With the multiplets of angular momentum in hand, this lecture connects them to wavefunctions and to three-dimensional problems. It introduces the spherical harmonics, separates the Schrödinger equation for a central potential into a **radial equation**, and draws the diagram that organises every such spectrum. It ends with the free particle in spherical coordinates and the idea of a phase shift (transcript pp. 1–23; notes chapter 9, sections 3, 4 and 5.1). (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Spherical harmonics

For orbital angular momentum the states are written $|l, m\rangle$, and only integer $l = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ occur (a homework result): half-integer angular momentum, such as spin, has no wavefunction of this kind (transcript pp. 1–2; notes p. 20). As differential operators on the angles, $\vec L^2$ is the angular part of the Laplacian found last time, and (transcript p. 2; notes eqs. (3.1)–(3.4))

<Formula label="Angular momentum as differential operators" tex="L_z = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}, \qquad L_\pm = \hbar\,e^{\pm i\phi}\left(i\cot\theta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi} \pm \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (3.3)–(3.4)" />

$L_z$ generates rotations about the $z$ axis, so it only changes $\phi$. (In the lecture $L_\pm$ was said as $L_x \pm iL_z$; it is $L_x \pm iL_y$, transcript p. 2.) The **spherical harmonics** $Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi)$ are the functions on the sphere with (transcript pp. 2–3)

<Formula label="Defining equations" tex="\vec L^2\,Y_{lm} = \hbar^2\,l(l+1)\,Y_{lm}, \qquad L_z\,Y_{lm} = \hbar m\,Y_{lm}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.10)" />

In quantum mechanics they are the wavefunctions of the states $|l, m\rangle$: $Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi) = \langle\theta, \phi|l, m\rangle$, with $|\theta, \phi\rangle$ a position state on the unit sphere, just as $\psi(x) = \langle x|\psi\rangle$ (transcript p. 3; notes eq. (3.6)). Splitting the completeness relation for position states into a radial and an angular part suggests the angular one, written with the **solid angle** element $d\Omega = \sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$ (transcript pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Completeness on the sphere" tex="\int d\Omega\;|\theta, \phi\rangle\langle\theta, \phi| = 1, \qquad \int d\Omega = \int_0^{\pi}\sin\theta\,d\theta\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi = \int_{-1}^{1}d(\cos\theta)\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (3.11)–(3.13)" />

The states $|l, m\rangle$ are orthonormal, as eigenstates of Hermitian operators with different eigenvalues; inserting the completeness relation gives the orthogonality of the spherical harmonics (transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="Orthonormality of the spherical harmonics" tex="\int d\Omega\;Y_{l'm'}^*(\theta, \phi)\,Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi) = \delta_{ll'}\,\delta_{mm'}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (3.16)" />

They can be built as the algebra suggests: $L_+$ kills $Y_{ll}$, a simple differential equation, and $L_-$ then gives the rest of the multiplet. Normalising them takes patience, so the lecture leaves the explicit forms to the textbooks (transcript pp. 4–5). The $\phi$ dependence is always simply $e^{im\phi}$ (notes eq. (3.18)).

## The radial equation

For a central potential, $H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2}r + \frac{1}{2mr^2}\vec L^2 + V(r)$ (transcript p. 5). Try $\psi_{Elm} = f_{Elm}(r)\,Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi)$. Then $\vec L^2$ acting on $Y_{lm}$ gives $\hbar^2l(l+1)$, the $Y_{lm}$ cancels, and the remaining equation involves $l$ but **not** $m$, since the Hamiltonian contains no $L_z$. So the radial function depends only on $E$ and $l$ (transcript pp. 5–6; notes eqs. (4.4)–(4.5)). Multiplying by $r$ suggests $u_{El} = rf_{El}$ (transcript p. 6):

<Formula label="Separated wavefunction" tex="\psi_{Elm}(\vec x) = \frac{u_{El}(r)}{r}\,Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (4.8)" />

<Formula label="Radial equation" tex="-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u_{El}}{dr^2} + V_{\text{eff}}(r)\,u_{El} = E\,u_{El}, \qquad V_{\text{eff}}(r) = V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2\,l(l+1)}{2mr^2}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (4.9)–(4.10)" />

The second term of $V_{\text{eff}}$ is the **centrifugal barrier**. The three-dimensional problem has become an infinite set of one-dimensional problems, one for each $l$, each with its own effective potential (transcript p. 7). The main difference from an ordinary one-dimensional problem is that $r$ runs from 0 to $\infty$, so the behaviour at $r = 0$ needs care (transcript pp. 7–8). A potential cannot depend on the $l$ of the state it acts on; magnetic fields, which split the $m$ levels, are handled later by perturbation theory (transcript p. 8). Normalisation is pleasantly simple: the angular integral gives 1 by orthonormality, and the $r^2$ of the volume element cancels the $1/r^2$ from $|u/r|^2$ (transcript pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Normalisation" tex="\int d^3x\,|\psi_{Elm}|^2 = \int_0^\infty dr\,|u_{El}(r)|^2 = 1" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (4.11)–(4.13)" />

so $u$ behaves like an ordinary one-dimensional wavefunction.

## The spectral diagram

The picture to keep in mind for any central potential: energy upwards, $l$ along the bottom, a short line for each bound state of the radial equation (transcript pp. 9–10; notes Figure 4). For each $l$ the radial equation is one-dimensional, so its bound states are **not** degenerate: the column above each $l$ holds single lines, $E_{1,l} < E_{2,l} < \dots$, the $n$-th solution with that $l$ (transcript p. 10; notes eq. (4.28)). Since the centrifugal barrier grows with $l$, the lowest line in each column rises with $l$ (transcript p. 10; notes p. 24).

Each line is one solution of the radial equation, but a whole **multiplet** of the Schrödinger equation: $2l + 1$ degenerate states, with $m = l, \dots, -l$. A line above $l = 0$ is one state, above $l = 1$ three, above $l = 2$ five, above $l = 3$ seven (transcript p. 11). The degeneracy is forced by the algebra: the multiplet's states are related by the $L_i$, which all commute with $H$ (transcript pp. 11–12). (In the lecture the energy of a multiplet was said not to depend on $l$; $m$ is meant, transcript p. 11.) Lines in **different** columns may also match; the diagram then signals extra degeneracy, which needs explaining (notes p. 24).

## Behaviour near the origin and far away

**Near $r = 0$**, normalisability alone would allow $u \to c \neq 0$. But for $l = 0$ that gives $\psi \approx c'/r$ near the origin, and since $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec x)$, the kinetic term would produce a delta function that nothing in the potential can cancel (transcript pp. 12–14; notes eqs. (4.15)–(4.17)). (A delta-function potential in three dimensions would be unacceptable anyway: it has infinitely many bound states, with energies going down to minus infinity, transcript p. 13.) So $u$ must vanish at the origin:

<Formula label="Condition at the origin" tex="\lim_{r\to 0}u_{El}(r) = 0" cite="reading2" at="eq. (4.14)" />

Assume the centrifugal barrier dominates as $r \to 0$, that is, $V$ is less singular than $1/r^2$ (true of any potential met in practice, transcript p. 18). Then the leading terms balance as $u'' = l(l+1)u/r^2$, and $u = r^s$ gives $s(s - 1) = l(l + 1)$, so $s = l + 1$ or $s = -l$ (transcript pp. 14–15). (The transcript has $s = 2 - l$ for the second root; it is $-l$, as the notes have.) The root $s = -l$ is ruled out, by the delta-function argument for $l = 0$ and by normalisation for $l \ge 1$ (transcript p. 15):

<Formula label="Behaviour at the origin" tex="u_{El} \simeq c\,r^{l+1}, \qquad f_{El} = \frac{u_{El}}{r} \simeq c\,r^{l} \qquad (r \to 0)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (4.20)–(4.23)" />

Only for $l = 0$ is the wavefunction non-zero at the origin; for higher $l$ the centrifugal barrier keeps the particle away from it (transcript p. 15).

**Far away**, if $V = 0$ beyond some radius or $rV(r) \to 0$, the whole effective potential can be dropped, leaving $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u'' = Eu$ (transcript pp. 15–16; notes eq. (4.24)). The hydrogen atom's $1/r$ potential does not satisfy this, and its wavefunctions carry an extra power of $r$ (transcript pp. 16–17). Otherwise (transcript p. 17):

<Formula label="Behaviour far away" tex="E < 0:\ \ u_{El} \sim \exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{2m|E|}}{\hbar}\,r\right); \qquad E > 0:\ \ u_{El} \sim e^{\pm ikr}, \quad k = \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (4.27)" />

(In the lecture the oscillating case was said with $E < 0$; it is $E > 0$, transcript p. 17.) Knowing both ends, one can sketch radial solutions as in 8.04: rising like $r^{l+1}$, perhaps oscillating, then decaying (transcript p. 17).

## The free particle in spherical coordinates

With $V = 0$ the states are usually labelled by three momentum components; here they are labelled by $E$, $l$ and $m$ instead, and these spherical waves are what scattering off a central potential is built from (transcript p. 18). The radial equation is (transcript pp. 18–19)

<Formula label="Free radial equation" tex="-\frac{d^2u_{El}}{dr^2} + \frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}\,u_{El} = k^2\,u_{El}, \qquad k^2 = \frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (5.29)–(5.30)" />

It looks like a one-dimensional problem with $E$ on the right, which would suggest quantised energies. But with $\rho = kr$ the energy disappears entirely, $-u'' + \frac{l(l+1)}{\rho^2}u = u$, so there is no quantisation (transcript p. 19; notes eq. (5.31)). A term in $1/x^2$ together with a constant term puts the equation in Bessel territory: the solution regular at the origin is a **spherical Bessel function** $j_l$, while the other kind, $n_l$, is singular there (transcript pp. 19–20):

<Formula label="Free particle" tex="u_{El} = r\,j_l(kr), \qquad \psi_{Elm} = j_l(kr)\,Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (5.32)–(5.33)" />

Its two limits agree with the general analysis (transcript pp. 20–21):

<Formula label="Limits of the spherical Bessel functions" tex="\rho\,j_l(\rho) \simeq \frac{\rho^{l+1}}{(2l+1)!!}\ \ (\rho \to 0), \qquad \rho\,j_l(\rho) \simeq \sin\left(\rho - \frac{l\pi}{2}\right)\ \ (\rho \to \infty)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.34)" />

(The transcript says $\rho^{l+2}$ in the first limit; it is $\rho^{l+1}$, which is what makes $u \propto r^{l+1}$, transcript p. 20.) The far phase is fixed, $-l\pi/2$, by the requirement that $u$ vanish at the origin (transcript p. 21).

## Phase shifts

If a potential vanishes beyond some radius, the solution far away is still a combination of sine and cosine, which can always be written as a sine with a shifted phase (transcript p. 21):

<Formula label="Phase shift" tex="u_{El} \simeq \sin\left(kr - \frac{l\pi}{2} + \delta_l(E)\right) \qquad (r \to \infty)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (5.36)" />

The **phase shift** $\delta_l(E)$ is zero without a potential and carries everything a distant observer can learn about the potential from scattering: roughly, an attractive potential pulls the wave in and gives a positive $\delta_l$, a repulsive one pushes it out and gives a negative one (transcript pp. 21–22; notes p. 25).

The lecture closed by setting up the **infinite spherical well**, $V = 0$ for $r < a$ and infinite beyond: inside, $u = rj_l(kr)$, and the energies are fixed by $j_l(ka) = 0$. Zwiebach warned that this most symmetric of potentials shows no pattern at all in its diagram, while the potential $\beta r^2$ shows enormous degeneracies that will need explaining (transcript p. 22).

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="Multiplets, a centrifugal barrier and a Bessel function">
<Problem>
(a) A central potential has exactly one bound radial state for each of $l = 0, 1, 2, 3$ and none for higher $l$. How many bound states does the particle have? (b) For an electron ($\hbar^2/2m_e = 3.81$ eV Å²), at what radius does the centrifugal barrier $\hbar^2l(l+1)/2m_er^2$ equal 13.6 eV, the binding energy of hydrogen, for $l = 1$ and for $l = 2$? (c) For $l = 1$, $\rho\,j_1(\rho) = \frac{\sin\rho}{\rho} - \cos\rho$. Compare it with $\rho^2/3$ at $\rho = 0.1$ and with $\sin(\rho - \pi/2)$ at $\rho = 20$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Counting states">
Each line is a multiplet of $2l + 1$ states: $1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16$ bound states, on four lines of the diagram (four distinct energies unless lines in different columns happen to coincide).
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Where the barrier bites">
$r = \sqrt{3.81\,l(l+1)/13.6}$ Å. For $l = 1$: $r = \sqrt{0.560} \approx 0.75$ Å. For $l = 2$: $r = \sqrt{1.68} \approx 1.30$ Å. Both are of the order of the Bohr radius, 0.53 Å: inside such distances the barrier outweighs typical atomic energies, and the larger $l$, the farther out it keeps the electron.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Small ρ">
$\sin(0.1)/0.1 = 0.998334$ and $\cos(0.1) = 0.995004$, so $\rho\,j_1 = 0.003330$, against $\rho^2/3 = 0.003333$ ($(2l+1)!! = 3$): agreement to 0.1%.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Large ρ">
$\sin(20)/20 = 0.0456$ and $\cos 20 = 0.4081$, so $\rho\,j_1 = -0.3624$, against $\sin(20 - \pi/2) = -\cos 20 = -0.4081$. The difference, $0.0456$, is the $\sin\rho/\rho$ term, which dies away like $1/\rho$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The spherical harmonics $Y_{lm} = \langle\theta, \phi|l, m\rangle$ satisfy $\vec L^2Y_{lm} = \hbar^2l(l+1)Y_{lm}$, $L_zY_{lm} = \hbar mY_{lm}$ and $\int d\Omega\,Y_{l'm'}^*Y_{lm} = \delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'}$; only integer $l$ occurs.
- For a central potential, $\psi = \frac{u_{El}}{r}Y_{lm}$, and $u$ obeys a one-dimensional equation with $V_{\text{eff}} = V + \hbar^2l(l+1)/2mr^2$, independent of $m$, normalised by $\int_0^\infty|u|^2dr = 1$.
- The spectral diagram: single lines in each $l$ column, each line a multiplet of $2l + 1$ states.
- $u \propto r^{l+1}$ near the origin; far away $u$ decays exponentially ($E < 0$) or oscillates ($E > 0$). The free particle has $u = rj_l(kr) \to \sin(kr - l\pi/2)$, and a short-range potential adds a phase shift $\delta_l$.
