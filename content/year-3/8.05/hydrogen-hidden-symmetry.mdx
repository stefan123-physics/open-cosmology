The last lecture of the course solves the hydrogen atom again, this time by algebra alone. The key is the quantum Runge–Lenz vector, a second conserved vector besides angular momentum, which together with $\vec L$ forms two hidden angular momenta. Their quantization gives the energy levels and their degeneracies at once (transcript pp. 1–19; notes chapter 10, section 7, pp. 9–16). Charges are in Gaussian units. (MIT's material for this lecture is the recorded lecture and Zwiebach's notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## What needs explaining

Two facts to start from. First, two angular momenta add as $j_1\otimes j_2 = (j_1 + j_2)\oplus(j_1 + j_2 - 1)\oplus\dots\oplus|j_1 - j_2|$, the product states reorganising into multiplets of the total (transcript p. 1). (The transcript calls the product states the "coupled" basis at that point; they are the uncoupled basis, transcript p. 1.) Second, hydrogen's spectrum (transcript p. 2; notes eqs. (7.1)–(7.3)):

<Formula label="Hydrogen spectrum" tex="E_n = -\frac{e^2}{2a_0}\,\frac{1}{n^2}, \qquad l = 0, 1, \dots, n - 1, \qquad n^2 \text{ states at level } n" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (7.1)–(7.2)" />

Level 1 has one state, level 2 has $1 + 3 = 4$, level 3 has $1 + 3 + 5 = 9$ (transcript p. 2). Rotational symmetry only explains the $2l + 1$ states of each multiplet, not why multiplets with different $l$ share an energy.

## The quantum Runge–Lenz vector

For $H = \frac{p^2}{2m} - \frac{e^2}{r}$, the classical vector $\frac{1}{me^2}\vec p\times\vec L - \frac{\vec r}{r}$ is conserved and points along the major axis of the orbit (transcript p. 2). Quantum mechanically $\vec p\times\vec L$ is not Hermitian, since the Hermitian conjugate of a cross product of non-commuting Hermitian operators is minus the product in the other order. Averaging the two orders gives a Hermitian operator, which reduces to the classical one if $\vec L$ and $\vec p$ commute (transcript pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Quantum Runge–Lenz vector" tex="\vec R = \frac{1}{2me^2}\left(\vec p\times\vec L - \vec L\times\vec p\right) - \frac{\vec r}{r}, \qquad \left[H, \vec R\right] = 0" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (7.6)–(7.7)" />

Its conservation is a homework computation, challenging but good practice (transcript p. 3). Using $\vec p\times\vec L = -\vec L\times\vec p + 2i\hbar\vec p$, it can also be written with one order only, $\vec R = \frac{1}{me^2}(\vec p\times\vec L - i\hbar\vec p) - \frac{\vec r}{r}$ (transcript pp. 3–4; notes eqs. (7.8)–(7.9)). Another computation gives its square (transcript p. 4):

<Formula label="R squared" tex="\vec R^2 = 1 + \frac{2H}{me^4}\left(\vec L^2 + \hbar^2\right)" cite="reading2" at="eq. (7.10)" />

The $\hbar^2$ has no classical counterpart, and the order of $H$ does not matter since it commutes with $\vec L$ (transcript p. 4).

## R and L together

**$\vec R$ is perpendicular to $\vec L$.** In $\vec R\cdot\vec L$, the terms $\vec r\cdot\vec L$ and $\vec p\cdot\vec L$ vanish. The remaining one, $(\vec p\times\vec L)\cdot\vec L = \epsilon_{ijk}p_jL_kL_i$, is zero for a subtler reason than symmetry in $k$ and $i$, since the $L$'s do not commute: moving the index gives $p_j(\vec L\times\vec L)_j = i\hbar\,\vec p\cdot\vec L = 0$ (transcript pp. 4–5; notes eqs. (7.11)–(7.14)). So $\vec R\cdot\vec L = 0$, and also $\vec L\cdot\vec R = 0$, shown below.

**$\vec R$ is a vector under rotations.** A vector $\vec V$ under rotations, $[L_i, V_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}V_k$, satisfies, after relabelling indices and contracting two epsilons (transcript pp. 6–7),

<Formula label="Vectors under rotations, in vector form" tex="\vec L\times\vec V + \vec V\times\vec L = 2i\hbar\,\vec V" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (7.15)–(7.17)" />

$\vec R$ is built from $\vec p$, $\vec L$ and $\vec r$ by cross products, so it is a vector under rotations: $[L_i, R_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}R_k$ and $\vec L\times\vec R + \vec R\times\vec L = 2i\hbar\vec R$ (transcript p. 7; notes eqs. (7.18)–(7.19)). (The transcript has $i\hbar\vec R$ on the right at that point; the factor 2 belongs there.) With equal indices the epsilon vanishes, so $L_i$ and $R_i$ commute and $\vec L\cdot\vec R = \vec R\cdot\vec L = 0$ (transcript p. 7).

## R × R by an argument, not a calculation

The remaining commutator, $[R_i, R_j]$, that is $\vec R\times\vec R$, would take a day by brute force (transcript p. 8). Instead, argue (transcript pp. 8–10):

- **It must be conserved.** If $S_1$ and $S_2$ commute with $H$, so does $[S_1, S_2]$, by the Jacobi identity $[[S_1, S_2], H] + [[S_2, H], S_1] + [[H, S_1], S_2] = 0$. Two conserved quantities give more by commuting them (transcript p. 9; notes eqs. (7.20)–(7.22)).
- **It must be a vector built from $\vec L$, $\vec R$ and $\vec L\times\vec R$**, times conserved scalars, since these are the only conserved vectors (transcript p. 9).
- **Parity decides.** Under $\vec r \to -\vec r$, $\vec p \to -\vec p$: $\vec L$ is unchanged and $\vec R$ changes sign. So $\vec R\times\vec R$ is unchanged, which excludes $\vec R$ and $\vec L\times\vec R$ and leaves only $\vec L$ (transcript p. 10; notes eqs. (7.25)–(7.27)).

Zwiebach credited the argument to Julian Schwinger, whose idiosyncratic quantum mechanics book finds a trick for every calculation, and who, in Zwiebach's words, invented the trick of using three-dimensional angular momentum for the two-dimensional oscillator (transcript pp. 9–10). A short calculation, shown neither in the lecture nor in the notes, then fixes the scalar (transcript p. 11):

<Formula label="R × R" tex="\vec R\times\vec R = i\hbar\left(-\frac{2H}{me^4}\right)\vec L" cite="reading2" at="eq. (7.28)" />

All the algebra is now known: $\vec L^2$, $\vec R^2$, $\vec L\cdot\vec R$, and the commutators of $\vec L$ with $\vec L$, $\vec L$ with $\vec R$, and $\vec R$ with $\vec R$ (transcript p. 11).

## Working on one energy level

Consider a subspace of degenerate states at one energy, perhaps a single state, perhaps several; this assumes nothing physical (transcript pp. 11–12). On it every operator acts within the subspace, and $H$, which commutes with $\vec L$ and $\vec R$, can be replaced by its value. Write that value, without knowing it, as (transcript p. 12)

<Formula label="The energy of the level" tex="H = -\frac{me^4}{2\hbar^2\nu^2}, \qquad \nu \in \mathbb{R}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (7.29)" />

Calling it $\nu$ rather than $n$ is deliberate: nothing yet says it is an integer (transcript pp. 12–13). Then $-\frac{2H}{me^4} = \frac{1}{\hbar^2\nu^2}$, and the relations become (transcript p. 13)

<Formula label="Algebra on the level" tex="\left(\hbar\nu\vec R\right)\times\left(\hbar\nu\vec R\right) = i\hbar\,\vec L, \qquad \vec L^2 + \hbar^2\nu^2\vec R^2 = \hbar^2\left(\nu^2 - 1\right)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (7.31)–(7.32)" />

So $\hbar\nu\vec R$ has the units of angular momentum and behaves almost like one (transcript pp. 13–14). Commutators of Hermitian operators are anti-Hermitian, which is why an $i$ always appears on the right (transcript p. 14).

## Two hidden angular momenta

Define (transcript pp. 14–15)

<Formula label="Two angular momenta" tex="\vec J_1 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\vec L + \hbar\nu\vec R\right), \qquad \vec J_2 = \frac{1}{2}\left(\vec L - \hbar\nu\vec R\right); \qquad \vec L = \vec J_1 + \vec J_2, \quad \hbar\nu\vec R = \vec J_1 - \vec J_2" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (7.33)–(7.34)" />

(When first defining $\vec J_1$ the factor $\frac{1}{2}$ was not said aloud; it is there for both, transcript p. 14.)

- **They commute.** In $[J_{1i}, J_{2j}]$ the $\vec L$–$\vec L$ and $\vec R$–$\vec R$ terms cancel, using the algebra above, and so do the two mixed terms, using $[L_i, R_j] = i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}R_k$ (transcript p. 15; notes eq. (7.35)).
- **They are angular momenta.** $\vec J_{1,2}\times\vec J_{1,2} = \frac{1}{4}(\vec L \pm \hbar\nu\vec R)\times(\vec L \pm \hbar\nu\vec R)$ uses $\vec L\times\vec L = i\hbar\vec L$, $(\hbar\nu\vec R)\times(\hbar\nu\vec R) = i\hbar\vec L$ and $\vec L\times\vec R + \vec R\times\vec L = 2i\hbar\vec R$, and comes out as $i\hbar\vec J_{1,2}$ (transcript p. 16; notes eqs. (7.36)–(7.37)).
- **They have equal size.** $\vec R\cdot\vec L = 0$ means $(\vec J_1 + \vec J_2)\cdot(\vec J_1 - \vec J_2) = 0$, and since $\vec J_1$ and $\vec J_2$ commute, $\vec J_1^2 = \vec J_2^2$ (transcript pp. 16–17; notes eq. (7.38)).

And that size is fixed by the energy (transcript p. 17):

<Formula label="The size of the hidden angular momenta" tex="\vec J_1^2 = \vec J_2^2 = \frac{1}{4}\left(\vec L^2 + \hbar^2\nu^2\vec R^2\right) = \frac{1}{4}\hbar^2\left(\nu^2 - 1\right) = \hbar^2\,j(j+1)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (7.39)–(7.40)" />

## The spectrum

Solving for $\nu$ (transcript pp. 17–18):

<Formula label="Quantization of the energy" tex="\nu^2 = 1 + 4j(j+1) = (2j+1)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nu = 2j + 1 = 1, 2, 3, \dots \quad \text{for } j = 0, \tfrac{1}{2}, 1, \dots" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (7.41)–(7.42)" />

The quantization of angular momentum has quantized the energy, without a differential equation: $\nu$ is the principal quantum number $n$. Every $j$, including half-integers, appears, even though no spin was ever put into this hydrogen atom (transcript p. 18). (The algebra only shows which $j$ are possible; that hydrogen uses all of them is an instance of "whatever is possible is compulsory", notes p. 15.)

Each level carries the $j$ multiplet of $\vec J_1$ and the $j$ multiplet of $\vec J_2$, so it is the tensor product with basis $|j, m_1\rangle\otimes|j, m_2\rangle$, $(2j + 1)^2 = n^2$ states (transcript p. 18; notes eq. (7.43)). Since $\vec L = \vec J_1 + \vec J_2$ is their sum, the addition rule gives the orbital multiplets directly (transcript p. 18):

<Formula label="The multiplets of a hydrogen level" tex="j\otimes j = 2j\oplus(2j - 1)\oplus\dots\oplus 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathcal{H}_n = (l = n - 1)\oplus(l = n - 2)\oplus\dots\oplus(l = 0)" cite="reading2" at="eqs. (7.44)–(7.46)" />

exactly the observed pattern. With that, the hydrogen atom, and 8.05, were done (transcript pp. 18–19).

## Worked example

<WorkedExample title="Inside hydrogen's n = 2 and n = 4 levels">
<Problem>
(a) For $n = 4$, give $j$, check $\vec J_1^2 = \frac{1}{4}\hbar^2(\nu^2 - 1)$ against $\hbar^2j(j+1)$, list the $l$ multiplets, count the states, and give the energy in eV (using $e^2/2a_0 = 13.6$ eV). (b) Using $\hbar^2\nu^2\vec R^2 = \hbar^2(\nu^2 - 1) - \vec L^2$, find the value of $\vec R^2$ in the ground state and in the $n = 2$, $l = 1$ states, and compare with the classical meaning of $|\vec R|$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) n = 4">
$j = \frac{n - 1}{2} = \frac{3}{2}$. Then $\frac{1}{4}(16 - 1) = \frac{15}{4}$ and $j(j+1) = \frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{5}{2} = \frac{15}{4}$: they agree. $\frac{3}{2}\otimes\frac{3}{2} = 3\oplus 2\oplus 1\oplus 0$, so $l = 3, 2, 1, 0$ with $7 + 5 + 3 + 1 = 16 = 4^2$ states, at $E = -13.6/16 = -0.85$ eV.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Ground state">
$\nu = 1$ and $l = 0$: $\vec R^2 = (1 - 1 - 0)/1 = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) n = 2, l = 1">
$\nu = 2$ and $\vec L^2 = 2\hbar^2$: $\vec R^2 = (4 - 1 - 2)/4 = \frac{1}{4}$; for $n = 2$, $l = 0$ it is $\frac{3}{4}$. Classically $|\vec R|$ is the eccentricity: the ground state is like a circular orbit, and at fixed energy, less angular momentum means a more eccentric orbit. (Because of the $\hbar^2$ in $\vec R^2$, an $l = 0$ state has $\vec R^2 = 1 - 1/n^2$, never the classical 1 of a straight-line orbit.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Hydrogen's $n^2$-fold degeneracy, with $l$ from 0 to $n - 1$ at each level, goes beyond rotational symmetry.
- The Hermitian Runge–Lenz vector $\vec R = \frac{1}{2me^2}(\vec p\times\vec L - \vec L\times\vec p) - \frac{\vec r}{r}$ is conserved, perpendicular to $\vec L$, a vector under rotations, with $\vec R^2 = 1 + \frac{2H}{me^4}(\vec L^2 + \hbar^2)$ and, by Jacobi and parity, $\vec R\times\vec R = i\hbar(-\frac{2H}{me^4})\vec L$.
- On a level with $H = -\frac{me^4}{2\hbar^2\nu^2}$, $\vec J_{1,2} = \frac{1}{2}(\vec L \pm \hbar\nu\vec R)$ are commuting angular momenta with $\vec J_1^2 = \vec J_2^2 = \frac{1}{4}\hbar^2(\nu^2 - 1)$, so $\nu = 2j + 1 = n$.
- Each level is $j\otimes j$, $n^2$ states, which as multiplets of $\vec L = \vec J_1 + \vec J_2$ are $l = n - 1, \dots, 0$.
