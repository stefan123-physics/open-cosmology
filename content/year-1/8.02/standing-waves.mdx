Session 30 has two halves: waves that stand still, and a careful derivation of how Maxwell's equations make [light](/courses/8-02/maxwell-equations-and-em-waves) (slides 1, 6, 13).

## Travelling waves, again

A sine wave $y = y_0\sin(kx - \omega t)$ travels in the $+x$ direction with wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/k$, period $T = 1/f$, angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$ and speed $v = \omega/k = \lambda f$; with $kx + \omega t$ instead it travels towards $-x$ (slides 2–5).

## Standing waves

Add two equal waves travelling in opposite directions (slide 7):

<Formula label="Standing wave" tex="E_0\sin(kx - \omega t) + E_0\sin(kx + \omega t) = 2E_0\sin kx\cos\omega t" cite="reading2" at="slide 7" />

The result no longer travels. Every point oscillates in time as $\cos\omega t$, with an amplitude $2E_0\sin kx$ that depends on where it is. At the **nodes**, where $\sin kx = 0$, that is $x = n\lambda/2$, the field is always zero; halfway between, at the **antinodes**, it swings with the full amplitude $2E_0$ (course notes, Section 13.5, p. 13).

Standing waves appear wherever waves bounce back and forth in a resonating system: musical instruments, microwave ovens, and the wind-driven oscillations of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge (slides 8–9).

**Standing electromagnetic waves.** With electromagnetic waves the notes take $E_y = E_0[\cos(kx - \omega t) - \cos(kx + \omega t)]$ and $B_z = B_0[\cos(kx - \omega t) + \cos(kx + \omega t)]$, (the left-moving wave's $\vec E$ is reversed so that $\vec E \times \vec B$ points along $-x$, Eq. 13.5.2), which give (Section 13.5, pp. 12–13)

<Formula label="Standing electromagnetic wave" tex="E_y = 2E_0\sin kx\sin\omega t, \qquad B_z = 2B_0\cos kx\cos\omega t" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (13.5.6)–(13.5.7), pp. 12–13" />

The nodal planes of $\vec E$, at $x = n\lambda/2$, are the antinodal planes of $\vec B$, and the other way round: the two patterns are shifted by a quarter wavelength, and in time they are a quarter period out of step (pp. 13–14). This is the same kind of pattern as the slides' sine form, with the same nodes for $\vec E$; the two differ only in the choice of the origin of time. A wave reflecting from a perfect conductor makes such a pattern, with a node of $\vec E$ at the conductor's surface, where the field must vanish; working this out, and the surface current it needs, is the session's group problem (slide 28), left for the problem pages.

## From Maxwell's equations to the wave equation

Take a wave travelling along $x$ with $\vec E$ along $y$ and $\vec B$ along $z$ (course notes, Section 13.4, p. 6; slides 18–22).

**Faraday's law** around a thin rectangle of height $\ell$ and width $dx$ in the $xy$-plane: the line integral is $[E_y(x + dx) - E_y(x)]\,\ell$ and the flux through it changes at $\ell\,dx\,\partial B_z/\partial t$, so (pp. 6–7; slide 22)

<Formula label="From Faraday's law" tex="\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.4.5), p. 7" />

**The Ampère–Maxwell law** around a thin rectangle in the $xz$-plane, with no current: $[B_z(x) - B_z(x + dx)]\,\ell = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\ell\,dx\,\partial E_y/\partial t$, so (pp. 7–8; slide 20)

<Formula label="From the Ampère–Maxwell law" tex="-\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.4.9), p. 8" />

Differentiate the first in $x$, swap the order of the mixed derivatives, and use the second (p. 8; slides 23, 25):

<Formula label="Wave equation" tex="\frac{\partial^2 E_y}{\partial x^2} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial^2 E_y}{\partial t^2}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 B_z}{\partial x^2} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial^2 B_z}{\partial t^2}" cite="reading2" at="slides 23 and 25" />

**Any shape travels.** Put in $E_y = f(x - vt)$: the left side is $f''$ and the right side $\mu_0\varepsilon_0 v^2 f''$, so this works for any function $f$ provided $v^2 = 1/\mu_0\varepsilon_0$ (slide 24; Section 13.4.1, pp. 9–10). The wave equation is linear, so solutions add: electromagnetic waves obey superposition, which is exactly what made the standing wave (p. 10).

**Amplitudes.** With $E_y = E_0 f(x - vt)$ and $B_z = B_0 f(x - vt)$, the Faraday relation gives $E_0 f' = vB_0 f'$ (slide 27; Eq. 13.4.26, p. 11):

<Formula label="Ratio of the amplitudes" tex="E_0 = vB_0 = cB_0" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.4.26), p. 11" />

$E_y$ and $B_z$ are "the same" wave with different amplitudes (slide 27), in phase (slide 16; p. 11).

<WorkedExample title="Standing waves in a microwave oven">
<Problem>
A microwave oven runs at 2.45 GHz. (a) Find the wavelength and the spacing of the nodes of a standing wave inside. (b) If the oven's field has $E_0 = 1000$ V/m in each travelling wave, what is the largest field at an antinode, and the amplitude $B_0$ of each wave?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Wavelength and nodes">
$\lambda = c/f = (2.998 \times 10^8)/(2.45 \times 10^9) \approx 0.122$ m, so nodes are $\lambda/2 \approx 6.1$ cm apart: cold spots, which is why ovens have turntables.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Fields">
At an antinode the two waves add to $2E_0 = 2000$ V/m. Each wave has $B_0 = E_0/c = 1000/(2.998 \times 10^8) \approx 3.3 \times 10^{-6}$ T.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Standing waves and the early universe">
A box of radiation is full of standing electromagnetic waves, one for each way a whole number of half wavelengths fits. In 8.286, counting these standing waves and giving each the share of thermal energy that quantum mechanics allows leads to the black-body law (the classical share would give the nonsense of infinite energy), which describes the radiation that filled the early universe and survives today as the cosmic microwave background.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Two opposite travelling waves make a standing wave $2E_0\sin kx\cos\omega t$, with nodes $\lambda/2$ apart.
- In a standing electromagnetic wave, $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ nodes are a quarter wavelength apart.
- Faraday and Ampère–Maxwell on thin rectangles give $\partial E_y/\partial x = -\partial B_z/\partial t$ and $-\partial B_z/\partial x = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial E_y/\partial t$, hence the wave equation with $v = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = c$.
- Any $f(x - vt)$ solves it; the amplitudes satisfy $E_0 = cB_0$.
