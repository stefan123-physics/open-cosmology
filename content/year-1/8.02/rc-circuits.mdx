Session 12 is a lab session: Experiment 4 first measures voltage, current and resistance, then watches a capacitor charge and discharge through a resistor (slides 1, 8, 22). It builds on [DC circuits](/courses/8-02/dc-circuits).

## Measuring voltage, current and resistance

Every meter disturbs the circuit it measures, so it is built to disturb it as little as possible (course notes, Section 7.5, p. 8; slides 4–7):

- A **voltmeter** measures the potential difference across an element, so it is connected **in parallel** with it. It must draw almost no current: an ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance (slide 5; p. 8).
- An **ammeter** measures the current through an element, so it is connected **in series**. The current must not lose much voltage crossing it: an ideal ammeter has zero resistance (slide 6; p. 8).
- An **ohmmeter** applies a voltage across an element, measures the current that flows and divides; it is connected in parallel with the element, which must not be powered: an ohmmeter does not work on a resistor connected to a battery (slide 7). (Disconnecting the element from the rest of the circuit also avoids measuring other resistors in parallel with it: the page's own advice.)

The notes add practical details (Section 7.5, pp. 8–9): the course's multimeter has a resistance of 1 Ω on its 250 mA range, so at full scale it drops 0.25 V; an ammeter becomes a voltmeter with a large resistor in series; and resistor values are marked with a colour code.

## Charging a capacitor

A capacitor $C$, initially uncharged, is connected through a resistor $R$ to a battery of emf $\varepsilon$ at time $t = 0$ (course notes, Section 7.6.1, pp. 9–10; slides 11–12). At first the empty capacitor has no voltage across it, so the current starts at $I_0 = \varepsilon/R$. With the current flowing towards the positive plate, $I = +dq/dt$ (slide 10), and Kirchhoff's loop rule gives

<Formula label="Charging: the loop equation" tex="\varepsilon - \frac{q}{C} - R\,\frac{dq}{dt} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (7.6.3), p. 10" />

As charge builds up, it opposes more charge, and the current falls. Separating variables, $dq/(q - C\varepsilon) = -dt/RC$, and integrating from $q = 0$ at $t = 0$ (p. 10; slide 13):

<Formula label="Charge on a charging capacitor" tex="q(t) = C\varepsilon\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (7.6.9), p. 10" />

The final charge is $Q = C\varepsilon$, when the capacitor's voltage $V_C = q/C = \varepsilon(1 - e^{-t/RC})$ has reached the emf (Eqs. 7.6.10–7.6.12, p. 11). Differentiating gives the current (p. 11; slide 14):

<Formula label="Current while charging" tex="I(t) = \frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{\varepsilon}{R}\,e^{-t/RC}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (7.6.13), p. 11" />

## The time constant

The product $\tau = RC$ has units of seconds and sets the timescale (course notes, Section 7.6.1, p. 12; slide 13). After each time constant the current falls by a factor $e^{-1} \approx 0.368$, and after one time constant the capacitor's voltage has reached $1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632$ of its final value (Eqs. 7.6.14 and 7.6.16, p. 12).

## Discharging a capacitor

Now a capacitor with charge $Q$ is connected across a resistor at $t = 0$ (course notes, Section 7.6.2, pp. 13–14; slides 16–18). The capacitor drives the current; it flows away from the positive plate, so $I = -dq/dt$, and the loop rule gives $q/C - IR = 0$, that is $q/C + R\,dq/dt = 0$. Integrating, and with $V_C = q/C$ decaying the same way (Eq. 7.6.23, p. 14):

<Formula label="Discharging capacitor" tex="q(t) = Q\,e^{-t/RC}, \qquad I(t) = -\frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{Q}{RC}\,e^{-t/RC}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (7.6.22) and (7.6.24), p. 14" />

## Exponential growth and decay

Every quantity in an RC circuit either approaches a final value as $1 - e^{-t/\tau}$ or decays from its initial value as $e^{-t/\tau}$, and $\tau$ is read off the differential equation as the factor in front of $d/dt$ (slide 19). This curve is everywhere in physics and nature. To measure $\tau$ from data, fit the curve (leaving out the data at both ends), or pick a point and find where the value has fallen to $1/e$ of it: that is one $\tau$ later (slide 20).

<WorkedExample title="A one-second circuit">
<Problem>
A 9 V battery charges a 100 μF capacitor through 10 kΩ. (a) Find τ and the initial current. (b) Find the charge and the capacitor's voltage after 2 s. (c) The capacitor, fully charged to 9 V, is then discharged through the same resistor. How long until its voltage halves?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Time constant and initial current">
$\tau = RC = (10^4)(10^{-4}) = 1$ s. $I_0 = \varepsilon/R = 9/10^4 = 0.9$ mA.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) After 2 s">
$Q = C\varepsilon = 900\ \mu$C, so $q = 900(1 - e^{-2}) \approx 778\ \mu$C and $V_C = q/C \approx 7.78$ V.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Halving">
$e^{-t/\tau} = \frac12$ gives $t = \tau\ln 2 \approx 0.693$ s, whatever the starting voltage.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Voltmeter: in parallel, huge resistance. Ammeter: in series, tiny resistance. Ohmmeter: applies a voltage and measures the current.
- Charging: $q = C\varepsilon(1 - e^{-t/RC})$, $I = (\varepsilon/R)e^{-t/RC}$. Discharging: $q = Qe^{-t/RC}$.
- $\tau = RC$: after one $\tau$, 63% charged; the current down to 37%.
