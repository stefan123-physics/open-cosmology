This set practises the lessons on [current, resistance and DC circuits](/courses/8-02/dc-circuits) and on [measuring circuits and RC circuits](/courses/8-02/rc-circuits). MIT's written homework for 8.02T is not on OpenCourseWare, and no in-class problem-solving session of the course covers these weeks, so this set has no MIT problem set or session behind it. It is built from the course notes instead: the Additional Problems at the end of Chapter 6 (Current and Resistance) and Chapter 7 (Direct-Current Circuits), plus three of their Conceptual Questions turned into choice boxes, all in our own words. The notes print no solutions for these problems, so the hints and the solutions here are ours.

Where each problem comes from: Problem 1 is Additional Problem 6.7.4, Problem 2 is Additional Problem 6.7.6 with Conceptual Questions 1 and 3 of Section 6.6, Problem 3 is Additional Problem 6.7.8, Problem 4 is Additional Problem 7.11.1, Problem 5 is Additional Problem 7.11.2 and Problem 6 is Additional Problem 7.11.5 with Conceptual Question 3 of Section 7.10. The notes draw the circuits of Problems 4 to 6; here each circuit is described in words, so draw it yourself first. Problem 6(c) is our addition. All six problems count for the score (100 points).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. For a circuit, draw it, mark a direction for every current, and write one equation per junction and per loop. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A formula box takes plain typing, for example `rho1*d/(2*A)` or `eps0*V/(3*d)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type $\varepsilon$ as `eps`, $\varepsilon_0$ as `eps0`, $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ as `rho1` and `rho2`. A number box accepts any value within 2% of ours and takes a bare number in the unit given in the statement. You may type a large number as `3.7e6`. Take the charge of the electron as $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C.

The course notes give no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem or part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## What you need from the lessons

Carriers of charge $q$, $n$ of them per unit volume, that drift with the velocity $\vec v_d$ make the current density

<Formula label="Current density" tex="\vec J = n q \vec v_d" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.1.5), p. 2" />

and the current through a wire of cross-section $A$ is $I = JA$ when $J$ is the same across it. An ohmic wire of length $\ell$, cross-section $A$ and resistivity $\rho$ obeys Ohm's law, and its resistance depends on its shape:

<Formula label="Ohm's law and resistance" tex="\Delta V = IR, \qquad R = \frac{\rho\,\ell}{A}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.2.7) and (6.2.10), p. 5" />

A resistor turns electrical energy into heat at the rate

<Formula label="Power in a resistor" tex="P = I\,\Delta V = I^2 R = \frac{(\Delta V)^2}{R}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.3.1) and (6.3.2), p. 7" />

A real battery of emf $\varepsilon$ has an internal resistance $r$. Driving a current $I$ through an outside resistor $R$, its terminal voltage and its current are

<Formula label="A battery with internal resistance" tex="\Delta V = \varepsilon - Ir, \qquad I = \frac{\varepsilon}{R + r}" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (7.2.5) and (7.2.7), pp. 3 and 4" />

Resistors in series and in parallel combine as

<Formula label="Resistors in series and in parallel" tex="R_\text{eq} = R_1 + R_2 \ \ (\text{series}), \qquad \frac{1}{R_\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \ \ (\text{parallel})" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (7.3.2) and (7.3.5), pp. 4 and 5" />

and any circuit is solved with Kirchhoff's two rules: the currents into a junction equal the currents out, and the potential changes around any closed loop add to zero:

<Formula label="Kirchhoff's rules" tex="\sum I_\text{in} = \sum I_\text{out}, \qquad \sum_{\text{closed loop}} \Delta V = 0" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (7.4.1) and (7.4.2), pp. 6 and 7" />

Walking around a loop, the potential rises by $\varepsilon$ across a battery crossed from $-$ to $+$, falls by $IR$ across a resistor crossed in the direction of its current, and falls by $q/C$ across a capacitor crossed from its positive to its negative plate. An uncharged capacitor $C$ that is charged through a resistor $R$ by an emf $\varepsilon$ from $t = 0$ on carries the charge

<Formula label="Charging a capacitor" tex="q(t) = C\varepsilon\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (7.6.9), p. 10" />

and the time constant is the factor in front of $dq/dt$ once the loop equation is written as $\tau\,dq/dt = (\text{final charge}) - q$.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="How slowly the electrons move" points={15}>

(Course notes, Chapter 6, Additional Problem 6.7.4, pp. 14 and 15.) Charges $q$, $n$ of them per unit volume, all move with the velocity $\vec v$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the current density $\vec J$ of these charges, in size and direction, and what is its unit? Work this out on paper. Then, for the box (our addition): in a copper wire the moving charges are electrons, with $q = -e$. Which way does their current density point? Choose in the box.

<Answer id="p1-a" label="The current density points:"
  choices={[
    "along the velocity of the electrons, because the current density always follows the motion of the carriers",
    "at right angles to the velocity of the electrons",
    "against the velocity of the electrons, because their charge is negative",
    "along or against the velocity, depending on how fast the electrons drift"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The current density is $\vec J = nq\vec v$: a positive number $n$ times the charge times the velocity.

<Step title="The direction">

With $q = -e$ the product $nq$ is negative, so $\vec J = -ne\,\vec v$ points against the velocity of the electrons. This is the usual convention: the current points the way positive charge would flow. Its size is $ne|\vec v|$, whatever the speed.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the units">

$n$ is in $\text{m}^{-3}$, $q$ in C and $v$ in m/s, so $nqv$ is in $\text{C}/(\text{s}\,\text{m}^2) = \text{A}/\text{m}^2$, a current per unit area, as a current density must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={12}>

When the ignition switch of a car is turned, a current of 115 A flows from the battery to the starter motor through a copper cable of cross-section $31.2\ \text{mm}^2$ and length 85.5 cm. Copper has $8.49 \times 10^{28}$ conduction electrons per cubic metre. Find the current density in the cable, in $\text{A}/\text{m}^2$ (first box), the drift speed of the electrons, in m/s (second box), and the time an electron takes to get from the battery to the motor, in minutes (third box).

<Hint>

Turn the area into square metres first: $1\ \text{mm}^2 = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The current density is the current per unit area. Then solve $J = nev_d$ for the drift speed, and divide the length of the cable by it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b1" label="$J =$" value="3.69e6" unit="A/m²" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-J" />

<Answer id="p1-b2" label="$v_d =$" value="2.71e-4" unit="m/s" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-vd" />

<Answer id="p1-b3" label="Time from battery to motor:" value="52.6" unit="min" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-time-min" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The current and the area give the current density; the density of electrons turns it into a speed; the speed and the length give the time.

<Step title="Current density">

$A = 31.2\ \text{mm}^2 = 3.12 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2$, so $J = I/A = 115/(3.12 \times 10^{-5}) = 3.69 \times 10^{6}\ \text{A}/\text{m}^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Drift speed">

The size of $\vec J = nq\vec v_d$ gives

<Formula label="Drift speed in the cable" tex="v_d = \frac{J}{ne} = \frac{3.686 \times 10^{6}}{(8.49 \times 10^{28})(1.602 \times 10^{-19})}\ \text{m/s} = 2.71 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m/s}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7.4, p. 15 (our own solution)" />

about a quarter of a millimetre per second.

</Step>

<Step title="Travel time">

$t = L/v_d = 0.855/(2.710 \times 10^{-4}) = 3155$ s, which is 52.6 minutes (the notes print 52.5 min; the difference is rounding). The motor starts at once all the same: the field that pushes the electrons is set up along the whole cable almost instantly, so electrons everywhere in it start drifting together.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Stretching a wire" points={10}>

(Course notes, Chapter 6, Additional Problem 6.7.6, pp. 15 and 16, and Conceptual Questions 1 and 3 of Section 6.6, p. 13.)

<Part label="a" points={4}>

A wire with a resistance of 6.0 Ω is pulled through a die until it is three times as long as before. Its resistivity and its density do not change. Find the resistance of the longer wire, in ohms, and type it in the box.

<Hint>

The amount of metal stays the same, so the volume $A\ell$ does not change. What happens to the cross-section when the length triples?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$R_\text{new} =$" value="54" unit="Ω" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-stretched" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Stretching changes two things at once: the wire gets longer and thinner.

<Step title="Length and area">

The volume $A\ell$ stays the same, so tripling $\ell$ divides $A$ by 3. In $R = \rho\ell/A$ both changes raise the resistance by 3:

<Formula label="Resistance of the stretched wire" tex="R_\text{new} = \frac{\rho\,(3\ell)}{A/3} = 9\,\frac{\rho\,\ell}{A} = 9 \times 6.0\ \Omega = 54\ \Omega" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7.6, p. 16 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Tripling only the length would give 18 Ω; the thinner wire adds another factor 3. In general a wire stretched by a factor $k$ has $k^2$ times its old resistance.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Two wires of circular cross-section are made of the same metal and are equally long, but the resistance of wire 1 is four times that of wire 2. Find the cross-sectional area of wire 1 divided by that of wire 2, and type it in the box.

<Answer id="p2-b" label="Area of wire 1 / area of wire 2 =" value="0.25" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-area-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With the same metal and the same length, $R = \rho\ell/A$ leaves $R \propto 1/A$.

<Step title="The ratio">

Four times the resistance means a quarter of the area: the ratio is $1/4 = 0.25$. Wire 1 has half the radius of wire 2.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Two conductors of the same length and the same radius are connected, one at a time, across the same potential difference. The resistance of the first is twice that of the second. Which one receives more power, and how much more? Choose in the box.

<Answer id="p2-c" label="More power goes to:"
  choices={[
    "the first, twice as much, because the power in a resistor grows as its resistance",
    "both the same, because the potential difference is the same",
    "the first, four times as much",
    "the second, twice as much"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With the potential difference fixed, use the form of the power that contains only $\Delta V$ and $R$.

<Step title="Compare">

$P = (\Delta V)^2/R$, so at equal $\Delta V$ the smaller resistance takes the larger power: the second conductor receives twice as much. The form $P = I^2R$ would suggest the opposite only if the currents were equal, and they are not: the second conductor carries twice the current.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Charge at the boundaries of a layered resistor" points={25}>

(Course notes, Chapter 6, Additional Problem 6.7.8, pp. 16 and 17.) A slab of total thickness $d$ and cross-section $A$ is made of three layers, each of thickness $d/3$. The two outer layers have the resistivity $\rho_1$, the middle layer the resistivity $\rho_2$. Metal plates (perfect conductors) cover the two outer faces, and a battery keeps a potential difference $V$ between them, the left plate at the higher potential. A steady current $I$ flows through the slab from left to right, with the current density $J = I/A$. The four boundaries are labelled a to d from left to right: a between the left plate and the first layer, b between the first and the middle layer, c between the middle and the third layer, d between the third layer and the right plate.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Explain why the current density must be the same in all three layers. Then find the field $E_1$ in the outer layers, in terms of $\rho_1$, $I$ and $A$, and type it in the box. (The notes call the layers "dielectric materials" in this part; they are the resistive layers.)

<Hint>

In a steady state nothing changes in time. What would happen at a boundary if more current arrived at it than left it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Ohm's law in a material: $\vec J = \sigma\vec E = \vec E/\rho$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$E_1 =$" expr="rho1*I/A"
  vars={{ rho1: [1, 3], I: [0.5, 2], A: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ rho1: "\\rho_1" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "I/(rho1*A)", hint: "Ohm's law in a material is J = E/rho, so E = rho J: a material that resists more needs a larger field to carry the same current." },
    { expr: "rho1*I/(3*A)", hint: "The field is the current density times the resistivity. The thickness of the layer does not enter the field; it enters the potential drop across the layer." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Charge conservation fixes the current density; Ohm's law in each material then fixes the field.

<Step title="The same current density everywhere">

If more current flowed into a boundary than out of it, charge would pile up there without limit. In a steady state the charge on each boundary is constant, so the same current, and on the same area the same current density $J = I/A$, crosses every layer.

</Step>

<Step title="The fields">

With $\vec E = \rho\vec J$ in each material, both fields point to the right (from high to low potential), with

<Formula label="Fields in the layers" tex="E_1 = \frac{\rho_1 I}{A}, \qquad E_2 = \frac{\rho_2 I}{A}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7.8, p. 16 (our own solution)" />

The field is larger in the layer that resists more.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the total resistance $R$ of the slab, in terms of $\rho_1$, $\rho_2$, $d$ and $A$, and type it in the box. Check that it gives the expected result when $\rho_1 = \rho_2$.

<Hint>

The three layers carry the same current one after the other. How do resistors combine then?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$R =$" expr="d*(2*rho1 + rho2)/(3*A)"
  vars={{ d: [0.5, 2], A: [0.5, 2], rho1: [1, 2], rho2: [3, 6] }}
  names={{ rho1: "\\rho_1", rho2: "\\rho_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "d*(2*rho1 + rho2)/A", hint: "Each layer is only d/3 thick. Use that thickness as the length in R = rho l / A." },
    { expr: "d*(rho1 + rho2)/(3*A)", hint: "There are two outer layers of resistivity rho1, and both are in series with the middle one." },
    { expr: "d*rho1*rho2/(3*A*(2*rho2 + rho1))", hint: "That combines the layers as if they were side by side. The current crosses them one after the other, so they are in series." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Three resistors in series, each a layer of length $d/3$ and area $A$.

<Step title="Add the layers">

<Formula label="Resistance of the slab" tex="R = \frac{\rho_1 d}{3A} + \frac{\rho_2 d}{3A} + \frac{\rho_1 d}{3A} = \frac{d\,(2\rho_1 + \rho_2)}{3A}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7.8, p. 17 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

With $\rho_1 = \rho_2 = \rho$ this is $d\cdot 3\rho/(3A) = \rho d/A$, the resistance of one uniform slab of thickness $d$, as expected.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the potential drop across each of the three layers, in terms of $V$, $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$. Type the drop across the middle layer in the box, and check that the three drops add up to $V$.

<Hint>

First find the current from $V$ and the resistance of part (b). Then each drop is the current times the resistance of that layer.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="Drop across the middle layer:" expr="V*rho2/(2*rho1 + rho2)"
  vars={{ V: [1, 12], rho1: [1, 2], rho2: [3, 6] }}
  names={{ rho1: "\\rho_1", rho2: "\\rho_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "V/3", hint: "The layers have the same thickness but not the same resistivity. The drops are shared in proportion to the resistances, not equally." },
    { expr: "V*rho2/(rho1 + rho2)", hint: "The slab has two outer layers of resistivity rho1. Both take part of V." },
    { expr: "V*rho1/(2*rho1 + rho2)", hint: "That is the drop across one of the outer layers. The middle layer, with the resistivity rho2, takes the share rho2/(2 rho1 + rho2)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In series, the potential difference is shared in proportion to the resistances, as in a voltage divider.

<Step title="The three drops">

The current is $I = V/R = 3AV/[d(2\rho_1 + \rho_2)]$, and a layer of resistivity $\rho$ has the drop $I\rho d/(3A)$. So

<Formula label="Potential drops across the layers" tex="\Delta V_1 = \frac{\rho_1 V}{2\rho_1 + \rho_2}, \qquad \Delta V_2 = \frac{\rho_2 V}{2\rho_1 + \rho_2}, \qquad \Delta V_3 = \frac{\rho_1 V}{2\rho_1 + \rho_2}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7.8, p. 17 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The three drops add to $V(2\rho_1 + \rho_2)/(2\rho_1 + \rho_2) = V$. Each drop is also the field of part (a) times the thickness $d/3$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={8}>

Find the charge per unit area on each of the four boundaries, $\sigma_a$ to $\sigma_d$. Use Gauss's law, and take the field inside the metal plates to be zero. Type $\sigma_a$ in the first box and $\sigma_b$ in the second, in terms of $\varepsilon_0$, $V$, $d$, $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$.

<Hint>

Draw a thin pillbox across a boundary, with one face on each side. Only the two faces parallel to the boundary have flux. What does Gauss's law then say about the jump of the field across the boundary?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The pillbox gives $\sigma = \varepsilon_0\,(E_\text{right} - E_\text{left})$, with both fields counted positive to the right. Write the fields of part (a) in terms of $V$ with the current of part (c).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-d1" label="$\sigma_a =$" expr="3*eps0*V*rho1/(d*(2*rho1 + rho2))"
  vars={{ eps0: [0.5, 2], V: [1, 12], d: [0.5, 2], rho1: [1, 2], rho2: [3, 6] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", rho1: "\\rho_1", rho2: "\\rho_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-3*eps0*V*rho1/(d*(2*rho1 + rho2))", hint: "Check the sign. The field points away from boundary a into the slab, and a field points away from positive charge." },
    { expr: "eps0*V*rho1/(d*(2*rho1 + rho2))", hint: "The first layer is d/3 thick, so its field is its potential drop divided by d/3, not by d." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-d2" label="$\sigma_b =$" expr="3*eps0*V*(rho2 - rho1)/(d*(2*rho1 + rho2))"
  vars={{ eps0: [0.5, 2], V: [1, 12], d: [0.5, 2], rho1: [1, 2], rho2: [3, 6] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", rho1: "\\rho_1", rho2: "\\rho_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*eps0*V*(rho1 - rho2)/(d*(2*rho1 + rho2))", hint: "Check the sign. Going right across b the field grows when rho2 is larger than rho1, and a field that grows across a boundary means positive charge on it." },
    { expr: "3*eps0*V*rho2/(d*(2*rho1 + rho2))", hint: "There is a field on both sides of b. The charge gives the jump of the field across the boundary, E2 minus E1, not the field on one side." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A steady current through materials of different resistivity needs different fields, and a field can only change from one value to another across a layer of charge. Gauss's law gives the charge from the jump of the field.

<Step title="Gauss's law at a boundary">

Take a flat pillbox of face area $A'$ across a boundary, with both fields counted positive to the right. The flux out is $(E_\text{right} - E_\text{left})A'$, and the charge inside is $\sigma A'$, so

<Formula label="Charge on a boundary" tex="\sigma = \varepsilon_0\,(E_\text{right} - E_\text{left})" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7.8, p. 17 (the notes say to use Gauss's law; our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The four boundaries">

With the current of part (c), $J = I/A = 3V/[d(2\rho_1 + \rho_2)]$, so $E_1 = \rho_1 J$ and $E_2 = \rho_2 J$. The field is zero in the plates. Then

<Formula label="Charges on the boundaries" tex="\sigma_a = -\sigma_d = \frac{3\varepsilon_0 V \rho_1}{d\,(2\rho_1 + \rho_2)}, \qquad \sigma_b = -\sigma_c = \frac{3\varepsilon_0 V (\rho_2 - \rho_1)}{d\,(2\rho_1 + \rho_2)}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.7.8, p. 17 (our own solution)" />

At a the field jumps from 0 to $E_1$; at b from $E_1$ to $E_2$; at c from $E_2$ back to $E_1$; at d from $E_1$ to 0.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The four charges add to zero: the slab as a whole is neutral. With $\rho_1 = \rho_2$ the inner boundaries carry no charge, and the outer ones carry $\pm\varepsilon_0 V/d$, the charge of a parallel-plate capacitor with the gap $d$. For $\rho_2 > \rho_1$, as in the box ranges, $\sigma_b$ is positive.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={4}>

What do these results become when the middle layer resists far more than the outer ones, $\rho_2 \gg \rho_1$? Choose in the box.

<Answer id="p3-e" label="When the middle layer resists far more:"
  choices={[
    "the current and every field go to zero, so all four boundary charges vanish",
    "nearly all of V falls across the middle layer; the boundary charges on a and d go to zero, while b and c carry about +3ε₀V/d and −3ε₀V/d, like the plates of a capacitor whose gap is the middle layer",
    "the potential difference is shared equally between the three layers, and the four boundary charges become equal",
    "nearly all of V falls across the two outer layers; the boundary charges on b and c go to zero, while a and d carry about +3ε₀V/d and −3ε₀V/d"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Take the limit in each result of parts (b) to (d).

<Step title="The limit">

$R \to \rho_2 d/(3A)$: the middle layer is almost the whole resistance. The drop across it tends to $V$, and those across the outer layers to zero. The current $J = 3V/[d(2\rho_1 + \rho_2)]$ does go to zero, but $E_2 = \rho_2 J$ tends to $3V/d$, the full $V$ across the thickness $d/3$, while $E_1$ goes to zero. So $\sigma_a$ and $\sigma_d$ vanish, and $\sigma_b \to 3\varepsilon_0 V/d$, $\sigma_c \to -3\varepsilon_0 V/d$.

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

The outer layers now conduct so well, compared with the middle one, that they act as extensions of the metal plates: the slab behaves like a capacitor whose plates are the two inner boundaries, a distance $d/3$ apart, with a little leakage current through the gap.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Two batteries, in series or in parallel" points={15}>

(Course notes, Chapter 7, Additional Problem 7.11.1, pp. 25 and 26.) Two identical batteries, each with emf $\varepsilon$ and internal resistance $r$, drive a current through a resistor $R$. In the first circuit the batteries are in series: the positive terminal of one is joined to the negative terminal of the other, and $R$ connects the two free terminals. In the second circuit they are in parallel: their positive terminals are joined, their negative terminals are joined, and $R$ connects the two joined pairs.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Mark a direction for the current on your sketch and use Kirchhoff's loop rule to find the current $I_s$ through $R$ in the series circuit, in terms of $\varepsilon$, $r$ and $R$. Type it in the box.

<Hint>

There is one loop. Walk around it once: two rises of $\varepsilon$, and three falls, one for each resistance the current crosses.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$I_s =$" expr="2*eps/(R + 2*r)"
  vars={{ eps: [1, 12], R: [3, 10], r: [0.1, 2] }}
  names={{ eps: "\\varepsilon" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*eps/(R + r)", hint: "Each battery has its own internal resistance, and the current crosses both of them." },
    { expr: "eps/(R + 2*r)", hint: "In series the two emfs add: going once around the loop you climb through both batteries." },
    { expr: "2*eps/R", hint: "That leaves out the internal resistances, which the current also crosses." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In series the same current crosses everything: both batteries and $R$.

<Step title="The loop">

Go around the loop in the direction of the current, from the negative terminal of the first battery: rise $\varepsilon$, fall $I_s r$, rise $\varepsilon$, fall $I_s r$, fall $I_s R$. The sum is zero:

<Formula label="Series connection" tex="2\varepsilon - 2I_s r - I_s R = 0, \qquad I_s = \frac{2\varepsilon}{R + 2r}" cite="mit" at="Section 7.11.1, p. 25 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

With $r = 0$ this is $2\varepsilon/R$, the current from one battery of emf $2\varepsilon$, as it should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the current $I_p$ through $R$ in the parallel circuit, in terms of $\varepsilon$, $r$ and $R$, and type it in the box.

<Hint>

The two batteries are identical, so by symmetry each one carries half of the current through $R$. Write the loop rule for the loop through one battery and $R$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$I_p =$" expr="2*eps/(2*R + r)"
  vars={{ eps: [1, 12], R: [3, 10], r: [0.1, 2] }}
  names={{ eps: "\\varepsilon" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "eps/(R + r)", hint: "That is the current from one battery alone. With two in parallel each battery carries only half the current, so less voltage is lost inside each." },
    { expr: "2*eps/(R + r)", hint: "In parallel the emfs do not add: the potential difference across the joined pair is at most one emf." },
    { expr: "eps/(R + 2*r)", hint: "In parallel the internal resistances combine as resistors in parallel, to r/2, not in series." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In parallel each battery carries half of the current, so the potential lost inside each is $(I_p/2)\,r$.

<Step title="The loop">

The loop through one battery and $R$: rise $\varepsilon$, fall $(I_p/2)r$ inside the battery, fall $I_p R$ across the resistor.

<Formula label="Parallel connection" tex="\varepsilon - \frac{I_p}{2}\,r - I_p R = 0, \qquad I_p = \frac{\varepsilon}{R + r/2} = \frac{2\varepsilon}{2R + r}" cite="mit" at="Section 7.11.1, p. 25 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The second form shows the pair acting as one battery of emf $\varepsilon$ and internal resistance $r/2$: the two internal resistances are in parallel.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

For which values of $r$ and $R$ are the two currents equal, and when is each one larger? Choose in the box.

<Hint>

Both currents have $2\varepsilon$ on top. Compare their denominators.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c" label="Which is right?"
  choices={[
    "The currents are equal when R = r; the parallel circuit gives the larger current when R > r.",
    "The series circuit always gives the larger current, because its emf is twice as large.",
    "The currents are equal when R = r; the series circuit gives the larger current when R > r.",
    "The currents are equal when R = 2r; the series circuit gives the larger current when R > 2r."
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The larger current has the smaller denominator.

<Step title="Compare">

$I_s > I_p$ when $R + 2r < 2R + r$, that is when $R > r$. The currents are equal when $R = r$, and the parallel circuit wins when $R < r$.

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

A large outside resistance limits the current, so doubling the emf (series) helps most. A small outside resistance leaves the internal resistances in charge, so halving them (parallel) helps most. Example (ours): with $\varepsilon = 1.5$ V and $r = 0.5$ Ω, a 10 Ω lamp gets $3/11 = 0.273$ A in series but only $3/20.5 = 0.146$ A in parallel.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Three batteries and three resistors" points={15}>

(Course notes, Chapter 7, Additional Problem 7.11.2, p. 26.) Three branches join at a top junction T and a bottom junction B. Leave out the internal resistance of the batteries.

- The left branch runs from B to T. It holds, in this order, a 10 V battery with its negative terminal towards B, a 2.0 Ω resistor, and a 20 V battery with its positive terminal towards the resistor (its negative terminal towards T).
- The middle branch is a 4.0 Ω resistor between T and B.
- The right branch runs from T to B. It holds a 5.0 Ω resistor and then a 30 V battery with its positive terminal towards the resistor (its negative terminal towards B).

Find the current through each of the three resistors, in amperes, and the direction in which it flows: the 2.0 Ω resistor in the first box, the 4.0 Ω resistor in the second and the 5.0 Ω resistor in the third.

<Hint>

Give each branch a current, with a direction of your choice, and write the junction rule at T. You need two more equations: one loop rule for the left and middle branches, one for the middle and right ones.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A quicker route: call the potential of T, measured from B, $U$. Then write each branch current in terms of $U$, and add them at T.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the left branch the two batteries work against each other. Going from B to T, the potential rises by 10 V across the first one and falls by 20 V across the second.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="Current in the 2.0 Ω resistor:" value="5.53" unit="A" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-I2ohm" />

<Answer id="p5-b" label="Current in the 4.0 Ω resistor:" value="0.263" unit="A" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-I4ohm" />

<Answer id="p5-c" label="Current in the 5.0 Ω resistor:" value="5.79" unit="A" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-I5ohm" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With only two junctions, one unknown potential settles the whole circuit: each branch current follows from it, and the junction rule fixes it.

<Step title="Branch currents in terms of the potential of T">

Put B at zero potential and T at $U$. Counting each current positive when it flows into T:

- Left branch, from B to T: the potential rises by 10 V, falls by $2.0\,\Omega \times I_L$ across the resistor, and falls by 20 V. So $0 + 10 - 2I_L - 20 = U$, which gives $I_L = (-10 - U)/2$ (in amperes, with $U$ in volts).
- Right branch, from B up through the 30 V battery to the 5.0 Ω resistor and into T: $0 + 30 - 5I_R = U$, so $I_R = (30 - U)/5$.
- Middle branch: the current flowing down from T to B is $I_M = U/4$.

</Step>

<Step title="The junction rule at T">

What flows in equals what flows out:

<Formula label="Junction rule at T" tex="\frac{-10 - U}{2} + \frac{30 - U}{5} = \frac{U}{4}, \qquad U = \frac{20}{19}\ \text{V} = 1.053\ \text{V}" cite="mit" at="Section 7.11.2, p. 26 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The currents">

$I_M = 1.053/4 = 0.263$ A, down through the 4.0 Ω resistor. $I_R = (30 - 1.053)/5 = 5.79$ A, through the 5.0 Ω resistor towards T. $I_L = (-10 - 1.053)/2 = -5.53$ A: the minus sign says this current flows the other way, from T down through the left branch, so 5.53 A flows through the 2.0 Ω resistor from the 20 V battery towards the 10 V one.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At T: 5.79 A in from the right, 5.53 A out to the left and 0.263 A down the middle, and $5.53 + 0.263 = 5.79$. A loop check: from T down the left branch, the potential rises by 20 V and falls by 10 V, a net 10 V that pushes the current the way it flows. Down the left branch and back up the middle one, the loop rule then reads $10 - 2.0 \times 5.53 + 4.0 \times 0.263 = 10 - 11.05 + 1.05 = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Charging a capacitor through a network" points={20}>

(Course notes, Chapter 7, Additional Problem 7.11.5, p. 27, and Conceptual Question 3 of Section 7.10, p. 25.) A battery of emf $\varepsilon = 40$ V, an open switch S and a resistor $R_1 = 8.0$ Ω are in series. From the far end of $R_1$ two branches lead back to the battery, side by side: one is a resistor $R_2 = 6.0$ Ω alone; the other is a resistor $R_3 = 4.0$ Ω in series with a capacitor $C = 4.0\ \mu\text{F}$. The capacitor is uncharged, and the battery has no internal resistance. At $t = 0$ the switch is closed.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Find the current through each resistor just after the switch is closed, in amperes: through $R_1$ in the first box, $R_2$ in the second and $R_3$ in the third.

<Hint>

At the first moment the capacitor carries no charge, so there is no potential difference across it. What does it then act like?

</Hint>

<Hint>

With the capacitor acting as a plain wire, $R_2$ and $R_3$ are in parallel, and that pair is in series with $R_1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-a1" label="$I_1 =$" value="3.85" unit="A" tolerance="2%" calc="p6-I1-0" />

<Answer id="p6-a2" label="$I_2 =$" value="1.54" unit="A" tolerance="2%" calc="p6-I2-0" />

<Answer id="p6-a3" label="$I_3 =$" value="2.31" unit="A" tolerance="2%" calc="p6-I3-0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The charge on a capacitor cannot jump, so just after the switch closes the capacitor is still empty and has $q/C = 0$ across it: for that instant it is a wire.

<Step title="The equivalent resistance">

$R_2$ and $R_3$ in parallel: $6.0 \times 4.0/(6.0 + 4.0) = 2.4$ Ω. In series with $R_1$: $10.4$ Ω.

</Step>

<Step title="The currents">

$I_1 = 40/10.4 = 3.85$ A. The pair has $3.846 \times 2.4 = 9.23$ V across it, so $I_2 = 9.23/6.0 = 1.54$ A and $I_3 = 9.23/4.0 = 2.31$ A. Check with the junction rule: $1.54 + 2.31 = 3.85$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the final charge on the capacitor, in microcoulombs, and type it in the box.

<Hint>

After a long time the capacitor is full and no current flows into it. Which resistors still carry current, and what potential difference does the capacitor then share with them?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-b" label="$Q_\text{final} =$" value="68.6" unit="μC" tolerance="2%" calc="p6-Qfinal" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** When the capacitor is full, its branch carries no current: for the steady state it is a break in the circuit.

<Step title="The steady current">

Only $R_1$ and $R_2$ carry current, in series: $I = 40/(8.0 + 6.0) = 2.857$ A.

</Step>

<Step title="The charge">

No current flows through $R_3$, so there is no drop across it, and the capacitor has the same potential difference as $R_2$: $2.857 \times 6.0 = 17.14$ V. So

<Formula label="Final charge on the capacitor" tex="Q_\text{final} = C\,\frac{\varepsilon R_2}{R_1 + R_2} = (4.0\ \mu\text{F})(17.14\ \text{V}) = 68.6\ \mu\text{C}" cite="mit" at="Section 7.11.5, p. 27 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

(Our addition.) How long does the charging take? Find the time constant $\tau$ of the circuit, in microseconds, and type it in the box.

<Hint>

Let $V_C$ be the capacitor's potential difference and $I_3 = C\,dV_C/dt$ the current into it. Write the junction rule where the branches meet and a loop rule for each branch, and eliminate everything but $V_C$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You should reach an equation of the form $\tau\,dV_C/dt = V_\text{final} - V_C$. The time constant is not $R_3 C$: the current into the capacitor also passes through $R_1$ and $R_2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-c" label="$\tau =$" value="29.7" unit="μs" tolerance="2%" calc="p6-tau" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Reduce the circuit to one first-order equation for the capacitor's potential difference; the factor in front of the derivative is the time constant.

<Step title="The equations">

Let $U$ be the potential where the branches meet, measured from the battery's negative terminal. Then $I_1 = (\varepsilon - U)/R_1$, $I_2 = U/R_2$, and $U = V_C + R_3 I_3$. The junction rule $I_1 = I_2 + I_3$ gives $U = (\varepsilon/R_1 - I_3)\,R_1R_2/(R_1 + R_2)$. Putting the two expressions for $U$ together, with $I_3 = C\,dV_C/dt$:

<Formula label="Charging equation of the network" tex="\left(R_3 + \frac{R_1R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\right) C\,\frac{dV_C}{dt} = \frac{\varepsilon R_2}{R_1 + R_2} - V_C" cite="mit" at="Section 7.11.5, p. 27 (our addition and solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The time constant">

The factor in front of $dV_C/dt$ is

<Formula label="Time constant of the network" tex="\tau = \left(R_3 + \frac{R_1R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\right) C = (4.0 + 3.43)\ \Omega \times 4.0\ \mu\text{F} = 29.7\ \mu\text{s}" cite="mit" at="Section 7.11.5, p. 27 (our addition and solution)" />

The capacitor sees $R_3$ in series with $R_1$ and $R_2$ in parallel (the battery counts as a wire here). The final value on the right is the potential difference of part (b), as it must be.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The solution is $V_C = V_\text{final}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, so the first current into the capacitor is $C V_\text{final}/\tau = Q_\text{final}/\tau = 68.57\ \mu\text{C}/29.71\ \mu\text{s} = 2.31$ A, the $I_3$ of part (a). After about $5\tau \approx 150\ \mu$s the capacitor is practically full.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

(The notes' Conceptual Question 3 asks whether the resistor of an RC circuit affects the largest charge the capacitor can hold; here it is applied to this circuit.) Which of the three resistors would change the final charge on the capacitor if its value were changed? Choose in the box.

<Answer id="p6-d" label="The final charge depends on:"
  choices={[
    "R₁ and R₂, but not R₃",
    "only R₃, the resistor in the capacitor's own branch",
    "all three resistors",
    "none of them: the final charge is always Cε"
  ]}
  answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The final charge is set by the steady state, in which the capacitor's branch carries no current.

<Step title="Read it off">

$Q_\text{final} = C\varepsilon R_2/(R_1 + R_2)$ contains $R_1$ and $R_2$ but not $R_3$: with no current through $R_3$ at the end, it has no drop across it. $R_3$ only changes how fast the capacitor fills, through $\tau$. In the simple RC circuit of the lesson, where the capacitor is alone in series with one resistor, the final charge $C\varepsilon$ does not depend on that resistor at all (the notes' Conceptual Question 3); here the divider $R_1$, $R_2$ sets the capacitor's final potential difference.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
