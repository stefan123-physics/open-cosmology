Session 18 opens with some fun, magnetic levitation, then meets the third of Maxwell's equations, **Ampère's law** (slides 1, 5, 21).

## Magnetic materials and levitation

[Magnetic dipoles](/courses/8-02/magnetic-dipoles) feel torques that align them with a field, and forces that pull them towards stronger field (slides 3–4). Matter is full of tiny magnetic moments, so materials respond to fields (slides 7–9):

- In **paramagnetic** and **ferromagnetic** materials, the moments of unpaired electrons tend to line up with an applied field.
- Every material is also slightly **diamagnetic**; in materials without unpaired electrons this effect dominates, and the material behaves like a magnet pointing **against** the field.

A diamagnet therefore seeks **lower** field. Make a very strong field that weakens upwards, and the upward push on a diamagnet can balance gravity: a living frog floats stably in a 16 T magnet, and so do strawberries, water droplets and crickets (slides 6, 11–12). A superconductor is a perfect diamagnet, a "magnetic mirror": a magnet above it is repelled by its mirror image and floats stably, whatever its angle (slides 13–15). Experiment 8 uses the force between current-carrying coils to measure $\mu_0$ (slide 18).

## Ampère's law

[Gauss's law](/courses/8-02/gauss-law) says that the flux of $\vec E$ out of a closed surface depends only on the charge inside. Ampère's law is the analogue for $\vec B$: to have a magnetic field circulating around a loop, current must pass through the loop (slides 21–23).

Start with a long straight wire, whose field circles it with $B = \mu_0 I/2\pi r$. Around a circle of radius $r$, $\vec B$ is parallel to $d\vec s$ and constant, so $\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec s = B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I$, independent of $r$ (course notes, Section 9.3, p. 14). The same holds for a loop crossing several field lines, and in fact for any closed loop around the wire, since only the angle swept matters: $\oint \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\,r\,d\varphi = \mu_0 I$ (pp. 14–15). In general (p. 16; slide 24):

<Formula label="Ampère's law" tex="\oint_C \vec B \cdot d\vec s = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}, \qquad I_{\text{enc}} = \int\int_S \vec J \cdot d\vec A" cite="reading2" at="slide 24" />

for any closed loop $C$ bounding a surface $S$; $I_{\text{enc}}$ is the current through $S$ (slide 24). This form holds for steady currents; [Session 28](/courses/8-02) adds a term for changing electric fields. The **Biot–Savart law** works for any current but takes integration; Ampère's law is always true but only gives the field when the currents are highly symmetric: infinite wires, infinite sheets, solenoids, toroids (Section 9.3, p. 16; slides 26, 28).

**The recipe** (slides 27, 35): find the direction of $\vec B$ by the right-hand rule and symmetry; choose an **Amperian loop** on which $\vec B$ is either constant and parallel to the loop, or perpendicular to it, or zero; compute $\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec s$ and $I_{\text{enc}}$; solve for $B$.

## A thick wire

A long wire of radius $R$ carries current $I$ spread uniformly over its cross-section (course notes, Example 9.3, pp. 16–17; slides 29–33). Take circles of radius $r$ around the axis. Outside, the circle encloses all the current; inside, only the fraction $r^2/R^2$:

<Formula label="Field of a thick wire" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\ \ (r \ge R), \qquad B = \frac{\mu_0 I r}{2\pi R^2}\ \ (r < R)" cite="reading1" at="Example 9.3, p. 17" />

The field is zero on the axis, grows linearly to the surface and then falls as $1/r$ (p. 17).

## The solenoid

A **solenoid** is a long, tightly wound coil. Its field is nearly uniform inside and weak outside; an ideal, infinitely long solenoid has a uniform field parallel to its axis inside and none outside (course notes, Section 9.4, pp. 19–20; slides 39–41). Take a rectangular loop of length $\ell$ with one side inside along the axis and one outside. Only the inside side contributes, $B\ell$, and the loop encloses $N = n\ell$ turns, each carrying $I$ (pp. 20–21; slide 42):

<Formula label="Field of an ideal solenoid" tex="B = \mu_0\frac{NI}{\ell} = \mu_0 nI" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.4.3), p. 21" />

where $n$ is the number of turns per unit length. A finite solenoid, built from the [ring fields](/courses/8-02/biot-savart-law) of its turns, has nearly this field over most of its length (Eq. 9.4.6, pp. 21–22); evaluating that formula at an end gives about half of it (the page's own check). A **toroid**, a solenoid bent into a ring, keeps its field entirely inside, where Ampère's law around a circle of radius $r$ gives $B = \mu_0 NI/2\pi r$, so the field is not uniform (Example 9.5, pp. 22–23). The slides also show pairs of coils, a **Helmholtz coil** and a closer pair, as steps from a single loop towards a solenoid (slides 37–38); with the two coils one radius apart the field between them is nearly uniform (the page's own addition).

## A current sheet

A sheet of thickness $b$ carrying current density $J_0$ is like many parallel wires side by side. The field is parallel to the sheet, opposite on its two sides. A rectangular loop straddling the sheet gives (course notes, Example 9.4, pp. 17–19; slides 44–45):

<Formula label="Field outside a current sheet" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 J_0 b}{2}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.3.8) and the text below it, p. 19" />

the same at every distance, like the electric field of a charged plane. Inside the sheet, at a distance $|z|$ from its middle plane, $B = \mu_0 J_0|z|$, zero in the middle (p. 19).

<WorkedExample title="A wire and a solenoid">
<Problem>
(a) A copper wire of radius 1 mm carries 10 A. Find $B$ at 0.5 mm from the axis, at the surface and 1 cm away. (b) A solenoid 20 cm long has 400 turns and carries 2 A. Find the field inside.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The wire">
$\mu_0 I/2\pi = 2 \times 10^{-6}$ T m. Inside at 0.5 mm: $(2 \times 10^{-6})(0.0005)/(0.001)^2 = 10^{-3}$ T. At the surface: $(2 \times 10^{-6})/0.001 = 2 \times 10^{-3}$ T. At 1 cm: $(2 \times 10^{-6})/0.01 = 2 \times 10^{-4}$ T.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The solenoid">
$n = 400/0.2 = 2000$ turns per metre, so $B = \mu_0 nI = (4\pi \times 10^{-7})(2000)(2) \approx 5.03 \times 10^{-3}$ T, about a hundred times the Earth's field.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Para- and ferromagnets align with a field; diamagnets oppose it and seek weaker field, which allows levitation.
- Ampère's law: $\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec s = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}$, always true, useful with symmetry.
- Thick wire: $\mu_0 Ir/2\pi R^2$ inside, $\mu_0 I/2\pi r$ outside. Ideal solenoid: $\mu_0 nI$ inside, zero outside. Current sheet: $\mu_0 J_0 b/2$ on each side.
