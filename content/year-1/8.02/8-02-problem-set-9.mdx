## What you need from the lessons

An inductor of inductance $L$ opposes changes of its current. Walking across it in the direction of the current, Kirchhoff's loop rule counts a drop of $L\,dI/dt$, so a battery of emf $\varepsilon$, a resistor $R$ and an inductor in series obey

<Formula label="Loop rule with an inductor" tex="\varepsilon - IR - L\,\frac{dI}{dt} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.4.5), p. 17" />

From $I = 0$ at $t = 0$ the current rises towards $\varepsilon/R$ with the time constant $L/R$, and a current that flows through $L$ and $R$ alone dies away with the same time constant:

<Formula label="Rising and decaying current" tex="I(t) = \frac{\varepsilon}{R}\left(1 - e^{-Rt/L}\right), \qquad I(t) = \frac{\varepsilon}{R}\,e^{-Rt/L}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (11.4.7) and (11.4.13), pp. 17 and 19" />

An inductor carrying the current $I$ stores the energy

<Formula label="Energy in an inductor" tex="U_B = \frac12 LI^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.3.4), p. 9" />

A capacitor $C$ and an inductor $L$ joined in a loop oscillate: the charge obeys the equation of a mass on a spring, with the angular frequency $\omega_0$, and the energy moves back and forth between the capacitor and the inductor while its total stays fixed:

<Formula label="LC circuit" tex="\frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{LC}\,Q = 0, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad \frac{Q^2}{2C} + \frac12 LI^2 = \text{constant}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (11.5.3), (11.5.6) and (11.5.12), pp. 21 and 22" />

A generator with the emf $V(t) = V_0\sin\omega t$ drives a resistor $R$, an inductor $L$ and a capacitor $C$ in series. Once the free oscillations have died away, the current oscillates at the driving frequency, $I(t) = I_0\sin(\omega t - \varphi)$, with

<Formula label="Driven series RLC circuit" tex="I_0 = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}}, \qquad \tan\varphi = \frac{\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}}{R}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (8.1), p. 1" />

A positive $\varphi$ means that the current lags behind the emf; a negative one, that it leads. The current is largest at the resonant frequency $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, where $\omega L = 1/(\omega C)$. Averaged over a cycle, the generator delivers the power

<Formula label="Average power" tex="\langle P\rangle = \langle I(t)\,V(t)\rangle = \frac12 I_0V_0\cos\varphi" cite="mit" at="Eq. (8.2), p. 1" />

(the time average of $\sin^2\omega t$ is $\frac12$, that of $\sin\omega t\cos\omega t$ is zero). The sharpness of a resonance is measured by the quality factor (written $Q$ like the charge, but a pure number)

<Formula label="Quality factor" tex="Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\Delta\omega} = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (12.4.16), p. 16" />

where $\Delta\omega = R/L$ is the width of the power peak at half its height.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A resistor, an inductor and a capacitor, each driven alone" points={20} mit="Examples 1 to 3">

A generator with the emf $V(t) = V_0\sin\omega t$ is connected to one circuit element at a time: first a resistor $R$, then an inductor $L$, then a capacitor $C$. Each time, write the current as $I(t) = I_0\sin(\omega t - \varphi)$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

**Resistor only.** The loop rule reads $IR - V(t) = 0$. Find on paper the amplitude and the phase of the current (MIT's Question 1). Which values of $L$ and $C$ turn the general result for the series circuit into this one? Choose in the first box. Then find the time-averaged power $\langle I(t)V(t)\rangle$ that the resistor takes, in terms of $V_0$ and $R$, and type it in the second box.

<Hint>

The general result has $\omega L - 1/(\omega C)$ wherever the inductor and capacitor appear. What must that combination be for a resistor alone?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $I(t)V(t)$ as a constant times $\sin^2\omega t$, and use the average $\frac12$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a1" label="To recover the resistor alone, take:"
  choices={[
    "L = 0 and C = 0, so that neither element is in the circuit",
    "L infinitely large and C = 0, so that both reactances are infinite",
    "L = 0 and C infinitely large, so that 1/(ωC) is zero"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Answer id="p1-a2" label="$\langle P_R\rangle =$" expr="V0^2/(2*R)"
  vars={{ V0: [1, 10], R: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ V0: "V_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "V0^2/R", hint: "That is the largest value of the power, when sin²ωt = 1. Its average over a cycle is half of it." },
    { expr: "V0^2/(4*R)", hint: "The factor 1/2 enters once, from the average of sin²ωt. Writing V0/√2 and I0/√2 already includes it; do not halve again." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** An inductor of zero inductance is a plain wire; a capacitor is a plain wire only when it never fills up, that is when its capacitance is infinite.

<Step title="Amplitude and phase (MIT's Question 1)">

The loop rule gives $I(t) = V(t)/R = (V_0/R)\sin\omega t$: the amplitude is $V_0/R$ and $\varphi = 0$, so the current rises and falls in step with the emf.

</Step>

<Step title="Which L and C">

With $L = 0$ the term $\omega L$ vanishes, and with $C \to \infty$ the term $1/(\omega C)$ vanishes too. Then $I_0 = V_0/\sqrt{R^2} = V_0/R$ and $\tan\varphi = 0$, the resistor's result. Taking $C = 0$ instead would make $1/(\omega C)$ infinite: a capacitor with no capacitance is a break in the circuit, and no current flows. Choosing $L$ and $C$ with $\omega^2LC = 1$ would also cancel the two reactances, but only at that one frequency, not at every $\omega$.

</Step>

<Step title="The power">

$I(t)V(t) = (V_0/R)\sin\omega t \cdot V_0\sin\omega t = (V_0^2/R)\sin^2\omega t$, and the average of $\sin^2\omega t$ is $\frac12$:

<Formula label="Average power in the resistor" tex="\langle P_R\rangle = \frac{V_0^2}{2R} = \frac12 I_{R0}V_0" cite="mit" at="Question 3, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

This is Eq. (8.2) with $\cos\varphi = 1$. In rms values, $V_\text{rms} = V_0/\sqrt 2$, it is $V_\text{rms}^2/R$, the form of the power for a steady current.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

**Inductor only.** Now the loop rule reads $V(t) = L\,dI/dt$. Solve it for the current (leave out any constant, which a resistor anywhere in a real circuit would remove). Find the amplitude $I_0$, in terms of $V_0$, $\omega$ and $L$, and type it in the first box; choose the phase in the second box. Then check on paper which values of $R$ and $C$ turn the general result into yours, and find the average power the inductor takes.

<Hint>

Integrate $dI/dt = (V_0/L)\sin\omega t$. The integral of $\sin\omega t$ is $-\cos\omega t/\omega$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

To read off $\varphi$, write $-\cos\omega t$ as a sine with a shifted argument, using $\sin(\omega t - \varphi) = \sin\omega t\cos\varphi - \sin\varphi\cos\omega t$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b1" label="$I_{L0} =$" expr="V0/(omega*L)"
  vars={{ V0: [1, 10], omega: [2, 10], L: [1, 5] }}
  names={{ V0: "V_0", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "V0*omega*L", hint: "Integrating sin ωt divides by ω. An inductor lets less current through at high frequency, not more." },
    { expr: "V0/L", hint: "The integral of sin ωt is −cos(ωt)/ω: the factor 1/ω is missing." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-b2" label="The phase of the inductor's current is:"
  choices={[
    "φ = +π/2: the current lags a quarter cycle behind the emf",
    "φ = −π/2: the current runs a quarter cycle ahead of the emf",
    "φ = 0: the current and the emf rise and fall together",
    "φ = π: the current is always opposite to the emf"
  ]}
  answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The emf fixes the slope of the current, not the current itself, so the current is the integral of a sine: a cosine, shifted by a quarter cycle.

<Step title="Integrate">

$dI/dt = (V_0/L)\sin\omega t$ gives $I(t) = -(V_0/\omega L)\cos\omega t$. With $\varphi = \pi/2$ the identity gives $\sin(\omega t - \pi/2) = -\cos\omega t$, so

<Formula label="Current through the inductor" tex="I_L(t) = \frac{V_0}{\omega L}\,\sin\!\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right), \qquad I_{L0} = \frac{V_0}{\omega L}, \qquad \varphi = +\frac{\pi}{2}" cite="mit" at="Question 4, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

A positive $\varphi$ means a lag: the current peaks a quarter period after the emf. The inductor's reactance is $X_L = \omega L$.

</Step>

<Step title="Which R and C (MIT's Question 5)">

Put $R = 0$ and $C \to \infty$ in Eq. (8.1): $I_0 = V_0/\sqrt{(\omega L)^2} = V_0/(\omega L)$, and $\tan\varphi = \omega L/0$ is infinite, so $\varphi = \pi/2$. Both agree.

</Step>

<Step title="The power (MIT's Question 6)">

$I(t)V(t) = -(V_0^2/\omega L)\sin\omega t\cos\omega t$, whose average is zero. The inductor takes energy from the generator for a quarter cycle while its current grows, and gives it all back in the next quarter cycle. This is Eq. (8.2) with $\cos(\pi/2) = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={8}>

**Capacitor only.** Now the loop rule reads $Q/C - V(t) = 0$. Differentiate it in time to find the current. Find the current amplitude $I_0$, in terms of $V_0$, $\omega$ and $C$, and type it in the first box. In the second box, choose when the current towards the capacitor is largest. In the third box, choose which of the three elements of this problem takes energy from the generator on average.

<Hint>

The current is $I = dQ/dt$, and $Q = CV(t)$. There is nothing to integrate this time. Compare $\cos\omega t$ with $\sin(\omega t - \varphi)$ for a suitable $\varphi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c1" label="$I_{C0} =$" expr="omega*C*V0"
  vars={{ V0: [1, 10], omega: [2, 10], C: [0.6, 2] }}
  names={{ V0: "V_0", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "V0/(omega*C)", hint: "Differentiating sin ωt multiplies by ω. A capacitor lets more current through at high frequency, not less." },
    { expr: "C*V0", hint: "That is the largest charge on the capacitor. The current is its rate of change, which brings in a factor ω." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-c2" label="The current is largest:"
  choices={[
    "when the emf is at its largest, as for a resistor",
    "when the emf is zero and rising, a quarter cycle before the emf peaks",
    "when the emf is zero and falling, a quarter cycle after the emf peaks",
    "when the emf is at its most negative"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-c3" label="On average, energy is taken by:"
  choices={[
    "all three elements, each at its own rate",
    "the resistor and the inductor, but not the capacitor",
    "the inductor and the capacitor, but not the resistor",
    "the resistor alone, not the inductor or the capacitor"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The charge follows the emf, $Q = CV$; the current is the rate of change of the charge, so it follows the slope of the emf.

<Step title="The current">

$I(t) = \omega CV_0\cos\omega t$. With $\varphi = -\pi/2$ the identity gives $\sin(\omega t + \pi/2) = \cos\omega t$, so

<Formula label="Current through the capacitor" tex="I_C(t) = \omega CV_0\,\sin\!\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right), \qquad I_{C0} = \omega CV_0 = \frac{V_0}{1/(\omega C)}, \qquad \varphi = -\frac{\pi}{2}" cite="mit" at="Question 7, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

The capacitor's reactance is $X_C = 1/(\omega C)$. In Eq. (8.1), $R = 0$ and $L = 0$ give the same amplitude, and $\tan\varphi = -\infty$, so $\varphi = -\pi/2$.

</Step>

<Step title="When the current peaks">

$\cos\omega t$ is largest at $\omega t = 0$, where $\sin\omega t$ is zero and rising. The capacitor charges fastest when the emf changes fastest. The current leads: it peaks a quarter period before the emf does.

</Step>

<Step title="The power (MIT's Question 8)">

$I(t)V(t) = \omega CV_0^2\sin\omega t\cos\omega t$ averages to zero: like the inductor, the capacitor stores energy for a quarter cycle and returns it in the next. Of the three elements only the resistor takes energy on average, $V_0^2/(2R)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Two black boxes" points={25} mit="Sample Problems 1 and 2">

In each circuit a generator with the emf $V(t) = V_0\sin\omega t$, a resistor $R$ and a closed "black box" are joined in one loop. We cannot look inside the box, but we can measure the current. (MIT names the emf with the Greek letter epsilon instead of V.)

<Part label="a" points={5}>

**First box.** It holds either an inductor or a capacitor, but not both. The amplitude of the emf is $V_0 = 100$ V and $\omega = 10$ rad/s, and the measured current is $I(t) = (10\ \text{A})\sin(\omega t - \pi/4)$. (The session gives the amplitude in "volts per metre", a slip: an emf is measured in volts.) Does the current lead or lag the emf, and what is in the box? Choose in the box.

<Answer id="p2-a" label="The current, and the box:"
  choices={[
    "leads the emf, so the box holds a capacitor",
    "lags behind the emf, so the box holds a capacitor",
    "leads the emf, so the box holds an inductor",
    "lags behind the emf, so the box holds an inductor"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compare the measured current with $I_0\sin(\omega t - \varphi)$: the sign of $\varphi$ tells which reactance wins.

<Step title="Read off the phase">

Here $\varphi = +\pi/4$, positive, so the current reaches each peak an eighth of a period after the emf: it lags.

</Step>

<Step title="Which element">

By Eq. (8.1), $\tan\varphi = (\omega L - 1/\omega C)/R$. With a capacitor alone ($L = 0$) this is negative, a lead. A lag needs $\omega L$ to win, so the box holds an inductor.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

With an inductor alone, $\tan\varphi = \omega L/R$ is positive at every frequency, so it always gives a lag; a capacitor would have given $-\pi/4$ instead of $+\pi/4$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

Find the resistance $R$, in ohms (first box), and the inductance or capacitance in the box, in henries or farads (second box).

<Hint>

The ratio of the amplitudes gives the size of the impedance, $\sqrt{R^2 + X^2}$. The phase gives the ratio $X/R$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\tan(\pi/4) = 1$, so the reactance equals the resistance.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b1" label="$R =$" value="7.07" unit="Ω" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-R" />

<Answer id="p2-b2" label="The element in the box:" value="0.707" unit="H or F" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-L" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two measurements, the amplitude and the phase, give two unknowns, $R$ and $\omega L$.

<Step title="Impedance and phase">

$\sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L)^2} = V_0/I_0 = 100/10 = 10$ Ω, and $\tan\varphi = \omega L/R = 1$, so $\omega L = R$ and $R\sqrt 2 = 10$ Ω:

<Formula label="The first black box" tex="R = \frac{10\ \Omega}{\sqrt 2} = 5\sqrt 2\ \Omega = 7.07\ \Omega, \qquad L = \frac{R}{\omega} = \frac{7.07\ \Omega}{10\ \text{rad/s}} = 0.707\ \text{H}" cite="mit" at="Questions 11 and 12, p. 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

One ohm per radian per second is one henry: $\Omega\,\text{s} = (\text{V/A})\,\text{s} = \text{H}$. Back in Eq. (8.1): $I_0 = 100/\sqrt{50 + 50} = 10$ A and $\tan\varphi = 7.07/7.07 = 1$, as measured.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

**Second box.** It may hold an inductor, a capacitor, or both in series. Now $R = 1$ Ω and $V_0 = 1$ V. At $\omega = 1$ rad/s the current is exactly in phase with the emf; at $\omega = 2$ rad/s it lags behind the emf by exactly $\pi/4$. What is in the box? Choose in the box.

<Answer id="p2-c" label="The second box holds:"
  choices={[
    "only an inductor, since the current lags at 2 rad/s",
    "only a capacitor, since nothing lags at 1 rad/s",
    "an inductor and a capacitor, in series",
    "nothing at all: a plain wire"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** "In phase" means $\tan\varphi = 0$, so $\omega L = 1/(\omega C)$ at that frequency.

<Step title="The two measurements">

With an inductor alone the current would lag at every frequency, and with a capacitor alone it would lead at every frequency. A plain wire would keep the current in phase at 2 rad/s too. In phase at one frequency and lagging at another needs both reactances: at $\omega = 1$ rad/s they cancel (resonance), and at the higher frequency the inductor's wins.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

With both elements the current leads below the resonant frequency, is in phase at it and lags above it, which fits both measurements; no single element fits both.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={6}>

Find the inductance or the capacitance in the box, or both, as the case may be. Type the inductance in henries in the first box, and $1/C$ in 1/F in the second box; type 0 in a box whose element is absent. Simple fractions are enough; no calculator is needed.

<Hint>

The condition at 1 rad/s gives one equation for $L$ and $C$; the lag of $\pi/4$ at 2 rad/s gives $\omega L - 1/(\omega C) = R$ there, a second one.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d1" label="$L =$" value="0.6667" unit="H" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-L2" />

<Answer id="p2-d2" label="$1/C =$" value="0.6667" unit="1/F" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-C2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two phase measurements give two equations for the two unknowns.

<Step title="Resonance at 1 rad/s">

$\tan\varphi = 0$ at $\omega = 1$ rad/s: $1 \cdot L = 1/(1 \cdot C)$, so $LC = 1\ \text{s}^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The lag at 2 rad/s">

$\tan(\pi/4) = 1$, so $2L - 1/(2C) = R = 1$ Ω. With $1/C = L$ (in these units) this is $2L - L/2 = 1$:

<Formula label="The second black box" tex="\frac32\,L = 1\ \Omega\,\text{s}, \qquad L = \frac23\ \text{H}, \qquad \frac1C = L \cdot (1\ \text{rad/s})^2 = \frac23\ \text{F}^{-1}" cite="mit" at="Question 14, p. 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At 2 rad/s: $\omega L = 4/3$ Ω and $1/(\omega C) = 1/3$ Ω, a difference of 1 Ω $= R$, so $\tan\varphi = 1$. At 1 rad/s both reactances are $2/3$ Ω. Both boxes hold a number other than 0, so the box holds both elements, as part (c) found. The capacitance is $C = 1.5$ F, a huge capacitor; the numbers are chosen to make the arithmetic simple.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

Find the time-averaged power the generator delivers at $\omega = 1$ rad/s, in watts, and type it in the box.

<Answer id="p2-e" label="$\langle P\rangle =$" value="0.5" unit="W" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-P" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At resonance the box acts as a wire: the impedance is $R$ and $\cos\varphi = 1$.

<Step title="The power">

$I_0 = V_0/R = 1$ A, so by Eq. (8.2) $\langle P\rangle = \frac12 I_0V_0\cos 0 = \frac12 (1\ \text{A})(1\ \text{V}) = 0.5$ W, all of it heat in the resistor.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

This is $\frac12 I_0^2R$, the resistor's heating alone: an inductor and a capacitor take no energy on average, as in Problem 1.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Opening the switch of an LR circuit" points={20}>

(Course notes, Chapter 11, Additional Problem 11.13.4, p. 48.) A battery of emf $\varepsilon$, a resistor $R_1$ and an inductor $L$ are joined in one loop, together with a second resistor $R_2$, much larger than $R_1$ ($R_2 \gg R_1$). A switch S is connected across $R_2$, side by side with it. While S is closed it carries the whole current around $R_2$; when it is opened, the current must pass through $R_2$. (The notes put a subscript zero on the emf.) The switch has been closed for a long time when it is opened at $t = 0$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the steady current $I_0$ that flows before $t = 0$, in terms of $\varepsilon$, $R_1$ and $R_2$, and type it in the box.

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$I_0 =$" expr="eps/R1"
  vars={{ eps: [1, 12], R1: [1, 2], R2: [50, 200] }}
  names={{ eps: "\\varepsilon", R1: "R_1", R2: "R_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "eps/(R1 + R2)", hint: "While the switch is closed, the current takes the path through the switch, which has no resistance; no current flows through R2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A steady current does not change, so $L\,dI/dt = 0$: after a long time the inductor is a plain wire.

<Step title="The steady current">

The closed switch shorts out $R_2$, so the only resistance in the loop is $R_1$: $I_0 = \varepsilon/R_1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Volts divided by ohms are amperes, and the result does not contain $R_2$, which carries no current while the switch is closed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Tuning an FM antenna" points={15}>

(Course notes, Chapter 12, Additional Problem 12.11.9, p. 39.) The circuit of an FM antenna is a resistor $R = 100$ Ω, an inductor $L = 10^{-6}$ H and a capacitor $C = 10^{-12}$ F in series, from the antenna to the ground. A passing radio wave induces in it an emf that oscillates with the amplitude $V_0 = 10^{-5}$ V.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

For which angular frequency $\omega_0$ of the incoming wave is the current in the circuit largest (the circuit is "in tune")? Give it in rad/s.

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$\omega_0 =$" value="1e9" unit="rad/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-omega0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The current amplitude $V_0/Z$ is largest where the impedance is smallest, at $\omega L = 1/(\omega C)$.

<Step title="The resonant frequency">

$\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC} = 1/\sqrt{10^{-6} \times 10^{-12}}\ \text{s}^{-1} = 1/10^{-9}\ \text{s}^{-1} = 10^{9}$ rad/s, that is $f_0 = \omega_0/2\pi = 159$ MHz.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At this frequency $\omega_0 L = 10^9 \times 10^{-6}$ Ω $= 1000$ Ω and $1/(\omega_0 C) = 1/(10^9 \times 10^{-12})$ Ω $= 1000$ Ω: the two reactances cancel, as they must.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the quality factor $Q$ of the resonance (a pure number).

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$Q =$" value="10" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-Q" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The quality factor compares the resonant frequency with the width of the peak, $R/L$.

<Step title="The number">

$Q = \omega_0 L/R = (10^9)(10^{-6})/100 = 10$. The peak is a tenth of $\omega_0$ wide: $\Delta\omega = R/L = 10^8$ rad/s.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$\omega_0 L$ and $R$ are both in ohms, so $Q$ is a pure number, as it should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the amplitude of the current at the in-tune frequency, in amperes.

<Answer id="p5-c" label="$I_0 =$" value="1e-7" unit="A" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-I0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At resonance the reactances cancel and only the resistance limits the current.

<Step title="The current">

$Z = R$, so $I_0 = V_0/R = 10^{-5}\ \text{V}/100\ \Omega = 10^{-7}$ A.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Volts divided by ohms are amperes; at any other frequency the reactances no longer cancel, $Z$ is larger than $R$ and the current is smaller.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Find the amplitude of the potential difference across the capacitor at the in-tune frequency, in volts.

<Hint>

The capacitor's voltage amplitude is the current amplitude times its reactance $1/(\omega C)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-d" label="$V_{C0} =$" value="1e-4" unit="V" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-VC0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At resonance the voltages across the inductor and the capacitor cancel each other, so each of them can be much larger than the emf that drives the circuit.

<Step title="The voltage">

$V_{C0} = I_0/(\omega_0 C) = 10^{-7}/(10^9 \times 10^{-12})\ \text{V} = 10^{-4}$ V.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

This is ten times the driving amplitude $10^{-5}$ V: at resonance $V_{C0}/V_0 = 1/(\omega_0 CR) = \omega_0 L/R = Q$. The tuned circuit amplifies the signal by its quality factor, which is why a sharp resonance both picks out one station and makes its signal larger.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
