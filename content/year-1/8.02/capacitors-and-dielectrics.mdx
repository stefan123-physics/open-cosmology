Session 8 finishes [conductors](/courses/8-02/conductors-and-capacitors) with shielding and the Faraday ice-pail experiment, then combines capacitors and fills them with insulators (slides 1–3, 11–13).

## Conductors as shields

A hollow conductor keeps inside and outside apart (slides 4–6):

- A charge $+q$ placed **inside** the cavity induces $-q$ on the cavity wall, since a Gaussian surface within the metal must enclose no net charge; the outer surface then carries the rest, so outside the conductor still "sees" the charge (slide 5; see also [Session 7](/courses/8-02/conductors-and-capacitors)).
- A charge placed **outside** only rearranges charge on the outer surface. The field inside the metal, and in the empty cavity, stays zero: the conductor **shields** its inside from outside charges (slide 6).

Experiment 3, the Faraday ice pail, uses this: lower a charged object into a metal pail without touching it, and the charge induced on the outside of the pail measures the object's charge (slide 11 gives only the experiment's title; this explanation is the page's own).

## Batteries

An **ideal battery** holds a fixed potential difference between its terminals, supplying whatever charge it takes to do so: think of it as making a hill of potential (slide 15). Two batteries in series add their voltages, two hills stacked (slide 16). Two equal batteries in parallel still give the same voltage, and the slides warn against connecting them that way (slide 17).

## Capacitors in parallel and in series

**In parallel**, both capacitors connect to the same two points, so they have the same voltage $\Delta V$ and each holds $Q_i = C_i\,\Delta V$. The battery supplies the total charge $Q = Q_1 + Q_2$ (course notes, Section 5.3.1, p. 9; slides 18–20):

<Formula label="Capacitors in parallel" tex="C_{\text{eq}} = \frac{Q}{\Delta V} = C_1 + C_2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (5.3.3), p. 9" />

**In series**, one after the other, the inner plates start uncharged, so the outer plates' charges $\pm Q$ induce $\mp Q$ on them: every capacitor holds the same charge. The voltages, $Q/C_1$ and $Q/C_2$, add up to the total (Section 5.3.2, p. 10; slides 21–23):

<Formula label="Capacitors in series" tex="\frac{1}{C_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (5.3.7), p. 10" />

Both extend to any number of capacitors (pp. 9, 11), and mixed networks are reduced step by step, a parallel pair first, then in series with the next (Example 5.4, p. 11).

## Dielectrics

A **dielectric** is an insulator, such as rubber, glass or waxed paper. Placed between the plates of a charged capacitor, it lowers the potential difference between them (slide 27). Experiments show that filling the gap multiplies the capacitance by the **dielectric constant** $\kappa$, always greater than 1 (course notes, Section 5.5, pp. 15–16):

<Formula label="Capacitance with a dielectric" tex="C = \kappa\,C_0" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (5.5.1), p. 15" />

Some values: air 1.00059, paper 3.7, glass 4 to 6, water 80 (Section 5.5, p. 16; slide 32). Each material also has a **dielectric strength**, the largest field it stands before it breaks down and conducts: $3 \times 10^6$ V/m for air, more for paper and glass (p. 16), so dielectrics also let capacitors take higher voltages (p. 15).

**Why?** Molecules in the field become aligned dipoles (Section 5.5, pp. 16–17; slides 28–29):

- **polar** molecules, such as water, have permanent dipole moments, randomly oriented until a field exerts a torque on them; thermal motion keeps the alignment partial;
- **non-polar** molecules, such as methane, get dipole moments induced by the field.

Inside the material the aligned dipoles cancel on average, but a layer of charge is left on each face: positive on the face towards the negative plate, negative on the other (Section 5.5.1, pp. 17–19). These induced surface charges, of density $\sigma'$, make a field against the original one (slides 30–31):

<Formula label="Field in a dielectric" tex="E = \frac{\sigma - \sigma'}{\varepsilon_0} = \frac{E_0}{\kappa}" cite="reading2" at="slides 31–32" />

so the field, and the voltage $Ed$, are reduced by $\kappa$. The notes describe the same thing through the **polarization** $\vec P$, the dipole moment per unit volume, which acts like a surface charge density $|\vec P|$ (Eqs. 5.5.2–5.5.5, pp. 18–19), with $\kappa = 1 + \chi_e$ where $\chi_e$ is the electric susceptibility (Eq. 5.5.11, p. 20). Gauss's law then counts only the **free** charge on the plates, if the field is multiplied by $\kappa$ (slide 32):

<Formula label="Gauss's law with a dielectric" tex="\oint_S \kappa\,\vec E \cdot d\vec A = \frac{q_{\text{free}}}{\varepsilon_0}" cite="reading2" at="slide 32" />

**Battery disconnected or connected** (Sections 5.5.2–5.5.3, pp. 20–22; slides 33–34). If the capacitor is charged and then disconnected, its charge $Q_0$ stays, and the dielectric lowers the voltage to $\Delta V_0/\kappa$. If the battery stays connected, the voltage stays and the battery supplies more charge, $\kappa Q_0$. Either way $C = \kappa C_0$. The session ends with a group problem, a capacitor only partly filled with a dielectric (slide 36), left for the problem pages.

<WorkedExample title="A network, then a dielectric">
<Problem>
(a) $C_1 = 2\ \mu$F and $C_2 = 4\ \mu$F in parallel, in series with $C_3 = 3\ \mu$F, across 12 V. Find the equivalent capacitance, the charge, the voltage across $C_3$ and the stored energy. (b) A 88.5 pF air capacitor is charged to 100 V, disconnected, and filled with paper ($\kappa = 3.7$). Find the new capacitance, voltage and energy.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The network">
$C_{12} = 2 + 4 = 6\ \mu$F, in series with $3\ \mu$F: $C_{\text{eq}} = 6 \times 3/(6 + 3) = 2\ \mu$F. Charge from the battery: $Q = (2\ \mu\text{F})(12\text{ V}) = 24\ \mu$C, the same on $C_{12}$ and $C_3$. So $\Delta V_3 = 24/3 = 8$ V and $\Delta V_{12} = 24/6 = 4$ V, adding to 12 V. Energy: $\frac12 C_{\text{eq}}\Delta V^2 = \frac12(2 \times 10^{-6})(144) = 1.44 \times 10^{-4}$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Adding paper">
The charge stays at $Q_0 = (88.5 \times 10^{-12})(100) = 8.85 \times 10^{-9}$ C. $C = 3.7 \times 88.5 \approx 327$ pF, $\Delta V = 100/3.7 \approx 27.0$ V, and the energy $Q_0^2/2C$ falls from $4.43 \times 10^{-7}$ J to $4.43 \times 10^{-7}/3.7 \approx 1.20 \times 10^{-7}$ J: the field does work pulling the dielectric in.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A hollow conductor shields its inside: charges outside leave the field inside zero; a charge inside induces its opposite on the inner wall.
- Ideal battery: fixed potential difference; in series voltages add.
- Parallel capacitors: $C_{\text{eq}} = C_1 + C_2$; series: $1/C_{\text{eq}} = 1/C_1 + 1/C_2$.
- A dielectric is polarized, weakens the field by $\kappa$ and raises the capacitance to $\kappa C_0$.
