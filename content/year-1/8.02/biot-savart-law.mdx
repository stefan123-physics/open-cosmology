Session 15 has two halves: currents **feel** magnetic forces (Experiment 6), and currents **create** magnetic fields (slides 1, 4–12 and 13–33).

## The force on a current-carrying wire

A charged particle moving through a field feels $q\vec v \times \vec B$, and with a perpendicular velocity it moves on a circle (slides 4–5; see [the previous session](/courses/8-02/magnetic-fields)). A current is a stream of moving charges, so a wire carrying current feels a force too: a wire between the poles of a magnet jumps one way or the other depending on the current's direction (course notes, Chapter 8, Section 8.3, p. 3; slides 6–9). For a straight segment of length $L$, with the charges drifting at $\vec v_d$ and $I = nqv_dA$ (Section 8.3, p. 4; slide 7):

<Formula label="Force on a straight wire" tex="\vec F_B = I\,\vec L \times \vec B" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.3.1), p. 4" />

where $\vec L$ points along the current. For a wire of any shape, add the forces on its small pieces $d\vec s$ (Section 8.3, p. 4):

<Formula label="Force on a curved wire" tex="\vec F_B = I\int_a^b d\vec s \times \vec B" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.3.3), p. 4" />

In a uniform field, a curved wire from $a$ to $b$ feels the same force as a straight one between the same ends, and a **closed loop** feels no net force, since its steps $d\vec s$ add up to zero (Section 8.3, p. 5). The notes check this for a semicircular loop: the forces on the straight side and on the arc, each $2IBR$, are opposite (Example 8.1, pp. 5–6).

## Currents create fields

What happens when two currents are side by side? Parallel currents attract and opposite currents repel (slides 13–14; the result is stated in the notes, Section 9.2, p. 13). The explanation: moving charges also **create** magnetic fields, and each wire feels the other's field (slide 15). Compare the field of a point charge, $\vec E = q\hat r/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$, with that of a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ (slides 17–18; Chapter 9, Section 9.1.1, p. 10):

<Formula label="Field of a moving charge" tex="\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{q\,\vec v \times \hat r}{r^2}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (9.1.20), p. 10" />

with $\hat r$ pointing from the charge to the field point, valid for speeds well below that of light (p. 10). The constant is the **permeability of free space**, $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}$ T m/A (Eq. 9.1.2, p. 2; slide 18).

## The Biot–Savart law

A short piece $d\vec s$ of wire carrying current $I$ creates the field (Chapter 9, Section 9.1, p. 2; slide 19):

<Formula label="Biot–Savart law" tex="d\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec s \times \hat r}{r^2}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (9.1.1), p. 2" />

It is remarkably like Coulomb's law for a charge element, $dE = dq/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$, with the cross product as the new ingredient: the field is perpendicular both to the current and to the line to the field point (right-hand rule, slide 20). The total field is the integral over the wire, really three integrals, one per component, as for [continuous charge](/courses/8-02/continuous-charge-distributions) (Eq. 9.1.4, p. 3).

## A long straight wire

For a straight wire along the $x$-axis and a point at distance $a$, every $d\vec s \times \hat r$ points the same way (out of the page, for a current in the $+x$ direction and the point above the wire), and the substitution $x = a\cot\theta$ gives $B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi a}(\cos\theta_2 + \cos\theta_1)$, with $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$ the angles to the two ends (Chapter 9, Example 9.1, pp. 3–5). For an infinitely long wire both cosines are 1 (p. 5):

<Formula label="Field of a long straight wire" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (9.1.7), p. 5" />

The field lines are circles around the wire; with the right thumb along the current, the fingers curl along $\vec B$ (p. 6).

## A circular loop

**At the centre** (slides 23–27): for a coil of radius $R$, the straight leads contribute nothing ($d\vec s$ parallel to $\hat r$), and on the ring $d\vec s \perp \hat r$ with $ds = R\,d\theta$ and $r = R$, so every piece adds $\mu_0 I\,d\theta/4\pi R$, all in the same direction. Integrating over $2\pi$ (slide 26):

<Formula label="Field at the centre of a ring" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R}" cite="reading3" at="slide 26" />

an easy Biot–Savart problem with no vectors to juggle (slide 27).

**On the axis**, at height $z$ (Chapter 9, Example 9.2, pp. 6–8): the components across the axis cancel by symmetry and only $B_z$ survives:

<Formula label="Field on the axis of a ring" tex="B_z = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2\,(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (9.1.15), p. 8" />

At $z = 0$ this is $\mu_0 I/2R$ again; far away it falls as $1/z^3$, the field of a magnetic dipole (the page's own remark).

## Two parallel wires

Wire 2, a distance $a$ from wire 1, makes the field $\mu_0 I_2/2\pi a$ at wire 1, perpendicular to it. A length $\ell$ of wire 1 then feels (Chapter 9, Section 9.2, pp. 12–13):

<Formula label="Force between parallel wires" tex="F_{12} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2\,\ell}{2\pi a}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (9.2.1), p. 13" />

towards wire 2 for currents in the same direction, away for opposite currents (p. 13).

## Field pressure and tension

The slides end with a way to picture these forces: electric and magnetic field lines behave like stretched elastic that also pushes sideways. They transmit a **tension** along themselves (they try to straighten) and a **pressure** across themselves (neighbouring lines repel). A charge moving through a uniform field piles up field lines on one side, and the imbalance pushes it the other way, while the bent field lines behind it pull on it like stretched elastic: together that is $q\vec v \times \vec B$ (slides 30–33; slide 32 shows the same for a charge in an electric field).

<WorkedExample title="Fields and forces from a few amperes">
<Problem>
(a) A long straight wire carries 10 A. Find its field 5 cm away. (b) A coil of radius 0.1 m with 100 turns carries 5 A. Find the field at its centre. (c) Two long parallel wires 5 cm apart each carry 10 A in the same direction. Find the force per metre. (d) A 0.2 m piece of wire carrying 10 A crosses a 0.5 T field at right angles. Find the force on it.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Straight wire">
$B = \mu_0 I/2\pi a = (2 \times 10^{-7})(10)/0.05 = 4 \times 10^{-5}$ T, comparable to the Earth's field.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Coil">
One turn: $\mu_0 I/2R = (4\pi \times 10^{-7})(5)/0.2 \approx 3.14 \times 10^{-5}$ T; 100 turns add up to about $3.14 \times 10^{-3}$ T.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Two wires">
$F/\ell = \mu_0 I_1I_2/2\pi a = (2 \times 10^{-7})(100)/0.05 = 4 \times 10^{-4}$ N per metre, attractive.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Wire in a field">
$F = ILB = (10)(0.2)(0.5) = 1$ N.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Currents feel forces: $\vec F = I\vec L \times \vec B$; zero net force on a closed loop in a uniform field.
- Currents create fields: Biot–Savart $d\vec B = (\mu_0/4\pi)\,I\,d\vec s \times \hat r/r^2$; a moving charge gives $(\mu_0/4\pi)\,q\vec v \times \hat r/r^2$.
- Long wire: $\mu_0 I/2\pi a$, circling it. Ring: $\mu_0 I/2R$ at the centre, $\mu_0 IR^2/2(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}$ on the axis.
- Parallel currents attract with $\mu_0 I_1I_2/2\pi a$ per unit length; opposite currents repel.
