Bar magnets [create and feel](/courses/8-02/magnetic-fields) dipole fields, and currents [create](/courses/8-02/biot-savart-law) magnetic fields. This session puts the two together: a current loop **is** a magnetic dipole (slides 1, 5).

## Torque on a current loop

Put a rectangular loop with sides $a$ and $b$, carrying current $I$, in a uniform field $\vec B = B\,\hat i$ lying in the plane of the loop (course notes, Chapter 8, Section 8.4, p. 7; slides 7–8). The two sides parallel to $\vec B$ feel no force. The other two, of length $a$, feel equal and opposite forces $IaB$, one out of the page and one into it, so the net force is zero (Eq. 8.4.2). But the forces act on opposite sides, a distance $b$ apart, and so they twist the loop: the torque about its centre is $IabB = IAB$ (Eq. 8.4.3, p. 7).

With the **area vector** $\vec A = A\,\hat n$, normal to the loop with its sense given by the right-hand rule (fingers along the current), this is $\vec\tau = I\vec A \times \vec B$ (Section 8.4, p. 8; slide 9), and it also holds when $\vec A$ makes any angle $\theta$ with $\vec B$, giving $\tau = IAB\sin\theta$. For a coil of $N$ turns the torque is $N$ times larger. The quantity $NI\vec A$ is the **magnetic dipole moment** (pp. 8–9; slide 10):

<Formula label="Magnetic dipole moment" tex="\vec\mu = NI\vec A" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.4.8), p. 8" />

in A m², and the torque is

<Formula label="Torque on a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec\tau = \vec\mu \times \vec B" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.4.9), p. 9" />

just like $\vec\tau = \vec p \times \vec E$ for an [electric dipole](/courses/8-02/electric-dipoles) (p. 9; slide 10). It turns $\vec\mu$ towards $\vec B$: that is how a compass needle lines up, and how a **galvanometer**, a coil on a spring in a magnet's field, measures current (slides 11–13). One way to picture it: the external field joins onto the field of the needle and pulls it into line (slides 11–12). A needle knocked out of line oscillates about the field direction; the torque on it is matched by an opposite torque on the currents in the Earth's core that make the field, so angular momentum is conserved (Chapter 8, Section 8.4, pp. 11–12).

## Energy

Turning the dipole from angle $\theta_0$ to $\theta$ against the torque takes work $\mu B(\cos\theta_0 - \cos\theta)$ (Section 8.4, p. 9). Taking $U = 0$ at $\theta = 90°$:

<Formula label="Energy of a magnetic dipole" tex="U = -\mu B\cos\theta = -\vec\mu \cdot \vec B" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.4.11), p. 9" />

It is lowest, $-\mu B$, with $\vec\mu$ along $\vec B$ (stable) and highest, $+\mu B$, against it (unstable) (p. 9).

## Force in a non-uniform field

A dipole in a uniform field rotates but does not move as a whole; yet magnets clearly pull on each other (slides 16–18). The difference is that their fields are **not uniform**. The force is minus the change of energy with position (Section 8.4.1, pp. 10–11; slide 19):

<Formula label="Force on a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec F_B = -\nabla U = \nabla\left(\vec\mu \cdot \vec B\right)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.4.15), p. 10" />

In a uniform field the gradient is zero: no force. For a dipole on the axis of a field $B_z(z)$, pointing along $z$, it is $F_z = \mu_z\,dB_z/dz$ (slides 19–20). A dipole aligned with the field is pulled towards **stronger** field. Above the north pole of a bar magnet the field points up and weakens upwards, so an upward-pointing dipole is pulled down: two magnets lined up north to south attract, and turned against each other they repel (slides 20–22, 24–25). Experiment 7 measures these forces (slide 23).

## The field of a dipole

Far from a small current loop, its field has the same shape as the field of an electric dipole (course notes, Chapter 9, Section 9.5, pp. 23–24):

<Formula label="Field of a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}\left[3(\vec\mu \cdot \hat r)\,\hat r - \vec\mu\right]" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (9.5.5), p. 24" />

compared with $\vec E = [3(\vec p \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec p]/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3)$. On the axis it gives the $1/z^3$ fall-off of the ring field. The Earth's field is close to such a dipole field, with the dipole's south pole in the northern hemisphere so that it attracts the north ends of compass needles (Section 9.5.1, p. 24).

<WorkedExample title="A small coil in a field">
<Problem>
A coil of 50 turns and radius 2 cm carries 0.5 A. (a) Find its magnetic moment. (b) In a uniform 0.2 T field at $30°$ to $\vec\mu$, find the torque. (c) How much work does it take to turn it from aligned to anti-aligned? (d) In a field pointing along $+z$ that changes along the axis at $dB_z/dz = -0.5$ T/m, with $\vec\mu$ along $+z$, find the force.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Moment">
$\mu = NI\pi R^2 = 50 \times 0.5 \times \pi(0.02)^2 \approx 0.0314$ A m².
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Torque">
$\tau = \mu B\sin 30° = 0.0314 \times 0.2 \times 0.5 \approx 3.14 \times 10^{-3}$ N m.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Work">
From $U = -\mu B$ to $U = +\mu B$: $2\mu B = 2 \times 0.0314 \times 0.2 \approx 0.0126$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Force">
$F_z = \mu\,dB_z/dz = 0.0314 \times (-0.5) \approx -0.0157$ N: towards $-z$, where the field is stronger.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A current loop in a uniform field: no net force, torque $\vec\tau = \vec\mu \times \vec B$, with $\vec\mu = NI\vec A$.
- Energy $U = -\vec\mu \cdot \vec B$: lowest when aligned.
- Force $\vec F = \nabla(\vec\mu \cdot \vec B)$, only in non-uniform fields; aligned dipoles move towards stronger field, so magnets aligned end to end attract (side by side, parallel dipoles repel).
- Far away a current loop makes a dipole field, $\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}[3(\vec\mu \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec\mu]$.
