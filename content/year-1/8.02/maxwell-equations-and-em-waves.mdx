Session 28 brings everything together: one missing term in Ampère's law, the complete set of Maxwell's equations, and their most famous consequence, light (slides 1–2).

## The displacement current

Take a capacitor being charged by a current $I$, and apply [Ampère's law](/courses/8-02/ampere-law) to a loop around the wire just outside the plate (course notes, Section 13.1, pp. 2–3; slide 4). Through a flat surface bounded by the loop, the current is $I$. But the loop also bounds a surface that bulges between the plates, where no charge flows: there $I_{\text{enc}} = 0$. Ampère's law gives two answers.

Between the plates, though, the **electric field is changing**. Its flux is $\Phi_E = EA = Q/\varepsilon_0$, so $\varepsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt = dQ/dt = I$. Maxwell called this the **displacement current** (course notes, Section 13.1, pp. 3–4; slide 5):

<Formula label="Displacement current" tex="I_d = \varepsilon_0\,\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.1.3), p. 3" />

Adding it to Ampère's law removes the ambiguity: through the bulging surface $I_d = I$ (pp. 3–4; slide 6):

<Formula label="Ampère–Maxwell law" tex="\oint_C \vec B \cdot d\vec s = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.1.4), p. 3" />

Now there is a symmetry: a changing magnetic field makes an electric field (Faraday), and a changing electric field makes a magnetic field.

## Maxwell's equations

Electric fields are created by charges and by changing magnetic fields; magnetic fields by moving charges (never magnetic charges) and by changing electric fields (slide 9). In integral form (course notes, Section 13.3, p. 5; slide 10):

<Formula label="Maxwell's equations" tex="\oint_S \vec E \cdot d\vec A = \frac{Q_{\text{in}}}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \oint_C \vec E \cdot d\vec s = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}, \qquad \oint_S \vec B \cdot d\vec A = 0, \qquad \oint_C \vec B \cdot d\vec s = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}" cite="reading2" at="slide 10" />

[Gauss's law](/courses/8-02/gauss-law), [Faraday's law](/courses/8-02/faradays-law), Gauss's law for magnetism (no monopoles, p. 4) and the Ampère–Maxwell law. With the **Lorentz force** $\vec F = q(\vec E + \vec v \times \vec B)$ they contain all of classical electromagnetism (slide 10). The differential forms, $\nabla \cdot \vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, $\nabla \times \vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t$, $\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0$, $\nabla \times \vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial\vec E/\partial t$, are the ones met in [18.02](/courses/18-02/div-curl-and-maxwell) (Eq. 13.3.1, p. 5).

## Travelling waves

A function $f(x - vt)$ is the shape $f(x)$ moving to the right at speed $v$ (slide 15). A sine wave $y = y_0\sin(kx - \omega t)$ has amplitude $y_0$, wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/k$ ($k$ is the wave number), angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$, period $T = 1/f$, and speed $v = \omega/k = \lambda f$ (slides 16–18). For light, $c = \lambda f$ (slide 19).

## Electromagnetic waves

In empty space, with no charges or currents, a changing $\vec E$ makes $\vec B$ and a changing $\vec B$ makes $\vec E$, so the fields can sustain each other (course notes, Section 13.3, p. 5). Take a wave travelling along $x$ with $\vec E$ along $y$ and $\vec B$ along $z$. Faraday's law around a small loop in the $xy$-plane, and the Ampère–Maxwell law around one in the $xz$-plane, give (Section 13.4, pp. 6–8):

<Formula label="Fields of a plane wave" tex="\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial t}, \qquad -\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial x} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (13.4.5) and (13.4.9), pp. 7–8" />

Differentiating again and combining, both fields satisfy the **wave equation**, with speed (Section 13.4, pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Speed of electromagnetic waves" tex="c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} \approx 2.998 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.4.15), p. 9" />

the measured speed of light. (The notes print 2.997; their own rounded constants give 2.998.) Light is an electromagnetic wave, and so are radio waves, microwaves, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays, at different frequencies (p. 9).

**Properties** (slides 23–24; Section 13.4, pp. 6, 10–11): the wave is **transverse**, with $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel, which is the direction of $\vec E \times \vec B$; the two fields oscillate **in phase**, as $E = E_0\cos(kx - \omega t)$ and $B = B_0\cos(kx - \omega t)$; and their sizes are in the ratio $E/B = c$.

## Energy and the Poynting vector

The wave carries the energy densities of both fields, $u_E = \frac12\varepsilon_0 E^2$ and $u_B = B^2/2\mu_0$, which are equal in a light wave; it moves at $c$, so the energy flowing per unit area per second is (course notes, Section 13.6, pp. 14–15; slides 28–29)

<Formula label="Poynting vector" tex="\vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\vec E \times \vec B" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.6.5), p. 15" />

in W/m², pointing along the direction of travel. Its time average is the **intensity** (p. 16; slide 29):

<Formula label="Intensity" tex="I = \langle S\rangle = \frac{E_0B_0}{2\mu_0} = \frac{E_0^2}{2\mu_0 c} = \frac{cB_0^2}{2\mu_0}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.6.8), p. 16" />

For example, sunlight at the top of the atmosphere has $\langle S\rangle = 1.35 \times 10^3$ W/m², which means fields $E_0 \approx 1.01 \times 10^3$ V/m and $B_0 \approx 3.4 \times 10^{-6}$ T (Example 13.1, pp. 16–17). From a point source, the intensity falls as $1/r^2$ (Eq. 13.6.13, p. 17). The Poynting vector also shows where energy goes in circuits: it points **into** a resistor's surface, into an inductor while its current grows and out while it falls (slides 30–33).

<WorkedExample title="Fields in a beam and the waves of a radio">
<Problem>
(a) A capacitor with circular plates of radius 5 cm is charged by a 2 A current. Find the magnetic field at the edge of the plates. (b) A 1 mW laser beam has a cross-section of 1 mm². Find its intensity and the amplitudes $E_0$ and $B_0$. (c) Find the wavelength and wave number of a 100 MHz radio wave.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Displacement current">
Between the plates $I_d = I = 2$ A. A circle of radius $R$ at the edge encloses all of it: $B = \mu_0 I_d/2\pi R = (2 \times 10^{-7})(2)/0.05 = 8 \times 10^{-6}$ T.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The laser">
$I = 10^{-3}/10^{-6} = 1000$ W/m². $E_0 = \sqrt{2\mu_0 c I} = \sqrt{2(4\pi \times 10^{-7})(2.998 \times 10^8)(1000)} \approx 868$ V/m and $B_0 = E_0/c \approx 2.9 \times 10^{-6}$ T.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The radio wave">
$\lambda = c/f = (2.998 \times 10^8)/10^8 \approx 3.0$ m and $k = 2\pi/\lambda \approx 2.10$ rad/m.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Light from the beginning">
Almost everything we know about the universe arrives as electromagnetic waves: starlight, radio waves from galaxies, and the cosmic microwave background, light left over from the hot early universe. That the intensity of a source falls as $1/r^2$ is the basis of measuring distances with "standard candles"; and in 8.286, treating the background radiation as electromagnetic waves in thermal equilibrium gives its energy density and spectrum.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Displacement current $I_d = \varepsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt$ completes Ampère's law: changing $\vec E$ makes $\vec B$.
- Maxwell's equations: Gauss for $\vec E$ and $\vec B$, Faraday, Ampère–Maxwell; with the Lorentz force, all of classical electromagnetism.
- In vacuum they give transverse waves at $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$, with $\vec E \perp \vec B$, in phase, $E = cB$.
- Energy flow: $\vec S = \vec E \times \vec B/\mu_0$; intensity $E_0B_0/2\mu_0$.
