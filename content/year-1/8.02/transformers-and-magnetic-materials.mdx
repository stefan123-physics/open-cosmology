Session 21 starts with Experiment 9 on [Faraday's law](/courses/8-02/faradays-law), then looks at three consequences: magnetic brakes, transformers, and magnetic materials (slides 1–4).

## A ring passing a magnet

A ring moving down past a magnet's dipole field first meets more flux, then less once it has passed the middle (slides 6–9). So the induced current flows one way while the flux grows and the other way while it falls, but it **always** opposes the change: first it repels the magnet, then it pulls back on it (slide 9). Experiment 9 records exactly this: a pulse of current one way, then the other (slides 11–12).

## Eddy-current brakes

Where does the falling magnet's kinetic energy go as it slows near the ring (slide 15)? Into heat in the ring (slides 19–20). In a solid sheet of metal the induced currents, **eddy currents**, circulate inside the metal itself. A magnet moving past a metal plate, or a metal disc turning between a magnet's poles, induces eddy currents whose magnetic force opposes the motion; their energy is lost as Joule heat, at the rate $\varepsilon^2/R$ (course notes, Chapter 10, Section 10.5, p. 13; slides 16–20). This is how **eddy-current brakes** work. Where the losses are unwanted, as in transformer cores, the metal is cut or laminated into thin insulated sheets to raise its resistance (Section 10.5, p. 13). A magnet over a superconductor floats (slide 21); in simple terms, the superconductor carries induced currents that meet no resistance and so do not die away (the page's own, simplified explanation; real superconductors also expel and pin magnetic flux).

## Mutual inductance

Two coils near each other: a current $I_1$ in coil 1 sends some flux $\Phi_{21}$ through each turn of coil 2. If $I_1$ changes, so does that flux, and coil 2 gets an emf proportional to $dI_1/dt$ (course notes, Chapter 11, Section 11.1, pp. 2–3; slide 24):

<Formula label="Mutual inductance" tex="\varepsilon_{21} = -M_{21}\,\frac{dI_1}{dt}, \qquad M_{21} = \frac{N_2\,\Phi_{21}}{I_1}" cite="reading2" at="Eqs. (11.1.1)–(11.1.3), p. 2" />

The unit is the **henry**, 1 H = 1 T m²/A (p. 3). $M$ depends only on the coils' numbers of turns, shapes and positions, and it is the same both ways, $M_{12} = M_{21} = M$ (Eq. 11.1.8, p. 3). For a small loop of radius $R_2$ at the centre of a large coplanar loop of radius $R_1$, the field of the large loop is nearly uniform across the small one, giving $M = \mu_0\pi R_2^2/2R_1$ (Example 11.1, p. 4). A "remote speaker" demonstration plays music through two coils that do not touch (slide 25).

## Transformers

A **transformer** winds two coils, the primary with $N_p$ turns and the secondary with $N_s$, on one iron core, so that the same changing flux $\Phi_B$ passes through every turn of both (slide 26). Each turn gets the same emf, so

<Formula label="Transformer" tex="\varepsilon_p = -N_p\,\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}, \qquad \varepsilon_s = -N_s\,\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}, \qquad \frac{\varepsilon_s}{\varepsilon_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p}" cite="reading4" at="slide 26" />

(The slide gives the sizes, without the minus signs.) With $N_s > N_p$ it steps the voltage **up**, with $N_s < N_p$ **down** (slide 26). A one-turn secondary carries a huge current and melts a nail; a many-turn secondary gives the high voltage that makes a spark climb a "Jacob's ladder" (slide 27).

**Power lines.** For a fixed power $P = IV$, a higher voltage means a smaller current, and the loss in the lines' resistance, $I^2R$, falls as the square of the current (slides 28–29). The slides' example: sending 120 kW through lines of 0.40 Ω at 240 V needs 500 A and loses 100 kW, 83% of it; at 24,000 V the current is 5 A and the loss only 10 W (slide 29). Transformers make the high voltage, and then bring it back down for use.

## Magnetic materials

Matter affects magnetic fields very differently from the way [dielectrics](/courses/8-02/capacitors-and-dielectrics) affect electric fields: a dielectric always weakens $\vec E$, but a magnetic material can weaken $\vec B$ a little (**diamagnets**), strengthen it a little (**paramagnets**), or strengthen it a lot (**ferromagnets**) (course notes, Chapter 9, Section 9.6, p. 27).

The **magnetization** $\vec M$ is the magnetic dipole moment per unit volume. A cylinder full of aligned dipoles acts like a solenoid: inside, their small current loops cancel, but along the surface they leave a net current, and the cylinder adds a field (Section 9.6.1, pp. 27–29; slide 35):

<Formula label="Field of a magnetized material" tex="\vec B = \vec B_0 + \mu_0\vec M" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (9.6.7), p. 30" />

The added field points **along** the dipoles, the opposite of the electric case, because inside a current loop the field is along its moment (p. 29).

- **Paramagnets** have permanent atomic moments, randomly oriented. An applied field $\vec B_0$ tends to align them, so $\vec M$ is along $\vec B_0$ and the field grows slightly; switch $B_0$ off and the order is lost (Section 9.6.2, p. 30; slides 32–34).
- For paramagnets and diamagnets, the effect is measured by the **magnetic susceptibility** $\chi_m$: $\vec M = \chi_m\vec B_0/\mu_0$, so $\vec B = (1 + \chi_m)\vec B_0 = \kappa_m\vec B_0$, with relative permeability $\kappa_m$; $\chi_m$ is small and positive for paramagnets, small and negative for diamagnets (Eqs. 9.6.8–9.6.11, pp. 30–31; slide 33).
- **Diamagnets** have no permanent moments; the applied field induces moments **against** it, so the field shrinks slightly; $\chi_m$ is about $-10^{-5}$ to $-10^{-9}$ (Section 9.6.3, p. 31).
- **Ferromagnets** have strong interactions between neighbouring moments, which line up in **domains**. An applied field aligns the domains, and the field inside can be $10^3$ to $10^4$ times the applied one. The alignment partly survives when the field is removed: the magnetization depends on the material's history (**hysteresis**), and this is what makes permanent magnets (Section 9.6.4, pp. 31–32; slides 34, 36).

How strong can a magnet be? Atomic moments are about $10^{-23}$ A m² and there are about $10^{29}$ atoms per cubic metre, so full alignment gives $M \sim 10^6$ A/m and $\mu_0 M$ of order 1 T (Eq. 9.6.12, p. 32).

## Summary

- A ring passing a magnet: the induced current reverses, always opposing the change; eddy currents brake moving metal and heat it.
- Mutual inductance: $\varepsilon_2 = -M\,dI_1/dt$, in henries.
- Transformer: $\varepsilon_s/\varepsilon_p = N_s/N_p$; high-voltage transmission cuts $I^2R$ losses.
- Magnetization adds $\mu_0\vec M$: paramagnets slightly up, ferromagnets strongly up with hysteresis, diamagnets slightly down.
