Session 20 introduces the fourth and last of Maxwell's equations (one more term will still be added to Ampère's law), and the basis of much of modern technology: **Faraday's law** (slide 8). It was met in its mathematical form in [18.02](/courses/18-02/div-curl-and-maxwell); here comes the physics.

## Induction

Some puzzling demonstrations (slides 9–14): a magnet slows down as it falls towards a copper ring cooled in liquid nitrogen; an aluminium ring jumps into the air when the coil beneath it is switched on. The rings behave like magnetic dipoles, as if currents flowed in them, though they are connected to nothing (slide 13). In 1831 Faraday found the reason: a magnet at rest near a loop does nothing, but while it moves towards or away from the loop a current flows, one way or the other (course notes, Section 10.1, p. 1). A changing magnetic field induces a current: **electromagnetic induction**.

## Magnetic flux and Faraday's law

The **magnetic flux** through a surface is defined like the electric flux of [Gauss's law](/courses/8-02/gauss-law) (course notes, Section 10.1.1, p. 2; slide 20):

<Formula label="Magnetic flux" tex="\Phi_B = \vec B \cdot \vec A = BA\cos\theta, \qquad \Phi_B = \int\int_S \vec B \cdot d\vec A" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (10.1.1)–(10.1.2), p. 2" />

the first for a uniform field, the second in general; the unit is the **weber**, 1 Wb = 1 T m² (p. 2). **Faraday's law**: the emf induced in a coil of $N$ turns is (Section 10.1.1, p. 2; slide 17):

<Formula label="Faraday's law" tex="\varepsilon = -N\,\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (10.1.4), p. 2" />

The emf is the line integral of the electric field around the loop, $\varepsilon = \oint \vec E \cdot d\vec s$: it looks like a potential difference and drives current like one (slide 18).

**Three ways to change the flux** $BA\cos\theta$ (Section 10.1.1, pp. 2–3; slides 25–32): change the size of $\vec B$ (a moving magnet), the area $A$ (a sliding rod), or the angle $\theta$ between $\vec B$ and the loop's normal (a turning coil).

## Lenz's law

The minus sign is **Lenz's law**: the induced current flows so that its own magnetic field opposes the change in flux that caused it (course notes, Section 10.1.2, p. 4; slide 22). Push the north pole of a magnet down towards a loop: the downward flux grows, so the induced current makes an upward field, counterclockwise seen from above. The loop acts like a magnet with its north pole up, repelling the approaching one (pp. 5–6). That is why the falling magnet slows near the ring, and why the ring jumps off the coil: an increasing field through it induces a current that makes it repel the coil (slides 12, 27–28). One picture: the flux "tries to stay the same", and field lines get hung up at the coil (slide 16).

## Motional emf: a sliding rod

A conducting rod of length $\ell$ moves at speed $v$ across a field $\vec B$. The charges in it feel $q\vec v \times \vec B$ along the rod and pile up at its ends until the electric field balances: $E = vB$, so the rod has an emf $B\ell v$ (course notes, Section 10.2, pp. 6–7). Now let it slide on two rails joined by a resistor $R$. The flux through the circuit is $B\ell x$ and grows as the rod moves (pp. 7–8; slide 30):

<Formula label="Emf and current of a sliding rod" tex="\varepsilon = -\frac{d}{dt}(B\ell x) = -B\ell v, \qquad I = \frac{B\ell v}{R}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (10.2.4)–(10.2.5), p. 7" />

The current in the rod feels a force $I\ell B = B^2\ell^2 v/R$ against its motion (Lenz again). To keep the rod moving, an outside force must supply power $B^2\ell^2 v^2/R$, exactly the power the resistor turns into heat (Eqs. 10.2.6–10.2.8, p. 8). Left alone, the rod slows exponentially, $v = v_0 e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = mR/B^2\ell^2$ (pp. 8–9).

## Induced electric fields

What pushes the charges round a **stationary** loop when the flux changes? (In the sliding rod it was $q\vec v \times \vec B$; here nothing moves.) Not the magnetic field, which does no work. It must be an **electric** field, and since its line integral around the loop is not zero, it is **not conservative**: it cannot be described by a potential (course notes, Section 10.3, p. 9). In Maxwell's form (slide 35):

<Formula label="Faraday's law in integral form" tex="\oint_C \vec E \cdot d\vec s = -\frac{d}{dt}\int\int_S \vec B \cdot d\vec A" cite="reading2" at="slide 35" />

Inside a long solenoid whose field grows at $dB/dt$, this gives a circling electric field $E = \frac{r}{2}\,\frac{dB}{dt}$ at radius $r$ inside, and $\frac{R^2}{2r}\,\frac{dB}{dt}$ outside a field region of radius $R$ (Eqs. 10.3.6 and 10.3.8, pp. 10–11), circling in the direction Lenz's law gives. With Gauss's law for $\vec E$ and for $\vec B$ and Ampère's law, this completes the list of Maxwell's equations, for now (slide 35).

## Generators and other applications

**Generator.** A coil of $N$ turns and area $A$ turns at angular speed $\omega$ in a uniform field, so $\Phi_B = BA\cos\omega t$ (course notes, Section 10.4, pp. 11–12; slide 38):

<Formula label="Emf of a generator" tex="\varepsilon = -N\,\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = NBA\omega\sin\omega t" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (10.4.3), p. 12" />

an alternating emf. The mechanical power needed to turn the coil against the torque on its induced current equals the electrical power delivered (Eqs. 10.4.5–10.4.9, p. 12). A motor does the reverse, turning electrical energy into mechanical work (p. 11; slides 37–38). **Eddy currents**, induced in solid conductors moving through fields, also oppose the motion and heat the metal; cutting or laminating the conductor reduces them (Section 10.5, p. 13). Faraday's law is also at work in microphones, metal detectors, induction cooktops, ground-fault interrupters and electric guitar pickups; loudspeakers and DC motors, also on the slides, run on the magnetic force on a current instead (slides 36–42).

<Problem>
(a) A rod 0.2 m long slides at 3 m/s on rails in a 0.5 T field; the circuit's resistance is 0.6 Ω. Find the emf, the current, the braking force and the power. (b) A generator coil has 100 turns of area 0.01 m² and turns 50 times a second in a 0.2 T field. Find the peak emf.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The rod">
$\varepsilon = B\ell v = (0.5)(0.2)(3) = 0.3$ V. $I = 0.3/0.6 = 0.5$ A. Force: $I\ell B = (0.5)(0.2)(0.5) = 0.05$ N, against the motion. Power: $Fv = 0.05 \times 3 = 0.15$ W $= I^2R$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The generator">
$\omega = 2\pi \times 50 \approx 314$ rad/s, so the peak emf is $NBA\omega = (100)(0.2)(0.01)(314.2) \approx 62.8$ V.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $\Phi_B = \int \vec B \cdot d\vec A$ (webers); Faraday: $\varepsilon = -N\,d\Phi_B/dt$.
- The flux changes with $B$, with the area, or with the angle.
- Lenz: induced currents oppose the change in flux.
- Sliding rod: $\varepsilon = B\ell v$, with a braking force; generator: $\varepsilon = NBA\omega\sin\omega t$.
- A changing magnetic field makes a non-conservative electric field: $\oint \vec E \cdot d\vec s = -d\Phi_B/dt$.
