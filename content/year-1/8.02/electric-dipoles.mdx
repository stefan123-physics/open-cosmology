Session 1 introduced the [electric field](/courses/8-02/fields-gravitational-and-electric) of a point charge. This session draws it with field lines, looks at how objects get charged, and studies the most important pair of charges: the electric dipole (slides 1–5).

## Field lines

For a single charge the field lines are radial: outwards for a positive charge, inwards for a negative one (course notes, Section 2.5, p. 8). Their properties (Section 2.5, p. 9; slide 5):

- The field at a point is tangent to the field line through it.
- The number of lines per unit area, through a surface across them, is proportional to the size of the field.
- Lines start on positive charges (or come in from infinity) and end on negative charges (or go off to infinity); the number of lines at a charge is proportional to its size.
- Two field lines never cross.

To sketch the pattern of several charges, use **symmetry**; the **near field**, where the nearest charge dominates and lines are radial; the **far field**, which looks like a single charge equal to the total (unless the total is zero); and **null points**, where $\vec E = \vec 0$ and no line passes (Section 2.5, p. 8).

## A charge in a field

A charge $q$ in a field $\vec E$ feels the force $\vec F_e = q\vec E$ and, by Newton's second law, accelerates (course notes, Section 2.6, pp. 9–10):

<Formula label="Acceleration in an electric field" tex="\vec a = \frac{\vec F_e}{m} = \frac{q\vec E}{m}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (2.6.2), p. 10" />

Here $\vec E$ is the field of the *other* charges, the sources: a charge exerts no force on itself (Section 2.6, p. 9). Between two oppositely charged plates the field is uniform, and a charge released from rest crosses a gap $y$ with kinetic energy $K = qE_y\,y$ (Section 2.6, p. 10).

## Charging objects

Objects can be charged by **friction** (rubbing), by **transfer** (touching a charged object), or by **induction**: a charged object brought near a neutral one separates its charges, the opposite sign gathering on the near side (slide 9). (If the near part is then cut off, or the object is grounded while the charged object is still near, it keeps a net charge of the opposite sign: this step is the page's own addition.)

## The electric dipole

An **electric dipole** is two charges $+q$ and $-q$ a distance $2a$ apart. Its **dipole moment** points from the negative to the positive charge (course notes, Section 2.7, p. 10; slide 12):

<Formula label="Dipole moment" tex="\vec p = 2qa\,\hat j" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (2.7.1), p. 10" />

with the charges on the $y$-axis at $\pm a$. Nature makes dipoles all the time: polar molecules such as HCl, CO and H₂O are dipoles, because the centres of their positive and negative charges do not coincide (Section 2.7, pp. 10–11; slide 13).

## The field of a dipole

The field at $(x, y)$ is the sum of the two point-charge fields; work in components (course notes, Section 2.7.1, p. 11; slide 15):

<Formula label="Field of a dipole" tex="E_x = k_e q\left[\frac{x}{(x^2 + (y - a)^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{x}{(x^2 + (y + a)^2)^{3/2}}\right], \qquad E_y = k_e q\left[\frac{y - a}{(x^2 + (y - a)^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{y + a}{(x^2 + (y + a)^2)^{3/2}}\right]" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (2.7.3) and (2.7.5), p. 11" />

Far away ($r \gg a$) this becomes the field of a **point dipole**, with $\theta$ the angle from the dipole's axis, $\sin\theta = x/r$ and $\cos\theta = y/r$ (Section 2.7.1, p. 11; slide 17):

<Formula label="Point dipole" tex="E_x = \frac{3p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}\sin\theta\cos\theta, \qquad E_y = \frac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^3}\,(3\cos^2\theta - 1)" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (2.7.6)–(2.7.7), p. 11" />

The field falls off as $1/r^3$, faster than the $1/r^2$ of a point charge: the net charge is zero, so the two $1/r^2$ fields almost cancel far away (Section 2.7.1, pp. 11–12).

## A dipole in a field

Put a dipole in a uniform field $\vec E = E\,\hat i$, with $\vec p$ now at an angle $\theta$ to the field (course notes, Section 2.8, pp. 12–13; slide 21). The forces $q\vec E$ and $-q\vec E$ cancel: no net force. But they act at different points, so there is a torque about the midpoint, of size $2a(qE)\sin\theta = pE\sin\theta$ (Section 2.8, p. 13):

<Formula label="Torque on a dipole" tex="\vec\tau = \vec p \times \vec E" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (2.8.3), p. 13" />

It turns $\vec p$ towards $\vec E$. Turning the dipole takes work, so it has a potential energy, chosen zero when $\vec p$ is perpendicular to $\vec E$ (Section 2.8.1, pp. 13–14):

<Formula label="Energy of a dipole" tex="U = -pE\cos\theta = -\vec p \cdot \vec E" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (2.8.7), p. 14" />

The energy is lowest, $-pE$, when $\vec p$ points along $\vec E$ (stable equilibrium) and highest, $+pE$, when it points against it (unstable) (Section 2.8.1, p. 14). In a **non-uniform** field the two forces no longer cancel; for a small dipole along $x$ the net force is $p\,dE/dx$ (Section 2.8.1, pp. 14–15). This is why a comb charged by rubbing it through hair picks up bits of paper: the comb's uneven field induces dipoles in the paper and then pulls on them (Section 2.8.1, p. 15).

<WorkedExample title="A small dipole">
<Problem>
Charges $\pm 1$ nC sit 2 mm apart ($a = 1$ mm). (a) Find $p$. (b) Find the field 10 cm away on the dipole's axis, exactly and with the point-dipole formula. (c) In a uniform field of $3 \times 10^4$ N/C at $30°$ to $\vec p$, find the torque, and the work needed to turn the dipole from aligned to perpendicular.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Dipole moment">
$p = 2qa = 2(10^{-9})(10^{-3}) = 2 \times 10^{-12}$ C m.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The field on the axis">
At $y = 0.1$ m on the axis, the charges are 0.099 m and 0.101 m away: $E = k_e q\,(1/0.099^2 - 1/0.101^2) \approx 8.99 \times 4.001 \approx 35.97$ N/C. On the axis $\theta = 0$, and the point-dipole formula gives $2k_e p/r^3 = 2(8.99 \times 10^9)(2 \times 10^{-12})/0.1^3 \approx 35.96$ N/C: already excellent at 50 times the size of the dipole.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Torque and work">
$\tau = pE\sin 30° = (2 \times 10^{-12})(3 \times 10^4)(0.5) = 3 \times 10^{-8}$ N m. From aligned ($U = -pE$) to perpendicular ($U = 0$) the energy rises by $pE = 6 \times 10^{-8}$ J, the work needed.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Field lines: tangent to $\vec E$, denser where it is stronger, from positive to negative charges, never crossing.
- A charge in a field feels $q\vec E$; objects are charged by friction, touching or induction.
- Dipole: $\vec p = 2qa$, from $-q$ to $+q$; its field falls off as $1/r^3$.
- In a uniform field: no net force, torque $\vec p \times \vec E$, energy $-\vec p \cdot \vec E$. In a non-uniform field there is also a net force.
