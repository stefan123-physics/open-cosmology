8.02 (Electricity and Magnetism) is taught at MIT in the "TEAL" studio format: groups at tables, desktop experiments and visualizations instead of large lectures (slides 8–11). It uses the mathematics of [18.02](/courses/18-02/vector-fields-and-line-integrals), gradients, line, surface and volume integrals, but the concepts matter more than the techniques (slides 19–22). Of the four fundamental forces, gravity ([8.01](/courses/8-01/universal-gravitation)) is long-range, the strong and weak forces are short-range, and the slides place electromagnetism in the middle ("mid range"; strictly its reach is unlimited too, but matter is usually neutral, so it rarely acts over large distances). 8.02 is about electromagnetism, and about a new way of thinking: **fields** (slides 24–25).

## Action at a distance and fields

Newton's gravity and Coulomb's electric force were first understood as **action at a distance**: two bodies attract or repel simply because they are there, instantly, through empty space (course notes, Section 1.1, p. 1). In **field theory** each body instead changes the space around it, and that "field" passes the force along, point by point and at a finite speed, to the other body (Section 1.1, pp. 1–2). The theory took its classic form in Maxwell's *Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism* of 1873, built on Faraday's ideas; classical electromagnetism was the first field theory (Section 1.1, pp. 1–2).

## Scalar fields

A **scalar field** gives one number at every point of space, for example a temperature $T(x, y, z)$ (course notes, Section 1.2, p. 3; slides 27, 29). A map of night-time temperatures on Mars shows such a field on a surface, but a scalar field really lives in three dimensions: the atmosphere's temperature also changes with height (Section 1.2, pp. 2–3; slide 29).

Since three dimensions are hard to draw, one fixes a coordinate, say $z = 0$, and shows the rest as a **contour map** (curves of equal value), a **colour map**, or a **relief map**, with the value as height (Section 1.2.1, pp. 4–6; slide 28).

## Vector fields and fluid flow

A **vector field** gives a vector, a size and a direction, at every point: the wind velocity is an example (course notes, Section 1.3, pp. 6–7; slide 30). In components (Section 1.4, p. 7):

<Formula label="A vector field" tex="\vec F(x, y, z) = F_x(x, y, z)\,\hat i + F_y(x, y, z)\,\hat j + F_z(x, y, z)\,\hat k" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (1.4.1), p. 7" />

Each component is itself a scalar field. Fluid flows are the easiest vector fields to picture (Section 1.4, p. 7; slide 31). Two kinds of behaviour appear:

- **Sources and sinks**: fluid appears at a point and flows away (a diverging flow), or flows in and disappears. In 18.02 language, the [divergence](/courses/18-02/divergence-theorem) is positive at a source and negative at a sink; a flow with zero divergence is incompressible (Section 1.4, pp. 7–9; slides 32–33).
- **Circulation**: fluid going round in circles, with nothing created or destroyed: non-zero [curl](/courses/18-02/line-integrals-in-space) and zero divergence (Section 1.4, pp. 9–10; slide 34).

The course notes state the plan for the whole course (Section 1.4, p. 10): static electric fields will turn out to be curl-free, like sources and sinks, and magnetic fields divergence-free, like circulations; only fields that change in time give electric fields with both, while magnetic fields stay divergence-free. In general any vector field is the sum of a curl-free part and a divergence-free part.

Electric and magnetic fields are not flows of anything. We still borrow the words, the "flux" of the electric field through a surface, the "circulation" of the magnetic field around a loop, because they help intuition; the analogy has limits (Section 1.4.1, p. 11).

## Drawing vector fields

There are three ways (course notes, Section 1.8, pp. 15–18; slide 35):

1. **Arrows on a grid**, pointing along the field, with length or colour showing its size (or showing direction only).
2. **Field lines**, which Faraday called lines of force: start anywhere and keep moving a short step along the local field. A field line is tangent to the field at every point, and two field lines never cross, since the field would then have two directions at one point (Section 1.8.2, pp. 16–17; slide 36).
3. **"Grass seeds"** (for magnetic fields, "iron filings"): a texture of short streaks along the field, the way grass seeds line up in a strong electric field. It gives the densest picture of the field's shape, but shows only the line of the field, not which way along it the field points (Section 1.8.3, pp. 17–18; slide 38).

What is between the field lines? More field lines that were not drawn: the field fills the space continuously (Section 1.8.3, p. 18).

## The gravitational field

Newton's law of gravitation for masses $M$ and $m$ a distance $r$ apart, with $\hat r$ the unit vector from $M$ towards $m$ (course notes, Section 1.5, pp. 11–12; slide 42):

<Formula label="Newton's law of gravitation" tex="\vec F_g = -G\,\frac{Mm}{r^2}\,\hat r" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (1.5.1), p. 12" />

with $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}$ N m²/kg². The **gravitational field** of $M$ is the force per unit mass on a small test mass (Section 1.5, p. 12; slides 42–43):

<Formula label="Gravitational field" tex="\vec g = \frac{\vec F_g}{m} = -G\,\frac{M}{r^2}\,\hat r" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (1.5.2), p. 12; slide 42" />

It points towards $M$. The field is **created** by $M$ and **felt** by $m$, as the force $\vec F_g = m\vec g$ (slide 43). Near the Earth's surface it is nearly constant, of size $g = GM/R_E^2 \approx 9.8$ m/s² (Section 1.5, p. 12). A mass does not have to move along the field: thrown sideways, it follows a parabola (Section 1.5, p. 13).

## Electric charge and Coulomb's law

Electric charge plays the part of mass, with differences (slides 46–47; course notes, Section 1.6, p. 13):

- There are **two kinds**, positive and negative; like charges repel, opposite charges attract.
- Charge is measured in coulombs (C). The electron has charge $-e$ and the proton $+e$, with $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C; every charge is a whole multiple of $e$ (charge is **quantized**), and the total charge never changes (charge is **conserved**).

The force of a charge $q_1$ on a charge $q_2$, with $\hat r$ the unit vector from $q_1$ to $q_2$ (slide 48):

<Formula label="Coulomb's law" tex="\vec F_{12} = k_e\,\frac{q_1 q_2}{r^2}\,\hat r, \qquad k_e = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \approx 8.99 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2" cite="reading2" at="slide 48" />

It is an inverse-square law like gravity, but it can attract or repel (Section 1.6, p. 13). With several charges, the force on one of them is the vector sum of the forces from all the others: the **superposition principle** (slide 50).

## The electric field

The electric field at a point is the force on a small test charge $q_0$ placed there, divided by $q_0$. For a point charge $q$ (course notes, Section 1.6, p. 13; slide 51):

<Formula label="Electric field of a point charge" tex="\vec E = \frac{\vec F_e}{q_0} = k_e\,\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat r" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (1.6.2), p. 13; slide 51" />

measured in newtons per coulomb. It points away from a positive charge and towards a negative one (Section 1.6, p. 13). The charge $q$ creates $\vec E$; another charge $q_0$ feels the force $\vec F = q_0\vec E$ (slide 53). And by superposition, the field of many charges is the vector sum of their fields (slide 52):

<Formula label="Superposition" tex="\vec E_{\text{total}} = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2 + \cdots = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \vec E_i" cite="reading2" at="slide 52" />

<WorkedExample title="Two fields with numbers">
<Problem>
(a) Find $g$ at the Earth's surface from $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}$ N m²/kg², $M = 5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg and $R_E = 6.37 \times 10^6$ m. (b) A charge $q_1 = +2\ \mu$C sits at the origin and $q_2 = -1\ \mu$C at $(0.3, 0)$ m. Find the electric field at $P = (0, 0.4)$ m.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Gravity">
$g = GM/R_E^2 = (6.67 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})/(6.37 \times 10^6)^2 \approx 9.81$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The field of each charge">
$q_1$ is 0.4 m below $P$, so $\vec E_1$ points straight up: $k_e q_1/r^2 = (8.99 \times 10^9)(2 \times 10^{-6})/0.16 \approx 1.12 \times 10^5$ N/C. $q_2$ is $\sqrt{0.3^2 + 0.4^2} = 0.5$ m away; its field points towards it, along $\langle 0.6, -0.8\rangle$, with size $(8.99 \times 10^9)(10^{-6})/0.25 \approx 3.60 \times 10^4$ N/C.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Adding">
$\vec E = \langle 0, 1.124\rangle \times 10^5 + \langle 2.16, -2.88\rangle \times 10^4 \approx \langle 2.16 \times 10^4,\ 8.36 \times 10^4\rangle$ N/C, of size about $8.6 \times 10^4$ N/C.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Why gravity rules the universe">
Between a proton and an electron the electric attraction is about $2.3 \times 10^{39}$ times the gravitational one. Yet gravity shapes stars, galaxies and the expansion of the universe, because charge comes in two signs that cancel: large bodies are almost exactly neutral, while mass only adds up. This is why cosmology uses gravity, through Newton and Einstein, to describe the expanding universe.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A field gives a quantity at every point: a number (scalar field) or a vector (vector field).
- Flows show sources and sinks (divergence) and circulation (curl); static electric fields are like the first, magnetic fields like the second.
- Vector fields are drawn as arrows, field lines (tangent to the field, never crossing) or grass seeds.
- Gravity: $\vec g = -GM\hat r/r^2$, felt as $m\vec g$. Electricity: $\vec E = k_e q\hat r/r^2$, felt as $q_0\vec E$; charges come in two signs, are quantized and conserved, and their fields add.
