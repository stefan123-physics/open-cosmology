## What you need from the lessons

A **capacitor** is two conductors carrying the charges $+Q$ and $-Q$. Its **capacitance** is the charge divided by the size of the potential difference between them, and depends only on the shape and size of the conductors:

<Formula label="Capacitance" tex="C = \frac{Q}{|\Delta V|}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (5.1.1), p. 2" />

The potential difference is found from the field, as minus its line integral from one conductor to the other; for two flat plates of area $A$ a distance $d$ apart (with $d$ small next to the size of the plates) this gives

<Formula label="Potential difference and the parallel-plate capacitor" tex="\Delta V = V_b - V_a = -\int_a^b \vec E \cdot d\vec s, \qquad C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (5.2.2) and (5.2.4), pp. 4 and 5" />

The notes' steps for any capacitor (Section 5.9, which the session repeats on p. 1): find the direction of the field from the symmetry, find the field everywhere (Gauss's law), integrate it to get $\Delta V$, and divide.

Capacitors **in parallel** share one potential difference and their capacitances add; capacitors **in series** carry one charge and their inverse capacitances add:

<Formula label="Capacitors in parallel and in series" tex="C_{\text{eq}} = C_1 + C_2 \ \ (\text{parallel}), \qquad \frac{1}{C_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{C_1} + \frac{1}{C_2} \ \ (\text{series})" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (5.3.3) and (5.3.7), pp. 9 and 10" />

The electric field stores energy. In empty space its **energy density** (energy per unit volume) is

<Formula label="Energy density of the electric field" tex="u_E = \frac12\,\varepsilon_0 E^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (5.4.4), p. 13" />

A **dielectric** is an insulator. In a field its molecules line up as small dipoles, which leave a bound (induced) charge $Q_P$ on its surfaces, opposite in sign to the free charge $Q$ next to them. This weakens the field by the **dielectric constant** $\kappa$ ($\kappa > 1$; the notes write $\kappa_e$). The dipole moment per unit volume is the **polarization** $\vec P$; for a slab between plates its size equals the bound charge per unit area, $\sigma_P = P$. Gauss's law still holds if only the free charge $q_{\text{free}}$ is counted and $\vec E$ is multiplied by $\kappa$, which defines the displacement vector $\vec D$:

<Formula label="Polarization and the bound charge" tex="\sigma_P = P, \qquad Q_P = Q\left(1 - \frac{1}{\kappa}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (5.5.5) and (5.5.21), pp. 19 and 23" />

<Formula label="Gauss's law in a dielectric" tex="\oint_S \kappa\,\vec E \cdot d\vec A = \frac{q_{\text{free}}}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \vec D = \kappa\,\varepsilon_0 \vec E, \qquad \oint_S \vec D \cdot d\vec A = q_{\text{free}}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (5.5.23) and (5.5.24), p. 23" />

Every material has a **dielectric strength**: the largest field it stands before it breaks down and conducts (for dry air about $3 \times 10^6$ V/m, for paper about $16 \times 10^6$ V/m, in the notes' table on p. 16).

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The cylindrical capacitor" points={12} mit="Questions 1 to 4, pp. 1 to 3">

Two thin coaxial metal cylinders have the radii $a$ and $b$, with $b > a$, and the same height $\ell$. The inner one carries the total charge $+Q$, the outer one $-Q$. Ignore the ends: treat the field between them as if the cylinders were very long. (MIT's sheet calls them "concentric spherical cylinders"; it means coaxial cylinders.) Use $r$ for the distance from the axis.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Use Gauss's law to find the size of the field between the cylinders, $a < r < b$, in terms of $Q$, $\ell$, $r$ and $\varepsilon_0$. (It points radially outwards.)

<Hint>

Take a closed Gaussian cylinder of radius $r$ and height $\ell$, coaxial with the two. Through which of its surfaces does the field pass?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$E =$" expr="Q/(2*pi*eps0*l*r)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 5], eps0: [0.5, 2], l: [1, 10], r: [0.2, 2] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q/(4*pi*eps0*r^2)", hint: "That is the field of a point charge. Here the charge is spread along a cylinder, and the Gaussian surface is a cylinder of area 2πrℓ." },
    { expr: "Q/(pi*eps0*l*r)", hint: "The curved side of the Gaussian cylinder has the area 2πrℓ, not πrℓ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Session step (1): by symmetry the field points straight out from the axis and has one size at each $r$. Step (2): Gauss's law.

The Gaussian cylinder of radius $r$ encloses the charge $Q$ of the inner cylinder. Its flat ends carry no flux (the field runs along them), and its curved side, of area $2\pi r\ell$, carries $E \cdot 2\pi r\ell$:

<Formula label="The field between the cylinders" tex="E\,(2\pi r \ell) = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vec E = \frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 \ell\, r}\,\hat r \qquad (a < r < b)" cite="mit" at="Question 1, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Check: with $\lambda = Q/\ell$ this is the field $\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$ of a long line of charge, as the lesson on Gauss's law finds. Outside ($r > b$) the enclosed charge is $Q - Q = 0$ and the field is zero; inside the inner cylinder it is zero too.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find the potential difference $\Delta V = V(a) - V(b)$ between the inner and the outer cylinder, in terms of $Q$, $a$, $b$, $\ell$ and $\varepsilon_0$.

<Hint>

$V(a) - V(b) = -\int_b^a E\,dr$: integrate the field of part (a) from $b$ in to $a$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$\Delta V =$" expr="Q*log(b/a)/(2*pi*eps0*l)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 5], eps0: [0.5, 2], l: [1, 10], a: [0.1, 0.5], b: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q*log(a/b)/(2*pi*eps0*l)", hint: "That is negative. The inner cylinder is positive, so it is at the higher potential: V(a) − V(b) > 0." },
    { expr: "Q*(b - a)/(2*pi*eps0*l*a*b)", hint: "That integrates 1/r² instead of 1/r. The field of a cylinder falls off as 1/r, and the integral of 1/r is a logarithm." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Session step (3): the line integral of the field along a radius.

<Formula label="The potential difference" tex="\Delta V = V(a) - V(b) = -\int_b^a \frac{Q\,dr}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 \ell\, r} = \frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 \ell}\ln\frac{b}{a}" cite="mit" at="Question 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Check: positive, since $b > a$; the unit is C/(F/m · m) = C/F = V.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

What is the capacitance $C$ of the pair? Give it in terms of $a$, $b$, $\ell$ and $\varepsilon_0$.

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$C =$" expr="2*pi*eps0*l/log(b/a)"
  vars={{ eps0: [0.5, 2], l: [1, 10], a: [0.1, 0.5], b: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "log(b/a)/(2*pi*eps0*l)", hint: "That is ΔV/Q, the inverse of the capacitance. C = Q/|ΔV|." },
    { expr: "2*pi*eps0*l*a/(b - a)", hint: "That treats the gap as two flat plates of area 2πaℓ. It is right only when the gap b − a is small next to a." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Session step (4): $C = Q/|\Delta V|$. The charge $Q$ cancels, as it must.

<Formula label="Capacitance of two coaxial cylinders" tex="C = \frac{Q}{|\Delta V|} = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0 \ell}{\ln(b/a)}" cite="mit" at="Question 3, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Check: it depends only on the shape ($a$, $b$, $\ell$), not on $Q$. For a narrow gap, $b = a + d$ with $d \ll a$, $\ln(b/a) \approx d/a$ and $C \approx \varepsilon_0(2\pi a\ell)/d$: two flat plates of area $2\pi a\ell$ a distance $d$ apart. The notes reach the same capacitance in Section 5.2, Eq. (5.2.7).

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

The energy stored in the field is $U = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0 \int E^2\,dV_{\text{vol}}$ over all space, with $dV_{\text{vol}}$ a small volume. Use the field of part (a) to find $U$, in terms of $Q$, $a$, $b$, $\ell$ and $\varepsilon_0$.

<Hint>

The field is non-zero only between the cylinders. Cut that space into thin cylindrical shells of radius $r$, thickness $dr$ and height $\ell$. What is the volume of one?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="$U =$" expr="Q^2*log(b/a)/(4*pi*eps0*l)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 5], eps0: [0.5, 2], l: [1, 10], a: [0.1, 0.5], b: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q^2*log(b/a)/(2*pi*eps0*l)", hint: "That is twice the energy: the energy density has the factor ½ in front of ε₀E²." },
    { expr: "Q^2*log(b/a)/(8*pi^2*eps0*l)", hint: "The shell of radius r and thickness dr has the volume 2πrℓ dr: the 2π of the volume cancels one 2π of E²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Add the energy density $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0 E^2$ over thin shells (this is also the session's homework Problem 2(b)).

<Step title="One shell">

The shell between $r$ and $r + dr$ has the volume $2\pi r\ell\,dr$ and the energy

<Formula label="Energy in one shell" tex="dU = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\left(\frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 \ell\, r}\right)^2 2\pi r \ell\,dr = \frac{Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 \ell}\,\frac{dr}{r}" cite="mit" at="Question 4, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="All the shells">

<Formula label="Energy stored in the cylindrical capacitor" tex="U = \frac{Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 \ell}\int_a^b \frac{dr}{r} = \frac{Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 \ell}\ln\frac{b}{a}" cite="mit" at="Question 4, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Check: the unit is C²/(F/m · m) = C²/F = J. Each shell between $r$ and $2r$ holds the same energy, since $\int dr/r$ depends only on the ratio of the radii.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={1}>

Write $U$ in terms of $Q$ and $C$ alone. (MIT's sheet says "your expression for $C$ from questions 4 above"; it means Question 3, part (c) here.)

<Answer id="p1-e" label="$U =$" expr="Q^2/(2*C)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 5], C: [0.5, 5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q^2/C", hint: "Compare with parts (c) and (d): U has 4π in its denominator where 1/C has 2π, so a factor ½ is left over." },
    { expr: "Q*C/2", hint: "Check the unit: Q·C is C²/V, not a joule. Divide by C instead." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Divide part (d) by $1/C$ from part (c).

$1/C = \ln(b/a)/(2\pi\varepsilon_0\ell)$, so $U = \tfrac12 Q^2 \ln(b/a)/(2\pi\varepsilon_0\ell) = Q^2/(2C)$. The shape of the cylinders has dropped out: Problem 2 shows why.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="f" points={1}>

(Our addition.) A coaxial cable has an inner wire of radius $a = 0.50$ mm and an outer sheath of radius $b = 2.0$ mm, with air between. What is the capacitance of a piece $\ell = 1.00$ m long, in pF?

<Answer id="p1-f" label="$C =$" value="40.1" unit="pF" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-cable" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into part (c).

$C = 2\pi(8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{F/m})(1.00\ \text{m})/\ln 4 = 5.561 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{F}/1.386 = 4.01 \times 10^{-11}$ F $= 40.1$ pF. Check: only the ratio $b/a = 4$ enters, so a cable twice as thick with the same ratio has the same capacitance per metre.

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Charging the capacitor by hand" points={8} mit="Question 5, p. 4">

Instead of using a battery, charge the cylindrical capacitor of Problem 1 yourself. Start with both cylinders uncharged, and carry charge, a little at a time, from the outer cylinder to the inner one. At some moment the inner cylinder carries the charge $q$ (and the outer one $-q$).

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the potential difference between the cylinders at that moment, in terms of $q$ and the capacitance $C$?

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$\Delta V =$" expr="q/C"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 5], C: [0.5, 5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "q*C", hint: "Check the unit: C is charge per volt, so the voltage is the charge divided by C." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The capacitance does not depend on the charge, so $C = q/\Delta V$ holds at every moment.

$\Delta V = q/C$: it grows in step with the charge, from zero at the start.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Now carry a little more charge $dq$ from the outer to the inner cylinder. How much work $dW$ does that take, in terms of $q$, $dq$ and $C$? (Type $dq$ as `dq`.)

<Hint>

Moving a charge through the potential difference $\Delta V$, against the field, takes the work charge times $\Delta V$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$dW =$" expr="q*dq/C"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 5], dq: [0.01, 0.1], C: [0.5, 5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "q^2/(2*C)", hint: "That is the work for the whole charge q. The question asks for the small extra piece dq only." },
    { expr: "dq^2/C", hint: "The extra charge dq is pushed through the potential difference q/C that the charge q already there makes." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The small charge $dq$ climbs the potential difference $q/C$ (its own effect on $\Delta V$ is negligible).

<Formula label="Work for one more piece of charge" tex="dW = \Delta V\,dq = \frac{q}{C}\,dq" cite="mit" at="Question 5(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

How much work does it take to bring the total charge $Q$ across, starting from uncharged cylinders? Give it in terms of $Q$ and $C$.

<Hint>

Add up $dW$ from $q = 0$ to $q = Q$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$W =$" expr="Q^2/(2*C)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 5], C: [0.5, 5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q^2/C", hint: "That moves all the charge through the final potential difference Q/C. The early charge crossed a much smaller one: on average half of it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Integrate part (b).

<Formula label="Work to charge a capacitor" tex="W = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C}" cite="mit" at="Question 5(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

Check: the same as the energy of Problem 1(e). With $Q = C\,\Delta V$ it can also be written $\tfrac12 C\,\Delta V^2$ or $\tfrac12 Q\,\Delta V$, where $\Delta V$ is the final potential difference.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

How does the work of part (c) compare with the energy stored in the field, found in Problem 1(d)?

<Answer id="p2-d" label="The work is"
  choices={[
    "larger, since heat is lost in the wires",
    "equal: all the work done is stored in the field",
    "smaller, since the field gives back part of the energy as we work",
    "half as large, since the field holds energy from both cylinders"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compare $Q^2/(2C)$ with $U$ of Problem 1.

They are equal. The charge was moved slowly, with no current through a resistor and no kinetic energy left over, so every joule of work went into building up the field between the cylinders. That is why the energy can be counted in either place: as the work to charge the capacitor, or as the energy density of the field added over its volume.

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A charged capacitor shares its charge" points={7} mit="Homework Problem 1, p. 5">

A capacitor $C_1 = 6.00$ μF is charged by connecting it to a battery of $\Delta V = 20.0$ V. The battery is then disconnected, and $C_1$ is connected to a second capacitor $C_2 = 3.00$ μF, which carries no charge at first: the positive plate of $C_1$ to one plate of $C_2$, the negative plate of $C_1$ to the other. (MIT's sheet draws this with two switches.)

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What charge does $C_1$ take from the battery, in μC?

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$Q_0 =$" value="120" unit="μC" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-Q0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $Q = C\,\Delta V$.

$Q_0 = (6.00\ \mu\text{F})(20.0\ \text{V}) = 120$ μC.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the final charge on each capacitor, in μC?

<Hint>

Once connected, the two capacitors are in parallel: they end with one potential difference. Which quantity is shared out, and which stays the same in total?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b1" label="$Q_1 =$" value="80" unit="μC" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-Q1" />

<Answer id="p3-b2" label="$Q_2 =$" value="40" unit="μC" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-Q2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The charge cannot leave the two connected plates, so the total stays 120 μC; it spreads until both capacitors have the same potential difference.

<Formula label="Sharing the charge" tex="\Delta V_f = \frac{Q_0}{C_1 + C_2} = \frac{120\ \mu\text{C}}{9.00\ \mu\text{F}} = 13.3\ \text{V}, \qquad Q_1 = C_1\,\Delta V_f, \quad Q_2 = C_2\,\Delta V_f" cite="mit" at="Homework Problem 1(b), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

$Q_1 = 6.00 \times 13.33 = 80$ μC and $Q_2 = 3.00 \times 13.33 = 40$ μC. Check: $80 + 40 = 120$ μC, and the charges split in the ratio of the capacitances, 2 to 1.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

(Our addition.) What is the total energy stored in the two capacitors at the end, in mJ? Compare it with the energy in $C_1$ before.

<Answer id="p3-c" label="$U_f =$" value="0.800" unit="mJ" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-Uf" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $U = Q^2/(2C)$ from Problem 2, for the pair as one capacitor of $9.00$ μF.

Before: $U_0 = \tfrac12 (6.00\ \mu\text{F})(20.0\ \text{V})^2 = 1.20$ mJ. After: $U_f = (120\ \mu\text{C})^2/(2 \times 9.00\ \mu\text{F}) = 0.800$ mJ. A third of the energy, 0.400 mJ, is gone: the charge rushes through the connecting wires, which heat up (and radiate a little), however small their resistance. The fraction lost, $C_2/(C_1 + C_2)$, does not depend on the wires.

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Dielectric between the plates" points={7} mit="Homework Problem 3, pp. 5 and 6">

A parallel-plate capacitor has plates of area $A$ a distance $d$ apart.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

The space between the plates is filled completely with a dielectric of dielectric constant $\kappa$. What is the capacitance, in terms of $\kappa$, $A$, $d$ and $\varepsilon_0$?

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$C =$" expr="kappa*eps0*A/d"
  vars={{ kappa: [1.5, 10], eps0: [0.5, 2], A: [0.5, 2], d: [0.1, 1] }}
  names={{ kappa: "\\kappa", eps0: "\\varepsilon_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "eps0*A/(kappa*d)", hint: "The dielectric weakens the field, so for the same charge the potential difference falls and the capacitance rises." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For the same free charge, the field and so $\Delta V = Ed$ are smaller by $\kappa$.

<Formula label="A filled parallel-plate capacitor" tex="C = \frac{Q}{Ed} = \frac{Q}{(Q/\kappa\varepsilon_0 A)\,d} = \frac{\kappa\varepsilon_0 A}{d}" cite="mit" at="Homework Problem 3(a), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Now the plates are squares of side $\ell$, so $A = \ell^2$, and the gap $d$ is much smaller than $\ell$. The left half of the space between them (width $\ell/2$, the full gap $d$) is filled with a dielectric $\kappa_1$. The right half is filled with two layers, each of thickness $d/2$: $\kappa_2$ against the top plate and $\kappa_3$ against the bottom plate. Find the capacitance, in terms of $A$, $d$, $\varepsilon_0$, $\kappa_1$, $\kappa_2$ and $\kappa_3$.

<Hint>

The two halves sit side by side between the same two plates: how do they combine? Inside the right half, the two layers carry the same charge one after the other.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$C =$" expr="eps0*A/d*(kappa1/2 + kappa2*kappa3/(kappa2 + kappa3))"
  vars={{ eps0: [0.5, 2], A: [0.5, 2], d: [0.1, 1], kappa1: [1.5, 10], kappa2: [1.5, 10], kappa3: [1.5, 10] }}

Negative: the dipoles of the dielectric turn their negative ends towards the positive line. (The notes' text calls the tube a "spherical shell" here; it means the cylindrical one.)

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

And on the outer surface, at $r = b$?

<Answer id="p9-c" label="$\lambda_{\text{outer}}/\lambda =$" value="0.9" tolerance="2%" calc="p9-outer" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The dielectric as a whole is neutral.

Outside, the field is again $\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$, so the bound charges add to zero: $\lambda_{\text{outer}} = +0.9\,\lambda$. Check: this matches $Q_P = Q(1 - 1/\kappa)$ of the lesson, with $\kappa = 10$.

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 11

<Exercise id="p11" title="The force between the plates" points={6}>

From the course notes, Chapter 5, Additional Problem 5.12.6 (pp. 44 and 45). The plates of a parallel-plate capacitor have the area $A$ and carry the charges $+Q$ and $-Q$, with empty space between them. Ignore the field near the edges. The aim is to show that the plates attract each other with the force $F = Q^2/(2\varepsilon_0 A)$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Find the force on the left plate from Coulomb's law. The force is the left plate's charge times the field made by the right plate alone. The box asks for that field, the step the force rests on: give its size, in terms of $Q$, $A$ and $\varepsilon_0$.

<Hint>

A plate cannot push itself. How large is the field of one large flat sheet of charge?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-a" label="Field of the right plate alone $=$" expr="Q/(2*eps0*A)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 5], A: [0.5, 2], eps0: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q/(eps0*A)", hint: "That is the field between the plates, made by both plates together. The left plate feels only the field of the right one, half of it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only the other plate's field pushes a plate.

The right plate is a sheet with the charge density $\sigma = Q/A$; its field on either side is $\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0) = Q/(2\varepsilon_0 A)$. The left plate's charge $Q$ sits in that field, so

<Formula label="Force between the plates" tex="F = Q\,\frac{Q}{2\varepsilon_0 A} = \frac{Q^2}{2\varepsilon_0 A}" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 5.12.6(a), p. 45 (our own solution)" />

attracting, since the charges are opposite. The field between the plates, $Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)$, is twice as large because both plates make it.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

Pull the plates apart, with $Q$ fixed, from the separation $x$ to $x + dx$. Show, by setting the work $F\,dx$ equal to the increase of the energy stored in the field (energy density $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0 E^2$), that you need the same force. (No box: compare with the solution.)

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only the volume filled with field grows; the field itself does not change.

With $Q$ fixed, $E = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)$ stays the same, and the volume between the plates grows by $A\,dx$. So $F\,dx = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0 E^2 A\,dx$, and

<Formula label="Force from the energy" tex="F = \frac12\varepsilon_0 E^2 A = \frac12\varepsilon_0\left(\frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}\right)^2 A = \frac{Q^2}{2\varepsilon_0 A}" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 5.12.6(b), p. 45 (our own solution)" />

the same as part (a).

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

Show that the force per unit area on either plate (the electrostatic stress) is $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0 E^2$, with $E$ the field between the plates. (No box: compare with the solution.)

<Solution>

**The idea.** Divide the force by the area.

$F/A = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0 E^2$, from part (b). The force per unit area equals the energy density of the field next to the plate. The notes add that this holds at the surface of a conductor of any shape.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

How strong would the field have to be for this force per unit area to equal atmospheric pressure, $1.013 \times 10^5$ N/m²? Give it in MV/m.

<Answer id="p11-d" label="$E =$" value="151" unit="MV/m" tolerance="2%" calc="p11-E" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Solve $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0 E^2 = p$ for $E$.

<Formula label="The field that presses like the air" tex="E = \sqrt{\frac{2p}{\varepsilon_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 1.013 \times 10^5}{8.85 \times 10^{-12}}}\ \text{V/m} = 1.51 \times 10^8\ \text{V/m}" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 5.12.6(d), p. 45 (our own solution)" />

that is, 151 MV/m. Check: dry air breaks down at about 3 MV/m, fifty times less, so in ordinary capacitors the force per unit area between the plates is far below atmospheric pressure. The notes add that the largest accelerators of the Van de Graaff type reach about 100 MV/m before breakdown. (The notes give atmospheric pressure as 14.7 lb/in², or 101,341 N/m²; 14.7 lb/in² is 101,353 N/m² and the standard atmosphere 101,325 N/m². All three give 151 MV/m.)

</Solution>

</Part>

<Exercise id="p12" title="Energy density in a dielectric" points={6}>

From the course notes, Chapter 5, Additional Problem 5.12.7 (p. 45). A dielectric with the dielectric constant $\kappa$ fills the whole space between the plates of a parallel-plate capacitor, with plates of area $A$ a distance $d$ apart. The aim is to show that the energy density of the field in the dielectric is $u_E = \tfrac12\vec E \cdot \vec D$. (The notes point here to an earlier problem for the field and potential of the capacitor; this page uses Problem 4(a) instead.)

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Write $u_E = \tfrac12\vec E \cdot \vec D$ in terms of $E$, $\kappa$ and $\varepsilon_0$, with no $D$ in it.

<Answer id="p12-a" label="$u_E =$" expr="kappa*eps0*E^2/2"

<Solution>

**The idea.** $\vec D = \kappa\varepsilon_0\vec E$ points along $\vec E$.

$u_E = \tfrac12 E\,(\kappa\varepsilon_0 E) = \tfrac12\kappa\varepsilon_0 E^2$.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Find the work needed to charge the capacitor from $q = 0$ to the free charge $q = Q$, in terms of $Q$, $d$, $A$, $\kappa$ and $\varepsilon_0$.

</Hint>

**The idea.** $W = \int_0^Q (q/C)\,dq = Q^2/(2C)$, with $C = \kappa\varepsilon_0 A/d$ from Problem 4(a).

<Formula label="Work to charge the filled capacitor" tex="W = \int_0^Q \frac{q\,d}{\kappa\varepsilon_0 A}\,dq = \frac{Q^2 d}{2\kappa\varepsilon_0 A}" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 5.12.7(b), p. 45 (our own solution)" />

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Divide the work of part (b) by the volume between the plates to find the energy density, in terms of $Q$, $A$, $\kappa$ and $\varepsilon_0$.

<Answer id="p12-c" label="$u_E =$" expr="Q^2/(2*kappa*eps0*A^2)"

## Problem 13

<Exercise id="p13" title="Four questions on capacitors" points={8}>

From the course notes, Chapter 5, Conceptual Questions 1 to 4 (pp. 41 and 42).

<Part label="a" points={2}>

The plates of a charged parallel-plate capacitor, not connected to anything, attract each other. To pull them further apart, is the work done on them from outside positive or negative, and where does it go?

<Answer id="p13-a" label="The outside work is"
  choices={[
    "negative, since the attraction does the work and gives energy to whoever pulls",
    "zero, since the charge on the plates does not change as they are moved",
    "positive, and it is stored as extra energy in the field between the plates",
    "positive, and it is all turned into heat in the plates as they move apart"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The pull and the motion point the same way.

The outside force pulls the plates apart, against their attraction and along the motion, so its work is positive. It goes into the field: with $Q$ fixed, the stored energy $Q^2 d/(2\varepsilon_0 A)$ grows with the gap, as in Problem 5.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

The potential difference across a capacitor is tripled. By what factor does the stored energy change?

<Answer id="p13-b" label="The stored energy"
  choices={[
    "grows by a factor of 9",
    "grows by a factor of 3",
    "stays the same, since C does not change",
    "grows by a factor of 6, twice the change of the voltage"
  ]}
  answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $U = \tfrac12 C\,\Delta V^2$, and $C$ depends only on the shape.

Three times the potential difference gives $3^2 = 9$ times the energy. (The charge triples too, and $U = \tfrac12 Q\,\Delta V$ grows by $3 \times 3$.)

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Does putting a dielectric between the plates raise or lower the highest potential difference the capacitor can safely take?

<Answer id="p13-c" label="The highest safe potential difference"
  choices={[
    "is lowered, since a dielectric conducts more easily than empty space does",
    "is unchanged: only the plates and the gap matter",
    "is lowered, since the dielectric makes the field between the plates stronger",
    "is raised, for a dielectric that stands a stronger field than air"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Breakdown happens when the field reaches the dielectric strength of what fills the gap.

The capacitor fails when the field in the gap reaches the dielectric strength of the material there. Air breaks down at about $3 \times 10^6$ V/m; paper, glass and most solid dielectrics stand several times more (the notes' table on p. 16 gives 16 and 9 MV/m). With the same gap, $\Delta V_{\max} = E_{\max}\,d$ is then larger. A second gain: with a dielectric the plates can be brought closer without sparking.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

A capacitor filled with a polar dielectric (one whose molecules are permanent dipoles, such as water) is cooled. What happens to its capacitance? (The notes ask about any dielectric; we take a polar one, our choice, for which the answer is clear.)

<Answer id="p13-d" label="The capacitance"
  choices={[
    "falls, since cold molecules no longer respond to the field at all",
    "rises: with less thermal motion the dipoles line up better",
    "stays the same, since the capacitance depends only on the shape",
    "falls, since the plates shrink a little as they get cold"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a polar dielectric the field lines up permanent dipoles, and thermal motion works against that.

The notes explain (Section 5.5, p. 16) that the alignment is incomplete because of the random thermal motion of the molecules. Cooling reduces that motion, so the dipoles line up better, the bound charges grow, the field inside is weakened more, and $\kappa$ and $C = \kappa C_0$ rise. (A slight shrinking of the plates and the gap changes $C$ far less.)

</Solution>

</Part>
