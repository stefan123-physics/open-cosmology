After electric fields and circuits, a new topic: **magnetic fields** (slides 1–2). The pattern is the same as before: something creates a field, and something else feels it (slides 3, 11).

## Magnets and their fields

A bar magnet has two **poles**, north and south; like poles repel and opposite poles attract (course notes, Section 8.1, p. 1; slide 4). A compass needle, itself a small magnet, lines up with the magnetic field $\vec B$ around a magnet, and the field lines leave the north pole and enter the south pole (p. 1; slides 5–7). The field is strongest at the poles (p. 1).

**No magnetic monopoles.** Cut an electric dipole in two and you get two separate charges. Cut a bar magnet and you get two smaller magnets, each with both poles: isolated magnetic poles, **monopoles**, have never been found (Section 8.1, p. 1; slides 8–10). Bar magnets are **magnetic dipoles**: they create a dipole field, and in a field they turn to line up with it, as an electric dipole does in an electric field (slides 8, 11). Since magnetic field lines have nowhere to start or end, as many leave any closed surface as enter it. Compared with [Gauss's law](/courses/8-02/gauss-law), this is the second of Maxwell's four equations (slide 10):

<Formula label="Gauss's law for magnetism" tex="\oint_S \vec B \cdot d\vec A = 0" cite="reading2" at="slide 10" />

The **Earth** is a magnetic dipole too. The slide puts it this way: the magnetic north pole of the Earth's dipole lies in the southern hemisphere (slide 13). (Geographers call the pole in the Arctic the "North Magnetic Pole", but as a magnet pole it is a south pole, which is why it attracts the north ends of compass needles.) Experiment 5 measures the components of the Earth's field at MIT with a bar magnet and a compass (slides 14–16).

## The force on a moving charge

Does anything else feel a magnetic field? Moving charges do, as an electron beam bent by a magnet shows (slides 17–19). Experiments show that the force on a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ is proportional to $q$ and $v$, vanishes when $\vec v$ is along $\vec B$, is perpendicular to both, grows as $\sin\theta$ with the angle between them, and reverses for a negative charge (course notes, Section 8.2, p. 2). All this is one cross product, which serves as the definition of $\vec B$: with no magnetic charges, $\vec B$ cannot be defined as force per unit "pole" the way $\vec E$ is force per unit charge (pp. 2–3; slide 19):

<Formula label="Magnetic force on a moving charge" tex="\vec F_B = q\,\vec v \times \vec B, \qquad |\vec F_B| = |q|\,vB\sin\theta" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (8.2.1)–(8.2.2), pp. 2–3" />

The unit of $B$ is the **tesla**: 1 T = 1 N/(C m/s) = 1 N/(A m); the non-SI **gauss** is $10^{-4}$ T (Section 8.2, p. 3; slide 20).

**The cross product** (slides 21–25; see [8.01](/courses/8-01/torque-and-cross-product)): $|\vec A \times \vec B| = AB\sin\theta$, the area of the parallelogram they span; its direction is given by the right-hand rule, fingers curling from $\vec A$ to $\vec B$ through the smaller angle, thumb along $\vec A \times \vec B$. Cyclically, $\hat i \times \hat j = \hat k$, $\hat j \times \hat k = \hat i$, $\hat k \times \hat i = \hat j$, and reversing the order changes the sign. In drawings, a dot means a vector out of the page (an arrow head) and a cross a vector into it (an arrow tail) (slide 22).

## The magnetic force does no work

$\vec F_B$ is always perpendicular to $\vec v$, so its work on the particle is zero (course notes, Section 8.2, p. 3):

<Formula label="No work by the magnetic force" tex="dW = \vec F_B \cdot d\vec s = q(\vec v \times \vec B) \cdot \vec v\,dt = 0" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.2.3), p. 3" />

A magnetic field cannot speed a particle up or slow it down; it can only turn it (p. 3). In a uniform field, a particle moving perpendicular to $\vec B$ therefore goes round a circle at constant speed, with the magnetic force as the centripetal force, $qvB = mv^2/r$ (Section 8.5, pp. 12–13; for a negative charge use $|q|$, and it circles the other way):

<Formula label="Circular motion in a magnetic field" tex="r = \frac{mv}{qB}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi m}{qB}, \qquad \omega = \frac{qB}{m}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (8.5.2)–(8.5.4), p. 13" />

The period does not depend on the speed: faster particles move on bigger circles. With a velocity component along $\vec B$ the path is a helix (p. 13).

## The Lorentz force and the velocity selector

With both fields present, the full force on a charge is the **Lorentz force** (Section 8.6.1, p. 15; slide 27):

<Formula label="Lorentz force" tex="\vec F = q\left(\vec E + \vec v \times \vec B\right)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.6.1), p. 15" />

the final word on the force on a charge (slide 27). In a **velocity selector**, an electric and a magnetic field are crossed so that their forces push opposite ways; only particles with the speed at which they cancel, $qE = qvB$, go straight on (p. 15; slides 28–29):

<Formula label="Velocity selector" tex="v = \frac{E}{B}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.6.3), p. 15" />

J. J. Thomson used this, with electrons accelerated through a known voltage, to measure their charge-to-mass ratio $e/m$ (pp. 15–16). A **mass spectrometer** sends particles from a velocity selector into a second field $B_0$, where the radius of their circle gives the mass, $m = qB_0Br/E$ (Section 8.6.2, p. 16).

<WorkedExample title="A proton in a magnetic field">
<Problem>
A proton ($m = 1.673 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, $q = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C) moves at $10^6$ m/s perpendicular to a 0.5 T field. Find (a) the force, (b) the radius and period of its circle, and (c) the electric field that would let it pass a velocity selector with this $B$ undeflected.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Force">
$F = qvB = (1.602 \times 10^{-19})(10^6)(0.5) \approx 8.01 \times 10^{-14}$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Circle">
$r = mv/qB = (1.673 \times 10^{-27})(10^6)/(1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.5) \approx 2.09$ cm. $T = 2\pi m/qB \approx 1.31 \times 10^{-7}$ s, the same for any speed.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Velocity selector">
$E = vB = (10^6)(0.5) = 5 \times 10^5$ V/m.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Where are the monopoles?">
Classical electromagnetism has no magnetic monopoles, and none has ever been seen. Yet grand unified theories of particle physics predict them, and in the standard hot Big Bang they would have been made in enormous numbers. 8.286 sets out this "monopole problem" (Lecture 22) and shows how inflation, an early burst of exponential expansion, dilutes the monopoles away (Lecture 23).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Magnets are dipoles: field lines leave N and enter S; there are no monopoles, so $\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec A = 0$.
- A moving charge feels $\vec F_B = q\vec v \times \vec B$; $B$ is in tesla.
- The magnetic force does no work: circles of radius $mv/qB$, period $2\pi m/qB$.
- Lorentz force $q(\vec E + \vec v \times \vec B)$; crossed fields select $v = E/B$.
