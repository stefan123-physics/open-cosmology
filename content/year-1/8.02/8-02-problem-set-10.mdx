## What you need from the lessons

In a changing situation, Maxwell's Ampère's law has two sources of a magnetic field: the conduction current $I_\text{enc}$ of real charges crossing a surface bounded by the loop, and the **displacement current** $I_d$, set by the rate of change of the electric flux $\Phi_E$ through the same surface:

<Formula label="The Ampère–Maxwell law" tex="\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec s = \mu_0 I_\text{enc} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt} = \mu_0 I_\text{enc} + \mu_0 I_d, \qquad I_d = \varepsilon_0\,\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (9.1) and (9.2), pp. 1 and 2" />

The line element $d\vec s$ runs around the loop in the right-handed sense with respect to the surface normal: thumb along the normal, fingers along $d\vec s$. The energy carried by the fields flows through space; the rate of flow per unit area is the **Poynting vector**, in watts per square metre:

<Formula label="The Poynting vector" tex="\vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\vec E \times \vec B" cite="mit" at="p. 4" />

and the energy per second through a surface is the flux of $\vec S$ through it, $\int \vec S \cdot d\vec A$ over the surface. In empty space the fields of a plane wave travelling along $+x$ have the form $E_0\cos(kx - \omega t)$; the wave moves at $v = \omega/k$, with wavelength $\lambda = 2\pi/k$ and frequency $f = \omega/2\pi$. Both fields are perpendicular to the direction of travel and to each other, $\vec E \times \vec B$ points along the direction of travel, and

<Formula label="Speed and amplitudes of a plane wave" tex="c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}, \qquad E_0 = cB_0" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (13.4.15) and (13.4.26), pp. 9 and 11" />

The two fields store energy with the densities $u_E = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0 E^2$ and $u_B = B^2/2\mu_0$, and the time average of the Poynting vector is the **intensity** of the wave:

<Formula label="Energy densities and intensity" tex="u_E = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E^2, \qquad u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}, \qquad I = \langle S\rangle = \frac{E_0 B_0}{2\mu_0} = \frac{E_0^2}{2\mu_0 c}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (13.6.2) and (13.6.8), pp. 15 and 16" />

Two equal waves running in opposite directions make a [standing wave](/courses/8-02/standing-waves), with nodes half a wavelength apart, and the nodes of $\vec E$ a quarter wavelength from those of $\vec B$.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The magnetic field inside a charging capacitor" points={30} mit="Questions 1 to 8, pp. 2 to 4">

A capacitor has two circular plates of radius $a$, a distance $d$ apart, with $d$ much smaller than $a$. The plates are horizontal, and $\hat k$ points up. A thin wire is joined to the centre of each plate and runs along the axis, up from the top plate and down from the bottom plate, to a voltage source. A switch is closed at $t = 0$, and from then on a current $I(t)$ flows up the lower wire into the bottom plate, which carries the charge $+Q(t)$, while the top plate carries $-Q(t)$; the charge grows as $I = dQ/dt$. Ignore edge effects: between the plates the electric field is uniform, and for $r > a$ it is zero.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Formula label="Displacement current through the disc" tex="I_d = \varepsilon_0\,\frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac{Q\,r^2}{\varepsilon_0 a^2}\right) = I\,\frac{r^2}{a^2}" cite="mit" at="Question 3, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Part label="e" points={6}>

Assume, as the axial symmetry suggests, that between the plates the magnetic field runs in circles around the axis and has the same size $B$ all around each circle. Take as Amperian loop a circle of radius $r < a$ in the midway plane, run anticlockwise as seen from above (right-handed with respect to $+\hat k$). First write $\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec s$ around it in terms of $B$ and $r$, on paper. Then use the Ampère–Maxwell law to find the size of the magnetic field at the distance $r < a$ from the axis, in terms of $r$, $I$, $\mu_0$ and $a$.

<Hint>

$\vec B$ is parallel to $d\vec s$ all around the circle and has the same size there, so the line integral is $B$ times the length of the circle.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the right-hand side, use your answers to (c) and (d).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e" label="$B =$" expr="mu0*I*r/(2*pi*a^2)"
  vars={{ mu0: [0.5, 2], I: [1, 5], r: [0.2, 0.8], a: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*I/(2*pi*r)", hint: "That is the field of the whole current I, as around the wire. A circle of radius r < a between the plates encloses only the displacement current I r^2/a^2." },
    { expr: "mu0*I/(pi*a^2)", hint: "The line integral is B times the length of the circle, 2 pi r, not B times its area." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The circle bounds the disc of (b) to (d). Through it the conduction current is zero and the displacement current is $I r^2/a^2$.

<Step title="Both sides of the law">

The left side is $\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec s = B\,2\pi r$. The right side is $\mu_0 \cdot 0 + \mu_0 I r^2/a^2$. So

<Formula label="Field between the plates" tex="B\,2\pi r = \mu_0 I\,\frac{r^2}{a^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad B = \frac{\mu_0 I\,r}{2\pi a^2}" cite="mit" at="Questions 5 and 6, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The field grows linearly from zero on the axis. At $r = a$ it is $\mu_0 I/2\pi a$, the same as the field at the distance $a$ from a long wire carrying $I$: the displacement current between the plates continues the current in the wire. The session prints this field on p. 5.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="f" points={3}>

Point your right thumb along the electric field while the plates charge up. Which way does the magnetic field circle? Choose in the box.

<Answer id="p1-f" label="The magnetic field circles:"
  choices={[
    "along the curl of the fingers, since the field between the plates grows",
    "against the curl of the fingers, since the new field opposes the change",
    "it does not circle: it points along the thumb, parallel to E",
    "it does not circle: with no current in the gap there is no field"
  ]}
  answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the Ampère–Maxwell law the displacement current plays the role of a current, with the direction of $d\vec E/dt$.

While the plates charge, $\vec E$ along $+\hat k$ grows, so $d\vec E/dt$ points along $+\hat k$ too, and $I_d > 0$ for the normal $+\hat k$. Then $\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec s > 0$ around the anticlockwise circle: $\vec B$ circles the way the fingers curl when the thumb points along $\vec E$, the same sense as around the wire that carries $I$ upwards. There is no minus sign here, unlike Faraday's law.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="g" points={3}>

Would the direction of the magnetic field change if the plates were discharging instead? Choose in the box.

<Answer id="p1-g" label="When the plates discharge:"
  choices={[
    "no change: the field still points from the positive plate to the negative one",
    "no change: B depends only on the charge on the plates, not on its rate",
    "it changes, since the electric field itself now points down between the plates",
    "it changes, since E now shrinks, so dE/dt and the circling of B reverse"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $\vec B$ follows the rate of change of $\vec E$, not $\vec E$ itself.

While discharging, $\vec E$ still points up but shrinks, so $d\vec E/dt$ points down and $I_d < 0$. The magnetic field reverses and circles against the curl of the fingers. This matches the wires: the current now flows down, out of the bottom plate.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="h" points={3}>

(Course notes, Chapter 13, Conceptual Question 1 of Section 13.13, p. 45.) Can a conduction current and a displacement current both be non-zero through the same surface? Choose in the box.

<Answer id="p1-h" label="Both non-zero at once:"
  choices={[
    "no: where charge flows across a surface, the field through it cannot change",
    "no: a displacement current exists only in the empty gap between conductors",
    "yes: in a leaky capacitor, charge crosses the gap while the field there changes",
    "yes, but only when the two currents point in opposite directions"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Nothing in the Ampère–Maxwell law stops both terms being present.

Fill the gap with a material that conducts a little. Charge then leaks across the gap, a conduction current, while the field in the gap still changes as the plates charge or discharge, a displacement current. Their directions can be the same or opposite. (A wire carrying a rapidly changing current also has a tiny displacement current inside it.)

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Energy flowing into a charging capacitor" points={20} mit="Questions 9 to 13, pp. 5 and 6">

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A wave in ferrite" points={20}>

(Course notes, Chapter 13, Additional Problem 13.14.10, pp. 49 and 50.) A plane electromagnetic wave has the electric field

<Formula label="The wave in the ferrite" tex="\vec E = (300\ \text{V/m})\,\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{3}\,x - 2\pi \times 10^{6}\,t\right)\hat k" cite="reading1" at="Section 13.14.10, p. 49" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="The wave travels:"
  choices={[

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow a crest: where is the phase $kx - \omega t$ constant as time goes on?

The phase stays constant when $x$ grows with $t$ as $x = (\omega/k)\,t + \text{constant}$, so the pattern moves along $+x$. The field itself points along $z$, across the direction of travel, as in every electromagnetic wave.

</Solution>

</Part>

Find the wavelength, in metres.

<Part label="e" points={5}>

Find the magnetic field that Maxwell's equations require to go with this electric field. Type the size of its amplitude, in tesla, in the first box, and choose its direction in the second.

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="The energy carried by a plane wave" points={15}>

(Course notes, Chapter 13, Additional Problem 13.14.6, p. 48, with part (a) of Additional Problem 13.14.7, p. 48, and Conceptual Question 7 of Section 13.13, p. 46.) A plane wave in vacuum has the fields $\vec E = E_0\sin(kx - \omega t)\,\hat j$ and $\vec B = B_0\sin(kx - \omega t)\,\hat k$, with $E_0 = cB_0$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Show that at every point of the wave the energy density of the electric field equals that of the magnetic field. Then find the time-averaged total energy density (electric plus magnetic), in terms of $E_0$ (first box) and in terms of $B_0$ (second box).

<Hint>

Write $u_B = B^2/2\mu_0$ with $B = E/c$ and $c^2 = 1/\mu_0\varepsilon_0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The average of $\sin^2$ over a period is $1/2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a1" label="$\langle u\rangle =$" expr="eps0*E0^2/2"
  vars={{ eps0: [0.5, 2], E0: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", E0: "E_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "eps0*E0^2", hint: "That is the largest total energy density, at a crest. The average of sin^2 over a period is 1/2." },
    { expr: "eps0*E0^2/4", hint: "That is the average of the electric part alone. The magnetic part adds as much again." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-a2" label="$\langle u\rangle =$" expr="B0^2/(2*mu0)"
  vars={{ mu0: [0.5, 2], B0: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", B0: "B_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "B0^2/mu0", hint: "That is the largest total energy density, at a crest. The average of sin^2 over a period is 1/2." },
    { expr: "B0^2/(4*mu0)", hint: "That is the average of the magnetic part alone. The electric part adds as much again." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a light wave $B = E/c$, which makes the two densities equal at every point.

<Step title="Equal densities">

$u_B = \dfrac{B^2}{2\mu_0} = \dfrac{E^2}{2\mu_0 c^2} = \dfrac{\mu_0\varepsilon_0 E^2}{2\mu_0} = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0 E^2 = u_E$, at every $x$ and $t$.

</Step>

<Step title="The average">

$u = u_E + u_B = \varepsilon_0 E^2 = \varepsilon_0 E_0^2\sin^2(kx - \omega t)$, and the average of $\sin^2$ is $1/2$:

<Formula label="Average energy density" tex="\langle u\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 = \frac{B_0^2}{2\mu_0}" cite="reading1" at="Section 13.14.6, p. 48 (our own solution; compare Eq. 13.6.11, p. 16)" />

The second form follows from $\varepsilon_0 E_0^2 = \varepsilon_0 c^2 B_0^2 = B_0^2/\mu_0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

The wave falls on an object that absorbs it completely. Show that the radiation pressure on the object equals the time-averaged total energy density of the wave. (Energy density and pressure have the same unit: $\text{J/m}^3 = \text{N/m}^2$.)

<Solution>

**The idea.** A wave that carries the energy $U$ carries the momentum $p = U/c$ (Eq. 13.7.1, p. 21); the absorber takes up that momentum.

<Step title="Momentum per second">

In the time $\Delta t$ the energy that falls on an area $A$ is the energy in a column of length $c\,\Delta t$: $U = \langle u\rangle A c\,\Delta t$. Its momentum is $U/c = \langle u\rangle A\,\Delta t$. The force is the momentum absorbed per second, $\langle u\rangle A$, and the pressure is that force per unit area:

<Formula label="Pressure on an absorber" tex="P = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{1}{A}\,\frac{\langle u\rangle A\,\Delta t}{\Delta t} = \langle u\rangle = \frac{I}{c}" cite="reading1" at="Section 13.14.6, p. 48 (our own solution; compare Eq. 13.7.4, p. 21)" />

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Sunlight reaches the Earth, just outside its atmosphere, with the average intensity 1350 W/m². Find the time-averaged total energy density of this sunlight, in J/m³. An object in orbit absorbs the sunlight completely: what radiation pressure does it feel?

<Hint>

The energy moves at $c$, so the intensity is the energy density times $c$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c" label="$\langle u\rangle =$" value="4.50e-6" unit="J/m³" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-u-sun" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $I = c\,\langle u\rangle$ (Eq. 13.6.12, p. 16).

$\langle u\rangle = I/c = (1350\ \text{W/m}^2)/(3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}) = 4.50 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{J/m}^3$. By (b), the pressure on the absorbing object is the same number, $4.50 \times 10^{-6}$ Pa: about a twenty-billionth of the air pressure at the ground.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

(Additional Problem 13.14.7(a).) The electric field of another electromagnetic wave has the rms (root-mean-square) strength $3.0 \times 10^{-2}$ V/m. How much energy does it carry across an area of $1.00\ \text{cm}^2$, at right angles to its direction of travel, in one hour? Give it in joules.

<Hint>

</Step>

</Solution>

(Course notes, Chapter 13, Additional Problem 13.14.2, "Reflections of True Love", p. 46.) A light bulb gives off 100 W of electromagnetic radiation. Treat it as radiating equally in all directions (our reading), and treat the light one metre away as a plane wave, as the notes ask.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the time-averaged intensity of the radiation at the distance of 1 m from the bulb, in W/m².

<Hint>

The 100 W crosses every sphere around the bulb.

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$I =$" value="7.96" unit="W/m²" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-I" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The power spreads over a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$ (Eq. 13.6.13, p. 17).

$I = P/4\pi r^2 = 100/(4\pi \times 1^2) = 7.96\ \text{W/m}^2$.

</Solution>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the amplitudes $E_0$, in V/m (first box), and $B_0$, in tesla (second box), of the fields at this distance.

<Hint>

Solve $I = E_0^2/2\mu_0 c$ for $E_0$.

<Part label="c" points={4}>

The face of your true love is 1 m from the bulb, and (what else) it is perfect: perfectly smooth and perfectly reflecting, with the light falling on it and reflected from it at right angles to its surface. What largest surface current per unit length must flow in the face to reflect the light? Give it in A/m.

<Hint>

Inside a perfect conductor there is no field. In front of it the incident and the reflected waves add, and at the surface their magnetic fields add to $2B_0$ at most.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Take a thin rectangular Amperian loop that straddles the surface, with one long side in front and one inside. Only the side in front has a field along it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-c" label="$K_\max =$" value="0.411" unit="A/m" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-K" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The surface current must switch the magnetic field off from its value in front of the face to zero inside, and a current sheet $K$ makes a jump $\mu_0 K$ in the field along the surface.

<Step title="The field at the surface">

At a perfect mirror the reflected wave has the same amplitude as the incident one. Their electric fields cancel at the surface, a node of $\vec E$, while their magnetic fields add: at the surface the magnetic field swings between $+2B_0$ and $-2B_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Ampère's law across the surface">

A loop of length $\ell$ straddling the surface gives $2B_0\,\ell = \mu_0 K\ell$ at the moment of largest field, so

<Formula label="Largest surface current" tex="K_\max = \frac{2B_0}{\mu_0} = \frac{2(2.58 \times 10^{-7})}{4\pi \times 10^{-7}}\ \text{A/m} = 0.411\ \text{A/m}" cite="reading1" at="Section 13.14.2, p. 46 (our own solution; compare Eq. 13.10.16, p. 38)" />

This is the surface current that the notes' appendix on reflection at a perfect conductor finds, $2B_0/\mu_0$ times $\cos\omega t$.

</Step>

</Solution>

**The idea.** In a standing electromagnetic wave the nodes of $\vec E$ are the antinodes of $\vec B$, and the nodes of the two fields lie a quarter wavelength apart.
