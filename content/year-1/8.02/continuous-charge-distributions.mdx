A few [point charges](/courses/8-02/electric-dipoles) can be added one by one. A charged rod, ring or plate holds far too many, so the sum becomes an integral (course notes, Section 2.9, p. 15; slides 24–25).

## Charge densities

Charge spread through a volume is described by its **volume charge density** (course notes, Section 2.9.1, pp. 15–16; slide 26):

<Formula label="Volume charge density" tex="\rho = \frac{dq}{dV}, \qquad Q = \int\int\int_V \rho\,dV" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (2.9.1)–(2.9.2), pp. 15–16" />

in coulombs per cubic metre, just as mass density gives the mass of a body (Section 2.9.1, p. 16). On a surface the **surface charge density** is $\sigma = dq/dA$ (C/m²), and along a line the **linear charge density** is $\lambda = dq/d\ell$ (C/m); the total charge is the integral over the surface or the line (Section 2.9.2–2.9.3, p. 16). For charge spread evenly, the densities are constant: $\rho = Q/V$, $\sigma = Q/A$, $\lambda = Q/L$ (p. 17; slide 26).

## The field as an integral

Each small charge $dq$ gives a Coulomb field, with $r$ the distance from $dq$ to the point $P$ and $\hat r$ the unit vector from $dq$ to $P$; by superposition the fields add (course notes, Section 2.10, p. 17; slide 25):

<Formula label="Field of a continuous distribution" tex="d\vec E = k_e\,\frac{dq}{r^2}\,\hat r, \qquad \vec E = k_e \int \frac{dq}{r^2}\,\hat r" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (2.10.1)–(2.10.2), p. 17" />

This is a vector integral, one integral for each component (Section 2.10, p. 17). The slides work the ring example (below) as a recipe (slides 32–35): first think about symmetry, which often kills some components; define variables, writing $dq$ in terms of the integration variable; write $dE$ for the surviving component; integrate; clean up the result; and check a limit.

## A charged rod

**Along its axis.** A rod of length $\ell$ with charge $Q$ ($\lambda = Q/\ell$) lies on the $x$-axis; $P$ is on the axis, a distance $x_0$ beyond one end. Every piece pushes $P$ the same way, and integrating $k_e\lambda\,dx/x^2$ from $x_0$ to $x_0 + \ell$ gives the size (course notes, Section 2.10, pp. 17–18):

<Formula label="Rod, on its axis" tex="E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q}{x_0(\ell + x_0)}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (2.10.3), p. 18" />

Far away ($x_0 \gg \ell$) this is $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 x_0^2)$: a point charge (p. 18).

**On its perpendicular bisector.** Now $P$ is a distance $y$ from the middle of the rod, on the line through its centre perpendicular to it. By symmetry the components along the rod cancel; integrating the rest, with the substitution $x = y\tan\theta$, gives (Section 2.10, pp. 18–19; slides 36–38):

<Formula label="Rod, on its bisector" tex="E_y = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\lambda\ell}{y\sqrt{y^2 + (\ell/2)^2}}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (2.10.9), p. 19" />

Far away ($y \gg \ell$) it is the point-charge field $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 y^2)$; close by ($y \ll \ell$) the rod looks infinitely long and $E_y \approx 2\lambda/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 y) = 2k_e\lambda/y$, which falls off only as $1/y$ (Section 2.10, p. 20; slide 38).

## A ring of charge

A ring of radius $R$ carries charge $Q$; $P$ is on its axis at height $z$. Each piece $dq$ is at the same distance $\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}$ from $P$. By symmetry only the component along the axis survives, a fraction $z/\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}$ of each $dE$; and since everything but $dq$ is constant, the integral of $dq$ is just $Q$ (course notes, Section 2.10, pp. 20–21; slides 31–35):

<Formula label="Ring, on its axis" tex="E_z = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Qz}{(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (2.10.14), p. 21" />

It is zero at the centre, as symmetry demands, and close to $k_e Q/z^2$ far away (p. 22; slide 35).

## A charged disc and an infinite plane

A disc of radius $R$ with surface density $\sigma$ is a set of rings: a ring of radius $r$ and width $dr$ has charge $dq = \sigma\,2\pi r\,dr$. Adding the ring fields from $r = 0$ to $R$ gives, for $z > 0$ (course notes, Section 2.10, pp. 22–23; slides 39–40):

<Formula label="Disc, on its axis" tex="E_z = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + R^2}}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (2.10.17), p. 23" />

Far away ($z \gg R$) a Taylor expansion recovers the point charge $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 z^2)$ (Section 2.10, p. 23). Close to a large disc ($R \gg z$) the field is $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$, the same at every distance: the field of an **infinite plane**, pointing away from it on both sides (for $\sigma > 0$). Crossing the sheet, the field jumps by $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ (Section 2.10, pp. 23–24; slide 40).

How fields fall off with distance (slide 41): a dipole as $1/r^3$, a point charge as $1/r^2$, an infinite line of charge as $1/r$, and an infinite plane not at all.

<WorkedExample title="A ring and a sheet">
<Problem>
(a) A ring of radius 0.1 m carries 5 nC. Where on the axis is its field largest, and how large is it? (b) A large sheet carries $\sigma = 1\ \mu$C/m². Find the field near it ($\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}$ in SI units).
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Where the ring field peaks">
$E_z \propto z/(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}$. Its derivative is zero when $R^2 + z^2 = 3z^2$, so $z = R/\sqrt 2 \approx 0.0707$ m. There $R^2 + z^2 = 0.015$ m², and $E_z = (8.99 \times 10^9)(5 \times 10^{-9})(0.0707)/0.015^{3/2} \approx 1.73 \times 10^3$ N/C.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The sheet">
$E = \sigma/2\varepsilon_0 = 10^{-6}/(2 \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12}) \approx 5.65 \times 10^4$ N/C, pointing away from the sheet on both sides if $\sigma > 0$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The same sums for gravity">
Gravity obeys the same inverse-square law, so the same integrals, with a mass density in place of the charge density, give gravitational fields. Cosmology needs one in particular: the field of a uniform ball of matter, which (as Newton showed) acts on a point at its surface as if all its mass sat at the centre. Guth uses it to derive how a uniformly filled, expanding universe slows down.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Spread-out charge: densities $\rho = dq/dV$, $\sigma = dq/dA$, $\lambda = dq/d\ell$.
- $\vec E = k_e \int dq\,\hat r/r^2$; use symmetry first, then integrate one component.
- Rod on its bisector: $\lambda\ell/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\,y\sqrt{y^2 + \ell^2/4})$; ring on its axis: $k_e Qz/(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}$; disc: $(\sigma/2\varepsilon_0)(1 - z/\sqrt{z^2 + R^2})$.
- Far away, every finite distribution looks like a point charge; an infinite line gives $2k_e\lambda/r$ and an infinite plane $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$.
