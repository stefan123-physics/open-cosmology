Microwaves of 2 GHz have a wavelength of 15 cm; red light, at about $4.6 \times 10^{14}$ Hz, has $6.5 \times 10^{-5}$ cm. How can anyone measure lengths below a ten-thousandth of a centimetre? With **interference**, which is also how we know light is a wave (slides 2–3).

## Waves versus bullets

Fire bullets through two slits and you get two piles behind them. Send a wave and the pieces spread out and **add**: in some places they reinforce, in others they cancel (slides 4–5). Interference is the combination of waves by superposition (course notes, Section 14.1, p. 1; slide 6). To see a steady pattern the sources must be **coherent**, keeping a fixed phase relation, and **monochromatic**, of one wavelength; the light of an ordinary bulb is neither (p. 3; slide 6).

## Phase shifts from path differences

Two waves can be out of step because their sources are, or because they travel different distances (slide 10). A wave that travels an extra distance $\Delta L$ arrives with its phase shifted (slide 17):

<Formula label="Phase shift from a path difference" tex="\sin\big(k(x + \Delta L)\big) = \sin(kx + \varphi), \qquad \varphi = 2\pi\,\frac{\Delta L}{\lambda}" cite="reading2" at="slide 17" />

So the waves reinforce (**constructive** interference) when $\Delta L = m\lambda$ and cancel (**destructive**) when $\Delta L = (m + \frac12)\lambda$, for whole numbers $m$ (slides 12–13, 17).

**Thin films.** Light reflected from the top and the bottom of a thin film of thickness $d$ travels an extra path of about $2d$; at near-normal incidence the rule $2d = m\lambda$ for reinforcement and $2d = (m + \frac12)\lambda$ for cancellation picks out wavelengths, giving the colours of soap bubbles, butterfly wings and oil on puddles, and the non-reflective coatings on lenses (slides 14–16). (This simple form, like the slide's, ignores two refinements: inside the film the wavelength is $\lambda/n$, and a reflection off a denser medium flips the wave by half a cycle, which for a soap bubble swaps the bright and dark conditions.)

## Two sources: Young's double slit

Two in-phase sources a distance $d$ apart, and a screen a distance $L \gg d$ away. Towards a point at angle $\theta$, the rays are nearly parallel and differ in path by $d\sin\theta$ (course notes, Section 14.2, pp. 4–5; slide 20):

<Formula label="Double-slit maxima" tex="d\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (14.2.5), p. 5" />

and dark fringes where $d\sin\theta = (m + \frac12)\lambda$ (p. 5). For small angles, $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta = y/L$, so the fringes on the screen are at (Section 14.2, p. 6; slide 21)

<Formula label="Fringe positions" tex="y_{\text{bright}} = m\,\frac{\lambda L}{d}, \qquad y_{\text{dark}} = \left(m + \frac12\right)\frac{\lambda L}{d}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (14.2.8)–(14.2.9), p. 6" />

Measure the spacing, and the wavelength follows: for light, with $\lambda$ about $2 \times 10^5$ times smaller than for microwaves (the slide says ten thousand times), it is enough to make $d$ small, a fraction of a millimetre (slides 22–23). This is **Young's double-slit experiment** (slides 24–27).

**Intensity.** Light intensity goes as the square of the field. For two coherent waves of equal strength the fields add before squaring, giving four times one source's intensity where they reinforce and zero where they cancel; incoherent sources would just add their intensities (Section 14.3, pp. 7–8). Across the screen (pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Double-slit intensity" tex="I = I_0\cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (14.3.14), p. 9" />

## Diffraction

Waves also **bend** as they pass obstacles and openings: **diffraction**. By Huygens's principle every point of a wavefront acts as a source of new wavelets (course notes, Section 14.4, p. 12; slides 28–29).

**A single slit** of width $a$: split it into two halves. Each point in one half has a partner $a/2$ away in the other, and their paths differ by $\frac{a}{2}\sin\theta$. When that is half a wavelength, the pairs cancel, so the first dark direction is at $a\sin\theta = \lambda$; dividing further gives all of them (Section 14.5, pp. 12–14; slides 30–31):

<Formula label="Single-slit minima" tex="a\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots" cite="reading2" at="slide 30" />

This looks like the double-slit maximum condition but means the opposite: here the waves from within one slit cancel (p. 14). The central bright band, between the first minima on either side, has width $2y_1$ (Example 14.3, pp. 14–15); for small angles that is twice the width of the other bands (Fig. 14.5.2, p. 14; slide 31).

**Two slits of finite width** combine both effects: the interference fringes of the two slits, spaced by $d\sin\theta = \lambda$, under the envelope of each slit's diffraction pattern, which falls to zero at $a\sin\theta = \lambda$ (Section 14.7, pp. 18–19; slides 34–35):

<Formula label="Double slit with finite width" tex="I = I_0\cos^2\!\left(\frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\right)\left[\frac{\sin(\pi a\sin\theta/\lambda)}{\pi a\sin\theta/\lambda}\right]^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (14.7.1), p. 19" />

**Babinet's principle.** A slit gives diffraction; a filled-in slit gives nothing; an obstacle the shape of the slit alone gives diffraction again. By superposition the two patterns must be the same away from the straight-through beam, so a thin hair diffracts like a slit of the same width (slide 37). Experiment 13 uses this: it measures the wavelength of red light with single slits of known width and with double slits of known spacing, the width of a human hair, and the track spacing of a CD acting as a diffraction grating (slide 38).

**Diffraction gratings.** Many equally spaced slits, a distance $d$ apart, all reinforce in the directions where neighbours differ in path by whole wavelengths, $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, the same condition as for two slits; with more slits the bright maxima become much sharper (Section 14.8, pp. 19–20). This is how spectrographs measure wavelengths precisely.

<Problem>
Red light of wavelength 633 nm falls on two slits 0.25 mm apart; the screen is 2 m away. (a) Find the spacing of the bright fringes and the position of the first dark fringe. (b) A single slit 0.1 mm wide replaces them. Find the first dark spot and the width of the central bright band.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Double slit">
$\lambda L/d = (633 \times 10^{-9})(2)/(0.25 \times 10^{-3}) \approx 5.06$ mm between bright fringes; the first dark fringe is half of that from the centre, about 2.53 mm.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Single slit">
First minimum at $y = \lambda L/a = (633 \times 10^{-9})(2)/(10^{-4}) \approx 12.7$ mm, so the central bright band is about 25.3 mm wide: a narrower slit spreads the light more.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Coherent waves add: path difference $m\lambda$ constructive, $(m + \frac12)\lambda$ destructive; phase shift $2\pi\Delta L/\lambda$.
- Double slit: $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, fringes $\lambda L/d$ apart; intensity $I_0\cos^2(\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda)$.
- Single slit: dark at $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$; narrower slits spread light more.
- Two finite slits: interference fringes under a diffraction envelope. Babinet: an obstacle diffracts like the matching slit.
