MIT's written homework for 8.02T is not on OpenCourseWare. This set is built instead from MIT's in-class Problem Solving 5 and from three Additional Problems of Chapter 8 of the 8.02 course notes. Problems 1 and 2 and parts (a) and (b) of Problem 3 follow the session, in our own words and in MIT's order; the rest of Problem 3 and Problems 4 and 5 come from the notes. Neither source prints solutions for these problems, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. The torque on a current loop and the magnetic dipole moment are met for the first time in this session; the lesson on [magnetic dipoles](/courses/8-02/magnetic-dipoles) that follows it treats them in full. All five problems count for the score (100 points).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first and draw the loop or wire with its axes: MIT's sheets have drawings, and here each situation is described in words. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A formula box takes plain typing, for example `rho*A*dx`, `I*a*b*B` or `I*l^2*B*cos(theta)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type $\rho$ as `rho`, $\theta$ as `theta`, $\ell$ as `l`, and subscripts on the line: `vd` for $v_d$. There is no `cot`: write $\cot x$ as `1/tan(x)`. A number box accepts any value within 2% of ours and takes a bare number in the unit shown next to the box. You may type a large number as `1.2e5`. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.

Neither the session nor the notes give points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem or part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## What you need from the lessons

A charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ through a magnetic field $\vec B$ feels a force perpendicular to both:

<Formula label="Magnetic force on a moving charge" tex="\vec F_B = q\,\vec v \times \vec B" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.2.1), p. 2" />

A current is moving charge, so a straight wire of length $L$ carrying the current $I$ feels a force, with $\vec L$ pointing along the current; a wire of any shape feels the sum of the forces on its short pieces $d\vec s$:

<Formula label="Force on a wire" tex="\vec F_B = I\,\vec L \times \vec B, \qquad \vec F_B = I\int d\vec s \times \vec B" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (8.3.1) and (8.3.3), p. 4" />

The torque of a force about a point is $\vec r \times \vec F$, with $\vec r$ the vector from that point to where the force acts. Problem Solving 5 adds the **magnetic dipole moment** of a flat loop of area $A$, with the unit normal $\hat n$ given by the right-hand rule (fingers curled along the current, thumb along $\hat n$), and the torque on it in a uniform field:

<Formula label="Magnetic dipole moment and its torque" tex="\vec\mu = I A\,\hat n, \qquad \vec\tau = \vec\mu \times \vec B_\text{ext}" cite="mit" at="p. 7" />

The torque on an electric dipole, $\vec\tau = \vec p \times \vec E_\text{ext}$, has the same form (p. 7). For Problem 5 you need the current density $J = I/A_\perp$ of a wire of cross-section $A_\perp$. A straight piece of that wire, of length $L$ and made of a material with the resistivity $\rho_e$, has the resistance $\rho_e L/A_\perp$, so the current $I$ turns the power $P$ into heat in it:

<Formula label="Current density, and the power turned into heat" tex="J = \frac{I}{A_\perp}, \qquad P = I^2\,\frac{\rho_e\,L}{A_\perp}" cite="reading2" at="Eqs. (6.1.3), (6.2.10) and (6.3.2), pp. 2, 5 and 7" />

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="From moving charges to the force on a wire" points={15} mit="Questions 1 to 4, pp. 2 to 3">

A straight wire with cross-sectional area $A$ carries the current $I$ through a uniform external field $\vec B_\text{ext}$. Take the moving charges to be positive, with $\rho$ coulombs of moving charge per cubic metre, all drifting along the wire with the drift velocity $\vec v_d$. Look at a short piece of the wire, of length $dx$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the charge $dq$ in this short piece, in terms of $\rho$, $A$ and $dx$.

<Hint>

The charge is the charge density times the volume. What is the volume of a thin slice of a wire?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$dq =$" expr="rho*A*dx"
  vars={{ rho: [1, 10], A: [2, 5], dx: [0.01, 0.1] }}
  names={{ rho: "\\rho" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "rho*dx/A", hint: "A slice of a wire is a short cylinder: its volume is the area of its end times its length, not the length divided by the area." },
    { expr: "rho*A", hint: "That is the charge per unit length of the wire. The piece has the length dx." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Charge per volume times volume.

The piece is a short cylinder with end area $A$ and length $dx$, so its volume is $A\,dx$ and

<Formula label="Charge in a short piece of wire" tex="dq = \rho\,A\,dx" cite="mit" at="Question 1, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Check: $\rho$ is in C/m³ and $A\,dx$ in m³, so $dq$ is in coulombs.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

In a time $dt$ the charges move the distance $v_d\,dt$, so all the charge in a piece of length $dx = v_d\,dt$ crosses the end of that piece. Use this to find the current $I$ in the wire, in terms of $\rho$, $A$ and $v_d$.

<Hint>

The current is the charge that passes a fixed point, divided by the time it takes to pass.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put $dx = v_d\,dt$ into your answer to part (a), then divide by $dt$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$I =$" expr="rho*A*vd"
  vars={{ rho: [1, 10], A: [2, 5], vd: [0.001, 0.01] }}
  names={{ rho: "\\rho", vd: "v_d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "rho*vd/A", hint: "A thicker wire carries more current at the same drift speed, so the area belongs in the numerator." },
    { expr: "rho*A/vd", hint: "Faster charges carry more charge past a point each second, so the current grows with v_d." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count the charge that crosses the end of the piece in the time $dt$.

<Step title="The charge that crosses">

In the time $dt$ every charge in a piece of length $v_d\,dt$ reaches the end and crosses it. By part (a) that charge is

<Formula label="Charge crossing in a time dt" tex="dq = \rho\,A\,v_d\,dt" cite="mit" at="Question 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The current">

Divide by $dt$:

<Formula label="Current from the drift velocity" tex="I = \frac{dq}{dt} = \rho\,A\,v_d" cite="mit" at="Question 3, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The units are C/m³ times m² times m/s, which is C/s, an ampere. With $\rho = nq$ for $n$ carriers of charge $q$ per cubic metre this is the familiar $I = nqv_dA$ of the lessons.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Each moving charge feels the magnetic force of the lessons, so the force on the charge $dq$ in the piece is $d\vec F_B = dq\,\vec v_d \times \vec B_\text{ext}$. Let $d\vec s$ be a vector of length $dx$ along the wire, pointing the way the current flows. Using parts (a) and (b), show that

<Formula label="Force on a current element" tex="d\vec F_B = dq\,\vec v_d \times \vec B_\text{ext} = I\,d\vec s \times \vec B_\text{ext}" cite="mit" at="Question 4, p. 3" />

and so that the force on the whole wire is the integral of $I\,d\vec s \times \vec B_\text{ext}$ along it.

<Hint>

The drift velocity and $d\vec s$ point the same way. Write $\vec v_d = v_d\,\hat s$ and $d\vec s = dx\,\hat s$, with $\hat s$ a unit vector along the current.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Move the scalar factors around: the charge, the speed and the length are numbers, and only the directions are vectors.

<Step title="The algebra">

With $dq = \rho A\,dx$ and $\vec v_d = v_d\,\hat s$,

<Formula label="Rearranging the force on a piece of wire" tex="dq\,\vec v_d \times \vec B_\text{ext} = \rho A\,dx\,v_d\,\hat s \times \vec B_\text{ext} = (\rho A v_d)\,(dx\,\hat s) \times \vec B_\text{ext} = I\,d\vec s \times \vec B_\text{ext}" cite="mit" at="Question 4, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

using $I = \rho A v_d$ from part (b) and $d\vec s = dx\,\hat s$.

</Step>

<Step title="The whole wire">

The forces on all the pieces add up, which turns the sum into an integral along the wire:

<Formula label="Force on a whole wire" tex="\vec F_B = \int I\,d\vec s \times \vec B_\text{ext}" cite="mit" at="p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For a straight wire in a uniform field, $\int d\vec s = \vec L$, and the integral gives back $\vec F_B = I\,\vec L \times \vec B$ of the lessons. The units of $I\,d\vec s \times \vec B$ are A m T, and 1 T = 1 N/(A m), so they are newtons.

</Step>

Mark this part yourself: full points if you wrote the steps in this order and kept track of which factors are vectors.

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Forces and torque on a rectangular loop" points={25} mit="Questions 5 to 15, pp. 4 to 7">

A rectangular loop carries the current $I$ in a uniform field $\vec B_\text{ext} = B\,\hat i$. Set up the axes like this: $\hat i$ points to the right, $\hat k$ points up, and $\hat j$ points away from you, into the page. The loop lies in the plane of the page, the plane of $\hat i$ and $\hat k$, so the field lies in the plane of the loop. Its two vertical sides have length $a$ and its two horizontal sides length $b$. Number the sides: side 1 is the top, side 2 the left, side 3 the bottom, side 4 the right. As you look at the page, the current flows anticlockwise: to the right along side 3, up side 4, to the left along side 1 and down side 2. By the right-hand rule the unit normal of the loop then points towards you, $\hat n = -\hat j$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Use $\vec F_B = \int I\,d\vec s \times \vec B_\text{ext}$, taken along the wire, to find the force on side 4 (size and direction).

<Hint>

On side 4 the current flows up, so $d\vec s$ points along $+\hat k$ and the side has length $a$. You need $\hat k \times \hat i$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="Force on side 4:"
  choices={[
    "size $IaB$, along −ĵ (out of the page)",
    "size $IbB$, along +ĵ (into the page)",
    "size $IaB$, along +ĵ (into the page)",
    "size $IaB$, along +k̂ (up)"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Side 4 is straight and the field is uniform, so the integral is the length vector of the side crossed with the field.

Side 4 runs upwards, so $\int d\vec s = a\,\hat k$ and

<Formula label="Force on side 4" tex="\vec F_4 = I\,(a\,\hat k) \times (B\,\hat i) = IaB\,(\hat k \times \hat i) = IaB\,\hat j" cite="mit" at="Question 5, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

It has size $IaB$ and points along $+\hat j$, into the page. Check with the right-hand rule: fingers up along the current, curled towards $\vec B$ on the right, the thumb points away from you.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the forces on sides 3, 2 and 1, and the total force on the loop.

<Hint>

Two of these sides run along the field, one of them with it and one against it. What is the cross product of two parallel vectors?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="Forces on sides 3, 2 and 1, and the total:"
  choices={[
    "sides 3 and 1: zero; side 2: $IaB$ along −ĵ; total: zero",
    "sides 3 and 1: zero; side 2: $IaB$ along +ĵ; total: $2IaB$ along +ĵ",
    "side 3: $IbB$ along +k̂; side 1: $IbB$ along −k̂; side 2: $IaB$ along −ĵ; total: zero",
    "sides 3 and 1: zero; side 2: zero; total: $IaB$ along +ĵ"
  ]}
  answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same rule for each straight side, then add.

<Step title="Sides 3 and 1">

On side 3 the current flows along $+\hat i$, parallel to $\vec B$; on side 1 along $-\hat i$, antiparallel. In both cases $d\vec s \times \vec B_\text{ext} = 0$, so $\vec F_3 = \vec F_1 = 0$ (Questions 6 and 8, pp. 4 and 5).

</Step>

<Step title="Side 2">

Side 2 runs downwards, so its length vector is $-a\,\hat k$:

<Formula label="Force on side 2" tex="\vec F_2 = I\,(-a\,\hat k) \times (B\,\hat i) = -IaB\,\hat j" cite="mit" at="Question 7, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The total">

$\vec F_1 + \vec F_2 + \vec F_3 + \vec F_4 = 0 - IaB\,\hat j + 0 + IaB\,\hat j = 0$ (Question 9, p. 5). This is the general rule of the lessons: a closed loop in a uniform field feels no net force, because its pieces $d\vec s$ add up to zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={8}>

The two forces you found are equal and opposite, but they act on opposite sides of the loop. Find the size of the total torque $\vec\tau = \vec\tau_4 + \vec\tau_2$ about the centre of the loop, in terms of $I$, $a$, $b$ and $B$. Treat the force on each side as acting at the middle of that side, so that $\vec\tau_4 = \vec r_4 \times \vec F_4$ with $\vec r_4$ the vector from the centre of the loop to the middle of side 4. (Why do sides 1 and 3 give no torque?)

<Hint>

The middle of side 4 is at $\vec r_4 = (b/2)\,\hat i$ from the centre, and the middle of side 2 at $\vec r_2 = -(b/2)\,\hat i$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Work out both cross products. Do the two torques add up or cancel?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$\tau =$" expr="I*a*b*B"
  vars={{ I: [0.5, 5], a: [0.05, 0.5], b: [0.05, 0.5], B: [0.1, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "I*a*b*B/2", hint: "That is the torque of one side only. The torque of side 2 points the same way as that of side 4: they add." },
    { expr: "2*I*a*b*B", hint: "The lever arm of each force is half the width of the loop, b/2, not b: the torque is taken about the centre." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each force has the lever arm $b/2$ from the centre. Because the forces point opposite ways on opposite sides, their torques point the same way and add.

<Step title="The two torques">

<Formula label="Torques of sides 4 and 2" tex="\vec\tau_4 = \frac{b}{2}\,\hat i \times IaB\,\hat j = \frac{IabB}{2}\,\hat k, \qquad \vec\tau_2 = -\frac{b}{2}\,\hat i \times \left(-IaB\,\hat j\right) = \frac{IabB}{2}\,\hat k" cite="mit" at="Questions 10 and 11, pp. 5 and 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Sides 1 and 3, and the total">

Sides 1 and 3 feel no force, so they give no torque (Question 12, p. 6). The total is

<Formula label="Total torque on the rectangular loop" tex="\vec\tau = \vec\tau_4 + \vec\tau_2 = IabB\,\hat k" cite="mit" at="Question 13, p. 6 (our own solution)" />

of size $\tau = IabB$, the current times the area $ab$ times the field.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The units are A m² T = A m² N/(A m) = N m, a torque. Doubling the current, the field or either side doubles the torque.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

What effect does this torque have on the loop, if nothing else holds it?

<Hint>

Which way does the total torque of part (c) point? A torque turns a body about the torque's own direction, by the right-hand rule. Then ask where the normal $\hat n = -\hat j$, which points towards you, swings to.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="The loop:"
  choices={[
    "turns about the vertical axis, and its normal n̂ swings away from the direction of the field",
    "is pushed upwards, along k̂, without turning",
    "turns about the vertical axis, and its normal n̂ swings towards the direction of the field",
    "turns about the horizontal axis through the middles of sides 2 and 4"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A torque changes the rotation about its own direction.

The torque points along $+\hat k$, up, so it turns the loop about the vertical axis through its centre, anticlockwise as seen from above. Side 4 is pushed into the page and side 2 out of it. The normal starts at $\hat n = -\hat j$, towards you, and a small turn about $+\hat k$ moves it towards $+\hat i$ (because $\hat k \times (-\hat j) = +\hat i$): towards the direction of the field (Question 14, p. 6). The torque is largest when the field lies in the plane of the loop, as here, and vanishes when $\hat n$ points along $\vec B_\text{ext}$. Without friction the loop swings past that position and back again: it oscillates about it, like a compass needle.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

Compute the torque again, now from $\vec\tau = \vec\mu \times \vec B_\text{ext}$ with $\vec\mu = IA\,\hat n$. Does it agree with part (c)?

<Hint>

The area is $A = ab$ and $\hat n = -\hat j$. You need $(-\hat j) \times \hat i$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the loop's moment into the dipole formula.

<Formula label="Torque from the dipole moment" tex="\vec\mu = Iab\,(-\hat j), \qquad \vec\tau = \vec\mu \times \vec B_\text{ext} = IabB\,(-\hat j \times \hat i) = IabB\,\hat k" cite="mit" at="Question 15, p. 7 (our own solution)" />

since $\hat j \times \hat i = -\hat k$. Size and direction agree with part (c). The dipole formula does the bookkeeping of sides and lever arms in one step, and it holds for a flat loop of any shape.

Mark this part yourself: full points if you found $IabB\,\hat k$, with its direction.

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A square loop on a hinge" points={30} mit="Questions 16 and 17, p. 8">

A square loop of wire with sides of length $\ell$ can swing about a horizontal axis that runs along its top side, like a flap hanging from a hinge. The loop makes the angle $\theta$ with the vertical: at $\theta = 0$ it hangs straight down. A uniform magnetic field of size $B$ points vertically downwards everywhere near the loop. The current $I$ flows anticlockwise as seen from above.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Use the magnetic dipole moment to find the size of the torque of the magnetic forces on the loop, in terms of $I$, $\ell$, $B$ and $\theta$. Take $0 < \theta < 90^\circ$.

<Hint>

The dipole moment is perpendicular to the plane of the loop. When the loop hangs at the angle $\theta$ from the vertical, what angle does its normal make with the horizontal?

</Hint>

<Hint>

An anticlockwise current seen from above gives a normal with an upward part, and the field points down. With the first hint, what is the angle between $\vec\mu$ and $\vec B$? Simplify the sine of that angle.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$\tau =$" expr="I*l^2*B*cos(theta)"
  vars={{ I: [0.5, 5], l: [0.05, 0.5], B: [0.01, 1], theta: [0.1, 0.7] }}
  names={{ l: "\\ell", theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "I*l^2*B*sin(theta)", hint: "θ is the angle of the loop's plane with the vertical, not the angle between the dipole moment and the field. Check θ = 0: the loop hangs straight down, its normal is horizontal and perpendicular to B, so the torque is largest there." },
    { expr: "I*l*B*cos(theta)", hint: "The dipole moment is the current times the area of the loop, and the area of a square is ℓ²." },
    { expr: "I*l^2*B", hint: "That is the largest torque, reached only when the normal is perpendicular to the field. The size of μ × B carries the sine of the angle between them." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The size of $\vec\tau = \vec\mu \times \vec B$ is $\mu B$ times the sine of the angle between the moment and the field.

<Step title="The dipole moment">

The area is $\ell^2$, so $\mu = I\ell^2$. The plane of the loop contains the horizontal hinge and a direction at the angle $\theta$ from the vertical, so its normal is perpendicular to both: it makes the angle $\theta$ with the horizontal. Curl the fingers of your right hand anticlockwise as seen from above and the thumb points upwards: $\vec\mu$ points out from the side the loop swings towards, tilted up by $\theta$.

</Step>

<Step title="The angle and the torque">

The field points straight down, so the angle between $\vec\mu$ and $\vec B$ is $90^\circ + \theta$, and $\sin(90^\circ + \theta) = \cos\theta$:

<Formula label="Torque on the hinged loop" tex="\tau = \mu B \sin(90^\circ + \theta) = I\ell^2 B\cos\theta" cite="mit" at="Question 16, p. 8 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check with the forces">

The two slanted sides carry opposite currents and feel equal and opposite forces along the hinge, which give no torque about it. The bottom side, of length $\ell$, carries the current horizontally, perpendicular to $\vec B$, and feels the horizontal force $I\ell B$. Its lever arm about the hinge is the vertical depth of the bottom side below the hinge, $\ell\cos\theta$, so the torque is $I\ell B \cdot \ell\cos\theta$, the same result. (The top side lies on the axis and has no lever arm.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Does this torque make the loop swing so that $\theta$ grows or shrinks?

<Hint>

The torque turns $\vec\mu$ towards $\vec B$. The moment has an upward part and the field points down. Which way must the loop swing for $\vec\mu$ to tip downwards by the shorter way?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="The angle θ:"
  choices={[
    "grows: the loop swings further out",
    "stays the same: the torque only presses the loop against its hinge",
    "shrinks: the loop swings back towards the vertical"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The torque always turns the dipole moment towards the field.

The moment is tilted up by $\theta$ above the horizontal. To point down along $\vec B$ by the shorter way it must tip down through the horizontal, which happens when the loop swings back towards the vertical: $\theta$ shrinks (Question 17, p. 8). The forces tell the same story. With the current anticlockwise from above, the current in the bottom side runs so that $I\,\vec\ell \times \vec B$ points back under the hinge, pulling the bottom side towards the vertical.

</Solution>

</Part>

Now give the loop numbers, as in the course notes (Chapter 8, Additional Problem 8.11.2, pp. 23 and 24). The loop has $\ell = 0.10$ m and mass $m = 50$ g, spread evenly along the wire. The field is 500 G $= 0.050$ T, still vertically downwards. The loop carries a current that holds it at rest at $\theta = 20^\circ$. Gravity now acts too.

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Which way must the current flow to hold the loop at $\theta = 20^\circ$?

<Hint>

Gravity alone would pull the loop back to the vertical, so the magnetic torque must push it outwards. Compare with part (b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="The current flows:"
  choices={[
    "anticlockwise as seen from above",
    "clockwise as seen from above",
    "either way: only the size of the current matters"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two torques must point opposite ways.

The weight acts at the centre of mass, which hangs out from below the hinge, so gravity turns the loop back towards the vertical, making $\theta$ smaller. The magnetic torque must therefore make $\theta$ larger. Part (b) showed that an anticlockwise current (seen from above) makes $\theta$ smaller, so the current must flow the other way: clockwise as seen from above. Then the force $I\ell B$ on the bottom side points outwards, away from under the hinge.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={8}>

Find the current $I$ that holds the loop at $\theta = 20^\circ$. Give it in amperes.

<Hint>

Balance the torques about the hinge. The magnetic torque is your result of part (a).

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the torque of gravity, take each of the four sides separately, each with a quarter of the mass at its own middle. The top side lies on the axis. How far out from below the hinge is the middle of each of the other three sides?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-d" label="$I =$" value="17.8" unit="A" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-current" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At rest, the torque of gravity about the hinge and the magnetic torque about it are equal and opposite.

<Step title="The torque of gravity">

Each side has the mass $m/4$. A point at the distance $s$ from the hinge along the tilted loop lies the horizontal distance $s\sin\theta$ out from below the hinge, and that is the lever arm of its weight. The middles of the two slanted sides are at $s = \ell/2$ and the bottom side is at $s = \ell$; the top side is on the axis. So

<Formula label="Torque of gravity on the hinged loop" tex="\tau_g = \frac{m}{4}\,g\sin\theta\left(\frac{\ell}{2} + \frac{\ell}{2} + \ell\right) = \frac{m g \ell}{2}\,\sin\theta" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 8.11.2(c), p. 24 (our own solution)" />

the same as the whole mass at the centre of the square, a distance $\ell/2$ from the hinge.

</Step>

<Step title="Balance and solve">

Set $I\ell^2 B\cos\theta = \tfrac12 m g \ell\sin\theta$:

<Formula label="Current that holds the loop" tex="I = \frac{m g \tan\theta}{2\,\ell B}" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 8.11.2(c), p. 24 (our own solution)" />

With $m = 0.050$ kg, $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, $\tan 20^\circ = 0.364$, $\ell = 0.10$ m and $B = 0.050$ T: $I = 0.49 \times 0.364/(2 \times 0.10 \times 0.050) = 17.8$ A.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The notes give "about 20 A", which agrees. A larger angle needs more current, and at $\theta = 90^\circ$ no finite current would do: the loop would be horizontal, its moment vertical and parallel to the field, and the magnetic torque zero. Both torques at 20° are $\tau_g = 0.49 \times 0.10 \times \sin 20^\circ/2 = 8.4 \times 10^{-3}$ N m.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={5}>

What force do the pivots of the hinge exert on the loop?

<Hint>

Add up all the forces on the loop. What is the total magnetic force on a closed loop in a uniform field?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-e" label="The pivots push with:"
  choices={[
    "0.49 N straight up, with a horizontal part of size IℓB as well",
    "less than 0.49 N, because the magnetic force carries part of the weight",
    "zero, because the magnetic torque holds the loop up",
    "0.49 N straight up"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The loop is at rest, so all the forces on it add up to zero.

The magnetic forces on a closed loop in a uniform field add up to zero (Problem 2(b)): here the forces on the two slanted sides are equal and opposite along the hinge, and the force on the hinge side is equal and opposite to the force on the bottom side. They exert a torque but no net force. The only other forces are the weight, $mg = 0.050 \times 9.8 = 0.49$ N downwards, and the push of the pivots. So the pivots push upwards with 0.49 N, straight up.

</Solution>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A loop shaped like an ice-cream cone" points={15}>

From the course notes, Chapter 8, Additional Problem 8.11.6 (p. 26). A flat current loop is made of a semicircle of radius $R$ and two straight pieces of wire. The straight pieces start at the two ends of the semicircle's diameter and meet at a point, the tip of the cone, where they make the angle $\theta$ with each other. Draw it with the tip at the top and the semicircle at the bottom, in the plane of the page. A uniform field $\vec B$ in the plane of the page points to the right, perpendicular to the loop's line of symmetry. The current $I$ flows round the loop clockwise as you look at the page.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

What is the net magnetic force on the loop?

<Hint>

You found the answer for a rectangle in Problem 2. Does the shape matter?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="The net force is:"
  choices={[
    "$2IRB$, the force on the curved part, which the straight parts do not cancel",
    "zero",
    "IB(π + 2)R, from the lengths of wire across the field",
    "along the line of symmetry, of size πIRB"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a uniform field, the field comes out of the integral.

<Formula label="Force on a closed loop in a uniform field" tex="\vec F_B = I\left(\oint d\vec s\right) \times \vec B = 0" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.3.5), p. 5" />

because the steps $d\vec s$ around a closed loop add up to zero: you end where you started. The shape of the loop does not matter.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

Find the size of the net torque on the loop. Give it in terms of $I$, $B$, $R$ and $\theta$ only (the length of the straight pieces is fixed by $R$ and $\theta$).

<Hint>

Use $\vec\tau = \vec\mu \times \vec B$. The normal is perpendicular to the page, so it is perpendicular to $\vec B$. All you need is the area of the loop.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The loop is a half disc plus a triangle. The triangle has its base on the diameter, of length $2R$, and its tip at the angle $\theta$. How high is it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$\tau =$" expr="I*B*R^2*(pi/2 + 1/tan(theta/2))"
  vars={{ I: [0.5, 5], B: [0.1, 2], R: [0.05, 0.3], theta: [0.5, 2.5] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "I*B*R^2*pi/2", hint: "That is the half disc alone. The triangle between the straight pieces is also inside the loop." },
    { expr: "I*B*R^2*(pi + 1/tan(theta/2))", hint: "The curved part is a semicircle: its area is half of πR²." },
    { expr: "I*B*R^2*(pi/2 + 2/tan(theta/2))", hint: "The area of a triangle is half its base times its height." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a uniform field the torque on any flat loop is $\vec\mu \times \vec B$, and $\mu$ is the current times the area enclosed.

<Step title="The height of the triangle">

The line of symmetry splits the triangle into two right triangles, each with the angle $\theta/2$ at the tip, the side $R$ opposite it and the height $h$ next to it. So $\tan(\theta/2) = R/h$ and $h = R\cot(\theta/2)$. (Each straight piece is $R/\sin(\theta/2)$ long.)

</Step>

<Step title="The area and the torque">

The half disc has the area $\pi R^2/2$ and the triangle $\tfrac12 \cdot 2R \cdot h = R^2\cot(\theta/2)$. The normal is perpendicular to the page and $\vec B$ lies in the page, so $\sin 90^\circ = 1$ and

<Formula label="Torque on the cone-shaped loop" tex="\tau = I B R^2\left(\frac{\pi}{2} + \cot\frac{\theta}{2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 8.11.6(b), p. 26 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

As $\theta$ grows towards $180^\circ$ the tip flattens onto the diameter, $\cot(\theta/2) \to 0$, and only the half disc is left. As $\theta$ shrinks the cone grows long and the torque grows without limit. A number (ours): with $\theta = 90^\circ$ the triangle has the area $R^2$, and the torque is $(\pi/2 + 1)\,IBR^2 = 2.57\,IBR^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Which way does the torque point?

<Hint>

A clockwise current, as you look at the page, gives a moment pointing into the page.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c" label="The torque points:"
  choices={[
    "into the page",
    "along the line of symmetry, from the tip towards the semicircle",
    "along the line of symmetry, from the semicircle towards the tip",
    "to the right, along the field"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Right-hand rule twice: once for $\vec\mu$, once for $\vec\mu \times \vec B$.

Take $\hat i$ to the right, $\hat j$ up the page (from the semicircle towards the tip) and $\hat k$ out of the page. Fingers curled clockwise as you look at the page put the thumb into the page, so $\vec\mu = -\mu\,\hat k$. With $\vec B = B\,\hat i$, $\vec\tau = -\mu B\,(\hat k \times \hat i) = -\mu B\,\hat j$: down the page, along the line of symmetry from the tip towards the semicircle. The torque lies in the plane of the loop, perpendicular to the field, and turns the loop about its line of symmetry so that $\vec\mu$ swings towards $\vec B$.

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A floating wire" points={15}>

From the course notes, Chapter 8, Additional Problem 8.11.8 (p. 27). At the Earth's equator the Earth's magnetic field is horizontal, points north and has the size $B = 0.5 \times 10^{-4}$ T. A straight copper wire lies horizontally there, running east to west. Copper has the mass density $\rho_m = 8.9 \times 10^3\ \text{kg/m}^3$ and the resistivity $\rho_e = 1.7 \times 10^{-8}$ Ω m. A current of uniform density $J$ flows along the wire.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

How large must $J$ be for the magnetic force to carry the weight of the wire, and which way must the current flow? Give $J$ in A/m².

<Hint>

Take a piece of the wire of length $L$ and cross-section $A_\perp$. Write its weight and the magnetic force on it, using $I = J A_\perp$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The current is perpendicular to the field, so the force has the size $ILB$. The diameter of the wire drops out.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$J =$" value="1.74e9" unit="A/m²" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-J" />

<Answer id="p5-a-dir" label="The current flows:"
  choices={[
    "towards the west",
    "towards the east",
    "either way: the force is upwards for both"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Per unit volume of wire, the magnetic force $JB$ must equal the weight $\rho_m g$.

<Step title="The balance">

A piece of length $L$ and cross-section $A_\perp$ has the mass $\rho_m A_\perp L$. It carries $I = JA_\perp$ perpendicular to $\vec B$, so the magnetic force on it has the size $JA_\perp L B$. Setting the two equal, $A_\perp L$ cancels:

<Formula label="Current density that floats the wire" tex="J = \frac{\rho_m\,g}{B}" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 8.11.8(a), p. 27 (our own solution)" />

which is $8.9 \times 10^3 \times 9.8/(0.5 \times 10^{-4}) = 1.74 \times 10^9\ \text{A/m}^2$, whatever the diameter.

</Step>

<Step title="The direction">

Take $\hat i$ east, $\hat j$ north and $\hat k$ up. The field is $B\,\hat j$, and the force must point along $+\hat k$. For a current to the east, $\hat i \times \hat j = \hat k$: upwards. For a current to the west the force would point down. So the current flows east.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

That is about 1700 A through every square millimetre of the wire. A household wire carries a few amperes per square millimetre, so this is hundreds of times more: the Earth's field is very weak.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

While the wire floats, how much power is turned into heat in each cubic centimetre of it? Give it in W/cm³.

<Hint>

For a piece of length $L$ and cross-section $A_\perp$, write the power with $I = JA_\perp$ and the resistance $\rho_e L/A_\perp$, then divide by the volume.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="Power per cubic centimetre:" value="5.17e4" unit="W/cm³" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-power" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Joule heating per unit volume is the resistivity times the current density squared.

<Step title="Power per volume">

<Formula label="Heating per unit volume" tex="\frac{P}{A_\perp L} = \frac{(J A_\perp)^2\,\rho_e L/A_\perp}{A_\perp L} = \rho_e\,J^2" cite="reading2" at="from Eqs. (6.2.10) and (6.3.2), pp. 5 and 7 (our own step)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

$\rho_e J^2 = 1.7 \times 10^{-8} \times (1.744 \times 10^9)^2 = 5.17 \times 10^{10}\ \text{W/m}^3$. A cubic metre is $10^6$ cubic centimetres, so each cubic centimetre turns $5.17 \times 10^4$ W, about 52 kW, into heat.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

That is the power of some twenty electric kettles in a sugar cube of copper: the wire would melt within a fraction of a second. Floating a wire in the Earth's field is possible on paper only.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
