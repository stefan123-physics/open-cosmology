Session 25 is built around Experiment 10: first measuring an inductor, then an oscillating circuit (slides 1, 8, 23). The new physics is that an inductor and a capacitor together **oscillate**.

## Real inductors

[In an LR circuit](/courses/8-02/inductance-and-lr-circuits) the current rises as $(\varepsilon/R)(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ (slides 2–4). A real inductor is a long wire, so it has some resistance $R_L$ as well as inductance: in the direction of the current its emf is $-L\,dI/dt - IR_L$ (slide 6). Experiment 10 measures both: $L$ from the time constant of the current or voltage (which is $L/(R + R_L)$, so it needs $R_L$ too), and $R_L$ from the voltage across the inductor just before the switch is thrown, when the current is steady (slide 7).

## A mass on a spring

The model: a mass $m$ on a spring of constant $k$ obeys $m\,d^2x/dt^2 + kx = 0$, so it moves in **simple harmonic motion** (slide 10; course notes, Section 11.5, p. 23; for springs, see [8.01](/courses/8-01/tension-and-springs)):

<Formula label="Simple harmonic motion" tex="x(t) = x_0\cos(\omega_0 t + \varphi), \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (11.5.16)–(11.5.17), p. 23" />

with amplitude $x_0$ and phase $\varphi$. Its energy goes back and forth between the kinetic energy of the mass and the potential energy of the spring, with the total $\frac12 kx_0^2$ fixed (slide 11; Eq. 11.5.18, p. 23). The period is $T = 2\pi/\omega_0$ and the frequency $f = 1/T$ (slide 12).

## The LC circuit

The analogy (slides 13–15): a mass does not like to accelerate, and an inductor does not like its current to change ($L\,dI/dt$ is like $m\,dv/dt$, with energy $\frac12 LI^2$ like $\frac12 mv^2$); a spring does not like to be stretched, and a capacitor does not like to be charged ($Q/C$ is like $kx$, with energy $Q^2/2C$ like $\frac12 kx^2$).

Charge a capacitor, then connect it across an inductor (slide 16). With $I = -dQ/dt$, the current flowing off the positive plate, the [modified loop rule](/courses/8-02/inductance-and-lr-circuits) gives $Q/C - L\,dI/dt = 0$ (course notes, Section 11.5, pp. 20–21; slide 19):

<Formula label="LC circuit" tex="\frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + \frac{1}{LC}\,Q = 0, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (11.5.3) and (11.5.6), p. 21" />

the equation of the spring. Starting with charge $Q_0$ and no current (p. 21):

<Formula label="Charge and current" tex="Q(t) = Q_0\cos\omega_0 t, \qquad I(t) = -\frac{dQ}{dt} = \omega_0 Q_0\sin\omega_0 t" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (11.5.8)–(11.5.9), p. 21" />

The charge and current are a quarter cycle out of step: the current is largest when the capacitor is empty (slides 17, 20).

**Energy.** The electric energy $Q^2/2C = (Q_0^2/2C)\cos^2\omega_0 t$ and the magnetic energy $\frac12 LI^2 = (Q_0^2/2C)\sin^2\omega_0 t$ trade places, and their sum is constant (course notes, Section 11.5, p. 22; slide 20):

<Formula label="Energy in an LC circuit" tex="U = \frac{Q^2}{2C} + \frac12 LI^2 = \frac{Q_0^2}{2C}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.5.12), p. 22" />

## Damping: the RLC circuit

A resistor in the loop dissipates energy at the rate $I^2R$, so the oscillation rings down (course notes, Section 11.6, p. 25; slides 21–22). The equation becomes that of a damped oscillator, $m\,d^2x/dt^2 + b\,dx/dt + kx = 0$ (pp. 25–26):

<Formula label="RLC circuit" tex="L\,\frac{d^2Q}{dt^2} + R\,\frac{dQ}{dt} + \frac{Q}{C} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.6.3), p. 25" />

For small $R$ (underdamping) the solution is an oscillation inside a shrinking envelope (pp. 25–26; slide 22):

<Formula label="Damped oscillation" tex="Q(t) = Q_0\,e^{-Rt/2L}\cos(\omega' t + \varphi), \qquad \omega' = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \left(\frac{R}{2L}\right)^2}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (11.6.4)–(11.6.6), pp. 25–26" />

The damping also lowers the frequency slightly. With $R = 0$ it is the LC circuit again (p. 26). This oscillating solution needs $R < 2\sqrt{L/C}$; with more resistance the circuit is critically damped or overdamped and does not oscillate at all (pp. 25–26). The full dictionary between the circuit and the mechanical oscillator: $Q \leftrightarrow x$, $I \leftrightarrow \pm v$ (the sign depends on the convention for $I$), $L \leftrightarrow m$, $R \leftrightarrow b$, $1/C \leftrightarrow k$ (Table 11.6.1, p. 26).

<WorkedExample title="An LC circuit, then some resistance">
<Problem>
A 1 μF capacitor charged to 5 μC is connected to a 10 mH inductor. (a) Find $\omega_0$, the frequency and the largest current. (b) Find the total energy. (c) With 20 Ω added, find the damping rate, the new angular frequency, and the time for the amplitude to fall by a factor $e$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Frequency and current">
$\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC} = 1/\sqrt{(10^{-2})(10^{-6})} = 10^4$ rad/s, so $f = \omega_0/2\pi \approx 1592$ Hz. $I_0 = \omega_0 Q_0 = (10^4)(5 \times 10^{-6}) = 0.05$ A.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Energy">
$Q_0^2/2C = (5 \times 10^{-6})^2/(2 \times 10^{-6}) = 1.25 \times 10^{-5}$ J, equal to $\frac12 LI_0^2 = \frac12(10^{-2})(0.05)^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Damping">
$R/2L = 20/0.02 = 1000$ s⁻¹; $\omega' = \sqrt{10^8 - 10^6} \approx 9950$ rad/s, half a percent lower. The amplitude falls by $e$ in $2L/R = 1$ ms, about 1.6 oscillations.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A real inductor has inductance $L$ and resistance $R_L$.
- LC circuit: $d^2Q/dt^2 = -Q/LC$, oscillating at $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, like a mass on a spring with $L \leftrightarrow m$, $1/C \leftrightarrow k$.
- The energy swings between $Q^2/2C$ and $\frac12 LI^2$, total $Q_0^2/2C$.
- With $R$: $Q = Q_0e^{-Rt/2L}\cos\omega' t$, $\omega' = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - (R/2L)^2}$.
