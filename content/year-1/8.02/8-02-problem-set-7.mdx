## What you need from the lessons

A short piece $d\vec s$ of a wire carrying current $I$ makes, at the relative position $\vec r$, the field given by the Biot–Savart law:

<Formula label="Biot–Savart law" tex="d\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec s \times \hat r}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.1.1), p. 2" />

Added up along a long straight wire, it gives a field that circles the wire, with the size

<Formula label="Field of a long straight wire" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.1.7), p. 5" />

at the distance $r$. Its direction follows from the right-hand rule: thumb along the current, fingers along the field. At the centre of a circular ring of radius $R$ the pieces all add up in the same direction, along the axis (Eq. (9.1.15), p. 8, with $z = 0$):

<Formula label="Field at the centre of a ring" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.1.15) with z = 0, p. 8" />

Ampère's law says that the line integral of $\vec B$ around any closed loop is $\mu_0$ times the steady current through any surface the loop bounds. The sign of that current follows from the right-hand rule: fingers along the direction in which you go round the loop, thumb along the current that counts as positive.

<Formula label="Ampère's law" tex="\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec s = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}" cite="mit" at="p. 1" />

The session's recipe for using it (p. 1, from Section 9.10.2 of the notes): (1) draw an Amperian loop on which $\vec B$ is constant and along the loop, or perpendicular to it, or zero; (2) find the current $I_{\text{enc}}$ through it; (3) work out the line integral; (4) set the two equal and solve for $B$.

A flat loop of $N$ turns with area $A$ carrying current $I$ is a magnetic dipole with the moment $\vec\mu = NI\vec A$, its direction given by the right-hand rule with the fingers along the current:

<Formula label="Magnetic dipole moment" tex="\vec\mu = NI\vec A" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (8.4.8), p. 8" />

Far from the dipole, at the position $\vec r$, its field is

<Formula label="Field of a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}\left[3(\vec\mu \cdot \hat r)\,\hat r - \vec\mu\right]" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.5.5), p. 24" />

In a field that changes from place to place, a dipole feels a force:

<Formula label="Force on a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec F_B = \nabla\left(\vec\mu \cdot \vec B\right)" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (8.4.15), p. 10" />

so a dipole that points along the field is pulled towards stronger field. A piece of magnetized material is full of aligned atomic dipoles. Its **magnetization** $M$ is the dipole moment per unit volume (Eq. (9.6.1), p. 28).

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A hollow cylindrical conductor" points={30} mit="Example 1">

A long straight copper tube has a hollow centre. Its inner wall has the radius $a$ and its outer wall the radius $b$, so the copper is $b - a$ thick. A steady current $I$ flows along the tube, spread evenly over the cross-section of the copper (the ring between $a$ and $b$). Nothing flows in the hollow centre. Let $r$ be the distance from the axis of the tube.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

What is the current density $J$, the current per unit of area, in the copper? Give it in terms of $I$, $a$ and $b$.

<Hint>

The current is spread evenly, so $J$ is the current divided by the area that carries it. Which area is that?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$J =$" expr="I/(pi*(b^2 - a^2))"
  vars={{ I: [1, 10], a: [1, 2], b: [3, 4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "I/(pi*b^2)", hint: "That spreads the current over the whole disc of radius b. The hollow centre carries none: leave its area out." },
    { expr: "I/(pi*(b - a)^2)", hint: "The copper is a ring between two circles. Its area is the difference of the two circle areas, which is not the area of a circle of radius b - a." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For an even spread, current density is current over area.

<Step title="The area of the copper">

The copper fills the ring between the circles of radius $a$ and $b$, so its area is $\pi b^2 - \pi a^2$. Then

<Formula label="Current density in the copper" tex="J = \frac{I}{\pi\,(b^2 - a^2)}" cite="mit" at="Question 1, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$J$ is a current over an area, in A/m². For a thin tube, with $b - a$ small, the area is close to the circumference $2\pi b$ times the thickness $b - a$, as it should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Take as Amperian loop a circle of radius $r$ around the axis, with $a < r < b$. What current $I_{\text{enc}}$ passes through it? Give it in terms of $I$, $r$, $a$ and $b$.

<Hint>

Only the part of the circle's area in which $J$ is not zero carries current. Which part is that?

</Hint>

<Hint>

That part is the ring between $a$ and $r$. Multiply its area by $J$ from part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$I_{\text{enc}} =$" expr="I*(r^2 - a^2)/(b^2 - a^2)"
  vars={{ I: [1, 10], a: [1, 2], b: [4, 5], r: [2.5, 3.5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "I*r^2/b^2", hint: "That is the share of a solid wire of radius b. The circle of radius r also takes in the hollow centre, which carries no current." },
    { expr: "I*(r - a)/(b - a)", hint: "The current is spread evenly over area, not over the radius. The area of a ring grows as the difference of the squares of its radii." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The enclosed current is $J$ times the part of the enclosed area that carries current.

<Step title="The enclosed current">

The circle of radius $r$ takes in the hollow centre (no current) and the copper ring from $a$ to $r$, of area $\pi(r^2 - a^2)$. So

<Formula label="Current enclosed for a < r < b" tex="I_{\text{enc}} = J\,\pi\,(r^2 - a^2) = I\,\frac{r^2 - a^2}{b^2 - a^2}" cite="mit" at="Question 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check (MIT's Question 3)">

At $r = a$ it is zero: the circle then has no copper inside it. At $r = b$ it is $I$: the circle then holds all of the copper and so all of the current.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Find the size of the magnetic field $B$ in the copper, $a < r < b$, in terms of $\mu_0$, $I$, $r$, $a$ and $b$.

<Hint>

By symmetry the field lines are circles around the axis, and $B$ is the same all the way round the Amperian circle. What does that make the line integral?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The line integral is $B$ times the length of the circle. Set it equal to $\mu_0$ times your answer to part (b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$B =$" expr="mu0*I*(r^2 - a^2)/(2*pi*r*(b^2 - a^2))"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], I: [1, 10], a: [1, 2], b: [4, 5], r: [2.5, 3.5] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*I/(2*pi*r)", hint: "That uses the whole current I. A circle inside the copper encloses only part of it." },
    { expr: "mu0*I*(r^2 - a^2)/(pi*r*(b^2 - a^2))", hint: "Check the length of the Amperian loop: a circle of radius r is 2 pi r long." },
    { expr: "mu0*I*r/(2*pi*b^2)", hint: "That is the field inside a solid wire of radius b. Here the centre is hollow, so less current is enclosed." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Steps 3 and 4 of the recipe: the line integral around the circle, set equal to $\mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}$.

<Step title="The line integral (MIT's Question 4)">

The current runs along the axis, so by the right-hand rule the field circles the axis. Turning the tube about its axis, or sliding it along, changes nothing, so $B$ depends on $r$ only. On the Amperian circle $\vec B$ is therefore along $d\vec s$ and of constant size, and

<Formula label="Line integral around a circle" tex="\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec s = B\,(2\pi r)" cite="mit" at="Question 4, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The field (MIT's Question 5)">

Ampère's law with part (b) gives $B\,(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I\,(r^2 - a^2)/(b^2 - a^2)$, so

<Formula label="Field in the copper" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\,\frac{r^2 - a^2}{b^2 - a^2}" cite="mit" at="Question 5, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

(MIT's Question 5 says to use "your answer in Question 3"; it means the enclosed current of Question 2.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $r = b$ this is $\mu_0 I/2\pi b$, the field of a thin wire carrying $I$. If the hole shrinks to nothing ($a \to 0$), it becomes $\mu_0 I r/2\pi b^2$, the field inside a solid wire.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

What is the magnetic field in the hollow centre, $r < a$?

<Hint>

Use a circle of radius $r < a$ as Amperian loop. How much current passes through it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="In the hollow centre the field is"
  choices={[
    "zero everywhere",
    "constant and not zero, pointing along the axis",
    "growing in proportion to r, as inside a solid wire",
    "largest on the axis and falling off towards the copper"
  ]}
  answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same Amperian circle, now with no current inside it.

<Step title="Ampère's law in the hole (MIT's Question 6)">

A circle of radius $r < a$ encloses no current, so $B\,(2\pi r) = 0$ and $B = 0$ everywhere in the hollow centre. The symmetry argument of part (c) still holds there, so a circling field of some other size is ruled out. A field along the axis is ruled out as well: the Biot–Savart law gives every current element of a current along the axis a field that circles the axis.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={4}>

Find the field $B$ outside the tube, $r > b$, in terms of $\mu_0$, $I$ and $r$.

<Hint>

A circle with $r > b$ encloses all the copper. The notes suggest picturing the whole tube as a single thin wire.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e" label="$B =$" expr="mu0*I/(2*pi*r)"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], I: [1, 10], r: [6, 10] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*I/(4*pi*r)", hint: "The line integral is B times 2 pi r; the factor 4 pi belongs to the Biot–Savart law for one short piece." },
    { expr: "mu0*I/(pi*r)", hint: "Check the length of the Amperian loop: a circle of radius r is 2 pi r long." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Outside, the circle encloses the whole current, whatever its spread.

<Step title="The field outside (MIT's Question 7)">

$I_{\text{enc}} = I$, so $B\,(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I$ and

<Formula label="Field outside the tube" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}" cite="mit" at="Question 7, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

the same as for a thin wire along the axis. The field circles the tube, in the sense given by the right-hand rule with the thumb along the current.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="f" points={4}>

Sketch $B(r)$ from $r = 0$ to $r = 4b$. Is $B$ continuous at $r = a$ and at $r = b$? What about the slope of the curve? (The sketch is MIT's Question 8; the questions about continuity are part (d) of the notes' Additional Problem 9.13.2.)

<Hint>

Put $r = a$ and $r = b$ into your formulas for the neighbouring regions and compare. For the slope, compare the derivatives with respect to $r$ on both sides.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-f" label="At $r = a$ and $r = b$:"
  choices={[
    "B jumps at both radii, where the copper begins and ends",
    "B is continuous at both radii; its slope jumps at both",
    "B and its slope are both continuous at both radii",
    "B is continuous at r = b but jumps at r = a"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compare the formulas of the three regions where they meet.

<Step title="The value">

At $r = a$ the hollow centre gives 0 and the formula of part (c) gives $0$ too. At $r = b$ part (c) gives $\mu_0 I/2\pi b$, and so does part (e). So $B$ is continuous at both radii. That had to be: the enclosed current changes smoothly with $r$, because the copper carries no current concentrated on a surface.

</Step>

<Step title="The slope">

Write the field in the copper as $B = k\,(r - a^2/r)$ with $k = \mu_0 I/[2\pi(b^2 - a^2)]$. Its slope is $k\,(1 + a^2/r^2)$: at $r = a$ that is $2k$, while the hollow centre has slope 0. At $r = b$ the slope inside the copper is $k\,(1 + a^2/b^2)$, which is positive, while just outside the slope of $\mu_0 I/2\pi r$ is $-\mu_0 I/2\pi b^2$, which is negative. So the slope jumps at both radii: the graph has corners there.

</Step>

<Step title="The sketch">

<Plot variable="u" domain={[0, 4]} range={[0, 1.15]} xLabel="r / b" yLabel="B"
  curves={[{ expr: "u < 0.5 ? 0 : (u <= 1 ? (u^2 - 0.25)/(0.75*u) : 1/u)", label: "a = b/2" }]}
  caption="The field of the tube with a = b/2, in units of μ₀I/2πb, computed from the formulas of parts (c) to (e): zero in the hole, rising through the copper to its largest value at the outer wall, then falling as 1/r." />

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="g" points={4}>

Now a very thin wire is added along the axis of the tube. It carries the same current $I$, but in the opposite direction. Find the size of the field in the copper, $a < r < b$, in terms of $\mu_0$, $I$, $r$, $a$ and $b$. (Part (e) of the notes' Additional Problem 9.13.2 asks only for a rough sketch.)

<Hint>

Fields add as vectors. Both the tube and the wire make fields that circle the axis. Do the two fields point the same way round, or opposite ways?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Or use Ampère's law again: what is the net current through a circle of radius $r$ in the copper now?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-g" label="$B =$" expr="mu0*I*(b^2 - r^2)/(2*pi*r*(b^2 - a^2))"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], I: [1, 10], a: [1, 2], b: [4, 5], r: [2.2, 2.8] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*I*(r^2 - a^2)/(2*pi*r*(b^2 - a^2))", hint: "That is the field of the tube alone. The wire on the axis passes through every Amperian circle too." },
    { expr: "mu0*I*(r^2 - a^2)/(2*pi*r*(b^2 - a^2)) + mu0*I/(2*pi*r)", hint: "The two currents run in opposite directions, so their fields circle the axis in opposite senses: subtract, do not add." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Superposition: the field of the tube plus the field of the wire. Ampère's law with the net enclosed current gives the same thing in one step.

<Step title="The net current">

A circle of radius $r$ in the copper encloses $I\,(r^2 - a^2)/(b^2 - a^2)$ of the tube's current one way and all of the wire's current $I$ the other way. The net current is

<Formula label="Net current with the wire on the axis" tex="I_{\text{enc}} = I\,\frac{r^2 - a^2}{b^2 - a^2} - I = -I\,\frac{b^2 - r^2}{b^2 - a^2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 9.13.2(e), p. 63 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The field">

So the field circles in the sense of the wire's current, with the size

<Formula label="Field in the copper with the wire" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\,\frac{b^2 - r^2}{b^2 - a^2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 9.13.2(e), p. 63 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The whole picture">

In the hollow centre only the wire counts: $B = \mu_0 I/2\pi r$, very large near the wire. In the copper the field falls from that same value at $r = a$ to zero at $r = b$. Outside, the net current is zero and so is the field: the arrangement is a coaxial cable, which keeps its field inside.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A thick slab of current" points={25} mit="Example 2">

A slab of conductor is infinite in the $x$ and $z$ directions and has the thickness $d$ in the $y$ direction, from $y = -d/2$ to $y = +d/2$. A steady current flows through it along $+z$, with the same current density $J$ (in A/m²) everywhere in the slab. Seen with the $z$ axis pointing out of the page, $x$ points to the right and $y$ up. There is no current outside the slab.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the magnetic field at the centre plane of the slab, $y = 0$? (MIT's Question 9 says "the exact center of the loop"; it means the centre of the slab.)

<Hint>

Pair each thin layer of current above the centre plane with its mirror image the same distance below. What do their fields do at $y = 0$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="At $y = 0$ the field is"
  choices={[
    "largest, because the point is surrounded by current on both sides",
    "along y, pointing away from the slab's centre",
    "zero, because the fields of the upper and lower halves cancel",
    "along z, parallel to the current"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Symmetry: the slab looks the same from above and from below.

<Step title="Pairs of layers">

A thin sheet of current along $+z$ makes a field along $-x$ above it and along $+x$ below it (right-hand rule, thumb along $z$). The centre plane lies below every layer of the upper half and above every layer of the lower half. A layer in the upper half and its mirror image in the lower half carry the same current at the same distance, so at $y = 0$ their fields are equal and opposite. All layers pair off, and $\vec B = 0$ at the centre plane.

</Step>

<Step title="Why not along y or z">

By the Biot–Savart law, $d\vec s \times \hat r$ is perpendicular to the current, so no part of the field points along $z$. A $y$ part would cancel between the strips of current to the left and to the right of a point, which are alike. So wherever it is not zero, the field is along $x$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

To find the field above the slab, $y > d/2$, which Amperian loop should you take?

<Hint>

You want a loop on which $\vec B$ is either along the loop with a known size, or perpendicular to it. The field is along $x$ and depends on $y$ only.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="The useful loop is"
  choices={[
    "a circle of radius y in the x–y plane, centred on the origin",
    "a rectangle with sides ℓ along x and z, lying flat in the plane at height y",
    "a rectangle with sides ℓ along y and z, standing in the y–z plane",
    "a rectangle with sides ℓ along x and y, from y = 0 up to height y"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The loop must cut through the slab, so that it encloses current, and run along $\vec B$ where $|B|$ is the same, or where $\vec B$ is known.

<Step title="The rectangle (MIT's Question 10)">

Take the rectangle in the $x$–$y$ plane with two sides of length $\ell$ along $x$, the bottom one on the centre plane $y = 0$ and the top one at height $y$, joined by sides along $y$. This is the loop MIT's hint points to: on the bottom side $\vec B = 0$ by part (a), so it adds nothing; along the top side $\vec B$ is along the loop and constant; on the sides along $y$, $\vec B$ is perpendicular to the loop and adds nothing. The current, along $z$, passes through the part of the rectangle that lies inside the slab.

</Step>

<Step title="Why not the others">

On a circle the field is neither along the loop nor of the same size everywhere. A rectangle lying flat at height $y$, or one in the $y$–$z$ plane, has no current passing through it, since the current runs along it rather than through it. Ampère's law then reads $0 = 0$ and tells you nothing about $B$. A rectangle from $-y$ to $+y$ works too: it encloses the whole thickness of the slab, and both of its sides along $x$ contribute, with the mirror symmetry of part (a). The one from $y = 0$ needs only the result of part (a).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

What current $I_{\text{enc}}$ passes through your loop of part (b), for $y > d/2$, if its sides along $x$ have the length $\ell$? Give it in terms of $J$, $\ell$ and $d$.

<Hint>

The current passes through the part of the rectangle that lies inside the slab. How large is that area?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$I_{\text{enc}} =$" expr="J*l*d/2"
  vars={{ J: [1, 5], l: [1.5, 3], d: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "J*l*d", hint: "That is the current through a rectangle from -y to +y, which cuts through the whole slab. The rectangle of part (b) starts at y = 0, so it cuts only through the upper half, of thickness d/2." },
    { expr: "J*d/2", hint: "A current is a current density times an area. The area inside the slab is the length of the rectangle times the thickness it cuts through." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Current density times the area through which it flows.

<Step title="The enclosed current (MIT's Question 11)">

The rectangle cuts through the upper half of the slab, from $y = 0$ to $y = d/2$, over a strip of length $\ell$ and height $d/2$; above the slab there is no current. So $I_{\text{enc}} = J\,\ell\,d/2$, out of the page.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

Find the $x$ component $B_x$ of the field above the slab, $y > d/2$, with its sign, in terms of $\mu_0$, $J$ and $d$.

<Hint>

Go round the rectangle counterclockwise, as seen with $z$ out of the page; then the current out of the page counts as positive. On the top side you move along $-x$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The bottom side lies on the centre plane, where the field is zero by part (a). So only the top side contributes.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="$B_x =$" expr="-mu0*J*d/2"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], J: [1, 5], d: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0" }}
  mistakes={[

<Solution>

**The idea.** Steps 3 and 4 of the recipe for the rectangle of part (b).

<Step title="The line integral (MIT's Question 12)">

Counterclockwise, the top side runs along $-x$ and contributes $-B_x(y)\,\ell$. The bottom side lies on $y = 0$, where $\vec B = 0$, and on the sides along $y$ the field is perpendicular to the loop. So

<Formula label="Line integral around the rectangle" tex="\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec s = -B_x(y)\,\ell" cite="mit" at="Question 12, p. 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The field (MIT's Question 13)">

Ampère's law with $I_{\text{enc}} = J\,\ell\,d/2$ gives $-B_x\,\ell = \mu_0 J\,\ell\,d/2$, so

<Formula label="Field above the slab" tex="B_x = -\frac{\mu_0 J d}{2} \qquad (y > d/2)" cite="mit" at="Question 13, p. 6 (our own solution)" />

Below the slab, by the mirror symmetry of part (a), $B_x = +\mu_0 J d/2$. A rectangle from $-y$ to $y$ gives the same result: twice the line integral, $-2B_x\,\ell$, and twice the current, $J\,\ell\,d$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The field does not depend on how far from the slab you are, just as the electric field of a large charged plane does not. It agrees with the field of the current sheet in the lesson on Ampère's law. The direction matches the right-hand rule: above a current out of the page, the field points to the left.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={5}>

Find $B_x$ inside the slab, for $0 < y < d/2$, in terms of $\mu_0$, $J$ and $y$.

<Hint>

You know the field at $y = 0$. Use the same kind of rectangle as in part (b), with its top side now inside the slab.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-e" label="$B_x =$" expr="-mu0*J*y"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], J: [1, 5], y: [0.1, 0.4], d: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*J*y", hint: "The size is right but the sign is not. Inside the slab, above the centre plane, more current flows below the point than above it, so the field points to the left, along -x." },
    { expr: "-mu0*J*d/2", hint: "Inside the slab the loop encloses only part of the current: the field there grows with y." },
    { expr: "-mu0*J*y/2", hint: "A rectangle from -y to y encloses the thickness 2y, not y. A rectangle from 0 to y has only one side that contributes, because B = 0 on the centre plane." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same recipe with a smaller rectangle, using $B = 0$ at the centre plane.

<Step title="The loop and the current (MIT's Questions 14 and 15)">

Take a rectangle of length $\ell$ along $x$ with its bottom side on the plane $y = 0$ and its top side at height $y < d/2$. It encloses the current $J\,\ell\,y$.

</Step>

<Step title="The line integral and the field (MIT's Questions 16 and 17)">

Counterclockwise, only the top side contributes, $-B_x(y)\,\ell$, because $B = 0$ on the bottom side. So $-B_x\,\ell = \mu_0 J\,\ell\,y$ and

<Formula label="Field inside the slab" tex="B_x = -\mu_0 J y \qquad (0 < y < d/2)" cite="mit" at="Question 17, p. 7 (our own solution)" />

The same field follows from a rectangle reaching from $-y$ to $y$: twice the line integral and twice the current.

</Step>

<Step title="Check and graph (MIT's Question 18)">

At $y = d/2$ this gives $-\mu_0 J d/2$, the value outside: the field is continuous at the surface. It grows in proportion to $y$ inside, like the field inside a solid wire.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="f" points={4}>

(Our addition.) A copper bar 5.0 mm thick and very wide carries the current density $J = 2.0 \times 10^6$ A/m². Treat it as an infinite slab. How large is the field just outside it, in mT?

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Four long wires at the corners of a square" points={13}>

This problem is the notes' Additional Problem 9.13.6 (Chapter 9, p. 64), in our own words.

Four long, straight, parallel wires each carry the same current $I$. Seen end-on, they sit at the corners of a square of side $a$: wire A at the bottom right, B at the bottom left, C at the top left and D at the top right. The currents in A and D flow out of the page, those in B and C into it. The point P is the centre of the square.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Find the size of the magnetic field at P, in terms of $\mu_0$, $I$ and $a$.

<Hint>

Each wire is at the same distance from P. How far is a corner of a square from its centre?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Draw each wire's field at P: perpendicular to the line from the wire to P, in the sense of the right-hand rule. Then add the four arrows by components.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$B =$" expr="2*mu0*I/(pi*a)"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], I: [1, 10], a: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "0", hint: "Only diagonally opposite wires with the same current cancel at the centre. Here A and C carry opposite currents, and so do B and D." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2)*mu0*I/(pi*a)", hint: "Check the distance from a corner to the centre: it is half the diagonal, a/√2, not a." },
    { expr: "2*sqrt(2)*mu0*I/(pi*a)", hint: "That adds the four sizes as if all four fields pointed the same way. They do not: add them by components." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Superposition of four straight-wire fields, each from the Biot–Savart law.

<Step title="One wire">

Each wire is half a diagonal, $a/\sqrt2$, from P, so each makes a field of size

<Formula label="Field of one wire at the centre" tex="B_1 = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi\,(a/\sqrt2)} = \frac{\sqrt2\,\mu_0 I}{2\pi a}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 9.13.6, p. 64 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The directions">

Put P at the origin with $x$ to the right and $y$ up. Wire A, at the bottom right with current out of the page, makes at P a field pointing to the lower left, along $(-1, -1)/\sqrt2$. In the same way, D (top right, out) gives $(1, -1)/\sqrt2$, B (bottom left, into) gives $(1, -1)/\sqrt2$ and C (top left, into) gives $(-1, -1)/\sqrt2$. The $x$ parts cancel and the $y$ parts all point down.

</Step>

<Step title="The sum">

<Formula label="Field at the centre of the square" tex="B = 4 \cdot \frac{\sqrt2\,\mu_0 I}{2\pi a} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt2} = \frac{2\mu_0 I}{\pi a}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 9.13.6, p. 64 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The two wires A and B form a pair with opposite currents, and so do D and C. Above the midpoint of two opposite currents their fields add along $y$, so no cancellation is expected. The result has the form $\mu_0 I$ over a length, as the field of a wire does.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Which way does the field at P point?

<Hint>

Look at the right-hand pair A and D first: both carry current out of the page. Which way does each one's field point at a point to its left?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="The field at P points"

    "to the right, from side BC towards side AD",
    "upwards, from side AB towards side CD",
    "downwards, from side CD towards side AB",
    "along the diagonal from B towards D"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The components found in part (a).

<Step title="The direction">

The sum points along $-y$: downwards, parallel to the sides DA and CB, from side CD towards side AB. A quick check: to the left of a current out of the page (A and D) the field points down, and to the right of a current into the page (B and C) it points down as well.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A spinning charged disc" points={14}>

This problem builds on the notes' Additional Problem 9.13.5 (Chapter 9, p. 64), in our own words; parts (b) and (c) are our additions.

A thin, flat disc of radius $R$ carries the charge $\sigma$ per unit of area, spread evenly over one face. It spins about its axis with the angular speed $\omega$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Show that the magnetic field at the centre of the disc is $B = \tfrac12\,\mu_0\,\sigma\,\omega\,R$.

<Hint>

Cut the disc into thin rings of radius $r$ and width $dr$. A ring carries the charge $\sigma\,2\pi r\,dr$, which goes round once every $2\pi/\omega$ seconds. What current is that?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each ring is a circular current, with the field $\mu_0\,dI/2r$ at its centre, along the axis. All rings give fields in the same direction: add them up.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A spinning charge is a current. Cut the disc into rings, each a circular current, and add their fields at the centre with the Biot–Savart result for a ring.

<Step title="The current of one ring">

A ring between $r$ and $r + dr$ has the area $2\pi r\,dr$ and so the charge $\sigma\,2\pi r\,dr$. All of it passes a fixed point once per turn, in the time $2\pi/\omega$, so

<Formula label="Current of a ring of the disc" tex="dI = \frac{\omega}{2\pi}\,\sigma\,2\pi r\,dr = \omega\,\sigma\,r\,dr" cite="reading1" at="Problem 9.13.5, hint, p. 64" />

</Step>

<Step title="Its field and the sum">

At its centre the ring makes $dB = \mu_0\,dI/2r = \tfrac12\mu_0\,\omega\,\sigma\,dr$, along the axis. The factor $r$ has dropped out, so every ring contributes the same per unit of width, and

<Formula label="Field at the centre of the spinning disc" tex="B = \int_0^R \frac{\mu_0\,\omega\,\sigma}{2}\,dr = \frac12\,\mu_0\,\sigma\,\omega\,R" cite="reading1" at="Problem 9.13.5, p. 64" />

For positive $\sigma$ it points along the axis in the sense given by the right-hand rule with the fingers along the spin.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$\sigma\omega R$ is a charge per area times a speed, which is a current per length (A/m), and $\mu_0$ times a current per length is a field, as for the field inside an ideal solenoid, which is $\mu_0$ times the current per unit of length.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

(Our addition.) A plastic disc of radius 10 cm carries $\sigma = 2.0\ \mu\text{C/m}^2$ and spins at 3000 revolutions per minute. How large is the field at its centre, in picotesla ($10^{-12}$ T)?

<Hint>

Convert the spin to an angular speed in rad/s first: one revolution is $2\pi$ radians.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$B =$" value="39.5" unit="pT" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-disc-field" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Numbers in the result of part (a).

<Step title="The angular speed">

3000 revolutions per minute is 50 per second, so $\omega = 2\pi \times 50 = 314$ rad/s.

</Step>

<Step title="The field">

$B = \tfrac12 (4\pi \times 10^{-7})(2.0 \times 10^{-6})(314)(0.10) = 3.95 \times 10^{-11}$ T, so 39.5 pT. That is more than a million times weaker than the Earth's field: the charges on everyday objects move far too slowly to make strong magnetic fields.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

(Our addition.) The spinning disc is also a magnetic dipole. Find the size of its magnetic moment $\mu$, in terms of $\sigma$, $\omega$ and $R$.

<Hint>

Each ring is a loop of area $\pi r^2$ carrying the current $dI$ of part (a). Its moment is current times area.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c" label="$\mu =$" expr="pi*sigma*omega*R^4/4"
  vars={{ sigma: [1, 3], omega: [1, 5], R: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ sigma: "\\sigma", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi*sigma*omega*R^4/2", hint: "This treats all the charge as if it went round at the rim. Charge closer to the centre circles a smaller area, so it adds less to the moment." },
    { expr: "sigma*omega*R^2/2", hint: "That is the total current of the disc. A moment is a current times the area it goes round." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Add up the moments of the rings, each $dI$ times its area.

<Step title="The sum">

<Formula label="Magnetic moment of the spinning disc" tex="\mu = \int_0^R \pi r^2\,\omega\,\sigma\,r\,dr = \frac{\pi\,\sigma\,\omega\,R^4}{4}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (8.4.8), p. 8, applied ring by ring (our own solution)" />

It points along the field at the centre.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The total current of the disc is $\int_0^R \omega\sigma r\,dr = \sigma\omega R^2/2$. If all of it went round the rim, the moment would be $\pi R^2$ times that, $\pi\sigma\omega R^4/2$. Ours is half of that, as it should be: most of the charge sits away from the rim and circles a smaller area.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="The field of a bar magnet and its pull on another" points={18}>

This problem builds on the notes' Additional Problem 9.13.11 (Chapter 9, p. 67), in our own words; parts (c) and (d) are our additions.

A permanent magnet is a cylindrical rod 4.8 cm long and 1.1 cm in diameter. It has a uniform magnetization of 5300 A/m along its axis.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the magnetic dipole moment of the magnet, in A m².

<Hint>

The magnetization is the dipole moment per unit volume. What is the volume of the rod?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$\mu =$" value="0.02418" unit="A m²" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-moment" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Moment = magnetization × volume.

<Step title="The volume">

The radius is 0.55 cm, so the volume is $\pi (0.0055)^2 (0.048) = 4.56 \times 10^{-6}$ m³.

</Step>

<Step title="The moment">

$\mu = MV = 5300 \times 4.56 \times 10^{-6} = 2.42 \times 10^{-2}$ A m², along the axis of the rod. (The notes give the same value.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the field on the axis of the magnet, 1 m from its centre, in nanotesla ($10^{-9}$ T).

<Hint>

At 1 m the magnet is tiny compared with the distance: use the dipole field. On the axis, $\hat r$ is along $\vec\mu$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$B =$" value="4.84" unit="nT" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-axis-field" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The far field of a dipole, on its axis.

<Step title="The field on the axis">

With $\hat r$ along $\vec\mu$, $3(\vec\mu \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec\mu = 2\vec\mu$, so on the axis at the distance $z$

<Formula label="Dipole field on its axis" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0\,\mu}{2\pi z^3}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.5.5), p. 24, on the axis (our own step)" />

the same as the ring field $\mu_0 I R^2/2z^3$ far along the axis, with $\mu = I\pi R^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

$B = (2 \times 10^{-7})(2.418 \times 10^{-2})/1^3 = 4.84 \times 10^{-9}$ T, so 4.84 nT, about ten thousand times weaker than the Earth's field. (The notes give $4.8 \times 10^{-9}$ T.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

(Our addition.) A second magnet with the same moment $\mu$ is placed on the axis of the first, at the distance $z$ from it, and points the same way (its south pole facing the first magnet's north pole). Take the first magnet at the origin with its moment along $+z$. Find the $z$ component $F_z$ of the force on the second magnet, with its sign, in terms of $\mu_0$, $\mu$ and $z$. Treat both as point dipoles.

<Hint>

The force on a dipole along a field that changes along $z$ is $\mu$ times the rate of change of that field, $F_z = \mu\,dB_z/dz$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiate the field of part (b) with respect to $z$. Does it grow or shrink with $z$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-c" label="$F_z =$" expr="-3*mu0*mu^2/(2*pi*z^4)"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], mu: [1, 3], z: [3.5, 6] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", mu: "\\mu" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*mu0*mu^2/(2*pi*z^4)", hint: "The size is right but the sign is not. The field weakens as z grows, so a dipole along the field is pulled back towards smaller z, towards the first magnet." },
    { expr: "-mu0*mu^2/(2*pi*z^4)", hint: "The derivative of 1/z^3 is -3/z^4: a factor 3 is missing." },
    { expr: "-mu0*mu^2/(2*pi*z^3)", hint: "That is the energy U = -mu B, not the force. The force is minus the rate of change of U with position." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** A dipole aligned with a non-uniform field is pulled towards stronger field, with the force $\nabla(\vec\mu \cdot \vec B)$.

<Step title="The force">

On the axis $\vec\mu \cdot \vec B = \mu\,B_z(z)$ with $B_z = \mu_0\mu/2\pi z^3$, so

The minus sign means the second magnet is pulled towards the first: the two magnets attract.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

For two magnets like that of part (a), 1 m apart, $|F_z| = 3 \times (2 \times 10^{-7}) \times (2.418 \times 10^{-2})^2 = 3.51 \times 10^{-10}$ N. </Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

(Our addition.) The second magnet is turned round, so that its moment points along $-z$. What happens to the force?

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-d" label="The force"
  choices={[
    "vanishes, because the two moments now cancel",
    "reverses: the magnets push apart, with the same size of force",

    "is replaced by a torque that turns the second magnet round"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The force is linear in the moment that feels it.

<Step title="The reversed magnet">

With $\vec\mu$ along $-z$, $\vec\mu \cdot \vec B = -\mu B_z$, so $F_z = -\mu\,dB_z/dz = +3\mu_0\mu^2/2\pi z^4$: the same size, pointing away from the first magnet. Like poles now face each other and repel. On the axis the moment is exactly opposite to the field, so the torque $\vec\mu \times \vec B$ is zero (though the position is unstable: a small tilt would grow).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
