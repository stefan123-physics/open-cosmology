## What you need from the lessons

The **electric flux** through a closed surface $S$ adds up $\vec E \cdot d\vec A$ over the surface, with $d\vec A$ pointing outwards. **Gauss's law** says it equals the charge inside, $q_{\text{in}}$, divided by $\varepsilon_0$, whatever the shape of the surface:

<Formula label="Gauss's law" tex="\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec E \cdot d\vec A = \frac{q_{\text{in}}}{\varepsilon_0}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.2.5), p. 4" />

The law always holds, but it gives the field only when the charge has a symmetry (cylindrical, planar or spherical) and the **Gaussian surface** is chosen so that on each piece of it the field is either perpendicular to the surface with one size, or along the surface (no flux). The notes' steps (Section 4.2, pp. 6 and 7): find the symmetry and the direction of $\vec E$, choose the surface, split space into regions, find the enclosed charge and the flux in each, and set them equal.

Once the field is known, the potential difference between two points is minus the line integral of the field between them:

<Formula label="Potential difference" tex="V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \vec E \cdot d\vec s" cite="reading1" at="p. 19" />

In a **conductor** in equilibrium the field is zero inside, any net charge sits on its surfaces, the whole conductor is at one potential, and just outside, the field is perpendicular to the surface with the size set by the local surface charge density $\sigma$ (Section 4.3, pp. 14 to 17):

<Formula label="Field just outside a conductor" tex="E_n = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.3.3), p. 17" />

Building up a charge takes work. When a body holds the charge $q$ and its potential is $V(q)$, bringing a further $dq$ from far away (where $V = 0$) takes the work $dW = V\,dq$ (Section 4.3, pp. 19 and 20):

<Formula label="Work to add a small charge" tex="dW = V\,dq" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.3.6), p. 20" />

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Two coaxial cylinders, step by step" points={30} mit="Problem Solving 3, pp. 1 to 4">

A long solid cylinder of radius $a$ and length $L$, made of an insulator, carries the charge $+Q$ spread evenly through its volume, so its charge density is

<Formula label="Charge density of the inner cylinder" tex="\rho = \frac{Q}{\pi a^2 L}" cite="mit" at="p. 2" />

Around it, on the same axis, sits a very thin insulating cylindrical shell of radius $b > a$ and the same length $L$, carrying the opposite charge $-Q$ spread evenly over it. The space between them, $a < r < b$, is empty; $r$ is the distance from the common axis. Take $L$ much larger than $b$, so that the ends do not matter and the cylinders can be treated as infinitely long. The session works through the eight steps of its method for Gauss's law (p. 1), and so do the parts below.

(The session's Step 4 asks for a surface on which "the flux integral is either case a) or case b) above", but the sheet does not print those two cases. We take them to be the two kinds of pieces of the lessons: the field perpendicular to the piece with one size, or the field along it.)

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Step 1: what is the symmetry of this charge distribution?

<Answer id="p1-a" label="The symmetry:"
  choices={[
    "spherical: the charge looks the same in every direction from the middle of the inner cylinder",
    "planar: the charge looks the same from every point of a plane through the axis",
    "cylindrical: it looks the same after any turn about the axis or shift along it",
    "none: the two cylinders have opposite charges, so no Gaussian surface can be used"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Ask what you can do to the charges without changing them.

Turning the two cylinders about their common axis, or sliding them along it (they are effectively infinitely long), leaves every charge where a charge of the same size already was. That is cylindrical symmetry. The opposite signs of the two cylinders do not spoil it: each one is symmetric on its own.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Step 2: in which direction does the field point, and on which surfaces does it have one size?

<Answer id="p1-b" label="The field"
  choices={[
    "points along the axis, with one size on every plane across the axis",
    "points straight out from the axis, with one size on each cylinder of radius r about the axis",
    "goes round the axis in circles, with one size on every cylinder of radius r around the axis",
    "points away from the middle of the inner cylinder, with one size on every sphere around that point"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Any other direction would pick out a preferred way round or a preferred end.

A component along the axis would have to point to one end, but the two ends are alike. A component going round the axis would pick a sense of rotation, but turning the charges over (end for end) swaps the two senses and leaves the charges unchanged. So the field is radial, perpendicular to the axis. By the symmetry, its size depends only on $r$: it is the same everywhere on a cylinder of radius $r$ about the axis.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Step 3: into how many regions does the charge distribution divide space? (A whole number.)

<Answer id="p1-c" label="Number of regions:" value="3" tolerance="0.1" calc="p1-regions" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A new region starts wherever the charge enclosed by a Gaussian cylinder starts to follow a different rule.

Inside the solid cylinder, $r < a$, the enclosed charge grows with $r$. Between the two, $a < r < b$, it is all of $+Q$ (per length $L$). Outside both, $r > b$, it is $+Q - Q = 0$. So there are three regions. (The sheet's later steps ask only for the first two; the third comes back in Step 8.)

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Step 4: which Gaussian surface do you choose in each region, and what variable describes it?

<Answer id="p1-d" label="The Gaussian surface:"
  choices={[
    "a sphere of radius r about the middle of the cylinders, with r from 0 to b",
    "a closed cylinder of radius r and length ℓ on the same axis, one for each region",
    "a flat pillbox across the surface of the inner cylinder, with its height as the variable",
    "a closed cylinder of radius a and length L, the same in every region, with ℓ as the variable"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Fit the surface to the symmetry of Step 2.

A closed cylinder of radius $r$ and any length $\ell$, on the same axis, has a curved side on which the field is perpendicular to the surface with one size, and two flat ends along which the field runs (no flux). Its radius $r$ is the variable: $0 < r < a$ in the first region, $a < r < b$ in the second, $r > b$ in the third. The length $\ell$ is free, and it will divide out.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

Step 5: for $r < a$, write the flux out of your Gaussian cylinder (radius $r$, length $\ell$) in terms of the unknown size $E$ of the field there.

<Hint>

Only the curved side counts. What is its area?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e" label="$\Phi_E =$" expr="2*pi*r*l*E"
  vars={{ r: [0.1, 2], l: [0.5, 5], E: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi*r^2*l*E", hint: "That is the field times the volume of the cylinder. The flux is the field times an area: the area of the curved side." },
    { expr: "2*pi*r*l*E + 2*pi*r^2*E", hint: "The field runs along the two flat ends, so they carry no flux." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the curved side $\vec E$ is parallel to $d\vec A$ and has one size; on the ends it is perpendicular to $d\vec A$.

<Formula label="Flux out of the Gaussian cylinder" tex="\Phi_E = \oint \vec E \cdot d\vec A = E \cdot 2\pi r\ell + 0 + 0 = 2\pi r\ell\,E" cite="mit" at="Step 5, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Check: the units are N/C times m², those of a flux.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="f" points={3}>

Step 6: for $r < a$, how much charge does your Gaussian cylinder enclose? Give it in terms of $Q$, $a$, $L$, $r$ and $\ell$.

<Hint>

The density is uniform: the enclosed charge is $\rho$ times the volume inside the Gaussian cylinder.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-f" label="$q_{\text{in}} =$" expr="Q*r^2*l/(a^2*L)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 5], r: [0.1, 1], a: [1, 2], l: [0.5, 5], L: [5, 20] }}
  names={{ l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q*r*l/(a*L)", hint: "The volume of a cylinder grows as the square of its radius, so the enclosed fraction of the cross-section is r²/a², not r/a." },
    { expr: "Q*r^2/a^2", hint: "That would be right for a Gaussian cylinder as long as the charged one. Yours has the length ℓ, so only the fraction ℓ/L of that." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Density times enclosed volume.

<Formula label="Charge inside the Gaussian cylinder" tex="q_{\text{in}} = \rho\,\pi r^2\ell = \frac{Q}{\pi a^2 L}\,\pi r^2 \ell = \frac{Q r^2 \ell}{a^2 L}" cite="mit" at="Step 6, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Check: at $r = a$ and $\ell = L$ this is all of $Q$.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="g" points={3}>

Step 7, first question: set the two sides of Gauss's law equal to find the size of the field for $r < a$.

<Answer id="p1-g" label="$E(r < a) =$" expr="Q*r/(2*pi*eps0*a^2*L)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 5], r: [0.1, 1], eps0: [0.5, 2], a: [1, 2], L: [5, 20] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q/(2*pi*eps0*L*r)", hint: "That is the field outside a line holding all of Q. Inside the solid cylinder your Gaussian surface encloses only part of the charge." },
    { expr: "Q*r/(4*pi*eps0*a^3)", hint: "That is the field inside a uniform ball. Here the Gaussian surface is a cylinder, and its side area is 2πrℓ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Flux from Step 5 equals charge from Step 6 over $\varepsilon_0$.

<Formula label="Field inside the solid cylinder" tex="2\pi r\ell\,E = \frac{Q r^2 \ell}{\varepsilon_0 a^2 L} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E = \frac{Q\,r}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2 L}" cite="mit" at="Step 7, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

pointing away from the axis. Check: $\ell$ has divided out, and the field is zero on the axis, where the symmetry allows no direction for it.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="h" points={3}>

Step 7, second question: do the same for $a < r < b$.

<Answer id="p1-h" label="$E(a < r < b) =$" expr="Q/(2*pi*eps0*L*r)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 5], r: [1, 3], a: [0.5, 1], eps0: [0.5, 2], L: [5, 20] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q/(4*pi*eps0*r^2)", hint: "That is the field of a point charge. Here the flux goes through the side of a cylinder, of area 2πrℓ, so the field falls as 1/r." },
    { expr: "Q*r/(2*pi*eps0*a^2*L)", hint: "That is the formula for inside the solid cylinder. Between the cylinders the enclosed charge stops growing with r: it is all of the inner charge over the length ℓ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Now the Gaussian cylinder encloses the whole charge of the inner cylinder over its length $\ell$, that is $Q\ell/L$, and none of the outer shell's.

<Formula label="Field between the cylinders" tex="2\pi r\ell\,E = \frac{Q\ell}{\varepsilon_0 L} \quad\Rightarrow\quad E = \frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 L\,r}" cite="mit" at="Step 7, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

pointing away from the axis. Check: at $r = a$ it agrees with part (g), $Q/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 a L)$; and it is the field of a line with charge per length $\lambda = Q/L$, as the lesson found.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="i" points={2}>

Step 8: which description fits the graph of $E$ against $r$ over all space? (The field for $r > b$ is part of the question.)

<Hint>

For $r > b$, how much charge does a Gaussian cylinder enclose?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-i" label="The graph of $E(r)$"
  choices={[
    "rises in a straight line up to r = a, falls as 1/r between a and b, and is zero for r > b",
    "is zero inside the solid cylinder, jumps at r = a, then falls as 1/r for all r > a",
    "rises in a straight line up to r = a, falls as 1/r² between a and b, then stays at its value at b for r > b",

**The idea.** Put the three regions together.

For $r > b$ a Gaussian cylinder of length $\ell$ encloses $Q\ell/L - Q\ell/L = 0$, so the flux is zero and, by the symmetry, so is the field. Together with parts (g) and (h):

<Formula label="Field of the two cylinders" tex="E(r) = \frac{Q\,r}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2 L}\ \ (r < a), \qquad \frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 L\,r}\ \ (a < r < b), \qquad 0\ \ (r > b)" cite="mit" at="Step 8, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

The field is continuous at $r = a$ (no surface charge there) and jumps down to zero at $r = b$, where the shell carries the surface charge $-Q/(2\pi b L)$: the jump is $Q/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 b L)$, that surface density over $\varepsilon_0$.

<Plot variable="u" domain={[0, 3]} range={[0, 1.1]} xLabel="r / a" yLabel="E"

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A sphere with a hollow inside" points={6} mit="Additional Problem 4.10.1">

A sphere of radius $2R$, made of an insulator that does not change the field, carries a uniform charge density $\rho$. A spherical hollow of radius $R$ is then cut out of it. The hollow's centre lies a distance $R$ from the centre of the big sphere, on the $+y$ axis, so the hollow passes through the centre of the big sphere and touches its surface at one point. (From the course notes, Chapter 4, Additional Problem 4.10.1, p. 36; the notes show this in a figure, described here in words.) Find the field inside the hollow.

<Hint>

The hollow is not symmetric, so Gauss's law alone does not give the field. But a hollow with no charge is the same as the full sphere plus a small sphere of density $-\rho$ in the place of the hollow.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Inside a uniform ball of density $\rho$, Gauss's law gives $\vec E = \rho\,\vec r/(3\varepsilon_0)$, with $\vec r$ measured from the ball's centre. Add the fields of the two balls at the same point.

</Hint>

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the size of the field inside the hollow.

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$E =$" expr="rho*R/(3*eps0)"
  vars={{ rho: [0.5, 5], R: [0.1, 2], eps0: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ rho: "\\rho", eps0: "\\varepsilon_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*rho*R/(3*eps0)", hint: "That is the field at the surface of the full sphere of radius 2R. Subtract the field of the ball that was cut out." },
    { expr: "rho*R/eps0", hint: "Check the field inside a uniform ball: Gauss's law gives E · 4πr² = ρ (4/3)πr³/ε₀, so E = ρr/(3ε₀)." }
  ]} />

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

What does the field inside the hollow look like?

<Answer id="p2-b" label="Inside the hollow the field"
  choices={[
    "is zero everywhere, as inside any empty hollow",
    "points away from the centre of the hollow and grows with the distance from it",
    "points towards the centre of the big sphere, with a size that grows towards it",
    "is uniform: one size and one direction, along +y"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

</Part>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Superposition: full sphere of density $\rho$ plus a ball of density $-\rho$ filling the hollow.

<Step title="The field inside a uniform ball">

A concentric Gaussian sphere of radius $r$ inside a ball of density $\rho$ encloses $\rho\,\tfrac43\pi r^3$, so $E\,4\pi r^2 = \rho\,\tfrac43\pi r^3/\varepsilon_0$, and the field points away from the centre:

<Formula label="Field inside a uniform ball" tex="\vec E = \frac{\rho}{3\varepsilon_0}\,\vec r" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 4.10.1, p. 36 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Adding the two balls">

Let $\vec r$ be the position of a point in the hollow, measured from the centre of the big sphere, and $\vec d = R\,\hat j$ the position of the hollow's centre. The full sphere gives $\rho\,\vec r/(3\varepsilon_0)$; the ball of density $-\rho$ gives $-\rho\,(\vec r - \vec d)/(3\varepsilon_0)$:

<Formula label="Field in the hollow" tex="\vec E = \frac{\rho}{3\varepsilon_0}\,\vec r - \frac{\rho}{3\varepsilon_0}\left(\vec r - \vec d\right) = \frac{\rho}{3\varepsilon_0}\,\vec d = \frac{\rho R}{3\varepsilon_0}\,\hat j" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 4.10.1, p. 36 (our own solution)" />

The position $\vec r$ has cancelled: the field is the same everywhere in the hollow, of size $\rho R/(3\varepsilon_0)$, along $+y$ (for $\rho > 0$).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At the centre of the big sphere, which is a point on the hollow's edge, the full sphere gives no field and the removed ball (whose centre is a distance $R$ away along $+y$) gives $-\rho\,(-R\hat j)/(3\varepsilon_0) = \rho R\,\hat j/(3\varepsilon_0)$: the same.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A model of a p-n junction" points={12} mit="Additional Problem 4.10.2">

Where a slab of n-type and a slab of p-type semiconductor touch, electrons move across from the n side to the p side. This leaves a layer of positive charge in the n-type material and a layer of negative charge in the p-type material. Model them as two infinite slabs, each of thickness $a$, with the junction in the plane $z = 0$ (from the course notes, Chapter 4, Additional Problem 4.10.2, pp. 36 and 37):

<Formula label="Charge density of the junction" tex="\rho(z) = \begin{cases} +\rho_0 & 0 < z < a \\ -\rho_0 & -a < z < 0 \\ 0 & |z| > a \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 4.10.2, p. 37" />

<Hint>

Each slab alone gives a uniform field outside it, of size $\rho_0 a/(2\varepsilon_0)$. Add the fields of the two slabs, with their directions, above and below both.

</Hint>

<Hint>

<Answer id="p8-a1" label="(i) Charge on the wall of the hollow:" value="2.0" unit="nC" tolerance="2%" calc="p8-inner" />

<Answer id="p8-a2" label="(ii) Charge on the outer surface:" value="3.0" unit="nC" tolerance="2%" calc="p8-outer" />

<Answer id="p8-a3" label="(iii) $E$ just outside:" value="2.70e3" unit="N/C" tolerance="2%" calc="p8-field" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The field in the metal is zero, so a Gaussian surface in the metal encloses no net charge.

<Step title="(i) and (ii) The two surfaces">

A Gaussian surface in the metal around the hollow encloses the point charge and the charge on the hollow's wall, and their sum must be zero: the wall carries $+2.0$ nC. The metal's net charge is $+5.0$ nC, so the outer surface carries $5.0 - 2.0 = 3.0$ nC (in the notes' words, $Q + q$ with $q = -2.0$ nC).

</Step>

<Step title="(iii) The field outside">

The metal shields the outside from the hollow: the field outside depends only on the outer surface, on which the charge spreads evenly over the sphere wherever the point charge sits inside. So outside it is the field of a point charge of 3.0 nC at the centre, and just outside the surface:

<Formula label="Field just outside the metal sphere" tex="E = \frac{k_e\,q_{\text{out}}}{r^2} = \frac{(8.99 \times 10^9)(3.0 \times 10^{-9})}{(0.10)^2} \approx 2.70 \times 10^3\ \text{N/C}" cite="reading1" at="Example 4.5, pp. 17 and 18 (our own problem and solution)" />

Check with $E_n = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$: $\sigma = 3.0 \times 10^{-9}/(4\pi \cdot 0.01) \approx 2.39 \times 10^{-8}$ C/m², and $\sigma/\varepsilon_0 \approx 2.70 \times 10^3$ N/C.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Two metal spheres of radii 2.0 cm and 6.0 cm, very far apart, are joined by a long thin wire. Together they hold 16 nC. Find the charge on the small sphere, in nC, and the ratio $E_1/E_2$ of the field just outside the small sphere to that just outside the large one. Neglect the charge on the wire.

<Hint>

Joined by a wire, the two spheres are one conductor: they have the same potential. Far apart, each one's potential is that of its own charge alone.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-b1" label="Charge on the small sphere:" value="4.0" unit="nC" tolerance="2%" calc="p8-q1" />

<Answer id="p8-b2" label="$E_1/E_2 =$" value="3" tolerance="2%" calc="p8-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Equal potentials give charges in proportion to the radii.

<Step title="The charges">

$k_e q_1/r_1 = k_e q_2/r_2$, so $q_1/q_2 = r_1/r_2 = 1/3$ (notes, Eq. (4.3.9), p. 20). With $q_1 + q_2 = 16$ nC, $q_1 = 4.0$ nC and $q_2 = 12.0$ nC.

</Step>

<Step title="The fields">

<Formula label="Ratio of the surface fields" tex="\frac{E_1}{E_2} = \frac{q_1/r_1^2}{q_2/r_2^2} = \frac{r_2}{r_1} = \frac{6.0}{2.0} = 3" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.3.11), p. 21 (our own numbers)" />

The small sphere has the smaller charge but the stronger field: charge crowds where a conductor is most sharply curved, which is why a lightning rod is pointed. Check: $q_1/r_1^2 = 4.0/4.0 = 1$ and $q_2/r_2^2 = 12/36 = 1/3$ (in nC/cm²), a ratio of 3.

</Step>

<Part label="a" points={2}>

The electric field is zero everywhere in some region of space. Does that mean there is no charge in the region?

<Hint>

Apply Gauss's law to any small closed surface inside the region.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-a" label="Zero field in a region means"
  choices={[
    "no charge of either sign can be anywhere inside it",
    "nothing about its charge: the region may hold any net charge",
    "no net charge in any part of it, though both signs may be present",
    "no net charge in it, but only if the region is inside a metal"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $\vec E = 0$ on every closed surface in the region, the flux through each is zero, so by Gauss's law each encloses zero net charge. The charge density is therefore zero everywhere in the region. That still allows positive and negative charges in equal amounts side by side: the inside of a piece of metal is full of electrons and positive ions, and its field is zero.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

A point charge $q$ is placed inside a hollow, closed metal pipe, which was uncharged (the notes do not say; we assume it). Describe the field outside the pipe.

<Hint>

Use a Gaussian surface inside the metal of the pipe, then think about the charge left on the outer surface.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-b" label="Outside the pipe the field"
  choices={[
    "is zero: the metal shields the outside from the charge inside",
    "is the field of q alone, as if the pipe were not there",
    "is zero if q is at the centre of the pipe, and not zero otherwise",
    "is not zero, and it does not change when q moves inside the pipe"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in Problem 8(a): the wall of the hollow takes $-q$, so the uncharged pipe has $+q$ left on its outer surface. That charge spreads over the outer surface as it would on the pipe with nothing inside (the cavity's charges and $q$ together give no field in the metal or outside), so the field outside is not zero, and it does not depend on where $q$ sits inside. Nor is it the field of $q$ alone: that would change as $q$ moves, and it would ignore the shape of the pipe.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Two isolated metal spheres each carry the same net charge $Q > 0$. Their radii are $a$ and $b$, with $b > a$. Which sphere is at the higher potential (with $V = 0$ far away)?

<Answer id="p9-c" label="The higher potential is on"
  choices={[
    "the sphere of radius b, because it is larger",
    "the sphere of radius a, the smaller one",
    "neither: with equal charges they are at equal potentials",
    "the one nearer to the other, so the radii do not decide it"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** An isolated sphere with charge $Q$ is at the potential $k_e Q/R$ (Example 4.6, p. 19). With the same $Q$, the smaller radius $a$ gives the larger potential, $k_e Q/a > k_e Q/b$. (Joined by a wire, as in Problem 8(b), charge would flow from the small sphere to the large one until the potentials were equal.)

</Solution>
