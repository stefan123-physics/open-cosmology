Session 5 is one long look at **Gauss's law**: the first of Maxwell's equations, and a quick way to find fields when there is symmetry (slides 1–7). It is the physics of the [divergence theorem](/courses/18-02/divergence-theorem) of 18.02. The idea: field lines start and end on charges, so the number of lines crossing any closed surface depends only on the charge inside it (slide 8).

## Electric flux

The **flux** $\Phi_E$ through a surface counts the field lines crossing it (course notes, Section 4.1, p. 1). For a flat surface of area $A$ with unit normal $\hat n$, in a uniform field at angle $\theta$ to $\hat n$ (Section 4.1, p. 1; slides 11–12):

<Formula label="Flux through a flat surface" tex="\Phi_E = \vec E \cdot \vec A = EA\cos\theta = E_n A" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.1.2), p. 1" />

where $E_n$ is the component of $\vec E$ along the normal. In general the field varies and the surface curves: cut it into small pieces $d\vec A = \hat n\,dA$ and add (Section 4.1, p. 2; slide 17):

<Formula label="Flux through a closed surface" tex="\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec E \cdot d\vec A" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.1.4), p. 2" />

A **closed** surface, such as a sphere, encloses a volume; an open one, such as a rectangle, does not (slide 15). On a closed surface $d\vec A$ points **outwards**, so the flux is positive where the field leaves and negative where it enters (Section 4.1, p. 2; slide 16).

## Gauss's law

Put a point charge $Q$ at the centre of a sphere of radius $r$. The field $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$ is radial and the same size everywhere on the sphere, whose area is $4\pi r^2$, so the flux is $Q/\varepsilon_0$, whatever the radius (course notes, Section 4.2, pp. 2–4; slide 21). The same holds for **any** closed surface around the charge (Section 4.2, p. 4; slide 22). With several charges the fluxes add, and a charge outside the surface contributes nothing, since every field line of it that enters also leaves (the page's own remark). So:

<Formula label="Gauss's law" tex="\Phi_E = \oint_S \vec E \cdot d\vec A = \frac{q_{\text{in}}}{\varepsilon_0}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.2.5), p. 4" />

where $q_{\text{in}}$ is the net charge inside $S$.

**Why any surface?** Through the **solid angle**. A small area $\Delta A$ at distance $r$, facing the charge, covers the solid angle $\Delta\Omega = \Delta A/r^2$ (in steradians; a whole sphere is $4\pi$). Tilt it by $\theta$ and the solid angle is $\Delta A\cos\theta/r^2$ (Section 4.2, pp. 4–5). The field falls as $1/r^2$ while the area for a given solid angle grows as $r^2$, so each solid angle carries the same flux, whatever the surface's shape or tilt (Section 4.2, pp. 5–6). Summed over all directions, any closed surface around the charge gets $Q/\varepsilon_0$. This only works because Coulomb's law is exactly inverse-square.

## Using Gauss's law

The law always holds, but gives the field only when symmetry makes $\vec E$ simple on a well-chosen **Gaussian surface** (course notes, Section 4.2, p. 6; slides 23–25):

1. Find the symmetry and the direction of $\vec E$.
2. Choose a closed surface where $\vec E$ is either perpendicular to the surface and constant (flux $\pm EA$) or parallel to it (flux zero).
3. For each region, find the enclosed charge $q_{\text{in}}$.
4. Compute the flux, set it equal to $q_{\text{in}}/\varepsilon_0$, and solve for $E$.

| Symmetry | Example | Gaussian surface |
|---|---|---|
| spherical | ball, spherical shell | concentric sphere |
| cylindrical | infinite rod | coaxial cylinder |
| planar | infinite plane | "pillbox" across the plane |

## Spherical symmetry

**A uniform ball.** Charge $Q$ is spread evenly through a solid sphere of radius $a$ (course notes, Section 4.2, Example 4.4, pp. 13–14; slides 27–31). The field is radial, so take a concentric Gaussian sphere of radius $r$; its flux is $E \cdot 4\pi r^2$. Outside ($r > a$) it encloses all of $Q$; inside it encloses the fraction $r^3/a^3$:

<Formula label="Field of a uniform ball" tex="E = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\ \ (r > a), \qquad E = \frac{Qr}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^3}\ \ (r < a)" cite="reading2" at="slides 30–31" />

Outside, the ball acts as if all its charge sat at the centre (p. 14).

**A thin shell.** For charge $Q$ on a shell of radius $a$, a Gaussian sphere inside encloses nothing, so $E = 0$ everywhere inside; outside, $E = k_e Q/r^2$. The field jumps by $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ across the shell, with $\sigma = Q/4\pi a^2$ (Section 4.2, Example 4.3, pp. 11–12).

## Cylindrical and planar symmetry

**An infinite line** of charge per unit length $\lambda$ (Section 4.2, Example 4.1, pp. 7–8; slides 33–35): take a coaxial cylinder of radius $r$ and length $\ell$. The field is radial, so no flux crosses the end caps, and the side gives $E \cdot 2\pi r\ell = \lambda\ell/\varepsilon_0$:

<Formula label="Infinite line of charge" tex="E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.2.16), p. 8" />

The length $\ell$ divides out, as it should, and the result matches the long-rod limit found [by integration](/courses/8-02/continuous-charge-distributions) (p. 8).

**An infinite plane** with density $\sigma$ (Section 4.2, Example 4.2, pp. 9–10; slides 36–38): take a pillbox with end caps of area $A$ on either side. Only the caps carry flux, $2EA = \sigma A/\varepsilon_0$:

<Formula label="Infinite plane of charge" tex="E = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.2.19), p. 10" />

pointing away from the plane on both sides (for $\sigma > 0$), again as found before, now with almost no work. The slides' group problems, a thick charged slab (slides 40 and 46) and two concentric shells with opposite charges (slide 47), are left for the problem pages.

## Potential from the field

Knowing $\vec E$, the potential follows from $V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \vec E \cdot d\vec s$ (slides 42–45). For the uniform ball, with $V = 0$ at infinity: outside, $V = k_e Q/r$, as for a point charge; inside, integrating the outside field down to $a$ and then the inside field down to $r$ gives (slide 44):

<Formula label="Potential inside a uniform ball" tex="V(r) = \frac{Q}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 a}\left(3 - \frac{r^2}{a^2}\right)" cite="reading2" at="slide 44" />

<WorkedExample title="A charged ball and a charged wire">
<Problem>
(a) A ball of radius 5 cm holds 3 nC spread evenly. Find the field at 2 cm, 5 cm and 10 cm from its centre. (b) A long straight wire carries 2 nC per metre. Find the field 10 cm from it.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The ball">
$k_e Q = (8.99 \times 10^9)(3 \times 10^{-9}) = 26.97$ V m. Inside, at 2 cm: $k_e Qr/a^3 = 26.97 \times 0.02/0.05^3 \approx 4.32 \times 10^3$ N/C. At the surface: $26.97/0.05^2 \approx 1.08 \times 10^4$ N/C, the largest value. At 10 cm: $26.97/0.1^2 \approx 2.70 \times 10^3$ N/C, a quarter of the surface value.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The wire">
$E = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r) = 2k_e\lambda/r = 2(8.99 \times 10^9)(2 \times 10^{-9})/0.1 \approx 360$ N/C.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Gauss's law for gravity">
Gravity is inverse-square too, so the same law holds with mass: the flux of $\vec g$ out of a closed surface is $-4\pi G$ times the mass inside. For a uniform ball of matter this means two things cosmology relies on: the pull at radius $r$ depends only on the mass inside $r$, and that mass acts as if it sat at the centre. In 8.286, Guth uses this result, in its older form as Newton's shell theorem, to follow a sphere of matter in an expanding universe: the first step towards the Friedmann equation.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Flux $\Phi_E = \oint \vec E \cdot d\vec A$, with $d\vec A$ outwards on a closed surface.
- Gauss's law: $\Phi_E = q_{\text{in}}/\varepsilon_0$ for any closed surface; it rests on the inverse-square law and the solid angle.
- With symmetry: sphere, cylinder or pillbox where $E$ is constant or parallel.
- Ball: $k_e Qr/a^3$ inside, $k_e Q/r^2$ outside; shell: zero inside; line: $\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0 r$; plane: $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$.
