[Mutual inductance](/courses/8-02/transformers-and-magnetic-materials) couples two coils. But a coil's current also sends flux through the coil itself: Session 24 is about this **self-inductance**, circuits with inductors, and the energy they store (slides 1–7).

## Self-inductance

If the current $I$ in a coil of $N$ turns changes, so does its own flux $\Phi_B$ through each turn, and Faraday's law gives a **self-induced emf**, or **back emf**, that opposes the change (course notes, Section 11.2, pp. 4–5; slides 7, 11):

<Formula label="Self-inductance" tex="\varepsilon_L = -L\,\frac{dI}{dt}, \qquad L = \frac{N\Phi_B}{I}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (11.2.2)–(11.2.3), p. 5" />

$L$ is measured in henries, 1 H = 1 V s/A (slide 8). It measures how strongly a coil resists changes in its current. The recipe (slide 8): assume a current $I$, find its field, find the flux, divide.

**A solenoid** of $N$ turns, length $\ell$ and radius $R$: the field inside is $\mu_0 nI$ with $n = N/\ell$, the flux per turn $\mu_0 nI\pi R^2$, so (Example 11.2, pp. 5–6; slide 9)

<Formula label="Inductance of a solenoid" tex="L = \frac{N\Phi_B}{I} = \mu_0 n^2\pi R^2\ell" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.2.6), p. 6" />

which depends only on the geometry, not on the current.

**Inductors in circuits.** An inductor is a circuit element with a large self-inductance. With a constant current it does nothing; it reacts only to changes, pushing against a rising current and keeping a falling one going (slides 10–12). Inductors dislike change and like steady states: the opposite of [capacitors](/courses/8-02/rc-circuits) (slide 12).

## Kirchhoff's rule with inductors

With changing magnetic fields the electric field is no longer conservative: $\oint \vec E \cdot d\vec s = -d\Phi_B/dt \neq 0$, and the potential difference between two points depends on the path (course notes, Section 11.4.1, pp. 14–15; slide 21). Kirchhoff's loop rule can be rescued if all the inductance sits in the inductors: walking across an inductor in the direction of the current, count a "potential drop" of $L\,dI/dt$ (pp. 16–17; slides 23–25). For a battery, a resistor and an inductor in series:

<Formula label="Loop rule for an LR circuit" tex="\varepsilon - IR - L\,\frac{dI}{dt} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.4.5), p. 17" />

This is a bookkeeping device rather than a true voltage drop: the notes call it misleading and, at some level, wrong, and inside an ideal inductor's wire the electric field is actually zero, so its emf is not a potential difference across it (pp. 16–17; slide 24). The slides' advice: be mindful of the physics, but don't think too hard doing it (slide 25).

## The LR circuit

**Rising current.** Close the switch at $t = 0$ with $I(0) = 0$. The current cannot jump; separating variables in the loop equation gives (course notes, Section 11.4.2, pp. 17–18; slides 14–15)

<Formula label="Rising current in an LR circuit" tex="I(t) = \frac{\varepsilon}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (11.4.7)–(11.4.8), pp. 17–18" />

At first the inductor fights as hard as it must to stop the change, and all of $\varepsilon$ appears across it; after a long time the current is steady at $\varepsilon/R$ and the inductor is just a wire (slides 16–17). The bigger $L$, the slower the rise (p. 18).

**Decaying current.** Remove the battery from a circuit carrying $\varepsilon/R$, leaving the inductor and resistor in a loop: the current dies away as $(\varepsilon/R)\,e^{-t/\tau}$, with the same $\tau$ (Section 11.4.3, p. 19). As with RC circuits, every quantity either approaches its final value as $1 - e^{-t/\tau}$ or decays as $e^{-t/\tau}$ (slide 18). Break a circuit with a large inductor suddenly and the large $dI/dt$ makes a large emf, enough to spark across the gap (demonstrations, slide 26). A car's ignition (slides 27–29) uses the same effect: interrupting the current in a coil, which also acts as a step-up transformer, produces the high voltage for the spark plug (the page's own summary).

## Energy in an inductor

Multiply the loop equation by $I$ (course notes, Section 11.4.2, pp. 18–19; slide 31):

<Formula label="Power balance" tex="\varepsilon I = I^2R + LI\,\frac{dI}{dt} = I^2R + \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12 LI^2\right)" cite="reading2" at="slide 31" />

The battery supplies power, the resistor dissipates it, and the inductor stores the rest. Unlike the resistor's heat, the inductor's energy comes back when the current falls (pp. 9–10). Building up a current $I$ stores (Section 11.3, p. 9; slide 32)

<Formula label="Energy stored in an inductor" tex="U_B = \frac12 LI^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.3.4), p. 9" />

**Where is it stored?** In the field. For a solenoid, with $B = \mu_0 nI$, $U_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\,\pi R^2\ell$, and $\pi R^2\ell$ is its volume (Example 11.5, p. 10; slide 33). So a magnetic field has energy density (p. 10; slide 34)

<Formula label="Magnetic energy density" tex="u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.3.8), p. 10" />

alongside $u_E = \frac12\varepsilon_0 E^2$ for the [electric field](/courses/8-02/conductors-and-capacitors) (slide 34). The session's group problem applies it to a non-uniform field, the energy and inductance per unit length of a coaxial cable (slide 35), left for the problem pages.

<WorkedExample title="A solenoid in a circuit">
<Problem>
A solenoid 20 cm long with 2000 turns per metre and radius 1 cm is connected through a total resistance of 10 Ω to a 12 V battery. Find (a) $L$, (b) $\tau$ and the final current, (c) the stored energy, both from $\frac12 LI^2$ and from $B^2/2\mu_0$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Inductance">
$L = \mu_0 n^2\pi R^2\ell = (4\pi \times 10^{-7})(2000)^2\pi(0.01)^2(0.2) \approx 3.16 \times 10^{-4}$ H.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Time constant and current">
$\tau = L/R \approx 3.16 \times 10^{-5}$ s, about 32 μs. Final current $\varepsilon/R = 1.2$ A.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Energy, two ways">
$\frac12 LI^2 = \frac12(3.16 \times 10^{-4})(1.2)^2 \approx 2.27 \times 10^{-4}$ J. Or: $B = \mu_0 nI \approx 3.02 \times 10^{-3}$ T, $u_B = B^2/2\mu_0 \approx 3.62$ J/m³, times the volume $\pi(0.01)^2(0.2) \approx 6.28 \times 10^{-5}$ m³: again $2.27 \times 10^{-4}$ J.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Self-inductance: $\varepsilon_L = -L\,dI/dt$, $L = N\Phi_B/I$; solenoid $\mu_0 n^2\pi R^2\ell$.
- Inductors oppose changes in current; in Kirchhoff's rule they drop $L\,dI/dt$.
- LR circuit: rise as $1 - e^{-t/\tau}$, decay as $e^{-t/\tau}$, $\tau = L/R$.
- Energy $\frac12 LI^2$, held in the field with density $B^2/2\mu_0$.
