## What you need from the lessons

A **line integral** of a function $f$ along a path $C$ adds up $f$ times the length $ds$ of each small step. If the points of the path are written in terms of the distance $s$ travelled along it, it becomes an ordinary integral:

<Formula label="Line integral of a scalar function" tex="\int_C f(x, y, z)\,ds = \int_{s_1}^{s_2} f\big(x(s), y(s), z(s)\big)\,ds" cite="mit" at="p. 1" />

For a vector field $\vec F$ the small step is a vector, $d\vec s = dx\,\hat i + dy\,\hat j + dz\,\hat k$, and only the part of $\vec F$ along the step counts. If $\vec F$ is a force, this is its work along the path:

<Formula label="Line integral of a vector field" tex="\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec s = \int_C \left(F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy + F_z\,dz\right)" cite="mit" at="p. 3" />

A charge density $\sigma$ (charge per unit area) on a surface $S$ gives the total charge as a **surface integral**. The small piece of area is $dA = dx\,dy$ in Cartesian coordinates and $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$ in polar coordinates:

<Formula label="Charge on a surface" tex="Q = \int\int_S \sigma\,dA" cite="mit" at="pp. 5 and 6" />

The **electric flux** through a surface adds up the part of $\vec E$ along the unit normal $\hat n$ of each small piece of area:

<Formula label="Electric flux" tex="\Phi_E = \int\int_S \vec E \cdot \hat n\,dA" cite="mit" at="p. 8" />

A point charge $q$ has the field below, with $r$ the distance from the charge and $\hat r$ the unit vector from the charge to the point; the fields of several charges add. A continuous charge is cut into small pieces $dq$, each with its own Coulomb field:

<Formula label="Field of a point charge" tex="\vec E = k_e\,\frac{q}{r^2}\,\hat r" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (1.6.2), p. 13" />

<Formula label="Field of a continuous charge" tex="d\vec E = k_e\,\frac{dq}{r^2}\,\hat r, \qquad \vec E = k_e \int \frac{dq}{r^2}\,\hat r" cite="reading2" at="Eqs. (2.10.1) and (2.10.2), p. 17" />

with $k_e = 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$. Two charges $+q$ and $-q$ a distance $2a$ apart form a dipole, with the dipole moment $\vec p$ of size $2qa$ pointing from $-q$ to $+q$. In a uniform field it feels no net force but a torque:

<Formula label="Dipole moment and torque" tex="p = 2qa, \qquad \vec\tau = \vec p \times \vec E" cite="reading2" at="Eqs. (2.7.1) and (2.8.3), pp. 10 and 13" />

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="One function, two paths" points={12} mit="Problem 1, p. 2">

The session's worked example integrates $f(x, y) = x + y$ along the straight line $C$ from the origin to the point $(1, 1)$, measuring $s$ from the origin, and finds

<Formula label="The worked example of the session" tex="I = \int_C (x + y)\,ds = \sqrt 2" cite="mit" at="Example 1, p. 1" />

Now integrate the same function along a different path $C'$ between the same two points. $C'$ has two straight pieces: $C_1$ runs along the $x$-axis from the origin to $(1, 0)$, and $C_2$ then runs straight up from $(1, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$. So $I' = I_1 + I_2$, with $I_1$ the integral along $C_1$ and $I_2$ the integral along $C_2$. Coordinates are pure numbers here, so the answers have no unit.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find $I_1 = \int_{C_1} (x + y)\,ds$.

<Hint>

Along $C_1$ the value of $y$ is fixed. What is it, and how is $ds$ related to $dx$ there?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$I_1 =$" value="0.5" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-I1" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On a straight piece along an axis, the distance travelled is the change of one coordinate.

Along $C_1$, $y = 0$ and $s = x$, so $ds = dx$ with $x$ from 0 to 1:

<Formula label="The first piece" tex="I_1 = \int_0^1 (x + 0)\,dx = \frac12" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Check: the function grows from 0 to 1 along a piece of length 1, so its average is $\tfrac12$.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find $I_2 = \int_{C_2} (x + y)\,ds$.

<Hint>

Along $C_2$ the value of $x$ is fixed, and the step is $ds = dy$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$I_2 =$" value="1.5" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-I2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same, now going up.

Along $C_2$, $x = 1$ and $ds = dy$ with $y$ from 0 to 1:

<Formula label="The second piece" tex="I_2 = \int_0^1 (1 + y)\,dy = 1 + \frac12 = \frac32" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Check: here the function grows from 1 to 2, with the average $\tfrac32$ over a length of 1.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Add the two pieces to find $I'$ and compare it with $I = \sqrt 2$ of the worked example. What does this tell you about line integrals?

<Hint>

$\sqrt 2$ is about 1.41.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="The comparison:"
  choices={[
    "I′ = √2, the same as I: a line integral depends only on its two end points",
    "I′ = 1, smaller than I, because the path along the axes avoids the large values of x + y",
    "I′ = 2, larger than I: a line integral can depend on the path between the end points",
    "I′ = 2, but the two cannot be compared, because a path with a corner has no arc length"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Add, then compare.

$I' = I_1 + I_2 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac32 = 2$, while the straight path gave $I = \sqrt 2 \approx 1.41$. The two paths join the same end points but give different results: the integral of $f\,ds$ depends on the path, not only on where it starts and ends (Problem 1(c), p. 2). One reason is plain to see: the path $C'$ is longer (length 2 against $\sqrt 2$), and every step adds a positive amount. A path with a corner is fine: it is split at the corner, as here.

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A vector field around a triangle" points={12} mit="Problem 2, pp. 3 and 4">

Take the vector field $\vec F(x, y) = y\,\hat i - x\,\hat j$ and the closed triangular path through the points $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, travelled anticlockwise: $C_1$ runs along the $x$-axis from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 0)$, $C_2$ along the slanted side from $(1, 0)$ to $(0, 1)$, and $C_3$ down the $y$-axis from $(0, 1)$ back to the origin. The session does the integral along $C_1$ for you: there $y = 0$, $d\vec s = dx\,\hat i$ and $\vec F = -x\,\hat j$, so $\vec F \cdot d\vec s = 0$ and $\int_{C_1} \vec F \cdot d\vec s = 0$. As in Problem 1, the answers have no unit.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find $\int_{C_3} \vec F \cdot d\vec s$.

<Hint>

On $C_3$, $x = 0$ and $d\vec s = dy\,\hat j$. Write $\vec F(0, y)$ first.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$\int_{C_3} \vec F \cdot d\vec s =$" value="0" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-C3" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the field on the side, then its part along the side.

On $C_3$, $\vec F(0, y) = y\,\hat i$, which points across the side, while the steps point along it:

<Formula label="The left side" tex="\vec F(0, y) \cdot d\vec s = (y\,\hat i) \cdot (dy\,\hat j) = 0, \qquad \int_{C_3} \vec F \cdot d\vec s = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Check: on both sides along the axes the field is perpendicular to the path, so neither adds anything. (Going down instead of up would change the sign of each step, but zero stays zero.)

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

On $C_2$, measure $s$ from the point $(1, 0)$. The side makes $45^\circ$ with the axes, so

<Formula label="The slanted side in terms of its length" tex="x = 1 - \frac{s}{\sqrt 2}, \quad y = \frac{s}{\sqrt 2}, \qquad dx = -\frac{ds}{\sqrt 2}, \quad dy = \frac{ds}{\sqrt 2}" cite="mit" at="p. 4" />

Use this to find $\int_{C_2} \vec F \cdot d\vec s$.

<Hint>

Write $F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy$ with $F_x = y$ and $F_y = -x$, and put in $x$, $y$, $dx$ and $dy$ in terms of $s$. Something simple happens to $x + y$ on this side.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The side runs from $s = 0$ to its full length. How long is it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$\int_{C_2} \vec F \cdot d\vec s =$" value="-1" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-C2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Turn the vector integral into an ordinary integral over $s$.

<Step title="The integrand">

<Formula label="The integrand on the slanted side" tex="F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy = y\left(-\frac{ds}{\sqrt 2}\right) - x\,\frac{ds}{\sqrt 2} = -\frac{x + y}{\sqrt 2}\,ds = -\frac{ds}{\sqrt 2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

since $x + y = 1$ all along this side.

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

The side runs from $(1, 0)$ to $(0, 1)$ and has the length $\sqrt 2$:

<Formula label="The slanted side" tex="\int_{C_2} \vec F \cdot d\vec s = -\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\int_0^{\sqrt 2} ds = -1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At the middle of the side, $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$, the field is $\tfrac12\hat i - \tfrac12\hat j$, pointing down and to the right, while the path goes up and to the left. The field works against the motion, so the result must be negative.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

(Our addition.) Add the three sides to find the integral once round the triangle. This part has no answer box. Then check it with Green's theorem from 18.02, $\oint_C (F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy) = \int\int \left(\partial F_y/\partial x - \partial F_x/\partial y\right) dA$, taken over the area inside the anticlockwise path.

<Solution>

**The idea.** A closed path is the sum of its pieces.

Round the triangle, $0 + (-1) + 0 = -1$. For Green's theorem, $\partial F_y/\partial x - \partial F_x/\partial y = -1 - 1 = -2$ everywhere, and the triangle has the area $\tfrac12$, so the double integral is $-2 \times \tfrac12 = -1$. The two agree. A field whose integral round a closed path is not zero cannot be the gradient of a potential, which is the lesson of Problem 1 again, now for a vector field: the result depends on the path.

Mark this part yourself: full points for $-1$ by both routes.

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Charge on a rectangle and on a disc" points={10} mit="Problem 3, p. 7">

<Part label="a" points={5}>

A rectangle has the length $a$ along $x$ (from $x = 0$ to $x = a$) and the width $b$ along $y$ (from $y = 0$ to $y = b$). It carries the surface charge density $\sigma(x, y) = k\,x\,y$, with $k$ a constant. Find the total charge $Q$ on it, in terms of $k$, $a$ and $b$.

<Hint>

Write $Q = \int\int \sigma\,dx\,dy$ with its limits. The integrand is a product of a function of $x$ and a function of $y$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$Q =$" expr="k*a^2*b^2/4"
  vars={{ k: [0.5, 5], a: [0.1, 2], b: [0.1, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "k*a*b", hint: "That is the density k times the area, as if σ were the constant k. The density grows with x and with y, so integrate x y over the rectangle." },
    { expr: "k*a^2*b^2/2", hint: "Each of the two integrals, of x from 0 to a and of y from 0 to b, carries its own factor ½." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A double integral over a rectangle with a product integrand splits into two single integrals.

<Formula label="Charge on the rectangle" tex="Q = \int_0^b \!\! \int_0^a k\,x\,y\,dx\,dy = k\left(\int_0^a x\,dx\right)\left(\int_0^b y\,dy\right) = \frac{k\,a^2 b^2}{4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 7 (our own solution)" />

Check: $\sigma$ has the unit C/m², so $k$ is in C/m⁴, and $k a^2 b^2$ is in coulombs. The average of $xy$ over the rectangle is $(a/2)(b/2)$, so $Q$ is $k\,ab/4$ times the area $ab$, which is the same.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

A circular plate of radius $R$ carries the surface charge density $\sigma(r, \theta) = k\,r\,(1 - \sin\theta)$, in polar coordinates centred on the plate, with $k$ another constant. Find the total charge on the plate.

<Hint>

In polar coordinates the small piece of area is $r\,dr\,d\theta$, not $dr\,d\theta$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

What is the integral of $\sin\theta$ over a full turn, from 0 to $2\pi$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$Q =$" expr="2*pi*k*R^3/3"
  vars={{ k: [0.5, 5], R: [0.1, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi*k*R^2", hint: "The area element in polar coordinates is r dr dθ. With it, the integrand over r is k r², not k r." },
    { expr: "k*R^3*(2*pi - 2)/3", hint: "The sine is positive on the upper half of the plate and negative on the lower half: over a full turn its integral is zero." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Use $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$, and again split the integral.

<Formula label="Charge on the disc" tex="Q = \int_0^R \!\! \int_0^{2\pi} k\,r\,(1 - \sin\theta)\,r\,d\theta\,dr = k\left(\int_0^R r^2\,dr\right)\left(\int_0^{2\pi} (1 - \sin\theta)\,d\theta\right) = k\,\frac{R^3}{3}\,(2\pi - 0) = \frac{2\pi k R^3}{3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 7 (our own solution)" />

Check: the term $-k r\sin\theta$ moves charge from the upper half to the lower half of the plate but adds none in total. What is left, $\sigma = kr$, rises from 0 at the centre to $kR$ at the rim, and the result is $\tfrac23$ of $kR$ times the area $\pi R^2$: less than the rim density times the area, as it must be, but more than half of it, because most of the area lies near the rim.

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Electric flux through flat and curved surfaces" points={14} mit="Problem 4, pp. 8 and 9">

<Part label="a" points={6}>

A uniform field $\vec E = a\,\hat i + b\,\hat j$, with constants $a$ and $b$ in N/C, crosses a flat surface of area $A$. Find the flux through it if the surface lies (i) in the $xz$ plane, with its normal along $+\hat j$, and (ii) in the $xy$ plane, with its normal along $+\hat k$. (The session works the case of the $yz$ plane with normal $+\hat i$ for you: the flux is $aA$.)

<Hint>

For a uniform field and a flat surface, the flux is $\vec E \cdot \hat n$ times the area.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a1" label="(i) $\Phi_E =$" expr="b*A"
  vars={{ a: [1, 10], b: [1, 10], A: [0.1, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "a*A", hint: "That is the flux through the surface in the yz plane, whose normal is î. Here the normal is ĵ: which component of E lies along it?" },
    { expr: "sqrt(a^2 + b^2)*A", hint: "That is the size of the field times the area. Only the part of E along the normal goes through the surface." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-a2" label="(ii) The flux through the surface in the $xy$ plane is"
  choices={[
    "aA, from the part of the field along x",
    "bA, from the part of the field along y",
    "the area times √(a² + b²)",
    "zero, since the field lies in the surface"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Take the dot product of the field with each normal.

<Formula label="Flux through the two flat surfaces" tex="\Phi_E^{(xz)} = (a\,\hat i + b\,\hat j) \cdot \hat j\,A = bA, \qquad \Phi_E^{(xy)} = (a\,\hat i + b\,\hat j) \cdot \hat k\,A = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 8 (our own solution)" />

In case (ii) the field lies in the plane of the surface and slides along it: nothing goes through. Check: the flux unit is (N/C) m², and each result uses only the component of $\vec E$ along its own normal.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

A cylinder of radius $R$ and height $h$ has its axis along $z$. A uniform field $\vec E = E_0\,\hat j$ crosses it. Find the flux through the half of the curved side with $y > 0$, with the normal pointing out of the cylinder. Let $\theta$ be the angle in the $xy$ plane, measured from the $x$-axis towards the $y$-axis.

<Hint>

A small piece of the side spans the angle $d\theta$ around the axis and the height $dz$. How large is it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The outward normal at the angle $\theta$ points straight away from the axis. Write it in terms of $\theta$, $\hat i$ and $\hat j$, then find $\vec E \cdot \hat n$. Which range of $\theta$ covers the side with $y > 0$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$\Phi_E =$" expr="2*E0*R*h"
  vars={{ E0: [1, 10], R: [0.1, 2], h: [0.1, 2] }}
  names={{ E0: "E_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi*E0*R*h", hint: "That is the field times the whole area πRh of the half side, as if the field went straight through every piece. Only E · n̂ = E₀ sin θ goes through." },
    { expr: "E0*R*h", hint: "Check the integral of sin θ from 0 to π: it is 2, not 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On a curved surface the normal changes from point to point, so integrate $\vec E \cdot \hat n$ over the surface.

<Step title="Area, normal and dot product">

A small piece of the side has the width $R\,d\theta$ along the rim and the height $dz$, so $dA = R\,d\theta\,dz$. The outward normal is $\hat n = \cos\theta\,\hat i + \sin\theta\,\hat j$, so $\vec E \cdot \hat n = E_0 \sin\theta$. The side with $y > 0$ runs from $\theta = 0$ to $\theta = \pi$.

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

<Formula label="Flux through the half side of the cylinder" tex="\int\int_S \vec E \cdot \hat n\,dA = \int_0^h \!\! \int_0^{\pi} E_0 \sin\theta\,R\,d\theta\,dz = E_0 R h\,\big[-\cos\theta\big]_0^{\pi} = 2E_0 R h" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), p. 9 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Every field line that crosses the curved half also crosses the flat rectangle that closes it, in the $xz$ plane, of width $2R$ and height $h$, with normal $+\hat j$. Its flux is $E_0 \times 2R \times h$: the same. The flux through the other half, with $y < 0$, is $-2E_0Rh$, and the top and bottom give nothing, so the whole closed cylinder has zero flux, as a uniform field must give.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Field lines of a circling field" points={10}>

From the course notes, Chapter 1, Additional Problems 1.11.8 and 1.11.1(a) (pp. 22, 24 and 25). A curve $y(x)$ is a **field line** of a field $\vec F(x, y) = F_x\,\hat i + F_y\,\hat j$ if, at every point on it, $\vec F$ is tangent to the curve.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Show that the field lines satisfy

<Formula label="Equation of the field lines" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{F_y(x, y)}{F_x(x, y)}" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 1.11.8, p. 25" />

This part has no answer box.

<Hint>

A short step along the curve is $dx\,\hat i + dy\,\hat j$. When are two vectors in the plane parallel?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Tangent means parallel to the step along the curve.

A step along the curve, $dx\,\hat i + dy\,\hat j$, has the slope $dy/dx$. The field $F_x\,\hat i + F_y\,\hat j$ has the slope $F_y/F_x$. Two vectors in the plane are parallel when their slopes agree, so $dy/dx = F_y/F_x$ at every point of a field line (where $F_x \neq 0$; where $F_x = 0$ the line runs straight up or down).

Mark this part yourself: full points if you compared the slopes, or wrote that $\vec F \times (dx\,\hat i + dy\,\hat j) = 0$, which gives $F_x\,dy - F_y\,dx = 0$.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Apply this to the field of Problem 2, $\vec F = y\,\hat i - x\,\hat j$. What do its field lines look like, and which way does the field point along them?

<Hint>

The equation is $dy/dx = -x/y$. Separate it: put everything with $y$ on one side.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the direction, look at one point, say $(0, 1)$ at the top.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="The field lines are"
  choices={[
    "straight lines through the origin, with the field pointing outwards",
    "circles round the origin, with the field going round clockwise",
    "hyperbolas xy = constant, with the field pointing away from the axes",
    "circles round the origin, with the field going round anticlockwise"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Solve the equation, then read off the direction at one point.

From $dy/dx = -x/y$, $y\,dy = -x\,dx$, and integrating gives $\tfrac12 y^2 = -\tfrac12 x^2 + \text{constant}$, so $x^2 + y^2$ is constant: circles round the origin. At the top, $(0, 1)$, the field is $\hat i$, pointing to the right, so it goes round clockwise. At every point the field is perpendicular to the position vector, since $(y\,\hat i - x\,\hat j) \cdot (x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j) = 0$, and it has the size $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = r$: it grows outwards. This is the pattern of the "circulation" in the lesson on fields.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

(Our addition.) Find $\oint \vec F \cdot d\vec s$ once round the circle of radius $R$ about the origin, travelled anticlockwise.

<Hint>

On the circle, the field has the size $R$ and is tangent to the circle. Does it point along the anticlockwise direction of travel or against it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-c" label="$\oint \vec F \cdot d\vec s =$" expr="-2*pi*R^2"
  vars={{ R: [0.1, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*pi*R^2", hint: "The field goes round clockwise, against the anticlockwise direction of travel, so each step gives a negative amount." },
    { expr: "-2*pi*R", hint: "That is minus the length of the circle. On the circle the field has the size R, not 1." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** A field tangent to the path, of constant size, gives its size times the length, with a sign.

<Step title="Directly">

On the circle $\vec F$ has the size $R$ and points clockwise, against every step, so $\vec F \cdot d\vec s = -R\,ds$. The circle has the length $2\pi R$:

<Formula label="Round the circle" tex="\oint \vec F \cdot d\vec s = -R \times 2\pi R = -2\pi R^2" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 1.11.1(a), p. 22 (our addition, our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

With $x = R\cos t$, $y = R\sin t$, the integrand is $y\,dx - x\,dy = (-R^2\sin^2 t - R^2\cos^2 t)\,dt = -R^2\,dt$, and $t$ runs from 0 to $2\pi$: the same. As for the triangle in Problem 2, the result is $-2$ times the area enclosed, $\pi R^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="The field of two opposite charges" points={10}>

**The idea.** Expand for large $|y|$ and compare with the dipole moment.

So far away the field falls off as $1/|y|^3$ and points opposite to $\vec p$, as the lesson's point-dipole field does at right angles to the axis.

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="A semicircle with a non-uniform charge" points={10}>

Find $\lambda_0$ in terms of $Q$ and $R$.

<Hint>

A small piece of the wire, spanning the angle $d\theta$, has the length $R\,d\theta$ and the charge $\lambda R\,d\theta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-a" label="$\lambda_0 =$" expr="Q/(2*R)"

<Hint>

</Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-b" label="$F_y =$" expr="-pi*ke*q*Q/(4*R^2)"

<Formula label="The y part of the force of one piece" tex="dF_y = -\frac{k_e\,q\,\lambda_0 \cos\theta\,R\,d\theta}{R^2}\,\cos\theta" cite="reading2" at="Additional Problem 2.15.4(b), p. 41 (our own solution)" />

## Problem 8

<Answer id="p8-b" label="$\tau =$" value="9.6e-28" unit="N m" tolerance="2%" calc="p8-tau" />

<Solution>

<Formula label="The torque on the dipole" tex="\vec\tau = p\,\hat j \times (3\,\hat i + 3\,\hat j)\ \text{N/C} = 3p\,(\hat j \times \hat i) = -3p\,\hat k = -9.6 \times 10^{-28}\ \text{N m}\,\hat k" cite="reading2" at="Additional Problem 2.15.8(b), p. 43 (our own solution)" />
