We know light is an electromagnetic wave (see [standing waves](/courses/8-02/standing-waves) and [Maxwell's equations](/courses/8-02/maxwell-equations-and-em-waves)). Session 32 asks how such waves are made (slides 1–5).

## Accelerating charges radiate

Stationary charges make static fields and steady currents make static magnetic fields: neither radiates. Electromagnetic waves are produced when charges **accelerate**; an accelerating charge must radiate energy (course notes, Section 13.8, p. 22).

## Shaking a sheet of charge

The simplest case is planar (course notes, Section 13.8.1, pp. 25–30; slides 6–12). Take an infinite sheet with surface charge $\sigma$, whose field is $E_0 = \sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ on each side, and at $t = 0$ pull it down at speed $v$. The field lines are rooted in the charges, so their feet move down with the sheet; but the news that the sheet is moving spreads outwards only at the speed of light. At time $T$ the lines beyond $|x| = cT$ are still undisturbed, and between, each line has a kink tilted by $v/c$ (pp. 26–27; slide 8). The kink is a new, transverse field (p. 28; slide 8):

<Formula label="Field radiated by a moving sheet" tex="E_1 = \frac{v}{c}\,E_0 = \frac{v\,\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0 c}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.8.6), p. 28" />

It points **upwards** on the sheet when the sheet moves down: field lines resist being dragged, as a string under tension does (p. 28). So the agent pulling the sheet must push with a force per unit area $\sigma^2 v/2\varepsilon_0 c$ and does work at the rate $\sigma^2 v^2/2\varepsilon_0 c$ per unit area (Eqs. 13.8.7–13.8.9, p. 28; slide 9).

The moving sheet is also a current sheet, so it makes a magnetic field $B_1 = \mu_0\sigma v/2$, opposite on the two sides, with $E_1 = cB_1$ as a wave must (pp. 28–29; slide 10). The Poynting vector carries energy away on **both** sides (p. 29; slide 11):

<Formula label="Energy flow from the sheet" tex="S = \frac{E_1B_1}{\mu_0} = \frac{\sigma^2 v^2}{4\varepsilon_0 c}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.8.12), p. 29" />

to each side, half of the work done per unit area each, so the two together carry away exactly the work done: the conversion into radiation is 100% efficient (pp. 29–30; slides 11–12). Shake the sheet up and down sinusoidally, with a velocity $v_0\cos\omega t$ alternating in direction, and the result is a sinusoidal plane wave travelling outwards on both sides (Section 13.8.2, p. 30; slide 12).

## Dipole radiation and antennas

The most common source is an oscillating **electric dipole**: an alternating voltage drives charge back and forth between the two rods of an antenna, so its ends are charged alternately $+$ and $-$ (course notes, Section 13.8, pp. 22–23; slides 15–17). Near the antenna the fields are complicated, with energy flowing out and back each cycle; far away, the changing $\vec E$ keeps making $\vec B$ and vice versa, and the fields detach as a wave carrying energy outwards (pp. 23–24). In this far region the fields fall off as $1/r$ and the radiated intensity as $\sin^2\theta/r^2$, where $\theta$ is measured from the antenna: strongest broadside, zero along the antenna (p. 23). A **quarter-wave antenna** has rods each a quarter wavelength long; the notes call the same antenna a half-wavelength antenna, after the length of the two rods together (pp. 23, 25; slides 17–20).

**Why are radio towers tall?** AM stations broadcast at 535 to 1605 kHz. At 700 kHz, $\lambda = c/f \approx 429$ m, so a quarter wavelength is about 107 m (slide 18).

## The spark-gap transmitter

A spark gap is a "lumped" LC oscillator, a small capacitor and inductor in one piece (slides 21–23). In the class's transmitter, the gap is charged through a large resistor, with an RC time of about $1.5 \times 10^{-4}$ s; when it breaks down, the spark makes the charge slosh back and forth at the antenna's resonant frequency, $c/4\ell \approx 2.4$ GHz with $4\ell = 12.4$ cm (rods about 3.1 cm long), and the cycle repeats (slide 24).

## Polarization

The electric field of dipole radiation oscillates along the antenna: the wave is **polarized** in that direction (slides 29–33). The slides show this with TV antennas and a microwave demonstration; in words (the page's own explanation), a receiving antenna works best when lined up with the field, and a grid of parallel wires blocks microwaves polarized along the wires and passes the component perpendicular to them. Experiment 12 sets up a standing microwave wave and measures its wavelength from the spacing of the nodes, which are half a wavelength apart (slides 33–35).

## Summary

- Accelerating charges radiate; static charges and steady currents do not.
- A sheet pulled at speed $v$ radiates $E_1 = v\sigma/2\varepsilon_0 c$, $B_1 = E_1/c$; the work done against $E_1$ all leaves as radiation.
- Antennas are oscillating dipoles; quarter-wave antennas are a quarter wavelength per rod, hence tall AM towers.
- Dipole radiation is polarized along the antenna.
