So far the charges have been at rest. Session 10 lets them flow: currents, resistance, and circuits of batteries and resistors (slides 1–5).

## Current and current density

**Current** is the rate at which charge crosses a surface (course notes, Chapter 6, Section 6.1, p. 1; slide 6):

<Formula label="Current" tex="I = \frac{dQ}{dt}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.1.2), p. 1" />

in **amperes**, 1 A = 1 C/s; currents range from mega-amperes in lightning to nano-amperes in nerves (p. 1). By convention the current points the way positive charge flows; in metal wires the moving charges are electrons going the other way (p. 1; slide 7). The **current density** $\vec J$ is current per unit area, and the current through a surface is its flux, $I = \int \vec J \cdot d\vec A$ (Section 6.1.1, p. 2; slide 8).

If carriers of charge $q$, $n$ per unit volume, move with **drift speed** $v_d$, then $I = nqv_dA$ and $\vec J = nq\vec v_d$ (Eqs. 6.1.4–6.1.5, p. 2). The electrons actually move erratically, colliding constantly; the field only adds a slow average drift (p. 2), typically about $4 \times 10^{-5}$ m/s, so slow that going one metre would take hours (slide 10). With current flowing, the field inside a conductor is no longer zero and the conductor is no longer an equipotential (slide 9).

## Ohm's law

Between collisions the field accelerates each electron; averaging over the mean free time $\tau$ gives a drift velocity proportional to $\vec E$ (Section 6.1.1, p. 3). In many materials, then, the current density is proportional to the field (Section 6.2, pp. 3–4; slides 11–12):

<Formula label="Microscopic Ohm's law" tex="\vec J = \sigma\vec E" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.2.1), p. 3" />

The **conductivity** $\sigma$, and the **resistivity** $\rho = 1/\sigma$, depend only on the material, not on its shape (slide 12); materials that obey this are **ohmic** (p. 4). For a wire of length $\ell$ and area $A$ with potential difference $\Delta V$, the field is $\Delta V/\ell$ and $J = I/A$, which gives the familiar form (Section 6.2, pp. 4–5; slides 16–17):

<Formula label="Ohm's law and resistance" tex="\Delta V = IR, \qquad R = \frac{\rho\,\ell}{A}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.2.7) and (6.2.10), p. 5" />

with $R$ in **ohms**, 1 Ω = 1 V/A. Resistivity ranges enormously: $1.72 \times 10^{-8}$ Ω m for copper, about $10^{10}$ to $10^{14}$ Ω m for glass; for metals it grows slowly with temperature (pp. 5–6; slide 13).

## Batteries and emf

A battery is a "charge pump": it does work moving charge from low to high potential. Its **electromotive force** (emf) $\varepsilon$ is the work per unit charge, measured in volts (course notes, Chapter 7, Section 7.2, p. 2). With one resistor, the potential rises by $\varepsilon$ across the battery and falls by $IR$ across the resistor, so $I = \varepsilon/R$ (p. 3). A real battery has a small **internal resistance** $r$ (Section 7.2, pp. 3–4; slide 30):

<Formula label="A battery with internal resistance" tex="\Delta V_{\text{terminal}} = \varepsilon - Ir, \qquad I = \frac{\varepsilon}{R + r}" cite="reading2" at="Eqs. (7.2.5) and (7.2.7), pp. 3–4" />

so even shorting the terminals does not give an infinite current (slide 30).

**Sign conventions** for walking around a circuit (slides 20–22): crossing a battery from $-$ to $+$, the potential rises by $\varepsilon$ (a ski lift); crossing a resistor in the direction of the current, it falls by $IR$ (a ski slope); crossing a capacitor from the negative to the positive plate, it rises by $Q/C$.

## Resistors in series and in parallel

**In series** the same current flows through each, and the voltage drops add (Chapter 7, Section 7.3, p. 4; slide 24); **in parallel** they share one voltage and the currents add (pp. 5–6; slide 25):

<Formula label="Resistors in series and in parallel" tex="R_{\text{eq}} = R_1 + R_2 \ \ (\text{series}), \qquad \frac{1}{R_{\text{eq}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \ \ (\text{parallel})" cite="reading2" at="Eqs. (7.3.2) and (7.3.5), pp. 4–5" />

The rules are the reverse of those for capacitors. In series the largest resistor dominates; in parallel the smallest does, so a short circuit takes nearly all the current (pp. 5–6).

## Kirchhoff's rules

Any circuit can be solved with two rules (Chapter 7, Section 7.4, pp. 6–7; slides 28–29):

1. **Junction rule**: the currents into a junction add up to the currents out, $\sum I_{\text{in}} = \sum I_{\text{out}}$; charge does not pile up.
2. **Loop rule**: around any closed loop the potential changes add to zero, because the electrostatic field is conservative:

<Formula label="Kirchhoff's loop rule" tex="\sum_{\text{closed loop}} \Delta V = -\oint \vec E \cdot d\vec s = 0" cite="reading3" at="slide 29" />

The slides' recipe (slide 31): straighten the circuit into rectangles, combine resistors in series and parallel, assign a current to each loop, write one loop equation per loop, and solve. The directions are arbitrary: a negative answer means the current flows the other way (Section 7.8, p. 16). A simple use: a **voltage divider**, two resistors in series across $V_{\text{in}}$, gives $V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}}\,R_2/(R_1 + R_2)$ across $R_2$ (pp. 7–8).

## Power

Moving charge $dq$ through a potential difference $\Delta V$ changes its energy by $dq\,\Delta V$, so the power is (Chapter 6, Section 6.3, pp. 6–7; slides 36–38):

<Formula label="Electrical power" tex="P = I\,\Delta V = I^2R = \frac{(\Delta V)^2}{R}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.3.1)–(6.3.2), p. 7" />

the last two forms for a resistor, which always dissipates energy as heat. A battery driving current from $-$ to $+$ supplies $I\varepsilon$, and energy balance says this equals what the resistors, including the internal one, dissipate: $I\varepsilon = I^2R + I^2r$ (Chapter 7, Eq. 7.2.8, p. 4). A capacitor being charged absorbs power $\frac{d}{dt}(Q^2/2C)$ (slides 39–40).

<WorkedExample title="A battery and three resistors">
<Problem>
A 9 V battery with internal resistance 0.5 Ω drives a 4 Ω resistor in series with a parallel pair of 6 Ω and 3 Ω. Find the current from the battery, the terminal voltage, the current in each branch of the pair, and check the power balance.
</Problem>
<Step title="Equivalent resistance and current">
The pair: $6 \times 3/(6 + 3) = 2$ Ω. In series with 4 Ω and the internal 0.5 Ω: $6.5$ Ω in all. $I = 9/6.5 \approx 1.385$ A.
</Step>
<Step title="Terminal voltage and branches">
$\Delta V_{\text{terminal}} = 9 - (1.385)(0.5) \approx 8.31$ V. Across the pair: $(1.385)(2) \approx 2.77$ V, so $2.77/6 \approx 0.462$ A through 6 Ω and $2.77/3 \approx 0.923$ A through 3 Ω, adding to 1.385 A (junction rule).
</Step>
<Step title="Power balance">
The battery's emf supplies $I\varepsilon = (1.385)(9) \approx 12.46$ W. Dissipated: $I^2(4 + 2 + 0.5) = (1.385)^2(6.5) \approx 12.46$ W, of which $I^2r \approx 0.96$ W heats the battery itself.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Two batteries, two loops">
<Problem>
A 12 V battery in series with 2 Ω forms the left branch, a 6 V battery in series with 4 Ω the right branch, and a 6 Ω resistor the middle branch, all three joining at a top junction and a bottom junction, with both batteries' positive terminals towards the top. Find the three currents (ideal batteries).
</Problem>
<Step title="Set up">
Let $I_1$ flow up through the left branch, $I_2$ up through the right one, and $I_3$ down through the middle. Junction rule: $I_3 = I_1 + I_2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Loop equations">
Left loop (up the left branch, down the middle): $12 - 2I_1 - 6(I_1 + I_2) = 0$, so $8I_1 + 6I_2 = 12$. Right loop: $6 - 4I_2 - 6(I_1 + I_2) = 0$, so $6I_1 + 10I_2 = 6$.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve">
$I_1 \approx 1.909$ A, $I_2 \approx -0.545$ A, $I_3 \approx 1.364$ A. The negative $I_2$ means current flows *down* through the 6 V battery: the stronger battery charges the weaker one. Check: the middle resistor has $6 \times 1.364 \approx 8.18$ V across it, and so does the left branch, $12 - 2 \times 1.909 \approx 8.18$ V.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $I = dQ/dt$; $\vec J = nq\vec v_d$; drift speeds are tiny.
- Ohm's law: $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$, or $\Delta V = IR$ with $R = \rho\ell/A$.
- A battery's emf $\varepsilon$ pumps charge; its terminal voltage is $\varepsilon - Ir$.
- Series resistors add; parallel resistors add as inverses.
- Kirchhoff: currents balance at junctions, voltages add to zero around loops. Power $P = I\Delta V$; resistors dissipate $I^2R$.
