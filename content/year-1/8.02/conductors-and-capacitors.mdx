Session 7 turns from charges in empty space to **conductors**, and builds the first electrical device of the course: the **capacitor** (slides 1–4). In between, Experiment 2 measures the electrostatic force (slides 15–17; the slides give only its title and a link to a simulation).

## Conductors and insulators

A **conductor**, such as a metal, holds charges that are free to move, because some electrons are only weakly bound to their atoms; in an **insulator**, such as glass or paper, the electrons are tied to particular atoms (course notes, Section 4.3, p. 14; slide 5). Put a conductor in a field and its free charges move until their own field cancels the outside one inside the conductor. A metal ball in a uniform field, for example, gathers charges of opposite signs on opposite sides, and outside it adds a dipole-like field (Section 4.3, p. 15). In **electrostatic equilibrium** (Section 4.3, pp. 14–17; slides 6, 13):

1. The field inside the conductor is zero.
2. Any net charge lies on the surface: a Gaussian surface just inside it has $\vec E = \vec 0$, so it encloses no charge (p. 15). Charge put inside moves out to the surface, as far as it can get from its like charges (slide 14).
3. The field has no component along the surface, so the conductor's surface, and the whole conductor, is one **equipotential** (p. 16).
4. Just outside, the field is perpendicular to the surface. A small pillbox through the surface gives (p. 17):

<Formula label="Field just outside a conductor" tex="E_n = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.3.3), p. 17" />

where $\sigma$ is the local surface charge density.

**Equipotentials** are the curves or surfaces of constant $V$, like the contour lines of a topographic map. Field lines cross them at right angles and point from high to low potential; the field has no component along them, so moving a charge along an equipotential takes no work (slides 7–12).

Two consequences from the notes. A charge $q$ inside a hollow conductor induces $-q$ on the cavity wall, so a conductor with net charge $Q$ carries $Q + q$ on its outer surface (Example 4.5, pp. 17–18). And charge crowds where the surface is most sharply curved: two spheres joined by a wire share one potential, which gives $\sigma_1/\sigma_2 = r_2/r_1$, so the field is strongest at sharp points, the principle of the lightning rod (pp. 20–21).

## Capacitors

A **capacitor** is two conductors with equal and opposite charges $\pm Q$ and a potential difference $\Delta V$ between them. The charge is proportional to the voltage, and the ratio is the **capacitance** (course notes, Chapter 5, Section 5.1, pp. 2–3; slide 19):

<Formula label="Capacitance" tex="C = \frac{Q}{|\Delta V|}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (5.1.1), p. 2" />

measured in **farads**, 1 F = 1 C/V. Typical capacitors range from picofarads ($10^{-12}$ F) to millifarads (Section 5.1, p. 3).

**Parallel plates.** Two plates of area $A$ a distance $d$ apart (Section 5.2, Example 5.1, pp. 3–5; slides 20–24). The charges go to the inner faces of the plates, as close as they can get to the opposite charges (slide 21). Ignoring the fringing field at the edges, a pillbox around the positive plate gives $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0 = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)$ between the plates, and zero outside (adding the two sheets' fields $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ gives the same, slide 23). Then $|\Delta V| = Ed$, so

<Formula label="Parallel-plate capacitor" tex="C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (5.2.4), p. 5" />

It depends only on the geometry: bigger plates hold more charge, and a smaller gap means a smaller voltage for the same charge (p. 5).

**Concentric spheres.** Shells of radii $a < b$ with charges $\pm Q$: the field between them is that of a point charge, and integrating it from $a$ to $b$ gives (Section 5.2, Example 5.3, p. 7; slides 26–27):

<Formula label="Spherical capacitor" tex="C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\frac{ab}{b - a}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (5.2.11), p. 7" />

Letting $b \to \infty$ leaves an **isolated sphere** of radius $a$: $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a$ (Section 5.2, p. 8; slide 27). (Coaxial cylinders, Example 5.2, give $C = 2\pi\varepsilon_0 L/\ln(b/a)$ for length $L$, pp. 5–6.)

A farad is a lot: the whole Earth, as an isolated sphere of radius $6.4 \times 10^6$ m, has only about $7 \times 10^{-4}$ F (slide 28). A 1 F capacitor at 12 V holds 12 C (slide 29).

## Energy stored in a capacitor

Charge a capacitor by carrying small charges $dq$ from one plate to the other. When the plates hold $\pm q$, the voltage is $q/C$, and moving $dq$ takes work $dW = (q/C)\,dq$ (course notes, Section 5.4, p. 12; slides 33–35). Adding up from 0 to $Q$:

<Formula label="Energy in a capacitor" tex="U = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac12 Q\,|\Delta V| = \frac12 C\,|\Delta V|^2" cite="reading3" at="slides 35–36" />

So the 1 F capacitor at 12 V stores 72 J (slide 38).

**Where is the energy?** In the field. For parallel plates, $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$ and $|\Delta V| = Ed$, so $U = \frac12\varepsilon_0 E^2 \cdot Ad$, and $Ad$ is the volume between the plates (Section 5.4.1, p. 13; slide 37). The **energy density** of an electric field is

<Formula label="Energy density of the electric field" tex="u_E = \frac{U}{\text{volume}} = \frac12\varepsilon_0 E^2" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (5.4.4), p. 13" />

This holds for any field, not just inside capacitors: integrating $u_E$ over all space outside a charged spherical shell gives back the work needed to charge it, $Q^2/(8\pi\varepsilon_0 a)$ (Example 5.6, pp. 14–15).

<WorkedExample title="A small parallel-plate capacitor">
<Problem>
Two square plates 10 cm on a side are 1 mm apart, connected to 100 V. Find (a) the capacitance, (b) the charge, (c) the field between the plates, and (d) the stored energy, both from $\frac12 C\Delta V^2$ and from the energy density.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) and (b)">
$C = \varepsilon_0 A/d = (8.854 \times 10^{-12})(0.01)/0.001 \approx 8.85 \times 10^{-11}$ F, about 89 pF. $Q = C\Delta V \approx 8.85 \times 10^{-9}$ C.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The field">
$E = \Delta V/d = 100/0.001 = 10^5$ V/m.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) The energy, two ways">
$\frac12 C\Delta V^2 = \frac12(8.854 \times 10^{-11})(100)^2 \approx 4.43 \times 10^{-7}$ J. Or: $u_E = \frac12\varepsilon_0 E^2 = \frac12(8.854 \times 10^{-12})(10^{10}) \approx 0.0443$ J/m³, times the volume $0.01 \times 0.001 = 10^{-5}$ m³, again $4.43 \times 10^{-7}$ J.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Energy in empty space">
The idea that a field stores energy, $\frac12\varepsilon_0 E^2$ per cubic metre for an electric field, is what lets light carry energy across the universe (in a light wave the energy is shared equally between its electric and magnetic fields). The early universe was filled with electromagnetic radiation whose energy density, in 8.286, follows the black-body law and grows as the fourth power of the temperature: for the first tens of thousands of years after the Big Bang, radiation outweighed all the matter.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Conductor in equilibrium: $\vec E = \vec 0$ inside, charge on the surface, one potential, $E_n = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$ just outside.
- Capacitance $C = Q/|\Delta V|$: $\varepsilon_0 A/d$ (plates), $4\pi\varepsilon_0 ab/(b - a)$ (spheres), $4\pi\varepsilon_0 a$ (isolated sphere).
- Stored energy $U = Q^2/2C = \frac12 C\,\Delta V^2$, held in the field with density $\frac12\varepsilon_0 E^2$.
