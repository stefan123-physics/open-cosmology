## What you need from the lessons

The magnetic flux through a flat loop of area $A$ in a uniform field $\vec B$ is $\Phi_B = \vec B \cdot \vec A$; for a field that changes across the loop, add up $\vec B \cdot d\vec A$ over its surface:

<Formula label="Magnetic flux" tex="\Phi_B = \int\int_S \vec B \cdot d\vec A" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (10.1.2), p. 2" />

The area vector $\vec A$ is normal to the loop; which of the two normals you take is your choice, and it fixes the positive sense round the loop by the right-hand rule (thumb along $\vec A$, fingers along the positive sense). Faraday's law gives the emf round a coil of $N$ turns:

<Formula label="Faraday's law" tex="\varepsilon = -N\,\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}" cite="mit" at="p. 1" />

In a closed loop of resistance $R$ the emf drives the current $I = |\varepsilon|/R$. The flux can change because $B$ changes, because the area inside the field changes (a conductor moving), or because the loop turns (session, p. 2). **Lenz's law** gives the sense of the current: its own magnetic field opposes the change in flux that caused it. A straight piece of wire of length $\ell$ moving at speed $v$ across a field $B$, all three at right angles, has the motional emf $B\ell v$ (Eq. (10.2.4), p. 7, of the notes). A current-carrying piece of wire $d\vec s$ in a field feels the force

<Formula label="Force on a piece of wire" tex="d\vec F = I\,d\vec s \times \vec B" cite="mit" at="Question 7, p. 4" />

Eddy currents are currents induced inside solid metal; they oppose the motion that makes them and turn its energy into heat (Section 10.5, p. 13, of the notes).

In matter, the **magnetization** $\vec M$ is the magnetic dipole moment per unit volume (Eq. (9.6.1), p. 28, of Chapter 9). In a paramagnet or a diamagnet it is proportional to the applied field $\vec B_0$, with the magnetic susceptibility $\chi_m$:

<Formula label="Magnetization of a para- or diamagnet" tex="\vec M = \frac{\chi_m}{\mu_0}\,\vec B_0, \qquad \vec B = \vec B_0 + \mu_0\vec M = (1 + \chi_m)\,\vec B_0" cite="reading2" at="Eqs. (9.6.7)–(9.6.9), p. 30" />

with $\chi_m$ a small number for para- and diamagnets.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A loop falling out of a magnetic field" points={32} mit="Example: a falling loop">

Take $x$ out of the page, $y$ to the right and $z$ up. A uniform magnetic field $\vec B = B\,\hat x$, out of the page, fills the region above a horizontal line and is zero below it. A rectangular loop of wire, with mass $m$, width $w$ (along $y$), height $\ell$ (along $z$) and resistance $R$, hangs in a vertical plane across that line and falls out of the field under gravity. Its top side is still in the field, its bottom side already below it. Let $z(t)$ be the height of the top side above the line where the field ends, so $0 < z < \ell$, and let the loop's velocity be $\vec V(t) = V(t)\,\hat z$, with $V(t) < 0$ because the loop falls. (The notes' Additional Problem 10.11.11, Chapter 10, p. 30, is the same situation.)

<Part label="a" points={3}>

How is $V(t)$ related to $z(t)$?

<Hint>

$z$ is positive and shrinks as the loop falls. What sign does $dz/dt$ have?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="The relation is"
  choices={[
    "V = −dz/dt, since V is negative while z is positive",
    "V = d(ℓ − z)/dt, the rate at which the part of the loop below the line grows",
    "V = dz/dt: both are negative while the loop falls",
    "V = −z/t, the average rate since the top left the field"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $z$ is the height of the top side above a fixed line, so it changes exactly as the loop's height does.

<Step title="The relation (MIT's Question 1)">

The top side is part of the loop, so it moves with the loop's velocity $V\,\hat z$: $dz/dt = V$. Both are negative while the loop falls. $d(\ell - z)/dt = -V$ is the speed $|V|$, positive, so it is not $V$; and $-dz/dt$ is also $|V|$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Take the area vector $\vec A$ out of the page. What is the magnetic flux $\Phi_B$ through the loop at time $t$? Give it in terms of $B$, $w$ and $z$.

<Hint>

The field is not the same over the whole loop: it is $B$ above the line and zero below it. Which part of the loop's area has field through it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$\Phi_B =$" expr="B*w*z"
  vars={{ B: [1, 2], w: [0.3, 0.6], z: [0.1, 0.4], l: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "B*w*l", hint: "That is the flux of a loop lying wholly in the field. Below the line the field is zero, so only the strip of height z counts." },
    { expr: "B*l*z", hint: "The strip inside the field is as wide as the loop, w, and as high as z." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Step 1 of the session's recipe: the flux of a field that is uniform on part of the loop and zero on the rest.

<Step title="The flux (MIT's Question 2)">

Only the strip of the loop above the line, of width $w$ and height $z$, has field through it, and there $\vec B$ is along $\vec A$. So

<Formula label="Flux through the falling loop" tex="\Phi_B = B\,w\,z(t)" cite="mit" at="Question 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

It is positive: outward flux, along $\vec A$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the rate of change $d\Phi_B/dt$ in terms of $B$, $w$ and $V$. Is it positive or negative?

<Hint>

Only $z$ changes with time. Use part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$d\Phi_B/dt =$" expr="B*w*V"
  vars={{ B: [1, 2], w: [0.3, 0.6], V: [-3, -1], l: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-B*w*V", hint: "That is positive, but the flux is falling as the loop leaves the field. dz/dt = V, with V negative." },
    { expr: "B*l*V", hint: "The area inside the field shrinks by w for each metre the loop falls: the width, not the height, multiplies V." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Step 2 of the recipe: here the flux changes because the area inside the field changes (case (ii) on p. 2).

<Step title="The rate (MIT's Question 3)">

$B$ and $w$ are constant, so

<Formula label="Rate of change of the flux" tex="\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = B\,w\,\frac{dz}{dt} = B\,w\,V(t)" cite="mit" at="Question 3, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Since $V < 0$, this is negative: the outward flux is falling.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Seen from the front (with $x$ towards you), which way does the induced current flow round the loop?

<Hint>

The emf has the sign opposite to that of $d\Phi_B/dt$. A positive emf drives current in the positive sense, the one your fingers give with your thumb along $\vec A$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="The induced current flows"
  choices={[
    "clockwise",
    "nowhere: the field inside the loop is uniform where it is not zero",
    "counterclockwise",
    "clockwise in the top side and counterclockwise in the bottom side"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Step 3 of the recipe: the sign of the emf.

<Step title="The sense (MIT's Question 4)">

$d\Phi_B/dt < 0$, so $\varepsilon = -d\Phi_B/dt > 0$: the current runs in the positive sense, right-handed about $\vec A$. With the thumb out of the page, the fingers curl counterclockwise as seen from the front.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

Which way does the magnetic field of the induced current alone point inside the loop, and what does it do to the flux?

<Hint>

Use the right-hand rule on the current of part (d), then compare with the change in flux of part (c).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e" label="Inside the loop the induced current's own field"
  choices={[
    "points into the page, against the outside field",
    "points out of the page, making up for some of the outward flux being lost",
    "points straight down, along the fall",
    "is zero, because the induced current flows only along the wire"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Lenz's law, as a check on part (d).

<Step title="The self-field (MIT's Question 5)">

A counterclockwise current, seen from the front, makes a field out of the page inside the loop. The outward flux is falling, so this field opposes the change: it tries to keep the flux where it was. That is Lenz's law, and it agrees with part (d).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="f" points={3}>

Let $v = -V = |V|$ be the speed of the loop. Find the size of the current $I$, in terms of $B$, $w$, $v$ and $R$.

<Hint>

$I = |\varepsilon|/R$, with $|\varepsilon| = |d\Phi_B/dt|$ from part (c).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-f" label="$I =$" expr="B*w*v/R"
  vars={{ B: [1, 2], w: [0.3, 0.6], v: [1, 3], R: [2, 4], l: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "B*l*v/R", hint: "Only the top side, of length w, cuts across the field lines as the loop falls. The height of the loop does not enter." },
    { expr: "B*w*v*R", hint: "Ohm's law: the current is the emf divided by the resistance." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Ohm's law with the emf of part (c).

<Step title="The current (MIT's Question 6)">

<Formula label="Current in the falling loop" tex="I = \frac{|\varepsilon|}{R} = \frac{B\,w\,v}{R}" cite="mit" at="Question 6, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The same follows from the motional emf $Bwv$ of the top side, the only side moving across field lines inside the field.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="g" points={4}>

Besides gravity, the field exerts a force on the loop along $z$. Find its $z$ component $F_z$, with its sign, in terms of $B$, $w$, $v$ and $R$.

<Hint>

Use $d\vec F = I\,d\vec s \times \vec B$ on each side of the loop. Which sides are in the field, and do any of their forces cancel?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the top side, which way does the current of part (d) run? Write it as a unit vector, then work out its cross product with $\hat x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-g" label="$F_z =$" expr="B^2*w^2*v/R"
  vars={{ B: [1, 2], w: [0.3, 0.6], v: [1, 3], R: [2, 4], l: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-B^2*w^2*v/R", hint: "The size is right but the sign is not. By Lenz's law the force opposes the fall, so it points up, along +z." },
    { expr: "B^2*w*l*v/R", hint: "The force acts on the top side, of length w; the current I = Bwv/R carries a second factor w." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The magnetic force on the current of part (f), side by side.

<Step title="The sides">

The bottom side is below the line, where $B = 0$. The two vertical sides are in the field over the same length $z$ and carry opposite currents, so their forces (along $\pm\hat y$) cancel. Only the top side counts.

</Step>

<Step title="The top side (MIT's Question 7)">

There the current runs along $-\hat y$ over the length $w$, so

<Formula label="Magnetic force on the falling loop" tex="\vec F = I\,w\,(-\hat y) \times B\,\hat x = I\,w\,B\,\hat z = \frac{B^2 w^2 v}{R}\,\hat z" cite="mit" at="Question 7, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

using $\hat y \times \hat x = -\hat z$. The force points up, against the fall, as Lenz's law demands: a magnetic brake.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="h" points={4}>

The loop reaches a terminal velocity, at which it no longer speeds up. Find its terminal speed $v_t$, in terms of $m$, $g$, $R$, $B$ and $w$.

<Hint>

At terminal speed the net force on the loop is zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-h" label="$v_t =$" expr="m*g*R/(B^2*w^2)"
  vars={{ m: [0.01, 0.05], g: [9, 10], R: [2, 4], B: [1, 2], w: [0.1, 0.3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*g/(B^2*w^2*R)", hint: "A larger resistance means a smaller current and a weaker brake, so the loop must fall faster: R belongs in the numerator." },
    { expr: "m*g*R/(B*w)", hint: "The braking force has B²w²: one factor Bw from the emf and one from the force on the top side." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Balance the magnetic force of part (g) against gravity.

<Step title="The balance (MIT's Question 8)">

$B^2 w^2 v_t/R = mg$, so

<Formula label="Terminal speed of the falling loop" tex="v_t = \frac{m\,g\,R}{B^2 w^2}" cite="mit" at="Question 8, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A heavier loop or a larger resistance falls faster; a stronger field or a wider loop brakes harder and falls more slowly. As $B \to 0$ the terminal speed grows without limit: free fall.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="i" points={2}>

At terminal speed, gravity does work on the loop at the rate $\vec F_g \cdot \vec V$, and the loop turns electrical energy into heat at the rate $I^2 R$. Write the equation that says these are equal, and show that it holds with your results of parts (f) and (h). (Mark yourself.)

<Hint>

Gravity pulls down and the loop moves down, so the rate is $m g v_t$. Put $v = v_t$ into the current of part (f).

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Energy conservation: at constant speed the kinetic energy does not change, so all the work done by gravity becomes Joule heat.

<Step title="The equation (MIT's Question 9)">

<Formula label="Power balance at terminal speed" tex="m\,g\,v_t = I^2 R" cite="mit" at="Question 9, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The check (MIT's Question 10)">

With $I = Bwv_t/R$, the right side is $B^2 w^2 v_t^2/R = (B^2 w^2 v_t/R)\,v_t$. By part (h), $B^2 w^2 v_t/R = mg$, so the right side is $m g v_t$, the left side.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A bar driven along rails by a battery" points={18}>

This problem builds on the notes' Additional Problem 10.11.1 (Chapter 10, p. 25), in our own words; parts (a), (b) and (d) are our additions.

Two long, frictionless, horizontal rails lie a distance $\ell$ apart and are joined at one end by a battery that keeps a constant emf $\varepsilon$. A conducting bar of mass $m$ and resistance $R$ lies across the rails and closes the circuit; the rails and the battery have no resistance. A uniform magnetic field $\vec B$ points straight out of the plane of the rails. The bar starts at rest. The battery's current through the bar feels a magnetic force along the rails, which sets the bar moving.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

When the bar moves at speed $v$ (in the direction the battery's current pushes it), what is the current $I$ in the circuit? Give it in terms of $\varepsilon$, $B$, $\ell$, $v$ and $R$.

<Hint>

The moving bar has a motional emf of its own. By Lenz's law, does it help the battery or work against it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$I =$" expr="(eps - B*l*v)/R"
  vars={{ eps: [1, 2], B: [0.5, 1], l: [0.1, 0.3], v: [0.5, 2], R: [0.5, 1] }}
  names={{ eps: "\\varepsilon", l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(eps + B*l*v)/R", hint: "The motion is caused by the current, so by Lenz's law the emf of the motion opposes that current: subtract it." },
    { expr: "eps/R", hint: "That holds only while the bar is at rest. Once it moves, the bar has a motional emf B l v of its own." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two emfs in one loop: the battery's and the moving bar's.

<Step title="The two emfs">

As the bar moves, the area of the circuit, and so the flux through it, changes at the rate $B\ell v$, which gives the bar a motional emf of size $B\ell v$. The battery's current drives the motion, so by Lenz's law the motional emf drives current the other way. The net emf is $\varepsilon - B\ell v$, and

<Formula label="Current with the bar moving" tex="I = \frac{\varepsilon - B\ell v}{R}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 10.11.1, p. 25 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

The bar speeds up until it reaches a steady speed. Find that terminal speed $v_\infty$ in terms of $\varepsilon$, $B$ and $\ell$.

<Hint>

When does the magnetic force on the bar stop pushing it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$v_\infty =$" expr="eps/(B*l)"
  vars={{ eps: [1, 2], B: [0.5, 1], l: [0.1, 0.3], R: [2, 4] }}
  names={{ eps: "\\varepsilon", l: "\\ell" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "eps*R/(B*l)", hint: "No current flows at the terminal speed, so the resistance plays no part in it." },
    { expr: "eps/(B^2*l^2)", hint: "Set the current of part (a) to zero: the motional emf B l v must equal the battery's emf." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The force on the bar is $I\ell B$; it stops pushing when the current stops.

<Step title="The terminal speed">

$I = 0$ when $B\ell v = \varepsilon$, so

<Formula label="Terminal speed of the bar" tex="v_\infty = \frac{\varepsilon}{B\ell}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 10.11.1, p. 25 (our own solution)" />

The bar's motional emf then exactly cancels the battery. With no current, there is no force, and the bar coasts on at that speed on the frictionless rails.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Show that the speed of the bar at time $t$ is $v = v_\infty\,(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, with $\tau = mR/B^2\ell^2$. (Mark yourself.)

<Hint>

Newton's second law: $m\,dv/dt = I\ell B$, with $I$ from part (a). Write it in terms of $v_\infty - v$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The force falls as the bar speeds up, so the speed creeps up towards $v_\infty$ exponentially.

<Step title="The equation of motion">

<Formula label="Equation of motion of the bar" tex="m\,\frac{dv}{dt} = I\ell B = \frac{B\ell}{R}\,(\varepsilon - B\ell v) = \frac{B^2\ell^2}{R}\,(v_\infty - v)" cite="reading1" at="Problem 10.11.1, p. 25 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The solution">

So $d(v_\infty - v)/dt = -(v_\infty - v)/\tau$, with $\tau = mR/B^2\ell^2$. The difference $v_\infty - v$ decays exponentially from its starting value $v_\infty$ (the bar starts at rest): $v_\infty - v = v_\infty e^{-t/\tau}$, which is

<Formula label="Speed of the bar" tex="v(t) = \frac{\varepsilon}{B\ell}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = \frac{mR}{B^2\ell^2}" cite="reading1" at="Problem 10.11.1, p. 25" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $t = 0$ it gives $v = 0$, and its slope there is $v_\infty/\tau = \varepsilon B\ell/mR$, which is the starting force $(\varepsilon/R)\ell B$ divided by $m$. As $t \to \infty$ it tends to $v_\infty$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

(Our addition.) The battery gives 1.5 V, the rails are 20 cm apart, the field is 0.50 T, and the bar has a mass of 10 g and a resistance of 0.50 Ω (numbers our choice). How long after the start does the bar reach 90% of its terminal speed, in seconds?

<Hint>

Find $\tau$ first. Then solve $1 - e^{-t/\tau} = 0.9$ for $t$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="$t =$" value="1.15" unit="s" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-time-90" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The result of part (c), solved for the time.

<Step title="The time constant">

$\tau = (0.010)(0.50)/[(0.50)^2(0.20)^2] = 0.005/0.01 = 0.50$ s. The terminal speed is $1.5/(0.50 \times 0.20) = 15$ m/s.

</Step>

<Step title="The time">

$e^{-t/\tau} = 0.1$ gives $t = \tau \ln 10 = 0.50 \times 2.303 = 1.15$ s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A loop in a field that rises, holds and falls" points={18}>

This problem is the notes' Additional Problem 10.11.9 (Chapter 10, pp. 29–30), in our own words; part (e) is our addition.

A one-turn circular loop of radius $r = 50$ cm, of negligible resistance, is connected in series, through two leads, with a resistor $R = 20\ \Omega$. A uniform magnetic field, perpendicular to the loop, fills the loop. Take $z$ out of the page, with the loop in the page. The positive sense round the circuit is counterclockwise as seen from the front. The field's $z$ component $B_z(t)$ rises steadily from 0 at $t = 0$ to 5.0 T at $t = 2.0$ s, stays at 5.0 T until $t = 4.0$ s, falls steadily to 0 at $t = 8.0$ s, and stays zero after that.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Which expression gives the emf $\varepsilon$ in the circuit, counted positive in the counterclockwise sense?

<Hint>

The counterclockwise sense is right-handed about $+\hat z$. Take the area vector along $+\hat z$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="The emf is"

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Magnetized iron and a paramagnetic rod" points={14}>

Parts (a) and (b) come from the notes' Additional Problem 9.13.9 (Chapter 9, p. 66), part (c) from Additional Problem 9.13.13 (Chapter 9, p. 68), in our own words; part (d) is our addition.

A disc of iron is 1.00 mm thick and has a radius of 1.00 cm. Each iron atom has the magnetic dipole moment $1.8 \times 10^{-23}$ A m². Iron has a molar mass of 55.85 g and a density of 7.9 g/cm³. Assume that every atom's moment is lined up along the axis of the disc. Take Avogadro's number as $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ per mole.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the magnetization $M$ of the disc, in A/m.

<Hint>

First find the number of atoms per cubic metre: the density over the molar mass gives moles per volume.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$M =$" value="1.533e6" unit="A/m" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-magnetization" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Magnetization is dipole moment per unit volume: the number of atoms per volume times the moment of each.

<Step title="Atoms per volume">

$n = (7.9\ \text{g/cm}^3 / 55.85\ \text{g/mol}) \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1} = 8.52 \times 10^{22}$ per cm³, so $8.52 \times 10^{28}$ per m³. The disc's volume is $\pi (0.0100)^2 (0.00100) = 3.14 \times 10^{-7}$ m³, so it holds $2.68 \times 10^{22}$ atoms.

</Step>

<Step title="The magnetization">

$M = n\mu = 8.52 \times 10^{28} \times 1.8 \times 10^{-23} = 1.53 \times 10^6$ A/m, along the axis. With every atom lined up, this is about as strongly as iron can be magnetized, close to the estimate of $10^6$ A/m in Eq. (9.6.12) of the notes. (The notes give the same value.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

A single loop of wire wound round the edge of the disc should have the same dipole moment as the disc. What current $I$ must it carry, in amperes?

<Hint>

The disc's moment is $M$ times its volume; the loop's is $I$ times its area. The area is the same for both.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$I =$" value="1533" unit="A" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-equivalent-current" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Equal moments: $I\,\pi r^2 = M\,\pi r^2 h$.

<Step title="The current">

The areas cancel, leaving $I = Mh = 1.533 \times 10^6 \times 1.00 \times 10^{-3} = 1533$ A. The disc's moment is $M\pi r^2 h = 0.48$ A m². (The notes print 1525 A, which is within 1% of ours, from rounded values.)

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

$Mh$ is a current per unit of height times the height: the surface current $K = M$ of Eq. (9.6.4) flowing round the rim. A permanent magnet acts like a short solenoid carrying a very large current, here 1.5 kA in a disc 1 mm thick.

</Step>

</Solution>

$\Delta B = \chi_m B_0 = 2.7 \times 10^{-4} \times 2.61 \times 10^{-3} = 7.06 \times 10^{-7}$ T, or 0.706 μT: less than a thousandth of a percent of the field. The rod's magnetization is $M = \chi_m B_0/\mu_0 = \chi_m n I = 0.56$ A/m.

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Lenz's law, eddy currents and a transformer" points={18}>

Two circular loops, A above B, lie in parallel horizontal planes, one above the other on a common vertical axis. Loop A carries a current that runs counterclockwise as seen from above.

<Answer id="p5-a" label="When A's current decreases"

**The idea.** Lenz's law for the flux through B, then the force between two parallel currents.

<Step title="The induced current">

A's counterclockwise current makes an upward field through B. As it decreases, the upward flux through B falls, so B's current makes an upward field of its own: counterclockwise from above, the same sense as A's.

Parallel currents attract, so the loops attract. That too opposes the change: drawing B towards A would raise the flux through it.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Hint>

The upward flux through B now grows.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="When A's current increases"

    "B's current is clockwise, and the loops repel",
    "B's current is counterclockwise, and the loops repel",
    "B's current is counterclockwise, and the loops attract",
    "B's current is clockwise, and the loops attract"

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same reasoning with the change reversed.

<Step title="The current and the force">
