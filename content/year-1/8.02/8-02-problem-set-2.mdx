## What you need from the lessons

Charge spread out is described by a density: $\lambda$ per unit length, $\sigma$ per unit area or $\rho$ per unit volume. A small piece of it carries the charge $dq$ and has its own Coulomb field; the field of the whole is the sum of these, an integral, with $r$ the distance from the piece to the point, $\hat r$ the unit vector from the piece to the point, and $ds$, $dA$ or $dV$ the length, area or volume of the piece:

<Formula label="Field of a continuous charge" tex="\vec E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int \frac{dq}{r^2}\,\hat r = k_e \int \frac{dq}{r^2}\,\hat r, \qquad dq = \lambda\,ds, \quad \sigma\,dA \quad\text{or}\quad \rho\,dV" cite="mit" at="p. 1" />

Two results of the notes help in Problem 4. On its axis, at the distance $z$ from its centre, a ring of radius $R$ and charge $Q$, and a disc of radius $R$ with the charge $\sigma$ per unit area, have the fields

<Formula label="Ring and disc, on the axis" tex="E_z = \frac{k_e Q z}{(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}\ \text{(ring)}, \qquad E_z = \frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}\left(1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + R^2}}\right)\ \text{(disc)}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (2.10.14) and (2.10.17), pp. 21 and 23" />

The potential difference between two points is minus the line integral of the field. A point charge $q$ has the potential below at the distance $r$ (zero far away), and potentials add as plain numbers:

<Formula label="Potential difference and the potential of point charges" tex="\Delta V = V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \vec E \cdot d\vec s, \qquad V = k_e \sum_i \frac{q_i}{r_i}" cite="reading2" at="Eqs. (3.1.9) and (3.3.4), pp. 3 and 6" />

<Formula label="Potential of a continuous charge" tex="V = k_e \int \frac{dq}{r}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (3.4.2), p. 9" />

The field is minus the gradient of the potential:

<Formula label="Field from the potential" tex="\vec E = -\nabla V = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\,\hat i + \frac{\partial V}{\partial y}\,\hat j + \frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\,\hat k\right)" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (3.5.5), p. 9" />

Bringing point charges together from far apart takes the work $U$, the sum over every pair, each pair counted once; moving a charge $q$ through the potential difference $\Delta V$ takes $q\,\Delta V$:

<Formula label="Energy of a group of charges" tex="U = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}} + \frac{q_1 q_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{q_2 q_3}{r_{23}}\right)" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (3.3.7), p. 8" />

Here $k_e = 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$; where a number is needed, $k_e = 8.99 \times 10^9$ N m²/C².

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Densities of a tube and of a rod of charge" points={8} mit="Problem 1, pp. 2 and 3">

A thin cylindrical tube has the length $L$ and the radius $R$, with $L \gg R$. The total charge $Q$ is spread evenly over its curved side; its two flat ends carry no charge.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Find the surface charge density $\sigma$ of the tube, in terms of $Q$, $R$ and $L$.

<Hint>

Charge per unit area: what is the area of the curved side? Unroll it into a flat rectangle.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$\sigma =$" expr="Q/(2*pi*R*L)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 5], R: [0.01, 0.5], L: [1, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q/(2*pi*R*L + 2*pi*R^2)", hint: "That spreads the charge over the two flat ends as well. Here the ends carry no charge." },
    { expr: "Q/(pi*R*L)", hint: "The curved side unrolls into a rectangle of length L and width 2πR, the length of the rim, not πR." },
    { expr: "Q/(pi*R^2*L)", hint: "That divides by the volume of the cylinder. The charge sits on a surface, so divide by an area." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A uniform surface density is the charge divided by the area that carries it.

The curved side unrolls into a rectangle of length $L$ and width $2\pi R$:

<Formula label="Surface density of the tube" tex="\sigma = \frac{Q}{2\pi R L}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, Question 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Check: coulombs over m², the unit of a surface density.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

Seen from a distance much larger than $R$, the tube looks like a line of charge. Find its charge per unit length $\lambda$, in terms of $Q$ and $L$.

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$\lambda =$" expr="Q/L"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 5], L: [1, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q*L", hint: "A density is the charge divided by the length, not multiplied by it: check the unit, C/m." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** From far away only the length counts.

The charge $Q$ is spread evenly along the length $L$, so $\lambda = Q/L$, in C/m.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Write $\lambda$ in terms of $\sigma$ and $R$.

<Hint>

A slice of the tube of length $ds$ is a thin ring. How much charge does it carry, in terms of $\sigma$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$\lambda =$" expr="2*pi*R*sigma"
  vars={{ sigma: [0.1, 5], R: [0.01, 0.5] }}
  names={{ sigma: "\\sigma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi*R^2*sigma", hint: "That is σ times the area of the cross-section. The charge sits only on the rim, whose length is 2πR." },
    { expr: "sigma/(2*pi*R)", hint: "Check the unit: σ is in C/m² and λ in C/m, so σ must be multiplied by a length." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One unit of length of the tube carries the charge on a ring of width 1.

A slice of length $ds$ has the area $2\pi R\,ds$ and the charge $\sigma\,2\pi R\,ds$, so

<Formula label="Line density from surface density" tex="\lambda = 2\pi R\,\sigma" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, Question 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Check: with $\sigma$ from part (a), $2\pi R\,Q/(2\pi R L) = Q/L$, the result of part (b). The unit is (C/m²) m = C/m.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Now the charge $Q$ fills a solid cylinder of the same length $L$ and radius $R$ evenly. Find its volume charge density $\rho$.

<Hint>

Divide by the volume of the cylinder.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="$\rho =$" expr="Q/(pi*R^2*L)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 5], R: [0.01, 0.5], L: [1, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q/(2*pi*R*L)", hint: "That is the surface density of the tube in part (a). The charge now fills the volume πR²L." },
    { expr: "3*Q/(4*pi*R^3)", hint: "That is the volume of a sphere of radius R. The cylinder has the volume πR²L." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in part (a), but per unit volume.

<Formula label="Volume density of the solid cylinder" tex="\rho = \frac{Q}{\pi R^2 L}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, Question 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Check: coulombs over m³.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={1}>

From far away the solid cylinder too looks like a line of charge (MIT asks for its $\lambda$ as well). Write that $\lambda$ in terms of $\rho$ and $R$.

<Answer id="p1-e" label="$\lambda =$" expr="pi*R^2*rho"
  vars={{ rho: [0.1, 5], R: [0.01, 0.5] }}
  names={{ rho: "\\rho" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*pi*R*rho", hint: "That multiplies by the length of the rim. Here the charge fills the whole cross-section, of area πR²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A slice of length $ds$ is now a thin disc, of volume $\pi R^2\,ds$.

Its charge is $\rho\,\pi R^2\,ds$, so

<Formula label="Line density from volume density" tex="\lambda = \pi R^2 \rho" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, Question 2(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Check: with $\rho$ from part (d), $\pi R^2\,Q/(\pi R^2 L) = Q/L$, the same line density as the tube's, since from far away only the charge per unit length shows. The unit is (C/m³) m² = C/m.

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A charged wire, seen along its axis" points={12} mit="Problem 2, pp. 3 and 4">

A straight wire of length $L$ carries the positive charge $Q$, spread evenly with the charge per unit length $\lambda = Q/L$. A point $P$ lies on the line of the wire, outside it, at the distance $a$ from its nearer end. (Our choice of axes: the wire runs along the $x$-axis from $x = 0$ to $x = L$, and $P$ is at $x = L + a$.)

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Which integral gives the field at $P$? (By symmetry it points along the $x$-axis.)

<Hint>

The piece of wire between $x$ and $x + dx$ carries $dq = \lambda\,dx$. How far is it from $P$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="The field at $P$ is $E_x =$"
  choices={[
    "k_e λ times the integral of dx/x², from x = 0 to L",
    "k_e λ times the integral of dx/(L + a − x)³, from x = 0 to L",
    "k_e λ times the integral of dx/(L + a − x)², from x = 0 to L",
    "k_e λ times the integral of dx/(a + x)², from x = 0 to L"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow the session's steps: write $dq$, find its distance from $P$, then add.

The piece at $x$ carries $dq = \lambda\,dx$ and lies the distance $L + a - x$ from $P$, to its left, so its field at $P$ points along $+x$ (for $\lambda > 0$):

<Formula label="The field on the axis as an integral" tex="E_x = k_e \int_0^{L} \frac{\lambda\,dx}{(L + a - x)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Check: the nearest piece, at $x = L$, is the distance $a$ away, and the farthest, at $x = 0$, the distance $L + a$. The other choices measure the distance from the wrong place, or use the wrong power of the distance.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Evaluate the integral: find the field $E$ at $P$, in terms of $k_e$, $Q$, $L$ and $a$.

<Hint>

Substitute $u = L + a - x$, the distance from the piece to $P$. Which values does $u$ run between?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$E =$" expr="ke*Q/(a*(a + L))"
  vars={{ ke: [1, 10], Q: [0.5, 5], L: [0.1, 2], a: [0.1, 2] }}
  names={{ ke: "k_e" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "ke*Q/a^2", hint: "That puts all the charge at the nearer end. The far pieces are further away and push less." },
    { expr: "ke*Q/(a + L/2)^2", hint: "That puts all the charge at the middle of the wire. Because of the 1/r², the near pieces count for more than the far ones, so the true field is a little larger." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $u = L + a - x$ the integrand is $1/u^2$, whose integral is $-1/u$.

<Step title="The integral">

As $x$ runs from 0 to $L$, $u$ runs from $L + a$ down to $a$, and $du = -dx$:

<Formula label="The field on the axis" tex="E_x = k_e\lambda \int_a^{L + a} \frac{du}{u^2} = k_e\lambda\left(\frac1a - \frac{1}{L + a}\right) = \frac{k_e \lambda L}{a(L + a)} = \frac{k_e Q}{a(L + a)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Far away, for $a \gg L$, the field is $k_eQ/a^2$: the wire looks like a point charge. The unit is (N m²/C²)(C)/m² = N/C. (This is the same rod the lesson works out, Eq. (2.10.3) of the notes, where the distance from the end has another name.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Now shrink the wire, $L \to 0$, keeping its charge $Q$. What happens to your answer?

<Answer id="p2-c" label="As $L \to 0$ with $Q$ fixed,"
  choices={[
    "E tends to k_e Q/a², the field of a point charge Q at the distance a",
    "E grows without limit, since the density λ = Q/L grows without limit",
    "E tends to zero, since the wire itself shrinks away to nothing at all",
    "E tends to k_e Q/(2a²), as half the charge is always beyond the middle"
  ]}
  answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Set $L = 0$ in the result, keeping $Q$ (not $\lambda$) fixed.

$k_eQ/(a(L + a)) \to k_eQ/a^2$ as $L \to 0$: all the charge now sits at one point, the distance $a$ from $P$, and Coulomb's law for a point charge comes back. That the density $\lambda$ grows does not matter, since the length it multiplies shrinks: their product $Q$ stays fixed. This is the session's third objective, checking a result against a limit we already know.

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A charged rod, seen from its side" points={12} mit="Problem 3, p. 4">

A straight rod of length $L$ carries the charge $\lambda$ per unit length, spread evenly. A point $P$ lies on its perpendicular bisector, at the distance $y$ from the middle of the rod. (Our choice of axes: the rod runs along the $x$-axis from $x = -L/2$ to $x = L/2$, and $P$ is at $(0, y)$.)

<Part label="a" points={4}>

The piece of rod between $x$ and $x + dx$ makes a small field at $P$. Write the $y$ component of that field as $dE_y = f(x)\,dx$ and type $f(x)$, in terms of $k_e$, $\lambda$, $x$ and $y$.

<Hint>

The piece is the distance $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ from $P$. Its field points along the line from the piece to $P$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The fraction of that field along $y$ is $y$ divided by the distance.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$dE_y/dx =$" expr="ke*lam*y/(x^2 + y^2)^(3/2)"
  vars={{ ke: [1, 10], lam: [0.5, 5], x: [-1, 1], y: [0.1, 2] }}
  names={{ ke: "k_e", lam: "\\lambda" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "ke*lam/(x^2 + y^2)", hint: "That is the full size of the piece's field. Only the fraction y/√(x² + y²) of it points along y." },
    { expr: "ke*lam*x/(x^2 + y^2)^(3/2)", hint: "That is the x component, the one that cancels between the pieces at x and −x. The y component has y in place of x." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The session's steps: $dq = \lambda\,dx$, its distance and direction, then the component that survives.

<Step title="One piece">

The piece at $x$ carries $dq = \lambda\,dx$ and lies the distance $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ from $P$. Its field points from $(x, 0)$ to $(0, y)$, along $(-x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j)/r$:

<Formula label="The field of one piece of the rod" tex="d\vec E = k_e\,\frac{\lambda\,dx}{x^2 + y^2}\cdot\frac{-x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}, \qquad dE_y = \frac{k_e \lambda\,y\,dx}{(x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The integral, and the symmetry">

The whole field is the integral from $x = -L/2$ to $L/2$, which is MIT's question:

<Formula label="The field on the bisector as an integral" tex="E_y = k_e \lambda y \int_{-L/2}^{L/2} \frac{dx}{(x^2 + y^2)^{3/2}}, \qquad E_x = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

The $x$ parts cancel: the pieces at $x$ and $-x$ carry the same charge and lie mirror-wise.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

(Our addition.) Evaluate the integral: find $E_y$, in terms of $k_e$, $\lambda$, $L$ and $y$.

<Hint>

Substitute $x = y\tan\theta$. Then $x^2 + y^2 = y^2/\cos^2\theta$ and $dx = y\,d\theta/\cos^2\theta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$E_y =$" expr="ke*lam*L/(y*sqrt(y^2 + L^2/4))"
  vars={{ ke: [1, 10], lam: [0.5, 5], L: [0.1, 2], y: [0.1, 3] }}
  names={{ ke: "k_e", lam: "\\lambda" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*ke*lam/y", hint: "That is the field of an endless line. It is right only close to a long rod, for y much smaller than L." },
    { expr: "ke*lam*L/y^2", hint: "That treats the whole charge λL as a point at the middle of the rod. It is right only far away, for y much larger than L." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The substitution $x = y\tan\theta$ turns the integrand into $\cos\theta$.

<Step title="The integral">

With $x = y\tan\theta$, the integrand becomes $\cos\theta\,d\theta/y^2$, and $\sin\theta = x/\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$:

<Formula label="The field on the perpendicular bisector" tex="E_y = \frac{k_e\lambda}{y}\Big[\sin\theta\Big] = \frac{k_e\lambda}{y}\left[\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}}\right]_{-L/2}^{L/2} = \frac{k_e \lambda L}{y\sqrt{y^2 + L^2/4}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 4 (our addition, our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $y \gg L$ the square root is $y$ and $E_y \to k_e\lambda L/y^2$, the field of a point charge $\lambda L$. For $y \ll L$ the square root is $L/2$ and $E_y \to 2k_e\lambda/y$, the field of an endless line, which falls off only as $1/y$. The notes and the lesson reach the same result, Eq. (2.10.9) (there with $\ell$ for $L$).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

(Our addition, the session's third objective.) Shrink the rod, $L \to 0$, keeping its charge $Q = \lambda L$. What does $E_y$ tend to?

<Answer id="p3-c" label="As $L \to 0$ with $\lambda L$ fixed,"
  choices={[
    "E_y tends to 2k_e λ/y, the field of an endless line of charge",
    "E_y tends to zero, since the rod shrinks away to nothing at all",
    "E_y grows without limit, since the density λ grows without limit",
    "E_y tends to k_e Q/y², the field of a point charge Q"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put $L = 0$ under the square root, keeping $\lambda L = Q$.

$k_e(\lambda L)/(y\sqrt{y^2 + L^2/4}) \to k_eQ/(y \cdot y) = k_eQ/y^2$: the field of a point charge, as for the wire of Problem 2. The endless-line field is the opposite limit, $L \to \infty$ with $\lambda$ fixed.

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A charged tube and a charged cylinder, on the axis" points={10}>

From the course notes, Chapter 2, Additional Problem 2.15.6 (pp. 41 and 42). A cylinder of radius $R$ and height $h$ stands on the $xy$ plane, with its axis along $z$: its bottom is at $z = 0$ and its top at $z = h$. A point $P$ lies on the axis above the top, at the height $z > h$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

The total charge $Q$ is spread evenly over the curved side only (a thin tube). Find the field $E_z$ at $P$, in terms of $k_e$, $Q$, $R$, $h$ and $z$.

<Hint>

Cut the tube into rings of height $dz'$ at the heights $z'$. Each carries $dQ = Q\,dz'/h$ and is the distance $z - z'$ below $P$. Use the ring field from "What you need".

</Hint>

<Hint>

Substitute $u = z - z'$; the integral of $u/(R^2 + u^2)^{3/2}$ is $-1/\sqrt{R^2 + u^2}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$E_z =$" expr="ke*Q/h*(1/sqrt(R^2 + (z - h)^2) - 1/sqrt(R^2 + z^2))"
  vars={{ ke: [1, 10], Q: [0.5, 5], R: [0.1, 1], h: [0.1, 1], z: [1.2, 3] }}
  names={{ ke: "k_e" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "ke*Q/h*(1/sqrt(R^2 + z^2) - 1/sqrt(R^2 + (z - h)^2))", hint: "That is negative. The top ring is the nearest and pushes hardest, so its term, with z − h, comes with the plus sign." },
    { expr: "ke*Q/(z - h/2)^2", hint: "That treats the tube as a point charge at its centre. It is right only far away; close by, the rings sit off to the side of the line to P." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As the notes suggest, build the tube from rings and add their axial fields.

<Step title="One ring">

The ring at the height $z'$ has the charge $dQ = Q\,dz'/h$ and sits the distance $z - z'$ below $P$:

<Formula label="The field of one ring of the tube" tex="dE_z = \frac{k_e Q}{h}\,\frac{(z - z')\,dz'}{\left(R^2 + (z - z')^2\right)^{3/2}}" cite="reading1" at="Additional Problem 2.15.6(a), p. 41 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="All the rings">

With $u = z - z'$, running from $z$ (the bottom ring) to $z - h$ (the top ring):

<Hint>

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Can two electric field lines cross?

<Answer id="p10-a" label="Field lines"
  choices={[
    "never cross: where the field is not zero, it has one direction at each point",
    "cross wherever the fields of two equal charges meet at an angle to each other",
    "cross halfway between two equal charges of opposite sign, on the axis",
    "may cross, since the fields of different charges each have their own lines"
  ]}
  answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A field line follows the direction of the total field.

At every point the total field (the sum of all the charges' fields) has one direction, so only one field line passes through it. Two lines crossing would give the point two directions. (Where the field is zero, it has no direction and no line passes through.)

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

What is the difference between the electric potential and the electric potential energy?

<Answer id="p10-b" label="The difference:"
  choices={[
    "none: they are two names for one quantity, measured in joules in both cases",
    "the potential is measured in joules, and the energy, its value per coulomb, in volts",
    "V belongs to a point, in J/C; U = qV to a charge there, in J",
    "the potential exists only near a point charge, while the energy exists everywhere"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One is per unit charge, the other is for the charge you place.

The potential $V$ is the potential energy per unit test charge: a property of the place, set by the charges that make the field, in volts (J/C). A charge $q$ placed there has the potential energy $U = qV$, in joules, which depends on $q$ too.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

A uniform field points along the $x$-axis. In which directions can a charge be moved without any work being done on it?

<Answer id="p10-c" label="Without work, the charge can move"
  choices={[
    "along +x only, so that the charge moves with the field",
    "along +x or −x, parallel to the field lines",
    "in no direction: any motion in a field takes some work",
    "in any direction at right angles to x"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The work is $q\vec E \cdot d\vec s$, which is zero for a step at right angles to the field.

Any step in the $y$ or $z$ direction, or any mix of them, is perpendicular to $\vec E$. Such a plane $x = \text{constant}$ is an equipotential surface: $V$ is the same all over it.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Why do equipotential surfaces always cross the field lines at right angles?

<Answer id="p10-d" label="Because"
  choices={[
    "the field is always at its strongest along an equipotential surface",
    "a step along the surface leaves V unchanged, so E · ds = 0 for every such step",
    "field lines always start and end on equipotential surfaces, which they meet at right angles",
    "charges cannot move along an equipotential surface, so no field acts there"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $dV = -\vec E \cdot d\vec s$.

On an equipotential surface $V$ does not change, so $dV = 0$ for every step $d\vec s$ in the surface, hence $\vec E \cdot d\vec s = 0$: the field has no part along the surface, and points across it. Equivalently, $\vec E = -\nabla V$, and a gradient is perpendicular to the level surfaces.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

The field inside a hollow sphere with an even charge on its surface is zero. Is the potential inside zero too?

<Answer id="p10-e" label="Inside the sphere, the potential is"
  choices={[
    "zero everywhere inside, since a zero field means a zero potential",
    "infinite at the centre, as for a point charge, and finite elsewhere",
    "the same everywhere, equal to its value k_e Q/R on the surface",
    "largest at the centre and falling smoothly towards the surface value"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A zero field means the potential does not change, not that it is zero.

With $\vec E = 0$ inside, $\Delta V = -\int \vec E \cdot d\vec s = 0$ between any two points inside, so $V$ is constant there. It equals its value on the surface, which for a sphere of charge $Q$ and radius $R$ is $k_eQ/R$ (with $V = 0$ far away). A charge moved about inside feels no force, but it still has the potential energy $qk_eQ/R$.

</Solution>

</Part>
