The first hour of Session 4 is about working in groups and the first experiment, on visualizing fields (slides 1–7). The second hour introduces the **electric potential**, starting, as so often, from gravity (slides 8–10).

## Gravity: work and potential energy

The work done by Earth's gravity on a mass $m$ moving from $A$ to $B$ is a line integral (course notes, Section 3.1, p. 1; slides 11–12):

<Formula label="Work done by gravity" tex="W_g = \int_A^B \vec F_g \cdot d\vec s = \int_{r_A}^{r_B} -\frac{GMm}{r^2}\,dr = GMm\left(\frac{1}{r_B} - \frac{1}{r_A}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.1.3), p. 1" />

It depends only on the end points, not on the path. Near the surface, with $g$ constant, $W_g = -mg(y_B - y_A)$ (Section 3.1, p. 2; slide 13). A force whose work around every closed loop is zero, $\oint \vec F \cdot d\vec s = 0$, is **conservative** (Section 3.1, p. 2), as in [8.01](/courses/8-01/potential-energy). It has a potential energy (Section 3.1, p. 2; slide 14):

<Formula label="Potential energy" tex="\Delta U = U_B - U_A = -\int_A^B \vec F \cdot d\vec s = -W" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.1.6), p. 2" />

For gravity, $U_g = -GMm/r + U_0$, or $mgy + U_0$ near the surface. Only differences matter, so the constant $U_0$ is set by a choice of reference point, usually $U = 0$ at infinity (Section 3.1, pp. 2–3; slide 14). The work done *by* the field and the work an external agent such as you must do differ only in sign: $W_g = -W_{\text{ext}}$ (Section 3.1, pp. 1–2). Dividing by the mass gives the **gravitational potential**, $\Delta V_g = \Delta U_g/m = -\int \vec g \cdot d\vec s$: as force becomes field, energy becomes potential, a property of one point rather than of a pair (Section 3.1, p. 3; slide 15).

## The electric potential

Coulomb's force is also inverse-square and conservative, so in exactly the same way (course notes, Section 3.1, p. 3; slides 18–19):

<Formula label="Electric potential difference" tex="\Delta V = V_B - V_A = -\int_A^B \frac{\vec F_e}{q_0} \cdot d\vec s = -\int_A^B \vec E \cdot d\vec s" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.1.9), p. 3" />

The potential is not the potential energy: the energy of a charge $q$ is $U = qV$, and the work an external agent (such as you) must do to move it from $A$ to $B$ is $q\Delta V$ (Section 3.1, p. 3; slide 19). Potential is measured in **volts**, 1 V = 1 J/C. At atomic scales the **electron volt** is handier: the energy an electron gains crossing 1 V, $1\text{ eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ J (Section 3.1, pp. 3–4).

The picture (slides 20–23): charges **create** potential landscapes, hills around positive charges and pits around negative ones; other charges **feel** them through $U = qV$.

**A uniform field.** Moving a distance $d$ along a uniform field $E_0$ gives $\Delta V = -E_0 d$: field lines point from high to low potential, and a positive charge loses potential energy moving along the field, like a falling mass (Section 3.2, p. 4). Moving across the field costs nothing: points on a line perpendicular to $\vec E$ share one potential (Section 3.2, p. 5).

## Point charges

For a point charge $Q$, with $\hat r \cdot d\vec s = dr$, the integral is the same as for gravity (course notes, Section 3.3, pp. 5–6; slide 26). Taking $V = 0$ at infinity:

<Formula label="Potential of a point charge" tex="V(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q}{r} = \frac{k_e Q}{r}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.3.3), p. 6" />

Several charges: the potentials add, and since they are numbers, not vectors, this is easier than adding fields (Section 3.3, p. 6):

<Formula label="Superposition of potentials" tex="V(\vec r) = k_e \sum_i \frac{q_i}{r_i}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.3.4), p. 6" />

## The energy of a configuration

How much work does it take to assemble charges, bringing them in from infinity? The first is free; the second, $q_2$, is moved into the potential of $q_1$ (course notes, Section 3.3.1, p. 7; slide 34):

<Formula label="Energy of two charges" tex="U_{12} = q_2 V_1 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.3.5), p. 7" />

It is positive for like charges (work against repulsion) and negative for opposite ones. A third charge feels both, and the total is a sum over pairs (Section 3.3.1, pp. 7–8; slide 35):

<Formula label="Energy of three charges" tex="U = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{q_1 q_2}{r_{12}} + \frac{q_1 q_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{q_2 q_3}{r_{23}}\right) = U_{12} + U_{13} + U_{23}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.3.7), p. 8" />

For $N$ charges, sum over all distinct pairs (Section 3.3.1, p. 8).

## Continuous charge

For a continuous distribution, add up $dV = k_e\,dq/r$ (course notes, Section 3.4, p. 9):

<Formula label="Potential of a continuous distribution" tex="V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int \frac{dq}{r}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.4.2), p. 9" />

It is simpler than the [field integrals](/courses/8-02/continuous-charge-distributions), with no directions to track. On the axis of a ring of radius $R$ and charge $Q$, every piece is at the same distance, so $V = k_e Q/\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}$ (Section 3.5, Example 3.2, pp. 14–15); for a disc of density $\sigma$, $V = (\sigma/2\varepsilon_0)(\sqrt{R^2 + z^2} - |z|)$ (Example 3.3, pp. 15–16); and for a rod of length $\ell$ there is a logarithm, which close to a long rod becomes $V \approx (\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(\ell/y)$ (Example 3.1, pp. 12–14). Far away the ring and the disc both tend to the point-charge value $k_e Q/|z|$ (pp. 15–16).

## The field from the potential

Over a small step, $dV = -\vec E \cdot d\vec s = -(E_x\,dx + E_y\,dy + E_z\,dz)$, so each component of $\vec E$ is minus a partial derivative of $V$ (course notes, Section 3.5, p. 9; slides 30–31):

<Formula label="Field as minus the gradient" tex="\vec E = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\,\hat i + \frac{\partial V}{\partial y}\,\hat j + \frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\,\hat k\right) = -\nabla V" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.5.5), p. 9" />

using the [gradient](/courses/18-02/gradient). For example, differentiating the ring's potential in $z$ gives back its field $k_e Qz/(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}$ (Section 3.5, p. 15). Since the gradient is perpendicular to level surfaces, $\vec E$ is perpendicular to the **equipotentials**, the surfaces of constant $V$, and points from high to low potential. Equipotentials are spheres around a point charge and planes across a uniform field; moving a charge along one takes no work (Section 3.5.1, pp. 10–12). A topographic map is the gravitational version: its contour lines are equipotentials of $V_g = gz$ (Section 3.5.1, p. 12).

<WorkedExample title="Three charges on a line">
<Problem>
Charges $q_1 = +2$ nC at $x = 0$, $q_2 = -1$ nC at $x = 0.3$ m and $q_3 = +1$ nC at $x = 0.6$ m. (a) Find the potential at $x = 0.9$ m. (b) Find the energy needed to assemble them from far apart. (c) An electron is accelerated from rest through 100 V. Find its kinetic energy.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Potential">
$V = k_e(q_1/0.9 + q_2/0.6 + q_3/0.3) = 8.99 \times (2.222 - 1.667 + 3.333) \approx 35.0$ V (with charges in nC, $k_e q$ is in V m).
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Energy">
$U = k_e(q_1q_2/0.3 + q_1q_3/0.6 + q_2q_3/0.3) = (8.99 \times 10^9)(10^{-18})(-6.667 + 3.333 - 3.333) \approx -6.0 \times 10^{-8}$ J. Negative: the configuration releases energy as it forms.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) An electron through 100 V">
It gains $e \times 100\text{ V} = 100$ eV $= 100 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 1.6 \times 10^{-17}$ J.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Energy in cosmology">
The gravitational potential energy $-GMm/r$ is the key to the simplest derivation of the expansion of the universe: a galaxy at the edge of an expanding ball of matter has kinetic energy $\frac12 mv^2$ and potential energy $-GMm/r$, and the sign of their sum decides its fate: positive or zero, the expansion goes on for ever; negative, it ends in collapse. Particle energies in the early universe are quoted in electron volts.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Conservative forces have potential energy: $\Delta U = -\int \vec F \cdot d\vec s$. Per unit charge it is the potential, $\Delta V = -\int \vec E \cdot d\vec s$, in volts.
- Point charge: $V = k_e Q/r$; potentials add as numbers. Energy of a charge: $U = qV$.
- Assembling charges stores $\sum k_e q_iq_j/r_{ij}$ over pairs.
- $\vec E = -\nabla V$: perpendicular to equipotentials, from high to low potential.
