[An RLC circuit](/courses/8-02/lc-and-rlc-circuits) left alone rings down. Session 26 drives it with an alternating voltage, and finds **resonance** (slides 1–4).

## Driven oscillations

Push a mass on a spring with a force $F_0\cos\omega t$ (slides 5–8): $m\,d^2x/dt^2 + kx = F_0\cos\omega t$. After the start, it moves at the **driving** frequency, $x = x_{\text{max}}\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$, not its natural one. The amplitude $x_{\text{max}}$ depends on how close $\omega$ is to the natural frequency $\omega_0$, peaking near it: resonance, as in swings, some cars, and musical instruments (slides 8–11). The RLC circuit is the electrical version: inductor as mass, capacitor as spring, the AC source as the driving force, charge as position and current as velocity (slides 12–13).

## AC sources and single elements

An **AC source** gives $V(t) = V_0\sin\omega t$, with amplitude $V_0$, angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$, frequency $f$ in hertz (course notes, Section 12.1, p. 1; slide 16). The current oscillates at the same frequency, with its own amplitude and phase: $I(t) = I_0\sin(\omega t - \varphi)$ (Eq. 12.1.2, p. 2; slide 17).

**Resistor.** $I = V/R$: $I_0 = V_0/R$, in phase with the voltage, $\varphi = 0$ (Section 12.2.1, p. 2; slide 18).

**Capacitor.** $Q = CV$, so $I = dQ/dt = \omega CV_0\cos\omega t$: the current **leads** the voltage by a quarter cycle, $\varphi = -\pi/2$ (Section 12.2.3, p. 7; slide 19). Its amplitude is $V_0/X_C$, with the **capacitive reactance**

<Formula label="Capacitive reactance" tex="X_C = \frac{1}{\omega C}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (12.2.23), p. 7" />

**Inductor.** $L\,dI/dt = V_0\sin\omega t$ gives $I = -(V_0/\omega L)\cos\omega t$: the current **lags** by a quarter cycle, $\varphi = +\pi/2$ (Section 12.2.2, pp. 5–6; slide 20), with the **inductive reactance**

<Formula label="Inductive reactance" tex="X_L = \omega L" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (12.2.16), p. 5" />

Reactances are measured in ohms like resistance, but depend on frequency: a capacitor blocks low frequencies, an inductor high ones (pp. 5–7; slide 21).

**RMS values.** Over a cycle the average of $\sin^2$ is $\frac12$, so the average power in a resistor is $\frac12 I_0^2R = I_{\text{rms}}^2R$ with $I_{\text{rms}} = I_0/\sqrt 2$ and likewise $V_{\text{rms}} = V_0/\sqrt 2$. US wall sockets supply 120 V rms at 60 Hz (Section 12.2.1, p. 4).

## Phasors

A **phasor** is an arrow whose length is the amplitude and which rotates counterclockwise at angular speed $\omega$; its projection on the vertical axis is the instantaneous value (course notes, Section 12.2.1, p. 3; slides 23–27). Draw the current and the voltage as phasors and their phase difference is the angle between them: together for a resistor, current a quarter turn ahead for a capacitor, a quarter turn behind for an inductor.

## The driven series RLC circuit

Put $R$, $L$ and $C$ in series with the source (course notes, Section 12.3, pp. 8–10; slides 29–33). One current $I(t) = I_0\sin(\omega t - \varphi)$ flows through all three. The voltage amplitudes are $I_0R$, $I_0X_L$ and $I_0X_C$, at different phases, and the source voltage is their sum **as phasors** (slide 32; Eq. 12.3.12, p. 10). The resistor's phasor is along the current, the inductor's a quarter turn ahead and the capacitor's a quarter turn behind, so reading the right triangle (pp. 10–11; slide 33):

<Formula label="Impedance and phase" tex="I_0 = \frac{V_0}{Z}, \qquad Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}, \qquad \tan\varphi = \frac{X_L - X_C}{R}" cite="reading2" at="slide 33" />

$Z$ is the **impedance**. The amplitudes do not simply add, $V_0 \neq V_{R0} + V_{L0} + V_{C0}$, because the voltages peak at different times (p. 11).

## Resonance

At low frequencies $X_C$ is large: the capacitor fills up and keeps the current low, and the current leads ($\varphi < 0$). At high frequencies $X_L$ dominates: the inductor fights the rapid changes, and the current lags. In between, $X_L = X_C$, the impedance is smallest, $Z = R$, and the current is largest, $I_0 = V_0/R$, in phase with the voltage (slides 36–38; course notes, Section 12.3.2, pp. 12–13):

<Formula label="Resonant frequency" tex="\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (12.3.17), p. 12" />

the same as the free LC frequency. The slides demonstrate this with a light bulb in an RLC circuit, which by these formulas glows brightest as the frequency sweeps through $\omega_0$ (the page's own description); Experiment 11 finds the resonance by watching the current (slides 39–42).

## Power and the width of the peak

The average power delivered is (course notes, Section 12.4, pp. 13–14)

<Formula label="Average power" tex="\langle P\rangle = I_{\text{rms}}V_{\text{rms}}\cos\varphi = I_{\text{rms}}^2R" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (12.4.4) and (12.4.6), p. 14" />

where $\cos\varphi = R/Z$ is the **power factor**; only the resistor absorbs energy on average. It is largest at resonance. The frequencies where the power falls to half its peak are a distance $\Delta\omega = R/L$ apart, so the **quality factor** $Q = \omega_0/\Delta\omega = \omega_0 L/R$ (not to be confused with the charge $Q$) measures how sharp the resonance is (Section 12.4.1, pp. 15–16).

<WorkedExample title="A resonant circuit">
<Problem>
A series circuit has $R = 10$ Ω, $L = 0.1$ H and $C = 10$ μF, driven with amplitude 10 V. (a) Find $\omega_0$, $f_0$ and the current amplitude at resonance. (b) At $\omega = 500$ rad/s, find $X_L$, $X_C$, $Z$, $I_0$ and $\varphi$. (c) Find $Q$ and the width of the peak. (d) What is the peak voltage of a 120 V rms socket?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Resonance">
$\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{(0.1)(10^{-5})} = 1000$ rad/s, $f_0 = \omega_0/2\pi \approx 159$ Hz, and $I_0 = V_0/R = 1$ A.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Below resonance">
$X_L = 500 \times 0.1 = 50$ Ω, $X_C = 1/(500 \times 10^{-5}) = 200$ Ω. $Z = \sqrt{10^2 + 150^2} \approx 150.3$ Ω, $I_0 = 10/150.3 \approx 0.067$ A, and $\tan\varphi = -150/10$, $\varphi \approx -86°$: capacitor-like, the current leads.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Sharpness">
$Q = \omega_0 L/R = 1000 \times 0.1/10 = 10$; $\Delta\omega = R/L = 100$ rad/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Peak voltage">
$V_0 = \sqrt 2 \times 120 \approx 170$ V.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A driven oscillator moves at the driving frequency, with an amplitude that peaks at resonance.
- Resistor: in phase. Capacitor: current leads, $X_C = 1/\omega C$. Inductor: current lags, $X_L = \omega L$.
- Series RLC: $I_0 = V_0/Z$, $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}$, $\tan\varphi = (X_L - X_C)/R$.
- Resonance at $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$: $Z = R$. Average power $I_{\text{rms}}V_{\text{rms}}\cos\varphi$; width $R/L$, $Q = \omega_0 L/R$.
