The last lecture gave the dot product, a way to find lengths and angles. This one finds areas and volumes, with two new tools: the determinant and the cross product (transcript, pp. 1, 3).

## Components along a direction

One more use of the dot product. Take a direction in space, given by a unit vector $\hat u$. The **component** of $\vec A$ along $\hat u$ is the length of the projection of $\vec A$ onto that direction: $|\vec A|\cos\theta$, by the right angle in the picture. Since $|\hat u| = 1$, this is a dot product (transcript, pp. 1–2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Component along a unit vector" tex="\text{component of } \vec A \text{ along } \hat u = |\vec A|\cos\theta = |\vec A|\,|\hat u|\cos\theta = \vec A \cdot \hat u" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

With $\hat u = \hat i = \langle 1, 0, 0\rangle$ this gives the first component, the $x$ component; with $\hat k$ it gives the $z$ component (p. 2). But any unit vector works. For a swinging pendulum, the useful directions are not $x$ and $y$ but the unit vector $\vec T$ tangent to the circle and the unit vector $\vec N$ normal to it. The component of the weight along $\vec T$ makes the mass swing back and forth; the component along $\vec N$ is what the lecture calls responsible for the tension in the string (pp. 2–3). (For a swinging mass the tension also has to supply the force that keeps it on its circle, so it is not just that component.)

## Area and the 2 × 2 determinant

To find the area of a polygon, cut it into triangles (p. 3). A triangle in the plane with sides $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ has area one half base times height: the base is $|\vec A|$, the height $|\vec B|\sin\theta$ (p. 4). The dot product gives a cosine, not a sine, and finding the sine from $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$ gives an ugly formula (p. 4; notes, p. 2).

The trick is a complementary angle. Rotate $\vec A$ by 90° counterclockwise to get $\vec A'$, with the same length. The angle $\theta'$ between $\vec A'$ and $\vec B$ is $\pi/2 - \theta$, so $\cos\theta' = \sin\theta$ and (p. 4):

<Formula label="Turning a sine into a cosine" tex="|\vec A|\,|\vec B|\sin\theta = |\vec A'|\,|\vec B|\cos\theta' = \vec A' \cdot \vec B" cite="reading2" at="pp. 4–5" />

Which vector is $\vec A'$? Turn the box with sides $a_1$ and $a_2$ on its side: the new $x$ component is $a_2$, pointing left, and the new $y$ component is $a_1$, so $\vec A' = \langle -a_2, a_1\rangle$ (clockwise would give $\langle a_2, -a_1\rangle$) (p. 5). Then $\vec A' \cdot \vec B = -a_2b_1 + a_1b_2$, which is the **determinant** of $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ (p. 5; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The 2 × 2 determinant" tex="\det(\vec A, \vec B) = \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ b_1 & b_2 \end{vmatrix} = a_1b_2 - a_2b_1" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

The determinant is the area of the **parallelogram** with sides $\vec A$ and $\vec B$; the triangle is half of it (p. 6). But an area is positive, and the determinant can have either sign: it is positive or negative according to whether $\vec B$ lies counterclockwise or clockwise from $\vec A$ (p. 6; notes, p. 2). So the honest statement is that the determinant is **plus or minus** the area, and the area is its absolute value (pp. 6–7).

## Determinants in space

In space, three vectors $\vec A$, $\vec B$, $\vec C$ have a 3 × 3 determinant, defined by **expansion along the first row**: each entry of the first row times the 2 × 2 determinant left after deleting that entry's row and column, with a minus sign on the middle term (transcript, p. 8; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The 3 × 3 determinant" tex="\begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} = a_1\begin{vmatrix} b_2 & b_3 \\ c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} - a_2\begin{vmatrix} b_1 & b_3 \\ c_1 & c_3 \end{vmatrix} + a_3\begin{vmatrix} b_1 & b_2 \\ c_1 & c_2 \end{vmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

Written out, that is six products. Why the minus sign? Without it the next theorem fails; the deeper reason is orientation: swapping two coordinate axes turns a right-handed system into a left-handed one, its mirror image (p. 9). The 2 × 2 determinant follows the same pattern, $a_1$ times $b_2$ minus $a_2$ times $b_1$, and the signs alternate in larger determinants too (more of this in 18.06, Linear Algebra) (pp. 8–9).

The theorem (p. 9; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Determinant and volume" tex="\det(\vec A, \vec B, \vec C) = \pm\,\text{volume of the parallelepiped with sides } \vec A, \vec B, \vec C" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

A **parallelepiped** is a box whose six faces are parallelograms (p. 9).

## The cross product

The **cross product** is defined only for two vectors in three-dimensional space, and it gives a **vector**, not a number like the dot product (transcript, p. 10; notes, p. 2). Its definition borrows the look of a determinant, with the unit vectors in the first row (pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="Cross product" tex="\vec A \times \vec B = \begin{vmatrix} \hat i & \hat j & \hat k \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a_2 & a_3 \\ b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix}\hat i - \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_3 \end{vmatrix}\hat j + \begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ b_1 & b_2 \end{vmatrix}\hat k" cite="reading2" at="pp. 10–11" />

A determinant should contain numbers, so the first form is only a way to remember the second; the expansion is the actual definition (pp. 10–11). (Write the × clearly, so it is not mistaken for a dot.)

Geometrically, a vector has a length and a direction, and both are remarkable (p. 11; notes, p. 2):

- **Length:** $|\vec A \times \vec B|$ is the area of the parallelogram in space with sides $\vec A$ and $\vec B$. No plus or minus this time, since a length is never negative. For a parallelogram given by coordinates, this is the simplest way to find its area (p. 11).
- **Direction:** perpendicular to the plane of the parallelogram. Of the two perpendicular directions, the **right-hand rule** picks one: point your right hand along $\vec A$, curl the fingers towards $\vec B$, and the thumb points along $\vec A \times \vec B$ (pp. 11–12).

Example: $\hat i \times \hat j$. The unit square in the $xy$-plane has area 1, the perpendicular direction is vertical, and the right hand gives up, so $\hat i \times \hat j = \hat k$ (p. 12). From the definition, with $\hat i = \langle 1, 0, 0\rangle$ and $\hat j = \langle 0, 1, 0\rangle$, the $\hat i$ and $\hat j$ coefficients are $0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 1 = 0$ and $-(1 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 0) = 0$, and the $\hat k$ coefficient is $1 \cdot 1 - 0 \cdot 0 = 1$: again $\hat k$ (pp. 12–13). For simple vectors the picture is quicker; for complicated ones, calculate (p. 13). (8.01 uses the cross product for [torque](/courses/8-01/torque-and-cross-product).)

## Volume and the triple product

The volume of a parallelepiped is the area of its base times its height (transcript, p. 14). Take the base to be the parallelogram with sides $\vec B$ and $\vec C$: its area is $|\vec B \times \vec C|$. The height is the component of $\vec A$ perpendicular to the base, $\vec A \cdot \hat n$, where $\hat n$ is a unit vector perpendicular to the base; the cross product gives that direction, and dividing by its length makes it a unit vector (pp. 14–15; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Base times height" tex="\text{volume} = |\vec B \times \vec C|\,(\vec A \cdot \hat n), \qquad \hat n = \frac{\vec B \times \vec C}{|\vec B \times \vec C|}" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 2, p. 2" />

The lengths cancel, leaving the **triple product** (p. 15). Like the determinant it can be negative, when $\vec A$ lies on the other side of the base from $\hat n$, so it is plus or minus the volume:

<Formula label="The triple product" tex="\vec A \cdot (\vec B \times \vec C) = \det(\vec A, \vec B, \vec C)" cite="reading2" at="p. 15" />

The order matters: $\vec A \cdot \vec B$ is a number and cannot be crossed with anything, so the cross product is done first (p. 15). The identity can also be checked by components: the first component of $\vec B \times \vec C$ is $\begin{vmatrix} b_2 & b_3 \\ c_2 & c_3 \end{vmatrix}$, the coefficient of $a_1$ in the determinant, and likewise for the others (p. 15). So the geometric route to volume still needs a determinant to compute it (p. 9).

<WorkedExample title="An area, a cross product and a volume">
<Problem>
(a) Find the area of the triangle in the plane with sides $\vec A = \langle 3, 1\rangle$ and $\vec B = \langle 1, 2\rangle$. (b) Find $\vec P \times \vec Q$ for $\vec P = \langle 1, 0, 2\rangle$ and $\vec Q = \langle 0, 3, 1\rangle$, check that it is perpendicular to both, and find the area of the parallelogram they span. (c) Find the volume of the parallelepiped with sides $\vec P$, $\vec Q$ and $\vec R = \langle 1, 1, 1\rangle$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A 2 × 2 determinant">
$\det(\vec A, \vec B) = 3 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot 1 = 5$, positive because $\vec B$ is counterclockwise from $\vec A$. The parallelogram has area 5, the triangle $5/2 = 2.5$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The cross product">
The $\hat i$ coefficient is $0 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 3 = -6$, the $\hat j$ coefficient is $-(1 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 0) = -1$, the $\hat k$ coefficient is $1 \cdot 3 - 0 \cdot 0 = 3$: $\vec P \times \vec Q = \langle -6, -1, 3\rangle$. Check: $\vec P \cdot \langle -6, -1, 3\rangle = -6 + 0 + 6 = 0$ and $\vec Q \cdot \langle -6, -1, 3\rangle = 0 - 3 + 3 = 0$, so it is perpendicular to both. The parallelogram has area $\sqrt{36 + 1 + 9} = \sqrt{46} \approx 6.78$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The triple product">
$\vec Q \times \vec R = \langle 3 - 1,\ -(0 - 1),\ 0 - 3\rangle = \langle 2, 1, -3\rangle$, so $\vec P \cdot (\vec Q \times \vec R) = 2 + 0 - 6 = -4$. The determinant by expansion agrees: $1(3 - 1) - 0 + 2(0 - 3) = -4$. The volume is $|-4| = 4$; the minus sign says $\vec P$, $\vec Q$, $\vec R$ form a left-handed set.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Why density falls as 1/a³">
In an expanding universe every distance between galaxies grows by the same scale factor $a(t)$. Take a box of galaxies with edges $\vec A$, $\vec B$, $\vec C$: later its edges are $a\vec A$, $a\vec B$, $a\vec C$, and by the expansion formula each of the three factors in every term of the determinant picks up a factor $a$, so the volume is multiplied by $a^3$. The galaxies inside stay inside, so their density falls as $1/a^3$. Cosmology courses use this from [the expanding sphere](/courses/8-286/expanding-sphere) onwards.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\vec A \cdot \hat u$ is the component of $\vec A$ along the unit vector $\hat u$.
- Rotating $\langle a_1, a_2\rangle$ by 90° counterclockwise gives $\langle -a_2, a_1\rangle$, and with it the **determinant** $a_1b_2 - a_2b_1$: plus or minus the area of the parallelogram with sides $\vec A$, $\vec B$.
- A 3 × 3 determinant, expanded along the first row with signs $+, -, +$, is plus or minus the volume of the parallelepiped with sides $\vec A$, $\vec B$, $\vec C$.
- The **cross product** $\vec A \times \vec B$ of two vectors in space is a vector: its length is the area of the parallelogram, its direction perpendicular to it by the right-hand rule; $\hat i \times \hat j = \hat k$.
- Volume = $\vec A \cdot (\vec B \times \vec C) = \det(\vec A, \vec B, \vec C)$, up to sign.
