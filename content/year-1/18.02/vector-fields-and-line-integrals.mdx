A new topic: vector fields and line integrals. Double integrals will return; for now it helps not to think of them at all, since line integrals are a completely different thing (transcript, p. 1).

## Vector fields

A **vector field** has components that depend on the point (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Vector field" tex="\vec F = M\,\hat i + N\,\hat j, \qquad M = M(x, y), \quad N = N(x, y)" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

At every point of the plane there is a vector (Auroux: "in a corn field, everywhere you have corn") (p. 1). Real examples are **velocity fields**, such as wind maps, where the arrow at each point is the velocity of the air, and **force fields**, such as the gravitational field of the Earth or of stars and planets (p. 1; notes, p. 2). To draw one, put arrows at enough points to see the pattern; precision is rarely the point (p. 1). The lecture's standard examples (pp. 1–2; notes, p. 2):

1. $\vec F = 2\hat i + \hat j$: the same vector everywhere.
2. $\vec F = x\,\hat i$: horizontal, zero on the $y$-axis, longer further right, pointing left where $x < 0$.
3. $\vec F = x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j$: the position vector copied to each point, pointing radially out, growing with distance, like a source pouring fluid out at the origin.
4. $\vec F = -y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j$: the position vector turned 90° counterclockwise, perpendicular to it. It is the velocity field of **uniform rotation** at unit angular velocity: a fluid particle goes round in time $2\pi$. ($\langle y, -x\rangle$ rotates the other way.)

## Work and the line integral

The work done by a force during a small displacement $\Delta\vec r$ is $\vec F \cdot \Delta\vec r$: it is the energy the force provides, and if it is the only force, the change in kinetic energy (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 2). (8.01 develops [work along a path](/courses/8-01/work-in-two-and-three-dimensions).) Along a curved trajectory $C$ with a varying force, cut $C$ into small pieces and add up; in the limit, the **line integral** (p. 2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Work as a line integral" tex="W = \int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \lim_{\Delta\vec r_i \to 0} \sum_i \vec F \cdot \Delta\vec r_i" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

To compute it, cut the motion into small time intervals: each step is about the velocity times $\Delta t$, so (p. 2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Computing a line integral" tex="\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \vec F \cdot \frac{d\vec r}{dt}\,dt" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

where $\vec F$ is evaluated at the point of the trajectory at time $t$ (p. 2).

**Example.** $\vec F = \langle -y, x\rangle$ along $x = t$, $y = t^2$, $0 \le t \le 1$: the piece of the parabola $y = x^2$ from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$. The force and the path are both given, independently of each other (p. 2). At time $t$, $\vec F = \langle -t^2, t\rangle$ and $d\vec r/dt = \langle 1, 2t\rangle$, so (p. 3; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The example" tex="\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_0^1 \langle -t^2, t\rangle \cdot \langle 1, 2t\rangle\,dt = \int_0^1 (-t^2 + 2t^2)\,dt = \int_0^1 t^2\,dt = \frac13" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

A different path between the same points generally gives a different answer (p. 3). For example, along the $x$-axis to $(1, 0)$ the field $\langle 0, x\rangle$ is perpendicular to the path (no work), and then straight up to $(1, 1)$ it has component 1 along the path, so that route gives work 1, not $\frac13$. (The line integral does not say how a particle would move under the force; it measures the work along a path that is given, p. 3.)

## The notation M dx + N dy

Writing $d\vec r = \langle dx, dy\rangle$, a differential, gives a second notation (transcript, p. 3; notes, pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Line integral in differentials" tex="\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_C M\,dx + N\,dy" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 19, p. 3" />

The notation is dangerous: this is not a sum of an $x$-integral and a $y$-integral. Along $C$, $x$ and $y$ are related, and there is only one parameter; **everything must be expressed in terms of it** (p. 3; notes, p. 3). For the example, $x = t$, $y = t^2$, $dx = dt$, $dy = 2t\,dt$ (p. 3):

<Formula label="The example again" tex="\int_C -y\,dx + x\,dy = \int_0^1 \left(-t^2\,dt + t \cdot 2t\,dt\right) = \int_0^1 t^2\,dt = \frac13" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

The line integral depends on the curve $C$ but **not on the parametrisation**: $x = \sin\theta$, $y = \sin^2\theta$, $0 \le \theta \le \pi/2$, traces the same parabola and gives $\frac13$ again, with more trigonometry. In practice, choose the simplest parametrisation, possibly $x$ itself, with $y = x^2$ and $dy = 2x\,dx$ (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 2).

## The geometric approach

A small step $\Delta\vec r$ along the curve points along the unit tangent $\hat T$ and has length $\Delta s$, the arc length: $d\vec r = \hat T\,ds$ (dividing by $dt$, this is $\vec v = \hat T\,ds/dt$) (transcript, p. 4; notes, p. 3). So (p. 4; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Tangential component" tex="\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_C \vec F \cdot \hat T\,ds" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

$\vec F \cdot \hat T$ is the component of the force along the curve. When the field and the curve fit together simply, this can save the computation (p. 4):

- $C$ the circle of radius $a$ about the origin, counterclockwise, and $\vec F = x\hat i + y\hat j$, radial: $\vec F \perp \hat T$ everywhere, so the work is $\int_C 0\,ds = 0$ (p. 4; notes, p. 3).
- Same $C$, $\vec F = -y\hat i + x\hat j$: $\vec F$ is tangent to the circle, with length $a$, so $\vec F \cdot \hat T = a$ and the work is $a \times 2\pi a = 2\pi a^2$. With $x = a\cos\theta$, $y = a\sin\theta$ the same answer takes longer (p. 4; notes, p. 3).

<WorkedExample title="Two paths, two answers">
<Problem>
For $\vec F = -y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j$, find the work from $(1, 0)$ to $(0, 1)$ (a) along the straight segment, and (b) along the quarter of the unit circle. (c) Then for $\vec G = y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j$, compute the work from $(0, 0)$ to $(2, 1)$ along the segment $x = 2t$, $y = t$, and along the parabola $x = 2t$, $y = t^2$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The segment">
$x = 1 - t$, $y = t$, $0 \le t \le 1$, so $dx = -dt$, $dy = dt$ and $\int -y\,dx + x\,dy = \int_0^1 \big(t + (1 - t)\big)\,dt = \int_0^1 1\,dt = 1$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The arc, geometrically">
On the unit circle $\vec F$ is tangent with length 1, so $\vec F \cdot \hat T = 1$ and the work is the arc length, $\pi/2 \approx 1.571$. Different from (a): the work depends on the path.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A field where the paths agree">
Segment: $\vec G = \langle t, 2t\rangle$, $d\vec r/dt = \langle 2, 1\rangle$, integrand $4t$, work $\int_0^1 4t\,dt = 2$. Parabola: $\vec G = \langle t^2, 2t\rangle$, $d\vec r/dt = \langle 2, 2t\rangle$, integrand $2t^2 + 4t^2 = 6t^2$, work $\int_0^1 6t^2\,dt = 2$. The same: for some fields the path does not matter, which is the subject of the next lecture.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Gravity as a field">
The gravitational field is the vector field the lecture mentions: at each point, the force per unit mass a test particle would feel there. Newton's attempt to describe an infinite static universe filled uniformly with matter runs into trouble precisely because of this field: it is not clear what the field of an infinite uniform distribution should be. See [why a static universe cannot work](/courses/8-286/newtons-static-universe).
</Callout>

## Summary

- A vector field $\vec F = M\hat i + N\hat j$ has a vector at each point; examples are velocity and force fields, and $\langle -y, x\rangle$ is uniform rotation.
- The work along $C$ is the line integral $\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \vec F \cdot \frac{d\vec r}{dt}\,dt$.
- Equivalently $\int_C M\,dx + N\,dy$: express $x$, $y$, $dx$, $dy$ in one parameter and integrate.
- The result depends on the path, but not on its parametrisation.
- $d\vec r = \hat T\,ds$, so $\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_C \vec F \cdot \hat T\,ds$: zero for a field perpendicular to the path, $2\pi a^2$ for $\langle -y, x\rangle$ around a circle of radius $a$.
