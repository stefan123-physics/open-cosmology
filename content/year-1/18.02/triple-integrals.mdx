From now to the end of the course, the plane is replaced by space: triple integrals, then work, flux, divergence and curl in three dimensions. Conceptually it is the same with one more coordinate, so anything shaky in the plane is worth reviewing first (transcript, p. 1).

## Triple integrals

For a function $f(x, y, z)$ and a solid region $R$ in space, the triple integral adds up $f$ over every small piece of $R$ times its volume (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Triple integral" tex="\int\int\int_R f\,dV, \qquad dV = dx\,dy\,dz \text{ (in any order)}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The new difficulty is setting up the bounds; the integrand matters less, and the lecture's examples use simple ones (p. 1).

## Example: between two paraboloids

Let $R$ be the region between the paraboloids $z = x^2 + y^2$ (opening up from the origin) and $z = 4 - x^2 - y^2$ (opening down from height 4); its volume is $\int\int\int_R 1\,dV$ (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 2). The solid has a curved bottom and top and, by its symmetry about the $z$-axis, a circular edge (p. 1).

**Inner integral.** Integrate over $z$ first: for given $x$ and $y$, the vertical line above $(x, y)$ enters the solid on the bottom surface and leaves on the top one (p. 1; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Bounds for z" tex="\int\int\int_R dV = \int\int \int_{x^2 + y^2}^{4 - x^2 - y^2} dz\,dy\,dx" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Integrating first in $x$ would mean finding the front and back of the solid on lines parallel to the $x$-axis, which is harder (p. 1). The inner bounds may depend on both outer variables (p. 2).

**The shadow.** The remaining $(x, y)$ are those in the **shadow** of the solid on the $xy$-plane. It is where the bottom surface lies below the top one: $x^2 + y^2 \le 4 - x^2 - y^2$, that is $x^2 + y^2 \le 2$, a disc of radius $\sqrt 2$ (p. 2; notes, p. 2). Setting up a double integral over it as usual (p. 2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The volume in rectangular coordinates" tex="V = \int_{-\sqrt2}^{\sqrt2}\int_{-\sqrt{2 - x^2}}^{\sqrt{2 - x^2}}\int_{x^2 + y^2}^{4 - x^2 - y^2} dz\,dy\,dx" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 25, p. 2" />

(Symmetry could cut the work by 2, 4 or 8, p. 2.) After the inner integral the integrand is $4 - 2x^2 - 2y^2$, the height of the solid, and polar coordinates make the rest easy (p. 2).

## Cylindrical coordinates

**Cylindrical coordinates** keep $z$ and use polar coordinates for the projection onto the $xy$-plane: $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, $z = z$. $r$ is the distance from the $z$-axis, $\theta$ the angle from the $x$-axis (which in the usual drawing points towards us) (transcript, pp. 2–3; notes, p. 2). With $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$, the example becomes (p. 2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The volume in cylindrical coordinates" tex="V = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\sqrt2}\int_{r^2}^{4 - r^2} dz\,r\,dr\,d\theta" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

(The lecture leaves it there; it comes to $2\pi\int_0^{\sqrt2}(4 - 2r^2)\,r\,dr = 4\pi$.)

The equation $r = a$ is a **cylinder** of radius $a$ about the $z$-axis, hence the name; $\theta = $ constant is a vertical **half**-plane, since $r \ge 0$ by convention (p. 3; notes, p. 2). The volume element: a small piece with base area $\Delta A$ and height $\Delta z$ has volume $\Delta A\,\Delta z$, so (p. 3; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Volume element" tex="dV = dx\,dy\,dz = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

Integrating $z$ first or last is the choice between the shell and disc methods of single-variable calculus (p. 3).

## Applications

As in the plane (transcript, p. 3; notes, pp. 2–3): the **mass** of a solid with density $\delta$ (mass per unit volume) is $M = \int\int\int_R \delta\,dV$; the **average** of $f$ is $\bar f = \frac{1}{\text{Vol}(R)}\int\int\int_R f\,dV$, or weighted, $\frac1M\int\int\int_R f\,\delta\,dV$; and the **centre of mass** is the average position (p. 3; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Centre of mass" tex="\bar x = \frac{1}{M}\int\int\int_R x\,\delta\,dV, \quad \bar y = \frac{1}{M}\int\int\int_R y\,\delta\,dV, \quad \bar z = \frac{1}{M}\int\int\int_R z\,\delta\,dV" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

Symmetry often saves work (in the example, $\bar x = \bar y = 0$, and by the mirror symmetry of the two paraboloids about $z = 2$, $\bar z = 2$), but only if the density is symmetric too (p. 3).

**Moments of inertia** use the squared distance to the axis; the distance to the $z$-axis is $r$, to the $x$-axis $\sqrt{y^2 + z^2}$ (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Moments of inertia about the axes" tex="I_z = \int\int\int_R (x^2 + y^2)\,\delta\,dV, \qquad I_x = \int\int\int_R (y^2 + z^2)\,\delta\,dV, \qquad I_y = \int\int\int_R (x^2 + z^2)\,\delta\,dV" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 25, p. 3" />

For a flat object in the $xy$-plane ($z = 0$) these become the [plane formulas](/courses/18-02/polar-double-integrals); the old "moment about the origin" was really about the $z$-axis (p. 4).

## Two more examples

**A solid cone.** The cone $z = ar$ (height proportional to the distance from the axis, slope $a$), up to the plane $z = b$, with $\delta = 1$ (transcript, p. 4; notes, p. 3). This time integrate $r$ and $\theta$ first: a horizontal slice at height $z$ is a disc of radius $z/a$; the first slice is $z = 0$, the last $z = b$ (p. 4):

<Formula label="Moment of inertia of a cone" tex="I_z = \int_0^b\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{z/a} r^2\,r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz = \frac{\pi b^5}{10\,a^4}" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

**A cap of the ball.** The region of the unit ball $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 \le 1$ above the plane $z = 1 - y$ (transcript, pp. 4–5; notes, p. 3). With $z$ first, $z$ runs from the plane up to the sphere. The shadow is where the plane is below the sphere, $1 - y < \sqrt{1 - x^2 - y^2}$; squaring gives $x^2 < 2y - 2y^2$ (which has solutions only for $0 \le y \le 1$), an ellipse, the projection of the slanted circle where the plane cuts the sphere (pp. 4–5; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="The cap" tex="\int_0^1\int_{-\sqrt{2y - 2y^2}}^{\sqrt{2y - 2y^2}}\int_{1 - y}^{\sqrt{1 - x^2 - y^2}} dz\,dx\,dy" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

For the volume alone one would rotate the cap onto the $z$-axis first; for other integrands that may not be possible (p. 5).

<WorkedExample title="A cylinder heavier at the top">
<Problem>
A solid cylinder $r \le 1$, $0 \le z \le 2$ has density $\delta = z$. Find its mass, its centre of mass, and its moment of inertia about the $z$-axis.
</Problem>
<Step title="Mass">
$M = \int_0^2\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 z\,r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz = \frac12 \cdot 2\pi \cdot \int_0^2 z\,dz = \pi \cdot 2 = 2\pi$.
</Step>
<Step title="Centre of mass">
By symmetry $\bar x = \bar y = 0$. $\int\int\int z \cdot z\,dV = \pi\int_0^2 z^2\,dz = \frac{8\pi}{3}$, so $\bar z = \frac{8\pi/3}{2\pi} = \frac43 \approx 1.33$: above the middle height 1, as the top is heavier.
</Step>
<Step title="Moment of inertia">
$I_z = \int_0^2\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r^2 \cdot z \cdot r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz = \frac14 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 2 = \pi$, which is $\frac12 M \cdot 1^2$, the familiar result for a cylinder: the vertical distribution of mass does not matter for spinning about the axis.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The mass inside a sphere">
For a ball of radius $R$ and uniform density $\rho$, the mass is the triple integral $\int\int\int \rho\,dV = \frac{4\pi}{3}R^3\rho$. Guth's Newtonian model of the expanding universe follows a sphere of uniform density: the mass inside each shell stays fixed while the radius grows, and its gravity slows the expansion. See [the expanding sphere](/courses/8-286/expanding-sphere).
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\int\int\int_R f\,dV$ adds $f$ over a solid. With $z$ first: bottom to top surface for fixed $(x, y)$, then a double integral over the shadow in the $xy$-plane.
- The shadow is where the bottom surface lies below the top one (for the two paraboloids, $x^2 + y^2 \le 2$).
- Cylindrical coordinates: $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, $z$; $dV = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$.
- Mass $\int\int\int \delta\,dV$; centre of mass $\bar x = \frac1M\int\int\int x\delta\,dV$ etc.; $I_z = \int\int\int (x^2 + y^2)\delta\,dV$, and likewise $I_x$, $I_y$.
- A solid cone $z = ar$, $z \le b$, of density 1: $I_z = \pi b^5/(10a^4)$.
