The [last lecture](/courses/18-02/vector-fields-and-line-integrals) computed line integrals by parametrising curves. This one shows how to avoid most of that work when the field is a gradient (transcript, p. 1).

## A line integral in three pieces

Recall $\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_C M\,dx + N\,dy = \int_C \vec F \cdot \hat T\,ds$ (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 3). The lecture's example: $\vec F = y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j$, around the closed curve $C = C_1 + C_2 + C_3$ that encloses the sector of the unit disc between angles 0 and $\pi/4$ (p. 1; notes, p. 3).

1. $C_1$, along the $x$-axis from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 0)$: $y = 0$ and $dy = 0$, so $\int y\,dx + x\,dy = 0$. Geometrically, on the $x$-axis $\vec F = x\,\hat j$ is perpendicular to the path (p. 1).
2. $C_2$, along the unit circle from angle 0 to $\pi/4$: $x = \cos\theta$, $y = \sin\theta$, $dx = -\sin\theta\,d\theta$, $dy = \cos\theta\,d\theta$, so (p. 1; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="The arc" tex="\int_{C_2} y\,dx + x\,dy = \int_0^{\pi/4} (\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta)\,d\theta = \int_0^{\pi/4} \cos 2\theta\,d\theta = \left[\tfrac12\sin 2\theta\right]_0^{\pi/4} = \frac12" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

3. $C_3$, back along the diagonal from $(1/\sqrt2, 1/\sqrt2)$ to the origin. Easier than parametrising it directly: go the other way, $-C_3$, with $x = y = t$, $0 \le t \le 1/\sqrt2$, which gives $\int 2t\,dt = \frac12$; the work along $C_3$ is the opposite, $-\frac12$ (p. 1; notes, p. 3).

The total is $0 + \frac12 - \frac12 = 0$: "a lot of calculations for nothing" (p. 1).

## The fundamental theorem for line integrals

A gradient, met in [Lecture 12](/courses/18-02/gradient), depends on $x$ and $y$: it is a vector field. Suppose $\vec F = \nabla f = f_x\,\hat i + f_y\,\hat j$, a **gradient field**; $f$ is called the **potential** (transcript, pp. 1–2; notes, p. 3). (Physicists write force $= -\nabla$(potential energy), so their potential has the opposite sign; just add a minus sign in physics, p. 2.) Then, for a curve $C$ from $P_0$ to $P_1$ (p. 2; notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Fundamental theorem of calculus for line integrals" tex="\int_C \nabla f \cdot d\vec r = f(P_1) - f(P_0)" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 20, p. 4" />

In differentials, $\int_C f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy = \int_C df = f(P_1) - f(P_0)$: integrating a differential gives back the change in the function, as in one variable (p. 2).

**Proof.** Parametrise $C$ by $x(t)$, $y(t)$, $t_0 \le t \le t_1$. Then $dx = x'(t)\,dt$, $dy = y'(t)\,dt$, and by the [chain rule](/courses/18-02/differentials-and-chain-rule) the integrand is the derivative of $f(x(t), y(t))$ (p. 2; notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Proof" tex="\int_C \nabla f \cdot d\vec r = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\left(f_x\frac{dx}{dt} + f_y\frac{dy}{dt}\right)dt = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\frac{d}{dt}f\big(x(t), y(t)\big)\,dt = f(P_1) - f(P_0)" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

by the ordinary fundamental theorem of calculus (p. 2).

**The example again.** $\langle y, x\rangle$ is the gradient of $f = xy$. The curve $C_1$ stays on the level $f = 0$: no work. Along $C_2$ the potential rises from 0 to $\frac12$; along $C_3$ it falls back to 0. Total zero, with no integration at all (p. 2; notes, p. 4).

## Consequences, for gradient fields only

**Warning: this applies only to gradient fields.** Many fields are not gradients; magnetic fields, for example, are not (p. 2). For a gradient field (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 4):

- **Path independence.** If $C_1$ and $C_2$ go from the same $P_0$ to the same $P_1$, both integrals equal $f(P_1) - f(P_0)$, so they are equal. This holds even if $f$ is not known, as long as $\vec F$ is known to be a gradient (p. 3).
- **Conservativeness.** If $C$ is a closed curve, $\oint_C \nabla f \cdot d\vec r = f(P) - f(P) = 0$ (p. 3; notes, p. 4). One closed curve with zero work proves nothing: conservative means zero for **every** closed curve (p. 3).

**A field that is not a gradient.** $\vec F = -y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j$ around the unit circle is tangent to it with length 1, so the work is the circumference (p. 3; notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Not conservative" tex="\oint_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \oint_C \vec F \cdot \hat T\,ds = \oint_C 1\,ds = 2\pi \ne 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

So it is not conservative, hence not a gradient field. Nor is it path independent: from $(1, 0)$ to $(-1, 0)$ the upper half circle gives $\pi$, the lower one $-\pi$ (p. 3).

## Physics: potentials and energy

If a force field is the gradient of a potential, its work is the change in potential between the endpoints. Gravity and the electric force are examples, with the gravitational potential and the electrical potential, the **voltage** (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 4). Conservative means that no energy can be extracted for free from the field by going around a loop: the change in kinetic energy equals the work, which is minus the change in the physicists' potential energy $U$, so kinetic energy plus $U$ stays constant: **total energy is conserved**, hence the name (p. 3; notes, p. 4). A non-conservative field can supply energy around a loop. Auroux's example is the transformer (p. 3); strictly, the field doing the work there is the electric field induced by a changing magnetic field, whose work around a loop is not zero (the magnetic force itself does no work).

## Four equivalent properties

For a vector field $\vec F = \langle M, N\rangle$, these say the same thing (transcript, pp. 3–4; notes, p. 4):

1. $\vec F$ is **conservative**: $\oint_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = 0$ for every closed curve $C$.
2. $\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r$ is **path independent**.
3. $\vec F$ is a **gradient field**: $\vec F = \nabla f$.
4. $M\,dx + N\,dy$ is an **exact differential**: it equals $df$ for some $f$.

(1) ⟺ (2): a closed curve has the same endpoints as staying put, which gives zero work; and two paths $C_1$, $C_2$ with the same endpoints make a closed curve $C_1 - C_2$, whose zero work means equal work along both (p. 4). (3) ⟹ (2) is the fundamental theorem; conversely, given path independence, choose the potential at one point and define it elsewhere as the work from there, the method for finding potentials in the next lecture (p. 4; notes, p. 4). (3) and (4) are the same statement in two notations (p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="A potential saves the work">
<Problem>
Let $\vec F = 2xy\,\hat i + x^2\,\hat j$. (a) Check that $\vec F = \nabla f$ for $f = x^2y$. (b) Find the work from $(0, 0)$ to $(2, 3)$, and check along the straight segment. (c) What is the work around any closed curve?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The potential">
$f_x = 2xy$ and $f_y = x^2$: indeed $\nabla f = \vec F$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Endpoints only">
$f(2, 3) - f(0, 0) = 4 \cdot 3 - 0 = 12$. Check on $x = 2t$, $y = 3t$, $0 \le t \le 1$: $\vec F = \langle 12t^2, 4t^2\rangle$, $d\vec r/dt = \langle 2, 3\rangle$, integrand $24t^2 + 12t^2 = 36t^2$, and $\int_0^1 36t^2\,dt = 12$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Closed curves">
Zero: the field is a gradient, so it is conservative.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Energy conservation for an expanding universe">
Newtonian gravity is conservative: it is minus the gradient of a potential, so kinetic plus potential energy is conserved. Applied to a shell of matter in a uniformly expanding sphere, that conserved energy is exactly what Guth turns into the Friedmann equation, with the sign of the energy deciding whether the universe expands forever; see [the expanding sphere](/courses/8-286/expanding-sphere) and [the Friedmann equation](/courses/8-286/friedmann-equation).
</Callout>

## Summary

- For a gradient field, $\int_C \nabla f \cdot d\vec r = f(P_1) - f(P_0)$: the work is the change in potential (proof: the chain rule and the ordinary fundamental theorem).
- Consequences, for gradient fields only: path independence, and zero work around every closed curve (conservative).
- $\langle -y, x\rangle$ does $2\pi$ of work around the unit circle, so it is not a gradient.
- Conservative ⟺ path independent ⟺ gradient field ⟺ $M\,dx + N\,dy$ exact.
- Physically, a conservative force conserves energy; physicists' potentials carry the opposite sign.
