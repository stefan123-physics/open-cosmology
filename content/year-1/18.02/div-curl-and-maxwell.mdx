The last new lecture of 18.02 shows what divergence and curl mean in physics: first for force fields and rotation, then in Maxwell's equations for electricity and magnetism (transcript, p. 1).

## Curl and rotation

For a velocity field, the curl is twice the angular velocity vector of the rotating part of the motion (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1). For a uniform rotation about the $z$-axis at angular velocity $\omega$ (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Curl of a rotation" tex="\vec v = \omega\,\langle -y, x, 0\rangle, \qquad \nabla \times \vec v = 2\omega\,\hat k" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 33, p. 1" />

(The transcript reads "minus y i plus y j", a slip for $-y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j$.)

The curl singles out the rotation in a motion, as the divergence singles out the stretching (notes, p. 1).

## Curl of a force field

Now let $\vec F$ be a force per unit mass (an acceleration field), so a small mass $\Delta m$ at $(x, y, z)$ feels the force $\vec F\,\Delta m$, as in a gravitational field (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1). Its **torque** about the origin, with $\vec r$ the position vector, is (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Torque" tex="\vec\tau = \vec r \times \vec F\,\Delta m" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The torque measures how the force tends to swing the mass around the origin; for a whole solid, the torques of its pieces are added up as a triple integral (notes, p. 1). The two laws of motion side by side (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

- translation: force / mass = acceleration = $\frac{d}{dt}$(velocity);
- rotation: torque / moment of inertia = angular acceleration = $\frac{d}{dt}$(angular velocity).

Auroux says this second law is a little simplified, and leaves the details aside (p. 1). Since the curl turns velocity into twice the angular velocity, it turns an acceleration field into twice the angular acceleration (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Curl of a force field" tex="\nabla \times \left(\frac{\text{force}}{\text{mass}}\right) = 2\,\frac{\text{torque}}{\text{moment of inertia}}" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 33, p. 1" />

So the curl says how fast a small test body placed in the field starts to spin. A field with zero curl makes nothing spin: a body at rest may accelerate but will not start rotating, and a spinning body keeps spinning as before, apart from friction (p. 1).

**Consequence.** A force with a potential, $\vec F = \nabla f$, has $\nabla \times \vec F = \vec 0$ (p. 1; notes, p. 1). So gravity by itself cannot make the Earth spin faster or slower, if the Earth is a rigid solid: the Earth spins because it formed spinning (p. 1). This is not strictly true, because real bodies deform: friction and tidal effects slowly lock rotations together, which is why the Moon turns once on its axis per orbit and always shows Earth the same face (p. 1; notes, p. 1).

## The electric and magnetic fields

Maxwell's equations describe how electric and magnetic fields are produced by charges and their motion; they explain electric devices and electromagnetic waves, including light (transcript, p. 2). A particle of charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ feels the force $q\vec E$ from the electric field and $q\,\vec v \times \vec B$ from the magnetic field, written on the board (p. 2). Classically $\vec E$ is minus the gradient of a potential, the voltage (p. 2). The equations give the divergence and the curl of both fields (p. 2; notes, p. 1). Of the four, most are not named after Maxwell (p. 2).

## The Gauss–Coulomb law

With $\rho$ the electric **charge density** (charge per unit volume, not the $\rho$ of spherical coordinates) and $\varepsilon_0$ a physical constant (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Gauss–Coulomb law" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 33, p. 1" />

Charges are the sources of divergence; where there is no charge, $\nabla \cdot \vec E = 0$ (p. 2). The equation is clearer after the [divergence theorem](/courses/18-02/divergence-theorem): for a closed surface $S$ enclosing a region $D$ with total charge $Q$ (p. 2; notes, p. 1),

<Formula label="Gauss's law, integral form" tex="\int\int_S \vec E \cdot \hat n\,dS = \int\int\int_D \nabla \cdot \vec E\,dV = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int\int\int_D \rho\,dV = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

It tells how charges shape the field around them; for example, it relates the charge on the plates of a capacitor to the voltage between them (p. 2; notes, p. 1).

## Faraday's law

The electric field is not always conservative. A changing magnetic field gives it a curl (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Faraday's law" tex="\nabla \times \vec E = -\frac{\partial \vec B}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 33, p. 1" />

With [Stokes' theorem](/courses/18-02/stokes-theorem), for a closed curve $C$ bounding a surface $S$ that does not move (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Faraday's law, integral form" tex="\oint_C \vec E \cdot d\vec r = \int\int_S (\nabla \times \vec E) \cdot \hat n\,dS = -\frac{d}{dt}\int\int_S \vec B \cdot \hat n\,dS" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

The left side is the voltage around a loop of wire: a changing magnetic flux through the loop produces a voltage "out of nowhere", the **induction** effect (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 1). A transformer, as in a laptop's power adapter, uses it: alternating current in one coil makes a changing magnetic field, whose flux through a second coil drives the output voltage (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 1).

## The other two equations

For the magnetic field (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1), with $\vec J$ the current density, the flow of charge, and $\mu_0$ a second constant:

<Formula label="Div and curl of B" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0, \qquad \nabla \times \vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \varepsilon_0\mu_0\,\frac{\partial \vec E}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 33, p. 1" />

$\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0$ fits with [surface independence](/courses/18-02/stokes-and-path-independence): it is what makes the flux of $\vec B$ in Faraday's law depend only on the loop (p. 3). The last equation says currents make magnetic fields, which is the first step in a transformer; currents make $\vec B$, changing $\vec B$ makes $\vec E$, and $\vec E$ drives currents (p. 3).

<WorkedExample title="Two laws with numbers">
<Problem>
(a) A charge $q = 1$ nC sits at the centre of a sphere of radius $a$. Its field on the sphere is $\vec E = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2}\,\hat n$. Compute the flux of $\vec E$ out of the sphere and compare with Gauss's law ($\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}$ in SI units). (b) A circular loop of radius 0.1 m lies in a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to it, which grows at 0.5 tesla per second. Find the size of the voltage around the loop.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Flux of the point charge">
$\vec E \cdot \hat n$ is constant on the sphere, so the flux is $\dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2} \cdot 4\pi a^2 = \dfrac{q}{\varepsilon_0}$, whatever the radius, as Gauss's law says. In numbers, $10^{-9}/(8.854 \times 10^{-12}) \approx 113$ volt metres.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Induced voltage">
The flux of $\vec B$ is $B$ times the area $\pi (0.1)^2 \approx 0.0314$ square metres, so it changes at $0.0314 \times 0.5 \approx 0.0157$ webers per second. By Faraday's law the voltage around the loop has the same size: about 15.7 millivolts.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Light from Maxwell's equations">
In empty space ($\rho = 0$, $\vec J = \vec 0$) the two curl equations feed each other: a changing $\vec E$ makes a changing $\vec B$ and back again, and together they give waves travelling at speed $1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0} \approx 3.00 \times 10^8$ m/s, the speed of light. Light is an electromagnetic wave. The oldest light in the universe, the cosmic microwave background, is the black-body radiation left over from the hot early universe, studied in 8.286.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Curl of a velocity field = twice the angular velocity; curl of a force per unit mass = twice the torque per unit moment of inertia. Gradient forces, such as gravity on a rigid body, cause no rotation.
- Gauss–Coulomb: $\nabla \cdot \vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$, or by the divergence theorem, flux out of a closed surface $= Q/\varepsilon_0$.
- Faraday: $\nabla \times \vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t$, or by Stokes, the voltage around a loop $= -\frac{d}{dt}$(flux of $\vec B$): induction and transformers.
- The magnetic field: $\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0$ and $\nabla \times \vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \varepsilon_0\mu_0\,\partial\vec E/\partial t$.
