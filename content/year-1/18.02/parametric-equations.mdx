A line can be described as the intersection of two planes, but two equations are not the easiest way to work with it. A better description is the **trajectory of a moving point**: a **parametric equation** (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 3).

## Parametric equations of lines

A line is given by two of its points, or by one point and a vector along it (p. 1). The lecture's example is the line through $Q_0 = (-1, 2, 2)$ and $Q_1 = (1, 3, -1)$. Let a point $Q(t)$ move along the line at constant speed, at $Q_0$ when $t = 0$ and at $Q_1$ when $t = 1$; at $t = 1/2$ it is halfway, at $t = 2$ beyond $Q_1$, at $t = -2$ on the other side (p. 2). The vector from $Q_0$ to $Q(t)$ is proportional to $\vec{Q_0Q_1}$, with factor $t$ (p. 2; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="A line as a moving point" tex="\vec{Q_0Q(t)} = t\,\vec{Q_0Q_1}, \qquad Q(t) = Q_0 + t\,\vec{Q_0Q_1}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3" />

Here $\vec{Q_0Q_1} = \langle 2, 1, -3\rangle$, so in coordinates (p. 3; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="The lecture's line" tex="x(t) = -1 + 2t, \qquad y(t) = 2 + t, \qquad z(t) = 2 - 3t" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 5, p. 3" />

Parametric equations of lines always look like this: each coordinate is a constant plus a constant times $t$. The coefficients of $t$ form a vector along the line; the constants are the starting point at $t = 0$ (p. 3).

## Where a line meets a plane

Take the plane $x + 2y + 4z = 7$. Are $Q_0$ and $Q_1$ on the same side of it? (transcript, p. 4; notes, p. 3.) At $Q_0$, $x + 2y + 4z = -1 + 4 + 8 = 11$, more than 7; at $Q_1$ it is $1 + 6 - 4 = 3$, less than 7 (pp. 4–5). Neither point is in the plane. The plane splits space into a half where $x + 2y + 4z < 7$ and a half where it is greater than 7, and along any path from $Q_0$ to $Q_1$ the value changes continuously from 11 to 3, so it passes 7: the points are on **opposite sides** (p. 5).

To find where the line crosses, substitute the moving point (p. 5; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Substituting the line" tex="x(t) + 2y(t) + 4z(t) = (-1 + 2t) + 2(2 + t) + 4(2 - 3t) = 11 - 8t" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

$Q(t)$ is in the plane when $11 - 8t = 7$, that is $t = 1/2$, no surprise since 7 is the average of 11 and 3. The crossing point is $Q(1/2) = (0, 5/2, 1/2)$ (pp. 5–6; notes, p. 3).

In general the substitution gives an equation of the form (number) $\cdot\, t$ + (number) = (number), which usually has exactly one solution. The exception is when the coefficient of $t$ is zero (pp. 6–7; notes, p. 3):

- if the constant then equals the plane's right-hand side, every $t$ works: the line **lies in** the plane;
- if not (say the substitution gives 13, never 7), no $t$ works: the line is **parallel** to the plane, outside it.

The coefficient of $t$ is the dot product of the plane's normal vector with the vector along the line, here $1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot (-3) = -8$; it is zero exactly when the line is perpendicular to the normal, that is parallel to the plane (p. 7).

## General parametric curves: the cycloid

Parametric equations describe any motion, in the plane or in space (transcript, p. 7; notes, p. 4). A famous example is the **cycloid**: a wheel of radius $a$ rolls along the floor (the $x$-axis), and a point $P$ on its rim, starting at the origin, traces a curve of arches, touching the floor once per turn (pp. 7–8). (You may see it at night, from a reflector on a bicycle wheel, p. 7.)

**Choice of parameter.** How fast the wheel rolls does not change the curve, so time is not the most natural parameter. The simplest formulas come from the angle $\theta$ by which the wheel has turned (pp. 8–9; notes, p. 4).

**Splitting the position vector.** Let $A$ be the point where the wheel touches the floor and $B$ its centre. Then $\vec{OP} = \vec{OA} + \vec{AB} + \vec{BP}$, three easier vectors (p. 9; notes, p. 4):

- $\vec{OA} = \langle a\theta, 0\rangle$: the wheel rolls without slipping, so the distance travelled on the floor equals the arc length $a\theta$ on the rim from $A$ to $P$ (with $\theta$ in radians, the reason mathematics always uses radians) (p. 10);
- $\vec{AB} = \langle 0, a\rangle$, straight up (p. 10);
- $\vec{BP}$ has length $a$ and makes the angle $\theta$ with the downward vertical, pointing left and down: $\vec{BP} = \langle -a\sin\theta, -a\cos\theta\rangle$ (pp. 10–11).

Adding them (p. 11; notes, p. 4):

## What happens at the bottom?

Near the bottom point, the curve looks pointed on a screen. Is it a corner, a tiny loop, a smooth curve with a horizontal tangent, or a **vertical tangent**? (transcript, p. 11; notes, p. 4.) Take the radius as the unit of length, $a = 1$, so $x = \theta - \sin\theta$ and $y = 1 - \cos\theta$ (p. 12). The rough approximations $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ and $\cos\theta \approx 1$ give $x \approx 0$, $y \approx 0$: not precise enough (p. 12). The **Taylor approximation** for small $t$ goes further, adding a term in $t^2$, then one in $t^3$ ($t^3/6$ times the third derivative at 0), and so on (pp. 12–13; notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Taylor approximation" tex="f(t) \approx f(0) + t\,f'(0) + \frac{t^2}{2}\,f''(0) + \cdots" cite="reading2" at="pp. 12–13" />

It gives $\sin\theta \approx \theta - \theta^3/6$ and $\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2$ (see 18.01's [Taylor series](/courses/18-01/taylor-series)), so near the bottom (p. 13; notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Near the bottom of the cycloid" tex="x \approx \frac{\theta^3}{6}, \qquad y \approx \frac{\theta^2}{2}, \qquad \frac{y}{x} \approx \frac{3}{\theta} \to \infty \ \ (\theta \to 0^+)" cite="reading2" at="p. 13" />

The cube of a small number is much smaller than its square, so $|x|$ is much smaller than $y$, and the slope $y/x$ with which the curve approaches the origin is infinite. The tangent is **vertical**, a **cusp** (as the notes call it): very close to the bottom, the point mainly moves down and back up, only slightly forward (pp. 13–14).

<WorkedExample title="A line through a plane, and a bicycle reflector">
<Problem>
(a) Find where the line through $Q_0 = (0, 1, 3)$ and $Q_1 = (2, -1, 4)$ meets the plane $x + y + 2z = 10$. Are $Q_0$ and $Q_1$ on the same side? (b) A reflector is on the rim of a bicycle wheel of radius 0.35 m, starting at the ground. Where is it after a quarter turn and after half a turn? (c) Check $y/x \approx 3/\theta$ at $\theta = 0.1$ (with $a = 1$).
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Substitute the line">
$\vec{Q_0Q_1} = \langle 2, -2, 1\rangle$, so $x = 2t$, $y = 1 - 2t$, $z = 3 + t$. Then $x + y + 2z = 2t + 1 - 2t + 6 + 2t = 7 + 2t$, which equals 10 at $t = 3/2$: the point $(3, -2, 9/2)$. At $Q_0$ the value is 7 and at $Q_1$ it is 9, both below 10, so they are on the **same** side, and indeed $t = 3/2$ lies beyond $Q_1$. The coefficient of $t$, 2, is $\langle 1, 1, 2\rangle \cdot \langle 2, -2, 1\rangle = 2 - 2 + 2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The cycloid with a = 0.35 m">
After a quarter turn, $\theta = \pi/2$: $x = 0.35(\pi/2 - 1) \approx 0.20$ m and $y = 0.35(1 - 0) = 0.35$ m, level with the hub. After half a turn, $\theta = \pi$: $x = 0.35\pi \approx 1.10$ m and $y = 2 \times 0.35 = 0.70$ m, at the top.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The slope near the bottom">
$x = 0.1 - \sin 0.1 \approx 0.000167$ and $y = 1 - \cos 0.1 \approx 0.00500$, so $y/x \approx 30.0$, while $3/\theta = 30$. As $\theta$ shrinks, the ratio grows without bound.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The universe as a cycloid">
In 8.286, Guth solves the Friedmann equation for a closed universe containing only matter, and the answer has exactly this form: $ct = \alpha(\theta - \sin\theta)$ and $a/\sqrt k = \alpha(1 - \cos\theta)$, with the scale factor playing the role of the height $y$ and time the role of $x$. (There the wheel's radius is $\alpha$; the letter $a$ is the scale factor.) One arch is the universe's whole life, from a big bang at $\theta = 0$, through its largest size at $\theta = \pi$, to a big crunch at $\theta = 2\pi$. The vertical tangent at the bottom is the scale factor rising infinitely steeply at the big bang. See [the closed universe](/courses/8-286/closed-universe-cycloid).
</Callout>

## Summary

- A line through $Q_0$ and $Q_1$: $Q(t) = Q_0 + t\,\vec{Q_0Q_1}$; each coordinate is a constant plus a constant times $t$.
- Substituting the line into the equation of a plane gives an equation in $t$: one solution where the line crosses, all $t$ if it lies in the plane, none if it is parallel outside it. The coefficient of $t$ is the normal vector dotted with the line's vector.
- Two points are on opposite sides of a plane when the left side of its equation is above the right side at one point and below it at the other.
- The cycloid traced by a point on a wheel of radius $a$: $x = a(\theta - \sin\theta)$, $y = a(1 - \cos\theta)$, found by adding $\vec{OA}$, $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{BP}$.
- Taylor approximations give $x \approx \theta^3/6$, $y \approx \theta^2/2$ near the bottom: a vertical tangent.
