The [last lecture](/courses/18-02/flux-through-surfaces) gave a formula for flux through a graph without proof. This one proves it, adds more kinds of surfaces, and ends with a way to avoid surface integrals altogether: the divergence theorem (transcript, p. 1).

## Flux through a graph

Let $S$ be the graph $z = f(x, y)$ over a region $R$ of the $xy$-plane, and use $x$ and $y$ as the variables (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 3). Take a small rectangle $\Delta x$ by $\Delta y$ at $(x, y)$. The piece of $S$ above it is nearly a parallelogram in space. By linear approximation, $f(x + \Delta x, y) \approx f + f_x\Delta x$ and $f(x, y + \Delta y) \approx f + f_y\Delta y$, so its sides are $\langle 1, 0, f_x\rangle\Delta x$ and $\langle 0, 1, f_y\rangle\Delta y$ (p. 1; notes, p. 3). The [cross product](/courses/18-02/determinants-and-cross-product) gives both the area and the normal direction at once (p. 1; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Flux element for a graph" tex="\hat n\,\Delta S = \langle 1, 0, f_x\rangle \times \langle 0, 1, f_y\rangle\,\Delta x\,\Delta y = \langle -f_x, -f_y, 1\rangle\,\Delta x\,\Delta y, \qquad \hat n\,dS = \pm\langle -f_x, -f_y, 1\rangle\,dx\,dy" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 28, p. 3" />

That is why $\hat n\,dS$ together is easier than $\hat n$ and $dS$ separately: $dS = \sqrt{f_x^2 + f_y^2 + 1}\,dx\,dy$, and the square root relates the area on $S$ to the area of its shadow (notes, p. 3). For a horizontal plane, $f_x = f_y = 0$ and it gives $\hat k\,dx\,dy$ (p. 1).

**Example** (transcript, pp. 1–2). The flux of $\vec F = z\,\hat k$ through the paraboloid $z = x^2 + y^2$ above the unit disc, with the normal pointing up (into the paraboloid). The field points up, so the flux should be positive (p. 2; notes, p. 3). Here $\hat n\,dS = \langle -2x, -2y, 1\rangle\,dx\,dy$, so $\vec F \cdot \hat n\,dS = z\,dx\,dy = (x^2 + y^2)\,dx\,dy$; replace $z$ by $f(x, y)$, then use polar coordinates on the disc (p. 2; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Flux through the paraboloid" tex="\int\int_S \vec F \cdot \hat n\,dS = \int\int_{\text{unit disc}} (x^2 + y^2)\,dx\,dy = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r^2\,r\,dr\,d\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

## Parametric surfaces

If $S$ is given by parametric equations $\vec r(u, v) = \langle x(u, v), y(u, v), z(u, v)\rangle$, a small change $\Delta u$, $\Delta v$ gives a small parallelogram with sides $\frac{\partial\vec r}{\partial u}\Delta u$ and $\frac{\partial\vec r}{\partial v}\Delta v$ (transcript, pp. 2–3; notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Parametric surfaces" tex="\hat n\,dS = \pm\left(\frac{\partial\vec r}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial\vec r}{\partial v}\right)du\,dv" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

With $u = x$, $v = y$ and $z = f(x, y)$ it gives the graph formula again; for the sphere, the earlier formulas (p. 3). (The notes say this general form won't be needed on the exam, notes, pp. 3–4.)

## Implicit surfaces

If $S$ is given by an equation $g(x, y, z) = 0$, a normal vector (not a unit one) is the [gradient](/courses/18-02/gradient), $\vec N = \nabla g$; for a slanted plane $ax + by + cz = d$, it is $\langle a, b, c\rangle$ (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 4). To find $\Delta S$, project the small piece onto the $xy$-plane: one direction is shrunk by $\cos\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between the surface and the horizontal, also the angle between $\vec N$ and $\hat k$, so $\cos\alpha = \vec N \cdot \hat k/|\vec N|$ and $\Delta A = \cos\alpha\,\Delta S$ (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 4). Multiplying $\Delta S$ by $\hat n$ (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Implicit surfaces" tex="dS = \frac{|\vec N|}{|\vec N \cdot \hat k|}\,dx\,dy, \qquad \hat n\,dS = \pm\frac{\vec N}{\vec N \cdot \hat k}\,dx\,dy" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 28, p. 4" />

(The notes add that the first formula needs an absolute value, which Auroux had first forgotten, notes, p. 4.) If $S$ is vertical, $\vec N \cdot \hat k = 0$ and one must project onto another plane, using $\hat i$ or $\hat j$ instead of $\hat k$ (p. 4; notes, p. 4). For a graph, $g = z - f(x, y)$ gives $\vec N = \langle -f_x, -f_y, 1\rangle$ and $\vec N \cdot \hat k = 1$: the same formula as before (p. 4).

## The divergence theorem

Instead of computing a surface integral, one can often avoid it. The **divergence theorem** (or Gauss–Green, or Gauss's theorem) is the space version of [Green's theorem for flux](/courses/18-02/flux) (transcript, p. 4; notes, p. 4). If $S$ is a **closed** surface enclosing a region $D$, with the normal pointing **outwards**, and $\vec F$ is defined and differentiable everywhere in $D$ (p. 4; notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Divergence theorem" tex="\int\int_S \vec F \cdot \hat n\,dS = \int\int\int_D \operatorname{div}\vec F\,dV, \qquad \operatorname{div}(P\,\hat i + Q\,\hat j + R\,\hat k) = P_x + Q_y + R_z" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 28, p. 4" />

**The example of last lecture.** For $z\,\hat k$ through the sphere of radius $a$: $\operatorname{div}\vec F = 0 + 0 + 1 = 1$, so the flux is the volume, $\frac43\pi a^3$, which was no accident (p. 4; notes, p. 4). (Here the transcript says "Green's theorem" three times: a slip for the divergence theorem.) And $\langle x, y, z\rangle$, with divergence 3, gave three times the volume, $4\pi a^3$ (p. 4). The theorem needs a closed surface: a hemisphere alone would need its flat bottom added (p. 4).

**Physically,** the divergence measures how much the flow is expanding, the sources or sinks per unit volume. The theorem then says what common sense suggests: the net amount of fluid leaving $D$ per unit time equals the total of the sources inside minus the sinks (p. 4; notes, p. 4). The proof comes next lecture (p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="A cube, a paraboloid and an area">
<Problem>
(a) Find the outward flux of $\vec F = \langle x, y, z\rangle$ through the surface of the unit cube $0 \le x, y, z \le 1$, directly and by the divergence theorem. (b) Find the upward flux of $\hat k$ through the paraboloid $z = x^2 + y^2$ over the unit disc. (c) Find the area of that piece of paraboloid.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Directly and by divergence">
On the face $x = 1$, $\hat n = \hat i$ and $\vec F \cdot \hat n = x = 1$, so its flux is its area, 1; likewise the faces $y = 1$ and $z = 1$. On the faces through the origin, $\vec F \cdot \hat n = 0$. Total 3. By the theorem: $\operatorname{div}\vec F = 3$, times the volume 1, gives 3.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A constant field">
$\hat k \cdot \langle -2x, -2y, 1\rangle\,dx\,dy = dx\,dy$, so the flux is the area of the shadow, $\pi$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Surface area">
$dS = \sqrt{4x^2 + 4y^2 + 1}\,dx\,dy$, so the area is $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 \sqrt{4r^2 + 1}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \frac{5\sqrt5 - 1}{12} \approx 5.33$, larger than the shadow's $\pi \approx 3.14$ because the surface is tilted.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Gauss's law in differential form">
For gravity, the divergence theorem turns Gauss's law (the outward flux of the gravitational field $\vec g$ through any closed surface is $-4\pi G$ times the mass inside) into a statement at each point: $\operatorname{div}\vec g = -4\pi G\rho$. With $\vec g = -\nabla\phi$ this is Poisson's equation, $\nabla^2\phi = 4\pi G\rho$, one of the equations behind the growth of galaxies from small density ripples; see [linear perturbation theory](/courses/8-902/linear-perturbations).
</Callout>

## Summary

- For a graph $z = f(x, y)$: $\hat n\,dS = \pm\langle -f_x, -f_y, 1\rangle\,dx\,dy$, from the cross product of the sides of a small parallelogram.
- The flux of $z\hat k$ up through $z = x^2 + y^2$ over the unit disc is $\pi/2$.
- Parametric surfaces: $\hat n\,dS = \pm(\vec r_u \times \vec r_v)\,du\,dv$. Implicit surfaces $g = 0$: $\hat n\,dS = \pm\frac{\nabla g}{\nabla g \cdot \hat k}\,dx\,dy$.
- Divergence theorem: $\int\int_S \vec F \cdot \hat n\,dS = \int\int\int_D \operatorname{div}\vec F\,dV$ for a closed surface oriented outwards; $\operatorname{div}\vec F = P_x + Q_y + R_z$.
- The divergence is the source rate; the outward flux is the total of the sources inside.
