The [last lecture](/courses/18-02/path-independence) showed that for gradient fields line integrals are easy. Two questions remain: how to tell whether a field is a gradient, and how to find its potential (transcript, p. 1).

## The test

If $\vec F = M\,\hat i + N\,\hat j$ is a gradient field, $M = f_x$ and $N = f_y$. Since the [mixed partial derivatives](/courses/18-02/second-derivative-test) are equal, $f_{xy} = f_{yx}$, so (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Gradient fields pass the test" tex="\vec F = \nabla f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N_x = M_y" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

**Conversely,** if $\vec F$ is **defined and differentiable everywhere in the plane** and $N_x = M_y$, then $\vec F$ is a gradient field (p. 1; notes, p. 1). The condition matters: a field defined everywhere except at one point can satisfy $N_x = M_y$ wherever it is defined and still not be a gradient, as an example on that week's problem set shows. The precise condition, a region without holes ("simply connected"), comes later (p. 1). So failing the test always proves a field is not a gradient, while passing it with a hole in the domain leaves the question open (pp. 1, 3).

**Examples** (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

- $\vec F = -y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j$: $M_y = -1$ but $N_x = 1$. Not a gradient field, as its work around the unit circle already showed.
- For which $a$ is $\vec F = (4x^2 + axy)\,\hat i + (3y^2 + 4x^2)\,\hat j$ a gradient field? $M_y = ax$ and $N_x = 8x$, which must agree **everywhere** (not just for some $x$): $a = 8$.

## Finding the potential: method 1, line integrals

Only try this once the test has been passed (transcript, p. 2). By the fundamental theorem, for any curve $C$ from the origin to $(x_1, y_1)$, $f(x_1, y_1) = f(0, 0) + \int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r$, where $f(0, 0)$ is an integration constant: adding a constant to a potential gives another potential (p. 2; notes, p. 1). Choose the simplest curve: along the $x$-axis to $(x_1, 0)$ (curve $C_1$), then straight up to $(x_1, y_1)$ ($C_2$). On each piece one of $dx$, $dy$ is zero (p. 2; notes, p. 1).

For $\vec F = (4x^2 + 8xy)\,\hat i + (3y^2 + 4x^2)\,\hat j$: on $C_1$, $y = 0$ and $dy = 0$; on $C_2$, $x = x_1$ and $dx = 0$ (p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The two pieces" tex="\int_{C_1} = \int_0^{x_1} 4x^2\,dx = \tfrac43 x_1^3, \qquad \int_{C_2} = \int_0^{y_1} (3y^2 + 4x_1^2)\,dy = y_1^3 + 4x_1^2 y_1" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

(The subscript 1 keeps the endpoint apart from the variables of integration, p. 2.) So (p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The potential" tex="f(x, y) = \tfrac43 x^3 + 4x^2y + y^3 \;(+\,c)" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 21, p. 1" />

The constant is optional: a potential plus 18 is still a potential (p. 2). Going up first and then across works as well; occasionally a radial path is better (p. 2).

## Method 2: antiderivatives

We want $f$ with (1) $f_x = 4x^2 + 8xy$ and (2) $f_y = 3y^2 + 4x^2$ (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1). Integrate (1) with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as a constant: $f = \frac43 x^3 + 4x^2y + g(y)$. The "constant" of integration does not depend on $x$, but it may depend on $y$ (pp. 2–3). Differentiate in $y$ and compare with (2) (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Matching f_y" tex="f_y = 4x^2 + g'(y) = 3y^2 + 4x^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g'(y) = 3y^2, \quad g(y) = y^3 + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

Substituting gives the same $f$. If $x$ were left in the equation for $g'(y)$, something would be wrong; check whether $\vec F$ really is a gradient (p. 3). A tempting shortcut, integrating (2) in $y$ as well and merging the two answers, is risky: equal terms can look different (such as $1 + \tan^2$ and $\sec^2$), so follow the procedure (p. 3). Both methods work in three variables too (p. 3).

## Curl

To summarise: $\vec F$ conservative ⟺ gradient field ⟹ $N_x = M_y$, and the converse holds when $\vec F$ is defined in the whole plane, or later in a simply connected region (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1). (An integral around a closed curve is often written $\oint_C$, p. 3.) The quantity that measures the failure to be conservative is the **curl** (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Curl" tex="\operatorname{curl}\vec F = N_x - M_y" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 21, p. 1" />

The test for a gradient field is $\operatorname{curl}\vec F = 0$ (p. 3).

**For a velocity field,** the curl measures the rotation, or **vorticity**, at each point: **twice the angular velocity** of the rotating part of the motion (pp. 3–4; notes, pp. 1–2). A uniform translation $\langle a, b\rangle$ and the expanding field $\langle x, y\rangle$ have curl 0; the rotation $\langle -y, x\rangle$ at unit angular velocity has curl $1 - (-1) = 2$ (p. 4; notes, p. 2). In weather maps, regions of high curl are hurricanes and tornadoes; the sign says clockwise or counterclockwise (p. 4).

**For a force field,** the curl measures the **torque** on a test object, per unit moment of inertia (p. 4; notes, p. 2). Just as force over mass is acceleration, the rate of change of velocity, torque over moment of inertia is angular acceleration, the rate of change of angular velocity (p. 4; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The rotational analogy" tex="\frac{\text{force}}{\text{mass}} = \frac{d}{dt}(\text{velocity}), \qquad \frac{\text{torque}}{\text{moment of inertia}} = \frac{d}{dt}(\text{angular velocity})" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 21, p. 2" />

(8.01 has these as [torque and angular acceleration](/courses/8-01/torque-and-angular-acceleration).) A light object floating in a fluid spins at half the curl of the velocity field (p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="Test, two methods, and a curl">
<Problem>
(a) Show that $\vec F = (2xy + 3)\,\hat i + (x^2 + 4y)\,\hat j$ is a gradient field and find a potential by antiderivatives. (b) Check $f(1, 2)$ by method 1. (c) Find the curl of $\vec G = x^2y\,\hat i$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Test and antiderivative">
$M_y = 2x = N_x$, and $\vec F$ is defined everywhere: a gradient field. Integrating $f_x = 2xy + 3$ in $x$: $f = x^2y + 3x + g(y)$. Then $f_y = x^2 + g'(y) = x^2 + 4y$, so $g'(y) = 4y$, $g = 2y^2$, and $f = x^2y + 3x + 2y^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Line integral to (1, 2)">
Along the $x$-axis ($y = 0$): $\int_0^1 3\,dx = 3$. Then up ($x = 1$): $\int_0^2 (1 + 4y)\,dy = 2 + 8 = 10$. Total 13, and indeed $f(1, 2) = 2 + 3 + 8 = 13$ (with $f(0, 0) = 0$).
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A curl">
$\operatorname{curl}\vec G = N_x - M_y = 0 - x^2 = -x^2$: not zero, so $\vec G$ is not a gradient field. The negative sign means clockwise rotation, strongest far from the $y$-axis.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="Constants and potentials">
A potential is defined only up to an added constant, like an antiderivative. In physics this is the familiar fact that only differences of potential energy, or of voltage, are meaningful: where the zero is put is a choice.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A gradient field $\vec F = \langle M, N\rangle$ has $N_x = M_y$; conversely, if $\vec F$ is defined everywhere in the plane and $N_x = M_y$, it is a gradient field.
- To find the potential: integrate along the $x$-axis and then vertically from the origin; or integrate $M$ in $x$, add $g(y)$, and match $f_y = N$.
- Example: $\langle 4x^2 + 8xy, 3y^2 + 4x^2\rangle = \nabla\left(\frac43 x^3 + 4x^2y + y^3\right)$.
- $\operatorname{curl}\vec F = N_x - M_y$ is zero for gradient fields; it is twice the angular velocity of a velocity field, and the torque per unit moment of inertia of a force field.
