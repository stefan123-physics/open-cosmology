This lecture uses partial derivatives to approximate a function, to find its minima and maxima, and to fit lines to experimental data (transcript, p. 1).

## The approximation formula

$f_x$ tells how $f$ changes when $x$ changes a little, $f_y$ when $y$ does. Changing both, the two effects add up, as if $x$ changed first and then $y$. For $z = f(x, y)$ (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Approximation formula" tex="\Delta z \approx f_x\,\Delta x + f_y\,\Delta y" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

It is one of the most important formulas about partial derivatives. It is only an approximation, for small $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$; there are higher-order terms with second and third derivatives (p. 1).

## The tangent plane

Why is the formula true? $f_x$ and $f_y$ are slopes of two lines tangent to the graph, in the slices parallel to the $xz$- and $yz$-planes. Writing $a = f_x(x_0, y_0)$ and $b = f_y(x_0, y_0)$, the two lines are (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Two tangent lines" tex="y = y_0,\ \ z = z_0 + a(x - x_0) \qquad\text{and}\qquad x = x_0,\ \ z = z_0 + b(y - y_0)" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Two lines through a point determine a plane, the **tangent plane** to the graph (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Tangent plane" tex="z = z_0 + f_x(x_0, y_0)\,(x - x_0) + f_y(x_0, y_0)\,(y - y_0)" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 9, p. 1" />

Holding $y = y_0$ gives the first line, holding $x = x_0$ the second. (The same plane comes out of the cross product of vectors along the two lines, as a normal vector, p. 1.) On the tangent plane $\Delta z$ would equal $f_x\Delta x + f_y\Delta y$ exactly; the approximation formula says that **the graph is close to its tangent plane** near the point (p. 1; notes, p. 1).

## Critical points

To minimise or maximise a function of one variable, set its derivative to zero. With several variables (p. 1): at a local minimum or maximum, **both** partial derivatives are zero. If $f_x = 0$ but $f_y \ne 0$, the point might be a minimum of the slice parallel to the $x$-axis, but changing $y$ would still make $f$ larger or smaller (pp. 1–2; notes, p. 1). And if both are zero, the approximation formula says $f$ does not change to first order: only quadratic or higher terms remain. Equivalently, the tangent plane is **horizontal**, $z = $ constant (p. 2; notes, p. 1).

<Formula label="Critical point" tex="f_x(x_0, y_0) = 0 \quad\text{and}\quad f_y(x_0, y_0) = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

These conditions are necessary but not sufficient (p. 2). A critical point can be a **local minimum** (like $x^2 + y^2$ at the origin; "local" because elsewhere the function may go lower), a **local maximum**, or a **saddle point** (like $y^2 - x^2$): a mountain pass, where the ground is level but some directions go up and others down. Saddles have no counterpart in one variable (p. 2; notes, p. 1).

## An example, and completing squares

Take the lecture's example $f(x, y) = x^2 - 2xy + 3y^2 + 2x - 2y$ (p. 2; notes, p. 1). Then $f_x = 2x - 2y + 2$ and $f_y = -2x + 6y - 2$. Setting both to zero: adding the equations gives $4y = 0$, so $y = 0$, and then $2x + 2 = 0$ gives $x = -1$. The only critical point is $(-1, 0)$ (p. 2; notes, p. 1). (A tip from the lecture: simplify two equations by combining them, but always keep two equations, p. 2.)

Is it a minimum? The general test comes next lecture; here **completing the square** works. $x^2 - 2xy$ starts $(x - y)^2$, and $2x - 2y = 2(x - y)$ completes a second square (p. 2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Completing the square" tex="f(x, y) = (x - y)^2 + 2y^2 + 2(x - y) = (x - y + 1)^2 + 2y^2 - 1 \ \ge\ -1" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

Squares are never negative, so $f \ge -1$, with equality only when $x - y + 1 = 0$ and $y = 0$: at the critical point $(-1, 0)$, which is therefore a **minimum** (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 2). (Auroux admits he built the example by expanding this form; in general things will not simplify so neatly, p. 2.)

## Least squares

In experimental science you measure pairs of numbers $(x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_n, y_n)$ that lie roughly on a line, and want the **best-fit line** $y = ax + b$ (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 2). The unknowns are **$a$ and $b$, not $x$ and $y$**: the data are given numbers (p. 3). (Here $a$ and $b$ are the line's slope and intercept, no longer the partial derivatives of the tangent plane.)

"Best" needs a definition. The usual choice measures, for each data point, the vertical **deviation** $y_i - (ax_i + b)$ between the measured and the predicted value (often more relevant than perpendicular distance, since $x$ and $y$ may be in different units), and minimises the **sum of the squares** (p. 3; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Total square deviation" tex="D(a, b) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(y_i - (ax_i + b)\big)^2" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

Minimising the largest deviation instead would give too much weight to one bad measurement; squares make the line look right and, above all, make the minimisation simple (p. 3). Look for a critical point of $D(a, b)$. By the chain rule (p. 3; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Critical point of D" tex="\frac{\partial D}{\partial a} = \sum 2\big(y_i - (ax_i + b)\big)(-x_i) = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial D}{\partial b} = \sum 2\big(y_i - (ax_i + b)\big)(-1) = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

Dropping the factors $-2$ and collecting the $a$'s and $b$'s gives a **2 × 2 linear system** for $a$ and $b$, whose coefficients are just numbers computed from the data (p. 4; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The least-squares equations" tex="\Big(\sum x_i^2\Big)a + \Big(\sum x_i\Big)b = \sum x_iy_i, \qquad \Big(\sum x_i\Big)a + n\,b = \sum y_i" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

The critical point found this way is in fact a minimum, which the lecture asks us to trust for now (p. 4).

## Other laws

The method is more general (p. 4; notes, p. 2). **Exponential laws** $y = ce^{ax}$: minimising the square error directly gives equations that cannot be solved easily, but $\ln y = \ln c + ax$ is linear, so fit a straight line to $\ln y$ against $x$, with $b = \ln c$. The lecture's example is **Moore's law**: the number of transistors on a chip, which does not follow a line but does on a log plot, growing exponentially (doubling every 18 months or two years, depending on the version). **Quadratic laws** $y = ax^2 + bx + c$: minimising the total square deviation gives a 3 × 3 linear system for $a$, $b$, $c$ (p. 4; notes, p. 2).

<WorkedExample title="A tangent plane, and a line through four points">
<Problem>
(a) Use the tangent plane of $f(x, y) = x^2 y$ at $(1, 2)$ to estimate $f(1.1, 1.9)$. (b) Find the least-squares line through $(0, 1)$, $(1, 3)$, $(2, 4)$, $(3, 6)$, and its total square deviation.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The tangent plane">
$f(1, 2) = 2$, $f_x = 2xy = 4$ and $f_y = x^2 = 1$, so the tangent plane is $z = 2 + 4(x - 1) + (y - 2)$. At $(1.1, 1.9)$: $z = 2 + 0.4 - 0.1 = 2.3$. Exactly, $f(1.1, 1.9) = 1.21 \times 1.9 = 2.299$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The sums">
With $n = 4$: $\sum x_i = 6$, $\sum x_i^2 = 0 + 1 + 4 + 9 = 14$, $\sum y_i = 14$, $\sum x_iy_i = 0 + 3 + 8 + 18 = 29$. The system is $14a + 6b = 29$ and $6a + 4b = 14$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Solve">
From the second equation $b = 3.5 - 1.5a$; then $14a + 21 - 9a = 29$, so $5a = 8$, $a = 1.6$ and $b = 1.1$: the line $y = 1.6x + 1.1$. Its deviations are $-0.1$, $0.3$, $-0.3$, $0.1$, so $D = 0.01 + 0.09 + 0.09 + 0.01 = 0.2$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Fitting Hubble's law">
Hubble's law $v = Hr$ is a straight line through the origin, and the Hubble constant is found from data by a fit of this kind. In the simplest version, with no $b$ to fit, $D(H) = \sum (v_i - Hr_i)^2$ and $dD/dH = 0$ gives $H = \sum r_iv_i / \sum r_i^2$. Real fits are more careful: the large uncertainties are in the distances rather than the velocities, so each galaxy is weighted by how well its distance is known, but the idea of minimising a total square deviation is the same. See [the homogeneous expanding universe](/courses/8-286/homogeneous-expansion).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Approximation formula: $\Delta z \approx f_x\Delta x + f_y\Delta y$; the graph is close to its tangent plane $z = z_0 + f_x(x - x_0) + f_y(y - y_0)$.
- At a local minimum or maximum $f_x = f_y = 0$: a critical point, with a horizontal tangent plane. Critical points can also be saddles.
- $x^2 - 2xy + 3y^2 + 2x - 2y = (x - y + 1)^2 + 2y^2 - 1$ has its minimum $-1$ at $(-1, 0)$.
- Least squares: minimise $D(a, b) = \sum (y_i - (ax_i + b))^2$; $\partial D/\partial a = \partial D/\partial b = 0$ is a 2 × 2 linear system for $a$ and $b$.
- Exponential laws are fitted as lines in $\ln y$; quadratic laws lead to a 3 × 3 system.
