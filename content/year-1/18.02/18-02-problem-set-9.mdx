## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Area as a line integral, and the area under a cycloid" points={4} mit="Part B, Problem 1">

The lesson on Green's theorem showed that the line integral $\oint_C x\,dy$ gives the area inside $C$. This problem adds a second line integral that does the same, and uses it on a curve that is known only by its parametric equations.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Let $C$ be a simple closed curve, positively oriented, and let $R$ be the region it encloses. Show that

<Formula label="Two line integrals for an area" tex="\text{area}(R) = \oint_C x\,dy = \oint_C -y\,dx" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

<Hint>

A line integral $\oint_C M\,dx + N\,dy$ has two functions in it, $M$ and $N$. Read off $M$ and $N$ for each of the two integrals. One of the two functions is zero each time.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use Green's theorem on each integral. The area of $R$ is the double integral of the function $1$ over $R$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Green's theorem turns each line integral into the double integral of $N_x - M_y$ over $R$, and for both integrals $N_x - M_y = 1$.

<Step title="Green's theorem">

For a positively oriented closed curve $C$ that encloses the region $R$:

<Formula label="Green's theorem" tex="\oint_C M\,dx + N\,dy = \int\int_R (N_x - M_y)\,dA" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 22, p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The first integral">

In $\oint_C x\,dy$ there is no $dx$ term, so $M = 0$ and $N = x$. Then $N_x = 1$ and $M_y = 0$:

<Formula label="The integral of x dy" tex="\oint_C x\,dy = \int\int_R (1 - 0)\,dA = \int\int_R 1\,dA = \text{area}(R)" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 22, p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="The second integral">

In $\oint_C -y\,dx$ there is no $dy$ term, so $M = -y$ and $N = 0$. Then $N_x = 0$ and $M_y = -1$:

<Formula label="The integral of minus y dx" tex="\oint_C -y\,dx = \int\int_R \big(0 - (-1)\big)\,dA = \int\int_R 1\,dA = \text{area}(R)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

Both functions, $x$ and $-y$, are defined and differentiable in the whole plane, so Green's theorem applies to every such curve $C$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Try the unit circle, $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$ with $t$ from $0$ to $2\pi$, which is counterclockwise. Then $x\,dy = \cos^2 t\,dt$ and $-y\,dx = \sin^2 t\,dt$. Each of $\cos^2 t$ and $\sin^2 t$ has the average value $\tfrac12$ over a full turn, so both integrals are $\tfrac12 \cdot 2\pi = \pi$, the area of the unit disk. Going around clockwise instead would change the sign of both integrals: that is why the curve must be positively oriented.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

A wheel of radius $a$ rolls along the $x$-axis. A point on its rim traces a cycloid, with the parametric equations

<Formula label="The cycloid" tex="x = a(t - \sin t), \qquad y = a(1 - \cos t)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

(The lesson on parametric equations calls the parameter $\theta$: it is the angle the wheel has turned.) One arch of the cycloid is the part with $0 \le t \le 2\pi$. Use the result of part (a) to find the area of the region between the $x$-axis and this arch, in terms of $a$. MIT's sheet adds a tip: the second line integral of part (a), $\oint_C -y\,dx$, leads to the easier calculation.

<Hint>

The boundary of the region has two pieces: a segment of the $x$-axis and the arch. On which of the two is $-y\,dx$ zero?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Mind the direction. With the region on your left, you go along the $x$-axis to the right and come back along the arch, so on the arch $t$ runs from $2\pi$ down to $0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the arch, $dx = a(1 - \cos t)\,dt$. You will need the integral of $\cos^2 t$ over a full turn: its average value is $\tfrac12$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="Area $=$" expr="3*pi*a^2"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-3*pi*a^2", hint: "An area is positive. With t increasing, the arch runs from left to right with the region on its right, which is the clockwise direction. Go around the region counterclockwise." },
    { expr: "2*pi*a^2", hint: "Check the square (1 − cos t)². It has a term cos² t, and the integral of cos² t over a full turn is π, not 0." },
    { expr: "pi*a^2", hint: "That is the area of the rolling wheel itself. Write out the square (1 − cos t)² and integrate all three of its terms." },
    { expr: "4*pi*a^2", hint: "That is the area of the rectangle around the arch, 2πa wide and 2a high. The arch does not fill it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Go around the region counterclockwise. The piece on the $x$-axis adds nothing to $\oint -y\,dx$, and on the arch everything is a function of $t$.

<Step title="The boundary and its direction">

The arch starts at the origin ($t = 0$) and comes back to the $x$-axis at $x = 2\pi a$ ($t = 2\pi$). The boundary $C$ of the region is made of two pieces (the names are ours): $C_1$, the segment of the $x$-axis from $(0, 0)$ to $(2\pi a, 0)$, and $C_2$, the arch. Going to the right along $C_1$ the region is above you, on your left, so that is the positive direction. To close the curve you return along the arch from right to left: on $C_2$ the parameter $t$ runs from $2\pi$ down to $0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The two pieces">

On $C_1$, $y = 0$, so $-y\,dx = 0$ and this piece adds nothing. On $C_2$, $dx = a(1 - \cos t)\,dt$ and $y = a(1 - \cos t)$:

<Formula label="The integral along the arch" tex="\oint_C -y\,dx = \int_{2\pi}^{0} -a^2(1 - \cos t)^2\,dt = a^2\int_0^{2\pi} (1 - \cos t)^2\,dt" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

Swapping the limits of the integral removed the minus sign.

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

Write out the square: $(1 - \cos t)^2 = 1 - 2\cos t + \cos^2 t$. Over a full turn, the $1$ gives $2\pi$, the term with $\cos t$ gives $0$, and $\cos^2 t$, with average value $\tfrac12$, gives $\pi$:

<Formula label="The area under one arch of the cycloid" tex="\text{area} = a^2(2\pi - 0 + \pi) = 3\pi a^2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The answer is a length squared, as an area must be. It is three times the area $\pi a^2$ of the rolling wheel. The arch fits in a rectangle $2\pi a$ wide and $2a$ high, of area $4\pi a^2$, and fills three quarters of it, which agrees with the plot.

A second check is the other line integral of part (a), $\oint_C x\,dy$. On $C_1$, $dy = 0$. On $C_2$, $dy = a\sin t\,dt$, with $t$ again from $2\pi$ down to $0$:

<Formula label="The same area from the integral of x dy" tex="\oint_C x\,dy = -a^2\int_0^{2\pi} (t\sin t - \sin^2 t)\,dt = -a^2(-2\pi - \pi) = 3\pi a^2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

Here $\int_0^{2\pi} t\sin t\,dt = -2\pi$ needs an integration by parts, which is why MIT's tip points to the other integral.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The curve that makes a line integral largest" points={6} mit="Part B, Problem 2">

Every simple closed curve $C$ in the plane, positively oriented, gives a value to the line integral

<Formula label="The line integral of Problem 2" tex="\oint_C (x^2y + y^3 - y)\,dx + (3x + 2y^2x + e^y)\,dy" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a), p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

For which curve $C$ does this line integral have its largest positive value? Use Green's theorem. First find the function $N_x - M_y$ that Green's theorem integrates over the region inside $C$, then decide which curve is best.

<Hint>

Here $M = x^2y + y^3 - y$ and $N = 3x + 2y^2x + e^y$. Differentiate $N$ with respect to $x$ and $M$ with respect to $y$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

By Green's theorem the line integral equals the double integral of $N_x - M_y$ over the region $R$ inside $C$. You are free to choose $R$. Where in the plane is $N_x - M_y$ positive, and where is it negative?

</Hint>

<Hint>

A piece of $R$ where the function is positive makes the double integral larger. A piece where it is negative makes it smaller.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a-curl" label="$N_x - M_y =$" expr="4 - x^2 - y^2"
  vars={{ x: [0.2, 1.2], y: [0.2, 1.2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x^2 + y^2 - 4", hint: "That is M_y − N_x. Green's theorem integrates N_x − M_y." },
    { expr: "3 - x^2 - y^2", hint: "Check the derivative of M with respect to y: its last term, −y, gives −1." },
    { expr: "4 - x^2 - y^2 + exp(y)", hint: "The term e^y of N does not depend on x, so its derivative with respect to x is 0." },
    { expr: "3 + 2*y^2", hint: "That is N_x alone. Subtract M_y, the derivative of x²y + y³ − y with respect to y." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-a-curve" label="The line integral is largest for"
  choices={["the unit circle x² + y² = 1, counterclockwise", "the circle x² + y² = 16, of radius 4, counterclockwise", "the circle x² + y² = 4, of radius 2, counterclockwise", "no curve: a large enough curve makes the integral as large as you like"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Green's theorem replaces the line integral by a double integral over the region inside $C$. The double integral is largest when the region holds every point where the integrated function is positive and no point where it is negative.

<Step title="The function that Green's theorem integrates">

With $M = x^2y + y^3 - y$ and $N = 3x + 2y^2x + e^y$:

<Formula label="The two partial derivatives" tex="N_x = 3 + 2y^2, \qquad M_y = x^2 + 3y^2 - 1" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="The curl of the field of Problem 2" tex="N_x - M_y = 4 - x^2 - y^2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

$M$ and $N$ are defined and differentiable in the whole plane, so Green's theorem holds for every simple closed curve $C$, with $R$ the region inside it:

<Formula label="The line integral as a double integral" tex="\oint_C M\,dx + N\,dy = \int\int_R (4 - x^2 - y^2)\,dA" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Where the function is positive">

The function $4 - x^2 - y^2$ is positive where $x^2 + y^2 < 4$, inside the circle of radius $2$ about the origin. It is zero on that circle and negative outside it.

</Step>

<Step title="The best region">

Take $R$ to be the whole disk $x^2 + y^2 < 4$, so that $C$ is the circle of radius $2$ about the origin, counterclockwise. Compare it with the region $R'$ inside any other curve. The part of $R'$ outside the disk adds a negative amount to its double integral, or nothing. The part of $R'$ inside the disk adds at most what the whole disk adds, because the part of the disk that $R'$ leaves out would have added a positive amount, or nothing. So the double integral over $R'$ is never larger than the one over the disk, and it is smaller as soon as $R'$ leaves out a piece of the disk with some area or holds a piece with some area outside the circle.

The answer: $C$ is the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 4$, with centre at the origin and radius $2$, traversed counterclockwise.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The last choice in the box fails because far from the origin the function is very negative: a huge curve gives a large negative value. The value for the best curve, and a comparison with other circles, follow in part (b).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Find this largest value of the line integral. Give it as a decimal number, to two decimal places.

<Hint>

Do not compute the line integral around the circle. Compute the double integral of part (a) over the disk.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use polar coordinates: $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$ and $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="Largest value:" value="25.13" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** By Green's theorem the largest value is the double integral of $4 - x^2 - y^2$ over the disk of radius $2$, which is easy in polar coordinates.

<Step title="Polar coordinates">

The disk is $0 \le r \le 2$, $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$, and the function is $4 - r^2$:

<Formula label="The double integral over the disk of radius 2" tex="\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2 (4 - r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two integrals">

The inner integral is $\int_0^2 (4r - r^3)\,dr = \big[2r^2 - \tfrac14 r^4\big]_0^2 = 8 - 4 = 4$. It does not depend on $\theta$, so the outer integral multiplies it by $2\pi$:

<Formula label="The largest value of the line integral" tex="\oint_C M\,dx + N\,dy = 2\pi \cdot 4 = 8\pi = 25.13" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Compare with other circles about the origin (this comparison is ours). For the circle of radius $b$, the same calculation with $b$ as upper limit gives

<Formula label="The line integral around a circle of radius b" tex="I(b) = 2\pi\left(2b^2 - \tfrac14 b^4\right)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own check)" />

Its derivative, $2\pi(4b - b^3)$, is zero for $b > 0$ only at $b = 2$, where it changes from positive to negative: among these circles the one of radius $2$ is the best, as part (a) says. The unit circle gives $I(1) = \tfrac72\pi = 11.00$, less than half of $8\pi$. The circle of radius $3$ already gives a negative value, $I(3) = -\tfrac92\pi = -14.14$, and $I(b)$ passes through zero at $b = 2\sqrt2 = 2.83$.

<Plot variable="b" domain={[0, 3.1]} range={[-30, 30]} xLabel="b" yLabel="I(b)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "2*pi*(2*b^2 - b^4/4)", label: "I(b)" }
  ]}
  caption="The value of the line integral around the circle of radius b about the origin, counterclockwise, computed from I(b) = 2π(2b² − b⁴/4). The curve is highest at b = 2." />

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Flux across the unit circle, twice" points={6} mit="Part B, Problem 3">

Let $C$ be the unit circle, oriented counterclockwise, and take the vector field

<Formula label="The field of Problem 3" tex="\vec F = xy\,\hat i + y^2\,\hat j" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(a), p. 1" />

The flux of $\vec F$ across $C$ is $\oint_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds$, where $\hat n$ is the unit normal vector that points to the right of the direction of travel. For a counterclockwise circle that is outwards.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Which portions of $C$ contribute positively to the flux of $\vec F$, and which negatively? To decide, find the normal component $\vec F \cdot \hat n$ at the point $(\cos t, \sin t)$ of the circle, as a function of $t$.

<Hint>

At the point $(x, y)$ of the unit circle, the outward unit normal is the position vector itself: $\hat n = \langle x, y\rangle$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Take the dot product of $\vec F$ and $\hat n$ and factor it. On the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$\vec F \cdot \hat n =$" expr="sin(t)"
  vars={{ t: [0.3, 1.2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "cos(t)*sin(t) + sin(t)^2", hint: "That is the sum of the two components of F. The normal component is the dot product of F with n̂ = ⟨x, y⟩." },
    { expr: "-sin(t)", hint: "That uses the normal that points into the circle. For a counterclockwise curve, n̂ points to the right of the direction of travel, which is outwards." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "That is the component of F along the circle, F · T̂: this field points along the radius, so it has no component along the circle. Flux uses the component along n̂." },
    { expr: "sin(t)^2", hint: "That is only the ĵ component of F. Take the dot product with n̂ = ⟨x, y⟩, using both components." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-a-sign" label="The contribution to the flux is"
  choices={["positive on the right half of the circle (x > 0) and negative on the left half", "positive in the first and third quadrants (where xy > 0) and negative in the other two", "positive on the whole circle except at the points (1, 0) and (−1, 0)", "positive on the upper half of the circle (y > 0) and negative on the lower half (y < 0)"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A piece of the curve contributes positively where the field crosses it outwards, where $\vec F \cdot \hat n > 0$, and negatively where the field crosses it inwards.

<Step title="The normal component">

On the unit circle the outward unit normal at $(x, y)$ is $\hat n = \langle x, y\rangle$, so

<Formula label="The normal component of the field on the unit circle" tex="\vec F \cdot \hat n = (xy)\,x + (y^2)\,y = y\,(x^2 + y^2) = y = \sin t" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The sign">

So $\vec F \cdot \hat n$ has the sign of $y$. The upper half of the circle ($y > 0$, $0 < t < \pi$) contributes positively and the lower half ($y < 0$, $\pi < t < 2\pi$) negatively. At the two points $(1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0)$ the field is zero and contributes nothing.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The field can be written as $\vec F = y\,\langle x, y\rangle$: at every point it is $y$ times the position vector. Above the $x$-axis it points away from the origin, so it leaves the circle. Below the $x$-axis it points towards the origin, so it enters the circle. At the top of the circle, $(0, 1)$, the field is $\hat j$ and $\hat n = \hat j$, so $\vec F \cdot \hat n = 1$, which is $\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Find the flux of $\vec F$ across $C$ by direct calculation, as a line integral. Explain your answer with part (a). As a step towards the explanation (this extra question is ours), find also the flux across the upper half of the circle alone, from $(1, 0)$ counterclockwise to $(-1, 0)$. Both values come out exact, so no rounding is needed.

<Hint>

In coordinates, $\hat n\,ds = \langle dy, -dx\rangle$, so the flux of $\langle P, Q\rangle$ is $\int_C -Q\,dx + P\,dy$. Put in $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

After the substitution, factor out $\sin t$ and use $\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t = 1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b-upper" label="Flux across the upper half:" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-b-upper" />

<Answer id="p3-b" label="Flux across the whole circle:" value="0" tolerance="0.001" calc="p3-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write the flux as an ordinary line integral in $x$ and $y$, and put in the parametric equations of the circle.

<Step title="Flux in coordinates">

For a field $\vec F = \langle P, Q\rangle$:

<Formula label="Flux as a line integral" tex="\int_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \int_C -Q\,dx + P\,dy" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 23, p. 3" />

Here $P = xy$ and $Q = y^2$, so the flux is $\oint_C -y^2\,dx + xy\,dy$.

</Step>

<Step title="On the circle">

With $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$: $dx = -\sin t\,dt$ and $dy = \cos t\,dt$, so

<Formula label="The flux integrand on the unit circle" tex="-y^2\,dx + xy\,dy = (\sin^3 t + \cos^2 t\,\sin t)\,dt = \sin t\,dt" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

This agrees with part (a): on the unit circle $ds = dt$, and $\vec F \cdot \hat n = \sin t$.

</Step>

<Step title="The two integrals">

<Formula label="Flux across the upper half and across the whole circle" tex="\int_0^{\pi} \sin t\,dt = 2, \qquad \int_0^{2\pi} \sin t\,dt = 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

The flux across the whole unit circle is $0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The explanation">

By part (a) the upper half of the circle contributes positively and the lower half negatively. The two halves cancel exactly, because the normal component $y$ has opposite values at the points $(x, y)$ and $(x, -y)$, which are mirror images in the $x$-axis: the upper half gives $2$ and the lower half $-2$. As much flows out through the top as flows in through the bottom.

  curves={[

<Hint>

The unit disk is symmetric about the $x$-axis. What does that do to the integral of a function that changes sign when $y$ does?

<Answer id="p3-c" label="$\operatorname{div}\vec F =$" expr="3*y"
  vars={{ x: [0.5, 2], y: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x + 2*y", hint: "The first term must be P_x, the derivative of xy with respect to x. You took its derivative with respect to y." },
    { expr: "-x", hint: "That is the curl, Q_x − P_y (N_x − M_y in the notation of Green's theorem). Flux needs the divergence, P_x + Q_y." },
    { expr: "2*y", hint: "That is Q_y alone. Add P_x, the derivative of xy with respect to x." },
    { expr: "y", hint: "That is P_x alone. Add Q_y, the derivative of y² with respect to y." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a closed curve, Green's theorem in normal form replaces the flux by the double integral of the divergence over the region inside.

<Step title="The theorem">

For $C$ counterclockwise around the region $R$, and $\vec F = \langle P, Q\rangle$:

<Formula label="Green's theorem in normal form" tex="\oint_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \int\int_R \operatorname{div}\vec F\,dA, \qquad \operatorname{div}\vec F = P_x + Q_y" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 23, p. 4" />

</Step>

<Step title="The divergence">

With $P = xy$ and $Q = y^2$:

<Formula label="The divergence of the field of Problem 3" tex="\operatorname{div}\vec F = y + 2y = 3y" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The double integral">

Here $R$ is the unit disk. In polar coordinates $y = r\sin\theta$ and $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$:

<Formula label="The flux by Green's theorem" tex="\int\int_R 3y\,dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 3r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \sin\theta\,d\theta = 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

The inner integral is $\int_0^1 3r^2\,dr = 1$. No calculation is needed either: the disk is symmetric about the $x$-axis and $3y$ changes sign when $y$ does, so the upper and lower half disks cancel. The flux is $0$, as in part (b).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The divergence $3y$ is positive above the $x$-axis and negative below it: the flow expands above the $x$-axis and contracts below it, by the same total amount. Take the upper half disk alone. Its double integral is $\int_0^{\pi} \sin\theta\,d\theta = 2$. Its boundary is the upper half of the circle and the diameter on the $x$-axis, where $\vec F = \vec 0$ because $y = 0$. So the flux out of the half disk is the flux across the upper half circle, which was $2$ in part (b). The two methods agree on the half as well as on the whole.

</Step>

</Solution>
