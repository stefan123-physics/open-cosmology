This lecture finishes the [divergence theorem](/courses/18-02/divergence-theorem): a notation, its physical meaning, a proof, and an application to diffusion (transcript, p. 1).

## The del notation

The symbol $\nabla$, read "del", is a **notation**: a formal vector of differentiation operators, not a real vector (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Del" tex="\nabla = \left\langle \frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial}{\partial z}\right\rangle, \qquad \nabla f = \langle f_x, f_y, f_z\rangle, \qquad \nabla \cdot \vec F = P_x + Q_y + R_z" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 29, p. 1" />

"Multiplying" it by a function gives the gradient; its dot product with $\vec F = \langle P, Q, R\rangle$ gives the divergence (p. 1). It becomes really useful for the curl, $\nabla \times \vec F$, next week (p. 1).

## What the theorem says physically

The divergence is the **source rate**: the flux generated per unit volume (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1). Think of an **incompressible** fluid, such as water, where a given mass always occupies the same volume (gases are compressible), flowing with velocity $\vec F$. With the normal pointing outwards (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Physical meaning" tex="\int\int\int_D \operatorname{div}\vec F\,dV = \int\int_S \vec F \cdot \hat n\,dS = \text{net amount leaving } D \text{ per unit time}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

which, since the fluid cannot be compressed, must be the total amount of sources (minus sinks) inside $D$ (p. 1; notes, p. 1). A purely rotating flow has zero divergence (p. 1). The divergence is not the gradient: the gradient takes a function to a vector field, the divergence a vector field to a function (pp. 1–2). And this fluid picture does not quite fit force fields such as electric fields (p. 2).

## Proof of the divergence theorem

**Split into components.** It is enough to prove $\int\int_S R\,\hat k \cdot \hat n\,dS = \int\int\int_D R_z\,dV$, and the same for $P$ and $Q$, then add (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1).

**A vertically simple region**: bottom and top are graphs, $z_1(x, y) \le z \le z_2(x, y)$, over a region $U$ of the $xy$-plane (p. 2; notes, p. 1). The triple integral, $z$ first (p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Triple integral side" tex="\int\int\int_D R_z\,dV = \int\int_U \int_{z_1}^{z_2} R_z\,dz\,dx\,dy = \int\int_U \Big(R\big(x, y, z_2(x, y)\big) - R\big(x, y, z_1(x, y)\big)\Big)dx\,dy" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 29, p. 1" />

The flux has three parts (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 1):

- **top**: $\hat n\,dS = \langle -\partial z_2/\partial x, -\partial z_2/\partial y, 1\rangle\,dx\,dy$ (the [graph formula](/courses/18-02/divergence-theorem)), so the flux of $R\,\hat k$ is $\int\int_U R(x, y, z_2)\,dx\,dy$;
- **bottom**: the outward normal points down, $\hat n\,dS = -\langle -\partial z_1/\partial x, -\partial z_1/\partial y, 1\rangle\,dx\,dy$, giving $-\int\int_U R(x, y, z_1)\,dx\,dy$;
- **sides**: vertical, so $\hat n$ is horizontal while $R\,\hat k$ is vertical: no flux.

The sum matches the triple integral (p. 3). **Any region** can be cut into vertically simple pieces (Auroux shows a doughnut): the triple integrals add up, and the fluxes through the cuts cancel, being counted twice with opposite normals (p. 3; notes, p. 1). The idea is the same as for Green's theorem (p. 3).

## The diffusion equation

**Diffusion** is how a substance spreads in a still fluid: smoke in air, ink dropped in water (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1). Let $u(x, y, z, t)$ be the concentration. The **diffusion equation** is a partial differential equation for it (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Diffusion equation" tex="\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = k\,\nabla^2 u = k\left(\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial z^2}\right)" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

where $\nabla^2 u = \nabla \cdot \nabla u$ is the **Laplacian**. It is also the **heat equation**, with $u$ the temperature (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 1). It comes from two inputs (p. 4; notes, p. 1):

1. **Physics.** Let $\vec F$ be the flow of smoke. It goes from high to low concentration, faster where the concentration changes more abruptly: $\vec F = -k\nabla u$.
2. **Conservation.** For a region $D$ with boundary $S$, the flux out of $D$ is minus the rate of change of the smoke inside. By the divergence theorem on the left, and by differentiating under the integral sign on the right (p. 4; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Conservation of smoke" tex="\int\int\int_D \operatorname{div}\vec F\,dV = \int\int_S \vec F \cdot \hat n\,dS = -\frac{d}{dt}\int\int\int_D u\,dV = -\int\int\int_D \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\,dV" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

Dividing by the volume of $D$: the averages of $\operatorname{div}\vec F$ and of $-\partial u/\partial t$ over $D$ are equal, for **every** region. Shrinking $D$ to a point gives $\partial u/\partial t = -\operatorname{div}\vec F$ (pp. 4–5; notes, p. 1). Combined with $\vec F = -k\nabla u$, this is $\partial u/\partial t = k\nabla \cdot \nabla u = k\nabla^2 u$ (p. 5; notes, p. 1).

<WorkedExample title="Checking two solutions of the diffusion equation">
<Problem>
(a) Find the Laplacian of $u = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$, and find $a$ so that $u = a\,t + x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ solves $u_t = k\nabla^2 u$. (b) Check that $u = e^{-3k t}\sin x\sin y\sin z$ solves the equation.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A steadily rising solution">
$\nabla^2(x^2 + y^2 + z^2) = 2 + 2 + 2 = 6$. With $u = at + x^2 + y^2 + z^2$, $u_t = a$ and $k\nabla^2 u = 6k$, so $a = 6k$: the concentration rises everywhere at the same rate, fed from far away where $u$ is large.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A decaying pattern">
Each second derivative of $\sin x\sin y\sin z$ in $x$, $y$ or $z$ gives minus the function, so $\nabla^2 u = -3u$ and $k\nabla^2 u = -3k u$. And $u_t = -3k u$. They agree: the pattern keeps its shape and fades exponentially.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Conservation laws">
The same argument (flux out equals the loss inside, then the divergence theorem, then shrinking the region) turns any conservation law into a local equation $\partial u/\partial t + \operatorname{div}\vec F = 0$, the continuity equation. In fluid descriptions of matter, as in the theory of [structure formation](/courses/8-902/linear-perturbations), it expresses conservation of mass.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\nabla = \langle \partial/\partial x, \partial/\partial y, \partial/\partial z\rangle$: $\nabla f$ is the gradient, $\nabla \cdot \vec F$ the divergence.
- For an incompressible flow, the divergence is the source rate, and the divergence theorem says outflow equals sources inside.
- Proof: one component at a time; on a vertically simple region the top and bottom fluxes of $R\hat k$ give $\int\int\int R_z\,dV$ and the sides give nothing; cut other regions into such pieces.
- Diffusion (heat) equation: $\partial u/\partial t = k\nabla^2 u$, from $\vec F = -k\nabla u$ and conservation, $\partial u/\partial t = -\operatorname{div}\vec F$.
