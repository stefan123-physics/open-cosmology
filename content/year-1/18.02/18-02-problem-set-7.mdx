## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="An integral done by exchanging the order of integration" points={3} mit="Part B, Problem 1">

Let $a$ be a positive number. The aim is the value of

<Formula label="The integral to evaluate" tex="\int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-x} - e^{-ax}}{x}\,dx" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1, p. 1" />

Neither of the two terms can be integrated on its own, and the quotient has no antiderivative in terms of the usual functions. A double integral gets round this, in the two steps below.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

Compute the integral of $e^{-xy}$ over $y$, from $y = 1$ to $y = a$. Here $x$ is a fixed positive number.

<Hint>

In this integral $x$ is a constant and $y$ is the variable. What is an antiderivative of $e^{ky}$ for a constant $k$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$\int_1^a e^{-xy}\,dy =$" expr="(exp(-x) - exp(-a*x))/x"
  vars={{ x: [1.5, 3], a: [1.5, 4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(exp(-a*x) - exp(-x))/x", hint: "The sign is the wrong way round. The antiderivative is −e^(−xy)/x, with a minus sign. For a > 1 the integral of a positive function from 1 to a is positive, and yours is negative." },
    { expr: "exp(-x) - exp(-a*x)", hint: "Integrating e^(−xy) over y divides by the number in front of y in the exponent. That number is −x, and your answer does not divide by x." },
    { expr: "x*(exp(-x) - exp(-a*x))", hint: "You multiplied by x. That is what differentiating over y does. Integrating divides by x." },
    { expr: "(1 - exp(-a*x))/x", hint: "This is the integral from y = 0 to y = a. The lower limit is y = 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $x$ held fixed, $e^{-xy}$ is an exponential function of $y$ with the constant $-x$ in front of $y$.

<Step title="Integrate over y">

An antiderivative of $e^{-xy}$ over $y$ is $-e^{-xy}/x$. So

<Formula label="The integral over y" tex="\int_1^a e^{-xy}\,dy = \left[-\frac{e^{-xy}}{x}\right]_{y=1}^{y=a} = \frac{e^{-x} - e^{-ax}}{x}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate $-e^{-xy}/x$ over $y$: the chain rule gives the factor $-x$, which cancels the $-1/x$, and $e^{-xy}$ is left. For $a = 1$ the result is $0$, as it must be for an integral from $1$ to $1$. And the result is exactly the function inside the integral of this problem: that is why MIT asks for it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Use part (a) to rewrite the integral of this problem as a double integral. Then evaluate the double integral by exchanging the order of integration. Your answer is a formula in $a$.

The region of this double integral is not bounded: it goes on without end in one direction. The lesson exchanged the order of integration only for bounded regions. Here you may take for granted that the exchange is allowed (the solution says a little more about this).

<Hint>

By part (a), the function inside the integral is itself an integral over $y$. Put that inner integral in its place. You now have an iterated integral: first over $y$, then over $x$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Draw the region in the $xy$-plane, as the lesson does before it exchanges the order: which values of $x$, and for each of them which values of $y$? Its sides are parallel to the axes, so the limits stay the same numbers when the order changes.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the new inner integral, over $x$ from $0$ to $\infty$, the number $y$ is a positive constant. This integral can be done.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$\int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-x} - e^{-ax}}{x}\,dx =$" expr="log(a)"
  vars={{ a: [1.5, 4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-log(a)", hint: "The sign is wrong. For a > 1 and x > 0, e^(−x) is larger than e^(−ax), so the integral is positive." },
    { expr: "a - 1", hint: "This is the integral of 1 over y from 1 to a. The inner integral over x, of e^(−xy) from 0 to ∞, is not 1: it depends on y." },
    { expr: "1 - 1/a", hint: "This is the integral of 1/y² from 1 to a. The inner integral over x gives 1/y, not 1/y²." },
    { expr: "1/a", hint: "This is the inner integral over x, taken at y = a. It still has to be integrated over y from 1 to a." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Part (a) turns the quotient into an integral over $y$. The integral over $x$ of that integral is a double integral over a strip, and in the other order both integrals are easy.

<Step title="Write it as a double integral">

Take $a > 1$ first. Replace the function inside the integral by the integral of part (a):

<Formula label="The integral as an iterated integral" tex="\int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-x} - e^{-ax}}{x}\,dx = \int_0^\infty \int_1^a e^{-xy}\,dy\,dx" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

This is the double integral of $e^{-xy}$ over the region $R$ where $x \ge 0$ and $1 \le y \le a$: a strip of height $a - 1$ that starts at the $y$-axis and runs to the right without end.

</Step>

<Step title="Exchange the order">

The lesson's method is to draw the region and read off the limits in the other direction. Fix a value of $y$ between $1$ and $a$: the slice at that height runs from $x = 0$ to $x = \infty$, whatever $y$ is. The first slice is $y = 1$ and the last is $y = a$:

<Formula label="The exchanged integral" tex="\int_0^\infty \int_1^a e^{-xy}\,dy\,dx = \int_1^a \int_0^\infty e^{-xy}\,dx\,dy" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The inner integral, over x">

Now $y$ is a positive constant. An antiderivative of $e^{-xy}$ over $x$ is $-e^{-xy}/y$. It is $-1/y$ at $x = 0$ and goes to $0$ as $x \to \infty$, because $y > 0$:

<Formula label="The inner integral" tex="\int_0^\infty e^{-xy}\,dx = \left[-\frac{e^{-xy}}{y}\right]_{x=0}^{x\to\infty} = 0 + \frac{1}{y} = \frac{1}{y}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The outer integral, over y">

<Formula label="The value of the integral" tex="\int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-x} - e^{-ax}}{x}\,dx = \int_1^a \frac{1}{y}\,dy = \ln a - \ln 1 = \ln a" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

For $0 < a < 1$ nothing changes in the formulas. The integral of part (a) then runs backwards, from $1$ down to $a$, which makes it negative, and $\ln a$ is negative too. So the result holds for every $a > 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $a = 1$ the function inside the integral is $0$ for every $x$, and $\ln 1 = 0$. For $a > 1$ and $x > 0$ the function is positive, and so is $\ln a$.

A number, for $a = 2$: the result is $\ln 2 = 0.693$. A computer that adds up the area under $(e^{-x} - e^{-2x})/x$ from $0$ to $\infty$ finds the same $0.693$ (this site's calculation file does that).

About the start of the integral: at $x = 0$ the quotient reads $0/0$. But for small $x$ the linear approximation $e^{-x} \approx 1 - x$ and $e^{-ax} \approx 1 - ax$ gives $(e^{-x} - e^{-ax})/x \approx a - 1$, a finite number. So only the infinite range needs care.

About the exchange: the lesson's rule (draw the region, then slice the other way) was shown for bounded regions. For this endless strip it needs a theorem from a later course in analysis, which says that the order may always be exchanged when the function is positive, as $e^{-xy}$ is. Here we take that for granted, as MIT's problem does.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The average distance to the centre of a disc" points={2} mit="Part B, Problem 2">

Take a disc of radius $a$. Show that the average distance from the points of the disc to its centre is $2a/3$.

This problem has no answer box: compare your proof with the solution and mark it yourself.

<Hint>

The lesson's average of a function $f$ over a region $R$ is the double integral of $f$ over $R$, divided by the area of $R$. Which function is the distance to the centre?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put the centre at the origin and use polar coordinates: the distance to the centre is then $r$ itself. Do not forget the factor $r$ in the area element.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** An average over a region is a double integral divided by the area. With the centre at the origin, the distance to the centre is the polar coordinate $r$.

<Step title="Set up">

The lesson's average value is

<Formula label="Average value of a function over a region" tex="\bar f = \frac{1}{\text{Area}(R)}\int\int_R f\,dA" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 17, p. 2" />

Put the centre of the disc at the origin. Then $R$ is the region $0 \le r \le a$, $0 \le \theta \le 2\pi$, with area $\pi a^2$, and the function to average is $f = r$. In polar coordinates the area element is $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$.

</Step>

<Step title="Integrate">

<Formula label="The integral of the distance over the disc" tex="\int\int_R r\,dA = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a r \cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{a^3}{3}\,d\theta = \frac{2\pi a^3}{3}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Divide by the area">

<Formula label="The average distance to the centre" tex="\bar r = \frac{1}{\pi a^2} \cdot \frac{2\pi a^3}{3} = \frac{2a}{3}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is a length, as it should be: $a^3$ divided by $a^2$. It lies between the smallest distance, $0$, and the largest, $a$. It is more than half the radius, and that makes sense: the points at a distance near $r$ from the centre fill a thin ring whose area grows with $r$, so there are more points far from the centre than close to it. Without the factor $r$ of the area element the result would have been $a/2$, the average of $r$ along one radius, which counts all distances equally often.

The lesson made the same point in words: for a disc centred at the origin the average of $r$ is positive, although the centre of mass is at the origin.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The parallel axis theorem" points={4} mit="Part B, Problem 3">

A flat plate fills a region $R$ of the plane and has density $\delta$, which may change from point to point. Its moment of inertia about any line $L$ in the plane is the integral, over the plate, of the squared distance to that line times the density:

<Formula label="Moment of inertia about a line L" tex="I_L = \int\int_R (\text{distance to } L)^2\,\delta\,dA" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3, p. 1" />

The lines parallel to the $y$-axis are the vertical lines $x = a$. One of them is the $y$-axis itself. Write $I$ for the moment of inertia about the $y$-axis:

<Formula label="Moment of inertia about the y-axis" tex="I = I_y = \int\int_R x^2\,\delta\,dA" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3, p. 1" />

Let $M$ be the mass of the plate and $(\bar x, \bar y)$ its centre of mass. Write $\bar I$ for the moment of inertia about the vertical line through the centre of mass, the line $x = \bar x$. Show that

<Formula label="The parallel axis theorem" tex="I = \bar I + M\bar x^2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3, p. 2" />

This problem has no answer box: compare your proof with the solution and mark it yourself.

<Hint>

Write $\bar I$ as a double integral. What is the distance from a point $(x, y)$ to the vertical line $x = \bar x$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Multiply out the square. The number $\bar x$ is a constant: it can be taken outside every integral.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Three integrals appear. Compare them with the lesson's formulas for the mass $M$ and for $\bar x$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write $\bar I$ with the squared distance $(x - \bar x)^2$, multiply out, and recognise the mass and the centre of mass in the pieces.

<Step title="The lesson's formulas">

<Formula label="Mass and centre of mass of a plate" tex="M = \int\int_R \delta\,dA, \qquad \bar x = \frac{1}{M}\int\int_R x\,\delta\,dA" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 17, p. 2" />

The second one will be used in the form $\int\int_R x\,\delta\,dA = M\bar x$.

</Step>

<Step title="The moment of inertia about the line through the centre of mass">

The distance from the point $(x, y)$ to the vertical line $x = \bar x$ is $|x - \bar x|$. So

<Formula label="Moment of inertia about the line through the centre of mass" tex="\bar I = \int\int_R (x - \bar x)^2\,\delta\,dA = \int\int_R \left(x^2 - 2\bar x\,x + \bar x^2\right)\delta\,dA" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Split into three integrals">

The number $\bar x$ does not depend on the point $(x, y)$, so it comes out of the integrals:

<Formula label="The three pieces" tex="\bar I = \int\int_R x^2\,\delta\,dA - 2\bar x\int\int_R x\,\delta\,dA + \bar x^2\int\int_R \delta\,dA = I - 2\bar x \cdot M\bar x + \bar x^2 \cdot M = I - M\bar x^2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

Move $M\bar x^2$ to the other side: $I = \bar I + M\bar x^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

Our own example: a plate with density $\delta = 1$ on the rectangle $0 \le x \le 3$, $0 \le y \le 2$. Its mass is the area, $M = 6$, and by symmetry $\bar x = 1.5$. About the $y$-axis, $I = \int_0^2\int_0^3 x^2\,dx\,dy = 2 \cdot 9 = 18$. About the line $x = 1.5$, $\bar I = \int_0^2\int_0^3 (x - 1.5)^2\,dx\,dy = 2 \cdot 2.25 = 4.5$. And $M\bar x^2 = 6 \cdot 2.25 = 13.5$. Indeed $4.5 + 13.5 = 18$.

</Step>

<Step title="What it says">

The term $M\bar x^2$ is never negative. So of the $y$-axis and the parallel line through the centre of mass, the line through the centre of mass has the smaller moment of inertia, or the same one if the $y$-axis itself passes through the centre of mass. The origin can be placed anywhere, so this holds for every pair of parallel lines of which one passes through the centre of mass: with $d$ the distance between the two lines, $I_L = \bar I + Md^2$.

The same steps with $y$ in place of $x$ give $I_x = \bar I_x + M\bar y^2$ for horizontal lines. The moment of inertia about the origin is $I_0 = \int\int_R (x^2 + y^2)\,\delta\,dA = I_x + I_y$, so adding the two results gives the theorem for turning about a point: $I_0$ equals the moment of inertia about the centre of mass plus $M$ times the squared distance from the origin to the centre of mass. The lesson's disc of radius $a$ with density $1$ shows it: about its centre $\pi a^4/2$, mass $\pi a^2$, and about a point on its rim, at distance $a$ from the centre, $\pi a^4/2 + \pi a^2 \cdot a^2 = 3\pi a^4/2$, the value the lesson found by a longer integral. (8.01 uses this theorem for solid bodies, in the lesson on [the moment of inertia](/courses/8-01/moment-of-inertia).)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="The average area of a triangle inscribed in the unit circle" points={5} mit="Part B, Problem 4">

Pick three points on the unit circle and join them to a triangle. Each of the three points is equally likely to be anywhere on the circle, and where one point lands has no influence on where the others land. Find the average area of such a triangle.

MIT recalls from its Problem Set 4 that the largest triangle inscribed in the unit circle is the equilateral one, with sides of length $\sqrt 3$ and area $3\sqrt 3/4$. How does this maximum compare with the average?

MIT's own hint: to turn the problem into a double integral, put one of the three vertices at the point $(1, 0)$, and use the polar angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ of the other two vertices as the variables. What is the region of integration?

<Part label="a" points={2}>

One vertex is at $(1, 0)$, and the other two are at the polar angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ on the unit circle, with $0 < \theta_1 < \theta_2 < 2\pi$. Find the area of the triangle as a formula in $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, without an absolute value.

In the box, type `t1` for $\theta_1$ and `t2` for $\theta_2$.

<Hint>

The vertices are $A = (1, 0)$, $B = (\cos\theta_1, \sin\theta_1)$ and $C = (\cos\theta_2, \sin\theta_2)$. The lesson on determinants gives the area of the parallelogram with sides $\vec{AB}$ and $\vec{AC}$. The triangle is half of it.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The determinant is plus or minus the area. Which sign belongs to $\theta_1 < \theta_2$? Try a simple triangle, for example $\theta_1 = \pi/2$ and $\theta_2 = \pi$, whose area you can see without a formula.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="Area $=$" expr="(sin(t1) + sin(t2 - t1) - sin(t2))/2"
  vars={{ t1: [0.3, 1.2], t2: [3.3, 4.5] }}
  names={{ t1: "\\theta_1", t2: "\\theta_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sin(t1) + sin(t2 - t1) - sin(t2)", hint: "This is the area of the parallelogram with sides AB and AC. The triangle is half of it." },
    { expr: "(sin(t2) - sin(t1) - sin(t2 - t1))/2", hint: "This is negative when θ₁ < θ₂, and an area is positive. Exchange the two rows of your determinant, or change all the signs." },
    { expr: "sin(t2 - t1)/2", hint: "This is the area of the triangle with one vertex at the centre of the circle and two on the circle. Here the third vertex is the point (1, 0), so the two sides start from (1, 0)." },
    { expr: "(sin(t1) + sin(t2 - t1) + sin(t2))/2", hint: "Check the sign of the last term with θ₁ = π/2 and θ₂ = 3π/2: the vertices are (1, 0), (0, 1) and (0, −1), a triangle with base 2 and height 1, so its area is 1. Your formula gives 0." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Half the determinant of two sides gives the area of a triangle, up to a sign. Here the sign can be settled once and for all.

<Step title="The vertices and two sides">

Call the vertices $A = (1, 0)$, $B = (\cos\theta_1, \sin\theta_1)$ and $C = (\cos\theta_2, \sin\theta_2)$. (These names are ours.) Two sides are

<Formula label="Two sides of the triangle" tex="\vec{AB} = \langle \cos\theta_1 - 1,\ \sin\theta_1\rangle, \qquad \vec{AC} = \langle \cos\theta_2 - 1,\ \sin\theta_2\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The determinant">

By the lesson on determinants, the determinant of two vectors in the plane is plus or minus the area of the parallelogram that has them as sides:

<Formula label="Determinant and area" tex="\begin{vmatrix} a_1 & a_2 \\ b_1 & b_2 \end{vmatrix} = a_1b_2 - a_2b_1 = \pm\,\text{area of the parallelogram}" cite="reading3" at="Lecture 2, p. 2" />

Here, with the addition formula $\sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1) = \sin\theta_2\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2\sin\theta_1$:

<Formula label="The determinant of the two sides" tex="\begin{vmatrix} \cos\theta_1 - 1 & \sin\theta_1 \\ \cos\theta_2 - 1 & \sin\theta_2 \end{vmatrix} = (\cos\theta_1 - 1)\sin\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1(\cos\theta_2 - 1) = \sin\theta_1 + \sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1) - \sin\theta_2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The sign">

The lesson says that the determinant is positive when the second vector lies counterclockwise from the first. For $0 < \theta_1 < \theta_2 < 2\pi$ the points $A$, $B$, $C$ follow each other counterclockwise round the circle, so the sign should be plus. A formula confirms it. With the product formulas for sines (a standard identity of trigonometry, not part of the lesson; this site's calculation file checks it with numbers):

<Formula label="The same determinant as a product" tex="\sin\theta_1 + \sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1) - \sin\theta_2 = 4\,\sin\frac{\theta_1}{2}\,\sin\frac{\theta_2 - \theta_1}{2}\,\sin\frac{\theta_2}{2}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

For $0 < \theta_1 < \theta_2 < 2\pi$ each of the three half angles lies between $0$ and $\pi$, where the sine is positive. So the determinant is positive, and the area is half of it:

<Formula label="Area of the triangle" tex="\text{Area} = \tfrac12\left(\sin\theta_1 + \sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1) - \sin\theta_2\right) \qquad (0 < \theta_1 < \theta_2 < 2\pi)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $\theta_1 = \pi/2$ and $\theta_2 = \pi$: the vertices are $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(-1, 0)$, a triangle with base $2$ and height $1$, so its area is $1$. The formula gives $\tfrac12(1 + 1 - 0) = 1$.

Take the equilateral triangle, $\theta_1 = 2\pi/3$ and $\theta_2 = 4\pi/3$: the formula gives $\tfrac12\left(\tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2} + \tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2} + \tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}\right) = \tfrac{3\sqrt 3}{4} = 1.299$, the maximum that MIT quotes.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find the average area. Then divide the maximum area, $3\sqrt 3/4$, by the average.

Give both numbers as decimals: the average to three decimal places, the quotient to two.

<Hint>

Why may one vertex be put at $(1, 0)$? Turning a triangle round the centre of the circle does not change its area. So turn every triangle until its first vertex is at $(1, 0)$. The other two vertices are then at angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, each equally likely to be anywhere from $0$ to $2\pi$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The pair $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ is a point of a square in the $\theta_1\theta_2$-plane. The average area is the double integral of the area over that square, divided by the area of the square.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The formula of part (a) holds only on the half of the square where $\theta_1 < \theta_2$. On the other half the two vertices have exchanged their names, which does not change the triangle. So both halves give the same integral.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the half with $\theta_1 < \theta_2$, integrate over $\theta_1$ first, from $0$ to $\theta_2$. The outer integral has a term $\theta_2\sin\theta_2$, which needs integration by parts.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b-average" label="Average area:" value="0.477" tolerance="0.002" calc="p4-b-average" />

<Answer id="p4-b-ratio" label="Maximum area divided by average area:" value="2.72" tolerance="0.01" calc="p4-b-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two free angles fill a square, each point of it equally likely. The average area is the double integral of the area over the square, divided by the area of the square. By symmetry, half the square is enough.

<Step title="The region of integration">

Turning a triangle round the centre of the circle changes neither its area nor the chances, because every position on the circle is equally likely. So every triangle may be turned until its first vertex is at $(1, 0)$. The other two vertices are then at polar angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, each equally likely to be anywhere between $0$ and $2\pi$, and independent of each other. That makes every point of the square

<Formula label="The region of integration" tex="S:\quad 0 \le \theta_1 \le 2\pi, \qquad 0 \le \theta_2 \le 2\pi" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

equally likely. This square, in the $\theta_1\theta_2$-plane, is the region of integration that MIT's hint asks about. Its area is $(2\pi)^2 = 4\pi^2$. The average area of the triangle is the lesson's average value over $S$:

<Formula label="The average as a double integral" tex="\overline{\text{Area}} = \frac{1}{4\pi^2}\int\int_S \text{Area}(\theta_1, \theta_2)\,d\theta_1\,d\theta_2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Half the square is enough">

The diagonal $\theta_1 = \theta_2$ cuts the square into two triangles. Call $T$ the one where $\theta_1 \le \theta_2$. On $T$ the area is the formula of part (a). A point $(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ of the other half is the mirror image of the point $(\theta_2, \theta_1)$ of $T$, and both describe the same triangle on the circle, with the names of two vertices exchanged. So the two halves contribute equally:

<Formula label="Twice the integral over one half" tex="\int\int_S \text{Area}\;d\theta_1\,d\theta_2 = 2\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\theta_2} \tfrac12\left(\sin\theta_1 + \sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1) - \sin\theta_2\right)d\theta_1\,d\theta_2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

The limits follow the lesson's method: for a fixed $\theta_2$, the slice of $T$ runs from $\theta_1 = 0$ to the diagonal $\theta_1 = \theta_2$. The first slice is $\theta_2 = 0$ and the last is $\theta_2 = 2\pi$.

</Step>

<Step title="The inner integral">

The factors $2$ and $\tfrac12$ cancel. With $\theta_2$ fixed, the first term gives $\left[-\cos\theta_1\right]_0^{\theta_2} = 1 - \cos\theta_2$. The second gives $\left[\cos(\theta_2 - \theta_1)\right]_0^{\theta_2} = 1 - \cos\theta_2$ as well. The third is a constant, integrated over a stretch of length $\theta_2$:

<Formula label="The inner integral" tex="\int_0^{\theta_2} \left(\sin\theta_1 + \sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1) - \sin\theta_2\right)d\theta_1 = 2 - 2\cos\theta_2 - \theta_2\sin\theta_2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The outer integral">

Over a whole turn, the integral of $2$ is $4\pi$ and the integral of $\cos\theta_2$ is $0$. For the last term, integration by parts gives the antiderivative $-\theta_2\cos\theta_2 + \sin\theta_2$ of $\theta_2\sin\theta_2$, which is $-2\pi$ at $\theta_2 = 2\pi$ and $0$ at $\theta_2 = 0$:

<Formula label="The outer integral" tex="\int_0^{2\pi} \left(2 - 2\cos\theta_2 - \theta_2\sin\theta_2\right)d\theta_2 = 4\pi - 0 - (-2\pi) = 6\pi" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The average, and the comparison">

<Formula label="The average area" tex="\overline{\text{Area}} = \frac{6\pi}{4\pi^2} = \frac{3}{2\pi} = 0.477" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

The maximum is $3\sqrt 3/4 = 1.299$. So

<Formula label="Maximum against average" tex="\frac{\text{maximum}}{\text{average}} = \frac{3\sqrt 3}{4} \cdot \frac{2\pi}{3} = \frac{\pi\sqrt 3}{2} = 2.72" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

The largest triangle is about $2.7$ times as large as the average one. Put the other way round, the average triangle has about 37% of the largest possible area.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The average, $0.477$, lies between $0$ and the maximum $1.299$, as an average must. It is far below the maximum because many triangles are thin: whenever two of the three points land close together, the area is small.

A second route (this site's calculation file follows it): by the product formula of part (a), the area on the whole square is $2\left|\sin\tfrac{\theta_1}{2}\,\sin\tfrac{\theta_2 - \theta_1}{2}\,\sin\tfrac{\theta_2}{2}\right|$. A computer that integrates this over the square and divides by $4\pi^2$ finds $0.477$ again.

For comparison, the unit disc itself has area $\pi = 3.142$: the average inscribed triangle covers $3/(2\pi^2)$ of it, about 15%.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

<Exercise id="p5" title="A moment of inertia in the coordinates u = xy, v = y/x" points={5} mit="Part B, Problem 5">

<Solution>

**The idea.** The lesson's three steps for a change of variables: the area element from the Jacobian, the function in the new variables, and the limits from the picture of the region. Here two sides of $R$ become straight lines through the origin of the $uv$-plane, and one side shrinks to a point.

<Step title="The integral in x and y">

With $\delta = 1$ the lesson's moment of inertia about the origin is

<Formula label="The polar moment of inertia of the region" tex="I_0 = \int\int_R (x^2 + y^2)\,dx\,dy, \qquad R:\quad 1 \le x \le 2, \quad 0 \le y \le \frac{1}{x}" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 17, p. 2 (the formula for I₀; the region is that of Exercise 3D-7)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The area element">

The lesson defines the Jacobian and its use:

<Formula label="Jacobian" tex="J = \frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = \begin{vmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y \end{vmatrix}, \qquad du\,dv = |J|\,dx\,dy" cite="reading2" at="Lecture 18, p. 1" />

Here $u_x = y$, $u_y = x$, $v_x = -y/x^2$ and $v_y = 1/x$:

<Formula label="The Jacobian of this change of coordinates" tex="\frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = \begin{vmatrix} y & x \\ -\dfrac{y}{x^2} & \dfrac{1}{x} \end{vmatrix} = \frac{y}{x} + \frac{y}{x} = \frac{2y}{x} = 2v" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

Inside $R$ both $x$ and $y$ are positive, so $v > 0$ and $|J| = 2v$:

<Formula label="The area element" tex="du\,dv = 2v\,dx\,dy, \qquad dx\,dy = \frac{1}{2v}\,du\,dv" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

(This is the relation between the two Jacobians that Notes CV states as its formula (22): the Jacobian of $x, y$ with respect to $u, v$ is one over the Jacobian of $u, v$ with respect to $x, y$.)

</Step>

<Step title="The function in u and v">

Multiplying and dividing the two new coordinates gives $uv = y^2$ and $u/v = x^2$. So

<Formula label="The function to integrate" tex="x^2 + y^2 = \frac{u}{v} + uv, \qquad g(u, v) = \left(\frac{u}{v} + uv\right)\frac{1}{2v} = \frac{u}{2v^2} + \frac{u}{2}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The sides of the region in u and v">

- The hyperbola $xy = 1$ is $u = 1$.
- On the line $x = 1$: $u = y$ and $v = y$, so it is the line $v = u$.
- On the line $x = 2$: $u = 2y$ and $v = y/2$, so it is the line $v = u/4$.
- On the $x$-axis: $y = 0$, so $u = 0$ and $v = 0$. The whole bottom side of $R$ goes to the single point $(0, 0)$ of the $uv$-plane.

So in the $uv$-plane the region is a triangle with corners $(0, 0)$, $(1, 1)$ and $(1, \tfrac14)$. The last two are the images of the corners $(1, 1)$ and $(2, \tfrac12)$ of $R$. The graph shows it.

  curves={[

</Step>

<Step title="The order of integration and the limits">

Fix a value of $u$ between $0$ and $1$. In the $uv$-plane the slice is vertical: it enters the triangle at the lower line, $v = u/4$, and leaves it at the upper line, $v = u$. (In the $xy$-plane this slice is a piece of the hyperbola $xy = u$, from the line $x = 2$ to the line $x = 1$.) The first slice is $u = 0$ and the last is $u = 1$. One iterated integral covers the region:

<Formula label="The iterated integral" tex="I_0 = \int_0^1\int_{u/4}^{u} \left(\frac{u}{2v^2} + \frac{u}{2}\right)dv\,du" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

In the other order, a slice at a fixed $v$ is horizontal. It always starts on the line $v = u$, at $u = v$. For $v < \tfrac14$ it ends on the line $v = u/4$, at $u = 4v$, and for $v > \tfrac14$ it ends on the line $u = 1$. That takes two iterated integrals with different limits. So integrating over $v$ first is the simpler order.

</Step>

<Step title="Check by working it out (our addition)">

The inner integral:

<Formula label="The inner integral" tex="\int_{u/4}^{u} \left(\frac{u}{2v^2} + \frac{u}{2}\right)dv = \left[-\frac{u}{2v} + \frac{uv}{2}\right]_{v=u/4}^{v=u} = \left(-\frac12 + \frac{u^2}{2}\right) - \left(-2 + \frac{u^2}{8}\right) = \frac32 + \frac{3u^2}{8}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

The outer integral:

<Formula label="The value in the new coordinates" tex="I_0 = \int_0^1 \left(\frac32 + \frac{3u^2}{8}\right)du = \frac32 + \frac18 = \frac{13}{8} = 1.625" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

The same moment of inertia straight from $x$ and $y$, without any change of coordinates:

<Formula label="The value in the old coordinates" tex="I_0 = \int_1^2\int_0^{1/x} (x^2 + y^2)\,dy\,dx = \int_1^2 \left(x + \frac{1}{3x^3}\right)dx = \left[\frac{x^2}{2} - \frac{1}{6x^2}\right]_1^2 = \frac{47}{24} - \frac{8}{24} = \frac{13}{8}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

The two agree.

</Step>

<Step title="Two remarks">

Inside $R$, where $x > 0$ and $y > 0$, the change of coordinates can be undone: $x = \sqrt{u/v}$ and $y = \sqrt{uv}$. So different points inside $R$ go to different points of the triangle. On the $x$-axis this fails: there the Jacobian $2v$ is $0$, and the whole side goes to one point. That side is only an edge of $R$, with no area, so the integral is not affected.

The function $g$ grows without limit near the corner $(0, 0)$ of the triangle, because of the $v^2$ below the line. The integral is finite all the same: the inner integral came out as $\tfrac32 + \tfrac{3u^2}{8}$, which stays finite as $u$ goes to $0$.

</Step>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="The area of a tilted ellipse" points={4} mit="Part B, Problem 6">

Find the area of the ellipse

<Formula label="The ellipse" tex="(2x + 5y - 3)^2 + (3x - 7y + 8)^2 < 1" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 6, p. 2" />

The first box asks for the absolute value of the Jacobian of your change of variables, with the new variables on top: $|\partial(u, v)/\partial(x, y)|$. Give the area as a decimal, to four decimal places.

<Hint>

The lesson found the area of the ellipse $(x/a)^2 + (y/b)^2 < 1$ by choosing new variables that turn it into the unit disc. Which new variables $u$ and $v$ do that here?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The area is the double integral of $1$ over the ellipse, in $x$ and $y$. Change it to an integral in $u$ and $v$: the Jacobian gives the relation between $dx\,dy$ and $du\,dv$. For a linear change of variables the Jacobian is a constant.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-jacobian" label="$\left|\dfrac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)}\right| =$" value="29" tolerance="0.01" calc="p6-jacobian" />

<Answer id="p6-area" label="Area of the ellipse:" value="0.1083" tolerance="0.0003" calc="p6-area" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Take the two brackets as new variables. The ellipse becomes the unit disc, whose area is known, and the Jacobian says how much the change of variables stretches areas.

<Step title="New variables">

<Formula label="The change of variables" tex="u = 2x + 5y - 3, \qquad v = 3x - 7y + 8" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

In these variables the ellipse is $u^2 + v^2 < 1$: the unit disc of the $uv$-plane, with area $\pi$.

</Step>

<Step title="The Jacobian">

The partial derivatives are the numbers in front of $x$ and $y$. The constants $-3$ and $8$ drop out: they only shift the region, without changing its size.

<Formula label="The Jacobian of the change of variables" tex="\frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 3 & -7 \end{vmatrix} = 2 \cdot (-7) - 5 \cdot 3 = -29, \qquad du\,dv = 29\,dx\,dy" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

The area element uses the absolute value, $29$.

</Step>

<Step title="The area">

<Formula label="The area of the ellipse" tex="\text{Area} = \int\int_{\text{ellipse}} dx\,dy = \int\int_{u^2 + v^2 < 1} \frac{1}{29}\,du\,dv = \frac{\pi}{29} = 0.1083" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ellipse is small, because the change of variables enlarges areas $29$ times on the way to the unit disc.

The lesson's simple ellipse fits the same pattern: for $u = x/a$ and $v = y/b$ the Jacobian is $1/(ab)$, and the area is $\pi$ divided by it, $\pi ab$.

A check from the other side: solve the two equations for $x$ and $y$. With $x_0 = -19/29$ and $y_0 = 25/29$ (the centre of the ellipse, where $u = v = 0$),

<Formula label="The change of variables, solved for x and y" tex="x = x_0 + \frac{7u + 5v}{29}, \qquad y = y_0 + \frac{3u - 2v}{29}, \qquad \frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)} = \frac{7 \cdot (-2) - 5 \cdot 3}{29^2} = -\frac{29}{841} = -\frac{1}{29}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

So $dx\,dy = \tfrac{1}{29}\,du\,dv$ again. The centre is right: $2x_0 + 5y_0 = (-38 + 125)/29 = 3$ and $3x_0 - 7y_0 = (-57 - 175)/29 = -8$, which makes both brackets zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>
