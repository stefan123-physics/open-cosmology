Three of them are written out below, with hints and solutions of our own (12 points). MIT labels each problem with the day from which it can be done. Problem 1 can be done at once: it uses the two lessons on flux and on regions with a hole, from before MIT's third exam. Problems 3 and 4 belong to the lecture on spherical coordinates, and use the triple integrals of the lecture before it. MIT's sheet counts its exam days as lectures, so its numbers are higher than this site's: MIT's Lectures 28 and 29 are Lectures 25 and 26 here.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Flux through circles that do and do not enclose the origin" points={4} mit="Part B, Problem 1">

A vector field in the plane points straight away from the origin, and gets weaker with distance:

<Formula label="The field" tex="\vec F = \frac{x}{r^2}\,\hat i + \frac{y}{r^2}\,\hat j, \qquad r^2 = x^2 + y^2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1, p. 1" />

It is not defined at the origin. Take a circle $C$ with centre $(1, 0)$ and radius $a$, where $a$ is not $1$. Find the flux of $\vec F$ outwards through $C$. The answer depends on whether $a$ is smaller or larger than $1$: do the two cases separately, and explain each answer with a diagram.

MIT also suggests, as an optional extra, an applet on its course web page that shows what happens as $a$ grows from below $1$ to above $1$. The graph with a slider at the end of the solution of part (b) takes its place here.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

The case $a < 1$. Draw the circle and the origin, and find the flux.

<Hint>

Where is the origin: inside the circle or outside it? What does that mean for the field on the disc that the circle encloses?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The normal form of Green's theorem turns the flux out of a closed curve into the double integral of the divergence over the region inside, if the field is defined and differentiable on that whole region. Work out $\operatorname{div}\vec F = P_x + Q_y$ with the quotient rule.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-flux" label="Flux when $a < 1$:" value="0" tolerance="0.001" calc="p1-a-flux" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For $a < 1$ the origin lies outside the circle, so the field is defined on the whole disc inside it. Green's theorem in normal form applies, and the divergence turns out to be zero.

<Step title="The diagram">

The centre $(1, 0)$ is at distance $1$ from the origin. A circle of radius $a < 1$ around it does not reach the origin: the nearest point of the circle is $(1 - a, 0)$, to the right of the origin.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-0.5, 2]} range={[-1, 1]} xLabel="x" yLabel="y"
  curves={[
    { x: "1 + 0.6*cos(s)", y: "0.6*sin(s)", range: [0, 6.2832] },
    { expr: "x >= 0 ? 0.5*x : NaN", dash: true },
    { expr: "x >= 0 ? -0.5*x : NaN", dash: true }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 0, y: 0, label: "origin", offCurve: true }]}
  caption="The circle with centre (1, 0) and radius a = 0.6, drawn from x = 1 + 0.6 cos s, y = 0.6 sin s, with the origin outside it. The dashed lines y = 0.5x and y = −0.5x are two of the rays from the origin along which the field points. Each of them crosses the circle twice. The two axes need not have the same scale, so the circle may look flattened." />

The field points along rays from the origin. A ray that meets the circle goes in on the near side and comes out on the far side. So part of the circle has flow going in (negative flux) and part has flow going out (positive flux). The calculation shows that the two cancel exactly.

</Step>

<Step title="The divergence">

Write $\vec F = \langle P, Q\rangle$ with $P = x/(x^2 + y^2)$ and $Q = y/(x^2 + y^2)$. By the quotient rule,

<Formula label="The two partial derivatives" tex="P_x = \frac{(x^2 + y^2) - x \cdot 2x}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{r^4}, \qquad Q_y = \frac{(x^2 + y^2) - y \cdot 2y}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{x^2 - y^2}{r^4}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="The divergence of the field" tex="\operatorname{div}\vec F = P_x + Q_y = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{r^4} + \frac{x^2 - y^2}{r^4} = 0 \qquad \text{at every point except the origin}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Green's theorem">

The lesson's theorem for flux is

<Formula label="Green's theorem, normal form" tex="\oint_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \int\int_R \operatorname{div}\vec F\,dA" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 23, p. 4" />

where $R$ is the region inside $C$ and $\hat n$ points out of it. It needs $\vec F$ to be defined and differentiable on all of $R$. For $a < 1$ that is so, because the origin is not in the disc. Then

<Formula label="The flux for a circle that does not enclose the origin" tex="\oint_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \int\int_R 0\,dA = 0 \qquad (a < 1)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A direct calculation agrees. On the circle, $x = 1 + a\cos t$ and $y = a\sin t$, the outward unit normal is $\hat n = \langle \cos t, \sin t\rangle$, and $ds = a\,dt$. Then $r^2 = 1 + 2a\cos t + a^2$ and $x\cos t + y\sin t = \cos t + a$, so

<Formula label="The flux as an ordinary integral" tex="\oint_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{a\,(a + \cos t)}{1 + 2a\cos t + a^2}\,dt" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own check)" />

A computer gives $0$ for this integral at $a = 0.5$ (to the accuracy of the calculation). The integrand is positive where $\cos t > -a$, on the far side of the circle, and negative where $\cos t < -a$, on the side that faces the origin.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

The case $a > 1$. Draw the circle and the origin again, and find the flux. Give it as a decimal, to three decimal places.

The first box is a check on the way: it asks for the value of $\vec F \cdot \hat n$ on a circle of radius $\varepsilon$ with its centre at the origin, where $\hat n$ points away from the origin.

<Hint>

Now the origin is inside the circle, and the field is not defined there. Green's theorem cannot be used on the whole disc. The lesson on simply connected regions meets the same trouble for work. What did it do?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Cut a small disc around the origin out of the big one. On the region that is left the field is defined and its divergence is zero. This region has two boundary curves. What does Green's theorem say about the flux through the two?

</Hint>

<Hint>

On a small circle centred at the origin, the field is perpendicular to the circle and has the same size at every point. The flux through it is that size times the length of the circle.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b-fn" label="$\vec F \cdot \hat n$ on the circle of radius $\varepsilon$ around the origin:" expr="1/eps"
  vars={{ eps: [0.1, 0.9] }}
  names={{ eps: "\\varepsilon" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/eps^2", hint: "The length of the vector (x, y) is r, so the field has size r/r² = 1/r, not 1/r²." },
    { expr: "eps", hint: "That is the size of the vector (x, y) on the small circle. The field divides it by r², which is ε² there." },
    { expr: "2*pi", hint: "That is a flux, the integral over a whole circle. This box asks for the integrand F · n at one point of the small circle." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-b-flux" label="Flux when $a > 1$:" value="6.283" tolerance="0.002" calc="p1-b-flux" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For $a > 1$ the origin is inside the circle, and Green's theorem cannot be used on the disc. Cut out a small disc around the origin. Between the two circles the divergence is zero, so the same flux passes through both. On the small circle the flux is easy to find.

<Step title="The diagram">

The point of the circle nearest to the origin is $(1 - a, 0)$, which is now to the left of the origin: the origin is inside. Choose a radius $\varepsilon$ smaller than $a - 1$. Then the circle $C_\varepsilon$ of radius $\varepsilon$ around the origin lies inside $C$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-1, 3]} range={[-2, 2]} xLabel="x" yLabel="y"
  curves={[
    { x: "1 + 1.6*cos(s)", y: "1.6*sin(s)", range: [0, 6.2832] },
    { x: "0.3*cos(s)", y: "0.3*sin(s)", range: [0, 6.2832], dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="The circle C with centre (1, 0) and radius a = 1.6 (solid), drawn from x = 1 + 1.6 cos s, y = 1.6 sin s, and a small circle of radius 0.3 around the origin (dashed), which lies inside C. The two axes need not have the same scale, so the circles may look flattened." />

Every ray from the origin now crosses $C$ once, going out. Nothing goes in, so the flux must be positive.

</Step>

<Step title="Green's theorem on the region with a hole">

Let $R$ be the region inside $C$ and outside $C_\varepsilon$. The field is defined and differentiable on all of $R$, and $\operatorname{div}\vec F = 0$ there (part (a)). The lesson on simply connected regions uses Green's theorem on such a region for work: the outer curve counts counterclockwise and the inner curve clockwise. The normal form is the same theorem applied to the field $\langle -Q, P\rangle$ (that is how the lesson on flux proves it), so it holds for $R$ too. The normal vector must point out of $R$ on both curves. On $C_\varepsilon$ that is towards the origin, the opposite of the outward normal of the small circle. So the flux out of $R$ is the outward flux through $C$ minus the outward flux through $C_\varepsilon$:

<Formula label="The same flux through both circles" tex="\oint_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds - \oint_{C_\varepsilon} \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \int\int_R \operatorname{div}\vec F\,dA = 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

Here $\hat n$ points away from the origin on $C_\varepsilon$.

</Step>

<Step title="The flux through the small circle">

On $C_\varepsilon$ we have $r = \varepsilon$, and the unit normal that points away from the origin is $\hat n = \langle x, y\rangle / \varepsilon$. Then

<Formula label="The field on the small circle" tex="\vec F \cdot \hat n = \frac{\langle x, y\rangle}{\varepsilon^2} \cdot \frac{\langle x, y\rangle}{\varepsilon} = \frac{x^2 + y^2}{\varepsilon^3} = \frac{1}{\varepsilon}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

This is the same at every point of $C_\varepsilon$, so the flux is this value times the length $2\pi\varepsilon$ of the circle:

<Formula label="The flux for a circle that encloses the origin" tex="\oint_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \oint_{C_\varepsilon} \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \frac{1}{\varepsilon} \cdot 2\pi\varepsilon = 2\pi = 6.283 \qquad (a > 1)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

The radius $\varepsilon$ drops out, as it must: the answer cannot depend on a circle we chose ourselves. It does not depend on $a$ either.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The integral of part (a)'s check holds for every $a$ that is not $1$. At $a = 2$ a computer gives $6.283$ for it.

The graph (our addition, in place of MIT's applet) shows the integrand of that integral as a function of the angle $t$ on the circle. Move the slider through $a = 1$.

<Plot variable="t" domain={[0, 6.2832]} range={[-6, 6]} xLabel="t" yLabel="integrand"
  curves={[
    { expr: "a*(a + cos(t))/(1 + 2*a*cos(t) + a^2)" }
  ]}
  params={{ a: { value: 0.6, min: 0.4, max: 2.5, step: 0.05, label: "a" } }}
  caption="The integrand a (a + cos t) / (1 + 2a cos t + a²) of the flux integral, computed from this formula, against the angle t on the circle, for the radius a set by the slider. For a < 1 the curve dips below zero around t = π, the side of the circle that faces the origin. For a > 1 it stays above zero. When a is close to 1 the curve leaves the frame near t = π." />

For $a < 1$ there is a stretch of negative flux around $t = \pi$, and it cancels the positive rest. As $a$ comes close to $1$ the circle passes close to the origin, where the field is very strong: the dip becomes deep and narrow. Once $a$ is past $1$ the origin is inside, the dip has turned into a peak, and the total is $2\pi$.

At $a = 1$ itself the circle passes through the origin, where $\vec F$ is not defined, so MIT leaves this case out. (At every other point of that circle the integrand is $\tfrac{1 + \cos t}{2 + 2\cos t} = \tfrac12$.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The average distance from a ball to a point on its surface" points={4} mit="Part B, Problem 3">

The average value of a function $f(x, y, z)$ over a region $D$ in space is its integral over $D$ divided by the volume of $D$:

<Formula label="Average value over a region in space" tex="\text{average of } f = \frac{1}{V(D)}\int\int\int_D f(x, y, z)\,dV, \qquad V(D) = \text{volume of } D" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3, p. 1" />

A solid ball (MIT says "solid sphere") has radius $a$. Fix one point on its surface. Find the average distance from the points of the ball to that fixed point. Set up the integral in two ways, in cylindrical and in spherical coordinates, and evaluate both.

MIT's advice for the placement: put the fixed point at the origin, and make it the South Pole of the ball. So the ball sits on top of the origin, with its centre on the positive $z$-axis.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$. Set up the integral of the distance over the ball and evaluate it.

The first box asks for the largest value of $r$ inside the ball at height $z$. The second asks for the value of the triple integral of the distance over the ball, before you divide by the volume.

<Hint>

Where is the centre of the ball? Write the equation of its surface in $x$, $y$, $z$, then put $r^2$ for $x^2 + y^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The distance from the point $(r, \theta, z)$ to the origin is $\sqrt{r^2 + z^2}$. With the volume element $r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$, the integral over $r$ can be done by substitution if you do it first.

</Hint>

<Hint>

After the integral over $r$, use the equation of the surface: at the largest $r$, the sum $r^2 + z^2$ is a simple expression in $z$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a-rmax" label="Largest $r$ at height $z$:" expr="sqrt(2*a*z - z^2)"
  vars={{ a: [2, 3], z: [0.5, 0.9] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(a^2 - z^2)", hint: "That is a ball with its centre at the origin. Here the origin is the South Pole, so the centre is at height a on the z-axis." },
    { expr: "2*a*z - z^2", hint: "This is r², the square of the largest r. Take the square root." },
    { expr: "sqrt(a*z - z^2)", hint: "At the height of the centre, z = a, the slice is widest: its radius is a. This formula gives 0 there." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-a-integral" label="Integral of the distance over the ball:" expr="8*pi*a^4/5"
  vars={{ a: [1, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "4*a^4/5", hint: "The integral over θ is still to be done. Nothing depends on θ, so it gives a factor 2π." },
    { expr: "6*a/5", hint: "That is the average. This box asks for the integral before it is divided by the volume." },
    { expr: "pi*a^4", hint: "That is the integral of the distance to the centre of the ball. Here the distance is measured from the South Pole." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With the South Pole at the origin, the distance to the fixed point is the distance to the origin, $\sqrt{r^2 + z^2}$. Slice the ball into horizontal discs, and integrate over $r$ first.

<Step title="The ball in cylindrical coordinates">

The centre is at $(0, 0, a)$, so the surface is $x^2 + y^2 + (z - a)^2 = a^2$. Multiplied out, with $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$:

<Formula label="The surface of the ball" tex="r^2 + z^2 = 2az, \qquad \text{so at height } z: \quad 0 \le r \le \sqrt{2az - z^2}, \qquad 0 \le z \le 2a" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

The graph shows half of a vertical slice through the axis, for $a = 1$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 1.3]} range={[0, 2.1]} xLabel="r" yLabel="z"
  curves={[
    { x: "sin(s)", y: "1 - cos(s)", range: [0, 3.1416] },
    { expr: "x <= 0.866 ? 0.5 : NaN", dash: true },
    { expr: "x <= 0.866 ? 0.57735*x : NaN" }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 0.866, y: 0.5, label: "surface", offCurve: true }]}
  caption="Half of a vertical slice of the ball of radius a = 1 that rests on the origin: the half circle r = sin s, z = 1 − cos s. The dashed line is the horizontal slice at height z = 0.5, which runs from the axis to r = 0.866. The sloping line is the ray from the origin at the angle φ = π/3 from the z-axis, which reaches the surface at the same point. The two axes need not have the same scale." />

The dashed line is what part (a) uses: at a fixed height, $r$ runs from the axis to the surface. The ray is what part (b) uses.

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

The lesson's volume element in cylindrical coordinates is $dV = r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz$. With the distance $\sqrt{r^2 + z^2}$ as the function:

<Formula label="The integral in cylindrical coordinates" tex="\int\int\int_D \sqrt{r^2 + z^2}\,dV = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{2a}\int_0^{\sqrt{2az - z^2}} \sqrt{r^2 + z^2}\;r\,dr\,dz\,d\theta" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Inner integral, over r">

Substitute $u = r^2 + z^2$, $du = 2r\,dr$. At $r = 0$, $u = z^2$. At the surface, $u = 2az$.

<Formula label="Inner integral" tex="\int_0^{\sqrt{2az - z^2}} \sqrt{r^2 + z^2}\;r\,dr = \left[\tfrac13 (r^2 + z^2)^{3/2}\right]_0^{\sqrt{2az - z^2}} = \tfrac13\left((2az)^{3/2} - z^3\right)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Middle integral, over z">

<Formula label="Middle integral" tex="\int_0^{2a} \tfrac13\left((2a)^{3/2} z^{3/2} - z^3\right) dz = \tfrac13\left(\tfrac25 (2a)^{3/2}(2a)^{5/2} - \tfrac14 (2a)^4\right) = \tfrac13\left(\tfrac25 - \tfrac14\right) 16a^4 = \frac{4a^4}{5}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Outer integral and the average">

Nothing depends on $\theta$, so the outer integral gives a factor $2\pi$:

<Formula label="The integral of the distance" tex="\int\int\int_D \sqrt{r^2 + z^2}\,dV = 2\pi \cdot \frac{4a^4}{5} = \frac{8\pi a^4}{5}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

The volume of the ball is $\tfrac43\pi a^3$, so

<Formula label="The average distance, from cylindrical coordinates" tex="\text{average distance} = \frac{8\pi a^4/5}{4\pi a^3/3} = \frac{8 \cdot 3}{5 \cdot 4}\,a = \frac{6a}{5}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The integral has the units of a length times a volume, $a^4$, and the average is a length. Every point of the ball is between $0$ and $2a$ from the pole, and $\tfrac65 a$ lies in between. Part (b) finds the same value by another route.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

    { expr: "3*a/4", hint: "That is the average distance to the centre of the ball. The fixed point is on the surface." },
    { expr: "12*a/5", hint: "This is more than the diameter 2a, and no point of the ball is that far from the pole. Did φ run to π? The ball lies above the xy-plane." },
    { expr: "8*pi*a^4/5", hint: "That is the integral of the distance over the ball. Divide it by the volume of the ball." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** From the origin, the distance is $\rho$, and the surface of the ball has a simple equation in spherical coordinates. The limits come from following a ray from the origin until it leaves the ball.

<Step title="The ball in spherical coordinates">

The surface is $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 2az$ (part (a)). With $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = \rho^2$ and $z = \rho\cos\varphi$ this is $\rho^2 = 2a\rho\cos\varphi$:

<Formula label="The surface of the ball in spherical coordinates" tex="\rho = 2a\cos\varphi, \qquad 0 \le \varphi \le \frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi" cite="reading3" at="Lecture 26, p. 2" />

The ball is above the $xy$-plane, so $\varphi$ stops at $\pi/2$, where the ray is tangent to the ball and its length inside is $0$. The lecture uses the same ball for Newton's theorem.

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

With the lesson's volume element $dV = \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta$ and the distance $\rho$ as the function:

<Formula label="The integral in spherical coordinates" tex="\int\int\int_D \rho\,dV = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^{2a\cos\varphi} \rho^3\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The three integrals">

<Formula label="Inner integral" tex="\int_0^{2a\cos\varphi} \rho^3\sin\varphi\,d\rho = \tfrac14 (2a\cos\varphi)^4\sin\varphi = 4a^4\cos^4\varphi\,\sin\varphi" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

For the middle integral substitute $u = \cos\varphi$, $du = -\sin\varphi\,d\varphi$:

<Formula label="Middle integral" tex="\int_0^{\pi/2} 4a^4\cos^4\varphi\,\sin\varphi\,d\varphi = 4a^4\left[-\tfrac15\cos^5\varphi\right]_0^{\pi/2} = \frac{4a^4}{5}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

The outer integral gives the factor $2\pi$ again:

<Formula label="The integral of the distance, again" tex="\int\int\int_D \rho\,dV = 2\pi \cdot \frac{4a^4}{5} = \frac{8\pi a^4}{5}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The average">

<Formula label="The average distance to a point on the surface" tex="\text{average distance} = \frac{1}{\tfrac43\pi a^3} \cdot \frac{8\pi a^4}{5} = \frac{6a}{5}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both coordinate systems give $\tfrac{8}{5}\pi a^4$ for the integral and $\tfrac65 a = 1.2\,a$ for the average. The spherical route is shorter, because the function is $\rho$ itself.

Compare with the average distance to the centre of the ball. With the centre at the origin, that integral is $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\int_0^a \rho^3\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta = \pi a^4$, and the average is $\pi a^4 / (\tfrac43\pi a^3) = \tfrac34 a = 0.75\,a$. (Exercise 5B-4a of MIT's Notes asks for it.) The points of the ball are on average farther from a point on the surface than from the centre, as they should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="The pull of a solid hemisphere on a mass at its pole" points={4} mit="Part B, Problem 4 (Notes, exercise 5C-5)">

A solid hemisphere, half of a solid ball, has radius $a$ and density $1$. A point mass of size $1$ sits at its pole: the point of the curved surface that is farthest from the flat face, on the axis of symmetry. Find the gravitational attraction of the hemisphere on the point mass. Write $G$ for the gravitational constant.

This is exercise 5C-5 of MIT's Notes. The tool is the Notes' formula for the pull of a solid on a unit mass at the origin, when the solid is symmetric about the $z$-axis. The pull is then along the $z$-axis, and for a solid $V$ of density $\delta$ its size is

<Formula label="Gravitational attraction on a unit mass at the origin" tex="F_z = G\int\int\int_V \frac{\cos\varphi}{\rho^2}\,\delta\,dV = G\int\int\int_V \delta\,\cos\varphi\,\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta" cite="notesG" at="equations (3) and (4), first page" />

This is the lesson's formula with $m = 1$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Put the point mass at the origin and the axis of the hemisphere along the positive $z$-axis. Describe the hemisphere in spherical coordinates: follow a ray from the origin at the angle $\varphi$ from the $z$-axis, and find where it leaves the solid. A drawing of a vertical slice helps.

There are two kinds of rays. The first box is for rays close to the $z$-axis, the second for rays close to the horizontal, and the third for the angle $\varphi$ at which one kind changes into the other (in radians, to three decimal places).

<Hint>

The hemisphere is the lower half of the ball of Problem 3: the part of the ball of radius $a$ resting on the origin that lies below the height of its centre. Which plane is its flat face?

</Hint>

<Hint>

A ray leaves the solid either through the flat face or through the curved surface. Write each of the two surfaces as $\rho = $ a function of $\varphi$, using $z = \rho\cos\varphi$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The two kinds of rays meet at the rim, the circle where the flat face meets the curved surface. What are $r$ and $z$ on the rim?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a-flat" label="Upper limit of $\rho$ for rays close to the $z$-axis:" expr="a/cos(phi)"
  vars={{ a: [1, 2], phi: [0.1, 0.6] }}
  names={{ phi: "\\varphi" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "a*cos(phi)", hint: "The flat face is the plane z = a, and z = ρ cos φ. Solve for ρ: a tilted ray has to travel farther to reach that height, not less far." },
    { expr: "a", hint: "ρ = a is a sphere around the origin. The flat face is a plane, at height z = a." },
    { expr: "2*a*cos(phi)", hint: "That is where the ray would leave the whole ball. Close to the axis the flat face stops it first." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-a-curved" label="Upper limit of $\rho$ for rays close to the horizontal:" expr="2*a*cos(phi)"
  vars={{ a: [1, 2], phi: [0.9, 1.4] }}
  names={{ phi: "\\varphi" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "a/cos(phi)", hint: "That is the flat face z = a. A ray this far from the axis leaves through the curved surface before it reaches that height." },
    { expr: "a", hint: "ρ = a is a sphere around the origin. The curved surface is part of a sphere whose centre is at height a on the z-axis." },
    { expr: "2*a*sin(phi)", hint: "A horizontal ray, at φ = π/2, only touches the solid at the pole, so its length inside is 0. This formula gives 2a there." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-a-angle" label="Angle $\varphi$ where the rays change:" value="0.785" tolerance="0.002" calc="p4-a-angle" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The hemisphere is the ball of Problem 3 cut off at the height of its centre. A ray from the origin leaves it through the flat top if it is steep, and through the curved surface if it is not.

<Step title="Place the solid">

With the pole at the origin and the axis along the positive $z$-axis, the solid is the part of the ball $x^2 + y^2 + (z - a)^2 \le a^2$ with $z \le a$. Its flat face is the disc of radius $a$ in the plane $z = a$, and its curved surface is the lower half of the sphere. The graph shows half of a vertical slice for $a = 1$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 1.3]} range={[0, 1.3]} xLabel="r" yLabel="z"
  curves={[
    { x: "sin(s)", y: "1 - cos(s)", range: [0, 1.5708] },
    { expr: "x <= 1 ? 1 : NaN" },
    { expr: "x <= 1 ? x : NaN", dash: true }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 1, y: 1, label: "rim", offCurve: true }]}
  caption="Half of a vertical slice of the hemisphere of radius a = 1 with its pole at the origin: the quarter circle r = sin s, z = 1 − cos s (the curved surface) and the horizontal line z = 1 from the axis to r = 1 (the flat face). The dashed line z = r is the ray at φ = π/4, which runs from the origin to the rim. The two axes need not have the same scale." />

</Step>

<Step title="The two surfaces in spherical coordinates">

The flat face is $z = a$, that is $\rho\cos\varphi = a$. The curved surface is part of the sphere of Problem 3(b):

<Formula label="The two surfaces of the hemisphere" tex="\text{flat face:} \quad \rho = \frac{a}{\cos\varphi}, \qquad\qquad \text{curved surface:} \quad \rho = 2a\cos\varphi" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Which one the ray meets first">

On the rim $r = a$ and $z = a$, so $\tan\varphi = r/z = 1$: the ray to the rim has $\varphi = \pi/4 = 0.785$. As a test, both formulas give the same $\rho$ there: $a/\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \sqrt2\,a$ and $2a\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \sqrt2\,a$.

A steeper ray, with $\varphi < \pi/4$, reaches the flat face while it is still inside the ball: it leaves at $\rho = a/\cos\varphi$. A flatter ray, with $\varphi > \pi/4$, leaves through the curved surface at $\rho = 2a\cos\varphi$, below the height $a$. So the solid is described by

<Formula label="The hemisphere in spherical coordinates" tex="0 \le \rho \le \frac{a}{\cos\varphi} \ \text{ for } 0 \le \varphi \le \frac{\pi}{4}, \qquad 0 \le \rho \le 2a\cos\varphi \ \text{ for } \frac{\pi}{4} \le \varphi \le \frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\varphi < \pi/4$ we have $\cos^2\varphi > \tfrac12$, so $a/\cos\varphi < 2a\cos\varphi$: the flat face does come first there, and for $\varphi > \pi/4$ it is the other way round. In both ranges the upper limit is the smaller of the two expressions.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Evaluate the integral for the attraction, with $\delta = 1$. Give its size as a formula in $G$ and $a$. In which direction does it point?

<Hint>

The integral splits into two, one for each range of $\varphi$ of part (a). The integrand $\cos\varphi\sin\varphi$ does not contain $\rho$, so the inner integral is the integrand times the upper limit of $\rho$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the first piece the cosines cancel. In the second, substitute $u = \cos\varphi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b-force" label="Size of the attraction:" expr="2*pi*G*a*(1 - sqrt(2)/3)"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 2], a: [1, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "4*pi*G*a/3", hint: "That is the pull of the whole ball of radius a on a mass on its surface (Newton's theorem in the lesson). Half of the ball is missing here, so the answer must be smaller." },
    { expr: "2*pi*G*a/3", hint: "That is half the pull of the whole ball. The two halves of the ball do not pull equally hard: the half that holds the mass at its pole is closer to it." },
    { expr: "pi*G*a", hint: "That is the pull on a mass at the centre of the flat face, where every ray has length a. The mass is at the pole." },
    { expr: "2*pi*G*a*(1 - sqrt(2)/2)", hint: "That is only the piece with φ between 0 and π/4. Add the rays between π/4 and π/2, which leave through the curved surface." },
    { expr: "2*pi*G*a*sqrt(2)/6", hint: "That is only the piece with φ between π/4 and π/2. Add the rays between 0 and π/4, which leave through the flat face." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the limits of part (a) into the Notes' formula. The inner integral only multiplies by the length of the ray, so two short integrals over $\varphi$ are left.

<Step title="The integral">

With $\delta = 1$:

<Formula label="The attraction as two integrals" tex="F_z = G\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/4}\int_0^{a/\cos\varphi} \cos\varphi\,\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta + G\int_0^{2\pi}\int_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2}\int_0^{2a\cos\varphi} \cos\varphi\,\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The rays through the flat face">

The inner integral gives $\tfrac{a}{\cos\varphi} \cdot \cos\varphi\sin\varphi = a\sin\varphi$. Then

<Formula label="First piece" tex="\int_0^{\pi/4} a\sin\varphi\,d\varphi = a\left[-\cos\varphi\right]_0^{\pi/4} = a\left(1 - \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The rays through the curved surface">

The inner integral gives $2a\cos\varphi \cdot \cos\varphi\sin\varphi = 2a\cos^2\varphi\,\sin\varphi$. With $u = \cos\varphi$:

<Formula label="Second piece" tex="\int_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2} 2a\cos^2\varphi\,\sin\varphi\,d\varphi = 2a\left[-\tfrac13\cos^3\varphi\right]_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2} = \frac{2a}{3}\left(\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{\sqrt2}{6}\,a" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Add up">

The integral over $\theta$ gives $2\pi$ for both pieces, and $-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2} + \tfrac{\sqrt2}{6} = -\tfrac{\sqrt2}{3}$:

<Formula label="The attraction of the hemisphere on a unit mass at its pole" tex="F_z = 2\pi G a\left(1 - \frac{\sqrt2}{2} + \frac{\sqrt2}{6}\right) = 2\pi G a\left(1 - \frac{\sqrt2}{3}\right) = 3.321\,G a" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

It is positive: the force on the point mass points along the positive $z$-axis, from the pole straight into the solid, along its axis of symmetry.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is the answer that MIT's Notes print for exercise 5C-5.

Cylindrical coordinates give it too (our own check). In the first form of the Notes' formula, $\cos\varphi/\rho^2 = z/\rho^3 = z/(r^2 + z^2)^{3/2}$, and the slice at height $z$ runs to $r = \sqrt{2az - z^2}$ as in Problem 3(a), now for $0 \le z \le a$. The inner integral is

<Formula label="Check in cylindrical coordinates: inner integral" tex="\int_0^{\sqrt{2az - z^2}} \frac{z\,r}{(r^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}\,dr = \left[\frac{-z}{\sqrt{r^2 + z^2}}\right]_0^{\sqrt{2az - z^2}} = 1 - \sqrt{\frac{z}{2a}}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own check)" />

<Formula label="Check in cylindrical coordinates: the result" tex="F_z = 2\pi G\int_0^a \left(1 - \sqrt{\frac{z}{2a}}\right) dz = 2\pi G\left(a - \frac{1}{\sqrt{2a}} \cdot \tfrac23 a^{3/2}\right) = 2\pi G a\left(1 - \frac{\sqrt2}{3}\right)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4 (our own check)" />

Compare with the whole ball. By the lesson's Newton's theorem, a ball of radius $a$ and density $1$ pulls a unit mass on its surface with $\tfrac43\pi G a = 4.189\,G a$. The near half, this hemisphere, gives $3.321\,G a$ of that, which is 79%. The far half gives the rest, $0.868\,G a$. The two halves have the same mass, but the near half is closer, so it pulls harder.

The units: with the density and the point mass written out, the result is $G \times$ density $\times$ mass $\times$ length, which is a force. Here the density and the mass are both $1$.

</Step>

</Solution>
