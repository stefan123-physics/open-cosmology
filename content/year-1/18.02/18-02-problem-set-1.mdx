This set practises the first three lessons of the course: [vectors and the dot product](/courses/18-02/dot-product), [determinants and the cross product](/courses/18-02/determinants-and-cross-product) and [matrices](/courses/18-02/matrices). It follows MIT's Problem Set 1, in our own words.

MIT's set has two parts. Part B holds MIT's five longer problems (27 points), for which OpenCourseWare has no solutions. All five are written out below, with hints and solutions of our own. Part A is a list of short exercises, most of them from MIT's own Supplementary Notes, which also hold their solutions. Those are not rewritten here: the last section of this page lists them and links to MIT's exercises and solutions.

MIT labels each problem with the day on which its lecture was given. Problems 1 and 2 belong to Lecture 1 (the dot product), except part (d) of Problem 1, which needs Lecture 2. Problem 3 belongs to Lecture 2 (the cross product), and Problems 4 and 5 to Lecture 3 (matrices). MIT's lecture numbers are the same as this site's here.

Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A tetrahedron in a cube, and the methane molecule" points={5} mit="Part B, Problem 1">

A cube with sides of length $2$ has its centre at the origin $(0, 0, 0)$ and its sides parallel to the axes. Its eight vertices (corners) are the points $(\pm 1, \pm 1, \pm 1)$, with every choice of the three signs.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Four of the eight vertices, one of which is $(1, 1, 1)$, are the vertices of a regular tetrahedron: a pyramid with four faces whose six edges all have the same length. Find the other three vertices. Confirm your answer by finding the length of an edge, and explain why all six edges have that length. Give the length as a decimal, to three decimal places.

<Hint>

Start from $(1, 1, 1)$. A neighbouring corner of the cube differs from it in one coordinate. What are the other possible distances between two corners of the cube?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The edges of the cube are too few to make a tetrahedron out of them: try the diagonals of the faces of the cube.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-vertices" label="The other three vertices:"
  choices={["(−1, 1, 1), (1, −1, 1) and (1, 1, −1)", "(1, −1, −1), (−1, 1, −1) and (−1, −1, 1)", "(−1, −1, −1), (1, −1, −1) and (−1, 1, −1)", "(−1, −1, −1), (−1, 1, 1) and (1, −1, 1)"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-a-edge" label="Length of an edge:" value="2.828" tolerance="0.002" calc="p1-a-edge" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two corners of the cube lie at one of three distances from each other: along an edge of the cube, along the diagonal of a face, or along a diagonal through the centre. Six equal edges are possible only with face diagonals.

<Step title="Choose the vertices">

Take the corners that differ from $(1, 1, 1)$ in exactly two coordinates:

<Formula label="The four vertices of the tetrahedron" tex="P_0 = (1, 1, 1), \qquad P_1 = (1, -1, -1), \qquad P_2 = (-1, 1, -1), \qquad P_3 = (-1, -1, 1)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

(The names $P_0$ to $P_3$ are ours.) These are the four corners of the cube with an even number of minus signs. A look at the list shows that any two of them differ in exactly two coordinates.

</Step>

<Step title="The length of an edge">

The vector from $P_0$ to $P_1$ is $\langle 0, -2, -2\rangle$. With the lesson's formula for the length of a vector,

<Formula label="Length of an edge of the tetrahedron" tex="|\vec{P_0P_1}| = \sqrt{0^2 + (-2)^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt 8 = 2\sqrt 2 = 2.828" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Why all six edges are equal">

Any two of the four vertices differ in exactly two coordinates, and each of those coordinates jumps from $1$ to $-1$ or back. So the vector between them has two components of size $2$ and one that is $0$, and its length is $\sqrt{4 + 4} = 2\sqrt 2$ every time. In the picture: each of the six edges is a diagonal of one of the six square faces of the cube, and all those diagonals are equally long.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A face of the cube is a square of side $2$, and the diagonal of such a square is $2\sqrt 2$ by Pythagoras. The three neighbours of $(1, 1, 1)$, which are $(-1, 1, 1)$, $(1, -1, 1)$ and $(1, 1, -1)$, do not work: each is at distance $2$ from $(1, 1, 1)$ but at distance $2\sqrt 2$ from the other two.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

In a molecule of methane, four hydrogen atoms sit at the vertices of a regular tetrahedron and a carbon atom sits at its centre. Find the bond angle: the angle between the vectors from the carbon atom to two of the hydrogen atoms. Use a calculator and give the angle in degrees, to one decimal place.

<Hint>

Where is the centre of the tetrahedron of part (a)? Then the vectors from the centre to the vertices are easy to write down.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The lesson finds an angle from a dot product: $\cos\theta$ is the dot product divided by the two lengths.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="Bond angle (1 decimal place):" value="109.5" unit="degrees" tolerance="0.05" calc="p1-b-bond-angle" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Use the tetrahedron of part (a): its size does not matter for an angle. Its centre is the origin, so the vectors from the carbon atom to the hydrogen atoms are the position vectors of the vertices.

<Step title="The centre">

The centre of the cube is the origin, and it is at the same distance, $\sqrt{1 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt 3$, from all eight corners. So it is the centre of the tetrahedron too. Take the vectors to two vertices, $\vec A = \langle 1, 1, 1\rangle$ and $\vec B = \langle 1, -1, -1\rangle$.

</Step>

<Step title="The angle">

The lesson's formula for the dot product is

<Formula label="The dot product and the angle" tex="\vec A \cdot \vec B = |\vec A|\,|\vec B|\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 1, p. 1" />

Here $\vec A \cdot \vec B = 1 - 1 - 1 = -1$ and both lengths are $\sqrt 3$:

<Formula label="The bond angle of methane" tex="\cos\theta = \frac{-1}{\sqrt 3\,\sqrt 3} = -\frac13, \qquad \theta = 109.47° \approx 109.5°" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The dot product is negative, so the angle must be more than 90°, as it is. Every other pair of vertices gives the same dot product $-1$, because two vertices always differ in two coordinates: two products are $-1$ and one is $+1$. A calculator that gives $70.5°$ was fed $+\tfrac13$: that is the angle between one bond and the line of another bond continued backwards through the carbon atom, and the two angles add up to 180°.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

Use the dot product to find the angle between two adjacent edges of the tetrahedron (two edges that share a vertex), and the angle between two opposite edges (two edges that share no vertex). Give both in degrees. Explain your answers by symmetry.

<Hint>

For the adjacent edges, take the two vectors from $(1, 1, 1)$ to two other vertices.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The edge opposite to the one between two vertices is the edge between the other two vertices.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c-adjacent" label="Angle between adjacent edges:" value="60" unit="degrees" tolerance="0.1" calc="p1-c-adjacent" />

<Answer id="p1-c-opposite" label="Angle between opposite edges:" value="90" unit="degrees" tolerance="0.1" calc="p1-c-opposite" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write each edge as a vector, the difference of two vertices, and use the dot product as in part (b).

<Step title="Adjacent edges">

Two edges from $P_0 = (1, 1, 1)$ are $\vec{P_0P_1} = \langle 0, -2, -2\rangle$ and $\vec{P_0P_2} = \langle -2, 0, -2\rangle$. Their dot product is $0 + 0 + 4 = 4$ and both have length $2\sqrt 2$:

<Formula label="Angle between adjacent edges" tex="\cos\theta = \frac{4}{2\sqrt 2 \cdot 2\sqrt 2} = \frac48 = \frac12, \qquad \theta = 60°" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Opposite edges">

The edge from $P_0$ to $P_1$ is opposite to the edge from $P_2 = (-1, 1, -1)$ to $P_3 = (-1, -1, 1)$:

<Formula label="Angle between opposite edges" tex="\vec{P_0P_1} \cdot \vec{P_2P_3} = \langle 0, -2, -2\rangle \cdot \langle 0, -2, 2\rangle = 0 + 4 - 4 = 0, \qquad \theta = 90°" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The symmetry behind 60°">

Two adjacent edges are two sides of one face. All six edges are equally long, so every face is an equilateral triangle, and the angles of an equilateral triangle are 60°.

</Step>

<Step title="The symmetry behind 90°">

Swap the $y$ and $z$ coordinates of every point: this is the reflection in the plane $y = z$. It leaves $P_0 = (1, 1, 1)$ and $P_1 = (1, -1, -1)$ where they are, and it exchanges $P_2 = (-1, 1, -1)$ and $P_3 = (-1, -1, 1)$. So the tetrahedron lands on itself, the edge $\vec{P_0P_1}$ stays as it is, and the opposite edge $\vec{P_2P_3}$ is reversed. A reflection does not change angles, so the angle $\theta$ between the two edges equals the angle between $\vec{P_0P_1}$ and the reversed edge, which is $180° - \theta$. That forces $\theta = 90°$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second way to see the right angle: the two opposite edges are diagonals of the two opposite faces $x = 1$ and $x = -1$ of the cube. Looking along the $x$-axis, they are the two different diagonals of a square, and those cross at a right angle.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={1}>

Find the area of a face of the tetrahedron, as a decimal to three decimal places. (This part needs the cross product of Lecture 2.)

<Hint>

A face is a triangle with two edges from $P_0$ as sides. Which parallelogram is it half of?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The lesson gives the area of a parallelogram in space as the length of a cross product.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="Area of a face (3 decimal places):" value="3.464" tolerance="0.002" calc="p1-d-area" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A triangle with sides $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ is half of the parallelogram with those sides, and the lesson gives the area of the parallelogram:

<Formula label="Area of a parallelogram in space" tex="\text{area} = |\vec A \times \vec B|" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 2, p. 2" />

<Step title="The cross product">

Take the face with vertices $P_0$, $P_1$, $P_2$, with sides $\vec{P_0P_1} = \langle 0, -2, -2\rangle$ and $\vec{P_0P_2} = \langle -2, 0, -2\rangle$:

<Formula label="Cross product of two edges" tex="\vec{P_0P_1} \times \vec{P_0P_2} = \big\langle (-2)(-2) - (-2)(0),\ -\big((0)(-2) - (-2)(-2)\big),\ (0)(0) - (-2)(-2)\big\rangle = \langle 4, 4, -4\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Half its length">

<Formula label="Area of a face of the tetrahedron" tex="\text{area} = \tfrac12\sqrt{4^2 + 4^2 + (-4)^2} = \tfrac12\sqrt{48} = 2\sqrt 3 = 3.464" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The face is an equilateral triangle with sides of length $2\sqrt 2$ and angles of 60°, so half base times height gives $\tfrac12 \cdot 2\sqrt 2 \cdot 2\sqrt 2\,\sin 60° = \tfrac12 \cdot 8 \cdot 0.8660 = 3.464$ as well. The cross product $\langle 4, 4, -4\rangle$ is perpendicular to both edges: its dot products with $\langle 0, -2, -2\rangle$ and $\langle -2, 0, -2\rangle$ are $-8 + 8 = 0$ both times. It points along $\langle 1, 1, -1\rangle$, away from the fourth vertex $P_3 = (-1, -1, 1)$ on the other side of the origin.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The three altitudes of a triangle meet in one point" points={3} mit="Part B, Problem 2">

A triangle in the plane has vertices $P_1$, $P_2$ and $P_3$. An **altitude** of the triangle is the whole line through a vertex that is perpendicular to the opposite side (not only the piece of it between the vertex and that side).

Let $P$ be a point of the plane, and let $\vec v_1$, $\vec v_2$ and $\vec v_3$ be the vectors from $P_1$, $P_2$ and $P_3$ to $P$.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

Write, with the dot product and the three vectors, the condition that $P$ lies on the altitude from the vertex $P_1$.

<Hint>

$P$ is on the altitude from $P_1$ exactly when the vector from $P_1$ to $P$ is perpendicular to the opposite side. Which two vertices does that side join?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the vector along that side with $\vec v_2$ and $\vec v_3$: go from $P_2$ to $P$, and then from $P$ to $P_3$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="P is on the altitude from P₁ exactly when"
  choices={["v₁ · (v₂ + v₃) = 0", "v₂ · v₃ = 0", "v₁ · (v₂ − v₃) = 0", "v₁ · v₂ = 0 and v₁ · v₃ = 0"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two vectors are perpendicular exactly when their dot product is zero. The altitude from $P_1$ is perpendicular to the side from $P_2$ to $P_3$.

<Step title="The side as a difference">

Going from $P_2$ to $P$ and then from $P$ to $P_3$ gives

<Formula label="The side opposite to the first vertex" tex="\vec{P_2P_3} = \vec{P_2P} + \vec{PP_3} = \vec v_2 - \vec v_3" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The condition">

$P$ is on the altitude from $P_1$ exactly when $\vec v_1 = \vec{P_1P}$ is perpendicular to that side (or when $P = P_1$, and then $\vec v_1$ is the zero vector and the dot product is zero too):

<Formula label="The condition for the altitude from the first vertex" tex="\vec v_1 \cdot (\vec v_2 - \vec v_3) = 0, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_2 = \vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_3" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $P = P_2$. Then $\vec v_2$ is the zero vector and the condition reads $\vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_3 = 0$: the sides from $P_2$ to $P_1$ and from $P_2$ to $P_3$ are perpendicular. That is right: $P_2$ lies on the altitude from $P_1$ only in a triangle with a right angle at $P_2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

Assume that $P$ is the point where the altitudes from $P_1$ and from $P_2$ cross. Show that

<Formula label="Three equal dot products" tex="\vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_2 = \vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_3 = \vec v_2 \cdot \vec v_3" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b), p. 2" />

<Hint>

Part (a) gives one equation for the altitude from $P_1$. Write the same kind of equation for the altitude from $P_2$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Use the condition of part (a) twice, once for each altitude.

<Step title="Two altitudes, two equations">

$P$ is on the altitude from $P_1$, so $\vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_2 = \vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_3$ by part (a). $P$ is also on the altitude from $P_2$, which is perpendicular to the side from $P_1$ to $P_3$, and $\vec{P_1P_3} = \vec v_1 - \vec v_3$:

<Formula label="The condition for the altitude from the second vertex" tex="\vec v_2 \cdot (\vec v_1 - \vec v_3) = 0, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \vec v_2 \cdot \vec v_1 = \vec v_2 \cdot \vec v_3" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Join them">

The dot product does not depend on the order, $\vec v_2 \cdot \vec v_1 = \vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_2$. So $\vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_2$ equals $\vec v_1 \cdot \vec v_3$ by the first equation and $\vec v_2 \cdot \vec v_3$ by the second: all three are equal.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

Our own example: $P_1 = (0, 0)$, $P_2 = (4, 0)$, $P_3 = (1, 3)$. The altitudes from $P_1$ and $P_2$ cross at $P = (1, 1)$, so $\vec v_1 = \langle 1, 1\rangle$, $\vec v_2 = \langle -3, 1\rangle$ and $\vec v_3 = \langle 0, -2\rangle$. The three dot products are $-3 + 1 = -2$, $0 - 2 = -2$ and $0 - 2 = -2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

Under the assumption of part (b), show that $P$ is also on the altitude from $P_3$. So the three altitudes of a triangle meet in one point. It is called the orthocentre of the triangle.

<Hint>

What is the condition of part (a) for the vertex $P_3$? Compare it with what part (b) has given you.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The condition for the third altitude is one more equality of dot products, and part (b) has already proved it.

<Step title="The condition for the third altitude">

The altitude from $P_3$ is perpendicular to the side from $P_1$ to $P_2$, and $\vec{P_1P_2} = \vec v_1 - \vec v_2$. By part (b), $\vec v_3 \cdot \vec v_1 = \vec v_3 \cdot \vec v_2$, so

<Formula label="The third altitude" tex="\vec v_3 \cdot (\vec v_1 - \vec v_2) = \vec v_3 \cdot \vec v_1 - \vec v_3 \cdot \vec v_2 = 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Conclude">

So $\vec{P_3P}$ is perpendicular to the side opposite to $P_3$ (or $P = P_3$): $P$ lies on the altitude from $P_3$. The point where two altitudes cross is on the third one as well.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

In the example of part (b), the side from $P_1 = (0, 0)$ to $P_2 = (4, 0)$ lies along the $x$-axis, so the altitude from $P_3 = (1, 3)$ is the vertical line $x = 1$. The point $P = (1, 1)$ is on it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The area vectors of a tetrahedron add up to zero" points={3} mit="Part B, Problem 3">

Take any tetrahedron, regular or not. On each of its four faces stands a vector that is perpendicular to the face, points outwards, and has a length equal to the area of the face. Show that the sum of these four vectors is the zero vector.

Do not use coordinates. This problem has no answer box: compare your proof with the solution and mark it yourself.

<Hint>

MIT's own hint: let $\vec A$, $\vec B$ and $\vec C$ be the three edges that start from one vertex. Write each of the four vectors with $\vec A$, $\vec B$ and $\vec C$, and add them.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A face with sides $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ is half a parallelogram, so $\tfrac12\,\vec A \times \vec B$ has the right length and is perpendicular to the face. It may point inwards: then use $\tfrac12\,\vec B \times \vec A$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The fourth face does not contain the chosen vertex. Two of its sides are $\vec B - \vec A$ and $\vec C - \vec A$. Multiply out their cross product.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Half the cross product of two sides of a triangle is perpendicular to the triangle and as long as its area. Written with the three edges from one vertex, the four cross products cancel in pairs.

<Step title="Set up">

Call the chosen vertex $O$, and let $\vec A$, $\vec B$, $\vec C$ be the edges from $O$ to the other three vertices. Name them in such an order that the triple product $\vec A \cdot (\vec B \times \vec C)$ is positive. (It is not zero, because a tetrahedron has a volume. If it is negative, exchange the names of $\vec B$ and $\vec C$.) The triple product keeps its value when the three vectors move round in a circle:

<Formula label="The triple product is positive" tex="\vec A \cdot (\vec B \times \vec C) = \vec B \cdot (\vec C \times \vec A) = \vec C \cdot (\vec A \times \vec B) = \det(\vec A, \vec B, \vec C) > 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

(The lesson gives $\vec A \cdot (\vec B \times \vec C) = \det(\vec A, \vec B, \vec C)$. That the three triple products are equal is not in the lesson: written out with the lesson's formulas, each of them is the same sum of six products of components.)

</Step>

<Step title="The three faces at the chosen vertex">

The face with sides $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ has area $\tfrac12|\vec A \times \vec B|$, and $\vec A \times \vec B$ is perpendicular to it. The rest of the tetrahedron lies on the side of this face where $\vec C$ points. Since $\vec C \cdot (\vec A \times \vec B) > 0$, the vector $\vec A \times \vec B$ points to that same side: inwards. The outward vector is the opposite one, $\tfrac12\,\vec B \times \vec A$. The same reasoning for the other two faces at $O$ gives

<Formula label="The three outward vectors at the chosen vertex" tex="\tfrac12\,\vec B \times \vec A, \qquad \tfrac12\,\vec C \times \vec B, \qquad \tfrac12\,\vec A \times \vec C" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The fourth face">

The fourth face has the end points of $\vec A$, $\vec B$ and $\vec C$ as vertices. Two of its sides are $\vec B - \vec A$ and $\vec C - \vec A$. The cross product can be multiplied out like an ordinary product, as long as the order of the factors is kept (each of its components is a sum of products of one component of each factor). With $\vec A \times \vec A = \vec 0$:

<Formula label="The vector of the fourth face" tex="\tfrac12\,(\vec B - \vec A) \times (\vec C - \vec A) = \tfrac12\left(\vec B \times \vec C - \vec B \times \vec A - \vec A \times \vec C\right) = \tfrac12\left(\vec B \times \vec C + \vec A \times \vec B + \vec C \times \vec A\right)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

It is perpendicular to the fourth face and as long as its area. Is it the outward one? The vertex $O$ is on the inner side of this face, and $\vec A$ runs from $O$ to a point of the face, so an outward vector has a positive dot product with $\vec A$. Here $\vec A \cdot (\vec A \times \vec B) = 0$ and $\vec A \cdot (\vec C \times \vec A) = 0$, because a cross product is perpendicular to both of its factors. What is left is $\tfrac12\,\vec A \cdot (\vec B \times \vec C)$, which is positive. So this vector points outwards.

</Step>

<Step title="Add the four vectors">

<Formula label="The sum of the four area vectors" tex="\tfrac12\left(\vec B \times \vec A + \vec C \times \vec B + \vec A \times \vec C\right) + \tfrac12\left(\vec A \times \vec B + \vec B \times \vec C + \vec C \times \vec A\right) = \vec 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

Each term in the first bracket is cancelled by a term in the second, because $\vec B \times \vec A = -\vec A \times \vec B$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Orthogonal matrices: rotations and reflections" points={9} mit="Part B, Problem 4">

The **transpose** $A^T$ of a matrix $A$ is the matrix with the rows of $A$ written as columns. A square matrix $A$ is called **orthogonal** when

<Formula label="Orthogonal matrix" tex="A\,A^T = I" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 4, p. 2" />

where $I$ is the identity matrix. A matrix that undoes a square matrix from one side also undoes it from the other (MIT points to exercise 1G-9b of its Notes for this), so $A^T A = I$ holds as well, and $A^T$ is the inverse of $A$. Entry by entry, $A A^T = I$ says that the rows of $A$ are perpendicular to each other and have length $1$: the entries of $A A^T$ are the dot products of the rows of $A$ with each other. In the same way $A^T A = I$ says that the columns of $A$ are perpendicular to each other and have length $1$.

The geometric meaning, which MIT states without proof: multiplying by an orthogonal matrix keeps the length of every vector, $|A\vec v| = |\vec v|$, and keeps the size of the angle between any two vectors. There are two types of orthogonal matrices: rotations and reflections.

<Formula label="Nutrients in each pastry" tex="NM = \begin{bmatrix} 2.85 & 5.82 & 5.65 \\ 34.77 & 40.54 & 41.05 \\ 8.92 & 10.00 & 19.04 \end{bmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 5(c) (our own solution)" />

Its columns are the grams of protein, carbohydrates and fat in one cookie, one doughnut and one croissant.

</Step>

<Step title="The inverse">

A computer gives the inverse at once. By hand, expand the determinant along the first row:

<Formula label="Determinant of NM" tex="\det(NM) = 2.85 \times 361.3816 - 5.82 \times 295.8548 + 5.65 \times (-13.9168) = -770.567" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 5(c) (our own solution)" />

The first of the three minors is $40.54 \times 19.04 - 41.05 \times 10.00 = 361.3816$, and it is also the cofactor that ends up in the top left corner of the inverse: $a_{11} = 361.3816/(-770.567) = -0.4690$. The other entries go the same way. To four significant digits:

<Formula label="The inverse of NM" tex="A = (NM)^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.4690 & 0.07048 & -0.01280 \\ 0.3839 & -0.005017 & -0.1031 \\ 0.01806 & -0.03039 & 0.1127 \end{bmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 5(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

One pastry of each kind holds $14.32$ grams of protein, $116.36$ grams of carbohydrates and $37.96$ grams of fat (the row sums of $NM$). The first row of $A$ must turn those back into one cookie: $-0.4690 \times 14.32 + 0.07048 \times 116.36 - 0.01280 \times 37.96 = 1.00$. The units fit as well: $NM$ is in grams per pastry, so $A$ is in pastries per gram.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

For a diet of 2000 calories a day, the recommended daily amounts that MIT quotes are 50 grams of protein, 300 grams of carbohydrates and 65 grams of fat. Suppose you wanted to follow those guidelines while eating nothing but cookies, doughnuts and croissants. How many pastries of each kind should you eat every day? What is wrong with your answer? Explain.

Give each number to two decimal places. Each box accepts a value within about 1% of ours, so use at least four significant digits of every entry of $A$.

<Hint>

Here $Y$ is the column $50, 300, 65$. Part (c) gives $X$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Look at the signs of the three numbers.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-d-x1" label="Cookies, $x_1 =$" value="-3.136" tolerance="0.03" calc="p5-d-x1" />

<Answer id="p5-d-x2" label="Doughnuts, $x_2 =$" value="10.99" tolerance="0.1" calc="p5-d-x2" />

<Answer id="p5-d-x3" label="Croissants, $x_3 =$" value="-0.889" tolerance="0.01" calc="p5-d-x3" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Multiply the matrix $A$ of part (c) by the column of the three recommended amounts.

<Step title="Multiply">

<Formula label="The diet of pastries" tex="X = A\begin{bmatrix} 50 \\ 300 \\ 65 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3.136 \\ 10.99 \\ -0.889 \end{bmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 5(d) (our own solution)" />

For example, the first entry is $-0.4690 \times 50 + 0.07048 \times 300 - 0.01280 \times 65 = -3.14$ with the rounded entries of $A$, and $-3.136$ with all digits.

</Step>

<Step title="What is wrong">

The answer says: eat about 11 doughnuts a day, and eat about minus 3 cookies and minus 0.9 croissants. Nobody can eat a negative number of pastries. The mathematics is not at fault: the three equations have exactly one solution, and this is it. So no diet of these three pastries meets the three guidelines exactly.

</Step>

<Step title="Why it had to fail (our explanation)">

Compare fat with carbohydrates. The guidelines ask for $65/300 = 0.217$ grams of fat for each gram of carbohydrates. From the columns of $NM$, a cookie has $8.92/34.77 = 0.257$, a doughnut $10.00/40.54 = 0.247$ and a croissant $19.04/41.05 = 0.464$. Every pastry is richer in fat, for its carbohydrates, than the guidelines allow. So is every mixture of them with positive amounts. The only way to bring the ratio down is to take pastries away that were never there.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Put the answer back into $Y = (NM)X$. The first row gives $2.85 \times (-3.136) + 5.82 \times 10.99 + 5.65 \times (-0.889) = 50.0$ grams of protein, as asked. The third row gives $8.92 \times (-3.136) + 10.00 \times 10.99 + 19.04 \times (-0.889) = 65.0$ grams of fat.

</Step>

</Solution>
