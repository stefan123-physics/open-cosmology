The [chain rule](/courses/18-02/differentials-and-chain-rule) can be written more compactly with a new vector, the gradient. This lecture explains what the gradient means and what it is good for (transcript, p. 1).

## The gradient vector

For $w = w(x, y, z)$ with $x$, $y$, $z$ depending on $t$, the chain rule says $dw/dt = w_x\,dx/dt + w_y\,dy/dt + w_z\,dz/dt$. That is a dot product (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The chain rule with the gradient" tex="\frac{dw}{dt} = \nabla w \cdot \frac{d\vec r}{dt}, \qquad \nabla w = \langle w_x, w_y, w_z\rangle" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 12, p. 1" />

The **gradient** $\nabla w$ is the vector of the partial derivatives. It depends on the point $(x, y, z)$: one vector at each point, what will later be called a vector field (p. 1; notes, p. 2). It holds the same information as the partial derivatives or the differential; so far it is notation (p. 1).

## Perpendicular to the level surfaces

The first property (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Gradient and level surfaces" tex="\nabla w \perp \{\,w = c\,\}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

For a function of two variables, $\nabla w$ is perpendicular to the level curves of its contour plot; for three variables, to the level surfaces (p. 1). It points towards **higher** values of $w$, which comes out of the directional derivative below (p. 1).

**Examples** (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

- $w = a_1x + a_2y + a_3z$: $\nabla w = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3\rangle$, and the level $w = c$ is a plane with exactly this normal vector. In a sense this is the general case, since linear approximation replaces level surfaces by their tangent planes.
- $w = x^2 + y^2$: the levels are circles, and $\nabla w = \langle 2x, 2y\rangle$ is twice the position vector, pointing radially out, perpendicular to the circles.
- $w = x^2 - y^2$, shown on an applet: the gradient, moved around the contour plot, stays perpendicular to the level curves.

**Proof.** Take a curve $\vec r(t)$ that stays on the level surface $w = c$. Its velocity $\vec v = d\vec r/dt$ is tangent to the curve, hence to the surface. By the chain rule (pp. 1–2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Moving along a level" tex="\frac{dw}{dt} = \nabla w \cdot \vec v = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

because $w$ stays equal to $c$. So $\nabla w \perp \vec v$. And any vector tangent to the surface is the velocity of some curve on it, so $\nabla w$ is perpendicular to the whole tangent plane (p. 2; notes, p. 2).

## Tangent planes to surfaces

So the gradient gives a normal vector to any surface given by an equation (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 2). The lecture's example is the hyperboloid $x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 4$ at $(2, 1, 1)$: the level $w = 4$ of $w = x^2 + y^2 - z^2$. Then $\nabla w = \langle 2x, 2y, -2z\rangle = \langle 4, 2, -2\rangle$, and the tangent plane is $4x + 2y - 2z = 8 + 2 - 2$ (p. 2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Tangent plane to the hyperboloid" tex="4x + 2y - 2z = 8" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

(Solving for $z$ and using the linear approximation would also work here, but not in general; notes, p. 2.) **Another way:** $dw = 2x\,dx + 2y\,dy - 2z\,dz = 4\,dx + 2\,dy - 2\,dz$ at the point, so $\Delta w \approx 4\Delta x + 2\Delta y - 2\Delta z$. Staying on the level means $\Delta w = 0$; setting the linear approximation exactly to zero gives the tangent plane, $4(x - 2) + 2(y - 1) - 2(z - 1) = 0$, the same plane (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 2).

## Directional derivatives

$w_x$ and $w_y$ are rates of change along $\hat i$ and $\hat j$. What about any other direction, given by a unit vector $\hat u = \langle a, b\rangle$? Move along the straight line $x(s) = x_0 + as$, $y(s) = y_0 + bs$ at unit speed, so that $s$ is the distance travelled (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 2). The **directional derivative** is the rate of change of $w$ along it, written $(dw/ds)_{\hat u}$. (The lecture writes $dw/ds$ with a vertical bar and the subscript $\hat u$; it means the same.) Geometrically, it is the slope of the slice of the graph by the vertical plane in the direction $\hat u$, no longer parallel to the $x$ or $y$ axis (p. 3; notes, p. 2). By the chain rule, with $d\vec r/ds = \hat u$ (p. 3; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Directional derivative" tex="\left(\frac{dw}{ds}\right)_{\hat u} = \nabla w \cdot \frac{d\vec r}{ds} = \nabla w \cdot \hat u" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

It is the component of the gradient in the direction $\hat u$; for $\hat u = \hat i$ it is $w_x$, as it should be (p. 3).

## The direction of fastest increase

Since $|\hat u| = 1$ (transcript, pp. 3–4; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Directional derivative and angle" tex="\left(\frac{dw}{ds}\right)_{\hat u} = |\nabla w|\,\cos\theta" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

with $\theta$ the angle between $\nabla w$ and $\hat u$. At a given point (p. 4; notes, p. 2):

- it is **largest** when $\cos\theta = 1$, that is $\hat u$ along $\nabla w$: **the gradient points in the direction of fastest increase**, and its length $|\nabla w|$ is the rate of increase in that direction;
- it is **smallest** when $\theta = 180°$, opposite to the gradient, the fastest decrease: facing up a mountain, the steepest way down is behind you;
- it is **zero** when $\theta = 90°$, perpendicular to the gradient: that is the direction tangent to the level, where $w$ does not change.

On a contour plot, then, the gradient at a point is perpendicular to the level curve and points towards the higher levels (p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="Gradient, tangent line and directional derivative">
<Problem>
Let $w = x^2 + 2y^2$ and the point $(1, 1)$. (a) Find $\nabla w$ and the tangent line to the level curve through the point. (b) Find the directional derivative in the direction $\hat u = \langle 3/5, 4/5\rangle$. (c) What is the largest rate of increase, and in which directions is the rate zero?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Gradient and tangent line">
$\nabla w = \langle 2x, 4y\rangle = \langle 2, 4\rangle$. The level curve is $x^2 + 2y^2 = 3$, an ellipse, and its tangent line has normal $\langle 2, 4\rangle$: $2x + 4y = 2 + 4 = 6$, or $x + 2y = 3$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A directional derivative">
$\hat u$ is a unit vector, since $(3/5)^2 + (4/5)^2 = 1$. Then $dw/ds = \langle 2, 4\rangle \cdot \langle 3/5, 4/5\rangle = 6/5 + 16/5 = 22/5 = 4.4$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Fastest increase and the level directions">
The largest rate is $|\nabla w| = \sqrt{4 + 16} = \sqrt{20} \approx 4.47$, along $\langle 2, 4\rangle/\sqrt{20}$; part (b), at an angle with $\cos\theta = 4.4/4.47 \approx 0.98$, is close to it. The rate is zero along $\pm\langle 2, -1\rangle/\sqrt 5$, perpendicular to the gradient and along the tangent line $x + 2y = 3$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Gravity is a gradient">
Newtonian gravity can be described by a potential $\phi(x, y, z)$, a function of position, and the gravitational acceleration is minus its gradient, $-\nabla\phi$: matter is pulled perpendicular to the surfaces of equal potential, towards lower potential, as fast as possible downhill. In the theory of how small density ripples in the early universe grew into galaxies, gravity enters exactly this way; see [linear perturbation theory](/courses/8-902/linear-perturbations).
</Callout>

## Summary

- The gradient $\nabla w = \langle w_x, w_y, w_z\rangle$ is a vector at each point; the chain rule is $dw/dt = \nabla w \cdot d\vec r/dt$.
- $\nabla w$ is perpendicular to the level curves or surfaces $w = c$; this gives tangent planes, such as $4x + 2y - 2z = 8$ for $x^2 + y^2 - z^2 = 4$ at $(2, 1, 1)$.
- The directional derivative along a unit vector $\hat u$ is $dw/ds = \nabla w \cdot \hat u = |\nabla w|\cos\theta$.
- $\nabla w$ points in the direction of fastest increase, with rate $|\nabla w|$; $-\nabla w$ is the fastest decrease; along the level the rate is zero.
