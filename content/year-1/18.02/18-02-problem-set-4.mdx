## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `r*cos(theta)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type `theta` for $\theta$, and `t1` and `t2` for the angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ of Problem 2. A number box wants a decimal number, not a fraction or a square root, and its question says how exact your value must be: keep all the digits your calculator shows while you work, and round only at the end. Unless a box asks for more digits, give 3 significant figures in the box; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. Angles are in radians. The points, and their split over the parts of a problem, are MIT's. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Parts that ask you to show or draw something have no answer box: compare your work with the solution and mark it yourself. Your score is kept in this browser only.

Problem 1 needs a tool that can add and multiply lists of five numbers. MIT's students used MATLAB. Use anything you like: a calculator, a spreadsheet, Python or another free program. Every sum in it has only five terms, so it can also be done by hand.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Least squares and five years of iPod sales" points={11} mit="Part B, Problem 1">

The lesson finds the line $y = ax + b$ that best fits $n$ data points $(x_i, y_i)$: the numbers $a$ and $b$ make the total square deviation as small as possible, and they solve two linear equations. This problem writes those equations with matrices, and then uses them on real data.

MIT asks its students to do parts (b) to (f) with MATLAB, or with other software or a calculator, on one condition that holds here too: carry out the calculation with your own formulas of part (a), and do not press a ready-made button for a best-fitting line (except to check your result).

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Put the data in three row vectors with $n$ entries each: $\mathbf{x} = [x_1\ x_2\ \dots\ x_n]$, $\mathbf{y} = [y_1\ y_2\ \dots\ y_n]$, and $\mathbf{u} = [1\ 1\ \dots\ 1]$, a row of $n$ ones. The lesson's two least-squares equations (equations (4) of MIT's Notes LS) are

<Formula label="The least-squares equations" tex="\Big(\sum x_i^2\Big)a + \Big(\sum x_i\Big)b = \sum x_iy_i, \qquad \Big(\sum x_i\Big)a + n\,b = \sum y_i" cite="notesLS" at="equations (4), p. 2" />

Write them as one matrix equation

<Formula label="The matrix form asked for" tex="A\,\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{r}, \qquad \mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

where $A$ is a 2 × 2 matrix and $\mathbf{r}$ a column with two entries. Write the entries of $A$ and $\mathbf{r}$ the way MATLAB wants them, without summation signs. In MATLAB, `*` is the matrix product and an apostrophe makes the transpose, so `u'` is the column of $n$ ones, and `x*u'` is the sum $\sum x_i$. Inside square brackets, a space separates the entries of a row and a semicolon starts a new row.

<Hint>

A row with $n$ entries times a column with $n$ entries is one number: the sum of the products of their entries. Which row and which column give $\sum x_i^2$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The number $n$ is a sum too: it is $1 + 1 + \dots + 1$, with $n$ terms.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="The matrix equation has"
  choices={["A = [x*u' x*x'; u*u' x*u'] and r = [x*y'; u*y']", "A = [x*x' x*u'; x*u' u*u'] and r = [u*y'; x*y']", "A = [x*x' x*u'; x*u' u*u'] and r = [x*y'; u*y']", "A = [x'*x x'*u; x'*u u'*u] and r = [x'*y; u'*y]"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Every sum in the two equations is a sum of products of entries of two of the three rows. Such a sum is the first row times the transpose of the second.

<Step title="The sums as products">

<Formula label="Each sum as a row times a column" tex="\sum x_i^2 = \mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{x}^T, \qquad \sum x_i = \mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{u}^T, \qquad n = \mathbf{u}\,\mathbf{u}^T, \qquad \sum x_iy_i = \mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{y}^T, \qquad \sum y_i = \mathbf{u}\,\mathbf{y}^T" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

Here $\mathbf{x}^T$ is the transpose of $\mathbf{x}$: the same numbers written as a column.

</Step>

<Step title="The matrix equation">

The first equation is the first row of $A$ times the column $\mathbf{z}$, and the second equation is the second row:

<Formula label="The least-squares equations in matrix form" tex="\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{x}^T & \mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{u}^T \\ \mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{u}^T & \mathbf{u}\,\mathbf{u}^T \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x}\,\mathbf{y}^T \\ \mathbf{u}\,\mathbf{y}^T \end{bmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

In MATLAB: `A = [x*x' x*u'; x*u' u*u']` and `r = [x*y'; u*y']`. The solution is then $\mathbf{z} = A^{-1}\mathbf{r}$, the lesson's way of solving a square system, typed as `z = inv(A)*r`.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The order of the factors matters. A row times a column, `x*x'`, is one number. A column times a row, `x'*x`, is a matrix with $n$ rows and $n$ columns, which is not what the equations need. The order of the unknowns matters too: the first column of $A$ multiplies $a$, so it must hold the coefficients of $a$, which are $\sum x_i^2$ and $\sum x_i$. Part (c) tests the formulas on numbers.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={0}>

MIT's sheet gives the number of iPods sold in the whole world in each year from 2001 to 2005, in millions (it names Wikipedia and Apple's quarterly reports as its sources):

| Year | 2001 | 2002 | 2003 | 2004 | 2005 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $x_i$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| Sales $y_i$ (millions) | 0.125 | 0.470 | 1.451 | 8.263 | 31.960 |

To keep the numbers small, $x$ is the year minus 2000, so $x_i$ runs from 1 to 5.

Make a scatter plot of the five data points: one mark for each point $(x_i, y_i)$, not joined by lines. This part earns no points at MIT, and the plot comes back in part (e).

<Solution>

**The idea.** Look at the data before fitting anything to them.

<Step title="What the plot shows">

The first three marks lie almost on the $x$-axis, at heights $0.125$, $0.470$ and $1.451$. The fourth is at $8.263$ and the fifth far above it at $31.960$. The points do not lie near a straight line: they bend upwards more and more steeply. The plot in the solution of part (e) shows them.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Use the data of part (b) and your formulas of part (a) to find the best-fitting line $y = ax + b$. Then compute, for each of the five years, the error: the actual value $y_i$ minus the value $ax_i + b$ that the line predicts. Report $a$, $b$ and the worst case, the error of largest size.

Give $a$ and $b$ to three decimal places, and the size of the largest error in millions of iPods, to two decimal places.

MIT recommends a check for those who use MATLAB: `polyfit(x,y,1)` returns the coefficients $[a, b]$ of the best line. Any ready-made line fit of your own tool does the same. Use it only as a check.

<Hint>

You need four sums: $\sum x_i$, $\sum x_i^2$, $\sum y_i$ and $\sum x_iy_i$. The number of points is $n = 5$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Two equations in two unknowns: solve them with the inverse of the 2 × 2 matrix, or take a multiple of the second equation away from the first so that $b$ drops out.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c-a" label="$a =$ (3 decimal places)" value="7.146" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-c-a" />

<Answer id="p1-c-b" label="$b =$ (3 decimal places)" value="-12.985" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-c-b" />

<Answer id="p1-c-worst" scale="1e6" label="Size of the largest error:" value="9.21" unit="million" tolerance="0.02" calc="p1-c-worst" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compute the four sums, fill in the matrix equation of part (a), and solve it.

<Step title="The sums">

$\sum x_i = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 15$ and $\sum x_i^2 = 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 = 55$. From the table, $\sum y_i = 42.269$, and

<Formula label="The sum of the products" tex="\sum x_iy_i = 1(0.125) + 2(0.470) + 3(1.451) + 4(8.263) + 5(31.960) = 198.27" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve the system">

<Formula label="The system for the iPod data" tex="\begin{bmatrix} 55 & 15 \\ 15 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} a \\ b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 198.27 \\ 42.269 \end{bmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

The determinant of the matrix is $55 \cdot 5 - 15 \cdot 15 = 50$. Three times the second equation, taken away from the first, leaves $10a = 198.27 - 126.807 = 71.463$. Then the second equation gives $b$:

<Formula label="The best-fitting line" tex="a = 7.1463, \qquad b = \frac{42.269 - 15a}{5} = -12.9851, \qquad y = 7.1463\,x - 12.9851" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The errors">

| Year | 2001 | 2002 | 2003 | 2004 | 2005 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Actual $y_i$ | 0.125 | 0.470 | 1.451 | 8.263 | 31.960 |
| Line $ax_i + b$ | −5.8388 | 1.3075 | 8.4538 | 15.6001 | 22.7464 |
| Error | 5.9638 | −0.8375 | −7.0028 | −7.3371 | 9.2136 |

The worst case is 2005: the line is $9.21$ million too low. The error of 2004 is nearly as large, $7.34$ million the other way.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The second least-squares equation, divided by $n$, says that the line passes through the point of the averages, $(3, 8.4538)$: indeed $7.1463 \times 3 - 12.9851 = 8.4538$. The same equation says that the five errors add up to zero, and they do: $5.9638 - 0.8375 - 7.0028 - 7.3371 + 9.2136 = 0.0000$. The fit is poor all the same. The errors are as large as the sales themselves, and the line predicts that minus $5.8$ million iPods were sold in 2001.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

In the first years after a new product comes on the market, its sales tend to grow exponentially and not along a line. The lesson's method for an exponential law is to take logarithms: find the best fit of the form

<Formula label="A line for the logarithm" tex="\ln y = a_1x + b_1" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(d), p. 2" />

to the same data. (In MATLAB and in most programs the natural logarithm is typed `log`.) Report $a_1$ and $b_1$, to three decimal places. Taking the exponential of both sides gives

<Formula label="The exponential law" tex="y = e^{b_1}\,e^{a_1x}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(d), p. 2" />

Compute the errors, the actual $y_i$ minus the value of this formula, and report the size of the largest one in millions of iPods, to two decimal places. Use all the digits of $a_1$ and $b_1$: the exponential is sensitive to rounding.

<Hint>

The data points are now $(x_i, \ln y_i)$. The matrix $A$ of part (c) stays the same, because the $x_i$ are the same. Only the right-hand side changes.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The errors asked for are differences of $y$ values, in millions of iPods, not differences of logarithms.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d-a1" label="$a_1 =$ (3 decimal places)" value="1.395" tolerance="0.002" calc="p1-d-a1" />

<Answer id="p1-d-b1" label="$b_1 =$ (3 decimal places)" value="-3.564" tolerance="0.003" calc="p1-d-b1" />

<Answer id="p1-d-worst" scale="1e6" label="Size of the largest error:" value="1.58" unit="million" tolerance="0.02" calc="p1-d-worst" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Replace each $y_i$ by $\ln y_i$ and repeat part (c).

<Step title="The logarithms and their sums">

| $x_i$ | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\ln y_i$ | −2.0794 | −0.7550 | 0.3723 | 2.1118 | 3.4645 |

Their sum is $\sum \ln y_i = 3.1141$, and $\sum x_i\ln y_i = 23.2968$.

</Step>

<Step title="Solve the system">

The matrix is the one of part (c). Three times the second equation, taken away from the first, leaves $10a_1 = 23.2968 - 3 \times 3.1141 = 13.9547$ (with all digits kept), so

<Formula label="The best fit for the logarithm" tex="a_1 = 1.3955, \qquad b_1 = \frac{3.1141 - 15a_1}{5} = -3.5636" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The errors">

| Year | 2001 | 2002 | 2003 | 2004 | 2005 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Actual $y_i$ | 0.125 | 0.470 | 1.451 | 8.263 | 31.960 |
| Exponential law | 0.114 | 0.462 | 1.864 | 7.525 | 30.379 |
| Error | 0.011 | 0.008 | −0.413 | 0.738 | 1.581 |

The worst case is again 2005, but now the law is only $1.58$ million too low, against $9.21$ million for the line.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The law reads $y = 0.02834 \times 4.037^{\,x}$, because $e^{b_1} = 0.02834$ and $e^{a_1} = 4.037$: the fitted sales grow by a factor of about $4$ every year. The data agree: the sales of each year divided by those of the year before are $3.76$, $3.09$, $5.69$ and $3.87$. One remark of our own: this fit makes the square deviations of $\ln y$ as small as possible, not those of $y$ itself, so it is the best line for the logarithms and not exactly the best exponential for the sales.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The largest triangle inscribed in the unit circle" points={8} mit="Part B, Problem 2">

A triangle is inscribed in the unit circle of the plane: its three vertices lie on the circle of radius $1$ about the origin. One vertex is the point $(1, 0)$. The other two are the points of the circle at the polar angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, and going round the circle counterclockwise from $(1, 0)$ you meet the vertex at $\theta_1$ first and the vertex at $\theta_2$ second.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Express the area $A$ of the triangle in terms of $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$. What is the set of possible values of $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$?

In the box, type `t1` for $\theta_1$ and `t2` for $\theta_2$.

<Hint>

The vertices are $(1, 0)$, $(\cos\theta_1, \sin\theta_1)$ and $(\cos\theta_2, \sin\theta_2)$. Take the two vectors from the first vertex to the other two.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The lesson on determinants gives the area of the parallelogram with two given sides in the plane as a 2 × 2 determinant, up to its sign. A triangle is half a parallelogram.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$A =$" expr="(sin(t1) + sin(t2 - t1) - sin(t2))/2"
  vars={{ t1: [0.3, 0.9], t2: [1.2, 2.0] }}
  names={{ t1: "\\theta_1", t2: "\\theta_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sin(t1) + sin(t2 - t1) - sin(t2)", hint: "That is the area of the parallelogram with the two sides from (1, 0). The triangle is half of it." },
    { expr: "(sin(t1) + sin(t2 - t1) + sin(t2))/2", hint: "Check a flat triangle: when θ₁ goes to 0, two vertices fall together and the area must go to 0. Yours goes to sin θ₂." },
    { expr: "sin(t2 - t1)/2", hint: "That is the area of the triangle with the origin and the two moving vertices as corners. The third vertex of this triangle is (1, 0), not the origin." },
    { expr: "(sin(t2) - sin(t1) - sin(t2 - t1))/2", hint: "That is minus the area: try θ₁ = π/2 and θ₂ = π, a triangle with area 1. The order of the two vectors in the determinant decides the sign." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-a-region" label="The possible values are"
  choices={["0 < θ₁ < 2π and 0 < θ₂ < 2π, without any other condition", "0 < θ₂ < θ₁ < 2π", "0 < θ₁ < π < θ₂ < 2π", "0 < θ₁ < θ₂ < 2π"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Half the determinant of two sides of a triangle is its area, with a plus sign when the second side lies counterclockwise from the first.

<Step title="Two sides as vectors">

Call the vertices $P_0 = (1, 0)$, $P_1 = (\cos\theta_1, \sin\theta_1)$ and $P_2 = (\cos\theta_2, \sin\theta_2)$ (the names are ours). Then

<Formula label="Two sides of the inscribed triangle" tex="\vec{P_0P_1} = \langle \cos\theta_1 - 1,\ \sin\theta_1\rangle, \qquad \vec{P_0P_2} = \langle \cos\theta_2 - 1,\ \sin\theta_2\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The determinant">

The vertices $P_0$, $P_1$, $P_2$ follow each other counterclockwise, so the determinant of the two sides, in this order, is positive and equal to the area of their parallelogram:

<Formula label="Area of the inscribed triangle" tex="A = \tfrac12\big((\cos\theta_1 - 1)\sin\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1(\cos\theta_2 - 1)\big) = \tfrac12\big(\sin\theta_1 + \sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1) - \sin\theta_2\big)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

The last step uses $\sin\theta_2\cos\theta_1 - \cos\theta_2\sin\theta_1 = \sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The possible values">

Measure both angles from the vertex $(1, 0)$, counterclockwise, between $0$ and $2\pi$. The vertex at $\theta_1$ comes first and the three vertices are different points, so

<Formula label="The region of the two angles" tex="0 < \theta_1 < \theta_2 < 2\pi" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

In a plane with $\theta_1$ along one axis and $\theta_2$ along the other, this region is the inside of a triangle with corners $(0, 0)$, $(0, 2\pi)$ and $(2\pi, 2\pi)$. On its three sides, $\theta_1 = 0$, $\theta_2 = 2\pi$ and $\theta_1 = \theta_2$, two vertices of the inscribed triangle fall together and it flattens to a line segment.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $\theta_1 = \pi/2$ and $\theta_2 = \pi$: the vertices are $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(-1, 0)$, a triangle with base $2$ and height $1$, so area $1$. The formula gives $\tfrac12(1 + 1 - 0) = 1$. A second check: the three terms are the areas, with signs, of the three triangles that join the origin to each side. The side from $P_0$ to $P_1$ gives $\tfrac12\sin\theta_1$, the side from $P_1$ to $P_2$ gives $\tfrac12\sin(\theta_2 - \theta_1)$, and the side from $P_2$ back to $P_0$ gives $\tfrac12\sin(2\pi - \theta_2) = -\tfrac12\sin\theta_2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Hint>

<Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A critical point is a point where both partial derivatives are zero.

<Step title="The partial derivatives">

<Formula label="The first derivatives of the area" tex="\frac{\partial A}{\partial\theta_1} = \tfrac12\big(\cos\theta_1 - \cos(\theta_2 - \theta_1)\big), \qquad \frac{\partial A}{\partial\theta_2} = \tfrac12\big(\cos(\theta_2 - \theta_1) - \cos\theta_2\big)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

Both are zero exactly when the three cosines are equal:

<Formula label="The condition for a critical point" tex="\cos\theta_1 = \cos(\theta_2 - \theta_1) = \cos\theta_2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two angles add up to a full turn">

On the way from $0$ to $2\pi$ the cosine falls from $1$ to $-1$ and climbs back, so two different angles in that range have the same cosine only when they lie mirrored about $\pi$. Since $\theta_1 < \theta_2$, the equation $\cos\theta_1 = \cos\theta_2$ gives $\theta_2 = 2\pi - \theta_1$, with $\theta_1 < \pi$.

</Step>

<Step title="The middle cosine">

Then $\theta_2 - \theta_1 = 2\pi - 2\theta_1$, and $\cos(2\pi - 2\theta_1) = \cos 2\theta_1 = 2\cos^2\theta_1 - 1$. The condition becomes

<Formula label="An equation for the first cosine" tex="2\cos^2\theta_1 - \cos\theta_1 - 1 = (2\cos\theta_1 + 1)(\cos\theta_1 - 1) = 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

$\cos\theta_1 = 1$ would mean $\theta_1 = 0$, which is not in the region. So $\cos\theta_1 = -\tfrac12$, and with $\theta_1 < \pi$:

<Formula label="The critical point of the area" tex="\theta_1 = \frac{2\pi}{3} = 2.094, \qquad \theta_2 = \frac{4\pi}{3} = 4.189" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

It is the only critical point in the region.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At this point $\theta_2 - \theta_1 = 2\pi/3$ as well, and the three cosines are $\cos(2\pi/3) = -\tfrac12$, $-\tfrac12$ and $\cos(4\pi/3) = -\tfrac12$. The solution $\cos\theta_1 = 1$ that was thrown out is not meaningless: at the three corners of the region, $(0, 0)$, $(0, 2\pi)$ and $(2\pi, 2\pi)$, all three cosines are $1$ and both derivatives are zero. But those points are on the boundary, where the triangle has shrunk to a single point.

</Step>

</Solution>

The picture agrees. At the point $(0, 5)$, moving outwards along $r$ is moving up, so $w_r = w_y$. Turning counterclockwise moves the point to the left, in the direction of $-x$, by $5$ units of length for each radian, so $w_\theta = -5\,w_x$. (This point is on the $y$-axis, where the formula $\tan^{-1}(y/x)$ of part (b) has no meaning. The entries of $B$ still hold there, as the check of part (b) explains.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## More practice: MIT's own solved exercises

Part A of MIT's Problem Set 4 (15 points at MIT, not scored here) is a list of short exercises.
