Multivariable calculus provides tools for problems with several parameters: functions of several variables (notes, p. 1). The first tool is the vector. (This page reviews vectors from [8.01](/courses/8-01/vectors) and the scalar product from [8.01's work in two and three dimensions](/courses/8-01/work-in-two-and-three-dimensions), and takes the dot product further.)

## Vectors

A **vector** has a direction and a length, and is drawn as an arrow (transcript, p. 1). In a coordinate system it is written by its components along the axes, using the unit vectors $\hat i$, $\hat j$, $\hat k$ of length one along the $x$, $y$ and $z$ axes (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Components of a vector" tex="\vec A = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3\rangle = a_1\,\hat i + a_2\,\hat j + a_3\,\hat k" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

Its length $|\vec A|$ is a **scalar**, an ordinary number; its direction can be obtained by scaling it down to unit length, $\vec A/|\vec A|$ (p. 2). A vector has no fixed starting point: the vector $\vec{PQ}$ from $P$ to $Q$ is the same vector wherever it is drawn (p. 2).

For the length of $\langle 3, 2, 1\rangle$, first find the length of $\vec B = 3\hat i + 2\hat j$ in the $xy$-plane, $\sqrt{9 + 4} = \sqrt{13}$, then go up one unit at a right angle: $|\vec A|^2 = |\vec B|^2 + 1 = 14$ (p. 3). In general (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Length of a vector" tex="|\vec A| = \sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

The same formula works with any number of components; in this course "multi" mostly means two or three, but the mathematics is the same for 10,000 variables (p. 3).

Every question about vectors has a geometric answer and a numerical one (p. 4). **Addition:** place $\vec B$ with its tail at the head of $\vec A$; the sum is the diagonal of the parallelogram, and $\vec A + \vec B = \vec B + \vec A$. Numerically, add the components (p. 4). **Scalar multiplication:** $2\vec A$ goes twice as far in the same direction, $-\vec A$ the opposite way; so $3\hat i + 2\hat j + \hat k$ means three steps along $x$, two along $y$, one up (pp. 4–5). $\vec A - \vec B$ is $\vec A$ plus $-\vec B$ (p. 5).

## The dot product

The **dot product** multiplies two vectors to give a number (transcript, p. 5; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Dot product" tex="\vec A \cdot \vec B = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3" cite="reading2" at="pp. 5–6" />

It is a **scalar**, not a vector, which is easy to forget in the middle of a calculation (p. 6). What makes it useful is its geometric meaning, the first theorem of the course (p. 6):

<Formula label="Geometric meaning" tex="\vec A \cdot \vec B = |\vec A|\,|\vec B|\cos\theta" cite="reading2" at="p. 6" />

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec A$ and $\vec B$: it tells us about lengths and angles at once, and it is easy to compute from components (p. 6).

**Why it is true** (pp. 7–8; notes, p. 1). First, $\vec A \cdot \vec A = a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2 = |\vec A|^2 = |\vec A|^2\cos 0$, so the two agree for a vector with itself. Next, take a triangle with sides $\vec A$, $\vec B$ and $\vec C = \vec A - \vec B$. The dot product expands like ordinary multiplication, and $\vec A \cdot \vec B = \vec B \cdot \vec A$, so

<Formula label="The law of cosines in vector form" tex="|\vec C|^2 = (\vec A - \vec B) \cdot (\vec A - \vec B) = |\vec A|^2 + |\vec B|^2 - 2\,\vec A \cdot \vec B" cite="reading2" at="pp. 7–8" />

The law of cosines says $|\vec C|^2 = |\vec A|^2 + |\vec B|^2 - 2|\vec A||\vec B|\cos\theta$. Comparing, $\vec A \cdot \vec B = |\vec A||\vec B|\cos\theta$: the theorem is the law of cosines in vector form, and either proves the other (p. 8).

## Lengths and angles

So $\cos\theta = \frac{\vec A \cdot \vec B}{|\vec A||\vec B|}$ (transcript, p. 9). Take the triangle with corners $P = (1, 0, 0)$, $Q = (0, 1, 0)$, $R = (0, 0, 2)$ and find the angle at $P$ (pp. 8–9). Then $\vec{PQ} = \langle -1, 1, 0\rangle$ and $\vec{PR} = \langle -1, 0, 2\rangle$ (pp. 9–10; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="An angle in space" tex="\cos\theta = \frac{\vec{PQ} \cdot \vec{PR}}{|\vec{PQ}|\,|\vec{PR}|} = \frac{1 + 0 + 0}{\sqrt 2\,\sqrt 5} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}, \qquad \theta \approx 71.6°" cite="reading2" at="pp. 9–10" />

(The lecture and notes give about 71.5°; the angle is 71.57°.) The **sign** of a dot product is the sign of $\cos\theta$, since lengths are positive: positive when the angle is less than 90° (the vectors point roughly the same way), zero at exactly 90°, negative when it is more (p. 10). The dot product measures how much two vectors go along each other (p. 10).

## Perpendicular vectors and planes

**Orthogonality**, a word from Greek for perpendicularity: $\vec A \cdot \vec B = 0$ exactly when $\cos\theta = 0$, that is, when $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ are perpendicular (transcript, pp. 10–12). What are the points with $x + 2y + 3z = 0$ (p. 11)? With $P = (x, y, z)$ and $\vec A = \langle 1, 2, 3\rangle$, the equation says (p. 11; notes, p. 1)

<Formula label="A plane through the origin" tex="x + 2y + 3z = 0 \Longleftrightarrow \vec{OP} \cdot \vec A = 0 \Longleftrightarrow \vec{OP} \perp \vec A" cite="reading2" at="p. 11" />

so the solutions form the **plane** through the origin perpendicular to $\vec A$; for a vertical $\vec A$, it would be the horizontal plane (p. 11). The next lecture uses the dot product to find components of a vector along any direction (p. 12).

<WorkedExample title="A length, an angle and a plane">
<Problem>
Let $\vec A = \langle 2, -1, 2\rangle$ and $\vec B = \langle 1, 1, 0\rangle$. (a) Find $|\vec A|$ and $|\vec B|$. (b) Find the angle between them. (c) Describe the points with $2x - y + 2z = 0$, and check that $(1, 2, 0)$ is one of them.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Lengths">
$|\vec A| = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 4} = 3$ and $|\vec B| = \sqrt 2 \approx 1.414$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The angle">
$\vec A \cdot \vec B = 2 - 1 + 0 = 1$, so $\cos\theta = \frac{1}{3\sqrt 2} \approx 0.236$ and $\theta \approx 76.4°$: a little less than a right angle, as the small positive dot product suggests.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A plane">
The equation says $\vec{OP} \cdot \vec A = 0$: it is the plane through the origin perpendicular to $\vec A$. For $(1, 2, 0)$: $2 - 2 + 0 = 0$, so it lies in the plane, and indeed $\langle 1, 2, 0\rangle \cdot \langle 2, -1, 2\rangle = 0$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Hubble's law is a vector law">
In a homogeneous expanding universe, the velocity of each galaxy relative to us is $\vec v = H\vec r$: parallel to its position vector, so $\vec v \cdot \vec r = H|\vec r|^2 > 0$ and every galaxy recedes. Because the law is linear in the vectors, the velocity of galaxy B as seen from galaxy A is $\vec v_B - \vec v_A = H(\vec r_B - \vec r_A)$: the same law, so every observer sees the same Hubble expansion.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A vector: $\vec A = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3\rangle = a_1\hat i + a_2\hat j + a_3\hat k$, length $\sqrt{a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2}$; add head to tail or componentwise.
- Dot product: $\vec A \cdot \vec B = a_1b_1 + a_2b_2 + a_3b_3 = |\vec A||\vec B|\cos\theta$, a scalar; it is the law of cosines.
- $\cos\theta = \frac{\vec A \cdot \vec B}{|\vec A||\vec B|}$; its sign tells whether the angle is acute, right or obtuse.
- $\vec A \cdot \vec B = 0$ exactly when $\vec A \perp \vec B$; $x + 2y + 3z = 0$ is the plane through the origin perpendicular to $\langle 1, 2, 3\rangle$.
