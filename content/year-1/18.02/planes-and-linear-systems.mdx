This lecture connects the [last one](/courses/18-02/matrices) in both directions: each equation of a linear system is a plane, and solving the system means finding where the planes meet (transcript, p. 1).

## Equations of planes

An equation $ax + by + cz = d$, with numbers $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$, is the condition for a point $(x, y, z)$ to be in a plane (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 2).

**Through the origin.** For the plane through the origin with normal vector $\vec N = \langle 1, 5, 10\rangle$, a point $P$ is in the plane exactly when $\vec{OP}$ makes a right angle with $\vec N$ (p. 1; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Plane through the origin" tex="\vec N \cdot \vec{OP} = \langle 1, 5, 10\rangle \cdot \langle x, y, z\rangle = x + 5y + 10z = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

**Through another point.** The plane through $P_0 = (2, 1, -1)$ with the same normal vector is parallel to the first one, shifted to pass through $P_0$. Now $\vec{P_0P}$ must be perpendicular to $\vec N$ (p. 1; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Plane through a point" tex="\vec N \cdot \vec{P_0P} = (x - 2) + 5(y - 1) + 10(z + 1) = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x + 5y + 10z = -3" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Only the constant changed. Faster: the left side must be $x + 5y + 10z$, and $P_0$ must satisfy the equation, so the constant is $2 + 5 \cdot 1 + 10 \cdot (-1) = -3$ (p. 1). The single most important point: **in $ax + by + cz = d$, the coefficients $\langle a, b, c\rangle$ are a normal vector** (p. 1; notes, p. 2). A plane has many equations, since $2x + 10y + 20z = -6$ describes the same plane, just as it has normal vectors of every length (p. 1). So finding a plane's equation really means finding a normal vector, for example as the cross product of two vectors in the plane (p. 1).

The constant $d$ measures roughly how far the plane is moved from the origin: $d = 0$ is a plane through the origin, and changing $d$ moves to a parallel plane. It is the distance from the origin only when $\langle a, b, c\rangle$ is a unit vector; otherwise it must be scaled by the length of the normal vector (p. 3).

## Parallel or perpendicular?

Is $\vec v = \langle 1, 2, -1\rangle$ parallel or perpendicular to the plane $x + y + 3z = 5$, or neither? (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 2.) A normal vector is $\vec N = \langle 1, 1, 3\rangle$. Being **perpendicular** to the plane means being parallel to $\vec N$, a multiple of it, and $\vec v$ is not. Being **parallel** to the plane means being perpendicular to $\vec N$ (p. 1):

<Formula label="Parallel to the plane" tex="\vec v \cdot \vec N = \langle 1, 2, -1\rangle \cdot \langle 1, 1, 3\rangle = 1 + 2 - 3 = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

So $\vec v$ is parallel to the plane. The point $(1, 2, -1)$ itself is not in the plane, since it gives $0$, not $5$; it is in the parallel plane $x + y + 3z = 0$ through the origin, which is another way to see the same thing (pp. 1–2).

## Three planes and a 3 × 3 system

Each equation of a 3 × 3 linear system is a plane, so solving the system means finding a point on all three planes (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 2). The lecture's example is $x + z = 1$, $x + y = 2$, $x + 2y + 3z = 3$. The first two planes meet in a line, and usually that line meets the third plane in one point: the solution (p. 2). In matrix form (p. 2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The unique solution" tex="AX = B \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X = A^{-1}B" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

(And not $BA^{-1}$, whose sizes do not fit, p. 2.) For this example the point is $(1, 1, 0)$: substituting $y = 2 - x$ and $z = 1 - x$ into the third equation gives $7 - 4x = 3$, so $x = 1$.

What else can happen? If the third plane is **parallel** to the line where the first two meet (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 3):

- the line lies **in** the third plane: infinitely many solutions, the whole line. Solving by hand, the third equation turns out to add nothing, $0 = 0$;
- the line is parallel to the third plane but **outside** it: no solution. Solving by hand gives a contradiction, such as $x + z = 1$ and $x + z = 2$ at once;

That picture assumed the first two planes meet in a line. They may instead be parallel and distinct, and then there is no solution already; or the same plane, and if the third is that plane too, there is a whole plane of solutions (p. 3). Exactly two solutions is impossible: if two points of the line are in the third plane, the whole line is (p. 2).

## When A has no inverse

Why can $X = A^{-1}B$ fail? The inverse is $\frac{1}{\det A}\operatorname{adj}(A)$; the adjoint can always be computed, but dividing by $\det A$ is impossible when it is zero. In fact (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Invertibility" tex="A \text{ is invertible} \Longleftrightarrow \det A \ne 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

When the determinant is zero there is no inverse at all, not just no formula for one (p. 3). Geometrically, $\det A \ne 0$ is the usual case of three planes meeting in a point, and $\det A = 0$ is the case of planes parallel to one line (pp. 3–4).

## Homogeneous systems

A system is **homogeneous** when the right-hand side is zero, $AX = 0$ (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 3). All three planes then pass through the origin, so $X = 0$ is always a solution, the **trivial solution** (p. 3).

- If $\det A \ne 0$, it is the only one: $X = A^{-1}0 = 0$ (p. 3).
- If $\det A = 0$: the rows of $A$ are the normal vectors $\vec N_1$, $\vec N_2$, $\vec N_3$ of the three planes, so their determinant is zero, the parallelepiped they span is flat, and they are **coplanar** (p. 3). The line through the origin perpendicular to the plane of the normals is parallel to all three planes, and since it passes through the origin, it lies in all of them: infinitely many solutions (pp. 3–4). A vector along it (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="A nontrivial solution" tex="\vec N_1 \times \vec N_2 \ \text{ is perpendicular to } \vec N_1,\ \vec N_2 \text{ and } \vec N_3" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

It is perpendicular to $\vec N_3$ too, because $\vec N_3$ lies in the plane of $\vec N_1$ and $\vec N_2$ (p. 4). (This needs $\vec N_1$ and $\vec N_2$ not parallel; otherwise their cross product is zero, and another pair of normals is used.)

## General systems

For $AX = B$ with any $B$, the planes are those of $AX = 0$ shifted to parallel positions (notes, p. 3). If $\det A \ne 0$ there is exactly one solution, $X = A^{-1}B$. If $\det A = 0$ there is either no solution or infinitely many, never exactly one (transcript, p. 4). Which of the two is not decided by the determinant; elimination by hand ends either in $0 = 0$ (infinitely many) or in a contradiction such as $1 = 2$ (none) (p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="A plane, and a system with det A = 0">
<Problem>
(a) Find the plane through $(1, 2, 3)$ with normal vector $\langle 2, -1, 4\rangle$. (b) Show that the system $x + y + z = 0$, $x - y = 0$, $2x + z = 0$ has nontrivial solutions, and find them. (c) With right-hand sides $1, 1, 3$ instead of zeros, does it have a solution?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Coefficients from the normal">
The equation is $2x - y + 4z = d$, and the point gives $d = 2 - 2 + 12 = 12$: the plane is $2x - y + 4z = 12$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The determinant and the line of solutions">
The normals are $\vec N_1 = \langle 1, 1, 1\rangle$, $\vec N_2 = \langle 1, -1, 0\rangle$, $\vec N_3 = \langle 2, 0, 1\rangle$. Expanding along the first row, $\det A = 1(-1 - 0) - 1(1 - 0) + 1(0 + 2) = 0$: indeed $\vec N_3 = \vec N_1 + \vec N_2$. A solution along the line is $\vec N_1 \times \vec N_2 = \langle 1 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot (-1),\ -(1 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot 1),\ 1 \cdot (-1) - 1 \cdot 1\rangle = \langle 1, 1, -2\rangle$. Check: $1 + 1 - 2 = 0$, $1 - 1 = 0$, $2 - 2 = 0$. All multiples $t\langle 1, 1, -2\rangle$ are solutions.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) No solution">
Adding the first two equations gives $2x + z = 1 + 1 = 2$, but the third says $2x + z = 3$: a contradiction, so there is no solution. With $2$ instead of $3$ on the right, there would be infinitely many.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- In $ax + by + cz = d$, $\langle a, b, c\rangle$ is a normal vector; $d$ shifts the plane and is fixed by any one point on it.
- A vector is perpendicular to a plane when it is a multiple of the normal, and parallel to the plane when its dot product with the normal is zero.
- A 3 × 3 system asks where three planes meet: usually one point, $X = A^{-1}B$.
- $A$ is invertible exactly when $\det A \ne 0$.
- $AX = 0$ always has $X = 0$; when $\det A = 0$ the normals are coplanar and there is a line of solutions, along $\vec N_1 \times \vec N_2$ if those two are not parallel, or a plane.
- $AX = B$ with $\det A = 0$ has no solution or infinitely many.
