The quarter-disc integral of the [last lecture](/courses/18-02/double-integrals) needed a page of trigonometry. In polar coordinates it takes two lines; and then come the uses of double integrals (transcript, p. 1).

## The area element in polar coordinates

Polar coordinates describe a point by its distance $r$ from the origin and its angle $\theta$ from the positive $x$-axis: $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 2). To integrate in the order $r$ first, then $\theta$ (the usual one), fix $\theta$, travel out along that **ray**, and see which values of $r$ lie in the region; then let $\theta$ run over the directions that meet the region (p. 1).

The small pieces are now pieces of rings, between radii $r$ and $r + \Delta r$ and angles $\theta$ and $\theta + \Delta\theta$. When they are small they are nearly rectangles, with sides $\Delta r$ and $r\,\Delta\theta$ (an arc of radius $r$, not $\Delta\theta$ itself) (p. 1; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Area element" tex="\Delta A \approx (\Delta r)(r\,\Delta\theta), \qquad dA = r\,dr\,d\theta" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

(Using $r + \Delta r$ for the outer arc makes no difference in the limit; a second proof comes with changes of variables, p. 2.) **The factor $r$ is essential.**

**The quarter disc again.** $1 - x^2 - y^2 = 1 - r^2$, since $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$; the region is $0 \le r \le 1$, $0 \le \theta \le \pi/2$ (p. 1; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The quarter disc in polar coordinates" tex="\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^1 (1 - r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_0^{\pi/2}\left[\frac{r^2}{2} - \frac{r^4}{4}\right]_0^1 d\theta = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac14\,d\theta = \frac{\pi}{8}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Polar coordinates help when the region or the integrand is simpler in them. There can be a trade-off: a simple integrand like $x$ becomes $r\cos\theta$ (pp. 1–2).

## Area and mass

Double integrals are less about volumes than about **adding up a quantity over a region** (transcript, p. 2). The **area** of a region is the integral of 1, and the **mass** of a flat plate with density $\delta$ (mass per unit area, $\delta = \lim \Delta m/\Delta A$, which may vary from point to point) is the integral of the density (p. 2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Area and mass" tex="\text{Area}(R) = \int\int_R 1\,dA, \qquad M = \int\int_R \delta\,dA" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 17, p. 2" />

(Thinking of volumes, the first is a slab of height 1 over $R$, p. 2.) For a uniform plate $\delta$ comes out and $M = \delta \times$ area (p. 2). Solids in space need triple integrals, a few weeks later (p. 2).

## Average values and the centre of mass

The **average value** of $f$ over $R$ adds all its values and divides by the size of the sample, the area (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Average value" tex="\bar f = \frac{1}{\text{Area}(R)}\int\int_R f\,dA" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

A **weighted** average weights each piece by its density, and divides by the total weight, the mass; with uniform density it is the plain average again (pp. 2–3). The **centre of mass** of a plate is the weighted average of the coordinates: the point where all the mass could be concentrated, for pushing it around, and where the plate would balance on a finger (p. 3; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Centre of mass" tex="\bar x = \frac{1}{M}\int\int_R x\,\delta\,dA, \qquad \bar y = \frac{1}{M}\int\int_R y\,\delta\,dA" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

With no information about the density, take it uniform, even $\delta = 1$ (p. 3). The integrals can be evaluated in any coordinates, but the averages are of $x$ and $y$: the average of $r$ or of $\theta$ does not give the centre of mass. For a disc centred at the origin the average of $r$ is positive, yet the centre is the origin; and the average of $\theta$ depends on whether it runs over $[0, 2\pi]$ or $[-\pi, \pi]$ (p. 3).

## Moment of inertia

Mass measures how hard it is to set an object moving; the **moment of inertia** measures how hard it is to set it **spinning** about an axis (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 2). A mass $m$ at distance $r$ turning at angular velocity $\omega = d\theta/dt$ has speed $r\omega$ and kinetic energy $\frac12 mv^2 = \frac12 mr^2\omega^2$; the coefficient $mr^2$ plays the role of the mass (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 2). Adding the pieces of a plate (p. 4; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Moment of inertia about the origin" tex="I_0 = \int\int_R r^2\,\delta\,dA, \qquad \text{rotational energy} = \tfrac12 I_0\,\omega^2" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

About an axis, use the squared distance to that axis. About the $x$-axis it is $|y|$, so $I_x = \int\int y^2\,\delta\,dA$: $y$ appears because it measures distance from the $x$-axis (p. 4; notes, p. 3). (8.01 met the same idea as [the moment of inertia](/courses/8-01/moment-of-inertia).)

**A uniform disc about its centre** ($\delta = 1$, radius $a$). Here $r$ is a variable running over the disc, not the radius $a$ (p. 4; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Disc about its centre" tex="I_0 = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^a r^2\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\,\frac{a^4}{4} = \frac{\pi a^4}{2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

**The same disc about a point on its rim**, like a Frisbee spun from its edge. Move the origin to that point, with the diameter along the $x$-axis. A ray at angle $\theta$ enters at $r = 0$ and leaves the circle at $r = 2a\cos\theta$ (a right triangle in a semicircle, hypotenuse $2a$); every direction with $-\pi/2 \le \theta \le \pi/2$ meets the disc (p. 4; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Disc about a point on its rim" tex="I_0 = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\int_0^{2a\cos\theta} r^2\,r\,dr\,d\theta = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} 4a^4\cos^4\theta\,d\theta = \frac{3\pi a^4}{2}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 4–5" />

(The integral of $\cos^4\theta$ is done with double-angle formulas, or with the formulas at the start of section 3B of the course notes; transcript, p. 5.) It is **three times** harder to spin a disc about a point on its rim than about its centre (p. 5).

<WorkedExample title="A centroid and a non-uniform disc">
<Problem>
(a) Find the centre of mass of the uniform half disc $x^2 + y^2 \le 4$, $y \ge 0$. (b) A disc of radius 1 has density $\delta = r$ (heavier towards the rim). Find its mass and its moment of inertia about the centre.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The centroid">
Take $\delta = 1$; the area is $\frac12\pi \cdot 2^2 = 2\pi$. By symmetry $\bar x = 0$. And $\int_0^{\pi}\int_0^2 (r\sin\theta)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = \frac{8}{3}\int_0^\pi \sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{16}{3}$, so $\bar y = \frac{16/3}{2\pi} = \frac{8}{3\pi} \approx 0.849$: below the middle height 1 of the half disc, because most of the area is near the flat side.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Mass">
$M = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r \cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \frac13 = \frac{2\pi}{3} \approx 2.09$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Moment of inertia">
$I_0 = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 r^2 \cdot r \cdot r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \frac15 = \frac{2\pi}{5} \approx 1.26$. Compare a uniform disc of the same mass: its density would be $M/\pi = 2/3$, giving $I_0 = \frac23 \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{3} \approx 1.05$. Moving mass outwards makes it harder to spin.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Averages over the sky and over space">
Cosmology constantly uses averages of this kind. "The density of the universe" means the average of a very lumpy density over a large region: the integral of the density divided by the volume, the three-dimensional version of $\bar f$. On large scales the universe is homogeneous, so this average is the same wherever it is taken; see [the homogeneous expanding universe](/courses/8-286/homogeneous-expansion).
</Callout>

## Summary

- In polar coordinates $dA = r\,dr\,d\theta$; set up bounds by sweeping the region with rays (fixed $\theta$), then letting $\theta$ run over the directions that meet it.
- The quarter-disc integral is $\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^1 (1 - r^2)\,r\,dr\,d\theta = \pi/8$.
- Area $= \int\int 1\,dA$; mass $= \int\int \delta\,dA$; average $\bar f = \frac{1}{\text{Area}}\int\int f\,dA$; centre of mass $\bar x = \frac1M\int\int x\delta\,dA$, $\bar y = \frac1M\int\int y\delta\,dA$.
- Moment of inertia $I = \int\int (\text{distance to axis})^2\,\delta\,dA$; rotational energy $\frac12 I\omega^2$.
- A uniform disc: $I_0 = \pi a^4/2$ about the centre, $3\pi a^4/2$ about a point on the rim.
