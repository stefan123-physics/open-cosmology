## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A line integral that is a moment of inertia" points={3} mit="Part B, Problem 1">

A vector field has only an $x$-component:

<Formula label="The field of Problem 1" tex="\vec F = \left(x^2 y + \tfrac13 y^3\right)\hat i" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1, p. 1" />

Let $f$ be a function that takes only positive values, and let $C$ be the part of the graph $y = f(x)$ that runs from the point $(x_1, f(x_1))$ to the point $(x_2, f(x_2))$, with $x_1 < x_2$. Let $R$ be the region that lies below $C$ and above the $x$-axis, so between the vertical lines $x = x_1$ and $x = x_2$.

Show that the line integral $\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r$ is equal to the moment of inertia about the origin of the region $R$, with density $\delta = 1$. (MIT calls it the polar moment of inertia. It is the $I_0$ of the lesson on polar coordinates.)

<Hint>

Write the line integral as $\int_C M\,dx + N\,dy$. Which of the two terms is left for this field? Then use $x$ itself as the parameter along the graph.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the moment of inertia, $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$. Write the double integral over $R$ as an iterated integral with $y$ on the inside: for a fixed $x$, between which two values does $y$ run? Work out the inner integral only.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both quantities can be reduced to one integral over $x$ from $x_1$ to $x_2$. The line integral gets there by using $x$ as the parameter. The double integral gets there by doing its inner integral over $y$. The two integrands turn out to be the same.

<Step title="The line integral">

The lesson writes a line integral in components:

<Formula label="Line integral in differentials" tex="\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_C M\,dx + N\,dy" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 19, p. 3" />

Here $M = x^2 y + \tfrac13 y^3$ and $N = 0$, so only the term $M\,dx$ is left. Along the graph take $x$ as the parameter: $y = f(x)$, and $x$ runs from $x_1$ to $x_2$. Everything is then in terms of $x$:

<Formula label="The line integral as an integral over x" tex="\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \left(x^2 f(x) + \tfrac13 f(x)^3\right)dx" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The moment of inertia">

The lesson's moment of inertia about the origin is

<Formula label="Moment of inertia about the origin" tex="I_0 = \int\int_R r^2\,\delta\,dA" cite="reading3" at="Lecture 17, p. 2" />

With $\delta = 1$ and $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$. The region $R$ is swept by vertical segments: for each $x$ between $x_1$ and $x_2$, the height $y$ runs from $0$ (the $x$-axis) up to $f(x)$ (the curve $C$). This is where $f(x) > 0$ is used: the curve lies above the axis.

<Formula label="The moment of inertia as an iterated integral" tex="I_0 = \int_{x_1}^{x_2}\int_0^{f(x)} \left(x^2 + y^2\right)dy\,dx" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

In the inner integral $x$ is a constant:

<Formula label="The inner integral" tex="\int_0^{f(x)} \left(x^2 + y^2\right)dy = \left[x^2 y + \tfrac13 y^3\right]_0^{f(x)} = x^2 f(x) + \tfrac13 f(x)^3" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

So

<Formula label="The moment of inertia as an integral over x" tex="I_0 = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} \left(x^2 f(x) + \tfrac13 f(x)^3\right)dx = \int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

The component $M$ of the field was chosen for this: $x^2 y + \tfrac13 y^3$ is the antiderivative of $x^2 + y^2$ with respect to $y$ that is zero at $y = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Try the simplest case (our own example): $f(x) = 1$ from $x_1 = 0$ to $x_2 = 1$. Then $R$ is the unit square. The line integral is $\int_0^1 \left(x^2 + \tfrac13\right)dx = \tfrac13 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac23$. Directly, the moment of inertia of the unit square about its corner at the origin is $\int_0^1\int_0^1 (x^2 + y^2)\,dy\,dx = \tfrac13 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac23$ as well.

A look ahead (our addition): [Green's theorem](/courses/18-02/greens-theorem), in the lesson after these three, turns a line integral around a closed curve into a double integral of the curl over the region inside. This problem is a case of it, worked by hand.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The polar angle as a potential" points={8} mit="Part B, Problem 2">

Consider the vector field

<Formula label="The field of Problem 2" tex="\vec F(x, y) = \frac{-y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j}{x^2 + y^2}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2, p. 1" />

It is defined at every point of the plane except the origin.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

In the right half-plane $x > 0$ the polar angle of the point $(x, y)$ is

<Formula label="The polar angle in the right half-plane" tex="\theta(x, y) = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{y}{x}\right), \qquad x > 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a), p. 1" />

Show that $\vec F$ is the gradient of this function $\theta$ in the right half-plane. (MIT's remark: the formula for $\theta$ has no meaning where $x = 0$.)

<Hint>

The derivative of $\tan^{-1} u$ with respect to $u$ is $1/(1 + u^2)$. Here $u = y/x$: use the chain rule, once with respect to $x$ and once with respect to $y$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Calculate the two partial derivatives of $\theta$ and compare them with the two components of $\vec F$.

<Step title="The partial derivatives">

With $u = y/x$ we have $u_x = -y/x^2$ and $u_y = 1/x$. The chain rule gives

<Formula label="The partial derivative with respect to x" tex="\theta_x = \frac{1}{1 + (y/x)^2}\cdot\left(-\frac{y}{x^2}\right) = \frac{-y}{x^2 + y^2}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="The partial derivative with respect to y" tex="\theta_y = \frac{1}{1 + (y/x)^2}\cdot\frac{1}{x} = \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

In both lines the top and the bottom were multiplied by $x^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Compare">

These are the two components of the field, so

<Formula label="The field is the gradient of the polar angle" tex="\nabla\theta = \theta_x\,\hat i + \theta_y\,\hat j = \frac{-y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j}{x^2 + y^2} = \vec F \qquad \text{for } x > 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At the point $(1, 0)$ the field is $\hat j$: it points straight up, and going up from $(1, 0)$ is indeed the direction in which the polar angle grows fastest. The size of the field is $\sqrt{y^2 + x^2}/(x^2 + y^2) = 1/r$, with $r$ the distance to the origin. That fits too: a step of length $\Delta s$ along a circle of radius $r$ changes the angle by $\Delta s / r$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Let $C$ be a smooth curve in the right half-plane $x > 0$ that runs from the point $A = (x_1, y_1)$ to the point $B = (x_2, y_2)$. The polar coordinates of $A$ are $(r_1, \theta_1)$ and those of $B$ are $(r_2, \theta_2)$, with both angles between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$.

Express $\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r$ in terms of these polar coordinates. In the box, type `r1`, `theta1`, `r2` and `theta2` for the letters that you need. You may not need all four.

<Hint>

By part (a) the field is a gradient field in the region that holds the whole curve. Which theorem of the lesson gives the line integral of a gradient field without any integration?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r =$" expr="theta2 - theta1"
  vars={{ r1: [1.2, 2], theta1: [-1.2, -0.2], r2: [2.5, 4], theta2: [0.3, 1.3] }}
  names={{ r1: "r_1", theta1: "\\theta_1", r2: "r_2", theta2: "\\theta_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "theta1 - theta2", hint: "This is the value at the start minus the value at the end. The theorem takes the potential at the end point B minus the potential at the start point A." },
    { expr: "r2*theta2 - r1*theta1", hint: "The potential is the polar angle alone. It has the same value all along a ray from the origin, so the distances cannot be part of the answer." },
    { expr: "theta2 + theta1", hint: "The theorem gives a difference of two values of the potential, not a sum. A curve that ends where it starts must give zero." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the right half-plane $\vec F = \nabla\theta$, so the fundamental theorem for line integrals applies with the potential $\theta$.

<Step title="The fundamental theorem">

<Formula label="Fundamental theorem of calculus for line integrals" tex="\int_C \nabla f \cdot d\vec r = f(P_1) - f(P_0)" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 20, p. 4" />

Here $f = \theta$, the curve starts at $P_0 = A$ and ends at $P_1 = B$, and the whole curve lies in the region $x > 0$ where part (a) holds:

<Formula label="The work of the field along the curve" tex="\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \theta(B) - \theta(A) = \theta_2 - \theta_1" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

The distances $r_1$ and $r_2$ do not appear: the line integral is the angle that the curve sweeps out as seen from the origin.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take a curve along a ray from the origin (our own example), where $\theta_2 = \theta_1$. The answer is zero. That is right: on a ray the direction of motion is along $x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j$, and the field $-y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j$ is perpendicular to it, because $(-y)(x) + (x)(y) = 0$. So the field does no work there.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Calculate two line integrals directly from the definition, with a parametrisation and without the result of part (b):

- $\int_{C_1} \vec F \cdot d\vec r$, where $C_1$ is the upper half of the unit circle, from $(1, 0)$ to $(-1, 0)$;
- $\int_{C_2} \vec F \cdot d\vec r$, where $C_2$ is the lower half of the unit circle, also from $(1, 0)$ to $(-1, 0)$.

Give both as decimals, to three decimal places.

<Hint>

On the unit circle take $x = \cos t$. For the upper half $y = \sin t$ and $t$ runs from $0$ to $\pi$. Write $M\,dx + N\,dy$ in terms of $t$ and $dt$. The bottom $x^2 + y^2$ of the field is simple on this circle.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the lower half, mind the direction: the curve must still start at $(1, 0)$ and end at $(-1, 0)$. One way is $x = \cos t$, $y = -\sin t$, with $t$ from $0$ to $\pi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c1" label="Along the upper half circle $C_1$:" value="3.142" tolerance="0.002" calc="p2-c1" />

<Answer id="p2-c2" label="Along the lower half circle $C_2$:" value="-3.142" tolerance="0.002" calc="p2-c2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Parametrise each half circle, put everything in terms of the parameter, and integrate. On the unit circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, so there $\vec F = -y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j$.

<Step title="The method">

<Formula label="Computing a line integral" tex="\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \vec F \cdot \frac{d\vec r}{dt}\,dt" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 19, p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The upper half">

On $C_1$: $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$, with $t$ from $0$ to $\pi$. Then $dx = -\sin t\,dt$ and $dy = \cos t\,dt$, and

<Formula label="Along the upper half circle" tex="\int_{C_1} -y\,dx + x\,dy = \int_0^{\pi} \left(\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t\right)dt = \int_0^{\pi} dt = \pi = 3.142" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The lower half">

On $C_2$: $x = \cos t$, $y = -\sin t$, with $t$ from $0$ to $\pi$. This starts at $(1, 0)$, passes through $(0, -1)$ and ends at $(-1, 0)$. Now $dx = -\sin t\,dt$ and $dy = -\cos t\,dt$, while $-y = \sin t$:

<Formula label="Along the lower half circle" tex="\int_{C_2} -y\,dx + x\,dy = \int_0^{\pi} \left(-\sin^2 t - \cos^2 t\right)dt = -\int_0^{\pi} dt = -\pi = -3.142" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson's geometric form is

<Formula label="Tangential component" tex="\int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_C \vec F \cdot \hat T\,ds" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 19, p. 3" />

On the unit circle the field has size $1$ and is tangent to the circle, in the counterclockwise direction. Along $C_1$ the motion is counterclockwise, so $\vec F \cdot \hat T = 1$ and the integral is the length of the half circle, $\pi$. Along $C_2$ the motion is clockwise, so $\vec F \cdot \hat T = -1$ and the integral is $-\pi$.

The two results also fit the picture of part (b), an angle swept out: going over the top, the direction from the origin to the moving point turns from angle $0$ to angle $\pi$. Going under, it turns the other way, from $0$ to $-\pi$. (Part (b) itself cannot be used here, because these curves leave the right half-plane.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={1}>

Show that the curl of $\vec F$ is zero at every point of the plane where $\vec F$ is defined, not only in the right half-plane $x > 0$.

<Hint>

The curl is $N_x - M_y$, with $M = -y/(x^2 + y^2)$ and $N = x/(x^2 + y^2)$. Use the quotient rule for both partial derivatives.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Calculate $N_x$ and $M_y$ with the quotient rule. The formulas hold wherever $x^2 + y^2$ is not zero, which is every point except the origin.

<Step title="The definition">

<Formula label="Curl" tex="\operatorname{curl}\vec F = N_x - M_y" cite="reading2" at="Lecture 21, p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two partial derivatives">

<Formula label="The derivative of N with respect to x" tex="N_x = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}\right) = \frac{(x^2 + y^2) - x \cdot 2x}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(d) (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="The derivative of M with respect to y" tex="M_y = \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\left(\frac{-y}{x^2 + y^2}\right) = \frac{-(x^2 + y^2) + y \cdot 2y}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = \frac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The curl">

The two are equal, so $\operatorname{curl}\vec F = N_x - M_y = 0$ at every point other than the origin. Nothing in this calculation needed $x > 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In the right half-plane this had to come out: there $\vec F = \nabla\theta$ by part (a), and the lesson shows that every gradient field has $N_x = M_y$. A check with numbers at a point outside that half-plane, $(-1, 2)$: both formulas give $(4 - 1)/(1 + 4)^2 = 3/25$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

Use the results of parts (a) to (d) to answer two questions, each with its reason. Is $\vec F$ conservative over its whole domain, the plane without the origin? Is it conservative over the right half-plane $x > 0$? A field is conservative (path-independent) in a region when its line integral between two points is the same along every curve in that region that joins them.

<Hint>

For the whole domain: do you already have two curves with the same start and the same end? For the right half-plane: what does part (b) say about any two curves from $A$ to $B$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-e1" label="Is $\vec F$ conservative over its whole domain, the plane without the origin?"
  choices={["Yes, because its curl is zero at every point where it is defined", "Yes, because it is the gradient of the function θ of part (a) at every point where it is defined", "No, because the two half circles of part (c) join the same two points and give different values", "No, because its curl is not zero at the points of the y-axis"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p2-e2" label="Is $\vec F$ conservative over the right half-plane $x > 0$?"
  choices={["No, because its line integral around the whole unit circle is not zero", "No, because it is not defined at the origin", "Yes, because a field with zero curl is conservative in every region", "Yes, because there it is the gradient of the function θ of part (a)"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One pair of curves with the same end points and different line integrals is enough to show that a field is not conservative. A potential that works in the whole region is enough to show that it is.

<Step title="The whole domain: not conservative">

The half circles $C_1$ and $C_2$ of part (c) both run from $(1, 0)$ to $(-1, 0)$, and both stay inside the domain of $\vec F$. But the line integrals are $\pi$ and $-\pi$. So the line integral depends on the path, and $\vec F$ is not conservative over its whole domain.

The same fact as a closed curve: going along $C_1$ and then back along $C_2$ reversed is one counterclockwise trip around the unit circle, and

<Formula label="Once around the unit circle" tex="\oint \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_{C_1} \vec F \cdot d\vec r - \int_{C_2} \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \pi - (-\pi) = 2\pi \neq 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(e) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Why zero curl does not help here">

Part (d) found $N_x = M_y$ on the whole domain. The lesson's test turns this into "gradient field" only under a condition:

<Formula label="The condition in the test for gradient fields" tex="N_x = M_y \ \text{and}\ \vec F \text{ defined and differentiable at every point of the plane} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec F \text{ is a gradient field}" cite="reading2" at="Lecture 21, p. 1" />

This field is not defined at the origin, so the test does not apply. The problem shows that the condition cannot be dropped. MIT's summary of the next lecture makes the same point with this very field: around the unit circle its line integral is not zero, although its curl is zero wherever it is defined (Lecture 22, p. 2 of the Week 9 summaries).

</Step>

<Step title="The right half-plane: conservative">

In the region $x > 0$ the field is the gradient of the function $\theta = \tan^{-1}(y/x)$, which is defined and differentiable in that whole region. By part (b), the line integral along any curve in the region from $A$ to $B$ is $\theta_2 - \theta_1$: it depends on the end points only. So $\vec F$ is conservative over the right half-plane. The closed curve that gave $2\pi$ is no objection, because the unit circle does not lie in the right half-plane.

</Step>

<Step title="MIT's closing note, in our words">

MIT ends the problem with a note (p. 2 of the sheet). In fact $\vec F = \nabla\theta$ at every point of the domain, if $\theta$ means the polar angle. But the polar angle cannot be made into one single-valued differentiable function on the whole plane without the origin: follow it once around the origin and it comes back $2\pi$ larger, so it is fixed only up to multiples of $2\pi$. That is the $2\pi$ of the closed curve above. Parts (a) and (b) use the right half-plane for this reason. Any other region where the polar angle can be chosen continuously, with one value at each point, would do as well. The upper half-plane $y > 0$ is one (our own example).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Radial fields with a power of the distance" points={4} mit="Part B, Problem 3">

Let $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ be the distance to the origin, let $n$ be a constant, and consider the field

<Formula label="The field of Problem 3" tex="\vec F = r^n\left(x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j\right)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3, p. 2" />

At every point other than the origin it points straight away from the origin, never sideways. For a negative $n$ the formula has no meaning at the origin, and the domain of $\vec F$ is the plane without the origin.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Calculate the curl of $\vec F$. MIT's advice: start by finding formulas for the partial derivatives $r_x$ and $r_y$.

In the two formula boxes, type `r` for the distance $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, so that your formulas stay short. They expect the letters `x`, `y`, `r` and `n`. Write every power of the distance with `r`, not with `x^2 + y^2` or `sqrt(x^2 + y^2)`: the boxes treat `r` as a letter of its own.

<Hint>

Differentiate both sides of $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$ with respect to $x$, keeping $y$ constant. That is quicker than differentiating the square root.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The components are $M = r^n x$ and $N = r^n y$. In $N_x$ the factor $y$ is a constant, and $r^n$ depends on $x$ through $r$: use the chain rule with $r_x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a-curl" label="The curl of $\vec F$ is"
  choices={["zero only when n = 0", "zero at every point where the field is defined, for every n", "2 n x y rⁿ⁻² at every point where the field is defined", "(n + 2) rⁿ at every point where the field is defined"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The field depends on $x$ and $y$ partly through $r$. Find $r_x$ and $r_y$ once, then use the chain rule in $N_x$ and $M_y$.

<Step title="The derivatives of r">

Differentiate $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$ with respect to $x$: $2r\,r_x = 2x$. In the same way with respect to $y$: $2r\,r_y = 2y$. So

<Formula label="The partial derivatives of the distance" tex="r_x = \frac{x}{r}, \qquad r_y = \frac{y}{r}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two partial derivatives of the field">

With $N = r^n y$ and $M = r^n x$:

<Formula label="The derivative of N with respect to x" tex="N_x = n\,r^{n-1}\,r_x \cdot y = n\,r^{n-1}\cdot\frac{x}{r}\cdot y = n\,x\,y\,r^{n-2}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="The derivative of M with respect to y" tex="M_y = n\,r^{n-1}\,r_y \cdot x = n\,r^{n-1}\cdot\frac{y}{r}\cdot x = n\,x\,y\,r^{n-2}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The curl">

<Formula label="The curl of the radial field" tex="\operatorname{curl}\vec F = N_x - M_y = n\,x\,y\,r^{n-2} - n\,x\,y\,r^{n-2} = 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

This holds for every $n$, at every point other than the origin.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $n = 2$, where no square root is left: $\vec F = (x^2 + y^2)(x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j)$, so $N = x^2 y + y^3$ and $M = x^3 + x y^2$. Directly, $N_x = 2xy$ and $M_y = 2xy$. The formula gives $n\,x\,y\,r^{n-2} = 2xy\,r^0 = 2xy$ as well.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Whenever possible, find a potential $g$ such that $\vec F = \nabla g$. MIT's advice: look for a potential that depends on $x$ and $y$ only through the distance, $g = g(r)$, and watch out for one negative value of $n$ for which the general formula does not work.

Give your potentials without an added constant: the general one with no constant term, and the one for the special value of $n$ so that it is zero at $r = 1$. The boxes for $g$ expect the letters `r` and `n`. For the natural logarithm type `ln(r)` or `log(r)`.

<Hint>

By the chain rule, $g_x = g'(r)\,r_x$ and $g_y = g'(r)\,r_y$, with $r_x$ and $r_y$ from part (a). Compare with the components $r^n x$ and $r^n y$ to find what $g'(r)$ must be.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You now need an antiderivative of a power of $r$. For which power does the power rule for antiderivatives fail, and what is the antiderivative then?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b-g" label="For all but one value of $n$: $g(r) =$" expr="r^(n+2)/(n+2)"
  vars={{ r: [0.3, 1.1], n: [-0.5, 3] }}

<Solution>

**The idea.** Try $g = g(r)$. Its gradient is $g'(r)$ times the gradient of $r$, which part (a) already found. Matching it with $\vec F$ gives an ordinary differential equation for $g(r)$ that one integration solves.

<Step title="The gradient of a function of r">

With $r_x = x/r$ and $r_y = y/r$, the chain rule gives

<Formula label="The gradient of g(r)" tex="\nabla g = g'(r)\,r_x\,\hat i + g'(r)\,r_y\,\hat j = \frac{g'(r)}{r}\left(x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j\right)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

This is to equal $r^n\left(x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j\right)$. So $g'(r)/r = r^n$:

<Formula label="The condition on g" tex="g'(r) = r^{n+1}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The general case">

When $n + 1 \neq -1$, the power rule gives

<Formula label="The potential when n is not −2" tex="g(r) = \frac{r^{n+2}}{n+2} \;(+\,c), \qquad n \neq -2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The special case">

When $n = -2$, the formula would divide by zero. Then $g'(r) = r^{-1} = 1/r$, whose antiderivative is the natural logarithm:

<Formula label="The potential when n is −2" tex="g(r) = \ln r \;(+\,c), \qquad n = -2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

So a potential exists for every $n$: "whenever possible" turns out to be always. For $n > -2$ the potential $r^{n+2}/(n+2)$ has a limit at the origin. For $n \le -2$ the potential ($r^{n+2}/(n+2)$, or $\ln r$ when $n = -2$) tends to $-\infty$ at the origin.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $n = 0$ the field is $x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j$ and the formula gives $g = r^2/2 = (x^2 + y^2)/2$, whose gradient is indeed $x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j$. For $n = -2$: $g = \ln r = \tfrac12\ln(x^2 + y^2)$, with $g_x = x/(x^2 + y^2) = r^{-2}x$, as it should be.

A second route is the lesson's method 1, a line integral, here along a ray from the point at distance $1$ to the point at distance $r$. On a ray the field points along the path and has size $r^n \cdot r = r^{n+1}$, so the work is $\int_1^r \rho^{n+1}\,d\rho = \left(r^{n+2} - 1\right)/(n+2)$: the same potential, with the constant chosen so that it is zero at $r = 1$. In this form the special case is the limit of the general one: for $r = 2$ and $n = -1.999$ it gives $0.6934$, close to $\ln 2 = 0.6931$.

</Step>

<Step title="Compare with Problem 2">

For a negative $n$ this field, like the field of Problem 2, has zero curl on a domain with a hole at the origin. The test for gradient fields promises nothing there, and Problem 2 shows that a potential can fail to exist. Here it does exist, because we found it. One sign of the difference: around a circle centred at the origin the field of Problem 2 is tangent to the circle and does the work $2\pi$, while this field is perpendicular to the circle and does no work.

One case is worth knowing (our remark): for $n = -3$ the field has size $1/r^2$ and the potential is $g = -1/r$. That is, in size, the inverse-square pattern of gravity and of the electric force, restricted to a plane and written with the mathematician's sign $\vec F = \nabla g$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
