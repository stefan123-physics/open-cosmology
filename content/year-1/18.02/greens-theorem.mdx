When the [curl](/courses/18-02/gradient-fields-and-curl) is not zero, the work around a closed curve can still often be found without parametrising the curve: by Green's theorem (transcript, p. 1).

## The theorem

Let $C$ be a closed curve enclosing a region $R$, traversed **counterclockwise**, and let $\vec F$ be defined and differentiable not only on $C$ but everywhere in $R$. Then (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Green's theorem" tex="\oint_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int\int_R \operatorname{curl}\vec F\,dA, \qquad \oint_C M\,dx + N\,dy = \int\int_R (N_x - M_y)\,dA" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

A strange statement: the left side lives only on the curve, where $x$ and $y$ are tied to one parameter; the right side lives on the whole region, where $x$ and $y$ vary independently between bounds (p. 1). The counterclockwise orientation matches the convention $N_x - M_y$ for the curl; for a clockwise curve, take the negative. And it is **only for closed curves**: an open curve must be parametrised, or first closed up with another piece (p. 1).

## An example: a hard line integral made easy

Let $C$ be the unit circle centred at $(2, 0)$, counterclockwise, and compute (transcript, pp. 1–2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Using Green's theorem" tex="\oint_C ye^{-x}\,dx + \left(\tfrac12 x^2 - e^{-x}\right)dy = \int\int_R \big((x + e^{-x}) - e^{-x}\big)\,dA = \int\int_R x\,dA" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Parametrising by $x = 2 + \cos\theta$, $y = \sin\theta$ would lead to integrals of $e^{-x}$ terms that look hopeless (p. 1). The double integral needs no bounds: $\int\int_R x\,dA$ is the area times $\bar x$, the $x$-coordinate of the [centre of mass](/courses/18-02/polar-double-integrals), and for this disc of area $\pi$ centred at $x = 2$ that gives $2\pi$ (pp. 1–2; notes, p. 2).

## The test for gradient fields, proved

If $\operatorname{curl}\vec F = 0$ and $\vec F$ is defined everywhere in the plane, then for any counterclockwise closed curve, $\oint_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int\int_R 0\,dA = 0$: $\vec F$ is conservative. (A clockwise curve, or a figure eight, is split into loops oriented counterclockwise.) This justifies last lecture's criterion (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Zero curl gives a gradient field" tex="N_x = M_y \text{ everywhere in the plane} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \oint_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = 0 \text{ for every closed } C" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 22, p. 2" />

The condition matters: the problem-set field undefined at the origin has zero curl wherever it is defined, yet gives $2\pi$ around the unit circle. Green's theorem cannot be applied to a curve around the origin, since the curl is not defined there (p. 2; notes, p. 2).

## Why the theorem is true

**First simplification.** It is enough to prove (transcript, pp. 2–3; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The two halves" tex="\oint_C M\,dx = -\int\int_R M_y\,dA \qquad\text{and}\qquad \oint_C N\,dy = \int\int_R N_x\,dA" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 22, p. 2" />

and add them. The second is the first with $x$ and $y$ swapped (using horizontally simple pieces), so only the first needs proof (p. 3).

**Second simplification.** If the theorem holds for two regions $R_1$ and $R_2$, it holds for their union: the curves $C_1$ and $C_2$ both run along the shared edge, in opposite directions, so those parts cancel (p. 3; notes, p. 2). Any region can be cut into **vertically simple** pieces, $a \le x \le b$ and $f_1(x) \le y \le f_2(x)$ (p. 3).

**The main step**, for one vertically simple region. Its boundary has four parts: the bottom $y = f_1(x)$, the right side $x = b$, the top $y = f_2(x)$ (run from $b$ back to $a$), the left side $x = a$. On the vertical sides $dx = 0$, so they contribute nothing (p. 3; notes, pp. 2–3; the notes call the bottom and top curves $f_0$ and $f_1$):

<Formula label="Line integral side" tex="\oint_C M\,dx = \int_a^b M\big(x, f_1(x)\big)\,dx - \int_a^b M\big(x, f_2(x)\big)\,dx" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

For the double integral, set it up $dy\,dx$; the inner integral of $M_y$ in $y$ gives $M$ back (p. 3; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Double integral side" tex="-\int\int_R M_y\,dA = -\int_a^b\int_{f_1(x)}^{f_2(x)} M_y\,dy\,dx = -\int_a^b \Big(M\big(x, f_2(x)\big) - M\big(x, f_1(x)\big)\Big)dx" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

The two agree. Gluing the pieces and adding the $N$ part completes the proof (p. 3).

## The planimeter

Taking $\vec F = \langle 0, x\rangle$, so $N_x - M_y = 1$ (transcript, p. 4; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Area as a line integral" tex="\oint_C x\,dy = \int\int_R 1\,dA = \text{area}(R)" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

Before computers, experimental scientists measured areas under curves drawn on graph paper with a **planimeter**: one moves an arm around the curve, a mechanism of wheels computes a line integral such as $\oint x\,dy$, and when the arm returns to its start a dial shows the area, although the device never visits the inside (p. 4; notes, p. 3).

<WorkedExample title="Green's theorem twice">
<Problem>
(a) Compute $\oint_C -y^3\,dx + x^3\,dy$ around the unit circle, counterclockwise, by Green's theorem, and check it directly. (b) Use $\oint x\,dy$ to find the area of the ellipse $x = 3\cos t$, $y = 2\sin t$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Green's theorem">
$N_x - M_y = 3x^2 + 3y^2 = 3r^2$. In polar coordinates, $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^1 3r^2\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 3 \cdot \frac14 \cdot 2\pi = \frac{3\pi}{2} \approx 4.71$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Directly">
With $x = \cos\theta$, $y = \sin\theta$: $-y^3\,dx + x^3\,dy = (\sin^4\theta + \cos^4\theta)\,d\theta$, and $\int_0^{2\pi} (\sin^4\theta + \cos^4\theta)\,d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \frac34 = \frac{3\pi}{2}$. The same.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) An area by a line integral">
$\oint x\,dy = \int_0^{2\pi} 3\cos t \cdot 2\cos t\,dt = 6\pi \approx 18.85$: the area $\pi ab$ with $a = 3$, $b = 2$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A family of theorems">
Green's theorem is the planar member of a family that relates what happens on a boundary to what happens inside. Its three-dimensional relative for flux, Gauss's (divergence) theorem, underlies Gauss's law for gravity: the flux of the gravitational field out of any closed surface measures the mass inside. Guth applies that law to closed surfaces in a uniform universe to show that it cannot be static; see [why a static universe cannot work](/courses/8-286/newtons-static-universe).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Green's theorem: $\oint_C M\,dx + N\,dy = \int\int_R (N_x - M_y)\,dA$ for $C$ counterclockwise around $R$, with $\vec F$ defined and differentiable on all of $R$; only for closed curves.
- It can turn a hard line integral into an easy double integral (example: $\int\int_R x\,dA = 2\pi$ for the unit disc at $(2, 0)$).
- It proves that zero curl everywhere in the plane means conservative; a point where $\vec F$ is undefined breaks this.
- Proof: $\oint M\,dx = -\int\int M_y\,dA$ on vertically simple regions, gluing regions, and the same for $N$.
- $\oint_C x\,dy$ is the area inside $C$, the principle of the planimeter.
