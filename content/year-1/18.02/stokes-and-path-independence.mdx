[Stokes' theorem](/courses/18-02/stokes-theorem) turns the work around a closed curve into the flux of the curl through a surface bounded by it. This lecture uses it to explain the test for gradient fields, looks at which surfaces are allowed, and shows why the choice of surface does not matter (transcript, p. 1).

## Simply connected regions

A region in space is **simply connected** if every closed loop inside it bounds some surface that also lies inside it (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 3).

- **Space without the origin is simply connected.** A loop that avoids the origin bounds some surface; if that surface passes through the origin, push it slightly aside (p. 1; notes, p. 3). Auroux calls this a trick question. (By contrast, the [plane without the origin](/courses/18-02/simply-connected-regions) is not simply connected; in space there is room to go around the hole.)
- **Space without the $z$-axis is not.** The unit circle in the $xy$-plane bounds no surface that misses the $z$-axis: any surface ending on the circle must cross the axis somewhere, since the axis goes on forever (p. 1; notes, p. 3). A doughnut around the axis does not help: either it has a second edge, or it closes up and then the circle is no longer an edge at all (p. 1).

## Zero curl means conservative

A gradient field has zero curl, because mixed partial derivatives are equal. The converse is the theorem (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 3): if $\vec F$ is defined on a simply connected region and $\nabla \times \vec F = \vec 0$ there, then $\vec F$ is a gradient field, its line integrals are path independent, and it is conservative.

**Proof.** Take two curves $C_1$ and $C_2$ with the same endpoints. Then $C = C_1 - C_2$ (along $C_1$, back along $C_2$) is a closed curve, so it bounds a surface $S$ in the region, and by Stokes (pp. 1–2; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Path independence from Stokes" tex="\int_{C_1} \vec F \cdot d\vec r - \int_{C_2} \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \oint_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int\int_S (\nabla \times \vec F) \cdot \hat n\,dS = 0" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 32, p. 3" />

The surface $S$ exists only because the region is simply connected. For a field defined only off the $z$-axis, paths on the two sides of the axis can give different values (p. 2). The planar example from the problem sets, $\langle -y, x\rangle/(x^2 + y^2)$, extended to space with no $z$ component, is such a field (p. 1); the worked example below computes it.

## Loops on surfaces

The same idea classifies shapes, one of the founding results of topology in the 19th century (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 3). The surface of a sphere is simply connected: every closed curve on it bounds a piece of the sphere. The surface of a doughnut (a **torus**) is not: a loop around the tube, or around the hole, bounds nothing that lies on the torus. It has two independent such loops, and counting them for surfaces with more holes is the start of the classification of surfaces (p. 2). Auroux adds that this is not an 18.02 topic.

## Orientability

Stokes' theorem can use any surface bounded by $C$, **provided it has a normal vector** that can be chosen consistently (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 3). A curve that winds twice around the $z$-axis bounds a **Möbius strip**: a band glued with a half twist, which has only one side. Following an upward normal around the strip brings it back pointing down, so no orientation exists and flux through it cannot be defined (p. 2; notes, p. 3). The strip is **non-orientable**. The same curve also bounds a two-sided surface (a hemisphere with a twisted slit cut into it), and that is the one to use for Stokes (p. 2; notes, p. 3).

## Why the surface does not matter

If $C$ bounds two surfaces $S_1$ and $S_2$, Stokes says the flux of $\nabla \times \vec F$ is the same through both. Why? Reverse the orientation of $S_2$: then $S = S_1 - S_2$ is a closed surface, with normal pointing outwards, enclosing a region $D$ (transcript, pp. 2–3; notes, p. 3). By the [divergence theorem](/courses/18-02/divergence-theorem):

<Formula label="The difference of the two fluxes" tex="\int\int_{S_1} (\nabla \times \vec F) \cdot \hat n\,dS - \int\int_{S_2} (\nabla \times \vec F) \cdot \hat n\,dS = \int\int\int_D \nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \vec F)\,dV" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

and the integrand is always zero. With $\vec F = \langle P, Q, R\rangle$ (p. 3; notes, pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="div curl = 0" tex="\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \vec F) = (R_y - Q_z)_x + (P_z - R_x)_y + (Q_x - P_y)_z = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

since the six terms cancel in pairs: $R_{yx}$ with $R_{xy}$, $Q_{zx}$ with $Q_{xz}$, $P_{zy}$ with $P_{yz}$ (p. 3). It matches a fact about real vectors: $\vec u \cdot (\vec u \times \vec v) = 0$, because $\vec u \times \vec v$ is perpendicular to $\vec u$ (p. 3; notes, p. 4). Del is not really a vector, but here it behaves like one.

## Three kinds of integrals

The lecture ends with a review of the unit (transcript, pp. 3–5; notes, p. 4). There are three kinds of integral in space, each evaluated in its own way:

1. **Triple integrals** $\int\int\int_D f\,dV$, in rectangular, cylindrical or spherical coordinates, with $dV = \rho^2 \sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta$ in spherical ones. The work is in the bounds: for $dz$ first, the bottom and top surfaces of the solid, then its shadow in the $xy$-plane (p. 3; notes, p. 4).
2. **Flux integrals** $\int\int_S \vec F \cdot \hat n\,dS$, from a formula for $\hat n\,dS$: for a plane parallel to the $yz$-plane, $\hat n = \pm\hat i$ and $dS = dy\,dz$; for a sphere of radius $a$, $dS = a^2 \sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta$; for a cylinder, $dS = a\,dz\,d\theta$; for a graph $z = f(x, y)$, $\hat n\,dS = \pm\langle -f_x, -f_y, 1\rangle\,dx\,dy$; and for a surface with a known normal vector $\vec N$ (p. 4; notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Flux element from a normal vector" tex="\hat n\,dS = \pm \frac{\vec N}{\vec N \cdot \hat k}\,dx\,dy" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 32, p. 4" />

   A surface integral has two integral signs and two variables; a $dx\,dy\,dz$ in it means something has gone wrong (p. 4).
3. **Line integrals** $\int_C P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz$, by parametrising $C$ (p. 4; notes, p. 4).

Bridges between them: the divergence theorem (closed surface to solid), Stokes' theorem (closed curve to surface), and the fundamental theorem for line integrals, $f(P_1) - f(P_0) = \int_C \nabla f \cdot d\vec r$, which links a line integral to no integral at all (pp. 4–5; notes, p. 4). Each relates a quantity with $n$ integral signs to one with $n + 1$, but once the div or curl is computed, the integral is evaluated as any other (pp. 4–5). One more topic for review: for a field with zero curl, [finding a potential](/courses/18-02/line-integrals-in-space), by antiderivatives or by a line integral (p. 5; notes, p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="A curl-free field that is not conservative">
<Problem>
Let $\vec F = \dfrac{\langle -y, x, 0\rangle}{x^2 + y^2}$, defined in space without the $z$-axis. (a) Show that $\nabla \times \vec F = \vec 0$. (b) Compute the work around the unit circle in the $xy$-plane, counterclockwise. (c) Check $\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \vec G) = 0$ for $\vec G = \langle xy, yz, zx\rangle$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The curl">
$R = 0$ and $P$, $Q$ do not depend on $z$, so the first two components are $0$. The third is $Q_x - P_y = \dfrac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} - \dfrac{y^2 - x^2}{(x^2 + y^2)^2} = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The work">
On the circle $\vec F = \langle -\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0\rangle$ is the unit tangent, so the work is the length, $\int_0^{2\pi} 1\,d\theta = 2\pi$, not zero. There is no contradiction: the region is not simply connected, and every surface bounded by the circle crosses the $z$-axis, where $\vec F$ is not defined.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) div curl">
$\nabla \times \vec G = \langle 0 - y,\ 0 - z,\ 0 - x\rangle = \langle -y, -z, -x\rangle$, and its divergence is $0 + 0 + 0 = 0$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="Curl-free and divergence-free">
Two identities now sit side by side: the curl of a gradient is zero, and the divergence of a curl is zero. On simply connected regions the first has a converse (zero curl gives a potential); the second has one too on suitable regions, such as all of space (zero divergence gives a "vector potential" $\vec A$ with $\vec F = \nabla \times \vec A$). Not on every region: on space without the origin, $\hat r/r^2$ has zero divergence, but its flux out of a sphere around the origin is $4\pi$, not 0, so it is not a curl. In magnetism, $\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0$ is why the magnetic field is written as such a curl, and it is what makes the flux of $\vec B$ through a loop independent of the surface (Lecture 33).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Simply connected: every loop bounds a surface in the region. Space without the origin is; space without the $z$-axis is not.
- On a simply connected region, $\nabla \times \vec F = \vec 0$ implies $\vec F$ is conservative, by Stokes on a surface bounded by $C_1 - C_2$.
- Stokes needs an orientable (two-sided) surface; the Möbius strip is not one.
- $\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \vec F) = 0$, so by the divergence theorem the flux of a curl depends only on the boundary curve.
- Three kinds of integrals (triple, flux, line), bridged by the divergence theorem, Stokes' theorem and the fundamental theorem for line integrals.
