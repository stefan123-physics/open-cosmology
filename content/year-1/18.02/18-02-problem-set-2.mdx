## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The distance between two lines in space" points={6} mit="Part B, Problem 1">

Four points in space are given:

<Formula label="The four points" tex="A = (2, 4, 0), \qquad B = (3, 1, 1), \qquad C = (1, 1, 3), \qquad D = (0, 5, 1)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1, p. 1" />

Write $(AB)$ for the whole line through $A$ and $B$, and $(CD)$ for the whole line through $C$ and $D$. The distance between the two lines is the distance between the two points, one on each line, that are closest to each other. Find it by the two methods of parts (a) and (b).

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find two planes that are both parallel to both lines, the first one containing the line $(AB)$ and the second one containing the line $(CD)$. Then find the distance between these two planes.

For the first two boxes, write both equations in the form $2x + by + cz = d$, with the number $2$ in front of $x$. Give the distance as a decimal, to three decimal places.

<Hint>

Two parallel planes have the same normal vector. This normal vector must be perpendicular to the direction of each line. Which operation makes a vector perpendicular to two given vectors?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The components of the normal vector are the numbers in front of $x$, $y$ and $z$ in the equation of each plane. You find $d$ by putting in one point of the line that the plane must contain.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the distance, go from a point of the first plane to a point of the second plane. Only the part of that step along the normal direction counts: use the dot product with a unit normal vector.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-d1" label="$d$ of the plane that contains $(AB)$:" value="8" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-d1" />

<Answer id="p1-a-d2" label="$d$ of the plane that contains $(CD)$:" value="6" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-d2" />

<Answer id="p1-a-distance" label="Distance between the planes:" value="0.816" tolerance="0.002" calc="p1-a-distance" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A plane is fixed by a normal vector and one point. Both planes must be parallel to both lines, so their common normal vector is perpendicular to both directions: take the cross product of the two directions.

<Step title="The directions of the lines">

<Formula label="Direction vectors of the two lines" tex="\vec{AB} = \langle 3 - 2,\ 1 - 4,\ 1 - 0\rangle = \langle 1, -3, 1\rangle, \qquad \vec{CD} = \langle 0 - 1,\ 5 - 1,\ 1 - 3\rangle = \langle -1, 4, -2\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The normal vector">

<Formula label="Normal vector of both planes" tex="\vec N = \vec{AB} \times \vec{CD} = \big\langle (-3)(-2) - (1)(4),\ -\big((1)(-2) - (1)(-1)\big),\ (1)(4) - (-3)(-1)\big\rangle = \langle 2, 1, 1\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

It is perpendicular to both directions: $\vec N \cdot \vec{AB} = 2 - 3 + 1 = 0$ and $\vec N \cdot \vec{CD} = -2 + 4 - 2 = 0$. By the lesson, a vector whose dot product with the normal vector is zero is parallel to the plane. So every plane $2x + y + z = d$ is parallel to both lines.

</Step>

<Step title="The two planes">

The first plane must contain $A = (2, 4, 0)$, so $d = 2 \cdot 2 + 4 + 0 = 8$. The second must contain $C = (1, 1, 3)$, so $d = 2 \cdot 1 + 1 + 3 = 6$:

<Formula label="The two parallel planes" tex="2x + y + z = 8 \quad\text{(contains the line through A and B)}, \qquad 2x + y + z = 6 \quad\text{(contains the line through C and D)}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

The first plane contains $A$ and is parallel to the line $(AB)$, so it contains that whole line. As a test, $B = (3, 1, 1)$ gives $6 + 1 + 1 = 8$. In the same way $D = (0, 5, 1)$ gives $0 + 5 + 1 = 6$.

</Step>

<Step title="The distance between the planes">

Go from $A$ in the first plane to $C$ in the second: $\vec{AC} = \langle -1, -3, 3\rangle$. The distance between the planes is the size of the component of this step along the unit normal vector $\vec N / |\vec N|$, with $|\vec N| = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 1} = \sqrt 6$:

<Formula label="Distance between the two planes" tex="\text{distance} = \frac{|\vec{AC} \cdot \vec N|}{|\vec N|} = \frac{|-2 - 3 + 3|}{\sqrt 6} = \frac{2}{\sqrt 6} = 0.816" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Any other pair of points gives the same number, because moving inside a plane does not change the dot product with $\vec N$: from $B$ to $D$ the step is $\langle -3, 4, 0\rangle$, and $\langle -3, 4, 0\rangle \cdot \vec N = -6 + 4 + 0 = -2$ again. The $2$ on top is also the difference $8 - 6$ of the two values of $d$: for two parallel planes written with the same normal vector, the distance is the difference of the two $d$ values divided by $|\vec N|$.

Why is this the distance between the lines? No point of one plane is closer than $0.816$ to a point of the other, so no two points of the lines are closer than that. Part (b) finds two points of the lines that are exactly this far apart.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find parametric equations of the lines $(AB)$ and $(CD)$. Then find the two times at which the segment from a point $P_1$ on $(AB)$ to a point $P_2$ on $(CD)$ is perpendicular to both lines. The length of that segment is the distance between the lines.

MIT's warning: $P_1$ and $P_2$ can be chosen independently of each other, so each line needs its own parameter. Call them $t_1$ and $t_2$.

So that the boxes have one answer, use the form of the lesson: $P_1$ is at $A$ when $t_1 = 0$ and at $B$ when $t_1 = 1$, and $P_2$ is at $C$ when $t_2 = 0$ and at $D$ when $t_2 = 1$. Give $t_2$ and the length as decimals, to three decimal places.

<Hint>

The lesson's line through $Q_0$ and $Q_1$ is $Q(t) = Q_0 + t\,\vec{Q_0Q_1}$. Write $P_1$ with $A$ and $\vec{AB}$, and $P_2$ with $C$ and $\vec{CD}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the vector $\vec{P_1P_2}$: its components contain both $t_1$ and $t_2$. Perpendicular to both lines means two dot products are zero. That gives two equations for two unknowns.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b-t1" label="$t_1 =$" value="-4" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-b-t1" />

<Answer id="p1-b-t2" label="$t_2 =$ (3 decimal places)" value="3.667" tolerance="0.002" calc="p1-b-t2" />

<Answer id="p1-b-distance" label="Length of the segment:" value="0.816" tolerance="0.002" calc="p1-b-distance" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A point on each line, each with its own parameter. The vector between them must have dot product zero with both directions: two linear equations for $t_1$ and $t_2$.

<Step title="Parametric equations">

With the directions of part (a), $\vec{AB} = \langle 1, -3, 1\rangle$ and $\vec{CD} = \langle -1, 4, -2\rangle$:

<Formula label="The two lines" tex="P_1 = A + t_1\,\vec{AB} = (2 + t_1,\ 4 - 3t_1,\ t_1), \qquad P_2 = C + t_2\,\vec{CD} = (1 - t_2,\ 1 + 4t_2,\ 3 - 2t_2)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The segment">

<Formula label="The vector from one moving point to the other" tex="\vec{P_1P_2} = \langle -1 - t_1 - t_2,\ -3 + 3t_1 + 4t_2,\ 3 - t_1 - 2t_2\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Two perpendicularity conditions">

The dot product with $\vec{AB} = \langle 1, -3, 1\rangle$ is $(-1 - t_1 - t_2) - 3(-3 + 3t_1 + 4t_2) + (3 - t_1 - 2t_2)$, and the dot product with $\vec{CD} = \langle -1, 4, -2\rangle$ is $-(-1 - t_1 - t_2) + 4(-3 + 3t_1 + 4t_2) - 2(3 - t_1 - 2t_2)$. Collecting terms:

<Formula label="The two conditions" tex="11 - 11t_1 - 15t_2 = 0, \qquad -17 + 15t_1 + 21t_2 = 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve the system">

This is a 2 × 2 system, $11t_1 + 15t_2 = 11$ and $15t_1 + 21t_2 = 17$. Its determinant is $11 \cdot 21 - 15 \cdot 15 = 6$, which is not zero, so there is one solution. Multiply the first equation by $21$ and the second by $15$ and subtract: $6t_1 = 231 - 255 = -24$. Multiply the first by $15$ and the second by $11$ and subtract: $6t_2 = 187 - 165 = 22$.

<Formula label="The two times" tex="t_1 = -4, \qquad t_2 = \frac{11}{3} = 3.667" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The closest points and their distance">

<Formula label="The closest points" tex="P_1 = (-2,\ 16,\ -4), \qquad P_2 = \left(-\tfrac83,\ \tfrac{47}{3},\ -\tfrac{13}{3}\right), \qquad \vec{P_1P_2} = \left\langle -\tfrac23,\ -\tfrac13,\ -\tfrac13\right\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Distance between the two lines" tex="|\vec{P_1P_2}| = \sqrt{\tfrac49 + \tfrac19 + \tfrac19} = \frac{\sqrt 6}{3} = 0.816" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two methods agree: $\sqrt 6 / 3 = 2/\sqrt 6$. The segment is $\vec{P_1P_2} = -\tfrac13\langle 2, 1, 1\rangle$, a multiple of the normal vector $\vec N$ of part (a), as it must be: a vector perpendicular to both lines is perpendicular to both planes. The point $P_1$ lies in the first plane, $2(-2) + 16 - 4 = 8$, and $P_2$ in the second, $-\tfrac{16}{3} + \tfrac{47}{3} - \tfrac{13}{3} = 6$.

The closest points are not between the given points: $t_1 = -4$ is before $A$ and $t_2 = 3.667$ is beyond $D$. That is fine, because the lines go on in both directions. With one parameter for both lines the problem would go wrong: the two conditions would become $11 - 26t = 0$ and $-17 + 36t = 0$, which no single $t$ satisfies.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Two gears and an elastic band" points={6} mit="Part B, Problem 2">

A machine has two circular gears in the $xy$-plane. The large gear has radius $2$ and its centre is at $(0, -2)$. The small gear has radius $1$ and its centre is at $(0, 1)$. The two gears touch at the origin. The large gear turns clockwise with angular velocity $1$: one radian per second. The small gear turns counterclockwise, and the gears do not slip at the point where they touch.

Each gear carries a small peg on its rim. An elastic band connects the two pegs. At time $t = 0$ both pegs are at the origin. Time $t$ is in seconds.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Write parametric equations $x(t)$, $y(t)$ for the motion of the midpoint $P$ of the elastic band. MIT's advice: use vectors. First write the position vector of $P$ as a combination of simpler vectors, then write each of those as a function of $t$.

<Hint>

Call the pegs $Q_1$ (on the large gear) and $Q_2$ (on the small gear). The position vector of the midpoint is half the sum of the position vectors of the two pegs. And the position vector of a peg is the vector to the centre of its gear plus the vector from the centre to the peg, as for the cycloid in the lesson.

</Hint>

<Hint>

No slipping means that at the point of contact the two rims move equally fast. A point on a rim of radius $r$ that turns with angular velocity $\omega$ has speed $r\omega$. How fast must the small gear turn?

</Hint>

<Hint>

At $t = 0$ the vector from the centre of the large gear to its peg points straight up, and the vector from the centre of the small gear to its peg points straight down. Turn the first one clockwise through the angle $t$, and the second one counterclockwise through the angle that the small gear has turned.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Break the position vector of $P$ into simple pieces, as the lesson does for the cycloid: to each peg through the centre of its gear, and then halfway between the pegs.

<Step title="Split the position vector">

Call the peg on the large gear $Q_1$ and the peg on the small gear $Q_2$, and the centres of the gears $C_1 = (0, -2)$ and $C_2 = (0, 1)$. (These names are ours.) With $O$ the origin:

<Formula label="The position vector of the midpoint" tex="\vec{OP} = \tfrac12\left(\vec{OQ_1} + \vec{OQ_2}\right), \qquad \vec{OQ_1} = \vec{OC_1} + \vec{C_1Q_1}, \qquad \vec{OQ_2} = \vec{OC_2} + \vec{C_2Q_2}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

The vectors to the centres do not change: $\vec{OC_1} = \langle 0, -2\rangle$ and $\vec{OC_2} = \langle 0, 1\rangle$.

</Step>

<Step title="How fast the small gear turns">

A point on the rim of the large gear moves with speed $2 \times 1 = 2$ (radius times angular velocity). Without slipping, the rim of the small gear moves equally fast where the gears touch. Its radius is $1$, so its angular velocity is $2$ radians per second: after $t$ seconds the small gear has turned through the angle $2t$.

</Step>

<Step title="The peg on the large gear">

At $t = 0$ the peg is at the origin, straight above the centre $(0, -2)$: the vector $\vec{C_1Q_1}$ has length $2$ and makes the angle $\pi/2$ with the positive $x$-axis. Turning clockwise makes this angle smaller, so at time $t$ it is $\pi/2 - t$:

<Formula label="From the centre of the large gear to its peg" tex="\vec{C_1Q_1} = \left\langle 2\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - t\right),\ 2\sin\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - t\right)\right\rangle = \langle 2\sin t,\ 2\cos t\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The peg on the small gear">

At $t = 0$ this peg is at the origin too, straight below the centre $(0, 1)$: the vector $\vec{C_2Q_2}$ has length $1$ and makes the angle $-\pi/2$ with the positive $x$-axis. Turning counterclockwise makes the angle larger, so at time $t$ it is $2t - \pi/2$:

<Formula label="From the centre of the small gear to its peg" tex="\vec{C_2Q_2} = \left\langle \cos\left(2t - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right),\ \sin\left(2t - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right)\right\rangle = \langle \sin 2t,\ -\cos 2t\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Add up">

The pegs are at $Q_1 = (2\sin t,\ -2 + 2\cos t)$ and $Q_2 = (\sin 2t,\ 1 - \cos 2t)$. Half their sum is

<Formula label="Parametric equations of the midpoint" tex="x(t) = \sin t + \tfrac12\sin 2t, \qquad y(t) = \cos t - \tfrac12\cos 2t - \tfrac12" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$: $x = 0$ and $y = 1 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0$, the origin, where both pegs start. At $t = \pi$ the large gear has made half a turn, so its peg is at its lowest point $(0, -4)$. The small gear has made a whole turn, so its peg is back at the origin. The midpoint should be $(0, -2)$, and the formulas give $x = 0 + 0 = 0$ and $y = -1 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = -2$. After $2\pi$ seconds both pegs are back at the origin and the motion repeats.

The graph shows the whole path (our addition). The midpoint leaves the origin to the right, swings down through $(0, -2)$ and comes back to the origin from the left.

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Write the velocity of the point $P$ as a function of $t$. Then calculate the velocity and the acceleration at $t = 0$.

The first two boxes ask for the two components $v_x(t)$ and $v_y(t)$ of the velocity. The last two ask for one component of each vector at $t = 0$. Find the other components as well.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Velocity and acceleration are the first and second derivatives of the position vector, taken component by component.

<Step title="The velocity">

The lesson's definition is

<Formula label="Velocity, component by component" tex="\vec v = \frac{d\vec r}{dt} = \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt},\ \frac{dy}{dt}\right\rangle" cite="reading2" at="Lecture 6, p. 1" />

With $x = \sin t + \tfrac12\sin 2t$ and $y = \cos t - \tfrac12\cos 2t - \tfrac12$, and the chain rule for the terms with $2t$:

<Formula label="Velocity of the midpoint" tex="\vec v(t) = \langle \cos t + \cos 2t,\ -\sin t + \sin 2t\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The acceleration">

Differentiate once more:

<Formula label="Acceleration of the midpoint" tex="\vec a(t) = \frac{d\vec v}{dt} = \langle -\sin t - 2\sin 2t,\ -\cos t + 2\cos 2t\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="At the start">

Put in $t = 0$, where every sine is $0$ and every cosine is $1$:

<Formula label="Velocity and acceleration at the start" tex="\vec v(0) = \langle 1 + 1,\ 0\rangle = \langle 2, 0\rangle, \qquad \vec a(0) = \langle 0,\ -1 + 2\rangle = \langle 0, 1\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

The midpoint starts off to the right with speed $2$, and its acceleration points straight up, with size $1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ both pegs are at the point of contact, where both rims move to the right with speed $2$. The midpoint of two points that have the same velocity $\langle 2, 0\rangle$ has that velocity too.

The acceleration can be checked with a fact from mechanics that is not part of this lesson: a point on a circle of radius $r$ that turns steadily with angular velocity $\omega$ has an acceleration of size $r\omega^2$, pointing to the centre. For the peg on the large gear that is $2 \times 1^2 = 2$, pointing down to $(0, -2)$: $\langle 0, -2\rangle$. For the peg on the small gear it is $1 \times 2^2 = 4$, pointing up to $(0, 1)$: $\langle 0, 4\rangle$. The midpoint has the average of the two, $\langle 0, 1\rangle$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
