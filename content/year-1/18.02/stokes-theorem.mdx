The [curl](/courses/18-02/line-integrals-in-space) measures the rotation in a flow: its direction is the axis, its length twice the angular velocity. This lecture shows its second role: as in the plane, it turns work around a closed curve into a surface integral (transcript, pp. 1–2).

## Curl again

A constant field has curl zero, and so does a stretching field such as $x\,\hat i$, whose stretching is measured by the divergence (1 here) instead. The rotation $\langle -y, x, 0\rangle$ about the $z$-axis has curl $\langle 0, 0, 2\rangle$ (transcript, pp. 1–2). In a river flowing towards the sea, the curl shows where the eddies are (p. 2). The curl decides whether a field is conservative (on a simply connected region); and, as in the plane with [Green's theorem](/courses/18-02/greens-theorem), it converts line integrals into double integrals (p. 2).

## The theorem

If $C$ is a closed curve in space and $S$ **any** surface bounded by $C$, then (transcript, pp. 2–3; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Stokes' theorem" tex="\oint_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int\int_S (\nabla \times \vec F) \cdot \hat n\,dS" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 31, p. 2" />

It is the space version of Green's theorem for work, as the [divergence theorem](/courses/18-02/divergence-theorem) is of Green for flux (notes, p. 2). For the unit circle in the $xy$-plane, $S$ could be the disc, a hemisphere, a pointed cone: the flux of $\nabla \times \vec F$ is the same through each (p. 3; notes, p. 2). The theorem can also be used backwards, to turn a surface integral into a line integral (p. 3). $\vec F$ must be defined and differentiable everywhere on $S$; what happens off $S$ does not matter (pp. 9–10). So if $\vec F$ is undefined at the origin, use the hemisphere rather than the disc for the unit circle; if it is undefined on the whole $z$-axis, every surface bounded by the circle crosses the axis and Stokes' theorem cannot be applied directly (pp. 9–10).

## Orientation

Reversing $C$ changes the sign of the work, and flipping $\hat n$ the sign of the flux, so the two must be **compatible** (transcript, pp. 3–4; notes, p. 2). The rule: if you walk along $C$ in its direction with $S$ on your **left**, then $\hat n$ points **up**, towards your head (pp. 4–5; notes, p. 2). Equivalently, a right-hand rule: thumb along $C$, index finger pointing into $S$, then the middle finger points along $\hat n$ (pp. 5–6; notes, p. 2). These conventions are no more arbitrary than the choice of sign in the curl, and both come from preferring right-handed coordinate systems (p. 9).

Some cases (pp. 4–7): if $C$ is the unit circle traversed clockwise (seen from above) and $S$ the disc, $\hat n$ points down. For a cone with $C$ as its rim, $\hat n$ points up and into the cone: deforming the disc into the cone keeps the normal on the same side. A cylinder (without top or bottom) has two boundary curves; with $\hat n$ pointing outwards, the bottom circle runs counterclockwise and the top one clockwise, seen from above, and the work is the sum over both.

## Stokes contains Green

If $C$ and $S$ lie in the $xy$-plane, with $C$ counterclockwise, then $\hat n = \hat k$ and $dS = dx\,dy$, and $dz = 0$ on $C$ (transcript, pp. 7–8; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Green's theorem as a special case" tex="\oint_C P\,dx + Q\,dy = \int\int_S (\nabla \times \vec F) \cdot \hat k\,dx\,dy = \int\int_S (Q_x - P_y)\,dx\,dy" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

which is Green's theorem (with $P$, $Q$ in place of $M$, $N$) (pp. 8–9).

## Why it is true

The idea of the proof (transcript, pp. 10–11; notes, pp. 2–3):

1. For $C$ and $S$ in the $xy$-plane it is Green's theorem, already proved.
2. In any other plane it reduces to Green's theorem in coordinates adapted to that plane: work, flux and curl can be defined geometrically, without reference to the coordinate system, so they look the same in any rotated right-handed coordinates (pp. 10–11; notes, pp. 2–3).
3. A general surface is cut into tiny, nearly flat pieces, like the panels of a ball. On each piece the work around its edge is about the flux of the curl through it. Adding up: the fluxes add to the flux through $S$, and the line integrals along inner edges are each done twice in opposite directions, so they cancel, leaving the work around $C$ (pp. 11–12; notes, p. 3). Auroux admits this argument cheats a little; it is an outline, not a full proof (p. 12).

## An example

Check the theorem for $\vec F = \langle z, x, y\rangle$, with $C$ the unit circle in the $xy$-plane, counterclockwise, and $S$ the piece of the paraboloid $z = 1 - x^2 - y^2$ above the plane (transcript, pp. 12–13; notes, p. 3).

**Directly:** on $C$, $x = \cos\theta$, $y = \sin\theta$, $z = 0$, so only $x\,dy$ survives (p. 12; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="The work around the circle" tex="\oint_C z\,dx + x\,dy + y\,dz = \oint_C x\,dy = \int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2\theta\,d\theta = \pi" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 31, p. 3" />

**By Stokes:** $\nabla \times \vec F = \langle 1, 1, 1\rangle$, and for the graph $z = f(x, y) = 1 - x^2 - y^2$, $\hat n\,dS = \langle -f_x, -f_y, 1\rangle\,dx\,dy = \langle 2x, 2y, 1\rangle\,dx\,dy$, pointing up (p. 13; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="The flux of the curl" tex="\int\int_S (\nabla \times \vec F) \cdot \hat n\,dS = \int\int_{\text{unit disc}} (2x + 2y + 1)\,dx\,dy = \int\int_{\text{unit disc}} 1\,dx\,dy = \pi" cite="reading2" at="p. 13" />

since $\int\int x\,dx\,dy = \int\int y\,dx\,dy = 0$ over the disc by symmetry (p. 13; notes, p. 3).

<WorkedExample title="One curve, two surfaces">
<Problem>
For $\vec F = \langle -y, x, z\rangle$ and $C$ the unit circle in the $xy$-plane, counterclockwise, compute the work directly, then the flux of the curl through the unit disc and through the upper unit hemisphere.
</Problem>
<Step title="Directly">
On $C$, $\vec F = \langle -\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0\rangle$ is tangent with length 1, so the work is the circumference, $2\pi$.
</Step>
<Step title="Through the disc">
$\nabla \times \vec F = \langle 0 - 0, 0 - 0, 1 - (-1)\rangle = \langle 0, 0, 2\rangle$. Through the disc with $\hat n = \hat k$: $2 \times \pi = 2\pi$.
</Step>
<Step title="Through the hemisphere">
On the upper hemisphere $\hat n = \langle x, y, z\rangle$, so $(\nabla \times \vec F) \cdot \hat n = 2z = 2\cos\varphi$, and the flux is $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2} 2\cos\varphi\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta = 2\pi$. The same, as Stokes' theorem promises.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="Circulation">
The work of a velocity field around a closed curve is called its **circulation**. Stokes' theorem says the circulation around a loop is the total curl (vorticity) passing through any surface spanning it: a loop in a river has circulation when more rotation of one sense than the other passes through it. Eddies spinning opposite ways can cancel, and a shear flow (faster at the surface than at the bottom) has curl without any visible eddy.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Stokes' theorem: $\oint_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int\int_S (\nabla \times \vec F) \cdot \hat n\,dS$ for any surface $S$ bounded by $C$, with $\vec F$ differentiable on $S$.
- Orientation: walking along $C$ with $S$ on your left, $\hat n$ points up (right-hand rule: thumb, index, middle finger).
- In the $xy$-plane it is Green's theorem. In other planes, by invariance under rotations; for curved surfaces, by cutting into flat pieces whose inner edges cancel.
- Example: $\langle z, x, y\rangle$ around the unit circle gives $\pi$, directly or through the paraboloid.
