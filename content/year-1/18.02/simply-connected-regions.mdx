Before reviewing for the exam, Auroux explains exactly when a field with zero curl is conservative, and why the field had to be defined everywhere (transcript, p. 1). (The rest of the lecture is the exam review, which has no page here.)

## When Green's theorem fails

Both forms of [Green's theorem](/courses/18-02/greens-theorem), for work and for [flux](/courses/18-02/flux), compare a line integral around a closed curve with a double integral over the region inside. The line integral only needs the field on the curve; the double integral needs it, and its derivatives, everywhere inside (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1).

The example is the problem-set field (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The field with a hole" tex="\vec F = \frac{-y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j}{x^2 + y^2}, \qquad \operatorname{curl}\vec F = 0 \text{ where defined}, \qquad \oint_{\text{unit circle}} \vec F \cdot d\vec r = 2\pi" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 24, p. 1" />

It is undefined at the origin. For a closed curve that does not enclose the origin, Green's theorem applies and the work is 0. For one that does, it cannot be applied, and pretending the curl is zero everywhere gives the wrong answer, 0 instead of $2\pi$ (p. 1).

## Green's theorem for a region with a hole

Take two curves around the origin, an outer $C'$ and an inner $C''$, both counterclockwise. Green's theorem still applies to the region **between** them, where the field is defined (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Region between two curves" tex="\oint_{C'} \vec F \cdot d\vec r - \oint_{C''} \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int\int_{\text{between}} \operatorname{curl}\vec F\,dA = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Why this is not a new theorem: cut a thin slit joining the two curves. Going counterclockwise around the region now means the outer curve counterclockwise, along the slit, the inner curve **clockwise**, and back along the slit. The two passes along the slit cancel, and the ordinary theorem applies (p. 1; notes, p. 1). So the two line integrals are equal: the work is $2\pi$ around **every** counterclockwise curve going once around the origin (p. 1). In general, for a region with several boundary curves, the outer boundary goes counterclockwise and the inner ones clockwise, or with a minus sign; the same works for flux and divergence (p. 1).

## Simply connected regions

A **connected** region is one piece. A connected region $R$ is **simply connected** if the inside of every closed curve in $R$ also lies in $R$: it has no holes (transcript, pp. 1–2; notes, p. 1). A disc is simply connected; a ring (annulus), or the plane with the origin removed, is not: a curve around the hole lies in the region, but its inside does not (p. 2).

If a field is defined and differentiable on a simply connected region, Green's theorem can always be applied to closed curves in it: wherever the field is defined on a curve, it is defined inside (p. 2; notes, p. 1). The domain of the field above, the plane minus the origin, is not simply connected, which is exactly why its line integral makes sense but Green's theorem does not apply. The best form of the test for gradient fields is therefore (p. 2):

<Formula label="The sharp test" tex="\operatorname{curl}\vec F = 0 \text{ on a simply connected domain} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec F \text{ is conservative, a gradient field}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

This is the starting point of **topology**, the branch of mathematics that studies the shapes of regions, such as whether they are simply connected, and Auroux's own field (p. 2).

<WorkedExample title="Work around different curves">
<Problem>
For $\vec F = (-y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j)/(x^2 + y^2)$, find the work (a) around the ellipse $x = 2\cos t$, $y = \sin t$, counterclockwise, and (b) around the circle of radius 3 centred at $(5, 0)$, counterclockwise. (c) Check (a) by direct computation.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) By the region between two curves">
The ellipse encloses the origin. The region between it and a small circle about the origin contains no point where $\vec F$ is undefined, and the curl is zero there, so the work equals that around the small circle: $2\pi$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A curve that misses the origin">
This circle lies between $x = 2$ and $x = 8$: the origin is outside it. Green's theorem applies inside, the curl is 0, so the work is 0.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Directly">
$-y\,dx + x\,dy = (\sin t \cdot 2\sin t + 2\cos t\cos t)\,dt = 2\,dt$ and $x^2 + y^2 = 4\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t$, so the work is $\int_0^{2\pi} \frac{2\,dt}{4\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t} = 2\pi$, as predicted.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="Holes and multi-valued potentials">
Why does a hole matter? (This remark is ours, not the lecture's.) Away from the origin, $\vec F$ is the gradient of the polar angle $\theta$. But $\theta$ cannot be defined consistently all around the origin: going once around increases it by $2\pi$, which is exactly the work found. On a simply connected region, such a mismatch cannot happen.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Green's theorem needs the field defined on the whole region inside the curve.
- $(-y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j)/(x^2 + y^2)$ has zero curl where defined, but its work around the unit circle is $2\pi$: the origin is a hole.
- For a region with holes, take the outer boundary counterclockwise and the inner ones clockwise; the work is $2\pi$ around every counterclockwise curve enclosing the origin.
- A region is simply connected if the inside of every closed curve in it lies in it.
- Zero curl on a simply connected domain implies conservative.
