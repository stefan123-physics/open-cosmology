The [last lecture](/courses/18-02/critical-points-and-least-squares) found critical points, where $f_x = f_y = 0$. This one decides what kind each is, a local minimum, a local maximum or a saddle, using second derivatives (transcript, p. 1).

## Local and global

On a contour plot, a maximum or minimum shows as level curves shrinking to a point (only the values tell which), and a saddle as a level curve crossing itself in a figure eight (transcript, p. 1). Two further questions (p. 1; notes, p. 2):

- To find the **global** maximum, compare the values at all the local maxima.
- The global minimum or maximum need not be at a critical point at all: it may be on the **boundary** of the domain, or **at infinity**. Both must be checked.

## The quadratic case

Start with a function that is just quadratic, $w = ax^2 + bxy + cy^2$, at its critical point, the origin (p. 1; notes, p. 2). Last time $x^2 - 2xy + 3y^2 = (x - y)^2 + 2y^2$, a sum of squares, showed a minimum (p. 1). In general, if $a \ne 0$, **complete the square** (pp. 1–2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Completing the square" tex="w = a\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}y\right)^2 + \left(c - \frac{b^2}{4a}\right)y^2 = \frac{1}{4a}\left(4a^2\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}y\right)^2 + (4ac - b^2)\,y^2\right)" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

Inside the brackets, the first term is never negative. Three cases (p. 2; notes, p. 2):

- $4ac - b^2 < 0$: a square minus a square, so $w$ takes both signs: a **saddle**.
- $4ac - b^2 > 0$: a sum of two squares, never negative, so $w$ has the sign of $a$: a **minimum** if $a > 0$, a **maximum** if $a < 0$.
- $4ac - b^2 = 0$: one square only, $w = a(x + \frac{b}{2a}y)^2$. There is a direction in which nothing happens, like the flat-bottomed valley $w = x^2$, with critical points all along a line: a **degenerate** critical point (p. 2).

The quantity $4ac - b^2$ is the discriminant of the quadratic formula with its sign changed, and there is a second way to see the cases, for $y \ne 0$ (p. 3; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Through the discriminant" tex="w = y^2\left(a\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)^2 + b\,\frac{x}{y} + c\right)" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

If $b^2 - 4ac > 0$, the quadratic $at^2 + bt + c$ has two roots and takes both signs, so $w$ does too: a saddle. If $b^2 - 4ac < 0$, it has no roots and keeps the sign of $a$, and so does $w$: a minimum or maximum (p. 3; notes, p. 2).

## The second derivative test

A function of two variables has second derivatives $f_{xx} = \partial^2 f/\partial x^2$, $f_{xy}$ (first $x$, then $y$), $f_{yx}$ and $f_{yy}$. The mixed ones are always equal, $f_{xy} = f_{yx}$, a fact the lecture admits without proof (p. 3; notes, p. 2). At a critical point $(x_0, y_0)$ let (p. 3; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The three numbers" tex="A = f_{xx}(x_0, y_0), \qquad B = f_{xy}(x_0, y_0), \qquad C = f_{yy}(x_0, y_0)" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 10, p. 2" />

Then (p. 3; notes, pp. 2–3):

- $AC - B^2 > 0$ and $A > 0$ (or equally $C > 0$): **local minimum**;
- $AC - B^2 > 0$ and $A < 0$: **local maximum**;
- $AC - B^2 < 0$: **saddle point**;
- $AC - B^2 = 0$: **no conclusion**.

**Check on the quadratic case.** For $w = ax^2 + bxy + cy^2$: $w_x = 2ax + by$, $w_{xx} = 2a$, $w_{xy} = b$; $w_y = bx + 2cy$, $w_{yx} = b$ (equal, as promised) and $w_{yy} = 2c$. So $A = 2a$, $B = b$, $C = 2c$, and $AC - B^2 = 4ac - b^2$: the same cases (p. 3; notes, p. 3).

**Why it works in general.** The **quadratic approximation** (Taylor's formula to second order) is (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Quadratic approximation" tex="\Delta f \approx f_x\,(x - x_0) + f_y\,(y - y_0) + \tfrac12 f_{xx}\,(x - x_0)^2 + f_{xy}\,(x - x_0)(y - y_0) + \tfrac12 f_{yy}\,(y - y_0)^2" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–4" />

(No $\frac12$ on the middle term: it collects the equal $xy$ and $yx$ terms, p. 4.) At a critical point the linear terms vanish, leaving a quadratic function with $a = A/2$, $b = B$, $c = C/2$, and the type of the critical point is the same as for that quadratic (p. 4; notes, p. 3). In the degenerate case the approximation is not good enough: the ignored cubic and higher terms, however small, can bend the flat direction up or down and turn the point into a minimum or a saddle, so higher derivatives would be needed (p. 4; notes, p. 3). (In one variable, $x^5$ at 0 has $f'' = 0$ too.) If it happens, the lecture suggests inspecting the function or plotting it (p. 4).

## An example with a boundary

The lecture's example is $f(x, y) = x + y + \frac{1}{xy}$ for $x > 0$, $y > 0$ (p. 4; notes, p. 3). Critical points: $f_x = 1 - \frac{1}{x^2y} = 0$ and $f_y = 1 - \frac{1}{xy^2} = 0$, so $x^2y = 1$ and $xy^2 = 1$. Dividing gives $x = y$, then $y^3 = 1$: the only critical point is $(1, 1)$ (p. 4). Second derivatives (p. 4; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Second derivatives at (1, 1)" tex="f_{xx} = \frac{2}{x^3y},\quad f_{xy} = \frac{1}{x^2y^2},\quad f_{yy} = \frac{2}{xy^3}; \qquad A = 2,\ B = 1,\ C = 2,\ \ AC - B^2 = 3 > 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

With $A > 0$, it is a local minimum, in fact the global one, $f(1, 1) = 3$. The maximum is not at a critical point: $f \to \infty$ as $x$ or $y$ goes to infinity, and also as $x$ or $y$ goes to 0, so there is no maximum (p. 4; notes, p. 3).

<WorkedExample title="Two critical points of different types">
<Problem>
Find and classify the critical points of $f(x, y) = x^3 - 3x + y^2$. Does $f$ have a global minimum?
</Problem>
<Step title="Critical points">
$f_x = 3x^2 - 3 = 0$ gives $x = \pm 1$, and $f_y = 2y = 0$ gives $y = 0$: the critical points are $(1, 0)$ and $(-1, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step title="The test">
$f_{xx} = 6x$, $f_{xy} = 0$, $f_{yy} = 2$. At $(1, 0)$: $A = 6$, $B = 0$, $C = 2$, $AC - B^2 = 12 > 0$ with $A > 0$: a local minimum, $f(1, 0) = 1 - 3 = -2$. At $(-1, 0)$: $A = -6$, so $AC - B^2 = -12 < 0$: a saddle, with $f(-1, 0) = -1 + 3 = 2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Global behaviour">
Along the $x$-axis, $f(x, 0) = x^3 - 3x \to -\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$, so there is no global minimum: the local minimum at $(1, 0)$ is only local. The lowest values are "at infinity".
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A local minimum that is not the lowest">
Guth's original version of inflation (published in 1981) used exactly this distinction. The universe was supposed to sit in a **false vacuum**: a local minimum of the potential energy of a field, stable to small changes but not the global minimum, so it could only escape by tunnelling. That version did not work, and "new inflation" (1982) uses a flat plateau instead; see [the false vacuum](/courses/8-286/false-vacuum).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Global minima and maxima may be at critical points, on the boundary, or at infinity: compare values and check all three.
- $w = ax^2 + bxy + cy^2$: completing the square shows a minimum ($4ac - b^2 > 0$, $a > 0$), maximum ($4ac - b^2 > 0$, $a < 0$), saddle ($4ac - b^2 < 0$) or degenerate point ($4ac - b^2 = 0$).
- Second derivative test: with $A = f_{xx}$, $B = f_{xy}$, $C = f_{yy}$ at the critical point, the same cases with $AC - B^2$ in place of $4ac - b^2$; $AC - B^2 = 0$ is inconclusive. $f_{xy} = f_{yx}$.
- It works because of the quadratic approximation, whose linear terms vanish at a critical point.
