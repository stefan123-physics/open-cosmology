The [last lecture](/courses/18-02/parametric-equations) described motions by their position as a function of time. This one studies how the position changes: velocity, speed, acceleration and arc length, and ends with a law of planetary motion that vectors make almost obvious (transcript, pp. 1, 3).

## Velocity and speed

The **position vector** $\vec r(t) = \langle x(t), y(t), z(t)\rangle$ is the vector $\vec{OP}$ from the origin to the moving point (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1). For the cycloid of a wheel of radius 1 rolling at unit speed, the angle equals the time, and $\vec r(t) = \langle t - \sin t, 1 - \cos t\rangle$ (p. 1; notes, p. 1).

Speed is a number; but how fast *and* in which direction is a vector, the **velocity**. A vector is differentiated component by component (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Velocity" tex="\vec v = \frac{d\vec r}{dt} = \left\langle \frac{dx}{dt}, \frac{dy}{dt}, \frac{dz}{dt}\right\rangle" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

For the cycloid, $\vec v = \langle 1 - \cos t, \sin t\rangle$. At $t = 0$ it is zero: at that instant the point on the rim is not moving at all, although it moves just before and just after (p. 1; notes, p. 1). The **speed** is the length of the velocity (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Speed of the cycloid" tex="|\vec v| = \sqrt{(1 - \cos t)^2 + \sin^2 t} = \sqrt{2 - 2\cos t}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

It is 0 at $t = 0$ and largest at $t = \pi$, the top of the arch, where it is $\sqrt 4 = 2$: twice the wheel's speed, because the wheel's forward motion and the rim's rotation, each of unit speed, add up there, while at the bottom they cancel (p. 1; notes, p. 1).

## Acceleration

In everyday language acceleration is a change of speed. Here it is the derivative of the velocity **vector** (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Acceleration" tex="\vec a = \frac{d\vec v}{dt}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

A car taking a tight curve at constant speed still accelerates sideways: the velocity keeps its length but turns. This is the $a$ of $F = ma$. Only for straight-line motion do the two notions agree (p. 1). For the cycloid, $\vec a = \langle \sin t, \cos t\rangle$; at $t = 0$ it is $\langle 0, 1\rangle$, straight up. So the point comes down, slows, stops, and is pushed back up: another way to see the vertical tangent at the bottom (pp. 1–2; notes, p. 1).

A warning: the speed is $|d\vec r/dt|$, which is **not** $d|\vec r|/dt$ (p. 2; notes, p. 1). There is no simple formula for the derivative of a length, and fortunately it is rarely needed (p. 2).

## Arc length and the unit tangent vector

The **arc length** $s$ is the distance travelled along the trajectory, counted from a chosen reference point, like a car's mileage counter (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1). Its rate of change is the speed (p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Arc length and speed" tex="\frac{ds}{dt} = |\vec v|" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

(Strictly, with an absolute value on the left, for motions that go back and forth along the curve, p. 2.) The length of a trajectory is the integral of the speed. For one arch of the cycloid that is $\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{2 - 2\cos t}\,dt$, which the lecture leaves unevaluated at this stage, promising a cleverer method later (p. 2; notes, p. 1). (The half-angle identity $1 - \cos t = 2\sin^2(t/2)$ does it: on $[0, 2\pi]$ the sine of $t/2$ is not negative, so the integrand is $2\sin(t/2)$, with antiderivative $-4\cos(t/2)$, which rises from $-4$ to $4$: the arch has length 8 times the radius.)

The velocity is tangent to the trajectory, so dividing it by its length gives the **unit tangent vector** $\hat T = \vec v/|\vec v|$ (p. 2; notes, p. 1). By the chain rule (p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Velocity as direction times speed" tex="\vec v = \frac{d\vec r}{dt} = \frac{d\vec r}{ds}\,\frac{ds}{dt} = \hat T\,\frac{ds}{dt}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

The velocity's direction is the tangent, its length the speed. Why is $d\vec r/ds = \hat T$? Over a short time $\Delta t$ the position changes by $\Delta\vec r \approx \hat T\,\Delta s$: nearly tangent, with length nearly the distance $\Delta s$ along the curve (the chord is slightly shorter than the arc and not exactly tangent). Dividing by $\Delta t$ and letting $\Delta t \to 0$ makes it exact (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 1).

## Kepler's second law

In 1609 Kepler stated, from observations, that a planet moves **in a plane**, and that the line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out **area at a constant rate**: closer to the Sun the planet moves faster (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1). Newton explained it with his law of gravitation late in the 17th century (p. 3). With vectors it becomes nearly obvious (p. 3).

Put the Sun at the origin. In a short time $\Delta t$ the planet moves by $\Delta\vec r \approx \vec v\,\Delta t$, and the swept area is nearly a triangle with sides $\vec r$ and $\vec v\,\Delta t$: half a parallelogram, given by a cross product (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Area swept in a short time" tex="\text{area} \approx \tfrac12\,|\vec r \times \Delta\vec r| \approx \tfrac12\,|\vec r \times \vec v|\,\Delta t" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 6, p. 1" />

A constant rate of sweeping means $|\vec r \times \vec v|$ is constant. And $\vec r$ and $\vec v$ both lie in the plane of motion, so $\vec r \times \vec v$ is normal to it; a fixed plane means a fixed direction. Both halves of the law together (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Kepler's second law in vector form" tex="\vec r \times \vec v = \text{constant vector}" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

To use it, differentiate. The product rule works for dot and cross products, as long as the factors of a cross product keep their order (p. 4; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Differentiating r × v" tex="\frac{d}{dt}(\vec r \times \vec v) = \frac{d\vec r}{dt} \times \vec v + \vec r \times \frac{d\vec v}{dt} = \vec v \times \vec v + \vec r \times \vec a = \vec r \times \vec a" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

since $\vec v \times \vec v = 0$. So Kepler's law holds exactly when $\vec r \times \vec a = 0$, that is when the acceleration is **parallel to the position vector**: the force on the planet points along the line to the Sun, a **central force** (p. 4; notes, p. 1). The law does not say whether towards or away from the Sun; it holds for any central force, for example a charged particle moving around a fixed point charge (p. 4; notes, p. 1). (8.01 says the same in terms of [angular momentum](/courses/8-01/angular-momentum-point-particle): $\vec r \times m\vec v$ is conserved when the torque is zero.)

<WorkedExample title="Circular motion, and the cycloid at a quarter turn">
<Problem>
(a) A point moves on the circle $\vec r(t) = \langle 2\cos 3t, 2\sin 3t\rangle$. Find its velocity, speed and acceleration, and check Kepler's law. (b) For the cycloid $\vec r(t) = \langle t - \sin t, 1 - \cos t\rangle$, find $\vec v$, the speed, $\hat T$ and $\vec a$ at $t = \pi/2$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Velocity and speed">
$\vec v = \langle -6\sin 3t, 6\cos 3t\rangle$, with speed $\sqrt{36\sin^2 3t + 36\cos^2 3t} = 6$, constant. Yet $|\vec r| = 2$ is constant too, so $d|\vec r|/dt = 0$ while the speed is 6: the two are different things.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Acceleration and Kepler">
$\vec a = \langle -18\cos 3t, -18\sin 3t\rangle = -9\,\vec r$: not zero though the speed is constant, and parallel to $\vec r$, pointing to the centre. So $\vec r \times \vec a = 0$, and indeed $\vec r \times \vec v = \langle 0, 0,\ 2\cos 3t \cdot 6\cos 3t - 2\sin 3t \cdot (-6\sin 3t)\rangle = \langle 0, 0, 12\rangle$, constant: area is swept at $12/2 = 6$ square units per unit time.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The cycloid at t = π/2">
$\vec v = \langle 1 - \cos(\pi/2), \sin(\pi/2)\rangle = \langle 1, 1\rangle$, speed $\sqrt 2 \approx 1.414$, matching $\sqrt{2 - 2\cos(\pi/2)} = \sqrt 2$. The unit tangent is $\hat T = \langle 1, 1\rangle/\sqrt 2$, at 45° upwards. The acceleration is $\vec a = \langle \sin(\pi/2), \cos(\pi/2)\rangle = \langle 1, 0\rangle$, forwards.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Central forces in the sky">
The same argument applies to anything pulled towards a fixed centre: a star orbiting in the gravity of a spherical galaxy or of the dark matter halo around it also sweeps out equal areas in equal times, whatever the force law. For a star on a circular orbit, the same central pull sets its speed, and that is what astronomers measure. Measuring how fast stars and gas orbit at each distance (rotation curves) is one of the main pieces of evidence for dark matter; see [galaxy rotation curves](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/rotation-curves).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Velocity $\vec v = d\vec r/dt$ and acceleration $\vec a = d\vec v/dt$ are vectors, differentiated component by component; speed $|\vec v|$ is a number.
- On the cycloid, $\vec v = \langle 1 - \cos t, \sin t\rangle$: zero at the bottom, speed 2 at the top; $\vec a = \langle 0, 1\rangle$ at the bottom.
- Acceleration is not the derivative of speed, and $|d\vec r/dt| \ne d|\vec r|/dt$.
- Arc length: $ds/dt = |\vec v|$; unit tangent $\hat T = \vec v/|\vec v|$; $\vec v = \hat T\,ds/dt$.
- Kepler's second law: $\vec r \times \vec v$ is constant, which by the product rule means $\vec r \times \vec a = 0$: the force is central.
