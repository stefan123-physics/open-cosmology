So far the variables of a min/max problem were independent. Often they are not: they are tied by a relation $g(x, y, z) = c$, the **constraint**, and $f$ is to be minimised or maximised only among the points that satisfy it (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 2). If the constraint is simple, solve it for one variable and substitute, and the problem is an ordinary one; when it is too complicated to solve, a new method is needed: **Lagrange multipliers** (p. 1). (Physics example: pressure, volume and temperature of a gas are tied by $PV = NRT$, and it can be handy to keep all three as constrained variables, p. 1.) The critical points of $f$ itself are no help, since they usually do not satisfy the constraint (p. 2).

## An example: closest to the origin

Find the point of the hyperbola $xy = 3$ closest to the origin (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 2). Minimising the distance $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ is the same as minimising its square, so (p. 2):

<Formula label="The problem" tex="\text{minimise } f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 \quad\text{subject to}\quad g(x, y) = xy = 3" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

On the contour plot of $f$ (circles) with the hyperbola on top: a large circle meets the hyperbola in four points; shrinking it, there is a smallest circle that still meets it, and that is the minimum. There the circle is **tangent** to the hyperbola (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 2). So at the minimum the level curve of $f$ is tangent to the level curve $g = 3$: they share a tangent line, so their normal vectors, the gradients, are parallel (p. 3; notes, p. 2).

## The Lagrange equations

Parallel vectors are proportional: there is a number $\lambda$, the **multiplier**, with (transcript, p. 4; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Lagrange condition" tex="\nabla f = \lambda\,\nabla g" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 13, p. 2" />

($\lambda$ may be positive or negative, depending on whether the gradients point the same way, p. 5. It is called a multiplier because it is what $\nabla g$ is multiplied by to give $\nabla f$, p. 9.) This replaces a min/max problem in two constrained variables by a system in three unknowns, $x$, $y$, $\lambda$. Two equations come from the components of the gradients, and the constraint is the third (p. 4; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The system" tex="f_x = \lambda g_x, \qquad f_y = \lambda g_y, \qquad g(x, y) = c" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

With a third variable, add $f_z = \lambda g_z$ (p. 4). For the hyperbola: $2x = \lambda y$, $2y = \lambda x$, $xy = 3$ (p. 5; notes, p. 2).

## Solving the equations

There is no general method: sometimes it is easy, sometimes it needs a computer (transcript, p. 7; notes, p. 3). Here the first two equations form a linear system (p. 7; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="A linear system for x and y" tex="\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -\lambda \\ \lambda & -2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="p. 7" />

The obvious solution $x = y = 0$ fails $xy = 3$, so we need [another solution](/courses/18-02/planes-and-linear-systems), which exists only when the determinant is zero: $-4 + \lambda^2 = 0$, so $\lambda = \pm 2$ (p. 7). For $\lambda = 2$: $x = y$ and $x^2 = 3$, giving $(\sqrt3, \sqrt3)$ and $(-\sqrt3, -\sqrt3)$. For $\lambda = -2$: $x = -y$ and $-x^2 = 3$, no solution (p. 8; notes, p. 3). At both points $f = 6$, and on the picture $\nabla f$ is indeed twice $\nabla g$ (pp. 6, 8).

## Why the method works

At a constrained minimum or maximum, moving along the level set $g = c$ changes $f$ only in one way (only up, or only down), so to first order it does not change. The derivatives are zero, but only in the **allowed directions**, those tangent to $g = c$ (transcript, p. 9; notes, p. 3). For every unit vector $\hat u$ tangent to the level set (pp. 9–10; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="No change along the constraint" tex="\left(\frac{df}{ds}\right)_{\hat u} = \nabla f \cdot \hat u = 0" cite="reading2" at="pp. 9–10" />

So $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to every vector tangent to the level set: it is normal to the level set of $g$. So is $\nabla g$. Hence the two are parallel, which is the same as the level sets of $f$ and $g$ being tangent (p. 10; notes, p. 3).

## Minimum or maximum?

**Warning:** the method does not say whether a solution is a minimum or a maximum, or (in three variables) even a saddle (transcript, pp. 6, 11; notes, p. 3). The second derivative test does not apply, because only the directions along the constraint count (p. 11). What is left is to **compare the values** of $f$ at all solutions, or to argue geometrically (p. 11; notes, p. 3). On the hyperbola the two solutions tie for the minimum, and there is no maximum, since points of the hyperbola go arbitrarily far from the origin (p. 11). (Here one could also substitute $y = 3/x$ and minimise $x^2 + 9/x^2$ directly, but that is not always possible, p. 6.)

## An advanced example: the cheapest pyramid

Build a pyramid on a given triangular base with a given volume, using as little surface area as possible (Auroux's story: a gold-covered building on a triangular plot) (transcript, pp. 11–12; notes, p. 3). Since the volume is $V = \frac13 A_{\text{base}}\,h$, the height $h$ is fixed: only the position of the top vertex is free (p. 12; notes, p. 3). In $x, y$ coordinates the face areas are lengths of cross products, and minimising them that way is hopelessly messy (p. 13; notes, p. 3).

**Better variables, adapted to the geometry.** Let $a_1, a_2, a_3$ be the side lengths of the base and $u_1, u_2, u_3$ the distances from the point below the top vertex to the three sides. Each side face is a triangle with base $a_i$ and, by Pythagoras, height $\sqrt{u_i^2 + h^2}$ (pp. 13–14; notes, p. 3). Cutting the base into three triangles with heights $u_i$ gives the constraint (p. 14; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="The pyramid problem" tex="f = \tfrac12 a_1\sqrt{u_1^2 + h^2} + \tfrac12 a_2\sqrt{u_2^2 + h^2} + \tfrac12 a_3\sqrt{u_3^2 + h^2}, \qquad g = \tfrac12 a_1u_1 + \tfrac12 a_2u_2 + \tfrac12 a_3u_3 = A_{\text{base}}" cite="reading2" at="p. 14" />

The Lagrange equations $\partial f/\partial u_i = \lambda\,\partial g/\partial u_i$ simplify, the factors $\frac12 a_i$ cancelling, to (pp. 14–15; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="The solution" tex="\frac{u_1}{\sqrt{u_1^2 + h^2}} = \frac{u_2}{\sqrt{u_2^2 + h^2}} = \frac{u_3}{\sqrt{u_3^2 + h^2}} = \lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_1 = u_2 = u_3" cite="reading2" at="p. 15" />

The point below the top is equally far from all three sides: the top goes above the **incenter** of the base triangle (p. 15; notes, p. 3).

<WorkedExample title="Closest point on a plane, and a maximum on a circle">
<Problem>
(a) Find the point of the plane $x + 2y + 2z = 9$ closest to the origin. (b) Find the largest and smallest values of $f = xy$ on the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 8$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Three variables">
Minimise $x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ with $g = x + 2y + 2z = 9$: $\langle 2x, 2y, 2z\rangle = \lambda\langle 1, 2, 2\rangle$ gives $x = \lambda/2$, $y = \lambda$, $z = \lambda$. The constraint gives $\lambda/2 + 2\lambda + 2\lambda = 9$, so $\lambda = 2$ and the point is $(1, 2, 2)$, at distance $\sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = 3$. (It lies along the normal vector $\langle 1, 2, 2\rangle$, as it should.)
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Two solutions, two values">
$\langle y, x\rangle = \lambda\langle 2x, 2y\rangle$ gives $y = 2\lambda x$ and $x = 2\lambda y$, so $x = 4\lambda^2 x$; $x = 0$ would force $y = 0$, off the circle, so $\lambda = \pm\frac12$. With $\lambda = \frac12$: $y = x$, $2x^2 = 8$, points $(2, 2)$ and $(-2, -2)$ with $f = 4$. With $\lambda = -\frac12$: $y = -x$, points $(2, -2)$ and $(-2, 2)$ with $f = -4$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Compare values">
The method alone does not say which is which; comparing values, the maximum of $xy$ on the circle is 4 and the minimum is $-4$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Temperature as a multiplier">
Statistical physics uses Lagrange multipliers at its foundation: the equilibrium distribution of energy among many particles is the one that maximises the entropy, subject to the constraint that the total energy is fixed. The multiplier for that constraint turns out to be $1/(kT)$ (when the entropy is counted in units of $k$), which is how temperature enters, and it leads to the thermal distributions behind [black-body radiation](/courses/8-286/black-body-radiation), the spectrum of the cosmic microwave background.
</Callout>

## Summary

- To minimise or maximise $f$ subject to $g = c$: solve $\nabla f = \lambda\nabla g$ together with $g = c$ (three equations in $x$, $y$, $\lambda$ for two variables).
- At a constrained extremum the level sets of $f$ and $g$ are tangent, because $\nabla f \cdot \hat u = 0$ for every direction $\hat u$ along $g = c$.
- Example: on $xy = 3$, the points closest to the origin are $(\pm\sqrt3, \pm\sqrt3)$ (same signs), with $\lambda = 2$ and $f = 6$.
- The method does not tell minimum from maximum, and the second derivative test does not apply: compare values or argue geometrically.
- The least-area pyramid on a given base with given volume has its top above the incenter.
