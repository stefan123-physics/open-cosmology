This set practises one lesson: [non-independent variables](/courses/18-02/non-independent-variables). It also uses the total differential of [differentials and the chain rule](/courses/18-02/differentials-and-chain-rule). It follows MIT's Problem Set 6, in our own words.

MIT's set has two parts. Part B holds MIT's two longer problems (8 points), for which OpenCourseWare has no solutions. Both are written out below, with hints and solutions of our own. Part A is a list of short exercises from MIT's own Supplementary Notes, which also hold their solutions. Those are not rewritten here: the last section of this page lists them and links to MIT's exercises and solutions.

MIT labels each problem with the day from which it can be done. Both problems belong to the lecture on non-independent variables. MIT's sheet calls it Lecture 15, because its calendar counts the day of the first exam as a lecture. On this site, and in Prof. Auroux's lecture summaries, it is Lecture 14.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then choose or type your result in the answer box and press Check. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `2*x*y + cos(x - y)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. A number box wants a decimal number, not a fraction. The points of the two problems, and the split of Problem 2 over its parts, are MIT's. The split of Problem 1 into two parts, and of its points, is ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

A word that both problems use: the **formal derivatives** of a function are its partial derivatives taken as if all its variables were independent, so $f_x$ is the derivative of $f(x, y, z)$ with respect to $x$ with both $y$ and $z$ held fixed. In the notation of the lesson, a subscript on a bracket names what is held fixed: $\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right)_y$ is the rate of change of $w$ when $x$ changes and $y$ is held fixed.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Which derivative makes sense?" points={4} mit="Part B, Problem 1">

Two of the three variables $x$, $y$, $z$ are tied to each other by a relation, and $w$ is a function of all three:

<Formula label="The relation and the function" tex="g(x, y) = c \quad (c \text{ a constant}), \qquad w = f(x, y, z)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1, p. 1" />

MIT asks which of the following three expressions makes sense as the derivative of $w$ with respect to $x$:

<Formula label="Three candidates" tex="\text{(i)}\ \left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right)_x \qquad \text{(ii)}\ \left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right)_y \qquad \text{(iii)}\ \left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right)_z" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1, p. 1" />

If an expression makes sense, compute it in terms of the formal derivatives $f_x$, $f_y$, $f_z$, $g_x$ and $g_y$. If it does not, say why not.

Our addition, so that the answers are definite: assume that neither $g_x$ nor $g_y$ is zero at the points you look at.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

For each of the three expressions, decide whether it makes sense, and why.

<Hint>

Read each expression aloud in full: "the rate of change of $w$ when $x$ changes, while ... is held fixed". Then ask whether the point $(x, y, z)$ can really move in that way without breaking the relation $g(x, y) = c$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Take the differential of the relation: $g_x\,dx + g_y\,dy = 0$. What does it say about $dx$ when $dy = 0$? And what does it say about $dz$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-i" label="(i), with $x$ held fixed:"
  choices={["It makes sense: it is the formal derivative of f with respect to x", "It makes sense: x changes while y follows the relation g(x, y) = c", "It makes no sense: it asks x to change while x is held fixed", "It makes no sense: w does not depend on x"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p1-a-ii" label="(ii), with $y$ held fixed:"
  choices={["It makes sense: it is the formal derivative of f with respect to x", "It makes sense: x changes while z follows from the relation g(x, y) = c", "It makes no sense: with y held fixed, the relation g(x, y) = c holds x fixed too", "It makes no sense: w does not depend on y"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p1-a-iii" label="(iii), with $z$ held fixed:"
  choices={["It makes sense: x changes, z is held fixed, and y follows the relation g(x, y) = c", "It makes sense: it is the formal derivative of f with respect to x, because y stays fixed as well", "It makes no sense: z is not in the relation, so z cannot be held fixed", "It makes no sense: the relation g(x, y) = c holds x fixed"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count the free variables. There are three variables and one relation, so two of them can be chosen freely and the third follows. A derivative with a subscript makes sense when the variable that changes and the variable in the subscript are such a free pair.

<Step title="What the relation allows">

The relation $g(x, y) = c$ does not contain $z$, so $z$ is always free. It ties $x$ and $y$ to each other. Its differential, as in the lesson, says how:

<Formula label="Differential of the relation" tex="dg = g_x\,dx + g_y\,dy = 0" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 14, p. 1 (there for a relation with three variables)" />

Because $g_y \neq 0$, a small change $dx$ fixes $dy = -(g_x/g_y)\,dx$: once $x$ is chosen, $y$ follows. So the free pairs are $x$ and $z$, or $y$ and $z$. The pair $x$ and $y$ is not free.

</Step>

<Step title="(i): x held fixed">

This asks for the rate of change of $w$ when $x$ changes while $x$ is held fixed. That is a contradiction: $dx$ cannot be zero and not zero. It makes no sense, with or without the relation.

</Step>

<Step title="(ii): y held fixed">

Here $x$ should change while $y$ is held fixed, so the expression treats $x$ and $y$ as the free pair. But put $dy = 0$ in the differential of the relation: $g_x\,dx = 0$, and because $g_x \neq 0$ this means $dx = 0$. With $y$ held fixed, the point cannot move in the $x$ direction at all without leaving the curve $g(x, y) = c$. There is a second problem: nothing says what $z$ does, because $z$ is not a function of $x$ and $y$. So (ii) makes no sense either.

</Step>

<Step title="(iii): z held fixed">

Here $x$ changes and $z$ is held fixed: $x$ and $z$ are a free pair, and $y$ does what the relation demands. This one makes sense. It is not the formal derivative $f_x$, because $y$ does not stay fixed. Part (b) computes it.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with a picture">

In the $xy$-plane the relation is a curve, for example a circle. A point $(x, y, z)$ that obeys the relation lies on the upright wall above and below that curve. On this wall you can move in two ways: up and down (change $z$), or along the curve (change $x$, and $y$ with it). Moving in the $x$ direction at constant $y$ leaves the wall, which is what goes wrong in (ii). Moving along the curve at constant height is (iii).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Compute the expression that makes sense, in terms of the formal derivatives.

Type the formal derivatives as `fx`, `fy`, `fz`, `gx` and `gy`.

<Hint>

Write the total differential of $w = f(x, y, z)$. It has three terms.

</Hint>

<Hint>

One of the three differentials is zero, because that variable is held fixed. Another one is not free: the differential of the relation gives it as a multiple of $dx$. What is left is $dw = (\ldots)\,dx$, and the bracket is the derivative.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="The derivative that makes sense $=$" expr="fx - fy*gx/gy"
  vars={{ fx: [1, 2], fy: [1, 2], fz: [1, 2], gx: [1, 2], gy: [3, 4] }}
  names={{ fx: "f_x", fy: "f_y", fz: "f_z", gx: "g_x", gy: "g_y" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "fx", hint: "This is the formal derivative, which holds both y and z fixed. Here only z is held fixed: y must change with x to keep g = c, and that changes w as well." },
    { expr: "fx + fy*gx/gy", hint: "Check the sign of the second term. The relation gives g_x dx + g_y dy = 0, so dy is minus (g_x/g_y) dx." },
    { expr: "fx - fy*gy/gx", hint: "The fraction is upside down. Solve g_x dx + g_y dy = 0 for dy: the derivative g_y goes underneath." },
    { expr: "fx + fy*gy/gx", hint: "Solve g_x dx + g_y dy = 0 for dy again: the derivative g_y goes underneath, and there is a minus sign." },
    { expr: "fx - fy*gx/gy + fz", hint: "The variable z is held fixed, so dz = 0 and the term with f_z drops out." },
    { expr: "-fy*gx/gy", hint: "This is only the change of w that comes through y. A change of x also changes w directly: add the term f_x." },
    { expr: "-gx/gy", hint: "This is dy/dx along the curve g = c. Use it in the differential of w, where it is multiplied by f_y." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The method of total differentials from the lesson: write $dw$, set the differential of the fixed variable to zero, remove the differential of the dependent variable with the relation, and read off the number in front of $dx$.

<Step title="The total differential of w">

<Formula label="Differential of w" tex="dw = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy + f_z\,dz" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 15, p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Use what is fixed and what is tied">

$z$ is held fixed, so $dz = 0$. The relation gives $g_x\,dx + g_y\,dy = 0$, so $dy = -(g_x/g_y)\,dx$. Put both in:

<Formula label="Differential of w along the relation, at fixed z" tex="dw = f_x\,dx + f_y\left(-\frac{g_x}{g_y}\right)dx = \left(f_x - \frac{f_y\,g_x}{g_y}\right)dx" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Read off the derivative">

<Formula label="The derivative that makes sense" tex="\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right)_z = f_x - \frac{f_y\,g_x}{g_y}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

The first term is the direct effect of $x$ on $w$. The second is the effect through $y$, which has to change at the rate $dy/dx = -g_x/g_y$ to stay on the curve $g = c$. The derivative $f_z$ does not appear, because $z$ does not change.

</Step>

<Step title="The same by the chain rule">

The lesson's second method gives the same result. Differentiate $w$ with respect to $x$ at fixed $z$, with $y$ a function of $x$:

<Formula label="By the chain rule" tex="\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}\right)_z = f_x + f_y\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}\right)_z + f_z\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)_z = f_x + f_y\,\frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad 0 = g_x + g_y\,\frac{dy}{dx}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

The second equation is the derivative of $g(x, y) = c$ with respect to $x$. It gives $dy/dx = -g_x/g_y$ again.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Compare with the lecture summary, which does the same for a relation between all three variables, $g(x, y, z) = c$, and the derivative with respect to $z$ at fixed $y$:

<Formula label="The lecture's example of a constrained derivative" tex="\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right)_y = -\frac{f_x\,g_z}{g_x} + f_z" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 15, p. 2" />

(The summary prints $g_z$ for the last term. That is a slip: the term comes from $f_z\,dz$.) Our result has the same shape: a direct term, plus the formal derivative with respect to the dependent variable times minus a ratio of two derivatives of $g$, with the derivative for the dependent variable underneath.

A test with functions of our own choice: $g = x^2 y$ and $f = xy^2 + xz$. The formal derivatives are $f_x = y^2 + z$, $f_y = 2xy$, $g_x = 2xy$ and $g_y = x^2$, so the formula gives $y^2 + z - (2xy)(2xy)/x^2 = z - 3y^2$. Directly: the relation gives $y = c/x^2$, so $w = c^2/x^3 + xz$, and its derivative with respect to $x$ at fixed $z$ is $-3c^2/x^4 + z = z - 3y^2$. The two agree.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A derivative with x held fixed, and the slope of a curve in space" points={4} mit="Part B, Problem 2">

Two short calculations with total differentials. They are not connected to each other.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Three variables $x$, $y$, $t$ are tied by one relation, and $w$ depends on all three:

<Formula label="The relation and the function" tex="t = \sin(x + y), \qquad w = x^3\,y\,t" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a), p. 1" />

Find $\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}\right)_x$.

Write your answer with $x$ and $y$ only: replace any $t$ by $\sin(x + y)$. Our addition: stay where $\cos(x + y)$ is not zero.

<Hint>

With $x$ held fixed and $t$ changing, the third variable $y$ cannot stay fixed. So this is not the formal derivative of $x^3 y t$ with respect to $t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the total differential of $w = x^3 y t$ (three terms) and the total differential of the relation $t = \sin(x + y)$. Then set $dx = 0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With $dx = 0$ the relation gives $dy$ as a multiple of $dt$. Put that into $dw$ and collect what stands in front of $dt$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}\right)_x =$" expr="x^3*y + x^3*tan(x + y)"
  vars={{ x: [0.4, 0.8], y: [0.2, 0.6] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x^3*y", hint: "This is the formal derivative, with both x and y held fixed. But if x and y are both fixed, t = sin(x + y) cannot change. With x fixed, y must change with t." },
    { expr: "x^3*tan(x + y)", hint: "This is only the change of w that comes through y. The function w = x^3 y t also contains t itself: add its formal derivative with respect to t." },
    { expr: "x^3*y + x^3*sin(x + y)*cos(x + y)", hint: "You multiplied by cos(x + y), the rate of change of t with y. Here you need the rate of change of y with t at fixed x, which is 1 over that." },
    { expr: "x^3*y - x^3*tan(x + y)", hint: "Check the sign of the second term. With dx = 0 the relation gives dt = cos(x + y) dy, with no minus sign." },
    { expr: "x^3*y + x^3/cos(x + y)", hint: "The term of dw with dy is x^3 t dy. The factor t = sin(x + y) is missing in your second term." },
    { expr: "x^3*y + x^3*sin(x + y)", hint: "Your second term is the coefficient of dy in dw. But dy is not dt: with dx = 0 the relation gives dt = cos(x + y) dy." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Three variables and one relation leave two free variables. The subscript says which: $t$ changes, $x$ is held fixed, and $y$ follows from the relation. Use total differentials, as in the lesson.

<Step title="Two differentials">

<Formula label="Differential of w and of the relation" tex="dw = 3x^2 y\,t\,dx + x^3 t\,dy + x^3 y\,dt, \qquad dt = \cos(x + y)\,(dx + dy)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Hold x fixed">

Set $dx = 0$. The relation becomes $dt = \cos(x + y)\,dy$, so

<Formula label="How y follows t at fixed x" tex="dy = \frac{dt}{\cos(x + y)}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

This needs $\cos(x + y) \neq 0$. Where the cosine is zero, $t = \pm 1$ is at its largest or smallest value and cannot be changed freely.

</Step>

<Step title="Put it into dw">

<Formula label="Differential of w at fixed x" tex="dw = x^3 t\,\frac{dt}{\cos(x + y)} + x^3 y\,dt = \left(x^3 y + \frac{x^3\,t}{\cos(x + y)}\right)dt" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

The bracket is the derivative. With $t = \sin(x + y)$:

<Formula label="The derivative at fixed x" tex="\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}\right)_x = x^3 y + \frac{x^3\,t}{\cos(x + y)} = x^3 y + x^3\tan(x + y)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

The first term is the formal derivative of $x^3 y t$ with respect to $t$. The second term is there because $y$ must change when $t$ does.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Here the relation can be solved, so substitution (the lesson's "method 0") is a test. For $x + y$ between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$ the relation gives $y = \arcsin t - x$, so $w = x^3 t\,(\arcsin t - x)$, a function of $x$ and $t$ only. Its derivative with respect to $t$ is

<Formula label="By substitution" tex="\left(\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}\right)_x = x^3(\arcsin t - x) + \frac{x^3\,t}{\sqrt{1 - t^2}} = x^3 y + x^3\tan(x + y)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

because $\arcsin t - x = y$ and $\sqrt{1 - t^2} = \cos(x + y)$ in that range. As a number: at $x = 1$, $y = 0$ both forms give $0 + \tan 1 = 1.557$ (angles in radians).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

The points $(x, y, z)$ that satisfy both of these equations form a curve in space:

<Formula label="Two equations for a curve" tex="x^5 + yz = 3, \qquad xy^2 + yz^2 + zx^2 = 7" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b), p. 1" />

Use the method of total differentials to find $dx/dy$ along the curve at the point $(x, y, z) = (1, 1, 2)$.

Give the answer as a decimal number.

<Hint>

First check that the point is on the curve. Then take the total differential of each equation. The right-hand sides are constants, so each differential is zero.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put in $x = 1$, $y = 1$, $z = 2$. You now have two equations with plain numbers for the three small changes $dx$, $dy$ and $dz$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You want a relation between $dx$ and $dy$ only. Remove $dz$ from the two equations.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$dx/dy$ at $(1, 1, 2)$:" value="-0.2" tolerance="0.002" calc="p2-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Three variables and two equations leave one free variable: a curve. Along it, $x$ and $z$ are functions of $y$. The differentials of the two equations are two linear relations between $dx$, $dy$ and $dz$. Removing $dz$ leaves $dx$ as a multiple of $dy$.

<Step title="The point is on the curve">

At $(1, 1, 2)$: $x^5 + yz = 1 + 2 = 3$ and $xy^2 + yz^2 + zx^2 = 1 + 4 + 2 = 7$.

</Step>

<Step title="Differentials of the two equations">

<Formula label="Differential of each equation" tex="5x^4\,dx + z\,dy + y\,dz = 0, \qquad (y^2 + 2xz)\,dx + (2xy + z^2)\,dy + (2yz + x^2)\,dz = 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

In the second one, each variable appears in two terms: for example $x$ in $xy^2$ and in $zx^2$, which give $y^2$ and $2xz$.

</Step>

<Step title="At the point">

With $x = 1$, $y = 1$, $z = 2$ the numbers are $5$, $2$, $1$ and $1 + 4 = 5$, $2 + 4 = 6$, $4 + 1 = 5$:

<Formula label="The two relations at the point" tex="5\,dx + 2\,dy + dz = 0, \qquad 5\,dx + 6\,dy + 5\,dz = 0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Remove dz">

The first relation gives $dz = -5\,dx - 2\,dy$. Put it into the second: $5\,dx + 6\,dy - 25\,dx - 10\,dy = 0$, which is $-20\,dx - 4\,dy = 0$. So

<Formula label="The slope of the curve" tex="dx = -\tfrac15\,dy, \qquad \frac{dx}{dy} = -\frac15 = -0.2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

Along the curve, near this point, $x$ goes down by $0.2$ for each unit that $y$ goes up. The same two relations give $dz = -5\,dx - 2\,dy = dy - 2\,dy = -dy$, so $dz/dy = -1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A test with numbers: take a small step $dy = 0.01$ from the point, with $dx = -0.002$ and $dz = -0.01$. The new point $(0.998, 1.01, 1.99)$ gives $x^5 + yz = 2.99994$ and $xy^2 + yz^2 + zx^2 = 6.99981$. Both equations still hold to within $0.0002$, about the square of the step, as they should for a step along the tangent of the curve.

A second test uses the [gradient](/courses/18-02/gradient) (our addition). Each equation is a level surface, and its gradient is perpendicular to it. At the point the gradients are $\langle 5, 2, 1\rangle$ and $\langle 5, 6, 5\rangle$: the numbers in the two relations above. The curve lies in both surfaces, so its direction is perpendicular to both gradients. Their cross product is such a direction:

<Formula label="A direction along the curve" tex="\langle 5, 2, 1\rangle \times \langle 5, 6, 5\rangle = \langle 2 \cdot 5 - 1 \cdot 6,\ 1 \cdot 5 - 5 \cdot 5,\ 5 \cdot 6 - 2 \cdot 5\rangle = \langle 4, -20, 20\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

Its $x$ component divided by its $y$ component is $4/(-20) = -0.2$, and its $z$ component divided by its $y$ component is $20/(-20) = -1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
