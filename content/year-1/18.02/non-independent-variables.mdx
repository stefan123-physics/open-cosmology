[Lagrange multipliers](/courses/18-02/lagrange-multipliers) found extremes when the variables are related. This lecture asks how to take partial derivatives at all when the variables are related (transcript, p. 1).

## Rates of change of related variables

In physics, a function often depends on pressure, volume and temperature, which are related by $PV = nRT$; one could substitute, but it is often more convenient to keep all three (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1). In general, let $x$, $y$, $z$ be related by $g(x, y, z) = c$. Then, in principle, $z$ is a function of $x$ and $y$, and we can ask for $\partial z/\partial x$ and $\partial z/\partial y$, even when the relation cannot be solved (p. 1; notes, p. 1).

**Example.** The lecture's relation is $x^2 + yz + z^3 = 8$, near $(2, 3, 1)$ (indeed $4 + 3 + 1 = 8$). Solving the cubic for $z$ would be painful. Instead, take the differential: since $g = 8$ is constant, $dg = 0$ (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Differential of the constraint" tex="2x\,dx + z\,dy + (y + 3z^2)\,dz = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 4\,dx + dy + 6\,dz = 0 \ \text{ at } (2, 3, 1)" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

This relates the changes of all three variables near the point. Solving for $dz$ (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Two partial derivatives" tex="dz = -\tfrac16(4\,dx + dy), \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac46 = -\frac23, \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} = -\frac16" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The coefficients of $dx$ and $dy$ are the partials. Equivalently, $\partial z/\partial x$ keeps $y$ constant, so set $dy = 0$; $\partial z/\partial y$ keeps $x$ constant, so set $dx = 0$ (p. 1). **In general**, from $g_x\,dx + g_y\,dy + g_z\,dz = 0$ with $dy = 0$ (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The general formula" tex="\frac{\partial z}{\partial x} = -\frac{g_x}{g_z}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The minus sign is surprising the first time; it comes straight out of the derivation (p. 1).

## The notation can be dangerous

A warning example (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1). Let $f(x, y) = x + y$, so $\partial f/\partial x = 1$. Change variables: $x = u$, $y = u + v$. Then $f = 2u + v$ and $\partial f/\partial u = 2$. So $x = u$, but $\partial f/\partial x \ne \partial f/\partial u$ (p. 2).

The reason: $\partial f/\partial x$ changes $x$ **keeping $y$ fixed**, while $\partial f/\partial u$ changes $u$ (the same thing) **keeping $v$ fixed**, that is keeping $y - x$ fixed, so that $y$ changes too (p. 2; notes, p. 1). The usual notation does not say what is held fixed. When there is any risk of confusion, we say it with a subscript, a notation Auroux blames on physicists and chemists (p. 2):

<Formula label="Saying what is held fixed" tex="\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)_y = 1, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial u}\right)_v = \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)_v = 2" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 14, p. 1" />

The subscript now names the variable held **constant**, not one being differentiated, a clash with the earlier notation $f_x$ (p. 2). Without a subscript, $\partial/\partial x$ still means the other natural variable ($y$) is fixed; the example of the last section was $(\partial z/\partial x)_y$ (notes, p. 1).

## Three rates for one triangle

The lecture's example is geometric (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1). A triangle with two sides $a$ and $b$ at an angle $\theta$ has area $A = \frac12 ab\sin\theta$. Suppose it is a **right** triangle with hypotenuse $b$, so the constraint is $a = b\cos\theta$. (The transcript first misstates it as $b = \sin\theta$, p. 2, before using $a = b\cos\theta$ on p. 3.) How does $A$ change with $\theta$? Three answers make sense (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 1):

1. $\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial\theta}\right)_{a,b}$: ignore the constraint and treat $a$, $b$, $\theta$ as independent. The triangle stops being a right triangle. This is the old partial derivative, $\frac12 ab\cos\theta$ (p. 2).
2. $\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial\theta}\right)_a$: keep $a$ fixed and the right angle; $b$ changes as it must.
3. $\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial\theta}\right)_b$: keep $b$ fixed and the right angle; $a$ changes as it must.

(Keeping the third side fixed would be yet another choice. Of the three variables, two are independent, and we must say which, p. 3.)

## Computing (∂A/∂θ) with a fixed

**Method 0: substitution.** Here $b = a/\cos\theta = a\sec\theta$, so $A = \frac12 a^2\tan\theta$ and (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="By substitution" tex="\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial\theta}\right)_a = \tfrac12 a^2\sec^2\theta" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

That only works when the constraint can be solved, which in general it cannot; there are two systematic methods (p. 3).

**Method 1: differentials.** $a$ is fixed, so $da = 0$. Differentiating the constraint $a = b\cos\theta$: $da = \cos\theta\,db - b\sin\theta\,d\theta = 0$, so $db = b\tan\theta\,d\theta$ (p. 3; notes, p. 1). Then, from $dA = \frac12 b\sin\theta\,da + \frac12 a\sin\theta\,db + \frac12 ab\cos\theta\,d\theta$ with $da = 0$ (pp. 3–4; notes, pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="By differentials" tex="dA = \left(\tfrac12 a\sin\theta \cdot b\tan\theta + \tfrac12 ab\cos\theta\right)d\theta, \qquad \left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial\theta}\right)_a = \tfrac12 ab\left(\frac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos\theta} + \cos\theta\right) = \tfrac12 ab\sec\theta" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

In short: write $dA$; set the differential of the fixed variable to 0; eliminate the unwanted one ($db$) using the differentiated constraint; read off the coefficient of $d\theta$ (p. 4). With $b = a\sec\theta$, this agrees with method 0.

**Method 2: the chain rule.** Differentiating with $a$ held fixed (p. 4; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="By the chain rule" tex="\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial\theta}\right)_a = A_\theta\left(\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial\theta}\right)_a + A_a\left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial\theta}\right)_a + A_b\left(\frac{\partial b}{\partial\theta}\right)_a = A_\theta + A_b\left(\frac{\partial b}{\partial\theta}\right)_a" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 14, p. 2" />

since $\theta$ changes at rate 1 with itself and $a$ does not change. The rate $(\partial b/\partial\theta)_a = b\tan\theta$ comes from the constraint, as before, and the result is the same: the two methods compute the same quantities, written differently (p. 4; notes, p. 2).

<WorkedExample title="A constrained partial, and three rates for one triangle">
<Problem>
(a) On the sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 9$, near $(1, 2, 2)$, find $(\partial z/\partial x)_y$ and check by solving for $z$. Then find $(\partial f/\partial x)_y$ for $f = x + y + z$. (b) For the right triangle with $a = 3$ and $\theta = \pi/4$, find all three rates of change of $A$ with $\theta$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Differentials">
$2x\,dx + 2y\,dy + 2z\,dz = 0$ gives $2\,dx + 4\,dy + 4\,dz = 0$ at the point. With $dy = 0$: $(\partial z/\partial x)_y = -2/4 = -1/2$. Check: $z = \sqrt{9 - x^2 - y^2}$ has $\partial z/\partial x = -x/z = -1/2$. Then $(\partial f/\partial x)_y = 1 + (\partial z/\partial x)_y = 1/2$, while treating $z$ as independent would give 1.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The three rates">
Here $b = a\sec\theta = 3\sqrt2 \approx 4.24$. With $a$ and $b$ independent: $\frac12 ab\cos\theta = \frac12 \cdot 3 \cdot 3\sqrt2 \cdot \frac{\sqrt2}{2} = 4.5$. With $a$ fixed: $\frac12 a^2\sec^2\theta = \frac12 \cdot 9 \cdot 2 = 9$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Holding the hypotenuse fixed">
With $b$ fixed, $a = b\cos\theta$, so $A = \frac12 b^2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \frac14 b^2\sin 2\theta$ and $(\partial A/\partial\theta)_b = \frac12 b^2\cos 2\theta = 0$ at $\theta = \pi/4$: for a given hypotenuse, the 45° right triangle has the largest area. Three different answers, 4.5, 9 and 0, to "how does the area change with $\theta$".
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="What is held fixed in an expanding box">
Thermodynamics is the classic home of this notation, because pressure, volume, temperature and energy are all related. In 8.286, Guth applies $dU = -p\,dV$ to a box of gas expanding with the universe, and gets how the energy density changes as the box grows. In this notation it is a constrained partial derivative: the rate of change of the energy with the volume **with the entropy held fixed** is $-p$, which is what holds when no heat flows in or out; see [matter–radiation equality](/courses/8-286/matter-radiation-equality).
</Callout>

## Summary

- If $g(x, y, z) = c$, set $dg = 0$ and solve for the dependent variable's differential: $(\partial z/\partial x)_y = -g_x/g_z$.
- With related variables, say what is held fixed: $(\partial f/\partial x)_y$. Example: $x = u$ but $(\partial f/\partial x)_y \ne (\partial f/\partial u)_v$.
- For $A = \frac12 ab\sin\theta$ with $a = b\cos\theta$, the rate of change with $\theta$ depends on whether $a$, $b$ or both are fixed; $(\partial A/\partial\theta)_a = \frac12 ab\sec\theta = \frac12 a^2\sec^2\theta$.
- Methods: substitution (if the constraint can be solved), differentials, or the chain rule with the dependent rate taken from the constraint.
