## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Drawing space on a screen: perspective" points={8} mit="Part B, Problem 1">

A program for 3D computer graphics has to show objects of space on a flat screen. It draws each point $P$ at the place where the straight line from $P$ to the eye of the viewer goes through the screen. Take the $yz$-plane (the plane $x = 0$) as the screen, and put the eye at the point

<Formula label="The eye" tex="E = (2, 0, 0)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={2}>

A point $P = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$ is drawn on the screen at a point $Q$ of the $yz$-plane. Express the coordinates $y$ and $z$ of $Q$ in $x_0$, $y_0$ and $z_0$. Assume that $x_0 < 2$. Why is it reasonable to assume that?

<Hint>

Write parametric equations for the line through $E$ and $P$, as in the lesson on lines: start at $E$ and add a multiple of the vector from $E$ to $P$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The screen is the plane $x = 0$. For which value of the parameter is the $x$-coordinate of the moving point equal to zero?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-y" label="$y =$" expr="2*y0/(2 - x0)"
  vars={{ x0: [-1.5, -0.2], y0: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ x0: "x_0", y0: "y_0" }}

  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-a-z" label="$z =$" expr="2*z0/(2 - x0)"
  vars={{ x0: [-1.5, -0.2], z0: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ x0: "x_0", z0: "z_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "z0", hint: "That moves P straight onto the screen along the x-direction and forgets where the eye is. With your formula a far object would look as large as a near one." },
    { expr: "2*z0/(2 + x0)", hint: "Check the distance from the eye to P along the x-axis: it is 2 − x₀, which is more than 2 when P is behind the screen, where x₀ is negative." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** The point $Q$ is where the line through $E$ and $P$ meets the plane $x = 0$. Write the line with a parameter and find the value of the parameter at which $x = 0$.

<Step title="The line of sight">

The lesson writes a line as a starting point plus $t$ times a vector along the line. Here the parameter is called $s$, because $t$ will be the time in part (d). Start at $E$ and move along $\vec{EP} = \langle x_0 - 2,\ y_0,\ z_0\rangle$:

<Formula label="The line from the eye to the point" tex="x = 2 + s\,(x_0 - 2), \qquad y = s\,y_0, \qquad z = s\,z_0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

At $s = 0$ the moving point is at the eye and at $s = 1$ it is at $P$.

</Step>

<Step title="Where it meets the screen">

On the screen $x = 0$, so $2 + s\,(x_0 - 2) = 0$, which gives $s = 2/(2 - x_0)$. Put this value in $y$ and $z$:

<Formula label="The picture of a point on the screen" tex="Q:\qquad y = \frac{2\,y_0}{2 - x_0}, \qquad z = \frac{2\,z_0}{2 - x_0}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Why the assumption is reasonable">

The viewer looks from $x = 2$ towards the screen at $x = 0$, in the direction of decreasing $x$. Everything the viewer can see through the screen lies in front of the eye, where $x_0 < 2$. A point with $x_0 > 2$ is behind the viewer's head. A point with $x_0 = 2$ is beside the eye: its line of sight is parallel to the screen and never meets it, and the formula divides by zero there.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A point on the screen, with $x_0 = 0$, is drawn at itself: $y = y_0$ and $z = z_0$. For a point behind the screen, $x_0 < 0$, the factor $2/(2 - x_0)$ is less than $1$: far objects are drawn smaller, as they should be. The factor is the distance from the eye to the screen, $2$, divided by the distance from the eye to $P$ measured along the $x$-axis, $2 - x_0$, as similar triangles give.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

What does the picture on the screen of a line segment in space look like? Justify your answer. (As in part (a), all of the segment lies where $x < 2$.)

<Hint>

Think of all the lines of sight from the eye to the points of the segment together. What kind of surface do they sweep out?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Two planes that are not parallel meet in a line.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="The picture of a line segment is"
  choices={["always a straight line segment of the same length as the segment in space", "a straight line segment, or a single point when the segment lies on a line through the eye", "an arc of a curve, because the far end of the segment is drawn smaller than the near end", "a straight line segment only when the segment in space is parallel to the screen, and a curve otherwise"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The eye and the segment lie in one plane. Every line of sight to a point of the segment stays in that plane, so the picture lies where that plane cuts the screen: on a straight line.

<Step title="The geometric reason">

Suppose first that the line of the segment does not go through $E$. Then $E$ and the segment lie in exactly one plane. The line of sight from $E$ to any point of the segment lies in that plane, so the picture of that point is in that plane and in the screen. The plane contains $E$, which is not on the screen, so it is not the screen itself, and it is not parallel to the screen either, because it holds lines of sight that cross the screen. Two planes that are not parallel meet in a straight line. So the picture lies on a straight line, and as the point runs along the segment from one end to the other, its picture runs from the picture of one end to the picture of the other end. The picture is a line segment.

If the line of the segment does go through $E$, all its points share one line of sight. The viewer sees the segment end on, and its picture is a single point.

</Step>

<Step title="The same with formulas">

Let the segment run from $P_0$ to $P_1$, and let $P(t) = P_0 + t\,\vec{P_0P_1}$ with $0 \le t \le 1$ be its points. Write $d_0 = 2 - x_0$ and $d_1 = 2 - x_1$ for the distances of the two ends from the eye along the $x$-axis (our names), and $Q_0$, $Q_1$ for the pictures of the ends. The point $P(t)$ is at the distance $(1 - t)\,d_0 + t\,d_1$, and part (a) gives for its picture

<Formula label="The picture of a segment" tex="Q(t) = (1 - \lambda)\,Q_0 + \lambda\,Q_1, \qquad \lambda = \frac{t\,d_1}{(1 - t)\,d_0 + t\,d_1}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

To see this, put $\lambda$ in: the $y$-coordinate of the right side is $\big((1 - t)\,d_0 \cdot 2y_0/d_0 + t\,d_1 \cdot 2y_1/d_1\big)$ divided by $(1 - t)\,d_0 + t\,d_1$, which is $2\big((1 - t)\,y_0 + t\,y_1\big)$ divided by the distance of $P(t)$: the formula of part (a). The same holds for $z$. The number $\lambda$ is $0$ at $t = 0$ and $1$ at $t = 1$, and it grows in between, because both $d_0$ and $d_1$ are positive. So $Q(t)$ runs along the straight segment from $Q_0$ to $Q_1$, once and without turning back.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The number $\lambda$ equals $t$ only when $d_0 = d_1$, when the segment is parallel to the screen. Otherwise the middle of the segment in space ($t = \tfrac12$) is not drawn at the middle of the picture: the half that is nearer to the eye takes up more of the picture. The picture is straight, but lengths along it are not kept. If $Q_0 = Q_1$, the formula gives that one point for every $t$: the case of the segment seen end on.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

A line segment joins the points

<Formula label="The two ends of the segment" tex="P_0 = (-1, -3, 1), \qquad P_1 = (-2, 4, 6)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

What is drawn on the screen? Give the coordinates of the pictures $Q_0$ of $P_0$ and $Q_1$ of $P_1$ as decimals, to three decimal places where they do not come out even.

<Hint>

By part (b) you only need the pictures of the two ends. Use the formulas of part (a) twice.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c-y0" label="$y$ of $Q_0$ $=$" value="-2" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-c-y0" />

<Answer id="p1-c-z0" label="$z$ of $Q_0$ $=$" value="0.667" tolerance="0.002" calc="p1-c-z0" />

<Answer id="p1-c-y1" label="$y$ of $Q_1$ $=$" value="2" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-c-y1" />

<Answer id="p1-c-z1" label="$z$ of $Q_1$ $=$" value="3" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-c-z1" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** By part (b) the picture is the straight segment between the pictures of the two ends, and part (a) gives those.

<Step title="The two ends">

For $P_0$ the distance from the eye along the $x$-axis is $2 - (-1) = 3$, and for $P_1$ it is $2 - (-2) = 4$:

<Formula label="The pictures of the two ends" tex="Q_0 = \left(\frac{2 \cdot (-3)}{3},\ \frac{2 \cdot 1}{3}\right) = \left(-2,\ \tfrac23\right), \qquad Q_1 = \left(\frac{2 \cdot 4}{4},\ \frac{2 \cdot 6}{4}\right) = (2,\ 3)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="What is drawn">

The screen shows the straight segment from $Q_0 = (-2,\ 0.667)$ to $Q_1 = (2,\ 3)$, in the coordinates $(y, z)$ of the screen.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take the middle of the segment in space, $(-1.5,\ 0.5,\ 3.5)$. Its picture is $(2 \cdot 0.5/3.5,\ 2 \cdot 3.5/3.5) = (0.286,\ 2)$. From $Q_0$ to $Q_1$ the screen coordinates change by $4$ and by $2.333$, and from $Q_0$ to this picture by $2.286$ and by $1.333$: both are $0.571$ of the way, so the picture of the middle is on the drawn segment. It is not at its middle, as part (b) warned.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

A bird leaves $P_0$ at the time $t = 0$ and flies in a straight line at constant speed, in such a way that it passes $P_1$ at $t = 1$. What does the path of the bird for $t \ge 0$ look like on the screen? Show that the picture of the bird tends to a limit point as $t$ tends to infinity. This point is called the **vanishing point**. Give its coordinates.

<Hint>

The lesson's moving point: $P(t) = P_0 + t\,\vec{P_0P_1}$. Write its three coordinates as functions of $t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put those coordinates in the formulas of part (a). For the limit, divide the top and the bottom of each fraction by $t$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d-y" label="$y$ of the vanishing point $=$" value="14" tolerance="0.02" calc="p1-d-y" />

<Answer id="p1-d-z" label="$z$ of the vanishing point $=$" value="10" tolerance="0.02" calc="p1-d-z" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The bird is the moving point of the lesson on parametric equations. Part (a) turns its position at each time into a point of the screen.

<Step title="The bird in space">

The vector from $P_0$ to $P_1$ is $\langle -1, 7, 5\rangle$, and it is the velocity of the bird:

<Formula label="The flight of the bird" tex="x(t) = -1 - t, \qquad y(t) = -3 + 7t, \qquad z(t) = 1 + 5t" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The bird on the screen">

Its distance from the eye along the $x$-axis is $2 - x(t) = 3 + t$. By part (a) its picture is at

<Formula label="The picture of the bird" tex="y = \frac{2\,(7t - 3)}{t + 3}, \qquad z = \frac{2\,(5t + 1)}{t + 3}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The limit">

Divide the top and the bottom of each fraction by $t$:

<Formula label="The vanishing point" tex="y = \frac{2\,(7 - 3/t)}{1 + 3/t} \to 14, \qquad z = \frac{2\,(5 + 1/t)}{1 + 3/t} \to 10 \qquad (t \to \infty)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

Call the limit point $V = (14, 10)$ (our name).

</Step>

<Step title="The shape of the path">

By part (b), every piece of the flight is drawn as a piece of one straight line. Subtracting $Q_0 = (-2, \tfrac23)$ from the picture of the bird gives $y + 2 = 16t/(t + 3)$ and $z - \tfrac23 = \tfrac{28}{3}\,t/(t + 3)$, so

<Formula label="The path on the screen" tex="Q(t) = Q_0 + \frac{t}{t + 3}\,\vec{Q_0V}, \qquad \vec{Q_0V} = \left\langle 16,\ \tfrac{28}{3}\right\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

The fraction $t/(t + 3)$ grows from $0$ towards $1$ and never reaches it. So the path on the screen is the straight segment from $Q_0$ to $V$ without its end point $V$. The picture of the bird moves along it more and more slowly: it is at a quarter of the way at $t = 1$ (that is $Q_1$), halfway at $t = 3$, and at three quarters only at $t = 9$. The bird flies on for ever, but on the screen it shrinks into the point $V$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 1$ the formula gives $Q_0 + \tfrac14\langle 16, \tfrac{28}{3}\rangle = (2, 3) = Q_1$, as in part (c). The vanishing point has a meaning of its own (our remark): it is where the line through the eye parallel to the flight meets the screen. That line is $x = 2 - s$, $y = 7s$, $z = 5s$, and it reaches $x = 0$ at $s = 2$, at the point $(14, 10)$ of the screen. So every bird flying in the direction $\langle -1, 7, 5\rangle$ heads for the same point of the screen, wherever it started: parallel lines are drawn as lines through one point.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

In fact a fence in front of the viewer hides part of the flight. The fence stands upright in the plane $x = 1$ and its top is at the height $z = 1$. (Our reading: the fence is long enough in the $y$-direction, and it hides every line of sight that crosses the plane $x = 1$ at or below $z = 1$.) Which part of the flight of the bird is hidden?

MIT's hint: the hidden points are exactly the points that lie below a certain plane through $E$.

The boxes ask for the time at which the bird comes into view, and for the place on the screen where it does, to three decimal places where it does not come out even.

<Hint>

The last line of sight that the fence blocks just touches its top edge. All such lines together make a plane. It contains $E$ and the whole top edge of the fence, the line $x = 1$, $z = 1$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Find a normal vector of that plane from two vectors in it, with a cross product, as in the lesson on planes. Then put the coordinates of the bird into the equation of the plane.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e-t" label="The bird comes into view at $t =$" value="0.5" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-e-t" />

<Answer id="p1-e-y" label="$y$ on the screen at that time $=$" value="0.286" tolerance="0.002" calc="p1-e-y" />

<Answer id="p1-e-z" label="$z$ on the screen at that time $=$" value="2" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-e-z" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The plane through the eye and the top edge of the fence separates what the viewer sees over the fence from what the fence hides. Find its equation and see when the bird crosses it.

<Step title="The plane through the eye and the top of the fence">

The top edge is the line of points $(1, y, 1)$. Two vectors in the plane are $\langle 0, 1, 0\rangle$, along the edge, and $\langle -1, 0, 1\rangle$, from $E$ to the point $(1, 0, 1)$ of the edge. Their cross product is a normal vector:

<Formula label="Normal vector of the plane of sight" tex="\langle 0, 1, 0\rangle \times \langle -1, 0, 1\rangle = \big\langle (1)(1) - (0)(0),\ -\big((0)(1) - (0)(-1)\big),\ (0)(0) - (1)(-1)\big\rangle = \langle 1, 0, 1\rangle" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(e) (our own solution)" />

The plane with this normal vector through $E = (2, 0, 0)$ is $(x - 2) + z = 0$:

<Formula label="The plane of sight over the fence" tex="x + z = 2" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(e) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Which side is hidden">

A point beyond the fence is hidden when it lies on or below this plane, where $x + z \le 2$: lowering a point lowers its $z$, and its line of sight then meets the fence. Directly: the line of sight of part (a) crosses the plane $x = 1$ at $s = 1/(2 - x_0)$, at the height $z_0/(2 - x_0)$. That is at most $1$, the top of the fence, exactly when $x_0 + z_0 \le 2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The bird">

For the bird, $x + z = (-1 - t) + (1 + 5t) = 4t$. It is hidden while $4t \le 2$:

<Formula label="The hidden part of the flight" tex="0 \le t \le \tfrac12" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(e) (our own solution)" />

At $t = \tfrac12$ the bird is at $(-1.5,\ 0.5,\ 3.5)$, exactly in line with the top edge of the fence. By our reading of the fence that is the last moment at which it is hidden. After that it is in view for good, because $4t$ keeps growing. The hidden part is the straight piece of the flight from $P_0$ to that point. On the screen it is the piece of the path from $Q_0 = (-2,\ 0.667)$ to the picture of that point,

<Formula label="Where the bird comes into view" tex="\left(\frac{2 \cdot 0.5}{3.5},\ \frac{2 \cdot 3.5}{3.5}\right) = \left(\tfrac27,\ 2\right) = (0.286,\ 2)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(e) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second route: draw the fence itself on the screen. By part (a) a point $(1, y_0, 1)$ of its top edge is drawn at the height $2 \cdot 1/(2 - 1) = 2$. So on the screen the fence covers everything on or below the horizontal line $z = 2$, and the bird is hidden while its picture is on or below that line: $2\,(5t + 1)/(t + 3) \le 2$, which is $5t + 1 \le t + 3$, or $t \le \tfrac12$. The same answer. In the formula of part (d), the fraction $t/(t + 3)$ is $\tfrac17$ at $t = \tfrac12$: only the first seventh of the path on the screen is hidden, and that includes $Q_0$ but not yet $Q_1$, which is at a quarter.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A power in which base and exponent both change" points={4} mit="Part B, Problem 2">

Let

<Formula label="A power as a function of two variables" tex="w = x^y = e^{\,y \ln x}" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 2(a), p. 1" />

for $x > 0$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Give a formula for the approximate small change $\Delta w$ in the value of $w$ that small changes $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ in $x$ and $y$ produce. Use it to calculate an approximate value of $1.98^{2.01}$, and compare it with the exact value.

The first two boxes ask for the two partial derivatives that the formula needs. The last two ask for the approximate value from the formula and for the value from a calculator, both to four decimal places.

<Hint>

The lesson's approximation formula says $\Delta w \approx w_x\,\Delta x + w_y\,\Delta y$, where $w_x$ and $w_y$ are the two partial derivatives.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $w_x$ the exponent $y$ is a constant: a power of $x$. For $w_y$ the base $x$ is a constant: use the form $e^{\,y \ln x}$, an exponential in $y$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$1.98^{2.01}$ is close to $2^2$. Which $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$ lead from $(2, 2)$ to $(1.98, 2.01)$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a-wx" label="$\partial w/\partial x =$" expr="y*x^(y-1)"
  vars={{ x: [1.2, 1.6], y: [2, 2.5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x^y*log(x)", hint: "That is the derivative with respect to y. For the derivative with respect to x, treat y as a constant: then w is a power of x." },
    { expr: "y*x^y", hint: "The power rule lowers the exponent by one. Test with y = 1: then w = x, and its derivative is 1, not x." },
    { expr: "x^(y-1)", hint: "The power rule also brings the exponent down as a factor. Test with y = 2: then w = x², and its derivative is 2x, not x." }

They close just below the $x$-axis, outside the plot, and shrink onto the point as the level rises. The level curve through the point itself, $f = 0.657$, has shrunk to that single point. This is the top of a hill.

At $(1.648, 0.824)$ the level curve through the point, $f = -0.325$, crosses itself: two branches of the same level go through the point, like the two diagonal lines at the saddle of the lesson. It is not drawn in the plot, because $-0.325$ is not one of the plotted levels. The plotted curves next to it show it, though: the two pieces of the level $-0.2$ come towards the point from the left and from the right and turn back, and the two curves of the level $-0.4$ come towards it from above and from below and turn back.

</Step>

<Step title="North and South">

By part (c), the slice in the $y$-direction through a point with $x_0 > 0$ is a parabola that opens downwards with its top at $y = x_0/2$. Both points have $y = x/2$: each is at the top of its parabola. So at both points $f$ goes down towards the North and towards the South.

</Step>

<Step title="East and West">

The slice in the $x$-direction is $g(x) = f(x, y_0)$ (our name). It has $g'(x_0) = f_x = 0$ at both points, and its second derivative is

<Formula label="Second derivative of the slice in the x-direction" tex="g''(x) = 6x - 8 + 2y_0" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 3(d) (our own solution)" />

At $(0.485, 0.243)$ this is $2.913 - 8 + 0.485 = -4.60$, negative: the slice has a maximum there, and $f$ goes down towards the East and towards the West. At $(1.648, 0.824)$ it is $9.887 - 8 + 1.648 = 3.53$, positive: the slice has a minimum there, and $f$ goes up towards the East and towards the West.

</Step>

<Step title="Conclusion">

At $(0.485, 0.243)$ the value of $f$ goes down in all four directions: a local maximum. At $(1.648, 0.824)$ it goes up towards the East and the West and down towards the North and the South: a saddle. In none of the four directions does $f$ stay exactly the same. (At the saddle $f$ does stay the same along the two branches of its level curve, but those run diagonally.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Step $0.1$ away from each point and compute $f$ with the formula. From the first point: $-0.005$ to the North and to the South, $-0.022$ to the East and $-0.024$ to the West. All four go down. From the second point: $-0.016$ to the North and to the South, $+0.019$ to the East and $+0.017$ to the West.

MIT asks for two points. The two equations have one more solution that touches the plot (our remark): with $x = 0$ the equation $f_y = 0$ holds for every $y$, and $f_x = 3 - y^2 = 0$ then gives $y = \sqrt 3 = 1.732$. The point $(0, 1.732)$ is on the left edge of the plot, where the solid curve of level $0$ meets the $y$-axis, which is itself part of the level $0$: two branches of one level crossing, as at the saddle.

</Step>

</Solution>
