After triple integrals, the next topic in space is vector fields, with flux first and work later (transcript, p. 1).

## Vector fields in space

A vector field in space has a vector at every point, with components depending on $x$, $y$, $z$: $\vec F = P\,\hat i + Q\,\hat j + R\,\hat k$ (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 2). Drawing them is hard, but it helps to know roughly where the vectors point (p. 1). Examples (p. 1; notes, p. 2):

- **force fields**: the gravitational attraction of a mass at the origin points towards the origin with strength proportional to $1/\rho^2$, for example $\vec F = -c\,\langle x, y, z\rangle/\rho^3$; electric fields have the same form, and there are magnetic fields;
- **velocity fields**, such as winds in the atmosphere, which vary with altitude;
- **gradient fields** $\nabla u = \langle u_x, u_y, u_z\rangle$, such as temperature or pressure gradients. The kinds overlap: gravitational and electric fields are gradients of potentials, up to sign (p. 1).

## Flux through a surface

In the plane, [flux](/courses/18-02/flux) was measured across a curve. In space, air cannot flow "through a curve": flux is measured through a **surface**, like a net, and it is a double integral (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Flux in space" tex="\text{flux} = \int\int_S \vec F \cdot \hat n\,dS, \qquad d\vec S = \hat n\,dS" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

where $\hat n$ is the unit normal vector to $S$ and $dS$ the area element of the surface (written $dS$ to keep $dA$ for areas in the coordinate planes) (p. 2). There are two choices of $\hat n$, one for each side of the surface; choosing one is **orienting** the surface. Usually it points out of a closed region, or up; the other choice flips the sign (pp. 1–2; notes, p. 2). The combination $d\vec S$ is often easier to compute than $\hat n$ and $dS$ separately, as $\hat n\,ds = \langle dy, -dx\rangle$ was in the plane (p. 2). A field tangent to the surface has zero flux (p. 2).

As in the plane, if $\vec F$ is a fluid's velocity, the flux is the **flow through $S$ per unit time**: through a small piece $\Delta S$ passes the contents of a slanted box with base $\Delta S$ and side $\vec F$, of volume base times height, $(\vec F \cdot \hat n)\,\Delta S$ (p. 4; notes, p. 2).

## Two examples on a sphere

**The radial field** $\vec F = \langle x, y, z\rangle$ through the sphere of radius $a$ about the origin, outwards (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 2). The outward normal is $\hat n = \langle x, y, z\rangle/a$, a unit vector since $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} = a$ on the sphere. $\vec F$ is parallel to $\hat n$ with length $a$, so $\vec F \cdot \hat n = (x^2 + y^2 + z^2)/a = a$, and (p. 2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Flux of the radial field" tex="\int\int_S \vec F \cdot \hat n\,dS = \int\int_S a\,dS = a \cdot 4\pi a^2 = 4\pi a^3" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

using everything on the surface only, where $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = a^2$ (p. 2).

**The field** $\vec H = z\,\hat k$ through the same sphere (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 3). Now $\vec H \cdot \hat n = z^2/a$, which must be integrated. On the sphere use $\varphi$ and $\theta$ as two parameters (not as spherical coordinates: there is no $\rho$), with the [surface element](/courses/18-02/spherical-coordinates) $dS = a^2\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta$ and $z = a\cos\varphi$ (p. 3):

<Formula label="Flux of z k through the sphere" tex="\int\int_S \frac{z^2}{a}\,dS = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi} \frac{a^2\cos^2\varphi}{a}\,a^2\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta = 2\pi a^3\left[-\frac{\cos^3\varphi}{3}\right]_0^{\pi} = \frac43\pi a^3" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

That this is the volume of the ball is no coincidence, as the divergence theorem will show next lecture (p. 3).

## Setting up flux integrals

Geometry sometimes gives the answer at once, but in general the surface is described by two parameters and $\vec F \cdot \hat n\,dS$ is expressed in them (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 3). The formulas to remember (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 3):

- **A horizontal plane** $z = a$ (or a piece of it): $\hat n = \pm\hat k$, $dS = dx\,dy$; replace $z$ by $a$ and it is an ordinary double integral (p. 3). Planes parallel to the other coordinate planes are similar, for example $x = $ constant with $\hat n = \pm\hat i$ and $dS = dy\,dz$ (p. 3).
- **A sphere** of radius $a$ about the origin: $\hat n = \pm\langle x, y, z\rangle/a$, $dS = a^2\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta$. Take the dot product with $\vec F$ first: symmetric combinations like $x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ may simplify (p. 4).
- **A cylinder** of radius $a$ about the $z$-axis: the sides are vertical, so the normal is horizontal, straight away from the axis, $\hat n = \pm\langle x, y, 0\rangle/a$. With $z$ and $\theta$ as parameters, a small piece is $\Delta z$ high and $a\,\Delta\theta$ wide, so $dS = a\,dz\,d\theta$ (p. 4; notes, p. 3).
- **A graph** $z = f(x, y)$: $\hat n$ and $dS$ are each complicated, but their product is simple, with $x$ and $y$ as parameters (p. 4; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Flux through a graph" tex="\hat n\,dS = \pm\langle -f_x, -f_y, 1\rangle\,dx\,dy" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 27, p. 3" />

  Remove every $z$ first, then set up the bounds on the shadow of $S$ in the $xy$-plane (p. 4). The proof comes next lecture.

<WorkedExample title="A cylinder, a hemisphere and a disc">
<Problem>
(a) Find the outward flux of $\vec F = \langle x, y, 0\rangle$ through the side of the cylinder of radius 1 about the $z$-axis, $0 \le z \le 2$. (b) Find the upward flux of $\hat k$ through the upper unit hemisphere. (c) Find the upward flux of $\vec G = \langle 0, 0, z\rangle$ through the disc of radius 1 in the plane $z = 2$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Cylinder">
$\vec F \cdot \hat n = \langle x, y, 0\rangle \cdot \langle x, y, 0\rangle = x^2 + y^2 = 1$ on the cylinder, so the flux is the area of the side, $2\pi \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 4\pi \approx 12.6$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Hemisphere">
$\hat k \cdot \hat n = z = \cos\varphi$, so the flux is $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2} \cos\varphi\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \frac12 = \pi$: the same as the flux of $\hat k$ through the flat disc underneath, the shadow of the hemisphere. A uniform flow upwards passes through both equally.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Disc">
$\hat n = \hat k$ and $\vec G \cdot \hat k = z = 2$, so the flux is $2 \times \pi = 2\pi$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Gauss's law for gravity">
The gravitational field of a mass $M$ at the origin is $\vec g = -GM\langle x, y, z\rangle/\rho^3$. On a sphere of radius $a$ it points straight in with strength $GM/a^2$, so its outward flux is $-\frac{GM}{a^2} \cdot 4\pi a^2 = -4\pi GM$, the same for every radius: the flux counts the mass inside. This is Gauss's law for gravity (for any closed surface, by the divergence theorem of the next lecture). Guth applies it to closed surfaces in a uniform universe to show that it cannot be static; see [why a static universe cannot work](/courses/8-286/newtons-static-universe).
</Callout>

## Summary

- A vector field in space: $\vec F = P\hat i + Q\hat j + R\hat k$, with $P$, $Q$, $R$ functions of $x$, $y$, $z$.
- Flux through a surface: $\int\int_S \vec F \cdot \hat n\,dS$, after choosing a side; $d\vec S = \hat n\,dS$. For a velocity field, the flow through $S$ per unit time.
- $\langle x, y, z\rangle$ through the sphere of radius $a$: $4\pi a^3$; $z\hat k$: $\frac43\pi a^3$.
- Planes: $\hat n = \pm\hat k$, $dS = dx\,dy$. Spheres: $\hat n = \pm\langle x, y, z\rangle/a$, $dS = a^2\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta$. Cylinders: $\hat n = \pm\langle x, y, 0\rangle/a$, $dS = a\,dz\,d\theta$. Graphs: $\hat n\,dS = \pm\langle -f_x, -f_y, 1\rangle\,dx\,dy$.
