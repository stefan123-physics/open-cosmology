After several weeks of derivatives, this lecture turns to integrals of functions of two variables (transcript, p. 1).

## The double integral

For a function of one variable, $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is the area under the graph over $[a, b]$, counted negatively below the axis. For a function of two variables, the graph is a surface, and the **double integral** over a region $R$ of the $xy$-plane is the **volume** under the graph above $R$ (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Double integral" tex="\int\int_R f(x, y)\,dA = \text{volume below } z = f(x, y) \text{ above } R" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

$dA$ stands for a small piece of area. As in one variable, it is defined by cutting $R$ into small pieces of area $\Delta A_i$ at points $(x_i, y_i)$, adding the volumes of the thin columns, and taking the limit as the pieces shrink (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Definition" tex="\int\int_R f\,dA = \lim_{\Delta A \to 0} \sum_i f(x_i, y_i)\,\Delta A_i" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Nobody computes it this way, any more than single integrals are computed from Riemann sums. Instead, a double integral is reduced to two single integrals, so all the single-variable techniques come back (p. 1). (A good moment to review [integration](/courses/18-01/trig-integrals) from 18.01.)

## Iterated integrals

As with partial derivatives, the idea is to **slice**. Scan the region with planes parallel to the $yz$-plane, $x = $ constant. Let $S(x)$ be the area of the slice under the graph at a given $x$. Two nearby slices enclose a volume of about $S(x)\,\Delta x$, so (transcript, pp. 1–2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Volume by slices" tex="\text{volume} = \int_{a}^{b} S(x)\,dx, \qquad S(x) = \int f(x, y)\,dy" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

Here $a$ and $b$ are the smallest and largest values of $x$ in the region. In $S(x)$, $x$ is a fixed parameter and $y$ the variable of integration, from the left end to the right end of the slice. This is an **iterated integral** (p. 2; notes, p. 1). The single most important point about double integrals (p. 2):

- the **inner** bounds, for $y$, can depend on $x$: different slices cross the region over different ranges of $y$;
- the **outer** bounds, for $x$, are **numbers**: the first and the last slice.

($x$ and $y$ still vary independently inside the region; it is only the bounds for $y$ that depend on $x$, p. 2.)

## Example: over a square

Integrate $f = 1 - x^2 - y^2$ (the downward paraboloid of [Lecture 8](/courses/18-02/partial-derivatives)) over the square $0 \le x \le 1$, $0 \le y \le 1$ (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1). For a rectangle every slice has the same range, so the bounds are easy (p. 2):

<Formula label="Over the unit square" tex="\int_0^1\int_0^1 (1 - x^2 - y^2)\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 \left[y - x^2y - \frac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^1 dx = \int_0^1 \left(\frac23 - x^2\right)dx = \frac13" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

The inner integral (with $x$ held constant) leaves a function of $x$ only, the area of the slice, which counts the part of the slice below the $xy$-plane negatively (p. 2). Then the outer integral gives $\frac23 - \frac13 = \frac13$ (p. 2; notes, p. 1). Here $dA$ became $dy\,dx$: the slicing cuts the region into small rectangles of area $\Delta x\,\Delta y$ (p. 3; notes, p. 1). The order is up to us; in theory both work, but one may be much easier, because of the shape of the region or because only one way can be integrated (p. 3).

## Example: over a quarter disc

To get only the volume above the $xy$-plane, integrate the same function over the quarter disc $x^2 + y^2 \le 1$ in the first quadrant, where the paraboloid is above the plane (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1). For a fixed $x$, $y$ runs from 0 to the circle, $y = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$; the first slice is $x = 0$ and the last $x = 1$ (p. 3):

<Formula label="Over the quarter disc" tex="\int_0^1\int_0^{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} (1 - x^2 - y^2)\,dy\,dx = \int_0^1 \frac23\,(1 - x^2)^{3/2}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{8}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–4" />

The inner bound depends on $x$, the outer ones are numbers (p. 3). The inner integral is $(1 - x^2)y - y^3/3$ at $y = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$, which is $(1 - x^2)^{3/2}$ minus a third of it (p. 3). The outer one needs the substitution $x = \sin\theta$, giving $\frac23\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^4\theta\,d\theta$, and double-angle formulas twice; Auroux leaves the end as an exercise and announces $\pi/8$ (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 1). That labour shows this is not the right way: polar coordinates, next lecture, make it easy (p. 4).

## Exchanging the order of integration

For a rectangle, the order can be swapped without thought: $\int_0^1\int_0^2 dx\,dy = \int_0^2\int_0^1 dy\,dx$, since both describe $0 \le x \le 2$, $0 \le y \le 1$ (transcript, p. 4; notes, p. 1). In general the bounds cannot simply be swapped: a bound like $\sqrt{1 - x^2}$ cannot become an outer bound. Draw the region (p. 4).

The lecture's example is $\int_0^1\int_x^{\sqrt x} \frac{e^y}{y}\,dy\,dx$, whose inner integral has no formula (p. 4; notes, p. 2). The region lies between $y = x$ and $y = \sqrt x$ for $0 \le x \le 1$, two curves that meet at $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 1)$:

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 1.2]} range={[0, 1.2]} xLabel="x" yLabel="y"

  points={[{ x: 1, y: 1, label: "(1, 1)" }]}
  caption="The region of the exchanged integral, for 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, lies between y = x (below) and y = √x (above), both computed from their formulas; they meet at (0, 0) and (1, 1), and beyond x = 1 the curves swap and the region ends. Sliced vertically, y runs from x to √x; sliced horizontally, x runs from y² to y." />

For a fixed $y$, $x$ runs from the curve $y = \sqrt x$, that is $x = y^2$, to the line $x = y$; and $y$ runs from 0 to 1 (p. 4; notes, p. 2). Now the inner integral is easy, because $e^y/y$ is a constant in $x$ (p. 4; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The exchanged integral" tex="\int_0^1\int_{y^2}^{y} \frac{e^y}{y}\,dx\,dy = \int_0^1 \frac{e^y}{y}(y - y^2)\,dy = \int_0^1 (e^y - ye^y)\,dy = \Big[2e^y - ye^y\Big]_0^1 = e - 2" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 16, p. 2" />

(The antiderivative of $ye^y$: guess $ye^y$, whose derivative is $e^y + ye^y$, and correct; or integrate by parts, p. 4.)

<WorkedExample title="A triangle both ways">
<Problem>
Compute $\int\int_R xy\,dA$ over the triangle $R$ with $0 \le y \le x \le 2$, in both orders.
</Problem>
<Step title="dy dx">
For fixed $x$, $y$ runs from 0 to $x$; $x$ from 0 to 2. Inner: $\int_0^x xy\,dy = x \cdot \frac{x^2}{2} = \frac{x^3}{2}$. Outer: $\int_0^2 \frac{x^3}{2}\,dx = \frac{2^4}{8} = 2$.
</Step>
<Step title="dx dy">
For fixed $y$, $x$ runs from $y$ to 2; $y$ from 0 to 2. Inner: $\int_y^2 xy\,dx = y\,\frac{4 - y^2}{2}$. Outer: $\int_0^2 \left(2y - \frac{y^3}{2}\right)dy = \left[y^2 - \frac{y^4}{8}\right]_0^2 = 4 - 2 = 2$. The same, as it must be.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Mass from a map">
Astronomers often know a galaxy or a cluster only as it appears on the sky: a surface density $\Sigma(x, y)$, mass per unit area projected onto the sky. Its total mass is the double integral $\int\int \Sigma\,dA$ over the region it covers. Gravitational lensing measures exactly such a projected mass, including dark matter; see [gravitational lensing](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/gravitational-lensing).
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\int\int_R f\,dA$ is the volume under the graph of $f$ above $R$, the limit of sums $\sum f(x_i, y_i)\,\Delta A_i$.
- It is computed as an iterated integral: inner over $y$ with $x$ fixed (bounds may depend on $x$), outer over $x$ (bounds are numbers).
- Over the unit square, $1 - x^2 - y^2$ integrates to $1/3$; over the quarter disc, to $\pi/8$.
- To exchange the order, draw the region and find the bounds the other way; $\int_0^1\int_x^{\sqrt x} \frac{e^y}{y}\,dy\,dx = e - 2$.
