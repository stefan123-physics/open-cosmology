After surfaces, back to curves: work along a curve in space. Computing it is as in the plane; testing for gradient fields takes more care (transcript, p. 1).

## Line integrals in space

For a force field $\vec F = \langle P, Q, R\rangle$ and a curve $C$ in space, with $d\vec r = \langle dx, dy, dz\rangle$ (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Work in space" tex="W = \int_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_C P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

It is still a line integral: parametrise $C$, express $x$, $y$, $z$, $dx$, $dy$, $dz$ in the parameter, and do a single integral, as in [the plane](/courses/18-02/vector-fields-and-line-integrals) (p. 1).

**Example.** $\vec F = \langle yz, xz, xy\rangle$ along $x = t^3$, $y = t^2$, $z = t$, $0 \le t \le 1$: $dx = 3t^2\,dt$, $dy = 2t\,dt$, $dz = dt$, and (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Along the twisted curve" tex="\int_C yz\,dx + xz\,dy + xy\,dz = \int_0^1 \big(t^3 \cdot 3t^2 + t^4 \cdot 2t + t^5\big)\,dt = \int_0^1 6t^5\,dt = 1" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

**The same field along three segments** parallel to the axes, from the origin to $(1, 0, 0)$, to $(1, 1, 0)$, to $(1, 1, 1)$: on the first two, $z = 0$ and $dz = 0$, so there is no work; on the last, $x = y = 1$ and $dx = dy = 0$, leaving $\int_0^1 dz = 1$ (p. 1; notes, p. 1). The same answer, because $\vec F$ is conservative: $\vec F = \nabla(xyz)$ (pp. 1–2).

## The fundamental theorem and gradient fields

As in the plane, for a gradient field (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Fundamental theorem for line integrals" tex="\int_{P_0}^{P_1} \nabla f \cdot d\vec r = f(P_1) - f(P_0)" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 30, p. 1" />

For $f = xyz$, $f(1, 1, 1) - f(0, 0, 0) = 1$ (p. 2). What changes in space is the **test**. If $\langle P, Q, R\rangle = \langle f_x, f_y, f_z\rangle$, equal mixed partial derivatives give three conditions (p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Test for a gradient field" tex="P_y = Q_x, \qquad P_z = R_x, \qquad Q_z = R_y" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

and conversely they are enough, if $\vec F$ is defined in all of space or in a [simply connected](/courses/18-02/simply-connected-regions) region (p. 2; notes, p. 1).

**Example.** For which $a$, $b$ is $axy\,\hat i + (x^2 + z^3)\,\hat j + (byz^2 - 4z^3)\,\hat k$ a gradient field? $P_y = ax$ must equal $Q_x = 2x$, so $a = 2$; $P_z = 0 = R_x$; and $Q_z = 3z^2$ must equal $R_y = bz^2$, so $b = 3$ (p. 2; notes, p. 1).

## Finding the potential

**By antiderivatives**, one variable at a time (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1). From $f_x = 2xy$: $f = x^2y + g(y, z)$, where the "constant" may depend on $y$ and $z$. Then $f_y = x^2 + g_y$ must be $x^2 + z^3$, so $g_y = z^3$ and $g = yz^3 + h(z)$. Then $f_z = 3yz^2 + h'(z)$ must be $3yz^2 - 4z^3$, so $h' = -4z^3$ and $h = -z^4 + c$ (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The potential" tex="f(x, y, z) = x^2y + yz^3 - z^4 + c" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 30, p. 1" />

**By a line integral**, $f(x_1, y_1, z_1) = f(0, 0, 0) + \int \vec F \cdot d\vec r$ along an easy path, such as three segments parallel to the axes: the same idea as in two variables, with three pieces instead of two (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 1).

## Curl

The **curl** measures how much $\vec F$ fails to be conservative (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1). With the [del](/courses/18-02/diffusion-equation) operator it is a cross product, best remembered as a determinant (p. 3; notes, pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Curl" tex="\nabla \times \vec F = \begin{vmatrix} \hat i & \hat j & \hat k \\ \partial/\partial x & \partial/\partial y & \partial/\partial z \\ P & Q & R \end{vmatrix} = (R_y - Q_z)\,\hat i + (P_z - R_x)\,\hat j + (Q_x - P_y)\,\hat k" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–4" />

An important difference from the plane: there the curl was a number, here it is a **vector field** (p. 3). Its components are exactly the three expressions of the test, so $\vec F$ is a gradient field (on a simply connected domain) exactly when its curl is zero (p. 3). Rather than memorise the formula, set up the determinant (p. 4).

**For a velocity field, the curl measures rotation** (p. 4; notes, p. 2). A rotation about the $z$-axis at angular velocity $\omega$ is $\vec v = \langle -\omega y, \omega x, 0\rangle$, and (p. 4; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Curl of a rotation" tex="\nabla \times \langle -\omega y, \omega x, 0\rangle = \langle 0, 0, 2\omega\rangle" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

Its length is twice the angular velocity and its direction is the axis of rotation. A complicated motion combines translation, expansion, shear and rotation; the curl picks out the rotation: it says how a small ball carried by the flow, such as a ping-pong ball, starts to spin (p. 4; notes, p. 2).

<WorkedExample title="A test, a potential, a work and a curl">
<Problem>
(a) Show that $\vec F = \langle 2xz, z^2, x^2 + 2yz\rangle$ is a gradient field and find a potential. (b) Find the work from the origin to $(1, 2, 3)$. (c) Find the curl of $\vec G = \langle y, -x, z\rangle$ and say what it means.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Test and potential">
$P_y = 0 = Q_x$; $P_z = 2x = R_x$; $Q_z = 2z = R_y$: a gradient field. From $f_x = 2xz$, $f = x^2z + g(y, z)$; $f_y = g_y = z^2$ gives $g = yz^2 + h(z)$; $f_z = x^2 + 2yz + h'(z)$ must be $x^2 + 2yz$, so $h$ is a constant: $f = x^2z + yz^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Work">
$f(1, 2, 3) - f(0, 0, 0) = 1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 9 = 21$, along any path.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Curl">
$\nabla \times \vec G = \langle 0 - 0,\ 0 - 0,\ -1 - 1\rangle = \langle 0, 0, -2\rangle$. The horizontal part $\langle y, -x\rangle$ is a clockwise rotation (seen from above) at angular velocity 1, hence $-2\hat k$; the part $z\,\hat k$ is a vertical stretching, which adds no rotation.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="A spinning planet">
A rigid body spinning at angular velocity $\Omega$ about the $z$-axis has velocity field $\langle -\Omega y, \Omega x, 0\rangle$, so its curl is $2\Omega\,\hat k$ at every point: length $2\Omega$, pointing along the axis. For the Earth, $\Omega \approx 7.29 \times 10^{-5}$ rad/s, so the curl of the velocity of the ground is about $1.46 \times 10^{-4}$ per second.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Work in space: $\int_C P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz$, computed by expressing everything in one parameter.
- $\int_{P_0}^{P_1} \nabla f \cdot d\vec r = f(P_1) - f(P_0)$; $\langle yz, xz, xy\rangle = \nabla(xyz)$.
- Test: $P_y = Q_x$, $P_z = R_x$, $Q_z = R_y$ (on a simply connected domain). Find the potential one variable at a time, or by a line integral along segments parallel to the axes.
- $\nabla \times \vec F = \langle R_y - Q_z, P_z - R_x, Q_x - P_y\rangle$; zero curl means conservative. For a velocity field the curl is twice the angular velocity, along the axis.
