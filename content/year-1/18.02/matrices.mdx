The first part of this lecture finishes the cross product with its rules and its main use, the equation of a plane. The second introduces matrices, which describe linear changes of coordinates (transcript, pp. 1–4).

## Rules for the cross product

The cross product does not commute. Swapping $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ leaves the parallelogram, its area and its plane unchanged, but the right-hand rule now gives the opposite direction; in the formula, every component changes sign, for example $a_2b_3 - a_3b_2$ (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Order matters, and A × A = 0" tex="\vec B \times \vec A = -\,\vec A \times \vec B, \qquad \vec A \times \vec A = \vec 0" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 3, p. 1" />

The parallelogram formed by $\vec A$ and $\vec A$ is flat, with area zero, so $\vec A \times \vec A$ is the zero vector (p. 1).

## The plane through three points

Three points $P_1$, $P_2$, $P_3$ that are not on one line determine a plane. The equation of the plane is a condition on the coordinates of a point $P = (x, y, z)$ that holds exactly when $P$ is in it (transcript, p. 1).

**First way:** the vectors $\vec{P_1P}$, $\vec{P_1P_2}$, $\vec{P_1P_3}$ form a parallelepiped. If $P$ is off the plane, it has some volume; if $P$ is in the plane, it is squashed flat. So (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The plane as a flat box" tex="\det\big(\vec{P_1P},\ \vec{P_1P_2},\ \vec{P_1P_3}\big) = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

**Second way:** a **normal vector** $\vec N$ is a vector perpendicular to the plane. $P$ is in the plane exactly when $\vec{P_1P}$ is perpendicular to $\vec N$, that is when their dot product is zero. And the cross product of two vectors in the plane is perpendicular to both (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The plane through its normal vector" tex="\vec{P_1P} \cdot \vec N = 0, \qquad \vec N = \vec{P_1P_2} \times \vec{P_1P_3}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Taking the points in another order gives $-\vec N$ or another multiple, and that is fine: a plane has infinitely many normal vectors, all multiples of each other (p. 1). The two ways give the same formula: $\vec{P_1P} \cdot (\vec{P_1P_2} \times \vec{P_1P_3})$ is the triple product, which equals the determinant for every $P$, and is zero only when $P$ is in the plane (p. 2).

## Matrices and linear changes of coordinates

Many quantities are related by linear formulas, or can be approximated by them. A typical case is a change of coordinates: a point with coordinates $x_1, x_2, x_3$ in one system has coordinates $u_1, u_2, u_3$ in another, better suited to the problem, and if the two systems share an origin the relation is linear (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1). The lecture's made-up example is $u_1 = 2x_1 + 3x_2 + 3x_3$, $u_2 = 2x_1 + 4x_2 + 5x_3$, $u_3 = x_1 + x_2 + 2x_3$, which a **matrix** (a table of numbers) writes as one product (p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="A linear relation as a matrix product" tex="\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 5 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \\ u_3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad AX = U" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 3, p. 1" />

Each entry of the product is the dot product of a row of $A$ with the column $X$: the first row gives $2x_1 + 3x_2 + 3x_3 = u_1$, and so on (p. 2). Here $A$ is a 3 × 3 matrix (three rows, three columns) and $X$ a column vector, a 3 × 1 matrix (p. 2).

## Multiplying matrices

The same rule multiplies any two matrices: **the entry of $AB$ in row $i$, column $j$ is the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$** (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1). A 3 × 4 matrix times a 4 × 2 matrix gives a 3 × 2 matrix. In the lecture's example, a first row $1, 2, 3, 4$ of $A$ and a second column $0, 3, 0, 2$ of $B$ give the entry $0 + 6 + 0 + 8 = 14$ in the first row, second column of $AB$ (p. 2).

A way to remember it: write $A$ on the left and $B$ above; each entry of $AB$ is the dot product of the row to its left and the column above it. This also shows the size of the answer: as tall as $A$, as wide as $B$ (p. 2). And it shows the condition for the product to exist (p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="When AB makes sense" tex="\text{width of } A = \text{height of } B" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

## What a product means

If $B$ transforms $X$ into $BX$ and $A$ then transforms the result, the product $AB$ does both at once, **first $B$, then $A$**: right to left, as $f(g(x))$ applies $g$ first (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Composing transformations" tex="(AB)X = A(BX)" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

This is **associativity**, which the matrix product has and the cross product does not (p. 3). But in general $AB \ne BA$: as the lecture puts it, converting oranges to bananas and then to carrots is not the same as the reverse, and one of the two products may not even have matching sizes (p. 3).

## The identity and a rotation

The **identity matrix** $I$ does nothing: $IX = X$ for every $X$. (Not the zero matrix, which sends everything to zero.) In size $n \times n$ it has 1 on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere; for example its first row is $\hat i = \langle 1, 0, 0\rangle$, whose dot product with $X$ is $x_1$ (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1).

A rotation of the plane by 90° counterclockwise is the matrix (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Rotation by 90°" tex="R = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad R\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -y \\ x \end{bmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 3, p. 1" />

It sends $\hat i$ to $\hat j$ and $\hat j$ to $-\hat i$, and $(x, y)$ to $(-y, x)$, the rotation formula from the [last lecture](/courses/18-02/determinants-and-cross-product). Its columns are the images of $\hat i$ and $\hat j$ (p. 3). Matrices make such calculations easy: $R^2 = -I$, because two quarter turns make a half turn, which sends $(x, y)$ to $(-x, -y)$; and $R^4 = I$ (p. 3).

## The inverse matrix

If $U = AX$ gives the new coordinates from the old, the reverse relation, $X$ from $U$, is again linear and given by a matrix, the **inverse** of $A$ (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The inverse matrix" tex="A A^{-1} = A^{-1} A = I" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

Only a square matrix can have an inverse (p. 3). The inverse solves linear systems: multiply $AX = B$ on the left by $A^{-1}$ (pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Solving a linear system" tex="AX = B \quad\Longrightarrow\quad X = A^{-1}AX = A^{-1}B" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–4" />

$A^{-1}$ must go on the left, where it cancels $A$; $XA$ is not even defined when $X$ is a column vector (p. 4).

**The cofactor method.** For small matrices (3 × 3, 4 × 4; computer software uses other methods for large ones) the inverse is the **adjoint matrix** divided by the determinant (p. 4; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Inverse by cofactors" tex="A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\,\operatorname{adj}(A)" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

The adjoint is found in three steps (p. 4; notes, p. 2):

1. **Minors:** for each entry, delete its row and column and take the 2 × 2 determinant that is left. For the lecture's matrix $A$ the top-left minor is $4 \cdot 2 - 5 \cdot 1 = 3$.
2. **Cofactors:** change the sign of the minors in the places marked minus on a checkerboard of signs starting with plus in the top-left corner (the same minus as on the middle term of a cross product).
3. **Transpose:** write the rows as columns.

Then divide by $\det A$. For the lecture's $A$, $\det A = 3$, and the result is (p. 4; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The lecture's inverse" tex="A^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 3 & -3 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 & -4 \\ -2 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 3, p. 2" />

It gives the old coordinates $x_1, x_2, x_3$ from the new ones (p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="A plane, and a system solved by the inverse">
<Problem>
(a) Find the equation of the plane through $P_1 = (1, 0, 0)$, $P_2 = (0, 2, 0)$ and $P_3 = (0, 0, 3)$. (b) Invert $M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and solve $MX = \langle 3, 3, 3\rangle$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A normal vector">
$\vec{P_1P_2} = \langle -1, 2, 0\rangle$ and $\vec{P_1P_3} = \langle -1, 0, 3\rangle$, so $\vec N = \langle 2 \cdot 3 - 0 \cdot 0,\ -((-1) \cdot 3 - 0 \cdot (-1)),\ (-1) \cdot 0 - 2 \cdot (-1)\rangle = \langle 6, 3, 2\rangle$. Then $\vec{P_1P} \cdot \vec N = 6(x - 1) + 3y + 2z = 0$, that is $6x + 3y + 2z = 6$. Check: $P_2$ gives $3 \cdot 2 = 6$ and $P_3$ gives $2 \cdot 3 = 6$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Minors, cofactors, transpose">
The minors are $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & -1 \\ 2 & 1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$; the checkerboard gives the cofactors $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \\ -2 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$; transposing gives $\operatorname{adj}(M) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Expanding along the first row, $\det M = 1 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot (-1) + 0 = 3$, so $M^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\operatorname{adj}(M)$. Check: the first row of $M$ times the first column of the adjoint is $1 + 2 + 0 = 3$, and times the second column $-2 + 2 + 0 = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The solution">
$X = M^{-1}\langle 3, 3, 3\rangle$: each entry is a row of the adjoint times $\langle 3, 3, 3\rangle$, divided by 3, which is the row's sum: $X = \langle 1, 1, 2\rangle$. Check: $M\langle 1, 1, 2\rangle = \langle 1 + 2,\ 1 + 2,\ 1 + 2\rangle = \langle 3, 3, 3\rangle$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Changing observers">
In special relativity, two observers moving relative to each other give the same event different coordinates, and the relation between their coordinates, the **Lorentz transformation**, is linear (when both take the same event as their origin): exactly the kind of change of coordinates a matrix describes, with the inverse matrix taking you back. Cosmology uses this in [the spacetime interval](/courses/8-286/spacetime-interval), the one combination of coordinate differences on which all such observers agree.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\vec B \times \vec A = -\vec A \times \vec B$ and $\vec A \times \vec A = \vec 0$.
- The plane through $P_1$, $P_2$, $P_3$: $\vec{P_1P} \cdot \vec N = 0$ with the normal vector $\vec N = \vec{P_1P_2} \times \vec{P_1P_3}$, the same condition as $\det(\vec{P_1P}, \vec{P_1P_2}, \vec{P_1P_3}) = 0$.
- A linear change of coordinates is a matrix product $U = AX$. Entries of $AB$ are dot products of rows of $A$ with columns of $B$, defined when the width of $A$ equals the height of $B$.
- $(AB)X = A(BX)$: first $B$, then $A$. In general $AB \ne BA$.
- $IX = X$; the rotation $R$ by 90° has $R^2 = -I$.
- $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\operatorname{adj}(A)$ (minors, cofactors, transpose), and $AX = B$ gives $X = A^{-1}B$.
