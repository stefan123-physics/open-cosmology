This set practises two lessons: [Stokes' theorem](/courses/18-02/stokes-theorem), and [Stokes' theorem, path independence and div curl = 0](/courses/18-02/stokes-and-path-independence). It also uses the [divergence theorem](/courses/18-02/divergence-theorem). It follows MIT's Problem Set 12, in our own words.

MIT's set has two parts. Part B holds a single problem of MIT's own (9 points), for which OpenCourseWare has no solutions. It is written out below, with hints and solutions of our own. Part A is a list of short exercises from MIT's own Supplementary Notes, which also hold their solutions. Those are not rewritten here: the last section of this page lists them and links to MIT's exercises and solutions. The textbook exercises that MIT also gave are left out, because the textbook may not be used here.

MIT's problem says it uses "the same tetrahedron as in Problem Set 11, Problem 2" ([Problem Set 11, Problem 2](/courses/18-02/18-02-problem-set-11#p2) on this site, where the flux of another field through its faces is found). This page does not need that set: the corners are given below, and every flux you need is computed in the solutions. MIT's sheet numbers its lectures with the day of an exam counted in, so the sheet calls Stokes' theorem Lecture 34. On this site it is Lecture 31, and the follow-up on div curl = 0 is Lecture 32.

## How to answer

Work the problem on paper first. Then choose or type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box wants a decimal number, not a fraction: write $0.5$, not $1/2$. In part (a) a choice box asks for the order of the corners; the three corners of a face can be started anywhere, so "P1, P2, P3" and "P2, P3, P1" are the same direction of travel. Part (d) asks for explanations: it has no answer box, you compare your own explanation with the solution and mark yourself. The points of the parts (2 + 2 + 3 + 2) are MIT's. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The work of a field round the four faces of a tetrahedron" points={9} mit="Part B, Problem 1">

A tetrahedron has its four corners at these points:

<Formula label="The corners" tex="P_0 = (0, 0, 0), \quad P_1 = (1, 0, 1), \quad P_2 = (1, 0, -1), \quad P_3 = (1, 1, 0)" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1, p. 1" />

A vector field is given by

<Formula label="The field" tex="\vec F = yz\,\hat j - y^2\,\hat k" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

Each of the four faces is a triangle. Name a face by its three corners, for example the face $P_0P_1P_3$. The normal vector of every face is chosen to point out of the tetrahedron.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

For each face, decide in which order of its corners you must walk round its edge so that the direction of travel is compatible with the outward normal.

<Hint>

Recall the rule from Stokes' theorem: if you walk along the boundary with the surface on your left, the normal points up, towards your head. Equivalently, with the right-hand rule, the fingers curl along the direction of travel and the thumb points along the normal.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the triangle with corners $A$, $B$, $C$ taken in this order, the vector $(B - A) \times (C - A)$ is the normal that goes with this order. Compute it, and compare it with the outward direction. Outward means: away from the fourth corner.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-012" label="Face $P_0P_1P_2$:"
  choices={["P0, P2, P1", "P0, P1, P2"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p1-a-013" label="Face $P_0P_1P_3$:"
  choices={["P0, P1, P3", "P0, P3, P1"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p1-a-023" label="Face $P_0P_2P_3$:"
  choices={["P0, P2, P3", "P0, P3, P2"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-a-123" label="Face $P_1P_2P_3$:"
  choices={["P1, P3, P2", "P1, P2, P3"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The order of the corners decides the direction of the normal through the right-hand rule. For each face, compute the normal that belongs to one order, and see whether it points away from the fourth corner. If it does not, reverse the order.

<Step title="The rule for one face">

For a triangle with corners $A$, $B$, $C$ in this order, the walk $A \to B \to C \to A$ is counterclockwise seen from the side the vector $(B - A) \times (C - A)$ points to. So this vector is the normal compatible with that order. A normal points out of the tetrahedron when it points away from the fourth corner $D$: when $\vec N \cdot (D - A) < 0$.

<Formula label="Compatibility of an order of corners with the normal" tex="\vec N = (B - A) \times (C - A), \qquad \text{outward} \iff \vec N \cdot (D - A) < 0" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 31, p. 2 (the left-hand and right-hand rules; the cross-product test is our own)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The face P0P1P3">

The order $P_0, P_1, P_3$ gives $\vec N = P_1 \times P_3 = \langle 1, 0, 1\rangle \times \langle 1, 1, 0\rangle = \langle -1, 1, 1\rangle$, since $P_0$ is the origin. The fourth corner is $P_2$, and $\vec N \cdot P_2 = -1 + 0 - 1 = -2 < 0$. So this normal points away from $P_2$: the order $P_0, P_1, P_3$ is compatible.

</Step>

<Step title="The face P0P2P3">

The order $P_0, P_2, P_3$ gives $\vec N = \langle 1, 0, -1\rangle \times \langle 1, 1, 0\rangle = \langle 1, -1, 1\rangle$. The fourth corner is $P_1$ and $\vec N \cdot P_1 = 1 + 0 + 1 = 2 > 0$: this normal points towards $P_1$, so it is inward. The reverse order $P_0, P_3, P_2$ gives the outward normal $\langle -1, 1, -1\rangle$.

</Step>

<Step title="The face P0P1P2">

The order $P_0, P_1, P_2$ gives $\vec N = \langle 1, 0, 1\rangle \times \langle 1, 0, -1\rangle = \langle 0, 2, 0\rangle$. This face lies in the plane $y = 0$ and the fourth corner $P_3$ has $y = 1$, so $\vec N \cdot P_3 = 2 > 0$: inward. The outward normal is $-\hat j$, and the compatible order is $P_0, P_2, P_1$.

</Step>

<Step title="The face P1P2P3">

The order $P_1, P_2, P_3$ gives $\vec N = (P_2 - P_1) \times (P_3 - P_1) = \langle 0, 0, -2\rangle \times \langle 0, 1, -1\rangle = \langle 2, 0, 0\rangle$. The fourth corner is $P_0$, and $\vec N \cdot (P_0 - P_1) = \langle 2, 0, 0\rangle \cdot \langle -1, 0, -1\rangle = -2 < 0$: outward. The compatible order is $P_1, P_2, P_3$. This face lies in the plane $x = 1$ and the tetrahedron is on its side $x < 1$, so the outward normal is $+\hat i$, as found.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A test of the whole list: every edge of the tetrahedron belongs to two faces, and the two faces must run along it in opposite directions (this is the point of part (d)). With the orders found, the faces run $P_0 \to P_1 \to P_3 \to P_0$, $P_0 \to P_3 \to P_2 \to P_0$, $P_0 \to P_2 \to P_1 \to P_0$ and $P_1 \to P_2 \to P_3 \to P_1$. Each of the six edges appears once in each direction, for example $P_0 \to P_1$ in the first face and $P_1 \to P_0$ in the third. A wrong order for one face would make some edge run the same way twice.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Compute the work done by $\vec F$ round the boundary of the face $P_0P_1P_3$ directly with line integrals, going round in the order found in part (a).

Give the answer as a decimal number.

<Hint>

The boundary has three straight edges: $P_0 \to P_1$, $P_1 \to P_3$ and $P_3 \to P_0$. Parametrise each one by $t$ from $0$ to $1$, as a point plus $t$ times the edge vector.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On each edge write $y$ and $z$ as functions of $t$, put them in $\vec F = \langle 0, yz, -y^2\rangle$, and take the dot product with $d\vec r/dt$. Notice that one edge lies in the plane $y = 0$, where $\vec F$ is zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="The work round the face $P_0P_1P_3$ $=$" value="0.5" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The work is the integral of $\vec F \cdot d\vec r$ along the closed path. Split the path into its three edges, parametrise each, and add.

<Step title="Edge P0 to P1">

Take $\vec r = \langle t, 0, t\rangle$ for $0 \le t \le 1$. Here $y = 0$, so $\vec F = \vec 0$ and the work is $0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Edge P1 to P3">

Take $\vec r = \langle 1, t, 1 - t\rangle$, so $d\vec r = \langle 0, 1, -1\rangle\,dt$ and $\vec F = \langle 0, t(1 - t), -t^2\rangle$:

<Formula label="Work along P1 to P3" tex="\int \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int_0^1 \left(t(1 - t) + t^2\right)dt = \int_0^1 t\,dt = \frac12" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Edge P3 to P0">

Take $\vec r = \langle 1 - s, 1 - s, 0\rangle$ for $0 \le s \le 1$, so $d\vec r = \langle -1, -1, 0\rangle\,ds$. On this edge $z = 0$, so $\vec F = \langle 0, 0, -y^2\rangle$ has only a $\hat k$ component, and its dot product with $d\vec r$ is zero. The work is $0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Add">

<Formula label="Work round the face P0P1P3" tex="\oint \vec F \cdot d\vec r = 0 + \frac12 + 0 = \frac12 = 0.5" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Part (c) gets this number again from Stokes' theorem, by a flux integral, which is a quite different calculation. Reversing the order of the corners would change the sign of every edge integral, so the sign of this answer depends on the order found in part (a).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Use Stokes' theorem to find the work of $\vec F$ round each of the four faces, going round each in the order found in part (a). The face $P_0P_1P_3$ is the one you did directly in part (b), and the result must agree.

Give each answer as a decimal number.

<Hint>

First find the curl of the field: $\nabla \times \vec F = \langle R_y - Q_z,\ P_z - R_x,\ Q_x - P_y\rangle$ for $\vec F = \langle P, Q, R\rangle$. Here $P = 0$, $Q = yz$ and $R = -y^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For each face the work is the flux of the curl through that face, with the outward normal. A face that lies in a plane $x = $ constant or $y = $ constant has a simple normal. For a tilted face write the plane as $x = $ (something in $y$ and $z$) and project the face onto the $yz$-plane: then $\hat n\,dS = \pm\langle 1, -x_y, -x_z\rangle\,dy\,dz$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Only the $\hat i$ component of the curl is not zero, so only the $\hat i$ component of $\hat n\,dS$ matters. Its size is $dy\,dz$ on every face except the one in the plane $y = 0$ (there it is zero), and its sign is that of the outward normal.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c-012" label="Work round the face $P_0P_1P_2$ $=$" value="0" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-c-012" />

<Answer id="p1-c-013" label="Work round the face $P_0P_1P_3$ $=$" value="0.5" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-c-013" />

<Answer id="p1-c-023" label="Work round the face $P_0P_2P_3$ $=$" value="0.5" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-c-023" />

<Answer id="p1-c-123" label="Work round the face $P_1P_2P_3$ $=$" value="-1" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-c-123" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Stokes' theorem turns each work integral into the flux of the curl through the face. The curl has one simple component, so each flux is a plain double integral over the projection of the face onto the $yz$-plane.

<Step title="The curl">

With $P = 0$, $Q = yz$, $R = -y^2$:

<Formula label="The curl of the field" tex="\nabla \times \vec F = \langle R_y - Q_z,\ P_z - R_x,\ Q_x - P_y\rangle = \langle -2y - y,\ 0,\ 0\rangle = \langle -3y,\ 0,\ 0\rangle" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 31, p. 2 (the formula for the curl)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Stokes' theorem">

<Formula label="Stokes' theorem" tex="\oint_C \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \int\int_S (\nabla \times \vec F) \cdot \hat n\,dS" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 31, p. 2" />

With the orders of part (a), the normal on each face is the outward one. Because the curl is $\langle -3y, 0, 0\rangle$, the flux through a face is $-3 \int\int y\,n_x\,dS$.

</Step>

<Step title="The face P0P1P2, in the plane y = 0">

The curl is $\vec 0$ there, because $y = 0$. Also $\hat n = -\hat j$ has no $\hat i$ component. The work is $0$. (A direct check: on every edge of this face $y = 0$, so $\vec F = \vec 0$.)

</Step>

<Step title="The face P1P2P3, in the plane x = 1">

Here $\hat n = \hat i$ and $dS = dy\,dz$. The face projects onto the triangle in the $yz$-plane with corners $(y, z) = (0, 1)$, $(0, -1)$, $(1, 0)$. It has area $1$ and, as for every triangle, its centre is at the average of its corners, so its mean value of $y$ is $\frac13$:

<Formula label="Flux through the face P1P2P3" tex="\int\int (-3y)\,dy\,dz = -3 \cdot \frac13 \cdot 1 = -1" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The face P0P1P3, in the plane x = y + z">

The plane through $P_0$, $P_1$, $P_3$ is $x = y + z$, so $x_y = x_z = 1$. The outward normal is $\langle -1, 1, 1\rangle$, whose $\hat i$ component is negative, so $\hat n\,dS = \langle -1, 1, 1\rangle\,dy\,dz$. The face projects onto the triangle with corners $(y, z) = (0, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0)$, of area $\frac12$ and mean $y$ equal to $\frac13$, so $\int\int y\,dy\,dz = \frac16$:

<Formula label="Flux through the face P0P1P3" tex="\int\int (-3y)(-1)\,dy\,dz = 3 \cdot \frac16 = \frac12" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

This agrees with the line integral of part (b).

</Step>

<Step title="The face P0P2P3, in the plane x = y - z">

The plane through $P_0$, $P_2$, $P_3$ is $x = y - z$. The outward normal is $\langle -1, 1, -1\rangle$, again with a negative $\hat i$ component, so $n_x\,dS = -\,dy\,dz$. The face projects onto the triangle with corners $(y, z) = (0, 0), (0, -1), (1, 0)$, with the same area and the same mean value of $y$ as before:

<Formula label="Flux through the face P0P2P3" tex="\int\int (-3y)(-1)\,dy\,dz = \frac{3}{6} = \frac12" cite="mit" at="Part B, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A direct line integral on the face $P_1P_2P_3$ (order $P_1 \to P_2 \to P_3 \to P_1$) gives the same as Stokes. On $P_1 \to P_2$ we have $y = 0$ and the work is $0$. On $P_2 \to P_3$, with $\vec r = \langle 1, s, s - 1\rangle$, the integrand is $-s$ and the integral is $-\frac12$. On $P_3 \to P_1$, with $\vec r = \langle 1, 1 - u, u\rangle$, the integrand is $-(1 - u)$ and the integral is $-\frac12$ as well. The total is $-1$, as found. The four values $0, \frac12, \frac12, -1$ add up to $0$, which part (d) explains.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

The four values of part (c) add up to zero. Explain why, in two different ways.

First, geometrically, by looking at the line integrals that are added together. Second, by using the divergence theorem to compute the flux of $\nabla \times \vec F$ out of the whole tetrahedron.

This part has no answer box. Write your two explanations, then open the solution and mark yourself.

<Hint>

For the first way, list the edges of the four faces and ask which of them appear more than once, and in which directions they are walked.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the second way, the sum of the four fluxes is the flux of the vector field $\nabla \times \vec F$ through the closed surface of the tetrahedron, with the outward normal. Which theorem turns that into a triple integral, and of which quantity?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The sum of the four works is the work round a boundary with no edge left over, and also the flux of a curl through a closed surface. Both are zero.

<Step title="(i) The edges cancel">

The work round each face is the sum of the works along its three edges. The four faces have twelve edges in all, but the tetrahedron has only six, and each of the six belongs to exactly two faces. The orders found in part (a) are exactly those that make the two faces run along a shared edge in opposite directions, as the check at the end of part (a) showed. Reversing the direction of a path along a line changes the sign of the work, so the two contributions of each edge cancel in the sum, and nothing is left:

<Formula label="The edges cancel" tex="\sum_{\text{faces}} \oint \vec F \cdot d\vec r = \sum_{\text{edges}} \left(\int_{\text{edge}} \vec F \cdot d\vec r - \int_{\text{edge}} \vec F \cdot d\vec r\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 31, p. 3 (the same cancellation inside a surface)" />

This is the same cancellation as in the lecture's argument for Stokes' theorem, where the inner edges of small pieces cancel.

</Step>

<Step title="(ii) The divergence theorem">

By Stokes' theorem each face's work is the flux of $\nabla \times \vec F$ through that face with the outward normal, so the sum is the flux of $\nabla \times \vec F$ out of the closed surface of the tetrahedron. The divergence theorem turns that into an integral over the solid, $D$, of $\operatorname{div}(\nabla \times \vec F)$:

<Formula label="The divergence theorem for the curl" tex="\int\int_{\text{closed surface}} (\nabla \times \vec F) \cdot \hat n\,dS = \int\int\int_D \operatorname{div}(\nabla \times \vec F)\,dV" cite="reading2" at="Lecture 28, p. 4" />

For any field the divergence of a curl is zero, because the second derivatives cancel in pairs: $(R_y - Q_z)_x + (P_z - R_x)_y + (Q_x - P_y)_z = 0$. For our field the curl is $\langle -3y, 0, 0\rangle$, whose divergence is $\partial(-3y)/\partial x = 0$. The integral of zero is zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Both explanations need the faces to be oriented with the outward normal, and that is where part (a) mattered: with one face in the wrong direction, the cancellation of edges in (i) would fail, and the flux in (ii) would have one inward face. A number test: the four values $0$, $\frac12$, $\frac12$ and $-1$ add to $0$, and the fluxes through the faces $y = 0$ and $x = 1$ alone, $0$ and $-1$, are balanced by the $+\frac12 + \frac12$ of the two tilted faces.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
