Flux is a second kind of line integral. Physics often meets flux through surfaces in space; that comes in a few weeks, and this lecture does the plane version first (transcript, p. 1).

## Flux across a curve

The **flux** of a vector field $\vec F$ across a plane curve $C$ is (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Flux" tex="\text{flux} = \int_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \lim_{\Delta s \to 0} \sum_i (\vec F \cdot \hat n)\,\Delta s_i" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

where $\hat n$ is the unit normal vector to $C$, rotated 90° **clockwise** from the unit tangent $\hat T$: pointing to the right as you travel along $C$ (p. 1; notes, p. 3).

The two line integrals side by side (p. 1; notes, p. 3):

- **work**, $\int_C \vec F \cdot \hat T\,ds$, adds up the tangential component: how much the motion goes with or against $\vec F$;
- **flux**, $\int_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds$, adds up the normal component: how much the field goes across the curve, to the right counted positively and to the left negatively.

For a mathematician they are set up and computed the same way; only their physical meanings differ (p. 1).

## What flux measures

Think of $\vec F$ as the **velocity field** of a fluid. Flux measures how much fluid passes through $C$ per unit time, like water through a dam with holes in it (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 3). On a small piece of $C$ of length $\Delta s$ the flow is nearly uniform, and what crosses it in a short unit of time fills a parallelogram with one side along $C$ and the other side $\vec F$. Its area is base times height (p. 1; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Flow through a small piece" tex="\text{area} = \Delta s\,(\vec F \cdot \hat n)" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Adding up gives the total flow per unit time. It is the **net** flow: what crosses from left to right counts positively, from right to left negatively (pp. 1–2). (In a round tank of rotating fluid, the flux across a diameter is zero: one half flows each way, p. 2.)

## Two geometric examples

Take $C$ the circle of radius $a$ about the origin, counterclockwise. Then $\hat n$, to the right, points **out**: for a closed counterclockwise curve, flux counts what flows out of the region (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 3).

- $\vec F = x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j$: on the circle, $\vec F$ is radial, parallel to $\hat n$, with length $a$. So $\vec F \cdot \hat n = a$ and (p. 2; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Flux out of a circle" tex="\oint_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \oint_C a\,ds = a \times 2\pi a = 2\pi a^2" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

  Positive, since fluid flows out.
- $\vec F = -y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j$: tangent to the circle, no normal component, so the flux is 0: the fluid just goes round (p. 2; notes, p. 3).

## Flux in coordinates

For a general curve, use components (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 3). A small step along $C$ is $\hat T\,\Delta s = \langle \Delta x, \Delta y\rangle$; rotating it 90° clockwise gives $\hat n\,\Delta s = \langle \Delta y, -\Delta x\rangle$. In the limit, $\hat n\,ds = \langle dy, -dx\rangle$ (p. 2). With $\vec F = \langle P, Q\rangle$ (new letters, to mark a different integral) (p. 3; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Flux in components" tex="\int_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \int_C \langle P, Q\rangle \cdot \langle dy, -dx\rangle = \int_C -Q\,dx + P\,dy" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

With $\vec F = \langle M, N\rangle$ it is $\int_C -N\,dx + M\,dy$; Auroux knows no trick for remembering where the minus sign goes (p. 3; the formula is also in the notes, p. 4). Then compute as usual: express $x$, $y$, $dx$, $dy$ in one parameter (p. 3).

## Green's theorem for flux

If $C$ encloses $R$ counterclockwise and $\vec F = \langle P, Q\rangle$ is defined and differentiable on all of $R$, then (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Green's theorem, normal form" tex="\oint_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \int\int_R \operatorname{div}\vec F\,dA, \qquad \operatorname{div}\vec F = P_x + Q_y" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 23, p. 4" />

The **divergence** is easier to remember than the curl: no signs, no swapping (p. 3). The left side is the flux **out** of $R$. This is Green's theorem in **normal form**; last lecture's was the tangential form (p. 3).

**Proof by renaming.** $\oint -Q\,dx + P\,dy$ is $\oint M\,dx + N\,dy$ with $M = -Q$ and $N = P$, so by the [tangential form](/courses/18-02/greens-theorem) it equals $\int\int (N_x - M_y)\,dA = \int\int (P_x + Q_y)\,dA$ (p. 3; notes, p. 4). (This is why the letters $P$, $Q$ were used. In $M$, $N$: $\oint -N\,dx + M\,dy = \int\int (M_x + N_y)\,dA$, p. 3.)

**The example again.** For $\vec F = x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j$, $\operatorname{div}\vec F = 1 + 1 = 2$, so the flux out of the disc is $\int\int 2\,dA = 2\pi a^2$, as before. And now the circle can be moved anywhere: part of the boundary then has inflow and part outflow, hard to compute directly, but the total is still twice the area, $2\pi a^2$ (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 4).

## What divergence means

Divergence measures how much things are **diverging**: a uniform translation and a rotation have divergence 0, while expanding motions have positive divergence and contracting ones negative (transcript, p. 4). For a gas, it measures how much the flow expands areas. For an incompressible fluid like water, which cannot expand, it is the **source rate**: how much fluid is added per unit area per unit time (p. 4; notes, p. 4). For $x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j$, divergence 2 everywhere means pumps adding water uniformly everywhere, since a single spring at the origin would give a flow that slows down with distance, while this one speeds up (p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="Flux three ways">
<Problem>
(a) Find the flux of $\vec F = \langle x, 0\rangle$ out of the unit square $0 \le x, y \le 1$, directly and by Green's theorem. (b) Find the flux of $\vec G = \langle 1, 0\rangle$ across the segment from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$. (c) Find the flux of $\vec K = \langle x^2, y^2\rangle$ out of the same square.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Directly and by divergence">
Directly: on the right side $x = 1$, $\hat n = \hat i$ and $\vec F \cdot \hat n = 1$ over length 1; on the left side $\vec F = 0$; on the top and bottom $\vec F$ is horizontal, along the sides. Flux 1. By Green: $\operatorname{div}\vec F = 1$, so the flux is the area, 1.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) An open curve">
Parametrise $x = y = t$, $0 \le t \le 1$; $P = 1$, $Q = 0$, so $\int -Q\,dx + P\,dy = \int_0^1 dt = 1$. Positive: travelling north-east, the right is south-east, and the flow goes east, across to the right.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A non-uniform field">
$\operatorname{div}\vec K = 2x + 2y$, and $\int_0^1\int_0^1 (2x + 2y)\,dx\,dy = 1 + 1 = 2$. Check directly: only the right side ($x = 1$, $\vec K \cdot \hat n = 1$) and the top ($y = 1$, $\vec K \cdot \hat n = 1$) contribute, each 1, so the flux is 2.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The Hubble flow has the same divergence everywhere">
The lecture's expanding field $x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j$, multiplied by a constant $H$, is the velocity field of a homogeneously expanding universe: Hubble's law $\vec v = H\vec r$. Its divergence, $2H$ in the plane and $3H$ in space, is the same at every point. For the universe the right reading is the lecture's first one, the flow expanding areas (or volumes), not pumps adding matter: the same amount of matter spreads over more volume, so its density falls, at the rate $\dot\rho = -3H\rho$. See [the homogeneous expanding universe](/courses/8-286/homogeneous-expansion) for the Hubble flow and [the expanding sphere](/courses/8-286/expanding-sphere) for the falling density.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Flux across $C$: $\int_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds$, with $\hat n$ 90° clockwise from $\hat T$ (to the right). Work adds the tangential component, flux the normal one.
- For a velocity field, flux is the net amount of fluid crossing $C$ per unit time.
- In components, $\hat n\,ds = \langle dy, -dx\rangle$, so the flux of $\langle P, Q\rangle$ is $\int_C -Q\,dx + P\,dy$.
- Green's theorem, normal form: $\oint_C \vec F \cdot \hat n\,ds = \int\int_R (P_x + Q_y)\,dA$ for $C$ counterclockwise (flux out of $R$).
- Divergence measures expansion: for an incompressible fluid, the source rate. For $x\hat i + y\hat j$ it is 2, so the flux out of any circle of radius $a$ is $2\pi a^2$.
