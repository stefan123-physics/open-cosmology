So far the course has done geometry in space: vectors, planes, motions. Calculus is about functions, and the next unit, about three weeks, studies **functions of several variables** and their derivatives (transcript, p. 1).

## Functions of two variables

A function of two variables gives a number $f(x, y)$ for each pair of values $x$, $y$, for example $f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2$ (transcript, p. 1). Like functions of one variable, some are only defined on part of the plane: $\sqrt y$ needs $y \ge 0$, $1/(x + y)$ needs $x + y \ne 0$ (p. 1). Real-world examples depend on more variables still: the temperature at a place depends on longitude and latitude, on elevation, and on time (p. 1). The course mostly uses two or three variables, because they can be pictured, but the tools (partial derivatives, the gradient) work the same in any number (p. 1).

## The graph

The **graph** of $f$ is the surface $z = f(x, y)$: above each point $(x, y)$ of the plane, the point at height $f(x, y)$ (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1). To draw it by hand, slice it with coordinate planes (p. 1; notes, p. 1).

- $f(x, y) = -y$: the graph $z = -y$ is a plane. In the $yz$-plane it is a line going down with slope 1, and changing $x$ changes nothing, so the plane contains the $x$-axis (p. 1).
- $f(x, y) = 1 - x^2 - y^2$: the slice $x = 0$ is the downward parabola $z = 1 - y^2$, the slice $y = 0$ is $z = 1 - x^2$, and the graph meets the $xy$-plane where $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, the unit circle. It is a surface of revolution about the $z$-axis, a dome (pp. 1–2).

On a computer, $z = x^2 + y^2$ is the same shape opening upwards, a bowl, and $z = y^2 - x^2$ is a **saddle**: an upward parabola in the $yz$-plane and a downward one in the $xz$-plane (p. 2; notes, p. 1). But pictures of surfaces are hard to draw and to read (p. 2).

## Contour plots

A **contour plot** is a map instead: it draws in the $xy$-plane the **level curves** where $f$ takes fixed values, usually evenly spaced (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Level curve" tex="f(x, y) = c" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 8, p. 1" />

It is the graph sliced by the horizontal planes $z = c$, with all slices pressed flat onto one map (p. 2). Walking along a level curve keeps your height constant, which is exactly what a **topographic map** shows; a coloured temperature map works the same way, with the boundaries between colours as level curves (p. 2; notes, p. 1). Reading the value of $f$ at a point from the nearby curves is much faster than from a graph (p. 2).

The two examples again (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 1):

- $f = -y$: the level curve $f = c$ is the horizontal line $y = -c$. The lines are evenly spaced, because $f$ changes at a constant rate as $y$ changes, and nothing happens along $x$ (p. 2).
- $f = 1 - (x^2 + y^2)$: the level $0$ is the unit circle, the level $1$ is the single point at the origin, the level $-1$ the circle of radius $\sqrt 2 \approx 1.4$, the level $-2$ radius $\sqrt 3 \approx 1.7$, the level $-3$ radius 2 (p. 3). Concentric circles show a surface of revolution, and circles getting closer together show it getting steeper: the top is flat, the sides steep (p. 3).

The contour plot of $x^2 + y^2$ looks the same; only the labels show that it increases outwards (p. 3). For $y^2 - x^2$ the level $0$ is the two diagonal lines $y = \pm x$, crossing at the saddle point like the paths that stay level through a mountain pass (p. 3).

## Reading changes from a contour plot

Moving east–west on the map changes $x$, moving north–south changes $y$. A contour plot shows at once whether $f$ increases or decreases (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1). In the lecture's sketch, at a point $(x_0, y_0)$ on the level $f = 1$ with the level $2$ to the right and above, increasing $x$ (or $y$) increases $f$, and decreasing it decreases $f$ (p. 3). To say **how fast** $f$ changes, we need derivatives (p. 3).

## Partial derivatives

For a function of one variable, $f'(x) = df/dx$ is the limit of $\Delta f/\Delta x$, the slope of the tangent line to the graph, and it gives the approximation $f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0)$ (transcript, p. 3). With two variables we can change $x$, or $y$, or both, and $f$ responds differently to each. So there is one derivative per variable, written with the curly $\partial$ ("partial"), not $d$ and not $\Delta$ (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Partial derivative in x" tex="\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x_0, y_0) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x, y_0) - f(x_0, y_0)}{\Delta x}" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

$y$ does not change at all; $\partial f/\partial y$ is defined the same way with $x$ fixed and $y$ changing (p. 4). The function is **differentiable** when these exist, which is the case for most functions in the course (p. 4).

**Geometrically,** fixing $y = y_0$ slices the graph by a vertical plane parallel to the $xz$-plane. The slice is a curve, and $\partial f/\partial x$ is the slope of its tangent line; $\partial f/\partial y$ is the slope of the slice $x = x_0$ (p. 4; notes, p. 1).

**To compute,** treat $y$ as a constant and differentiate in $x$; for $f_y$ the other way round. The notations $\partial f/\partial x$ and $f_x$ mean the same (p. 4; notes, p. 1). The lecture's example (p. 4; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="An example" tex="f(x, y) = x^3 y + y^2: \qquad f_x = 3x^2 y, \qquad f_y = x^3 + 2y" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

In $f_x$, $x^3$ times the constant $y$ gives $3x^2 y$, and $y^2$ is a constant with derivative 0; in $f_y$, $x^3$ is the constant factor (p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="Partial derivatives and a first estimate">
<Problem>
Let $f(x, y) = x^2 y + 3y^2$. (a) Find $f_x$ and $f_y$ at $(1, 2)$. (b) Estimate $f(1.1, 2)$ from $f_x$, and compare with the exact value. (c) Describe the level curve $f = 14$ near $(1, 2)$: if $x$ increases a little, must $y$ increase or decrease to stay on it?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Treat the other variable as a constant">
$f_x = 2xy$, which is $2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 4$ at $(1, 2)$. $f_y = x^2 + 6y$, which is $1 + 12 = 13$. And $f(1, 2) = 2 + 12 = 14$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Change only x">
Moving $\Delta x = 0.1$ with $y$ fixed, $f$ changes by about $f_x\,\Delta x = 4 \times 0.1 = 0.4$: $f(1.1, 2) \approx 14.4$. Exactly, $f(1.1, 2) = 1.21 \times 2 + 12 = 14.42$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Staying on the level curve">
Both partial derivatives are positive, so increasing $x$ raises $f$ and increasing $y$ raises it too. To keep $f = 14$ when $x$ increases, $y$ must **decrease**: the level curve slopes downwards through $(1, 2)$, as in the lecture's sketch.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A map of the whole sky">
The temperature of the cosmic microwave background depends on the direction you look, given by two angles on the sky: a function of two variables. Its maps are colour-coded contour plots like the weather maps in this lecture, with the boundaries between colours as level curves. The temperature is the same in every direction to about one part in 100,000, which is the starting point of [the horizon problem](/courses/8-286/horizon-problem); the spectrum is on [the CMB spectrum](/courses/8-286/cmb-spectrum) page.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A function of two variables can be pictured by its graph $z = f(x, y)$, drawn slice by slice, or by its contour plot.
- Level curves $f(x, y) = c$ are horizontal slices of the graph; evenly spaced levels that crowd together mean a steep graph.
- The partial derivative $\partial f/\partial x = f_x$ is the rate of change of $f$ when only $x$ changes: the slope of the slice $y = y_0$. Likewise $f_y$.
- To compute $f_x$, treat $y$ as a constant: for $f = x^3 y + y^2$, $f_x = 3x^2 y$ and $f_y = x^3 + 2y$.
