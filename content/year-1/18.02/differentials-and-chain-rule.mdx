After partial derivatives and extremes, the next step is to describe how a function of several variables changes in every way at once. The tool is the **differential** (transcript, p. 1).

## Differentials in one variable

In one variable, $y = f(x)$ gives $dy = f'(x)\,dx$, relating small changes in $y$ and $x$; this is implicit differentiation (transcript, p. 1). It finds $dy/dx$ and also its reciprocal. For $y = \sin^{-1}x$: $x = \sin y$, so $dx = \cos y\,dy$, and (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Derivative of the inverse sine" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{\cos y} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

(18.01 did the same in [implicit differentiation](/courses/18-01/implicit-differentiation).)

## The total differential

For a function of several variables, the **total differential** collects all the contributions that can make $f$ change (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Total differential" tex="df = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy + f_z\,dz = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}dx + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}dy + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}dz" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Differentials are a new kind of object: not numbers, not vectors, not matrices, with their own rules. In particular **$df$ is not the same as $\Delta f$**: $\Delta f$ is a number once $\Delta x$, $\Delta y$, $\Delta z$ are, while $df$ can only be expressed in terms of other differentials (p. 1; notes, p. 1). (Auroux warns that the course textbook mixes $d$ and $\Delta$ in a few formulas, and says not to, p. 1.) A differential records how changes in $x$, $y$, $z$ affect $f$, and it can be used in two ways (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

1. **As a placeholder** for small changes: replacing $dx$, $dy$, $dz$ by $\Delta x$, $\Delta y$, $\Delta z$ gives the approximation formula, the tangent plane, $\Delta f \approx f_x\,\Delta x + f_y\,\Delta y + f_z\,\Delta z$. Note $=$ for differentials but $\approx$ for the numbers.
2. **Divided by $dt$**: if $x$, $y$, $z$ depend on a parameter $t$, so does $f$, and its rate of change is the **chain rule** (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Chain rule" tex="\frac{df}{dt} = f_x\,\frac{dx}{dt} + f_y\,\frac{dy}{dt} + f_z\,\frac{dz}{dt}" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 11, p. 1" />

## Why the chain rule holds

**First argument:** $dx = x'(t)\,dt$, $dy = y'(t)\,dt$, $dz = z'(t)\,dt$; substituting into $df$ gives $df = (f_x x'(t) + f_y y'(t) + f_z z'(t))\,dt$, and the coefficient of $dt$ is $df/dt$ (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1). But that uses differentials before we trust them (p. 2).

**Better:** start from the approximation formula, which we trust, for the changes in a short time $\Delta t$, and divide by the number $\Delta t$ (p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Dividing the approximation by Δt" tex="\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta t} \approx f_x\,\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} + f_y\,\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t} + f_z\,\frac{\Delta z}{\Delta t}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

As $\Delta t \to 0$ each ratio tends to a derivative and the approximation becomes exact (p. 2).

**Check on an example.** The lecture takes $w = x^2 y + z$ with $x = t$, $y = e^t$, $z = \sin t$ (p. 2; notes, p. 1). The chain rule gives $dw/dt = 2xy \cdot 1 + x^2 \cdot e^t + 1 \cdot \cos t = 2te^t + t^2e^t + \cos t$. Substituting first, $w = t^2e^t + \sin t$, and the one-variable product rule gives the same (p. 2). Here both ways are equally long; when $f$ is complicated, or only its partial derivatives are known, the chain rule is the way (p. 2).

## Product and quotient rules

The chain rule explains the rules of single-variable calculus (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1). Take $f = uv$ as a function of two variables, with $u$ and $v$ functions of $t$. Since $f_u = v$ and $f_v = u$ (p. 2):

<Formula label="Product rule from the chain rule" tex="\frac{d(uv)}{dt} = f_u\,\frac{du}{dt} + f_v\,\frac{dv}{dt} = v\,\frac{du}{dt} + u\,\frac{dv}{dt}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

Likewise $g = u/v$ has $g_u = 1/v$ and $g_v = -u/v^2$, so $\frac{d}{dt}\frac uv = \frac1v\frac{du}{dt} - \frac{u}{v^2}\frac{dv}{dt}$, which over the common denominator $v^2$ is the usual quotient rule (pp. 2–3; notes, p. 1).

## The chain rule with new variables

Now let $w = f(x, y)$ where $x$ and $y$ are themselves functions of **two** variables $u$ and $v$, as when switching between rectangular and polar coordinates. To find $\partial w/\partial u$ without substituting (possibly messy) formulas, we need $f_x$, $f_y$ and the partials $x_u$, $x_v$, $y_u$, $y_v$ (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1). Differentials do it (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Substituting differentials" tex="dw = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy = f_x(x_u\,du + x_v\,dv) + f_y(y_u\,du + y_v\,dv) = (f_x x_u + f_y y_u)\,du + (f_x x_v + f_y y_v)\,dv" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

The coefficient of $du$ is the rate of change with $u$ when $v$ is held fixed, so (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Chain rule in two new variables" tex="\frac{\partial f}{\partial u} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial u} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial u}, \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial v} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial v} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial v}" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

The structure is easy to remember: $f$ depends on $x$ and $y$, which depend on $u$. Changing $u$ changes $x$ at the rate $\partial x/\partial u$, which changes $f$ at $\partial f/\partial x$ times that; $y$ changes at the same time, and the two effects add (p. 3). More variables just mean more terms, and repeated substitutions can be done one chain rule at a time (pp. 3–4).

**Do not "simplify".** Cancelling the $\partial x$'s and $\partial y$'s would give $\partial f/\partial u = \partial f/\partial u + \partial f/\partial u$, which is absurd. Partial derivatives are not fractions, because each holds different variables fixed; the curly $\partial$ is there to warn against this (p. 3; notes, p. 1). And "$\partial f$" alone means nothing; only $\partial f/\partial(\text{a variable})$ does, while $df$ is the total change (p. 4).

**Polar coordinates.** With $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$, a function of $x$ and $y$ becomes a function of $r$ and $\theta$, and (p. 4; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The r-derivative in polar coordinates" tex="f_r = f_x\,x_r + f_y\,y_r = \cos\theta\,f_x + \sin\theta\,f_y" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

and $f_\theta$ the same way. (Next lecture: the **gradient**, a vector whose components are the partial derivatives, p. 4.)

<WorkedExample title="A chain rule along a circle, and a polar check">
<Problem>
(a) Let $w = xy^2$ with $x = \cos t$, $y = \sin t$. Find $dw/dt$ at $t = \pi/4$ by the chain rule and by substitution. (b) For $f = x^2 + y^2$, find $f_r$ from the polar formula and check it directly.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Chain rule">
$w_x = y^2$, $w_y = 2xy$, $dx/dt = -\sin t$, $dy/dt = \cos t$. At $t = \pi/4$, $x = y = \sqrt2/2 \approx 0.7071$, so $dw/dt = y^2(-\sin t) + 2xy\cos t = 0.5(-0.7071) + 2(0.5)(0.7071) = -0.3536 + 0.7071 \approx 0.354$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Substitution">
$w = \cos t\sin^2 t$, so $dw/dt = -\sin^3 t + 2\sin t\cos^2 t$. At $t = \pi/4$: $-0.3536 + 0.7071 \approx 0.354$. The same.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Polar coordinates">
$f_x = 2x$, $f_y = 2y$, so $f_r = \cos\theta \cdot 2r\cos\theta + \sin\theta \cdot 2r\sin\theta = 2r$. Directly, $f = r^2$ in polar coordinates, and $df/dr = 2r$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Densities in an expanding universe">
Cosmology uses the chain rule constantly, because quantities depend on time through the scale factor $a(t)$. For matter, the density is $\rho \propto a^{-3}$, so $\frac{d\rho}{dt} = \frac{d\rho}{da}\frac{da}{dt} = -3\frac{\rho}{a}\,\dot a = -3H\rho$, with $H = \dot a/a$. Guth derives this, and the general version $\dot\rho = -3H(\rho + p/c^2)$, in [matter–radiation equality](/courses/8-286/matter-radiation-equality); the general version is used again in [the cosmological constant](/courses/8-286/cosmological-constant) to show why vacuum energy needs a negative pressure.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The total differential $df = f_x\,dx + f_y\,dy + f_z\,dz$ is not a number; $df \ne \Delta f$.
- Replacing $dx, dy, dz$ by small changes gives the approximation $\Delta f \approx f_x\Delta x + f_y\Delta y + f_z\Delta z$; dividing by $dt$ gives the chain rule $df/dt = f_x\,dx/dt + f_y\,dy/dt + f_z\,dz/dt$.
- The chain rule gives the product and quotient rules.
- With new variables: $\partial f/\partial u = f_x x_u + f_y y_u$ (and likewise for $v$); in polar coordinates $f_r = \cos\theta f_x + \sin\theta f_y$.
- Partial derivatives cannot be cancelled like fractions.
