Polar coordinates are one change of variables. This lecture handles any change of variables in a double integral: how to substitute (transcript, p. 1).

## A simple rescaling: the ellipse

Find the area of the ellipse $(x/a)^2 + (y/b)^2 < 1$ with semi-axes $a$ and $b$ (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1). Setting up $x$, $y$ bounds is unpleasant; but an ellipse is a squashed circle. Put $u = x/a$ and $v = y/b$; the region becomes the unit disc $u^2 + v^2 < 1$. Here $x$ and $y$ are changed separately, so $du = \frac1a dx$, $dv = \frac1b dy$, and $du\,dv = \frac{1}{ab}\,dx\,dy$ (p. 1; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Area of an ellipse" tex="\int\int_{(x/a)^2 + (y/b)^2 < 1} dx\,dy = \int\int_{u^2 + v^2 < 1} ab\,du\,dv = ab \cdot \pi = \pi ab" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

When $u$ and $v$ both depend on $x$ and $y$, more care is needed: what is the **scale factor** between $du\,dv$ and $dx\,dy$? (p. 1; notes, p. 1.)

## A linear change: the factor is a determinant

Take $u = 3x - 2y$, $v = x + y$, chosen perhaps to simplify the integrand or the bounds (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1). A small $xy$ rectangle of area $\Delta A = \Delta x\,\Delta y$ becomes, in the $uv$-plane, a **parallelogram** of area $\Delta A'$: a linear map, like a matrix, sends straight lines to straight lines and parallel lines to parallel lines (p. 1). And the ratio is the same everywhere, so the unit square will do (pp. 1–2; notes, p. 1). Its corners $(0, 0)$, $(1, 0)$, $(0, 1)$, $(1, 1)$ go to $(0, 0)$, $(3, 1)$, $(-2, 1)$, $(1, 2)$: a parallelogram with sides $\langle 3, 1\rangle$ and $\langle -2, 1\rangle$, whose area is a [determinant](/courses/18-02/determinants-and-cross-product) (p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The scale factor of a linear change" tex="\begin{vmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 3 + 2 = 5 = \begin{vmatrix} 3 & -2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{vmatrix}, \qquad du\,dv = 5\,dx\,dy" cite="reading2" at="p. 2; second form: notes, p. 1" />

So $\int\int \dots\,dx\,dy = \int\int \dots\,\frac15\,du\,dv$, with the integrand and the bounds also rewritten in $u$ and $v$ (p. 2; notes, p. 1).

## The Jacobian

For a general change of variables the exchange rate varies from point to point (like changing dollars to euros in different places, as Auroux puts it), but near a point the **linear approximation** applies (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Linear approximation" tex="\begin{bmatrix} \Delta u \\ \Delta v \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta y \end{bmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

The sides $\langle \Delta x, 0\rangle$ and $\langle 0, \Delta y\rangle$ of a small rectangle go to $\langle u_x\Delta x, v_x\Delta x\rangle$ and $\langle u_y\Delta y, v_y\Delta y\rangle$, and the area of that parallelogram is the determinant times $\Delta x\,\Delta y$ (p. 2). The determinant of a linear transformation is how it scales areas (p. 2). This defines the **Jacobian** (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Jacobian" tex="J = \frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = \begin{vmatrix} u_x & u_y \\ v_x & v_y \end{vmatrix}, \qquad du\,dv = |J|\,dx\,dy" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

$\partial(u, v)/\partial(x, y)$ is only a notation, not a partial derivative of anything (p. 3). The **absolute value** is needed because a determinant is the area only up to sign: a Jacobian of $-10$ still means $du\,dv = 10\,dx\,dy$ (p. 3; notes, p. 1). Rows or columns, the determinant is the same (pp. 2–3).

**Polar coordinates.** With $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Jacobian of polar coordinates" tex="\frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(r, \theta)} = \begin{vmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{vmatrix} = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 18, p. 1" />

so $dx\,dy = r\,dr\,d\theta$ ($r \ge 0$), a second proof of the [polar area element](/courses/18-02/polar-double-integrals) (p. 3).

**Both directions.** The relation works either way, and the two Jacobians are inverses: $\frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} \cdot \frac{\partial(x, y)}{\partial(u, v)} = 1$, because the two matrices are inverse matrices. So compute whichever is easier (p. 3). But in general the factor is a function of the variables, not a constant (p. 3).

## A complete example

The lecture's deliberately silly example: compute $\int_0^1\int_0^1 x^2y\,dx\,dy$ with $u = x$, $v = xy$, where the substitution simplifies nothing but shows every step (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 1).

**1. Area element.** $\frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ y & x \end{vmatrix} = x$, positive on the square, so $du\,dv = x\,dx\,dy$ and $dx\,dy = \frac1x\,du\,dv$ (p. 4; notes, p. 1).

**2. Integrand.** $x^2y\,dx\,dy = x^2y\,\frac1x\,du\,dv = xy\,du\,dv = v\,du\,dv$ (p. 4; notes, p. 1).

**3. Bounds**, the hardest part (p. 4; notes, pp. 1–2). Integrating $du$ first means moving along curves $v = xy = $ constant, hyperbolas, on which $u = x$ increases. A hyperbola enters the square through the top side $y = 1$, where $y = v/u = 1$, so $u = v$; it leaves through the right side $x = 1$, where $u = 1$. The first hyperbola is $v = 0$, the last $v = 1$ (through the corner) (p. 4):

<Formula label="The new integral" tex="\int_0^1\int_0^1 x^2y\,dx\,dy = \int_0^1\int_v^1 v\,du\,dv = \int_0^1 v(1 - v)\,dv = \frac16" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 18, p. 2" />

**Another way to the bounds:** draw the region in the $uv$-plane. The side $x = 1$ becomes $u = 1$; $y = 1$ becomes $u = v$; $y = 0$ becomes $v = 0$; and the whole side $x = 0$ is squashed to the single point $u = v = 0$ (as the origin is in polar coordinates). The square becomes a triangle, and the bounds can be read off it (p. 4; notes, p. 2). (The final evaluation, $\frac16$, is ours; the lecture stops at the bounds. Computed directly, $\int_0^1\int_0^1 x^2y\,dx\,dy = \frac13 \cdot \frac12$ gives the same.)

<WorkedExample title="A tilted square, and an ellipse">
<Problem>
(a) Compute $\int\int_R (x + y)^2\,dA$ over the tilted square $R$: $0 \le x + y \le 2$, $0 \le x - y \le 2$. (b) Find the area of the ellipse $x^2/9 + y^2/4 < 1$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Jacobian">
Let $u = x + y$, $v = x - y$, so $R$ becomes the square $0 \le u, v \le 2$. $\frac{\partial(u, v)}{\partial(x, y)} = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = -2$, so $du\,dv = 2\,dx\,dy$ and $dx\,dy = \frac12\,du\,dv$. (Check: the area of $R$ is $\frac12 \cdot 4 = 2$; it is a square with side $\sqrt2$.)
</Step>
<Step title="(a) The integral">
$\int\int_R (x + y)^2\,dA = \int_0^2\int_0^2 u^2\,\frac12\,du\,dv = \frac12 \cdot \frac83 \cdot 2 = \frac83 \approx 2.67$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Rescaling">
With $a = 3$ and $b = 2$, the area is $\pi ab = 6\pi \approx 18.85$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Comoving and physical volumes">
In an expanding universe the physical position of a galaxy is $a(t)$ times its fixed comoving position, a change of variables that rescales all three coordinates by $a$. By the three-dimensional version of today's rule, its Jacobian is $a^3$, so a region of comoving volume $V$ has physical volume $a^3 V$, and the mass density of matter falls as $1/a^3$; see [the expanding sphere](/courses/8-286/expanding-sphere).
</Callout>

## Summary

- A change of variables needs a scale factor between area elements: $du\,dv = |J|\,dx\,dy$.
- The Jacobian $J = \partial(u, v)/\partial(x, y)$ is the determinant of the matrix of partial derivatives $u_x, u_y, v_x, v_y$: small rectangles become small parallelograms. Take its absolute value.
- For a linear change the factor is constant (5 for $u = 3x - 2y$, $v = x + y$); in general it varies from point to point.
- Polar coordinates: $\partial(x, y)/\partial(r, \theta) = r$. The two Jacobians of a change are inverses.
- Steps: area element, integrand, bounds (slice in the $xy$ picture, or draw the region in the $uv$-plane).
