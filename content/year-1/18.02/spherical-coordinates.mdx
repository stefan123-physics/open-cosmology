After rectangular and [cylindrical coordinates](/courses/18-02/triple-integrals), the third system for triple integrals: spherical coordinates, a space version of polar coordinates (transcript, p. 1).

## Spherical coordinates

A point is located by its distance to the origin and two angles (transcript, pp. 1–2; notes, p. 1):

- $\rho$ (rho, the Greek r), the **distance from the origin**, $0 \le \rho < \infty$;
- $\varphi$ (phi), the **angle down from the positive $z$-axis**: 0 on the positive $z$-axis, $\pi/2$ on the $xy$-plane, $\pi$ on the negative $z$-axis;
- $\theta$, the same angle as in cylindrical coordinates, counterclockwise from the $x$-axis.

On a sphere $\rho = a$, $\varphi$ is like **latitude**, except that it is 0 at the North Pole and $\pi$ at the South Pole, and $\theta$ like **longitude** (p. 2; notes, p. 1). Another way to see them: in the vertical half-plane at angle $\theta$, with axes $r$ (distance from the $z$-axis) and $z$, $\rho$ and $\varphi$ are polar coordinates, with the angle measured from the $z$-axis (pp. 2–3). So (p. 3; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Spherical to cylindrical and rectangular" tex="z = \rho\cos\varphi, \quad r = \rho\sin\varphi, \qquad x = \rho\sin\varphi\cos\theta, \quad y = \rho\sin\varphi\sin\theta, \qquad \rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

These coordinates, like cylindrical ones, favour the $z$-axis, so a symmetric problem should be centred on it (p. 3).

**Some surfaces** (p. 4; notes, p. 1): $\rho = a$ is the sphere of radius $a$ about the origin. $\varphi = \pi/4$ is a **cone**: at 45° from the $z$-axis, $\cos\varphi = \sin\varphi$, so $z = r$. In general $\varphi = $ constant is a cone about the $z$-axis, and $\varphi = \pi/2$ is the flattest of cones, the $xy$-plane; $\varphi < \pi/2$ is the upper half-space (p. 4).

## The volume element

First the **area** of a small piece of the sphere of radius $a$, between $\varphi$ and $\varphi + \Delta\varphi$ and between $\theta$ and $\theta + \Delta\theta$: the grid of a globe (transcript, pp. 5–6; notes, p. 1). Its east–west side is an arc of a parallel, a circle of radius $r = a\sin\varphi$, so its length is $a\sin\varphi\,\Delta\theta$. Its north–south side is an arc of a meridian, a great circle of radius $a$, of length $a\,\Delta\varphi$ (p. 6). So (p. 7; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Surface element of a sphere" tex="\Delta S \approx a^2\sin\varphi\,\Delta\varphi\,\Delta\theta, \qquad dS = a^2\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta" cite="reading2" at="p. 7" />

Adding a thickness $\Delta\rho$ between two spheres gives a small box of volume $\Delta S\,\Delta\rho$, with $a$ now $\rho$ (p. 7; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Volume element in spherical coordinates" tex="dV = \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 26, p. 1" />

Integrating in this order, $\rho$ first, means shooting a ray from the origin in a fixed direction $(\varphi, \theta)$ and finding where it is in the solid; then how far north and south the solid extends; then around the $z$-axis (p. 5).

## Example: a cap of the ball

Last lecture's slanted cap, rotated to sit on the $z$-axis: the part of the unit ball above the plane $z = 1/\sqrt2$ (transcript, p. 8; notes, p. 1). A ray in direction $(\varphi, \theta)$ enters the region through the plane, where $\rho\cos\varphi = 1/\sqrt2$, that is $\rho = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\sec\varphi$, and leaves through the sphere $\rho = 1$ (pp. 8–9). The rays that meet the cap have $0 \le \varphi \le \pi/4$: at the edge, $\rho = 1$ and $\cos\varphi = 1/\sqrt2$; a slice by a vertical half-plane shows the 45° angle too (pp. 9–10; notes, p. 1). And $\theta$ goes all the way around (p. 10):

<Formula label="Volume of the cap" tex="V = \int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/4}\int_{\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\sec\varphi}^{1} \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta = \frac{2\pi}{3} - \frac{5\pi}{6\sqrt2}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 10–11" />

(Evaluation left as an exercise; it involves $\sec^3\varphi$ and a substitution, p. 11.) A horizontal plane has an odd-looking equation, like a line not through the origin in polar coordinates (p. 10). Spherical coordinates also suit averages of the distance $\rho$ from the origin, and moments of inertia about the $z$-axis (p. 11).

## Gravitational attraction

Two masses attract each other with a force along the line between them, proportional to both masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance (transcript, p. 12; notes, p. 1). A solid attracts as the sum of all its pieces (the ground below pulls an object down, the far side of the Earth pulls it too, and the total points down) (p. 12). Put the test mass $m$ at the origin; a small mass $\Delta M$ at $(x, y, z)$, at distance $\rho$, pulls it with magnitude $G\,\Delta M\,m/\rho^2$ towards $\langle x, y, z\rangle/\rho$ (pp. 12–13; notes, p. 1). With $\Delta M = \delta\,\Delta V$ (p. 13; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Gravitational force of a solid" tex="\vec F = \int\int\int_R \frac{G\,m\,\langle x, y, z\rangle}{\rho^3}\,\delta\,dV" cite="reading2" at="p. 13" />

(a vector integral, component by component). In rectangular coordinates $\rho^3 = (x^2 + y^2 + z^2)^{3/2}$ is unpleasant (p. 13). Instead place the solid so that the $z$-axis is an axis of symmetry: then by symmetry the force is along the $z$-axis, and in spherical coordinates the powers of $\rho$ cancel (p. 14; notes, pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="The z component" tex="F_z = G\,m\int\int\int_R \frac{z}{\rho^3}\,\delta\,dV = G\,m\int\int\int_R \delta\,\cos\varphi\,\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta" cite="reading2" at="p. 14" />

## Newton's theorem

**The gravitational attraction of a spherical planet of uniform density (or of density depending only on the distance to its centre), on a mass outside it or on its surface, is the same as that of a point mass with the same total mass at its centre** (transcript, pp. 14–15; notes, p. 2). If the Earth collapsed to a black hole of the same mass, your weight would not change (p. 15). The notes do the computation, for uniform density $\delta$ and a test mass on the surface: the sphere of radius $a$ sits on the positive $z$-axis, touching the $xy$-plane at the origin, so its spherical equation is $\rho = 2a\cos\varphi$ (a right triangle inscribed in a vertical slice, with the diameter $2a$ as hypotenuse) (notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Newton's theorem" tex="F_z = G\,m\,\delta\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2}\int_0^{2a\cos\varphi} \cos\varphi\,\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta = \tfrac43\pi G\,m\,\delta\,a = \frac{G\,M\,m}{a^2}, \qquad M = \tfrac43\pi a^3\delta" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 26, p. 2" />

<WorkedExample title="Two spherical integrals">
<Problem>
(a) Find the average distance from the centre of the points of the unit ball. (b) Find the volume of the "ice-cream cone" inside the unit sphere and inside the cone $\varphi = \pi/4$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Average of ρ">
$\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi\int_0^1 \rho \cdot \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta = \frac14 \cdot 2 \cdot 2\pi = \pi$. Dividing by the volume $\frac43\pi$: the average distance is $\frac34$. Most of the volume of a ball is near its surface.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The cone">
$\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/4}\int_0^1 \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta = \frac13 \cdot \left(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right) \cdot 2\pi \approx 0.613$. Subtracting the cap above, the part of the cone below the plane $z = 1/\sqrt2$ is $0.613 - 0.243 = 0.370$, which is $\frac13\pi r^2 h$ for a cone of radius and height $1/\sqrt2$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Newton's theorem and the expanding universe">
Guth's Newtonian model of the universe follows a sphere of uniform density. Newton's theorem is what makes it work: the matter inside a sphere pulls a galaxy on its surface exactly as if it were all at the centre. (The companion fact, not proved in this lecture, that a uniform spherical shell exerts no net force on anything inside it, lets the matter outside be ignored.) See [the expanding sphere](/courses/8-286/expanding-sphere).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Spherical coordinates: $\rho$ (distance to origin), $\varphi$ (down from the $z$-axis, $0$ to $\pi$), $\theta$; $z = \rho\cos\varphi$, $r = \rho\sin\varphi$, $\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$.
- $\rho = a$ is a sphere, $\varphi = $ constant a cone, $\varphi = \pi/2$ the $xy$-plane.
- $dS = a^2\sin\varphi\,d\varphi\,d\theta$ on a sphere; $dV = \rho^2\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta$.
- The cap of the unit ball above $z = 1/\sqrt2$ has volume $\frac{2\pi}{3} - \frac{5\pi}{6\sqrt2}$.
- Gravitational force of a solid: $\vec F = \int\int\int G m\langle x, y, z\rangle\delta/\rho^3\,dV$; with axial symmetry $F_z = Gm\int\int\int \delta\cos\varphi\sin\varphi\,d\rho\,d\varphi\,d\theta$, which proves Newton's theorem for a mass outside a uniform sphere or on its surface.
