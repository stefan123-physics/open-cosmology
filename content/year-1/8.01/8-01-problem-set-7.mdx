This set practises the lessons on [kinetic energy and work in one dimension](/courses/8-01/work-and-kinetic-energy), on [work in two and three dimensions](/courses/8-01/work-in-two-and-three-dimensions) and on [conservative and non-conservative forces](/courses/8-01/conservative-forces). The three problems follow MIT's Problem Set 7, in our own words. MIT's set comes with a drawing for each problem. Here each situation is described in words, so make your own sketch first. MIT publishes no solutions for this set, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. One remark: MIT's course notes work the inclined plane of Problem 1 as Example 13.10, with the same numbers, and that example gives the work done by each force on the way down. Try the problem before you look there. All three problems count for the score (42 points).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first: sketch the situation, draw the forces, and decide for each force whether it pushes along the motion, against it, or across it. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A number box accepts any value within 1% of ours, so keep at least three significant figures, and type it in the unit shown next to the box. Work is positive or negative: type the minus sign where it belongs. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ where a number is needed. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `-mu*m*g*d`, `sqrt(2*K/m)` or `pi*R*cos(phi)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Sliding down an inclined plane and along a rough floor" points={16} mit="Problem 1">

An object of mass $m = 4.0$ kg starts from rest at the top of an inclined plane and slides down it. The plane has the length $l = 3.0$ m and makes the angle $\theta = 30°$ with the level ground. Between the object and the plane the coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.2. At the bottom of the plane the object slides on along a rough, level floor, where the coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.3, until it stops. The aim is to find how far it slides along the floor.

Below, $\mu_k$ stands for the coefficient of the surface that the object is on at that moment: 0.2 on the plane and 0.3 on the floor. Take the join between the plane and the floor to be smooth, so that the object keeps its speed as it passes from one to the other. (MIT does not say this. The assumption is ours.)

<Part label="a" points={3}>

How much work does the friction force do on the object while it slides down the plane, in joules? Type it with its sign.

<Hint>

The friction force is $\mu_k$ times the normal force. On an inclined plane the normal force is not the full weight. Which part of the weight presses the object into the plane?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The friction force is constant here, and it points along the plane, against the motion. For a constant force the work is the component of the force along the displacement times the displacement.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$W_f =$" value="-20.4" unit="J" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-work-friction" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Kinetic friction has a fixed size, $\mu_k N$, and always points against the motion. Over a straight slide of length $l$ its work is minus its size times $l$.

<Step title="The normal force">

The object does not move at right angles to the plane, so the forces in that direction cancel: the normal force balances the part of the weight that presses into the plane. The notes do this step in their Example 13.10:

<Formula label="Incline: normal force and friction force" tex="N = m g\cos\theta, \qquad f_k = \mu_k N = \mu_k\,m g\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="Example 13.10, pp. 13-25 and 13-26" />

With the numbers: $N = 4.0 \times 9.8 \times \cos 30° = 33.95$ N and $f_k = 0.2 \times 33.95 = 6.79$ N.

</Step>

<Step title="The work">

The displacement has the length $l$ and points down the plane. The friction force points up the plane, opposite to it:

<Formula label="Incline: work done by friction" tex="W_f = -\mu_k\,m g\cos\theta\; l = -6.79 \times 3.0 = -20.4\ \text{J}" cite="reading1" at="Example 13.10, p. 13-26" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A newton times a metre is a joule. The work is negative, as it must be for a force that points against the motion: friction takes kinetic energy away from the object. On a level floor ($\theta = 0$) the same slide would cost more, $0.2 \times 4.0 \times 9.8 \times 3.0 = 23.5$ J, because the normal force would be the full weight.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

How much work does the gravitational force do on the object while it slides down the plane, in joules? Type it with its sign.

<Hint>

Only the component of a force along the displacement does work. What is the component of the weight along the plane?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Another way: gravity is a conservative force. Its work depends only on how far the object comes down, not on the path.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$W_g =$" value="58.8" unit="J" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-work-gravity" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The weight points straight down, and the displacement points down the plane. The work is the component of the weight along the plane, $m g\sin\theta$, times the length of the slide.

<Step title="The work">

<Formula label="Incline: work done by gravity" tex="W_g = m g\sin\theta\; l = 4.0 \times 9.8 \times \sin 30° \times 3.0 = 58.8\ \text{J}" cite="reading1" at="Example 13.10, p. 13-26" />

It is positive: the weight has a component along the motion.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson on [conservative forces](/courses/8-01/conservative-forces) shows that the work of gravity is the weight times the drop in height, on any path. The object comes down by $l\sin\theta = 3.0 \times \sin 30° = 1.5$ m, and its weight is $4.0 \times 9.8 = 39.2$ N. That gives $39.2 \times 1.5 = 58.8$ J, the same.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

What is the kinetic energy of the object at the moment it reaches the bottom of the plane, in joules?

<Hint>

Three forces act on the object on its way down. How much work does the third one, the normal force, do?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The work–kinetic energy theorem: the work done by all forces together equals the change in kinetic energy. The object starts from rest.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$K =$" value="38.4" unit="J" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-kinetic-energy" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Add the work of all forces. By the work–kinetic energy theorem the sum is the change in kinetic energy, and the object starts with none.

<Step title="The theorem">

<Formula label="Work–kinetic energy theorem" tex="W = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F_x\,dx = \tfrac12 m v_f^2 - \tfrac12 m v_i^2 = K_f - K_i" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.6.1), p. 13-16" />

</Step>

<Step title="The total work">

The normal force is at right angles to the displacement, so it does no work. That leaves gravity and friction:

<Formula label="Incline: kinetic energy at the bottom" tex="K = W_g + W_f = m g\,l\,(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta) = 58.8 - 20.4 = 38.4\ \text{J}" cite="reading1" at="Example 13.10, p. 13-27 (the total work; setting it equal to K is our step)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Check with Newton's second law, without any work. Along the plane the net force is $m g\sin\theta - \mu_k m g\cos\theta$, so the acceleration is $g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta) = 9.8 \times (0.5 - 0.2 \times 0.866) = 3.20\ \text{m/s}^2$. Starting from rest, $v^2 = 2 a l = 2 \times 3.20 \times 3.0 = 19.2\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, and $\tfrac12 m v^2 = 0.5 \times 4.0 \times 19.2 = 38.4$ J. The speed at the bottom is $\sqrt{19.2} = 4.38$ m/s. Without friction it would be $\sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 1.5} = 5.42$ m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

The object now slides along the floor and stops at the distance $d$ from the bottom of the plane. Find, as a formula, the work that the friction force does on the object during this slide, with its sign. Use any of $m$, $\mu_k$, $g$ and $d$ that you need. Here $\mu_k$ is the coefficient of the floor.

<Hint>

On a level floor nothing but the floor holds the object up. What is the normal force?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The friction force is constant and points against the motion over the whole distance $d$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="$W_f =$" expr="-mu*m*g*d"
  vars={{ mu: [0.1, 0.5], m: [1, 5], g: [9, 10], d: [1, 5] }}
  names={{ mu: "\\mu_k" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu*m*g*d", hint: "The size is right, but the friction force points against the motion, so its work is negative." },
    { expr: "-mu*m*d", hint: "Check the units: a coefficient times kilograms times metres is not a joule. The normal force on a level floor is the weight, m g." },
    { expr: "-mu*g*d", hint: "Check the units: this is in m²/s², the work for each kilogram. The friction force is proportional to the mass." },
    { expr: "-m*g*d", hint: "That would be the work of a force as large as the whole weight. The friction force is the coefficient times the normal force." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same reasoning as in part (a), on a level surface.

<Step title="The force">

The floor is level, so the normal force balances the whole weight: $N = m g$. The friction force has the size $\mu_k m g$ and points against the motion.

</Step>

<Step title="The work">

Take $x$ along the floor, from the bottom of the plane ($x = 0$) in the direction of the motion. The $x$ component of the friction force is $-\mu_k m g$, the same everywhere:

<Formula label="Floor: work done by friction" tex="W_f = \int_0^d (-\mu_k\,m g)\,dx = -\mu_k\,m g\,d" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A coefficient of friction has no unit, so the result is a force times a distance: joules. It is negative, and it grows with the distance. This is why friction is a non-conservative force: its work depends on the length of the path, as the lesson on [conservative forces](/courses/8-01/conservative-forces) shows.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={4}>

How far from the bottom of the plane does the object stop, in metres?

<Hint>

At the start of the slide along the floor the object has the kinetic energy of part (c). At the end it has none.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the floor the weight and the normal force are at right angles to the motion. Only friction does work, and part (d) gives it in terms of $d$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e" label="$d =$" value="3.27" unit="m" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-distance" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the floor friction is the only force that does work. It has to take away all of the kinetic energy that the object brought down from the plane.

<Step title="The theorem on the floor">

The kinetic energy goes from $K$ of part (c) to zero, and the work is that of part (d), now with the coefficient 0.3 of the floor:

<Formula label="Floor: stopping distance" tex="-\mu_k\,m g\,d = 0 - K \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d = \frac{K}{\mu_k\,m g}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Numbers">

The friction force on the floor is $0.3 \times 4.0 \times 9.8 = 11.76$ N, so $d = 38.4/11.76 = 3.27$ m.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Joules divided by newtons are metres. Putting the result of part (c) into the formula, the mass and $g$ drop out:

<Formula label="Floor: stopping distance from the given numbers" tex="d = \frac{l\,(\sin 30° - 0.2\cos 30°)}{0.3} = 3.27\ \text{m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e) (our own solution)" />

So a heavier object would stop at the same place. Over the whole trip the three amounts of work add up to zero, $58.8 - 20.4 - 38.4 = 0$ J, as they must for an object that starts at rest and ends at rest.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A collision, a rough strip and a spring" points={12} mit="Problem 2">

Block A, of mass $m_A$, slides with speed $v_A$ along a level, frictionless surface, in the $+x$ direction. It hits block B, of mass $m_B$, which is at rest. The two blocks stick together. At $x = 0$ the pair enters a rough strip. On the strip the coefficient of kinetic friction grows in proportion to the distance:

<Formula label="Strip: coefficient of kinetic friction" tex="\mu_k(x) = b\,x \qquad (0 \le x \le d)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

where $b$ is a positive constant. The strip ends at $x = d$. From there on the surface is frictionless again, and there the pair meets the free end of a spring of spring constant $k$, which lies along the $x$ direction at its natural length, with its far end fixed. Gravity pulls downward with the acceleration $g$.

By what distance is the spring compressed at the moment the blocks first come to rest? Use any of $v_A$, $b$, $d$, $g$, $k$, $m_A$ and $m_B$ that you need.

Three points are our reading, not MIT's words. The blocks are small compared with $d$, so that the pair can be treated as one point at the position $x$. The collision is over before the pair reaches $x = 0$. And the pair is fast enough to cross the whole strip.

<Hint>

Split the motion into three stages: the collision, the rough strip, and the spring. Each stage needs its own law. In which of them is kinetic energy lost, and where does momentum help?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The collision: no outside force acts along $x$, so the momentum is the same before and after. That gives the speed of the pair. Kinetic energy is not the same before and after, because the blocks stick.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The strip: the friction force on the pair is $\mu_k(x)$ times the normal force on both blocks together. It changes with $x$, so its work is an integral from 0 to $d$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The spring: use the work–kinetic energy theorem from $x = d$ to the point where the pair stops. The spring force also changes with position, and the lesson on work in one dimension gives its work.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-s" label="Compression $=$" expr="sqrt((mA^2*vA^2/(mA + mB) - (mA + mB)*g*b*d^2)/k)"
  vars={{ vA: [8, 12], mA: [2, 4], mB: [1, 2], b: [0.05, 0.2], d: [0.5, 1.5], g: [9, 10], k: [50, 200] }}
  names={{ vA: "v_A", mA: "m_A", mB: "m_B" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt((mA*vA^2 - (mA + mB)*g*b*d^2)/k)", hint: "This starts from the kinetic energy of block A before the collision. The blocks stick together, and in such a collision kinetic energy is lost. Use momentum to find the speed of the pair." },
    { expr: "sqrt((mA^2*vA^2/(mA + mB) - 2*(mA + mB)*g*b*d^2)/k)", hint: "This takes the friction force to have its largest value, the one at x = d, over the whole strip. The force grows from zero, so its work is an integral." },
    { expr: "sqrt((mA^2*vA^2/(mA + mB) - mB*g*b*d^2)/k)", hint: "The two blocks cross the strip together, so the friction force comes from the normal force on both of them." },
    { expr: "sqrt((mA^2*vA^2/(mA + mB) - mA*g*b*d^2)/k)", hint: "The two blocks cross the strip together, so the friction force comes from the normal force on both of them." },
    { expr: "(mA^2*vA^2/(mA + mB) - (mA + mB)*g*b*d^2)/k", hint: "Check the units: this is in m², the square of the compression. Take the root." },
    { expr: "sqrt(mA^2*vA^2/((mA + mB)*k))", hint: "The rough strip is missing. This is the compression if the pair lost no kinetic energy between the collision and the spring." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Three stages, three tools. In the collision the blocks stick, so kinetic energy is lost and only momentum can be trusted. On the strip a known force acts along a known path, so its work can be calculated. On the spring the same is true. The work–kinetic energy theorem then links the speed after the collision to the point where the pair stops.

<Step title="The collision">

Along $x$ no outside force acts on the two blocks, so their total momentum does not change (the lesson on [conservation of momentum](/courses/8-01/conservation-of-momentum)). Before the collision only block A moves. Afterwards both move with one speed $V$:

<Formula label="Collision: speed of the pair" tex="m_A\,v_A = (m_A + m_B)\,V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V = \frac{m_A\,v_A}{m_A + m_B}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

The pair arrives at $x = 0$ with this speed, because the surface before the strip is frictionless. Its kinetic energy there is

<Formula label="Collision: kinetic energy of the pair" tex="K_0 = \tfrac12\,(m_A + m_B)\,V^2 = \frac{m_A^2\,v_A^2}{2\,(m_A + m_B)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The rough strip">

The surface is level, so the normal force on the pair is its weight, $(m_A + m_B)\,g$. At the position $x$ the friction force has the size $\mu_k(x)\,N = b\,x\,(m_A + m_B)\,g$ and points in the $-x$ direction. It is not constant, so the work is the integral of the lesson on [work in one dimension](/courses/8-01/work-and-kinetic-energy):

<Formula label="Strip: work done by friction" tex="W_f = \int_0^d \bigl(-b\,x\,(m_A + m_B)\,g\bigr)\,dx = -\tfrac12\,(m_A + m_B)\,g\,b\,d^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The spring">

Call the compression at the stopping point $s$. While the spring is pressed in, it pushes back with a force that is proportional to the compression. The notes give the work of the spring force between two states of the spring:

<Formula label="Work done by a spring" tex="W = -\tfrac12\,k\,(x_f^2 - x_i^2)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.5.5), p. 13-15" />

Here the spring starts at its natural length and ends pressed in by $s$, so $W_s = -\tfrac12 k s^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The theorem from the collision to the stop">

From $x = 0$ to the stopping point the weight and the normal force are at right angles to the motion. Friction and the spring do all the work, and the kinetic energy goes from $K_0$ to zero:

<Formula label="Strip and spring: work and kinetic energy" tex="-\tfrac12\,(m_A + m_B)\,g\,b\,d^2 - \tfrac12\,k\,s^2 = 0 - \frac{m_A^2\,v_A^2}{2\,(m_A + m_B)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

Solve for $s$:

<Formula label="Compression of the spring" tex="s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{k}\left(\frac{m_A^2\,v_A^2}{m_A + m_B} - (m_A + m_B)\,g\,b\,d^2\right)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\mu_k = b\,x$ has no unit, so $b$ is in $\text{m}^{-1}$, and $(m_A + m_B)\,g\,b\,d^2$ is in joules, like the first term. Joules divided by N/m are $\text{m}^2$, so the root is a length.

Limits: with no friction ($b = 0$) the result is $s = V\sqrt{(m_A + m_B)/k}$, which says $\tfrac12 k s^2 = K_0$. A stiffer spring is pressed in less. The root must be of a positive number: that is the condition $V^2 > g\,b\,d^2$, which says that the pair is fast enough to cross the strip.

An example with our own numbers: $m_A = 2.0$ kg, $m_B = 1.0$ kg, $v_A = 6.0$ m/s, $b = 0.20\ \text{m}^{-1}$, $d = 1.5$ m and $k = 300$ N/m. The pair moves at $V = 2.0 \times 6.0/3.0 = 4.0$ m/s and has $K_0 = 24$ J. Block A alone had 36 J: the collision took 12 J. Friction does $-0.5 \times 3.0 \times 9.8 \times 0.20 \times 1.5^2 = -6.6$ J, so the pair reaches the spring with 17.4 J, and $s = \sqrt{2 \times 17.4/300} = 0.34$ m. The strip ends with $\mu_k = 0.20 \times 1.5 = 0.30$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A circle on a rough inclined plane" points={14} mit="Problem 3">

A flat plane is tilted at the angle $\phi$ to the horizontal. A string of length $R$ has one end fixed to a point of the plane. At its other end is a small body of mass $m$, which slides on the plane and moves in a circle of radius $R$ around the fixed point, with the string taut. The circle lies in the plane, so one point of it is lowest (point A, straight down the slope from the centre) and the opposite point is highest (point B, straight up the slope from the centre). Seen from the side, A and B lie on the slope a distance $2R$ apart.

At A the body has the speed $v_0$. Between the body and the plane the coefficient of sliding (kinetic) friction is $\mu$. Gravity pulls downward with the acceleration $g$. Take $v_0$ to be large enough for the string to stay taut all the way from A to B.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

How much work does the friction force do on the body as it moves from A to B? Give a formula, with its sign, using any of $m$, $\phi$, $v_0$, $g$, $\mu$ and $R$ that you need.

<Hint>

The friction force is $\mu$ times the normal force. The string lies in the plane, so it does not press the body into the plane or lift it off. What is the normal force?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The size of the friction force is the same at every point of the circle, but its direction turns: it always points against the velocity. So on each small step of the path, the work is minus the size of the force times the length of the step.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Add up the steps. How long is the path from A to B along the circle?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$W_f =$" expr="-pi*mu*m*g*R*cos(phi)"
  vars={{ mu: [0.05, 0.3], m: [0.5, 2], g: [9, 10], R: [0.5, 2], phi: [0.2, 1.2] }}
  names={{ mu: "\\mu", phi: "\\phi" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi*mu*m*g*R*cos(phi)", hint: "The size is right, but the friction force points against the motion at every point, so its work is negative." },
    { expr: "-2*mu*m*g*R*cos(phi)", hint: "This uses the straight distance 2R from A to B. Friction is not a conservative force: its work depends on the path, and the body moves along half a circle." },
    { expr: "-pi*mu*m*g*R", hint: "This takes the normal force to be the whole weight. On a tilted plane only the part of the weight at right angles to the plane presses the body into it." },
    { expr: "-pi*mu*m*g*R*sin(phi)", hint: "Check a level table, where phi is zero: there friction is at its strongest, but your work vanishes. The normal force is the part of the weight at right angles to the plane." },
    { expr: "-2*pi*mu*m*g*R*cos(phi)", hint: "That is the work over a whole turn. From A to B the body covers half of the circle." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Kinetic friction has a fixed size here and always points against the velocity. So its work is minus its size times the length of the path, whatever the shape of the path.

<Step title="The size of the friction force">

At right angles to the plane the body does not move. The string pulls along the plane, so in that direction only the normal force and a part of the weight act, and they cancel: $N = m g\cos\phi$. The friction force has the size

<Formula label="Circle: size of the friction force" tex="f = \mu\,N = \mu\,m g\cos\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

at every point of the circle.

</Step>

<Step title="The work along the half circle">

Work along a curved path is the sum of $\vec F\cdot d\vec s$ over its small steps (the lesson on [work in two and three dimensions](/courses/8-01/work-in-two-and-three-dimensions)). The friction force is opposite to each step $d\vec s$, so $\vec f\cdot d\vec s = -f\,ds$, where $ds$ is the length of the step. The steps from A to B add up to half the circumference, $\pi R$:

<Formula label="Circle: work done by friction from A to B" tex="W_f = \int_A^B \vec f\cdot d\vec s = -\mu\,m g\cos\phi\int_A^B ds = -\pi\,\mu\,m g R\cos\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

The speed $v_0$ does not appear.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A coefficient and a cosine have no unit, so the result is a force $m g$ times a length $R$: joules. It is negative. On a level table ($\phi = 0$) it is $-\mu\,m g$ times the path length $\pi R$, as on any level floor. On a vertical wall ($\phi = 90°$) the body is not pressed against the surface, and the work is zero. Had the body gone straight from A to B, the work would have been $-2\,\mu\,m g R\cos\phi$, smaller in size: the work of friction depends on the path.

</Step>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two steps. The work–kinetic energy theorem gives the speed at B, because the work of every force from A to B is known. Newton's second law in the direction of the string then gives the tension, because a body on a circle of radius $R$ with speed $v$ needs the net force $m v^2/R$ towards the centre.

<Step title="The work done on the way from A to B">

Four forces act on the body. The normal force is at right angles to the plane, and the tension points along the string, towards the centre. Both are at right angles to the velocity at every moment, so they do no work. Friction does the work of part (a). Gravity is conservative: its work is minus the weight times the gain in height. Seen from the side, B lies a distance $2R$ up the slope from A, which is $2R\sin\phi$ higher:

<Formula label="Circle: work done by gravity from A to B" tex="W_g = -m g\,(2R\sin\phi) = -2\,m g R\sin\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The speed at B">

The theorem holds along any path in space:

<Formula label="Work–kinetic energy theorem in three dimensions" tex="\int_i^f \vec F\cdot d\vec r = K_f - K_i" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.11.8), p. 13-35" />

Here it reads

<Formula label="Circle: kinetic energy at B" tex="\tfrac12 m v_B^2 - \tfrac12 m v_0^2 = -2\,m g R\sin\phi - \pi\,\mu\,m g R\cos\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

Multiply by $2/R$:

<Formula label="Circle: centripetal force needed at B" tex="\frac{m v_B^2}{R} = \frac{m v_0^2}{R} - 4\,m g\sin\phi - 2\pi\,\mu\,m g\cos\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Newton's second law at B, along the string">

For motion on a circle the notes write the radial part of the second law as

<Formula label="Radial component of the second law" tex="F_r = -m r\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (9.1.2), p. 9-2" />

With the speed $v = r\,d\theta/dt$ and $r = R$ this says that the forces towards the centre add up to $m v^2/R$. At B the centre lies straight down the slope. The tension $T$ points there, and so does the down-slope part of the weight, $m g\sin\phi$. The friction force points along the circle, against the velocity, and the normal force points out of the plane, so neither has a part along the string:

<Formula label="Circle: second law at B towards the centre" tex="T + m g\sin\phi = \frac{m v_B^2}{R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The tension">

Put in $m v_B^2/R$ from the step before:

<Formula label="Circle: tension at B" tex="T = \frac{m v_0^2}{R} - 5\,m g\sin\phi - 2\pi\,\mu\,m g\cos\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Every term is a force. For a vertical plane ($\phi = 90°$) the friction term vanishes and $T = m v_0^2/R - 5\,m g$: the known result for a body on a string in a vertical circle, whose tension at the top is $6\,m g$ less than at the bottom, where it is $m v_0^2/R + m g$. For a level table ($\phi = 0$) gravity plays no part in the plane, and the tension falls only because friction slows the body: $T = m v_0^2/R - 2\pi\,\mu\,m g$.

A string can pull but not push, so the result only makes sense when it is positive. That is the condition on $v_0$ in the statement of the problem.

An example with our own numbers: $m = 0.50$ kg, $R = 0.80$ m, $\phi = 30°$, $\mu = 0.10$ and $v_0 = 6.0$ m/s. The body starts with $0.5 \times 0.50 \times 6.0^2 = 9.0$ J. Gravity does $-2 \times 0.50 \times 9.8 \times 0.80 \times \sin 30° = -3.92$ J and friction does $-\pi \times 0.10 \times 0.50 \times 9.8 \times 0.80 \times \cos 30° = -1.07$ J. That leaves 4.01 J at B, a speed of 4.01 m/s. The body then needs $2 \times 4.01/0.80 = 10.0$ N towards the centre. The weight supplies $0.50 \times 9.8 \times \sin 30° = 2.45$ N of it, and the string the other 7.58 N. The formula gives the same: $22.5 - 12.25 - 2.67 = 7.58$ N.

</Step>
