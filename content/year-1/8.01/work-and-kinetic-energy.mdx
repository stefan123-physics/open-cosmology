Momentum came from integrating Newton's second law over **time**. This lesson integrates it over **position**, which brings in two new quantities, kinetic energy and work, linked by one theorem. Energy is a scalar, which often makes it much easier to use than vectors.

## Kinetic energy

The kinetic energy of an object of mass $m$ and speed $v$ is (20.1 transcript, p. 1; notes, Eq. (13.2.1), p. 13-5)

<Formula label="Kinetic energy" tex="K = \tfrac12\,m v^2" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

It is a **scalar**, zero or positive, never negative, and its SI unit is the **joule**, $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}^2$. When an object speeds up from $v_i$ to $v_f$, its kinetic energy changes by $\Delta K = \tfrac12 m v_f^2 - \tfrac12 m v_i^2$; in two or three dimensions $v^2 = v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2$ (p. 1). Multiplying top and bottom by $m$ links it to the momentum $p = mv$ (p. 1; the transcript says "m squared over v squared", meaning $m^2v^2$):

<Formula label="Kinetic energy and momentum" tex="K = \frac{m^2 v^2}{2m} = \frac{p^2}{2m}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

A gain in speed costs more kinetic energy the faster you already go. The notes compare a car going from 10 to 20 mph with one going from 50 to 60 mph: the second gains $11/3$ times as much kinetic energy. Seen from a frame moving at 10 mph the ratio becomes 9: changes in kinetic energy depend on the frame (notes, Example 13.1, pp. 13-5–13-6).

## Work done by a constant force

A constant force $F_x$ moves an object from $x_i$ to $x_f$. The **work** it does is the force times the displacement (20.2 transcript, p. 1; notes, Eq. (13.4.2), p. 13-9):

<Formula label="Work by a constant force" tex="W = F_x\,(x_f - x_i) = F_x\,\Delta x" cite="reading3" at="p. 1" />

Work is a scalar with a **sign**: positive when the force points along the displacement, negative when it opposes it (p. 1). Kinetic friction always opposes the motion, so its work is negative, $W = -\mu_k mg\,\Delta x$ on a level surface (notes, Eq. (13.4.5), p. 13-10). Its unit is the newton metre, again the joule, $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N·m}$. On a graph of $F_x$ against $x$, the work is the **area** of the rectangle under the line between $x_i$ and $x_f$ (pp. 1–2).

The notes add a subtlety: the displacement in the formula is that of the **point where the force acts**. When you start walking, static friction from the ground pushes you forward, but your foot does not move while it pushes, so static friction does **no work** on you; the energy comes from your muscles (notes, Example 13.2, p. 13-10). Forces perpendicular to the motion, such as gravity and the normal force on a box sliding across a floor, do no work either (pp. 13-10–13-11). In the notes' numbers, a 2.0 N push over 0.5 m does 1.0 J, while friction ($\mu_k = 0.10$, a 0.2 kg cup) does $-0.10$ J (Example 13.3, p. 13-11).

## Work done by a varying force

If the force depends on position, as for a spring, cut the path into $N$ small steps $\Delta x_j$ on which the force is nearly constant, and add up the small amounts of work (20.3 transcript, pp. 1–3). Letting $N \to \infty$ turns the sum into an integral (pp. 2–3; notes, Eq. (13.5.3), p. 13-14):

<Formula label="Work by a varying force" tex="W = \lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{N} F_x(x_j)\,\Delta x_j = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F_x(x')\,dx'" cite="reading4" at="pp. 2–3" />

Again the work is the **area under the force–position curve**; the rectangles' small errors vanish in the limit (p. 3). For a spring with force $F_x = -kx$ (notes, Eq. (13.5.5), p. 13-15):

<Formula label="Work done by a spring" tex="W = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} (-k x)\,dx = -\tfrac12\,k\,(x_f^2 - x_i^2)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (13.5.5), p. 13-15" />

It is positive when the spring ends **less** stretched or compressed than it started.

## Integrating acceleration over time and over position

Two integrals lie behind momentum and energy (20.4 transcript, pp. 1–3). Over **time**, since $a_x = dv_x/dt$:

<Formula label="Over time" tex="\int_{t_i}^{t_f} a_x\,dt' = \int_{v_{x,i}}^{v_{x,f}} dv_x' = v_{x,f} - v_{x,i}" cite="reading5" at="pp. 1–2" />

Over **position**, use $a_x\,dx = \frac{dv_x}{dt}\,dx = dv_x\,\frac{dx}{dt} = v_x\,dv_x$ (a change of integration variable, as in [substitution](/courses/18-01/antiderivatives)):

<Formula label="Over position" tex="\int_{x_i}^{x_f} a_x\,dx' = \int_{v_{x,i}}^{v_{x,f}} v_x'\,dv_x' = \tfrac12\left(v_{x,f}^2 - v_{x,i}^2\right)" cite="reading5" at="pp. 2–3" />

Integrating acceleration over time gives the change in velocity; over space it gives half the change in the **square** of the velocity (p. 3). The notes reach the same result by summing over small intervals, starting from constant acceleration (notes, Eqs. (13.3.1)–(13.3.14), pp. 13-6–13-8).

## The work–kinetic energy theorem

Take the $x$ component of $F = ma$ and integrate over position (20.5 transcript, p. 1). The previous section turns the right-hand side into a change of kinetic energy:

<Formula label="Work–kinetic energy theorem" tex="W = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} F_x\,dx' = m\int_{x_i}^{x_f} a_x\,dx' = \tfrac12 m v_f^2 - \tfrac12 m v_i^2 = \Delta K" cite="reading6" at="p. 1" />

The **work done on an object** (by the total force) equals the **change in its kinetic energy** (notes, Eq. (13.6.1), p. 13-16). Positive work speeds it up, negative work slows it down, zero work leaves its speed unchanged. For an extended body it applies to the motion of the centre of mass (notes, p. 13-9). The notes' falling ball: 0.2 kg dropping 10 m receives $W = mg \times 10\ \text{m} \approx 20$ J from gravity, so it reaches $v = \sqrt{2W/m} \approx 14$ m/s (Example 13.7, p. 13-16).

<WorkedExample title="Braking and launching">
<Problem>
(a) A 1200 kg car brakes from 20 m/s to rest in 40 m. What average force do the brakes need? (b) A spring of $k = 200$ N/m, compressed by 0.10 m, launches a 0.050 kg ball along a smooth table. How fast does the ball leave? (c) How much work does a force $F_x = 3x^2$ N do from $x = 0$ to 2.0 m?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Braking">
$\Delta K = 0 - \tfrac12 \times 1200 \times 20^2 = -240\,000$ J. This equals the work $F_x \times 40$ m, so $F_x = -6000$ N: 6000 N against the motion.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Launching">
From $x_i = -0.10$ m to $x_f = 0$: $W = -\tfrac12 k(0 - 0.10^2) = 1.0$ J $= \tfrac12 m v^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2 \times 1.0/0.050} \approx 6.3$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A varying force">
A force $F_x = 3x^2$ N acts from $x = 0$ to 2.0 m: $W = \int_0^2 3x^2\,dx = 8.0$ J, the area in the graph above.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Power

**Power** is the rate of doing work (20.6 transcript, p. 1). A force $\vec F$ moving its point of application through $d\vec s$ does work $\vec F\cdot d\vec s$, so

<Formula label="Power" tex="P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \vec F\cdot\frac{d\vec s}{dt} = \vec F\cdot\vec v" cite="reading7" at="p. 1" />

In one dimension, $P = F_x v_x$; the unit is the **watt**, $1\ \text{W} = 1$ J/s (notes, Eqs. (13.7.2)–(13.7.3), p. 13-18). When $F_x$ is the total force, this power is also the rate of change of kinetic energy, $dK/dt = m v_x\,dv_x/dt = F_x v_x$ (notes, Eqs. (13.7.18)–(13.7.19), p. 13-21). (The dot is the scalar product, the subject of the next lesson. The lecture also warns that the letter $P$, or $p$, is used for power, pressure and momentum.) For the falling ball, gravity's power grows with the speed: about 13.7 W on average over the fall, and about 27.4 W at the end, twice the average, because the speed grows steadily (notes, Example 13.8, pp. 13-18–13-20).

<WorkedExample title="Cycling power">
<Problem>
A cyclist rides steadily at 10 m/s, producing 250 W. What is the total resistive force (air and rolling resistance)?
</Problem>
<Step title="Balance">
At steady speed the driving force balances the resistance, and $P = Fv$: $F = 250/10 = 25$ N.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Gravity's work on a receding galaxy">
Picture a galaxy at the edge of a uniform sphere of mass $M$ that is expanding with the universe. As it recedes from $r_i$ to $r_f$, the sphere's gravity, $F = -GMm/r^2$, does work $W = \int_{r_i}^{r_f}(-GMm/r^2)\,dr = GMm\,(1/r_f - 1/r_i)$, which is negative. By the work–kinetic energy theorem the galaxy's kinetic energy drops by exactly that much. Whether gravity can take away **all** of it decides whether the expansion stops and reverses, or goes on forever: the question at the heart of Newtonian cosmology in 8.286.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Kinetic energy $K = \tfrac12 m v^2 = p^2/2m$: a scalar, never negative, in joules.
- Work by a constant force: $W = F_x\,\Delta x$, signed; by a varying force, $W = \int F_x\,dx$, the area under the force–position graph. Use the displacement of the point where the force acts.
- Integrating $a_x$ over time gives $\Delta v_x$; over position, $\tfrac12\Delta(v_x^2)$.
- Work–kinetic energy theorem: $W = \Delta K$.
- Power: $P = \vec F\cdot\vec v$, in watts.
