This set practises the lessons on [position and velocity on a circle](/courses/8-01/circular-motion-position-velocity), on [uniform circular motion](/courses/8-01/uniform-circular-motion), on [tangential and angular acceleration](/courses/8-01/circular-motion-acceleration), on [Newton's second law for circular motion](/courses/8-01/newtons-second-law-circular-motion) and on its [worked problems](/courses/8-01/circular-motion-dynamics-examples). The six problems follow MIT's Problem Set 3, in our own words. MIT's set comes with drawings; here each situation is described in words, so make your own sketch first. MIT publishes no solutions for this set, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. All six problems count for the score (60 points).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first: draw the body on its own with all the forces on it (a free-body diagram), find the circle it moves on and the centre of that circle, and write Newton's second law along the line to the centre and at right angles to it. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A formula box takes plain typing, for example `sqrt(2*g*h)`, `m*r*omega^2` or `l*cos(beta)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type Greek letters by name: `omega`, `theta`, `beta`, `alpha`, and `mu` for $\mu_s$. Angles are in radians, so type `theta` and `beta` as they stand. For a fourth root type `( )^(1/4)`.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A bead on a spinning hoop" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A circular hoop of radius $R$ stands upright and spins about its vertical diameter with a constant angular speed $\omega$. A small bead of mass $m$ is threaded on the hoop and can slide along it without friction. Gravity, of magnitude $g$, points down.

The position of the bead is given by the angle $\theta$ between two lines from the centre of the hoop: the line straight down to the lowest point of the hoop, and the line to the bead. So $\theta = 0$ is the bottom of the hoop.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

The bead stays at one place on the hoop, at an angle $\theta$ larger than zero, and goes round with the hoop. What angular speed $\omega$ does that need? Give it in terms of $\theta$, $R$ and $g$.

<Hint>

The bead moves on a horizontal circle around the axis of the hoop. What is the radius of that circle? It is not $R$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Only two forces act on the bead: its weight, and the normal force of the hoop, which points along the line from the bead to the centre of the hoop. The bead has no vertical acceleration, and its horizontal acceleration points to the axis.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the second law for the vertical direction and for the horizontal direction to the axis, with the acceleration $r\omega^2$ for a circle of radius $r$. Divide one equation by the other: the normal force drops out.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$\omega =$" expr="sqrt(g/(R*cos(theta)))"
  vars={{ theta: [0.3, 1.3], R: [0.1, 1], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(g*tan(theta)/R)", hint: "This follows if the bead's circle has the radius R. The bead goes round the vertical axis, and its distance from that axis is smaller than R." },
    { expr: "sqrt(g*cos(theta)/R)", hint: "Test your result for a bead level with the centre of the hoop (theta = 90 degrees). There the normal force is horizontal and cannot carry the weight, so no finite angular speed is enough. Your result goes to zero instead." },
    { expr: "sqrt(g/(R*sin(theta)))", hint: "Test your result for a bead level with the centre of the hoop (theta = 90 degrees). There the normal force is horizontal and cannot carry the weight, so no finite angular speed is enough. Your result stays finite." },
    { expr: "sqrt(g/R)", hint: "The angle must appear: a bead that sits higher on the hoop needs a faster spin. Your result is the limit for a very small angle." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A bead that keeps its place on the hoop moves on a horizontal circle at constant speed. Its acceleration points to the axis, and the normal force of the hoop must supply it while also carrying the weight.

<Step title="The circle and the forces">

The bead is at the distance $R$ from the centre of the hoop, on a line that makes the angle $\theta$ with the vertical. Its distance from the vertical axis is therefore $r = R\sin\theta$, and that is the radius of its circle.

Two forces act on the bead. Its weight $mg$ points down. The hoop is frictionless, so it pushes at right angles to itself: the normal force, of size $N$, points from the bead to the centre of the hoop. It has the part $N\cos\theta$ upwards and the part $N\sin\theta$ horizontally towards the axis.

</Step>

<Step title="The second law in components">

For motion on a circle of radius $r$ at the angular speed $\omega$, the forces along the radius must add up to

<Formula label="Radial component of the second law" tex="F_r = -m\,r\,\omega^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.1.2), p. 9-2" />

The minus sign says that the net force points inwards. Here the only inward force is $N\sin\theta$. The bead does not move up or down, so the vertical forces balance:

<Formula label="Bead on a hoop: the two components" tex="N\sin\theta = m\,(R\sin\theta)\,\omega^2, \qquad N\cos\theta - m g = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The angular speed">

For $\theta > 0$ we may divide the first equation by $\sin\theta$, which gives $N = mR\omega^2$. Put this in the second equation, $mR\omega^2\cos\theta = mg$, and solve:

<Formula label="Angular speed that holds the bead at the angle θ" tex="\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{R\cos\theta}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The unit of $g/R$ is $(\text{m/s}^2)/\text{m} = 1/\text{s}^2$, so $\omega$ comes out in radians per second. The mass has dropped out. A bead that sits higher needs a faster spin, and as $\theta$ goes to $90°$ the angular speed grows without limit: a horizontal normal force cannot carry the weight. The result makes sense only below the centre of the hoop, where $\cos\theta > 0$. For example, a hoop of radius $0.20$ m with $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ holds the bead at $60°$ if $\omega = \sqrt{9.8/(0.20 \times 0.5)} = 9.9$ rad/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Look at your result of part (a) the other way round: for a given $\omega$, at which angle does the bead sit? You will find that slow spins, $0 < \omega < \omega_0$, allow no such angle at all. The bead then keeps its place at $\theta = 0$, the bottom of the hoop, and nowhere else on the lower half. Find $\omega_0$ in terms of $R$ and $g$.

<Hint>

Solve your result of part (a) for $\cos\theta$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A cosine can never be larger than 1. What does that say about $\omega$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$\omega_0 =$" expr="sqrt(g/R)"
  vars={{ R: [0.1, 1], g: [9, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(R/g)", hint: "Check the unit: the square root of R/g is a time, in seconds. An angular speed is in radians per second." },
    { expr: "g/R", hint: "Check the unit: g/R is in 1 over seconds squared. That is the square of the angular speed you are after." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The result of part (a) fixes the cosine of the angle. A cosine cannot be larger than 1, and that sets a smallest angular speed.

<Step title="The angle for a given spin">

Squaring the result of part (a) and solving for the cosine gives

<Formula label="Angle of the bead for a given spin" tex="\cos\theta = \frac{g}{R\,\omega^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The smallest angular speed">

An angle exists only if the right-hand side is at most 1, so $\omega^2 \ge g/R$. For a slower spin no angle $0 < \theta < 90°$ (no other point of the lower half) satisfies both component equations of part (a). They are still satisfied at $\theta = 0$: there the radius of the circle is zero, the first equation reads $0 = 0$, and $N = mg$. So

<Formula label="Angular speed below which the bead stays at the bottom" tex="\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{g}{R}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The unit is again 1/s. At $\omega = \omega_0$ the cosine is exactly 1, so the tilted position starts at the bottom and climbs as the spin gets faster. For a hoop of radius $0.20$ m, $\omega_0 = \sqrt{9.8/0.20} = 7.0$ rad/s. At $\omega = 10$ rad/s the cosine is $9.8/(0.20 \times 10^2) = 0.49$, so the bead sits at $\theta = 60.7°$.

(A remark of ours, which MIT does not make: the very top of the hoop is a balanced position as well, at any spin, but the smallest push sends the bead away from it.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A banked turn with friction" points={14} mit="Problem 2">

A car of mass $m$ drives at a constant speed $v$ around a turn. Its path is a horizontal circle of radius $R$. The road is banked: seen from behind the car, the road surface is a slope that rises away from the centre of the turn, at an angle $\beta$ to the horizontal. There is friction between the tyres and the road, with a coefficient of static friction $\mu_s$, and the tyres do not slip. The magnitude of the acceleration of gravity is $g$.

If the car goes too slowly it slides down the slope, towards the centre of the turn. If it goes too fast it slides up the slope, outwards. In between there is a range of safe speeds, $v_\text{min} \le v \le v_\text{max}$. MIT asks for this range in one go. The split into two parts is ours.

Take $\tan\beta > \mu_s$ and $\mu_s\tan\beta < 1$. (These two conditions are our addition. Without the first, the car can stand still on the slope and $v_\text{min} = 0$. Without the second, no speed is too high.)

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Find the smallest safe speed $v_\text{min}$, in terms of $\mu_s$, $\beta$, $R$ and $g$.

<Hint>

At the smallest speed the car is about to slide down the slope. Which way does static friction point then, and how large is it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Three forces act on the car: its weight, the normal force at right angles to the road, and friction along the road. The acceleration is horizontal and points to the centre of the turn, so horizontal and vertical axes are the simplest choice.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the second law vertically (no acceleration) and horizontally (acceleration $v^2/R$ to the centre), with friction equal to $\mu_s N$. Both friction and the normal force have a part along each axis. Then divide the two equations.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$v_\text{min} =$" expr="sqrt(R*g*(sin(beta) - mu*cos(beta))/(cos(beta) + mu*sin(beta)))"
  vars={{ beta: [0.5, 0.7], mu: [0.1, 0.4], R: [20, 100], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ beta: "\\beta", mu: "\\mu_s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(R*g*tan(beta))", hint: "That is the one speed at which the car needs no friction at all. With friction a slower speed is safe too, so the coefficient of friction must appear." },
    { expr: "sqrt(R*g*(sin(beta) + mu*cos(beta))/(cos(beta) - mu*sin(beta)))", hint: "That is the largest safe speed: it has friction pointing down the slope. At the smallest speed the car is about to slide down, so friction points up the slope." },
    { expr: "sqrt(R*g*(tan(beta) - mu))", hint: "Your vertical equation is missing a force. Friction lies along the sloping road, so it has a vertical part as well as a horizontal one." },
    { expr: "sqrt(R*g*cos(beta)*(sin(beta) - mu*cos(beta)))", hint: "This uses N = m g cos(beta), which holds for a body with no acceleration at right angles to the slope. The car's acceleration to the centre has a part at right angles to the road, so the normal force is larger." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A car that is slower than the road is banked for tends to slide down the slope. Static friction holds it by pointing up the slope, and at the smallest safe speed friction has its largest value.

<Step title="Forces and axes">

Take one axis horizontal, pointing to the centre of the turn, and one axis vertical, pointing up. The weight $mg$ points down. The normal force, of size $N$, is at right angles to the road, so it is tilted by $\beta$ from the vertical towards the centre: it has the part $N\cos\beta$ upwards and the part $N\sin\beta$ to the centre. Friction, of size $f$, points up the slope, which is upwards and away from the centre: it has the part $f\sin\beta$ upwards and the part $f\cos\beta$ away from the centre.

</Step>

<Step title="The second law in components">

The car moves on a horizontal circle at constant speed. Its acceleration points to the centre and has the size

<Formula label="Centripetal acceleration" tex="a = \frac{v^2}{r}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (6.4.7), p. 8" />

with $r = R$ here. There is no vertical acceleration. So

<Formula label="Slow car on a banked turn: the two components" tex="N\sin\beta - f\cos\beta = \frac{m v^2}{R}, \qquad N\cos\beta + f\sin\beta - m g = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Friction at its limit">

Static friction can take any value up to a largest one:

<Formula label="Largest static friction" tex="(f_s)_\text{max} = \mu_s N" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (8.3.7), p. 8-15" />

At the smallest speed $f = \mu_s N$. The two equations become $N(\sin\beta - \mu_s\cos\beta) = mv^2/R$ and $N(\cos\beta + \mu_s\sin\beta) = mg$. Dividing the first by the second removes $N$ and $m$:

<Formula label="Smallest safe speed on a banked turn" tex="v_\text{min} = \sqrt{R\,g\,\frac{\sin\beta - \mu_s\cos\beta}{\cos\beta + \mu_s\sin\beta}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The fraction has no unit, and $Rg$ is in $\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so the result is a speed. The mass has dropped out. Without friction ($\mu_s = 0$) the result becomes $\sqrt{Rg\tan\beta}$, the one speed of a frictionless banked turn in the lesson. With friction the smallest speed is lower than that. It reaches zero at $\tan\beta = \mu_s$, which is the steepest slope on which a block can rest.

A number, with values of our own: $R = 60$ m, $\beta = 25°$, $\mu_s = 0.30$ and $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ give $v_\text{min} = \sqrt{588 \times 0.1459} = 9.3$ m/s, against $\sqrt{588 \times \tan 25°} = 16.6$ m/s without friction.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

Find the largest safe speed $v_\text{max}$, in terms of $\mu_s$, $\beta$, $R$ and $g$.

<Hint>

At the largest speed the car is about to slide up the slope. Which way does friction point now?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the same axes and the same two equations as in part (a). Only the direction of friction changes, and with it two signs.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$v_\text{max} =$" expr="sqrt(R*g*(sin(beta) + mu*cos(beta))/(cos(beta) - mu*sin(beta)))"
  vars={{ beta: [0.5, 0.7], mu: [0.1, 0.4], R: [20, 100], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ beta: "\\beta", mu: "\\mu_s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(R*g*tan(beta))", hint: "That is the one speed at which the car needs no friction at all. With friction a faster speed is safe too, so the coefficient of friction must appear." },
    { expr: "sqrt(R*g*(sin(beta) - mu*cos(beta))/(cos(beta) + mu*sin(beta)))", hint: "That is the smallest safe speed: it has friction pointing up the slope. At the largest speed the car is about to slide up, so friction points down the slope." },
    { expr: "sqrt(R*g*(tan(beta) + mu))", hint: "Your vertical equation is missing a force. Friction lies along the sloping road, so it has a vertical part as well as a horizontal one." },
    { expr: "sqrt(R*g*cos(beta)*(sin(beta) + mu*cos(beta)))", hint: "This uses N = m g cos(beta), which holds for a body with no acceleration at right angles to the slope. The car's acceleration to the centre has a part at right angles to the road, so the normal force is larger." },
    { expr: "sqrt(mu*R*g)", hint: "That is the largest speed on a flat road. The angle of the banked road must appear." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A car that is faster than the road is banked for tends to slide up the slope, outwards. Friction now points down the slope and helps the normal force to pull the car to the centre.

<Step title="The second law in components">

With the axes of part (a), friction down the slope has the part $f\cos\beta$ to the centre and the part $f\sin\beta$ downwards:

<Formula label="Fast car on a banked turn: the two components" tex="N\sin\beta + f\cos\beta = \frac{m v^2}{R}, \qquad N\cos\beta - f\sin\beta - m g = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Friction at its limit">

At the largest speed $f = \mu_s N$ again. Dividing the first equation by the second, as before:

<Formula label="Largest safe speed on a banked turn" tex="v_\text{max} = \sqrt{R\,g\,\frac{\sin\beta + \mu_s\cos\beta}{\cos\beta - \mu_s\sin\beta}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

The safe speeds are those with $v_\text{min} \le v \le v_\text{max}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\mu_s = 0$ this is again $\sqrt{Rg\tan\beta}$, and for a flat road ($\beta = 0$) it is $\sqrt{\mu_s R g}$, the speed at which the largest friction $\mu_s mg$ equals $mv^2/R$. The bottom of the fraction goes to zero as $\mu_s\tan\beta$ goes to 1: on so steep and rough a road no speed is too high, which is why we asked for $\mu_s\tan\beta < 1$.

With our numbers ($R = 60$ m, $\beta = 25°$, $\mu_s = 0.30$): $v_\text{max} = \sqrt{588 \times 0.8909} = 22.9$ m/s. A second route to both numbers: write $\mu_s = \tan\varphi$, here $\varphi = 16.7°$. The two results then become $\sqrt{Rg\tan(\beta - \varphi)}$ and $\sqrt{Rg\tan(\beta + \varphi)}$, and $\sqrt{588 \times \tan 8.3°} = 9.3$ m/s and $\sqrt{588 \times \tan 41.7°} = 22.9$ m/s agree with the values found above.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A ball on a string breaks loose" points={12} mit="Problem 3">

A small ball of mass $m$ hangs on a string of length $l$ from a fixed point. The ball goes round on a horizontal circle at a constant angular speed $\omega$, so that the string sweeps out a cone: it keeps a constant angle $\beta$ with the vertical. The plane of the circle is at a height $h$ above the level ground. Ignore air resistance and the size of the ball. (MIT calls $g$ "the gravitational constant" here; it is the acceleration of gravity, as in the other problems.)

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the angular speed $\omega$, in terms of $l$, $\beta$ and $g$.

<Hint>

What is the radius of the ball's circle? It is not the length of the string.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Two forces act on the ball: its weight and the pull of the string, along the string. Split the pull into a vertical and a horizontal part.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Vertically nothing accelerates. Horizontally the pull of the string supplies $m r \omega^2$. Divide the two equations to remove the tension.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$\omega =$" expr="sqrt(g/(l*cos(beta)))"
  vars={{ beta: [0.3, 1.3], l: [0.5, 2], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ beta: "\\beta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(g*tan(beta)/l)", hint: "This follows if the ball's circle has the radius l. The ball's distance from the vertical line through the fixed point is smaller than l." },
    { expr: "sqrt(g*cos(beta)/l)", hint: "Test your result for a string that is nearly horizontal (beta close to 90 degrees). Such a string can hardly carry the weight, so the ball must go round very fast. Your result goes to zero instead." },
    { expr: "sqrt(g/l)", hint: "The angle must appear: a wider cone needs a faster turn. Your result is the limit for a very small angle." },
    { expr: "sqrt(g/(l*sin(beta)))", hint: "Test your result for a string that is nearly horizontal (beta close to 90 degrees). Such a string can hardly carry the weight, so the ball must go round very fast. Your result stays finite." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The ball is in uniform circular motion on a horizontal circle. The vertical part of the string's pull carries the weight, and the horizontal part supplies the acceleration to the centre.

<Step title="The circle and the forces">

The string has the length $l$ and makes the angle $\beta$ with the vertical, so the ball is at the horizontal distance $r = l\sin\beta$ from the vertical line through the fixed point. That is the radius of the circle. The pull of the string, of size $T$, has the part $T\cos\beta$ upwards and the part $T\sin\beta$ to the centre of the circle. The weight $mg$ points down.

</Step>

<Step title="The second law in components">

With the radial second law $F_r = -mr\omega^2$ of Problem 1:

<Formula label="Ball on a string: the two components" tex="T\sin\beta = m\,(l\sin\beta)\,\omega^2, \qquad T\cos\beta - m g = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

The first equation gives $T = ml\omega^2$. Put this in the second:

<Formula label="Angular speed of the ball on a string" tex="\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{l\cos\beta}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The unit of $g/l$ is $1/\text{s}^2$, so $\omega$ is in radians per second, and the mass has dropped out. A wider cone needs a faster turn, and a horizontal string ($\beta = 90°$) would need an endless one. This is the same result as for the bead on the hoop in Problem 1, with $l$ in place of $R$: the string pulls along the line to a fixed point just as the hoop pushes along the line to its centre.

A number, with values of our own: $l = 1.5$ m and $\beta = 35°$ give $\omega = \sqrt{9.8/(1.5 \times \cos 35°)} = 2.82$ rad/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

Later the ball comes loose from the string at one point of its circle. (In MIT's drawing a horizontal $x$ axis and $y$ axis lie on the ground, and the ball comes loose as it passes above the $x$ axis.) It flies through the air and lands on the ground. Find the horizontal distance $d$ between the point where it came loose and the point where it lands, in terms of $l$, $\beta$ and $h$.

<Hint>

What is the velocity of the ball at the moment it comes loose: how large, and in which direction?

</Hint>

<Hint>

After that only gravity acts. The ball starts with a horizontal velocity, so horizontally it moves at constant speed while it falls the height $h$ from rest.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Find the time of the fall from $h$ and $g$, and multiply it by the speed $v = r\omega$ with your $\omega$ of part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$d =$" expr="sin(beta)*sqrt(2*h*l/cos(beta))"
  vars={{ beta: [0.3, 1.3], l: [0.5, 2], h: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ beta: "\\beta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2*h*l/cos(beta))", hint: "This follows if the ball's speed is l times omega. The radius of the circle is smaller than l. Test a very small angle: the ball then hardly moves, so d should go to zero." },
    { expr: "sin(beta)*sqrt(h*l/cos(beta))", hint: "Check the time of the fall. A body that falls from rest covers the height g t^2/2, with the factor one half." },
    { expr: "sin(beta)*sqrt(2*h*l*cos(beta))", hint: "Test your result for a string that is nearly horizontal (beta close to 90 degrees). The ball then goes round very fast and should fly very far. Your result goes to zero instead." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At the moment the string lets go, the ball has the velocity of its circular motion: horizontal, along the tangent of the circle. From then on it is a projectile thrown horizontally from the height $h$.

<Step title="The speed at the start">

On a circle of radius $r$ the speed is

<Formula label="Speed on a circle" tex="v = r\,\omega" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (6.2.15), p. 5" />

With $r = l\sin\beta$ and the result of part (a), $v = l\sin\beta\,\sqrt{g/(l\cos\beta)}$. The velocity is horizontal, at right angles to the line from the centre of the circle to the ball.

</Step>

<Step title="The time of the fall">

The ball has no vertical velocity at the start, and it falls with the constant acceleration $g$. After the time $t$ it has fallen $\tfrac12 g t^2$. It lands when this equals $h$:

<Formula label="Time to fall the height h from rest" tex="h = \tfrac12\,g\,t^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The distance">

Nothing pushes the ball horizontally, so it keeps the horizontal speed $v$ and covers $d = vt$:

<Formula label="Horizontal distance of the flight" tex="d = l\sin\beta\,\sqrt{\frac{g}{l\cos\beta}}\,\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}} = \sin\beta\,\sqrt{\frac{2\,h\,l}{\cos\beta}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The root holds a length times a length, so $d$ is a length. Gravity has dropped out: a stronger $g$ makes the ball go round faster and fall sooner, and the two effects cancel. For a very small $\beta$ the ball hardly moves and $d$ goes to zero. The distance is measured along the ground in the direction of the tangent, so the ball does not land on the $x$ axis of MIT's drawing but to the side of it.

With our numbers ($l = 1.5$ m, $\beta = 35°$) and $h = 2.0$ m: the radius is $1.5 \times \sin 35° = 0.860$ m, the speed is $0.860 \times 2.82 = 2.43$ m/s, the fall takes $\sqrt{2 \times 2.0/9.8} = 0.639$ s, and $d = 2.43 \times 0.639 = 1.55$ m. The formula gives $\sin 35° \times \sqrt{2 \times 2.0 \times 1.5/\cos 35°} = 1.55$ m as well.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Two boxes around a shaft" points={8} mit="Problem 4">

A vertical shaft turns with a constant angular velocity of magnitude $\omega$. String A runs straight out from the shaft to box 1, of mass $m_1$, and string B runs on from box 1, in the same line, to box 2, of mass $m_2$. Both boxes go round with the shaft: box 1 on a circle of radius $d$ and box 2 on a circle of radius $2d$. The strings have no mass, and you may leave gravity out.

The course notes work through the same situation as their Example 9.3, and the lesson on [worked problems](/courses/8-01/circular-motion-dynamics-examples) follows it with numbers. Try it here on your own first.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find $T_B$, the tension in string B (between the two boxes), in terms of $d$, $\omega$, $m_1$ and $m_2$.

<Hint>

Start with the outer box. Which forces act on it along the line to the shaft?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Only string B pulls on box 2, and box 2 moves on a circle of radius $2d$. Write the radial second law for it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$T_B =$" expr="2*m2*d*omega^2"
  vars={{ m1: [0.5, 3], m2: [0.5, 3], d: [0.2, 1], omega: [1, 6] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m2*d*omega^2", hint: "Box 2 is at the distance 2d from the axis, not d." },
    { expr: "(m1 + 2*m2)*d*omega^2", hint: "That is the tension in string A, which has to hold both boxes. String B holds only box 2." },
    { expr: "(m1 + m2)*2*d*omega^2", hint: "String B is tied to the outside of box 1 and pulls box 2 only. The mass of box 1 cannot appear in its tension." },
    { expr: "4*m2*d*omega^2", hint: "Your result is twice too large. With the radius 2d the acceleration is the radius times omega squared, 2 d omega^2. Check that you did not square the 2, or divide v^2 by d instead of 2d." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each box is in uniform circular motion, so the forces on it along the radius must add up to its mass times $r\omega^2$, pointing to the shaft. The outer box feels one string only.

<Step title="The second law for box 2">

String B pulls box 2 towards the shaft with the force $T_B$. Nothing else acts along the radius. With $F_r = -mr\omega^2$ and $r = 2d$:

<Formula label="Tension in the outer string" tex="-T_B = -m_2\,(2d)\,\omega^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_B = 2\,m_2\,d\,\omega^2" cite="reading1" at="Example 9.3, p. 9-11 (there the tension is called T₂)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A mass times a length times $1/\text{s}^2$ is a force. The tension does not depend on $m_1$: string B has only box 2 to hold.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find $T_A$, the tension in string A (between the shaft and box 1), in terms of $d$, $\omega$, $m_1$ and $m_2$.

<Hint>

Now look at box 1. Two strings pull on it, in opposite directions.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A string without mass pulls equally hard at both ends, so string B pulls box 1 outwards with your $T_B$ of part (a). Write the radial second law for box 1, on its circle of radius $d$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$T_A =$" expr="(m1 + 2*m2)*d*omega^2"
  vars={{ m1: [0.5, 3], m2: [0.5, 3], d: [0.2, 1], omega: [1, 6] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m1*d*omega^2", hint: "That would be the tension if box 1 were alone. String B also pulls box 1 outwards, so string A has to pull harder." },
    { expr: "(m1 + m2)*d*omega^2", hint: "The two boxes are at different distances from the axis, so they have different accelerations. They cannot be treated as one body at the distance d." },
    { expr: "(m1 - 2*m2)*d*omega^2", hint: "String B pulls box 1 outwards, away from the shaft. String A must make up for that pull and supply the inward force on box 1 as well, so the two terms add." },
    { expr: "(m1 + m2)*2*d*omega^2", hint: "Only box 2 is at the distance 2d. Box 1 moves on the smaller circle, of radius d." },
    { expr: "2*m2*d*omega^2", hint: "That is the tension in string B. String A holds box 1 as well." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Box 1 is pulled inwards by string A and outwards by string B. The difference of the two pulls must supply its acceleration to the shaft.

<Step title="The second law for box 1">

String B has no mass, so it pulls on box 1 as hard as on box 2, with $T_B$, and this pull points outwards. String A pulls inwards with $T_A$. With $r = d$:

<Formula label="Tension in the inner string" tex="T_B - T_A = -m_1\,d\,\omega^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_A = m_1 d\,\omega^2 + T_B = (m_1 + 2m_2)\,d\,\omega^2" cite="reading1" at="Example 9.3, pp. 9-11 and 9-12 (there the tensions are called T₁ and T₂)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Look at the two boxes and string B together as one system. The only outside force along the radius is $T_A$, and it must equal the sum of the two masses times their accelerations: $m_1 d\omega^2 + m_2 (2d)\omega^2$. That is the same result. For two equal boxes of mass $m$ the tensions are $T_A = 3md\omega^2$ and $T_B = 2md\omega^2$: the inner string always carries more.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Two satellites" points={6} mit="Problem 5">

Two satellites, A and B, of the same mass, go round the Earth on circular orbits with the centre of the Earth as their centre. Satellite A is on the larger circle (the higher orbit) and satellite B on the smaller one.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Which satellite moves at the higher speed $v$?

<Hint>

The only force on a satellite is the pull of the Earth's gravity. It must supply the acceleration to the centre.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Set the force of gravity at the distance $r$ from the centre of the Earth equal to $mv^2/r$ and see how $v$ changes with $r$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="The faster satellite is:" choices={["A, on the larger orbit", "B, on the smaller orbit", "neither: they have the same mass, so they have the same speed", "it depends on which way round each one goes"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Gravity alone holds a satellite on its circle. Setting the pull of gravity equal to the mass times the centripetal acceleration fixes the speed for each radius.

<Step title="The second law along the radius">

Let $m_E$ be the mass of the Earth, $m$ the mass of a satellite and $r$ the radius of its orbit, measured from the centre of the Earth. The pull of gravity has the size

<Formula label="Size of the force of gravity" tex="F = G\,\frac{m_E\,m}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.2.2), p. 9-3" />

It points to the centre of the Earth and must equal $m v^2/r$:

<Formula label="Speed on a circular orbit" tex="G\,\frac{m_E\,m}{r^2} = \frac{m\,v^2}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{G\,m_E}{r}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compare the two orbits">

The speed falls as the radius grows. Satellite B, on the smaller orbit, is the faster one.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The mass of the satellite has dropped out, so MIT's remark that the two masses are equal is not needed. Closer to the Earth gravity is stronger and the circle is tighter, and both ask for more speed. For example, on an orbit 4 times as large the speed is $1/\sqrt{4} = 1/2$ as large.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Which satellite has the longer period $T$ (the time for one orbit)?

<Hint>

One orbit is a full circle, of length $2\pi r$, covered at the speed $v$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="The satellite with the longer period is:" choices={["A, on the larger orbit", "B, on the smaller orbit", "neither: they have the same mass, so they have the same period", "it depends on which way round each one goes"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The period is the length of the orbit divided by the speed. The outer satellite has further to go and moves more slowly, so it loses twice.

<Step title="The period">

For uniform circular motion

<Formula label="Period of circular motion" tex="T = \frac{2\pi r}{v}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (6.4.4), p. 8" />

With the speed of part (a):

<Formula label="Period of a circular orbit" tex="T = 2\pi r\,\sqrt{\frac{r}{G\,m_E}} = 2\pi\,\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{G\,m_E}}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.2.4), p. 9-5 (written there for the Moon)" />

The period grows with the radius, so satellite A, on the larger orbit, takes longer.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Squaring gives $T^2 \propto r^3$, which is Kepler's third law for circular orbits, as in the lesson. On an orbit 4 times as large the path is 4 times as long and the speed is half, so the period is $4 \times 2 = 8$ times as long, and indeed $4^{3/2} = 8$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="A coin on a disk that speeds up" points={10} mit="Problem 6">

A coin of mass $m$ lies on a horizontal, rigid disk, at the distance $d$ from the centre. Between the coin and the disk the coefficient of static friction is $\mu_s$. The disk is at rest until the time $t = 0$. Then it starts to turn about the vertical axis through its centre, with a constant angular acceleration of magnitude $\alpha$. The magnitude of the acceleration of gravity is $g$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

As long as the coin does not slip it goes round with the disk. Find the magnitude $f_s$ of the static friction force on the coin at the time $t$, in terms of $m$, $d$, $\alpha$ and $t$.

<Hint>

Friction is the only horizontal force on the coin, so it must supply the whole acceleration of the coin. The coin is on a circle, but not at constant speed.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The acceleration has two parts at right angles: one along the circle, because the coin speeds up, and one to the centre, because it turns. What is the angular speed of the disk at the time $t$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The tangential part is $d\alpha$ and the radial part is $d\omega^2$ with $\omega = \alpha t$. The size of a vector with two parts at right angles follows from Pythagoras.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-a" label="$f_s =$" expr="m*d*alpha*sqrt(1 + alpha^2*t^4)"
  vars={{ m: [0.01, 0.1], d: [0.1, 0.5], alpha: [0.5, 3], t: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*d*alpha", hint: "That is the tangential part only, which is the whole force at t = 0. Once the disk turns, the coin also has an acceleration to the centre." },
    { expr: "m*d*alpha^2*t^2", hint: "That is the part to the centre only. The coin also speeds up along its circle, and friction must supply that tangential acceleration too." },
    { expr: "m*d*alpha*(1 + alpha*t^2)", hint: "The two parts of the acceleration are at right angles, so their sizes do not simply add. Use Pythagoras." },
    { expr: "m*d*alpha*sqrt(1 + alpha^2*t^2)", hint: "The acceleration to the centre is d times omega squared, and omega is alpha t. Check the power of t." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The coin moves on a circle of radius $d$ with a growing speed. Its acceleration has a tangential and a radial part, and static friction, the only horizontal force on it, supplies both.

<Step title="The two parts of the acceleration">

For motion on a circle of radius $r$ the acceleration has the components

<Formula label="Tangential and radial acceleration on a circle" tex="a_\theta = r\,\alpha, \qquad a_r = -r\,\omega^2" cite="reading2" at="Eqs. (6.3.7), p. 6, and (6.3.8), p. 7 (written there with the second and the first time derivative of θ in place of α and ω)" />

Here $\alpha$ is the rate at which the angular speed $\omega$ grows (we take the sense of turning as positive), and the radius is the coin's distance from the centre, $r = d$. The angular acceleration is constant and the disk starts from rest, so $\omega = \alpha t$. The tangential part is $d\alpha$, constant in time, and the radial part has the size $d\,\alpha^2 t^2$, which grows.

</Step>

<Step title="The friction force">

Vertically the weight and the normal force balance. Horizontally friction is the only force, so $\vec f_s = m\vec a$. The two parts of $\vec a$ are at right angles:

<Formula label="Static friction on the coin" tex="f_s = m\sqrt{(d\alpha)^2 + (d\,\alpha^2 t^2)^2} = m\,d\,\alpha\,\sqrt{1 + \alpha^2 t^4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The unit of $\alpha$ is $1/\text{s}^2$, so $\alpha^2 t^4$ has no unit and $m d\alpha$ is a mass times $\text{m/s}^2$: a force. At $t = 0$ the force is $md\alpha$, purely tangential: the disk drags the coin into motion. Much later the root is close to $\alpha t^2$ and the force is close to $md\,\omega^2$, the force for uniform circular motion. The friction force does not point to the centre: it leans forwards, in the direction of turning, and less so as time goes on.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

At which angular speed $\omega$ of the disk does the coin start to slip? Give it in terms of $d$, $\mu_s$, $\alpha$ and $g$. (Take $\mu_s g > d\alpha$. This remark is ours: otherwise the coin slips at once.)

<Hint>

The coin slips when the friction it needs is larger than the friction the disk can give. How large can static friction be here?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the needed friction of part (a) with $\omega$ in place of $\alpha t$, set it equal to $\mu_s N$ with the normal force of a horizontal disk, and solve for $\omega$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-b" label="$\omega =$" expr="((mu*g/d)^2 - alpha^2)^(1/4)"
  vars={{ mu: [0.3, 0.8], g: [9, 10], d: [0.1, 0.5], alpha: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ mu: "\\mu_s", alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(mu*g/d)", hint: "That is the result for a disk that turns at a constant rate. Here friction must also supply the tangential acceleration, so the coin slips a little sooner." },
    { expr: "((mu*g/d)^2 + alpha^2)^(1/4)", hint: "A larger angular acceleration uses up more of the friction, so the coin should slip at a lower angular speed. In your result it slips at a higher one. Check the sign." },
    { expr: "sqrt((mu*g/d)^2 - alpha^2)", hint: "Check the unit: mu g/d and alpha are both in 1 over seconds squared, so this root is in 1 over seconds squared too. That is the square of an angular speed." },
    { expr: "sqrt(mu*g/d - alpha)", hint: "The two parts of the acceleration are at right angles, so it is their squares that add up to the square of mu g. Use Pythagoras before you solve for omega." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Static friction supplies whatever the motion needs, up to its largest value $\mu_s N$. The coin slips at the moment the friction of part (a) reaches that value.

<Step title="The largest friction">

The disk is horizontal and the coin has no vertical acceleration, so $N = mg$. The largest static friction is $(f_s)_\text{max} = \mu_s N = \mu_s m g$.

</Step>

<Step title="The condition for slipping">

In terms of the angular speed $\omega = \alpha t$ the friction of part (a) is $f_s = m d\sqrt{\alpha^2 + \omega^4}$. Set it equal to the largest value:

<Formula label="Coin on a disk that speeds up: the moment of slipping" tex="m\,d\,\sqrt{\alpha^2 + \omega^4} = \mu_s\,m\,g" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b) (our own solution)" />

Divide by $md$, square, and solve for $\omega$:

<Formula label="Angular speed at which the coin slips" tex="\omega = \left[\left(\frac{\mu_s\,g}{d}\right)^2 - \alpha^2\right]^{1/4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both $\mu_s g/d$ and $\alpha$ are in $1/\text{s}^2$, so the fourth root is in 1/s. The mass has dropped out. For a disk that speeds up very gently ($\alpha$ going to zero) the result becomes $\sqrt{\mu_s g/d}$, which is the largest angular speed for a coin on a turntable at a constant rate:

<Formula label="Largest angular speed on a steady turntable" tex="\omega_\max = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_s\,g}{R}}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.3.21), p. 9-16 (there the coin is at the distance R)" />

A larger $\alpha$ lowers the result, and at $d\alpha = \mu_s g$ it is zero: the tangential force alone then needs all the friction there is.

A number, with values of our own: $d = 0.15$ m, $\mu_s = 0.40$, $\alpha = 2.0\ \text{rad/s}^2$ and $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ give $\mu_s g/d = 26.1\ \text{s}^{-2}$ and $\omega = (26.13^2 - 2.0^2)^{1/4} = 5.10$ rad/s, a little below the $\sqrt{26.13} = 5.11$ rad/s of a steady disk. The disk reaches this angular speed after $5.10/2.0 = 2.55$ s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
