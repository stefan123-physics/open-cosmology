The [previous lesson](/courses/8-01/torque-and-cross-product) defined torque. This one derives how torque changes rotation, and uses it, together with Newton's second law, on pulleys that have mass.

## Torque and angular acceleration

Take a body rotating about the $z$-axis with angular acceleration $\alpha_z$, and split it into elements $\Delta m_j$, each pushed by an external force $\vec F_j$ (31.1 transcript, p. 1). Write each force in cylindrical components, $F_{j,r}\hat r + F_{j,\theta}\hat\theta + F_{j,z}\hat k$, and the vector from S as a part along the axis plus a part $r_j\,\hat r$ out from it (notes, Eq. (17.3.1), p. 17-15). Anything crossed with $\hat k$, and $\hat r$ crossed with $\hat k$, is perpendicular to the axis, and $\hat r \times \hat r = 0$; so only the **tangential** part of the force, crossed with $r_j\,\hat r$, gives torque along the axis, using $\hat r \times \hat\theta = \hat k$ (pp. 2–3; notes, Eqs. (17.3.3)–(17.3.8), pp. 17-16–17-17). Newton's second law for the element in the tangential direction, with the tangential acceleration $a_{j,\theta} = r_j\alpha_z$ that is shared through $\alpha_z$, gives $F_{j,\theta} = \Delta m_j\,r_j\,\alpha_z$ (pp. 3–4). Adding up (p. 4; notes, Eqs. (17.3.12)–(17.3.14), p. 17-17):

<Formula label="Torque and angular acceleration" tex="\tau_{S,z} = \sum_{j=1}^{N} r_j F_{j,\theta} = \Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N}\Delta m_j\,r_j^2\Big)\,\alpha_z = I_S\,\alpha_z" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–4" />

This holds for any body rotating about a fixed axis, not just a disc (p. 4).

## Internal torques cancel in pairs

Why only external forces? Take two elements $i$ and $j$ with third-law forces $\vec F_{j,i} = -\vec F_{i,j}$ between them (31.2 transcript, p. 1). Their torques about S add to (p. 1; notes, Eqs. (17.3.19)–(17.3.21), p. 17-18):

<Formula label="A pair of internal torques" tex="\vec r_{S,i} \times \vec F_{j,i} + \vec r_{S,j} \times \vec F_{i,j} = (\vec r_{S,i} - \vec r_{S,j}) \times \vec F_{j,i} = 0" cite="reading3" at="pp. 1–2" />

because $\vec r_{S,i} - \vec r_{S,j}$ points along the line joining the elements, and we **assume** the forces between them act along that line, so the two vectors are parallel or antiparallel (pp. 1–2). The notes call this a stronger form of the third law (notes, p. 17-19). So $\tau^{\text{ext}}_{S,z} = I_S\,\alpha_z$.

## The rope and the pulley

(This page takes the non-slip condition, segment 31.6, and the method of segment 31.5 before the worked examples of 31.3 and 31.4, which use them.) A rope wrapped round a pulley of radius $R$ ties the pulley's rotation to the motion of a hanging block. If the rope does not slip and has fixed length, a point of the rope on the rim moves with the rim, and the hanging part unwinds by the same arc length $s = R\theta$ (segment 31.6, text page):

<Formula label="Non-slip condition" tex="v = \frac{ds}{dt} = R\,\frac{d\theta}{dt}, \qquad a = R\,\alpha" cite="reading7" at="text page" />

The sign depends on the positive directions chosen. The method for massive-pulley problems (31.5 transcript, pp. 1–2) is: first choose the positive $x$ and $y$ directions, which by the right-hand rule fix the positive sense of rotation; write $F = ma$ for each block and $\tau = I\alpha$ for each pulley, leaving the unknown accelerations with plus signs; then write the constraint. For a block hanging from a pulley with $y$ up, raising the block turns the pulley clockwise, the negative sense, so $\Delta y = -R\,\Delta\theta$ and $a_1 = -R\alpha$ (p. 2). Choosing the directions at the start saves "a negative sign nightmare" later (p. 1).

## Measuring a moment of inertia

A wheel of radius $R$ turns on its axle; a string wound round it holds a small mass $m$, which is released and falls a height $h$ in a time $t$ (31.3 transcript, p. 1). For the wheel, the string's tension gives $RT = I_{\text{cm}}\alpha$; for the mass, $mg - T = ma$; the non-slip condition gives $a = R\alpha$; and for constant acceleration from rest $h = \frac{1}{2}at^2$ (pp. 1–3). Eliminating $T$, $\alpha$ and $a$ leaves only measurable quantities (p. 3):

<Formula label="Moment of inertia from a falling mass" tex="I_{\text{cm}} = mR^2\left(\frac{g}{a} - 1\right) = mR^2\left(\frac{gt^2}{2h} - 1\right)" cite="reading4" at="pp. 2–3" />

The notes do the same experiment while also measuring the friction in the axle, from how the wheel slows down afterwards (notes, Example 17.11, pp. 17-23–17-25).

## An Atwood machine with a massive pulley

Two blocks, $m_2 > m_1$, hang from a rope over a pulley of radius $R$ and moment of inertia $I$; the rope does not slip (31.4 transcript, p. 1). Now the tension is **not** the same on both sides: call them $T_1$ and $T_2$ (pp. 1–2). Choose $\hat j_1$ up for block 1 and $\hat j_2$ down for block 2, so both accelerations are the same positive $a$ (p. 2):

<Formula label="Atwood machine: the three equations" tex="T_1 - m_1g = m_1a, \qquad m_2g - T_2 = m_2a, \qquad (T_2 - T_1)R = I\alpha" cite="reading5" at="pp. 2–4" />

If the tensions were equal, there would be no torque and the pulley could not speed up (p. 4). With the constraint $a = R\alpha$, consistent with the chosen directions (p. 4), solving gives (p. 5):

<Formula label="Atwood machine with a massive pulley" tex="a = \frac{(m_2 - m_1)\,g}{m_1 + m_2 + I/R^2}" cite="reading5" at="p. 5" />

Checks: $a > 0$ when $m_2 > m_1$, and $I/R^2$ has units of mass, so $a$ has units of $g$ (p. 5). The pulley acts like an extra mass $I/R^2$ to be accelerated; for a massless pulley the old result returns.

## The same problem with energy

Energy conservation, now with rotational kinetic energy, gives the same kind of answer (31.7 transcript, p. 1; notes, Section 16.4 and Example 16.4, pp. 16-12–16-14, where block 2 slides on an incline instead). Block 1 lies on a frictionless table, joined by a rope over a pulley (moment of inertia $I$, radius $R$) to block 2, which hangs. Released from rest, block 2 drops a height $h$. With no friction, mechanical energy is conserved, and the kinetic energy now includes the pulley's $\frac{1}{2}I\omega^2$ (pp. 1–2). The rope gives $v_1 = v_2 = v$ and $\omega = v/R$ (p. 3), so

<Formula label="Two blocks and a pulley, by energy" tex="0 = \frac{1}{2}\left(m_1 + m_2 + \frac{I}{R^2}\right)v^2 - m_2gh \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{2m_2gh}{m_1 + m_2 + I/R^2}}" cite="reading8" at="pp. 3–4" />

(pp. 3–4). Again the pulley enters as an extra mass $I/R^2$.

<WorkedExample title="A wheel, an Atwood machine and a table">
<Problem>
(a) A 0.50 kg mass on a string wound round a wheel of radius 0.20 m falls 1.0 m in 2.0 s from rest. Find the wheel's moment of inertia. (b) Blocks of 2.0 kg and 3.0 kg hang over a uniform disc pulley of mass 2.0 kg and radius 0.10 m. Find the acceleration and both tensions. (c) A 1.0 kg block on a frictionless table is pulled by a 0.50 kg hanging block over a pulley with $I/R^2 = 0.50$ kg. How fast is it going after the hanging block drops 0.80 m?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The wheel">
$a = 2h/t^2 = 2 \times 1.0/4.0 = 0.50$ m/s², so $I = 0.50 \times 0.20^2 \times (9.8/0.50 - 1) = 0.020 \times 18.6 \approx 0.37$ kg m².
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The Atwood machine">
For a uniform disc $I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2$, so $I/R^2 = 1.0$ kg and $a = 1.0 \times 9.8/(2.0 + 3.0 + 1.0) \approx 1.63$ m/s². Then $T_1 = m_1(g + a) \approx 22.9$ N and $T_2 = m_2(g - a) \approx 24.5$ N. Check: $T_2 - T_1 \approx 1.63$ N equals $(I/R^2)\,a$, as the pulley equation requires.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Energy">
$v = \sqrt{2 \times 0.50 \times 9.8 \times 0.80/(1.0 + 0.50 + 0.50)} = \sqrt{3.92} \approx 1.98$ m/s. With a massless pulley it would be $\sqrt{7.84/1.5} \approx 2.29$ m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="A star being braked">
The Crab pulsar slows down: its period of 0.0334 s (catalogue epoch 1991) grows by about $4.2 \times 10^{-13}$ s every second (ATNF pulsar catalogue). That is an angular deceleration of about $2.4 \times 10^{-9}$ rad/s². With a moment of inertia of about $1 \times 10^{38}$ kg m² ([Lesson 29](/courses/8-01/moment-of-inertia)), $\tau = I\alpha$ means a braking torque of about $2.4 \times 10^{29}$ N m, from the star's spinning magnetic field. The power drained from the spin, $\tau\omega$, is about $4.5 \times 10^{31}$ W, enough to keep the Crab Nebula shining.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Fixed-axis rotation: $\tau^{\text{ext}}_{S,z} = I_S\,\alpha_z$; only tangential forces contribute.
- Internal torques cancel in pairs if internal forces act along the lines joining the elements.
- A rope that does not slip: $a = R\alpha$, with signs fixed by the directions chosen at the start.
- Massive pulleys: the tensions on the two sides differ; the pulley acts like an extra mass $I/R^2$.
- Energy conservation with $\frac{1}{2}I\omega^2$ gives speeds directly.
