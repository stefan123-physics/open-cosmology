The [previous lesson](/courses/8-01/angular-momentum-point-particle) defined the angular momentum of a single particle. Now add up the particles of a rotating body.

## Two particles circling an axis

Two particles of mass $m$ go round a circle of radius $R$, at opposite ends of a diameter, counterclockwise about the $z$-axis, so $\vec\omega = \omega_z\hat k$ with $\omega_z > 0$ (33.1 transcript, p. 1). Take S on the axis, a height $h$ below the plane of the circle (p. 1). For particle 1, use cylindrical unit vectors with $\hat r_1$ pointing out to it: $\vec r_{S,1} = R\,\hat r_1 + h\,\hat k$ and $\vec p_1 = mR\omega_z\,\hat\theta_1$ (p. 2). With $\hat r \times \hat\theta = \hat k$ and $\hat k \times \hat\theta = -\hat r$ (pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="One particle, about S" tex="\vec L_{S,1} = (R\,\hat r_1 + h\,\hat k) \times mR\omega_z\,\hat\theta_1 = mR^2\omega_z\,\hat k - hmR\omega_z\,\hat r_1" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3" />

The second term is perpendicular to the axis, so $\vec L_{S,1}$ as a whole is tilted away from it. But particle 2 is on the opposite side, $\hat r_2 = -\hat r_1$, so its tilted term is opposite and the two cancel (p. 3; notes, Example 19.5, Eq. (19.5.10), pp. 19-14–19-15):

<Formula label="Two particles, about S" tex="\vec L_S = \vec L_{S,1} + \vec L_{S,2} = 2mR^2\omega_z\,\hat k" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

The total points along the axis, and $2m$ is the total mass. Adding more pairs round the rim builds a ring, with the same form: total mass times $R^2\omega_z$ (p. 4).

## Symmetric bodies

For a ring of mass $M$ and radius $R$ rotating about its symmetry axis, pair each small piece with the piece opposite it. Each pair gives (pair mass)$\,R^2\vec\omega$ about any point S on the axis, and summing the pairs gives (33.2 transcript, pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Ring about its axis" tex="\vec L_S = MR^2\,\vec\omega = I\,\vec\omega" cite="reading3" at="pp. 1–2" />

Any body that is symmetric about the rotation axis (the lecture draws a pear shape) is a stack of discs, each a set of rings, so the same argument works (p. 2): **for a body symmetric about its rotation axis, the angular momentum about any point on the axis points along $\vec\omega$, and $\vec L = I\vec\omega$**, with the moment of inertia about that axis (p. 2).

## Any body on a fixed axis

What if the body is not symmetric? The notes split each element's position from S into a part along the axis and a part $r_{\perp,j}$ out from it (notes, Section 19.7, Eq. (19.5.22), pp. 19-18–19-19). Just as for one particle above, the part along the axis produces a term perpendicular to the axis, so the component **along** the axis comes only from the perpendicular distances (Eqs. (19.5.23)–(19.5.27), p. 19-19):

<Formula label="Fixed axis: the component along the axis" tex="L_{S,z} = \Big(\sum_j m_j r_{\perp,j}^2\Big)\,\omega_z = I_S\,\omega_z" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (19.5.27), p. 19-19" />

This always holds. Then $\tau_{S,z} = dL_{S,z}/dt = I_S\alpha_z$, the torque equation of the earlier lesson (Eq. (19.5.29), p. 19-19). But the perpendicular part need not cancel. The course summarises (segment 33.3, text page):

- If the axis is **not** a symmetry axis (say it passes through one end of an object), the angular momentum about a point on the axis depends on where that point is. (If the axis passes through the centre of mass, the total momentum is zero and, by the section below, it does not.)
- In that case $\vec L$ is parallel to $\vec\omega$ only about the point where the axis crosses the object, $\vec L = I\vec\omega$ there. (This holds for a flat body turning in its own plane, like a rod swung about one end.)
- For a symmetric object spinning about its symmetry axis, $\vec L_S = I_{\text{cm}}\vec\omega$ for every point S on the axis.

## When the total momentum is zero

Segment 33.4 proves the result already met in the [previous lesson](/courses/8-01/angular-momentum-point-particle): for any two fixed points A and B, $\vec r_{B,j} = \vec r_{B,A} + \vec r_{A,j}$, and since the vector product distributes over sums (33.4 transcript, pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Angular momentum about two points" tex="\vec L_B = \vec r_{B,A} \times \vec p_{\text{sys}} + \vec L_A" cite="reading5" at="p. 2" />

So if $\vec p_{\text{sys}} = 0$, the angular momentum is the same about every point (p. 2). In the centre-of-mass frame the total momentum is zero by definition, which is one more reason that frame matters: "the angular momentum of a system" usually means the angular momentum in the centre-of-mass frame, which needs no point to be named (p. 2).

## Kinetic energy in terms of angular momentum

For a symmetric body spinning about its axis, $L = I\omega$ and $K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2$. Eliminating $\omega = L/I$ (33.5 transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Rotational kinetic energy" tex="K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2}I\omega^2 = \frac{L^2}{2I}" cite="reading6" at="p. 1" />

just as $p = mv$ turns $\frac{1}{2}mv^2$ into $\frac{p^2}{2m}$: angular momentum and moment of inertia play the roles of momentum and mass (p. 1).

<WorkedExample title="A dumbbell, a lopsided rotor and a flywheel">
<Problem>
(a) Two 0.30 kg balls on a light rod, each 0.40 m from the axis, spin at 5.0 rad/s about an axis through the middle of the rod. Find $L$ and $K$. (b) A single 0.50 kg ball goes round a circle of radius 0.20 m at 4.0 rad/s. Find its angular momentum about a point on the axis 0.30 m below the circle's plane. (c) A uniform 10 kg disc of radius 0.30 m spins at 100 rad/s. Find $L$ and $K$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A symmetric pair">
$I = 2 \times 0.30 \times 0.40^2 = 0.096$ kg m², so $L = 0.096 \times 5.0 = 0.48$ kg m²/s along the axis, and $K = \frac{L^2}{2I} = \frac{0.2304}{0.192} = 1.2$ J, the same as $\frac{1}{2}I\omega^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) No partner">
From the one-particle formula: along the axis, $mR^2\omega = 0.50 \times 0.04 \times 4.0 = 0.080$ kg m²/s; perpendicular to it, $hmR\omega = 0.30 \times 0.50 \times 0.20 \times 4.0 = 0.12$ kg m²/s, pointing inward towards the axis and rotating with the ball. The perpendicular part is larger than the axial part, and it keeps changing direction; changing angular momentum needs a torque (next lesson), and keeping an off-centre mass on its circle needs a rotating force too ($mR\omega^2 = 1.6$ N here), which is why a lopsided rotor makes its bearings shake.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A flywheel">
$I = \frac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 0.30^2 = 0.45$ kg m², $L = 45$ kg m²/s and $K = \frac{45^2}{2 \times 0.45} = 2250$ J.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="Spinning up by shrinking">
$K = L^2/2I$ hints at what the coming lessons show: when no external torque acts, $L$ stays the same, so a body that pulls its mass inwards, lowering $I$, spins faster and gains kinetic energy from the work done in pulling in. A skater drawing in her arms is the classic example; a collapsing star core, shrinking to a neutron star, is the extreme one.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Two particles symmetric about the axis: the parts of $\vec L$ perpendicular to the axis cancel, leaving $2mR^2\omega_z\hat k$.
- A body symmetric about its rotation axis: $\vec L = I\vec\omega$ about any point on the axis.
- Any body on a fixed axis: $L_{S,z} = I_S\omega_z$; there may also be a perpendicular part, which in general depends on S (but not if the centre of mass is on the axis).
- If the total momentum is zero, $\vec L$ is the same about every point; in the centre-of-mass frame this is always so.
- $K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{L^2}{2I}$, the analogue of $\frac{p^2}{2m}$.
