This set practises the lessons on [relative velocity and recoil](/courses/8-01/relative-velocity-and-recoil) and on [the rocket equation](/courses/8-01/rocket-equation). The five problems follow MIT's Problem Set 6, in our own words. MIT's set comes with drawings; here each situation is described in words, so make your own sketch first. MIT publishes no solutions for this set, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. One remark: MIT's course notes work the rocket of Problem 3 as two examples (Examples 12.4 and 12.5), and the lesson on the rocket equation quotes their results. Try the problem before you look there. All five problems count for the score (60 points).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first: sketch the situation, say which observer measures each velocity, choose a positive direction, and write down the momentum before and after. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A formula box takes plain typing, for example `v1 + m*u/(m1 + m)`, `sqrt(g/k)` or `m0*v0/(m0 + b*t)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. A number box accepts any value within 1% of ours, so keep at least three significant figures, and type it in the unit shown next to the box. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ where a number is needed.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Rowing across the river" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A student wants to row across a river whose banks are straight and parallel, 800 m apart. The water flows downstream at a constant 1.0 m/s. A second boat drifts with the current, without rowing. Seen from that drifting boat, the student rows at 0.5 m/s at right angles to the current, straight towards the far bank.

Use axes fixed to the bank: $x$ points straight across the river, from the starting bank to the far bank, and $y$ points downstream.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

An observer stands still on the bank. For this observer, what is the speed of the student, and what is the angle $\theta$ between the student's velocity and the straight-across direction ($+x$)? In MIT's figure the angle is measured in this way: from the line straight across the river, turning towards downstream.

<Hint>

The drifting boat moves with the water, so it is a reference frame that moves at 1.0 m/s downstream relative to the bank.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The velocity seen from the bank is the velocity seen from the drifting boat plus the velocity of the drifting boat itself. Add them as vectors: they are at right angles.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-speed" label="Speed $=$" value="1.12" unit="m/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-speed" />

<Answer id="p1-a-angle" label="$\theta =$" value="63.4" unit="degrees" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-angle" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The drifting boat is a moving reference frame. The lesson's law of addition of velocities turns a velocity measured there into a velocity measured from the bank.

<Step title="The law of addition of velocities">

Let $\vec V$ be the velocity of the drifting boat relative to the bank, $\vec v\,'$ the student's velocity seen from the drifting boat and $\vec v$ the student's velocity seen from the bank. The notes give

<Formula label="Law of addition of velocities" tex="\vec v\,' = \vec v - \vec V" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.3.2), p. 11-4" />

so that $\vec v = \vec v\,' + \vec V$.

</Step>

<Step title="Components">

Seen from the drifting boat the student moves straight across: $\vec v\,'$ has the $x$ part 0.5 m/s and no $y$ part. The drifting boat moves with the water: $\vec V$ has no $x$ part and the $y$ part 1.0 m/s. The sum has the parts

<Formula label="Rowing: velocity seen from the bank" tex="v_x = 0.5\ \text{m/s}, \qquad v_y = 1.0\ \text{m/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Speed and angle">

The speed is $\sqrt{0.5^2 + 1.0^2} = 1.12$ m/s. The angle from the $+x$ direction follows from $\tan\theta = v_y/v_x = 1.0/0.5 = 2$, so $\theta = 63.4°$, turned towards downstream.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The current is faster than the rowing, so the student moves more downstream than across, and the angle from the straight-across line must be larger than 45°. It is. The speed is larger than each of its two parts and smaller than their sum, 1.5 m/s, as the long side of a right-angled triangle must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

How far downstream of the starting point does the student land on the far bank? Give the distance along the bank, in metres.

<Hint>

Only the $x$ part of the velocity carries the student across the 800 m. How long does the crossing take?

</Hint>

<Hint>

During all of that time the $y$ part of the velocity carries the student downstream.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="Distance downstream $=$" value="1600" unit="m" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-drift" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two parts of the motion do not disturb each other. The $x$ part decides how long the crossing takes, and the $y$ part decides how far the student drifts in that time.

<Step title="Time, then distance">

The crossing takes $800/0.5 = 1600$ s. In that time the student moves $1.0 \times 1600 = 1600$ m downstream.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The path seen from the bank is a straight line at the angle $\theta$ of part (a), so the drift is also $800 \times \tan\theta = 800 \times 2 = 1600$ m. The two routes agree.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How long does the student take to reach the far bank, in seconds?

<Hint>

The width of the river is measured along $x$. Use the $x$ part of the velocity only.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="Time $=$" value="1600" unit="s" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-time" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The current is along the bank, so it neither helps nor hinders the crossing.

<Step title="The crossing time">

The time is the width divided by the $x$ part of the velocity: $800/0.5 = 1600$ s, a little under 27 minutes.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Seen from the drifting boat there is no current at all: the student rows 800 m straight across at 0.5 m/s, which also takes 1600 s. Both observers use the same clocks, so they must agree on the time. Seen from the bank, the student covers a path of $\sqrt{800^2 + 1600^2} = 1789$ m at 1.118 m/s, which takes $1789/1.118 = 1600$ s again.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Astronauts playing catch" points={12} mit="Problem 2">

Two astronauts float far from any planet or star, so that no gravity acts on them. Both drift to the right along the same line. Astronaut 1, of mass $m_1$, is in front and moves with speed $v_1$. Astronaut 2, of mass $m_2$, is behind her (to her left) and moves with speed $v_2 > v_1$.

Astronaut 1 holds a ball of mass $m$. She throws it backwards, against her own direction of motion, towards astronaut 2. The ball leaves with speed $u$ **relative to astronaut 1**. Astronaut 2 catches the ball and keeps it. In the end astronaut 1 moves with speed $v_{f,1}$ and astronaut 2 with speed $v_{f,2}$.

Two points that MIT's text leaves open are fixed here, and both are our reading: $m_1$ is the mass of astronaut 1 without the ball (MIT's figure labels the ball $m$ apart from her), and $u$ is the speed of the ball relative to astronaut 1 as she moves just after the throw, which is the convention of the lesson on recoil.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the speed $v_{f,1}$ of astronaut 1 after the throw, using any of $m$, $m_1$, $m_2$, $u$ and $v_1$ that you need.

<Hint>

Take astronaut 1 and the ball as the system. No force acts on it from outside, so its momentum is the same before and after the throw.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Momentum must be counted by one observer. Take the direction of motion as positive. If astronaut 1 moves at $v_{f,1}$ after the throw and the ball moves backwards at $u$ relative to her, what is the velocity of the ball in the frame in which $v_1$ was measured?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Before the throw, astronaut 1 and the ball move together at $v_1$. Write the momentum before and after, and solve for $v_{f,1}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$v_{f,1} =$" expr="v1 + m*u/(m1 + m)"
  vars={{ v1: [1, 3], u: [5, 20], m: [5, 20], m1: [40, 60] }}
  names={{ v1: "v_1", m1: "m_1" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v1 + m*u/m1", hint: "This follows if the ball moves at u relative to astronaut 1 as she moved before the throw, so that its velocity is v1 - u. Here u is measured relative to her after the throw, when she already moves at her final speed." },
    { expr: "v1 - m*u/(m1 + m)", hint: "The ball is thrown backwards, so the astronaut is pushed forwards. She must end up faster than v1, not slower." },
    { expr: "((m1 + m)*v1 + m*u)/m1", hint: "This takes the velocity of the ball to be -u in the frame in which v1 is measured. The speed u is relative to astronaut 1, who is herself moving: add her velocity." },
    { expr: "m*u/(m1 + m)", hint: "That is the speed she gains. She already moved at v1 before the throw." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A throw is a recoil. The system of astronaut 1 and the ball feels no outside force, so its momentum does not change. The one trap is the speed $u$, which is given relative to the thrower and has to be turned into a velocity for the observer who measures $v_1$.

<Step title="The velocity of the ball">

Take the direction of motion (to the right) as positive. After the throw astronaut 1 has the velocity $v_{f,1}$. The ball moves backwards at $u$ relative to her, so by the law of addition of velocities its velocity is

<Formula label="Catch: velocity of the thrown ball" tex="v_\text{ball} = v_{f,1} - u" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Momentum before and after the throw">

Before the throw the astronaut and the ball move together at $v_1$:

<Formula label="Catch: momentum of astronaut 1 and the ball" tex="(m_1 + m)\,v_1 = m_1\,v_{f,1} + m\,(v_{f,1} - u)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

Collect the terms with $v_{f,1}$: $(m_1 + m)\,v_{f,1} = (m_1 + m)\,v_1 + m u$, so

<Formula label="Catch: speed of astronaut 1 after the throw" tex="v_{f,1} = v_1 + \frac{m\,u}{m_1 + m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

The mass $m_2$ does not appear: astronaut 2 has no part in the throw.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The second term is a speed times a ratio of masses, so it is a speed. For a ball of no mass the astronaut keeps $v_1$. For a ball much heavier than the astronaut the gain goes to $u$: the heavy ball hardly changes its velocity, and she pushes herself away from it at $u$. An example with our own numbers: $m_1 = 60$ kg, $m = 4$ kg, $v_1 = 1.0$ m/s and $u = 8.0$ m/s give $v_{f,1} = 1.0 + 32/64 = 1.5$ m/s. The ball then moves at $1.5 - 8.0 = -6.5$ m/s. The momentum before is $64 \times 1.0 = 64$ kg·m/s, and after it is $60 \times 1.5 - 4 \times 6.5 = 64$ kg·m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

How fast must the ball be thrown (the speed $u$ relative to astronaut 1) for the two astronauts to end with the same speed, $v_{f,1} = v_{f,2}$? Use any of $m$, $m_1$, $m_2$, $v_1$ and $v_2$ that you need.

<Hint>

Now take both astronauts and the ball as the system. Its momentum is the same at the start and at the very end.

</Hint>

<Hint>

At the very end astronaut 1 moves at the common speed, and astronaut 2, holding the ball, moves at that same speed. That gives the common speed without any $u$ in it.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put the common speed in the place of $v_{f,1}$ in your result of part (a), and solve for $u$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$u =$" expr="m2*(m1 + m)*(v2 - v1)/(m*(m1 + m2 + m))"
  vars={{ v1: [1, 3], v2: [4, 8], m: [5, 20], m1: [40, 60], m2: [70, 100] }}
  names={{ v1: "v_1", v2: "v_2", m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m1*m2*(v2 - v1)/(m*(m1 + m2 + m))", hint: "This follows if u is measured relative to astronaut 1 as she moved before the throw. Here u is relative to her after the throw, as in part (a)." },
    { expr: "m2*(m1 + m)*(v1 - v2)/(m*(m1 + m2 + m))", hint: "A speed is positive. Astronaut 2 is the faster one, so the difference to use is v2 - v1." },
    { expr: "(m1 + m)*(v2 - v1)/(2*m)", hint: "This takes the common final speed to be the average of v1 and v2. The common speed follows from the momentum of the whole system, and it depends on the masses." },
    { expr: "(m1 + m)*(v2 - v1)/m", hint: "This would bring astronaut 1 up to v2. But the ball that astronaut 2 catches is slower than he is, so he slows down, and the common speed is below v2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Throw and catch together are one long exchange inside the system of two astronauts and a ball. No outside force acts, so the total momentum at the very end equals the total momentum at the start. If everything ends with one common speed, that speed is fixed by the total momentum alone.

<Step title="The common final speed">

Call the common final speed $v_f$. At the start astronaut 1 and the ball move at $v_1$ and astronaut 2 at $v_2$. At the end all three move at $v_f$:

<Formula label="Catch: common final speed" tex="(m_1 + m)\,v_1 + m_2\,v_2 = (m_1 + m_2 + m)\,v_f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_f = \frac{(m_1 + m)\,v_1 + m_2\,v_2}{m_1 + m_2 + m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The speed that astronaut 1 has to gain">

Subtract $v_1$:

<Formula label="Catch: gain of astronaut 1" tex="v_f - v_1 = \frac{m_2\,(v_2 - v_1)}{m_1 + m_2 + m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The throw that gives this gain">

By part (a) a throw at $u$ gives astronaut 1 the gain $m u/(m_1 + m)$. Setting the two equal:

<Formula label="Catch: throwing speed for equal final speeds" tex="u = \frac{m_1 + m}{m}\,(v_f - v_1) = \frac{m_2\,(m_1 + m)\,(v_2 - v_1)}{m\,(m_1 + m_2 + m)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is a speed times ratios of masses. It is positive because $v_2 > v_1$, and it is zero for $v_2 = v_1$: astronauts who already move together need no throw. A light ball must be thrown fast, since $u$ grows as $1/m$ for small $m$. If astronaut 2 is very heavy, $u$ goes to $(m_1 + m)(v_2 - v_1)/m$: he keeps his speed $v_2$, and astronaut 1 has to gain all of $v_2 - v_1$.

With the numbers of part (a) and $m_2 = 76$ kg, $v_2 = 2.0$ m/s: $v_f = (64 \times 1.0 + 76 \times 2.0)/140 = 1.543$ m/s and $u = 76 \times 64 \times 1.0/(4 \times 140) = 8.69$ m/s. Now follow the catch on its own. The ball arrives at $1.543 - 8.686 = -7.143$ m/s, so astronaut 2 and the ball end with $(76 \times 2.0 - 4 \times 7.143)/80 = 1.543$ m/s, the same as astronaut 1.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A rocket with one stage or two, in empty space" points={16} mit="Problem 3">

A rocket floats in empty space, where no gravity acts. With its fuel it has the mass $m_{r,i} = 2.81 \times 10^7$ kg. Of this, $2.46 \times 10^7$ kg is fuel, so the dry mass of the rocket (the rocket empty of fuel) is $m_{r,d} = 0.35 \times 10^7$ kg. The burned fuel leaves at the speed $u = 3000$ m/s relative to the rocket. The fuel burns at a constant rate, and burning all of it takes 510 s.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

The rocket starts from rest and burns all of its fuel in one go. What is its final speed, in m/s?

<Hint>

The lesson on the rocket equation gives the speed gained in empty space in terms of the mass at the start and the mass at the end of the burn.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The mass at the end of the burn is the dry mass. You do not need the burn time. Why not?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$v_f =$" value="6249" unit="m/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-one-stage" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With no outside force, the speed a rocket gains depends only on the exhaust speed and on the ratio of its mass before and after the burn.

<Step title="The rocket in empty space">

The notes integrate the rocket equation with no external force:

<Formula label="Speed gained by a rocket in empty space" tex="v_{r,f} - v_{r,i} = u\ln\frac{m_{r,i}}{m_{r,f}}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (12.3.84), p. 12-21" />

Here $m_{r,i}$ and $m_{r,f}$ are the mass of the rocket (with the fuel still on board) at the start and at the end of the burn.

</Step>

<Step title="Numbers">

The rocket starts from rest and ends at its dry mass. The mass ratio is $2.81/0.35 = 8.03$, so

<Formula label="One stage: final speed" tex="v_f = 3000 \times \ln\frac{2.81}{0.35} = 6249\ \text{m/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A logarithm has no unit, so the result has the unit of $u$. The final speed is about twice the exhaust speed, which fits: $\ln 8.03 = 2.08$. The burn time of 510 s is not needed, because in empty space the gain depends only on how much mass is thrown out and how fast, not on how quickly. (In a gravitational field the burn time does matter, as the lesson shows.) MIT's course notes work this same rocket as Example 12.4 and give 6250 m/s, which is our value rounded to three figures.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Now the same rocket burns its fuel in two stages, each time with the exhaust speed $u = 3000$ m/s. Stage one burns $2.03 \times 10^7$ kg of fuel in 150 s. When it ends, the rocket and everything still on board have a mass of $0.78 \times 10^7$ kg. By how much does the speed change during stage one, in m/s?

<Hint>

Use the same formula as in part (a), with the mass at the start and at the end of stage one.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$\Delta v_1 =$" value="3845" unit="m/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-stage-one" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Stage one is a burn like any other: only its starting mass and its end mass count.

<Step title="Numbers">

The mass falls from $2.81 \times 10^7$ kg to $0.78 \times 10^7$ kg, a ratio of $2.81/0.78 = 3.603$:

<Formula label="Two stages: gain in stage one" tex="\Delta v_1 = 3000 \times \ln\frac{2.81}{0.78} = 3845\ \text{m/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The masses fit together: $2.81 - 2.03 = 0.78$, in units of $10^7$ kg. Stage one burns less fuel than the single burn of part (a), so it must gain less than 6249 m/s. It does.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Next the empty fuel tank of stage one and its fittings, with a mass of $1.4 \times 10^6$ kg together, are let go. That leaves a rocket with the dry mass $2.1 \times 10^6$ kg, carrying the remaining $4.3 \times 10^6$ kg of fuel. Stage two burns all of that fuel in 360 s. By how much does the speed change during stage two, in m/s?

Take the tank to be released gently, with no push, so that letting it go does not change the speed of the rocket. (MIT does not say this. The assumption is ours.)

<Hint>

What is the mass of the rocket, with its fuel, at the start of stage two? The dropped parts no longer count.

</Hint>

<Hint>

At the end of stage two only the new, smaller dry mass is left.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="$\Delta v_2 =$" value="3343" unit="m/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-stage-two" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** After the tank is dropped, a smaller rocket starts a new burn. The formula is the same, with the masses of the smaller rocket.

<Step title="The masses of stage two">

At the start of stage two the rocket has the mass $(2.1 + 4.3) \times 10^6 = 6.4 \times 10^6$ kg. This agrees with the mass after stage one less the dropped parts: $7.8 - 1.4 = 6.4$, in units of $10^6$ kg. At the end it has its dry mass, $2.1 \times 10^6$ kg.

</Step>

<Step title="Numbers">

The mass ratio is $6.4/2.1 = 3.048$:

<Formula label="Two stages: gain in stage two" tex="\Delta v_2 = 3000 \times \ln\frac{6.4}{2.1} = 3343\ \text{m/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The dropped parts and the new dry mass add up to the old dry mass: $1.4 + 2.1 = 3.5$, in units of $10^6$ kg. So nothing has been lost or counted twice.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

What is the final speed of the two-stage rocket, in m/s? It starts from rest, as in part (a).

<Hint>

The rocket starts stage two with the speed it had at the end of stage one.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-d" label="$v_f =$" value="7188" unit="m/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-two-stage" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each stage adds its gain to the speed the rocket already has, and the gentle release of the tank changes nothing.

<Step title="Add the gains">

<Formula label="Two stages: final speed" tex="v_f = \Delta v_1 + \Delta v_2 = 3845 + 3343 = 7188\ \text{m/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The sum of two logarithms is the logarithm of the product, so $v_f = 3000 \times \ln(3.603 \times 3.048) = 3000 \times \ln 10.98 = 7188$ m/s as well. MIT's course notes work this rocket as Example 12.5 and give 3840 m/s, 3340 m/s and 7190 m/s for the two gains and the final speed: our values rounded to three figures.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

Compare the final speeds of parts (a) and (d). Which statement is right, and gives the right reason?

<Hint>

Both rockets burn the same amount of fuel at the same exhaust speed. What is different about the mass that the fuel of stage two has to speed up?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Imagine two stages with nothing dropped in between. What would the two logarithms add up to?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-e" label="The two rockets:"
  choices={[
    "Both end with the same speed, because the same fuel leaves at the same speed relative to the rocket",
    "The two-stage rocket ends faster, because the fuel of stage two no longer has to speed up the empty tank of stage one",
    "The one-stage rocket ends faster, because it burns its fuel without a break",
    "The two-stage rocket ends faster, because a burn split in two always gains more, even with nothing dropped"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The gain of a burn depends on the ratio of the masses before and after it. Dropping dead mass between two burns makes the ratio of the second burn larger.

<Step title="Compare">

The two-stage rocket ends at 7188 m/s and the one-stage rocket at 6249 m/s: staging gains $7188 - 6249 = 939$ m/s, with the same fuel and the same exhaust speed.

</Step>

<Step title="Why">

In the single burn, every kilogram of fuel also has to speed up the whole dry rocket of $3.5 \times 10^6$ kg, including the tank of stage one long after it is empty. In the two-stage rocket the last $4.3 \times 10^6$ kg of fuel speeds up a dry mass of only $2.1 \times 10^6$ kg.

</Step>

<Step title="Check: two stages with nothing dropped">

Splitting the burn is not what helps. If the tank stayed on board, stage two would take the rocket from $7.8 \times 10^6$ kg to $3.5 \times 10^6$ kg, a gain of $3000 \times \ln(7.8/3.5) = 2404$ m/s. Together with stage one that is $3845 + 2404 = 6249$ m/s, exactly the one-stage result: the product of the two mass ratios is then the single ratio again. So the answer makes sense: the whole gain of 939 m/s comes from leaving the empty tank behind.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A falling raindrop that grows" points={10} mit="Problem 4">

A raindrop starts from rest with the mass $m_0$ and falls under gravity through a cloud. On its way it takes up water from the cloud, so its mass grows. Assume that the mass grows at a rate proportional to the momentum of the drop:

<Formula label="Raindrop: growth of the mass" tex="\frac{dm_r}{dt} = k\,m_r\,v_r" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 4" />

Here $m_r$ is the mass of the drop at the time $t$, $v_r$ is its speed at that time (downwards), and $k$ is a constant with the unit $\text{m}^{-1}$. Air resistance may be left out.

Take the water in the cloud to be at rest before the drop takes it up. (MIT does not say this. The assumption is ours, and the result depends on it.)

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the acceleration $dv_r/dt$ of the drop in terms of $k$, $g$ and the speed $v_r$ at that moment.

<Hint>

The mass of the drop changes, so "force = mass times acceleration" for the drop alone is not enough. Use the momentum principle for a system that keeps all of its mass: the drop together with the bit of water it takes up during a short time $\Delta t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The bit of water is at rest before it is taken up, so at first it adds nothing to the momentum. Afterwards it moves with the drop. The outside force is gravity.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You will find a term with $v_r\,dm_r/dt$. Replace $dm_r/dt$ by the growth law, and divide by $m_r$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$\dfrac{dv_r}{dt} =$" expr="g - k*v^2"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], k: [0.01, 0.1], v: [1, 8] }}
  names={{ v: "v_r" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "g", hint: "That is free fall with a fixed mass. The drop takes up water that was at rest, and bringing that water up to speed holds the drop back." },
    { expr: "g - k*v", hint: "Check the units: k has the unit 1/m, so k times a speed is 1/s, not an acceleration." },
    { expr: "g + k*v^2", hint: "Water that was at rest has to be brought up to speed by the drop. That can only reduce the acceleration, not add to it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow a fixed amount of matter over a short time: the drop and the water it is about to take up. Gravity changes the momentum of this system. Because the new water starts at rest, some of the momentum goes into bringing it up to speed.

<Step title="The momentum principle">

For a system that keeps its mass, the notes write the momentum principle as

<Formula label="Momentum principle for a system" tex="\vec F_\text{ext} = \frac{d\vec p_\text{sys}}{dt}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (12.2.1), p. 12-3" />

</Step>

<Step title="Momentum at two moments">

Take downwards as positive. At the time $t$ the drop has the mass $m_r$ and the speed $v_r$, and the bit of cloud water $\Delta m_r$ is at rest. At the time $t + \Delta t$ they move together at $v_r + \Delta v_r$:

<Formula label="Raindrop: momentum at the two moments" tex="p(t) = m_r\,v_r, \qquad p(t + \Delta t) = (m_r + \Delta m_r)(v_r + \Delta v_r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

The change is $m_r\,\Delta v_r + v_r\,\Delta m_r + \Delta m_r\,\Delta v_r$. Divide by $\Delta t$ and let $\Delta t$ go to zero: the last term, a product of two small quantities, drops out.

</Step>

<Step title="The equation of motion">

The outside force is the weight. The weight of the small bit $\Delta m_r$ vanishes in the limit, so the force is $m_r g$:

<Formula label="Raindrop: momentum principle" tex="m_r\,g = m_r\,\frac{dv_r}{dt} + v_r\,\frac{dm_r}{dt}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

Put in the growth law $dm_r/dt = k\,m_r\,v_r$ and divide by $m_r$:

<Formula label="Raindrop: acceleration" tex="\frac{dv_r}{dt} = g - k\,v_r^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $k$ in $\text{m}^{-1}$ and $v_r^2$ in $\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, the term $k v_r^2$ is an acceleration. For $k = 0$ the drop does not grow and falls freely with $g$. At the start, when $v_r = 0$, the acceleration is $g$ as well: a drop at rest takes up nothing. The mass of the drop has dropped out. The equation has the same form as the one for a body falling with a drag force proportional to $v^2$ in the lesson on [resistive forces](/courses/8-01/resistive-forces), although here nothing rubs: the "drag" is the cost of speeding up new water.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

What is the terminal speed $v_T$ of the drop, in terms of $k$ and $g$?

<Hint>

At the terminal speed the speed no longer changes. What does that say about $dv_r/dt$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$v_T =$" expr="sqrt(g/k)"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], k: [0.01, 0.1] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "g/k", hint: "Check the units: g/k is in m²/s². That is the square of the terminal speed." },
    { expr: "sqrt(k/g)", hint: "Check the units: k/g is in s²/m², so its root is one over a speed. The ratio is upside down." },
    { expr: "sqrt(g*k)", hint: "Check the units: g times k is in 1/s², so its root is one over a time, not a speed." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The drop speeds up as long as $g$ is larger than $k v_r^2$. The speed stops changing where the two are equal.

<Step title="Set the acceleration to zero">

<Formula label="Raindrop: terminal speed" tex="0 = g - k\,v_T^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_T = \sqrt{\frac{g}{k}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ratio $g/k$ is in $\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so its root is a speed. A larger $k$ means faster growth and a lower terminal speed. As $k$ goes to zero the terminal speed grows without limit, as it must: a drop of fixed mass with no air resistance never stops speeding up. An example with our own number: $k = 0.2\ \text{m}^{-1}$ gives $v_T = \sqrt{9.8/0.2} = 7.0$ m/s.

The drop approaches this speed and stays below it: below $v_T$ the acceleration is positive, and it shrinks to zero as $v_r$ comes close to $v_T$. The drop still gains mass and momentum at the terminal speed. All of the momentum that gravity supplies then goes into new water.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A vehicle that fills with rain" points={12} mit="Problem 5">

An open vehicle with no engine rolls along level ground without friction. Its mass when dry is $m_0$. Rain falls straight down. In every time interval $\Delta t$ an amount of rain $\Delta m_r = b\,\Delta t$ lands in the vehicle and stays there, moving along with it from then on. The rain starts at the time $t = 0$, when the vehicle moves with speed $v_0$ as seen from the ground. The vehicle is seen to slow down.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

At the time $t$ the vehicle moves with speed $v(t)$. Look at the interval from $t$ to $t + \Delta t$ and find how the speed changes. MIT asks for the change $\Delta v$ during $\Delta t$. Here, type the rate $dv/dt$, the limit of $\Delta v/\Delta t$, in terms of $v$, $t$, $m_0$ and $b$. (MIT's list of symbols for this part leaves out the speed, but the relation needs one. This remark is ours.)

<Hint>

Take as the system the vehicle with the rain already in it, together with the bit of rain that lands during $\Delta t$. Which outside forces act on it, and do any of them point along the ground?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Rain that falls straight down has no momentum along the ground. What is the mass of the vehicle and its water at the time $t$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the momentum along the ground at $t$ and at $t + \Delta t$ and set them equal. Drop the product of two small quantities.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$\dfrac{dv}{dt} =$" expr="-b*v/(m0 + b*t)"
  vars={{ b: [0.5, 2], v: [1, 10], m0: [50, 200], t: [10, 60] }}
  names={{ m0: "m_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-b*v/m0", hint: "That holds only at t = 0. By the time t the vehicle also carries the rain that has landed in it so far." },
    { expr: "b*v/(m0 + b*t)", hint: "The vehicle slows down, so the rate of change of its speed must be negative." },
    { expr: "-v/t", hint: "The dry mass of the vehicle is missing: the mass at the time t is the dry mass plus the rain collected so far." },
    { expr: "-b*v*t/(m0 + b*t)", hint: "Check the units: this is a speed, not a speed per time. The rain that lands during the short interval is b times that interval, not b times t." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** No outside force acts along the ground: gravity and the push of the ground are vertical, and there is no friction. So the momentum along the ground stays the same. The rain brings mass but no momentum in that direction, and the same momentum shared by more mass means less speed.

<Step title="The system and its mass">

At the time $t$ the vehicle holds the rain that fell since $t = 0$, so its mass is $m_0 + b\,t$. The system is this vehicle plus the bit of rain $\Delta m_r = b\,\Delta t$ that lands during the interval.

</Step>

<Step title="Momentum along the ground">

At the time $t$ the bit of rain moves straight down, so along the ground only the vehicle has momentum. At $t + \Delta t$ the rain rides along at the new speed $v + \Delta v$:

<Formula label="Rain: momentum along the ground at the two moments" tex="(m_0 + b\,t)\,v = (m_0 + b\,t + b\,\Delta t)(v + \Delta v)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The change of speed">

Multiply out and drop the term $b\,\Delta t\,\Delta v$, a product of two small quantities. What is left is $0 = (m_0 + b\,t)\,\Delta v + b\,v\,\Delta t$:

<Formula label="Rain: change of speed in a short interval" tex="\Delta v = -\frac{b\,v\,\Delta t}{m_0 + b\,t} \qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{dv}{dt} = -\frac{b\,v}{m_0 + b\,t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The unit of $b$ is kg/s, so $b\,v/(m_0 + b\,t)$ is a speed per second. The sign is negative: the vehicle slows down, as observed. With no rain ($b = 0$) the speed does not change. The slowing is strongest at the start and fades as the vehicle gets heavier and slower. The vehicle slows although no force along the ground acts on the system: inside the system, the vehicle has to bring each new bit of rain up to its own speed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Integrate your result of part (a) to find the speed $v(t)$ of the vehicle, in terms of $v_0$, $b$, $m_0$ and $t$.

<Hint>

Bring everything with $v$ to one side and everything with $t$ to the other: $dv/v$ on the left, a function of $t$ times $dt$ on the right.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate from $v_0$ to $v(t)$ on the left and from 0 to $t$ on the right. Both integrals give logarithms.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$v(t) =$" expr="m0*v0/(m0 + b*t)"
  vars={{ b: [0.5, 2], v0: [1, 10], m0: [50, 200], t: [10, 60] }}
  names={{ m0: "m_0", v0: "v_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v0*exp(-b*t/m0)", hint: "This follows if the mass stays m0 in part (a). The mass grows as m0 + b t, which makes the speed fall more slowly than an exponential." },
    { expr: "v0 - b*v0*t/m0", hint: "This follows if both the mass and the speed in part (a) keep their starting values. Check long times: your speed turns negative, but falling rain cannot turn the vehicle round." },
    { expr: "v0*(m0 + b*t)/m0", hint: "The vehicle slows down as it fills. Your speed grows with time: the ratio of the masses is upside down." },
    { expr: "v0*b*t/(m0 + b*t)", hint: "Check the start: at t = 0 the speed must be v0. Yours is zero." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Separate the variables and integrate. The result can be checked at once, because the momentum along the ground never changes.

<Step title="Separate and integrate">

From part (a), $dv/v = -b\,dt/(m_0 + b\,t)$. Integrate from the start to the time $t$:

<Formula label="Rain: integrating the change of speed" tex="\int_{v_0}^{v(t)}\frac{dv}{v} = -\int_0^t\frac{b\,dt'}{m_0 + b\,t'} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln\frac{v(t)}{v_0} = -\ln\frac{m_0 + b\,t}{m_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The speed">

Take the exponential of both sides:

<Formula label="Rain: speed of the vehicle" tex="v(t) = \frac{m_0\,v_0}{m_0 + b\,t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ the speed is $v_0$. The result says $(m_0 + b\,t)\,v(t) = m_0 v_0$: the momentum along the ground at the time $t$ equals the momentum at the start, which is the conservation law that part (a) began with. The speed falls towards zero but never reaches it. An example with our own numbers: $m_0 = 100$ kg, $b = 0.5$ kg/s and $v_0 = 3.0$ m/s. After 200 s the vehicle holds 100 kg of water, its mass has doubled to 200 kg, and its speed has halved to 1.5 m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
