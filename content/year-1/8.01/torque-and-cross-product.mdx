So far a force on a rigid body has told us how its centre of mass moves. This lesson asks how a force makes the body turn, and introduces the mathematics that describes it.

## Why a new quantity

Push a rod with a force $\vec F$. Its centre of mass accelerates by $\vec F = m\vec a_{\text{cm}}$, and that is true **wherever** on the rod the force acts (30.1 transcript, p. 1). Yet pushing at the middle or at one end clearly gives different motions. Chasles' theorem says the most general displacement of a rigid body is a translation of the centre of mass plus a rotation about it; the force decides the first, but the rotation depends also on **where** the force is applied (p. 1).

The rotational equation of motion, derived in the coming lessons, is (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Rotational equation of motion" tex="\vec\tau = I\,\vec\alpha" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

(The notes derive it for rotation about a fixed axis, in components along that axis, $\tau_{S,z} = I_S\,\alpha_z$, Eq. (17.3.14), p. 17-17; the next lesson does the same.) The **torque** $\vec\tau$ plays the role of force, the angular acceleration $\vec\alpha$ that of acceleration (kinematics), and the moment of inertia $I$ that of mass (p. 2). "Inertia" means resistance to a change of motion: a mass ten times larger needs ten times the force (p. 3). Rotational inertia, $I = \sum_j \Delta m_j r_j^2$, can be increased in two ways: more mass, or the same mass **farther from the axis** (p. 3). A wheel with its mass in the rim is harder to roll up a hill than a solid disc of the same mass (p. 4). Defining torque needs a new way of multiplying vectors (p. 2; notes, Section 17.1, pp. 17-1–17-2).

## The vector product

Two vectors $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ at an angle $\theta$ span a plane, which has two unit normals (30.2 transcript, p. 1). The **vector product** or **cross product** $\vec C = \vec A \times \vec B$ is defined by (p. 1; notes, Eq. (17.2.1), p. 17-2):

<Formula label="Magnitude of the vector product" tex="|\vec A \times \vec B| = |\vec A|\,|\vec B|\sin\theta, \qquad 0 \le \theta \le \pi" cite="reading3" at="pp. 1–2" />

with its direction perpendicular to the plane, given by the **right-hand rule**: curl the fingers of your right hand from $\vec A$ to $\vec B$, and the thumb points along $\vec A \times \vec B$ (p. 1; notes, Section 17.2.1, p. 17-3). Restricting $\theta$ to $[0, \pi]$ keeps $\sin\theta \ge 0$ (p. 2).

The magnitude has a picture: it is the **area of the parallelogram** spanned by $\vec A$ and $\vec B$. The base is $|\vec A|$ and the height is $B_\perp = |\vec B|\sin\theta$, the part of $\vec B$ perpendicular to $\vec A$; or equally the base is $|\vec B|$ and the height $A_\perp = |\vec A|\sin\theta$ (pp. 1–2; notes, Eqs. (17.2.2)–(17.2.3), p. 17-3). Parallel or antiparallel vectors give zero (notes, p. 17-3). Reversing the order reverses the direction, while numbers factor out and sums distribute as usual, $(c\vec A) \times \vec B = c(\vec A \times \vec B)$ and $\vec A \times (\vec B + \vec C) = \vec A \times \vec B + \vec A \times \vec C$ (notes, Eqs. (17.2.4)–(17.2.8), p. 17-4):

<Formula label="Anti-commutative" tex="\vec A \times \vec B = -\vec B \times \vec A" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (17.2.4), p. 17-4" />

## In Cartesian coordinates

Unit vectors at right angles span a square of area 1, so $\hat i \times \hat j$ has magnitude 1; define $\hat k = \hat i \times \hat j$, which by the right-hand rule points out of the page: a **right-handed** coordinate system (30.3 transcript, p. 1). The products follow the **cyclic** order $ijk$ (pp. 1–2; notes, Eqs. (17.2.9)–(17.2.13), pp. 17-4–17-5):

<Formula label="Unit vectors" tex="\hat i \times \hat j = \hat k, \quad \hat j \times \hat k = \hat i, \quad \hat k \times \hat i = \hat j, \qquad \hat j \times \hat i = -\hat k, \quad \hat i \times \hat i = 0" cite="reading4" at="pp. 1–2" />

An anti-cyclic order gives a minus sign, and any unit vector crossed with itself gives zero; knowing one product and the cyclic rule gives all nine (p. 2). Write $\vec A = A_x\hat i + A_y\hat j + A_z\hat k$ and $\vec B$ likewise, multiply out the nine terms, drop the three zeros and collect (pp. 2–4; notes, Eq. (17.2.20), p. 17-6):

<Formula label="Vector product in components" tex="\vec A \times \vec B = (A_yB_z - A_zB_y)\,\hat i + (A_zB_x - A_xB_z)\,\hat j + (A_xB_y - A_yB_x)\,\hat k" cite="reading4" at="pp. 3–4" />

(On p. 3 the transcript first writes $\hat j \times \hat k$ as $\hat k$, and corrects it to $\hat i$ on p. 4.) The same rules hold for the cylindrical unit vectors used for rotation, $\hat r \times \hat\theta = \hat k$ (notes, Eq. (17.2.21), p. 17-6).

## Torque

Let a force $\vec F$ act on a body at a point P, and let $\vec r$ be the vector from a point S (often a pivot) to P (30.4 transcript, p. 1). The torque about S is (p. 1; notes, Eqs. (17.2.28)–(17.2.29), pp. 17-9–17-10):

<Formula label="Torque about a point" tex="\vec\tau_S = \vec r_{S,P} \times \vec F, \qquad |\vec\tau_S| = rF\sin\theta" cite="reading5" at="p. 1" />

Its unit is the newton metre, N m (notes, p. 17-10). There are two ways to read the magnitude (30.4 transcript, p. 1; notes, Eq. (17.2.30), p. 17-10):

- $F \times (r\sin\theta)$: the force times the **moment arm** $r_\perp$, the perpendicular distance from S to the line of action of the force;
- $r \times (F\sin\theta)$: the distance times the component of the force **perpendicular** to $\vec r$.

Be careful with $\sin\theta$: the angle given in a problem is often not the angle between $\vec r$ and $\vec F$, and in the lecture's example the sine of the given angle gives the parallel component instead. Think "perpendicular part", not "sine of theta" (30.4 transcript, p. 1). The torque depends on the choice of S, is zero when the force points along the line through S, and is perpendicular to the plane of $\vec r$ and $\vec F$ (notes, p. 17-11). When all the forces and position vectors lie in one plane, the torque can be treated as a number with a sign instead: choose, say, counterclockwise as positive, and give a torque a plus sign when it turns that way and a minus sign otherwise (notes, Section 17.3.2, pp. 17-11–17-12).

## Torque from gravity

A body is a system of particles $m_j$ at positions $\vec r_{S,j}$ from S, each pulled down by $m_j\vec g$ (30.5 transcript, p. 1). Because $m_j$ is a number and $\vec g$ is the same for all, $\vec g$ comes out of the sum, and $\sum_j m_j\vec r_{S,j}$ is the total mass times the position of the centre of mass (pp. 1–2; notes, Eqs. (17.3.25)–(17.3.27), pp. 17-19–17-20):

<Formula label="Torque from gravity" tex="\vec\tau_S = \sum_{j=1}^{N} \vec r_{S,j} \times m_j\vec g = \Big(\sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j\vec r_{S,j}\Big) \times \vec g = \vec R_{S,\text{cm}} \times M\vec g" cite="reading6" at="pp. 1–2" />

So in a uniform gravitational field the **whole weight acts at the centre of mass** for torques too (p. 2). The notes call this point the centre of gravity; it coincides with the centre of mass when $\vec g$ is constant across the body (notes, pp. 17-1, 17-20). (On p. 2 the transcript says "the torque about the center of mass" where it means the torque about S.)

<WorkedExample title="A cross product, a wrench and a shelf">
<Problem>
(a) Compute $\vec A \times \vec B$ for $\vec A = 2\hat i + \hat j$ and $\vec B = \hat i + 3\hat j + 2\hat k$, and check it. (b) A 40 N force pulls on a wrench 0.25 m from the bolt, at 60° to the handle. Find the torque. (c) A uniform 3.0 kg shelf, 0.80 m long, sticks out horizontally from a wall. Find the torque of its weight about the wall.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Components">
With $A_z = 0$: $\hat i$ part $1 \times 2 - 0 \times 3 = 2$; $\hat j$ part $0 \times 1 - 2 \times 2 = -4$; $\hat k$ part $2 \times 3 - 1 \times 1 = 5$. So $\vec A \times \vec B = 2\hat i - 4\hat j + 5\hat k$. Check: its dot product with $\vec A$ is $4 - 4 + 0 = 0$ and with $\vec B$ is $2 - 12 + 10 = 0$, so it is perpendicular to both; the parallelogram's area is $\sqrt{4 + 16 + 25} = \sqrt{45} \approx 6.7$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The wrench">
$\tau = rF\sin 60° = 0.25 \times 40 \times 0.866 \approx 8.7$ N m. Equivalently, the moment arm is $0.25 \times 0.866 \approx 0.22$ m. Pulling straight along the handle would give no torque at all.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The shelf">
The weight acts at the centre of mass, 0.40 m from the wall, perpendicular to the shelf: $\tau = 0.40 \times 3.0 \times 9.8 \approx 11.8$ N m. The wall's supports must supply an equal and opposite torque.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Where galaxies get their spin">
In a uniform field, gravity's torque acts as if all the weight sat at the centre of mass; in a non-uniform field it need not. A forming dark matter halo is not a perfect sphere, and the lumps of matter around it pull on its different parts unequally. While the region is still expanding, before it turns round and collapses, these tidal forces exert torques on it and set it slowly spinning. Simulated halos end up with only a little rotation (a small spin parameter), but that angular momentum is what the gas later carries into spinning galactic discs.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A force moves the centre of mass the same way wherever it acts; how it turns the body depends on where it acts.
- $\vec\tau = I\vec\alpha$: torque, moment of inertia and angular acceleration are the rotational force, mass and acceleration.
- $|\vec A \times \vec B| = AB\sin\theta$, the area of the parallelogram; direction by the right-hand rule; $\vec A \times \vec B = -\vec B \times \vec A$.
- $\hat i \times \hat j = \hat k$ and cyclic; in components, $(A_yB_z - A_zB_y,\; A_zB_x - A_xB_z,\; A_xB_y - A_yB_x)$.
- $\vec\tau_S = \vec r \times \vec F$, magnitude force times moment arm; gravity's torque acts at the centre of mass.
