So far everything moved along a line. Real motion happens in space, but the step from one dimension to two (or three) is smaller than it looks. With [vectors](/courses/8-01/vectors), a plane is just two lines handled at the same time.

## Position, velocity and acceleration as vectors

Choose an origin and Cartesian axes, with unit vectors $\hat i$ and $\hat j$. The object's location is the **position vector**, and its velocity and acceleration are its time derivatives, taken one component at a time:

<Formula label="Kinematics in two dimensions" tex="\vec r(t) = x(t)\,\hat i + y(t)\,\hat j, \qquad \vec v(t) = \frac{dx}{dt}\,\hat i + \frac{dy}{dt}\,\hat j, \qquad \vec a(t) = \frac{dv_x}{dt}\,\hat i + \frac{dv_y}{dt}\,\hat j" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (5.1.1)–(5.1.3)" />

This works because the unit vectors of a Cartesian system never change direction, so only the components change with time. Each component behaves exactly like the one-dimensional motion of the previous pages: $v_x = dx/dt$, $a_x = dv_x/dt$, and the same for $y$.

The velocity vector always points along the path, tangent to it, in the direction of motion. The acceleration vector need not. It points in the direction the velocity is *changing*, which can be sideways or even backwards.

## Projectile motion

A **projectile** is anything thrown, kicked or launched that then moves only under gravity. Near Earth's surface, and ignoring air resistance, gravity gives a constant acceleration of size $g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ pointing straight down. With $+y$ up, the force is $\vec F_g = -mg\,\hat j$ and Newton's second law gives

<Formula label="Accelerations of a projectile" tex="a_x = 0, \qquad a_y = -g" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 5.2, Eqs. (5.1.14)–(5.1.15)" />

The mass drops out: a heavy ball and a light ball follow the same path. This is Galileo's law of free fall, now in two dimensions.

Launch at speed $v_0$ and angle $\theta_0$ above the horizontal. The initial velocity components are

<Formula label="Initial velocity components" tex="v_{x,0} = v_0\cos\theta_0, \qquad v_{y,0} = v_0\sin\theta_0" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (5.1.5)–(5.1.6)" />

Now apply the constant-acceleration results from [the previous lesson](/courses/8-01/acceleration-1d) to each direction separately:

<Formula label="Projectile position and velocity" tex="\vec r(t) = \big(x_0 + v_{x,0}\,t\big)\,\hat i + \big(y_0 + v_{y,0}\,t - \tfrac12 g t^2\big)\,\hat j, \qquad \vec v(t) = v_{x,0}\,\hat i + \big(v_{y,0} - g t\big)\,\hat j" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (5.1.16)–(5.1.17) with a_y = −g" />

<Callout kind="key">
Horizontal and vertical motion are **independent**. Horizontally, the projectile moves at constant velocity. Vertically, it moves exactly like a ball thrown straight up. The only thing they share is the clock.
</Callout>

## The path is a parabola

Solve $x(t) = x_0 + v_{x,0}t$ for $t$ and substitute it into $y(t)$. With the origin at the launch point ($x_0 = y_0 = 0$) the result is the **orbit equation**:

<Formula label="Trajectory (orbit equation)" tex="y(x) = \frac{v_{y,0}}{v_{x,0}}\,x - \frac{g}{2\,v_{x,0}^2}\,x^2" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 5.2.1, Eq. (5.1.33)" />

That is a downward-opening parabola. Its slope at any point, $dy/dx = v_y/v_x$, gives the *direction* of the velocity there, but not the speed. Change the launch speed, angle and height below.

<WorkedExample title="A ball rolled off a cliff">
<Problem>
A ball rolls off the edge of a 20 m high cliff with a horizontal speed of $8.0\ \text{m/s}$. Ignoring air resistance, how long is it in the air, how far from the foot of the cliff does it land, and how fast is it moving when it lands?
</Problem>
<Step title="Set up coordinates">
Origin at the foot of the cliff, $+x$ outward, $+y$ up. Then $x_0 = 0$, $y_0 = 20$ m, $v_{x,0} = 8.0\ \text{m/s}$, $v_{y,0} = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Use the vertical motion to find the time">
The ball lands when $y = 0$: $0 = 20 - \tfrac12(9.8)t^2$, so $t = \sqrt{40/9.8} = 2.02$ s. The horizontal speed played no part: a ball simply dropped from the cliff lands at the same moment.
</Step>
<Step title="Use the horizontal motion to find the distance">
$x = v_{x,0}\,t = 8.0 \times 2.02 = 16.2$ m from the foot of the cliff.
</Step>
<Step title="Find the landing velocity">
$v_x = 8.0\ \text{m/s}$ (unchanged) and $v_y = -9.8 \times 2.02 = -19.8\ \text{m/s}$. The speed is $\sqrt{8.0^2 + 19.8^2} = 21.4\ \text{m/s}$, at $\tan^{-1}(19.8/8.0) = 68^\circ$ below the horizontal.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Range on level ground">
<Problem>
A ball is kicked from the ground at $15\ \text{m/s}$, $35^\circ$ above the horizontal, and lands at the same height. Find its time of flight, maximum height and range.
</Problem>
<Step title="Components of the initial velocity">
$v_{x,0} = 15\cos 35^\circ = 12.3\ \text{m/s}$, $v_{y,0} = 15\sin 35^\circ = 8.60\ \text{m/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Time of flight">
It lands when $y = v_{y,0}t - \tfrac12 g t^2 = 0$, so $t = 2v_{y,0}/g = 2(8.60)/9.8 = 1.76$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Maximum height">
At the top $v_y = 0$, halfway through the flight at $t = 0.88$ s. Then $y_\text{max} = v_{y,0}^2/(2g) = 8.60^2/19.6 = 3.78$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Range">
$x = v_{x,0}\,t = 12.3 \times 1.76 = 21.6$ m. In general the range is $2v_{x,0}v_{y,0}/g = v_0^2\sin(2\theta_0)/g$, which is largest at $\theta_0 = 45^\circ$, as the plot above shows.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Aiming at a falling target

MIT's lesson ends with a classic demonstration: a target is released at the instant a ball is launched straight at it. The ball hits whatever its speed, as long as it reaches the target before either hits the ground. The equations above explain why. Both the ball and the target fall the same distance $\tfrac12 g t^2$ in the same time, so gravity shifts both down by exactly the same amount. Relative to the target, the ball still travels along the straight line it was aimed along.

## Try it

Fire the cannon at different angles and speeds, and turn air resistance on and off. Without air resistance the paths are the parabolas above; with it, the range is shorter and the path is no longer symmetric.

<Callout kind="warning" title="When the model breaks">
Real balls feel air drag, which grows with speed, so fast projectiles fall short of the parabola. The MIT notes give an example where drag shortens the range to about 72% of the drag-free value. Spin can also curve a ball. The parabola is the right answer only when gravity is the only force that matters.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="cosmology">
In cosmology, every galaxy has a position vector too, but it is written in **comoving coordinates**: a grid that stretches as the universe expands. Galaxies stay at nearly fixed grid positions, and the real (physical) distance is the grid distance times the scale factor $a(t)$. Light still crosses that grid, and its coordinate speed shrinks as the grid stretches:

<Formula label="Light in comoving coordinates" tex="\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{c}{a(t)}" cite="guth4" at="lecture transcript" />

This is the same idea as on this page: describe the motion one coordinate at a time, then put the pieces back together.
</Callout>

## Summary

- In a plane, $\vec r$, $\vec v$ and $\vec a$ are vectors; each Cartesian component obeys the one-dimensional rules.
- A projectile has $a_x = 0$ and $a_y = -g$: constant horizontal velocity, free fall vertically, sharing one clock.
- Eliminating time gives a parabolic path $y(x)$; its slope gives the direction of motion.
- On level ground the range is $v_0^2 \sin(2\theta_0)/g$, largest at $45^\circ$ (without air resistance).
