Velocity tells us how fast position changes. **Acceleration** tells us how fast velocity changes. It is the quantity that Newton's second law will connect to forces, and the last ingredient we need to describe motion along a line.

## Average and instantaneous acceleration

We follow exactly the same steps as for velocity. Over a time interval $\Delta t$ the velocity changes by $\Delta v = v(t + \Delta t) - v(t)$. The **average acceleration** is

<Formula label="Average acceleration" tex="a_\text{ave} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}, \qquad \vec a_\text{ave} = a_\text{ave}\,\hat i" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.3.24)" />

measured in metres per second squared, $\text{m/s}^2$. On a graph of $v$ against $t$, it is the slope of a secant line. Shrinking the interval to zero gives the slope of the tangent, the **instantaneous acceleration**:

<Formula label="Instantaneous acceleration" tex="a(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{v(t + \Delta t) - v(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d^2 x}{dt^2}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (4.3.25)–(4.3.27)" />

Since velocity is the derivative of position, acceleration is the **second derivative** of position. So if you know $x(t)$, you get $a(t)$ by differentiating twice. For example, $x(t) = 3 + 2t - 4t^3$ gives $v(t) = 2 - 12t^2$ and $a(t) = -24t$, all in SI units.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Negative acceleration does not mean slowing down">
The sign of $a$ only gives a direction along the axis. An object speeds up when $v$ and $a$ have the **same** sign and slows down when they have **opposite** signs. A car moving toward $-x$ and braking has a *positive* acceleration.
</Callout>

## Constant acceleration

The most important special case is constant acceleration $a$. A dropped stone near Earth's surface is close to it, with $a = -g \approx -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ if $+x$ points up. When $a$ is constant, the velocity changes by the same amount every second, so $v(t)$ is a straight line:

<Formula label="Velocity, constant acceleration" tex="v(t) = v_0 + a\,t" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 4.5, Eq. (4.4.2)" />

Here is a useful way to see it. The change in velocity, $a\,t$, is the **area under the $a(t)$ graph**, a rectangle of height $a$ and width $t$.

The same idea gives the position. The displacement is the **area under the $v(t)$ graph**. For constant acceleration that region is a rectangle ($v_0 t$) plus a triangle ($\tfrac12 \cdot t \cdot a t$), so

<Formula label="Position, constant acceleration" tex="x(t) = x_0 + v_0\,t + \tfrac12\,a\,t^2" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 4.5.2, Eq. (4.4.9)" />

Differentiating this gives back $v = v_0 + at$, and differentiating again gives $a$. The two descriptions agree.

<WorkedExample title="A ball thrown upward">
<Problem>
A ball is thrown straight up at $12\ \text{m/s}$ from a hand $1.5$ m above the ground. Ignore air resistance and take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. How high does it rise, and how fast is it moving when it hits the ground?
</Problem>
<Step title="Choose coordinates">
Origin at the ground, $+x$ upward, $t = 0$ at the throw. Then $x_0 = 1.5$ m, $v_0 = +12\ \text{m/s}$ and $a = -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Time to the top">
At the top, $v = 0$: $0 = 12 - 9.8\,t_\text{top}$, so $t_\text{top} = 1.22$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Maximum height">
$x(t_\text{top}) = 1.5 + 12(1.22) - \tfrac12 (9.8)(1.22)^2 = 1.5 + 7.35 = 8.85$ m above the ground.
</Step>
<Step title="When it lands">
Set $x(t) = 0$: $4.9\,t^2 - 12\,t - 1.5 = 0$. The quadratic formula gives $t = \dfrac{12 + \sqrt{144 + 29.4}}{9.8} = 2.57$ s. The other root is negative, before the throw, so we discard it.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed at impact">
$v = 12 - 9.8(2.57) = -13.2\ \text{m/s}$. The minus sign means it is moving downward, at $13.2\ \text{m/s}$, a bit faster than it was thrown, because it falls 1.5 m further than it rose from the hand.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## When the acceleration is not constant: integration

If $a$ changes with time, the formulas above no longer apply, but the area idea still works. Cut the time interval into thin slices. In each slice the velocity changes by about $a\,\Delta t$. Adding up the slices and letting them shrink to zero turns the sum into an **integral**:

<Formula label="Velocity and position by integration" tex="v(t) = v_0 + \int_0^t a(t')\,dt', \qquad x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t v(t')\,dt'" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 4.6, Eqs. (4.5.19)–(4.5.21)" />

Integration undoes differentiation. The one integral you need most often is the reverse of the power rule:

<Formula label="Power rule for integrals" tex="\int_0^t t'^{\,n}\,dt' = \frac{t^{\,n+1}}{n+1} \qquad (n \neq -1)" cite="mit" at="segment 2.5 List of Useful Integrals" />

<WorkedExample title="Non-constant acceleration">
<Problem>
An object moves with acceleration $a(t) = 6t - 2$ (in $\text{m/s}^2$, with $t$ in seconds). At $t = 0$ it is at $x_0 = 0$ with $v_0 = 1\ \text{m/s}$. Find its velocity and position at $t = 2$ s.
</Problem>
<Step title="Integrate the acceleration">
$v(t) = 1 + \int_0^t (6t' - 2)\,dt' = 1 + 3t^2 - 2t$ (m/s).
</Step>
<Step title="Integrate the velocity">
$x(t) = 0 + \int_0^t (1 - 2t' + 3t'^2)\,dt' = t - t^2 + t^3$ (m).
</Step>
<Step title="Evaluate at t = 2 s">
$v(2) = 1 + 12 - 4 = 9\ \text{m/s}$ and $x(2) = 2 - 4 + 8 = 6$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by differentiating">
$\dfrac{dx}{dt} = 1 - 2t + 3t^2 = v(t)$ ✓, and $\dfrac{dv}{dt} = 6t - 2 = a(t)$ ✓.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology">
Cosmologists describe the whole universe with one function, the scale factor, also written $a(t)$ (a different $a$ from the acceleration on this page). Its first derivative gives the expansion rate, and its **second derivative** says whether the expansion is speeding up or slowing down. Gravity from all the matter in the universe, ordinary and dark, should slow it down. In 1998 two teams measured distant Type Ia supernovae and found the opposite:

<Formula label="Accelerating expansion" tex="\frac{d^2 a}{dt^2} > 0 \quad \text{(today)}" cite="guth20" />

The expansion is accelerating. That discovery was the first direct evidence for **dark energy**, and it rests on exactly the idea from this page: acceleration is the rate of change of a rate of change.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity: $a = dv/dt = d^2x/dt^2$, the slope of the $v(t)$ graph.
- Speed increases when $v$ and $a$ have the same sign and decreases when they differ.
- With constant $a$: $v = v_0 + at$ and $x = x_0 + v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2$.
- In general, velocity is the area under $a(t)$ and displacement is the area under $v(t)$. Integration undoes differentiation.
