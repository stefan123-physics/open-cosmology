The last two lessons showed that only external forces change a system's total momentum. This lesson gives that momentum a home: a single point, the **centre of mass**, that moves as if the whole system were one particle.

## Centre of mass and centre of gravity

Balance a rod on one finger and it settles at one point, its **centre of gravity**, where gravity's pull on the rod can be thought to act. Far out in space, with no gravity, that phrase means nothing, yet the point still exists: it is the **centre of mass** (17.1 transcript, p. 1). (Near the Earth, where gravity is the same throughout a small object, the two points coincide.)

## The definition

Think of a body as many small pieces, piece $j$ having mass $m_j$. The centre of mass is the point for which the mass-weighted position vectors of all the pieces, measured **from that point**, add up to zero (17.1 transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Defining property of the centre of mass" tex="\sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j\,\vec r_{\text{cm},j} = \vec 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

That is hard to use when you do not yet know where the point is. So choose any origin $S$ instead. If $\vec r_{s,j}$ points from $S$ to piece $j$ and $\vec R_\text{cm}$ from $S$ to the centre of mass, the vector triangle gives $\vec r_{s,j} = \vec R_\text{cm} + \vec r_{\text{cm},j}$. Multiply by $m_j$ and add: $\vec R_\text{cm}$ is the same for every piece, so it comes out of the sum, and the sum over $m_j\vec r_{\text{cm},j}$ is zero by the definition (p. 2). What is left is

<Formula label="Centre of mass from any origin" tex="\vec R_\text{cm} = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j\,\vec r_{s,j}}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j} = \frac{1}{m_\text{sys}}\sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j\,\vec r_{s,j}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

The bottom is the total mass of the system, $m_\text{sys}$. The centre of mass is the **mass-weighted average** of the positions. The notes write the same thing first for two particles, $\vec R_\text{cm} = (m_1\vec r_1 + m_2\vec r_2)/(m_1 + m_2)$, then for $N$ (notes, Eqs. (10.5.1)–(10.5.3), pp. 10-6–10-7). The vector $\vec R_\text{cm}$ depends on the origin you choose; the point it reaches does not.

## Three planets

Segment 17.2 finds the centre of mass of three planets drawn on a grid: planet 1 of mass $m_1$ at $(35, 10)$, planet 2 of mass $3m_1$ at $(5, 20)$ and planet 3 of mass $0.5\,m_1$ at $(40, 30)$, so the total mass is $4.5\,m_1$ (17.2 transcript, pp. 1–2). Do the $x$ and $y$ components separately:

<Formula label="Three planets" tex="\vec R_\text{cm} = \frac{m_1}{4.5\,m_1}\big[(35 + 15 + 20)\,\hat i + (10 + 60 + 15)\,\hat j\big] = \frac{70\,\hat i + 85\,\hat j}{4.5} \approx 15.6\,\hat i + 18.9\,\hat j" cite="reading3" at="pp. 2–3" />

$m_1$ cancels: only the **ratios** of the masses matter. (The lecture rounds $70/4.5$ to 15.5; it is 15.6.) The lecture then swaps in real planets, Earth (one Earth mass), Saturn and Pluto 0.0025 Earth masses, and the centre of mass moves almost onto Saturn (p. 3). (The lecture gives Saturn 318 Earth masses, which is Jupiter's mass; Saturn has about 95. With 95 the centre of mass lands at about $(5.3, 19.9)$, right next to Saturn at $(5, 20)$.)

The notes do the same for the Earth and the Moon (notes, Example 10.2, pp. 10-7–10-8). With the origin at the Earth's centre, the Earth's own term is zero, and

<Formula label="Earth–Moon centre of mass" tex="\vec R_\text{cm} = \frac{m_m r_{em}}{m_e + m_m}\,\hat i = 4.66\times 10^{6}\ \text{m}\ \hat i" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (10.5.6)–(10.5.7), p. 10-7" />

That is less than the Earth's radius, $6.37\times 10^6$ m: the Earth and Moon turn about a point inside the Earth.

<WorkedExample title="Does Jupiter pull the Sun's centre outside the Sun?">
<Problem>
The Sun has mass $1.988\times 10^{30}$ kg and radius $6.957\times 10^8$ m; Jupiter has mass $1.898\times 10^{27}$ kg and orbits at an average $7.785\times 10^{11}$ m (NASA fact sheets). Where is the centre of mass of the two, ignoring the other planets?
</Problem>
<Step title="Set up">
Put the origin at the Sun's centre and $\hat i$ towards Jupiter. Then $X = m_J d/(M_\odot + m_J)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compute">
$X = (1.898\times 10^{27})(7.785\times 10^{11})/(1.9899\times 10^{30}) \approx 7.43\times 10^{8}$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
That is about $1.07$ times the Sun's radius: the point lies just **outside** the Sun. The Sun wobbles around it once every Jupiter year (about 12 years).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Continuous bodies

A rod or a plate is not a list of particles. Segment 17.3 turns the sum into an integral: each particle becomes a small mass element $\Delta m_j$, its position vector becomes the vector $\vec r$ to that element, and the sum over $j$ becomes an integral over the body as $\Delta m_j \to dm$ (17.3 transcript, p. 1). The total mass is $\int_{\text{body}} dm$, and

<Formula label="Centre of mass of a continuous body" tex="\vec R_\text{cm} = \frac{\int_{\text{body}}\vec r\,dm}{\int_{\text{body}} dm}" cite="reading4" at="pp. 1–2" />

The notes give the same formula (notes, Eq. (10.5.4), p. 10-7).

## Bodies made of parts

For a system of several **extended** objects, there is no need to integrate over all of it at once (17.4 text page). Replace each object by a point particle with the object's whole mass, placed at the object's own centre of mass, then find the centre of mass of those point particles. The MIT page tries it on two stacked boxes.

<WorkedExample title="Two stacked cubes">
<Problem>
A uniform 3.0 kg cube of side 0.40 m stands on the floor, and a uniform 1.0 kg cube of side 0.20 m sits centred on top of it. How high is the centre of mass of the pair?
</Problem>
<Step title="Replace each cube by a point">
Each cube's centre is its centre of mass: the big one at height 0.20 m, the small one at $0.40 + 0.10 = 0.50$ m. Both are on the same vertical line, so only the height needs working out.
</Step>
<Step title="Combine">
$Y = (3.0 \times 0.20 + 1.0 \times 0.50)/4.0 = 1.10/4.0 = 0.275$ m, inside the bottom cube, which holds three quarters of the mass.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A uniform rod, step by step

Segment 17.5 applies the integral to a uniform rod of mass $M$ and length $L$, and makes a recipe out of it (17.5 transcript, pp. 1–4):

1. **Choose a coordinate system.** The rod lies along $+x$ with the origin at one end, so the other end is at $x = L$. Any origin works; this one is convenient.
2. **Pick a general mass element $dm$**, somewhere in the middle, not at the origin or an end, and draw the vector $\vec r$ to it.
3. **Introduce an integration variable.** The lecture calls this the crucial step. With $x'$ the element's distance from the origin, $\vec r = x'\,\hat i$, and $x'$ runs from $0$ to $L$: those are the limits. The element has length $dx'$, so its mass is $dm = (M/L)\,dx'$, where $M/L$ is the **linear mass density**.

Every piece is now in terms of $x'$ (pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Uniform rod" tex="\vec R_\text{cm} = \frac{\int_0^L \frac{M}{L}\,x'\,dx'\ \hat i}{\int_0^L \frac{M}{L}\,dx'} = \frac{\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{M}{L}\,L^2}{M}\,\hat i = \frac{L}{2}\,\hat i" cite="reading6" at="pp. 3–4" />

Halfway along, as you would guess. The notes solve the same rod (notes, Example 10.3(a), Eqs. (10.5.9)–(10.5.10), pp. 10-8–10-9), and then one whose density grows along it, $\lambda(x) = \lambda_0 x^2/L^2$. There the mass $M = \int_0^L \lambda\,dx = \lambda_0 L/3$ fixes $\lambda_0 = 3M/L$, and the centre of mass moves to $\tfrac{3}{4} L$, towards the heavy end (notes, Eqs. (10.5.11)–(10.5.14), p. 10-9).

<WorkedExample title="A rod that thickens">
<Problem>
A 2.0 m pole has a linear mass density $\lambda(x) = 1.0 + 0.5\,x$ (kg/m, $x$ in metres from the thin end). Find its mass and centre of mass.
</Problem>
<Step title="Mass">
$M = \int_0^{2} (1.0 + 0.5\,x)\,dx = 2.0 + 0.25 \times 4 = 3.0$ kg.
</Step>
<Step title="Centre of mass">
$\int_0^{2} x\,(1.0 + 0.5\,x)\,dx = 2.0 + \tfrac{0.5}{3}\times 8 \approx 3.33$ kg·m, so $X = 3.33/3.0 \approx 1.11$ m: past the middle, towards the heavy end.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Velocity and acceleration of the centre of mass

Differentiate $\vec R_\text{cm}$ with respect to time (17.6 transcript, pp. 1–2). The masses are constant, and the derivative of each $\vec r_{s,j}$ is the velocity $\vec v_j$ of piece $j$. That velocity does not depend on the origin: if $P$ is another fixed point, $\vec r_{s,j} = \vec r_{s,p} + \vec r_{p,j}$, and $\vec r_{s,p}$ is constant, so its derivative is zero (p. 1). So

<Formula label="Velocity of the centre of mass" tex="\vec V_\text{cm} = \frac{1}{m_\text{sys}}\sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j\,\vec v_j" cite="reading7" at="pp. 1–2" />

<Formula label="Acceleration of the centre of mass" tex="\vec A_\text{cm} = \frac{1}{m_\text{sys}}\sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j\,\vec a_j" cite="reading7" at="p. 2" />

The top of the velocity formula is the total momentum. So the system's momentum is its total mass times the velocity of its centre of mass (notes, Eqs. (10.6.1)–(10.6.2), p. 10-10):

<Formula label="Momentum of a system" tex="\vec p_\text{sys} = m_\text{sys}\,\vec V_\text{cm}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (10.6.2), p. 10-10" />

## A system as one point particle

Now combine this with Lesson 15 (17.7 transcript, p. 1). The momentum of the system is $\vec p_\text{sys} = \sum_j m_j\vec v_j$, so its time derivative is $\sum_j m_j\vec a_j = m_\text{sys}\vec A_\text{cm}$. Newton's second and third laws together said that only the external force changes that momentum. Therefore

<Formula label="Motion of the centre of mass" tex="\vec F_\text{ext} = \frac{d\vec p_\text{sys}}{dt} = m_\text{sys}\,\vec A_\text{cm}" cite="reading8" at="p. 1" />

The notes reach the same result (notes, Eq. (10.6.3), p. 10-10). It is a big simplification (17.7 transcript, pp. 1–2): put all the mass at the centre of mass, put the total external force there too, and the centre of mass moves like a point particle of mass $m_\text{sys}$. The equation says nothing about how each piece moves or how the pieces push on each other; for the motion of the system **as a whole**, it does not need to.

The notes' baseball bat shows what that means (notes, Example 10.4, pp. 10-10–10-11). A bat lies on a nearly frictionless table. Push it with the same force at its centre of mass or at either end: the centre of mass accelerates **equally** in all three cases. What differs is the rest of the motion. Pushed at the centre of mass, the bat slides without turning; pushed at an end, it also rotates about its centre of mass.

<WorkedExample title="Skaters pushing apart">
<Problem>
A 60 kg skater and a 90 kg skater stand at rest 3.0 m apart on smooth ice, then push each other away. Where is their centre of mass, and how far has the 90 kg skater moved when the 60 kg skater has moved 2.4 m?
</Problem>
<Step title="Centre of mass">
With the origin at the 60 kg skater, $X = (60 \times 0 + 90 \times 3.0)/150 = 1.8$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="It stays put">
The pushes are internal, and gravity and the ice's normal force cancel, so $\vec A_\text{cm} = \vec 0$. The centre of mass was at rest, so it stays at 1.8 m.
</Step>
<Step title="The other skater">
For $X$ to stay the same, $60 \times 2.4 = 90 \times d$, so the 90 kg skater has moved $d = 1.6$ m the other way. The same holds for their speeds at every moment: if the lighter one moves at 1.5 m/s, the heavier one moves at $60 \times 1.5/90 = 1.0$ m/s, and $\vec V_\text{cm} = \vec 0$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

This is also why the exploding projectile of the previous lesson could also be solved quickly with the centre of mass: the explosion forces are internal, so the centre of mass of the pieces carries on along the original parabola until a piece lands.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Where is the mass of a galaxy cluster?">
Gravitational lensing measures where a cluster's mass is, so it can locate its centre of mass, and X-rays show where its hot gas is. In the Bullet Cluster (the lensing map was published in 2006), two clusters have passed through each other. The hot gas, which holds most of the ordinary matter, was slowed in the collision and lags behind, yet the mass found by lensing sits ahead of it, with the galaxies. Most of the mass is something that passed straight through: dark matter.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The centre of mass is the mass-weighted average position: $\vec R_\text{cm} = \frac{1}{m_\text{sys}}\sum_j m_j\vec r_j$. Measured from it, the mass-weighted positions add up to zero.
- For a continuous body, $\vec R_\text{cm} = \int\vec r\,dm \big/ \int dm$. Choose coordinates, pick a general $dm$, then write $\vec r$ and $dm$ in one integration variable (for a rod, $dm = \lambda\,dx'$). A uniform rod's centre of mass is at its middle.
- A system of extended objects: replace each by a point with its mass at its own centre of mass.
- $\vec V_\text{cm} = \frac{1}{m_\text{sys}}\sum_j m_j\vec v_j$, so $\vec p_\text{sys} = m_\text{sys}\vec V_\text{cm}$; likewise for $\vec A_\text{cm}$.
- $\vec F_\text{ext} = m_\text{sys}\vec A_\text{cm}$: the centre of mass moves like one particle carrying all the mass, pushed by the total external force, whatever the internal forces do.
