The [last lesson](/courses/8-01/energy-conservation) described a system by its energy in each state. This one turns the potential energy function $U(x)$ into a picture from which you can read off forces, equilibria and the possible motions, without solving Newton's equations.

## Force is minus the slope

In one dimension, the change in potential energy is minus the integral of the conservative force (25.1 transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Potential energy from force" tex="U(x_f) - U(x_i) = -\int_{x_i}^{x_f} F_x\,dx" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

By the [fundamental theorem of calculus](/courses/18-01/fundamental-theorem-1), the same difference is $\int_{x_i}^{x_f}\frac{dU}{dx}\,dx$. Comparing the two (p. 1; notes, Eqs. (14.1)–(14.3), pp. 14-15–14-16):

<Formula label="Force from potential energy" tex="F_x = -\frac{dU}{dx}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The force does **not** depend on the reference point: adding a constant to $U$ changes nothing, since the derivative of a constant is zero (p. 1). For the spring, $U = \tfrac12 kx^2$ gives $F_x = -kx$, Hooke's law back again (p. 2). Where the slope of $U$ is positive the force points to the left; where it is negative, to the right: for the spring, always back towards $x = 0$ (p. 2).

## Equilibrium: stable and unstable

Take $U(x) = ax^2 - bx^3$ with $a = 4$ J/m² and $b = 2$ J/m³ (25.2 transcript, pp. 1–4). Then

<Formula label="Force for the cubic potential" tex="F_x = -\frac{dU}{dx} = -2ax + 3bx^2" cite="reading3" at="p. 1" />

(The transcript says $2ax - 3bx^2$, with the signs reversed; the zeros, and so the conclusions, are unchanged, p. 1.) The force vanishes at $x = 0$ and at $x = \frac{2a}{3b} = \frac43$ m. For large positive $x$ the $-bx^3$ term wins and $U \to -\infty$; for large negative $x$, $U \to +\infty$. $U$ is zero at $x = 0$ and at $x = a/b = 2$ m (pp. 1–2). The value of $U$ at the equilibrium points depends on the reference; that the force vanishes there does not (p. 2).

What happens near each point where $F_x = 0$? Look at the slope on either side (pp. 3–4):

- At $x = \tfrac43$ m, a **hill top**: displaced slightly either way, the force points away from the point. This is an **unstable equilibrium**.
- At $x = 0$, the bottom of a **valley**: on either side the force points back towards it. This is a **stable equilibrium**; an object with too little energy to get over the hill stays near it.

The notes analyse a potential of exactly this shape, $U(x) = -U_1\left[\left(\frac{x}{x_1}\right)^3 - \left(\frac{x}{x_1}\right)^2\right]$, (the lesson\'s with $x_1 = a/b = 2$ m and $U_1 = a^3/b^2 = 16$ J) and classify the points with the second derivative: $U'' > 0$ at a minimum (stable), $U'' < 0$ at a maximum (unstable) (notes, Example 14.1, Eqs. (14.10)–(14.16), pp. 14-18–14-20).

## Reading the diagram: turning points

With no non-conservative work, the mechanical energy $E$ is constant: draw it as a **horizontal line** on the diagram (25.3 transcript, pp. 1–3; notes, Eqs. (14.8)–(14.9), pp. 14-17–14-18). At any $x$, the gap between the line and the curve is the kinetic energy:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy from the diagram" tex="K(x) = E - U(x)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (14.9), p. 14-18" />

Follow an object trapped in the valley (pp. 1–2): where $U = E$ it is momentarily at rest; moving towards the bottom, $K$ grows, largest at the stable equilibrium where the force is zero; then it climbs against the force, slows, and stops where the line meets the curve again. The two points $x_A$ and $x_B$ where $K = 0$ are the **turning points** (p. 2). Regions where $U > E$ are classically forbidden: $K$ would be negative (notes, p. 14-18, which add that in quantum mechanics there is a small chance of finding the object there).

With more energy, enough to clear the hill top, an object moving towards the hill goes over it and away to infinity, gaining speed on the far side. Picture a marble rolling on a landscape shaped like $U$ (pp. 2–3). (The model eventually fails: nothing can reach the speed of light, p. 3.) The notes find the speed needed at the bottom of the valley to just reach the hill top, $v_0 = \sqrt{8U_1/(27m)}$ for their potential (notes, Eqs. (14.19)–(14.21), pp. 14-20–14-21).

<WorkedExample title="A double well">
<Problem>
A particle moves along $x$ with $U(x) = x^4 - 2x^2$ (joules, $x$ in metres). Find the equilibrium points and classify them. If $E = -0.5$ J and the particle starts near $x = 1$ m, between which turning points does it move?
</Problem>
<Step title="Equilibria">
$dU/dx = 4x^3 - 4x = 4x(x^2 - 1) = 0$ at $x = 0$ and $x = \pm1$ m. $U'' = 12x^2 - 4$: $-4$ at 0 (a hill top, unstable, $U = 0$) and $+8$ at $\pm1$ (valleys, stable, $U = -1$ J).
</Step>
<Step title="Turning points">
$x^4 - 2x^2 = -0.5$ gives $x^2 = 1 \pm \sqrt{0.5}$, so $x \approx 0.541$ m and $x \approx 1.307$ m. With $E = -0.5$ J below the hill top at $U = 0$, the particle oscillates between these two points and can never cross to the other valley.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A false vacuum">
In inflationary cosmology, a field filling space has a potential energy that depends on its value, with its own landscape of valleys and hills. A **false vacuum** is a valley that is not the lowest one: stable against small nudges, like the valley in the diagram above, but higher than the true minimum. In Guth's original model the universe inflated while trapped there, and could only leave by tunnelling through the hill, a quantum effect that classical energy diagrams forbid.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $F_x = -dU/dx$: the force is minus the slope of $U(x)$, whatever the reference point.
- Equilibrium where $dU/dx = 0$: stable at a minimum (a valley, $U'' > 0$), unstable at a maximum (a hill top, $U'' < 0$).
- Draw the energy $E$ as a horizontal line: $K = E - U$; turning points where $E = U$; regions with $U > E$ are forbidden.
- Enough energy to clear a hill lets the object escape; too little keeps it trapped in its valley.
