Gravity acts at a distance. Most of the other forces in everyday mechanics act only where things **touch**: a table holding up a cup, a floor pushing back on your feet, a road gripping a tyre. MIT's Lesson 6 introduces these **contact forces**, and splits them into two parts that behave very differently: the **normal force** and **friction**.

## The contact force and its two parts

Rest your hand on a table and press. The atoms of your hand and of the table come closer, their electrons repel, and the table pushes back. Newton's third law says the table pushes on your hand exactly as hard as your hand pushes on the table. Push harder and the force grows. Slide your hand forwards and you also feel a drag along the surface, against the motion.

The force of the surface on your hand is the **contact force** $\vec C$. Like any vector, it can be split into components: one **perpendicular** (normal) to the surface, the **normal force** $\vec N$, and one **along** (tangential to) the surface, the **friction force** $\vec f$:

<Formula label="Contact force" tex="\vec C = \vec C_\perp + \vec C_\parallel \equiv \vec N + \vec f" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.3.1), p. 8-11" />

These are not two separate forces, but two components of one force. And the contact force is spread over the whole area of contact; for most problems it can be treated as acting at a single point, but sometimes (for an object that tips over) its spread matters.

Contact forces are **constraint forces**: no force law gives them in advance. Their size and direction are whatever is needed for the object to obey its constraint (to stay on the table, or not to slide), and they change with how hard and in which direction you push.

## The normal force is not "weight"

Hold a block at rest in your palm. Two forces act on the block: gravity, $\vec F^{\,g} = m\vec g$, and the contact force of your hand, which here has only a normal part. The block does not accelerate, so Newton's second law gives

<Formula label="Block at rest in your hand" tex="\vec N + \vec F^{\,g} = \vec 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N - mg = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N = mg" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (8.3.2)–(8.3.4), p. 8-12" />

taking upwards as positive. The word "weight" is used in two ways. It often means the gravitational force on an object; but what a bathroom scale reads, and how heavy the block feels in your hand, is the **normal force**. To avoid this confusion MIT's notes do not use "weight", and say "gravitational force" for the pull of the Earth. When you jump, you feel weightless because nothing pushes up on you; gravity has not switched off.

Two warnings follow:

- $N = mg$ holds only in special cases: a horizontal surface, no other vertical forces, and no **vertical** acceleration. (Sliding sideways at any speed, as in the crate example below, does not change it.) Push down on the block, tilt the surface, or accelerate up or down, as in a lift, and the normal force changes.
- The normal force and gravity on the block are **not** a third-law pair: they act on the same object. The third-law partner of the hand's push on the block is the block's push on the hand; the partner of the Earth's pull on the block is the block's pull on the Earth.

<WorkedExample title="Standing in a lift">
<Problem>
A person of mass 60 kg stands on a bathroom scale in a lift that is accelerating upwards at 1.5 m/s². What does the scale read? Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².
</Problem>
<Step title="Forces on the person">
Upwards: the normal force $N$ of the scale. Downwards: gravity, $mg$. Taking upwards as positive, Newton's second law gives $N - mg = ma$.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve">
$N = m(g + a) = 60 \times (9.8 + 1.5) = 678$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Interpret">
The scale reads 678 N, about 69 kg on a scale calibrated in kilograms, although gravity on the person is still $60 \times 9.8 = 588$ N. The scale measures the normal force, not gravity.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Kinetic friction

When two surfaces slide over each other, the friction is **kinetic** (or sliding) friction. Leonardo da Vinci recorded measurements of it between about 1493 and 1515; Guillaume Amontons rediscovered his results and published them in 1699; Charles Augustin Coulomb added a third property. Together they make an experimental law:

<Formula label="Kinetic friction" tex="f_k = \mu_k N" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.3.5), p. 8-14" />

The magnitude is proportional to the normal force, with a constant $\mu_k$, the **coefficient of kinetic friction**, that depends on the two surfaces. Its direction is always opposite to the sliding of one surface relative to the other. And two properties are surprising:

1. It is **independent of the contact area**. Two blocks of the same mass on the same surface, one resting on a large face and one on a small face, need the same push to slide at constant speed.
2. It is **independent of the speed**, at least for ordinary sliding speeds.

As MIT's lecturer notes, there is still no complete theory explaining these properties from the physics of the surfaces, although contemporary research has made much progress in understanding it.

<WorkedExample title="Pushing a crate">
<Problem>
You push a 25 kg crate horizontally across a floor at constant speed. The coefficient of kinetic friction is 0.30. How hard do you push? Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².
</Problem>
<Step title="Normal force">
Vertically nothing accelerates and your push is horizontal, so $N = mg = 25 \times 9.8 = 245$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Constant speed means balance">
With no acceleration, your push equals kinetic friction: $F = \mu_k N = 0.30 \times 245 \approx 74$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Pushing at 74 N on a crate that is turned on its side changes nothing: friction does not depend on the area of contact.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Static friction

If you push gently on a heavy box and it does not move, friction is still acting: **static friction**, between surfaces that do not slide. It behaves very differently from kinetic friction. With no push, there is no static friction at all. As you push harder, static friction grows to match you, keeping the box still. But it cannot grow without limit: at some push the box suddenly starts to slide. Static friction can take any value from zero up to a maximum:

<Formula label="Range of static friction" tex="0 \le f_s \le (f_s)_\text{max}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.3.6), p. 8-15" />

and that maximum, like kinetic friction, is proportional to the normal force:

<Formula label="Maximum static friction" tex="(f_s)_\text{max} = \mu_s N" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.3.7), p. 8-15" />

with $\mu_s$ the **coefficient of static friction**. The moment the applied force along the surface exceeds $(f_s)_\text{max}$ is the **just-slipping condition**. Usually $\mu_s$ is slightly larger than $\mu_k$, so when an object starts to slide the friction drops a little, the cause of the squeak of chalk on a board, fingernails on glass, or a violin bow on a string.

Static friction points against the applied force (as long as nothing accelerates): push left and it points right; push right and it points left. In more complicated systems, as later lessons show, its direction is not obvious in advance: it depends on all the other constraints.

<WorkedExample title="When does a block start to slide down a slope?">
<Problem>
A block rests on a board that you slowly tilt. The coefficients of friction between them are $\mu_s = 0.45$ and $\mu_k = 0.35$. (a) At what angle does the block start to slide? (b) What is its acceleration once it slides? Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².
</Problem>
<Step title="Forces along and across the slope">
At angle $\theta$, gravity has a component $mg\sin\theta$ down the slope and $mg\cos\theta$ into it. Across the slope nothing accelerates, so $N = mg\cos\theta$. Along the slope, static friction holds the block as long as $mg\sin\theta \le \mu_s N = \mu_s mg\cos\theta$.
</Step>
<Step title="Just slipping">
The block slips when $\tan\theta = \mu_s$: $\theta = \arctan 0.45 \approx 24.2°$. The mass does not matter, which makes this a quick way to measure $\mu_s$.
</Step>
<Step title="Sliding">
Once sliding, friction is kinetic: $ma = mg\sin\theta - \mu_k mg\cos\theta$, so $a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta) = 9.8 \times (0.410 - 0.35 \times 0.912) \approx 0.89$ m/s². Because $\mu_k < \mu_s$, the block that was just held at rest now accelerates.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A surface in contact exerts a contact force $\vec C = \vec N + \vec f$: the normal force perpendicular to the surface and friction along it. Both are constraint forces, set by what the motion requires rather than by a fixed law.
- What a scale reads, and what we feel as our "weight", is the normal force; it equals $mg$ only in special cases (a horizontal surface, no other vertical forces and no vertical acceleration). Normal force and gravity on one object are not a third-law pair.
- Kinetic friction: $f_k = \mu_k N$, against the sliding, independent of contact area and (for ordinary speeds) of speed.
- Static friction: anything from 0 up to $\mu_s N$, against the applied force; beyond that the object slips. Usually $\mu_s > \mu_k$, so friction drops when sliding starts.
