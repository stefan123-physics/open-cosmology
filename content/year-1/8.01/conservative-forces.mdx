The work integral $\int_A^B \vec F\cdot d\vec s$ of the [last lesson](/courses/8-01/work-in-two-and-three-dimensions) is in general a line integral: to evaluate it, you need the path. This lesson shows that for some forces the path does not matter, and that this property sorts forces into two kinds.

## Gravity: the path does not matter

Near the Earth, gravity is constant, $\vec F = -mg\,\hat j$. Go from an initial point to a final point by two paths made of vertical and horizontal legs: up then across, or across then up (22.1 transcript, pp. 1–2; notes, Eqs. (14.2)–(14.7), pp. 14-3–14-4). On a horizontal leg the force is perpendicular to the motion, so the work is zero; on a vertical leg it is $-mg\,\Delta y$. Both paths give the same $-mg(y_f - y_i)$.

Any path at all can be cut into tiny horizontal and vertical steps, and only the vertical ones count (p. 2). Analytically, $d\vec s = dx\,\hat i + dy\,\hat j$, and since $\hat j\cdot\hat i = 0$:

<Formula label="Work done by gravity on any path" tex="W = \int_i^f (-mg\,\hat j)\cdot(dx\,\hat i + dy\,\hat j) = -mg\int_{y_i}^{y_f} dy = -mg\,(y_f - y_i)" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3" />

The result depends only on the heights of the end points: gravity's work is **path independent** (p. 3).

## Friction: the path matters

Slide an object along a rough floor from $x_i$ to $x_f$ (22.2 transcript, pp. 1–3; notes, Eqs. (14.8)–(14.9), pp. 14-4–14-5). Path 1 goes straight there. Kinetic friction, $-\mu_k mg\,\hat i$, opposes the motion, and $d\vec s = dx\,\hat i$ (the sign of the motion shows up in the limits, not in $dx$):

<Formula label="Friction on the direct path" tex="W_1 = \int_{x_i}^{x_f} (-\mu_k mg\,\hat i)\cdot dx\,\hat i = -\mu_k mg\,(x_f - x_i)" cite="reading3" at="pp. 1–2" />

(The transcript drops the minus sign at this point, p. 2.) Path 2 goes past $x_f$ to a point $x_a$ and comes back. On the way back friction points the other way, $+\mu_k mg\,\hat i$, while $x$ decreases (pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Friction on the longer path" tex="W_2 = -\mu_k mg\,(x_a - x_i) + \mu_k mg\,(x_f - x_a) = -\mu_k mg\,(2x_a - x_i - x_f)" cite="reading3" at="pp. 2–3" />

Both legs give negative work, because $x_f - x_a < 0$. (The transcript first writes the second leg as "$x$ final minus $x$ initial" and then corrects it to $x_f - x_a$, p. 3.) The answer is larger in size than on path 1: friction's work is minus $\mu_k N$ times the **total distance** travelled, so it depends on the path (notes, p. 14-5).

## Conservative forces

A force whose work between any two points is the same on every path is called **conservative** (22.3 transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 14-5). Gravity near the Earth, the inverse-square gravitational and electric forces, and spring forces are all conservative (p. 1). Go from $i$ to $f$ on path 1 and come back on path 2: reversing the limits of path 2 flips its sign, and path independence makes the two cancel (pp. 1–2; notes, Eqs. (14.10)–(14.16), p. 14-6):

<Formula label="Conservative force: closed path" tex="\oint \vec F_c\cdot d\vec s = \int_{i,\,\text{path }1}^{f}\vec F_c\cdot d\vec s + \int_{f,\,\text{path }2}^{i}\vec F_c\cdot d\vec s = 0" cite="reading4" at="pp. 1–2" />

The circle on the integral sign marks a **closed path**. Work done by a conservative force around any closed path is zero.

## Non-conservative forces

A force whose work depends on the path is **non-conservative**; friction is the standard example (22.4 transcript, p. 1). Around a closed path its work is in general **not** zero; for some particular closed path it might happen to vanish, but not in general (p. 1).

<WorkedExample title="Two kinds of force">
<Problem>
(a) A 3.0 kg box is lowered 2.0 m, once straight down and once along a long winding ramp. How much work does gravity do? (b) A 20 kg crate ($\mu_k = 0.30$) ends 3.0 m from where it started, either pushed straight there or pushed 5.0 m forward and 2.0 m back. How much work does friction do in each case?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Gravity">
$W = mg\,(y_i - y_f) = 3.0 \times 9.8 \times 2.0 = 58.8$ J, on both paths.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Friction">
Straight: $-0.30 \times 20 \times 9.8 \times 3.0 \approx -176$ J. Forward and back (7.0 m travelled): $\approx -412$ J. Same end points, different work.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Friction is not the only way to be non-conservative. A force can fail the test even without any surface rubbing.

<WorkedExample title="A force that circulates">
<Problem>
A force field is $\vec F = -y\,\hat i + x\,\hat j$ (in newtons when $x$ and $y$ are in metres; strictly the coefficient is 1 N/m). Find its work going once anticlockwise round the circle of radius 1 m about the origin. Is it conservative?
</Problem>
<Step title="Around the circle">
On the circle, $\vec F$ is tangent to the path with magnitude 1 N, pointing along the motion, so the work is $1\ \text{N} \times 2\pi\ \text{m} = 2\pi \approx 6.28$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Verdict">
The work around a closed path is not zero, so the force is not conservative. Another check: from $(1, 0)$ to $(0, 1)$ along the quarter circle the work is $\pi/2$ J, but along the axes through the origin it is 0, because on the $x$-axis $\vec F$ points along $\hat j$ and on the $y$-axis along $-\hat i$, perpendicular to the motion each time.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## When is work useful?

The week's summary (22.5 transcript, pp. 1–3) puts the pieces together. The work–kinetic energy theorem, $\int_A^B \vec F\cdot d\vec s = \tfrac12 m v_B^2 - \tfrac12 m v_A^2$, is Newton's second law in another form. But the integral generally needs the path, which you may not know until you have solved the motion. Two special cases make it useful anyway (p. 2):

1. **Constrained motion:** the object is made to follow a known track, such as a circle or a wire, so the path is known in advance.
2. **Conservative forces:** the work depends only on the end points, whatever the path.

When both kinds of force act, split the work (pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Splitting the work" tex="W = W_c + W_{nc} = \int_A^B \vec F_c\cdot d\vec s + \int_A^B \vec F_{nc}\cdot d\vec s" cite="reading6" at="pp. 2–3" />

The first integral depends only on $A$ and $B$; the second depends on the path taken. (The transcript's "F sub mc" means $\vec F_{nc}$, p. 3.)

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Why the expanding universe has an energy">
Newton's inverse-square gravity is conservative. So the work gravity does on a galaxy moving away from a mass $M$ depends only on its starting and final distances, not on the route. That is what lets the work be written as a change in a function of position, a **potential energy**, the next lesson's subject, and what makes the energy $E$ of a galaxy on the edge of an expanding sphere a conserved quantity in 8.286 (Lecture 6). The sign of $E$ then decides whether the expansion goes on forever (Lecture 7).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Gravity near the Earth does work $-mg\,(y_f - y_i)$ on any path: it is conservative.
- Kinetic friction does work $-\mu_k N$ times the distance travelled, which depends on the path: it is non-conservative.
- A conservative force does zero work around any closed path, $\oint\vec F_c\cdot d\vec s = 0$; a non-conservative one in general does not.
- The work integral is useful when the path is known (constrained motion) or the force is conservative; in general $W = W_c + W_{nc}$.
