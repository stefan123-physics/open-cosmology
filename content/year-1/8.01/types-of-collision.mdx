[Conservation of momentum](/courses/8-01/conservation-of-momentum) already handled collisions in which objects stick together. This week looks at collisions in general: momentum may still be constant, but the kinetic energy may change, and how it changes sorts collisions into types (notes, Section 15.1, p. 15-1).

## Momentum in a collision

Object 1, moving with $\vec v_{1,i}$, collides with object 2, moving with $\vec v_{2,i}$, along a line; afterwards they move with $\vec v_{1,f}$ and $\vec v_{2,f}$ (26.1 transcript, pp. 1–2). The impulse of any external force during the collision changes the momentum. On a frictionless surface, ignoring air resistance, there is no external force, so the momentum of the system is constant (p. 1). In components along $\hat i$:

<Formula label="Momentum in a one-dimensional collision" tex="m_1 v_{1x,i} + m_2 v_{2x,i} = m_1 v_{1x,f} + m_2 v_{2x,f}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

Writing components rather than magnitudes means you do not have to know beforehand which way each object moves afterwards: the signs come out of the algebra (p. 2).

## What happens to kinetic energy

Without external forces, no external work is done; but internal forces can still change the kinetic energy of the system (26.2 transcript, p. 1). That defines the types of collision (p. 1; notes, Eqs. (15.3.2)–(15.3.5), p. 15-7):

- **Elastic:** $\Delta K_\text{sys} = 0$. Kinetic energy is unchanged.
- **Inelastic:** $\Delta K_\text{sys} < 0$. Some kinetic energy goes into deforming the objects, heat or sound; total energy is still conserved.
- **Superelastic:** $\Delta K_\text{sys} > 0$. Some other energy is released, as if the colliding objects carried chemicals that exploded.
- **Totally inelastic:** the objects stick together. For given masses and initial velocities, with momentum conserved, this loses the most kinetic energy that can be lost.

The notes measure this with the **coefficient of restitution**, the ratio of the relative speeds after and before: $e = 1$ for elastic, $e < 1$ inelastic, $e = 0$ totally inelastic, $e > 1$ superelastic (notes, Eq. (15.3.1), p. 15-7).

Which type a collision is must be assumed as part of the model (pp. 1–2). For an elastic collision, energy gives a second equation alongside momentum; since a component squared is the speed squared (p. 2):

<Formula label="Elastic collision: kinetic energy" tex="m_1 v_{1x,i}^2 + m_2 v_{2x,i}^2 = m_1 v_{1x,f}^2 + m_2 v_{2x,f}^2" cite="reading3" at="p. 2" />

Given the masses and initial velocities, the two equations determine the two final velocities, although the energy equation makes it a quadratic. The next lesson shows a quicker way (pp. 2–3). (The transcript keeps a stray ½ in one term after cancelling the halves, p. 2.)

## Totally inelastic collisions

Now let the objects stick, with object 2 initially at rest (26.3 transcript, pp. 1–3). The internal forces cancel in pairs, there is no external force, and a single final velocity describes both:

<Formula label="Totally inelastic: common velocity" tex="m_1 v_{1x,i} = (m_1 + m_2)\,v_{x,f} \;\Rightarrow\; v_{x,f} = \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{1x,i}" cite="reading4" at="pp. 1–2" />

Kinetic energy cannot be constant here: the internal forces deform the objects irreversibly (p. 1). Compute the change (p. 2):

<Formula label="Kinetic energy lost" tex="\Delta K = \tfrac12(m_1 + m_2)\,v_{x,f}^2 - \tfrac12 m_1 v_{1x,i}^2 = \tfrac12 m_1 v_{1x,i}^2\left(\frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2} - 1\right) = -\frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,K_i" cite="reading4" at="pp. 2–3" />

The **fraction** of kinetic energy lost depends only on the mass ratio (pp. 2–3; notes, Example 15.2, Eqs. (15.5.3)–(15.5.6), pp. 15-13–15-14). It went into other forms of energy (p. 3).

<WorkedExample title="A crash and a ballistic pendulum">
<Problem>
(a) A 1500 kg car at 20 m/s hits a parked 1000 kg car, and they lock together. Find their speed just after and the kinetic energy lost. (b) A 10 g bullet at 400 m/s embeds itself in a 2.0 kg block hanging from strings. How fast does the block start to move, what fraction of the bullet's kinetic energy is lost, and how high does the block swing?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The crash">
$v_f = 1500 \times 20/2500 = 12$ m/s. $K_i = \tfrac12 \times 1500 \times 20^2 = 300$ kJ and $K_f = \tfrac12 \times 2500 \times 12^2 = 180$ kJ: 120 kJ, or 40%, is lost, as $m_2/(m_1 + m_2) = 0.4$ says.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The bullet">
The impact is so quick that the strings stay vertical and give no horizontal impulse, so horizontal momentum is conserved: $v_f = 0.010 \times 400/2.01 \approx 1.99$ m/s. The fraction lost is $2.0/2.01 \approx 0.995$, or 99.5%. After the collision mechanical energy is conserved, so the block rises $h = v_f^2/2g \approx 0.20$ m.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Colliding clusters">
When two clusters of galaxies collide, their hot gas clouds collide inelastically: kinetic energy turns into heat, a shock front forms, and the gas is slowed and lags behind. The galaxies and the dark matter behave almost as if there were no collision at all: they pass through. In the Bullet Cluster (whose lensing map was published in 2006) the two kinds of matter have separated in exactly this way, which is why the lensing mass and the X-ray gas are seen in different places.
</Callout>

## Summary

- With no external force, momentum is constant in a collision: write it in components.
- Elastic: $\Delta K = 0$; inelastic: $\Delta K < 0$; superelastic: $\Delta K > 0$; totally inelastic: the objects stick together.
- An elastic collision gives a second equation, $m_1 v_{1x,i}^2 + m_2 v_{2x,i}^2 = m_1 v_{1x,f}^2 + m_2 v_{2x,f}^2$.
- Totally inelastic with object 2 at rest: $v_{x,f} = m_1 v_{1x,i}/(m_1 + m_2)$, and the fraction of kinetic energy lost is $m_2/(m_1 + m_2)$.
