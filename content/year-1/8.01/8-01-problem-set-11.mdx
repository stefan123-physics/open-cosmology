This set practises the lessons on the [angular momentum of a point particle](/courses/8-01/angular-momentum-point-particle), on the [angular momentum of a rigid body](/courses/8-01/angular-momentum-rigid-body) and on [torque, angular impulse and the conservation of angular momentum](/courses/8-01/torque-and-angular-impulse). The five problems follow MIT's Problem Set 11, in our own words. MIT's set comes with drawings; here each situation is described in words, so make your own sketch first. MIT publishes no solutions for this set, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Some problems need earlier lessons too. Problems 1 to 4 use the [moment of inertia](/courses/8-01/moment-of-inertia) and its parallel axis theorem. Problems 3 and 4 are elastic collisions, as in the lesson on [elastic collisions](/courses/8-01/elastic-collisions). Problem 5 uses the potential energy of gravitation from the lesson on [potential energy](/courses/8-01/potential-energy). It is about an elliptical orbit, for which this site has no lesson: the one fact about such an orbit that you need is explained in the solution of its part (b).

Two remarks. The lesson on torque and angular impulse works a relative of Problem 4: the same pivoted ring, hit along the same line by an object that sticks to it. And MIT's course notes work relatives of Problems 3 and 5: a rod on a pivot that is hit by an object (Example 19.7) and a meteor that flies past the Earth (Example 19.4). None of these is the same problem, but try the problems here before you look there. All five problems count for the score (60 points).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first: sketch the situation seen from above, choose the system, and choose the point about which you take the angular momentum. Ask which outside forces act and whether they have a torque about that point. Then write the angular momentum before and after, type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A formula box takes plain typing, for example `3*v/(8*d)` or `sqrt(G*mp/R)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type the names as the box shows them: `m1` for $m_1$, `m2` for $m_2$, `vi` for $v_i$, `v0` for $v_0$, `mR` for $m_R$ and `mp` for $m_p$. A number box takes a decimal number, such as `-0.25`.

Mind the signs. Each problem says which sense of rotation counts as positive, and it is not the same in every problem, because we follow MIT.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A bug walks around a pivoted ring" points={12} mit="Problem 1">

A thin ring of radius $R$ and mass $m_1$ lies flat on a frictionless table. One point of its rim is pivoted to the table, so the ring can turn freely about that point. A bug of mass $m_2$ walks along the ring with the constant speed $v$ relative to the ring. It starts at the pivot, and at that moment the ring is at rest.

MIT's drawing shows the table from above, with the pivot at the bottom of the ring and an arrow at the bug that points anticlockwise around the ring. The unit vector $\hat k$ points out of the page, towards the viewer. So the bug walks around $\hat k$ in the positive sense.

The angular velocity of the ring is a vector along $\hat k$. Write it as $\omega_z\,\hat k$: a positive $\omega_z$ means that the ring turns anticlockwise, the way the bug walks, and a negative $\omega_z$ means clockwise. (MIT asks for the vector. The boxes below ask for $\omega_z$.)

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Find $\omega_z$ at the moment the bug is halfway around the ring, using any of $m_1$, $m_2$, $v$ and $R$ that you need.

<Hint>

Take the ring and the bug together as the system. Which outside forces act on it, and about which point do they have no torque?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The angular momentum about the pivot is zero at the start, so it stays zero. It has two parts: that of the ring, which turns about the pivot, and that of the bug.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the ring you need its moment of inertia about the pivot, a point of its rim: use the parallel axis theorem. For the bug you need its velocity relative to the table. That is its velocity relative to the ring plus the velocity of the piece of ring it stands on.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$\omega_z =$" expr="-m2*v/((m1 + 2*m2)*R)"
  vars={{ m1: [0.5, 3], m2: [0.1, 1], v: [0.5, 3], R: [0.2, 2] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m2*v/((m1 + 2*m2)*R)", hint: "The size is right, the sign is not. The total angular momentum has to stay zero, so the ring must turn against the bug: clockwise, which is the negative sense here." },
    { expr: "-m2*v/(m1*R)", hint: "This takes v as the velocity of the bug relative to the table. The speed v is relative to the ring, and the ring carries the bug along as it turns: add the velocity of the piece of ring under the bug." },
    { expr: "-2*m2*v/((m1 + 4*m2)*R)", hint: "This uses the moment of inertia of the ring about its centre, m1 R². The ring turns about the pivot on its rim: use the parallel axis theorem." },
    { expr: "-m2*v/((2*m1 + m2)*R)", hint: "This puts the bug at the distance R from the pivot. Halfway around, the bug is at the far end of the diameter through the pivot, a distance 2R away." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Take the ring and the bug as one system. The forces between the bug's feet and the ring are inside the system. The pivot force acts at the pivot, so it has no torque about the pivot. Gravity and the push of the table are vertical and balance each other, and the table is frictionless, so they give no torque along the vertical axis. So the angular momentum about the pivot is constant. It is zero at the start, and it stays zero.

<Step title="Conservation about the pivot">

Call the pivot $P$. The notes state the principle as

<Formula label="Conservation of angular momentum about a point" tex="\vec L_{S,f}^{\,\text{sys}} = \vec L_{S,i}^{\,\text{sys}}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (19.5.36), p. 19-21" />

Here $S$ is the pivot $P$ and the starting value is zero. Only the $z$ components matter, because everything moves in the plane of the table.

</Step>

<Step title="The ring">

The ring turns about a fixed axis through $P$, so its angular momentum is its moment of inertia about $P$ times $\omega_z$ (notes, Eq. (19.5.27), p. 19-19). About its centre a ring has the moment of inertia $m_1R^2$ (notes, Eq. (19.5.31), p. 19-21). The pivot is a distance $R$ from the centre, so by the parallel axis theorem

<Formula label="Parallel axis theorem" tex="I_S = I_\text{cm} + m\,d_{S,\text{cm}}^2" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (16.2.16), p. 16-10" />

<Formula label="Bug: the ring about its pivot" tex="I_P = m_1R^2 + m_1R^2 = 2m_1R^2, \qquad L_{\text{ring},z} = 2m_1R^2\,\omega_z" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The bug">

Halfway around, the bug is at the far end of the diameter through $P$, a distance $2R$ from $P$. Relative to the ring it moves with the speed $v$ along the ring, which is at right angles to that diameter, in the anticlockwise sense. The piece of ring under the bug moves on a circle of radius $2R$ about $P$, so its velocity is at right angles to the diameter as well, with the component $2R\,\omega_z$ in the same sense. The velocity of the bug relative to the table is the sum of the two. It is at right angles to the line from $P$ to the bug, so the angular momentum is the distance times the momentum:

<Formula label="Bug: its angular momentum halfway around" tex="L_{\text{bug},z} = 2R\,m_2\,(v + 2R\,\omega_z)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

The sum is zero: $2m_1R^2\,\omega_z + 2m_2Rv + 4m_2R^2\,\omega_z = 0$, so

<Formula label="Bug: angular velocity of the ring halfway around" tex="\omega_z = -\frac{m_2\,v}{(m_1 + 2m_2)\,R}, \qquad \vec\omega = -\frac{m_2\,v}{(m_1 + 2m_2)\,R}\,\hat k" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A speed divided by a length is an angular velocity. The sign is negative: the ring turns clockwise, against the bug, as it must if the two angular momenta are to cancel. A very heavy ring ($m_1$ much larger than $m_2$) hardly turns. For a ring of almost no mass, $\omega_z$ goes to $-v/(2R)$, and then the bug's velocity relative to the table, $v + 2R\,\omega_z$, goes to zero: the bug walks on the spot and the ring slides back under its feet.

With our own numbers, $m_1 = 0.050$ kg, $m_2 = 0.010$ kg, $v = 0.020$ m/s and $R = 0.10$ m: $\omega_z = -0.010 \times 0.020/(0.070 \times 0.10) = -0.029$ rad/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

The bug walks on, all the way around the ring. What is the angular velocity of the ring at the moment the bug is back at the pivot?

<Hint>

The angular momentum about the pivot is still zero. How much of it can the bug have when it is at the pivot itself?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The angular momentum of a particle about a point is $\vec r \times \vec p$, with $\vec r$ the vector from that point to the particle.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="When the bug is back at the pivot, the angular velocity of the ring is:" choices={["zero", "the same as in part (a)", "as large as in part (a), but in the opposite sense", "twice as large as in part (a), in the same sense"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The total angular momentum about the pivot is still zero. A particle at the pivot has no angular momentum about the pivot, whatever its velocity.

<Step title="The bug at the pivot">

The angular momentum of the bug about $P$ is $\vec r_P \times m_2\vec v$, where $\vec r_P$ is the vector from $P$ to the bug (the definition in the lesson on the angular momentum of a point particle). At the pivot $\vec r_P = 0$, so the bug's angular momentum is zero.

</Step>

<Step title="The ring">

Then the ring has to have zero angular momentum on its own:

<Formula label="Bug: back at the pivot" tex="2m_1R^2\,\omega_z = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec\omega = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

At that moment the ring is at rest.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A remark of our own, beyond MIT's question: the same steps work at every point of the walk. Let $\varphi$ be the angle at the centre of the ring between the pivot and the bug, so that $\varphi = 0$ at the start, $\varphi = \pi$ halfway and $\varphi = 2\pi$ back at the pivot. The bug is then a distance $2R\sin(\varphi/2)$ from $P$, and the line along its velocity relative to the ring passes $P$ at the distance $R\,(1 - \cos\varphi)$. With $4R^2\sin^2(\varphi/2) = 2R^2(1 - \cos\varphi)$, the total angular momentum is $2m_1R^2\,\omega_z + m_2\,(1 - \cos\varphi)\,(vR + 2R^2\,\omega_z) = 0$, so

<Formula label="Bug: angular velocity of the ring at any point of the walk" tex="\omega_z = -\frac{m_2\,v\,(1 - \cos\varphi)}{2R\,\big[m_1 + m_2\,(1 - \cos\varphi)\big]}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own addition)" />

For $\varphi = \pi$ this is the result of part (a), and for $\varphi = 0$ and $\varphi = 2\pi$ it is zero. In between it is negative everywhere. So the ring turns clockwise during the whole walk, fastest when the bug is halfway, and it comes to rest when the bug arrives at the pivot. It does not turn back: it ends at rest, but turned clockwise away from where it started.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Two objects stick to a pivoted rod" points={12} mit="Problem 2">

A rigid uniform rod of length $d$ and mass $m$ lies at rest on a frictionless horizontal table. One of its ends is pivoted to the table at the point $P$. MIT's drawing shows the table from above, with the rod pointing up the page from $P$.

Two point-like objects, each of mass $m$ as well, slide towards the rod at right angles to it, each with the speed $v$. The first comes from the left and moves to the right. It hits the rod at a distance $2d/3$ from the pivot and sticks. The second comes from the right and moves to the left. It hits the rod at the same instant, at a distance $d/3$ from the pivot, and sticks too. (MIT's text says "sticks" for the first object only. Its drawing of the situation after the collision shows both on the rod, and its text has the rod and the two particles all rotate together.) After the collision the rod and the two objects turn about $P$ as one body with the angular speed $\omega_f$.

MIT gives the moment of inertia of the rod about an axis through its centre of mass, at right angles to the table: $I_\text{cm} = \tfrac{1}{12}md^2$.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Find $\omega_f$ just after the collision, using any of $d$, $m$ and $v$ that you need. Count clockwise as positive, as MIT does (for a clockwise rotation the angular velocity points into the page).

<Hint>

The pivot pushes on the rod during the collision with a force that nobody knows. About which point does that force have no torque?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Before the collision each object has an angular momentum about $P$: its momentum times the distance from $P$ to its line of motion. Do the two objects go around $P$ in the same sense?

</Hint>

<Hint>

After the collision everything turns about $P$ together. Add up three moments of inertia about $P$: the rod (about its end, not its centre) and the two objects.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$\omega_f =$" expr="3*v/(8*d)"
  vars={{ v: [0.5, 5], d: [0.3, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "9*v/(8*d)", hint: "This adds the two angular momenta. The objects move in opposite directions on the same side of the pivot, so they go around P in opposite senses: subtract." },
    { expr: "-3*v/(8*d)", hint: "The size is right, the sign is not. The object that is further from the pivot has the larger angular momentum, and it moves to the right above P: that is clockwise, the positive sense here." },
    { expr: "3*v/(5*d)", hint: "This leaves out the rod. After the collision the rod turns too: add its moment of inertia about P." },
    { expr: "12*v/(23*d)", hint: "This uses the rod's moment of inertia about its centre, md²/12. The rod turns about its end P: use the parallel axis theorem." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Take the rod and the two objects as the system. During the collision the pivot pushes on the rod with an unknown force, so the momentum of the system is not conserved. That force acts at $P$, so it has no torque about $P$. The forces between the objects and the rod are inside the system. So the angular momentum about $P$ is the same just before and just after the collision (the lesson on torque and angular impulse does this for one object that hits a pivoted ring, and the notes for a pivoted rod in Example 19.7, pp. 19-21 to 19-26).

<Step title="Before the collision">

Only the objects move. Each has the momentum $mv$, at right angles to the rod, so its angular momentum about $P$ is $mv$ times its distance from $P$ along the rod. The first object passes above $P$ in the drawing and moves to the right: clockwise about $P$, positive. The second passes above $P$ too and moves to the left: anticlockwise, negative.

<Formula label="Rod and two objects: angular momentum before" tex="L_i = mv\,\frac{2d}{3} - mv\,\frac{d}{3} = \frac{mvd}{3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The moment of inertia after the collision">

By the parallel axis theorem (Problem 1) the rod has, about its end,

<Formula label="Uniform rod about its end" tex="I_\text{rod} = \tfrac{1}{12}md^2 + m\left(\tfrac{d}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac13 md^2" cite="reading1" at="Example 19.7, Eq. (19.5.39), p. 19-24" />

Each object adds its mass times the square of its distance from $P$:

<Formula label="Rod and two objects: moment of inertia about the pivot" tex="I_P = \tfrac13 md^2 + m\left(\tfrac{2d}{3}\right)^2 + m\left(\tfrac{d}{3}\right)^2 = \tfrac89 md^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Angular momentum is conserved">

After the collision the whole body turns about the fixed point $P$, so $L_f = I_P\,\omega_f$:

<Formula label="Rod and two objects: angular speed after the collision" tex="\frac{mvd}{3} = \tfrac89 md^2\,\omega_f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega_f = \frac{3v}{8d}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A speed divided by a length is an angular velocity, and the mass has dropped out, because every mass in the problem is the same $m$. The result is positive: the body turns clockwise, the sense of the object that hit further from the pivot. With our own numbers, $v = 2.0$ m/s and $d = 0.60$ m: $\omega_f = 3 \times 2.0/(8 \times 0.60) = 1.25$ rad/s.

The momentum shows what the pivot did. Before the collision the total momentum is zero ($mv$ to the right and $mv$ to the left). After it, every part moves to the right: the centre of the rod with the speed $\omega_f d/2$ and the objects with $2\omega_f d/3$ and $\omega_f d/3$, which gives $m\omega_f d\,(\tfrac12 + \tfrac23 + \tfrac13) = \tfrac32 m\omega_f d = \tfrac{9}{16}mv$. This momentum came from the pivot force during the collision.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Let $K_i$ be the kinetic energy of the system just before the collision and $K_f$ the kinetic energy just after. Find the ratio $(K_f - K_i)/K_i$. It is a pure number: type it as a decimal number, with its sign.

<Hint>

Before the collision two objects move and the rod is at rest. After it one rigid body turns about $P$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The kinetic energy of a body that turns about a fixed axis is $\tfrac12 I\omega^2$, with the moment of inertia about that axis. You found both in part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$(K_f - K_i)/K_i =$" value="-0.9375" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-energy-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The objects stick, so kinetic energy is lost. Compare the kinetic energy of the two moving objects before with the rotational kinetic energy of the one body after.

<Step title="Before and after">

Before: two objects of mass $m$ with the speed $v$, so $K_i = 2 \times \tfrac12 mv^2 = mv^2$. After: the body turns about $P$ with the moment of inertia and the angular speed of part (a). The kinetic energy of a body that turns about a fixed axis is $\tfrac12 I\omega^2$ (the notes use it in this form in Example 19.7, p. 19-25):

<Formula label="Rod and two objects: kinetic energy after the collision" tex="K_f = \tfrac12 I_P\,\omega_f^2 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac89 md^2 \cdot \frac{9v^2}{64\,d^2} = \tfrac{1}{16}\,mv^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Rod and two objects: relative change of the kinetic energy" tex="\frac{K_f - K_i}{K_i} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{16}mv^2 - mv^2}{mv^2} = -\frac{15}{16} = -0.9375" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ratio has no unit and does not depend on $d$, $m$ or $v$. It is negative, as it must be when bodies stick together, and it lies between $-1$ (everything lost) and 0 (nothing lost). Almost all of the kinetic energy is lost here: only one sixteenth of it remains as rotation. With the numbers of part (a) and $m = 0.30$ kg: $K_i = 0.30 \times 2.0^2 = 1.2$ J and $K_f = 1.2/16 = 0.075$ J.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="An elastic collision with a pivoted rod" points={12} mit="Problem 3">

A rigid rod of length $d$ and mass $m$ lies on a frictionless horizontal table, at rest. One of its ends is pivoted to the table at the point $P$. In MIT's drawing, a top view, the rod points up the page from $P$.

A point-like object of the same mass $m$ slides in from the left, at right angles to the rod, with the speed $v_i$. It hits the rod at its midpoint. The collision is elastic. Afterwards the object moves back the way it came, with the speed $v_f$, and the rod turns clockwise about $P$ with the angular speed $\omega_f$.

MIT gives the moment of inertia of the rod about its centre of mass: $I_\text{cm} = \tfrac{1}{12}md^2$.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Find the angular speed $\omega_f$ of the rod, using any of $d$, $m$ and $v_i$ that you need.

<Hint>

There are two unknowns, $\omega_f$ and $v_f$, so you need two equations. One conservation law holds because of the pivot, and one because the collision is elastic.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The pivot force has no torque about $P$, so the angular momentum about $P$ is conserved. Mind the sign of the object's angular momentum after the collision: it now moves the other way.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Elastic means that the kinetic energy is the same before and after. Write both equations so that $v_i$ and $v_f$ are on one side and $\omega_f$ on the other. The energy equation then has a difference of two squares, which you can divide by the angular momentum equation.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$\omega_f =$" expr="12*vi/(7*d)"
  vars={{ vi: [0.5, 5], d: [0.3, 2] }}
  names={{ vi: "v_i" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "6*vi/(7*d)", hint: "This is the angular speed if the object sticks to the rod. Here the collision is elastic and the object bounces back." },
    { expr: "3*vi/(2*d)", hint: "This lets the object stop dead at the rod. It bounces back with a speed that you do not know yet: use the conservation of kinetic energy as the second equation." },
    { expr: "3*vi/d", hint: "This uses the rod's moment of inertia about its centre, md²/12. The rod turns about its end P: use the parallel axis theorem." },
    { expr: "2*vi/d", hint: "This treats the rod as a free body of mass m that takes over the object's speed, as equal masses do on a line, and then divides that speed by d/2. The pivot holds one end of the rod, so momentum is not conserved. Use the angular momentum about P and the kinetic energy." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two unknowns need two equations. The pivot force has no torque about $P$, so the angular momentum about $P$ is conserved, as in Problem 2. The collision is elastic, so the kinetic energy is conserved as well. (The pivot does no work, because the point where its force acts does not move.) The momentum is not conserved, because of the pivot force.

<Step title="Angular momentum about the pivot">

Count clockwise as positive. The object moves along a line a distance $d/2$ from $P$. Before the collision it moves to the right, above $P$ in the drawing, which is clockwise about $P$. After it, it moves to the left, which is anticlockwise. The rod has the moment of inertia $\tfrac13 md^2$ about its end (Problem 2):

<Formula label="Elastic rod: angular momentum" tex="mv_i\,\frac{d}{2} = -mv_f\,\frac{d}{2} + \tfrac13 md^2\,\omega_f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_i + v_f = \tfrac23\,d\,\omega_f" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Kinetic energy">

<Formula label="Elastic rod: kinetic energy" tex="\tfrac12 mv_i^2 = \tfrac12 mv_f^2 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13 md^2\,\omega_f^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_i^2 - v_f^2 = \tfrac13\,d^2\omega_f^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Divide the two equations">

The left side of the energy equation is $(v_i - v_f)(v_i + v_f)$. Divide it by the angular momentum equation (allowed, because $v_i + v_f$ is not zero):

<Formula label="Elastic rod: the linear equation" tex="v_i - v_f = \frac{\tfrac13\,d^2\omega_f^2}{\tfrac23\,d\,\omega_f} = \frac{d}{2}\,\omega_f" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

Add this to $v_i + v_f = \tfrac23 d\,\omega_f$. The unknown $v_f$ drops out: $2v_i = \left(\tfrac23 + \tfrac12\right) d\,\omega_f = \tfrac76\,d\,\omega_f$, so

<Formula label="Elastic rod: angular speed after the collision" tex="\omega_f = \frac{12\,v_i}{7\,d}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A speed divided by a length is an angular velocity. The mass has dropped out, because the object and the rod have the same mass. The linear equation has a meaning: $\omega_f d/2$ is the speed of the midpoint of the rod, the point that was hit, so the equation says $v_i = v_f + \omega_f d/2$. The object and the struck point move apart after the collision as fast as they came together before it. This is the rule that the lesson on elastic collisions finds for collisions along a line.

If the object had stuck to the rod, the angular speed would have been $mv_i(d/2)$ divided by $\tfrac13 md^2 + \tfrac14 md^2$, which is $6v_i/(7d)$. The elastic collision gives the rod twice as much here. With our own numbers, $v_i = 3.5$ m/s and $d = 1.2$ m: $\omega_f = 12 \times 3.5/(7 \times 1.2) = 5.0$ rad/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

This part is our own addition. MIT asks only for $\omega_f$. Find the speed $v_f$ with which the object moves back, using any of $d$, $m$ and $v_i$ that you need.

<Hint>

Part (a) already contains an equation with $v_f$, $v_i$ and $\omega_f$ only.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$v_f =$" expr="vi/7"
  vars={{ vi: [0.5, 5] }}
  names={{ vi: "v_i" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "vi", hint: "Then the object would keep all of its kinetic energy and the rod would get none. The rod turns after the collision, so the object must be slower than before." },
    { expr: "vi/2", hint: "This size follows from the rod's moment of inertia about its centre, md²/12. The rod turns about its end P: use the parallel axis theorem." },
    { expr: "5*vi/7", hint: "This is the size of v_i minus ω_f d, and ω_f d is the speed of the far end of the rod. The object hits the midpoint, which moves with ω_f d/2." },
    { expr: "-vi/7", hint: "The size is right. A speed is positive: the direction (back to the left) is already in the statement." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Use the linear equation of part (a), $v_i - v_f = \omega_f d/2$, with the angular speed found there.

<Step title="Solve">

<Formula label="Elastic rod: speed of the object after the collision" tex="v_f = v_i - \frac{d}{2}\,\omega_f = v_i - \tfrac67\,v_i = \tfrac17\,v_i" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is positive, so the object does move back, as MIT's statement says. The energies add up: the object keeps $\tfrac12 m\,(v_i/7)^2 = \tfrac{1}{49}$ of its kinetic energy, and the rod gets $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac13 md^2\,\omega_f^2 = \tfrac{24}{49}\,mv_i^2$, which is $\tfrac{48}{49}$ of $\tfrac12 mv_i^2$. Together that is all of it.

There is a second way to see the result. In both equations of part (a) the rod appears only through its moment of inertia about $P$ and the speed $u = \omega_f d/2$ of the struck point: the angular momentum equation is $m(v_i + v_f) = \tfrac43 m\,u$ and the energy equation is $\tfrac12 mv_i^2 = \tfrac12 mv_f^2 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac43 m\,u^2$. These are the equations of a collision along a line with a free body of mass $\tfrac43 m$ at rest (the moment of inertia about $P$ divided by the square of $d/2$). The formula of the lesson on elastic collisions then gives the rebound speed $(\tfrac43 m - m)/(\tfrac43 m + m)\,v_i = v_i/7$. With the numbers of part (a): $v_f = 3.5/7 = 0.50$ m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="An elastic collision with a pivoted hoop" points={10} mit="Problem 4">

A rigid hoop of radius $R$ and mass $m_R$ lies on a frictionless horizontal table, at rest. One point of the hoop is pivoted to the table at the point $P$. In MIT's drawing, a top view, $P$ is the point of the hoop at the top of the page.

A point-like object of mass $m$ slides in from the left and moves to the right with the speed $v_0$. Its line of motion passes through the centre of the hoop, so it is a distance $R$ from $P$, and the object hits the hoop at the point on its left side that is level with the centre. (MIT's text says that it hits the hoop "at its midpoint". Its drawing shows the object level with the centre of the hoop.) The collision is elastic. Afterwards the object moves back to the left with the speed $v_f$, and the hoop turns anticlockwise about $P$ with the angular speed $\omega_f$.

MIT gives the moment of inertia of the hoop about an axis through its centre of mass, at right angles to the table: $I_\text{cm} = m_RR^2$.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Find the speed $v_f$ of the object just after the collision, using any of $R$, $m$, $m_R$ and $v_0$ that you need. Your answer must not contain $\omega_f$.

<Hint>

The method is that of Problem 3: the angular momentum about the pivot and the kinetic energy are both conserved. Two equations for $v_f$ and $\omega_f$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the angular momentum of the object you need the distance from $P$ to its line of motion, not the distance from $P$ to the point where it hits. For the hoop you need its moment of inertia about $P$, a point of the hoop itself.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Divide the energy equation by the angular momentum equation to get a linear equation, as in Problem 3. Then remove $\omega_f$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$v_f =$" expr="(2*mR - m)*v0/(2*mR + m)"
  vars={{ m: [0.1, 1], mR: [1, 3], v0: [0.5, 5] }}
  names={{ mR: "m_R", v0: "v_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(mR - m)*v0/(mR + m)", hint: "This is the rebound speed from a free body of mass m_R, or from a hoop that turns about its centre. The hoop turns about the point P of its rim: its moment of inertia about P is larger than m_R R²." },
    { expr: "(m - 2*mR)*v0/(2*mR + m)", hint: "The size is right. This is the velocity component to the right, which is negative. MIT asks for the speed with which the object moves to the left." },
    { expr: "2*m*v0/(2*mR + m)", hint: "That is ω_f R, the speed that the struck point of the hoop gets along the object's line of motion. The speed of the object is v0 minus this." },
    { expr: "v0", hint: "Then the object would keep all of its kinetic energy and the hoop would get none. The hoop turns after the collision, so the object must be slower than before." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in Problem 3: the pivot force has no torque about $P$, so the angular momentum about $P$ is conserved, and the collision is elastic, so the kinetic energy is conserved. (The lesson on torque and angular impulse treats this ring and this line of motion for an object that sticks.)

<Step title="The hoop about its pivot">

The pivot is a point of the hoop, a distance $R$ from its centre. By the parallel axis theorem (Problem 1):

<Formula label="Hoop: moment of inertia about the pivot" tex="I_P = m_RR^2 + m_RR^2 = 2m_RR^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Angular momentum about the pivot">

Count anticlockwise as positive. The object's line of motion passes $P$ at the distance $R$, below $P$ in the drawing. Moving to the right below $P$ is anticlockwise about $P$, and moving to the left is clockwise:

<Formula label="Hoop: angular momentum" tex="mv_0R = -mv_fR + 2m_RR^2\,\omega_f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m\,(v_0 + v_f) = 2m_RR\,\omega_f" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Kinetic energy">

<Formula label="Hoop: kinetic energy" tex="\tfrac12 mv_0^2 = \tfrac12 mv_f^2 + \tfrac12\cdot 2m_RR^2\,\omega_f^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m\,(v_0^2 - v_f^2) = 2m_RR^2\,\omega_f^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Divide and solve">

Divide the energy equation by the angular momentum equation:

<Formula label="Hoop: the linear equation" tex="v_0 - v_f = R\,\omega_f" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

Put $R\,\omega_f = v_0 - v_f$ into the angular momentum equation: $m\,(v_0 + v_f) = 2m_R\,(v_0 - v_f)$. Collect the terms with $v_f$:

<Formula label="Hoop: speed of the object after the collision" tex="v_f = \frac{2m_R - m}{2m_R + m}\,v_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A ratio of masses times a speed is a speed, and $R$ does not appear. A very heavy hoop ($m_R$ much larger than $m$) gives $v_f = v_0$: the object bounces back as from a wall. The result is positive only for $m < 2m_R$. For $m = 2m_R$ the object stops dead, and a heavier object would go on to the right. MIT's statement, in which the object moves back to the left, therefore needs $m < 2m_R$.

As in Problem 3(b), the two equations are those of a collision along a line with a free body at rest, now of mass $I_P/R^2 = 2m_R$, and the result is the formula of the lesson on elastic collisions with that mass. With our own numbers, $m = 0.10$ kg, $m_R = 0.20$ kg and $v_0 = 3.0$ m/s: $v_f = (0.40 - 0.10)/(0.40 + 0.10) \times 3.0 = 1.8$ m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

This part is our own addition. Find the angular speed $\omega_f$ of the hoop just after the collision, using any of $R$, $m$, $m_R$ and $v_0$ that you need.

<Hint>

The linear equation of part (a) connects $\omega_f$ with the two speeds of the object.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$\omega_f =$" expr="2*m*v0/((2*mR + m)*R)"
  vars={{ m: [0.1, 1], mR: [1, 3], v0: [0.5, 5], R: [0.2, 2] }}
  names={{ mR: "m_R", v0: "v_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*m*v0/(2*mR + m)", hint: "That is a speed: the speed ω_f R of the struck point along the object's line of motion. Divide by R." },
    { expr: "2*m*v0/((mR + m)*R)", hint: "This uses the moment of inertia m_R R² about the centre. The hoop turns about the point P of its rim: use 2 m_R R²." },
    { expr: "m*v0/(2*(mR + m)*R)", hint: "This is the angular speed if the object sticks to the hoop, the case of the lesson. Here the object bounces back." },
    { expr: "m*v0/(2*mR*R)", hint: "This lets the object stop dead. It bounces back with the speed of part (a), so it gives the hoop more angular momentum than that." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Use the linear equation $v_0 - v_f = R\,\omega_f$ with the speed of part (a).

<Step title="Solve">

<Formula label="Hoop: angular speed after the collision" tex="\omega_f = \frac{v_0 - v_f}{R} = \frac{v_0}{R}\left(1 - \frac{2m_R - m}{2m_R + m}\right) = \frac{2m\,v_0}{(2m_R + m)\,R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A speed divided by a length is an angular velocity. It is positive, so with our sign choice the hoop turns anticlockwise, as MIT's drawing shows. A very heavy hoop hardly turns. With the numbers of part (a) and $R = 0.25$ m: $\omega_f = 2 \times 0.10 \times 3.0/(0.50 \times 0.25) = 4.8$ rad/s, and $R\,\omega_f = 1.2$ m/s is indeed $v_0 - v_f = 3.0 - 1.8$ m/s. The energies: before $\tfrac12 \times 0.10 \times 3.0^2 = 0.45$ J, after $\tfrac12 \times 0.10 \times 1.8^2 = 0.162$ J for the object and $\tfrac12 \times 2 \times 0.20 \times 0.25^2 \times 4.8^2 = 0.288$ J for the hoop, together 0.45 J.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A spaceship and a planet" points={14} mit="Problem 5">

Two spaceships move around a planet of mass $m_p$, in the same plane. The planet is very much heavier than either spaceship, so take it to stay at rest. $G$ is the gravitational constant.

Spaceship 1 has the mass $m_1$ and moves with the speed $v_1$ on a circular orbit of radius $R$.

Spaceship 2 has the mass $m_2$ and moves on an elliptical orbit. At its furthest point it is a distance $3R$ from the centre of the planet and has the speed $v_2$. At its closest point it is a distance $R$ from the centre and has the speed $v_p$. In MIT's drawing the furthest point is on the left of the planet and the closest point on the right, where the ellipse touches the circle of spaceship 1, and both spaceships go around the planet in the same sense.

At some moment both spaceships arrive at almost the same time at the closest point of spaceship 2. There spaceship 2 fires its rockets, for a time that is very short compared with the time of an orbit, to change its speed to $v_1$, so that the two can dock.

MIT asks only for the change of the speed and for its direction, parts (c) and (d). Parts (a) and (b) are steps on the way, which we have made parts of their own.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the speed $v_1$ of spaceship 1, using any of $G$, $R$ and $m_p$ that you need.

<Hint>

On a circular orbit the speed is constant, and gravity is the only force. Use Newton's second law along the radius.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$v_1 =$" expr="sqrt(G*mp/R)"
  vars={{ G: [1, 10], mp: [1, 10], R: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ mp: "m_p" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2*G*mp/R)", hint: "This is the escape speed from the distance R. A spaceship with that speed does not stay on a circle. Use the second law with gravity as the centripetal force." },
    { expr: "sqrt(G*mp/R^2)", hint: "Under the root this is an acceleration, not a speed squared. The centripetal acceleration is the speed squared over R: one factor R cancels." },
    { expr: "G*mp/R", hint: "That has the unit of a speed squared. Take the square root." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On a circular orbit gravity is the centripetal force, as for the Moon's orbit in the lesson on [Newton's second law and circular motion](/courses/8-01/newtons-second-law-circular-motion).

<Step title="The second law along the radius">

<Formula label="Spaceship 1: circular orbit" tex="\frac{G\,m_p m_1}{R^2} = \frac{m_1\,v_1^2}{R} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{G\,m_p}{R}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$Gm_p/R$ is a potential energy per mass, so it is a speed squared. The mass of the spaceship cancels. With our own numbers, the Earth as the planet ($Gm_p = 3.986\times10^{14}$ m³/s², NASA's Earth Fact Sheet) and $R = 7.0\times10^6$ m: $v_1 = 7.55$ km/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the speed $v_p$ of spaceship 2 at its closest point, before it fires its rockets, using any of $G$, $R$ and $m_p$ that you need.

<Hint>

On the ellipse the speed changes all the time, so the second law for a circle does not help. Which quantities of spaceship 2 stay the same along its orbit?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Gravity points straight at the centre of the planet, so it has no torque about that centre. And gravity is a conservative force.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the angular momentum about the centre of the planet, and the mechanical energy, at the furthest and at the closest point. At these two points the velocity is at right angles to the line to the planet. That gives two equations for $v_2$ and $v_p$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$v_p =$" expr="sqrt(3*G*mp/(2*R))"
  vars={{ G: [1, 10], mp: [1, 10], R: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ mp: "m_p" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(G*mp/R)", hint: "This is the speed of the circular orbit of radius R. Spaceship 2 swings out to 3R from the same point, so it must be faster there than spaceship 1." },
    { expr: "sqrt(4*G*mp/(3*R))", hint: "This lets spaceship 2 stand still at its furthest point. There it still moves, with the speed v2: connect v2 to v_p with the angular momentum." },
    { expr: "sqrt(G*mp/(2*R))", hint: "This is the speed of a circular orbit of radius 2R, halfway between the two distances. On an ellipse the speed changes along the orbit: use the angular momentum and the energy at the two end points." },
    { expr: "sqrt(G*mp/(6*R))", hint: "That is v2, the speed at the furthest point. At the closest point the spaceship is three times as fast." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two quantities of spaceship 2 stay constant along its orbit. Gravity points at the centre of the planet, so it has no torque about that centre, and the angular momentum about the centre is constant. Gravity is conservative, so the mechanical energy is constant too. The notes use the same two laws for a meteor that flies past the Earth (Example 19.4, pp. 19-10 to 19-12).

<Step title="The one fact about the ellipse">

At the furthest and at the closest point the distance to the planet is as large or as small as it gets. At such a moment the distance is not changing, so the velocity has no part along the line to the planet: it is at right angles to that line. The angular momentum about the centre is then simply the distance times the momentum.

</Step>

<Step title="Angular momentum">

<Formula label="Spaceship 2: angular momentum at the two end points" tex="m_2\,v_2\,(3R) = m_2\,v_p\,R \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_2 = \tfrac13\,v_p" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

(The notes write the same law for their meteor, far away and at its closest point: Example 19.4, p. 19-12.)

</Step>

<Step title="Energy">

With the zero at infinity, the notes give the potential energy of gravitation of two bodies as

<Formula label="Potential energy of gravitation (two bodies)" tex="U^G(r) = -\frac{G\,m_1 m_2}{r}" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (14.16), p. 14-14" />

(In this formula $m_1$ and $m_2$ stand for any two bodies. Here they are the planet and spaceship 2.) The mechanical energy at the closest point equals that at the furthest point:

<Formula label="Spaceship 2: energy at the two end points" tex="\tfrac12 m_2 v_p^2 - \frac{G\,m_p m_2}{R} = \tfrac12 m_2 v_2^2 - \frac{G\,m_p m_2}{3R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

Put in $v_2 = v_p/3$ and divide by $m_2$: $\tfrac12 v_p^2\left(1 - \tfrac19\right) = \frac{Gm_p}{R}\left(1 - \tfrac13\right)$, that is $\tfrac49 v_p^2 = \tfrac23\,Gm_p/R$, so

<Formula label="Spaceship 2: speed at the closest point" tex="v_p = \sqrt{\frac{3\,G\,m_p}{2R}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The unit is that of part (a). The speed is $\sqrt{3/2} = 1.225$ times $v_1$: larger than the circular speed at that point, which is why spaceship 2 swings out beyond the circle, and smaller than the escape speed $\sqrt{2Gm_p/R} = 1.414\,v_1$, which is why it comes back. At the furthest point $v_2 = v_p/3 = \sqrt{Gm_p/(6R)} = 0.408\,v_1$, less than the speed $\sqrt{Gm_p/(3R)} = 0.577\,v_1$ of a circular orbit of radius $3R$, so there the spaceship falls back inwards. With the numbers of part (a): $v_p = 9.24$ km/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the change of the speed of spaceship 2 that is needed for docking, $\Delta v = v_1 - v_p$, using only $G$, $R$ and $m_p$.

<Hint>

During the short burn the spaceship hardly moves, so it is still at the distance $R$. You have both speeds at that point.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-c" label="$\Delta v =$" expr="(1 - sqrt(3/2))*sqrt(G*mp/R)"

  names={{ mp: "m_p" }}
  mistakes={[

<Solution>

**The idea.** The burn is so short that the spaceship is still at the closest point when it ends. Its speed has to change from $v_p$ to $v_1$.

<Step title="The difference">

<Formula label="Docking: the change of the speed" tex="\Delta v = v_1 - v_p = \sqrt{\frac{G\,m_p}{R}} - \sqrt{\frac{3\,G\,m_p}{2R}} = \left(1 - \sqrt{\tfrac32}\right)\sqrt{\frac{G\,m_p}{R}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The number in front is $1 - 1.225 = -0.225$, so the change is a little less than a quarter of the circular speed, and it is negative. The masses of the spaceships do not appear. With the numbers of part (a): $\Delta v = 7.55 - 9.24 = -1.70$ km/s (from the unrounded speeds).

Equal speeds are enough for docking here. At the closest point the velocity of spaceship 2 is at right angles to the line to the planet (part (b)), and so is the velocity of spaceship 1 on its circle. Both go around in the same sense, so after the burn the two velocities are equal as vectors.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Does spaceship 2 have to speed up or slow down in order to dock?

<Hint>

Look at the sign of $\Delta v$. Or compare the two orbits: which spaceship swings out further from the point where they meet?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-d" label="To dock, spaceship 2 must:" choices={["speed up", "slow down", "do neither: the two speeds are already equal at that point", "speed up or slow down, depending on the masses of the spaceships"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The sign of $\Delta v$ decides.

<Step title="Slow down">

$\Delta v = v_1 - v_p$ is negative, so $v_p$ is larger than $v_1$: spaceship 2 arrives at the meeting point faster than spaceship 1 and has to slow down. Its rockets must push against its motion.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This fits the two orbits. Both spaceships pass the same point, at the same distance from the planet, so they have the same potential energy per mass there. The one that is faster has more energy per mass and climbs further away: that is spaceship 2, which reaches $3R$, while spaceship 1 stays at $R$. To come down to the circle, spaceship 2 has to give up speed. The masses of the spaceships play no part, because they cancelled in parts (a) and (b).

</Step>

</Solution>
