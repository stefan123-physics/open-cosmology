## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. For a collision, draw the system just before and just after it and compare the momentum. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A formula box takes plain typing, for example `m1*u/(m1 + m2)`, `sqrt(2*h/g)` or `(v0^2 - 2*g*h0)/(2*g)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type $\mu_k$ as `mu`, and type subscripts on the line: `m1`, `mA`, `h0`, `v0`, `t1`. A number box accepts any value within 2% of ours (1% where the box says so), and takes a bare number in the unit shown next to the box. You may type a large number as `1.2e5`. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem or part ends with its full solution. ## What you need from the lessons

The lessons of this week give the momentum of a body and what changes it. A force that acts for some time gives an impulse, and the impulse is the change of momentum:

<Formula label="Impulse and change of momentum" tex="\vec p = m\,\vec v, \qquad \vec I = \int_{t}^{t + \Delta t} \vec F(t')\,dt' = \Delta\vec p" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (10.2.1) and (10.2.5), pp. 10-1 and 10-2" />

The average force over the time $\Delta t$ is the constant force that would give the same impulse:

<Formula label="Average force" tex="\vec F_\text{ave}\,\Delta t = \Delta\vec p" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (10.2.6) to (10.2.8), pp. 10-2 and 10-3" />

When no force acts on a system from outside (or when a collision is over so quickly that the outside forces have no time to give an impulse), the momentum of the system stays the same:

<Formula label="Conservation of momentum" tex="\vec p_{\text{sys},i} = \vec p_{\text{sys},f}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (10.7.5), p. 10-12" />

The centre of mass of a set of particles, and of a body whose mass is spread out, is

<Formula label="Centre of mass" tex="\vec R_\text{cm} = \frac{1}{m_\text{sys}}\sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i\,\vec r_i, \qquad \vec R_\text{cm} = \frac{\int_{\text{body}}\vec r\,dm}{\int_{\text{body}} dm}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (10.5.3) and (10.5.4), p. 10-7" />

and it moves as one particle that carries the whole mass and feels only the outside forces:

<Formula label="Motion of the centre of mass" tex="\vec p_\text{sys} = m_\text{sys}\,\vec V_\text{cm}, \qquad \vec F^{\,\text{ext}} = m_\text{sys}\,\vec A_\text{cm}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (10.6.2) and (10.6.3), p. 10-10" />

The lessons on energy come later in the course, so the solutions here do not use energy. They use the two results for constant acceleration from the first week,

<Formula label="Constant acceleration" tex="v(t) = v_0 + a\,t, \qquad x(t) = x_0 + v_0\,t + \tfrac12\,a\,t^2" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 4.5, Eqs. (4.4.2) and (4.4.9), pp. 11 and 12" />

Solve the first for $t$ and put it in the second. The time drops out, and what is left ties the speed to the distance covered (this step is ours):

<Formula label="Speed and distance at constant acceleration" tex="v^2 = v_0^2 + 2\,a\,(x - x_0)" cite="reading2" at="follows from Eqs. (4.4.2) and (4.4.9), pp. 11 and 12, by removing t (our own step)" />

Air resistance is left out everywhere, and $g$ is the size of the acceleration of a falling body.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Stopping a bullet" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

A block of mass $m_2$ lies at rest on a horizontal ledge. A bullet of mass $m_1$ flies horizontally with the speed $u$, hits the block and stays stuck in it. The block, with the bullet inside, then slides over the ledge towards its edge, which is a distance $d$ away. Over this whole distance the ledge is rough: the coefficient of kinetic friction between block and ledge is $\mu_k$. At the edge the ledge ends in a straight drop of height $h$ to the level ground below.

The collision takes almost no time. The block is small: all distances are large compared with its size. Leave out air resistance.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Find $u_\text{min}$, the smallest speed of the bullet for which the block reaches the edge and falls off, in terms of some or all of $m_1$, $m_2$, $\mu_k$, $d$, $h$ and $g$.

<Hint>

Split the motion into two stages: the collision, which is over almost at once, and the slide over the rough ledge. Which quantity stays the same in the first stage? What does friction do in the second?

</Hint>

<Hint>

During the collision friction has no time to give an impulse, so the momentum of bullet and block together stays the same. That gives their common speed just after the collision.

</Hint>

<Hint>

During the slide the acceleration is constant. The block just reaches the edge when its speed there is zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$u_\text{min} =$" expr="(m1 + m2)/m1*sqrt(2*mu*g*d)"
  vars={{ m1: [0.01, 0.05], m2: [1, 3], mu: [0.2, 0.8], g: [9, 10], d: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2", mu: "\\mu_k" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2*mu*g*d)", hint: "That is the speed the block, with the bullet in it, needs just after the collision. The bullet's speed before the collision is larger: its momentum is shared with the block." },
    { expr: "m1/(m1 + m2)*sqrt(2*mu*g*d)", hint: "Check a limit: a very heavy block is hard to set moving, so the bullet must then be very fast. Your result goes to zero instead." },
    { expr: "(m1 + m2)/m2*sqrt(2*mu*g*d)", hint: "Before the collision only the bullet moves, so the momentum is m1 u. Check a limit: a very light bullet must be very fast, but your result then stays near the speed of the block." },
    { expr: "sqrt((m1 + m2)/m1)*sqrt(2*mu*g*d)", hint: "This would follow if the sum of the m v^2 of the bodies were the same before and after the collision. When two bodies stick together it is not. The sum of the m v is." },
    { expr: "(m1 + m2)/m1*sqrt(mu*g*d)", hint: "With the constant deceleration mu g over the distance d, the speed squared drops by 2 mu g d. A factor 2 is missing." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two stages, each with its own rule. In the collision the momentum of bullet and block together does not change. In the slide, kinetic friction slows the block at a constant rate. The smallest bullet speed is the one for which the block arrives at the edge with no speed left.

<Step title="The collision">

Take the direction of the bullet as positive. Just before the collision only the bullet moves, so the momentum of the system (bullet and block) is $m_1 u$. Friction on the block is an outside force, but the collision is so short that its impulse is negligible. Just after the collision the two move as one body of mass $m_1 + m_2$ with the speed $V$:

<Formula label="Bullet and block: speed after the collision" tex="m_1 u = (m_1 + m_2)\,V, \qquad V = \frac{m_1 u}{m_1 + m_2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The slide">

The ledge is level, so the normal force on the block is $N = (m_1 + m_2)\,g$. The lessons give the size of kinetic friction:

<Formula label="Kinetic friction" tex="f_k = \mu_k N" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (8.3.5), p. 8-14" />

Friction is the only horizontal force, and it points against the motion, so the acceleration is $a = -\mu_k g$. The masses have dropped out. With the relation between speed and distance, the speed $v_e$ at the edge follows from

<Formula label="Speed of the block at the edge" tex="v_e^2 = V^2 - 2\,\mu_k g\,d" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The smallest speed">

The block falls off when it still moves at the edge. In the limiting case $v_e = 0$, so $V = \sqrt{2\mu_k g d}$ and

<Formula label="Smallest speed of the bullet" tex="u_\text{min} = \frac{m_1 + m_2}{m_1}\,\sqrt{2\,\mu_k g\,d}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

Any faster bullet sends the block over the edge. The height $h$ plays no part here.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The root is the root of an acceleration times a length, a speed, and the ratio of the masses has no unit. Without friction, or with the block at the very edge ($d = 0$), any bullet will do. A heavier block needs a faster bullet. If the block had almost no mass, the bullet would only need the speed $\sqrt{2\mu_k g d}$, the speed with which any body must start to slide a distance $d$ against this friction.

A number (ours): a bullet of 0.010 kg and a block of 1.99 kg make 2.00 kg together, 200 times the mass of the bullet. With $\mu_k = 0.40$ and $d = 1.5$ m the block must start at $\sqrt{2 \times 0.40 \times 9.8 \times 1.5} = 3.43$ m/s, so $u_\text{min} = 200 \times 3.43 = 686$ m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Now the bullet is faster than $u_\text{min}$, so the block does fall off. It lands on the ground at a horizontal distance $x_f$ from the foot of the drop (the point straight below the edge). Find $x_f$ in terms of some or all of $m_1$, $m_2$, $\mu_k$, $u$, $d$, $h$ and $g$.

<Hint>

There are now three stages: the collision, the slide and the fall. You have the speed at the edge from part (a), now for any $u$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The block leaves the edge moving horizontally. How long does it take to fall the height $h$, starting with no vertical speed? What is its horizontal motion during that time?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$x_f =$" expr="sqrt((m1*u/(m1 + m2))^2 - 2*mu*g*d)*sqrt(2*h/g)"
  vars={{ m1: [0.02, 0.05], m2: [1, 2], u: [600, 900], mu: [0.1, 0.3], g: [9, 10], d: [0.5, 1.5], h: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2", mu: "\\mu_k" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m1*u/(m1 + m2)*sqrt(2*h/g)", hint: "This lets the block leave the edge with the speed it has just after the collision. Friction slows it down on the way to the edge." },
    { expr: "sqrt(u^2 - 2*mu*g*d)*sqrt(2*h/g)", hint: "The block does not start its slide with the bullet's speed u. Find the common speed of bullet and block after the collision first." },
    { expr: "(m1*u/(m1 + m2) - sqrt(2*mu*g*d))*sqrt(2*h/g)", hint: "At constant deceleration over a given distance it is the squares of the speeds that differ by 2 mu g d, not the speeds themselves." },
    { expr: "sqrt((m1*u/(m1 + m2))^2 - 2*mu*g*d)*sqrt(2*h/g) + d", hint: "The distance is measured from the foot of the drop, not from where the block started." },
    { expr: "sqrt((m1*u/(m1 + m2))^2 - 2*mu*g*d)*sqrt(h/g)", hint: "A fall from rest through the height h takes the time t with h = g t^2/2. Check the factor 2 in your time of fall." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The first two stages are those of part (a), now with speed left over at the edge. From the edge on, the block is a projectile that starts horizontally: its horizontal speed stays the same while it falls the height $h$.

<Step title="The speed at the edge">

From part (a), with $V = m_1 u/(m_1 + m_2)$:

<Formula label="Speed at the edge for any bullet speed" tex="v_e = \sqrt{\left(\frac{m_1 u}{m_1 + m_2}\right)^2 - 2\,\mu_k g\,d}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

The root is real because $u > u_\text{min}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The fall">

After the edge only gravity acts. Vertically the block starts with no speed and falls with the acceleration $g$, so $h = \tfrac12 g\,t_f^2$ and the fall takes the time $t_f = \sqrt{2h/g}$. Horizontally nothing acts, so the block keeps the speed $v_e$.

</Step>

<Step title="The landing point">

In the time $t_f$ the block moves the horizontal distance $v_e t_f$:

<Formula label="Where the block lands" tex="x_f = v_e\,t_f = \sqrt{\left(\frac{m_1 u}{m_1 + m_2}\right)^2 - 2\,\mu_k g\,d}\;\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A speed times a time is a length. For $u = u_\text{min}$ the first root is zero: the block tips over the edge and lands at the foot of the drop. A higher ledge gives a longer fall and a landing further out. Without friction the block would leave with the full speed $V$.

A number (ours): with the bullet and block of part (a) and $u = 900$ m/s, the speed after the collision is $900/200 = 4.5$ m/s. At the edge $v_e = \sqrt{4.5^2 - 11.76} = 2.91$ m/s. A drop of $h = 0.80$ m takes $\sqrt{2 \times 0.80/9.8} = 0.404$ s, so $x_f = 2.91 \times 0.404 = 1.18$ m.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Acrobat and clown" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

An acrobat of mass $m_A$ jumps straight up from a trampoline, which is at ground level, with the starting speed $v_0$. A clown of mass $m_B$ stands at rest on the edge of a platform at the height $h_0$ above the ground. On the way up, at the height $h_0$, the acrobat grabs the clown and the two go on upwards together. The grab takes so little time that you may treat it as instantaneous.

Find $h_f$, the greatest height above the ground that the acrobat and the clown reach, in terms of some or all of $m_A$, $m_B$, $g$, $h_0$ and $v_0$. (The acrobat is fast enough to arrive at $h_0$ still moving up: $v_0^2 > 2 g h_0$. This remark is ours.)

<Hint>

There are three stages: the acrobat rises alone, the grab, and the two rise together. Treat each on its own.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The grab is a collision in which two bodies stick together. It is over so quickly that gravity gives no impulse during it. What stays the same?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Before and after the grab the acceleration is $-g$. The relation between speed and distance gives the acrobat's speed at $h_0$, and later the extra height gained from the speed just after the grab.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-h" label="$h_f =$" expr="h0 + (mA/(mA + mB))^2*(v0^2 - 2*g*h0)/(2*g)"
  vars={{ mA: [50, 80], mB: [40, 70], g: [9, 10], h0: [1, 3], v0: [10, 15] }}
  names={{ mA: "m_A", mB: "m_B", h0: "h_0", v0: "v_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v0^2/(2*g)", hint: "That is the height the acrobat would reach alone. Grabbing the clown, who was at rest, slows the acrobat down." },
    { expr: "h0 + mA/(mA + mB)*(v0^2 - 2*g*h0)/(2*g)", hint: "The grab cuts the speed by the factor mA/(mA + mB). The height gained after it goes with the square of the speed." },
    { expr: "(mA/(mA + mB))^2*v0^2/(2*g)", hint: "This is the result for a clown who is grabbed at ground level. The acrobat first rises alone to the height h0 and only then shares the momentum." },
    { expr: "h0 + (mA/(mA + mB))^2*v0^2/(2*g)", hint: "At the height h0 the acrobat is slower than v0: gravity has acted during the climb." },
    { expr: "h0 + (mA/(mA + mB))^2*(v0^2 - 2*g*h0)/g", hint: "A body that starts upwards with the speed v rises by v^2/(2 g). Check the factor 2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Gravity alone acts before and after the grab, so there the acceleration is constant. The grab itself is a collision that makes two bodies one: momentum is conserved across it, because gravity has no time to act.

<Step title="The acrobat rises alone">

Take up as positive. From the trampoline to the height $h_0$ the acceleration is $-g$, so the speed $v_1$ of the acrobat just before the grab follows from the relation between speed and distance:

<Formula label="Speed of the acrobat at the platform" tex="v_1^2 = v_0^2 - 2\,g\,h_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The grab">

Just before the grab the clown is at rest, so the momentum of the pair is $m_A v_1$. Just after it both move up with the same speed $v_2$:

<Formula label="Speed just after the grab" tex="m_A v_1 = (m_A + m_B)\,v_2, \qquad v_2 = \frac{m_A}{m_A + m_B}\,v_1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The pair rises together">

From the height $h_0$ the pair starts with the speed $v_2$ and again has the acceleration $-g$. At the top the speed is zero, so $0 = v_2^2 - 2g\,(h_f - h_0)$:

<Formula label="Greatest height of acrobat and clown" tex="h_f = h_0 + \frac{v_2^2}{2g} = h_0 + \left(\frac{m_A}{m_A + m_B}\right)^2 \frac{v_0^2 - 2\,g\,h_0}{2g}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A speed squared divided by an acceleration is a length. A clown with no mass changes nothing: then $h_f = h_0 + v_0^2/(2g) - h_0 = v_0^2/(2g)$, the height the acrobat reaches alone. A very heavy clown stops the acrobat at once and $h_f = h_0$. If the acrobat only just reaches the platform ($v_0^2 = 2gh_0$), there is no speed left to share and again $h_f = h_0$.

A number (ours): $m_A = 60$ kg, $m_B = 40$ kg, $v_0 = 10$ m/s and $h_0 = 3.0$ m. Alone the acrobat would reach $10^2/19.6 = 5.10$ m. At the platform $v_1^2 = 100 - 58.8 = 41.2\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so $v_1 = 6.42$ m/s. The grab leaves $v_2 = 0.60 \times 6.42 = 3.85$ m/s, which lifts the pair by another $0.36 \times 41.2/19.6 = 0.757$ m, to $h_f = 3.76$ m.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="How much force a leg bone can take" points={25} mit="Problem 3">

The shin bone (tibia) of the lower leg breaks when it is squeezed along its length with a force per unit of area of about $1.6 \times 10^8\ \text{N/m}^2$. The bone is thinnest just above the ankle, where its cross-section has an area of about $3.2\ \text{cm}^2$.

A person of mass $m = 60$ kg jumps down from a height $h_0 = 2.0$ m and lands on the ground. The knees bend only a little: from the moment the feet touch the ground until the person stands still, the centre of mass sinks by just $d = 1.0$ cm. Take the deceleration during this landing as constant.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

How long does the landing take? Give this collision time $\Delta t_\text{col}$ in milliseconds, to two significant figures.

<Hint>

First find the speed with which the person reaches the ground after falling the height $h_0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

During the landing the speed drops from that value to zero at a constant rate. What is the average speed during the landing? The distance covered at that average speed is $d$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$\Delta t_\text{col} =$" value="3.2" unit="ms" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-tcol" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The fall gives the speed at the ground. During the landing the speed falls evenly to zero, so the average speed is half the landing speed, and that average speed carries the centre of mass down by $d$.

<Step title="The speed at the ground">

The person falls from rest through $h_0$ with the acceleration $g$. The relation between speed and distance gives $v^2 = 2 g h_0 = 2 \times 9.8 \times 2.0 = 39.2\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so $v = 6.26$ m/s.

</Step>

<Step title="The collision time">

At constant deceleration from $v$ to zero the average speed is $v/2$. In the time $\Delta t_\text{col}$ the centre of mass sinks by $d = \tfrac12 v\,\Delta t_\text{col}$:

<Formula label="Collision time at constant deceleration" tex="\Delta t_\text{col} = \frac{2d}{v} = \frac{2 \times 0.010}{6.26}\ \text{s} = 3.2 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

That is 3.2 ms.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The deceleration is $v/\Delta t_\text{col} = v^2/(2d) = 39.2/0.020 = 1960\ \text{m/s}^2$, which is 200 times $g$. That ratio is $h_0/d$: the speed gained over 2.0 m of falling is lost again over 1.0 cm, a distance 200 times shorter.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

Find $N_\text{ave}$, the magnitude of the average force of the ground on the person during the landing, in newtons.

<Hint>

Two forces act on the person during the landing: the force of the ground, upwards, and gravity, downwards. Together they give the impulse that takes the momentum to zero.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the average total force times the collision time, and set it equal to the change of momentum. Mind the signs: the momentum is downwards before the landing and zero after it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$N_\text{ave} =$" value="1.18e5" unit="N" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-nave" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The impulse of all forces on the person equals the change of momentum. The average force of the ground follows from the momentum that must be removed and the time in which this happens.

<Step title="The momentum principle">

Take up as positive. Just before the landing the momentum is $-mv$, and at the end it is zero, so the change of momentum is $+mv$. The total force is $N - mg$, with average $N_\text{ave} - mg$ because the weight is constant:

<Formula label="Average force of the ground" tex="(N_\text{ave} - m g)\,\Delta t_\text{col} = m v, \qquad N_\text{ave} = \frac{m v}{\Delta t_\text{col}} + m g" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

The first term is $60 \times 6.261/(3.194 \times 10^{-3}) = 1.176 \times 10^5$ N and the weight is $60 \times 9.8 = 588$ N. Together:

<Formula label="Average force of the ground: value" tex="N_\text{ave} = 1.176 \times 10^5\ \text{N} + 588\ \text{N} = 1.18 \times 10^5\ \text{N}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Newton's second law gives the same. With the constant deceleration of $1960\ \text{m/s}^2$ found in part (a), $N_\text{ave} - mg = ma$, so $N_\text{ave} = 60 \times (1960 + 9.8) = 1.18 \times 10^5$ N. In symbols both routes give $N_\text{ave} = mg\,(1 + h_0/d)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

What is the ratio of this average force of the ground to the gravitational force on the person? Decide also whether gravity can be left out during the collision, then compare with the solution. This box accepts any value within 1% of ours.

<Hint>

The gravitational force on the person is the weight $mg$. Divide your result of part (b) by it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="$N_\text{ave}/(mg) =$" value="201" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compare the two forces that act during the landing.

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Force of the ground compared with the weight" tex="\frac{N_\text{ave}}{m g} = \frac{1.182 \times 10^5}{588} = 201" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

In symbols this is $1 + h_0/d = 1 + 200$.

</Step>

<Step title="Can gravity be left out?">

Yes. During the landing the ground pushes about 200 times harder than gravity pulls. Leaving gravity out changes $N_\text{ave}$ from $1.182 \times 10^5$ N to $1.176 \times 10^5$ N, a change of half a percent, far less than the uncertainty in a number such as "about 1.0 cm". This is the usual situation in a collision: the collision forces are so large that ordinary outside forces give almost no impulse during it. Problems 1 and 2 of this set used the same idea.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

Does the bone break just above the ankle? Assume that the bone at its thinnest place has to pass on the whole force of the ground on that foot (this assumption is ours), and look at a landing on one leg and at a landing on two legs that share the force equally.

<Hint>

From the breaking force per unit of area and the area of the cross-section, find the force at which one tibia breaks. Mind the unit of the area.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Compare that breaking force with the force on one leg: $N_\text{ave}$ for a landing on one leg, half of it for a landing on two.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-d" label="The bone:"
  choices={[
    "does not break, on one leg or on two",
    "breaks only in a landing on one leg",
    "breaks, even when two legs share the force equally"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A force per unit of area times an area is a force. That gives the largest force one tibia can pass on, to compare with the force of the ground on one foot.

<Step title="The breaking force of one tibia">

The area is $3.2\ \text{cm}^2 = 3.2 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$, so one tibia breaks at

<Formula label="Breaking force of one tibia" tex="F_\text{break} = 1.6 \times 10^8\ \text{N/m}^2 \times 3.2 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2 = 5.1 \times 10^4\ \text{N}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compare">

In a landing on one leg the bone has to pass on $1.18 \times 10^5$ N, more than twice the breaking force. In a landing on two legs each bone has to pass on half, $5.9 \times 10^4$ N. That is still above $5.1 \times 10^4$ N: both legs together can take $1.02 \times 10^5$ N at most. So in this model the bone breaks either way.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The other way round: on two legs the force per unit of area in each bone is $5.9 \times 10^4\ \text{N}/(3.2 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2) = 1.8 \times 10^8\ \text{N/m}^2$, above the $1.6 \times 10^8\ \text{N/m}^2$ that the bone can take.

The margin is small, and it shows what bending the knees is for. The force goes as $h_0/d$, so a landing in which the centre of mass sinks by 10 cm instead of 1.0 cm takes ten times longer and needs a force about ten times smaller, far below the breaking force. The average force is also a lower bound for the largest force during a real landing, in which the force is not constant.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Centre of mass of a rod" points={20} mit="Problem 4">

A thin rod of length $L$ and total mass $M$ lies along the $x$ axis, with its left end at the origin. The unit vector $\hat i$ points along the rod, from the left end to the right end. The centre of mass is at $\vec R_\text{cm} = x_\text{cm}\,\hat i$: the boxes ask for $x_\text{cm}$, the distance of the centre of mass from the left end.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

The rod is uniform: every piece of the same length has the same mass. Find $x_\text{cm}$ in terms of some or all of $L$ and $M$.

<Hint>

Cut the rod into small pieces of length $dx$. What is the mass $dm$ of one piece of a uniform rod?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put $dm$ in the integral for the centre of mass of a body, with $x$ running from 0 to $L$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$x_\text{cm} =$" expr="L/2"
  vars={{ L: [1, 3], M: [1, 5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "M*L/2", hint: "A position is a length. The mass of each piece is divided by the total mass, so M drops out." },
    { expr: "L^2/2", hint: "That is the integral of x from 0 to L. The mass of a piece is (M/L) dx, and the integral is divided by M: a factor 1/L is missing." },
    { expr: "L", hint: "That is the right end of the rod. A uniform rod balances at its middle." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a body whose mass is spread out, the sum over particles becomes an integral over small pieces. In a uniform rod each piece of length $dx$ has the same share of the mass.

<Step title="The mass of a piece">

A uniform rod has the mass per unit of length $M/L$, so a piece of length $dx$ at the position $x$ has the mass $dm = (M/L)\,dx$.

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

With $\vec r = x\,\hat i$ in the formula for the centre of mass of a body:

<Formula label="Centre of mass of the uniform rod" tex="\vec R_\text{cm} = \frac{1}{M}\int_0^L x\,\frac{M}{L}\,dx\;\hat i = \frac{1}{L}\,\frac{L^2}{2}\,\hat i = \frac{L}{2}\,\hat i" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

So $x_\text{cm} = L/2$. The lesson on the centre of mass works this out in the same way.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The middle is the only point that a uniform rod can single out: seen from the middle, the left half and the right half are mirror images. The mass $M$ has dropped out, as it must for a position.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={15}>

Now the rod is not uniform. Its mass per unit of length (the linear mass density) grows with the distance $x$ from the left end as

<Formula label="Density of the rod" tex="\lambda(x) = \frac{\lambda_0}{L^3}\,x^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), p. 4" />

where $\lambda_0$ is a constant with the unit kg/m. The total mass is still $M$. Find $\lambda_0$, and then $x_\text{cm}$, each in terms of some or all of $L$ and $M$.

<Hint>

A piece of length $dx$ at the position $x$ now has the mass $dm = \lambda(x)\,dx$. The masses of all pieces must add up to $M$. That fixes $\lambda_0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the centre of mass, integrate $x\,dm$ over the rod and divide by $M$. Then put in your $\lambda_0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b-lambda" label="$\lambda_0 =$" expr="4*M/L"
  vars={{ L: [1, 3], M: [1, 5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "M/L", hint: "That is the density of a uniform rod. Here lambda0 is the density at the right end, and everywhere else the rod is lighter, so lambda0 must be larger than M/L." },
    { expr: "3*M/L", hint: "That is the result for a density that grows as x^2. Here it grows as x^3." },
    { expr: "4*M/L^4", hint: "Check the unit: lambda0 is a mass per length. The integral of x^3 from 0 to L gives L^4/4, and the L^3 of the density divides it." },
    { expr: "M/(4*L)", hint: "The integral of x^3/L^3 from 0 to L is L/4, and it multiplies lambda0. Solve lambda0 L/4 = M for lambda0." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-b-x" label="$x_\text{cm} =$" expr="4*L/5"
  vars={{ L: [1, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "L/2", hint: "That is the centre of mass of the uniform rod. This rod is heavier towards its right end." },
    { expr: "3*L/4", hint: "That is the result for a density that grows as x^2. Here it grows as x^3." },
    { expr: "L/5", hint: "This follows with lambda0 = M/L. Use the lambda0 you found. A rod that is heavier on the right must also have its centre of mass to the right of the middle." },
    { expr: "5*L/4", hint: "The centre of mass of a rod cannot lie beyond its end. Have the two integrals changed places?" }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same integral as in part (a), but now each piece has its own mass $\lambda(x)\,dx$. The total mass fixes the constant $\lambda_0$.

<Step title="The constant from the total mass">

Add up the masses of all pieces:

<Formula label="Total mass of the rod" tex="M = \int_0^L \frac{\lambda_0}{L^3}\,x^3\,dx = \frac{\lambda_0}{L^3}\,\frac{L^4}{4} = \frac{\lambda_0 L}{4}, \qquad \lambda_0 = \frac{4M}{L}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The centre of mass">

<Formula label="Centre of mass of the rod that grows heavier" tex="\vec R_\text{cm} = \frac{1}{M}\int_0^L x\,\frac{\lambda_0}{L^3}\,x^3\,dx\;\hat i = \frac{\lambda_0}{M L^3}\,\frac{L^5}{5}\,\hat i = \frac{\lambda_0 L^2}{5M}\,\hat i = \frac{4L}{5}\,\hat i" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b) (our own solution)" />

So $x_\text{cm} = 4L/5$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $x = L$ the density is $\lambda_0$, so $\lambda_0$ is the density at the right end. It is four times the average density $M/L$, which fits a rod that weighs almost nothing near its left end. The centre of mass lies to the right of the middle and inside the rod, as it must.

MIT's course notes work out a rod whose density grows as $x^2$ (Example 10.3 of Chapter 10). There the same two integrals give $\lambda_0 = 3M/L$ and $x_\text{cm} = 3L/4$. The pattern: for a density that grows as $x^n$, $\lambda_0 = (n+1)\,M/L$ and $x_\text{cm} = (n+1)\,L/(n+2)$ (this remark is ours). The faster the density grows, the closer the centre of mass moves to the right end.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Two particles that collide" points={20} mit="Problem 5">

Two small particles, of mass $m_1$ and of mass $m_2$, attract each other with a force that falls off as one over the cube of the distance between them. No other forces act. At the time $t_0$ the particles are a distance $d$ apart. Particle 2 is then at rest, and particle 1 moves straight towards it with a velocity of magnitude $v_0$. At the later time $t_1$ the two particles collide.

Find $L$, the distance that particle 1 travels between $t_0$ and $t_1$, in terms of some or all of $m_1$, $m_2$, $t_0$, $t_1$, $v_0$ and $d$.

<Hint>

The force changes all the time, and you are not even told how strong it is. Look for something about the two particles together that this force cannot change.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The attraction is a pair of inside forces of the system of two particles. No force acts from outside. How does the centre of mass of the system move?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Where is the centre of mass at $t_0$, measured from particle 1? Where is it at $t_1$, when the two small particles are at the same place?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-dist" label="$L =$" expr="(m2*d + m1*v0*(t1 - t0))/(m1 + m2)"
  vars={{ m1: [1, 2], m2: [3, 5], d: [10, 20], v0: [1, 2], t0: [0, 1], t1: [2, 3] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2", v0: "v_0", t0: "t_0", t1: "t_1" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v0*(t1 - t0)", hint: "That is the distance at the constant speed v0. The attraction speeds particle 1 up on the way." },
    { expr: "d", hint: "That would be right if particle 2 stayed where it is. The attraction pulls particle 2 towards particle 1, so they meet somewhere in between." },
    { expr: "m1*v0*(t1 - t0)/(m1 + m2)", hint: "That is how far the centre of mass moves. Particle 1 does not start at the centre of mass: add the distance between them at the time t0." },
    { expr: "m2*d/(m1 + m2)", hint: "That is the distance from particle 1 to the centre of mass at the time t0. The centre of mass does not stay there: the system has momentum." },
    { expr: "(m1*d + m2*v0*(t1 - t0))/(m1 + m2)", hint: "Check a limit: a very heavy particle 2 hardly moves, so particle 1 must then travel the whole distance d. Your result does not give that. Have the two masses changed places?" }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The attraction acts inside the system of the two particles, and nothing acts from outside. So the centre of mass moves at a constant velocity, whatever the force between the particles does. When the particles collide they are both at the centre of mass. Particle 1 therefore travels from its starting point to the place where the centre of mass is at $t_1$.

<Step title="The velocity of the centre of mass">

Take the $x$ axis along the line from particle 1 to particle 2, with particle 1 at the origin at the time $t_0$. Then the momentum of the system is $m_1 v_0$, and it stays that. With $\vec p_\text{sys} = m_\text{sys}\vec V_\text{cm}$, the centre of mass moves in the $+x$ direction with the constant speed

<Formula label="Speed of the centre of mass of the two particles" tex="V_\text{cm} = \frac{m_1 v_0}{m_1 + m_2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Where the centre of mass is">

At $t_0$ particle 1 is at $x = 0$ and particle 2 at $x = d$, so the centre of mass is at $m_2 d/(m_1 + m_2)$. It moves at constant speed, so at the time $t_1$ it is at

<Formula label="Centre of mass at the time of the collision" tex="X_\text{cm}(t_1) = \frac{m_2\,d}{m_1 + m_2} + \frac{m_1 v_0}{m_1 + m_2}\,(t_1 - t_0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The distance travelled">

The particles are small, so at the collision both are at one point, and the centre of mass of two particles at one point is that point. Particle 1 is pulled forwards all the time, so it never turns back, and the distance it travels equals its change of position:

<Formula label="Distance travelled by particle 1" tex="L = X_\text{cm}(t_1) - 0 = \frac{m_2\,d + m_1 v_0\,(t_1 - t_0)}{m_1 + m_2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both terms are lengths. If particle 2 is very heavy it hardly moves, and $L$ goes to $d$: particle 1 crosses the whole gap. If particle 1 is very heavy it is hardly speeded up, and $L$ goes to $v_0\,(t_1 - t_0)$: it coasts while particle 2 falls towards it.

The inverse-cube law was never used. The result holds for any force between the two particles, as long as nothing pushes from outside. The force law does set the time $t_1$ of the collision, which the problem gives.

A number (ours): $m_1 = 1.0$ kg, $m_2 = 3.0$ kg, $d = 12$ m, $v_0 = 2.0$ m/s and $t_1 - t_0 = 4.0$ s. The centre of mass starts $3.0 \times 12/4.0 = 9.0$ m ahead of particle 1 and moves at $1.0 \times 2.0/4.0 = 0.50$ m/s, so it covers 2.0 m. Particle 1 travels $L = 9.0 + 2.0 = 11$ m and particle 2 comes the remaining 1.0 m to meet it. Without the attraction particle 1 would cover only $2.0 \times 4.0 = 8.0$ m in this time.

</Step>

</Solution>
