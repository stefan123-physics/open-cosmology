This set practises the lessons on [potential energy](/courses/8-01/potential-energy), on [energy conservation](/courses/8-01/energy-conservation) and on [potential energy diagrams](/courses/8-01/potential-energy-diagrams). The five problems follow MIT's Problem Set 8, in our own words. MIT's set comes with drawings; here each situation is described in words, so make your own sketch first. MIT publishes no solutions for this set, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. One remark: MIT's course notes work the block of Problem 1 as Example 14.3. Try the problem before you look there. Problems 3 and 4 also contain a collision, for which you need the lesson on [conservation of momentum](/courses/8-01/conservation-of-momentum). All five problems count for the score (60 points).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first: sketch the situation, choose the initial and the final state, choose the zero point of each potential energy, and write down the kinetic and the potential energy in both states. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A formula box takes plain typing, for example `sqrt(7*m*g*R/k)`, `m*v0^2/l` or `exp(-x^2/a^2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type the names as the box shows them: `v0` for $v_0$, `U0` for $U_0$, `ME` for $M_E$ and `RE` for $R_E$. For a power of the number $e$ type `exp( )`, for example `exp(-1)` for $1/e$.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. A part without an answer box (the sketch of Problem 5) is marked by yourself after you open its solution. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A spring, a block and a loop" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A small block of mass $m$ is pressed against a spring of spring constant $k$ on a horizontal track, and held there by a catch. The spring is compressed by an unknown distance $x$. When the catch is removed, the spring pushes the block away. The block leaves the spring, slides along the track and then goes around the inside of a circular loop of radius $R$ that stands upright on the track. There is no friction anywhere.

At the top of the loop, the track pushes on the block with a normal force that is twice as large as the gravitational force on the block.

MIT asks only for the compression. Part (a) is a step on the way, which we have made a part of its own.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the speed $v_\text{top}$ of the block at the top of the loop, using any of $g$, $k$, $R$ and $m$ that you need.

<Hint>

Energy alone cannot give this speed, because the compression is not known yet. Which other law holds for a body on a circle?

</Hint>

<Hint>

At the top of the loop the block is on the inside of the track, below it. Draw the forces on the block there: in which direction does each one point, and where is the centre of the circle?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write Newton's second law along the radius, with the centripetal acceleration $v_\text{top}^2/R$ towards the centre.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$v_\text{top} =$" expr="sqrt(3*g*R)"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], R: [0.2, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2*g*R)", hint: "This counts the normal force only. At the top of the loop gravity points towards the centre too, so both forces together make the centripetal force." },
    { expr: "sqrt(g*R)", hint: "This takes the normal force and gravity to point in opposite directions at the top, or the normal force to be zero. The block is under the track there: the track pushes it down, the same way as gravity, with 2mg." },
    { expr: "3*g*R", hint: "That has the unit of a speed squared. Take the square root." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For circular motion, energy is not the whole story: the lesson on energy conservation uses the radial part of Newton's second law next to it. The given normal force fixes the speed at the top.

<Step title="Forces at the top">

At the top the block is on the inside of the loop, so the track is above it and pushes it down. Gravity pulls it down as well. The centre of the circle is below the block, so both forces point towards the centre. With the radial direction pointing outwards, as in the notes:

<Formula label="Loop: radial second law at the top" tex="-mg - N = -\frac{m\,v_\text{top}^2}{R}" cite="reading1" at="Example 14.3, Eq. (14.12), p. 14-26" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put in the normal force">

With $N = 2mg$ the left side is $-3mg$, so $v_\text{top}^2 = 3gR$:

<Formula label="Loop: speed at the top" tex="v_\text{top} = \sqrt{3gR}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$gR$ is an acceleration times a length, m²/s², so its root is a speed. The mass has dropped out, as it does whenever gravity sets the scale of every force. A block that only just stays on the track has $N = 0$ and $v_\text{top}^2 = gR$. Here the track still has to push, so the block must be faster than that, and it is. With our own numbers, $R = 0.25$ m and $g = 9.8$ m/s²: $v_\text{top} = \sqrt{3 \times 9.8 \times 0.25} = 2.71$ m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

By what distance $x$ was the spring compressed at the start? Use any of $g$, $k$, $R$ and $m$ that you need.

<Hint>

No friction acts, and the normal force is always at right angles to the motion. So the mechanical energy at the start equals the mechanical energy at the top of the loop.

</Hint>

<Hint>

At the start the block is at rest and all the energy is in the spring. At the top the block has left the spring: it has kinetic energy, and it is higher than at the start. How much higher?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the speed of part (a) in the kinetic energy at the top.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$x =$" expr="sqrt(7*m*g*R/k)"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], R: [0.2, 2], m: [0.1, 2], k: [100, 500] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(3*m*g*R/k)", hint: "This gives the block its kinetic energy at the top, but no energy for the climb. The top of the loop is 2R above the track." },
    { expr: "sqrt(5*m*g*R/k)", hint: "This is the compression for a block that only just stays on the track at the top, with no normal force there. Here the track pushes with 2mg, so the block is faster." },
    { expr: "sqrt(6*m*g*R/k)", hint: "This follows from a speed at the top with v² = 2gR, which counts the normal force only. Gravity points towards the centre at the top as well." },
    { expr: "7*m*g*R/k", hint: "That has the unit of a length squared. The spring energy has x squared in it: take the square root." },
    { expr: "sqrt(7*m*g*R/(2*k))", hint: "Check the factors of one half: the spring energy and the kinetic energy both carry one, and the height energy 2mgR does not." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The spring force and gravity are conservative, and the normal force does no work. So the mechanical energy is the same in the initial state (spring compressed, block at rest) and in the final state (block at the top of the loop).

<Step title="The two states">

Take the zero of the gravitational potential energy at the level of the track, and the zero of the spring's potential energy at its relaxed length. The notes give the potential energy of a spring:

<Formula label="Potential energy of a spring" tex="U(x) = \tfrac12 k x^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (14.4), p. 14-16" />

Initial state: kinetic energy zero, spring energy $\tfrac12 kx^2$, height zero. Final state: kinetic energy $\tfrac12 m v_\text{top}^2$, spring relaxed, height $2R$.

</Step>

<Step title="Energy is conserved">

<Formula label="Loop: energy at the start and at the top" tex="\tfrac12 k x^2 = \tfrac12 m\,v_\text{top}^2 + 2mgR" cite="reading1" at="Example 14.3, Eq. (14.10), p. 14-26" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

With $v_\text{top}^2 = 3gR$ from part (a), the right side is $\tfrac32 mgR + 2mgR = \tfrac72 mgR$, so

<Formula label="Loop: compression of the spring" tex="x = \sqrt{\frac{7\,m g R}{k}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$mgR$ is an energy and $k$ is an energy per length squared, so the ratio is a length squared. A stiffer spring needs less compression, and a heavier block or a larger loop needs more. With our own numbers, $m = 0.20$ kg, $k = 350$ N/m, $R = 0.25$ m and $g = 9.8$ m/s²: $x = \sqrt{7 \times 0.20 \times 9.8 \times 0.25/350} = 0.099$ m, about 10 cm. The notes reach the same formula in Example 14.3 (Eq. (14.17), p. 14-27).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A ball on a string, let go at the top" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

A small ball of mass $m$ hangs at rest from a string of length $l$, whose upper end is fixed. The ball is struck sideways, and just after the blow it has the speed $v_0$. It then moves on a vertical circle of radius $l$ around the fixed end. After the blow only gravity does work on the ball. The magnitude of the gravitational acceleration is $g$.

Assume that $v_0$ is large enough for the string to stay taut all the way to the top of the circle. (This condition is our addition. MIT's figure shows the ball passing the top on its circle.)

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the tension $T$ in the string when the ball is at the top of the circle, using any of $m$, $g$, $l$ and $v_0$ that you need.

<Hint>

Two steps: first the speed at the top, from energy. Then the tension, from Newton's second law along the string.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The tension is at right angles to the motion, so it does no work. How much higher is the top of the circle than the bottom?

</Hint>

<Hint>

At the top the ball is above the fixed end. In which direction does the string pull, and in which direction does gravity pull?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$T =$" expr="m*v0^2/l - 5*m*g"
  vars={{ m: [0.1, 2], g: [9, 10], l: [0.5, 1.5], v0: [10, 15] }}
  names={{ v0: "v_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*v0^2/l - 4*m*g", hint: "This is the mass times the centripetal acceleration at the top. That is the sum of the tension and the weight, which both point to the centre there. Take the weight off." },
    { expr: "m*v0^2/l - 3*m*g", hint: "This takes gravity to point away from the centre at the top. At the top the centre is below the ball, so gravity points towards the centre, like the tension." },
    { expr: "m*v0^2/l + m*g", hint: "That is the tension at the bottom, just after the blow. At the top the ball is slower, and gravity helps to hold it on the circle." },
    { expr: "m*v0^2/l - m*g", hint: "This uses the speed v0 at the top. The ball climbs by 2l on the way up and loses speed." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The tension does no work, because the string is always at right angles to the velocity. So mechanical energy is conserved between the bottom and the top, which gives the speed at the top. The tension then follows from the radial part of the second law.

<Step title="The speed at the top">

Take the zero of the gravitational potential energy at the bottom of the circle. The top is higher by the diameter, $2l$. The notes write the conservation of mechanical energy as

<Formula label="Conservation of mechanical energy" tex="K_f + U_f = K_i + U_i" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (14.25), p. 14-15" />

Here that is $\tfrac12 m v_\text{top}^2 + 2mgl = \tfrac12 m v_0^2$, so

<Formula label="String: speed at the top" tex="v_\text{top}^2 = v_0^2 - 4gl" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The second law at the top">

At the top the fixed end is straight below the ball. The string pulls the ball down, and gravity pulls it down. Both point to the centre, and together they give the centripetal acceleration:

<Formula label="String: radial second law at the top" tex="T + mg = \frac{m\,v_\text{top}^2}{l}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

<Formula label="String: tension at the top" tex="T = \frac{m\,(v_0^2 - 4gl)}{l} - mg = \frac{m\,v_0^2}{l} - 5mg" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both terms are forces. A string can only pull, so the result makes sense only for $T \ge 0$, that is for $v_0^2 \ge 5gl$. This is the condition "large enough" of the statement: a slower ball leaves its circle before the top. With our own numbers, $m = 0.15$ kg, $l = 0.80$ m, $v_0 = 7.0$ m/s and $g = 9.8$ m/s²: the smallest allowed speed is $\sqrt{5 \times 9.8 \times 0.80} = 6.26$ m/s, so 7.0 m/s is enough. Then $v_\text{top}^2 = 49 - 31.36 = 17.64$ m²/s², $v_\text{top} = 4.2$ m/s, and $T = 0.15 \times 17.64/0.80 - 0.15 \times 9.8 = 1.84$ N.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

At the moment the ball is exactly at the top of the circle, it comes loose from the string. It then flies freely and comes down to the level of the bottom of the circle. There it is a horizontal distance $d$ from the lowest point of the circle. Find $d$, using any of $m$, $g$, $l$ and $v_0$ that you need.

<Hint>

At the top of the circle the velocity is horizontal. From there on the ball is a projectile, as in the lesson on motion in two dimensions.

</Hint>

<Hint>

How far does the ball have to fall, and how long does that take if it starts with no vertical velocity?

</Hint>

<Hint>

During the fall the horizontal velocity stays what it was at the top. You found that speed in part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$d =$" expr="2*sqrt(l*(v0^2 - 4*g*l)/g)"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], l: [0.5, 1.5], v0: [10, 15] }}
  names={{ v0: "v_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2*l*(v0^2 - 4*g*l)/g)", hint: "This is the distance for a fall through the height l. The top of the circle is 2l above the bottom." },
    { expr: "2*v0*sqrt(l/g)", hint: "This uses the speed v0 for the flight. At the top the ball is slower than at the bottom: use the speed at the top." },
    { expr: "2*sqrt(l*(v0^2 - 2*g*l)/g)", hint: "This takes the climb from the bottom to the top as l. The ball climbs the whole diameter, 2l." },
    { expr: "sqrt(v0^2 - 4*g*l)", hint: "That is the speed at the top, not a distance. Multiply it by the time of the fall." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A ball that leaves the string at the top is a projectile thrown horizontally from the height $2l$ with the speed $v_\text{top}$. The fall fixes the time, and the horizontal speed times that time is the distance. (See [motion in two dimensions](/courses/8-01/kinematics-2d).)

<Step title="The time of the fall">

The ball starts with no vertical velocity and falls through $2l$, so $2l = \tfrac12 g t^2$:

<Formula label="String: time of the fall" tex="t = \sqrt{\frac{4l}{g}} = 2\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The distance">

The top of the circle is straight above its lowest point, so $d$ is the horizontal distance covered during the fall, $d = v_\text{top}\,t$:

<Formula label="String: landing distance" tex="d = 2\sqrt{\frac{l\,(v_0^2 - 4gl)}{g}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

The mass does not appear.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Under the root, a length times a speed squared is divided by an acceleration, which gives a length squared. Since $v_0^2 \ge 5gl$, the smallest possible distance is $2\sqrt{l \times gl/g} = 2l$: even the slowest ball that completes the half circle lands a full diameter away. With the numbers of part (a): $t = 2\sqrt{0.80/9.8} = 0.571$ s and $d = 4.2 \times 0.571 = 2.40$ m, three times the length of the string.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Two beads on a ring" points={14} mit="Problem 3">

A circular ring of radius $R$ stands in a vertical plane. Two objects (think of beads) slide on it without friction.

The heavier object has the mass $3m$. It is tied to a spring of spring constant $k$ whose unstretched length is zero (MIT admits that this is not a physical spring: it means that the spring force is $k$ times the full length of the spring). The other end of the spring is fixed at a point $P$ at the same height as the centre of the ring, a horizontal distance $2R$ to the left of the centre. So $P$ is outside the ring.

The lighter object has the mass $m$ and rests at the bottom of the ring. The heavier object is let go from rest at the top of the ring. It slides down, hits the lighter object and sticks to it. The combined object, of mass $4m$, still tied to the spring, moves on. The point $A$ is the point of the ring at the same height as the centre on the right, the side away from $P$.

The value of $m$ is to be chosen so that the combined object just reaches $A$ and goes no higher. MIT asks only for this value. Parts (a) and (b) are steps on the way, which we have made parts of their own.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the speed $v$ of the heavier object at the bottom of the ring, just before the collision. Use any of $k$, $R$, $g$ and $m$ that you need.

<Hint>

Two conservative forces act on the heavier object: gravity and the spring. The ring pushes at right angles to the motion and does no work.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The energy of this spring is $\tfrac12 k$ times the square of its full length. How long is the spring when the object is at the top of the ring, and how long when it is at the bottom? Use the right-angled triangles with one corner at $P$ and one at the centre of the ring.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$v =$" expr="2*sqrt(g*R)"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], R: [0.2, 2], m: [0.1, 2], k: [1, 20] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2*g*R)", hint: "This is the speed after a fall through the height R. From the top of the ring to the bottom the object drops by the diameter, 2R." },
    { expr: "sqrt(4*g*R + 5*k*R^2/(3*m))", hint: "This lets the spring give up all of its energy on the way down. The spring is just as long at the bottom as at the top, so its energy is the same in both places." },
    { expr: "4*g*R", hint: "That has the unit of a speed squared. Take the square root." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Gravity and the spring are conservative, and the normal force of the ring does no work, so the mechanical energy of the heavier object is conserved from the top to the bottom. The spring turns out to play no part in this stretch.

<Step title="The length of the spring">

Put the origin at the centre of the ring, with $x$ to the right and $y$ up. Then $P$ is at $(-2R, 0)$, the top of the ring at $(0, R)$ and the bottom at $(0, -R)$. By Pythagoras the spring has the same length in both places:

<Formula label="Ring: spring length at the top and at the bottom" tex="d_\text{top}^2 = d_\text{bottom}^2 = (2R)^2 + R^2 = 5R^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

With an unstretched length of zero, the stretch of the spring is its full length $d$, so its potential energy is $\tfrac12 k d^2$ (the spring formula of Problem 1 with $x = d$). It is $\tfrac52 kR^2$ at the top and at the bottom.

</Step>

<Step title="Energy from the top to the bottom">

The object starts at rest and drops by $2R$. The spring energy is the same at both ends and cancels:

<Formula label="Ring: energy of the heavier object" tex="3m\,g\,(2R) = \tfrac12\,(3m)\,v^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{4gR} = 2\sqrt{gR}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is the speed of free fall through the height $2R$, as it must be when only gravity does net work. The spring is not idle on the way: it pulls the object to the left at the start, which is why the object slides down the left half of the ring and arrives at the bottom moving to the right, towards $A$. With our own numbers, $R = 0.50$ m and $g = 9.8$ m/s²: $v = \sqrt{4 \times 9.8 \times 0.50} = 4.43$ m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find the speed $V$ of the combined object just after the collision. Use any of $k$, $R$, $g$ and $m$ that you need.

<Hint>

The two objects stick together. Is mechanical energy conserved in such a collision? Which quantity is?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The collision is over in a moment, so the spring and gravity have no time to change the momentum. Compare the momentum just before and just after.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$V =$" expr="3*sqrt(g*R)/2"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], R: [0.2, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(3*g*R)", hint: "This keeps the kinetic energy through the collision. Objects that stick together lose kinetic energy: use the momentum." },
    { expr: "2*sqrt(g*R)", hint: "That is the speed before the collision. The moving mass grows from 3m to 4m, so the speed must drop." },
    { expr: "sqrt(g*R)/2", hint: "This lets the lighter object carry the momentum in. The object that moves before the collision is the heavy one, of mass 3m." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A collision in which the bodies stick together does not conserve mechanical energy. During the very short collision the outside forces (spring, gravity, ring) give almost no impulse along the direction of motion, so the momentum along the ring is the same just before and just after.

<Step title="Momentum before and after">

At the bottom of the ring the velocity is horizontal. Before the collision only the heavier object moves:

<Formula label="Ring: momentum through the collision" tex="3m\,v = 4m\,V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V = \tfrac34\,v = \tfrac32\sqrt{gR}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The kinetic energy before is $\tfrac12 (3m)(4gR) = 6mgR$, and after it is $\tfrac12 (4m)\,\tfrac94 gR = \tfrac92 mgR$. One quarter of it is lost in the collision, as heat and deformation. With the numbers of part (a): $V = 0.75 \times 4.43 = 3.32$ m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={7}>

Find the mass $m$ for which the combined object just reaches the point $A$, and goes no higher. Use any of $k$, $R$ and $g$ that you need.

<Hint>

After the collision the mechanical energy is conserved again. "Just reaches $A$" tells you the kinetic energy at $A$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Two potential energies change between the bottom and $A$. How much higher is $A$ than the bottom? And how long is the spring when the object is at $A$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The change of the spring energy is $\tfrac12 k$ times the difference of the squares of the two lengths.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="$m =$" expr="4*k*R/g"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], R: [0.2, 2], k: [1, 20] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "k*R/g", hint: "This keeps the mechanical energy through the collision. The objects stick together, so kinetic energy is lost there: use momentum for the collision, and energy before and after it." },
    { expr: "(3 - sqrt(5))^2*k*R/g", hint: "The energy of a spring goes with the square of its length. Its change is half k times the difference of the squares of the two lengths, not the square of the difference of the lengths." },
    { expr: "4*k*R", hint: "That is a force, not a mass. Divide by g." },
    { expr: "g/(4*k*R)", hint: "This is one over a mass. A stiffer spring holds the object back more, so it takes a larger mass to reach A." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** From just after the collision to the point $A$, only gravity and the spring do work, so the mechanical energy of the combined object is conserved. On the way to $A$ the object climbs and the spring gets longer, so both potential energies grow, and the kinetic energy has to pay for both.

<Step title="The two states">

Initial state: the combined object at the bottom, with the speed $V$ of part (b). Its kinetic energy is $\tfrac12 (4m) V^2 = \tfrac92 mgR$. Final state: at rest at $A$, which is at $(R, 0)$. The object has climbed by $R$. The spring now runs from $(-2R, 0)$ to $(R, 0)$, so its length is $3R$:

<Formula label="Ring: change of the spring energy" tex="\tfrac12 k\,(3R)^2 - \tfrac12 k\,(5R^2) = 2kR^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Energy is conserved">

<Formula label="Ring: energy from the bottom to A" tex="\tfrac92\,mgR = 4m\,g\,R + 2kR^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

Bring the terms with $m$ together: $\tfrac12 mgR = 2kR^2$, so

<Formula label="Ring: the mass that just reaches A" tex="m = \frac{4kR}{g}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$kR$ is a force, and a force divided by $g$ is a mass. Does the object really get as far as $A$, and turn back there? Let $\varphi$ be the angle at the centre of the ring between the downward direction and the object, so that $\varphi = 0$ at the bottom and $\varphi = 90°$ at $A$. The height above the bottom is $R(1 - \cos\varphi)$ and the square of the spring length is $R^2(5 + 4\sin\varphi)$. So the potential energy, counted from the bottom, is $4mgR(1 - \cos\varphi) + 2kR^2\sin\varphi$. Both terms grow all the way from the bottom to $A$, and at $A$ the first one is still growing. The object therefore slows down steadily on the way, stops for a moment at $A$ and slides back.

With our own numbers, $k = 4.9$ N/m, $R = 0.50$ m and $g = 9.8$ m/s²: $m = 4 \times 4.9 \times 0.50/9.8 = 1.0$ kg. The kinetic energy after the collision is $\tfrac92 \times 1.0 \times 9.8 \times 0.50 = 22.05$ J. The climb takes $4 \times 1.0 \times 9.8 \times 0.50 = 19.6$ J and the spring $2 \times 4.9 \times 0.50^2 = 2.45$ J, together 22.05 J.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A projectile hits a satellite" points={14} mit="Problem 4">

A projectile of mass $m$ is fired straight up from the surface of the Earth. Its launch speed is equal to the escape speed (MIT says escape velocity). The Earth has the radius $R_E$ and the mass $M_E$, and $G$ is the gravitational constant. (MIT writes both with small letters and a small subscript e. We use the capital letters of the lesson on [universal gravitation](/courses/8-01/universal-gravitation).)

Leave out air resistance and the rotation of the Earth, and take the Earth to stay at rest. (These simplifications are ours. MIT's text does not mention them.)

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the launch speed $v_\text{esc}$ of the projectile? Use any of $m$, $G$, $M_E$ and $R_E$ that you need.

<Hint>

A body launched at the escape speed just reaches a very large distance, and arrives there with no speed left.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With the zero of the gravitational potential energy at infinity, what is the mechanical energy of such a body? It has the same value at the surface.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$v_\text{esc} =$" expr="sqrt(2*G*ME/RE)"
  vars={{ G: [1, 10], ME: [1, 10], RE: [1, 10], m: [0.1, 2] }}
  names={{ ME: "M_E", RE: "R_E" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(G*ME/RE)", hint: "This is the speed of a circular orbit that skims the surface. To escape, the kinetic energy must equal the full size of the potential energy, not half of it." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2*G*ME/RE^2)", hint: "Under the root this has G times a mass over a length squared, which is an acceleration (it is 2g). The potential energy goes as 1/r, not as 1/r²." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2*G*ME*m/RE)", hint: "Under the root this is an energy, not a speed squared. The mass of the projectile is on both sides of the energy equation and cancels." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only gravity acts, so the mechanical energy is conserved. With the zero of the potential energy at infinity, a body that just escapes ends with no kinetic and no potential energy: its mechanical energy is zero, at infinity and so everywhere.

<Step title="The potential energy of gravitation">

For two bodies of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ a distance $r$ apart, the notes give

<Formula label="Potential energy of gravitation" tex="U(r) = -\frac{G\,m_1 m_2}{r}, \qquad U(\infty) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (14.16), p. 14-14" />

</Step>

<Step title="Zero energy at the surface">

At the surface, $r = R_E$:

<Formula label="Projectile: energy at the launch" tex="\tfrac12 m\,v_\text{esc}^2 - \frac{G\,M_E m}{R_E} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_\text{esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2G\,M_E}{R_E}}" cite="reading1" at="Example 14.2, Eq. (14.6), p. 14-25" />

(The notes work this out for an asteroid, with its mass and radius in the place of $M_E$ and $R_E$.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$GM_E/R_E$ is a potential energy per mass, so it is a speed squared. The mass of the projectile cancels. For the Earth, with $GM_E = 3.986\times10^{14}$ m³/s² and $R_E = 6.371\times10^6$ m (NASA's Earth Fact Sheet, the values of the lesson on potential energy), this gives 11.2 km/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

The projectile climbs. When it is a distance $2R_E$ from the centre of the Earth, what is its speed $v_p$? Use any of $m$, $G$, $M_E$ and $R_E$ that you need.

<Hint>

The mechanical energy of the projectile keeps the value it had at the launch.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the kinetic and the potential energy at $r = 2R_E$ and set their sum equal to the value from part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$v_p =$" expr="sqrt(G*ME/RE)"
  vars={{ G: [1, 10], ME: [1, 10], RE: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ ME: "M_E", RE: "R_E" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(G*ME/(2*RE))", hint: "This is the speed of a circular orbit of radius 2R_E. The projectile is not in orbit: its mechanical energy is zero, so its kinetic energy equals the full size of its potential energy." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2*G*ME/RE)/2", hint: "This halves the speed when the distance doubles. It is the potential energy, and with it the kinetic energy, that halves. The speed goes with the root of the kinetic energy." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2*G*ME/RE)", hint: "That is the launch speed. The projectile has climbed against gravity since then, so it is slower." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The mechanical energy stays zero during the whole climb. So at every distance the kinetic energy is as large as the potential energy is negative.

<Step title="Energy at twice the radius">

<Formula label="Projectile: speed at twice the Earth's radius" tex="\tfrac12 m\,v_p^2 - \frac{G\,M_E m}{2R_E} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_p = \sqrt{\frac{G\,M_E}{R_E}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is $v_\text{esc}/\sqrt2$: the distance has doubled, the potential energy and the kinetic energy have halved, and the speed has dropped by the factor $\sqrt2$. In general a body with zero mechanical energy has $v = \sqrt{2GM_E/r}$ at the distance $r$, the escape speed from that distance. For the Earth: 7.91 km/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

A satellite, also of mass $m$, moves around the Earth on a circular orbit of radius $2R_E$. What is its speed $v_\text{sat}$? Use any of $m$, $G$, $M_E$ and $R_E$ that you need.

<Hint>

For a circular orbit, energy conservation says nothing new: the speed never changes. Use Newton's second law along the radius.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The only force is gravity, at the distance $2R_E$. It must equal the mass times the centripetal acceleration for a circle of radius $2R_E$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c" label="$v_\text{sat} =$" expr="sqrt(G*ME/(2*RE))"
  vars={{ G: [1, 10], ME: [1, 10], RE: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ ME: "M_E", RE: "R_E" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(G*ME/RE)", hint: "This is the speed of a body with zero mechanical energy at 2R_E (the projectile), or of a circular orbit at the surface. Use the second law with the distance 2R_E in the force and in the centripetal acceleration." },
    { expr: "sqrt(G*ME/(4*RE))", hint: "This puts 2R_E squared in the force but only R_E in the centripetal acceleration. The radius of the circle is 2R_E as well." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2*G*ME/RE)", hint: "That is the escape speed from the surface. A satellite on a circle is bound: use the second law with gravity as the centripetal force." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On a circular orbit gravity is the centripetal force. This is the satellite of the lesson on [Newton's second law and circular motion](/courses/8-01/newtons-second-law-circular-motion).

<Step title="The second law along the radius">

<Formula label="Satellite: circular orbit of radius twice the Earth's" tex="\frac{G\,M_E m}{(2R_E)^2} = \frac{m\,v_\text{sat}^2}{2R_E} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_\text{sat} = \sqrt{\frac{G\,M_E}{2R_E}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The satellite is slower than the projectile at the same place by the factor $\sqrt2$: at any distance, the speed of a circular orbit is the escape speed from there divided by $\sqrt2$. The kinetic energy of the satellite is half the size of its potential energy, so its mechanical energy is negative: it is bound. For the Earth: 5.59 km/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

At the distance $2R_E$ the projectile hits the satellite, and the two stick together. The collision takes almost no time. Just before it, the velocities of the projectile and of the satellite are at right angles to each other. What is the speed $V$ of the two together just after the collision? Use any of $m$, $G$, $M_E$ and $R_E$ that you need.

<Hint>

The two bodies stick together, so kinetic energy is not conserved. During the very short collision gravity gives almost no impulse, so the momentum of the two bodies together is conserved.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Momentum is a vector. Take one axis along the velocity of the projectile and one along the velocity of the satellite, and write the conservation of momentum for each axis.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each component of the final velocity is half of one of the speeds of parts (b) and (c). Combine the two components with Pythagoras.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-d" label="$V =$" expr="sqrt(3*G*ME/(8*RE))"
  vars={{ G: [1, 10], ME: [1, 10], RE: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ ME: "M_E", RE: "R_E" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(sqrt(G*ME/RE) + sqrt(G*ME/(2*RE)))/2", hint: "This adds the two momenta as plain numbers. They are at right angles, so they add as the sides of a right-angled triangle." },
    { expr: "sqrt(3*G*ME/(4*RE))", hint: "This keeps the kinetic energy through the collision. Bodies that stick together lose kinetic energy: use the momentum." },
    { expr: "sqrt(3*G*ME/(2*RE))", hint: "This is the total momentum divided by m. After the collision the moving mass is 2m." },
    { expr: "sqrt(G*ME/RE)/2", hint: "This is the part of the final velocity along the projectile's direction only. The satellite brings momentum at right angles to it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two bodies stick together, so kinetic energy is lost and only momentum can be used. Gravity is an outside force on the two bodies, but in a collision that takes almost no time its impulse is negligible. So each component of the total momentum is the same just before and just after.

<Step title="Momentum along two axes">

The projectile moves straight up, along the radius, and the satellite moves along its circle, at right angles to the radius. Take the $x$ axis along the velocity of the satellite and the $y$ axis along the velocity of the projectile. The combined body has the mass $2m$:

<Formula label="Collision: the two components of momentum" tex="m\,v_\text{sat} = 2m\,V_x, \qquad m\,v_p = 2m\,V_y" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The speed">

So $V_x = v_\text{sat}/2$ and $V_y = v_p/2$, and

<Formula label="Collision: speed just after" tex="V = \tfrac12\sqrt{v_\text{sat}^2 + v_p^2} = \tfrac12\sqrt{\frac{G\,M_E}{2R_E} + \frac{G\,M_E}{R_E}} = \sqrt{\frac{3G\,M_E}{8R_E}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is a number times $\sqrt{GM_E/R_E}$, a speed. It is smaller than both speeds before the collision, as it should be: the two momenta do not point the same way, so part of the motion is lost. The kinetic energy before is $\tfrac12 m\,(v_p^2 + v_\text{sat}^2) = \tfrac34\,GM_E m/R_E$ and after it is $\tfrac12 (2m) V^2 = \tfrac38\,GM_E m/R_E$: half of it is lost. For the Earth: $V = 4.84$ km/s.

A remark of our own, beyond MIT's question: the potential energy of the combined body at $2R_E$ is $-GM_E(2m)/(2R_E) = -GM_E m/R_E$, which is larger in size than its kinetic energy. So its mechanical energy is negative, and the projectile, which could have escaped on its own, is now bound to the Earth.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A particle in a potential well" points={12} mit="Problem 5">

A particle of mass $m$ moves along the $x$ axis. Its potential energy is

<Formula label="Problem 5: the potential energy" tex="U(x) = -U_0\,e^{-x^2/a^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 6" />

where $U_0$ and $a$ are positive constants. ($U_0$ is an energy and $a$ a length. MIT says only that they are constants. The well and the turning points of parts (a) and (c) need them positive.)

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Draw an energy diagram: a graph of $U(x)$ against $x$. Choose a value of the mechanical energy $E$ with $-U_0 < E < 0$ and draw it in. At one position of your choice, mark the potential energy, the kinetic energy and the total energy of the particle.

This part has no answer box. Draw your diagram, then open the solution and compare.

<Hint>

Find the value of $U$ at $x = 0$ and for $x$ far from zero on both sides. Is the function even or odd in $x$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The mechanical energy is the same at every position, so in the diagram it is a horizontal line. The kinetic energy at a position is the gap between that line and the curve.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** An energy diagram is the graph of $U(x)$ with a horizontal line at the height $E$. The lesson on potential energy diagrams reads the kinetic energy off as the gap between the two.

<Step title="The shape of the curve">

At $x = 0$ the exponential is 1, so $U(0) = -U_0$, the lowest value. The function depends on $x^2$ only, so the curve is the same on both sides. Far from the origin the exponential goes to zero, so $U$ rises towards zero from below and never gets there. The curve is a well of depth $U_0$, with a width set by $a$: at $x = \pm a$ the potential energy is $-U_0/e = -0.368\,U_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The energy line and the kinetic energy">

The graph below shows the curve with the example $E = -U_0/2$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-3, 3]} range={[-1.2, 0.3]} xLabel="x / a" yLabel="U / U_0"
  curves={[
    { expr: "-exp(-x^2)", label: "U" },
    { expr: "-0.5", label: "E", dash: true }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 0.8326, y: -0.5, label: "turning point" }, { x: -0.8326, y: -0.5 }]}
  caption="The potential energy U(x) = −U₀ exp(−x²/a²), computed from the formula, with position in units of a and energy in units of U₀. The dashed line is the example E = −U₀/2. It meets the curve at the two turning points, x = ±0.833 a." />

At a position $x$ the height of the curve is the potential energy, and the height of the line is the total energy. The notes give the kinetic energy as the difference:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy from the diagram" tex="K(x) = E - U(x)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (14.9), p. 14-18" />

(The notes write it for a spring. It holds for every potential energy.) For example at $x = 0$, with $E = -U_0/2$: $U = -U_0$, $E = -U_0/2$ and $K = U_0/2$, the gap between the bottom of the well and the dashed line. Your own diagram should show the same three things at your position: $U$ up to the curve (a negative amount, measured down from zero), $E$ up to the line, and $K$ as the gap between them.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The kinetic energy cannot be negative, so the particle can only be where the curve is below the line. For any $E$ between $-U_0$ and 0 that is a stretch around the origin, between two turning points where the line meets the curve: the particle is trapped and moves back and forth. For $E = -U_0/2$ the turning points are where $e^{-x^2/a^2} = \tfrac12$, at $x = \pm a\sqrt{\ln 2} = \pm 0.833\,a$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the force $F_x$ on the particle as a function of its position, using any of $x$, $a$ and $U_0$ that you need. Count a force in the $+x$ direction as positive.

<Hint>

The lesson on potential energy diagrams connects the force with the slope of the potential energy curve.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiate with the chain rule: the exponent $-x^2/a^2$ has its own derivative. Then mind the two minus signs.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$F_x =$" expr="-2*U0*x/a^2*exp(-x^2/a^2)"

  names={{ U0: "U_0" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "U0*exp(-x^2/a^2)", hint: "That is minus the potential energy, not its derivative. Differentiate U with respect to x." },
    { expr: "-U0*exp(-x^2/a^2)", hint: "That is the potential energy itself. The force is minus its derivative with respect to x." },
    { expr: "-2*U0*x*exp(-x^2/a^2)", hint: "The exponent is -x²/a², so its derivative is -2x/a². The factor 1/a² is missing: as it stands this is not a force." },

  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The force is minus the slope of the potential energy.

<Step title="Force from potential energy">

<Formula label="Force from potential energy" tex="F_x = -\frac{dU}{dx}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (14.3), p. 14-16" />

(The notes derive it for a spring and then use it for a general $U(x)$ in Example 14.1.)

</Step>

<Step title="Differentiate">

By the chain rule the derivative of $e^{-x^2/a^2}$ is $-(2x/a^2)\,e^{-x^2/a^2}$. So $dU/dx = (2U_0x/a^2)\,e^{-x^2/a^2}$, and

<Formula label="Problem 5: the force" tex="F_x = -\frac{2U_0\,x}{a^2}\,e^{-x^2/a^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$U_0 x/a^2$ is an energy divided by a length, which is a force. The force is negative for $x > 0$ and positive for $x < 0$: it always points back to the origin, the bottom of the well. It is zero at $x = 0$, which is the stable equilibrium point. Far from the origin it dies away, as the flat ends of the curve show. Close to the origin the exponential is nearly 1 and $F_x \approx -(2U_0/a^2)\,x$: the force of a spring with the spring constant $2U_0/a^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

The particle passes the origin $x = 0$ with a speed $v_0$. Then it moves out, stops for a moment when it reaches $x = a$ (or $x = -a$) and turns back. Find $v_0$, using any of $a$, $m$ and $U_0$ that you need. (The name $v_0$ is ours. MIT calls it the speed at the origin.)

<Hint>

The point where the particle stops and turns back is a turning point. What is the kinetic energy there, and so what is $E$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

**The idea.** At a turning point the kinetic energy is zero, so the mechanical energy equals the potential energy there. The same mechanical energy at the origin gives the speed.

<Step title="The energy of the motion">

The particle stops at $x = a$, so $E = U(a) = -U_0\,e^{-1}$. Because $U$ depends on $x^2$ only, $x = -a$ is the other turning point of the same motion.

At the origin $U(0) = -U_0$:

<Formula label="Problem 5: energy at the origin and at the turning point" tex="\tfrac12 m\,v_0^2 - U_0 = -U_0\,e^{-1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c) (our own solution)" />
