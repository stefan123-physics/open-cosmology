The same force that pulls an apple to the ground keeps the Moon in its orbit, holds galaxies together and, on the largest scales, slows the expansion of the universe. Newton's great step was to see that one law covers all of these.

## Newton's law of gravity

Every two point masses attract each other. The force acts along the line joining them, grows with both masses, and falls off with the square of the distance:

<Formula label="Universal law of gravitation" tex="\vec F^{\,G}_{1,2} = -G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{r_{1,2}^2}\,\hat r_{1,2}, \qquad G = 6.67\times10^{-11}\ \text{N}\,\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 8.2.1, Eq. (8.2.1)" />

As in [Newton's laws](/courses/8-01/newtons-laws), $\vec F_{1,2}$ is the force **on** body 2 **due to** body 1. The unit vector $\hat r_{1,2}$ points from 1 to 2, and the minus sign turns the force around so that it points back toward body 1: gravity attracts.

Swap the labels and only the unit vector changes sign, so $\vec F_{2,1} = -\vec F_{1,2}$. Gravity automatically obeys Newton's third law.

The **inverse-square** dependence is the heart of the law: double the distance and the force drops to a quarter. The graph measures distance in units of some fixed reference distance $R$, and force in units of its value at $r = R$.

## Adding gravitational forces

When several bodies pull on one object, the forces add as vectors. This **superposition** is what MIT's worked example 5.2 practises.

<WorkedExample title="Where Earth and Moon pull equally">
<Problem>
A spacecraft travels in a straight line from the Earth to the Moon. At what distance from the Earth's centre do the two gravitational pulls cancel? Use $M_E = 5.97\times10^{24}$ kg, $M_M = 7.35\times10^{22}$ kg and an Earth–Moon distance $D = 3.84\times10^{8}$ m.
</Problem>
<Step title="Write both forces">
At distance $x$ from the Earth, the Earth pulls back toward it with $GM_E m/x^2$, and the Moon pulls forward with $GM_M m/(D - x)^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Set the forces equal">
$\dfrac{M_E}{x^2} = \dfrac{M_M}{(D - x)^2}$, so $\dfrac{D - x}{x} = \sqrt{\dfrac{M_M}{M_E}} = 0.111$. The spacecraft's own mass $m$ and $G$ cancel.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve">
$x = \dfrac{D}{1 + 0.111} = 3.46\times10^{8}$ m, about 90% of the way to the Moon.
</Step>
<Step title="Sanity check">
The balance point should be much closer to the Moon, because the Moon is about 81 times less massive. It is: the distance ratio is $\sqrt{81} = 9$, and $3.46 : 0.38 \approx 9 : 1$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Gravity at Earth's surface: the value of g

A sphere with its mass spread evenly in layers attracts an outside object as if all its mass sat at its centre. So a body of mass $m$ on the surface of the Earth (mass $M_E$, radius $R_E$) feels a force of size $mg$, with

<Formula label="The value of g" tex="F = mg, \qquad g = \frac{G M_E}{R_E^2} \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2" cite="seg53" />

With $M_E = 5.97\times10^{24}$ kg and $R_E = 6.37\times10^{6}$ m this gives $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, the constant used for [projectiles](/courses/8-01/kinematics-2d).

In fact $g$ varies a little over the Earth's surface. The MIT notes give about $9.78\ \text{m/s}^2$ at the equator and $9.83\ \text{m/s}^2$ at the poles, mostly because the Earth spins and bulges at the equator. Geologists map tiny local changes in $g$ to find dense rock, oil and water underground.

Higher up, $g$ shrinks as $1/r^2$:

## Why everything falls the same way

The mass $m$ in the law of gravity, the **gravitational mass**, measures how strongly a body is pulled. The mass in $\vec F = m\vec a$, the **inertial mass**, measures how hard it is to accelerate. Nothing in Newton's theory says these must be equal, but every experiment finds that they are. This is the **principle of equivalence**:

<Formula label="Principle of equivalence" tex="m_\text{grav} = m_\text{inertial} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{G M}{r^2}\ \ \text{for every body}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 8.2.2" />

Einstein later made this coincidence the foundation of general relativity, where gravity is no longer a force at all but the curvature of spacetime.

<Callout kind="cosmology">
In 8.286, the first model of an expanding universe uses this page's law plus one more fact about spheres. Picture a huge, uniform ball of matter whose every particle moves away from the centre according to Hubble's law. Newton's gravity pulls each particle back. Matter in shells *outside* a particle exerts no net force on it (the shell theorem), so only the mass inside counts, and the deceleration rate $\ddot a/a$ depends only on the density $\rho$:

<Formula label="Newtonian deceleration of the expansion" tex="\frac{d^2 a}{dt^2} = -\frac{4\pi}{3}\,G\rho\,a" cite="guth6" at="lecture transcript" />

Whether the expansion stops and reverses, or goes on forever, then depends on whether gravity can win, just as a ball thrown upward either falls back or escapes. That question, and the open, closed and flat universes it leads to, is the subject of Lecture 7.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Gravity between point masses: $F = Gm_1m_2/r^2$, attractive, along the line joining them.
- Gravitational forces add as vectors (superposition) and obey Newton's third law.
- At Earth's surface $g = GM_E/R_E^2 \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$; it decreases as $1/r^2$ with height.
- Gravitational and inertial mass are equal (principle of equivalence), so all bodies fall alike.
