The [previous lesson](/courses/8-01/rolling-kinematics) described rolling. This one asks what forces and torques make a body roll, and when it stops rolling and starts to slip.

## Friction on a rolling wheel

When a wheel rolls without slipping, its contact point is momentarily at rest, so any friction there is **static** friction, which takes whatever value the situation requires (36.1 transcript, p. 1). A wheel rolling at constant velocity on level ground needs no friction at all: in the ideal case, a hard wheel in a vacuum, it would roll forever (real wheels are slowed by rolling resistance and air resistance) (p. 1). But a wheel rolling down an incline speeds up, so it needs an angular acceleration. Gravity acts at the centre of mass and the normal force points through it, so neither has a torque about the centre; the torque must come from static friction at the contact point (pp. 1–2). Static friction can be anything from zero up to its maximum, depending on the other constraints (p. 2).

## Two equations for a rolling body

(MIT states this general rule last, in segment 36.5; it is put first here.) For a body that translates and rotates, analyse the two motions separately (36.5 transcript, p. 1; notes, Section 21.4, Eqs. (21.4.1) and (21.4.4), pp. 21-6–21-7):

<Formula label="Translation and rotation" tex="\vec F^{\,\text{ext}} = m\vec A_{\text{cm}}, \qquad \tau^{\,\text{ext}}_{\text{cm},z} = I_{\text{cm}}\,\alpha_z" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (21.4.1), (21.4.4), pp. 21-6–21-7" />

Taking torques about the centre of mass is what makes this work: about the centre, only the spin angular momentum $I_{\text{cm}}\omega$ changes, so the torque about the centre of mass gives the rotational dynamics, while $F = mA_{\text{cm}}$ gives the linear dynamics, even though the centre itself is accelerating (36.5 transcript, p. 1; notes, Section 21.3, pp. 21-1–21-6).

## Down an incline: the torque method

A wheel of radius $R$ rolls without slipping down an incline at angle $\phi$ (36.2 transcript, p. 1). Along the incline, gravity's component and friction give the translational equation; about the centre, only friction (moment arm $R$, at right angles) gives a torque; and rolling without slipping links the two, $A_{\text{cm}} = R\alpha$ (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Rolling down an incline: the equations" tex="mg\sin\phi - f_s = m a_x, \qquad f_s R = I_{\text{cm}}\,\alpha, \qquad a_x = R\alpha" cite="reading3" at="pp. 1–2" />

Three equations, three unknowns. Substituting $\alpha = a_x/R$ gives $f_s = I_{\text{cm}}a_x/R^2$, and then (pp. 2–3; the notes do it for a uniform cylinder, Example 21.4, pp. 21-16–21-21):

<Formula label="Acceleration down the incline" tex="a_x = \frac{mg\sin\phi}{m + I_{\text{cm}}/R^2}" cite="reading3" at="p. 3" />

The acceleration is constant, so after rolling a distance $s = h/\sin\phi$, dropping a height $h$, $v^2 = 2sa_x$ gives (p. 3):

<Formula label="Speed at the bottom" tex="v_{\text{cm}} = \sqrt{\frac{2mgh}{m + I_{\text{cm}}/R^2}}" cite="reading3" at="p. 3" />

which agrees with the energy method (p. 3; notes, Section 21.5, Eq. (21.5.3), p. 21-7: the work done equals the change in translational plus rotational kinetic energy). The rotation enters as an extra mass $I_{\text{cm}}/R^2$, as for the massive pulley.

## A yo-yo pulled along the ground

A yo-yo with inner radius $b$ (where the string is wound) and outer radius $R$ sits on the ground; the string, pulled horizontally with force $F$, leaves from the bottom of the inner spool (36.4 transcript, p. 1; where it leaves follows from the directions of the torques on p. 2). Pull gently and it rolls without slipping; pull too hard and it slips. The forces: $F$ forward, static friction $f_s$ backward at the contact point, gravity and the normal force, which have no torque about the centre (pp. 1–2). Friction gives a torque $f_sR$ one way, the string $bF$ the other (p. 2):

<Formula label="The yo-yo equations" tex="F - f_s = ma, \qquad f_sR - bF = I_{\text{cm}}\,\alpha, \qquad a = R\alpha" cite="reading4" at="p. 2" />

Eliminating $a$ and $\alpha$ and solving for $F$ (p. 2):

<Formula label="Pull and friction on the yo-yo" tex="F = f_s\,\frac{R + I_{\text{cm}}/(mR)}{b + I_{\text{cm}}/(mR)}" cite="reading4" at="p. 2" />

The harder you pull, the larger the static friction must be, but it cannot exceed $\mu_sN = \mu_smg$. So the largest pull for rolling without slipping is (p. 3):

<Formula label="Maximum pull" tex="F_{\max} = \mu_s mg\,\frac{R + I_{\text{cm}}/(mR)}{b + I_{\text{cm}}/(mR)}" cite="reading4" at="p. 3" />

Because $\frac{R + I_{\text{cm}}/(mR)}{b + I_{\text{cm}}/(mR)} > 1$, the pull is larger than the friction, so $a > 0$: surprisingly, the yo-yo rolls **towards** the person pulling it, winding up its string. Segment 36.3, which comes before the yo-yo in MIT's order, shows this with a spool (a video without a transcript).

<WorkedExample title="A race down an incline and a yo-yo">
<Problem>
(a) A solid sphere, a solid cylinder and a thin hoop roll without slipping down a 30° incline. Find each acceleration, and the smallest coefficient of static friction the cylinder needs. (b) A 0.20 kg yo-yo, treated as a uniform disc of radius $R = 0.050$ m, has its string wound at $b = 0.020$ m; $\mu_s = 0.40$. Find the largest horizontal pull for rolling without slipping, and the acceleration then.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The race">
$a = \frac{g\sin\phi}{1 + I_{\text{cm}}/mR^2}$ with $g\sin 30° = 4.9$ m/s². Sphere ($\frac{2}{5}$): $\frac{4.9}{1.4} = 3.5$ m/s². Cylinder ($\frac{1}{2}$): $\frac{4.9}{1.5} \approx 3.27$ m/s². Hoop (1): $2.45$ m/s². The cylinder needs $f_s = \frac{1}{2}ma \approx 1.63\,m$ newtons, against a normal force $mg\cos 30° \approx 8.49\,m$, so $\mu_s \ge 0.19$; in general $\mu_s \ge \frac{1}{3}\tan\phi$ for a cylinder (notes, Eq. (21.6.47), p. 21-18).
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The yo-yo">
$I_{\text{cm}} = \frac{1}{2}mR^2$, so $I_{\text{cm}}/(mR) = \frac{1}{2}R = 0.025$ m. Then $F_{\max} = 0.40 \times 0.20 \times 9.8 \times \frac{0.050 + 0.025}{0.020 + 0.025} \approx 0.784 \times 1.67 \approx 1.31$ N. The friction is then 0.784 N, so $a = \frac{1.31 - 0.784}{0.20} \approx 2.6$ m/s².
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="Galileo's rolling balls">
Galileo timed balls rolling down gentle inclines to study falling. On a flat incline a uniform ball rolling without slipping accelerates at $\frac{5}{7}g\sin\phi$, not $g\sin\phi$, because part of the energy goes into spin (his balls ran in a groove, which slows them a little more). The pattern he found, distance growing as the square of the time, is unaffected, because the acceleration is still constant.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Translation: $\vec F^{\,\text{ext}} = m\vec A_{\text{cm}}$; rotation about the centre of mass: $\tau_{\text{cm},z} = I_{\text{cm}}\alpha_z$; plus constraints such as $A_{\text{cm}} = R\alpha$.
- Static friction at the contact point takes whatever value rolling requires, from zero up to $\mu_sN$.
- Down an incline: $a = \frac{g\sin\phi}{1 + I_{\text{cm}}/mR^2}$, the same as from energy; spheres beat cylinders beat hoops.
- A yo-yo pulled by its string rolls without slipping only up to $F_{\max} = \mu_smg\,\frac{R + I_{\text{cm}}/(mR)}{b + I_{\text{cm}}/(mR)}$.
