So far every velocity has been measured by one observer, usually standing on the ground. This lesson asks how velocities look to an observer who is moving, and uses the answer for **recoil**: one part of a system pushes off another, and the push is described relative to a moving object.

## Reference frames and Galilean transformations

A coordinate system with an observer at its origin is a **reference frame** (notes, Section 11.1, p. 11-2). Take a second frame S′ whose origin is at $\vec R$ as seen from frame S. An object at $\vec r$ in S is at (notes, Eq. (11.2.1), p. 11-3)

<Formula label="Galilean coordinate transformation" tex="\vec r\,' = \vec r - \vec R" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.2.1), p. 11-3" />

and the frames move relative to each other with velocity $\vec V = d\vec R/dt$ (Eq. (11.2.2)). If $\vec V$ is constant, the frames are **relatively inertial**: an object with no net force on it moves uniformly in both. The notes read Newton's first law this way, as a **principle of relativity** (pp. 11-3–11-4).

## Adding velocities

Differentiate the transformation and you get the **law of addition of velocities** (notes, Eqs. (11.3.1)–(11.3.2), p. 11-4):

<Formula label="Law of addition of velocities" tex="\vec v\,' = \vec v - \vec V" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.3.2), p. 11-4" />

Segment 18.1 practises it in one dimension (18.1 transcript, p. 1). A car drives to the right at $v_\text{car}$ past someone on the sidewalk, and a bike rides to the left at $v_\text{bike}$. To the biker, the sidewalk moves right at $v_\text{bike}$, so the car moves right at $v_\text{car} + v_\text{bike}$. If the biker throws an apple forwards (to the left) at $v_\text{apple}$ relative to herself, the person on the sidewalk sees it go left at $v_\text{apple} + v_\text{bike}$. If instead the person on the sidewalk throws it to the right at $v_\text{apple}$, the car sees it go left at $v_\text{car} - v_\text{apple}$, and the bike sees it go right at $v_\text{apple} + v_\text{bike}$.

The notes' first example does the same in two dimensions, for two aircraft flying north-east and south-east: the velocity of one as seen from the other is the vector difference (notes, Example 11.1, pp. 11-4–11-6).

<WorkedExample title="Rain on a windscreen">
<Problem>
Rain falls straight down at 8.0 m/s. A car drives through it at 20 m/s. How fast, and at what angle, does the rain hit the windscreen, as seen from the car?
</Problem>
<Step title="Velocities">
Ground frame: rain $\vec v = (0, -8.0)$ m/s. The car's frame moves with $\vec V = (20, 0)$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Subtract">
$\vec v\,' = \vec v - \vec V = (-20, -8.0)$ m/s: speed $\sqrt{20^2 + 8.0^2} \approx 21.5$ m/s, coming from ahead of the car, slanting back at $\tan^{-1}(20/8.0) \approx 68°$ from the vertical.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Setting up a recoil problem

A person stands on a cart; both are at rest. The person jumps off horizontally and the cart recoils the other way (18.2 transcript, pp. 1–3). There is no horizontal external force, so the horizontal momentum of person and cart is conserved.

Segment 18.2 draws the **momentum diagrams** twice:

- **Ground frame.** Initially both are at rest. Finally the cart moves with $v_c$ and the person with $v_p$.
- **A frame moving with the cart's final velocity $v_c$.** Initially person and cart both move with $-v_c$. Finally the cart is at rest, and the person moves with velocity $u$: the person's velocity **relative to the cart** (pp. 2–3).

A recoil problem usually gives you $u$, the speed of the jump as the jumper feels it, not $v_p$. Relating the two is the law of addition of velocities: the ground velocity is the frame's velocity plus the velocity in the moving frame (p. 3):

<Formula label="Ground velocity from relative velocity" tex="v_p = v_c + u" cite="reading3" at="p. 3" />

## Solving in the ground frame

Momentum is zero before the jump, so it is zero after (18.3 transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Recoil in the ground frame" tex="0 = m_c v_c + m_p\,(v_c + u) \;\Rightarrow\; v_c = -\frac{m_p\,u}{m_c + m_p}" cite="reading4" at="p. 1" />

and then $v_p = v_c + u = m_c u/(m_c + m_p)$ (p. 1). The cart moves backwards (the minus sign), and the person's ground speed is less than $u$, because the cart moves backwards: of the relative speed $u$, the cart takes the share $m_p/(m_c + m_p)$.

## Solving in the moving frame

In the frame moving with the cart, the momentum before is $-(m_p + m_c)v_c$, and after the jump only the person moves, with momentum $m_p u$ (18.4 transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Recoil in the moving frame" tex="-(m_p + m_c)\,v_c = m_p\,u \;\Rightarrow\; v_c = -\frac{m_p\,u}{m_p + m_c}" cite="reading5" at="p. 1" />

Exactly the same result, and quicker: in this frame the given speed $u$ goes straight into the momentum equation. Momentum conservation holds in every inertial frame, so you can pick whichever frame makes the algebra easiest.

<WorkedExample title="Jumping off a sled">
<Problem>
A 60 kg skater stands on a 15 kg sled at rest on smooth ice and jumps off forwards at 3.0 m/s relative to the sled. Find the velocities of sled and skater relative to the ice.
</Problem>
<Step title="Sled">
$v_c = -m_p u/(m_p + m_c) = -60 \times 3.0/75 = -2.4$ m/s: backwards at 2.4 m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Skater">
$v_p = v_c + u = -2.4 + 3.0 = 0.6$ m/s. Check: $60 \times 0.6 + 15 \times (-2.4) = 36 - 36 = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="The usual mistake">
Treating 3.0 m/s as the skater's ground velocity gives $v_c = -60 \times 3.0/15 = -12$ m/s, five times too fast. Always check which frame a given speed is measured in.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Throwing at an angle

The notes add a ball thrown at an angle (notes, Example 11.3, pp. 11-7–11-10). A person of mass $m_1$ on a cart of mass $m_2$ throws a ball of mass $m_3$ at speed $v_0$ and angle $\theta$ above the horizontal, **as measured on the cart**. Horizontal momentum is conserved; vertical momentum is not, because while throwing, the person pushes down on the cart and the ground pushes up harder than gravity (p. 11-9). The cart recoils with

<Formula label="Recoil of the cart" tex="v_{f,\text{cart}} = \frac{m_3\,v_0\cos\theta}{m_1 + m_2 + m_3}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.4.19), p. 11-9" />

and the ball's horizontal velocity on the ground is $v_0\cos\theta - v_{f,\text{cart}}$, smaller than on the cart, while its vertical velocity $v_0\sin\theta$ is the same. So the ground observer sees the ball leave more steeply, at an angle $\varphi$ with (Eq. (11.4.22), p. 11-10)

<Formula label="Angle seen from the ground" tex="\tan\varphi = \frac{m_1 + m_2 + m_3}{m_1 + m_2}\,\tan\theta" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (11.4.22), p. 11-10" />

For a light ball the two angles are nearly equal.

<WorkedExample title="A throw from a cart">
<Problem>
A 50 kg person on a 30 kg cart throws a 2.0 kg ball at 10 m/s and 40° above the horizontal, measured on the cart. Find the cart's recoil speed and the angle seen from the ground.
</Problem>
<Step title="Recoil">
$v_{f,\text{cart}} = 2.0 \times 10 \times \cos 40°/82 \approx 0.187$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="The ball on the ground">
Horizontal: $10\cos 40° - 0.187 \approx 7.47$ m/s; vertical: $10 \sin 40° \approx 6.43$ m/s. The angle is $\tan^{-1}(6.43/7.47) \approx 40.7°$, a little steeper than 40°, as $\tan\varphi = (82/80)\tan 40°$ says.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Hubble's law seen from another galaxy">
Hubble's law says a galaxy at position $\vec r$ moves away from us with velocity $\vec v = H_0\,\vec r$. What does an observer in galaxy A, at $\vec r_A$, see? Subtract A's velocity: galaxy B moves relative to A with $\vec v_B - \vec v_A = H_0(\vec r_B - \vec r_A)$, which is Hubble's law again, measured from A. With $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc, a galaxy 50 Mpc from A recedes from A at 3500 km/s, wherever A is. (Subtracting velocities this way is fine for speeds well below the speed of light.) So no galaxy is the centre of the expansion: this is how a homogeneous universe can expand.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A reference frame is a coordinate system with an observer. Galilean transformation: $\vec r\,' = \vec r - \vec R$, with $\vec V = d\vec R/dt$.
- Law of addition of velocities: $\vec v\,' = \vec v - \vec V$. Ground velocity = velocity of the moving frame + velocity in that frame.
- Recoil: the jump speed $u$ is usually relative to the cart, so $v_p = v_c + u$. Momentum conservation then gives $v_c = -m_p u/(m_p + m_c)$.
- The result is the same in any inertial frame; the frame moving with the cart is the quickest.
- Throwing at an angle from a cart: only horizontal momentum is conserved; the ground observer sees a steeper throw.
