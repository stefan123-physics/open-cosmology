Week 5 of 8.01 moves from forces to a new quantity, **momentum**. This lesson defines it, rewrites Newton's second law in terms of it, integrates that law over time to get the **impulse**, and extends both ideas from one particle to a whole system.

## Momentum and Newton's second law

A particle of mass $m$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ has **momentum** (15.1 transcript, p. 1; notes, Eq. (10.2.1), p. 10-1)

<Formula label="Momentum" tex="\vec p = m\,\vec v" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Momentum is a vector, in the direction of the velocity, and it depends on the reference frame. Its SI units are kg·m/s, which are the same as a force times a time, N·s (p. 1; notes, p. 10-1).

For one particle of constant mass, Newton's second law can be rewritten (p. 1; notes, Eqs. (10.2.2)–(10.2.3), p. 10-2):

<Formula label="Second law in terms of momentum" tex="\vec F = m\vec a = m\,\frac{d\vec v}{dt} = \frac{d(m\vec v)}{dt} = \frac{d\vec p}{dt}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The lecture stresses this form. It is the **most general** form of the second law: it applies not just to a single particle but to systems of many particles, and to systems whose mass changes or flows, like a fluid (so the lecture says; for changing mass the law must be applied with care, to a system that keeps the same particles, as the course does later for rockets). $\vec F = m\vec a$ is the special case of one point mass (pp. 1–2). [The transcript says "the time derivative of the acceleration, dv/dt" at one point; it means the derivative of the velocity.]

## Impulse

A law with a derivative can always be integrated. Integrate both sides of $\vec F = d\vec p/dt$ from $t_1$ to $t_2$ (15.1 transcript, p. 2):

<Formula label="Impulse equals change in momentum" tex="\vec I \equiv \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \vec F(t)\,dt = \vec p(t_2) - \vec p(t_1) = \Delta\vec p" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (10.2.4)–(10.2.5), p. 10-2" />

The integral of the force is the **impulse**. The name suggests a short, sharp blow, but it equally describes a weak force acting for a long time. The force can vary in any way: the change in momentum depends only on its integral, the **area under the force–time graph**, not on the shape of the curve (pp. 2–3).

That suggests an **average force**: the constant force that would give the same area over the same interval $\Delta t$ (p. 3; notes, Eq. (10.2.6), p. 10-2):

<Formula label="Average force" tex="\vec F_\text{ave} = \frac{1}{\Delta t}\int_0^{\Delta t}\vec F(t)\,dt, \qquad \vec F_\text{ave}\,\Delta t = \Delta\vec p" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

## Impulse is a vector

Segment 15.2 does the same derivation with care (15.2 transcript, pp. 1–2). The force $\vec F(t')$ is a function of time (not force times time), with $t'$ the dummy variable of integration. Substituting $\vec F = d\vec p/dt'$, the $dt'$ cancels and the integral of $d\vec p$ is just the difference of the end values: impulse equals final minus initial momentum.

Because the impulse is a vector integral, it is really **three integrals**, one per component: $I_x = \Delta p_x$, $I_y = \Delta p_y$, $I_z = \Delta p_z$ (p. 2). And its SI units are those of momentum.

<WorkedExample title="A tennis ball and a racket">
<Problem>
The force of a racket on a 58 g tennis ball rises steadily from 0 to 400 N in 2.0 ms and falls back to 0 in another 2.0 ms (the graph above). The ball arrives at 15 m/s. Treat the motion as along one line and take the racket's push as positive. Find the impulse, the average force and the ball's final velocity. (The notes' Example 10.1 is a slower push of the same triangular shape.)
</Problem>
<Step title="Impulse">
The area of the triangle: $I = \tfrac12 \times 400 \times 0.0040 = 0.80$ N·s.
</Step>
<Step title="Average force">
$F_\text{ave} = I/\Delta t = 0.80/0.0040 = 200$ N: half the peak, as for any triangular pulse.
</Step>
<Step title="Final velocity">
$\Delta v = I/m = 0.80/0.058 \approx 13.8$ m/s. Arriving at $-15$ m/s, the ball leaves at $-15 + 13.8 \approx -1.2$ m/s: this push does not quite stop it: it still moves the original way. To send it back at 15 m/s would need an impulse of $0.058 \times 30 = 1.74$ N·s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Worked example: the average force of a bounce

A ball is dropped from height $h_i$, hits the ground and bounces up to a lower height $h_f$: some energy goes into the deformation of the rubber and ends up as heat (15.3 transcript, p. 1). What is the average force of the ground on the ball during the collision?

Take **up** as positive. During the contact the ball feels the normal force $N$ up and gravity $mg$ down. Call the moments just before and just after the collision $t_b$ and $t_a$, with speeds $v_b$ (down) and $v_a$ (up), and $\Delta t = t_a - t_b$ (p. 2). The momentum principle, with the normal force replaced by its average:

<Formula label="Momentum principle for the bounce" tex="\int_{t_b}^{t_a}(N - mg)\,dt = (N_\text{ave} - mg)\,\Delta t = m v_a - (-m v_b) = m\,(v_a + v_b)" cite="reading4" at="pp. 2–3" />

The velocity reverses, so the speeds **add**. From kinematics, a fall from $h_i$ gives $v_b = \sqrt{2gh_i}$ and a rise to $h_f$ needs $v_a = \sqrt{2gh_f}$ (p. 3):

<Formula label="Average normal force" tex="N_\text{ave} = \frac{m\left(\sqrt{2gh_f} + \sqrt{2gh_i}\right)}{\Delta t} + mg" cite="reading4" at="p. 3" />

The shorter the collision, the larger the average force (p. 3).

<WorkedExample title="A bouncing ball, in numbers">
<Problem>
A 60 g ball is dropped from 1.8 m, bounces back to 1.2 m, and is in contact with the floor for 5.0 ms. Find the average force of the floor.
</Problem>
<Step title="Speeds">
$v_b = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 1.8} \approx 5.94$ m/s and $v_a = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 1.2} \approx 4.85$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Average force">
$N_\text{ave} = 0.060 \times (4.85 + 5.94)/0.0050 + 0.060 \times 9.81 \approx 129.5 + 0.59 \approx 130$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Comparison">
That is about 220 times the ball's weight (0.59 N). During a short collision gravity hardly matters, which is why it is often left out.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The momentum of a system of particles

Take $N$ particles, labelled $j = 1, \ldots, N$, with masses $m_j$ and velocities $\vec v_j$ in one reference frame (15.4 transcript, p. 1). The **momentum of the system** is the vector sum (pp. 1–2; notes, Eq. (10.4.8), p. 10-6):

<Formula label="Momentum of a system" tex="\vec p_\text{sys} = \sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec p_j = \sum_{j=1}^{N} m_j\,\vec v_j" cite="reading5" at="pp. 1–2" />

If $\vec F_j$ is the total force on particle $j$, Newton's second law for each particle, added up, gives (p. 2):

<Formula label="Total force and the system's momentum" tex="\sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec F_j = \sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{d\vec p_j}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec p_j = \frac{d\vec p_\text{sys}}{dt}" cite="reading5" at="p. 2" />

So far this is only the second law, rewritten. The next step uses the **third** law.

## Internal forces cancel in pairs

Split the force on particle $j$ into an **external** part, from objects outside the system (for a system of moons, the planet they orbit), and an **internal** part, from the other particles in the system (15.5 transcript, pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="External and internal forces on one particle" tex="\vec F_j = \vec F_{j,\text{ext}} + \sum_{k=1,\,k\neq j}^{N}\vec F_{kj}" cite="reading6" at="p. 2" />

Here $\vec F_{kj}$ is the internal force on particle $j$ from particle $k$; the sum leaves out $k = j$, since a particle exerts no force on itself. By Newton's third law the forces between any two particles are equal and opposite, $\vec F_{kj} + \vec F_{jk} = \vec 0$. When the internal forces on all the particles are added, they cancel **in pairs**, and the double sum is zero (pp. 3–4; notes, Eqs. (10.4.3)–(10.4.5), p. 10-5). What remains:

<Formula label="Second law for a system" tex="\vec F_\text{ext} = \sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec F_{j,\text{ext}} = \frac{d\vec p_\text{sys}}{dt}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (10.4.9), p. 10-6" />

The external force changes the momentum of the system. However complicated the interactions inside, they do not change the total momentum (pp. 4–5). In terms of impulse, $\Delta\vec p_\text{sys} = \int\vec F_\text{ext}\,dt$ (notes, Eq. (10.4.10), p. 10-6).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Light has momentum too">
Momentum is not only for things with mass. Special relativity gives a photon of energy $E$ the momentum $E/c$, and 8.286 uses this to explain why radiation in the early universe has pressure. In an expanding universe, freely moving particles also lose momentum as the universe grows: the momentum of each falls in proportion to $1/a(t)$, where $a$ is the scale factor. For light this is the cosmological redshift; for slow particles it means that their motion relative to the expansion dies away.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Momentum: $\vec p = m\vec v$, in kg·m/s = N·s.
- The most general form of Newton's second law: $\vec F = d\vec p/dt$.
- Impulse: $\vec I = \int\vec F\,dt = \Delta\vec p$, the area under the force–time graph; average force $\vec F_\text{ave} = \Delta\vec p/\Delta t$. It holds component by component.
- A bounce reverses the velocity, so the momentum changes by $m(v_a + v_b)$; a short contact means a large average force.
- For a system: $\vec p_\text{sys} = \sum m_j\vec v_j$, and only the external force changes it, $\vec F_\text{ext} = d\vec p_\text{sys}/dt$, because internal forces cancel in pairs.
