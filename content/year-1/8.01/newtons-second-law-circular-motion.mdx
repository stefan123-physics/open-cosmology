Lessons 8 to 10 described circular motion: how an object goes round. Lesson 11 asks **why**: which forces make it do so. The bridge is Newton's second law. The left side, the forces, is the physics of the situation; the right side, mass times acceleration, is the geometry of the motion that we have already worked out. The law says the two must be equal.

## The second law in polar components

The acceleration on a circle has a radial and a tangential part (Lesson 10):

<Formula label="Acceleration on a circle" tex="\vec a(t) = -r\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2\hat r(t) + r\,\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\,\hat\theta(t)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.1.1), p. 9-2" />

$\vec F = m\vec a$ is a vector equation, so it holds component by component. In the radial direction the forces, found from a free-body diagram, must add up to mass times the inward acceleration; in the tangential direction to mass times the tangential acceleration:

<Formula label="Radial component" tex="F_r = -mr\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2 = -mr\omega^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.1.2), p. 9-2" />

<Formula label="Tangential component" tex="F_\theta = mr\,\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = mr\,\alpha_z" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.1.3), p. 9-2" />

In **uniform** circular motion $d^2\theta/dt^2 = 0$, so the tangential forces must cancel:

<Formula label="Uniform circular motion" tex="F_\theta = 0" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.1.4), p. 9-2" />

The radial forces, on the other hand, can never add up to zero while the object moves: the right-hand side $-mr\omega^2$ always points inwards. Something, a string, a road, gravity, must pull or push the object towards the centre. [In the video (transcript 11.1, p. 2) the lecturer says the tangential force is "r d²θ/dt²", leaving out the mass; the notes, Eq. (9.1.3), have $m r\,d^2\theta/dt^2$.]

## A car on a banked turn

Segment 11.2 works a classic example. A car goes round a circular track of radius $r$, banked at an angle $\varphi$, with **no friction**. At what speed can it keep going round at the same height, without sliding up or down the slope?

The key is to remember that this is circular motion: the car accelerates **horizontally towards the centre**. That suggests unit vectors along the horizontal radius ($\hat r$, outwards) and the vertical ($\hat k$, up), rather than along the slope as for a block on a fixed incline. Only two forces act: the normal force $\vec N$ of the road, perpendicular to the slope, and gravity, $m\vec g$, straight down. The normal force is tilted by $\varphi$ from the vertical, so its inward component is $N\sin\varphi$ and its upward component $N\cos\varphi$:

<Formula label="Radial direction" tex="-N\sin\varphi = -m\,\frac{v^2}{r}" cite="reading3" at="transcript 11.2, p. 2" />

<Formula label="Vertical direction" tex="N\cos\varphi - mg = 0" cite="reading3" at="transcript 11.2, p. 3" />

The vertical acceleration is zero because the car stays on its circle. Two equations, two unknowns ($N$ and $v$): instead of solving for $N$ and substituting, divide one equation by the other. The mass cancels too:

<Formula label="Speed on a frictionless banked turn" tex="\tan\varphi = \frac{v^2}{rg} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{rg\tan\varphi}" cite="reading3" at="transcript 11.2, p. 3" />

What if the car goes faster, $v' > v$? It would need a larger radius, $r' = v'^2/(g\tan\varphi)$, so it slides **up** the slope. It overshoots the new radius a little and then oscillates about it (not as a simple sine wave). A slower car slides **down** to a smaller radius in the same way. [The lecturer writes these speeds as $v'$ and $v''$; the primes only label different speeds, not derivatives.]

<WorkedExample title="Designing a banked curve">
<Problem>
A curve of radius 120 m is banked at 15°. If the road were perfectly icy (no friction), at what speed could a car take it?
</Problem>
<Step title="Formula">
$v = \sqrt{rg\tan\varphi} = \sqrt{120 \times 9.81 \times \tan 15°}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
$\tan 15° \approx 0.268$, so $v = \sqrt{120 \times 9.81 \times 0.268} \approx \sqrt{315} \approx 17.8$ m/s, about 64 km/h. Real roads have friction, which allows a range of speeds around this one.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The rotating bucket

Segment 11.3 is a demonstration: ping-pong balls in a bucket that is swung round a vertical circle stay in, even upside down at the top, as long as the swing is fast enough; too slowly, or simply turned over, and they drop out. The demonstration has no equations; the short calculation here is this page's own. At the top of the circle, radius $R$, both the bucket's push $N$ on a ball and the ball's weight point down, towards the centre:

<Formula label="At the top of the circle" tex="N + mg = m\,\frac{v^2}{R}" cite="seg113" at="this page's own calculation for the demonstration in segment 11.3" />

The bucket can push but not pull, so $N \ge 0$, which requires $v^2/R \ge g$: the speed at the top must be at least $\sqrt{gR}$. Then gravity alone is not enough to supply the inward acceleration, and the bucket supplies the rest. At exactly $\sqrt{gR}$ the balls are "weightless" at the top; any slower and gravity pulls them in faster than the circle bends, so they fall out.

<WorkedExample title="How fast must the bucket go round?">
<Problem>
A bucket is swung on a vertical circle of radius 0.80 m. What is the least speed at the top, and the longest time per turn if it went round at that speed?
</Problem>
<Step title="Least speed">
$v = \sqrt{gR} = \sqrt{9.81 \times 0.80} \approx 2.80$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Time per turn">
At that speed one turn would take $2\pi R/v = 2\pi \times 0.80/2.80 \approx 1.79$ s. Any slower and the balls fall. (A real swing is faster at the bottom, but this is the limit at the top.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Gravity on a circle: the Moon's month

The notes apply the same law to the most important circle of all for Newton: the Moon's orbit. Moving nearly in a circle of radius $R_{e,m}$ with period $T$, the Moon accelerates towards the Earth with

<Formula label="Moon's centripetal acceleration" tex="a_r = -\frac{4\pi^2 R_{e,m}}{T^2}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.2.1), p. 9-3" />

and the force that supplies it is the Earth's gravity, from [the universal law of gravitation](/courses/8-01/universal-gravitation). With the Moon's mass $m_1$ and the Earth's mass $m_2$, the radial second law reads

<Formula label="Radial second law for the Moon" tex="-G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{R_{e,m}^2} = -m_1\,\frac{4\pi^2 R_{e,m}}{T^2}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.2.3), p. 9-4" />

so the period is

<Formula label="Period of the orbit" tex="T = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^2 R_{e,m}^3}{G\,m_2}}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.2.4), p. 9-5" />

With $R_{e,m} = 3.82 \times 10^8$ m and $m_2 = 5.98 \times 10^{24}$ kg the notes find $T \approx 2.35 \times 10^6$ s, or 27.2 days: the **sidereal month**, the time for the Moon to return to the same place among the stars. The time between full moons, the **synodic month**, is longer, 29.53 days on average, because in the meantime the Earth has moved along its own orbit round the Sun, and the Moon must go a little further than one full circle to line up again.

## Kepler's third law

Two things stand out. The Moon's own mass has cancelled, so the period does not depend on it. (Strictly, this treats the Earth as fixed, which is a good approximation because the Moon is much lighter, $m_1 \ll m_2$; when the two masses are comparable, as for a double star, both bodies circle their common centre and the period depends on the total mass.) And the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the radius:

<Formula label="Kepler's third law for a circular orbit" tex="T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G\,m_2}\,R_{e,m}^3" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.2.7), p. 9-6" />

This is **Kepler's third law**, found from the planets' motions decades before Newton. That his law of gravity reproduced it was, for Newton, convincing evidence that the inverse-square law was right.

<Callout kind="cosmology">
Kepler's law can be turned round to **weigh** what lies inside an orbit: $m_2 = 4\pi^2R^3/(GT^2) = v^2R/G$ for a circle. The Sun circles the centre of the Milky Way at about 229 km/s (Eilers and colleagues, 2019) at about 8178 parsecs (GRAVITY collaboration, 2019). That gives about $2.0 \times 10^{41}$ kg, roughly $10^{11}$ solar masses, inside the Sun's orbit. Doing the same with the speeds of stars and gas at larger radii, a galaxy's rotation curve, shows more mass than the stars and gas account for: the evidence for **dark matter** on the scale of galaxies.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Newton's second law on a circle splits into $F_r = -mr\omega^2$ (the forces must pull inwards) and $F_\theta = mr\,d^2\theta/dt^2$ (which changes the speed); in uniform circular motion $F_\theta = 0$.
- Choose unit vectors that fit the motion: for a car on a banked turn, horizontal radius and vertical, since the acceleration is horizontal and inwards.
- Frictionless banked turn: $N\sin\varphi = mv^2/r$ and $N\cos\varphi = mg$ give $v = \sqrt{rg\tan\varphi}$; faster cars slide up, slower ones down.
- A bucket swung on a vertical circle keeps its contents if $v \ge \sqrt{gR}$ at the top.
- Gravity supplying the centripetal force gives the Moon's period, $T^2 = 4\pi^2R^3/(Gm_2)$: Kepler's third law, independent of the orbiting mass.
