The previous lessons described *how* things move. Newton's laws explain *why*: they connect motion to the forces that act on a body. Isaac Newton published them in 1687 in the *Principia*, building on Galileo's work on falling bodies and inertia.

## What is a force?

In everyday language a force is a push or a pull. Physics needs something we can measure. The MIT notes define force operationally, through the acceleration it produces on a chosen **standard body** of mass $m_s$:

<Formula label="Force, defined with a standard body" tex="\vec F \equiv m_s\,\vec a" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 7.1, Eq. (7.1.1)" />

A force has a size and a direction, the direction of the acceleration it causes. Experiments then show something that is not obvious: when two forces act together, the accelerations add as vectors, so the forces add as vectors too. That is why we can find the **net force** on a body by vector addition.

## Measuring mass

Apply the same force to the standard body and to an unknown body. The unknown body's **inertial mass** follows from the ratio of the accelerations:

<Formula label="Mass from equal forces" tex="m_\text{in} = m_s\,\frac{a_s}{a_\text{in}}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 7.1.1, Eq. (1.9) as printed" />

A body that accelerates half as much under the same push has twice the mass. Mass is a measure of **inertia**, of how hard it is to change a body's motion.

## The first law: inertia

> With no net force acting on it, a body stays at rest or keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed.

Before Galileo, it seemed natural that moving things need a push to keep going, because on Earth friction always slows them down. The first law says the opposite. Motion at constant velocity needs no cause; only *changes* in velocity do. Any change in speed or direction means a net force is acting.

The first law also tells us which reference frames to use. A frame in which it holds is called an **inertial frame**. Sitting in a train moving smoothly at constant velocity, no experiment inside the carriage can tell you whether you are moving or the station is. Both frames are inertial. A train that is braking or turning is not: objects in it start to move with no force pushing them.

MIT's segments 4.3 and 4.4 make this precise. If one frame moves at constant velocity relative to another, both measure the **same acceleration** for any object, so $\vec F = m\vec a$ holds equally in both: every frame moving at constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is itself inertial. If a frame accelerates with $\vec A$, objects in it seem to feel an extra **fictitious force** $-m\vec A$, the push back into your seat when a car speeds up. No real body exerts that force; it only appears because the frame is accelerating.

## The second law: force changes momentum

Newton described motion through the **momentum** $\vec p = m\vec v$. A force acting for a short time $\Delta t$ changes the momentum by $\Delta\vec p = \vec F\,\Delta t$, the **impulse**. In the limit of a very short time:

<Formula label="Newton's second law" tex="\vec F = \frac{d\vec p}{dt} \qquad\text{and, for constant mass,}\qquad \vec F = m\,\frac{d\vec v}{dt} = m\,\vec a" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 7.3, Eqs. (7.3.1)–(7.3.5)" />

Here $\vec F$ is the **total** force, the vector sum of every force on the body. This equation is more than a definition, because each kind of force (gravity, springs, electric forces) has its own **force law** found by experiment. Put a force law into $\vec F = m\vec a$ and you can predict motion.

<WorkedExample title="Two forces on a puck">
<Problem>
A $2.0$ kg puck rests on frictionless ice. Two people pull on it with horizontal ropes: $\vec F_1 = (6.0\ \text{N})\,\hat i$ and $\vec F_2 = (-2.0\ \text{N})\,\hat i + (4.0\ \text{N})\,\hat j$. Find its acceleration and its velocity after $3.0$ s.
</Problem>
<Step title="Add the forces as vectors">
$\vec F_\text{net} = (6.0 - 2.0)\,\hat i + (0 + 4.0)\,\hat j = (4.0\,\hat i + 4.0\,\hat j)$ N. (Gravity and the ice's upward push cancel, so they are left out.)
</Step>
<Step title="Apply the second law">
$\vec a = \vec F_\text{net}/m = (2.0\,\hat i + 2.0\,\hat j)\ \text{m/s}^2$, with magnitude $2.83\ \text{m/s}^2$, pointing at $45^\circ$ between the $+x$ and $+y$ axes.
</Step>
<Step title="Velocity after 3.0 s">
The acceleration is constant and the puck starts at rest, so $\vec v = \vec a\,t = (6.0\,\hat i + 6.0\,\hat j)\ \text{m/s}$, a speed of $8.5\ \text{m/s}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The third law: forces come in pairs

<Formula label="Newton's third law" tex="\vec F_{1,2} = -\vec F_{2,1}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 7.4, Eq. (7.4.1)" />

Here $\vec F_{1,2}$ means the force **on** body 2 **due to** body 1. Every force is one half of an interaction: if you push on a wall, the wall pushes back on you just as hard. The two forces act on *different* bodies, so they never cancel each other out in one body's equation of motion.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Equal forces, different accelerations">
When a car hits a truck, the forces on the two are equal in size. The car is changed far more because $a = F/m$ is larger for the smaller mass.
</Callout>

## Where Newton's laws stop working

The MIT notes stress that Newtonian mechanics is a model with limits. At speeds approaching the speed of light it is replaced by special relativity. For electrons in atoms it is replaced by quantum mechanics. And for the structure of the universe on the largest scales, it is replaced by general relativity. Those are exactly the subjects of later years on this site.

<Callout kind="cosmology">
In an expanding universe, which observers are inertial? In 8.286 Alan Guth argues that an observer who stays at rest in the comoving coordinates of the expanding universe is, locally, an inertial observer. Because the speed of light does not depend on the motion of its source, light passes every such observer at speed $c$. In comoving coordinates this becomes

<Formula label="Light seen by comoving observers" tex="\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{c}{a(t)}" cite="guth4" />

The first law, and the idea of an inertial frame, carry straight over into cosmology.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A force is defined by the acceleration it produces; forces add as vectors.
- Mass measures inertia: under the same force, $m_\text{in}/m_s = a_s/a_\text{in}$.
- First law: no net force means constant velocity (and defines inertial frames).
- Second law: $\vec F = d\vec p/dt = m\vec a$, with $\vec F$ the total force.
- Third law: $\vec F_{1,2} = -\vec F_{2,1}$; the pair acts on two different bodies.
