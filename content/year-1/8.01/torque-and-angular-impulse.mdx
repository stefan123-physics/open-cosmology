Newton's second law says force changes momentum. This lesson shows the rotational counterpart: torque changes angular momentum (34.1 transcript, p. 1), and when there is no torque, angular momentum is conserved (notes, Section 19.4, p. 19-10).

## Torque changes angular momentum: one particle

For a particle with momentum $\vec p$ and a fixed point S, $\vec L_S = \vec r_S \times \vec p$ (34.1 transcript, p. 1). Differentiate with the product rule. The rate of change of $\vec r_S$ is the velocity $\vec v$, whatever S is, and $\vec v \times m\vec v = 0$; in the other term $d\vec p/dt = \vec F$ by the second law (p. 1; notes, Eqs. (19.3.3)–(19.3.8), p. 19-9):

<Formula label="Torque and angular momentum: a particle" tex="\frac{d\vec L_S}{dt} = \frac{d\vec r_S}{dt} \times \vec p + \vec r_S \times \frac{d\vec p}{dt} = \vec v \times m\vec v + \vec r_S \times \vec F = \vec\tau_S" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

The torque about S causes the angular momentum about S to change (p. 2; notes, Eq. (19.3.9), p. 19-9).

## A system of particles

For $N$ particles, $\vec L_S = \sum_j \vec r_{S,j} \times \vec p_j$, and the same steps give $\frac{d\vec L_S}{dt} = \sum_j \vec r_{S,j} \times \vec F_j$, the sum of the torques on all the particles (34.2 transcript, p. 1; notes, Eqs. (19.5.2)–(19.5.6), pp. 19-13–19-14). The forces include internal ones, but if each internal pair acts along the line joining the two particles, their torques cancel in pairs (p. 1), as shown in an [earlier lesson](/courses/8-01/torque-and-angular-acceleration). What remains is (p. 2; notes, Eq. (19.5.9), p. 19-14):

<Formula label="Torque and angular momentum: a system" tex="\vec\tau_S^{\,\text{ext}} = \frac{d\vec L_S^{\,\text{sys}}}{dt}" cite="reading3" at="p. 2" />

For rotation about a fixed axis its component along the axis is the familiar $\tau_{S,z} = I_S\alpha_z$ (notes, Eqs. (19.5.20)–(19.5.29), pp. 19-18–19-19).

## Angular impulse

Integrating Newton's second law over time gives the impulse: $\int\vec F\,dt' = \vec p(t) - \vec p(0)$, so zero impulse means unchanged momentum (34.3 transcript, p. 1). In the same way, the **angular impulse** about S is the time integral of the torque about S (pp. 1–2; notes, Eqs. (19.4.1)–(19.4.2), p. 19-12):

<Formula label="Angular impulse" tex="\vec J_S = \int_{t_i}^{t_f}\vec\tau_S\,dt = \vec L_{S,f} - \vec L_{S,i}" cite="reading4" at="p. 2" />

If the angular impulse is zero, the angular momentum about S stays the same (p. 2). One difference from momentum: angular momentum and torque both depend on the chosen point S (p. 2). For a system, the external angular impulse equals the change in the system's angular momentum (notes, Section 19.9, Eqs. (19.5.47)–(19.5.48), p. 19-26).

## Conservation of angular momentum

If a point S can be found about which the external torque is zero, then the angular momentum of the system about S is constant (notes, Sections 19.4 and 19.8, Eqs. (19.3.14)–(19.3.16), p. 19-10, and (19.5.35)–(19.5.36), p. 19-21):

<Formula label="Conservation of angular momentum" tex="\vec\tau_S^{\,\text{ext}} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vec L_{S,f}^{\,\text{sys}} = \vec L_{S,i}^{\,\text{sys}}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (19.5.36), p. 19-21" />

The notes add that no isolated system has ever been found whose angular momentum changes, so the assumption that internal torques cancel can be taken as an experimental fact (notes, p. 19-21). For central forces, S is the centre of force (34.5 transcript, p. 1); segment 34.4 demonstrates the principle with a spinning bicycle wheel (a video without a transcript).

## A particle hits a pivoted ring

Seen from above, a ring of mass $m_1$ and radius $r$ is pinned at a point P on its rim, about which it can turn (take it to lie on a frictionless table). An object of mass $m_2$ slides in with speed $v_i$ along a line a distance $r$ from P, hits the ring and sticks to it, at a point a distance $\sqrt 2\,r$ from P (34.5 transcript, pp. 1–3). During the collision the pivot pushes with some unknown force (p. 1). Choose P as the point: the pivot force has no torque about P, and with ring and object as one system the collision forces are internal, so the angular momentum about P is constant (pp. 1–2). Before, it is just the object's, $m_2v_ir$ (moment arm $r$). After, everything turns rigidly about P with $\omega_f$, and by the parallel axis theorem the ring's moment of inertia about P is $m_1r^2 + m_1r^2$, the object's $m_2(\sqrt 2\,r)^2$ (pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Particle hits pivoted ring" tex="m_2v_ir = \left(2m_1r^2 + 2m_2r^2\right)\omega_f \quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega_f = \frac{m_2v_i}{2(m_1 + m_2)\,r}" cite="reading5" at="pp. 3–4" />

(On p. 3 the transcript says "angular momentum about the center of mass" where it means the moment of inertia.) The momentum of the system is **not** conserved here, because the pivot force is an external force; only the angular momentum about the pivot is. The notes do the same with a pivoted rod (notes, Example 19.7, pp. 19-21–19-26).

<WorkedExample title="A spun-up wheel and a ball that sticks to a rod">
<Problem>
(a) A torque of 2.0 N m acts for 0.50 s on a wheel at rest with $I = 0.25$ kg m². Find its angular velocity afterwards. (b) A uniform 1.2 kg rod of length $\ell = 1.0$ m hangs from a frictionless pivot at its top end. A 0.20 kg ball of clay moving horizontally at 6.0 m/s hits the bottom end and sticks. Find the angular velocity just after, and the kinetic energy lost.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Angular impulse">
$J = 2.0 \times 0.50 = 1.0$ kg m²/s $= I\omega - 0$, so $\omega = 1.0/0.25 = 4.0$ rad/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) About the pivot">
Before: $L = mv\ell = 0.20 \times 6.0 \times 1.0 = 1.2$ kg m²/s. After: $I = \frac{1}{3}M\ell^2 + m\ell^2 = 0.40 + 0.20 = 0.60$ kg m², so $\omega = 1.2/0.60 = 2.0$ rad/s. The kinetic energy drops from $\frac{1}{2} \times 0.20 \times 6.0^2 = 3.6$ J to $\frac{1}{2} \times 0.60 \times 2.0^2 = 1.2$ J: two thirds is lost in the sticky collision.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="Equal areas in equal times">
Gravity from the Sun points straight at the Sun, so it has no torque about the Sun, and a planet's angular momentum about the Sun stays the same. Its magnitude, $m r v_\perp$, is $2m$ times the rate at which the line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out area, so equal areas are swept in equal times: Kepler's second law.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\vec\tau_S = d\vec L_S/dt$ for a particle; for a system, with internal torques cancelling in pairs, $\vec\tau_S^{\,\text{ext}} = d\vec L_S^{\,\text{sys}}/dt$.
- Angular impulse: $\int\vec\tau_S\,dt = \Delta\vec L_S$, the analogue of $\int\vec F\,dt = \Delta\vec p$; both depend on the point S.
- If the external torque about S is zero, $\vec L_S^{\,\text{sys}}$ is conserved.
- For a pivoted body, take S at the pivot: the unknown pivot force drops out, while momentum is not conserved.
