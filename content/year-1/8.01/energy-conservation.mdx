The [last lesson](/courses/8-01/potential-energy) introduced potential energy and the mechanical energy $K + U$. This one puts them to work: what non-conservative forces do to the mechanical energy, a systematic method for energy problems, and when energy alone is not enough.

## Non-conservative work changes mechanical energy

The total mechanical energy is $E_\text{mech} = K + U$, with $U$ the sum of the potential energies of all conservative forces, each with its own zero point (24.1 transcript, p. 1). The total work is conservative plus non-conservative, and it equals the change in kinetic energy: $W_c + W_{nc} = \Delta K$. Since $W_c = -\Delta U$ (p. 1; notes, Eqs. (14.1)–(14.7), p. 14-21):

<Formula label="Non-conservative work" tex="W_{nc} = \Delta K + \Delta U = \Delta E_\text{mech}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The **only** thing that changes the mechanical energy is non-conservative work (p. 1). With only conservative forces, $\Delta E_\text{mech} = 0$: mechanical energy is **conserved** (p. 2).

## Where does the energy go?

Non-conservative work is usually negative, removing mechanical energy (24.1 transcript, p. 2). With friction, it reappears as **non-mechanical** energy, loosely called heat, shared between the sliding object and the surface; where you draw the boundary of the system decides what counts as external (notes, Section 14.8, p. 14-22). Counting both kinds, the **total** energy of a system is conserved even with non-conservative forces, but the heat is hard to turn back into mechanical energy: these processes are **irreversible**, the subject of thermodynamics and statistical mechanics (p. 2; notes, Eq. (14.8), p. 14-22). In 8.01, non-conservative forces are treated simply as removing (or adding) mechanical energy (p. 3).

The notes add a twist: when you start walking, static friction does no work on you, yet your kinetic energy grows. Chemical energy from your body becomes kinetic and thermal energy (notes, Section 14.8.1, p. 14-23).

## Energy state diagrams

A method for using $W_{nc} = \Delta K + \Delta U$ (24.2 transcript, pp. 1–3). The example: an object starts at rest at the top of a smooth dome of radius $R$ (the lecture has MIT's dome in mind) and slides down.

1. **Choose the initial and final states**, and draw each: here at the top, and at an angle $\theta_f$ from the vertical.
2. **Choose a zero point** for each potential energy and mark it on the drawings: here $U = 0$ at the level of the dome's centre.
3. **Find $K$ and $U$ in each state:** $K_i = 0$, $U_i = mgR$; $K_f = \tfrac12 m v_f^2$, $U_f = mgR\cos\theta_f$.
4. **Find the non-conservative work:** the dome is frictionless, so $W_{nc} = 0$.
5. **Apply the energy principle**, every term now written out (pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Dome: energy principle" tex="0 = \left(\tfrac12 m v_f^2 - 0\right) + \left(mgR\cos\theta_f - mgR\right)" cite="reading3" at="pp. 2–3" />

## A ramp, rough patch and spring

A worked example (24.3 transcript, pp. 1–4). A block starts at rest at height $h$ on a smooth curved ramp, crosses a rough stretch of length $d$ whose friction coefficient grows along it, $\mu = \mu_0 + \mu_1 x$, and then compresses a spring on a smooth floor until it stops. How far is the spring compressed? Choose the initial state at the top and the final state with the spring compressed by $u_f$; zero points: $U_\text{grav} = 0$ at the floor, $U_\text{spring} = 0$ at the spring's relaxed length. Then $E_i = mgh$ and $E_f = \tfrac12 k u_f^2$. Friction is $\mu N = (\mu_0 + \mu_1 x)\,mg$, so its work is an integral (p. 3):

<Formula label="Work of a varying friction" tex="W_{nc} = -\int_0^d (\mu_0 + \mu_1 x')\,mg\,dx' = -\mu_0 mgd - \mu_1 mg\frac{d^2}{2}" cite="reading4" at="p. 3" />

Setting $W_{nc} = E_f - E_i$ and solving (pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Spring compression" tex="u_f = \sqrt{\frac{2}{k}\left(mgh - \mu_0 mgd - \mu_1 mg\frac{d^2}{2}\right)}" cite="reading4" at="pp. 3–4" />

There is no need to split the motion into stages: pick the two states, describe each, and apply the principle once (p. 4). (The transcript writes the principle with "W external": with the block alone as the system, friction is an external force, as in the notes, Section 14.7.1. It also drops the ½ in $\tfrac12 k u_f^2$ for one line on p. 3; the final answer has it.)

<WorkedExample title="Down a ramp, across a rough patch, into a spring">
<Problem>
A 2.0 kg block is released from rest 1.5 m up a smooth curved ramp. At the bottom it crosses 2.0 m of floor with $\mu = 0.10 + 0.05\,x$ ($x$ in metres), then hits a spring with $k = 500$ N/m on smooth floor. How far is the spring compressed?
</Problem>
<Step title="Energies">
$E_i = mgh = 2.0 \times 9.8 \times 1.5 = 29.4$ J; $E_f = \tfrac12 k u_f^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Friction">
$W_{nc} = -mg\left(0.10 \times 2.0 + 0.05 \times \tfrac{2.0^2}{2}\right) = -19.6 \times 0.30 \approx -5.88$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Compression">
$\tfrac12 \times 500\,u_f^2 = 29.4 - 5.88 = 23.52$ J, so $u_f \approx 0.31$ m.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Energy is not the whole story

When should you use energy and when Newton's second law? For circular motion you need **both** (24.4 transcript, pp. 1–3). On the smooth dome, the normal force is perpendicular to the displacement, so it does no work and simply does not appear in the energy principle (p. 1). The energy principle is Newton's second law **integrated along the direction of motion**: only gravity's tangential component, $mg\sin\theta$, works (p. 2). Information about forces perpendicular to the motion is lost, so the radial component of Newton's second law must be added separately (p. 2; notes, Eq. (14.35), p. 14-32):

<Formula label="Radial Newton's law on the dome" tex="N - mg\cos\theta = -\frac{m v^2}{R}" cite="reading5" at="p. 2" />

The notes do the full problem: when does the object leave the dome? It leaves when $N = 0$, so $v_1^2 = gR\cos\theta_1$; energy gives $\tfrac12 m v_1^2 + mgR\cos\theta_1 = mgR$; together $\cos\theta_1 = \tfrac23$ (notes, Example 14.6, Eqs. (14.34)–(14.44), pp. 14-31–14-33):

<Formula label="Where the object leaves the sphere" tex="\theta_1 = \cos^{-1}\tfrac23 \approx 48.2^\circ, \qquad v_1 = \sqrt{\tfrac23 gR}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (14.43)–(14.44), p. 14-33" />

<WorkedExample title="Sliding off a snow dome">
<Problem>
A child on a frictionless sled slides from rest off the top of a hemispherical snow dome of radius 6.0 m. Where does the sled leave the surface, and how fast is it going?
</Problem>
<Step title="Angle">
$\cos\theta = \tfrac23$: $\theta \approx 48.2°$ from the vertical, after dropping $R - R\cos\theta = R/3 = 2.0$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed">
$v = \sqrt{2gR/3} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 6.0/3} \approx 6.3$ m/s, the same as falling 2.0 m. It does not depend on the mass.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The Friedmann equation is energy conservation">
In the Newtonian model of 8.286, a galaxy on the edge of an expanding uniform sphere feels only gravity, a conservative force, and nothing does non-conservative work on it. So its mechanical energy is constant: $\tfrac12\dot r^2 - GM/r$ per unit mass never changes. Written with the scale factor, that statement is the Friedmann equation for a matter-filled universe, and the sign of the conserved energy decides whether the expansion goes on forever.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $W_{nc} = \Delta K + \Delta U = \Delta E_\text{mech}$: only non-conservative work changes the mechanical energy; without it, $E_\text{mech}$ is conserved.
- Friction turns mechanical energy into heat; total energy is still conserved, but the process is irreversible.
- Energy state diagrams: states, zero points, $K$ and $U$ in each state, $W_{nc}$, then the energy principle.
- Energy is Newton's second law integrated along the motion; for circular motion add the radial equation, as for an object leaving a smooth sphere at $\cos\theta = \tfrac23$.
