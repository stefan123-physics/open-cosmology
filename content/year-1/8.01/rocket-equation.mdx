So far a system has kept its mass, or changed it in one sudden event, like the person jumping off the cart in the [previous lesson](/courses/8-01/relative-velocity-and-recoil). A rocket throws its fuel out **continuously**: its mass changes all the time, and so does the speed of the fuel it throws out. This lesson turns the momentum principle into a differential equation for that case, the **rocket equation**.

## Choosing the states

A rocket starts at rest, burns fuel and throws it backwards; the thrust pushes it up (19.1 transcript, p. 1). Its mass at the start is the **dry mass** plus all the fuel. Later the mass is smaller and the speed larger. But you cannot simply compare start and end, as in a recoil problem: during the burn, fuel kept leaving at a speed fixed relative to a rocket that was itself speeding up (p. 1).

The way out is to compare **two states a short time apart**, at $t$ and $t + \Delta t$, and then let $\Delta t \to 0$ (pp. 1–2). Choose a reference frame (here the ground) and, as the **system**, everything that is in the rocket at time $t$: dry mass plus the fuel still on board. The system keeps that mass between $t$ and $t + \Delta t$, even though part of it has been thrown out by then (p. 2).

The notes put this into a general setting: material can flow into an object or out of it, with or without carrying momentum along the direction of motion (rain falling into a moving cart, sand leaking from a skater's bag, a fire hose pushing a boat, fuel leaving a rocket), and in each case you must keep track of the material that flows during $\Delta t$ (notes, Section 12.1, pp. 12-2–12-4).

## Momentum at the two states

The momentum principle, with the derivative written as a limit (19.2 transcript, p. 1; notes, Eq. (12.2.2), p. 12-3):

<Formula label="Momentum principle as a limit" tex="\vec F_\text{ext} = \lim_{\Delta t\to 0}\frac{\vec p_\text{sys}(t + \Delta t) - \vec p_\text{sys}(t)}{\Delta t}" cite="reading3" at="p. 1" />

At time $t$ the system is the rocket with mass $m_r(t)$ and velocity $\vec v_r(t)$, so $\vec p_\text{sys}(t) = m_r(t)\,\vec v_r(t)$ (p. 1). At $t + \Delta t$ there are two parts: the rocket, with less mass and velocity $\vec v_r(t + \Delta t)$, and the ejected fuel $\Delta m_f$. The fuel leaves with velocity $\vec u$ **relative to the rocket**, so in the ground frame it moves with $\vec u + \vec v_r(t + \Delta t)$ (p. 2): the addition of velocities from Lesson 18.

## Mass relations

Two pieces of notation tidy this up (19.3 transcript, p. 1). Write $m_r$ for $m_r(t)$ and $\Delta m_r = m_r(t + \Delta t) - m_r(t)$ for the change, always defined as "later minus earlier", even though it is **negative** here. The system's mass does not change, so the fuel thrown out is exactly what the rocket lost (notes, Eq. (12.3.72), p. 12-20):

<Formula label="Conservation of mass" tex="\Delta m_f = -\Delta m_r" cite="reading4" at="p. 1" />

Then (19.3 transcript, p. 1; notes, Eq. (12.3.73), p. 12-20)

<Formula label="Momentum at the two states" tex="\vec p_\text{sys}(t) = m_r\,\vec v_r(t), \qquad \vec p_\text{sys}(t + \Delta t) = (m_r + \Delta m_r)\,\vec v_r(t + \Delta t) - \Delta m_r\,\big(\vec u + \vec v_r(t + \Delta t)\big)" cite="reading4" at="p. 1" />

## The rocket equation

Put both into the limit (19.4 transcript, pp. 1–3). The two terms $\Delta m_r\,\vec v_r(t + \Delta t)$ cancel, and what remains is $m_r$ times the change in velocity over $\Delta t$, which becomes $d\vec v_r/dt$, minus $\vec u$ times $\Delta m_r/\Delta t$, which becomes $dm_r/dt$:

<Formula label="The rocket equation" tex="\vec F_\text{ext} = m_r\,\frac{d\vec v_r}{dt} - \frac{dm_r}{dt}\,\vec u" cite="reading5" at="pp. 2–3" />

This is the rocket equation (pp. 2–3; notes, Eq. (12.3.75), p. 12-20), the "rocket science" of the saying.

## Thrust

Move the second term to the other side (19.5 transcript, pp. 1–2). If you now think of the system as **just the rocket**, $m_r\,d\vec v_r/dt$ is its mass times its acceleration, and it is pushed by the external force (gravity, say) plus a new term, the **thrust**. For a rocket going up ($\hat j$ up), the exhaust velocity is $-u\,\hat j$ with $u > 0$ (the transcripts read "minus mu j-hat" here and in 19.7, for $-u\,\hat j$), and since $dm_f/dt = -dm_r/dt$,

<Formula label="Rocket equation in components, with thrust" tex="-m_r g + \frac{dm_f}{dt}\,u = m_r\,\frac{dv_{r,y}}{dt}" cite="reading6" at="pp. 1–2" />

The thrust $\frac{dm_f}{dt}u$ points forwards: the rate at which fuel is burned times its speed relative to the rocket. The notes stress that it is not an extra force from outside but the forward recoil from throwing out the fuel (notes, Eq. (12.3.79), pp. 12-20–12-21).

## No external force

Far from any planet, $\vec F_\text{ext} = 0$ (19.6 transcript, pp. 1–2). With $+\hat i$ forwards and the exhaust at $-u\,\hat i$, the equation becomes $-\frac{dm_r}{dt}u = m_r\frac{dv_r}{dt}$. Without a model of how fast the fuel burns you cannot find $v_r(t)$, but you can eliminate time: multiply by $dt$, **separate the variables** and integrate from the initial to the final state:

<Formula label="Separated" tex="-u\,\frac{dm_r}{m_r} = dv_r \;\Rightarrow\; v_{r,f} - v_{r,i} = -u\ln\frac{m_{r,f}}{m_{r,i}} = u\ln\frac{m_{r,i}}{m_{r,f}}" cite="reading7" at="pp. 1–2" />

The mass decreases, so the logarithm of $m_{r,f}/m_{r,i}$ is negative and the speed increases, as it should (p. 2; notes, Eqs. (12.3.80)–(12.3.85), pp. 12-21–12-22; the notes write "$\ln(m_{r,i}/m_{r,f}) > 1$" where they mean $> 0$). If all the fuel is burned, the final mass is the dry mass and the gain depends only on the **mass ratio** $R = m_{r,i}/m_{r,d}$ (notes, Eqs. (12.3.86)–(12.3.87), p. 12-22):

<Formula label="Speed gained, all fuel burned" tex="v_{r,f} = v_{r,i} + u\ln R" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (12.3.87), p. 12-22" />

The notes' examples: a rocket of $2.81\times10^7$ kg, of which $2.46\times10^7$ kg is fuel, ejected at 3000 m/s, has $R = 8.03$ and reaches $u\ln R \approx 6250$ m/s from rest (notes, Example 12.4, Single-Stage Rocket, p. 12-23). Burn the same fuel in **two stages**, dropping the empty first-stage tank ($1.4\times10^6$ kg) in between, and the stages give $3000\ln 3.60 \approx 3840$ m/s and $3000\ln 3.05 \approx 3340$ m/s, together about 7190 m/s (Example 12.5, pp. 12-23–12-24). That is why rockets have stages: carrying an empty tank wastes thrust.

<WorkedExample title="The tyranny of the logarithm">
<Problem>
(a) A probe in empty space has a mass ratio of 4 and an exhaust speed of 2500 m/s. What speed does it gain? (b) What fraction of a rocket's starting mass must be fuel for it to reach twice its exhaust speed from rest?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a)">
$\Delta v = 2500 \ln 4 \approx 3470$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b)">
$u\ln R = 2u$ gives $R = e^2 \approx 7.39$, so the dry mass is $1/7.39$ of the start mass and the fuel $1 - e^{-2} \approx 0.86$, about 86%. Each further step of $u$ in speed multiplies the mass ratio by another $e \approx 2.72$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## In a gravitational field

Launching from the ground, the external force is gravity, $-m_r g\,\hat j$ (19.7 transcript, pp. 1–3). Divide the rocket equation by $m_r$ and multiply by $dt$: $dv_r = -g\,dt - u\,\frac{dm_r}{m_r}$. Integrate from launch at rest, with mass $m_{r,i}$ = dry mass + all the fuel, to burn-out at $t_f$, when only the dry mass is left (pp. 1–2; notes, Eqs. (12.3.96)–(12.3.101), pp. 12-24–12-25):

<Formula label="Burn-out speed in constant gravity" tex="v_r(t_f) = u\ln\frac{m_{r,d} + m_f}{m_{r,d}} - g\,t_f = u\ln R - g\,t_f" cite="reading8" at="p. 2" />

The first term does not depend on how fast the fuel burns; the second does. A long burn wastes speed fighting gravity, so the fastest final speed comes from the **shortest burn** (pp. 2–3). Before burn-out, the same integration gives $v_r(t) = u\ln\big(m_{r,i}/m_r(t)\big) - gt$; if the fuel burns at a constant rate $b$, the mass falls linearly, $m_r(t) = m_{r,i} - bt$ (notes, Eqs. (12.3.88)–(12.3.91) and (12.3.100), pp. 12-22 and 12-25).

<WorkedExample title="A small launcher">
<Problem>
A rocket of 50 000 kg, 40 000 kg of it fuel, burns its fuel at a steady rate for 120 s with exhaust speed 2800 m/s, rising vertically from rest ($g = 9.8$ m/s²; ignore air resistance). Does it lift off at once, and how fast is it going at burn-out?
</Problem>
<Step title="Thrust at launch">
The burn rate is $40\,000/120 \approx 333$ kg/s, so the thrust is $333 \times 2800 \approx 9.33\times10^5$ N, against a weight of $50\,000 \times 9.8 = 4.9\times10^5$ N. It lifts off at once, with acceleration $(9.33 - 4.9)\times10^5/50\,000 \approx 8.9$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Burn-out speed">
$R = 50\,000/10\,000 = 5$, so $v = 2800\ln 5 - 9.8 \times 120 \approx 4506 - 1176 \approx 3330$ m/s. In empty space it would have reached 4506 m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- For continuous mass transfer, apply the momentum principle between states at $t$ and $t + \Delta t$, with the system fixed: rocket plus everything it contains at $t$.
- The ejected fuel moves at $\vec u + \vec v_r$ in the ground frame; mass conservation gives $\Delta m_f = -\Delta m_r$.
- Rocket equation: $\vec F_\text{ext} = m_r\,d\vec v_r/dt - (dm_r/dt)\,\vec u$. The thrust, $(dm_f/dt)\,u$ forwards, is the recoil from the ejected fuel.
- No external force: $v_{r,f} - v_{r,i} = u\ln(m_{r,i}/m_{r,f})$; with all fuel burned, $u\ln R$. Staging raises $R$ for the later stages.
- In constant gravity from rest: $v(t_f) = u\ln R - g t_f$, so burn fast.
