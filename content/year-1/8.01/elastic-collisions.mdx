[Types of collision](/courses/8-01/types-of-collision) ended with two equations for an elastic collision: momentum and kinetic energy. This lesson solves them, first the long way, then with a trick that turns the quadratic into a linear rule about relative velocity, and finally looks at collisions in two dimensions.

## The long way: a quadratic

Take the **laboratory frame**, in which the target, object 2, is at rest and object 1 comes in along $\hat i$ on a frictionless surface (27.1 transcript, p. 1). Let $m_2 = 2m_1$. With $v_{2x,i} = 0$, the halves and the $m_1$ cancel from energy and momentum (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Energy and momentum for m₂ = 2m₁" tex="v_{1x,i}^2 = v_{1x,f}^2 + 2v_{2x,f}^2, \qquad v_{1x,i} = v_{1x,f} + 2v_{2x,f}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

Two equations, two unknowns, but one of them squared. Solve the momentum equation for $v_{2x,f} = \frac{1}{2}(v_{1x,i} - v_{1x,f})$, substitute into the energy equation and collect terms (pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="The quadratic for v₁ₓ,f" tex="v_{1x,f}^2 - \frac{2}{3}v_{1x,i}\,v_{1x,f} - \frac{1}{3}v_{1x,i}^2 = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

(The transcript says "divide through by 2/3"; it means dividing by 3/2, p. 3.) The quadratic formula gives two roots, $v_{1x,f} = v_{1x,i}$ and $v_{1x,f} = -\frac{1}{3}v_{1x,i}$ (p. 4). The first just repeats the initial state: put it into the momentum equation and $v_{2x,f} = 0$. One root always describes the start, the other the end (p. 4). So cart 1 bounces back at a third of its speed and

<Formula label="Result for m₂ = 2m₁" tex="v_{1x,f} = -\frac{1}{3}v_{1x,i}, \qquad v_{2x,f} = \frac{2}{3}v_{1x,i}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 4–5" />

as in the notes' Example 15.1 (notes, Eq. (15.5.1), pp. 15-12–15-13). Getting the initial state back as one root is itself a check on the algebra (27.1 transcript, p. 5). Energy and momentum together always lead to a quadratic somewhere; there is an easier way (p. 5).

## Relative velocity

The **relative velocity** of object 1 with respect to object 2 is (27.2 transcript, p. 1; notes, Eq. (15.4.8), p. 15-8):

<Formula label="Relative velocity" tex="\vec v_{12} = \vec v_1 - \vec v_2" cite="reading3" at="p. 1" />

It is what you notice on a motorway: a car at 60 mph overtaking one at 50 mph seems, from the slower car, to approach at only 10 mph (p. 1). If instead the two move towards each other at the same speed, $v_{2x} = -v_{1x}$, the relative velocity is $2v_{1x}$: twice either speed (p. 2). The relative velocity is the same in every inertial frame, because a frame's own velocity cancels in the difference (notes, Section 15.2.1, pp. 15-3–15-4).

## The energy-momentum principle

Now let both objects move, with any masses (27.3 transcript, p. 1). Move every $m_1$ term to one side and every $m_2$ term to the other, in both equations (p. 2; notes, Eqs. (15.4.3)–(15.4.4), p. 15-8):

<Formula label="Momentum, rearranged" tex="m_1(v_{1x,i} - v_{1x,f}) = m_2(v_{2x,f} - v_{2x,i})" cite="reading4" at="p. 2" />

The energy equation has a difference of squares on each side, and $a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b)$ (p. 2):

<Formula label="Energy, factored" tex="m_1(v_{1x,i} - v_{1x,f})(v_{1x,i} + v_{1x,f}) = m_2(v_{2x,f} - v_{2x,i})(v_{2x,f} + v_{2x,i})" cite="reading4" at="p. 2" />

The momentum factors appear on both sides, so dividing one equation by the other cancels them and leaves a linear equation, with no masses in it (pp. 2–3; notes, Eq. (15.4.6), p. 15-8):

<Formula label="Linear equation" tex="v_{1x,i} + v_{1x,f} = v_{2x,f} + v_{2x,i}" cite="reading4" at="pp. 2–3" />

Rearranged, it says something about relative velocity (p. 3):

<Formula label="Energy-momentum principle" tex="v_{1x,i} - v_{2x,i} = -(v_{1x,f} - v_{2x,f})" cite="reading4" at="p. 3" />

In a one-dimensional elastic collision, **the relative velocity keeps its size and reverses its direction**, whatever the masses (p. 3). MIT calls this the energy-momentum principle, because it combines the two (notes, Eq. (15.4.9), p. 15-9). (Dividing assumed the collision actually happened: if nothing changes, both sides are zero.)

## Solving it the easy way

Use the energy-momentum principle with momentum: two **linear** equations (27.4 transcript, p. 1). (On p. 1 the transcript drops the minus sign when recalling the principle, and the $m_1$ in the momentum equation.) For $m_2 = 2m_1$ and the target at rest:

<Formula label="The linear system for m₂ = 2m₁" tex="v_{1x,i} = -v_{1x,f} + v_{2x,f}, \qquad v_{1x,i} = v_{1x,f} + 2v_{2x,f}" cite="reading5" at="p. 1" />

Adding them gives $2v_{1x,i} = 3v_{2x,f}$, so $v_{2x,f} = \frac{2}{3}v_{1x,i}$, and then $v_{1x,f} = -\frac{1}{3}v_{1x,i}$: the same result without a quadratic (p. 2). Check: the relative velocity after is $-\frac{1}{3}v_{1x,i} - \frac{2}{3}v_{1x,i} = -v_{1x,i}$, the reverse of the relative velocity before (p. 2).

Doing the same with letters gives the general result (notes, Eqs. (15.4.14) and (15.4.17), pp. 15-9–15-10):

<Formula label="Final velocities" tex="v_{1x,f} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{1x,i} + \frac{2m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{2x,i}, \qquad v_{2x,f} = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{1x,i} + \frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{2x,i}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (15.4.14), (15.4.17), pp. 15-9–15-10" />

Three cases are worth knowing (notes, p. 15-10):

- **Equal masses** swap velocities: $v_{1x,f} = v_{2x,i}$ and $v_{2x,f} = v_{1x,i}$ (Eq. (15.4.21)). A moving cart hitting an identical one at rest stops dead.
- **A heavy object hitting a light one** hardly changes its velocity (Eq. (15.4.18)).
- **A light object hitting a heavy one** bounces off with the same speed relative to the heavy object (Eqs. (15.4.19)–(15.4.20)).

<WorkedExample title="Two elastic collisions on a track">
<Problem>
(a) A 0.50 kg cart moving at 4.0 m/s hits a 1.5 kg cart at rest, elastically. (b) A 2.0 kg cart moving at +3.0 m/s meets a 1.0 kg cart moving at −3.0 m/s, elastically. Find the final velocities in each case.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Target at rest">
Relative velocity: $4.0 = v_{2x,f} - v_{1x,f}$. Momentum: $0.50 \times 4.0 = 2.0 = 0.50\,v_{1x,f} + 1.5\,v_{2x,f}$. Substituting $v_{2x,f} = 4.0 + v_{1x,f}$ gives $2.0 = 2.0\,v_{1x,f} + 6.0$, so $v_{1x,f} = -2.0$ m/s and $v_{2x,f} = +2.0$ m/s. Check the kinetic energy: 4.0 J before, $1.0 + 3.0 = 4.0$ J after.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Both moving">
Relative velocity before: $3.0 - (-3.0) = 6.0$ m/s, so $v_{2x,f} - v_{1x,f} = 6.0$ m/s. Momentum: $2.0 \times 3.0 + 1.0 \times (-3.0) = 3.0$ kg m/s $= 2.0\,v_{1x,f} + 1.0\,v_{2x,f}$. Then $3.0 = 3.0\,v_{1x,f} + 6.0$, so $v_{1x,f} = -1.0$ m/s and $v_{2x,f} = +5.0$ m/s. The kinetic energy is 13.5 J before and after.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The gravitational slingshot

To reach the outer solar system, spacecraft borrow speed from big planets such as Jupiter or Saturn: they fly close past the planet and come away faster (27.5 transcript, p. 1). There is no contact, but gravity does the job of the collision: long before and long after, the planet's pull is negligible, and the kinetic energy of the pair is the same, so it behaves like an elastic collision. Idealise it as one-dimensional: the spacecraft arrives along $+\hat i$ with speed $v_i$, the planet moves along $-\hat i$ with speed $V$, and the spacecraft leaves along $-\hat i$ with speed $v_f$ (pp. 1–2). The planet is so much heavier that its velocity does not change (p. 2). The energy-momentum principle then gives (p. 2):

<Formula label="Slingshot" tex="v_i + V = v_f - V \;\Rightarrow\; v_f = v_i + 2V" cite="reading6" at="p. 2" />

Relative to the planet, the spacecraft arrives and leaves with the same speed, $v_i + V$; seen from the Sun it has gained twice the planet's speed. In MIT's example the spacecraft arrives at $3V$ and leaves at $5V$ (pp. 2–3). New Horizons, for example, swung past Jupiter in 2007 on its way to Pluto.

With round numbers for Jupiter, which orbits at about 13 km/s (13.1 km/s in NASA's Jupiter fact sheet): a probe arriving head-on at 10 km/s would leave at $10 + 2 \times 13 = 36$ km/s. Real flybys are not head-on and bend the path through a smaller angle, so they gain less; the planet loses the same energy the probe gains, a far too small amount to notice.

## Two-dimensional collisions

In two dimensions, take the lab frame again: object 1 comes in along $\hat i$ with speed $v_{1,i}$ and hits object 2 at rest. Afterwards object 1 leaves at speed $v_{1,f}$ and angle $\theta_{1,f}$ below the $\hat i$ axis, object 2 at speed $v_{2,f}$ and angle $\theta_{2,f}$ above it (27.6 transcript, pp. 1–2). Experiments usually measure the directions, so it is natural to use magnitudes and angles (p. 1). With no external forces, each component of momentum is constant (p. 2; notes, Eqs. (15.6.5)–(15.6.6), p. 15-18):

<Formula label="Momentum in two dimensions" tex="m_1 v_{1,i} = m_1 v_{1,f}\cos\theta_{1,f} + m_2 v_{2,f}\cos\theta_{2,f}, \qquad 0 = m_2 v_{2,f}\sin\theta_{2,f} - m_1 v_{1,f}\sin\theta_{1,f}" cite="reading7" at="pp. 2–3" />

If the collision is elastic, kinetic energy adds a third equation (p. 3; notes, Eq. (15.6.8), p. 15-18):

<Formula label="Energy in two dimensions" tex="\frac{1}{2}m_1 v_{1,i}^2 = \frac{1}{2}m_1 v_{1,f}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_2 v_{2,f}^2" cite="reading7" at="p. 3" />

But there are **four unknowns**: $v_{1,f}$, $v_{2,f}$, $\theta_{1,f}$ and $\theta_{2,f}$ (p. 3). Three equations cannot fix four unknowns, so one more quantity must be measured, typically one of the scattering angles or one final speed. Reading a problem, the first step is to see which of the four is given (pp. 3–4).

For **equal masses** with the target at rest, the result is neat: $v_{1,f} = v_{1,i}\cos\theta_{1,f}$, and the two objects leave at right angles, $\theta_{1,f} + \theta_{2,f} = 90°$ (notes, Examples 15.5 and 15.6, Eqs. (15.6.20)–(15.6.29), pp. 15-20–15-22). Billiard players know it roughly: after a glancing hit on a still ball, the two balls separate at close to a right angle (only roughly, because real balls also roll and spin).

<WorkedExample title="A glancing collision of equal pucks">
<Problem>
An air-hockey puck at 5.0 m/s strikes an identical puck at rest, elastically, and is deflected by $\theta_{1,f} = 60°$. Find both final speeds and the angle of the second puck.
</Problem>
<Step title="Use the equal-mass results">
$v_{1,f} = 5.0\cos 60° = 2.5$ m/s and $\theta_{2,f} = 90° - 60° = 30°$. The $\hat j$ equation gives $v_{2,f} = v_{1,f}\sin\theta_{1,f}/\sin\theta_{2,f} = 2.5 \times 0.866/0.5 \approx 4.33$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Along $\hat i$: $2.5\cos 60° + 4.33\cos 30° = 1.25 + 3.75 = 5.0$ m/s. Energy (per unit mass, times 2): $2.5^2 + 4.33^2 = 6.25 + 18.75 = 25 = 5.0^2$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Bouncing light in the early universe">
For its first few hundred thousand years the universe was filled with free electrons, and photons kept bouncing off them (Thomson scattering). A photon is not a little cart, but at those low energies the scattering is elastic in the same sense as here, with the electron as the heavy target: the photon changes direction and keeps almost all its energy. Because a photon could not travel far before its next bounce, the universe was opaque. Only when the electrons were captured into neutral atoms did the photons fly free: that light is the cosmic microwave background.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Energy and momentum in a one-dimensional elastic collision lead to a quadratic; one root is the initial state, the other the final state.
- The relative velocity $\vec v_{12} = \vec v_1 - \vec v_2$ is the same in every inertial frame.
- **Energy-momentum principle:** $v_{1x,i} - v_{2x,i} = -(v_{1x,f} - v_{2x,f})$; with momentum it gives two linear equations.
- Equal masses swap velocities; a light object bounces off a heavy one with its relative speed reversed.
- The slingshot: $v_f = v_i + 2V$ in the idealised head-on case.
- In two dimensions: four unknowns, three equations; equal masses with the target at rest separate at a right angle.
