## What you need from the lessons

A massless rope that runs over a massless pulley without friction has the same tension all along it, and a massless pulley has zero net force on it. A rope of fixed length ties the accelerations of the things it joins: write the length in coordinates and differentiate twice (the constraint condition).

A rope that slides, or is about to slide, round a fixed post does not have the same tension on both sides. Problem 4 uses the capstan result of the lesson on a rope with mass:

<Formula label="Capstan equation" tex="T_B = T_A\,e^{-\mu_s \theta_{BA}}" cite="reading1" at="Example 8.11, Eq. (8.6.108), p. 8-49" />

Here $T_A$ is the larger tension, on the side of the load (the side towards which the rope is about to slide), $T_B$ is the smaller tension on the other side, and $\theta_{BA}$ the angle (in radians) over which the rope touches the post.

Throughout, $g$ is the size of the acceleration of a falling object.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A block on an incline and a hanging pulley" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

Block 1, of mass $m_1$, rests on a fixed incline that makes the angle $\theta$ with the horizontal. The coefficient of static friction between the block and the incline is $\mu_s$. A massless string that cannot stretch is tied to the uphill side of the block. It runs up the incline, parallel to the surface, and over pulley 1, which is fixed at the top of the incline. From there it hangs down, passes under pulley 2 and goes straight up again to a fixed support. Pulley 2 is not fixed to anything: it hangs in the loop of the string, and the two sections of string that leave it are vertical. Both pulleys are massless. Block 2, of mass $m_2$, hangs from pulley 2. Gravity points down.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Find $m_{2,\text{min}}$, the smallest mass of block 2 at which block 1 is on the point of sliding **up** the incline. Use some or all of $m_1$, $\theta$, $\mu_s$ and $g$.

<Hint>

Nothing moves yet, so every force balance is a balance of statics. Which way does the friction force on block 1 point when the block is about to slide up?

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the point of sliding, the static friction has its largest size, $\mu_s N$. Balance the forces on block 1 along the incline to find the tension.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Then look at pulley 2 together with block 2. How many sections of string hold them up?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$m_{2,\text{min}} =$" expr="2*m1*(sin(th) + mus*cos(th))"
  vars={{ m1: [1, 3], th: [0.3, 0.8], mus: [0.2, 0.6] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", th: "\\theta", mus: "\\mu_s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m1*(sin(th) + mus*cos(th))", hint: "That is the tension divided by g. Two sections of string hold pulley 2, so each carries only half of the weight of block 2." },
    { expr: "m1*(sin(th) + mus*cos(th))/2", hint: "The factor of 2 is on the wrong side. Two sections of string hold block 2, so its weight is twice the tension, not half of it." },
    { expr: "2*m1*(sin(th) - mus*cos(th))", hint: "In your formula friction helps the string. A block that is about to slide up is held back by friction, which points down the incline, the same way as gravity along the incline." },
    { expr: "2*m1*(cos(th) + mus*sin(th))", hint: "Try a level surface without friction (th = 0, mus = 0): no hanging mass is needed there, but your formula gives 2 m1. The part of gravity along the incline goes with the sine." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At the smallest $m_2$ that makes block 1 slide up, the block is still at rest, with the static friction at its largest size and pointing down the incline. The force balance on block 1 gives the tension, and the force balance on pulley 2 with block 2 turns the tension into a mass.

<Step title="Block 1">

Take one axis up along the incline and one at right angles to it. At right angles to the incline the normal force balances a part of gravity, $N = m_1 g\cos\theta$. Along the incline the tension $T$ pulls up, and gravity and the friction $\mu_s N$ pull down:

<Formula label="Block 1 on the point of sliding up" tex="T - m_1 g\sin\theta - \mu_s\,m_1 g\cos\theta = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Pulley 2 and block 2">

The string is massless and the pulleys turn freely, so the tension is $T$ all along the string. Two sections of string pull up on pulley 2, and the weight of block 2 pulls down. At rest:

<Formula label="Pulley 2 with block 2 at rest" tex="2T - m_2 g = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The smallest mass">

Put the tension of the first balance into the second:

<Formula label="Smallest mass that pulls block 1 up" tex="m_{2,\text{min}} = \frac{2T}{g} = 2\,m_1\left(\sin\theta + \mu_s\cos\theta\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The answer is a mass, and $g$ has dropped out, as it must: every force in the balance is proportional to $g$.

On a vertical wall without friction ($\theta = \pi/2$) the string carries the whole weight of block 1, and the formula gives $m_2 = 2m_1$: block 2 hangs from two sections of string, so it must be twice as heavy to balance. More friction asks for a heavier block 2, and so does a steeper incline as long as $\tan\theta < 1/\mu_s$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find $m_{2,\text{max}}$, the largest mass of block 2 at which block 1 is on the point of sliding **down** the incline. Use some or all of $m_1$, $\theta$, $\mu_s$ and $g$.

<Hint>

Only one thing changes from part (a). Which force on block 1 turns round?

</Hint>

<Hint>

A block that is about to slide down is held back by friction that points up the incline.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$m_{2,\text{max}} =$" expr="2*m1*(sin(th) - mus*cos(th))"
  vars={{ m1: [1, 3], th: [0.7, 1.0], mus: [0.2, 0.5] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", th: "\\theta", mus: "\\mu_s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*m1*(sin(th) + mus*cos(th))", hint: "That is the answer of part (a), with friction pointing down the incline. A block that is about to slide down is held back by friction that points up the incline." },
    { expr: "m1*(sin(th) - mus*cos(th))", hint: "That is the tension divided by g. Two sections of string hold pulley 2, so each carries only half of the weight of block 2." },
    { expr: "2*m1*(mus*cos(th) - sin(th))", hint: "Check the sign with no friction (mus = 0): your mass is negative there, yet a block on a frictionless incline needs a real hanging mass to hold it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same two balances as in part (a), with the friction turned round: it now points up the incline and helps the string.

<Step title="Block 1">

<Formula label="Block 1 on the point of sliding down" tex="T + \mu_s\,m_1 g\cos\theta - m_1 g\sin\theta = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The largest mass">

With $2T = m_2 g$ for pulley 2 and block 2, as before:

<Formula label="Largest mass that lets block 1 slide down" tex="m_{2,\text{max}} = 2\,m_1\left(\sin\theta - \mu_s\cos\theta\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Without friction the two answers agree, $2m_1\sin\theta$: there is then one mass only at which the system stays at rest. With friction, block 1 stays at rest for every $m_2$ between the answer of this part and the answer of part (a).

A remark of our own, which MIT's set does not make: the formula is positive only if $\tan\theta > \mu_s$. On a gentler incline the block does not slide down even with no string at all, and no mass $m_2$ can make it do so.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={7}>

Now block 1 is sliding up the incline. The coefficient of kinetic friction between the block and the incline is $\mu_k$. Find the acceleration $a_1$ of block 1, taken positive up the incline (MIT's name for it is a with the subscript x1). Use some or all of $m_1$, $m_2$, $\theta$, $\mu_k$ and $g$.

<Hint>

There are now three unknowns: the tension and the two accelerations. Newton's second law for block 1 and for pulley 2 with block 2 gives two equations. Where does a third one come from?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The string has a fixed length. If block 1 moves a distance $d$ up the incline, how far does pulley 2 move down? Remember that both sections of string next to pulley 2 get longer.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Block 2 is accelerating, so the tension is no longer half of its weight. Write the second law for it with its own acceleration.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$a_1 =$" expr="2*g*(m2 - 2*m1*(sin(th) + muk*cos(th)))/(4*m1 + m2)"
  vars={{ m1: [1, 2], m2: [8, 12], th: [0.3, 0.8], muk: [0.1, 0.4], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2", th: "\\theta", muk: "\\mu_k" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "g*(m2 - m1*(sin(th) + muk*cos(th)))/(m1 + m2)", hint: "This is the result for two blocks joined by one string, moving equally far. Block 2 hangs from a moving pulley: two sections of string hold it, and it moves half as far as block 1." },
    { expr: "g*(m2/2 - m1*(sin(th) + muk*cos(th)))/m1", hint: "This uses a tension of half the weight of block 2. That holds only while block 2 is not accelerating. Write the second law for block 2 with its own acceleration." },
    { expr: "2*g*(m2 - 2*m1*(sin(th) - muk*cos(th)))/(4*m1 + m2)", hint: "In your formula friction speeds block 1 up. The block slides up, so the kinetic friction points down the incline." },
    { expr: "g*(m2 - 2*m1*(sin(th) + muk*cos(th)))/(4*m1 + m2)", hint: "That is the acceleration of block 2. Block 1 moves twice as far as block 2 in the same time." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Newton's second law for block 1 and for pulley 2 with block 2, plus the constraint that the string keeps its length.

<Step title="The constraint">

Let block 1 move a distance $d$ up the incline. The string between block 1 and pulley 1 gets shorter by $d$. The fixed support does not move, so that length must go to the two vertical sections next to pulley 2, which share it equally: pulley 2 and block 2 move down by $d/2$. The same holds for the accelerations. With $a_1$ up the incline and $a_2$ downward:

<Formula label="Constraint of the string" tex="a_2 = \tfrac12\,a_1" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The second law">

For block 1, along the incline (up positive), with the kinetic friction $\mu_k N = \mu_k m_1 g\cos\theta$ pointing down the incline. For the massless pulley 2 together with block 2, downward positive:

<Formula label="Block 1, and block 2 with its pulley" tex="T - m_1 g\sin\theta - \mu_k\,m_1 g\cos\theta = m_1 a_1, \qquad m_2 g - 2T = m_2 a_2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

Put $a_2 = a_1/2$ in the second equation and solve it for the tension: $T = \tfrac12 m_2 g - \tfrac14 m_2 a_1$. Put that in the first equation and collect the terms with $a_1$:

<Formula label="Acceleration of block 1" tex="a_1 = \frac{2g\,\big[\,m_2 - 2\,m_1\left(\sin\theta + \mu_k\cos\theta\right)\big]}{4m_1 + m_2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The fraction of masses has no unit, so the result is an acceleration.

If block 1 has almost no mass ($m_1 \to 0$), block 2 falls freely with $g$, and block 1 must go twice as fast: the formula gives $2g$.

The acceleration is zero when $m_2 = 2m_1(\sin\theta + \mu_k\cos\theta)$. That is the answer of part (a) with $\mu_k$ in place of $\mu_s$. A remark of our own: kinetic friction is usually weaker than static friction, so a block 2 that is heavy enough to start the motion keeps block 1 speeding up.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A force that depends on the velocity" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

A particle of mass $m$ moves along the $x$-axis, in the $+x$ direction. A force acts on it against its motion, and the size of the force depends on the speed $v$ of the particle:

<Formula label="The velocity-dependent force" tex="\vec F = -b\,e^{cv}\,\hat i" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

Here $b$ is a positive constant in newtons and $c$ a positive constant in seconds per metre (in this problem $c$ is not the speed of light). At $t = 0$ the speed is $v_0$. Find the speed $v(t)$ as a function of time. Use some or all of $b$, $m$, $t$, $c$ and $v_0$.

<Hint>

The force is not constant, so the formulas for constant acceleration do not hold. Write Newton's second law as an equation for $dv/dt$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Separate the variables: bring everything with $v$ to the side of $dv$ and everything else to the side of $dt$. Dividing by $e^{cv}$ is the same as multiplying by $e^{-cv}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate the left side from $v_0$ to $v$ and the right side from $0$ to $t$. Then solve for $v$ with a natural logarithm.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-v" label="$v(t) =$" expr="-log(exp(-c*v0) + b*c*t/m)/c"
  vars={{ b: [1, 3], c: [0.1, 0.3], v0: [2, 5], m: [1, 3], t: [0.02, 0.1] }}
  names={{ v0: "v_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v0 - b*exp(c*v0)*t/m", hint: "This keeps the force at the size it has at the start. The force changes as the particle slows down, so separate the variables and integrate." },
    { expr: "log(exp(c*v0) - b*c*t/m)/c", hint: "Differentiate your formula: it gives dv/dt = -(b/m) e^(-cv), but the second law asks for -(b/m) e^(+cv). When the exponential moves to the side of dv it becomes e^(-cv)." },
    { expr: "-log(exp(-c*v0) + b*t/m)/c", hint: "Check the units inside the logarithm: e^(-c v0) is a pure number, but b t/m is a speed. Integrating e^(-cv) over v brings in a factor 1/c." },
    { expr: "-log(exp(-c*v0) - b*c*t/m)/c", hint: "Your speed grows with time. The force points against the motion, so the particle slows down. Check the signs when you integrate." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Newton's second law gives a differential equation for $v(t)$. The force depends on $v$ only, so the variables separate.

<Step title="The equation of motion">

The particle moves in the $+x$ direction, so its velocity component is the speed $v$:

<Formula label="Second law for the particle" tex="m\,\frac{dv}{dt} = -b\,e^{cv}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Separate and integrate">

Multiply by $e^{-cv}\,dt/m$, and integrate from the start ($t = 0$, speed $v_0$) to the time $t$ (speed $v$):

<Formula label="Separation of variables" tex="\int_{v_0}^{v} e^{-cv'}\,dv' = -\frac{b}{m}\int_0^t dt' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -\frac{1}{c}\left(e^{-cv} - e^{-cv_0}\right) = -\frac{b}{m}\,t" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for the speed">

So $e^{-cv} = e^{-cv_0} + bct/m$. Take the natural logarithm of both sides:

<Formula label="Speed of the particle" tex="v(t) = -\frac{1}{c}\,\ln\!\left(e^{-cv_0} + \frac{b\,c\,t}{m}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $cv_0$ is a pure number, and so is $bct/m$ (N × s/m × s / kg = 1). Dividing by $c$ gives a speed.

At $t = 0$ the logarithm is $\ln e^{-cv_0} = -cv_0$, so $v = v_0$.

The number inside the logarithm grows with time, so the speed falls, as it should under a force against the motion.

For a very small $c$ the force is nearly the constant $b$. With $e^{-cv_0} \approx 1 - cv_0$ and $\ln(1 + \epsilon) \approx \epsilon$ the formula becomes $v \approx v_0 - bt/m$: constant braking.

A remark of our own, which MIT's set does not make: the speed reaches zero when the number inside the logarithm reaches 1, at the time $m\,(1 - e^{-cv_0})/(bc)$. The formula holds up to that moment only. After it the particle no longer moves, and a force "against the motion" has no direction any more.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A spinning rope with a mass at its end" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

A vertical shaft turns at the constant angular speed $\omega$. One end of a uniform rope of mass $m_1$ and length $l$ is tied to the shaft. A small object of mass $m_2$ is tied to the other end. The shaft is much thinner than $l$. It turns so fast that the rope and the object lie almost in a horizontal plane, so gravity can be left out. Find the tension $T(r)$ in the rope at the distance $r$ from the shaft. Use some or all of $m_1$, $m_2$, $\omega$, $r$ and $l$.

<Hint>

Every piece of the rope moves on a circle, so every piece has an acceleration towards the shaft. What size does it have for a piece at the distance $r'$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Cut the rope at the distance $r$. The tension there is the only force that pulls everything beyond the cut towards the shaft: the outer part of the rope and the object.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The pieces of the outer part circle at different distances, so add their needs up with an integral over $r'$ from $r$ to $l$. A piece of length $dr'$ has the mass $(m_1/l)\,dr'$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-t" label="$T(r) =$" expr="m2*om^2*l + m1*om^2*(l^2 - r^2)/(2*l)"
  vars={{ m1: [1, 3], m2: [1, 3], om: [2, 5], l: [1, 2], r: [0.2, 0.9] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2", om: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m1*om^2*(l^2 - r^2)/(2*l)", hint: "That is the rope alone. At r = l your tension is zero, yet the end of the rope must hold the object on its circle." },
    { expr: "m2*om^2*l + m1*om^2*(l - r)", hint: "This puts all the rope beyond r at the distance l. Each piece circles at its own distance, so integrate from r to l." },
    { expr: "m2*om^2*l + m1*om^2*r^2/(2*l)", hint: "Your tension grows towards the outer end. The rope at r must pull in everything beyond r, so integrate from r to l, not from 0 to r." },
    { expr: "m2*om^2*r + m1*om^2*(l^2 - r^2)/(2*l)", hint: "The object circles at the distance l, wherever you cut the rope. Your first term uses r." },
    { expr: "m2*om^2*l + m1*om^2*(l^2 - r^2)/2", hint: "Check the units of the rope term: it is a force times a length. The mass of a piece of rope is its length times the mass per length, m1/l." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Differential analysis, as in the lesson on a rope with mass. Each small piece of rope needs a net inward force to stay on its circle, so the tension falls from the shaft outwards. At the outer end it is just enough to hold the object.

<Step title="One piece of rope">

A piece between $r'$ and $r' + dr'$ has the mass $dm = (m_1/l)\,dr'$ and moves on a circle of radius $r'$, with the inward acceleration $r'\omega^2$. The rope on its inner side pulls it inward with $T(r')$ and the rope on its outer side pulls it outward with $T(r' + dr')$:

<Formula label="Second law for a piece of rope" tex="T(r') - T(r' + dr') = \frac{m_1}{l}\,dr'\;r'\omega^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dT}{dr'} = -\frac{m_1\,\omega^2}{l}\,r'" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The outer end">

At $r' = l$ the rope holds the object, which moves on a circle of radius $l$:

<Formula label="Tension at the outer end" tex="T(l) = m_2\,l\,\omega^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Integrate">

Integrate the differential equation from $r$ to $l$:

<Formula label="Tension in the spinning rope" tex="T(r) = T(l) + \int_r^l \frac{m_1\,\omega^2}{l}\,r'\,dr' = m_2\,l\,\omega^2 + \frac{m_1\,\omega^2}{2l}\left(l^2 - r^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: a mass times a length times $\omega^2$ is kg m/s², a force, in both terms.

At $r = l$ only the object's term is left. At the shaft, $r = 0$, the tension is $(m_2 + \tfrac12 m_1)\,l\,\omega^2$: the rope counts as if its whole mass sat at its middle, at the distance $l/2$.

The tension falls all the way from the shaft to the object, because each piece of rope has less to hold than the piece before it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A rope over a rod with friction" points={20} mit="Problem 4">

Two blocks hang from the ends of a rope that lies over a fixed, round rod. The rod is horizontal and does not turn. Block 1, of mass $m_1$, hangs on the right, and block 2, of mass $m_2$, hangs on the left, with $m_1 > m_2$. The rope touches the top half of the rod. It leaves the rod at the point A on the right, above block 1, and at the point B on the left, above block 2, and both hanging sections are vertical. Between the rope and the rod the coefficient of static friction is $\mu_s$ and the coefficient of kinetic (sliding) friction is $\mu_k$, with $\mu_k < \mu_s$. The rope has no mass.

MIT lets you use the capstan result of its course notes (see "What you need from the lessons"). The rope touches the rod over the angle $\pi$, so when the rope is on the point of sliding, the tensions $T_A$ at A and $T_B$ at B obey

<Formula label="Tensions when the rope is about to slide" tex="T_B = T_A\,e^{-\mu_s \pi}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 4" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Let $T_1$ be the size of the force with which the rope pulls on block 1. How does the tension $T_A$ compare with $T_1$?

<Hint>

Look at the straight section of rope between A and block 1. It touches nothing. What is its mass, and so what is the net force on it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="The tension at A:" choices={["$T_A$ is greater than $T_1$", "$T_A$ is less than $T_1$", "$T_A$ is equal to $T_1$"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The tension can change only where something pushes or rubs on the rope, or where the rope has mass.

<Step title="The hanging section">

The section of rope between A and block 1 hangs free: the rod does not touch it. It has no mass, so by Newton's second law the net force on it is zero, whatever its acceleration. The two forces on it are $T_A$ upward at A and the pull $T_1$ of block 1 downward (the partner, by the third law, of the rope's pull on the block). So $T_A = T_1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The same argument gives $T_B = T_2$ on the left. The two tensions differ from each other only because of the friction along the half turn round the rod.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

For which value of $m_1$ does the rope begin to slide? Use $\mu_s$ and $m_2$.

<Hint>

Just before the rope slides, both blocks are at rest. What are the two tensions then?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put the two tensions in the relation given in the problem.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$m_1 =$" expr="m2*exp(mus*pi)"
  vars={{ m2: [1, 3], mus: [0.1, 0.5] }}
  names={{ m2: "m_2", mus: "\\mu_s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m2*exp(-mus*pi)", hint: "Your block 1 is lighter than block 2. Block 1 is the heavy one, and the larger tension, at A, is on its side." },
    { expr: "m2*exp(mus)", hint: "The exponent holds the angle over which the rope touches the rod. Here that is half a turn, the angle pi." },
    { expr: "m2*exp(2*pi*mus)", hint: "The rope touches only the top half of the rod: half a turn, the angle pi, not a full turn." },
    { expr: "m2*(1 + mus*pi)", hint: "That is what the exponential comes to for very small friction only. Use the full relation between the tensions." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the point of sliding nothing moves yet, so each tension equals a weight, and the two tensions obey the capstan relation.

<Step title="The tensions at rest">

Each block is at rest, so with the result of part (a): $T_A = T_1 = m_1 g$ and $T_B = T_2 = m_2 g$.

</Step>

<Step title="The capstan relation">

<Formula label="Mass at which the rope begins to slide" tex="m_2\,g = m_1\,g\,e^{-\mu_s \pi} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad m_1 = m_2\,e^{\mu_s \pi}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Without friction ($\mu_s = 0$) the rope slides as soon as $m_1$ is larger than $m_2$ at all. With friction, block 1 can be heavier by the factor $e^{\mu_s\pi}$, which is larger than 1, before anything moves. For $\mu_s = 0.3$, a number of our own, the factor is $e^{0.3\pi} \approx 2.6$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={9}>

Now $m_1$ is so large that the rope slides and the blocks move. Find the size $a$ of the acceleration of the blocks once the sliding has begun. Use some or all of $\mu_k$, $m_1$, $m_2$ and $g$. MIT's tip: while the rope slides, the relation between the tensions is the same with $\mu_k$ in place of $\mu_s$, that is $T_B = T_A\,e^{-\mu_k\pi}$ (the argument of the capstan example goes through in the same way).

<Hint>

The blocks accelerate now, so the tensions are no longer the weights. Write Newton's second law for each block. Which block goes down?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The rope keeps its length, so both blocks have the same size of acceleration, $a$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You have three equations (two second laws and the relation between the tensions) for $T_A$, $T_B$ and $a$. Solve the second laws for the tensions and put them in the relation.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c" label="$a =$" expr="g*(m1 - m2*exp(muk*pi))/(m1 + m2*exp(muk*pi))"
  vars={{ m1: [8, 12], m2: [1, 2], muk: [0.1, 0.4], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2", muk: "\\mu_k" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "g*(m1 - m2)/(m1 + m2)", hint: "That is two blocks over a pulley without friction: your formula has no friction in it. While the rope slides over the rod, the two tensions differ by the factor with muk." },
    { expr: "g*(m1*exp(muk*pi) - m2)/(m1*exp(muk*pi) + m2)", hint: "Try very strong friction: the blocks should hardly speed up, but your acceleration goes to g. Friction makes the tension at B, on the side of block 2, the smaller one." },
    { expr: "g*(m1 - m2*exp(muk*pi))/(m1 + m2)", hint: "The exponential multiplies the whole second law of block 2, also its term with the acceleration. It belongs in the denominator too." },
    { expr: "g*(m1 - m2*exp(-muk*pi))/(m1 + m2*exp(-muk*pi))", hint: "In your formula friction makes the blocks speed up faster than they would without it. Check which tension is the larger one: it is the one on the side of the heavy block 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Block 1 moves down and block 2 moves up, both with the acceleration $a$. Newton's second law for each block gives the tensions, and the sliding relation ties the tensions together.

<Step title="The second law for each block">

For block 1 take downward as positive, for block 2 upward:

<Formula label="The two blocks" tex="m_1 g - T_A = m_1 a, \qquad T_B - m_2 g = m_2 a" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c) (our own solution)" />

(By part (a) the rope pulls on the blocks with $T_A$ and $T_B$.)

</Step>

<Step title="The sliding relation">

Put $T_A = m_1(g - a)$ and $T_B = m_2(g + a)$ in $T_B = T_A\,e^{-\mu_k\pi}$, and multiply by $e^{\mu_k\pi}$:

<Formula label="The relation with the tensions filled in" tex="m_2\,(g + a)\,e^{\mu_k\pi} = m_1\,(g - a)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for the acceleration">

<Formula label="Acceleration of the blocks" tex="a = \frac{m_1 - m_2\,e^{\mu_k\pi}}{m_1 + m_2\,e^{\mu_k\pi}}\;g" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Without friction ($\mu_k = 0$) this is the result for two blocks over a frictionless pulley, $(m_1 - m_2)\,g/(m_1 + m_2)$. Friction makes the acceleration smaller, and it is always smaller than $g$.

The acceleration is positive when $m_1 > m_2\,e^{\mu_k\pi}$. A remark of our own: this is always so once the rope has begun to slide, because by part (b) sliding begins at $m_1 = m_2\,e^{\mu_s\pi}$ and $\mu_k < \mu_s$. So the blocks start with a jump in acceleration: at the moment the rope lets go, the acceleration is not zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Drag at low speed" points={18} mit="Problem 5">

At low speeds, and more so in liquids than in gases, the drag force on an object is proportional to its velocity: $\vec F = -C\,\vec v$, where $C$ is a constant. At $t = 0$ a small ball of mass $m$ is shot into a liquid with a horizontal velocity of size $u$, in the $+x$ direction; its vertical velocity is zero at that moment. The gravitational acceleration is $g$. Use the axes of MIT's drawing: $+x$ to the right and $+y$ **downward**. (Our note: MIT's set names no force besides drag and gravity, so the solutions here leave buoyancy out.)

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the $x$-component of the acceleration, $a_x$, for $t > 0$. Use $v_x$ (the $x$-component of the velocity) and some or all of $C$, $g$, $m$ and $u$.

<Hint>

Which forces act on the ball, and which of them have a part along $x$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$a_x =$" expr="-C*vx/m"
  vars={{ C: [0.5, 2], vx: [1, 5], m: [1, 3], g: [9, 10], u: [6, 9] }}
  names={{ vx: "v_x" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "g - C*vx/m", hint: "Gravity points along +y. It has no part along x." },
    { expr: "-C*u/m", hint: "That is the acceleration at the first moment only. The drag follows the velocity the ball has now, vx, not the velocity u it started with." },
    { expr: "C*vx/m", hint: "Your acceleration points the same way as the velocity, so the ball would speed up. Drag points against the velocity." },
    { expr: "-C*vx", hint: "That is the force, not the acceleration. Divide by the mass." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Newton's second law, one component at a time. The drag force $-C\vec v$ has the components $-Cv_x$ and $-Cv_y$.

<Step title="The x-component">

Along $x$ only the drag acts:

<Formula label="Acceleration along x" tex="m\,a_x = -C\,v_x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_x = -\frac{C}{m}\,v_x" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$C$ is a force per speed, in kg/s, so $Cv_x/m$ is in m/s². The sign is opposite to that of $v_x$: the ball slows down along $x$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find the $y$-component of the acceleration, $a_y$, for $t > 0$. Use $v_y$ (the $y$-component of the velocity) and some or all of $C$, $g$, $m$ and $u$.

<Hint>

Two forces have a part along $y$. Mind the direction of the $y$-axis.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$a_y =$" expr="g - C*vy/m"
  vars={{ C: [0.5, 2], vy: [1, 5], m: [1, 3], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ vy: "v_y" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-g - C*vy/m", hint: "The y-axis points downward here, so gravity is along +y." },
    { expr: "g + C*vy/m", hint: "In your formula the drag speeds a falling ball up. Drag points against the velocity." },
    { expr: "-C*vy/m", hint: "Gravity is missing. It acts along y." },
    { expr: "g - C*vy", hint: "The two terms do not have the same unit: C vy is a force. Divide the drag force by the mass." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in part (a), now with gravity, which points along $+y$.

<Step title="The y-component">

<Formula label="Acceleration along y" tex="m\,a_y = m g - C\,v_y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_y = g - \frac{C}{m}\,v_y" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ the ball has no vertical velocity, and the formula gives $a_y = g$: the ball begins to fall as if there were no liquid. The two equations of motion do not contain each other's velocity, so the horizontal and the vertical motion can be solved apart. (That is special to drag proportional to $v$.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

With the result of part (a), find $v_x(t)$. Use some or all of $C$, $g$, $m$, $u$ and $t$.

<Hint>

Part (a) is a differential equation, $dv_x/dt = -(C/m)\,v_x$. Separate the variables.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate $dv_x/v_x$ from $u$ to $v_x$, and $dt$ from $0$ to $t$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-c" label="$v_x(t) =$" expr="u*exp(-C*t/m)"
  vars={{ C: [0.5, 2], m: [1, 3], u: [1, 5], t: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "u - C*u*t/m", hint: "This keeps the acceleration at its first value. It is right for very short times only: the drag gets weaker as the ball slows, so separate the variables and integrate." },
    { expr: "u*exp(-C*t)", hint: "The exponent must be a pure number. C t is in kilograms." },
    { expr: "u*exp(C*t/m)", hint: "Your velocity grows without end. Drag slows the ball down." },
    { expr: "u*(1 - exp(-C*t/m))", hint: "Try t = 0: the ball should have the velocity u there, and your formula gives zero." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Separate the variables in the equation of part (a) and integrate from the start.

<Step title="Separate and integrate">

<Formula label="Horizontal velocity" tex="\int_u^{v_x} \frac{dv_x'}{v_x'} = -\frac{C}{m}\int_0^t dt' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln\frac{v_x}{u} = -\frac{C}{m}\,t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_x(t) = u\,e^{-Ct/m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ it gives $u$. Its derivative is $-(C/m)\,u\,e^{-Ct/m} = -(C/m)\,v_x$, the equation of part (a). The exponent $Ct/m$ is a pure number: $m/C$ is a time, the time in which the velocity falls by the factor $e$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

With the result of part (b), find $v_y(t)$. Use some or all of $C$, $g$, $m$, $u$ and $t$.

<Hint>

Part (b) is the differential equation $dv_y/dt = g - (C/m)\,v_y$: the falling ball of the lesson on resistive forces.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Separate the variables: $dv_y/(g - Cv_y/m) = dt$. Integrate from $v_y = 0$ at $t = 0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-d" label="$v_y(t) =$" expr="m*g/C*(1 - exp(-C*t/m))"
  vars={{ C: [0.5, 2], m: [1, 3], g: [9, 10], t: [0.5, 2], u: [6, 9] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "g*t", hint: "That is free fall, with no drag at all. It is right for very short times only." },
    { expr: "m*g/C*exp(-C*t/m)", hint: "Try t = 0: the ball starts with no vertical velocity, and your formula gives m g/C." },
    { expr: "g*(1 - exp(-C*t/m))", hint: "The bracket is a pure number, so your formula is an acceleration. Check the factor in front." },
    { expr: "m*g/C*(1 - exp(-C*t/m)) + u", hint: "The velocity u is horizontal. It does not add to the vertical component." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Separate the variables in the equation of part (b). The start is $v_y = 0$ at $t = 0$.

<Step title="Separate and integrate">

<Formula label="Separation of variables for the fall" tex="\int_0^{v_y} \frac{dv_y'}{g - \frac{C}{m}\,v_y'} = \int_0^t dt' \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -\frac{m}{C}\,\ln\!\left(1 - \frac{C\,v_y}{m g}\right) = t" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for the velocity">

<Formula label="Vertical velocity" tex="v_y(t) = \frac{m g}{C}\left(1 - e^{-Ct/m}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d) (our own solution)" />

This is the result of the lesson on [resistive forces](/courses/8-01/resistive-forces), with $C$ in the place of the lesson's $\alpha$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ it gives zero. For short times $e^{-Ct/m} \approx 1 - Ct/m$, so $v_y \approx gt$: free fall, as long as the ball is too slow to feel the drag. Units: $mg/C$ is N/(kg/s) = m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

Find $v_{xT}$, the horizontal component of the ball's terminal velocity (the velocity it approaches after a long time). Use some or all of $C$, $g$, $m$ and $u$.

<Hint>

At the terminal velocity the velocity no longer changes. What does part (a) say then? Or look at your result of part (c) for a very large $t$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-e" label="$v_{xT} =$" expr="0"
  vars={{ C: [0.5, 2], m: [1, 3], g: [9, 10], u: [1, 5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "u", hint: "That is the horizontal velocity at the start. Nothing pushes the ball along x, and the drag keeps slowing it for as long as it moves that way." },
    { expr: "m*g/C", hint: "That belongs to the vertical component. Gravity has no part along x." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A terminal velocity is one at which the acceleration is zero.

<Step title="The horizontal component">

Setting $a_x = -(C/m)\,v_x$ to zero gives $v_{xT} = 0$. The result of part (c) says the same: $u\,e^{-Ct/m}$ goes to zero as $t$ grows.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ball never quite stops moving sideways, but after a few times $m/C$ its horizontal velocity is a small fraction of $u$: after the time $5m/C$ it is the fraction $e^{-5} \approx 0.007$ of $u$, less than 1 percent.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="f" points={3}>

Find $v_{yT}$, the vertical component of the ball's terminal velocity. Use some or all of $C$, $g$, $m$ and $u$.

<Hint>

Set the acceleration of part (b) to zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-f" label="$v_{yT} =$" expr="m*g/C"
  vars={{ C: [0.5, 2], m: [1, 3], g: [9, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(m*g/C)", hint: "The square root belongs to drag proportional to the square of the speed. Here the drag is proportional to the speed itself." },
    { expr: "C/(m*g)", hint: "In your formula a heavier ball sinks more slowly. Balance the drag C v against the weight m g." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "Gravity keeps pulling, so the vertical velocity does not die out. It settles where the drag balances the weight." },
    { expr: "g/C", hint: "Check the units: g/C is not a speed. The weight is m g." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At the terminal velocity the drag balances the weight.

<Step title="The vertical component">

<Formula label="Terminal velocity" tex="0 = g - \frac{C}{m}\,v_{yT} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_{yT} = \frac{m g}{C}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(f) (our own solution)" />

The result of part (d) goes to the same value as $t$ grows, because $e^{-Ct/m}$ goes to zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A heavier ball sinks faster, and a larger drag constant slows it. In the end the ball sinks straight down at the speed $mg/C$, whatever the velocity $u$ it was shot in with: the terminal velocity has forgotten the start.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Two pulleys, two ropes and two blocks" points={12} mit="Problem 6">

Two blocks, of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, hang from two massless pulleys and two massless ropes that cannot stretch. MIT's drawing shows how:

- Pulley A hangs from the ceiling and cannot move.
- The first rope runs over pulley A. Block 1 hangs from its left end. Its right end goes down to the centre of pulley B and carries that pulley.
- The second rope is tied to the floor. It goes straight up, over pulley B, and down again on the other side, where block 2 hangs from its end.

All hanging sections of rope are vertical. Find the acceleration $a_2$ of block 2, with **downward as positive**. Use some or all of $g$, $m_1$ and $m_2$.

<Hint>

The unknowns are the two tensions and the accelerations of block 1, block 2 and pulley B. The second law gives one equation for each block and one for pulley B (which has no mass). Each rope gives a constraint.

</Hint>

<Hint>

First rope: if block 1 goes down by $d$, what does pulley B do?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Second rope: one end is fixed to the floor. If pulley B goes down by $d$, the section between the floor and the pulley gets shorter by $d$. Where does that length go, and how far does block 2 move, counting the motion of the pulley too?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-a2" label="$a_2 =$" expr="2*g*(2*m2 - m1)/(m1 + 4*m2)"
  vars={{ m1: [1, 3], m2: [1, 3], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "g*(m2 - m1)/(m1 + m2)", hint: "That is two blocks on one rope over one pulley. Here block 2 hangs from a rope that runs over the moving pulley B to the floor, so it moves twice as far as block 1 and its rope has half the tension." },
    { expr: "g*(2*m2 - m1)/(m1 + 4*m2)", hint: "That is the acceleration of pulley B. Block 2 moves twice as far as pulley B, because the other end of its rope is fixed to the floor." },
    { expr: "g*(m1 - 2*m2)/(m1 + 4*m2)", hint: "That is the acceleration of block 1. Block 2 moves the other way and twice as far." },
    { expr: "2*g*(m1 - 2*m2)/(m1 + 4*m2)", hint: "Check the sign: make block 2 much heavier than block 1. It should go down, which is positive here, and your formula is negative." },
    { expr: "g*(2*m2 - m1)/(m1 + 2*m2)", hint: "This gives block 2 the same acceleration as pulley B. One end of block 2's rope is fixed to the floor: when pulley B goes down by d, block 2 goes down by 2d." },
    { expr: "2*g*(m2 - m1)/(m1 + 2*m2)", hint: "This uses one tension for both ropes. Pulley B has no mass, so the rope that carries it balances the two sections of the second rope: its tension is twice as large." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Second law for block 1, block 2 and the massless pulley B, and one constraint for each rope. Measure every position downward from the ceiling: $y_1$ for block 1, $y_B$ for pulley B, $y_2$ for block 2.

<Step title="The constraints">

The first rope has a fixed length. Apart from constants (the piece that lies on pulley A), its length is $y_1 + y_B$. The second rope runs from the floor, at the fixed depth $h$, up to pulley B and down to block 2, so apart from constants its length is $(h - y_B) + (y_2 - y_B)$. Differentiate both lengths twice:

<Formula label="Constraints of the two ropes" tex="a_1 + a_B = 0, \qquad a_2 - 2a_B = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a_B = \tfrac12\,a_2, \qquad a_1 = -\tfrac12\,a_2" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

In words: block 2 moves twice as far as pulley B, and block 1 moves as far as pulley B but the other way.

</Step>

<Step title="The second law">

Let $T_1$ be the tension in the first rope and $T_2$ the tension in the second. On pulley B the first rope pulls up with $T_1$ and the two sections of the second rope pull down with $T_2$ each. The pulley has no mass, so these balance. For the blocks, downward is positive:

<Formula label="Pulley B and the two blocks" tex="T_1 = 2\,T_2, \qquad m_1 g - T_1 = m_1 a_1, \qquad m_2 g - T_2 = m_2 a_2" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

From block 2, $T_2 = m_2(g - a_2)$. Put $T_1 = 2T_2$ and $a_1 = -a_2/2$ in the equation of block 1: $m_1 g - 2m_2(g - a_2) = -\tfrac12 m_1 a_2$. Collect the terms with $a_2$:

<Formula label="Acceleration of block 2" tex="a_2 = \frac{2\,(2m_2 - m_1)}{m_1 + 4m_2}\;g" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The system stays at rest when $m_1 = 2m_2$: the first rope then carries $m_1 g$, and each section of the second rope carries half of that, which is the weight of block 2.

If block 1 has almost no mass, the tensions go to zero and block 2 falls freely: the formula gives $a_2 = g$. If block 2 has almost no mass, block 1 falls freely ($a_1 = g$) and block 2 goes up twice as fast: the formula gives $a_2 = -2g$.

The answer is positive, so block 2 goes down, when $m_2 > m_1/2$.

The system of the lesson on [pulleys and constraint conditions](/courses/8-01/pulleys-and-constraints) also has a moving pulley B, there with its rope tied to the ceiling and block 2 hanging from the pulley itself. In both systems the constraint has a factor of 2 between a moving pulley and the free end of the rope that runs round it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>
