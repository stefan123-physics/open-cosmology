The previous lesson found that only the **external** force changes a system's momentum. This lesson asks when that force does nothing, so that the momentum stays constant, and uses the answer to solve collisions and explosions.

## When momentum is constant

Start from the impulse equation for a system (16.1 transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Impulse and change of momentum" tex="\int_{t_i}^{t_f}\vec F^{\,\text{ext}}\,dt = \vec p_{\text{sys},f} - \vec p_{\text{sys},i}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

If the external impulse is zero, then $\vec p_{\text{sys},i} = \vec p_{\text{sys},f}$: the momentum of the system is the same at the start and the end. The notes call a system with zero external force **isolated** (notes, Eqs. (10.7.1)–(10.7.5), pp. 10-11–10-12):

<Formula label="Conservation for an isolated system" tex="\vec p_{\text{sys},i} = m_1\vec v_{1,i} + m_2\vec v_{2,i} + \cdots = m_1\vec v_{1,f} + m_2\vec v_{2,f} + \cdots = \vec p_{\text{sys},f}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (10.7.3)–(10.7.5), pp. 10-11–10-12" />

Even when a system is not isolated, momentum is not lost: by the third law, whatever momentum the system gains, its **surroundings** lose. The total change of the system and its surroundings is zero, which the notes call the **principle of conservation of momentum** (notes, Eq. (10.8.4), p. 10-12).

## Choosing the system: a collision in one dimension

A block slides along a horizontal surface and hits a second block at rest; they stick together (16.1 transcript, p. 1). Which system? Take **both blocks**. The external forces, gravity and the normal force, cancel, and the large collision forces are **internal**, so they do not change the total momentum. With only one block as the system, you would need to know the collision force (p. 1).

Choose $\hat i$ along the motion, the initial state just before the collision and the final state just after (p. 2):

<Formula label="Two blocks stick together" tex="m_1 v_{1,i}\,\hat i + m_2\cdot 0\,\hat i = (m_1 + m_2)\,v_f\,\hat i" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

One equation: it gives whichever quantity is not given, usually the final velocity $v_f = m_1 v_{1,i}/(m_1 + m_2)$.

## Two dimensions

Now one block moves up the page and another moves in from the side; they collide and stick (16.1 transcript, p. 2). Momentum is a vector, so the one vector equation is **two** equations, one for each component:

<Formula label="Components of momentum" tex="x:\ m_1 v_{1x,i} + m_2 v_{2x,i} = (m_1 + m_2)\,v_{x,f}, \qquad y:\ m_1 v_{1y,i} + m_2 v_{2y,i} = (m_1 + m_2)\,v_{y,f}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3" />

Check each direction **separately**: momentum can be conserved in one direction but not another, if there is a net external force in only one direction (p. 3).

## Collisions with friction

What if the blocks slide on a rough surface? Compare the moments just before and just after the collision: the impulse of friction over that very short time is tiny, so the momentum is **approximately** constant during the collision. Afterwards friction does slow the blocks, and momentum is certainly not conserved (16.1 transcript, p. 3). The same holds for gravity in a short explosion: choose the two states a very short time apart (p. 3; notes, Eq. (10.9.2), p. 10-13).

<WorkedExample title="Coupling railway wagons">
<Problem>
A 12 000 kg wagon rolling at 2.0 m/s runs into an 8000 kg wagon at rest, and they couple. What is their speed just after? How much momentum does the second wagon gain?
</Problem>
<Step title="Common speed">
$v_f = 12\,000 \times 2.0/(12\,000 + 8000) = 24\,000/20\,000 = 1.2$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Momentum transfer">
The second wagon gains $8000 \times 1.2 = 9600$ kg·m/s, exactly what the first loses: $12\,000 \times (2.0 - 1.2) = 9600$ kg·m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="A crossroads collision">
<Problem>
A 1200 kg car heading east at 15 m/s collides with an 1800 kg truck heading north at 10 m/s, and they lock together. Find the velocity of the wreck just after.
</Problem>
<Step title="Components">
$x$ (east): $1200 \times 15 = 18\,000$ kg·m/s $= 3000\,v_{x,f}$, so $v_{x,f} = 6.0$ m/s. $y$ (north): $1800 \times 10 = 18\,000$ kg·m/s, so $v_{y,f} = 6.0$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed and direction">
$v_f = \sqrt{6.0^2 + 6.0^2} \approx 8.5$ m/s, at 45° north of east.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Momentum diagrams

Segment 16.2 turns this into a method, **momentum diagrams** (16.2 transcript, pp. 1–3):

1. Choose the **system** and a **reference frame** (for example two objects on a frictionless surface, in the ground frame).
2. Draw the **initial** and **final** states, each object with its velocity vector. In the final state you often do not know the directions, so draw them in some direction and let the algebra decide.
3. Write the momentum principle. With no external force, $\vec 0 = \vec p_f - \vec p_i$:

<Formula label="Momentum diagram equation" tex="m_1\vec v_{1,f} + m_2\vec v_{2,f} = m_1\vec v_{1,i} + m_2\vec v_{2,i}" cite="reading3" at="p. 2" />

4. Choose a **coordinate system** (the lecture counts this as its third step, after choosing the system and drawing the states) and write the equation in components, for example along $\hat i$: $m_1 v_{1x,f} + m_2 v_{2x,f} = m_1 v_{1x,i} + m_2 v_{2x,i}$. Components can be positive or negative (pp. 2–3).

The notes call these **momentum flow diagrams** and add a naming habit: $(v_{x,i})_1$ for the $x$ component of object 1's velocity in the initial state (notes, pp. 10-13–10-14).

## Example: an exploding projectile

A projectile explodes at the top of its flight, at horizontal distance $x_i$ from the launch point, into two pieces flying horizontally: a small one of a quarter of the mass, and a large one of three quarters. The small piece lands back at the launch point. Where does the large one land (notes, Example 10.5, pp. 10-14–10-17)?

During the brief explosion, gravity's impulse is negligible, so horizontal momentum is conserved. The small piece must leave with velocity $-v_1$ (it retraces the path back), so

<Formula label="Momentum across the explosion" tex="m_1 v_1 = -\tfrac14 m_1 v_1 + \tfrac34 m_1 v_3 \;\Rightarrow\; v_3 = \tfrac53\,v_1" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (10.9.11)–(10.9.12), p. 10-17" />

Both pieces take as long to fall as the projectile took to rise, so the large piece travels a further $\tfrac53 x_i$ and lands at $\tfrac83 x_i$ (notes, Eqs. (10.9.13)–(10.9.14), p. 10-17). The notes also get the answer more quickly from the **centre of mass**, which keeps moving on the original parabola (the next lesson's subject).

The notes' last example (Example 10.6, pp. 10-17–10-20) combines both stages: a landing plane snags a sandbag (momentum conserved during the snag), and then friction and brakes stop them (Newton's second law and kinematics).

<WorkedExample title="A shell splits in two">
<Problem>
A shell reaches the top of its path 400 m (horizontally) from the gun and splits into two equal halves. One half stops dead and falls straight down. Where does the other half land?
</Problem>
<Step title="Momentum">
Horizontally, $m v_1 = \tfrac{m}{2}\cdot 0 + \tfrac{m}{2} v_3$, so $v_3 = 2v_1$.
</Step>
<Step title="Distance">
The fall takes as long as the rise, during which the shell covered 400 m at $v_1$. At $2v_1$ the second half covers 800 m more, landing at $400 + 800 = 1200$ m from the gun. Check with the centre of mass: it lands at 800 m, halfway between the pieces at 400 m and 1200 m.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Looking for dark matter in collisions">
If the dark matter in our galaxy is made of particles, some of them pass through the Earth all the time. Underground detectors wait for one to hit an atomic nucleus. Momentum (and energy) conservation in that collision fixes how hard the nucleus can recoil: for a particle about as heavy as a xenon nucleus, moving at a few hundred km/s, the recoil is at most a few tens of keV. Searching for such tiny, rare kicks is one of the main ways of testing what dark matter is.
</Callout>

## Summary

- If the external impulse on a system is zero, its momentum is conserved: $\vec p_{\text{sys},i} = \vec p_{\text{sys},f}$. Check each component.
- Choose the system so that the collision forces are internal, and the states just before and just after the collision, so that friction or gravity give negligible impulse.
- Sticking collision in one dimension: $v_f = m_1 v_{1,i}/(m_1 + m_2)$. In two dimensions, solve the $x$ and $y$ equations separately.
- Momentum diagrams: system and frame, initial and final states with velocity vectors, momentum principle, components.
- In the exploding projectile, conservation across the explosion plus kinematics gives the landing point; the centre of mass gives it faster.
