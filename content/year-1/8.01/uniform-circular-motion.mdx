Lesson 8 found the velocity of an object going round a circle: $\vec v = r\,(d\theta/dt)\,\hat\theta$, tangent to the circle. Lesson 9 takes the simplest case, a circle traversed at a **steady rate**, and finds its acceleration. The answer is a surprise at first: even at constant speed, the object accelerates, and always towards the centre.

## Constant speed, changing velocity

Take $d\theta/dt$ to be constant. Then the speed

<Formula label="Speed on the circle" tex="v = r\left|\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right|" cite="reading2" at="transcript 9.1, p. 1" />

is constant too. But the **velocity vector** is not: it always points along the tangent, so as the object goes round, its direction turns with it. Acceleration is the rate of change of the velocity vector, not of the speed, so a vector that keeps its length but changes direction still has a derivative. This special case, with $d\theta/dt$ constant, is called **uniform circular motion**.

In the notes' language: if the total force along the tangent is zero, Newton's second law gives no tangential acceleration,

<Formula label="No tangential acceleration" tex="a_\theta = 0" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.4.1), p. 7" />

so the speed stays the same. Whatever acceleration there is must point along the radius.

## The acceleration points to the centre

Write the velocity with $\hat\theta$ in Cartesian components, as in Lesson 8:

<Formula label="Velocity in Cartesian components" tex="\vec v(t) = r\,\frac{d\theta}{dt}\bigl(-\sin\theta(t)\,\hat i + \cos\theta(t)\,\hat j\bigr)" cite="reading2" at="transcript 9.1, pp. 1–2" />

Here $r$ and $d\theta/dt$ are constants, so only the sine and cosine change, and each needs the chain rule once more. Differentiating gives a second factor $d\theta/dt$; taking out a minus sign leaves the bracket $\cos\theta\,\hat i + \sin\theta\,\hat j$, which is exactly $\hat r$:

<Formula label="Acceleration in uniform circular motion" tex="\vec a(t) = \frac{d\vec v}{dt} = -r\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2\bigl(\cos\theta(t)\,\hat i + \sin\theta(t)\,\hat j\bigr) = -r\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2\hat r(t)" cite="reading2" at="transcript 9.1, p. 2; the result is stated in 9.2, p. 1" />

Whatever the sign of $d\theta/dt$, its square is positive, so the minus sign always wins: the acceleration points along $-\hat r$, **towards the centre**. Its radial component is

<Formula label="Radial component" tex="a_r = -r\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2 = -r\,\omega^2 < 0" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.3.8), p. 7, and (6.4.2), p. 8" />

with $\omega = |d\theta/dt|$ the angular speed. An acceleration that points to the centre is called **centripetal** ("centre-seeking").

## Why inwards: the geometric picture

Segment 9.2 checks the direction without any algebra. Pick two moments, $t_1$ and $t_2$. The two velocity vectors are tangent to the circle and have the same length. Move them so their tails meet; the change in velocity is the arrow from the tip of the first to the tip of the second:

<Formula label="Change in velocity" tex="\Delta\vec v = \vec v(t_2) - \vec v(t_1)" cite="reading3" at="transcript 9.2, p. 1" />

As $t_2$ approaches $t_1$, the two vectors become almost parallel, and the short arrow joining their tips stands at right angles to them. A right angle to the tangent is along the radius, and the arrow points inwards. Since $\vec a = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \Delta\vec v/\Delta t$, the acceleration points to the centre.

The notes make this quantitative. If the velocity turns through a small angle $\Delta\theta$, the change has length

<Formula label="Size of the change in velocity" tex="|\Delta\vec v| = 2v\sin(\Delta\theta/2) \approx v\,\Delta\theta" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.5.1)–(6.5.2), p. 10" />

using the small-angle approximation from Lesson 8. Dividing by $\Delta t$ and taking the limit:

<Formula label="Radial acceleration from the geometry" tex="a = |a_r| = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{|\Delta\vec v|}{\Delta t} = v\lim_{\Delta t \to 0}\frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t} = v\,\frac{d\theta}{dt} = v\,\omega" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.5.3), p. 10" />

the same size as before (a size, so positive, while the component $a_r$ is negative), since $v\,\omega = r\omega \cdot \omega = r\omega^2$. [The notes number these three equations (6.5.1) to (6.5.3) although they sit in Section 6.4.1, before Section 6.5; we cite them as printed.]

## Period and frequency

Because the speed is constant, every turn takes the same time, the **period** $T$. In one period the object travels once round the circumference:

<Formula label="One turn" tex="s = 2\pi r = vT" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.4.3), p. 8" />

so

<Formula label="Period" tex="T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi r}{r\omega} = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.4.4), p. 8" />

The **frequency** is the number of turns per second, the reciprocal of the period:

<Formula label="Frequency" tex="f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{\omega}{2\pi}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.4.5), p. 8" />

Its SI unit is the inverse second, called the **hertz** (Hz). Angular speed and frequency differ only by the factor $2\pi$, because one turn is $2\pi$ radians; this is why $\omega$ is also called the **angular frequency**, the rotation rate in radians per second. MIT's short text segment 9.3 puts the relations together:

<Formula label="Period, frequency and speed together" tex="f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{\omega}{2\pi}, \qquad v = r\omega = \frac{2\pi r}{T}" cite="seg93" at="segment 9.3" />

## Five ways to write the same acceleration

Since $v = r\omega$, $\omega = 2\pi f$ and $f = 1/T$, the size of the centripetal acceleration can be written in several equivalent forms. Besides $a = v\,\omega$ from the geometric picture, the notes list

<Formula label="In terms of radius and angular speed" tex="a = r\,\omega^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.4.6), p. 8" />

<Formula label="In terms of speed and radius" tex="a = \frac{v^2}{r}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.4.7), p. 8" />

<Formula label="In terms of radius and frequency" tex="a = 4\pi^2 r f^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.4.8), p. 8" />

<Formula label="In terms of radius and period" tex="a = \frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.4.9), p. 9" />

[The notes call the last one "the fourth expression"; counting $v\,\omega$, it is the fifth.] Which form to use depends on what you know about the orbit: its radius, and one of the speed, the angular speed, the frequency or the period. Any one of these gives the other three.

<WorkedExample title="A washing machine on its spin cycle">
<Problem>
A washing machine drum of radius 0.25 m spins at 1200 turns per minute. Find the frequency, period, angular speed and speed of a sock on the drum's wall, and its centripetal acceleration in all five forms.
</Problem>
<Step title="Frequency and period">
$f = 1200/60 = 20$ Hz, so $T = 1/f = 0.050$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Angular speed and speed">
$\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi \times 20 \approx 125.7$ rad/s, and $v = r\omega = 0.25 \times 125.7 \approx 31.4$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Centripetal acceleration, five ways">
$r\omega^2 = 0.25 \times 125.7^2 \approx 3950$ m/s². Check: $v^2/r = 31.4^2/0.25 \approx 3950$; $v\omega = 31.4 \times 125.7 \approx 3950$; $4\pi^2 r f^2 = 39.48 \times 0.25 \times 400 \approx 3950$; $4\pi^2 r/T^2 = 39.48 \times 0.25/0.0025 \approx 3950$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Compared with gravity">
$3950/9.81 \approx 400$: the wall pushes the sock inwards about 400 times harder than its weight. The water, which can escape through the holes, flies off along the tangent.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Newton's test with the Moon">
<Problem>
The Moon circles the Earth at a distance of 384,400 km once every 27.32 days (the sidereal period). Find its centripetal acceleration, and compare it with $g$ divided by $60^2$, as the Moon is about 60 Earth radii away.
</Problem>
<Step title="Period in seconds">
$T = 27.32 \times 86{,}400 \approx 2.36 \times 10^6$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Centripetal acceleration">
$a = 4\pi^2 r/T^2 = 39.48 \times 3.844 \times 10^8 / (2.36 \times 10^6)^2 \approx 2.72 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
$g/60^2 = 9.81/3600 \approx 2.73 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s². The two agree: the pull that keeps the Moon on its circle is the Earth's gravity, weakened by the square of the distance, as in [the universal law of gravitation](/courses/8-01/universal-gravitation).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology">
The Sun circles the centre of the Milky Way at about 229 km/s (Eilers and colleagues, 2019), at a distance of 8178 parsecs, about 2.5 × 10²⁰ m (GRAVITY collaboration, 2019). The formulas of this lesson give a period $T = 2\pi r/v$ of about 220 million years, and a centripetal acceleration $v^2/r$ of only about 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s². Measuring $v$ at many radii, a galaxy's **rotation curve**, tells us how much mass must pull inwards to supply $v^2/r$; far out, the visible stars and gas are not enough, one of the main arguments for **dark matter**. That is only about twice the acceleration below which an alternative idea, Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), proposes that gravity itself changes, about 10⁻¹⁰ m/s².
</Callout>

## Summary

- In **uniform circular motion** $d\theta/dt$ is constant, so the speed $v = r\,|d\theta/dt|$ is constant, but the velocity turns with the tangent.
- Differentiating $\vec v = r\,(d\theta/dt)(-\sin\theta\,\hat i + \cos\theta\,\hat j)$ gives $\vec a = -r\,(d\theta/dt)^2\,\hat r$: a **centripetal** acceleration, towards the centre, with radial component $a_r = -r\omega^2$.
- Geometrically, $\Delta\vec v$ between two nearby moments points inwards, and $|\Delta\vec v| \approx v\,\Delta\theta$ gives $a = v\omega$.
- Period and frequency: $T = 2\pi r/v = 2\pi/\omega$, $f = 1/T = \omega/2\pi$, in hertz.
- The size of the centripetal acceleration: $a = r\omega^2 = v^2/r = v\omega = 4\pi^2 r f^2 = 4\pi^2 r/T^2$.
