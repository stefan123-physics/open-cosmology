Kinematics is the description of motion, without yet asking what causes it. We start with the simplest case: an object moving back and forth along a straight line, treated as a single point.

## Choosing a coordinate system

As in [Vectors](/courses/8-01/vectors), the first step is a coordinate system. In one dimension that means one axis: an origin, a positive direction, and a unit vector $\hat i$ pointing toward increasing $x$. Together with a clock, this is a **reference frame**.

## Position

The object's location at time $t$ is given by its **position coordinate** $x(t)$, a function of time that can be positive, zero or negative. Because the location relative to the origin has both a size and a direction, it is a vector, the **position vector**:

<Formula label="Position vector" tex="\vec r(t) = x(t)\,\hat i" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.2.1)" />

The position at $t = 0$ gets its own symbol, $x_0 \equiv x(0)$. Position is measured in metres.

## Time interval and displacement

A **time interval** $[t_1, t_2]$ has length $\Delta t = t_2 - t_1$. The **displacement** during that interval is the change in position:

<Formula label="Displacement" tex="\Delta \vec r = \vec r(t_2) - \vec r(t_1) = \big(x(t_2) - x(t_1)\big)\,\hat i \equiv \Delta x\,\hat i" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (4.2.3)" />

Displacement only depends on where the motion starts and ends, not on the path in between. A car that drives 3 km east and then 3 km back west has travelled 6 km, but its displacement is zero.

## Average velocity

The **average velocity** over an interval is the displacement divided by the time it took:

<Formula label="Average velocity" tex="v_\text{ave} = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}, \qquad \vec v_\text{ave} = v_\text{ave}\,\hat i" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (4.3.1)–(4.3.3)" />

This is not the same as distance divided by time (that is the average *speed*). On a graph of $x$ against $t$, $v_\text{ave}$ is the slope of the straight line, the **secant**, joining the two points $(t_1, x(t_1))$ and $(t_2, x(t_2))$.

## Instantaneous velocity

A speedometer does not show an average over an interval; it shows the velocity *now*. To get that, shrink the interval. As $\Delta t$ gets smaller, the secant line through $(t, x(t))$ and $(t + \Delta t, x(t + \Delta t))$ turns into the **tangent line** at $t$. Its slope is the **instantaneous velocity**:

<Formula label="Instantaneous velocity" tex="v(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{x(t + \Delta t) - x(t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{dx}{dt}, \qquad \vec v(t) = v(t)\,\hat i" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (4.3.5)–(4.3.6)" />

In the language of calculus, velocity is the **derivative** of position with respect to time. Its sign tells you the direction: positive means moving toward $+x$, negative toward $-x$, and zero means momentarily at rest.

## Derivatives you will use

You rarely need to take the limit by hand. A constant has derivative zero, the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, and a constant factor just comes along. Add a short table of rules and you can handle most of mechanics:

<Formula label="Derivatives you will use" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\,t^n = n\,t^{n-1}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\,e^{bt} = b\,e^{bt}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\ln t = \frac{1}{t}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\sin(bt) = b\cos(bt), \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\cos(bt) = -b\sin(bt)" cite="mit" at="segment 1.6 Derivatives" />

Still, doing it once from the definition shows where the rules come from.

<WorkedExample title="Velocity from the definition">
<Problem>
A cart's position is $x(t) = 2 + 3t - t^2$, with $x$ in metres and $t$ in seconds. Find $v(t)$ from the limit definition, then find when and where the cart turns around.
</Problem>
<Step title="Write the position a short time later">
$x(t + \Delta t) = 2 + 3(t + \Delta t) - (t + \Delta t)^2 = 2 + 3t + 3\Delta t - t^2 - 2t\,\Delta t - \Delta t^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Form the displacement">
$\Delta x = x(t + \Delta t) - x(t) = 3\Delta t - 2t\,\Delta t - \Delta t^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Divide by Δt and take the limit">
$\dfrac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = 3 - 2t - \Delta t$. As $\Delta t \to 0$ the last term vanishes, so $v(t) = 3 - 2t$ m/s. The power rule gives the same thing: $\frac{d}{dt}(2 + 3t - t^2) = 3 - 2t$.
</Step>
<Step title="Find the turning point">
The cart turns around when it is momentarily at rest: $3 - 2t = 0$ gives $t = 1.5$ s. There, $x(1.5) = 2 + 4.5 - 2.25 = 4.25$ m. Before $t = 1.5$ s, $v > 0$ (moving toward $+x$); afterwards $v < 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with an average">
Between $t = 1$ s and $t = 2$ s: $x(1) = 4$ m and $x(2) = 4$ m, so $v_\text{ave} = 0$. That fits: the interval is centred on the turning point, and the cart goes out and comes back.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Average velocity is not always the mean of two velocities

It is tempting to compute an average velocity as $\big(v(t_1) + v(t_2)\big)/2$. MIT's notes are careful here. That shortcut gives the right answer only when the velocity changes linearly with time. In general, the average velocity is the displacement divided by the time interval, and nothing else.

<Callout kind="cosmology">
In cosmology the distance between two distant galaxies is written $\ell(t) = a(t)\,\ell_c$. Here $\ell_c$ is a fixed "comoving" distance and $a(t)$ is the **scale factor**, which says how much the universe has expanded. The galaxies separate at the rate

<Formula label="Hubble's law from a derivative" tex="v = \frac{d\ell}{dt} = \frac{da}{dt}\,\ell_c = \frac{\dot a}{a}\,\ell \equiv H(t)\,\ell" cite="guth" />

This is just the derivative from this page, with $\dot a$ standing for $da/dt$. The Hubble parameter $H(t) = \dot a / a$ measures how fast the universe is expanding at time $t$; its value today is called $H_0$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Position along a line: $\vec r(t) = x(t)\,\hat i$. Displacement $\Delta x$ is the change in position.
- Average velocity $v_\text{ave} = \Delta x / \Delta t$ is the slope of a secant on the $x(t)$ graph.
- Instantaneous velocity $v = dx/dt$ is the slope of the tangent, the limit of the average as $\Delta t \to 0$.
- The sign of $v$ gives the direction of motion. Where $v = 0$ the object is momentarily at rest; it turns around there if $v$ changes sign.
