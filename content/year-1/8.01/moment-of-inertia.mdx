The [previous lesson](/courses/8-01/rigid-body-kinematics) described how a rigid body turns. This one asks how much kinetic energy the turning carries, which leads to the quantity that plays the role of mass for rotation.

## Kinetic energy of rotation

Let a rigid body rotate about an axis through a point S, with $\hat k$ along the axis (29.1 transcript, p. 1). Split it into small elements $\Delta m_j$, at distances $r_j$ from the axis. Every element moves in a circle with the same $\omega_z$, so its speed is $v_j = r_j|\omega_z|$ and its kinetic energy is $\frac{1}{2}\Delta m_j r_j^2\omega_z^2$ (pp. 1–2; notes, Eq. (16.2.1), p. 16-7). Add them all up; since $\omega_z$ is the same for every element it comes out of the sum (p. 2):

<Formula label="Moment of inertia" tex="I_S = \lim_{\Delta m_j \to 0}\sum_{j=1}^{N} \Delta m_j\,r_j^2 = \int_{\text{body}} r^2\,dm" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3" />

Here $r$ is the **perpendicular** distance from the mass element $dm$ to the axis through S (p. 3). Then (p. 3; notes, Eqs. (16.2.3)–(16.2.4), p. 16-7):

<Formula label="Rotational kinetic energy" tex="K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2}\,I_S\,\omega_z^2" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

Compare the translational kinetic energy $\frac{1}{2}m v_{\text{cm}}^2$ (p. 3): the moment of inertia plays the role of mass, and $\omega_z$ the role of speed. $\omega_z$ may be positive or negative, but its square is never negative (p. 3). The moment of inertia is a property of the body **and of the chosen axis**; its SI unit is kg m² (notes, p. 16-7).

## A uniform rod

Take a thin uniform rod of mass $m$ and length $L$, and an axis through its centre, perpendicular to the rod (29.2 transcript, p. 1). The hard part of any such integral is setting it up, so do it step by step (p. 1): put the origin at the centre of mass, pick an element $dm$ at position $x$, and use $x$ as the integration variable. Then $r = |x|$, and since the rod is uniform, $dm = (m/L)\,dx$ (p. 2). "Integrating over the body" means $x$ runs from $-L/2$ to $L/2$ (p. 2):

<Formula label="Uniform rod about its centre" tex="I_{\text{cm}} = \int_{-L/2}^{L/2} \frac{m}{L}\,x^2\,dx = \frac{m}{L}\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-L/2}^{L/2} = \frac{1}{12}\,m L^2" cite="reading3" at="pp. 2–3" />

This is a measure of how the mass is spread about that axis (p. 3; notes, Eq. (16.2.5), p. 16-8). The notes add that the rod's width is neglected (p. 16-8).

## A uniform disc

For a thin uniform disc of mass $m$ and radius $R$, about the axis through its centre perpendicular to the disc, slice it into **rings** of radius $r$ and width $dr$ (29.3 transcript, p. 1). The mass per unit area is $\sigma = m/\pi R^2$, and the area of a ring is $\pi(r + dr)^2 - \pi r^2 = 2\pi r\,dr + \pi(dr)^2$; as $dr \to 0$ the last term is negligible, so the area is the circumference times the width (p. 2; notes, Eq. (16.2.12), p. 16-9). Every part of the ring is at distance $r$ from the axis (p. 1):

<Formula label="Uniform disc about its centre" tex="dm = \frac{m}{\pi R^2}\,2\pi r\,dr, \qquad I_{\text{cm}} = \int_0^R \frac{2m}{R^2}\,r^3\,dr = \frac{1}{2}\,m R^2" cite="reading4" at="pp. 2–3" />

(notes, Example 16.2, Eqs. (16.2.13)–(16.2.15), p. 16-10; the notes also do it with area patches $r\,dr\,d\theta$, Eqs. (16.2.6)–(16.2.11), p. 16-9.)

## A uniform sphere, by symmetry

(This page takes the sphere, segment 29.5, before the parallel axis theorem of segment 29.4, to keep the three examples together.) For a uniform sphere of mass $m$ and radius $R$, take axes $x$, $y$, $z$ through the centre (29.5 transcript, p. 1). About the $z$-axis, the perpendicular distance of $dm$ is $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, so $I_z = \int(x^2 + y^2)\,dm$; likewise $I_x = \int(y^2 + z^2)\,dm$ and $I_y = \int(z^2 + x^2)\,dm$ (p. 1). By the symmetry of the sphere all three are equal, $I_{\text{cm}}$. Adding them gives $3I_{\text{cm}} = 2\int(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)\,dm = 2\int r^2\,dm$, where $r$ is now the distance from the centre (pp. 1–2). Slice into thin spherical shells: $dm = \frac{m}{\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3}\,4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{3m}{R^3}r^2\,dr$ (p. 2). So

<Formula label="Uniform sphere about its centre" tex="3I_{\text{cm}} = 2\int_0^R \frac{3m}{R^3}\,r^4\,dr \quad\Rightarrow\quad I_{\text{cm}} = \frac{2}{5}\,m R^2" cite="reading6" at="pp. 2–3" />

Symmetry turned a hard three-dimensional integral into an easy one (p. 3).

## The parallel axis theorem

Often the body turns about some other axis, such as a rod swung about one end (29.4 transcript, p. 1). If the axis through S is parallel to an axis through the centre of mass, a distance $d$ away (p. 1, where the lecturer leaves out the word "squared"; notes, Eq. (16.2.16), p. 16-10):

<Formula label="Parallel axis theorem" tex="I_S = I_{\text{cm}} + m\,d^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (16.2.16), p. 16-10" />

For the rod about its end, $d = L/2$ and $I_{\text{end}} = \frac{1}{12}mL^2 + \frac{1}{4}mL^2 = \frac{1}{3}mL^2$ (p. 2). Integrating directly from 0 to $L$ gives the same, $\int_0^L \frac{m}{L}x'^2\,dx' = \frac{1}{3}mL^2$ (p. 2; notes, Eqs. (16.2.17)–(16.2.18), p. 16-11). Likewise, a disc spinning about a point on its rim has $I = \frac{1}{2}mR^2 + mR^2 = \frac{3}{2}mR^2$ (notes, Example 16.3, p. 16-11). So the moment about the centre of mass is all you need to know (29.4 transcript, p. 2).

  caption="Moment of inertia of a thin uniform rod about a perpendicular axis a distance d from its centre, in units of mL², computed from the parallel axis theorem I = mL²/12 + md². It is smallest for the axis through the centre of mass." />

**Why it works** (29.6 transcript, pp. 1–3; notes, Appendix 16A, pp. 16-18–16-20). For each $dm$, the perpendicular vector from the axis through S equals the fixed vector $\vec d$ between the axes plus the perpendicular vector $\vec r_{\text{cm},\perp}$ from the axis through the centre of mass. Squaring, $r_{S,\perp}^2 = d^2 + 2\,\vec d\cdot\vec r_{\text{cm},\perp} + r_{\text{cm},\perp}^2$, and integrating over the body:

<Formula label="Proof of the parallel axis theorem" tex="I_S = \int d^2\,dm + 2\,\vec d\cdot\int \vec r_{\text{cm},\perp}\,dm + \int r_{\text{cm},\perp}^2\,dm = m\,d^2 + 0 + I_{\text{cm}}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (16.A.8)–(16.A.12), pp. 16-19–16-20" />

The middle integral vanishes because positions measured from the centre of mass average to zero: that is the definition of the centre of mass (29.6 transcript, pp. 2–3). (The transcript writes the perpendicular distance as $d$ plus the $x$-component only, as if every element lay on the line joining the axes; with the vectors above the cross term vanishes in the same way, and the result is unchanged.)

<WorkedExample title="A baton and a ball">
<Problem>
(a) A 2.0 kg, 1.2 m rod (a baton) spins at 5.0 rad/s about its centre. Find its moment of inertia and kinetic energy; then repeat for the same rod swung about one end. (b) A 7.0 kg bowling ball of radius 0.11 m, treated as uniform, spins at 30 rad/s. Find its rotational kinetic energy.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The rod">
$I_{\text{cm}} = \frac{1}{12} \times 2.0 \times 1.2^2 = 0.24$ kg m², so $K = \frac{1}{2} \times 0.24 \times 5.0^2 = 3.0$ J. About the end, $I = \frac{1}{3} \times 2.0 \times 1.44 = 0.96$ kg m², four times as much, and at the same angular velocity $K = 12$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The ball">
$I = \frac{2}{5} \times 7.0 \times 0.11^2 \approx 0.0339$ kg m², so $K = \frac{1}{2} \times 0.0339 \times 900 \approx 15$ J. If the ball rolls without slipping, its centre moves at $v = R\omega = 0.11 \times 30 = 3.3$ m/s, so its translational energy is $\frac{1}{2} \times 7.0 \times 3.3^2 \approx 38$ J; the spin adds $\frac{2}{5}$ of that, since $\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{5}mR^2\omega^2 = \frac{2}{5}\cdot\frac{1}{2}mv^2$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="A spinning star">
A neutron star packs more than the Sun's mass into a ball about 10 km in radius, and the youngest pulsars spin many times a second. Treated as a uniform sphere of 1.4 solar masses and radius 10 km, $I = \frac{2}{5}mR^2 \approx 1 \times 10^{38}$ kg m². The Crab pulsar turns about 30 times a second (ATNF pulsar catalogue), $\omega \approx 190$ rad/s, so its rotational kinetic energy is about $2 \times 10^{42}$ J. As it slowly spins down, that energy powers the glowing Crab Nebula around it.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Rotational kinetic energy: $K_{\text{rot}} = \frac{1}{2}I_S\,\omega_z^2$, with $I_S = \int r^2\,dm$ and $r$ the perpendicular distance from the axis.
- Set up the integral with a coordinate system, an integration variable, $dm$ in terms of it, and the limits.
- About the centre: rod $\frac{1}{12}mL^2$, disc $\frac{1}{2}mR^2$, sphere $\frac{2}{5}mR^2$.
- Parallel axis theorem: $I_S = I_{\text{cm}} + md^2$; the rod about its end has $\frac{1}{3}mL^2$.
