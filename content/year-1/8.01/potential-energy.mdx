For a conservative force, the [last lesson](/courses/8-01/conservative-forces) showed, the work depends only on where you start and where you end. This lesson turns that into a new quantity, the **potential energy**, and with it the idea that energy is conserved.

## From path independence to potential energy

Take a conservative force $\vec F_c$ and its work from $A$ to $B$ (23.1 transcript, p. 1). Because the integral depends only on the end points, it can be written as a function of the final point minus the same function of the initial point. Calling that function $-U$ (the minus sign is a choice that pays off in a moment):

<Formula label="Potential energy" tex="W_c = \int_A^B \vec F_c\cdot d\vec s = -U(\vec r_B) + U(\vec r_A) = -\Delta U" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The change in potential energy is minus the work done by the conservative force (p. 1). For a system of two objects interacting through an internal conservative force, the notes define it the same way, with the relative displacement of the two objects (notes, Eq. (14.4), p. 14-9).

## Mechanical energy is conserved

Put this into the [work–kinetic energy theorem](/courses/8-01/work-and-kinetic-energy), $W = K_B - K_A$ (23.1 transcript, p. 2): $-U_B + U_A = K_B - K_A$. Collect each point on its own side:

<Formula label="Conservation of mechanical energy" tex="\tfrac12 m v_A^2 + U_A = \tfrac12 m v_B^2 + U_B \;\Rightarrow\; K + U = E_\text{mech} = \text{constant}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

The points $A$ and $B$ were arbitrary, so $K + U$ has the same value everywhere: the total **mechanical energy** $E_\text{mech}$ is constant when only conservative forces act, $\Delta K + \Delta U = 0$ (pp. 2–3; notes, Eqs. (14.17)–(14.18), p. 14-14). Kinetic energy lost is stored as potential energy, and can be fully recovered.

Only **changes** in $U$ have physical meaning, so you may choose any convenient zero, just as you choose an origin for positions (p. 3). When non-conservative forces also do work, the total work is $W_c + W_{nc}$, and then (23.1 transcript, p. 3; 23.3 transcript, p. 1)

<Formula label="With non-conservative forces" tex="W_{nc} = \Delta K + \Delta U" cite="reading4" at="p. 1" />

so the mechanical energy is no longer constant: friction, for example, removes it.

## Gravity near the Earth

Raise a mass $m$ from height $y_i$ to $y_f$ (23.2 transcript, pp. 1–2). Gravity, $-mg\,\hat j$, does work $-mg(y_f - y_i)$ on any path, so

<Formula label="Gravitational potential energy near the Earth" tex="\Delta U = U_f - U_i = -W_g = mg\,(y_f - y_i) = mg\,\Delta y" cite="reading3" at="p. 2" />

Raising an object ($\Delta y > 0$) increases $U$: gravity points down, the displacement up, so the work is negative and $\Delta U$ positive. Moving sideways leaves $U$ unchanged, and lowering decreases it (p. 2; notes, Eq. (14.4), p. 14-11).

## Choosing a reference point

Pick a reference point $y_p$ with potential energy $U_p$ and measure every other value from it (23.3 transcript, pp. 1–2). Then $(U_f - U_p) - (U_i - U_p) = U_f - U_i$: the reference cancels from every change. For gravity, the convenient choice is $U = 0$ at $y = 0$, which gives the familiar formula (p. 2; notes, Eq. (14.7), p. 14-12):

<Formula label="Potential energy function, gravity near the Earth" tex="U(y) = mgy, \qquad U(0) = 0" cite="reading4" at="p. 2" />

<WorkedExample title="How high does it go?">
<Problem>
A 0.50 kg ball is thrown straight up at 12 m/s. Ignoring air resistance, how high does it rise? What is its potential energy at the top, taking $U = 0$ at the launch point?
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy at the start and the top">
Start: $K = \tfrac12 \times 0.50 \times 12^2 = 36$ J, $U = 0$. Top: $K = 0$, $U = mgh$.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve">
$mgh = 36$ J gives $h = 36/(0.50 \times 9.8) \approx 7.3$ m, the same as $v^2/2g$: the mass cancels. At the top $U = 36$ J.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A spring

A spring with force $-kx\,\hat i$, where $x$ is the stretch from the relaxed length, positive or negative (23.4 transcript, pp. 1–3). With $d\vec s = dx\,\hat i$, the change in potential energy from stretch $x_i$ to $x_f$ is

<Formula label="Spring: change in potential energy" tex="\Delta U = -\int_{x_i}^{x_f} (-k x')\,dx' = \tfrac12 k x_f^2 - \tfrac12 k x_i^2" cite="reading5" at="p. 2" />

This is minus the work done **by the spring on the block**, not the work of whoever stretches it (p. 2). The natural reference is the relaxed length, $U(0) = 0$, which gives a parabola (p. 3; notes, Eqs. (14.9)–(14.12), pp. 14-12–14-13):

## Inverse-square gravity

Anywhere outside a planet, gravity is the inverse-square force $-\frac{GMm}{r^2}\hat r$ from a planet of mass $M$ (23.5 transcript, pp. 1–4). In polar coordinates $d\vec s = dr\,\hat r + r\,d\theta\,\hat\theta$; since $\hat r\cdot\hat\theta = 0$, only $dr$ counts. Three minus signs (from the definition, from the scalar product with the inward force, and from the integral of $1/r^2$) give

<Formula label="Gravity: change in potential energy" tex="U(r_f) - U(r_i) = -\int_{r_i}^{r_f}\left(-\frac{GMm}{r'^2}\right)dr' = -GMm\left(\frac{1}{r_f} - \frac{1}{r_i}\right)" cite="reading6" at="pp. 2–3" />

Here the convenient reference is **infinitely far away**, $U(\infty) = 0$, because $1/\infty = 0$ (p. 3; notes, Eqs. (14.15)–(14.16), p. 14-14):

<Formula label="Potential energy of gravitation" tex="U(r) = -\frac{GMm}{r}, \qquad U(\infty) = 0 \quad (r > r_p)" cite="reading6" at="pp. 3–4" />

It is negative everywhere, rising towards zero far away. In words: $U(r)$ is minus the work gravity does as the object comes in from infinity to distance $r$ (p. 4). It holds outside the planet and at its surface, $r \ge r_p$ (the transcript says $r > r_p$), because inside, the force is no longer $GMm/r^2$ (p. 3). (The transcript says $\hat\theta$ points "towards the center"; it is perpendicular to $\hat r$, along the circle, p. 2.)

<WorkedExample title="Escape speed and the limits of mgh">
<Problem>
For the Earth, $GM = 3.986\times10^{14}$ m³/s² and $R = 6.371\times10^6$ m (NASA fact sheet). (a) What launch speed lets an object just escape to infinity? (b) How much potential energy does a 1000 kg satellite gain going from the surface to 400 km up? Compare with $mgh$, using $g = 9.81$ m/s².
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Escape">
Just escaping means $K + U = 0$: $\tfrac12 m v^2 - GMm/R = 0$, so $v = \sqrt{2GM/R} \approx 11.2$ km/s, for any mass.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Up to 400 km">
$\Delta U = GMm\left(\frac1R - \frac{1}{R + h}\right) \approx 3.70\times10^9$ J, against $mgh = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 4\times10^5 \approx 3.92\times10^9$ J. Near the surface $mgy$ is excellent, but 400 km up it is already about 6% too high, because gravity weakens with height.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The universe's escape speed">
Treat a galaxy at distance $r$ from us as a test mass at the edge of a uniform sphere of density $\rho$, receding at Hubble's speed $v = Hr$. Its energy is $K + U = \tfrac12 m H^2 r^2 - \frac{G m}{r}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}\rho r^3$. It just escapes, with total energy zero, when $\rho = \frac{3H^2}{8\pi G}$: the **critical density**. For $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc that is about $9.2\times10^{-27}$ kg/m³, roughly five hydrogen atoms per cubic metre. Whether the universe is above or below it decides, in this Newtonian picture of a universe of matter alone, whether the expansion stops. (Dark energy, discovered in 1998, changes that conclusion.)
</Callout>

## Summary

- For a conservative force, $\Delta U = -W_c = -\int_A^B\vec F_c\cdot d\vec s$. Only changes in $U$ matter; the reference point is free.
- Mechanical energy $E_\text{mech} = K + U$ is constant when only conservative forces do work; in general $W_{nc} = \Delta K + \Delta U$.
- Gravity near the Earth: $U = mgy$. Spring: $U = \tfrac12 kx^2$. Inverse-square gravity: $U = -GMm/r$, zero at infinity, valid outside the planet.
