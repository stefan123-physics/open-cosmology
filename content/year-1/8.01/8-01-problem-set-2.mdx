This set practises the lessons on [Newton's laws](/courses/8-01/newtons-laws), on [the weight of a body near the Earth](/courses/8-01/universal-gravitation), on [the normal force and friction](/courses/8-01/contact-forces) and on [ropes and springs](/courses/8-01/tension-and-springs). The five problems follow MIT's Problem Set 2, in our own words. MIT's set comes with drawings; here each situation is described in words, so make your own sketch first. MIT publishes no solutions for this set, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. All five problems count for the score (60 points).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first: draw every body on its own with all the forces on it (a free-body diagram), choose axes, and write Newton's second law for each axis. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A formula box takes plain typing, for example `mu*g*(m1 + m2)`, `M*sqrt(g^2 + a^2)` or `atan(a/g)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Write `atan( )` for the angle whose tangent is the number in the brackets, and `asin( )`, `acos( )` likewise. Angles are in radians, so type `theta` and `alpha` as they stand. A number box accepts any value within 1% of ours, so keep at least three significant figures, and type it in the unit shown next to the box. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem or part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Stacked blocks" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A block of mass $m_2 = 4.8$ kg lies on a table. The table is so smooth that there is no friction between it and the block. A second block, of mass $m_1 = 2.2$ kg, lies on top of the first. Between the two blocks the coefficient of static friction is $\mu_s = 0.5$.

You push the **upper** block with a horizontal force of magnitude $F$. If the push is gentle, the two blocks move off together. If it is too hard, the upper block slips over the lower one.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find $F_\text{max}$, the largest push for which the two blocks still move together, in terms of $m_1$, $m_2$, $\mu_s$ and $g$. (MIT asks for the number only. This step with symbols is our addition.)

<Hint>

While the blocks move together they have the same acceleration. Which horizontal force makes the **lower** block accelerate? The table cannot do it.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Static friction can be no larger than $\mu_s N$, where $N$ is the normal force between the two blocks. That sets a largest acceleration for the lower block.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With that largest acceleration known, look at the two blocks as one body of mass $m_1 + m_2$. The push $F$ is the only horizontal force on it from outside.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$F_\text{max} =$" expr="mu*m1*g*(m1 + m2)/m2"
  vars={{ mu: [0.2, 0.9], m1: [1, 3], m2: [4, 8], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ mu: "\\mu_s", m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu*m1*g", hint: "That is the largest friction force between the blocks. The push must be larger than the friction, because the upper block accelerates too." },
    { expr: "mu*(m1 + m2)*g", hint: "That is the answer when the push acts on the lower block, so that friction has to accelerate the upper one. Here the push acts on the upper block, and friction has to accelerate the lower one." },
    { expr: "mu*m2*g*(m1 + m2)/m1", hint: "The normal force between the blocks is the weight of the upper block only, and the block that friction has to accelerate is the lower one. Have the two masses changed places?" },
    { expr: "mu*m1*g*m2/(m1 + m2)", hint: "Check a limit: a very heavy lower block hardly moves, so the push can be no larger than the largest friction force. Does your result agree?" }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The push acts on the upper block only. The lower block is dragged along by static friction, the one horizontal force on it. Static friction has a largest value, so the lower block has a largest acceleration. The blocks stay together as long as the pair needs no more acceleration than that.

<Step title="Forces on each block">

Take the direction of the push as positive. On the upper block act its weight $m_1 g$, the normal force $N$ of the lower block, the push $F$ and the static friction $f$ of the lower block, which points against the push. By Newton's third law the upper block pushes the lower one forwards with a friction force of the same size $f$. The vertical forces on the upper block balance, so $N = m_1 g$.

</Step>

<Step title="The second law for each block">

Let $a$ be the acceleration that the two blocks share. The lessons give the second law as

<Formula label="Newton's second law" tex="\vec F = m\,\vec a" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 7.3, Eq. (7.3.5), p. 7-7" />

Along the table this reads, for the upper and for the lower block:

<Formula label="Stacked blocks: the two equations of motion" tex="F - f = m_1 a, \qquad f = m_2 a" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

Adding them gives $F = (m_1 + m_2)\,a$: the pair moves as one body.

</Step>

<Step title="The limit of static friction">

Static friction can take any value up to a largest one:

<Formula label="Largest static friction" tex="(f_s)_\text{max} = \mu_s N" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (8.3.7), p. 8-15" />

With $N = m_1 g$ the lower block can be given at most the acceleration $a_\text{max} = \mu_s m_1 g / m_2$. The push that goes with it is

<Formula label="Largest push on the upper block" tex="F_\text{max} = (m_1 + m_2)\,a_\text{max} = \mu_s\,m_1 g\,\frac{m_1 + m_2}{m_2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The coefficient $\mu_s$ has no unit, so the result is a mass times an acceleration: a force. If the lower block is very heavy, $(m_1 + m_2)/m_2$ goes to 1 and $F_\text{max}$ goes to $\mu_s m_1 g$, the largest friction force: the lower block then hardly moves and the upper one slips as it would on a fixed surface. If the lower block is very light, friction can give it a large acceleration and $F_\text{max}$ becomes large.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

What is $F_\text{max}$ for the numbers given, in newtons?

<Hint>

Put $m_1 = 2.2$ kg, $m_2 = 4.8$ kg, $\mu_s = 0.5$ and $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ in your result of part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$F_\text{max} =$" value="15.7" unit="N" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-fmax" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers in the result of part (a), one step at a time, so that each step can be checked.

<Step title="The largest friction force and acceleration">

The largest static friction is $\mu_s m_1 g = 0.5 \times 2.2 \times 9.8 = 10.78$ N. It gives the lower block the acceleration $a_\text{max} = 10.78 / 4.8 = 2.25\ \text{m/s}^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The largest push">

The pair has a mass of $2.2 + 4.8 = 7.0$ kg, so $F_\text{max} = 7.0 \times 10.78 / 4.8 = 15.7$ N.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Look at the upper block alone. The push minus the friction is $15.72 - 10.78 = 4.94$ N, and its mass times the acceleration is $2.2 \times 2.246 = 4.94$ N. The two agree.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A truck that accelerates, with a bucket hanging behind it" points={20} mit="Problem 2">

A truck drives in a straight line on level ground and speeds up with a constant acceleration of magnitude $a$. A light rope that does not stretch is tied to the top of the truck's back door, and a bucket of mass $M$ hangs from its other end. When the truck starts to speed up the bucket swings about, but after a while it settles: it then trails behind the truck at a fixed distance, and the straight rope makes a fixed angle $\theta$ with the back door. On level ground the back door is vertical.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the angle $\theta$ at which the rope settles, in terms of $a$ and $g$.

<Hint>

Once the bucket has settled it moves exactly as the truck does. What is its acceleration, in size and in direction?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Only two forces act on the bucket: its weight and the pull of the rope. Write the second law for the horizontal and for the vertical direction.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Divide one of your two equations by the other. The tension drops out.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$\theta =$" expr="atan(a/g)"
  vars={{ a: [1, 5], g: [9, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "atan(g/a)", hint: "That is the angle between the rope and the level road. The question asks for the angle between the rope and the vertical back door." },
    { expr: "asin(a/g)", hint: "This would follow if the tension were as large as the weight. Here the rope must hold the bucket up and pull it forwards as well, so the tension is larger." },
    { expr: "a/g", hint: "That is the tangent of the angle, not the angle itself. Use atan( )." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A settled bucket has the truck's acceleration: $a$, horizontal and forwards. Its weight and the pull of the rope must add up to $M a$ in that direction.

<Step title="Forces and axes">

Take $x$ horizontal, in the direction of driving, and $y$ upwards. The weight is $Mg$ downwards. The bucket trails behind the truck, so the rope runs from the bucket forwards and upwards to the door, at the angle $\theta$ from the vertical. The pull of the rope on the bucket, of size $T$, has the part $T\sin\theta$ forwards and the part $T\cos\theta$ upwards.

</Step>

<Step title="The second law in components">

The bucket has no vertical acceleration and the horizontal acceleration $a$:

<Formula label="Bucket on level ground: the two components" tex="T\sin\theta = M a, \qquad T\cos\theta - M g = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The angle">

Divide the first equation by $T\cos\theta = Mg$:

<Formula label="Angle of the rope on level ground" tex="\tan\theta = \frac{a}{g}, \qquad \theta = \arctan\frac{a}{g}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ratio $a/g$ has no unit. The mass $M$ has dropped out: a heavy bucket and a light one hang at the same angle. For $a = 0$ the rope hangs straight down, and the harder the truck speeds up, the further the bucket trails. For example, $a = 3.0\ \text{m/s}^2$ gives $\theta = \arctan(3.0/9.8) = 17.0°$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the tension $T$ in the rope at this angle, in terms of $M$, $g$ and $a$.

<Hint>

You already have two equations with $T$ in them: one for $T\sin\theta$ and one for $T\cos\theta$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Square the two equations and add them. What is $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$T =$" expr="M*sqrt(g^2 + a^2)"
  vars={{ M: [1, 5], a: [1, 5], g: [9, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "M*g", hint: "That is the tension when the truck does not speed up. Here the rope must also pull the bucket forwards." },
    { expr: "M*(g + a)", hint: "The two parts of the tension, M g upwards and M a forwards, are at right angles. Their sizes do not simply add." },
    { expr: "M*g^2/sqrt(g^2 + a^2)", hint: "That is the part of the weight along the rope. It would equal the tension only if the bucket had no acceleration along the rope, but the bucket's forward acceleration has a part along the rope." },
    { expr: "M*sqrt(g^2 - a^2)", hint: "The two parts of the tension are at right angles, so their squares add. Check the sign under the root." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The rope's pull has an upward part that carries the weight and a forward part that gives the acceleration. The tension is the length of the vector with these two parts.

<Step title="Square and add">

From part (a), $T\sin\theta = Ma$ and $T\cos\theta = Mg$. Squaring and adding gives $T^2 = M^2 (a^2 + g^2)$, so

<Formula label="Tension on level ground" tex="T = M\sqrt{g^2 + a^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is a mass times an acceleration: a force. For $a = 0$ it is $Mg$, the weight, and for any other $a$ it is larger. With $M = 5.0$ kg and $a = 3.0\ \text{m/s}^2$ it is $5.0 \times \sqrt{9.8^2 + 3.0^2} = 51.2$ N, a little more than the weight of 49.0 N.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

The truck comes to a stretch of road that slopes **downhill** at the angle $\alpha$ to the horizontal. It drives down the slope and still speeds up with an acceleration of magnitude $a$, now pointing down along the road. The back door is at right angles to the road, so it leans with the truck. The rope settles again, at an angle $\theta$ to the back door that may differ from before. Count $\theta$ as positive when the bucket trails behind the truck.

Find this new angle $\theta$, in terms of $g$, $\alpha$ and $a$. (You may take $a > g\sin\alpha$, so that the bucket does trail behind. This remark is ours, not MIT's.)

<Hint>

The acceleration now points along the sloping road, and $\theta$ is measured from a line at right angles to the road. Which axes make both of these simple?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Take one axis along the road and one at right angles to it. Split the weight into its parts along these axes: the tension and the acceleration are easy already.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Along the road, gravity now helps to speed the bucket up. Write the second law along both axes and divide, as in part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$\theta =$" expr="atan((a - g*sin(alpha))/(g*cos(alpha)))"
  vars={{ a: [5, 8], g: [9, 10], alpha: [0.1, 0.4] }}
  names={{ alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "atan(a/g)", hint: "That is the angle on level ground. On the slope the weight has a part along the road, and its part at right angles to the road is smaller." },
    { expr: "atan((a + g*sin(alpha))/(g*cos(alpha)))", hint: "This would hold for an acceleration that points up the slope. The truck speeds up while driving downhill, so the part of the weight along the road points the same way as the acceleration." },
    { expr: "atan(a*cos(alpha)/(g - a*sin(alpha)))", hint: "That is the angle between the rope and the true vertical. The back door leans with the truck, by the angle alpha." },
    { expr: "atan((a - g*sin(alpha))/g)", hint: "At right angles to the road the rope balances only a part of the weight, not all of it." },
    { expr: "(a - g*sin(alpha))/(g*cos(alpha))", hint: "That is the tangent of the angle, not the angle itself. Use atan( )." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same two forces act, and the bucket again has the truck's acceleration. Only the directions have changed: the acceleration lies along the road and the angle is counted from the normal to the road. So use axes along the road and at right angles to it.

<Step title="Forces and axes">

Take $x$ along the road, pointing downhill, and $y$ at right angles to the road, pointing away from it. The back door lies along $y$. The weight makes the angle $\alpha$ with the $y$ axis, so it has the part $Mg\sin\alpha$ along $+x$ (downhill) and the part $Mg\cos\alpha$ along $-y$ (into the road). The bucket trails behind the truck, which is uphill of the door, so the rope pulls it with the part $T\sin\theta$ along $+x$ and the part $T\cos\theta$ along $+y$.

</Step>

<Step title="The second law in components">

The acceleration is $a$ along $+x$ and zero along $y$:

<Formula label="Bucket on the slope: the two components" tex="T\sin\theta + M g\sin\alpha = M a, \qquad T\cos\theta - M g\cos\alpha = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The angle">

Bring the weight to the right in the first equation and divide by the second:

<Formula label="Angle of the rope on the slope" tex="\tan\theta = \frac{a - g\sin\alpha}{g\cos\alpha}, \qquad \theta = \arctan\frac{a - g\sin\alpha}{g\cos\alpha}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\alpha = 0$ this is $\tan\theta = a/g$, the result of part (a). For $a = g\sin\alpha$ the angle is zero. That is the acceleration of a body that slides down the slope without friction: a truck that rolls freely and the bucket then speed up together under gravity alone, and the rope only has to hold the bucket off the road, so it lies along the door. For a smaller $a$ the formula gives a negative angle: the bucket would have to hang in front of the door, and in practice it rests against it.

A number: with $a = 3.0\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $\alpha = 10°$ the angle is $\arctan(1.298/9.651) = 7.7°$, less than the 17.0° on level ground.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

Find the new tension $T$ in the rope, in terms of $M$, $g$, $\alpha$ and $a$.

<Hint>

Use the same step as in part (b), now with the two equations of part (c).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="$T =$" expr="M*sqrt(g^2 + a^2 - 2*a*g*sin(alpha))"
  vars={{ M: [1, 5], a: [5, 8], g: [9, 10], alpha: [0.1, 0.4] }}
  names={{ alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "M*sqrt(g^2 + a^2)", hint: "That is the tension on level ground. On the slope the weight helps to speed the bucket up, so the rope has less to do." },
    { expr: "M*sqrt(g^2 + a^2 + 2*a*g*sin(alpha))", hint: "This would hold for an acceleration that points up the slope. Here the acceleration and the part of the weight along the road point the same way." },
    { expr: "M*g*cos(alpha)", hint: "That is only the part of the tension along the back door. The rope also pulls the bucket along the road." },
    { expr: "M*(a - g*sin(alpha))", hint: "That is only the part of the tension along the road. The rope also holds the bucket off the road." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in part (b): the tension is the length of a vector whose two parts are known from the component equations.

<Step title="Square and add">

From part (c), $T\sin\theta = M(a - g\sin\alpha)$ and $T\cos\theta = Mg\cos\alpha$. Squaring and adding, and using $\sin^2\alpha + \cos^2\alpha = 1$:

<Formula label="Tension on the slope" tex="T = M\sqrt{(a - g\sin\alpha)^2 + g^2\cos^2\alpha} = M\sqrt{g^2 + a^2 - 2 a g\sin\alpha}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\alpha = 0$ this is $M\sqrt{g^2 + a^2}$, the result of part (b). For $a = g\sin\alpha$ it is $Mg\cos\alpha$, the force that holds a body on a slope at right angles to it, which fits the check of part (c). For $\alpha = 90°$ and $a = g$ it is zero: truck and bucket are in free fall together and the rope is slack.

A number: with $M = 5.0$ kg, $a = 3.0\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $\alpha = 10°$ the tension is 48.7 N, less than the 51.2 N on level ground.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A wedge against a wall" points={10} mit="Problem 3">

A wedge stands on a horizontal floor with its upright side against a wall. Its sloping side makes the angle $\theta$ with the floor and runs down, away from the wall. A box of mass $m$ slides down this slope. There is no friction anywhere: not between the box and the wedge, not between the wedge and the floor, and not between the wedge and the wall. The wedge does not move. Give your answers in terms of $\theta$, $m$ and $g$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find $F_\text{total}$, the magnitude of the sum of all the forces that act on the box.

<Hint>

Which forces act on the box? There are only two. You may add them as vectors, but there is a shorter way.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The sum of all forces on a body is its mass times its acceleration. In which direction can the box accelerate, and which force has a part in that direction?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$F_\text{total} =$" expr="m*g*sin(theta)"
  vars={{ m: [1, 5], g: [9, 10], theta: [0.2, 0.7] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*g", hint: "That is the weight alone. The wedge also pushes on the box, and that push cancels a part of the weight." },
    { expr: "m*g*cos(theta)", hint: "That is the size of the normal force, one of the two forces on the box. The question asks for the size of the sum of both." },
    { expr: "m*g*(1 + cos(theta))", hint: "The weight and the normal force point in different directions, so add them as vectors, not their sizes." },
    { expr: "m*g*tan(theta)", hint: "Check the case of a very steep slope: the box then nearly falls freely, and the sum of the forces can be no larger than its weight." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two forces act on the box: its weight and the normal force of the slope. At right angles to the slope the box does not accelerate, so there the forces cancel. What is left is the part of the weight along the slope.

<Step title="Forces and axes">

Take one axis along the slope, pointing downhill, and one at right angles to the slope. The weight $mg$ makes the angle $\theta$ with the normal to the slope, so it has the part $mg\sin\theta$ along the slope and the part $mg\cos\theta$ into the slope. The normal force $N$ points out of the slope. There is no friction.

</Step>

<Step title="The second law in components">

The box stays on the slope, so it has no acceleration at right angles to it. Along the slope it has the acceleration $a$:

<Formula label="Box on the wedge: the two components" tex="N - m g\cos\theta = 0, \qquad m g\sin\theta = m a" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The sum of the forces">

The sum of the forces has no part at right angles to the slope, and along the slope it is $mg\sin\theta$:

<Formula label="Total force on the box" tex="F_\text{total} = m a = m g\sin\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

It points down the slope.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\theta = 0$ the box lies on a level surface and the total force is zero. For $\theta = 90°$ the slope is a vertical wall that does not push on the box at all, and the total force is the weight $mg$: free fall. With $m = 2.0$ kg and $\theta = 30°$ the total force is $2.0 \times 9.8 \times 0.5 = 9.8$ N.

Adding the two forces as vectors gives the same. With horizontal and vertical axes, the normal force $N = mg\cos\theta$ has the horizontal part $N\sin\theta$ and the vertical part $N\cos\theta$, so the sum has the parts $mg\sin\theta\cos\theta$ (horizontal) and $mg - mg\cos^2\theta = mg\sin^2\theta$ (downwards). Its length is $mg\sin\theta\sqrt{\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta} = mg\sin\theta$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find $F_{ww}$, the magnitude of the force that the wall exerts on the wedge.

<Hint>

The wedge is at rest, so the forces on it add up to zero. Draw the wedge on its own. Which bodies touch it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Without friction, the wall can only push horizontally and the floor can only push vertically. Which other force on the wedge has a horizontal part?

</Hint>

<Hint>

By Newton's third law the box pushes on the wedge with a force as large as the normal force on the box, at right angles to the slope. Take its horizontal part.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$F_{ww} =$" expr="m*g*sin(theta)*cos(theta)"
  vars={{ m: [1, 5], g: [9, 10], theta: [0.2, 0.7] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*g*cos(theta)", hint: "That is the whole force of the box on the wedge. The wall only has to balance its horizontal part." },
    { expr: "m*g*sin(theta)", hint: "That is the size of the total force on the box, which points along the slope. The wall pushes horizontally: which horizontal force on the wedge must it balance?" },
    { expr: "m*g*cos(theta)^2", hint: "That is the vertical part of the push of the box on the wedge. The floor balances it. The wall balances the horizontal part." },
    { expr: "m*g*sin(theta)^2", hint: "That is the vertical part of the total force on the box. The wall pushes horizontally: take the horizontal part of the push of the box on the wedge." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The wedge stays at rest, so the horizontal forces on it cancel. Only two forces on the wedge have a horizontal part: the push of the box and the push of the wall.

<Step title="Forces on the wedge">

On the wedge act its own weight (downwards), the normal force of the floor (upwards, as there is no friction), the normal force of the wall (horizontal, away from the wall) and the push of the box. By Newton's third law,

<Formula label="Newton's third law" tex="\vec F_{1,2} = -\vec F_{2,1}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 7.4, Eq. (7.4.1), p. 7-8" />

the box pushes on the wedge with a force of the size $N = mg\cos\theta$ of part (a), at right angles to the slope and into the wedge. The slope runs down, away from the wall, so this push points down and towards the wall. That is why the wedge stays pressed against the wall and does not move.

</Step>

<Step title="The horizontal direction">

The normal to the slope makes the angle $\theta$ with the vertical, so the push of the box has the horizontal part $N\sin\theta$, towards the wall. The wall must balance it:

<Formula label="Force of the wall on the wedge" tex="F_{ww} = N\sin\theta = m g\sin\theta\cos\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take the box and the wedge together as one system. The only horizontal force on it from outside is the push of the wall, so that push must equal the mass of the box times the horizontal part of its acceleration (the wedge has none). The box has the acceleration $g\sin\theta$ along the slope, with the horizontal part $g\sin\theta\cos\theta$, away from the wall. This gives $F_{ww} = mg\sin\theta\cos\theta$ again.

The force is zero for $\theta = 0$ (the box does not move) and for $\theta = 90°$ (the box falls freely and does not touch the wedge), and it is largest at $\theta = 45°$, where it is half the weight of the box. With $m = 2.0$ kg and $\theta = 30°$ it is $2.0 \times 9.8 \times 0.5 \times 0.866 = 8.49$ N.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Dragging two blocks" points={10} mit="Problem 4">

Block 1, of mass $m_1$, lies on a horizontal table, and block 2, of mass $m_2$, lies on top of block 1. A light cord joins the two blocks. It runs horizontally from the right side of block 2 to a pulley that is fixed to the table, passes around the pulley and runs horizontally back to the right side of block 1. The pulley is fixed in place and turns without friction. The coefficient of kinetic friction is $\mu$ at both sliding surfaces: between the two blocks, and between block 1 and the table.

Find $F$, the magnitude of the horizontal force that drags block 2 to the **left** at constant speed, in terms of $\mu$, $m_1$, $m_2$ and $g$.

<Hint>

When block 2 moves to the left, the cord makes block 1 move to the right at the same speed. Both blocks move at constant speed. What does that say about the sum of the forces on each?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Kinetic friction on a body points against its motion relative to the surface it slides on. Block 1 slides on two surfaces at once. Find the normal force at each surface first.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the balance of horizontal forces for block 1 to find the tension in the cord. Then write it for block 2, on which $F$, the cord and one friction force act.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-f" label="$F =$" expr="mu*g*(m1 + 3*m2)"
  vars={{ mu: [0.1, 0.8], m1: [1, 5], m2: [1, 5], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ mu: "\\mu", m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu*g*(m1 + m2)", hint: "That is only the friction between block 1 and the table. The two blocks also rub against each other, because they move in opposite directions." },
    { expr: "mu*g*(m1 + 2*m2)", hint: "The friction between the blocks acts on both blocks: on block 2 directly, and on block 1, where it adds to the tension in the cord. Have you counted it on both?" },
    { expr: "2*mu*m2*g", hint: "Block 1 also slides over the table. What is the normal force between block 1 and the table?" },
    { expr: "mu*g*(2*m1 + 3*m2)", hint: "The table carries the weight of both blocks, so the friction there is mu (m1 + m2) g. It acts once, on block 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both blocks move at constant speed, so the forces on each add up to zero. Block 2 goes left and block 1 goes right. Kinetic friction opposes each sliding motion, and the cord passes the friction on block 1 on to block 2.

<Step title="Normal forces and friction forces">

Block 2 does not accelerate vertically, so block 1 carries it with the normal force $N_2 = m_2 g$. Block 1 is pressed down by its own weight and by block 2, so the table carries it with the normal force $N_1 = (m_1 + m_2)\,g$. The lessons give the size of kinetic friction:

<Formula label="Kinetic friction" tex="f_k = \mu_k N" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (8.3.5), p. 8-14" />

With the one coefficient $\mu$ of this problem, the friction between the blocks has the size $\mu m_2 g$ and the friction between block 1 and the table has the size $\mu (m_1 + m_2)\,g$.

</Step>

<Step title="Block 1">

Block 1 moves to the right. The cord pulls it to the right with the tension $T$. The table rubs against it to the left. Block 2 moves to the left over it, so block 2 also drags it to the left. The balance is

<Formula label="Dragging two blocks: block 1" tex="T = \mu m_2 g + \mu (m_1 + m_2)\,g" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Block 2">

The cord is light and the pulley turns freely, so the tension is the same $T$ at block 2, where it pulls to the right. The friction of block 1 on block 2 also points to the right, against the motion of block 2. The force $F$ to the left balances both:

<Formula label="Dragging two blocks: block 2" tex="F = T + \mu m_2 g" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The force">

Put the tension of block 1 into the balance of block 2:

<Formula label="Force that drags block 2 at constant speed" tex="F = \mu m_2 g + \mu m_2 g + \mu (m_1 + m_2)\,g = \mu g\,(m_1 + 3 m_2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is a force, as $\mu$ has no unit. Without friction ($\mu = 0$) no force is needed to keep the blocks moving. If block 2 has almost no mass, only the friction of block 1 on the table is left, $\mu m_1 g$, which the cord passes on. The mass $m_2$ counts three times: once in the friction on block 2 itself, once in the friction it causes on top of block 1, and once in the extra friction it causes under block 1.

A number: with $\mu = 0.25$, $m_1 = 3.0$ kg and $m_2 = 1.0$ kg the tension is $0.25 \times 9.8 \times 5.0 = 12.25$ N and the force is $0.25 \times 9.8 \times 6.0 = 14.7$ N.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Blocks and springs" points={10} mit="Problem 5">

<Part label="a" points={5}>

In arrangement A, a spring with spring constant $k$ hangs from the ceiling, and a block of mass $m$ hangs at rest from its lower end. The spring is then longer than its natural length by the distance $d$.

In arrangement B, a spring with the same constant $k$ lies horizontally. A rope is tied to each of its ends. Each rope runs over a pulley and down to a block of mass $m/2$ that hangs at rest. Take the ropes as light and the pulleys as free of friction.

Find $x$, the distance by which the spring in B is longer than its natural length, in terms of $d$.

<Hint>

Draw the spring of A on its own. The block pulls its lower end down. What pulls on its upper end, and how hard?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The stretch of a spring is set by its tension: the size of the pull at each of its ends. Find the tension of the spring in A and in B.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$x =$" expr="d/2"
  vars={{ d: [0.05, 0.5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "d", hint: "Adding the two pulls of B gives m g, but look at A again: there the ceiling pulls on the spring as hard as the block does. A stretched spring always has a pull at each end, and the stretch is set by one of them." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "The two pulls do cancel as a net force on the spring, which is why it stays in place. But a spring that is pulled at both ends is stretched." },
    { expr: "2*d", hint: "Each block of B has half the mass of the block of A. Is the pull at each end of the spring larger or smaller than in A?" }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A spring at rest is pulled equally hard at both of its ends, and its stretch is set by the size of that pull. In A the pulls come from the block and from the ceiling. In B they come from the two ropes.

<Step title="Arrangement A">

The block is at rest, so the spring pulls it up with a force as large as its weight, $mg$. The lessons give the size of the force of a spring that is stretched by $x$:

<Formula label="Size of the spring force" tex="F = k\,|x|" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (8.1.1), p. 8-3" />

So $k d = m g$. The spring itself is at rest and light, so the ceiling pulls its upper end up with $mg$ as well: the spring is pulled with $mg$ at each end.

</Step>

<Step title="Arrangement B">

Each block is at rest, so each rope carries the tension $\tfrac{1}{2} m g$. A free pulley only turns the direction of a light rope, so each rope pulls its end of the spring with $\tfrac{1}{2} m g$. The spring is pulled with $\tfrac{1}{2} m g$ at each end, so $k x = \tfrac{1}{2} m g$.

</Step>

<Step title="Compare">

Divide the result of B by that of A:

<Formula label="Stretch of the spring between two blocks" tex="\frac{x}{d} = \frac{\tfrac{1}{2} m g}{m g} = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad x = \frac{d}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Replace one block of B, with its rope and pulley, by a hook in a wall. The spring cannot tell the difference: the hook pulls with $\tfrac{1}{2} m g$, as the block did. What is left is arrangement A turned on its side, with a block of mass $m/2$. Half the mass gives half the stretch.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

A platform of mass $M$ rests on a pillar, at the height $h$ above the floor. The pillar stands under the centre of the platform. Two equal springs, each with spring constant $k_1$, join the platform to the floor below it. Two more equal springs, each with spring constant $k_2$, join the platform to the ceiling above it. The four springs are fixed to the platform at equal distances from its centre, one spring of each kind on either side. While the pillar is in place, all four springs have their natural length.

The pillar is taken away. Find the height $h_f$ at which the platform comes to rest, in terms of $h$, $M$, $g$, $k_1$ and $k_2$.

<Hint>

Suppose the platform ends up lower by a distance $y$. What happens to the length of the springs below it, and of the springs above it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

A spring that is squeezed pushes, and a spring that is stretched pulls. In which direction does each of the four springs act on the platform?

</Hint>

<Hint>

At rest, the four spring forces balance the weight. Solve for $y$, then take it from $h$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$h_f =$" expr="h - M*g/(2*(k1 + k2))"
  vars={{ h: [2, 3], M: [1, 5], g: [9, 10], k1: [200, 400], k2: [50, 150] }}
  names={{ k1: "k_1", k2: "k_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "h - M*g/(k1 + k2)", hint: "There are two springs of each kind, and all four act on the platform." },
    { expr: "h + M*g/(2*(k1 + k2))", hint: "Without the pillar the platform sinks, so the new height is less than h." },
    { expr: "h - M*g/(2*(k1 - k2))", hint: "When the platform sinks, the lower springs are squeezed and push up, and the upper springs are stretched and pull up. Both kinds act against the weight." },
    { expr: "M*g/(2*(k1 + k2))", hint: "That is the distance by which the platform sinks. The question asks for its new height above the floor." },
    { expr: "h - M*g*(k1 + k2)/(2*k1*k2)", hint: "This treats a lower and an upper spring as joined end to end. Here both are fixed to the platform and change length by the same amount, so their forces add." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** When the platform sinks by $y$, each lower spring gets shorter by $y$ and each upper spring gets longer by $y$. All four then push or pull the platform upwards. It comes to rest where these four forces balance its weight.

<Step title="The spring forces">

The springs sit at equal distances on both sides of the centre, so the platform stays level and every spring changes its length by the same $y$. A lower spring is squeezed by $y$ and pushes the platform up with $k_1 y$. An upper spring is stretched by $y$ and pulls the platform up with $k_2 y$.

</Step>

<Step title="Balance of forces">

At rest the four spring forces carry the weight:

<Formula label="Platform at rest on four springs" tex="2 k_1 y + 2 k_2 y = M g, \qquad y = \frac{M g}{2\,(k_1 + k_2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The new height">

The platform started at the height $h$ and has sunk by $y$:

<Formula label="Height of the platform at rest" tex="h_f = h - y = h - \frac{M g}{2\,(k_1 + k_2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A force divided by a spring constant (in N/m) is a length, as it must be. A heavier platform sinks further and stiffer springs let it sink less. Without the upper springs ($k_2 = 0$) the platform rests on the two lower springs alone and sinks by $Mg/(2k_1)$. The four springs together act as one spring with the constant $2(k_1 + k_2)$.

A number: with $M = 12$ kg, $k_1 = 300$ N/m, $k_2 = 100$ N/m and $h = 0.50$ m the platform sinks by $12 \times 9.8/800 = 0.147$ m, to $h_f = 0.353$ m.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
