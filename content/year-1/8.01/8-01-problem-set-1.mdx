(MIT's course notes do work through the car and the bicycle of Problem 1, as Example 4.6 of Chapter 4. Our solution was written on its own and reaches the same results.) All five problems count for the score (100 points).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `v0*t - g*t^2/2` or `sqrt(2*h/g)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type an angle as `th` and use `sin(th)` and `cos(th)`. A number box takes a bare number in the unit shown next to the box. Unless a box asks for more digits, give 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. MIT's set gives no points, so the points per problem and per part are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem or part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## What you need from the lessons

Three of the five problems have an acceleration that changes with time. The formulas for constant acceleration do not hold there. Use the integrals of the lesson on acceleration instead:

<Formula label="Velocity and position by integration" tex="v(t) = v(t_i) + \int_{t_i}^{t} a(t')\,dt', \qquad x(t) = x(t_i) + \int_{t_i}^{t} v(t')\,dt'" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 4.6, Eqs. (4.5.19) and (4.5.21)" />

Here $t_i$ is any moment at which you already know the velocity and the position. Where the acceleration is constant, these integrals give the two familiar results:

<Formula label="Constant acceleration" tex="v(t) = v_0 + a\,t, \qquad x(t) = x_0 + v_0\,t + \tfrac12\,a\,t^2" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 4.5, Eqs. (4.4.2) and (4.4.9)" />

Air resistance is left out everywhere, and $g$ is the size of the acceleration of a falling object.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A car and a bicycle rider" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

A car drives along a straight road, the $x$-axis, at the constant speed $v_0$. At $t = 0$ it passes the origin. At the time $t_1$ the driver begins to brake, harder and harder, until the car stands still at the time $t_2$. The $x$-component of the car's acceleration is

<Formula label="Acceleration of the car" tex="a_c(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & 0 < t < t_1 \\ -c\,(t - t_1) & t_1 < t < t_2 \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

where $c$ is a positive constant, an acceleration per unit of time (in this problem $c$ is not the speed of light).

<Part label="a" points={12}>

Find the $x$-component of the car's velocity, $v_c(t)$, and its position, $x_c(t)$, as functions of time. Use some or all of $v_0$, $c$, $t$, $t_1$ and $t_2$. Before the braking begins the answers are simple; write them down too. The two boxes ask for the braking stage, $t_1 < t < t_2$.

<Hint>

During the braking the acceleration is not constant, so $v = v_0 + at$ cannot be used. Which operation turns an acceleration into a change of velocity?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate the acceleration from the moment the braking begins, $t_1$, to the time $t$. What is the velocity at $t_1$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the position, integrate the velocity from $t_1$ to $t$, and do not forget where the car already is at $t_1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-v" label="$v_c(t) =$" expr="v0 - c*(t - t1)^2/2"
  vars={{ v0: [10, 20], c: [1, 3], t: [2, 3], t1: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ v0: "v_0", t1: "t_1" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "v0 - c*(t - t1)*(t - t1)", hint: "That multiplies the acceleration at the time t by the braking time so far, as if it had been that large all along. The acceleration grew from zero, so add it up with an integral." },
    { expr: "v0 - c*t^2/2 + c*t1*t", hint: "Try t = t1 in your formula: the velocity should still be v0 there. The braking begins at t1, so the integral starts at t1, not at zero." },
    { expr: "v0 + c*(t - t1)^2/2", hint: "This car speeds up. The acceleration is negative during the braking." },
    { expr: "-c*(t - t1)^2/2", hint: "That is only the change in velocity since t1. Add the velocity the car had at t1." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-a-x" label="$x_c(t) =$" expr="v0*t - c*(t - t1)^3/6"
  vars={{ v0: [10, 20], c: [1, 3], t: [2, 3], t1: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ v0: "v_0", t1: "t_1" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v0*(t - t1) - c*(t - t1)^3/6", hint: "That is the distance covered since t1. The car was not at the origin at t1: add the distance it covered before the braking began." },
    { expr: "v0*t - c*(t - t1)^3/3", hint: "The braking term of the velocity carries a factor of one half. Integrating the square of (t − t1) then adds a factor of one third." },
    { expr: "v0*t - c*(t - t1)^3/2", hint: "The integral of the square of (t − t1) is its cube divided by 3, and the velocity already carries a factor of one half." },
    { expr: "v0*t + c*(t - t1)^3/6", hint: "This car gets further than one that never brakes. Check the sign of the braking term." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Treat the two stages one after the other. In each stage, integrate the acceleration to get the velocity and the velocity to get the position, starting from what is known at the beginning of that stage.

<Step title="Before the braking">

For $0 < t < t_1$ the acceleration is zero, so the velocity stays what it was and the position grows evenly from the origin:

<Formula label="The car before it brakes" tex="v_c(t) = v_0, \qquad x_c(t) = v_0\,t \qquad (0 < t \le t_1)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

At the end of this stage the car has the velocity $v_0$ and is at $x_c(t_1) = v_0 t_1$. These are the starting values of the next stage.

</Step>

<Step title="Velocity during the braking">

Integrate the acceleration from $t_1$ to $t$:

<Formula label="Velocity of the braking car" tex="v_c(t) = v_0 + \int_{t_1}^{t} \big(-c\,(t' - t_1)\big)\,dt' = v_0 - \tfrac12\,c\,(t - t_1)^2 \qquad (t_1 \le t \le t_2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Position during the braking">

Integrate that velocity from $t_1$ to $t$ and add the position at $t_1$:

<Formula label="Position of the braking car" tex="x_c(t) = v_0\,t_1 + \int_{t_1}^{t} \Big(v_0 - \tfrac12\,c\,(t' - t_1)^2\Big)\,dt' = v_0\,t - \tfrac16\,c\,(t - t_1)^3 \qquad (t_1 \le t \le t_2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

The terms $v_0 t_1$ and $v_0 (t - t_1)$ have joined into $v_0 t$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $c$ is in m/s³, so $c\,(t - t_1)^2$ is a velocity and $c\,(t - t_1)^3$ is a length.

At $t = t_1$ both formulas of the braking stage give the values of the first stage, $v_0$ and $v_0 t_1$: the velocity and the position do not jump.

Differentiate back: the derivative of $x_c$ is $v_0 - \tfrac12 c\,(t - t_1)^2 = v_c$, and the derivative of $v_c$ is $-c\,(t - t_1) = a_c$.

The answers do not need $t_2$. It only marks the end of the interval, and it follows from the other constants, as part (b) shows.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

A bicycle rider rides along the same road at the constant speed $v_b$. At $t = 0$ the rider is 17 m behind the car. The rider reaches the car at the moment the car comes to rest. The car has $v_0 = 12$ m/s, $t_1 = 1$ s and $c = 6$ m/s³. Find the speed of the bicycle, in metres per second, to two significant figures.

<Hint>

First find when the car stops. What is its velocity at that moment?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Then find where the car is at that moment, with your result of part (a).

</Hint>

<Hint>

The rider starts at $x = -17$ m and must be at the same place as the car at the time $t_2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$v_b =$" value="15" unit="m/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-bicycle-speed" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The car's velocity is zero at $t_2$: that gives $t_2$. The position formula then says where the car stops, and the rider must cover that distance plus the 17 m in the time $t_2$.

<Step title="When the car stops">

Set the velocity of the braking stage to zero at $t = t_2$:

<Formula label="The time at which the car stops" tex="0 = v_0 - \tfrac12\,c\,(t_2 - t_1)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_2 = t_1 + \sqrt{\frac{2 v_0}{c}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

The positive root is the right one, because $t_2$ comes after $t_1$. With the numbers, $\sqrt{2 \times 12/6} = 2$ s, so $t_2 = 3$ s.

</Step>

<Step title="Where the car stops">

$x_c(t_2) = v_0 t_2 - \tfrac16 c\,(t_2 - t_1)^3 = 12 \times 3 - \tfrac16 \times 6 \times 2^3 = 36 - 8 = 28$ m.

</Step>

<Step title="The bicycle">

The rider moves at constant velocity from $x = -17$ m, so $x_b(t) = -17\ \text{m} + v_b t$. Meeting the car at $t_2$ means $x_b(t_2) = x_c(t_2)$:

<Formula label="Speed of the bicycle" tex="v_b = \frac{x_c(t_2) + 17\ \text{m}}{t_2} = \frac{45\ \text{m}}{3\ \text{s}} = 15\ \text{m/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The rider is faster than the car ever was, 15 m/s against 12 m/s. That is needed: the rider has 17 m to make up in 3 s, while the car covers 12 m in its first second and only 16 m more in the two seconds of braking.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A trip in a lift" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

A person stands in a lift (an elevator) that is at rest on the first floor of a tall building. The lift goes up to the sixth floor, a known height $h$ above the start. First it speeds up for a time $T$ with a constant acceleration of unknown size $a$. Then it moves at constant velocity for a time $4T$. Last, it brakes for a time $T$ with an acceleration of the same size $a$, and stops at the sixth floor.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Sketch the velocity $v(t)$ of the lift during the trip. The shape should be right; the vertical scale does not matter. (MIT's tip: the graph of $v(t)$ makes part (b) much easier.)

<Hint>

During each of the three stages, is the acceleration zero, positive or negative? What does a constant acceleration look like in a graph of velocity against time?

</Hint>

<Hint>

What is the velocity at the end of the first stage, in terms of $a$ and $T$? The velocity cannot jump from one stage to the next.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The slope of a graph of velocity against time is the acceleration. A constant acceleration gives a straight sloping line, and zero acceleration gives a level line.

<Step title="The three stages">

From $t = 0$ to $T$ the velocity rises along a straight line from zero to $aT$. From $T$ to $5T$ it stays at $aT$. From $5T$ to $6T$ it falls along a straight line, as steep as the first one, back to zero. The graph is a trapezium (a trapezoid):

<Plot variable="t" domain={[0, 6.5]} range={[0, 1.4]} xLabel="t / T" yLabel="v / (aT)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "t < 1 ? t : (t < 5 ? 1 : (t < 6 ? 6 - t : 0))", label: "v(t)" }
  ]}
  caption="The velocity of the lift, computed from the three stages: time in units of T, velocity in units of aT. The area under the line is the height h." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check your sketch">

Your sketch is right if it starts and ends at zero, has two sloping straight parts of equal steepness and equal duration, and has a level part four times as long between them.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Find $a$, the size of the acceleration, in terms of $h$ and $T$.

<Hint>

In a graph of velocity against time, what is the meaning of the area under the line?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Split the area into two triangles and a rectangle. Their sides are known in terms of $a$ and $T$, and the whole area is $h$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$a =$" expr="h/(5*T^2)"
  vars={{ h: [10, 30], T: [1, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "h/(18*T^2)", hint: "That treats the whole trip of 6T as one stage of constant acceleration. The lift speeds up only during the first T." },
    { expr: "h/(4*T^2)", hint: "That counts only the stage at constant velocity. The lift also gains height while it speeds up and while it brakes." },
    { expr: "h/(6*T^2)", hint: "That counts the two sloping parts as if the lift had its full velocity there. Under a sloping line the area is a triangle, half of the rectangle." },
    { expr: "h/(9*T^2/2)", hint: "The lift also gains height while it brakes: there are two triangles under the graph, not one." },
    { expr: "2*h/T^2", hint: "That would bring the lift to the sixth floor in the first stage alone. Only part of h is covered while it speeds up." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The displacement is the area under the graph of velocity against time. The whole area must be $h$.

<Step title="The area under the graph">

The top velocity is $aT$. Each sloping part is a triangle of width $T$ and height $aT$, and the level part is a rectangle of width $4T$ and height $aT$:

<Formula label="Height gained by the lift" tex="h = \tfrac12\,(aT)\,T + (aT)(4T) + \tfrac12\,(aT)\,T = 5\,a\,T^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for the acceleration">

<Formula label="Acceleration of the lift" tex="a = \frac{h}{5\,T^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: a length divided by a time squared is an acceleration.

The same follows without the graph. The first stage covers $\tfrac12 aT^2$ and ends with the velocity $aT$. The second covers $aT \times 4T = 4aT^2$. The third covers $aT \times T - \tfrac12 aT^2 = \tfrac12 aT^2$. The sum is $5aT^2$ again.

A number of our own: for $h = 20$ m and $T = 2$ s the acceleration is $20/(5 \times 4) = 1$ m/s², the top velocity is 2 m/s and the trip takes 12 s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A rocket and a falling stone" points={20} mit="Problem 3">

A model rocket stands at the foot of a vertical cliff. Take the $y$-axis upwards (unit vector $\hat j$), with the origin at the foot of the cliff. At $t = 0$ the rocket is launched straight up from rest. The $y$-component of its acceleration changes with time:

<Formula label="Acceleration of the rocket" tex="a_y(t) = A - B\,t" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 3" />

where $A$ and $B$ are positive constants. Also at $t = 0$, a small stone is let go from rest at the top of the cliff, at a height $h$ straight above the rocket. This height is greater than the greatest height the rocket reaches. The stone falls with the acceleration of gravity, of size $g$, downwards. The stone hits the rocket at the very moment the rocket is at its greatest height.

Find the height $h$ in terms of $A$, $B$ and $g$.

<Hint>

What is true of the rocket's velocity at the moment it is highest?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate the rocket's acceleration to get its velocity, and find the time at which the velocity is zero again. Integrate once more for the rocket's height at that time.

</Hint>

<Hint>

At that same time the stone, which started at $y = h$, is at the height of the rocket. Write the stone's height as a function of time.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-h" label="$h =$" expr="2*A^2*(A + 3*g)/(3*B^2)"
  vars={{ A: [5, 30], B: [1, 6], g: [9, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*A^3/(3*B^2)", hint: "That is the greatest height of the rocket. The stone started above it and has fallen down to there." },
    { expr: "2*g*A^2/B^2", hint: "That is how far the stone has fallen. Add the height at which it meets the rocket." },
    { expr: "A^2*(2*A + 3*g)/(6*B^2)", hint: "You used the moment at which the acceleration is zero. The rocket is then at its fastest. It is highest when its velocity is zero." },
    { expr: "2*A^2*(A - 3*g)/(3*B^2)", hint: "The stone starts above the meeting point, so h is the rocket's height plus the distance the stone fell." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The rocket is highest when its velocity is zero. That fixes the time of the hit. At that time the rocket and the stone are at the same height, which fixes $h$.

<Step title="The rocket's velocity and height">

The rocket starts from rest at $y = 0$. Integrate the acceleration once for the velocity and once more for the height:

<Formula label="Velocity and height of the rocket" tex="v_r(t) = \int_0^t (A - B\,t')\,dt' = A\,t - \tfrac12\,B\,t^2, \qquad y_r(t) = \int_0^t v_r(t')\,dt' = \tfrac12\,A\,t^2 - \tfrac16\,B\,t^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The time of greatest height">

The velocity is zero at $t = 0$, the launch, and once more at the time we call $t_m$:

<Formula label="Time at which the rocket is highest" tex="A\,t_m - \tfrac12\,B\,t_m^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_m = \frac{2A}{B}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

Before $t_m$ the velocity is positive and after it negative, so this is the highest point. The height there is

<Formula label="Greatest height of the rocket" tex="y_r(t_m) = \tfrac12\,A\left(\frac{2A}{B}\right)^2 - \tfrac16\,B\left(\frac{2A}{B}\right)^3 = \frac{2A^3}{B^2} - \frac{4A^3}{3B^2} = \frac{2A^3}{3B^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The stone">

The stone starts from rest at $y = h$ and has the constant acceleration $-g$:

<Formula label="Height of the stone" tex="y_s(t) = h - \tfrac12\,g\,t^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The hit">

At $t_m$ the two heights are equal, $y_s(t_m) = y_r(t_m)$:

<Formula label="Height of the cliff" tex="h = \frac{2A^3}{3B^2} + \tfrac12\,g\left(\frac{2A}{B}\right)^2 = \frac{2A^3}{3B^2} + \frac{2gA^2}{B^2} = \frac{2A^2\,(A + 3g)}{3B^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $A$ and $g$ are in m/s² and $B$ is in m/s³, so $A^3/B^2$ is in m³/s⁶ divided by m²/s⁶, a length.

Limits: a larger $B$ cuts the rocket's flight short, and $h$ gets smaller. Without gravity ($g = 0$) the stone would hang where it was let go, and $h$ would be the rocket's greatest height.

A number of our own: for $A = 30$ m/s², $B = 6$ m/s³ and $g = 9.81$ m/s² the rocket is highest at $t_m = 10$ s, at 500 m. In those 10 s the stone falls 490.5 m, so $h = 990.5$ m.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A throw and a catch" points={25} mit="Problem 4">

A ball is thrown with speed $v_0$ at an angle $\theta$ above the level ground. Take the $x$-axis along the ground in the direction of the throw (unit vector $\hat i$) and the $y$-axis upwards (unit vector $\hat j$), with the origin at the point from which the ball is thrown. A person stands at a horizontal distance $d$ from that point, and the ball is going to pass over this person's head. At the moment the ball is thrown, the person starts to run along the $x$-axis, from rest, with an acceleration that grows with time: its $x$-component is $B\,t$, where $B$ is a positive constant. The person catches the ball at exactly the height from which it was thrown. Gravity points down and has size $g$.

Find the constant $B$ in terms of some or all of $\theta$, $g$, $d$ and $v_0$.

<Hint>

The ball does not care about the runner. When does it come back to the height from which it was thrown, and where is it then?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The runner's acceleration is not constant. Integrate $B\,t$ twice to find the runner's position, starting from rest at $x = d$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

At the time of the catch the runner and the ball have the same $x$. That is one equation for the one unknown $B$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$B =$" expr="3*g^2*(v0^2*sin(2*th) - g*d)/(4*v0^3*sin(th)^3)"
  vars={{ v0: [20, 30], th: [0.5, 1.0], g: [9, 10], d: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ v0: "v_0", th: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*g^2*cos(th)/(2*v0*sin(th)^2)", hint: "That holds for a runner who starts at the point of the throw. This runner starts a distance d ahead and has less ground to cover." },
    { expr: "3*g^2*(v0^2*sin(2*th) + g*d)/(4*v0^3*sin(th)^3)", hint: "The runner starts ahead of the thrower, on the side where the ball lands. The runner covers the ball's range minus d." },
    { expr: "g*(v0^2*sin(2*th) - g*d)/(2*v0^2*sin(th)^2)", hint: "Check the units: this is an acceleration, and B is an acceleration per unit of time. The distance run is not half the acceleration times the time squared, because the acceleration grows." },
    { expr: "3*g^2*(v0^2*sin(2*th) - g*d)/(8*v0^3*sin(th)^3)", hint: "Integrate B t twice. The velocity is B t squared over 2, and that factor of one half stays when you integrate again." },
    { expr: "g^2*(v0^2*sin(2*th) - g*d)/(4*v0^3*sin(th)^3)", hint: "Integrate B t twice. The velocity is B t squared over 2, and integrating the square of t adds a factor of one third." },
    { expr: "6*g^2*(v0^2*sin(2*th) - g*d)/(v0^3*sin(th)^3)", hint: "Check the time of flight. The ball is back at its starting height after twice the time it needs to reach the top." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The ball fixes the time and the place of the catch. The runner must cover the distance from $x = d$ to that place in that time, and the distance run depends on $B$.

<Step title="The ball">

The lesson on projectiles gives the ball's position, here with the origin at the point of the throw:

<Formula label="Position of the ball" tex="x(t) = v_0\cos\theta\;t, \qquad y(t) = v_0\sin\theta\;t - \tfrac12\,g\,t^2" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 5.2, Eqs. (5.1.5), (5.1.6) and (5.1.16) with a_y = −g" />

The ball is back at $y = 0$ when $t\,(v_0\sin\theta - \tfrac12 g t) = 0$. The root $t = 0$ is the throw. The other root is the time of the catch, which we call $t_f$, and the ball is then at the distance we call $R$:

<Formula label="Time and place of the catch" tex="t_f = \frac{2 v_0\sin\theta}{g}, \qquad R = v_0\cos\theta\;t_f = \frac{2 v_0^2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{g} = \frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The runner">

The runner starts from rest at $x = d$ with the acceleration $B\,t$. Integrate twice:

<Formula label="Velocity and position of the runner" tex="v_p(t) = \int_0^t B\,t'\,dt' = \tfrac12\,B\,t^2, \qquad x_p(t) = d + \int_0^t \tfrac12\,B\,t'^{\,2}\,dt' = d + \tfrac16\,B\,t^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The catch">

At $t_f$ the runner is where the ball is, $x_p(t_f) = R$:

<Formula label="The constant B" tex="B = \frac{6\,(R - d)}{t_f^{\,3}} = \frac{3 g^3}{4 v_0^3\sin^3\theta}\left(\frac{v_0^2\sin 2\theta}{g} - d\right) = \frac{3 g^2\left(v_0^2\sin 2\theta - g\,d\right)}{4 v_0^3\sin^3\theta}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $g^2 v_0^2/v_0^3$ is (m/s²)² divided by m/s, which is m/s³, an acceleration per unit of time. The term with $g\,d$ has the same units.

Limits: if the person already stands where the ball comes down, $d = R$, then $B = 0$ and there is no need to run. The ball passes over the person's head only if $d$ is less than $R$, and then $B$ is positive, as the problem says.

A number of our own: for $v_0 = 12$ m/s, $\theta = 60^\circ$, $d = 8$ m and $g = 9.81$ m/s² the ball flies for $t_f = 2.12$ s and lands at $R = 12.7$ m. The runner must cover 4.71 m, which needs $B = 2.97$ m/s³, and catches the ball while running at $\tfrac12 B t_f^2 = 6.67$ m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Two cans that collide in the air" points={20} mit="Problem 5">

Mary throws a can straight up. Then she throws a second can straight up, from the same point and with the same speed, so that it hits the first one. She wants the cans to collide at the height $h = 5.0$ m above the point of the throw, and she needs $t_1 = 4.0$ s between the two throws. Take $g = 9.81$ m/s².

<Part label="a" points={10}>

How long after the first throw do the cans collide? Give the time in seconds, to four significant figures.

<Hint>

Take the $y$-axis upwards with the origin at the point of the throw, and call the unknown speed $v_0$. Write the height of each can as a function of time. The second can has been in the air for a time $t_1$ less than the first.

</Hint>

<Hint>

At the collision both cans are at the height $h$. That gives two equations for two unknowns: the speed $v_0$ and the time of the collision.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Subtract one equation from the other. The result connects $v_0$ and the time of the collision without $h$. Use it to remove $v_0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$t_c =$" value="4.240" unit="s" tolerance="0.05%" calc="p5-collision-time" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both cans follow the same motion, the second one $t_1$ later. At the collision both are at the height $h$: the first on its way down, the second on its way up. Two equations, two unknowns.

<Step title="The heights of the two cans">

With the first throw at $t = 0$, the speed $v_0$ and the constant acceleration $-g$:

<Formula label="Heights of the two cans" tex="y_1(t) = v_0\,t - \tfrac12\,g\,t^2, \qquad y_2(t) = v_0\,(t - t_1) - \tfrac12\,g\,(t - t_1)^2 \quad (t \ge t_1)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Equal heights">

Call the time of the collision $t_c$. Setting $y_1(t_c) = y_2(t_c)$ and working out the brackets leaves $0 = -v_0 t_1 + g\,t_c t_1 - \tfrac12 g\,t_1^2$. Divide by $t_1$:

<Formula label="Speed and time of collision" tex="v_0 = g\left(t_c - \tfrac12\,t_1\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

In words: the first can is at its top at the time $v_0/g$, and the collision comes half a waiting time, $\tfrac12 t_1$, after that.

</Step>

<Step title="The height of the collision">

Put this $v_0$ into $y_1(t_c) = h$:

<Formula label="Equation for the time of the collision" tex="h = g\left(t_c - \tfrac12\,t_1\right)t_c - \tfrac12\,g\,t_c^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t_c^2 - t_1\,t_c - \frac{2h}{g} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

The collision comes after the second throw, so take the positive root:

<Formula label="Time of the collision" tex="t_c = \tfrac12\left(t_1 + \sqrt{t_1^2 + \frac{8h}{g}}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put in the numbers">

$8h/g = 40/9.81 = 4.077$ s², so $t_c = \tfrac12\left(4.0 + \sqrt{16 + 4.077}\right) = \tfrac12\,(4.0 + 4.4808) = 4.240$ s.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

If the collision were at the height of the throw, $h = 0$, the formula would give $t_c = t_1$: the first can would come back just as the second leaves, which is right: a can thrown up comes back to the point of the throw after $2v_0/g$, and here that time equals $t_1$. For $h = 5.0$ m the collision comes only 0.240 s after the second throw, because the second can is still fast and low.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Find the speed with which Mary throws the cans, in metres per second, to four significant figures.

<Hint>

Part (a) gives a relation between the speed and the time of the collision.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$v_0 =$ (4 significant figures)" value="21.98" unit="m/s" tolerance="0.05%" calc="p5-throw-speed" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equal heights of part (a) tie the speed to the time of the collision.

<Step title="The speed">

<Formula label="Speed of the throw" tex="v_0 = g\left(t_c - \tfrac12\,t_1\right) = \tfrac12\,g\,\sqrt{t_1^2 + \frac{8h}{g}} = 9.81 \times 2.2404 = 21.98\ \text{m/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

Use the unrounded time here, or the square root directly: with the rounded 2.240 s the last figure can come out one too low.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With this speed a can is at its top after $v_0/g = 2.240$ s, at the height $v_0^2/(2g) = 24.62$ m. The first can then falls for $\tfrac12 t_1 = 2.000$ s, which is $\tfrac12 \times 9.81 \times 2.000^2 = 19.62$ m, and $24.62 - 19.62 = 5.00$ m: the height Mary wanted.

The whole flight of a can back to her hand takes $2 v_0/g = 4.481$ s, more than the 4.0 s between the throws, so the first can is indeed still in the air.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
