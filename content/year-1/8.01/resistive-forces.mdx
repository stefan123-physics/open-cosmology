So far friction has not depended on speed. An object moving through a **fluid**, a liquid or a gas, feels a drag force that does: it depends on the object's speed, size and shape, and on the fluid's density, **viscosity** (how strongly it resists flowing) and compressibility. In general this is complicated and is measured, not derived. Two simple cases can be solved exactly, and they are the subject of this lesson (14.1 transcript, p. 1; notes, Section 8.6, p. 8-27).

## Two models of drag

**Fast objects in air.** The drag grows as the square of the speed, and depends on the cross-sectional area $A$ the object presents to the motion and on the density $\rho$ of the air. Traditionally it is written with a factor ½ and a dimensionless **drag coefficient** $C_D$ that depends on the shape (14.1 transcript, pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Air drag at high speed" tex="F_\text{drag} = \tfrac12\,C_D\,\rho\,A\,v^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.6.1), p. 8-27" />

For ordinary shapes $C_D$ is of order 1: the notes' table gives 0.47 for a sphere and 1.05 for a cube facing the flow (notes, Table 8.1, p. 8-27).

**Slow objects in a viscous fluid.** A marble dropped in molasses and the same marble dropped in water move very differently: the difference is the **viscosity** $\eta$ of the fluid. At low speed the drag is proportional to the speed and to the viscosity. For a sphere of radius $R$, fluid dynamics gives an exact result, **Stokes' law** (p. 2):

<Formula label="Stokes' law" tex="\vec F_\text{drag} = -6\pi\,\eta\,R\,\vec v" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

The minus sign means the drag opposes the velocity. Turned round, Stokes' law measures viscosity: drop spheres through a fluid and time them (p. 2). The SI unit of $\eta$ is the pascal second, kg m⁻¹ s⁻¹ (notes, p. 8-27).

## Low speed: separating variables

Drop an object, a marble in a vat of oil, from rest, with drag $-\alpha\vec v$ and the positive direction **down** (14.2 transcript, p. 1). The coefficient $\alpha$ is a force divided by a speed, so its units are kg/s. [The transcript says "kilogram per inverse second"; the units are kilograms per second.] For a sphere, $\alpha = 6\pi\eta R$.

Newton's second law: the acceleration is not constant, so write it as $dv_y/dt$ (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Equation of motion with linear drag" tex="mg - \alpha\,v_y = m\,\frac{dv_y}{dt} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{dv_y}{dt} = g - \frac{\alpha}{m}\,v_y" cite="reading3" at="pp. 1–2" />

This is a first-order linear differential equation with a constant term. Separate the variables, writing the left side as $dv_y\big/\bigl[-\tfrac{\alpha}{m}\bigl(v_y - \tfrac{mg}{\alpha}\bigr)\bigr] = dt$, and integrate from rest at $t = 0$ (p. 2). The left side gives a natural logarithm; exponentiating both sides (p. 3):

<Formula label="Velocity with linear drag" tex="v_y(t) = \frac{mg}{\alpha}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = \frac{m}{\alpha}" cite="reading3" at="p. 3" />

The **time constant** $\tau$ has units kg/(kg/s) = s, as it must for $t/\tau$ to be a pure number. As $t \to \infty$ the exponential vanishes, and the velocity levels off at the **terminal velocity** (pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Terminal velocity with linear drag" tex="v_\text{term} = \frac{mg}{\alpha}" cite="reading3" at="pp. 3–4" />

**Check.** At terminal velocity the velocity no longer changes, so the acceleration is zero and the drag balances gravity: $mg = \alpha v_\text{term}$. The same answer, with no integration (p. 4).

**Heavier falls faster.** $\alpha$ depends only on the object's size and shape, not on how dense it is. So of two balls of the same radius, the heavier reaches the larger terminal speed (p. 4). The notes add that for a sphere of density $\rho_m$ the terminal speed grows as $R^2$, so bigger marbles fall faster too (notes, Eq. (8.6.12), p. 8-31); the same effect is used to sort molecules of different sizes in a fluid (notes, p. 8-28).

<WorkedExample title="A ball bearing in syrup">
<Problem>
A steel ball bearing of radius 1.0 mm and mass $3.3 \times 10^{-5}$ kg falls through a fluid of viscosity 1.0 Pa s (about that of glycerol). Ignore buoyancy, as the notes do. Find $\alpha$, the terminal speed and the time constant.
</Problem>
<Step title="Drag coefficient">
$\alpha = 6\pi\eta R = 6\pi \times 1.0 \times 1.0 \times 10^{-3} \approx 0.0188$ kg/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Terminal speed">
$v_\text{term} = mg/\alpha = 3.3 \times 10^{-5} \times 9.81/0.0188 \approx 0.017$ m/s: under 2 cm per second.
</Step>
<Step title="Time constant">
$\tau = m/\alpha \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-3}$ s. After a few milliseconds the ball is at terminal speed; the rest of the fall is at constant velocity, which is why timing such a fall gives the viscosity.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## High speed: drag proportional to v²

Now an object falling through **air** at speed, with drag $-\beta v^2\,\hat j$ (14.3 transcript, p. 1). The coefficient $\beta$ is a force over a speed squared, so its units are kg/m; comparing with the air-drag formula, $\beta = \tfrac12 C_D\rho A$. Newton's second law, down positive:

<Formula label="Equation of motion with quadratic drag" tex="mg - \beta v^2 = m\,\frac{dv}{dt} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{dv}{dt} = g - \frac{\beta}{m}\,v^2" cite="reading4" at="p. 1" />

This equation is **nonlinear** ($v$ appears squared), but separation of variables still works (p. 1). After pulling out $g$, substitute $u = \sqrt{\beta/(mg)}\;v$, which turns the left side into $\int du/(1 - u^2)$ (pp. 2–3). [The lecture also points out a limit of the model: an object starting from rest moves slowly at first, where linear drag would be the better model. A full treatment would switch models during the fall; the lecture keeps one model for simplicity (p. 2).]

The integral is $\tfrac12\ln\bigl[(1 + u)/(1 - u)\bigr]$ (p. 3). [The transcript says "integration by parts"; the usual route is partial fractions, $\tfrac{1}{1 - u^2} = \tfrac12\bigl(\tfrac1{1 - u} + \tfrac1{1 + u}\bigr)$.] Exponentiating and solving for $v$ (pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Velocity with quadratic drag" tex="v(t) = \sqrt{\frac{mg}{\beta}}\;\frac{1 - e^{-t/\tau}}{1 + e^{-t/\tau}}, \qquad \tau = \frac{1}{2g}\sqrt{\frac{mg}{\beta}}" cite="reading4" at="p. 4" />

As $t \to \infty$ the exponentials vanish, leaving the terminal velocity (pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="Terminal velocity with quadratic drag" tex="v_\text{term} = \sqrt{\frac{mg}{\beta}}" cite="reading4" at="pp. 4–5" />

[The transcript first says "mg over beta" (p. 4); the square root, found again from $mg - \beta v_\text{term}^2 = 0$ on p. 5, is correct.] **Check**, as before: at terminal velocity the two forces balance, $mg = \beta v_\text{term}^2$ (p. 5).

**A neater form** (this page's own step): since $\tau = v_\text{term}/(2g)$, the fraction is a hyperbolic tangent, $v(t) = v_\text{term}\tanh(g t/v_\text{term})$. For small $t$ it starts as $v \approx g t$, free fall, as it must.

<WorkedExample title="A skydiver">
<Problem>
A skydiver of mass 80 kg falls spread out, with $C_D \approx 1.0$ and area 0.70 m², through air of density 1.2 kg/m³. Find $\beta$, the terminal speed, the time constant and the time to reach 90% of terminal speed.
</Problem>
<Step title="Drag coefficient">
$\beta = \tfrac12 C_D\rho A = \tfrac12 \times 1.0 \times 1.2 \times 0.70 = 0.42$ kg/m.
</Step>
<Step title="Terminal speed">
$v_\text{term} = \sqrt{80 \times 9.81/0.42} \approx 43.2$ m/s, about 156 km/h.
</Step>
<Step title="How long it takes">
$\tau = v_\text{term}/(2g) \approx 2.20$ s. From $\tanh(gt/v_\text{term}) = 0.9$, $gt/v_\text{term} \approx 1.472$, so $t \approx 1.472 \times 43.2/9.81 \approx 6.5$ s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The notes add one more case: an object **coasting** horizontally at high speed with no other force. Then $dv/dt = -(\beta/m)v^2$, and separating variables gives $v(t) = v_0/(1 + \beta v_0 t/m)$: the object slows down but, in this model, never quite stops (notes, Example 8.6, Eq. (8.6.18), p. 8-32).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A cosmic terminal velocity">
In the "new inflation" picture of the early universe, a field rolls down a very flat hill of energy. The expansion of the universe acts on it like a drag force (often called Hubble friction), so the field soon stops accelerating and rolls at a steady, slow speed set by the balance of the push downhill and the friction: a terminal velocity. This "slow roll" is what lets inflation last long enough, and then end smoothly. [The friction term is standard inflation theory; the 8.286 lectures describe the slow roll but do not derive it. Like Stokes drag, this friction is proportional to the speed.]
</Callout>

## Summary

- Drag depends on the object (speed, size, shape) and the fluid (density, viscosity). Two models: $F \propto v$ at low speed (Stokes: $6\pi\eta R v$ for a sphere) and $F = \tfrac12 C_D\rho A v^2$ at high speed.
- With drag, Newton's second law is a differential equation for $v(t)$; separate the variables and integrate.
- Linear drag, from rest: $v = (mg/\alpha)(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, $\tau = m/\alpha$.
- Quadratic drag, from rest: $v = v_\text{term}\,(1 - e^{-t/\tau})/(1 + e^{-t/\tau})$, with $v_\text{term} = \sqrt{mg/\beta}$ and $\tau = v_\text{term}/(2g)$.
- Terminal velocity: the speed at which drag balances gravity, found directly from $\sum F = 0$. Heavier objects of the same shape fall faster.
