The [previous page](/courses/8-01/newtons-second-law-circular-motion) set out how to use Newton's second law for circular motion. The notes for Lesson 11 then work through six examples. They all follow the same recipe:
1. recognise the circular motion, so that the acceleration points to the centre with size $r\omega^2 = v^2/r$;
2. draw a free-body diagram;
3. write the second law along the radius (and, if needed, along the vertical);
4. solve.

This page explains each of the notes' methods and works it with a new situation and new numbers.

## A stationary orbit

A satellite that circles a planet once per rotation of the planet stays above the same spot. Gravity supplies its inward acceleration, so with the satellite's mass $m_s$, the planet's mass $m$ and the planet's rotation rate $\omega$:

<Formula label="Radial second law for the satellite" tex="G\,\frac{m_s m}{r_s^2} = m_s\,\omega^2 r_s" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.3.4), p. 9-7" />

The satellite's mass cancels, and the orbit radius follows:

<Formula label="Radius of a stationary orbit" tex="r_s = \left(\frac{G\,m}{\omega^2}\right)^{1/3}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.3.5), p. 9-7" />

The notes apply this to the Earth: a geostationary orbit at about 6.6 Earth radii from the centre (notes, p. 9-7).

<WorkedExample title="A satellite that stays above one spot on Mars">
<Problem>
Mars has $Gm = 4.2828 \times 10^{13}$ m³/s² and turns once in 24.6229 hours (its sidereal day). How far from its centre must a satellite orbit to stay above the same spot?
</Problem>
<Step title="Rotation rate">
$\omega = 2\pi/(24.6229 \times 3600\ \text{s}) \approx 7.088 \times 10^{-5}$ s⁻¹.
</Step>
<Step title="Orbit radius">
$r_s = (Gm/\omega^2)^{1/3} = \bigl(4.2828 \times 10^{13}/(7.088 \times 10^{-5})^2\bigr)^{1/3} \approx 2.04 \times 10^7$ m, about 20,400 km from the centre.
</Step>
<Step title="Height">
Mars's mean radius is 3389.5 km, so the satellite would be about 17,000 km above the surface.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A double star

When the two bodies have comparable masses, neither stays put: both circle a central point between them (later called the centre of mass), at fixed distances $r_1$ and $r_2$ with $r_1 + r_2 = s$, the separation. Each star feels the same gravitational pull $Gm_1m_2/s^2$ and has the same angular speed $\omega$. The second law for each, solved for its radius, gives

<Formula label="Radius of each star's circle" tex="r_1 = \frac{G\,m_2}{\omega^2 s^2}, \qquad r_2 = \frac{G\,m_1}{\omega^2 s^2}" cite="reading1" at="Example 9.2, pp. 9-9–9-10" />

and adding them to get $s$:

<Formula label="Period of a double star" tex="T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = \left(\frac{4\pi^2 s^3}{G\,(m_1 + m_2)}\right)^{1/2}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.3.8), p. 9-10" />

Both masses appear. Kepler's law on the previous page had only the central mass because it assumed $m_1 \ll m_2$; for the Earth and Moon the difference is small, about 0.17 days in a month (notes, p. 9-10).

<WorkedExample title="Two Suns one astronomical unit apart">
<Problem>
Two stars, each of one solar mass, orbit each other at a fixed separation of 1 astronomical unit (the Earth–Sun distance). How long is their period?
</Problem>
<Step title="Compare with the Earth">
The Earth orbits one solar mass at 1 AU in 1 year. Here $s$ is the same and $m_1 + m_2$ is twice as large, so $T$ is smaller by $\sqrt2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Result">
$T = 1\ \text{yr}/\sqrt2 \approx 0.707$ yr, about 258 days. Measuring such periods and separations is how astronomers **weigh stars**.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Masses on a whirling string

Two small masses are tied along one string to a shaft that turns at a constant $\omega$: $m_1$ at distance $d$, $m_2$ at $2d$ (gravity neglected). The outer string pulls only the outer mass; the inner string must also hold the inner mass and the pull of the outer string. The second law along the radius for each mass gives the two tensions:

<Formula label="Tensions in the two strings" tex="T_2 = m_2\,(2d)\,\omega^2, \qquad T_1 = d\,\omega^2\,(m_1 + 2m_2)" cite="reading1" at="Example 9.3, pp. 9-11–9-12" />

<WorkedExample title="A whirling toy">
<Problem>
A 0.20 kg mass is 0.30 m from the shaft and a 0.10 kg mass is 0.60 m out, on the same string, turning at 10 rad/s. Find both tensions.
</Problem>
<Step title="Outer string">
$T_2 = 0.10 \times 0.60 \times 10^2 = 6.0$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Inner string">
$T_1 = 0.30 \times 10^2 \times (0.20 + 2 \times 0.10) = 12.0$ N, twice as much: it holds both masses on their circles.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A spinning rope

A uniform rope of mass $m$ and length $L$ spins about a shaft at its end. Each short piece $\Delta r$ at distance $r$ has mass $(m/L)\,\Delta r$ and is pulled inwards by the difference of the tensions at its two ends. Dividing by $\Delta r$ and letting it shrink turns the second law into a **differential equation**:

<Formula label="Tension gradient" tex="\frac{dT}{dr} = -\frac{m}{L}\,\omega^2 r" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.3.11), p. 9-13" />

Integrate from the free end, where the tension is zero, inwards:

<Formula label="Tension along a spinning rope" tex="T(r) = \frac{m\,\omega^2}{2L}\,\bigl(L^2 - r^2\bigr)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.3.13), p. 9-13" />

The tension is largest at the shaft and falls to zero at the end, like the whirling string with infinitely many small masses.

## A ball circling inside a cone

A small object slides without friction round the inside of a cone whose axis is vertical, with constant speed $v$. Only two forces act: the normal force of the wall and gravity. With $\theta$ the half-angle of the cone measured from the vertical axis, the normal force makes the angle $\theta$ with the horizontal, so its horizontal (inward) part is $N\cos\theta$ and its vertical part $N\sin\theta$:

<Formula label="Second law inside the cone" tex="N\cos\theta = \frac{mv^2}{r}, \qquad N\sin\theta = mg" cite="reading1" at="Example 9.5, p. 9-14" />

Dividing one by the other, as for the banked turn, gives the radius at which it circles:

<Formula label="Radius of the circle in the cone" tex="\tan\theta = \frac{rg}{v^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{v^2\tan\theta}{g}" cite="reading1" at="Example 9.5, p. 9-14" />

independent of the mass, because both the needed inward force and the normal force are proportional to it.

<WorkedExample title="A marble in a funnel">
<Problem>
A marble circles inside a smooth funnel whose wall makes 30° with the vertical axis, at 2.0 m/s. Find the radius of its circle and the time per turn.
</Problem>
<Step title="Radius">
$r = v^2\tan\theta/g = 2.0^2 \times \tan 30°/9.81 \approx 4.0 \times 0.577/9.81 \approx 0.235$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Period">
$T = 2\pi r/v = 2\pi \times 0.235/2.0 \approx 0.74$ s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A coin on a turntable

A coin rides on a turntable at distance $R$ from the centre. Here **static friction** supplies the inward force; the normal force balances the weight, $N = mg$. Friction can grow only up to $\mu_s N$, so the coin is on the point of slipping when

<Formula label="Largest angular speed without slipping" tex="\mu_s\,m g = m R\,\omega_{\max}^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_s\,g}{R}}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (9.3.20)–(9.3.21), p. 9-16" />

<WorkedExample title="A coin on a record player">
<Problem>
A coin lies 0.15 m from the centre of a record player; the coefficient of static friction is 0.40. Does it slip at 45 or at 78 turns per minute?
</Problem>
<Step title="Limit">
$\omega_{\max} = \sqrt{0.40 \times 9.81/0.15} \approx 5.11$ rad/s, which is $5.11 \times 60/(2\pi) \approx 48.8$ turns per minute.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
45 rpm is 4.71 rad/s, below the limit: the coin stays. 78 rpm is 8.17 rad/s, well above: the coin slides off.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology">
The double star shows how motion **weighs** a system: the period and separation give the total mass, $m_1 + m_2 = 4\pi^2 s^3/(GT^2)$. In 1933 Fritz Zwicky used the same principle, that the speeds of bodies held together by gravity reveal the mass holding them, for galaxies in the Coma cluster (with the virial theorem, since the galaxies move on random orbits rather than circles). They moved so fast that the cluster needed far more mass than its galaxies showed: an early sign of **dark matter**.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Every problem uses the same recipe: inward acceleration $r\omega^2 = v^2/r$, a free-body diagram, the second law along the radius (and the vertical).
- Stationary orbit: $r_s = (Gm/\omega^2)^{1/3}$, with ω the planet's rotation rate.
- Double star: both stars circle a common point; $T = 2\pi\sqrt{s^3/(G(m_1 + m_2))}$.
- Whirling masses and a spinning rope: the tension is largest near the shaft; for the rope $T(r) = (m\omega^2/2L)(L^2 - r^2)$.
- Inside a cone, the normal force supplies the inward pull: $r = v^2\tan\theta/g$. On a turntable, static friction does, up to $\omega_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s g/R}$.
