Momentum describes how things move along; for turning we need its rotational counterpart. Newton's second law for a system says the external force is the rate of change of momentum; the notes' chapter on angular momentum builds the analogue, with torque and **angular momentum** (notes, Section 19.1, p. 19-2).

## Definition

A particle of mass $m$ has momentum $\vec p = m\vec v$. Angular momentum is always defined **about a point** S: draw the vector $\vec r_S$ from S to the particle (32.1 transcript, p. 1; notes, Eq. (19.2.1), p. 19-3):

<Formula label="Angular momentum about a point" tex="\vec L_S = \vec r_S \times \vec p" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Its direction comes from the right-hand rule: put $\vec r_S$ and $\vec p$ tail to tail and curl the fingers from the first to the second (pp. 1–2; notes, Section 19.2.2, pp. 19-4–19-5). Like every vector product, $\vec L_S$ is perpendicular to both $\vec r_S$ and $\vec p$, so perpendicular to the plane they span (p. 2). Its SI unit is kg m²/s, which is the same as N m s or J s, with no special name (notes, p. 19-3).

## Calculating it

Angular momentum is not only for rotating things: a particle moving in a **straight line** has it too (32.2 transcript, p. 1). And it has no single value: it depends on the choice of S, which is arbitrary, although some choices are easier or more useful (p. 1). As for torque, the magnitude can be read two ways (p. 1, which uses the moment arm; notes, Eqs. (19.2.2)–(19.2.6), p. 19-4):

<Formula label="Magnitude of the angular momentum" tex="|\vec L_S| = r_S\,p\sin\theta = r_\perp\,p = r_S\,p_\perp" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (19.2.2)–(19.2.6), p. 19-4" />

Here $r_\perp = r_S\sin\theta$ is the **moment arm**, the perpendicular distance from S to the line of the motion, and $p_\perp$ is the part of the momentum perpendicular to $\vec r_S$. For a ball moving straight up past S, the moment arm stays the same, so the angular momentum does not change as the ball moves; any point at the same perpendicular distance on the same side sees the same value (on the other side, the opposite sign), and a point farther away sees a larger one (pp. 1–2). For **circular** motion about the centre, $\vec r$ and $\vec v$ are always perpendicular, and the sign tells the sense: with "out of the page" positive, positive angular momentum means counterclockwise and negative clockwise (p. 2).

## A system about different points

Two particles move in opposite directions with equal momentum magnitude $p_1$, on lines a distance $2r$ apart, so the total momentum is zero (32.3 transcript, p. 1). About a point A midway between them, with $\vec r_{A,1} = -r\,\hat i$, $\vec p_1 = -p_1\,\hat j$, $\vec r_{A,2} = r\,\hat i$, $\vec p_2 = p_1\,\hat j$ (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Two particles, about A" tex="\vec L_A = (-r\,\hat i) \times (-p_1\,\hat j) + (r\,\hat i) \times (p_1\,\hat j) = 2r\,p_1\,\hat k" cite="reading4" at="p. 2" />

About a point B on the same axis through A and both particles, a distance $d$ beyond particle 2, the terms are $(2r + d)p_1\,\hat k$ and $-d\,p_1\,\hat k$, which again add to $2r\,p_1\,\hat k$ (pp. 2–3). That is no coincidence: **whenever the total momentum is zero, the angular momentum is the same about every point** (p. 4). The notes show why (the video course proves it in segment 33.4, in the next lesson): shifting the point from B to A adds $\vec r_{A,B} \times \vec p_{\text{sys}}$ (notes, Example 19.6, Eqs. (19.5.14)–(19.5.19), pp. 19-16–19-17):

<Formula label="Changing the point" tex="\vec L_A = \vec L_B + \vec r_{A,B} \times \vec p_{\text{sys}}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (19.5.18), p. 19-17" />

In particular, in the centre-of-mass frame, where $\vec p_{\text{sys}} = 0$, the angular momentum is the same about any point (notes, p. 19-17).

## Circular motion

A particle moves in a circle of radius $r$ about the $z$-axis with $\omega_z > 0$ (32.4 transcript, p. 1). With a central point, cylindrical coordinates are natural: $\vec r_S = r\,\hat r$ from the centre, and $\vec p = mr\omega_z\,\hat\theta$ tangent to the circle (pp. 1–2). Since $\hat r \times \hat\theta = \hat k$ (p. 2; notes, Example 19.2, p. 19-6):

<Formula label="Angular momentum in circular motion" tex="\vec L_S = r\,\hat r \times mr\omega_z\,\hat\theta = mr^2\omega_z\,\hat k = I_S\,\vec\omega" cite="reading5" at="p. 2" />

where $mr^2$ is the moment of inertia of a point particle about the axis. The angular momentum about the centre points along the axis because $\vec r_S$ and $\vec p$ both lie in the plane of motion (p. 2). (Taken about a point on the axis but outside the plane of the circle, it would also have a part that is not along the axis: notes, Example 19.3, pp. 19-6–19-8.)

<WorkedExample title="A ball, two skaters and a sling">
<Problem>
(a) A 0.50 kg ball moves at 4.0 m/s in the $+x$ direction along the line $y = 3.0$ m. Find its angular momentum about the origin. (b) Two 1.0 kg skaters glide at 2.0 m/s in opposite directions on parallel lines 1.5 m apart. Find their total angular momentum. (c) A 0.20 kg ball on a 0.80 m string goes round at 3.0 rad/s. Find its angular momentum about the centre.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Straight-line motion">
$\vec p = 2.0\,\hat i$ kg m/s and $\vec r = x\,\hat i + 3.0\,\hat j$ m. The $\hat i \times \hat i$ term vanishes, so $\vec L = 3.0 \times 2.0\,(\hat j \times \hat i) = -6.0\,\hat k$ kg m²/s, whatever $x$ is: constant, with moment arm 3.0 m. It is negative because, seen from the origin, the ball goes round clockwise.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Total momentum zero">
The total momentum is zero, so any point will do; take one on one skater's line. That skater contributes nothing, and the other contributes $1.5 \times 1.0 \times 2.0 = 3.0$ kg m²/s. The total is 3.0 kg m²/s about every point.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Circular motion">
$L = mr^2\omega = 0.20 \times 0.80^2 \times 3.0 \approx 0.38$ kg m²/s, along the axis.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Spinning discs">
Every star in a disc galaxy carries angular momentum about the galaxy's centre, $m r v$ for a circular orbit. When gas falls into a dark matter halo it keeps most of its angular momentum, so it cannot fall straight to the centre: it settles into a rotating disc whose size is set by how much angular momentum it brought in. Simulations of galaxy formation are tested on exactly this: early ones, without the feedback from stars and black holes that later models added, lost too much angular momentum and made discs that were too small.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\vec L_S = \vec r_S \times \vec p$, defined about a chosen point S; perpendicular to $\vec r_S$ and $\vec p$, by the right-hand rule.
- $|\vec L_S| = r_\perp p = r_S p_\perp$; a particle moving in a straight line at constant velocity has constant angular momentum about any point.
- $\vec L_A = \vec L_B + \vec r_{A,B} \times \vec p_{\text{sys}}$: if the total momentum is zero, the angular momentum is the same about every point.
- Circular motion about the centre: $\vec L = mr^2\omega_z\,\hat k = I\vec\omega$.
