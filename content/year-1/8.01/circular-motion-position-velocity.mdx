Many motions happen around a central point: a stone whirled on a string, a car on a roundabout, the Moon around the Earth, the Sun around the centre of our Galaxy. Week 3 of MIT's 8.01 is about **circular motion**, and Lesson 8 starts where every kinematics problem starts: with a coordinate system, then the position and the velocity.

## Why circular motion needs a force

The course notes open the chapter with the big picture. In straight-line motion, velocity and acceleration lie along the line. On a circle, the velocity is always along the **tangent**, so its direction keeps changing, and a changing velocity means an acceleration. That acceleration points towards the centre (the **centripetal** acceleration; Lesson 10 derives it), so by Newton's second law something must pull the object inwards. Newton realised in 1666 that the Moon is "falling" towards the Earth all the time: without a pull towards the centre, the first law says it would fly off in a straight line. For the radial direction the second law reads

<Formula label="Second law, radial component" tex="F_r = m\,a_r" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.1.1), p. 2" />

Before forces, though, we need a good description of the motion itself.

## Polar coordinates

For motion around a point $P$, the natural coordinates are **polar coordinates**. Choose a reference line through $P$ (say, horizontal) and a direction of increasing angle. Any point in the plane is then given by two numbers: its distance $r$ from $P$ ($r \ge 0$) and the angle $\theta$ from the reference line (running from $0$ to $2\pi$ once round).

Every coordinate system needs **unit vectors** at each point, and the rule is always the same: a unit vector points the way its coordinate **increases**.

- $\hat r$ points radially **outwards**, the way $r$ increases.
- $\hat\theta$ points along the circle through the point, the way $\theta$ increases. It is perpendicular to $\hat r$.

Here is the catch. Take two different points $S_1$ and $S_2$ on a circle. At each, $\hat r$ and $\hat\theta$ are built by the same rule, but they point in **different directions**: on opposite sides of the circle, $\hat r_1$ and $\hat r_2$ even point opposite ways. In Cartesian coordinates $\hat i$ and $\hat j$ are the same everywhere; in polar coordinates the unit vectors **depend on where you are**. That makes the analysis a little more involved, as the next section shows.

## Polar unit vectors in Cartesian form

To compare the two systems, put the Cartesian axes at the same point, with $\hat i$ along the reference line and $\hat j$ at right angles. Decomposing each polar unit vector (both have length 1) into horizontal and vertical parts:

<Formula label="Polar unit vectors in Cartesian form" tex="\hat r = \cos\theta\,\hat i + \sin\theta\,\hat j, \qquad \hat\theta = -\sin\theta\,\hat i + \cos\theta\,\hat j" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.2.2)–(6.2.3), p. 2" />

For $\hat r$, the horizontal part is adjacent to the angle (cosine) and the vertical part is opposite (sine). $\hat\theta$ is $\hat r$ turned by a right angle anticlockwise: its horizontal part, opposite the angle, points in the $-\hat i$ direction.

For a moving object $\theta$ is a function of time, $\theta(t)$, so these unit vectors **change with time**: $\hat r(t) = \cos\theta(t)\,\hat i + \sin\theta(t)\,\hat j$. The Cartesian ones never do.

## The position vector

The position vector runs from the origin (the centre $P$) to the object. On a circle of radius $r$ it has constant length $r$ and points along $\hat r(t)$:

<Formula label="Position on a circle" tex="\vec r(t) = r\,\hat r(t) = r\cos\theta(t)\,\hat i + r\sin\theta(t)\,\hat j" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.2.1)–(6.2.2), p. 2" />

## The velocity vector

The velocity is the time derivative of the position. The radius $r$ is constant, and $\cos\theta(t)$ and $\sin\theta(t)$ need the **chain rule**, because their argument depends on time:

<Formula label="Chain rule for the angle" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\cos\theta(t) = -\sin\theta(t)\,\frac{d\theta}{dt}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\sin\theta(t) = \cos\theta(t)\,\frac{d\theta}{dt}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.2.5)–(6.2.6), p. 3" />

So

<Formula label="Velocity on a circle" tex="\vec v(t) = \frac{d\vec r}{dt} = r\,\frac{d\theta}{dt}\bigl(-\sin\theta\,\hat i + \cos\theta\,\hat j\bigr) = r\,\frac{d\theta}{dt}\,\hat\theta(t)" cite="reading3" at="transcript 8.2, p. 3" />

The bracket is exactly $\hat\theta$. The velocity points along $\hat\theta$: **tangent to the circle**, at right angles to the position vector. Its $\theta$-component is

<Formula label="Tangential component of velocity" tex="v_\theta = r\,\frac{d\theta}{dt}, \qquad \vec v = v_\theta\,\hat\theta" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.2.8)–(6.2.9), p. 3" />

A component can have either sign. If $d\theta/dt > 0$ the angle increases and the object moves in the $+\hat\theta$ direction (anticlockwise, with the usual choice); if $d\theta/dt < 0$ it moves the other way; if $d\theta/dt = 0$ it is at rest.

The same calculation gives the time derivatives of the unit vectors themselves, which Lesson 10 needs for the acceleration:

<Formula label="Derivatives of the polar unit vectors" tex="\frac{d\hat r}{dt} = \frac{d\theta}{dt}\,\hat\theta, \qquad \frac{d\hat\theta}{dt} = -\frac{d\theta}{dt}\,\hat r" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.2.4) and (6.2.7), p. 3" />

<WorkedExample title="A car on a roundabout">
<Problem>
A car drives anticlockwise round a circular roundabout of radius 50 m, its angle increasing at $d\theta/dt = 0.40$ rad/s. Find its speed, and its velocity in Cartesian components at the moment when $\theta = 30°$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Tangential component">
$v_\theta = r\,d\theta/dt = 50 \times 0.40 = 20$ m/s, positive, so the car moves the way θ increases.
</Step>
<Step title="The unit vector θ̂ at 30°">
$\hat\theta = -\sin 30°\,\hat i + \cos 30°\,\hat j = -0.500\,\hat i + 0.866\,\hat j$.
</Step>
<Step title="Velocity">
$\vec v = 20\,\hat\theta = (-10.0\,\hat i + 17.3\,\hat j)$ m/s. Check: its length is $\sqrt{10.0^2 + 17.3^2} \approx 20$ m/s, and it is perpendicular to $\hat r = 0.866\,\hat i + 0.500\,\hat j$, since $(-10.0)(0.866) + (17.3)(0.500) \approx 0$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Angular velocity

Whether a motion counts as "anticlockwise" depends on where you look from: an observer above a tilted plane sees a circle traversed anticlockwise, while one below sees the same motion clockwise. So the direction of rotation is given by a vector, using the **right-hand rule**: curl the fingers of your right hand the way the object goes round, and your thumb points along a unit vector $\hat n$ perpendicular to the plane. That one vector fixes both the plane of the motion and the positive sense of turning.

For motion in the plane of the page, with $\hat r$ and $\hat\theta$ in the page, the third direction is $\hat k$, pointing **out of** the page (drawn ⊙; into the page is ⊗). The **angular velocity** is

<Formula label="Angular velocity" tex="\vec\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}\,\hat k = \omega_z\,\hat k" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.5.4), p. 6-11" />

If $d\theta/dt > 0$ (anticlockwise as seen from above the page), $\vec\omega$ points out of the page; if $d\theta/dt < 0$, into it, exactly what the right-hand rule gives. Its component $\omega_z = d\theta/dt$ carries the sign; the **angular speed** is its size,

<Formula label="Angular speed" tex="\omega = |\omega_z| = \left|\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right|" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.2.10), p. 3, and (6.5.5), p. 6-11" />

measured in rad/s. The velocity can now be written with the angular velocity:

<Formula label="Velocity and angular velocity" tex="\vec v = r\,\omega_z\,\hat\theta" cite="reading4" at="transcript 8.3, p. 3" />

So a circling object has two velocities that describe it: an ordinary velocity along the circle, and an angular velocity perpendicular to the plane of the circle. For the car on the roundabout above, seen from above, $\omega_z = +0.40$ rad/s and $\vec\omega = 0.40\,\hat k$ rad/s, pointing upwards (out of the page in a map view); driving the other way round would give $-0.40\,\hat k$ rad/s, pointing down.

## A geometric view of the same result

The notes also reach the speed without unit vectors. In a short time $\Delta t$ the object turns through a small angle $\Delta\theta$, and its displacement is the chord joining the two positions:

<Formula label="Length of the chord" tex="|\Delta\vec r| = 2r\sin(\Delta\theta/2)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.2.11), p. 4" />

For a small angle (in radians), $\sin(\Delta\theta/2) \approx \Delta\theta/2$ (the **small-angle approximation**, from the series $\sin x = x - x^3/3! + x^5/5! - \cdots$), so the chord is almost the arc, $|\Delta\vec r| \approx r\,|\Delta\theta|$. Dividing by $\Delta t$ and letting $\Delta t \to 0$:

<Formula label="Speed on a circle" tex="v = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{|\Delta\vec r|}{\Delta t} = r \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{|\Delta\theta|}{\Delta t} = r\left|\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right| = r\,\omega" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.2.15), p. 5" />

And as $\Delta t \to 0$ the chord turns into the tangent line, so the velocity is tangent to the circle and perpendicular to $\vec r$, the same answer as before.

<WorkedExample title="How good is the small-angle approximation?">
<Problem>
Compare the chord and the arc for a turn of $\Delta\theta = 10°$.
</Problem>
<Step title="In radians">
$10° = 0.1745$ rad, so the arc is $0.1745\,r$.
</Step>
<Step title="The chord">
$2r\sin 5° = 2 \times 0.08716\,r = 0.1743\,r$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
The chord is shorter by only about 0.13%. For smaller angles the agreement is better still, which is why the limit $\Delta t \to 0$ gives exactly $r\,|d\theta/dt|$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology">
The notes mention one of the largest circular motions we know: the Sun's orbit around the centre of the Milky Way. (The notes give its distance from the central black hole as 50,000 light years, about twice the true value: the GRAVITY collaboration measured 8178 parsecs in 2019, about 26,700 light years, in line with the roughly 8 kiloparsecs accepted for decades.) Measuring how fast stars and gas circle a galaxy at different radii (the galaxy's **rotation curve**, built from exactly the $v_\theta$ of this lesson) gave some of the first strong evidence for **dark matter**: far from the centre the speeds stay high, as if much more mass were there than we can see.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Motion around a central point is described in polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$. The unit vectors point the way each coordinate increases: $\hat r$ outwards, $\hat\theta$ along the circle.
- Unlike $\hat i$ and $\hat j$, the polar unit vectors depend on position: $\hat r = \cos\theta\,\hat i + \sin\theta\,\hat j$, $\hat\theta = -\sin\theta\,\hat i + \cos\theta\,\hat j$. For a moving object they change with time.
- On a circle of radius $r$: $\vec r = r\,\hat r(t)$, and with the chain rule $\vec v = r\,(d\theta/dt)\,\hat\theta$: tangent to the circle, with component $v_\theta = r\,d\theta/dt$, positive when θ increases.
- Angular velocity: $\vec\omega = (d\theta/dt)\,\hat k$, perpendicular to the plane by the right-hand rule (out of the page for anticlockwise motion); angular speed $\omega = |d\theta/dt|$ in rad/s; $\vec v = r\,\omega_z\,\hat\theta$.
- Geometrically, the chord $2r\sin(\Delta\theta/2)$ approaches the arc $r\,\Delta\theta$ for small angles, giving speed $v = r\,|d\theta/dt| = r\omega$.
