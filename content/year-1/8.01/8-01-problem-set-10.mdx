This set practises the four lessons of MIT's week on rotational motion: [rigid bodies and rotational kinematics](/courses/8-01/rigid-body-kinematics), the [moment of inertia](/courses/8-01/moment-of-inertia), [torque and the cross product](/courses/8-01/torque-and-cross-product), and [torque and angular acceleration](/courses/8-01/torque-and-angular-acceleration). The six problems follow MIT's Problem Set 10, in our own words. MIT's set has a drawing for every problem. Here each drawing is described in words, so make your own sketch first.

Problems 3, 4 and 5 are about bodies that do not speed up: a rod and a person at rest, and a cylinder that turns at a steady rate. Problems 3 and 4 need the two conditions of static equilibrium, which MIT's course notes treat in Chapter 18 and which have no lesson of their own on this site. The conditions are stated in Problem 3, where they are first used.

MIT publishes no solutions for this set, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. One remark: MIT's course notes work Problem 3 as Example 18.2, with the same numbers, and Problem 4 as Example 18.3. Try the problems before you look there. All six problems count for the score (46 points).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first: sketch the situation, draw every force at the point where it acts, and choose the point or the axis about which you take torques. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A number box accepts any value within 1% of ours, so keep at least three significant figures, and type it in the unit shown next to the box. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ where a number is needed. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `3*M*R^2/2`, `sqrt(2*d/a)` or `m*g*sin(beta)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type Greek letters by name (`alpha`, `beta`, `mu`), $M_1$ as `M1`, $M_b$ as `Mb` and $m_p$ as `mp`.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Moment of inertia: a disc and a washer" points={8} mit="Problem 1">

<Part label="a" points={4}>

A thin uniform disc has the mass $M$ and the radius $R$. Find its moment of inertia about two axes, both at right angles to the plane of the disc: (i) the axis through the centre of mass of the disc, and (ii) a parallel axis through a point on the rim, at the distance $R$ from the centre. Use $M$ and $R$. (MIT's drawing for this part is a circle with its radius $R$ marked.)

<Hint>

For (i): cut the disc into thin rings around the centre. Every bit of one ring is at the same distance from the axis. What is the mass of a ring of radius $r$ and width $dr$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For (ii) no new integral is needed. The two axes are parallel, and one of them passes through the centre of mass. The lesson on the moment of inertia has a theorem for exactly this case.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a1" label="(i) $I_{\text{cm}} =$" expr="M*R^2/2"
  vars={{ M: [1, 5], R: [0.2, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "M*R^2", hint: "That is the moment of inertia of a thin ring, with all of its mass at the distance R. Most of a disc's mass is closer to the axis." },
    { expr: "3*M*R^2/2", hint: "That is the value for the axis through the rim, part (ii)." },
    { expr: "M*R^2/4", hint: "Too small by a factor of 2. The integral of r³ from 0 to R is R⁴/4, and the factor in front of it is 2M/R²." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-a2" label="(ii) $I_{\text{rim}} =$" expr="3*M*R^2/2"
  vars={{ M: [1, 5], R: [0.2, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "M*R^2/2", hint: "That is the value for the axis through the centre. An axis through the rim has more of the mass far from it." },
    { expr: "M*R^2", hint: "That is only the term M d² of the parallel axis theorem, with d = R. Add the moment of inertia about the centre of mass." },
    { expr: "5*M*R^2/2", hint: "This is too large. The distance between the two axes is R, so the theorem adds M R² to the value of (i)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The moment of inertia is the sum of mass times the square of the distance from the axis. For the axis through the centre, rings do the sum. For the axis through the rim, the parallel axis theorem turns the first result into the second.

<Step title="(i) The axis through the centre">

The disc has the mass per unit area $M/(\pi R^2)$. A ring of radius $r$ and width $dr$ has the area $2\pi r\,dr$, so its mass is $dm = (2M/R^2)\,r\,dr$. All of it is at the distance $r$ from the axis. This is the calculation of the lesson on the [moment of inertia](/courses/8-01/moment-of-inertia), and of the notes:

<Formula label="Uniform disc about the axis through its centre" tex="I_{\text{cm}} = \int r^2\,dm = \frac{2M}{R^2}\int_0^R r^3\,dr = \frac{2M}{R^2}\,\frac{R^4}{4} = \frac12\,M R^2" cite="reading1" at="Example 16.2, Eqs. (16.2.11) and (16.2.15), pp. 16-9 and 16-10" />

</Step>

<Step title="(ii) The axis through the rim">

The parallel axis theorem links the moment of inertia about an axis through the centre of mass to the one about a parallel axis at the distance $d$ from it:

<Formula label="Parallel axis theorem" tex="I_S = I_{\text{cm}} + m\,d^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (16.2.16), p. 16-10" />

Here the mass is $M$ and the distance between the axes is $d = R$:

<Formula label="Uniform disc about an axis through its rim" tex="I_{\text{rim}} = \tfrac12\,M R^2 + M R^2 = \tfrac32\,M R^2" cite="reading1" at="Example 16.3, Eqs. (16.2.19) and (16.2.20), p. 16-11" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both results are a mass times a length squared, $\text{kg}\,\text{m}^2$. The first is smaller than $M R^2$, the value for a ring with all of its mass at the distance $R$: in a disc most of the mass is closer to the axis. The second is three times the first, and larger, as the theorem says it must be: of all parallel axes, the one through the centre of mass gives the smallest moment of inertia, because $m\,d^2$ is never negative.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

A steel washer (a flat ring) has the inner radius $r_i = 13.5$ mm, the outer radius $r_o = 31.0$ mm and the thickness $d = 4.0$ mm. MIT calls it a 1 inch US standard washer and gives the thickness as approximate. The density of the steel is $\rho = 7.8 \times 10^3\ \text{kg/m}^3$. Calculate the moment of inertia of the washer about the axis through its centre of mass, at right angles to its plane. Give it in units of $10^{-5}\ \text{kg}\,\text{m}^2$: for $2.5 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{kg}\,\text{m}^2$ you would type 2.5. (MIT's drawing shows the washer face on: two circles around one centre, with the inner and the outer radius marked.)

<Hint>

The washer is a disc with a hole. The ring method of part (a) still works: only the limits of the integral change.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The mass of a ring of radius $r$ and width $dr$ is the density times its volume, and the volume is $2\pi r\,dr$ times the thickness. Change the millimetres into metres before you put in numbers.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$I_{\text{cm}}$, in units of $10^{-5}\ \text{kg}\,\text{m}^2$:" value="4.36" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-washer-inertia" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Cut the washer into thin rings, as for the disc. The only changes are that the mass now comes from the density, and that the rings start at the inner radius.

<Step title="The integral">

A ring of radius $r$ and width $dr$ has the volume $2\pi r\,dr\,d$ and the mass $dm = \rho\,d\,2\pi r\,dr$. It is at the distance $r$ from the axis:

<Formula label="Washer about the axis through its centre" tex="I_{\text{cm}} = \int_{r_i}^{r_o} r^2\,\rho\,d\,2\pi r\,dr = \tfrac12\,\pi\,\rho\,d\,\bigl(r_o^4 - r_i^4\bigr)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Numbers">

In metres: $r_i = 0.0135$ m, $r_o = 0.0310$ m and $d = 0.0040$ m. Then $r_o^4 = 9.235 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4$ and $r_i^4 = 0.332 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4$, with the difference $8.903 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4$. The factor in front is $\tfrac12 \pi \times 7.8 \times 10^3 \times 0.0040 = 49.0\ \text{kg/m}^2$. So

<Formula label="Washer: the number" tex="I_{\text{cm}} = 49.0 \times 8.903 \times 10^{-7} = 4.36 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{kg}\,\text{m}^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (MIT's numbers, our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\text{kg/m}^3$ times a length times $\text{m}^4$ is $\text{kg}\,\text{m}^2$.

A second route: the washer is a full disc of radius $r_o$ with a disc of radius $r_i$ taken out, and moments of inertia about the same axis add. The full disc would have the mass $\rho\,\pi r_o^2 d = 0.0942$ kg and, by part (a), the moment of inertia $\tfrac12 \times 0.0942 \times 0.0310^2 = 4.526 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{kg}\,\text{m}^2$. The missing disc has the mass $0.0179$ kg and $\tfrac12 \times 0.0179 \times 0.0135^2 = 0.163 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{kg}\,\text{m}^2$. The difference is $4.36 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{kg}\,\text{m}^2$, the same. The hole takes away 19% of the mass of the full disc but less than 4% of its moment of inertia, because the missing mass was close to the axis.

The mass of the washer is $0.0942 - 0.0179 = 0.0763$ kg, about 76 grams. Because $r_o^4 - r_i^4 = (r_o^2 - r_i^2)(r_o^2 + r_i^2)$, the result can also be written $I_{\text{cm}} = \tfrac12\,m\,(r_o^2 + r_i^2)$, with $m$ the mass of the washer. For $r_i = 0$ this is the disc of part (a).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A compound pulley" points={8} mit="Problem 2">

A wheel is made of two uniform discs that are fixed to each other, with their centres on one axis: a large disc of radius $R$ and mass $M_1$, and a small disc of radius $R/2$ and mass $M_2$. The wheel can turn freely about a frictionless pivot at its centre, and the pivot does not move. A rope is wound around the small disc. It leaves the small disc, passes over a small frictionless post at the side of the wheel, and from there hangs straight down. A block of mass $M_b$ hangs on its end. As the block falls, the rope unwinds and the two discs turn together. Gravity pulls downward with the acceleration $g$. The block is released from rest.

MIT draws the wheel twice: face on (two circles of radius $R$ and $R/2$, with the rope running level from the small disc to the post and then down to the block), and at a slant, to show that the small disc sits on the face of the large one. MIT asks only for the time of part (c). Parts (a) and (b) are our own steps towards it. Take the rope to be massless, and to unwind without slipping or stretching (MIT's set does not say this. The assumption is ours).

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the moment of inertia of the wheel about its axis? Use any of $R$, $M_1$ and $M_2$ that you need.

<Hint>

Both discs turn about the same axis, and each is a uniform disc about its own centre. Moments of inertia about one axis add.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$I =$" expr="M1*R^2/2 + M2*R^2/8"
  vars={{ M1: [1, 5], M2: [1, 5], R: [0.2, 1] }}
  names={{ M1: "M_1", M2: "M_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(M1 + M2)*R^2/2", hint: "This gives both discs the radius R. The small disc has the radius R/2." },
    { expr: "M1*R^2/2 + M2*R^2/4", hint: "For the small disc the radius R/2 has to be squared: (R/2)² is R²/4, and half of that is R²/8." },
    { expr: "M1*R^2 + M2*R^2/4", hint: "These are the values for two thin rings. A uniform disc has half the moment of inertia of a ring of the same mass and radius." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The moment of inertia is a sum over all the mass of the body, so for two bodies on one axis it is the sum of their two moments of inertia.

<Step title="The sum">

Each disc is a uniform disc about the axis through its centre, with $I = \tfrac12 M R^2$ from Problem 1(a):

<Formula label="Moment of inertia of the wheel" tex="I = \tfrac12\,M_1 R^2 + \tfrac12\,M_2 \left(\frac{R}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac12\,M_1 R^2 + \tfrac18\,M_2 R^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both terms are a mass times a length squared. With equal masses the small disc adds only a quarter of what the large one does: half the radius, a quarter of the moment of inertia.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

What is the acceleration of the block as it falls? Use any of $R$, $M_1$, $M_2$, $M_b$ and $g$ that you need.

<Hint>

Treat the block and the wheel one at a time. The block: Newton's second law, with the weight and the tension of the rope. The wheel: the rotational equation of motion about the axis. Which forces on the wheel have a torque about its axis?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The post is frictionless, so the tension is the same on both sides of it. The rope pulls on the wheel at the edge of the small disc: what is the moment arm of that pull?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The rope does not slip, so the speed of the block equals the speed of the edge of the disc that the rope leaves. That links the acceleration of the block to the angular acceleration of the wheel.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$a =$" expr="Mb*g/(Mb + 2*M1 + M2/2)"
  vars={{ M1: [1, 5], M2: [1, 5], Mb: [0.5, 3], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ M1: "M_1", M2: "M_2", Mb: "M_b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Mb*g/(Mb + M1/2 + M2/8)", hint: "This has the rope leaving the wheel at the rim of the large disc, at the distance R. It is wound around the small disc, at R/2." },
    { expr: "Mb*g/(Mb + M1 + M2/4)", hint: "The radius R/2 comes in twice: as the moment arm of the tension, and in the link between the acceleration of the block and the angular acceleration. You used it once." },
    { expr: "g", hint: "That is free fall. The rope holds the block back, because the block has to set the wheel turning." },
    { expr: "Mb*g/(2*M1 + M2/2)", hint: "The weight of the block also has to speed up the block itself. Its mass belongs in the denominator too." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Three equations: Newton's second law for the block, the rotational equation of motion for the wheel, and the condition that the rope does not slip. The lesson on [torque and angular acceleration](/courses/8-01/torque-and-angular-acceleration) treats a wheel with a mass falling on a string wound around it with the same three equations.

<Step title="The block">

Take down as positive. The weight $M_b g$ pulls the block down and the rope pulls it up with the tension $T$:

<Formula label="Compound pulley: the block" tex="M_b\,g - T = M_b\,a" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The wheel">

For a rigid body on a fixed axis the torque about the axis equals the moment of inertia times the angular acceleration:

<Formula label="Rotational equation of motion" tex="\bigl(\tau^{\text{ext}}_S\bigr)_z = I_S\,\alpha_z" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (17.3.23), p. 17-19" />

Three forces act on the wheel: its weight, the force of the pivot, and the pull of the rope. The first two act at the axis and have no torque about it. The post is frictionless, so the rope has the same tension $T$ on both sides of it. The rope leaves the small disc along a tangent, so the moment arm of its pull is the radius $R/2$. With $\alpha$ for the size of the angular acceleration:

<Formula label="Compound pulley: the wheel" tex="T\,\frac{R}{2} = I\,\alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The rope does not slip">

The rope moves with the edge of the small disc, and the block moves with the rope. The notes write this condition for a string on a pulley of radius $R$ as $a = R\,\alpha_z$ (Eq. (17.3.40), p. 17-22). Here the radius is $R/2$:

<Formula label="Compound pulley: no slipping" tex="a = \frac{R}{2}\,\alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

The last two equations give the tension: $T = I\alpha/(R/2) = I a/(R/2)^2 = 4 I a/R^2$. With $I$ from part (a), $T = \bigl(2 M_1 + \tfrac12 M_2\bigr)\,a$. Put this into the equation of the block:

<Formula label="Compound pulley: acceleration of the block" tex="a = \frac{M_b\,g}{M_b + 2 M_1 + \tfrac12 M_2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The fraction is a mass over a mass, so $a$ is in the units of $g$, and it is smaller than $g$. The radius $R$ has dropped out: a larger wheel is harder to turn, but the rope also pulls on a longer arm. With a wheel of no mass the block is in free fall, $a = g$. For a very heavy wheel $a$ goes to zero.

The wheel acts as an extra mass $I/(R/2)^2 = 2M_1 + \tfrac12 M_2$ that the block has to pull along. The large disc counts for twice its own mass, because the rope pulls at half its radius: much of its mass moves faster than the rope.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

How long does the block take to fall the distance $d$? Use any of $R$, $M_1$, $M_2$, $M_b$, $g$ and $d$ that you need.

<Hint>

The acceleration of part (b) does not change during the fall, and the block starts from rest.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$t =$" expr="sqrt(d*(2*Mb + 4*M1 + M2)/(Mb*g))"
  vars={{ M1: [1, 5], M2: [1, 5], Mb: [0.5, 3], g: [9, 10], d: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ M1: "M_1", M2: "M_2", Mb: "M_b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(d*(2*Mb + 4*M1 + M2)/(2*Mb*g))", hint: "From rest, d = ½ a t², so t² is 2d/a. You have d/a." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2*d/g)", hint: "That is the time of a free fall. The wheel holds the block back: use the acceleration of part (b)." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2*d*(Mb + M1/2 + M2/8)/(Mb*g))", hint: "This has the rope leaving the wheel at the rim of the large disc, at the distance R. It is wound around the small disc, at R/2." },
    { expr: "d*(2*Mb + 4*M1 + M2)/(Mb*g)", hint: "Check the units: this is in s². Take the root." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Motion from rest with a constant acceleration.

<Step title="The time">

From rest, the distance grows as $d = \tfrac12 a t^2$. With $a$ of part (b):

<Formula label="Compound pulley: time to fall the distance d" tex="t = \sqrt{\frac{2d}{a}} = \sqrt{\frac{2d\,\bigl(M_b + 2M_1 + \tfrac12 M_2\bigr)}{M_b\,g}} = \sqrt{\frac{d\,(2M_b + 4M_1 + M_2)}{M_b\,g}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A length divided by an acceleration is in $\text{s}^2$, so the root is a time. With a wheel of no mass the result is the time of a free fall, $\sqrt{2d/g}$. A heavier wheel makes it longer.

An example with our own numbers: $M_1 = 4.0$ kg, $M_2 = 2.0$ kg, $M_b = 1.0$ kg and $d = 1.0$ m. The acceleration is $9.8/(1.0 + 8.0 + 1.0) = 0.98\ \text{m/s}^2$, a tenth of $g$, and $t = \sqrt{2 \times 1.0/0.98} = 1.43$ s. In free fall the block would take $0.45$ s.

The same example with energy, the method of the section "The same problem with energy" in the lesson on [torque and angular acceleration](/courses/8-01/torque-and-angular-acceleration). The block ends with $v^2 = 2 a d = 1.96\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$, so its kinetic energy is $0.5 \times 1.0 \times 1.96 = 0.98$ J. The wheel turns at $\omega = v/(R/2)$, so its kinetic energy $\tfrac12 I \omega^2$ is $\tfrac12 \bigl(2M_1 + \tfrac12 M_2\bigr) v^2 = 0.5 \times 9.0 \times 1.96 = 8.82$ J. Together that is $9.8$ J, which is the potential energy $M_b\,g\,d = 1.0 \times 9.8 \times 1.0$ J that the block has lost. Nine tenths of the energy went into the wheel.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A suspended rod" points={8} mit="Problem 3">

A uniform rod of length $L = 2.0$ m and mass $m = 4.0$ kg sticks out level from a wall. Its left end is hinged to the wall. Its right end is held up by a cable that runs back to a point of the wall above the hinge. The cable makes the angle $\beta = 30°$ with the rod, and its mass can be ignored. MIT's drawing shows this from the side: the wall on the left, the level rod, and the cable as the sloping side of the triangle.

The hinge (the pivot) pushes on the rod with a contact force whose size and direction are not known beforehand. Call its size $F$ and the angle it makes with the rod $\alpha$.

The rod is at rest and stays at rest. A rigid body in this state is in static equilibrium, and the notes give two conditions for it:

<Formula label="Static equilibrium: the two conditions" tex="\vec F_1 + \vec F_2 + \cdots = \vec 0, \qquad \vec\tau_{S,1} + \vec\tau_{S,2} + \cdots = \vec 0" cite="reading3" at="Eqs. (18.1.1) and (18.1.2), p. 18-2" />

The forces on the body add up to zero, and so do their torques about a point S. The notes state the second condition for any point S, so you are free to choose the point that makes the work easiest.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the size of the tension in the cable, in newtons?

<Hint>

Two of the three unknowns belong to the pivot force. Take torques about a point where the pivot force has no torque at all.

</Hint>

<Hint>

About the hinge, two forces have a torque: the weight and the tension. The weight of a uniform rod acts at its middle. For the tension, only the part at right angles to the rod turns the rod.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$T =$" value="39.2" unit="N" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-tension" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Take torques about the hinge. The unknown pivot force acts there, so it drops out, and the tension is the only unknown left.

<Step title="The forces">

Three forces act on the rod. The weight $m g$ points down and acts at the centre of mass, the middle of the rod (the lesson on [torque and the cross product](/courses/8-01/torque-and-cross-product) shows that for torques the whole weight acts there). The tension $T$ acts at the right end and points along the cable, back towards the wall and upward, at the angle $\beta$ to the rod. The pivot force acts at the left end.

</Step>

<Step title="Torques about the hinge">

Count a torque as positive when it would turn the rod counterclockwise. The weight has the moment arm $L/2$ and would turn the rod clockwise. Of the tension, the part $T\sin\beta$ is at right angles to the rod, at the distance $L$, and would turn it counterclockwise:

<Formula label="Rod: torques about the hinge" tex="T L\sin\beta - m g\,\frac{L}{2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = \frac{m g}{2\sin\beta}" cite="reading3" at="Example 18.2, Eq. (18.4.2), p. 18-8" />

</Step>

<Step title="Numbers">

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A cylinder spinning in a V-shaped groove" points={8} mit="Problem 5">

Each wall of the groove makes the angle $45°$ with the horizontal, so the two walls are at right angles to each other, and the cylinder touches both. Between the cylinder and each wall the coefficient of friction is $\mu$. Gravity pulls downward with the acceleration $g$.

MIT's drawing shows the groove from the end, with the round cylinder resting in the V and a curved arrow for the rotation, which is counterclockwise. MIT's title speaks of a rolling cylinder, but the cylinder stays where it is and its surface slides over both walls. So $\mu$ is a coefficient of kinetic friction. <Hint>

<Step title="The directions">

</Step>

<Step title="The force condition">

The surfaces slide, so the friction is kinetic, the coefficient times the normal force (the lesson on [contact forces](/courses/8-01/contact-forces)): $f_1 = \mu N_1$ and $f_2 = \mu N_2$. Then

<Formula label="Groove: two equations for the normal forces" tex="N_1 - \mu N_2 = \frac{m g}{\sqrt 2}, \qquad N_2 + \mu N_1 = \frac{m g}{\sqrt 2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 5 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Groove: torque of the friction forces" tex="\tau_f = R\,(f_1 + f_2) = \mu R\,(N_1 + N_2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 5 (our own solution)" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same three equations as in Problem 2: Newton's second law for the block, the rotational equation of motion for the pulley, and the condition that the string does not slip.

<Step title="The block">

Take the direction down the plane as positive. The plane is frictionless, so along it only the part $m g\sin\beta$ of the weight and the tension $T$ of the string act:

<Formula label="Incline: the block" tex="m g\sin\beta - T = m\,a" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The pulley">

The weight of the pulley and the force of the pivot act at the axis. Only the string has a torque about it, with the moment arm $R$. With $\alpha$ for the size of the angular acceleration, and the string not slipping:

<Formula label="Incline: the pulley" tex="T R = \tfrac12\,m_p R^2\,\alpha, \qquad a = R\,\alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 6 (our own solution; the second equation is Eq. (17.3.40) of the notes, p. 17-22)" />

Together they give $T = \tfrac12 m_p\,a$.

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

<Formula label="Incline: acceleration of the block" tex="m g\sin\beta = \bigl(m + \tfrac12 m_p\bigr)\,a \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = \frac{m g\sin\beta}{m + \tfrac12 m_p}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A mass over a mass times $g$ is an acceleration. The radius has dropped out. With a pulley of no mass the block slides freely, $a = g\sin\beta$. On a level plane it does not move. For $\beta = 90°$ the block hangs straight down, and the result is that of a mass falling on a string wound around a wheel, $a = m g/(m + I/R^2)$ with $I/R^2 = \tfrac12 m_p$.

</Step>

</Solution>

How long does the block take to move the distance $d$ down the plane? Use any of $R$, $m$, $g$, $d$, $m_p$ and $\beta$ that you need.

<Hint>

The acceleration of part (a) is constant, and the block starts from rest.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-b" label="$t =$" expr="sqrt(d*(2*m + mp)/(m*g*sin(beta)))"
  vars={{ m: [0.5, 3], mp: [1, 5], g: [9, 10], beta: [0.2, 1.2], d: [0.5, 3] }}

<Solution>

**The idea.** Motion from rest with a constant acceleration, as in Problem 2(c).

<Step title="The time">

<Formula label="Incline: time to slide the distance d" tex="t = \sqrt{\frac{2d}{a}} = \sqrt{\frac{2d\,\bigl(m + \tfrac12 m_p\bigr)}{m g\sin\beta}} = \sqrt{\frac{d\,(2m + m_p)}{m g\sin\beta}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A length over an acceleration is in $\text{s}^2$. A heavier pulley or a flatter plane makes the time longer.

The notes solve a larger system with energy: the same pulley and block, with a second block of mass $m_1$ hanging from the other end of the cord (Example 16.4). Their result for the speed after the distance $d$, with $m_2$ the block on the plane, is

<Formula label="Notes: speed of the block from energy" tex="v = \sqrt{\frac{2 g d\,(-m_1 + m_2\sin\beta)}{m_1 + m_2 + \tfrac12 m_p}}" cite="reading1" at="Example 16.4, Eq. (16.3.15), p. 16-14" />

Without the hanging block ($m_1 = 0$, $m_2 = m$) this is $v^2 = 2 a d$ with the acceleration of part (a), the relation between speed and distance for a constant acceleration from rest. The two methods agree.

An example with our own numbers: $m = 2.0$ kg, $m_p = 4.0$ kg, $\beta = 30°$ and $d = 1.0$ m. Then $a = 2.0 \times 9.8 \times 0.5/(2.0 + 2.0) = 2.45\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $t = \sqrt{2 \times 1.0/2.45} = 0.90$ s. Sliding freely the block would have $a = 4.9\ \text{m/s}^2$ and take $0.64$ s.

</Step>
