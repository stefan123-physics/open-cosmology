The [previous lesson](/courses/8-01/work-and-kinetic-energy) defined work for motion along a line. When an object moves along a curved path in a plane or in space, only the part of the force **along the motion** does work. Measuring "how much of one vector lies along another" needs a new operation, the **scalar product** (21.1 transcript, p. 1).

## The scalar product

Take two vectors $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ at an angle $\theta$, with $0 \le \theta \le \pi$. The part of $\vec B$ along $\vec A$ is $B_\parallel = |\vec B|\cos\theta$; it is positive for $\theta < \pi/2$, negative for $\theta > \pi/2$, and zero when the vectors are perpendicular (21.1 transcript, pp. 1–2). The **scalar product** (or dot product) is $|\vec A|$ times that part, or equally $|\vec B|$ times the part of $\vec A$ along $\vec B$ (p. 2; notes, Section 13.8.1, pp. 13-21–13-22):

<Formula label="Scalar product" tex="\vec A\cdot\vec B = |\vec A|\,|\vec B|\cos\theta = |\vec A|\,B_\parallel = A_\parallel\,|\vec B|" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

Two rules make it easy to compute (pp. 2–3): a number $c$ comes out, $(c\vec A)\cdot\vec B = c(\vec A\cdot\vec B)$, and it distributes over addition, $(\vec A + \vec B)\cdot\vec C = \vec A\cdot\vec C + \vec B\cdot\vec C$. (The lecture leaves the second one, drawn with components along $\vec C$, as an exercise.)

## In Cartesian coordinates

Unit vectors along the same axis give $\hat i\cdot\hat i = \hat j\cdot\hat j = 1$ ($\cos 0 = 1$); perpendicular ones give $\hat i\cdot\hat j = 0$ (21.2 transcript, p. 1). Write $\vec A = A_x\hat i + A_y\hat j$ and $\vec B = B_x\hat i + B_y\hat j$, expand with the two rules into four terms, and only the matching ones survive (pp. 1–2, in two dimensions; the $z$ term is from the notes, Eqs. (13.8.6)–(13.8.7), p. 13-23):

<Formula label="Scalar product in components" tex="\vec A\cdot\vec B = A_xB_x + A_yB_y \;(+\,A_zB_z\text{ in three dimensions})" cite="reading3" at="p. 2" />

A vector dotted with itself gives its length squared, $\vec A\cdot\vec A = A_x^2 + A_y^2 = |\vec A|^2$, so $|\vec A| = \sqrt{\vec A\cdot\vec A}$, the positive root (pp. 2–3).

<WorkedExample title="The angle between two vectors">
<Problem>
Find the angle between $\vec A = 3\hat i + 4\hat j$ and $\vec B = 2\hat i - \hat j$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Both ways">
Components: $\vec A\cdot\vec B = 6 - 4 = 2$. Lengths: $|\vec A| = 5$, $|\vec B| = \sqrt5 \approx 2.236$.
</Step>
<Step title="Angle">
$\cos\theta = 2/(5\sqrt5) \approx 0.179$, so $\theta \approx 79.7°$: nearly perpendicular, so the scalar product is small.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Kinetic energy as a scalar product

Since $\vec v\cdot\vec v = v^2$, kinetic energy is a scalar product of the velocity with itself (21.3 transcript, p. 1; notes, Eq. (13.8.12), p. 13-24):

<Formula label="Kinetic energy" tex="K = \tfrac12\,m\,\vec v\cdot\vec v = \tfrac12\,m\left(v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2\right)" cite="reading4" at="p. 1" />

It depends on the reference frame, because the velocity does (p. 1).

## Work along a path

A particle moves along a curve while a force $\vec F$ acts on it. Cut the path into small displacements $\Delta\vec s_j$, each tangent to the path (21.4 transcript, pp. 1–2). On the $j$-th piece, only the component of $\vec F_j$ along $\Delta\vec s_j$ does work, $|\vec F_j|\cos\theta_j$ times $|\Delta\vec s_j|$: exactly the scalar product $\vec F_j\cdot\Delta\vec s_j$. Add the pieces and let them shrink: the sum becomes a **line integral** (p. 2; notes, Eq. (13.8.19), p. 13-28):

<Formula label="Work as a line integral" tex="W = \lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\vec F_j\cdot\Delta\vec s_j = \int_A^B \vec F\cdot d\vec s" cite="reading5" at="p. 2" />

In Cartesian coordinates $d\vec s = dx\,\hat i + dy\,\hat j + dz\,\hat k$, so $dW = F_x\,dx + F_y\,dy + F_z\,dz$ (notes, Eqs. (13.8.20)–(13.8.24), p. 13-29). For a **constant** force and a straight displacement it is simply $W = \vec F\cdot\Delta\vec x = |\vec F|\cos\theta\,\Delta x$ (Eq. (13.8.16), p. 13-25). The notes also work out the line integral for constant gravity, $-mg(y_f - y_0)$, for a spring, and for the Sun's inverse-square gravity (Examples 13.11–13.13, pp. 13-30–13-33).

<WorkedExample title="Pulling at an angle">
<Problem>
(a) You pull a sled 20 m across level snow with a 50 N force at 30° above the horizontal. How much work do you do? (b) A constant force $\vec F = 3\hat i + 2\hat j$ N acts while an object moves $\Delta\vec s = 4\hat i - \hat j$ m. How much work does it do?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a)">
$W = |\vec F|\,|\Delta\vec x|\cos 30° = 50 \times 20 \times 0.866 \approx 866$ J. The vertical part of the pull lightens the sled but does no work.
</Step>
<Step title="(b)">
$W = \vec F\cdot\Delta\vec s = 3 \times 4 + 2 \times (-1) = 10$ J.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The work–kinetic energy theorem in two and three dimensions

Start from Newton's second law, $\vec F = m\,d\vec v/dt$, and use $d\vec s = \vec v\,dt$ (21.5 transcript, pp. 1–2). The $dt$'s cancel and time drops out:

<Formula label="Work–kinetic energy theorem" tex="W = \int_A^B \vec F\cdot d\vec s = \int_A^B m\,\frac{d\vec v}{dt}\cdot\vec v\,dt = m\int_A^B \vec v\cdot d\vec v = \tfrac12 m v_B^2 - \tfrac12 m v_A^2 = \Delta K" cite="reading6" at="pp. 1–2" />

In components, $\vec v\cdot d\vec v = v_x\,dv_x + v_y\,dv_y$, and each part integrates exactly as in one dimension, to $\tfrac12(v_{x,f}^2 - v_{x,i}^2)$ and so on (pp. 1–2; notes, Eqs. (13.11.1)–(13.11.8), pp. 13-34–13-35). The theorem combines the definition of work with Newton's second law, in any number of dimensions. The notes show in the same way that the power is $P = \vec F\cdot\vec v$, and that it is the rate of change of kinetic energy (Eqs. (13.11.10)–(13.11.11), p. 13-35).

## A block down a ramp

A block slides down a ramp inclined at $\theta$, from $x_i$ to $x_f$, with $+\hat i$ down the slope (21.6 transcript, pp. 1–3). Gravity, the normal force and kinetic friction act. Perpendicular to the slope, $N = mg\cos\theta$, so friction is $\mu_k mg\cos\theta$, up the slope. Only the forces along the motion do work, both constant:

<Formula label="Block down a ramp" tex="mg\sin\theta\,(x_f - x_i) - \mu_k\,mg\cos\theta\,(x_f - x_i) = \tfrac12 m v_f^2 - \tfrac12 m v_i^2" cite="reading7" at="p. 2" />

Written with scalar products, friction is $-f_k\hat i\cdot dx\,\hat i$, and gravity, $mg\sin\theta\,\hat i - mg\cos\theta\,\hat j$, dotted with $dx\,\hat i$ leaves only $mg\sin\theta\,dx$: the normal force and the perpendicular part of gravity drop out because $\hat j\cdot\hat i = 0$ (pp. 2–3). Whichever of the initial and final quantities are unknown, the equation relates them to the others (p. 2). The notes' numbers: a 4.0 kg object sliding 3.0 m down a 30° plane with $\mu_k = 0.2$ gets $+58.8$ J from gravity, $-20.4$ J from friction and none from the normal force, $38.4$ J in all (Example 13.10, pp. 13-25–13-27; the friction line there prints the factor 3.0 m twice, but the result uses it once).

<WorkedExample title="Down a 25° ramp">
<Problem>
A 2.0 kg block starts from rest and slides 4.0 m down a 25° ramp with $\mu_k = 0.15$. How fast is it moving at the bottom?
</Problem>
<Step title="Work">
Gravity: $2.0 \times 9.8 \times 4.0 \times \sin 25° \approx 33.1$ J. Friction: $-0.15 \times 2.0 \times 9.8 \times \cos 25° \times 4.0 \approx -10.7$ J. Total $\approx 22.5$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed">
$\tfrac12 \times 2.0 \times v^2 = 22.5$ J gives $v \approx 4.7$ m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Only the line of sight counts">
For a nearby galaxy, the redshift tells us how fast it moves **towards or away from us**, not across the sky. (For distant galaxies it mostly measures the stretching of space, and at speeds close to that of light even sideways motion shifts the light a little.) If $\hat n$ is the unit vector from us to the galaxy, the Doppler shift measures $\vec v\cdot\hat n = |\vec v|\cos\theta$, for speeds well below that of light. A galaxy moving at 300 km/s at 60° to our line of sight shows only 150 km/s. For the Hubble expansion this is no problem, since that motion is radial, but the extra "peculiar" velocities of galaxies are seen only along the line of sight.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Scalar product: $\vec A\cdot\vec B = |\vec A||\vec B|\cos\theta = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z$; zero for perpendicular vectors; $|\vec A| = \sqrt{\vec A\cdot\vec A}$.
- Kinetic energy: $K = \tfrac12 m\,\vec v\cdot\vec v$.
- Work along a path: $W = \int_A^B\vec F\cdot d\vec s$; only the component of the force along the motion counts.
- Work–kinetic energy theorem in any dimension: $W = \Delta K$.
- On a ramp, only gravity's component along the slope and friction do work.
