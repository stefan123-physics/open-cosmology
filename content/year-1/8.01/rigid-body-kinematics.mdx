Until now, objects have been points, or systems of points whose centre of mass we followed. This week starts on bodies that turn: wheels, discs, pulleys. Segment 28.1 is a short video without a transcript, so this page follows the notes there (notes, Section 16.1, p. 16-1).

## Rigid bodies

Real objects stretch, compress or shear when forces act on them, and in fluids and gases the particles are only loosely bound, so the distances between them change. The ideal case is a **rigid body**: one in which the distance between any two points never changes (notes, Section 16.1, p. 16-1). Unlike a point mass, a rigid body can have forces applied at different points of it (p. 16-1); where a force acts will turn out to matter.

The simplest motion is **rotation about a fixed axis**, like a disc spinning in a CD player around an axis through its centre, perpendicular to the disc (notes, Section 16.2.1, p. 16-1).

## Translation and rotation

A rolling bicycle wheel does two things at once: its centre of mass moves with velocity $\vec v_{\text{cm}}$, and the wheel rotates about the centre of mass with angular velocity $\vec\omega$, whose direction is given by the right-hand rule (28.2 transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 16-2). Seen from the ground there is both translation and rotation.

Now ride along with an observer moving at $\vec v_{\text{cm}}$. In that **centre-of-mass frame** the centre of mass is at rest and only the rotation about it is left (p. 1). That splits the problem in two (pp. 1–2):

- the total external force accelerates the centre of mass, as for a system of particles;
- in the centre-of-mass frame there is only rotation, in which torque produces angular acceleration and there is a rotational kinetic energy (the coming lessons).

As long as the bicycle goes straight, the axis of each wheel keeps its direction. Turning the handlebars changes the axis, as do a spinning top, a gyroscope and the slow change in direction of the Earth's axis; such motion is much harder, and this chapter keeps to a fixed axis, with or without translation (notes, p. 16-2).

## Angular velocity and angular acceleration

Divide the body into small elements of mass $\Delta m_i$; each moves in a circle of radius $r_i$ about the axis (notes, Section 16.2.2, p. 16-2). Take the axis as the $z$-axis, measure the angle $\theta$ in the plane of rotation, and use a right-handed coordinate system so that $\hat k$ points along the axis (28.3 transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 16-3). Then (notes, Eqs. (16.1.1)–(16.1.2), p. 16-3):

<Formula label="Angular velocity and angular acceleration" tex="\vec\omega = \frac{d\theta}{dt}\,\hat k = \omega_z\,\hat k, \qquad \vec\alpha = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\,\hat k = \alpha_z\,\hat k" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (16.1.1)–(16.1.2), p. 16-3" />

Because the body is rigid, **every element has the same angular velocity and the same angular acceleration**: otherwise some parts would catch up with or pass others (p. 16-3).

The signs follow the right-hand rule (notes, Section 16.2.3, p. 16-4). With $\theta$ increasing counterclockwise seen from above, counterclockwise rotation has $\omega_z > 0$ and $\vec\omega$ along $+\hat k$; clockwise rotation has $\omega_z < 0$. More generally: if $\vec\omega$ and $\vec\alpha$ point the same way the body is speeding up; if they point opposite ways, it is slowing down, whichever direction you called positive (p. 16-4).

Each element moves in a circle, so circular motion gives its velocity and acceleration (notes, Section 16.2.4, Eqs. (16.1.3)–(16.1.5), pp. 16-4–16-5):

<Formula label="Tangential and radial components" tex="v_{\theta,i} = r_i\,\omega_z, \qquad a_{\theta,i} = r_i\,\alpha_z, \qquad a_{r,i} = -\frac{v_{\theta,i}^2}{r_i} = -r_i\,\omega_z^2" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (16.1.3)–(16.1.5), pp. 16-4–16-5" />

The angular quantities are shared; the speeds and accelerations grow in proportion to the distance from the axis.

## From angular acceleration to angle

Suppose $\alpha_z$ is given, possibly as a function of time. Choose a point P of the body and its angle $\theta(t)$, with $\alpha_z = d\omega_z/dt = d^2\theta/dt^2$ and $\omega_z = d\theta/dt$ (28.3 transcript, p. 1). Integrate once, exactly as for a point moving in a circle (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Angular velocity from angular acceleration" tex="\omega_z(t) = \omega_{z,0} + \int_0^t \alpha_z(t')\,dt'" cite="reading3" at="p. 1" />

and once more for the angle (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Angle from angular velocity" tex="\theta(t) = \theta_0 + \int_0^t \omega_z(t')\,dt'" cite="reading3" at="pp. 1–2" />

with the dummy variable $t'$ and the initial values $\omega_{z,0}$ and $\theta_0$ at $t = 0$. This works when $\alpha_z$ is known as a function of time (p. 1). For constant $\alpha_z$ it gives $\omega_z = \omega_{z,0} + \alpha_z t$ and $\theta = \theta_0 + \omega_{z,0}t + \frac{1}{2}\alpha_z t^2$, the same forms as for motion along a line.

<WorkedExample title="A drum spinning up and a record stopping">
<Problem>
(a) A washing-machine drum of radius 0.25 m starts from rest with an angular acceleration that grows in time, $\alpha_z = bt$ with $b = 1.5$ rad/s³. After 4.0 s, find its angular velocity, the angle turned, the speed of the rim and both components of the rim's acceleration. (b) A record turning at 45 rpm slows uniformly to a stop in 5.0 s. Find its angular acceleration and how many turns it makes while stopping.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Integrate twice">
$\omega_z = \int_0^t bt'\,dt' = \frac{1}{2}bt^2 = 0.75 \times 16 = 12$ rad/s, and $\theta = \int_0^t \frac{1}{2}bt'^2\,dt' = \frac{1}{6}bt^3 = 16$ rad, about 2.5 turns. The rim moves at $r\omega_z = 0.25 \times 12 = 3.0$ m/s. Its tangential acceleration is $r\alpha_z = 0.25 \times 6.0 = 1.5$ m/s² and its radial acceleration $-r\omega_z^2 = -0.25 \times 144 = -36$ m/s², nearly four times $g$, towards the axis.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Constant angular acceleration">
$\omega_{z,0} = 45 \times 2\pi/60 \approx 4.71$ rad/s, so $\alpha_z = -4.71/5.0 \approx -0.94$ rad/s². The angle is the average angular velocity times the time: $\frac{1}{2} \times 4.71 \times 5.0 \approx 11.8$ rad, or 1.9 turns.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Galaxies are not rigid">
The notes open this chapter with a quotation from Vera Rubin about the rotation of spiral galaxies. A rigid disc has one angular velocity, so the speed of its parts grows in proportion to the distance from the axis. A galaxy is not rigid: each star orbits on its own. If most of its mass sat in the bright centre, stars far out would move more slowly, as the outer planets do. Rubin and her colleagues found instead that the orbital speeds stay roughly constant far out, or even keep rising, so the angular velocity falls off no faster than $1/r$ and the enclosed mass keeps growing at least in proportion to $r$, beyond the visible galaxy: evidence for dark matter. (Only the inner parts of a galaxy turn roughly like a rigid disc.)
</Callout>

## Summary

- A rigid body keeps the distance between any two of its points fixed.
- General motion = translation of the centre of mass + rotation about it; in the centre-of-mass frame only rotation is left.
- Fixed-axis rotation: $\vec\omega = (d\theta/dt)\,\hat k$, $\vec\alpha = (d^2\theta/dt^2)\,\hat k$, the same for every point; same directions mean speeding up.
- A point at distance $r$: $v_\theta = r\omega_z$, $a_\theta = r\alpha_z$, $a_r = -r\omega_z^2$.
- $\omega_z(t) = \omega_{z,0} + \int_0^t \alpha_z\,dt'$ and $\theta(t) = \theta_0 + \int_0^t \omega_z\,dt'$.
