A rolling wheel both moves along and turns. This lesson describes that motion by splitting it in two, using the [centre-of-mass frame](/courses/8-01/rigid-body-kinematics).

## Two frames

Follow a point P on the rim of a wheel of radius $R$ (35.1 transcript, p. 1). In the frame fixed to the ground its position is complicated, so introduce a second frame moving with the centre of mass. If $\vec R_{\text{cm}}$ is the position of the centre and $\vec r'_P$ the position of P relative to it, then $\vec r_P = \vec R_{\text{cm}} + \vec r'_P$, and differentiating gives the law of addition of velocities (p. 1; notes, Eq. (20.2.1), p. 20-2):

<Formula label="Velocity of a point on the wheel" tex="\vec v_P = \vec V_{\text{cm}} + \vec v'_P" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

with $\vec V_{\text{cm}}$ the velocity of the centre and $\vec v'_P$ the velocity of P in the centre-of-mass frame.

In the **centre-of-mass frame** the centre is at rest and every point of the rim simply goes round in a circle, its velocity tangent to the circle with speed $R\frac{d\theta}{dt} = R\omega$ (35.2 transcript, p. 1; notes, Eq. (20.2.5), p. 20-3). In the **ground frame**, add $\vec V_{\text{cm}}$, the same for every point, to that circular velocity (35.3 transcript, p. 1). At the top the two point the same way and add; at the bottom they point opposite ways and partly or wholly cancel; at the front and back they add at right angles (p. 1).

## Rolling, slipping and skidding

In a time $\Delta t$ the centre moves $\Delta X_{\text{cm}} = V_{\text{cm}}\,\Delta t$, while a point of the rim turns through $\Delta\theta$ and covers an arc $\Delta s = R\,\Delta\theta$ (35.4 transcript, p. 1; notes, Eqs. (20.2.10)–(20.2.11), pp. 20-4–20-5). Comparing the two, and letting $\Delta t \to 0$, gives three cases (pp. 1–3; notes, Eqs. (20.2.12)–(20.2.17), p. 20-5):

<Formula label="Rolling without slipping" tex="\Delta X_{\text{cm}} = \Delta s \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_{\text{cm}} = R\,\omega" cite="reading5" at="pp. 1–2" />

- **Rolling without slipping:** the arc length equals the distance along the ground, $V_{\text{cm}} = R\omega$ (pp. 1–2).
- **Slipping** (spinning): $R\omega > V_{\text{cm}}$; in the extreme case a wheel spins on ice without moving forward (p. 2).
- **Skidding:** $V_{\text{cm}} > R\omega$; in the extreme case a braked wheel slides along without turning at all (pp. 2–3).

## The contact point

For a wheel rolling without slipping, look at the point P touching the ground (35.5 transcript, p. 1). The two velocities cancel (p. 1); in components (notes, Eq. (20.2.18), p. 20-5), $\vec v'_P$ points backward with size $R\omega$, while $\vec V_{\text{cm}}$ points forward with size $V_{\text{cm}}$. Rolling without slipping makes the sizes equal, so (p. 1; notes, Eq. (20.2.18), p. 20-5):

<Formula label="The contact point" tex="\vec v_{P,\text{contact}} = \vec V_{\text{cm}} + \vec v'_P = (V_{\text{cm}} - R\omega)\,\hat i = 0" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (20.2.18), p. 20-5" />

The contact point is **instantaneously at rest** relative to the ground, which is the surprising heart of rolling without slipping (p. 1). Other points are not: at the top the two velocities add, so the top moves at $2V_{\text{cm}}$ (p. 2). The accelerations are simpler still: if $V_{\text{cm}}$ is constant, the centre does not accelerate, and every rim point has the same acceleration in both frames, $V_{\text{cm}}^2/R$ towards the centre (notes, Eqs. (20.2.25) and (20.2.38), pp. 20-7–20-9). So the contact point, though momentarily at rest, accelerates upward.

Following one point of the rim over time, measured from the top at $t = 0$, its position in the ground frame is (notes, Example 20.1, Eq. (20.2.39), p. 20-9)

<Formula label="Path of a point on the rim" tex="x = V_{\text{cm}}t + R\sin\frac{V_{\text{cm}}t}{R}, \qquad y = R\left(1 + \cos\frac{V_{\text{cm}}t}{R}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (20.2.39), p. 20-9" />

a curve called a **cycloid** (notes, p. 20-9). Each time the point touches the ground, $y = 0$, it stops for an instant and turns back up.

<WorkedExample title="A bicycle, a braking car and a wheel on ice">
<Problem>
(a) A bicycle moves at 6.0 m/s on wheels of radius 0.35 m, rolling without slipping. Find the angular speed, the speed of the top of a wheel, and the speed of the point level with the axle at the front. (b) A braking car moves at 20 m/s while its wheels (radius 0.30 m) turn at 40 rad/s. Classify the motion and find the velocity of the contact point. (c) A wheel of radius 0.30 m spins at 50 rad/s on ice while the car creeps forward at 2.0 m/s. Same questions.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Rolling without slipping">
$\omega = V_{\text{cm}}/R = 6.0/0.35 \approx 17$ rad/s. The top moves at $2 \times 6.0 = 12$ m/s. At the front, the rim velocity in the centre-of-mass frame points straight down with size 6.0 m/s, at right angles to $\vec V_{\text{cm}}$, so the speed is $\sqrt{6.0^2 + 6.0^2} \approx 8.5$ m/s, pointing 45° below the horizontal.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Skidding">
$R\omega = 0.30 \times 40 = 12$ m/s $< 20$ m/s: skidding. The contact point moves at $20 - 12 = 8.0$ m/s forward, sliding along the road.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Slipping">
$R\omega = 0.30 \times 50 = 15$ m/s $> 2.0$ m/s: slipping. The contact point moves at $2.0 - 15 = -13$ m/s, that is, 13 m/s backward, polishing the ice.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The same curve in cosmology">
The cycloid turns up again in the history of a closed universe filled with matter (8.286, Lecture 9). Its scale factor and the time since the Big Bang follow $a/\sqrt k = \alpha(1 - \cos\theta)$ and $ct = \alpha(\theta - \sin\theta)$: the path of a point on a wheel of radius $\alpha$ rolling along the time axis, with the Big Bang and the Big Crunch where the point touches the ground.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A point on a rolling wheel: $\vec v_P = \vec V_{\text{cm}} + \vec v'_P$, translation plus circular motion of speed $R\omega$.
- Rolling without slipping: $V_{\text{cm}} = R\omega$; slipping: $R\omega > V_{\text{cm}}$; skidding: $V_{\text{cm}} > R\omega$.
- Rolling without slipping: the contact point is instantaneously at rest, the top moves at $2V_{\text{cm}}$, and a rim point traces a cycloid.
