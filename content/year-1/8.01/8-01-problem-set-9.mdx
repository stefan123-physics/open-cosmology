This set practises the lessons on the [types of collision](/courses/8-01/types-of-collision) and on [elastic collisions](/courses/8-01/elastic-collisions). It also leans on [potential energy](/courses/8-01/potential-energy) and on [mechanical energy and energy conservation](/courses/8-01/energy-conservation), because in four of the problems a collision is followed by a climb, a spring or a rough table. The six problems follow MIT's Problem Set 9, in our own words. MIT's set comes with a drawing for each problem. Here each situation is described in words, so make your own sketch first. MIT publishes no solutions for this set, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. All six problems count for the score (50 points).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Sketch the situation before and after, choose a positive direction, and decide which of the two tools you may use: momentum (when no outside force acts along the motion) and mechanical energy (when nothing is lost to friction or to a sticking collision). Then type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A formula box takes plain typing, for example `-vp0 - vb0`, `mb*v0/(M + mb)` or `v1*sqrt(m1/k)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type the names as they are shown next to each box. The number box of Problem 6 accepts any value within 1% of ours.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A ping-pong ball meets a bowling ball" points={6} mit="Problem 1">

A ping-pong ball and a bowling ball move towards each other along the $x$ axis on a frictionless surface. The ping-pong ball, of mass $m_p$, moves in the $+x$ direction with speed $v_{p,0}$. The bowling ball, of mass $m_b$, moves in the $-x$ direction with speed $v_{b,0}$. They collide elastically, head-on. The bowling ball is very much heavier than the ping-pong ball ($m_p \ll m_b$), so take its velocity to be unchanged by the collision.

What is the $x$ component of the velocity of the ping-pong ball after the collision? Mind the sign. Use any of $v_{p,0}$ and $v_{b,0}$ that you need.

<Hint>

The bowling ball hardly notices the collision. In which frame of reference does this look like a ball that bounces off a wall at rest?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In a one-dimensional elastic collision the relative velocity of the two bodies reverses, whatever the masses. Write the relative velocity before the collision, with the signs of both velocities, and reverse it.

</Hint>

<Hint>

After the collision the bowling ball still has the velocity $-v_{b,0}$. The relative velocity after the collision is the velocity of the ping-pong ball minus this.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-v" label="$v_{px,f} =$" expr="-vp0 - 2*vb0"
  vars={{ vp0: [1, 5], vb0: [1, 5] }}
  names={{ vp0: "v_{p,0}", vb0: "v_{b,0}" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "vp0 + 2*vb0", hint: "The size is right, but the question asks for the x component. After the bounce the ping-pong ball moves in the −x direction." },
    { expr: "-vp0", hint: "That is the bounce off a heavy body at rest. The bowling ball is moving towards the ping-pong ball, so the bounce is harder." },
    { expr: "-vp0 - vb0", hint: "That is the velocity of the ping-pong ball relative to the bowling ball after the collision. To get its velocity over the surface, add the velocity of the bowling ball, which is −v_b,0." },
    { expr: "-vp0 + 2*vb0", hint: "Check the sign of the bowling ball's velocity: it moves in the −x direction, so its x component is −v_b,0. A bowling ball that comes towards the ping-pong ball makes it bounce back faster, not slower." },
    { expr: "vp0 + vb0", hint: "That is the speed at which the two balls approach each other. After the collision they separate at this speed, but the question asks for the x component of the ping-pong ball's velocity over the surface." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** An elastic collision in one dimension reverses the relative velocity of the two bodies. The bowling ball is so heavy that its own velocity stays the same, so the ping-pong ball has to take the whole change.

<Step title="The rule for elastic collisions">

The lesson on [elastic collisions](/courses/8-01/elastic-collisions) combines momentum and kinetic energy into one linear rule, which the notes call the energy-momentum principle. For two bodies 1 and 2 on a line:

<Formula label="Energy-momentum principle" tex="v_{1x,i} - v_{2x,i} = v_{2x,f} - v_{1x,f}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (15.4.7), p. 15-8" />

It holds for any two masses.

</Step>

<Step title="Apply it to the two balls">

Let body 1 be the ping-pong ball and body 2 the bowling ball. Before the collision the $x$ components are $v_{p,0}$ and $-v_{b,0}$. After it the bowling ball still has $-v_{b,0}$, and the ping-pong ball has the unknown $v_{px,f}$:

<Formula label="Ping-pong ball: relative velocity before and after" tex="v_{p,0} - (-v_{b,0}) = (-v_{b,0}) - v_{px,f}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

Solve for the unknown:

<Formula label="Ping-pong ball: velocity after the collision" tex="v_{px,f} = -v_{p,0} - 2\,v_{b,0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Look at the collision from the bowling ball. In its frame the ping-pong ball arrives at the speed $v_{p,0} + v_{b,0}$, bounces off a body that stays at rest, and leaves at the same speed in the opposite direction, with the velocity $-(v_{p,0} + v_{b,0})$. Going back to the surface means adding the velocity of the bowling ball, $-v_{b,0}$, which gives $-v_{p,0} - 2\,v_{b,0}$ again.

If the bowling ball is at rest ($v_{b,0} = 0$) the result is $-v_{p,0}$: the ball bounces back at its own speed, as off a wall.

The result is the limit of a very heavy bowling ball. The notes give the general result for two masses:

<Formula label="Elastic collision: final velocity of body 1" tex="v_{1x,f} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{1x,i} + \frac{2\,m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{2x,i}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (15.4.14), p. 15-9" />

When $m_2$ is much greater than $m_1$ the first fraction goes to $-1$ and the second to $2$, so $v_{1x,f} \to -v_{1x,i} + 2\,v_{2x,i}$, which is our result with $v_{1x,i} = v_{p,0}$ and $v_{2x,i} = -v_{b,0}$.

An example with our own numbers: $v_{p,0} = 3.0$ m/s and $v_{b,0} = 2.0$ m/s give $v_{px,f} = -3.0 - 2 \times 2.0 = -7.0$ m/s. With the masses $m_p = 2.7$ g and $m_b = 7.0$ kg the general formula gives $-6.996$ m/s: the limit is very close.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A satellite flies round the Earth" points={6} mit="Problem 2">

A satellite flies past the Earth on a hyperbolic orbit. Its mass is negligible compared with the mass of the Earth. In the frame in which the velocities are measured (think of the frame of the Sun), the Earth moves in the $+x$ direction with speed $v_e$. The satellite comes from the $-x$ side and, far from the Earth, moves in the $+x$ direction with speed $v_i$: it catches up with the Earth from behind. It swings round the Earth and flies back the way it came. Far from the Earth again, it moves in the $-x$ direction with speed $v_f$.

MIT's drawing shows the path as a curve that is open towards $-x$: the satellite comes in along the upper branch, rounds the Earth at the tip, and leaves along the lower branch. Treat the motion far from the Earth as one-dimensional, as the lesson does for its slingshot.

For the case $v_i = 4\,v_e$, what is the final speed $v_f$ of the satellite, in terms of $v_e$?

<Hint>

The satellite never touches the Earth. Still, long before and long after the fly-by the pull of gravity is negligible, and no mechanical energy is lost on the way. Which kind of collision does that make it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The Earth is far heavier, so its velocity stays $+v_e$. Seen from the Earth, how fast does the satellite approach? Note that both move in the $+x$ direction before the fly-by.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Seen from the Earth the satellite leaves at the speed at which it came, in the opposite direction. Then go back to the frame in which the Earth moves.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-v" label="$v_f =$" expr="2*ve"
  vars={{ ve: [1, 5] }}
  names={{ ve: "v_e" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "6*ve", hint: "That is the result when the Earth comes towards the satellite, as the planet does in the lesson's slingshot. Here both move in the +x direction before the fly-by, so the satellite approaches the Earth at the difference of the two speeds." },
    { expr: "4*ve", hint: "The satellite keeps its speed in the frame of the Earth. In a frame in which the Earth moves, its speed changes." },
    { expr: "3*ve", hint: "That is the speed of the satellite relative to the Earth, before and after. To go back to the frame in which the Earth moves, add the Earth's velocity, which points opposite to the satellite's final velocity." },
    { expr: "-2*ve", hint: "The size is right. The question asks for the speed, which is positive: the direction is already in the statement." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Far from the Earth the satellite and the Earth do not interact, and gravity is a conservative force, so the kinetic energy of the pair is the same long before and long after. The fly-by acts as a one-dimensional elastic collision with a very heavy body, as in Problem 1: the relative velocity reverses and the Earth keeps its velocity.

<Step title="The relative velocity before and after">

Take the satellite as body 1 and the Earth as body 2 in the energy-momentum principle (Problem 1). The $x$ components before are $v_i$ and $v_e$. After, they are $-v_f$ and $v_e$:

<Formula label="Fly-by: relative velocity before and after" tex="v_i - v_e = v_e - (-v_f)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Fly-by: final speed" tex="v_f = v_i - 2\,v_e" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The case of the problem">

With $v_i = 4\,v_e$ the satellite approaches the Earth at the relative speed $3\,v_e$ and leaves it at $3\,v_e$ the other way. In the frame in which the Earth moves, that is

<Formula label="Fly-by: final speed for the given case" tex="v_f = 4\,v_e - 2\,v_e = 2\,v_e" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For an Earth at rest ($v_e = 0$) the formula gives $v_f = v_i$. That is what energy asks for: an Earth that stays at rest takes no kinetic energy, so the satellite leaves with the speed it came with.

Here the satellite comes back at half its speed, so it keeps a quarter of its kinetic energy. The rest went to the Earth, whose speed changes far too little to notice. Compare the slingshot of the lesson on [elastic collisions](/courses/8-01/elastic-collisions): there the planet comes towards the spacecraft, the relative speed is the sum of the two speeds, and the spacecraft gains $2V$. A satellite that passes round the Earth from behind and turns back, as here, loses $2\,v_e$ instead.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A cube slides up a curved block" points={12} mit="Problem 3">

A block of mass $m_b$ rests on a frictionless table. One face of the block is curved: seen from the side it is an arc of a circle of radius $R$, which starts level with the table and gets steeper as it rises. (MIT's drawing shows this side view, with the cube to the left of the block.) A small cube of mass $m_c$, with $m_c < m_b$, slides along the table with speed $v_{c,0}$ towards the foot of the curved face and then up the face. There is no friction between the cube and the table, nor between the cube and the block. The block is not fixed to the table, so it moves too. Gravity pulls downward with the acceleration $g$.

Take the curved face to meet the table smoothly, so that the cube slides onto it without a jolt. (This is our reading of the drawing.)

<Part label="a" points={5}>

What is the greatest height $h$ above the table that the cube reaches? Take $v_{c,0}$ to be small enough that $h < R$. Use any of $v_{c,0}$, $g$, $m_b$ and $m_c$ that you need.

<Hint>

Take the cube and the block together as the system. Which outside forces act on it, and in which direction do they point? What does that say about the horizontal momentum?

</Hint>

<Hint>

At its highest point the cube is, for a moment, at rest relative to the block. So at that moment the two have the same velocity, and it is horizontal.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Nothing is lost to friction, so the kinetic energy plus the gravitational potential energy of the system is the same at the start and at the highest point. At the highest point the system still has kinetic energy.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$h =$" expr="mb*vc0^2/(2*g*(mb + mc))"
  vars={{ vc0: [1, 3], g: [9, 10], mb: [2, 5], mc: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ vc0: "v_{c,0}", mb: "m_b", mc: "m_c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "vc0^2/(2*g)", hint: "That is the height on a block that is fixed to the table, where all the kinetic energy turns into height. This block moves, and at the cube's highest point the two still share some kinetic energy." },
    { expr: "mc*vc0^2/(2*g*(mb + mc))", hint: "Check a very heavy block: it should act as a fixed one, with the height v_c,0²/(2g), but your height goes to zero. The two masses are the wrong way round." },
    { expr: "mc^2*vc0^2/(2*g*(mb + mc)^2)", hint: "This is V²/(2g), with V the common speed at the highest point. The kinetic energy of that common motion is the part that has not turned into height." },
    { expr: "mb*vc0^2/(g*(mb + mc))", hint: "Check the factor one half in the kinetic energy: the height is twice too large." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** While the cube is on the curved face, the cube and the block push on each other. Those forces are inside the system of the two. The outside forces, the weights and the push of the table, are all vertical, so the horizontal momentum of the system stays the same. No friction acts, so the mechanical energy stays the same too. At the highest point the cube does not move relative to the block: the two then share one horizontal velocity.

<Step title="Horizontal momentum">

Take $x$ along the table, in the direction of the cube's first motion. At the start only the cube moves. At the highest point both move with the same velocity $V$:

<Formula label="Cube and block: common velocity at the highest point" tex="m_c\,v_{c,0} = (m_b + m_c)\,V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V = \frac{m_c\,v_{c,0}}{m_b + m_c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

This is the same equation as for a collision in which two bodies stick together (the lesson on [types of collision](/courses/8-01/types-of-collision)), but here nothing is lost: the missing kinetic energy is stored as height.

</Step>

<Step title="Mechanical energy">

With no friction and no outside work the mechanical energy is constant:

<Formula label="Conservation of mechanical energy" tex="K_f + U_f = K_i + U_i" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (14.25), p. 14-15" />

Take the gravitational potential energy $U = m g y$ of the notes (Eq. (14.7), p. 14-12), with $y = 0$ at the table. The block stays on the table, so only the cube's height counts:

<Formula label="Cube and block: energy at the start and at the highest point" tex="\tfrac12\,(m_b + m_c)\,V^2 + m_c\,g\,h = \tfrac12\,m_c\,v_{c,0}^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The height">

Put in $V$:

<Formula label="Cube and block: energy stored as height" tex="m_c\,g\,h = \tfrac12\,m_c\,v_{c,0}^2\left(1 - \frac{m_c}{m_b + m_c}\right) = \tfrac12\,m_c\,v_{c,0}^2\;\frac{m_b}{m_b + m_c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Greatest height of the cube" tex="h = \frac{m_b}{m_b + m_c}\;\frac{v_{c,0}^2}{2g}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A speed squared divided by an acceleration is a length. For a very heavy block the fraction goes to 1 and $h = v_{c,0}^2/(2g)$, the height on a fixed ramp. For a very light block $h$ goes to zero: the cube pushes the block away and hardly climbs.

The fraction $m_b/(m_b + m_c)$ is the fraction of kinetic energy that is lost when a body of mass $m_c$ sticks to a body of mass $m_b$ at rest (the lesson on types of collision). Here that part is not lost. It becomes potential energy.

An example with our own numbers: $m_c = 0.20$ kg, $m_b = 0.60$ kg and $v_{c,0} = 2.0$ m/s, with $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. The common velocity is $V = 0.20 \times 2.0/0.80 = 0.50$ m/s. The cube starts with $0.5 \times 0.20 \times 2.0^2 = 0.40$ J. At the highest point the two share $0.5 \times 0.80 \times 0.50^2 = 0.10$ J, so 0.30 J is stored as height: $h = 0.30/(0.20 \times 9.8) = 0.153$ m. The formula gives the same: $0.75 \times 2.0^2/(2 \times 9.8) = 0.153$ m.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

After its highest point the cube slides back down the curved face and onto the table. Once the cube and the block have separated, what is the speed (the magnitude of the velocity) of each? Use any of $v_{c,0}$, $g$, $m_b$ and $m_c$ that you need.

<Hint>

Compare the state long before the cube reaches the block with the state after it has left it. In both the cube is on the table. What is the same in the two states?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Horizontal momentum and kinetic energy are both the same before and after: these are the two equations of an elastic collision in one dimension, with the block at rest at the start.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the rule that the relative velocity reverses, together with momentum. Then look at the sign of the cube's velocity, remembering that $m_c < m_b$. A speed is positive.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b-cube" label="Speed of the cube $=$" expr="(mb - mc)*vc0/(mb + mc)"

<Answer id="p3-b-block" label="Speed of the block $=$" expr="2*mc*vc0/(mb + mc)"
  vars={{ vc0: [1, 3], mb: [2, 5], mc: [0.5, 1.5] }}

<Solution>

**The idea.** Before the cube reaches the block and after it has left, the cube is on the table, at the same height. So the potential energy is the same in the two states, and with it the kinetic energy. The horizontal momentum is the same as well. Those are the two conditions of a one-dimensional elastic collision: the whole trip up and down the curved face is a slow elastic collision.

<Step title="The two conditions">

Call the final $x$ components $v_{cx,f}$ for the cube and $v_{bx,f}$ for the block. Momentum along $x$ is the first condition:

<Formula label="Cube and block: momentum before and after" tex="m_c\,v_{c,0} = m_c\,v_{cx,f} + m_b\,v_{bx,f}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

Instead of the kinetic energy itself, use the energy-momentum principle of Problem 1, which follows from the two together. The block starts at rest, so the relative velocity before is $v_{c,0}$:

<Formula label="Cube and block: relative velocity before and after" tex="v_{c,0} = v_{bx,f} - v_{cx,f}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

Put $v_{bx,f} = v_{c,0} + v_{cx,f}$ into the momentum equation. That gives $(m_c - m_b)\,v_{c,0} = (m_c + m_b)\,v_{cx,f}$, so

<Formula label="Cube and block: final velocities" tex="v_{cx,f} = -\frac{m_b - m_c}{m_b + m_c}\,v_{c,0}, \qquad v_{bx,f} = \frac{2\,m_c}{m_b + m_c}\,v_{c,0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

These agree with Eqs. (15.4.14) and (15.4.17) of the notes (pp. 15-9 and 15-10) for a target at rest. Because $m_c < m_b$, the cube's velocity is negative: it slides back the way it came. The speeds are

<Formula label="Cube and block: final speeds" tex="|v_{cx,f}| = \frac{m_b - m_c}{m_b + m_c}\,v_{c,0}, \qquad |v_{bx,f}| = \frac{2\,m_c}{m_b + m_c}\,v_{c,0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

Neither contains $g$ or $R$: the shape of the face and the strength of gravity decide how long the trip takes, not how it ends.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a very heavy block the cube comes back at $v_{c,0}$ and the block stays at rest, as for a fixed ramp. The block ends with twice the common velocity $V$ of part (a). The reason lies in this frictionless motion: seen from a frame that moves with the velocity $V$, the trip down is the trip up run backwards, so the block gains as much velocity on the way down as on the way up.

In the example of part (a) ($m_c = 0.20$ kg, $m_b = 0.60$ kg, $v_{c,0} = 2.0$ m/s) the cube ends at $0.40 \times 2.0/0.80 = 1.0$ m/s, moving backwards, and the block at $2 \times 0.20 \times 2.0/0.80 = 1.0$ m/s, moving forwards. Momentum: $0.20 \times (-1.0) + 0.60 \times 1.0 = 0.40$ kg m/s, equal to $0.20 \times 2.0 = 0.40$ kg m/s at the start. Kinetic energy: $0.10 + 0.30 = 0.40$ J, the cube's kinetic energy at the start.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A block runs into a spring on a second block" points={7} mit="Problem 4">

Block 1, of mass $m_1$, slides to the right with speed $v_1$ on a level, frictionless table. Ahead of it block 2, of mass $m_2$, is at rest. A spring of spring constant $k$ is fixed to block 2 on the side that faces block 1, and its other end is free. (MIT's drawing shows the two blocks on the table, with the spring on the left side of block 2 and block 1 coming from the left.) Block 1 runs into the free end of the spring. Everything moves along one line. Take the spring to have no mass (our assumption).

What is the greatest compression of the spring? Use any of $k$, $m_1$, $m_2$ and $v_1$ that you need.

<Hint>

Block 2 is free, so it starts to move as soon as the spring is pressed in. At the moment the spring is shortest, what can you say about the velocities of the two blocks?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The spring is shortest when the distance between the blocks stops shrinking: at that moment the two blocks have the same velocity. Momentum gives that velocity.

</Hint>

<Hint>

There is no friction, and the spring force is conservative, so kinetic energy plus spring potential energy is constant. At the moment of greatest compression the blocks still have kinetic energy.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-x" label="Compression $=$" expr="v1*sqrt(m1*m2/(k*(m1 + m2)))"
  vars={{ v1: [1, 5], m1: [1, 4], m2: [1, 4], k: [50, 200] }}
  names={{ v1: "v_1", m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v1*sqrt(m1/k)", hint: "This puts all of block 1's kinetic energy into the spring. That happens only when block 2 cannot move. Here block 2 is free, and at the greatest compression both blocks are moving." },
    { expr: "m1*v1/sqrt(k*(m1 + m2))", hint: "This sets the spring energy equal to the kinetic energy of the common motion. The spring holds the rest: the kinetic energy at the start minus the kinetic energy of the common motion." },
    { expr: "m1*m2*v1^2/(k*(m1 + m2))", hint: "Check the units: this is in m², the square of the compression. Take the root." },
    { expr: "v1*sqrt(m1*m2/(2*k*(m1 + m2)))", hint: "Check the factors one half: both the kinetic energy and the spring energy have one, so they cancel." },
    { expr: "v1*sqrt(m2/k)", hint: "The kinetic energy at the start belongs to block 1, and only a part of it goes into the spring. At the greatest compression both blocks move with the same velocity." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** While the spring is being pressed in, block 1 slows down and block 2 speeds up. As long as block 1 is the faster one, the spring gets shorter. It is shortest at the moment the two velocities are equal. Momentum fixes that common velocity, and energy then says how much is stored in the spring.

<Step title="The common velocity">

No outside force acts along the table, so the momentum of the two blocks is constant. At the greatest compression both move with the velocity $V$:

<Formula label="Two blocks: common velocity at the greatest compression" tex="m_1\,v_1 = (m_1 + m_2)\,V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V = \frac{m_1\,v_1}{m_1 + m_2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Energy">

The notes give the potential energy of a spring that is stretched or pressed in by $x$ from its natural length:

<Formula label="Potential energy of a spring" tex="U(x) = \tfrac12\,k\,x^2" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (14.12), p. 14-13" />

Mechanical energy is constant (Problem 3). At the start the spring is at its natural length. At the greatest compression $x_\text{max}$ the blocks share the velocity $V$:

<Formula label="Two blocks: energy at the start and at the greatest compression" tex="\tfrac12\,m_1\,v_1^2 = \tfrac12\,(m_1 + m_2)\,V^2 + \tfrac12\,k\,x_\text{max}^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The compression">

Put in $V$:

<Formula label="Two blocks: energy in the spring" tex="\tfrac12\,k\,x_\text{max}^2 = \tfrac12\,m_1\,v_1^2\left(1 - \frac{m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\right) = \tfrac12\,\frac{m_1\,m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_1^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Greatest compression of the spring" tex="x_\text{max} = v_1\,\sqrt{\frac{m_1\,m_2}{k\,(m_1 + m_2)}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: a mass divided by a spring constant is in $\text{s}^2$, so the root is a time, and a speed times a time is a length.

For a very heavy block 2 the mass under the root goes to $m_1$, and $x_\text{max} = v_1\sqrt{m_1/k}$: all of block 1's kinetic energy goes into the spring, as against a wall. The result does not change when $m_1$ and $m_2$ are swapped.

The notes reach the same energy another way. They split the kinetic energy of two bodies into a part that belongs to the motion of the centre of mass, which cannot change here, and a part that depends on the relative velocity $v_{1,2}$:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy of two bodies" tex="K = \tfrac12\,\mu\,v_{1,2}^2 + \tfrac12\,(m_1 + m_2)\,v_\text{cm}^2, \qquad \frac{1}{\mu} = \frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (15.2.14) and (15.2.19), pp. 15-5 and 15-6" />

At the greatest compression the relative velocity is zero, so the whole first part, $\tfrac12\,\mu\,v_1^2$, is in the spring. That is our result, because $\mu = m_1 m_2/(m_1 + m_2)$.

An example with our own numbers: $m_1 = 1.0$ kg, $m_2 = 3.0$ kg, $v_1 = 2.0$ m/s and $k = 300$ N/m. The common velocity is $V = 1.0 \times 2.0/4.0 = 0.50$ m/s. Block 1 starts with $0.5 \times 1.0 \times 2.0^2 = 2.0$ J, and the common motion holds $0.5 \times 4.0 \times 0.50^2 = 0.50$ J. The spring stores the other 1.5 J, so $x_\text{max} = \sqrt{2 \times 1.5/300} = 0.10$ m.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A bullet stops in a block on a spring" points={13} mit="Problem 5">

A block of mass $M$ rests on a level, frictionless table. A spring of spring constant $k$ and of negligible mass joins the block to a wall: one end is fixed to the block, the other to the wall, and the spring is at its natural length. A bullet of mass $m_b$ is fired along the line of the spring, from the side away from the wall, with speed $v_0$. It hits the block and comes to rest inside it. Take this to happen so fast that the block has not yet moved when the bullet has stopped in it.

Call the position of the block at that moment $x = 0$, with $x$ growing towards the wall. (MIT's first two drawings show the bullet, the block at $x = 0$ and the spring to the wall on the right, before the hit and just after it.)

<Part label="a" points={2}>

How fast does the block move just after the bullet has come to rest in it? Use any of $M$, $m_b$ and $v_0$ that you need.

<Hint>

The bullet stays in the block. Which kind of collision is that, and which quantity can you trust in it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

During the very short collision the spring is still at its natural length, so it does not push yet. No outside force acts along the line of motion.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="Speed $=$" expr="mb*v0/(M + mb)"
  vars={{ M: [1, 5], mb: [0.01, 0.1], v0: [100, 400] }}
  names={{ mb: "m_b", v0: "v_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mb*v0/M", hint: "After the collision the bullet rides along inside the block, so the moving mass is that of the block and the bullet together." },
    { expr: "v0*sqrt(mb/(M + mb))", hint: "This keeps the kinetic energy the same. In a collision in which the bodies stick together, kinetic energy is lost. Use momentum." },
    { expr: "v0", hint: "The bullet now has to carry the block along. The momentum is shared by a much greater mass, so the speed drops." },
    { expr: "(M + mb)*v0/mb", hint: "The masses are upside down: this speed is greater than the bullet's own. The pair is heavier than the bullet, so it moves more slowly." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The bullet and the block stick together: a totally inelastic collision. Kinetic energy is lost, but the collision is over before the spring starts to push, so the momentum along the line is the same just before and just after.

<Step title="Momentum">

The notes write the momentum condition for a body that hits a body at rest and sticks to it:

<Formula label="Totally inelastic collision with a body at rest" tex="m_1\,v_{1,i} = (m_1 + m_2)\,v_f" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (15.5.3), p. 15-13" />

With the bullet as body 1 and the block as body 2, and $v_a$ for the speed just after the collision (the name in MIT's drawing):

<Formula label="Bullet and block: speed just after the collision" tex="v_a = \frac{m_b\,v_0}{M + m_b}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The fraction of the masses has no unit, so the result is a speed. It is smaller than $v_0$, and much smaller when the block is much heavier than the bullet. An example with our own numbers, used in all parts: $m_b = 0.020$ kg, $M = 0.98$ kg and $v_0 = 200$ m/s give $v_a = 0.020 \times 200/1.00 = 4.0$ m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

The block, with the bullet in it, now moves and presses in the spring. How far has it travelled from $x = 0$ when it first comes to rest? Use any of $M$, $m_b$, $k$ and $v_0$ that you need.

<Hint>

The collision is over. From now on the table is frictionless and the spring force is conservative. What stays constant?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Start from the kinetic energy just after the collision, with the speed of part (a), not from the kinetic energy of the bullet before the collision.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="Distance $=$" expr="mb*v0/sqrt(k*(M + mb))"
  vars={{ M: [1, 5], mb: [0.01, 0.1], v0: [100, 400], k: [100, 1000] }}
  names={{ mb: "m_b", v0: "v_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v0*sqrt(mb/k)", hint: "This puts the bullet's kinetic energy before the collision into the spring. Most of that energy is lost in the collision. Start from the kinetic energy just after it." },
    { expr: "mb*v0/sqrt(k*M)", hint: "The bullet is in the block, so the moving mass is that of the block and the bullet together, in the momentum and in the kinetic energy." },
    { expr: "mb^2*v0^2/(k*(M + mb))", hint: "Check the units: this is in m², the square of the distance. Take the root." },
    { expr: "mb*v0/(k*(M + mb))", hint: "Check the units: a speed divided by a spring constant is not a length. The spring energy has the square of the distance, so a root is needed." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** After the collision only the spring does work, and its force is conservative. The kinetic energy that the block and the bullet have just after the collision is all stored in the spring at the moment they stop.

<Step title="Energy after the collision">

With the spring potential energy of Problem 4 and constant mechanical energy, between $x = 0$ (spring at its natural length, speed $v_a$) and the stopping point $x = x_1$ (speed zero):

<Formula label="Bullet and block: energy from the collision to the first stop" tex="\tfrac12\,(M + m_b)\,v_a^2 = \tfrac12\,k\,x_1^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The distance">

<Formula label="Bullet and block: distance to the first stop" tex="x_1 = v_a\,\sqrt{\frac{M + m_b}{k}} = \frac{m_b\,v_0}{\sqrt{k\,(M + m_b)}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $k\,(M + m_b)$ is in $\text{kg}^2/\text{s}^2$, so its root is in kg/s, and a momentum divided by that is a length. A stiffer spring stops the block sooner. A heavier block also travels less far: it leaves the collision with the same momentum $m_b v_0$ but with less kinetic energy.

In the example of part (a), with $k = 400$ N/m: $x_1 = 4.0 \times \sqrt{1.00/400} = 0.20$ m.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Just after the bullet has come to rest in the block, what is the ratio of the kinetic energy of the block with the bullet to the kinetic energy that the bullet had before the collision? Use any of $M$, $m_b$ and $v_0$ that you need.

<Hint>

Write both kinetic energies with the speeds you know, and divide. Take care that the mass after the collision is not the mass before it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-c" label="Ratio $=$" expr="mb/(M + mb)"
  vars={{ M: [1, 5], mb: [0.01, 0.1] }}
  names={{ mb: "m_b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "M/(M + mb)", hint: "That is the fraction of the kinetic energy that is lost in the collision. The question asks for the fraction that is left." },
    { expr: "mb^2/(M + mb)^2", hint: "That is the ratio of the squares of the speeds. The moving mass also changes: it is the bullet alone before, and the block with the bullet after." },
    { expr: "mb/M", hint: "The bullet is in the block, so the mass that moves after the collision is that of the block and the bullet together." },
    { expr: "(M + mb)/mb", hint: "This ratio is greater than 1. A collision in which the bodies stick together cannot add kinetic energy: the ratio is upside down." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two kinetic energies, each one half times a mass times a speed squared. The speed after the collision is known from part (a).

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Bullet and block: kinetic energy after over kinetic energy before" tex="\frac{K_\text{after}}{K_\text{before}} = \frac{\tfrac12\,(M + m_b)\,v_a^2}{\tfrac12\,m_b\,v_0^2} = \frac{M + m_b}{m_b}\left(\frac{m_b}{M + m_b}\right)^2 = \frac{m_b}{M + m_b}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c) (our own solution)" />

The speed $v_0$ drops out.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ratio has no unit and lies between 0 and 1. The notes find, for a body of mass $m_1$ that sticks to a body of mass $m_2$ at rest, that the relative change in kinetic energy is

<Formula label="Totally inelastic collision: relative change in kinetic energy" tex="\frac{\Delta K}{K_\text{initial}} = -\frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (15.5.5), p. 15-14" />

With $m_1 = m_b$ and $m_2 = M$ the part that is left is $1 - M/(M + m_b) = m_b/(M + m_b)$, the same.

In the example of part (a) the ratio is $0.020/1.00 = 0.020$. The bullet arrives with $0.5 \times 0.020 \times 200^2 = 400$ J, and the block with the bullet leaves with $0.5 \times 1.00 \times 4.0^2 = 8.0$ J. A light bullet that stops in a heavy block loses almost all of its kinetic energy, here 98% of it, to heat and to damage in the block.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

Now the table is rough: the coefficient of kinetic friction between the block and the table is $\mu_k$. The bullet is fired as before, and the collision is again so short that the speed just after it is that of part (a). The block moves towards the wall, stops, and comes back. When it first returns to $x = 0$ its speed is found to be half the speed of part (a). (MIT's third drawing shows the block at $x = 0$ at that moment, moving away from the wall.) Gravity pulls downward with the acceleration $g$.

What is the total distance that the block travelled before it returned to $x = 0$? Use any of $M$, $m_b$, $v_0$, $\mu_k$ and $g$ that you need.

<Hint>

Compare the two moments at which the block is at $x = 0$: just after the collision and at its first return. What is the state of the spring at each of them?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The spring has its natural length at both moments, so its potential energy is the same. The whole change in kinetic energy is the work of friction.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The friction force has a constant size and points against the motion, on the way out and on the way back. So its work is minus its size times the total distance travelled.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-d" label="Total distance $=$" expr="3*mb^2*v0^2/(8*mu*g*(M + mb)^2)"
  vars={{ M: [1, 5], mb: [0.01, 0.1], v0: [100, 400], mu: [0.1, 0.6], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ mb: "m_b", v0: "v_0", mu: "\\mu_k" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*mb^2*v0^2/(16*mu*g*(M + mb)^2)", hint: "That is how far the block gets from x = 0. It comes back over the same stretch, and the question asks for the total distance travelled." },
    { expr: "mb^2*v0^2/(4*mu*g*(M + mb)^2)", hint: "Half the speed means a quarter of the kinetic energy, not half. Three quarters of the kinetic energy are lost." },
    { expr: "mb^2*v0^2/(2*mu*g*(M + mb)^2)", hint: "This takes all of the kinetic energy to be lost. The block still moves when it returns to x = 0, at half its first speed." },
    { expr: "3*v0^2/(8*mu*g)", hint: "This uses the bullet's speed v_0 for the block. The block with the bullet starts at the much smaller speed of part (a)." },
    { expr: "3*mb^2*v0^2/(8*mu*(M + mb)^2)", hint: "Check the units: without g this is not a length. The friction force is the coefficient times the normal force, which is the weight of the block with the bullet." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Between the two moments at $x = 0$ the spring gives back all the energy it took, because it is at its natural length both times. Friction alone accounts for the lost kinetic energy, and its work is fixed by the total length of the path.

<Step title="The work of friction">

The table is level, so the normal force on the block with the bullet is its weight, and the friction force has the size $\mu_k\,(M + m_b)\,g$. It points against the velocity all the time. Over a path of total length $D$ (out and back) its work is

<Formula label="Rough table: work done by friction" tex="W_\text{nc} = -\mu_k\,(M + m_b)\,g\,D" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The energy balance">

The work of a non-conservative force changes the mechanical energy (the lesson on [energy conservation](/courses/8-01/energy-conservation)):

<Formula label="Non-conservative work" tex="W_\text{nc} = \Delta K + \Delta U" cite="reading2" at="Section 14.7, p. 14-21 (the equation numbered (14.6) there)" />

The spring is at its natural length at both moments, so $\Delta U = 0$. The speed goes from $v_a$ to $v_a/2$:

<Formula label="Rough table: energy between the two passages of x = 0" tex="-\mu_k\,(M + m_b)\,g\,D = \tfrac12\,(M + m_b)\left(\frac{v_a}{2}\right)^2 - \tfrac12\,(M + m_b)\,v_a^2 = -\tfrac38\,(M + m_b)\,v_a^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The distance">

The mass $M + m_b$ cancels. With $v_a$ from part (a):

<Formula label="Rough table: total distance travelled" tex="D = \frac{3\,v_a^2}{8\,\mu_k\,g} = \frac{3\,m_b^2\,v_0^2}{8\,\mu_k\,g\,(M + m_b)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A speed squared divided by an acceleration is a length. A rougher table gives a shorter path for the same loss of speed.

The spring constant does not appear. This does not mean that the spring plays no part: it is the spring that turns the block round. For a given $\mu_k$ the return speed depends on $k$ (a stiffer spring turns the block sooner, so that less is lost to friction), and the measured return speed stands in for $k$.

The block travels the same stretch out and back, so the farthest point is at $D/2$ from $x = 0$. In the example of part (a), with $\mu_k = 0.50$ and $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$: $D = 3 \times 4.0^2/(8 \times 0.50 \times 9.8) = 1.22$ m. Energy: the block with the bullet starts with 8.0 J and returns with a quarter of that, 2.0 J. The friction force is $0.50 \times 1.00 \times 9.8 = 4.9$ N, and $4.9 \times 1.22 = 6.0$ J is the difference.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="An elastic collision in two dimensions" points={6} mit="Problem 6">

Particle 1, of mass $m_1 = m$, moves in the $+x$ direction with speed $v_{1,i}$. It collides elastically with particle 2, of mass $m_2 = 2m$, which is at rest. After the collision particle 2 moves with an unknown speed $v_{2,f}$ in a direction that makes the angle $\theta_{2,f} = 45°$ with the $+x$ direction, on the $+y$ side. Particle 1 moves with an unknown speed $v_{1,f}$ in a direction that makes an unknown angle $\theta_{1,f}$ with the $+x$ direction, on the $-y$ side.

Both angles are positive numbers, measured from the $+x$ axis: $\theta_{2,f}$ towards $+y$ and $\theta_{1,f}$ towards $-y$. This is the convention of MIT's drawing, which shows the two particles before the collision and their two velocities after it, and it is also that of the lesson on elastic collisions.

Find $\tan\theta_{1,f}$. It is a simple number.

<Hint>

Count the unknowns: $v_{1,f}$, $v_{2,f}$ and $\theta_{1,f}$. You need three equations. Which three does an elastic collision with no outside forces give?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Momentum along $x$, momentum along $y$, and kinetic energy. Put in $m_2 = 2m$ and $\cos 45° = \sin 45°$, and divide every equation by $m$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

From the two momentum equations, write $v_{1,f}\cos\theta_{1,f}$ and $v_{1,f}\sin\theta_{1,f}$ in terms of $v_{1,i}$ and $v_{2,f}$. Squaring and adding them gives $v_{1,f}^2$, which the energy equation also gives. That fixes $v_{2,f}$, and the ratio of the two expressions is the tangent.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-tan" label="$\tan\theta_{1,f} =$" value="2" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-tan" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two components of momentum and the kinetic energy give three equations for the three unknowns. The tangent of the angle of particle 1 is the ratio of the $y$ and $x$ components of its final velocity, so the aim is to find those two components.

<Step title="The three equations">

The lesson on [elastic collisions](/courses/8-01/elastic-collisions) and the notes write them for a target at rest, with the angles measured as here:

<Formula label="Two dimensions: momentum along x" tex="m_1\,v_{1,i} = m_1\,v_{1,f}\cos\theta_{1,f} + m_2\,v_{2,f}\cos\theta_{2,f}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (15.6.5), p. 15-18" />

<Formula label="Two dimensions: momentum along y" tex="0 = m_1\,v_{1,f}\sin\theta_{1,f} - m_2\,v_{2,f}\sin\theta_{2,f}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (15.6.6), p. 15-18" />

<Formula label="Two dimensions: kinetic energy" tex="\tfrac12\,m_1\,v_{1,i}^2 = \tfrac12\,m_1\,v_{1,f}^2 + \tfrac12\,m_2\,v_{2,f}^2" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (15.6.8), p. 15-18" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put in the given values">

With $m_1 = m$, $m_2 = 2m$ and $\cos 45° = \sin 45° = 1/\sqrt2$, each component of particle 2's momentum is $2m\,v_{2,f}/\sqrt2 = \sqrt2\,m\,v_{2,f}$. Divide by $m$:

<Formula label="Particle 1: components of the final velocity" tex="v_{1,f}\cos\theta_{1,f} = v_{1,i} - \sqrt2\,v_{2,f}, \qquad v_{1,f}\sin\theta_{1,f} = \sqrt2\,v_{2,f}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Energy for the given masses" tex="v_{1,i}^2 = v_{1,f}^2 + 2\,v_{2,f}^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Find the speed of particle 2">

Square the two components and add them. Because $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$, the left side is $v_{1,f}^2$:

<Formula label="Particle 1: final speed squared" tex="v_{1,f}^2 = \left(v_{1,i} - \sqrt2\,v_{2,f}\right)^2 + 2\,v_{2,f}^2 = v_{1,i}^2 - 2\sqrt2\,v_{1,i}\,v_{2,f} + 4\,v_{2,f}^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

Put this into the energy equation:

<Formula label="Equation for the speed of particle 2" tex="v_{1,i}^2 = v_{1,i}^2 - 2\sqrt2\,v_{1,i}\,v_{2,f} + 6\,v_{2,f}^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_{2,f} = \frac{\sqrt2}{3}\,v_{1,i}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

(The other root, $v_{2,f} = 0$, is the case of no collision at all.)

</Step>

<Step title="The tangent">

Then $\sqrt2\,v_{2,f} = \tfrac23\,v_{1,i}$, and the components of particle 1's final velocity are

<Formula label="Particle 1: components of the final velocity, solved" tex="v_{1,f}\cos\theta_{1,f} = \tfrac13\,v_{1,i}, \qquad v_{1,f}\sin\theta_{1,f} = \tfrac23\,v_{1,i}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

Divide the second by the first:

<Formula label="Tangent of the angle of particle 1" tex="\tan\theta_{1,f} = \frac{2/3}{1/3} = 2" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The tangent has no unit, and $v_{1,i}$ has dropped out: the angles of an elastic collision depend on the mass ratio and on how the particles meet, not on the speed.

The other unknowns follow. The angle is $\theta_{1,f} = 63.4°$, and the final speeds are $v_{1,f} = (\sqrt5/3)\,v_{1,i} = 0.745\,v_{1,i}$ and $v_{2,f} = (\sqrt2/3)\,v_{1,i} = 0.471\,v_{1,i}$. Put them back into the kinetic energy, in units of $\tfrac12 m\,v_{1,i}^2$: particle 1 keeps $5/9$ and particle 2, with twice the mass, gets $2 \times 2/9 = 4/9$. Together that is 1, so the collision is elastic.

The two particles fly apart at $63.4° + 45° = 108.4°$. For equal masses the lesson finds a right angle. Here the target is the heavier particle, and the angle between the two final velocities is greater than a right angle.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>
