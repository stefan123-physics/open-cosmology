In [uniform circular motion](/courses/8-01/uniform-circular-motion) the angle changes at a steady rate and the only acceleration points to the centre. Lesson 10 lets the rate change: a car accelerating out of a roundabout, a wheel spinning up, a fan winding down. The acceleration then gains a second part, along the circle.

## When dθ/dt is not constant

If $d\theta/dt$ changes, the object speeds up or slows down as it goes round. At every instant there is still the inward, radial part of the acceleration found in Lesson 9, $-r\,(d\theta/dt)^2$. What is new is a **tangential** part, along the direction of motion. Write the acceleration as the sum of the two:

<Formula label="Radial and tangential components" tex="\vec a(t) = a_r\,\hat r(t) + a_\theta\,\hat\theta(t)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.3.1), p. 5" />

Both unit vectors turn as the object moves, so neither is constant in time.

## The product rule gives both parts

The velocity is a product of two factors that both change in time:

<Formula label="Velocity on a circle" tex="\vec v(t) = r\,\frac{d\theta}{dt}\,\hat\theta(t)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.3.2), p. 6" />

so its derivative needs the **product rule** from calculus: the derivative of the first factor times the second, plus the first times the derivative of the second. The radius $r$ is constant, so only $d\theta/dt$ and $\hat\theta$ change:

<Formula label="Applying the product rule" tex="\vec a(t) = \frac{d\vec v}{dt} = r\,\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\,\hat\theta(t) + r\,\frac{d\theta}{dt}\,\frac{d\hat\theta}{dt}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.3.3), p. 6" />

The second term is the one already worked out for uniform motion. From Lesson 8, the unit vector $\hat\theta$ turns towards $-\hat r$ as the angle grows, $d\hat\theta/dt = -(d\theta/dt)\,\hat r$; the notes reach the same result by writing $\hat\theta$ in Cartesian components and using the chain rule. So

<Formula label="Acceleration on a circle" tex="\vec a(t) = r\,\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\,\hat\theta(t) - r\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2\hat r(t)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.3.6), p. 6" />

Reading off the components:

<Formula label="Tangential component" tex="a_\theta = r\,\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.3.7), p. 6" />

<Formula label="Radial component" tex="a_r = -r\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2 \le 0" cite="reading2" at="transcript 10.1, pp. 1–2" />

The radial part is never positive: whatever happens to the speed, as long as the object moves the velocity keeps turning, so there is an inward pull. [The notes write $a_r < 0$ and the lecturer calls it non-zero; it vanishes only at an instant when the object is momentarily at rest, $d\theta/dt = 0$, which is why this page writes $\le 0$.] The tangential part changes only the speed.

## Speeding up and slowing down

The sign of $a_\theta$ compared with the direction of motion tells you which is happening. If $d\theta/dt > 0$ (moving the way θ increases, anticlockwise with the usual choice) and $d^2\theta/dt^2 > 0$, then $d\theta/dt$ grows and the object **speeds up**. If instead $d^2\theta/dt^2 < 0$, then $d\theta/dt$ decreases: the object slows down, and, if the tangential acceleration goes on long enough, stops and starts moving the other way. [In the video the lecturer calls the first case motion "in this clockwise direction"; with θ increasing anticlockwise, as everywhere in the notes, it is anticlockwise.]

<WorkedExample title="A car accelerating round a circular track">
<Problem>
A car starts from rest on a circular track of radius 50 m. Its angle grows with a constant $d^2\theta/dt^2 = 0.020$ rad/s². Find the car's angular speed, speed, and both components of its acceleration 10 s after the start, and the direction of the total acceleration.
</Problem>
<Step title="Angular speed and speed">
Starting from rest with a constant rate of change, $d\theta/dt = 0.020 \times 10 = 0.20$ rad/s, so $v = r\,d\theta/dt = 50 \times 0.20 = 10$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Tangential component">
$a_\theta = r\,d^2\theta/dt^2 = 50 \times 0.020 = 1.0$ m/s², forwards. It stays the same all the time.
</Step>
<Step title="Radial component">
$a_r = -r\,(d\theta/dt)^2 = -50 \times 0.20^2 = -2.0$ m/s², towards the centre. It grows with the square of the speed.
</Step>
<Step title="Total acceleration">
$|\vec a| = \sqrt{1.0^2 + 2.0^2} \approx 2.24$ m/s², tilted forwards from the inward radius by an angle whose tangent is $1.0/2.0$: about 26.6°. At the start the acceleration pointed straight along the track; after $t = 1/\sqrt{0.020} \approx 7.07$ s the two parts are equal, and from then on the inward part dominates.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Angular acceleration

Lesson 8 described the rate of turning by the angular velocity vector $\vec\omega = (d\theta/dt)\,\hat k$, perpendicular to the plane of the circle. Differentiating once more gives the **angular acceleration**:

<Formula label="Angular acceleration" tex="\vec\alpha = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\,\hat k = \alpha_z\,\hat k, \qquad \alpha_z = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = \frac{d\omega_z}{dt}" cite="reading3" at="transcript 10.2, p. 1" />

The notes give the same definition, with SI unit rad/s², and call the size of $\vec\alpha$ simply $\alpha$:

<Formula label="Angular acceleration in the notes" tex="\vec\alpha = \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\,\hat k, \qquad \alpha = \left|\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\right|" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (6.5.7)–(6.5.8), pp. 6-12–6-13" />

With it, the tangential acceleration is $a_\theta = r\,\alpha_z$. For turning, $\omega_z$ and $\alpha_z$ play the roles that the velocity component $v_x$ and the acceleration component $a_x$ play for motion along a line.

## Four cases for the signs

As along a line, the sign of $\alpha_z$ on its own does not say whether the object speeds up: what matters is whether it has the same sign as $\omega_z$. Taking $\theta$ to increase anticlockwise, with $\hat k$ pointing out of the plane:

| $\omega_z$ | $\alpha_z$ | Motion | Like a line with |
|---|---|---|---|
| > 0 (anticlockwise) | > 0 | speeding up | $v_x > 0$, $a_x > 0$ |
| > 0 (anticlockwise) | < 0 | slowing down | $v_x > 0$, $a_x < 0$ (braking) |
| < 0 (clockwise) | > 0 | slowing down; if it lasts, stops and turns back | $v_x < 0$, $a_x > 0$ |
| < 0 (clockwise) | < 0 | speeding up | $v_x < 0$, $a_x < 0$ |

The third case is the one that confuses: a **positive** angular acceleration slows down a clockwise turn. On a graph of $\omega_z$ against time, $\alpha_z$ is the slope; a line with positive slope starting below zero rises towards zero (slowing down), crosses it (the object stops for an instant) and continues upwards (now turning anticlockwise). The lecture goes through all four cases on pp. 1–3 of the transcript; the notes list them with the same signs.

In one line (this page's summary of the four cases the notes list in words):

<Formula label="Speeding up or slowing down" tex="\omega_z\,\alpha_z > 0 \ \Rightarrow\ \text{speeding up}, \qquad \omega_z\,\alpha_z < 0 \ \Rightarrow\ \text{slowing down}" cite="reading1" at="the four cases, p. 6-13" />

## From angular acceleration back to angle

Along the circle the motion is, in a sense, one-dimensional: the tangential component can be integrated just as in one-dimensional kinematics, from acceleration to velocity to position. Segment 10.3 works an example in which the tangential acceleration falls off steadily, $a_\theta = A - Bt$ with $A$ and $B$ positive constants, starting with tangential velocity $v_0$. Integrating from 0 to $t$ (with $t'$ as the integration variable) gives the tangential velocity, and integrating once more the arc length $s(t) = r\,(\theta(t) - \theta(0))$ travelled:

<Formula label="Integrating the tangential acceleration" tex="v_\theta(t) = v_0 + \int_0^t (A - Bt')\,dt' = v_0 + At - \tfrac12 Bt^2, \qquad s(t) = v_0 t + \tfrac12 At^2 - \tfrac16 Bt^3" cite="reading4" at="transcript 10.3, p. 1" />

The notes do the same with an angular acceleration that falls linearly to zero (Example 6.3, p. 6-14). In the simplest case, a **constant** $\alpha_z$ (set $B = 0$ and divide by $r$), the same two integrations give this page's familiar forms, starting at $\theta_0$ with $\omega_{z,0}$:

<Formula label="Constant angular acceleration" tex="\omega_z(t) = \omega_{z,0} + \alpha_z t, \qquad \theta(t) = \theta_0 + \omega_{z,0}\,t + \tfrac12\,\alpha_z t^2" cite="reading4" at="method of transcript 10.3, p. 1, with B = 0" />

For the car above, $\theta_0 = 0$ and $\omega_{z,0} = 0$: after 10 s it has turned through $\tfrac12 \times 0.020 \times 10^2 = 1.0$ rad, an arc of $50 \times 1.0 = 50$ m along the track.

MIT's short review page 10.4 collects the results of the week in one list: $\vec\omega = \omega_z\,\hat k$ with $\omega_z = d\theta/dt$, $\vec\alpha = \alpha_z\,\hat k$ with $\alpha_z = d\omega_z/dt$, and

<Formula label="The two components, in review" tex="a_r = -\frac{v^2}{r} = -r\left(\frac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2, \qquad a_\theta = r\,\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = r\,\alpha_z" cite="seg104" at="segment 10.4" />

## Summary

- When $d\theta/dt$ changes, the acceleration on a circle has two parts: $\vec a = r\,\dfrac{d^2\theta}{dt^2}\,\hat\theta - r\left(\dfrac{d\theta}{dt}\right)^2\hat r$, found with the product rule on $\vec v = r\,(d\theta/dt)\,\hat\theta$.
- The tangential component $a_\theta = r\,d^2\theta/dt^2$ changes the speed; the radial component $a_r = -r(d\theta/dt)^2 \le 0$ points inwards and turns the velocity.
- Angular acceleration: $\vec\alpha = (d^2\theta/dt^2)\,\hat k = \alpha_z\,\hat k$, with $\alpha_z = d\omega_z/dt$, in rad/s²; $a_\theta = r\alpha_z$.
- The object speeds up when $\omega_z$ and $\alpha_z$ have the same sign and slows down when the signs differ, just as $v_x$ and $a_x$ do along a line.
- The tangential component integrates like one-dimensional motion: $a_\theta = A - Bt$ gives $v_\theta = v_0 + At - \tfrac12Bt^2$ and $s = v_0t + \tfrac12At^2 - \tfrac16Bt^3$; for constant $\alpha_z$, $\omega_z = \omega_{z,0} + \alpha_z t$ and $\theta = \theta_0 + \omega_{z,0}t + \tfrac12\alpha_z t^2$.
