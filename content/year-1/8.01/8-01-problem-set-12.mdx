This set practises the lessons on [rolling kinematics](/courses/8-01/rolling-kinematics), on [rolling dynamics](/courses/8-01/rolling-dynamics) and on the [kinetic energy and angular momentum of a rolling body](/courses/8-01/rolling-energy-and-angular-momentum). The five problems follow MIT's Problem Set 12, the last set of the course, in our own words. MIT's set comes with drawings; here each situation is described in words, so make your own sketch first. MIT publishes no solutions for this set, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Two remarks. MIT's course notes work the bead of Problem 1 as Example 20.1, with a different origin, and the lesson on rolling kinematics quotes their result. Try the problem before you look there. Problems 3 and 5 also use the conservation of angular momentum about a point, from the lesson on [torque and angular impulse](/courses/8-01/torque-and-angular-impulse), and Problem 3 uses the parallel axis theorem of the lesson on the [moment of inertia](/courses/8-01/moment-of-inertia). All five problems count for the score (60 points).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first: sketch the body, mark its centre of mass and the point where it touches the surface, and decide which way you count positions and rotations as positive. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A formula box takes plain typing, for example `x0 + V*t`, `3*v0/(4*s)` or `m*g*sin(phi)/3`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type the names as the box shows them: `x0` for $x_0$, `v0` for $v_0$, `omega` for $\omega$, `Omega` for the capital $\Omega$, `omega0` for $\omega_0$, `mu` for $\mu_s$ and `phi` for $\phi$. Capital and small letters are different names: `V` is not `v`.

MIT's set gives no points. The points here, and their split between parts, are ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A bead on a bicycle wheel" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A bicycle wheel of radius $R$ rolls without slipping along a level road. Its centre moves at the constant speed $V$ in the positive $x$ direction. (MIT writes "the center of mass of the bicycle". Its figure shows the wheel alone, with the centre of the wheel marked.) A bead is stuck on the rim of the wheel.

Use axes fixed to the ground: $x$ along the road and $y$ upwards, with the road at $y = 0$. At $t = 0$ the bead is at the top of the wheel, at $x = x_0$ and $y = 2R$. In MIT's figure the wheel moves to the right, and the bead is shown a little later, turned clockwise away from the top by an angle $\theta$.

MIT asks for both components of the position in one question. We have made them two parts.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the $x$ component of the position of the bead as a function of time, for an observer on the ground. Use any of $x_0$, $V$, $t$ and $R$ that you need.

<Hint>

The lesson on rolling kinematics splits the motion in two: the motion of the centre of the wheel, and the motion of the bead around the centre. Write the position of each.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Seen from the centre, the bead moves on a circle of radius $R$ at a steady rate. How fast does the angle $\theta$ grow if the wheel rolls without slipping?

</Hint>

<Hint>

At $t = 0$ the bead is straight above the centre. A wheel rolling to the right turns clockwise, so a moment later the bead is ahead of the centre. Is its distance ahead $R\sin\theta$ or $R\cos\theta$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$x(t) =$" expr="x0 + V*t + R*sin(V*t/R)"
  vars={{ x0: [1, 5], V: [1, 5], t: [0.1, 2], R: [0.2, 1] }}
  names={{ x0: "x_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x0 + V*t", hint: "That is the position of the centre of the wheel. The bead also goes around the centre." },
    { expr: "V*t + R*sin(V*t/R)", hint: "This starts the bead at x = 0. Here it starts at x = x0." },
    { expr: "x0 + V*t - R*sin(V*t/R)", hint: "This turns the wheel the wrong way. A wheel that rolls in the +x direction carries the bead forward from the top, so just after t = 0 the bead is ahead of the centre." },
    { expr: "x0 + V*t + R*cos(V*t/R)", hint: "At t = 0 this puts the bead a distance R ahead of the centre. It starts straight above the centre, so the distance ahead must start at zero." },
    { expr: "x0 + R*sin(V*t/R)", hint: "This is the motion around the centre only. The centre itself moves on with the speed V." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The position of the bead is the position of the centre of the wheel plus the position of the bead as seen from the centre. The centre moves in a straight line at a steady speed, and around it the bead moves on a circle at a steady rate.

<Step title="The centre of the wheel">

At $t = 0$ the bead is at the top, straight above the centre, so the centre starts at $x = x_0$. It stays at the height $R$ and moves with the speed $V$. So at the time $t$ the centre is at $x = x_0 + Vt$, $y = R$.

</Step>

<Step title="The angle">

Let $\theta$ be the angle between the upward direction and the line from the centre to the bead, counted clockwise, so that $\theta = 0$ at $t = 0$. The wheel rolls without slipping, and for that the notes give

<Formula label="Rolling without slipping" tex="V_\text{cm} = R\,\omega" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (20.2.13), p. 20-5" />

Here $V_\text{cm}$ is the speed of the centre, our $V$, and $\omega$ is the angular speed of the wheel (the notes write it with a subscript cm). So the wheel turns at the steady rate $V/R$, and $\theta = Vt/R$.

</Step>

<Step title="Add the two positions">

Seen from the centre, the bead is at the distance $R$ in the direction $\theta$ from the vertical: $R\sin\theta$ ahead of the centre and $R\cos\theta$ above it. The notes add the two positions for a bead at any distance $b$ from the centre, for a wheel whose centre starts at $x = 0$:

<Formula label="Bead on a wheel, from the notes" tex="x_b(t) = V_\text{cm}t + b\sin\!\left(\frac{V_\text{cm}t}{R}\right), \qquad y_b(t) = R + b\cos\!\left(\frac{V_\text{cm}t}{R}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Example 20.1, Eqs. (20.2.30) and (20.2.31), p. 20-8" />

With the bead on the rim ($b = R$) and the start at $x_0$:

<Formula label="Bead: the x component" tex="x(t) = x_0 + Vt + R\sin\!\left(\frac{Vt}{R}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

All three terms are lengths, and $Vt/R$ is a pure number, as the argument of a sine must be. At $t = 0$ the result is $x_0$. The velocity is the derivative, $V + V\cos(Vt/R)$. At the top ($t = 0$) it is $2V$, twice the speed of the centre. Half a turn later, at $t = \pi R/V$, the bead touches the road and its velocity is $V - V = 0$: the point of contact is at rest for a moment, as it must be for rolling without slipping. With our own numbers, $R = 0.30$ m, $V = 4.0$ m/s and $x_0 = 1.0$ m: one full turn takes $2\pi R/V = 0.471$ s, and in that time the bead moves on by $2\pi R = 1.88$ m, the circumference of the wheel, to $x = 2.88$ m.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the $y$ component of the position of the bead as a function of time. Use any of $x_0$, $V$, $t$ and $R$ that you need.

<Hint>

The centre of the wheel stays at one height. Which one?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Add the height of the bead above the centre. It is $R$ at $t = 0$ and $-R$ half a turn later.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$y(t) =$" expr="R + R*cos(V*t/R)"
  vars={{ V: [1, 5], t: [0.1, 2], R: [0.2, 1] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "R*cos(V*t/R)", hint: "That is the height of the bead above the centre of the wheel. The centre is at the height R above the road." },
    { expr: "R - R*cos(V*t/R)", hint: "This starts the bead on the road, at y = 0. It starts at the top of the wheel, at y = 2R." },
    { expr: "R + R*sin(V*t/R)", hint: "At t = 0 this gives y = R, the height of the centre. The bead starts at the top, a distance R above the centre." },
    { expr: "2*R*cos(V*t/R)", hint: "This goes down to -2R, below the road. The bead moves between y = 0 and y = 2R: the height R of the centre, plus or minus at most R." },
    { expr: "2*R", hint: "That is the height at t = 0 only. The bead goes around with the wheel." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same sum as in part (a), now for the vertical direction: the height of the centre plus the height of the bead above the centre.

<Step title="Add the two heights">

The centre stays at the height $R$. The bead is $R\cos\theta$ above it, with $\theta = Vt/R$ as in part (a):

<Formula label="Bead: the y component" tex="y(t) = R + R\cos\!\left(\frac{Vt}{R}\right) = R\left(1 + \cos\frac{Vt}{R}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Example 20.1, Eq. (20.2.39), p. 20-9" />

(The notes write the same result with $V_\text{cm}$ for our $V$.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ this is $2R$, the top of the wheel. Half a turn later, at $t = \pi R/V$, the cosine is $-1$ and $y = 0$: the bead is on the road. It never goes below the road or above $2R$. The start $x_0$ does not appear, because moving the origin along the road changes no height. Together, parts (a) and (b) describe a curve called a cycloid, which the lesson on rolling kinematics draws.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A ball rolling inside a turning ring" points={8} mit="Problem 2">

A ring of radius $R$ turns about its own centre, which stays in place, with the angular speed $\Omega$. A ball of radius $b$ (smaller than $R$) and mass $M$ rolls without slipping on the inside surface of the ring. The ball spins about its own centre with the angular speed $\omega$.

MIT's figure is a hand drawing of the lower part of the ring, with the ball touching it from the inside. We read the directions from the curved arrows in that drawing: the ring turns counterclockwise, the ball spins clockwise, and the centre of the ball moves along the ring in the same direction as the part of the ring it touches. Both angular speeds are meant as an observer who does not turn with the ring measures them. (MIT's text does not say this. It is the usual meaning of an angular speed in the course.)

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the speed $v_\text{cm}$ of the centre of mass of the ball. Use any of $b$, $R$, $\omega$ and $\Omega$ that you need.

<Hint>

Rolling without slipping means that the two surfaces do not slide over each other. On a road at rest, the point of the wheel that touches the road is at rest. What is the matching statement when the surface itself moves?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Find the velocity of the point of the ring that the ball touches. Then write the velocity of the lowest point of the ball as the velocity of its centre plus the velocity from its spin.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Think of the moment the ball is at the bottom of the ring. The ring moves to the right there. Which way does a clockwise spin carry the bottom point of the ball, as seen from the centre of the ball?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$v_\text{cm} =$" expr="R*Omega + b*omega"
  vars={{ R: [1, 3], b: [0.1, 0.5], Omega: [1, 5], omega: [2, 10] }}
  names={{ Omega: "\\Omega", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "b*omega", hint: "That is the speed of a ball that rolls on a ring at rest. This ring turns, so the surface under the ball moves." },
    { expr: "R*Omega", hint: "That is the speed of the surface of the ring. A ball with this speed and a spin would slide over the ring: the spin moves its contact point relative to its centre." },
    { expr: "R*Omega - b*omega", hint: "This has the ball spinning the other way. In MIT's figure the ring turns counterclockwise and the ball spins clockwise, so at the contact point the spin carries the ball's surface backwards relative to its centre." },
    { expr: "(R - b)*Omega + b*omega", hint: "The ring touches the ball at the distance R from the centre of the ring, not at R minus b. Use the speed of the ring's surface." },
    { expr: "(R - b)*Omega", hint: "That is the speed of a ball that is fixed to the ring and carried around with it. This ball rolls on the ring with its own spin." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two surfaces that roll on each other without slipping have the same velocity at the point where they touch. One side of this condition is the velocity of the ring at the contact point. The other is the velocity of the ball there, which is the velocity of its centre plus the velocity from its spin.

<Step title="The ring at the contact point">

The contact point is on the inside surface of the ring, at the distance $R$ from the centre of the ring. The ring turns with the angular speed $\Omega$, so this point moves along the ring with the speed $R\,\Omega$. Take the direction in which it moves as positive.

</Step>

<Step title="The ball at the contact point">

The notes write the velocity of a point $P$ of a rolling body as the sum of two velocities:

<Formula label="Law of addition of velocities" tex="\vec v_P = \vec v'_P + \vec V_\text{cm}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (20.2.1), p. 20-2" />

Here $\vec v'_P$ is the velocity of the point as seen from the centre of mass. Picture the ball at the bottom of the ring, where the ring moves to the right. The contact point is then the lowest point of the ball. A clockwise spin moves the lowest point of a body to the left with the speed $b\,\omega$, so along the positive direction $v'_P = -b\,\omega$. The centre moves in the positive direction with the speed $v_\text{cm}$. So the contact point of the ball has the velocity $v_\text{cm} - b\,\omega$.

</Step>

<Step title="No slipping">

Set the two velocities equal:

<Formula label="Ring: rolling without slipping on a moving surface" tex="v_\text{cm} - b\,\omega = R\,\Omega \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_\text{cm} = R\,\Omega + b\,\omega" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

The mass $M$ is not needed.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both terms are a length times an angular speed, which is a speed. For a ring at rest ($\Omega = 0$) the result is $b\,\omega$, the rolling condition of Problem 1 for a ball of radius $b$. For a ball that does not spin ($\omega = 0$) it is $R\,\Omega$: the ball's surface is carried along with the ring.

A second route gives the same answer. Watch from a frame that turns with the ring. In that frame the ring is at rest, and the clockwise spin of the ball is larger, $\omega + \Omega$, because the frame itself turns counterclockwise. So in that frame the centre of the ball has the speed $b\,(\omega + \Omega)$. Back in the frame at rest, a point at the distance $R - b$ from the centre of the ring gains the speed $(R - b)\,\Omega$ of the turning frame, and $b\,(\omega + \Omega) + (R - b)\,\Omega = R\,\Omega + b\,\omega$.

With our own numbers, $R = 0.50$ m, $b = 0.050$ m, $\Omega = 2.0$ rad/s and $\omega = 12$ rad/s: $v_\text{cm} = 0.50 \times 2.0 + 0.050 \times 12 = 1.6$ m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

This part is our addition. The centre of the ball moves on a circle around the centre of the ring. With which angular speed $\Omega_b$ does it go around? Use any of $b$, $R$, $\omega$ and $\Omega$ that you need.

<Hint>

What is the radius of the circle on which the centre of the ball moves? The ball is inside the ring.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$\Omega_b =$" expr="(R*Omega + b*omega)/(R - b)"
  vars={{ R: [1, 3], b: [0.1, 0.5], Omega: [1, 5], omega: [2, 10] }}
  names={{ Omega: "\\Omega", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(R*Omega + b*omega)/R", hint: "The centre of the ball is not on the ring. It is one ball radius closer to the centre of the ring." },
    { expr: "(R*Omega + b*omega)/(R + b)", hint: "This is for a ball that rolls on the outside of the ring. Inside the ring, the centre of the ball is at the distance R minus b." },
    { expr: "Omega", hint: "That holds only for a ball that is carried around rigidly with the ring. Divide the speed of part (a) by the radius of the circle on which the centre moves." },
    { expr: "R*Omega + b*omega", hint: "That is the speed of part (a). Divide it by the radius of the circle on which the centre of the ball moves." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A point that moves with the speed $v$ on a circle of radius $r$ goes around with the angular speed $v/r$.

<Step title="The circle of the centre">

The ball touches the ring from the inside, so its centre is at the distance $R - b$ from the centre of the ring:

<Formula label="Ring: angular speed of the ball's centre" tex="\Omega_b = \frac{v_\text{cm}}{R - b} = \frac{R\,\Omega + b\,\omega}{R - b}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our addition, our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A speed divided by a length is an angular speed. For a ball that does not roll on the ring at all, but is carried around with it as if glued on, every point of the ball turns around the ring's centre with $\Omega$. Such a ball spins counterclockwise with $\Omega$, which is $\omega = -\Omega$ in our way of counting, and the result is then $(R - b)\,\Omega/(R - b) = \Omega$, as it must be. With the numbers of part (a): $\Omega_b = 1.6/0.45 = 3.56$ rad/s, faster than the ring.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A cube hits a low ridge" points={14} mit="Problem 3">

A uniform cube of mass $m$ and edge length $s$ slides without friction on a level surface with the speed $v_0$. (MIT's text calls the speed $v$ at first and $v_0$ in its questions. We write $v_0$ everywhere.) Its leading bottom edge hits a low ridge on the surface. The collision is inelastic and takes no time. After it, the cube turns about the ridge as about a hinge without friction. MIT's figure shows the cube before the collision, flat on the surface and moving towards the ridge, and after it, tilted and turning about the edge that rests on the ridge.

The moment of inertia of a cube about an axis through its centre of mass, at right angles to two of its faces, is $ms^2/6$. The gravitational acceleration is $g$, downwards.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Find the angular speed $\omega_a$ of the cube just after the collision. Use any of $m$, $s$, $v_0$ and $g$ that you need.

<Hint>

The ridge stops the edge with a large force during a very short time. Mechanical energy is not conserved in an inelastic collision, and momentum is not either, because the ridge pushes on the cube. Is there a point about which the force of the ridge has no torque?

</Hint>

<Hint>

About the ridge, the angular momentum of the cube is the same just before and just after the collision. Before, the cube does not rotate: use the angular momentum of a body that moves in a straight line, with the mass at the centre of mass. How high is the centre above the ridge?

</Hint>

<Hint>

After the collision the cube rotates about the ridge. You need its moment of inertia about that edge: use the parallel axis theorem. How far is the edge from the centre of the cube?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$\omega_a =$" expr="3*v0/(4*s)"
  vars={{ v0: [0.5, 3], s: [0.1, 1] }}
  names={{ v0: "v_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*v0/s", hint: "This uses the moment of inertia about the centre of the cube. The cube turns about the ridge, which is an edge: add m times the square of the distance from the centre to that edge." },
    { expr: "6*v0/(5*s)", hint: "This takes the distance from the centre to the edge as s/2. The edge is at a corner of the square face: the distance is half the diagonal of the face." },
    { expr: "sqrt(3/2)*v0/s", hint: "This keeps the kinetic energy through the collision. The collision is inelastic, so kinetic energy is lost: use the angular momentum about the ridge." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2)*v0/s", hint: "This lets the centre of the cube keep its speed v0. The ridge gives the cube a sudden push, so the speed of the centre changes in the collision." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** During the collision the ridge pushes on the cube with a large force, which acts at the ridge. About the ridge this force has no lever arm and so no torque. Gravity and the normal force of the surface stay of ordinary size, and in a collision that takes no time they give no angular impulse. So the angular momentum of the cube about the ridge is the same just before and just after.

<Formula label="Conservation of angular momentum" tex="\left(\vec L_S^{\,\text{sys}}\right)_f = \left(\vec L_S^{\,\text{sys}}\right)_i" cite="reading4" at="Eq. (19.5.36), p. 19-21" />

<Step title="Before the collision">

The cube slides without rotating, so its angular momentum about the ridge is that of its whole mass moving with the centre of mass. The centre is at the height $s/2$ above the surface, and the ridge is low, so we take it at the level of the surface. The lever arm of the momentum $mv_0$ is then $s/2$:

<Formula label="Cube: angular momentum before the collision" tex="L_i = \frac{m\,v_0\,s}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

Its sense is the sense in which the cube is going to tip over the ridge.

</Step>

<Step title="The moment of inertia about the ridge">

After the collision the cube rotates about the edge on the ridge. That edge is parallel to the axis through the centre for which the moment of inertia is given. The notes give the parallel axis theorem:

<Formula label="Parallel axis theorem" tex="I_S = I_\text{cm} + m\,d^2" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (16.2.16), p. 16-10" />

Here $d$ is the distance between the axis through the centre of mass and the parallel axis through $S$ (the notes give it subscripts). Seen from the side the cube is a square of side $s$, with the edge at a corner and the centre in the middle. The distance between them is half the diagonal, $d = s/\sqrt2$, so

<Formula label="Cube: moment of inertia about an edge" tex="I_S = \frac{m s^2}{6} + \frac{m s^2}{2} = \frac{2\,m s^2}{3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="After the collision">

The cube now rotates about a fixed axis, so its angular momentum about the ridge is $I_S\,\omega_a$. Setting it equal to $L_i$:

<Formula label="Cube: angular speed just after the collision" tex="\frac{2\,m s^2}{3}\,\omega_a = \frac{m\,v_0\,s}{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega_a = \frac{3\,v_0}{4\,s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A speed divided by a length is an angular speed. Neither $m$ nor $g$ appears: the mass cancels, and gravity has no time to act. Just after the collision the centre moves on a circle of radius $s/\sqrt2$ about the ridge, with the speed $\omega_a s/\sqrt2 = 3v_0/(4\sqrt2) = 0.53\,v_0$, so it has been slowed down. The kinetic energy before is $\tfrac12 m v_0^2$, and after it is $\tfrac12 I_S\,\omega_a^2 = \tfrac{3}{16} m v_0^2$. Only three eighths of the kinetic energy is left, which fits an inelastic collision.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

If the cube is fast enough, it tips right over the ridge and ends up lying on the other side. Find the largest value of $v_0$ for which this does not happen. Use any of $m$, $s$ and $g$ that you need.

<Hint>

After the collision the hinge has no friction, so mechanical energy is conserved while the cube turns about the ridge. The collision itself is not part of this stretch: start just after it, with the angular speed of part (a).

</Hint>

<Hint>

As the cube tips, its centre of mass rises. In which position of the cube is the centre highest, and how high is it then? Beyond that position the cube falls over to the other side.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the limiting case the cube just comes to rest in that highest position. Set the kinetic energy just after the collision equal to the gain in potential energy.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="largest $v_0 =$" expr="sqrt(8*g*s*(sqrt(2) - 1)/3)"
  vars={{ s: [0.1, 1], g: [9, 10] }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "sqrt(8*sqrt(2)*g*s/3)", hint: "This counts the full height of the centre in the highest position. The centre starts at the height s/2, so only the rise counts." },
    { expr: "8*g*s*(sqrt(2) - 1)/3", hint: "That has the unit of a speed squared. Take the square root." },
    { expr: "sqrt(4*g*s*(sqrt(2) - 1)/3)", hint: "Check the factors of one half: the kinetic energy has one, and the rise of the centre is s/√2 minus s/2, which is s(√2 − 1)/2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** After the collision only gravity does work on the cube, because the hinge has no friction and its force acts at a point that does not move. So the mechanical energy is conserved from just after the collision onwards. The cube tips over if its centre of mass gets past its highest possible position, straight above the ridge.

<Step title="The two states">

Initial state, just after the collision: the cube still lies flat, its centre is at the height $s/2$, and it rotates about the ridge with $\omega_a$. For a body that rotates about a fixed axis through $S$, the notes give the kinetic energy $\tfrac12 I_S\,\omega^2$:

<Formula label="Cube: kinetic energy just after the collision" tex="K_i = \tfrac12 I_S\,\omega_a^2 = \tfrac12\cdot\frac{2\,m s^2}{3}\cdot\frac{9\,v_0^2}{16\,s^2} = \frac{3}{16}\,m v_0^2" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (16.3.2), p. 16-12, with our values" />

Final state, in the limiting case: the cube stands on its edge with the centre straight above the ridge, at the height $s/\sqrt2$, and has just come to rest.

</Step>

<Step title="Energy is conserved">

The centre rises by $s/\sqrt2 - s/2 = \tfrac12 s\,(\sqrt2 - 1)$:

<Formula label="Cube: energy from the collision to the highest position" tex="\frac{3}{16}\,m v_0^2 = \tfrac12\,m g s\,(\sqrt2 - 1)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

<Formula label="Cube: the largest speed that does not tip it over" tex="v_0 = \sqrt{\frac{8\,g s\,(\sqrt2 - 1)}{3}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

A slower cube tips up, stops before its centre is above the ridge and falls back. A faster one goes over. (At exactly this speed the cube would end balanced on its edge, so strictly this value is the border between the two cases.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$gs$ is an acceleration times a length, a speed squared. The mass cancels. A larger cube needs a larger speed, as expected: its centre has further to rise. With our own numbers, $s = 0.20$ m and $g = 9.8$ m/s²: $v_0 = \sqrt{8 \times 9.8 \times 0.20 \times 0.414/3} = 1.47$ m/s, and the cube then starts to turn with $\omega_a = 3v_0/(4s) = 5.52$ rad/s (from the unrounded speed). The centre rises by only $0.207 \times 0.20 = 0.041$ m, about 4 cm.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A yo-yo pulled up an inclined plane" points={14} mit="Problem 4">

A yo-yo of mass $m$ has a spool of radius $R$ and an axle of radius $R/3$. Its moment of inertia about the axis through its centre is taken as $I_0 = \tfrac12 mR^2$. The yo-yo stands upright on a plane that is inclined at the angle $\phi$ to the horizontal: it rests on the rim of its spool, and its axis is horizontal and runs across the slope (in MIT's side view, at right angles to the page). The gravitational acceleration is $g$, downwards, and the coefficient of static friction between the yo-yo and the plane is $\mu_s$.

The string is pulled with a force of magnitude $F$. In MIT's figure the string leaves the axle on the side that faces the plane, a distance $R/3$ from the centre, and runs parallel to the plane. The force points up the slope.

MIT asks only for the largest pull. Part (a) is a step on the way, which we have made a part of its own.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

The yo-yo rolls without slipping. Let $f$ be the component along the slope of the static friction force on the yo-yo, counted as positive when it points up the slope. Find $f$, using any of $F$, $\phi$, $m$, $R$ and $g$ that you need.

<Hint>

The lesson on rolling dynamics writes two equations for a rolling body: one for the motion of the centre of mass and one for the rotation about the centre of mass. A third one ties the two motions together.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Three forces have a part along the slope: the pull, gravity and friction. Two forces have a torque about the centre: the pull, with the lever arm $R/3$, and friction, with the lever arm $R$. Gravity and the normal force have none.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Mind the senses of rotation. Both the pull and a friction force up the slope act on the side of the centre that faces the plane. Do they turn the yo-yo in the sense of rolling up the slope, or of rolling down?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$f =$" expr="m*g*sin(phi)/3 - 5*F/9"
  vars={{ m: [0.1, 2], g: [9, 10], phi: [0.1, 0.6], F: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ phi: "\\phi" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "5*F/9 - m*g*sin(phi)/3", hint: "This is the right size with the other sign: it counts friction down the slope as positive. The question counts f as positive up the slope." },
    { expr: "m*g*sin(phi)/3 - F/9", hint: "This is for a string that leaves the axle on the side away from the plane. In MIT's figure it leaves on the side that faces the plane, so about the centre the pull turns the yo-yo in the sense of rolling down the slope." },
    { expr: "(m*g*sin(phi) - F)/3", hint: "This leaves out the torque of the pull about the centre. The string acts at the distance R/3 from the centre." },
    { expr: "m*g*sin(phi) - F", hint: "This is the friction that would hold the centre of the yo-yo at rest or at a steady speed. The yo-yo accelerates: write the second law for the centre and the torque equation for the spin." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A rolling body obeys two equations: the external force moves the centre of mass, and the external torque about the centre of mass changes the spin. Rolling without slipping ties the two accelerations together. The three equations give the acceleration and the friction force.

<Formula label="Translation and rotation" tex="\vec F^{\,\text{ext}} = m\vec A_\text{cm}, \qquad \tau^{\,\text{ext}}_{\text{cm},z} = I_\text{cm}\,\alpha_z" cite="reading2" at="Eqs. (21.4.1) and (21.4.4), pp. 21-6 and 21-7" />

<Step title="Directions">

Take the $x$ axis up the slope, and let $a$ be the $x$ component of the acceleration of the centre. Count the angular acceleration $\alpha$ as positive in the sense of rolling up the slope. Then rolling without slipping means $a = R\alpha$.

</Step>

<Step title="The centre of mass">

Along the slope act the pull $F$, the friction $f$ and the part of gravity along the slope, $mg\sin\phi$, which points down the slope:

<Formula label="Yo-yo: the centre of mass" tex="F + f - mg\sin\phi = m a" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

At right angles to the slope nothing accelerates, and the pull has no part in that direction, so the normal force is $N = mg\cos\phi$.

</Step>

<Step title="The rotation about the centre">

Gravity acts at the centre and the normal force points through it, so neither has a torque about the centre. The pull acts on the axle a distance $R/3$ from the centre, on the side that faces the plane, and points up the slope. The friction acts at the contact point, a distance $R$ from the centre on the same side. A force up the slope that acts on that side of the centre turns the yo-yo in the sense of rolling down the slope (think of the yo-yo at rest with a push up the slope on its lowest point: that point moves up the slope, as it does on a body that turns in the sense of rolling down). So both torques are negative in our way of counting:

<Formula label="Yo-yo: the rotation about the centre" tex="-\frac{R}{3}\,F - R\,f = \tfrac12 m R^2\,\alpha = \tfrac12 m R\,a" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

The second equation gives $f = -\tfrac13 F - \tfrac12 ma$. Put this into the first: $\tfrac23 F - mg\sin\phi = \tfrac32 ma$, so

<Formula label="Yo-yo: the acceleration" tex="a = \frac{4F}{9m} - \tfrac23\,g\sin\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

and then

<Formula label="Yo-yo: the friction force" tex="f = \tfrac13\,mg\sin\phi - \tfrac59\,F" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

The radius $R$ has dropped out.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both terms are forces. With no pull ($F = 0$) the yo-yo rolls down the slope with $a = -\tfrac23 g\sin\phi$, and the friction is $\tfrac13 mg\sin\phi$ up the slope. This agrees with the lesson on rolling dynamics: its formula for the acceleration down an incline, with $I_\text{cm} = \tfrac12 mR^2$, gives $\tfrac23 g\sin\phi$. A stronger pull makes $f$ smaller, and for $F > \tfrac35 mg\sin\phi$ it is negative: the friction then points down the slope.

A second route: take torques about the contact point. For a body that rolls without slipping this point is at rest for a moment, and friction and the normal force have no torque about it. The pull has the lever arm $R - R/3 = \tfrac23 R$ and gravity the lever arm $R\sin\phi$, and the moment of inertia about the contact point is $\tfrac12 mR^2 + mR^2 = \tfrac32 mR^2$. So $\tfrac23 RF - mgR\sin\phi = \tfrac32 mR^2\,(a/R)$, the same acceleration.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

What is the largest pull $F$ for which the yo-yo rolls without slipping? Use any of $\mu_s$, $\phi$, $m$, $R$ and $g$ that you need.

<Hint>

Static friction cannot be larger than $\mu_s$ times the normal force. What is the normal force on a slope?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Look at the result of part (a). As the pull grows, in which direction does the friction force point, and how does its size change?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="largest $F =$" expr="3*m*g*(3*mu*cos(phi) + sin(phi))/5"
  vars={{ m: [0.1, 2], g: [9, 10], phi: [0.1, 0.6], mu: [0.2, 0.9] }}
  names={{ phi: "\\phi", mu: "\\mu_s" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "9*mu*m*g/5", hint: "That is the result for level ground. On the slope the normal force is smaller, and gravity pulls along the slope as well." },
    { expr: "3*m*g*(3*mu + sin(phi))/5", hint: "This takes the normal force as mg. On a slope it is mg cos φ." },
    { expr: "m*g*(3*mu*cos(phi) + sin(phi))", hint: "This leaves out the torque of the pull about the centre. The string acts at the distance R/3 from the centre." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The yo-yo rolls without slipping as long as the friction it needs is not larger than static friction can give, $\mu_s N$. Part (a) tells how much friction is needed for a given pull.

<Step title="The limit of static friction">

With $N = mg\cos\phi$ from part (a), the condition is $|f| \le \mu_s\,mg\cos\phi$. By part (a), $f$ falls steadily as $F$ grows. For a strong pull it is negative (down the slope) and its size is $\tfrac59 F - \tfrac13 mg\sin\phi$. The largest pull is the one for which this size reaches the limit:

<Formula label="Yo-yo: friction at its limit" tex="\tfrac59\,F - \tfrac13\,mg\sin\phi = \mu_s\,mg\cos\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

<Formula label="Yo-yo: the largest pull" tex="F = \tfrac35\,mg\,\left(3\mu_s\cos\phi + \sin\phi\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is $mg$ times a pure number, a force. More friction allows a stronger pull. On level ground ($\phi = 0$) it becomes $\tfrac95\mu_s mg$. The lesson on rolling dynamics has this case, a yo-yo on the ground pulled by a string from the bottom of its axle: its formula "Maximum pull", with the axle radius $R/3$ and $I_\text{cm} = \tfrac12 mR^2$, gives $\mu_s mg\,(R + \tfrac12 R)/(\tfrac13 R + \tfrac12 R) = \tfrac95\mu_s mg$ as well.

With our own numbers, $m = 0.10$ kg, $\phi = 20°$, $\mu_s = 0.30$ and $g = 9.8$ m/s²: the largest pull is $0.698$ N, a little less than the weight of the yo-yo, 0.98 N. At that pull the friction is 0.276 N down the slope, equal to $\mu_s N$, and the yo-yo rolls up the slope with $a = 0.87$ m/s².

A remark of our own, beyond MIT's question: there is a smallest pull too. For a weak pull the friction points up the slope, and it must not be larger than $\mu_s N$ in that direction either, which needs $F \ge \tfrac35 mg\,(\sin\phi - 3\mu_s\cos\phi)$. This matters only on a steep slope, with $\tan\phi > 3\mu_s$, where a yo-yo left to itself would slip.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A billiard ball struck above its centre" points={14} mit="Problem 5">

A billiard ball is a uniform sphere of mass $m$ and radius $R$, with the moment of inertia $I_\text{cm} = \tfrac25 mR^2$ about its centre. The ball is at rest on a table. A cue stick gives it a short, sharp, horizontal blow. In MIT's figure the cue is level and hits the ball at a height $h$ above the level of the centre of the ball. The height $h$ is not known.

The coefficient of sliding friction between the ball and the table is $\mu_k$. The force of the blow is so much larger than the friction that the friction may be left out while the blow lasts. Just after the blow, the centre of the ball has the given speed $v_0$ and the ball spins with an angular speed $\omega_0$ that is not known. Because of this spin the ball slides over the table at first. Its centre speeds up, and when the ball has started to roll without slipping, its speed is $\tfrac97 v_0$.

MIT asks only for the ratio $h/R$. Parts (a) and (b) are steps on the way, which we have made parts of their own.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the angular speed $\omega_0$ just after the blow, using any of $v_0$, $h$, $R$ and $m$ that you need.

<Hint>

The blow is an impulse: a large force during a short time. It changes the momentum of the ball, and because it acts off-centre, it also changes the angular momentum about the centre of the ball.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Call the impulse of the blow $J$. Write the momentum of the ball after the blow with $J$. Then write the angular momentum about the centre with $J$ and its lever arm, and take $J$ out of the two equations.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$\omega_0 =$" expr="5*v0*h/(2*R^2)"
  vars={{ v0: [0.5, 3], h: [0.005, 0.025], R: [0.026, 0.03] }}
  names={{ v0: "v_0" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "2*v0*h/(5*R^2)", hint: "Check where the moment of inertia goes: the angular momentum of the blow is divided by (2/5)mR², which puts 5/2 in front." },
    { expr: "v0*h/R^2", hint: "This uses the moment of inertia mR², which is that of a thin ring. For the uniform ball it is (2/5)mR²." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** During the blow only the cue matters. Its impulse gives the ball its momentum, and the angular impulse of the same blow about the centre gives the ball its spin.

<Step title="Momentum and angular momentum">

Let $J$ be the impulse of the blow, the force of the cue summed over the short time it lasts. The ball starts at rest, so its momentum after the blow is $mv_0 = J$. The blow is horizontal and acts on a line that passes the centre at the distance $h$, so its angular impulse about the centre is $hJ$. This is the spin angular momentum after the blow:

<Formula label="Billiard ball: the blow" tex="m\,v_0 = J, \qquad I_\text{cm}\,\omega_0 = h\,J" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

Divide the second equation by the first and use $I_\text{cm} = \tfrac25 mR^2$:

<Formula label="Billiard ball: spin after the blow" tex="\omega_0 = \frac{m\,v_0\,h}{I_\text{cm}} = \frac{5\,v_0\,h}{2R^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

The cue hits above the centre, so the top of the ball spins forwards.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A speed times a length over a length squared is an angular speed. The mass cancels. A blow through the centre ($h = 0$) gives no spin. A ball that rolls without slipping from the start needs $\omega_0 = v_0/R$, which this formula gives for $h = \tfrac25 R$. A higher blow makes the ball spin faster than rolling asks for.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

After the blow, sliding friction acts until the ball rolls without slipping. Find the speed $v_f$ of the centre of the ball from then on, in terms of $v_0$, $\omega_0$ and $R$ (and $m$ and $\mu_k$ if you need them). Take the spin of part (a): the top of the ball moves forwards.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Take a fixed point on the table, on the line along which the ball moves. The friction force points along that line. Gravity and the normal force cancel each other. So the angular momentum about that point does not change.

<Solution>

About a fixed point on the table, on the line of motion, it has no torque, and the torques of gravity and the normal force cancel. So the angular momentum about such a point stays the same. The notes use this for a bowling ball in Example 21.5.

<Step title="Orbital plus spin">

The angular momentum about a point $S$ is the sum of two parts:

<Formula label="Orbital plus spin angular momentum" tex="\vec L_S^{\,\text{total}} = \vec L_S^{\,\text{orbital}} + \vec L_\text{cm}^{\,\text{spin}}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (20.3.15), p. 20-15" />

The orbital part is that of the whole mass moving with the centre of mass. The centre is at the height $R$ above the table, so for a point $S$ on the table it is $mRv$. The spin part is $I_\text{cm}\,\omega$. For a ball that moves forwards while its top spins forwards, both have the same sense.

</Step>

<Step title="Before and after">

Just after the blow the angular momentum about $S$ is $mRv_0 + I_\text{cm}\,\omega_0$. When the ball rolls without slipping, $\omega = v_f/R$, and it is $mRv_f + \tfrac25 mR^2\,(v_f/R) = \tfrac75\,mRv_f$, as in the notes:

<Formula label="Rolling ball: angular momentum about a point on the table" tex="L_{S,f} = \tfrac75\,m R\,v_f" cite="reading2" at="Example 21.5, Eq. (21.6.68), p. 21-23" />

Set the two equal:

<Formula label="Billiard ball: angular momentum about a point on the table" tex="m R\,v_0 + \tfrac25\,m R^2\,\omega_0 = \tfrac75\,m R\,v_f" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Billiard ball: speed when it rolls" tex="v_f = \frac{5\,v_0 + 2R\,\omega_0}{7}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

Both terms in the numerator are speeds. Neither $m$ nor $\mu_k$ appears: the friction decides how long the sliding lasts, not how it ends. With no spin at the start the result is $\tfrac57 v_0$, the bowling ball of the notes (Eq. (21.6.70), p. 21-23). With $\omega_0 = v_0/R$ the ball rolls from the start and the result is $v_0$: nothing changes. With a faster spin, $R\omega_0 > v_0$, the bottom of the ball slides backwards over the table, friction pushes the ball forwards, and $v_f$ is larger than $v_0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="c" points={5}>
