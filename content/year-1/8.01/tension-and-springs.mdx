Lesson 6 dealt with surfaces that push. MIT's Lesson 7 turns to the two other everyday ways of exerting a force through contact: a **rope** that pulls, and a **spring** that both pulls and pushes. Both lead to the same habit of thought: look inside the object, cut it in imagination, and apply Newton's laws to the pieces.

## Pushing, pulling and tension

You can push a block with a stick, or pull it with a string. A string can only do the second: try to push with it and it goes slack. So what is the force that a string transmits?

Think of a tug of war. Two teams pull on the ends of a rope in opposite directions. Now make an **imaginary cut** through the rope at some point $P$, a distance $x_P$ from one end. The piece on the left and the piece on the right pull on each other. The force of the right piece on the left piece, $\vec F_{R,L}$, and the force of the left piece on the right piece, $\vec F_{L,R}$, form an interaction pair, so by Newton's third law

<Formula label="Third-law pair at a cut" tex="\vec F_{R,L}(x_P) = -\vec F_{L,R}(x_P)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.5.4), p. 8-19" />

Seen from outside, these internal pairs cancel. Inside the rope they are what carries the pull from one end to the other. The **tension** at $P$ is defined as the **magnitude** of this pair:

<Formula label="Tension at a point" tex="T(x_P) = \bigl|\vec F_{R,L}(x_P)\bigr| = \bigl|\vec F_{L,R}(x_P)\bigr|" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.5.5), p. 8-20" />

Tension is therefore a positive number at each point of the rope, not a vector. Its direction only appears when you draw a force diagram for one particular piece: on the left piece it points to the right, on the right piece to the left.

## When is the tension the same all along the rope?

Pull on the two ends of a rope, with tension $T_a$ at end $a$ and $T_b$ at end $b$, and take the direction from $a$ to $b$ as positive. Newton's second law for the whole rope, of mass $m_\text{rope}$, gives

<Formula label="Second law for a rope" tex="T_b - T_a = m_\text{rope}\, a_\text{rope}" cite="reading3" at="transcript 7.2, p. 2" />

(The recorded segment at first says "non-zero" where zero acceleration is meant, and drops the factor $a_\text{rope}$ when first stating this equation; the page uses the corrected form.)

Two cases make the tension **uniform**, the same everywhere:

1. **The rope does not accelerate.** Then the right-hand side is zero, whatever the rope's mass. The same argument applies to any piece between two cuts, so the tension is the same at every point.
2. **The rope is very light.** Even if it accelerates, $m_\text{rope}\,a_\text{rope}$ is negligible. This is the usual **massless rope** of mechanics problems. No real rope is massless; the phrase means "light enough that its mass does not matter here". For a light rope pulled at one end, the tension everywhere equals the applied pull:

<Formula label="Light rope" tex="T = F_{A,R}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.5.6), p. 8-20" />

where $F_{A,R}$ is the size of the force pulling at the end $A$. The notes reach the same conclusion for a rope that pulls a block (Eqs. 8.5.1–8.5.3, pp. 8-18 to 8-19): the pull is passed on unchanged to the block.

If the rope is heavy *and* accelerating, the tension is larger at the end that does the pulling: that end has to accelerate the rope as well as whatever it is attached to. A rope with mass is the subject of Lesson 13.

<WorkedExample title="How much does the rope matter?">
<Problem>
You pull a sled across snow with a rope of mass 0.40 kg, pulling on the rope with a force of 30 N, and the sled accelerates at 2.0 m/s². What is the tension at the sled end of the rope? Treat the rope as horizontal.
</Problem>
<Step title="Second law for the rope">
The rope moves with the sled, so $a_\text{rope} = 2.0$ m/s². Taking the direction of motion as positive: $T_\text{hand} - T_\text{sled} = m_\text{rope}\, a$.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve">
$T_\text{sled} = 30 - 0.40 \times 2.0 = 29.2$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Is the rope light?">
The difference is 0.8 N, under 3% of the pull. For most purposes this rope counts as massless; its mass matters only when $m_\text{rope}\,a$ is not small compared with the forces involved.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Solving a pulley system

A classic problem: a light rope runs over a pulley hanging from the ceiling, with block 1 (mass $m_1$) on one side and block 2 (mass $m_2$) on the other. MIT's lecturer works through it as a model of method.

**Assumptions.** The contact between rope and pulley is frictionless and the rope is light, so the tension is the same on both sides: $T_1 = T_2 = T$. (Later lessons on rotation drop these assumptions; with a massive pulley the two tensions differ.)

**Free-body diagrams and third-law pairs.** Draw one for each moving object: block 1, block 2 and the rope. On each block act gravity and the pull of the rope. The pull of the rope on block 1 and the pull of block 1 on the rope are a third-law pair; so are the corresponding forces at block 2. The rope also feels the upward push of the pulley.

**Unit vectors.** Suppose $m_1 > m_2$, so block 1 should go down and block 2 up. Choose a **separate unit vector for each block**, pointing the way that block is expected to move: $\hat j_1$ downwards for block 1, $\hat j_2$ upwards for block 2. Then both accelerations are positive. (A single shared axis also works, but then the two accelerations have opposite signs, an easy place to slip.)

**Newton's second law** along each block's own axis:

<Formula label="Block 1 (positive downwards)" tex="m_1 g - T = m_1 a_1" cite="reading4" at="transcript 7.3, p. 3" />

<Formula label="Block 2 (positive upwards)" tex="T - m_2 g = m_2 a_2" cite="reading4" at="transcript 7.3, p. 4" />

That is two equations for three unknowns, $T$, $a_1$ and $a_2$. The third is the **constraint**: the blocks are tied together by a rope of fixed length, so with these unit vectors $a_1 = a_2 \equiv a$. Adding the two equations removes $T$:

<Formula label="Acceleration of the two blocks" tex="a = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\, g" cite="reading4" at="transcript 7.3, p. 4" />

**Checks**, as the lecturer insists: the units are those of an acceleration (a ratio of masses times $g$); for equal masses $a = 0$, as it must be; and for $m_1 > m_2$ the result is positive, as the choice of unit vectors expected. Putting $a$ back into the equation for block 2 also gives the tension, $T = m_2(g + a) = 2m_1m_2 g/(m_1 + m_2)$.

<WorkedExample title="Two blocks over a pulley">
<Problem>
Blocks of 3.0 kg and 2.0 kg hang on either side of a light rope over a light, frictionless pulley. Find their acceleration, the tension in the rope, and the force in the rope that holds the pulley up. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².
</Problem>
<Step title="Acceleration">
$a = \dfrac{3.0 - 2.0}{3.0 + 2.0} \times 9.8 = 1.96$ m/s², the 3.0 kg block going down.
</Step>
<Step title="Tension">
$T = m_2(g + a) = 2.0 \times (9.8 + 1.96) \approx 23.5$ N. As a check, block 1 gives $T = m_1(g - a) = 3.0 \times (9.8 - 1.96) \approx 23.5$ N too. The tension lies between the two weights (19.6 N and 29.4 N): enough to lift the light block, not enough to hold up the heavy one.
</Step>
<Step title="Support of the pulley">
The rope pulls down on the (light) pulley on both sides, so the support carries $2T \approx 47.0$ N, less than the 49 N the two blocks would weigh at rest: the 1.96 N difference is what accelerates the pair's centre of mass downwards.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Hooke's law: the force of a spring

Fix one end of a spring to a wall and the other to a block on a frictionless surface. MIT's lecturer draws two pictures: an **equilibrium picture**, with the spring at its relaxed length, and a **dynamic picture** at some time $t$, with the block moved away. Put the origin, $x = 0$, where the block sits when the spring is relaxed, with $\hat i$ pointing away from the wall. Then the block's position $x$ is also how far the spring is stretched ($x > 0$) or compressed ($x < 0$).

Experiments show that, within a range of stretches and compressions, the size of the spring's force is proportional to $|x|$:

<Formula label="Size of the spring force" tex="F = k\,|x|" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.1.1), p. 8-3" />

and it always points back towards the relaxed position. In components this is **Hooke's law**:

<Formula label="Hooke's law" tex="F_x = -k x" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.1.2), p. 8-4" />

The minus sign says it all. Stretch the spring ($x > 0$) and it pulls the block back towards the wall ($F_x < 0$); compress it ($x < 0$) and it pushes the block out ($F_x > 0$). Either way the force points towards equilibrium: a **restoring force**. Because it depends on the first power of $x$, it is a **linear restoring force**. The **spring constant** $k$ measures how stiff the spring is, in newtons per metre (N/m); on a graph of $F_x$ against $x$ it is minus the slope.

Where does the force come from? Stretching pulls the atoms of the spring's material apart, compressing pushes them together, and the electric forces between them resist both.

**Hooke's law as a model.** The law was found by measuring: stretch a spring by $x$, release the block, measure its acceleration, and use $F = ma$. Repeating this for many springs shows the same proportionality, each with its own $k$. Generalising from a sample of springs to *all* ideal springs is what the notes call **Newtonian induction**, and it is what makes the law useful: it predicts the acceleration of a block before you measure it,

<Formula label="Predicted acceleration" tex="a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{k\,|x|}{m}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.1.3), p. 8-5" />

If a measurement disagrees with the prediction, the model has to be changed. Real springs do depart from it: the law holds only between a largest stretch and a largest compression, and many springs are wound with **pre-tension**, so that a force $F_0$ is needed before they start to stretch at all (and $F_1$ before they compress):

<Formula label="Spring with pre-tension" tex="F_x = -F_0 - kx \;\;(x > 0), \qquad F_x = +F_1 - kx \;\;(x < 0)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (8.1.4), p. 8-5" />

<WorkedExample title="Measuring a spring, then predicting">
<Problem>
A 0.25 kg block hung from a spring stretches it by 4.9 cm. The same spring is then laid on a frictionless table, attached to a wall and to the block, and the block is pulled out 6.0 cm and released. What is the block's acceleration at the moment of release? Take $g = 9.8$ m/s².
</Problem>
<Step title="Find k">
Hanging at rest, the spring's upward pull balances gravity: $k \times 0.049 = 0.25 \times 9.8$, so $k = 2.45 / 0.049 = 50$ N/m.
</Step>
<Step title="Predict the acceleration">
$a = k|x|/m = 50 \times 0.060 / 0.25 = 12$ m/s², directed towards the wall.
</Step>
<Step title="Comment">
That is more than $g$: a modest spring can produce a large acceleration on a light block. Measuring it and comparing with 12 m/s² is exactly the kind of test that checks the force law.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A rope can only pull. Cut it in imagination at a point: the two pieces pull on each other with a third-law pair, and the **tension** there is the magnitude of that pair.
- From $T_b - T_a = m_\text{rope}\,a$: the tension is uniform if the rope does not accelerate or if its mass is negligible. A light rope passes a pull on unchanged.
- In pulley problems, draw a free-body diagram for each object, mark the third-law pairs, give each object its own unit vector, and add the constraint that links their accelerations. Two blocks over a light, frictionless pulley accelerate at $a = (m_1 - m_2)g/(m_1 + m_2)$. Check units and special cases.
- **Hooke's law**: a spring's force is $F_x = -kx$, measured from the relaxed position, a linear restoring force with spring constant $k$ in N/m. It is a model found by experiment, valid within limits; real springs may have pre-tension.
