This set practises four lessons: [arc length, surface area and parametric curves](/courses/18-01/arc-length-and-surface-area), [parametric arc length and polar coordinates](/courses/18-01/polar-coordinates), [indeterminate forms and L'Hôpital's rule](/courses/18-01/lhopital-rule) and [improper integrals](/courses/18-01/improper-integrals). Its last problem looks back at [volumes](/courses/18-01/volumes) and at [maxima and minima](/courses/18-01/max-min-problems). It follows MIT's Problem Set 8, the last set of the course, in our own words.

MIT handed out Problem Set 8 in two halves, 8A and 8B, and each half has a Part I and a Part II. Part II holds MIT's longer problems, for which MIT publishes no solutions. All five are written out below, with hints and solutions of our own (42 points), and so is the extra-credit part of one of them, as an optional problem of its own (5 points). Part I is a list of short exercises from MIT's course reader, most of which MIT has solved itself. Those are not rewritten here: the last section of this page lists them and links to MIT's exercises and solutions.

One item of each half is left out: Problem 0 (3 points each), which asks students to name the people they worked with. That is course administration. With it, Part II of 8A is worth 30 points, as MIT's sheet says. For 8B the sheet's heading says 17 points and 5 extra, but its three items carry 3, 6 and 9 points, which is 18. (The 17 seems to be a slip in the heading. We use the points printed with the problems.)

MIT's sheet counts lectures by the Fall 2006 calendar: its Lectures 30 and 31 are Lectures 31 and 32 on OpenCourseWare and on this site, and its Lectures 34 to 37 are Lectures 35 to 38 here. The lecture notes for L'Hôpital's rule and improper integrals keep MIT's numbers 34 and 35 in their headings, so the solutions cite them as "Lecture 34 notes" and "Lecture 35 notes". MIT marks the first problem of 8B "Lecs 34-36", but the problem needs only the first two of those (L'Hôpital's rule and improper integrals, Lectures 35 and 36 here), not infinite series.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `a*k*sin(t)` or `pi*r^2`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type `sqrt(2)` for $\sqrt2$, `pi` for $\pi$, `theta` for $\theta$, `x1` for $x_1$, and `sinh(x1)` and `cosh(x1)` for the hyperbolic functions (or write them out with `exp`). A number box wants a decimal number, not a fraction, and where rounding matters its question says how exact your value must be. The points per problem and per part are MIT's. Where a part has more than one box, or a box asks for a step on the way (the speed in 8A Problem 1, the side of the triangle and the constant in 8A Problem 3, the value $A(5)$ and the choice boxes), that is our own addition, and so are the letters (c-i), (c-ii). If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Parts that ask you to show something or to make a sketch have no answer box: compare your work with the solution and mark it yourself. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## 8A, Problem 1

<Exercise id="a1" title="Three curves from one corner to the other" points={7} mit="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 1">

For a constant $a > 0$ and a power $k$, a point moves along the curve

<Formula label="The curves of Problem 1" tex="x = a\cos^k t, \qquad y = a\sin^k t" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 1, p. 1" />

Only the piece with $0 \le t \le \pi/2$ is used in this problem. It starts at the point $(a, 0)$ and ends at $(0, a)$. (That $a$ is positive is our addition.)

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Remove $t$: find the equation in $x$ and $y$ of this piece of the curve. It can be written in the form $(x/a)^p + (y/a)^p = 1$, with a power $p$ that depends on $k$. Then draw the piece for $k = 1$, $k = 2$ and $k = 3$.

<Hint>

The only link between $\cos t$ and $\sin t$ that holds for every $t$ is $\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$. Express $\cos t$ and $\sin t$ in $x$ and $y$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On this piece $\cos t$ and $\sin t$ are not negative, so you may take a $k$-th root: $\cos t = (x/a)^{1/k}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="a1-a" label="The power $p =$" expr="2/k"
  vars={{ k: [1, 5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "k/2", hint: "For k = 1 the curve is a circle, x² + y² = a², so p must be 2 there. Your power gives ½." },
    { expr: "1/k", hint: "That power turns x/a into cos t. The identity you need is about the square of cos t." },
    { expr: "2*k", hint: "Raising x/a to a power with k in it gives a higher power of cos t. You need a power that undoes the k." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Solve each equation for $\cos t$ or $\sin t$, and put the results into $\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$.

<Step title="Remove t">

For $0 \le t \le \pi/2$ neither $\cos t$ nor $\sin t$ is negative, so $\cos t = (x/a)^{1/k}$ and $\sin t = (y/a)^{1/k}$. Squaring and adding:

<Formula label="The curve without t" tex="\left(\frac{x}{a}\right)^{2/k} + \left(\frac{y}{a}\right)^{2/k} = 1, \qquad\text{so } p = \frac{2}{k}" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The three cases">

- $k = 1$: $x^2 + y^2 = a^2$, a quarter of the circle of radius $a$ about the origin. This is the circle of the lesson on [parametric curves](/courses/18-01/arc-length-and-surface-area#parametric-curves).
- $k = 2$: $x + y = a$, the straight line segment from $(a, 0)$ to $(0, a)$.
- $k = 3$: $x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = a^{2/3}$. This curve sags towards the origin. Its halfway point, at $t = \pi/4$, is $x = y = a/(2\sqrt2) = 0.354\,a$, well below the middle of the straight line, $x = y = 0.5\,a$.

<Plot variable="s" domain={[0, 1.1]} range={[0, 1.1]} xLabel="x" yLabel="y"
  curves={[
    { x: "cos(s)", y: "sin(s)", range: [0, 1.5708], label: "k = 1" },
    { x: "cos(s)^2", y: "sin(s)^2", range: [0, 1.5708], label: "k = 2" },
    { x: "cos(s)^3", y: "sin(s)^3", range: [0, 1.5708], label: "k = 3" }
  ]}
  caption="The three curves for a = 1, computed from the parametric formulas for t from 0 to π/2: the quarter circle (k = 1), the straight segment (k = 2) and the sagging curve (k = 3). All three run from (1, 0) to (0, 1)." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ the formula gives $(1)^p + 0 = 1$ and at $t = \pi/2$ it gives $0 + (1)^p = 1$, for every $k$: both end points lie on the curve. At $t = \pi/4$ with $k = 3$: $(x/a)^{2/3} = \left(2^{-3/2}\right)^{2/3} = \tfrac12$, and the same for $y$, so the sum is $1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

Without calculating an integral, give the length of the piece for $k = 1$ and for $k = 2$.

<Hint>

Look at your drawings. You know the circumference of a circle and the theorem of Pythagoras.

</Hint>

<Answer id="a1-b-1" label="Length for $k = 1$:" expr="pi*a/2"
  vars={{ a: [1, 4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*pi*a", hint: "That is the whole circle. The piece from t = 0 to π/2 is a quarter of it." },
    { expr: "pi*a", hint: "That is half the circle. The piece from t = 0 to π/2 is a quarter of it." },
    { expr: "pi*a^2/4", hint: "That is the area of the quarter disc, not the length of its curved edge." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="a1-b-2" label="Length for $k = 2$:" expr="sqrt(2)*a"
  vars={{ a: [1, 4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*a", hint: "That is the way round by the origin, along both axes. The segment is the hypotenuse of that right angle." },
    { expr: "a", hint: "That is the length of one of the two short sides of the right triangle. The segment is its hypotenuse." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both curves are known shapes.

<Step title="k = 1">

A quarter of a circle of radius $a$: a quarter of $2\pi a$, which is $\pi a/2 = 1.571\,a$.

</Step>

<Step title="k = 2">

The segment from $(a, 0)$ to $(0, a)$ is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with two sides of length $a$: its length is $\sqrt{a^2 + a^2} = \sqrt2\,a = 1.414\,a$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A straight line is the shortest path between two points, so the quarter circle must be longer than the segment, and it is.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

MIT's sheet recalls the rule for the length of a curve given by $x(t)$ and $y(t)$: integrate $ds$ from the first value of $t$ to the last, with

<Formula label="Arc length element of a parametric curve" tex="ds = \sqrt{(dx)^2 + (dy)^2} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part I, Lecture 30, p. 1" />

Find $ds/dt$ for general $k$ and $0 < t < \pi/2$, and with it a definite integral for the length of the piece. Then work out the integral for $k = 1$, $k = 2$ and $k = 3$. The first two must agree with part (b).

<Hint>

Differentiate $x$ and $y$ with the chain rule. Both derivatives contain the factor $ak\sin t\cos t$: take it out of the square root.

</Hint>

<Hint>

After that the root holds $\cos^{2k-4} t + \sin^{2k-4} t$. Look at what this is for $k = 2$ and for $k = 3$ before you integrate. For $k = 1$, put the two fractions on one denominator.

</Hint>

<Answer id="a1-c-speed" label="$\dfrac{ds}{dt} =$" expr="a*k*sin(t)*cos(t)*sqrt(cos(t)^(2*k-4) + sin(t)^(2*k-4))"
  vars={{ a: [1, 3], k: [1, 4], t: [0.2, 1.3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "a*k*sqrt(cos(t)^(2*k-2) + sin(t)^(2*k-2))", hint: "The chain rule gives each derivative one more factor: the derivative of cos t is −sin t and that of sin t is cos t." },
    { expr: "a*sin(t)*cos(t)*sqrt(cos(t)^(2*k-4) + sin(t)^(2*k-4))", hint: "When you differentiate a k-th power, the power k comes down as a factor." },
    { expr: "a*sqrt(cos(t)^(2*k) + sin(t)^(2*k))", hint: "That is the distance from the origin, the root of x² + y². The length of the curve needs the derivatives dx/dt and dy/dt." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="a1-c-3" label="Length for $k = 3$:" expr="3*a/2"
  vars={{ a: [1, 4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*a", hint: "The integral of sin t cos t from 0 to π/2 is ½, not 1." },
    { expr: "a/2", hint: "The factor k = 3 from the derivatives is missing." },
    { expr: "6*a", hint: "That is the length of the whole closed curve, for t from 0 to 2π. The question is about the piece up to π/2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The lesson calls $ds/dt$ the speed along the curve. Find it from the two derivatives, then integrate it over $0 \le t \le \pi/2$.

<Step title="The derivatives">

<Formula label="Derivatives of x and y" tex="\frac{dx}{dt} = -ak\cos^{k-1} t\,\sin t, \qquad \frac{dy}{dt} = ak\sin^{k-1} t\,\cos t" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The speed">

Square, add, and take out the common factor $a^2k^2\sin^2 t\cos^2 t$. Between $0$ and $\pi/2$ the product $\sin t\cos t$ is positive, so its square root is itself:

<Formula label="Speed along the curve" tex="\frac{ds}{dt} = ak\,\sin t\cos t\,\sqrt{\cos^{2k-4} t + \sin^{2k-4} t}" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Length for general k" tex="L = ak\int_0^{\pi/2} \sin t\cos t\,\sqrt{\cos^{2k-4} t + \sin^{2k-4} t}\;dt" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

(The name $L$ is ours.)

</Step>

<Step title="k = 1">

The root is $\sqrt{\dfrac{1}{\cos^2 t} + \dfrac{1}{\sin^2 t}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t}{\cos^2 t\,\sin^2 t}} = \dfrac{1}{\sin t\cos t}$. It cancels the factor in front, so the speed is the constant $a$ and $L = a \cdot \pi/2 = \pi a/2$.

</Step>

<Step title="k = 2 and k = 3">

Both need one integral: with $u = \sin t$, $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin t\cos t\,dt = \int_0^1 u\,du = \tfrac12$.

For $k = 2$ the root is $\sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt2$, so $L = 2a \cdot \sqrt2 \cdot \tfrac12 = \sqrt2\,a$.

For $k = 3$ the root is $\sqrt{\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t} = 1$, so

<Formula label="Length for k = 3" tex="L = 3a\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin t\cos t\,dt = \frac{3a}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The cases $k = 1$ and $k = 2$ agree with part (b). The three lengths are $1.571\,a$, $1.414\,a$ and $1.5\,a$. The straight segment is the shortest, as it must be: no curve from $(a, 0)$ to $(0, a)$ can be shorter than $\sqrt2\,a$. All three are lengths, $a$ times a pure number.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## 8A, Problem 2

<Exercise id="a2" title="Hyperbolic functions, the hanging chain and the soap film" points={9} mit="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 2">

The hyperbolic cosine and the hyperbolic sine are defined by

<Formula label="Hyperbolic cosine and sine" tex="\cosh x = \frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2}, \qquad \sinh x = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 2, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Show that:

1. $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sinh x = \cosh x$ and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\cosh x = \sinh x$;
2. $\cosh^2 x = 1 + \sinh^2 x$;
3. $\cosh^2 x = \dfrac{1 + \cosh 2x}{2}$.

<Hint>

For the derivatives, differentiate the definitions. The derivative of $e^{-x}$ is $-e^{-x}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the other two, square the definitions. The product $e^x \cdot e^{-x}$ is $1$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Everything follows from the definitions and from $e^x \cdot e^{-x} = 1$.

<Step title="The derivatives">

<Formula label="Derivatives of the hyperbolic functions" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2} = \frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2} = \cosh x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\,\frac{e^x + e^{-x}}{2} = \frac{e^x - e^{-x}}{2} = \sinh x" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

Unlike the ordinary cosine, the hyperbolic cosine brings no minus sign.

</Step>

<Step title="The squares">

<Formula label="Squares of the hyperbolic functions" tex="\cosh^2 x = \frac{e^{2x} + 2 + e^{-2x}}{4}, \qquad \sinh^2 x = \frac{e^{2x} - 2 + e^{-2x}}{4}" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

Subtracting gives $\cosh^2 x - \sinh^2 x = \tfrac44 = 1$, which is the second statement. For the third, $e^{2x} + e^{-2x} = 2\cosh 2x$ by the definition, so

<Formula label="Hyperbolic half-angle formula" tex="\cosh^2 x = \frac{2\cosh 2x + 2}{4} = \frac{1 + \cosh 2x}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Try $x = 0$, where $\cosh 0 = 1$ and $\sinh 0 = 0$: the second statement reads $1 = 1 + 0$ and the third $1 = (1 + 1)/2$. (A test at one point can catch a wrong formula, but it does not prove a right one. The proof is the algebra above.) The three statements look like $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$ and $\cos^2 x = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2x)$ for the ordinary functions, with some signs changed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

Which curve do the equations $x = \cosh t$, $y = \sinh t$ describe, when $t$ runs through all numbers? Give its equation and its name. (MIT asks for the equation and the name. The choice between the whole curve and a part of it is our addition.)

<Hint>

Statement 2 of part (a) links $\cosh t$ and $\sinh t$ without $t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Can $\cosh t$ be negative? Look at its definition.

</Hint>

<Answer id="a2-b" label="The curve is"
  choices={["the whole hyperbola x^2 - y^2 = 1, both branches", "the right-hand branch of the hyperbola x^2 - y^2 = 1", "the circle x^2 + y^2 = 1", "the right-hand half of the circle x^2 + y^2 = 1"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Part (a) gives $\cosh^2 t - \sinh^2 t = 1$.

<Step title="The equation">

<Formula label="The curve of part (b)" tex="x^2 - y^2 = \cosh^2 t - \sinh^2 t = 1" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

This is a hyperbola, which is where the hyperbolic functions get their name, just as $\cos t$ and $\sin t$ trace the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Which part of it">

$\cosh t$ is half the sum of two positive numbers whose product is $1$, so $x = \cosh t \ge 1$: the point stays on the right-hand branch. It covers all of that branch, because $y = \sinh t$ takes every value: it goes to $-\infty$ as $t \to -\infty$ and to $+\infty$ as $t \to +\infty$, without a jump.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ the point is $(1, 0)$, the tip of the right-hand branch. At $t = 1$ it is $(1.5431, 1.1752)$, and $1.5431^2 - 1.1752^2 = 2.3811 - 1.3811 = 1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

The curve $y = \cosh x$ is called a **catenary**. A chain that hangs from two points at the same height takes this shape.

(c-i) Sketch the curve.

(c-ii) Find the length of the curve from its lowest point to the point $(x_1, \cosh x_1)$, for a fixed $x_1 > 0$.

<Hint>

For the sketch: what is $\cosh 0$, what is the slope there, what happens for large $x$, and how are $\cosh(-x)$ and $\cosh x$ related?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the length, use $ds = \sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2}\,dx$ from the lesson. Part (a) gives the derivative, and then it turns the root into something simple.

</Hint>

<Answer id="a2-c" label="Length from the lowest point to $x = x_1$:" expr="sinh(x1)"
  vars={{ x1: [0.3, 3] }}
  names={{ x1: "x_1" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "cosh(x1) - 1", hint: "That is how far the curve has risen, the integral of the slope sinh x. The length is the integral of the root of 1 + sinh² x." },
    { expr: "cosh(x1)", hint: "That is the height of the end point. A length that starts at the lowest point must be zero when x₁ is zero." },
    { expr: "sqrt(x1^2 + (cosh(x1) - 1)^2)", hint: "That is the straight-line distance between the two points. The curve between them is longer." },
    { expr: "x1", hint: "That is the horizontal distance only. The curve also rises, so it is longer." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $y = \cosh x$ the slope is $\sinh x$, and $1 + \sinh^2 x = \cosh^2 x$ is a perfect square. The root in $ds$ disappears.

<Step title="The sketch">

$\cosh(-x) = \cosh x$, so the graph is its own mirror image in the $y$-axis. At $x = 0$ the height is $1$ and the slope $\sinh 0 = 0$. For $x > 0$ the slope $\sinh x$ is positive and grows, and the second derivative $\cosh x$ is positive everywhere, so the curve bends upwards: $(0, 1)$ is the lowest point. For large $x$ the term $e^{-x}$ dies out and $\cosh x$ is close to $\tfrac12 e^x$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-2.2, 2.2]} range={[0, 4.5]} xLabel="x" yLabel="y"
  curves={[
    { expr: "cosh(x)", label: "cosh x" }
  ]}
  caption="The catenary y = cosh x, computed from the formula. Its lowest point is (0, 1). (The two axes may be drawn at different scales.)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The length">

The lowest point is at $x = 0$. With $dy/dx = \sinh x$ and the arc length formula of the [lesson](/courses/18-01/arc-length-and-surface-area#arc-length):

<Formula label="Length of the catenary" tex="s = \int_0^{x_1}\sqrt{1 + \sinh^2 x}\;dx = \int_0^{x_1}\cosh x\,dx = \Big[\sinh x\Big]_0^{x_1} = \sinh x_1" cite="mit" at="Problem Set 8A, Part II, Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

The root of $\cosh^2 x$ is $\cosh x$ itself, because $\cosh x$ is positive.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The curve must be longer than the straight line between its end points. For $x_1 = 1$ the straight line from $(0, 1)$ to $(1, 1.5431)$ has length $\sqrt{1 + 0.5431^2} = 1.138$, and the curve has length $\sinh 1 = 1.175$: a little longer, as a gently bent curve should be. For $x_1 = 0$ the length is $\sinh 0 = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

The piece of the catenary of part (c), from $x = 0$ to $x = x_1$, is turned about the $x$-axis. Find the area of the surface it sweeps out. This surface is called a **catenoid**.

MIT's set adds why it is interesting. For circles that are not too far apart, the catenoid has the least area of all surfaces that join them. (For circles far apart, the two flat discs that fill them have less area, and a stretched film breaks into those.) Dip two circles of wire in soapy water and, with some coaxing, a soap film forms between them in this shape. In general a soap film tries to span its wire frame with as little area as possible.

<Hint>

The lesson gives the area of the band swept out by a small piece of curve: $dA = 2\pi y\,ds$. You found $ds$ in part (c).

<Solution>

<Step title="A positive whole number m">

<Formula label="L'Hôpital's rule, m times" tex="\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{x^m}{e^x} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{m\,x^{m-1}}{e^x} = \cdots = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{m!}{e^x} = 0" cite="reading4" at="pp. 3–4, Example 8 (there for the tenth power, upside down)" />

The integral is improper at both ends: the integrand blows up at $x = 0$, and the interval is endless. Split it at $x = 1$ and treat each piece with a comparison.

</Hint>

<Hint>

To evaluate it, choose the new variable so that the root disappears: $x = u^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You then need $\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}\,du$. The lesson on [average value and probability](/courses/18-01/average-value-and-probability) finds $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}\,dx$ by computing one volume in two ways. The integrand is even, so you need half of that.

</Hint>

<Answer id="b1e-value" label="$A(-\tfrac12) =$" value="1.772" tolerance="0.001" calc="b1e-value" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Near $x = 0$ the integrand behaves like $1/\sqrt x$, whose integral converges. Far out it is smaller than $e^{-x}$. The substitution $x = u^2$ then turns the integral into the Gaussian integral.

<Step title="Convergence near 0">

For $0 < x \le 1$, $e^{-x} \le 1$, so the integrand is at most $x^{-1/2}$. The lesson's example of an improper integral of the second type is exactly

<Formula label="The comparison near zero" tex="\int_0^1 \frac{dx}{\sqrt x} = \lim_{a\to0^+}\left(2 - 2a^{1/2}\right) = 2" cite="reading5" at="p. 6" />

So the areas $\int_a^1 x^{-1/2}e^{-x}\,dx$ grow as $a$ shrinks but stay below $2$: this piece converges.

</Step>

<Step title="Convergence at infinity">

For $x \ge 1$, $1/\sqrt x \le 1$, so the integrand is at most $e^{-x}$, and $\int_1^\infty e^{-x}\,dx = e^{-1}$ is finite. By comparison (Lecture 35 notes, p. 4) this piece converges too.

</Step>

<Step title="Change variables">

Put $x = u^2$ with $u > 0$: $dx = 2u\,du$ and $\sqrt x = u$. For $0 < a < N$,

<Formula label="The change of variables" tex="\int_a^N \frac{e^{-x}}{\sqrt x}\,dx = \int_{\sqrt a}^{\sqrt N}\frac{e^{-u^2}}{u}\,2u\,du = 2\int_{\sqrt a}^{\sqrt N} e^{-u^2}\,du" cite="reading1" at="Part II, Problem 1(e) (our own solution)" />

Let $a \to 0$ and $N \to \infty$. The notes recall the value of the integral that appears (Lecture 35 notes, Example 3, p. 3):

<Formula label="The value at n = −1/2" tex="A\!\left(-\tfrac12\right) = 2\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}\,du = 2\cdot\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2} = \sqrt\pi = 1.772" cite="reading5" at="p. 3, Example 3 (the value √π/2; the rest is our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two comparisons give bounds. From above: $A(-\tfrac12) \le 2 + e^{-1} = 2.368$. From below, keeping only $0 < x \le 1$, where $e^{-x} \ge e^{-1}$: $A(-\tfrac12) \ge 2e^{-1} = 0.736$. The value $1.772$ lies between them.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## 8B, Problem 2

<Exercise id="b2" title="A soap bubble on a table" points={9} mit="Problem Set 8B, Part II, Problem 2">

MIT calls this a review problem on solids and surfaces of revolution. A **spherical cap** of height $h$ is the part of a ball of radius $r$ that lies above a horizontal plane at a distance $h$ below the top of the ball.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Show that the volume of the cap, enclosed by the piece of sphere on top and by the plane underneath, is

<Formula label="Volume of a spherical cap" tex="V = \pi\left(rh^2 - \frac{h^3}{3}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Part II, Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

Draw a picture. Test the formula in three cases: nothing ($h = 0$), half the ball ($h = r$) and the whole ball ($h = 2r$). From here on $0 \le h \le 2r$. Why?

<Hint>

Slice the cap into horizontal disks. Measure the depth $y$ of a slice from the top of the ball, so that $y$ runs from $0$ to $h$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The slice at depth $y$ is at a distance $r - y$ from the centre of the ball. Pythagoras gives the radius of the slice.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The method of disks: a horizontal slice of thickness $dy$ is a disk, with volume $\pi \times (\text{its radius})^2 \times dy$ (Lecture 22 notes, p. 1).

<Step title="The picture">

Draw a circle of radius $r$ and a horizontal line that cuts it at a distance $h$ below its top. The cap is the part of the circle above the line, turned about the vertical line through the centre. Its flat bottom is a disk. For $h < r$ the cap is shallower than half a ball, and for $h > r$ it is more than half.

</Step>

<Step title="The radius of a slice">

A slice at depth $y$ below the top lies $r - y$ from the centre (above it if $y < r$, below it if $y > r$). Its edge is on the sphere, at distance $r$ from the centre. With $\rho$ for the radius of the slice (our name):

<Formula label="Radius of a slice" tex="\rho^2 = r^2 - (r - y)^2 = 2ry - y^2" cite="reading1" at="Part II, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Add the disks">

<Formula label="Volume of the cap by disks" tex="V = \int_0^h \pi\left(2ry - y^2\right)dy = \pi\left[ry^2 - \frac{y^3}{3}\right]_0^h = \pi\left(rh^2 - \frac{h^3}{3}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Part II, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

This is the integral with which the lesson on [volumes](/courses/18-01/volumes#the-method-of-disks) finds the volume of a whole ball, stopped at $h$.

</Step>

<Step title="The three cases, and the range of h">

- $h = 0$: $V = 0$. Nothing.
- $h = r$: $V = \pi\left(r^3 - \tfrac13 r^3\right) = \tfrac23\pi r^3$, half of $\tfrac43\pi r^3$.
- $h = 2r$: $V = \pi\left(4r^3 - \tfrac83 r^3\right) = \tfrac43\pi r^3$, the whole ball.

The height of a piece of a ball cannot be negative and cannot be more than the diameter $2r$. Beyond $h = 2r$ the slices have $\rho^2 = 2ry - y^2 < 0$: there is no ball there.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Find the area of the curved surface of the cap, in terms of $r$ and $h$. Test your result for $h = 0$, $h = r$ and $h = 2r$.

<Hint>

The lesson on surface area finds the area of a slice of a sphere between two parallel planes. What is special about its result?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The area of such a slice depends only on the radius of the sphere and on the distance between the two planes.

</Hint>

<Answer id="b2-b" label="Area of the curved surface:" expr="2*pi*r*h"
  vars={{ r: [1, 3], h: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi*(2*r*h - h^2)", hint: "That is the area of the flat disk under the cap. The question is about the curved surface above it." },
    { expr: "4*pi*r*h", hint: "Test h = 2r: the result should be the area of the whole sphere, 4πr². Yours is twice that." },
    { expr: "pi*r*h", hint: "Test h = 2r: the result should be the area of the whole sphere, 4πr². Yours is half of that." },
    { expr: "2*pi*h^2", hint: "For h = r this is right, by accident. Test h = 2r: the whole sphere has area 4πr², and yours gives twice that." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Lay the axis of revolution along the $x$-axis. The sphere comes from turning the half circle $y = \sqrt{r^2 - x^2}$ about it, and the cap is the part with $r - h \le x \le r$. Add the bands $2\pi y\,ds$.

<Step title="The band">

The slope of the half circle is $dy/dx = -x/\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}$, so

<Formula label="Arc length element on the circle" tex="ds = \sqrt{1 + \frac{x^2}{r^2 - x^2}}\;dx = \frac{r}{\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}}\,dx" cite="reading1" at="Part II, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Add the bands">

The root cancels against $y$:

<Formula label="Area of the cap" tex="S = \int_{r-h}^{r} 2\pi\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}\cdot\frac{r}{\sqrt{r^2 - x^2}}\,dx = \int_{r-h}^{r} 2\pi r\,dx = 2\pi r h" cite="reading1" at="Part II, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

(The name $S$ is ours.) This is the lesson's [slice of a sphere](/courses/18-01/arc-length-and-surface-area#surface-area-of-revolution), with the slice at the end of the sphere. The lecture notes do the same calculation for a sphere of radius $1$ (Lecture 31 notes, pp. 3–4).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

- $h = 0$: $S = 0$.
- $h = r$: $S = 2\pi r^2$, half the sphere.
- $h = 2r$: $S = 4\pi r^2$, the whole sphere.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Now the shape of a soap bubble that sits on a table. Suppose the bubble is a cap of a sphere as above, that it holds a fixed volume $V$ of air, and that the area of its curved surface is as small as possible. (The flat bottom is the table and does not count.) Find the shape with the least area and describe it. MIT's hint: find $h/r$.

<Hint>

Two unknowns, $r$ and $h$, and one condition, the fixed volume. Use the condition to remove one unknown. Which of the two is easy to solve for in the volume formula?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Solve the volume formula for $r$ and put it into $S = 2\pi rh$. You get the area as a function of $h$ alone. The lesson on [maxima and minima](/courses/18-01/max-min-problems) treats an open can of least surface area in the same way.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Set the derivative to zero. That gives $V$ in terms of $h$. Put it back into your formula for $r$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="b2-c-ratio" label="At the least area, $h/r =$" value="1" tolerance="0.005" calc="b2-c-ratio" />

<Answer id="b2-c-shape" label="The bubble is"
  choices={["a whole sphere that touches the table in one point", "a cap flatter than half a sphere", "exactly half a sphere", "a cap between half a sphere and a whole one"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A problem of least value with a side condition. The fixed volume ties $r$ to $h$, so the area becomes a function of $h$ alone. Then look for its critical point, and compare with the ends of the range.

<Step title="Remove r">

From $V = \pi\left(rh^2 - \tfrac13 h^3\right)$:

<Formula label="The radius for a given volume and height" tex="r = \frac{V}{\pi h^2} + \frac{h}{3}" cite="reading1" at="Part II, Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="The area as a function of the height" tex="S(h) = 2\pi r h = \frac{2V}{h} + \frac{2\pi h^2}{3}" cite="reading1" at="Part II, Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The critical point">

<Formula label="Derivative of the area" tex="\frac{dS}{dh} = -\frac{2V}{h^2} + \frac{4\pi h}{3} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V = \frac{2\pi h^3}{3}" cite="reading1" at="Part II, Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

Put this back into the formula for $r$:

<Formula label="The shape of least area" tex="r = \frac{2\pi h^3/3}{\pi h^2} + \frac{h}{3} = \frac{2h}{3} + \frac{h}{3} = h, \qquad\text{so } \frac{h}{r} = 1" cite="reading1" at="Part II, Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

The height equals the radius: the bubble is exactly half a sphere, with its flat side on the table.

</Step>

<Step title="It is the least area, not the greatest">

The derivative $dS/dh$ is negative for $h^3 < 3V/(2\pi)$ and positive for $h^3 > 3V/(2\pi)$. So $S$ falls until the critical point and rises after it: that point gives the least area on the whole range of $h$, ends included. The range runs from $h$ near $0$ (a very flat, very wide cap, where $2V/h$ is huge) to the whole sphere, $h = 2r$, which is $h^3 = 6V/\pi$ (put $r = h/2$ in the volume formula). The critical point, $h^3 = 3V/(2\pi)$, lies inside that range.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Half a ball of radius $h$ has the volume $\tfrac23\pi h^3$: the critical point is the hemisphere of part (a). Compare with the other candidate one might guess, a whole sphere of the same volume. With our own number $V = 1$: the half sphere has $h = r = (3/(2\pi))^{1/3} = 0.782$ and area $2\pi r^2 = 3.838$. The whole sphere has radius $(3/(4\pi))^{1/3} = 0.620$ and area $4.836$ (four times $\pi$ times the square of the unrounded radius), which is 26 percent more. The table gives the bubble its flat side for free.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
