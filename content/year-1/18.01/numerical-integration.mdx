The lecture starts with a correction: the energy in the boiling-cauldron example **is** heat. Energy, heat and work are the same physical quantity, measured in different units: calories, kilowatt-hours, foot-pounds (transcript, p. 1). Then it finishes the dartboard problem announced last time, and turns to integrals that have no formula.

## The dartboard

Someone throws darts at a target; your little brother stands next to it. How likely is he to be hit (transcript, pp. 1–8; notes for Lecture 23, pp. 4–6)? The model: the number of hits **per unit area** at distance $r$ from the centre is proportional to $c\,e^{-r^2}$. The probability of any region is the part over the whole, so the unknown $c$ cancels (pp. 1–3).

Hits per area mean that a ring between $r_1$ and $r_2$ is weighted by a **volume of revolution**: shells of circumference $2\pi r$, height $e^{-r^2}$ and thickness $dr$ (p. 2). The antiderivative is easy, $-\pi e^{-r^2}$ (p. 5). For the whole, let $r$ run from 0 to infinity: unrealistic for a basement wall, but the tail is negligible and the numbers come out cleaner (p. 6). So

<Formula label="Probability of a ring" tex="P(r_1 < r < r_2) = \frac{\int_{r_1}^{r_2} e^{-r^2}\,2\pi r\,dr}{\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}\,2\pi r\,dr} = \frac{\pi\left(e^{-r_1^2} - e^{-r_2^2}\right)}{\pi} = e^{-r_1^2} - e^{-r_2^2}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 5–6" />

Two realistic assumptions finish it (pp. 7–8). A seven-year-old hits the target, radius $a$, about half the time: $1 - e^{-a^2} = \tfrac12$, so $e^{-a^2} = \tfrac12$. The brother stands between $2a$ and $3a$, covering two hours of the clock, from 3 to 5 o'clock, a sixth of the ring. Then

<Formula label="The little brother's risk" tex="P \approx \frac16\left(e^{-4a^2} - e^{-9a^2}\right) = \frac16\left[\left(\tfrac12\right)^4 - \left(\tfrac12\right)^9\right] \approx \frac16\cdot\frac{1}{16} \approx \frac{1}{100}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 7–8" />

about 1% per dart: stand there for a hundred throws and a hit is likely (p. 8). The lecturer adds that this kind of model was used to analyse where the V-2 rockets fell on London (p. 4). Notice the weight in $r$ is $2\pi c\,r\,e^{-r^2}$, which is **zero** at the centre: although $e^{-r^2}$ is largest there, the centre has so little area that a dart rarely lands near it (pp. 4, 8–9).

## Why numerical integration?

Many integrals cannot be done in closed form, such as $\int_0^x \cos(t^2)\,dt$ or $\int_0^3 e^{-x^2}\,dx$, and others are just a mess; then you compute the number directly (transcript, p. 9; notes, p. 1). Sample the function at $x_0 = a, x_1, \dots, x_n = b$, equally spaced by $\Delta x$, with $y_i = f(x_i)$, and combine the $y$'s (pp. 9–10).

**Riemann sums** use rectangles: $(y_0 + y_1 + \cdots + y_{n-1})\,\Delta x$ with left ends, or $(y_1 + \cdots + y_n)\,\Delta x$ with right ends. They are "incredibly inefficient" (pp. 9–10; notes, p. 1).

## The trapezoidal rule

Join neighbouring points by straight lines instead (transcript, pp. 10–11; notes, p. 2). Each strip is a trapezoid of area $\Delta x\,\frac{y_i + y_{i+1}}{2}$, base times average height. Adding them, every inner value appears twice with a half:

<Formula label="Trapezoidal rule" tex="\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \Delta x\left(\frac{y_0}{2} + y_1 + y_2 + \cdots + y_{n-1} + \frac{y_n}{2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

It is exactly the **average of the left and right Riemann sums**, treating the two ends symmetrically. Better, but still not very efficient (p. 11; notes, p. 3).

## Simpson's rule

Now fit a **parabola** through each three neighbouring points, which needs an even number of intervals (transcript, pp. 11–12; notes, pp. 3–4). For one pair of intervals the area under the parabola is the base $2\Delta x$ times a weighted average height, which emphasizes the middle (the computation is on the problem set):

<Formula label="Area under a parabola" tex="2\Delta x\cdot\frac{y_0 + 4y_1 + y_2}{6}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 11–12" />

Add the pairs: the weights $1, 4, 1$ overlap where pairs meet, giving $1, 4, 2, 4, 2, \dots, 2, 4, 1$ (p. 12; notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Simpson's rule (n even)" tex="\int_a^b f(x)\,dx \approx \frac{\Delta x}{3}\left(y_0 + 4y_1 + 2y_2 + 4y_3 + \cdots + 2y_{n-2} + 4y_{n-1} + y_n\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

As a check, for $f = 1$ the weights add up to $3n$, so the rule gives $n\,\Delta x = b - a$, as it should (notes, p. 4). On a small enough piece a typical function has no inflection point, so parabolas fit it extremely well (transcript, p. 11). The notes compare on $\int_0^1 \frac{dx}{1 + x^2} = \frac{\pi}{4} \approx 0.785$ with $n = 2$: the trapezoidal rule gives 0.775 and Simpson's rule 0.7833, with an error roughly of order $(\Delta x)^4$ (notes, p. 5). The lecture promises to show how much better Simpson's rule is next time (p. 12).

</Step>
<Step title="Trapezoidal">

</Step>

$\frac{0.25}{3}\,(1 + 4 \times 0.93941 + 2 \times 0.77880 + 4 \times 0.56978 + 0.36788) \approx 0.74686$: off by only about 0.00003, a hundred times better for the same five values.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Distances that need a computer">
In a flat universe with matter and vacuum energy, the comoving distance to redshift $z$ is $\frac{c}{H_0}\int_0^z \frac{dz'}{\sqrt{\Omega_m(1 + z')^3 + \Omega_\Lambda}}$, an integral with no elementary formula. With $\Omega_m = 0.3$, $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.7$ and $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc, Simpson's rule with just $n = 2$ gives about 3300 Mpc to $z = 1$, within 0.1% of the exact value. Cosmologists compute such integrals numerically all the time, for distances to supernovae and for the age of the universe.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Dartboard: with hits per area $\propto e^{-r^2}$, $P(r_1 < r < r_2) = e^{-r_1^2} - e^{-r_2^2}$; standing beside the board means about a 1% chance per dart.
- Numerical integration samples $y_i = f(x_i)$ at spacing $\Delta x$ and combines them.
- Trapezoidal rule: $\Delta x\,(\tfrac12 y_0 + y_1 + \cdots + y_{n-1} + \tfrac12 y_n)$, the average of left and right sums.
- Simpson's rule ($n$ even): $\frac{\Delta x}{3}(y_0 + 4y_1 + 2y_2 + \cdots + 4y_{n-1} + y_n)$, from parabolas; far more accurate.
