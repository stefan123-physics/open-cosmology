Calculus begins with one question: how fast is something changing? MIT's 18.01 opens its first unit, on **differentiation**, with two goals. The first, for this lecture, is to understand what a derivative *is*, from a geometric and a physical point of view. The second, for the next few weeks, is to be able to differentiate any function you can write down, even something as untidy as $e^x \arctan x$. Professor Jerison stresses why this matters so widely: derivatives are central to measurement of almost every kind, in science, engineering, economics and opinion polling alike.

## The geometric problem: a tangent line

Draw the graph of a function $y = f(x)$ and pick a point $P$ on it, above $x = x_0$. Drawing the tangent line at $P$ by eye is easy. The task is to do it **analytically**: with a calculation that a machine could follow as well as a person.

From school algebra, any line through the point $(x_0, y_0)$ has the equation

<Formula label="A line through a point" tex="y - y_0 = m\,(x - x_0)" cite="reading2" at="transcript p. 2" />

To pin down the tangent line we need two things. The point is no problem: $y_0 = f(x_0)$. The slope $m$ is the calculus part. It gets its own name, the **derivative of $f$ at $x_0$**, written $f'(x_0)$: by definition, the slope of the tangent line to $y = f(x)$ at $P$.

But what *is* a tangent line? It is **not** simply a line that meets the graph at only one point: a wiggly curve can cross its own tangent line several times, and a vertical line meets the parabola $y = x^2$ only once without being its tangent. The right idea is a limit. Take a second point $Q$ on the graph and draw the line through $P$ and $Q$, a **secant line**. Now slide $Q$ along the curve towards $P$, keeping $P$ fixed. The secant lines swing round and settle on one line: the tangent line is the **limit of the secant lines $PQ$ as $Q \to P$**.

## From secant to derivative: the difference quotient

Put this into symbols. Let $Q$ lie a horizontal distance $\Delta x$ (the **change in $x$**) to the right of $P$. Then $P = (x_0, f(x_0))$ and $Q = (x_0 + \Delta x,\, f(x_0 + \Delta x))$, and the rise from $P$ to $Q$ is $\Delta f = f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)$, the **change in $f$**. The slope of the secant is $\Delta f/\Delta x$; letting $Q \to P$ means letting $\Delta x \to 0$. This gives the formula Jerison boxes as the most important of the day:

<Formula label="Definition of the derivative" tex="f'(x_0) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)}{\Delta x}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2; boxed on the board, transcript p. 4" />

The fraction is called the **difference quotient**: its numerator is a difference of two values of $f$, and its denominator, $\Delta x = (x_0 + \Delta x) - x_0$, is a difference in disguise.

One warning from the notes: setting $\Delta x = 0$ straight away always gives $0/0$, which means nothing. The method is always to **simplify first**, to cancel the factor of $\Delta x$ hidden in the numerator, and only then let $\Delta x \to 0$.

<WorkedExample title="The derivative of x² at x = 3, from the definition">
<Problem>
Find $f'(3)$ for $f(x) = x^2$, using only the definition.
</Problem>
<Step title="Difference quotient">
$\dfrac{f(3 + \Delta x) - f(3)}{\Delta x} = \dfrac{9 + 6\Delta x + (\Delta x)^2 - 9}{\Delta x} = \dfrac{6\Delta x + (\Delta x)^2}{\Delta x}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Cancel, then take the limit">
Dividing by $\Delta x$ leaves $6 + \Delta x$, and as $\Delta x \to 0$ this tends to $f'(3) = 6$.
</Step>
<Step title="See it in numbers">
With $\Delta x = 0.1$ the secant slope is 6.1; with $\Delta x = 0.01$ it is 6.01; with $\Delta x = 0.001$, 6.001. The secant slopes close in on the tangent slope, 6.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Example: the derivative of 1/x

MIT's first example is $f(x) = 1/x$, whose graph is a hyperbola. The difference quotient is

<Formula label="Difference quotient of 1/x" tex="\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = \frac{1}{\Delta x}\left[\frac{1}{x_0 + \Delta x} - \frac{1}{x_0}\right] = \frac{1}{\Delta x}\left[\frac{x_0 - (x_0 + \Delta x)}{(x_0 + \Delta x)\,x_0}\right] = \frac{-1}{(x_0 + \Delta x)\,x_0}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Putting the two fractions over a common denominator makes the $x_0$'s cancel in the numerator, leaving $-\Delta x$, and that $\Delta x$ cancels the one outside. Now the limit is easy: set $\Delta x = 0$.

<Formula label="Derivative of 1/x" tex="f'(x_0) = -\frac{1}{x_0^2}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 2–3" />

Two quick checks against the graph: the answer is **negative**, and the hyperbola does slope downwards; and as $x_0$ grows it gets smaller in size, as the curve flattens out to the right. In answer to a student's question, Jerison also put in a number: at $x_0 = 3$ the expression $-1/((3 + \Delta x)\cdot 3)$ tends to $-1/9$.

## A word problem: triangles under the hyperbola

Real problems mix a little calculus with a lot of other mathematics. Jerison's example: find the area of the triangle formed by the two axes and a tangent line to $y = 1/x$ (in the first quadrant).

Name the point of tangency $(x_0, y_0)$, with $y_0 = 1/x_0$. The only calculus step is the tangent line itself:

<Formula label="Tangent line to y = 1/x" tex="y - y_0 = -\frac{1}{x_0^2}\,(x - x_0)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Everything else is algebra. The tangent meets the $x$-axis where $y = 0$: then $-1/x_0 = -(x - x_0)/x_0^2$, which gives $x = 2x_0$. For the $y$-intercept there is a shortcut by **symmetry**: $y = 1/x$ is the same equation as $xy = 1$ and as $x = 1/y$, so swapping the roles of $x$ and $y$ turns any true statement about the graph into another true one, and the $y$-intercept is $2y_0$. (Putting $x = 0$ into the tangent line gives the same.) The area is

<Formula label="Area of the triangle" tex="\text{Area} = \tfrac12\,(2x_0)(2y_0) = 2x_0y_0 = 2" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

whatever point you choose: a curious property of this particular curve.

Jerison adds two remarks. The first is about what Jerison calls the hardest part of "one-variable" calculus: it is happy to juggle many variables at once, and this small problem already has four, $x$, $y$, $x_0$ and $y_0$.

The second is subtler: the letter $y$ itself was used in two roles. In the equation of the curve, $y = 1/x$, it is the height of a point on the hyperbola; that is where $y_0 = 1/x_0$ came from. In the tangent-line equation it is the height of a point on the *tangent line*, and putting $y = 0$ there found where that line meets the axis. Putting $y = 0$ into $y = 1/x$ instead would be a mistake. Such reuse of letters is everywhere, because it is convenient; so always keep track of which curve or line an equation describes.

<WorkedExample title="The triangle at x₀ = 4">
<Problem>
Find the tangent line to $y = 1/x$ at $x_0 = 4$, where it meets the axes, and the area of the triangle it forms with them.
</Problem>
<Step title="Point and slope">
$y_0 = 1/4$ and $f'(4) = -1/16$, so the tangent line is $y - \tfrac14 = -\tfrac{1}{16}(x - 4)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Intercepts">
Setting $y = 0$: $x = 4 + 16 \times \tfrac14 = 8 = 2x_0$. Setting $x = 0$: $y = \tfrac14 + \tfrac{4}{16} = \tfrac12 = 2y_0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Area">
$\tfrac12 \times 8 \times \tfrac12 = 2$, as promised.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Notations

Calculus was developed by several people, and their notations all survive. With $y = f(x)$, the change $\Delta y$ means the same as $\Delta f$, and in the limit $\Delta x \to 0$

<Formula label="Notations for the derivative" tex="\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \to \frac{dy}{dx} \;\;\text{(Leibniz)}, \qquad \frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} \to f'(x_0) \;\;\text{(Newton)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

Other forms in use are $\dfrac{df}{dx}$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}f$, $\dfrac{d}{dx}y$ and $Df$. They are used interchangeably. Leibniz's $dy/dx$ has one drawback: it does not show the point $x_0$ where the derivative is taken, so that has to be filled in from the context.

## Example: the derivative of xⁿ

Now take $f(x) = x^n$ for $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ To save writing, drop the subscript: $x$ is the fixed point and $\Delta x$ the change. The difference quotient needs $(x + \Delta x)^n$, the product of $n$ equal factors $(x + \Delta x)$. Multiplying out, the term $x^n$ comes from choosing $x$ in every factor, and a term $x^{n-1}\Delta x$ comes from choosing $\Delta x$ in exactly one factor, which can happen in $n$ ways. Everything else contains $\Delta x$ at least twice. This is the start of the **binomial theorem**:

<Formula label="Binomial expansion, first terms" tex="(x + \Delta x)^n = x^n + n\,x^{n-1}\,\Delta x + O\bigl((\Delta x)^2\bigr)" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

where $O((\Delta x)^2)$ stands for all the terms with $(\Delta x)^2$, $(\Delta x)^3$ and so on: "junk", as Jerison calls it, because it will disappear. Subtract $x^n$ and divide by $\Delta x$:

<Formula label="Difference quotient of xⁿ" tex="\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} = \frac{(x + \Delta x)^n - x^n}{\Delta x} = n\,x^{n-1} + O(\Delta x)" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

As $\Delta x \to 0$ the junk vanishes:

<Formula label="Power rule" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\,x^n = n\,x^{n-1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

The rule extends straight away to polynomials, term by term. For example, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left(4x^3 + x^6\right) = 12x^2 + 6x^5$. (Why one may differentiate a sum term by term, and pull out constant factors, comes in the next lectures.) For $n = 2$ the rule gives $2x$, which at $x = 3$ is the 6 found above.

## Rates of change: the physical meaning

The same limit has a second meaning: the derivative is a **rate of change**. The lecture notes illustrate it with MIT's Halloween tradition of dropping pumpkins from the roof of a tall building. If $y(t)$ is the height of a falling object at time $t$, the difference quotient $\Delta y/\Delta t$ is its **average velocity** over the interval $\Delta t$ (its size is the distance travelled divided by the time taken: the average speed), and the derivative $dy/dt$ is its **instantaneous velocity**: how fast it is going at a single moment. The notes take the equation of motion near the Earth's surface as given for now; the physics course derives it.

<WorkedExample title="A dropped stone">
<Problem>
A stone is dropped from a cliff 44.1 m high. Its height above the ground is $y = 44.1 - 4.9t^2$ (metres, with $t$ in seconds). How long does it take to land, what is its average speed on the way down, and how fast is it going when it lands?
</Problem>
<Step title="Time to land">
The stone lands when $y = 0$: $4.9t^2 = 44.1$, so $t^2 = 9$ and $t = 3$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Average speed">
It falls 44.1 m in 3 s: an average of $44.1/3 = 14.7$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed on landing">
By the power rule, $y'(t) = -9.8\,t$, so at $t = 3$ s, $y' = -29.4$ m/s. The minus sign says the height is decreasing: the stone is moving down, at 29.4 m/s, twice its average speed, because it started from rest and sped up steadily.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology">
Cosmology is full of rates of change. The size of the universe is described by a scale factor $a(t)$, and how fast the universe is expanding today is set by its derivative: the **Hubble rate** is $H = \dot a/a$, where the dot is yet another notation for a derivative, with respect to time ($\dot a = da/dt$). Measuring the expansion of the universe means measuring a derivative.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The derivative $f'(x_0)$ is the slope of the tangent line to $y = f(x)$ at $x_0$. The tangent line is the limit of secant lines $PQ$ as $Q \to P$, so $f'(x_0) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \bigl(f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)\bigr)/\Delta x$, the limit of the difference quotient.
- Never put $\Delta x = 0$ straight in (that gives $0/0$): simplify and cancel first, then take the limit.
- From the definition: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac1x = -\dfrac{1}{x^2}$, and $\dfrac{d}{dx}x^n = nx^{n-1}$ (from the binomial theorem), which extends to polynomials.
- The tangent line at $x_0$ is $y - y_0 = f'(x_0)(x - x_0)$. Tangents to $y = 1/x$ always cut off a triangle of area 2 with the axes.
- Notations: $f'$ (Newton), $df/dx$ and $dy/dx$ (Leibniz), $Df$.
- Physically, a derivative is a rate of change: $\Delta y/\Delta t$ is an average velocity, $dy/dt$ the instantaneous velocity.
