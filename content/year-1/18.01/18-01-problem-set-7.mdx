Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `C/sqrt(2)` or `2*pi^2*a^2*b`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. The answer of Problems 3(b) and 4(a) is an antiderivative, which is fixed only up to a constant. So that the box can check it, give the antiderivative that is zero at $x = 0$: that condition is ours. A number box wants a decimal number, and its question says how exact your value must be. The points per problem are MIT's, and so are the splits of Problems 3 and 4. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem or part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Parts that ask you to show something have no answer box: compare your work with the solution and mark it yourself. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The voltage a meter shows" points={4} mit="Part II, Problem 1">

The voltage of the electricity supply in a house swings back and forth. MIT models it by

<Formula label="Voltage of house current" tex="V(t) = C\,\sin(120\pi t)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1, p. 1" />

Here $t$ is the time in seconds and $C$ is a constant, the amplitude: the peak voltage is $\pm C$. A period (or cycle) is the time after which $V(t)$ repeats itself.

Take the average value of $V^2$ over one period, and then the square root of that average. The result is called the **root-mean-square** voltage, RMS for short. It is the voltage that a meter on the house shows. ($V^2$ is in square volts. The square root after averaging brings the result back to volts.)

Find the RMS voltage in terms of $C$.

<Hint>

First find the period. The sine repeats when its angle has grown by $2\pi$: how much time does that take here?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The average of a function over an interval is its integral divided by the length of the interval. Apply that to $V^2$ over one period.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The square of a sine is an even power, so use the lesson's half-angle formula for $\sin^2\theta$ to remove the square before you integrate.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-rms" label="RMS voltage $=$" expr="C/sqrt(2)"
  vars={{ C: [50, 400] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "C^2/2", hint: "That is the average of V² itself, in square volts. The RMS is the square root of it." },
    { expr: "C/2", hint: "You halved C. The half belongs to the average of V², which is C²/2, so the square root has to be taken of the 2 as well." },
    { expr: "2*C/pi", hint: "That is the average of the size of V, with its sign removed. The RMS squares V first, averages, and only then takes the root." },
    { expr: "C", hint: "That is the peak voltage. The voltage is below its peak nearly all the time, so the RMS must be smaller." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "That is the average of V itself: its positive and negative halves cancel. Square V before you average, so that nothing cancels." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Average $V^2$ over one period: integrate it and divide by the length of the period. The integrand is an even power of a sine, which the half-angle formula turns into a constant and a cosine.

<Step title="The period">

The sine repeats when its angle $120\pi t$ grows by $2\pi$. That takes a time $2\pi/(120\pi) = 1/60$ second, so one period runs from $t = 0$ to $t = 1/60$.

</Step>

<Step title="Remove the square">

The lesson's half-angle formula is

<Formula label="Half-angle formula for the sine" tex="\sin^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

With $\theta = 120\pi t$ the squared voltage becomes

<Formula label="The squared voltage without a square" tex="V^2 = C^2\sin^2(120\pi t) = \frac{C^2}{2}\left(1 - \cos(240\pi t)\right)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Average over one period">

Dividing by the length $1/60$ of the period is multiplying by 60:

<Formula label="Average of the squared voltage" tex="\text{average of } V^2 = 60\int_0^{1/60} \frac{C^2}{2}\left(1 - \cos(240\pi t)\right)dt = 30\,C^2\left[t - \frac{\sin(240\pi t)}{240\pi}\right]_0^{1/60} = 30\,C^2 \cdot \frac{1}{60} = \frac{C^2}{2}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

The sine term is zero at both ends, because $240\pi/60 = 4\pi$: the cosine goes through two full cycles and averages to nothing.

</Step>

<Step title="Take the square root">

<Formula label="Root-mean-square voltage" tex="V_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{C^2}{2}} = \frac{C}{\sqrt2} \approx 0.7071\,C" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

(The name $V_{\text{rms}}$ is ours.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$C$ is in volts, and so is $C/\sqrt2$. The result lies between $0$ and the peak $C$, as an average of this kind must. As an example with our own number: a peak voltage of $C = 170$ volts gives a meter reading of $170/\sqrt2 = 120.2$ volts.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The volume of a torus" points={4} mit="Part II, Problem 2">

A disk of radius $a$ has its centre on the $x$-axis, at a distance $b$ from the origin:

<Formula label="The disk that is rotated" tex="(x - b)^2 + y^2 \le a^2" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2, p. 1" />

Rotating this disk around the $y$-axis sweeps out a solid ring, the shape of a doughnut. It is called a solid torus. Take $b \ge a$, so that the disk does not reach across the axis (that condition is our addition).

Find the volume $V$ of the torus by the method of shells, in terms of $a$ and $b$.

MIT's hint is to substitute for $x - b$. MIT also remarks on the result: it happens to equal the area of the disk times the distance that the centre of the disk travels in one turn. Use that only as a check afterwards. (MIT's sheet points to a page of the course textbook for this remark. That book is not used on this site.)

<Hint>

A shell at distance $x$ from the axis has circumference $2\pi x$ and thickness $dx$. Its height is the length of the vertical line through the disk at that $x$: the disk reaches below the $x$-axis as far as above it.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put $u = x - b$. The limits become symmetric about zero, and the integrand splits into two terms. One of them is an odd function of $u$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

What remains is the integral of $\sqrt{a^2 - u^2}$. The lesson on inverse substitution removes that root with $u = a\sin\theta$. Or read the integral as an area.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-volume" label="$V =$" expr="2*pi^2*a^2*b"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 2], b: [3, 6] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi^2*a^2*b", hint: "That is half the volume. Check the height of a shell: it runs from the bottom of the disk to the top, twice the square root. Check also the integral of the root from −a to a: it is the area of half a disk." },
    { expr: "4*pi^2*a^2*b", hint: "That is twice the volume. The integral of the square root from −a to a is the area of half a disk of radius a, not of a whole one." },
    { expr: "2*pi^2*a*b^2", hint: "Here a and b have changed places. A thicker ring (larger a, same b) must gain volume like the area of the disk, which goes as a²." },
    { expr: "4*pi*a^2*b", hint: "One factor π is missing. One π comes from the circumference of the shells, and a second one from the integral of the square root, which is part of the area of a circle." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Cut the torus into thin cylindrical shells around the $y$-axis. Shifting the variable to the centre of the disk makes the integral symmetric, and what is left is the area of half a disk.

<Step title="One shell">

The lesson on volumes builds a shell from its height, its circumference $2\pi x$ and its thickness $dx$:

<Formula label="Volume of a thin shell" tex="dV = (\text{height})\,(2\pi x)\,dx" cite="reading5" at="p. 3" />

The vertical line at $x$ cuts the disk between $y = -\sqrt{a^2 - (x - b)^2}$ and $y = +\sqrt{a^2 - (x - b)^2}$, so the height is twice the root. The disk covers the values of $x$ from $b - a$ to $b + a$:

<Formula label="The torus by shells" tex="V = \int_{b-a}^{b+a} 2\pi x \cdot 2\sqrt{a^2 - (x - b)^2}\,dx" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Shift to the centre of the disk">

Put $u = x - b$, so $x = u + b$ and $dx = du$. The limits become $-a$ and $a$:

<Formula label="After the shift" tex="V = 4\pi\int_{-a}^{a} (u + b)\sqrt{a^2 - u^2}\,du = 4\pi\int_{-a}^{a} u\sqrt{a^2 - u^2}\,du + 4\pi b\int_{-a}^{a} \sqrt{a^2 - u^2}\,du" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

The first integrand, $u\sqrt{a^2 - u^2}$, is an odd function of $u$: its values at $u$ and $-u$ are opposite. Over an interval that is symmetric about zero it integrates to zero.

</Step>

<Step title="The remaining integral">

Use the trigonometric substitution of the lesson, $u = a\sin\theta$ with $du = a\cos\theta\,d\theta$, which turns the root into $a\cos\theta$ (Lecture 28 notes, p. 1). As $u$ runs from $-a$ to $a$, $\theta$ runs from $-\pi/2$ to $\pi/2$, where $\cos\theta \ge 0$:

<Formula label="The area of half a disk" tex="\int_{-a}^{a} \sqrt{a^2 - u^2}\,du = a^2\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} \cos^2\theta\,d\theta = a^2\left[\frac{\theta}{2} + \frac{\sin 2\theta}{4}\right]_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} = \frac{\pi a^2}{2}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

This is the area under the upper half of a circle of radius $a$, so $\pi a^2/2$ was to be expected.

</Step>

<Step title="The volume">

<Formula label="Volume of the torus" tex="V = 4\pi b \cdot \frac{\pi a^2}{2} = 2\pi^2 a^2 b" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The area of the disk is $\pi a^2$, and in one turn its centre travels along a circle of radius $b$, a distance $2\pi b$. Their product is $(\pi a^2)(2\pi b) = 2\pi^2a^2b$, as MIT's remark says. The units fit: $a^2 b$ is a length cubed. A second route gives the same (our own check): cut the torus into flat rings at height $y$, with outer radius $b + \sqrt{a^2 - y^2}$ and inner radius $b - \sqrt{a^2 - y^2}$. The area of a ring is $\pi$ times the difference of the squares of the radii, $4\pi b\sqrt{a^2 - y^2}$, and integrating that from $y = -a$ to $a$ gives $4\pi b \cdot \pi a^2/2$ again. With our own numbers $a = 1$ and $b = 3$ the volume is $6\pi^2 = 59.22$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A reduction formula for powers of the tangent" points={4} mit="Part II, Problem 3">

A reduction formula expresses an integral that contains a power in terms of the same integral with a lower power. The lesson on integration by parts has two of them. This one needs only a trigonometric identity and a substitution.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Let $n$ be a whole number, $n \ge 0$. Use the identity $\tan^2 x = \sec^2 x - 1$ to show that

<Formula label="Reduction formula for the tangent" tex="\int \tan^{n+2}x\,dx = \frac{1}{n+1}\tan^{n+1}x - \int \tan^n x\,dx" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(a), p. 1" />

<Hint>

Split off two of the $n + 2$ factors: $\tan^{n+2}x = \tan^n x \cdot \tan^2 x$. Use the identity on the $\tan^2 x$ only.

</Hint>

<Hint>

One of the two integrals you get contains $\sec^2 x\,dx$. Of which function is $\sec^2 x$ the derivative?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Trade two factors of the tangent for $\sec^2 x - 1$. The piece with $\sec^2 x$ can be integrated by substitution, because $\sec^2 x$ is the derivative of $\tan x$. The other piece is the integral with the lower power.

<Step title="Use the identity on two factors">

<Formula label="Splitting the power" tex="\int \tan^{n+2}x\,dx = \int \tan^n x\left(\sec^2 x - 1\right)dx = \int \tan^n x\,\sec^2 x\,dx - \int \tan^n x\,dx" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

The identity is the lesson's $\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x$ (Lecture 26 notes, p. 2), with the $1$ moved to the other side.

</Step>

<Step title="Substitute in the first integral">

Put $u = \tan x$, so $du = \sec^2 x\,dx$:

<Formula label="The integral with the squared secant" tex="\int \tan^n x\,\sec^2 x\,dx = \int u^n\,du = \frac{u^{n+1}}{n+1} + c = \frac{\tan^{n+1}x}{n+1} + c" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

The power rule needs $n + 1 \ne 0$, which holds because $n \ge 0$. Putting this into the first step gives the formula. (The constant $c$ is not written in the formula: it is taken up in the integral that remains.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate both sides. On the left the derivative is $\tan^{n+2}x$. On the right the chain rule gives $\tan^n x\,\sec^2 x - \tan^n x = \tan^n x\,(\sec^2 x - 1) = \tan^{n+2}x$. For $n = 0$ the formula says $\int \tan^2 x\,dx = \tan x - x + c$, and indeed the derivative of $\tan x - x$ is $\sec^2 x - 1 = \tan^2 x$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Use the formula of part (a) to find $\int \tan^4 x\,dx$. In the box, give the antiderivative that is zero at $x = 0$.

<Hint>

Which $n$ puts $\tan^4 x$ on the left of the formula? What integral is then left on the right?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the formula once more on the integral that is left, until the power of the tangent is zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$\int \tan^4 x\,dx =$" expr="tan(x)^3/3 - tan(x) + x"
  vars={{ x: [0.2, 1.3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "tan(x)^3/3 - tan(x) - x", hint: "Watch the brackets. The integral of tan² x is tan x − x, and all of it is subtracted, so the x comes back with a plus sign." },
    { expr: "tan(x)^3/3 - tan(x)", hint: "This stops one step too early. With n = 0 the formula still leaves the integral of the zeroth power of tan x, which is the integral of 1." },
    { expr: "tan(x)^3/3 + tan(x) + x", hint: "The formula of part (a) has a minus sign in front of the integral that is left. Follow that sign through both uses of the formula." },
    { expr: "tan(x)^5/5", hint: "The power rule is for powers of x itself. By the chain rule the derivative of this is tan⁴ x times sec² x, not tan⁴ x." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Use the formula twice: with $n = 2$ to go from the fourth power to the second, and with $n = 0$ to go from the second power to the integral of $1$.

<Step title="From the fourth power to the second">

With $n = 2$:

<Formula label="The formula with n = 2" tex="\int \tan^4 x\,dx = \frac13\tan^3 x - \int \tan^2 x\,dx" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="From the second power to a constant">

With $n = 0$, where $\tan^0 x = 1$:

<Formula label="The formula with n = 0" tex="\int \tan^2 x\,dx = \tan x - \int 1\,dx = \tan x - x + c" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put them together">

<Formula label="Integral of the fourth power of the tangent" tex="\int \tan^4 x\,dx = \frac13\tan^3 x - \tan x + x + c" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

At $x = 0$ the three terms are zero, so the antiderivative that the box asks for has $c = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate: $\tan^2 x\,\sec^2 x - \sec^2 x + 1 = \tan^2 x\,\sec^2 x - \tan^2 x = \tan^2 x\,(\sec^2 x - 1) = \tan^4 x$. A number as a second check (our own): from $x = 0$ to $\pi/4$, where $\tan x$ rises from $0$ to $1$, the formula gives $\tfrac13 - 1 + \tfrac{\pi}{4} = 0.1187$. That is positive and well below $\pi/4 = 0.785$, as it must be: on this interval $\tan^4 x$ lies between $0$ and $1$, and the interval has length $\pi/4$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="The integral of the secant by partial fractions" points={4} mit="Part II, Problem 4">

The lessons find $\int \sec x\,dx$ by a trick: multiply and divide by $\sec x + \tan x$. Here you find it by a route that needs no trick, only a substitution and partial fractions.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Check that

<Formula label="The secant rewritten" tex="\sec x = \frac{\cos x}{1 - \sin^2 x}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(a), p. 2" />

Then find $\int \sec x\,dx$ by making a substitution for $\sin x$ and using partial fractions. The result must be written in terms of $x$.

In the box, give the antiderivative that is zero at $x = 0$, for $-\pi/2 < x < \pi/2$. The box accepts every correct form, so it cannot see whether you used this route: mark that yourself with the solution.

<Hint>

For the check: what is $1 - \sin^2 x$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put $u = \sin x$. The $\cos x$ on top is just what $du$ needs, and what is left is a fraction in $u$ with no trigonometry in it.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Factor the denominator $1 - u^2$ and split the fraction into two, with the cover-up method. Take care with the sign when you integrate a fraction with $1 - u$ underneath.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$\int \sec x\,dx =$" expr="(1/2)*log((1 + sin(x))/(1 - sin(x)))"
  vars={{ x: [0.2, 1.3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(1/2)*log((1 - sin(x))/(1 + sin(x)))", hint: "The fraction is upside down, which makes this minus the right answer. The integral of 1/(1 − u) is −ln|1 − u|: the chain rule brings in a minus sign." },
    { expr: "log((1 + sin(x))/(1 - sin(x)))", hint: "That is twice the right answer. Each of the two partial fractions has a coefficient: find it with the cover-up method." },
    { expr: "(1/2)*log(1 - sin(x)^2)", hint: "This equals ln cos x, whose derivative is −tan x. The two logarithms must come with opposite signs, because the integral of 1/(1 − u) is −ln|1 − u|." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $u = \sin x$ the integral of the secant becomes the integral of $1/(1 - u^2)$, a rational function. Partial fractions split it into two fractions that each integrate to a logarithm.

<Step title="Check the rewriting">

Since $1 - \sin^2 x = \cos^2 x$,

<Formula label="Checking the rewritten secant" tex="\frac{\cos x}{1 - \sin^2 x} = \frac{\cos x}{\cos^2 x} = \frac{1}{\cos x} = \sec x" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Substitute for the sine">

Put $u = \sin x$, so $du = \cos x\,dx$:

<Formula label="The secant integral as a rational integral" tex="\int \sec x\,dx = \int \frac{\cos x\,dx}{1 - \sin^2 x} = \int \frac{du}{1 - u^2}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Partial fractions">

The denominator factors as $(1 - u)(1 + u)$, so set up

<Formula label="Set-up of the partial fractions" tex="\frac{1}{(1 - u)(1 + u)} = \frac{A}{1 - u} + \frac{B}{1 + u}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

The cover-up method of the lesson (Lecture 29 notes, pp. 1–2) gives the coefficients. For $A$, cover the factor $1 - u$ and put $u = 1$ in what is left: $A = 1/(1 + 1) = \tfrac12$. For $B$, cover $1 + u$ and put $u = -1$: $B = 1/(1 - (-1)) = \tfrac12$.

</Step>

<Step title="Integrate">

<Formula label="Integrating the two fractions" tex="\int \frac{du}{1 - u^2} = \frac12\int \frac{du}{1 - u} + \frac12\int \frac{du}{1 + u} = -\frac12\ln|1 - u| + \frac12\ln|1 + u| + c" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

The minus sign of the first logarithm comes from the chain rule: the derivative of $\ln|1 - u|$ is $-1/(1 - u)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Back to x">

Combine the logarithms and put $u = \sin x$:

<Formula label="Integral of the secant, first form" tex="\int \sec x\,dx = \frac12\ln\left|\frac{1 + \sin x}{1 - \sin x}\right| + c" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

For $-\pi/2 < x < \pi/2$ both $1 + \sin x$ and $1 - \sin x$ are positive, so the bars can be left out there. At $x = 0$ the fraction is $1$ and its logarithm is $0$: the antiderivative for the box has $c = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate $\tfrac12\ln(1 + \sin x) - \tfrac12\ln(1 - \sin x)$:

<Formula label="Differentiating the first form" tex="\frac12\cdot\frac{\cos x}{1 + \sin x} + \frac12\cdot\frac{\cos x}{1 - \sin x} = \frac{\cos x}{2}\cdot\frac{(1 - \sin x) + (1 + \sin x)}{1 - \sin^2 x} = \frac{\cos x}{\cos^2 x} = \sec x" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

The lessons give the integral of the secant in another form:

<Formula label="Integral of the secant, familiar form" tex="\int \sec x\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + c" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–4" />

(The notes print it without the bars.) Show that your result of part (a) is the same formula. MIT's hint: multiply the fraction inside the logarithm by $1 + \sin x$ on top and underneath, and remember that $\tfrac12\ln u = \ln\sqrt{u}$.

<Hint>

After multiplying, the bottom is $(1 - \sin x)(1 + \sin x)$. Which square is that?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Top and bottom are now both squares. The factor $\tfrac12$ in front of the logarithm takes the square root of the fraction.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Multiplying by $1 + \sin x$ turns the top and the bottom into squares. The factor $\tfrac12$ then removes the squares.

<Step title="Make squares">

<Formula label="The fraction as a square" tex="\frac{1 + \sin x}{1 - \sin x} = \frac{(1 + \sin x)^2}{(1 - \sin x)(1 + \sin x)} = \frac{(1 + \sin x)^2}{1 - \sin^2 x} = \left(\frac{1 + \sin x}{\cos x}\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Take the square root">

Half the logarithm of a square is the logarithm of the size of what was squared:

<Formula label="From the first form to the familiar form" tex="\frac12\ln\left(\frac{1 + \sin x}{\cos x}\right)^2 = \ln\left|\frac{1 + \sin x}{\cos x}\right| = \ln\left|\frac{1}{\cos x} + \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right| = \ln|\sec x + \tan x|" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Try a number (our own): at $x = \pi/6$, where $\sin x = \tfrac12$, the first form gives $\tfrac12\ln(1.5/0.5) = \tfrac12\ln 3 = 0.5493$. There $\sec x = 2/\sqrt3$ and $\tan x = 1/\sqrt3$, so the familiar form gives $\ln(3/\sqrt3) = \ln\sqrt3 = 0.5493$ as well.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="The first hump of the cosine, rotated" points={3} mit="Part II, Problem 5">

The graph of $y = \cos x$ starts at its top, $y = 1$ at $x = 0$, and comes down to the $x$-axis at $x = \pi/2$. Take the region under this first hump: below the graph, above the $x$-axis, for $0 \le x \le \pi/2$. (MIT's sheet says only "the first hump". That it means this region is our reading: rotating it around the $y$-axis also sweeps out the half of the hump on the left of the axis.)

Rotate the region around the $y$-axis. Find the volume $V$ of the solid by the method of shells. Give it as a decimal, correct to three decimal places.

<Hint>

A shell at distance $x$ from the axis has circumference $2\pi x$, thickness $dx$ and the height of the graph at that $x$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The integrand is a product of $x$ and a trigonometric function. Integrate by parts, and choose for $u$ the factor that becomes simpler when you differentiate it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-volume" label="$V =$" value="3.586" tolerance="0.002" calc="p5-volume" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each shell has volume $2\pi x\cos x\,dx$. The product $x\cos x$ is integrated by parts, with $x$ as the factor that is differentiated.

<Step title="Set up the shells">

A shell at $x$ has height $\cos x$, circumference $2\pi x$ and thickness $dx$ (Lecture 22 notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="The hump by shells" tex="V = \int_0^{\pi/2} 2\pi x\cos x\,dx" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Integrate by parts">

The lesson's formula is

<Formula label="Integration by parts" tex="\int u\,v'\,dx = u\,v - \int u'\,v\,dx" cite="reading4" at="p. 1" />

Take $u = x$ and $v' = \cos x$. Then $u' = 1$ and $v = \sin x$:

<Formula label="The product of x and the cosine" tex="\int x\cos x\,dx = x\sin x - \int \sin x\,dx = x\sin x + \cos x + c" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put in the limits">

<Formula label="Volume under the hump" tex="V = 2\pi\Big[x\sin x + \cos x\Big]_0^{\pi/2} = 2\pi\left(\frac{\pi}{2} - 1\right) = \pi^2 - 2\pi = 3.586" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

At $x = \pi/2$ the bracket is $\tfrac{\pi}{2}\cdot 1 + 0$, and at $x = 0$ it is $0 + 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The derivative of $x\sin x + \cos x$ is $\sin x + x\cos x - \sin x = x\cos x$, as it must be. The size is reasonable too. The solid fits inside a cylinder of radius $\pi/2$ and height $1$, with volume $\pi(\pi/2)^2 = 7.752$. It contains the cone with the same base and height, because the graph of the cosine lies above the straight line from $(0, 1)$ to $(\pi/2, 0)$; the cone has a third of the volume of the cylinder, $2.584$. And $3.586$ lies between the two.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>
