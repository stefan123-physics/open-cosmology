Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `x1*x2/(y1 - y2)` or `1/cos(alpha)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer that uses the letters named in the question. A number box says how close you have to be: type the number in the unit named in the question, and keep all the digits your calculator shows while you work. Unless a box asks for more digits, give 3 significant figures in the box; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. The points per problem and their split over the parts are MIT's. Only the split of Problem 2 into two parts is ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Parts that ask you to show or to explain something have no answer box, or only a box for one step: compare your work with the solution and mark it yourself. Your score is kept in this browser only.

<Exercise id="p2" title="A curve that cuts length 1 from every tangent" points={2} mit="Part II, Problem 2">

MIT's set calls the following curve a hypocycloid:

<Formula label="The curve of Problem 2" tex="x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = 1" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2, p. 1" />

Look only at the first quadrant, where $x > 0$ and $y > 0$. There the curve runs from $(0, 1)$ down to $(1, 0)$. Draw the tangent line at any point of that arc. The two axes cut a piece out of the line. Show that this piece has length 1, whichever point you chose.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

MIT's set says to use implicit differentiation. Find the slope $dy/dx$ of the curve at a point $(x, y)$ of the arc, written with $x$ and $y$.

Type the answer with both letters, as implicit differentiation gives it. (The box cannot know that $x$ and $y$ lie on the curve, so do not replace $y$ by its formula in $x$.)

<Hint>

Differentiate both sides of the equation of the curve with respect to $x$. Treat $y$ as a function of $x$: the chain rule then gives the derivative of $y^{2/3}$ a factor $dy/dx$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The power rule holds for the power $2/3$ as well: the power drops by one, to $-1/3$. Solve the equation you get for $dy/dx$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-slope" label="$\dfrac{dy}{dx} =$" expr="-(y/x)^(1/3)"
  vars={{ x: [0.1, 0.9], y: [0.1, 0.9] }}
  mistakes={[

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equation cannot be differentiated as $y = \ldots$ without first solving for $y$, but it can be differentiated as it stands.

<Step title="Differentiate the equation">

Take the derivative of both sides with respect to $x$. The power rule for fractional powers is the one the lesson on implicit differentiation derives:

<Formula label="The equation of the curve, differentiated" tex="\tfrac23\,x^{-1/3} + \tfrac23\,y^{-1/3}\,\frac{dy}{dx} = 0" cite="reading4" at="pp. 1–2 (the power rule for rational powers and the method of the circle example, used on this curve)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for the slope">

<Formula label="Slope of the curve" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x^{-1/3}}{y^{-1/3}} = -\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^{1/3}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The slope is negative, as it must be on an arc that goes down from $(0,1)$ to $(1,0)$. On the line $y = x$ it is $-1$, which fits a curve that looks the same when $x$ and $y$ are swapped. Near $(1, 0)$, where $y$ is small, the slope goes to zero: the curve arrives level at the $x$-axis.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

Now show that the axes cut a piece of length 1 from the tangent line at any point of the arc.

<Hint>

Give the point a name, for instance $(x_0, y_0)$, and write the equation of the tangent line through it with the slope of part (a).

</Hint>

<Hint>

Find where the line meets the $x$-axis (put $y = 0$) and where it meets the $y$-axis (put $x = 0$). The piece runs between these two points.

</Hint>

<Hint>

When you simplify the two crossings, take out a common factor and look for $x_0^{2/3} + y_0^{2/3}$. You know what that is.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the two points where the tangent line meets the axes. The piece between them is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle with its corner at the origin, so Pythagoras gives its length.

<Step title="The tangent line">

Let $(x_0, y_0)$ be a point of the arc, so $x_0^{2/3} + y_0^{2/3} = 1$. (The names $x_0$ and $y_0$ are ours.) With the slope of part (a) the tangent line is

<Formula label="Tangent line at a point of the curve" tex="y - y_0 = -\left(\frac{y_0}{x_0}\right)^{1/3}(x - x_0)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Where it meets the x-axis">

Put $y = 0$ and solve for $x$:

<Formula label="Crossing of the x-axis" tex="x = x_0 + y_0\left(\frac{x_0}{y_0}\right)^{1/3} = x_0 + x_0^{1/3}\,y_0^{2/3} = x_0^{1/3}\left(x_0^{2/3} + y_0^{2/3}\right) = x_0^{1/3}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

The last step uses that the point lies on the curve.

</Step>

<Step title="Where it meets the y-axis">

Put $x = 0$:

<Formula label="Crossing of the y-axis" tex="y = y_0 + x_0\left(\frac{y_0}{x_0}\right)^{1/3} = y_0 + x_0^{2/3}\,y_0^{1/3} = y_0^{1/3}\left(x_0^{2/3} + y_0^{2/3}\right) = y_0^{1/3}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The length of the piece">

The piece runs from $(x_0^{1/3}, 0)$ to $(0, y_0^{1/3})$. By Pythagoras its length squared is

<Formula label="Length of the piece of tangent line" tex="\left(x_0^{1/3}\right)^2 + \left(y_0^{1/3}\right)^2 = x_0^{2/3} + y_0^{2/3} = 1" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

So the length is 1, for every point of the arc.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Try the point with $x_0 = 1/8$. Then $x_0^{2/3} = 1/4$, so $y_0^{2/3} = 3/4$ and $y_0 = 0.6495$. The crossings are at $x_0^{1/3} = 0.5$ on the $x$-axis and at $y_0^{1/3} = 0.8660$ on the $y$-axis. The length is $\sqrt{0.25 + 0.75} = 1$. The slope of this tangent line is $-0.8660/0.5 = -1.732$, and part (a) gives the same: $-(0.6495/0.125)^{1/3} = -1.732$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="How sensitive is a measurement? The derivative answers" points={7} mit="Part II, Problem 3">

This problem returns to the flat planet Quirk of MIT's Problem Set 1. Here is what you need from there.

A navigation satellite hovers 20,000 km above the flat ground. A receiver on the ground measures $h$, the straight-line distance from the satellite to itself. From $h$ it works out $L$, the distance along the ground from the point directly below the satellite to itself. The three lengths are the sides of a right-angled triangle, so

<Formula label="The measured distance and the position" tex="L^2 + 20{,}000^2 = h^2" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(a), p. 1" />

All lengths are in kilometres. An error $\Delta h$ in the measured distance gives an error $\Delta L$ in the position. In Problem Set 1 the ratio $\Delta L/\Delta h$ was worked out with a calculator, for $\Delta h = \pm 1$, $\pm 0.1$ and $\pm 0.01$ km around two values of $h$. These were the results (our own calculation):

| $\Delta h$ (km) | $\Delta L/\Delta h$ near $h = 25{,}000$ | $\Delta L/\Delta h$ near $h = 20{,}001$ |
|---|---|---|
| $+1$ | 1.66661 | 82.85 |
| $-1$ | 1.66673 | 200.00 |
| $+0.1$ | 1.666661 | 97.62 |
| $-0.1$ | 1.666673 | 102.64 |
| $+0.01$ | 1.6666661 | 99.76 |
| $-0.01$ | 1.6666673 | 100.25 |

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Use implicit differentiation to find $dL/dh$. Type it with the letters $h$ and $L$. (The form with $h$ alone is right too, but this box checks the form with $L$.)

Then compare. The linear approximation says that $\Delta L/\Delta h$ is close to $dL/dh$. How well does that fit the table?

Next, explain why

<Formula label="The difference quotient stays below the derivative" tex="\frac{\Delta L}{\Delta h} \le \frac{dL}{dh} \qquad (\Delta h > 0)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(a), p. 1" />

when the derivative is taken at the starting value of $h$. A measurement $h \pm \Delta h$ only says that the true distance lies somewhere in an interval. MIT's set calls it the interval of uncertainty. The statement then reads: over the whole interval, the ratio is at most the derivative at the left end of the interval.

Last, for which values of $h$ does this guarantee $|\Delta L| \le 2\,|\Delta h|$?

<Hint>

Differentiate both sides of $L^2 + 20{,}000^2 = h^2$ with respect to $h$. Here $L$ is a function of $h$, and $20{,}000^2$ is a constant.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the inequality, take two distances $h_0 < h_1$ with their positions $L_0$ and $L_1$. Subtract the two Pythagoras equations and factor both sides as a difference of two squares. That gives $\Delta L/\Delta h$ exactly.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the last question, ask where the derivative itself is at most 2. Write it with $h$ alone and solve the inequality for $h$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a-deriv" label="$\dfrac{dL}{dh} =$" expr="h/L"
  vars={{ h: [20500, 60000], L: [500, 50000] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "L/h", hint: "That is upside down. Differentiating gives 2L dL/dh = 2h, so L ends up below. A test: the receiver is almost under the satellite when L is small, and there your ratio is small, while the table shows large ratios." },
    { expr: "-h/L", hint: "Check the sign. A receiver that is further from the satellite is also further along the ground, so L grows with h and the derivative is positive. Yours is negative." },
    { expr: "2*h", hint: "That is the derivative of the right-hand side only. The left-hand side gives 2L times dL/dh by the chain rule: divide by 2L." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-a-near" label="$dL/dh$ at $h = 20{,}001$ km:" value="100.0" tolerance="0.1%" calc="p3-a-derivative-20001" />

<Answer id="p3-a-range" label="$|\Delta L| \le 2|\Delta h|$ is guaranteed when the whole interval lies at or above $h =$" value="23094" unit="km" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p3-a-bound-2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The derivative is the limit of the ratios in the table, and it can be found without solving for $L$. The graph of $L$ against $h$ gets less steep as $h$ grows, which is what puts the difference quotient below the derivative at the left end.

<Step title="Implicit differentiation">

Differentiate $L^2 + 20{,}000^2 = h^2$ with respect to $h$. MIT's notes for Lecture 12 do the same with a general height in place of 20,000 km:

<Formula label="Sensitivity of the position" tex="2L\,\frac{dL}{dh} = 2h \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dL}{dh} = \frac{h}{L}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4 (Figure 4 and the lines below it; there with time derivatives)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compare with the table">

At $h = 25{,}000$ the position is $L = 15{,}000$, so $dL/dh = 25{,}000/15{,}000 = 1.666667$. All six ratios in the first column of the table differ from this by less than 0.004%, and they close in on it as $\Delta h$ shrinks.

At $h = 20{,}001$ the position is $L = \sqrt{40{,}001} = 200.0025$, so $dL/dh = 20{,}001/200.0025 = 100.0$. The ratios for $\Delta h = \pm 0.01$ are within 0.3% of this. Those for $\Delta h = \pm 1$ are far off: 82.85 and 200.00. The linear approximation is good only while $\Delta h$ is small compared with the distance over which the slope changes, and close to $h = 20{,}000$ the slope changes very quickly.

</Step>

<Step title="The difference quotient, exactly">

Take $h_0 < h_1$, with positions $L_0$ and $L_1$. Subtracting $L_0^2 + 20{,}000^2 = h_0^2$ from $L_1^2 + 20{,}000^2 = h_1^2$ gives $L_1^2 - L_0^2 = h_1^2 - h_0^2$, that is $(L_1 - L_0)(L_1 + L_0) = (h_1 - h_0)(h_1 + h_0)$. So

<Formula label="Exact difference quotient" tex="\frac{\Delta L}{\Delta h} = \frac{L_1 - L_0}{h_1 - h_0} = \frac{h_1 + h_0}{L_1 + L_0}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Why it stays below the derivative at the left end">

The derivative, written with $h$ alone, is

<Formula label="The derivative as a function of the distance" tex="\frac{dL}{dh} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{h^2 - 20{,}000^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 20{,}000^2/h^2}}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

As $h$ grows, $20{,}000^2/h^2$ shrinks, the square root grows and the whole fraction falls. So $h_1/L_1 \le h_0/L_0$, or $h_1 L_0 \le h_0 L_1$. Add $h_0 L_0$ to both sides: $(h_1 + h_0)\,L_0 \le h_0\,(L_1 + L_0)$. Divide by the positive numbers $L_0$ and $L_1 + L_0$:

<Formula label="Bound by the derivative at the left end" tex="\frac{\Delta L}{\Delta h} = \frac{h_1 + h_0}{L_1 + L_0} \le \frac{h_0}{L_0}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

The right-hand side is $dL/dh$ at $h_0$, the left end. In a picture: the graph of $L$ against $h$ bends downward, because its slope $h/L$ falls. A chord of such a graph is less steep than the tangent line at its left end.

</Step>

<Step title="Where the error is at most doubled">

The bound just found gives $|\Delta L| \le 2|\Delta h|$ whenever the derivative at the left end of the interval is at most 2:

<Formula label="Where the derivative is at most 2" tex="\frac{h}{\sqrt{h^2 - 20{,}000^2}} \le 2 \iff h^2 \le 4\left(h^2 - 20{,}000^2\right) \iff h \ge \frac{40{,}000}{\sqrt3} = 23{,}094 \text{ km}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

So the estimate holds when the whole interval of uncertainty lies at or above $h = 23{,}094$ km.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The value $h = 25{,}000$ lies in this range, and Problem Set 1 found that 2 was a safe round number there. The value $h = 20{,}001$ does not, and there the errors were multiplied by about 100.

The inequality can be seen in the table too. For $\Delta h = +1$ at $h = 25{,}000$ the ratio is 1.66661, just under the derivative at the left end, 1.666667. For $\Delta h = -1$ the interval runs from 24,999 to 25,000. Its left end is 24,999, where $dL/dh = 1.666785$, and the ratio 1.66673 is under that. Near $h = 20{,}001$ with $\Delta h = -1$ the left end is $h = 20{,}000$, where $L = 0$ and the derivative is infinite: there the inequality allows any ratio, which fits the large value in the table.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Now suppose Quirk is not only flat but has one dimension: it is a straight line, and $x$ measures position along it. Several satellites hover at a height of 20,000 km. Your receiver reports:

- satellite 1 is directly above the point $x_1$, known to within $10^{-10}$, at a distance $h_1 = 21{,}000 \pm 10^{-2}$ from you;
- satellite 2 is directly above the point $x_2$, known to within $10^{-10}$, at a distance $h_2 = 52{,}000 \pm 10^{-2}$ from you.

MIT's set gives no units for these error margins. We read all of them in kilometres, like the height.

Where are you, and how accurately do you know it? MIT's set gives a hint: treat the case in which satellite 1 is to the left of satellite 2 ($x_1 < x_2$) apart from the case in which it is to the right. (As printed, the hint names the first case twice.)

<Hint>

Each satellite tells you how far you are from the point below it, but not on which side. Work out the two ground distances first. We call them $L_1$ and $L_2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Compare $L_1$ and $L_2$. Which points of the line are nearer to $x_1$ than to $x_2$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the accuracy, use the linear approximation $\Delta L \approx (dL/dh)\,\Delta h$ with the derivative of part (a), once for each satellite. Which satellite pins you down better?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b-l2" label="Ground distance from the point below satellite 2, $L_2 =$" value="48000" unit="km" tolerance="0.1%" calc="p3-b-ground-2" />

<Answer id="p3-b-side" label="If $x_1 < x_2$, your position is"
  choices={["x2 - 48,000 km", "x2 + 48,000 km", "either of the two: the readings cannot tell"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p3-b-acc" label="The best accuracy of your position, in metres: plus or minus" value="10.8" unit="m" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-b-error-2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each reading puts you at a known ground distance from the point below its satellite. Two readings settle on which side you are. The derivative $h/L$ turns the error in each $h$ into an error in the position, and the better of the two readings sets the accuracy.

<Step title="The two ground distances">

<Formula label="Ground distances from the two satellites" tex="L_1 = \sqrt{21{,}000^2 - 20{,}000^2} = 6403.12 \text{ km}, \qquad L_2 = \sqrt{52{,}000^2 - 20{,}000^2} = 48{,}000 \text{ km}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(b), pp. 1–2 (MIT's numbers, our own solution)" />

The second triangle is a 5, 12, 13 triangle, enlarged 4000 times. So you are 6403.12 km from $x_1$ and 48,000 km from $x_2$.

</Step>

<Step title="On which side?">

Suppose $x_1 < x_2$. You are much closer to $x_1$ than to $x_2$. A point to the right of $x_2$ is closer to $x_2$ than to anything on its left, so you are to the left of $x_2$:

<Formula label="Your position on the line" tex="x = x_2 - 48{,}000 \quad (x_1 < x_2), \qquad x = x_2 + 48{,}000 \quad (x_1 > x_2)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

The second case is the mirror image of the first. One satellite alone could not have told you this.

The readings also have to fit each other. If $x_1 < x_2$, you are either between the two points, and then $x_2 - x_1 = L_1 + L_2 = 54{,}403$ km, or to the left of both, and then $x_2 - x_1 = L_2 - L_1 = 41{,}597$ km. Either way $x = x_2 - 48{,}000$.

</Step>

<Step title="The accuracy from each satellite">

By part (a), an error $\Delta h$ in a distance gives an error of about $(h/L)\,\Delta h$ in the ground distance:

<Formula label="Errors in the two ground distances" tex="\Delta L_1 \approx \frac{21{,}000}{6403.12}\times 10^{-2} = 3.28\times 10^{-2} \text{ km}, \qquad \Delta L_2 \approx \frac{52{,}000}{48{,}000}\times 10^{-2} = 1.083\times 10^{-2} \text{ km}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

That is 32.8 m from satellite 1 and 10.8 m from satellite 2. The uncertainty of $10^{-10}$ km in $x_1$ and $x_2$ is a ten-thousandth of a millimetre and plays no part.

</Step>

<Step title="The answer">

Both readings are true, so you are inside both ranges. The narrower one is that of satellite 2:

<Formula label="Position and accuracy" tex="x = x_2 \mp 48{,}000 \pm 0.011 \text{ km}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

with the upper sign of $\mp$ for $x_1 < x_2$. You know where you are to about 11 metres.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The satellite that is far away gives the better position, by a factor of three, although both distances are measured equally well. The reason is the derivative $h/L$: it is 3.28 for satellite 1, which stands high in your sky, and 1.083 for satellite 2, which is low. Part (c) makes this precise.

The linear approximation is safe here. The distance $h_2$ changes by 0.01 km out of 52,000 km, and over so short a stretch $h/L$ does not change in its first seven figures.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

Let $\alpha$ be the angle between your line of sight to the satellite and the horizontal ground. (The name $\alpha$ is ours.) Express $dL/dh$ in terms of $\alpha$.

MIT's set adds a remark, which we do not prove here: written with this angle, the formula also holds on a curved planet like Earth.

<Step title="Check the result">

The linear approximation, without the last term, is $2\,(1 + \tfrac1{24}) = 2.08333$. That is exactly Newton's $x_1$ of part (a), and not by chance. Both follow the same tangent line: the graph of the cube root is the graph of $y = x^3$ with the two axes swapped, and the point $(2, 8)$ of one is the point $(8, 2)$ of the other. Its error is $3.2\times10^{-3}$, so the quadratic term has brought the error down about 15 times. Newton's method gains more per step: its second value, $x_2$, is already off by only $5.1\times10^{-6}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 6 (optional)

<Exercise id="p6" title="The curve swept out by a sliding ladder" points={8} mit="Part II, Problem 6" optional>

MIT's extra-credit problem builds the curve of Problem 2 from a moving ladder. MIT's set warns that this takes a good deal longer than Problem 2, and promises an easier view of the same curve later in the course.

Picture the first quadrant of the $xy$-plane as the space to the right of a wall: the positive $x$-axis is the ground and the positive $y$-axis is the wall. A ladder of length 1 stands upright against the wall. Its foot, at first at $x = 0$, slides to the right along the ground until the ladder lies flat. All the while its top slides down the wall, and it ends at the origin. A camera with its shutter open the whole time would record a blurred region: all the points the ladder has passed over. This problem finds the edge of that region.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Take two straight segments: ladder 1 from $(0, y_1)$ to $(x_1, 0)$ and ladder 2 from $(0, y_2)$ to $(x_2, 0)$. Find the point $(x_3, y_3)$ where they cross. The formulas hold all four of $x_1$, $x_2$, $y_1$ and $y_2$; simplify them as far as you can.

In the boxes, type `x1`, `x2`, `y1`, `y2`.

MIT's set lists four ways to test your formulas before you go on. They are in the third hint.

<Hint>

Write an equation for each line. A line that cuts the axes at $(x_1, 0)$ and $(0, y_1)$ can be written as $x/x_1 + y/y_1 = 1$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You now have two equations with the two unknowns $x$ and $y$. Get rid of $y$ to find $x_3$. Then you do not need to start again for $y_3$: what happens to the whole problem when every $x$ is swapped with the matching $y$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

MIT's four tests. (i) If $y_2 = 0$, ladder 2 lies on the ground and $x_3$ must be $x_1$. (ii) Swapping all $x$'s with $y$'s mirrors the picture in the line $y = x$, so it must turn the formula for $x_3$ into the one for $y_3$. (iii) Parallel ladders do not cross, so the denominator must be zero when the two slopes are equal. (iv) Multiplying all four numbers by the same factor enlarges the picture by that factor, so the numerator must have a degree one higher than the denominator.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-a-x" label="$x_3 =$" expr="x1*x2*(y1 - y2)/(x2*y1 - x1*y2)"
  vars={{ x1: [0.2, 0.4], x2: [0.6, 0.9], y1: [0.7, 0.95], y2: [0.2, 0.5] }}
  names={{ x1: "x_1", x2: "x_2", y1: "y_1", y2: "y_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x1*x2*(y2 - y1)/(x2*y1 - x1*y2)", hint: "Check the sign with MIT's first test: put y2 = 0. Ladder 2 then lies on the ground and the crossing must be at x1. Your formula gives -x1." },
    { expr: "y1*y2*(x2 - x1)/(x2*y1 - x1*y2)", hint: "That is the height of the crossing, y3. A test: put y2 = 0, so that ladder 2 lies on the ground. Your formula then gives 0, which is the height, while x3 must be x1." },
    { expr: "(y1 - y2)/(x2*y1 - x1*y2)", hint: "Check the scaling. If all four numbers are doubled, the picture doubles and so must x3. Your formula is divided by 2 instead: the factor x1*x2 is missing on top." },
    { expr: "x1*x2*(y1 - y2)/(x1*y1 - x2*y2)", hint: "Check the denominator with parallel ladders, for example x1 = 1, y1 = 2, x2 = 2, y2 = 4. They never cross, so the denominator must be 0. Yours is 2 - 8 = -6." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p6-a-y" label="$y_3 =$" expr="y1*y2*(x2 - x1)/(x2*y1 - x1*y2)"
  vars={{ x1: [0.2, 0.4], x2: [0.6, 0.9], y1: [0.7, 0.95], y2: [0.2, 0.5] }}
  names={{ x1: "x_1", x2: "x_2", y1: "y_1", y2: "y_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "y1*y2*(x1 - x2)/(x2*y1 - x1*y2)", hint: "Check the sign: put x2 = 0, so that ladder 2 stands against the wall. The crossing is then on the wall at height y1. Your formula gives -y1." },
    { expr: "x1*x2*(y1 - y2)/(x2*y1 - x1*y2)", hint: "That is x3 again. A test: put x2 = 0, so that ladder 2 stands against the wall. The crossing is then at height y1, but your formula gives 0." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each ladder lies on a straight line. Write both lines in the form that shows where they cut the axes, and solve the two equations together.

<Step title="The two lines">

<Formula label="The lines of the two ladders" tex="\frac{x}{x_1} + \frac{y}{y_1} = 1, \qquad \frac{x}{x_2} + \frac{y}{y_2} = 1" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(a), p. 2 (MIT's two segments, written as equations by us)" />

Each equation is true at both ends of its ladder, so it is the line through them.

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for the crossing">

Multiply the first equation by $y_1$ and the second by $y_2$, and subtract. The terms with $y$ cancel:

<Formula label="Eliminating y" tex="x\left(\frac{y_1}{x_1} - \frac{y_2}{x_2}\right) = y_1 - y_2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_3 = \frac{x_1 x_2\,(y_1 - y_2)}{x_2 y_1 - x_1 y_2}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(a) (our own solution)" />

Swapping every $x$ with the matching $y$ swaps the roles of the two axes, so it gives $y_3$ without new work:

<Formula label="Height of the crossing" tex="y_3 = \frac{y_1 y_2\,(x_1 - x_2)}{y_2 x_1 - y_1 x_2} = \frac{y_1 y_2\,(x_2 - x_1)}{x_2 y_1 - x_1 y_2}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

MIT's four tests. (i) With $y_2 = 0$: $x_3 = x_1 x_2 y_1/(x_2 y_1) = x_1$. (ii) The swap of $x$ and $y$ is the reflection in the line $y = x$, and it turns each formula into the other. (iii) The slopes are $-y_1/x_1$ and $-y_2/x_2$; they are equal when $x_2 y_1 = x_1 y_2$, which is when the denominator is zero. (iv) The numerators have degree 3 and the denominator degree 2.

With numbers: ladder 1 from $(0, 0.8)$ to $(0.6, 0)$ and ladder 2 from $(0, 0.6)$ to $(0.8, 0)$ are mirror images in the line $y = x$, so they must cross on that line. The formulas give $x_3 = 0.6 \times 0.8 \times 0.2/(0.64 - 0.36) = 0.343$ and $y_3 = 0.8 \times 0.6 \times 0.2/0.28 = 0.343$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Write the equation between $x_2$ and $y_2$ which says that ladder 2 has length 1. Now keep ladder 1 fixed and let ladder 2 move towards it:

<Formula label="Ladder 2 close to ladder 1" tex="x_2 = x_1 + \Delta x, \qquad y_2 = y_1 + \Delta y" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(b), p. 3" />

Use implicit differentiation to find the limit of $\Delta y/\Delta x$ as $\Delta x \to 0$, as a function of $x_1$ and $y_1$.

<Hint>

The ladder is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle whose other sides lie along the ground and the wall.

</Hint>

<Hint>

As the ladder slides, its top height is a function of the position of its foot. The limit of $\Delta y/\Delta x$ is the derivative of that function at ladder 1.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-b" label="$\lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} =$" expr="-x1/y1"
  vars={{ x1: [0.2, 0.9], y1: [0.2, 0.9] }}
  names={{ x1: "x_1", y1: "y_1" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x1/y1", hint: "Check the sign. When the foot slides to the right, the top comes down, so the change in y is negative while the change in x is positive. Your limit is positive." },
    { expr: "-y1/x1", hint: "That is the slope of the ladder itself. The question is how the top moves when the foot moves: differentiating x^2 + y^2 = 1 gives 2x + 2y dy/dx = 0, so y1 ends up below." },
    { expr: "y1/x1", hint: "That is the steepness of the ladder, without its sign. The question is how the top moves when the foot moves: differentiate x^2 + y^2 = 1 and solve for dy/dx. The result is negative, because the top comes down." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Every position of the ladder obeys Pythagoras. That ties the height of the top to the position of the foot, and the limit asked for is the derivative of one with respect to the other.

<Step title="The length of the ladder">

<Formula label="Ladder 2 has length 1" tex="x_2^2 + y_2^2 = 1" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(b) (our own solution)" />

The same holds for ladder 1: $x_1^2 + y_1^2 = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Implicit differentiation">

Along the motion, the foot $x$ and the top $y$ satisfy $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. This is the circle of the lesson on implicit differentiation:

<Formula label="How the top moves with the foot" tex="2x + 2y\,\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x_1}{y_1}" cite="reading4" at="p. 2 (the circle, done by implicit differentiation)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take ladder 1 with $x_1 = 0.6$ and $y_1 = 0.8$, and move the foot to $0.601$. The top is then at $\sqrt{1 - 0.601^2} = 0.799249$, so $\Delta y/\Delta x = -0.000751/0.001 = -0.751$. The formula gives $-0.6/0.8 = -0.75$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Put $x_2 = x_1 + \Delta x$ and $y_2 = y_1 + \Delta y$ into your formula for $x_3$, and use part (b) to work out

<Formula label="The limit of the crossing point" tex="X = \lim_{x_2 \to x_1} x_3 = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} x_3" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(c), p. 3" />

Simplify as far as you can: the result holds $x_1$ only. Then find $Y = \lim y_3$ by symmetry alone, with $y_1$ only.

<Hint>

After the substitution, both the numerator and the denominator of $x_3$ go to zero. Multiply out the denominator and see what is left. Then divide the top and the bottom by $\Delta x$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Now let $\Delta x \to 0$: $x_2$ becomes $x_1$, and $\Delta y/\Delta x$ becomes the limit of part (b). To finish, remember that ladder 1 has length 1.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-c-x" label="$X =$" expr="x1^3"
  vars={{ x1: [0.2, 0.9] }}
  names={{ x1: "x_1" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x1", hint: "That is the foot of ladder 1. The crossing point of two nearby ladders lies on ladder 1 between the wall and the foot, so X is smaller than x1." },
    { expr: "-x1^3", hint: "Check the signs. The numerator of x3 is -x1 x2 times the change in y, and that change is negative, so X comes out positive. A crossing point of two ladders in the first quadrant cannot have a negative x." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p6-c-y" label="$Y =$" expr="y1^3"
  vars={{ y1: [0.2, 0.9] }}
  names={{ y1: "y_1" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "y1", hint: "That is the top of ladder 1, on the wall. The limit point lies on the ladder below its top, so Y is smaller than y1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two ladders that are close together cross at a point that settles down as they close in. That limit point is where ladder 1 touches the edge of the region.

<Step title="Substitute">

In the formula for $x_3$ of part (a), the numerator is $x_1 x_2\,(y_1 - y_2) = -x_1 x_2\,\Delta y$. The denominator is

<Formula label="The denominator for nearby ladders" tex="x_2 y_1 - x_1 y_2 = (x_1 + \Delta x)\,y_1 - x_1\,(y_1 + \Delta y) = y_1\,\Delta x - x_1\,\Delta y" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(c) (our own solution)" />

Divide the top and the bottom by $\Delta x$:

<Formula label="The crossing point of nearby ladders" tex="x_3 = \frac{-x_1 x_2\,\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}}{y_1 - x_1\,\dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Take the limit">

As $\Delta x \to 0$, $x_2 \to x_1$ and $\Delta y/\Delta x \to -x_1/y_1$:

<Formula label="The limit point" tex="X = \frac{x_1^2\cdot\dfrac{x_1}{y_1}}{y_1 + \dfrac{x_1^2}{y_1}} = \frac{x_1^3}{y_1^2 + x_1^2} = x_1^3" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(c) (our own solution)" />

The last step uses $x_1^2 + y_1^2 = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="The other coordinate, by symmetry">

Swapping $x$ and $y$ everywhere turns the whole problem into itself, and $x_3$ into $y_3$. So

<Formula label="The limit point, second coordinate" tex="Y = \lim_{x_2 \to x_1} y_3 = y_1^3" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take ladder 1 with $x_1 = 0.6$, $y_1 = 0.8$ and ladder 2 with $x_2 = 0.601$, $y_2 = 0.799249$, as in the check of part (b). The formula of part (a) gives $x_3 = 0.2165$, close to $0.6^3 = 0.216$.

The point $(X, Y)$ lies on ladder 1, as it should: $X/x_1 + Y/y_1 = x_1^2 + y_1^2 = 1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={1}>

Show that $X^{2/3} + Y^{2/3} = 1$.

MIT's set explains what this means. The point $(X, Y)$ of part (c) depends on $x_1$ and $y_1$. It is the one point of ladder 1 that also lies on the edge of the region swept out by all the ladders.

<Hint>

Take the power $2/3$ of $X = x_1^3$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The powers undo each other, and what is left is the length of the ladder.

<Step title="The equation of the edge">

<Formula label="The edge of the swept region" tex="X^{2/3} + Y^{2/3} = \left(x_1^3\right)^{2/3} + \left(y_1^3\right)^{2/3} = x_1^2 + y_1^2 = 1" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(d) (our own solution)" />

So the edge of the region is the curve of Problem 2.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This fits Problem 2 from the other side. There the tangent line at a point $(x_0, y_0)$ of the curve met the axes at $x_0^{1/3}$ and $y_0^{1/3}$. At the point $(X, Y) = (x_1^3, y_1^3)$ those crossings are $x_1$ and $y_1$: the tangent line to the edge is ladder 1 itself. Every ladder touches the edge at one point, and the edge is the curve to which all the ladders are tangent. That is why each tangent line of Problem 2 carries a piece of length 1 between the axes: the piece is a ladder.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
