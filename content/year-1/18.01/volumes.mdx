The last lecture found areas by slicing a region into strips. This one slices **solids** (transcript, p. 1). Think of a loaf of bread cut into slices: a slice of thickness $dx$ whose face has area $A(x)$ has volume $\Delta V \approx A(x)\,\Delta x$, and adding the slices gives the volume:

<Formula label="Volume by slicing" tex="dV = A(x)\,dx, \qquad V = \int A(x)\,dx" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The lecture deals systematically with one kind of solid, the **solid of revolution**: revolve a region of the plane about an axis (pp. 1–2). The advantage is that you never have to draw the awkward 3D picture: everything is read off a 2D diagram (p. 2).

## The method of disks

Revolve the region under $y = f(x)$ about the $x$-axis. A thin vertical strip of width $dx$ sweeps out a thin **disk** of radius $y$ (transcript, pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Method of disks" tex="dV = \pi y^2\,dx" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3" />

Two things are still needed: $y$ as a function of $x$, and the limits (p. 3).

**The ball.** Take the circle of radius $a$ centred at $(a, 0)$. From $(x - a)^2 + y^2 = a^2$ you only need $y^2$, not $y$: $y^2 = a^2 - (x - a)^2 = 2ax - x^2$. The ball runs from $x = 0$ to $x = 2a$ (pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Volume of a ball" tex="V = \int_0^{2a}\pi\,(2ax - x^2)\,dx = \pi\left[ax^2 - \frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^{2a} = \pi\left(4a^3 - \frac{8a^3}{3}\right) = \frac43\pi a^3" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–4" />

More comes for free: the volume of the slab of the ball from 0 to $x$ is $V(x) = \pi\left(ax^2 - \tfrac{x^3}{3}\right)$, and $V(a) = \tfrac23\pi a^3$, half the ball, as it should be (p. 4). The lecturer mentions that such pieces of a ball came up that year in research on why large particles in a fluid clump together, pushed by smaller ones (pp. 4–5). (The Lecture 21 notes do the ball with the circle centred at the origin, using its symmetry, notes for Lecture 21, pp. 5–6.)

## The method of shells

Now revolve about the **$y$-axis**. The example is a witches' cauldron: the region between $y = x^2$ and the level $y = a$, spun around the $y$-axis (transcript, pp. 5–7; notes, pp. 1–3). A thin vertical strip at distance $x$ from the axis now sweeps out a thin **cylinder**, a **shell**. Unroll it like a sheet of paper and it is a slab: thickness $dx$, height $a - y = a - x^2$ (top minus bottom), length the circumference $2\pi x$ (p. 6):

<Formula label="Method of shells" tex="dV = 2\pi x\,(a - x^2)\,dx" cite="reading2" at="p. 6" />

Only the strips to the **right** of the axis count, from $x = 0$ to $x = \sqrt a$: sweeping them round already covers the left half (p. 7). Then

<Formula label="Volume of the cauldron" tex="V = \int_0^{\sqrt a} 2\pi\,(ax - x^3)\,dx = 2\pi\left[\frac{ax^2}{2} - \frac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^{\sqrt a} = 2\pi\left(\frac{a^2}{2} - \frac{a^2}{4}\right) = \frac{\pi}{2}\,a^2" cite="reading2" at="pp. 6–7" />

Disks give the same answer: horizontal slices of the cauldron are disks of radius $x$, area $\pi x^2 = \pi y$, so $V = \int_0^a \pi y\,dy = \tfrac{\pi}{2}a^2$ (notes, p. 1). Revolving a region that does not reach the axis gives disks with a hole, washers, handled the same way (transcript, p. 5). Either slicing can always be set up; one may be much easier, and occasionally only one is possible (transcript, p. 8). (The transcript reads "the $(8 - y)$ dimension", almost certainly a transcription slip for $a - y$, p. 6.)

## A paradox about units

$V = \tfrac{\pi}{2}a^2$ looks like an area, not a volume (transcript, pp. 8–10; notes, p. 2). Try $a = 100$ cm: $V = \tfrac{\pi}{2} \times 100^2$ cm³ $\approx 16$ litres. Try $a = 1$ m: $V = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$ m³ $\approx 1600$ litres. Both are right, because $y = x^2$ means different shapes in different units. In centimetres, the top at $y = 100$ cm has $x = 10$ cm: a deep, skinny cauldron 20 cm across. In metres, the top at $y = 1$ m is 2 m across: a big vat (pp. 9–10). The equation $y = x^2$ is **not scale-invariant**: changing units changes the shape (notes, p. 2).

<WorkedExample title="A cone and a ring-shaped solid">
<Problem>
(a) Revolve the triangle under $y = \tfrac34 x$, $0 \le x \le 4$ cm, about the $x$-axis. (b) Revolve the region between $y = x$ and $y = x^2$, $0 \le x \le 1$, about the $y$-axis.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Disks: a cone">
$V = \int_0^4 \pi\left(\tfrac34 x\right)^2 dx = \pi\cdot\tfrac{9}{16}\cdot\tfrac{64}{3} = 12\pi \approx 37.7$ cm³: a cone of radius 3 cm and height 4 cm, as $\tfrac13\pi r^2 h$ says.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Shells">
Height of a shell: $x - x^2$. $V = \int_0^1 2\pi x\,(x - x^2)\,dx = 2\pi\left(\tfrac13 - \tfrac14\right) = \tfrac{\pi}{6} \approx 0.524$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## More cumulative sums

The lecture ends by setting up a problem for next time: heat the cauldron (with $a = 1$ m) from 0 °C to a temperature that falls from 100 °C at the bottom to 70 °C at the top, $T = 100 - 30y$ (transcript, pp. 11–12). The temperature is constant on each **horizontal** layer, so slice horizontally, into disks (p. 12) of volume $\pi x^2\,dy = \pi y\,dy$ (notes, pp. 1 and 3). The notes complete it (notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Heat for the boiling cauldron" tex="H = \int_0^1 (100 - 30y)\,\pi y\,dy = \pi\left[50y^2 - 10y^3\right]_0^1 = 40\pi\ \ (^\circ\text{C})\,\text{m}^3" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

At 1 calorie per cm³ per degree, that is $40\pi\times10^6$ cal $\approx 1.26\times10^5$ kcal, about 500 chocolate bars at 250 kcal each (notes, p. 3). The notes add two more examples of shells (notes, pp. 4–5): the flow through a pipe with Poiseuille's velocity profile $v = c(R^2 - r^2)$, whose rings $2\pi r\,dr$ give a total flow $\pi c R^4/2$, so doubling the radius multiplies the flow by 16; and darts landing with density $c\,e^{-r^2}$ at distance $r$ from the centre.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Building a sphere from shells">
The mass of a sphere of density $\rho(r)$ is a sum of thin shells, $M = \int_0^R 4\pi r^2\rho(r)\,dr$: the shell method with the whole surface $4\pi r^2$ in place of $2\pi x$. For constant density it gives $\tfrac43\pi R^3\rho$, the mass that pulls on a galaxy at the edge of the expanding sphere in 8.286. Dark matter halos, whose density falls off with radius, are weighed the same way.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Volume by slicing: $V = \int A(x)\,dx$, the cross-sectional area times the thickness, added up.
- Disks (slices perpendicular to the axis of revolution): $dV = \pi y^2\,dx$; ball: $\tfrac43\pi a^3$.
- Shells (thin cylinders around the axis): $dV = 2\pi x\,(\text{height})\,dx$, counting only one side of the axis; cauldron: $\tfrac{\pi}{2} a^2$.
- Get the integrand and the limits from a 2D drawing. Check units: $y = x^2$ is not scale-invariant.
- Slice where the quantity is constant: horizontal disks for the cauldron's temperature, rings for pipe flow.
