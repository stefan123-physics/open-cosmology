Lecture 6 brings in the last standard functions, the exponentials and logarithms, as fundamental as the trigonometric ones. The derivation follows the same plan as for sine and cosine: find the slope at one point, and let a law of algebra carry it everywhere. The new twist is that the slope at that one point is at first unknown, and the lecture deliberately "begs the question" to get round it.

## Exponentials

Start with a positive number $a$, the **base**. The exponential function obeys these rules (the lecture deduces the last one from the first three):

<Formula label="Rules of exponents" tex="a^0 = 1, \quad a^1 = a, \quad a^{x_1 + x_2} = a^{x_1}\,a^{x_2}, \quad \bigl(a^{x_1}\bigr)^{x_2} = a^{x_1 x_2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

A rational power $a^{m/n}$ is the $n$-th root of $a^m$; every other power, such as $a^{\sqrt2}$ or $a^\pi$, is "filled in" by continuity, which is what a calculator does with a long decimal. The graph of $2^x$ passes through $(-1, \tfrac12)$, $(0, 1)$ and $(1, 2)$, and rises very fast to the right.

## The derivative of aˣ, up to one number

Put the rule $a^{x + \Delta x} = a^x a^{\Delta x}$ into the difference quotient. The factor $a^x$ does not change as $\Delta x \to 0$ (here $x$ is fixed and $\Delta x$ moves), so it comes out of the limit:

<Formula label="Derivative of an exponential" tex="\frac{d}{dx}a^x = \lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{a^{x+\Delta x} - a^x}{\Delta x} = a^x \lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{a^{\Delta x} - 1}{\Delta x}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

Call the remaining limit the **mystery number** $M(a)$:

<Formula label="The mystery number" tex="M(a) = \lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{a^{\Delta x} - 1}{\Delta x}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}a^x = M(a)\,a^x" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

Setting $x = 0$ shows what it means: $M(a)$ is the **slope of the graph of $a^x$ at $x = 0$**. Know that one slope, and the slope everywhere follows. But unlike sine, where radians gave the slope 1 at 0, there is no obvious way yet to find $M(2)$.

## Begging the question: the number e

Instead of computing $M(a)$, define a base $e$ by the property we want:

<Formula label="Definition of e" tex="M(e) = 1 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

$e^x$ is its own derivative, with slope 1 at $x = 0$. Why should such a base exist? Stretch the graph of $f(x) = 2^x$ horizontally: $f(kx) = 2^{kx} = (2^k)^x = b^x$ with $b = 2^k$. By the chain rule its slope at 0 is $k\,M(2)$, so choosing $k = 1/M(2)$ gives slope exactly 1, and that base $b$ is $e$ (pp. 4–5). The notes add a geometric check that $e$ lies between 2 and 4 (notes, p. 2): the graph of $2^x$ curves up, so its slope at 0 is less than that of the secant from $(0, 1)$ to $(1, 2)$, which is 1; and the slope of $4^x$ at 0 is more than that of the secant from $(-\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ to $(0, 1)$, also 1. [The notes print that second point as $(1, 0)$; it is $(0, 1)$.]

## The natural logarithm

The **natural logarithm** $\ln x$ is the inverse of $e^x$: if $y = e^x$, then $\ln y = x$. It inherits the laws of exponents:

<Formula label="Properties of the logarithm" tex="\ln(x_1 x_2) = \ln x_1 + \ln x_2, \qquad \ln 1 = 0, \qquad \ln e = 1" cite="reading2" at="p. 6" />

Its graph is that of $e^x$ reflected in the line $y = x$; it passes through $(1, 0)$ with slope 1, and is defined only for $x > 0$, because $e^x$ is always positive. Its derivative comes from implicit differentiation, as for every inverse function in [Lecture 5](/courses/18-01/implicit-differentiation). Write $w = \ln x$ as $e^w = x$ and differentiate:

<Formula label="Derivative of ln x" tex="e^w\,\frac{dw}{dx} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dw}{dx} = \frac{1}{e^w} = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\ln x = \frac1x" cite="reading2" at="pp. 6–7" />

## Any base: M(a) = ln a

Now the mystery number can be found. **Method 1** converts to base $e$: since $a = e^{\ln a}$, $a^x = e^{x\ln a}$. Here $\ln a$ is just a constant, like the 3 in $e^{3x}$, so the chain rule gives

<Formula label="Derivative of aˣ" tex="\frac{d}{dx}a^x = \frac{d}{dx}e^{x\ln a} = (\ln a)\,e^{x\ln a} = (\ln a)\,a^x" cite="reading2" at="p. 7" />

The mystery number was the natural logarithm all along: $M(a) = \ln a$. So even someone who insists on base 2 or base 10 meets natural logarithms, $\frac{d}{dx}2^x = (\ln 2)\,2^x$ and $\frac{d}{dx}10^x = (\ln 10)\,10^x$ (p. 8). That is one sense in which they are "natural": they do not depend on our choice of base.

**Method 2**, **logarithmic differentiation**, differentiates the logarithm of a function instead of the function. By the chain rule and $\dfrac{d}{du}\ln u = \dfrac1u$:

<Formula label="Logarithmic derivative" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\ln u = \frac{1}{u}\,\frac{du}{dx}, \qquad (\ln u)' = \frac{u'}{u}" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

For $u = a^x$, $\ln u = x\ln a$ has derivative $\ln a$, so $u' = u\ln a$: the same answer, without converting to base $e$ (pp. 8–9).

## Moving exponents: xˣ

Logarithmic differentiation shines when both the base and the exponent move. For $v = x^x$, $\ln v = x\ln x$; the product rule gives its derivative, and multiplying back by $v$:

<Formula label="Derivative of xˣ" tex="\ln v = x\ln x, \qquad \frac{v'}{v} = \ln x + x\cdot\frac1x = 1 + \ln x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}x^x = x^x(1 + \ln x)" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

<WorkedExample title="Three derivatives with the new rules">
<Problem>
Find (a) the slope of $3^x$ at $x = 2$; (b) the slope of $x^x$ at $x = 2$; (c) the derivative of $\ln(x^2 + 1)$ at $x = 1$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Any base">
$(\ln 3)\,3^2 \approx 1.0986 \times 9 \approx 9.89$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Moving exponent">
$2^2(1 + \ln 2) \approx 4 \times 1.693 \approx 6.77$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Logarithmic derivative">
$(\ln u)' = u'/u$ with $u = x^2 + 1$: $\dfrac{2x}{x^2 + 1} = \dfrac{2}{2} = 1$ at $x = 1$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Closing the loop: e as a limit

We still have not said what $e$ **is** numerically. Take the limit $\lim_{n\to\infty}(1 + 1/n)^n$, which has a moving exponent, and work with its logarithm. With $\Delta x = 1/n$ and $\ln 1 = 0$ subtracted in:

<Formula label="The logarithm of (1 + 1/n)ⁿ" tex="\ln\Bigl(1 + \frac1n\Bigr)^n = n\ln\Bigl(1 + \frac1n\Bigr) = \frac{\ln(1 + \Delta x) - \ln 1}{\Delta x} \;\to\; \left.\frac{d}{dx}\ln x\right|_{x=1} = 1" cite="reading2" at="p. 10" />

The logarithm tends to 1, so the quantity itself tends to $e^1$:

<Formula label="e as a limit" tex="\lim_{n\to\infty}\Bigl(1 + \frac1n\Bigr)^n = e" cite="reading2" at="pp. 10–11" />

This gives an honest way to compute $e$: $n = 100$ gives $1.01^{100} \approx 2.705$, $n = 1000$ gives about $2.717$, closing in on $e \approx 2.71828$. The notes end with a remark on why logarithms matter in every science: a change $f'$ means little without the size $f$ it changes, and the **relative rate** $f'/f$ is exactly $(\ln f)'$ (notes, p. 7).

<Callout kind="cosmology">
In MIT's cosmology course, a universe dominated by vacuum energy (the false vacuum of inflation, and today's dark energy in the far future) grows **exponentially**: the scale factor is $a(t) \propto e^{Ht}$ with a constant $H$. Since $e^{Ht}$ is its own derivative up to the factor $H$, the relative expansion rate $\dot a/a = (\ln a)^{\cdot} = H$ stays the same for ever. Inflation is measured in **e-folds**, the number of times $a$ grows by a factor $e$: $\ln$ of the total growth.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\dfrac{d}{dx}a^x = M(a)\,a^x$, where $M(a) = \lim\,(a^{\Delta x} - 1)/\Delta x$ is the slope of $a^x$ at 0.
- $e$ is defined by $M(e) = 1$, so $\dfrac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x$; stretching $2^x$ shows such a base exists, between 2 and 4.
- $\ln x$ is the inverse of $e^x$: $\ln(x_1x_2) = \ln x_1 + \ln x_2$, $\ln 1 = 0$, $\ln e = 1$, and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\ln x = \dfrac1x$.
- $a^x = e^{x\ln a}$ gives $\dfrac{d}{dx}a^x = (\ln a)\,a^x$: $M(a) = \ln a$.
- Logarithmic differentiation: $(\ln u)' = u'/u$; for example $(x^x)' = x^x(1 + \ln x)$.
- $e = \lim_{n\to\infty}(1 + 1/n)^n \approx 2.718$.
