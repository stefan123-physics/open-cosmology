Three things are left out: Problem 0 (a note on who you worked with, 3 points), Problem 3(a) (an exercise from the course textbook, 2 points, which we may not reproduce), and Part I, which is described at the end of this page. That leaves 25 points that count for the score. Part (c) of Problem 1 was extra credit at MIT (5 points); it is set here as an optional problem of its own. Problem 2 is long, so it is split here into two blocks: parts (a) to (d), and part (e).

A number box takes a decimal number. Where a number box needs a certain precision, the problem says so; the other number boxes accept any value within 1% of ours. MIT gives points per problem and per part; where we split a part further or add a check of our own, we say so. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem or part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Several parts ask for a sketch or an argument as well as a result. Compare your sketch with the solution yourself. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A warming summer and cooling degree days" points={6} mit="Problem Set 5, Part II, Problem 1(a) and (b)">

At the start of day 0, some time in a past summer, the temperature in Boston is 65 degrees Fahrenheit. Over the next 50 days the temperature $y(t)$ rises according to the rule

<Formula label="The rule for the temperature" tex="y'(t) = \frac{y(t)}{100}, \qquad y(0) = 65" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

with the time $t$ in days. Day 0 runs from $t = 0$ to $t = 1$, day 1 from $t = 1$ to $t = 2$, and so on.

MIT's set reminds you what the average of a function over an interval is (the course treats it in a later lecture):

<Formula label="Continuous average" tex="\text{average of } f \text{ on } a \le x \le b \;=\; \frac{1}{b-a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx" cite="mit" at="Part II, footnote 2, p. 1" />

For one day $b - a = 1$, so the average temperature of a day is simply the integral of $y$ over that day.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the formula for $y(t)$. Draw the graph of the temperature for $3 \le t \le 5$, that is, for days 3 and 4. Label each of the two days and shade the two regions whose areas are the average temperatures of those days.

The second box is our own check of your shading: work out the average temperature of day 3, in degrees Fahrenheit, to two decimal places.

<Hint>

The rule says that the rate of change of $y$ is proportional to $y$ itself. Separate the variables: bring everything with $y$ to one side and everything with $t$ to the other.

</Hint>

<Hint>

After integrating you have $\ln y$ in terms of $t$, with a constant. The starting value $y(0) = 65$ fixes the constant.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the shading: an integral of $y$ is the area between the graph and the $t$-axis. Which values of $t$ belong to day 3?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$y(t) =$" expr="65*exp(t/100)"
  vars={{ t: [1, 50] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "65*exp(t)", hint: "This function satisfies y′ = y: it grows 100 times too fast. The rule has a factor 1/100, and it must end up in the exponent." },

    { expr: "65 + t/100", hint: "This function has the constant slope 1/100. The rule asks for the slope y/100, which grows as y grows." },
    { expr: "65*(1 + t/100)", hint: "This is a straight line with the constant slope 0.65. That is right only at t = 0: the slope must be y/100 at every moment, so it grows with y." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-a2" label="Average temperature of day 3:" value="67.32" unit="°F" tolerance="0.05" calc="p1-day3-average" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The rule is a differential equation that separates. Its solution is an exponential, and the average over a day is the area under the graph for that day.

<Step title="Solve the differential equation">

Write the rule as $dy/dt = y/100$ and separate the variables, as in the lesson on separation of variables:

<Formula label="Separable equation" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x)\,g(y) \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dy}{g(y)} = f(x)\,dx" cite="reading7" at="p. 2" />

Here the variable is $t$, and $dy/y = dt/100$. Integrating both sides gives $\ln y = t/100 + c$, so $y = A\,e^{t/100}$ with $A = e^c$. At $t = 0$ this must be 65, so $A = 65$:

<Formula label="The temperature" tex="y(t) = 65\,e^{t/100}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The graph for days 3 and 4">

On $3 \le t \le 5$ the graph rises gently from $y(3) = 67.0$ through $y(4) = 67.7$ to $y(5) = 68.3$ degrees. It bends upwards, but so little that it looks like a straight line.

Day 3 is the interval $3 \le t \le 4$ and day 4 is $4 \le t \le 5$. Draw vertical lines at $t = 3$, $t = 4$ and $t = 5$. The region for day 3 lies between the lines at 3 and 4, below the graph and above the $t$-axis. The region for day 4 lies next to it, between the lines at 4 and 5. Each region is one day wide, so its area is the average temperature of its day. The regions reach all the way down to the axis at $y = 0$, not just to 65 degrees.

<Plot variable="t" domain={[3, 5]} range={[0, 80]} xLabel="t (days)" yLabel="y (°F)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "65*exp(t/100)", label: "y" }
  ]}
  caption="The temperature on days 3 and 4, computed from the formula. The area under the curve from t = 3 to t = 4, down to the t-axis, is the average temperature of day 3. The area from t = 4 to t = 5 is the average of day 4." />

</Step>

<Step title="The average of day 3">

An antiderivative of $65\,e^{t/100}$ is $6500\,e^{t/100}$. By the first fundamental theorem:

<Formula label="First fundamental theorem of calculus (FTC 1)" tex="F'(x) = f(x) \;\Rightarrow\; \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

<Formula label="Average temperature of day 3" tex="\int_3^4 65\,e^{t/100}\,dt = 6500\left(e^{0.04} - e^{0.03}\right) = 67.32" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate: $y' = 65\cdot\tfrac{1}{100}e^{t/100} = y/100$, and $y(0) = 65$. The average 67.32 lies between the temperatures at the start and the end of day 3, 67.0 and 67.7, and close to the middle, as it should for a graph that is nearly straight.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

The number of **cooling degree days** is found as follows: for each day, take the average temperature of that day minus 65 degrees, and add these differences for all the days. MIT's set notes that the number is used to estimate how much electricity air conditioning will need.

Draw a second graph of $y$, now for all 50 days, and shade the region whose area is the total number of degree days. Write this total as a definite integral minus $65 \cdot 50$, and evaluate the integral with the fundamental theorem. (MIT adds that you can also write the whole quantity as one integral, the area between two curves.)

Type the value of the definite integral and the number of degree days, each rounded to the nearest whole number or more precisely.

<Hint>

Day number $i$ contributes the integral of $y$ from $i$ to $i + 1$, minus 65. What do the 50 integrals add up to?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the graph, 65 times 50 is the area of a rectangle. Where does it sit under the curve?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b1" label="$\int_0^{50} y\,dt =$" value="4216.69" tolerance="0.6" calc="p1-integral" />

<Answer id="p1-b2" label="Degree days:" value="966.69" tolerance="0.6" calc="p1-degree-days" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Integrals over neighbouring intervals add up to one integral over the whole interval. The 50 daily averages together are the area under the whole curve.

<Step title="From a sum over days to one integral">

Day $i$ has the average temperature $\int_i^{i+1} y\,dt$. Adding the 50 differences:

<Formula label="Cooling degree days" tex="\sum_{i=0}^{49}\left(\int_i^{i+1} y(t)\,dt - 65\right) = \int_0^{50} 65\,e^{t/100}\,dt - 65\cdot 50" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The graph">

Over the 50 days the curve rises from 65 to $65\,e^{0.5} = 107.2$ degrees. The rectangle of height 65 and width 50 lies under it. The degree days are the area of what is left: the region between the horizontal line $y = 65$ and the curve, from $t = 0$ to $t = 50$. It looks like a triangle with a slightly sagging top side.

<Plot variable="t" domain={[0, 50]} range={[0, 115]} xLabel="t (days)" yLabel="y (°F)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "65*exp(t/100)", label: "y" },
    { expr: "65", label: "65", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="The temperature over the 50 days (solid) and the level of 65 degrees (dashed), computed from the formula. The area between the two is the number of cooling degree days." />

</Step>

<Step title="Evaluate the integral">

With the antiderivative $6500\,e^{t/100}$ and the first fundamental theorem:

<Formula label="The integral over 50 days" tex="\int_0^{50} 65\,e^{t/100}\,dt = \Big[6500\,e^{t/100}\Big]_0^{50} = 6500\left(e^{1/2} - 1\right) = 4216.69" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The rectangle has the area $65 \cdot 50 = 3250$. So the number of cooling degree days is $4216.69 - 3250 = 966.69$.

</Step>

<Step title="The same thing as an area between curves">

The lesson on areas gives the area between an upper curve $f$ and a lower curve $g$:

<Formula label="Area between two curves" tex="A = \int_a^b \big(f(x) - g(x)\big)\,dx" cite="reading3" at="p. 3" />

With the temperature curve on top and the line at 65 below, the degree days are $\int_0^{50}\left(65\,e^{t/100} - 65\right)dt$, which is the same number.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

If the top side were straight, the region would be a triangle with base 50 and height $107.2 - 65 = 42.2$, with area $\tfrac12\cdot 50\cdot 42.2 = 1055$. The curve sags below that straight side, so the true area should be somewhat less: 967 fits. The unit is degrees Fahrenheit times days.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 1(c) (optional)

<Exercise id="p1c" title="The same integral from a Riemann sum" points={5} mit="Problem Set 5, Part II, Problem 1(c)" optional>

Compute the integral of part (b), $\int_0^{50} 65\,e^{t/100}\,dt$, directly: as the limit of lower Riemann sums, without the fundamental theorem.

MIT asks you to follow the procedure of Problem 5 of its Problem Set 4, which finds $\int_0^1 e^x\,dx$ in this way. The procedure is: cut the interval into $n$ equal pieces; on each piece build a rectangle whose height is the lowest value of the function on that piece; add the areas of the rectangles, which gives a geometric series, and write that sum in closed form; then let $n$ grow without limit, using the linear approximation of the exponential function for the one limit that is needed. Here the scale factors are different from those in Problem Set 4.

Type the lower sum $L_n$ for $n$ pieces in closed form (without a long sum or dots). Then find its limit and compare with part (b).

<Hint>

The pieces have the width $50/n$. The function rises, so its lowest value on each piece is at the left end. Write down the left ends and the heights there.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each height is the one before it times the same number $r$. A geometric series has the sum $1 + r + r^2 + \cdots + r^{n-1} = \dfrac{r^n - 1}{r - 1}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the limit you need to know what $n\left(e^{1/(2n)} - 1\right)$ does when $n$ is large. The exponent $1/(2n)$ is then small: use $e^x \approx 1 + x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1c-sum" label="$L_n =$" expr="3250*(exp(1/2) - 1)/(n*(exp(1/(2*n)) - 1))"
  vars={{ n: [4, 40] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3250*exp(1/(2*n))*(exp(1/2) - 1)/(n*(exp(1/(2*n)) - 1))", hint: "That is the upper sum: it takes the temperature at the right end of each piece. The function rises, so its lowest value on a piece is at the left end." },
    { expr: "65*(exp(1/2) - 1)/(exp(1/(2*n)) - 1)", hint: "You added the heights. Each rectangle also has a width, 50/n." },
    { expr: "6500*(exp(1/2) - 1)", hint: "That is the limit, the value of the integral. This box asks for the sum before the limit, which still depends on n." },
    { expr: "3250*(exp(1/2) - 1)/(n*(exp(1/n) - 1))", hint: "Check the ratio of the series. From one piece to the next, t grows by 50/n, so t/100 grows by 1/(2n), not by 1/n." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For an exponential function the heights of the rectangles form a geometric series, and that can be added exactly.

<Step title="The rectangles">

A Riemann sum, in the words of the lesson on definite integrals:

<Formula label="Riemann sum and definite integral" tex="\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(c_i)\,\Delta x = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx" cite="reading5" at="p. 4" />

Here the pieces have the width $\Delta t = 50/n$, and the left ends are $t_i = 50\,i/n$ for $i = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1$. Because $y$ rises, its lowest value on a piece is at the left end: $y(t_i) = 65\,e^{i/(2n)}$. The lower sum is

<Formula label="The lower sum" tex="L_n = \frac{50}{n}\cdot 65\left(1 + r + r^2 + \cdots + r^{n-1}\right), \qquad r = e^{1/(2n)}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Add the geometric series">

The sum in brackets is $(r^n - 1)/(r - 1)$, and $r^n = e^{1/2}$. So

<Formula label="The lower sum in closed form" tex="L_n = \frac{3250}{n}\cdot\frac{e^{1/2} - 1}{e^{1/(2n)} - 1}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The limit">

Only the product $n\left(e^{1/(2n)} - 1\right)$ depends on $n$. The lesson on approximations lists

<Formula label="Linear approximation of the exponential" tex="e^x \approx 1 + x \quad (x \approx 0)" cite="reading6" at="p. 2" />

With $x = 1/(2n)$, which goes to 0 as $n$ grows, $e^{1/(2n)} - 1 \approx 1/(2n)$, so $n\left(e^{1/(2n)} - 1\right)$ goes to $\tfrac12$. (This limit is the derivative of $e^{x/2}$ at $x = 0$, written with $\Delta x = 1/n$.) Then

<Formula label="The limit of the lower sums" tex="\lim_{n\to\infty} L_n = \frac{3250\left(e^{1/2} - 1\right)}{1/2} = 6500\left(e^{1/2} - 1\right)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

This is the value found in part (b) in one line. As MIT's set remarks, the long calculation shows how much work the fundamental theorem saves.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Some numbers of our own: the lower sum is 4112.1 for $n = 10$, 4206.2 for $n = 100$ and 4215.6 for $n = 1000$. They rise towards 4216.69 and stay below it, as lower sums must. For $n = 50$, one rectangle per day, the sum is 4195.6: this is what you get by taking each day's temperature at the start of the day.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2, parts (a) to (d)

<Exercise id="p2" title="A function known only as an integral" points={10} mit="Problem Set 5, Part II, Problem 2(a) to (d)">

Consider the function

<Formula label="The Fresnel cosine integral" tex="f(x) = \int_0^x \cos(t^2)\,dt" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2, p. 2" />

It cannot be written in terms of the standard functions (powers, roots, exponentials, logarithms, sines and cosines). It is called a **Fresnel integral**; there is a matching one with the sine. The lecture notes call it $C(x)$ and say that it arises in optics.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Make a rough sketch of the graph of $\cos(t^2)$ that shows its first positive zero and its first negative zero. What does the curve look like at $t = 0$? Is the function even or odd?

<Hint>

The cosine is zero when its argument is $\pi/2$ plus a whole number of times $\pi$. Here the argument is $t^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the shape near $t = 0$, think of $\cos u \approx 1 - \tfrac12 u^2$ with $u = t^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a1" label="First positive zero: $t =$" value="1.2533" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-first-zero" />

<Answer id="p2-a2" label="The function $\cos(t^2)$ is" choices={["even", "odd", "neither even nor odd"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

<Step title="Zeros">

$\cos(t^2) = 0$ first happens when $t^2 = \pi/2$:

<Formula label="First zeros of the integrand" tex="t = \pm\sqrt{\pi/2} \approx \pm 1.2533" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Shape at t = 0 and symmetry">

At $t = 0$ the value is $\cos 0 = 1$, the largest value a cosine can have: a maximum, with a horizontal tangent. The curve is very flat there. With the quadratic approximation of the cosine from the lesson on approximations,

<Formula label="Quadratic approximation of the cosine" tex="\cos x \approx 1 - \frac{x^2}{2} \quad (x \approx 0)" cite="reading6" at="p. 5" />

used with $t^2$ in place of $x$, the function is close to $1 - \tfrac12 t^4$: flatter than a parabola.

Replacing $t$ by $-t$ does not change $t^2$, so $\cos\big((-t)^2\big) = \cos(t^2)$: the function is even.

</Step>

<Step title="The sketch">

The curve starts level at height 1, falls through zero at $t = 1.2533$, reaches $-1$ where $t^2 = \pi$, and then swings between $-1$ and 1 faster and faster, because $t^2$ grows faster and faster. The left half is the mirror image of the right half.

<Plot variable="t" domain={[-3, 3]} range={[-1.3, 1.3]} xLabel="t" yLabel="cos(t²)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "cos(t^2)" }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 1.2533, y: 0, label: "1.2533" }, { x: -1.2533, y: 0, label: "−1.2533" }]}
  caption="The graph of cos(t²), computed from the formula: level at height 1 at t = 0, first zeros at t = ±1.2533, and faster swings further out." />

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

List the critical points of $f$ on the whole line $-\infty < x < \infty$. Which of them are local maxima and which are local minima?

As our own check, type the second smallest positive critical point, and choose the description that fits.

<Hint>

You cannot write $f$ as a formula, but you can write its derivative. What does the second fundamental theorem say about the derivative of an integral with respect to its upper limit?

</Hint>

<Hint>

To tell a maximum from a minimum, follow the sign of $f'$ from left to right through each critical point. Take care on the negative side: there you pass the critical points in the opposite order.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b1" label="Second positive critical point: $x =$" value="2.1708" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-second-critical" />

<Answer id="p2-b2" label="The critical points:" choices={["For x > 0 they alternate, starting with a maximum at the smallest one; each maximum at x goes with a minimum at −x", "For x > 0 they alternate, starting with a maximum at the smallest one; each maximum at x goes with a maximum at −x", "Every critical point with x > 0 is a maximum, and every one with x < 0 is a minimum", "For x > 0 they alternate, starting with a minimum at the smallest one; each minimum at x goes with a maximum at −x"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The second fundamental theorem gives $f'$ at once, and the sign of $f'$ tells where $f$ rises and falls.

<Step title="The derivative">

The lecture notes state it for this very function, which they call $C$:

<Formula label="Derivative of the Fresnel integral" tex="C'(x) = \cos(x^2)" cite="reading2" at="p. 4 (the second fundamental theorem itself is on p. 1)" />

So $f'(x) = \cos(x^2)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The critical points">

$f'(x) = 0$ when $x^2 = \pi/2 + k\pi$ for $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$:

<Formula label="Critical points of the Fresnel integral" tex="x = \pm\sqrt{\left(k + \tfrac12\right)\pi}, \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The first ones are $\pm 1.2533$ ($k = 0$) and $\pm 2.1708$ ($k = 1$). There are infinitely many, and they lie closer and closer together.

</Step>

<Step title="Maxima and minima">

For $x > 0$: $f' = \cos(x^2)$ is positive from 0 up to the first critical point, so $f$ rises; then negative up to the second, so $f$ falls; then positive again. So $f$ has a local maximum at the critical points with $k = 0, 2, 4, \ldots$ and a local minimum at those with $k = 1, 3, 5, \ldots$

For $x < 0$: $f'$ is even, so it has the same signs at $-x$ as at $x$. But going from left to right you now meet the changes in the opposite order. At $x = -\sqrt{\pi/2}$ the derivative goes from negative to positive: a local minimum. In general there is a local minimum at $-\sqrt{(k + \frac12)\pi}$ for even $k$ and a local maximum for odd $k$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The integrand is even, so $f$ is odd: $f(-x) = \int_0^{-x}\cos(t^2)\,dt = -\int_0^{x}\cos(s^2)\,ds = -f(x)$, with the change of variable $t = -s$. The graph of an odd function is unchanged by a half turn about the origin, and a half turn takes every hilltop to a valley bottom. That agrees with the result.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Answer id="p2-c1" label="Inflection point: $x =$" value="1.7725" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-inflection" />

<Answer id="p2-c2" label="Estimate of the maximum value:" value="0.977" tolerance="10%" calc="p2-maximum-value" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Sketch $f$ from its derivatives, as in the lesson on curve sketching: $f'$ gives rising and falling, $f''$ gives the bending. Areas under the graph of part (a) give the heights.

<Step title="Critical points in the interval">

Of the critical points of part (b), only $x = \pm\sqrt{\pi/2} = \pm 1.2533$ lie between $-2$ and 2 (the next ones are at $\pm 2.1708$). There is a maximum at $1.2533$ and a minimum at $-1.2533$.

</Step>

<Step title="Inflection points">

By the chain rule,

<Formula label="Second derivative of the Fresnel integral" tex="f''(x) = -2x\,\sin(x^2)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

This is zero at $x = 0$ and where $x^2 = \pi$, that is, at $x = \pm\sqrt\pi = \pm 1.7725$ (the next zeros, at $\pm\sqrt{2\pi}$, are outside the interval). For $0 < x < \sqrt\pi$ the sine is positive, so $f'' < 0$: concave down. For $\sqrt\pi < x < 2$ the sine is negative, so $f'' > 0$: concave up. On the negative side the signs are reversed, because of the factor $x$. So the bending changes at all three points: $0$ and $\pm 1.7725$ are inflection points.

</Step>

<Step title="Heights">

$f(0) = 0$ and $f'(0) = \cos 0 = 1$: the graph passes through the origin with slope 1.

The maximum value $f(1.2533)$ is the area under the first arch of $\cos(t^2)$, from 0 to its first zero. That arch fits in a rectangle of width 1.2533 and height 1 and fills most of it, because the curve stays close to 1 for a long while. So the value is somewhat below 1.25, about 1. A computer gives 0.977.

Between $1.2533$ and 2 the integrand is negative, so $f$ falls again: to about 0.66 at the inflection point $x = 1.7725$, where the slope is $\cos\pi = -1$, and to about 0.46 at $x = 2$. It is still falling there, because the next critical point comes only at 2.1708.

</Step>

<Step title="The sketch">

$f$ is odd (see the check in part b), so the left half follows from the right half by a half turn about the origin.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-2, 2]} range={[-1.3, 1.3]} xLabel="x" yLabel="f(x)"
  curves={[

  ]}

(The power series behind the plot comes from a later part of the course and is not needed for the sketch.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At the maximum $f''(1.2533) = -2(1.2533)\sin(\pi/2)$ is negative, as the second derivative test asks of a maximum. And the graph is steepest where $f''$ is zero: slope 1 at the origin and slope $-1$ at $x = \pm 1.7725$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

Estimate $f(0.1)$. Give your answer rounded to six decimal places.

<Hint>

For $0 \le t \le 0.1$ the argument $t^2$ is at most 0.01, which is very small. Replace the cosine by an approximation that you can integrate.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The linear approximation $\cos u \approx 1$ gives 0.1. Is that good to six decimal places? Use the quadratic approximation to find out.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="$f(0.1) \approx$" value="0.099999" tolerance="0.0000004" calc="p2-f-at-tenth" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Near $t = 0$ the integrand is very close to a polynomial, and a polynomial can be integrated.

<Step title="Approximate the integrand">

The quadratic approximation $\cos u \approx 1 - \tfrac12 u^2$ (see part a), with $u = t^2$, gives $\cos(t^2) \approx 1 - \tfrac12 t^4$. Then

<Formula label="Approximation of the Fresnel integral near zero" tex="f(x) \approx \int_0^x \left(1 - \frac{t^4}{2}\right)dt = x - \frac{x^5}{10}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put in the number">

For $x = 0.1$: $x^5/10 = 10^{-5}/10 = 0.000001$, so

<Formula label="The estimate" tex="f(0.1) \approx 0.1 - 0.000001 = 0.099999" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Is the sixth decimal safe? The next term in the approximation of the cosine is $+u^4/24$ (the course meets it later, with Taylor series). It adds $x^9/216$ to $f(x)$, which is about $5 \times 10^{-12}$ for $x = 0.1$: far too small to matter. The linear approximation alone would have given 0.100000, which is wrong in the sixth decimal.

## Problem 2(e)

<Exercise id="p2e" title="Three other ways to write the Fresnel integral" points={6} mit="Problem Set 5, Part II, Problem 2(e)">

Fresnel integrals are sometimes written with the variables scaled differently. As before, $f(x) = \int_0^x \cos(t^2)\,dt$. MIT gives 6 points for this part; the split into 2 points for each of (i), (ii) and (iii) is ours.

<Part label="i" points={2}>

Let

<Formula label="A rescaled Fresnel integral" tex="g(x) = \int_0^x \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}u^2\right)du" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(e)(i), p. 2" />

Make a change of variable to show that $f(x) = c_1\,g(c_2\,x)$ for some constants $c_1$ and $c_2$. Type the two constants as decimal numbers; take both positive. Why would someone choose the factor $\pi/2$?

<Hint>

You want $\cos(t^2)$ to turn into $\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}u^2\right)$. So $t^2 = \tfrac{\pi}{2}u^2$: what is $t$ in terms of $u$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

A change of variable in a definite integral changes three things: the integrand, the $dt$, and the limits.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the last question: where are the zeros of $\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}u^2\right)$? What does that mean for the critical points of $g$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2e-i1" label="$c_1 =$" value="1.2533" tolerance="1%" calc="p2e-c1" />

<Answer id="p2e-i2" label="$c_2 =$" value="0.7979" tolerance="1%" calc="p2e-c2" />

<Solution>

<Step title="The change of variable">

The lesson's rule for a definite integral:

<Formula label="Change of variables" tex="\int_{x_1}^{x_2} g\big(u(x)\big)\,u'(x)\,dx = \int_{u_1}^{u_2} g(u)\,du, \qquad u_1 = u(x_1),\; u_2 = u(x_2)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4 (in this formula g stands for any function, not for the g of this problem)" />

Put $t = \sqrt{\pi/2}\;u$. Then $t^2 = \tfrac{\pi}{2}u^2$ and $dt = \sqrt{\pi/2}\;du$. When $t$ runs from 0 to $x$, $u$ runs from 0 to $\sqrt{2/\pi}\;x$:

<Formula label="The Fresnel integral in terms of g" tex="f(x) = \int_0^x \cos(t^2)\,dt = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\int_0^{\sqrt{2/\pi}\,x} \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{2}u^2\right)du = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\;g\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\,x\right)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(e)(i), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So $c_1 = \sqrt{\pi/2} \approx 1.2533$ and $c_2 = \sqrt{2/\pi} \approx 0.7979$. (The pair $-c_1$, $-c_2$ works as well, because $g$ is odd.)

</Step>

<Step title="Why the factor π/2?">

MIT's set does not give its answer; this is our reading. With the factor $\pi/2$ the integrand $\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}u^2\right)$ is zero exactly where $u^2 = 1, 3, 5, \ldots$ So the critical points of $g$ are at the tidy places $x = \pm 1, \pm\sqrt3, \pm\sqrt5, \ldots$, with the first maximum at $x = 1$. In the same way its inflection points are at $x = 0, \pm\sqrt2, \pm 2, \ldots$ For $f$ these points sit at awkward numbers such as 1.2533. (A fact from outside this course: with this scaling the function $g$ also levels off at exactly one half for large $x$.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate $c_1\,g(c_2 x)$ with the chain rule and the second fundamental theorem: $c_1 c_2\cos\left(\tfrac{\pi}{2}c_2^2x^2\right)$. With $c_1c_2 = 1$ and $\tfrac{\pi}{2}c_2^2 = 1$ this is $\cos(x^2) = f'(x)$. Both sides are 0 at $x = 0$, so they are equal for all $x$. The first maximum of $g$ at 1 corresponds to $x = 1/c_2 = 1.2533$ for $f$, as found in Problem 2(b).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="ii" points={2}>

Let

<Formula label="A Fresnel integral with a square root" tex="h(x) = \int_0^x \frac{\cos v}{\sqrt v}\,dv" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(e)(ii), p. 2" />

This integral is called **improper**, because $1/\sqrt v$ is infinite at $v = 0$. MIT's set notes that the area under the curve is finite all the same, and that $h$ is continuous with $h(0) = 0$, although its graph starts with an infinite slope.

Make a different change of variable to show that $f(x) = c\,h(x^2)$ for some constant $c$, for $x > 0$. Type the constant.

<Hint>

The argument of the cosine in $f$ is $t^2$, and in $h$ it is $v$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With $v = t^2$, what are $dv$ and $\sqrt v$ in terms of $t$ (for positive $t$)?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2e-ii" label="$c =$" value="0.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p2e-c" />

<Solution>

<Step title="The change of variable">

Start from $h(x^2)$ and put $v = t^2$ with $t > 0$. Then $dv = 2t\,dt$ and $\sqrt v = t$. When $v$ runs from 0 to $x^2$, $t$ runs from 0 to $x$ (this is where $x > 0$ is used):

<Formula label="The square-root form in terms of f" tex="h(x^2) = \int_0^{x^2} \frac{\cos v}{\sqrt v}\,dv = \int_0^x \frac{\cos(t^2)}{t}\,2t\,dt = 2\int_0^x \cos(t^2)\,dt = 2\,f(x)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(e)(ii), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So $f(x) = \tfrac12\,h(x^2)$: the constant is $c = \tfrac12$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The factor $t$ from $dv$ cancels the $t$ from $\sqrt v$: the change of variable removes the infinity at 0, which shows again that the area is finite. For small $x$, $f(x) \approx x$, so $h(x^2) \approx 2x$ and $h(x) \approx 2\sqrt x$: a graph through the origin with infinite slope there, as MIT's note says.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="iii" points={2}>

Let

<Formula label="A Fresnel integral with a parameter" tex="k(x) = \sqrt{x}\int_0^1 \cos(x\,t^2)\,dt, \qquad x > 0" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(e)(iii), p. 2" />

Use the change of variable $z = x\,t^2$ and part (ii) to find how the functions $k$ and $f$ are related. MIT's hint: ask yourself which quantities are variable and which are constant.

<Hint>

The integral is over $t$. While you integrate, $x$ is a fixed number, like the 2 in $\int_0^1 \cos(2t^2)\,dt$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With $z = x\,t^2$: $dz = 2x\,t\,dt$, and $t = \sqrt{z/x}$. What are the limits for $z$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2e-iii" label="The relation:" choices={["k(x) = f(√x)", "k(x) = f(x²)", "k(x) = ½ f(√x)", "k(x) = √x · f(x)", "k(x) = 2 f(√x)"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

<Step title="The change of variable">

Hold $x$ fixed and put $z = x\,t^2$. Then $dz = 2x\,t\,dt$ and $t = \sqrt{z/x}$, so $dt = dz/(2x\,t) = dz/\big(2\sqrt{x}\sqrt{z}\big)$. When $t$ runs from 0 to 1, $z$ runs from 0 to $x$:

<Formula label="The parameter form in terms of h" tex="k(x) = \sqrt{x}\int_0^x \frac{\cos z}{2\sqrt{x}\,\sqrt{z}}\,dz = \frac12\int_0^x \frac{\cos z}{\sqrt z}\,dz = \frac12\,h(x)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(e)(iii), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Use part (ii)">

Part (ii) says $f(x) = \tfrac12 h(x^2)$ for $x > 0$. Put $\sqrt x$ in place of $x$: $f(\sqrt x) = \tfrac12 h(x)$. So

<Formula label="Relation between k and f" tex="k(x) = f\left(\sqrt{x}\right), \qquad\text{or}\qquad f(x) = k(x^2) \quad (x > 0)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(e)(iii), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

MIT's set mentions a second route (not assigned), a scaling like the one in part (i). Put $s = \sqrt{x}\,t$: then $x\,t^2 = s^2$, $dt = ds/\sqrt{x}$ and $s$ runs from 0 to $\sqrt x$, so $k(x) = \int_0^{\sqrt x}\cos(s^2)\,ds = f(\sqrt x)$ directly. Also, for small $x$ the cosine is close to 1, so $k(x) \approx \sqrt x$, which agrees with $f(\sqrt x) \approx \sqrt x$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3(b)

<Exercise id="p3" title="A volume from horizontal slices" points={3} mit="Problem Set 5, Part II, Problem 3(b)">

Find the volume of the region in space that consists of the points with $x > 0$, $y > 0$ and $z > 0$ for which

<Formula label="The region" tex="\frac{z^2}{2} < x + y < z" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(b), p. 2" />

MIT's hint: first find the area of the horizontal cross-sections.

Type the area $A(z)$ of the cross-section at height $z$, and then the volume as a decimal number.

<Hint>

Fix a height $z$. The cross-section is then a region in the plane of $x$ and $y$. Draw the lines $x + y = z$ and $x + y = z^2/2$ in the first quadrant.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each line cuts a right triangle off the corner at the origin. The cross-section is what lies between the two lines.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For which heights $z$ is there any cross-section at all? You need $z^2/2 < z$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-area" label="$A(z) =$" expr="z^2/2 - z^4/8"
  vars={{ z: [0.5, 1.8] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "z - z^2/2", hint: "That is the difference of the two bounds on x + y, which is a length. The cross-section is a flat region: find its area." },
    { expr: "z^2 - z^4/4", hint: "You took squares with sides z and z²/2. Each line cuts a triangle off the corner, which is half of such a square." },
    { expr: "z^2/2 + z^4/8", hint: "The region lies between the two lines. The small triangle below the line x + y = z²/2 must be taken away, not added." },
    { expr: "z^2/2", hint: "That is the whole triangle below the line x + y = z. The points with x + y below z²/2 do not belong to the region." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-volume" label="$V =$" value="0.5333" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-volume" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Slice the region horizontally. Each slice is a flat shape whose area can be found with plain geometry, and the volume is the integral of that area over the height.

<Step title="One cross-section">

At a fixed height $z$, the points with $x > 0$, $y > 0$ and $x + y < z$ fill a right triangle with its corner at the origin and two sides of length $z$ along the axes: area $\tfrac12 z^2$. The points with $x + y \le z^2/2$ fill a smaller triangle of the same shape, with sides $z^2/2$: area $\tfrac12\left(z^2/2\right)^2 = z^4/8$. The cross-section is the large triangle without the small one, a strip between two parallel lines:

<Formula label="Area of a horizontal cross-section" tex="A(z) = \frac{z^2}{2} - \frac{z^4}{8}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 1.1]} range={[0, 1.1]} xLabel="x" yLabel="y"
  curves={[
    { expr: "1 - x >= 0 ? 1 - x : NaN", label: "x + y = 1" },
    { expr: "0.5 - x >= 0 ? 0.5 - x : NaN", label: "x + y = ½", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="The cross-section at height z = 1, computed from the two bounds: the strip in the first quadrant between the dashed line x + y = ½ and the solid line x + y = 1." />

</Step>

<Step title="The range of heights">

There are points between the lines only if $z^2/2 < z$, that is, for $0 < z < 2$. At $z = 2$ the two lines fall together and $A = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Add the slices">

A slice of thickness $dz$ has the volume $A(z)\,dz$. The lesson on volumes writes this with $x$ as the direction across the slices:

<Formula label="Volume by slicing" tex="dV = A(x)\,dx, \qquad V = \int A(x)\,dx" cite="reading4" at="p. 1" />

Here the slices are stacked along $z$:

<Formula label="The volume" tex="V = \int_0^2 \left(\frac{z^2}{2} - \frac{z^4}{8}\right)dz = \left[\frac{z^3}{6} - \frac{z^5}{40}\right]_0^2 = \frac{8}{6} - \frac{32}{40} = \frac{4}{3} - \frac{4}{5} = \frac{8}{15} \approx 0.5333" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The area $A(z)$ is zero at $z = 0$ and at $z = 2$ and positive in between, as it should be. Its largest value is at $z = \sqrt2$, where $A = 1 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac12$. So the volume must be less than that of a box with base $\tfrac12$ and height 2, which is 1, and $8/15$ is about half of that: reasonable for a shape that tapers to nothing at both ends.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>
