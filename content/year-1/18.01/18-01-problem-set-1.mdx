## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `(x^2 + 3)/(x - 1)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. A number box accepts any value within half a percent of ours, so keep at least four significant figures while you calculate, and type the number in the unit named in the question. (The round number $C$ of Problem 2 has to be exact.) The points per problem are MIT's, and so are the splits in Problems 2, 5 and 7; the split between the two parts of Problem 1 is ours. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem or part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Parts that ask you to explain or to prove something have no answer box: compare your work with the solution and mark it yourself. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="An even part and an odd part" points={3} mit="Part II, Problem 1">

MIT's Notes G call a function even when changing the sign of $x$ leaves its value unchanged, and odd when it reverses the sign of the value:

<Formula label="Even and odd functions" tex="f(-x) = f(x) \quad \text{(even)}, \qquad f(-x) = -f(x) \quad \text{(odd)}" cite="notesG" at="Section 3, equations (6) and (7)" />

For example, $x^2$ and $\cos x$ are even, and $x^3$ and $\sin x$ are odd. Most functions are neither. The function of this problem is one of those:

<Formula label="The function of Problem 1" tex="f(x) = \frac{x - 1}{x + 1}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1, p. 2" />

Write $f$ as a sum $f(x) = p(x) + q(x)$, in which $p$ is an even function and $q$ is an odd function. Simplify both as far as you can. (The names $p$ and $q$ are ours.)

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Find the even part, $p(x)$.

<Hint>

Suppose you already had $f(x) = p(x) + q(x)$. Replace $x$ by $-x$ on both sides. What do the definitions of even and odd turn the right-hand side into?

</Hint>

<Hint>

You now have two equations, one for $f(x)$ and one for $f(-x)$, with the two unknowns $p(x)$ and $q(x)$. Add them to get rid of $q$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

To simplify, work out $f(-x)$ first and tidy its signs. Then bring $f(x)$ and $f(-x)$ onto one common denominator.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-even" label="$p(x) =$" expr="(x^2 + 1)/(x^2 - 1)"
  vars={{ x: [1.5, 4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(2*x^2 + 2)/(x^2 - 1)", hint: "That is the sum of f(x) and f(-x). The even part is half of that sum." },
    { expr: "-2*x/(x^2 - 1)", hint: "Replace x by -x in your answer: its sign flips, so this function is odd. It belongs in part (b)." },
    { expr: "(x^2 - 1)/(x^2 + 1)", hint: "Check x = 3. There f(3) = 1/2 and f(-3) = 2, so the even part, their average, is 1.25. Your answer gives 0.8 at x = 3: it is upside down." },
    { expr: "(x - 1)/(x + 1)", hint: "That is f itself, which is not even: f(2) = 1/3 but f(-2) = 3." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** If $f = p + q$ with $p$ even and $q$ odd, then $f(-x) = p(x) - q(x)$. Together with $f(x) = p(x) + q(x)$ that is two equations for two unknowns. Adding them gives $p$, and subtracting them gives $q$.

<Step title="The even part of any function">

Add $f(x) = p(x) + q(x)$ and $f(-x) = p(x) - q(x)$, then divide by 2:

<Formula label="Even part of a function" tex="p(x) = \frac{f(x) + f(-x)}{2}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

This really is even: replacing $x$ by $-x$ only swaps the two terms on top.

</Step>

<Step title="The function at −x">

Replace $x$ by $-x$, then multiply the top and the bottom by $-1$:

<Formula label="The function at −x" tex="f(-x) = \frac{-x - 1}{-x + 1} = \frac{x + 1}{x - 1}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

So $f(-x)$ is the reciprocal of $f(x)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Add and simplify">

The common denominator is $(x + 1)(x - 1) = x^2 - 1$:

<Formula label="Even part of the function" tex="p(x) = \frac12\left[\frac{x - 1}{x + 1} + \frac{x + 1}{x - 1}\right] = \frac{(x - 1)^2 + (x + 1)^2}{2\,(x^2 - 1)} = \frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 1}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

In the numerator, $(x-1)^2 + (x+1)^2 = 2x^2 + 2$: the two middle terms $-2x$ and $+2x$ cancel.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Only $x^2$ appears in $p$, so changing the sign of $x$ changes nothing: $p$ is even. A number check follows in part (b).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

Find the odd part, $q(x)$.

<Hint>

Use the same two equations as in part (a), but subtract them. Or, if you trust your $p$, take $q = f - p$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-odd" label="$q(x) =$" expr="-2*x/(x^2 - 1)"
  vars={{ x: [1.5, 4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*x/(x^2 - 1)", hint: "Check the sign at x = 2. There f(2) = 1/3 and f(-2) = 3, so f(2) - f(-2) is negative, but your answer is positive at x = 2." },
    { expr: "-4*x/(x^2 - 1)", hint: "That is the difference f(x) - f(-x). The odd part is half of that difference." },
    { expr: "(x^2 + 1)/(x^2 - 1)", hint: "Replace x by -x in your answer: nothing changes, so this function is even. It is the answer to part (a)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Subtract the two equations of part (a) instead of adding them.

<Step title="The odd part of any function">

<Formula label="Odd part of a function" tex="q(x) = \frac{f(x) - f(-x)}{2}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Subtract and simplify">

<Formula label="Odd part of the function" tex="q(x) = \frac12\left[\frac{x - 1}{x + 1} - \frac{x + 1}{x - 1}\right] = \frac{(x - 1)^2 - (x + 1)^2}{2\,(x^2 - 1)} = \frac{-2x}{x^2 - 1}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

This time the squares cancel and the middle terms add up: $(x-1)^2 - (x+1)^2 = -4x$. The answer can also be written as $2x/(1 - x^2)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Changing the sign of $x$ changes the sign of the top and leaves the bottom alone, so $q$ is odd.

The two parts add up to $f$ again:

<Formula label="The two parts together" tex="p(x) + q(x) = \frac{x^2 - 2x + 1}{x^2 - 1} = \frac{(x - 1)^2}{(x - 1)(x + 1)} = \frac{x - 1}{x + 1}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

With numbers: at $x = 3$, $p(3) = 10/8 = 1.25$ and $q(3) = -6/8 = -0.75$. Their sum is 0.5, which is $f(3) = 2/4$, and their difference is 2, which is $f(-3) = (-4)/(-2)$.

One remark. The original $f$ is undefined only at $x = -1$. The two parts are undefined at $x = 1$ as well, because each of them needs $f(-x)$. So the split holds for every $x$ except $1$ and $-1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="How sensitive is a measurement?" points={6} mit="Part II, Problem 2">

Suppose a quantity $f$ depends on $x$. If $x$ moves from $x_0$ to $x_0 + \Delta x$, the value of $f$ changes by $\Delta f = f(x_0 + \Delta x) - f(x_0)$. The ratio $\Delta f/\Delta x$ tells how strongly a change in $x$, for instance an error of measurement, passes into $f$. It is the difference quotient of the lesson, used here before any limit is taken.

MIT's set applies this on the flat planet Quirk. A navigation satellite hovers 20,000 km above the flat ground. (On Earth, MIT remarks, such satellites do not hover: they circle the planet twice a day.) A receiver on the ground measures $h$, the straight-line distance from the satellite to itself. The letter $h$ stands for hypotenuse. From $h$ the receiver works out $L$, the distance along the ground from the point directly below the satellite to itself. An error $\Delta h$ in the measured distance gives an error $\Delta L$ in the position.

All lengths in this problem are in kilometres.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Take $h_0 = 25{,}000$ km, and call $L_0$ the value of $L$ that belongs to it. With a calculator, work out $\Delta L/\Delta h$ for the six values $\Delta h = \pm 1$, $\pm 0.1$ and $\pm 0.01$ km. Then write an estimate of the form

<Formula label="Estimate of the error in the position" tex="|L - L_0| = |\Delta L| \le C\,|\Delta h|" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

with the simplest round number $C$ that holds in all six cases.

<Hint>

Draw the triangle: the satellite, the point on the ground directly below it, and the receiver. Which side is $h$, which is $L$, and which is the 20,000 km?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The triangle has a right angle at the point below the satellite, so Pythagoras gives $L$ as a function of $h$. Work out $L_0$ first; it is a whole number.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For each $\Delta h$, compute $L$ at $h_0 + \Delta h$, subtract $L_0$ and divide by $\Delta h$. The differences are small, so keep all the digits your calculator shows.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a-ratio" label="$\Delta L/\Delta h$ for $\Delta h = 1$ km:" value="1.667" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p2-a-ratio-plus-1" />

<Answer id="p2-a-c" label="$C =$" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-a-bound" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Pythagoras gives $L$ as a function of $h$. Then $\Delta L/\Delta h$ is a difference quotient that a calculator can work out for each $\Delta h$.

<Step title="The position as a function of the measured distance">

The satellite, the point below it and the receiver form a right-angled triangle with hypotenuse $h$. One of the other sides is the altitude, 20,000 km, and the remaining side is $L$:

<Formula label="Distance along the ground" tex="L(h) = \sqrt{h^2 - 20{,}000^2}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

For $h_0 = 25{,}000$ this gives $L_0 = 15{,}000$ km: the triangle is a 3, 4, 5 triangle, enlarged 5000 times.

</Step>

<Step title="The six difference quotients">

For example, $\Delta h = 1$ gives $L = \sqrt{25{,}001^2 - 20{,}000^2} = 15{,}001.66661$, so $\Delta L = 1.66661$ km. All six:

| $\Delta h$ (km) | $\Delta L/\Delta h$ |
|---|---|
| $+1$ | 1.66661 |
| $-1$ | 1.66673 |
| $+0.1$ | 1.666661 |
| $-0.1$ | 1.666673 |
| $+0.01$ | 1.6666661 |
| $-0.01$ | 1.6666673 |

To four significant figures all six are 1.667.

</Step>

<Step title="The bound">

All six ratios are a little under 1.67. The simplest round number above them is 2:

<Formula label="Bound near 25,000 km" tex="|\Delta L| \le 2\,|\Delta h|" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

An error of 10 metres in $h$ therefore puts the position off by at most 20 metres.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The six ratios close in on $1.6667 = 5/3$ as $\Delta h$ shrinks, from below for positive $\Delta h$ and from above for negative. That limit is the derivative of $L$ at $h_0$. Once you know the chain rule (Lecture 4) you can find it directly: $dL/dh = h/\sqrt{h^2 - 20{,}000^2} = h/L$, which is $25{,}000/15{,}000 = 5/3$ here. MIT's set promises to come back to this problem with calculus.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Do the same for $h_0 = 20{,}001$ km, with the same six values of $\Delta h$. Is $L$ now found more accurately or less accurately than in part (a)?

<Hint>

The method is the same as in part (a). This time $L_0$ is not a whole number: keep all its digits.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Look at $\Delta h = -1$ separately. Where is the receiver when $h$ is exactly 20,000 km?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the last question, compare the two bounds: for the same error in $h$, which case allows the larger error in $L$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b-ratio-plus" label="$\Delta L/\Delta h$ for $\Delta h = +1$ km:" value="82.85" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p2-b-ratio-plus-1" />

<Answer id="p2-b-ratio-minus" label="$\Delta L/\Delta h$ for $\Delta h = -1$ km:" value="200.0" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p2-b-ratio-minus-1" />

<Answer id="p2-b-accuracy" label="Compared with part (a), the same error in $h$ now gives a position $L$ that is found"
  choices={["more accurately", "equally accurately", "less accurately"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same difference quotients, at a point where the receiver is almost directly below the satellite. There the hypotenuse is hardly longer than the altitude, and a small change in $h$ moves $L$ a long way.

<Step title="The starting value">

$L_0 = \sqrt{20{,}001^2 - 20{,}000^2} = \sqrt{40{,}001} = 200.0025$ km. The receiver is only 200 km from the point below the satellite.

</Step>

<Step title="The six difference quotients">

For $\Delta h = +1$: $L = \sqrt{20{,}002^2 - 20{,}000^2} = \sqrt{80{,}004} = 282.8498$, so $\Delta L = 82.8473$ km. For $\Delta h = -1$ the distance is $h = 20{,}000$ km exactly, the receiver sits directly below the satellite, $L = 0$ and $\Delta L = -200.0025$ km. All six:

| $\Delta h$ (km) | $\Delta L/\Delta h$ |
|---|---|
| $+1$ | 82.85 |
| $-1$ | 200.00 |
| $+0.1$ | 97.62 |
| $-0.1$ | 102.64 |
| $+0.01$ | 99.76 |
| $-0.01$ | 100.25 |

</Step>

<Step title="The bound">

The largest ratio is the one for $\Delta h = -1$. To four more digits it is 200.0025, a hair above 200. So $C = 200$ holds in five of the six cases and misses the sixth by 2.5 metres. Taken strictly, the simplest round number that covers all six is $C = 300$:

<Formula label="Bound near 20,001 km" tex="|\Delta L| \le 300\,|\Delta h|" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

If you answered $C = 200$, you have the size right; only the case $\Delta h = -1$ slips past it, by a hair. (MIT gives no solution, so we cannot say which number it had in mind. That is why this bound has no answer box.)

</Step>

<Step title="More or less accurate?">

Less accurate, by a wide margin. Near $h_0 = 25{,}000$ km an error in $h$ is at most doubled. Near $h_0 = 20{,}001$ km it is multiplied by about 100, and by up to 200 within this range. An error of 10 metres in $h$ now moves the position by about a kilometre.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

As $\Delta h$ shrinks, the ratios close in on a value near 100. The derivative from the check of part (a) agrees: $h_0/L_0 = 20{,}001/200.0025 = 100.0$. That is 60 times the $5/3$ of part (a).

The ratios also differ far more from one another than in part (a), from 83 to 200. The reason is the square root: $L^2 = h^2 - 20{,}000^2$ goes to zero at $h = 20{,}000$, and the graph of a square root is vertical where it starts. Close to that point the slope changes quickly, so one number $C$ describes the sensitivity only roughly. Below $h = 20{,}000$ km there is no $L$ at all, so $\Delta h$ could not be more negative than $-1$ here.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="When the ant sees the tower" points={4} mit="Part II, Problem 3">

Also on Quirk, a mobile phone mast stands on a green mound. The mast is a pole 100 feet tall. The mound is 1000 feet high, and its outline is the parabola

<Formula label="Outline of the mound" tex="y = 1000 - x^2" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3, p. 2" />

with $x$ and $y$ in feet. The pole stands on the very top, so it runs from $(0, 1000)$ up to $(0, 1100)$. An ant starts at ground level, $y = 0$, and climbs up the mound. Treat the ant as a point on the outline. At first the bulge of the mound hides the pole from it.

At what height $y$ does the ant first see the tower? Give $y$ in feet.

<Plot
  variable="x"
  domain={[-40, 40]}
  range={[0, 1250]}
  xLabel="x (feet)"
  yLabel="y (feet)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "1000 - x^2 >= 0 ? 1000 - x^2 : NaN", label: "mound" },
    { x: "0", y: "1000 + 100*s", range: [0, 1] }
  ]}
  points={[
    { x: 0, y: 1100, label: "top of the pole" },
    { x: 31.62, y: 0, label: "ant starts" }
  ]}
  caption="The mound and the pole, drawn from the formula of the outline. The two axes have very different scales: the real mound is about 63 feet wide at the ground and 1000 feet high, a needle rather than a hill."
/>

<Hint>

The ant sees a point when the straight line from the ant to that point does not pass through the mound. Which part of the pole comes into view first?

</Hint>

<Hint>

From a point on a curved hill, the lowest line of sight that just clears the hill runs along the hill at that point. What is that line called?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put the ant at $(x_0,\ 1000 - x_0^2)$ and write the equation of the tangent line there. Where does that line cross the vertical line $x = 0$, on which the pole stands?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-y" label="$y =$" value="900" unit="feet" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p3-height" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The mound curves downward everywhere, so it lies below each of its tangent lines. From a point on the mound, the ant can see exactly what lies on or above the tangent line at that point. The top of the pole is the highest point, so it comes into view first, at the moment the ant's tangent line passes through it.

<Step title="The slope of the mound">

The power rule gives the slope of the outline:

<Formula label="Slope of the mound" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = -2x" cite="reading1" at="p. 6 (the power rule, used on a polynomial)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The tangent line at the ant">

Put the ant at $(x_0, y_0)$ with $y_0 = 1000 - x_0^2$, on the right-hand side of the mound, so $x_0 > 0$. The lesson gives the tangent line through a point of a graph:

<Formula label="Tangent line at the ant" tex="y - y_0 = -2x_0\,(x - x_0)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3 (the tangent line, with our slope)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Where the tangent line meets the pole">

The pole stands on the line $x = 0$. There the tangent line has the height

<Formula label="Height of the line of sight at the pole" tex="y = y_0 + 2x_0^2 = 1000 - x_0^2 + 2x_0^2 = 1000 + x_0^2" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

At the start, $x_0^2 = 1000$ and the line of sight passes the pole at a height of 2000 feet, far above its top. As the ant climbs, $x_0$ shrinks and this height comes down.

</Step>

<Step title="The first sight of the tower">

The top of the pole is at 1100 feet. The line of sight reaches it when $1000 + x_0^2 = 1100$, so $x_0^2 = 100$ and $x_0 = 10$. The ant is then at the height

<Formula label="Height at which the ant first sees the tower" tex="y_0 = 1000 - 10^2 = 900 \text{ feet}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

From there on it sees more and more of the pole. The whole pole, down to its foot at 1000 feet, is in view only when the ant reaches the top itself.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $(10, 900)$ the slope of the mound is $-2 \times 10 = -20$. The straight line from $(10, 900)$ to the top of the pole, $(0, 1100)$, rises 200 feet over 10 feet backwards: slope $-20$ as well. So the line of sight is the tangent line.

A second check needs no calculus. A line through the top of the pole with slope $m$ is $y = 1100 + mx$. It meets the mound where $1100 + mx = 1000 - x^2$, that is $x^2 + mx + 100 = 0$. The line only touches the mound when this equation has one double solution, which requires $m^2 = 400$. On the right-hand side that is $m = -20$, and the point of contact is $x = -m/2 = 10$, $y = 900$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A leaking water cooler" points={4} mit="Part II, Problem 5">

A water cooler leaks. At the time $t$, in minutes after the leak begins, the volume of water left in it is

<Formula label="Volume of water in the cooler" tex="V(t) = \frac{(10 - t)^2}{5} \text{ litres}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 5, p. 3" />

(The name $V$ is ours.) In both parts, give the rate at which water leaves the cooler as a positive number, in litres per minute.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Find the average rate at which the water drains during the first 5 minutes.

<Hint>

An average rate of change is a change divided by the time it took. How much water is there at the start, and how much 5 minutes later?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="Average rate of outflow:" value="3" unit="litres per minute" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-average-rate" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The average rate is the difference quotient $\Delta V/\Delta t$ over the 5 minutes. No limit is needed.

<Step title="The volume at the two times">

$V(0) = 10^2/5 = 20$ litres and $V(5) = 5^2/5 = 5$ litres.

</Step>

<Step title="The average rate">

<Formula label="Average rate of change of the volume" tex="\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta t} = \frac{V(5) - V(0)}{5 - 0} = \frac{5 - 20}{5} = -3 \text{ litres per minute}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1 (the average rate of change, with our numbers)" />

The minus sign says the volume goes down. Water leaves at an average of 3 litres per minute.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Three litres per minute for 5 minutes is 15 litres, which is the 20 litres at the start less the 5 that remain.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

At what rate is the water flowing out 5 minutes after the leak began?

<Hint>

Now the question is about one moment, not about an interval. Which quantity of the lesson is the rate of change at one moment?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Multiply out $(10 - t)^2$. The result is a polynomial in $t$, which you can differentiate term by term with the power rule.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="Rate of outflow at $t = 5$:" value="2" unit="litres per minute" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-rate-at-5" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The rate at one moment is the derivative $dV/dt$ at that moment.

<Step title="Differentiate the volume">

Multiplied out, $V(t) = 20 - 4t + t^2/5$. Term by term, the power rule gives

<Formula label="Rate of change of the volume" tex="\frac{dV}{dt} = -4 + \frac{2t}{5}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6 (the power rule, used on a polynomial)" />

</Step>

<Step title="At five minutes">

At $t = 5$ this is $-4 + 2 = -2$ litres per minute. The water flows out at 2 litres per minute.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Go back to the definition. In the short time from $5$ to $5 + \Delta t$ the average rate of outflow is

<Formula label="Average outflow just after five minutes" tex="\frac{V(5) - V(5 + \Delta t)}{\Delta t} = \frac{25 - (5 - \Delta t)^2}{5\,\Delta t} = 2 - \frac{\Delta t}{5}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

For $\Delta t = 1$ minute that is 1.8 litres per minute, for $\Delta t = 0.1$ it is 1.98, and in the limit it is 2.

The answers to (a) and (b) fit together. The outflow falls steadily, from 4 litres per minute at the start to 2 litres per minute at $t = 5$, and it stops at $t = 10$, when the cooler is empty. Because it falls along a straight line, its average over the first 5 minutes is the value halfway, at $t = 2.5$: that is $4 - 1 = 3$ litres per minute, the answer to part (a).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="The product rule for many factors" points={6} mit="Part II, Problem 7">

Throughout this problem $D$ stands for the derivative with respect to $x$, as in the lesson on higher derivatives, and every function is differentiable.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Let $u$, $v$ and $w$ be functions of $x$. Find the formula for the derivative of their product, $D(uvw)$.

In the answer box, type `du`, `dv` and `dw` for the derivatives $u'$, $v'$ and $w'$.

<Hint>

The product rule of the lesson is for two factors. Can you see $uvw$ as a product of two factors?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Group it as $(uv)\cdot w$. Use the product rule once with the factors $uv$ and $w$, and then once more for $(uv)'$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-a" label="$D(uvw) =$" expr="du*v*w + u*dv*w + u*v*dw"
  vars={{ u: [0.5, 3], v: [0.5, 3], w: [0.5, 3], du: [0.5, 3], dv: [0.5, 3], dw: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ du: "u'", dv: "v'", dw: "w'" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "du*dv*dw", hint: "The derivative of a product is not the product of the derivatives. Test it with u = v = w = x: the product is x cubed, whose derivative is 3x squared, but your formula gives 1." },
    { expr: "du*v + u*dv", hint: "That is the product rule for two factors, u and v. The third factor w is missing." },
    { expr: "(du*v + u*dv)*dw", hint: "Group uvw as (uv) times w. The product rule then gives two terms: (uv)' times w, plus (uv) times w'." },
    { expr: "du*v*w + u*dv*w", hint: "Two of the three terms are there. The term in which w is the factor that gets differentiated is missing." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A product of three factors is a product of two, one of which is itself a product. Use the two-factor rule twice.

<Step title="The rule for two factors">

<Formula label="Product rule" tex="(uv)' = u'v + uv'" cite="reading3" at="PDF p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="Use it twice">

Take $uv$ as the first factor and $w$ as the second, then expand $(uv)'$:

<Formula label="Product rule for three factors" tex="D(uvw) = (uv)'\,w + (uv)\,w' = (u'v + uv')\,w + uv\,w' = u'vw + uv'w + uvw'" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 7(a) (our own solution)" />

There are three terms. In each of them one factor is differentiated and the other two are left alone.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $u = v = w = x$. The product is $x^3$ and each derivative is 1, so the formula gives $x^2 + x^2 + x^2 = 3x^2$, which is the power rule for $x^3$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Guess the general formula for $D(u_1 u_2 \cdots u_n)$, the derivative of a product of $n$ functions. Then prove your formula by mathematical induction: assume it is true for every product of $n$ functions, and show that it is then true for a product of $n + 1$ functions.

<Hint>

Look at the pattern for two and for three factors. How many terms are there, and what happens to the factors in each term?

</Hint>

<Hint>

A proof by induction has two steps. First check the smallest case, here $n = 2$ (or even $n = 1$). Then show that the formula for $n$ factors forces the formula for $n + 1$ factors. Together the two steps reach every $n$, one after the other.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the second step, group the product of $n + 1$ functions as $(u_1 u_2 \cdots u_n)\cdot u_{n+1}$. That is a product of two factors, and the assumption tells you the derivative of the first one.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Part (a) shows the pattern: one term for each factor, in which that factor alone is differentiated. The step from three factors to $n$ is the same as the step from two to three: split off the last factor and use the two-factor rule.

<Step title="The guess">

<Formula label="Product rule for n factors" tex="D(u_1 u_2 \cdots u_n) = u_1'\,u_2 \cdots u_n + u_1\,u_2' \cdots u_n + \cdots + u_1\,u_2 \cdots u_n'" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 7(b) (our own solution)" />

There are $n$ terms. In term number $k$ the factor $u_k$ is replaced by $u_k'$ and all the others stay as they are.

</Step>

<Step title="The first case">

For $n = 2$ the formula reads $D(u_1 u_2) = u_1' u_2 + u_1 u_2'$. That is the product rule of the lesson, so the formula is true for $n = 2$. (For $n = 1$ it only says $D(u_1) = u_1'$.)

</Step>

<Step title="From n factors to n + 1">

Assume the formula holds for every product of $n$ differentiable functions. Take $n + 1$ such functions and write $P = u_1 u_2 \cdots u_n$ for the product of the first $n$. (The name $P$ is ours.) A product of differentiable functions is differentiable, so the two-factor rule applies to $P \cdot u_{n+1}$:

<Formula label="Splitting off the last factor" tex="D(u_1 \cdots u_n\,u_{n+1}) = D(P\,u_{n+1}) = (DP)\,u_{n+1} + P\,u_{n+1}'" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 7(b) (our own solution)" />

By the assumption, $DP$ is the sum of the $n$ terms in which one of $u_1, \ldots, u_n$ is differentiated. Multiplying each of them by $u_{n+1}$ gives the first $n$ terms of the formula for $n + 1$ factors: one factor among the first $n$ differentiated, all the others, now including $u_{n+1}$, left alone. The remaining piece, $P\,u_{n+1}' = u_1 u_2 \cdots u_n\,u_{n+1}'$, is the last term: the one in which $u_{n+1}$ is differentiated. Together:

<Formula label="The formula for n + 1 factors" tex="D(u_1 \cdots u_{n+1}) = u_1'\,u_2 \cdots u_{n+1} + u_1\,u_2' \cdots u_{n+1} + \cdots + u_1\,u_2 \cdots u_{n+1}'" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 7(b) (our own solution)" />

This is the guessed formula with $n + 1$ in place of $n$.

</Step>

<Step title="Conclusion">

The formula is true for $n = 2$, and whenever it is true for some $n$ it is true for $n + 1$. So it is true for $n = 3$ (which is part (a)), then for $n = 4$, and so on: for every whole number $n \ge 2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take all $n$ functions equal to $x$. The product is $x^n$. Each of the $n$ terms is $x^{n-1}$, because one factor $x$ has been replaced by its derivative, 1. The formula gives $D\,x^n = n\,x^{n-1}$: the power rule of Lecture 1, found there from the binomial expansion, comes out again.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
