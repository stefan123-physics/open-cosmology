The second of two lectures by the substitute lecturer, and in his words maybe the most technical part of the course (transcript, p. 1). It finishes the trigonometric integrals with secants and tangents, then uses trigonometric identities to integrate square roots of quadratics.

## Secants and tangents

The secant is $1/\cos x$, the cosecant $1/\sin x$, the tangent $\sin x/\cos x$ and the cotangent $\cos x/\sin x$: putting "co" in front exchanges sine and cosine (transcript, p. 1). Dividing $\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1$ by $\cos^2 x$ gives the identity behind this lecture, $\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x$ (p. 1). The quotient rule gives the derivatives (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Derivatives of tangent and secant" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\tan x = \frac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\sec x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos^2 x} = \sec x\tan x" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

so $\int\sec^2 x\,dx = \tan x + c$ and $\int\sec x\tan x\,dx = \sec x + c$ (p. 2). With $u = \cos x$, $\int\tan x\,dx = -\ln|\cos x| + c$ (p. 2), as in the [previous lecture](/courses/18-01/trig-integrals).

For the secant the lecture uses a different trick from the one in the previous lecture's notes. Add the two derivatives: $(\sec x + \tan x)' = \sec^2 x + \sec x\tan x = \sec x\,(\sec x + \tan x)$. Call $u = \sec x + \tan x$; then $u' = u\sec x$, so $\sec x = u'/u$, the **logarithmic derivative** of $u$ (p. 3):

<Formula label="Integral of the secant" tex="\sec x = \frac{u'}{u} = \frac{d}{dx}\ln u \quad\Rightarrow\quad \int \sec x\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

(The lecture and the notes write $\ln(\sec x + \tan x)$; the absolute value is needed where $\sec x + \tan x < 0$.) With these, most trigonometric polynomials can be integrated (p. 3). For example, replace two of four secants with $1 + \tan^2 x$ and substitute $u = \tan x$, $du = \sec^2 x\,dx$ (pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Fourth power of the secant" tex="\int \sec^4 x\,dx = \int (1 + \tan^2 x)\sec^2 x\,dx = \int (1 + u^2)\,du = \tan x + \frac{\tan^3 x}{3} + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

## A trigonometric substitution

Now a hard-looking integral, $\int\frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{1 + x^2}}$ (transcript, p. 4). The square root is the ugly part. The identity $1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta$ writes $1 + x^2$ as a square if $x = \tan\theta$: then $\sqrt{1 + x^2} = \sec\theta$ and $dx = \sec^2\theta\,d\theta$ (p. 4). This is an **inverse substitution**: instead of naming a piece of the integrand $u$, we write $x$ itself as a function of a new variable. Substituting, cancelling, and writing everything in sines and cosines (pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="After the substitution x = tan θ" tex="\int \frac{\sec^2\theta\,d\theta}{\tan^2\theta\,\sec\theta} = \int \frac{\sec\theta}{\tan^2\theta}\,d\theta = \int \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin^2\theta}\,d\theta" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

Rewriting in sines and cosines is the systematic move when it is not clear how a mix of trig functions will simplify (p. 5). Now $u = \sin\theta$, $du = \cos\theta\,d\theta$ gives $\int u^{-2}\,du = -1/u + c = -\frac{1}{\sin\theta} + c$, minus the cosecant (pp. 5–6). Choose $u = \sin\theta$ rather than $\cos\theta$ because then $du$ appears in the numerator (p. 5).

## Undoing the substitution with a triangle

The answer is in terms of $\theta$, but $x = \tan\theta$. Draw a right triangle with angle $\theta$ and choose the sides to make that true: opposite $x$, adjacent 1, so the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{1 + x^2}$ (transcript, p. 6). Then $1/\sin\theta$, the cosecant, is hypotenuse over opposite (pp. 6–7):

<Formula label="Result" tex="\int \frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{1 + x^2}} = -\frac{1}{\sin\theta} + c = -\frac{\sqrt{1 + x^2}}{x} + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 7" />

Writing minus the cosecant of $\tan^{-1}x$ would also be correct, but the triangle gives a far more useful answer (pp. 7–8). (On p. 7 the transcript says "cosine" and "arctan of theta" where it means minus the cosecant of $\tan^{-1}x$.) The same trick evaluates any trig function of an inverse trig function. For the tangent of the angle whose cosecant is $x$ (the arccosecant): take $\theta$ with $1/\sin\theta = x$, so hypotenuse over opposite is $x$; choose opposite 1 and hypotenuse $x$, so the adjacent side is $\sqrt{x^2 - 1}$ and $\tan\theta = 1/\sqrt{x^2 - 1}$ (p. 8).

## The three substitutions

Each identity removes one kind of root (transcript, pp. 8–9; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Trigonometric substitutions" tex="\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}:\; x = a\sin\theta \;\Rightarrow\; a\cos\theta; \qquad \sqrt{a^2 + x^2}:\; x = a\tan\theta \;\Rightarrow\; a\sec\theta; \qquad \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}:\; x = a\sec\theta \;\Rightarrow\; a\tan\theta" cite="reading2" at="pp. 8–9" />

For the first, $x = a\cos\theta$ works equally well and gives $a\sin\theta$ (p. 8). This is how the previous lecture found the area of a piece of a disk, which the notes complete with the triangle trick: $\int_0^x\sqrt{a^2 - t^2}\,dt = \frac{a^2}{2}\sin^{-1}\frac{x}{a} + \frac{x}{2}\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$, a sector plus a triangle (notes, pp. 1–3). The notes remark that when the answer is this complicated, the route to it cannot be simple (p. 2).

## Completing the square

Not every root of a quadratic has one of these forms: there may be a middle term (transcript, p. 9). **Complete the square**: write the quadratic as a square plus or minus a constant. For $x^2 + 4x$, the middle term $2ax$ forces $a = 2$, and $(x + 2)^2 = x^2 + 4x + 4$, so (pp. 9–10; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Completing the square" tex="x^2 + 4x = (x + 2)^2 - 4" cite="reading2" at="pp. 9–10" />

Substitute $u = x + 2$, $du = dx$, to get $\int\frac{du}{\sqrt{u^2 - 4}}$, the third form with $a = 2$. So $u = 2\sec\theta$, $du = 2\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{u^2 - 4} = 2\tan\theta$; everything cancels except $\int\sec\theta\,d\theta$ (pp. 10–11). Then $\sec\theta = u/2$ and $\tan\theta = \sqrt{u^2 - 4}/2$ (p. 11), and the constant $-\ln 2$ can be absorbed (notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Result after completing the square" tex="\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + 4x}} = \ln\left(\frac{x + 2}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{x^2 + 4x}}{2}\right) + c = \ln\left(x + 2 + \sqrt{x^2 + 4x}\right) + \tilde c" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

(For $x > 0$; for $x < -4$ the logarithm needs an absolute value.) The notes end with a preview: next comes integrating any ratio of polynomials, by partial fractions (notes, pp. 4–5).

<WorkedExample title="Three square roots">
<Problem>
Find (a) $\int\frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{4 + x^2}}$, (b) $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 + x^2}}$, (c) $\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 - 6x + 5}}$ for $x > 5$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) x = 2 tan θ">
$dx = 2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{4 + x^2} = 2\sec\theta$, so the integral is $\int\frac{2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta}{4\tan^2\theta\cdot 2\sec\theta} = \frac{1}{4}\int\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin^2\theta}\,d\theta = -\frac{1}{4\sin\theta} + c$. The triangle has opposite $x$, adjacent 2, hypotenuse $\sqrt{4 + x^2}$, so the answer is $-\frac{\sqrt{4 + x^2}}{4x} + c$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) x = tan θ">
The integrand becomes $\sec\theta\,d\theta$, and $x$ from 0 to 1 means $\theta$ from 0 to $\pi/4$: $\ln(\sec\theta + \tan\theta)$ at $\pi/4$ is $\ln(\sqrt 2 + 1) \approx 0.881$, and at 0 it is $\ln 1 = 0$. So the integral is about 0.881.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Complete the square">
$x^2 - 6x + 5 = (x - 3)^2 - 4$. With $u = x - 3 = 2\sec\theta$ this is the integral just done: $\ln\left(x - 3 + \sqrt{x^2 - 6x + 5}\right) + c$. Check by differentiating: the derivative of the inside is $1 + \frac{x - 3}{\sqrt{x^2 - 6x + 5}}$, which divided by the inside is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 - 6x + 5}}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The age of a flat universe">
For a flat universe with matter and vacuum energy, the time it took to grow to scale factor $x$ is $\frac{1}{H_0}\int_0^x\frac{x'^{1/2}\,dx'}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x'^3}}$. The substitution $u = x'^{3/2}$ turns it into $\frac{2}{3H_0}\int\frac{du}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}u^2}}$: a constant plus a square under the root, the tangent form, with $u = \sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}/\Omega_{\text{vac},0}}\,\tan\theta$. The secant integral then gives $\frac{2}{3H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\text{vac},0}}}\ln\left(w + \sqrt{1 + w^2}\right)$ with $w = \sqrt{\Omega_{\text{vac},0}/\Omega_{m,0}}\;x^{3/2}$: an inverse hyperbolic sine. Today, at $x = 1$, it equals the inverse hyperbolic tangent in Lecture 19's formula for the age of the universe.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x$; $(\tan x)' = \sec^2 x$, $(\sec x)' = \sec x\tan x$; $\int\sec x\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + c$.
- Trigonometric substitutions: $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$ with $x = a\sin\theta$, $\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}$ with $x = a\tan\theta$, $\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}$ with $x = a\sec\theta$.
- Undo the substitution with a right triangle whose sides make it true.
- A quadratic with a middle term: complete the square, shift the variable, then substitute.
