Despite its title on OCW, this lecture has no work in the physicist's sense. It puts off boiling the witches' cauldron for a moment to take up one of the most important uses of integrals: **averages** (transcript, p. 1). The cauldron then returns as an example of a weighted average, and averages lead straight to **probability**.

## The average value of a function

Sample $f$ at equally spaced points $x_1, \dots, x_n$ in $[a, b]$, with spacing $\Delta x = (b - a)/n$, and average the values $y_i = f(x_i)$ (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1). The Riemann sum $(y_1 + \cdots + y_n)\Delta x$ tends to the integral; dividing both by $b - a$, and noting $\Delta x/(b - a) = 1/n$:

<Formula label="Continuous average" tex="\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{y_1 + \cdots + y_n}{n} = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The average and the integral differ only by the division by the length of the interval (p. 1). That factor is exactly what makes the average of a constant equal to the constant: $\frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b c\,dx = c$ (p. 2; notes, p. 2).

## The average depends on the variable

Average height of a point on the upper unit semicircle, $y = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$, **with respect to $x$** (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 2): the integral is the area of the half-disk, $\pi/2$, so

<Formula label="Average height, with respect to x" tex="\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{1 - x^2}\,dx = \frac12\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = \frac{\pi}{4}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

Now average the height **with respect to arc length**, the angle $\theta$ from 0 to $\pi$, where the height is $\sin\theta$ (pp. 3–4; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Average height, with respect to θ" tex="\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^\pi \sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{1}{\pi}\Big[-\cos\theta\Big]_0^\pi = \frac{2}{\pi}" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

A different answer, and smaller, since $\pi^2 > 8$. Near the ends of the semicircle a short step in $x$ is a long step along the arc, so averaging over $\theta$ gives more weight to the low parts (pp. 3–5). "The average" means nothing until you say what you average over: picking $x$ at random or picking a point on the arc at random are different experiments (p. 4).

## Weighted averages

More flexible, and closer to real problems, is the **weighted average** (transcript, pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="Weighted average" tex="\frac{\int_a^b f(x)\,w(x)\,dx}{\int_a^b w(x)\,dx}" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

Divide by the **total weight**, so that again the average of a constant $c$ is $c$ (p. 5). The discrete version: shares bought at 10, 20 and 30 dollars in different amounts; the average price is the total spent divided by the total number of shares (p. 6). Unlike a constant, a general $f$ cannot be pulled out, so a weighted average needs two integrals (p. 6).

**The cauldron.** Heat the 1 m cauldron $y = x^2$ to a final temperature $T = 100 - 30y$ °C (transcript, pp. 6–9; notes, p. 3). $T$ is constant on horizontal layers, so slice into disks of volume $\pi x^2\,dy = \pi y\,dy$. The total heat, in degrees × cubic metres, is $\int_0^1 (100 - 30y)\,\pi y\,dy = 40\pi$, about 500 candy bars' worth, as in [Lecture 22](/courses/18-01/volumes) (pp. 7–8). The average final temperature is a weighted average with weight $\pi y$, the volume at each height (pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Average temperature of the cauldron" tex="\bar T = \frac{\int_0^1 T\,\pi y\,dy}{\int_0^1 \pi y\,dy} = \frac{40\pi}{\pi/2} = 80^\circ\text{C}" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

That is lower than the plain average of the hottest and coolest water, $(100 + 70)/2 = 85$ °C, because the cauldron is widest at the top, where the water is cooler (p. 9; notes, pp. 3–4).

<WorkedExample title="A weighted average price">
<Problem>
You buy 100 shares at 12 dollars each, then 50 at 18 dollars and 50 at 30 dollars. What did you pay per share on average? Compare with the average of the three prices.
</Problem>
<Step title="Weighted">
$\dfrac{100 \times 12 + 50 \times 18 + 50 \times 30}{100 + 50 + 50} = \dfrac{3600}{200} = 18.00$ dollars.
</Step>
<Step title="Unweighted">
$(12 + 18 + 30)/3 = 20$ dollars: too high, because more shares were bought at the lowest price.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Probability: the part over the whole

Probability is a special weighted average, where the "function" is 1 on the part you care about and 0 elsewhere (transcript, pp. 9–11). Pick a point at random, uniformly by area, under $y = 1 - x^2$ for $-1 \le x \le 1$. The probability that $x > \tfrac12$ is the part divided by the whole (p. 10):

<Formula label="A probability as a ratio of areas" tex="P\left(x > \tfrac12\right) = \frac{\int_{1/2}^{1}(1 - x^2)\,dx}{\int_{-1}^{1}(1 - x^2)\,dx} = \frac{5/24}{4/3} = \frac{5}{32}" cite="reading2" at="p. 10" />

(The lecture quotes $\tfrac{5}{18}$, then doubts it, since the answer should be less than $\tfrac14$; it is $\tfrac{5}{32} \approx 0.16$, pp. 10–11.) In general, for a point chosen with weight $w$ on $[a, b]$ (p. 11):

<Formula label="Probability with a weight" tex="P(x_1 < x < x_2) = \frac{\int_{x_1}^{x_2} w(x)\,dx}{\int_a^b w(x)\,dx}" cite="reading2" at="p. 11" />

<WorkedExample title="A random point in a triangle">
<Problem>
A point is chosen at random, uniformly by area, in the triangle under $y = x$, $0 \le x \le 1$. Find the probability that $x > \tfrac12$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Part over whole">
$\dfrac{\int_{1/2}^{1} x\,dx}{\int_0^1 x\,dx} = \dfrac{1/2 - 1/8}{1/2} = \dfrac{3/8}{1/2} = \dfrac34$.
</Step>
<Step title="Why not one half?">
Half the $x$-range, but three quarters of the area: the triangle is taller on the right, so points there are more likely.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Darts and the Gaussian integral

The lecture ends by announcing a darts problem; the notes work it out (notes, pp. 4–10). Darts land at distance $r$ from the centre with weight $c\,e^{-r^2}$ per unit area, so a thin ring of radius $r$ and width $dr$ has weight $c\,e^{-r^2}\,2\pi r\,dr$, the shells of the last lecture. Someone standing in a sixth of the ring $2r_1 < r < 3r_1$, for a player who hits the board ($r < r_1$) half the time, gets hit by about 1% of the darts (notes, pp. 5–6).

The notes then compute one of the most important integrals of all (notes, pp. 7–10). Let $Q = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx$. The volume under the surface $e^{-r^2}$, with $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, is $\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}\,2\pi r\,dr = \pi$ by shells. By slices at fixed $y$, each cross-section has area $e^{-y^2}Q$, so the volume is $Q^2$. Hence

<Formula label="The Gaussian integral" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt\pi" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

which gives the limit $\sqrt\pi/2$ promised in [Lecture 21](/courses/18-01/logarithm-and-areas) and the normalization of the normal distribution $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2}$ of statistics (notes, p. 10).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Gaussian fluctuations and the first galaxies">
The tiny density fluctuations left by inflation are, to a very good approximation, Gaussian: at any place the density contrast $\delta$ is drawn from a normal distribution. The probability that a region is dense enough to collapse into a halo is then a part-over-whole integral of a Gaussian, $\int_{\delta_c}^\infty e^{-\delta^2/2\sigma^2}d\delta$ divided by the whole: the starting point of the Press–Schechter count of dark matter halos.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Average of $f$ on $[a, b]$: $\frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f\,dx$. The average of a constant is the constant.
- The average depends on the variable: the semicircle's height averages $\pi/4$ over $x$ but $2/\pi$ over the arc.
- Weighted average: $\int f w\,dx \big/ \int w\,dx$; the cauldron's water averages 80 °C, not 85 °C.
- Probability: part over whole, $\int_{x_1}^{x_2} w\,dx \big/ \int_a^b w\,dx$.
- $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2}dx = \sqrt\pi$, by computing one volume with shells and with slices.
