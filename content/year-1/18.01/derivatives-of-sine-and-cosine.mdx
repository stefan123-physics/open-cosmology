The goal of the first part of 18.01 is to be able to differentiate every function we meet. Lecture 3 adds the trigonometric functions and two powerful general rules. (OpenCourseWare lists David Jerison as the instructor of the recorded lectures; the written notes for this lecture record the 2006 class as presented by Kobi Kremnizer.)

## Two kinds of derivative formulas

Derivative formulas come in two kinds. **Specific** ones give the derivative of one particular function, such as $x^n$ or $1/x$ from Lecture 1. **General** ones say how derivatives combine, whatever the functions are: the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives, and a constant factor comes along unchanged.

<Formula label="Sum and constant rules" tex="(u + v)' = u' + v', \qquad (cu)' = cu' \quad (c \text{ constant})" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Both follow at once from the definition: the difference quotient of $u + v$ splits into the difference quotient of $u$ plus that of $v$, and each tends to its derivative.

<Formula label="Proof of the sum rule" tex="\frac{(u+v)(x+\Delta x) - (u+v)(x)}{\Delta x} = \frac{u(x+\Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x} + \frac{v(x+\Delta x) - v(x)}{\Delta x} \;\to\; u'(x) + v'(x)" cite="reading1" at="PDF p. 2" />

With a few specific formulas and these general rules we can already differentiate every polynomial. This lecture adds sine and cosine.

## The derivative of sin x

Start, as always, with the difference quotient, and use the addition formula for sine:

<Formula label="Addition formula for sine" tex="\sin(a + b) = \sin a\cos b + \cos a\sin b" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

with $a = x$ and $b = \Delta x$:

<Formula label="Difference quotient of sine" tex="\frac{\sin(x + \Delta x) - \sin x}{\Delta x} = \frac{\sin x\cos\Delta x + \cos x\sin\Delta x - \sin x}{\Delta x}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

Setting $\Delta x = 0$ gives $0/0$, as every derivative does. The trick is to **group the terms so that each zero stays over a zero**. Since $\cos 0 = 1$, the term $\sin x\cos\Delta x$ only cancels against $-\sin x$, so they go together:

<Formula label="Grouping the terms" tex="\frac{\sin(x + \Delta x) - \sin x}{\Delta x} = \sin x\,\frac{\cos\Delta x - 1}{\Delta x} + \cos x\,\frac{\sin\Delta x}{\Delta x}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

Two limits from Lecture 2 finish the job. Call them A and B:

<Formula label="The limits A and B" tex="\text{A:}\ \lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{\cos\Delta x - 1}{\Delta x} = 0, \qquad \text{B:}\ \lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{\sin\Delta x}{\Delta x} = 1" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3" />

The first term goes to $\sin x \cdot 0$, the second to $\cos x \cdot 1$:

<Formula label="Derivative of sine" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

## The derivative of cos x

The same steps work for cosine, with its own addition formula:

<Formula label="Addition formula for cosine" tex="\cos(a + b) = \cos a\cos b - \sin a\sin b" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

<Formula label="Difference quotient of cosine, regrouped" tex="\frac{\cos(x + \Delta x) - \cos x}{\Delta x} = \cos x\,\frac{\cos\Delta x - 1}{\Delta x} - \sin x\,\frac{\sin\Delta x}{\Delta x}" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

By A the first term vanishes, by B the second tends to $-\sin x$:

<Formula label="Derivative of cosine" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

The minus sign is the crucial difference.

## Everything comes from one place

Look at the derivatives **at zero**. By definition, and because $\cos 0 = 1$ and $\sin 0 = 0$,

<Formula label="Derivatives at zero" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\cos x\,\Big|_{x=0} = \lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{\cos\Delta x - 1}{\Delta x}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\sin x\,\Big|_{x=0} = \lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{\sin\Delta x}{\Delta x}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–4" />

These are exactly A and B. So the proof shows something striking: the rate of change of sine and cosine at **one** point, $x = 0$, determines it everywhere. The addition formulas carry it round the circle.

## Why A and B are true: short curves are nearly straight

Sine and cosine are defined geometrically, so the proofs are geometric. Rename $\Delta x$ as an angle $\theta$, and draw it at the centre of the **unit circle**. Then $\sin\theta$ is the vertical distance from the circle to the horizontal axis, and, measured in radians, $\theta$ is also the **length of the arc** it cuts off.

**B.** Double the picture, with a copy of the angle below the axis. It looks like a bow and its string: the string is the vertical chord, of length $2\sin\theta$, and the bow is the arc, of length $2\theta$. So

<Formula label="Bow string over bow" tex="\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = \frac{2\sin\theta}{2\theta} = \frac{\text{length of the string}}{\text{length of the bow}}" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

As $\theta$ shrinks, the bow straightens and the two lengths become nearly equal, so the ratio tends to 1. The principle: **short pieces of curves are nearly straight**.

**A.** Write the limit with a positive numerator, $(1 - \cos\theta)/\theta$. In the same bow, the distance from the centre to the string is $\cos\theta$, so $1 - \cos\theta$ is the small **gap** between the string and the middle of the bow. Zoom in as $\theta$ shrinks, keeping the picture big enough to see: the bow flattens onto the string and the gap disappears. Both the gap and $\theta$ tend to 0, but the gap does so much faster, so

<Formula label="The gap goes to zero faster" tex="\lim_{\theta\to 0}\frac{1 - \cos\theta}{\theta} = 0" cite="reading2" at="pp. 5–6 and 9" />

## Radians, not degrees

The proof of B compares a **straight length** with an **arc length** on the unit circle. It only works if the angle is measured by that arc length, and that is exactly what a radian is. (For A the unit hardly matters: changing it only rescales a quantity that tends to 0.) In degrees, which divide the circle into 360 arbitrary parts, B and so both formulas fail:

<Formula label="Only in radians" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x \qquad (x \text{ in radians})" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

As [Lecture 2](/courses/18-01/limits-and-continuity) showed, if $x$ were in degrees, sine at 0 would rise by only $\pi/180 \approx 0.01745$ per degree, not by 1: the formulas would carry this extra factor everywhere.

## A second, purely geometric proof

The lecture then derives $d(\sin\theta)/d\theta = \cos\theta$ for every $\theta$ at once, directly from a picture. A point $P$ moves round the unit circle; its height is $y = \sin\theta$. Move it a little further, by an angle $\Delta\theta$, to a point $Q$, and let $R$ be the corner of the right-angled triangle with $PR$ vertical and $RQ$ horizontal. Then the change in height is $\Delta y = PR$.

- The arc from $P$ to $Q$ has length $\Delta\theta$, and, short curves being nearly straight, the straight segment $PQ$ has almost the same length: $PQ \approx \Delta\theta$.
- The segment $PQ$ is nearly perpendicular to the radius $OP$ (in the limit it is the tangent). The radius makes the angle $\theta$ with the horizontal; turn both lines by 90° and the horizontal becomes the vertical $PR$, while $OP$ becomes $PQ$. So the angle between $PQ$ and $PR$ is also about $\theta$.

In the right-angled triangle $PQR$, the vertical side is the hypotenuse times the cosine of that angle:

<Formula label="The geometric proof" tex="\Delta y = PR \approx PQ\cos\theta \approx \Delta\theta\cos\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dy}{d\theta} = \lim_{\Delta\theta\to 0}\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta\theta} = \cos\theta" cite="reading2" at="pp. 9–12" />

At any visible scale the result is an approximation; in the limit it is exact. Reasoning like this, with small $\Delta x$'s and $\Delta y$'s, is how arc length is developed later, in 18.02 (multivariable calculus).

<WorkedExample title="The derivative of sine, checked numerically">
<Problem>
Estimate the slope of $\sin x$ at $x = 1$ with a difference quotient, $\Delta x = 0.001$, and compare with $\cos 1$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Difference quotient">
$(\sin 1.001 - \sin 1)/0.001 \approx 0.53988$.
</Step>
<Step title="The formula">
$\cos 1 \approx 0.54030$. The two agree to within 0.0005, and the agreement improves as $\Delta x$ shrinks.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The product and quotient rules

Two more general rules, stated at the end of the lecture and proved next time, let us differentiate products and quotients of functions we already know.

<Formula label="Product rule" tex="(uv)' = u'v + uv'" cite="reading2" at="p. 12" />

The way to remember it, and to prove it, is to **change one factor at a time**: change $u$ while $v$ stays fixed, then change $v$ while $u$ stays fixed, and add the two effects.

<Formula label="Quotient rule" tex="\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2} \qquad (v \neq 0)" cite="reading2" at="p. 12" />

The lecture notes already give the proof of the product rule. Add and subtract $u(x + \Delta x)\,v(x)$ in the numerator of the difference quotient:

<Formula label="Proof of the product rule" tex="\frac{(uv)(x+\Delta x) - (uv)(x)}{\Delta x} = \frac{u(x+\Delta x) - u(x)}{\Delta x}\,v(x) + u(x+\Delta x)\,\frac{v(x+\Delta x) - v(x)}{\Delta x} \;\to\; u'(x)v(x) + u(x)v'(x)" cite="reading1" at="PDF p. 3" />

using $u(x + \Delta x) \to u(x)$, which holds because $u$, being differentiable, is continuous (Lecture 2). The notes (PDF p. 4) picture this as a rectangle with sides $u$ and $v$: increasing $v$ by $\Delta v$ adds a strip of area $u\,\Delta v$, increasing $u$ adds $v\,\Delta u$, and the small corner $\Delta u\,\Delta v$ is negligible, so the area changes by about $u\,\Delta v + v\,\Delta u$. They also begin the proof of the quotient rule (PDF p. 5), writing $u(x + \Delta x)/v(x + \Delta x) - u(x)/v(x)$ over a common denominator; the next lecture completes both proofs.

<WorkedExample title="Using the new rules">
<Problem>
Differentiate (a) $x^2\sin x$, and evaluate it at $x = \pi/2$; (b) $\tan x = \sin x/\cos x$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Product rule">
$(x^2\sin x)' = 2x\sin x + x^2\cos x$. At $x = \pi/2$: $2 \cdot \frac{\pi}{2} \cdot 1 + \frac{\pi^2}{4} \cdot 0 = \pi \approx 3.14$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Quotient rule">
With $u = \sin x$, $v = \cos x$: $(\tan x)' = \dfrac{\cos x\cdot\cos x - \sin x\cdot(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} = \dfrac{1}{\cos^2 x}$, using $\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x = 1$. At $x = \pi/4$ this is $1/(1/2) = 2$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology">
In MIT's cosmology course, a closed universe (one with enough matter to stop its own expansion) grows and shrinks like a cycloid: its scale factor $a$ and the time $t$ are written as $a/\sqrt k = \alpha(1 - \cos\theta)$ and $ct = \alpha(\theta - \sin\theta)$, with $\theta$ a parameter. How fast it expands at any moment, $da/dt$, starts from the two derivatives on this page (and the chain rule of the next lecture, which combines them): $d(a/\sqrt k)/d\theta = \alpha\sin\theta$ and $c\,dt/d\theta = \alpha(1 - \cos\theta)$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Derivative formulas are specific (one function) or general (how derivatives combine): $(u + v)' = u' + v'$, $(cu)' = cu'$.
- With the addition formulas and the limits A, $(\cos\Delta x - 1)/\Delta x \to 0$, and B, $\sin\Delta x/\Delta x \to 1$: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$ and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\cos x = -\sin x$.
- A and B are the derivatives of cosine and sine at 0; they determine the derivatives everywhere.
- Geometrically: short pieces of curves are nearly straight (the bow and its string). The formulas hold only for angles in radians.
- A second picture, a point moving round the unit circle, gives $\Delta y \approx \Delta\theta\cos\theta$ directly.
- Product rule $(uv)' = u'v + uv'$ (change one factor at a time); quotient rule $(u/v)' = (u'v - uv')/v^2$ where $v \neq 0$.
