The [first fundamental theorem](/courses/18-01/fundamental-theorem-1) was used to **evaluate** integrals. This lecture reads it the other way: what the derivative $f = F'$ tells us about the function $F$ (transcript, p. 1).

## The average of the derivative

Write $\Delta F = F(b) - F(a)$ and $\Delta x = b - a$. Dividing the first theorem by $\Delta x$ (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="FTC 1 as an average" tex="\frac{\Delta F}{\Delta x} = \frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f(x)\,dx" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The right side is the **average value** of $f$ on $[a, b]$. It is the continuous version of an ordinary average: with $a = 0$, $b = n$ and steps of 1, the Riemann sum $\sum f(i)$ divided by $n$ is the average of $n$ values (p. 2). So the change in $F$ is the average of $F'$ times $\Delta x$.

## Better than the mean value theorem

The [mean value theorem](/courses/18-01/mean-value-theorem) said $\Delta F = F'(c)\,\Delta x$ for some unknown $c$, so all it could give was bounds: $\Delta F$ lies between the minimum and the maximum of $F'$, times $\Delta x$ (transcript, pp. 2–3). The fundamental theorem gives an exact formula, the average, and the same bounds follow at once, since an average lies between the smallest and largest value. As soon as you have integrals, the lecturer says, you can drop the mean value theorem (p. 3).

The lecture redoes a problem from the second exam (pp. 3–4): $F'(x) = \frac{1}{1 + x}$ and $F(0) = 1$; find bounds for $F(4)$. By the mean value theorem, $F(4) - F(0) = \frac{4}{1 + c}$ for some $0 < c < 4$, between $\frac45$ and 4, so $\frac95 < F(4) < 5$. By the fundamental theorem,

<Formula label="The same bounds from an integral" tex="\int_0^4 \frac{1}{5}\,dx \;\le\; F(4) - F(0) = \int_0^4 \frac{dx}{1 + x} \;\le\; \int_0^4 1\,dx" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–4" />

the same answer: the integrand lies between $\tfrac15$ and 1. The two outer integrals are the crudest possible lower and upper Riemann sums, a single rectangle each; more rectangles would give far better bounds (p. 4). (In fact $F(4) = 1 + \ln 5 \approx 2.61$.)

<WorkedExample title="Bounding an arctangent">
<Problem>
$F'(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + x^2}$ and $F(0) = 0$. Without knowing $F$, bound $F(1)$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Bounds on the derivative">
On $[0, 1]$, $1 + x^2$ runs from 1 to 2, so $\tfrac12 \le F'(x) \le 1$.
</Step>
<Step title="Integrate">
$\int_0^1 \tfrac12\,dx \le F(1) - F(0) \le \int_0^1 1\,dx$, so $0.5 \le F(1) \le 1$. In fact $F = \tan^{-1}$ and $F(1) = \pi/4 \approx 0.785$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The second fundamental theorem

Start with a continuous $f$ and define a new function by a definite integral with a **variable upper limit** (transcript, pp. 5–6; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Second fundamental theorem of calculus (FTC 2)" tex="G(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt \;\Rightarrow\; G'(x) = f(x)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Here $t$ is the **dummy variable**, running from $a$ to $x$, while $x$ stays fixed during the integration. Writing $x$ for both is common, even in textbooks, and the lecturer warns that it leads to confusion, especially in multivariable calculus (p. 5). $G$ is well defined, because Riemann sums give a way to compute it for any $x$. And it solves the differential equation $y' = f$ with $y(a) = 0$ (since $\int_a^a = 0$): this is why the course could treat $dy/dx = f(x)$ as always solvable (p. 6).

An example that "everybody gets wrong until they practise it" (pp. 6–7):

<Formula label="Integrate, then differentiate" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\int_1^x \frac{dt}{t^2} = \frac{1}{x^2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 6" />

Nothing to integrate: just copy the integrand with $t$ replaced by $x$. Differentiating an integral gives back the integrand exactly, with no constant: any constant in $G$ disappears when you differentiate (p. 7). Check with FTC 1: $G(x) = \big[-1/t\big]_1^x = 1 - \frac1x$, and $G'(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}$ (p. 7).

<WorkedExample title="Derivatives of integrals">
<Problem>
Find $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_0^x \sqrt{1 + t^3}\,dt$ and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_x^5 \cos(t^2)\,dt$.
</Problem>
<Step title="First">
Copy the integrand: $\sqrt{1 + x^3}$. (No formula for the integral itself is needed, or even known.)
</Step>
<Step title="Second">
The variable is in the lower limit. Reverse the limits first: $\int_x^5 = -\int_5^x$, so the derivative is $-\cos(x^2)$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why FTC 2 is true

A picture proof, with the integral as area (transcript, pp. 7–8; notes, pp. 1–2). $G(x)$ is the area under $y = f(t)$ from $a$ to $x$. Move to $x + \Delta x$: the extra area $\Delta G$ is almost a rectangle of base $\Delta x$ and height $f(x)$:

<Formula label="Proof of FTC 2" tex="\Delta G \approx f(x)\,\Delta x \;\Rightarrow\; G'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x\to 0}\frac{\Delta G}{\Delta x} = f(x)" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

The limit needs $f$ to be continuous, so that its values near $x$ are close to $f(x)$ (p. 8). The notes put it another way: $\Delta G/\Delta x = \frac{1}{\Delta x}\int_x^{x + \Delta x} f(t)\,dt$ is the **average** of $f$ over the small interval, and as the interval shrinks, that average tends to $f(x)$ (notes, p. 2).

## Why FTC 1 is true

Now FTC 1 follows (transcript, pp. 8–9; notes, p. 2). Suppose $F' = f$, with $f$ continuous. Define $G(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$; by FTC 2, $G' = f = F'$. Two functions with the same derivative differ by a constant, $F = G + c$: this step is the one that needs the mean value theorem. The lecturer calls this fact, that functions with the same derivative differ by a constant, the underpinning of all calculus, and it is why the course took the trouble to discuss the mean value theorem (pp. 8–9). Then

<Formula label="Proof of FTC 1" tex="F(b) - F(a) = \big(G(b) + c\big) - \big(G(a) + c\big) = G(b) - G(a) = \int_a^b f(t)\,dt - 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

since $G(a) = \int_a^a f = 0$. Any antiderivative works, because the constant cancels: with $F(x) = -1/x$ or $G(x) = 1 - 1/x$, $\int_1^2 dt/t^2$ comes out the same (p. 10). (The notes illustrate this with "$f(x) = \sin x$" but then integrate $\cos t$, and on p. 3 write $\int_a^b \sin x\,dx = \sin b - \sin a$; they mean $f(x) = \cos x$, notes, pp. 2–3.)

## New functions from integrals

All powers integrate easily except one, $1/x$. The solution of $L'(x) = \frac1x$, $L(1) = 0$, is (transcript, pp. 10–11)

<Formula label="The logarithm as an integral" tex="L(x) = \int_1^x \frac{dt}{t}" cite="reading2" at="p. 10" />

which we know as $\ln x$. Starting from a simple ratio of polynomials, an integral produces a **transcendental** function, and the next lecture derives the logarithm's properties from this definition, more quickly than before (pp. 10–11).

More: the solution of $y' = e^{-x^2}$, $y(0) = 0$ is $\int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt$, the area under the bell curve, and it cannot be written in terms of any function met so far, not logs, exponentials or trig functions (p. 11; notes, p. 1). Yet it is a perfectly good function. The lecturer compares it with $\pi$: the Greeks could define it geometrically, as the area of a unit circle, but it is not the root of any polynomial equation with rational coefficients (p. 11). The notes list more such functions (notes, pp. 3–4): the **error function** $\operatorname{erf}(x) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt$ of statistics, which tends to 1 as $x \to \infty$; the **logarithmic integral** $\operatorname{Li}(x) = \int_2^x \frac{dt}{\ln t}$, which approximates the number of primes below $x$; the Fresnel integrals $C(x) = \int_0^x \cos(t^2)\,dt$ of optics, with $C'(x) = \cos(x^2)$ by FTC 2; and the Bessel function $J_0$.

The notes estimate how many primes lie between $10^{200}$ and $10^{201}$: $\ln t$ is between about 460 and 462 there, which they round to 500 "to one significant figure", so roughly $\int_{10^{200}}^{10^{201}} \frac{dt}{\ln t} \approx \frac{9\times10^{200}}{500}$, which they give as about $10^{198}$ (more precisely $1.8\times10^{198}$): far too many for a code-breaker to try them all (notes, pp. 3–4). (The notes call these "200-digit primes", but numbers from $10^{200}$ to $10^{201}$ have 201 digits.)

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A horizon that grows">
The comoving distance light has travelled since the big bang is $\ell_c(t) = \int_0^t \frac{c\,dt'}{a(t')}$, an integral with a variable upper limit. (The physical horizon distance of 8.286 is $a(t)$ times this.) By FTC 2 its rate of growth is simply the integrand: $\frac{d\ell_c}{dt} = \frac{c}{a(t)}$. Early on, when $a$ was small, the horizon grew fast in comoving terms; later it slows. The horizon problem that inflation addresses starts from exactly this integral.
</Callout>

## Summary

- FTC 1 read backwards: $\Delta F$ = (average of $F'$) × $\Delta x$, with average $\frac{1}{b - a}\int_a^b f$. It gives the mean value theorem's bounds and better ones.
- FTC 2: if $f$ is continuous and $G(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$, then $G'(x) = f(x)$. Keep the dummy variable $t$ distinct from $x$.
- Proof of FTC 2: the extra area is about $f(x)\,\Delta x$. Proof of FTC 1: from FTC 2 and "same derivative, differ by a constant" (the mean value theorem).
- Integrals define new functions: $\ln x = \int_1^x dt/t$, $\int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt$, erf, Li, Fresnel integrals.
