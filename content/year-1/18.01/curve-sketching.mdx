Jerison starts Lecture 10 by finishing approximations, the subject of the previous lecture, and only then begins the topic in the title: drawing a graph from what the first and second derivatives tell you.

## Why linear relations matter

Take the time dilation formula from last time, $T' \approx T(1 + \tfrac12 v^2/c^2)$. Subtract $T$ and divide by $T$ (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Relative change is proportional" tex="\frac{\Delta T}{T} \approx \frac12\,\frac{v^2}{c^2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Both sides are dimensionless, and the content of the formula is a **proportionality**: the fractional change in time is a fixed multiple, here $\tfrac12$, of $v^2/c^2$. Jerison's point is that this is what engineering looks for everywhere: a linear relation between a small change in an input and the change in an output. A real design (a satellite system, a weather or economic forecast, an asteroid's path) involves dozens of such effects, and only after each is simplified to a rule of this kind can anyone see what is going on (pp. 1–2). [He mentions, too, that for GPS the Earth's rotation and the difference in gravity between the ground and the satellite, from general relativity, add further corrections of the same simple kind.]

The constant of proportionality is a **rate of change**, and it depends a little on the base point, but that is a second-order effect that usually changes the answer very little (pp. 2–3).

## Why the quadratic term has a ½

Use the quadratic approximation only when the linear one is not enough (p. 3). With base point 0 it reads $f(x) \approx f(0) + f'(0)\,x + \tfrac12 f''(0)\,x^2$. The ½ is forced: the formula must be **exact for a parabola**, since a parabola is its own best quadratic approximation. For $f(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2$ (the lecture calls the coefficients a, b and c), $f(0) = a_0$ and $f'(0) = a_1$, while $f'' = 2a_2$, so $a_2$ is half of $f''(0)$, and no other factor could work (pp. 3–4).

Jerison then works out the two entries of the quadratic list he had not yet derived last time, $\ln(1 + x) \approx x - \tfrac12 x^2$ and $(1 + x)^r \approx 1 + rx + \tfrac12 r(r-1)\,x^2$, from $f(0)$, $f'(0)$ and $f''(0)$ (pp. 4, 8). The previous page lists all five.

## How fast (1 + 1/k)ᵏ reaches e

Earlier in the course, $a_k = (1 + 1/k)^k \to e$ was shown by taking logarithms. With the linear approximation $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$ at $x = 1/k$ (transcript, p. 5):

<Formula label="The limit by linear approximation" tex="\ln a_k = k\,\ln\!\left(1 + \frac1k\right) \approx k\cdot\frac1k = 1 \qquad (k \to \infty)" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

To see **how fast** $\ln a_k$ approaches 1, you need the next term, which is what the quadratic approximation supplies; the lecture leaves the details to the problem set (p. 5). [The page's own step: with $\ln(1 + x) \approx x - \tfrac12 x^2$, $\ln a_k \approx 1 - \tfrac1{2k}$, so the error shrinks like $1/k$. For $k = 100$, $\ln(1.01^{100}) \approx 0.99503$, against $1 - 0.005 = 0.995$.]

## A quadratic approximation worked in full

When is linear enough? In real problems nobody says; start with the linear approximation, and use the quadratic one only when forced to, because it is much messier (transcript, p. 6). The lecture's example is the function from last time, $e^{-3x}(1 + x)^{-1/2}$ near 0. Multiply the two quadratic approximations, $1 - 3x + \tfrac92 x^2$ and $1 - \tfrac12 x + \tfrac12\left(-\tfrac12\right)\left(-\tfrac32\right)x^2 = 1 - \tfrac12 x + \tfrac38 x^2$, and keep only terms up to $x^2$ (pp. 6–7):

<Formula label="Quadratic approximation of the product" tex="e^{-3x}(1 + x)^{-1/2} \approx 1 - \tfrac72\,x + \left(\tfrac32 + \tfrac92 + \tfrac38\right)x^2 = 1 - \tfrac72\,x + \tfrac{51}{8}\,x^2" cite="reading2" at="p. 7" />

The one good thing about quadratic approximations: all the $x^3$, $x^4$, … terms are dropped, so the long product can be done in your head. For $x \approx 1/100$ they are of order $10^{-6}$, while the answer keeps four decimal places (p. 7). [In the lecture Jerison first calls the last quadratic term $-\tfrac38$; it is $+\tfrac38$, which is what gives $\tfrac{51}{8}$.]

## Curve sketching: what the derivatives say

The goal is to draw the graph of $f$ using the signs of $f'$ and $f''$ (transcript, p. 8; notes, p. 1). A warning first: **do not abandon precalculus skills and common sense.** They often trump the calculus, which only fills in what you did not already know (p. 9).

<Formula label="First and second derivative" tex="f' > 0 \Rightarrow f \text{ increasing}, \quad f' < 0 \Rightarrow f \text{ decreasing}; \qquad f'' > 0 \Rightarrow f \text{ concave up}, \quad f'' < 0 \Rightarrow f \text{ concave down}" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

The second rule is the first applied one step up: $f'' > 0$ means $f'$ is increasing. A graph can be **falling** and concave up at once: a slope that goes from $-4$ to $-3$ is increasing (p. 9).

**Critical points.** If $f'(x_0) = 0$, $x_0$ is a **critical point** and $y_0 = f(x_0)$ is the **critical value** (p. 10). Jerison calls this "the most important definition in this subject". The graph need not turn round at a critical point, which is why "turning point" is not used. (A sharp corner, where $f'$ does not exist, is a different kind of special point, discussed later.)

## Example: y = 3x − x³

Work through $f(x) = 3x - x^3$ (transcript, pp. 10–13; notes, pp. 2, 7):

1. **Sign of f′.** $f'(x) = 3 - 3x^2 = 3(1 - x)(1 + x)$. It is positive for $-1 < x < 1$ (rising) and negative for $|x| > 1$ (falling).
2. **Critical points and values.** $f' = 0$ at $x = \pm1$; $f(1) = 2$ and $f(-1) = -2$. Plot $(1, 2)$ and $(-1, -2)$: the graph is level there.
3. **Common sense.** $f(0) = 0$, and $f$ is odd (only odd powers), so the left half mirrors the right.
4. **The ends.** For large $|x|$ the $-x^3$ term wins: $f \to -\infty$ as $x \to +\infty$, and $f \to +\infty$ as $x \to -\infty$. Jerison stresses this step: it is the part a graphing calculator cannot show you, because it is off the screen (p. 12).
5. **Decoration.** $f''(x) = -6x$: concave up for $x < 0$, concave down for $x > 0$. At $x = 0$, where $f'' = 0$, the bending changes: an **inflection point** (p. 13).

<Formula label="The example's derivatives" tex="f(x) = 3x - x^3, \qquad f'(x) = 3(1 - x)(1 + x), \qquad f''(x) = -6x" cite="reading2" at="pp. 10, 13" />

## The notes' rubric, and three examples

The notes (written for this lecture, while the transcript reaches only the first example) collect the steps into a rubric (notes, p. 2):

1. Plot the **discontinuities** of $f$, especially the infinite ones.
2. Find the **critical points**, where $f'(x) = 0$.
3. Plot the critical points and values, if easy; decide the **sign of f′** between them.
4. Find the **zeros** of $f$, if easy.
5. Find the behaviour at the **ends** (or as $x \to \pm\infty$).

Three examples show why each step matters (notes, pp. 3–5):

- **$y = 1/x$.** $y' = -1/x^2$ is never positive, so you might think $1/x$ decreases everywhere. It does, on each side of 0, but at $x = 0$ it jumps from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$. Find the discontinuities first.
- **$y = x^3 - 3x^2 + 3x$.** $y' = 3(x - 1)^2$: a critical point at $x = 1$, but $y' > 0$ on both sides, so the function increases everywhere and only levels off for a moment at $(1, 1)$.
- **$y = \ln x/x$** (for $x > 0$). As $x \to 0^+$, $y \to -\infty$. $y' = (1 - \ln x)/x^2$, so the only critical point is $x = e$, with critical value $1/e$. The only zero is $x = 1$, and $y \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$.

<Formula label="Critical point of ln x / x" tex="y' = \frac{1 - \ln x}{x^2} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = e, \qquad y(e) = \frac1e" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

## The second derivative test

The sign of $f''$ has two uses (notes, pp. 6–7). It decorates the graph with the right concavity, and it decides what kind of critical point you have:

<Formula label="Second derivative test" tex="f'(x_0) = 0 \text{ and } f''(x_0) < 0 \Rightarrow \text{maximum}; \qquad f'(x_0) = 0 \text{ and } f''(x_0) > 0 \Rightarrow \text{minimum}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Points where $f'' = 0$ are **inflection points**; usually the graph changes there from concave up to concave down or back (notes, p. 7). [Not always: $x^4$ has $f''(0) = 0$ but is concave up on both sides. Many books therefore require a change of sign; the lecture uses the simpler definition.]

<WorkedExample title="Sketch y = x⁴ − 2x²">
<Problem>
Sketch $f(x) = x^4 - 2x^2$: critical points and values, the type of each, inflection points, zeros and ends.
</Problem>
<Step title="Common sense first">
No discontinuities. Only even powers, so $f$ is even: the graph is symmetric about the $y$-axis.
</Step>
<Step title="Critical points">
$f'(x) = 4x^3 - 4x = 4x(x - 1)(x + 1)$, zero at $x = 0, \pm1$. Critical values: $f(0) = 0$, $f(\pm1) = 1 - 2 = -1$.
</Step>
<Step title="Second derivative test">
$f''(x) = 12x^2 - 4$. $f''(0) = -4 < 0$: a maximum at $(0, 0)$. $f''(\pm1) = 8 > 0$: minima at $(\pm1, -1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Inflection points, zeros, ends">
$f'' = 0$ at $x = \pm1/\sqrt3 \approx \pm0.577$, where $f = \tfrac19 - \tfrac23 = -\tfrac59 \approx -0.556$. Zeros: $x^2(x^2 - 2) = 0$ gives $x = 0$ and $x = \pm\sqrt2 \approx \pm1.414$. As $x \to \pm\infty$, $x^4$ wins and $f \to +\infty$. The graph is a "W".
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Sketch y = x e⁻ˣ">
<Problem>
Sketch $g(x) = x\,e^{-x}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Derivatives">
By the product rule $g'(x) = (1 - x)\,e^{-x}$ and $g''(x) = (x - 2)\,e^{-x}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Critical point">
$g' = 0$ only at $x = 1$; $g''(1) = -1/e < 0$, so a maximum, with value $1/e \approx 0.368$.
</Step>
<Step title="Inflection and ends">
$g'' = 0$ at $x = 2$, where $g = 2/e^2 \approx 0.271$: concave down before, concave up after. The only zero is $x = 0$. As $x \to +\infty$, $e^{-x}$ wins and $g \to 0$; as $x \to -\infty$, $g \to -\infty$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The bending of the scale factor">
In cosmology the size of the universe is described by the scale factor $a(t)$. Gravity pulls the expansion back, so for a universe of matter (ordinary and dark) or radiation $\ddot a < 0$: the graph of $a(t)$ is **concave down**, a growing curve that bends over. Dark energy (a cosmological constant) works the other way and, once it dominates, makes $\ddot a > 0$: concave up, an accelerating expansion. Observations of distant supernovae in 1998 showed that our universe changed from one to the other a few billion years ago: an inflection point in its own history.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Approximations are about **proportionality**: a small change in the input gives a proportional change in the output, and the constant is a rate of change.
- The quadratic approximation's ½ makes it exact for parabolas. Quadratic approximations are messy; use them only when the linear one is not enough. Example: $e^{-3x}(1 + x)^{-1/2} \approx 1 - \tfrac72 x + \tfrac{51}{8}x^2$.
- $f' > 0$: rising; $f' < 0$: falling. $f'' > 0$: concave up; $f'' < 0$: concave down.
- Critical point: $f'(x_0) = 0$; critical value $f(x_0)$. Inflection point: $f'' = 0$, where the bending usually changes.
- Rubric: discontinuities, critical points, values and sign of $f'$, zeros, ends. Use common sense (symmetry, easy values) throughout.
- Second derivative test: at a critical point, $f'' < 0$ is a maximum and $f'' > 0$ a minimum.
