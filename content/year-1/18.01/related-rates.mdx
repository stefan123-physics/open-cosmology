Lecture 12 begins by finishing maximum–minimum problems and then introduces **related rates**, which use the same skills: turning words into a diagram, naming the quantities, and writing the equations that link them (transcript, pp. 1, 11).

## Minimum value or where the minimum is?

Jerison opens with the wire cut into two squares from the previous page, and uses it for a point of language (transcript, pp. 1–5). Its least area is $\tfrac1{32}$, and it occurs at $x = \tfrac12$. "What is the minimum?" should be answered with the **value**, $\tfrac1{32}$; "where is the minimum achieved?" with $x = \tfrac12$. Some authors mean yet another thing by the "minimum point": the point $(\tfrac12, \tfrac1{32})$ on the graph. So ask, and answer, clearly (pp. 3, 5).

Two more remarks from that example. Search for both the largest and the smallest value, since you do not know which a critical point is until you compare (p. 4). And the "largest area", $\tfrac1{16}$, is only reached in the limit, when one square shrinks to nothing, which is strictly outside the problem's rules: an honest answer says so (p. 4).

## A box without a top

Find the box without a top, of fixed volume $V$, with the least surface area (transcript, pp. 5–9). Jerison gives one fact in advance: the best box has a **square base**. (That is a separate one-variable problem, about the rectangle of least perimeter; doing both at once is a job for multivariable calculus, pp. 6, 9.) Let the base be $x$ by $x$ and the height $y$:

<Formula label="Volume and area of the open box" tex="V = x^2 y, \qquad A = x^2 + 4xy" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

The volume is a **constraint**: it fixes $y = V/x^2$, so $A = x^2 + 4V/x$ (p. 6). Then

<Formula label="Critical point" tex="A' = 2x - \frac{4V}{x^2} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x^3 = 2V, \quad x = 2^{1/3}V^{1/3}" cite="reading2" at="p. 6" />

[The transcript briefly has $2v/x^2$ and $2x^3$ in this step; Jerison corrects it to $x^3 = 2V$.] Is this the best box or the worst? Check the ends: $x$ runs from 0 to $\infty$ (with $V$ fixed, nothing limits $x$ from above), and $A \to \infty$ at both ends, so the one critical point is the minimum (p. 7). The second derivative agrees, $A'' = 2 + 8V/x^3 > 0$, though Jerison does not recommend that test in general (pp. 7–8).

**What is the answer?** The dimensions are $x = 2^{1/3}V^{1/3}$ and $y = V/x^2 = 2^{-2/3}V^{1/3}$, and the least area is

<Formula label="Least area" tex="A = x^2 + \frac{4V}{x} = 3\cdot 2^{2/3}\,V^{2/3}" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

[The lecture states the area as $3\cdot 2^{1/3}V^{2/3}$ and the height as $2^{2/3}V^{1/3}$ before using $2^{-2/3}V^{1/3}$; the values here follow from $x^3 = 2V$. The ratio $x/y = 2$ below is unaffected; the dimensionless area $A/V^{2/3}$ is therefore $3\cdot 2^{2/3}$, not the lecture's $3\cdot 2^{1/3}$.]

The better answers are **dimensionless** (pp. 8–9). $A/V^{2/3} = 3\cdot 2^{2/3}$ is a pure number, the same in any units and at any scale. Best of all is the **shape**:

<Formula label="The optimal shape" tex="\frac{x}{y} = \frac{2^{1/3}V^{1/3}}{2^{-2/3}V^{1/3}} = 2" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

The best open box has a base twice as wide as it is tall, whatever its size.

## The same problem by implicit differentiation

A faster route to the shape: keep $y$ as an implicit function of $x$ (transcript, p. 10). Differentiate the constraint, with $V$ constant, and the area:

<Formula label="Implicit differentiation" tex="0 = 2xy + x^2 y' \;\Rightarrow\; y' = -\frac{2y}{x}; \qquad \frac{dA}{dx} = 2x + 4y + 4xy' = 2x - 4y" cite="reading2" at="p. 10" />

Setting $dA/dx = 0$ gives $x/y = 2$ directly. It is faster and lands on the scale-free answer; its drawback is that it does not tell you whether the critical point is a maximum, a minimum or neither (pp. 10–11).

## Related rates: a radar trap

Police sit 30 ft from a straight road. Their radar shows your car 50 ft away, approaching along the line of the radar at 80 ft/s. The speed limit, 65 mph, is about 95 ft/s. Are you speeding (transcript, pp. 11–12; notes, p. 1)?

First, decide **what changes**. Let $x$ be the car's distance along the road from the point nearest the police, and $D$ its distance from the radar. Both change with time $t$; the 30 ft does not. Right now $D = 50$ and, from the 3-4-5 triangle, $x = 40$. The radar reads $\dot D = dD/dt = -80$ ft/s, negative because $D$ is shrinking (notes, p. 1). **Do not put in the numbers yet.** Write the equation that holds at every moment and differentiate it:

<Formula label="Pythagoras, differentiated" tex="30^2 + x^2 = D^2 \;\Rightarrow\; 2x\,\dot x = 2D\,\dot D \;\Rightarrow\; \dot x = \frac{D\,\dot D}{x}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Now put in the values: $\dot x = 50 \times (-80)/40 = -100$ ft/s. At 100 ft/s you are speeding (notes, p. 1). [The transcript sets up this example at the end of the lecture and leaves the computation for next time; the notes complete it.]

The notes compare routes (p. 2): differentiating $D = (30^2 + x^2)^{1/2}$ gives the same answer with more work, so differentiate the equation in its **simplest algebraic form**. The general strategy:

1. Draw a picture. Set up variables and equations.
2. Take derivatives.
3. Put in the given values, only **after** taking derivatives.

## Related rates: filling a cone

A conical tank, point down, is 10 ft high with a top radius of 4 ft. Water flows in at 2 ft³/min. How fast does the level rise when the water is 5 ft deep (notes, pp. 2–3)?

The water forms a smaller cone of radius $r$ and height $h$, with $V = \tfrac13\pi r^2 h$. The key is the cross-section: by similar triangles $r/h = 4/10$, so $r = \tfrac25 h$ and

<Formula label="Volume in terms of height" tex="V = \frac{\pi}{3}\left(\frac{2}{5}h\right)^2 h = \frac{4\pi}{75}\,h^3, \qquad \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{4\pi}{25}\,h^2\,\frac{dh}{dt}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

With $dV/dt = 2$ and $h = 5$: $2 = \tfrac{4\pi}{25} \times 25\,\dot h$, so $\dot h = 1/(2\pi) \approx 0.16$ ft/min (notes, p. 3).

The notes end with two more cases of the same idea (one from Problem Set 3), where the question is how sensitive one measured quantity is to another, as in judging whether an asteroid will hit the Earth (notes, pp. 3–4).

<WorkedExample title="A sliding ladder">
<Problem>
A 5.0 m ladder leans against a wall. Its foot slides away from the wall at 0.40 m/s. How fast does the top slide down when the foot is 3.0 m from the wall?
</Problem>
<Step title="Equation">
With $x$ the foot's distance from the wall and $y$ the top's height: $x^2 + y^2 = 5.0^2$ at all times.
</Step>
<Step title="Differentiate">
$2x\dot x + 2y\dot y = 0$, so $\dot y = -x\dot x/y$.
</Step>
<Step title="Values">
At $x = 3.0$ m, $y = 4.0$ m: $\dot y = -3.0 \times 0.40/4.0 = -0.30$ m/s. The top moves down at 0.30 m/s, and faster and faster as the ladder flattens, since $y$ shrinks in the denominator.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Blowing up a balloon">
<Problem>
Air is pumped into a spherical balloon at 10 litres per second ($0.010$ m³/s). How fast is the radius growing when it is 20 cm?
</Problem>
<Step title="Equation and derivative">
$V = \tfrac43\pi r^3$, so $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2\,\dfrac{dr}{dt}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Values">
$\dot r = 0.010/(4\pi \times 0.20^2) \approx 0.020$ m/s, about 2 cm per second. At twice the radius the surface is four times larger, so the radius grows four times more slowly.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Hubble's law as a related rate">
In an expanding universe, the distance between two galaxies is $\ell = a(t)\,\ell_c$, where $\ell_c$ is a fixed comoving distance and $a(t)$ the scale factor. Differentiate with respect to time, as in any related-rates problem: $v = d\ell/dt = \dot a\,\ell_c = (\dot a/a)\,\ell$. The recession speed is proportional to the distance, with the same factor $H = \dot a/a$ for every pair of galaxies. That is Hubble's law, and 8.286 derives it in exactly this way.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Distinguish the minimum **value** from **where** it is achieved; find both the largest and the smallest values.
- Constrained problems: use the constraint to remove a variable, then find critical points and check the ends. The open box of fixed volume has base $= 2 \times$ height.
- The best answers are often dimensionless ratios, valid at every scale. Implicit differentiation reaches them fastest, but does not say whether the point is a maximum or a minimum.
- Related rates: picture and variables, equation, differentiate with respect to time, then put in the numbers.
- Radar trap: $\dot x = D\dot D/x = -100$ ft/s. Cone: $\dot h = 1/(2\pi)$ ft/min at $h = 5$ ft.
