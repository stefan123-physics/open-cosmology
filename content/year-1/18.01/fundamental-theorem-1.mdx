This lecture brings "probably the most important thing" in the course: the theorem that turns the rectangles of the [last lecture](/courses/18-01/definite-integrals) into a calculation with [antiderivatives](/courses/18-01/antiderivatives) (transcript, p. 1).

## The theorem

<Formula label="First fundamental theorem of calculus (FTC 1)" tex="F'(x) = f(x) \;\Rightarrow\; \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

That is the whole theorem (transcript, p. 1; the notes add that $f$ is continuous, notes, p. 1). It links the two uses of the integral sign: the indefinite integral gives the function $F$, up to a constant, and the definite integral gives a number. The constant does not matter: it cancels in $F(b) - F(a)$. The difference is written with a vertical bar, $F(x)$ followed by a bar with $a$ below and $b$ above, the top value minus the bottom value, naming the variable when there is any doubt (pp. 1–2; notes, p. 1). This site writes it with square brackets instead, which read-aloud can say:

<Formula label="Notation" tex="\Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b = \Big[F(x)\Big]_{x=a}^{x=b} = F(b) - F(a)" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

## Examples

The parabola from last time now takes seconds (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1): $x^3/3$ has derivative $x^2$, so

<Formula label="The parabola again" tex="\int_0^b x^2\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^b = \frac{b^3}{3} - \frac{0^3}{3} = \frac{b^3}{3}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

The rectangles still matter, though: later integrals will be used to find volumes, which means running the process the other way (p. 2). Next, the area under one hump of the sine curve, which would be hard by Riemann sums (p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="One hump of sin x" tex="\int_0^\pi \sin x\,dx = \Big[-\cos x\Big]_0^\pi = -\cos\pi - (-\cos 0) = 1 + 1 = 2" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

The lecturer warns that this last, "easy" step is where most arithmetic mistakes happen (p. 2). Any power is now easy too: the transcript does $\int_0^1 x^{100}\,dx = \left[\frac{x^{101}}{101}\right]_0^1 = \frac{1}{101}$ (pp. 2–3), the notes $\int_0^1 x^5\,dx = \frac16$ (notes, p. 1).

<WorkedExample title="Three quick integrals">
<Problem>
Evaluate $\int_1^4 \sqrt x\,dx$, $\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos x\,dx$ and $\int_1^e \dfrac{dx}{x}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Antiderivatives">
$\tfrac23 x^{3/2}$, $\sin x$ and $\ln x$.
</Step>
<Step title="Evaluate">
$\tfrac23(4^{3/2} - 1) = \tfrac23 \times 7 = \tfrac{14}{3} \approx 4.67$; $\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2} - \sin 0 = 1$; $\ln e - \ln 1 = 1$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Speedometer and odometer

An intuitive reading, with time and distance instead of area (transcript, pp. 3–4; notes, p. 2). Let $x(t)$ be your position and $v(t) = x'(t)$ your velocity. Then the theorem says

<Formula label="FTC 1 for motion" tex="\int_a^b v(t)\,dt = x(b) - x(a)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The right side is the difference between two **odometer** readings. The left side adds up **speedometer** readings: an obsessive driver who reads the speedometer every second and multiplies by one second gets $\sum v(t_i)\,\Delta t$, a Riemann sum, very close to the distance travelled because the speed hardly changes within a second (pp. 3–4).

## Area below the axis counts negatively

On a round trip you come back where you started: $x(b) - x(a) = 0$, although you moved. The velocities in the two directions have opposite signs and cancel (transcript, p. 4; notes, p. 2). To keep that reading, integrals allow negative $f$, with no change in the formulas (p. 4):

<Formula label="Two humps of sin x" tex="\int_0^{2\pi}\sin x\,dx = \Big[-\cos x\Big]_0^{2\pi} = -1 - (-1) = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

So the lecturer corrects his own statement of the last lecture: the definite integral is the area **above** the axis **minus** the area **below** it (pp. 4–5; notes, p. 2). An odometer, by contrast, measures the **total** distance; that is the integral of $|v(t)|$, a different question (p. 5).

<WorkedExample title="Net distance and total distance">
<Problem>
A cart moves along a track with velocity $v(t) = 3 - t$ m/s for $0 \le t \le 5$ s. How far is it from where it started, and how far did it travel?
</Problem>
<Step title="Net">
$\int_0^5 (3 - t)\,dt = \left[3t - \frac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^5 = 15 - 12.5 = 2.5$ m forward.
</Step>
<Step title="Total">
It turns round at $t = 3$ s. Forward: $\int_0^3 (3 - t)\,dt = 4.5$ m; back: $\int_3^5 (t - 3)\,dt = 2$ m. Total distance $6.5$ m, net $4.5 - 2 = 2.5$ m.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Properties of integrals

The lecture lists six rules (transcript, pp. 5–7; notes, pp. 3–4). The integral sign is a stretched S for "sum", and integrals behave like sums (p. 5):

1. The integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals.
2. **Constants** (not functions) come out: $\int_a^b c\,f(x)\,dx = c\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$.
3. **Intervals add:** $\int_a^b f\,dx + \int_b^c f\,dx = \int_a^c f\,dx$.
4. $\int_a^a f(x)\,dx = 0$: no width, no area; also $F(a) - F(a) = 0$.
5. A **definition**: reversing the limits changes the sign.

<Formula label="Reversed limits" tex="\int_b^a f(x)\,dx = -\int_a^b f(x)\,dx" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

It is chosen so that the fundamental theorem stays true whichever limit is larger, $F(a) - F(b) = -(F(b) - F(a))$, and with it rule 3 holds for $a$, $b$, $c$ in any order (transcript, pp. 6–7; notes, p. 3).

6. **Estimation:** if $f(x) \le g(x)$ and $a < b$, then $\int_a^b f\,dx \le \int_a^b g\,dx$: if I always go more slowly than you, you get further (p. 7). Here the order of the limits matters.

Estimation reproduces the inequalities of [Lecture 14](/courses/18-01/mean-value-theorem) without thinking (transcript, pp. 7–8; notes, p. 4). Integrate $e^x \ge 1$ from 0 to $b > 0$: $e^b - 1 \ge b$, so $e^b \ge 1 + b$. Integrate that again: $e^b - 1 \ge b + b^2/2$, so $e^b \ge 1 + b + b^2/2$. With $b = 1$ the notes get $e \ge 2$ and then $e \ge \tfrac52$ (notes, p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="One more step towards e">
<Problem>
Integrate $e^x \ge 1 + x + x^2/2$ (for $x \ge 0$) from 0 to 1. What lower bound for $e$ follows?
</Problem>
<Step title="Integrate both sides">
$\int_0^1 e^x\,dx = e - 1$, and $\int_0^1 (1 + x + x^2/2)\,dx = 1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac53$.
</Step>
<Step title="Conclude">
$e - 1 \ge \tfrac53$, so $e \ge \tfrac83 \approx 2.667$. (The true value is 2.718; each step gets closer.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Changing variables in a definite integral

Substitution works as before, with one new feature: the **limits change too** (transcript, pp. 8–10; notes, p. 4). If $u = u(x)$ and $du = u'(x)\,dx$,

<Formula label="Change of variables" tex="\int_{x_1}^{x_2} g\big(u(x)\big)\,u'(x)\,dx = \int_{u_1}^{u_2} g(u)\,du, \qquad u_1 = u(x_1),\; u_2 = u(x_2)" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

The transcript's example is $\int_1^2 (x^3 + 2)^5\,x^2\,dx$: with $u = x^3 + 2$ and $\tfrac13 du = x^2\,dx$, the limits $x = 1, 2$ become $u = 3, 10$, giving $\left[\frac{1}{18}u^6\right]_3^{10} = \frac{1}{18}(10^6 - 3^6)$ (pp. 9–10). The notes do the fourth power instead, $\tfrac{1}{15}(10^5 - 3^5)$ (notes, p. 4). The commonest mistake is to keep the old limits 1 and 2: the $dx$ or $du$ tells you which variable the limits belong to (p. 9).

**A warning:** use it only where $u'$ does **not change sign** (p. 8). Try $\int_{-1}^1 x^2\,dx$ with $u = x^2$: both limits become $u = 1$, so the "answer" would be 0, yet the integral of a positive function is positive (it is $\tfrac23$). The step $dx = du/(2\sqrt u)$ is wrong for negative $x$, where $x = -\sqrt u$. Split the interval where $u'$ changes sign, or avoid such substitutions (pp. 10–11).

<WorkedExample title="A substitution with limits">
<Problem>
Evaluate $\int_0^1 x\,e^{x^2}\,dx$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Substitute">
$u = x^2$, $du = 2x\,dx$ ($u' = 2x \ge 0$ on $[0, 1]$, so no sign change). The limits $x = 0, 1$ become $u = 0, 1$.
</Step>
<Step title="Evaluate">
$\tfrac12\int_0^1 e^u\,du = \tfrac12(e - 1) \approx 0.859$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The age of the universe as a definite integral">
Since $dt = da/\dot a$, the time from the big bang ($a = 0$) to today ($a = 1$) is the definite integral $t_0 = \int_0^1 \frac{da}{\dot a}$. For a flat universe of matter, $\dot a = H_0\,a^{-1/2}$, so $t_0 = \frac{1}{H_0}\int_0^1 a^{1/2}\,da = \frac{1}{H_0}\cdot\left[\frac{2}{3}a^{3/2}\right]_0^1 = \frac{2}{3H_0}$: two thirds of a Hubble time, by the fundamental theorem.
</Callout>

## Summary

- FTC 1: if $F' = f$, then $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) = \big[F(x)\big]_a^b$.
- For motion: the integral of the velocity is the change in position. Area below the axis counts negatively; the total distance is $\int |v|\,dt$.
- Rules: sums and constants come out; $\int_a^b + \int_b^c = \int_a^c$; $\int_a^a = 0$; $\int_b^a = -\int_a^b$; estimation, for $a < b$.
- Change of variables: change the limits too, and only where $u'$ keeps one sign.
