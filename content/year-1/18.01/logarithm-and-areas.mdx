The lecture opens with a correction to the last one: in the discrete average, the Riemann sum must be **divided by $n$**, the number of terms (transcript, p. 1). Then it finishes the second fundamental theorem by using integrals to build functions, before turning to integrals as the answers to geometric questions.

## The logarithm, from its integral

The differential equation $y' = 1/x$ is solved by an integral (transcript, p. 1); the notes introduce it as the missing antiderivative of $1/x$ (notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The logarithm as an integral" tex="L(x) = \int_1^x \frac{dt}{t} \quad (x > 0)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Take this as the **definition** of the logarithm and derive everything from it. That is easier than the earlier route through $a^x$ and the number $e$ (notes, p. 1). It is defined only for $x > 0$, since $1/t$ blows up at $t = 0$. Two facts come straight from the definition (transcript, pp. 1–2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Derivative and starting value" tex="L'(x) = \frac{1}{x}, \qquad L(1) = \int_1^1 \frac{dt}{t} = 0" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

Together they fix $L$ completely. Differentiate once more: $L''(x) = -1/x^2 < 0$, so $L$ is **concave down**; and $L' > 0$, so it is **increasing**. At $x = 1$ it passes through 0 with slope 1 (p. 2). Since it increases, it crosses the level $y = 1$ exactly once, and that point **defines $e$**: $L(e) = 1$ (p. 2).

Why is $L(x) < 0$ for $0 < x < 1$? Two reasons (p. 3): $L$ is increasing and $L(1) = 0$, so it is negative before 1. Or reverse the limits: $L(x) = -\int_x^1 \frac{dt}{t}$, and for $x < 1$ that integral is an ordinary positive area. At the ends, $L \to -\infty$ as $x \to 0^+$ and $L \to \infty$ as $x \to \infty$ (p. 3; the notes prove both from the rule $L(ab) = L(a) + L(b)$ below, notes, p. 2).

## Products become sums

The key property of a logarithm also follows from the definition (transcript, pp. 3–4; notes, pp. 1–2). Split the integral at $a$:

<Formula label="Splitting the integral" tex="L(ab) = \int_1^{ab}\frac{dt}{t} = \int_1^{a}\frac{dt}{t} + \int_a^{ab}\frac{dt}{t} = L(a) + \int_a^{ab}\frac{dt}{t}" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

In the last integral substitute $t = au$, $dt = a\,du$. The limits change too: $t = a$ becomes $u = 1$, and $t = ab$ becomes $u = b$ (p. 4):

<Formula label="Change of variables" tex="\int_a^{ab}\frac{dt}{t} = \int_1^{b}\frac{a\,du}{au} = \int_1^b \frac{du}{u} = L(b) \;\Rightarrow\; L(ab) = L(a) + L(b)" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

The name of the dummy variable, $t$ or $u$, does not matter (p. 4). The notes go on to $L(2^n) = nL(2)$, which shows $L \to \infty$, and $L(1/x) = -L(x)$, which gives the left end (notes, p. 2; the lecture sets this one as homework, transcript, p. 3). From $L$ one can then define $e$, $e^x$ as the inverse of $L$, and $a^x = e^{xL(a)}$ (notes, p. 2).

<WorkedExample title="Logarithms without a calculator">
<Problem>
Given $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$, find $\ln 8$ and $\ln \tfrac12$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Products">
$\ln 8 = \ln(2\cdot2\cdot2) = 3\ln 2 \approx 2.079$.
</Step>
<Step title="Reciprocals">
$0 = L(1) = L(2\cdot\tfrac12) = L(2) + L(\tfrac12)$, so $\ln\tfrac12 = -\ln 2 \approx -0.693$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Sketching a new function

The same method works for a function that is genuinely new, $F(x) = \int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt$ (transcript, pp. 4–6). From FTC 2, $F'(x) = e^{-x^2} > 0$, so $F$ increases; $F(0) = 0$ and $F'(0) = 1$. The second derivative, $F''(x) = -2x\,e^{-x^2}$, makes $F$ concave down for $x > 0$ and concave up for $x < 0$, with an inflection point at 0 (pp. 4–5). $F$ is **odd**, $F(-x) = -F(x)$: it is the integral of an even function starting at 0, and area to the left of 0 counts negatively (p. 5). And it levels off at an asymptote (p. 6):

<Formula label="The limit of the area under the bell curve" tex="\lim_{x\to\infty}\int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt = \frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 6" />

That value is far from obvious, and the course promises to prove it later (pp. 6–7). Rescaling by $2/\sqrt\pi$ gives the **error function** $\operatorname{erf}(x)$, which tends to 1 (p. 6). Others of the same kind: the Fresnel integrals, and the logarithmic integral $\operatorname{Li}(x)$, which approximates the number of primes below $x$; how good that approximation is, is the subject of the Riemann hypothesis (pp. 6–7). The lecturer calls this part cultural: what you need is to handle such functions through their derivatives (p. 7).

## Areas between curves

Now back to integrals as the answer to a question, closer to the first fundamental theorem (transcript, pp. 7–8). For a region between a top curve $f(x)$ and a bottom curve $g(x)$, from $x = a$ to $x = b$, a thin vertical strip has height $f(x) - g(x)$ and width $dx$ (p. 8; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Area between two curves" tex="A = \int_a^b \big(f(x) - g(x)\big)\,dx" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Every such problem comes down to finding three things: the **integrand** and the two **limits**. Leave any one out and the region is not specified (p. 8). And first, **draw a picture** (p. 9).

## Example: a parabola and a line

Find the area between $x = y^2$ and $y = x - 2$ (transcript, pp. 9–12; notes, pp. 3–4). The first is a parabola opening to the right, the second a line of slope 1 through $(2, 0)$. They meet where $y + 2 = y^2$, that is $(y - 2)(y + 1) = 0$: at $(1, -1)$ and $(4, 2)$ (p. 10).

**The hard way: vertical slices.** The top is always $y = \sqrt x$, but the bottom changes: the lower half of the parabola, $y = -\sqrt x$, for $0 \le x \le 1$, then the line for $1 \le x \le 4$. So the area needs two integrals (pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="Vertical slices" tex="A = \int_0^1 \big(\sqrt x - (-\sqrt x)\big)\,dx + \int_1^4 \big(\sqrt x - (x - 2)\big)\,dx" cite="reading2" at="p. 11" />

**The easy way: horizontal slices.** Slice horizontally instead: a strip of height $dy$ runs from the parabola $x = y^2$ on the left to the line $x = y + 2$ on the right, for $-1 \le y \le 2$ (pp. 11–12):

<Formula label="Horizontal slices" tex="A = \int_{-1}^{2}\big((y + 2) - y^2\big)\,dy = \left[\frac{y^2}{2} + 2y - \frac{y^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^{2} = \frac{9}{2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 12" />

Always **right minus left**, so that the integrand is positive; as a check, it vanishes at the crossing points $y = -1$ and $y = 2$ (p. 12). (The transcript says "the right-hand is $y^2$" at this point, meaning the left; the notes write "$x_\text{left} - x_\text{right}$" and "subtract the right curve from the left one" while computing right minus left, and put $dx$ where $dy$ is meant, notes, p. 4.)

<WorkedExample title="Two areas">
<Problem>
(a) Find the area between $y = x$ and $y = x^2$. (b) Find the area between $x = y^2$ and the line $x = 4$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Vertical slices">
They cross at $x = 0$ and $x = 1$, and $x \ge x^2$ in between: $A = \int_0^1 (x - x^2)\,dx = \tfrac12 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac16$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Horizontal slices">
They cross at $y = \pm 2$; the line is on the right: $A = \int_{-2}^{2} (4 - y^2)\,dy = 16 - \tfrac{16}{3} = \tfrac{32}{3} \approx 10.7$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The notes add volumes of solids of revolution, such as a ball, which the next lecture takes up (notes, pp. 5–6).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Counting expansion in e-folds">
Cosmologists measure enormous expansions with the natural logarithm. If the scale factor grows from $a_1$ to $a_2$, the number of **e-folds** is $N = \ln(a_2/a_1)$, and since $\dot a/a = H$, it is $N = \int H\,dt$. Because $\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b$, e-folds from successive stages simply add. Inflation needs at least about 60 of them (Guth's sample numbers give about 65): a growth by $e^{60} \approx 10^{26}$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $L(x) = \int_1^x dt/t$: $L' = 1/x$, $L(1) = 0$, increasing, concave down, $L < 0$ for $x < 1$; $L(e) = 1$; $L(ab) = L(a) + L(b)$ by the substitution $t = au$. This is $\ln x$.
- New functions such as $\int_0^x e^{-t^2}dt$ (odd, tending to $\sqrt\pi/2$), erf and Li are studied through their derivatives.
- Area between curves: $\int_a^b (y_\text{top} - y_\text{bottom})\,dx$, or $\int_c^d (x_\text{right} - x_\text{left})\,dy$. Draw a picture, find the crossing points, and choose the slicing that needs fewer pieces.
