The lecture finishes parametric curves, then introduces polar coordinates (transcript, pp. 1, 8). Area in polar coordinates, which the lecture leaves for next time, is in the notes for this lecture.

## Speed and arc length along a parametric curve

For a curve $x = x(t)$, $y = y(t)$, start from $(ds)^2 = (dx)^2 + (dy)^2$ and divide by $dt$ (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Arc length of a parametric curve" tex="ds = \sqrt{\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{dy}{dt}\right)^2}\,dt" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Rigorously, divide $(\Delta s)^2 \approx (\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2$ by $(\Delta t)^2$, which is small but not zero, and then take the limit, as with any derivative (p. 2). For the circle $x = a\cos t$, $y = a\sin t$, $dx/dt = -a\sin t$ and $dy/dt = a\cos t$, so $ds = a\,dt$ (p. 1). Then $ds/dt = a$ is the **speed**: the circle is traced counterclockwise at constant speed $a$ (p. 1). With $x = a\cos kt$, $y = a\sin kt$ the same circle is traced at speed $ak$ (p. 2).

Squared differentials need care: one writes $(ds)^2 = (dx)^2 + (dy)^2$, and the square of a derivative as $\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right)^2$, never with the square moved inside onto $dx$ and $dt$. The second derivative $\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}$ looks similar but is a legitimate notation of a different kind; for the circle it is $-a\cos t$ (pp. 2–3).

## An ellipse and its perimeter

Take $x = 2\sin t$, $y = \cos t$ (transcript, p. 3). Since $\sin^2 t + \cos^2 t = 1$, the rectangular equation is $\frac{1}{4}x^2 + y^2 = 1$, an ellipse with half-axes 2 and 1 (p. 3; notes for Lecture 30, p. 8). At $t = 0$ it is at $(0, 1)$, at $t = \pi/2$ at $(2, 0)$: this one goes **clockwise**, and once round as $t$ goes from 0 to $2\pi$ (pp. 3–5). Its speed and perimeter are (p. 4):

<Formula label="Perimeter of the ellipse" tex="\frac{ds}{dt} = \sqrt{4\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t}, \qquad \text{perimeter} = \int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{4\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t}\,dt" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

This integral is **not elementary**: no substitution will ever find an antiderivative in terms of familiar functions. That is good news, in a way: the integral is the answer to the question (p. 4). Numerically it is about 9.69. Note also that a parametric curve is not $y$ as a function of $x$: here two points share each $x$, and $x$ and $y$ are now functions of the parameter $t$ (pp. 4–6).

Spinning the right half of this ellipse about the $y$-axis gives an ellipsoid. The distance to the axis is now $x = 2\sin t$, and half a turn of the parameter covers the curve once (pp. 6–7):

<Formula label="Surface area of the ellipsoid" tex="A = \int_0^{\pi} 2\pi\,(2\sin t)\sqrt{4\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t}\,dt" cite="reading2" at="p. 7" />

This one can be done in elementary terms, starting with $u = \cos t$, but it is long (p. 7).

## Polar coordinates

A point in the plane can be described by its distance $r$ from the origin and the angle $\theta$ of the ray from the origin with the horizontal axis (transcript, p. 8; notes, p. 1). The definitions to trust are (p. 8):

<Formula label="Polar coordinates" tex="x = r\cos\theta, \qquad y = r\sin\theta" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

From them, $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and $\theta = \tan^{-1}(y/x)$, but these are ambiguous: $r$ may carry a sign, and $y/x$ is the same as $(-y)/(-x)$, so you must look at a picture (p. 8). The rectangular grid of lines $x = $ constant and $y = $ constant is replaced by a "radar screen" of circles $r = $ constant and rays $\theta = $ constant (p. 9).

The point $(1, -1)$, for example, has $r = \sqrt 2$, $\theta = 7\pi/4$; or $r = \sqrt 2$, $\theta = -\pi/4$; or even $r = -\sqrt 2$, $\theta = 3\pi/4$, pointing the other way and walking backwards (pp. 9–10; notes, pp. 1–2). The usual conventions are $r \ge 0$ with $-\pi < \theta \le \pi$ or $0 \le \theta < 2\pi$, but not everyone follows them (p. 10; notes, p. 1).

## Curves in polar coordinates

- $r = a$ is a circle; $\theta = $ constant is a ray if $r \ge 0$, or a whole line through the origin if $r$ may be negative (transcript, p. 10; notes, pp. 5–6).
- The line $y = 1$: put $y = r\sin\theta$, so $r = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}$, with $0 < \theta < \pi$ (p. 11; notes, p. 6). In this course curves are written as $r$ as a function of $\theta$ (p. 11).
- A circle of radius $a$ centred at $(a, 0)$, passing through the origin (pp. 11–12; notes, pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Off-centre circle" tex="(x - a)^2 + y^2 = a^2 \;\Rightarrow\; r^2 - 2ar\cos\theta = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; r = 2a\cos\theta, \quad -\frac{\pi}{2} < \theta < \frac{\pi}{2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 12" />

Expanding, the $a^2$ cancel and $x^2 + y^2 = r^2$; dividing by $r$ loses only the origin, which the equation reaches at the ends of the range, $\theta = \pm\pi/2$ (p. 12). At $\theta = 0$, $r = 2a$ (the far side of the circle, which is how to remember the $2a$), and $r$ shrinks to 0 at $\theta = \pm\pi/2$ (p. 12). The range matters: the range $\pi/2 < \theta < 3\pi/2$ traces the same circle again with negative $r$ (notes, p. 3).

The notes add two more: $r = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{1}{2}\sin\theta}$ becomes $x^2 + \frac{3}{4}y^2 + y = 1$, an ellipse with a focus at the origin; and $r = \cos 2\theta$ is a four-petalled rose (notes, p. 7).

## Area in polar coordinates

For a region bounded by $r = f(\theta)$ between two rays, cut it into thin wedges. A wedge of angle $d\theta$ is nearly a triangle with height $r$ and base $r\,d\theta$, so its area is $\frac{1}{2}r^2\,d\theta$ (notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Area in polar coordinates" tex="A = \int_{\theta_1}^{\theta_2}\frac{1}{2}r^2\,d\theta" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

For the off-centre circle, $A = \int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{1}{2}(2a\cos\theta)^2\,d\theta = \pi a^2$, using the half-angle formula; for the circle $r = a$ centred at the origin, $\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{1}{2}a^2\,d\theta = \pi a^2$ (notes, p. 5).

<WorkedExample title="A fast circle, a point, a line and a cardioid">
<Problem>
(a) Find the speed along $x = 3\cos 2t$, $y = 3\sin 2t$. (b) Give polar coordinates, with $r > 0$, of the point $(-2, 2)$. (c) Find a polar equation for the line $x = 2$. (d) Find the area inside the cardioid $r = 1 + \cos\theta$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Speed">
$\frac{dx}{dt} = -6\sin 2t$, $\frac{dy}{dt} = 6\cos 2t$, so the speed is $\sqrt{36} = 6$, constant: $ak$ with $a = 3$, $k = 2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A point">
$r = \sqrt{4 + 4} = 2\sqrt 2 \approx 2.83$. The point is in the second quadrant, so $\theta = 3\pi/4$, not the $-\pi/4$ that $\tan^{-1}(2/(-2))$ gives on a calculator.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A line">
$r\cos\theta = 2$, so $r = \frac{2}{\cos\theta}$ for $-\pi/2 < \theta < \pi/2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) The cardioid">
$A = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi}(1 + \cos\theta)^2\,d\theta = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{2\pi}\left(1 + 2\cos\theta + \cos^2\theta\right)d\theta = \frac{1}{2}(2\pi + 0 + \pi) = \frac{3\pi}{2} \approx 4.71$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Distances in angle coordinates">
In polar coordinates the flat-plane rule for distances becomes $(ds)^2 = (dr)^2 + r^2(d\theta)^2$: a step outward counts fully, a step round the circle counts $r$ times the angle. Cosmology writes distances in a closed universe the same way, with angle coordinates on a sphere, where the circle at a given angle from a pole has a radius that is not simply proportional to that angle.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Parametric arc length: $ds = \sqrt{(dx/dt)^2 + (dy/dt)^2}\,dt$; $ds/dt$ is the speed.
- Some arc lengths, such as an ellipse's perimeter, have no elementary formula: the integral is the answer.
- Polar coordinates: $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$; $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ and $\theta = \tan^{-1}(y/x)$ need a picture.
- $y = 1$ is $r = 1/\sin\theta$, $0 < \theta < \pi$; the circle through the origin centred at $(a, 0)$ is $r = 2a\cos\theta$, $-\pi/2 < \theta < \pi/2$.
- Polar area: $A = \int\frac{1}{2}r^2\,d\theta$.
