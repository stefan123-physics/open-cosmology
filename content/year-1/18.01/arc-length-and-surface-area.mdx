After the techniques of integration, the course returns to geometry, as preparation for multivariable calculus (transcript, p. 1). The notes for this lecture are split: arc length starts in the Lecture 30 notes, and surface area is in the Lecture 31 notes.

## Arc length

Think of mileage markers along a winding road: the distance $s$ along the road is the **arc length** (transcript, p. 1). As with Riemann sums, place points above $x_1, x_2, \ldots$ and join them by straight segments. Each segment has horizontal side $\Delta x$ and vertical side $\Delta y$, so its length is close to the length $\Delta s$ of the curve between the points, and in the limit it is exact (p. 1):

<Formula label="Arc length element" tex="(ds)^2 = (dx)^2 + (dy)^2, \qquad ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2} = \sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\,dx" cite="reading3" at="pp. 1–2" />

Here $ds$ is one symbol, and $ds^2$ means $(ds)^2$; this is the first time squares of infinitesimals appear (pp. 1–2; notes for Lecture 30, pp. 4–5). The length of the graph of $y = f(x)$ from $x = a$ to $x = b$ is (transcript, p. 2):

<Formula label="Arc length" tex="s = \int_a^b \sqrt{1 + f'(x)^2}\,dx" cite="reading3" at="p. 2" />

Note that $f'(x)^2$, the square of the slope, is not $f''(x)$ (p. 2). And the limits $a$, $b$ are positions on the $x$-axis, not mileage markers $s_0$, $s_n$ (p. 3).

**A straight line** checks the formula: for $y = mx$, $ds = \sqrt{1 + m^2}\,dx$ and the length from 0 to 10 is $10\sqrt{1 + m^2}$, the hypotenuse given by Pythagoras (p. 3). The point is that once the straight-line case is right, calculus handles every curve by breaking it into tiny straight pieces (p. 3).

## The circle and the meaning of radians

Take the semicircle $y = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$ and measure the arc from $x = 0$ to $x = a$; call its length $\alpha$ (transcript, p. 4). Then $y' = -x/\sqrt{1 - x^2}$ and $1 + (y')^2 = \frac{1}{1 - x^2}$ (p. 4; notes for Lecture 30, p. 6):

<Formula label="Arc of a circle" tex="\alpha = \int_0^a \frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} = \sin^{-1}a, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \sin\alpha = a" cite="reading3" at="p. 4" />

This is more than a calculation (pp. 4–5). It says that the arc length along the unit circle, the angle in radians, is the arcsine of the horizontal distance. Radians were always described as arc length; now they are **defined** that way, and the sine is defined as the inverse of the arcsine, just as the logarithm was defined first as an integral and the exponential after it (p. 5).

## A parabola

For $y = x^2$, $ds = \sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\,dx$, so the length from 0 to $a$ is $\int_0^a\sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\,dx$ (transcript, p. 5). That integral is already an answer, but it can be evaluated with the substitution $x = \frac{1}{2}\tan u$, a long calculation (pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="Length of a parabola" tex="\int_0^a \sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\,dx = \left[\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(2x + \sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\right) + \frac{1}{2}x\sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\right]_0^a" cite="reading3" at="p. 6" />

The factor $\frac{1}{2}$ in the substitution makes $4x^2 = \tan^2 u$ (p. 6). Back in the variable $x$ the limits are the original ones (pp. 6–7). The notes' example is the parametric curve $x = t^2$, $y = t^3$ (see the last section), whose length from $t = 0$ to 1 is $\frac{1}{27}\left(13^{3/2} - 4^{3/2}\right)$ (notes for Lecture 31, p. 1).

## Surface area of revolution

Spin $y = x^2$ about the $x$-axis: it makes a trumpet, and we want the area of its metal, not its inside (transcript, p. 7). A small piece of curve of length $ds$ at height $y$ sweeps a band. Its area is the length of the piece times the circumference: a tilted piece gives more area than a horizontal one, in proportion to its length (p. 7; notes for Lecture 31, p. 2):

<Formula label="Surface area element" tex="dA = 2\pi y\,ds" cite="reading3" at="p. 7" />

For the trumpet from 0 to $a$ the area is $\int_0^a 2\pi x^2\sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\,dx$, which the same substitution evaluates, at length (pp. 7–8). (The notes revolve the curve $x = t^2$, $y = t^3$ instead, with a similar substitution: notes for Lecture 31, pp. 2–3.) Setting up such integrals is what matters; a program can evaluate them (p. 8).

For a **sphere** of radius $a$, $y = \sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$ and the same algebra as for the circle gives $ds = \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}}\,dx$ (pp. 8–9). The section between $x_1$ and $x_2$ has area (pp. 8–9; notes for Lecture 31, pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="A slice of a sphere" tex="A = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} 2\pi\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}\cdot\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}}\,dx = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} 2\pi a\,dx = 2\pi a\,(x_2 - x_1)" cite="reading3" at="p. 9" />

The roots cancel. A hemisphere ($x_1 = 0$, $x_2 = a$) has area $2\pi a^2$ and the whole sphere ($x_1 = -a$, $x_2 = a$) $4\pi a^2$ (p. 9). So every slice of the same width has the same area, near the equator or near a pole. (The transcript has "$x_1 = a$" for the whole sphere where it means $-a$, p. 9.) Rotating about the $y$-axis instead works the same way (p. 9).

## Parametric curves

The last topic starts the move to thinking in several variables (transcript, p. 10). A **parametric curve** gives $x = x(t)$ and $y = y(t)$; think of the parameter $t$ as time, and of the curve as a **trajectory** that passes $(x(0), y(0))$, then $(x(1), y(1))$, and so on (p. 10). Example (p. 10; notes for Lecture 30, p. 7):

<Formula label="A circle, parametrized" tex="x = a\cos t, \quad y = a\sin t \quad\Rightarrow\quad x^2 + y^2 = a^2(\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t) = a^2" cite="reading3" at="p. 10" />

So the trajectory is a circle of radius $a$. To find the direction, plug in values: $t = 0$ gives $(a, 0)$, $t = \pi/2$ gives $(0, a)$, so it goes **counterclockwise**, round again at $t = 2\pi$, like a planet in its orbit (pp. 10–11). Its arc length element is $ds = \sqrt{(dx)^2 + (dy)^2} = a\,dt$ (notes for Lecture 30, p. 8). The notes also do $x = 2\sin t$, $y = \cos t$, an ellipse $\frac{x^2}{4} + y^2 = 1$ traced clockwise (notes for Lecture 30, p. 8).

<WorkedExample title="A curve, a planet and an ellipse">
<Problem>
(a) Find the length of $y = x^{3/2}$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 4$. (b) What fraction of the Earth's surface (radius 6371 km, taken as a sphere) lies within 1000 km of the equatorial plane, measured along the axis? (c) Describe the curve $x = 2\cos t$, $y = 3\sin t$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Arc length">
$y' = \frac{3}{2}x^{1/2}$, so $ds = \sqrt{1 + \frac{9x}{4}}\,dx$ and the length is $\frac{8}{27}\Big[\left(1 + \frac{9x}{4}\right)^{3/2}\Big]_0^4 = \frac{8}{27}\left(10^{3/2} - 1\right) \approx 9.07$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A band of the sphere">
The band has width $x_2 - x_1 = 2000$ km along the axis, so its area is $2\pi a \times 2000$, and the whole sphere is $2\pi a \times 2a$. The fraction is $\frac{2000}{2 \times 6371} \approx 0.157$, about 16%. Because the area of a slice depends only on its width, there is no need to know anything else about the shape of the band.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) An ellipse">
$\frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{y^2}{9} = \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$: an ellipse with half-axes 2 and 3. At $t = 0$ it is at $(2, 0)$, at $t = \pi/2$ at $(0, 3)$: counterclockwise.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Measuring distance in curved space">
The rule $(ds)^2 = (dx)^2 + (dy)^2$ is Pythagoras for an infinitesimal step, and it describes a flat plane. Gauss realised that a curved surface is fixed by its own rule for $ds^2$, its **metric**, and cosmology describes the geometry of the whole universe the same way: in a closed or open universe, the distance along a path is the integral of $ds$ with a metric that is not Euclidean.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Arc length: $ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2} = \sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2}\,dx$; length $= \int_a^b\sqrt{1 + f'(x)^2}\,dx$.
- For the unit circle the arc length is the arcsine: this defines radians, and then the sine.
- Surface of revolution about the $x$-axis: $dA = 2\pi y\,ds$; a slice of a sphere has area $2\pi a$ times its width.
- Parametric curve $x(t)$, $y(t)$: eliminate $t$ for the shape, plug in values of $t$ for the direction; $x = a\cos t$, $y = a\sin t$ is a circle traced counterclockwise.
