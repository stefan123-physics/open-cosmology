With the techniques of integration done, the course clears up loose ends; this one deals with infinity (transcript, p. 1). Limits such as $x\ln x$ as $x \to 0^+$, $xe^{-x}$ and $\frac{\ln x}{x}$ as $x \to \infty$ can be guessed with a calculator, but understanding them needs a tool: **L'Hôpital's rule**, also spelled L'Hospital, since the circumflex in French marks a dropped s (p. 1).

## Where the rule comes from

Take $\lim_{x \to 1}\frac{x^{10} - 1}{x^2 - 1}$ (transcript, p. 1). Plugging in gives $\frac{0}{0}$, an **indeterminate form**. Divide top and bottom by $x - 1$: each becomes a difference quotient, which tends to a derivative. The top tends to $10x^9 = 10$ at $x = 1$, the bottom to $2x = 2$, so the limit is 5 (pp. 1–2). In general, if $f(a) = g(a) = 0$ (p. 2; the notes call this the easy version of the rule, notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="The rule from difference quotients" tex="\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a}\frac{\dfrac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}}{\dfrac{g(x) - g(a)}{x - a}} = \frac{f'(a)}{g'(a)}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3" />

provided $g'(a) \ne 0$ (p. 3). Underneath, it compares the linear approximations of $f$ and $g$ at $a$ (p. 3).

## The rule

The lecture's Version 1, which it calls much better, drops the condition on $g'(a)$ (transcript, p. 3):

<Formula label="L'Hôpital's rule" tex="\lim_{x \to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x \to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)} \qquad\text{if } f(a) = g(a) = 0 \text{ and the right-hand limit exists}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–4" />

Differentiate the numerator and the denominator **separately**: this is nothing like the quotient rule (p. 3). For example, $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin 5x}{\sin 2x} = \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{5\cos 5x}{2\cos 2x} = \frac{5}{2}$ (p. 4). The rule may be used again if the new ratio is still indeterminate:

<Formula label="Applying the rule twice" tex="\frac{\cos x - 1}{x^2} \sim \frac{-\sin x}{2x} \sim \frac{-\cos x}{2} \to -\frac{1}{2} \quad (x \to 0)" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

The logic runs backwards: once the last limit exists, so does the one before it, and so on back to the original (pp. 4–5). Neither $f'(a)$ nor even $f(a)$ and $g(a)$ themselves need to exist; what matters is the behaviour near $a$ (p. 5). (On p. 4 the lecturer first writes $x \to 1$ in the sine example and is corrected to $x \to 0$.) The method of approximations gives the same answers: $\sin u \approx u$ makes $\frac{\sin 5x}{\sin 2x} \approx \frac{5x}{2x}$, and $\cos x \approx 1 - \frac{x^2}{2}$ makes $\frac{\cos x - 1}{x^2} \approx -\frac{1}{2}$ (p. 6; notes, pp. 2–3).

The rule also holds when $a = \pm\infty$, when $f$ and $g$ both tend to $\pm\infty$ (the form $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$), and when the right-hand limit is $\pm\infty$; only wild oscillation defeats it (transcript, pp. 6–7). It applies **only** to the forms $\frac{0}{0}$ and $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$ (p. 9).

## Other indeterminate forms

A **product** like $x\ln x$ as $x \to 0^+$ is of the form $0 \cdot (-\infty)$: rewrite it as a ratio, choosing the logarithm on top (transcript, p. 7):

<Formula label="Zero times infinity" tex="\lim_{x \to 0^+} x\ln x = \lim_{x \to 0^+}\frac{\ln x}{1/x} = \lim_{x \to 0^+}\frac{1/x}{-1/x^2} = \lim_{x \to 0^+}(-x) = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 7" />

The 0 wins (p. 7). The notes add that the other choice, $\frac{x}{1/\ln x}$, leads nowhere (notes, p. 4). For a **moving exponent**, such as $x^x$ (the form $0^0$), use base $e$: $x^x = e^{x\ln x}$, and since $x\ln x \to 0$, $x^x \to e^0 = 1$ (transcript, pp. 9–10; notes, p. 4). Forms like $1^\infty$ and $0 \cdot \infty$ are handled with the same algebra, exponentials and logarithms (notes, p. 4).

## Rates of growth

With $p > 0$, $xe^{-px} = \frac{x}{e^{px}}$ is $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$, and one step gives $\frac{1}{pe^{px}} \to 0$: $x$ grows more slowly than $e^{px}$ (transcript, p. 8). Against $x^{100}$, rather than differentiating 100 times, take a 100th root first (p. 8):

<Formula label="Exponentials beat powers" tex="\lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{e^{px}}{x^{100}} = \lim_{x \to \infty}\left(\frac{e^{px/100}}{x}\right)^{100} = \lim_{x \to \infty}\left(\frac{p}{100}\,e^{px/100}\right)^{100} = \infty" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

So $e^{px}$ grows faster than any power of $x$. And logarithms are slower than any positive power (p. 9):

<Formula label="Logarithms lose to powers" tex="\lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{\ln x}{x^{1/3}} = \lim_{x \to \infty}\frac{1/x}{\frac{1}{3}x^{-2/3}} = \lim_{x \to \infty}3x^{-1/3} = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

Together: $\ln x \ll x^{1/3} \ll x \ll x^{10} \ll e^{ax}$ as $x \to \infty$, for $a > 0$ (notes, p. 4). These rates are what the next lecture needs (transcript, p. 8).

## Look before you L'Hôp

Apply the rule twice to $\frac{\sin x}{x^2}$ as $x \to 0$ and you get $\frac{\cos x}{2x}$, then $\frac{-\sin x}{2} \to 0$. But $\sin x \approx x$ says $\frac{\sin x}{x^2} \approx \frac{1}{x}$, which blows up (transcript, p. 10; notes, p. 3). The second step was wrong: $\frac{\cos x}{2x}$ is of the form $\frac{1}{0}$, not indeterminate, so the rule did not apply (p. 10). **Look before you L'Hôp.**

And do not use the rule as a crutch. For an expression like $\frac{x^5 + 2x^4 + 1}{x^4 + 2}$ as $x \to \infty$ (reconstructed here from the lecture's working), the main terms $\frac{x^5}{x^4} = x$ show at once that it tends to infinity, without four rounds of differentiation (p. 11).

<WorkedExample title="Four limits">
<Problem>
Find (a) $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{e^x - 1 - x}{x^2}$, (b) $\lim_{x \to \infty}x^2e^{-x}$, (c) $\lim_{x \to \infty}\left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right)^x$, and (d) check (a) numerically at $x = 0.1$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Twice">
$\frac{0}{0}$: $\frac{e^x - 1}{2x}$ is still $\frac{0}{0}$; again, $\frac{e^x}{2} \to \frac{1}{2}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A product">
$\frac{x^2}{e^x}$ is $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$: $\frac{2x}{e^x}$, then $\frac{2}{e^x} \to 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The form 1 to the power infinity">
Use base $e$: the exponent is $x\ln\left(1 + \frac{1}{x}\right) = \frac{\ln(1 + 1/x)}{1/x}$, of the form $\frac{0}{0}$. Differentiating top and bottom, $\frac{-1/x^2}{1 + 1/x}$ over $-\frac{1}{x^2}$ gives $\frac{1}{1 + 1/x} \to 1$. So the limit is $e^1 = e \approx 2.718$.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) A check">
At $x = 0.1$: $\frac{e^{0.1} - 1.1}{0.01} = \frac{1.105171 - 1.1}{0.01} \approx 0.517$, close to $\frac{1}{2}$, as it should be.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Switching off the vacuum energy">
For a flat universe of matter and vacuum energy, the age is $t_0 = \frac{2}{3H_0}\,\frac{\tanh^{-1}u}{u}$ with $u = \sqrt{1 - \Omega_{m,0}}$. With no vacuum energy, $\Omega_{m,0} = 1$, so $u = 0$ and the ratio is $\frac{0}{0}$. L'Hôpital's rule gives $\frac{\tanh^{-1}u}{u} \to \frac{1/(1 - u^2)}{1} \to 1$, so $t_0 = \frac{2}{3H_0}$: the age of a flat, matter-only universe, as it must be.
</Callout>

## Summary

- For $\frac{0}{0}$ or $\frac{\infty}{\infty}$: $\lim\frac{f}{g} = \lim\frac{f'}{g'}$, if the right side exists or is $\pm\infty$; also as $x \to \pm\infty$.
- Differentiate top and bottom separately; repeat while the form stays indeterminate.
- Rewrite $0 \cdot \infty$ as a ratio and moving exponents with base $e$.
- $\ln x \ll x^p \ll e^{qx}$ as $x \to \infty$ ($p, q > 0$).
- Look before you L'Hôp, and use simple algebra when the main terms are obvious.
