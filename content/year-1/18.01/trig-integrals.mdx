This lecture, given by a substitute lecturer, collects techniques for integrals involving trigonometric functions: powers of sines and cosines, then secants and tangents (in the notes), then a substitution that uses trigonometry to integrate a square root (transcript, p. 1). The notes for it are numbered Lecture 26; in their calendar Lecture 27 is the third exam (notes, p. 5).

## Trigonometric identities

Everything rests on the unit circle: the point at angle $\theta$ is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$, so $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$, where $\sin^2\theta$ means $(\sin\theta)^2$ (transcript, p. 1). The double-angle formulas are $\cos 2\theta = \cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta$ and $\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$ (p. 1). Replacing $\sin^2\theta$ by $1 - \cos^2\theta$ in the first gives $\cos 2\theta = 2\cos^2\theta - 1$, and solving for the square (pp. 1–2; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Half-angle formulas" tex="\cos^2\theta = \frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}, \qquad \sin^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

For integration they are a way to **remove squares**, at the cost of doubling the angle (p. 2). The starting integrals are $\int\cos x\,dx = \sin x + c$ and $\int\sin x\,dx = -\cos x + c$ (p. 2; notes, p. 1).

## Powers of sine and cosine: an odd exponent

The family is $\int\sin^n x\,\cos^m x\,dx$ with $n$ and $m$ whole numbers, $0, 1, 2, \ldots$; such integrals come up in Fourier series and elsewhere (transcript, p. 2). The **easy case** is when at least one exponent is odd (p. 2; notes, Method A, p. 1). With $m = 1$, the factor $\cos x\,dx$ is exactly $d(\sin x)$, so substitute $u = \sin x$, $du = \cos x\,dx$ (pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="One factor of cosine" tex="\int \sin^n x\,\cos x\,dx = \int u^n\,du = \frac{u^{n+1}}{n + 1} + c = \frac{\sin^{n+1}x}{n + 1} + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

Then substitute back: you introduced $u$, but the question was asked in terms of $x$ (p. 3). When the odd power is higher, keep one factor and turn the remaining **even** power into the other function with $\sin^2 x = 1 - \cos^2 x$ (p. 3):

<Formula label="An odd power of sine" tex="\int \sin^3 x\,\cos^2 x\,dx = \int (1 - \cos^2 x)\cos^2 x\,\sin x\,dx = -\int (u^2 - u^4)\,du = -\frac{\cos^3 x}{3} + \frac{\cos^5 x}{5} + c" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–4" />

using $u = \cos x$, $du = -\sin x\,dx$; mind the minus sign (p. 4; notes, Example 1, p. 1). Differentiating the answer is an easy check (p. 4). With no cosine at all, $\int\sin^3 x\,dx = \int(1 - \cos^2 x)\sin x\,dx = -\cos x + \frac{\cos^3 x}{3} + c$ (p. 4). (The transcript drops the minus sign on $\cos x$ in this last answer, p. 4; differentiating confirms $-\cos x$.) The notes do $\int\cos^3 x\,dx = \sin x - \frac{\sin^3 x}{3} + c$ the same way, with $u = \sin x$ (notes, Example 2, p. 1).

## Powers of sine and cosine: all even

The **harder case** is when every exponent is even: there is no spare factor to serve as $du$, and writing $\cos^2 x = 1 - \sin^2 x$ only swaps one even power for another (transcript, p. 5). Use the half-angle formulas instead (p. 5; notes, Method B, p. 2):

<Formula label="Cosine squared" tex="\int \cos^2 x\,dx = \int \frac{1 + \cos 2x}{2}\,dx = \frac{x}{2} + \frac{\sin 2x}{4} + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

The $\frac{1}{4}$ comes from the chain rule: differentiating $\sin 2x$ brings out a 2 (p. 5). Note that a non-trigonometric term, $x/2$, appears (p. 5). In the same way $\int\sin^2 x\,dx = \frac{x}{2} - \frac{\sin 2x}{4} + c$ (notes, p. 2).

For $\sin^2 x\cos^2 x$, do the trigonometry on the side first (pp. 5–6). Both half-angle formulas, then $(a - b)(a + b) = a^2 - b^2$, then the half-angle formula once more:

<Formula label="Sine squared times cosine squared" tex="\sin^2 x\cos^2 x = \frac{1 - \cos^2 2x}{4} = \frac{1}{8} - \frac{\cos 4x}{8}, \qquad \int \sin^2 x\cos^2 x\,dx = \frac{x}{8} - \frac{\sin 4x}{32} + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 6" />

A shortcut: $\sin x\cos x = \frac{1}{2}\sin 2x$, so $\sin^2 x\cos^2 x = \frac{1}{4}\sin^2 2x$, and one half-angle step gives the same expression (pp. 6–7; notes, Example 3, p. 2). Alternating these two procedures handles any $\sin^n x\,\cos^m x$ (transcript, p. 6; notes, p. 2).

## Secants and tangents

The notes start a second family, $\int\sec^n x\,\tan^m x\,dx$, with the identity $\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x$ and the derivatives $\frac{d}{dx}\tan x = \sec^2 x$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\sec x = \sec x\tan x$ (notes, p. 2). The same ideas work:

- $\int\tan x\,dx = \int\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\,dx = -\ln|\cos x| + c$, with $u = \cos x$ (notes, p. 3).
- An **even power of secant**: $\int\sec^4 x\,dx = \int(1 + \tan^2 x)\sec^2 x\,dx = \tan x + \frac{\tan^3 x}{3} + c$, with $u = \tan x$ (p. 3).
- An **odd power of tangent**: $\int\tan^3 x\,\sec x\,dx = \int(\sec^2 x - 1)\sec x\tan x\,dx = \frac{\sec^3 x}{3} - \sec x + c$, with $u = \sec x$ (p. 3).

And one integral found by "knowing the answer ahead of time": multiply and divide by $\sec x + \tan x$, and the numerator becomes the derivative of the denominator (notes, pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Integral of the secant" tex="\int \sec x\,dx = \int \frac{\sec^2 x + \sec x\tan x}{\sec x + \tan x}\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + c" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–4" />

(The notes write these logarithms without the absolute value signs, which are needed where the inside is negative.) Odd powers of secant with even powers of tangent are left for later (p. 4).

## Trigonometric substitution

Now a problem with no trigonometry in it. A disk of radius $a$ is centred at the origin; cut out the piece in the first quadrant below the height $y = b$. What is its area (transcript, p. 7)? Vertical strips are awkward, because the top edge is flat and then curved. Horizontal strips of width $dy$ and length $x = \sqrt{a^2 - y^2}$ work in one piece (p. 7):

<Formula label="Area of the piece" tex="A = \int_0^b \sqrt{a^2 - y^2}\,dy" cite="reading2" at="p. 7" />

The square root comes from the circle, so use the circle: put $y = a\sin\theta$, as in polar coordinates (pp. 7–8). Then $\sqrt{a^2 - y^2} = \sqrt{a^2 - a^2\sin^2\theta} = a\cos\theta$ and $dy = a\cos\theta\,d\theta$, so the square root disappears and only trigonometry is left (p. 8; notes, p. 5):

<Formula label="The substitution y = a sin θ" tex="\int \sqrt{a^2 - y^2}\,dy = a^2\int \cos^2\theta\,d\theta = a^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2} + \frac{\sin 2\theta}{4}\right) + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

using the cosine-squared integral from before. To go back to $y$, write $\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$, $\theta = \arcsin(y/a)$, $a\sin\theta = y$ and $a\cos\theta = \sqrt{a^2 - y^2}$ (pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Integral of the square root" tex="\int \sqrt{a^2 - y^2}\,dy = \frac{a^2}{2}\arcsin\frac{y}{a} + \frac{y\sqrt{a^2 - y^2}}{2} + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

Whenever you see a constant minus a square under a root, think of $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$; the substitution $y = a\cos\theta$ works just as well (p. 9). At $y = 0$ both terms vanish, so with $\theta_0 = \arcsin(b/a)$, the angle at the corner (pp. 9–10):

<Formula label="The area, read geometrically" tex="A = \frac{a^2\theta_0}{2} + \frac{b\sqrt{a^2 - b^2}}{2}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 9–10" />

The first term is the area of a **sector** of angle $\theta_0$, the second the area of a right **triangle** with legs $b$ and $\sqrt{a^2 - b^2}$: plain geometry gives the same answer as the calculus (p. 10).

<WorkedExample title="Three integrals">
<Problem>
Find (a) $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^3 x\,dx$, (b) $\int_0^\pi\sin^2 x\cos^2 x\,dx$, (c) the area of the piece of a disk of radius 2 below the height 1, in the first quadrant.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) An odd power">
$\int\sin^3 x\,dx = -\cos x + \frac{\cos^3 x}{3} + c$. At $\pi/2$ this is 0; at 0 it is $-1 + \frac{1}{3} = -\frac{2}{3}$. So the integral is $\frac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Even powers">
$\Big[\frac{x}{8} - \frac{\sin 4x}{32}\Big]_0^\pi = \frac{\pi}{8} \approx 0.393$, since $\sin 4\pi = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The trigonometric substitution">
With $a = 2$, $b = 1$: $\theta_0 = \arcsin\frac{1}{2} = \frac{\pi}{6}$, so $A = \frac{4}{2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{1 \cdot \sqrt 3}{2} = \frac{\pi}{3} + \frac{\sqrt 3}{2} \approx 1.047 + 0.866 = 1.913$. The piece is a little less than the rectangle of width 2 and height 1: it must be, since the length $x = \sqrt{4 - y^2}$ of each horizontal strip is at most 2.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A closed universe">
For a closed universe filled with matter, Friedmann's equation leads to an integral of the form $\int \tilde a\,d\tilde a/\sqrt{2\alpha\tilde a - \tilde a^2}$. Completing the square turns the root into $\sqrt{\alpha^2 - x^2}$, and the substitution of this lecture, with a cosine instead of a sine, removes it. The result is the parametric solution $ct = \alpha(\theta - \sin\theta)$, $a/\sqrt k = \alpha(1 - \cos\theta)$: the universe grows and recollapses along a cycloid.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Half-angle formulas: $\cos^2\theta = \frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}$, $\sin^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2}$.
- $\int\sin^n x\cos^m x\,dx$ with an odd exponent: keep one factor for $du$, convert the rest with $\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1$, substitute.
- All exponents even: lower the powers with the half-angle formulas, repeatedly if needed.
- Secants and tangents: $\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x$; $\int\tan x\,dx = -\ln|\cos x| + c$, $\int\sec x\,dx = \ln|\sec x + \tan x| + c$.
- $\sqrt{a^2 - y^2}$: substitute $y = a\sin\theta$; the area of a piece of a disk splits into a sector and a triangle.
