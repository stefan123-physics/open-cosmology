Lecture 11 is two lectures in one. Jerison first finishes curve sketching with two more examples and a general strategy, and then begins the subject in the title: finding the largest and smallest values of a function (transcript, pp. 1, 11). The notes for this lecture contain only the maximum–minimum part, so the sketching half here follows the transcript.

## A function with no critical points

Sketch $f(x) = (x + 1)/(x + 2)$. Its derivative is $f'(x) = 1/(x + 2)^2$, which is **never zero**: there are no critical points (transcript, p. 1). The lesson: do not freeze. Go back to precalculus and **plot points**, and the most important point to plot is the one that is not there, $x = -2$, where $f$ is undefined (p. 1).

Approach $-2$ from each side. The notation $(-2)^+$ means "a little more than $-2$", and $(-2)^-$ "a little less" (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="One-sided values at the singularity" tex="f\bigl((-2)^+\bigr) = \frac{-1}{0^+} = -\infty, \qquad f\bigl((-2)^-\bigr) = \frac{-1}{0^-} = +\infty" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

Then the **ends**, $x \to \pm\infty$: divide top and bottom by $x$ to get $(1 + 1/x)/(1 + 2/x) \to 1$ (p. 3). So there is a vertical asymptote at $x = -2$ and a horizontal one at $y = 1$. Can the graph cross the level 1 and come back? No: that would need a horizontal tangent, and $f'$ is never zero, so the graph **cannot backtrack** (p. 3).

Two checks (pp. 4–5). Writing $f(x) = 1 - 1/(x + 2)$ shows at once that $f' = 1/(x + 2)^2 > 0$ and that the graph is a hyperbola. And

<Formula label="Second derivative" tex="f''(x) = \frac{-2}{(x + 2)^3}" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

is negative for $x > -2$ (concave down) and positive for $x < -2$ (concave up): the second derivative only rules out a wiggle.

**A warning** (p. 4): $f$ is increasing on $x < -2$ and on $x > -2$, but it is **not** increasing on the whole line, since it jumps from $+\infty$ down to $-\infty$ at $x = -2$. Ignoring that point is like ignoring Mount Everest, the most important feature of the function. And a remark (p. 5): $f' > 0$ always means increasing, but not the reverse; a function can increase while $f'$ is zero at a few points, like $x^3$ at 0.

## A general strategy for sketching

Jerison's scheme (transcript, pp. 5–6):

1. **Plot** the discontinuities (especially the infinite ones), the ends ($x \to \pm\infty$, or the endpoints of the domain), and any easy points. All precalculus.
2. **Solve** $f'(x) = 0$; plot the critical points and values.
3. **Decide** the sign of $f'$ on each interval between critical points and discontinuities. If steps 1 and 2 worked, this is only a **double check**, and a valuable one: an error here shows up as a graph that does not fit.
4. **Decide** the sign of $f''$: concavity and inflection points. Usually skip this: second derivatives are often awful to compute, so do it only when asked (pp. 6–7).
5. **Combine** everything.

## Example: x / ln x

Sketch $f(x) = x/\ln x$ for $x > 0$ (transcript, pp. 6–11).

**Step 1.** The singularity is where $\ln x = 0$, at $x = 1$: $f(1^+) = +\infty$ and $f(1^-) = -\infty$. The ends: $f(0^+) = 0^+/\ln(0^+) = 0^+/(-\infty) = 0$; and at $x = 10^{10}$, $f = 10^{10}/(10\ln 10) \approx 10^{10}/23$, huge, so $f \to \infty$ as $x \to \infty$ (p. 7). [The transcript says the denominator is "maybe 230"; $10\ln 10 \approx 23$. The conclusion is the same.]

**Step 2.** By the quotient rule (p. 8):

<Formula label="Derivative of x / ln x" tex="f'(x) = \frac{\ln x - 1}{(\ln x)^2} = \frac{1}{\ln x} - \frac{1}{(\ln x)^2}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 8–9" />

It vanishes at $x = e$, with critical value $f(e) = e/\ln e = e$. With only one critical point, the graph is fixed: from 0 it goes down to $-\infty$ at $x = 1$; from $+\infty$ it comes down to $(e, e)$ and turns back up (pp. 8–9). The second form also shows that $f' \to 0$ as $x \to 0^+$: the graph starts out **horizontally** (p. 10).

**Step 3** (the check): $f' < 0$ on $0 < x < 1$ and $1 < x < e$, $f' > 0$ for $x > e$: down, down, up (p. 9).

**Step 4.** Differentiating the second form (p. 10):

<Formula label="Second derivative of x / ln x" tex="f''(x) = \frac{2 - \ln x}{x\,(\ln x)^3}" cite="reading2" at="p. 10" />

Concave down on $0 < x < 1$, concave up on $1 < x < e^2$, concave down again for $x > e^2$. So there is an **inflection point** at $(e^2, e^2/2)$, beyond the minimum: a wiggle that the first sketch missed (pp. 10–11).

## Maxima and minima: where to look

Given a sketch, finding the largest and smallest values is easy. But a sketch is a lot of work, and the goal is to find the maximum and minimum with **shortcuts**, without the second derivative or any decoration (transcript, p. 11).

The key idea: look at the **critical points**, but not only there. The sentence does not end there (p. 11; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Where extremes can be" tex="\text{the largest and smallest values of } f \text{ occur at critical points } \bigl(f'(x) = 0\bigr), \text{ endpoints, or points of discontinuity}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

In the sketch of $x/\ln x$ there are five candidate places for extreme behaviour (the ends at 0 and at infinity, the two sides of the asymptote, and the critical point), and only one of them is a critical point (transcript, p. 11).

The notes add a warning on language (notes, p. 1). For $y = \ln x/x$ from the previous lecture, the **maximum value** is $1/e$, and it is **achieved at** $x = e$. So "what is the maximum?" is answered by $1/e$, not by $e$. Finding $x = e$ is the calculus step, but a full answer gives the value too, and the two together locate the top of the graph at $(e, 1/e)$.

## Example: the open can with least surface area

The notes then work two problems; the first is set out in numbered steps, condensed here (notes, pp. 2–3). Find the open-topped can (a cylinder with a bottom but no top) of fixed volume $V$ with the least surface area.

1. Draw the picture; name the variables: radius $r$, height $h$, volume $V$, area $S$.
2. Write the constraint and the quantity to minimise:

<Formula label="Constraint and surface area" tex="V = \pi r^2 h = \text{constant}, \qquad S = \pi r^2 + 2\pi r h" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

3. Use the constraint to write everything in terms of $r$: $h = V/(\pi r^2)$, so $S = \pi r^2 + 2V/r$ for $0 < r < \infty$.
4. Critical points and endpoints:

<Formula label="Critical point of the surface area" tex="\frac{dS}{dr} = 2\pi r - \frac{2V}{r^2} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; r^3 = \frac{V}{\pi}, \quad r = \left(\frac{V}{\pi}\right)^{1/3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

At both ends $S \to \infty$ (the $2V/r$ term as $r \to 0$, the $\pi r^2$ term as $r \to \infty$), so the minimum is at the critical point. There $h = V/(\pi r^2) = (V/\pi)^{1/3}$ and $S = 3\pi^{1/3}V^{2/3}$. The neatest form of the answer is the **shape**: $h/r = 1$, the height equals the radius (notes, p. 3).

## Example: a wire cut into two squares

Cut a wire of length 1 at $x$ and bend each piece into a square: sides $x/4$ and $(1 - x)/4$ (notes, pp. 4–5). The total area is

<Formula label="Area of the two squares" tex="A = \left(\frac{x}{4}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{1 - x}{4}\right)^2, \qquad A' = \frac{x}{8} - \frac{1 - x}{8} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \frac12" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

At $x = \tfrac12$, $A = 2 \times (1/8)^2 = 1/32$. But the endpoints give $A(0) = A(1) = 1/16$, which is **larger**. So the critical point is the **minimum**, and the maximum is at the endpoints: use all the wire for one square. The moral: if you look only at critical points, you may find the worst answer rather than the best (notes, p. 5).

<WorkedExample title="A closed can">
<Problem>
A can **with** a lid must hold 1.0 litre (1000 cm³). What radius and height use the least metal, and how much?
</Problem>
<Step title="Area in terms of r">
Now there are two discs: $S = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h$, and $h = V/(\pi r^2)$ gives $S = 2\pi r^2 + 2V/r$.
</Step>
<Step title="Critical point">
$dS/dr = 4\pi r - 2V/r^2 = 0$ gives $r^3 = V/(2\pi)$, so $r = (1000/2\pi)^{1/3} \approx 5.42$ cm. Again $S \to \infty$ at both ends, so this is the minimum.
</Step>
<Step title="Shape and area">
$h = V/(\pi r^2) = 2r \approx 10.8$ cm: the height equals the **diameter**. $S = 6\pi r^2 \approx 554$ cm².
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="A square and a circle">
<Problem>
A wire of length 1 is cut at $x$; the first piece makes a square, the second a circle. Where should you cut to make the total area as small as possible, and as large as possible?
</Problem>
<Step title="Area">
The square has side $x/4$; the circle has circumference $1 - x$, so radius $(1 - x)/(2\pi)$ and area $(1 - x)^2/(4\pi)$. Then $A = x^2/16 + (1 - x)^2/(4\pi)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Critical point">
$A' = x/8 - (1 - x)/(2\pi) = 0$ gives $x = 4/(\pi + 4) \approx 0.560$, with $A = 1/\bigl(4(\pi + 4)\bigr) \approx 0.0350$: the minimum.
</Step>
<Step title="Endpoints">
$A(1) = 1/16 = 0.0625$ (all square) and $A(0) = 1/(4\pi) \approx 0.0796$ (all circle). The maximum is at an endpoint: put all the wire into the circle.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The largest size of a closed universe">
A closed universe expands from the Big Bang, reaches a largest size and then recollapses. Its maximum size is a critical point of the scale factor $a(t)$: the moment when the expansion rate $\dot a$ is zero. In 8.286 the solution is written with a parameter $\theta$ (the development angle), and the maximum comes at $\theta = \pi$, exactly halfway between the Big Bang and the Big Crunch.
</Callout>

## Summary

- When there are no critical points, go back to precalculus: plot discontinuities (from each side), the ends, and easy points. A function with $f' \ne 0$ cannot turn back.
- Sketching strategy: (1) discontinuities, ends, easy points; (2) critical points and values; (3) sign of $f'$ as a check; (4) sign of $f''$ only if asked; (5) combine.
- Do not say a function is increasing across a discontinuity.
- Maxima and minima occur at critical points, endpoints or discontinuities. Give both where the extreme is achieved and its value.
- Open can of fixed volume with least area: $h = r$. Two squares from one wire: the critical point is the minimum; the maximum is at an endpoint.
