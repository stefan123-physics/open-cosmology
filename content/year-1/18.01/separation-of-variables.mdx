## The simplest kind

The simplest differential equation is $dy/dx = f(x)$. Its solution is the antiderivative, $y = \int f(x)\,dx$, and for this lecture that counts as solved (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1). Written with differentials it is $dy = f(x)\,dx$, and integrating both sides gives the same thing (p. 5).

## A first real example

Now the rate of change depends on $y$ as well as $x$ (transcript, pp. 1–3; notes, pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Example 2" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} + xy = 0, \quad\text{that is}\quad \frac{dy}{dx} = -xy" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

(The operator $\frac{d}{dx} + x$ is called the **annihilation operator** in quantum mechanics, where this equation describes the ground state of the harmonic oscillator, pp. 1–2.) The trick is to **separate the variables**: divide by $y$ and multiply by $dx$, so all the $y$'s are on the left and all the $x$'s on the right. Then take antiderivatives of both sides (p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Separate and integrate" tex="\frac{dy}{y} = -x\,dx \;\Rightarrow\; \ln|y| = -\frac{x^2}{2} + c \;\Rightarrow\; y = a\,e^{-x^2/2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Exponentiating gives $|y| = e^c e^{-x^2/2}$, so $y = \pm e^c e^{-x^2/2}$; together with $y = 0$ (below), that is every constant $a$, positive, negative or zero (transcript, pp. 2–4). Check by differentiating: $\frac{d}{dx}\big(ae^{-x^2/2}\big) = a(-x)e^{-x^2/2} = -xy$ (p. 3). This bell-shaped function is also the **normal distribution** of probability (p. 3).

## The general method

The method works whenever the equation has the form (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 2)

<Formula label="Separable equation" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = f(x)\,g(y) \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dy}{g(y)} = f(x)\,dx \;\Rightarrow\; H(y) = F(x) + c" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

where $H(y) = \int dy/g(y)$ and $F(x) = \int f(x)\,dx$, functions of different variables. The separation step is the only clever one, and it works because Leibniz's notation lets you treat differentials like ordinary numbers (p. 3). $H(y) = F(x) + c$ is an **implicit** equation for $y$. To make it **explicit**, invert: $y = H^{-1}(F(x) + c)$. The inverse is often messy, so the implicit form is sometimes left as it is (p. 3). In Example 2, $f(x) = -x$, $g(y) = y$, $H(y) = \ln|y|$ and $F(x) = -x^2/2$ (transcript, p. 3; the notes, p. 2, print $f(x) = x$, dropping the minus sign).

## Remarks on constants and lost solutions

The lecture adds three remarks, and then redoes $dy/dx = f(x)$ by separation, as in the first section (transcript, pp. 4–6):

- **Lost solutions.** Dividing by $y$ assumed $y \ne 0$, so the method misses the boring but genuine solution $y = 0$. Whenever you divide by something, check what happens if it is zero.
- **One constant is enough.** Writing $\ln|y| + c_1 = -x^2/2 + c_2$ gives nothing new: $c_2 - c_1$ is just one constant.
- **The constant changes shape.** It is additive right after integrating, but after a nonlinear step such as exponentiating it becomes a multiplying constant, and in general it can end up anywhere in the formula.

The family $y = ae^{-x^2/2}$ is the **general solution**. Extra information about the values, not the rate of change, picks out one member: if $y(0) = 3$, then $a e^0 = 3$, so $y = 3e^{-x^2/2}$ (pp. 5–6). Differential equations in applications usually express a balance, for example between the acceleration and the air resistance of a falling object (p. 6).

<WorkedExample title="Half-lives">
<Problem>
The amount $N$ of a radioactive isotope obeys $dN/dt = -\lambda N$, and it halves every 8.0 days. Find $N(t)$, $\lambda$, and the fraction left after 24 days.
</Problem>
<Step title="Separate">
$dN/N = -\lambda\,dt$, so $\ln N = -\lambda t + c$ and $N = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$, where $N_0 = N(0)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Rate">
$e^{-\lambda \cdot 8.0} = \tfrac12$ gives $\lambda = \ln 2/8.0 \approx 0.0866$ per day.
</Step>
<Step title="After 24 days">
24 days is three half-lives: $N/N_0 = e^{-3\ln 2} = (\tfrac12)^3 = \tfrac18$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A geometric example

Find curves whose tangent line, at every point, is **twice as steep** as the ray from the origin to that point (transcript, pp. 7–8; notes, p. 3). The ray to $(x, y)$ has slope $y/x$, so

<Formula label="Example 3" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2y}{x} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dy}{y} = \frac{2\,dx}{x} \;\Rightarrow\; \ln|y| = 2\ln|x| + c \;\Rightarrow\; y = a x^2" cite="reading2" at="pp. 7–8" />

The solutions are parabolas, opening up or down, plus $y = 0$. Check: $dy/dx = 2ax = 2ax^2/x = 2y/x$ for every $a$ (p. 8). (The notes' list of examples includes "$y = -2y^2$" where they mean $y = -2x^2$, notes, p. 3.)

A subtlety: the equation is **undefined at $x = 0$** (we divided by $x$). The equation gives no slope there, so the value at $x = 0$ does not fix the solution: a curve can come in along one half-parabola and leave along a different one. Think of the solutions as half-parabolas leaving the origin (pp. 8–9).

## Curves at right angles

Now find the curves that cross all those parabolas at **right angles** (transcript, pp. 9–10; notes, p. 4). Their slope is the negative reciprocal of the parabola's slope $2y/x$:

<Formula label="Example 4" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{2y} \;\Rightarrow\; 2y\,dy = -x\,dx \;\Rightarrow\; y^2 = -\frac{x^2}{2} + c \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{x^2}{2} + y^2 = a^2" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

This time **not every constant works**: $c$ must be positive, so write it as $a^2$. The curves are ellipses, with horizontal semi-axis $\sqrt2\,a$ and vertical semi-axis $a$: a little wider than they are high (p. 9). (The notes say the ratio of the axes is 2; it is $\sqrt2$, notes, p. 4.)

Solved explicitly, $y = \pm\sqrt{a^2 - x^2/2}$: an ellipse is not a function, so each solution is a top half or a bottom half. Each one **stops** at $y = 0$, where $x = \pm\sqrt2\,a$ and the slope $-x/(2y)$ is infinite: the equation itself predicts a vertical tangent there (p. 10). Before, we had to add solutions back; here some constants give none at all.

<WorkedExample title="A solution that runs out">
<Problem>
Solve $dy/dx = y^2$ with $y(0) = 1$. For which $x$ does the solution make sense?
</Problem>
<Step title="Separate">
$dy/y^2 = dx$, so $-1/y = x + c$. At $x = 0$, $y = 1$ gives $c = -1$, so $y = \dfrac{1}{1 - x}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check and interpret">
$dy/dx = \dfrac{1}{(1 - x)^2} = y^2$. The solution grows faster and faster and becomes infinite at $x = 1$, although the equation itself looks harmless everywhere. So the solution makes sense only for $x < 1$, the interval around the starting point where it stays finite. Separation finds such solutions, but you have to read off where they live.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Exponential expansion">
The Hubble rate is $H = \dot a/a$. In a universe dominated by vacuum energy, $H$ stays constant, so $da/dt = Ha$: the same kind of equation as radioactive decay, with the opposite sign. Separate: $da/a = H\,dt$, so $\ln a = Ht + c$ and $a(t) = a_0 e^{Ht}$. Distances double every $\ln 2/H$. This is the expansion of inflation in the early universe; and if dark energy is a cosmological constant, it is also where our universe is heading as dark energy takes over (constant $H$ then holds exactly for a flat universe).
</Callout>

## Summary

- $dy/dx = f(x)$ is solved by an antiderivative. For $dy/dx = f(x)\,g(y)$, separate: $dy/g(y) = f(x)\,dx$, integrate to $H(y) = F(x) + c$, and invert if you can.
- Example: $dy/dx = -xy$ gives $y = ae^{-x^2/2}$ for every constant $a$; $y(0)$ fixes $a$.
- One constant is enough, and after exponentiating it multiplies instead of adding.
- Dividing can lose solutions ($y = 0$); points where the equation is undefined ($x = 0$, $y = 0$) can make solutions branch or stop; and sometimes not every constant gives a solution.
- Geometry: curves whose tangent is twice as steep as the ray from the origin are the parabolas $y = ax^2$; the curves at right angles to them are the ellipses $x^2/2 + y^2 = a^2$.
