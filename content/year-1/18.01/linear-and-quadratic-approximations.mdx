This lecture opens Unit 2 of 18.01, **applications of differentiation**. The first application is approximation: replacing a hard function by a simple one that is nearly equal to it close to a chosen point.

## The tangent line approximation

The whole idea fits in one formula (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Linear approximation" tex="f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)\,(x - x_0) \qquad (x \text{ near } x_0)" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The right-hand side is the equation of the tangent line at the **base point** $x_0$. So the formula says: near the point of tangency, the curve and its tangent line are close. Far away, the curve can go anywhere it likes, and the approximation means nothing.

**Example.** Take $f(x) = \ln x$ with base point $x_0 = 1$, the one place where the logarithm is known exactly. Then $f(1) = 0$ and $f'(1) = 1/1 = 1$, so (p. 1; notes, p. 1)

<Formula label="Logarithm near 1" tex="\ln x \approx 0 + 1\cdot(x - 1) = x - 1 \qquad (x \text{ near } 1)" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

The line $y = x - 1$ touches the graph of $\ln x$ at $(1, 0)$ and stays close to it only for $x$ near 1.

## The same idea from the definition of the derivative

The derivative is the limit of $\Delta f/\Delta x$ as $\Delta x \to 0$. Unit 1 sometimes read this backwards, using known derivatives to find limits. Now read it as an approximation: when $\Delta x$ is small, the average rate of change is close to the instantaneous one (transcript, p. 2):

<Formula label="Average rate close to the derivative" tex="\frac{\Delta f}{\Delta x} \approx f'(x_0), \qquad \Delta f \approx f'(x_0)\,\Delta x" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

Writing $\Delta f = f(x) - f(x_0)$ and $\Delta x = x - x_0$ and moving $f(x_0)$ across gives the boxed formula back. The two statements are the same algebra (p. 2).

## Five standard approximations near zero

To keep things simple, the standard list always uses the base point $x_0 = 0$, where the formula reads $f(x) \approx f(0) + f'(0)\,x$ (transcript, p. 3). For each function, find $f(0)$ and $f'(0)$ (pp. 3–5):

| $f(x)$ | $f'(x)$ | $f(0)$ | $f'(0)$ | approximation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\sin x$ | $\cos x$ | 0 | 1 | $\sin x \approx x$ |
| $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ | 1 | 0 | $\cos x \approx 1$ |
| $e^x$ | $e^x$ | 1 | 1 | $e^x \approx 1 + x$ |
| $\ln(1 + x)$ | $1/(1 + x)$ | 0 | 1 | $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$ |
| $(1 + x)^r$ | $r(1 + x)^{r-1}$ | 1 | $r$ | $(1 + x)^r \approx 1 + rx$ |

<Formula label="Basic linear approximations (x near 0)" tex="\sin x \approx x, \quad \cos x \approx 1, \quad e^x \approx 1 + x, \quad \ln(1 + x) \approx x, \quad (1 + x)^r \approx 1 + rx" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Geometrically: the tangent to $\sin x$ at 0 is $y = x$; the tangent to $\cos x$ is the horizontal line $y = 1$, since the cosine has its top there; the tangent to $e^x$ is $y = 1 + x$ (p. 4).

Why $\ln(1 + x)$ and $(1 + x)^r$ rather than $\ln x$ and $x^r$? At $x = 0$ the logarithm goes down to minus infinity and $x^r$ can have an infinite slope, so 0 is a bad base point for them. Shifting by 1 moves the good base point to 0: with $u = 1 + x$, the earlier result $\ln u \approx u - 1$ becomes $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$. It is the same computation in a new variable (p. 5; notes, p. 1).

## Hard on the left, easy on the right

What is the approximation for? Take $\ln 1.1$. With $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$ and $x = 1/10$, at a glance $\ln 1.1 \approx 0.1$ (p. 5).

Jerison stresses which side is which. The left side, $\ln 1.1$, needs a calculator; the right side, one tenth, is easy. Down the whole table, the hard functions are on the left and simple polynomials on the right. That is the point of a linear approximation: if it is valid, it replaces something hard by something you can work with (pp. 5–6). [The true value is $\ln 1.1 = 0.0953\ldots$]

## Combining approximations by algebra

The five formulas can be combined without any further calculus. In the lecture's Example 3, find the linear approximation near 0 of $e^{-3x}/\sqrt{1 + x}$ (transcript, pp. 6–7). Write it as a product, $e^{-3x}(1 + x)^{-1/2}$, and use $e^u \approx 1 + u$ with $u = -3x$ and $(1 + x)^r \approx 1 + rx$ with $r = -\tfrac12$:

<Formula label="Combining two approximations" tex="e^{-3x}(1 + x)^{-1/2} \approx (1 - 3x)\left(1 - \tfrac12 x\right) = 1 - \tfrac72 x + \tfrac32 x^2 \approx 1 - \tfrac72 x" cite="reading2" at="p. 7" />

The last step **drops the $x^2$ term**. That is not carelessness: each factor was already approximated by throwing away its own $x^2$ terms, so the $\tfrac32 x^2$ is only one of many quadratic terms, and keeping it alone would be wrong. Only the linear part is valid (p. 7). [The notes work the same example with $e^{-2x}$ and get $1 - \tfrac52 x$ (notes, pp. 2–3).]

A student asked whether differentiating directly gives the same answer. It must: the approximation is $f(0) + f'(0)\,x$, so this shortcut shows at once that $f(0) = 1$ and $f'(0) = -\tfrac72$, without a long product-rule computation (p. 9).

## A real use: clocks on GPS satellites

Special relativity says that a clock moving at speed $v$ runs slow. If $T$ is the time on the satellite's clock and $T'$ the time on a watch on the ground (transcript, p. 7; notes, pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Time dilation" tex="T' = \frac{T}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}" cite="reading2" at="p. 7" />

With $u = v^2/c^2$ this has the form $(1 - u)^{-1/2}$, so $(1 + x)^r \approx 1 + rx$ with $x = -u$ and $r = -\tfrac12$ gives (p. 8):

<Formula label="Time dilation, linear approximation" tex="T' \approx T\left(1 + \frac12\,\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

[The transcript says "$(1 - u)^{1/2}$" at this point; the exponent is $-\tfrac12$, as the next step uses.] GPS satellites move at about 4 km/s and light at $3 \times 10^5$ km/s, so $v^2/c^2$ is about $10^{-10}$ (p. 8). Jerison reports that the engineers used exactly this approximation to offset the satellites' transmitter frequency, and that the next term, of size $u^2 \sim 10^{-20}$, is far too small to matter (p. 8). [The full GPS clock correction also includes a larger effect of gravity, from general relativity; the lecture discusses only the speed part.]

<WorkedExample title="Three quick estimates">
<Problem>
Estimate (a) $\sqrt{1.02}$, (b) the linear approximation of $\sqrt{1 + 4x}\;e^{-x}$ near 0, and (c) how much a clock moving at 8.0 km/s falls behind in one day, using the linear approximation.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A square root">
$\sqrt{1.02} = (1 + 0.02)^{1/2} \approx 1 + \tfrac12 \times 0.02 = 1.01$. The true value is $1.00995\ldots$
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A product">
$(1 + 4x)^{1/2} \approx 1 + 2x$ and $e^{-x} \approx 1 - x$, so the product is $\approx 1 + 2x - x = 1 + x$, dropping the $-2x^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A fast clock">
$v^2/c^2 = (8.0/(3.0 \times 10^5))^2 \approx 7.1 \times 10^{-10}$, so $T' - T \approx \tfrac12 \times 7.1 \times 10^{-10} \times 86{,}400\ \text{s} \approx 3.1 \times 10^{-5}$ s: about 31 microseconds a day.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Quadratic approximation

When more accuracy is needed, add one more term, built from the second derivative, with a factor $\tfrac12$ (transcript, p. 9; notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Quadratic approximation" tex="f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0) + \frac{f''(x_0)}{2}\,(x - x_0)^2 \qquad (x \text{ near } x_0)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

The first two terms are the linear part; the last is the quadratic part. For $\ln 1.1$ the quadratic version gives $\tfrac1{10} - \tfrac12\left(\tfrac1{10}\right)^2 = 0.1 - 0.005 = 0.095$, a little less than 0.1 and closer to the true $0.0953$ (transcript, p. 10).

**Where the ½ comes from.** The quadratic approximation should be exact for a quadratic. Take $f(x) = a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2$: then $f(0) = a_0$, $f'(0) = a_1$ and $f''(x) = 2a_2$, so $a_2 = f''(0)/2$ (notes, p. 4). The lecture leaves this for the next time (transcript, p. 10).

## The quadratic list and the best-fitting parabola

Each function needs one more number, $f''(0)$. The lecture works out the first three and then runs out of time, saving the rest for next time (transcript, pp. 10–11); the last two entries below are from the notes (notes, p. 5):

- $\sin x$: $f'' = -\sin x$, so $f''(0) = 0$ and there is **no** $x^2$ term. The linear approximation $\sin x \approx x$ is already quadratic, which is why it is so good.
- $\cos x$: $f'' = -\cos x$, $f''(0) = -1$, so $\cos x \approx 1 - \tfrac12 x^2$.
- $e^x$: $f''(0) = 1$, so $e^x \approx 1 + x + \tfrac12 x^2$.
- $\ln(1 + x)$: $f'' = -1/(1 + x)^2$, $f''(0) = -1$.
- $(1 + x)^r$: $f'' = r(r - 1)(1 + x)^{r-2}$, $f''(0) = r(r - 1)$.

<Formula label="Basic quadratic approximations (x near 0)" tex="\cos x \approx 1 - \tfrac12 x^2, \quad e^x \approx 1 + x + \tfrac12 x^2, \quad \ln(1 + x) \approx x - \tfrac12 x^2, \quad (1 + x)^r \approx 1 + rx + \tfrac{r(r-1)}{2}\,x^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

**The geometric meaning.** For $\cos x$ the tangent line $y = 1$ does not even say whether the curve is above or below 1 nearby. The parabola $y = 1 - \tfrac12 x^2$ does: it bends down with the curve and is the **best-fitting parabola** at that point (transcript, pp. 10–11).

The notes apply the quadratic list in two more places (notes, pp. 5–6):

- **The GPS clock again.** The quadratic term in $(1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ is $\tfrac38\,(v^2/c^2)^2$, of order $10^{-20}$: smaller than any clock can measure, so the linear approximation is enough.
- **Example 5:** the quadratic approximation of $e^{-2x}/\sqrt{1 + x}$. Multiply the two quadratic approximations and keep terms up to $x^2$:

<Formula label="A quadratic product" tex="\frac{e^{-2x}}{\sqrt{1 + x}} \approx \left(1 - 2x + 2x^2\right)\left(1 - \tfrac12 x + \tfrac38 x^2\right) \approx 1 - \tfrac52 x + \tfrac{27}{8}\,x^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

<WorkedExample title="Quadratic estimates">
<Problem>
Estimate (a) $e^{0.1}$ and (b) $\sqrt{1.02}$ with the quadratic approximations, and compare with the linear ones.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The exponential">
$e^{0.1} \approx 1 + 0.1 + \tfrac12(0.1)^2 = 1.105$, against 1.1 from the linear one. The true value is $1.10517\ldots$
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The square root">
With $r = \tfrac12$, $\tfrac{r(r-1)}{2} = -\tfrac18$, so $\sqrt{1.02} \approx 1 + 0.01 - \tfrac18(0.02)^2 = 1.00995$. The error drops from about $5 \times 10^{-5}$ to under $10^{-6}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Small redshifts">
For light from a source moving away at speed $v$, special relativity gives the Doppler shift $1 + z = \sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}$ with $\beta = v/c$. Write it as $(1 + \beta)^{1/2}(1 - \beta)^{-1/2}$ and use the quadratic list: $z \approx \beta + \tfrac12\beta^2$. For nearby galaxies, where $\beta$ is small, the linear part alone gives the familiar $v \approx cz$, which is how Hubble's law is first written. The 8.286 page on the Doppler effect uses exactly this approximation.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Near a base point, a function is close to its tangent line: $f(x) \approx f(x_0) + f'(x_0)(x - x_0)$. It is the same as saying $\Delta f/\Delta x \approx f'(x_0)$ for small $\Delta x$.
- Near 0: $\sin x \approx x$, $\cos x \approx 1$, $e^x \approx 1 + x$, $\ln(1 + x) \approx x$, $(1 + x)^r \approx 1 + rx$.
- Combine them by algebra, then **drop** every term beyond the order you are working to.
- The approximation replaces a hard function by an easy one; time dilation for GPS clocks is a real example.
- Quadratic approximation adds $\tfrac12 f''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2$, the best-fitting parabola; the ½ makes it exact for quadratics.
