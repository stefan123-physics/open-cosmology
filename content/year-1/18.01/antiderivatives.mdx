After the mean value theorem, the course turns to what fills its second half: **integration**. This lecture brings in the notation for it, Leibniz's differentials, and the first kind of integral, the antiderivative.

## Differentials

For $y = f(x)$, the **differential** of $y$ is defined as (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1)

<Formula label="Differential" tex="dy = f'(x)\,dx" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

Divide formally by $dx$ and you get $dy/dx = f'(x)$: the derivative as a ratio of two **differentials**, which is how Leibniz thought of it. Both $dy$ and $f'(x)\,dx$ are called differentials (notes, p. 1). The lecture stresses how much good notation matters: Leibniz's was adopted on the continent, Newton's in Britain, and the lecturer argues that Britain fell behind by one or two hundred years in calculus as a result (p. 1).

## Linear approximation in differentials

$dx$ takes the place of $\Delta x$, and $dy$ that of $\Delta y$ (transcript, pp. 1–2). The lecture's example: approximate $64.1^{1/3}$. With $y = x^{1/3}$ at $x = 64$, $y = 4$ and

<Formula label="The differential at 64" tex="dy = \tfrac13\,x^{-2/3}\,dx = \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac{1}{16}\,dx = \tfrac{1}{48}\,dx" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

With $dx = 1/10$, $64.1^{1/3} \approx y + dy = 4 + 1/480 \approx 4.002$ (pp. 2–3). Strictly, $dy$ is how far you rise along the **tangent line**, not along the curve, so this is only approximate; that small ambiguity in the notation is something to live with (p. 2). It is the same calculation as the linear approximation $f(x) \approx f(a) + f'(a)(x - a)$ with $a = 64$ (p. 3). The notes do $65^{1/3} \approx 4 + 1/48$ three ways and get the same answer each time (notes, pp. 1–2).

<WorkedExample title="√101 again">
<Problem>
Use differentials to approximate $\sqrt{101}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Differential">
$y = \sqrt x$, so $dy = \dfrac{dx}{2\sqrt x}$. At $x = 100$: $y = 10$ and $dy = dx/20$.
</Step>
<Step title="Approximate">
With $dx = 1$: $\sqrt{101} \approx 10 + 1/20 = 10.05$. The true value is $10.04988$, inside the bounds the [mean value theorem](/courses/18-01/mean-value-theorem) gave, and just below the estimate, because the curve bends below its tangent line.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Antiderivatives

The new idea is reading derivatives **backwards** (transcript, p. 3; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Antiderivative" tex="G(x) = \int g(x)\,dx \quad\text{means}\quad G'(x) = g(x)" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

$G$ is an **antiderivative** of $g$, also called the **indefinite integral**; $\int$ is the integral sign, and the differential $dx$ is part of the notation. "Indefinite" because a constant can always be added: the derivative of a constant is zero. For example, $-\cos x$ has derivative $\sin x$, so (p. 3)

<Formula label="Sine" tex="\int \sin x\,dx = -\cos x + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

## A table read backwards

Each differentiation rule gives an antiderivative (transcript, pp. 4–5; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Powers" tex="\int x^a\,dx = \frac{x^{a+1}}{a+1} + c \qquad (a \ne -1)" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

It comes from $d(x^{a+1}) = (a+1)\,x^a\,dx$, divided by $a + 1$, which fails at $a = -1$ (the transcript says "$a = 1$" at that point; it means $-1$). That exceptional case is the logarithm (p. 4):

<Formula label="The exceptional power" tex="\int \frac{dx}{x} = \ln|x| + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

The absolute value makes it right for negative $x$ too: for $x < 0$, $\ln|x| = \ln(-x)$, and by the chain rule $\frac{d}{dx}\ln(-x) = \frac{1}{-x}\cdot(-1) = \frac1x$ (p. 4; notes, p. 2, which label this the proof of "Property 2" but mean the logarithm formula). The rest of the lecture's list (p. 5; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Three more" tex="\int \sec^2 x\,dx = \tan x + c, \qquad \int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}} = \sin^{-1}x + c, \qquad \int\frac{dx}{1 + x^2} = \tan^{-1}x + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

For a while you will sometimes integrate when you meant to differentiate, and the other way round; practice fixes it, and helps you remember the derivative formulas too (p. 5).

## Only a constant

Adding a constant is not just one freedom: it is the **only** one (transcript, pp. 5–6; notes, p. 3).

<Formula label="Uniqueness up to a constant" tex="F' = G' \;\Rightarrow\; F(x) = G(x) + c" cite="reading2" at="pp. 5–6" />

Proof: $(F - G)' = F' - G' = 0$, and by the [previous lecture](/courses/18-01/mean-value-theorem) a function with zero derivative is constant. The lecture calls this the reason calculus makes sense: the rate of change determines the function, once you know where it starts (p. 6).

## The method of substitution

Integration is much harder than differentiation, and sometimes impossible in formulas; for now there is one method (transcript, p. 6). Take $\int x^3(x^4 + 2)^5\,dx$. Expanding the fifth power would be a mess. Instead let $u$ be the messiest part (pp. 6–7; notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Substitution" tex="u = x^4 + 2,\ \ du = 4x^3\,dx: \quad \int x^3(x^4 + 2)^5\,dx = \int u^5\,\frac{du}{4} = \frac{u^6}{24} + c = \frac{(x^4 + 2)^6}{24} + c" cite="reading2" at="pp. 6–7" />

The last step matters: the problem was posed in $x$, so the answer must be too. (The lecturer first says $\tfrac1{20}$, then corrects it to $\tfrac1{24}$.)

## Advanced guessing

With practice you can see two steps ahead: guess the answer, differentiate it, and fix the constant factor (transcript, pp. 7–8; notes, pp. 3–4). The lecture recommends this over substitution for simple cases:

- $\int \dfrac{x\,dx}{\sqrt{1 + x^2}}$: guess $(1 + x^2)^{1/2}$; its derivative is $\tfrac12(1 + x^2)^{-1/2}\cdot 2x$, exactly right, so the answer is $\sqrt{1 + x^2} + c$ (p. 7).
- $\int e^{6x}\,dx$: the guess $e^{6x}$ differentiates to $6e^{6x}$, so the answer is $\tfrac16 e^{6x} + c$ (p. 7).
- $\int x e^{-x^2}\,dx$: the guess $e^{-x^2}$ differentiates to $-2x e^{-x^2}$, off by $-2$, so the answer is $-\tfrac12 e^{-x^2} + c$. Write the check out: people who skip it make sign and factor mistakes (pp. 7–8).

<WorkedExample title="Three antiderivatives">
<Problem>
Find $\int x^2(x^3 + 1)^4\,dx$, $\int \cos 3x\,dx$ and $\int \dfrac{x}{x^2 + 4}\,dx$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Substitution">
$u = x^3 + 1$, $du = 3x^2\,dx$: $\int u^4\,\dfrac{du}{3} = \dfrac{u^5}{15} + c = \dfrac{(x^3 + 1)^5}{15} + c$.
</Step>
<Step title="Guess and check">
Guess $\sin 3x$: its derivative is $3\cos 3x$, so $\int\cos 3x\,dx = \tfrac13\sin 3x + c$.
</Step>
<Step title="A logarithm">
Guess $\ln(x^2 + 4)$: its derivative is $\dfrac{2x}{x^2 + 4}$, twice too big, so the answer is $\tfrac12\ln(x^2 + 4) + c$ (no absolute value needed, as $x^2 + 4 > 0$).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Two answers, one family

$\int \sin x\cos x\,dx$ has two natural guesses (transcript, pp. 8–9; notes, p. 4). $\tfrac12\sin^2 x$ differentiates to $\sin x\cos x$, and so does $-\tfrac12\cos^2 x$. Both are right. Their difference is

<Formula label="The two answers differ by a constant" tex="\tfrac12\sin^2 x - \left(-\tfrac12\cos^2 x\right) = \tfrac12\left(\sin^2 x + \cos^2 x\right) = \tfrac12" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

not zero but a constant, so the two families $\tfrac12\sin^2 x + c_1$ and $-\tfrac12\cos^2 x + c_2$ are the same set of functions, with $c_2 - c_1 = \tfrac12$ (p. 9; the lecture says $c_1 - c_2 = \tfrac12$, but setting the two equal gives $c_2 - c_1 = \tfrac12$, as in the notes' $\tfrac12\sin^2 x - \tfrac12 = -\tfrac12\cos^2 x$). Two antiderivatives that look different may still be the same; checking can take a trigonometric identity.

The lecture ends with a substitution: in $\int \dfrac{dx}{x\ln x}$, $u = \ln x$ has the simpler differential $du = dx/x$, so the integral is $\int du/u = \ln u + c = \ln(\ln x) + c$ (transcript, p. 9; notes, p. 4). This needs $x > 1$, so that $\ln x > 0$ (the notes say $x > 0$); otherwise write $\ln|\ln x|$, as the lecture remarks.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="How a flat universe grows">
For a flat universe filled with matter, the Friedmann equation gives $\dot a = C/\sqrt a$ for some constant $C$. In differentials, $\sqrt a\,da = C\,dt$. Take an antiderivative of each side: $\tfrac23 a^{3/2} = Ct + c$. Count time from the moment $a = 0$, so $c = 0$, and $a \propto t^{2/3}$: the matter-dominated expansion of 8.286, found with the power rule.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Differentials: $dy = f'(x)\,dx$. In a linear approximation, $\Delta y \approx dy$ when $dx = \Delta x$; for example $64.1^{1/3} \approx 4 + 1/480$.
- $\int g(x)\,dx = G(x)$ means $G' = g$: the antiderivative or indefinite integral, defined up to $+\,c$.
- Basic antiderivatives: $\int\sin x\,dx = -\cos x$, $\int x^a\,dx = x^{a+1}/(a+1)$ for $a \ne -1$, $\int dx/x = \ln|x|$, $\int\sec^2 x\,dx = \tan x$, $\int dx/\sqrt{1 - x^2} = \sin^{-1}x$, $\int dx/(1 + x^2) = \tan^{-1}x$.
- If $F' = G'$, then $F = G + c$: the constant is the only ambiguity (from the mean value theorem).
- Substitution: $u$ = the messiest part, rewrite in $u$, integrate, return to $x$. Or guess, differentiate, and fix the factor.
