This lecture is about integrating **rational functions**, ratios $P(x)/Q(x)$ of two polynomials (transcript, p. 1). The method, **partial fractions**, splits $P/Q$ into easier pieces; most of the work is algebra (p. 1).

## The disguise

Some rational functions are easy: $\int\left(\frac{1}{x - 1} + \frac{3}{x + 2}\right)dx = \ln|x - 1| + 3\ln|x + 2| + c$ (transcript, p. 1; notes, p. 1). But add the fractions over a common denominator, and the same function is disguised (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="The same function, disguised" tex="\frac{1}{x - 1} + \frac{3}{x + 2} = \frac{(x + 2) + 3(x - 1)}{(x - 1)(x + 2)} = \frac{4x - 1}{x^2 + x - 2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Faced with the right-hand side, it is not clear what to do. The job is to undo the disguise (transcript, p. 2).

## Factor, set up, cover up

The method has three steps (transcript, pp. 2–4):

1. **Factor** the denominator: $x^2 + x - 2 = (x - 1)(x + 2)$. This can be the hard part; here it is given (p. 2).
2. **Set up** the form of the answer, with unknown coefficients: $\frac{4x - 1}{(x - 1)(x + 2)} = \frac{A}{x - 1} + \frac{B}{x + 2}$ (p. 2).
3. **Solve** for the coefficients (p. 2).

The slow way clears all denominators, $4x - 1 = A(x + 2) + B(x - 1)$, and solves the simultaneous equations $A + B = 4$, $2A - B = -1$ (notes, p. 1). The quick way multiplies by just one factor. Multiplying by $x - 1$ gives $\frac{4x - 1}{x + 2} = A + \frac{B}{x + 2}(x - 1)$, and at $x = 1$ the $B$ term drops out (transcript, p. 2):

<Formula label="Cover-up for A" tex="A = \frac{4 \cdot 1 - 1}{1 + 2} = 1" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3" />

Setting $x = 1$ looks illegal, since the original equation has $x - 1$ in a denominator. But the multiplied equation holds for every $x \ne 1$, so we may take the limit $x \to 1$ (p. 3). In the same way, multiplying by $x + 2$ and setting $x = -2$ gives $B = \frac{-8 - 1}{-2 - 1} = 3$ (p. 3; notes, p. 2), and the disguise is undone.

In practice this is the **cover-up method**: cover up the factor $x - 1$ on the left, plug $x = 1$ into what is left, and read off $A$, with no writing in between (transcript, p. 4). It works when $Q$ has **distinct linear factors** and the degree of $P$ is less than the degree of $Q$ (pp. 4–5). With more factors, put in one term each: $\frac{x^2 + 3x + 8}{(x - 1)(x - 2)(x + 5)} = \frac{A}{x - 1} + \frac{B}{x - 2} + \frac{C}{x + 5}$ (p. 5).

## Repeated factors

When a factor repeats, the set-up needs one term for **each power** (transcript, p. 5; notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Set-up with a repeated factor" tex="\frac{x^2 + 2}{(x - 1)^2(x + 2)} = \frac{A}{x - 1} + \frac{B}{(x - 1)^2} + \frac{C}{x + 2}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 5–6" />

It is like writing a number in base 2: $\frac{7}{16} = \frac{0}{2} + \frac{1}{2^2} + \frac{1}{2^3} + \frac{1}{2^4}$ needs every power up to the highest (p. 5). Cover-up still gives $C$ (set $x = -2$: $\frac{4 + 2}{9} = \frac{2}{3}$) and $B$ (cover up the whole $(x - 1)^2$, set $x = 1$: $\frac{1 + 2}{1 + 2} = 1$), but not $A$: multiplying by just $x - 1$ leaves $B/(x - 1)$, which blows up at $x = 1$ (p. 6). For $A$, plug in an easy value not used yet, $x = 0$: $\frac{2}{2} = -A + 1 + \frac{1}{3}$, so $A = \frac{1}{3}$ (p. 7). The three unknowns really solve a 3-by-3 system in disguise, and the bigger the denominator, the more arithmetic (p. 7):

<Formula label="Repeated factor, solved" tex="\frac{x^2 + 2}{(x - 1)^2(x + 2)} = \frac{1/3}{x - 1} + \frac{1}{(x - 1)^2} + \frac{2/3}{x + 2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

(The notes write the numerator as $x^2 + 1$ in one line where they mean $x^2 + 2$, p. 2.)

## Quadratic factors

A quadratic such as $x^2 + 1$ does not factor without complex numbers; with them, cover-up comes back, which is how Heaviside first used it (transcript, p. 8). Over a quadratic factor the numerator is one degree lower, $Bx + C$ (p. 8). For $\frac{x^2}{(x - 1)(x^2 + 1)} = \frac{A}{x - 1} + \frac{Bx + C}{x^2 + 1}$, cover-up gives $A = \frac{1}{1 + 1} = \frac{1}{2}$ (p. 8). For $B$ and $C$, clear the denominators, $x^2 = A(x^2 + 1) + (Bx + C)(x - 1)$, and compare coefficients: the $x^2$ terms give $1 = \frac{1}{2} + B$, the constant terms $0 = \frac{1}{2} - C$, so $B = C = \frac{1}{2}$ (p. 9). The highest and lowest powers are the easiest to compare; plugging in numbers such as $x = 0$ works too (p. 9). Then each piece integrates (p. 11):

<Formula label="Integrating with a quadratic factor" tex="\int \frac{x^2\,dx}{(x - 1)(x^2 + 1)} = \frac{1}{2}\ln|x - 1| + \frac{1}{4}\ln(x^2 + 1) + \frac{1}{2}\tan^{-1}x + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 11" />

using $(\ln u)' = u'/u$ for the middle term (p. 11). The notes do the similar $\frac{1}{(x^2 + 1)(x - 1)}$ (notes, pp. 2–3). A cubic such as $x^3 + 1$ is not fully factored: it is $(x + 1)(x^2 - x + 1)$, and every polynomial with real coefficients eventually splits into linear and quadratic factors (transcript, p. 10). (In the lecture the sign in $x^2 - x + 1$ is left uncertain, p. 10.)

## When the numerator is too big

If the degree of $P$ is at least the degree of $Q$, like an improper fraction, the set-up gives a **wrong** answer (transcript, pp. 8, 11). Multiply the denominator back out and use **long division** (pp. 11–12):

<Formula label="Long division first" tex="\frac{x^3}{(x - 1)(x + 2)} = \frac{x^3}{x^2 + x - 2} = x - 1 + \frac{3x - 2}{x^2 + x - 2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 12" />

The quotient $x - 1$ is easy to integrate, and the remainder has a lower degree than the denominator, so cover-up now works (p. 12).

The notes add two cases that need no partial fractions at all: $\int\frac{dx}{(x - 1)^{10}} = -\frac{1}{9}(x - 1)^{-9} + c$, and $\int\frac{dx}{(x^2 + 1)^{10}}$, which $x = \tan u$ turns into $\int\cos^{18}u\,du$, a trigonometric integral (notes, p. 3).

<WorkedExample title="Three rational functions">
<Problem>
(a) Evaluate $\int_2^3\frac{x + 5}{(x - 1)(x + 3)}\,dx$. (b) Split $\frac{1}{x^2(x + 1)}$. (c) Split $\frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 1}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Distinct linear factors">
Cover-up: $A = \frac{1 + 5}{1 + 3} = \frac{3}{2}$ and $B = \frac{-3 + 5}{-3 - 1} = -\frac{1}{2}$. So the integral is $\Big[\frac{3}{2}\ln|x - 1| - \frac{1}{2}\ln|x + 3|\Big]_2^3 = \frac{3}{2}\ln 2 - \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{6}{5} \approx 1.040 - 0.091 = 0.949$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A repeated factor">
Set up $\frac{A}{x} + \frac{B}{x^2} + \frac{C}{x + 1}$. Cover up $x^2$ and set $x = 0$: $B = 1$. Cover up $x + 1$ and set $x = -1$: $C = 1$. At $x = 1$: $\frac{1}{2} = A + 1 + \frac{1}{2}$, so $A = -1$. Check at $x = 2$: $-\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{12} = \frac{1}{4 \cdot 3}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Divide first">
The degrees are equal, so divide: $\frac{x^2 + 1}{x^2 - 1} = 1 + \frac{2}{x^2 - 1}$. Then cover-up on $\frac{2}{(x - 1)(x + 1)}$ gives $\frac{1}{x - 1} - \frac{1}{x + 1}$, so the function is $1 + \frac{1}{x - 1} - \frac{1}{x + 1}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The age of the universe, again">
The age of a flat universe with matter and vacuum energy involves the inverse hyperbolic tangent. That function is a partial-fraction integral: for $|y| < 1$, $\frac{1}{1 - y^2} = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{1 - y} + \frac{1}{1 + y}\right)$, so $\int_0^y\frac{dy'}{1 - y'^2} = \frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1 + y}{1 - y} = \tanh^{-1}y$. With $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$ and $\Omega_{\text{vac},0} = 0.7$, $\tanh^{-1}\sqrt{0.7} \approx 1.21$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Partial fractions split a rational function $P/Q$ into simple pieces: logarithms from linear factors, negative powers from repeated factors, logarithms and inverse tangents from quadratic factors, plus a polynomial from any long division.
- Steps: factor $Q$; set up one term per linear factor, one per power of a repeated factor, $\frac{Bx + C}{\text{quadratic}}$ per quadratic factor; solve for the coefficients.
- Cover-up: cover the factor $x - a$, set $x = a$ in the rest. It finds the coefficients of distinct linear factors and of the highest power of a repeated one.
- If the degree of $P$ is not less than that of $Q$, do long division first.
