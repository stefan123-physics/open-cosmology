## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Trapping the logarithm between polynomials" points={10} mit="Part II, Problem 1">

The lesson on the mean value theorem proved inequalities for $e^x$. Here you do the same for the logarithm. The tool is the theorem in the form that compares a function at $x$ with the same function at a fixed point $a$:

<Formula label="Mean value theorem, third form" tex="f(x) = f(a) + f'(c)\,(x - a) \quad \text{for some } c,\ a < c < x" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

It holds when $f$ is continuous from $a$ to $x$, with both ends included, and has a derivative everywhere in between.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Suppose that $f(0) = 0$ and that $f'(x) \ge 0$ for every $x \ge 0$. Use the mean value theorem to show that $f(x) \ge 0$ for all $x \ge 0$.

<Hint>

Take $a = 0$ in the third form of the theorem. What is left of the right-hand side?

</Hint>

<Hint>

You do not know where $c$ is, only that it lies between $0$ and $x$. What do you know about $f'(c)$ all the same?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The theorem writes $f(x)$ as $f(0)$ plus a slope multiplied by a distance. Neither of the two factors can be negative.

<Step title="Apply the theorem between 0 and x">

For $x = 0$ there is nothing to prove, because $f(0) = 0$. Take any $x > 0$. The theorem with $a = 0$ says that there is a number $c$ between $0$ and $x$ with

<Formula label="The theorem between 0 and x" tex="f(x) = f(0) + f'(c)\,(x - 0) = f'(c)\,x" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Read off the sign">

The number $c$ is positive, so $f'(c) \ge 0$ by what was given. The factor $x$ is positive too. So the product $f'(c)\,x$ is not negative, and $f(x) \ge 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the argument">

The theorem does not say where $c$ is, and the proof never needs to know: the sign of $f'$ is given at every point where $c$ could be. The same argument works on a shorter stretch. If $f'(x) \ge 0$ is known only for $0 \le x < b$, then $f(x) \ge 0$ for $0 \le x < b$, because $c$ lies between $0$ and $x$. Part (e) uses this. (This remark is ours.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Deduce from part (a) that

<Formula label="First inequality for the logarithm" tex="\ln(1 + x) \le x \quad \text{for } x \ge 0" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

MIT's hint: use the function $f(x) = x - \ln(1 + x)$.

<Hint>

Part (a) needs two things from $f$: its value at $0$ and the sign of its derivative. Work out both.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put $f'(x)$ on one denominator. Then its sign for $x \ge 0$ can be read off.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** An inequality says that one side minus the other is not negative. Part (a) proves exactly that for a difference that starts at zero and never decreases.

<Step title="Check the two conditions of part (a)">

With $f(x) = x - \ln(1 + x)$:

<Formula label="Value at 0 and derivative" tex="f(0) = 0 - \ln 1 = 0, \qquad f'(x) = 1 - \frac{1}{1 + x} = \frac{x}{1 + x}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

For $x \ge 0$ the top of the fraction is not negative and the bottom is positive, so $f'(x) \ge 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Apply part (a)">

Part (a) gives $f(x) \ge 0$ for all $x \ge 0$, that is $x - \ln(1 + x) \ge 0$, which is the inequality.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with a number">

At $x = 1$: $\ln 2 = 0.693$, which is less than $1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Use the same method to show these two inequalities for $x \ge 0$:

<Formula label="Second and third inequalities" tex="\ln(1 + x) \ge x - \frac{x^2}{2}, \qquad \ln(1 + x) \le x - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

<Hint>

For each inequality, choose $f$ as the larger side minus the smaller side, so that the claim reads $f(x) \ge 0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put each derivative on the denominator $1 + x$. The tops simplify a great deal.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in part (b): the difference of the two sides is $0$ at $x = 0$, and its derivative turns out to be a power of $x$ divided by $1 + x$.

<Step title="The second inequality">

Take $f(x) = \ln(1 + x) - x + \dfrac{x^2}{2}$. Then $f(0) = 0$ and

<Formula label="Derivative for the second inequality" tex="f'(x) = \frac{1}{1 + x} - 1 + x = \frac{1 - (1 - x)(1 + x)}{1 + x} = \frac{x^2}{1 + x}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

This is not negative for $x \ge 0$, so part (a) gives $f(x) \ge 0$: the logarithm is at least $x - x^2/2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The third inequality">

Now the polynomial is the larger side. Take $f(x) = x - \dfrac{x^2}{2} + \dfrac{x^3}{3} - \ln(1 + x)$. Then $f(0) = 0$ and

<Formula label="Derivative for the third inequality" tex="f'(x) = 1 - x + x^2 - \frac{1}{1 + x} = \frac{(1 - x + x^2)(1 + x) - 1}{1 + x} = \frac{x^3}{1 + x}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

Here $(1 - x + x^2)(1 + x) = 1 + x^3$. Again $f'(x) \ge 0$ for $x \ge 0$, so $f(x) \ge 0$: the logarithm is at most $x - x^2/2 + x^3/3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with a number">

At $x = 0.5$ the three values are $0.375$, $\ln 1.5 = 0.4055$ and $0.4167$, in the right order.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Find the pattern in parts (b) and (c) and state a general conjecture. (A conjecture is a claim you expect to be true but have not proved.) Then answer the two boxes, which are our addition: the next polynomial in the pattern after $x - x^2/2 + x^3/3$, and the side of $\ln(1 + x)$ it lies on for $x \ge 0$.

<Hint>

Write the three polynomials under each other. What is the next term, and what is its sign?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The inequality sign turned around each time a term was added. Which polynomials lie above the logarithm: those ending in a plus term or those ending in a minus term?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d-poly" label="Next polynomial $=$" expr="x - x^2/2 + x^3/3 - x^4/4"
  vars={{ x: [0.2, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x - x^2/2 + x^3/3 + x^4/4", hint: "The signs alternate: a plus term is followed by a minus term." },
    { expr: "x - x^2/2 + x^3/3 - x^4/24", hint: "The numbers under the powers in parts (b) and (c) are 1, 2, 3: the whole numbers themselves. A factorial such as 24 belongs to the exponential function." },
    { expr: "x - x^2/2 + x^3/3", hint: "That is the polynomial of part (c). The question asks for the next one, with one more term." },
    { expr: "x - x^2/2 + x^3/3 - x^4/4 + x^5/5", hint: "That is two steps on. The question asks for the polynomial with one more term than in part (c)." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-d-side" label="For $x \ge 0$, $\ln(1 + x)$ is" choices={["greater than or equal to that polynomial", "less than or equal to that polynomial", "greater for small $x$ and less for large $x$"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each new term is the next power of $x$ divided by its own exponent, with alternating sign, and each new term turns the inequality around.

<Step title="The conjecture">

Call the polynomial with $n$ terms $P_n(x)$ (the name is ours):

<Formula label="The polynomials of the pattern" tex="P_n(x) = x - \frac{x^2}{2} + \frac{x^3}{3} - \cdots + (-1)^{n+1}\,\frac{x^n}{n}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

The conjecture: for $x \ge 0$,

<Formula label="Conjecture" tex="\ln(1 + x) \le P_n(x) \ \text{ for odd } n, \qquad \ln(1 + x) \ge P_n(x) \ \text{ for even } n" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

So the next polynomial is the one with four terms, $x - x^2/2 + x^3/3 - x^4/4$, and it lies below the logarithm.

</Step>

<Step title="Why the conjecture is true (our addition, not asked by MIT)">

The derivatives in parts (b) and (c) were $x/(1 + x)$, $x^2/(1 + x)$ and $x^3/(1 + x)$. That continues. The derivative of $P_n$ is a geometric sum with ratio $-x$:

<Formula label="Derivative of the polynomial" tex="P_n'(x) = 1 - x + x^2 - \cdots + (-x)^{n-1} = \frac{1 - (-x)^n}{1 + x}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Derivative of the difference" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\Big[P_n(x) - \ln(1 + x)\Big] = \frac{1 - (-x)^n}{1 + x} - \frac{1}{1 + x} = -\frac{(-x)^n}{1 + x}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

For odd $n$ this is $x^n/(1 + x) \ge 0$, so part (a) gives $P_n(x) \ge \ln(1 + x)$. For even $n$ it is $-x^n/(1 + x)$, so part (a) applied to $\ln(1 + x) - P_n(x)$ gives the opposite inequality.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with a number">

At $x = 0.5$ the polynomial with four terms is $0.4010$, below $\ln 1.5 = 0.4055$, which is below the $0.4167$ of the polynomial with three terms. The logarithm is trapped between two polynomials that follow each other.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

Show that the first inequality also holds to the left of zero:

<Formula label="The first inequality for negative x" tex="\ln(1 + x) \le x \quad \text{for } -1 < x \le 0" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(e), p. 2" />

MIT's hint: change the variable to $u = -x$.

<Hint>

Part (a) only speaks about numbers to the right of zero. For which values of $u$ must you prove something, and what does the inequality say in terms of $u$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Build a function of $u$ that is $0$ at $u = 0$ and whose being non-negative is the inequality. Then check the sign of its derivative for $0 \le u < 1$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $u = -x$ the stretch $-1 < x \le 0$ becomes $0 \le u < 1$, to the right of zero, where part (a) can be used.

<Step title="Rewrite the claim">

Put $x = -u$. The claim $\ln(1 + x) \le x$ becomes $\ln(1 - u) \le -u$ for $0 \le u < 1$, or

<Formula label="The claim in the new variable" tex="g(u) = -u - \ln(1 - u) \ge 0 \quad \text{for } 0 \le u < 1" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(e) (our own solution)" />

(The name $g$ is ours.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the two conditions">

<Formula label="Value at 0 and derivative in the new variable" tex="g(0) = 0 - \ln 1 = 0, \qquad g'(u) = -1 + \frac{1}{1 - u} = \frac{u}{1 - u}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(e) (our own solution)" />

The chain rule gives the plus sign: the derivative of $\ln(1 - u)$ is $-1/(1 - u)$. For $0 \le u < 1$ the top is not negative and the bottom is positive, so $g'(u) \ge 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Apply part (a)">

By part (a), used on the stretch $0 \le u < 1$ (see the remark at the end of its solution), $g(u) \ge 0$ there. That is the claim.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with a number">

At $x = -0.5$: $\ln 0.5 = -0.693$, which is less than $-0.5$. Together with part (b), $\ln(1 + x) \le x$ for every $x > -1$: the graph of the logarithm never rises above its tangent line at $x = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Two answers to one integral" points={2} mit="Part II, Problem 2(b)">

Two students work out the same antiderivative and find

<Formula label="Two answers" tex="\int \tan x\,\sec^2 x\,dx = \tfrac12 \tan^2 x \qquad \text{and} \qquad \int \tan x\,\sec^2 x\,dx = \tfrac12 \sec^2 x" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(b), p. 2" />

Show that both are correct, and explain how that can be. (The two students are our way of putting it. Here $\sec x = 1/\cos x$. MIT writes both answers without an added constant, and so do we.) Then type the exact value of $\tfrac12\sec^2 x - \tfrac12\tan^2 x$ in the box, which is our addition.

<Hint>

An antiderivative is checked by differentiating it. Do that for both answers.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The lesson on antiderivatives says how much two antiderivatives of one function can differ. Which identity links $\sec^2 x$ and $\tan^2 x$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-const" label="$\tfrac12\sec^2 x - \tfrac12\tan^2 x =$" value="0.5" tolerance="0.001" calc="p2-difference" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both functions have the derivative $\tan x\,\sec^2 x$. Two antiderivatives of one function differ by a constant, and here the constant is one half.

<Step title="Differentiate both answers">

The derivative of $\tan x$ is $\sec^2 x$, and the derivative of $\sec x$ is $\sec x\,\tan x$. With the chain rule:

<Formula label="Both derivatives" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\Big(\tfrac12 \tan^2 x\Big) = \tan x\,\sec^2 x, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\Big(\tfrac12 \sec^2 x\Big) = \sec x \cdot \sec x\,\tan x = \tan x\,\sec^2 x" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

So both are antiderivatives of $\tan x\,\sec^2 x$.

</Step>

<Step title="Explain">

The lecture proves that an antiderivative is fixed only up to an added constant:

<Formula label="Uniqueness up to a constant" tex="F'(x) = f(x) \ \text{ and } \ G'(x) = f(x) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad G(x) = F(x) + c" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

The two answers must therefore differ by a constant. They do, because $\sec^2 x = 1 + \tan^2 x$:

<Formula label="The difference of the two answers" tex="\tfrac12 \sec^2 x - \tfrac12 \tan^2 x = \tfrac12\left(1 + \tan^2 x\right) - \tfrac12 \tan^2 x = \tfrac12" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

Written in full, the answers are $\tfrac12\tan^2 x + c$ and $\tfrac12\sec^2 x + c$. They describe the same family of functions: the constant of one is the constant of the other shifted by $\tfrac12$. The lecture shows the same thing for $\sin x\cos x$, whose antiderivative can be written as $\tfrac12\sin^2 x + c$ or as $-\tfrac12\cos^2 x + c$ (Lecture 15 notes, p. 4).

</Step>

<Step title="Check: where the two answers come from">

Each answer is what one substitution gives. With $u = \tan x$, $du = \sec^2 x\,dx$ and the integral is $\int u\,du = \tfrac12 u^2$. With $u = \sec x$, $du = \sec x\tan x\,dx$ and the integral is again $\int u\,du$, now with a different $u$. At $x = 0$ the answers are $0$ and $\tfrac12$: one half apart, as they must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Air pressure on a mountain and on a tower" points={7} mit="Part II, Problem 4 (exercise 3F-5 of the course reader)">

MIT's Problem 4 is exercise 3F-5, parts (a) to (c), of the course reader. The reader has no solution for it. It models how air pressure $p$ falls with the altitude $h$ above sea level, measured in kilometres:

<Formula label="Air pressure and altitude" tex="\frac{dp}{dh} = -0.13\,p" cite="exercisesE" at="Exercise 3F-5" />

At sea level the pressure is $1$ kg/cm². (That is the reader's unit: the weight of one kilogram on every square centimetre, which is close to the usual pressure at sea level. The remark is ours.)

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Solve the differential equation. Then find the pressure at the top of Mount Everest, whose height the reader rounds to $10$ km. Give the pressure in kg/cm², within 1%.

<Hint>

The right-hand side is a function of $p$ alone. Which method of the lesson on differential equations fits?

</Hint>

<Hint>

After integrating you have a constant. The pressure at sea level, $h = 0$, fixes it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a-formula" label="$p(h) =$" expr="exp(-0.13*h)"
  vars={{ h: [0.5, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(0.13*h)", hint: "This pressure grows as you climb. Check the sign in the exponent." },
    { expr: "1 - 0.13*h", hint: "That is a straight line, the linear approximation of part (c). It falls at a fixed rate, but the equation says the rate of fall shrinks as the pressure shrinks." },
    { expr: "-0.13*exp(-0.13*h)", hint: "That is the rate of change dp/dh, not the pressure itself. At sea level the pressure must be 1." },
    { expr: "exp(-0.13*h) + 1", hint: "At sea level this gives 2, not 1. The constant of integration multiplies the exponential: it is not added to it." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-a-everest" label="$p(10) =$" value="0.2725" unit="kg/cm²" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-a-everest" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The rate of change of $p$ is proportional to $p$ itself. Separating the variables gives an exponential.

<Step title="Separate the variables and integrate">

Bring $p$ to the left and $h$ to the right, as the lecture does with $dy/dx = -xy$ (Lecture 16 notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Separation of variables for the pressure" tex="\frac{dp}{p} = -0.13\,dh \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln p = -0.13\,h + c \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = A\,e^{-0.13h}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

Here $A = e^c$. No absolute value is needed, because a pressure is positive.

</Step>

<Step title="Use the pressure at sea level">

At $h = 0$ the exponential is $1$, so $A = p(0) = 1$ kg/cm²:

<Formula label="Pressure at altitude h" tex="p(h) = e^{-0.13h} \ \text{kg/cm}^2 \qquad (h \text{ in km})" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The top of Mount Everest">

At $h = 10$ km the exponent is $-1.3$, and $p(10) = e^{-1.3} = 0.2725$ kg/cm², a little more than a quarter of the pressure at sea level.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate: $dp/dh = -0.13\,e^{-0.13h} = -0.13\,p$, and $p(0) = 1$. The number $0.13$ is in units of 1/km, so the exponent $0.13 \times 10$ is a pure number, as it must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

MIT's Green Building is a tower. Pretend, as the reader does, that it is $100$ metres tall and stands at sea level. First find the difference in pressure between its bottom and its top from your solution, with a calculator, in kg/cm² and correct to four significant figures. Then use the linear approximation of $e^x$ near $x = 0$ to estimate by what percentage the pressure drops from bottom to top. Type the estimate itself, not the exact drop.

<Hint>

The altitude in the equation is in kilometres. What is $h$ at the top of the tower?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the estimate, replace $e^x$ by the first two terms that the lesson on linear approximations gives for it near $x = 0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b-diff" label="$p(0) - p(0.1) =$" value="0.01292" unit="kg/cm²" tolerance="0.3%" calc="p4-b-diff" />

<Answer id="p4-b-percent" label="Estimated drop, from the linear approximation:" value="1.3" unit="%" tolerance="0.3%" calc="p4-b-percent" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The top of the tower is at $h = 0.1$ km. The exponent is then so small that $e^x$ is very nearly $1 + x$.

<Step title="The exact difference">

<Formula label="Pressure difference over the tower" tex="p(0) - p(0.1) = 1 - e^{-0.013} = 1 - 0.98708 = 0.01292 \ \text{kg/cm}^2" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The linear approximation">

Near $x = 0$:

<Formula label="Linear approximation of the exponential" tex="e^x \approx 1 + x \qquad (x \text{ near } 0)" cite="reading5" at="p. 2" />

With $x = -0.013$ this gives $p(0.1) \approx 1 - 0.013$. The pressure falls by about $0.013$ of its value at the bottom, that is by about 1.3%.

</Step>

<Step title="Check: compare the two">

The exact drop is 1.292% of the pressure at the bottom, so the estimate of 1.3% is off by less than one part in a hundred of itself. It is slightly too large: the graph of $e^x$ bends upwards and lies above its tangent line at $x = 0$, so the true pressure at the top is a little higher than $1 - 0.013$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Now forget the solution of part (a). Use the linear approximation $\Delta p \approx p'(0)\,\Delta h$, and get $p'(0)$ straight from the differential equation, to find the drop in pressure from the bottom to the top of the Green Building (in kg/cm², as a positive number). Type the linear estimate, not the exact value of part (b). Compare with part (b). Then find what the same approximation gives for the pressure at the top of Mount Everest, in kg/cm² (the box wants it within 1%), and say what you make of it.

<Hint>

The differential equation gives the slope $p'$ at any altitude where you know the pressure. You know the pressure at $h = 0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For Mount Everest the approximation reads $p(10) \approx p(0) + p'(0) \times 10$. Work it out before you judge it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c-drop" label="Drop over the tower $\approx$" value="0.013" unit="kg/cm²" tolerance="0.3%" calc="p4-c-drop" />

<Answer id="p4-c-everest" label="Linear approximation of $p(10)$:" value="-0.3" unit="kg/cm²" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-c-everest" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A differential equation hands you the slope at the starting point without being solved. Slope multiplied by a small step is the linear approximation.

<Step title="The slope at sea level">

Put $h = 0$ and $p(0) = 1$ in the equation:

<Formula label="Slope of the pressure at sea level" tex="p'(0) = -0.13\,p(0) = -0.13 \ \text{kg/cm}^2 \text{ per km}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The Green Building">

With $\Delta h = 0.1$ km:

<Formula label="Linear estimate over the tower" tex="\Delta p \approx p'(0)\,\Delta h = -0.13 \times 0.1 = -0.013 \ \text{kg/cm}^2" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Mount Everest">

With $\Delta h = 10$ km the same rule gives

<Formula label="Linear estimate at the top of Mount Everest" tex="p(10) \approx p(0) + p'(0) \times 10 = 1 - 1.3 = -0.3 \ \text{kg/cm}^2" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 4(c) (our own solution)" />

Over $10$ km the pressure, and with it the slope, falls to about a quarter. In the language of part (b): $e^x \approx 1 + x$ needs $x$ near $0$, and here $x = -1.3$.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="The area under the exponential function, by rectangles" points={6} mit="Part II, Problem 5">

Calculate

<Formula label="The integral of Problem 5" tex="\int_0^1 e^x\,dx" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 5, p. 2" />

from lower Riemann sums, without an antiderivative. A lower sum is a Riemann sum in which every rectangle is as high as the lowest point of the graph above its strip, so that the rectangles stay under the graph. Cut the interval from $0$ to $1$ into $n$ strips of equal width.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Write the lower sum with $n$ strips, and add it up to a formula without a long sum in it. MIT's hint: you will need the sum of a geometric series.

<Hint>

The function $e^x$ is increasing. At which end of each strip is it lowest?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the heights of the first three rectangles. Each is the one before it multiplied by the same number $r$. What is $r$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The geometric sum is $1 + r + r^2 + \cdots + r^{n-1} = \dfrac{r^n - 1}{r - 1}$. What is $r^n$ here?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="Lower sum $=$" expr="(exp(1) - 1)/(n*(exp(1/n) - 1))"
  vars={{ n: [2, 40] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(1/n)*(exp(1) - 1)/(n*(exp(1/n) - 1))", hint: "That is the upper sum: its heights are taken at the right-hand end of each strip. The exponential function is lowest at the left-hand end." },
    { expr: "(exp(1) - 1)/(exp(1/n) - 1)", hint: "That is the sum of the heights alone, which grows without limit as n grows. Each rectangle also has a width." },
    { expr: "(exp(1) - 1)/(n*(1 - exp(1/n)))", hint: "This is negative, but every rectangle has a positive area. Check the order of the subtractions in the geometric sum." },
    { expr: "(exp(1 + 1/n) - 1)/(n*(exp(1/n) - 1))", hint: "This adds up n + 1 rectangles, with left-hand ends from 0 up to 1 itself. There are only n strips: the last one starts at 1 - 1/n." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Because $e^x$ increases, the lowest point above each strip is at its left-hand end. The heights then form a geometric series.

<Step title="Set up the lower sum">

Each strip has width $\Delta x = 1/n$. The strips start at $x = 0, \tfrac1n, \tfrac2n, \ldots, \tfrac{n-1}{n}$, and the rectangle on the strip that starts at $i/n$ has height $e^{i/n}$. In the lecture's definition of a Riemann sum (Lecture 18 notes, p. 4), which allows any point of each strip, this is the choice of the left-hand end. Call the sum $S_n$ (the name is ours):

<Formula label="Lower Riemann sum" tex="S_n = \frac1n\left(1 + e^{1/n} + e^{2/n} + \cdots + e^{(n-1)/n}\right)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Sum the geometric series">

With $r = e^{1/n}$ the bracket is $1 + r + r^2 + \cdots + r^{n-1}$, and $r^n = e$:

<Formula label="Lower sum in closed form" tex="S_n = \frac1n \cdot \frac{r^n - 1}{r - 1} = \frac{e - 1}{n\left(e^{1/n} - 1\right)}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

For $n = 4$ the four rectangles have heights $1$, $1.2840$, $1.6487$ and $2.1170$, and a quarter of their sum is $1.5124$. The formula gives $(e - 1)/(4 \times 0.28403) = 1.5124$ as well.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

To take the limit of the lower sums you need the limit of $n\left(e^{1/n} - 1\right)$ as $n$ goes to infinity. Find it (the box wants the exact value). MIT's hint: use the standard linear approximation of the exponential function.

<Hint>

For large $n$ the number $1/n$ is near $0$. What is $e^x$ approximately for $x$ near $0$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$\lim_{n\to\infty} n\left(e^{1/n} - 1\right) =$" value="1" tolerance="0.001" calc="p5-b-limit" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For large $n$, $e^{1/n}$ is close to $1 + 1/n$.

<Step title="Use the linear approximation">

With $e^x \approx 1 + x$ for $x$ near $0$ (Lecture 9 notes, p. 2) and $x = 1/n$:

<Formula label="The limit needed for the Riemann sum" tex="n\left(e^{1/n} - 1\right) \approx n \cdot \frac1n = 1, \qquad \lim_{n\to\infty} n\left(e^{1/n} - 1\right) = 1" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Why the approximation becomes exact in the limit (our remark)">

Write $x = 1/n$. Then $n\left(e^{1/n} - 1\right) = \dfrac{e^x - e^0}{x}$, the difference quotient of the exponential function at $0$. As $n$ grows, $x$ goes to $0$ and the quotient goes to the derivative of $e^x$ at $0$, which is $1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

For $n = 10$, $100$ and $1000$ the expression is $1.0517$, $1.0050$ and $1.0005$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Take the limit of the lower sums and give the value of the integral, correct to three decimal places.

<Hint>

In your formula from part (a) only the bottom depends on $n$, and part (b) gives its limit.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-c" label="$\int_0^1 e^x\,dx =$" value="1.718" tolerance="0.001" calc="p5-c-integral" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The definite integral is the limit of the Riemann sums as the strips get thin.

<Step title="Take the limit">

<Formula label="The integral as a limit of lower sums" tex="\int_0^1 e^x\,dx = \lim_{n\to\infty} S_n = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{e - 1}{n\left(e^{1/n} - 1\right)} = \frac{e - 1}{1} = e - 1" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

As a decimal, $e - 1 = 1.718$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lower sums rise towards this value: for $n = 4$, $100$ and $1000$ they are $1.5124$, $1.7097$ and $1.7174$. The upper sum with four strips (heights at the right-hand ends) is $1.9420$, so the area is trapped between $1.5124$ and $1.9420$, and $1.718$ lies in between. The next lesson, on the first fundamental theorem, finds the same number at once as $e^1 - e^0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="The curve whose tangent lines all have the same length" points={10} mit="Part II, Problem 6" optional>

This is MIT's extra-credit problem. MIT's Problem Set 3 asked for a proof that the curve $x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = 1$, a hypocycloid, has a striking property: take the tangent line at any point of the curve in the first quadrant, and the piece of that line between the two axes has length $1$. Here you go the other way. You start from the property and use a differential equation to find the curve. (The first quadrant is the part of the plane with $x \ge 0$ and $y \ge 0$.)

<Part label="a" points={2}>

A line passes through the point $(x_0, y_0)$ in the first quadrant and has slope $m_0$. Let $L$ be the length of the piece of the line that lies in the first quadrant. Find $L^2$ in terms of $x_0$, $y_0$ and $m_0$. Do not expand or simplify. (For this piece to have two ends the line must slope downwards, $m_0 < 0$. That condition is our addition.)

<Hint>

The piece runs from the point where the line meets the $y$-axis to the point where it meets the $x$-axis. Find those two points from the equation of the line.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The two points and the origin form a right-angled triangle, and the piece of the line is its longest side.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-a" label="$L^2 =$" expr="(x0 - y0/m0)^2 + (y0 - m0*x0)^2"
  vars={{ x0: [1, 3], y0: [1, 3], m0: [-2, -0.5] }}
  names={{ x0: "x_0", y0: "y_0", m0: "m_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(x0 + y0/m0)^2 + (y0 + m0*x0)^2", hint: "Check the signs of the two intercepts. On the line, y = 0 where x - x0 = -y0/m0, and x = 0 gives y - y0 = -m0*x0." },
    { expr: "sqrt((x0 - y0/m0)^2 + (y0 - m0*x0)^2)", hint: "That is the length L itself. The question asks for its square, which is easier to work with in the next parts." },
    { expr: "(x0 - y0*m0)^2 + (y0 - x0/m0)^2", hint: "The slope is in the wrong place in both intercepts. Going down by y0 to the x-axis takes a step y0/m0 in x, and going back by x0 to the y-axis changes y by m0*x0." },
    { expr: "x0^2 + y0^2", hint: "That is the squared distance from the origin to the point. The piece of the line runs from one axis to the other, and its length depends on the slope." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The piece of the line in the first quadrant ends on the two axes. Find the two intercepts and use Pythagoras.

<Step title="The two intercepts">

The line is $y - y_0 = m_0\,(x - x_0)$. Putting $y = 0$ gives the point on the $x$-axis, and putting $x = 0$ the point on the $y$-axis:

<Formula label="Intercepts of the line" tex="x = x_0 - \frac{y_0}{m_0} \quad (\text{on the } x\text{-axis}), \qquad y = y_0 - m_0\,x_0 \quad (\text{on the } y\text{-axis})" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(a) (our own solution)" />

With $m_0 < 0$ both are positive, so both ends lie on the edges of the first quadrant.

</Step>

<Step title="Pythagoras">

<Formula label="Squared length of the piece in the first quadrant" tex="L^2 = \left(x_0 - \frac{y_0}{m_0}\right)^2 + \left(y_0 - m_0\,x_0\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

The line through $(1, 1)$ with slope $-1$ meets the axes at $(2, 0)$ and $(0, 2)$. The formula gives $L^2 = 2^2 + 2^2 = 8$, the squared distance between those two points.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Suppose $y = f(x)$ is a graph on $0 \le x \le L$ with $f(0) = L$ and $f(L) = 0$, and suppose that the piece in the first quadrant of every tangent line to the graph has the same length $L$. Find the differential equation that $f$ satisfies, in terms of $L$, $x$, $y$ and $y' = dy/dx$. MIT's hints: this needs only thought, not computation; keep $L^2$ and do not take a square root; do not expand or simplify. Type the side of your equation that equals $L^2$, with `yp` for $y'$.

<Hint>

A tangent line is a line through a point with a slope. Which point, and which slope, for the tangent at the point of the graph above $x$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-b" label="$L^2 =$" expr="(x - y/yp)^2 + (y - x*yp)^2"
  vars={{ x: [1, 3], y: [1, 3], yp: [-2, -0.5] }}
  names={{ yp: "y'" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(x + y/yp)^2 + (y + x*yp)^2", hint: "The signs of both intercepts are off. Take your result of part (a) and only rename its three letters." },
    { expr: "sqrt((x - y/yp)^2 + (y - x*yp)^2)", hint: "That is L, not its square. MIT advises against the square root: it makes the next part much harder." },
    { expr: "(x - y*yp)^2 + (y - x/yp)^2", hint: "The slope is in the wrong place in both brackets. Compare with part (a): y is divided by the slope, and x is multiplied by it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The tangent line at the point $(x, y)$ of the graph is the line of part (a) with $x_0 = x$, $y_0 = y = f(x)$ and $m_0 = y' = f'(x)$.

<Step title="Rename the letters of part (a)">

The length of every such piece is $L$, so at every point of the graph

<Formula label="Differential equation of the curve" tex="\left(x - \frac{y}{y'}\right)^2 + \left(y - x\,y'\right)^2 = L^2" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check: it is a differential equation">

The equation links $x$, the unknown function $y$ and its derivative $y'$, and $L$ is a fixed number. It is meant for $0 < x < L$. (At the two ends of the curve found in part (d) the tangent lines are the axes themselves, and $y'$ is infinite or zero there. This remark is ours.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Differentiate the equation of part (b) with respect to $x$. Simplify, and bring the result to the form $(\text{something})\,(x\,y' - y)\,y'' = 0$. MIT's remark: it looks horrendous at first but simplifies a great deal. To make your result checkable, write it as

<Formula label="The form to aim for" tex="2\,(x + G)\,(x\,y' - y)\,y'' = 0" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(c), p. 2 (the factor 2 and the name G are ours)" />

and type $G$ in the box, with `yp` for $y'$.

<Hint>

Differentiate each of the two brackets on its own first. In each of them most terms cancel.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Both brackets of part (b) contain the factor $x\,y' - y$: write $x - y/y'$ as a single fraction, and compare $y - x\,y'$ with $x\,y' - y$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-c" label="$G =$" expr="y/yp^3"
  vars={{ y: [1, 3], yp: [-2, -0.5] }}
  names={{ yp: "y'" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "y/yp^2", hint: "One power of y' has gone missing. The derivative of the first bracket has y'^2 underneath, and the bracket itself, written as (x*y' - y)/y', brings one more." },
    { expr: "-y/yp^3", hint: "Check the sign. The second bracket, y - x*y', is minus (x*y' - y), and its derivative, -x*y'', has a minus sign too." },
    { expr: "y*yp^3", hint: "The powers of y' belong underneath: they come from dividing by y' in the first bracket." },
    { expr: "y/yp", hint: "That is the term of the first bracket before differentiating. Its derivative is y*y''/y'^2, and one more y' comes from writing the bracket as (x*y' - y)/y'." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Differentiate the two squares with the chain rule. The derivatives of the brackets are short, and both products share the factors $x\,y' - y$ and $y''$.

<Step title="Differentiate the two brackets">

With the quotient rule for $y/y'$ and the product rule for $x\,y'$:

<Formula label="Derivatives of the two brackets" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\left(x - \frac{y}{y'}\right) = 1 - \frac{y'\,y' - y\,y''}{y'^2} = \frac{y\,y''}{y'^2}, \qquad \frac{d}{dx}\left(y - x\,y'\right) = y' - y' - x\,y'' = -x\,y''" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Differentiate the equation">

The right-hand side $L^2$ is a constant. So

<Formula label="The differentiated equation" tex="2\left(x - \frac{y}{y'}\right)\frac{y\,y''}{y'^2} - 2\left(y - x\,y'\right)x\,y'' = 0" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Take out the common factors">

Use $x - \dfrac{y}{y'} = \dfrac{x\,y' - y}{y'}$ and $y - x\,y' = -(x\,y' - y)$:

<Formula label="The factored equation" tex="2\,\frac{(x\,y' - y)\,y\,y''}{y'^3} + 2\,(x\,y' - y)\,x\,y'' = 2\left(x + \frac{y}{y'^3}\right)(x\,y' - y)\,y'' = 0" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(c) (our own solution)" />

So $G = y/y'^3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with a function">

Try $y = x^2$ at $x = 1$, where $y = 1$, $y' = 2$ and $y'' = 2$. The left side of part (b) is $\left(x - x/2\right)^2 + \left(x^2 - 2x^2\right)^2 = x^2/4 + x^4$, with derivative $x/2 + 4x^3 = 4.5$ at $x = 1$. The factored form gives $2 \times \left(1 + \tfrac18\right) \times (2 - 1) \times 2 = 4.5$ too.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Show that $x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = L^{2/3}$ is one solution of the equation of part (c). Then say what the other two possibilities give: the curves with $y'' = 0$ and the curves with $x\,y' - y = 0$.

<Hint>

A product is zero when one of its factors is zero. Which factor would the hypocycloid make zero? Find its $y'$ by implicit differentiation.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the other two factors, solve $y'' = 0$ and $x\,y' = y$ (the second by separating variables). Then put each family of curves back into the equation of part (b).

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equation of part (c) is a product of three factors. Each factor set to zero gives a family of curves. The hypocycloid makes the first factor zero.

<Step title="The slope of the hypocycloid">

Differentiate $x^{2/3} + y^{2/3} = L^{2/3}$ implicitly:

<Formula label="Slope of the hypocycloid" tex="\tfrac23\,x^{-1/3} + \tfrac23\,y^{-1/3}\,y' = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y' = -\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)^{1/3}, \qquad y'^3 = -\frac{y}{x}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The first factor is zero">

<Formula label="The first factor on the hypocycloid" tex="x + \frac{y}{y'^3} = x + y \cdot \left(-\frac{x}{y}\right) = 0" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(d) (our own solution)" />

So the hypocycloid solves the equation of part (c). Because part (c) came from differentiating, this only says that the left side of part (b) is some constant along the curve. That the constant is $L^2$ needs a separate check (this check is ours). With the slope found above, $x - y/y' = x^{1/3}\left(x^{2/3} + y^{2/3}\right) = x^{1/3}L^{2/3}$ and $y - x\,y' = y^{1/3}L^{2/3}$, so

<Formula label="The hypocycloid satisfies the equation of part (b)" tex="\left(x - \frac{y}{y'}\right)^2 + \left(y - x\,y'\right)^2 = L^{4/3}\left(x^{2/3} + y^{2/3}\right) = L^{4/3} \cdot L^{2/3} = L^2" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 6(d) (our own solution)" />

It also passes through $(0, L)$ and $(L, 0)$, as part (b) asked. This is the curve of Problem Set 3, enlarged by the factor $L$.

</Step>

<Step title="The curves with y'' = 0">

These are the straight lines $y = m\,x + b$. A straight line is its own tangent line at every point, so the piece cut off by the axes is the same for all its points. A line therefore satisfies the equation of part (b) exactly when its own piece in the first quadrant has length $L$. Those lines are the tangent lines of the hypocycloid (think of a ladder of length $L$ sliding down a wall: each position of the ladder is one of them). They are solutions of the differential equation, but none of them is the curve we want: a straight line cannot pass through both $(0, L)$ and $(L, 0)$ and have length $L$ between the axes, because those two points are $\sqrt2\,L$ apart.

</Step>

<Step title="The curves with x y' − y = 0">

Separating variables, $dy/y = dx/x$, gives $y = C\,x$: the straight lines through the origin. For them both brackets of part (b) are zero, $x - y/y' = x - x = 0$ and $y - x\,y' = 0$, so the left side is $0$ and not $L^2$. Both intercepts of such a line are the origin itself, so the formula of part (a) measures a piece of length zero. These lines solve the differentiated equation of part (c) but not the equation of part (b), unless $L = 0$. They are not solutions of the problem.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

Take $L = 1$ and the point of the hypocycloid with $x = \tfrac18$. Then $y^{2/3} = 1 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac34$, so $y = 0.6495$, and the slope is $y' = -(y/x)^{1/3} = -1.732$. The tangent line meets the axes at $x = 0.5$ and $y = 0.8660$, and $0.5^2 + 0.8660^2 = 1$: a piece of length $1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
