With the chain rule from [Lecture 4](/courses/18-01/chain-rule-and-higher-derivatives) in hand, Lecture 5 uses it in a clever algebraic way. Many functions are easier to describe by an equation they satisfy than by a formula. **Implicit differentiation** gets their derivatives straight from that equation.

## Fractional powers

So far the power rule $\frac{d}{dx}x^a = ax^{a-1}$ is proved for whole numbers $a$, positive and negative. The first example extends it to **rational** exponents $a = m/n$, with $m$ and $n$ integers; with $m = 1$ these are the $n$-th roots. Start from

<Formula label="A rational power" tex="y = x^{m/n} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y^n = x^m" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

We cannot yet differentiate the first form, but the second involves only whole-number powers. Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$. On the left, $y$ is a function of $x$, so the chain rule is needed:

<Formula label="Differentiating the equation" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\,y^n = \frac{d}{dy}\bigl(y^n\bigr)\,\frac{dy}{dx} = n\,y^{n-1}\,\frac{dy}{dx} = m\,x^{m-1}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

One factor, $dy/dx$, is unknown; everything else is known. Solve for it and put back $y = x^{m/n}$:

<Formula label="Solving for dy/dx" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{m}{n}\,\frac{x^{m-1}}{y^{n-1}} = \frac{m}{n}\,\frac{x^{m-1}}{x^{(m/n)(n-1)}} = \frac{m}{n}\,x^{\,m - 1 - m + m/n}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

The exponent simplifies to $m/n - 1 = a - 1$:

<Formula label="Power rule for rational exponents" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\,x^{a} = a\,x^{a-1}, \qquad a = \frac{m}{n}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

just what we hoped. [At the end of this step the transcript has "$a\,x^{n-1}$"; the exponent is $a - 1$, as the notes have.] Everything after the substitution is arithmetic of exponents; the calculus is in the one chain-rule step.

## A circle, two ways

The equation $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ is a circle. It does not give $y$ as a single function of $x$, but it can be solved, $y = \pm\sqrt{1 - x^2}$; take the upper half. The **explicit** route rewrites the square root as a power and uses the chain rule with $a = \tfrac12$ (the case just proved):

<Formula label="Explicit derivative" tex="y = (1 - x^2)^{1/2}, \qquad y' = \tfrac12(1 - x^2)^{-1/2}(-2x) = \frac{-x}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–5" />

The **implicit** route leaves the equation in its simplest form and differentiates it as it stands, applying the chain rule to $y^2$:

<Formula label="Implicit derivative" tex="2x + 2y\,y' = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y' = -\frac{x}{y}" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

The two agree, since $y = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$. The implicit answer is even better: it also holds on the lower half, where $y$ is negative. For $x > 0$ the slope is negative on top (the circle tilts down) and positive at the bottom (it tilts up), exactly as $-x/y$ says.

## When y cannot be found easily

Implicit differentiation shines when solving for $y$ is hard. The lecture's example is a fourth-degree equation:

<Formula label="A quartic curve" tex="y^4 + x\,y^2 - 2 = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 5" />

It happens to be quadratic in $y^2$, so it can be solved, but the result is a tangle of up to four branches:

<Formula label="Explicit solution" tex="y^2 = \frac{-x \pm\sqrt{x^2 + 8}}{2}, \qquad y = \pm\sqrt{\frac{-x \pm\sqrt{x^2 + 8}}{2}}" cite="reading2" at="p. 6" />

Differentiating the original equation instead needs the chain rule on $y^4$ and the product rule on $x\,y^2$:

<Formula label="Implicit differentiation" tex="4y^3\,y' + y^2 + x\,(2y\,y') = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y' = \frac{-y^2}{4y^3 + 2xy}" cite="reading2" at="p. 6" />

This gives the slope in terms of $x$ **and** $y$, so you still need the point on the curve. At $x = 1$, $y = 1$ works ($1 + 1 - 2 = 0$), and there

<Formula label="Slope at (1, 1)" tex="y' = \frac{-1^2}{4 \cdot 1^3 + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 1} = -\frac16" cite="reading2" at="pp. 6–7" />

At another $x$, such as $x = 2$, you would have to find $y$ first (from the explicit formula, or numerically), but you never have to differentiate that formula: once the point is known, the slope comes from simple algebra. As the lecturer puts it, we can differentiate any function we can describe, but not one we cannot find at all. (The notes work a similar example, $y^3 + xy^2 + 1 = 0$, which gives $y' = -y^2/(3y^2 + 2xy)$; notes, p. 2.)

<WorkedExample title="A slope on an ellipse">
<Problem>
Find the slope of the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{4} + y^2 = 1$ at the point $\bigl(\sqrt2, \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\bigr)$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Check the point">
$\tfrac{2}{4} + \tfrac{2}{4} = 1$: it lies on the ellipse.
</Step>
<Step title="Differentiate implicitly">
$\dfrac{2x}{4} + 2y\,y' = 0$, so $y' = -\dfrac{x}{4y}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Evaluate">
$y' = -\dfrac{\sqrt2}{4 \cdot \sqrt2/2} = -\dfrac{1}{2} = -0.5$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Inverse functions

The most important use of implicit differentiation is for **inverse functions**. If $y = \sqrt x$ (for $x > 0$), squaring gives $y^2 = x$; the function $g(y) = y^2$ undoes $f(x) = \sqrt x$. In general, if $y = f(x)$ and $g(y) = x$, then $g(f(x)) = x$, and $g$ is called the inverse of $f$, written $f^{-1}$:

<Formula label="Inverse function" tex="y = f(x), \qquad g(y) = x, \qquad g(f(x)) = x, \qquad g = f^{-1}" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

To draw both on the same axes, write the inverse with $x$ as its variable again. That swaps the roles of $x$ and $y$: each point $(a, b)$ on the graph of $f$ becomes $(b, a)$, so the graph of $f^{-1}$ is the graph of $f$ **reflected in the line** $y = x$ (notes, p. 4). The square root and the square are such a pair.

Differentiating $f^{-1}(y) = x$ with respect to $x$, with the chain rule on the left, gives the derivative of any inverse function from the derivative of the function itself:

<Formula label="Derivative of an inverse function" tex="\frac{d}{dy}\bigl(f^{-1}(y)\bigr)\,\frac{dy}{dx} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dy}f^{-1}(y) = \frac{1}{dy/dx}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

## The derivative of arctan

The inverse tangent, also written $\tan^{-1}$, is defined by

<Formula label="Arctangent" tex="y = \arctan x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tan y = x, \qquad -\frac{\pi}{2} < y < \frac{\pi}{2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

Its graph is the tangent's graph (between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$) reflected in the diagonal: defined for every $x$, rising from the horizontal asymptote $y = -\pi/2$ to $y = \pi/2$. The one ingredient needed is the derivative of the tangent, which the quotient rule gives (as on the [Lecture 3 page](/courses/18-01/derivatives-of-sine-and-cosine)):

<Formula label="Derivative of the tangent" tex="\frac{d}{dy}\tan y = \frac{1}{\cos^2 y} = \sec^2 y" cite="reading2" at="p. 10" />

Differentiate $\tan y = x$ implicitly and solve:

<Formula label="Implicit step" tex="\frac{1}{\cos^2 y}\,y' = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y' = \cos^2 y = \cos^2(\arctan x)" cite="reading2" at="p. 10" />

That is correct but clumsy. A right triangle simplifies it: $\tan y = x$ means an angle $y$ with opposite side $x$ and adjacent side 1, so the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{1 + x^2}$ and $\cos y = 1/\sqrt{1 + x^2}$. Hence

<Formula label="Derivative of arctan" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\arctan x = \frac{1}{1 + x^2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 11" />

## The derivative of arcsin

The inverse sine is even quicker, because its trigonometric identity is simpler. From $\sin y = x$:

<Formula label="Derivative of arcsin" tex="(\cos y)\,y' = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y' = \frac{1}{\cos y} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}" cite="reading2" at="p. 11" />

using $\cos y = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2 y} = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$. A warning: these formulas need a choice of **branch**. For the arcsine, $y$ is taken between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/2$, where $\cos y \ge 0$; drawing the graph shows where the formula makes sense.

<WorkedExample title="Using the new derivatives">
<Problem>
Find (a) the slope of $y = x^{2/3}$ at $x = 8$; (b) the slopes of $\arctan x$ at $x = 1$ and of $\arcsin x$ at $x = 0.5$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Rational power">
$y' = \tfrac23 x^{-1/3} = \tfrac23 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac13 \approx 0.333$, since $8^{1/3} = 2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Inverse functions">
$\dfrac{1}{1 + 1^2} = 0.5$ and $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.25}} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{0.75}} \approx 1.155$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology">
Cosmology often knows things "the wrong way round": how fast the universe's size $x = a/a(t_0)$ grows at each size, $dx/dt$, rather than the size at each time. The derivative of an inverse function turns this round: $dt/dx = 1/(dx/dt)$. Integrating it over all sizes from 0 to today gives the **age of the universe**, the method of MIT's cosmology course for a universe with matter, radiation and vacuum energy.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Implicit differentiation: differentiate both sides of an equation with respect to $x$, using the chain rule on every $y$, then solve the resulting linear equation for $y'$.
- From $y^n = x^m$ it proves the power rule for rational exponents, $\frac{d}{dx}x^{m/n} = \frac{m}{n}x^{m/n - 1}$.
- On the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 1$: $y' = -x/y$, valid on both halves; on $y^4 + xy^2 - 2 = 0$: $y' = -y^2/(4y^3 + 2xy)$, which is $-1/6$ at $(1, 1)$. The slope needs the point, not a formula for $y$.
- The inverse $f^{-1}$ undoes $f$; its graph is the reflection in $y = x$, and $\dfrac{d}{dy}f^{-1}(y) = \dfrac{1}{dy/dx}$.
- $\dfrac{d}{dx}\arctan x = \dfrac{1}{1 + x^2}$ and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\arcsin x = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}$; choose the branch with care.
