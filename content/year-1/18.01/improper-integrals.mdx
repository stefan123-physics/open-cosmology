The last unit of the course is about dealing with infinity (transcript, p. 1). Last time, L'Hôpital's rule ranked rates of growth; today the same ranking, turned upside down into **rates of decay**, decides which functions can be integrated out to infinity (pp. 1–2).

## Rates of growth and decay

For positive functions, write $f \ll g$ when $f(x)/g(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$: $f$ is a lot smaller, asymptotically (transcript, p. 1). Then, for $p > 0$ (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Rates of growth and of decay" tex="\ln x \ll x^p \ll e^x \ll e^{x^2}, \qquad \frac{1}{\ln x} \gg \frac{1}{x^p} \gg e^{-x} \gg e^{-x^2} \qquad (x \to \infty)" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

Taking reciprocals reverses the order: $\frac{1}{\ln x}$ goes to 0 slowly, $e^{-x^2}$ incredibly fast (p. 2).

## The definition

An integral with an infinite limit is defined as a limit of ordinary integrals (transcript, p. 2; notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Improper integral" tex="\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx = \lim_{N \to \infty}\int_a^N f(x)\,dx" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

It **converges** if the limit exists, meaning a finite number, and **diverges** if not (pp. 2, 4). For a positive function, converging means the total area under the endless curve is finite (pp. 2–3). (Functions that change sign, with cancellation, are a subtler story that the course leaves aside, p. 4.)

**The basic example**, for $k > 0$ (pp. 3–4; notes, Example 1, p. 1):

<Formula label="The decaying exponential" tex="\int_0^N e^{-kx}\,dx = \frac{1}{k}\left(1 - e^{-kN}\right) \;\to\; \int_0^\infty e^{-kx}\,dx = \frac{1}{k}" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

The shorthand is to "plug in infinity", $e^{-k\cdot\infty} = 0$, which works only because $k > 0$ (p. 3). The limit is simpler than any finite version: infinity is easier than any finite number, just as derivatives are easier than difference quotients (p. 5; notes, p. 1). In radioactive decay, if $R$ atoms decay per second at the start and the rate falls as $Re^{-kt}$, the total number of atoms that ever decay is $\int_0^\infty Re^{-kt}\,dt = R/k$; letting time run to infinity is an idealisation, but a useful one (transcript, pp. 4–5; notes, p. 1). The most famous example after this one is $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt\pi$, the key constant of probability, from the normal curve to opinion polls (pp. 5–6).

## The borderline: powers of x

Tails matter: a computer handles a finite region, and the mathematician must show the tail is negligible, or find that it is not (transcript, p. 6). The borderline cases are the powers (p. 7). For $p = 1$, $\int_1^N\frac{dx}{x} = \ln N \to \infty$: divergent (p. 7; notes, Example 4, p. 3). For $p \ne 1$ (pp. 7–8; notes, Examples 5–6, p. 3):

<Formula label="Integrals of powers to infinity" tex="\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^p} = \Big[\frac{x^{1-p}}{1 - p}\Big]_1^\infty = \begin{cases}\dfrac{1}{p - 1} & p > 1 \text{ (converges)}\\[1ex] \infty & p \le 1 \text{ (diverges)}\end{cases}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 7–8" />

When $p > 1$, $N^{1-p} \to 0$; when $p < 1$, $N^{1-p} \to \infty$. The formula with $1 - p$ in the denominator breaks down at $p = 1$, which is why the logarithm case had to be done separately: it fails at the edge (p. 8).

## Limit comparison

Often the integral cannot be computed, but its convergence can still be decided (transcript, p. 10). If $f(x) \sim g(x)$ as $x \to \infty$, meaning $f(x)/g(x) \to 1$, then (pp. 8–9; notes, p. 5):

<Formula label="Limit comparison" tex="f(x) \sim g(x) \;(x \to \infty) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx \text{ and } \int_a^\infty g(x)\,dx \text{ both converge or both diverge}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 8–9" />

Only the tail matters, so stay away from other trouble spots: $\int_{100}^\infty\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + 10}}$ behaves like $\int_{100}^\infty\frac{dx}{x}$ and diverges, and the comparison should not start at 0, where $\frac{1}{x}$ blows up for unrelated reasons (p. 9). $\int_{10}^\infty\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^3 + 3}}$ has no formula, but it behaves like $\int x^{-3/2}\,dx$ and converges (p. 10).

For very fast decay, ordinary comparison is enough. For positive functions, a larger integrand has a larger integral, so if $0 \le f \le g$ and $\int_a^\infty g$ converges, so does $\int_a^\infty f$ (notes, p. 4). For example, for $x \ge 1$, $x^2 \ge x$, so $e^{-x^2} \le e^{-x}$, and $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx = 2\int_0^1 e^{-x^2}\,dx + 2\int_1^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx$ is at most a finite part plus $2\int_1^\infty e^{-x}\,dx$, so it converges (pp. 10–11; the notes make the same comparison for $e^{-x^3}$, notes, Example 8, p. 5). The notes use limit comparison for $\int_0^\infty x^ne^{-x}\,dx$, comparing with $e^{-x/2}$ (notes, Example 10, p. 6).

## The second type: a singular integrand

An integral can also be improper because the integrand blows up at an endpoint, like $\frac{1}{\sqrt x}$, $\frac{1}{x}$ or $\frac{1}{x^2}$ at $x = 0$ (transcript, p. 11). Then cut the interval short and take a limit (notes, p. 6):

<Formula label="Improper integral of the second type" tex="\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt x} = \lim_{a \to 0^+}\int_a^1 x^{-1/2}\,dx = \lim_{a \to 0^+}\left(2 - 2a^{1/2}\right) = 2" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

In general $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x^p}$ converges to $\frac{1}{1 - p}$ for $p < 1$ and diverges for $p \ge 1$, the reverse of the behaviour at infinity (notes, p. 6). Ignoring a singularity can fool you: $\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2}$ "=" $\Big[-\frac{1}{x}\Big]_{-1}^{1} = -2$, a negative area for a positive function. The calculation is nonsense: the area near $x = 0$ is infinite and the integral diverges (transcript, p. 11). (The transcript first says "2" and then refers to it as $-2$, p. 11.)

<WorkedExample title="Four improper integrals">
<Problem>
Decide whether each converges, and evaluate where you can: (a) $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{x^{3/2}}$, (b) $\int_1^\infty\frac{x\,dx}{x^3 + 1}$, (c) $\int_1^\infty\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x + 1}}$, (d) $\int_0^8\frac{dx}{x^{1/3}}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A power above 1">
$p = \frac{3}{2} > 1$, so it converges to $\frac{1}{p - 1} = 2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Limit comparison">
$\frac{x}{x^3 + 1} \sim \frac{1}{x^2}$ as $x \to \infty$, and $\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx = 1$ converges, so (b) converges.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A fat tail">
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{x + 1}} \sim x^{-1/2}$, and $p = \frac{1}{2} \le 1$: it diverges.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) The second type">
$p = \frac{1}{3} < 1$, so it converges: $\lim_{a \to 0^+}\Big[\frac{3}{2}x^{2/3}\Big]_a^8 = \frac{3}{2} \times 4 = 6$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="How far can we see?">
Light emitted at the Big Bang has travelled a finite comoving distance by now, even though the scale factor $a(t)$ was zero at $t = 0$. The horizon distance is $a(t)\int_0^t\frac{c\,dt'}{a(t')}$, an improper integral of the second type. For a flat matter-dominated universe, $a \propto t^{2/3}$, the integrand behaves like $t'^{-2/3}$; since $\frac{2}{3} < 1$ the integral converges, and the horizon distance is $3ct$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\int_a^\infty f\,dx = \lim_{N \to \infty}\int_a^N f\,dx$: converges if the limit is finite.
- $\int_0^\infty e^{-kx}\,dx = \frac{1}{k}$ ($k > 0$); $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx = \sqrt\pi$.
- $\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx$ converges (to $\frac{1}{p - 1}$) iff $p > 1$; $\int_0^1 x^{-p}\,dx$ converges iff $p < 1$.
- Limit comparison: integrands with $f/g \to 1$ have tails that converge or diverge together.
- Watch for singularities inside the interval: they make an integral improper.
