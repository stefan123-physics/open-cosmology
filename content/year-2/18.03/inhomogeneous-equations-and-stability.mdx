After [the theory of the homogeneous equation](/courses/18-03/linear-operators-and-wronskian), the lecture starts seriously on the inhomogeneous one (transcript, p. 1).

## The inhomogeneous equation

Put a function on the right (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Inhomogeneous equation" tex="y'' + p(x)\,y' + q(x)\,y = f(x)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Often $x$ is time. The right-hand side is called the **input**, signal, driving term or forcing term, depending on the course; the solution is the **response** (or output), as for first-order equations (p. 1). The **associated homogeneous equation** (or reduced equation), with zero on the right, is an essential part of the solution (p. 1). Its general solution $c_1y_1 + c_2y_2$ is called the **complementary solution**, written $y_c$ (or $y_h$, for homogeneous) (p. 2).

## Two models

**The spring–mass–dashpot with an external force** (transcript, pp. 2–3). Newton's law with the spring force $-kx$, the damping force $-bx'$ and an outside force $f(t)$ (an electromagnet pulling on a load of iron ore, say) gives

<Formula label="Forced spring" tex="m\,x'' + b\,x' + k\,x = f(t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

With $f = 0$ the system is **passive**: started off, it does what it wants by itself. With $f \ne 0$ it is **forced** from outside: a different problem, but the passive behaviour remains part of its solution (pp. 3–4).

**The RLC circuit** (pp. 4–5). An inductance $L$ (a coil), a resistance $R$ and a capacitance $C$, driven by an electromotive force $E(t)$, a battery or an alternating source. Kirchhoff's voltage law adds the drops $Li'$, $Ri$ and $q/C$, where $i$ is the current and $q$ the charge, with $q' = i$ (pp. 4–5). Differentiating removes $q$ (p. 5):

<Formula label="RLC circuit" tex="L\,i' + R\,i + \frac{q}{C} = E(t), \qquad L\,i'' + R\,i' + \frac{i}{C} = E'(t)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 4–5" />

On the right is now the derivative of the electromotive force, so a battery gives the passive, homogeneous case (p. 5). Left alone, a charged capacitor discharges through the circuit, swinging back and forth if underdamped, until the current dies away; normally one drives the circuit instead (pp. 5–6). (Here $L$ is the inductance; below it is again the operator.)

## The structure of the solutions

Write the equation with the linear operator $L = D^2 + pD + q$ as $Ly = f(x)$ (transcript, p. 6). The main theorem (pp. 6–7):

<Formula label="General solution of the inhomogeneous equation" tex="y = y_p + y_c = y_p + c_1y_1 + c_2y_2" cite="reading1" at="pp. 6–7" />

where $y_1$, $y_2$ are independent solutions of the homogeneous equation and $y_p$ is a **particular solution** of $Ly = f$. "Particular" means **any one** solution: any one will do, and no one has found a better word (p. 7). So solving takes two steps: find $y_c$ from the reduced equation, then find one $y_p$ (p. 7).

**Proof** (pp. 7–9), using only linearity, so it works for any order:

1. All these are solutions: $L(y_p + c_1y_1 + c_2y_2) = Ly_p + L(c_1y_1 + c_2y_2) = f + 0 = f$ (p. 8).
2. There are no others: if $Lu = f$, then $L(u - y_p) = Lu - Ly_p = f - f = 0$, so $u - y_p$ solves the homogeneous equation and equals $\tilde c_1y_1 + \tilde c_2y_2$ for some particular constants. Hence $u = y_p + \tilde c_1y_1 + \tilde c_2y_2$ is not new (pp. 8–9).

With constant coefficients, $y_c$ is already known from exponentials, sines and cosines, so what remains is finding one $y_p$. That takes the next two weeks: operator methods for standard inputs, Fourier series, series or approximation methods, and, if all else fails, a computer (pp. 9–10).

## The first-order case revisited

For $y' + ky = q(t)$ the solution was (transcript, p. 10)

<Formula label="First-order solution" tex="y = e^{-kt}\int q(t)\,e^{kt}\,dt + C\,e^{-kt}" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

The term $Ce^{-kt}$ solves $y' + ky = 0$: it is $y_c$. The first term is a $y_p$, once the indefinite integral is taken as one function, or made definite from 0 to $t$ (pp. 10–11). For $k > 0$, $y_c$ is the **transient**, carrying the initial value and dying away, and $y_p$ is the **steady-state** solution that all solutions approach. For $k < 0$, $y_c$ grows and dominates, and the analysis is meaningless (pp. 11–12).

## Stability

Now take constant coefficients and time as the variable (transcript, p. 12):

<Formula label="Constant-coefficient inhomogeneous equation" tex="y'' + Ay' + By = f(t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 12" />

In $y = y_p + c_1y_1 + c_2y_2$ the constants carry the initial values. The same split into steady state and transient works exactly when (pp. 12–13)

<Formula label="Stability" tex="c_1y_1 + c_2y_2 \to 0 \text{ as } t \to \infty \text{ for all } c_1, c_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

Then the equation is called **stable** (some say asymptotically stable): $c_1y_1 + c_2y_2$ is the transient and $y_p$ the steady-state solution, what lasts after the rest has gone (p. 13). Only the reduced equation matters for this (p. 15).

**Case by case** (pp. 14–15):

- Distinct real roots: $c_1e^{r_1t} + c_2e^{r_2t} \to 0$ for all $c_1$, $c_2$ exactly when $r_1 < 0$ and $r_2 < 0$. A zero root would give a constant, which does not go to 0. (The transcript gives the right answer, but says the constant "will go to zero" as the reason, p. 15.)
- A double root: $(c_1 + c_2t)e^{r_1t} \to 0$ exactly when $r_1 < 0$; it may rise at first, but the exponential wins. With $r_1 = 0$, $c_1 + c_2t$ grows (if $c_2 
e 0$).
- Complex roots $a \pm bi$: $e^{at}(c_1\cos bt + c_2\sin bt)$; the bracket is a pure oscillation, so everything depends on the exponential: $a < 0$.

In one breath (pp. 15–16):

<Formula label="Stability criterion" tex="\mathrm{Re}\,r < 0 \text{ for every characteristic root } r" cite="reading1" at="pp. 15–16" />

For real roots the real part is the root itself; only in the complex case is it the $a$ (p. 16). The lecture also promises a condition stated directly in terms of the coefficients $A$ and $B$ (p. 14), but the recording ends before it is given.

<WorkedExample title="Stable or not?">
<Problem>
(a) Solve $y'' + 3y' + 2y = 6$ and say what all solutions approach. (b) Is $y'' - y' + 2y = \cos t$ stable? (c) An RLC circuit has $L = 0.5$ H, $R = 4$ Ω and $C = 0.02$ F. Is its current equation stable?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A constant input">
A constant $y_p = 3$ works, since $2 \cdot 3 = 6$. The roots of $r^2 + 3r + 2 = (r + 1)(r + 2)$ are $-1$ and $-2$, so $y = 3 + c_1e^{-t} + c_2e^{-2t}$. Both roots are negative: the equation is stable, and every solution tends to the steady state 3.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A positive real part">
$r^2 - r + 2 = 0$ gives $r = \frac{1 \pm i\sqrt7}{2}$, with real part $\frac12 > 0$. Not stable: the complementary solution grows like $e^{t/2}$, so no steady state is approached.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The circuit">
$0.5\,i'' + 4\,i' + 50\,i = E'(t)$, so $r^2 + 8r + 100 = 0$ and $r = -4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 100} = -4 \pm 9.17i$. The real part $-4$ is negative: stable, an underdamped circuit.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $Ly = f$: input $f$, response $y$; the associated homogeneous equation has the complementary solution $y_c = c_1y_1 + c_2y_2$.
- Every solution is $y_p + y_c$, with $y_p$ any one particular solution; the proof uses only linearity.
- Stable: $y_c \to 0$ for all constants; then $y_c$ is the transient and $y_p$ the steady state.
- Stable exactly when all characteristic roots have negative real parts.
