Complex numbers will be used constantly in 18.03. This lecture reviews how to divide them, then introduces the polar form and the complex exponential (transcript, p. 1).

## Division

For $z = a + bi$, the **complex conjugate** is $\bar z = a - bi$, and their product is real (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Conjugate product" tex="z\,\bar z = (a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 + b^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

So to divide, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator. For example (p. 1):

<Formula label="A division" tex="\frac{2 + i}{1 - 3i} = \frac{(2 + i)(1 + 3i)}{(1 - 3i)(1 + 3i)} = \frac{-1 + 7i}{10} = -\frac{1}{10} + \frac{7}{10}\,i" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Collect the real and imaginary parts directly rather than multiplying out four terms (p. 1).

## Polar form and Euler's definition

If $a + bi$ has polar coordinates $r$, $\theta$, then $a + bi = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$ (transcript, pp. 1–2). Euler took the decisive step of writing (p. 2)

<Formula label="Euler's definition" tex="e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

It is a definition, not a formula to be proved, but one can ask why it is the right one: what should an exponential do (pp. 2–4)?

**The exponential law.** Multiply $(\cos\theta_1 + i\sin\theta_1)(\cos\theta_2 + i\sin\theta_2)$: the real part is $\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2 = \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$ and the imaginary part $\sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2 = \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$ (pp. 4–5). So

<Formula label="Exponential law" tex="e^{i\theta_1}\,e^{i\theta_2} = e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 4–5" />

which says exactly the same thing as the two addition formulas of trigonometry, in far less space (p. 5).

**The derivative.** $e^{at}$ is the solution of $dy/dt = ay$ with $y(0) = 1$ (p. 3). A complex-valued function of a real variable, $u(t) + iv(t)$, is differentiated part by part, as a velocity is found from its components (pp. 6–7). Then (pp. 7–8)

<Formula label="Derivative of the complex exponential" tex="\frac{d}{dt}e^{it} = -\sin t + i\cos t = i(\cos t + i\sin t) = i\,e^{it}, \qquad e^{i0} = 1" cite="reading1" at="pp. 7–8" />

so $e^{it}$ solves $y' = iy$, $y(0) = 1$, as it should. The value at 0 comes from $\cos 0 + i\sin 0 = 1$, not by first simplifying the exponent $i \cdot 0$ to 0 (p. 8). (The lecture also mentions that the power series agree, without going into it, pp. 4 and 8.) For a general complex exponent, $e^{a + ib} = e^a e^{ib}$ is again a definition (p. 9).

## Multiplication in polar form

Every complex number $\alpha$ can be written $re^{i\theta}$, with **modulus** $r = |\alpha|$ and **argument** $\theta$ (written arg α) (transcript, p. 9). Then by the exponential law (pp. 9–10):

<Formula label="Polar multiplication" tex="r_1e^{i\theta_1}\cdot r_2e^{i\theta_2} = r_1r_2\,e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

**Multiply the moduli and add the arguments**: the geometric meaning of complex multiplication, invisible in the Cartesian formula (p. 10).

## Complexifying an integral

Integrating $e^{-x}\cos x$ by parts takes two rounds and a trick (transcript, p. 10). Instead, note that $e^{-x}\cos x$ is the real part of $e^{-x}e^{ix} = e^{(-1 + i)x}$, and exponentials are easy to integrate (p. 11):

<Formula label="Complexified integral" tex="\int e^{-x}\cos x\,dx = \mathrm{Re}\int e^{(-1 + i)x}\,dx = \mathrm{Re}\,\frac{e^{(-1 + i)x}}{-1 + i}" cite="reading1" at="p. 11" />

To take the real part, first divide: $\frac{1}{-1 + i} = \frac{-1 - i}{2}$ (p. 12). The real part of $\frac{-1 - i}{2}\,e^{-x}(\cos x + i\sin x)$ is (pp. 12–13)

<Formula label="Result" tex="\int e^{-x}\cos x\,dx = \frac{e^{-x}}{2}\,(\sin x - \cos x) + C" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

Whenever $e^{ax}\cos bx$ or $e^{ax}\sin bx$ appears, think of passing to the complex domain (p. 13).

## Roots of unity

In the real numbers, 1 has one or two $n$-th roots; in the complex numbers it always has $n$ (transcript, pp. 13–14). Since multiplying adds angles and all the roots have modulus 1, they are **equally spaced on the unit circle**. For $n = 5$, the first one past 1 is (p. 14)

<Formula label="A fifth root of unity" tex="\zeta = e^{2\pi i/5}, \qquad \zeta^5 = e^{2\pi i} = 1" cite="reading1" at="pp. 14–15" />

The lecture stops there (p. 15); the others follow in the same way: $\zeta^2, \zeta^3, \zeta^4$ and $\zeta^5 = 1$.

<WorkedExample title="Practice with polar form">
<Problem>
(a) Write $\frac{3 + 4i}{1 + 2i}$ as $a + bi$. (b) Compute $(1 + i)^8$ in polar form. (c) Find $\int e^x\sin x\,dx$ by complexifying. (d) Find the cube roots of 1.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Division">
Multiply by $1 - 2i$: $\frac{(3 + 4i)(1 - 2i)}{5} = \frac{11 - 2i}{5} = \frac{11}{5} - \frac25 i$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A power">
$1 + i = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}$, so $(1 + i)^8 = (\sqrt2)^8 e^{2\pi i} = 16$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) An integral">
$e^x\sin x = \mathrm{Im}\,e^{(1 + i)x}$, and $\int e^{(1 + i)x}dx = \frac{1 - i}{2}e^x(\cos x + i\sin x)$, whose imaginary part is $\frac{e^x}{2}(\sin x - \cos x)$, plus a constant $C$.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Cube roots">
$e^{2\pi ik/3}$: $1$ and $-\frac12 \pm \frac{\sqrt3}{2}i$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Divide by multiplying by the conjugate: $z\bar z = a^2 + b^2$.
- $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$; it obeys the exponential law and $\frac{d}{dt}e^{it} = ie^{it}$.
- $a + bi = re^{i\theta}$; multiplying multiplies moduli and adds arguments.
- Complexify integrals of $e^{ax}\cos bx$; the $n$-th roots of 1 are $e^{2\pi ik/n}$, equally spaced on the unit circle.
