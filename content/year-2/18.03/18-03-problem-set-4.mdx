This set practises the lessons on [the sine and cosine combined](/courses/18-03/sinusoids-and-linear-models), [damped oscillations](/courses/18-03/damped-oscillations), [the exponential input theorem](/courses/18-03/exponential-input-theorem) and [resonance and practical resonance](/courses/18-03/resonance-and-beats). It follows MIT's Problem Set 4, in our own words.

MIT's set has two parts. Part II holds MIT's four own problems, and all four are written out below, with hints and solutions of our own. Part I is a short list of exercises from MIT's notes and from a textbook; the last section of this page lists them. MIT labels each problem with the day of the lecture that it belongs to. Problem 13 belongs to MIT's lecture on forced systems and the exponential response formula, Problem 14 to the one on phase lag and complex gain, Problem 15 to the one on operators and resonance, and Problem 16 to the one on frequency response. This site's lessons cover these topics in Lectures 8, 10, 12, 13 and 14.

Three of the problems use MIT's "Mathlets", small interactive programs (Forced Damped Vibrations; Amplitude and Phase: Second Order I). You do not need them here: where MIT asks you to measure something with a Mathlet, we ask you to compute it, and a plot from any tool you like (a graphing calculator, a spreadsheet, a few lines of code) is a good way to compare. The sheet does not give the mass of the Amplitude and Phase Mathlet; MIT's solution uses $m = 1$, and so do we.

MIT's solutions, which are on OpenCourseWare, stop after part (b) of Problem 15: Problem 15(c) and Problem 16 have no solution there. Those solutions are ours alone.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `exp(3*t)*cos(4*t)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Type `t` for $t$ and `w` for $\omega$. A number box wants a decimal number, not a fraction or a square root. Keep all the digits your calculator shows while you work. Angles are in radians. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem or part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

MIT gives the points of Problems 13, 14 and 15(a) and (b) in its solutions, and we use them. MIT's solutions give no points for Problem 15(c) and Problem 16: we gave 6 points to 15(c) and 26 to Problem 16, split over the parts by ourselves. In every problem we also split the points over the answer boxes ourselves. The sheet's page numbers count its pages, from 1.

## Problem 13

<Exercise id="p13" title="Superposition and the exponential response formula" points={21} mit="Part II, Problem 13">

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the general real solution of

<Formula label="A first-order equation with a sinusoidal, growing input" tex="\dot x + 2x = e^{3t}\cos 4t" cite="mit" at="Problem 13(a), p. 2" />

MIT suggests writing the right side as the real part of a complex exponential and using the exponential response formula (ERF). The box asks for the particular solution $x_p$ that this method gives. The general solution is $x_p$ plus a solution of the homogeneous equation.

<Hint>

Which complex exponential has $e^{3t}\cos 4t$ as its real part? Replace the right side by it, and call the unknown $z$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The ERF divides the exponential by the characteristic polynomial $p(s)$, evaluated at the exponent. Here $p(s) = s + 2$. Evaluate it at the complex exponent and rationalise: $1/(u + iv) = (u - iv)/(u^2 + v^2)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Multiply the result by $e^{3t}(\cos 4t + i\sin 4t)$ and take the real part.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p13-a" label="$x_p(t) =$" expr="exp(3*t)*(5*cos(4*t) + 4*sin(4*t))/41"

<Solution>

**The idea.** The input is the real part of $e^{(3+4i)t}$. The equation is linear with real coefficients, so the real part of the solution for the complex input solves the real equation. The ERF gives the complex solution directly.

<Step title="Complexify and use the ERF">

Let $z$ solve $\dot z + 2z = e^{(3+4i)t}$. The characteristic polynomial is $p(s) = s + 2$, and the exponent is $\alpha = 3 + 4i$, so $p(\alpha) = 5 + 4i \neq 0$ and the exponential input theorem applies:

<Formula label="Exponential response formula" tex="z_p = \frac{e^{\alpha t}}{p(\alpha)} = \frac{e^{(3+4i)t}}{5 + 4i}" cite="reading2" at="p. 4" />

</Step>

<Step title="Take the real part">

Since $\dfrac{1}{5 + 4i} = \dfrac{5 - 4i}{41}$ and $e^{(3+4i)t} = e^{3t}(\cos 4t + i\sin 4t)$,

<Formula label="The real particular solution" tex="x_p = \mathrm{Re}\, z_p = \frac{e^{3t}}{41}\,\mathrm{Re}\big[(5 - 4i)(\cos 4t + i\sin 4t)\big] = \frac{e^{3t}}{41}\,(5\cos 4t + 4\sin 4t)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 13(a), p. 1" />

The homogeneous equation $\dot x + 2x = 0$ has the solution $c\,e^{-2t}$, so the general real solution is $x = x_p + c\,e^{-2t}$. [MIT's solution writes the factor as $(5 + 4i)/(25 + 16)$, which should be $(5 - 4i)/41$, and its last line has $3/25$ and $4/25$ in place of $5/41$ and $4/41$. Its $x_p$ line is right.]

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$: $x_p = 5/41$, and $\dot x_p = 3 \cdot 5/41 + (16)/41 = 31/41$, because the derivative of the bracket at $0$ is $4 \cdot 4 = 16$. So $\dot x_p + 2x_p = 31/41 + 10/41 = 1 = \cos 0 \cdot e^{0}$, as the equation demands.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the general real solution of

<Formula label="A damped, driven oscillator" tex="\ddot x + \dot x + 2x = \cos t" cite="mit" at="Problem 13(b), p. 2" />

MIT asks you to write the sinusoidal solution in two ways: as $a\cos t + b\sin t$, and as $A\cos(t - \phi)$. The first box asks for $a$ (it turns out that $b$ is the same number), the second for the phase lag $\phi$, in radians, between $0$ and $\pi/2$.

<Hint>

Replace $\cos t$ by $e^{it}$, whose real part it is, and use the ERF with $p(s) = s^2 + s + 2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Rationalise $1/(1 + i)$, multiply by $\cos t + i\sin t$ and take the real part. For the second form, write $1 + i$ in polar form (a size times $e$ to the power $i$ times an angle), or use the sine and cosine identity of Lecture 8.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p13-b-a" label="$a =$" value="0.5" tolerance="0.005" calc="p13-b-a" />

<Answer id="p13-b-phi" label="Phase lag $\phi$ (radians) $=$" value="0.7854" tolerance="0.002" calc="p13-b-phi" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same method as in (a), now with a second-order polynomial, and then a change from the Cartesian form $a\cos t + b\sin t$ to the polar form $A\cos(t - \phi)$.

<Step title="The ERF">

With $\alpha = i$: $p(i) = i^2 + i + 2 = 1 + i$, which is not zero, so

<Formula label="Complex particular solution" tex="z_p = \frac{e^{it}}{1 + i} = \frac{1 - i}{2}\,(\cos t + i\sin t)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 13(b), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Two forms of the real part">

The real part is $x_p = \tfrac12\cos t + \tfrac12\sin t$, so $a = b = \tfrac12$. In polar form $1 + i = \sqrt 2\,e^{i\pi/4}$, so $z_p = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}e^{i(t - \pi/4)}$:

<Formula label="Polar form of the particular solution" tex="x_p = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\cos\!\left(t - \frac{\pi}{4}\right), \qquad A = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2} = 0.7071, \quad \phi = \frac{\pi}{4} = 0.7854" cite="solutions" at="Problem 13(b), p. 1" />

The general solution is $x = x_p + x_h$. The characteristic polynomial $s^2 + s + 2 = (s + \tfrac12)^2 + \tfrac74$ has the roots $-\tfrac12 \pm i\tfrac{\sqrt 7}{2}$, so

<Formula label="The homogeneous solution" tex="x_h = e^{-t/2}\left(c_1\cos\frac{\sqrt 7}{2}\,t + c_2\sin\frac{\sqrt 7}{2}\,t\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 13(b), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two forms agree: $a^2 + b^2 = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12 = A^2$, and $\tan\phi = b/a = 1$. Direct substitution at $t = 0$: $x_p = \tfrac12$, $\dot x_p = \tfrac12$, $\ddot x_p = -\tfrac12$, so $\ddot x_p + \dot x_p + 2x_p = -\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 + 1 = 1 = \cos 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

MIT now has you open the Mathlet Forced Damped Vibrations, with the mass $m = 1$, the damping constant $b = 1$ and the spring constant $k = 2$, so that the equation of part (b) is the equation of the Mathlet with its forcing amplitude $A$ set to $0$. Start it from $x(0) = 0$, $\dot x(0) = 1$ and measure the pseudoperiod of the oscillation, which is the time between successive times at which the solution crosses zero in the same direction. MIT asks how well the measurement agrees with part (b).

Here you compute it instead. The box asks for the pseudoperiod, in the time units of the equation.

<Hint>

With no forcing, the equation is $\ddot x + \dot x + 2x = 0$. Its solutions are those of $x_h$ in part (b).

</Hint>

<Hint>

The oscillating factor of $x_h$ has the angular frequency $\omega_d$ (the damped, or pseudo, frequency). The period is $2\pi$ divided by it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p13-c" label="Pseudoperiod $=$" value="4.7496" tolerance="0.01" calc="p13-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The damping factor $e^{-t/2}$ only shrinks the oscillation; the zeros are those of the cosine and sine, which repeat with the period of their argument.

<Step title="The pseudoperiod">

The roots of the characteristic polynomial, found in part (b), are $-\tfrac12 \pm i\omega_d$ with $\omega_d = \sqrt 7/2$. A solution crosses zero (in one direction) once per period of $\cos\omega_d t$:

<Formula label="Pseudoperiod" tex="T_d = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_d} = \frac{4\pi}{\sqrt 7} = 4.7496" cite="solutions" at="Problem 13(c), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compare with a measurement">

MIT measured the first six zeros of the Mathlet's solution at $2.36, 4.76, 7.12, 9.48, 11.89, 14.19$. Zeros of a damped oscillation come every half pseudoperiod, so the average gap, $2.366$, doubled, gives $4.73$, which agrees with $4.7496$ to about half a per cent.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Keep $m = 1$, $b = 1$, $k = 2$ and set the forcing to $\cos t$, as in part (b). MIT has you select the steady state curve $x_p$ in the Mathlet, measure its amplitude and its value at $t = 0$, compare them with part (b), and also compute $\dot x_p(0)$. Do it by computation. The three boxes ask for the amplitude of $x_p$, for $x_p(0)$ and for $\dot x_p(0)$.

<Hint>

Use the polar form of part (b), $x_p = A\cos(t - \phi)$, with $A$ and $\phi$ from there.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $\dot x_p(0)$ differentiate the polar form first: the derivative of $\cos(t - \phi)$ is $-\sin(t - \phi)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p13-d-amp" label="Amplitude of $x_p$ $=$" value="0.7071" tolerance="0.002" calc="p13-d-amp" />

<Answer id="p13-d-x0" label="$x_p(0) =$" value="0.5" tolerance="0.002" calc="p13-d-x0" />

<Answer id="p13-d-v0" label="$\dot x_p(0) =$" value="0.5" tolerance="0.002" calc="p13-d-v0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Everything follows from the one formula $x_p = A\cos(t - \phi)$ of part (b).

<Step title="The three numbers">

The amplitude is $A = 1/\sqrt 2 = 0.7071$. At $t = 0$,

<Formula label="Value and velocity of the steady state at t = 0" tex="x_p(0) = A\cos\!\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac12, \qquad \dot x_p(0) = -A\sin\!\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right) = \frac12" cite="solutions" at="Problem 13(d), p. 1" />

MIT measured the amplitude as $0.71$ and $x_p(0)$ as about $0.50$ on the Mathlet: good agreement.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The values agree with the Cartesian form $x_p = \tfrac12\cos t + \tfrac12\sin t$ of part (b): at $0$ it is $\tfrac12$, and its derivative $-\tfrac12\sin t + \tfrac12\cos t$ is $\tfrac12$ at $0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={5}>

Now choose rest initial conditions: $x(0) = 0$ and $\dot x(0) = 0$. The solution is $x = x_p + x_h$, with the $x_p$ of part (b) and the transient $x_h$ (the Mathlet draws all three curves). Compute $x_h$. The box asks for the formula of $x_h(t)$.

<Hint>

$x_h$ must have the form of part (b), with two constants. Which two conditions do the rest conditions put on $x_h(0)$ and $\dot x_h(0)$, given $x_p(0)$ and $\dot x_p(0)$ from part (d)?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the form $e^{-t/2}(a\cos\omega_d t + b\sin\omega_d t)$. The constant $a$ is $x_h(0)$. For $\dot x_h(0)$ remember that the factor $e^{-t/2}$ also has to be differentiated.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The particular solution of part (b) does not satisfy the rest conditions. The transient $x_h$ is chosen to cancel its value and its velocity at $t = 0$.

<Step title="The conditions on x_h">

The full solution $x = x_p + x_h$ has $x(0) = 0$ and $\dot x(0) = 0$, so with part (d):

<Formula label="Conditions for rest initial conditions" tex="x_h(0) = -x_p(0) = -\frac12, \qquad \dot x_h(0) = -\dot x_p(0) = -\frac12" cite="solutions" at="Problem 13(e), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The constants">

Write $x_h = e^{-t/2}(a\cos\omega_d t + b\sin\omega_d t)$, with $\omega_d = \sqrt 7/2$. Then $x_h(0) = a$, so $a = -\tfrac12$. Differentiating, $\dot x_h(0) = -\tfrac12 a + \omega_d b = \tfrac14 + \tfrac{\sqrt 7}{2}b$, and this must be $-\tfrac12$:

<Formula label="The transient for rest initial conditions" tex="b = \frac{2}{\sqrt 7}\left(-\frac12 - \frac14\right) = -\frac{3}{2\sqrt 7}, \qquad x_h = e^{-t/2}\left(-\frac12\cos\frac{\sqrt 7}{2}t - \frac{3}{2\sqrt 7}\sin\frac{\sqrt 7}{2}t\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 13(e), p. 1" />

</Step>

At $t = 0$ the formula gives $x_h(0) = -\tfrac12$, and $\dot x_h(0) = -\tfrac12\cdot(-\tfrac12) + \tfrac{\sqrt 7}{2}\cdot\left(-\tfrac{3}{2\sqrt 7}\right) = \tfrac14 - \tfrac34 = -\tfrac12$. Added to $x_p$ they give $x(0) = 0$ and $\dot x(0) = 0$. And $x_h \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$: the solution settles on the steady state.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 14

<Exercise id="p14" title="A spring driven through its top end: complex gain" points={16} mit="Part II, Problem 14">

MIT's problem uses the Mathlet Amplitude and Phase: Second Order I. A mass hangs on a spring and a dashpot. The dashpot is fixed, and the top end of the spring is moved up and down: its position is the input signal $y(t) = \cos\omega t$. The position of the mass is the response $x(t)$, of which we look only at the steady state. The spring pulls on the mass with the force $k(y - x)$ and the dashpot with $-b\dot x$, so Newton's law, with $m = 1$, gives

<Formula label="The mass driven through the spring" tex="\ddot x + b\,\dot x + k\,x = k\cos\omega t" cite="mit" at="Problem 14, p. 2" />

(Our statement of the model: MIT's sheet describes the system in words.) The input has amplitude $1$, so the amplitude of the response is the gain. Take $b = 0.5$, $k = 4$ and $\omega = 2$.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

MIT asks you to verify the gain and the time lag that the Mathlet displays. Compute them: the gain, and the time lag, which is the time by which the response lags behind the input (the phase lag divided by $\omega$).

<Hint>

Complexify: replace $\cos\omega t$ by $e^{i\omega t}$. The ERF needs the characteristic polynomial $p(s) = s^2 + bs + k$ at $s = i\omega$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With the given numbers $p(2i)$ is a very simple number. Write the response as $\mathrm{Re}\,[\,z_p\,]$, put it in the form $g\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ and read off $g$ and $\phi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p14-a-gain" label="Gain $=$" value="4" tolerance="0.02" calc="p14-a-gain" />

<Answer id="p14-a-lag" label="Time lag $=$" value="0.7854" tolerance="0.002" calc="p14-a-lag" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Replace the input by a complex exponential, divide by the characteristic polynomial at $i\omega$ (the ERF), and take the real part. The size of the complex number in front is the gain, and minus its argument is the phase lag.

<Step title="The ERF at ω = 2">

The equation is $\ddot x + \tfrac12\dot x + 4x = 4\cos 2t$. Complexified, the input is $4e^{2it}$, and

<Formula label="The characteristic polynomial at 2i" tex="p(2i) = (2i)^2 + \tfrac12(2i) + 4 = -4 + i + 4 = i" cite="solutions" at="Problem 14(a), p. 1" />

so $z_p = 4e^{2it}/i = -4i\,e^{2it}$, and its real part is $x_p = 4\sin 2t = 4\cos(2t - \pi/2)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Gain and lag">

The gain is the amplitude $4$, and the phase lag is $\phi = \pi/2$ (the response is a sine where the input is a cosine). The time lag is

<Formula label="Time lag" tex="t_0 = \frac{\phi}{\omega} = \frac{\pi/2}{2} = \frac{\pi}{4} = 0.7854" cite="solutions" at="Problem 14(a), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $\omega = 2 = \sqrt k$ the term $k - \omega^2$ is zero and only the damping term is left, so $p(2i)$ is purely imaginary, which makes the phase lag exactly a quarter of a period. Substituting $x_p = 4\sin 2t$ into the equation gives $-16\sin 2t + \tfrac12\cdot 8\cos 2t + 16\sin 2t = 4\cos 2t$, as required.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

For this system find $H(\omega)$, the gain $g(\omega) = |H(\omega)|$ as a function of $\omega$, and $\tan\phi$, where $\phi = -\mathrm{Arg}\,H$ is the phase lag, for $0 < \omega < 2$ (where the real part of $p(i\omega)$ is positive). </Hint>

    { expr: "(w/2)/(w^2 - 4)", hint: "The sign is reversed. For a slow input the response lags a little, so the phase lag is a small positive angle and its tangent is positive." },
    { expr: "w/(4 - w^2)", hint: "The imaginary part of p(iω) is bω = ω/2, not ω." },
    { expr: "(4 - w^2)/(w/2)", hint: "This is the cotangent. As ω → 0 the phase lag goes to 0, so tan φ must go to 0, but yours blows up." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For $e^{i\omega t}$ in place of $\cos\omega t$ the ERF gives $z_p = \dfrac{k}{p(i\omega)}\,e^{i\omega t}$. The complex gain is that coefficient, and its polar form $H = g\,e^{-i\phi}$ holds both the gain and the phase lag.

<Step title="The complex gain">

The polynomial at $i\omega$ is $p(i\omega) = (i\omega)^2 + \tfrac12(i\omega) + 4 = 4 - \omega^2 + \tfrac12 i\omega$. Therefore

<Formula label="Complex gain" tex="H(\omega) = \frac{4}{p(i\omega)} = \frac{4}{4 - \omega^2 + \tfrac12\,i\omega}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 14(b), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Gain and phase lag">

Then $z_p = H(\omega)e^{i\omega t}$ and the real response is $g\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ with

<Formula label="Gain" tex="g(\omega) = |H(\omega)| = \frac{4}{\sqrt{(4 - \omega^2)^2 + \tfrac14\,\omega^2}}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 14(b), p. 1" />

The polar form of a reciprocal (Lecture 7) says that the argument of $1/p$ is minus the argument of $p$, so $\phi = \mathrm{Arg}\,p(i\omega)$, and

<Formula label="Phase lag" tex="\tan\phi = \frac{\mathrm{Im}\,p(i\omega)}{\mathrm{Re}\,p(i\omega)} = \frac{\omega/2}{4 - \omega^2}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 14(b), p. 1" />

For $\omega > 2$ the real part of $p$ is negative and $\phi$ lies between $\pi/2$ and $\pi$: the formula for $\tan\phi$ still holds, but $\phi$ is then not the arc tangent of it.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $\omega = 2$ the formulas give $g = 4/\sqrt{0 + 1} = 4$ and $\tan\phi = 1/0$, which means $\phi = \pi/2$: the values of part (a). At $\omega = 0$: $g = 1$ and $\phi = 0$, because a very slow input is followed exactly.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 15

<Exercise id="p15" title="Resonance and undetermined coefficients" points={18} mit="Part II, Problem 15">

Find the general real solution of each equation. MIT's solutions give 6 points to each of (a) and (b). In each part the box asks for one particular solution $x_p$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

<Formula label="An exponential input at a root" tex="\dot x + x = e^{-t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 15(a), p. 2" />

MIT asks you to use the resonant case of the exponential response formula.

<Hint>

The input is $e^{at}$ with $a = -1$. Is $a$ a root of the characteristic polynomial $p(s) = s + 1$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

If it is a simple root, the ERF divides $t\,e^{at}$ by $p'(a)$ instead.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p15-a" label="$x_p(t) =$" expr="t*exp(-t)"

<Solution>

**The idea.** The input $e^{-t}$ is already a solution of the homogeneous equation, so the ordinary ERF fails; the resonant ERF puts one factor $t$ in front.

<Step title="The resonant ERF">

The characteristic polynomial is $p(s) = s + 1$, and $p(-1) = 0$, so we are in the resonant case. Since $p'(s) = 1 \neq 0$, the root is simple, and

<Formula label="Resonant exponential response" tex="x_p = \frac{t\,e^{-t}}{p'(-1)} = t\,e^{-t}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 13–14" />

With the homogeneous solution $c\,e^{-t}$ the general solution is $x = (t + c)e^{-t}$.

</Step>

$\dot x_p = e^{-t} - t\,e^{-t}$, so $\dot x_p + x_p = e^{-t}$, as required.

</Step>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

<Formula label="A third-order equation with a polynomial input" tex="\frac{d^3x}{dt^3} - \frac{dx}{dt} = t^2 + 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 15(b), p. 2" />

The box asks for the particular solution of the form of a polynomial in $t$ with no constant term (a constant would be a homogeneous solution, so it can be added freely).

Undetermined coefficients do not work directly with the guess $at^2 + bt + c$: the polynomial $p(s) = s^3 - s$ has $p(0) = 0$. Look at what the equation says about $u = \dot x$ instead.

<Hint>

For $u$ the equation is $\ddot u - u = t^2 + 1$, which has $p(0) \ne 0$. Try $u = at^2 + bt + c$ and match the coefficients of $t^2$, $t$ and $1$. Then integrate $u$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p15-b" label="$x_p(t) =$" expr="-t^3/3 - 3*t"
  vars={{ t: [0.3, 2] }}
  names={{ t: "t" }}

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equation contains $x$ only through its derivatives, so it is a second-order equation for $u = \dot x$.

<Step title="The equation for u">

With $u = \dot x$ the equation reads $\ddot u - u = t^2 + 1$. Try $u = at^2 + bt + c$. Then $\ddot u - u = -at^2 - bt + (2a - c)$, which equals $t^2 + 1$ when $a = -1$, $b = 0$ and $2a - c = 1$, so $c = -3$:

<Formula label="Particular solution for u" tex="u_p = -t^2 - 3" cite="solutions" at="Problem 15(b), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Integrate">

A function $x$ with $\dot x = u_p$ and no constant term is

<Formula label="Particular solution of the third-order equation" tex="x_p = -\frac{t^3}{3} - 3t" cite="solutions" at="Problem 15(b), p. 1" />

The characteristic polynomial factors as $s(s - 1)(s + 1)$, so the homogeneous solution is $c_1 + c_2e^t + c_3e^{-t}$, and the general solution is $x = x_p + c_1 + c_2e^t + c_3e^{-t}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\dot x_p = -t^2 - 3$ and $\dfrac{d^3x_p}{dt^3} = -2$, so $\dfrac{d^3x_p}{dt^3} - \dot x_p = -2 + t^2 + 3 = t^2 + 1$. The degree also fits: $0$ is a root of $p$, so the polynomial input of degree $2$ gives a polynomial solution of degree $3$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

<Formula label="An exponential times a polynomial" tex="2\ddot x + \dot x = (t^2 + 1)\,e^{2t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 15(c), p. 2" />

The box asks for the particular solution of the form $e^{2t}$ times a polynomial of degree two.

<Hint>

The input is $e^{2t}$ times a polynomial, so write $x = e^{2t}u$ and use the exponential shift rule: $p(D)(e^{2t}u) = e^{2t}\,p(D + 2)\,u$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Here $p(D) = 2D^2 + D$. Work out $p(D + 2)$, and solve $p(D + 2)u = t^2 + 1$ with $u = at^2 + bt + c$. Is $p(2)$ zero?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p15-c" label="$x_p(t) =$" expr="exp(2*t)*(t^2/10 - 9*t/50 + 111/500)"
  vars={{ t: [0.1, 0.6] }}
  names={{ t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(2*t)*(t^2 + 1)/10", hint: "You divided by p(2) = 10 only. That works for a pure exponential input, but here the polynomial factor is differentiated too, which adds terms. Substitute your formula: the t-terms of 2x'' + x' do not cancel." },
    { expr: "exp(2*t)*(t^2/10 + 9*t/50 + 111/500)", hint: "Check the sign of the linear term. In 2u'' + 9u' + 10u the t-terms are 10b + 9·2a, and they must cancel, so b = −9/50." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The exponential shift rule turns an equation with the input $e^{2t}\times\text{polynomial}$ into one with a plain polynomial input, which undetermined coefficients solve.

<Step title="Shift the operator">

With $p(D) = 2D^2 + D$ and $x = e^{2t}u$,

<Formula label="The shifted operator" tex="p(D + 2) = 2(D + 2)^2 + (D + 2) = 2D^2 + 9D + 10" cite="reading2" at="pp. 9–10" />

and the equation becomes $2\ddot u + 9\dot u + 10u = t^2 + 1$. Since $p(2) = 10 \neq 0$, a polynomial of degree two works.

</Step>

<Step title="Match coefficients">

For $u = at^2 + bt + c$: $2\ddot u + 9\dot u + 10u = 10a\,t^2 + (18a + 10b)\,t + (4a + 9b + 10c)$. Matching $t^2 + 1$:

<Formula label="Particular solution of the shifted equation" tex="a = \frac{1}{10}, \qquad b = -\frac{9}{50}, \qquad c = \frac{111}{500}, \qquad x_p = e^{2t}\left(\frac{t^2}{10} - \frac{9t}{50} + \frac{111}{500}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 15(c) (our own solution)" />

The homogeneous equation $2\ddot x + \dot x = 0$ has the solutions $c_1 + c_2e^{-t/2}$, so the general solution is $x = x_p + c_1 + c_2e^{-t/2}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Put the values back into the left side of the shifted equation. The $t^2$ coefficient is $10a = 1$. The $t$ coefficient is $18a + 10b = 9/5 - 9/5 = 0$. The constant term is $4a + 9b + 10c = 20/50 - 81/50 + 111/50 = 50/50 = 1$. So $2\ddot u + 9\dot u + 10u = t^2 + 1$, as required.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 16

<Exercise id="p16" title="Frequency response, practical resonance and the Nyquist plot" points={26} mit="Part II, Problem 16">

This problem uses the system of Problem 14 again: $\ddot x + b\,\dot x + k\,x = k\cos\omega t$ with $b = 0.5$ and $k = 4$ for parts (a) to (c). MIT uses the Mathlet's Bode plots and Nyquist plot; you can do everything with the formulas of Problem 14, and any plotting tool will show the curves.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

In Problem 14 you found the gain as a function of $\omega$. At $\omega = 2$ it is $4$, but is that the largest gain? Find the input frequency $\omega_r$ that makes the gain a maximum (the practical resonance frequency): MIT's hint is that it is easier to minimise the square of the denominator. Find also the largest gain. The first box asks for $\omega_r$, the second for the largest gain.

<Hint>

The gain is large where the square of its denominator, $(4 - \omega^2)^2 + \omega^2/4$, is small. Call $u = \omega^2$: it is then a quadratic polynomial in $u$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Find the $u$ where the derivative of that quadratic vanishes, take the square root for $\omega_r$, and put it back into the gain.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p16-a-wr" label="$\omega_r =$" value="1.9685" tolerance="0.002" calc="p16-a-wr" />

<Answer id="p16-a-gmax" label="Largest gain $=$" value="4.0316" tolerance="0.005" calc="p16-a-gmax" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The gain is $4/\sqrt{D}$ with $D(\omega) = (4 - \omega^2)^2 + \omega^2/4$, so it is largest where $D$ is smallest. In the variable $u = \omega^2$ the function $D$ is a simple parabola.

<Step title="Minimise D">

$D = (4 - u)^2 + u/4$ has the derivative $dD/du = -2(4 - u) + \tfrac14$, which is zero at $u = 4 - \tfrac18 = \tfrac{31}{8}$. This is a minimum (the parabola opens upwards) and $u > 0$, so it is allowed:

<Formula label="Practical resonance frequency" tex="\omega_r = \sqrt{\frac{31}{8}} = 1.9685" cite="reading3" at="p. 15" />

It is not $2$, but a little below it. [This is the lecture's $\omega_r = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2p^2}$ with $\omega_0^2 = k = 4$ and $2p = b = \tfrac12$.]

</Step>

<Step title="The largest gain">

At the minimum, $D = \tfrac18^2 + \tfrac{31}{8}\cdot\tfrac14 = \tfrac{1}{64} + \tfrac{31}{32} = \tfrac{63}{64}$, so

<Formula label="Largest gain" tex="g_{\max} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{63/64}} = \frac{32}{\sqrt{63}} = 4.0316" cite="mit" at="Problem 16(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The largest gain, $4.0316$, is just above the value $4$ at $\omega = 2$, as it must be, since $\omega_r$ is the point of the maximum and $2$ is not: with a damping this light, the peak is flat and close to $2$. As $b$ shrinks to $0$ the peak $\omega_r$ moves to $\omega = 2 = \sqrt k$, where the gain grows without limit.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Step title="Solve for ω">

<Part label="c" points={4}>

The Nyquist plot is the curve traced out by the complex gain $H(\omega)$ in the complex plane as $\omega$ runs from $0$ to $\infty$. The length of the line from the origin to $H(\omega)$ is the gain, and the angle below the positive real axis is the phase lag. MIT asks for a sketch of the plot with the point where the phase lag is as close to $\pi/4$ as possible. <Hint>

Where does the curve start, at $\omega = 0$? What are the real and imaginary parts of $H(\omega)$ for large $\omega$, and what is the sign of the imaginary part for all $\omega$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p16-c" label="Gain at the point with phase lag $\pi/4$ $=$" value="3.204" tolerance="0.01" calc="p16-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write $H = 4\,\overline{p(i\omega)}/|p(i\omega)|^2$, so that the real and imaginary parts are visible.

<Step title="The shape of the curve">

With $p(i\omega) = (4 - \omega^2) + \tfrac12 i\omega$,

<Formula label="Real and imaginary parts of the complex gain" tex="H(\omega) = \frac{4\,(4 - \omega^2) - 2i\omega}{(4 - \omega^2)^2 + \tfrac14\omega^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 16(c) (our own solution)" />

It starts at $H(0) = 1$, passes through $H(2) = -4i$ (the lowest point is near there), crosses to the negative real part for $\omega > 2$, and tends to $0$ along the negative real axis as $\omega \to \infty$. </Step>

<Step title="The gain there">

At $\omega = 1.7656$, $p(i\omega) = 0.8828\,(1 + i)$ has size $0.8828\sqrt 2 = 1.2484$ (from the unrounded values), so the gain is $4/1.2484 = 3.204$. This is the length of the line from the origin to the marked point.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The gain is less than the peak $4.0316$ of part (a), as it must be, and more than the value $1$ at $\omega = 0$: the point lies on the way from $1$ to $-4i$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={6}>

<Answer id="p16-d-rel" label="Phase lag $\pi/2$ happens exactly when:"

<Step title="The condition">

<Formula label="Frequency of the 90 degree phase lag" tex="\omega^2 = k" cite="mit" at="Problem 16(d) (our own solution)" />
