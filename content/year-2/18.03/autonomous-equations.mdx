Another day without solving a differential equation (transcript, p. 1): the subject is equations whose right side contains no independent variable.

## Autonomous equations

An **autonomous** equation has the form (transcript, p. 1)

<Formula label="Autonomous equation" tex="\frac{dy}{dt} = f(y)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

It can always be separated, but the integrals may be hard or not worth the effort; the aim is **qualitative** information about the solutions without solving (p. 1). Every horizontal line $y = y_0$ is an isocline, with line elements of slope $f(y_0)$; so sliding an integral curve sideways gives another integral curve: the solutions are **translates** of one another (p. 2).

## Critical points

$y_0$ is a **critical point** when it is a zero of the right side, $f(y_0) = 0$ (transcript, p. 3). Equivalently, the constant function $y = y_0$ is a solution, since both sides of the equation are then zero (pp. 3–4). These horizontal solutions are barriers: [no integral curve can cross them](/courses/18-03/direction-fields) (p. 4).

**The method** (p. 5):

1. Find the critical points by solving $f(y) = 0$.
2. Sketch the graph of $f(y)$ against $y$ and see where it is positive or negative. Where $f > 0$, $dy/dt > 0$ and solutions **increase**; where $f < 0$ they **decrease**. Mark this with arrows on the $y$-axis (p. 7).

**Example: a bank account.** With continuous interest at rate $r$, $dy/dt = ry$, exponential growth. Add an embezzler withdrawing at a constant rate $w$ (pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="Bank account with embezzlement" tex="\frac{dy}{dt} = ry - w" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

The only critical point is $y = w/r$. The graph of $f(y) = ry - w$ is a line of slope $r$: above $w/r$ the money grows, faster and faster; below it, it drains away faster and faster (pp. 6–8).

## The logistic equation

Population growth $dy/dt = ky$ with a constant growth rate $k$ cannot last: as resources run out, the growth rate should fall as $y$ rises. The simplest choice is a linear decrease, $k = a - by$, giving the **logistic equation** (transcript, pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Logistic equation" tex="\frac{dy}{dt} = (a - by)\,y = ay - by^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

It also models the spread of a disease or a rumour (p. 9). Solving it takes partial fractions, but the critical points tell the story: $y(a - by) = 0$ at $y = 0$ and $y = a/b$ (p. 9). The graph of $f$ is a downward parabola through these points, positive between them and negative outside (p. 10). So a population starting between 0 and $a/b$ rises towards $a/b$; one starting above $a/b$ falls towards it; negative solutions, with no physical meaning, fall away from 0 (pp. 10–11).

## Stability

The two constant solutions behave very differently (transcript, pp. 11–12). Solutions near $a/b$ approach it: a **stable** critical point. Solutions near 0 move away from it: an **unstable** critical point. On the $y$-axis picture: arrows pointing towards a critical point mean stable, arrows pointing away mean unstable (p. 12). If $f$ only touches the axis, the arrows on both sides point the same way: the point is approached from one side and left on the other, **semi-stable** (pp. 12–13).

## Harvesting

Now harvest the population at a constant rate $h$ (salmon taken out of a fish farm, so many tonnes a day) (transcript, p. 13):

<Formula label="Logistic growth with harvesting" tex="\frac{dy}{dt} = ay - by^2 - h" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

Raising $h$ lowers the parabola (p. 14). For small $h$ there are still two critical points, moved inwards. At one special rate $h_m$, the height of the parabola's peak, they merge into a single point, semi-stable by the definition above (the page's own description); for larger $h$ there are none, and every solution decreases: the population dies out (pp. 14–16). $h_m$ is the **maximum harvesting rate** (p. 15). (The peak of $ay - by^2$ is at $y = a/(2b)$, which gives $h_m = \frac{a^2}{4b}$; the lecture calls the calculation easy and leaves it.)

<WorkedExample title="A fish farm">
<Problem>
A fish population (in tonnes) grows as $dy/dt = 0.4y - 0.002y^2$ per year. (a) Find its critical points and their stability. (b) Find the maximum harvesting rate. (c) With harvesting at 15 tonnes a year, find the critical points.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Without harvesting">
$y(0.4 - 0.002y) = 0$ at $y = 0$ (unstable) and $y = 0.4/0.002 = 200$ tonnes (stable).
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Maximum harvest">
$h_m = a^2/4b = 0.16/0.008 = 20$ tonnes a year, at a population of 100 tonnes.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Harvesting 15 tonnes a year">
$0.002y^2 - 0.4y + 15 = 0$ gives $y = (0.4 \pm 0.2)/0.004$: $y = 150$ (stable) and $y = 50$ (unstable). A stock above 50 tonnes settles at 150; below 50 it collapses.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Autonomous $dy/dt = f(y)$: solutions are translates in $t$.
- Critical points $f(y_0) = 0$ give constant solutions that others cannot cross; the sign of $f$ decides rise or fall.
- Stable (arrows in), unstable (arrows out), semi-stable (one of each).
- Logistic $ay - by^2$: stable at $a/b$. With harvesting $h$, survival needs $h \le a^2/4b$.
