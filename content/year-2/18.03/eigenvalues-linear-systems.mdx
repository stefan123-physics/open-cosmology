This lecture solves the [egg system](/courses/18-03/first-order-systems) again, by the method normally used in practice; elimination is mostly for people who have forgotten any other (transcript, p. 1). It uses matrices, which are a prerequisite; $2 \times 2$ and $3 \times 3$ are enough (pp. 1–2).

## Matrix form

With $x = T_1$ and $y = T_2$, the egg system and its solution by elimination were (transcript, p. 1)

<Formula label="The egg system again" tex="x' = -2x + 2y, \quad y' = 2x - 5y; \qquad x = c_1e^{-t} + c_2e^{-6t}, \quad y = \frac{c_1}{2}e^{-t} - 2c_2e^{-6t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Differentiate a column vector by differentiating each entry; then the system is one matrix equation (p. 2):

<Formula label="Matrix form" tex="\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}' = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 2 & -5 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The solution is a sum of two column vectors. Pull out of each everything scalar, constants in front and functions behind, the standard order for readability (pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Solution in vector form" tex="\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = c_1\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1/2 \end{pmatrix}e^{-t} + c_2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}e^{-6t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

## The trial solution

Matrices were only used to solve systems of differential equations from about the 1950s (transcript, p. 4). The natural guess, a different exponential in each component, does not work. The solution above shows why: each piece is a **constant vector times one exponential**, the same in both components (p. 4). So try

<Formula label="Trial solution" tex="\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{pmatrix}e^{\lambda t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

Substituting, the derivative brings down $\lambda$, and the exponential cancels: the calculus has been reduced to algebra, as with the Laplace transform (p. 5). Written out (p. 6):

<Formula label="Algebraic equations" tex="\lambda a_1 = -2a_1 + 2a_2, \qquad \lambda a_2 = 2a_1 - 5a_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Two nonlinear equations in three unknowns look hopeless. The one idea needed: demote $\lambda$ to a parameter. Then the equations are **linear and homogeneous** in $a_1$, $a_2$ (p. 6):

<Formula label="Homogeneous linear system" tex="(-2 - \lambda)\,a_1 + 2a_2 = 0, \qquad 2a_1 + (-5 - \lambda)\,a_2 = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

## The characteristic equation

A square homogeneous system always has the trivial solution $a_1 = a_2 = 0$, which here means the egg stays at 0 for ever: no interest (transcript, p. 7). It has a nontrivial solution exactly when the determinant of coefficients is zero (p. 7):

<Formula label="Characteristic equation of the egg" tex="\begin{vmatrix} -2 - \lambda & 2 \\ 2 & -5 - \lambda \end{vmatrix} = (\lambda + 2)(\lambda + 5) - 4 = \lambda^2 + 7\lambda + 6 = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

the same characteristic equation that elimination produced, with $\lambda$ for $r$ (p. 7). Its roots are $\lambda = -1$ and $\lambda = -6$ (p. 8).

**For each root, find $(a_1, a_2)$** (pp. 8–10).

- $\lambda = -1$: the equations become $-a_1 + 2a_2 = 0$ and $2a_1 - 4a_2 = 0$. The second is twice the first, as it must be: if not, the only solution would be zero. This is a built-in check; if the equations are not multiples, recheck the substitution first, then $\lambda$ (pp. 8–9). Taking $a_2 = 1$ gives $(2, 1)$, and the solution $\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}e^{-t}$ (p. 9).
- $\lambda = -6$: $4a_1 + 2a_2 = 0$ and $2a_1 + a_2 = 0$; with $a_1 = 1$, $a_2 = -2$, giving $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}e^{-6t}$ (pp. 9–10).

By superposition, since the two solutions are not multiples of each other (p. 10):

<Formula label="General solution" tex="\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \tilde c_1\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}e^{-t} + \tilde c_2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{pmatrix}e^{-6t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

the same as before, with $\tilde c_1 = c_1/2$ and $\tilde c_2 = c_2$ (p. 10). The two methods meet only at the characteristic equation: its roots are intrinsic to the problem (pp. 10–11).

## The general method

For a general $2 \times 2$ system $\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}' = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$ the same steps give (transcript, pp. 11–12)

<Formula label="Characteristic equation of a 2 × 2 matrix" tex="\begin{vmatrix} a - \lambda & b \\ c & d - \lambda \end{vmatrix} = \lambda^2 - (a + d)\,\lambda + (ad - bc) = \lambda^2 - (\mathrm{tr}\,A)\,\lambda + \det A = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 12" />

where the **trace** $\mathrm{tr}\,A = a + d$ is the sum of the diagonal entries (p. 12). (The transcript says "$a$ plus $b$" here.) Remember this equation and write it down at once, without redoing the derivation, and watch the minus sign (pp. 12–13). Its roots $\lambda_1$, $\lambda_2$, here real and distinct, are the **eigenvalues** of $A$: hidden in the entries, but the numbers that control the system (p. 13). The word mixes German and English; some prefer "characteristic values", and older books say "proper values", proper in the sense of belonging to the matrix (pp. 13–14). For each $\lambda_i$, the nonzero solutions $\vec\alpha_i$ of $(A - \lambda_iI)\vec\alpha = 0$ are the **eigenvectors** belonging to it (p. 14), and (p. 15)

<Formula label="General solution, distinct eigenvalues" tex="\vec x = c_1\,\vec\alpha_1\,e^{\lambda_1t} + c_2\,\vec\alpha_2\,e^{\lambda_2t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 15" />

**Condensed form**, valid for $n \times n$ systems too (pp. 15–16). The system is $\vec x\,' = A\vec x$; the trial solution $\vec x = \vec\alpha\,e^{\lambda t}$ gives $A\vec\alpha = \lambda\vec\alpha$. Subtracting a scalar from a matrix is not allowed; writing it out shows that $\lambda$ comes off the main diagonal, which is what $\lambda I$ does (p. 16):

<Formula label="Eigenvalue equation" tex="(A - \lambda I)\,\vec\alpha = 0, \qquad \det(A - \lambda I) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 16" />

<WorkedExample title="A system that grows">
<Problem>
Solve $x' = x + 2y$, $y' = 2x + y$, first in general, then with $x(0) = 3$, $y(0) = 1$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Eigenvalues">
$\mathrm{tr}\,A = 2$ and $\det A = 1 - 4 = -3$, so $\lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 3 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda + 1) = 0$: $\lambda = 3$ and $\lambda = -1$.
</Step>
<Step title="Eigenvectors">
$\lambda = 3$: $-2a_1 + 2a_2 = 0$ (and $2a_1 - 2a_2 = 0$), so $(1, 1)$. $\lambda = -1$: $2a_1 + 2a_2 = 0$, so $(1, -1)$. The general solution is $c_1\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}e^{3t} + c_2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}e^{-t}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Initial values">
$c_1 + c_2 = 3$ and $c_1 - c_2 = 1$, so $c_1 = 2$, $c_2 = 1$: $x = 2e^{3t} + e^{-t}$, $y = 2e^{3t} - e^{-t}$. The positive eigenvalue makes the solution grow.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Write the system as $\vec x\,' = A\vec x$ and try $\vec x = \vec\alpha\,e^{\lambda t}$, one exponential for all components.
- This gives $(A - \lambda I)\vec\alpha = 0$: nonzero solutions need $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$, for $2 \times 2$ matrices $\lambda^2 - (\mathrm{tr}\,A)\lambda + \det A = 0$.
- The roots are the eigenvalues; for each, an eigenvector (the equations must be redundant, a check).
- With distinct real eigenvalues, $\vec x = c_1\vec\alpha_1e^{\lambda_1t} + c_2\vec\alpha_2e^{\lambda_2t}$.
