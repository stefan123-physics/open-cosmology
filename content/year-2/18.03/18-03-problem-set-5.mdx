## Problem 17

<Exercise id="p17" title="A series RLC circuit: amplitude and phase" points={28} mit="Part II, Problem 17">

A resistor (resistance $R$, in ohms), a coil (inductance $L$, in henries) and a capacitor (capacitance $C$, in farads) are connected in series to a source with the voltage $V(t)$, in volts. The current $I(t)$, in amperes, is the same at every point of the circuit and obeys

<Formula label="Current in a series RLC circuit" tex="L\ddot I + R\dot I + \frac{1}{C}\,I = \dot V" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 17, p. 2" />

(the lesson on [inhomogeneous equations](/courses/18-03/inhomogeneous-equations-and-stability) has the same equation, with other letters for the current and the voltage). The input is the sinusoidal voltage $V(t) = V_0\sin(\omega t)$, and the response is the current. After the transient has died out the current is a sinusoid of the same angular frequency. Write it as $I(t) = A\sin(\omega t - \phi)$, with the amplitude $A > 0$ and the *phase lag* $\phi$ behind the voltage. The current is *in phase* with the voltage when $\phi = 0$: both curves then cross zero and reach their peaks at the same moments.

MIT's Mathlet "Series RLC Circuit" shows the voltage and the current as curves and has a slider for each of $R$, $L$, $C$, $V_0$ and $\omega$. It shows $L$ in millihenries (1 mH is $10^{-3}$ H) and $C$ in microfarads (1 μF is $10^{-6}$ F); the equation above needs henries and farads.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Experiment: find some values of the parameters for which the current is exactly in phase with the voltage. In particular, take $L = 500$ mH and $\omega = 200$ radians per second. Which values of $R$, $C$ and $V_0$ put $I$ in phase with $V$?

Our graph for this experiment has $L = 500$ mH and $\omega = 200$ rad/s fixed. So that both curves fit on one scale, it shows $V/V_0$ (dashed) and $RI/V_0$. Move the sliders for $C$ and $R$ and watch the zero crossings of the two curves.

<Answer id="p17-a" label="With $L = 500$ mH and $\omega = 200$ rad/s, the current is in phase with the voltage when"
  choices={["C is about 50 μF, whatever R and V₀ are", "R is about 100 Ω, whatever C and V₀ are", "C is about 20 μF and R is about 100 Ω together", "V₀ is large enough, whatever R and C are"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Look for the slider that moves the current sideways until its zero crossings sit on those of the voltage.

<Step title="What the sliders do">

The slider for $C$ shifts the current curve sideways: for small $C$ the current runs ahead of the voltage, for large $C$ it lags behind, and near $C = 50$ μF the two curves cross zero together. Changing $R$ at $C = 50$ μF leaves the curves in phase. (Away from $50$ μF, $R$ changes the size of the phase difference, but it never removes it.) $V_0$ multiplies the voltage and the current by the same factor, so it cannot shift either curve; that is why our graph can leave it out.

</Step>

<Step title="The observation">

With $L = 500$ mH and $\omega = 200$ rad/s the current is in phase when $C$ is close to $50$ μF, and the values of $R$ and $V_0$ do not seem to matter. This is what MIT's solution reports from the Mathlet. Other in-phase settings can be found in the same way: for each pair $L$, $\omega$ there is one value of $C$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

An experiment gives "about 50 μF", not a proof. Part (b) computes the condition and gives exactly $50$ μF for these values.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Now calculate: which relation between the parameters makes $I$ in phase with $V$? Compare it with your experiment.

<Hint>

With $V = V_0\sin\omega t$ the right side of the equation is $\dot V = V_0\,\omega\cos\omega t$, the real part of $V_0\,\omega\,e^{i\omega t}$. Replace the equation by a complex one and use the [exponential input theorem](/courses/18-03/exponential-input-theorem).

</Hint>

<Hint>

The complex solution is $z_p = V_0\,\omega\,e^{i\omega t}/p(i\omega)$ with the characteristic polynomial $p(s) = Ls^2 + Rs + 1/C$. Its real part must be a positive multiple of $\sin\omega t$. Since $\operatorname{Re}(-i\,e^{i\omega t}) = \sin\omega t$, what kind of number must $p(i\omega)$ be?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p17-b-cap" label="In phase when $C =$" expr="1/(L*omega^2)"
  vars={{ L: [0.1, 2], omega: [50, 500] }}
  names={{ omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "L*omega^2", hint: "That is 1/C, not C: the condition says that 1/C equals Lω²." },
    { expr: "1/(L*omega)", hint: "Check the units: Lω is measured in ohms, so 1/(Lω) is not a capacitance. Look again at the real part of p(iω)." },
    { expr: "1/(L^2*omega^2)", hint: "This would make 1/(Cω) equal to L²ω, and those two do not have the same units. The term Ls² of p(s) has one factor L." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p17-b-num" label="Our own check with other numbers: for $L = 200$ mH and $\omega = 500$ rad/s, the current is in phase when $C$, in μF, is" value="20" tolerance="0.2" calc="p17-b-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A sinusoidal input is the real part of a complex exponential, and the exponential input theorem gives the response to that at once. The phase of the response is the phase of one complex number.

<Step title="The complex equation">

The right side is $\dot V = V_0\,\omega\cos\omega t = \operatorname{Re}\bigl(V_0\,\omega\,e^{i\omega t}\bigr)$. So solve the complex equation instead; the current is the real part of its solution $z_p$:

<Formula label="Complex replacement" tex="L\ddot z + R\dot z + \frac{1}{C}\,z = V_0\,\omega\,e^{i\omega t}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 17(b), p. 1" />

(MIT's solution prints $I\dot z$ for the middle term, a misprint for $R\dot z$, and leaves the factor $V_0$ out of the next formula.)

</Step>

<Step title="The exponential solution">

With $p(s) = Ls^2 + Rs + 1/C$, the exponential input theorem gives

<Formula label="Exponential response of the circuit" tex="z_p = \frac{V_0\,\omega}{p(i\omega)}\,e^{i\omega t}, \qquad p(i\omega) = \Bigl(\frac{1}{C} - L\omega^2\Bigr) + i\,R\,\omega" cite="solutions" at="Problem 17(b), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="When is the real part a positive multiple of sin ωt?">

The real part of $e^{i\omega t}$ divided by a positive real number is a cosine. Divided by a positive multiple of $i$ it is a positive multiple of $\sin\omega t$, because $e^{i\omega t}/i = -i\,e^{i\omega t} = \sin\omega t - i\cos\omega t$. For any other complex number in the denominator the real part is a sinusoid shifted away from $\sin\omega t$. So the current is in phase exactly when $p(i\omega)$ is a positive multiple of $i$. Its imaginary part $R\omega$ is positive already, so the condition is that its real part is zero:

<Formula label="Condition for current in phase with voltage" tex="\frac{1}{C} = L\,\omega^2, \qquad C = \frac{1}{L\,\omega^2}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 17(b), p. 1" />

Neither $R$ nor $V_0$ appears in it, as the experiment suggested.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $L = 500$ mH $= 0.5$ H and $\omega = 200$ rad/s: $C = 1/(0.5 \times 200^2) = 1/20000 = 50 \times 10^{-6}$ F $= 50$ μF, the value found in part (a). For the box: $L = 0.2$ H and $\omega = 500$ rad/s give $C = 1/(0.2 \times 500^2) = 2 \times 10^{-5}$ F $= 20$ μF. Units: $L\omega$ and $1/(C\omega)$ are both in ohms, so $1/C = L\omega^2$ compares like with like.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

Confirm the three observations of part (c) by calculation, for general $R$, $L$, $C$ and $V_0$: find the amplitude of $I(t)$ as a function of $\omega$, the frequency $\omega_r$ at which it is largest, the largest amplitude $I_r$, and the phase lag at $\omega_r$.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p17-d-ir" label="$I_r =$" expr="V0/R"
  vars={{ V0: [100, 500], R: [50, 200], L: [0.5, 2], C: [0.00005, 0.0002] }}
  names={{ V0: "V_0" }}

<Solution>

**The idea.** The amplitude is the modulus of the complex coefficient of $e^{i\omega t}$. It is largest where the modulus of its reciprocal is smallest, and that reciprocal moves on a straight line in the complex plane.

<Step title="The amplitude">

From part (b), the steady current is the real part of $z_p = \dfrac{V_0\,\omega}{p(i\omega)}\,e^{i\omega t}$, a sinusoid with the amplitude $V_0\,\bigl|\omega/p(i\omega)\bigr|$. Divide $p(i\omega)$ by $\omega$:

<Formula label="The reciprocal of the gain" tex="\frac{p(i\omega)}{\omega} = \Bigl(\frac{1}{C\omega} - L\omega\Bigr) + R\,i" cite="solutions" at="Problem 17(d), p. 1" />

Its modulus follows from Pythagoras, so

<Formula label="Amplitude of the current" tex="A(\omega) = \frac{V_0}{\sqrt{R^2 + \bigl(L\omega - \frac{1}{C\omega}\bigr)^2}}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 17(d), p. 1" />

(MIT's solution leaves this as $V_0\,|\omega/p(i\omega)|$; the square root is the same thing written out.)

</Step>

<Step title="Where the amplitude is largest">

The number $p(i\omega)/\omega$ has the fixed imaginary part $R$. As $\omega$ changes it moves along the horizontal line at height $R$ in the complex plane. The point of that line closest to the origin is $Ri$, straight above the origin, and it is reached when the real part is zero: $\dfrac{1}{C\omega} = L\omega$. (MIT's solution prints this condition as "$C/\omega = L\omega$", a misprint; its next formula is the correct one.)

<Formula label="Resonant frequency of the current" tex="\omega_r = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 17(d), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The largest amplitude and the phase lag">

At $\omega_r$ the modulus of $p(i\omega)/\omega$ is $R$, so

<Formula label="Largest amplitude of the current" tex="I_r = \frac{V_0}{R}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 17(d), p. 1" />

It depends on $V_0$ and $R$ only, not on $L$ or $C$. The condition $1/(C\omega) = L\omega$ is the in-phase condition $1/C = L\omega^2$ of part (b), so the phase lag at $\omega_r$ is zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $R = 100$ Ω, $L = 1$ H and $C = 10^{-4}$ F: $\omega_r = 1/\sqrt{10^{-4}} = 100$ rad/s, and with $V_0 = 500$ V, $I_r = 500/100 = 5$ A, as observed in part (c). A little to either side the formula for $A(\omega)$ gives less: $4.89$ A at $\omega = 90$ rad/s and $4.91$ A at $\omega = 110$ rad/s. Units: volts divided by ohms is amperes, and $LC$ is in seconds squared.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 18

<Exercise id="p18" title="The damping ratio" points={12} mit="Part II, Problem 18">

A mass $m$ on a spring with constant $k$, damped by a force $-b\dot x$, obeys the homogeneous equation $m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = 0$. Its *natural frequency* is $\omega_n = \sqrt{k/m}$, and it is critically damped when $b/m = 2\omega_n$. Write $b/m$ as a multiple of that critical value,

<Formula label="Definition of the damping ratio" tex="\frac{b}{m} = 2\,\omega_n\,\zeta" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 18, p. 2" />

The number $\zeta$ is the *damping ratio*: the system is overdamped if $\zeta > 1$ and underdamped if $\zeta < 1$. (In the notation of the lesson on [damped oscillations](/courses/18-03/damped-oscillations), $\ddot x + 2p\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0$, this means $\omega_0 = \omega_n$ and $p = \zeta\omega_n$.)

Suppose the system is underdamped, and a solution is seen to lose half its amplitude in $n$ cycles: the exponential factor of the solution falls to half its starting value in the time of $n$ full oscillations. Find $\zeta$ in terms of $n$ and the constant

<Formula label="The constant α" tex="\alpha = \frac{\ln 2}{2\pi} \simeq 0.1103178" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 18, p. 2" />

Then find $\zeta$ for $n = 10$ and compare the exact value with the engineer's approximation $\alpha/n$.

<Hint>

Write the underdamped solution as an exponential factor times a sinusoid. Which combination of $\zeta$ and $\omega_n$ is the decay rate, and what is the angular frequency $\omega_d$ of the sinusoid? The lesson on damped oscillations calls it the pseudo-frequency $\omega_1$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

One cycle takes the time $2\pi/\omega_d$. Put $n$ of these times into the exponential factor and set the result equal to $\frac{1}{2}$. The frequency $\omega_n$ cancels.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You get an equation of the form $\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} = $ a known number. Square it and collect the terms with $\zeta^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p18-zeta" label="$\zeta =$ (type alpha for $\alpha$)" expr="(alpha/n)/sqrt(1 + (alpha/n)^2)"
  vars={{ alpha: [0.1103178, 0.1103178001], n: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "alpha/n", hint: "That is the engineer's approximation, good when ζ is small. The exact relation also contains the factor √(1 − ζ²), from the frequency of the damped oscillation." },
    { expr: "(alpha/n)/sqrt(1 - (alpha/n)^2)", hint: "This is larger than α/n. But ζ/√(1 − ζ²) = α/n makes ζ smaller than α/n. Check the sign when you collect the ζ² terms." },
    { expr: "alpha*n/sqrt(1 + (alpha*n)^2)", hint: "This grows with n. A system that needs more cycles to lose half its amplitude is damped less, not more." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p18-num" label="For $n = 10$: $\zeta =$ (4 significant figures)" value="0.01103" tolerance="0.00001" calc="p18-num" />

<Answer id="p18-ratio" label="For $n = 10$: the exact $\zeta$ divided by the approximation $\alpha/n$ (6 decimal places)" value="0.999939" tolerance="0.000002" calc="p18-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The exponential factor decays at the rate $\zeta\omega_n$, and one cycle lasts $2\pi/\omega_d$. Their product, the decay per cycle, depends on $\zeta$ alone.

<Step title="The damped frequency">

Dividing the equation by $m$ gives $\ddot x + 2\zeta\omega_n\dot x + \omega_n^2x = 0$, with the characteristic roots $-\zeta\omega_n \pm i\,\omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}$ when $\zeta < 1$. In terms of the original constants $\zeta^2 = b^2/(4mk)$, so the imaginary part is the familiar $\sqrt{k/m - b^2/(4m^2)}$:

<Formula label="Damped frequency and the damping ratio" tex="\omega_d = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m} - \frac{b^2}{4m^2}} = \omega_n\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 18, p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The underdamped solution">

<Formula label="Underdamped solution with the damping ratio" tex="x = A\,e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\cos(\omega_d t - \phi)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 18, p. 1" />

Its derivative is $e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}$ times another sinusoid of angular frequency $\omega_d$, so it vanishes at times spaced $\pi/\omega_d$ apart, and every second one of these is a maximum. The peaks are spaced $2\pi/\omega_d$ apart: that is one cycle.

</Step>

<Step title="The decay over n cycles">

In one cycle the exponential factor is multiplied by $e^{-\zeta\omega_n(2\pi/\omega_d)} = e^{-2\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}}$, and in $n$ cycles by the $n$th power of that. Setting it equal to one half and taking logarithms:

<Formula label="Half the amplitude after n cycles" tex="\frac{1}{2} = e^{-2\pi n\zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2}} = \frac{\ln 2}{2\pi n} = \frac{\alpha}{n}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 18, p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for the damping ratio">

Squaring gives $\zeta^2 = (\alpha/n)^2(1 - \zeta^2)$, so $\zeta^2\bigl(1 + (\alpha/n)^2\bigr) = (\alpha/n)^2$, and with $\zeta > 0$

<Formula label="Damping ratio from the number of cycles" tex="\zeta = \frac{\alpha/n}{\sqrt{1 + (\alpha/n)^2}}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 18, p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Ten cycles">

For $n = 10$, $\alpha/n = 0.01103178$ and $1/\sqrt{1 + (\alpha/n)^2} = 0.99993916$, so $\zeta = 0.0110311$. The exact value is smaller than the approximation $\alpha/n$ by only about 6 parts in 100 000: for light damping $\zeta \approx \alpha/n$ is an excellent rule.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Put $\zeta = 0.0110311$ back: $2\pi \times 10 \times \zeta/\sqrt{1 - \zeta^2} = 0.6931 = \ln 2$, so the factor is $e^{-\ln 2} = \frac{1}{2}$. A limit: if the amplitude halves in very few cycles ($n \to 0$) the formula gives $\zeta \to 1$, critical damping, and never more than 1, as it must for a system that still oscillates.

</Step>

<Formula label="Fourier series of the shifted square wave" tex="\operatorname{sq}\Bigl(t - \frac{\pi}{4}\Bigr) = \frac{2\sqrt 2}{\pi}\left(-\cos t - \frac{\cos 3t}{3} + \frac{\cos 5t}{5} + \frac{\cos 7t}{7} - \cdots\right) + \frac{2\sqrt 2}{\pi}\left(\sin t - \frac{\sin 3t}{3} - \frac{\sin 5t}{5} + \frac{\sin 7t}{7} + \cdots\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 21(c), p. 2" />

The signs of the cosine terms go $-, -, +, +$ and then repeat; those of the sine terms go $+, -, -, +$. So the coefficient of $\cos t$ is $-0.900$, that of $\sin 3t$ is $-\frac{0.9003}{3} = -0.300$ and that of $\cos 5t$ is $\frac{0.9003}{5} = 0.180$.

</Step>

<Step title="The graph and the period">

The graph is the square wave moved to the right by $\frac{\pi}{4}$: it is $+1$ for $\frac{\pi}{4} < t < \frac{5\pi}{4}$ and $-1$ for the next stretch of length $\pi$. Shifting does not change the period: the smallest period is $2\pi$. The function is now neither even nor odd, which is why both sines and cosines appear.

    { expr: "0.9003*(-cos(t) - cos(3*t)/3 + cos(5*t)/5 + cos(7*t)/7 + sin(t) - sin(3*t)/3 - sin(5*t)/5 + sin(7*t)/7)", label: "8 terms" }
  ]}
  caption="The square wave shifted to the right by π/4 (dashed) and the sum of the eight terms of its series up to n = 7, computed from the formula above. Our own graph." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = \frac{\pi}{4}$ the shifted wave jumps, so the series must give the midpoint $0$ there, and term by term it does: each $\sin(nt - n\pi/4)$ is $\sin 0$. A shift cannot change the size of a term: for each $n$ the two coefficients are $\pm\frac{2\sqrt 2}{\pi n}$, and $\sqrt{2}\cdot\frac{2\sqrt 2}{\pi n} = \frac{4}{\pi n}$, the coefficient of the unshifted wave.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

The function $g(t)$ of period $2\pi$ with $g(t) = t$ for $-\frac{\pi}{2} \le t \le \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $g(t) = \pi - t$ for $\frac{\pi}{2} \le t \le \frac{3\pi}{2}$. MIT's hint: what is $g'(t)$ in terms of $f(t)$?

<Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** $g$ is a triangle wave whose slope is a square wave. Integrating a Fourier series term by term gives the series of the integral.

<Step title="The derivative">

$g' = 1$ for $-\frac{\pi}{2} < t < \frac{\pi}{2}$ and $g' = -1$ for $\frac{\pi}{2} < t < \frac{3\pi}{2}$. That is $f$ scaled from $\pm\frac{\pi}{4}$ to $\pm 1$:

<Formula label="The slope of the triangle wave" tex="g'(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\,f(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\left(\cos t - \frac{1}{3}\cos 3t + \frac{1}{5}\cos 5t - \cdots\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 21(f), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Integrate term by term">

An antiderivative of $\cos nt$ is $\sin(nt)/n$, which brings a second factor $\frac{1}{n}$. $g$ is odd, so its series has sine terms only and the constant of integration is zero:

<Formula label="Fourier series of the triangle wave" tex="g(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\left(\sin t - \frac{1}{3^2}\sin 3t + \frac{1}{5^2}\sin 5t - \cdots\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 21(f), p. 2" />

The coefficient of $\sin t$ is $\frac{4}{\pi} = 1.273$ and that of $\sin 3t$ is $-\frac{4}{9\pi} = -0.141$.

</Step>

<Step title="The graph and the period">

The graph is a zigzag: it rises with slope 1 from $-\frac{\pi}{2}$ at $t = -\frac{\pi}{2}$ to $\frac{\pi}{2}$ at $t = \frac{\pi}{2}$, then falls with slope $-1$ back to $-\frac{\pi}{2}$ at $t = \frac{3\pi}{2}$. The smallest period is $2\pi$. The coefficients fall off like $1/n^2$, faster than those of the square wave, and two terms already follow the zigzag closely:

</Step>
