One of the harder lectures of the term: the convolution, a peculiar way of combining two functions $f$ and $g$ into a third, written $f * g$ (transcript, p. 1). It has a formal motivation from the [Laplace transform](/courses/18-03/laplace-transform-of-derivatives) and a physical one.

## Why a convolution

Transforms are a pain to compute, so one wants to build new ones from old (transcript, p. 1). Sums are easy. Is there a formula for the transform of a product $f(t)g(t)$? No simple one, and Mattuck says there never will be. But the other question has an answer: the product $F(s)G(s)$ of the transforms is the transform of something built from $f$ and $g$, the convolution (p. 1).

The power-series analogy explains why to expect this (p. 1). Multiplying the coefficients of $\sum a_nx^n$ and $\sum b_nx^n$ term by term gives nothing related to the sums. But multiplying the two series gives a new series $\sum c_nx^n$ whose coefficients can be computed from the two sets of coefficients, and that formula is a discrete convolution. (Collecting the terms in $x^n$, each $c_n$ is the sum of all products of an $a$ and a $b$ whose indices add up to $n$; the lecture leaves this to the listener.)

## The definition

<Formula label="Convolution" tex="(f * g)(t) = \int_0^{t}f(u)\,g(t - u)\,du" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

$u$ is a dummy variable, integrated out (p. 1). (The transcript says "$dt$" here; the lecture corrects the integration variable to $du$ a few lines later, p. 1.) The operation is **commutative**, $f * g = g * f$, although the formula treats $f$ and $g$ differently: the transform of either side is $FG = GF$ (p. 1).

**Example** (pp. 1–2). $t^2 * t = \int_0^t u^2(t - u)\,du = \frac{t^4}{3} - \frac{t^4}{4} = \frac{t^4}{12}$. By transforms: $\frac{2!}{s^3}\cdot\frac{1}{s^2} = \frac{2}{s^5}$, and since $\frac{4!}{s^5}$ is the transform of $t^4$, this is $\frac{1}{12}t^4$ (p. 2). Not $t^3$, and with an unpredictable constant: convolution is not ordinary multiplication (p. 2).

## The convolution formula

<Formula label="Convolution formula" tex="\mathcal{L}(f * g) = F(s)\,G(s)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 1–3" />

**Proof** (pp. 2–3), a marvellous exercise in changing variables in a double integral. Write the product of the two transforms with separate dummy variables and read the easy rule for a product of integrals backwards (p. 2):

<Formula label="Product as a double integral" tex="F(s)\,G(s) = \int_0^{\infty}e^{-su}f(u)\,du\int_0^{\infty}e^{-sv}g(v)\,dv = \int_0^{\infty}\int_0^{\infty}e^{-s(u + v)}f(u)\,g(v)\,du\,dv" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Introduce $t = u + v$ and keep $u$, so $v = t - u$ (p. 2). The Jacobian of $(u, v)$ with respect to $(u, t)$ is the determinant with rows $1, 0$ and $-1, 1$, which is 1 (p. 3). The new limits are the harder part: integrating first in $u$ with $t$ fixed moves along a line $u + v = t$, which enters the quadrant at $u = 0$ and leaves at $v = 0$, that is $u = t$; then $t$ runs from 0 to $\infty$ (p. 3). Since $e^{-st}$ does not involve $u$, it moves outside (p. 3):

<Formula label="Result of the change of variables" tex="F(s)\,G(s) = \int_0^{\infty}e^{-st}\left(\int_0^{t}f(u)\,g(t - u)\,du\right)dt = \mathcal{L}(f * g)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

## Convolution for its own sake

Most people who use convolution do not use it in connection with the Laplace transform (transcript, p. 3). The lecture mentions acid rain leaching from soil into a river, and climate deduced from ice cores (p. 3). An all-purpose model is **radioactive dumping** (pp. 3–4). A plant dumps radioactive waste at rate $f(t)$, so the amount dumped between $u_i$ and $u_i + \Delta u$ is about $f(u_i)\Delta u$. By time $t$ it has decayed for $t - u_i$, leaving $f(u_i)\,e^{-k(t - u_i)}\Delta u$. Adding up and letting $\Delta u \to 0$ turns the Riemann sum into an integral (p. 4):

<Formula label="Amount in the pile" tex="\int_0^{t}f(u)\,e^{-k(t - u)}\,du = f(t) * e^{-kt}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

the convolution of the dumping function, which knows only how much was added, with the decay function, which knows only the material (p. 4). Waste that does not decay gives $f * 1 = \int_0^tf(u)\,du$, the familiar total (p. 4). And to interpret a convolution with $t$, think of something that grows linearly, such as newly hatched chicks early in life: $f * t$ is the total mass of chickens on a farm where chicks appear at rate $f$ (pp. 4–5).

<WorkedExample title="Three convolutions">
<Problem>
(a) Compute $e^t * e^{2t}$ from the definition and check it by transforms. (b) Waste is dumped at 3 kg a year and decays with $k = 0.1$ per year. How much is left after 10 years? (c) Find the function whose transform is $\frac{1}{s^2(s + 1)}$ as a convolution.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Two exponentials">
$\int_0^te^u\,e^{2(t - u)}\,du = e^{2t}\int_0^te^{-u}\,du = e^{2t}(1 - e^{-t}) = e^{2t} - e^t$. Check: $\frac{1}{s - 1}\cdot\frac{1}{s - 2} = \frac{1}{s - 2} - \frac{1}{s - 1}$, the transform of $e^{2t} - e^t$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A decaying pile">
At $t = 10$: $(3 * e^{-0.1t})(10) = \int_0^{10}3e^{-0.1(10 - u)}\,du = 30(1 - e^{-1}) \approx 19.0$ kg, of the 30 kg dumped.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) An inverse by convolution">
$\frac{1}{s^2}\cdot\frac{1}{s + 1}$ is the transform of $t * e^{-t} = \int_0^tu\,e^{-(t - u)}\,du = t - 1 + e^{-t}$. Check: $\frac{1}{s^2} - \frac1s + \frac{1}{s + 1} = \frac{1}{s^2(s + 1)}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $(f * g)(t) = \int_0^tf(u)g(t - u)\,du$, and $\mathcal{L}(f * g) = F(s)G(s)$; convolution is commutative.
- $t^2 * t = \frac{t^4}{12}$; $f * 1 = \int_0^tf\,du$.
- Proof: change variables to $t = u + v$ in a double integral (Jacobian 1, limits $0 \le u \le t$).
- Dumping at rate $f$ with decay $e^{-kt}$ leaves $f * e^{-kt}$ in the pile.
