This lecture produces a **formula** for the solutions of $\vec x\,' = A\vec x$, one that needs no eigenvalues or eigenvectors (transcript, p. 1). It builds on the [fundamental matrix](/courses/18-03/variation-of-parameters-systems).

## All fundamental matrices

A fundamental matrix $X$ has independent solutions as columns, so $\det X \ne 0$ for every $t$ and $X' = AX$ (transcript, p. 1). It is not unique: there is no "the" fundamental matrix, only "a" fundamental matrix (p. 1). With it the general solution $c_1\vec x_1 + c_2\vec x_2$ can be written as compactly as possible (p. 1):

<Formula label="General solution" tex="\vec x = X\vec c, \qquad \vec c = \begin{pmatrix} c_1 \\ c_2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Each column of another fundamental matrix is such a solution, $X\vec c_1$ and $X\vec c_2$, so by matrix multiplication (p. 1)

<Formula label="Every fundamental matrix" tex="X\,C, \qquad C \text{ a constant matrix}, \quad \det C \ne 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

## A formula, not a method

The eigenvalue method is an algorithm: fine for problems with numbers, but a paper with only letters wants to say "the solution is given by the formula …" (transcript, p. 2). Look at the one-dimensional case, $x' = ax$, solved by $e^{at}$ (p. 2). Use the **series** definition of the exponential, not the inverse of the logarithm or repeated multiplication (p. 2):

<Formula label="Exponential series" tex="e^{at} = 1 + at + \frac{a^2t^2}{2!} + \frac{a^3t^3}{3!} + \cdots, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}e^{at} = a + a^2t + \frac{a^3t^2}{2!} + \cdots = a\,e^{at}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Differentiating term by term shows at once that it solves the equation, and this argument generalizes (p. 2). For a matrix, the naive definitions of $e^A$ make no sense, but the series does, with the identity matrix in place of 1 (p. 2):

<Formula label="Matrix exponential" tex="e^{At} = I + At + \frac{A^2t^2}{2!} + \frac{A^3t^3}{3!} + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Each term is a $2 \times 2$ matrix, so the sum is one (p. 2). The same term-by-term differentiation shows that $e^{At}$ satisfies $X' = AX$, and at $t = 0$ it is $I$, with determinant 1 (p. 3). Since the Wronskian of two solutions is either never zero or always zero, nonzero at one point is enough (p. 3; see the previous lecture). So **$e^{At}$ is a fundamental matrix**: no eigenvalues, no eigenvectors, a formula (p. 3). The catch: the series can rarely be summed by hand. Software such as Matlab, Maple or Mathematica computes it as readily as an ordinary exponential; what matters here is its theoretical use (p. 3).

## An example

Take $x' = y$, $y' = x$, so $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ (transcript, p. 3). The powers are easy: $A^2 = I$, $A^3 = A^2A = A$, $A^4 = A^3A = A^2 = I$, alternating (p. 3). Collecting each entry of the series (pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Exponential of the example" tex="e^{At} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + \frac{t^2}{2!} + \frac{t^4}{4!} + \cdots & t + \frac{t^3}{3!} + \cdots \\ t + \frac{t^3}{3!} + \cdots & 1 + \frac{t^2}{2!} + \cdots \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cosh t & \sinh t \\ \sinh t & \cosh t \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–4" />

the series of cosine and sine without the alternating signs: the hyperbolic functions, $\cosh t = \frac{e^t + e^{-t}}{2}$ and $\sinh t = \frac{e^t - e^{-t}}{2}$ (pp. 3–4).

## The initial value problem

What people love $e^{At}$ for (transcript, p. 4): the solution with $\vec x(0) = \vec x_0$ is $e^{At}\vec c$ with $e^{A\cdot0}\vec c = I\vec c = \vec x_0$, so (p. 4)

<Formula label="Solution of the initial value problem" tex="\vec x = e^{At}\,\vec x_0" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

just as for one equation, except that the constant vector must stand **after** the matrix: a column vector cannot multiply a square matrix from the left, and software will refuse it (p. 4).

## The exponential law

Does $e^{A + B} = e^Ae^B$? Both sides make sense, but in general they are not equal (transcript, p. 4). Comparing the series term by term works only if $AB + BA$ can be replaced by $2AB$: essentially, only when $A$ and $B$ **commute** (p. 4). That is almost never, but three cases matter (p. 4): $A = cI$, which commutes with everything; $B = -A$; and $B = A^{-1}$. The second gives the main survivor of the law (p. 4):

<Formula label="Inverse of the exponential" tex="e^{A}\,e^{-A} = e^{A - A} = e^{0} = I, \qquad (e^{A})^{-1} = e^{-A}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

## Computing e^(At)

Series rarely work (transcript, pp. 4–5). For a matrix with equal diagonal entries and equal off-diagonal entries, split it as $aI + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & b \\ b & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, two commuting matrices, and use the exponential law (p. 5). For everything else, take **any** fundamental matrix $X$, for instance from eigenvalues and eigenvectors (p. 5):

<Formula label="e^(At) from any fundamental matrix" tex="e^{At} = X(t)\,X(0)^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

The right side is $X$ times a constant matrix of nonzero determinant, so it is a fundamental matrix, and at $t = 0$ it is $I$. The exponential has the same two properties, so they are equal (p. 5). For the lecture's example, the fundamental matrix with columns $(e^t, e^t)$ and $(e^{-t}, -e^{-t})$, used on the previous page, gives exactly the cosh–sinh matrix above.

<WorkedExample title="Exponentials of two matrices">
<Problem>
(a) Find $e^{At}$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ by splitting. (b) Find $e^{At}$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 0 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$ from a fundamental matrix, and solve $\vec x\,' = A\vec x$ with $\vec x(0) = (2, 1)$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Splitting">
$A = I + 2B$ with $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and $I$ commutes with $B$. So $e^{At} = e^{It}e^{2Bt} = e^t\begin{pmatrix} \cosh 2t & \sinh 2t \\ \sinh 2t & \cosh 2t \end{pmatrix}$, using the lecture's example with $2t$ for $t$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A fundamental matrix">
The eigenvalues are $-3$, with eigenvector $(1, -1)$, and $-1$, with $(1, 0)$. So $X = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-3t} & e^{-t} \\ -e^{-3t} & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $X(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ with determinant 1 and inverse $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Then $e^{At} = X(t)X(0)^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-t} & e^{-t} - e^{-3t} \\ 0 & e^{-3t} \end{pmatrix}$, which is $I$ at 0.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The initial value problem">
$\vec x = e^{At}\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3e^{-t} - e^{-3t} \\ e^{-3t} \end{pmatrix}$. Check: $y' = -3y$ and $x' = -3e^{-t} + 3e^{-3t} = -x + 2y$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- General solution $X\vec c$; all fundamental matrices are $XC$ with $\det C \ne 0$.
- $e^{At} = I + At + \frac{A^2t^2}{2!} + \cdots$ is a fundamental matrix with value $I$ at 0: a formula for the solutions.
- The initial value problem: $\vec x = e^{At}\vec x_0$.
- $e^{A + B} = e^Ae^B$ only for commuting matrices; $(e^A)^{-1} = e^{-A}$. In practice $e^{At} = X(t)X(0)^{-1}$.
