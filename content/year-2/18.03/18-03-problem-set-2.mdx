## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A decay chain on Tatooine" points={20} mit="Part II, Problem 4">

On the small world Tatooine (MIT's invented setting) a certain isotope of Startium, St, decays with a half-life $t_S$. It decays with equal chance into an isotope of Midium, Mi, or directly into the stable element Endium, En. Midium is radioactive too: it decays into Endium with a half-life $t_M$, and $t_M \neq t_S$. All the St was there at the start, and all the Mi and En come from this decay chain. Let $x(t)$, $y(t)$ and $z(t)$ be the amounts of St, Mi and En at time $t$, in units for which $x(0) = 1$. So $y(0) = 0$ and $z(0) = 0$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Sketch $x$, $y$ and $z$ against $t$. What does each tend to as $t \to \infty$?

<Hint>

$x$ only decays. Think about what each of $y$ and $z$ gets and loses.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Nothing is created or destroyed in the chain: every atom that leaves St ends up as En in the end.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a-limits" label="The limits of $(x, y, z)$ as $t \to \infty$ are"
  choices={["(0, 0, 1)", "(0, 1, 0)", "(0, 0, 0)", "(1/2, 0, 1/2)"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow the atoms: St feeds Mi and En, Mi feeds En, and En never decays.

<Step title="The shapes of the curves">

$x$ starts at $1$ and only loses atoms, so it falls towards $0$. $y$ starts at $0$, gains atoms from St, and loses them by its own decay: it first rises, and when there is little St left it falls. $z$ only gains atoms and rises. Here is the picture for the values $\sigma = 0.1$ and $\mu = 0.2$ per unit of time (the decay constants of part (b)), computed from the solutions found in part (c):

<Plot variable="t" domain={[0, 40]} range={[0, 1.05]} xLabel="t" yLabel="amount"
  curves={[
    { expr: "exp(-0.1*t)", label: "x" },
    { expr: "0.5*(exp(-0.1*t) - exp(-0.2*t))", label: "y" },
    { expr: "1 - exp(-0.1*t) - 0.5*(exp(-0.1*t) - exp(-0.2*t))", label: "z" }
  ]}
  caption="The amounts of St (x), Mi (y) and En (z) with decay constants 0.1 and 0.2, computed from the solutions of part (c). The curve x falls, y rises and then falls, z rises towards 1." />

</Step>

<Step title="The limits">

As $t \to \infty$, $x \to 0$ and $y \to 0$: both radioactive elements die out. The total $x + y + z$ never changes (part (b)) and starts at $1$, so $z \to 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ the three amounts are $(1, 0, 0)$, with total $1$. At large $t$ the limits are $(0, 0, 1)$ with total $1$ again, as they must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Write the differential equations for $x$, $y$ and $z$. Use $\sigma$ for the decay constant of St and $\mu$ for that of Mi, and first relate $\sigma$ to $t_S$ and $\mu$ to $t_M$. As a check, the equations must give $\dot x + \dot y + \dot z = 0$.

<Hint>

Decay is exponential: St obeys $\dot x = -\sigma x$. How much is left after one half-life?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The half of the St atoms that decay into Mi arrive at Mi at the rate $\frac{1}{2}\sigma x$. The other half go to En, and so do all the Mi atoms that decay.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b-sigma" label="$\sigma =$" expr="log(2)/tS"
  vars={{ tS: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ tS: "t_S" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/tS", hint: "With σ = 1/t_S, the amount x = e^(−σt) has fallen to 1/e, about 37 percent, after a time t_S, not to one half." },
    { expr: "tS/log(2)", hint: "That has the dimension of a time. σ is a rate, with dimension 1 over time." },
    { expr: "log(2)*tS", hint: "That has the dimension of time squared if t_S is a time. A rate is a number divided by a time." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-b-ydot" label="The equation for Midium is"
  choices={["ẏ = −μy + σx", "ẏ = −μy + ½σx", "ẏ = μy + ½σx", "ẏ = −μy"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p4-b-zdot" label="The equation for Endium is"
  choices={["ż = ½σx + μy", "ż = σx + μy", "ż = ½σx − μy", "ż = μy"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each element loses atoms at a rate proportional to its amount, and what it loses goes to the next element or elements. Write each rate of change as gains minus losses.

<Step title="The decay constants">

An amount that decays like St obeys $\dot x = -\sigma x$, with solution $x = x(0)e^{-\sigma t}$. Half is left after the half-life $t_S$ when $e^{-\sigma t_S} = \frac{1}{2}$, so $\sigma = \ln 2/t_S$. Mi decays in the same way, with $\mu$ and $t_M$:

<Formula label="Decay constants from half-lives" tex="\sigma = \frac{\ln 2}{t_S}, \qquad \mu = \frac{\ln 2}{t_M}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 4(b), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The three equations">

St only loses atoms, at the rate $\sigma x$. Mi gains half of the atoms that St loses, and loses atoms at the rate $\mu y$. En gains the other half of St's losses and all of Mi's losses:

<Formula label="The decay chain" tex="\dot x = -\sigma x, \qquad \dot y = -\mu y + \frac{1}{2}\sigma x, \qquad \dot z = \frac{1}{2}\sigma x + \mu y" cite="solutions" at="Problem 4(b), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Add the three equations. Every term cancels, so $\dot x + \dot y + \dot z = 0$: the total is constant, as the problem says it must be. A units check: each term of each equation has the dimension of an amount per time. The wrong choices fail the sum: with $\dot y = -\mu y + \sigma x$ the sum is $\dot x + \dot y + \dot z = \frac{1}{2}\sigma x \neq 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Solve the equations one after another: first $x$, then $y$, then $z$. Use $x(0) = 1$, $y(0) = 0$ and $z(0) = 0$, and take $\sigma \neq \mu$.

<Hint>

Start with $x$, which does not depend on the others. Put it into the equation for $y$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The equation for $y$ is first-order linear with constant coefficients: $\dot y + \mu y = \frac{1}{2}\sigma e^{-\sigma t}$. Multiply by the integrating factor $e^{\mu t}$ and integrate.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $z$ you need no new integration: the total $x + y + z$ is constant.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c-y" label="$y(t) =$" expr="sigma/(2*(mu-sigma))*(exp(-sigma*t) - exp(-mu*t))"
  vars={{ sigma: [0.1, 0.5], mu: [0.6, 1.5], t: [0.2, 3] }}
  names={{ sigma: "\\sigma", mu: "\\mu", t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sigma/(mu-sigma)*(exp(-sigma*t) - exp(-mu*t))", hint: "Look at the slope of y at t = 0: the equation gives ẏ(0) = ½σ. Your formula has slope σ there." },
    { expr: "sigma/(2*(mu-sigma))*(exp(-mu*t) - exp(-sigma*t))", hint: "In this range μ is larger than σ, so e^(−μt) falls faster and your y is negative. An amount cannot be negative." },
    { expr: "sigma/(2*(mu+sigma))*(exp(-sigma*t) - exp(-mu*t))", hint: "Put the formula back into ẏ + μy at t = 0. The integration gives μ − σ in the denominator, not μ + σ." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-c-num" label="With $\sigma = 0.1$ and $\mu = 0.2$ per unit of time: $y(10) =$" value="0.1163" tolerance="0.0005" calc="p4-c-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The chain is solved from the top: $x$ is a plain decay, then $y$ is a linear equation driven by $x$, then $z$ follows from conservation of the total.

<Step title="x">

<Formula label="Solution for St" tex="x = e^{-\sigma t}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 4(c), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="y with an integrating factor">

Put $x$ into the equation for $y$ and write it in standard linear form, $\dot y + \mu y = \frac{1}{2}\sigma e^{-\sigma t}$. With the integrating factor $e^{\mu t}$ the left side becomes the derivative of $e^{\mu t}y$:

<Formula label="The integrating factor at work" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{\mu t}y\right) = \frac{1}{2}\sigma\,e^{(\mu - \sigma)t}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 4(c), p. 1" />

Integrate. Since $\sigma \neq \mu$, the integral of $e^{(\mu-\sigma)t}$ is $e^{(\mu-\sigma)t}/(\mu-\sigma)$, so $e^{\mu t}y = \dfrac{\sigma}{2(\mu - \sigma)}e^{(\mu-\sigma)t} + c$. The condition $y(0) = 0$ gives $c = -\dfrac{\sigma}{2(\mu-\sigma)}$. Multiply by $e^{-\mu t}$:

<Formula label="Solution for Mi" tex="y = \frac{\sigma}{2(\mu - \sigma)}\left(e^{-\sigma t} - e^{-\mu t}\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 4(c), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="z from conservation">

The total is constant and equal to $1$, so $z = 1 - x - y$:

<Formula label="Solution for En" tex="z = 1 + \frac{\sigma/2 - \mu}{\mu - \sigma}\,e^{-\sigma t} + \frac{\sigma/2}{\mu - \sigma}\,e^{-\mu t}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 4(c), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ the formula for $y$ gives $0$, and its slope there is $\dfrac{\sigma}{2(\mu-\sigma)}(-\sigma + \mu) = \frac{1}{2}\sigma$, as the equation says. For large $t$ it tends to $0$. With $\sigma = 0.1$ and $\mu = 0.2$ the prefactor is $\dfrac{0.1}{2 \times 0.1} = \frac{1}{2}$, so $y(10) = \frac{1}{2}(e^{-1} - e^{-2}) = 0.1163$. The graph of part (a) shows the same value at $t = 10$. In the same way $z(10) = 0.5158$ from the formula for $z$, and $x(10) + y(10) + z(10) = 1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

At what time does the quantity of Midium peak? The answer depends on $\sigma$ and $\mu$.

<Hint>

At a peak of $y$ the slope is zero. Use either the differential equation for $y$ or the formula you found.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Setting $\dot y = 0$ in the formula gives $\sigma e^{-\sigma t} = \mu e^{-\mu t}$. Take logarithms of both sides, or divide to get one exponential.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-d-formula" label="$t_{\max} =$" expr="(log(mu) - log(sigma))/(mu - sigma)"
  vars={{ sigma: [0.1, 0.5], mu: [0.6, 1.5] }}
  names={{ sigma: "\\sigma", mu: "\\mu" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(log(sigma) - log(mu))/(mu - sigma)", hint: "Your time is negative in this range, and the peak happens after t = 0. Check which logarithm comes first: the equation to solve is e^((μ−σ)t) = μ/σ." },
    { expr: "(log(mu) - log(sigma))/(mu + sigma)", hint: "The exponent of the exponential in the equation is (μ − σ)t, so the division is by μ − σ." },
    { expr: "log(mu/sigma)", hint: "Check the units: the time must come out as 1 over a rate, so divide by a rate." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-d-num" label="With $\sigma = 0.1$ and $\mu = 0.2$ per unit of time: $t_{\max} =$" value="6.93" tolerance="0.01" calc="p4-d-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The peak of $y$ is where its slope is zero.

<Step title="Set the slope to zero">

From the equation, $\dot y = -\mu y + \frac{1}{2}\sigma x = 0$ means $\mu y = \frac{1}{2}\sigma e^{-\sigma t}$. Put in $y$ from part (c):

<Formula label="Condition for the peak" tex="\mu\,\frac{\sigma}{2(\mu - \sigma)}\left(e^{-\sigma t} - e^{-\mu t}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\sigma e^{-\sigma t}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 4(d), p. 1" />

Cancel $\sigma/2$ and multiply by $(\mu - \sigma)$: $\mu e^{-\sigma t} - \mu e^{-\mu t} = (\mu - \sigma)e^{-\sigma t}$, so $\sigma e^{-\sigma t} = \mu e^{-\mu t}$. Divide by $\sigma e^{-\mu t}$ to get $e^{(\mu - \sigma)t} = \mu/\sigma$.

</Step>

<Step title="Take the logarithm">

<Formula label="Time of the peak of Midium" tex="t_{\max} = \frac{\ln\mu - \ln\sigma}{\mu - \sigma}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 4(d), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The time is positive, as it must be, because $\ln\mu - \ln\sigma$ and $\mu - \sigma$ always have the same sign. As $\mu \to \sigma$ it tends to $1/\sigma$, which is the peak of $\frac{1}{2}\sigma t e^{-\sigma t}$, the limit of the solution for equal constants. With $\sigma = 0.1$ and $\mu = 0.2$ it gives $\ln 2/0.1 = 6.93$, between $1/\mu = 5$ and $1/\sigma = 10$, and the graph of part (a) peaks at about that time.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

Suppose that $x(0) = 2$ instead of $1$, and $y(0) = z(0) = 0$ as before. What changes in $x$, $y$ and $z$?

<Hint>

Look at how $x(0)$ enters the equations. Are they linear in $x$, $y$, $z$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-e" label="With $x(0) = 2$:"
  choices={["x, y and z are all twice as large at every time", "only x is twice as large; y and z do not change", "x, y and z are all unchanged, but everything happens twice as fast", "the peak of y comes twice as late"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equations are linear and the starting values are zero except for $x$, so the solution is proportional to $x(0)$.

<Step title="Scale the solution">

If $(x, y, z)$ solves the equations with $x(0) = 1$, then $(2x, 2y, 2z)$ solves them too, because each term is linear, and it has $x(0) = 2$, $y(0) = z(0) = 0$. By uniqueness of the solution of the initial-value problem it is the solution. Every amount is doubled at every time. The times do not change, in particular $t_{\max}$ stays the same: the decay constants did not change.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The limits are $(0, 0, 2)$, as the conserved total $x + y + z = 2$ requires.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="f" points={4}>

A question apart from the chain. Suppose that $x(t) = e^t$ is a solution of the equation $t\dot x + 2x = q(t)$. What is $q(t)$? What is the general solution?

<Hint>

Put $x = e^t$ into the left side. There is no integration to do.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The general solution is one solution plus the general solution of the homogeneous equation $t\dot x + 2x = 0$. That one is separable.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-f-q" label="$q(t) =$" expr="(t + 2)*exp(t)"
  vars={{ t: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(t + 1)*exp(t)", hint: "Check the term 2x: with x = eᵗ it is 2eᵗ, not eᵗ." },
    { expr: "(t + 2)*exp(2*t)", hint: "The derivative of eᵗ is eᵗ, and no exponential is doubled: x and ẋ are both eᵗ." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-f-general" label="The general solution is"
  choices={["eᵗ + C/t²", "eᵗ + Ce⁻²ᵗ", "eᵗ + Ct²", "eᵗ + C/t"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a linear equation the general solution is one particular solution plus the general solution of the homogeneous equation.

<Step title="q(t)">

Put $x = e^t$, $\dot x = e^t$ into the left side:

<Formula label="The right side" tex="q(t) = t\,e^t + 2e^t = (t + 2)\,e^t" cite="solutions" at="Problem 4(f), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The homogeneous equation">

$t\dot x + 2x = 0$ is separable: $\dfrac{dx}{x} = -\dfrac{2\,dt}{t}$, so $\ln|x| = -2\ln|t| + c$ and $x = C/t^2$. Add the particular solution:

<Formula label="General solution" tex="x = e^t + \frac{C}{t^2}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 4(f), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Substituting $C/t^2$ gives $t(-2C/t^3) + 2C/t^2 = 0$, so it solves the homogeneous equation, and $e^t$ solves the full equation by the first step. The equation is singular at $t = 0$, so the formula holds on $t > 0$ and on $t < 0$ separately, with a separate $C$ on each side.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Complex numbers and roots of unity" points={18} mit="Part II, Problem 5">

<Part label="a" points={10}>

For each of the five complex numbers below find its rectangular form $a + bi$, its polar form (the modulus $|z|$ and the argument $\operatorname{Arg} z$) and mark it in the complex plane. Take the argument in the interval $(-\pi, \pi]$ unless you wish otherwise.

1. $1 - i$.
2. The number $z$ with $|z| = 2$ and $\operatorname{Arg} z = \pi/6$.
3. A square root of $i$ with negative imaginary part.
4. A sixth root of $1$ with argument $\theta$ such that $0 < \theta < \pi/2$.
5. $\left(\dfrac{1 + i}{\sqrt 2}\right)^{-13}$.

(MIT asks you to arrange the five numbers in a table with a picture of the complex plane for each; the exponential form of Problem 6(a) is added to the same table.)

<Hint>

For a number $a + bi$, the modulus is $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, and the argument is the angle of the point $(a, b)$ measured from the positive real axis. For number 2 go the other way: $a = |z|\cos\theta$ and $b = |z|\sin\theta$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Numbers 3, 4 and 5 are easiest in polar form: a square root halves the argument, the sixth roots of $1$ have arguments $2\pi k/6$, and a power multiplies the argument by the exponent. An argument can be changed by a multiple of $2\pi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a-1" label="Number 1: the modulus $|z| =$" value="1.414" tolerance="0.002" calc="p5-a-1" />

<Answer id="p5-a-2" label="Number 2: the real part $a =$" value="1.732" tolerance="0.002" calc="p5-a-2" />

<Answer id="p5-a-3" label="Number 3: the argument in $(-\pi, \pi]$, in radians:" value="-2.356" tolerance="0.002" calc="p5-a-3" />

<Answer id="p5-a-4" label="Number 4: the imaginary part $b =$" value="0.866" tolerance="0.001" calc="p5-a-4" />

<Answer id="p5-a-5" label="Number 5: the argument in $(-\pi, \pi]$, in radians:" value="2.356" tolerance="0.002" calc="p5-a-5" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Convert between $a + bi$ and polar form with $a = |z|\cos\theta$, $b = |z|\sin\theta$, $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, and use the rules of exponents for roots and powers.

<Step title="Numbers 1 and 2">

For $1 - i$: $|z| = \sqrt{1 + 1} = \sqrt 2$, and the point $(1, -1)$ is at an angle of $-\pi/4$. For number 2: $a = 2\cos(\pi/6) = \sqrt 3$ and $b = 2\sin(\pi/6) = 1$:

<Formula label="Numbers 1 and 2" tex="1 - i = \sqrt 2\,\bigl(\cos(-\frac{\pi}{4}) + i\sin(-\frac{\pi}{4})\bigr), \qquad z = 2\bigl(\cos\frac{\pi}{6} + i\sin\frac{\pi}{6}\bigr) = \sqrt 3 + i" cite="solutions" at="Problem 5(a), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Numbers 3 and 4">

The square roots of $i = e^{i\pi/2}$ are $\pm e^{i\pi/4} = \pm\dfrac{1 + i}{\sqrt 2}$. The one with negative imaginary part is $-\dfrac{1 + i}{\sqrt 2}$: modulus $1$, argument $5\pi/4$, which is $-3\pi/4$ in $(-\pi, \pi]$. The sixth roots of $1$ are $e^{2\pi ik/6}$, and $k = 1$ gives the argument $\pi/3$, the only one in $(0, \pi/2)$. It is $\dfrac{1 + \sqrt 3\, i}{2}$, with imaginary part $\dfrac{\sqrt 3}{2} = 0.866$.

</Step>

<Step title="Number 5">

$\dfrac{1 + i}{\sqrt 2} = e^{i\pi/4}$, so the number is $e^{-13\pi i/4}$. Adding $4\pi$ to the argument does not change the number: $-13\pi/4 + 4\pi = 3\pi/4$. So it is $e^{3\pi i/4} = \dfrac{-1 + i}{\sqrt 2}$, with modulus $1$ and argument $3\pi/4 = 2.356$.

</Step>

<Step title="The table">

<Formula label="The five numbers in four forms" tex="\begin{matrix} 1 - i & \sqrt 2,\ -\frac{\pi}{4} & \sqrt 2\,e^{-i\pi/4} \\ \sqrt 3 + i & 2,\ \frac{\pi}{6} & 2e^{i\pi/6} \\ -\frac{1 + i}{\sqrt 2} & 1,\ \frac{5\pi}4 & e^{5\pi i/4} \\ \frac{1 + \sqrt 3\,i}{2} & 1,\ \frac{\pi}{3} & e^{i\pi/3} \\ \frac{-1 + i}{\sqrt 2} & 1,\ \frac{3\pi}4 & e^{3\pi i/4} \end{matrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 5(a) and 6(a), p. 2" />

The rectangular form gives the coordinates for the picture of each point. An angle is only fixed up to a multiple of $2\pi$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A complex number $c$ times $e^{i\omega t}$ has real part $A\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ where $A = |c|$ and $\phi = -\operatorname{Arg}\,c$. The cartesian route (conjugate, then multiply out) and the polar route (modulus and argument) must agree.

<Step title="First route: the conjugate">

Multiply top and bottom by $\sqrt 3 - i$. The denominator is $(\sqrt 3 + i)(\sqrt 3 - i) = 3 + 1 = 4$:

<Formula label="Cartesian route" tex="\frac{e^{3it}}{\sqrt 3 + i} = \frac{(\sqrt 3 - i)(\cos 3t + i\sin 3t)}{4}, \qquad \operatorname{Re} = \frac{\sqrt 3}{4}\cos 3t + \frac{1}{4}\sin 3t" cite="solutions" at="Problem 7(a), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The right triangle">

The triangle with legs $a = \sqrt 3/4$ and $b = 1/4$ has hypotenuse $A = \sqrt{3/16 + 1/16} = \frac{1}{2}$ and $\tan\phi = b/a = 1/\sqrt 3$, so $\phi = \pi/6 = 0.5236$.

</Step>

<Step title="Second route: polar form">

The number $\sqrt 3 + i$ has modulus $2$ and argument $\pi/6$ (the same triangle again), so $\sqrt 3 + i = 2e^{i\pi/6}$. Then

<Formula label="Polar route" tex="\frac{e^{3it}}{\sqrt 3 + i} = \frac{e^{3it}}{2e^{i\pi/6}} = \frac{1}{2}\,e^{i(3t - \pi/6)}, \qquad B = \frac{1}{2},\ \phi = \frac{\pi}{6}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 7(a), p. 2" />

and the real part is $B\cos(3t - \phi) = \frac{1}{2}\cos(3t - \pi/6)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two routes agree: $\frac{1}{2}\cos(3t - \pi/6) = \frac{1}{2}\cos 3t\cos\frac{\pi}{6} + \frac{1}{2}\sin 3t\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt 3}{4}\cos 3t + \frac{1}{4}\sin 3t$. At $t = 0$ both give $\sqrt 3/4 = 0.4330$.

</Step>

</Solution>
