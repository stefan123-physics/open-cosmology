The previous lecture's [exponential input theorem](/courses/18-03/exponential-input-theorem) has an exceptional case, $p(\alpha) = 0$. This lecture explains what it means physically: resonance (transcript, pp. 1–2).

## Recap

$p(D) = D^2 + AD + B$ is both a formal polynomial, to be factored, differentiated and evaluated, and a linear operator on functions (transcript, p. 1). For the input $e^{\alpha t}$ (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="The particular solutions so far" tex="y_p = \frac{e^{\alpha t}}{p(\alpha)} \;\; (p(\alpha) \ne 0), \qquad y_p = \frac{t\,e^{\alpha t}}{p'(\alpha)} \;\; (\alpha \text{ a simple root})" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

(The board first lacked the $t$; the lecture corrects it, p. 2.)

## Driving an undamped oscillator

Everyone knows one case of resonance: to make a child's swing go high, push at its natural frequency; to stop it, push at the wrong time (transcript, pp. 2–3). The lecture also cites the collapse of the Tacoma bridge (p. 3). (This page's note: engineers now describe that collapse as aeroelastic flutter rather than simple resonance.)

Model an undamped pendulum or spring, with natural frequency $\omega_0$, driven at a different input frequency $\omega_1$ (p. 3):

<Formula label="Driven undamped oscillator" tex="(D^2 + \omega_0^2)\,y = \cos\omega_1t, \qquad \omega_1 \ne \omega_0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Complexify: $(D^2 + \omega_0^2)\tilde y = e^{i\omega_1t}$. Substituting $i\omega_1$ for $D$ gives $(i\omega_1)^2 + \omega_0^2 = \omega_0^2 - \omega_1^2$, which is real, so (pp. 3–4)

<Formula label="Response to cos ω₁t" tex="\tilde y_p = \frac{e^{i\omega_1t}}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_1^2}, \qquad y_p = \frac{\cos\omega_1t}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_1^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

Whatever its own frequency, the system responds at the **driving** frequency; only the amplitude changes, dramatically so when $\omega_1$ is close to $\omega_0$, because the denominator is then nearly zero (pp. 4–5). (The transcript says "always zero" for nearly zero, and once "omega two" for $\omega_0$.) That, to all intents and purposes, is resonance (p. 5).

## Exact resonance

If $\omega_1 = \omega_0$, the formula fails: $i\omega_0$ is a simple root of $D^2 + \omega_0^2$ (transcript, p. 5). With $p'(D) = 2D$ (p. 6),

<Formula label="Resonant solution" tex="\tilde y_p = \frac{t\,e^{i\omega_0t}}{2i\omega_0}, \qquad y_p = \frac{t\sin\omega_0t}{2\omega_0}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

The $i$ in the denominator turns the real part into a sine (p. 6). Read it as an oscillation $\sin\omega_0t$ whose amplitude $t/(2\omega_0)$ grows **linearly**: it stays between the lines $\pm t/(2\omega_0)$ (pp. 6–7).

## From near resonance to resonance

How does $\cos\omega_1t/(\omega_0^2 - \omega_1^2)$ turn into $t\sin\omega_0t/(2\omega_0)$ as $\omega_1 \to \omega_0$ (transcript, p. 7)? It cannot: it just gets taller, like watching a giraffe pass a first-floor window, and it already starts high at $t = 0$ (p. 7). But it is not the only particular solution. Adding the complementary-solution piece $-\cos\omega_0t/(\omega_0^2 - \omega_1^2)$ gives another (pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="Another particular solution" tex="y_p = \frac{\cos\omega_1t - \cos\omega_0t}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_1^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

Now top and bottom both tend to 0, so use L'Hôpital's rule, differentiating with respect to $\omega_1$ with $t$ and $\omega_0$ fixed (pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="The limit" tex="\lim_{\omega_1 \to \omega_0}\frac{-t\sin\omega_1t}{-2\omega_1} = \frac{t\sin\omega_0t}{2\omega_0}" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

exactly the resonant solution (p. 9).

## Beats

To see what the new solution looks like, use the identity (transcript, pp. 9–10)

<Formula label="Difference of cosines" tex="\cos B - \cos A = 2\sin\frac{A - B}{2}\,\sin\frac{A + B}{2}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 9–10" />

(The lecture checks the factor 2 by looking it up; it is best proved with Euler's formula backwards, p. 10.) With $B = \omega_1t$ and $A = \omega_0t$ (p. 10):

<Formula label="Near-resonant solution as a product" tex="\frac{\cos\omega_1t - \cos\omega_0t}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_1^2} = \frac{2}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_1^2}\,\sin\frac{(\omega_0 - \omega_1)t}{2}\,\sin\frac{(\omega_0 + \omega_1)t}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

The last factor oscillates at about $\omega_0$. The rest is a slowly varying amplitude: a sine of very low frequency, multiplied by a large constant (pp. 10–11). So the curve oscillates quickly inside an envelope that swells and shrinks slowly (pp. 11–12). As $\omega_1 \to \omega_0$ the envelope's period goes to infinity and only its rising start is left, the growing resonant solution (p. 12).

This is the phenomenon of **beats**: two pure oscillations of nearly equal frequency combine into this curve, and what you hear is the envelope. String players and piano tuners tune by listening for the beats and turning the peg until they disappear (p. 12).

## Damping and practical resonance

Notation needs care (transcript, p. 13). The spring $mx'' + cx' + kx = f(t)$ becomes, after dividing by $m$, $y'' + 2py' + \omega_0^2y = f(t)$, and only then is $\omega_0$ the **natural (undamped) frequency**. The course's computer visual uses $x'' + bx' + kx$, whose $k$ is not the same; check how each equation is written before using a formula (p. 13). The [pseudo-frequency](/courses/18-03/damped-oscillations), or natural damped frequency, satisfies $\omega_1^2 = \omega_0^2 - p^2$: more damping, lower frequency (pp. 13–14). From here on, as in the lecture, $\omega_1$ means this pseudo-frequency, no longer the driving frequency.

Now drive the damped spring at a third frequency $\omega$ (p. 14):

<Formula label="Driven damped oscillator" tex="y'' + 2p\,y' + \omega_0^2\,y = \cos\omega t" cite="reading1" at="p. 14" />

With damping there is no true resonance: the amplitude cannot build up without limit (p. 14). But which input frequency gives the largest response? Not $\omega_1$, as one might expect, but something a little lower, the **practical resonance** frequency (pp. 14–15):

<Formula label="Practical resonance" tex="\omega_r = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2p^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 15" />

(Why, by this page's own working: the exponential input theorem gives the amplitude, one over the modulus of the characteristic polynomial at $i\omega$, as $1/\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4p^2\omega^2}$, and the expression under the root, a quadratic in $\omega^2$, is smallest at $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 - 2p^2$. This needs $\omega_0^2 > 2p^2$; with heavier damping the response is largest at $\omega = 0$.)

<WorkedExample title="A spring with ω₀ = 3">
<Problem>
For $y'' + 9y = f(t)$: (a) find $y_p$ for $f = \cos 2t$; (b) find $y_p$ for $f = 4\cos 3t$; (c) solve with $f = \cos 2.8t$ from rest at 0, and describe the beats; (d) with damping $p = 0.5$, find the pseudo-frequency and the practical resonance frequency.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Off resonance">
$y_p = \frac{\cos 2t}{9 - 4} = \frac15\cos 2t$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) At resonance">
By linearity, $y_p = 4\cdot\frac{t\sin 3t}{2\cdot 3} = \frac23\,t\sin 3t$: the amplitude grows by $\frac23$ every unit of time.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Beats">
$y = \frac{\cos 2.8t - \cos 3t}{9 - 7.84} = \frac{\cos 2.8t - \cos 3t}{1.16}$ satisfies $y(0) = 0$ and $y'(0) = 0$. It equals $\frac{2}{1.16}\sin(0.1t)\sin(2.9t)$: oscillations at about 2.9 inside an envelope of height $\frac{2}{1.16} \approx 1.72$, which returns to zero every $\pi/0.1 \approx 31.4$ time units.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) With damping">
$\omega_1 = \sqrt{9 - 0.25} \approx 2.958$ and $\omega_r = \sqrt{9 - 0.5} \approx 2.915$: the largest response comes slightly below the pseudo-frequency, which is itself below $\omega_0 = 3$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Undamped, $\omega_1 \ne \omega_0$: $y_p = \cos\omega_1t/(\omega_0^2 - \omega_1^2)$, at the driving frequency, large near $\omega_0$.
- Resonance, $\omega_1 = \omega_0$: $y_p = t\sin\omega_0t/(2\omega_0)$, amplitude growing linearly; it is the limit of $(\cos\omega_1t - \cos\omega_0t)/(\omega_0^2 - \omega_1^2)$.
- That near-resonant solution shows beats: a fast oscillation in a slow envelope.
- Damped: practical resonance at $\omega_r = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2p^2}$.
