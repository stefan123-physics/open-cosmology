## Problem 0

<Exercise id="p0" title="A herd of oryx whose growth rate fades away" points={15} mit="Part II, Problem 0">

In a nature reserve, the number of oryx at time $t$ is $x(t)$, measured in thousands of animals (kilo-oryx), with $t$ in years. When the herd is small compared with its food, it grows at a *natural growth rate* $k$: in a short time interval $\Delta t$ the population changes by about $k\,x(t)\,\Delta t$. (MIT's recitation also studied hunting; we leave that aside, as MIT does.)

A virus infects the herd, and the growth rate then fades with time according to

<Formula label="The growth rate of the infected herd" tex="k(t) = \frac{k_0}{(a + t)^2}, \qquad t \ge 0" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 0, p. 2" />

where $a$ and $k_0$ are positive constants.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

What are the units of the constant $a$ and of the constant $k_0$?

<Hint>

The growth rate $k$ multiplies $x\,\Delta t$ to give a change in $x$. What must the units of $k$ be, so that $k\,x\,\Delta t$ is in thousands of animals?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In a sum such as $a + t$, both terms must have the same units. Then ask what $k_0$ must be, so that $k_0$ divided by (years)² gives the units of $k$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p0-a" label="The units are:"
  choices={["a in years, k0 in years", "a in years, k0 in 1/years", "a in years, k0 in years squared", "a is a pure number, k0 in 1/years"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Every term of an equation, and every term of a sum, must have the same units.

<Step title="The units of the rate">

A change $k\,x\,\Delta t$ has the units of $x$. So $k\,\Delta t$ has no units, and $k$ is in 1/years: it is a rate per year.

</Step>

<Step title="The units of a and k₀">

The constant $a$ is added to $t$, which is in years, so $a$ is in years. The fraction $k_0/(a+t)^2$ must be in 1/years. The denominator is in years squared, so $k_0$ is in years:

<Formula label="The units of the constants" tex="[k_0] = [k]\,[a + t]^2 = \frac{1}{\text{years}} \cdot \text{years}^2 = \text{years}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 0(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $k_0 = 2$ years and $a = 1$ year. At $t = 0$ the rate is $2/1^2 = 2$ per year, a rate indeed. If $k_0$ were in 1/years, the rate would be in 1/years³ and $k\,x\,\Delta t$ would not be in thousands of animals.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Write down the differential equation for $x(t)$. Give the right-hand side, the formula for $x'$.

<Hint>

Start from the change in a short interval: $x(t + \Delta t) - x(t) \approx k(t)\,x(t)\,\Delta t$. Divide by $\Delta t$ and let $\Delta t$ go to zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p0-b" label="$x' =$" expr="k0*x/(a+t)^2"
  vars={{ k0: [0.5, 2], x: [0.5, 3], a: [0.5, 3], t: [0.2, 4] }}
  names={{ k0: "k_0", x: "x", a: "a", t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "k0*x/(a+t)", hint: "Your denominator has $a + t$ to the first power. The rate $k(t)$ has the square of $a + t$." },
    { expr: "k0/(a+t)^2", hint: "That is the rate $k(t)$ alone. The change in the population is the rate times the population itself, times the time interval." },
    { expr: "k0*x*(a+t)^2", hint: "Here $(a+t)^2$ multiplies instead of dividing: your rate would grow with time, but the virus makes it fade." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a short time the change of the population is the growth rate times the population times the length of the time. Divide by the length and shrink it.

<Step title="The change in a short time">

Over a short interval, with $k$ evaluated at the start,

<Formula label="Population change in a short interval" tex="x(t + \Delta t) - x(t) \approx k(t)\,x(t)\,\Delta t" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 0, p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The differential equation">

Dividing by $\Delta t$ and taking the limit $\Delta t \to 0$:

<Formula label="The differential equation of the infected herd" tex="x' = \frac{k_0}{(a+t)^2}\,x" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 0(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The right side is in thousands of animals per year: $k_0$ in years divided by years² gives 1/years, times $x$ in thousands of animals. That is also the unit of $x'$. And $x' > 0$ when $x > 0$, but the growth slows down as $t$ increases, as the virus is meant to do.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the general solution of your equation, for all values of $t$ and $x$ where it makes sense, not only those that matter for oryx. MIT's two warnings: use the absolute value in $\int dx/x = \ln|x| + c$ correctly, and do not forget solutions that are lost when you divide by $x$.

Write the answer as $C$ times an exponential, with $C$ an arbitrary constant.

<Hint>

Separate the variables: put everything with $x$ on the left and everything with $t$ on the right. You divide by $x$ here. Which function $x$ would you lose by doing so?

</Hint>

<Hint>

An antiderivative of $(a + t)^{-2}$ with respect to $t$ is $-(a+t)^{-1}$. After integrating you have $\ln|x|$ on one side. Take the exponential of both sides and deal with the absolute value.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p0-c" label="$x(t) =$" expr="C*exp(-k0/(a+t))"
  vars={{ C: [0.5, 3], k0: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 3], t: [0.2, 4] }}
  names={{ C: "C", k0: "k_0", a: "a", t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "C*exp(k0/(a+t))", hint: "Differentiate your formula: you get $x' = -k_0 x/(a+t)^2$, with the opposite sign to the equation." },
    { expr: "C*exp(-k0/(a+t)^2)", hint: "You have put the rate $k(t)$ itself into the exponent. Differentiating that gives an extra factor $2/(a+t)$ and a different power of $a + t$." },
    { expr: "C - k0/(a+t)", hint: "That is the logarithm of $|x|$ up to a constant, not $x$. Take the exponential of both sides. Your formula's derivative also has no factor $x$ in it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equation is separable. Divide by $x$, integrate both sides, take exponentials, and then put back the solution lost in the division.

<Step title="Separate and integrate">

For $x \ne 0$, divide by $x$ and multiply by $dt$:

<Formula label="Separated form" tex="\frac{dx}{x} = k_0\,(a+t)^{-2}\,dt" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 0(c) (our own solution)" />

Integrating, with an arbitrary constant $c$ (the constants on the two sides are merged into one):

<Formula label="After integrating" tex="\ln|x| = -\frac{k_0}{a+t} + c" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 0(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Remove the logarithm and the absolute value">

Exponentiating gives $|x| = e^{c}\,e^{-k_0/(a+t)}$. So $x$ is $e^{c}\,e^{-k_0/(a+t)}$ or minus that, and both are covered by writing $x = C\,e^{-k_0/(a+t)}$ with $C = \pm e^{c}$, any nonzero number.

</Step>

<Step title="The lost solution">

Dividing by $x$ excluded $x = 0$. But $x(t) = 0$ for all $t$ does solve $x' = k_0 x/(a+t)^2$, since both sides are zero. It is the case $C = 0$ of the formula, so the formula holds for every real $C$:

<Formula label="The general solution" tex="x(t) = C\,e^{-k_0/(a+t)}, \qquad C \text{ any real number}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 0(c) (our own solution)" />

The formula and the equation make sense for $t \ne -a$. For $t \ge 0$, and in fact for every $t > -a$, this is one solution for each $C$. On the other side, for $t < -a$, the constant $C$ can be chosen independently, because the point $t = -a$ cannot be crossed.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate: $x' = C\,e^{-k_0/(a+t)}\cdot \dfrac{k_0}{(a+t)^2} = \dfrac{k_0}{(a+t)^2}\,x$, the equation of part (b). The exponent $-k_0/(a+t)$ has no units, as an exponent must be: $k_0$ is in years, $a + t$ in years.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

At $t = 0$ there is a positive population $x_0$. Does the fading growth rate make the population settle at a limit for large $t$, or does the population grow without bound? If it settles, give the limiting population as $t \to \infty$, in terms of $x_0$, $k_0$ and $a$.

<Hint>

Use $x(0) = x_0$ to find $C$. Then look at the exponent $-k_0/(a+t)$ when $t$ is very large.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p0-d" label="$\lim_{t\to\infty} x(t) =$" expr="x0*exp(k0/a)"
  vars={{ x0: [0.5, 3], k0: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ x0: "x_0", k0: "k_0", a: "a" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x0", hint: "The limit is $C$, and $C$ is not $x_0$: at $t = 0$ the exponential is $e^{-k_0/a}$, not 1." },
    { expr: "x0*exp(-k0/a)", hint: "Check the sign: $x_0 = C e^{-k_0/a}$, so to find $C$ you must multiply $x_0$ by $e^{+k_0/a}$." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For large $t$ the exponent goes to zero, so the exponential goes to 1 and $x$ goes to $C$.

<Step title="Find C">

With $x(0) = x_0$ the general solution gives $x_0 = C\,e^{-k_0/a}$, so $C = x_0\,e^{k_0/a}$ and

<Formula label="The solution with the starting population" tex="x(t) = x_0\,e^{k_0/a}\,e^{-k_0/(a+t)}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 0(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The limit">

As $t \to \infty$ the exponent $-k_0/(a+t)$ goes to $0$ and its exponential to 1. The population does not grow without bound. It settles at

<Formula label="The limiting population" tex="\lim_{t \to \infty} x(t) = x_0\,e^{k_0/a}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 0(d) (our own solution)" />

The reason is that the total growth is limited: $\int_0^\infty k(t)\,dt = k_0/a$ is finite, because $k(t)$ falls off like $1/t^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The factor $e^{k_0/a}$ is more than 1, so the herd ends up larger than it began, as it should: the growth rate stays positive. For a very large $a$ (a virus that acts only very late) the factor is near 1, and for $k_0 = 0$ (no growth at all) it is exactly 1.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The direction field of y′ = y² − x" points={18} mit="Part II, Problem 1">

This problem studies the solutions of

<Formula label="The equation of Problem 1" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = y^2 - x" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1, p. 2" />

None of its solutions can be written with the functions of ordinary calculus, but a lot can be learned from the direction field. MIT asks you to use one of the course's *Mathlets*, the Isoclines mathlet ([MIT's Mathlets page](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/pages/mathlets/); the sheet gives the address math.mit.edu/mathlets/mathlets). The mathlets were Java applets, and a modern computer may not run them. Any other tool that draws direction fields and solution curves will do just as well: a graphing calculator, a notebook with a numerical solver, or an online direction-field plotter. The problem does not depend on the tool.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

On a large pair of axes, with $x$ and $y$ both from $-4$ to $4$, sketch the isoclines of slopes $m = -1$, $m = 0$, $m = 1$ and $m = 2$, and draw a few short line elements on each. Then sketch a couple of solution curves, using only that information. Afterwards, draw the same isoclines in your tool and compare the solution curves with your sketch. (The sheet's instructions for the mathlet name the slopes $-2$, $0$, $1$ and $2$ and its list above names $-1$, $0$, $1$ and $2$: use the list above.)

This part has no answer box: compare your sketch with the solution and mark it yourself.

<Hint>

The isocline of slope $m$ is the set of points where the right side $y^2 - x$ equals $m$. Solve that equation for $x$ in terms of $y$: each isocline is a parabola opening in one direction.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On an isocline all the line elements are parallel. Then draw a solution so that it crosses each isocline with the slope of that isocline: it is steeper where $m$ is large.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equation $y' = y^2 - x = m$ defines the isocline of slope $m$. It is easiest to solve it for $x$, because $x$ appears only once.

<Step title="The isoclines">

Setting $y^2 - x = m$ and solving for $x$:

<Formula label="The isocline of slope m" tex="x = y^2 - m" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

Each isocline is a parabola opening to the right, with its vertex at $(-m, 0)$ on the $x$-axis. For $m = 0$ the vertex is at the origin; for $m = 2$ it is at $(-2, 0)$; for $m = -1$ it is at $(1, 0)$. The line elements along the parabola for $m = 0$ are horizontal, those for $m = 1$ have slope 1, those for $m = 2$ have slope 2, and those for $m = -1$ have slope $-1$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-4, 4]} range={[-4, 4]} xLabel="x" yLabel="y"
  curves={[
    { x: "s^2 + 1", y: "s", range: [-1.7, 1.7], label: "m = −1" },
    { x: "s^2", y: "s", range: [-2, 2], label: "m = 0" },
    { x: "s^2 - 1", y: "s", range: [-2.2, 2.2], label: "m = 1" },
    { x: "s^2 - 2", y: "s", range: [-2.4, 2.4], label: "m = 2" }
  ]}
  caption="The isoclines of y′ = y² − x for the slopes m = −1, 0, 1 and 2, computed from the formula x = y² − m. Each is a parabola opening to the right." />

</Step>

<Step title="The solution curves">

Where $y^2 > x$, that is to the left of the parabola for $m = 0$, all slopes are positive. Where $y^2 < x$, between the two branches of that parabola, the slopes are negative. A solution rises as it moves through the left region, flattens out on the parabola $x = y^2$ (the *nullcline*, where $m = 0$), and where it enters the region on the right of it, it falls. Solutions that stay to the left keep rising steeply: the slope grows like $y^2$, and they shoot up.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At the origin, $y' = 0 - 0 = 0$: the origin is on the isocline $m = 0$, and the line element there is flat. At $(0, 1)$ the slope is $1 - 0 = 1$: it is on the parabola $x = y^2 - 1$, which is the isocline for $m = 1$. [MIT's solutions point out that the printed picture lacks the $m = 1$ isocline.]

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

A *separatrix* is a curve that separates solutions with two different kinds of behaviour. For this equation there is a separatrix such that solutions above it grow without bound as $x$ increases (they have a vertical asymptote), while solutions below it keep going, and eventually fall. With your tool, find the separatrix and sketch it.

Our addition: the box asks which curve the separatrix itself approaches as $x \to +\infty$.

<Hint>

Start solutions at $x = 0$ with different values of $y$, and look at where they end up. Between the ones that blow up and the ones that fall, there is a narrow gap. That gap holds the separatrix.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For large $x$ the separatrix is also a solution that lives for ever, but does not fall. For such a solution $y' = y^2 - x$ cannot be much more than zero and cannot be much less. What does that say about $y^2$ and $x$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="The separatrix is asymptotic, as $x \to +\infty$, to"
  choices={["the upper branch of the nullcline, y = √x", "the lower branch of the nullcline, y = −√x", "the line y = x", "the line y = 0"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow solutions with a numerical solver, starting each at the same $x$ and at different $y$. A solution slightly too high goes to infinity in finite $x$; one slightly too low drops. The separatrix is the borderline solution.

<Step title="What you see">

In your tool, start solutions on the line $x = 0$ with $y$ ranging from $-1$ to $2$, say. Those that start high blow up: they go to infinity at a finite $x$ (a vertical asymptote). Those that start lower rise, reach a maximum where they meet the parabola $x = y^2$ (on its upper half if they start between $y = 0$ and the separatrix, on its lower half if they start below $y = 0$), and then fall and follow its lower half, $y = -\sqrt x$. The borderline solution rises more slowly and stays near the upper half of the parabola, the curve $y = \sqrt x$.

Computed with a numerical solver (our own check, not from the sheet), the separatrix passes through $x = 0$ at about $y = 0.73$ and then follows $y = \sqrt x$ closely.

</Step>

<Step title="Why the upper branch">

Write $y = \sqrt x + u$ with $u$ small. Then $y^2 - x \approx 2\sqrt x\,u$, and the equation says $u' \approx 2\sqrt x\,u - \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}$, because $\sqrt x$ itself rises with slope $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}$. A solution with $u$ a little more than $\dfrac{1}{4x}$ has $u' > 0$ and is pushed further above the curve: it goes up and blows up. One with $u$ a little less is pushed down and falls through the lower region. So the region around the upper half of the nullcline repels, and only the separatrix itself, with $u$ close to $\dfrac{1}{4x}$, can stay there as $x$ grows. [MIT's solutions describe it in the same way: the separatrix lives for ever, has no maximum, and is asymptotic to $y = \sqrt x$.]

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Try $y = \sqrt x$ itself in the equation: the left side is $y' = 1/(2\sqrt x)$ and the right side is $y^2 - x = 0$. They are both small for large $x$, but not equal, so the separatrix is near the curve $y = \sqrt x$ and not equal to it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Suppose a solution $y(x)$ is tangent to the $m = -1$ isocline: it touches the isocline at a point $(a, b)$, and the two curves have the same slope there. Find that point in your tool first, and then calculate $a$ and $b$ exactly. Enter the two numbers as decimals.

<Hint>

On the isocline $m = -1$ the solution has slope $-1$. At the point of tangency the isocline also has slope $-1$. The isocline is the curve $x = y^2 + 1$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiate $x = y^2 + 1$ with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as a function of $x$: $1 = 2y\,y'$. Put $y' = -1$ and find $y$. Then use the isocline to find $x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c-a" label="$a =$" value="1.25" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-c-a" />

<Answer id="p1-c-b" label="$b =$" value="-0.5" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-c-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At the point of tangency the slope of the isocline must be $-1$, which is also the slope of every solution that crosses or touches the isocline $m = -1$.

<Step title="The slope of the isocline">

The isocline is $x = y^2 + 1$. Differentiating with respect to $x$, with $y$ a function of $x$ along the curve (the chain rule gives $d(y^2)/dx = 2y\,y'$):

<Formula label="Slope of the isocline m = −1" tex="1 = 2y\,\frac{dy}{dx}, \qquad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{2y}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The point">

Setting the slope of the isocline equal to $-1$ gives $y = -\tfrac12$. The point is on the isocline, so $x = y^2 + 1 = \tfrac14 + 1$:

<Formula label="The point of tangency" tex="(a, b) = \left(\tfrac54,\ -\tfrac12\right) = (1.25,\ -0.5)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

The lower branch of the isocline is the one with $y < 0$, and that is where the tangency takes place: the upper branch $y > 0$ has positive slope $1/(2y)$ and never reaches $-1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $(1.25, -0.5)$ the equation gives $y' = (-0.5)^2 - 1.25 = 0.25 - 1.25 = -1$, so the point is on the isocline $m = -1$ and a solution through it has slope $-1$, the same as the isocline: a tangency.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Now let $y(x)$ be a solution with $y(a) < b$, where $(a, b)$ is the point of part (c). What happens to it as $x \to \infty$? More exactly: find a function $f(x)$ whose graph $y(x)$ comes closer and closer to as $x$ grows, and say whether, for large $x$, $y(x)$ is above $f(x)$, below it, or whether this depends on $y(a)$.

The box takes $f$ as a constant times a power of $x$. (MIT also accepts $-\sqrt{x + m}$ for any constant $m$, the same curve shifted sideways, which differs from $-\sqrt x$ by an amount that goes to $0$; a single form is easier for a box to check.) The second box is our addition. Then explain, in your own words, why the following two observations are true, and how they give your answer. (MIT's sheet states three observations; its second and third are only loosely worded, and MIT's own solution replaces them by the second statement below. We follow MIT's solution.) These have no box: mark them yourself.

1. The graph of $y(x)$ cannot cross the isocline $m = -1$ at a point with $x > a$.
2. A solution that is above the lower half of the nullcline $y = -\sqrt x$ stays above it; a solution that is below it at some $x$ must eventually reach it.

<Hint>

Use part (c): can the solution cross the isocline $m = -1$ for $x > a$? Where must it be, compared with the nullcline, once $x$ is large?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the lower branches of the isocline $m = -1$ and of the nullcline as functions of $x$. What happens to the distance between them as $x$ grows?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d-f" label="$f(x) =$" expr="-sqrt(x)"
  vars={{ x: [1, 9] }}
  names={{ x: "x" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(x)", hint: "That is the upper branch of the nullcline. The solutions of this part are below the isocline, in the lower half-plane." },
    { expr: "-x", hint: "For $y = -x$ the right side is $y^2 - x = x^2 - x$, which is huge, while the slope of $-x$ is $-1$. These cannot match for large $x$." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-d-side" label="For large $x$, $y(x)$ is"
  choices={["above f(x), whatever y(a) is", "below f(x), whatever y(a) is", "above or below f(x), depending on y(a)", "equal to f(x)"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Trap the solution between two curves that come together: the isocline $m = -1$ from above, and the nullcline from below.

<Step title="The solution stays below the isocline">

Let $y(a) < b$, so the point $(a, y(a))$ is below the lower branch of the isocline $m = -1$, which is $y = -\sqrt{x - 1}$. Suppose the solution did reach this branch at a point with $x > a$, and let it be the first such point. It would arrive from below, so its slope there would be at least the slope of the isocline. The slope of the lower branch is $-1/(2\sqrt{x-1})$ (from $dy/dx = 1/(2y)$ with $y = -\sqrt{x-1}$), which for $x > a = 5/4$ is larger than $-1$, because $\sqrt{x-1} > \tfrac12$ there. But on the isocline $m = -1$ every solution has slope exactly $-1$, which is smaller. The two requirements contradict each other, so the solution never reaches the isocline for $x > a$ (observation 1):

<Formula label="The upper bound" tex="y(x) < -\sqrt{x - 1} \qquad (x > a)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(d)(i) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The solution ends up above the nullcline">

The lower branch of the nullcline is $y = -\sqrt x$. Its slope $-1/(2\sqrt x)$ is negative. First, a solution above it stays above it: to cross it downward it would need a slope at most equal to the (negative) slope of the nullcline, but a solution crossing the nullcline has slope $y^2 - x = 0$. Second, suppose the solution is below the nullcline at some point and never reaches it. Below the nullcline $y^2 > x$, so the slope is positive and $y$ keeps increasing from that point on. But being below the nullcline also means $y < -\sqrt x$, which goes to $-\infty$. A function that keeps increasing cannot stay below $-\sqrt x$ for ever. So it does reach the nullcline, and from then on stays above (observation 2).

<Formula label="The lower bound" tex="y(x) > -\sqrt{x} \qquad (\text{for large } x)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(d)(iii) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Squeeze">

Combining the two bounds, for large $x$

<Formula label="The squeeze" tex="-\sqrt{x} < y(x) < -\sqrt{x-1}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

The two bounds differ by $\sqrt x - \sqrt{x-1} = 1/(\sqrt x + \sqrt{x-1})$, which goes to $0$. So $y(x)$ comes closer and closer to $f(x) = -\sqrt x$, and it stays above it, whatever the value of $y(a)$ was, as long as it was below $b$. [MIT writes $f(x) = -\sqrt x$ "or indeed $-\sqrt{x + m}$ for any $m$": since $\sqrt{x + m} - \sqrt x \to 0$, all of these are asymptotic to the same solutions. MIT then notes that for $m < 0$ some solutions lie below $-\sqrt{x + m}$ for some $x > a$, so with those the comparison would depend on $m$. With the plain $-\sqrt x$ the answer is always "above".]

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A closer look at $y = -\sqrt x + u(x)$ with a small $u$: putting it into $y' = y^2 - x$ gives $u' \approx \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt x} - 2\sqrt x\,u$, so $u \approx \dfrac{1}{4x} > 0$. A numerical solver agrees (our own check): for starting values $y(5/4) = -0.6, -1, -2, -4, -8$ the difference $y(x) + \sqrt x$ at $x = 100$ is $0.0025$ in every case, which is $1/(4\cdot 100)$ to four decimal places. The solution lies above $-\sqrt x$ by about $1/(4x)$, and the starting value no longer matters.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## More practice: MIT's own solved exercises

Part I of MIT's Problem Set 1 is worth 23 points at MIT and is not scored here. It lists routine exercises. Some come from the textbook of the course (Edwards and Penney), which is not openly licensed and is not used on this site, so they are not part of this page. The others come from MIT's *Notes and Exercises*, which is on MIT OpenCourseWare under the same licence as the rest of the course, with solutions. They are not rewritten here: open [the exercises of section 1, first-order ODE's](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/resources/mit18_03s10_1ex/), try them, then compare with [MIT's solutions](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/resources/mit18_03s10_1sol/).
