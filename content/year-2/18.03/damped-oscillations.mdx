Oscillations come from complex roots of the characteristic equation (transcript, p. 1). The lecture first clears up two questions about [the complex case](/courses/18-03/second-order-constant-coefficients), then studies what damped oscillations look like.

## What about the other root?

For roots $a \pm bi$, the root $a + bi$ gave the real solutions $y_1 = e^{at}\cos bt$ and $y_2 = e^{at}\sin bt$ (transcript, p. 1). Using $a - bi$ instead changes $\sin bt$ to $\sin(-bt) = -\sin bt$ and leaves the cosine alone, so it gives $y_1$ and $-y_2$. In $c_1y_1 + c_2y_2$ that only replaces $c_2$ by $-c_2$, which is just as arbitrary: **nothing new** (p. 2).

## The complex form of the solution

Many engineers and physicists write the general solution with both roots (transcript, pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Complex form" tex="y = C_1\,e^{(a + bi)t} + C_2\,e^{(a - bi)t}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 2–3" />

Capital letters, because $C_1$ and $C_2$ must be allowed to be complex to get real solutions out of complex functions (pp. 3–4). Which of these are real? One could multiply everything out and set the imaginary part to zero, which works but fills a board (p. 3). Better: **an expression is real exactly when changing $i$ to $-i$ leaves it unchanged** (p. 4). That change turns each constant into its complex conjugate and swaps the two exponentials (pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="Changing i to −i" tex="\bar C_1\,e^{(a - bi)t} + \bar C_2\,e^{(a + bi)t}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 4–5" />

For this to equal the original, $C_2 = \bar C_1$; the other condition, $\bar C_2 = C_1$, says the same thing (p. 5). So the real solutions are (p. 5)

<Formula label="Real solutions in complex form" tex="y = (C + id)\,e^{(a + bi)t} + (C - id)\,e^{(a - bi)t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

with $C$ and $d$ real: the second term is the conjugate of the first. This form is convenient later, for Fourier series (p. 6).

**Back to sines and cosines** (pp. 6–8). Pull out the real factor $e^{at}$ and group by the constants: $e^{at}\left[C(e^{ibt} + e^{-ibt}) + id(e^{ibt} - e^{-ibt})\right]$. Now use Euler's formula read backwards, which should be known even better than Euler's formula itself (p. 7):

<Formula label="Euler's formula backwards" tex="\cos\alpha = \frac{e^{i\alpha} + e^{-i\alpha}}{2}, \qquad \sin\alpha = \frac{e^{i\alpha} - e^{-i\alpha}}{2i}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

(Do not forget the $i$ in the sine.) The first bracket is $2\cos bt$ and the second $2i\sin bt$, so (pp. 7–8)

<Formula label="The same solution, real form" tex="y = e^{at}\,(2C\cos bt - 2d\sin bt)" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

which is the old form, since $2C$ and $-2d$ are just as arbitrary. Both forms have two real arbitrary constants (p. 8).

## The spring equation in standard form

For the spring–mass–dashpot $mx'' + cx' + kx = 0$, divide by $m$ and rename the constants (transcript, pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Standard form" tex="y'' + 2p\,y' + \omega_0^2\,y = 0, \qquad 2p = \frac{c}{m}, \quad \omega_0^2 = \frac{k}{m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

The factor 2 is there to avoid twos and fours in the quadratic formula (p. 10), and $\omega_0^2$ is positive, so it is the square of something. The characteristic equation $r^2 + 2pr + \omega_0^2 = 0$ has roots (pp. 9–10)

<Formula label="Characteristic roots" tex="r = -p \pm \sqrt{p^2 - \omega_0^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

**Undamped** ($p = 0$, no dashpot): $y'' + \omega_0^2y = 0$, simple harmonic motion. The roots are $\pm i\omega_0$, and (pp. 10–11)

<Formula label="Undamped oscillation" tex="y = c_1\cos\omega_0t + c_2\sin\omega_0t = A\cos(\omega_0t - \phi)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11" />

a pure oscillation whose angular frequency $\omega_0$ can be read straight off the equation; that is why the constant was called $\omega_0^2$ rather than $k/m$ (pp. 10–11).

## Damped oscillations

The roots are genuinely complex when $p^2 - \omega_0^2 < 0$, that is (all constants positive) when $p < \omega_0$ (transcript, p. 11). The solution then oscillates inside the guiding curves $\pm e^{-pt}$ (pp. 11–12). It crosses the axis at regular intervals; call the spacing between crossings $\pi/\omega_1$, so a full "period" is $2\pi/\omega_1$. It is not truly periodic, since the amplitude keeps shrinking, so $\omega_1$ is called the **pseudo-frequency** (pp. 12–13).

**Question** (p. 13): if the damping goes up, does the pseudo-frequency go up or down? Intuitively, down. To see it, write the roots as $-p \pm \sqrt{-(\omega_0^2 - p^2)} = -p \pm i\,\omega_1$ (p. 13), with

<Formula label="Pseudo-frequency" tex="\omega_1^2 = \omega_0^2 - p^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 15" />

Then (pp. 13–14)

<Formula label="Damped oscillation" tex="y = e^{-pt}\,(c_1\cos\omega_1t + c_2\sin\omega_1t) = A\,e^{-pt}\cos(\omega_1t - \phi)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 13–14" />

This $\omega_1$ is the same one as in the picture: if $\omega_1t_1 - \phi = \pi/2$, the next downward crossing comes when the angle has grown by $2\pi$, at $t_1 + 2\pi/\omega_1$ (p. 14). The relation $\omega_1^2 + p^2 = \omega_0^2$ is Pythagoras: a right triangle with hypotenuse $\omega_0$ and legs $\omega_1$ and $p$ (p. 15). So more damping (larger $p$) means a smaller $\omega_1$, as guessed.

**Four parameters** (pp. 15–16). $p$ (about $c/2m$) sets how fast the envelope comes down, and $\omega_1$ how fast the curve bobs up and down; both depend only on the equation: on the damping, the mass and the spring. $A$ and $\phi$ depend on the initial conditions (p. 15). (The transcript starts to name $\phi$ as one of the constants fixed by the equation, then breaks off.)

<WorkedExample title="A damped spring">
<Problem>
A 2 kg mass hangs on a spring with $k = 50$ N/m, damped by a dashpot with $c = 0.8$ kg/s. (a) Find $p$, $\omega_0$ and $\omega_1$. (b) Compare the pseudo-period with the undamped period. (c) By what factor has the envelope shrunk after 10 s? (d) What damping constant would make the motion critically damped?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The constants">
$p = c/2m = 0.2$ per second, $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{25} = 5$ rad/s, and $\omega_1 = \sqrt{25 - 0.04} \approx 4.996$ rad/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Periods">
Pseudo-period $2\pi/\omega_1 \approx 1.2576$ s against $2\pi/\omega_0 \approx 1.2566$ s: light damping barely slows the oscillation.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The envelope">
$e^{-pt} = e^{-2} \approx 0.135$: after 10 s the swings are about 13.5% of their starting size.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Critical damping">
Oscillation stops when $p = \omega_0$, i.e. $c = 2m\omega_0 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 5 = 20$ kg/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The second complex root gives nothing new. Complex form: $C_1e^{(a + bi)t} + C_2e^{(a - bi)t}$ is real exactly when $C_2 = \bar C_1$.
- Euler backwards: $\cos\alpha = (e^{i\alpha} + e^{-i\alpha})/2$, $\sin\alpha = (e^{i\alpha} - e^{-i\alpha})/2i$.
- $y'' + 2py' + \omega_0^2y = 0$: undamped motion has angular frequency $\omega_0$.
- Damped, $p < \omega_0$: $Ae^{-pt}\cos(\omega_1t - \phi)$ with $\omega_1 = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - p^2}$, falling as damping grows.
