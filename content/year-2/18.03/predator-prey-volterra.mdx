The last lecture of the series works out one nonlinear example that shows three things worth knowing about nonlinear systems (transcript, p. 1). It brings together [limit cycles and nonlinear systems](/courses/18-03/limit-cycles) with the [first day of the course](/courses/18-03/direction-fields).

## From a system to a first-order equation

For an autonomous system the right-hand sides contain no $t$, and the $t$ on the left is only in the denominators; so divide one equation by the other (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Eliminating t" tex="\frac{dx}{dt} = f(x, y), \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = g(x, y) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{g(x, y)}{f(x, y)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

That is the equation of the first day of the term (p. 1). Information is lost: without $t$, there is no speed and no direction of travel. Each arrow of the velocity field is snipped to a standard length, pointy end and all, and becomes a line element of a **direction field**; each trajectory becomes an **integral curve**, the graph of an explicit or implicit solution, which only has the right slope (p. 1). The gain: the first-order equation may be solvable, where the system almost certainly is not (p. 1).

**Example** (p. 2). $x' = y$, $y' = -x$ gives $\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y}$, separable: $y\,dy = -x\,dx$, so $x^2 + y^2 = C$, the circles already known; eliminating $t$ from the solutions $x = c_1\cos t$, $y = -c_1\sin t$ gives the same (p. 2). In the lecture's words, borrowing T. S. Eliot, in my end is my beginning (p. 2).

## Sharks and food fish

Predators $x$ ("sharx") eat prey $y$ ("yumfish"); $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ are positive constants (transcript, p. 2):

<Formula label="Volterra's predator–prey system" tex="x' = -ax + bxy, \qquad y' = cy - dxy" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The sharks die out without food fish; the food fish grow exponentially without sharks. Unlike the robin–earthworm homework model, the predators have nothing else to eat and the prey's growth is not logistic (p. 2).

**Critical points** (p. 2). $x(-a + by) = 0$ and $y(c - dx) = 0$ give $(0, 0)$ and $\left(\frac{c}{d}, \frac{a}{b}\right)$. At the origin the linearization is $\begin{pmatrix} -a & 0 \\ 0 & c \end{pmatrix}$; for a diagonal matrix, read the eigenvalues off the diagonal: $-a$ and $c$, opposite signs, a **saddle** (pp. 2–3). Along the shark axis the sharks die out; along the food-fish axis the fish grow (p. 3).

**The interesting point.** To save writing, set $a = b = c = d = 1$: $x' = -x + xy$, $y' = y - xy$ (p. 3). The Jacobian at $(1, 1)$ is (p. 3)

<Formula label="Linearization at (1, 1)" tex="J = \begin{pmatrix} -1 + y & x \\ -y & 1 - x \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(1, 1) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

the matrix of the concentric circles: a **centre** (p. 3).

## Trouble: a borderline case

In the trace–determinant picture the centres lie on a single line, the boundary between spiral sinks and spiral sources (transcript, p. 3). Wiggle the coefficients a little and the type changes. Since the linearization only approximates the nonlinear system, it cannot decide: the true critical point could be a centre, a spiral sink or a spiral source, though not a saddle or a node, which are too far away (p. 3).

## Volterra's way out

Volterra, who introduced and studied these equations, eliminated $t$ (transcript, p. 3). (Lotka had published the same equations a few years earlier; they are usually called the Lotka–Volterra equations.)

<Formula label="The first-order equation" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y(1 - x)}{x(y - 1)}, \qquad \frac{y - 1}{y}\,dy = \frac{1 - x}{x}\,dx" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–4" />

No partial fractions are needed: divide first (p. 4). Integrating, $y - \ln y = \ln x - x + c_1$, and exponentiating (p. 4):

<Formula label="Integral curves" tex="x\,e^{-x}\cdot y\,e^{-y} = C" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

These are the **contour curves** of $h(x, y) = xe^{-x}\,ye^{-y}$ (p. 4). The one-variable function $ue^{-u}$ starts at 0, rises like $u$, has a single maximum $\frac1e$ at $u = 1$, and tends to 0 by L'Hôpital's rule (p. 4). So $h$ is a single mountain with its peak at the critical point $(1, 1)$ and value 0 on the axes; its contour curves are closed curves around the peak. They cannot be spirals: a horizontal line meets each contour at most twice, as it meets the graph of $ue^{-u}$ (p. 4).

<Plot variable="u" domain={[0, 6]} range={[0, 0.45]} xLabel="u" yLabel="u e^(−u)"
  curves={[{ expr: "u*exp(-u)", label: "u e^(−u)" }]}
  points={[{ x: 1, y: 0.3679, label: "max 1/e" }]}
  caption="The factor u e^(−u) of Volterra's conserved quantity, computed from the formula: a single maximum 1/e at u = 1, so h(x, y) = x e^(−x) · y e^(−y) is a single peak at (1, 1) whose contour curves are closed." />

So $(1, 1)$ is a genuine centre and the populations **cycle** (p. 4). The direction is clockwise: sharks die out on their axis and fish grow on theirs, and neighbouring curves must agree (p. 4). Many sharks eat the fish down; the sharks then starve; with few sharks left the fish multiply; the sharks find them again and multiply; and the cycle repeats (p. 4).

## Fishing: Volterra's principle

Now fish with nets at a constant rate $k$, removing the same fraction of sharks and food fish (transcript, pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="With fishing" tex="x' = -(a + k)\,x + bxy, \qquad y' = (c - k)\,y - dxy" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

Only the coefficients change, so the new critical point is (p. 5)

<Formula label="New equilibrium" tex="\left(\frac{c - k}{d},\; \frac{a + k}{b}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

(The transcript first writes $d$ for the second denominator, then $b$.) The critical point gives the average populations over a cycle (as long as $k < c$, so that it stays in the first quadrant). Fishing **lowers the sharks and raises the food fish**: not intuitive, but observed, in a slightly different context, and the reason Volterra took up the problem. This is **Volterra's principle** (p. 5). A modern application, as the lecture tells it (p. 5): in the 1950s, stagnant ponds were sprayed with DDT to kill mosquito larvae. It also killed the fish, which prey on the larvae, and like harvesting both, it moved the equilibrium towards more mosquitoes and fewer fish, as was indeed observed. The same happened with birds and insects when trees were sprayed (p. 5).

<WorkedExample title="A different predator and prey">
<Problem>
For $x' = -2x + xy$, $y' = 3y - xy$: (a) find the critical points and linearize at the interior one; (b) find the conserved quantity; (c) find the equilibrium with fishing rate $k = 1$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Critical points">
$x(-2 + y) = 0$ and $y(3 - x) = 0$ give $(0, 0)$ and $(3, 2)$. The Jacobian $\begin{pmatrix} -2 + y & x \\ -y & 3 - x \end{pmatrix}$ at $(3, 2)$ is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ -2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$: $\lambda^2 + 6 = 0$, $\lambda = \pm i\sqrt6$, a centre for the linearization, undecided for the system.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Eliminate t">
$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y(3 - x)}{x(y - 2)}$, so $\frac{y - 2}{y}dy = \frac{3 - x}{x}dx$ and $y - 2\ln y = 3\ln x - x + c$. Exponentiating: $x^3e^{-x}\cdot y^2e^{-y} = C$, with a single peak at $(3, 2)$, since $u^3e^{-u}$ peaks at 3 and $u^2e^{-u}$ at 2. So the orbits are closed: a true centre.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Fishing">
With $a = 2$, $b = 1$, $c = 3$, $d = 1$, $k = 1$: the equilibrium moves from $(3, 2)$ to $\left(\frac{3 - 1}{1}, \frac{2 + 1}{1}\right) = (2, 3)$: fewer predators, more prey.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Eliminating $t$ turns $x' = f$, $y' = g$ into $dy/dx = g/f$: direction field and integral curves, without speed, but possibly solvable.
- Volterra's model: saddle at the origin, linearization a centre at $(c/d, a/b)$, a borderline case.
- Eliminating $t$ gives the closed contour curves of $xe^{-x}\,ye^{-y}$ (with $a = b = c = d = 1$): the populations cycle clockwise.
- Fishing at rate $k$ moves the equilibrium to $((c - k)/d, (a + k)/b)$: Volterra's principle.
