After two lectures without solving a single equation, 18.03 now solves one of the most important kinds: the **first-order linear equation**, important because it can always be solved and because it turns up in many models (transcript, p. 1). With [separation of variables](/courses/18-01/separation-of-variables) it is one of the two basic methods.

## Linear equations and the standard form

A first-order equation is **linear** when it is linear in $y$ and $y'$; $x$ may appear in any way (transcript, pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="First-order linear equation" tex="a(x)\,y' + b(x)\,y = c(x)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

like a linear equation $a_1y_1 + a_2y_2 = c$ in two variables. If the right side is zero it is **homogeneous** (p. 2). Dividing by $a(x)$ gives the **standard linear form** (p. 2):

<Formula label="Standard linear form" tex="y' + p(x)\,y = q(x)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The equation could also be written in the general first-order form $y' = -p(x)y + q(x)$, but this course always uses the linear form: getting the sign of $p$ wrong gives disastrously wrong answers (pp. 2–3).

## Models

Linear equations model temperature and concentration (conduction and diffusion), mixing, radioactive decay, bank interest, and some motion problems such as a rocket (transcript, pp. 3–4).

**Conduction.** A metal box with partly insulated walls sits in a water bath at temperature $T_e$; its inside has temperature $T$. Heat flows in by conduction at a rate proportional to the temperature difference, **Newton's law of cooling** (pp. 4–6):

<Formula label="Newton's law of cooling" tex="\frac{dT}{dt} = k\,(T_e - T)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 5–6" />

Write the difference this way round so that the **conductivity** $k$ is positive: if the outside is warmer, $T$ must rise (p. 6). An initial condition $T(0) = T_0$ completes the problem (p. 6).

**Diffusion.** The same equation with concentrations: a salt solution of concentration $C$ inside a semi-permeable membrane, in a bath of concentration $C_e$, gives $dC/dt = k_1(C_e - C)$ (pp. 6–7). One equation handles both situations: that is the power of the method (pp. 7–8). In standard linear form the cooling law is $dT/dt + kT = kT_e$, and if both $k$ and $T_e$ are allowed to vary with time it is a general linear equation (p. 8).

## The integrating factor

To solve $y' + py = q$, multiply both sides by a function $u(x)$ chosen so that the left side becomes the derivative of a product (transcript, pp. 9–10):

<Formula label="Choosing the integrating factor" tex="uy' + puy = (uy)' \quad\text{if}\quad u' = pu" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

Separating variables, $du/u = p\,dx$, so $\ln u = \int p\,dx$, and (pp. 10–11)

<Formula label="Integrating factor" tex="u = e^{\int p(x)\,dx}" cite="reading1" at="p. 11" />

No arbitrary constant is needed: one integrating factor is enough (p. 11). **The method** (pp. 11–12): put the equation in standard linear form; find $u$; multiply **both** sides by $u$; then integrate, $uy = \int uq\,dx + C$. The common mistakes: not using the standard form, and forgetting to multiply the right side $q$ by $u$ (pp. 13–14).

**Example 1.** $xy' - y = x^3$. In standard form $y' - \frac1x y = x^2$, so $u = e^{-\ln x} = 1/x$ (pp. 12–13). Multiplying both sides, $\frac1x y' - \frac{1}{x^2}y = x$, and the left side is $\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)'$, which is worth checking with the product rule (pp. 12–13). Integrating, $y/x = \frac{x^2}{2} + C$, so (p. 13)

<Formula label="Example 1" tex="y = \frac{x^3}{2} + Cx" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

(On $x > 0$, where $\ln x$ is defined. Mattuck hesitates between $x^3$ and $x^2$ on the right of the original equation; his working, with $x^2$ in the standard form, belongs to $x^3$, used here.)

**Example 2.** $(1 + \cos x)\,y' - (\sin x)\,y = 2x$. In standard form $p = -\sin x/(1 + \cos x)$, whose integral is $\ln(1 + \cos x)$, so $u = 1 + \cos x$, and multiplying by $u$ gives back the original equation (pp. 13–14). That is not wasted work: the left side is now known to be $\big((1 + \cos x)\,y\big)'$ (p. 15). So $(1 + \cos x)\,y = x^2 + C$, and with $y(0) = 1$, $C = 2$ (p. 15):

<Formula label="Example 2" tex="y = \frac{x^2 + 2}{1 + \cos x}" cite="reading1" at="p. 15" />

valid on $-\pi < x < \pi$: the solution blows up at $x = \pm\pi$, where $1 + \cos x = 0$.

## Constant coefficients: steady state and transient

For the cooling law with constant $k$, $T' + kT = kT_e$; note the $k$ on the right, which units demand (transcript, p. 16). The integrating factor is $e^{kt}$, so $(e^{kt}T)' = kT_e e^{kt}$ and (pp. 16–17)

<Formula label="Solution of the cooling law" tex="T = e^{-kt}\int k\,T_e(t)\,e^{kt}\,dt + C\,e^{-kt}" cite="reading1" at="p. 17" />

With an initial value it is cleaner to use a definite integral from 0 to $t$, so that $C = T_0$ (p. 17). Because $k > 0$, the term $Ce^{-kt}$ dies away as $t \to \infty$: it is the **transient**. What remains is the **steady-state** solution, the same whatever the initial temperature: in the long run the initial condition makes no difference (pp. 17–18).

<WorkedExample title="A cooling cup of coffee">
<Problem>
Coffee at 90 °C stands in a room at 20 °C and obeys Newton's law with $k = 0.05$ per minute. Find $T(t)$, the temperature after 20 minutes, and when it reaches 40 °C.
</Problem>
<Step title="Solve">
$T' + 0.05\,T = 0.05 \times 20$. With $u = e^{0.05t}$: $(e^{0.05t}T)' = e^{0.05t}$, so $e^{0.05t}T = 20e^{0.05t} + C$ and $T = 20 + Ce^{-0.05t}$. $T(0) = 90$ gives $C = 70$.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
$T(20) = 20 + 70e^{-1} \approx 45.8$ °C. $T = 40$ when $e^{-0.05t} = 2/7$, $t = \ln(3.5)/0.05 \approx 25.1$ minutes.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Linear: $a(x)y' + b(x)y = c(x)$; standard linear form $y' + p(x)y = q(x)$.
- Integrating factor $u = e^{\int p\,dx}$; multiply both sides, then $uy = \int uq\,dx + C$.
- Newton's law of cooling $dT/dt = k(T_e - T)$ and the diffusion law are linear, with $k > 0$.
- Constant $k$: solution = steady state + transient $Ce^{-kt}$, which dies away.
