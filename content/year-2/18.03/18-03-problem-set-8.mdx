## Problem 29

<Exercise id="p29" title="Poles" points={26} mit="Part II, Problem 29">

Think of the variable $s$ of a Laplace transform $F(s)$ as a complex number. A **pole** of $F(s)$ is a complex number at which $F$ becomes infinite. For a quotient of two polynomials without common factors, the poles are the roots of the denominator. The **pole diagram** of $F(s)$ is the complex plane with the poles of $F$ marked on it. This page writes a pole diagram as the set of its poles: the diagram of $\dfrac{1}{s^2 + 1}$ is $\{i, -i\}$. (These definitions follow MIT's Supplementary Notes, section 22.1.)

The same pole diagram belongs to many functions. For example, an exponential factor such as $e^{-s}$ is never infinite and never zero, so it neither adds a pole nor removes one.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

For each of the four pole diagrams below, (i) describe what all functions $f(t)$ whose Laplace transforms have that pole diagram have in common, and (ii) write down two examples of such an $f(t)$, each with its $F(s)$.

1. $\{1,\ i,\ -i\}$
2. $\{-1 + 4i,\ -1 - 4i\}$
3. $\{-1\}$
4. The empty diagram: no poles at all.

The four boxes test one piece of each diagram. Your descriptions and your other examples have no box: compare them with the solution.

<Hint>

Start from examples. Which function of $t$ has the transform $\dfrac{1}{s - z}$ for a complex number $z$? What do the real part and the imaginary part of $z$ do to that function as $t$ grows?

</Hint>

<Hint>

If there are several poles, the function behaves for large $t$ like a sum of such terms, and the pole furthest to the right wins in the long run. For the empty diagram, think of a function that is switched off after some time, and compute its transform from the defining integral.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p29-a-4" label="Diagram 4. The box function $f(t) = u(t) - u(t - b)$ with $b > 0$ has, for $s \ne 0$, $F(s) =$" expr="(1 - exp(-b*s))/s"

<Solution>

**The idea.** A term $\dfrac{c}{s - z}$ in $F(s)$ is the transform of $c\,e^{zt}$. If $z = a + i\omega$, this is $e^{at}$ times an oscillation of circular frequency $\omega$ (angular frequency, in radians per unit of time). So the real part of a pole is a rate of growth or decay, and the imaginary part is a circular frequency. For large $t$ the pole furthest to the right decides how fast the function grows or decays.

<Step title="Diagram 1: {1, i, −i}">

The pole at $1$ gives growth like $e^{t}$. MIT makes this precise: for any numbers $a < 1 < c$, the size of $f(t)$ lies between $e^{at}$ and $e^{ct}$ once $t$ is large. The poles at $\pm i$ add an oscillation of circular frequency $1$ whose amplitude stays about the same, so beside the growing part it becomes small. MIT's example, with $a$ and $b$ not zero:

<Formula label="Example for diagram 1" tex="f(t) = a\,e^{t} + b\sin t, \qquad F(s) = \frac{a}{s - 1} + \frac{b}{s^2 + 1}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 29(a)(1), p. 1" />

A second example, of our own: $f(t) = e^{t} + \cos t$ with $F(s) = \dfrac{1}{s - 1} + \dfrac{s}{s^2 + 1}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Diagram 2: {−1 + 4i, −1 − 4i}">

The real part $-1$ gives decay like $e^{-t}$, and the imaginary part $\pm 4$ gives an oscillation of circular frequency $4$. MIT's example, with $a \ne 0$:

<Formula label="Example for diagram 2" tex="f(t) = a\,e^{-t}\sin 4t, \qquad F(s) = \frac{4a}{(s + 1)^2 + 16}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 29(a)(2), p. 1" />

The denominator $(s + 1)^2 + 16$ is zero for $s + 1 = \pm 4i$, as it should be. A second example, of our own: $f(t) = e^{-t}\cos 4t$ with $F(s) = \dfrac{s + 1}{(s + 1)^2 + 16}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Diagram 3: {−1}">

One real pole: decay like $e^{-t}$ and no oscillation.

<Formula label="Example for diagram 3" tex="f(t) = a\,e^{-t}, \qquad F(s) = \frac{a}{s + 1} \qquad (a \ne 0)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 29(a)(3), p. 1" />

A second example, of our own: the delayed function $f(t) = u(t - 1)\,e^{-(t - 1)}$ with $F(s) = \dfrac{e^{-s}}{s + 1}$. The exponential factor changes the function early on (it is zero until $t = 1$), but not the pole and not the behaviour for large $t$. In the box, $e^{-t} + 1$ has the poles $\{-1, 0\}$, $e^{-t}\cos t$ has $\{-1 + i, -1 - i\}$, and $e^{t}$ has $\{1\}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Diagram 4: no poles">

MIT's answer: for large $t$ these functions go to zero faster than any exponential. MIT's two examples are a delayed delta function and a box, with $a \ne 0$:

<Formula label="Examples for the empty diagram" tex="f(t) = a\,\delta(t - b),\quad F(s) = a\,e^{-bs}\quad (b \ge 0); \qquad f(t) = a\left(u(t) - u(t - b)\right),\quad F(s) = a\,\frac{1 - e^{-bs}}{s}\quad (b > 0)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 29(a)(4), p. 1" />

(MIT's solution prints the first transform as $a\,e^{-bt}$, a slip for $a\,e^{-bs}$.) Both examples are zero after the time $b$. The second transform looks as if it had a pole at $s = 0$, but the numerator $1 - e^{-bs}$ is zero there too: near $s = 0$ it is about $bs$, so $F(s)$ is close to $ab$, a finite number.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A number for the box of diagram 2: at $s = 1$ the formula gives $\dfrac{4}{4 + 16} = 0.2$, and so does the defining integral $\int_0^{\infty}e^{-t}\sin(4t)\,e^{-t}\,dt$, computed numerically. For the box function with $b = 1$ and $s = 1$: $1 - e^{-1} = 0.6321$, the same number as in Problem 26(c) of Problem Set 7, which was this function.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b (i)" points={4}>

A mechanical system is found at an archaeological dig in Ethiopia. The investigators do not want to break it open, so they give it a unit impulse and watch. The motion in response to the unit impulse turns out to be

<Formula label="The measured unit impulse response" tex="w(t) = u(t)\,e^{-t/2}\sin\frac{3t}{2}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 29(b), p. 1" />

Take the system to be of second order, $p(D)x = m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx$, as MIT's solution does. What is its characteristic polynomial $p(s)$? What is its transfer function $W(s)$?

<Hint>

There are two ways. The transfer function is the Laplace transform of the unit impulse response, and $W(s) = \dfrac{1}{p(s)}$. Or: after the kick, $w$ solves the homogeneous equation, so its exponent and its frequency tell you the roots of $p$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The roots fix $p(s)$ only up to a constant factor. For that factor, remember what a unit impulse does to a second-order system $m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx$: the velocity jumps by $\dfrac{1}{m}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p29-b-i-p" label="$p(s) =$" expr="(2/3)*(s^2 + s + 5/2)"
  vars={{ s: [0.3, 3] }}
  names={{ s: "s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "s^2 + s + 5/2", hint: "The roots are right, but the constant factor is not. With leading coefficient 1 the velocity just after the kick would be 1. Differentiate w and see what it is." },
    { expr: "(3/2)*(s^2 + s + 5/2)", hint: "The velocity just after the kick is 1/m, not m. Turn your factor upside down." },
    { expr: "(2/3)*(s^2 + s + 9/4)", hint: "Your roots are not −1/2 ± 3i/2. The constant term of (s + 1/2)² + 9/4 has two pieces." },
    { expr: "(2/3)*(s^2 - s + 5/2)", hint: "Your roots have the real part +1/2, which belongs to a growing response. This one decays like e^(−t/2)." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p29-b-i-w" label="$W(s) =$" expr="(3/2)/(s^2 + s + 5/2)"
  vars={{ s: [0.3, 3] }}
  names={{ s: "s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/(s^2 + s + 5/2)", hint: "That is the transform of (2/3)·e^(−t/2)·sin(3t/2). The transform of sin(ωt) has ω above." },
    { expr: "(3/2)/(s^2 + 9/4)", hint: "That is the transform of sin(3t/2) alone. The factor e^(−t/2) shifts the transform: s becomes s + 1/2." },
    { expr: "(2/3)/(s^2 + s + 5/2)", hint: "The constant above is the circular frequency 3/2 of the sine, not its reciprocal." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The unit impulse response carries everything about the system: its Laplace transform is the transfer function, and the transfer function is $1/p(s)$.

<Step title="The quick way: transform w">

With $\mathcal{L}(\sin\omega t) = \dfrac{\omega}{s^2 + \omega^2}$ and the exponential shift rule (the factor $e^{-t/2}$ turns $s$ into $s + \frac12$):

<Formula label="Transfer function" tex="W(s) = \mathcal{L}(w) = \frac{3/2}{\left(s + \frac12\right)^2 + \frac94} = \frac{3/2}{s^2 + s + \frac52} = \frac{1}{\frac23\left(s^2 + s + \frac52\right)}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 29(b)(i), Method II, p. 1" />

<Formula label="Characteristic polynomial" tex="p(s) = \frac{1}{W(s)} = \frac23\left(s^2 + s + \frac52\right) = \frac23\,s^2 + \frac23\,s + \frac53" cite="solutions" at="Problem 29(b)(i), Method II, p. 1" />

So the system behaves like a mass $m = \frac23$ on a damper $b = \frac23$ and a spring $k = \frac53$, with $b/m = 1$ and $k/m = \frac52$.

</Step>

<Step title="The other way: roots and the kick">

For $t > 0$ the response solves the homogeneous equation. A solution $e^{-t/2}\sin\frac{3t}{2}$ means the roots $-\frac12 \pm \frac32 i$, so

<Formula label="From the roots" tex="p(s) = m\left(s + \frac12 - \frac32 i\right)\left(s + \frac12 + \frac32 i\right) = m\left(s^2 + s + \frac52\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 29(b)(i), Method I, p. 1" />

A unit impulse makes the velocity of a second-order system jump from $0$ to $\dfrac{1}{m}$ ([Problem Set 6](/courses/18-03/18-03-problem-set-6) and the check of Problem 28(a) in Problem Set 7). For $t > 0$,

<Formula label="The velocity after the kick" tex="\dot w(t) = e^{-t/2}\left(\frac32\cos\frac{3t}{2} - \frac12\sin\frac{3t}{2}\right), \qquad \dot w(0^+) = \frac32 = \frac{1}{m}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 29(b)(i), Method I, p. 1" />

so $m = \frac23$, the same as before. (MIT's printed line for $\dot w$ has a doubled minus sign in the sine term; the value at $0^+$ does not depend on it.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $s = 1$: $W(1) = \dfrac{3/2}{1 + 1 + 5/2} = \dfrac13$, and the defining integral $\int_0^{\infty}e^{-t/2}\sin\frac{3t}{2}\,e^{-t}\,dt$, computed numerically, gives $0.3333$ as well. And $p(1) = \frac23\cdot\frac92 = 3$, the reciprocal.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b (iii)" points={4}>

The team wants to move the artifact to a museum by truck. The vibrations of the truck will make the system vibrate, and the team wants to avoid the circular frequency at which the response of the system has the largest amplitude. Which circular frequency $\omega_r$ should the truck avoid? (MIT's sheet numbers this part (ii) a second time; its solutions call it (iii).)

Give $\omega_r$ to three decimal places.

<Hint>

MIT calls this a throwback problem: it is practical resonance, as in Problem 16 of [Problem Set 4](/courses/18-03/18-03-problem-set-4). For a sinusoidal input of circular frequency $\omega$ the gain is $|W(i\omega)|$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The gain is largest where the square of the size of its denominator, $\left(\frac52 - \omega^2\right)^2 + \omega^2$, is smallest. Differentiate with respect to $\omega$, or with respect to $\omega^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p29-b-iii" label="$\omega_r =$" value="1.414" tolerance="0.005" calc="p29-b-iii" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For the input $e^{i\omega t}$ the steady response is $W(i\omega)\,e^{i\omega t}$, so $W(i\omega)$ is the complex gain and its size is the gain. Find the $\omega$ that makes the gain largest.

<Step title="The gain">

<Formula label="Complex gain and gain" tex="W(i\omega) = \frac{3/2}{\left(\frac52 - \omega^2\right) + i\omega}, \qquad |W(i\omega)| = \frac{3/2}{\sqrt{\left(\frac52 - \omega^2\right)^2 + \omega^2}}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 29(b)(iii), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Minimise the denominator">

The gain is largest where $\left(\frac52 - \omega^2\right)^2 + \omega^2$ is smallest. Its derivative with respect to $\omega$ is

<Formula label="Derivative of the squared denominator" tex="2\left(\frac52 - \omega^2\right)(-2\omega) + 2\omega = 2\omega\left(2\omega^2 - 4\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 29(b)(iii), p. 1" />

which is zero at $\omega = 0$ and at $\omega = \pm\sqrt2$. Between $0$ and $\sqrt2$ the derivative is negative and beyond $\sqrt2$ it is positive, so $\sqrt2$ is the minimum of the denominator and the maximum of the gain:

<Formula label="The frequency to avoid" tex="\omega_r = \sqrt2 = 1.414" cite="solutions" at="Problem 29(b)(iii), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The formula of [the lesson](/courses/18-03/resonance-and-beats), $\omega_r = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2p^2}$ for $\ddot x + 2p\,\dot x + \omega_0^2x$, gives the same with $\omega_0^2 = \frac52$ and $p = \frac12$: $\sqrt{\frac52 - \frac12} = \sqrt2$. Note that $\omega_r = 1.414$ is neither the circular frequency $1.5$ of the impulse response nor the natural circular frequency $\sqrt{5/2} = 1.581$ of the system without damping: it lies below both. The gain there is $\dfrac{3/2}{\sqrt{\frac14 + 2}} = 1$, against $0.6$ at $\omega = 0$.

</Step>

  choices={["spiral into the origin, turning clockwise", "spiral into the origin, turning counterclockwise", "spiral away from the origin, turning clockwise", "run around the origin on closed curves"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in part (d), the point gives two starting values. Because the roots are complex, the solution oscillates while it decays, and the trajectory winds around the origin.

<Step title="The solution through (0, 1)">

With $x = e^{-t/2}\left(c_1\cos\frac{3t}{2} + c_2\sin\frac{3t}{2}\right)$ we have $x(0) = c_1$ and $\dot x(0) = -\frac12c_1 + \frac32c_2$. So $c_1 = 0$ and $c_2 = \frac23$:

<Formula label="The solution through (0, 1)" tex="x(t) = \frac23\,e^{-t/2}\sin\frac{3t}{2}, \qquad y(t) = \dot x(t) = e^{-t/2}\left(\cos\frac{3t}{2} - \frac13\sin\frac{3t}{2}\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 32(f), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Crossing the y axis">

The point is on the $y$ axis when $x(t) = 0$, which means $\sin\frac{3t}{2} = 0$:

<Formula label="Times on the y axis" tex="t = \frac{2\pi}{3}\,n \quad (n \text{ any integer}), \qquad \text{first later crossing: } t = \frac{2\pi}{3} = 2.094" cite="solutions" at="Problem 32(f), pp. 2–3" />

At $t = \frac{2\pi}{3}$ the point is at $y = e^{-\pi/3}\cos\pi = -0.351$, on the negative $y$ axis (this number is our own). After two such steps, at $t = \frac{4\pi}{3} = 4.189$, it is back on the positive $y$ axis, closer to the origin by the factor $e^{-2\pi/3} = 0.123$.

</Step>

<Step title="The phase plane">

Every trajectory is a spiral that winds into the origin. For the direction, take the point $(0, 1)$: there $\dot x = y = 1$ and $\dot y = -\frac52x - y = -1$, so the point moves to the right and down. At the top of the picture the motion is to the right: the spirals turn **clockwise**. For a companion matrix this is always so, because $\dot x = y$ is positive in the upper half of the plane.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-1.2, 1.2]} range={[-1.4, 1.4]} xLabel="x" yLabel="y"
  curves={[
    { x: "(2/3)*exp(-s/2)*sin(3*s/2)", y: "exp(-s/2)*(cos(3*s/2) - sin(3*s/2)/3)", range: [-0.55, 12] }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 0, y: 1 }]}
  caption="Our own graph. The trajectory of the second equation through the point (0, 1) (the dot), computed from the solution, from a little before t = 0 on. It turns clockwise and winds into the origin, crossing the y axis at −0.351 and then at 0.123." />

</Step>

<Step title="The graphs of x(t) and y(t)">

Both are damped sinusoids with the same decay $e^{-t/2}$ and the same circular frequency $\frac32$. The graph of $x$ starts at $0$ with slope $1$; the graph of $y$ starts at $1$ with slope $\dot y(0) = -1$. Wherever $y = 0$, the graph of $x$ has a maximum or a minimum.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$x(0) = 0$ and $y(0) = \cos 0 = 1$. The equation holds at $t = 0$: $\ddot x(0) = \dot y(0) = -1$, and $-\dot x(0) - \frac52x(0) = -1$ as well.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="g" points={4}>

In these companion matrix examples, whenever a trajectory crosses the $x$ axis it seems to cross it at a right angle: its tangent vector is vertical there. Explain why.

<Hint>

The tangent vector of a trajectory is the velocity vector $\left(\dot x, \dot y\right)$. What is its first entry at a point of the $x$ axis?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a companion matrix the horizontal part of the velocity is $y$ itself, and on the $x$ axis $y$ is zero.

<Step title="The velocity vector on the x axis">

The velocity of $\vec u = \left(x, \dot x\right)$ is $\left(\dot x, \ddot x\right)$. At a point of the $x$ axis $\dot x = 0$, so the velocity is $\left(0, \ddot x\right)$: it has no horizontal part. In terms of the matrix, for a point $(x, 0)$:

<Formula label="Velocity at a point of the x axis" tex="\frac{d\vec u}{dt} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = x\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ c \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 32(g), p. 3" />

which is vertical. Here $c$ and $d$ are the entries of the bottom row of the companion matrix (in this problem $c = -3$ or $c = -\frac52$).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The velocity is zero only if $cx = 0$. In both examples $c \ne 0$, so away from the origin the velocity on the $x$ axis is a vertical vector that is not zero, and the trajectory really crosses at a right angle. For the hook of part (d): at $(1, 0)$ the velocity is $(0, -3)$, straight down, as in the graph of part (c). Said without matrices: the trajectory crosses the $x$ axis exactly when $x(t)$ has a maximum or a minimum, and at that moment $x$ is not changing while $y$ is.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 33

<Exercise id="p33" title="Eigenvalues, eigenvectors and eigenlines" points={20} mit="Part II, Problem 33">

For a matrix $A$, an eigenvector $\vec\alpha$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$ gives the solution $\vec\alpha\,e^{\lambda t}$ of the system with matrix $A$ ([the lesson](/courses/18-03/eigenvalues-linear-systems)). The line through the origin in the direction of an eigenvector is called an **eigenline**.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Find the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors of the companion matrix of $\ddot x + 4\dot x + 3x = 0$. Draw the eigenlines in the $(x, y)$ plane. For each of the two eigenlines, write down a solution that moves along it. Compare with your work in Problem 32, part (c) above all.

The boxes ask for the two eigenvalues and, for each, the second entry of the eigenvector whose first entry is $1$.

<Hint>

The characteristic equation of a $2 \times 2$ matrix is $\lambda^2 - (\mathrm{tr}\,A)\lambda + \det A = 0$. For each root, solve $(A - \lambda I)\vec\alpha = 0$: its two equations must say the same thing.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p33-a-l1" label="The larger eigenvalue $=$" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p33-a-l1" />

<Answer id="p33-a-v1" label="Its eigenvector $(1, \ldots)$: second entry $=$" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p33-a-v1" />

<Answer id="p33-a-l2" label="The smaller eigenvalue $=$" value="-3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p33-a-l2" />

<Answer id="p33-a-v2" label="Its eigenvector $(1, \ldots)$: second entry $=$" value="-3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p33-a-v2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The eigenvalues of a companion matrix are the characteristic roots of its equation, and the solutions along the eigenlines are the pure exponential solutions seen in the phase plane.

<Step title="Eigenvalues">

<Formula label="Characteristic polynomial of the companion matrix" tex="\det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & 1 \\ -3 & -4 - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = -\lambda(-4 - \lambda) + 3 = \lambda^2 + 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda + 1)(\lambda + 3)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 33(a), p. 3" />

This is the characteristic polynomial of $D^2 + 4D + 3I$ again, as MIT points out. So $\lambda_1 = -1$ and $\lambda_2 = -3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Eigenvectors">

For $\lambda = -1$, the equation $(A + I)\vec\alpha = 0$ has the matrix with rows $(1, 1)$ and $(-3, -3)$. Both rows say $a_1 + a_2 = 0$. For $\lambda = -3$, the matrix $A + 3I$ has the rows $(3, 1)$ and $(-3, -1)$, and both say $3a_1 + a_2 = 0$:

<Formula label="Eigenvectors of the companion matrix" tex="\lambda_1 = -1:\ \ \vec\alpha_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}; \qquad \lambda_2 = -3:\ \ \vec\alpha_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 33(a), p. 3" />

or any multiple of these that is not zero. (MIT's solution says "eigenvalue" twice here where it means eigenvector.)

</Step>

<Step title="Eigenlines and their solutions">

The eigenlines are $y = -x$ and $y = -3x$: the two dashed lines in the graph of Problem 32(c). The solutions along them are

<Formula label="Solutions along the eigenlines" tex="\vec u = c\,e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \vec u = c\,e^{-3t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 33(a), p. 3" />

for any constant $c$. For $c = 1$ they are the trajectories of $x_1 = e^{-t}$ and $x_2 = e^{-3t}$ found in Problem 32(c) (MIT's solution says "3(c)", a slip for 32(c)): the first entry is the solution of the second-order equation, and the second entry is its derivative. For $c > 0$ the point moves along one half of the line, for $c < 0$ along the other half, and for $c = 0$ it rests at the origin.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Multiply out: $A\vec\alpha_1$ has the entries $0\cdot 1 + 1\cdot(-1) = -1$ and $-3\cdot 1 - 4\cdot(-1) = 1$, which is $-1$ times $(1, -1)$. And $A\vec\alpha_2$ has the entries $-3$ and $-3 + 12 = 9$, which is $-3$ times $(1, -3)$. An eigenvector of a companion matrix always has the form $(1, \lambda)$: the second entry is the velocity of $e^{\lambda t}$ at the moment the position is $1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

Find the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors of the matrix

<Formula label="The matrix of parts (b) and (c)" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -4 \\ 1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="secondHalf" at="Part II, Problem 33(b), p. 2" />

Sketch the eigenlines, and for each eigenline write down all the solutions whose trajectories lie on that line.

The boxes ask for the two eigenvalues, for one entry of each eigenvector, and for the direction of the motion.

<Hint>

The trace is $2 - 3$ and the determinant is $2\cdot(-3) - (-4)\cdot 1$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For each eigenvalue, write out the first row of $(A - \lambda I)\vec\alpha = 0$ and choose a simple solution. Then check it in the second row. A solution on an eigenline is a constant times $\vec\alpha\,e^{\lambda t}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p33-b-lneg" label="The negative eigenvalue $=$" value="-2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p33-b-lneg" />

<Answer id="p33-b-vneg" label="Its eigenvector $(1, \ldots)$: second entry $=$" value="1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p33-b-vneg" />

<Answer id="p33-b-lpos" label="The positive eigenvalue $=$" value="1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p33-b-lpos" />

<Answer id="p33-b-vpos" label="Its eigenvector $(\ldots, 1)$: first entry $=$" value="4" tolerance="0.02" calc="p33-b-vpos" />

<Answer id="p33-b-direction" label="On the eigenline of the positive eigenvalue, a solution (other than the one resting at the origin)"
  choices={["moves towards the origin and reaches it after a finite time", "moves towards the origin without ever reaching it", "moves away from the origin, faster and faster", "moves back and forth along the line"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same steps as in part (a). This time the eigenvalues have opposite signs, so the motion along one eigenline is inwards and along the other outwards.

<Step title="Eigenvalues">

<Formula label="Characteristic polynomial" tex="\det(A - \lambda I) = (2 - \lambda)(-3 - \lambda) + 4 = \lambda^2 + \lambda - 2 = (\lambda + 2)(\lambda - 1)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 33(b), p. 3" />

so $\lambda_1 = -2$ and $\lambda_2 = 1$ (the order does not matter).

</Step>

<Step title="Eigenvectors">

For $\lambda = -2$ the matrix $A + 2I$ has the rows $(4, -4)$ and $(1, -1)$: both say $a_1 = a_2$. For $\lambda = 1$ the matrix $A - I$ has the rows $(1, -4)$ and $(1, -4)$: $a_1 = 4a_2$.

<Formula label="Eigenvectors" tex="\lambda_1 = -2:\ \ \vec\alpha_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}; \qquad \lambda_2 = 1:\ \ \vec\alpha_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 33(b), p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="Eigenlines and their solutions">

The eigenlines are $y = x$ and $y = \frac14x$. All the solutions whose trajectories lie on them:

<Formula label="Solutions on the eigenlines" tex="\vec u = c\,e^{-2t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \vec u = c\,e^{t}\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 33(b), p. 3" />

for any constant $c$. On the line $y = x$ the factor $e^{-2t}$ shrinks: the point moves towards the origin and never arrives. On the line $y = \frac14x$ the factor $e^{t}$ grows: the point moves away from the origin, faster and faster. For $c = 0$ both give the solution that rests at the origin, which lies on both lines.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Multiply out: $A\vec\alpha_1$ has the entries $2 - 4 = -2$ and $1 - 3 = -2$, which is $-2$ times $(1, 1)$. And $A\vec\alpha_2$ has the entries $8 - 4 = 4$ and $4 - 3 = 1$, which is $1$ times $(4, 1)$. The eigenvalues add up to the trace $-1$ and multiply to the determinant $-2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Sketch the phase plane of the system with the matrix $A$ of part (b). (MIT has you set the Mathlet Linear Phase Portraits: Matrix Entry to this matrix and copy its picture.) Put in at least one trajectory in each wedge between the eigenlines, and arrows for the direction of time.

<Hint>

The general solution is $c_1e^{-2t}\vec\alpha_1 + c_2e^{t}\vec\alpha_2$. Which term is the larger one for very negative $t$, and which for large positive $t$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p33-c" label="A trajectory that starts on neither eigenline"
  choices={["runs into the origin alongside the line y = x", "comes in roughly along the direction of y = x, turns, and leaves alongside the line y = x/4", "comes in roughly along the direction of y = x/4, turns, and leaves alongside the line y = x", "winds around the origin while it moves away"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One eigenvalue is negative and one is positive. Far back in time the shrinking term $e^{-2t}$ was the large one; far ahead the growing term $e^{t}$ is. So each trajectory comes in along one eigen-direction and leaves along the other.

<Step title="The picture">

<Formula label="General solution" tex="\vec u(t) = c_1e^{-2t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + c_2e^{t}\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="secondHalf" at="Part II, Problem 33(c), p. 2 (our own solution; MIT's solution shows the Mathlet's picture only)" />

The two eigenlines cut the plane into four wedges. A trajectory with $c_1 \ne 0$ and $c_2 \ne 0$ stays in one wedge. For very negative $t$ it is far out, nearly parallel to the line $y = x$, and moves inwards. It turns near the origin without reaching it, and for large $t$ it moves away, closer and closer to the line $y = \frac14x$. The signs of $c_1$ and $c_2$ decide the wedge. Only the solutions on the line $y = x$ come into the origin. Every other solution, apart from the one that rests at the origin, leaves in the end: the origin is unstable. The later lesson on [sketching](/courses/18-03/sketching-linear-systems) calls this picture a saddle.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A number, for $c_1 = c_2 = 1$: the trajectory starts at $(5, 2)$ at $t = 0$. At $t = 2$ it is at $(e^{-4} + 4e^{2},\ e^{-4} + e^{2}) = (29.57, 7.41)$, and the ratio $y/x = 0.2505$ is already close to the slope $\frac14$ of the outgoing eigenline. The velocity at $(5, 2)$ is $A$ times $(5, 2)$, which is $(2, -1)$: to the right and down, towards that line.

</Step>
