Most of this lecture is geometric (transcript, p. 1). A [nonlinear autonomous system](/courses/18-03/nonlinear-autonomous-systems) $x' = f(x, y)$, $y' = g(x, y)$ is pictured by its velocity field $\vec F = f\,\vec i + g\,\vec j$; its trajectories are curves with the right velocity at every point, and its critical points are where the field is zero (p. 1).

## Closed trajectories

So far trajectories either sat at or approached a critical point, or went off to infinity (transcript, p. 2). A third behaviour: a **closed trajectory**, a closed curve travelled round and round, returning to the same state in a finite time. It represents **periodic** behaviour (p. 2). It is always a simple curve: at a self-crossing the field would have to point two ways (p. 5).

**A dull example** (pp. 2–3). $x' = y$, $y' = -x$ has eigenvalues $\pm i$, and its solutions $x = \cos t$, $y = -\sin t$ and $x = \sin t$, $y = \cos t$ trace circles clockwise:

<Formula label="Concentric circles" tex="x' = y, \quad y' = -x: \qquad x = c_1\cos t + c_2\sin t, \quad y = -c_1\sin t + c_2\cos t" cite="reading1" at="pp. 2–3" />

A whole family of concentric circles, none standing out from the rest (p. 3).

## Limit cycles

A **limit cycle** is a closed trajectory that is (transcript, pp. 3–4):

1. **isolated**: no other closed trajectories nearby;
2. **stable**: trajectories starting nearby, inside or outside, spiral towards it.

(This is the lecture's convention; many books call any isolated closed trajectory a limit cycle, stable or not.) Nearby trajectories get arbitrarily close but can never join it, since two trajectories cannot pass through one point; on a screen they only seem to merge (p. 4). A closed trajectory that repels nearby ones is unstable and is not called a limit cycle; one attracting from one side and repelling on the other is semi-stable (pp. 4–5). The name: a cycle, because it goes round, and a limit, because nearby curves approach it (p. 4).

Why care? Many natural systems have a periodic motion that returns after a disturbance (p. 5). Breathing is the lecture's example: you can breathe faster on purpose, but as soon as you stop thinking about it, the chemistry of the blood brings back your natural rhythm. Running changes the parameters, and with them the rhythm (pp. 5–6). Whether breathing really is modelled by a limit cycle is a question for biologists; it is the kind of behaviour where one looks for one (p. 6).

## Existence: hard

Given a system, does it have limit cycles? In general nobody knows how to tell (transcript, p. 6). The Poincaré–Bendixson theorem was for 60 or 70 years about the only tool, and is now little used; today one searches by computer. But a screen shows only part of the plane, and a limit cycle may be elsewhere, or so large that the screen sits inside it. So one looks only when the physical problem suggests periodic behaviour and roughly where (p. 6).

## Nonexistence: Bendixson's criterion

Ruling limit cycles out is easier (transcript, p. 7). Ivar Bendixson, a Swedish mathematician of around 1900, gave the first of two criteria (p. 7):

<Formula label="Bendixson's criterion" tex="\mathrm{div}\,\vec F = f_x + g_y \ne 0 \text{ everywhere in } D \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{no closed trajectories in } D" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

assuming $f_x + g_y$ is continuous (p. 7). **Example** (pp. 7–8): $x' = x^3 + y^3$, $y' = 3x + y^3 + 2y$. Even its critical points take some work, but its divergence $3x^2 + 3y^2 + 2$ is positive everywhere: no closed trajectories anywhere in the plane. A computer search could never prove that (p. 8).

**Proof, by contradiction** (pp. 8–11). Suppose a closed trajectory $C$ in $D$ bounds a region $R$. Compute the **flux** of $\vec F$ across $C$, taken in the positive sense, by two methods (p. 9):

<Formula label="Flux across a trajectory" tex="\oint_C \vec F\cdot\vec n\,ds = 0" cite="reading1" at="pp. 9–10" />

because $\vec F$ is tangent to the trajectory everywhere, so perpendicular to the normal $\vec n$ (p. 10). But by Green's theorem in flux form (pp. 10–11),

<Formula label="Green's theorem" tex="\oint_C \vec F\cdot\vec n\,ds = \int\int_R \mathrm{div}\,\vec F\,dA \ne 0" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11" />

since a continuous function that is never zero in $R$ keeps one sign there; to change sign it would have to pass through zero (pp. 10–11). The contradiction shows there is no closed trajectory (p. 11).

## Nonexistence: the critical point criterion

When the divergence vanishes somewhere, say $2x - 2y$, which is zero along the line $x = y$, Bendixson only rules out closed trajectories that avoid that line (transcript, pp. 11–12). A second criterion (p. 12): **inside every closed trajectory there is a critical point.** This does not help to find closed trajectories: knowing they must surround a critical point says nothing about how large they are (pp. 12–13). It is used in the contrapositive, "A implies B" being equivalent to "not B implies not A" (p. 13):

<Formula label="Critical point criterion" tex="D \text{ has no critical points} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad D \text{ has no closed trajectories}" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

For the lecture's example, whose first component is $x^2 + y^2 + 1$, never zero, there are no critical points at all, so no limit cycles (p. 13). The proof is postponed (p. 13).

## An open problem

The simplest systems after linear ones are **quadratic** systems: $f$ and $g$ polynomials of degree 2, with twelve coefficients (transcript, p. 14). How many limit cycles can a quadratic system have? The lecture tells the story as Mattuck recalls it (pp. 14–15). It was first asked in the late nineteenth century and is still unsolved: mathematicians cannot even agree on a conjecture. (It is usually counted as part of Hilbert's sixteenth problem, posed in 1900.) In his account, around 1950 Petrovskii and Landis published a long proof that the maximum is three, but Arnold pointed out a serious gap. In the 1970s a Chinese mathematician found a quadratic system with four, with coefficients far too extreme to plot (p. 15). Mattuck later met that mathematician's son, who was being recruited to the MIT department (p. 15).

<WorkedExample title="Proving and disproving cycles">
<Problem>
(a) Show that $x' = x - y - \frac{1}{4}x(x^2 + y^2)$, $y' = x + y - \frac{1}{4}y(x^2 + y^2)$ has the circle of radius 2 as a limit cycle. (b) Show that $x' = -x + y^2$, $y' = -y + x^3$ has no closed trajectories. (c) Show that $x' = 1 + y^2$, $y' = xy$ has none.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Polar coordinates">
With $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, $rr' = xx' + yy' = r^2 - \frac{1}{4}r^4$, so $r' = r(1 - \frac{1}{4}r^2)$, and $\theta' = \frac{xy' - yx'}{r^2} = 1$. On $r = 2$, $r' = 0$: a closed trajectory. Inside, $r' > 0$; outside, $r' < 0$: nearby trajectories spiral towards it from both sides, so it is a stable, isolated closed trajectory, a limit cycle.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Bendixson">
$f_x + g_y = -1 - 1 = -2$, never zero in the whole plane: no closed trajectories.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) No critical points">
$x' = 1 + y^2 \ge 1$ is never zero, so there are no critical points, hence no closed trajectories. (Here Bendixson fails: the divergence $0 + x$ vanishes on the $y$-axis.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Closed trajectories describe periodic behaviour; a limit cycle is an isolated, stable one that nearby trajectories approach.
- Finding limit cycles is hard; computer search must be guided by the physics.
- Bendixson: $f_x + g_y \ne 0$ in $D$ rules out closed trajectories in $D$ (flux and Green's theorem).
- Every closed trajectory surrounds a critical point, so no critical points means no closed trajectories.
- How many limit cycles a quadratic system can have is still open.
