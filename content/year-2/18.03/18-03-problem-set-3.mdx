This set practises [first-order autonomous equations](/courses/18-03/autonomous-equations), then the damped spring of [second-order equations with constant coefficients](/courses/18-03/second-order-constant-coefficients) and [complex characteristic roots](/courses/18-03/damped-oscillations). It follows MIT's Problem Set 3, in our own words. MIT handed the sheet out in two halves, which is why OpenCourseWare has two files for it.

MIT's set has two parts. Part II holds MIT's own four problems (60 points), written out below with hints and solutions of our own, based on MIT's published solutions. Part I is a list of short exercises, most of them from MIT's "18.03 Notes and Exercises", which are not rewritten here: the last section of this page lists them with links. MIT numbers the problems 8, 9, 11 and 12 (MIT's problem numbers follow its lecture numbers through the term, and its Lecture 10 was an exam, so there is no Problem 10), and this page keeps those numbers.

MIT labels each problem with the day of the lecture it goes with. Problems 8 and 9 go with MIT's Lectures 8 and 9, the autonomous equations, which are this site's [Lesson 5](/courses/18-03/autonomous-equations) (the linearization of Problem 9 is what MIT's "Linear vs Nonlinear" wrap-up adds to it). Problem 11 goes with MIT's Lecture 11, the spring–mass–dashpot model with real roots, and Problem 12 with its Lecture 12, complex roots: on this site those are [Lesson 9](/courses/18-03/second-order-constant-coefficients) and [Lesson 10](/courses/18-03/damped-oscillations).

## Problem 8

<Exercise id="p8" title="Hunting oryx: equilibria, stability and the bifurcation curve" points={16} mit="Part II, Problem 8">

The Kenyan government has a game preserve that supports a population of oryx. Without hunting, the population follows the logistic equation, with a stable population of one kilo-oryx (one thousand animals). The government wants to study the effect of different hunting rates $a$, measured in kilo-oryx per year. MIT's model, with the population $y$ in kilo-oryx and the time $t$ in years, is

<Formula label="The oryx population under hunting" tex="\dot y = (1 - y)\,y - a" cite="mit" at="first half, Problem 9, p. 2" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

MIT says that this is a good model of the situation. Explain why. (What does the equation say when $a = 0$? What does the term $-a$ add?)

<Hint>

Compare the right side with the logistic equation $\dot y = k_0(1 - y/p)\,y$. Which values of $k_0$ and $p$ turn it into $(1 - y)\,y$?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Match each part of the equation with a feature of the story.

<Step title="Without hunting">

For $a = 0$ the equation is $\dot y = (1 - y)\,y$. This is the logistic equation of [the lesson](/courses/18-03/autonomous-equations),

<Formula label="Logistic equation" tex="\frac{dy}{dt} = (a - by)\,y = ay - by^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

with its two constants both equal to $1$ (in the lesson's letters, $a = b = 1$; the letter $a$ is hunting in this problem, so we do not use it for the growth rate). Small populations grow at the rate $\dot y \approx y$, and the equilibrium is $y = 1$, one kilo-oryx, as the story demands. The only assumption MIT adds is that the growth rate for a small population is $1$ per year.

</Step>

<Step title="The hunting term">

Hunting at a constant rate removes $a$ kilo-oryx every year, whatever the population is, and so the equation gets the term $-a$. This is the logistic equation with constant harvesting of [the lesson](/courses/18-03/autonomous-equations), with the rate $h = a$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $a = 0$ the model has the equilibria $y = 0$ and $y = 1$, as the story says. For $a > 0$ the right side is lower by the same amount for all $y$, so the population is always smaller than it would be without hunting. That is how hunting should act.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

For which values of $a$ does the equation have two equilibria (critical points), exactly one, and none? For each $a$, find the critical points $y$ and say whether each is stable, unstable or semi-stable.

<Hint>

A critical point is a value of $y$ at which $\dot y = 0$. That is a quadratic equation in $y$, with the number $a$ in it.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The number of real roots of a quadratic equation depends on the sign of its discriminant. For the stability, sketch the parabola $f(y) = (1 - y)\,y - a$ and see where it is above and below the $y$-axis.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-b-count" label="The value of $a$ at which the two equilibria merge into one (in kilo-oryx per year):" value="0.25" tolerance="1%" calc="p8-b-merge" />

<Answer id="p8-b-upper" label="The larger equilibrium, for $a$ below that value:" expr="1/2 + sqrt(1/4 - a)"
  vars={{ a: [0.02, 0.2] }}
  names={{ a: "a" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/2 - sqrt(1/4 - a)", hint: "That is the smaller of the two roots. Which one is the larger?" },
    { expr: "1/2 + sqrt(1/4 + a)", hint: "Put it back into $y^2 - y + a$: you get $2a$, not $0$. Look at the sign under the square root." },
    { expr: "1/2 + sqrt(1 - 4*a)", hint: "The quadratic formula gives (1 ± √(1 − 4a))/2: the whole square root is halved, not only the 1." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p8-b-kind" label="At the value of $a$ where there is exactly one equilibrium, that equilibrium is"
  choices={["stable", "unstable", "semi-stable", "not an equilibrium at all"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Solve $\dot y = 0$ for $y$ with the quadratic formula, then read the stability from the sign of $\dot y$ on each side.

<Step title="The critical points">

Setting the right side to zero and multiplying out gives $y^2 - y + a = 0$, and the quadratic formula gives

<Formula label="Critical points" tex="y = \frac12 \pm \sqrt{\frac14 - a}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 8(b), p. 1" />

The square root is real exactly when $a \le \tfrac14$. So there are two critical points for $a < \tfrac14$, one for $a = \tfrac14$ (namely $y = \tfrac12$), and none for $a > \tfrac14$.

</Step>

<Step title="Stability">

The graph of $f(y) = (1 - y)\,y - a$ against $y$ is a parabola that opens downward. Where it is above the $y$-axis, $\dot y > 0$ and the population grows; where it is below, the population falls. For $a < \tfrac14$ the parabola lies above the axis between the two roots and below it outside them. Hence the population grows towards the larger root from below and falls towards it from above: the larger root is **stable**. It falls away from the smaller root on one side and grows away from it on the other: the smaller root is **unstable**.

<Plot variable="y" domain={[-0.3, 1.3]} range={[-0.6, 0.4]} xLabel="y" yLabel="f"
  curves={[{ expr: "(1 - y)*y - a" }, { expr: "0", dash: true }]}
  params={{ a: { value: 0.1875, min: 0, max: 0.35, step: 0.0125, label: "a" } }}
  caption="The right side f(y) = (1 − y)y − a of the oryx equation. Slide a: the two roots of f approach each other, merge at a = 1/4 and disappear. Where f is positive, the population grows; where it is negative, it falls." />

For $a = \tfrac14$ the parabola just touches the axis from below at $y = \tfrac12$: $f \le 0$ on both sides, so the population falls on both sides. A critical point with the same sign of $\dot y$ on both sides is **semi-stable**: solutions starting just above it fall back to it, but those starting below it fall away from it. For $a > \tfrac14$ the parabola is entirely below the axis, and every solution falls.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $a = 0$ the formula gives $y = \tfrac12 \pm \tfrac12$, so $0$ and $1$, the unstable extinction and the stable carrying capacity of the logistic equation. Also $f(\tfrac12) = \tfrac14 - a$ is the top of the parabola, and it is above the axis exactly when $a < \tfrac14$: the same threshold.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

The government hopes to allow 187.5 oryx to be killed in an average year. Find the stable population that results. Also find the critical population below which the oryx die out if the same yearly harvest is kept up.

<Hint>

The unit of $y$ is a kilo-oryx, that is, a thousand animals. What is 187.5 oryx per year in those units?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the formula of part (b) for the two critical points. Which one is stable, and which marks the threshold of a crash?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-c-stable" label="The stable population, in animals:" value="750" tolerance="1%" calc="p8-c-stable" />

<Answer id="p8-c-crash" label="The population below which it crashes, in animals:" value="250" tolerance="1%" calc="p8-c-crash" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Convert the harvest to kilo-oryx per year, then use the critical points of part (b).

<Step title="The hunting rate">

187.5 animals is $0.1875$ kilo-oryx, so $a = 0.1875 = \tfrac{3}{16}$ per year, below the limit $\tfrac14$.

</Step>

<Step title="The critical points">

<Formula label="The critical points for a = 3/16" tex="y = \frac12 \pm \sqrt{\frac14 - \frac3{16}} = \frac12 \pm \frac14 \quad\Rightarrow\quad y = \frac34 \ \text{ and } \ y = \frac14" cite="solutions" at="Problem 8(c), p. 1" />

The larger one is stable: a population of $\tfrac34$ kilo-oryx, that is **750 animals**, results in the long run. The smaller one, $\tfrac14$ kilo-oryx or **250 animals**, is unstable: if the population falls below it, the harvest of 187.5 a year is more than the herd can replace, and it is hunted to extinction.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $y = \tfrac34$: $(1 - \tfrac34)\cdot\tfrac34 = \tfrac3{16}$, the harvest, so the growth just balances it. The same at $y = \tfrac14$: $\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac14 = \tfrac3{16}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

For this value $a = \tfrac3{16}$, there are five different kinds of behaviour of the population. (Two solutions are of the same kind if one is the other shifted in time.) Sketch one solution of each kind, showing what it does for very early times and for very late times, and match each with a stretch of the phase line.

<Hint>

The equilibria cut the $y$-axis into three intervals. Count: two equilibria, three intervals.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On each interval, $\dot y$ has one sign, so the solution is monotone. As $t \to \pm\infty$ it must either tend to an equilibrium or run off to $\pm\infty$ (possibly in finite time).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-d-low" label="A herd that starts at $y = 0.1$ kilo-oryx (below the critical population)"
  choices={["falls, and has no animals left after a finite time", "grows to 750 animals", "stays near 250 animals for ever", "falls towards 250 animals but never reaches it"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each of the five kinds is either an equilibrium or an interval between them. On an interval the solution is monotone, so we only need to know where it comes from and where it goes.

<Step title="The five kinds">

1. $y > \tfrac34$: $\dot y < 0$. As $t$ grows the solution falls towards $\tfrac34$, which it approaches but does not reach. Going backward in time it rises, and since $\dot y \approx -y^2$ for large $y$ it escapes to $+\infty$ in finite time.
2. $y = \tfrac34$: the constant stable solution.
3. $\tfrac14 < y < \tfrac34$: $\dot y > 0$. The solution rises towards $\tfrac34$ as $t \to \infty$ and, going backward, falls towards $\tfrac14$ as $t \to -\infty$.
4. $y = \tfrac14$: the constant unstable solution.
5. $y < \tfrac14$: $\dot y < 0$. Going backward in time the solution rises towards $\tfrac14$ (as $t \to -\infty$). Going forward it falls, reaches $y = 0$ after a finite time (the herd is gone: the model is only meaningful until then) and goes on falling to $-\infty$ in finite time.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Count: two constant solutions and three intervals, so five. The three intervals are the three stretches of the phase line, in which the arrow points down, up and down. Solutions of kinds 1 and 3 meet the stable point at $\tfrac34$ from above and below, kinds 3 and 5 leave the unstable point at $\tfrac14$ in opposite directions.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 9

<Exercise id="p9" title="Linearizing the oryx equation near equilibrium" points={12} mit="Part II, Problem 9">

Keep the model $\dot y = (1 - y)\,y - a$ with $a = \tfrac3{16}$. Let $y_0$ be its stable critical point, and write $u = y - y_0$ for the excess of the population over equilibrium (so $u < 0$ if the population is below the equilibrium).

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Write the differential equation as an equation for $u$. Check that the new equation is again autonomous and that $u = 0$ is a critical point of it.

<Hint>

By Problem 8(c) the stable critical point is $y_0 = \tfrac34$, so $y = u + \tfrac34$. Substitute that into the right side. Note that $\dot y = \dot u$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-a-y0" label="The stable critical point $y_0 =$" value="0.75" tolerance="1%" calc="p9-a-y0" />

<Answer id="p9-a" label="$\dot u =$" expr="-u/2 - u^2"
  vars={{ u: [0.1, 0.5] }}
  names={{ u: "u" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-u/2 + u^2", hint: "Expand (1/4 − u)(u + 3/4): the u² term comes from −u · u, so it carries a minus sign." },
    { expr: "-u^2", hint: "Look at the terms linear in u: u/4 − 3u/4. They do not cancel." },
    { expr: "-u/2", hint: "You left out the $u^2$ term. Part (b) asks for that to be dropped, but only after you have found it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Substitute $y = u + y_0$ and expand. Since $y_0$ is a constant, $\dot y = \dot u$.

<Step title="The substitution">

With $y_0 = \tfrac34$, we have $y = u + \tfrac34$ and $1 - y = \tfrac14 - u$. Then

<Formula label="The equation for the excess" tex="\dot u = \left(\tfrac14 - u\right)\left(u + \tfrac34\right) - \tfrac3{16} = -\tfrac12\,u - u^2" cite="solutions" at="Problem 9(a), p. 1" />

[MIT's printed solution writes the second factor as $(u - \tfrac34)$. That is a sign slip: the factor is $y = u + \tfrac34$. The result $-\tfrac12 u - u^2$ is the same as ours.]

</Step>

<Step title="Autonomous, with a critical point at zero">

The right side contains only $u$, and not $t$: the equation is autonomous. At $u = 0$ it is zero, so $u = 0$ is a critical point, and it is the old critical point $y = y_0$ in the new variable.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The other critical point $y = \tfrac14$ is $u = -\tfrac12$: indeed $-\tfrac12\cdot(-\tfrac12) - \tfrac14 = 0$, so the new equation has the second critical point where it should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

For small $u$ the higher powers ($u^2$ and beyond) can be neglected: this is **linearization** near the equilibrium. What is the linearized equation near $u = 0$, and what is its general solution?

<Hint>

When $u$ is small, $u^2$ is much smaller than $u$ itself. Drop it from the equation of part (a).

</Hint>

<Hint>

What is left is a first-order linear equation with a constant coefficient.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-b" label="Take the arbitrary constant to be $c = 1$. The solution is $u(t) =$" expr="exp(-t/2)"
  vars={{ t: [0.5, 4] }}
  names={{ t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(t/2)", hint: "The sign of the rate: the equation says that a positive u falls. Does your function fall?" },
    { expr: "exp(-t)", hint: "Check it: its derivative is −e^(−t), which is −u, not −u/2." },
    { expr: "exp(-2*t)", hint: "Differentiate: you get $-2u$. The rate in the equation is one half." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Dropping $u^2$ from the equation leaves a linear equation, which is solved by an exponential.

<Step title="Linearize">

The terms of $\dot u = -\tfrac12 u - u^2$ are the linear term $-\tfrac12 u$ and the quadratic term $-u^2$. For small $u$ the second is much smaller than the first: at $u = 0.1$ it is $0.01$ against $0.05$. Dropping it,

<Formula label="The linearized equation" tex="\dot u = -\tfrac12\,u" cite="solutions" at="Problem 9(b), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

An equation $\dot u = -\tfrac12 u$ is solved by separation of variables, or by the first-order linear methods of the lessons:

<Formula label="Its general solution" tex="u = c\,e^{-t/2}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 9(b), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate: $\dot u = -\tfrac12\,c\,e^{-t/2} = -\tfrac12 u$. The rate is the number $-\tfrac12 = f'(y_0)$, the slope of the right side $f(y) = (1 - y)y - \tfrac3{16}$ at the critical point $y_0 = \tfrac34$: $f'(y) = 1 - 2y$, which is $-\tfrac12$ at $y_0$. This is the general fact behind linearization: near a critical point, the rate is the derivative of the right side there.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Near equilibrium the solutions of the original equation are well approximated by those of its linearization. Give an approximation of $y$ near equilibrium. Use it to answer this: if $y(10) - y_0 = b$, estimate $y(11)$ and $y(12)$. Give the two excesses $y(11) - y_0$ and $y(12) - y_0$ as multiples of $b$.

<Hint>

By part (b) the excess $u = y - y_0$ is approximately $c\,e^{-t/2}$. Each unit of time multiplies it by the same factor. What is that factor?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-c-11" label="$\left(y(11) - y_0\right)/b \approx$" value="0.6065" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p9-c-11" />

<Answer id="p9-c-12" label="$\left(y(12) - y_0\right)/b \approx$" value="0.3679" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p9-c-12" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the linear approximation the excess decays by a fixed factor in each unit of time.

<Step title="The approximation">

From $y = y_0 + u$ and part (b),

<Formula label="The population near equilibrium" tex="y \approx \tfrac34 + c\,e^{-t/2}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 9(c), p. 1" />

The population relaxes exponentially towards the equilibrium, with decay rate $\tfrac12$ per year.

</Step>

<Step title="The estimates">

At $t = 10$ the excess is $b = c\,e^{-5}$, so $c = b\,e^{5}$. Then at $t = 11$ the excess is $c\,e^{-11/2} = b\,e^{-1/2}$, and at $t = 12$ it is $c\,e^{-6} = b\,e^{-1}$:

<Formula label="The two estimates" tex="y(11) \approx \tfrac34 + b\,e^{-1/2} = \tfrac34 + 0.6065\,b, \qquad y(12) \approx \tfrac34 + b\,e^{-1} = \tfrac34 + 0.3679\,b" cite="solutions" at="Problem 9(c), p. 1 (our own working out of MIT's last question)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The excess shrinks each year: $1 > 0.6065 > 0.3679$, and each year multiplies it by the same factor $e^{-1/2} = 0.6065$: with the rounded values, $0.3679/0.6065 = 0.6066$, the same up to rounding.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Suppose that the linear equation $\dot x + p(t)\,x = q(t)$ is autonomous. What can you say about the functions $p$ and $q$?

<Hint>

Write the equation as $\dot x = q(t) - p(t)\,x$. What does "autonomous" say about the right side, as a function of $x$ and $t$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-d" label="Then"
  choices={["$p$ and $q$ are both constants", "$p$ is constant and $q$ can be any function of $t$", "$q$ is constant and $p$ can be any function of $t$", "$q$ must be zero and $p$ can be anything"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** An autonomous equation has a right side that does not depend on $t$ at all, for any value of $x$.

<Step title="The argument">

The right side is $F(x, t) = q(t) - p(t)\,x$. It must have the same value at every time $t$, for every $x$. At $x = 0$ this says that $q(t)$ does not depend on $t$, so $q$ is a constant. Then at $x = 1$ it says that $q - p(t)$ does not depend on $t$, so $p$ is a constant too.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

So an autonomous linear equation is $\dot x + px = q$ with constants $p$ and $q$, as in the examples of [the lesson](/courses/18-03/autonomous-equations) (the bank account $\dot y = ry - w$ is one). Conversely, an equation of this form is autonomous. The linearization of part (b) is of this type, with $p = \tfrac12$ and $q = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

Try them in [MIT's exercises](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/pages/readings/notes_exe/): the exercises of Unit 1 are in [the Unit 1 file](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/resources/mit18_03s10_1ex/) (with [its solutions](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/resources/mit18_03s10_1sol/)) and those of Unit 2 in [the Unit 2 file](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/resources/mit18_03s10_2ex/) (with [its solutions](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/resources/mit18_03s10_2sol/)). An exercise number such as 2C-1 means section C of Unit 2, exercise 1; the letters after it are its parts.

| For | Exercises in MIT's Notes |
|---|---|
| Lesson 5: [autonomous equations](/courses/18-03/autonomous-equations) | 1E-1 (sketch the phase line and typical solutions of several autonomous equations) |
| Lesson 9: [second-order equations with constant coefficients](/courses/18-03/second-order-constant-coefficients) | 2C-1 a, b (general and initial-value solutions with real roots) |
| Lesson 10: [complex characteristic roots](/courses/18-03/damped-oscillations) | 2C-1 c, d, e (complex roots, and the double root); 2G-1 (the three cases of $y'' + 2y' + cy = 0$ for different $c$, and for which $c$ the equation is stable, which also uses [stability](/courses/18-03/inhomogeneous-equations-and-stability)) |

MIT's sheet also has a short exercise of its own on $\dot x + 2x = 1$ (solve it three ways, sketch its phase line, and take three steps of Euler's method), which comes with a short answer at the end of the sheet and with the [first half of the problem set](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/18-03-differential-equations-spring-2010/resources/mit18_03s10_ps3apdf/). Its Part I lists further exercises from the course textbook (Edwards and Penney, *Elementary Differential Equations*, 6th edition), which is not openly licensed and is not used on this site, so they are not part of this page.
