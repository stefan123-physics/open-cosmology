So far, two kinds of first-order equation can be solved: [separable](/courses/18-01/separation-of-variables) and [linear](/courses/18-03/linear-first-order-equations). In a sense those are the only two general methods; most other solvable equations are solved by a change of variables that turns them into one of these (transcript, p. 1).

## Scaling

The most common change of variables simply rescales the axes (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Scaling" tex="x_1 = \frac{x}{a}, \qquad y_1 = \frac{y}{b}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

with constants $a$, $b$. It is used to change units, to make variables **dimensionless**, and to reduce the number of constants (p. 1).

**Example.** At high temperatures Newton's cooling law is replaced by a fourth-power law, with external temperature $M$ (p. 1):

<Formula label="Radiation cooling law" tex="\frac{dT}{dt} = k\,(M^4 - T^4)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Let $T_1 = T/M$, which is dimensionless. To substitute, use the **inverse** form $T = MT_1$, since it is $T$ that appears in the equation. Then $M\,dT_1/dt = kM^4(1 - T_1^4)$, so (p. 1)

<Formula label="Scaled form" tex="\frac{dT_1}{dt} = k_1\,(1 - T_1^4), \qquad k_1 = kM^3" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

It is no easier to solve, but simpler: $T_1$ has no units, and the **lumped constant** $k_1$ has units of inverse time only (p. 1).

**Direct and inverse substitution** (p. 2). A direct substitution defines the new variable from the old ones ($u = 1 - x^2$ for $\int x\sqrt{1 - x^2}\,dx$); an inverse one writes the old variables in terms of the new ($x = \sin u$ for $\int\sqrt{1 - x^2}\,dx$). The two integrals look alike but need different kinds of substitution. The rule: look at what you have to replace in the equation, and substitute for that.

## Bernoulli equations

A **Bernoulli equation** has the form (transcript, p. 2)

<Formula label="Bernoulli equation" tex="y' = p(x)\,y + q(x)\,y^n" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

for any number $n$: 2, 3, $\frac12$, $-5$. For $n = 0$ it is linear and for $n = 1$ separable. It must have exactly these two terms: adding even a constant spoils it (p. 2). **Method**: divide by $y^n$, giving $y'/y^n = p\,y^{1-n} + q$. With $v = y^{1-n}$, $v' = (1 - n)y^{-n}y'$, and the equation becomes linear (p. 2):

<Formula label="Bernoulli made linear" tex="\frac{v'}{1 - n} = p(x)\,v + q(x), \qquad v = y^{1-n}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Solve it as a linear equation, then convert back to $y$. Learn the method, not the final formula (p. 2).

**Example** (pp. 2–3). $y' = y/x - y^2$: divide by $y^2$, set $v = 1/y$, $v' = -y'/y^2$. Then $-v' = v/x - 1$, i.e. $v' + v/x = 1$. The integrating factor is $x$: $(xv)' = x$, so $xv = x^2/2 + C$ and $v = x/2 + C/x$. Turning it upside down (p. 3):

<Formula label="Example solution" tex="y = \frac{2x}{x^2 + C_1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The arbitrary constant is tucked inside the solution, not added on (p. 3).

## Homogeneous equations

A first-order equation is **homogeneous** (a different use of the word from homogeneous linear equations) if it can be written (transcript, p. 3)

<Formula label="Homogeneous equation" tex="y' = F\!\left(\frac{y}{x}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

For example $y' = x^2y/(x^3 + y^3)$, after dividing top and bottom by $x^3$, is $\frac{y/x}{1 + (y/x)^3}$; and $xy' = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ is $\sqrt{1 + (y/x)^2}$ (p. 3). The name comes from **zooming**: under $x = ax_1$, $y = ay_1$, both $dy/dx$ and $y/x$ are unchanged, so the equation stays the same (p. 3).

**Method** (pp. 3–4). The direct substitution $z = y/x$ leads to a mess if you differentiate it by the quotient rule. Use the inverse form instead, $y = zx$, because $y'$ is what the equation contains:

<Formula label="Substitution for homogeneous equations" tex="y = zx, \qquad y' = z'x + z, \qquad x\,\frac{dz}{dx} = F(z) - z" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

and the variables separate. At the end, go back to $y$ and $x$.

## The boat and the lighthouse

A lighthouse at the origin keeps its beam on a boat, which escapes by always steering at 45° to the beam (transcript, p. 4). Why is this a differential equation? We want a curve, and we know something about its slope. If the beam makes angle $\alpha$ with the horizontal, $\tan\alpha = y/x$, and the path's slope is $\tan(\alpha + 45°)$, so by the tangent addition formula (p. 4)

<Formula label="The boat's path" tex="y' = \frac{y/x + 1}{1 - y/x} = \frac{x + y}{x - y}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

a homogeneous equation. With $y = zx$: $xz' = \frac{1 + z}{1 - z} - z = \frac{1 + z^2}{1 - z}$, so $\frac{1 - z}{1 + z^2}\,dz = \frac{dx}{x}$ and (pp. 4–5)

<Formula label="Integrated" tex="\arctan z - \tfrac12\ln(1 + z^2) = \ln x + c" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

Put back $z = y/x$ (taking $x > 0$): $\arctan(y/x) = \ln\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} + c$. A mess, until it is read in polar coordinates, where $\arctan(y/x) = \theta$ and $\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = r$: $\theta = \ln r + c$, that is (p. 5)

<Formula label="Exponential spiral" tex="r = C\,e^{\theta}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

The boat sails along an **exponential spiral** (p. 5).

<WorkedExample title="A logistic Bernoulli equation and a homogeneous one">
<Problem>
Solve (a) $y' = y - y^2$ and (b) $y' = (x^2 + y^2)/(xy)$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Bernoulli with n = 2">
Divide by $y^2$: $y'/y^2 = 1/y - 1$. With $v = 1/y$, $v' = -y'/y^2$, so $-v' = v - 1$, i.e. $v' + v = 1$. Integrating factor $e^x$: $(e^xv)' = e^x$, $v = 1 + Ce^{-x}$, so $y = \dfrac{1}{1 + Ce^{-x}}$: every positive solution approaches 1, the logistic curve. (Dividing by $y^2$ loses the solution $y = 0$.)
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Homogeneous">
Dividing top and bottom by $x^2$: $y' = \dfrac{1 + z^2}{z}$ with $z = y/x$. Then $xz' = \dfrac{1 + z^2}{z} - z = \dfrac1z$, so $z\,dz = dx/x$, $\frac12 z^2 = \ln|x| + c$, and $y^2 = x^2(2\ln|x| + C)$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Scaling makes variables dimensionless and lumps constants; substitute for what the equation contains (often an inverse substitution).
- Bernoulli $y' = py + qy^n$: $v = y^{1-n}$ makes it linear.
- Homogeneous $y' = F(y/x)$ (zoom-invariant): $y = zx$ makes it separable.
- The 45° boat follows the exponential spiral $r = Ce^\theta$.
