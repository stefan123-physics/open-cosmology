The topic is inhomogeneous systems; the subtext is that thinking in matrices makes things easier (transcript, p. 1). First some theory, for a $2 \times 2$ system $\vec x\,' = A\vec x$ with a constant matrix, as on the [previous pages](/courses/18-03/sketching-linear-systems) (p. 1).

## Theory

**Theorem A** (transcript, pp. 1–3). The general solution is

<Formula label="General solution of the homogeneous system" tex="\vec x = c_1\vec x_1 + c_2\vec x_2" cite="reading1" at="pp. 1–2" />

where $\vec x_1$, $\vec x_2$ are two **linearly independent** solutions: neither is a constant multiple of the other (p. 2). As for second-order equations, the easy part is that these are all solutions, by linearity and superposition; the hard part is that there are no others (pp. 2–3).

**Theorem B** (pp. 3–4). The **Wronskian** of two solutions is the determinant of the square matrix whose columns they are (p. 3):

<Formula label="Wronskian of two solutions" tex="W(\vec x_1, \vec x_2) = \det(\vec x_1, \vec x_2)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

It is a function of $t$, and like the earlier [Wronskian](/courses/18-03/linear-operators-and-wronskian), of which it is a generalization (a homework problem), it has only two possibilities: it is **identically zero**, if $\vec x_1$ and $\vec x_2$ are dependent, or **never zero**, if they are independent (pp. 3–4).

## The fundamental matrix

Those who work with systems usually put the solutions together in one matrix (transcript, p. 4). The **fundamental matrix** for $A$ is the matrix whose columns are two independent solutions (p. 5):

<Formula label="Fundamental matrix" tex="X = \begin{pmatrix} \vec x_1 & \vec x_2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

It has two properties (pp. 5–7):

1. $\det X \ne 0$ for every $t$: its determinant is the Wronskian of independent solutions.
2. It satisfies a **matrix differential equation**, whose unknown is a whole matrix (p. 6):

<Formula label="Matrix differential equation" tex="X' = AX" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

A matrix is differentiated entry by entry, so $X' = (\vec x_1\,'\;\;\vec x_2\,')$; and by the rule of matrix multiplication $AX = (A\vec x_1\;\;A\vec x_2)$. The equation says, in one breath, that both columns solve the system (pp. 6–7).

## Inhomogeneous systems

Add given functions of $t$ to the right (transcript, pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="Inhomogeneous system" tex="\vec x\,' = A\vec x + \vec r(t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

**Theorem C** (p. 8). The general solution is $\vec x = \vec x_c + \vec x_p$: the complementary solution, which solves $\vec x\,' = A\vec x$, plus one particular solution, by linearity and superposition. For single equations most of the work was in finding $\vec x_p$, by special methods for special inputs (pp. 8–9). For systems it turns out to be easy, and for **any** input: even a single equation with input $\tan t$, which neither the Laplace transform nor Fourier series can handle, can be solved this way once it is turned into a system (a homework problem, p. 9).

**Where such a system comes from** (pp. 9–12). Two one-litre tanks exchange liquid: 3 L/h flows from the first to the second and 2 L/h back, 2 L/h leaves the second, and to keep both tanks full, 1 L/h enters each from outside. The water entering the first carries salt at concentration $5e^{-t}$; that entering the second is pure (p. 10). With $x$ and $y$ the amounts of salt, which equal the concentrations in one-litre tanks, inflow minus outflow gives (pp. 11–12)

<Formula label="Two tanks with salt coming in" tex="\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}' = \begin{pmatrix} -3 & 2 \\ 3 & -4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 5e^{-t} \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–12" />

The outside pipes make the system inhomogeneous (p. 12).

## Variation of parameters

The method, from about two centuries ago (the lecture guesses Lagrange), takes the general solution $c_1\vec x_1 + c_2\vec x_2$ of the homogeneous system and lets the constants vary: look for a particular solution $v_1(t)\vec x_1 + v_2(t)\vec x_2$ (transcript, pp. 12–13). The first step is to write it with the fundamental matrix, with the column vector $\vec v$ **on the right** (pp. 13–14):

<Formula label="Trial particular solution" tex="\vec x_p = X\vec v, \qquad \vec v = \begin{pmatrix} v_1(t) \\ v_2(t) \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

(Written on the left, the product would mean something else, p. 14.) Substitute into $\vec x\,' = A\vec x + \vec r$, differentiating the product of matrices by the ordinary product rule, keeping the order (pp. 14–15):

<Formula label="Substitution" tex="X'\vec v + X\vec v\,' = AX\vec v + \vec r" cite="reading1" at="pp. 14–15" />

Since $X' = AX$, the first terms cancel, leaving $X\vec v\,' = \vec r$ (p. 15). (The lecture first writes $X\vec v = \vec r$ and adds the prime.) $X$ has an inverse because its determinant is never zero; multiplying on the **left** by $X^{-1}$ and integrating each entry (pp. 15–16):

<Formula label="Variation of parameters" tex="\vec v\,' = X^{-1}\vec r, \qquad \vec x_p = X\int X^{-1}\vec r\,dt" cite="reading1" at="pp. 15–16" />

Inverting a $2 \times 2$ matrix is quick: swap the diagonal entries, change the signs of the others, divide by the determinant (p. 16). If the integral cannot be done, leave it as an integral. No integration constants are needed: they are already in $\vec x_c$ (p. 16). The lecture leaves the tank problem for recitation (p. 16).

<WorkedExample title="Variation of parameters in action">
<Problem>
Solve $x' = y$, $y' = x + e^{2t}$: find a fundamental matrix, its Wronskian, and a particular solution.
</Problem>
<Step title="Fundamental matrix">
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $\pm 1$ with eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, so $X = \begin{pmatrix} e^t & e^{-t} \\ e^t & -e^{-t} \end{pmatrix}$. Its determinant is $-1 - 1 = -2$, never zero.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve for v">
$X^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} e^{-t} & e^{-t} \\ e^t & -e^t \end{pmatrix}$, so with $\vec r = (0, e^{2t})$, $\vec v\,' = X^{-1}\vec r = \frac{1}{2}(e^t, -e^{3t})$ and $\vec v = (\frac{1}{2}e^t, -\frac{1}{6}e^{3t})$.
</Step>
<Step title="Particular solution">
$\vec x_p = X\vec v = \left(\frac{1}{2}e^{2t} - \frac{1}{6}e^{2t},\; \frac{1}{2}e^{2t} + \frac{1}{6}e^{2t}\right) = \left(\frac{1}{3}e^{2t}, \frac{2}{3}e^{2t}\right)$. Check: $x' = \frac{2}{3}e^{2t} = y$, and $y' = \frac{4}{3}e^{2t} = \frac{1}{3}e^{2t} + e^{2t}$. The general solution adds $c_1(1, 1)e^t + c_2(1, -1)e^{-t}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The general solution of $\vec x\,' = A\vec x$ is $c_1\vec x_1 + c_2\vec x_2$ with independent solutions; their Wronskian is never zero.
- The fundamental matrix $X = (\vec x_1\;\vec x_2)$ has $\det X \ne 0$ and $X' = AX$.
- $\vec x\,' = A\vec x + \vec r$: general solution $\vec x_c + \vec x_p$.
- Variation of parameters: $\vec x_p = X\vec v$ gives $X\vec v\,' = \vec r$, so $\vec x_p = X\int X^{-1}\vec r\,dt$, for any input.
