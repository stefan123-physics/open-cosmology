This lecture puts the [first-order linear equation](/courses/18-03/linear-first-order-equations) to work with constant coefficients, and uses [complex exponentials](/courses/18-03/complex-exponentials) to solve it for an oscillating input. (The recording starts partway into the lecture.)

## Steady state and transient

The temperature and concentration models are naturally written with the constant $k$ on the right as well (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Standard form for these models" tex="y' + ky = k\,q_e(t)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 1, 4" />

Mixing problems have this form too; others, such as the RC circuit or radioactive decay, do not separate out so naturally (p. 1). For a general right-hand side $q(t)$, the integrating factor $e^{kt}$ gives (p. 1)

<Formula label="Solution with constant k" tex="y = e^{-kt}\int q(t)\,e^{kt}\,dt + C\,e^{-kt}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The opposite signs in the exponents are easy to remember: for a constant input they must cancel to give a constant solution (p. 1). If $k > 0$, which is essential, the second term tends to 0 as $t \to \infty$ whatever $C$ is. It is the **transient**, and it carries the initial value. The first term remains: the **steady-state** (or long-term) solution (p. 1).

So every solution, wherever it starts, approaches the steady-state solution, without crossing it (pp. 1–2). But then nothing makes that one special: all solutions approach one another, so there are many steady-state solutions, and "the" steady-state solution usually means the simplest-looking one, often the one with $C = 0$ (p. 2). For $k < 0$ the words steady state and transient make no sense (p. 4).

## Input and response

The right-hand side is called the **input** and the solution the **response** (transcript, pp. 2–3). Thinking of the temperature model, the water bath's temperature drives the temperature inside: the bath plus the inner chamber is a small system, and its response is governed by the driving temperature. Strictly the input is $q(t) = kq_e(t)$; the lecture calls $q_e$ the **physical input** (the temperature or concentration input) (p. 3). Dividing by $k$ gives another common way of writing the equation, $\frac1k y' + y = q_e(t)$, in which $q_e$ is simply called the input (p. 3).

**Superposition** (p. 4). If the input $q_1$ produces the response $y_1$ and $q_2$ produces $y_2$, then

<Formula label="Superposition principle" tex="q_1 + q_2 \;\to\; y_1 + y_2, \qquad c\,q_1 \;\to\; c\,y_1" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

This follows from the **linearity** of the equation; with $y^2$ in place of $y$ none of it would be true (p. 4).

## Sinusoidal input

The most important case is a trigonometric input, because of Fourier series, which come later in the course (transcript, p. 4). Take $q_e = \cos\omega t$, so the equation is $y' + ky = k\cos\omega t$ (pp. 4–5). Here $\omega$ is the **angular** (or circular) frequency: the number of complete oscillations in a $t$-interval of length $2\pi$, not in unit time (p. 5).

**Complexify** (p. 6). Since $\cos\omega t = \mathrm{Re}\,e^{i\omega t}$, replace the equation by

<Formula label="Complexified equation" tex="\tilde y' + k\tilde y = k\,e^{i\omega t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Its solution $\tilde y$ is complex, so it gets its own name. Write $\tilde y = y_1 + iy_2$: its real part $y_1$ solves the original real equation, by separating real and imaginary parts (p. 7). (Here $y_1$ means the real part, not the response of the superposition principle above.)

**Solve with the integrating factor** $e^{kt}$ (pp. 7–8). Exponentials combine and integrate easily:

<Formula label="Integrating the complex equation" tex="(\tilde y\,e^{kt})' = k\,e^{(k + i\omega)t}, \qquad \tilde y\,e^{kt} = \frac{k}{k + i\omega}\,e^{(k + i\omega)t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

Multiply by $e^{-kt}$ and divide top and bottom by $k$ (p. 8):

<Formula label="Complex solution" tex="\tilde y = \frac{1}{1 + i\,(\omega/k)}\,e^{i\omega t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

Only the ratio $\omega/k$ matters (p. 8).

## Amplitude and phase lag

To take the real part, the lecture **goes polar** (transcript, p. 8). For a complex number $\alpha$, $\frac1\alpha\cdot\alpha = 1$, so taking moduli and adding arguments (p. 9):

<Formula label="Polar form of a reciprocal" tex="\left|\frac{1}{\alpha}\right| = \frac{1}{|\alpha|}, \qquad \text{argument of } \frac{1}{\alpha} = -(\text{argument of } \alpha)" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

Draw $1 + i\,\omega/k$: a right triangle with sides 1 and $\omega/k$ and angle $\phi$ at the origin. So (pp. 9–10)

<Formula label="Polar form of the coefficient" tex="\frac{1}{1 + i\,(\omega/k)} = A\,e^{-i\phi}, \qquad A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (\omega/k)^2}}, \qquad \phi = \arctan\frac{\omega}{k}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11" />

Then $\tilde y = A\,e^{i(\omega t - \phi)}$, and its real part is the response (pp. 10–12):

<Formula label="Response to cos ωt" tex="y_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (\omega/k)^2}}\,\cos(\omega t - \phi)" cite="reading1" at="p. 12" />

$A$ is the **amplitude** and $\phi$ the **phase lag** (or phase delay) (pp. 11–12). The response is the input shifted along: $\phi$ is subtracted from the angle $\omega t$, not from $t$. For example $\phi = \pi/2$ shifts the cosine by a quarter of its cycle, giving $\sin\omega t$ (p. 12).

**Effect of the conductivity** (pp. 12–13). If $k$ goes up (heat gets in faster), $\omega/k$ falls, so the amplitude rises towards 1 and the phase lag falls towards 0: the inside follows the bath closely and with little delay. The lecture ends by leaving "the other one" to the class (p. 13). (The effect of $\omega$, worked out here: a faster oscillation, larger $\omega$, has the opposite effect: smaller amplitude and a lag approaching $\pi/2$.)

**Going Cartesian** instead (announced on p. 8 but not reached in this recording; it opens the next lecture, so this is the page's own working): multiply top and bottom by the conjugate, $\frac{1}{1 + i\omega/k} = \frac{1 - i\omega/k}{1 + (\omega/k)^2}$, and take the real part of the product with $\cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t$. The result, $y_1 = \frac{\cos\omega t + (\omega/k)\sin\omega t}{1 + (\omega/k)^2}$, is the same function as the polar answer, written as a combination of cosine and sine.

<WorkedExample title="A chamber in a water bath">
<Problem>
A small chamber sits in a bath whose temperature oscillates as $20 + 8\cos(0.25t)$ °C ($t$ in minutes), and its walls give $k = 0.25$ per minute. (a) Find the steady-state temperature inside. (b) By how many minutes do its peaks lag behind the bath's? (c) Repeat with walls that conduct four times better, $k = 1$ per minute.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Superposition">
The constant input 20 gives the response 20. For the oscillating part, $\omega/k = 1$, so $A = 1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.707$ and $\phi = \arctan 1 = \pi/4$. The steady state is $20 + 5.66\cos(0.25t - \pi/4)$ °C.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Time lag">
The cosine reaches its peak when $0.25t - \pi/4 = 0$, so the lag is $\phi/\omega = (\pi/4)/0.25 = \pi \approx 3.14$ minutes.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Better walls">
Now $\omega/k = 0.25$: $A = 1/\sqrt{1.0625} \approx 0.970$, so the inside swings by $\pm 7.76$ °C, and $\phi = \arctan 0.25 \approx 0.245$ rad (14.0°), a lag of only $0.98$ minutes.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- For $y' + ky = q(t)$ with $k > 0$, solutions are a steady-state part plus a transient $Ce^{-kt}$ that dies away.
- Input $q_e$ drives response $y$; by linearity, responses to added inputs add.
- Complexify $\cos\omega t$ to $e^{i\omega t}$; the complex solution is $e^{i\omega t}/(1 + i\omega/k)$.
- The response is $A\cos(\omega t - \phi)$, $A = 1/\sqrt{1 + (\omega/k)^2}$, $\phi = \arctan(\omega/k)$.
