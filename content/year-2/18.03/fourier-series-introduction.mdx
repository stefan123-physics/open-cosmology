So far the inputs have been very special: exponentials, sines and cosines (transcript, p. 1). Fourier series show that they are not as special as they look. This lecture and the next two cover them (p. 1).

## Why Fourier series

Any reasonable function $f(t)$ with period $2\pi$, even one with some jumps, can be written as an infinite sum of sines and cosines (transcript, pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Fourier series" tex="f(t) = c_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(a_n\cos nt + b_n\sin nt\right)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 1–2" />

($2\pi$ need only be *a* period: $\sin 2t$ also has it.) Why does this help with $y'' + Ay' + By = f(t)$? For each input $\cos nt$ or $\sin nt$ we know how to find the response, by complexifying (p. 2). Because the equation is linear, the input $a_n\cos nt$ has $a_n$ times that response, and adding up, the response to $f$ is the same combination of the responses to the cosines and sines; the constant term counts as $\cos 0t$ (pp. 2–3). That is the superposition principle. The programme: find the Fourier series of the input, then add up the known responses (p. 3). Its main payoff will be a new view of resonance: some terms of the input's series can be picked out and produce unexpectedly large responses (p. 4).

## Orthogonality

Two functions $u(t)$ and $v(t)$, each with period $2\pi$, are **orthogonal** on $[-\pi, \pi]$ if (transcript, pp. 4–5)

<Formula label="Orthogonality" tex="\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}u(t)\,v(t)\,dt = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

The word really is connected with orthogonal vectors in 18.02 (p. 5). The theorem that makes Fourier series work (p. 5): **any two distinct functions from the collection $\sin nt$ ($n \ge 1$) and $\cos mt$ ($m \ge 0$) are orthogonal on $[-\pi, \pi]$.** So $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin 3t\cos 4t\,dt = 0$, and so is $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin 3t\sin 60t\,dt$. A function with itself cannot give 0, since a square is positive; instead, for $n \ge 1$ (p. 6)

<Formula label="Normalization" tex="\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin^2 nt\,dt = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\cos^2 nt\,dt = \pi" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

The relations can be proved with trigonometric identities or with complex exponentials, but the lecture uses a third method, through the differential equation, because it is the one that generalizes to the many other families of orthogonal functions (pp. 6–7).

**Proof via the ODE** (pp. 7–11). Both $\sin nt$ and $\cos nt$ satisfy (p. 7)

<Formula label="The shared equation" tex="u'' + n^2u = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

Let $u_n$ and $v_m$ be any two of the functions, $m \ne n$, so $u_n'' = -n^2u_n$ and $v_m'' = -m^2v_m$ (pp. 7–8). Integrate the unsymmetric expression $\int u_n''v_m$ by parts, "the most important theoretical method" of 18.01 (p. 8):

<Formula label="Integration by parts" tex="\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}u_n''\,v_m\,dt = \Big[u_n'\,v_m\Big]_{-\pi}^{\pi} - \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}u_n'\,v_m'\,dt" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

The bracket is always 0: a sine vanishes at $\pm\pi$, and a product of cosines, being even, has the same value at both ends (pp. 8–9). What remains, $-\int u_n'v_m'$, is **symmetric** in $u$ and $v$. But using the equation, $\int u_n''v_m = -n^2\int u_nv_m$, which favours $u$ (p. 9). Doing the same with the roles exchanged gives $-m^2\int u_nv_m$ for the same quantity, and since $m \ne n$ (p. 10)

<Formula label="Conclusion" tex="-n^2\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}u_nv_m\,dt = -m^2\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}u_nv_m\,dt \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}u_nv_m\,dt = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

Something both symmetric and not symmetric must be zero (p. 10). One case is left: $\cos nt\sin nt$, with the same $n$. It is $\frac{1}{2}\sin 2nt$, whose integral over a whole period is 0 (p. 11).

## Finding the coefficients

To find $a_n$, multiply the whole series by $\cos nt$ and integrate from $-\pi$ to $\pi$ (transcript, pp. 11–12). By orthogonality every term vanishes except $a_n\int\cos^2nt\,dt = \pi a_n$ (p. 12). The same works for $b_n$ (pp. 12–13):

<Formula label="Fourier coefficients" tex="a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t)\cos nt\,dt, \qquad b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t)\sin nt\,dt, \qquad n \ge 1" cite="reading1" at="pp. 12–13" />

**The constant term** is an exception, a source of gross errors (p. 13). Multiply by 1 (that is, $\cos 0t$) and integrate: $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f\,dt = 2\pi c_0$, because $\cos nt$ and $\sin nt$ integrate to 0 over a full period (p. 13). So $c_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f\,dt$: putting $n = 0$ into the $a_n$ formula gives twice it. Hence the usual convention (p. 14):

<Formula label="Constant term" tex="c_0 = \frac{a_0}{2} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(t)\,dt" cite="reading1" at="pp. 13–14" />

Either learn one formula for all the $a_n$ and write the constant as $\frac{a_0}{2}$, or learn a separate formula for $c_0$ (p. 14).

## The square wave

Take $f = -1$ on $(-\pi, 0)$ and $f = +1$ on $(0, \pi)$, continued periodically: an odd function, equally above and below the axis (transcript, pp. 14–15). Its $a_n$ turn out to be 0 (p. 15). For $b_n$, split the integral where $f$ jumps (p. 15):

<Formula label="Square wave coefficients" tex="b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\int_{-\pi}^{0}(-\sin nt)\,dt + \int_{0}^{\pi}\sin nt\,dt\right) = \frac{2}{n\pi}\,(1 - \cos n\pi) = \frac{4}{n\pi} \text{ for odd } n, \quad 0 \text{ for even } n" cite="reading1" at="p. 15" />

since $\cos n\pi$ is $-1$ for odd $n$ and $+1$ for even $n$. (The lecture, sketching this in the last minutes, drops the factor $\frac{1}{\pi}$ and says "four over n".) So

<Formula label="Square wave series" tex="f(t) = \frac{4}{\pi}\left(\sin t + \frac{\sin 3t}{3} + \frac{\sin 5t}{5} + \cdots\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 15" />

<WorkedExample title="Two more series">
<Problem>
Find the Fourier series of (a) the half-wave $f = 0$ on $(-\pi, 0)$, $f = 1$ on $(0, \pi)$, and (b) $f(t) = |t|$, both on $(-\pi, \pi)$ and continued with period $2\pi$. (c) Check (b) at $t = 0$, using $1 + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{25} + \cdots = \frac{\pi^2}{8}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A half-wave">
It is half the square wave plus $\frac{1}{2}$, so no new integrals are needed: $f = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{\pi}\left(\sin t + \frac{\sin 3t}{3} + \frac{\sin 5t}{5} + \cdots\right)$. Directly: the constant term is $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi}dt = \frac{1}{2}$, and $b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}\sin nt\,dt = \frac{1 - \cos n\pi}{n\pi}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A triangle wave">
$|t|\sin nt$ is odd, so every $b_n = 0$. The constant term is $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}|t|\,dt = \frac{\pi}{2}$, and $a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}t\cos nt\,dt = \frac{2}{\pi n^2}\left((-1)^n - 1\right)$, which is $-\frac{4}{\pi n^2}$ for odd $n$ and 0 for even $n$. So $|t| = \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{4}{\pi}\left(\cos t + \frac{\cos 3t}{9} + \frac{\cos 5t}{25} + \cdots\right)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A check">
At $t = 0$: $\frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{4}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{8} = \frac{\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{2} = 0 = |0|$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A periodic input is a sum of sines and cosines; by superposition its response is the same sum of known responses.
- Distinct functions among $\sin nt$, $\cos mt$ are orthogonal on $[-\pi, \pi]$; squares integrate to $\pi$. A proof uses only $u'' + n^2u = 0$ and integration by parts.
- $a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int f\cos nt$, $b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int f\sin nt$, constant term $\frac{a_0}{2}$.
- Square wave: $\frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n \text{ odd}}\frac{\sin nt}{n}$.
