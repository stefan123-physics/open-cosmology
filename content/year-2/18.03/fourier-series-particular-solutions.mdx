How does the ear hear three notes in a chord? The air carries one messy periodic wave, the sum of three pure oscillations, and most people hear the three tones in it: that is Fourier analysis, here with just three terms (transcript, p. 1). But the brain has no little integrator computing Fourier coefficients. The lecture builds up to what really happens (p. 1), using [Fourier series with other periods](/courses/18-03/fourier-series-even-odd-and-periods).

## A shortcut by shifting and stretching

Take the square wave $f(t)$ that is 0 on $(-1, 0)$ and 1 on $(0, 1)$, with period 2 (transcript, p. 2). It is neither even nor odd, and its half-period is $L = 1$, so the natural functions are $\sin n\pi t$ and $\cos n\pi t$ (p. 2). Rather than compute, reduce it to a known series (pp. 2–4):

1. Lower it by $\frac{1}{2}$: the result $S(t)$ is odd, jumping between $-\frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{1}{2}$.
2. The [square wave of Lecture 15](/courses/18-03/fourier-series-introduction), $g(u) = \frac{4}{\pi}\sum_{n \text{ odd}}\frac{\sin nu}{n}$, jumps between $-1$ and 1 with half-period $\pi$ (p. 3).
3. With $u = \pi t$, $S(t) = \frac{1}{2} g(\pi t)$ (p. 4).

So (p. 4)

<Formula label="Square wave of period 2" tex="f(t) = \frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{n \text{ odd}}\frac{\sin n\pi t}{n}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

## Responses term by term

To keep the calculation short, take the undamped spring (transcript, p. 5):

<Formula label="Undamped driven equation" tex="x'' + \omega_0^2\,x = f(t)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 5, 8" />

From the [lecture on resonance](/courses/18-03/resonance-and-beats), the input $\cos\omega t$ or $\sin\omega t$ has the particular solution $\cos\omega t/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)$ or $\sin\omega t/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)$ (pp. 5–6). Now expand a general input with period $2L$ (p. 6):

<Formula label="Input as a Fourier series" tex="f(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(a_n\cos\omega_nt + b_n\sin\omega_nt\right), \qquad \omega_n = \frac{n\pi}{L}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

By superposition, the responses add, and the constant term is the case $\omega = 0$ (p. 7); this assumes no $\omega_n$ equals $\omega_0$:

<Formula label="Response as a Fourier series" tex="x_p = \frac{a_0}{2\omega_0^2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_n\cos\omega_nt + b_n\sin\omega_nt}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_n^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

As soon as the input's Fourier series is known, the response's can be written down: the fundamental idea of Fourier series in this setting (p. 7).

## A hidden resonance

For the square wave above, $\omega_n = n\pi$ and (transcript, p. 8; the factor $\frac1n$ is restored as on p. 15)

<Formula label="Response to the square wave" tex="x_p = \frac{1}{2\omega_0^2} + \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{n \text{ odd}}\frac{\sin n\pi t}{n\,(\omega_0^2 - n^2\pi^2)}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 8, 15" />

What it looks like matters less than the sizes of the coefficients (p. 8). Take natural frequency $\omega_0 = 10$ (pp. 8–10). The constant term is $\frac{1}{200} = 0.005$. The $\sin\pi t$ term has denominator $100 - \pi^2 \approx 90$, the $\sin 3\pi t$ term $100 - 9\pi^2 \approx 11.2$, the $\sin 5\pi t$ term $100 - 25\pi^2 \approx -147$, and after that they are negligible. Working it out:

<Formula label="Coefficients for ω₀ = 10" tex="x_p \approx 0.005 + 0.0071\sin\pi t + 0.0190\sin 3\pi t - 0.0009\sin 5\pi t - \cdots" cite="reading1" at="pp. 9–10" />

(The lecture estimates these mentally, with rough arithmetic, and finds the $3\pi$ term about five times its neighbours; the exact ratios are about 2.7 and 22. The conclusion is the same.) The frequency $3\pi$ dominates the response: **near resonance** (p. 10). Yet $3\pi$ is nowhere to be seen in the input's graph. The Fourier coefficients reveal how much of each frequency is hidden in it, and the system picks out and resonates with the one closest to its natural frequency (p. 10). With $\omega_0 = 5$, no frequency of the input would be close: no resonance, and the slowest term would simply dominate (p. 11).

## How we hear

This, in the lecture's own simplified picture (Mattuck stresses that he is hand-waving about physiology), is how we hear (transcript, pp. 11–12). The eardrum vibrates with the wave $f(t)$; three small bones pass the vibration to a membrane of the fluid-filled inner ear. In the cochlea, the lecture says, are about 20,000 hair-like cells of graded lengths, like organ pipes, each with its own natural frequency (p. 11). Each resonates with the frequency in the wave closest to its own; only the groups near the three notes respond strongly, and they signal the auditory nerve. **The Fourier analysis is done by resonance** (p. 12).

## The book's method

The textbook and the notes substitute instead (transcript, pp. 12–14). Assume a particular solution shaped like the input, here $x_p = c_0 + \sum c_n\sin n\pi t$, differentiate twice term by term, substitute into $x'' + \omega_0^2x = f(t)$, and equate coefficients with the Fourier series of $f$ (pp. 13–14):

<Formula label="Equating coefficients" tex="c_0\,\omega_0^2 = \frac{1}{2}, \qquad c_n\,(\omega_0^2 - n^2\pi^2) = \frac{2}{n\pi} \;\; (n \text{ odd}), \qquad c_n = 0 \;\; (n \text{ even})" cite="reading1" at="pp. 14–15" />

the same answer as before (p. 15). It needs no memorised response formula, and takes about as long (pp. 12–13). (The transcript's last formula reads "$n$ over $\pi$ squared" for $(n\pi)^2$, p. 15.)

<WorkedExample title="Which frequency wins?">
<Problem>
The square wave $f$ above drives $x'' + \omega_0^2x = f(t)$. Find the largest terms of the response for (a) $\omega_0 = 6$ and (b) $\omega_0 = 15.5$. (c) Find the constant term for $\omega_0 = 6$ by equating coefficients.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) No resonance at 6">
The coefficients $\frac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{n(\omega_0^2 - n^2\pi^2)}$ are about $0.0244$ ($n = 1$), $-0.0040$ ($n = 3$) and $-0.0006$ ($n = 5$). The natural frequency 6 is close to $2\pi \approx 6.28$, but the square wave has no even terms, so nothing resonates: the slowest term simply dominates.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Resonance at 15.5">
Now $5\pi \approx 15.71$ is close: the coefficients are about $0.0028$ ($n = 1$), $0.0014$ ($n = 3$) and $-0.0196$ ($n = 5$). The $n = 5$ term is about seven times the next.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The constant term">
$c_0\cdot 36 = \frac{1}{2}$, so $c_0 = \frac{1}{72} \approx 0.0139$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Shift and stretch known series: the 0–1 square wave of period 2 is $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{\pi}\sum_{n \text{ odd}}\frac{\sin n\pi t}{n}$.
- For $x'' + \omega_0^2x = f(t)$, divide each term of the input's series by $\omega_0^2 - \omega_n^2$ to get a particular solution.
- If $\omega_0$ is close to some $\omega_n$, that term dominates: resonance with a frequency hidden in the input.
- The ear does Fourier analysis by resonance; the book's method of equating coefficients gives the same series.
