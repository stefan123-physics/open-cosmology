The lecture finishes the sinusoidal problem of [the previous lecture](/courses/18-03/sinusoidal-input-response) by a second method, proves the identity that method needs, and then sorts first-order linear equations into kinds and models (transcript, p. 1).

## Going Cartesian

The complex solution of $y' + ky = k\cos\omega t$ was $\tilde y = \frac{1}{1 + i\,(\omega/k)}\,e^{i\omega t}$, and going polar gave the response $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (\omega/k)^2}}\cos(\omega t - \phi)$ (transcript, p. 1). Instead, write both factors as $a + bi$: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate, and expand the exponential (p. 2):

<Formula label="Complex solution in Cartesian form" tex="\tilde y = \frac{1 - i\,(\omega/k)}{1 + (\omega/k)^2}\,(\cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Only the real part is wanted, so pick out just the two real terms of the product, $\cos\omega t$ and $(-i)(i)\,(\omega/k)\sin\omega t$, without multiplying out all four (p. 2):

<Formula label="Real part" tex="y_1 = \frac{1}{1 + (\omega/k)^2}\left(\cos\omega t + \frac{\omega}{k}\sin\omega t\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

This must equal the polar answer, unless mathematics contradicts itself; seeing that it does needs one formula that will be used all term (p. 3).

## The sine–cosine identity

A combination, with real coefficients, of a cosine and a sine **of the same angle** is a single cosine (transcript, p. 3):

<Formula label="Sine–cosine identity" tex="a\cos\theta + b\sin\theta = c\cos(\theta - \phi), \qquad c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}, \qquad \cos\phi = \frac{a}{c}, \quad \sin\phi = \frac{b}{c}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–4" />

The angles must match: with $\theta_1$ in one term and $\theta_2$ in the other there is a formula, but nobody remembers it (p. 3). (These formulas for $c$ and $\phi$ are this page's; the lecture gives only the picture.) Rather than memorise them, remember the picture: **$a$ and $b$ are the sides of a right triangle, $c$ is its hypotenuse and $\phi$ the angle opposite $b$** (pp. 3–4).

Applied to the Cartesian answer, $a = 1$ and $b = \omega/k$: the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{1 + (\omega/k)^2}$ and $\phi = \arctan(\omega/k)$, and the factor in front cancels down to exactly the polar answer (p. 4). The identity is how to read an answer like this: answer books often give a messy $a$ and a messy $b$, while the useful information is the amplitude and phase lag (p. 4). Both sides have two parameters; the right side shows the result is just a pure oscillation of the same frequency, with a new amplitude and a shift (p. 5).

**Three proofs** (pp. 5–7).

1. The **high-school proof** expands $\cos(\theta - \phi)$ by the difference formula and matches coefficients: $a = c\cos\phi$, $b = c\sin\phi$. Correct and simplest, but it runs in the wrong direction: in practice one starts from the left side (p. 5).
2. The **18.02 proof** (p. 6): the left side is the dot product of the vector $\langle a, b\rangle$ with the unit vector $\langle\cos\theta, \sin\theta\rangle$. Evaluated geometrically, it is the product of the lengths, $c \cdot 1$, times the cosine of the angle between them, $\theta - \phi$.
3. The **18.03 proof** (p. 7) uses complex numbers. The left side is the real part of $(a - bi)(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$. In polar form $a - bi$ has modulus $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$ and angle $-\phi$ (it lies below the axis), so

<Formula label="The complex proof" tex="(a - bi)(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\,e^{-i\phi}\,e^{i\theta} = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}\,e^{i(\theta - \phi)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

and taking real parts of both sides gives the identity (p. 7). It took mathematicians three or four hundred years to get used to complex numbers, so a few weeks is not bad (p. 8).

## Three kinds of first-order linear equation

From the most special to the most general (transcript, p. 8):

<Formula label="Three forms" tex="y' + ky = k\,q_e(t), \qquad y' + ky = q(t), \qquad y' + p(t)\,y = q(t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

The first, with $k$ constant and appearing providentially on the right as well, is the temperature–concentration model, or conduction–diffusion model. The second keeps $k$ constant but has no natural $k$ on the right; the third is the general linear equation, solved by an integral (p. 8). Cutting across the first two is a second distinction: whether $k > 0$, assumed so far (p. 9).

## A mixing tank

The first form also fits mixing (transcript, p. 9). A tank of volume $V$ is kept full: liquid flows in at rate $r$ and, since it cannot pile up, out at rate $r$. The incoming liquid has concentration $c_e(t)$ of salt (grams per litre, say), and $x(t)$ is the amount of salt in the tank (pp. 9–10). Work with the amount, which you can think about physically (p. 10):

<Formula label="Salt balance" tex="\frac{dx}{dt} = r\,c_e - r\,\frac{x}{V}" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

Salt flows in at flow rate times incoming concentration, and out at flow rate times the tank's concentration, $x/V$ (p. 10). Solve it in this form; but to understand it, write it for the tank's concentration $c = x/V$, so $x' = Vc'$ (p. 11; the transcript says $c$ times $dC/dt$ here, but the factor is the volume):

<Formula label="Mixing in concentration form" tex="c' + \frac{r}{V}\,c = \frac{r}{V}\,c_e(t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 11" />

This is exactly the first form, so the language of input and response applies, even though mixing has nothing physically to do with diffusion through a membrane (pp. 11–12). The analogue of the conductivity is $k = r/V$, the **fractional rate of outflow**: if $r/V = 1/10$ per minute, a tenth of the tank is replaced each minute. It has units of reciprocal time, and the separate quantities $r$ and $V$ no longer matter (p. 12). (In the transcript the tank concentration is a capital C; this page uses $c$, to keep $C$ for capacitance below.)

If a factory upstream releases a pollutant on a daily cycle, $c_e$ is roughly sinusoidal (p. 12). The tank follows it closely, with amplitude near 1 and little lag, when $k$ is large: a fast flow or a small tank (p. 13).

## When the right side has no natural k

**An RC circuit** (transcript, pp. 13–14). With a resistance $R$, a capacitance $C$ and an electromotive force $E(t)$, use the charge $q$ on the capacitor as the variable; the current is $i = dq/dt$. Kirchhoff's voltage law adds the drops $Ri$ across the resistor and $q/C$ across the capacitor (p. 14):

<Formula label="RC circuit" tex="R\,\frac{dq}{dt} + \frac{q}{C} = E(t), \qquad q' + \frac{1}{RC}\,q = \frac{E(t)}{R}" cite="reading1" at="p. 14" />

Here $k = 1/RC$, but writing the right side as $\frac{1}{RC}\,(CE)$ would be unnatural, so this is the second form (pp. 14–15). (The lecture calls $q/C$ a form of Coulomb's law; it is more usually described as the definition of capacitance.)

**A radioactive chain** (p. 15). $A$ decays into $B$, which decays further. $B$ gains what $A$ loses, $k_1A$, with $A = A_0e^{-k_1t}$, and loses $k_2B$:

<Formula label="Decay chain" tex="B' + k_2B = k_1A_0\,e^{-k_1t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 15" />

There is no reason the constant on the right should have anything to do with $k_2$ (pp. 15–16).

## When k is negative

If $k < 0$ the method of solution is identical, but none of the language of transient, steady state, input and response applies (transcript, p. 16). Write $k = -a$ with $a > 0$:

<Formula label="Solution for k = −a" tex="y' - ay = q(t), \qquad y = e^{at}\int q(t)\,e^{-at}\,dt + C\,e^{at}" cite="reading1" at="p. 16" />

Now $Ce^{at}$ grows without bound (unless $C = 0$): it is not transient, and the solution depends heavily on the initial condition for ever (p. 16). This is typical of biology and economics: population growth $dP/dt = aP$ has $k = -a$. In the lecture's words, if life is involved $k$ is likely to be negative; for inanimate things it is positive (pp. 16–17).

<WorkedExample title="A pond downstream of a factory">
<Problem>
A pond of $2000\ \mathrm{m^3}$ has water flowing through at $50\ \mathrm{m^3}$ per hour. The incoming concentration of a pollutant follows a daily cycle, $c_e = 3 + 2\cos\omega t$ mg/L with a 24-hour period. (a) Find $k$ and $\omega$. (b) Find the steady-state swing and lag in the pond. (c) Repeat with ten times the flow, $500\ \mathrm{m^3}$ per hour. (d) Write $3\cos\theta + 4\sin\theta$ as a single cosine.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The constants">
$k = r/V = 50/2000 = 0.025$ per hour, and $\omega = 2\pi/24 \approx 0.262$ per hour, so $\omega/k \approx 10.47$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The pond's response">
By superposition the constant 3 passes straight through. $A = 1/\sqrt{1 + 10.47^2} \approx 0.0951$, so the pond swings by only $\pm 0.19$ mg/L. $\phi = \arctan 10.47 \approx 1.476$ rad (84.5°), a lag of $\phi/\omega \approx 5.64$ hours.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Ten times the flow">
$k = 0.25$ per hour and $\omega/k \approx 1.047$: $A \approx 0.691$, a swing of $\pm 1.38$ mg/L, and $\phi \approx 0.808$ rad (46.3°), a lag of about 3.09 hours. Faster flushing makes the pond follow the inflow more closely.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) The identity">
The triangle has sides 3 and 4, so $c = 5$ and $\phi = \arctan\frac43 \approx 0.927$ rad (53.1°): $3\cos\theta + 4\sin\theta = 5\cos(\theta - 0.927)$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Going Cartesian gives $\frac{\cos\omega t + (\omega/k)\sin\omega t}{1 + (\omega/k)^2}$, the same response as going polar.
- $a\cos\theta + b\sin\theta = c\cos(\theta - \phi)$: $a$, $b$ the legs, $c$ the hypotenuse, $\phi$ opposite $b$.
- Three kinds: $y' + ky = kq_e$, $y' + ky = q$, $y' + p(t)y = q$. Mixing is the first kind with $k = r/V$; RC circuits and decay chains the second.
- For $k < 0$ the method is the same, but the initial value never stops mattering.
