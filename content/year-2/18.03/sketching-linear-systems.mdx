A computer plots the solutions of a system very accurately, but sketching them yourself is a skill needed for nonlinear equations, and it involves some important ideas (transcript, p. 1). The tool is the [eigenvalue method](/courses/18-03/repeated-and-complex-eigenvalues).

## A model of competition

Two neighbouring states compete for tourists by advertising (transcript, pp. 1–2). $x$ and $y$ are each state's departure from its normal advertising budget (p. 1):

<Formula label="Competition model" tex="x' = -x + b\,y, \qquad y' = c\,x - 3y" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The diagonal terms pull each budget back to normal: overspending is criticised, underspending makes the local chambers of commerce protest, three times more loudly in the second state (pp. 1–2). The off-diagonal terms are the warlike ones: normally positive, a matching response to the rival's extra spending. The same model was long used for arms races (p. 2).

## A nodal sink

Take $b = 2$, $c = 0$: the second state ignores the first (transcript, pp. 2–3). Then $A = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 0 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$, with $\lambda^2 + 4\lambda + 3 = (\lambda + 3)(\lambda + 1) = 0$ (p. 3). For $\lambda = -3$ the equations reduce to $2a_1 + 2a_2 = 0$, so $(1, -1)$; for $\lambda = -1$ they say only $2a_2 = 0$, so $(1, 0)$ (pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Solution" tex="\vec x = c_1\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}e^{-3t} + c_2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}e^{-t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

These are parametric equations, as in 18.02, but for curves of a kind not met there; the aim is a sketch without calculation (p. 4).

**Step 1: the four easy solutions** (pp. 5–6). With $c_1 = \pm1$, $c_2 = 0$, the solution starts at $\pm(1, -1)$ and moves along that line, shrinking like $e^{-3t}$, towards the origin without reaching it; it came in from infinity. With $c_1 = 0$, $c_2 = \pm1$ the same happens along $(1, 0)$. **The eigenvector gives the line, the sign of the eigenvalue the direction** (p. 6).

**Step 2: fill in by continuity** (pp. 6–7). The velocity field changes continuously, so nearby trajectories behave alike: all go to the origin. But how?

**Step 3: dominance** (pp. 7–9). As $t \to \infty$, $e^{-3t}$ is negligible beside $e^{-t}$ (compare $e^{-30}$ with $e^{-10}$), so trajectories come into the origin **along $(1, 0)$**. As $t \to -\infty$, $e^{-3t}$ dominates, so far from the origin trajectories run **parallel to $(1, -1)$**: parallel, not on it (pp. 8–9). So each trajectory comes in parallel to the fast direction and turns to join the slow one (p. 9).

This picture is a **node**; since everything flows into the origin, a **nodal sink** (or sink node), and the system is **asymptotically stable** (pp. 9–11). For the tourists, peace: both budgets return to normal (p. 9). With both eigenvalues positive, say $e^{3t}$ and $e^{t}$, the same picture with reversed arrows is a **nodal source**, unstable (pp. 10–11).

## A saddle

Make both states combative: $b = 3$, $c = 5$ (transcript, p. 11). Now $A = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 3 \\ 5 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$ with $\lambda^2 + 4\lambda - 12 = (\lambda + 6)(\lambda - 2) = 0$ (p. 11). For $\lambda = -6$: $5a_1 + 3a_2 = 0$, eigenvector $(3, -5)$; for $\lambda = 2$: $-3a_1 + 3a_2 = 0$, eigenvector $(1, 1)$ (pp. 11–12):

<Formula label="Saddle solution" tex="\vec x = c_1\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -5 \end{pmatrix}e^{-6t} + c_2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}e^{2t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 12" />

Along $(3, -5)$ solutions come in; along $(1, 1)$ they go out (p. 12). Filling in by continuity, a trajectory starting near the incoming line heads for the origin, then turns away along the outgoing one (p. 13). The pattern is that of contour lines near a **saddle** point in 18.02, a mountain pass, and the picture is called a saddle (pp. 13–14). It is neither source nor sink, and it is **unstable**: only solutions starting exactly on the incoming line reach the origin, a physical impossibility (p. 14). In the part of the picture that makes sense for the model, out along $(1, 1)$, the states spend without limit and nobody gains tourists: a model of what not to do (p. 14).

## A spiral sink

Now let the first state answer extra spending by spending less: $b = -1$, $c = 2$ (transcript, pp. 14–15). $A = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 2 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\lambda^2 + 4\lambda + 5 = 0$ (p. 15):

<Formula label="Complex eigenvalues" tex="\lambda = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 20}}{2} = -2 \pm i" cite="reading1" at="p. 15" />

A real solution has the form $e^{-2t}$ times $x = a_1\cos t + b_1\sin t$, $y = a_2\cos t + b_2\sin t$ (pp. 15–16). That curve is bounded and periodic, and satisfies an equation $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 = D$, so it is a conic, and the only bounded, repeating conics are ellipses (pp. 16–17). With the shrinking factor, each turn is pulled closer to the origin: a **spiral sink** (p. 17). Which way does it turn? Draw one velocity vector: at $(1, 0)$ the system gives $(-1, 2)$, pointing up and left, so the motion is counterclockwise (p. 17). For the tourists, peace again (p. 17).

<Step title="(a) Two positive eigenvalues">
The matrix is triangular, so the eigenvalues are 1 and 2: a nodal source, unstable.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Opposite signs">
$\lambda^2 - 1 = 0$, so $\lambda = \pm 1$: a saddle, with eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ outgoing and $(1, -1)$ incoming.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A spiral">
Trace $-2$, determinant $1 + 4 = 5$: $\lambda^2 + 2\lambda + 5 = 0$, $\lambda = -1 \pm 2i$, a spiral sink. At $(1, 0)$ the velocity is $(-1, -1)$, pointing down: clockwise.
</Step>

## Summary

- Draw the normal modes first: eigenvector lines, arrows set by the signs of the eigenvalues.
- Fill in by continuity, using dominance: the term with the larger eigenvalue wins as $t \to \infty$, the one with the smaller as $t \to -\infty$.
- Same-sign eigenvalues: node (sink stable, source unstable). Opposite signs: saddle, unstable. Complex with negative real part: spiral sink; one velocity vector gives the direction.
