The aim sounds modest: the Fourier series of the simple periodic function that equals $t$ between $-\pi$ and $\pi$ (transcript, p. 1). The first half of the lecture shows how to shorten such calculations; the second extends Fourier series beyond period $2\pi$ (p. 2). It continues [the introduction to Fourier series](/courses/18-03/fourier-series-introduction).

## One function, one series

Since $a_n$ and $b_n$ are given by formulas, a function determines its coefficients: **if $f(t) = g(t)$, they have the same Fourier series**, coefficient by coefficient (transcript, p. 1). What happens at single points does not matter, since the integrals ignore them (p. 1).

## Even and odd functions

If $f$ is **even**, $f(-t) = f(t)$, its Fourier series has only cosine terms: every $b_n = 0$ (transcript, p. 2). Proof by uniqueness (pp. 2–3): substituting $-t$ for $t$ leaves each $\cos nt$ alone and changes the sign of each $\sin nt$, so

<Formula label="Series of f(−t)" tex="f(-t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(a_n\cos nt - b_n\sin nt\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

For even $f$ this is the same function as $f(t)$, so the two series agree: $b_n = -b_n$, and $b_n = 0$ (p. 3). This proves the converse of the obvious fact that a sum of cosines is even (p. 4). Likewise, if $f$ is **odd**, every $a_n = 0$, including $a_0$ (p. 4).

**Half the integral** (pp. 4–5). If $f$ is even, so is $f(t)\cos nt$, and the integral of an even function over $[-\pi, \pi]$ is twice that over $[0, \pi]$, a much friendlier interval with fewer minus signs to lose (p. 5). If $f$ is odd, $f(t)\sin nt$ is **odd times odd, which is even**: these are statements about functions, not about odd and even numbers (pp. 5–6). So (p. 5)

<Formula label="Half-interval formulas" tex="f \text{ even: } a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}f(t)\cos nt\,dt; \qquad f \text{ odd: } b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}f(t)\sin nt\,dt" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

## The sawtooth

The function $t$ on $(-\pi, \pi)$, repeated with period $2\pi$, is odd, so only $b_n$ is needed (transcript, pp. 6–7). Integrating by parts (pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="Sawtooth coefficients" tex="b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi}t\sin nt\,dt = \frac{2}{\pi}\left(\Big[-\frac{t\cos nt}{n}\Big]_0^{\pi} + \Big[\frac{\sin nt}{n^2}\Big]_0^{\pi}\right) = \frac{2}{n}(-1)^{n+1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

using $\cos n\pi = (-1)^n$ (p. 8). So (pp. 8–9)

<Formula label="Sawtooth series" tex="t = 2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin nt = 2\left(\sin t - \frac{\sin 2t}{2} + \frac{\sin 3t}{3} - \cdots\right)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 8–9" />

**Not a Taylor series** (p. 9). The first term $2\sin t$ leaves the origin with slope 2, while $t$ has slope 1: thinking "Taylor", that looks wrong. But a Taylor series concentrates on one point and improves there term by term, while a Fourier series tries to approximate the function as well as possible over the whole interval. Each new term corrects the sum everywhere, and the earlier coefficients never need changing (pp. 9–10).

## Convergence

The theorem, too far outside the course to prove, for the piecewise smooth functions of applications (the lecture states it without conditions; for an arbitrary continuous function it can fail) (transcript, pp. 10–12):

- If $f$ is continuous at $t_0$, the Fourier series converges there, to $f(t_0)$ (p. 10).
- If $f$ has a **jump** at $t_0$, the usual kind of discontinuity in applications, the series converges to **the midpoint of the jump** (p. 11).

So the sawtooth series converges to $t$ on $(-\pi, \pi)$, but at $\pm\pi$, where the function jumps from $\pi$ down to $-\pi$, it converges to 0, whatever value $f$ was given there (pp. 11–12). Here one can check it directly: at $t = \pi$ every term is 0 (p. 12).

## Period 2L

Applications rarely have period $2\pi$; often the half-period $L$ is 1 (transcript, p. 15). Changing the variable stretches the axis: $u = \pi t/L$ sends $t = L$ to $u = \pi$ (p. 13). The natural functions are $\cos(n\pi t/L)$ and $\sin(n\pi t/L)$, which have period $2L$, the sine vanishing at every multiple of $L$ (pp. 13–14), and the formulas are the old ones with $\pi$ replaced by $L$ (p. 14):

<Formula label="Period 2L" tex="f(t) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(a_n\cos\frac{n\pi t}{L} + b_n\sin\frac{n\pi t}{L}\right), \qquad a_n = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L}f(t)\cos\frac{n\pi t}{L}\,dt" cite="reading1" at="p. 14" />

and $b_n$ likewise with the sine. The even and odd shortcuts carry over: for even $f$, $a_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^Lf(t)\cos\frac{n\pi t}{L}\,dt$ (pp. 14–15).

## Functions on a finite interval

Most functions are not periodic, but Fourier series still apply to a function defined on a finite interval $[0, L]$ (functions on the whole line need Fourier transforms instead) (transcript, p. 15). Make it periodic (pp. 15–16):

- The **even periodic extension** reflects $f$ into $[-L, 0]$ and repeats with period $2L$; it has a cosine series.
- The **odd periodic extension** reflects $f$ through the origin and repeats; it has a sine series.

For $t^2$ on $[0, 1]$ the even extension is continuous, while the odd one jumps at $\pm1$, where its series converges to the midpoints (pp. 15–16). Only the part on $[0, L]$ is then used, for instance as the input of a differential equation (p. 16).

<WorkedExample title="Extensions and a convergence check">
<Problem>
(a) Find the cosine series of the even extension of $f(t) = t$ on $[0, 2]$. (b) Find the sine series of the odd extension of $f(t) = 1$ on $[0, 2]$; what does it converge to at $t = 0$? (c) Evaluate the sawtooth series at $t = \pi/2$, using $1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \cdots = \frac{\pi}{4}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A triangle wave of period 4">
$L = 2$. The constant term is $\frac{a_0}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^2t\,dt = 1$, and $a_n = \int_0^2t\cos\frac{n\pi t}{2}\,dt = \frac{4}{n^2\pi^2}\left((-1)^n - 1\right)$: $-\frac{8}{n^2\pi^2}$ for odd $n$, 0 for even. So $f = 1 - \frac{8}{\pi^2}\left(\cos\frac{\pi t}{2} + \frac{1}{9}\cos\frac{3\pi t}{2} + \cdots\right)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A square wave of period 4">
$b_n = \int_0^2\sin\frac{n\pi t}{2}\,dt = \frac{2}{n\pi}(1 - \cos n\pi)$: $\frac{4}{n\pi}$ for odd $n$, 0 for even. At $t = 0$ the extension jumps from $-1$ to $1$, and every term of the series is 0: it converges to the midpoint, 0.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The sawtooth at π/2">
$\sin\frac{n\pi}{2}$ is $1, 0, -1, 0, \dots$, so the series is $2\left(1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \cdots\right) = 2\cdot\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac{\pi}{2}$, which is $f(\pi/2)$, as the theorem says at a point of continuity.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A function has only one Fourier series: even functions have only cosines, odd ones only sines.
- For even or odd $f$, integrate over $[0, \pi]$ and double.
- Sawtooth: $t = 2\sum(-1)^{n+1}\sin(nt)/n$. Fourier series approximate over the whole interval, converging to $f$ where it is continuous and to the midpoint at jumps.
- Period $2L$: use $\cos(n\pi t/L)$, $\sin(n\pi t/L)$ and replace $\pi$ by $L$; on a finite interval, use an even or odd periodic extension.
