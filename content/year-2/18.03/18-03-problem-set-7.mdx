This set practises five lessons: [the Laplace transform](/courses/18-03/laplace-transform-introduction), [transforms of derivatives](/courses/18-03/laplace-transform-of-derivatives), [the convolution formula](/courses/18-03/convolution), [the unit step and discontinuous inputs](/courses/18-03/laplace-discontinuous-inputs), and [impulse inputs, the delta function and the weight function](/courses/18-03/impulse-inputs-delta-function). It follows MIT's Problem Set 7, in our own words.

MIT's sheet has two parts. Part II holds MIT's own three problems (68 points), and MIT's solutions are on OpenCourseWare. All three are written out below, with hints and solutions in our own words, following the method of MIT's solutions and checked again independently. Where MIT's solutions leave a piece out, the solution is our own, and the page says so. Part I (28 points at MIT) is a list of shorter exercises; the last section of this page lists it.

MIT labels each problem with the day of its lecture in the Spring 2010 calendar, whose numbers differ from the lecture numbers of this site. MIT's Problem 26 belongs to Lectures 19, 21 and 22 here, Problem 27 to Lectures 20, 21 and 23, and Problem 28 to Lectures 19, 20 and 23.

Two words of MIT's. In this set the weight function $w(t)$ of the lessons is called the **unit impulse response**, and the response to the unit step $u(t)$ is the **unit step response** $v(t)$, as in [Problem Set 6](/courses/18-03/18-03-problem-set-6). The variable $s$ of the transform is the **frequency domain**, and $t$ is the **time domain**. This page writes the transform as $\mathcal{L}(f)$ or $F(s)$, as the lessons do (MIT writes $\mathcal{L}[f]$).

## Problem 26

<Exercise id="p26" title="Three facts about the transform" points={26} mit="Part II, Problem 26">

The Laplace transform of a function $f(t)$ is defined by an integral ([the lesson](/courses/18-03/laplace-transform-introduction)):

<Formula label="Laplace transform" tex="F(s) = \mathcal{L}(f) = \int_0^{\infty}f(t)\,e^{-st}\,dt" cite="solutions" at="Problem 26(a), p. 1" />

Each part of this problem goes back to that integral.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Let $F(s)$ be the Laplace transform of $f(t)$, and let $a > 0$. Find a formula for the Laplace transform of $g(t) = f(at)$ in terms of $F$, by writing out the integral and changing the variable. Then verify your formula: compute $\mathcal{L}(f(t))$ and $\mathcal{L}(f(at))$ for $f(t) = t^n$ with the known transforms and rules, and compare.

<Hint>

Write $G(s) = \int_0^{\infty}f(at)\,e^{-st}\,dt$. It would look like the integral for $F$ if the function were taken at the variable of integration itself.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Take $p = at$ as the new variable. Replace $t$ in the exponent and $dt$, and check what happens to the two limits (this is where $a > 0$ is used). Then read the new exponent as $e^{-(\text{something})\,p}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p26-a" label="$\mathcal{L}(f(at)) =$"
  choices={["a·F(as)", "F(s/a)", "(1/a)·F(s/a)", "(1/a)·F(as)"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p26-a-cube" label="For $f(t) = t^3$ (the case $n = 3$, our own choice): $\mathcal{L}(f(at)) =$" expr="6*a^3/s^4"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 2], s: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ a: "a", s: "s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "6*a/s^4", hint: "(at)³ is a³t³: the constant comes out cubed." },
    { expr: "6*a^4/s^4", hint: "That is F(s/a) alone. The change of variable also turns dt into a multiple of the new differential, and that factor is missing." },
    { expr: "6*a^3/s^3", hint: "The transform of t³ has s⁴ below: the power of s is one more than the power of t." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The substitution $p = at$ turns the integral for $G$ into the integral for $F$, taken at $s/a$ instead of $s$.

<Step title="Change the variable">

With $p = at$ we have $t = p/a$ and $dt = dp/a$. Because $a > 0$, the limits stay $0$ and $\infty$. (MIT calls the new variable $u$; this page keeps $u$ for the unit step.)

<Formula label="The scaling rule" tex="G(s) = \int_0^{\infty}f(at)\,e^{-st}\,dt = \frac{1}{a}\int_0^{\infty}f(p)\,e^{-(s/a)p}\,dp = \frac{1}{a}\,F\!\left(\frac{s}{a}\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 26(a), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Verify with a power">

For $f(t) = t^n$ [the lesson](/courses/18-03/laplace-transform-introduction) gives $F(s) = \dfrac{n!}{s^{n+1}}$. Directly: $g(t) = (at)^n = a^nt^n$, and by linearity $G(s) = \dfrac{a^n\,n!}{s^{n+1}}$. With the rule:

<Formula label="The rule for a power" tex="\frac{1}{a}\,F\!\left(\frac{s}{a}\right) = \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{n!}{(s/a)^{n+1}} = \frac{a^{n+1}}{a}\cdot\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}} = \frac{a^n\,n!}{s^{n+1}}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 26(a), p. 1" />

The two agree. For $n = 3$ this is $\dfrac{6a^3}{s^4}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second test, of our own: $f(t) = e^{-t}$ has $F(s) = \dfrac{1}{s + 1}$, and $f(at) = e^{-at}$ has the transform $\dfrac{1}{s + a}$. The rule gives $\dfrac{1}{a}\cdot\dfrac{1}{s/a + 1} = \dfrac{1}{s + a}$, the same. The rule also makes sense: for $a > 1$ the function $f(at)$ runs through its values $a$ times faster, and its transform is spread out $a$ times wider in $s$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Use what you know about double integrals ([change of variables](/courses/18-02/change-of-variables) in 18.02): show that if $h(t) = f(t) * g(t)$, then $H(s) = F(s)\,G(s)$. Do it in these steps. Write $F(s) = \int_0^{\infty}f(x)\,e^{-sx}\,dx$ and $G(s) = \int_0^{\infty}g(y)\,e^{-sy}\,dy$; write the product as a double integral; and change coordinates by $x = t - \tau$, $y = \tau$. The convolution is taken in MIT's order:

<Formula label="Convolution" tex="f(t) * g(t) = \int_0^{t}f(t - \tau)\,g(\tau)\,d\tau" cite="solutions" at="Problem 26(b), p. 1" />

<Hint>

The product of two single integrals with different variables is one double integral. Over which region of the $(x, y)$ plane?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the new coordinates you need three things: what $x + y$ becomes, the Jacobian of the change, and the description of the region. For the region, translate $x > 0$ and $y > 0$ into conditions on $t$ and $\tau$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p26-b-region" label="In the coordinates $(t, \tau)$ the region $x > 0$, $y > 0$ is"
  choices={["all t > 0 and all τ > 0", "0 < t < τ", "0 < τ < t", "0 < τ < 1 and t > 0"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The product $F(s)\,G(s)$ is a double integral over the first quadrant. The new coordinate $t = x + y$ makes the exponential $e^{-st}$, and what is left inside is the convolution.

<Step title="The product as a double integral">

<Formula label="Over the first quadrant" tex="F(s)\,G(s) = \int_0^{\infty}\!\!\int_0^{\infty}f(x)\,e^{-sx}\,g(y)\,e^{-sy}\,dx\,dy = \int\!\!\int_R f(x)\,g(y)\,e^{-s(x + y)}\,dx\,dy" cite="solutions" at="Problem 26(b), p. 1" />

where $R$ is the first quadrant, $x > 0$ and $y > 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The new coordinates">

With $x = t - \tau$ and $y = \tau$ we get $x + y = t$. The Jacobian is the determinant of the four partial derivatives of $x$ and $y$ with respect to $t$ and $\tau$:

<Formula label="The Jacobian" tex="\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1, \qquad\text{so}\qquad dx\,dy = dt\,d\tau" cite="solutions" at="Problem 26(b), p. 1" />

The region: $y > 0$ says $\tau > 0$, and $x > 0$ says $\tau < t$. So $t$ runs over all positive numbers, and for a fixed $t$ the variable $\tau$ runs from $0$ to $t$.

</Step>

<Step title="Put it together">

<Formula label="The convolution formula" tex="F(s)\,G(s) = \int_0^{\infty}\left(\int_0^{t}f(t - \tau)\,g(\tau)\,d\tau\right)e^{-st}\,dt = \int_0^{\infty}h(t)\,e^{-st}\,dt = H(s)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 26(b), p. 1" />

The inner integral is the convolution $h(t) = f(t) * g(t)$, and the outer integral is its Laplace transform.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A test of our own: $f = g = 1$. Then $f * g = \int_0^t d\tau = t$, with the transform $\dfrac{1}{s^2}$, and $F(s)\,G(s) = \dfrac{1}{s}\cdot\dfrac{1}{s}$ is the same. [The lesson on convolution](/courses/18-03/convolution) gives this proof with the variables $u$ and $v$ in place of $x$ and $y$, and with the two functions in the other order.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Let $f(t) = 1$ for $0 < t < 1$ and $f(t) = 0$ for $t > 1$. Find its Laplace transform from the integral that defines it. For which $s$ does the integral converge (its region of convergence)? (MIT's sheet prints the second condition as $t > 0$, a misprint: MIT's solution takes $f = 0$ from $t = 1$ on.)

<Hint>

Split the integral at $t = 1$. What does the part from $1$ to $\infty$ contribute?

</Hint>

<Hint>

What is left is an integral over a finite interval of a continuous function. Can it fail to converge for any $s$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p26-c" label="For $s \ne 0$: $F(s) =$" expr="(1 - exp(-s))/s"
  vars={{ s: [0.3, 4] }}
  names={{ s: "s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/s", hint: "That is the transform of the constant 1 for all t > 0. This f is switched off at t = 1, so the integral stops there." },
    { expr: "exp(-s)/s", hint: "That is the integral of e^(−st) from 1 to ∞, the stretch where f is 0. You need the stretch from 0 to 1." },
    { expr: "(exp(-s) - 1)/s", hint: "For positive s your answer is negative, but the integrand is never negative. Check the sign that the antiderivative of e^(−st) brings." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p26-c-region" label="The integral converges for"
  choices={["every complex number s", "s with real part larger than 0, and no other s", "s with real part larger than 1, and no other s", "real s, and no other s"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The function is zero after $t = 1$, so the improper integral is really an integral from $0$ to $1$.

<Step title="The integral">

<Formula label="Transform of the box" tex="F(s) = \int_0^{1}e^{-st}\,dt + \int_1^{\infty}0\cdot e^{-st}\,dt = \left[\frac{e^{-st}}{-s}\right]_0^1 = \frac{1 - e^{-s}}{s}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 26(c), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The region of convergence">

The integral from $0$ to $1$ of the continuous function $e^{-st}$ exists for every $s$, real or complex: nothing has to die away at infinity. So the region of convergence is the whole complex plane. Compare $\mathcal{L}(1) = \dfrac{1}{s}$, which needs the real part of $s$ to be positive.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $s = 0$ the formula reads $\frac{0}{0}$, but the integral itself is $\int_0^1 1\,dt = 1$, and the formula has the limit $1$ there too (MIT's remark: by l'Hôpital's rule, for example). A number: $F(1) = 1 - e^{-1} = 0.6321$. The function is the unit box $u(t) - u(t - 1)$ of [the lesson on the unit step](/courses/18-03/laplace-discontinuous-inputs), whose transform there, $\dfrac{e^{-as} - e^{-bs}}{s}$ with $a = 0$ and $b = 1$, is the same.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 27

<Exercise id="p27" title="A first-order operator in the frequency domain" points={22} mit="Part II, Problem 27">

Let $a$ and $b$ be real numbers with $a \ne 0$. The operator $aD + bI$ stands for the system $a\dot x + bx = q(t)$ with input $q$: $D$ means differentiation and $I$ leaves a function as it is. Its unit impulse response $w(t)$ is the solution with input $\delta(t)$ that is zero for $t < 0$ (rest initial conditions), and its unit step response $v(t)$ is the solution with input $u(t)$ that is zero for $t < 0$.

MIT's problem advises one rule for the whole problem. If $x(t)$ is zero for $t < 0$ and $\dot x$ means the generalized derivative (with a delta function for a jump at $t = 0$, as in [Problem Set 6](/courses/18-03/18-03-problem-set-6)), then

<Formula label="Transform of a generalized derivative" tex="\mathcal{L}(\dot x) = s\,X(s)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 27(a), p. 2" />

with no term for the starting value. (How this fits the rule $\mathcal{L}(f') = sF(s) - f(0)$ of [the lesson](/courses/18-03/laplace-transform-of-derivatives), in our own words: if $x$ jumps from $0$ to the value $x(0^+)$ at $t = 0$, its generalized derivative contains the extra term $x(0^+)\,\delta(t)$, whose transform $x(0^+)$ cancels the $-x(0^+)$ of the lesson's rule.)

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the unit impulse response $w(t)$ and the unit step response $v(t)$ of $aD + bI$: apply the Laplace transform to the equation, solve for the transform, and go back to a function of $t$. For $v$ take $b \ne 0$ first, then say what happens for $b = 0$ (this case is our own addition).

<Hint>

The transform of $\delta(t)$ is $1$ and the transform of $u(t)$ is $\dfrac{1}{s}$. With the rule above, the left side $a\dot x + bx$ becomes $(as + b)X(s)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $w$: write $\dfrac{1}{as + b}$ with $s$ plus a constant below, and compare with the transform of an exponential. For $v$ you need partial fractions first.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p27-a-w" label="For $t > 0$: $w(t) =$" expr="exp(-b*t/a)/a"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2], t: [0.2, 3] }}
  names={{ a: "a", b: "b", t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-b*t/a)", hint: "Your response starts at the value 1. In aẋ + bx = δ(t) the kick is shared out over the coefficient a: the jump of x is not 1 unless a = 1." },
    { expr: "exp(-a*t/b)/a", hint: "Your decay rate is a/b. The rate comes from the pole of 1/(as + b): solve as + b = 0 for s once more." },
    { expr: "exp(-b*t)/a", hint: "Your decay rate is b. Write as + b as a times (s + a constant) before you read off the exponent." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p27-a-v" label="For $t > 0$ and $b \ne 0$: $v(t) =$" expr="(1 - exp(-b*t/a))/b"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2], t: [0.2, 3] }}
  names={{ a: "a", b: "b", t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(1 - exp(-b*t/a))/a", hint: "For large t (with a and b positive) your answer settles at 1/a. Put a constant x into aẋ + bx = 1 to see where the system must settle." },
    { expr: "1 - exp(-b*t/a)", hint: "For large t (with a and b positive) your answer settles at 1. Put a constant x into aẋ + bx = 1 to see where the system must settle." },
    { expr: "(1 - exp(-b*t/a))/(a*b)", hint: "Your slope at t = 0 is 1/a². Just after t = 0, where x is still 0, the equation says aẋ = 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the frequency domain the differential equation is an algebraic one: $(as + b)$ times the transform of the response equals the transform of the input.

<Step title="The impulse response">

The transform of $a\dot w + bw = \delta(t)$ is $asW(s) + bW(s) = 1$, so

<Formula label="Unit impulse response" tex="W(s) = \frac{1}{as + b} = \frac{1/a}{s + b/a}, \qquad w(t) = \frac{1}{a}\,u(t)\,e^{-bt/a}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(a), p. 1" />

because $\dfrac{1}{s + b/a}$ is the transform of $e^{-bt/a}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The step response (our own solution)">

MIT's printed solution gives only $w$. For the step response, the transform of $a\dot v + bv = u(t)$ is $(as + b)V(s) = \dfrac{1}{s}$. For $b \ne 0$, partial fractions give

<Formula label="Unit step response" tex="V(s) = \frac{1}{s\,(as + b)} = \frac{1}{b}\left(\frac{1}{s} - \frac{1}{s + b/a}\right), \qquad v(t) = \frac{1}{b}\,u(t)\left(1 - e^{-bt/a}\right)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 27(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

For $b = 0$ the transform is $V(s) = \dfrac{1}{as^2}$, and $v(t) = u(t)\,\dfrac{t}{a}$: with nothing to hold it back, the response to a constant input grows steadily.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

As in Problem 24(c) of Problem Set 6, the derivative of the step response must be the impulse response: $\dot v = \dfrac{1}{b}\cdot\dfrac{b}{a}\,e^{-bt/a} = w$ for $t > 0$, and $v$ has no jump at $0$. The limit $b \to 0$ also fits: $1 - e^{-bt/a} \approx \dfrac{bt}{a}$ for small $b$, so $v \to \dfrac{t}{a}$. A number, for $a = 2$ and $b = 1$: $v(2) = 1 - e^{-1} = 0.6321$, and the slope of $v$ there is $w(2) = \frac{1}{2}e^{-1} = 0.1839$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b (i)" points={6}>

Now the input is a ramp: solve $a\dot x + bx = t$ for $t > 0$ with rest initial conditions, so $x(0) = 0$. (MIT's solution calls $x$ the unit ramp response.) Parts (b)(i) to (b)(iii) do this in three ways.

The first way: find a particular solution $x_p$ by undetermined coefficients, and add the solution of the homogeneous equation that makes $x(0) = 0$ (MIT calls it the transient; it dies away when $b/a > 0$). Treat $b \ne 0$ and $b = 0$ apart.

<Hint>

The input is a polynomial of degree 1, so try $x_p = c_1t + c_0$. Put it into the equation and compare the terms with $t$ and the constant terms.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The homogeneous equation $a\dot x + bx = 0$ has the solutions $c\,e^{-bt/a}$. Choose $c$ so that $x_p(0) + c = 0$. For $b = 0$ the trial solution fails: integrate $a\dot x = t$ directly.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p27-b-i" label="For $t > 0$ and $b \ne 0$: $x(t) =$" expr="t/b - a*(1 - exp(-b*t/a))/b^2"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2], t: [0.3, 3] }}
  names={{ a: "a", b: "b", t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "t/b - a/b^2", hint: "That is the particular solution alone. At t = 0 it is not 0: add the solution of the homogeneous equation that brings it to rest." },
    { expr: "t/b - (1 - exp(-b*t/a))/b^2", hint: "Your constant term has no a in it. Put c₁t + c₀ into the equation again and collect the constant terms: the derivative of c₁t contributes there." },
    { expr: "t/b + a*(1 - exp(-b*t/a))/b^2", hint: "Your slope at t = 0 is 2/b. At t = 0 both x and the input are 0, so the equation says the slope is 0." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p27-b-i-zero" label="For $t > 0$ and $b = 0$: $x(t) =$" expr="t^2/(2*a)"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 2], t: [0.3, 3] }}
  names={{ a: "a", t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "t^2/2", hint: "With b = 0 the equation is aẋ = t: divide by a before you integrate." },
    { expr: "t^2/a", hint: "Integrating t gives a factor ½, which is missing." },
    { expr: "t/a", hint: "That is ẋ, the velocity. Integrate once more." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A linear input calls for a linear particular solution. The homogeneous solution $c\,e^{-bt/a}$ then repairs the starting value.

<Step title="The particular solution">

Put $x_p = c_1t + c_0$ into the equation: $ac_1 + b(c_1t + c_0) = t$. The terms with $t$ give $bc_1 = 1$, so $c_1 = \dfrac{1}{b}$ (this needs $b \ne 0$). The constant terms give $ac_1 + bc_0 = 0$, so $c_0 = -\dfrac{a}{b^2}$:

<Formula label="Particular solution" tex="x_p = \frac{1}{b}\,t - \frac{a}{b^2}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(b)(i), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Rest initial conditions">

The general solution is $x = x_p + c\,e^{-bt/a}$. From $x(0) = -\dfrac{a}{b^2} + c = 0$ we get $c = \dfrac{a}{b^2}$:

<Formula label="Unit ramp response" tex="x(t) = u(t)\left(\frac{1}{b}\,t - \frac{a}{b^2}\left(1 - e^{-bt/a}\right)\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(b)(i), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="The case b = 0">

Then the equation is $a\dot x = t$, with the general solution $x = \dfrac{t^2}{2a} + c$, and $x(0) = 0$ gives $c = 0$:

<Formula label="Ramp response for b = 0" tex="x(t) = u(t)\,\frac{t^2}{2a}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(b)(i), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The slope at $t = 0$ is $\dfrac{1}{b} - \dfrac{a}{b^2}\cdot\dfrac{b}{a} = 0$, as the equation demands there. And the two cases fit together: for small $b$, $1 - e^{-bt/a} \approx \dfrac{bt}{a} - \dfrac{b^2t^2}{2a^2}$, which turns the formula into $\dfrac{t}{b} - \dfrac{t}{b} + \dfrac{t^2}{2a} = \dfrac{t^2}{2a}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b (ii)" points={6}>

The second way: compute the convolution $w(t) * t$, with the unit impulse response $w$ of part (a). Treat $b \ne 0$ and $b = 0$ apart.

<Hint>

Write the integral out: $\int_0^t \dfrac{1}{a}\,e^{-b(t - \tau)/a}\,\tau\,d\tau$. The factor $e^{-bt/a}$ does not depend on $\tau$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate $\tau\,e^{b\tau/a}$ by parts: differentiate $\tau$ and integrate the exponential.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p27-b-ii-num" label="Our own extra: for $a = 2$ and $b = 1$, the value of $w(t) * t$ at $t = 2$ (to three significant figures) is" value="0.736" tolerance="0.004" calc="p27-b-ii-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The response to any input with rest initial conditions is the convolution of the unit impulse response with that input.

<Step title="Set up">

<Formula label="The convolution integral" tex="w(t) * t = \int_0^{t}\frac{1}{a}\,e^{-b(t - \tau)/a}\,\tau\,d\tau = \frac{1}{a}\,e^{-bt/a}\int_0^{t}\tau\,e^{b\tau/a}\,d\tau" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(b)(ii), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Integrate by parts">

For $b \ne 0$, an antiderivative of $e^{b\tau/a}$ is $\dfrac{a}{b}\,e^{b\tau/a}$, so

<Formula label="The integral" tex="\int_0^{t}\tau\,e^{b\tau/a}\,d\tau = \left[\tau\,\frac{a}{b}\,e^{b\tau/a}\right]_0^t - \int_0^{t}\frac{a}{b}\,e^{b\tau/a}\,d\tau = \frac{a}{b}\,t\,e^{bt/a} - \frac{a^2}{b^2}\left(e^{bt/a} - 1\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(b)(ii), p. 2" />

Multiply by $\dfrac{1}{a}\,e^{-bt/a}$:

<Formula label="The convolution" tex="w(t) * t = \frac{1}{b}\,t - \frac{a}{b^2}\left(1 - e^{-bt/a}\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(b)(ii), p. 2" />

the same as in part (b)(i).

</Step>

<Step title="The case b = 0">

Then $w = \dfrac{1}{a}$ for $t > 0$ and

<Formula label="Convolution for b = 0" tex="w(t) * t = \int_0^{t}\frac{1}{a}\,\tau\,d\tau = \frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{t^2}{2}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(b)(ii), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A number of our own, for $a = 2$, $b = 1$ and $t = 2$: the formula gives $2 - 2\left(1 - e^{-1}\right) = 2e^{-1} = 0.736$, and computing the integral $\int_0^2\frac{1}{2}\,e^{-(2 - \tau)/2}\,\tau\,d\tau$ numerically gives the same value.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b (iii)" points={6}>

The third way: apply the Laplace transform to $a\dot x + bx = t$, solve for $X(s)$, and transform back. For $b \ne 0$ write

<Formula label="Partial fractions" tex="X(s) = \frac{A}{s} + \frac{B}{s^2} + \frac{C}{as + b}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(b)(iii), p. 2" />

and find $A$, $B$ and $C$. Treat $b = 0$ apart.

<Hint>

The transform of $t$ is $\dfrac{1}{s^2}$, so $(as + b)X = \dfrac{1}{s^2}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The cover-up method gives $B$ (multiply by $s^2$, then set $s = 0$) and $C$ (multiply by $as + b$, then set $s = -b/a$). It does not give $A$. For $A$, multiply the whole decomposition by $s$ and look at very large $s$, or put in one more value of $s$.

</Hint>

  ]} />

<Answer id="p27-b-iii-b" label="$B =$" expr="1/b"
  vars={{ b: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ b: "b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "b", hint: "Cover s² and set s = 0 in what is left, 1/(as + b): that is one over something." },
    { expr: "1/b^2", hint: "Cover s² and set s = 0 in what is left, 1/(as + b): b appears to the first power." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p27-b-iii-c" label="$C =$" expr="a^2/b^2"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ a: "a", b: "b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "a/b^2", hint: "That is the coefficient when the last term is written with s + b/a below. With as + b below, it is a times as large." },

    { expr: "-a^2/b^2", hint: "What is left after covering as + b is 1/s², and a square is positive at s = −b/a too." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Transform, solve the algebraic equation, split into partial fractions, and read each piece back as a function of $t$.

<Step title="Solve in the frequency domain">

With rest initial conditions the transform of the equation is $asX + bX = \dfrac{1}{s^2}$, so

<Formula label="The transform of the response" tex="X(s) = \frac{1}{s^2\,(as + b)}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(b)(iii), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="The three coefficients">

Multiply by $s^2$ and set $s = 0$: $B = \dfrac{1}{b}$. Multiply by $as + b$ and set $s = -\dfrac{b}{a}$: $C = \dfrac{1}{(b/a)^2} = \dfrac{a^2}{b^2}$. For $A$, MIT's solution multiplies the decomposition by $s$ and takes $s$ very large: the left side tends to $0$ and the right side to $A + \dfrac{C}{a}$, so $A = -\dfrac{C}{a} = -\dfrac{a}{b^2}$.

<Formula label="The decomposition" tex="X(s) = -\frac{a/b^2}{s} + \frac{1/b}{s^2} + \frac{a/b^2}{s + b/a}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(b)(iii), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Back to the time domain">

The three pieces are the transforms of a constant, of $t$ and of an exponential:

<Formula label="The response" tex="x(t) = -\frac{a}{b^2} + \frac{1}{b}\,t + \frac{a}{b^2}\,e^{-bt/a} \qquad (t > 0)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(b)(iii), p. 2" />

the same function as in parts (b)(i) and (b)(ii).

</Step>

<Step title="The case b = 0">

Then $asX = \dfrac{1}{s^2}$, so $X = \dfrac{1}{a}\cdot\dfrac{1}{s^3}$. Since $\dfrac{2}{s^3}$ is the transform of $t^2$:

<Formula label="The response for b = 0" tex="x(t) = u(t)\,\frac{1}{a}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\,t^2" cite="solutions" at="Problem 27(b)(iii), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Test the decomposition with $a = 1$, $b = 1$ and $s = 1$: the left side is $\dfrac{1}{1\cdot 2} = 0.5$, and the right side is $-1 + 1 + \dfrac{1}{2} = 0.5$. A link with Problem 26(b), of our own: $X(s) = W(s)\cdot\dfrac{1}{s^2}$ is the product of the transforms of $w$ and of $t$, so by the convolution formula $x = w * t$, which is why the second way and the third way had to agree.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 28

<Exercise id="p28" title="Impulse responses by partial fractions" points={20} mit="Part II, Problem 28">

Find the unit impulse response of each operator by means of the Laplace transform. For an operator $p(D)$ the transform of the equation $p(D)\,w = \delta(t)$ with rest initial conditions is $p(s)\,W(s) = 1$, so $W(s) = \dfrac{1}{p(s)}$: the transfer function of [the lesson](/courses/18-03/impulse-inputs-delta-function).

<Part label="a" points={6}>

The operator $3D^2 + 6D + 6I$.

<Hint>

Take the factor $3$ out of $3s^2 + 6s + 6$ and complete the square in what is left.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You should find a function of $s + 1$. Which transform do you know with that function of $s$ alone, and what does the exponential shift formula of [the lesson](/courses/18-03/laplace-transform-introduction) do with it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p28-a" label="For $t > 0$: $w(t) =$" expr="exp(-t)*sin(t)/3"
  vars={{ t: [0.2, 3] }}
  names={{ t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-t)*sin(t)", hint: "Your slope just after t = 0 is 1. Take the factor 3 out of 3s² + 6s + 6 before you complete the square, and keep it." },
    { expr: "exp(-t)*cos(t)/3", hint: "Your response starts at the value 1/3. A kick on a second-order system changes the velocity at once, but not the position." },
    { expr: "exp(t)*sin(t)/3", hint: "Your response grows without bound. A function of s + 1 is a transform shifted to the left, and the exponential shift formula then gives a decaying factor." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Complete the square, recognise the transform of a sine shifted in $s$, and undo the shift with an exponential factor.

<Step title="Complete the square">

<Formula label="The transfer function" tex="W(s) = \frac{1}{3s^2 + 6s + 6} = \frac{1}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{(s + 1)^2 + 1}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 28(a), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Shift back">

Since $\mathcal{L}(\sin t) = \dfrac{1}{s^2 + 1}$, the exponential shift formula $\mathcal{L}(e^{-t}f(t)) = F(s + 1)$ gives

<Formula label="Unit impulse response" tex="w(t) = \frac{1}{3}\,u(t)\,e^{-t}\sin t" cite="solutions" at="Problem 28(a), p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$w(0) = 0$, and the slope just after the kick is $\frac{1}{3}$, which is $1$ divided by the coefficient of $D^2$, as it must be for a second-order operator. Compare Problem 24(a) of [Problem Set 6](/courses/18-03/18-03-problem-set-6): the operator $2D^2 + 4D + 4I$ has the same roots $-1 \pm i$ and the impulse response $\frac{1}{2}e^{-t}\sin t$, found there without the Laplace transform.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={14}>

The operator $D^4 - I$.

(MIT's sheet prints the operator $D^4 + I$ here. MIT's solutions say that $D^4 - I$ was meant, apologise for the mistake, and take $D^4 + I$ only as far as its four roots. So this part asks for $D^4 - I$, with MIT's 14 points and MIT's solution. The operator as printed is the optional problem after this one, with a solution of our own.)

<Hint>

Find the four roots of $s^4 - 1$ and write $\dfrac{1}{s^4 - 1}$ as a sum of four terms, one constant over $s$ minus each root.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The cover-up method gives each constant: cover one factor of $(s - 1)(s + 1)(s - i)(s + i)$ and put its root into the other three. Then combine the two real exponentials into a hyperbolic function and the two complex ones into a sine or a cosine.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p28-b" label="For $t > 0$: $w(t) =$" expr="(sinh(t) - sin(t))/2"
  vars={{ t: [0.3, 3] }}
  names={{ t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(cosh(t) - sin(t))/2", hint: "At t = 0 your answer is 1/2, but the impulse response of a fourth-order operator starts at 0. Check the sign of the constant for the root s = −1." },
    { expr: "(sinh(t) + sin(t))/2", hint: "Your slope at t = 0 is 1. A kick on a fourth-order system leaves the position, the velocity and the acceleration at 0. Check the product i·i when you combine the two complex terms." },
    { expr: "(sinh(t) - sin(t))/4", hint: "Your third derivative at t = 0 is 1/2. For this operator the kick makes the third derivative jump to 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The roots of $s^4 - 1$ are simple, so $\dfrac{1}{s^4 - 1}$ splits into four terms of the form constant over $s$ minus a root, and each is the transform of an exponential.

<Step title="Roots and partial fractions">

$s^4 = 1$ has the roots $\pm 1$ and $\pm i$:

<Formula label="Four simple poles" tex="W(s) = \frac{1}{s^4 - 1} = \frac{c_1}{s - 1} + \frac{c_2}{s + 1} + \frac{c_3}{s - i} + \frac{c_4}{s + i}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 28(b), p. 2" />

(MIT names the constants $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$; this page has used $a$ and $b$ already.)

</Step>

<Step title="Cover-up">

Covering $s - 1$ and setting $s = 1$ in $\dfrac{1}{(s + 1)(s^2 + 1)}$ gives $c_1 = \dfrac{1}{4}$. Covering $s + 1$ and setting $s = -1$ in $\dfrac{1}{(s - 1)(s^2 + 1)}$ gives $c_2 = -\dfrac{1}{4}$. Covering $s - i$ and setting $s = i$ in $\dfrac{1}{(s^2 - 1)(s + i)}$ gives $c_3 = \dfrac{1}{(-2)(2i)} = \dfrac{i}{4}$, and in the same way $c_4 = -\dfrac{i}{4}$.

(MIT's printed solution gives the constant for $s + 1$ as $+\frac{1}{4}$ and has $e^t + e^{-t}$ in the line that follows. The cover-up gives $-\frac{1}{4}$, and with it that line leads to the final answer that MIT prints, which is correct.)

</Step>

<Step title="Back to the time domain">

<Formula label="Unit impulse response" tex="w(t) = \frac{1}{4}\,u(t)\left(e^{t} - e^{-t} + i\,e^{it} - i\,e^{-it}\right) = \frac{1}{2}\,u(t)\left(\sinh t - \sin t\right)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 28(b), p. 2" />

because $e^t - e^{-t} = 2\sinh t$ and $i\left(e^{it} - e^{-it}\right) = i\cdot 2i\sin t = -2\sin t$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second route, of our own, with real numbers only: $\dfrac{1}{s^4 - 1} = \dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{s^2 - 1} - \dfrac{1}{s^2 + 1}\right)$, and these two are the transforms of $\sinh t$ and $\sin t$. The starting values fit a fourth-order operator: $w$, $\dot w$ and $\ddot w$ are $0$ at $t = 0$, and the third derivative $\frac{1}{2}(\cosh t + \cos t)$ is $1$ there. Differentiating four times gives back $\frac{1}{2}(\sinh t - \sin t) = w$, so $(D^4 - I)w = 0$ for $t > 0$. A number: $w(2) = 1.359$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 28 as printed (optional)

<Exercise id="p28x" title="The operator D⁴ + I" points={6} mit="Part II, Problem 28(b), as printed on the sheet" optional>

Find the unit impulse response of $D^4 + I$ by means of the Laplace transform. This is the operator that MIT's sheet prints in Problem 28(b). MIT's solutions give its four roots, call the rest "not pretty" and do not expect more. The answer below is our own, and so are its 6 points. It takes more algebra than the other problems, but the result is a neat real formula.

<Hint>

The roots of $s^4 + 1$ are the four fourth roots of $-1$: the numbers $\dfrac{\pm 1 \pm i}{\sqrt 2}$. With $r = \dfrac{1 + i}{\sqrt 2}$ they are $r$, $\bar r$, $-r$ and $-\bar r$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For a simple root $\rho$ of a polynomial $p(s)$, the cover-up constant of $\dfrac{1}{p(s)}$ at $\rho$ is $\dfrac{1}{p'(\rho)}$. Here $p'(\rho) = 4\rho^3$, and $\rho^4 = -1$ lets you write $\dfrac{1}{4\rho^3}$ as a multiple of $\rho$ itself.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The four terms come in complex-conjugate pairs, so their sum is twice a real part. Write $rt = \dfrac{t}{\sqrt 2} + i\,\dfrac{t}{\sqrt 2}$ and use $\sinh(\alpha + i\beta) = \sinh\alpha\cos\beta + i\cosh\alpha\sin\beta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p28x-w" label="For $t > 0$: $w(t) =$" expr="(cosh(t/sqrt(2))*sin(t/sqrt(2)) - sinh(t/sqrt(2))*cos(t/sqrt(2)))/sqrt(2)"
  vars={{ t: [0.5, 4] }}
  names={{ t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(cosh(t)*sin(t) - sinh(t)*cos(t))/4", hint: "That is the impulse response of D⁴ + 4I, whose roots are ±1 ± i. The roots of s⁴ + 1 have absolute value 1." },
    { expr: "cosh(t/sqrt(2))*sin(t/sqrt(2)) - sinh(t/sqrt(2))*cos(t/sqrt(2))", hint: "Your third derivative at t = 0 is √2. For this operator the kick makes the third derivative jump to 1." },
    { expr: "(cosh(t/sqrt(2))*sin(t/sqrt(2)) + sinh(t/sqrt(2))*cos(t/sqrt(2)))/sqrt(2)", hint: "Your slope at t = 0 is 1. A kick on a fourth-order system leaves the position, the velocity and the acceleration at 0." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As for $D^4 - I$: four simple roots, four exponentials. The roots are complex, so the work is in bringing the sum to a real form.

<Step title="The roots">

<Formula label="Fourth roots of −1" tex="s^4 + 1 = (s - r)(s - \bar r)(s + r)(s + \bar r), \qquad r = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\,(1 + i)" cite="solutions" at="Problem 28(b), p. 2" />

Indeed $r^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1 + i)^2 = i$, so $r^4 = -1$. MIT's solution stops here. Everything that follows is our own.

</Step>

<Step title="The constants">

For a simple root $\rho$ of $p(s) = s^4 + 1$, covering the factor $s - \rho$ leaves the product of the other three factors at $\rho$, which is $p'(\rho) = 4\rho^3$. Since $\rho^4 = -1$, we have $\dfrac{1}{4\rho^3} = \dfrac{\rho}{4\rho^4} = -\dfrac{\rho}{4}$:

<Formula label="Partial fractions" tex="\frac{1}{s^4 + 1} = -\frac{1}{4}\left(\frac{r}{s - r} + \frac{\bar r}{s - \bar r} - \frac{r}{s + r} - \frac{\bar r}{s + \bar r}\right)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 28(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Back to the time domain">

Each term is the transform of an exponential. The terms with $\bar r$ are the complex conjugates of the terms with $r$, so the sum is twice a real part:

<Formula label="A real part" tex="w(t) = -\frac{1}{4}\left(r\,e^{rt} + \bar r\,e^{\bar rt} - r\,e^{-rt} - \bar r\,e^{-\bar rt}\right) = -\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}\left(r\,e^{rt} - r\,e^{-rt}\right) = -\operatorname{Re}\bigl(r\sinh(rt)\bigr)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 28(b) (our own solution)" />

Now $rt = \dfrac{t}{\sqrt 2} + i\,\dfrac{t}{\sqrt 2}$, and $\sinh(\alpha + i\beta) = \sinh\alpha\cos\beta + i\cosh\alpha\sin\beta$. Multiplying by $r = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}(1 + i)$ and taking the real part gives $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}\left(\sinh\alpha\cos\beta - \cosh\alpha\sin\beta\right)$ with $\alpha = \beta = \dfrac{t}{\sqrt 2}$. With the minus sign in front:

<Formula label="Unit impulse response of D⁴ + I" tex="w(t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\,u(t)\left(\cosh\frac{t}{\sqrt 2}\,\sin\frac{t}{\sqrt 2} - \sinh\frac{t}{\sqrt 2}\,\cos\frac{t}{\sqrt 2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 28(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate, for $t > 0$. Each derivative brings a factor $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}$, and terms cancel in pairs:

<Formula label="Four derivatives" tex="\dot w = \sinh\frac{t}{\sqrt 2}\,\sin\frac{t}{\sqrt 2}, \qquad \ddot w = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\left(\cosh\frac{t}{\sqrt 2}\,\sin\frac{t}{\sqrt 2} + \sinh\frac{t}{\sqrt 2}\,\cos\frac{t}{\sqrt 2}\right), \qquad \frac{d^3w}{dt^3} = \cosh\frac{t}{\sqrt 2}\,\cos\frac{t}{\sqrt 2}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 28(b) (our own solution)" />

At $t = 0$ the values of $w$, $\dot w$ and $\ddot w$ are $0$ and the third derivative is $1$, as a unit kick on a fourth-order operator requires. One more derivative gives $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}\left(\sinh\dfrac{t}{\sqrt 2}\cos\dfrac{t}{\sqrt 2} - \cosh\dfrac{t}{\sqrt 2}\sin\dfrac{t}{\sqrt 2}\right) = -w$, so $(D^4 + I)w = 0$ for $t > 0$.

A number: $w(2) = 1.308$. Compare $D^4 - I$, where $w(2) = 1.359$. Both lie close to $\dfrac{t^3}{6} = 1.333$, the response of $D^4$ alone, one below it and one above it by nearly the same amount, about $0.025$.

<Plot variable="t" domain={[0, 3.5]} range={[0, 9]} xLabel="t" yLabel="w"
  curves={[

  ]}
  caption="Our own graph. The unit impulse responses of D⁴ + I (solid) and of D⁴ − I (dashed), computed from the two formulas. Both start flat, like t³/6; later the solid curve falls behind the dashed one." />

</Step>

</Solution>
