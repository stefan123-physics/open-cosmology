So far there are two ways to solve the homogeneous system $\vec x\,' = A\vec x$: eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and the "royal road" $\vec x = e^{At}\vec x_0$, which in practice needs a fundamental matrix anyway (transcript, p. 1). This lecture adds a third, **decoupling**, which saves no work and is a change of language; it is taught because engineering departments asked for it, since many engineers and scientists talk about systems only in this way (p. 1). Everything works for $n \times n$ systems, though $2 \times 2$ is enough for calculations (p. 1). It continues the [matrix exponential](/courses/18-03/matrix-exponentials).

## What decoupling means

Introduce new variables by a linear change of variables (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Linear change of variables" tex="u = ax + by, \qquad v = cx + dy" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

chosen so that the system becomes **decoupled** (p. 1):

<Formula label="Decoupled system" tex="u' = k_1u, \qquad v' = k_2v" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

That is not really a system at all: two first-order equations side by side, solved at once by $u = c_1e^{k_1t}$, $v = c_2e^{k_2t}$ (p. 1). In a coupled system both variables appear on both right-hand sides. New variables that decouple a system usually have a physical meaning, which gives insight (p. 1).

## The ice-cube tray

An old metal ice-cube tray has two compartments joined by a hole at the bottom, the second with twice the cross-section of the first (transcript, p. 1). Let $x$ and $y$ be the water heights. The flow through the hole is proportional to the height difference, which sets the pressure there (pp. 1–2). The rate of flow into the second compartment is the rate of change of $2y$, because of its larger area (p. 2). With the constant chosen as 2 (p. 2):

<Formula label="Ice-cube tray" tex="x' = -2x + 2y, \qquad y' = x - y" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

**Decoupling by insight** (p. 2). Two quantities matter more than the heights: the **height difference** $v = x - y$, which drives the flow, and the **total water** $u = x + 2y$, which cannot change, since none enters or leaves. Substituting (p. 2):

<Formula label="The decoupled tray" tex="u' = x' + 2y' = 0, \qquad v' = x' - y' = -3(x - y) = -3v" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

So $u = c_1$ and $v = c_2e^{-3t}$ (p. 2). To return to the heights, invert the change of variables (p. 2):

<Formula label="Back to the heights" tex="x = \frac{1}{3}(u + 2v) = \frac{1}{3}(c_1 + 2c_2e^{-3t}), \qquad y = \frac{1}{3}(u - v) = \frac{1}{3}(c_1 - c_2e^{-3t})" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

which is $\frac{1}{3}c_1(1, 1) + \frac{1}{3}c_2(2, -1)e^{-3t}$: the eigenvectors appear (p. 2).

## The general method

A system can be decoupled only if the eigenvalues are **real and complete**: no complex eigenvalues and no defective ones; this holds in particular when $A$ is real and symmetric, as in many equilibrium problems (transcript, p. 3). Write the change of variables as $\vec u = D\vec x$, with the **decoupling matrix** $D$ (p. 3). But for substituting, what is needed is the old variables in terms of the new ones, $\vec x = E\vec u$ with $E = D^{-1}$, and the right choice is (p. 3)

<Formula label="Matrix of eigenvectors" tex="E = \begin{pmatrix} \vec\alpha_1 & \vec\alpha_2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Choosing new variables amounts to choosing new basis vectors in place of $\vec i$ and $\vec j$, and the only vectors the matrix singles out are its eigenvectors. Indeed $u = 1$, $v = 0$ gives $\vec x = \vec\alpha_1$, and $u = 0$, $v = 1$ gives $\vec\alpha_2$ (p. 3).

**Another definition of eigenvectors** (pp. 3–4). Most of the world writes the eigenvector equation as

<Formula label="Eigenvector equation" tex="A\vec\alpha_1 = \lambda_1\vec\alpha_1" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The geometric view: $A$ is a linear transformation of the plane, and an eigenvector is a vector it keeps on the same line, stretching or shrinking it by the factor $\lambda$, and reversing it if $\lambda$ is negative (pp. 3–4). Applied to both columns of $E$ (p. 4):

<Formula label="AE = EΛ" tex="AE = \begin{pmatrix} A\vec\alpha_1 & A\vec\alpha_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1\vec\alpha_1 & \lambda_2\vec\alpha_2 \end{pmatrix} = E\begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

**The substitution** (p. 4). With $\Lambda$ the diagonal matrix of the eigenvalues, put $\vec x = E\vec u$ into $\vec x\,' = A\vec x$: $E\vec u\,' = AE\vec u = E\Lambda\vec u$, and multiplying by $E^{-1}$,

<Formula label="Decoupled form" tex="\vec u\,' = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{pmatrix}\vec u: \qquad u' = \lambda_1u, \quad v' = \lambda_2v" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

decoupled, with the eigenvalues as constants: $u = c_1e^{\lambda_1t}$, $v = c_2e^{\lambda_2t}$ (p. 4).

**The tray again** (p. 4). $A = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ has trace $-3$ and determinant 0, so $\lambda^2 + 3\lambda = 0$: $\lambda = 0$ with $\vec\alpha_1 = (1, 1)$, and $\lambda = -3$ with $\vec\alpha_2 = (-2, 1)$, the negative of the $(2, -1)$ found by insight; the sign of an eigenvector does not matter. Then $E = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, determinant 3, and (p. 4)

<Formula label="Decoupling matrix of the tray" tex="D = E^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad u = \frac{x + 2y}{3}, \quad v = \frac{-x + y}{3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

the same variables as found by insight, up to the factors $\frac{1}{3}$ and $-\frac{1}{3}$, which do not change the equations $u' = 0$, $v' = -3v$ (p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="Decoupling a symmetric system">
<Problem>
Decouple $x' = 3x + y$, $y' = x + 3y$, and solve it with $x(0) = 3$, $y(0) = 1$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Eigenvalues and eigenvectors">
The matrix is symmetric, so decoupling is possible. Trace 6, determinant 8: $\lambda^2 - 6\lambda + 8 = (\lambda - 4)(\lambda - 2)$. For 4: $(1, 1)$; for 2: $(1, -1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="New variables">
$E = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, determinant $-2$, so $E^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$: $u = \frac{x + y}{2}$, $v = \frac{x - y}{2}$, with $u' = 4u$ and $v' = 2v$.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve and return">
$u(0) = 2$ and $v(0) = 1$, so $u = 2e^{4t}$, $v = e^{2t}$. Then $x = u + v = 2e^{4t} + e^{2t}$ and $y = u - v = 2e^{4t} - e^{2t}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Decoupling finds variables in which the system becomes $u' = k_1u$, $v' = k_2v$; they often have physical meaning (total water, height difference).
- It is possible when the eigenvalues are real and complete, for example for real symmetric $A$.
- Take $\vec x = E\vec u$ with the eigenvectors as columns; $AE = E\Lambda$ gives $\vec u\,' = \Lambda\vec u$.
- The new variables are $\vec u = E^{-1}\vec x$; the constants of the decoupled equations are the eigenvalues.
