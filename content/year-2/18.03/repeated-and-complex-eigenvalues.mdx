The [eigenvalue method](/courses/18-03/eigenvalues-linear-systems) runs smoothly when the eigenvalues are real and distinct (transcript, p. 1). This lecture handles the two cases that do not: a repeated eigenvalue, which takes most of the time, and complex eigenvalues (p. 1).

## Three fish tanks

A circular fish tank is divided into three identical compartments, for tropical, arctic and ordinary fish (transcript, p. 1). The heater is off, so starting from their different temperatures the compartments trade heat through identical glass partitions; the outer wall is insulated and the fish keep the water stirred (p. 1). Let $x_i$ be the temperature in compartment $i$. Each compartment exchanges heat with **both** others, at a rate set by the temperature differences, with conductivity $a$ (p. 1). Taking $a = 1$ and using the symmetry (p. 1):

<Formula label="Fish-tank system" tex="x_1' = -2x_1 + x_2 + x_3, \qquad x_2' = x_1 - 2x_2 + x_3, \qquad x_3' = x_1 + x_2 - 2x_3" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

For a $3 \times 3$ matrix the trace-and-determinant formula does not apply; use $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$ (p. 1). The main diagonal gives $-(\lambda + 2)^3$, the two other products give $1 + 1$, and the three anti-diagonal products each give $-(-2 - \lambda)$ (pp. 1–2). Changing all the signs and expanding (p. 2):

<Formula label="Characteristic equation" tex="\lambda^3 + 6\lambda^2 + 9\lambda = \lambda(\lambda + 3)^2 = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The constant term $8 - 2 - 6$ vanishes, which makes the problem easy (p. 2). The eigenvalues are $0$ and $-3$, a **double** root: only two values for three solutions (p. 2).

## The eigenvalue 0

For $\lambda = 0$ the system is $A\vec\alpha = 0$ (transcript, p. 2). An inverse matrix cannot solve it: the whole point is that the determinant is zero (p. 2). Use elimination, or physical insight: a solution $\vec\alpha\,e^{0t}$ is **constant**, and the obvious constant solution is all compartments at the same temperature (pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Constant solution" tex="\vec x = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

(Ask where an equation comes from: each field has its own tricks, p. 2.)

## The double eigenvalue −3

Subtracting $-3$ from the diagonal turns every diagonal entry into 1, and all three equations become the same (transcript, p. 3):

<Formula label="Equation for the eigenvectors" tex="a_1 + a_2 + a_3 = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

This has **two** independent solutions, for example $(1, 0, -1)$ and $(1, -1, 0)$: neither is a multiple of the other (p. 3). A third, such as $(0, 1, -1)$, is not new: it is the first minus the second (p. 3). So (p. 3)

<Formula label="General solution" tex="\vec x = c_1\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}e^{-3t} + c_2\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}e^{-3t} + c_3\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

As $t \to \infty$ the first two terms die, and every solution tends to equal temperatures (p. 3).

## Complete and defective eigenvalues

A repeated eigenvalue with enough independent eigenvectors to give the needed number of solutions, two for a double root, three for a triple, is **complete** (transcript, p. 3). One with fewer, for instance a double eigenvalue whose eigenvectors are all multiples of one, is **incomplete**, or in today's accepted term **defective**; it needs another method, given in the problem set (p. 3).

**The spectral theorem** (pp. 3–4), also called the principal axis theorem, far from obvious: if $A$ is a real $n \times n$ **symmetric** matrix, equal to its transpose (unchanged by flipping about the main diagonal), then all its eigenvalues are complete. The fish-tank matrix is symmetric; so are the matrices of whole areas such as equilibrium theory, where repeated eigenvalues therefore cause no trouble (p. 4).

## Complex eigenvalues

Follow the same programme (transcript, p. 4): find a complex eigenvector, form $\vec\alpha\,e^{(a + bi)t}$, and take its real and imaginary parts, two real solutions, exactly as for second-order equations.

**Example** (pp. 4–5), which the lecture dresses up as a model of a couple in a TV sitcom: her feelings $x$ grow when his do, while his $y$, neurotically, fall the more he loves her, both measured from equilibrium:

<Formula label="The system" tex="x' = x + 2y, \qquad y' = -x - y" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

The trace is 0 and the determinant $-1 + 2 = 1$, so $\lambda^2 + 1 = 0$ and $\lambda = \pm i$; one of them is enough (p. 4). For $\lambda = i$: $(1 - i)a_1 + 2a_2 = 0$, so with $a_1 = 1$, $a_2 = -\frac{1 - i}{2}$ (p. 4). Split into real and imaginary vectors, and multiply by $\cos t + i\sin t$ (pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="Complex solution" tex="\left[\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1/2 \end{pmatrix} + i\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1/2 \end{pmatrix}\right](\cos t + i\sin t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

Its real part is a real solution (p. 5):

<Formula label="Real solution" tex="x = \cos t, \qquad y = -\frac{1}{2}\cos t - \frac{1}{2}\sin t" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

It repeats with period $2\pi$: the solution curves are ellipses, travelled round and round, enough for a season of episodes (p. 5). (The imaginary part gives a second solution, $x = \sin t$, $y = \frac{1}{2}(\cos t - \sin t)$; at $(1, -\frac{1}{2})$ the velocity points down, so the ellipses are travelled clockwise.) (The transcript's final line for $y$ has an extra "plus sine $t$"; the form above satisfies the equations. The second eigenvector equation is also misread there; it is $-a_1 + (-1 - i)a_2 = 0$.)

<WorkedExample title="Complete, defective and complex">
<Problem>
(a) Show that the fish tanks conserve total heat. (b) Is the eigenvalue of $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ complete? (c) Find two real solutions of $x' = -x - 2y$, $y' = 2x - y$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Conservation">
Adding the three equations, every $x_i$ appears with coefficient $-2 + 1 + 1 = 0$, so $(x_1 + x_2 + x_3)' = 0$: the total is constant, and the common final temperature is the average of the starting ones.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A defective eigenvalue">
$(\lambda - 2)^2 = 0$, a double eigenvalue 2. Then $(A - 2I)\vec\alpha = 0$ says only $a_2 = 0$: every eigenvector is a multiple of $(1, 0)$. One eigenvector for a double root: defective. (The matrix is not symmetric.)
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Complex eigenvalues">
Trace $-2$, determinant $1 + 4 = 5$: $\lambda^2 + 2\lambda + 5 = 0$, $\lambda = -1 \pm 2i$. For $-1 + 2i$: $-2i\,a_1 - 2a_2 = 0$, so $\vec\alpha = (1, -i) = (1, 0) + i(0, -1)$. The real and imaginary parts of $\vec\alpha\,e^{-t}(\cos 2t + i\sin 2t)$ are $e^{-t}(\cos 2t, \sin 2t)$ and $e^{-t}(\sin 2t, -\cos 2t)$: spirals into the origin.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A repeated eigenvalue is complete if it has enough independent eigenvectors (the fish tanks: $-3$ with two), defective if not.
- For an eigenvalue 0, the solutions are constant; inverse matrices never help in finding eigenvectors.
- Real symmetric matrices have only complete eigenvalues.
- Complex eigenvalues: take real and imaginary parts of $\vec\alpha\,e^{(a + bi)t}$; purely imaginary ones give closed orbits (for $2 \times 2$ systems).
