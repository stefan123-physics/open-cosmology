Differential equations of the simplest kind, first order in standard form, look like $y' = f(x, y)$ (transcript, p. 1). Some are easy: $y' = x/y$ can be solved by [separating variables](/courses/18-01/separation-of-variables). Others look just as simple but have no solution that can be written with elementary functions: $y' = y - x^2$ can be solved (next lectures), while its look-alike $y' = x - y^2$ cannot, and such equations are the rule rather than the exception (pp. 1–2). So 18.03 begins without solving anything: it looks at differential equations **geometrically**, and numerically (pp. 1–2).

## Direction fields and integral curves

The **analytic** view has the equation $y' = f(x, y)$ and its solutions $y_1(x)$. The **geometric** view has two matching objects (transcript, pp. 2–3):

- the **direction field**: at each point $(x, y)$ of the plane, a short **line element** of slope $f(x, y)$;
- an **integral curve**: a curve that at every point is tangent to the line element there.

The key fact (pp. 3–5):

<Formula label="Solutions are integral curves" tex="y_1(x) \text{ solves } y' = f(x, y) \Longleftrightarrow y_1'(x) = f\big(x, y_1(x)\big) \Longleftrightarrow \text{the graph of } y_1 \text{ is an integral curve}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–5" />

Both sides say the same thing: the slope of the graph, $y_1'(x)$, equals the slope of the field at that point, $f(x, y_1(x))$. Solving the equation analytically is the same as drawing an integral curve geometrically.

## Drawing the field: isoclines

A computer draws a direction field the naive way: pick equally spaced points, compute $f(x, y)$ at each, draw the line element (transcript, p. 5). A human should work the other way round. Pick a slope $C$ first, and find all the points where the line elements have that slope (p. 6):

<Formula label="Isocline" tex="f(x, y) = C" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

This curve, a level curve of $f$, is called an **isocline**. Draw it dashed (it is usually not a solution) and put as many line elements of slope $C$ along it as you like; a few values of $C$ fill in the field (pp. 6–7).

**Example: $y' = -x/y$.** The isoclines $-x/y = C$ are the lines $y = -x/C$ through the origin (p. 7). Their slope, $-1/C$, is the negative reciprocal of the slope $C$ of the line elements on them, so the line elements are perpendicular to the isoclines (p. 8). Writing the isocline as $Cy = -x$, the case $C = 0$ is the $y$-axis; the $x$-axis is counted, loosely, as the isocline "$C = \infty$", with vertical line elements (pp. 8–9). Curves perpendicular to every ray from the origin are circles, $x^2 + y^2 = C_1$, which separation of variables confirms (p. 9). A solution such as $y = \sqrt{C_1 - x^2}$ lives only on $-\sqrt{C_1} < x < \sqrt{C_1}$: there is no way to tell from the equation in advance how far a solution extends (p. 10).

**Example: $y' = 1 + x - y$.** Variables cannot be separated, but the picture comes quickly (transcript, pp. 10–15). The isoclines $1 + x - y = C$ are the parallel lines $y = x + 1 - C$. On the isocline for $C = 1$, the line $y = x$, the line elements have slope 1, the same as the line: it is an isocline and an integral curve at once, so $y = x$ is a solution (pp. 12–15). Between the isoclines for $C = 0$ and $C = 2$ every line element points into the strip, so an integral curve that enters it cannot escape, "like a lobster trap" (p. 13); all solutions approach $y = x$ as $x \to \infty$ (p. 15).

## Integral curves cannot cross or touch

Two principles help in sketching (transcript, pp. 14–16):

1. **Two integral curves cannot cross** at an angle: at the crossing point the field has only one slope (p. 14).
2. **Two integral curves cannot even touch** (be tangent). This is less obvious, and follows from the **existence and uniqueness theorem** (pp. 15–16):

<Formula label="Existence and uniqueness" tex="f \text{ and } \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \text{ continuous near } (x_0, y_0) \Longrightarrow y' = f(x, y) \text{ has one and only one solution through } (x_0, y_0)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 15–16" />

The solution exists at least on some interval around $x_0$. Continuity of $f$ gives **existence**, at least one solution; continuity of $\partial f/\partial y$ gives **uniqueness** (p. 16). So the curves heading towards $y = x$ in the example above can only approach it, never join it (p. 15).

**When the theorem does not apply.** Take $xy' = y - 1$. Separating variables, $\ln|y - 1| = \ln|x| + c$, gives the solutions $y = 1 + Cx$: all the lines through $(0, 1)$ (pp. 16–17). (The transcript writes the equation with $1 - y$ and drops a sign when integrating; with $1 - y$ the solutions would be the hyperbolas $y = 1 - C/x$. The lines it describes belong to $xy' = y - 1$, used here. The board itself was not checked.) Through a point on the $y$-axis other than $(0, 1)$ there is no solution at all; through $(0, 1)$ there are infinitely many. This does not contradict the theorem: in standard form the equation is $y' = (y - 1)/x$, whose right side is not even defined on the $y$-axis (pp. 17–18). In practice, this is how existence and uniqueness usually fail: $f$ is undefined somewhere (p. 18). (Euler's numerical method, planned for this lecture, was left to the notes and comes in Lecture 2, p. 16.)

<WorkedExample title="Sketching y′ = x + y">
<Problem>
For $y' = x + y$: (a) find the isoclines; (b) find one isocline that is also an integral curve; (c) check that $y = Ce^x - x - 1$ solves the equation and describe the integral curves.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Isoclines">
$x + y = C$, the parallel lines $y = C - x$, all of slope $-1$. On them the line elements have slope $C$: horizontal on $y = -x$, slope 1 on $y = 1 - x$, and so on.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A straight solution">
On the isocline for $C = -1$, $y = -x - 1$, the line elements have slope $-1$, the slope of the line itself, so it is an integral curve: indeed $y' = -1$ and $x + y = -1$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) All the solutions">
$y = Ce^x - x - 1$ gives $y' = Ce^x - 1$ and $x + y = Ce^x - 1$: they agree for every $C$. For $C > 0$ the curves turn upward, for $C < 0$ downward, and they move away from the line $y = -x - 1$ as $x$ grows; the line repels instead of attracting.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $y' = f(x, y)$: the direction field has slope $f(x, y)$ at each point; solutions are exactly the integral curves.
- Sketch the field with isoclines $f(x, y) = C$, drawn dashed.
- Integral curves cannot cross, and, by existence and uniqueness (needing $f$ and $\partial f/\partial y$ continuous), cannot touch.
- Where $f$ is undefined, as for $xy' = y - 1$ on the $y$-axis, solutions may not exist or may not be unique.
