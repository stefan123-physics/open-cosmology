The systems now become **nonlinear**: still two first-order autonomous equations, but with quadratic terms, sines and cosines on the right (transcript, p. 1). There are generally no formulas for the solutions, only computer pictures, but a quick qualitative sketch is often what is needed, using the [linear pictures](/courses/18-03/sketching-linear-systems) (p. 1). The running example is the nonlinear pendulum, whose behaviour we know, so the mathematics can be checked against intuition (p. 1).

## The pendulum

A mass $m$ on a rigid rod of length $l$ swings in a circle; $\theta$ is the angle from the vertical (transcript, pp. 1–2). Along the circle, the acceleration is $l\theta''$, gravity contributes $-mg\sin\theta$, and damping resists the velocity $l\theta'$ (pp. 2–3). Dividing by $ml$ and lumping the constants (p. 3):

<Formula label="Damped pendulum" tex="\theta'' + c\,\theta' + k\sin\theta = 0, \qquad k = \frac{g}{l}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

A nonlinear equation with no exact solution (p. 3). A computer would first turn it into a first-order system, since numerical methods (Euler, Runge–Kutta) are written for systems; the natural new variable is the angular velocity $\omega$ (pp. 3–4). With the lightly damped values $c = 1$, $k = 2$ (p. 4):

<Formula label="The pendulum system" tex="\theta' = \omega, \qquad \omega' = -2\sin\theta - \omega" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

## Step 1: critical points

The simplest solutions are single points: **critical points** $(x_0, y_0)$, where $f$ and $g$ both vanish (transcript, p. 5). The velocity vector there is zero, so a solution that starts there stays there for all time (p. 5). Finding them means solving two nonlinear equations, a hard problem in general, even by computer; in practice the physical problem suggests where to look (pp. 5–6).

For the pendulum, $\omega = 0$ and $\sin\theta = 0$, so the critical points are $(n\pi, 0)$ for every integer $n$ (p. 6). Physically there are only two: hanging straight down at rest, stable, and balanced straight up, unstable (pp. 6–7). But all the others are needed for the full picture (p. 7).

## Step 2: linearize

At each critical point, find a linear system that approximates the nonlinear one nearby, and sketch its trajectories (transcript, pp. 7–8). Near $(0, 0)$, the textbook's way works (though the lecture would rather students use the faster Jacobian method below, p. 8): $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, giving $\theta' = \omega$, $\omega' = -2\theta - \omega$ (p. 8). Its matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\lambda^2 + \lambda + 2 = 0$, so $\lambda = \frac{-1 \pm i\sqrt7}{2}$ (p. 9). Complex, with negative real part: a **spiral sink**. At $(1, 0)$ the velocity is the first column, $(0, -2)$, pointing down: the spiral turns clockwise (p. 9).

This is exactly the swinging pendulum: released at a small positive angle with $\omega = 0$, $\theta$ decreases while $\omega$ grows negative, largest at the bottom; then $\theta$ goes negative and $\omega$ returns to 0, and so on, each swing a little smaller than the last (pp. 9–10).

**The Jacobian** (pp. 10–12). At $(\pi, 0)$, replacing $\sin\theta$ by $\theta$ no longer works. In general the matrix of the linearized system is the **Jacobian matrix**, evaluated at the critical point (p. 11):

<Formula label="Jacobian matrix" tex="J = \begin{pmatrix} f_x & f_y \\ g_x & g_y \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J_0 = J(x_0, y_0)" cite="reading1" at="p. 11" />

For the pendulum (pp. 11–12):

<Formula label="Pendulum Jacobian" tex="J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2\cos\theta & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(0, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J(\pi, 0) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–12" />

At the origin this gives back the matrix found before (p. 12). At $(\pi, 0)$: $\lambda^2 + \lambda - 2 = (\lambda + 2)(\lambda - 1) = 0$. For $\lambda = 1$ the eigenvector is $(1, 1)$, going out; for $\lambda = -2$ it is $(1, -2)$, coming in more steeply. A **saddle** (pp. 12–13). Physically: started slightly short of upright, the pendulum falls back with growing negative speed; slightly past upright, it falls forward towards $2\pi$ (pp. 13–14).

## Step 3: the big picture

Draw the local pictures at every critical point: clockwise spiral sinks at $0, \pm2\pi, \dots$, where the Jacobian is the same since $\cos 2\pi = 1$, and saddles at $\pm\pi, \pm3\pi, \dots$ (transcript, pp. 14–15). Then add trajectories compatible with them. Trajectories cannot cross, and arrows close together cannot point opposite ways; if they would, there is a mistake in the local pictures (p. 15). One trajectory starts at $\theta = 0$ with a large positive $\omega$: the pendulum goes over the top, past $\pi$, keeps going, and finally winds into the spiral at $2\pi$ (p. 16). That is why all the critical points are needed, not just the two physical ones: they describe a pendulum going round and round before it settles (p. 16).

<WorkedExample title="Two competing species">
<Problem>
For $x' = x(4 - x - y)$, $y' = y(6 - x - 3y)$ with $x, y \ge 0$: (a) find the critical points; (b) classify $(4, 0)$ and the interior critical point by linearizing.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Critical points">
$x = 0$ or $x + y = 4$, and $y = 0$ or $x + 3y = 6$. This gives $(0, 0)$, $(0, 2)$, $(4, 0)$, and from $x + y = 4$, $x + 3y = 6$: $(3, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="The Jacobian">
$f = 4x - x^2 - xy$ and $g = 6y - xy - 3y^2$, so $J = \begin{pmatrix} 4 - 2x - y & -x \\ -y & 6 - x - 6y \end{pmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Two classifications">
At $(4, 0)$: $J = \begin{pmatrix} -4 & -4 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues $-4$ and $2$: a saddle, so a small population of the second species can invade. At $(3, 1)$: $J = \begin{pmatrix} -3 & -3 \\ -1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$, trace $-6$, determinant $9 - 3 = 6$, so $\lambda^2 + 6\lambda + 6 = 0$ and $\lambda = -3 \pm \sqrt3$, both negative: a nodal sink, stable coexistence.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Nonlinear autonomous systems: sketch instead of solve.
- Step 1: critical points, $f = g = 0$, constant solutions.
- Step 2: linearize with the Jacobian $J_0$ and sketch the linear picture (spiral, node, saddle).
- Step 3: join the pictures; trajectories cannot cross. The damped pendulum has spiral sinks hanging down and saddles pointing up.
