## Words used in this set

The system is written $\dot{\vec u} = A\vec u$, with $\vec u = (x, y)$ and a dot for the derivative with respect to $t$ (the lessons write $\vec x\,' = A\vec x$). MIT's sheet uses a few words that the lessons do not, or use only in passing:

- A **normal mode** is a solution of the form $e^{\lambda t}\vec v$, with $\lambda$ a number and $\vec v$ a constant vector: an eigenvalue of $A$ with one of its eigenvectors.
- An **eigenline** is the straight line through the origin along a real eigenvector. A solution that starts on it stays on it.
- A **trajectory** is the curve that a solution $(x(t), y(t))$ traces in the $(x, y)$ plane, and the **phase portrait** is the picture of all trajectories, with arrows for the direction of time.
- The **trace** $\operatorname{tr}A$ of a matrix is the sum of its two diagonal entries.
- "Stable" and "unstable" stand for the lessons' "sink" and "source" (MIT's Mathlet uses the lessons' words).

## Problem 34

<Exercise id="p34" title="Complex and repeated eigenvalues" points={26} mit="Part II, Problem 34">

Part (a) is about long-tailed weasels and meadow voles on an island. Biologists measure both populations from a baseline, in hundreds of animals: $x(t)$ for the weasels and $y(t)$ for the voles. So $x = 5$ means 500 weasels more than the baseline, and $y = -5$ means 500 voles fewer than the baseline. MIT's model for the two is

<Formula label="Weasels and voles" tex="\dot x = 0.5\,x + y, \qquad \dot y = -2.25\,x + 0.5\,y" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 34(a), p. 1" />

Left alone, each species grows at the rate $0.5$. The cross terms say that extra voles are good for the weasels, and that extra weasels are very bad for the voles. MIT splits part (a) into no smaller pieces; the three pieces below, with 6 points each, are our split of its 18 points.

<Part label="a (i)" points={6}>

Write the system as $\dot{\vec u} = A\vec u$. Find the eigenvalues of $A$. For one of them find an eigenvector and the exponential solution $e^{\lambda t}\vec v$. Then take its real part and its imaginary part to get two real solutions.

<Hint>

The characteristic polynomial of a 2 × 2 matrix is $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}A)\,\lambda + \det A$. Here its roots are not real.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the eigenvector, put the eigenvalue into the first row of $(A - \lambda I)\vec v = 0$ and choose the first entry of $\vec v$ to be $1$. Then multiply $\vec v$ by $e^{\lambda t} = e^{pt}(\cos qt + i\sin qt)$ and collect the terms without $i$ and the terms with $i$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p34-im" label="$q =$" value="1.5" tolerance="0.005" calc="p34-im" />

<Answer id="p34-vec" label="For the eigenvalue $p + qi$, the eigenvector with first entry $1$ has the second entry"

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in [the lesson](/courses/18-03/repeated-and-complex-eigenvalues): a complex eigenvalue and its eigenvector give one complex solution, and its real and imaginary parts are two real solutions.

<Step title="The eigenvalues">

The matrix is $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 & 1 \\ -2.25 & 0.5 \end{pmatrix}$, with trace $1$ and determinant $0.25 + 2.25 = 2.5$:

<Formula label="Characteristic polynomial" tex="p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - \lambda + 2.5, \qquad \lambda = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 10}}{2} = \frac{1 \pm 3i}{2}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 34(a), p. 1" />

So $p = 0.5$ and $q = 1.5$.

</Step>

<Step title="An eigenvector">

Take $\lambda_1 = \dfrac{1 + 3i}{2}$. The first row of $(A - \lambda_1I)\vec v = 0$ reads $-\dfrac{3i}{2}\,v_1 + v_2 = 0$, so

<Formula label="Eigenvector and normal mode" tex="\vec v = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3i/2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad e^{(1 + 3i)t/2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3i/2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 34(a), p. 1" />

(The second row, $-\dfrac{9}{4}\,v_1 - \dfrac{3i}{2}\,v_2 = 0$, says the same: it is the first row times $-\dfrac{3i}{2}$.)

</Step>

<Step title="Two real solutions">

Write $e^{(1 + 3i)t/2} = e^{t/2}\left(\cos\dfrac{3t}{2} + i\sin\dfrac{3t}{2}\right)$ and multiply out. The second entry is $\dfrac{3i}{2}\,e^{t/2}\left(\cos\dfrac{3t}{2} + i\sin\dfrac{3t}{2}\right) = e^{t/2}\left(-\dfrac{3}{2}\sin\dfrac{3t}{2} + \dfrac{3i}{2}\cos\dfrac{3t}{2}\right)$. The real and the imaginary parts are

<Formula label="Two real solutions" tex="\vec u_1 = e^{t/2}\begin{pmatrix} \cos(3t/2) \\ -\frac{3}{2}\sin(3t/2) \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec u_2 = e^{t/2}\begin{pmatrix} \sin(3t/2) \\ \frac{3}{2}\cos(3t/2) \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 34(a), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Put $\vec u_1$ into the first equation. Its $x$ has the derivative $e^{t/2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\cos\frac{3t}{2} - \frac{3}{2}\sin\frac{3t}{2}\right)$, and $0.5\,x + y$ is the same expression. The other three checks (the second equation, and both equations for $\vec u_2$) go the same way.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Solution>

**The idea.** One of the two real solutions of part (a)(i) already has the right starting values.

<Step title="The solution">

At $t = 0$ the solution $\vec u_1$ is $(1, 0)$, which is the initial condition. So

<Formula label="The initial value problem" tex="x(t) = e^{t/2}\cos\frac{3t}{2}, \qquad y(t) = -\frac{3}{2}\,e^{t/2}\sin\frac{3t}{2}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 34(a), p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Increasing or decreasing">

The system gives the velocity at any point directly. At $(1, 0)$:

<Formula label="Velocity at the start" tex="\dot{\vec u}(0) = A\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.5 \\ -2.25 \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 34(a), p. 1" />

So $\dot x(0) = 0.5 > 0$: the number of weasels is increasing. And $\dot y(0) = -2.25 < 0$: the number of voles is decreasing.

</Step>

<Step title="Back at the baseline">

$y(t) = 0$ when $\sin\dfrac{3t}{2} = 0$. The first time after $t = 0$ is $\dfrac{3t}{2} = \pi$, so $t = \dfrac{2\pi}{3} = 2.094$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate the formulas: $\dot x(0) = \frac{1}{2}$ and $\dot y(0) = -\frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{3}{2} = -2.25$, as the system said. At $t = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}$ the weasels are at $x = e^{\pi/3}\cos\pi = -2.850$: when the voles are back at their baseline, the weasels are 285 below theirs.

</Step>

and let $b$ run from $-4$ to $4$. ([The lesson](/courses/18-03/repeated-and-complex-eigenvalues) explains "complete" and "defective".)

For a defective eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ with eigenvector $\vec v$, MIT's sheet gives no method for this. MIT's solutions use this form (the lesson leaves the method to the problem set):

<Formula label="The extra solution for a defective eigenvalue" tex="\vec u_2 = e^{\lambda_1t}\left(t\,\vec v + \vec w\right), \qquad\text{where}\qquad (A - \lambda_1I)\,\vec w = \vec v" cite="solutions" at="Problem 34(b), p. 1" />

<Answer id="p34-b-w" label="For $b \ne 0$, with $\vec v = (1, 0)$ and $\vec w = (0, w_2)$: $w_2 =$" expr="1/b"

<Solution>

**The idea.** The eigenvalue $1$ is repeated for every $b$. Whether there are enough eigenvectors depends on whether $b$ is zero.

<Step title="Eigenvalue and eigenvectors">

<Formula label="A repeated eigenvalue" tex="p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 1 = (\lambda - 1)^2, \qquad A - I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 34(b), p. 1" />

So $\lambda_1 = 1$, twice, for every $b$. The equation $(A - I)\vec v = 0$ says $b\,v_2 = 0$.

- For $b \ne 0$ this forces $v_2 = 0$: the eigenvectors are the nonzero multiples of $\vec v = (1, 0)$ and nothing else. One direction for a double eigenvalue: the matrix is defective.
- For $b = 0$ the equation says nothing. Then $A = I$ and every nonzero vector is an eigenvector: the matrix is complete.

</Step>

<Step title="Normal modes">

For $b \ne 0$ the normal modes are $e^t\begin{pmatrix} c \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ with $c$ a real constant. For $b = 0$ they are $e^t\vec v$ for every constant vector $\vec v$, and these are all the solutions.

</Step>

<Step title="The extra solution for b ≠ 0">

Solve $(A - I)\vec w = \vec v$:

<Formula label="The vector w" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 0 & b \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\vec w = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec w = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1/b \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec u_2 = e^t\begin{pmatrix} t \\ 1/b \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 34(b), p. 1" />

(The first entry of $\vec w$ is free. Another choice adds a multiple of the normal mode to $\vec u_2$.) The general solution for $b \ne 0$ is $c_1e^t\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + c_2e^t\begin{pmatrix} t \\ 1/b \end{pmatrix}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The two phase portraits (our own)">

MIT's printed solution has no sketches for this part. These are ours.

Complete, $b = 0$: every solution is $e^t\vec u(0)$, so every trajectory is a straight ray from the origin, travelled outwards. Every line through the origin is an eigenline. This picture is an unstable **star**.

Defective, for example $b = 1$: the only eigenline is the $x$ axis, travelled outwards on both sides. Every other trajectory leaves the origin along the $x$ axis, on one side, and then bends over to the other side: for $c_2 \ne 0$ the ratio $\dfrac{x}{y} = b\left(\dfrac{c_1}{c_2} + t\right)$ runs from very negative values to very positive ones. This picture is an unstable **defective node**. For $b > 0$ the trajectories bend clockwise, and for $b < 0$ counterclockwise. The larger $|b|$ is, the flatter they lie.

<Formula label="The matrix" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} a & -3 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 35, p. 2" />

The kind of phase portrait can be read from two numbers, the trace and the determinant, because the characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}A)\,\lambda + \det A$. Three curves in the plane of trace and determinant are the walls between the kinds. MIT's sheet names them:

<Formula label="The three walls" tex="\det A = 0, \qquad \det A = \left(\frac{\operatorname{tr}A}{2}\right)^2, \qquad \operatorname{tr}A = 0" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 35(b), p. 2" />

MIT's sheet lists the names of the kinds and sends you to its Supplementary Notes for what they mean. Here is a summary in our own words. The [lesson](/courses/18-03/sketching-linear-systems) treats the node, the saddle and the spiral.

| Trace and determinant | Eigenvalues | Phase portrait |
|---|---|---|
| $\det A < 0$ | real, of opposite signs | **saddle** |
| $0 < \det A < (\operatorname{tr}A/2)^2$ | real, different, of the same sign | **node**: stable for a negative trace, unstable for a positive one |
| $\det A > (\operatorname{tr}A/2)^2$, $\operatorname{tr}A \ne 0$ | a complex pair with real part $\operatorname{tr}A/2$ | **spiral**: stable for a negative trace, unstable for a positive one; clockwise or counterclockwise |
| $\det A > 0$, $\operatorname{tr}A = 0$ | a purely imaginary pair | **center**: closed curves (ellipses) round the origin; clockwise or counterclockwise |
| $\det A = (\operatorname{tr}A/2)^2$, $\operatorname{tr}A \ne 0$ | one repeated eigenvalue $\operatorname{tr}A/2$ | **star** if the matrix is complete (then it is a multiple of $I$), **defective node** if it is defective; stable or unstable by the sign of the trace, and a defective node also turns, clockwise or counterclockwise |
| $\det A = 0$, $\operatorname{tr}A \ne 0$ | $0$ and $\operatorname{tr}A$ | **comb**: a whole line of points at rest, and straight trajectories that run towards it (stable) or away from it (unstable) |
| $\det A = 0$, $\operatorname{tr}A = 0$ | $0$, repeated | **constant** (every point at rest, for the zero matrix) or **parallel lines** |

The comb, the constant picture and the parallel lines are called degenerate. For the direction of turning, draw one velocity vector, as in the lesson.

It stands for multiplication by $z$: if $z\,(x + yi) = v + wi$, then $A(z)\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} v \\ w \end{pmatrix}$. (MIT's sheet takes this from one of its recitations; nothing more from there is needed.)

<Answer id="p36-a-same" label="The matrix $A(e^{zt})$ is"
  choices={["equal to e^(A(z)t)", "the transpose of e^(A(z)t), and not equal to it", "the inverse of e^(A(z)t)", "e^(A(z)t) without its factor e^(at)"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compute $e^{A(z)t}$ with eigenvalues, read $A(e^{zt})$ straight from Euler's formula, and find that they are the same matrix.

<Step title="Eigenvalues and a normal mode">

<Formula label="Characteristic polynomial" tex="p_A(\lambda) = \lambda^2 - 2a\lambda + (a^2 + b^2) = (\lambda - a)^2 + b^2, \qquad \lambda = a \pm bi" cite="solutions" at="Problem 36(a), p. 2" />

For $\lambda_1 = a + bi$ (and $b \ne 0$) the first row of $(A - \lambda_1I)\vec v = 0$ is $-bi\,v_1 - b\,v_2 = 0$, so $\vec v = (1, -i)$. The normal mode is

<Formula label="Normal mode" tex="e^{(a + bi)t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{pmatrix} = e^{at}\begin{pmatrix} \cos bt + i\sin bt \\ \sin bt - i\cos bt \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 36(a), p. 2" />

Its real part is $e^{at}(\cos bt, \sin bt)$ and its imaginary part is $e^{at}(\sin bt, -\cos bt)$. (MIT's solution prints the imaginary part with $+\cos bt$ in one line; the fundamental matrix in its next line has the correct $-\cos bt$.)

</Step>

<Step title="The exponential">

<Formula label="Fundamental matrix" tex="\Phi(t) = e^{at}\begin{pmatrix} \cos bt & \sin bt \\ \sin bt & -\cos bt \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Phi(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = \Phi(0)^{-1}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 36(a), p. 3" />

Multiplying by $\Phi(0)^{-1}$ on the right changes the sign of the second column:

<Formula label="The exponential of A(z)t" tex="e^{A(z)t} = \Phi(t)\,\Phi(0)^{-1} = e^{at}\begin{pmatrix} \cos bt & -\sin bt \\ \sin bt & \cos bt \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 36(a), p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="The matrix of e^(zt)">

$e^{zt} = e^{(a + bi)t} = e^{at}\cos bt + i\,e^{at}\sin bt$. Put the real part where $a$ stood and the imaginary part where $b$ stood:

<Formula label="The same matrix" tex="A\left(e^{zt}\right) = \begin{pmatrix} e^{at}\cos bt & -e^{at}\sin bt \\ e^{at}\sin bt & e^{at}\cos bt \end{pmatrix} = e^{A(z)t}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 36(a), p. 3" />

In words: the exponential of "multiply by $zt$" is "multiply by $e^{zt}$". For $a = 0$ the matrix is a rotation by the angle $bt$, as multiplication by $e^{ibt}$ should be.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second route, of our own, with the exponential law of the lesson. Write $A(z) = aI + bJ$ with $J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Since $I$ commutes with everything, $e^{A(z)t} = e^{at}\,e^{bJt}$. And $J^2 = -I$, so the series for $e^{bJt}$ sorts into $I\left(1 - \dfrac{(bt)^2}{2!} + \cdots\right) + J\left(bt - \dfrac{(bt)^3}{3!} + \cdots\right) = I\cos bt + J\sin bt$, the same matrix. (This route also covers $b = 0$, where the answer is $e^{at}I$.) At $t = 0$ the result is $I$, and its derivative there is $\begin{pmatrix} a & -b \\ b & a \end{pmatrix} = A(z)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

A pair of solutions $x_1(t)$, $x_2(t)$ of a second-order linear homogeneous equation with constant coefficients is called **normalized at $t = 0$** when

<Formula label="A normalized pair" tex="x_1(0) = 1, \quad \dot x_1(0) = 0, \qquad x_2(0) = 0, \quad \dot x_2(0) = 1" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 36(b), p. 3" />

([The lesson on the Wronskian](/courses/18-03/linear-operators-and-wronskian) introduces normalized solutions.) Find the normalized pair of solutions of

<Formula label="The equation" tex="\ddot x + 2\dot x + 2x = 0" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 36(b), p. 3" />

Then find $e^{At}$ for the **companion matrix** $A$ of the operator $D^2 + 2D + 2I$: the matrix of the first-order system that you get with $y = \dot x$ as the second unknown ([the lesson on first-order systems](/courses/18-03/first-order-systems)), which is $\dot x = y$, $\dot y = -2x - 2y$:

<Formula label="Companion matrix" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 36(b), p. 3" />

<Hint>

The characteristic polynomial $s^2 + 2s + 2$ has complex roots; write down two basic real solutions. Their values and slopes at $t = 0$ are not yet the wanted ones: take combinations of the two.

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The normalized pair is found from two basic solutions. The exponential of the companion matrix turns out to contain the same pair.

<Step title="The normalized pair">

$s^2 + 2s + 2 = (s + 1)^2 + 1$ has the roots $-1 \pm i$, so two basic solutions are

<Formula label="Basic solutions" tex="y_1 = e^{-t}\cos t, \qquad y_2 = e^{-t}\sin t" cite="solutions" at="Problem 36(b), p. 3" />

(MIT's solution writes $y$ here because $x_1$ and $x_2$ are taken for the normalized pair. These $y_1$, $y_2$ are not the second unknown $y = \dot x$ of the system.) Their starting values are $y_1(0) = 1$, $\dot y_1(0) = -1$ and $y_2(0) = 0$, $\dot y_2(0) = 1$. So $y_2$ is already right, and adding $y_2$ to $y_1$ repairs the slope of $y_1$:

<Formula label="The normalized pair" tex="x_1(t) = e^{-t}\left(\cos t + \sin t\right), \qquad x_2(t) = e^{-t}\sin t" cite="solutions" at="Problem 36(b), p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="The exponential of the companion matrix">

The characteristic polynomial of $A$ is $\lambda^2 + 2\lambda + 2$ again, with the eigenvalues $-1 \pm i$. For $-1 + i$ the first row of $(A - \lambda I)\vec v = 0$ is $(1 - i)\,v_1 + v_2 = 0$, so $\vec v = (1, -1 + i)$. The real and the imaginary part of the normal mode $e^{(-1 + i)t}\,\vec v$ are

<Formula label="Two real solutions of the system" tex="\vec u_1 = e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix} \cos t \\ -\cos t - \sin t \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec u_2 = e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix} \sin t \\ \cos t - \sin t \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 36(b), p. 3" />

With these as columns, $\Phi(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $\Phi(0)^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Multiplying by $\Phi(0)^{-1}$ on the right replaces the first column by the sum of the two columns:

<Formula label="The exponential" tex="e^{At} = \Phi(t)\,\Phi(0)^{-1} = e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix} \cos t + \sin t & \sin t \\ -2\sin t & \cos t - \sin t \end{pmatrix}" cite="solutions" at="Problem 36(b), p. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="The comment">

The top row of $e^{At}$ is the normalized pair $x_1$, $x_2$: MIT's remark. The reason, in our own words: the first column of $e^{At}$ is the solution $(x, y)$ of the system that starts at $(1, 0)$, and the second column the one that starts at $(0, 1)$. Because $y = \dot x$, these are the solutions of the second-order equation with $x(0) = 1$, $\dot x(0) = 0$ and with $x(0) = 0$, $\dot x(0) = 1$: exactly the normalized pair. The bottom row holds their derivatives, so

<Formula label="The exponential of a companion matrix" tex="e^{At} = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \\ \dot x_1 & \dot x_2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Part II, Problem 36(b), p. 3 (our own remark)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate the top row: $\dot x_1 = e^{-t}(-\cos t - \sin t - \sin t + \cos t) = -2e^{-t}\sin t$ and $\dot x_2 = e^{-t}(\cos t - \sin t)$, the bottom row. At $t = 0$ the matrix is $I$. The derivative of the matrix at $t = 0$ is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -2 & -2 \end{pmatrix} = A$.

</Step>

</Solution>
