For the rest of the term the subject is **systems** of differential equations: several equations, solved simultaneously, like systems of linear equations (transcript, p. 1). They follow the [Laplace transform lectures](/courses/18-03/impulse-inputs-delta-function).

## Vocabulary

With one independent variable $t$ and two dependent variables $x$ and $y$, a **first-order system** has the form (transcript, p. 1)

<Formula label="First-order system" tex="x' = f(x, y; t), \qquad y' = g(x, y; t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

It is **linear** if $x$ and $y$ occur linearly, although $t$ may appear in any way (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Linear system" tex="x' = ax + by + r_1(t), \qquad y' = cx + dy + r_2(t)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 1–2" />

If $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ are constants it has **constant coefficients**; they may also be functions of $t$ (p. 2). It is **homogeneous** if $r_1$ and $r_2$ are identically zero, which is where the study begins (p. 2).

**Initial conditions** (pp. 2–3). The number of arbitrary constants in the solution is the **total order** of the system: two for two first-order equations, three if one of them is second order. So a $2 \times 2$ first-order system needs two conditions, and they are the natural ones: the starting values $x(t_0)$ and $y(t_0)$, with no velocities required (p. 3).

## Where systems come from

The same models as for single equations, mixing, decay, heat conduction, diffusion, give systems whenever there are two of something that used to be one (transcript, pp. 3–4): two tanks, one feeding the other, or an electrical circuit with two loops. Add a third loop and the system has three equations; put a coil in one loop and that equation becomes second order, so the system has total order four (p. 4).

## The egg

An egg sits in a water bath at temperature $T_e(t)$ (transcript, p. 5). A real egg has a yolk, at temperature $T_1$, inside a white at temperature $T_2$, with different conductivities through the yolk's membrane and through the shell (p. 5). By Newton's law, the yolk gets heat only from the white, and the white from both the yolk and the bath (pp. 5–6). Written in standard form, with the variables in the same order on both sides, a habit that prevents many mistakes (p. 6):

<Formula label="The egg system" tex="T_1' = -a\,T_1 + a\,T_2, \qquad T_2' = a\,T_1 - (a + b)\,T_2 + b\,T_e(t)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 6–7" />

To coddle an egg, put it in boiling water and turn off the heat, so $T_e$ starts at 100 °C and decays exponentially (p. 7). To avoid the inhomogeneous term, take instead a hard-boiled egg dropped into an ice bath, $T_e = 0$, with the "nice numbers" $a = 2$, $b = 3$ (pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="Egg in an ice bath" tex="T_1' = -2T_1 + 2T_2, \qquad T_2' = 2T_1 - 5T_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

## Solving by elimination

The method that always works, and that you can rebuild in forty years without remembering anything, is to eliminate a variable (transcript, p. 8). From the first equation (p. 9),

<Formula label="T₂ in terms of T₁" tex="T_2 = \frac{T_1' + 2T_1}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

Substitute it into the second equation, carefully on paper, and multiply out (pp. 9–11):

<Formula label="Eliminated equation" tex="T_1'' + 7T_1' + 6T_1 = 0" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11" />

Two first-order equations become one second-order equation: in general the order of the single equation is the sum of the orders. You trade one complexity for another, the "law of conservation of mathematical difficulty" (p. 10). The coefficients must be positive, because the system must be stable: an egg in ice water cools to 0 (p. 11). The characteristic equation $r^2 + 7r + 6 = (r + 1)(r + 6) = 0$ gives (pp. 11–12)

<Formula label="Yolk temperature" tex="T_1 = c_1e^{-t} + c_2e^{-6t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 12" />

**Do not** find $T_2$ by eliminating $T_1$ and solving again: that is extra work and brings two more, unrelated arbitrary constants, while the system's solution has only two (p. 12). Use the relation already found instead (pp. 12–13):

<Formula label="White temperature" tex="T_2 = \frac{T_1' + 2T_1}{2} = \frac{1}{2}c_1e^{-t} - 2c_2e^{-6t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

**Initial values** (pp. 12–14). Say the yolk is at 40 °C and the white at 45 °C. Then $c_1 + c_2 = 40$ and $\frac{1}{2}c_1 - 2c_2 = 45$; doubling the second and subtracting gives $c_2 = -10$, $c_1 = 50$ (p. 13):

<Formula label="Solution" tex="T_1 = 50e^{-t} - 10e^{-6t}, \qquad T_2 = 25e^{-t} + 20e^{-6t}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 13–14" />

## Autonomous systems and velocity fields

As on the first day of the course, the geometric meaning matters as much as the algorithms, since systems are mostly solved by computer (transcript, p. 14). For a picture, there must be no $t$ on the right (p. 14):

<Formula label="Autonomous system" tex="x' = f(x, y), \qquad y' = g(x, y)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 14–15" />

It may be thoroughly nonlinear, but it is **autonomous** (pp. 14–15). A solution is a pair of functions $x(t)$, $y(t)$: a **parametrized curve**, travelled in a certain direction, and through every point passes one (pp. 15–16). $(x', y')$ is its velocity vector, tangent to the curve; the picture of the curves shows the direction of motion but not its speed (p. 16). So the system assigns a velocity vector to every point of the plane: it is a **velocity field**, and a solution is a parametrized curve with the right velocity everywhere (pp. 16–17). Its connection with the [direction fields](/courses/18-03/direction-fields) of the first lecture comes later (p. 17).

<WorkedExample title="A coupled system">
<Problem>
Solve $x' = -3x + y$, $y' = x - 3y$ with $x(0) = 2$, $y(0) = 0$ by elimination.
</Problem>
<Step title="Eliminate y">
From the first equation $y = x' + 3x$, so $y' = x'' + 3x'$. Substituting into the second: $x'' + 3x' = x - 3(x' + 3x)$, that is $x'' + 6x' + 8x = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve for x, then y">
$r^2 + 6r + 8 = (r + 2)(r + 4)$, so $x = c_1e^{-2t} + c_2e^{-4t}$ and $y = x' + 3x = c_1e^{-2t} - c_2e^{-4t}$, with the same two constants.
</Step>
<Step title="Initial values">
$c_1 + c_2 = 2$ and $c_1 - c_2 = 0$, so $c_1 = c_2 = 1$: $x = e^{-2t} + e^{-4t}$, $y = e^{-2t} - e^{-4t}$. Both tend to 0, as they must for a stable system with positive coefficients $6$ and $8$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A first-order system: $x' = f(x, y, t)$, $y' = g(x, y, t)$; linear, constant-coefficient and homogeneous as for single equations. It needs as many initial values as its total order.
- Systems arise when there are two of something: two tanks, two loops, a yolk and a white.
- Eliminate one variable to get a second-order equation; find the other variable from the first, with the same constants.
- An autonomous system is a velocity field; its solutions are parametrized curves following it.
