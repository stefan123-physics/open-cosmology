The rest of this part of the course studies the second-order linear equation with constant coefficients (transcript, p. 1). It builds on [first-order linear models](/courses/18-03/sinusoids-and-linear-models) and on [complex exponentials](/courses/18-03/complex-exponentials).

## The equation and its general solution

In standard form, with constant coefficients $A$ and $B$ and right-hand side zero (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Homogeneous second-order equation" tex="y'' + Ay' + By = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

A zero right-hand side makes the equation **homogeneous**; a function of $t$ there makes it inhomogeneous, which comes later (p. 1). The general theory waits two lectures; for now, assume that the general solution has the form (pp. 1–2)

<Formula label="General solution" tex="y = c_1y_1 + c_2y_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

with two arbitrary constants, as the order is two: roughly, solving means integrating twice (p. 2). Taking $c_1 = 1$, $c_2 = 0$ shows that $y_1$ is itself a solution, and likewise $y_2$, so **solving the equation means finding two solutions**. Initial values are then met by choosing $c_1$ and $c_2$ (p. 2). The two must be **independent**: $y_2$ must not be a constant multiple of $y_1$, otherwise there is really only one (pp. 4–5).

## The spring, the mass and the dashpot

The standard model is a mass $m$ on a horizontal track, attached to a spring and slowed by a **dashpot**, a piston in a chamber like the closer at the top of a door (transcript, pp. 2–3). With $x$ measured from the equilibrium position, the spring pulls back with force $-kx$ (Hooke's law) and the dashpot resists the velocity with force $-cx'$ (pp. 3–4). Newton's law gives (p. 4)

<Formula label="Spring–mass–dashpot" tex="m\,x'' + c\,x' + k\,x = 0, \qquad x'' + \frac{c}{m}\,x' + \frac{k}{m}\,x = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

$c$ is the **damping constant** and $k$ the **spring constant** (p. 4). (Here $c$ and $k$ are physical constants, not the arbitrary constants $c_1$, $c_2$.)

## The characteristic equation

The basic method, used all term, is to try an exponential, $y = e^{rt}$, and choose $r$ (transcript, p. 5). Substituting gives $r^2e^{rt} + Are^{rt} + Be^{rt} = 0$, and since $e^{rt}$ is never zero it cancels (pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="Characteristic equation" tex="r^2 + Ar + B = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

That is why quadratic equations are taught in school (p. 6). (The transcript reads the coefficients in lower case here; they are the $A$ and $B$ of the equation.) In practice, write the characteristic equation down at once without redoing the substitution (p. 7). The roots may be real and distinct, complex conjugates, or real and equal, and each case behaves differently (p. 6).

## Case 1: distinct real roots

Two roots $r_1 \ne r_2$ give two solutions at once (transcript, p. 6):

<Formula label="Distinct real roots" tex="y = c_1e^{r_1t} + c_2e^{r_2t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

**Example** (pp. 7–9). With $m = 1$, damping 4 and spring constant 3, the characteristic equation is $r^2 + 4r + 3 = (r + 3)(r + 1) = 0$, so $y = c_1e^{-3t} + c_2e^{-t}$. Release the mass from rest one unit from equilibrium: $y(0) = 1$, $y'(0) = 0$. Then $c_1 + c_2 = 1$ and $-3c_1 - c_2 = 0$: two simultaneous linear equations, giving $c_1 = -\frac12$, $c_2 = \frac32$ (pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="The example's solution" tex="y = -\tfrac12\,e^{-3t} + \tfrac32\,e^{-t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

Both exponentials decline. With other starting slopes the mass may cross equilibrium once, but it cannot cross back again; in the long run it just returns, without oscillating. This is the **overdamped** case (p. 10).

## Case 2: complex roots

With little damping we expect oscillation, and the roots are complex, $a \pm bi$ (transcript, p. 10). Formally that gives a complex solution $e^{(a + bi)t}$, but a real solution is needed (p. 11). The rescue (pp. 11–12):

**Theorem.** If $u + iv$ is a complex solution of $y'' + Ay' + By = 0$ with **real** $A$ and $B$, then $u$ and $v$ are real solutions.

**Proof.** Substituting, $(u'' + Au' + Bu) + i(v'' + Av' + Bv) = 0$. A complex number is zero only if its real and imaginary parts are both zero, so $u$ and $v$ each satisfy the equation. Calling the first bracket the real part used that $A$ and $B$ are real: with $A = 1 + i$ it would fail (p. 12). (The theorem only needs the equation to be linear with real coefficients, p. 11.)

The real and imaginary parts of $e^{(a + bi)t} = e^{at}(\cos bt + i\sin bt)$ give (pp. 12–13)

<Formula label="Complex roots a ± bi" tex="y = e^{at}\,(c_1\cos bt + c_2\sin bt)" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

The bracket is a combination of a cosine and a sine of the same frequency, so by the [sine–cosine identity](/courses/18-03/sinusoids-and-linear-models) it is a single oscillation, with the exponential controlling its amplitude (p. 13).

**Example** (pp. 13–15). Stiffen the spring to 5: $r^2 + 4r + 5 = 0$ gives $r = \frac{-4 \pm \sqrt{16 - 20}}{2} = -2 \pm i$. With the same initial values, $c_1 = 1$ and $c_2 = 2$ (p. 14):

<Formula label="Underdamped example" tex="y = e^{-2t}(\cos t + 2\sin t) = \sqrt5\,e^{-2t}\cos(t - \phi), \qquad \tan\phi = 2" cite="reading1" at="p. 14" />

The triangle has sides 1 and 2, so the amplitude factor is $\sqrt5$ (p. 14). (The lecture estimates $\phi$ at about 70°, pp. 14–15; in fact $\arctan 2 \approx 63.4°$.) The curve is a delayed cosine squeezed between $\pm\sqrt5\,e^{-2t}$, crossing the axis again and again: the **underdamped** case. In theory the oscillation never stops (pp. 14–15).

## Case 3: a double root

Between the two lies the **critically damped** case, the one people aim for when damping unwanted motion (transcript, p. 15). The roots are equal; call the root $-a$ with $a > 0$. Then the characteristic polynomial is $(r + a)^2 = r^2 + 2ar + a^2$, and the equation (pp. 15–16)

<Formula label="Critically damped equation" tex="y'' + 2a\,y' + a^2y = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 16" />

For a given spring constant exactly one damping gives this case (p. 16). The trouble: there is only one exponential solution, $e^{-at}$ (p. 16). One way to find another (the problem set has two, and a third comes later): if $y_1$ solves a linear equation $y'' + p(t)y' + q(t)y = 0$, there is a second solution of the form $y_1u$ (pp. 16–17). (The transcript states this equation incompletely.)

Try $y = e^{-at}u$ (p. 17). Then $y' = -ae^{-at}u + e^{-at}u'$ and $y'' = a^2e^{-at}u - 2ae^{-at}u' + e^{-at}u''$. Multiply the three lines by $a^2$, $2a$ and 1 and add: everything cancels except (pp. 17–18)

<Formula label="Reduction for the double root" tex="e^{-at}u'' = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u = c_1t + c_2" cite="reading1" at="pp. 17–18" />

One new solution is enough, so take $u = t$ (p. 18):

<Formula label="Second solution" tex="y = t\,e^{-at}" cite="reading1" at="p. 18" />

and so (combining the two, as in the general form above) the general solution is $e^{-at}(c_1 + c_2t)$.

<WorkedExample title="Three springs">
<Problem>
Solve (a) $x'' + 5x' + 6x = 0$ with $x(0) = 0$, $x'(0) = 1$; (b) $x'' + 2x' + 10x = 0$ with $x(0) = 2$, $x'(0) = 1$, and write the answer with a single cosine; (c) for $x'' + 6x' + kx = 0$, find the spring constant that makes the motion critically damped, and the general solution.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Overdamped">
$r^2 + 5r + 6 = (r + 2)(r + 3)$, so $x = c_1e^{-2t} + c_2e^{-3t}$. Then $c_1 + c_2 = 0$ and $-2c_1 - 3c_2 = 1$, so $c_1 = 1$, $c_2 = -1$: $x = e^{-2t} - e^{-3t}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Underdamped">
$r = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 - 40}}{2} = -1 \pm 3i$, so $x = e^{-t}(c_1\cos 3t + c_2\sin 3t)$. Then $c_1 = 2$ and $-c_1 + 3c_2 = 1$, so $c_2 = 1$. The triangle with sides 2 and 1 gives $x = \sqrt5\,e^{-t}\cos(3t - \phi)$ with $\phi = \arctan\frac12 \approx 0.464$ rad (26.6°).
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Critically damped">
A double root needs $r^2 + 6r + k = (r + 3)^2$, so $k = 9$, and $x = e^{-3t}(c_1 + c_2t)$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $y'' + Ay' + By = 0$: the general solution $c_1y_1 + c_2y_2$ needs two independent solutions.
- Try $e^{rt}$: the characteristic equation is $r^2 + Ar + B = 0$.
- Distinct real roots: $c_1e^{r_1t} + c_2e^{r_2t}$ (overdamped).
- Complex roots $a \pm bi$: $e^{at}(c_1\cos bt + c_2\sin bt)$ (underdamped), since real and imaginary parts of a complex solution are solutions.
- Double root $-a$: $e^{-at}(c_1 + c_2t)$ (critically damped).
