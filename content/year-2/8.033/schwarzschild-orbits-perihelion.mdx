Lecture 25 applies the orbital equations: circular orbits, near-circular orbits and the precession of Mercury's perihelion, radial orbits, and what really happens near the event horizon (slide 5). After a recap of the [evidence for black holes](/courses/8-033/evidence-for-black-holes) (slides 2–3), the lecture used a Java orbit simulator (slide 6). (MIT's material for this lecture is a set of slides, in a deck titled "Lecture 23"; this site numbers lectures by MIT's class sessions. This page follows the slides and marks what is not on them.)

## The orbit equations

Two quantities stay fixed along any free orbit in the Schwarzschild metric, its energy $E$ and its angular momentum $L$ (slide 4). It is convenient to divide both by the particle's rest mass: $\tilde E$ is then a pure number, and $\tilde L$ a length (with $c = G = 1$). With them, the motion obeys

<Formula label="Orbits in the Schwarzschild metric" tex="\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2 = \tilde E^2 - \tilde V\left(\tilde L, r\right)^2, \qquad \frac{d\varphi}{d\tau} = \frac{\tilde L}{r^2}, \qquad \tilde V\left(\tilde L, r\right)^2 = \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)\left(1 + \frac{\tilde L^2}{r^2}\right), \qquad \frac{dt}{d\tau} = \frac{\tilde E}{1 - 2M/r} = \gamma_r^2\tilde E" cite="mit" at="slide 4" />

where $\tilde V$ plays the role of a potential (per unit rest mass), $\tau$ is the particle's proper time, $t$ the far-away time and $\gamma_r = 1/\sqrt{1 - 2M/r}$. A particle can reach $r = \infty$ only if $\tilde E \ge 1$ (slide 4). (These are the equations previewed on the [last lecture's page](/courses/8-033/evidence-for-black-holes).)

## Circular orbits

Slide 8 plots the radius of circular orbits against $\tilde L$ (slides 7–9). (The page's own reading: a circular orbit sits at a minimum of $\tilde V$, stable, or a maximum, unstable; for $\tilde L$ above $\sqrt{12}M$ there are two, a stable outer and an unstable inner one, which merge at $6M$, while the unstable ones approach $3M$ as $\tilde L$ grows.) Slide 9 plots $\tilde L$, $\tilde E$ and the orbital speed measured by a shell observer, $v_{\text{shell}}$, against the radius. Four circular orbits are especially interesting (slide 10):

| Orbit | $r$ | $v_{\text{shell}}$ | $\tilde E$ | $\tilde L$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| photon orbit | $3M$ | 1 | $\infty$ | $\infty$ |
| tourist orbit | $4M$ | $1/\sqrt 2$ | 1 | $4M$ |
| last stable orbit | $6M$ | $\frac12$ | $\sqrt{8/9}$ | $\sqrt{12}M$ |
| classical limit | $\infty$ | 0 | 1 | $\infty$ |

At $3M$ only light can circle; at $4M$ (the page's reading of the name) a "tourist" who comes in from far away with just enough energy ($\tilde E = 1$) can orbit, though, being inside $6M$, only unstably; and inside $6M$ no circular orbit is stable. (The page's own formulas behind the table: $\tilde L^2 = Mr^2/(r - 3M)$, $\tilde E = (1 - 2M/r)/\sqrt{1 - 3M/r}$ and $v_{\text{shell}} = \sqrt{M/(r - 2M)}$.)

## Near-circular orbits: Mercury

General orbits (slide 11) do not close: the perihelion advances a little each orbit. For Mercury the advance is 43 arcseconds per century (slide 12); the page adds that this is the general-relativistic part, an anomaly Newtonian theory could not explain. (The page's own formula, for an orbit of semi-major axis $a$ and eccentricity $e$:)

<Formula label="Perihelion advance per orbit" tex="\Delta\varphi = \frac{6\pi GM}{c^2a\left(1 - e^2\right)}" cite="mit" at="slide 12 (the 43 arcseconds; the formula is the page's own)" />

Slides 13–15 recall how GPS works, with its 24 satellites; the page adds that GPS needs relativistic clock corrections every day (see the [page for Lecture 7](/courses/8-033/doppler-aberration-twin-paradox)).

## Radial orbits: spaghetti or pancake?

Radial "orbits" are a one-way trip (slide 16). Spaghetti or pancake (slide 17)? Slides 18–19 release two particles a short time apart, from $r = 100M$ and from $r = 30M$, and plot "bookkeeper" (far-away) time against radius; on slide 18 the gap between them grows. (The answer is the page's own: the leading particle, always deeper in the field, pulls ahead, so a body falling in is stretched along the radius, and squeezed sideways: spaghetti, not pancake. Close to the horizon the gap in bookkeeper $r$ shrinks again, an effect of the coordinates, not of the bodies.) Slides 20–26 return to the river-model plots of free-fall time against radius, with light rays inside and outside the horizon, from the [last lecture but one](/courses/8-033/schwarzschild-black-holes).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Conserved quantities in cosmology">
The trick that makes these orbits solvable, a conserved quantity for every coordinate the metric does not depend on, works in cosmology too: 8.286 uses the same reasoning with [Christoffel symbols](/courses/8-286/christoffel-symbols), where a coordinate missing from the metric gives a conserved quantity and a freely moving particle's momentum falls in proportion to $1/a$ as the universe expands.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Mercury's advance and a black hole's efficiency">
<Problem>
(a) Mercury has $a = 5.791 \times 10^{10}$ m, $e = 0.2056$ and a period of 87.969 days; the Sun has $GM = 1.327 \times 10^{20}$ m³/s². Compute the perihelion advance per century. (b) Gas in a disk around a non-rotating black hole spirals slowly in through circular orbits and plunges in from the last stable orbit. What fraction of its rest energy has it radiated?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Mercury">
$\Delta\varphi = 6\pi \times 1.327 \times 10^{20}/((2.998 \times 10^8)^2 \times 5.791 \times 10^{10} \times (1 - 0.2056^2)) \approx 5.02 \times 10^{-7}$ rad per orbit. A century has $36{,}525/87.969 \approx 415$ orbits, giving $2.08 \times 10^{-4}$ rad, or about 43 arcseconds.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Efficiency">
At $6M$, $\tilde E = \sqrt{8/9} \approx 0.943$, so the gas has radiated $1 - 0.943$, about 5.7%, of its rest energy: eight times more than hydrogen fusion (0.7%). This is why accreting black holes, such as quasars, are the most efficient engines known; around a fast-spinning hole the figure rises to about 42%, as the next lecture shows.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- With conserved $\tilde E$ and $\tilde L$, a Schwarzschild orbit is motion in the effective potential $\tilde V^2 = (1 - 2M/r)(1 + \tilde L^2/r^2)$.
- Circular orbits: the photon orbit at $3M$, the tourist orbit at $4M$, and the last stable orbit at $6M$, where the binding energy is 5.7% of the rest energy (for a non-rotating hole).
- Orbits precess (43 arcseconds per century for Mercury), and a body falling straight in is stretched: spaghetti, not pancake.
