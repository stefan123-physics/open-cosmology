Lecture 19, the second of four on cosmology, covers the Friedmann equation and its solutions, the age of the universe and a brief history of the universe (slide 5). It opens with Edwin Hubble (1889–1953), credited on the slide with a 1930 paper (slide 1; Hubble's velocity–distance paper appeared in 1929). (MIT's material for this lecture is a set of slides, in a deck titled "Lecture 18"; this site numbers lectures by MIT's class sessions. Two slides were short films, which are not on OCW. This page follows the slides and marks what is not on them.)

## Two key facts

Key fact 1, from the [last lecture](/courses/8-033/frw-metric-expansion): the observable universe is homogeneous and isotropic on large scales (slide 2), so it is described by the FRW metric, with the scale factor $a(t)$, comoving coordinates $r$, $\theta$, $\varphi$ attached to galaxies, and the Hubble parameter $H = \dot a/a$ (slide 3).

Key fact 2: the universe is expanding (slide 4). Galaxies recede from us with speeds proportional to their distance:

<Formula label="Hubble's law" tex="v = Hr" cite="mit" at="slide 4" />

This follows from the metric: the physical distance to a galaxy at fixed comoving coordinate is proportional to $a(t)$, so it grows at the rate $\dot a/a$ times itself (the page's own one-line derivation).

## What expansion means

What does it *mean* that the universe is expanding? Slide 7 shows the balloon picture: dots on an inflating balloon all move apart, and each dot sees the others recede in proportion to distance, with no centre on the surface. Slide 8 lists thirteen questions that confuse many people (demonstrated in class with a bungee cord, slides 7–8). The questions below are the slide's, lightly shortened; the short answers are the page's own:

1. *What is the universe expanding into?* Nothing: the expansion is a growth of distances within space, described by $a(t)$; it needs no outside.
2. *How can things be more than 14 billion light years away when the universe is only about 14 billion years old?* (The slide says "14 billion light years old", a slip.) Light that left them long ago has been travelling through expanding space, and the distance has kept growing since.
3. and 4. *Where did the big bang happen, and at a single point?* Everywhere. The whole of space was hot and dense; there is no centre.
5. and 6. *How could an infinite space be created in finite time, or could space be finite?* If space is infinite now, it was infinite from the start, just denser; if $k > 0$, space is finite but has no edge, like the balloon's surface.
7. *If the universe is only 10 billion years old, how can we see objects now 30 billion light years away?* (In a flat, matter-only universe, the most distant matter we can see is now exactly three times $ct$ away: the [horizon distance](/courses/8-286/horizon-distance).) Their distance *now* is larger than the distance their light has travelled, for the same reason as in question 2.
8. *Don't galaxies receding faster than $c$ violate relativity?* No. Relativity forbids overtaking a light ray locally; the separation of distant galaxies can grow faster than $c$.
9. *Are galaxies moving, or is space expanding?* Both descriptions work locally; the expanding-space one is natural for the whole universe.
10. *Is the Milky Way expanding?* No: it is held together by its own gravity, as are stars, planets and people.
11. *Is there evidence for a big bang singularity?* There is strong evidence for a hot, dense early state; whether it began with a singularity is not known.
12. *What came before the big bang?* Unknown; in inflationary models the hot big bang was preceded by inflation.
13. *Should I feel insignificant?* The slides leave that one to you.

## The Friedmann equation and the age

The Friedmann equation (slides 6 and 9) is

<Formula label="Friedmann equation" tex="H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2} = H_0^2\left[\Omega_\gamma\left(1 + z\right)^4 + \Omega_m\left(1 + z\right)^3 + \Omega_k\left(1 + z\right)^2 + \Omega_\Lambda\right]" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

with the age $t(z) = \int_z^\infty dz'/((1 + z')H(z'))$. Slide 6 now gives the 2006 measurements: $\Omega_b \approx 0.04$, $\Omega_d \approx 0.21$, $\Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.75$, $\Omega_k \approx 0$, $h \approx 0.7$, and $\Omega_m \approx 0.25$ (the [previous deck](/courses/8-033/frw-metric-expansion), headed 2005, had 0.05, 0.25, 0.7 and 0.3).

**Solutions** (the page's own, for a flat universe dominated by one ingredient; the slides list the topic, slide 5). With one term in the bracket, $\dot a/a \propto a^{-n/2}$ for a density falling as $a^{-n}$, which gives

<Formula label="Solutions of the Friedmann equation" tex="\text{radiation: } a \propto t^{1/2}, \qquad \text{matter: } a \propto t^{2/3}, \qquad \text{vacuum energy: } a \propto e^{Ht}" cite="mit" at="slide 5 (topic; the solutions are the page's own)" />

For matter alone, $H = 2/(3t)$, so the age is $t_0 = \frac23/H_0$, younger than the Hubble time. Vacuum energy makes the universe older than that, because the expansion speeds up at late times.

## A brief history of our universe

Slide 11 summarizes the history. **Inflation**, in the first fraction of a second, is labelled the fluctuation generator, and the later evolution the fluctuation amplifier (the page's own reading: inflation leaves the universe almost perfectly smooth, with tiny seed fluctuations, and gravity then makes the dense spots denser). An arrow runs from hot, dense and smooth at early times to cool, rarefied and clumpy today. At 379,000 years the cosmic microwave background was released at "last scattering", showing those seeds. The first stars formed after a few hundred million years (the slide's "about 200 million years" is struck through and corrected by hand to 400). Today, at 13.7 billion years, the universe is cool, rarefied and clumpy. Slides 12 and 13 were films of structure formation and of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey.

<WorkedExample title="Ages and speeds from Hubble's law">
<Problem>
Take $h = 0.7$. (a) How old would a flat, matter-only universe be? (b) How old is a flat universe with the 2006 values $\Omega_m = 0.25$, $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.75$? (c) At what distance does Hubble's law give a recession speed equal to $c$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Matter only">
The Hubble time is $9.778/0.7 \approx 14.0$ billion years (slide 3 gives the conversion as 9.7846 Gyr, with a 365-day year), so the age would be $\frac23 \times 14.0 \approx 9.3$ billion years: younger than the oldest stars, one of the old arguments against this model.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) With vacuum energy">
$t_0 = \frac{2}{3H_0\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}}\sinh^{-1}\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda/\Omega_m} \approx 14.2$ billion years. (The 13.7 billion years on slide 11 is the WMAP-era value of the age; the rounded parameters on slide 6 are not precise enough to reproduce it, and the age is sensitive to $h$: WMAP-like values, $h = 0.71$ and $\Omega_m = 0.27$, give 13.7.)
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The Hubble distance">
$r = c/H_0 = 299{,}800/70 \approx 4280$ Mpc, about 14 billion light years. Beyond it, galaxies recede faster than light.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The universe is homogeneous and isotropic, and expanding: $v = Hr$, with distances growing as $a(t)$ and no centre.
- Flat solutions: $a \propto t^{1/2}$ for radiation, $t^{2/3}$ for matter, $e^{Ht}$ for vacuum energy; vacuum energy makes the universe older than $\frac23/H_0$.
- History: inflation, then the CMB at 379,000 years, first stars after a few hundred million years, and today's clumpy universe at 13.7 billion years.
