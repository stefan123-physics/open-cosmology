## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The redshift of a quasar" points={3} mit="Problem 1">

MIT's Figure 1 shows the visible spectrum of a distant quasar, from 3400 to 7000 Å (1 Å $= 10^{-10}$ m). Five emission lines stand out above the smooth background. We read their positions from MIT's figure, to about 20 Å each:

| Line in the spectrum | Observed wavelength $\lambda$ (Å) |
|---|---|
| 1 (the strongest) | 3560 |
| 2 | 5210 |
| 3 | 5510 |
| 4 | 6170 |
| 5 | 6350 |

MIT's sheet also gives a table of the laboratory wavelengths $\lambda_0$ of the emission lines seen most often in quasars. These are measured facts:

| Line | $\lambda_0$ (Å) | Line | $\lambda_0$ (Å) |
|---|---|---|---|
| H α | 6563 | [Ne III] | 3869 |
| [O III] | 5007 | [O II] | 3728 |
| [O III] | 4959 | Mg II | 2799 |
| H β | 4861 | C III] | 1909 |
| [O III] | 4363 | C IV | 1549 |
| H γ | 4340 | Si IV and O IV] | 1402 |
| H δ | 4102 | N V | 1241 |
| H ε and [Ne III] | 3969 | Lyman α | 1216 |

(MIT's table prints 4956 for the second [O III] line. The accepted value is 4959 Å. This line is not needed for the answer.)

Every line of a source is shifted by the same factor, so the redshift is defined as

<Formula label="Redshift" tex="z = \frac{\lambda - \lambda_0}{\lambda_0}, \qquad \frac{\lambda}{\lambda_0} = 1 + z" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Identify the five lines, and find the redshift $z$ of the quasar.

<Hint>

All five ratios $\lambda/\lambda_0$ must come out the same. The table is sorted by wavelength and so is the spectrum, so the five laboratory lines you pick must be in the same order as the observed ones.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Try the strongest line first. If line 1 were Lyman α, the ratio would be $3560/1216 = 2.93$, and C IV and C III] would have to show up near 4540 and 5590 Å. Nothing is there. Try the next candidates for line 1 until four more lines land on the observed positions.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The spacing helps: lines 2, 3 and 4 have the ratios $5510/5210$ and $6170/5510$. Look for three laboratory lines (not necessarily next to each other in the table) with the same ratios.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-lines" choices={["Lyman α, C IV, C III], Mg II and [O II]", "Mg II, H δ, H γ, H β and [O III] 5007", "[O II], H γ, H β, [O III] 5007 and H α", "C IV, C III], Mg II, [O II] and H δ"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-z" label="$z =$" value="0.270" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-z" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A redshift multiplies every wavelength by the same factor $1 + z$. So the pattern of the lines, the ratios between them, is the same in the quasar as in the laboratory. Find the set of laboratory lines with the same pattern.

<Step title="Match the pattern">

The observed ratios between neighbours are $5510/5210 = 1.058$ and $6170/5510 = 1.120$. In the table, the Balmer lines of hydrogen have $4340/4102 = 1.058$ (H γ over H δ) and $4861/4340 = 1.120$ (H β over H γ). So lines 2, 3 and 4 are H δ, H γ and H β, with a factor of about 1.27. Dividing the other two observed wavelengths by 1.27 gives 2803 and 5000 Å: Mg II (2799) and [O III] (5007).

</Step>

<Step title="The five ratios">

| Observed $\lambda$ (Å) | Line | $\lambda_0$ (Å) | $\lambda/\lambda_0$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3560 | Mg II | 2799 | 1.2719 |
| 5210 | H δ | 4102 | 1.2701 |
| 5510 | H γ | 4340 | 1.2696 |
| 6170 | H β | 4861 | 1.2693 |
| 6350 | [O III] | 5007 | 1.2682 |

The mean of the five ratios is 1.2698, so

<Formula label="The redshift of the quasar" tex="z = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda_0} - 1 \approx 0.270" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The five ratios agree to within 0.004, which is what a reading error of 20 Å gives ($20/5000 = 0.004$). No other choice in the table does this. With Lyman α as line 1, for example, the ratio is 2.93 and no listed line falls on any of the other four positions. The identification also makes sense physically: Mg II and the Balmer lines are among the strongest lines of quasars, and at this redshift H α lies at $6563 \times 1.27 \approx 8330$ Å, beyond the red end of the spectrum.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The jets of SS433" points={12} mit="Problem 2">

SS433 is a double star in our Galaxy. A collapsed star orbits a normal star and pulls gas from it. Part of that gas is thrown out in two narrow beams (jets) that leave in exactly opposite directions, $180°$ apart. The atoms in the jets emit spectral lines, so each jet has its own Doppler shift. The shifts change with time because the axis of the jets slowly swings round a cone (it precesses): we see the jets from a changing angle.

MIT's Figure 2 shows the measured Doppler shifts $(\lambda - \lambda_0)/\lambda_0$ of the two jets against time, from 1979 to 1983: two curves that cross each other twice in every cycle of about 162 days (our reading). We read three values from MIT's figure, each to about 0.005 (the crossings lie between 0.033 and 0.036):

| Reading | $(\lambda - \lambda_0)/\lambda_0$ |
|---|---|
| Where the two curves cross | $0.035$ |
| Highest point of either curve | $0.175$ |
| Lowest point of either curve | $-0.10$ |

The graph shows the shape of the two curves over two and a half cycles.

<Plot variable="t" domain={[0, 400]} range={[-0.15, 0.2]} xLabel="time (days)" yLabel="(λ − λ₀)/λ₀"
  curves={[
    { expr: "0.035 + 0.049 + 0.090*cos(2*pi*t/162)", label: "jet 1" },
    { expr: "0.035 - 0.049 - 0.090*cos(2*pi*t/162)", label: "jet 2", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="The Doppler shifts of the two jets of SS433 against time, after MIT's Figure 2. The curves are computed from a cosine formula of our own that follows the smooth curves of MIT's figure (MIT's figure shows the measured points as well). Jet 1 swings between about 0.174 and −0.006, jet 2 between about 0.076 and −0.104, and the two cross at 0.035. The table above the graph gives the readings to use." />

The relativistic Doppler formula of the lesson, written for wavelengths, is

<Formula label="Doppler shift of a moving source" tex="\frac{\lambda}{\lambda_0} = \gamma\,(1 - \beta\cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3" />

Here $\lambda$ is the wavelength we measure, $\lambda_0$ is the wavelength in the frame of the moving gas, $\beta c$ is the speed of the gas, and $\theta$ is the angle between the velocity of the gas and the direction from the source to the Earth. Both $\lambda$ and $\theta$ are measured in the Earth's frame. ($\theta = 0$ is a jet coming straight at us.)

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the Doppler shift $(\lambda - \lambda_0)/\lambda_0$ at the moments when both jets show the same wavelength?

<Hint>

Both jets have the same wavelength where the two curves meet.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-cross" label="$(\lambda - \lambda_0)/\lambda_0 =$" value="0.035" tolerance="10%" calc="p2-cross" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** This is a reading. The two jets show the same wavelength where their curves cross.

<Step title="Read the crossing">

The curves cross at $(\lambda - \lambda_0)/\lambda_0 \approx 0.035$, the first row of the table. The same value comes back at every crossing.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The crossing is not at zero shift. Both jets are redshifted at that moment, although (as part (b) shows) neither moves towards us or away from us then. That is the sign of a relativistic effect.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Show from the Doppler formula that the two jets can have the same wavelength only when $\theta = 90°$, that is, when both jets move at right angles to our line of sight.

<Hint>

If one jet moves at the angle $\theta$ to the direction of the Earth, at which angle does the opposite jet move? Both have the same speed.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The jets are opposite, so their angles add up to $180°$ and their cosines are each other's opposite.

<Step title="Two jets, one formula">

Jet 1 has the angle $\theta$, jet 2 the angle $180° - \theta$, with $\cos(180° - \theta) = -\cos\theta$. With the same $\beta$ and $\gamma$ for both:

<Formula label="The two jets" tex="\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_0} = \gamma\,(1 - \beta\cos\theta), \qquad \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_0} = \gamma\,(1 + \beta\cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

They are equal when $-\beta\cos\theta = +\beta\cos\theta$. Since $\beta$ is not zero, this needs $\cos\theta = 0$, so $\theta = 90°$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $\theta = 90°$ both jets give $\lambda/\lambda_0 = \gamma$, which is larger than 1: a redshift with no motion along the line of sight. This is the transverse Doppler effect of the lesson, which is time dilation: the atoms in the jet are moving clocks, and they run slow by the factor $\gamma$ (Lecture 6 slides, slide 28).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Use parts (a) and (b) to find $\beta$ for the jets. Use the value $0.035$ of the table.

<Hint>

At the crossing $\lambda/\lambda_0 = \gamma$. What is $\lambda/\lambda_0$ there, from part (a)?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Solve $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}$ for $\beta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-beta" label="$\beta =$" value="0.258" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-beta" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At the crossing the shift is pure time dilation, so it gives $\gamma$ directly.

<Step title="From the crossing to the speed">

At the crossing $\lambda/\lambda_0 = 1 + 0.035 = 1.035 = \gamma$. Then

<Formula label="The speed of the jets" tex="\beta = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{1.035^2}} = \sqrt{0.0665} = 0.258" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

The gas leaves at about a quarter of the speed of light.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a small shift $\gamma \approx 1 + \beta^2/2$, so $\beta \approx \sqrt{2 \times 0.035} = 0.265$, close to the exact value. The answer is sensitive to the reading: crossings of 0.030 and 0.040 would give $\beta = 0.240$ and $0.275$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

The largest Doppler shift in the table is $0.175$. Use the Doppler formula and your $\beta$ to find the largest angle $\theta$ that a jet makes with the direction to the Earth, in degrees.

<Hint>

Put $\lambda/\lambda_0 = 1.175$ and $\gamma = 1.035$ in the Doppler formula and solve for $\cos\theta$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A redshift larger than $\gamma$ means the jet moves away from us: expect a negative cosine, an angle beyond $90°$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-theta" label="$\theta_{\max} =$" value="121.6" unit="degrees" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-theta" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The largest redshift belongs to the moment when a jet points as far away from us as it ever does.

<Step title="Solve for the angle">

<Formula label="The largest angle" tex="\cos\theta_{\max} = \frac{1}{\beta}\left(1 - \frac{1.175}{\gamma}\right) = \frac{1 - 1.1353}{0.2579} = -0.525, \qquad \theta_{\max} = 121.6°" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(d) (our own solution)" />

At that moment the jet points $31.6°$ beyond the perpendicular, away from us.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

An independent check uses the third row of the table. When one jet is at its largest angle, the opposite jet is at its smallest, and the two angles must add up to $180°$. The lowest shift, $-0.10$, gives $\cos\theta = (1 - 0.90/1.035)/0.2579 = 0.506$, so $\theta = 59.6°$. The sum is $121.6° + 59.6° = 181.2°$: equal to $180°$ within the accuracy of the readings.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The headlight effect" points={9} mit="Problem 3">

(MIT took this problem from a textbook. The physics is the same here; the numbers of parts (b) and (c) are ours.)

A source of light is at rest in frame $S'$ and sends out light equally in all directions. Frame $S'$ moves at the speed $\beta c$ through frame $S$. Seen from $S$, the light is crowded towards the direction of motion. Call $\theta_{0.5}$ the half-angle, in $S$, of the forward cone that holds half of the light.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Show that at high speed ($\beta \to 1$) the half-angle is, in radians, close to

<Formula label="The half-light cone at high speed" tex="\theta_{0.5} \approx \sqrt{2\,(1 - \beta)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 4" />

<Hint>

In the frame of the source, which directions hold half of the light? Find the edge of that set of directions.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Transform the edge direction to $S$ with the aberration formula of the lesson. Mind which frame is the primed one, and use $\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2$ for a small angle.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** In its own frame the source sends half of its light into the forward hemisphere, $\theta' \le 90°$. Aberration maps that hemisphere to a cone in $S$, and it maps the edge to the edge.

<Step title="The edge of the cone">

The lesson gives the direction of a light ray in a frame $S'$ that moves at $\beta$ along the axis from which the angles are measured:

<Formula label="Aberration" tex="\cos\theta' = \frac{\cos\theta - \beta}{1 - \beta\cos\theta} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \cos\theta = \frac{\cos\theta' + \beta}{1 + \beta\cos\theta'}" cite="reading3" at="slide 27 (the second form is the first with the frames exchanged, β → −β)" />

The second form increases steadily with $\cos\theta'$, so directions inside the forward hemisphere of $S'$ stay inside the cone of the edge ray. The edge ray has $\cos\theta' = 0$:

<Formula label="The half-light cone, exact" tex="\cos\theta_{0.5} = \beta" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="High speed">

For $\beta$ close to 1 the angle is small and $\cos\theta_{0.5} \approx 1 - \theta_{0.5}^2/2$. Then $1 - \theta_{0.5}^2/2 \approx \beta$, which gives $\theta_{0.5} \approx \sqrt{2(1 - \beta)}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $\beta = 0$ the exact result gives $\theta_{0.5} = 90°$: half of the light in the forward hemisphere, as it must be for a source at rest. With MIT's hint, $1 - \beta \approx 1/(2\gamma^2)$ at high speed, the result reads $\theta_{0.5} \approx 1/\gamma$: the beam narrows in step with the Lorentz factor.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

A beam of unstable particles moves at $\beta = 0.98$ and each particle emits light equally in all directions in its own frame. What is $\theta_{0.5}$, in degrees? Use the high-speed formula of part (a).

<Hint>

The formula gives radians. Multiply by $180/\pi$ for degrees.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-angle" label="$\theta_{0.5} =$" value="11.46" unit="degrees" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-angle" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the number in.

<Step title="The number">

$\theta_{0.5} \approx \sqrt{2 \times 0.02} = \sqrt{0.04} = 0.200$ rad, which is $0.200 \times 180/\pi = 11.46°$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The exact result of part (a) gives $\theta_{0.5} = \arccos 0.98 = 11.48°$. The high-speed formula is off by only $0.02°$ here.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How fast must a source move towards an observer to have half of its light inside a forward cone of half-angle $2.0°$? Give $1 - \beta$, in units of $10^{-3}$.

<Hint>

Turn the angle into radians first, then solve the formula of part (a) for $1 - \beta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-speed" scale="1e-3" label="$1 - \beta =$ ($10^{-3}$)" value="0.609" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-speed" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Invert the high-speed formula: $1 - \beta \approx \theta_{0.5}^2/2$.

<Step title="The number">

$2.0° = 0.03491$ rad, so $1 - \beta \approx 0.03491^2/2 = 6.09 \times 10^{-4}$, or $0.609 \times 10^{-3}$. The speed is $\beta = 0.99939$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The exact result, $1 - \cos 2.0° = 6.09 \times 10^{-4}$, agrees to three figures. In terms of the Lorentz factor, $\gamma \approx 1/\theta_{0.5} = 1/0.03491 \approx 29$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A lamp with no Doppler shift" points={3} mit="Problem 4">

A lamp moves past you in a straight line at the speed $\beta c$. While it approaches, its light is blueshifted; long after it has passed, its light is redshifted. Somewhere in between you see it with no shift at all.

Call $\theta$ the angle, in your frame, between the velocity of the lamp and the direction in which the light travels from the lamp to you. (Make a drawing: the straight track of the lamp, the point where the light was emitted, yourself beside the track, and the angle at the emission point between the track ahead and the line to you. $\theta = 0$ would be a lamp coming straight at you.) Find $\cos\theta$ for the light that reaches you unshifted, as a formula in $\beta$.

<Hint>

Use the Doppler formula of Problem 2, $\lambda/\lambda_0 = \gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta)$. No shift means $\lambda = \lambda_0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

At $\theta = 90°$ the formula gives $\lambda/\lambda_0 = \gamma$, a redshift. So the unshifted light must have left the lamp earlier, while the lamp was still approaching a little.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-cos" label="$\cos\theta =$" expr="(1 - sqrt(1 - b^2))/b" vars={{ b: [0.2, 0.9] }} names={{ b: "\\beta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "0", hint: "At 90° in your frame the lamp is redshifted by the factor γ: the transverse Doppler effect. The unshifted light left the lamp a little earlier." },
    { expr: "b", hint: "With cos θ = β the formula gives λ/λ₀ = γ(1 − β²) = 1/γ, which is still a blueshift. The angle you want lies between this one and 90°." },
    { expr: "b/2", hint: "That is the low-speed limit of the answer. Solve γ(1 − β cos θ) = 1 without approximating." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Approaching makes the light bluer and time dilation makes it redder. At one angle the two cancel exactly.

<Step title="Set the shift to zero">

<Formula label="No Doppler shift" tex="\gamma\,(1 - \beta\cos\theta) = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cos\theta = \frac{1}{\beta}\left(1 - \frac{1}{\gamma}\right) = \frac{1 - \sqrt{1 - \beta^2}}{\beta}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

The cosine is positive, so $\theta$ is a little less than $90°$: you see the unshifted lamp slightly before the point of its closest approach, while it still comes towards you a little.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For low speed $\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} \approx 1 - \beta^2/2$, so $\cos\theta \approx \beta/2$ and the angle is just under $90°$. For $\beta \to 1$, $\cos\theta \to 1$: the lamp must come almost straight at you. A number: $\beta = 0.6$ gives $\cos\theta = (1 - 0.8)/0.6 = 1/3$, so $\theta = 70.5°$.

A second route gives the same answer. Multiplying top and bottom by $1 + \sqrt{1 - \beta^2}$ gives $\cos\theta = \gamma\beta/(\gamma + 1)$. With the rapidity $\eta$ of the lesson ($\beta = \tanh\eta$, $\gamma = \cosh\eta$) this is $\tanh(\eta/2)$: the speed of the frame "halfway" between you and the lamp. In that frame you and the lamp move at equal and opposite speeds. By symmetry, light that travels at right angles to the motion in that frame is shifted down at emission by exactly the factor by which it is shifted up at reception, so it is the unshifted light. Seen from your frame, the direction of that ray has $\cos\theta$ equal to the speed of the halfway frame, by the aberration formula of Problem 3. For $\beta = 0.6$ the halfway frame moves at $1/3$, because $(1/3 + 1/3)/(1 + 1/9) = 0.6$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Three frames" points={12} mit="Problem 5">

Take three inertial frames $S$, $S'$ and $S''$. Frame $S'$ moves at $v' = \frac35 c$ along the $x$-axis of $S$. Frame $S''$ moves at $v'' = \frac45 c$ along the $y'$-axis of $S'$. The axes of the three frames are parallel, and the three origins coincide at $t = t' = t'' = 0$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Write down the Lorentz transformation from $S$ to $S'$ and the one from $S'$ to $S''$: give $(x', y', z', ct')$ in terms of $(x, y, z, ct)$, and $(x'', y'', z'', ct'')$ in terms of $(x', y', z', ct')$. Matrices are a convenient way to write them.

<Hint>

The lesson's transformation is for a boost along $x$. For a boost along $y$, the roles of $x$ and $y$ are exchanged: now $y$ mixes with $ct$ and $x$ is left alone.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You need $\gamma$ and $\gamma\beta$ for each boost. Both come out as simple fractions.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A boost mixes the time with the one space coordinate along the motion and leaves the other two alone.

<Step title="From S to S′">

For $\beta = 3/5$: $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 9/25} = 5/4$ and $\gamma\beta = 3/4$. The transformation of the lesson gives

<Formula label="Boost along x at 3c/5" tex="\begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ z' \\ ct' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac54 & 0 & 0 & -\frac34 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\frac34 & 0 & 0 & \frac54 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \\ ct \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="slide 16 (the general matrix; the numbers are ours)" />

<Exercise id="p6" title="The twin who left is older this time" points={12} mit="Problem 6">

(MIT took this problem from a textbook. The physics is the same here; the names and the numbers are ours.)

Vera and Hugo are twins. Vera leaves Hugo at the steady speed $0.6c$ and never turns round. Hugo knows her plan. <Part label="a" points={3}>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-speed" label="$\beta_H =$" value="0.8824" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p6-speed" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Hugo's 4 travelling years are proper time. In $S$ they last $4\gamma_H$ years, and in that time he must make up Vera's head start.

<Step title="Two conditions">

With times in years and distances in light years, both twins are at the same place at the meeting time $t$:

<Formula label="Catching up" tex="0.6\,t = \beta_H\,(t - 4), \qquad t - 4 = 4\gamma_H" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a) (our own solution)" />

Put the second in the first: $0.6\,(4 + 4\gamma_H) = 4\beta_H\gamma_H$, so $0.6 = \gamma_H(\beta_H - 0.6)$, or $0.6\sqrt{1 - \beta_H^2} = \beta_H - 0.6$. Squaring gives $0.36 - 0.36\beta_H^2 = \beta_H^2 - 1.2\beta_H + 0.36$, so $1.36\,\beta_H = 1.2$:

<Formula label="Hugo's speed" tex="\beta_H = \frac{1.2}{1.36} = \frac{15}{17} = 0.8824, \qquad \gamma_H = \frac{17}{8}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second route. Look from Vera's frame, where she is at rest. Hugo first moves away from her at $0.6c$ for 4 years of his clock, then comes back to her in 4 more years of his clock. The two legs cover the same distance in Vera's frame in the same proper time, so they have the same speed: Hugo approaches Vera at $0.6c$ in her frame. His speed in $S$ is then $0.6c$ added to $0.6c$ with the velocity addition of the lesson: $(0.6 + 0.6)/(1 + 0.36) = 15/17$. The same.

Note that "4 more years" must mean Hugo's own clock. Four more years of $S$ time would need a speed above $c$: Vera is then $4.8$ light years away, more than light covers in 4 years.

## Problem 8

<Exercise id="p8" title="A twin paradox with smooth acceleration" points={9} mit="Problem 8">

Two clocks, A and B, start at rest at the same place in frame $S$ and show the same time. Clock A stays at rest in $S$. Clock B makes a trip along the $x$-axis and comes back to rest beside clock A. The whole trip takes the time $T$ on clock A and the time $T'$ on clock B. All times, speeds and accelerations below are measured in $S$.

The way out has three stages:

1. Clock B has the constant acceleration $a_0$ for the time $fT/4$. Here $f$ is the fraction of the whole trip that is spent accelerating.
2. Clock B coasts at the constant speed $\beta c$ for the time $(1 - f)T/2$.
3. Clock B has the acceleration $-a_0$ for the time $fT/4$ and comes to rest.

The way back is the way out run backwards, so it takes the same time, $T/2$, and clock B ends at rest beside clock A. MIT notes that in $S$ the ordinary relations $v = v_0 + at$ and $x = x_0 + v_0t + \frac12 at^2$ still hold, because $a_0$ is the acceleration measured in $S$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find $T'/T$. MIT asks for it as a function of $a_0$, $\beta$ and $f$; you will find that $a_0$, $\beta$, $f$ and $T$ are tied together, so the ratio can be written with $\beta$ and $f$ alone. Give it in that form.

<Hint>

The proper time of a moving clock is $\int\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}\,dt$ (the lesson on geodesics). During the coasting stages the integrand is constant. During an accelerating stage $v = a_0t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The first stage ends at the speed $\beta c$, so $a_0\,fT/4 = \beta c$. Change the variable of the integral from $t$ to $u = a_0t/c$, which runs from 0 to $\beta$. The four accelerating stages all give the same proper time.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You need $\int\sqrt{1 - u^2}\,du = \frac12\left(u\sqrt{1 - u^2} + \arcsin u\right)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-ratio" label="$T'/T =$" expr="(1 - f)*sqrt(1 - b^2) + (f/2)*(sqrt(1 - b^2) + asin(b)/b)" vars={{ b: [0.2, 0.95], f: [0.1, 0.9] }} names={{ b: "\\beta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(1 - b^2)", hint: "That is the result when no time is spent accelerating (f = 0). During the accelerating stages the clock is slower than βc, so it loses less time." },
    { expr: "(1 - f)*sqrt(1 - b^2) + f", hint: "You let clock B tick at the full rate while it accelerates. Its speed is not zero then: integrate √(1 − v²/c²) with v = a₀t." },
    { expr: "(1 - f)*sqrt(1 - b^2) + f*(sqrt(1 - b^2) + asin(b)/b)", hint: "Check the factor ½ in the integral of √(1 − u²). At β → 0 your ratio is 1 + f, larger than 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Add up the proper time of clock B stage by stage. The speed of clock B is known at every moment in $S$, so each stage is one integral.

<Step title="Proper time">

<Formula label="Proper time of a moving clock" tex="T' = \int_0^T \sqrt{1 - \frac{v(t)^2}{c^2}}\;dt" cite="reading5" at="slide 8 (written there with c = 1)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The coasting stages">

There are two, each of length $(1 - f)T/2$, at the speed $\beta c$. Together they give $(1 - f)\,T\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The accelerating stages">

In the first stage $v = a_0t$, from $t = 0$ to $t_1 = fT/4$, where the speed reaches $\beta c$:

<Formula label="The link between the givens" tex="a_0\,\frac{fT}{4} = \beta c" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(a) (our own solution)" />

With $u = a_0t/c$, so that $dt = (c/a_0)\,du$:

<Formula label="One accelerating stage" tex="\int_0^{t_1}\sqrt{1 - \frac{a_0^2t^2}{c^2}}\;dt = \frac{c}{a_0}\int_0^{\beta}\sqrt{1 - u^2}\;du = \frac{c}{2a_0}\left(\beta\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} + \arcsin\beta\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(a) (our own solution)" />

The other three accelerating stages run through the same speeds, from $\beta c$ down to 0 or back, so each gives the same proper time: the integrand depends only on the size of the speed.

</Step>

<Step title="The total">

<Formula label="The trip on clock B" tex="T' = (1 - f)\,T\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} + \frac{2c}{a_0}\left(\beta\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} + \arcsin\beta\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(a) (our own solution)" />

This is the form with $a_0$, $\beta$ and $f$. The link above gives $c/a_0 = fT/(4\beta)$, and then

<Formula label="The ratio of the two clocks" tex="\frac{T'}{T} = (1 - f)\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} + \frac{f}{2}\left(\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} + \frac{\arcsin\beta}{\beta}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For low speed, $\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} \approx 1 - \beta^2/2$ and $\arcsin\beta/\beta \approx 1 + \beta^2/6$, which gives $T'/T \approx 1 - \beta^2\left(\frac12 - \frac{f}{3}\right)$. That is what the low-speed rule "a clock loses the fraction $\frac12 v^2/c^2$" predicts: the mean of $v^2/c^2$ over the trip is $\beta^2$ for the fraction $1 - f$ of the time and $\beta^2/3$ for the fraction $f$ (the mean of $t^2$ over a ramp), and $\frac12\left[(1 - f) + \frac{f}{3}\right] = \frac12 - \frac{f}{3}$. The ratio is below 1 for every $\beta > 0$: clock B always comes back behind.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 9

<Exercise id="p9" title="The shortest path on a sphere" points={12} mit="Problem 9">

Use the calculus of variations to find the shortest path between two points on a sphere of radius 1. A point of the sphere is given by the polar angle $\theta$ (measured from the $z$-axis) and the azimuth $\phi$:

<Formula label="Points of the unit sphere" tex="x = \sin\theta\cos\phi, \qquad y = \sin\theta\sin\phi, \qquad z = \cos\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 9, Eq. (9.5), p. 5" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

<Formula label="Line element of the unit sphere" tex="ds = \left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)^{1/2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 9(a), Eq. (9.1), p. 5" />

<Step title="The differentials">

<Formula label="Small steps on the sphere" tex="dx = \cos\theta\cos\phi\,d\theta - \sin\theta\sin\phi\,d\phi, \quad dy = \cos\theta\sin\phi\,d\theta + \sin\theta\cos\phi\,d\phi, \quad dz = -\sin\theta\,d\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 9(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

Along a meridian ($d\phi = 0$) the step is $d\theta$: an arc of a unit circle. Along a circle of latitude ($d\theta = 0$) it is $\sin\theta\,d\phi$: an arc of a circle of radius $\sin\theta$. Both are right.

</Step>

</Solution>

Take $\theta$ as the independent variable, so that a path is a function $\phi(\theta)$. Use the Euler–Lagrange equation to show that the shortest path obeys

where $a$ is a constant of integration. (MIT writes the cosecant here, which is $1/\sin\theta$.)

</Hint>

<Hint>

If $\partial L/\partial\phi = 0$, the Euler–Lagrange equation says that $\partial L/\partial\phi'$ is constant. Call the constant $a$ and solve for $\phi'$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The integrand does not contain $\phi$, only its derivative. Then the Euler–Lagrange equation can be integrated once at sight.

<Step title="The functional">

<Formula label="Length of a path on the sphere" tex="s = \int L\,d\theta, \qquad L(\theta, \phi, \phi') = \sqrt{1 + \sin^2\theta\;\phi'^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 9(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The Euler–Lagrange equation">

In the lesson's form, with $\theta$ in the place of $x$ and $\phi$ in the place of $y$:

<Formula label="Euler–Lagrange equation for the path" tex="\frac{d}{d\theta}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\phi'} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\phi} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial L}{\partial\phi'} = \frac{\sin^2\theta\;\phi'}{\sqrt{1 + \sin^2\theta\;\phi'^2}} = a" cite="reading5" at="slide 3 (listed there, not written out; the form is the lesson's, applied to the sphere by us)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for the slope">

Square it: $\sin^4\theta\,\phi'^2 = a^2 + a^2\sin^2\theta\,\phi'^2$, so $\phi'^2\sin^2\theta\,(\sin^2\theta - a^2) = a^2$ and

<Formula label="The slope of the path" tex="\phi' = \frac{a}{\sin\theta\sqrt{\sin^2\theta - a^2}} = \frac{a/\sin^2\theta}{\sqrt{1 - a^2/\sin^2\theta}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 9(b) (our own solution)" />

The second form is the first with top and bottom divided by $\sin^2\theta$. The sign of $\phi'$ is the sign of $a$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$a = 0$ gives $\phi' = 0$: a meridian, which is certainly a shortest path. The square root needs $\sin\theta \ge |a|$: the path never comes closer to a pole than the angle with $\sin\theta = |a|$, and it turns round there. For $|a| = 1$ only $\theta = 90°$ is allowed: the equator.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Integrate the equation of part (b) to show that

<Formula label="The geodesic" tex="\cot\theta = -\frac{\sqrt{1 - a^2}}{a}\,\sin(\phi - \alpha)" cite="mit" at="Problem 9(c), Eq. (9.3), p. 5" />

where $\alpha$ is a second constant of integration.

<Hint>

Try the substitution $w = \cot\theta$. Its derivative is $-1/\sin^2\theta$, and $1/\sin^2\theta = 1 + \cot^2\theta$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You should reach an integral of the form $\int dw/\sqrt{b^2 - w^2} = \arcsin(w/b)$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The factor $d\theta/\sin^2\theta$ is, up to a sign, the differential of $\cot\theta$.

<Step title="Substitute">

With $w = \cot\theta$: $dw = -d\theta/\sin^2\theta$ and $1 - a^2/\sin^2\theta = 1 - a^2 - a^2w^2$. Then

<Formula label="The integral" tex="\phi = \int\frac{a\,d\theta}{\sin^2\theta\sqrt{1 - a^2/\sin^2\theta}} = -\int\frac{a\,dw}{\sqrt{(1 - a^2) - a^2w^2}} = -\arcsin\!\left(\frac{a\,w}{\sqrt{1 - a^2}}\right) + \alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 9(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for the cotangent">

So $\sin(\alpha - \phi) = a\cot\theta/\sqrt{1 - a^2}$, and since $\sin(\alpha - \phi) = -\sin(\phi - \alpha)$:

<Formula label="The path" tex="\cot\theta = -\frac{\sqrt{1 - a^2}}{a}\,\sin(\phi - \alpha)" cite="mit" at="Problem 9(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate the result along the path: $-1/\sin^2\theta = -\frac{\sqrt{1 - a^2}}{a}\cos(\phi - \alpha)\,\phi'$. With $\cos(\phi - \alpha) = \sqrt{1 - a^2\cot^2\theta/(1 - a^2)} = \sqrt{1 - a^2/\sin^2\theta}/\sqrt{1 - a^2}$ this is the equation of part (b) again (on the branch where the cosine is positive). The closest approach to the pole is where the sine is $\pm 1$: $\cot\theta = \sqrt{1 - a^2}/|a|$, which is $\sin\theta = |a|$, as found in part (b).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Show that the result of part (c) can be written in Cartesian coordinates as

<Formula label="A plane through the centre" tex="A\,y - B\,x = z" cite="mit" at="Problem 9(d), Eq. (9.4), p. 5" />

with constants $A$ and $B$. This is a plane through the centre of the sphere, and it holds the two chosen points. What kind of curve is a geodesic of the sphere?

<Hint>

Multiply the result of part (c) by $\sin\theta$ and expand $\sin(\phi - \alpha)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-curve" choices={["A circle of constant latitude through both points", "An arc of a great circle: a circle whose centre is the centre of the sphere", "A spiral that crosses every meridian at the same angle", "An arc of the smallest circle on the sphere through both points"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The products $\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $\sin\theta\cos\phi$ and $\cos\theta$ are exactly $y$, $x$ and $z$.

<Step title="To Cartesian coordinates">

Multiply by $\sin\theta$ and use $\sin(\phi - \alpha) = \sin\phi\cos\alpha - \cos\phi\sin\alpha$:

<Formula label="The geodesic in space" tex="\cos\theta = -\frac{\sqrt{1 - a^2}}{a}\left(\cos\alpha\;\sin\theta\sin\phi - \sin\alpha\;\sin\theta\cos\phi\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = A\,y - B\,x" cite="mit" at="Problem 9(d) (our own solution)" />

with $A = -\frac{\sqrt{1 - a^2}}{a}\cos\alpha$ and $B = -\frac{\sqrt{1 - a^2}}{a}\sin\alpha$.

</Step>

<Step title="What the curve is">

The equation $Ay - Bx - z = 0$ is linear and has no constant term, so it is a plane through the origin, the centre of the sphere. The two constants $A$ and $B$ are fixed by making the plane pass through the two chosen points. The geodesic is where this plane cuts the sphere: a **great circle**, a circle with the same centre and the same radius as the sphere. The shortest path is the shorter of the two arcs of that circle between the points.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Two points and the centre fix one plane (unless the points are exactly opposite each other, when every great circle through them is equally short). The special cases of part (b) fit: $|a| = 1$ gives $A = B = 0$, the plane $z = 0$, the equator. A circle of latitude other than the equator lies in a plane $z = \text{constant}$ that misses the centre, so it is not a geodesic: flying along a parallel of latitude is longer than the great-circle route.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 10 (optional)

<Exercise id="p10" title="The Fresnel drag coefficient" points={6} mit="Optional Problem 10" optional>

This problem is optional at MIT and here: it does not count towards the score of the set.

In a medium with index of refraction $n$, light travels at the speed $c/n$ relative to the medium. Now the medium (water in a pipe, say) flows at the speed $v$ through the laboratory, in the same direction as the light.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Use the relativistic addition of velocities to find the speed $u$ of the light in the laboratory. Give $u/c$ as a formula in $n$ and $\beta = v/c$.

<Hint>

In the frame of the medium the light has the speed $c/n$. The medium's frame moves at $v$ through the laboratory. Add the two with the formula for parallel velocities.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-u" label="$u/c =$" expr="(1/n + b)/(1 + b/n)" vars={{ n: [1.2, 2], b: [0.05, 0.5] }} names={{ b: "\\beta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/n + b", hint: "That is the Galilean sum. Divide by 1 + (speed 1)(speed 2)/c², as the relativistic addition of velocities says." },
    { expr: "1/n + b*(1 - 1/n^2)", hint: "That is the result to first order in β (part (b)). Here the exact speed is asked." },
    { expr: "(1/n + b)/(1 + b*n)", hint: "Check the product of the two speeds in the denominator: (c/n)(v)/c² is β/n, not βn." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The speed $c/n$ is an ordinary speed below $c$, so it adds to the speed of the medium like the speed of any body.

<Step title="Add the velocities">

<Formula label="Light in a moving medium" tex="u = \frac{\frac{c}{n} + v}{1 + \frac{(c/n)\,v}{c^2}} = \frac{\frac{c}{n} + v}{1 + \frac{v}{nc}}" cite="reading2" at="slide 14 (velocity addition; applied to the medium by us)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $n = 1$ (no medium) $u = (c + v)/(1 + v/c) = c$: light in vacuum has the speed $c$ in every frame. For $v = 0$, $u = c/n$. For $v \to c$, $u \to c$: the light is never pushed past $c$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Show that to lowest order in $v/c$

<Formula label="Fresnel drag" tex="u \approx \frac{c}{n} + v\left(1 - \frac{1}{n^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 10, p. 5" />

The factor in brackets is called the Fresnel drag coefficient.

<Hint>

For a small number $\epsilon$, $1/(1 + \epsilon) \approx 1 - \epsilon$. Multiply out and keep only the terms with one factor of $v$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Expand the denominator for small $v/c$.

<Step title="Expand">

<Formula label="First order in v/c" tex="u \approx \left(\frac{c}{n} + v\right)\left(1 - \frac{v}{nc}\right) = \frac{c}{n} + v - \frac{v}{n^2} - \frac{v^2}{nc} \approx \frac{c}{n} + v\left(1 - \frac{1}{n^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 10 (our own solution)" />

The dropped term $v^2/(nc)$ is smaller than the kept ones by a factor of order $v/c$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $n = 1$ the coefficient is 0: light in vacuum is not carried along at all. For very large $n$ it goes to 1: very slow light is carried along fully, like a sound wave or a swimmer, the Galilean result. As MIT remarks, there was never a need for a "drag" mechanism: the formula is just the relativistic addition of velocities. (MIT's sheet dates Fresnel's prediction of the effect to 1817 and Fizeau's measurement of it to 1851.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

(This part is our own addition.) MIT quotes $n \approx 1.5$ as a typical index of refraction for water and glass. What is the drag coefficient for $n = 1.5$?

<Hint>

Put $n = 1.5$ in the bracket of part (b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-coeff" label="$1 - 1/n^2 =$" value="0.556" tolerance="1%" calc="p10-coeff" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the number in.

<Step title="The number">

$1 - 1/1.5^2 = 1 - 0.444 = 0.556$. Light in such a medium picks up a little more than half of the medium's speed.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Compare with the exact formula of part (a) at $\beta = 0.01$: $u/c = (0.66667 + 0.01)/(1 + 0.0066667) = 0.672185$. The first-order formula gives $0.666667 + 0.01 \times 0.555556 = 0.672222$. They differ by $4 \times 10^{-5}$, of the order of $\beta^2$, as expected.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 11 (optional)

<Exercise id="p11" title="Calibrating a Minkowski diagram" points={9} mit="Optional Problem 11" optional>

This problem is optional at MIT and here: it does not count towards the score of the set. (MIT names a textbook among the sources of this problem and uses a frame speed of $4c/5$. The physics is the same here; the speed and the drawing are ours.)

A Minkowski diagram of frame $S$ has $x$ along the horizontal axis and $ct$ along the vertical axis, with the same scale on both. A second frame $S'$ moves at $\beta = 3/5$ (so $\gamma = 5/4$) along $x$. Its $ct'$-axis (the points with $x' = 0$) and its $x'$-axis (the points with $ct' = 0$) are tilted lines through the origin. The graph shows them, with the path of a light ray between them.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 4]} range={[0, 4]} xLabel="x" yLabel="ct"
  curves={[
    { expr: "x/0.6", label: "ct′ axis" },
    { expr: "0.6*x", label: "x′ axis" },
    { expr: "x", dash: true },
    { expr: "x >= 1 ? sqrt(x^2 - 1) : NaN" }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 1.25, y: 0.75, label: "A" }, { x: 1, y: 0, label: "B" }, { x: 0.8, y: 0, label: "C" }, { x: 1.25, y: 0, label: "D", offCurve: true }]}
  caption="A Minkowski diagram of frame S, computed from the Lorentz transformation for β = 3/5. The steep line is the ct′-axis of the moving frame and the shallow line is its x′-axis; the dashed line between them is a light ray, ct = x. Point A lies on the x′-axis, one unit of x′ from the origin. The curve through B (at x = 1 on the x-axis) and A is the calibration curve of part (c). The points C and D on the x-axis are those of parts (b) and (a)." />

Point $A$ lies on the $x'$-axis and has the coordinates $(x', ct') = (1, 0)$. Point $B$ is the point $(x, ct) = (1, 0)$ on the $x$-axis.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

From $A$ a line is drawn parallel to the $ct$-axis (straight down). It meets the $x$-axis at point $D$. Use the Lorentz transformation to find the coordinate $x$ of $D$, and compare with the graph.

<Hint>

$D$ has the same $x$ as $A$. Find the $S$ coordinates of $A$ from its $S'$ coordinates with the inverse transformation, $x = \gamma(x' + \beta ct')$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-d" label="$x_D =$" value="1.25" tolerance="1%" calc="p11-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A line parallel to the $ct$-axis is a line of constant $x$. So $x_D$ is simply the $x$ of $A$.

<Step title="Transform A to S">

The inverse of the lesson's transformation is the same with $-\beta$ in place of $\beta$:

<Formula label="Point A in frame S" tex="x = \gamma\,(x' + \beta\,ct') = \tfrac54(1 + 0) = 1.25, \qquad ct = \gamma\,(ct' + \beta\,x') = \tfrac54 \cdot \tfrac35 = 0.75" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 11(i) (our own solution)" />

So $x_D = 1.25 = \gamma$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

On the graph $A$ sits at $x = 1.25$, $ct = 0.75$, on the line $ct = 0.6x$. In words: the unit stick of $S'$ (from the origin to $A$, taken at one moment of $S'$) spans more than one unit of $x$, because its two ends are not taken at the same time $t$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

From $A$ a second line is drawn parallel to the $ct'$-axis. It meets the $x$-axis at point $C$. Find the coordinate $x$ of $C$, and compare with the graph.

<Hint>

A line parallel to the $ct'$-axis is a line of constant $x'$: here $x' = 1$. Point $C$ is where it has $ct = 0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-c" label="$x_C =$" value="0.8" tolerance="1%" calc="p11-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The line through $A$ parallel to the $ct'$-axis is the world line of the point $x' = 1$, which is at rest in $S'$.

<Step title="Where x′ = 1 crosses t = 0">

<Formula label="Point C" tex="x' = \gamma\,(x - \beta\,ct) = 1 \ \text{ with } \ ct = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_C = \frac{1}{\gamma} = 0.8" cite="reading1" at="slide 16 (the Lorentz transformation; applied to the point by us)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

On the graph: start at $A = (1.25, 0.75)$ and go down along a line with the slope of the $ct'$-axis, $1/0.6$. Dropping $0.75$ in $ct$ moves $x$ back by $0.6 \times 0.75 = 0.45$, to $x = 0.80$. This is length contraction: the stick of rest length 1 in $S'$, with both ends taken at the same time $t = 0$ of $S$, has the length $1/\gamma$ (Lecture 5 slides, slide 12). Parts (a) and (b) together show that $\gamma$ and $1/\gamma$ both appear, depending on which frame's "same time" is used.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

The curve through $B$ and $A$ is drawn so that it crosses the $x$-axis at $x = 1$, the $x'$-axis at $x' = 1$, and the $x''$-axis of any other frame $S''$ (moving at any other speed along $x$) at $x'' = 1$. Find the equation $x(t)$ of this curve.

<Hint>

Which combination of $x$ and $ct$ has the same value in every frame?

</Hint>

<Hint>

On each frame's own $x$-axis its time coordinate is zero. What is the invariant there?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-curve" label="$x(t) =$" expr="sqrt(1 + c^2*t^2)" vars={{ c: [0.5, 1], t: [0.1, 0.9] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(1 - c^2*t^2)", hint: "That is a circle, x² + (ct)² = 1, which rotations keep fixed. Boosts keep x² − (ct)² fixed: mind the sign." },
    { expr: "sqrt(1 + t^2)", hint: "Keep the c: the vertical axis is ct, so the invariant is x² − (ct)²." },
    { expr: "1 + c*t", hint: "A straight line cannot work: it crosses the tilted x′ axes at different values of x′. Use the invariant interval." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The curve is the set of all events at the same interval from the origin. The interval is the one thing all frames agree on.

<Step title="The invariant">

The crossing point with the $x''$-axis of any frame has $(x'', ct'') = (1, 0)$, so $x''^2 - (ct'')^2 = 1$ there. The interval has the same value in $S$ (Lecture 5 slides, slide 21), so every point of the curve obeys

<Formula label="The calibration hyperbola" tex="x^2 - (ct)^2 = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x(t) = \sqrt{1 + c^2t^2}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 11(iii) (our own solution)" />

It is a hyperbola that leaves $B$ straight upwards and approaches the light ray $ct = x$ from below.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Point $A$ is on it: $1.25^2 - 0.75^2 = 1.5625 - 0.5625 = 1$. Point $B$ is on it: $1 - 0 = 1$. This is why the hyperbola "calibrates" the diagram: it marks the unit of length on the $x$-axis of every frame at once, and the tilted axes have stretched scales on the page (the unit of $x'$ is drawn $\sqrt{1.25^2 + 0.75^2} = 1.46$ page units long).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
