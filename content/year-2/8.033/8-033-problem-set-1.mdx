## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The first measurement of the speed of light" points={3} mit="Problem 1">

In the 1670s the Danish astronomer Ole Rømer showed for the first time that light takes time to travel. MIT's sheet tells the story like this. Jupiter's moon Io circles Jupiter with a steady period, and once per circuit it comes out from behind the planet. Rømer worked with timings of these reappearances by the astronomer Giovanni Cassini. The time between two reappearances in a row, as seen from Earth, was a little longer while Earth was moving away from Jupiter than while it was approaching.

Picture Earth's orbit as a circle around the Sun, with Jupiter far outside it. Call A the point of the orbit nearest to Jupiter and D the opposite point, farthest from Jupiter. Earth takes six months from A to D, passing two points B and C on the way. During those six months every observed period of Io was slightly longer than the period observed around A. Added together, the excesses of all those periods came to 22 minutes. Jupiter needs 12 years for one orbit of the Sun, so treat it as standing still.

The effect is not a Doppler shift of light. It comes from the time light needs to reach Earth, which changes as Earth moves along its orbit.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

Explain where the 22 minutes come from. Which length does light cross in that time?

<Hint>

Suppose Io reappears at regular moments at Jupiter. Each flash of news then travels to wherever Earth is. Compare the travel times of two flashes in a row while Earth moves away.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Add the excesses of all periods between A and D. In the sum, every distance in between appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Io is a clock at Jupiter. We read the clock by light, and while Earth moves away each tick has farther to travel than the one before.

<Step title="One period">

Let Io reappear at the moments $t_n = nT$, where $T$ is its true period, and let $d_n$ be the distance from Jupiter to Earth when the light of reappearance number $n$ arrives. That light arrives at the time $t_n + d_n/c$. The period seen from Earth is the time between two arrivals:

<Formula label="Observed period of Io" tex="T_\text{obs} = \left(t_{n+1} + \frac{d_{n+1}}{c}\right) - \left(t_n + \frac{d_n}{c}\right) = T + \frac{d_{n+1} - d_n}{c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

While Earth moves away, $d_{n+1} > d_n$ and the observed period is too long. While Earth approaches it is too short. Around A and around D Earth moves across the line of sight, the distance hardly changes, and the observed period is the true one.

</Step>

<Step title="The sum of the excesses">

Adding the excesses of all periods from A to D, the distances in between cancel in pairs and only the first and the last remain:

<Formula label="Sum of the period excesses" tex="\Delta t = \sum_n \frac{d_{n+1} - d_n}{c} = \frac{d_D - d_A}{c} = \frac{2R}{c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Here $R$ is the radius of Earth's orbit. With the Sun at distance $D_J$ from Jupiter, $d_A = D_J - R$ and $d_D = D_J + R$, so the difference is the diameter $2R$ of Earth's orbit, whatever $D_J$ is. The 22 minutes are the time light needs to cross Earth's orbit.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The answer does not depend on Io's period or on how many periods were added, as it should not: the last reappearance near D is simply seen late by the extra travel time $2R/c$. If light travelled instantly ($c \to \infty$) the excess would vanish.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Estimate the speed of light from the 22 minutes. Take the distance from Earth to the Sun to be $1.5\times 10^{11}$ m (the value MIT gives in Problem 2). Answer in m/s.

<Hint>

Speed is distance over time. The distance is the diameter of Earth's orbit, not its radius.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$c \approx$" value="2.27e8" unit="m/s" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Light crosses the diameter of Earth's orbit in 22 minutes.

<Step title="The estimate">

<Formula label="Rømer's speed of light" tex="c \approx \frac{2R}{\Delta t} = \frac{2 \times 1.5\times 10^{11}\ \text{m}}{22 \times 60\ \text{s}} = \frac{3.0\times 10^{11}\ \text{m}}{1320\ \text{s}} = 2.27\times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The modern value is $c = 2.998\times 10^8$ m/s, so this estimate is 24% too low. The orbit's size is not to blame: light really crosses $3.0\times 10^{11}$ m in 1001 s, which is 16.7 minutes, so the 22 minutes were too long. The great result was not the number but the fact that the speed of light is finite.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="How good an inertial frame is the Earth?" points={6} mit="Problem 2">

The Earth is not a perfect inertial frame, because it accelerates in several ways at once. (MIT titles the problem with Hamlet's words about "this goodly frame, the Earth".) Calculate the size of each acceleration below, in m/s². Use these values. MIT gives: the distance from Earth to the Sun is $1.5\times 10^{11}$ m; the distance from Earth to the Moon is $3.8\times 10^{8}$ m; Earth has 80 times the mass of the Moon; the Sun is 10 kpc from the centre of the Galaxy and moves around it at 300 km/s; one parsec is $3.09\times 10^{16}$ m, so one kiloparsec is $3.09\times 10^{19}$ m. MIT's sheet leaves the rest to the student. Take: Earth's radius $6.4\times 10^{6}$ m; one turn of the Earth in 24 hours; one year of $3.156\times 10^{7}$ s; one orbit of the Moon in 27.3 days.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

The acceleration of a point on the equator, from Earth's rotation about its axis.

<Hint>

A point moving on a circle of radius $r$ with angular speed $\omega$ accelerates towards the centre at $\omega^2 r$, with $\omega = 2\pi$ divided by the time of one turn.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$a_\text{rot} =$" value="0.0338" unit="m/s²" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-rot" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Uniform motion on a circle has the centripetal acceleration $\omega^2 r = u^2/r$.

<Step title="The rotation">

<Formula label="Acceleration at the equator" tex="a_\text{rot} = \omega^2 r = \left(\frac{2\pi}{86400\ \text{s}}\right)^2 \times 6.4\times 10^{6}\ \text{m} = 0.0338\ \text{m/s}^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The speed of the equator is $2\pi r$ per day, 465 m/s, and $u^2/r$ gives the same acceleration. It is 0.35% of $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$: small, but it is the largest of the four. (Earth turns once relative to the stars in 4 minutes less than 24 hours. Using that time raises the result by half a percent.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

The acceleration from Earth's orbit around the Sun.

<Hint>

The same formula, with one turn per year and the radius of the orbit.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$a_\text{orb} =$" value="5.95e-3" unit="m/s²" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-orb" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A circle again, now with the radius of the orbit and a period of one year.

<Step title="The orbit">

<Formula label="Acceleration of the orbit round the Sun" tex="a_\text{orb} = \left(\frac{2\pi}{3.156\times 10^{7}\ \text{s}}\right)^2 \times 1.5\times 10^{11}\ \text{m} = 5.95\times 10^{-3}\ \text{m/s}^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The orbital speed that goes with these numbers is $\omega r = 29.9$ km/s, the familiar 30 km/s of the lesson. The acceleration is about six times smaller than that of the rotation, although the speed is 64 times larger: the circle is so much bigger.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

The acceleration from the orbit of the solar system around the centre of the Galaxy.

<Hint>

Here you know the speed and the radius, not the period. Use $u^2/r$, with both in SI units.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$a_\text{gal} =$" value="2.91e-10" unit="m/s²" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-gal" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With speed and radius known, use $u^2/r$.

<Step title="The Galactic orbit">

The radius is $10\ \text{kpc} = 10 \times 3.09\times 10^{19}\ \text{m} = 3.09\times 10^{20}$ m and the speed is $3.0\times 10^{5}$ m/s:

<Formula label="Acceleration of the orbit round the Galaxy" tex="a_\text{gal} = \frac{u^2}{r} = \frac{(3.0\times 10^{5}\ \text{m/s})^2}{3.09\times 10^{20}\ \text{m}} = 2.91\times 10^{-10}\ \text{m/s}^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

One trip round the Galaxy takes $2\pi r/u$, about 205 million years with these numbers. So slow a turn gives a tiny acceleration, 20 million times smaller than that of the orbit round the Sun.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

The acceleration from Earth's motion around the centre of mass of Earth and Moon.

<Hint>

Earth and Moon both circle their common centre of mass once per orbit of the Moon. How far is Earth's centre from that point, if Earth is 80 times as heavy as the Moon?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The centre of mass divides the Earth–Moon distance in the ratio 1 to 80, so Earth's centre is 1/81 of the distance away from it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="$a_\text{EM} =$" value="3.33e-5" unit="m/s²" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-moon" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Earth moves on a small circle around the centre of mass, with the Moon's period.

<Step title="Earth's circle">

With masses in the ratio 80 to 1, the distances from the centre of mass are in the ratio 1 to 80. Earth's centre is at

<Formula label="Earth's distance from the centre of mass" tex="r_E = \frac{3.8\times 10^{8}\ \text{m}}{1 + 80} = 4.69\times 10^{6}\ \text{m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

from it, which is inside the Earth. One turn takes 27.3 days, which is $2.359\times 10^{6}$ s:

<Formula label="Acceleration towards the Earth–Moon centre of mass" tex="a_\text{EM} = \left(\frac{2\pi}{2.359\times 10^{6}\ \text{s}}\right)^2 \times 4.69\times 10^{6}\ \text{m} = 3.33\times 10^{-5}\ \text{m/s}^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second route uses gravity. The Moon pulls on the Earth as hard as the Earth pulls on the Moon, and the Moon's pull on Earth gives the acceleration $GM_\text{Moon}/d^2$. Since $g = GM_\text{Earth}/r^2$ at Earth's surface, this is $g\,(r/d)^2/80 = 9.8 \times (6.4\times 10^{6}/3.8\times 10^{8})^2/80 = 3.47\times 10^{-5}\ \text{m/s}^2$. The two routes agree to about 4%, which is what the rounded inputs allow.

In order of size: rotation, orbit round the Sun, the wobble about the Earth–Moon centre of mass, orbit round the Galaxy. All are far below $g$, which is why the Earth is a good inertial frame for most purposes.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The Galilean transformation in any direction" points={6} mit="Problem 3">

A textbook usually writes the Galilean transformation for a frame S′ that moves along the $x$ axis of a frame S with speed $v$: $x' = x - vt$, $y' = y$, $z' = z$, together with $t' = t$. MIT's sheet takes this problem from a textbook and describes it in one line: generalise those equations. Here is the task in full. Let S′ move with a constant velocity $\vec v = (v_x, v_y, v_z)$ in any direction relative to S. The axes of the two frames stay parallel, and the origins coincide at $t = t' = 0$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Write the transformation of the coordinates $(x, y, z, t)$ of an event to $(x', y', z', t')$. Type your $x'$ in the box.

<Hint>

Where is the origin of S′, seen from S, at time $t$? The position of an event relative to that origin is its position in S′.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-x" label="$x' =$" expr="x - vx*t"
  vars={{ x: [1, 5], vx: [0.5, 2], t: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ vx: "v_x" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x + vx*t", hint: "That sign belongs to the opposite transformation, from S′ back to S. A point at rest in S (fixed x) must drift backwards in S′." },
    { expr: "x - vx", hint: "The origin of S′ has moved a distance v_x times t along x, not v_x. Check the units." },
    { expr: "x", hint: "That is true only if the velocity has no x component. Here it points in any direction." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The origin of S′ sits at $\vec vt$ in S. Subtract it.

<Step title="The transformation">

At time $t$ the origin of S′ is at the position $\vec vt$ of S. An event at $\vec r$ in S is therefore at $\vec r - \vec vt$ relative to that origin, and time is the same for both observers. This is the transformation of the lesson:

<Formula label="Galilean transformation, any direction" tex="\vec r' = \vec r - \vec vt, \qquad t' = t" cite="reading1" at="slide 10" />

In components:

<Formula label="Galilean transformation in components" tex="x' = x - v_x t, \qquad y' = y - v_y t, \qquad z' = z - v_z t, \qquad t' = t" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $\vec v = (v, 0, 0)$ this is the textbook form $x' = x - vt$, $y' = y$, $z' = z$. An object moving in S as $x = x_0 + u_x t$ has $x' = x_0 + (u_x - v_x)t$ in S′: its velocity is $u_x - v_x$, the velocity addition of the lesson (slide 12). The origin of S′ itself ($\vec r = \vec vt$) gets $\vec r' = 0$ at all times.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Write the same transformation as one matrix equation: a $4\times 4$ matrix that turns the column $(x, y, z, t)$ into the column $(x', y', z', t')$. Type the entry in the second row and fourth column in the box.

<Hint>

Each row of the matrix is one of the four equations of part (a). The row for $y'$ must give $1 \cdot y$ plus something times $t$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-entry" label="Row 2, column 4:" expr="-vy"
  vars={{ vy: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ vy: "v_y" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "vy", hint: "The sign is wrong: y′ = y − v_y t, so the number that multiplies t is −v_y." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "Zero would mean y′ = y. That is true only if the velocity has no y component." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The transformation is linear in $x$, $y$, $z$ and $t$, so it is a matrix acting on a column of four numbers.

<Step title="The matrix">

<Formula label="Galilean transformation as a matrix" tex="\begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ z' \\ t' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & -v_x \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -v_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & -v_z \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \\ t \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The first three rows are the equations for $x'$, $y'$ and $z'$. The last row says $t' = t$: time does not mix with space at all. Space, however, does pick up a share of time, through the last column.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Multiply the matrix for a velocity $\vec v$ by the matrix for a velocity $\vec w$. The product has the same shape, with $-(\vec v + \vec w)$ in the last column: two boosts in a row are one boost with the sum of the velocities. Taking $\vec w = -\vec v$ gives the unit matrix, so the inverse transformation is the same matrix with $+\vec v$. MIT's sheet notes that the same problem comes back in special relativity, where it is a good deal harder. There the last row changes too.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Momentum conservation seen from a train" points={9} mit="Problem 4">

An observer on the ground watches two particles of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ collide. Measuring the velocities before the collision, $\vec u_1$ and $\vec u_2$, and after it, $\vec U_1$ and $\vec U_2$, the observer finds that the total momentum is the same before and after. A second observer rides on a train that moves with constant velocity $\vec v$ relative to the ground.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Use the classical addition of velocities to show that the observer on the train also finds that momentum is conserved in this collision.

<Hint>

Each velocity measured on the train is the ground velocity minus $\vec v$. Write the total momentum on the train before the collision in terms of the total momentum on the ground.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Changing frame subtracts the same amount, the total mass times $\vec v$, from the total momentum before and after.

<Step title="The total momentum on the train">

The lesson's velocity addition is $\vec u' = \vec u - \vec v$ (slide 12). Before the collision the train observer measures

<Formula label="Total momentum in the train frame" tex="\vec P' = m_1(\vec u_1 - \vec v) + m_2(\vec u_2 - \vec v) = \vec P - (m_1 + m_2)\,\vec v" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

where $\vec P = m_1\vec u_1 + m_2\vec u_2$ is the ground observer's total. After the collision the same steps give $\vec P'_\text{after} = \vec P_\text{after} - (m_1 + m_2)\,\vec v$.

</Step>

<Step title="Conservation">

The ground observer finds $\vec P_\text{after} = \vec P$. The term $(m_1 + m_2)\vec v$ is the same before and after, so $\vec P'_\text{after} = \vec P'$: momentum is conserved on the train as well.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two observers do not agree on the value of the momentum. They differ by $(m_1 + m_2)\vec v$, the lesson's $\vec p' = \vec p + m\vec v$ with its opposite sign convention (slide 15). They agree on the law.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Now suppose that mass passes from one particle to the other during the collision. The masses are $m_1$ and $m_2$ before and $M_1$ and $M_2$ after. (MIT's sheet writes the final masses with primes. Here a prime always means the train frame, so the final masses and velocities get capital letters.) The ground observer again finds that momentum is conserved. Show that the train observer finds momentum conserved only if mass is conserved too, that is, if $M_1 + M_2 = m_1 + m_2$.

For the box, let everything move along one line, with the train's velocity $v$ along that line. By how much does the total momentum measured on the train change in the collision (after minus before)? Write `M1`, `M2` for the final masses.

<Hint>

Repeat part (a), but with the masses $M_1$, $M_2$ in the momentum after the collision. The term with $\vec v$ is no longer the same before and after.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-dp" label="$P'_\text{after} - P'_\text{before} =$" expr="-(M1 + M2 - m1 - m2)*v"
  vars={{ m1: [1, 2], m2: [1, 2], M1: [3, 4], M2: [3, 4], v: [5, 20] }}
  names={{ m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2", M1: "M_1", M2: "M_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(M1 + M2 - m1 - m2)*v", hint: "The sign is wrong. The train observer subtracts v from every velocity, so extra mass after the collision lowers the momentum along v." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "That holds only if the total mass is unchanged. Keep the final masses different from the initial ones." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The frame change subtracts the total mass times $\vec v$. If the total mass changes, that term no longer cancels.

<Step title="Before and after on the train">

<Formula label="Train-frame momentum with mass transfer" tex="\vec P'_\text{before} = \vec P_\text{before} - (m_1 + m_2)\,\vec v, \qquad \vec P'_\text{after} = M_1(\vec U_1 - \vec v) + M_2(\vec U_2 - \vec v) = \vec P_\text{after} - (M_1 + M_2)\,\vec v" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The change">

The ground observer finds $\vec P_\text{after} = \vec P_\text{before}$, so subtracting leaves

<Formula label="Change of momentum in the train frame" tex="\vec P'_\text{after} - \vec P'_\text{before} = -\left(M_1 + M_2 - m_1 - m_2\right)\vec v" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The train moves, so $\vec v \neq 0$, and the right-hand side vanishes only if $M_1 + M_2 = m_1 + m_2$. Momentum is conserved in both frames only if the total mass is conserved.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units are mass times velocity. With no transfer of mass the result is zero, which is part (a). A transfer that keeps the total (say $M_1 = m_1 - \mu$ and $M_2 = m_2 + \mu$) also gives zero. Read the other way: if momentum conservation is to be a law for every inertial observer, Galilean relativity forces the conservation of mass.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="An elastic collision is elastic in every frame" points={9} mit="Problem 5">

A collision of two particles is called elastic if kinetic energy is conserved in it. As MIT's sheet allows, let all velocities lie along the $x$ axis. Two particles of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ collide, and the masses do not change. In a frame S the total kinetic energy is $K$ and the total momentum is $P$. A frame S′ moves with velocity $v$ along $x$ relative to S.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Use the Galilean velocity transformation to express the total kinetic energy $K'$ measured in S′ through $K$, $P$, the total mass $M = m_1 + m_2$ and $v$ (the box). Then show that a collision that is elastic in S is elastic in every other inertial frame.

<Hint>

Write $K' = \tfrac12 m_1(u_1 - v)^2 + \tfrac12 m_2(u_2 - v)^2$ and multiply out the squares. Collect the terms without $v$, with $v$ and with $v^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use your formula once before and once after the collision, and subtract. What does every collision conserve, elastic or not?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-k" label="$K' =$" expr="K - v*P + M*v^2/2"
  vars={{ K: [30, 50], P: [1, 4], M: [1, 4], v: [1, 5] }}
  names={{ K: "K", P: "P", M: "M" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "K + v*P + M*v^2/2", hint: "The sign of the middle term is wrong. In S′ every velocity is u − v, and the cross term of (u − v)² is −2uv." },
    { expr: "K - M*v^2/2", hint: "Kinetic energies do not simply subtract. Multiply out (u − v)²: there is a cross term with the momentum, and the v² term comes with a plus sign." },
    { expr: "K + M*v^2/2", hint: "The cross term of (u − v)² is missing. It is −v times the total momentum." },
    { expr: "K", hint: "Kinetic energy is not invariant: the speeds differ between the frames. Use u′ = u − v." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The kinetic energy in S′ differs from that in S by terms that contain only the total momentum and the total mass, and a collision changes neither.

<Step title="Kinetic energy in the moving frame">

With $u_i' = u_i - v$ (slide 12 of the lesson):

<Formula label="Kinetic energy in the moving frame" tex="K' = \tfrac12 m_1(u_1 - v)^2 + \tfrac12 m_2(u_2 - v)^2 = K - vP + \tfrac12 Mv^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

with $K = \tfrac12 m_1u_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2u_2^2$, $P = m_1u_1 + m_2u_2$ and $M = m_1 + m_2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Before and after">

The same formula holds after the collision, with the values after it. Subtracting:

<Formula label="Change of kinetic energy in the two frames" tex="K'_\text{after} - K'_\text{before} = \left(K_\text{after} - K_\text{before}\right) - v\left(P_\text{after} - P_\text{before}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The term $\tfrac12 Mv^2$ has dropped out because the masses do not change. The particles push only on each other during the collision, so momentum is conserved and the last bracket is zero. Hence the change of kinetic energy is the same in both frames. If it is zero in S, it is zero in S′, for every $v$: an elastic collision is elastic in all inertial frames.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $vP$ and $Mv^2$ are both a mass times a velocity squared, an energy. For $v = 0$ the formula gives $K' = K$. In the frame where the total momentum vanishes, $K' = K - vP + \tfrac12Mv^2$ with $v = P/M$ gives $K' = K - P^2/(2M)$: the kinetic energy is smallest in that frame.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Could this result have been predicted from the principle of conservation of energy?

<Hint>

When kinetic energy is lost in a collision, where does it go? Would two observers disagree about how warm or how dented the particles are afterwards?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Yes, if energy conservation is taken to hold in every inertial frame, as a law of physics should.

<Step title="The argument">

The total energy is the kinetic energy of the particles plus their internal energy: heat, deformation, vibration. Conservation of energy says that kinetic energy lost in a collision turns up as internal energy. The internal state of a particle (its temperature, its dents) is the same for every observer, because it has to do with motions inside the particle relative to its own centre, and relative velocities are invariant. So every observer must find the same loss of kinetic energy. In an elastic collision that loss is zero, and then it is zero for everyone.

</Step>

<Step title="Check against part (a)">

Part (a) showed exactly this without mentioning internal energy: the change of kinetic energy is the same in all frames, whether it is zero or not. The calculation needed the conservation of momentum, and the energy argument assumes that energy is conserved in every frame. The two conservation laws have to hold together for the principle of relativity to work, just as momentum and mass did in Problem 4. Part (c) tries it with numbers.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

(Our own addition, not on MIT's sheet.) A cart of 2 kg moving at 3 m/s hits a cart of 1 kg at rest, and the two stick together. An observer on a train moving at 5 m/s in the same direction watches. How much kinetic energy is lost in the collision according to the train observer, in joules?

<Hint>

Find the common final velocity from momentum conservation on the ground. Then subtract 5 m/s from every velocity and compute the kinetic energy before and after.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-loss" label="Loss of kinetic energy on the train:" value="3" unit="J" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-loss-train" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The loss is the same in both frames, although the kinetic energies themselves are very different.

<Step title="On the ground">

Momentum: $2 \times 3 = (2 + 1)\,U$, so the carts move on at $U = 2$ m/s. Kinetic energy before: $\tfrac12 \times 2 \times 3^2 = 9$ J. After: $\tfrac12 \times 3 \times 2^2 = 6$ J. The loss is 3 J.

</Step>

<Step title="On the train">

The velocities are $3 - 5 = -2$ m/s and $0 - 5 = -5$ m/s before, and $2 - 5 = -3$ m/s after. Kinetic energy before: $\tfrac12 \times 2 \times 2^2 + \tfrac12 \times 1 \times 5^2 = 16.5$ J. After: $\tfrac12 \times 3 \times 3^2 = 13.5$ J. The loss is again 3 J.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The formula of part (a) gives the same: before, $K' = 9 - 5 \times 6 + \tfrac12 \times 3 \times 5^2 = 16.5$ J. The collision is not elastic, and it is not elastic in either frame.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="The work-energy theorem in two frames" points={6} mit="Problem 6">

Observer G stands on the ground. Observer T rides on a train that moves with constant velocity $v$ relative to the ground. A particle of mass $m$ is at first at rest relative to the train. Then a constant force $F$ acts on it in the forward direction (the direction of $v$) for a time $t$. Both observers watch.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Show that the two observers find for the work done on the particle by the force

<Formula label="Work in the two frames" tex="W_T = \tfrac12 ma^2t^2, \qquad W_G = \tfrac12 ma^2t^2 + mvat" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 2" />

where $a$ is the acceleration of the particle, the same for both observers.

<Hint>

Work is force times the distance moved in the direction of the force. The force and the acceleration are the same in both frames. The distance is not.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For T the particle starts from rest. For G it starts with the velocity $v$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both observers agree on the force and on the acceleration, but G sees the particle cover more ground while the force acts.

<Step title="What the two observers agree on">

Acceleration, mass and force are invariant under a Galilean transformation (slides 12 and 13 of the lesson), so both observers find $F = ma$ with the same $a = F/m$.

</Step>

<Step title="Observer T">

For T the particle starts at rest and moves $\tfrac12at^2$ in the time $t$:

<Formula label="Work found by the train observer" tex="W_T = F \times \tfrac12at^2 = ma \times \tfrac12at^2 = \tfrac12ma^2t^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Observer G">

For G the particle starts with velocity $v$ and moves $vt + \tfrac12at^2$:

<Formula label="Work found by the ground observer" tex="W_G = ma\left(vt + \tfrac12at^2\right) = \tfrac12ma^2t^2 + mvat" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both expressions are a mass times a velocity squared ($at$ and $v$ are velocities). The extra work $mvat = F \times vt$ is the force times the distance the train itself moves in the time $t$. For $v = 0$ the two observers are the same and so are the results.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Show that the changes of kinetic energy that the two observers calculate, $\Delta K_T$ and $\Delta K_G$, are given by the same two expressions. The work-energy theorem $W = \Delta K$ then holds in both frames.

<Hint>

After the time $t$ the particle has gained the velocity $at$ in both frames. Add it to the starting velocity of each frame and take the difference of $\tfrac12m(\text{velocity})^2$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The gain of velocity is the same in both frames, but kinetic energy goes with the square of the velocity, so the gain of kinetic energy is not.

<Step title="Observer T">

The velocity goes from 0 to $at$:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy gained in the train frame" tex="\Delta K_T = \tfrac12m(at)^2 - 0 = \tfrac12ma^2t^2 = W_T" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Observer G">

The velocity goes from $v$ to $v + at$:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy gained in the ground frame" tex="\Delta K_G = \tfrac12m(v + at)^2 - \tfrac12mv^2 = mvat + \tfrac12ma^2t^2 = W_G" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Work and kinetic energy both depend on the frame, by the same amount $mvat$, so $W = \Delta K$ holds for both observers. That had to be so: the lesson derives the theorem from $F = ma$ alone (slide 14), and $F = ma$ is invariant. The lesson's worked example is this problem with numbers: 25 J on the train, 175 J on the ground, with $mvat = 0.50 \times 30 \times 10 = 150$ J between them.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="Initial conditions depend on the frame" points={6} mit="Problem 7">

A person stands on a train that moves with speed $v$ relative to the ground and lets go of a ball, from rest, at a height $h$ above the floor. On the train the ball falls straight down and reaches the floor after the time $t = \sqrt{2h/g}$, where $g$ is the acceleration of gravity. One side of the train is made of glass, so an observer standing on the ground can watch.

For the ground observer take $x$ along the track in the direction of the train's motion and $y$ upwards from the floor of the train, with the ball released at $x = 0$, $y = h$ at $t = 0$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Sketch the path of the ball as the ground observer sees it. Give its equation $y(x)$, and the distance $x_\text{floor}$ the ball travels along the track before it reaches the floor (the equation and the distance are our addition: MIT asks for the sketch).

<Hint>

At the moment of release the ball shares the motion of the train. What is its velocity relative to the ground at $t = 0$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ for the ground observer, then remove $t$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-path" label="$y(x) =$" expr="h - g*x^2/(2*v^2)"
  vars={{ h: [1, 2], g: [9, 10], v: [5, 20], x: [0.2, 2] }}
  names={{ h: "h", g: "g" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "h - g*x^2/2", hint: "That is the height as a function of time with x in the place of t. The ball needs the time t = x/v to get to x." },
    { expr: "h - g*x^2/v^2", hint: "The factor one half of the fall distance, g t²/2, got lost." },
    { expr: "h - g*x/(2*v)", hint: "The fall distance grows with the square of the time, and the time is x/v. Check the units: g x / v is a velocity, not a length." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p7-range" label="$x_\text{floor} =$" expr="v*sqrt(2*h/g)"
  vars={{ h: [1, 2], g: [9, 10], v: [5, 20] }}
  names={{ h: "h", g: "g" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2*h/g)", hint: "That is the time of the fall. The ball moves along the track at the speed v during that time." },
    { expr: "v*sqrt(h/g)", hint: "The fall time comes from h = g t²/2, so t² = 2h/g. The factor 2 is missing." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For the ground observer the ball starts with the train's velocity, so it is a projectile thrown horizontally.

<Step title="The motion seen from the ground">

On the train the ball has $x' = 0$ and $y' = h - \tfrac12gt^2$. The Galilean transformation from the train to the ground, $x = x' + vt$, $y = y'$, gives

<Formula label="The ball seen from the ground" tex="x = vt, \qquad y = h - \tfrac12gt^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y = h - \frac{g\,x^2}{2v^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The path is a parabola that starts horizontally at height $h$ and bends down, as the lesson says of the dropped basketball (slide 15).

</Step>

<Step title="Where it lands">

The ball reaches the floor ($y = 0$) at the same time for both observers, $t = \sqrt{2h/g}$, because time is invariant. By then it has moved along the track by

<Formula label="Distance along the track" tex="x_\text{floor} = v\sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The train has moved exactly as far, so the ball lands at the person's feet, as it must.

</Step>

<Step title="A picture with numbers">

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 7]} range={[0, 2.2]} xLabel="x (m)" yLabel="y (m)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "(2 - 9.8*x^2/200) >= 0 ? (2 - 9.8*x^2/200) : NaN" }
  ]}
  caption="The path of the ball for the ground observer, drawn from y = h − g x²/(2v²) with our own example values: a drop of 2 m on a train moving at 10 m/s, with g = 9.8 m/s². The ball is in the air for 0.639 s and reaches the floor 6.39 m along the track. On the train the same fall is a vertical line 2 m long." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $g x^2/v^2$ is $(\text{m/s}^2)(\text{m}^2)/(\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2)$, a length. For a very fast train the parabola is very flat. For $v \to 0$ the ball does not move along the track at all, and the path becomes the vertical line of the train frame.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

A straight line in one frame and a parabola in the other. How do you reconcile these two paths, with numbers and formulas, with the fact that Newton's second law $\vec F = m\vec a$ is supposed to work equally well in both inertial frames?

<Hint>

Compare, for the two observers: the force on the ball, its acceleration, its position at $t = 0$ and its velocity at $t = 0$. Which of the four differ?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A law of motion fixes the acceleration. The path also needs the starting position and the starting velocity, and the starting velocity depends on the frame.

<Step title="The same law">

Both observers see one force on the ball, its weight $mg$ downwards. Both find the same acceleration: differentiating twice,

<Formula label="The acceleration in the two frames" tex="\text{train: } \ddot x' = 0,\ \ \ddot y' = -g; \qquad \text{ground: } \ddot x = \frac{d^2}{dt^2}(vt) = 0,\ \ \ddot y = -g" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So $\vec F = m\vec a$ reads $(0, -mg) = m\,(0, -g)$ for both. The law holds in the same form in both frames, as the lesson states (slide 13).

</Step>

<Step title="Different initial conditions">

The law is a second-order differential equation, so its solution needs two starting values per direction. The starting position is the same in both frames here, $(0, h)$. The starting velocity is not:

<Formula label="Initial velocity in the two frames" tex="\text{train: } (\dot x', \dot y')_{t=0} = (0, 0); \qquad \text{ground: } (\dot x, \dot y)_{t=0} = (v, 0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The same equation with the starting velocity $(0, 0)$ gives the straight drop, and with $(v, 0)$ it gives the parabola of part (a). The difference between the two paths, $x - x' = vt$, is exactly the Galilean transformation.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson puts it in one line: the physical law is invariant, the initial conditions are not (slide 13). With the numbers of the picture in part (a): both observers time the fall at 0.639 s and measure the acceleration $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ downwards; they disagree only on the horizontal velocity, 0 against 10 m/s, and so on the shape of the path.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem A (optional)

<Exercise id="pa" title="The speed of light does not depend on frequency" points={3} mit="Problem A" optional>

A pulsar is an astronomical object that sends out sharp, regular pulses. For one pulsar, at a distance of 6000 light-years from Earth, the pulses in X-rays, in visible light and in radio waves are all observed to arrive at the same moment, to within 200 microseconds. (The radio pulses have first been corrected for the delay they pick up in the thin plasma between the stars.) Use this to estimate how much the speed of electromagnetic radiation in vacuum could vary with frequency over this wide range. Give your answer as an upper limit on the fractional difference in speed, $\Delta c/c$. Take one year to be $3.156\times 10^{7}$ s.

<Hint>

All three pulses leave together and cover the same distance $d$. A pulse with speed $c$ takes the time $t = d/c$. How much does $t$ change if the speed changes by a small $\Delta c$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For small changes $\Delta t/t = \Delta c/c$ in size. The travel time $t$ is 6000 years, by the meaning of a light-year.

</Hint>

<Answer id="pa-limit" label="$\Delta c/c \lesssim$" value="1.06e-15" tolerance="2%" calc="pa-limit" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A small difference in speed, kept up over a very long trip, becomes a difference in arrival time. No such difference is seen, so the speeds are equal to within the ratio of the timing error to the travel time.

<Step title="Speed and travel time">

From $t = d/c$, a small change of speed $\Delta c$ changes the travel time by $\Delta t = -(d/c^2)\,\Delta c$. In size:

<Formula label="Fractional difference in speed" tex="\frac{|\Delta c|}{c} = \frac{|\Delta t|}{t}" cite="reading2" at="slide 13 (the same argument for gamma-ray bursts)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

Light needs 6000 years for 6000 light-years, so $t = 6000 \times 3.156\times 10^{7}\ \text{s} = 1.894\times 10^{11}$ s. The pulses arrive together to within $\Delta t = 2.0\times 10^{-4}$ s:

<Formula label="The pulsar's limit" tex="\frac{\Delta c}{c} \lesssim \frac{2.0\times 10^{-4}\ \text{s}}{1.894\times 10^{11}\ \text{s}} = 1.06\times 10^{-15}" cite="mit" at="Problem A, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is a ratio of two times, a pure number. In speed it is $1.06\times 10^{-15} \times 3.0\times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s} \approx 3\times 10^{-7}$ m/s, a third of a micrometre per second. The lesson's gamma-ray bursts give a limit of the same size, about $10^{-15}$ (slide 13): they are about half a million times farther away, but their arrival is known half a million times less sharply.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>
