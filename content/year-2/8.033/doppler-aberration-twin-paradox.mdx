Lecture 7 wraps up relativistic kinematics: the Doppler effect and aberration, now with intuition and applications, the question of travel faster than light, and the twin paradox (slides 1–4). (MIT's material for this lecture is a set of slides, several of them pictures and plots; this page follows their order and gives the reasoning in words, marking what is not on the slides.)

## The Doppler effect

From the [last lecture](/courses/8-033/four-vectors-doppler-aberration), for light travelling at angle $\theta$ to the direction of a boost (slide 5):

<Formula label="Aberration and Doppler shift" tex="\phi' = \phi, \qquad \cos\theta' = \frac{\cos\theta - \beta}{1 - \beta\cos\theta}, \qquad k' = k\gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="slide 5" />

**Einstein against the classical Doppler effects** (slide 9). Slide 9 plots the Doppler shift $\omega'/\omega$ along the line of sight against $\beta$ for three formulas. Classically it matters who moves through the medium: a moving observer gives $1 - \beta$ (a straight line), a moving source $1/(1 + \beta)$. Relativity has only relative motion and gives a curve between the two (the formulas below are the page's own reading of the three curves):

<Formula label="Three Doppler factors" tex="\left(\frac{\omega'}{\omega}\right)_{\text{moving observer}} = 1 - \beta, \qquad \left(\frac{\omega'}{\omega}\right)_{\text{moving source}} = \frac{1}{1 + \beta}, \qquad \left(\frac{\omega'}{\omega}\right)_{\text{Einstein}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}}" cite="mit" at="slide 9 (read from the plot)" />

The relativistic factor is exactly the geometric mean of the two classical ones, so for small $\beta$ all three agree to first order. The slides list three ways to understand the Doppler effect: in terms of momentum, energy, or pulses (slide 10). In the pulse picture, for example, a source emitting a pulse every $T$ in its own frame emits them every $\gamma T$ in ours (time dilation), while moving away at $\beta$ adds $\beta c\gamma T$ to the distance each pulse travels, so they arrive every $\gamma(1 + \beta)T$, which gives the same factor (this derivation is the page's own). An interactive applet illustrated the effect in class (slide 8).

## Aberration

Slide 13 plots $\theta'$ against $\theta$ for a range of speeds. For small $\beta$ the curves hug the diagonal; for $\beta$ near 1, almost every direction in one frame is squeezed towards $\theta' = 0$ or $180°$ in the other. Seen from a fast-moving body, light and particles crowd into the forward direction. Applications on the slides (slides 14–16):

- particle tracks in the first CERN liquid hydrogen bubble chamber (the image is withheld on OCW for copyright reasons), where fast particles fly out in a narrow forward cone;
- jets from quasars, imaged in X-rays by Chandra and in radio by the VLA (quasar 3C175).

(Not on the slides: jets moving close to $c$ nearly towards us can even seem to cross the sky faster than light, at an apparent speed $\beta\sin\theta/(1 - \beta\cos\theta)$ in units of $c$; the light from later positions has less far to travel.)

## Faster than light?

Can anything go faster than light (slide 18)? Accelerating a body will not do it: the velocity-addition formula never takes a speed past $c$. A new kind of signal might, in principle, so suppose one existed and linked two events A and B with a spacelike separation. For such a pair the time order is not absolute: some inertial frame sees B first (the slide leaves the proof to a problem set; a Minkowski diagram shows it). Since the laws are the same in every inertial frame, B could answer with a signal of the same kind that reaches A before A sent anything, and so stop A from sending it. The paradox shows that $c$ limits every signal, not only light (slide 18).

## The twin paradox

One twin stays home; the other flies off fast and returns (slide 19). Each sees the other's clock running slow on the way, so who is younger at the reunion? The situations are not symmetric: the twin at home stays in one inertial frame, while the traveller changes frame when she turns round. Between the two meetings her world line has less proper time, and she is younger (this explanation is the page's own; the slide only poses the question). The slide's question, "is it right?", is answered by experiment (slides 20–21).

**Time dilation in practice** (slide 21): gamma-ray bursts and supernovae (whose light curves at large distances are stretched), clocks flown on aircraft, and GPS. GPS uses a constellation of 24 "NAVSTAR" satellites, which the slides put 11,000 miles above the Earth (slide 22; that is about 20,000 km, so nautical miles). A receiver finds its position from the travel times of signals from several satellites (slide 23). The slide pictures each satellite as fixing a circle of possible positions and a fourth as giving the elevation; in three dimensions each signal places the receiver on a sphere, three spheres locate it, and in practice the fourth is needed to correct the receiver's own clock. Since a timing error of a nanosecond is an error of 30 cm, relativity matters (the numbers in the worked example below are not on the slides).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Clocks across the universe">
The slides list gamma-ray bursts and supernovae first among the tests of time dilation (slide 21). One way to read that, the page's own: the light curve of a supernova billions of light years away lasts longer than that of a nearby one, by the factor $1 + z$. In an [expanding universe](/courses/8-286/cosmological-redshift) the same stretching that reddens the light slows every process we watch there, and observations of distant supernovae confirm it.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Twins and satellites of your own">
<Problem>
(a) A twin travels to a star 4.2 light years away at $0.6c$ and straight back. How much time passes for each twin? (b) A GPS satellite orbits at $3.87$ km/s. How much does special-relativistic time dilation alone slow its clock per day? (c) Gravity makes it run fast by about $45.7$ μs per day. What is the net effect?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The twins">
At home: $2 \times 4.2/0.6 = 14.0$ years. With $\gamma = 1.25$, the traveller ages $14.0/1.25 = 11.2$ years: 2.8 years younger.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The satellite's speed">
$\frac12(v/c)^2 = \frac12(3.87 \times 10^3/3.00 \times 10^8)^2 \approx 8.3 \times 10^{-11}$; times 86,400 s per day, about 7.2 μs per day slow.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Net">
$45.7 - 7.2 \approx 38.5$ μs per day fast: about 11.5 km of position error per day if uncorrected (at 30 cm per nanosecond). The gravitational part belongs to general relativity, later in the course.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Along the line of sight the relativistic Doppler factor $\sqrt{(1 - \beta)/(1 + \beta)}$ lies between the classical moving-observer and moving-source factors, their geometric mean.
- Aberration crowds directions forward for fast observers and sources: bubble-chamber tracks, quasar jets.
- Nothing travels faster than light; otherwise causes could follow their effects in some frame.
- The travelling twin, who changes frames, ages less; moving clocks run slow in GPS satellites and in distant supernovae.
