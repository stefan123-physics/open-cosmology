## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance of the box (0.1% to 1%), so keep at least four significant figures and type the number in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `sqrt(2*M/r)` or `(1 - 2*M/r)^(3/2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them.

The points per problem are MIT's, and so is the split between parts where MIT gives one (Problems 1, 5, 9 and 12). Every other split is ours. MIT gives no points for the optional problems: the points of Problems A to H are ours and do not count towards the score. Parts that ask you to show or explain something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Try before you open a solution: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

Notation and numbers, as in the lessons:

- Unless a problem says otherwise, $c = G = 1$, so masses, lengths and times share one unit. The Schwarzschild radius is then $2M$; in ordinary units it is $r_s = 2GM/c^2$.
- $\beta_r = \sqrt{2M/r}$ is the escape speed at $r$ and $\gamma_r = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta_r^2}$.
- The "bookkeeper" coordinates are the $r$ and $t$ of the Schwarzschild metric: $t$ is the time of a clock at rest far away. A "shell observer" stands still at a fixed $r$.
- For numbers use $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², $c = 2.998 \times 10^8$ m/s and, as on MIT's sheet, a solar mass of $M_\odot = 2 \times 10^{30}$ kg.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Concept questions" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

<Part label="1" points={2}>

According to the no-hair theorem, which three physical quantities describe a black hole completely?

<Hint>

The lesson on the Schwarzschild metric lists the three properties that can be measured from outside.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-hair" choices={["Mass, radius and temperature", "Mass, angular momentum and electric charge", "Mass, density and angular momentum", "Mass, electric charge and the number of particles that fell in"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A black hole keeps no record of what it was made from. From outside only three numbers can be measured.

<Step title="The three quantities">

Mass, angular momentum and charge (Lecture 23 slides, slides 12 and 13). The radius is not a separate quantity: for a hole without spin or charge it follows from the mass, $r = 2M$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The Schwarzschild metric of this set contains only $M$. It is the special case with no angular momentum and no charge.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="2" points={3}>

Most astrophysicists are now convinced that black holes exist. Give one piece of evidence for supermassive black holes and one piece of evidence for black holes with the mass of a star. (MIT's hint points to a handout that this site does not use. The lesson on the evidence for black holes has what you need.)

<Hint>

For each kind, look for an observation that shows a large mass and an observation that shows that the mass sits in a very small region.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A good piece of evidence shows a lot of mass in a space too small for anything else, or an object that behaves as if it had no surface. Mark yourself right if you have one such observation for each kind.

<Step title="Supermassive black holes">

The lecture's list (Lecture 24 slides, slide 11), any one of which will do:

- quasars and huge jets, such as the jet of the galaxy M87;
- the motions of stars near the centres of galaxies, which need central masses of $10^6$ to $10^9$ solar masses;
- orbiting gas disks, which show that such a mass sits within less than 0.4 light years, too small a space for a cluster of stars;
- a star that was torn apart when it came too close, which limits the size of the central object further;
- X-ray spectra of disks that reach in to between $6M$ and $20M$.

</Step>

<Step title="Black holes with the mass of a star">

The lecture's list (Lecture 24 slides, slide 30), again any one:

- a star that orbits a massive companion that cannot be seen;
- such a companion with more than about 3 solar masses, the largest mass a neutron star can have. The lecture's best example is V404 Cygni (the 2006 estimate was $12 \pm 2$ solar masses); an older one is Cygnus X-1;
- X-ray flickering so fast that the source must be very small;
- no sign of a surface: gas that falls onto a neutron star flares when it lands, gas that falls into a black hole just disappears.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Later developments, after this 2006 course, made the case stronger for both kinds, as the lesson on [the evidence for general relativity](/courses/8-033/lensing-evidence-for-gr) notes: in 2015 LIGO detected gravitational waves from two merging black holes, and in 2019 and 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope imaged the shadows of two supermassive black holes. These count as good answers today, but they were not available to MIT's students.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="3" points={10}>

Through a miscalculation, your friend falls into the supermassive black hole at the centre of our Galaxy. Assume that this black hole has no charge and does not rotate. Say of each statement whether it is true or false. Read "kills him" as the direct cause of death. (One point each.)

(a) He dies just as he passes the event horizon.

<Answer id="p1-a" label="(a)" choices={["True", "False"]} answer={1} />

(b) He dies only after he has passed the event horizon.

<Answer id="p1-b" label="(b)" choices={["True", "False"]} answer={0} />

(c) Tidal forces kill him.

<Answer id="p1-c" label="(c)" choices={["True", "False"]} answer={0} />

(d) The singularity kills him.

<Answer id="p1-d" label="(d)" choices={["True", "False"]} answer={1} />

(e) He comes out unharmed from a white hole in another universe.

<Answer id="p1-e" label="(e)" choices={["True", "False"]} answer={1} />

(f) You see him disappear through the event horizon.

<Answer id="p1-f" label="(f)" choices={["True", "False"]} answer={1} />

(g) You see him for ever, as if frozen on the event horizon.

<Answer id="p1-g" label="(g)" choices={["True", "False"]} answer={1} />

(h) You see him as if frozen on the event horizon, until his light is redshifted out of sight.

<Answer id="p1-h" label="(h)" choices={["True", "False"]} answer={0} />

(i) Most physicists now think that this black hole will last for ever, because nothing can come out of it.

<Answer id="p1-i" label="(i)" choices={["True", "False"]} answer={1} />

(j) Inside the event horizon the ideas of space and time as we know them no longer hold (that is, general relativity can no longer describe the region just inside the horizon).

<Answer id="p1-j" label="(j)" choices={["True", "False"]} answer={1} />

<Hint>

For (a) to (d): the worked example of the lesson on the Schwarzschild metric compares the tidal stretching at the horizon of a small and of a very large black hole. Which one is this?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For (f) to (h): think of the light he sends out as he nears the horizon, in the river model. How long does it take to reach you, and what happens to its wavelength?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For (i) and (j): the river model says where something singular happens and where not. The lecture's review song names what Hawking found.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two views of the same fall. For the faller the horizon is nothing special, and the trouble grows towards the centre. For the watcher far away, the light from the faller arrives later and later and redder and redder.

<Step title="What happens to him: (a) to (e)">

(a) False and (b) true. In the river model nothing singular happens at the horizon (Lecture 23 slides, slide 24). The tidal stretching there falls with the square of the mass of the hole. For a hole of 4 million solar masses the lesson's worked example finds about $1.3 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s² across a 2 m body: far too little to feel. He passes the horizon alive.

(c) True and (d) false. The tidal stretching goes as $M/r^3$ (Problem 3) and grows without limit as $r \to 0$. It pulls him apart at some radius larger than zero, so the tides kill him before he reaches the singularity. (The two statements ask which of the two does the killing. If you argued that the singularity is the cause of the tides, you understood the physics too.)

(e) False. The full Schwarzschild solution, drawn in Kruskal's coordinates, has "another side" (Lecture 26 slides, slides 40 and 41). But no path at or below the speed of light leads there: every path inside the horizon ends on the singularity.

</Step>

<Step title="What you see: (f) to (h)">

(f) False, (g) false, (h) true. Light that he sends outward just before the horizon is moving against a river that flows at almost the speed of light, so it takes longer and longer to get out, and light from the horizon itself never arrives. You never see him cross: his image slows down and seems to freeze just outside the horizon. But that image does not last for ever. The light is redshifted more and more and the photons come more and more rarely, so he fades from sight.

</Step>

<Step title="The black hole itself: (i) and (j)">

(i) False. Hawking showed that black holes radiate and slowly evaporate (the review song on Lecture 24 slides, slide 24, ends with it). For a hole this heavy that takes an enormously long time, but not for ever.

(j) False. Nothing special happens to space and time at the horizon or just inside it: the Gullstrand-Painlevé metric of Problem 2 is perfectly regular there, and general relativity describes the inside. Our ideas of space and time break down at the singularity, not at the horizon. (Inside the horizon it is true that going inward is as unavoidable as going forward in time. If you answered "true" for that reason, note the difference: that is a new feature of the geometry, not a failure of the theory.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The answers hang together. (a) and (b) are opposites, and so are (c) and (d). Of (f), (g) and (h) only one can be true. Problem 9 gives numbers for the two views: the fall from the horizon to the centre takes a finite time on his watch, and an infinite bookkeeper time passes before he reaches the horizon.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Two metrics for one spacetime" points={9} mit="Problem 2">

The Gullstrand-Painlevé (GP) metric is the metric of the river model of the lesson. Its time coordinate $t_{\text{ff}}$ is the time on the clocks of observers who fall freely from rest far away:

<Formula label="Gullstrand-Painlevé metric" tex="d\tau^2 = dt_{\text{ff}}^2 - \left(dr + \beta_r\,dt_{\text{ff}}\right)^2 - r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

The standard Schwarzschild metric is

<Formula label="Schwarzschild metric" tex="d\tau^2 = \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)dt^2 - \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2 - r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

Here $\beta_r = \sqrt{2M/r}$ is the escape speed. Show in three steps that the two metrics describe the same spacetime.

<Part label="1" points={3}>

Show that the Schwarzschild metric can be written with $\gamma_r = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta_r^2}$ as

<Formula label="Schwarzschild metric with the escape speed" tex="d\tau^2 = \gamma_r^{-2}\,dt^2 - \gamma_r^2\,dr^2 - r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, part 1, p. 2" />

<Hint>

Write $1 - 2M/r$ with $\beta_r$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The factor $1 - 2M/r$ is $1 - \beta_r^2$, and that is $1/\gamma_r^2$.

<Step title="Rewrite the two factors">

$\beta_r^2 = 2M/r$, so $1 - 2M/r = 1 - \beta_r^2 = \gamma_r^{-2}$ and $(1 - 2M/r)^{-1} = \gamma_r^2$. Putting these in the Schwarzschild metric gives the form asked for. The angular part does not change.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Far away $\beta_r \to 0$ and $\gamma_r \to 1$: the metric becomes the Minkowski metric in spherical coordinates. At $r = 2M$, $\beta_r = 1$ and $\gamma_r$ is infinite: the horizon.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="2" points={3}>

The free-fall time is defined by

<Formula label="Free-fall time" tex="dt_{\text{ff}} = dt + \beta_r\gamma_r^2\,dr" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, part 2, p. 2" />

Show that then

<Formula label="The combination in the GP metric" tex="dr + \beta_r\,dt_{\text{ff}} = \beta_r\,dt + \gamma_r^2\,dr" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, part 2, p. 2" />

<Hint>

Multiply the definition by $\beta_r$ and add $dr$. You will need a relation between $\beta_r^2\gamma_r^2$ and $\gamma_r^2$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Substitute, and use the identity $1 + \beta_r^2\gamma_r^2 = \gamma_r^2$, which holds for any speed and its Lorentz factor.

<Step title="Substitute">

<Formula label="Working out the combination" tex="dr + \beta_r\,dt_{\text{ff}} = dr + \beta_r\,dt + \beta_r^2\gamma_r^2\,dr = \beta_r\,dt + \left(1 + \beta_r^2\gamma_r^2\right)dr = \beta_r\,dt + \gamma_r^2\,dr" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, part 2 (our own solution)" />

The last step uses $1 + \beta_r^2\gamma_r^2 = (1 - \beta_r^2 + \beta_r^2)/(1 - \beta_r^2) = \gamma_r^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Far away $\beta_r \to 0$: both sides become $dr$, and $t_{\text{ff}}$ becomes $t$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="3" points={3}>

Put the two relations of part 2 into the GP metric and show that the Schwarzschild metric of part 1 comes out.

<Hint>

Square both relations. The two terms with $dt\,dr$ must cancel.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The GP metric is a difference of two squares. Work out both and collect the terms with $dt^2$, $dt\,dr$ and $dr^2$.

<Step title="The two squares">

<Formula label="The two squares" tex="dt_{\text{ff}}^2 = dt^2 + 2\beta_r\gamma_r^2\,dt\,dr + \beta_r^2\gamma_r^4\,dr^2, \qquad \left(dr + \beta_r\,dt_{\text{ff}}\right)^2 = \beta_r^2\,dt^2 + 2\beta_r\gamma_r^2\,dt\,dr + \gamma_r^4\,dr^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, part 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Subtract">

The cross terms are equal and cancel. What is left is

<Formula label="The difference" tex="dt_{\text{ff}}^2 - \left(dr + \beta_r\,dt_{\text{ff}}\right)^2 = \left(1 - \beta_r^2\right)dt^2 - \gamma_r^4\left(1 - \beta_r^2\right)dr^2 = \gamma_r^{-2}\,dt^2 - \gamma_r^2\,dr^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, part 3 (our own solution)" />

With the unchanged angular part this is the Schwarzschild metric in the form of part 1.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two metrics give the same $d\tau$ for the same pair of events, so they describe one spacetime in two sets of coordinates. The change of time coordinate, $dt_{\text{ff}} = dt + \beta_r\gamma_r^2\,dr$, blows up at $r = 2M$, where $\gamma_r$ is infinite. That is the fault of $t$, not of $t_{\text{ff}}$: the GP metric has no infinite term at the horizon.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Why tidal forces make you tall and slim" points={6} mit="Problem 3">

Use classical mechanics and Newton's gravity in this problem, with $G$ written out.

A small closed capsule falls freely and radially outside a spherical body of mass $M$. At some moment its centre is at the distance $r$ from the centre of the mass. Use Cartesian coordinates with their origin at the centre of the capsule: $y$ points along the radial direction, away from the mass, and $x$ is at right angles to it. The capsule is very small compared with $r$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Show that for $|x| \ll r$ and $|y| \ll r$ the acceleration of a free test particle at $(x, y)$, relative to an observer at $(0, 0)$, is

<Formula label="Tidal acceleration" tex="\vec a = \frac{GM}{r^3}\left(-x\,\hat x + 2y\,\hat y\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 3" />

<Hint>

Seen from the centre of the mass, the particle is at $(x, r + y)$. Write Newton's acceleration for that point and for the origin of the capsule, and subtract.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Keep only terms of first order in $x/r$ and $y/r$. You need $(1 + \epsilon)^{-3/2} \approx 1 - \frac32\epsilon$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The observer falls too. What he sees is the difference between the pull on the particle and the pull on himself.

<Step title="The pull on the particle">

From the centre of the mass the particle is at $\vec R = (x, r + y)$, at the distance $R$ with $R^2 = x^2 + (r + y)^2 \approx r^2(1 + 2y/r)$ to first order. So $R^{-3} \approx r^{-3}(1 - 3y/r)$, and

<Formula label="Newton's acceleration at the particle" tex="\vec g = -\frac{GM}{R^3}\,\vec R \approx -\frac{GM}{r^3}\left(1 - \frac{3y}{r}\right)\left(x\,\hat x + (r + y)\,\hat y\right) \approx -\frac{GM}{r^3}\left(x\,\hat x + (r - 2y)\,\hat y\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Subtract the observer's own fall">

The observer at the origin has the acceleration $-(GM/r^2)\,\hat y$. The difference is

<Formula label="Relative acceleration" tex="\vec a = \vec g + \frac{GM}{r^2}\,\hat y = \frac{GM}{r^3}\left(-x\,\hat x + 2y\,\hat y\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $GM/r^3$ is an acceleration per length, as it should be. The radial factor 2 is the derivative of $1/r^2$: the pull at $r + y$ differs from the pull at $r$ by $(2GM/r^3)\,y$. This is the stretching, $2GM/r^3$ times the length of the body, that the lesson's worked example uses.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Four test particles start at rest in the capsule at $(0, +y)$, $(0, -y)$, $(x, 0)$ and $(-x, 0)$. Find how each moves at first, before it has moved far, and sketch the four paths. In the box, give the distance that the particle at $(0, +y)$ has moved away from the origin after a short time $t$.

<Hint>

For a short time the acceleration is constant. A particle that starts at rest then moves $\frac12 a t^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-move" label="distance moved $=$" expr="G*M*y*t^2/r^3" vars={{ G: [1, 2], M: [1, 2], y: [0.5, 1.5], t: [0.5, 1.5], r: [2, 4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*G*M*y*t^2/r^3", hint: "That is a t², without the factor 1/2 of motion from rest at constant acceleration." },
    { expr: "G*M*y*t^2/(2*r^3)", hint: "That is the size of the sideways motion of the particles at (±x, 0), with y in place of x. The radial acceleration is twice as large." },
    { expr: "G*M*t^2/(2*r^2)", hint: "That is the fall of the whole capsule towards the mass. The question asks for the motion relative to the centre of the capsule." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-sketch" label="The four particles:" choices={["all four move towards the centre of the capsule", "all four move away from the centre of the capsule", "the two on the radial line move apart, the two beside it move together", "the two on the radial line move together, the two beside it move apart"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each particle starts at rest and gets the constant acceleration of part (a) for its own position.

<Step title="The four motions">

The particles at $(0, \pm y)$ have the acceleration $\pm(2GM/r^3)\,y$ along the radial line, away from the origin. After a short time $t$ each has moved

<Formula label="Radial particles move apart" tex="\Delta y = \frac12\cdot\frac{2GM}{r^3}\,y\,t^2 = \frac{GM}{r^3}\,y\,t^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

away from the centre. The particles at $(\pm x, 0)$ have the acceleration $\mp(GM/r^3)\,x$, towards the origin, and move $\frac12 (GM/r^3)\,x\,t^2$ inward.

</Step>

<Step title="The sketch">

Four short straight arrows: the upper and the lower particle move outward along the radial line, the left and the right particle move inward along the line at right angles to it. For equal starting distances the outward arrows are twice as long as the inward ones.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The sideways squeeze has a simple cause: the two side particles both fall towards the same centre, along lines that meet there. Their separation $2x$ shrinks in step with $r$, which gives the relative acceleration $-(GM/r^2)(x/r)$, as in part (a).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

If you fall feet first into a Schwarzschild black hole, why do you become tall and slim before you die?

<Hint>

Your feet and your head are two test particles on the radial line. Your shoulders are two test particles beside it.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Your body is a set of test particles, held together by forces that are in the end too weak.

<Step title="Tall and slim">

Feet and head lie on the radial line, so they are pulled apart: you are stretched. Left and right side lie beside it, so they are pushed together: you are squeezed. Both effects grow as $1/r^3$ on the way in, without limit.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lecture calls this "spaghetti, not pancake" (Lecture 25 slides, slide 17). The Newtonian result of part (a) is used here beyond its range, but the lesson's worked example uses the same formula for the stretching at the horizon.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Shell distance and the coordinate r" points={6} mit="Problem 4">

The coordinate $r$ of the Schwarzschild metric is defined through the circumference of a circle. The distance that shell observers measure along the radial direction with their rulers is longer. (MIT's sheet points to a textbook for the steps of the integration. The result you need is in the lesson and is restated here.) A shell observer at $r$ measures for a small radial step

<Formula label="Radial distance on a shell" tex="dr_{\text{shell}} = \frac{dr}{\sqrt{1 - 2M/r}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 3 (the formula is the lesson's)" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Add up the shell distances from a starting radius $r_0$ to $r$. Take $r_0 = 2M$, the horizon, and call the result $\sigma(r)$: the radial distance from the horizon to $r$ as laid out with shell rulers. Find $\sigma(r)$.

<Hint>

The integrand is $\sqrt{r/(r - 2M)}$. Try a substitution that turns $r - 2M$ into a square, such as $r = 2M\cosh^2 u$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

After the substitution you integrate $4M\cosh^2 u$. Use $\cosh^2 u = \frac12(1 + \cosh 2u)$, and change back to $r$ at the end.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-sigma" label="$\sigma(r) =$" expr="sqrt(r*(r - 2*M)) + 2*M*log(sqrt(r/(2*M)) + sqrt(r/(2*M) - 1))" vars={{ M: [0.5, 1], r: [2.5, 12] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "r - 2*M", hint: "That is the difference in the coordinate r. Shell rulers measure more: divide each step dr by √(1 − 2M/r) before adding up." },
    { expr: "sqrt(r*(r - 2*M))", hint: "That is only one of the two terms of the integral. Differentiate it and compare with the integrand: a part is missing." },
    { expr: "(r - 2*M)/sqrt(1 - 2*M/r)", hint: "The factor 1/√(1 − 2M/r) changes along the way, so it cannot be taken outside the sum. Integrate." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** Integrate $dr_{\text{shell}}$ from $2M$ to $r$. The integrand is infinite at the horizon, but the integral is finite.

<Step title="Substitute">

With $r' = 2M\cosh^2 u$: $dr' = 4M\cosh u\sinh u\,du$ and $\sqrt{r'/(r' - 2M)} = \cosh u/\sinh u$. The horizon is $u = 0$, where the result below vanishes. So

<Formula label="The integral" tex="\sigma(r) = \int_{2M}^{r}\frac{dr'}{\sqrt{1 - 2M/r'}} = \int 4M\cosh^2u\,du = 2M\left(u + \sinh u\cosh u\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Back to r">

$\cosh u = \sqrt{r/2M}$ and $\sinh u = \sqrt{r/2M - 1}$, so $2M\sinh u\cosh u = \sqrt{r(r - 2M)}$, and $u = \ln(\cosh u + \sinh u)$:

<Formula label="Shell distance from the horizon" tex="\sigma(r) = \sqrt{r\,(r - 2M)} + 2M\ln\left(\sqrt{\frac{r}{2M}} + \sqrt{\frac{r}{2M} - 1}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $r = 2M$ both terms vanish. The derivative of the result is $\sqrt{r/(r - 2M)}$, the integrand. The lesson's worked example gives the distance between $3M$ and $4M$ as $1.54M$: here $\sigma(4M) - \sigma(3M) = 4.591M - 3.049M = 1.542M$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

<Solution>

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The coordinate $r$ changes by only $2M$ between the horizon and $4M$. The shell distance is more than twice that.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Far away space is nearly flat, so each new step of $dr$ adds almost exactly $dr$ of distance. But "almost" adds up.

<Step title="The plot">

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Gravitational redshift from a neutron star" points={9} mit="Problem 5">

A radioactive iron source emits an X-ray line of $E_0 = 6$ keV in its own rest frame. The source lies on the surface of a neutron star of mass $M = 2.8 \times 10^{30}$ kg and radius $R = 10$ km. Assume that the strong gravity and any magnetic field do not change the atomic transition itself, and that the star does not rotate. Use ordinary units here, with $r_s = 2GM/c^2$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What energy do these X-rays have when they reach a very distant observer, an astronomer on Earth? (Leave out the gravity of the Earth.) Give the energy in keV.

<Hint>

A clock on a shell at $r$ runs slow by the factor $\sqrt{1 - r_s/r}$ compared with a clock far away. The emitting atom is such a clock, and the energy of a photon is proportional to its frequency.

</Hint>

<Hint>

First find $r_s$ for this star, in km, and then $r_s/R$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-far" label="$E_\infty =$" value="4.586" unit="keV" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p5-far" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The atom ticks at the rate of a shell clock at $R$. Far away, every period of the wave is seen stretched by the factor $1/\sqrt{1 - r_s/R}$, so the frequency and the photon energy are lower by that factor.

<Step title="The Schwarzschild radius of the star">

$r_s = 2GM/c^2 = 2 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 2.8 \times 10^{30}/(2.998 \times 10^8)^2 = 4158$ m, so $r_s/R = 0.4158$.

</Step>

<Step title="The redshifted energy">

<Formula label="Energy far away" tex="E_\infty = E_0\sqrt{1 - \frac{r_s}{R}} = 6\ \text{keV} \times \sqrt{0.5842} = 6\ \text{keV} \times 0.7643 = 4.586\ \text{keV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a) (our own solution; the shell-clock factor is the lesson's)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The line loses almost a quarter of its energy: a redshift of $1/0.7643 - 1 = 0.308$. The weak-field estimate, $E_0(1 - GM/(Rc^2)) = 6 \times (1 - 0.2079) = 4.75$ keV, is close but not good enough here: this star is only 2.4 times larger than its own Schwarzschild radius.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Another observer stands still on a shell at a radius $r$ larger than $R$. Find the energy $E_r$ of the line, coming from the surface, that this observer detects. Write it with $E_0$, $r_s$, $R$ and $r$.

<Hint>

Compare the two shell clocks, at $R$ and at $r$, with the far-away clock and so with each other.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-shell" label="$E_r =$" expr="E*sqrt((1 - s/R)/(1 - s/r))" vars={{ E: [1, 2], s: [1, 2], R: [3, 4], r: [5, 9] }} names={{ E: "E_0", s: "r_s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "E*sqrt(1 - s/R)", hint: "That is the energy far away. An observer at a finite r sits deeper in the field: the photon has climbed less and lost less." },
    { expr: "E*sqrt((1 - s/r)/(1 - s/R))", hint: "That is larger than E₀ for r above R: a blueshift. A photon that climbs loses energy." },
    { expr: "E*(1 - s/R)/(1 - s/r)", hint: "The clock rates are the square roots of these factors: dτ = √(1 − r_s/r) dt." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The number of wave crests is the same for everybody, and the bookkeeper time between two crests is the same at every radius (the metric does not depend on $t$). Each shell observer turns that bookkeeper time into his own time with his own factor.

<Step title="Two shell clocks">

Between two crests the bookkeeper time $\Delta t$ passes. The atom at $R$ measures $\sqrt{1 - r_s/R}\,\Delta t$, the observer at $r$ measures $\sqrt{1 - r_s/r}\,\Delta t$. Energy goes as one over the period, so

<Formula label="Energy seen on a shell" tex="E_r = E_0\sqrt{\frac{1 - r_s/R}{1 - r_s/r}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $r = R$ the observer stands next to the source and $E_r = E_0$. For $r \to \infty$ the result becomes that of part (a). In between it falls steadily: at $r = 20$ km, for example, $E_r = 6 \times \sqrt{0.5842/0.7921} = 5.153$ keV.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

The shell observer now sends X-rays of the energy $E_r$ that he saw on to the astronomer on Earth, as a report. What energy does the astronomer detect?

<Hint>

Apply the result of part (a) to a source at $r$ in place of $R$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-relay" label="The astronomer detects:" choices={["6 keV, the energy in the rest frame of the iron", "the same energy as in part (a)", "less than in part (a), because the light was redshifted twice", "an energy between that of part (a) and 6 keV"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The redshift from $R$ to far away can be done in one step or in two. The factors multiply to the same result.

<Step title="Two steps">

A photon of energy $E_r$ sent from $r$ arrives far away with

<Formula label="The relayed line" tex="E_r\sqrt{1 - \frac{r_s}{r}} = E_0\sqrt{\frac{1 - r_s/R}{1 - r_s/r}}\,\sqrt{1 - \frac{r_s}{r}} = E_0\sqrt{1 - \frac{r_s}{R}} = E_\infty" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c) (our own solution)" />

The same 4.586 keV as in part (a), whatever $r$ is.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

It has to be so. If the relayed light arrived with a different energy than the direct light, one could gain energy by sending photons round the loop. The redshift depends only on where a photon starts and where it ends.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Formula label="The same rate with shell quantities" tex="\frac{d\tau}{dt} = \left\{\left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)\left[1 - \left(\frac{dr_{\text{shell}}}{dt_{\text{shell}}}\right)^2 - r^2\left(\frac{d\varphi}{dt_{\text{shell}}}\right)^2\right]\right\}^{1/2}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem G, p. 9" />

Argue that the square bracket is $1/\gamma_{\text{shell}}^2$, where $\gamma_{\text{shell}}$ is the Lorentz factor of the particle as the shell observer measures it. Then use the conserved energy, $E/m_0 = (1 - 2M/r)\,dt/d\tau$, to show that

<Formula label="Energy far away and energy on the shell" tex="E = \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}E_{\text{shell}}, \qquad E_{\text{shell}} = m_0\gamma_{\text{shell}}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem G, p. 9" />

<Hint>

Take the factor $1 - 2M/r$ out of the bracket, and use $dt_{\text{shell}}^2 = (1 - 2M/r)\,dt^2$ and $dr_{\text{shell}}^2 = dr^2/(1 - 2M/r)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

What are $dr_{\text{shell}}/dt_{\text{shell}}$ and $r\,d\varphi/dt_{\text{shell}}$ for the shell observer?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The shell observer is at rest in a small region where special relativity holds. In his own rulers and clocks the particle has an ordinary velocity and an ordinary Lorentz factor.

<Step title="Take out the factor">

Write $1 - 2M/r = \gamma_r^{-2}$. Then $dr = dr_{\text{shell}}/\gamma_r$ and $dt = \gamma_r\,dt_{\text{shell}}$, so $\gamma_r^2\,(dr/dt)^2 = \gamma_r^{-2}\,(dr_{\text{shell}}/dt_{\text{shell}})^2$ and $r^2(d\varphi/dt)^2 = \gamma_r^{-2}\,r^2(d\varphi/dt_{\text{shell}})^2$. Every term in the bracket now has the factor $\gamma_r^{-2} = 1 - 2M/r$, which gives the second form.

</Step>

<Step title="The Lorentz factor">

$dr_{\text{shell}}/dt_{\text{shell}}$ is the radial velocity and $r\,d\varphi/dt_{\text{shell}}$ the tangential velocity that the shell observer measures (in units of $c$). The square bracket is $1 - v_{\text{shell}}^2 = 1/\gamma_{\text{shell}}^2$, so

<Formula label="Rate of the proper time" tex="\frac{d\tau}{dt} = \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)^{1/2}\frac{1}{\gamma_{\text{shell}}}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem G, p. 9" />

</Step>

<Step title="The energy">

$E/m_0 = \gamma_r^{-2}\,dt/d\tau = \gamma_r^{-2}\,\gamma_r\gamma_{\text{shell}} = \gamma_{\text{shell}}/\gamma_r$, so $E = (1 - 2M/r)^{1/2}\,m_0\gamma_{\text{shell}} = (1 - 2M/r)^{1/2}E_{\text{shell}}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The rate $d\tau/dt$ is a product of two slow-downs: the shell clock against the far-away clock, and the moving clock against the shell clock. The energy relation is the redshift of Problem 5 for a particle with mass: energy measured locally on a shell is worth less far away, by the same factor $\sqrt{1 - 2M/r}$. With $E/m_0 = 1$ it gives $\gamma_{\text{shell}} = \gamma_r$, so $v_{\text{shell}} = \beta_r$: Problem 10, part (a).

</Step>

</Solution>

<Exercise id="ph" title="The speed of light in the Schwarzschild metric" points={3} mit="Optional Problem H" optional>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

Show that for a photon that moves radially, the speed of light as "reckoned" by the bookkeeper is

<Formula label="Bookkeeper speed of radial light" tex="\frac{dr}{dt} = \pm\left(1 - \frac{2GM}{c^2r}\right)c" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem H(b), p. 9" />

<Hint>

For light $d\tau = 0$. Use the metric of Problem 6 with $d\varphi = 0$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Light moves along paths of zero proper time.

<Step title="Set the proper time to zero">

With $d\tau = 0$ and $d\varphi = 0$ the metric of Problem 6 gives $(1 - r_s/r)\,dt^2 = dr^2/\left(c^2(1 - r_s/r)\right)$, so $dr/dt = \pm(1 - r_s/r)\,c$ with $r_s = 2GM/c^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Far away the speed is $c$. At the horizon it is zero: in the bookkeeper's account light stands still there, as the stone of Problem 10 does. A shell observer still measures exactly $c$: $dr_{\text{shell}}/dt_{\text{shell}} = \gamma_r^2\,dr/dt = \pm c$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

In the weak field of a star of $1\,M_\odot$ ($2 \times 10^{30}$ kg), a photon moves radially from $r_1 = 10^8$ m to $r_2 = 10^{10}$ m. Find the bookkeeper time for the trip, in seconds. How much longer is that than the time for the same trip without the star, in microseconds?

<Hint>

$dt = dr/\left(c(1 - r_s/r)\right)$, and in a weak field $1/(1 - r_s/r) \approx 1 + r_s/r$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="ph-time" label="bookkeeper time:" value="33.02" unit="s" tolerance="0.5%" calc="ph-time" />

<Answer id="ph-delay" label="extra time:" value="45.62" unit="μs" tolerance="1%" calc="ph-delay" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Add up $dt = dr/(\text{speed})$ along the path. The slower bookkeeper speed near the star adds a little time.

<Step title="Integrate">

<Formula label="Bookkeeper time for radial light" tex="\Delta t = \frac1c\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\frac{dr}{1 - r_s/r} \approx \frac1c\int_{r_1}^{r_2}\left(1 + \frac{r_s}{r}\right)dr = \frac{r_2 - r_1}{c} + \frac{r_s}{c}\ln\frac{r_2}{r_1}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem H(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

Without the star: $(10^{10} - 10^8)/(2.998 \times 10^8) = 33.02$ s. The extra time, with $r_s = 2970$ m from Problem 8: $(2970/2.998 \times 10^8) \times \ln 100 = 9.907 \times 10^{-6} \times 4.605$ s $= 45.62$ μs. The bookkeeper time is $33.02$ s plus 45.62 μs, which is still 33.02 s to four figures.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The exact integral, without the weak-field step, is $(r_2 - r_1)/c + (r_s/c)\ln\left((r_2 - r_s)/(r_1 - r_s)\right)$ and gives the same 45.62 μs. This extra time is the Shapiro time delay of the lesson on [the evidence for general relativity](/courses/8-033/lensing-evidence-for-gr), which was measured with radar signals that pass the Sun.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
