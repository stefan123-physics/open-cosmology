MIT's first lecture of 8.033 (Max Tegmark, Fall 2006) is an overview. Most of its slides are about how the course runs: other MIT courses that overlap with it, grading, books and a timetable (slides 1–4). This page leaves those out and keeps what the lecture says about physics: where relativity sits in the history of physics, the first hint of it in Maxwell's equations, and the lessons the course will reach. (MIT's material for this course is a set of lecture slides; this page follows their order and fills in the reasoning between them.)

## The road through the course

Slide 2 warns that this course will feel different from first-year mechanics and electricity. Special relativity will be **derived** from just two postulates of Einstein. General relativity and cosmology go the other way: the course starts in the middle, takes certain equations on trust, and works out what follows from them. For many students this is the first real break with intuition, and it brings one new piece of mathematics, the calculus of variations.

The lecture plan on slide 4 runs in this order:

1. background (symmetry, and what the speed of light does under a change of frame);
2. special relativistic kinematics: how space and time look to observers in relative motion;
3. special relativistic dynamics: momentum, energy and particle physics;
4. the relativity of electricity and magnetism;
5. cosmology;
6. curved spacetime and black holes.

General relativity is covered only in part: the course mostly works out the consequences of two solutions of Einstein's equations, the **FRW metric** of an expanding universe and the **Schwarzschild metric** around a mass, and leaves the full theory to a later course. (Slide 4 numbers the plan by class meetings, up to 39; MIT published 26 sets of slides. On this site the background is Lectures 2–3, kinematics 4–8, dynamics and particle physics 9–14, electromagnetism 15–16, the equivalence principle and cosmology 17–22, black holes 23–26.)

## Relativity in the history of physics

Slide 5 sketches the history of physics in a few steps, each with the MIT courses that teach it: Newtonian mechanics in the 1600s; electromagnetism from about 1800 to 1875; statistical mechanics and thermodynamics from about 1850 to 1900; relativity (Einstein, 1905 and 1916); quantum mechanics, from 1900 to 1926; and then the breakthroughs of the twentieth century and the question of what is left to discover.

Slides 6 and 7 put names to each step: Brahe, Kepler and Newton for mechanics; Euler, Lagrange and Laplace, who turned Newton's gravity into precise astronomy in the 1700s; Gauss, Ampère, Faraday and Maxwell for electricity and magnetism; Clausius, Kelvin, Maxwell and Boltzmann for heat; Planck, Bohr, Heisenberg, Schrödinger and others for quantum mechanics. Relativity has Einstein at its centre, with a row of names that will come up later in the course: Lorentz, Riemann, Schwarzschild, Kerr, the FRW authors, Wheeler, Kruskal and Hawking. (Two dates on these slides are slips: Newton's *Principia* appeared in 1687, not 1678, and Einstein was born in 1879, not 1899.)

Slide 8 dwells on Einstein. In 1905, at 26, while working at a patent office and almost cut off from universities, he published three papers that each changed physics: on the photoelectric effect, on Brownian motion, and on special relativity. He completed general relativity in 1916.

## A hint of relativity in Maxwell's equations

The trouble that relativity solves is already in the physics of 8.02. In empty space, Maxwell's equations combine into a wave equation for the electric field (slide 9):

<Formula label="Wave equation from Maxwell's equations" tex="\nabla^2\vec E - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\vec E}{\partial t^2} = 0" cite="mit" at="slide 9" />

Here $\vec E$ is the electric field and $\nabla^2$ the Laplacian, the sum of second derivatives in $x$, $y$ and $z$ (the slide writes the time derivative with two dots). The equation has travelling waves as solutions, and every one of them moves at the same speed $c$, whatever its frequency. In the nineteenth century this was a shock; today electromagnetic waves are seen over a range of frequencies from about $10^3$ Hz to $10^{27}$ Hz (slide 9).

<WorkedExample title="How wide is that range?">
<Problem>
Electromagnetic waves are observed from $f = 10^3$ Hz to $f = 10^{27}$ Hz. With $c = 3.00\times10^8$ m/s, what wavelengths $\lambda = c/f$ do the two ends correspond to?
</Problem>
<Step title="Low end">
$\lambda = (3.00\times10^8\ \text{m/s})/(10^3\ \text{Hz}) = 3.00\times10^5$ m, or 300 km: a radio wave longer than a small country.
</Step>
<Step title="High end">
$\lambda = (3.00\times10^8\ \text{m/s})/(10^{27}\ \text{Hz}) = 3.00\times10^{-19}$ m, far smaller than a proton: a gamma ray of very high energy.
</Step>
<Step title="What the equation says">
Twenty-four powers of ten apart, and the wave equation gives both the same speed $c$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

But speed $c$ **relative to what**? The equation refers to no particular frame (slide 9). Picture a flashlight switched on in a moving train. If velocities simply add, as they do for thrown balls, the light should travel at $c$ plus the speed of the train for someone standing beside the track, and at only $c$ for the passenger. Then the wave equation could hold in one frame at most. Either the equation is missing something, or something else we take for granted is wrong (slide 9). The course will find that it is the second: our ideas of space and time.

<WorkedExample title="How big an effect would that be?">
<Problem>
A train moves at $v = 50$ m/s. If the light of a flashlight on the train moved at $c + v$ relative to the ground, by what fraction would its speed differ from $c$?
</Problem>
<Step title="The ratio">
$v/c = (50\ \text{m/s})/(3.00\times10^8\ \text{m/s}) = 1.67\times10^{-7}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Why this is hard to see">
About two parts in ten million: far too small to notice in daily life, which is why the question needs careful experiments. Lecture 3 describes them.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The key lessons of the course

Slide 11 lists what the course will show. From **special relativity**:

- space and time are not separate: they are tied together into a single four-dimensional **spacetime**;
- clocks that move fast run slow, and moving bodies are shorter and behave as if heavier (the slide's words; later lectures say it with energy and momentum);
- mass and energy are equivalent:

<Formula label="Mass and energy" tex="E = mc^2" cite="mit" at="slide 11" />

Because $c^2$ is so large, a small mass stands for an enormous energy: for $m = 1$ g, $E = (10^{-3}\ \text{kg})(3.00\times10^8\ \text{m/s})^2 = 9.0\times10^{13}$ J.

From **general relativity**:

- spacetime is not a fixed stage but takes part in physics: on the largest scales it expands, and locally it curves and can collapse into black holes;
- matter curves spacetime;
- bodies that move as straight as they can through curved spacetime appear to be deflected, and that is what we feel as gravity.

The slide ends with a blank line labelled "your theory here" and three pieces of advice: think for yourself, question authority, and do not dismiss an idea only because it sounds strange. Slide 12 adds that physics should be *expected* to feel weird; the lecture showed films of driving at nearly the speed of light and of a black hole.

Some of what the slides promise has since been observed directly. (Later developments, after 2006: gravitational waves, ripples of curving spacetime, were detected by LIGO in 2015, and the Event Horizon Telescope published an image of the shadow of the black hole in the galaxy M87 in 2019.) The next lecture begins the course proper, with symmetry in physics (slide 13).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Where this leads in cosmology">
The overview ends where cosmology starts. The "globally expanding" spacetime of slide 11 is the FRW metric, which 8.033 meets in Lectures 18–22 and which is the backbone of 8.286 *The Early Universe*. The speed $c$ that Maxwell's equations fix also sets the horizon: how far light can have travelled since the Big Bang, and so how much of the universe we can see.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Lecture 1 of 8.033 is an overview. Special relativity will be derived from Einstein's two postulates; general relativity and cosmology are entered in the middle, through the FRW and Schwarzschild metrics.
- Relativity came after Newtonian mechanics, electromagnetism and thermodynamics: Einstein published special relativity in 1905, aged 26, and completed general relativity in 1916.
- Maxwell's equations in vacuum give the wave equation $\nabla^2\vec E - (1/c^2)\,\partial^2\vec E/\partial t^2 = 0$: waves at speed $c$ at every frequency, observed from about $10^3$ to $10^{27}$ Hz, with no frame named. A flashlight on a train shows the conflict with ordinary addition of velocities.
- Special relativity: spacetime is four-dimensional, moving clocks run slow, moving bodies are shorter, and $E = mc^2$.
- General relativity: spacetime expands and curves, matter curves it, and free motion through curved spacetime is gravity.
