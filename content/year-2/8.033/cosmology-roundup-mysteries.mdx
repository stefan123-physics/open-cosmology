Lecture 22 is the cosmology roundup: the evidence for the big bang, what we know and the common misconceptions, what we don't know and the hot research questions, and what the lecturer works on himself (slide 9). It opens with a portrait of Alan Guth, the founder of inflation, whose MIT course [8.286](/courses/8-286/inflation-overview) is the core of this site's cosmology (slide 1). After a recap of the formulas and of the evidence (slides 2–8), the slides set three mysteries for the students to solve (slide 11): what is dark matter? How did it all begin (buzz word: inflation)? How will it all end (buzz word: dark energy)? (MIT's material for this lecture is a set of slides, in a deck titled "Lecture 20"; this site numbers lectures by MIT's class sessions, in which the second quiz took one. Many slides are figures from research papers, several of them removed from the OCW file for copyright reasons; this page describes them in words only.)

## Mystery 1: how will it end?

Slide 10 calls cosmology a "smörgåsbord" of data: the CMB, galaxy surveys, distant supernovae, gravitational lensing, big bang nucleosynthesis, galaxy clusters and the Lyman alpha forest. A decelerating universe may recollapse in a "big crunch"; a coasting one, older than a decelerating one, expands forever; an accelerating one, older still, keeps speeding up (slide 13). Distant light is redshifted and dimmed, and standard candles measure the dimming (slides 14–16). Slide 17 was a film from Saul Perlmutter's web site, and slide 18 credits a figure of high-redshift supernovae by Riess and colleagues, removed from the OCW file. (The page's own summary of the result: distant Type Ia supernovae look dimmer than they would in a decelerating universe, so the expansion has been accelerating, driven by dark energy.)

**The history of the density** (slides 19–20). With $H = d\ln a/dt$ and $H^2 \propto \rho$, a plot of the logarithm of the density against the logarithm of $a$ traces the whole history: inflation at the top, then radiation domination, where $\rho \propto a^{-4}$, through the energies of the LHC (1 TeV), of a proton's rest energy (1 GeV) and of nucleosynthesis (1 MeV), then matter domination, $\rho \propto a^{-3}$, and finally dark energy, whose density stays constant. (The page adds: why dark energy takes over just now, and how the universe will end, depend on what dark energy is.)

## Mystery 2: what is dark matter?

The clue is in the **fine details of cosmic clumpiness** (slides 21–58). Inflation generated tiny fluctuations, seen in the CMB at a redshift of 1000 (slides 25–26; the fluctuations are about one part in $10^5$, a figure the page adds), and gravity amplified them. Simulations of our own cosmic neighbourhood, in a universe with cold dark matter and vacuum energy (with $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.7$, $\Omega_m = 0.3$, $h = 0.7$), show the cosmic web forming at redshifts 2.4, 0.8 and today (slides 27–29, from Mathis and colleagues, 2001). Slide 31 combines all the measurements of density fluctuations, from the CMB on the largest scales to hydrogen clouds on the smallest, on one curve, and slides 33 and 51 show the galaxy power spectrum measured by 1999. Simulated galaxy maps look just like the real surveys (slide 37, from Springel, Frenk and White, 2006), and the measured clustering of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey's luminous red galaxies agrees with the theory (slide 38): "theory and measurement agree!".

The CMB's own fluctuations, plotted against angular scale (the multipole $\ell$), have a pattern of peaks that rules models in and out (slide 42, after Guth and Kaiser, 2005, and slide 54, after Tegmark and Zaldarriaga with updates): inflation with vacuum energy fits the WMAP, CBI, ACBAR and BOOMERANG data, while an open universe, inflation without vacuum energy, and cosmic strings do not. Other probes are the Lyman alpha forest, absorption by hydrogen clouds along the line to a distant quasar (slide 45), and gravitational lensing, which distorts the shapes of background galaxies (slides 48–49). "But the best is yet to come" (slide 53).

**The cosmic recipe** (slide 57). Combining WMAP's three-year data with the SDSS luminous red galaxies gives:

<Formula label="Cosmological parameters (WMAP3 + SDSS LRGs)" tex="\Omega_{\text{tot}} = 1.003^{+0.010}_{-0.009}, \quad \Omega_\Lambda = 0.761^{+0.017}_{-0.018}, \quad \omega_b = 0.0222 \pm 0.0007, \quad \omega_c = 0.1050^{+0.0041}_{-0.0040}, \quad w = -0.941^{+0.087}_{-0.101}" cite="mit" at="slide 57" />

and, for the seed fluctuations, a scalar spectral index $n_s = 0.953 \pm 0.016$ and a tensor-to-scalar ratio $r$ below 0.30. Here $\omega_b = \Omega_bh^2$ and $\omega_c = \Omega_ch^2$ are the physical densities of baryons and cold dark matter, and $w$ is the dark energy's ratio of pressure to density ($w = -1$ for vacuum energy). In round numbers the universe is 75% dark energy, 21% cold dark matter and 4% ordinary matter. Slide 58 shows the Bullet Cluster (Clowe and colleagues, 2006). The page's own explanation: in these two colliding galaxy clusters, the mass mapped by lensing has passed through while the hot gas, most of the ordinary matter, has been slowed, which is hard to explain without dark matter as a separate kind of stuff.

## Mystery 3: how did it begin?

Slide 60 sketches the answer: the dawn of time, then inflation in a tiny fraction of a second, the CMB at 380,000 years, and today at 13.7 billion years. **Evidence number 1 for inflation: space is very flat** (slides 63–64),

<Formula label="Flatness" tex="\Omega_{\text{tot}} = 1.003 \pm 0.010" cite="mit" at="slide 63" />

(from Tegmark and colleagues, 2006). Inflation stretches any initial curvature away, so it predicts $\Omega_{\text{tot}} = 1$ to high precision. In this picture, as slide 66 puts it after Linde's 1994 article in *Scientific American*, "the Big Bang" was "not the beginning, but the end" of inflation: the hot expansion began when inflation stopped and its energy turned into hot matter and radiation.

The lecture closes with the three mysteries again (slide 67), a smiling CMB map (slide 68), a summary of what we know about our metric (slide 69), and the next topic: black holes (slide 70).

<WorkedExample title="Reading the cosmic recipe">
<Problem>
(a) With $\omega_b = 0.0222$, $\omega_c = 0.105$ and $h = 0.73$ (an assumed value close to the WMAP3 results; the slides elsewhere use about 0.70), what are $\Omega_b$ and $\Omega_c$? Do they add up with $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.761$ to 1? (b) If $\left|\Omega_{\text{tot}} - 1\right|$ is below 0.01, how large must the radius of curvature of space be, at least, given $R = (c/H_0)/\sqrt{\left|\Omega_{\text{tot}} - 1\right|}$ and $c/H_0 = 4100$ Mpc?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Baryons and dark matter">
$h^2 = 0.533$, so $\Omega_b = 0.0222/0.533 \approx 0.042$ and $\Omega_c = 0.105/0.533 \approx 0.197$. Together, $0.239 + 0.761 = 1.000$: a flat universe, of about 4% ordinary matter, 20% cold dark matter and 76% dark energy, close to the slide's pie chart.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A very flat space">
$R \ge 4100/\sqrt{0.01} = 41{,}000$ Mpc, at least ten times the Hubble distance: any curvature is far too slight to see in the part of the universe we can observe.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Three mysteries: dark energy (how it ends), dark matter, and the beginning (inflation).
- The fine details of clumpiness in the CMB, galaxy surveys and simulations agree with a flat universe of about 75% dark energy, 21% cold dark matter and 4% ordinary matter.
- Space is flat, $\Omega_{\text{tot}} = 1.003 \pm 0.010$, as inflation predicts; the big bang was the end of inflation.
