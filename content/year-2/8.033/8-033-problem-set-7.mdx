## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within 1% of ours unless the problem says otherwise, so keep at least four significant figures, and type it in the unit shown next to the box. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `g*(Ey - b*Bz)` or `sqrt(2*E0*(E + E0))`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Write the symbols of the box exactly as the problem names them (`g` stands for $\gamma$, `b` for $\beta$, `Ey` for $E_y$, `E0` for a rest energy $m_0c^2$). A box that names both `g` and `b` treats them as two separate symbols: leave $\gamma$ as `g` and do not write it out in terms of `b`.

MIT gives the points per problem. The split between parts is ours. Parts that ask you to show or to explain something have no box, or only a box for the main point: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons: $m_0$ is a rest mass, $E_0 = m_0c^2$ a rest energy, $E$ a total energy, $K = E - E_0$ a kinetic energy and $p$ a momentum. For rest energies use the values of the lecture's table (Lecture 14, slide 11): electron 0.511 MeV, neutron 939.55 MeV, one atomic mass unit 931.5 MeV. For the proton use 938.3 MeV (0.938 GeV), which is today's value of 938.272 MeV rounded (the slide has 938.26 MeV; the difference changes no answer here; today's value is from the Particle Data Group's tables, listed with the sources of this page), unless a problem gives its own value. In Problem 2 the fields are in Gaussian units, as in the lesson, so that $E$ and $B$ have the same unit.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Key concepts: three questions about particles" points={9} mit="Problem 1">

Three short questions about which particle processes can happen, and about what matter is made of.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

A physicist reports that he has seen a photon with an energy of 1 GeV turn into an electron and a positron, far from any other particle and with no outside electromagnetic field. Can this happen, by relativity and the conservation laws of the lesson? If it cannot, say which conservation law it breaks, and show it with a short calculation.

<Hint>

Go through the list of the lesson: energy-momentum, charge, baryon number, lepton number. The electron and the positron have opposite charge and opposite lepton number.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A calculation with two unknown electron directions is messy. Look at the process from the frame in which the electron and the positron together have zero momentum. What would the photon's momentum, and so its energy, be in that frame?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="The decay of a lone photon into an electron and a positron:" choices={["Can happen: 1 GeV is far more than the 1.022 MeV that the pair needs", "Cannot happen: charge would not be conserved", "Cannot happen: lepton number would not be conserved", "Cannot happen: energy and momentum cannot both be conserved"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Charge and lepton number are fine, and there is plenty of energy. What fails is that energy and momentum cannot be conserved together. The quickest proof uses a quantity that is the same in every frame.

<Step title="The laws that hold">

The photon has charge 0, the pair has charge $-1 + 1 = 0$. The photon has lepton number 0, the pair has $+1 - 1 = 0$ (the positron is an antilepton). No baryons take part. Energy alone is no obstacle either: the pair needs $2 \times 0.511 = 1.022$ MeV of rest energy and the photon has a thousand times more.

</Step>

<Step title="Energy and momentum together">

For any system, $E^2 - c^2p^2$ of the total energy and total momentum is the same in every frame, and it is the same before and after a process if both are conserved. For one photon $E = cp$, so the quantity is zero:

<Formula label="Invariant of a single photon" tex="E^2 - c^2p^2 = 0" cite="reading1" at="slide 5 (E = cp for a photon; the application is our own)" />

For the pair, go to the frame in which its total momentum is zero. There its total energy is at least the two rest energies, so

<Formula label="Invariant of an electron-positron pair" tex="E_\text{tot}^2 - c^2p_\text{tot}^2 \ge \left(2m_ec^2\right)^2 = \left(1.022\ \text{MeV}\right)^2 > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Zero cannot equal a positive number, so the 4-momentum $P$ cannot be conserved. The process is forbidden, whatever the photon's energy.

</Step>

<Step title="Check: the same in words">

In the frame where the pair has zero total momentum, the photon would need zero momentum too. A photon with zero momentum has zero energy, so it is no photon at all, while the pair has at least 1.022 MeV. This also shows why the lesson's examples of pair creation have a second body: two photons, $\gamma + \gamma \to e^- + e^+$, or a photon passing a particle that takes up the recoil (Lecture 14, slide 21). The physicist's remark that nothing else was nearby is what makes his report impossible.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

A free neutron decays by beta decay into three particles: a proton, an electron and an antineutrino, $n \to p^+ + e^- + \bar\nu$. For each of the three products, name at least one conservation law that would be broken if that particle were missing from the decay. The box asks about the antineutrino only. Mark the other two yourself.

<Hint>

For each product, cross it out and count again on both sides: charge, baryon number (1 for a proton or a neutron), lepton number (1 for an electron or a neutrino, $-1$ for their antiparticles).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="Without the antineutrino, the decay would break the conservation of:" choices={["Charge", "Baryon number", "Lepton number", "None of these: only the energy balance needs the antineutrino"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count charge, baryon number and lepton number on both sides, with one product left out each time.

<Step title="The full decay">

The neutron has charge 0, baryon number 1 and lepton number 0. The products have charge $+1 - 1 + 0 = 0$, baryon number $1 + 0 + 0 = 1$ and lepton number $0 + 1 - 1 = 0$. Everything balances.

</Step>

<Step title="One product missing">

- **No proton** ($n \to e^- + \bar\nu$): baryon number would go from 1 to 0. Charge would fail as well (0 to $-1$).
- **No electron** ($n \to p^+ + \bar\nu$): charge would go from 0 to $+1$. Lepton number would fail as well (0 to $-1$).
- **No antineutrino** ($n \to p^+ + e^-$): lepton number would go from 0 to $+1$. Charge and baryon number would be fine, which is why the neutrino was so hard to find.

</Step>

<Step title="Check: the history and the energy">

The lesson tells how the antineutrino was found on paper first: without it, energy and momentum seemed not to be conserved in beta decay, and Pauli proposed a light neutral particle to carry the rest (Lecture 14, slide 19). With only two products, the electron would always come out with the same energy. In fact its energy varies from decay to decay, because three particles share the energy in many ways. The energy to share is the difference of the rest energies, $939.55 - 938.26 - 0.511 = 0.78$ MeV with the slide's values, a positive number, so the decay can happen. (Angular momentum needs the third particle as well: a proton and an electron alone, each with spin one half, cannot carry the neutron's spin of one half. Spin is not on the lesson's list.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

List what a water molecule is made of, in elementary particles: how many quarks of each type and how many leptons of each type. Take the common isotopes: each hydrogen nucleus is one proton, and the oxygen nucleus has 8 protons and 8 neutrons. Count only the matter particles (not the photons and gluons that hold them together). A proton is two up quarks and one down quark, and a neutron is one up quark and two down quarks.

<Hint>

First count protons, neutrons and electrons. The molecule is neutral.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c-up" label="Up quarks:" value="28" tolerance="0.1%" calc="p1-up" />

<Answer id="p1-c-down" label="Down quarks:" value="26" tolerance="0.1%" calc="p1-down" />

<Answer id="p1-c-el" label="Electrons:" value="10" tolerance="0.1%" calc="p1-electrons" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count nucleons and electrons, then open the nucleons.

<Step title="Protons, neutrons, electrons">

Water is $\text{H}_2\text{O}$. The two hydrogen atoms bring 2 protons and the oxygen atom 8 protons and 8 neutrons: 10 protons and 8 neutrons. The molecule is neutral, so it has 10 electrons.

</Step>

<Step title="Quarks and leptons">

Up quarks: $2 \times 10 + 1 \times 8 = 28$. Down quarks: $1 \times 10 + 2 \times 8 = 26$. Leptons: 10 electrons, and no muons, taus or neutrinos. So the inventory is 28 up quarks, 26 down quarks and 10 electrons: 64 matter particles of only three types.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

There are $28 + 26 = 54$ quarks, three for each of the 18 nucleons. The charge is $28 \times \tfrac23 - 26 \times \tfrac13 - 10 = \tfrac{56 - 26}{3} - 10 = 0$, as it must be for a neutral molecule. (A rarer molecule with a heavier isotope of hydrogen or oxygen has more neutrons, so three more quarks for each extra neutron.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Transforming the electromagnetic field" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

The lesson writes the force on a charge $q$ as $Q = \frac{q}{c}MU$, where $M$ is the field matrix (Lecture 15, slide 16). In a frame $S'$ that moves with speed $v = \beta c$ along $x$, the matrix is $M' = \Lambda M\Lambda^{-1}$:

<Formula label="The transformed field matrix" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 0 & B_z' & -B_y' & E_x' \\ -B_z' & 0 & B_x' & E_y' \\ B_y' & -B_x' & 0 & E_z' \\ E_x' & E_y' & E_z' & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \Lambda\begin{pmatrix} 0 & B_z & -B_y & E_x \\ -B_z & 0 & B_x & E_y \\ B_y & -B_x & 0 & E_z \\ E_x & E_y & E_z & 0 \end{pmatrix}\Lambda^{-1}, \qquad \Lambda = \begin{pmatrix} \gamma & 0 & 0 & -\gamma\beta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\gamma\beta & 0 & 0 & \gamma \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1" />

The inverse $\Lambda^{-1}$ is the same matrix with $-\beta$ replaced by $\beta$. The rows and columns are in the order $x$, $y$, $z$, $t$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Carry out the two matrix multiplications and read off the six new field components $E_x'$, $E_y'$, $E_z'$, $B_x'$, $B_y'$, $B_z'$ in terms of the old ones. Then compare your result with the summary of the lecture (MIT's sheet asks for a comparison with a textbook, which is not used here). Type two of the six in the boxes, with `g` for $\gamma$ and `b` for $\beta$.

<Hint>

Multiply in two steps. Multiplying by $\Lambda$ on the left mixes rows 1 and 4 and leaves rows 2 and 3 alone. Multiplying by $\Lambda^{-1}$ on the right mixes columns 1 and 4 and leaves columns 2 and 3 alone.

</Hint>

<Hint>

When two terms with $\gamma^2$ appear, use $\gamma^2\left(1 - \beta^2\right) = 1$. The result must again be antisymmetric in its upper left block and symmetric in its last row and column: a good test of your algebra.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ey" label="$E_y' =$" expr="g*(Ey - b*Bz)"
  vars={{ g: [1.2, 3], b: [0.2, 0.9], Ey: [1, 3], Bz: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ g: "\\gamma", b: "\\beta", Ey: "E_y", Bz: "B_z" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "g*(Ey + b*Bz)", hint: "That is the field in a frame that moves the other way, along minus x. Check the signs of the entries of Λ and of Λ⁻¹." },
    { expr: "Ey - b*Bz", hint: "The factor γ is missing. Row 2 of ΛM is unchanged, but the last column of the product with Λ⁻¹ multiplies it by γ." },
    { expr: "g*Ey", hint: "That is right only when there is no magnetic field in S. Keep the entry B_z of the first column: it mixes into the last column." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-by" label="$B_y' =$" expr="g*(By + b*Ez)"
  vars={{ g: [1.2, 3], b: [0.2, 0.9], By: [1, 3], Ez: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ g: "\\gamma", b: "\\beta", By: "B_y", Ez: "E_z" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "g*(By - b*Ez)", hint: "The sign is wrong. Read B_y' from row 3, column 1 (or from row 1, column 3, which holds −B_y')." },
    { expr: "By + b*Ez", hint: "The factor γ is missing." },
    { expr: "g*By", hint: "That is right only when there is no electric field in S." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Do the product in two steps, rows first and columns second, and then compare the result entry by entry with the pattern of a field matrix.

<Step title="Multiply by Λ on the left">

Multiplying by $\Lambda$ on the left combines rows. Row 1 of $\Lambda M$ is $\gamma$ times row 1 of $M$ minus $\gamma\beta$ times row 4. Rows 2 and 3 are unchanged. Row 4 is $-\gamma\beta$ times row 1 plus $\gamma$ times row 4:

<Formula label="First product" tex="\Lambda M = \begin{pmatrix} -\gamma\beta E_x & \gamma\left(B_z - \beta E_y\right) & -\gamma\left(B_y + \beta E_z\right) & \gamma E_x \\ -B_z & 0 & B_x & E_y \\ B_y & -B_x & 0 & E_z \\ \gamma E_x & \gamma\left(E_y - \beta B_z\right) & \gamma\left(E_z + \beta B_y\right) & -\gamma\beta E_x \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Multiply by the inverse on the right">

Multiplying by $\Lambda^{-1}$ on the right combines columns of $\Lambda M$. The new column 1 is $\gamma$ times column 1 plus $\gamma\beta$ times column 4. Columns 2 and 3 are unchanged. The new column 4 is $\gamma\beta$ times column 1 plus $\gamma$ times column 4. In the corners, $\gamma^2\left(1 - \beta^2\right) = 1$ tidies up: for example the top right entry is $\gamma\beta\left(-\gamma\beta E_x\right) + \gamma\left(\gamma E_x\right) = E_x$, and the top left entry is $-\gamma^2\beta E_x + \gamma^2\beta E_x = 0$. The result is

<Formula label="The transformed matrix, worked out" tex="M' = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \gamma\left(B_z - \beta E_y\right) & -\gamma\left(B_y + \beta E_z\right) & E_x \\ -\gamma\left(B_z - \beta E_y\right) & 0 & B_x & \gamma\left(E_y - \beta B_z\right) \\ \gamma\left(B_y + \beta E_z\right) & -B_x & 0 & \gamma\left(E_z + \beta B_y\right) \\ E_x & \gamma\left(E_y - \beta B_z\right) & \gamma\left(E_z + \beta B_y\right) & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Read off the fields">

Comparing with the pattern on the left of the problem's equation:

<Formula label="Lorentz transformation of the field" tex="E_x' = E_x, \quad E_y' = \gamma\left(E_y - \beta B_z\right), \quad E_z' = \gamma\left(E_z + \beta B_y\right), \quad B_x' = B_x, \quad B_y' = \gamma\left(B_y + \beta E_z\right), \quad B_z' = \gamma\left(B_z - \beta E_y\right)" cite="reading4" at="slide 18" />

This is the summary of the lecture, entry for entry. The whole derivation is two matrix products.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The new matrix has the right pattern: zeros on the diagonal, an antisymmetric upper left block, a symmetric last row and column, and every field appears twice with the same value. With $\beta = 0$ nothing changes. A second check comes from a different route. A charge at rest in $S'$ moves with velocity $v$ along $x$ in $S$, where the Lorentz force on it has the $y$ component $q\left(E_y - \beta B_z\right)$. A force across the motion is larger by the factor $\gamma$ in the charge's rest frame ([Lecture 12](/courses/8-033/force-acceleration-interstellar-travel), slide 13, with $u_x = v$). In $S'$ the charge is at rest, so that force is $qE_y'$. Hence $E_y' = \gamma\left(E_y - \beta B_z\right)$, as found.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

To see what the result means, look at two special cases.

(A) In $S$ both $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ point along $x$, the direction in which $S'$ moves. What are the fields in $S'$?

(B) In $S$ there is only an electric field along $y$, across the motion: $E_x = E_z = B_x = B_y = B_z = 0$. Describe the fields in $S'$, and type the magnetic component that appears, with `g` for $\gamma$ and `b` for $\beta$.

<Hint>

Put the zeros into the six formulas of part (a), one case at a time.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-case-a" label="Case (A): in S′," choices={["both fields are the same as in S", "both fields are larger by the factor γ", "the electric field is the same, and the magnetic field is larger by the factor γ", "the fields along x are the same, and new components across the motion appear"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p2-bz" label="Case (B): $B_z' =$" expr="-g*b*Ey"
  vars={{ g: [1.2, 3], b: [0.2, 0.9], Ey: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ g: "\\gamma", b: "\\beta", Ey: "E_y" }}

    { expr: "0", hint: "A field that is purely electric in S is not purely electric in S′: put E_y into the formula for B_z'." },

<Solution>

**The idea.** Fields along the motion do not change at all. Fields across the motion grow by $\gamma$ and bring a partner field of the other kind with them.

<Step title="(A) Fields along the motion">

With only $E_x$ and $B_x$ different from zero, the formulas give $E_x' = E_x$, $B_x' = B_x$ and zero for the other four. The fields in $S'$ are the same as in $S$, for any speed.

</Step>

<Step title="(B) A pure electric field across the motion">

With only $E_y$ different from zero:

<Formula label="A pure electric field seen from a moving frame" tex="E_y' = \gamma E_y, \qquad B_z' = -\gamma\beta E_y, \qquad E_x' = E_z' = B_x' = B_y' = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The electric field still points along $y$ and is stronger by the factor $\gamma$. A magnetic field has appeared, along $z$: at right angles to the electric field and to the motion, and smaller than the new electric field by the factor $\beta$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At low speed, $B_z' \approx -\beta E_y$ is small and $E_y' \approx E_y$: only an electric field, as everyday experience has it. The combination $E'^2 - B'^2 = \gamma^2E_y^2\left(1 - \beta^2\right) = E_y^2$ has the same value as in $S$, and it is positive, so the magnetic field can never become as large as the electric one, however fast $S'$ moves. The lesson's worked example uses this speed: for $\beta = 0.6$, $\gamma = 1.25$ and $B_z' = -0.75\,E_y$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

In what sense does the result of case (B) show that the electric and the magnetic field cannot be seen as two separate things?

<Hint>

Two observers look at the same field at the same place and time. What does each of them say is there?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Whether a field is "electric" or "magnetic" depends on who looks.

<Step title="The argument">

In case (B) the observer in $S$ says: there is an electric field and nothing else. The observer in $S'$ looks at the same place at the same moment and finds an electric field and a magnetic field. Both are right. If the magnetic field were a thing of its own, it would have to be there for both or for neither. So the question "is there a magnetic field here?" has no answer that holds for every observer.

What is the same for both is the matrix $M$ as a whole, which obeys one transformation rule, $M' = \Lambda M\Lambda^{-1}$. Its six independent entries are one object, the electromagnetic field. Splitting it into three numbers called $\vec E$ and three called $\vec B$ is like splitting a 4-vector into a space part and a time part: each observer does it differently.

</Step>

<Step title="Check: what the force does">

The force on a charge shows the same thing. A charge at rest in $S$ feels $qE_y$. Seen from $S'$ the same charge moves, and the force on it has an electric part and a magnetic part, $q\left(\vec E' + \frac1c\vec u' \times \vec B'\right)$, with $\vec u' = \left(-v, 0, 0\right)$. Its $y$ component is $q\left(\gamma E_y - \gamma\beta^2E_y\right) = qE_y/\gamma$: exactly the force $qE_y$ of $S$ transformed to a frame in which the charge moves (a force across the motion is smaller by $\gamma$ there). The magnetic part is needed for the two observers to agree on how the charge moves. That is the lesson's point: electricity and relativity together demand magnetism.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Suppose $\vec B = 0$ in $S$ and $\vec E$ is not zero. In which other frames is the magnetic field zero as well? In other words: in which directions can $S'$ move and still have no magnetic field?

<Hint>

The formulas of part (a) are for motion along $x$, but you are free to choose the axes. Put the $x$ axis along the motion of $S'$ and let $\vec E$ point in any direction.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="The magnetic field is also zero:" choices={["only in frames that move along the line of E, parallel or opposite to it", "only in frames that move at right angles to E", "in every frame: a change of frame cannot create a magnetic field", "in no other frame: any motion creates a magnetic field"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Choose the $x$ axis along the motion. A magnetic field appears unless $\vec E$ has no component across the motion.

<Step title="The condition">

Take any frame $S'$ and put the $x$ axis along its velocity. With $\vec B = 0$ the formulas give

<Formula label="Magnetic field made by the motion" tex="B_x' = 0, \qquad B_y' = \gamma\beta E_z, \qquad B_z' = -\gamma\beta E_y" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

For $\beta \ne 0$ these all vanish only if $E_y = E_z = 0$: the electric field must point along $x$, the direction of the motion. So the magnetic field stays zero in the frames that move along the line of $\vec E$, in either sense and at any speed, and in no others. In those frames the electric field is unchanged too (case (A)).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

</Step>

<Exercise id="p3" title="Colliding beams" points={6} mit="Problem 3">

(MIT took this problem from a textbook. The physics is MIT's: the energy that two colliding beams make available. The situation and the numbers are our own.)

In a collider, a beam of protons and a beam of antiprotons circle a ring in opposite directions and meet head-on. Each proton and each antiproton has the same rest mass $m_0$ and the same kinetic energy $K$ in the laboratory. When a proton and an antiproton annihilate, their rest energy and all their kinetic energy can go into new particles and into the kinetic energy of those particles. Call this the available energy $\epsilon$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Show that the available energy is

<Formula label="Available energy of two colliding beams" tex="\epsilon = 2m_0c^2\left(1 + \frac{K}{m_0c^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2" />

(MIT's sheet prints a small $k$ in this formula. It is the kinetic energy $K$ of the text.)

<Hint>

What is the total momentum of the two particles in the laboratory? What does that say about the energy that must stay kinetic after the collision?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two momenta cancel, so the laboratory is the frame of zero total momentum, and nothing has to keep moving afterwards.

<Step title="Total energy and momentum">

Each particle has the energy $E = m_0c^2 + K$. The momenta are equal and opposite, so the total momentum is zero and the total energy is $2\left(m_0c^2 + K\right)$. Momentum conservation asks only that the products have zero total momentum, which a single particle at rest satisfies, or several particles flying apart. So all of the total energy is free to become rest energy and kinetic energy of new particles:

<Formula label="Available energy, derived" tex="\epsilon = E_\text{tot} = 2\left(m_0c^2 + K\right) = 2m_0c^2\left(1 + \frac{K}{m_0c^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The invariant of the system gives the same: $\sqrt{E_\text{tot}^2 - c^2p_\text{tot}^2} = E_\text{tot}$ when $p_\text{tot} = 0$ ([Lecture 12](/courses/8-033/force-acceleration-interstellar-travel), slide 6). For $K = 0$ the formula gives $2m_0c^2$, the rest energy of the pair. The available energy grows in proportion to $K$: no energy is lost to the motion of the centre of mass. Problem 7 shows how different that is for a target at rest.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-pions" label="Largest number of pions:" value="9771" tolerance="4" calc="p6-pions" mistakes={[{ value: "10133", tolerance: "4", calc: "p6-neutral", hint: "That uses the neutral pion's 135.0 MeV. The statement says to take 140 MeV for every pion, as MIT does." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the centre-of-mass frame the products can all be at rest. Then all of $E^*$ is rest energy: two protons and $N$ pions.

<Step title="The energy in the centre-of-mass frame">

With $E = 10^{15}\ \text{eV} = 10^6$ GeV and $E_0 = 0.9383$ GeV:

<Formula label="Centre-of-mass energy of the cosmic-ray collision" tex="E^* = \sqrt{2 \times 0.9383 \times \left(10^6 + 0.9383\right)}\ \text{GeV} = 1369.89\ \text{GeV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The number of pions">

<Formula label="Largest number of pions" tex="N \le \frac{E^* - 2E_0}{140\ \text{MeV}} = \frac{\left(1369.89 - 1.88\right)\ \text{GeV}}{0.140\ \text{GeV}} = 9771.5" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The number of pions $N$ is a whole number, so the largest whole number is $N = 9771$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Of the million GeV that the cosmic ray brings, only 1370 GeV, a little over a thousandth, can make particles. The rest must remain kinetic energy, because the products have to carry the cosmic ray's momentum. Had all $10^6$ GeV been available, over seven million pions could be made. The answer does not depend on whether $10^{15}$ eV is read as the total or as the kinetic energy of the proton, nor on the last digits of the proton's rest energy (938.26 MeV gives 9771 too). It does depend on the pion's rest energy: with the 135.0 MeV of the neutral pion (Lecture 14, slide 11) the count would be 10133. Charge is no obstacle: neutral pions, or equal numbers of positive and negative ones, keep the charge at $+2$. In a real collision far fewer pions are made, because the products do not come out at rest.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="Creating a heavy particle: fixed target against colliding beams" points={6} mit="Problem 7">

Two protons collide and create a new particle with a rest energy of 300 GeV. The two protons are still there afterwards. Take the rest energy of a proton to be 1 GeV here, as MIT does. Find the threshold, the least energy at which this can happen, in the two cases below. Give the **total** energy (rest energy included) of one moving proton, in GeV.

<Part label="i" points={2}>

One proton is at rest before the collision. What total energy must the other have?

<Hint>

At threshold the three products are at rest in the centre-of-mass frame, so the energy $E^*$ there is the sum of the three rest energies. Problem 6(a) connects $E^*$ to the energy of the moving proton.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-fixed" label="$E =$" value="45601" unit="GeV" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-fixed" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At threshold the invariant of the system equals the square of the sum of the rest energies of the products.

<Step title="The threshold">

The products have rest energies $1 + 1 + 300 = 302$ GeV. With Problem 6(a), $2E_0\left(E + E_0\right) = \left(302\ \text{GeV}\right)^2$, so with $E_0 = 1$ GeV:

<Formula label="Threshold with a target at rest" tex="E = \frac{\left(302\ \text{GeV}\right)^2}{2 \times 1\ \text{GeV}} - 1\ \text{GeV} = 45602\ \text{GeV} - 1\ \text{GeV} = 45601\ \text{GeV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Putting it back: the total energy is 45602 GeV and $c^2p^2 = 45601^2 - 1$, so $E^{*2} = 45602^2 - 45601^2 + 1 = 91203 + 1 = 91204 = 302^2$ (in GeV²). The kinetic energy of the moving proton is 45600 GeV: 152 times the rest energy of the particle that is to be made.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="ii" points={2}>

Both protons have the same energy and collide head-on. What total energy must each have? Here the box accepts only 0.3%, so that it can tell the total energy from the kinetic energy.

<Hint>

The total momentum is zero, so the laboratory is the centre-of-mass frame. Use Problem 3.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-collider" label="$E =$" value="151" unit="GeV" tolerance="0.3%" calc="p7-collider" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With zero total momentum everything can be at rest afterwards, so the two protons need only bring the rest energies of the products.

<Step title="The threshold">

<Formula label="Threshold with colliding beams" tex="2E = 302\ \text{GeV}, \qquad E = 151\ \text{GeV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Each proton has a kinetic energy of 150 GeV: half the rest energy of the new particle.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two answers are linked by a change of frame. A proton with total energy 151 GeV has $\gamma = 151$. Seen from the other proton, which moves the opposite way with the same $\gamma$, it has $\gamma' = \gamma^2\left(1 + \beta^2\right) = 2\gamma^2 - 1 = 45601$: the answer of part (i).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="iii" points={2}>

Compare the two results. What do they say about accelerators with colliding beams against accelerators that shoot at a target at rest?

<Hint>

Compare the kinetic energy that the machine must give to the protons in the two cases. How does each grow with the rest energy of the particle to be made?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** With a target at rest most of the energy is wasted on the motion of the centre of mass. With colliding beams none is.

<Step title="The comparison">

To make the 300 GeV particle, colliding beams need 150 GeV of kinetic energy per proton, 300 GeV in all. A beam on a target at rest needs 45600 GeV: 152 times as much in all, and 304 times as much per accelerated proton. Of those 45600 GeV only 300 GeV end up as the new particle. The rest must stay kinetic energy of the three products, which have to carry the beam's momentum.

</Step>

<Step title="How it scales">

For a new particle of rest energy $Mc^2$ much larger than the proton's $E_0$, colliding beams need a beam energy of about $Mc^2/2$, in proportion to $M$. A fixed target needs about $\left(Mc^2\right)^2/2E_0$, in proportion to $M^2$. So the heavier the particle one hopes to find, the larger the advantage of colliding beams. That is one reason why the largest machines, such as the ring at CERN shown in the lesson, collide two beams. (A fixed target has other advantages, which this comparison leaves out: a solid target holds far more protons than a thin beam, so collisions are much more frequent.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 11 (optional)

<Exercise id="p11" title="A relativistic elastic collision" points={5} mit="Optional Problem 11" optional>

MIT's optional Problems 8, 9 and 10 are textbook references and are left out. This is MIT's optional Problem 11.

A particle of rest mass $M_0$ moves along the $x$ axis of the frame $S$ with speed $\beta_0c$ and Lorentz factor $\gamma_0$. It hits an identical particle at rest. The collision is elastic: the same two particles come out, with unchanged rest masses. Afterwards the two particles are seen to move at the angles $+\theta$ and $-\theta$ to the $x$ axis.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

Show that the centre-of-mass frame $S'$ moves relative to $S$ with

<Formula label="Speed and Lorentz factor of the centre-of-mass frame" tex="\beta_\text{CM} = \frac{\gamma_0\beta_0}{\gamma_0 + 1}, \qquad \gamma_\text{CM} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma_0 + 1}{2}}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 11(a), eq. (11.1), p. 2" />

<Hint>

MIT's hint: use $\beta_\text{CM} = p_\text{tot}c/E_\text{tot}$, the velocity formula of the lesson applied to the whole system.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A system moves as a whole with $\beta = cp/E$ of its total momentum and energy.

<Step title="The speed">

The total momentum is that of the moving particle, $\gamma_0\beta_0M_0c$. The total energy is $\gamma_0M_0c^2 + M_0c^2$. So

<Formula label="Speed of the centre of mass, derived" tex="\beta_\text{CM} = \frac{cp_\text{tot}}{E_\text{tot}} = \frac{\gamma_0\beta_0M_0c^2}{\left(\gamma_0 + 1\right)M_0c^2} = \frac{\gamma_0\beta_0}{\gamma_0 + 1}" cite="reading1" at="slide 3 (β = cp/E; the application is our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The Lorentz factor">

With $\gamma_0^2\beta_0^2 = \gamma_0^2 - 1 = \left(\gamma_0 - 1\right)\left(\gamma_0 + 1\right)$:

<Formula label="Lorentz factor of the centre of mass, derived" tex="1 - \beta_\text{CM}^2 = \frac{\left(\gamma_0 + 1\right)^2 - \left(\gamma_0 - 1\right)\left(\gamma_0 + 1\right)}{\left(\gamma_0 + 1\right)^2} = \frac{2}{\gamma_0 + 1}, \qquad \gamma_\text{CM} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma_0 + 1}{2}}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 11(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a slow particle, $\gamma_0 \to 1$, $\beta_\text{CM} \to \beta_0/2$: the centre of mass of two equal masses moves at half the speed, as in Newton's mechanics. For a very fast one, $\beta_\text{CM} \to 1$. The result agrees with Problem 6(a): $E^* = E_\text{tot}/\gamma_\text{CM} = \left(\gamma_0 + 1\right)M_0c^2\sqrt{2/\left(\gamma_0 + 1\right)} = \sqrt{2\left(\gamma_0 + 1\right)}\,M_0c^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

Argue that before the collision the two particles have the same energy and the same size of momentum in the centre-of-mass frame, and that $\beta_\text{CM}$ and $\gamma_\text{CM}$ give both.

<Hint>

One of the two particles is at rest in $S$. How does it move in a frame that moves with $\beta_\text{CM}$ relative to $S$?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The target is easy: it is at rest in $S$, so in $S'$ it moves with the speed of $S'$, backwards.

<Step title="The argument">

In $S'$ the target moves with speed $\beta_\text{CM}c$ along $-x'$. Its energy is $\gamma_\text{CM}M_0c^2$ and its momentum has the size $\gamma_\text{CM}\beta_\text{CM}M_0c$. The total momentum in $S'$ is zero by the definition of that frame, so the other particle has a momentum of the same size along $+x'$. The two particles have the same rest mass, and the energy follows from momentum and rest mass, $E = \sqrt{\left(M_0c^2\right)^2 + \left(cp\right)^2}$, so the energies are equal too:

<Formula label="Both particles in the centre-of-mass frame" tex="E' = \gamma_\text{CM}M_0c^2, \qquad p' = \gamma_\text{CM}\beta_\text{CM}M_0c" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 11(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The velocity addition rule gives the same for the moving particle: its speed in $S'$ is $\left(\beta_0 - \beta_\text{CM}\right)/\left(1 - \beta_0\beta_\text{CM}\right)$, which equals $\beta_\text{CM}$. For example $\gamma_0 = 7$ gives $\beta_0 = 0.98974$, $\beta_\text{CM} = 0.86603$ and $\left(0.98974 - 0.86603\right)/\left(1 - 0.98974 \times 0.86603\right) = 0.8660$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

Argue that after the collision both particles move at right angles to the $x'$ axis in the centre-of-mass frame.

<Hint>

In $S$ the two particles leave at $+\theta$ and $-\theta$. Use momentum conservation across the $x$ axis to compare their momenta, and then their energies.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The symmetric angles make the two particles equal in every way along $x$. In $S'$ their $x'$ momenta must also be opposite. Both can hold only if they are zero.

<Step title="The argument in S">

The total momentum across the $x$ axis is zero before the collision, so afterwards $p_{y}$ of one particle is minus that of the other. Their directions make the same angle with the $x$ axis, so $p_x = \left|p_y\right|/\tan\theta$ is the same for both. With equal momentum sizes and equal rest masses they have equal energies $E$.

</Step>

<Step title="The argument in S'">

The momentum along the motion transforms as $p_x' = \gamma_\text{CM}\left(p_x - \beta_\text{CM}E/c\right)$. Equal $p_x$ and equal $E$ give equal $p_x'$ for the two particles. But the total momentum in $S'$ is zero, so the two $p_x'$ are also opposite. A number equal to its own negative is zero: $p_x' = 0$ for both. All their momentum in $S'$ is along $\pm y'$, at right angles to the $x'$ axis.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In $S'$ an elastic collision of equal particles only turns the two opposite momenta without changing their size (energy conservation with unchanged rest masses). The turn can be through any angle. The observation of equal angles in $S$ picks out the turn through 90°.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={1}>

For each particle after the collision, find $p_x$ and $p_y$ in the frame $S$, in terms of $\beta_\text{CM}$ and $\gamma_\text{CM}$ (and $M_0$, $c$).

<Hint>

In $S'$ each particle has $p_x' = 0$, $p_y' = \pm p'$ and the energy $E'$ of part (b). Transform back to $S$, which moves with $-\beta_\text{CM}$ relative to $S'$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Transform the 4-momentum of part (c) back to $S$. The component across the motion does not change.

<Step title="The transformation">

<Formula label="Momenta after the collision, in S" tex="p_x = \gamma_\text{CM}\left(p_x' + \beta_\text{CM}\frac{E'}{c}\right) = \gamma_\text{CM}^2\beta_\text{CM}M_0c, \qquad p_y = p_y' = \pm\gamma_\text{CM}\beta_\text{CM}M_0c" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 11(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two particles together have $p_x = 2\gamma_\text{CM}^2\beta_\text{CM}M_0c$. With part (a), $2\gamma_\text{CM}^2 = \gamma_0 + 1$ and $\beta_\text{CM} = \gamma_0\beta_0/\left(\gamma_0 + 1\right)$, so this is $\gamma_0\beta_0M_0c$: the momentum of the incoming particle. Momentum is conserved. Each particle has the energy $\gamma_\text{CM}E' = \gamma_\text{CM}^2M_0c^2 = \tfrac12\left(\gamma_0 + 1\right)M_0c^2$, half of the total. Energy is conserved too.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={1}>

Work out $\tan\theta = p_y/p_x$ and show that $\tan\theta = 1/\gamma_\text{CM} = \sqrt{2/\left(\gamma_0 + 1\right)}$. Then a number, which is our own addition: for $\gamma_0 = 7$, find the angle between the directions of the two outgoing particles, in degrees.

<Hint>

The angle between the two particles is $2\theta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-angle" label="Angle between the two particles:" value="53.13" unit="°" tolerance="1%" calc="p11-angle" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Divide the two results of part (d).

<Step title="The angle">

<Formula label="Angle of the outgoing particles" tex="\tan\theta = \frac{p_y}{p_x} = \frac{\gamma_\text{CM}\beta_\text{CM}M_0c}{\gamma_\text{CM}^2\beta_\text{CM}M_0c} = \frac{1}{\gamma_\text{CM}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\gamma_0 + 1}}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 11(e), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

For $\gamma_0 = 7$, $\gamma_\text{CM} = \sqrt{8/2} = 2$ and $\tan\theta = \tfrac12$, so $\theta = 26.57°$ and the angle between the two particles is $2\theta = 53.13°$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At low speed, $\gamma_0 \to 1$, $\tan\theta \to 1$ and the two particles leave at 45° each, 90° apart: the familiar right angle between two equal billiard balls after a collision. In relativity the angle between them is always less than 90° and closes towards zero as $\gamma_0$ grows. Tracks of fast particles show this narrowing.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
