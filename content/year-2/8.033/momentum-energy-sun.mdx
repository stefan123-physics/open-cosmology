Lecture 10 moves from kinematics to dynamics: fixing (generalizing) the momentum and energy of 8.01, the momentum 4-vector, kinetic energy, rest energy and the equivalence of mass and energy, and "the three unifications" (slide 4). The second half applies $E = mc^2$ to the Sun. (MIT's material for this lecture is a set of slides; the momentum and energy formulas are listed as topics but not written out on them, so this page gives them in its own words. The slides' title line says "Fall 2005", a leftover from the year before.)

## Fixing Newton's momentum

In 8.01, momentum $m\vec u$ is conserved in collisions. That law cannot survive in relativity as it stands: a collision that conserves $m\vec u$ in one inertial frame does not in another, because velocities no longer add simply. Since the laws of physics must be the same in every inertial frame, momentum has to be redefined (slide 4).

The natural candidate is a 4-vector, which transforms with the same Lorentz matrix in every frame. Multiply the [velocity 4-vector](/courses/8-033/four-vectors-doppler-aberration) $U = \gamma(u_x, u_y, u_z, c)$ by the mass:

<Formula label="Momentum 4-vector" tex="P = mU = \left(\gamma m u_x, \gamma m u_y, \gamma m u_z, \gamma m c\right) = \left(p_x, p_y, p_z, \frac{E}{c}\right)" cite="mit" at="slide 4 (topic only; the formula is the page's own)" />

so that

<Formula label="Momentum and energy" tex="\vec p = \gamma m\vec u, \qquad E = \gamma mc^2" cite="mit" at="slide 4 (topic only; the formula is the page's own)" />

If the four components of $P$ add up to the same totals before and after a collision in one frame, they do so in every frame, because $\Lambda$ is linear. At low speed $\vec p \approx m\vec u$, so 8.01 is the slow limit.

## Energy, rest energy and kinetic energy

Expanding $\gamma$ for small $u$ shows what the time component means (slide 4):

<Formula label="Rest energy plus kinetic energy" tex="E = \gamma mc^2 = mc^2 + \left(\gamma - 1\right)mc^2 \approx mc^2 + \frac12 mu^2" cite="mit" at="slide 4 (topic only; the formula is the page's own)" />

The second term is the kinetic energy, Newton's $\frac12 mu^2$ at low speed; the first, $mc^2$, is energy a body has at rest. Mass is a form of energy, and in reactions mass can turn into other forms of energy and back.

The length of $P$ is invariant, like that of every 4-vector. Since $U^t\eta U = -c^2$,

<Formula label="Energy–momentum relation" tex="P^t\eta P = p^2 - \frac{E^2}{c^2} = -m^2c^2, \qquad\text{so}\qquad E^2 = \left(pc\right)^2 + \left(mc^2\right)^2" cite="mit" at="slide 4 (topic only; the formula is the page's own)" />

A massless particle, such as a photon, has $E = pc$.

**The three unifications** (slide 4): space and time merge into spacetime; energy and momentum merge into one 4-vector; and energy and mass become one quantity, $E = mc^2$.

## The Sun

The second half of the lecture applies this to the Sun (slides 5–18). Its basic properties (slide 7): distance 1 AU, mass $2 \times 10^{30}$ kg, radius about 100 times the Earth's (more precisely 109), density similar to water's, and luminosity $4 \times 10^{26}$ W. By mass it is about 70% hydrogen and 28% helium, with small amounts of oxygen, carbon and iron (slide 8). We know this from the dark absorption lines in its spectrum, which are formed in the photosphere (slide 9).

Within living memory, the questions were open: what is the Sun, why does it shine, and why for so long (slide 10)? No chemical fuel could keep it shining for billions of years. The answer is nuclear fusion in its core (slide 11).

**Why fusion needs the core** (slide 12). Slow nuclei never touch: their electric charges repel them first. Only very fast ones get near enough to each other for the short-range strong nuclear force to take over and hold them. Only the hot core has nuclei fast enough. Fusion builds heavier nuclei from lighter ones; fission, the reverse, splits heavy nuclei into lighter ones. In 2006, the slide notes, humans had achieved controlled fission but not controlled fusion, because "it is easier to destroy than to create" (slide 13). (A later development: laboratory fusion experiments have since released more energy than the laser light delivered to the fuel, at the National Ignition Facility in 2022, but there is still no fusion power plant.)

**Neutrons and neutrinos** (slide 14). A neutron on its own does not last: it decays within minutes into a proton. The slide pairs this with the opposite conversion of a proton into a neutron, which it calls inverse β-decay:

<Formula label="Beta decay and inverse beta decay" tex="n \to p^+ + e^- + \bar\nu_e, \qquad p^+ \to n + e^+ + \nu_e" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

The $\nu_e$ is a neutrino: it interacts only through the weak force, its mass is tiny, and it moves almost at $c$. (Not on the slide: the second reaction is today usually called β⁺ decay, while "inverse beta decay" more often means $\bar\nu_e + p^+ \to n + e^+$. It needs an energy input, so a free proton is stable; in the Sun it happens when two protons fuse into deuterium.)

**The proton–proton chain** (slide 15). In: six hydrogen nuclei (and two electrons). Out: one helium nucleus, two hydrogen nuclei, two neutrinos and gamma rays. Effectively four hydrogen nuclei become one helium nucleus, and energy is released.

**Why the Sun shines** (slide 16). The rest mass of a helium nucleus is only 99.3% of four times the rest mass of hydrogen. The missing $0.007 \times 4\,m_H$ has gone into other forms of energy:

<Formula label="Mass-energy equivalence" tex="E = mc^2" cite="mit" at="slide 16" />

**Seeing inside** (slides 17–18). The Sun's interior is opaque, so we cannot see into it with light. Instead, computer models give temperature, pressure and density against depth from known physics, and are tested against what can be observed. "Sunquakes", sound waves seen through Doppler shifts at the surface, probe the interior and check the models; and neutrinos, which escape straight from the core, let us see into it directly.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Mass and energy in the early universe">
In the early universe the same equivalence runs the other way: when the temperature is high enough, $kT \gtrsim mc^2$, radiation creates particle–antiparticle pairs out of energy, and the universe's [mass-energy density](/courses/8-286/mass-energy-and-radiation) includes all of them. The first helium was fused not in stars but in the first few minutes, by the same release of rest mass.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Burning the Sun">
<Problem>
(a) How much energy is released when four hydrogen nuclei ($m_H = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}$ kg each) become helium? (b) How much mass does the Sun lose each second as light? (c) If the Sun can burn 10% of its hydrogen, which is 70% of its mass, how long can it shine at its present luminosity?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) One helium nucleus">
$E = 0.007 \times 4 \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27} \times (3.00 \times 10^8)^2 \approx 4.2 \times 10^{-12}$ J, about 26 MeV.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Mass lost as light">
$L/c^2 = 4 \times 10^{26}/(9.0 \times 10^{16}) \approx 4.4 \times 10^9$ kg per second.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The Sun's lifetime">
Each kilogram of hydrogen burned gives $0.007\,c^2 \approx 6.3 \times 10^{14}$ J. The fuel is $0.1 \times 0.7 \times 2 \times 10^{30} = 1.4 \times 10^{29}$ kg, worth $8.8 \times 10^{43}$ J; divided by $4 \times 10^{26}$ W, that is $2.2 \times 10^{17}$ s, or about 7 billion years.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Newton's momentum is replaced by the momentum 4-vector $P = mU$, with $\vec p = \gamma m\vec u$ and $E = \gamma mc^2$; its components are conserved in every frame.
- $E = mc^2 + (\gamma - 1)mc^2$: rest energy plus kinetic energy; $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$ in every frame.
- The Sun shines by fusing hydrogen into helium, whose rest mass is 0.7% less; neutrinos and sunquakes let us look inside.
