After the course overview, Lecture 2 opens with a question it calls the million-dollar question: what are the symmetries of physics (slide 2)? The course is built on the answer. Its key ideas are frames, inertial frames, transformations and invariance; its key people are Galileo and Emmy Noether (slides 1–2), whose theorem, not on the slides, ties symmetries to conservation laws. (MIT's material for this course is a set of lecture slides; this page follows their order and fills in the reasoning between them.)

## What is a symmetry?

A **transformation** changes the coordinates we use to describe the world: shifting, turning or mirroring the lab, or describing it from a moving train. A quantity is **invariant** if it has the same value after the transformation, and a law is invariant if it keeps the same form. A **symmetry** of physics is a transformation under which the laws are invariant (slides 2–4).

**Translation** (slide 5). No experiment inside a closed lab can tell whether the lab has been shifted. Newton's gravity between masses at $\vec r_1$ and $\vec r_2$ is unchanged when both are moved by the same $\vec a$:

<Formula label="Gravity under translation" tex="F' = \frac{GmM}{|\vec r_2' - \vec r_1'|^2} = \frac{GmM}{|(\vec r_2 + \vec a) - (\vec r_1 + \vec a)|^2} = \frac{GmM}{|\vec r_2 - \vec r_1|^2} = F" cite="mit" at="slide 5" />

Only differences of positions enter, so the law is translationally invariant.

**Rotation** (slide 6). No experiment inside a spaceship can tell whether it has been rotated. With $\vec r_i' = R\vec r_i$ for a rotation matrix $R$, distances are unchanged, $|R(\vec r_2 - \vec r_1)| = |\vec r_2 - \vec r_1|$, and so is gravity. Maxwell's equations in vacuum give the wave equation $\nabla^2\vec E = \frac{1}{c^2}\,\partial^2\vec E/\partial t^2$, and $\nabla^2$ is also unchanged by rotations. A field pointing "up" at MIT points "down" near Australia, but both observers agree that Maxwell was right (slide 6).

**Reflection** (slide 7). For all of classical physics, a mirror image of a possible process is also possible, and this was considered self-evident. In 1956 Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen Ning Yang proposed that the weak interactions violate it; Chien-Shiung Wu showed it with cobalt-60, and Leon Lederman with an accelerator. Lee and Yang received the 1957 Nobel Prize.

Symmetry is at the heart of modern physics (slide 8): special relativity is about Lorentz symmetry, general relativity about symmetry under any smooth change of coordinates, and particle physics about C, P and T symmetries and gauge symmetry. (The slides of 2006 add that the Large Hadron Collider would soon search for supersymmetry; it began work in 2008–2010, and as of the mid-2020s no supersymmetric particles had been found.)

## Galilean boosts

Colliding carts and a ball, the slides suggest, behave the same in a lab moving at constant velocity (slide 10). An **inertial frame** is one in which a body with no force on it does not accelerate, $\vec a = 0$ if $\vec F = 0$. An **event** is a point in space and time, $(x, y, z, t)$. Between two inertial frames, the second moving at $\vec v$ relative to the first, the **Galilean transformation** is (slide 10)

<Formula label="Galilean transformation" tex="\vec r' = \vec r - \vec vt, \qquad t' = t" cite="mit" at="slide 10" />

Lengths are invariant, $\Delta\vec r' = \vec r_2' - \vec r_1' = (\vec r_2 - \vec vt) - (\vec r_1 - \vec vt) = \Delta\vec r$, provided $\vec r_1'$ and $\vec r_2'$ are measured at the same time, and that is unambiguous, because time itself is invariant (slide 10).

All the symmetries so far combine into one (slide 11):

<Formula label="Spacetime transformations" tex="\text{translation: } \vec r' = \vec r + \Delta\vec r,\ t' = t + \Delta t; \quad \text{rotation: } \vec r' = R\vec r,\ t' = t; \quad \text{combined: } \vec r' = R\vec r + \Delta\vec r + \vec vt,\ t' = t + \Delta t" cite="mit" at="slide 11" />

(Slides 11 and 15 write the boost with $+\vec vt$, which describes the other frame moving at $-\vec v$; slide 10 uses $-\vec vt$. Only the sign convention differs.)

## What changes and what does not

**Velocity and acceleration** (slide 12). Differentiating:

<Formula label="Velocity and acceleration" tex="\vec u' = \frac{d\vec r'}{dt'} = \frac{d}{dt}(\vec r - \vec vt) = \vec u - \vec v, \qquad \vec a' = \frac{d\vec u'}{dt'} = \frac{d}{dt}(\vec u - \vec v) = \vec a" cite="mit" at="slide 12" />

Velocities add and subtract as expected, and acceleration is invariant. (Slide 12 writes $\frac{d}{dt}(\vec u + \vec v)$ in the second step, against its own $\vec u' = \vec u - \vec v$; since $\vec v$ is constant the result is the same.) But what about a flashlight on a train: does its light move at $c$ plus the train's speed (slide 12)? That is the next lecture.

**$\vec F = m\vec a$** (slide 13). Forces that depend on separations, like a spring, $F = k(x_2 - x_1)$, or gravity, are invariant, since lengths are; $m$ is invariant; $\vec a$ is invariant. So $\vec F = m\vec a$ has the same form in every inertial frame: the physical law is invariant, though the initial conditions are not.

**Energy and momentum** (slide 14). Neither is invariant, since $\vec v$ is not; but their conservation laws hold in every frame. The **work-energy theorem** follows from $F = ma$ alone:

<Formula label="Work-energy theorem" tex="W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2}F\,dx = \int_{x_1}^{x_2}ma\,dx = m\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{dv}{dt}\,dx = m\int_{v_1}^{v_2}v\,dv = \frac{mv_2^2}{2} - \frac{mv_1^2}{2} = \Delta KE" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

so it too is invariant, even though $W$ and $KE$ on their own are not.

**Trajectories** (slide 15). A basketball dropped straight down in one frame follows a parabola in another. The key non-invariants of a Galilean boost (slide 15):

<Formula label="Galilean non-invariants" tex="\vec r' = \vec r + \vec vt, \qquad \vec u' = \vec u + \vec v, \qquad \vec p' = \vec p + m\vec v" cite="mit" at="slide 15" />

(in the slides' convention). So classical mechanics is invariant under Galilean transformations. And electromagnetism? "Which do you trust more: classical mechanics, or E&M?" (slide 16).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="No centre to the universe">
In an expanding universe every galaxy sees the others receding with speeds proportional to distance. A [Galilean change of velocity](/courses/8-286/homogeneous-expansion) to any other galaxy's frame gives exactly the same picture: Hubble's law has the symmetry that no galaxy is special, which is why the expansion has no centre.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Work in two frames">
<Problem>
On a train moving at $30$ m/s, a constant force speeds a $0.50$ kg ball from rest to $10$ m/s (relative to the train) in a time $T$, along the direction of travel. Find the work and the change of kinetic energy in the train frame and in the ground frame.
</Problem>
<Step title="Train frame">
$\Delta KE = \frac12(0.50)(10^2) - 0 = 25$ J. The acceleration is $10/T$, the force $F = 0.50 \times 10/T = 5/T$ newtons, and the ball moves $\frac12\cdot\frac{10}{T}\cdot T^2 = 5T$ metres, so $W = (5/T)(5T) = 25$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Ground frame">
The speed goes from 30 to 40 m/s: $\Delta KE = \frac12(0.50)(40^2 - 30^2) = 175$ J. The force is the same, but the ball now moves $5T + 30T = 35T$ metres, so $W = (5/T)(35T) = 175$ J. Work and kinetic energy differ between the frames, but $W = \Delta KE$ holds in both.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A symmetry is a transformation under which the laws keep their form: translation, rotation, reflection (broken by the weak interaction) and boosts.
- Galilean transformation: $\vec r' = \vec r - \vec vt$, $t' = t$. Time, simultaneous lengths, acceleration, mass and $\vec F = m\vec a$ are invariant; velocities, momentum, energy and trajectories are not.
- Laws such as $W = \Delta KE$ stay valid in every frame even when the quantities in them change.
- Classical mechanics is Galilean invariant; whether electromagnetism is, is the next question.
