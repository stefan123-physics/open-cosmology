## Supermassive black holes

The slides list the evidence for supermassive black holes in the centres of most, perhaps all, galaxies (slides 10–18):

- **quasars and huge jets**, such as the jet shooting out of the active nucleus of the galaxy M87 (slides 11–12);
- **stellar motions**, implying central masses of $10^6$ to $10^9$ solar masses (slides 13–14);
- **orbiting gas disks**, showing that the mass sits within less than 0.4 light years, too small to be a cluster of stars (slide 15);
- **a devoured star**, which the slides use to limit the size of the central object to less than 0.4 AU (slides 15–17). (Slide 17 labels its picture "star strays too close to Sagittarius A*"; the NASA image, an illustration with Chandra X-ray and ESO optical panels, actually shows a star torn apart in 2004 in the distant galaxy RX J1242-11, about 700 million light years away. Our own galaxy's black hole, Sagittarius A*, is tightly constrained by the orbits of nearby stars.)
- **X-ray spectra**, which reveal disks extending in to between 6 and 20 $M$ (in units with $c = G = 1$), with different line shapes for spinning and non-spinning holes (slides 15 and 18).

## Stellar-mass black holes

The evidence for black holes of a few solar masses comes after the review and the orbit simulator on the slides (slides 28–30), but is gathered here:

- stars orbiting a massive invisible companion;
- the maximum mass of a neutron star is about 3 solar masses (a conservative theoretical bound; the heaviest neutron stars measured since have about 2), so a heavier compact companion must be a black hole;
- the best example is V404 Cygni, whose companion has $12 \pm 2$ solar masses (the 2006 estimate; later measurements give about 9); an older example is Cygnus X-1;
- X-ray variability puts an upper limit on the size of the source;
- and the X-ray emission shows no sign of a "surface": matter falling onto a neutron star flares when it hits, matter falling into a black hole simply disappears (slide 30 contrasts the two).

## Orbits in the Schwarzschild metric

The third topic, orbital equations (slide 9; slide 27 was a Java orbit simulator), has no slides of its own in this deck; the equations appear on slide 4 of the next lecture's slides. In outline (the page's own, with $c = G = 1$): the Schwarzschild metric does not depend on $t$ or $\varphi$, so a free particle's energy per unit mass $E$ and angular momentum per unit mass $L$ are conserved, as in the [calculus of variations](/courses/8-033/geodesics-calculus-of-variations) when the Lagrangian lacks a variable. For an orbit in the plane $\theta = \pi/2$, the metric then gives

<Formula label="Radial equation for orbits" tex="\left(\frac{dr}{d\tau}\right)^2 = E^2 - \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)\left(1 + \frac{L^2}{r^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="slide 9 (topic; the equation is the page's own)" />

The whole product on the right, $(1 - 2M/r)(1 + L^2/r^2)$, acts as an effective potential. Far from the hole it reproduces Newton's orbits; close in, an extra attraction, proportional to $ML^2/r^3$, appears. As a result, circular orbits are stable only outside

<Formula label="Innermost stable circular orbit" tex="r_{\text{ISCO}} = 6M = \frac{6GM}{c^2}" cite="mit" at="slides 11 and 15 (disks reaching in to 6–20M; the formula is the page's own)" />

which is why a gas disk around a non-spinning black hole stops near $6M$: inside, gas plunges in. Around a spinning hole the disk can reach closer, which the X-ray spectra on slide 18 distinguish.

<WorkedExample title="Sizes and periods near black holes">
<Problem>
(a) An X-ray source flickers on a timescale of 1 ms. How large can it be? (b) For a black hole of 10 solar masses ($GM = 1.327 \times 10^{21}$ m³/s²), where is the innermost stable circular orbit, and what is the orbital period there (using Kepler's law, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{r^3/GM}$, which holds exactly in Schwarzschild time for circular orbits)? (c) Compare the slides' size limit of 0.4 AU with the Schwarzschild radius of a 4-million-solar-mass black hole, like the one at the centre of our galaxy, $1.18 \times 10^{10}$ m (1 AU $= 1.496 \times 10^{11}$ m).
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Light-crossing time">
$c\Delta t = 3.00 \times 10^8 \times 10^{-3} = 300$ km at most.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The innermost orbit">
$r = 6GM/c^2 \approx 88.6$ km, and $T = 2\pi\sqrt{(8.86 \times 10^4)^3/1.327 \times 10^{21}} \approx 4.5$ ms: matter there circles hundreds of times a second, matching the fast X-ray flickering.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A size limit">
$1.18 \times 10^{10}$ m is about 0.079 AU, so a limit of 0.4 AU is only about five Schwarzschild radii: no room for anything but a black hole.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Supermassive black holes of $10^6$ to $10^9$ solar masses sit in galaxy centres: quasars and jets, stellar and gas motions, a star torn apart by a central black hole, and X-ray spectra of disks reaching in to 6–20 $M$.
- Stellar-mass black holes are invisible companions heavier than any neutron star (about 3 solar masses), such as V404 Cygni and Cygnus X-1.
- In the Schwarzschild metric, circular orbits are stable only outside $6GM/c^2$, where accretion disks end.
