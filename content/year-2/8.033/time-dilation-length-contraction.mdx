The [last lecture](/courses/8-033/lorentz-transformation) derived the Lorentz transformation. Is it right, and what does it imply (slides 1–3)? Today: time dilation, length contraction, the relativity of simultaneity, and proper time and rest length (slide 2).

## Time dilation

A clock at rest at the origin of $S$ ticks at $t = 0$ and $t = \Delta t$. Transforming the two ticks to $S'$, moving at $v$ along $x$ (slide 4):

<Formula label="Two ticks in the moving frame" tex="\vec x_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \vec x_1' = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec x_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ c\Delta t \end{pmatrix}, \quad \vec x_2' = \begin{pmatrix} -\gamma v\Delta t \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ \gamma c\Delta t \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="slide 4" />

So in $S'$ the clock ticks at intervals $\Delta t' = \gamma\Delta t > \Delta t$: it runs **slow** (slide 4). The slides point to a light-clock movie (slides 5–6); in words (the page's own explanation), a light clock, a pulse bouncing between two mirrors, shows it directly: seen from a frame where the clock moves, the light travels a longer diagonal path at the same speed $c$ (slides 4–5).

**The cosmic-ray muon puzzle** (slide 6). Muons are created by cosmic rays about 10 km above the ground. Their half-life is $1.56 \times 10^{-6}$ s, in which even light travels only 0.47 km. How can they reach the ground? Because their clocks run slow: at $v \approx 0.99c$, $\gamma \approx 7$, and at $0.9999c$, $\gamma \approx 71$. Taken further, this leads to the twin paradox (slide 6).

## Relativity of simultaneity

Two events simultaneous in $S$, at $x = 0$ and $x = L$ at $t = 0$, become in $S'$ (slide 9)

<Formula label="Simultaneous events in another frame" tex="\vec x_1' = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec x_2' = \begin{pmatrix} \gamma L \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ -\gamma\beta L \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="slide 9" />

So in $S'$ the second event happened first. Clocks synchronized in $S$ look unsynchronized in $S'$: those at larger $x$ run further ahead (slide 9). The inverse transformation checks out: $\Lambda(-v)\Lambda(v)$ is the identity, so $\Lambda(-v) = \Lambda(v)^{-1}$ (slide 10).

## Length contraction

How long does a yardstick at rest in $S$ look in $S'$: longer, shorter, or a yard (slides 7–8)? A naive argument says longer: at $t = 0$ the transformation gives $x' = \gamma x$, with $\gamma > 1$ (slide 7). The answer is "trickier than time dilation, opposite result": shorter (slide 11). The ends of a stick of length $L$ at rest along $x$ trace the world lines $(0, 0, 0, ct)$ and $(L, 0, 0, ct)$; in $S'$ they become $(-\gamma\beta ct, 0, 0, \gamma ct)$ and $(\gamma L - \gamma\beta ct, 0, 0, \gamma ct - \gamma\beta L)$ (slide 11). Eliminating $t$ gives the world lines of the ends in $S'$ (slide 12):

<Formula label="Ends of the stick in the moving frame" tex="x_1' = -vt_1', \qquad x_2' = \gamma L - \beta(ct_2' + \gamma\beta L) = \frac{L}{\gamma} - vt_2', \qquad x_2' - x_1' = \frac{L}{\gamma} + v(t_1' - t_2') = \frac{L}{\gamma}" cite="mit" at="slide 12" />

measuring both ends at the same time, $t_1' = t_2'$. The key point: an observer in $S'$ measures the length as $x_2' - x_1'$ at the same time $t'$, not at the same time $t$ (slide 13). At $t' = 0$: $t_1' = 0$ when $t = 0$, where $x_1' = 0$; $t_2' = 0$ when $ct = \beta L$, where $x_2' = \gamma L - \gamma\beta^2L = L/\gamma$. So in $S'$ the measured length is $L' = L/\gamma$: shorter (slide 13).

## The toolbox revisited

**Velocity addition** (slide 14), simpler with $2 \times 2$ matrices for $(x, ct)$: if $S'$ moves at $v_1$ relative to $S$, and $S''$ at $v_2$ relative to $S'$, then $\Lambda(v_3) = \Lambda(v_2)\Lambda(v_1)$. Multiplying out and taking the ratio of the $(1, 4)$ and $(1, 1)$ entries:

<Formula label="Adding parallel velocities" tex="\beta_3 = -\frac{\Lambda(v_3)_{14}}{\Lambda(v_3)_{11}} = \frac{\beta_1 + \beta_2}{1 + \beta_1\beta_2}, \qquad v_3 = \frac{v_1 + v_2}{1 + \frac{v_1v_2}{c^2}}" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

**Perpendicular components** (slides 15–16). For a particle with velocity $\vec u'$ in $S'$, the inverse transformation applied to two nearby points on its world line gives $dx = \gamma(dx' + v\,dt')$, $dy = dy'$, $dz = dz'$, $dt = \gamma(dt' + v\,dx'/c^2)$, and dividing,

<Formula label="Velocity components" tex="u_x = \frac{u_x' + v}{1 + \frac{u_x'v}{c^2}}, \qquad u_y = \frac{u_y'\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}{1 + \frac{u_x'v}{c^2}}, \qquad u_z = \frac{u_z'\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}{1 + \frac{u_x'v}{c^2}}" cite="mit" at="slide 16" />

**Boosts as rotations** (slides 17–18). A rotation about the $z$-axis mixes $x$ and $y$ with $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$; a boost mixes $x$ and $ct$ with $\cosh\eta$ and $\sinh\eta$, where $\eta = \tanh^{-1}\beta$ is the **rapidity**. With the hyperbolic identities, such as $\cosh(\tanh^{-1}x) = 1/\sqrt{1 - x^2}$, $\sinh(\tanh^{-1}x) = x/\sqrt{1 - x^2}$ and $\tanh^{-1}x = \frac12\ln\frac{1 + x}{1 - x}$, it follows that for boosts in the same direction rapidities add, and the order does not matter (slides 17–18).

## The Lorentz invariant

The Minkowski metric $\eta = \text{diag}(1, 1, 1, -1)$ is left unchanged by all Lorentz matrices, $\Lambda^t\eta\Lambda = \eta$, just as $\Lambda^t\Lambda = I$ defines rotations; this can be checked for boosts along $x$ and rotations, and the general case follows by combining them (slide 19). Then (slide 20)

<Formula label="Invariance of the squared 4-length" tex="\vec x'^t\eta\vec x' = (\Lambda\vec x)^t\eta(\Lambda\vec x) = \vec x^t(\Lambda^t\eta\Lambda)\vec x = \vec x^t\eta\vec x = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - (ct)^2" cite="mit" at="slide 20" />

the analogue of a vector's length being unchanged by rotations, except that it can be positive or negative. For two events, the invariant **interval** (slide 21) is

<Formula label="Interval" tex="\Delta s^2 \equiv \Delta\vec x^t\eta\Delta\vec x = \Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + \Delta z^2 - (c\Delta t)^2; \qquad \Delta\sigma \equiv \sqrt{\Delta s^2}\ (\Delta s^2 > 0), \qquad \Delta\tau \equiv \sqrt{-\Delta s^2}\ (\Delta s^2 < 0)" cite="mit" at="slide 21" />

- $\Delta s^2 = 0$: **null**, as for two points on a light ray;
- $\Delta s^2 > 0$: **spacelike**; $\Delta\sigma$ is the **proper distance**, measured in a frame where the events are simultaneous;
- $\Delta s^2 < 0$: **timelike**; $\Delta\tau$ is the **proper time interval** (as a length; divide by $c$ for a time), measured in a frame where the events happen at the same place.

**"Everything is relative", or is it** (slide 22)? All observers agree on rest length, on proper time and, as later lectures show, on rest mass.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A clock everyone agrees on">
Proper time, the time ticked by a clock along its own world line, is the same for every observer. Cosmology builds its "cosmic time" on it: each galaxy moving with the expansion carries a clock, and the [age of the universe](/courses/8-286/age-of-the-universe) is the proper time that has passed on such clocks since the Big Bang (see also [cosmic time](/courses/8-286/homogeneous-expansion)).
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Muons of your own">
<Problem>
Muons made $15$ km up travel straight down at $0.995c$. Their half-life at rest is $1.56 \times 10^{-6}$ s. (a) Find $\gamma$ and the travel time in the ground frame. (b) What fraction survives to the ground, with and without time dilation? (c) How thick is the atmosphere they cross, in the muons' own frame?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The trip">
$\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.995^2} \approx 10.0$. The trip takes $15 \times 10^3/(0.995 \times 3.00 \times 10^8) \approx 5.03 \times 10^{-5}$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Survival">
In the ground frame the half-life is dilated to $\gamma \times 1.56$ μs ≈ 15.6 μs, so the trip lasts about 3.2 half-lives and $2^{-3.2} \approx 0.11$ of the muons survive. Without dilation it would be about 32 half-lives: a fraction near $2 \times 10^{-10}$, essentially none.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The muons' view">
In their frame the clocks are fine but the atmosphere is contracted: $15\ \text{km}/\gamma \approx 1.5$ km, crossed in $5.03 \times 10^{-6}$ s, again about 3.2 half-lives. Both frames agree on how many muons arrive.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Moving clocks run slow: $\Delta t' = \gamma\Delta t$; cosmic-ray muons reach the ground because of it.
- Simultaneity is relative: events at the same $t$ in $S$ differ by $c\Delta t' = -\gamma\beta L$ in $S'$.
- Moving rods are shorter: $L' = L/\gamma$, because both ends must be located at the same time in the measuring frame.
- Velocities combine as $(v_1 + v_2)/(1 + v_1v_2/c^2)$; rapidities add.
- $\Lambda^t\eta\Lambda = \eta$ makes $\Delta s^2$ invariant: null, spacelike (proper distance) or timelike (proper time). Rest length, proper time and rest mass are absolute.
