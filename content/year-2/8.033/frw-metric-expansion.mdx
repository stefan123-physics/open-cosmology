Lecture 18 is the first of four on cosmology: the FRW metric, the expansion of the universe, its age, and a brief history (slide 6). It opens with a portrait of Alexander Friedmann, whose 1922 paper first described an expanding universe, and a line from a 1993 biography comparing his achievement with that of Copernicus (slide 1). (MIT's material for this lecture is a set of slides, in a deck titled "Lecture 17"; this site numbers lectures by MIT's class sessions, in which the first quiz took one. This page follows the slides and marks what is not on them.)

## General relativity so far

The recap (slides 2–5): gravitation is equivalent to acceleration; free bodies move along geodesics; and the key to problems is working with metrics and the calculus of variations. In the [last lecture's](/courses/8-033/equivalence-principle-metrics) gallery of metrics, the Minkowski metric implies special relativity, and the Newtonian metric $d\tau^2 = (1 + 2\phi)dt^2 - dx^2 - dy^2 - dz^2$ implies both gravitational redshift and Newtonian gravity (slides 3–4). Slide 4 sets an exercise with $\phi = -M/r$ (and $c = G = 1$): how much time elapses on a clock at rest far away, moving far away, at rest on the ground, and in a GPS satellite? (The page's own answer, with $R$ the Earth's radius and $r$ the satellite's orbital radius: $dt$, $dt\sqrt{1 - v^2}$, $dt\sqrt{1 - 2M/R}$, and $dt\sqrt{1 - 2M/r - v^2}$; for GPS see the [page for Lecture 7](/courses/8-033/doppler-aberration-twin-paradox). Slide 5 repeats the $1 - 2\phi$ slip noted on the last page.)

## Key fact: a homogeneous, isotropic universe

The first key fact of cosmology: the observable universe is homogeneous (the same everywhere) and isotropic (the same in every direction) on large scales (slide 9). The slides show the evidence: maps of the galaxies around us, the Hubble Ultra Deep Field, the Kitt Peak observatory and two wedge-shaped slices of a galaxy redshift survey, in which galaxies cluster on small scales but look uniform on large ones (slides 10–13). Slide 14 makes this quantitative: the size of density fluctuations falls from a few on scales of ten million light years, through 1 at about a hundred million light years, to about $10^{-4}$ on the largest scales probed by the cosmic microwave background, with galaxy clustering, gravitational lensing, cluster counts and hydrogen clouds in between all on one curve.

Three people found the metric (slide 15): Alexander Friedmann (1888–1925, Russian, 1922 paper); Georges Lemaître (1894–1966, Belgian, independently in 1927, with a PhD from MIT; the slide gives his death year as 1936, which is a slip); and Arthur Geoffrey Walker (British). The slide credits Walker with a "1935 paper with H P Robertson" showing that this is the *only* homogeneous and isotropic metric; in fact Robertson (1935) and Walker (submitted 1935, published 1937) proved it independently. Hence the name FRW, or FLRW.

## The FRW metric and the expansion

With $c = 1$, the metric is (slides 7 and 16):

<Formula label="FRW metric" tex="d\tau^2 = dt^2 - a(t)^2\left(\frac{dr^2}{1 - kr^2} + r^2d\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2\right)" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

The coordinates $r$, $\theta$, $\varphi$ label galaxies, which stay at fixed coordinates; all physical distances between them grow with the scale factor $a(t)$, like dots on an inflating balloon (slide 17). The constant $k$ sets the curvature of space: positive, zero or negative. The expansion rate is the Hubble parameter, and its value today is quoted through a dimensionless $h$ (slide 7):

<Formula label="Hubble parameter" tex="H \equiv \frac{\dot a}{a}, \qquad h \equiv \frac{H_0}{100\text{ km s}^{-1}\text{Mpc}^{-1}} \approx H_0 \times 9.7846\text{ Gyr}" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

(The page's own check gives $1/(100$ km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹$) = 9.778$ Gyr with the Julian year of 365.25 days; the slide's 9.7846 corresponds to a 365-day year. The difference does not matter for any result.)

General relativity applied to the FRW metric gives the **Friedmann equation** (slide 8), written on the slide both with the density $\rho$ and with the density parameters $\Omega$ of radiation ($\gamma$), matter ($m$), curvature ($k$) and vacuum energy ($\Lambda$), and the redshift $1 + z = a_0/a$:

<Formula label="Friedmann equation" tex="H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2} = H_0^2\left[\Omega_\gamma\left(1 + z\right)^4 + \Omega_m\left(1 + z\right)^3 + \Omega_k\left(1 + z\right)^2 + \Omega_\Lambda\right]" cite="mit" at="slide 8" />

Each kind of content dilutes differently as the universe expands: radiation as $(1 + z)^4$, matter as $(1 + z)^3$, the curvature term as $(1 + z)^2$, and vacuum energy not at all. The measured values in 2005, as listed on slide 8: baryons $\Omega_b \approx 0.05$, dark matter $\Omega_d \approx 0.25$, so matter $\Omega_m = \Omega_b + \Omega_d$ about 0.3; vacuum energy $\Omega_\Lambda \approx 0.7$; curvature $\Omega_k \approx 0$; and $h \approx 0.70$. (Later developments: the Planck satellite's 2018 values are close to these, with $h \approx 0.674$.)

**The age of the universe** (slide 8). Since $dt = da/\dot a = -dz/((1 + z)H)$, the time since the big bang at the moment we see redshift $z$ is

<Formula label="Age at redshift z" tex="t(z) = \int_z^\infty\frac{dz'}{\left(1 + z'\right)H\left(z'\right)}" cite="mit" at="slide 8" />

and today's age is $t(0)$. Slide 18 sketches how the history depends on the deceleration: galaxies' recession velocity against distance, and the size of the universe against time, for deceleration parameters $q_0 = 0$, between 0 and $\frac12$, $\frac12$, and above $\frac12$. All the curves pass through today's size with the same slope, set by $H_0$; for the coasting case $q_0 = 0$, a straight line, the time back to zero size is exactly $1/H_0$, and the more the expansion decelerates, the younger the universe. Slide 19 credits a figure from a review by Charles Lineweaver on what the universe is made of and how old it is (the figure itself is not in the OCW file).

<WorkedExample title="Ages from the Friedmann equation">
<Problem>
For a flat universe with $\Omega_m = 0.3$, $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.7$ (radiation neglected) and $h = 0.70$: (a) what is the Hubble time $1/H_0$? (b) What is the age $t(0)$? (c) What was the expansion rate at $z = 1$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The Hubble time">
$1/H_0 = 9.778/0.70 \approx 14.0$ billion years.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The age">
$t(0) = \frac{1}{H_0}\int_0^\infty\frac{dz}{(1 + z)\sqrt{0.3(1 + z)^3 + 0.7}}$, and the integral is about 0.964 (numerically, or from the closed form $\frac{2}{3\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}}\sinh^{-1}\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda/\Omega_m}$). So the age is about 13.5 billion years, a little less than the Hubble time: the early deceleration by matter outweighs the later acceleration by vacuum energy.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Earlier expansion">
$H(z = 1) = H_0\sqrt{0.3 \times 8 + 0.7} \approx 1.76H_0$, about 123 km/s/Mpc.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- On large scales the universe is homogeneous and isotropic; the only such metric is the FRW metric, with scale factor $a(t)$ and curvature $k$.
- The expansion rate $H = \dot a/a$ obeys the Friedmann equation, with radiation, matter, curvature and vacuum energy diluting as $(1 + z)^4$, $(1 + z)^3$, $(1 + z)^2$ and not at all.
- The age is $t = \int dz/((1 + z)H)$: about 13.5 billion years for the 2005 values on the slides.
