The [last lecture](/courses/8-033/speed-of-light-michelson-morley) showed that the Galilean transformation cannot be right for light. Today: how to transform between inertial frames, and what it all means; key people Lorentz and Einstein (slides 1–3).

## Two postulates

The plan follows Einstein: derive everything from two postulates (slide 4).

1. The laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames.
2. The speed of light is the same in all inertial frames.

The second follows from the first if the speed of light counts as a law of physics. Einstein called invariance under changes of inertial frame "special relativity", as opposed to "general relativity", its generalization to non-inertial frames (slide 4).

**Inertial frames done carefully** (slide 5). Build coordinates from three perpendicular rigid measuring rods, and time from local clocks at each place, synchronized with light pulses. Don't confuse simultaneity in a frame with seeing things at the same time: if you saw the 1987 supernova and a camera flash at the same moment, the two did not go off at the same time in your frame. The time $t$ of an inertial frame is the "bookkeeper's time", not the time you see something happen. And the first shocker: simultaneity is relative, so $t' = t$ must be abandoned (slide 5).

## Narrowing down the transformation

Write events as **4-vectors** with units of length, and ask which function the moving frame's $\vec x'$ is of $\vec x$ (slide 6):

<Formula label="4-vectors and linearity" tex="\vec x = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \\ ct \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec x' = \Lambda(\vec v)\vec x + \vec x_0" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

for some offset $\vec x_0$ and a $4 \times 4$ **Lorentz matrix** $\Lambda$. Why linear? Translational invariance requires $\vec x_2' - \vec x_1' = \Lambda(\vec v)(\vec x_2 - \vec x_1)$, and for any non-linear (strictly, non-affine) relation the difference would not depend only on $\vec x_2 - \vec x_1$ (slide 7). The slides warn that the textbook uses $t$ rather than $ct$ as fourth coordinate and $a$ for $\Lambda$, and fix the sign convention: the primed frame moves at $\vec v$ in the unprimed one (slide 7).

Without loss of generality (slides 7–11):

- no translation, $\vec x_0 = 0$, so only $\Lambda(\vec v)$ is needed;
- for $\vec v = 0$, $\Lambda = $ the identity;
- $\vec v$ along $x$; then symmetry under rotation about the $x$-axis means $x'$ and $t'$ cannot depend on $y$ or $z$: $\Lambda_{12} = \Lambda_{13} = \Lambda_{42} = \Lambda_{43} = 0$; and the $x$-axis must map onto the $x'$-axis, so $\Lambda_{21} = \Lambda_{31} = 0$;
- events with $x = t = 0$ are simultaneous in both frames, so rods in the $(y, z)$-plane can be compared directly: that block is the identity, $\Lambda_{22} = \Lambda_{33} = 1$, $\Lambda_{23} = \Lambda_{32} = 0$;
- an object at rest at the primed origin moves as $x = vt$, so $\Lambda_{24} = \Lambda_{34} = 0$ and $\Lambda_{14} = -\beta\Lambda_{11}$, with $\beta \equiv v/c$ (just $v$ in units where $c = 1$).

So far (slide 11)

<Formula label="Progress so far" tex="\Lambda(v) = \begin{pmatrix} \Lambda_{11} & 0 & 0 & -\beta\Lambda_{11} \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \Lambda_{41} & 0 & 0 & \Lambda_{44} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ z' \\ ct' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \Lambda_{11}(x - vt) \\ y \\ z \\ \Lambda_{41}x + \Lambda_{44}ct \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="slide 11" />

Nothing about light has been assumed yet, so both Galileo and Einstein still fit.

## Galileo and Einstein part ways

**Galileo** (slide 12). Assume $t' = t$: then $\Lambda_{41} = 0$ and $\Lambda_{44} = 1$; assume rods have the same length in both frames: $\Lambda_{11} = 1$. The result is the Galilean matrix $G(v)$, with ones on the diagonal and $-\beta$ in the top-right corner.

**Einstein** (slides 13–16). Instead, let a flash at the origin expand at $c$ in both frames, making the same light cone:

<Formula label="Light cone in both frames" tex="x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - (ct)^2 = 0, \qquad x'^2 + y'^2 + z'^2 - (ct')^2 = 0" cite="mit" at="slide 13" />

Substituting the progress-so-far form and rearranging gives a quadratic that must vanish on the same cone, so its coefficients must match those of the first (slide 14):

<Formula label="Matching the light cones" tex="(\Lambda_{11}^2 - \Lambda_{41}^2)x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - (\Lambda_{44}^2 - \beta^2\Lambda_{11}^2)(ct)^2 - 2(\beta\Lambda_{11}^2 + \Lambda_{41}\Lambda_{44})ctx = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Lambda_{11}^2 - \Lambda_{41}^2 = 1, \ \ \Lambda_{44}^2 - \beta^2\Lambda_{11}^2 = 1, \ \ \beta\Lambda_{11}^2 + \Lambda_{41}\Lambda_{44} = 0" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

Solving (slide 15), $\Lambda_{11} = \Lambda_{44} = \gamma$ and $\Lambda_{41} = \Lambda_{14} = -\beta\gamma$, where

<Formula label="Lorentz factor" tex="\gamma \equiv \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}" cite="mit" at="slide 15" />

(the second solution, $\Lambda_{11} = -\gamma$, just flips the signs of $t$ and $x$, and is discarded). **The Lorentz transformation** (slide 16):

<Formula label="Lorentz transformation" tex="\Lambda(v) = \begin{pmatrix} \gamma & 0 & 0 & -\gamma\beta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\gamma\beta & 0 & 0 & \gamma \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \begin{pmatrix} x' \\ y' \\ z' \\ ct' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \gamma(x - \beta ct) \\ y \\ z \\ \gamma(ct - \beta x) \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="slide 16" />

as in Einstein's 1905 paper (slide 16).

## The toolbox

Everything else follows (slides 17–21):

- **Inverse**: $\Lambda(v)^{-1} = \Lambda(-v)$.
- **Parallel velocities**: $\Lambda(v_1)\Lambda(v_2) = \Lambda\left(\frac{v_1 + v_2}{1 + v_1v_2/c^2}\right)$ (written with $c = 1$ on the slide); arbitrary velocities transform as

<Formula label="Velocity addition" tex="u_x = \frac{u_x' + v}{1 + \frac{u_x'v}{c^2}}, \qquad u_y = \frac{u_y'\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}{1 + \frac{u_x'v}{c^2}}, \qquad u_z = \frac{u_z'\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}{1 + \frac{u_x'v}{c^2}}" cite="mit" at="slide 18" />

- **Boosts as rotations**: slide 19 writes $\Lambda(-v)$ with $\cosh\eta$ and $\sinh\eta$ in place of $\gamma$ and $\gamma\beta$, where $\eta = \tanh^{-1}\beta$: a boost is a "rotation" by a hyperbolic angle. (Not on the slide: $\eta$ is called the **rapidity**, and rapidities of boosts in the same direction simply add.)
- **The interval** (slide 20): every Lorentz matrix satisfies $\Lambda^t\eta\Lambda = \eta$, with the Minkowski metric $\eta = \text{diag}(1, 1, 1, -1)$ (the same letter as the rapidity, as on the slides), so

<Formula label="Invariant interval" tex="\Delta s^2 \equiv \Delta\vec x^t\eta\Delta\vec x = \Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + \Delta z^2 - (c\Delta t)^2" cite="mit" at="slide 20" />

  is the same in every inertial frame.
- **Four-vectors for waves and velocities** (slide 21): the wave 4-vector $(k_x, k_y, k_z, \omega/c)$ and the velocity 4-vector $\gamma_u(u_x, u_y, u_z, c)$ with $\gamma_u = 1/\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$ (the slide also puts a factor $\gamma_u$ in front of the wave 4-vector, which belongs only to the velocity). Transforming them gives **aberration** and the **Doppler effect**:

<Formula label="Aberration and Doppler effect" tex="\cos\theta' = \frac{\cos\theta - \beta}{1 - \beta\cos\theta}, \qquad \omega' = \omega\gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="slide 21" />

The lecture ends with a light-clock movie (slide 22) and a quotation from Walter Fox Smith, who says the Lorentz transformation has given him every relation he has ever needed (slide 23).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Redshift starts here">
The Doppler formula $\omega' = \omega\gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta)$ is the special-relativistic version of the [Doppler effect](/courses/8-286/doppler-effect) that 8.286 uses to read the motion of galaxies. In an expanding universe the redshift turns out to be best understood as the stretching of space itself, but for nearby galaxies it is just this formula at small $\beta$.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="A boost of your own">
<Problem>
A frame moves at $v = 0.6c$ along $x$. (a) Find $\gamma$. (b) Transform the event $x = 5.0$, $ct = 3.0$ (in light-seconds) and check that the interval is unchanged. (c) Add $0.6c$ to $0.6c$, directly and through rapidities.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The factor">
$\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.36} = 1/0.8 = 1.25$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The event">
$x' = 1.25(5.0 - 0.6 \times 3.0) = 4.0$ and $ct' = 1.25(3.0 - 0.6 \times 5.0) = 0$: in the moving frame this event happens at the same time as the origin event. The interval: $5.0^2 - 3.0^2 = 16 = 4.0^2 - 0^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Adding velocities">
Directly: $(0.6 + 0.6)/(1 + 0.36) \approx 0.882$. Through rapidities: $\tanh^{-1}0.6 \approx 0.693$, twice that is $1.386$, and $\tanh 1.386 \approx 0.882$: the same, and still below 1.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Two postulates: same laws, and same speed of light, in all inertial frames. Simultaneity is relative.
- Linearity, symmetry and the motion of the origin reduce $\Lambda$ to three unknowns; $t' = t$ gives Galileo, the invariant light cone gives Lorentz: $x' = \gamma(x - \beta ct)$, $ct' = \gamma(ct - \beta x)$.
- Consequences: $\Lambda(v)^{-1} = \Lambda(-v)$, velocity addition $(v_1 + v_2)/(1 + v_1v_2/c^2)$, rapidities add, the interval $\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + \Delta z^2 - (c\Delta t)^2$ is invariant, and aberration and the Doppler effect follow.
