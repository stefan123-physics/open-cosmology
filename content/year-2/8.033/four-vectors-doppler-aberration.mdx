Lecture 6 opens with Christian Doppler and a summary: time dilation and length contraction, which go in opposite senses, the relativity of simultaneity, and tips for solving problems (slides 1–2). Today: the unification of space and time, proper time, more 4-vectors, $\vec U$ and $\vec k$, velocity addition, and the Doppler effect and aberration (slide 3).

## Review: velocity addition and the unification of space and time

The [velocity-addition](/courses/8-033/time-dilation-length-contraction) results of the last lecture, by matrix multiplication (slide 5 notes it is simpler with $2 \times 2$ matrices) and by differentials, are repeated (slides 4–7). Not everything is relative: observers agree on rest length, proper time and rest mass, just as Australians and Bostonians agree on the length of a 3-vector although they disagree on which way is up (slide 9).

**Space and time unified** (slides 8, 10–14). A rotation by $\theta$ about the $z$-axis mixes $x$ and $y$; a boost mixes $x$ and $ct$ like a rotation by an imaginary angle, with $\cosh\eta$ and $\sinh\eta$ for $\cos$ and $\sin$, where $\eta = \tanh^{-1}\beta$ is the rapidity (slides 10, 12). The Minkowski metric $\eta = \text{diag}(1, 1, 1, -1)$ is preserved by all Lorentz matrices, $\Lambda^t\eta\Lambda = \eta$, so

<Formula label="Invariant squared length" tex="\vec x^t\eta\vec x = x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - (ct)^2" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

is the same in every frame: "the only difference between space and time is a minus sign" (slide 13). Separations are **null**, **spacelike** or **timelike** according to the sign of $\Delta s^2$, the last two with proper distance $\Delta\sigma = \sqrt{\Delta s^2}$ and proper time $\Delta\tau = \sqrt{-\Delta s^2}$ (slides 15–17). A figure on slide 16 shows the three kinds: spacelike vectors reach outside the light cone, null ones lie on it, timelike ones inside, pointing to the future or the past.

## Velocity as a 4-vector

Since $d\tau$, the proper time along a world line, is invariant (slide 20), dividing the 4-vector $d\vec X$ by it gives another 4-vector (slide 21):

<Formula label="Velocity 4-vector" tex="\vec U \equiv \frac{d\vec X}{d\tau} = \gamma_u\begin{pmatrix} u_x \\ u_y \\ u_z \\ c \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \gamma_u = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2}}}, \qquad \vec U' = \Lambda\vec U, \qquad \vec U^t\eta\vec U = -c^2" cite="mit" at="slide 21" />

using $d\tau = dt/\gamma_u$. Every velocity 4-vector has the same squared length, $-c^2$: it is timelike (slide 21). Transforming it with $\Lambda(-v)$ gives at once (slide 22)

<Formula label="Velocity addition from the 4-vector" tex="\vec U' = \gamma_{u'}\begin{pmatrix} u_x' \\ u_y' \\ u_z' \\ c \end{pmatrix} = \Lambda(-v)\vec U = \gamma_u\begin{pmatrix} \gamma(u_x + v) \\ u_y \\ u_z \\ \gamma\left(1 + \frac{u_xv}{c^2}\right)c \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \gamma_{u'} = \gamma_u\gamma\left[1 + \frac{u_xv}{c^2}\right]" cite="mit" at="slide 22" />

The first three components are the velocity-addition formulas (dividing by the fourth); the last relation follows from the normalization $\vec u'^t\eta\vec u' = -1$ (in units $c = 1$; slide 22).

## The wave 4-vector

This is how things *are* in $S'$; how do they *look* (slide 23)? A plane wave (slide 24)

<Formula label="Plane wave and its 4-vector" tex="E(\vec x) = \sin(k_xx + k_yy + k_zz - \omega t), \qquad \vec k \equiv \begin{pmatrix} k_x \\ k_y \\ k_z \\ \omega/c \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \frac{\omega}{c} = k \equiv \sqrt{k_x^2 + k_y^2 + k_z^2}" cite="mit" at="slide 24" />

is defined by four numbers; the last relation holds for waves moving at $c$, such as light or gravitational waves (slide 24). With the Minkowski matrix the wave is $E(\vec x) = \sin(\vec k^t\eta\vec x)$. Transform to $S'$, $\vec x \to \vec x' = \Lambda\vec x$ and $\vec k \to \vec k'$ (slide 25):

<Formula label="The wave in the new frame" tex="E' = \sin(\vec k'^t\eta\vec x') = \sin(\vec k'^t\eta\Lambda\vec x) = \sin\left[(\Lambda^{-1}\vec k')^t(\Lambda^t\eta\Lambda)\vec x\right] = \sin\left[(\Lambda^{-1}\vec k')^t\eta\vec x\right]" cite="mit" at="slide 25" />

This equals $E$ if $\Lambda^{-1}\vec k' = \vec k$, that is if $\vec k' = \Lambda\vec k$: a plane wave in $S$ is a plane wave in $S'$, and the wave 4-vector transforms exactly like $\vec x$. (The argument assumes $E' = E$; the fields themselves change under Lorentz transformations, as later lectures show, but not so as to spoil the result: the phase $\vec k^t\eta\vec x$ must be invariant; slide 25.)

## Aberration and the Doppler effect

Light travelling at speed $c$ in direction $(\theta, \phi)$ in $S$ (slide 26), seen from $S'$ moving at $v$ along $z$ (slide 27):

<Formula label="Transforming a light wave" tex="\vec k = k\begin{pmatrix} \sin\theta\cos\phi \\ \sin\theta\sin\phi \\ \cos\theta \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec k' = \Lambda\vec k = k\begin{pmatrix} \sin\theta\cos\phi \\ \sin\theta\sin\phi \\ \gamma(\cos\theta - \beta) \\ \gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta) \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="slide 27" />

Comparing with $k'(\sin\theta'\cos\phi', \sin\theta'\sin\phi', \cos\theta', 1)$ (slide 27):

<Formula label="Aberration and Doppler shift" tex="\phi' = \phi, \qquad \cos\theta' = \frac{\cos\theta - \beta}{1 - \beta\cos\theta}, \qquad k' = k\gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="slide 27" />

The change of direction is **aberration**; the change of frequency $ck$ is the **Doppler shift**, and since $k = 2\pi/\lambda$, $\lambda'/\lambda = k/k'$ (slide 28). Taking the ratio of the transverse and $z$ components instead gives the equivalent form $\tan\theta' = \sin\theta/\gamma(\cos\theta - \beta)$, as in Resnick (slide 28). In the classical limit these reduce to the familiar results; and light moving at right angles to the boost in $S$, $\cos\theta = 0$, gives $\omega' = \gamma\omega$: the **transverse Doppler effect**, simply time dilation (slide 28). These match the ray-tracing handout used in the course (slide 28).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Reading motion from light">
The longitudinal case, light along the line of motion, $\omega'/\omega = \gamma(1 \mp \beta) = \sqrt{(1 \mp \beta)/(1 \pm \beta)}$ (upper signs for a source and observer moving apart), is the relativistic [Doppler effect](/courses/8-286/doppler-effect). Astronomers first measured galaxies moving away from us with its small-speed form, $z \approx v/c$. In cosmology the redshift is understood as the stretching of space during the light's journey; this special-relativity formula is the special case of a universe without gravity, and for nearby galaxies the two agree to first order in $v/c$.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Starlight of your own">
<Problem>
An observer moves at $\beta = 0.5$ along $z$. (a) Light travels at right angles to $z$ in the frame $S$ of a star field. In what direction, and with what frequency, does the observer see it? (b) Light travels along $+z$, the same way as the observer. By what factor is its frequency changed?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Sideways light">
$\gamma = 1/\sqrt{0.75} \approx 1.155$. With $\cos\theta = 0$: $\cos\theta' = -0.5$, so $\theta' = 120°$: the light now moves partly backwards relative to the observer, so the star appears shifted forward, towards the direction of motion. The frequency is $\gamma\omega \approx 1.155\omega$: the transverse Doppler shift.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Light from behind">
With $\cos\theta = 1$: $k' = k\gamma(1 - \beta) = k\sqrt{(1 - 0.5)/(1 + 0.5)} \approx 0.577k$: the observer, running away from the source, sees the light redshifted by a factor of about 0.58.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A boost is a rotation by an imaginary angle; $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - (ct)^2$ is invariant, and 4-vectors are null, spacelike or timelike.
- With invariant $d\tau$, $\vec U = d\vec X/d\tau = \gamma_u(\vec u, c)$ is a 4-vector with $\vec U^t\eta\vec U = -c^2$; transforming it gives velocity addition.
- The wave 4-vector $(\vec k, \omega/c)$ transforms like $\vec x$, because the phase is invariant.
- Aberration: $\cos\theta' = (\cos\theta - \beta)/(1 - \beta\cos\theta)$; Doppler: $k' = k\gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta)$; at $90°$, $\omega' = \gamma\omega$.
