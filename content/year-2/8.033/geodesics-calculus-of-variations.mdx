Lecture 8 turns to a new tool, the calculus of variations, and its central result, the Euler–Lagrange equation, named after Leonhard Euler (whose portrait opens the slides, slide 1) and Joseph Louis Lagrange. The plan for the lecture: the equation, its derivation, and its use for metrics and geodesics in Euclidean space and in Minkowski spacetime, for gravitational redshift, for the brachistochrone and for the hanging chain (slide 3). (MIT's material for this lecture is a set of slides; the Euler–Lagrange equation and its derivation are listed on slide 3 but not written out on the slides, so this page gives them in its own words. The brachistochrone and the hanging chain are on the [next page](/courses/8-033/brachistochrone-catenary); gravitational redshift returns with general relativity later in the course.)

## The question: which path is best?

So far the course has been about converting between inertial frames (slide 2). The new question is of a different kind: among all curves joining two points, which one makes some quantity, a length or a time, as small (or large) as possible? The classic example is the brachistochrone: the shape of a slide on which a bead falls from one point to another in the least time. Slides 4–6 show the experiment (the photograph is withheld on OCW for copyright reasons) and a figure comparing three shapes.

Such a quantity has the form of an integral along the curve $y(x)$:

<Formula label="A functional" tex="I[y] = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} L\left(x, y, y'\right)\,dx" cite="mit" at="slide 3 (listed; written out by the page)" />

(this notation is the page's own). $I$ is a number that depends on the whole curve, a *functional*, and $L$ is a function of the position on the curve and its slope.

## The Euler–Lagrange equation

Suppose $y(x)$ makes $I$ stationary, and change it slightly to $y + \epsilon\,h(x)$, with $h = 0$ at both ends so the end points stay fixed. To first order in $\epsilon$,

<Formula label="First-order change" tex="\delta I = \epsilon\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial y}\,h + \frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}\,h'\right)dx = \epsilon\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'}\right)h\,dx" cite="mit" at="slide 3 (listed; written out by the page)" />

where the second form comes from integrating the $h'$ term by parts (the boundary term vanishes because $h$ does). For $y$ to be stationary, $\delta I$ must vanish for every $h$, and that is only possible if the bracket vanishes everywhere:

<Formula label="Euler–Lagrange equation" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial y}" cite="mit" at="slide 3 (listed; written out by the page)" />

(The derivation is the page's own; the slides list it as "deriving it", slide 3.) Two special cases save work. If $L$ does not depend on $y$, then $\partial L/\partial y'$ is constant along the curve. If $L$ does not depend on $x$, then $L - y'\,\partial L/\partial y'$ is constant (this second form is used on the next page).

## Metrics and geodesics

In an $n$-dimensional space, the *metric* is a symmetric $n \times n$ matrix $\mathrm{g}$, usually depending on position, that says how distances are measured (slide 7). The length of a curve is $\int d\sigma$, with

<Formula label="Line element" tex="d\sigma^2 = d\vec r^{\,t}\,\mathrm{g}\,d\vec r" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

where $\vec r$ holds whatever coordinates are in use. Change the coordinates and the metric changes with them, so that $d\sigma$ stays the same: $d\sigma$ is invariant under all coordinate transformations (slide 7). A curve that makes the length stationary is a *geodesic*.

**Example: the Euclidean plane** in Cartesian coordinates (slide 7). Here $\mathrm{g}$ is the $2 \times 2$ unit matrix, so

<Formula label="Length in the plane" tex="d\sigma^2 = dx^2 + dy^2, \qquad \int d\sigma = \int\sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2} = \int\sqrt{1 + y'(x)^2}\,dx" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

Applying the Euler–Lagrange equation gives the shortest path between two points: a straight line (slide 7). In steps (the page's own): $L = \sqrt{1 + y'^2}$ has no $y$ in it, so $\partial L/\partial y' = y'/\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$ is constant; then $y'$ is constant, and $y$ is linear in $x$.

## Geodesics in Minkowski spacetime

The same machinery works in 4D Minkowski spacetime, with Cartesian coordinates and $c = 1$ for simplicity (slide 8). The metric is $\mathrm{g} = \eta$, the diagonal matrix with entries $1, 1, 1, -1$ for $x, y, z, t$, and the proper time along a world line is

<Formula label="Proper time" tex="d\tau^2 = -d\sigma^2 = dt^2 - dx^2 - dy^2 - dz^2" cite="mit" at="slide 8" />

(On the slide, the middle step reads $-d\sigma^2 = d\vec x^{\,t}\mathrm{g}\,d\vec x$; with this $\mathrm{g}$ the product $d\vec x^{\,t}\mathrm{g}\,d\vec x$ is $d\sigma^2$ itself, so a minus sign is missing there, and the last line has a typo, "$xy^2$" for $dy^2$. The result above is the slide's intended one.) Along a world line with velocity $u$:

<Formula label="Proper time along a world line" tex="\Delta\tau = \int d\tau = \int\sqrt{1 - \dot x^2 - \dot y^2 - \dot z^2}\,dt = \int\sqrt{1 - u^2}\,dt = \int\frac{dt}{\gamma}" cite="mit" at="slide 8" />

Applying the Euler–Lagrange equation shows that the extremal interval between two events is a straight line through spacetime (slide 8), by the same steps as in the plane: the integrand depends only on the velocity, so the velocity is constant. The extremum is a *maximum*: in the frame where the straight world line is at rest, any detour has speed somewhere, and there $\sqrt{1 - u^2}$ is less than 1, while the proper time is the same in every frame (this remark is the page's own). The free particle, moving in a straight line at constant speed, is the one that ages most between two events: the [twin paradox](/courses/8-033/doppler-aberration-twin-paradox) seen as a geometry problem.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Free fall as a geodesic">
In general relativity gravity is no longer a force: a freely falling particle follows a geodesic of a curved spacetime metric, found with exactly this Euler–Lagrange machinery. In cosmology the metric is that of an expanding universe, and its [geodesic equation](/courses/8-286/geodesic-equation) describes how freely moving particles travel through it; with the [Christoffel symbols](/courses/8-286/christoffel-symbols) it shows that their momentum falls as the universe expands.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Longer paths, less time">
<Problem>
(a) Compare the length of the curve $y = x^2$ from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 1)$ with the straight line. (b) With $c = 1$, a clock goes out at $u = 0.6$ for 5 years of coordinate time and comes back at the same speed, while another clock stays at rest. How much proper time does each record?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Two lengths">
The parabola has $y' = 2x$, so its length is $\int_0^1\sqrt{1 + 4x^2}\,dx \approx 1.479$. The straight line has length $\sqrt 2 \approx 1.414$: shorter, as the Euler–Lagrange equation promises.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Two proper times">
The clock at rest records 10 years. The moving one records $\int\sqrt{1 - u^2}\,dt = 0.8 \times 10 = 8$ years. In spacetime the straight world line is the longest: the extremum is a maximum.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A metric $\mathrm{g}$ turns coordinate steps into lengths, $d\sigma^2 = d\vec r^{\,t}\mathrm{g}\,d\vec r$; a geodesic makes the length stationary.
- The Euler–Lagrange equation, $\frac{d}{dx}\partial L/\partial y' = \partial L/\partial y$, finds the curve that makes $\int L\,dx$ stationary.
- In the Euclidean plane geodesics are straight lines; in Minkowski spacetime the straight world line has the extremal (largest) proper time, $\Delta\tau = \int dt/\gamma$.
