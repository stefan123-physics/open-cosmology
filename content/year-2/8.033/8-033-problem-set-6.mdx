## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check, or pick one of the choices. A number box accepts any value within 1% of ours (2% in Problems 6(c) and 7(c)), so keep at least three significant figures, and type it in the unit shown next to the box. Small and large numbers can be typed as `6.3e-6` or `6.3 × 10^-6`. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `sqrt(2*(gamma + 1))` or `(cos(theta) - beta)/sqrt(1 - beta^2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Write the symbols of the box exactly as the box names them (`gamma` for $\gamma$, `beta` for $\beta$, `theta` for $\theta$, `phi` for $\phi$, `M0` for $M_0$).

MIT gives the points per problem and, for Problems 1, 4, 5 and 6, per part. Every other split between parts is ours. Parts that ask you to show or to discuss something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

Throughout, $m_0$ or $M_0$ is a rest mass, $u$ is the speed of a particle, $v = \beta c$ is the speed of one frame relative to another and $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}$, as in the lessons. A prime marks a quantity measured in the frame S′. Four-vectors are written with the three space components first and the time component last, as on MIT's slides.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Colliders, a burning airship and what you are made of" points={11} mit="Problem 1">

MIT took this problem from its 2004 exam.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

A particle of rest mass $m_0$ moves with Lorentz factor $\gamma$ and hits an identical particle that is at rest. Find the total energy (rest energy plus kinetic energy) of the two particles together, first in the lab frame S, where the target is at rest, and then in the centre-of-momentum (CM) frame S′, where the total momentum is zero. Then find the ratio $E_\text{tot}/E'_\text{tot}$. Type the two energies in units of $m_0c^2$.

<Hint>

The energy and the momentum of the pair form a 4-vector. Which combination of total energy and total momentum has the same value in every frame?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the lab frame only the moving particle has momentum, $p = \gamma m_0 u$, and $(pc)^2 = (\gamma^2 - 1)(m_0c^2)^2$. In the CM frame the total momentum is zero, so the invariant is the square of the total energy alone.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-lab" label="$E_\text{tot}/(m_0c^2) =$" expr="gamma + 1"
  vars={{ gamma: [1.5, 20] }}
  names={{ gamma: "\\gamma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "gamma", hint: "That is the energy of the moving particle alone. The target at rest still has its rest energy." },
    { expr: "2*gamma", hint: "Only one of the two particles moves in the lab frame. The target at rest has the energy m0 c², not gamma m0 c²." },
    { expr: "gamma - 1", hint: "That is the kinetic energy of the moving particle. The total energy also holds both rest energies." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-cm" label="$E'_\text{tot}/(m_0c^2) =$" expr="sqrt(2*(gamma + 1))"
  vars={{ gamma: [1.5, 20] }}
  names={{ gamma: "\\gamma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "gamma + 1", hint: "That is the lab value. Energy is not invariant: only the combination E² − (pc)² is the same in both frames." },
    { expr: "2*gamma", hint: "In the CM frame both particles do move with the same Lorentz factor, but that factor is smaller than the lab's gamma." },
    { expr: "2*(gamma + 1)", hint: "That is the square of the answer: the invariant gives the square of the CM energy." },
    { expr: "2", hint: "That is the rest energy of the two particles only. In the CM frame both of them move." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-ratio" label="$E_\text{tot}/E'_\text{tot} =$" expr="sqrt((gamma + 1)/2)"
  vars={{ gamma: [1.5, 20] }}
  names={{ gamma: "\\gamma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2/(gamma + 1))", hint: "That is the CM energy over the lab energy. MIT asks for the lab energy over the CM energy, which is greater than 1." },
    { expr: "(gamma + 1)/2", hint: "The square root is missing: the CM energy goes with the square root of gamma + 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The total energy depends on the frame, but $E^2 - (pc)^2$ of the whole system does not. In the CM frame the momentum is zero, so there this invariant is the energy squared.

<Step title="The lab frame">

The moving particle has the energy $\gamma m_0c^2$ and the target has $m_0c^2$:

<Formula label="Total energy in the lab frame" tex="E_\text{tot} = \gamma m_0c^2 + m_0c^2 = (\gamma + 1)\,m_0c^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Only the moving particle has momentum. From the lesson's relation $E^2 = (m_0c^2)^2 + (cp)^2$ (slide 5 of Lecture 12), its momentum obeys

<Formula label="Total momentum in the lab frame" tex="(p_\text{tot}c)^2 = (\gamma m_0c^2)^2 - (m_0c^2)^2 = (\gamma^2 - 1)\,(m_0c^2)^2" cite="reading2" at="slide 5" />

</Step>

<Step title="The CM frame">

The total momentum 4-vector is the sum of two 4-vectors, so its invariant square has the same value in S and in S′. In S′ the total momentum vanishes:

<Formula label="Total energy in the CM frame" tex="E'^2_\text{tot} = E_\text{tot}^2 - (p_\text{tot}c)^2 = \left[(\gamma + 1)^2 - (\gamma^2 - 1)\right](m_0c^2)^2 = 2(\gamma + 1)\,(m_0c^2)^2, \qquad E'_\text{tot} = \sqrt{2(\gamma + 1)}\;m_0c^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Lab energy over CM energy" tex="\frac{E_\text{tot}}{E'_\text{tot}} = \frac{\gamma + 1}{\sqrt{2(\gamma + 1)}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma + 1}{2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a slow particle ($\gamma \to 1$) both energies are $2m_0c^2$ and the ratio is 1. A second route: the CM frame moves through the lab at the speed $\beta_\text{cm} = cp_\text{tot}/E_\text{tot}$ (the lesson's velocity formula, slide 3 of Lecture 12), which is $\sqrt{\gamma^2 - 1}/(\gamma + 1)$. Its Lorentz factor is $\gamma_\text{cm} = \sqrt{(\gamma + 1)/2}$. In S′ the target moves at that speed, and so does the other particle in the opposite direction, so $E'_\text{tot} = 2\gamma_\text{cm}m_0c^2 = \sqrt{2(\gamma + 1)}\,m_0c^2$, the same. The ratio of the two energies is $\gamma_\text{cm}$ itself: the pair behaves like one object of rest energy $E'_\text{tot}$ moving with $\gamma_\text{cm}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

CERN's Large Hadron Collider (still being built when MIT set this problem) lets two beams of particles hit each other head on, where older machines shot one beam at a target at rest. Use your result of part (a) to say in one sentence why that is a good idea for making new, heavy forms of matter.

Then put a number on it (the number is our addition). Take the LHC's design values: two beams of protons, each proton with an energy of 7 TeV, so that 14 TeV is available in the CM frame, which here is the lab. What energy would a single proton beam need to give the same CM energy with protons at rest as the target? The rest energy of the proton is 0.938272 GeV. Answer in TeV.

<Hint>

A new particle can be made only from the energy that is available in the CM frame. The rest of the lab energy is the kinetic energy of the motion of the whole system, which momentum conservation does not let you spend.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the number, write the invariant of part (a) again with the beam energy $E_\text{beam}$ in the place of $\gamma m_0c^2$, and solve it for $E_\text{beam}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-fixed" label="$E_\text{beam} =$" value="1.04e5" unit="TeV" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-fixed-target" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only the CM energy can be turned into the rest energy of new particles. On a fixed target it grows only as the square root of the beam energy. In a collider it is the whole energy of both beams.

<Step title="The sentence">

With a target at rest the useful energy is $E'_\text{tot} = \sqrt{2(\gamma + 1)}\,m_0c^2$, which for large $\gamma$ is a fraction $\sqrt{2/(\gamma + 1)}$ of the lab energy and grows only as $\sqrt{\gamma}$, while two beams that meet head on with equal energies already are in their CM frame, so all of their energy is available.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

Writing the invariant of part (a) with $E_\text{beam} = \gamma m_0c^2$:

<Formula label="CM energy on a fixed target" tex="E'^2_\text{tot} = 2\,m_0c^2\left(E_\text{beam} + m_0c^2\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_\text{beam} = \frac{E'^2_\text{tot}}{2\,m_0c^2} - m_0c^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

With $E'_\text{tot} = 14\,000$ GeV and $m_0c^2 = 0.938272$ GeV:

<Formula label="Beam energy for 14 TeV on a fixed target" tex="E_\text{beam} = \frac{(14\,000\ \text{GeV})^2}{2 \times 0.938272\ \text{GeV}} - 0.938\ \text{GeV} = 1.04\times 10^{8}\ \text{GeV} = 1.04\times 10^{5}\ \text{TeV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

That is about 15 000 times the 7 TeV of one LHC beam. Read the other way: a 7 TeV proton ($\gamma \approx 7460$) hitting a proton at rest gives $\sqrt{2(\gamma + 1)} \times 0.938$ GeV, only about 115 GeV in the CM frame, less than 2% of the beam energy. The lesson's worked example finds the LHC's bending radius from $r = p/(qB)$: in a ring of fixed size the magnetic field has to grow with the momentum, so the magnets set the highest energy ([Lecture 12](/courses/8-033/force-acceleration-interstellar-travel)). (Later development: the LHC started in 2009 and has run at up to 6.8 TeV per proton.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

When the airship Hindenburg burned in 1937, the fire was mainly the chemical reaction $2\,\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \to 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$. What does that tell you about the rest masses? Compare the rest mass of two hydrogen molecules plus one oxygen molecule with the rest mass of two water molecules.

<Hint>

A fire gives off energy. Where does that energy come from, if energy is conserved and mass is a form of energy?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="Two H₂ and one O₂ together have a rest mass that is" choices={["smaller than that of two water molecules", "larger than that of two water molecules", "exactly equal to that of two water molecules, because no atoms are lost"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The reaction releases energy as heat and light. That energy was rest energy of the molecules before, so the products are lighter.

<Step title="The argument">

Take the molecules at rest before and the water molecules brought to rest afterwards, once the heat and light have left. Energy conservation says: rest energy before equals rest energy after plus the energy that left. That energy is positive, so the two hydrogen molecules and the oxygen molecule together have the larger rest mass, by the released energy divided by $c^2$. The atoms are the same before and after. What has changed is the (negative) potential energy that binds them, and the rest energy of a system is the sum of all its energy contributions (slide 27 of Lecture 13).

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

Burning hydrogen to water vapour releases 241.826 kJ per mole of water, which is 5.01 eV for the two water molecules of the reaction. Their rest energy is $2 \times 18.015 \times 931.494\ \text{MeV} = 3.356\times 10^{10}$ eV. So the rest mass drops by a fraction $1.5\times 10^{-10}$: real, but far too small to weigh, which is why chemists could believe that mass is conserved.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={1}>

Put these particles in order of increasing rest mass: proton, neutron, photon, Z boson, electron.

<Hint>

The lesson lists rest energies of common particles ([Lecture 14](/courses/8-033/particle-processes-unsolved-problems)). A free neutron can decay into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino. What does that say about the neutron and the proton?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="From lightest to heaviest:" choices={["electron, photon, proton, neutron, Z boson", "photon, electron, neutron, proton, Z boson", "photon, electron, proton, neutron, Z boson", "photon, electron, proton, Z boson, neutron", "photon, Z boson, electron, proton, neutron"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Know the rough rest energies: nothing, half an MeV, about a GeV (twice), and about 91 GeV.

<Step title="The order">

Photon (zero rest mass), electron (0.511 MeV), proton (938.272 MeV), neutron (939.565 MeV), Z boson (91.188 GeV, which is 91 188 MeV). The first four are on slide 11 of Lecture 14, with the slide's 2006 values for the proton and the neutron, 938.26 and 939.55 MeV.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The neutron must be heavier than the proton and the electron together, or it could not decay into them: $939.565 - 938.272 - 0.511 = 0.782$ MeV is left over as kinetic energy. The Z boson is a force carrier of the weak interaction, and unlike the photon it is very heavy: about 97 times the proton.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={1}>

MIT's sheet has a sentence with three blanks: you consist of quarks called (blank) and (blank), and of leptons called (blank). Fill them in.

<Hint>

Your atoms have nuclei of protons and neutrons, with electrons around them. Which of these are made of quarks?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e" label="You consist of" choices={["the quarks proton and neutron, and leptons called electrons", "the quarks up and down, and leptons called photons", "the quarks up and down, and leptons called electrons", "the quarks top and bottom, and leptons called muons"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Ordinary matter is atoms: nuclei of protons and neutrons, and electrons.

<Step title="The blanks">

A proton is two up quarks and one down quark (uud) and a neutron is one up and two down (udd), as slide 28 of Lecture 13 notes. The electron is a lepton. So you consist of quarks called up and down, and of leptons called electrons.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Protons and neutrons are not quarks: they are built from them, and they count as baryons. The photon is a force carrier, not a lepton. Top and bottom quarks and muons exist only for an instant in collisions and in cosmic rays. (Most of your mass is neither quark mass nor electron mass. It is the energy of the strong field inside the protons and neutrons, as the lesson explains.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Which conservation law forbids it?" points={4} mit="Problem 2">

MIT took this problem from its 2003 final exam. None of the four reactions below is ever seen. For each one, choose the conservation law that it violates, or say that it violates none of the listed laws. MIT's sheet marks the options with the letters L, B, P, E, Q and N, and so do the boxes. Count a lepton number of $+1$ for the electron and $-1$ for the positron, and a baryon number of $+1$ for the proton and for the neutron.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

A single photon with an energy of 1 GeV, far from all other matter, turns into an electron and a positron.

<Hint>

The pair has a frame in which its total momentum is zero. What is its energy there, at the very least? Does a photon have such a frame?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="The reaction violates" choices={["L: lepton number conservation", "B: baryon number conservation", "P: parity conservation", "E: energy-momentum conservation", "Q: charge conservation", "N: none of these laws"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Energy alone is no problem: 1 GeV is far more than the pair's rest energy. Energy and momentum together cannot both be conserved.

<Step title="The invariant">

For the photon $E = cp$, so the invariant $E^2 - (cp)^2$ of its momentum 4-vector is zero. For the pair the same invariant is the square of its energy in its own CM frame, which is at least $(2m_ec^2)^2 = (1.022\ \text{MeV})^2$. An invariant cannot be zero before and positive after, so the 4-vector $P$ is not conserved: answer E.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the other laws">

Charge: $0 \to -1 + 1$. Lepton number: $0 \to 1 - 1$. Baryon number: $0 \to 0$. All fine. The word "isolated" matters: next to a nucleus, which can take up momentum, a photon of more than 1.022 MeV does make pairs (the lesson's pair creation with a recoiling particle).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

A neutron decays into a proton and an electron, and nothing else.

<Hint>

Go down the list: charge, baryon number, lepton number, each before and after. Then ask whether the masses allow it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="The reaction violates" choices={["L: lepton number conservation", "B: baryon number conservation", "P: parity conservation", "E: energy-momentum conservation", "Q: charge conservation", "N: none of these laws"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The real decay has a third particle, an antineutrino. Without it the count of leptons is wrong.

<Step title="The count">

Lepton number: 0 before, $+1$ after (the electron). That is violated: answer L. Charge: $0 \to +1 - 1$, fine. Baryon number: $1 \to 1$, fine. Energy and momentum: the neutron's rest energy exceeds that of the proton and electron together by 0.782 MeV, so the two could fly apart back to back with that kinetic energy. Fine too.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In the lesson's beta decay a neutron turns into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino: the antineutrino has lepton number $-1$ and restores the count. Slide 19 of Lecture 14 tells how the missing particle gave itself away: momentum seemed not to be conserved in beta decay, until Pauli proposed the neutrino in 1931.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

A neutron decays into an electron and a positron.

<Hint>

Count the baryons before and after.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="The reaction violates" choices={["L: lepton number conservation", "B: baryon number conservation", "P: parity conservation", "E: energy-momentum conservation", "Q: charge conservation", "N: none of these laws"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The neutron is a baryon and nothing on the right is.

<Step title="The count">

Baryon number: 1 before, 0 after. Violated: answer B. Charge: $0 \to -1 + 1$, fine. Lepton number: $0 \to 1 - 1$, fine. Energy and momentum: the neutron's 939.565 MeV is far more than the pair's 1.022 MeV, and the two can fly apart back to back, so nothing forbids it there.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

If baryon number were not conserved, all nuclei could decay away into light particles like this. That no such decay has ever been seen is the evidence for the law. The lesson adds that the law may still fail very slightly: if protons decay at all, it takes far longer than $10^{32}$ years (slide 19 of Lecture 14).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={1}>

A neutron decays into a proton and a photon.

<Hint>

Add up the electric charge on each side.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="The reaction violates" choices={["L: lepton number conservation", "B: baryon number conservation", "P: parity conservation", "E: energy-momentum conservation", "Q: charge conservation", "N: none of these laws"]} answer={4} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A neutral particle cannot turn into a charged one and a neutral one.

<Step title="The count">

Charge: 0 before, $+1$ after. Violated: answer Q. Baryon number: $1 \to 1$, fine. Lepton number: $0 \to 0$, fine. Energy and momentum: the neutron is heavier than the proton by 1.293 MeV, and a proton and a photon can share that back to back. Fine.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Compare with part (b): there the electron carried away the negative charge, and the trouble was the lepton count. Here there are no leptons, and the trouble is the charge. The real decay, with an electron and an antineutrino, satisfies both.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Which has the larger rest mass?" points={6} mit="Problem 3">

For each pair, choose the one with the larger rest mass. MIT's sheet adds two rules. For a system of several particles, its rest mass means its total energy divided by $c^2$ in the frame where its total momentum is zero. And you may ignore the kinetic energy of any motion of the particles inside a system relative to each other.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

(A) A proton. (B) A neutron.

<Hint>

Which of the two decays into the other when it is free?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="The larger rest mass:" choices={["A: the proton", "B: the neutron"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A free neutron decays into a proton (plus an electron and an antineutrino), and a decay can only go downhill in rest mass.

<Step title="The answer">

B, the neutron: 939.565 MeV against 938.272 MeV, a difference of 1.293 MeV or 0.14%.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

If the proton were the heavier one, hydrogen would not be stable and there would be no water, no stars like the Sun and no chemistry as we know it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

(A) A proton. (B) 1000 electrons at rest, far from each other.

<Hint>

Far apart and at rest, the electrons have neither potential nor kinetic energy to count. How many electron masses make one proton mass?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="The larger rest mass:" choices={["A: the proton", "B: the 1000 electrons"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Far apart and at rest, the electrons simply add their rest masses.

<Step title="The answer">

A, the proton. The electrons have $1000 \times 0.511\ \text{MeV} = 511$ MeV together, and the proton has 938.272 MeV.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The proton is 1836 times as heavy as the electron (slide 11 of Lecture 14), so it would take 1837 electrons to beat it. The words "far from each other" matter: packed close together, the electrons' mutual repulsion would add positive potential energy and so add mass, as in part (d).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

(A) A muon with an energy of 10 GeV. (B) An electron with an energy of 100 GeV.

<Hint>

Does the rest mass of a particle depend on how fast it moves?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="The larger rest mass:" choices={["A: the 10 GeV muon", "B: the 100 GeV electron"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Rest mass is the invariant $m_0 = \sqrt{E^2 - c^2p^2}/c^2$ (slide 5 of Lecture 12). It does not change when a particle is given more energy.

<Step title="The answer">

</Step>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Speeding up on a circle" points={9} mit="Problem 5">

MIT took this problem from the second quiz of this course in 2001. A particle of rest mass $M_0$ and charge $q$ (the sheet does not name the charge) is kept on a circular orbit of fixed radius $R$ by a magnetic field $B$ whose field lines are at right angles to the plane of the orbit. A force of constant size $F$ acts on the particle, always along its velocity. As the particle gains speed, the strength of the magnetic field is adjusted so that the radius of the orbit stays $R$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Start from $\vec F = d\vec p/dt$ and derive an equation for the growth of the particle's speed $u$ with time: $du/dt$ as a function of $\gamma$, $M_0$ and $F$. Here $\gamma$ is the particle's own Lorentz factor, $1/\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$. You need not solve the equation.

<Hint>

A force along the velocity changes the size of the momentum, $p = \gamma M_0u$, and a force at right angles to the velocity only turns it. So $F = dp/dt$ for the size $p$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Both $\gamma$ and $u$ depend on time. Differentiate the product $\gamma u$, using $d\gamma/dt = \gamma^3\,(u/c^2)\,du/dt$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$du/dt =$" expr="F/(gamma^3*M0)"
  vars={{ F: [1, 5], gamma: [1.5, 5], M0: [1, 4] }}
  names={{ gamma: "\\gamma", M0: "M_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "F/(gamma*M0)", hint: "That holds for a force at right angles to the velocity. Along the velocity, gamma itself grows as the speed grows, which gives two more factors of gamma." },

    { expr: "F/(gamma^2*M0)", hint: "Check the derivative of gamma u: it is gamma³ du/dt, because gamma(1 + gamma² u²/c²) = gamma³." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The tangential force changes the size of the momentum. The magnetic force only bends the path.

<Step title="Split the force">

The magnetic force is at right angles to the velocity. It does no work, and it supplies the change of the momentum's direction. The applied force $F$ is along the velocity and supplies the change of the momentum's size:

<Formula label="Tangential part of the equation of motion" tex="F = \frac{dp}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\gamma M_0 u\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Differentiate">

From $\gamma = (1 - u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$ follows $d\gamma/dt = \gamma^3 (u/c^2)\,du/dt$. Then

<Formula label="Derivative of gamma times u" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\gamma u\right) = \gamma\frac{du}{dt} + \gamma^3\frac{u^2}{c^2}\frac{du}{dt} = \gamma\left(1 + \gamma^2\beta^2\right)\frac{du}{dt} = \gamma^3\,\frac{du}{dt}" cite="reading2" at="slide 10 (the special case of parallel force and acceleration)" />

because $1 + \gamma^2\beta^2 = \gamma^2$. So

<Formula label="Growth of the speed" tex="\frac{du}{dt} = \frac{F}{\gamma^3 M_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For low speeds $\gamma \to 1$ and this is Newton's $F/M_0$. As $u \to c$ the factor $\gamma^3$ grows without limit and the speed creeps up ever more slowly, so $c$ is never reached. It agrees with the lesson's rule for a force along the motion (slide 10 of Lecture 12), which in the symbols of this problem reads $F = \gamma^3M_0\,du/dt$. </Step>

</Solution>

<Step title="The numbers">

$V_1 - V_2 = 1.85 - 0.82 = 1.03$ V. The frequencies are $c/\lambda_1 = 9.993\times 10^{14}$ Hz and $c/\lambda_2 = 7.495\times 10^{14}$ Hz, which differ by $2.498\times 10^{14}$ Hz:

<Formula label="The value of Planck's constant from the data" tex="h = \frac{1.602\times 10^{-19}\ \text{C} \times 1.03\ \text{V}}{2.498\times 10^{14}\ \text{Hz}} = 6.60\times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The accepted value is $6.626\times 10^{-34}$ J s, so these two measurements give $h$ to 0.3%. The units fit: a coulomb times a volt is a joule, and a joule divided by a hertz is a joule second. In the units of the experiment, $h = 4.12\times 10^{-15}$ eV s, or $hc = 1236$ eV nm.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find the work function $W_e$ of sodium from the same data. Answer in eV.

<Hint>

Put your $h$ back into either of the two equations. It is easiest to keep $hc$ in eV nm.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-w" label="$W_e =$" value="2.27" unit="eV" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-w" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The work function is what is missing between the photon's energy and the electron's kinetic energy.

<Step title="The photon energies">

From part (a), $hc = 1.03\ \text{eV}/(1/300\ \text{nm} - 1/400\ \text{nm}) = 1236$ eV nm. The 300 nm photons carry $1236/300 = 4.12$ eV:

<Formula label="Work function of sodium from the data" tex="W_e = \frac{hc}{\lambda_1} - q_eV_1 = 4.12\ \text{eV} - 1.85\ \text{eV} = 2.27\ \text{eV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The other wavelength must give the same: $1236/400 - 0.82 = 3.09 - 0.82 = 2.27$ eV. It does, as it has to, because $h$ was chosen to make the two agree. In the lesson's picture this is where the straight line of kinetic energy against frequency, continued downwards, cuts the energy axis.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Find the cutoff wavelength of sodium: the longest wavelength that still frees electrons. Answer in nm.

<Hint>

At the cutoff the photon's energy is just the work function, and the electrons come out with no kinetic energy.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-cutoff" label="$\lambda_0 =$" value="544" unit="nm" tolerance="2%" calc="p6-cutoff" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Below the photon energy $W_e$ no electron can escape, however bright the light.

<Step title="The cutoff">

<Formula label="Cutoff wavelength of sodium from the data" tex="\frac{hc}{\lambda_0} = W_e \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda_0 = \frac{hc}{W_e} = \frac{1236\ \text{eV nm}}{2.27\ \text{eV}} = 544\ \text{nm}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

544 nm is green light. It is longer than both wavelengths of the experiment, as it must be, since both of them freed electrons. Yellow and red light, with longer wavelengths, free no electrons from sodium at any brightness: Lenard's observation, which Einstein's photons explained (slides 20 to 22 of Lecture 13). The cutoff frequency is $c/\lambda_0 = 5.51\times 10^{14}$ Hz.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="The photon rocket" points={9} mit="Problem 7">

MIT's sheet asks the student to review the photon rocket problem of a textbook, to derive a quadratic equation for $f$, the mass of the payload as a fraction of the rocket's rest mass, and to say how feasible such a rocket is. The textbook is not used here. This is our own statement of the task, with our own numbers.

A rocket of rest mass $M_i$ is at rest in space. Its engine turns part of the rocket's rest mass completely into light and sends all of that light out of the back in one direction. When the engine stops, the rocket moves with the Lorentz factor $\gamma$ (speed $\beta c$), and what is left of it, the payload, has the rest mass $M_f = fM_i$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Use the conservation of energy and of momentum to derive a quadratic equation for $f$ in which only $\gamma$ appears, and solve it for $f$.

<Hint>

Call the total energy of the emitted light $E_\text{light}$. Its momentum is $E_\text{light}/c$, backwards. Write the conservation of energy and of momentum for the rocket and the light.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Move the light's energy and momentum to one side of each equation, square both, and subtract: for the payload $E^2 - (cp)^2 = (fM_ic^2)^2$. That gives $E_\text{light}$ in terms of $f$. Put it back into the energy equation.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-f" label="$f =$" expr="gamma - sqrt(gamma^2 - 1)"
  vars={{ gamma: [1.5, 6] }}
  names={{ gamma: "\\gamma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "gamma + sqrt(gamma^2 - 1)", hint: "That is the other root of the quadratic. It is greater than 1: a payload heavier than the rocket it came from." },
    { expr: "1/gamma", hint: "That would be right if the fuel's energy simply became the payload's energy. But the light carries energy away too: it has to, to carry the momentum." },
    { expr: "1/(2*gamma)", hint: "That is only the limit for large gamma. The exact root has a square root in it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Before, there is a rocket at rest. After, there is a moving payload and a pulse of light going the other way. Energy and momentum are conserved between the two.

<Step title="The two conservation laws">

<Formula label="Energy and momentum of the photon rocket" tex="M_ic^2 = \gamma fM_ic^2 + E_\text{light}, \qquad 0 = \gamma fM_i\,\beta c - \frac{E_\text{light}}{c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Eliminate the speed">

The payload has energy $M_ic^2 - E_\text{light}$ and momentum $E_\text{light}/c$, and the rest mass $fM_i$. The lesson's invariant (slide 5 of Lecture 12) gives

<Formula label="Energy of the light" tex="\left(M_ic^2 - E_\text{light}\right)^2 - E_\text{light}^2 = \left(fM_ic^2\right)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_\text{light} = \frac{1 - f^2}{2}\,M_ic^2" cite="reading2" at="slide 5" />

The payload's energy is then $M_ic^2 - E_\text{light} = \tfrac12(1 + f^2)M_ic^2$, and it must equal $\gamma fM_ic^2$:

<Formula label="Quadratic equation for the payload fraction" tex="f^2 - 2\gamma f + 1 = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

The two roots are $\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - 1}$. Their product is 1, so one is greater than 1 and the other smaller. The payload cannot be heavier than the rocket, so

<Formula label="Payload fraction of the photon rocket" tex="f = \gamma - \sqrt{\gamma^2 - 1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\gamma = 1$ (no burn) $f = 1$. For large $\gamma$, $f \approx 1/(2\gamma)$: to reach $\gamma = 10$ the payload can be only about 5% of the starting mass. The light's energy $\tfrac12(1 - f^2)M_ic^2$ is positive only for $f < 1$, which confirms the choice of root. (The other root, $f > 1$, describes light absorbed instead of emitted: a body at rest that swallows a pulse of light from behind is pushed to the Lorentz factor $\gamma$ and becomes heavier.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Write $f$ in terms of the final speed $\beta$ alone, and compare it with the photon rocket formula of the [lesson of Lecture 12](/courses/8-033/force-acceleration-interstellar-travel).

<Hint>

Divide the momentum equation of part (a) by $c$ and add it to the energy equation divided by $c^2$. The light drops out.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-beta" label="$f =$" expr="sqrt((1 - beta)/(1 + beta))"
  vars={{ beta: [0.1, 0.9] }}
  names={{ beta: "\\beta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt((1 + beta)/(1 - beta))", hint: "That is upside down: it is the starting mass over the payload mass, which is greater than 1." },
    { expr: "sqrt(1 - beta^2)", hint: "That is 1/gamma. The light takes energy away as well, so the payload is lighter than that." },
    { expr: "(1 - beta)/(1 + beta)", hint: "The square root is missing: gamma(1 + beta) is the square root of (1 + beta)/(1 − beta)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Adding the two conservation laws removes the light at once.

<Step title="Add the equations">

The energy equation divided by $c^2$ plus the momentum equation divided by $c$ gives $M_i = \gamma fM_i(1 + \beta)$, so

<Formula label="Payload fraction in terms of the speed" tex="f = \frac{1}{\gamma\left(1 + \beta\right)} = \frac{\sqrt{(1 - \beta)(1 + \beta)}}{1 + \beta} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compare with the lesson">

The lesson gives $M_i/M_f = \sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}$ for the photon rocket (its own addition to the slides' rocket discussion, slide 28 of Lecture 12). That is $1/f$: the same result.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

It also agrees with part (a): $1/f = \gamma(1 + \beta)$ and $f = \gamma(1 - \beta)$, since their product is $\gamma^2(1 - \beta^2) = 1$, and their sum is $2\gamma$, which is the quadratic $f + 1/f = 2\gamma$. The factor is the Doppler factor of Problem 4(d) for $\theta = 0$. The lesson's example, $\beta = 0.5$, gives $f = 1/\sqrt3 = 0.577$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How feasible is the photon rocket? Take a round trip to a star: the rocket speeds up to $\gamma = 10$, brakes to rest at the star, speeds up to $\gamma = 10$ again for the way home and brakes at home. Every one of the four burns is a photon burn as in part (a). What fraction of the rocket's starting mass comes home? (The trip and its numbers are ours.)

<Hint>

Braking from $\gamma$ to rest is a speeding-up burn seen from the frame in which the rocket ends at rest, so it costs the same factor $f$. Each burn starts with what the last one left.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-trip" label="Fraction that comes home:" value="6.31e-6" tolerance="2%" calc="p7-trip" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each burn multiplies the rest mass by $f$. Four burns multiply it by $f^4$.

<Step title="One burn">

<Formula label="Payload fraction for one burn to gamma = 10" tex="f = 10 - \sqrt{99} = 0.0501" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own numbers)" />

The speed reached is $\beta = \sqrt{1 - 1/\gamma^2} = 0.995$.

</Step>

<Step title="Four burns">

<Formula label="Fraction of the mass that comes home" tex="f^4 = \left(0.0501\right)^4 = 6.31\times 10^{-6}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own numbers)" />

Every kilogram that comes home needs about 158 000 kg at the start.

</Step>

<Step title="How feasible?">

Not at all, with anything we know how to build. The 158 tonnes per kilogram are the smallest part of the trouble, because this is the best possible rocket: light is the fastest exhaust there is. The fuel must turn into light completely, which only matter meeting antimatter does, so about half of the starting mass would be antimatter. The slides give the scale of that problem (slide 31 of Lecture 12): all the antiprotons made at CERN in a year would light a 100 W bulb for three seconds, and making them has an efficiency of about $10^{-8}$ percent. All of the light must then leave in one direction, though annihilation sends its products out in all directions and gamma rays cannot be steered with ordinary mirrors. (This judgement is our own.) The formula is exact, but the rocket is a thought experiment.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With the form of part (b): $f = \sqrt{(1 - 0.99499)/(1 + 0.99499)} = 0.0501$, the same. For a one-way trip without braking, one burn, 5% arrives. A trip at a modest $\gamma = 2$ ($\beta = 0.866$) has $f = 2 - \sqrt3 = 0.268$ and brings home $f^4 = 0.0052$, about half a percent of the starting mass.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 8 (optional)

<Exercise id="p8" title="The surface of revolution with the smallest area" points={5} mit="Problem 8" optional>

MIT calls this problem "More on the Calculus of Variations". Two points in the $xy$ plane, $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$, are joined by a curve $y(x)$. MIT's sketch shows both points to the right of the $y$ axis ($0 < x_1 < x_2$), with the second one higher up. The curve is then turned about the $y$ axis, so that it sweeps out a surface. Which curve $y(x)$ gives the surface with the smallest area?

<Part label="a" points={1}>

Show that a small piece of the curve has the length $ds = dx\,\sqrt{1 + (dy/dx)^2}$.

<Hint>

A very short piece of a smooth curve is straight. Use Pythagoras for its steps $dx$ and $dy$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Pythagoras for a short, nearly straight piece.

<Step title="The length">

The piece goes $dx$ sideways and $dy$ upwards, so $ds^2 = dx^2 + dy^2$. Taking $dx$ out of the root:

<Formula label="Element of arc length" tex="ds = \sqrt{dx^2 + dy^2} = dx\,\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}" cite="reading5" at="slide 7" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a horizontal piece $dy/dx = 0$ and $ds = dx$. For a line at 45° the length is $\sqrt2\,dx$, the diagonal of a square.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

Show that the area of the surface of revolution is

<Formula label="Area of the surface of revolution" tex="A = 2\pi\int_{x_1}^{x_2} x\,\sqrt{1 + y'^2}\;dx, \qquad y' = \frac{dy}{dx}" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(b), eq. (8.1), p. 3" />

(MIT's sheet prints the two limits of the integral the other way round, which would make the area negative. The order here is the one that fits its sketch.)

<Hint>

Turned about the $y$ axis, the short piece $ds$ at the distance $x$ from the axis sweeps out a thin band. How long is the band, and how wide?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each piece of the curve sweeps out a band whose length is the circumference of a circle of radius $x$.

<Step title="One band">

The piece $ds$ at distance $x$ from the axis moves round a circle of circumference $2\pi x$. The band it sweeps has that length and the width $ds$, so its area is $dA = 2\pi x\,ds$.

</Step>

<Step title="Add the bands">

With $ds$ from part (a), and adding from $x_1$ to $x_2$, the result is the integral above.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A horizontal line ($y' = 0$) from $x_1$ to $x_2$ sweeps a flat ring, and the integral gives $\pi(x_2^2 - x_1^2)$, the area of a ring. The formula needs $x > 0$ along the curve, as in the sketch.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

Apply the Euler–Lagrange equation to the integrand $L(x, y, y') = x\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$ (MIT's sheet calls the integrand $f$; here $f$ was the payload fraction of Problem 7), in the form

<Formula label="Euler–Lagrange equation" tex="\frac{\partial L}{\partial y} - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(c), eq. (8.2), p. 3" />

and show that the curve obeys

<Formula label="First integral for the smallest surface" tex="\frac{x\,y'}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = a = \text{constant}" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(c), eq. (8.3), p. 3" />

<Hint>

Does the integrand depend on $y$ itself? If not, the first term of the equation is zero, and the equation says that something has zero derivative.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The integrand contains $y'$ but not $y$, so $\partial L/\partial y'$ is a constant along the curve.

<Step title="The two derivatives">

$\partial L/\partial y = 0$, and

<Formula label="Derivative of the integrand with respect to the slope" tex="\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} = \frac{x\,y'}{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The equation">

The Euler–Lagrange equation becomes $\frac{d}{dx}\left(\partial L/\partial y'\right) = 0$, so this quantity does not change along the curve. Calling the constant $a$ gives the result. The constant $2\pi$ in front of the integral does not affect which curve is best.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$a$ is a length, like $x$. Since $|y'|/\sqrt{1 + y'^2} < 1$, the curve must stay at $x > |a|$. The choice $a = 0$ gives $y' = 0$, the flat ring, which is the answer when both points are at the same height.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={1}>

Solve for $dy/dx$ and show that the curve is given by the integral

<Formula label="The curve as an integral" tex="y = \int\frac{a}{\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}}\;dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(d), eq. (8.4), p. 3" />

Type your $dy/dx$ in the box (for a curve that rises, with $a > 0$).

<Hint>

Square the result of part (c) and collect the terms with $y'^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-slope" label="$dy/dx =$" expr="a/sqrt(x^2 - a^2)"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 1], x: [1.5, 4] }}
  names={{ a: "a" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "a/sqrt(x^2 + a^2)", hint: "Check the sign when you collect the terms: x² y′² − a² y′² = a²." },
    { expr: "a/x", hint: "That drops the square root of 1 + y′². It is right only far from the axis, where the slope is small." },
    { expr: "sqrt(x^2 - a^2)/a", hint: "That is upside down: it is dx/dy." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The first integral is an algebraic equation for $y'$.

<Step title="Solve for the slope">

Squaring, $x^2y'^2 = a^2(1 + y'^2)$, so $y'^2(x^2 - a^2) = a^2$ and

<Formula label="Slope of the curve" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(d), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Integrating over $x$ gives the integral of the statement.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Put it back: $\sqrt{1 + y'^2} = x/\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}$, so $xy'/\sqrt{1 + y'^2} = a$, as required. The slope is vertical at $x = a$, the point of the curve nearest to the axis, and falls off as $a/x$ far away.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={1}>

Look up the integral and show that the curve can be written as

<Formula label="The catenary" tex="y = a\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + b \qquad\text{or}\qquad x = a\cosh\left(\frac{y - b}{a}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(e), eqs. (8.5) and (8.6), pp. 3 and 4" />

where $b$ is a second constant of integration. This curve is called a catenary: it has the shape of a chain hanging freely between two supports.

<Hint>

Substitute $x = a\cosh w$. Then $\sqrt{x^2 - a^2} = a\sinh w$ and $dx = a\sinh w\,dw$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The substitution $x = a\cosh w$ turns the integrand into a constant.

<Step title="The integral">

With $x = a\cosh w$: $\sqrt{x^2 - a^2} = a\sinh w$ and $dx = a\sinh w\,dw$, so

<Formula label="Evaluating the integral" tex="y = \int\frac{a}{a\sinh w}\,a\sinh w\;dw = a\,w + b = a\cosh^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{a}\right) + b" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(e), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Solving for $x$: $(y - b)/a = \cosh^{-1}(x/a)$, so $x = a\cosh[(y - b)/a]$.

</Step>

<Step title="The two constants">

$a$ is the smallest distance of the curve from the axis, reached at the height $y = b$. The two constants are fixed by the demand that the curve passes through $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate $x = a\cosh[(y - b)/a]$: $dx/dy = \sinh[(y - b)/a] = \sqrt{x^2 - a^2}/a$, the inverse of the slope of part (d). The lesson's hanging chain is a hyperbolic cosine too, with the height $y$ as a cosh of the horizontal position $x$ ([Lecture 9](/courses/8-033/brachistochrone-catenary)): the same curve with $x$ and $y$ exchanged, because here the axis of rotation is the $y$ axis. The surface it sweeps out is called a catenoid, and a soap film stretched between two rings takes this shape, since a soap film makes its area as small as it can. (If the two points are too far apart along the axis compared with their distances from it, no catenary joins them, and the film breaks into two flat discs.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
