Lecture 16 completes electromagnetism: the second half of the theory, then "more advanced" and "even more advanced" material, and why the classical theory is fundamentally flawed (slide 3). (MIT's material for this lecture is a set of slides, in a deck titled "Lecture 15"; this site numbers lectures by MIT's class sessions, in which the first quiz took one. The slides use Gaussian, or c.g.s., units, in which $\vec B$ is measured in the same units as $\vec E$. This page follows the slides and marks what is not on them.)

## The second half of the theory

The [last lecture](/courses/8-033/electricity-implies-magnetism) derived the first half, how fields affect matter: the Lorentz force law $\vec F = q(\vec E + \frac1c\vec u\times\vec B)$, with the field transformations and the current 4-vector $J_4 = (\vec J, \rho c) = \rho_0U$ (slides 2, 4–5). The second half, how matter determines the fields, is Maxwell's equations (slide 6):

<Formula label="Maxwell's equations (Gaussian units)" tex="\nabla\cdot\vec E = 4\pi\rho, \qquad \nabla\times\vec B - \frac1c\dot{\vec E} = \frac{4\pi}{c}\vec J, \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec B = 0, \qquad \nabla\times\vec E + \frac1c\dot{\vec B} = 0" cite="mit" at="slide 6" />

Magnetism followed from assuming the first half is Lorentz invariant. Is the second half invariant too, as it must be for the theory to hold together (slide 7)? The wave equation, which gives Maxwell's equations' solutions in empty space, was already shown to be invariant; the full equations, with charges and currents, need more. (According to slide 7, the rest of this section is beyond what students are tested on; it is there to show how compact the theory becomes once it is written with 4-vectors.)

**Potentials** (slide 8). Vector calculus offers a shortcut: whenever a field's divergence vanishes it can be built as a curl, and whenever its curl vanishes it can be built as a gradient. Applied to the last two of Maxwell's equations, this gives

<Formula label="Potentials" tex="\vec E = -\nabla\phi - \frac1c\dot{\vec A}, \qquad \vec B = \nabla\times\vec A" cite="mit" at="slide 8" />

with the scalar potential $\phi$ and the vector potential $\vec A$. The magnetic field has zero divergence, so it is a curl; put that into the fourth equation and the combination $\vec E + \frac1c\dot{\vec A}$ turns out curl-free, so it is a gradient. The four potentials form the **4-potential** $A_4 = (A_x, A_y, A_z, \phi)$ (slide 8).

**The d'Alembertian** (slide 9). The operator

<Formula label="d'Alembertian and the wave equation" tex="\Box \equiv \left(\frac{\partial}{c\,\partial t}\right)^2 - \nabla^2, \qquad \Box\psi = 0" cite="mit" at="slide 9" />

is the spacetime version of the Laplacian $\nabla^2$, and it is Lorentz invariant; with it the wave equation for a field $\psi$ takes the compact form $\Box\psi = 0$.

**Gauge freedom and the result** (slide 10). The potentials are not unique: adding $\nabla\psi$ to $\vec A$ and $-\frac1c\partial\psi/\partial t$ to $\phi$, for any field $\psi$ (the slide asks that $\psi$ obey the wave equation, which keeps the Lorentz gauge below intact), leaves $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ unchanged. Using this freedom we can impose the Lorentz gauge condition, and Maxwell's first two equations then become

<Formula label="Lorentz gauge and Maxwell's equations as one wave equation" tex="\nabla\cdot\vec A + \frac1c\dot\phi = 0, \qquad \Box A_4 = \frac{4\pi}{c}J_4" cite="mit" at="slide 10" />

(The slide writes $\phi + \partial\psi/c\partial t$, $\nabla\cdot\vec A - \frac1c\dot\phi = 0$ and $\Box A_4 = -\frac{4\pi}{c}J_4$. With $\vec E = -\nabla\phi - \frac1c\dot{\vec A}$ and $\Box$ as on slide 9, the signs must be as above, which give for example $\Box\phi = 4\pi\rho$, Poisson's equation for static charges.) That solves Maxwell's equations: the 4-current determines the 4-potential, and where there are no charges each of the four components separately obeys the wave equation. And it proves what was wanted: $\Box$ is Lorentz invariant and $A_4$ and $J_4$ are 4-vectors, so the second half of the theory is Lorentz invariant too (slide 10). Slide 12 adds, for those who know index notation, a five-line synopsis of all of electromagnetism (there $\Box$ has the opposite sign, $\nabla^2 - \partial^2/c^2\partial t^2$, so its wave equation carries a minus sign); slide 11 shows how differently Maxwell himself wrote the equations: notation keeps evolving.

## Invariants and Einstein's puzzle

Two combinations of the fields are the same in every frame (slide 15):

<Formula label="Field invariants" tex="E^2 - B^2, \qquad \vec E\cdot\vec B" cite="mit" at="slide 15" />

(in Gaussian units). A field that is purely electric in one frame (positive $E^2 - B^2$) can never look purely magnetic in another, and fields that are perpendicular in one frame are perpendicular in all.

Slide 13 asks: what about Einstein's puzzle? (The page's own summary: Einstein's 1905 paper opens with a magnet and a conducting loop. If the magnet moves, classical theory says an electric field drives the current; if the loop moves, a magnetic force on its charges does. The current is the same either way; in relativity the two descriptions are the same field seen from two frames.)

## The field of a moving charge

To build intuition (slides 16–17), take a charge $q$ at rest at the origin of frame $S$. Its fields are $\vec E = q\vec r/r^3$ and $\vec B = 0$. In a frame $S'$ where the charge moves with velocity $v$ along $x$, the transformation rules give $E_x' = E_x$, $E_y' = \gamma E_y$, $E_z' = \gamma E_z$. Rewriting this at time $t' = 0$, when the charge passes the origin of $S'$, in the new coordinates ($x = \gamma x'$, $y = y'$, $z = z'$):

<Formula label="Field of a moving charge" tex="\vec E' = \frac{\gamma q\,\vec r\,'}{\left(\gamma^2x'^2 + y'^2 + z'^2\right)^{3/2}}" cite="mit" at="slide 17" />

Two conclusions (slide 17). First, the field is no longer the same in all directions: ahead of and behind the charge it is weaker by a factor $\gamma^2$, and across the motion stronger by a factor $\gamma$. Second, the field still points directly away from where the charge is *now*. That is surprising, because news of the charge's position travels at most at the speed of light: the field here and now was set up when the charge was at an earlier, "retarded" position. For steady motion the two pictures agree exactly; for an accelerating charge they do not, and that is where the trouble below begins.

## A fly in the ointment

For all its elegance, the classical theory contains a fatal problem, which only quantum mechanics removes (slide 19). The field of an accelerating electron acts back on the electron itself. For speeds $v \ll c$, this Abraham–Lorentz force is

<Formula label="Abraham–Lorentz force" tex="\vec F = \frac23\frac{q^2}{c^3}\dot{\vec a}, \qquad \dot{\vec a} = \omega\vec a, \qquad \omega = \frac32\frac{c^3m}{q^2} \approx \left(6.266 \times 10^{-24}\text{ s}\right)^{-1}" cite="mit" at="slide 19" />

a force set by how fast the acceleration itself changes (the third time derivative of position). Setting $\vec F = m\vec a$ gives $\dot{\vec a} = \omega\vec a$, with the number for an electron. The solution is $\vec a = \vec a_0e^{\omega t}$: an isolated electron would speed itself up exponentially, its velocity doubling roughly every $10^{-23}$ s. Real electrons do nothing of the kind (slide 19).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Light filling the universe">
Maxwell's wave equation is the theory of light, and the universe is full of it: the cosmic microwave background, [black-body radiation](/courses/8-286/black-body-radiation) left over from the hot early universe, whose energy density falls as the fourth power of the expansion. Because the theory is relativistic, a moving observer still sees a black-body spectrum in every direction, only with a temperature that depends on the direction, $T_0/(\gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta))$: the CMB dipole, which reveals our own motion. The fall of the temperature over cosmic time is a separate effect, due to the expansion.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Squeezed fields and a runaway electron">
<Problem>
(a) A charge passes you at $\gamma = 10$. By what factors are its field ahead and to the side changed compared with the static field at the same distance? (b) In SI units, the Abraham–Lorentz time for an electron is $\tau = 2e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 \cdot 3m_ec^3)$. Compute it with $e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C, $m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg, and find the doubling time $\tau\ln 2$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The pancake">
Ahead, $y = z = 0$: $E' = \gamma q x'/(\gamma^2x'^2)^{3/2} = q/(\gamma^2x'^2)$, a factor $1/\gamma^2 = 1/100$. To the side, $x' = 0$: $E' = \gamma q/y'^2$, a factor 10. The field is squeezed into a thin disc across the motion.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The runaway">
$\tau \approx 6.3 \times 10^{-24}$ s, matching the slide's $6.266 \times 10^{-24}$ s, and the doubling time is $\tau\ln 2 \approx 4.3 \times 10^{-24}$ s: of the order of $10^{-23}$ s, as the slide says.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Maxwell's equations give the fields from the charges; with the potentials combined into the 4-vector $A_4$ and the Lorentz gauge, they become $\Box A_4 = \frac{4\pi}{c}J_4$, manifestly Lorentz invariant.
- $E^2 - B^2$ and $\vec E\cdot\vec B$ are the same in every frame.
- A moving charge's field is weaker ahead and behind, stronger to the side, and points from its present position; an accelerating charge runs into the Abraham–Lorentz runaway, which only quantum mechanics cures.
