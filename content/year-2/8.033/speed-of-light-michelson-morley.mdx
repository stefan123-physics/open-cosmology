The [last lecture](/courses/8-033/symmetry-galilean-invariance) found classical mechanics invariant under translations, rotations and Galilean boosts (slides 2, 5). This one asks the same of electromagnetism: does light's speed depend on its wavelength, on the motion of the source, or on the motion of the observer, and how can 8.01 be reconciled with 8.02 (slide 3)?

## The wave equation is not Galilean invariant

The classical wave equation, met in [8.03](/courses/8-03/electromagnetic-waves) (slide 7):

<Formula label="Classical wave equation" tex="\nabla^2E - \frac{1}{c_w^2}\frac{\partial^2E}{\partial t^2} = 0, \qquad \frac{d^2E}{dx^2} - \frac{1}{c_w^2}\frac{d^2E}{dt^2} = 0, \qquad y = Af(x - c_wt) + Bf(x + c_wt)" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

Here $E$ can be a component of the electric or magnetic field, the air density, the height of a water surface or the displacement of a guitar string, and $c_w$ is the wave speed; in one dimension any smooth shape moving at $\pm c_w$ is a solution, and in three dimensions wavefronts still move at $c_w$ (slide 7). The wave equation can be derived from classical mechanics, which is Galilean invariant; so is the wave equation invariant too (slide 8)?

Apply $x' = x - vt$, $t' = t$, with the chain rule, $\frac{\partial}{\partial x} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x'}$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial t} = -v\frac{\partial}{\partial x'} + \frac{\partial}{\partial t'}$ (slide 9; the slide's hint uses $x' = x + vt$ and the matching $+v$ in the chain rule, but its result, with $+2v$ in the cross term, belongs to $x' = x - vt$; the slide also prints the cross-term coefficient as $2v/c$, for $2v/c^2$):

<Formula label="Wave equation after a Galilean boost" tex="\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)\frac{d^2E}{dx'^2} - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{d^2E}{dt'^2} + 2\frac{v}{c^2}\frac{d^2E}{dx'dt'} = 0" cite="mit" at="slide 9" />

The wave equation is **not** invariant. The new equation has solutions $Af(x - c't) + Bf(x + c't)$ moving at $c - v$ in one direction and $c + v$ in the other: just what one expects for waves in a substance, an "aether", where velocities add (slide 10). For sound that is fine: there is air. But how can this square with the wave equation coming from Galilean-invariant mechanics, and which do we trust more, classical mechanics or electromagnetism (slides 10–11)?

## What experiments say about the speed of light

**Wavelength** (slide 13). Light's speed in glass does depend on wavelength; in vacuum? **Gamma-ray bursts** last from a few seconds to minutes and were once blamed on everything from nearby neutron stars to "civilization annihilation"; they turned out to come from cosmological distances, a few billion light years, about $10^{17}$ light-seconds. Their flashes arrive in X-rays and visible light within about $10^2$ s, so

<Formula label="Speed of light versus wavelength" tex="\frac{\Delta t}{t} \lesssim \frac{10^2\ \text{s}}{10^{17}\ \text{s}} = 10^{-15}, \qquad c = \frac{d}{t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\Delta c}{c} \approx \frac{\Delta t}{t} \lesssim 10^{-15}" cite="mit" at="slide 13" />

No dependence, at least not more than about $10^{-15}c \approx 300$ nm/s.

**Motion of the source** (slide 14). A bullet's speed depends on the rifle's; sound's speed does not depend on the gun's. Binary stars give a sharp test. If velocities added, light from a star moving at $v$ towards and away from us across a distance $d$ would take $t_1 = d/(c - v)$ and $t_2 = d/(c + v)$:

<Formula label="Binary-star test" tex="\Delta t \equiv t_1 - t_2 = \frac{2dv}{c^2 - v^2} \approx 2\frac{d}{c}\frac{v}{c} = 2t\frac{v}{c} \approx 200\ \text{years}" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

for, say, a pulsar in the Large Magellanic Cloud, $v = 300$ km/s, $d = 100{,}000$ light years. But half an orbit takes only about two days: we would see a scrambled "Doppler effect" proportional to the acceleration instead of the velocity, and even see the star move backwards in time whenever $a > c^2/d$ towards us (slide 14). Nothing of the kind is seen: no dependence on source motion.

**Motion of the observer** (slides 15–17). No first-order effect had been seen, so Albert Michelson, the first American to win a Nobel Prize in the sciences (1907; the slides say simply "first American to win Nobel Prize"), and Edward Morley "hammered it". Their **interferometer** splits light into two arms, $L_\parallel$ along its motion through the supposed aether and $L_\perp$ across it, and recombines it. With the apparatus moving at $v$, light going along and back takes

<Formula label="Round-trip times" tex="t_\parallel = \frac{L_\parallel}{c - v} + \frac{L_\parallel}{c + v} = \frac{2L_\parallel}{c}\gamma^2, \qquad t_\perp = \frac{2L_\perp}{c}\gamma, \qquad \gamma \equiv \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}" cite="mit" at="slide 16" />

the second because across the arm the light must cover $\sqrt{L_\perp^2 + (vt_\perp/2)^2}$ each way. The difference is $\Delta t = t_\perp - t_\parallel = \frac{2L_\perp}{c}\gamma - \frac{2L_\parallel}{c}\gamma^2$; rotating the apparatus by 90° swaps the roles of the arms, and to lowest order in $v/c$, with $\gamma \approx 1 + \frac12(v/c)^2$ (slide 17, which drops the ½):

<Formula label="Expected change on rotation" tex="\Delta t' - \Delta t \approx \frac{L_\parallel + L_\perp}{c}\left(\frac vc\right)^2, \qquad \frac{\Delta t' - \Delta t}{t} \approx \left(\frac vc\right)^2" cite="mit" at="slide 17" />

With Earth's orbital speed, $v \approx 30$ km/s, $(v/c)^2 \sim 10^{-8}$: tough to measure. But their $L_\parallel + L_\perp \approx 11$ m was about $2 \times 10^7$ wavelengths of $\lambda \sim 500$ nm light, and they could see fringe shifts as small as $0.01\lambda$ (slide 17). They saw no shift at all: $c$ appears not to depend on the frame (slide 17). The answer to all three questions: "No, no and no!" (slide 18).

**Rescue attempts**, following Resnick's table (slides 19–20):

- **Lorentz–FitzGerald contraction**: the arm along the motion shrinks, $L_\parallel \to L_\parallel/\gamma$, cancelling the effect; ruled out by Kennedy and Thorndike (1932) with an interferometer of unequal arms.
- **Aether drag** (the Earth carries its aether along): ruled out by stellar aberration and by light propagation in moving water (Fizeau, 1851).
- **Emission theories** ($v$ depends on the source's speed): ruled out by binary stars, by Michelson–Morley with extraterrestrial light, and by the speed of γ-rays from particle decays at CERN.

## What to make of it

Which quantities each symmetry leaves unchanged can be tabulated (slide 21): time, lengths, $\nabla^2$, acceleration, mass and Newtonian mechanics are invariant under Galilean boosts; electromagnetism is not. (The table marks $F = ma$ as not invariant, although, as the last lecture showed, it is.) So what is wrong: the idea that all inertial frames are equivalent, our mechanics (8.01), or our electromagnetism (8.02) (slide 22)? Parity applies to some things and not others; is Galilean symmetry the same (slide 23)? Experiments say physics is the same in all inertial frames, for mechanics and electromagnetism alike (slide 23). Then (slide 24):

- the Galilean transformation works for mechanics but fails for electromagnetism;
- the Lorentz transformation works for electromagnetism but fails for Newtonian mechanics;
- no transformation works for both, so at least one theory must be wrong;
- making electromagnetism Galilean invariant is ruled out by experiment;
- so change mechanics to be Lorentz invariant: "BINGO!" It agrees with the old experiments and triumphed with new ones.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Light as the universe's messenger">
Every distance and velocity in cosmology rests on light whose speed is the same for all observers: the [Doppler shift](/courses/8-286/doppler-effect) of a galaxy's spectrum measures its motion, and the light travel time sets how far back we see. Gamma-ray bursts, the slides' test of $c$, come from billions of light years away, from when the universe was markedly younger.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Tests of your own">
<Problem>
(a) A tabletop interferometer has two arms of $1.2$ m each and uses light of $633$ nm. If it moved through an aether at $30$ km/s, what fringe shift would rotating it by 90° produce? (b) A binary star $1{,}000$ light years away has an orbital speed of $100$ km/s. If light's speed added to the star's, by how much would light emitted while approaching overtake light emitted while receding?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Interferometer">
The shift in fringes is $(L_\parallel + L_\perp)/\lambda \times (v/c)^2 = (2.4/633 \times 10^{-9}) \times 10^{-8} \approx 0.038$: smaller than Michelson and Morley's 0.2, but still detectable at their sensitivity of 0.01.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Binary star">
$\Delta t \approx 2t\,v/c = 2 \times 1{,}000\ \text{years} \times (100/300{,}000) \approx 0.67$ years, about 240 days. An orbit taking days would arrive hopelessly scrambled; real binaries look perfectly orderly.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The wave equation changes under a Galilean boost: its waves would move at $c \pm v$, as in an aether.
- Light's speed depends neither on wavelength ($\Delta c/c \lesssim 10^{-15}$ from gamma-ray bursts), nor on the source (binary stars), nor on the observer (Michelson–Morley: no fringe shift where about 0.2 was expected).
- Aether contraction, aether drag and emission theories all fail.
- Since physics is the same in all inertial frames and electromagnetism cannot be made Galilean, mechanics must become Lorentz invariant: special relativity.
