(MIT's material for this lecture is five slides; the solutions are not on them, so this page gives them in its own words, and recomputes the figure's times.)

## The brachistochrone

Slide 2 sums up the last two lectures with a figure: a bead starts at rest and slides without friction to a point 100 feet across and 100 feet down, along three shapes. The times shown are 3.54 s on a flat board, 3.23 s on a cycloid and 3.23 s on a circular arc (slide 2; the experiment itself is on a photograph withheld on OCW for copyright reasons, slide 4).

Measure $y$ downward from the start. A bead that has fallen a height $y$ has speed $v = \sqrt{2gy}$, so the time along a curve $y(x)$ is

<Formula label="Time of descent" tex="T = \int\frac{ds}{v} = \int\frac{\sqrt{1 + y'^2}}{\sqrt{2gy}}\,dx" cite="mit" at="slide 2 (figure and ds² only; the integral is the page's own)" />

using $ds^2 = dx^2 + dy^2$ from the figure. The integrand does not depend on $x$, so $L - y'\,\partial L/\partial y'$ is constant along the best curve. Working this out:

<Formula label="First integral" tex="\frac{1}{\sqrt{2gy}\sqrt{1 + y'^2}} = \text{constant}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad y\left(1 + y'^2\right) = 2R" cite="mit" at="slide 3 (problem listed; the formula is the page's own)" />

for some length $R$. Its solution, starting at the origin, is a cycloid, the curve traced by a point on the rim of a wheel of radius $R$ rolling along the underside of the line $y = 0$:

<Formula label="Cycloid" tex="x = R\left(\theta - \sin\theta\right), \qquad y = R\left(1 - \cos\theta\right), \qquad T = \theta\sqrt{\frac{R}{g}}" cite="mit" at="slide 3 (problem listed; the formula is the page's own)" />

The time follows because along the cycloid $ds = 2R\sin(\theta/2)\,d\theta$ and $v = 2\sqrt{gR}\sin(\theta/2)$. (The derivation is the page's own.)

**The figure's numbers, recomputed** (with $g = 32$ ft/s², which reproduces them). A flat board at 45°, 141.4 ft long with acceleration $g/\sqrt 2$, takes 3.54 s. The cycloid through (100 ft, 100 ft) has $\theta \approx 2.41$ and $R \approx 57.3$ ft, and takes 3.23 s. The circular arc (a quarter circle of radius 100 ft, vertical at the start) takes about 3.28 s by numerical integration, not 3.23 s as the figure says: slower than the cycloid, as it must be, though only by 0.05 s.

The lesson of the brachistochrone: the fastest path is not the shortest. Dropping steeply at first gains speed early, which more than pays for the longer path.

## The hanging chain

A uniform chain hanging between two supports takes the shape that makes its potential energy, $\int y\,ds$ times the weight per length, as small as possible, at a fixed length $\int ds$ (slide 3 lists this "catenary" problem; the slide's spelling "caternary" is a typo). Adding the length condition with a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ gives the integrand $(y - \lambda)\sqrt{1 + y'^2}$, again without $x$, and the same first integral leads to

<Formula label="Catenary" tex="y = \lambda + a\cosh\frac{x - x_0}{a}" cite="mit" at="slide 3 (problem listed; the formula is the page's own)" />

with $a$ set by the chain's length and the supports (the derivation is the page's own). Near its lowest point the catenary looks like a parabola, $y \approx \lambda + a + (x - x_0)^2/(2a)$, but it rises faster further out.

## Lagrangians in physics

The same equation runs all of mechanics (this section is the page's own, beyond the slides' list). With time as the variable and the Lagrangian $L = \frac12 m\dot x^2 - V(x)$, kinetic minus potential energy, the Euler–Lagrange equation reads $\frac{d}{dt}(m\dot x) = -dV/dx$: Newton's second law. In relativity a free particle's Lagrangian is $L = -mc^2\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$, which is $-mc^2$ times the rate of proper time, so the stationary path is the straight world line of the [last lecture](/courses/8-033/geodesics-calculus-of-variations); and $\partial L/\partial u = \gamma m u$ is the relativistic momentum, the subject of the next lecture.

The last slide shows how far this reaches: the whole Standard Model of particle physics, every known particle and force except gravity, written as a single Lagrangian (slide 5, after T. D. Gutierrez). The rest of the lecture was midterm review (slide 3).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A universe shaped like a cycloid">
The cycloid returns in cosmology. For a closed, matter-dominated universe, the scale factor and time are $a \propto 1 - \cos\theta$ and $t \propto \theta - \sin\theta$: the [scale factor of a closed universe](/courses/8-286/closed-universe-cycloid) traces exactly the brachistochrone's curve (turned upside down), rising from the big bang to a maximum and falling back to a big crunch.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="A slide and a chain">
<Problem>
(a) A playground designer builds a cycloid slide with $R = 5$ m, from the top to its lowest point ($\theta = \pi$). How long does the ride take (no friction, $g = 9.81$ m/s²), and how long would a straight ramp between the same points take? (b) A chain hangs as $y = a\cosh(x/a)$ with $a = 2$ m between supports at $x = \pm 3$ m. How deep is the sag, and how long is the chain?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The slide">
The end point is $\pi R \approx 15.7$ m across and $2R = 10$ m down. On the cycloid, $T = \pi\sqrt{R/g} \approx 2.24$ s. The straight ramp is 18.6 m long with acceleration $g \times 10/18.6 \approx 5.27$ m/s², so it takes $\sqrt{2 \times 18.6/5.27} \approx 2.66$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The chain">
The sag is $a(\cosh 1.5 - 1) \approx 2.70$ m, and the length is $\int\sqrt{1 + \sinh^2(x/a)}\,dx = 2a\sinh 1.5 \approx 8.52$ m.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The curve of fastest descent, the brachistochrone, is a cycloid; it beats the straight board and the circular arc.
- A hanging chain is a catenary, $y = \lambda + a\cosh((x - x_0)/a)$.
- With $L$ = kinetic minus potential energy, the Euler–Lagrange equation gives Newton's laws; with $L = -mc^2\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}$ it gives the relativistic momentum $\gamma mu$.
