It opens with a recap of [Schwarzschild orbits](/courses/8-033/schwarzschild-orbits-perihelion), with the table of interesting circular orbits and Mercury's perihelion advance (slides 2–4), and a simulated view of a black hole in front of the Milky Way, by Andrew Hamilton (slides 1 and 6). (MIT's material for this lecture is a set of slides, in a deck titled "Lecture 24"; this site numbers lectures by MIT's class sessions. Several slides were films, and several images are used with permission from their owners, Andrew Hamilton's black-hole simulations and NASA's images among them; this page describes them in words only.)

## Gravitational lensing

A galaxy between us and a more distant source bends the source's light; when the three are perfectly aligned we see an Einstein ring, otherwise several images (slides 7–9). Slide 10 shows a real example, the source 1938+666: a foreground galaxy turns its light into a ring in a Hubble Space Telescope infrared image, while a MERLIN radio image shows only an arc, because the radio source is slightly off the line of sight.

**Photon orbits** (slides 11–12). The orbit equations of the last lecture do not work for photons, because $d\tau = 0$ for them. Eliminating $d\tau$ and letting the rest mass go to zero gives, as Taylor and Wheeler show,

<Formula label="Photon orbits" tex="\left(\frac{dr}{dt}\right)^2 = \gamma_r^{-4}\left[1 - \left(\frac{b}{\gamma_rr}\right)^2\right], \qquad r\frac{d\varphi}{dt} = \pm\frac{b}{r\gamma_r^2}, \qquad b \equiv \frac{L}{E}" cite="mit" at="slide 12" />

where the impact parameter $b$ is the only constant of motion to keep track of. It is related to the distance of closest approach by $b = \gamma_{r_{\text{min}}}r_{\text{min}}$; with $M = 0$, $b = r_{\text{min}}$, so $b$ is the closest the photon would come if the mass were not there and it moved in a straight line. The only closed photon orbit is the circle at $r = 3M$ (slide 13).

**The deflection angle** (slide 14). A photon arriving from far away is deflected by

<Formula label="Deflection angle" tex="\Delta\varphi = -\pi + 2\int_0^1\left[1 - u^2 - \frac{2M}{r_{\text{min}}}\left(1 - u^3\right)\right]^{-1/2}du, \qquad b = \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r_{\text{min}}}\right)^{-1/2}r_{\text{min}}" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

Slide 15 plots this integral, done numerically, against $b/M$, and slide 16 lists what it shows. For $b \gg M$, $\Delta\varphi \approx 4M/b$: twice the Newtonian guess, $2M/b$ (Einstein used this for starlight passing the Sun, confirmed by Eddington in 1919 and later to 0.1% with radio waves from the quasar 3C273). As $b$ falls towards $\sqrt{27}M \approx 5.196M$, $\Delta\varphi$ grows without limit: the photon circles many times near $r = 3M$. For $b$ below $\sqrt{27}M$ the photon is captured, so a black hole looks like a black disk of radius $\sqrt{27}M$, not $2M$, with 27/4 times the area one might naively expect. At $b \approx 5.357M$, $\Delta\varphi = \pi$: light comes back towards you, so you could see your own image in a ring around the black hole, and further rings of images pile up towards the edge of the disk (slide 16). Slides 17–19 show lensing films, the lensing galaxy cluster Abell 2218, and the lecturer's own face distorted by a black hole; slides 20–23 show what black holes look like and a film of the galactic centre.

What's wrong with the familiar artist's picture of a black hole with its disk (slide 24)? (The page's own answer: it draws the disk as if light travelled in straight lines; in reality lensing would bend the far side of the disk into view above and below the hole.)

**The Einstein field equations** (slides 25–26, optional): how matter curves spacetime. In index notation, $G_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi GT_{\mu\nu}$, where the Einstein tensor $G_{\mu\nu}$ is built from the metric and its first and second derivatives, and $T_{\mu\nu}$, the stress-energy tensor, describes the matter. Setting $T_{\mu\nu} = 0$ outside a spherical mass gives the Schwarzschild metric; for the FRW metric, $T_{\mu\nu}$ is diagonal and the same everywhere (slide 26). (The page adds that the field equations then become the Friedmann equations.)

## Evidence for general relativity

The evidence (slides 27–28): gravitational redshift (in GPS, say); the factor of 2 in the small-angle light deflection; Mercury's perihelion shift; the Shapiro time delay of radar signals passing the Sun; the apparent existence of black holes; the cosmological redshift in an expanding universe; and the Hulse–Taylor pulsar. Future tests in 2006: Gravity Probe B and gravitational waves (LIGO, LISA and others). (Later developments: Gravity Probe B confirmed frame dragging in 2011, LIGO detected gravitational waves from merging black holes in 2015, and the Event Horizon Telescope imaged black hole shadows in 2019 and 2022.)

**The Hulse–Taylor pulsar**, PSR 1913+16 (slides 29–32). Discovered in 1974, it earned Hulse and Taylor the 1993 Nobel Prize. A neutron star spinning once every 59 milliseconds circles a companion on an eccentric 7.75-hour orbit, coming within 1.1 solar radii of it and receding to 4.8. Gravitational waves carry energy away, so the orbit shrinks by about 3.1 mm on each pass, just as general relativity predicts; in about 300 million years the two will merge (the figure on slide 31 says 250 million). The closeness and density of the pair make the orbit precess by 4.2° per year, whereas Mercury needs about 8400 years for 1°. (Slide 31 labels the shrinkage "3.1 mm/year"; per orbit is right, which is about 3.5 m per year.) Slide 32 shows a mass–mass diagram for a binary pulsar, in which each measured relativistic effect is a curve and their crossing pins down both masses (the slide does not say which pulsar).

**42** (slides 33–37). What is the answer to the question of life, the universe and everything? 42, but what is the question? Two candidates: the outer radius of a rainbow, about 42°; and the maximum accretion efficiency of a black hole, for one spinning as fast as possible, $1 - 3^{-1/2} \approx 0.42$, or 42% (compared with 5.7% for a non-spinning hole).

## Time travel and wormholes

A brief history of time travel (slides 38–39): H. G. Wells's *The Time Machine* (1895); special relativity makes travel to the future possible (1905); general relativity (1916); Gödel's demonstration that a whole universe could act as a time machine (dated 1937 on the slide; his rotating universe appeared in 1949); a huge rotating cylinder that allows time travel, found by Tipler (1974); Thorne's proposal to use wormholes (1988); and Gott's cosmic strings (1991).

Kruskal's coordinates show that the singularity is not simply a point, and that the full Schwarzschild solution has another side: a diagram with our universe, another universe, a future and a past singularity (slides 40–41). Slides 42–44 picture a black hole's funnel, a wormhole joining two sheets of space, and a wormhole joining two places in one universe. But can a wormhole be made stable (slide 45)?

## Mysteries

The course ends with the unsolved problems (slides 46–48): where the constants come from and why there are 3 + 1 dimensions; quantum gravity; proton decay; supersymmetry; the Higgs; neutrino properties; and dark energy, dark matter and inflation, which raise the question whether general relativity is correct on the largest scales. Many questions for you to answer. Some final words: think for yourself (slides 49–50).

<WorkedExample title="Starlight, a shadow and a shrinking orbit">
<Problem>
(a) By how much does the Sun ($GM = 1.327 \times 10^{20}$ m³/s², $R = 6.957 \times 10^8$ m) bend starlight grazing its edge? (b) The black hole at the centre of our galaxy has about 4 million solar masses and is about 8.0 kpc away ($1\text{ kpc} = 3.086 \times 10^{19}$ m). How large, in microarcseconds, is the diameter of its shadow, $2\sqrt{27}GM/c^2$? (c) How much does the Hulse–Taylor orbit shrink per year, at 3.1 mm per orbit of 7.75 hours?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The Sun">
$\Delta\varphi = 4GM/(c^2R) = 4 \times 1.327 \times 10^{20}/((2.998 \times 10^8)^2 \times 6.957 \times 10^8) \approx 8.5 \times 10^{-6}$ rad, about 1.75 arcseconds.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The shadow of Sagittarius A*">
$GM/c^2 \approx 5.9 \times 10^9$ m, so the shadow's diameter is $2\sqrt{27} \times 5.9 \times 10^9 \approx 6.1 \times 10^{10}$ m. At $2.47 \times 10^{20}$ m that subtends about 51 microarcseconds. (A later development: in 2022 the Event Horizon Telescope measured a ring of about 52 microarcseconds.)
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The pulsar">
A year has about $8766/7.75 \approx 1131$ orbits, so the orbit shrinks by about $1131 \times 3.1$ mm $\approx 3.5$ m per year.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Light passing a mass is deflected by $4GM/(c^2b)$, twice the Newtonian guess; light with $b$ below $\sqrt{27}GM/c^2$ is captured, giving a black hole its shadow.
- General relativity has passed every test: redshift, light bending, Mercury, the Shapiro delay, black holes, the expanding universe, and the Hulse–Taylor pulsar's gravitational waves.
- Time travel to the future is everyday relativity; to the past would need exotic spacetimes such as stable wormholes, and many mysteries remain.
