Lecture 20, the third of four on cosmology, covers the brief history of the universe again, now with the evidence (slide 5). It opens with George Gamow (1904–1968), a pioneer of the hot big bang (slide 1). After a recap of the FRW metric, the Friedmann equation with the 2006 parameters, the list of confusing questions and the brief history from the [last lecture](/courses/8-033/expanding-universe-history) (slides 2–8), it turns to what we actually know. (MIT's material for this lecture is a set of slides, in a deck titled "Lecture 18", the same number as the deck before it; this site numbers lectures by MIT's class sessions. Many slides are pictures credited to NASA, WMAP and others; this page describes them in words.)

## Evidence for what?

The slides list the evidence for the big bang (slide 9):

- the observed recession of galaxies (Hubble's law);
- the existence of the cosmic microwave background (CMB);
- the correct predictions of big bang nucleosynthesis;
- the darkness of the night sky (Olbers' paradox);
- distant objects look younger.

But evidence for *what*, exactly? The slide's answer: our entire observable universe was once as hot as the core of the Sun (and hotter still before that), and expanding so fast that it doubled in size in under a second. That is plenty of "bang" for most people. It is *not* evidence for a singularity (slide 9). (The page's own gloss: in the radiation era $a \propto t^{1/2}$, so sizes double each time the age quadruples; at an age of a tenth of a second, the universe doubled in size in three tenths of a second, and nucleosynthesis, a few minutes later, is the earliest epoch that we test in detail.)

**Olbers' paradox** (the explanation is the page's own; the slide only lists it). If the universe were infinite, eternal, static and filled with stars, every line of sight would end on a star's surface, and the whole sky would be as bright as the Sun. The night sky is dark because the universe has a finite age, so light from beyond a certain distance has not reached us yet, and because the expansion redshifts and dims the light of distant sources.

## Evidence 1: the universe is expanding

Hubble's law, $v = Hr$ (slide 10). Slides 11–12 show an artist's sketch of the expanding universe, from the quantum fluctuations of inflation through the afterglow light pattern at 400,000 years, the dark ages, the first stars at about 400 million years, and the development of galaxies to today's accelerated expansion, 13.7 billion years in all. Distant light is **redshifted**, its wavelength stretched by the expansion, and **dimmed** (slides 12–14). Measuring the expansion needs both a redshift and a distance, and distances come from "standard candles, rulers or clocks", objects whose true brightness, size or timescale we know (slide 14).

Hubble's own data (1929) gave about 550 km/s/Mpc, from galaxies out to about 2 Mpc; Riess and colleagues (1996), using supernovae out to 500 Mpc, found about 70 km/s/Mpc (slide 16, under the heading "How measure distance & redshift?"). Slide 15 shows Hubble and a photograph captioned "Mt. Wilson Observatory 1931". Slides 17–19 return to the deceleration parameter $q_0$: decelerating universes, which may recollapse, are younger than a coasting one ($q_0 = 0$), and an accelerating universe is older still, its expansion sped up by a repulsion between galaxies (slide 18, an illustration credited to STScI/NASA).

## Evidence 2: the cosmic microwave background exists

The universe is filled with radiation at a temperature of about 2.726 K (slide 21). A very brief history (slides 22 and 31): nucleosynthesis at 3 minutes, the last scattering of the CMB photons at about $3 \times 10^5$ years, and galaxy formation by about $3 \times 10^9$ years. Slides 22–24 show WMAP and its sky maps, including a foreground-cleaned map from a 2003 paper by Tegmark and colleagues.

Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson discovered the CMB in 1965 (slides 25–26). The COBE satellite then measured its spectrum: COBE's data points lie on a black-body curve, with measurements from other experiments scattered around it (slides 27–28). The 2006 Nobel Prize in Physics went to George Smoot, an MIT graduate, and John Mather for this work; several others associated with MIT worked on COBE: Chuck Bennett, Ed Cheng, Steve Meyer, Rai Weiss and Ned Wright (slide 27; Mather's name is cut off on the slide).

## Evidence 3: big bang nucleosynthesis

In the first few minutes, the universe was a nuclear reactor. Gamow's idea (slides 29–30) is that the light elements were made then, and the theory correctly predicts their abundances (slide 29). Slide 32 credits a comparison of predicted and measured abundances from a 2000 review by Tytler and colleagues (the figure is not in the OCW file). (The page's own note: helium-4 and deuterium agree with one value of the density of ordinary matter; lithium-7 is observed at about a third of the predicted amount, the "lithium problem", still unsolved.)

<Problem>
(a) What Hubble time does Hubble's 1929 value, $H \approx 550$ km/s/Mpc, give, and why was that a problem? (b) What is the typical energy $kT$ of a CMB photon at $T = 2.726$ K ($k = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/K), and at what wavelength does the black-body spectrum peak (Wien's law, $\lambda_{\max} = 2.898 \times 10^{-3}$ m K$/T$)?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A universe younger than the Earth">
$1/H = 9.778\text{ Gyr}/5.5 \approx 1.8$ billion years, much less than the age of the Earth, about 4.5 billion years. Hubble's distances were too small: Baade found in the 1950s that two kinds of Cepheid variable stars had been confused, which roughly doubled the distances, and Sandage's later corrections (bright gas clouds mistaken for stars, among others) supplied most of the rest of the factor of about 7.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The CMB">
$kT = 8.617 \times 10^{-5} \times 2.726 \approx 2.3 \times 10^{-4}$ eV. The spectrum peaks at $2.898 \times 10^{-3}/2.726 \approx 1.06$ mm: microwaves.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The evidence: galaxies recede (Hubble's law), the CMB exists with a black-body spectrum at 2.726 K, nucleosynthesis predicts the light elements, the night sky is dark, and distant objects look younger.
- It shows that our observable universe was once hot, dense and rapidly expanding; it does not show a singularity.
- Distant light is redshifted and dimmed; distances come from standard candles, rulers and clocks.
