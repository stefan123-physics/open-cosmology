## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within 2% of ours (1% where the box says so), so keep at least three significant figures, and type it in the unit shown next to the box. Small and large numbers can be typed as `7.2e23` or `7.2 × 10^23`. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `g*t/sqrt(1 + (g*t/c)^2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Write the symbols of the box exactly as the box names them (`ux` stands for $u'_x$, `phi` for $\phi$).

MIT gives the points per problem and, for Problems 1 and 6, per part. The other splits between parts are ours. Much of this set asks you to show something: those parts have no box or only a small one, so open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

Throughout, $u$ is the velocity of an object within one frame and $v$ the velocity of one frame relative to another, with $\beta = v/c$ and $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$, as in the lessons. The frame S′ moves with speed $v$ along the $x$ axis of the frame S, and a prime marks a quantity measured in S′. The rest mass is $m_0$ (or $M_0$ for the rocket), as on the slides of Lecture 12. Where a number is needed, take $c = 2.998\times 10^{8}$ m/s.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The great neutron shoot-out" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

Sue and Jim are two experimenters at rest relative to each other, at different places in space. Each fires neutrons at the other, and every neutron leaves its gun at the speed $0.60c$ relative to the gun. Jim reports five observations, (a) to (e) below.

A third observer, Sam, uses a frame S in which Sue's neutrons are at rest. For each of Jim's observations, say what Sam reports in its place.

To fix the directions (our choice), let the $x$ axis point from Jim to Sue. Jim's neutrons then fly in the $+x$ direction and Sue's in the $-x$ direction. Jim's frame is S′, and Jim's values carry a prime.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Jim: "My separation from Sue is 10 km." What separation does Sam measure, in km?

<Hint>

How fast, and in which direction, do Jim and Sue move in Sam's frame? Sam's frame is the one in which Sue's neutrons stand still.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Jim and Sue are like the two ends of a stick 10 km long that is at rest in Jim's frame. Sam measures a moving stick.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-sep" label="Separation for Sam:" value="8.0" unit="km" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-sep" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In Sam's frame Jim and Sue both move at $0.60c$, so the distance between them is a moving length, and it is contracted.

<Step title="How the frames move">

Sue's neutrons move at $-0.60c$ along $x$ in Jim's frame S′ and are at rest in S. So S moves at $-0.60c$ relative to S′, and S′ (with Jim and Sue in it) moves at $v = +0.60c$ relative to S. The Lorentz factor is

<Formula label="Lorentz factor between Jim and Sam" tex="\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - 0.60^2}} = \frac{1}{0.80} = 1.25" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The contracted distance">

The 10 km is a rest length: Jim and Sue are at rest in S′. Sam, who marks the two positions at one moment of his own time, finds the contracted length of the lesson (Lecture 5, slide 13):

<Formula label="Separation in Sam's frame" tex="L = \frac{L'}{\gamma} = \frac{10\ \text{km}}{1.25} = 8.0\ \text{km}" cite="reading4" at="slide 13" />

Sam reports: "Jim and Sue are 8.0 km apart, and both move at $0.60c$ in the $+x$ direction."

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The moving length is shorter than the rest length, as it must be. For a slow neutron gun ($v \to 0$) the factor $\gamma$ goes to 1 and both observers agree on 10 km.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Jim: "The speed of my neutron is $0.60c$." What speed does Sam measure for Jim's neutrons, as a fraction of $c$?

<Hint>

Jim's neutron moves at $u' = +0.60c$ in a frame that itself moves at $v = +0.60c$ relative to Sam. Velocities do not simply add.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-speed" label="$u/c =$" value="0.882" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-speed" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Add two parallel velocities of $0.60c$ with the relativistic rule.

<Step title="Velocity addition">

With $u'_x = 0.60c$ and $v = 0.60c$, the lesson's rule for the component along the motion gives

<Formula label="Speed of Jim's neutron for Sam" tex="u_x = \frac{u'_x + v}{1 + u'_x v/c^2} = \frac{0.60c + 0.60c}{1 + 0.36} = \frac{1.20}{1.36}\,c = \frac{15}{17}\,c = 0.882c" cite="reading4" at="slide 16" />

Sam reports: "Jim's neutrons move at $0.882c$. Sue's neutrons are at rest."

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The same rule for Sue's neutron, $u'_x = -0.60c$, gives $u_x = 0$, as the choice of Sam's frame demands. The result is below $c$, although the Galilean sum would be $1.20c$. The Lorentz factor of Jim's neutron in S is $1/\sqrt{1 - (15/17)^2} = 17/8 = 2.125$, a number used in part (c).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Jim: "Two of our neutrons have collided. Relativistic momentum and kinetic energy are conserved." What does Sam report about this collision (the choice box)? Then find the total kinetic energy of the two neutrons before the collision as Sam measures it, in units of the rest energy $m_0c^2$ of one neutron (the number is our addition).

<Hint>

Is the conservation of momentum and energy a law of physics or a property of one frame? And what does the first postulate say about laws of physics?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the number: in S one neutron is at rest and the other has the speed of part (b). The kinetic energy of a particle is $(\gamma_u - 1)\,m_0c^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-law" choices={["Both are conserved for Sam too, but he finds other values for them than Jim does", "Momentum is conserved for Sam, but kinetic energy is not", "Both are conserved for Sam, with the same values that Jim finds", "Neither is conserved for Sam, because for him the collision is not symmetric"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p1-ke" label="Total kinetic energy for Sam, in units of $m_0c^2$:" value="1.125" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-ke-sam" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Conservation laws hold in every inertial frame. The conserved amounts depend on the frame.

<Step title="The law">

The total momentum 4-vector of the two neutrons transforms from S′ to S with one Lorentz matrix (Lecture 12, slide 6). If its four components are the same before and after the collision in Jim's frame, they are the same before and after in Sam's frame. So Sam also finds momentum and total energy conserved. The neutrons are neutrons before and after, with unchanged rest masses, so the kinetic energy, total energy minus rest energy, is conserved as well. Sam reports: "Two neutrons collided. Momentum and kinetic energy are conserved."

</Step>

<Step title="The values">

For Jim the two neutrons have equal and opposite momenta, so the total momentum is zero, and each has the kinetic energy $(1.25 - 1)\,m_0c^2$: together $0.50\,m_0c^2$. For Sam one neutron is at rest and the other has $\gamma_u = 2.125$:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy and momentum before the collision, for Sam" tex="K = (\gamma_u - 1)\,m_0c^2 = 1.125\,m_0c^2, \qquad p = \gamma_u m_0 u = 2.125 \times \tfrac{15}{17}\,m_0c = 1.875\,m_0c" cite="reading1" at="slide 4 (kinetic energy) and slide 3 (momentum)" />

Sam finds more than twice Jim's kinetic energy, and a total momentum that is not zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

One quantity must agree between the two: the invariant $E^2 - (cp)^2$ of the whole system (slide 6). For Jim, $E = 2 \times 1.25\,m_0c^2 = 2.5\,m_0c^2$ and $p = 0$, so its square root is $2.5\,m_0c^2$. For Sam, $E = (2.125 + 1)\,m_0c^2 = 3.125\,m_0c^2$ and $cp = 1.875\,m_0c^2$, and $\sqrt{3.125^2 - 1.875^2} = 2.5$. They agree.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Jim: "After this collision, one of the neutrons was scattered through an angle of $30^\circ$." (MIT's sheet names only one neutron. The rest follows from part (c): Jim's frame is the zero-momentum frame of the two colliding neutrons and the collision is elastic, so both neutrons leave at $0.60c$, back to back, each turned by $30^\circ$ from its old direction.) Take the motion after the collision in the $xy$ plane.

What does Sam report? Give the angle through which Jim's neutron is turned in S, and the angle between the path of Sue's neutron after the collision and the $x$ axis in S. Both in degrees.

<Hint>

Write the velocity components of each neutron after the collision in Jim's frame, and transform both components to S. The component across the motion changes too.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The lesson's rules are $u_x = (u'_x + v)/(1 + u'_xv/c^2)$ and $u_y = u'_y/[\gamma(1 + u'_xv/c^2)]$. The angle to the $x$ axis follows from $\tan\theta = u_y/u_x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-angle-jim" label="Jim's neutron is turned by:" value="12.1" unit="degrees" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-angle-jim" />

<Answer id="p1-angle-sue" label="Sue's neutron leaves at:" value="71.5" unit="degrees" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-angle-sue" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** An angle is a ratio of velocity components, and the two components transform differently. So angles change between frames.

<Step title="Jim's neutron">

After the collision Jim's neutron has, in S′, the velocity $\vec u' = 0.60c\,(\cos 30^\circ, \sin 30^\circ) = (0.520c,\ 0.300c)$. With $v = 0.60c$ and $1 + u'_xv/c^2 = 1.312$:

<Formula label="Jim's neutron after the collision, in Sam's frame" tex="u_x = \frac{0.520c + 0.60c}{1.312} = 0.854c, \qquad u_y = \frac{0.300c}{1.25 \times 1.312} = 0.183c, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{u_y}{u_x} = 0.214" cite="reading4" at="slide 16" />

so $\theta = 12.1^\circ$. Before the collision this neutron moved along $+x$ in S, so Sam says it was turned by $12.1^\circ$, not by $30^\circ$.

</Step>

<Step title="Sue's neutron">

In S′ Sue's neutron leaves with $\vec u' = 0.60c\,(-\cos 30^\circ, -\sin 30^\circ) = (-0.520c,\ -0.300c)$. Now $1 + u'_xv/c^2 = 0.688$:

<Formula label="Sue's neutron after the collision, in Sam's frame" tex="u_x = \frac{-0.520c + 0.60c}{0.688} = 0.117c, \qquad u_y = \frac{-0.300c}{1.25 \times 0.688} = -0.349c, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{|u_y|}{u_x} = 2.99" cite="reading4" at="slide 16" />

so its path makes $71.5^\circ$ with the $x$ axis, on the other side of the axis from Jim's neutron. In S this neutron was at rest before, so "scattered through an angle" has no meaning for it. Sam reports: "A moving neutron hit one at rest. The moving one was turned by $12.1^\circ$ and the struck one moved off at $71.5^\circ$ on the other side."

</Step>

<Step title="A shorter form">

Because the neutron's speed in S′ equals the frame speed $v$, the two results simplify to

<Formula label="The two angles in Sam's frame" tex="\tan\theta_\text{Jim} = \frac{\sin\theta'}{\gamma\,(1 + \cos\theta')} = \frac{\tan(\theta'/2)}{\gamma}, \qquad \tan\theta_\text{Sue} = \frac{\sin\theta'}{\gamma\,(1 - \cos\theta')} = \frac{1}{\gamma\tan(\theta'/2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

with $\theta' = 30^\circ$: $\tan 15^\circ/1.25 = 0.214$ and $1/(1.25\tan 15^\circ) = 2.99$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Sam's momentum across the motion must stay zero. The speeds in S are $0.873c$ and $0.368c$, with Lorentz factors 2.050 and 1.075, and $2.050 \times 0.183 - 1.075 \times 0.349 = 0$ to the rounding. The two energies add to $(2.050 + 1.075)\,m_0c^2 = 3.125\,m_0c^2$, the total of part (c). In Newton's mechanics two equal masses, one at rest, always fly apart at a right angle. Here the angle between the paths is $12.1^\circ + 71.5^\circ = 83.6^\circ$, less than $90^\circ$: for slow neutrons $\gamma \to 1$ and the two angles would be $15^\circ$ and $75^\circ$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

Jim: "I am firing neutrons at the rate of 10,000 per second." At what rate does Sam say Jim fires, in neutrons per second?

<Hint>

Jim's gun is a clock that ticks 10,000 times per second in its own rest frame. Sam watches a moving clock.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-rate" label="Jim's firing rate for Sam:" value="8000" unit="per second" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-rate" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The firings are ticks of a clock at rest in Jim's frame. Sam sees that clock run slow.

<Step title="Time dilation">

Two firings in a row happen at the same place in S′, $\Delta t' = 10^{-4}$ s apart. In S the time between them is longer (Lecture 5, slide 4):

<Formula label="Firing rate in Sam's frame" tex="\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t' = 1.25\times 10^{-4}\ \text{s}, \qquad \text{rate} = \frac{1}{\Delta t} = \frac{10\,000\ \text{s}^{-1}}{1.25} = 8000\ \text{s}^{-1}" cite="reading4" at="slide 4" />

Sam reports: "Jim fires 8000 neutrons per second."

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Count the neutrons in flight in two ways. In Jim's frame they are $0.60c \times 10^{-4}\ \text{s} = 17.99$ km apart. That row of neutrons moves at $0.60c$, so in its own rest frame the spacing is $1.25 \times 17.99 = 22.49$ km, and in S, where the row moves with $\gamma_u = 2.125$, it is $22.49/2.125 = 10.58$ km. Sam's own bookkeeping gives the same: each neutron ($0.882c$) pulls ahead of the gun ($0.60c$) for $1.25\times 10^{-4}$ s before the next one is fired, which is $(0.882 - 0.60)\,c \times 1.25\times 10^{-4}\ \text{s} = 10.58$ km.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A ton of sunlight" points={3} mit="Problem 2">

The Sun radiates energy at the rate of $4.0\times 10^{26}$ W. Energy has mass, $m = E/c^2$. How many tonnes of sunlight does the Earth intercept each day? (One tonne is 1000 kg.) MIT's sheet gives only the Sun's power. Take Earth's distance from the Sun to be $1.5\times 10^{11}$ m (the value of MIT's Problem Set 1) and Earth's radius $6.4\times 10^{6}$ m (our own value, as in our Problem Set 1).

<Hint>

The sunlight spreads evenly over a sphere whose radius is the Earth–Sun distance. Which area does the Earth cut out of that sphere: its whole surface, or the disc it shows to the Sun?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Fraction caught = area of Earth's disc divided by the area of the sphere. Multiply the Sun's power by that fraction and by the seconds in a day, then divide by $c^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-tonnes" label="Sunlight caught per day:" value="175" unit="tonnes" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-tonnes" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the share of the Sun's power that falls on the Earth, turn a day of it into energy, and divide by $c^2$.

<Step title="Earth's share">

At the distance $d$ the power is spread over a sphere of area $4\pi d^2$. The Earth blocks a disc of area $\pi R_E^2$:

<Formula label="Fraction of sunlight that hits the Earth" tex="f = \frac{\pi R_E^2}{4\pi d^2} = \frac{(6.4\times 10^{6}\ \text{m})^2}{4\,(1.5\times 10^{11}\ \text{m})^2} = 4.55\times 10^{-10}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The Earth receives $4.55\times 10^{-10} \times 4.0\times 10^{26}\ \text{W} = 1.82\times 10^{17}$ W.

</Step>

<Step title="A day of sunlight as mass">

In one day (86,400 s) this is $1.57\times 10^{22}$ J. With the mass-energy equivalence of the lesson (Lecture 10, slide 16):

<Formula label="Mass of a day of sunlight" tex="m = \frac{E}{c^2} = \frac{1.57\times 10^{22}\ \text{J}}{(2.998\times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s})^2} = 1.75\times 10^{5}\ \text{kg} = 175\ \text{tonnes}" cite="reading2" at="slide 16" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The Sun as a whole loses $L/c^2 = 4.45\times 10^{9}$ kg each second, the figure of the lesson's worked example, which is $3.85\times 10^{14}$ kg per day. Earth's share $4.55\times 10^{-10}$ of that is again $1.75\times 10^{5}$ kg. The title is modest: it is 175 tonnes a day, about 2 kg every second.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="An enormous source of energy" points={3} mit="Problem 3">

Quasars are thought to be the nuclei of active galaxies in an early stage of their formation. A typical quasar radiates energy at the rate of $10^{41}$ W. At what rate does the quasar lose mass to supply this energy? Give the answer in solar masses per year, with one solar mass equal to $2\times 10^{30}$ kg and one year equal to $3.156\times 10^{7}$ s.

<Hint>

A power is an energy per second. Dividing it by $c^2$ gives a mass per second.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-rate" label="Mass loss:" value="17.6" unit="solar masses per year" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-rate" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The radiated energy carries away the mass $E/c^2$.

<Step title="Mass per second">

<Formula label="Mass loss of the quasar per second" tex="\frac{dm}{dt} = \frac{P}{c^2} = \frac{10^{41}\ \text{W}}{(2.998\times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s})^2} = 1.11\times 10^{24}\ \text{kg/s}" cite="reading2" at="slide 16" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solar masses per year">

In one year, $3.156\times 10^{7}$ s, the quasar loses $1.11\times 10^{24} \times 3.156\times 10^{7} = 3.51\times 10^{31}$ kg. In solar masses:

<Formula label="Mass loss of the quasar per year" tex="\frac{3.51\times 10^{31}\ \text{kg}}{2\times 10^{30}\ \text{kg}} = 17.6" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The quasar turns 17.6 solar masses into radiation every year.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The quasar's power is $10^{41}/(4\times 10^{26}) = 2.5\times 10^{14}$ times the Sun's. The Sun loses $4.45\times 10^{9}$ kg/s (Problem 2), and $2.5\times 10^{14}$ times that is $1.11\times 10^{24}$ kg/s again. Every second the quasar radiates away about a fifth of the mass of the Earth ($6\times 10^{24}$ kg).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="How acceleration transforms" points={9} mit="Problem 4">

A particle has the velocity $\vec u'$ and the acceleration $\vec a' = d\vec u'/dt'$ in a frame S′ that moves with speed $v$ along the $x$ axis of the frame S. Start from the definition of acceleration, $\vec a = d\vec u/dt$, and derive the $x$ and $y$ components of the acceleration in S:

<Formula label="Transformation of acceleration" tex="a_x = \frac{a_x'}{\gamma^3\left(1 + vu_x'/c^2\right)^3}, \qquad a_y = \frac{a_y'}{\gamma^2\left(1 + vu_x'/c^2\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, eqs. (4.1) and (4.2), p. 1" />

The second formula holds only if $u_y' = 0$ or $a_x' = 0$. In general the transformation of $a_y$ is messier. (The lesson quotes this pair from slide 14 of Lecture 12, which leaves the derivation to a problem set.)

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Derive the formula for $a_x$. On the way you need the rate $dt/dt'$ at which the time of S passes per unit time of S′ along the particle's path. Type it in the box, in terms of $v$, $u'_x$ (typed `ux`) and $c$.

<Hint>

$a_x = du_x/dt$, and $u_x$ is known as a function of $u'_x$. Use the chain rule: $du_x/dt = (du_x/dt')\,/\,(dt/dt')$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The inverse Lorentz transformation gives $dt = \gamma\,(dt' + v\,dx'/c^2)$. Along the particle's path $dx' = u'_x\,dt'$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-dt" label="$dt/dt' =$" expr="(1 + v*ux/c^2)/sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)"
  vars={{ v: [0.1, 0.7], ux: [-0.7, 0.7], c: [1, 1.5] }}
  names={{ ux: "u'_x" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(1 - v*ux/c^2)/sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)", hint: "The sign is wrong. Going from S′ back to S uses the inverse transformation, t = γ(t′ + v x′/c²), with a plus sign." },
    { expr: "1/sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)", hint: "That is time dilation for a clock at rest in S′. This particle moves in S′, so dx′ is not zero along its path." },
    { expr: "(1 + v*ux/c^2)*sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)", hint: "The factor γ multiplies: t = γ(t′ + v x′/c²). You divided by it." },
    { expr: "1 + v*ux/c^2", hint: "The factor γ of the Lorentz transformation is missing." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Differentiate the velocity addition formula with respect to $t'$, then convert $dt'$ to $dt$.

<Step title="Time along the path">

The inverse Lorentz transformation for two nearby events on the particle's path is $dx = \gamma(dx' + v\,dt')$, $dy = dy'$, $dt = \gamma(dt' + v\,dx'/c^2)$ (Lecture 5, slide 15). With $dx' = u'_x\,dt'$:

<Formula label="Rate of S time per S′ time along the path" tex="\frac{dt}{dt'} = \gamma\left(1 + \frac{vu'_x}{c^2}\right)" cite="reading4" at="slide 15" />

</Step>

<Step title="Differentiate the velocity">

Write $D = 1 + vu'_x/c^2$, so that $dD/dt' = va'_x/c^2$. The velocity addition formula is $u_x = (u'_x + v)/D$ (slide 16). Its derivative with respect to $t'$ is

<Formula label="Derivative of the x velocity" tex="\frac{du_x}{dt'} = \frac{a'_x}{D} - \frac{(u'_x + v)}{D^2}\,\frac{va'_x}{c^2} = \frac{a'_x}{D^2}\left(1 + \frac{vu'_x}{c^2} - \frac{vu'_x}{c^2} - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right) = \frac{a'_x}{\gamma^2D^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

because $1 - v^2/c^2 = 1/\gamma^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Divide by the rate of time">

<Formula label="The x component of the acceleration" tex="a_x = \frac{du_x}{dt} = \frac{du_x/dt'}{dt/dt'} = \frac{a'_x}{\gamma^2D^2}\cdot\frac{1}{\gamma D} = \frac{a'_x}{\gamma^3\left(1 + vu'_x/c^2\right)^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, eq. (4.1), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $v \to 0$, $\gamma \to 1$ and $a_x = a'_x$: Galileo's invariant acceleration. For a particle at rest in S′ ($u'_x = 0$) the formula gives $a_x = a'_x/\gamma^3$. That fits the lesson's force law: along the motion $F = \gamma^3m_0a$ in S (slide 10), in the rest frame $F' = m_0a'$, and a force along the motion is the same in both frames (slide 13). So $\gamma^3m_0a_x = m_0a'_x$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Derive the formula for $a_y$, and show where the condition "$u_y' = 0$ or $a_x' = 0$" comes from.

<Hint>

The same two steps, starting from $u_y = u'_y/(\gamma D)$ with $D = 1 + vu'_x/c^2$. Both $u'_y$ and $D$ change with time.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same chain rule. The derivative of the denominator brings in a term with $u'_y\,a'_x$, and the simple formula is what remains when that term vanishes.

<Step title="Differentiate the velocity">

The lesson's rule for the component across the motion is $u_y = u'_y/(\gamma D)$ (Lecture 5, slide 16). Then

<Formula label="Derivative of the y velocity" tex="\frac{du_y}{dt'} = \frac{a'_y}{\gamma D} - \frac{u'_y}{\gamma D^2}\,\frac{va'_x}{c^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Divide by the rate of time">

Dividing by $dt/dt' = \gamma D$ gives the general result:

<Formula label="The y component of the acceleration, general case" tex="a_y = \frac{a'_y}{\gamma^2\left(1 + vu'_x/c^2\right)^2} - \frac{vu'_y\,a'_x/c^2}{\gamma^2\left(1 + vu'_x/c^2\right)^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, eq. (4.2), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The second term is the "messy" part. It is zero when $u'_y = 0$ or when $a'_x = 0$, and then MIT's formula (4.2) is left.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $v \to 0$ the result is $a_y = a'_y$. For a particle at rest in S′, $a_y = a'_y/\gamma^2$. The lesson's force law agrees: across the motion $F_y = \gamma m_0a_y$ in S (slide 10), in the rest frame $F'_y = m_0a'_y$, and a force across the motion is smaller by $\gamma$ in the frame where the particle moves, $F_y = F'_y/\gamma$ (slide 13). So $\gamma m_0a_y = m_0a'_y/\gamma$. The second term has a clear meaning as well: an acceleration along $x'$ changes the rate $dt/dt'$, and with it the sideways velocity $u_y$, even if $a'_y = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Constant acceleration" points={6} mit="Problem 5">

MIT's sheet sets a problem from French's textbook here and adds a hint: you should end up solving $d(\gamma u)/dt = g$ for $u(t)$, and then integrate $u\,dt = dx$ for $x(t)$. The textbook is not used on this site, so this is our own problem built on that hint.

A particle of rest mass $m_0$ is at rest at $x = 0$ at the time $t = 0$ in the frame S. **The idea.** A constant force makes the momentum, not the velocity, grow in proportion to time.

<Step title="Integrate once">

Integrating from $t = 0$, where $u = 0$:

<Formula label="Momentum per unit mass grows linearly" tex="\gamma_u u = \frac{u}{\sqrt{1 - u^2/c^2}} = gt" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Squaring, $u^2 = (gt)^2\,(1 - u^2/c^2)$, so $u^2\,[1 + (gt/c)^2] = (gt)^2$ and

For $gt \ll c$ the root is 1 and $u = gt$, Newton's result. For $gt \gg c$ the root is $gt/c$ and $u \to c$ from below: the particle never reaches the speed of light. The Lorentz factor is $\gamma_u = \sqrt{1 + (gt/c)^2}$, and differentiating gives $du/dt = g/\gamma_u^3$, the lesson's $F = \gamma_u^3m_0a$ for a force along the motion (Lecture 12, slide 10).

</Step>

</Solution>

Find the position $x(t)$ of the particle in S.

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="The relativistic rocket" points={21} mit="Problem 6">

A rocket of rest mass $M_0$ pushes itself forward by throwing exhaust out of its back. The exhaust leaves at the speed $V_\text{ex}$ relative to the rocket. The rest mass $M_0$ falls as the fuel is used, so its change $\Delta M_0$ in a short time is negative. The frame S′ is the frame in which the rocket is at rest at the moment considered, and S is a fixed frame in which the rocket has the speed $u$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Show that in the rest frame of the rocket, with non-relativistic dynamics,

<Formula label="Rocket equation in the rocket's rest frame" tex="\Delta M_0\,V_\text{ex} = -M_0\,\Delta u' \qquad\text{or}\qquad \frac{dM_0}{dt'}\,V_\text{ex} = -M_0\,\frac{du'}{dt'}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), eqs. (6.1) and (6.2), pp. 1–2" />

where $\Delta u'$ is the change of the rocket's speed after it has thrown out the mass $|\Delta M_0|$ of exhaust. This is correct unless $V_\text{ex}$ is comparable with the speed of light. (On MIT's sheet the right-hand side of (6.2) is printed with "du0", a misprint for $du'$.)

<Hint>

In the rocket's rest frame the total momentum is zero before the bit of exhaust leaves. Write the momentum of the exhaust and of the rocket just after.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Momentum conservation in the frame where the rocket is at rest for a moment.

<Step title="Before and after">

Before, the rocket (mass $M_0$) is at rest in S′: the momentum is zero. In a short time it throws out exhaust of mass $-\Delta M_0$ (a positive number) backwards at the speed $V_\text{ex}$, and the rest, of mass $M_0 + \Delta M_0$, moves forward at $\Delta u'$. Both speeds are small against $c$, so Newton's momentum will do:

<Formula label="Momentum balance in the rocket's rest frame" tex="0 = \left(M_0 + \Delta M_0\right)\Delta u' - \left(-\Delta M_0\right)V_\text{ex}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Small steps">

The product $\Delta M_0\,\Delta u'$ of two small quantities can be dropped against the others. What is left is $M_0\,\Delta u' = -\Delta M_0\,V_\text{ex}$, which is (6.1). Dividing by the short time $\Delta t'$ in which it happens and letting it go to zero gives (6.2).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The signs fit: $\Delta M_0$ is negative, so $\Delta u'$ is positive, and the rocket speeds up. Both sides are a mass times a velocity. Why the exhaust must be slow: the rocket's loss of rest mass and the rest mass of the exhaust are equal only if the kinetic energy of the exhaust, seen from the rocket, is negligible next to its rest energy, and Newton's momentum was used for it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Now let the rocket's motion in the fixed frame S be non-relativistic too. Then $a = a'$, and the rocket equation in S is

<Formula label="Rocket equation in the fixed frame" tex="\frac{dM_0}{dt}\,V_\text{ex} = -M_0\,\frac{du}{dt}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), eq. (6.3), p. 2" />

Cancel the $dt$ on both sides and integrate to show that a rocket starting from rest reaches

<Formula label="The classical rocket equation" tex="u = V_\text{ex}\,\ln\frac{M_\text{initial}}{M_\text{final}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), eq. (6.4), p. 2" />

Then a number (our own): a rocket of 10,000 kg burns fuel until 2000 kg are left, with $V_\text{ex} = 3.0$ km/s. What speed does it reach, in km/s?

<Hint>

After cancelling $dt$: $du = -V_\text{ex}\,dM_0/M_0$. Integrate $u$ from 0 and $M_0$ from the initial to the final mass.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-u" label="$u =$" value="4.83" unit="km/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-classical" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each equal fraction of mass thrown out adds the same speed, which makes a logarithm.

<Step title="Integrate">

<Formula label="Integrating the rocket equation" tex="\int_0^u du_1 = -V_\text{ex}\int_{M_\text{initial}}^{M_\text{final}}\frac{dM_0}{M_0} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u = -V_\text{ex}\,\ln\frac{M_\text{final}}{M_\text{initial}} = V_\text{ex}\,\ln\frac{M_\text{initial}}{M_\text{final}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The time does not appear: the final speed depends only on the two masses, however fast or slowly the fuel is burnt.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

<Formula label="Speed of the example rocket" tex="u = 3.0\ \text{km/s} \times \ln\frac{10\,000}{2000} = 3.0\ \text{km/s} \times \ln 5 = 3.0 \times 1.609\ \text{km/s} = 4.83\ \text{km/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 2 (our own numbers)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

If no fuel is burnt the masses are equal, $\ln 1 = 0$ and $u = 0$. The rocket ends up faster than its own exhaust when the mass ratio is above $e = 2.72$, as here. The lesson's worked example uses the same formula for a probe to Alpha Centauri.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Let the rocket of part (b) fire its engine straight up in a gravitational field. Neglect the drag of an atmosphere (wrong on Earth, fine on the Moon), and let the engine burn only over a height that is small against the radius of the body, so that the acceleration of gravity $g$ is constant. The rocket starts from rest and burns its fuel at a steady rate: $dM_0/dt = \dot M$ is a (negative) constant and $M_0 = M_\text{initial} + \dot M t$. Show that the rocket equation becomes

<Formula label="Rocket equation with gravity" tex="\frac{du}{dt} = -\frac{V_\text{ex}\,\dot M}{M_\text{initial} + \dot M t} - g" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), eq. (6.5), p. 2" />

and that after a burn of total time $T$

<Formula label="Speed after a burn against gravity" tex="u(T) = V_\text{ex}\,\ln\left[\frac{M_\text{initial}}{M_\text{initial} + \dot M T}\right] - gT = V_\text{ex}\,\ln\left[\frac{M_\text{initial}}{M_\text{final}}\right] - gT" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), eq. (6.6), p. 2" />

Then a number (our own): the rocket of part (b) takes off from the Moon, where $g = 1.62\ \text{m/s}^2$, and burns its 8000 kg of fuel in 120 s. What is its speed at the end of the burn, in km/s?

<Hint>

Equation (6.3) says that the thrust on the rocket is $-V_\text{ex}\,dM_0/dt$. Add the weight $-M_0g$ to the forces and divide by $M_0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The first term has the form $-V_\text{ex}\,\dot M/(\text{something linear in } t)$, and $\dot M$ is the derivative of that something. Its integral is a logarithm.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-moon" label="$u(T) =$" value="4.63" unit="km/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-moon" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Gravity adds a second force. Because it gives every mass the same acceleration, it simply subtracts $gT$ from the speed.

<Step title="The forces">

Read (6.3) as Newton's law for the rocket, $M_0\,du/dt = \text{thrust}$, with the thrust $-V_\text{ex}\,dM_0/dt$ (positive, because the mass falls). With the weight $M_0g$ pulling down:

<Formula label="Newton's law for the rocket with gravity" tex="M_0\,\frac{du}{dt} = -V_\text{ex}\,\frac{dM_0}{dt} - M_0g" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Dividing by $M_0 = M_\text{initial} + \dot Mt$ and writing $\dot M$ for $dM_0/dt$ gives (6.5).

</Step>

<Step title="Integrate over the burn">

The derivative of $\ln(M_\text{initial} + \dot Mt)$ is $\dot M/(M_\text{initial} + \dot Mt)$, so

<Formula label="Integrating over the burn" tex="u(T) = \int_0^T\left[-\frac{V_\text{ex}\,\dot M}{M_\text{initial} + \dot Mt} - g\right]dt = -V_\text{ex}\,\ln\frac{M_\text{initial} + \dot MT}{M_\text{initial}} - gT" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Turning the fraction over changes the sign of the logarithm, and $M_\text{initial} + \dot MT$ is the mass at the end of the burn, $M_\text{final}$. This is (6.6).

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

For the Moon rocket, $gT = 1.62 \times 120 = 194$ m/s:

<Formula label="Speed of the Moon rocket" tex="u(T) = 4828\ \text{m/s} - 194\ \text{m/s} = 4634\ \text{m/s} = 4.63\ \text{km/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 2 (our own numbers)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $g = 0$ the result is that of part (b). Unlike there, the time now matters: a slower burn loses more speed to gravity, so a rocket should burn its fuel fast. The rocket leaves the ground only if the thrust beats the weight at the start. Here $\dot M = -8000/120 = -66.7$ kg/s, the thrust is $3000 \times 66.7 = 2.0\times 10^{5}$ N, and the weight on the Moon is $10\,000 \times 1.62 = 1.62\times 10^{4}$ N, twelve times less.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={6}>

Next let the rocket's motion be at least somewhat relativistic, while the exhaust speed is not. Part (a) then still holds in the rocket's rest frame S′, where the acceleration is

<Formula label="Acceleration in the rocket's rest frame" tex="a' = \frac{du'}{dt'} = -\frac{V_\text{ex}}{M_0}\,\frac{dM_0}{dt'}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(d), eq. (6.7), p. 2" />

<Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** In its own rest frame the rocket obeys part (a). The transformation of acceleration and time dilation carry that to S, where the speed gained per unit of fuel shrinks by $\gamma^2$.

<Step title="Transform the acceleration">

At any moment take S′ to be the frame that moves with the rocket: $v = u$ and $u'_x = 0$. Problem 4 then gives $a = a'/\gamma^3$. With (6.7) this is (6.8).

</Step>

<Step title="Time dilation">

The rocket's own clock is at rest in S′, so $dt = \gamma\,dt'$ (Lecture 5, slide 4). The rest mass of the rocket at a given moment of its flight is an invariant number, so $dM_0/dt' = \gamma\,dM_0/dt$. Putting this into (6.8) removes one factor of $\gamma$:

<Formula label="From rocket time to the time of the fixed frame" tex="\frac{du}{dt} = -\frac{1}{\gamma^3}\,\frac{V_\text{ex}}{M_0}\,\gamma\,\frac{dM_0}{dt} = -\frac{1}{\gamma^2}\,\frac{V_\text{ex}}{M_0}\,\frac{dM_0}{dt}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(d), eq. (6.9), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Integrate">

Cancel $dt$ and put everything with $u$ on the left: $\gamma^2\,du = du/(1 - u^2/c^2) = -V\,dM_0/M_0$. With partial fractions,

<Formula label="Integrating the relativistic rocket equation" tex="\int_0^u\frac{du_1}{1 - u_1^2/c^2} = \frac{c}{2}\,\ln\frac{1 + u/c}{1 - u/c} = -V\int_{M_i}^{M_f}\frac{dM_0}{M_0} = V\,\ln\frac{M_i}{M_f}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for the speed">

So $(1 + u/c)/(1 - u/c) = (M_i/M_f)^{2V/c}$. Calling the right-hand side $R$, $1 + u/c = R\,(1 - u/c)$ gives $u/c = (R - 1)/(R + 1)$, which is (6.10).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For any mass ratio $R$ is finite, so $u/c = (R - 1)/(R + 1)$ stays below 1: the rocket never reaches the speed of light. With no fuel burnt, $M_i = M_f$, $R = 1$ and $u = 0$. The result can also be written $u/c = \tanh[(V/c)\ln(M_i/M_f)]$. The lesson's photon rocket is the case $V = c$: then $(1 + u/c)/(1 - u/c) = (M_i/M_f)^2$, the lesson's $M_i/M_f = \sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}$. (Our derivation assumed a slow exhaust, so it does not prove that case. The lesson gets it from energy and momentum conservation.)

</Step>

<Answer id="p6-ratio" label="$M_i/M_f =$" value="7.18e23" tolerance="2%" calc="p6-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The speed is set by the product $(V/c)\ln(M_i/M_f)$. A logarithm grows so slowly that only a large $V/c$ can make the product large.

<Step title="The classical limit">

Write $\epsilon = (2V/c)\ln(M_i/M_f)$. If $V \ll c$ and the mass ratio is not astronomically large, $\epsilon \ll 1$ and $(M_i/M_f)^{2V/c} = e^\epsilon \approx 1 + \epsilon$. Then

<Formula label="Low exhaust speed" tex="\frac{u}{c} = \frac{e^\epsilon - 1}{e^\epsilon + 1} \approx \frac{\epsilon}{2 + \epsilon} \approx \frac{\epsilon}{2} = \frac{V}{c}\,\ln\frac{M_i}{M_f} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u \approx V\,\ln\frac{M_i}{M_f}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(e), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

which is the result (6.4) of part (b).

</Step>

<Step title="The mass ratio for a given speed">

Solving $(M_i/M_f)^{2V/c} = (1 + u/c)/(1 - u/c)$ for the mass ratio:

<Formula label="Mass ratio needed for a given speed" tex="\frac{M_i}{M_f} = \left(\frac{1 + u/c}{1 - u/c}\right)^{c/(2V)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(e), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

For a relativistic speed the bracket is a number well above 1 (it is 3 for $u = 0.50c$). The exponent $c/(2V)$ is huge unless $V$ is a fair fraction of $c$, and then the mass ratio is beyond any rocket. For $V = 0.010c$ and $u = 0.50c$:

<Formula label="Mass ratio for half the speed of light" tex="\frac{M_i}{M_f} = 3^{50} = 7.18\times 10^{23}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(e), p. 2 (our own numbers)" />

Each kilogram that arrives needs $7\times 10^{23}$ kg at the start, about a tenth of the mass of the Earth. And an exhaust at $0.010c$, 3000 km/s, is already far beyond any chemical engine. With $V = 0.50c$ the same speed needs a mass ratio of only $3^1 = 3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

How good is the classical formula? For a mass ratio of 5 and $V = 0.010c$, (6.10) gives $u/c = 0.016093$ and the classical formula $0.016094$: the same to four figures. For $V = 0.30c$ they give $0.449$ and $0.483$, and for $V = c$ they give $0.923$ and an impossible $1.61$. A chemical exhaust of 4.5 km/s is $1.5\times 10^{-5}c$. To reach even $0.10c$ with it, the mass ratio would have to be about $10^{2900}$, in the same hopeless class as the numbers of the lesson's slides on interstellar travel.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Exercise id="p7" title="The power per kilogram of a person and of the Sun" points={3} mit="Optional Problem 7" optional>

A typical person has a mass of about 60 kg and gives off about 60 W of power while at rest. The Sun has a luminosity (power output) of $4\times 10^{26}$ W and a mass of $2\times 10^{30}$ kg. Compare the power produced per unit of mass by the two: how many times more power per kilogram does the person produce (the box)? How do you make sense of the result?

<Hint>

Divide each power by its mass. Then ask which of the two could keep its output up for longer without new fuel.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-ratio" label="Person's W/kg divided by the Sun's:" value="5000" tolerance="2%" calc="p7-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Per kilogram the Sun is a very weak source. Its strength lies in its size and in how long its fuel lasts.

<Step title="The two numbers">

<Formula label="Power per unit of mass" tex="\text{person: } \frac{60\ \text{W}}{60\ \text{kg}} = 1\ \text{W/kg}, \qquad \text{Sun: } \frac{4\times 10^{26}\ \text{W}}{2\times 10^{30}\ \text{kg}} = 2\times 10^{-4}\ \text{W/kg}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 7, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The person produces 5000 times more power per kilogram than the Sun.

</Step>

<Step title="Making sense of it">

The Sun is bright because it is enormous, not because its matter works hard. Fusion in the Sun is extremely slow. It happens only in the hot core, and even there a given hydrogen nucleus waits billions of years before it fuses (the lesson explains why fusion needs the hot core: the nuclei repel each other, and only the fastest get close enough; the long wait comes mostly from the first step, which turns a proton into a neutron through the weak interaction and so is very rare). A slow burn is what lets the Sun shine for so long.

A person runs on chemical energy and has to keep eating. Over a life of 80 years ($2.5\times 10^{9}$ s) a person gives off about $2.5\times 10^{9}$ J for each kilogram of body, all of it from food taken in along the way. The Sun, using only what it was born with, gives off $2\times 10^{-4}\ \text{W/kg}$ for the $2.2\times 10^{17}$ s of the lesson's lifetime estimate: $4.4\times 10^{13}$ J for each of its kilograms, about 18,000 times more.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson's worked example found the same energy the other way round: 10% of the hydrogen, which is 70% of the mass, turns 0.7% of its rest mass into energy, and $0.1 \times 0.7 \times 0.007 \times c^2 = 4.4\times 10^{13}$ J/kg.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 9 (optional)

<Exercise id="p9" title="Snell's law from Fermat's principle" points={3} mit="Optional Problem 9" optional>

Fermat's principle says that light travels from one point to another along the path that takes the least time. A source in air at $(x, y) = (0, y_0)$ sends light towards a thick slab of glass that fills the half-space below $y = 0$. The light enters the glass and reaches the point $(x_0, -y_0)$. The speed of light is $c$ in air and $c/n$ in glass, where $n$ is the index of refraction, typically between 1.4 and 1.6. (MIT's sheet mentions a diagram that its PDF does not contain. Sketch the situation yourself: a ray going down to the surface at the angle $\theta_i$ from the vertical, and on from there at the smaller angle $\theta_t$ from the vertical.)

Take it as known that within one medium light travels in a straight line. Use Fermat's principle to prove Snell's law:

<Formula label="Snell's law" tex="\sin\theta_i = n\,\sin\theta_t" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 9, p. 3" />

The calculus of variations is not needed. Then a number (our own): for $n = 1.5$ and $\theta_i = 45^\circ$, what is $\theta_t$ in degrees?

<Hint>

MIT's hint: the distances travelled are $y_0/\cos\theta_i$ in air and $y_0/\cos\theta_t$ in glass, and $x_0 = y_0\,(\tan\theta_i + \tan\theta_t)$ ties the two angles together.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the travel time as a function of the two angles. A small change $d\theta_i$ forces a change $d\theta_t$ through the constraint. At the minimum the time does not change to first order.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-angle" label="$\theta_t =$" value="28.1" unit="degrees" tolerance="1%" calc="p9-angle" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The only freedom is the point where the ray crosses the surface. The time is least where a small shift of that point changes nothing to first order.

<Step title="The travel time and the constraint">

<Formula label="Travel time and constraint" tex="T = \frac{y_0}{c\,\cos\theta_i} + \frac{n\,y_0}{c\,\cos\theta_t}, \qquad \tan\theta_i + \tan\theta_t = \frac{x_0}{y_0}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 9, hint, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Vary the angles">

The derivative of $1/\cos\theta$ is $\sin\theta/\cos^2\theta$, and that of $\tan\theta$ is $1/\cos^2\theta$. For a small change of the two angles:

<Formula label="First-order changes" tex="dT = \frac{y_0}{c}\left[\frac{\sin\theta_i}{\cos^2\theta_i}\,d\theta_i + \frac{n\,\sin\theta_t}{\cos^2\theta_t}\,d\theta_t\right] = 0, \qquad \frac{d\theta_i}{\cos^2\theta_i} + \frac{d\theta_t}{\cos^2\theta_t} = 0" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 9, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The second equation holds because the end points are fixed. It gives $d\theta_t/\cos^2\theta_t = -d\theta_i/\cos^2\theta_i$. Put into the first:

<Formula label="Snell's law from the least time" tex="\frac{y_0}{c}\,\frac{d\theta_i}{\cos^2\theta_i}\left[\sin\theta_i - n\,\sin\theta_t\right] = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sin\theta_i = n\,\sin\theta_t" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 9, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="The number">

<Formula label="Refraction angle of the example" tex="\sin\theta_t = \frac{\sin 45^\circ}{1.5} = 0.4714, \qquad \theta_t = 28.1^\circ" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 9, p. 3 (our own numbers)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second route uses the crossing point $x$ itself as the variable: $T(x) = \sqrt{x^2 + y_0^2}/c + n\sqrt{(x_0 - x)^2 + y_0^2}/c$. Its derivative is $[\sin\theta_i - n\sin\theta_t]/c$, zero under the same condition. The second derivative is positive, so it is a minimum. For $n = 1$ the law gives $\theta_t = \theta_i$: no surface, a straight line. For the example with $y_0 = 1$ the ray crosses the surface at $x = 1$ and ends at $x_0 = 1.535$. Its travel time is $3.115\,y_0/c$, against $3.151\,y_0/c$ for the straight line between the two end points: the bent path is quicker because it shortens the slow stretch in the glass.

## Problem 10 (optional)

<Exercise id="p10" title="Lagrange's equations of motion" points={6} mit="Optional Problem 10" optional>

Hamilton's principle (1834 to 1835) says this: fix the configuration of a system at a start time and at an end time; then the motion that really happens in between makes the time integral of kinetic minus potential energy stationary, so that small changes of the path leave it unchanged to first order, as in the lesson. (MIT's sheet, quoting a textbook, says "minimizes"; stationary is the exact condition.) That difference is called the Lagrangian:

<Formula label="The Lagrangian" tex="L \equiv K - V" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 10, eq. (10.1), p. 3" />

where $K$ is the kinetic and $V$ the potential energy. (MIT's sheet writes a script L. This page writes $L$, as the lesson does.)

<Part label="a" points={2}>

(On MIT's sheet this part comes before its parts (a) and (b), without a letter.) Let $K$ and $V$ depend on coordinates $q_k$ and their time derivatives $\dot q_k$. Show, with the Euler–Lagrange equation of the lesson, that the equations that satisfy Hamilton's principle are Lagrange's equations of motion, one for every coordinate:

<Formula label="Lagrange's equations of motion" tex="\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k} - \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_k} = 0" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 10, eq. (10.2), p. 3" />

<Hint>

The lesson made $\int L(x, y, y')\,dx$ stationary and found $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial y}$. Which symbols of Hamilton's principle play the parts of $x$, $y$ and $y'$?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Hamilton's principle is a problem of the calculus of variations with the time as the variable of integration.

<Step title="One coordinate">

The quantity to make stationary is $I = \int L(q, \dot q, t)\,dt$ between two fixed times, with $q$ fixed at both. This is the lesson's functional $I[y] = \int L(x, y, y')\,dx$ with $t$ in the place of $x$, $q(t)$ in the place of $y(x)$ and $\dot q$ in the place of $y'$. The lesson's Euler–Lagrange equation (Lecture 8, slide 3, written out in the lesson),

<Formula label="Euler–Lagrange equation of the lesson" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial L}{\partial y'} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial y}" cite="reading3" at="slide 3 (listed there; written out in the lesson)" />

then reads $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}$, which is (10.2) for one coordinate.

</Step>

<Step title="Several coordinates">

With several coordinates, change one of them, $q_k \to q_k + \epsilon h(t)$ with $h = 0$ at both ends, and leave the others alone. The first-order change of $I$ is, after the lesson's integration by parts,

<Formula label="First-order change for one coordinate" tex="\delta I = \epsilon\int\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k}\,h + \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_k}\,\dot h\right)dt = \epsilon\int\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_k} - \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_k}\right)h\,dt" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 10, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

It must vanish for every choice of $h$, so the bracket is zero. The same holds for each coordinate in turn, which gives one equation (10.2) per coordinate.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For one particle on a line with $L = \tfrac12m\dot x^2 - V(x)$: $\partial L/\partial x = -dV/dx$ and $\partial L/\partial\dot x = m\dot x$, so (10.2) is $-dV/dx - m\ddot x = 0$, Newton's second law with the force $-dV/dx$. The lesson on the brachistochrone makes the same point.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
