For numbers, take $c = 2.998\times 10^8$ m/s. As in the lessons, $\beta = v/c$ and $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-\beta^2}$, and the frame $S'$ moves at speed $v$ in the $+x$ direction of the frame $S$ unless a problem says otherwise, with the two origins together at $t = t' = 0$.

Parts that ask you to show, explain or sketch have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Four quick questions" points={4} mit="Problem 1">

Four multiple-choice questions from an MIT exam of 2004. Choose one answer for each.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

A sphere moves at 90% of the speed of light, at right angles to your line of sight. What shape does it have in your rest frame, that is, when you measure where all its points are at one instant of your time?

<Hint>

Which lengths of a moving body does the Lorentz transformation change: those along the motion, those across it, or both?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="In your frame it is:"
  choices={[
    "A sphere",
    "A prolate ellipsoid: one long axis and two equal shorter ones, like a rugby ball",
    "An oblate ellipsoid: one short axis and two equal longer ones, like a lentil"
  ]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Length contraction acts along the direction of motion only.

<Step title="Contract one axis">

The diameter along the motion is shorter by the factor $1/\gamma$, and the two diameters across the motion keep their length (the Lorentz transformation leaves $y$ and $z$ alone). One axis shorter than the other two: an oblate ellipsoid.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $\beta = 0.9$ the factor is $\sqrt{1 - 0.81} = 0.436$, so the sphere is squeezed to less than half its diameter along its path. That is far from a sphere, which makes part (b) the more surprising.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

What does the same sphere look like to you, that is, what outline would a photograph taken from your position show?

<Hint>

Seeing is not the same as measuring. A photograph collects the light that arrives at the camera at one instant. That light left different parts of the body at different times.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the sphere's own frame, the rays that reach your eye from its rim form a circular cone. Going to your frame changes the direction of every ray (aberration). Ask what aberration does to a circular cone of rays.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="To you it looks like:"
  choices={[
    "A sphere",
    "A prolate ellipsoid",
    "An oblate ellipsoid"
  ]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** What you see is decided by the directions of the light rays that enter your eye together. Aberration turns a circular cone of rays into another circular cone, so the outline of a sphere is always a circle.

<Step title="The rim in the sphere's frame">

In the sphere's rest frame the sphere really is round. The rays that graze it and meet at one point form a circular cone: every one of them makes the same angle $\alpha$ with the line to the centre. With the wave 4-vector of the lesson, $\vec k = k(\hat n, 1)$ for a ray in the direction of the unit vector $\hat n$, the cone is one linear condition on $\vec k$:

<Formula label="A circular cone of rays" tex="\hat n\cdot\hat a = \cos\alpha \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \vec k^t\eta\,\vec a = 0, \qquad \vec a = \begin{pmatrix} \hat a \\ \cos\alpha \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

Here $\hat a$ is the axis of the cone and $\eta$ is the Minkowski metric of the lessons.

</Step>

<Step title="Go to your frame">

Your eye receives the same rays, at the same event. A product $\vec k^t\eta\,\vec a$ of two 4-vectors is the same in every frame (Lecture 5, slide 20), so in your frame the rays obey $\vec k'^t\eta\,\vec a' = 0$ with $\vec a' = \Lambda\vec a$. That is again a statement of the form "the direction makes a fixed angle with a fixed axis": a circular cone, with a new axis and a new opening angle. A circular cone of rays is what a sphere sends to the eye, so the fast sphere looks like a sphere. Its surface looks turned, and it is seen in a direction shifted by aberration.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The flattening of part (a) has not gone away: it is what a measurement at one instant of your time gives. The light from the far side of the sphere set out earlier than the light from the near side, when the sphere was further back, and this delay makes up for the contraction in the picture. (This result is known as the Penrose–Terrell effect. Lecture 6 raises the question on slide 23: this is how it is, but how does it look?)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

A lamp moves around you in a circle at 50% of the speed of light. You stand at the centre. How do you observe its light?

<Hint>

The distance between you and the lamp never changes, so nothing stretches or squeezes the waves on the way. What is left is the rate of the lamp's own clock.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="The light is:"
  choices={[
    "Redshifted",
    "Blueshifted",
    "Neither"
  ]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The lamp is a moving clock. Its distance from you is constant, so the only effect is time dilation: the transverse Doppler effect.

<Step title="Time dilation">

In your frame the lamp's clock runs slow by the factor $\gamma$ (Lecture 5, slide 4). Each wave crest travels the same distance to you, so the crests arrive at the slowed rate: you receive the frequency $\omega_0/\gamma$, lower than the frequency $\omega_0$ the lamp sends in its own frame. The light is redshifted.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The Doppler formula of the lesson says the same. Take your frame as $S$ and the lamp's momentary rest frame as $S'$. The light that reaches you travels along the radius, at right angles to the lamp's velocity, so $\cos\theta = 0$ and $\omega' = \omega\gamma$ (Lecture 6, slide 28). Here $\omega'$ is the frequency in the lamp's frame, so you observe $\omega = \omega'/\gamma$. At $\beta = 0.5$ this is $0.866$ of the emitted frequency.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={1}>

An aeroplane flies around you in a circle at 50% of the speed of sound, in still air. You stand at the centre. How do you hear its engine?

<Hint>

The classical Doppler effect comes from the changing distance between source and listener. Is there any other effect at these speeds?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="The sound is:"
  choices={[
    "Redshifted (lower frequency)",
    "Blueshifted (higher frequency)",
    "Neither"
  ]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For sound there is no time dilation to speak of, and the distance does not change.

<Step title="No change of distance, no shift">

Every sound crest travels the same distance through the still air to you and takes the same time. The crests therefore arrive as often as they are sent: the pitch is unchanged. The classical Doppler effect needs a velocity along the line of sight, and here there is none.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The aeroplane's clocks do run slow, but at half the speed of sound (about 170 m/s) $\beta$ is below $10^{-6}$ and $\gamma - 1 \approx \beta^2/2$ is below $10^{-12}$: nothing an ear could notice. The redshift of part (c) is purely relativistic.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A moving, slanting rod" points={6} mit="Problem 2">

A thin rod of length $L'$ is at rest in the frame $S'$. It lies in the $x'y'$ plane and makes an angle $\theta'$ with the $x'$ axis. An observer in $S$ sees the rod move at speed $\beta c$ in the direction of increasing $x$. (MIT's Figure 1 shows the rod, slanting upwards to the right, with the two sets of axes.)

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What length $L$ does the observer in $S$ measure for the rod?

<Hint>

Split the rod into a part along $x'$ and a part along $y'$. Treat the two parts separately.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Only the part along the motion is contracted. Put the two parts together again with Pythagoras.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-len" label="$L =$" expr="L0*sqrt(1 - b^2*cos(th)^2)"
  vars={{ L0: [0.5, 2], b: [0.2, 0.9], th: [0.2, 1.3] }}
  names={{ L0: "L'", b: "\\beta", th: "\\theta'" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "L0*sqrt(1 - b^2)", hint: "That contracts the whole rod. Only its component along the motion, L' cos θ', is contracted; the component across the motion keeps its length." },
    { expr: "L0*sqrt(1 - b^2*sin(th)^2)", hint: "You have contracted the component across the motion. The motion is along x, so it is the x component, L' cos θ', that shrinks." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A length is measured by marking both ends at the same time. Along the motion that gives the contracted length; across the motion nothing changes.

<Step title="The two components">

In $S'$ the rod has the components $L'\cos\theta'$ along $x'$ and $L'\sin\theta'$ along $y'$. In $S$ the first is shorter by $1/\gamma$ (Lecture 5, slide 12) and the second is unchanged:

<Formula label="The rod's components in S" tex="\Delta x = \frac{L'\cos\theta'}{\gamma} = L'\cos\theta'\sqrt{1-\beta^2}, \qquad \Delta y = L'\sin\theta'" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The length">

<Formula label="Length of the moving, slanting rod" tex="L = \sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2} = L'\sqrt{(1-\beta^2)\cos^2\theta' + \sin^2\theta'} = L'\sqrt{1 - \beta^2\cos^2\theta'}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\theta' = 0$ the rod lies along the motion and $L = L'/\gamma$, the full contraction. For $\theta' = 90^\circ$ it stands across the motion and $L = L'$. The interval gives the same answer by another route: the two marks are simultaneous in $S$, so $\Delta s^2 = L^2$, and in $S'$ the same two events are separated by $L'$ in space and by $c\,\Delta t' = -\beta L'\cos\theta'$ in time, so $\Delta s^2 = L'^2 - \beta^2L'^2\cos^2\theta'$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

What angle $\theta$ does the moving rod make with the $x$ axis? Give $\theta$ in radians as a formula (the box knows `atan`, `acos` and `asin`).

<Hint>

The tangent of the angle is the ratio of the two components you found in part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-theta" label="$\theta =$" expr="atan(tan(th)/sqrt(1 - b^2))"
  vars={{ b: [0.2, 0.9], th: [0.2, 1.3] }}
  names={{ b: "\\beta", th: "\\theta'" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "atan(tan(th)*sqrt(1 - b^2))", hint: "This makes the moving rod flatter. The x component shrinks while the y component stays, so the rod must stand steeper: tan θ is larger than tan θ'." },
    { expr: "th", hint: "The angle does change: the x component of the rod is contracted and the y component is not." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The rod's direction follows from its two components in $S$.

<Step title="The angle">

<Formula label="Angle of the moving rod" tex="\tan\theta = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{L'\sin\theta'}{L'\cos\theta'/\gamma} = \gamma\tan\theta'" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Since $\gamma > 1$, the moving rod stands steeper than the rod at rest, as it must when only its horizontal extent shrinks. For $\beta \to 0$ the angle is unchanged, and for $\beta \to 1$ the rod is squeezed towards the vertical, $\theta \to 90^\circ$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Evaluate both for $L' = 1.00$ m, $\theta' = 30^\circ$ and $\beta = 0.40$. Give $L$ in metres and $\theta$ in degrees.

<Hint>

First find $\gamma$ for $\beta = 0.40$. Keep four significant figures.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-len-num" label="$L =$" value="0.938" unit="m" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-len-num" />

<Answer id="p2-theta-num" label="$\theta =$" value="32.2" unit="degrees" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-theta-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into the two formulas.

<Step title="The numbers">

$\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.16} = 1.0911$. Then $L = 1.00\,\sqrt{1 - 0.16 \times 0.75} = \sqrt{0.88} = 0.938$ m, and $\tan\theta = 1.0911 \times 0.57735 = 0.6299$, so $\theta = 32.2^\circ$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

From the components: $\Delta x = 0.8660/1.0911 = 0.7937$ m and $\Delta y = 0.500$ m. Their ratio is $0.630$, and $\sqrt{0.7937^2 + 0.500^2} = 0.938$ m. The rod is 6% shorter and about $2^\circ$ steeper.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Decay in flight" points={3} mit="Problem 3">

An unstable particle of high energy enters a detector and leaves a track $1.05$ mm long before it decays. Its speed relative to the detector was $0.992c$. What is its proper lifetime, in picoseconds ($1\ \text{ps} = 10^{-12}$ s)? In other words, what lifetime would the same particle have if it sat still in the detector?

<Hint>

First find how long the particle lived according to the detector's clocks: a distance and a speed give a time.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The particle is a moving clock. Its own time between entering and decaying is shorter than the detector's time by the factor $\gamma$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-tau" label="$\Delta\tau =$" value="0.446" unit="ps" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-tau" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The track gives the lifetime in the detector frame. The proper lifetime is shorter by the factor $\gamma$ (time dilation).

<Step title="The lifetime in the detector frame">

$\Delta t = d/v = 1.05\times 10^{-3}\ \text{m} / (0.992 \times 2.998\times 10^8\ \text{m/s}) = 3.53\times 10^{-12}$ s.

</Step>

<Step title="The proper lifetime">

<Formula label="Proper lifetime from the track" tex="\Delta\tau = \frac{\Delta t}{\gamma} = \frac{d}{v}\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} = 3.53\times 10^{-12}\ \text{s} \times \sqrt{1 - 0.992^2} = 4.46\times 10^{-13}\ \text{s}" cite="reading2" at="slide 4 (time dilation, applied to our own solution)" />

Here $\sqrt{1 - 0.992^2} = \sqrt{0.015936} = 0.1262$, that is $\gamma = 7.92$. The proper lifetime is $0.446$ ps.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In the particle's own frame the detector rushes past at $0.992c$ and is contracted: the track is only $1.05\ \text{mm}/7.92 = 0.133$ mm long there. Covering it at $0.992c$ takes $0.133\times 10^{-3}/(0.992 \times 2.998\times 10^8) = 4.46\times 10^{-13}$ s, the same answer. Without relativity the particle could only have gone $0.992c \times 0.446\ \text{ps} = 0.133$ mm in the detector.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="The pole vaulter and the barn" points={12} mit="Problem 4">

A pole lies along the $x'$ axis and is at rest in the frame $S'$ of a pole vaulter. $S'$ moves to the right relative to $S$ at the speed $v = (\sqrt 3/2)\,c$, so that $\gamma = 2$. The pole is 10 m long in its own rest frame: its left end is at $x' = 0$ and its right end at $x' = 10$ m. A barn is at rest in $S$ and is 8 m long there: its left door is at $x = 0$ and its right door at $x = 8$ m. At first the left door is open and the right door is closed, and the pole enters through the left door.

At $t = 0$ the left door closes, with the left end of the pole just inside it, at $x = 0$. At that same time in $S$ the right end of the pole is at $x = 5$ m, well inside the barn. Call the event $(x = 0,\ t = 0)$ event A and the event $(x = 5\ \text{m},\ t = 0)$ event B. For a moment, then, the pole is shut inside the barn with both doors closed. Shortly afterwards the right door is opened, and the pole runs out without damage.

The times in this problem are asked as $ct$ and $ct'$, in metres. The vaulter's clocks are set so that $t'_A = 0$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the time of event B in the vaulter's frame. Give $ct'_B$ in metres, with its sign.

<Hint>

Events A and B are simultaneous in $S$ but lie at different places. Use the Lorentz transformation for the time, $ct' = \gamma(ct - \beta x)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$ct'_B =$" value="-8.66" unit="m" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Simultaneity is relative: two events at the same $t$ and different $x$ have different $t'$.

<Step title="Transform event B">

<Formula label="Event B in the vaulter's frame" tex="ct'_B = \gamma\,(ct_B - \beta x_B) = 2\left(0 - \tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}\times 5\ \text{m}\right) = -5\sqrt 3\ \text{m} = -8.66\ \text{m}" cite="reading1" at="slide 16 (the Lorentz transformation, applied to our own solution)" />

In seconds this is $t'_B = -8.66\ \text{m}/c = -28.9$ ns. For the vaulter, event B happens before event A.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The position of event B must be the right end of the pole: $x'_B = \gamma(x_B - \beta ct_B) = 2 \times 5 = 10$ m. It is. The interval agrees too: in $S$, $\Delta s^2 = 5^2 = 25\ \text{m}^2$, and in $S'$, $10^2 - 8.66^2 = 25\ \text{m}^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Take the instant $t' = t'_B$ of the vaulter's frame. Where are the two ends of the pole at that instant, in the coordinates of $S$? The right end is event B itself, at $x = 5$ m and $ct = 0$. Give $x$ and $ct$ of the left end, in metres.

<Hint>

The left end at that instant is the event $x' = 0$, $ct' = ct'_B$. Transform it back to $S$ with the inverse transformation: $x = \gamma(x' + \beta ct')$ and $ct = \gamma(ct' + \beta x')$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b-x" label="Left end: $x =$" value="-15" unit="m" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-b-x" />

<Answer id="p4-b-t" label="Left end: $ct =$" value="-17.3" unit="m" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-b-t" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** "Both ends at the same time" means something different in each frame. Take the two events that are simultaneous for the vaulter and express them in $S$.

<Step title="The left end at the vaulter's time of B">

The event is $x' = 0$, $ct' = -5\sqrt 3$ m. The inverse transformation (Lecture 5, slide 10: change the sign of $v$) gives

<Formula label="The left end at the vaulter's time of B" tex="x = \gamma\,(x' + \beta ct') = 2\times\tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}\times(-5\sqrt 3\ \text{m}) = -15\ \text{m}, \qquad ct = \gamma\,(ct' + \beta x') = 2\times(-5\sqrt 3\ \text{m}) = -17.3\ \text{m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b) (our own solution)" />

So when the right end of the pole is 5 m inside the barn, the vaulter says the left end is still 15 m in front of the left door.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two ends are 20 m apart in $x$. That is $\gamma$ times the rest length, as it must be for two events that are simultaneous in the pole's frame and 10 m apart there: $\Delta x = \gamma\,\Delta x'$. As a second check, in $S$ the left end moves at $\beta = \sqrt 3/2$ and reaches $x = 0$ at $ct = 0$, so at $ct = -17.3$ m it was at $x = -\tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}\times 17.3 = -15$ m.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Now take the instant $t' = t'_A = 0$. The left end of the pole is event A itself, at $x = 0$ and $ct = 0$. Give $x$ and $ct$ of the right end at that instant, in metres.

<Hint>

The right end at that instant is the event $x' = 10$ m, $ct' = 0$. Transform it back to $S$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c-x" label="Right end: $x =$" value="20" unit="m" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-c-x" />

<Answer id="p4-c-t" label="Right end: $ct =$" value="17.3" unit="m" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-c-t" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same step as in part (b), for the other end and the other instant.

<Step title="The right end at the vaulter's time of A">

<Formula label="The right end at the vaulter's time of A" tex="x = \gamma\,(x' + \beta ct') = 2\times 10\ \text{m} = 20\ \text{m}, \qquad ct = \gamma\,(ct' + \beta x') = 2\times\tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}\times 10\ \text{m} = 17.3\ \text{m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c) (our own solution)" />

When the left door closes behind the pole, the vaulter says the right end is already at $x = 20$ m: 12 m beyond the right door, which stands at $x = 8$ m.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In $S$ the right end is at $x = 5$ m at $ct = 0$ and moves at $\beta = \sqrt 3/2$. At $ct = 17.3$ m it has reached $5 + \tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2}\times 17.3 = 20$ m. The two ends are again 20 m apart in $x$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Sketch the pole and the barn in the vaulter's frame $S'$ at the two instants $t'_B$ and $t'_A$, and say in a few sentences how the vaulter understands what happened. Was the pole ever shut inside the barn for the vaulter?

<Hint>

In $S'$ the pole is at rest and 10 m long. How long is the barn, and which way does it move?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Place the barn at each instant with what you know: at $t'_B$ the point of the barn 5 m (in $S$) from its left door is at the right end of the pole, and at $t'_A$ the left door is at the left end of the pole.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** For the vaulter the barn is the moving body, so the barn is contracted and the pole is not. The pole never fits. The two doors do not act at the same time in the vaulter's frame, and that removes the contradiction.

<Step title="The barn in the vaulter's frame">

The barn moves to the left at $v$ and is $8\ \text{m}/\gamma = 4$ m long. The pole stays between $x' = 0$ and $x' = 10$ m.

</Step>

<Step title="The first sketch: the instant of B">

At $ct' = -8.66$ m the point of the barn that lies 5 m from its left door (in $S$) is at the right end of the pole, $x' = 10$ m. Lengths of the barn are halved, so the left door is $2.5$ m behind that point, at $x' = 7.5$ m, and the right door is $1.5$ m ahead of it, at $x' = 11.5$ m. The right door is still closed and has not yet reached the pole. The left $7.5$ m of the pole are still outside, in front of the open left door.

</Step>

<Step title="The second sketch: the instant of A">

At $ct' = 0$ the left door is at $x' = 0$ and closes just behind the left end of the pole. The right door is at $x' = 4$ m. The right 6 m of the pole stick out through the right door, which must therefore be open already.

</Step>

In the vaulter's frame it is at $ct' = \gamma(ct - \beta x) = 2\,(3.464 - 6.928) = -6.93$ m, before the left door closes at $ct' = 0$. So the vaulter sees this order: the short barn's closed right door comes towards the tip of the pole; the right door opens and the tip passes through; later the left door reaches the back end of the pole and closes behind it. The two frames disagree about the order of "left door closes" and "right door opens", which they may, because these two events are too far apart for light to connect them: their separation is spacelike.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The opening of the right door, transformed directly, lies at $x' = \gamma(x - \beta ct) = 2\,(8 - 3) = 10$ m: at the tip of the pole.

</Step>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="The wave equation under a Lorentz transformation" points={9} mit="Problem 6">

The lesson on [the speed of light](/courses/8-033/speed-of-light-michelson-morley) showed that the wave equation changes its form under a Galilean transformation. Here you show that the Lorentz transformation leaves it alone.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

A function $f$ can be written with the coordinates of $S$ or with those of $S'$, which moves at speed $v$ along the $x$ axis. </Step>

<Formula label="How the derivatives transform" tex="\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \gamma\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x'} - \frac{v}{c^2}\frac{\partial f}{\partial t'}\right), \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = \gamma\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t'} - v\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x'}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Show that the wave equation for light,

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Square the two operators of part (a). The mixed terms cancel and the rest carries a factor $\gamma^2(1 - v^2/c^2) = 1$.

<Step title="The second derivatives">

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="Two flashes: same time difference, same separation, opposite order" points={6} mit="Problem 7">

A red flash follows $5.00\ \mu\text{s}$ later, and it is the one further from his origin. A second observer $S'$ finds exactly the same size of the time difference and the same size of the distance between the two flashes, but she says that the red flash came first.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-beta" label="$\beta =$" value="0.899" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-beta" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two events are too far apart for light to connect them ($c\,\Delta t = 1499$ m is less than $2400$ m), so their order depends on the frame. Ask for the frame in which the time difference has the same size and the opposite sign.

<Step title="The condition">

With $a = c\,\Delta t/\Delta x = 1499/2400 = 0.6246$:

<Formula label="Reversing the time difference" tex="c\,\Delta t' = \gamma\,(c\,\Delta t - \beta\,\Delta x) = -c\,\Delta t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \gamma\,(a - \beta) = -a" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for the speed">

Squaring and using $\gamma^2 = 1/(1 - \beta^2)$ gives $(a - \beta)^2 = a^2(1 - \beta^2)$, that is $\beta\left[\beta(1 + a^2) - 2a\right] = 0$. The root $\beta = 0$ is the frame $S$ itself (it solves the squared equation with the wrong sign). The other root is

<Formula label="The speed of the second observer" tex="\beta = \frac{2a}{1 + a^2} = \frac{2\times 0.6246}{1 + 0.6246^2} = 0.899" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a) (our own solution)" />

So $S'$ moves at $0.899c$, about $2.69\times 10^8$ m/s, in the $+x$ direction ($\beta$ is positive).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $\beta = 0.8986$, $\gamma = 2.279$ and $\gamma(a - \beta) = 2.279 \times (0.6246 - 0.8986) = -0.6246$: the time difference is reversed, $\Delta t' = -5.00\ \mu\text{s}$. The result has a neat reading. In the frame that moves at $\beta = a$ the two flashes are simultaneous, and $2a/(1 + a^2)$ is the speed you get by adding the velocity $a$ to itself with the relativistic rule: $S'$ is as far beyond the "simultaneous" frame as $S$ is behind it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Step title="The size">

</Solution>

## Problem 9

<Exercise id="p9" title="Transformation of speeds" points={3} mit="Problem 9">

A particle has the velocity components $u_x'$ and $u_y'$ in the frame $S'$, and $S'$ moves to the right at speed $v$ relative to $S$. Find the speed $u$ of the particle in $S$ as a function of $u_x'$, $u_y'$ and $v$, written out without $\gamma$. Then show that $u$ stays below $c$ as long as the particle is slower than light in $S'$ and $v$ is below $c$, and that $u$ goes to $c$ when $u_x'$, $u_y'$ or $v$ goes to $c$. (MIT's sheet points to a textbook problem for a form of the answer that makes the limit plain. That form is derived in the solution here.)

In the box type `ux` for $u_x'$ and `uy` for $u_y'$.

<Hint>

Start from the two velocity components in $S$ that the lessons give: $u_x = (u_x' + v)/(1 + u_x'v/c^2)$ and $u_y = u_y'\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}/(1 + u_x'v/c^2)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the limit, work out $1 - u^2/c^2$ and try to write its numerator as a product of two factors.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-u" label="$u =$" expr="sqrt((ux + v)^2 + uy^2*(1 - v^2/c^2))/(1 + ux*v/c^2)"
  vars={{ ux: [0.1, 0.6], uy: [0.1, 0.6], v: [0.2, 0.9], c: [1, 1.5] }}
  names={{ ux: "u_x'", uy: "u_y'", v: "v", c: "c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt((ux + v)^2 + uy^2)", hint: "That is the Galilean sum. Both components are divided by 1 + u_x'v/c², and the y component also carries the factor √(1 − v²/c²)." },
    { expr: "sqrt((ux + v)^2 + uy^2)/(1 + ux*v/c^2)", hint: "The y component is missing its factor √(1 − v²/c²): the clocks of S' and of S differ, which slows the sideways motion too." },
    { expr: "sqrt((ux + v)^2 + uy^2*(1 - v^2/c^2))", hint: "The denominator 1 + u_x'v/c² is missing. It divides both components." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Add the squares of the two components. The limit becomes clear once $1 - u^2/c^2$ is written as a product.

<Step title="The speed">

<Formula label="The velocity components in S" tex="u_x = \frac{u_x' + v}{1 + \frac{u_x'v}{c^2}}, \qquad u_y = \frac{u_y'\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}}{1 + \frac{u_x'v}{c^2}}" cite="reading2" at="slide 16" />

<Formula label="The speed in S" tex="u = \sqrt{u_x^2 + u_y^2} = \frac{\sqrt{(u_x' + v)^2 + u_y'^2\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)}}{1 + \frac{u_x'v}{c^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 9 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="A form that shows the limit">

Write $u'^2 = u_x'^2 + u_y'^2$. Then

<Formula label="One minus the squared speed" tex="1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2} = \frac{\left(1 + \frac{u_x'v}{c^2}\right)^2 - \frac{(u_x' + v)^2}{c^2} - \frac{u_y'^2}{c^2}\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)}{\left(1 + \frac{u_x'v}{c^2}\right)^2} = \frac{\left(1 - \frac{u'^2}{c^2}\right)\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)}{\left(1 + \frac{u_x'v}{c^2}\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 9 (our own solution)" />

In the numerator the terms $\pm 2u_x'v/c^2$ cancel, and what is left, $1 - v^2/c^2 - u'^2/c^2 + u'^2v^2/c^4$, is the product shown. (This is the relation $\gamma_{u} = \gamma_{u'}\gamma\,[1 + u_x'v/c^2]$ of Lecture 6, slide 22, squared and turned upside down, with the roles of the two frames as in this problem.)

</Step>

<Step title="The limits">

If $u' < c$ and $v < c$, both factors in the numerator are positive, so $1 - u^2/c^2 > 0$: the speed $u$ is below $c$. If $u' \to c$ (through $u_x'$, through $u_y'$ or through both) or $v \to c$, one factor goes to zero and $u \to c$. No combination of speeds below $c$ adds up to more than $c$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Three special cases from the first formula. With $u_y' = 0$ and $u_x' = c$: $u = (c + v)/(1 + v/c) = c$. With $u_x' = 0$ and $u_y' = c$: $u^2 = v^2 + c^2(1 - v^2/c^2) = c^2$. With $v = c$: $u = (u_x' + c)/(1 + u_x'/c) = c$. And for speeds far below $c$ the formula becomes the Galilean $\sqrt{(u_x' + v)^2 + u_y'^2}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 10

<Exercise id="p10" title="Quasar, quasar, burning bright" points={6} mit="Problem 10">

The spectrum of the quasar 3C 9 shows familiar lines of hydrogen, but shifted so far to the red that each wavelength is three times as long as the wavelength measured for hydrogen at rest in the laboratory.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Show that the classical Doppler formula for a source that moves away through a medium at rest, $\lambda_{\text{obs}} = \lambda\,(1 + v/c)$, gives a speed of recession greater than $c$. What value of $v/c$ does it give?

<Hint>

Put $\lambda_{\text{obs}} = 3\lambda$ into the formula.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-a" label="$v/c =$" value="2" tolerance="1%" calc="p10-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the classical picture a receding source stretches each wavelength by the distance it moves in one period.

<Step title="The classical speed">

$3\lambda = \lambda(1 + v/c)$ gives $v/c = 2$: twice the speed of light. In the lesson's language this is the "moving source" factor $\omega'/\omega = 1/(1 + \beta) = 1/3$ (the lesson reads it off the plot on slide 9 of Lecture 7).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The classical formula for a moving observer, $\omega'/\omega = 1 - \beta$, would give $\beta = 2/3$ instead. The two classical formulas disagree because they need a medium to say who is moving. Light has no medium, and relativity replaces both by one formula, used in part (b).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Assume that the quasar moves straight away from the Earth. What speed of recession does the relativistic Doppler formula give? Give $v/c$.

<Hint>

For a source moving straight away, $\lambda_{\text{obs}}/\lambda = \sqrt{(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)}$. Square it and solve for $\beta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-b" label="$v/c =$" value="0.8" tolerance="1%" calc="p10-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The relativistic factor lies between the two classical ones and never asks for a speed above $c$.

<Step title="The relativistic speed">

<Formula label="Recession speed from the relativistic Doppler factor" tex="\frac{\lambda_{\text{obs}}}{\lambda} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}} = 3 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 9\,(1 - \beta) = 1 + \beta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \beta = \frac{8}{10} = 0.8" cite="reading3" at="slides 27 and 28 (k′ = kγ(1 − β cos θ) along the line of sight, with λ′/λ = k/k′; solved for β in our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\sqrt{1.8/0.2} = \sqrt 9 = 3$. However large the factor, the formula gives $\beta = (r^2 - 1)/(r^2 + 1)$ for a ratio $r$, always below 1. The value $0.8$ lies between the two classical answers, $2/3$ and $2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Is a quasar's redshift a Doppler shift?">

This problem treats the redshift as motion through space. In cosmology the redshift of a distant quasar is understood as the stretching of light while space expands, and the ratio of wavelengths, here 3, is the factor by which the universe has grown since the light set out. The lesson on [the cosmological redshift](/courses/8-286/cosmological-redshift) works this out.

## Problem 11

<Exercise id="p11" title="Two laser beams from a starship" points={3} mit="Problem 11">

A question from an MIT exam of 2004. A starship fires two identical laser guns in opposite directions (opposite in the ship's own frame). In your frame the two beams make an angle of $120^\circ$ with each other, and you find the same frequency for both. How fast does the ship move in your frame, and in which direction? Draw a picture first.

<Hint>

In the ship's frame the two beams have the same frequency. A beam that points partly forward is shifted up in your frame, one that points partly backward is shifted down. What does "the same frequency for both" tell you about the angle between the beams and the ship's velocity, in the ship's frame?

</Hint>

<Hint>

A beam sent at right angles to the motion in the ship's frame is tilted forward in your frame (aberration). Use $\cos\theta' = (\cos\theta - \beta)/(1 - \beta\cos\theta)$ with the frames in the right roles.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-beta" label="$v/c =$" value="0.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p11-beta" />

<Answer id="p11-dir" label="The ship moves:"
  choices={[
    "Along the line that halves the 120 degree angle between the beams, towards the side the beams lean to",
    "Along the same line, but away from the side the beams lean to",
    "Along one of the two beams",
    "At right angles to the plane of the two beams"
  ]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Equal frequencies force the beams to be at right angles to the motion in the ship's frame. Aberration then tilts both forward by the same amount, and the tilt gives the speed.

<Step title="Why the beams are sideways in the ship's frame">

Let the beams make the angles $\theta$ and $180^\circ - \theta$ with the ship's velocity, in the ship's frame $S$. You are the frame $S'$, which moves at $-\beta$ relative to the ship. The Doppler formula of the lesson (Lecture 6, slide 27, with $-\beta$ in place of $\beta$) gives your frequencies as $\omega\gamma(1 + \beta\cos\theta)$ and $\omega\gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta)$. They are equal only if $\cos\theta = 0$: both beams leave the ship at right angles to its motion.

</Step>

<Step title="The tilt in your frame">

<Formula label="Aberration of a beam sent sideways" tex="\cos\theta' = \frac{\cos\theta + \beta}{1 + \beta\cos\theta} = \beta \qquad (\cos\theta = 0)" cite="reading3" at="slide 27 (with −β in place of β, applied to our own solution)" />

In your frame each beam makes the angle $\theta'$ with the ship's velocity, one on each side of it, so the angle between the beams is $2\theta' = 120^\circ$. Then $\theta' = 60^\circ$ and $\beta = \cos 60^\circ = 0.5$.

</Step>

<Step title="The picture">

The ship moves at $c/2$ along the line that halves the $120^\circ$ angle, towards the side on which the beams lie: the two beams lean forward, like the arms of a letter V whose open side points in the direction of flight, with the ship at the tip of the V at the moment of firing.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A light pulse sent sideways in the ship's frame keeps up with the ship: its velocity component along the flight direction must be $v$. A pulse moving at $c$ at $60^\circ$ to that direction has the component $c\cos 60^\circ = c/2$. That agrees. Both beams have the frequency $\gamma\omega = 1.155\,\omega$ in your frame.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Exercise id="p12" title="The Ives–Stilwell experiment" points={9} mit="Problem 12">

Light that arrives at $t = 5.00\ \mu\text{s}$ left the clock at a time $t_e$ when the clock was at the distance $0.6\,c\,t_e$: so $t_e + 0.6\,t_e = 5.00\ \mu\text{s}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p14-b" label="He reads:" value="2.50" unit="μs" tolerance="1%" calc="p14-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Seeing adds the travel time of the light to time dilation. Together they make the Doppler factor.

<Step title="When the light left">

The light seen at $t = 5.00\ \mu\text{s}$ left the moving clock at $t_e$, with $t_e(1 + 0.6) = 5.00\ \mu\text{s}$, so $t_e = 3.125\ \mu\text{s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="What the clock showed then">

At that moment the clock of $S'$ showed $t_e/\gamma = 3.125/1.25 = 2.50\ \mu\text{s}$. That is the reading he sees.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ratio of the seen reading to his own time is $1/[\gamma(1 + \beta)] = \sqrt{(1 - \beta)/(1 + \beta)} = \sqrt{0.4/1.6} = 0.5$, the Doppler factor for a receding source (Lecture 7, slide 9). Indeed $0.5\times 5.00 = 2.50\ \mu\text{s}$. A receding clock is seen to run slow by more than time dilation alone ($0.5$ against $0.8$).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 15 (optional)

<Exercise id="p15" title="The unreachable goal" points={3} mit="Optional Problem 15" optional>

A spaceship is at rest in a frame $S$. It is given a speed increment of $0.50c$. Then, in its new rest frame, it is given another increment of $0.50c$, and so on. How many increments does it take until the ship's speed relative to $S$ is more than $0.999c$? The box wants the whole number.

<Hint>

Each increment combines with the speed so far by the relativistic rule for parallel velocities: from a speed $\beta$ the next one is $(\beta + 0.5)/(1 + 0.5\,\beta)$. You can simply go step by step.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A shortcut: look at the ratio $(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)$ before and after one increment.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p15-n" label="Number of increments:" value="7" tolerance="0.1" calc="p15-n" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Speeds do not add, but the factors $(1 + \beta)/(1 - \beta)$ multiply.

<Step title="One increment">

<Formula label="Adding parallel velocities" tex="\beta_3 = \frac{\beta_1 + \beta_2}{1 + \beta_1\beta_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1 + \beta_3}{1 - \beta_3} = \frac{(1 + \beta_1)(1 + \beta_2)}{(1 - \beta_1)(1 - \beta_2)}" cite="reading1" at="slide 18 (the addition rule; the product form is our own step)" />

For $\beta = 0.5$ the factor is $1.5/0.5 = 3$. After $n$ increments it is $3^n$, so the speed is then $\beta = (3^n - 1)/(3^n + 1)$.

</Step>

<Step title="How many">

$\beta > 0.999$ needs a factor above $1.999/0.001 = 1999$. Since $3^6 = 729$ and $3^7 = 2187$, it takes 7 increments.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Step by step: $0.5$, $0.8$, $0.9286$, $0.9756$, $0.9918$, $0.99726$, $0.99909$. The sixth is still below $0.999$, the seventh is above. In the lesson's language the rapidities add (Lecture 5, slide 17): each increment adds $\tanh^{-1}0.5 = 0.549$, and $\tanh^{-1}0.999 = 3.800$ is $6.92$ times that. However many increments follow, the speed stays below $c$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 16 (optional)

<Exercise id="p16" title="A philosophical difficulty" points={3} mit="Optional Problem 16" optional>

Suppose an event A causes an event B, and the effect travels from A to B at a speed $u$ greater than $c$ in the frame $S$. Show with the relativistic transformation of velocities that there is an inertial frame $S'$, moving relative to $S$ at a speed below $c$, in which the two events happen in the opposite order. So if cause must come before effect for everyone, no signal can travel faster than light.

In the box give the speed that $v$ must exceed, in terms of `u` and `c`.

<Hint>

Let the signal run along $+x$ and let $S'$ move along $+x$ at $v$. In $S'$ the signal's velocity is $u' = (u - v)/(1 - uv/c^2)$. For which $v$ does the denominator change sign?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Then look at the two events themselves: $\Delta x' = \gamma\,\Delta t\,(u - v)$ and $\Delta t' = \gamma\,\Delta t\,(1 - uv/c^2)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p16-v" label="The order is reversed for $v >$" expr="c^2/u"
  vars={{ u: [2, 4], c: [1.1, 1.5] }}
  names={{ u: "u", c: "c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "c/u", hint: "That is a pure number, not a speed. Multiply by c." },
    { expr: "u", hint: "A frame as fast as the signal would itself be faster than light. The order already flips at a smaller speed, where 1 − uv/c² changes sign." },
    { expr: "c", hint: "No frame moves at c. The order already flips at a smaller speed, where 1 − uv/c² changes sign." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a signal faster than light the two events are spacelike separated, and for such a pair the order in time depends on the frame.

<Step title="The signal's velocity in the moving frame">

In $S$ let A be at the origin at $t = 0$ and B at $x = u\,\Delta t$ at the time $\Delta t > 0$. The inverse of the velocity rule of the lessons (change the sign of $v$) gives the signal's velocity in $S'$:

<Formula label="The signal's velocity in S'" tex="u' = \frac{u - v}{1 - \frac{uv}{c^2}}" cite="reading2" at="slide 16 (solved for the primed velocity)" />

The numerator is positive for every $v < c$, because $u > c$. The denominator is positive for small $v$ but negative once $v > c^2/u$. This threshold is below $c$, because $u > c$. So for $c^2/u < v < c$ the velocity $u'$ is negative, although B still lies on the $+x'$ side of A.

</Step>

<Step title="What a negative velocity means here">

Transform the two events directly (Lecture 4, slide 16):

<Formula label="The two events in S'" tex="\Delta x' = \gamma\,(\Delta x - v\,\Delta t) = \gamma\,\Delta t\,(u - v) > 0, \qquad \Delta t' = \gamma\left(\Delta t - \frac{v\,\Delta x}{c^2}\right) = \gamma\,\Delta t\left(1 - \frac{uv}{c^2}\right) < 0 \quad \text{for } v > \frac{c^2}{u}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 16 (our own solution)" />

B lies further along $+x'$ than A but happens earlier: the "signal" runs from B to A in $S'$, which is what the negative $u' = \Delta x'/\Delta t'$ says. The effect comes before its cause.

</Step>

<Step title="The conclusion">

Such a frame exists for every $u > c$, with a speed $v$ below $c$. If all inertial frames are equally good and causes must come before their effects in each of them, signals faster than light are impossible (Lecture 7, slide 18, which also notes the worse consequence: an answer could be sent back to arrive before the first signal left).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $u \le c$ the threshold $c^2/u$ is at least $c$, so no allowed frame reverses the order: ordinary causes and effects are safe. For $u \to \infty$ the threshold goes to zero: an instantaneous signal is reversed by any motion at all. An example: for $u = 2c$ every frame faster than $c/2$ sees B first.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>
