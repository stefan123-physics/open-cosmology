Today's topics: the Schwarzschild metric and black holes, the interpretation of $r$ and $t$, and the propagation of radial light rays (slide 3). ## What the course expects

Slides 4–6 list what students should be able to do about black holes. In *depth*: solve problems with the Schwarzschild metric, interpret it (the time coordinate, the $r$ coordinate, shell coordinates, gravitational redshift, the event horizon, the Schwarzschild radius), and work out its geodesics (radial and angular motion, stable and unstable circular orbits, general geodesics with an effective potential, weak light deflection, Mercury's perihelion shift and tidal forces), as well as the definition of a black hole and the Shapiro time delay. In *breadth*: the evidence that general relativity is correct and that black holes exist (both of stellar mass and supermassive), how black holes probably form, the no-hair theorem, the singularity, Hawking radiation, what falling into a black hole feels like and looks like from afar, the river model, time travel and wormholes, and the Taylor–Hulse binary pulsar.

## The Schwarzschild metric

The board topics (slide 8): escape velocity, the Newtonian metric, the Schwarzschild metric, and the quantities $\beta_r$ and $\gamma_r$. In outline (the page's own): a body launched from radius $r$ escapes a mass $M$ if its speed exceeds $\sqrt{2GM/r}$. Write this escape speed in units of $c$ as $\beta_r$, with $\gamma_r = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta_r^2}$. With $c = G = 1$, the Schwarzschild metric from the [gallery of metrics in Lecture 17](/courses/8-033/equivalence-principle-metrics) then reads

<Formula label="Schwarzschild metric" tex="d\tau^2 = \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)dt^2 - \left(1 - \frac{2M}{r}\right)^{-1}dr^2 - r^2d\theta^2 - r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2 = \frac{dt^2}{\gamma_r^2} - \gamma_r^2\,dr^2 - r^2d\theta^2 - r^2\sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2" cite="mit" at="slide 8 (topics; the metric is on slide 30 of the Lecture 17 slides, the γ form is the page's own)" />

since $\beta_r^2 = 2M/r$. Far away ($r \gg 2M$) it becomes the Minkowski metric in spherical coordinates. To first order in $M/r$ its time part, $(1 - 2M/r)dt^2$, matches the Newtonian metric's $(1 + 2\phi)dt^2$ with $\phi = -M/r$; its radial part, $(1 + 2M/r)dr^2$ to that order, is the curvature of space that the Newtonian metric leaves out.

**Interpreting $r$ and $t$** (the page's own reading of slide 3's topic). At fixed $r$, $\theta = \pi/2$, a circle has circumference $2\pi r$: $r$ is *defined* by circumferences, not by distances to the centre. A clock at rest far away measures $t$. Observers standing on a shell at fixed $r$ measure

<Formula label="Shell observers" tex="d\tau_{\text{shell}} = \frac{dt}{\gamma_r} = \sqrt{1 - \frac{2M}{r}}\,dt, \qquad dr_{\text{shell}} = \gamma_r\,dr = \frac{dr}{\sqrt{1 - 2M/r}}" cite="mit" at="slide 8 (topics; the formulas are the page's own)" />

so their clocks run slow by $\gamma_r$, which is the gravitational redshift, and radial distances are longer than the change in $r$. The distance to the origin is therefore *more* than circumference over $2\pi$, the "excess radius" (slide 9 states the opposite, "distance to origin < circumference/2π", above a picture of space stretched into two joined funnels; the funnels themselves show the radial distance being the longer one).

## What is a black hole?

**An object contained within its own event horizon** (slides 10–11). At $r = 2M$, the Schwarzschild radius ($2GM/c^2$ in ordinary units), $\beta_r = 1$: the escape speed is $c$, $\gamma_r$ is infinite, and nothing from inside can get out. A black hole has just **three measurable properties: mass, angular momentum and charge** (slides 12–13): the no-hair theorem.

**Radial light rays** (the page's own, for slide 3's topic). Setting $d\tau = 0$ for light moving radially gives $dr/dt = \pm(1 - 2M/r)$: seen in the far-away observer's time, light slows down near the horizon and never seems to reach it. That is a statement about the coordinate $t$, which breaks down at the horizon, not about what the light does.

## The river model

For a feel of what a black hole is *really* like, the slides turn to a picture by Andrew Hamilton (2004), the river model (slides 15–16; see also Hamilton and Lisle, *American Journal of Physics* 76, 519, 2008). Space itself flows inward, like a river, at the Newtonian escape speed $\beta_r = \sqrt{2M/r}$, and light (and everything else) moves through the flowing space by the rules of special relativity. Measured in the time of observers falling with the flow, light moves at

<Formula label="Light in the river" tex="\frac{dr}{dt_{\text{ff}}} = -\beta_r \pm 1 = -\sqrt{\frac{2M}{r}} \pm 1" cite="mit" at="slides 17–23 (plots; the formula is the page's own)" />

Outside the horizon, outward light ($+$) gains ground; at $r = 2M$ the river flows at the speed of light, and outward light stands still; inside, even outward light is swept in. Slides 17–23 plot this: free-fall time against $r/M$, with a vertical line at the horizon, $r = 2M$. The light rays follow the formula above: outside the horizon, outgoing rays escape; inside, every ray falls to the centre. Other curves show observers falling freely from rest far away, and (dotted, on the last plots) lines of constant Schwarzschild time $t$, which crowd against the horizon: this is why $t$ breaks down there (the identification of these curves is the page's own, from recomputing them).

The river model explains (slide 24): the event horizon and the interior, where nothing singular happens at the horizon itself (like swimming past the point above Niagara Falls where the current outruns you); tidal forces, as the flow speeds up towards the centre; why the coordinate $t$ breaks down at the horizon; and why there is an "excess radius" near the horizon. How would you die (slide 25)? The worked example answers that.

<WorkedExample title="Horizons, clocks and tides">
<Problem>
(a) How large would the Earth's Schwarzschild radius be ($M = 5.972 \times 10^{24}$ kg)? (b) How much radial distance lies between $r = 3M$ and $r = 4M$ (with $c = G = 1$), compared with the difference $1M$ in $r$? (c) A 2 m tall astronaut falls feet first. The tidal stretching across the body is about $2GM\ell/r^3$. Compare it at the horizon of a 10-solar-mass black hole ($GM = 1.327 \times 10^{21}$ m³/s²) and of a supermassive black hole of 4 million solar masses.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The Earth">
$2GM/c^2 = 2 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.972 \times 10^{24}/(2.998 \times 10^8)^2 \approx 8.9$ mm: the Earth would have to be squeezed to the size of a marble.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Excess radius">
$\int_{3M}^{4M}dr/\sqrt{1 - 2M/r} \approx 1.54M$: more than half as much again as the change in $r$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) How you would die">
For 10 solar masses, $r = 2GM/c^2 \approx 29.5$ km, and the stretching is $2 \times 1.327 \times 10^{21} \times 2/(2.95 \times 10^4)^3 \approx 2 \times 10^8$ m/s², twenty million times Earth's gravity: you would be torn apart ("spaghettified") before reaching the horizon. For 4 million solar masses, $r \approx 1.18 \times 10^{10}$ m and the stretching is only about $1.3 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s²: you would cross the horizon without feeling anything special, and the tides would kill you only deep inside.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The Schwarzschild metric describes spacetime outside a spherical mass; $t$ is far-away time, $r$ is circumference over $2\pi$, and shell observers see slow clocks and stretched radial distances.
- A black hole is an object inside its own event horizon, $r = 2GM/c^2$, with only mass, angular momentum and charge.
- In the river model, space flows inward at the escape speed, faster than light inside the horizon; nothing special happens at the horizon for a falling observer, but tides grow towards the centre.
