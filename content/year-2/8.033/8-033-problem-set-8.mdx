## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Geodesics in the expanding universe" points={12} mit="Problem 1">

For flat space ($k = 0$) the FRW metric of the lesson can be written in Cartesian coordinates. Dividing by $dt^2$ shows how fast a moving clock ticks:

<Formula label="Flat FRW metric" tex="d\tau^2 = dt^2 - a(t)^2\left(dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2\right) = \left[1 - a(t)^2\,|\dot{\vec r}|^2\right]dt^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Here $\vec r = (x, y, z)$ is the **comoving position** and $\dot{\vec r} = d\vec r/dt$. The scale factor $a(t)$ is some given positive function of time.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Two events have the same comoving position: $(t, \vec r) = (t_0, \vec r_0)$ and $(t_1, \vec r_0)$, with $t_1 > t_0$. Find the geodesic between them, which is the path of longest proper time (maximal aging). You do not need the calculus of variations: a short argument with an inequality is enough.

<Hint>

Write the proper time of any path $\vec r(t)$ between the two events as an integral over $t$ of the square root of the bracket in the metric.

</Hint>

<Hint>

How large can the square root be at any moment? For which motion does it have that largest value at every moment, and is that motion allowed by the two end points?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-path" choices={["The clock stays at the comoving position r₀ the whole time", "The clock moves out and back at a constant coordinate speed", "The clock moves out and back, and moves most while a(t) is smallest", "Every path between the two events gives the same proper time"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In this metric any motion in $\vec r$ only ever takes proper time away. The path with no motion at all therefore wins, and it happens to connect the two events.

<Step title="The proper time of any path">

<Formula label="Aging along a path" tex="\Delta\tau = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\sqrt{1 - a(t)^2\,|\dot{\vec r}|^2}\;dt \;\le\; \int_{t_0}^{t_1} dt" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

The inequality holds because $a^2|\dot{\vec r}|^2 \ge 0$, so the square root is at most 1 at every moment. It equals 1 at every moment only if $\dot{\vec r} = 0$ throughout.

</Step>

<Step title="The geodesic">

The path $\vec r(t) = \vec r_0$ has $\dot{\vec r} = 0$ throughout, and it starts and ends at the right events. No other path reaches the upper limit, because a path that moves at all has a square root below 1 for some stretch of time. So the geodesic is the clock that stays at $\vec r_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $a(t) = 1$ the metric is the Minkowski metric, and the result is the familiar one of the twin paradox: between two events at the same place, the clock that stays at rest ages most. The function $a(t)$ plays no part in the argument, so the result holds for every expansion history.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

How much time passes on a clock that follows this geodesic between the two events? Give a formula in $t_0$ and $t_1$.

<Hint>

Put $\dot{\vec r} = 0$ in the integral for the proper time.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-time" label="$\Delta\tau =$" expr="t1 - t0" vars={{ t0: [1, 5], t1: [6, 12] }} names={{ t0: "t_0", t1: "t_1" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "t0 - t1", hint: "The sign is the wrong way round: the proper time is positive, later minus earlier." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With no motion in $\vec r$, the metric says $d\tau = dt$.

<Step title="Integrate">

<Formula label="Aging of a comoving clock" tex="\Delta\tau = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} dt = t_1 - t_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This gives the coordinate $t$ its meaning: $t$ is the time shown by clocks that stay at a fixed comoving position. It is often called cosmic time. The result has the unit of time and does not depend on $a(t)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

An object is called **comoving** if $\dot{\vec r} = 0$. A galaxy in this spacetime is comoving early on, and no force other than gravity acts on it. Use part (a) to describe its later motion in one sentence, then choose.

<Hint>

A free body follows a geodesic. Part (a) holds for any two times $t_0$ and $t_1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-future" choices={["It slowly drifts back towards the origin of the coordinates", "It stays at the same comoving position for ever", "It keeps the same proper distance to every other galaxy", "It picks up comoving speed as the expansion carries it along"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Free bodies follow geodesics, and part (a) found that staying at a fixed $\vec r$ is a geodesic between any two of its events.

<Step title="One sentence">

A comoving galaxy stays comoving: its world line $\vec r = \vec r_0$ is a geodesic, so it keeps its comoving position for ever.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This does not mean that distances stay the same. Problem 2 shows that the proper distance between two comoving galaxies grows with $a(t)$. The comoving coordinates are the ones in which freely falling galaxies stand still, which is why the lesson calls them labels attached to galaxies.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Cosmic expansion" points={12} mit="Problem 2">

Two comoving galaxies sit in the flat FRW universe of Problem 1: one at $\vec r = (0, 0, 0)$ and one at $\vec r = (x_0, 0, 0)$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the distance $\sigma$ between them at the fixed time $t = t_0$. This is the length of the spacelike geodesic from event $A$, $(x, y, z, t) = (0, 0, 0, t_0)$, to event $B$, $(x_0, 0, 0, t_0)$. You may use without proof that this geodesic is the straight line $(x, 0, 0, t_0)$ with $x$ running from 0 to $x_0$. The proper space interval $d\sigma$ is defined by

<Formula label="Proper distance" tex="d\sigma^2 = -d\tau^2, \qquad \sigma = \int d\sigma = \int_0^{x_0}\frac{d\sigma}{dx}\,dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1" />

Give $\sigma$ as a formula in $a(t_0)$ and $x_0$.

<Hint>

Along the line, which of $dt$, $dx$, $dy$, $dz$ are zero? Put that into the metric of Problem 1.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You get $d\sigma^2$ as a multiple of $dx^2$. The multiple is constant along the line, because the whole line is at the one time $t_0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-dist" label="$\sigma =$" expr="a*x0" vars={{ a: [0.2, 3], x0: [1, 10] }} names={{ a: "a(t_0)", x0: "x_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x0", hint: "That is the comoving (coordinate) distance. The metric multiplies dx by the scale factor to give a real distance." },
    { expr: "a^2*x0", hint: "The metric gives dσ² = a² dx². Take the square root before you integrate." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the line only $x$ changes, so the metric reduces to one term.

<Step title="The metric on the line">

With $dt = dy = dz = 0$, the metric gives $d\tau^2 = -a(t_0)^2\,dx^2$, so $d\sigma = a(t_0)\,dx$.

</Step>

<Step title="Integrate">

<Formula label="Distance between two comoving galaxies" tex="\sigma = \int_0^{x_0} a(t_0)\,dx = a(t_0)\,x_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $a = 1$ this is the ordinary distance $x_0$ of flat space. The comoving distance $x_0$ never changes, and the real distance is that fixed number times the scale factor: this is what "the scale factor" means.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Give the ratio $\sigma(t_1)/\sigma(t_2)$ of this separation at two different times, as a formula in $a(t_1)$ and $a(t_2)$.

<Hint>

Use part (a) twice. What is the same at both times?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ratio" label="$\sigma(t_1)/\sigma(t_2) =$" expr="a1/a2" vars={{ a1: [0.2, 3], a2: [4, 9] }} names={{ a1: "a(t_1)", a2: "a(t_2)" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "a2/a1", hint: "That is the ratio the other way up: σ(t₂)/σ(t₁)." },
    { expr: "(a1/a2)^2", hint: "Distances grow with the first power of the scale factor. Areas grow with the square." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The comoving separation $x_0$ is the same at both times and cancels.

<Step title="Divide">

<Formula label="All distances grow by the same factor" tex="\frac{\sigma(t_1)}{\sigma(t_2)} = \frac{a(t_1)\,x_0}{a(t_2)\,x_0} = \frac{a(t_1)}{a(t_2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ratio does not contain $x_0$, and the origin was not special: the same steps work for any pair of comoving galaxies (shift and turn the coordinates so that one galaxy is at the origin and the other on the $x$-axis). So when the universe expands, all distances between comoving galaxies grow by the same factor.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

The Hubble parameter is the recession speed divided by the distance:

<Formula label="Hubble parameter from the separation" tex="H \equiv \frac{\dot\sigma}{\sigma}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2" />

Use part (a), now at a general time $t$, to work out $H$. Then choose.

<Hint>

In $\sigma(t) = a(t)\,x_0$, which factor depends on time?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-hubble" choices={["ȧ/a", "ä/ȧ", "2ȧ/a", "1/t for every a(t)"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only the scale factor changes with time, so the speed is $\dot a$ times the fixed $x_0$.

<Step title="Differentiate and divide">

<Formula label="Hubble parameter" tex="\dot\sigma = \dot a\,x_0, \qquad H = \frac{\dot\sigma}{\sigma} = \frac{\dot a\,x_0}{a\,x_0} = \frac{\dot a}{a}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is the definition $H \equiv \dot a/a$ of the lesson (Lecture 18, slide 7), now derived from the metric. It also gives Hubble's law: $\dot\sigma = H\sigma$, a recession speed proportional to the distance, with the same $H$ for every pair of galaxies. The unit of $H$ is 1 over time. ($H = 1/t$ holds only for $a \propto t$, the empty universe of Problem 4(b).)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

MIT's Problem 3 has two halves, I and II, with their own points (15 and 18). They are two exercises here.

<Exercise id="p3-1" title="Deriving the Friedmann equation, part I: escape speed" points={15} mit="Problem 3, part I">

The rigorous way to the Friedmann equation is general relativity. Classical mechanics gives exactly the same equation, and that is what you will show. Use classical mechanics throughout this problem, not special relativity.

A large mass $M$ is at rest. A very small mass $m$ is at the distance $a$ from it and moves straight away from it at the speed $v$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Make a drawing. Write down the total energy $E$ of the system: the kinetic energy plus the gravitational potential energy.

<Hint>

The potential energy of two masses is zero when they are infinitely far apart and negative when they are closer.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-energy" label="$E =$" expr="m*v^2/2 - G*M*m/a" vars={{ m: [0.5, 2], v: [1, 5], G: [0.5, 2], M: [5, 20], a: [1, 4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*v^2/2 + G*M*m/a", hint: "The gravitational potential energy is negative: it takes work to pull the masses apart." },
    { expr: "m*v^2/2 - G*M*m/a^2", hint: "G M m/a² is the force. The potential energy falls off as 1/a." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only $m$ moves, so the kinetic energy is that of $m$. The potential energy is Newton's.

<Step title="The drawing">

Draw $M$ as a large dot, $m$ as a small dot at the distance $a$, and an arrow of length $v$ on $m$ that points away from $M$.

</Step>

<Step title="The energy">

<Formula label="Total energy" tex="E = \frac12 mv^2 - \frac{GMm}{a}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 I(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both terms have the unit of energy: $GMm/a$ is a force $GMm/a^2$ times a distance. Far away ($a \to \infty$) only the kinetic energy is left.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find the escape speed: the smallest speed $v$ at the distance $a$ that lets $m$ get infinitely far away in the end.

<Hint>

At infinity the potential energy is zero. With the smallest speed that still escapes, the mass arrives there with no speed left. What is $E$ then?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-escape" label="$v =$" expr="sqrt(2*G*M/a)" vars={{ G: [0.5, 2], M: [5, 20], a: [1, 4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(G*M/a)", hint: "That is the speed of a circular orbit at the distance a. For escape, set the total energy to zero." },
    { expr: "2*G*M/a", hint: "That is v². Take the square root." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Energy is conserved, and the mass that just escapes has $E = 0$.

<Step title="Set E to zero">

<Formula label="Escape speed" tex="\frac12 mv^2 = \frac{GMm}{a} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{a}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 I(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$GM/a$ has the unit m²/s², so the root is a speed. The escape speed does not depend on $m$, and it is smaller far away. It is $\sqrt2$ times the speed of a circular orbit at the same distance.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

For the special case $E = 0$, find the distance $a$ as a function of the time $t$. Assume $m \ll M$, so that the large mass stays at rest. Take $t = 0$ as the time when $a = 0$.

<Hint>

With $E = 0$ the speed at every distance is the escape speed of that distance: $da/dt = \sqrt{2GM/a}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Either bring all the $a$ to one side and integrate, or try $a(t) = At^B$ and find the constants $A$ and $B$ that make both sides equal at all times.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a-of-t" label="$a(t) =$" expr="(9*G*M/2)^(1/3)*t^(2/3)" vars={{ G: [0.5, 2], M: [5, 20], t: [0.5, 4] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(9*G*M/2)^(1/3)*t^(3/2)", hint: "The exponent of t is upside down: a^(3/2) is proportional to t." },
    { expr: "(3*G*M)^(1/3)*t^(2/3)", hint: "The power of t is right. Check the constant: integrating √a da gives (2/3)a^(3/2), and the right side is √(2GM) t." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2*G*M)*t", hint: "The speed is not constant: it falls as the distance grows." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $E = 0$ turns energy conservation into a first-order differential equation that separates.

<Step title="Separate and integrate">

<Formula label="Distance against time for E = 0" tex="\sqrt a\,da = \sqrt{2GM}\,dt \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac23 a^{3/2} = \sqrt{2GM}\,t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a(t) = \left(\frac{9GM}{2}\right)^{1/3}t^{2/3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 I(c) (our own solution)" />

The constant of integration is zero because $a = 0$ at $t = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With the trial form $a = At^B$: the left side $da/dt = ABt^{B-1}$ and the right side $\sqrt{2GM/A}\,t^{-B/2}$ have the same power of $t$ only for $B - 1 = -B/2$, so $B = \frac23$. Then $\frac23 A = \sqrt{2GM/A}$ gives $A^3 = \frac92 GM$, as found. Units: $GM$ is m³/s², so $(GM)^{1/3}t^{2/3}$ is a length.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

The separation is now $a_0$. Use part (c) to find how long ago the two masses were at the same place.

<Hint>

Set $a(t) = a_0$ in the result of part (c) and solve for $t$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-age" label="$t =$" expr="sqrt(2*a0^3/(9*G*M))" vars={{ a0: [1, 4], G: [0.5, 2], M: [5, 20] }} names={{ a0: "a_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(a0^3/(2*G*M))", hint: "That is a₀ divided by the present speed: the time it would take at today's speed. The mass moved faster earlier, so the real time is shorter." },
    { expr: "(2*a0^3/(9*G*M))^(1/3)", hint: "Solve a₀³ = (9GM/2) t² for t: that needs a square root, not a cube root." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Invert the result of part (c).

<Step title="Solve for the time">

<Formula label="Time since the masses coincided" tex="a_0^3 = \frac{9GM}{2}\,t^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad t = \sqrt{\frac{2a_0^3}{9GM}} = \frac23\,\frac{a_0}{\sqrt{2GM/a_0}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 I(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The last form says: the time is $\frac23$ of the present distance divided by the present speed. It is less than distance over speed because the mass was faster in the past. This factor $\frac23$ returns in part II as the age of a universe of matter.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

Describe in words what happens in the end in the two cases $E < 0$ and $E > 0$.

<Hint>

Use the energy of part (a). Can the kinetic energy become zero at some distance? What is left of it at infinity?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The kinetic energy $E + GMm/a$ can never be negative.

<Step title="E below zero">

For $E < 0$ the kinetic energy reaches zero at the distance $a_{\max} = GMm/|E|$. The mass stops there, turns round and falls back onto $M$.

</Step>

<Step title="E above zero">

For $E > 0$ the kinetic energy never reaches zero. The mass escapes, and far away it still has the speed $\sqrt{2E/m}$: it moves away for ever at a speed that settles to a constant.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$E = 0$ is the border between the two: the mass escapes, but its speed goes to zero. Units: $GMm/|E|$ is a length and $\sqrt{2E/m}$ is a speed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

<Exercise id="p3-2" title="Deriving the Friedmann equation, part II: the universe" points={18} mit="Problem 3, part II">

Part I already describes the expanding universe. It only needs to be read differently.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Problem 2 showed that a comoving object moves away from the origin at a distance $\sigma(t) \propto a(t)$ and at the speed $v = H\sigma$. So no matter overtakes other matter, and the mass inside an expanding sphere of radius $a(t)$ stays the same. If the universe is filled evenly with comoving matter of density $\rho(t)$, this constant mass is

<Formula label="Mass inside the sphere" tex="M = \frac{4\pi}{3}\rho a^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 II(a), p. 2" />

Follow a small blob of matter, of mass $m$, at the edge of this sphere. Make a drawing. The matter is spread with spherical symmetry about the origin, so the blob feels the same force as from a single mass $M$ at the origin, and the matter outside the sphere has no effect. (This is a theorem of Newton. It holds in general relativity too, where it is known as Birkhoff's theorem.)

Use this to remove $M$ from your energy of part I(a): write the kinetic plus gravitational energy $E$ of the blob in terms of $\rho$ and $a$.

<Hint>

Replace $M$ in $-GMm/a$ by the mass of the sphere.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-blob" label="$E =$" expr="m*v^2/2 - 4*pi*G*rho*m*a^2/3" vars={{ m: [0.5, 2], v: [1, 5], G: [0.5, 2], rho: [0.5, 3], a: [1.5, 4] }} names={{ rho: "\\rho" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*v^2/2 - 4*pi*G*rho*m*a^3/3", hint: "You put in the mass of the sphere but lost the 1/a of the potential energy: a³/a = a²." },
    { expr: "m*v^2/2 + 4*pi*G*rho*m*a^2/3", hint: "The potential energy stays negative: gravity holds the blob back." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The blob at the edge of the sphere is the small mass of part I, and the sphere is the large mass.

<Step title="The drawing">

Draw a sphere of radius $a$ filled evenly with dots, a blob $m$ on its surface, and an arrow $v$ on the blob that points straight outwards.

</Step>

<Step title="The energy">

<Formula label="Energy of the blob" tex="E = \frac12 mv^2 - \frac{Gm}{a}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}\rho a^3 = \frac12 mv^2 - \frac{4\pi}{3}G\rho\,m\,a^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 II(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$G\rho$ has the unit 1/s², so $G\rho ma^2$ is a mass times a speed squared: an energy. $E$ is constant in time, because $M$ is constant, even though $\rho$ and $a$ both change.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

    { expr: "t*(1 + r^2)", hint: "T² − R² must equal t², the square of the proper time since the start. Check that with your T and R." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In flat spacetime, a galaxy that leaves the origin event at the constant velocity $v$ is at $R = vT$, and its clock shows the proper time $t = T/\gamma$.

<Step title="The radius">

The angular parts agree if $R^2 = t^2r^2$:

<Formula label="The new radius" tex="R = t\,r" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 10 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The time">

For a galaxy on a straight world line from the origin event, the proper time obeys $t^2 = T^2 - R^2$. So $T^2 = t^2 + t^2r^2$:

<Formula label="The new time" tex="T = t\sqrt{1 + r^2}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 10 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $r = 0$, $T = t$. A galaxy with the label $r$ has the constant velocity $R/T = r/\sqrt{1 + r^2}$, which is below 1 (the speed of light) for every $r$, and $\gamma = \sqrt{1 + r^2}$, so $T = \gamma t$: time dilation. Part (b) checks the whole metric.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Show that with these $T$ and $R$ the Minkowski metric turns into the FRW metric of the empty universe.

<Hint>

Work out $dT$ and $dR$ in terms of $dt$ and $dr$, and form $dT^2 - dR^2$. The mixed terms with $dt\,dr$ must cancel.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Substitute and collect terms.

<Step title="The differentials">

<Formula label="Differentials of the new coordinates" tex="dT = \sqrt{1 + r^2}\,dt + \frac{t\,r}{\sqrt{1 + r^2}}\,dr, \qquad dR = r\,dt + t\,dr" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 10 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The difference of the squares">

<Formula label="Time and radial parts" tex="dT^2 - dR^2 = \left(1 + r^2 - r^2\right)dt^2 + \left(2tr - 2tr\right)dt\,dr + t^2\left(\frac{r^2}{1 + r^2} - 1\right)dr^2 = dt^2 - \frac{t^2\,dr^2}{1 + r^2}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 10 (our own solution)" />

Together with $R^2 = t^2r^2$ in the angular part, this is the FRW metric of the empty universe.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

So the empty expanding universe is flat spacetime, described with the clocks and labels of a cloud of particles that fly apart from one event. Its space, the surface of constant $t$, is the surface $T^2 - R^2 = t^2$ in the Minkowski diagram: a hyperbola, which is why the space has negative curvature although the spacetime is flat. The coordinates cover only the inside of the future light cone of the origin event, $T > R$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 11 (optional)

<Exercise id="p11" title="A balloon world" points={6} mit="Optional Problem 11" optional>

This problem is optional at MIT and here: it does not count towards the score of the set. MIT's sheet quotes its Optional Problem 11 from a textbook that this site does not use. The physics here is the same (an expanding surface with flat-minded creatures on it obeys Hubble's law), and the situation and the numbers are our own.

A round balloon is being blown up so that its radius grows steadily: $R(t) = R_0 + ut$ with $R_0 = 6$ cm and $u = 0.5$ cm/s. On a great circle of the balloon (a circle whose centre is the centre of the balloon, like the equator) 24 ink dots are drawn, evenly spaced. Tiny mites live on the rubber. They can only crawl and look along the surface, and they know nothing of the third dimension or of the centre of the balloon. All distances below are measured along the surface, at the time $t = 8$ s.

<Part label="a" points={1}>

What is the distance $s$ between two neighbouring dots at $t = 8$ s, in cm?

<Hint>

Seen from the centre of the balloon, two neighbours are a fixed angle apart. An arc is the radius times the angle in radians.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-s" label="$s =$" value="2.618" unit="cm" tolerance="1%" calc="p11-s" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The dots keep their angles as the balloon grows.

<Step title="Arc length">

The radius is $R = 6 + 0.5 \times 8 = 10$ cm, and the angle between neighbours is $\theta = 2\pi/24 = 0.2618$ rad. So $s = R\theta = 2.618$ cm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

All 24 gaps together are the whole circle: $24 \times 2.618 = 62.83$ cm $= 2\pi \times 10$ cm.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

At what speed $v$ does a dot move away from its neighbour at that time, in cm/s? What is the ratio $H = v/s$, in 1/s?

<Hint>

In $s = R(t)\,\theta$ only $R$ changes with time.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-v" label="$v =$" value="0.1309" unit="cm/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p11-v" />

<Answer id="p11-h" label="$H =$" value="0.05" unit="1/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p11-h" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The speed is the rate of change of the arc.

<Step title="Speed and ratio">

$v = \dot s = \dot R\,\theta = 0.5 \times 0.2618 = 0.1309$ cm/s, and $H = v/s = 0.1309/2.618 = 0.05$ per second.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$H = \dot R/R = 0.5/10 = 0.05$ per second: the angle has dropped out.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={1}>

Another dot is a quarter of the circle away from the first one. At what speed does it move away from the first dot at $t = 8$ s, in cm/s?

<Hint>

Its angle is six times that of the neighbour.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-vfar" label="$v =$" value="0.785" unit="cm/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p11-vfar" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same steps with a larger angle.

<Step title="Speed">

$\theta = \pi/2$, so $s = 10 \times \pi/2 = 15.71$ cm and $v = 0.5 \times \pi/2 = 0.785$ cm/s.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$v/s = 0.785/15.71 = 0.05$ per second, the same $H$ as for the neighbour: six times as far, six times as fast.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Now in general. Two dots are separated by the angle $\theta$ as seen from the centre of the balloon, and the radius is any function $R(t)$. Show that the mites find Hubble's law, $v = H(t)\,s$, with an $H$ that depends only on the history of the radius and not on $\theta$. Why does it matter that $\theta$ drops out?

<Hint>

Write $s$ and $\dot s$ for a fixed $\theta$ and divide.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A fixed angle times a growing radius.

<Step title="Hubble's law on the balloon">

<Formula label="Hubble's law for the mites" tex="s = R(t)\,\theta, \qquad v = \dot s = \dot R\,\theta = \frac{\dot R}{R}\,s = H(t)\,s, \qquad H(t) = \frac{\dot R(t)}{R(t)}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 11 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Why it matters">

The mites cannot measure $\theta$: it is an angle at a centre that is outside their world. They can measure distances and speeds along the surface, and the law that connects these does not contain $\theta$. Every mite finds the same law with the same $H$, whichever dot it lives on, so no dot is the centre of the expansion.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

It is Problem 2 again: $R(t)$ plays the part of the scale factor $a(t)$, $\theta$ that of the comoving distance $x_0$, and $H = \dot R/R$ that of $H = \dot a/a$. For this balloon $H = u/(R_0 + ut)$ falls with time, and $1/H = 20$ s at $t = 8$ s is the time the balloon would have needed to grow from nothing at its present rate.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 12 (optional)

<Exercise id="p12" title="Vacuum energy against matter: the map of universes" points={6} mit="Optional Problem 12" optional>

This problem is optional at MIT and here: it does not count towards the score of the set.

MIT's sheet shows a figure from the research literature, which is not reproduced here. It is a map: $\Omega_m$ runs along the horizontal axis (from 0 to 2.5) and $\Omega_\Lambda$ along the vertical axis (from $-1$ to 3), so each point is one universe (with photons neglected, and $\Omega_k = 1 - \Omega_m - \Omega_\Lambda$). On the map lie the regions allowed by the supernova measurements of two teams, and three curves that each split the map in two:

1. universes that expand for ever, against universes that collapse again;
2. universes whose expansion accelerates today, against those whose expansion decelerates today;
3. universes that began with a big bang, against universes with no big bang.

MIT asks you either to explain these curves in words or to compute them. All three follow from the function

<Formula label="Expansion speed squared, with curvature" tex="\frac{\dot a^2}{H_0^2} = f(a) = \frac{\Omega_m}{a} + \left(1 - \Omega_m - \Omega_\Lambda\right) + \Omega_\Lambda a^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3 (the formula for H(a), times a², with the photons left out)" />

which is 1 today ($a = 1$) and can never be negative.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Find the curve between acceleration and deceleration today: give $\Omega_\Lambda$ on that curve as a formula in $\Omega_m$.

<Hint>

The expansion accelerates today if $\dot a^2$ is growing at $a = 1$. See Optional Problem 7.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p12-accel" label="$\Omega_\Lambda =$" expr="m/2" vars={{ m: [0.2, 2.5] }} names={{ m: "\\Omega_m" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m", hint: "Equal densities are not the border. In the slope of f at a = 1, the vacuum term counts twice: −Ω_m + 2Ω_Λ." },
    { expr: "2*m", hint: "The factor 2 is on the wrong side: the slope of f at a = 1 is −Ω_m + 2Ω_Λ." },
    { expr: "1 - m", hint: "That is the line of the flat universes (Ω_k = 0). Acceleration is about the slope of f at a = 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $\dot a$ increases today if $f$ has a positive slope at $a = 1$.

<Step title="The slope today">

<Formula label="Border between acceleration and deceleration" tex="\left.\frac{df}{da}\right|_{a=1} = -\Omega_m + 2\Omega_\Lambda = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Omega_\Lambda = \frac{\Omega_m}{2}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 12 (our own solution)" />

Above this straight line through the origin the expansion accelerates today, and below it the expansion decelerates.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The favoured universe, $\Omega_m = 0.3$ and $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.7$, lies above the line ($0.7 > 0.15$): it accelerates, as Optional Problem 7 found. A universe without vacuum energy always decelerates.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Explain the curve between expanding for ever and collapsing again. Then compute one point of it (the number is our own addition): for a universe with twice the critical density of matter (the point 2 on the horizontal axis), what is the smallest $\Omega_\Lambda$ with which it still expands for ever?

<Hint>

The expansion stops if $\dot a$ reaches zero, so if $f(a)$ comes down to zero at some $a > 1$. On the border, $f$ just touches zero: there is an $a = s$ with $f(s) = 0$ and also $df/da = 0$ there.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The condition on the slope gives $\Omega_\Lambda$ in terms of $\Omega_m$ and $s$. Put that into $f(s) = 0$. You get an equation for $s$ alone, to be solved numerically (look for the root above 1).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p12-forever" label="$\Omega_\Lambda =$" value="0.0419" tolerance="2%" calc="p12-forever" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A universe collapses again if its expansion speed can fall to zero in the future.

<Step title="In words">

For $\Omega_\Lambda < 0$ the last term of $f$ becomes as negative as you like for large $a$, so $f$ reaches zero: every such universe collapses. For $\Omega_\Lambda \ge 0$ and $\Omega_m \le 1$, write $f = 1 + \Omega_m(1/a - 1) + \Omega_\Lambda(a^2 - 1)$: for $a > 1$ this is more than $1 - \Omega_m \ge 0$, so $f$ never reaches zero and the universe expands for ever. So for $\Omega_m \le 1$ the border is the horizontal axis, $\Omega_\Lambda = 0$. For $\Omega_m > 1$ the matter alone would bring the universe to a stop (Problem 8), and a positive vacuum energy must be large enough to take over before that happens: there the border bends upwards.

</Step>

<Step title="The border for more than the critical density">

On the border $f$ touches zero at some $a = s > 1$. The slope condition $-\Omega_m/s^2 + 2\Omega_\Lambda s = 0$ gives $\Omega_\Lambda = \Omega_m/(2s^3)$. Putting that into $f(s) = 0$ and solving for $\Omega_m$:

<Formula label="Border curve, with the touching point s as parameter" tex="\Omega_m = \frac{2s^3}{\left(s - 1\right)^2\left(2s + 1\right)}, \qquad \Omega_\Lambda = \frac{1}{\left(s - 1\right)^2\left(2s + 1\right)}" cite="mit" at="Optional Problem 12 (our own solution)" />

(The steps: $f(s) = \frac32\Omega_m/s + 1 - \Omega_m - \Omega_m/(2s^3) = 0$, and $2s^3 - 3s^2 + 1 = (s - 1)^2(2s + 1)$.)

</Step>

<Step title="The point asked for">

With 2 on the left of the first formula: $s^3 = (s - 1)^2(2s + 1) = 2s^3 - 3s^2 + 1$, so $s^3 - 3s^2 + 1 = 0$. The root above 1 is $s = 2.879$, and the vacuum parameter is $1/s^3 = 0.0419$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Without vacuum energy this universe would stop at $a = 2$ (Problem 8). With a vacuum parameter of 0.0419 it almost stops at $a = 2.879$, hangs there, and then expands for ever. For $s \to \infty$ the curve runs into the point $\Omega_m = 1$, $\Omega_\Lambda = 0$, where it joins the horizontal axis.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Explain the curve between "big bang" and "no big bang". Then open the solution and compare with the map drawn there.

<Hint>

Follow the universe back in time, to $a < 1$. What happens if $f(a)$ comes down to zero at some $a$ between 0 and 1?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** If $f$ reaches zero at some $a$ below 1, the universe was never smaller than that: it has no big bang.

<Step title="In words">

Going back in time, $a$ shrinks. With matter present, $f$ grows without limit for $a \to 0$, and $f = 1$ today. If $f$ dips to zero in between, at $a = s < 1$, the universe cannot have come from $a = 0$: it shrank from a very large size, stopped at $a = s$ and bounced back, and it is expanding now after the bounce. That needs a deep dip, so a large vacuum energy. On the border $f$ just touches zero at $s < 1$. The algebra is the same as in part (b), so the border is the same pair of formulas, now with $0 < s < 1$. It starts at the point $(0, 1)$ of the map (for $s \to 0$) and rises to the right.

</Step>

<Step title="The map">

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 2.5]} range={[-1, 3]} xLabel="Ω_m" yLabel="Ω_Λ"
  curves={[
    { expr: "x/2" },
    { expr: "1 - x", dash: true },
    { expr: "x <= 1 ? 0 : NaN" },
    { x: "2*s^3/((s - 1)^2*(2*s + 1))", y: "1/((s - 1)^2*(2*s + 1))", range: [2.37, 60] },
    { x: "2*s^3/((s - 1)^2*(2*s + 1))", y: "1/((s - 1)^2*(2*s + 1))", range: [0.001, 0.612] }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 0.3, y: 0.7, label: "favoured" }]}
  caption="The map of universes, computed from the formulas of this problem (it is not MIT's figure and shows no measurements). The curve that runs along the horizontal axis up to Ω_m = 1 and then bends slowly upwards separates expanding for ever (above) from collapsing again (below). The steep curve that starts at Ω_Λ = 1 on the vertical axis separates no big bang (above and to the left) from a big bang (below). The dashed line holds the flat universes, Ω_m + Ω_Λ = 1: closed universes lie above it and open ones below. The point marks Ω_m = 0.3, Ω_Λ = 0.7." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A number: for $\Omega_m = 0.3$ the border lies at $s = 0.444$, where the vacuum parameter is 1.713. The favoured universe, with $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.7$, is far below it and did have a big bang. The dip of $f$ lies at $s^3 = \Omega_m/(2\Omega_\Lambda)$, and $s < 1$ means $\Omega_\Lambda > \Omega_m/2$: every universe without a big bang lies above the line of part (a) and accelerates today, as the map shows.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
