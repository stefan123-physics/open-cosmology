## The particles

After a recap of atomic and nuclear physics and of the photon 4-momentum $P = \hbar K$ (slides 2–3, on the [last page](/courses/8-033/atoms-photons-nuclei)), the slides return to CERN and Fermilab and to the radius of curvature of particle tracks (slides 6–8; the answer, $r = p/(qB)$, is on the [page for Lecture 12](/courses/8-033/force-acceleration-interstellar-travel)). Two charts sort the particles (slides 9–10): matter particles, the leptons (electron, muon, tau and their neutrinos) and the quarks (up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom); force carriers, the gluons, photon, W and Z bosons and the graviton; and for each particle an antiparticle.

Slide 11 lists the **rest energies of common particles**: electron 0.511 MeV, muon 105.6 MeV, tau 1777 MeV, proton 938.26 MeV, neutron 939.55 MeV, charged pion 139.6 MeV, neutral pion 135.0 MeV, the neutrinos less than 0.14 eV, photon and graviton 0, and one twelfth of a carbon-12 atom (the atomic mass unit) 931.5 MeV. Why these values? Why is the proton 1836 times heavier than the electron? Nobody knows (slide 11).

The Standard Model Lagrangian, which fills a whole slide in tiny print (slide 12), contains about two dozen free parameters, and cosmology adds a few more; tables list them all, measured in units where $c = \hbar = G = k_B = \left|q_e\right| = 1$ (slides 13–16). For each one we can ask: how do we measure it, and why does it have that value (slide 14)?

## Which processes happen?

There are four fundamental interactions, gravitational, electromagnetic, weak and strong; in particle physics gravity is negligible (slide 17). The processes considered in the course are absorption (two particles in, one out), emission or decay (one in, two out) and collision, scattering, annihilation or creation (two in, two out). (Quantum field theory reduces a two-in, two-out process to two simpler ones with an intermediate particle: two electrons scatter by exchanging a photon, slide 17.)

Which processes are allowed? All that are not forbidden by a conservation law (slide 18):

- energy-momentum conservation ($P$ is conserved);
- charge conservation;
- baryon number conservation;
- lepton number conservation;
- parity conservation, except in weak interactions.

All of this is provisional (slide 19). In beta decay, momentum seemed not to be conserved (energy too, though the slide mentions momentum) until Pauli proposed the neutrino in 1931; Reines and Cowan detected it in 1956. Parity was thought to hold everywhere until the shock of 1956 (Yang, Lee, Wu). Many physicists suspect that lepton or baryon number is violated very slightly, so that protons decay, if one waits far longer than $10^{32}$ years. Computing lifetimes and probabilities needs quantum field theory; this course draws only what follows from energy-momentum conservation.

Processes come in three energy scales (slides 20 and 28): chemical reactions, where atoms are rearranged and photons or electrons emitted or absorbed, non-relativistically; nuclear reactions, where nucleons are rearranged, and electrons or positrons and neutrinos must take part whenever protons and neutrons convert, to conserve charge and lepton number; and elementary particle interactions, where particles are scattered, destroyed and created. Examples (slide 21): molecules reacting and emitting a photon; a photon exciting or ionizing an atom (the photoelectric effect); nuclear reactions; beta decay, $n \to p^+ + e^- + \bar\nu$; pair creation, $\gamma + \gamma \to e^- + e^+$, or by a photon passing a particle that takes up the recoil; and Compton scattering. Historically, before anyone knew what they were, helium nuclei, electrons and energetic photons were called α, β and γ rays, and the names stuck (slide 22).

## Emission, absorption and recoil

Energy-momentum conservation alone says a lot about photons (slide 23). A particle of mass $m_0$ at rest that **absorbs** a photon of frequency $\omega$ ($X + \gamma \to X^*$) must recoil with the photon's momentum, at speed

<Formula label="Recoil after absorption" tex="\beta' = \frac{\hbar\omega}{m_0c^2 + \hbar\omega}" cite="mit" at="slide 23" />

A particle that **emits** a photon ($X^* \to X + \gamma$), lowering its rest mass from $m_0$ to $m_0' = m_0 - Q_0/c^2$, gives the photon slightly less than $Q_0$:

<Formula label="Photon emission with recoil" tex="\hbar\omega = \left(1 - \frac{Q_0}{2m_0c^2}\right)Q_0" cite="mit" at="slide 23" />

because some of the released energy becomes kinetic energy of the recoiling particle. In reverse, to raise its rest energy by $Q_0$ a particle must absorb a photon with $\hbar\omega$ greater than $Q_0$ (slide 24). For atomic transitions (about 10 eV) the recoil term is only about $10^{-8}$, smaller than the Doppler broadening of the lines, $\beta \sim 10^{-6}$ for atoms moving at hundreds of metres per second. Nuclear transitions are about a thousand times more energetic, and there the recoil matters (Moon's experiment, 1951). The Mössbauer effect (1961 Nobel Prize, for PhD thesis work) all but removes the recoil: in a crystal, the whole crystal recoils, so $m_0$ is enormous, and the line becomes almost as sharp as its natural width: Doppler shifts from moving the source at a few centimetres per second (slide 24 says 2 cm/s) can be measured.

**The Harvard tower** (slide 25). Pound and Rebka used this in 1960 to measure the tiny gravitational redshift: over 22.6 m the shift is only about $5 \times 10^{-15}$ (comparing photons sent up and down), but the sharp 14.4 keV gamma line of iron-57, emitted and absorbed without recoil by the Mössbauer effect, resolved it (slides 24–25). The gravitational redshift returns in the general relativity lectures still to come.

**Compton scattering** (slides 26–27). A photon bouncing elastically off an electron at rest, $\gamma + e^- \to \gamma + e^-$, loses energy:

<Formula label="Compton scattering" tex="h\nu' = \frac{h\nu}{1 + \frac{h\nu}{m_ec^2}\left(1 - \cos\theta\right)}" cite="mit" at="slide 27" />

It is called Compton scattering when the photon gives energy to the electron, as when X-rays shine on matter, and inverse Compton scattering when a fast electron gives energy to the photon, which happens often in astrophysics (slide 26 shows the supernova remnant Cassiopeia A). The two are the same process seen from different frames: a Lorentz transformation can always make either particle the more energetic one (slide 27).

## The greatest unsolved problems

The slides' list of open questions (slide 30): Where do the "constants" come from, and why are there 3 + 1 dimensions? Is there a quantum-gravity theory of everything (M-theory? black hole evaporation?)? Do protons decay? Is there supersymmetry? A Higgs particle? What are the neutrinos' properties? What are dark energy and dark matter? Did inflation happen? (Later developments: the Higgs boson was found at the LHC in 2012, and proton-decay searches now require lifetimes above about $10^{34}$ years. Neutrino oscillations, already established before 2006, measure only differences of the squared neutrino masses; the masses themselves and their ordering are still open, as are the other questions.)

**Predicting parameters** (slides 31–36). Some measured numbers are *environmental*: the mass of the Earth or the size of its orbit are accidents of where we live, and no theory will predict them. Others, such as the electron's mass or the Bohr radius, look *fundamental* (slides 33–34). But the line moves (slide 35). Over history, things once taken as fundamental have turned out to be effective laws or initial conditions: with Kepler and Newton, that planets are spherical and orbits circular; in classical physics, the initial distribution of matter. Inflation together with a landscape of possible vacua might do the same for the Standard Model's symmetry, the dimensionality of space and the constants; and a "Level IV multiverse" might do it even for the equations of a theory of everything. Tegmark's four levels of multiverse (slide 36): (1) the same effective laws with different initial conditions, (2) the same fundamental laws with different effective laws, (3) nothing qualitatively new, and (4) different fundamental laws.

**Three little numbers** (slide 37). In units with $c = \hbar = G = k_B = \left|q_e\right| = 1$:

<Formula label="Three little numbers" tex="\alpha = \frac{q_e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c} \approx \frac{1}{137.03599976}, \qquad \beta = \frac{m_e}{m_p} \approx \frac{1}{1836.15}, \qquad m_p = \frac{m_p}{m_{\text{pl}}} \approx 7.68417 \times 10^{-20}" cite="mit" at="slide 37" />

(The slide uses $\beta$ for the electron-to-proton mass ratio, not for a speed.) Remarkably, these three fix the sizes and masses of everyday things (slides 38–44, after Weisskopf 1975 and Carr and Rees 1979): an atom has radius about $1/(m_p\alpha\beta)$, the Bohr radius, and mass about $m_p$; a star has mass about $1/m_p^2$; and similar combinations give asteroids, planets, mountains, galaxies and the known universe. A plot of mass against size shows all these objects between the region "below the quantum limit" and the region "inside a black hole" (slides 45–46). Since all of them follow from just three numbers, their sizes are no evidence of fine tuning, slide 46 stresses.

**Fine tuning** (slides 47–52). Some constants seem finely tuned for life; the most spectacular are the dark energy density and the Higgs field's vacuum value (slide 48), $v = 246$ GeV (slide 49). Changing $v$ by modest factors would be drastic (slide 49, after Agrawal, Barr, Donoghue and Seckel 1998): below half its value, protons would decay into neutrons; below 0.8, diprotons and dineutrons would be bound; above twice its value, deuterium would be unstable; above five times, neutrons would decay even inside nuclei; and above a thousand times, protons would decay into $\Delta^{++}$ particles. Carbon itself is made in stars by a delicate chain (slide 50; the page's reading: the step to carbon works only because carbon-12 has an energy level in just the right place, and slightly different constants could leave stars making little carbon or burning it all to oxygen), $\alpha + \alpha \to {}^8$Be, then ${}^8$Be $+\ \alpha \to {}^{12}$C, then ${}^{12}$C $+\ \alpha \to {}^{16}$O (slide 50). And with four large space dimensions there would be no stable orbits and no stable atoms (slide 51; Ehrenfest 1917, Tangherlini 1963).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Why these values?">
Several of the slides' unsolved problems, dark energy, dark matter and inflation, are the central questions of cosmology. Inflation may also answer the "why these values?" question in an unexpected way: if it produces many regions with different effective laws, we may simply live in one where life is possible. Guth discusses this [multiverse and anthropic reasoning](/courses/8-286/anthropic-multiverse) in the opening lectures of 8.286.
</Callout>

<Problem>
(a) A 100 keV X-ray photon scatters through $90°$ off an electron at rest. What is its new energy? (b) Iron-57 (rest energy $57 \times 931.5$ MeV) emits a 14.4 keV gamma ray as a free atom. By what fraction does recoil lower the photon energy, and what source speed would give the same Doppler shift? Compare with $gh/c^2$ over 22.6 m. (c) Estimate a star's mass as $m_{\text{pl}}^3/m_p^2$, with $m_{\text{pl}} = 2.176 \times 10^{-8}$ kg and $m_p = 1.6726 \times 10^{-27}$ kg.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Compton">
$h\nu' = 100/(1 + 100/511) \approx 83.6$ keV; the electron takes the other 16.4 keV.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Recoil against gravity">
$Q_0/2m_0c^2 = 14.4 \times 10^3/(2 \times 5.31 \times 10^{10}) \approx 1.4 \times 10^{-7}$, the Doppler shift of a source moving at about 41 m/s. The tower's shift, $gh/c^2 = 9.81 \times 22.6/(3.00 \times 10^8)^2 \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-15}$, is fifty million times smaller. (Thermal motion broadens the line of free atoms even more, by about $10^{-6}$ at room temperature.) Only the Mössbauer effect, which removes both the recoil and most of the broadening, makes the measurement possible.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A star">
$m_{\text{pl}}^3/m_p^2 = (2.176 \times 10^{-8})^3/(1.6726 \times 10^{-27})^2 \approx 3.7 \times 10^{30}$ kg, about twice the mass of the Sun.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Particle reactions allowed in nature are all those not forbidden by conservation of energy-momentum, charge, baryon and lepton number (and parity, except in weak interactions).
- Energy-momentum conservation gives the recoil of emitting particles, the Mössbauer effect behind the Pound–Rebka redshift measurement, and Compton scattering.
- Why the constants have their values is open; some may be environmental, and several, such as the Higgs vacuum value, seem finely tuned for life.
