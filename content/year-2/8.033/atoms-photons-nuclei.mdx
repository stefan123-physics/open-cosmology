Lecture 13 starts the particle-physics part of the course: atomic, nuclear and particle physics, plus parallel and transverse force and accelerators, which the [last lecture's page](/courses/8-033/force-acceleration-interstellar-travel) already covered (slides 2–3). The aim, as the slides put it, is an overview of atomic, nuclear and particle physics, to see the big picture, get more out of popular talks and articles, and pass the "cocktail party test", while applying two core results of relativity: mass-energy unification and energy-momentum conservation (slide 4). (MIT's material for this lecture is a set of slides, many of them pictures; this page follows their order and marks what is not on them. The deck is titled "Lecture 12": this site numbers the lectures by MIT's list of class sessions, in which the first quiz took one session.)

## Atomic physics

Atoms have a small, heavy nucleus. That was found by firing alpha particles from a radioactive source at a thin gold foil: most passed through, but a few bounced back (slide 6; the experiment of Geiger and Marsden, interpreted by Rutherford, not named on the slide). In the simple Bohr picture the electron of hydrogen can only occupy orbits labelled $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$, and jumping between them it emits or absorbs light with energy equal to the difference (slide 7):

<Formula label="Energy of the emitted light" tex="\Delta E = h\nu" cite="mit" at="slide 7" />

The periodic table orders the elements by atomic number; its slide marks element 118 as "just confirmed" (slide 8; that was in 2006, and element 118 was named oganesson in 2016).

**Spectral lines** (slides 9–13). A hot dense body gives a continuous spectrum; seen through a cloud of cooler gas it shows dark absorption lines; the gas viewed on its own gives bright emission lines at the same wavelengths (slide 10). A flame test makes the same point in the lab (slide 12). Every element has its own pattern: hydrogen's emission spectrum has a few lines, iron's a great many (slide 13). The slides also show the cosmic microwave background, mapped by COBE and WMAP, whose spectrum COBE found to agree with theory (slide 11).

**How do we know what stars are made of** (slides 14–17)? Their light shows the absorption lines of the elements in their outer layers. For hydrogen the lines fall into series by the level the electron ends on (slide 17): the Lyman series (ultraviolet, ending on $n = 1$; 122, 103, 97.3, 95.0 and 93.8 nm), the Balmer series (visible, ending on $n = 2$; 656, 486, 434 and 410 nm) and the Paschen series (infrared, ending on $n = 3$; 1875, 1282 and 1094 nm). (Not on the slides: all of them follow the Rydberg formula $1/\lambda = R(1/n_1^2 - 1/n_2^2)$ with $R \approx 1.097 \times 10^7$ per metre; for example $n_2 = 3 \to n_1 = 2$ gives 656 nm.)

## Photons

**The photoelectric effect** (slides 19–23). Light falling on a metal plate in a vacuum tube can knock electrons out and make a current flow. In 1902 Lenard found that red light ejects no electrons however bright it is, while blue light does (slides 20–21). Einstein's model explained this (slide 22): light carries energy in photons of $h\nu$, and an electron needs a certain work $W_e$ to escape from the metal. So

<Formula label="Photoelectric effect" tex="K = h\nu - W_e" cite="mit" at="slide 22" />

Light with $h\nu$ below $W_e$ frees no electrons at all; above it, electrons come out with kinetic energy $h\nu - W_e$; and making the light more intense (more photons) does not change either. The bottom line: a photon can be treated as just another particle (slide 22). Plotting the electrons' kinetic energy against frequency gives a straight line whose slope is Planck's constant $h$ (slide 23).

**Working with photons** (slide 24). A photon's 4-momentum is

<Formula label="Photon 4-momentum" tex="P = \hbar\begin{pmatrix} \vec k \\ k \end{pmatrix}, \qquad k = \frac{\omega}{c}, \qquad P = \hbar K" cite="mit" at="slide 24" />

so $p = E/c$ for photons, and comparing with the [wave 4-vector](/courses/8-033/four-vectors-doppler-aberration), written $K$ here (the earlier page called it $k$), gives $P = \hbar K$. This relation holds for *all* particles, even massive ones: that is wave-particle duality in quantum mechanics, and in field theory it follows from the Klein–Gordon equation (slide 24). The Doppler effect is then just the Lorentz transformation of $P$ for zero rest mass (a problem-set exercise, slide 24).

## Nuclear physics

**Terminology** (slide 26). The *atomic number* $Z$ of a nucleus is its number of protons and determines the element; the *atomic weight* $A$ (the slide's term; today it is called the mass number) is its number of nucleons, protons plus neutrons. Nuclei with the same $Z$ but different $A$ are *isotopes* of the same element. The slide writes iron with $Z = 26$ and $A = 56$ as Fe⁵⁶ (today usually ⁵⁶Fe). The *mass excess* of a nucleus is $m_0 - A$ amu, its rest mass minus the number of nucleons times the atomic mass unit; by definition it is zero for carbon-12. (Strictly, the atomic mass unit is a twelfth of a neutral carbon-12 *atom*, so mass excesses are tabulated for atoms; a bare carbon-12 nucleus, without its six electrons, would have a mass excess of about −3.07 MeV.)

**Rest mass and binding energy** (slide 27). The rest energy of an object is its energy in the frame where it has zero momentum, and it is the sum of *all* energy contributions: positive ones, such as the rest masses and kinetic energies of its parts, and negative ones, such as the potential energy of the forces holding them together. The *binding energy* of a nucleus is the rest energy of its protons and neutrons when free, minus the rest energy of the nucleus.

The nucleons themselves show how far this goes (slide 28): the neutron (udd) has about 939.6 MeV and the proton (uud) about 938.3 MeV of rest energy, while the up and down quarks have only about 4 and 7 MeV (the slide's 2006 values; today's are about 2 and 5 MeV), and the electromagnetic contribution about 1.7 MeV. So a proton's mass is mostly glue: the energy of the strong field. In a nucleus, the electric repulsion between protons *increases* the mass, and the strong attraction between nucleons *decreases* it; only nuclei with positive binding energy can exist. Von Weizsäcker's semi-empirical formula (1935) estimates the binding energy (slide 28):

<Formula label="Semi-empirical binding energy" tex="E_{\text{binding}} \approx \left[15.8A - 18.3A^{2/3} - 0.714\frac{Z^2}{A^{1/3}} - 23.2\frac{\left(A - 2Z\right)^2}{A} + \left(-1\right)^Z\frac{12}{A^{1/2}}\right]\text{ MeV}" cite="mit" at="slide 28" />

where the last term is left out when $A$ is odd. (The slide writes $E_{\text{binding}}/c^2$ on the left; with MeV on the right, it is $E_{\text{binding}}$ itself.) The terms are, in order (the page's own reading): a volume term, a surface correction, the electric repulsion, a penalty for unequal numbers of protons and neutrons, and a pairing term. Much work remains to be done in this field, the slide adds.

Slide 29 shows the chart of nuclides, every known nucleus plotted by proton and neutron number; the stable ones form a narrow valley. Slide 30 plots the average binding energy per nucleon against the number of nucleons: it rises steeply from hydrogen, has bumps at helium-4, carbon-12 and oxygen-16, peaks near iron-56 at almost 9 MeV (strictly, nickel-62 is a little higher), and falls slowly towards uranium. Moving towards the peak releases energy: **fusion** of light nuclei on the left, **fission** of heavy ones on the right. The lecture closes with the periodic table as set to song by Tom Lehrer (slide 31).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The cosmic background, a perfect spectrum">
Slide 11's spectrum is the [cosmic microwave background](/courses/8-286/cmb-spectrum), the cooled glow of the hot early universe, whose black-body shape COBE measured to fit theory perfectly. The same atomic physics decides when it was released: once the universe cooled enough for electrons and nuclei to combine into atoms, the photons stopped scattering and have travelled freely ever since.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Spectral lines, emission and absorption at energies $\Delta E = h\nu$ between atomic levels, reveal what stars are made of.
- The photoelectric effect, $K = h\nu - W_e$, shows that light comes in photons with 4-momentum $P = \hbar K$.
- The binding energy of a nucleus is missing rest mass; per nucleon it peaks near iron-56, so fusion of light nuclei and fission of heavy ones both release energy.
