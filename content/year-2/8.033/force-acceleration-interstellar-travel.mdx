Lecture 12 collects the dynamics so far into a problem-solving toolbox, adds more on mass-energy equivalence, kinetic and rest energy, treats acceleration and force, and then turns to particle accelerators and interstellar rocket travel (slide 2). (MIT's material for this lecture is a set of slides, in a deck titled "Lecture 11": this site numbers lectures by MIT's list of class sessions, in which the first quiz took one session. The slides write $m_0$ for the rest mass and $m = \gamma m_0$ for the "relativistic mass"; this page follows them. Most physicists today use $m$ for the rest mass only, as the [last lecture's page](/courses/8-033/momentum-energy-sun) did.)

## The momentum and energy toolbox

The first slides list the tools (slide 3): the relativistic mass $m = \gamma m_0$, mass-energy unification $E = mc^2$, and the momentum 4-vector

<Formula label="Momentum 4-vector" tex="P \equiv m_0U = m_0\frac{dX}{d\tau} = m_0\gamma_u\begin{pmatrix} u_x \\ u_y \\ u_z \\ c \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} p_x \\ p_y \\ p_z \\ E/c \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="slide 3" />

(upper case $X$, $U$ and $P$ for 4-vectors, lower case for the 3-vectors). Dividing the momentum by the energy gives a handy velocity formula:

<Formula label="Velocity from energy and momentum" tex="\beta = \frac{cp}{E}" cite="mit" at="slide 3" />

The **rest energy** $E_0 = m_0c^2$ is a particle's total energy in its own rest frame, and the **kinetic energy** is what it has in addition (slide 4):

<Formula label="Kinetic energy" tex="K = E - E_0 = mc^2 - m_0c^2 = m_0c^2\left(\gamma - 1\right) = \frac12 m_0u^2 + O\left(\frac{m_0u^4}{c^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="slide 4" />

(The slide drops the $c^2$ in the third expression and writes the correction as $O(u^4/c)$; the correction is in fact $\frac38 m_0u^4/c^2$.)

**The rest mass is invariant** (slide 5). The length of the momentum 4-vector gives

<Formula label="Rest mass invariant" tex="m_0 = \frac1c\sqrt{-P^t\eta P} = \frac{1}{c^2}\sqrt{E^2 - c^2p^2}, \qquad E = \sqrt{\left(m_0c^2\right)^2 + \left(cp\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="slide 5" />

At low speed ($\beta \ll 1$) this gives back $E \approx m_0c^2 + \frac12 m_0u^2$ and $p \approx m_0u$. At high speed ($\gamma \gg 1$, $E \gg E_0$), $E \approx cp$, which is exact for particles moving at the speed of light, such as photons and gravitons (slide 5).

**Systems of particles** (slide 6). The same invariant works for a whole system: the total momentum 4-vector is a sum of 4-vectors that all transform with the same $\Lambda$, so it transforms with $\Lambda$ too, and $(E/c)^2 - p^2$ of the total is the same in every frame. The slide adds a reassurance: $\vec p = m_0\gamma\vec u$ was derived from a one-dimensional collision, but any collision is one-dimensional in the frame where the total momentum is zero.

## Acceleration and force

The acceleration and force 4-vectors are less useful than their cousins $X$, $U$, $P$ and $K$ (the slides mark this part "optional"), but they are needed for electromagnetic fields later (slide 7). The **acceleration 4-vector** is $A = dU/d\tau$, and the **force 4-vector** (an outlined F on the slides; this page calls it $Q$ to keep it apart from the 3-vector $\vec F$) is

<Formula label="Force 4-vector" tex="Q \equiv \frac{dP}{d\tau} = m_0A = \gamma_u\begin{pmatrix} \vec F \\ \dot E/c \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="slide 8" />

where $\vec F = d\vec p/dt$ is the familiar force 3-vector and $\dot E$ is the power, the energy gained per unit time (written $P$ on the slides). Note that $Q = m_0A$ does *not* give the Newtonian $\vec F = m\vec a$ (slide 8). The work-energy theorem keeps its form (slide 9):

<Formula label="Work and power" tex="dE = \vec F\cdot d\vec r = \vec F\cdot\vec u\,dt, \qquad \dot E = \vec u\cdot\vec F" cite="mit" at="slide 9" />

Writing $Q = m_0A$ out, using that $d\gamma_u/dt = \gamma_u^3\,\vec u\cdot\vec a/c^2$ (slide 7), gives the force in terms of the acceleration:

<Formula label="Force and acceleration" tex="\frac{\vec F}{m_0\gamma_u} = \vec a + \gamma_u^2\frac{\vec u\cdot\vec a}{c^2}\,\vec u" cite="mit" at="slide 10" />

Two special cases (slide 10):

- **along the motion** ($\vec u$ parallel to $\vec a$, as in linear motion): $\vec F = \gamma_u^3 m_0\vec a$;
- **across the motion** ($\vec u$ perpendicular to $\vec a$, as in circular motion): $\vec F = \gamma_u m_0\vec a$.

In general $\vec F$ and $\vec a$ are *not* parallel; only in these two cases are they. Solved for the acceleration (slide 11), $\vec a = \vec F/(m_0\gamma_u) - (\dot E/(m_0\gamma_u c^2))\,\vec u$: the extra term, the departure from $\vec F = m\vec a$, has the shape of a friction force, growing with the power fed into the particle.

## Transforming force and acceleration

Transforming $Q$ with $\Lambda$ to a frame $S'$ moving at $v$ along $x$, and using $\gamma_{u'} = \gamma_u\gamma(1 - u_xv/c^2)$ from the velocity 4-vector, gives the force transformation (slides 12–13):

<Formula label="Transformation of force" tex="F_x' = \frac{F_x - v\dot E/c^2}{1 - u_xv/c^2}, \qquad F_y' = \frac{F_y}{\gamma\left(1 - u_xv/c^2\right)}, \qquad F_z' = \frac{F_z}{\gamma\left(1 - u_xv/c^2\right)}, \qquad \dot E' = \frac{\dot E - vF_x}{1 - u_xv/c^2}" cite="mit" at="slide 13" />

If $S$ is the particle's rest frame, $\vec u = 0$ and $\dot E = 0$, so $F_x' = F_x$ and $F_y' = F_y/\gamma$, $F_z' = F_z/\gamma$: in a frame where the particle moves, the force along the motion is unchanged and the force across it is reduced by $\gamma$ (slide 13).

Acceleration transforms in a messier way, best handled problem by problem (slide 14). A useful special case, to be derived on a problem set: an acceleration $\vec a'$ in $S'$ appears in $S$ as

<Formula label="Transformation of acceleration" tex="a_x = \frac{a_x'}{\gamma^3\left(1 + vu_x'/c^2\right)^3}, \qquad a_y = \frac{a_y'}{\gamma^2\left(1 + vu_x'/c^2\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="slide 14" />

where the formula for $a_y$ holds only if $u_y' = 0$ or $a_x' = 0$. Slide 16 summarizes the toolbox: $E = mc^2 = m_0\gamma c^2$, $P = m_0U$, $E = \sqrt{(m_0c^2)^2 + (cp)^2}$ and $\beta = cp/E$.

## Particle accelerators

Who needs relativity? Particle physicists, astrophysicists, and anyone using electromagnetism (slide 17). How do we accelerate particles to near the speed of light (slide 18)? Slide 19 shows CERN's chain of accelerators, protons or lead ions passing from the PS to the SPS and into the 27 km ring of the LHC with its detectors (ATLAS, CMS, LHCb, ALICE); slide 20, Fermilab's ring. (Slide 20 is titled "linear particle accelerator" but shows Fermilab's ring. The LHC was still being built in 2006; it began collisions in 2009.)

The slides then ask how to derive the radius of curvature of a particle's path (slide 21). The answer (the page's own): a magnetic field $B$ exerts on a charge $q$ a force $quB$ at right angles to the motion, so the speed stays constant, and by the "across the motion" case above, $quB = \gamma m_0 u^2/r$. Hence

<Formula label="Radius in a magnetic field" tex="r = \frac{\gamma m_0 u}{qB} = \frac{p}{qB}" cite="mit" at="slide 21 (question only; the answer is the page's own)" />

with the *relativistic* momentum. To keep a faster particle on the same ring, the field must grow with $p$, not with $u$, which barely changes near $c$.

## Interstellar travel

The rest of the lecture is about interstellar space travel (slides 22–36): where might we want to go, as far as galaxies like M100, and why is it so hard (slides 23–24)? On the slide, the Galileo probe, swinging by Jupiter, reached about 100,000 miles per hour; most others, such as the Pioneers and Voyagers, travel at 25,000 to 35,000 miles per hour (slide 25). Slide 26 gives travel times to the nearest star, Proxima Centauri, of about 44,500 years at Galileo's speed and 74,100 years at Voyager's. (Later developments: Helios 2 had already reached about 157,000 miles per hour in 1976, and the Parker Solar Probe about 430,000 in 2024. The slide's two travel times do not scale with the speeds; at a constant 100,000 and 30,000 miles per hour the 4.24 light years take about 28,000 and 95,000 years.)

Some ideas (slides 27 and 35): antimatter-powered rockets, laser light sails, warp drive, wormholes and "beam me up" universal constructors. The problem with rockets is the propellant (slide 28). For one canister to pass Alpha Centauri (the slide says "Centauri Cluster") within 900 years, the slide estimates the propellant needed by engine type, labelled by what is presumably the specific impulse (the exhaust speed divided by $g$): chemical (500 s), about $10^{137}$ kg, more than the known universe contains; fission (5,000 s), about $10^{17}$ kg; fusion (10,000 s), about $10^{11}$ kg; ion or antimatter (50,000 s), about $10^5$ kg. The conclusion: interstellar travel needs a propulsion breakthrough, ideally one with no propellant.

**Antimatter** (slides 29–32) would be the perfect fuel: annihilation releases about ten billion times more energy than burning hydrogen with oxygen, a thousand times more than fission and 300 times more than fusion, since it turns all the rest mass into energy. But we cannot make or store enough of it. All the antiprotons CERN produced in a year would light a 100 W bulb for three seconds; the production efficiency is about $10^{-8}$ percent; and, by an estimate quoted on the slides (probably from the late 1990s), antimatter cost about \$62.5 trillion per gram, with only about a billionth of a gram made per year. The last slides (33–36) show a sample spacecraft and candidate technologies: fission fragments, inertial confinement fusion, beam-core antimatter rockets, laser light sails and relativistic particle beams, and combinations such as the Bussard interstellar ramjet.

**The relativistic rocket** (the page's own addition). For a rocket that throws out photons, energy and momentum conservation give the ratio of initial to final rest mass, $M_i/M_f$, needed to reach speed $\beta$:

<Formula label="Photon rocket" tex="\frac{M_i}{M_f} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}}" cite="mit" at="slide 28 (topic; the formula is the page's own)" />

the inverse of the Doppler factor. Reaching $\beta = 0.5$ needs a mass ratio of only $\sqrt 3 \approx 1.73$, but only if the fuel turns entirely into light, which is why antimatter is so tempting.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Accelerators as time machines">
In the hot early universe, a typical particle had an energy of about $kT$. So a collider whose particles reach a given energy recreates, for an instant, conditions that filled the whole universe when $kT$ was that large. For the LHC, that was when the universe was far less than a nanosecond old; [temperature and time](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling) were tied together then, so each energy marks a moment.
</Callout>

<Problem>
(a) A proton has momentum 7 TeV/$c$ in a ring whose magnets give 8.33 T. What is the radius of its path? (b) A probe should pass Alpha Centauri, 4.37 light years away, within 900 years. What speed does it need, and with a chemical exhaust speed of $9.81 \times 500 \approx 4.9$ km/s, what mass ratio $M_i/M_f$ does the (nonrelativistic) rocket equation, $\Delta v$ = exhaust speed times $\ln(M_i/M_f)$, demand?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The ring">
In the units of particle physics, $r = p/(qB) = (7 \times 10^{12}\text{ V})/(3.00 \times 10^8 \times 8.33) \approx 2.8$ km, close to the LHC's real bending radius.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The probe">
$v = 4.37/900$ of $c$, about 0.0049 $c$ or 1,460 km/s. Then $\ln(M_i/M_f) = 1456/4.905 \approx 297$, and $M_i/M_f \approx 10^{129}$: hopeless. For a probe of any reasonable mass the propellant would be some $10^{130}$ kg or more, as absurd as the slide's $10^{137}$ kg (whose exact assumptions the slide does not give).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Toolbox: $E = \gamma m_0c^2$, $P = m_0U$, $K = (\gamma - 1)m_0c^2$, $m_0c^2 = \sqrt{E^2 - (cp)^2}$ for a particle or a system, and $\beta = cp/E$.
- Force $\vec F = d\vec p/dt$ and acceleration are parallel only along or across the motion, where $\vec F = \gamma^3m_0\vec a$ or $\gamma m_0\vec a$; forces transform like the parts of the 4-vector $Q$.
- Accelerators bend particles on circles of radius $p/(qB)$; interstellar rockets need a propulsion breakthrough.
