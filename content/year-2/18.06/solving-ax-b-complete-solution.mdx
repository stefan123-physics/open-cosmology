This is the lecture where linear equations are solved completely: decide whether $Ax = b$ has a solution, and if so, find them all (transcript, p. 1). It continues [solving Ax = 0](/courses/18-06/solving-ax-0-special-solutions) with the same matrix.

## When is there a solution?

The matrix of the last lecture has rows $(1, 2, 2, 2)$, $(2, 4, 6, 8)$ and $(3, 6, 8, 10)$; the third row is the sum of the first two (transcript, p. 1). So the third left-hand side is the sum of the first two, and $b_3$ must be $b_1 + b_2$: with $b_1 = 1$ and $b_2 = 5$, only $b_3 = 6$ works, and $(1, 5, 17)$ would have no solution (p. 1). Elimination discovers this. Work on the **augmented matrix** $[A\;\;b]$, doing to $b$ what is done to the rows (p. 2):

<Formula label="Elimination on [A b] (last column: the right-hand side)" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 & b_1 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 & 8 & b_2 \\ 3 & 6 & 8 & 10 & b_3 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 2 & 2 & b_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 & b_2 - 2b_1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & b_3 - b_2 - b_1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The last equation says $0 = b_3 - b_2 - b_1$, the **condition for solvability** (pp. 2–3). For $b = (1, 5, 6)$ the right side becomes $(1, 3, 0)$, and the last equation is $0 = 0$ (p. 3).

Two ways to say when $Ax = b$ is solvable (pp. 3–4):

- **$b$ is in the column space** $C(A)$: $b$ is a combination of the columns (p. 3);
- **if a combination of the rows of $A$ gives the zero row, the same combination of the entries of $b$ gives 0** (p. 4).

They are equivalent, though not obviously so, because both are equivalent to solvability (p. 4).

## The complete solution

**Step 1, a particular solution** (transcript, p. 5). Set all **free variables to zero** and solve for the pivot variables. Here $x_2 = x_4 = 0$, leaving $x_1 + 2x_3 = 1$ and $2x_3 = 3$, so $x_3 = \frac32$ and $x_1 = -2$ (pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="A particular solution" tex="x_p = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 3/2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Strang urges checking it in the original equations (p. 6): $-2 + 3 = 1$, $-4 + 9 = 5$, $-6 + 12 = 6$.

**Step 2, add the nullspace** (pp. 6–7). Any $x_n$ with $Ax_n = 0$ can be added, since (p. 7)

<Formula label="Particular plus nullspace" tex="A x_p = b, \quad A x_n = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; A(x_p + x_n) = b + 0 = b" cite="reading1" at="pp. 6–7" />

This pattern turns up throughout mathematics, wherever equations are linear (p. 6). With the special solutions of Lecture 7 (pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="The complete solution" tex="x_{\text{complete}} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 0 \\ 3/2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + c_1\begin{pmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + c_2\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 7–8" />

There is no free constant in front of $x_p$: $A(3x_p) = 3b$, not $b$. The nullspace part is a whole subspace (p. 8). (Strang recalls the special solutions on the spot and slightly misquotes the first equation while checking them; the values above are the ones from Lecture 7.)

**The picture** (pp. 9–10). $x$ has four components, so the picture lives in $\mathrm{R}^4$. The nullspace is a two-dimensional subspace (two free constants), and the solutions are that flat surface **shifted** to pass through $x_p$: flat like a subspace, but not containing zero, so **not a subspace** (pp. 9–10).

## Rank and the number of solutions

Now any $m \times n$ matrix of rank $r$, the number of pivots (transcript, p. 10). There can be no more pivots than rows, and none than columns, since a row or column holds at most one pivot (pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="Bounds on the rank" tex="r \le m, \qquad r \le n" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11" />

**Full column rank, $r = n$** (pp. 11–15). A pivot in every column, no free variables, so $N(A)$ is only the zero vector, and $x = x_p$ is the **unique** solution if it exists: **0 or 1 solutions** (pp. 11–12). Many applications are like this (p. 12). The example is tall and thin (p. 13):

<Formula label="Full column rank" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 6 & 1 \\ 5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 13–14" />

Four equations in two unknowns usually have no solution. But $b = (4, 3, 7, 6)$, the sum of the two columns, has exactly one: $x = (1, 1)$ (pp. 14–15).

**Full row rank, $r = m$** (pp. 15–17). A pivot in every row, so elimination produces no zero rows and puts **no condition on $b$**: $Ax = b$ is solvable for **every** $b$ (p. 15). There are $n - r = n - m$ free variables (p. 16). The example is the transpose of the last one, with rows $(1, 2, 6, 5)$ and $(3, 1, 1, 1)$: rank 2, pivots in columns 1 and 2, and $R$ has the identity in those columns and a free part $F$ beside it, with no zero rows (pp. 16–17).

**Full rank, $r = m = n$** (pp. 17–18). The matrix is square and **invertible**, like $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$; its reduced form is $R = I$, its nullspace is only zero, every $b$ is allowed, and the solution is unique (pp. 17–18).

<Formula label="The four cases" tex="\begin{array}{ccc} r = m = n & R = I & \text{1 solution} \\ r = n < m & R = \begin{pmatrix} I \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} & \text{0 or 1 solution} \\ r = m < n & R = \begin{pmatrix} I & F \end{pmatrix} & \infty \text{ solutions} \\ r < m,\; r < n & R = \begin{pmatrix} I & F \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} & 0 \text{ or } \infty \text{ solutions} \end{array}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 18–19" />

When $r = m < n$, the $I$ and $F$ may be interleaved if the pivot columns do not come first (p. 19). (Strang first labels the row with $R = \begin{pmatrix} I \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and 0 or 1 solution as $r = m < n$, then corrects it to $r = n < m$; for $r = m < n$ he first says one or infinitely many, then settles on infinitely many, since there is always a nullspace, pp. 18–19.) **The rank tells everything about the number of solutions**; only the actual entries need the matrix itself (p. 20).

<WorkedExample title="A complete solution of your own">
<Problem>
For $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 & 0 & 2 \\ 2 & 6 & 1 & 7 \\ 1 & 3 & 2 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$ (the example of the last page): (a) find the condition on $b$ for $Bx = b$ to be solvable; (b) solve $Bx = (1, 4, 5)$ completely.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The condition">
Eliminating: row 2 becomes $(0, 0, 1, 3)$ with right side $b_2 - 2b_1$; row 3 becomes $(0, 0, 2, 6)$ with right side $b_3 - b_1$, and then a zero row with right side $b_3 - b_1 - 2(b_2 - 2b_1) = b_3 - 2b_2 + 3b_1$. So $Bx = b$ is solvable exactly when $b_3 = 2b_2 - 3b_1$. Indeed row 3 of $B$ is $2(\text{row } 2) - 3(\text{row } 1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Particular solution">
$b = (1, 4, 5)$ passes: $2 \cdot 4 - 3 = 5$. The right side becomes $(1, 2, 0)$. With $x_2 = x_4 = 0$: $x_1 = 1$ and $x_3 = 2$, so $x_p = (1, 0, 2, 0)$. Check: $B x_p = (1, 2 + 2, 1 + 4) = (1, 4, 5)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Complete solution">
Add the special solutions found before: $x = (1, 0, 2, 0) + c_1(-3, 1, 0, 0) + c_2(-2, 0, -3, 1)$. The rank 2 is less than both $m = 3$ and $n = 4$, the "0 or infinitely many" case; this $b$ gives infinitely many.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Eliminate on $[A\;\;b]$; zero rows of $A$ give conditions on $b$. Solvable exactly when $b$ is in $C(A)$.
- Particular solution: free variables 0, solve for the pivot variables. Complete solution: $x_p$ plus the whole nullspace.
- $r \le m$ and $r \le n$. Full column rank: 0 or 1 solution. Full row rank: a solution for every $b$. Square full rank: invertible, exactly one. Otherwise 0 or infinitely many.
