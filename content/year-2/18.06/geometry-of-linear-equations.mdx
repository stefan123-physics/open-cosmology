The first lecture of 18.06 states the fundamental problem of linear algebra, solving a system of linear equations, and looks at it in three ways: the **row picture**, the **column picture** and the **matrix form** (transcript, p. 1). It builds on the vectors and [dot products](/courses/18-02/dot-product) of 18.02 and on [planes and linear systems](/courses/18-02/planes-and-linear-systems) there.

## Two equations, two unknowns

Take (transcript, p. 2)

<Formula label="A 2 × 2 system" tex="2x - y = 0, \qquad -x + 2y = 3; \qquad \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers; here the **coefficient matrix** $A$ multiplies the vector of unknowns $x$ to give the right-hand side $b$: $Ax = b$ (p. 2).

**Row picture** (pp. 2–4). Plot all points satisfying each equation. $2x - y = 0$ passes through $(0, 0)$ and $(1, 2)$: a line, which is where the word linear comes from (p. 3). $-x + 2y = 3$ does not pass through the origin; it contains $(-3, 0)$ and $(-1, 1)$ (p. 4). The two lines meet at the solution $x = 1$, $y = 2$; check: $-1 + 4 = 3$ (p. 4).

**Column picture** (pp. 5–8), the key one. Read the equations a column at a time (p. 5):

<Formula label="Column picture" tex="x\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} + y\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

The left side is a **linear combination** of the columns, the most fundamental operation in the course: multiply each column by a number and add (p. 5). The problem asks for the right numbers. Draw column 1, $(2, -1)$, then attach two copies of column 2, $(-1, 2)$, at its tip: the arrows end at $(0, 3)$, which is $b$ (pp. 6–8). Componentwise, $2 - 2 = 0$ and $-1 + 4 = 3$ (p. 8). All combinations of these two columns would fill the whole plane, so every right-hand side could be reached (p. 8).

## Three equations, three unknowns

Now (transcript, p. 9)

<Formula label="A 3 × 3 system" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

**Row picture** (pp. 10–12). Each equation is a plane in three dimensions. The second, $-x + 2y - z = -1$, misses the origin but contains $(1, 0, 0)$, $(0, 0, 1)$ and $(0, -\frac{1}{2}, 0)$ (p. 10). Two planes meet in a line, and the third plane cuts that line at a point, the solution (p. 11). But three planes are much harder to picture than two lines, and four dimensions harder still (p. 12).

**Column picture** (pp. 12–14). Find the combination (p. 12)

<Formula label="Column picture in three dimensions" tex="x\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + y\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \\ -3 \end{pmatrix} + z\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 12" />

In this specially chosen example $b$ is the third column itself, so $x = 0$, $y = 0$, $z = 1$ (pp. 13–14). With the new right-hand side column 1 + column 2 $= (1, 1, -3)$, the solution would be $(1, 1, 0)$ (p. 14). In general neither picture shows the answer directly; the systematic method is **elimination**, the next lecture (p. 14).

## Every right-hand side?

Can $Ax = b$ be solved for **every** $b$? In combination language: **do the linear combinations of the columns fill three-dimensional space?** (transcript, p. 15). For this matrix, yes: it is **non-singular**, or **invertible** (p. 16). It fails if the three columns lie in one plane, for example if column 3 is column 1 plus column 2: then all combinations stay in that plane, and right-hand sides off it are unreachable. That matrix is **singular** (p. 16).

The same holds in nine dimensions: nine columns of nine components each. A random $9 \times 9$ matrix, Strang says, is invertible (with probability one), its columns' combinations filling nine-dimensional space; if the ninth column equals the eighth, they only fill a kind of eight-dimensional plane inside it (pp. 17–18).

## Matrix times vector

How do you multiply a matrix by a vector? Strang's favourite way is **by columns** (transcript, pp. 18–19):

<Formula label="Multiplication by columns" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = 1\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + 2\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 12 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 19" />

By rows it is two dot products: $(2, 5)\cdot(1, 2) = 12$ and $(1, 3)\cdot(1, 2) = 7$ (p. 19). Both give the same answer, but think of it this way: **$Ax$ is a combination of the columns of $A$** (p. 19). So if $b$ were $(12, 7)$, the solution of $Ax = b$ would be $x = (1, 2)$ (p. 20).

<WorkedExample title="Pictures of a new system">
<Problem>
(a) Solve $x + 2y = 5$, $3x - y = 1$ in both pictures. (b) Is $b = (1, 2, 3)$ reachable from the columns $(1, 0, 1)$, $(0, 1, 1)$, $(1, 1, 2)$? (c) Compute $\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}$ by columns.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Both pictures">
Row picture: the lines $y = (5 - x)/2$ and $y = 3x - 1$ meet where $5 - x = 6x - 2$, at $x = 1$, $y = 2$. Column picture: $1\cdot(1, 3) + 2\cdot(2, -1) = (5, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A singular case">
The third column is the sum of the first two, so all combinations lie in one plane: vectors $(p, q, p + q)$. Here $1 + 2 = 3$, so $b$ is in the plane and reachable, for example as $1\cdot(1, 0, 1) + 2\cdot(0, 1, 1)$. But $(1, 2, 4)$ would not be.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) By columns">
$2\cdot(3, -2) - 1\cdot(1, 4) = (6 - 1, -4 - 4) = (5, -8)$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $Ax = b$: $n$ linear equations in $n$ unknowns.
- Row picture: lines (or planes) meeting at the solution. Column picture: the combination of columns that produces $b$.
- $Ax = b$ is solvable for every $b$ when the columns' combinations fill the space (invertible); not when the columns lie in a lower-dimensional plane (singular).
- $Ax$ is a combination of the columns of $A$.
