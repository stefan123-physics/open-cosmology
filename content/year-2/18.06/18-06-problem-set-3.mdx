## What you need from the lessons

**Subspaces** (Lecture 6). A subspace is closed under linear combinations, so it always contains the zero vector:

<Formula label="Closed under linear combinations" tex="v, w \text{ in the subspace} \;\Rightarrow\; cv + dw \text{ in the subspace, for all numbers } c, d" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The intersection of two subspaces is a subspace; their union usually is not (pp. 3–6 of the transcript). The **column space** $C(A)$ of an $m \times n$ matrix holds all combinations of its columns and lies in $\mathrm{R}^m$; the **nullspace** $N(A)$ holds all solutions of $Ax = 0$ and lies in $\mathrm{R}^n$ (pp. 7–8, 15–16). The key fact:

<Formula label="When Ax = b is solvable" tex="Ax = b \text{ has a solution} \iff b \text{ is in } C(A)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 12–13" />

**Solving Ax = 0** (Lecture 7). Elimination does not change the nullspace. On a rectangular matrix it skips a column with no pivot and ends in an echelon matrix $U$. The **rank** $r$ is the number of pivots. Columns with a pivot are pivot columns; the others are free, and their variables can be given any values (pp. 2–5):

<Formula label="Counting" tex="r \text{ pivot variables}, \qquad n - r \text{ free variables}" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

A **special solution** sets one free variable to 1, the others to 0, and solves for the pivot variables; the nullspace is all combinations of the special solutions (pp. 5–8). Eliminating upwards and dividing by the pivots gives the reduced row echelon form $R$. With the pivot columns put first,

<Formula label="Reduced form and nullspace matrix" tex="R = \begin{pmatrix} I & F \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad x_{\text{pivot}} = -F\,x_{\text{free}}, \qquad N = \begin{pmatrix} -F \\ I \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 12–14" />

so the columns of $N$ are the special solutions.

**Solving Ax = b** (Lecture 8). Eliminate on the augmented matrix $[A\;\;b]$. A zero row of $A$ turns into a condition on $b$: if a combination of the rows of $A$ gives the zero row, the same combination of the entries of $b$ must give 0 (pp. 2–4). A **particular solution** $x_p$ sets every free variable to 0 and solves for the pivot variables (pp. 5–6). Then

<Formula label="The complete solution" tex="Ax_p = b, \quad Ax_n = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; A(x_p + x_n) = b, \qquad x = x_p + (\text{any vector in } N(A))" cite="reading3" at="pp. 6–8" />

The rank obeys $r \le m$ and $r \le n$ (pp. 10–11), and it decides how many solutions there can be:

- $r = m = n$ (square and invertible, $R = I$): exactly one solution for every $b$;
- $r = n < m$ (full column rank): no free variables, so 0 or 1 solution;
- $r = m < n$ (full row rank): no zero rows, so infinitely many solutions for every $b$;
- $r < m$ and $r < n$: 0 or infinitely many solutions, depending on $b$ (pp. 11–19).

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Which sets are subspaces?" points={14}>

All the sets in this problem lie in $\mathrm{R}^3$, and a vector there is written $x = (x_1, x_2, x_3)$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Decide for each set whether it is a subspace of $\mathrm{R}^3$.

- $S_1$: all $x$ with $x_1 + 2x_2 - x_3 = 0$.
- $S_2$: all $x$ with $x_1x_2 = 0$.
- $S_3$: all $x$ with $x_1 - x_3 = 2$.
- $S_4$: all $x$ whose three components are all at least $0$.
- $S_5$: all $x$ with $x_1 = 2x_2$ and $x_2 = -x_3$.

<Hint>

First ask whether the zero vector is in the set. If it is, try to break the rule with two vectors of the set whose sum, or a multiple, falls outside.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$x_1x_2 = 0$ means $x_1 = 0$ or $x_2 = 0$: think of $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$. For a set described by inequalities, try multiplying by a negative number.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-1" label="$S_1$ is a subspace:" choices={["Yes", "No"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p1-a-2" label="$S_2$ is a subspace:" choices={["Yes", "No"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-a-3" label="$S_3$ is a subspace:" choices={["Yes", "No"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-a-4" label="$S_4$ is a subspace:" choices={["Yes", "No"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-a-5" label="$S_5$ is a subspace:" choices={["Yes", "No"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A subspace must contain zero and keep every combination $cv + dw$ inside. Equations of the form "combination of components $= 0$" always give subspaces; anything else needs a check.

<Step title="The two subspaces">

$S_1$ is the nullspace of the $1 \times 3$ matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, and a nullspace is always a subspace (Lecture 6 transcript, p. 20). $S_5$ is the intersection of the two planes $x_1 - 2x_2 = 0$ and $x_2 + x_3 = 0$, each a subspace, and an intersection of subspaces is a subspace (pp. 5–6). It is the line of multiples of $(2, 1, -1)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The three that fail">

$S_2$ contains $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$, but not their sum $(1, 1, 0)$, since $1 \cdot 1 \neq 0$: it is the union of the planes $x_1 = 0$ and $x_2 = 0$, and a union of two planes is not a subspace. $S_3$ does not contain the zero vector, since $0 - 0 \neq 2$. $S_4$ contains zero and is closed under addition, but $(1, 1, 1)$ is in it and $(-1)(1, 1, 1)$ is not.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$S_1$, $S_3$ and $S_5$ are all described by equations, but only the right-hand side $0$ keeps zero in the set: $S_3$ is a plane parallel to the subspace $x_1 - x_3 = 0$, shifted away from the origin, like the solutions of $Ax = b$ with $b \neq 0$ in the lecture (p. 22).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Let $P$ be the plane $S_1$ of part (a), $L_1$ the line of all multiples of $(1, 1, 3)$, and $L_2$ the line of all multiples of $(1, 2, 1)$. What is the intersection of $P$ with $L_1$, and of $P$ with $L_2$?

<Hint>

A line through the origin either lies in the plane or meets it only at the origin. Put the direction vector into the equation of the plane.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b-1" label="The intersection of $P$ and $L_1$ is"
  choices={["only the zero vector", "the whole of the line L₁", "the whole of the plane P", "two points, zero and one other"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-b-2" label="The intersection of $P$ and $L_2$ is"
  choices={["the line L₂", "the whole of the plane P", "only the zero vector", "two points, zero and one other"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A multiple $c\,v$ lies in $P$ when $c$ times the left side of the equation of $P$, taken at $v$, is zero.

<Step title="The two lines">

For $(1, 1, 3)$: $1 + 2 \cdot 1 - 3 = 0$, so every multiple of it lies in $P$, and the intersection is the whole line $L_1$. For $(1, 2, 1)$: $1 + 2 \cdot 2 - 1 = 4$, so $c(1, 2, 1)$ is in $P$ only when $4c = 0$, that is $c = 0$: the intersection is the zero vector alone.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Both intersections are subspaces, as they must be (Lecture 6 transcript, pp. 4–6): a line through the origin and the zero subspace. "Two points" cannot be the intersection of two subspaces, since with a nonzero vector the intersection would contain all its multiples.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Show that the union of $P$ and $L_2$ (all vectors that are in $P$ or in $L_2$ or in both) is not a subspace, by finding a vector of $P$ and a vector of $L_2$ whose sum is in neither.

This part has no answer box. Write your argument, then open the solution and mark yourself.

<Hint>

Take the direction vector of $L_2$, and any nonzero vector of $P$ that is not on $L_2$. Then test the sum against both sets.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A union is closed under multiples but, as Strang shows for a plane and a line, usually not under addition (Lecture 6 transcript, pp. 3–4).

<Step title="A sum that escapes">

$v = (1, 0, 1)$ is in $P$, since $1 + 0 - 1 = 0$, and $w = (1, 2, 1)$ is in $L_2$. Their sum is $v + w = (2, 2, 2)$. It is not in $P$: $2 + 4 - 2 = 4 \neq 0$. It is not in $L_2$ either: a multiple $c(1, 2, 1)$ with first component $2$ has $c = 2$ and second component $4$, not $2$. So the union is not closed under addition and is not a subspace.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Any choice works as long as $v$ is in $P$ but not on $L_2$ (and $w \neq 0$): if $v + w$ were in $P$, then $w = (v + w) - v$ would be in $P$ too, which it is not by part (b); if $v + w$ were on $L_2$, then $v = (v + w) - w$ would be on $L_2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

<Exercise id="p2" title="Column space and nullspace by eye" points={16}>

Take the $4 \times 3$ matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & -3 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, whose third column is a combination of the first two.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the vector in $N(A)$ whose first component is $1$. Then describe $N(A)$ and $C(A)$.

<Hint>

Find numbers $c$ and $d$ with column 3 $= c\,$(column 1) $+ d\,$(column 2): the second row gives $d$ at once. Then move column 3 to the other side.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Columns 1 and 2 are not multiples of each other. So the only way to combine the three columns into zero is the one you found, up to a multiple.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a-x2" label="Its second component is" value="-2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-a-x2" />

<Answer id="p2-a-x3" label="Its third component is" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-a-x3" />

<Answer id="p2-a-n" label="The nullspace $N(A)$ is"
  choices={["a line through the origin in R³", "a plane through the origin in R³", "a line through the origin in R⁴", "a plane through the origin in R⁴"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p2-a-c" label="The column space $C(A)$ is"
  choices={["a line through the origin in R⁴", "a plane through the origin in R³", "a plane through the origin in R⁴", "every vector of R⁴, the whole space"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A dependence among the columns is a vector in the nullspace: $Ax$ is $x_1$ times column 1 plus $x_2$ times column 2 plus $x_3$ times column 3.

<Step title="The dependence">

The second row says column 3 $= c\,$(column 1) $+ d\,$(column 2) needs $d = -2$; the first row then gives $c + 2(-2) = -3$, so $c = 1$. Check on all four rows: $(1, 0, 2, 1) - 2(2, 1, 1, -1) = (-3, -2, 0, 3)$, which is column 3. So column 1 minus 2 times column 2 minus column 3 is zero:

<Formula label="A nullspace vector" tex="A\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - 4 + 3 \\ 0 - 2 + 2 \\ 2 - 2 + 0 \\ 1 + 2 - 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 16–18 (the nullspace by eye; the numbers are our own)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two subspaces">

Any $x$ in $N(A)$ gives $x_1\,$(column 1) $+ x_2\,$(column 2) $+ x_3\,$(column 1 $- 2\,$column 2) $= 0$, that is $(x_1 + x_3)\,$(column 1) $+ (x_2 - 2x_3)\,$(column 2) $= 0$. Columns 1 and 2 are not multiples of each other, so $x_1 = -x_3$ and $x_2 = 2x_3$: $x = -x_3(1, -2, -1)$. So $N(A)$ is the line of multiples of $(1, -2, -1)$ in $\mathrm{R}^3$. Column 3 adds nothing to the combinations of columns 1 and 2, so $C(A)$ is the plane of combinations of $(1, 0, 2, 1)$ and $(2, 1, 1, -1)$ in $\mathrm{R}^4$, like the example of the lecture (Lecture 6 transcript, pp. 13–15).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The nullspace has three components and lives in $\mathrm{R}^3$, the column space has four and lives in $\mathrm{R}^4$: $n = 3$ and $m = 4$ (p. 16). Two vectors cannot fill $\mathrm{R}^4$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Which of these four vectors is in $C(A)$?

<Hint>

Write $b = x_1\,$(column 1) $+ x_2\,$(column 2). The second row gives $x_2$, the first row then gives $x_1$, and the last two rows test the result.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="The vector in $C(A)$ is"
  choices={["$(1, 1, 1, 1)$", "$(0, 1, -2, -2)$", "$(2, 0, 4, 3)$", "$(3, 1, 3, 0)$"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $Ax = b$ is solvable exactly when $b$ is in $C(A)$ (Lecture 6 transcript, pp. 12–13), and by part (a) only columns 1 and 2 are needed.

<Step title="Test each vector">

With $b = x_1(1, 0, 2, 1) + x_2(2, 1, 1, -1)$, row 2 says $x_2 = b_2$ and row 1 says $x_1 = b_1 - 2b_2$; then rows 3 and 4 must agree.

- $(1, 1, 1, 1)$: $x_2 = 1$, $x_1 = -1$, row 3 gives $-2 + 1 = -1 \neq 1$. Not in $C(A)$.
- $(0, 1, -2, -2)$: $x_2 = 1$, $x_1 = -2$, row 3 gives $-4 + 1 = -3 \neq -2$. Not in $C(A)$.
- $(2, 0, 4, 3)$: $x_2 = 0$, $x_1 = 2$, row 3 gives $4$ (right) but row 4 gives $2 \neq 3$. Not in $C(A)$.
- $(3, 1, 3, 0)$: $x_2 = 1$, $x_1 = 1$, row 3 gives $2 + 1 = 3$ and row 4 gives $1 - 1 = 0$. In $C(A)$: it is column 1 plus column 2.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$A(1, 1, 0) = (1 + 2, 0 + 1, 2 + 1, 1 - 1) = (3, 1, 3, 0)$. The vector $(2, 0, 4, 3)$ shows why every row must be tested: it passes three of the four.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Answer id="p3-a-free" label="The free variables are"
  choices={["$x_3$, $x_4$ and $x_5$", "$x_2$ and $x_3$ only", "$x_2$, $x_4$ and $x_5$", "$x_1$, $x_2$ and $x_4$"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Elimination on a rectangular matrix: when a column has no pivot, go on to the next column.

<Step title="Eliminate">

Subtract 3 times row 1 from row 2 and 2 times row 1 from row 3. Both new rows start with two zeros: $(0, 0, 2, 4, -2)$ and $(0, 0, -2, -4, 2)$. Column 2 has no pivot, so move to column 3: the second pivot is $2$. Adding the new row 2 to row 3 gives a zero row:

<Formula label="The echelon form" tex="U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 & 4 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3 (the method; the numbers are our own)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Rank and free variables">

There are two pivots, so $r = 2$. They sit in columns 1 and 3, so $x_1$ and $x_3$ are pivot variables and $x_2$, $x_4$, $x_5$ are free: $n - r = 5 - 2 = 3$ of them (Lecture 7 transcript, pp. 4, 9).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The zero row says row 3 of $A$ is a combination of rows 1 and 2. Indeed $5\,$(row 1) $-$ (row 2) $= (5 - 3, 10 - 6, 5 - 5, 5 - 7, 10 - 4) = (2, 4, 0, -2, 6)$, which is row 3. Column 2 is twice column 1, which is why it has no pivot.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Step title="The equations">

<Formula label="Ux = 0 written out" tex="x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3 + x_4 + 2x_5 = 0, \qquad 2x_3 + 4x_4 - 2x_5 = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 5 (the method; the numbers are our own)" />

The nullspace is all their combinations (Lecture 7 transcript, pp. 7–8).

</Step>

</Step>

</Solution>
