## What you need from the lessons

**Matrix spaces** (Lecture 11). Matrices of one shape can be added and multiplied by numbers, so they form a vector space, and basis and dimension work as in $\mathrm{R}^n$. The $3 \times 3$ matrices form a space $M$ of dimension 9, with the nine matrices that have a single 1 as a basis; the upper triangular $3 \times 3$ matrices form a subspace $U$ of dimension 6 (transcript, pp. 1–4). For two subspaces $V$ and $W$ of one space, the intersection (the matrices in both) is a subspace, and so is the sum $V + W$, which holds all $v + w$ with $v$ in $V$ and $w$ in $W$ (pp. 4–6). Their dimensions fit:

<Formula label="Dimensions of intersection and sum" tex="\text{dim}\, V + \text{dim}\, W = \text{dim}(V \text{ intersect } W) + \text{dim}(V + W)" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

The solutions of a linear differential equation with zero right-hand side form a vector space too, a nullspace: for $y'' + y = 0$ the basis is $\cos x$, $\sin x$ (pp. 7–9).

**Rank one** (Lecture 11). Every matrix of rank one is a column times a row, and ranks of sums obey an inequality:

<Formula label="Rank one and the rank of a sum" tex="A = uv^T, \qquad \operatorname{rank}(A + B) \le \operatorname{rank}(A) + \operatorname{rank}(B)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–13" />

**Graphs** (Lecture 12). A graph has $n$ nodes and $m$ edges, each with a direction. Its $m \times n$ incidence matrix $A$ has one row per edge: $-1$ in the column of the node the edge leaves and $+1$ in the column of the node it enters (transcript, pp. 3–5). For potentials $x$ at the nodes, $Ax$ lists the potential differences along the edges (p. 7), so for a connected graph $N(A)$ is the line of constant vectors $c(1, 1, \dots, 1)$ and the rank is $n - 1$ (pp. 8–10). For currents $y$ on the edges, $A^Ty = 0$ is Kirchhoff's current law: at every node the current in equals the current out (pp. 14–15). Its solutions are the currents around loops, and

<Formula label="Loops and Euler's formula" tex="\text{loops} = \text{dim}\, N(A^T) = m - r, \qquad \text{nodes} - \text{edges} + \text{loops} = 1" cite="reading2" at="pp. 16–21" />

Independent rows of $A$ belong to edges that form a tree: a graph with no loop (pp. 19–20). With conductances on the diagonal of $C$ and current sources $f$ at the nodes, the three steps of a network combine into one equation (pp. 22–23), whose matrix is symmetric (p. 24):

<Formula label="The network equation" tex="e = Ax, \qquad y = Ce, \qquad A^Ty = f \qquad \Rightarrow \qquad A^TCAx = f" cite="reading2" at="pp. 22–23" />

**Orthogonality** (Lecture 14). Two vectors are orthogonal when their dot product is zero, and Pythagoras says the same thing:

<Formula label="Orthogonal vectors" tex="x^Ty = x_1y_1 + \cdots + x_ny_n = 0 \iff \|x\|^2 + \|y\|^2 = \|x + y\|^2, \qquad \|x\|^2 = x^Tx" cite="reading3" at="pp. 2–6" />

Two subspaces $S$ and $T$ are orthogonal when every vector of $S$ is orthogonal to every vector of $T$ (p. 8). $Ax = 0$ says that $x$ is orthogonal to every row of $A$, and so to every combination of the rows (pp. 10–12):

<Formula label="The nullspace is orthogonal to the row space" tex="Ax = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (c_1\,\text{row}_1 + c_2\,\text{row}_2 + \cdots)^T x = 0" cite="reading3" at="pp. 10–12" />

In the same way the column space is orthogonal to the left nullspace (p. 12). More than that: the nullspace holds **all** vectors orthogonal to the row space, and the left nullspace all vectors orthogonal to the column space. They are orthogonal complements, with dimensions $r$ and $n - r$ in $\mathrm{R}^n$, and $r$ and $m - r$ in $\mathrm{R}^m$ (pp. 13–15). Finally, $A^TA$ is square and symmetric, and

<Formula label="Nullspace of AᵀA" tex="N(A^TA) = N(A), \qquad \operatorname{rank}(A^TA) = \operatorname{rank}(A)" cite="reading3" at="p. 23" />

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Spaces of matrices and of functions" points={16}>

In the space $M$ of all $3 \times 3$ matrices, take three subspaces: the skew-symmetric matrices $K$ (those with $A^T = -A$), the matrices $Z$ whose three rows each add up to zero, and the upper triangular matrices $U$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the dimension of $K$ and the dimension of $Z$.

<Hint>

Count the entries you may choose freely. For $K$, start with the diagonal: what does $A^T = -A$ say about $a_{11}$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $Z$, each row is a vector of $\mathrm{R}^3$ with one condition on it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-k" label="$\text{dim}\, K =$" value="3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-k" />

<Answer id="p1-a-z" label="$\text{dim}\, Z =$" value="6" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-z" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A dimension is the number of free choices; write the general matrix of each subspace.

<Step title="Skew-symmetric">

$A^T = -A$ says $a_{ji} = -a_{ij}$. On the diagonal this gives $a_{ii} = -a_{ii}$, so $a_{ii} = 0$; above the diagonal the three entries are free and fix the three below:

<Formula label="The general skew-symmetric matrix" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 0 & p & q \\ -p & 0 & s \\ -q & -s & 0 \end{pmatrix} = p\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + q\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + s\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 2–4 (counting free entries, as for the symmetric matrices; the subspace is our own)" />

The three matrices on the right are a basis, so $\text{dim}\, K = 3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Rows adding to zero">

In each row the first two entries are free and the third is minus their sum: $2$ free entries per row, $3 \cdot 2 = 6$ in all. So $\text{dim}\, Z = 6$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$Z$ is the set of matrices with $A(1, 1, 1) = 0$: nine entries with three conditions, $9 - 3 = 6$. And $K$ together with the symmetric matrices, which have $3 + 3 = 6$ free entries, fills $M$: $3 + 6 = 9 = \text{dim}\, M$, since every matrix is half of $A + A^T$ plus half of $A - A^T$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the dimension of the intersection $Z \text{ intersect } U$ and the dimension of the sum $Z + U$.

<Hint>

Write a general upper triangular matrix and ask each of its rows to add up to zero. Start from the last row.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b-int" label="$\text{dim}(Z \text{ intersect } U) =$" value="3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-b-int" />

<Answer id="p1-b-sum" label="$\text{dim}(Z + U) =$" value="9" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-b-sum" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the intersection by putting both conditions together; then the dimension formula gives the sum.

<Step title="The intersection">

An upper triangular matrix has rows $(a_{11}, a_{12}, a_{13})$, $(0, a_{22}, a_{23})$ and $(0, 0, a_{33})$. Row sums zero: $a_{33} = 0$; $a_{23} = -a_{22}$ with $a_{22}$ free; $a_{13} = -a_{11} - a_{12}$ with $a_{11}$, $a_{12}$ free. Three free entries:

<Formula label="Upper triangular with zero row sums" tex="\begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} & -a_{11} - a_{12} \\ 0 & a_{22} & -a_{22} \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 4–5 (an intersection of subspaces; the subspaces are our own)" />

So $\text{dim}(Z \text{ intersect } U) = 3$.

</Step>

<Step title="The sum">

<Formula label="Dimension of the sum" tex="\text{dim}(Z + U) = \text{dim}\, Z + \text{dim}\, U - \text{dim}(Z \text{ intersect } U) = 6 + 6 - 3 = 9" cite="reading1" at="p. 7 (the formula; the numbers are our own)" />

$Z + U$ is all of $M$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Directly: any matrix of $M$ is its lower part (below the diagonal) plus the rest. The lower part with suitable diagonal entries has zero row sums: for example $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 4 & -4 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$ is in $Z$, and what is left over is upper triangular. So every matrix is in $Z + U$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the dimension of $K \text{ intersect } U$. Is the union of $K$ and $U$ (the matrices that are in $K$, in $U$, or in both) a subspace of $M$?

<Hint>

A skew-symmetric matrix that is upper triangular has zeros below the diagonal. What does skew-symmetry then say about the entries above it, and about the diagonal?

</Hint>

<Hint>

A subspace must contain the sum of any two of its members. Try one matrix from $K$ and one from $U$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c-int" label="$\text{dim}(K \text{ intersect } U) =$" value="0" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-c-int" />

<Answer id="p1-c-union" label="The union of $K$ and $U$:"
  choices={["No, since a sum of two of its matrices can fall outside", "No, since the zero matrix does not belong to it", "Yes, since K and U are both subspaces of the space M", "Yes, since it holds every multiple of each of its matrices"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two conditions together leave nothing free; a union fails the test of sums.

<Step title="The intersection">

Upper triangular means $a_{ij} = 0$ below the diagonal. Skew-symmetry then gives $a_{ij} = -a_{ji} = 0$ above it as well, and the diagonal of a skew-symmetric matrix is zero anyway. Only the zero matrix is left: $K \text{ intersect } U$ is the zero subspace, of dimension $0$ (its basis is empty).

</Step>

<Step title="The union">

Take $k = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ in $K$ and the identity $I$ in $U$. Their sum $k + I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has a nonzero entry below the diagonal, so it is not in $U$, and a nonzero diagonal, so it is not in $K$. The union is not closed under addition and is not a subspace, like two lines in a plane (Lecture 11 transcript, p. 5). It does contain the zero matrix and all multiples of its matrices; that is not enough.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The formula of "What you need" gives $\text{dim}(K + U) = 3 + 6 - 0 = 9$: every matrix is a skew-symmetric matrix plus an upper triangular one, in exactly one way, since a second split would differ by a matrix in $K \text{ intersect } U$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Functions $y(x)$ can be added and multiplied by numbers too. Find the dimension of the space of solutions of $y'' + 4y = 0$ and of the space of solutions of $\frac{d^3y}{dx^3} = 0$. Then decide whether the solutions of $y'' + 4y = 8$ form a vector space.

<Hint>

Look for solutions of $y'' + 4y = 0$ among sines and cosines. Which functions have a third derivative that is zero?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Every vector space contains a zero vector. Here that is the function that is zero for every $x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d-osc" label="The dimension of the solution space of $y'' + 4y = 0$ is" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-d-osc" />

<Answer id="p1-d-cubic" label="The dimension of the solution space of $\frac{d^3y}{dx^3} = 0$ is" value="3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-d-cubic" />

<Answer id="p1-d-inhom" label="The solutions of $y'' + 4y = 8$:"
  choices={["Yes, because the sum of two solutions is again a solution", "No, because it has no solution at all", "No, because the zero function is not a solution", "Yes, because it is a linear equation in y"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A linear equation with zero on the right has a nullspace of solutions; with a nonzero right-hand side it does not, just as for $Ax = b$.

<Step title="y'' + 4y = 0">

$\cos 2x$ and $\sin 2x$ are solutions: their second derivatives are $-4\cos 2x$ and $-4\sin 2x$. Every solution is a combination of the two, and neither is a multiple of the other, so they are a basis:

<Formula label="Solutions of the first equation" tex="y = c_1\cos 2x + c_2\sin 2x" cite="reading1" at="p. 8 (the solution space as a nullspace; the equation is our own)" />

The dimension is $2$, as for every linear second-order equation of this kind (Lecture 11 transcript, pp. 8–9).

</Step>

<Step title="The third derivative zero">

Integrating three times: $y'' = c_3$, $y' = c_3x + c_2$, $y = \frac12c_3x^2 + c_2x + c_1$. The solutions are the polynomials of degree at most 2, with basis $1$, $x$, $x^2$: dimension $3$.

</Step>

<Step title="y'' + 4y = 8">

The constant $y = 2$ is a solution ($0 + 8 = 8$), so there are solutions. But the zero function gives $0 + 0 = 0 \neq 8$, so it is not one, and the solutions are not a vector space. Indeed $2 + 2 = 4$ gives $0 + 16 = 16 \neq 8$: sums of solutions are not solutions. The solutions are $2 + c_1\cos 2x + c_2\sin 2x$, a shifted copy of the first space, like the solutions of $Ax = b$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$y = 3\cos 2x - \sin 2x$: $y'' = -12\cos 2x + 4\sin 2x = -4y$. And $y = x^2$ has $\frac{d^3y}{dx^3} = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Rank-one matrices and the rank of a sum" points={14}>

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Write

<Formula label="A matrix of rank one" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 & -2 \\ 12 & 4 & -8 \\ -3 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11 (a rank-one matrix; the matrix is our own)" />

as $A = uv^T$ with $u = (u_1, u_2, u_3)$ and $u_1 = 1$. Give $u_2$, $u_3$ and the third component of $v$.

<Hint>

Every row of a rank-one matrix is a multiple of one row. Row $i$ of $uv^T$ is $u_i$ times $v^T$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a-u2" label="$u_2 =$" value="4" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-a-u2" />

<Answer id="p2-a-u3" label="$u_3 =$" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-a-u3" />

<Answer id="p2-a-v3" label="The third component of $v$ is" value="-2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-a-v3" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $u_1 = 1$, the first row of $A$ is $v^T$ itself, and $u$ holds the multiples of that row.

<Step title="The row and the multiples">

Row 1 of $uv^T$ is $u_1v^T = v^T$, so $v = (3, 1, -2)$. Row 2 is $(12, 4, -8) = 4v^T$ and row 3 is $(-3, -1, 2) = -1 \cdot v^T$, so $u = (1, 4, -1)$:

<Formula label="Column times row" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 11 (column times row; the numbers are our own)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Multiply back entry by entry: $a_{ij} = u_iv_j$, for example $a_{23} = 4 \cdot (-2) = -8$ and $a_{32} = (-1) \cdot 1 = -1$. Read by columns instead, every column of $A$ is a multiple of $u$: column 3 is $-2u$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the rank of each of the two sums of rank-one matrices

<Formula label="Two sums of rank-one matrices" tex="B_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–13 (sums of rank-one matrices; the matrices are our own)" />

<Hint>

Compare the row factors of the two terms in each sum. When they are multiples of each other, the two terms can be combined into one.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Or add up the matrices and count the independent rows.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b-1" label="$\operatorname{rank}(B_1) =$" value="1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-b-1" />

<Answer id="p2-b-2" label="$\operatorname{rank}(B_2) =$" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-b-2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The rank of a sum of two rank-one matrices is at most $1 + 1 = 2$; it drops to 1 when the two terms share a row (or a column) direction.

<Step title="B₁">

The second row factor $(2, 0, 4)$ is twice the first $(1, 0, 2)$, so

<Formula label="B₁ in one term" tex="B_1 = \left[\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + 2\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\right]\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 0 & 6 \\ 2 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

a column times a row: rank 1.

</Step>

<Step title="B₂">

<Formula label="B₂ added up" tex="B_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 13 (the rank of a sum; the numbers are our own)" />

Row 2 is row 1 plus row 3, and rows 1 and 3 are independent (look at their first two entries). So the rank is 2, the most the inequality allows.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Every column of $B_2$ is a combination of $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$, so its column space is the plane they span: dimension 2. The two sums also show that the matrices of rank at most 1 are not a subspace: $B_2$ is a sum of two of them and has rank 2.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

$A$ and $B$ are $4 \times 6$ matrices with $\operatorname{rank}(A) = 2$ and $\operatorname{rank}(B) = 3$. What is the largest possible rank of $A + B$, and what is the smallest possible rank?

<Hint>

The inequality of "What you need" is one limit for the largest rank. The shape of the matrix is another.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the smallest rank, apply the same inequality to $B = (A + B) + (-A)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c-max" label="The largest possible rank of $A + B$ is" value="4" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-c-max" />

<Answer id="p2-c-min" label="The smallest possible rank of $A + B$ is" value="1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-c-min" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two inequalities bound the rank; then an example shows that each bound is reached.

<Step title="Largest">

The inequality allows $\operatorname{rank}(A + B) \le 2 + 3 = 5$. But a $4 \times 6$ matrix has only 4 rows, so its rank is at most 4. The largest possible rank is $4$, if an example reaches it.

</Step>

<Step title="Smallest">

<Formula label="A lower bound for the rank of a sum" tex="\operatorname{rank}(B) = \operatorname{rank}\big((A + B) + (-A)\big) \le \operatorname{rank}(A + B) + \operatorname{rank}(A) \;\Rightarrow\; \operatorname{rank}(A + B) \ge 3 - 2 = 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

($-A$ has the same rank as $A$.) The smallest possible rank is $1$, if an example reaches it.

</Step>

<Step title="Two examples">

Write $E_{ij}$ for the $4 \times 6$ matrix with a single 1 in row $i$, column $j$. Take $A = E_{11} + E_{22}$, of rank 2. With $B = -E_{11} - E_{22} + E_{33}$, of rank 3, the sum $A + B = E_{33}$ has rank 1. With $B = E_{33} + E_{44} + E_{15}$, of rank 3, the sum $A + B = E_{11} + E_{15} + E_{22} + E_{33} + E_{44}$ has a nonzero entry in a new column of each of its 4 rows, so its rank is 4.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Between the bounds every rank occurs too: $B = -E_{11} + E_{33} + E_{44}$ gives $A + B = E_{22} + E_{33} + E_{44}$, of rank 3. In Lecture 11 Strang asks whether the matrices of rank at most 4 among the $5 \times 17$ matrices form a subspace: no, because the sum of two of them can have rank 5; the inequality only caps that sum at 8, and the shape caps it at 5 (transcript, pp. 12–13).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A graph with five nodes and seven edges" points={20}>

A graph has $n = 5$ nodes and $m = 7$ edges, with these directions:

- edge 1 from node 1 to node 2, edge 2 from node 1 to node 3, edge 3 from node 2 to node 3,
- edge 4 from node 2 to node 4, edge 5 from node 3 to node 5, edge 6 from node 4 to node 5,
- edge 7 from node 2 to node 5.

Let $A$ be its $7 \times 5$ incidence matrix.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Write down $A$ (this has no box). For the potentials $x = (0, 2, 5, 3, 4)$ at the five nodes, find entry 5 and entry 7 of $Ax$.

<Hint>

Row 5 of $A$ belongs to edge 5. It has $-1$ in the column of the node the edge leaves and $+1$ in the column of the node it enters, so row 5 times $x$ is a difference of two potentials.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a-5" label="Entry 5 of $Ax$ is" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-a-5" />

$(2, 1, 3)$ is $2$ times column 1 minus $3$ times column 3: $(2, 4, 6) - (0, 3, 3) = (2, 1, 3)$. And $Bx = (1, 0, 2)$ has no solution: row 1 plus row 2 minus row 3 of the equations gives $0 = 1 + 0 - 2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 6 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$, find the entry of $A^TA$ in row 1, column 2, and the nonzero vector $x$ with $A^TAx = 0$ whose first component is $3$: give its second component.

<Hint>

Entry $(i, j)$ of $A^TA$ is column $i$ of $A$ times column $j$. For the second question, use $N(A^TA) = N(A)$: look at the columns of $A$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-d-12" label="Entry $(1, 2)$ of $A^TA$ is" value="18" tolerance="0.01" calc="p6-d-12" />

<Answer id="p6-d-x2" label="The second component of $x$ is" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p6-d-x2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $A$ has rank one, so $A^TA$ has rank one too, with the same nullspace.

<Step title="The product">

The columns of $A$ are $(1, 2, 1)$ and $(3, 6, 3)$:

<Formula label="AᵀA for a rank-one matrix" tex="A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + 4 + 1 & 3 + 12 + 3 \\ 3 + 12 + 3 & 9 + 36 + 9 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 18 \\ 18 & 54 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading3" at="pp. 21–23 (AᵀA and its rank; the matrix is our own)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The nullspace">

Column 2 of $A$ is 3 times column 1, so $A(3, -1) = 3(1, 2, 1) - (3, 6, 3) = 0$. Since $N(A^TA) = N(A)$, the vector is $x = (3, -1)$, with second component $-1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$A^TA(3, -1) = (18 - 18, 54 - 54) = (0, 0)$. $A^TA$ is symmetric and singular, of rank 1 like $A$: the second row is 3 times the first.

</Step>

</Solution>
