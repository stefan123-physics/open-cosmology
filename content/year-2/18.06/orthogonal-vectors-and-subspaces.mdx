A new chapter, about **orthogonality**: of vectors, of subspaces, of bases (transcript, p. 1). The big picture of the four subspaces already has their dimensions, $r$ and $n - r$ in $\mathrm{R}^n$, $r$ and $m - r$ in $\mathrm{R}^m$. Now it turns out that the two subspaces on each side meet at **ninety degrees** (p. 1). It continues [the four fundamental subspaces](/courses/18-06/four-fundamental-subspaces), and uses the [dot product](/courses/18-02/dot-product).

## Orthogonal vectors

Orthogonal means perpendicular: in $n$ dimensions, the angle between the vectors is ninety degrees (transcript, p. 2). The test is the dot product, written as a row times a column (p. 2):

<Formula label="Test for orthogonality" tex="x^Ty = x_1y_1 + x_2y_2 + \cdots + x_ny_n = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

**Why, from Pythagoras** (pp. 3–6). For a right triangle with sides $x$, $y$ and hypotenuse $x + y$, $\|x\|^2 + \|y\|^2 = \|x + y\|^2$, which is not true for other triangles (p. 3). The length squared of a vector is $x^Tx = x_1^2 + \cdots + x_n^2$, positive unless $x = 0$; for $x = (1, 2, 3)$ it is $1 + 4 + 9 = 14$ (p. 4). (Strang says the length is fourteen; the length squared is 14, the length $\sqrt{14}$.) Take $y = (2, -1, 0)$, with $x^Ty = 0$: then $\|y\|^2 = 5$, $x + y = (3, 1, 3)$ has $\|x + y\|^2 = 19$, and indeed $14 + 5 = 19$ (pp. 4–5). In letters (pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="Pythagoras gives the dot product test" tex="x^Tx + y^Ty = (x + y)^T(x + y) = x^Tx + y^Ty + x^Ty + y^Tx \;\Rightarrow\; 2x^Ty = 0" cite="reading1" at="pp. 5–6" />

since $x^Ty = y^Tx$ for real vectors (p. 5). The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector (pp. 6–7).

## Orthogonal subspaces

<Formula label="Orthogonal subspaces" tex="S \perp T: \quad s^Tt = 0 \text{ for every } s \text{ in } S \text{ and every } t \text{ in } T" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

Subspace $S$ is orthogonal to subspace $T$ when **every vector in $S$ is orthogonal to every vector in $T$** (transcript, pp. 7–8). Are the blackboard plane and the floor plane (with the origin at a corner) orthogonal? **No**: a vector along the line where they meet lies in both, and it is not orthogonal to itself (pp. 8–9). Orthogonal subspaces meet only at zero, and need more than that (p. 9). In the plane: a line through the origin is never orthogonal to the whole plane, always orthogonal to the zero subspace, and orthogonal to another line exactly when they meet at ninety degrees (pp. 9–10).

## The row space is orthogonal to the nullspace

All that is known about $x$ in the nullspace is $Ax = 0$ (transcript, p. 10). Written out by rows (pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="Ax = 0 row by row" tex="\begin{pmatrix} \text{row } 1 \\ \text{row } 2 \\ \cdots \\ \text{row } m \end{pmatrix} x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \cdots \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11" />

each equation says that a row dotted with $x$ is zero: **$x$ is orthogonal to every row**. It is then orthogonal to every combination of rows, the whole row space (pp. 11–12):

<Formula label="Orthogonal to combinations" tex="(c_1\,\text{row}_1 + c_2\,\text{row}_2 + \cdots)^T x = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 12" />

The same argument for $A^T$ shows that the **column space is orthogonal to the left nullspace** (p. 12). So $\mathrm{R}^n$ is cut into two perpendicular subspaces, and so is $\mathrm{R}^m$ (pp. 12–13).

**Orthogonal complements** (pp. 13–15). Two perpendicular lines in $\mathrm{R}^3$ cannot be the row space and nullspace of a matrix, because $r + (n - r)$ must be 3 (p. 13). Take the rank-one matrix (p. 13)

<Formula label="A rank-one example" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 5 \\ 2 & 4 & 10 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

Its row space is the line through $(1, 2, 5)$; its nullspace has dimension $3 - 1 = 2$, the **plane perpendicular to $(1, 2, 5)$**. The second row changes nothing: the single equation $x_1 + 2x_2 + 5x_3 = 0$ gives the plane, and $(1, 2, 5)$ is the normal vector from calculus (pp. 14–15). So the nullspace contains **all** vectors perpendicular to the row space, not just some: the two are **orthogonal complements** in $\mathrm{R}^n$ (p. 15). Strang calls this part of the fundamental theorem of linear algebra (the dimensions being one part, orthogonality another, and orthogonal bases still to come) (p. 16).

## Coming next: solving Ax = b with no solution

The main problem of the chapter is to "solve" $Ax = b$ when there is no solution, $b$ not being in the column space (transcript, pp. 16–17). This is done all the time: a thousand measurements of a satellite with six unknown parameters, or a pulse rate measured many times, give too many equations, with noise in $b$ but information too (p. 17). Throwing away equations until the system is square is not satisfactory: all measurements should be used (p. 18). The key matrix is $A^TA$, and the central equation of the chapter, for a best solution $\hat x$, will be (pp. 19–21)

<Formula label="The central equation" tex="A^TA\hat x = A^Tb" cite="reading1" at="pp. 20–21" />

$A^TA$ is **square** ($n \times n$) and **symmetric**, since $(A^TA)^T = A^TA$ (pp. 19–20). For three equations in two unknowns (pp. 21–22):

<Formula label="An example of AᵀA" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 8 \\ 8 & 30 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 21–22" />

which is invertible. But not always: Strang changes $A$ to a rank-one matrix and finds entries 9, 9 and 27, a singular $A^TA$ (pp. 22–23). The transcript does not read out the new $A$; keeping the first column $(1, 1, 1)$, the only second column that fits is $(3, 3, 3)$, giving $A^TA$ with entries 3, 9, 9 and 27. A product involving a rank-one matrix cannot have rank above one (p. 23). In general (p. 23):

<Formula label="Nullspace and rank of AᵀA" tex="N(A^TA) = N(A), \qquad \operatorname{rank}(A^TA) = \operatorname{rank}(A)" cite="reading1" at="p. 23" />

so **$A^TA$ is invertible exactly when the columns of $A$ are independent** (p. 23). Strang promises the reason as soon as he can; the proof comes in Lecture 16.

<WorkedExample title="Orthogonality of your own">
<Problem>
(a) For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, find a basis of the nullspace and check that it is orthogonal to the row space. (b) Check Pythagoras for $x = (2, 1, 2)$ and $y = (1, 0, -1)$. (c) Is $A^TA$ invertible for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Nullspace">
The rank is 1; the one equation is $x_1 - x_2 + 2x_3 = 0$, with free $x_2$, $x_3$. Special solutions: $(1, 1, 0)$ and $(-2, 0, 1)$. Both are orthogonal to $(1, -1, 2)$: $1 - 1 + 0 = 0$ and $-2 + 0 + 2 = 0$. Dimensions $1 + 2 = 3$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A right triangle">
$x^Ty = 2 + 0 - 2 = 0$. $\|x\|^2 = 9$, $\|y\|^2 = 2$, and $x + y = (3, 1, 1)$ has $\|x + y\|^2 = 11 = 9 + 2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) AᵀA">
$A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 4 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $30 - 16 = 14 \ne 0$: invertible, as it must be, since the columns of $A$ are independent.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $x$ and $y$ are orthogonal when $x^Ty = 0$, which is Pythagoras in disguise; $\|x\|^2 = x^Tx$.
- Subspaces are orthogonal when every vector of one is orthogonal to every vector of the other.
- The row space and nullspace are orthogonal complements in $\mathrm{R}^n$; the column space and left nullspace in $\mathrm{R}^m$.
- $A^TA$ is square, symmetric, with $N(A^TA) = N(A)$; invertible exactly when $A$ has independent columns. Next: $A^TA\hat x = A^Tb$.
