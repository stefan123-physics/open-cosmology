This lecture is the lift-off on differential equations: systems of first-order linear equations with constant coefficients (transcript, p. 1). Their solutions are **exponentials**, and finding what goes in the exponent and what multiplies it is linear algebra. Everything runs parallel to [powers of a matrix](/courses/18-06/diagonalization-and-powers), with exponentials in place of powers (p. 1). The same subject from the differential-equations side is in 18.03's [first-order systems](/courses/18-03/eigenvalues-linear-systems).

## Strang's example

Two unknowns $u_1, u_2$, everything in $u_1$ at the start (p. 1):

<Formula label="The system" tex="\frac{du}{dt} = Au, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad u(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Because of the $u_1$ term, $du_2/dt$ is positive at first: the flow moves out of $u_1$ into $u_2$ (p. 1). First job, before anything else: the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A$ (pp. 1–2).

$A$ is singular (its second column is $-2$ times the first), so one eigenvalue is $0$. The trace is $-3$, so the other is $-3$ (p. 2). The characteristic polynomial confirms it (p. 2):

<Formula label="Eigenvalues" tex="\det(A - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 + 3\lambda = \lambda(\lambda + 3), \qquad \lambda_1 = 0,\ \lambda_2 = -3" cite="reading1" at="pp. 2–3" />

The eigenvalues already say a lot (p. 3). The answer will have an $e^{-3t}$ part, which becomes very small, and an $e^{0t} = 1$ part, which does not move: a **steady state**, which comes from the zero eigenvalue. The eigenvectors are $x_1 = (2, 1)$, in the nullspace of $A$, and $x_2 = (1, -1)$, in the nullspace of $A + 3I$ (pp. 4–5).

## Pure exponential solutions

Each eigenvalue gives a pure exponential solution, and the general solution combines them (p. 5):

<Formula label="General solution" tex="u(t) = c_1e^{\lambda_1t}x_1 + c_2e^{\lambda_2t}x_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

To check $u = e^{\lambda_1t}x_1$: the time derivative brings down $\lambda_1$, so the left side is $\lambda_1e^{\lambda_1t}x_1$, and the right side is $Ae^{\lambda_1t}x_1 = e^{\lambda_1t}\lambda_1x_1$ because $Ax_1 = \lambda_1x_1$ (p. 6). These pure exponentials are the analogue of last lecture's pure powers $c_1\lambda_1^kx_1 + c_2\lambda_2^kx_2$ for $u_{k+1} = Au_k$ (pp. 6–7).

In the example, $e^{0t} = 1$ (p. 7):

<Formula label="Example solution" tex="u(t) = c_1\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + c_2e^{-3t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

The constants come from the initial condition. At $t = 0$: $c_1(2, 1) + c_2(1, -1) = (1, 0)$. With both $c$'s equal to 1 the sum is $(3, 0)$, so $c_1 = c_2 = \frac13$ (pp. 7–8). As $t \to \infty$ the second part disappears (p. 8):

<Formula label="Steady state" tex="u(\infty) = \frac13\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1/3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

The equations for $c$ are $Sc = u(0)$, with the eigenvector matrix $S$ (pp. 13–14; Strang comes back to this after the stability test below). Get each pure exponential's amount right at the start, and each goes its own way for ever (p. 14).

## Stability, steady state, blow-up

Not every system has a steady state; solutions may go to zero or blow up, and the eigenvalues decide (pp. 8–9). Eigenvalues can be complex, so take $\lambda = -3 + 6i$ (pp. 9–10):

<Formula label="Size of a complex exponential" tex="\left|e^{(-3 + 6i)t}\right| = e^{-3t}\left|e^{6it}\right| = e^{-3t}, \qquad e^{6it} = \cos 6t + i\sin 6t" cite="reading1" at="pp. 9–10" />

$e^{6it}$ runs around the unit circle, with $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$; the imaginary part only makes the solution oscillate, and the **real part** decides (p. 10). So (pp. 9–10):

- **Stability**, $u(t) \to 0$: every eigenvalue has $\text{Re}\,\lambda < 0$.
- **Steady state**: one eigenvalue $\lambda_1 = 0$ and all the others with $\text{Re}\,\lambda < 0$.
- **Blow-up**: some eigenvalue has $\text{Re}\,\lambda > 0$.

Changing $A$ to $-A$ reverses every eigenvalue: the example would get $e^{+3t}$ and blow up, while the zero eigenvalue stays zero (p. 11).

**The $2 \times 2$ test** (pp. 11–13). For $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, stability means both eigenvalues have negative real parts. Their sum is the trace $a + d$, so the trace must be negative (complex eigenvalues come as $\pm$ the same imaginary part, which cancels) (pp. 11–12). That is not enough: $\begin{pmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has trace $-1$ but eigenvalue $+1$, and blows up (p. 12). The second condition is on the determinant, the product of the eigenvalues, which is positive when both are negative (p. 13):

<Formula label="Two by two stability" tex="\operatorname{trace} = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 < 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \det A = \lambda_1\lambda_2 > 0" cite="reading1" at="pp. 12–13" />

## Uncoupling with S

$A$ couples $u_1$ and $u_2$; the eigenvectors uncouple them (p. 14). Set $u = Sv$. Then $S\,dv/dt = ASv$, and multiplying by $S^{-1}$ gives the diagonal system (p. 15):

<Formula label="Uncoupled system" tex="\frac{dv}{dt} = S^{-1}AS\,v = \Lambda v, \qquad \frac{dv_i}{dt} = \lambda_iv_i" cite="reading1" at="p. 15" />

Each equation is solved on its own, $v(t) = e^{\Lambda t}v(0)$, and going back to $u$ (p. 15):

<Formula label="Solution with the exponential" tex="u(t) = Se^{\Lambda t}S^{-1}u(0) = e^{At}u(0)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 15–16" />

## The matrix exponential

What does $e$ to a matrix mean (p. 16)? The power series for $e^x$ works for matrices too, with $I$ for 1 and $At$ for $x$ (pp. 16–17):

<Formula label="Exponential series" tex="e^{At} = I + At + \frac{(At)^2}{2} + \frac{(At)^3}{6} + \cdots + \frac{(At)^n}{n!} + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="pp. 16–17" />

Strang calls it one of the two beautiful Taylor series. The other is the geometric series $1 + x + x^2 + \cdots = 1/(1 - x)$, which for matrices gives (pp. 17–18)

<Formula label="Geometric series" tex="(I - At)^{-1} = I + At + (At)^2 + (At)^3 + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="pp. 17–18" />

For small $t$, $(I - At)^{-1} \approx I + At$ (p. 17). The exponential series is the better one: the factorials make its terms go to zero for **any** $A$ and $t$, so it always converges. The geometric series converges only when all eigenvalues of $At$ are below 1 in size (p. 18).

To connect the series with $S$ and $\Lambda$, write $A = S\Lambda S^{-1}$, so $A^2 = S\Lambda^2S^{-1}$ and so on, and write $I = SS^{-1}$ (pp. 19–20):

<Formula label="Exponential by diagonalisation" tex="e^{At} = S\left(I + \Lambda t + \frac{(\Lambda t)^2}{2} + \cdots\right)S^{-1} = Se^{\Lambda t}S^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 19–20" />

The series always works; this formula assumes $A$ can be diagonalised, with $n$ independent eigenvectors (p. 20). (For the few matrices without, Strang mentions that one can still make it triangular.) The exponential of the diagonal $\Lambda$ is diagonal (p. 21):

<Formula label="Exponential of a diagonal matrix" tex="e^{\Lambda t} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{\lambda_1t} & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & e^{\lambda_nt} \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 21" />

So $e^{At} \to 0$ exactly when every $\text{Re}\,\lambda < 0$ (pp. 21–22). In the complex plane the two stability regions differ (p. 22): for differential equations, the **left half-plane** $\text{Re}\,\lambda < 0$; for powers $A^k$, the **unit disc** $|\lambda| < 1$.

## A second-order equation as a system

One last example: $y'' + by' + ky = 0$ (p. 22). As for Fibonacci, add the trivial equation $y' = y'$ and use the vector $u = (y', y)$ (p. 23):

<Formula label="First-order form" tex="u = \begin{pmatrix} y' \\ y \end{pmatrix}, \qquad u' = \begin{pmatrix} y'' \\ y' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -b & -k \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} y' \\ y \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 23" />

The first row is the equation, the second row the trivial one (p. 23). (Strang says "a single first order equation" at this point; he means the second-order one.) A fifth-order equation becomes a $5 \times 5$ system in the same way: its coefficients (with minus signs, like $-b$ and $-k$) in the top row and four trivial equations giving ones just below the diagonal, the other entries zero (p. 23). The eigenvalues of such a matrix come out naturally connected to the differential equation (pp. 23–24).

<WorkedExample title="A flow of your own">
<Problem>
(a) Solve $du/dt = Au$ with $A = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 3 \\ 1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}$ and $u(0) = (0, 4)$, and find the steady state. (b) Without finding eigenvalues, decide whether $B = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ -3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $C = \begin{pmatrix} -4 & 1 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ are stable.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Eigenvalues and eigenvectors">
$A$ is singular (column 2 is $-3$ times column 1), and the trace is $-4$: $\lambda_1 = 0$, $\lambda_2 = -4$. The nullspace of $A$ holds $x_1 = (3, 1)$; $A + 4I = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $x_2 = (1, -1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Constants and solution">
$c_1(3, 1) + c_2(1, -1) = (0, 4)$: $3c_1 + c_2 = 0$ and $c_1 - c_2 = 4$, so $c_1 = 1$, $c_2 = -3$. Then $u(t) = (3, 1) - 3e^{-4t}(1, -1) = (3 - 3e^{-4t},\ 1 + 3e^{-4t})$. At $t = 0$ this is $(0, 4)$. The total $u_1 + u_2 = 4$ never changes (each column of $A$ adds to zero), and $u \to (3, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Trace and determinant">
$B$: trace $-1 < 0$, determinant $-2 + 3 = 1 > 0$: stable. (Its eigenvalues are complex, $(-1 \pm i\sqrt3)/2$, with real part $-\frac12$.) $C$: trace $-3 < 0$ but determinant $-4 - 2 = -6 < 0$: unstable, with one positive eigenvalue $(-3 + \sqrt{33})/2 \approx 1.37$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $du/dt = Au$: pure solutions $e^{\lambda t}x$; $u(t) = \sum c_ie^{\lambda_it}x_i$ with $Sc = u(0)$.
- Re $\lambda < 0$ for all: decay. One $\lambda = 0$, the rest Re $< 0$: steady state. Any Re $\lambda > 0$: blow-up. For $2 \times 2$: trace $< 0$, det $> 0$.
- $u = Sv$ uncouples the system; $u(t) = e^{At}u(0) = Se^{\Lambda t}S^{-1}u(0)$.
- $e^{At} = I + At + (At)^2/2! + \cdots$ always converges; $e^{\Lambda t}$ is diagonal.
- Powers: stable inside the unit disc. Exponentials: stable in the left half-plane.
