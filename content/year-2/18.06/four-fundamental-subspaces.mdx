This lecture has two jobs: to correct an error from the last lecture, and to complete the picture of the **four subspaces** that come with every matrix (transcript, p. 1). It continues [independence, basis and dimension](/courses/18-06/independence-basis-dimension).

## Correcting Lecture 9

Last time Strang added $(3, 3, 8)$ to $(1, 1, 2)$ and $(2, 2, 5)$, expecting a basis of $\mathrm{R}^3$ (transcript, p. 1). After class a student pointed out that it is not: the matrix with these three columns is **not invertible, because it has two equal rows** (pp. 1–2). Its rows are dependent, so its row space is only two-dimensional, its rank is 2, and so only two of its columns can be independent (p. 2). The rows say something about the columns, which this lecture makes precise (pp. 2, 8).

## The four subspaces

For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ (transcript, pp. 3–5):

- the **column space** $C(A)$, all combinations of the columns;
- the **nullspace** $N(A)$, all solutions of $Ax = 0$;
- the **row space**, all combinations of the rows. To keep column vectors, transpose: it is the column space of $A^T$, written $C(A^T)$ (pp. 3–4);
- the **left nullspace** $N(A^T)$, the nullspace of $A^T$ (p. 4).

The rows span the row space; they are a basis only if they are independent (p. 3). Where do the spaces live? Solutions of $Ax = 0$ and rows of $A$ have $n$ components, columns of $A$ have $m$ (p. 5):

<Formula label="Where the four subspaces live" tex="C(A^T) \text{ and } N(A) \text{ lie in } \mathrm{R}^n; \qquad C(A) \text{ and } N(A^T) \text{ lie in } \mathrm{R}^m" cite="reading1" at="pp. 5–6" />

Understanding a space means having a **basis** and its **dimension** for each of the four (p. 6).

## Their dimensions

The short answers first (transcript, pp. 6–10). For rank $r$:

<Formula label="The four dimensions" tex="\text{dim}\, C(A) = r, \quad \text{dim}\, C(A^T) = r, \quad \text{dim}\, N(A) = n - r, \quad \text{dim}\, N(A^T) = m - r" cite="reading1" at="pp. 7–10" />

The row space and the column space have the **same dimension**, a wonderful fact (p. 8). It explains the correction: the rows of the Lecture 9 matrix plainly span only a plane, so the column space does too (p. 8). In $\mathrm{R}^n$ the dimensions add up, $r + (n - r) = n$, just as $n$ variables split into $r$ pivot and $n - r$ free ones (p. 10). The same rule applied to $A^T$, which has $m$ columns, gives $m - r$ for the left nullspace (p. 10).

## Bases

**Column space** (transcript, p. 7): the $r$ **pivot columns of $A$** (the original $A$, not $R$).

**Nullspace** (pp. 9–10): the **special solutions**, one for each of the $n - r$ free variables.

**Row space** (pp. 11–15). The example from last time:

<Formula label="Row reduction of the example" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \to R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–12" />

Subtract row 1 from rows 2 and 3, multiply row 2 by $-1$, and subtract 2 of it from row 1 (pp. 11–12). The **column space changed**: $(1, 1, 1)$ is in $C(A)$ but not in $C(R)$, whose columns all end in 0 (p. 12). But the **row space did not**: every step replaced a row by a combination of rows, so the rows of $R$ lie in the row space of $A$; and reversing the steps writes the rows of $A$ as combinations of the rows of $R$ (pp. 12–15). So the first $r$ rows of $R$ are a basis, the cleanest possible one (pp. 13–14):

<Formula label="Row space basis" tex="C(A^T) = C(R^T), \quad \text{basis } (1, 0, 1, 1),\; (0, 1, 1, 0)" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

There are $r$ of them, they are independent (each has a 1 where the others have 0), and they span: the dimension is $r$ (p. 14). The first $r$ rows of $A$ itself would not always do (p. 13).

**Left nullspace** (pp. 15–21). It holds the $y$ with $A^Ty = 0$. Transposing puts $y$ on the left, as a row (pp. 15–16):

<Formula label="Why 'left'" tex="A^Ty = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; y^TA = 0^T" cite="reading1" at="pp. 15–16" />

So $N(A^T)$ holds the **combinations of the rows that give the zero row** (p. 20). $R$ does not show them directly; the steps from $A$ to $R$ do. Run **Gauss–Jordan** on $[A\;\;I]$, with $I$ of size $m \times m$ (pp. 16–18):

<Formula label="Gauss–Jordan on a rectangular matrix" tex="\begin{pmatrix} A & I \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} R & E \end{pmatrix}, \qquad EA = R" cite="reading1" at="pp. 17–18" />

$E$ records all the row operations. When $A$ is square and invertible, $R = I$ and so $E = A^{-1}$, as in chapter two (p. 18). For the example (pp. 19–20):

<Formula label="E for the example" tex="E = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 2 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad EA = R" cite="reading1" at="pp. 19–20" />

Here $m - r = 3 - 2 = 1$, so the left nullspace is a line. The last row of $E$ produced the zero row of $R$, so $y = (-1, 0, 1)$ is a basis: $-1$ times row 1 plus row 3 is zero, which could be seen from the start (p. 20). Strang shows this on the example; in general the last $m - r$ rows of $E$, the ones that produce the zero rows of $R$, are a basis for $N(A^T)$, found without starting over on $A^T$ (p. 21).

<Callout kind="key" title="The four subspaces at a glance">
The row space and nullspace are in $\mathrm{R}^n$, with dimensions $r$ and $n - r$ adding to $n$. The column space and left nullspace are in $\mathrm{R}^m$, with dimensions $r$ and $m - r$ adding to $m$ (p. 21).
</Callout>

## A new vector space: matrices

All $3 \times 3$ matrices form a vector space $M$: its "vectors" are matrices, which can be added and multiplied by numbers, with a zero matrix, and the eight rules hold (transcript, pp. 21–22). That matrices can also be multiplied together does not matter here (p. 22). Subspaces of $M$ include the **upper triangular** matrices and the **symmetric** matrices; their intersection, symmetric with zeros below the diagonal, is the **diagonal** matrices (pp. 22–23). The diagonal matrices have dimension 3: three diagonal matrices, each with one nonzero entry in a different diagonal position, are a basis (p. 24). The other dimensions are left for next time (p. 23).

<WorkedExample title="All four subspaces of your own">
<Problem>
Find bases and dimensions of the four subspaces of $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 & 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Row reduce, keeping E">
Subtract 2 times row 1 from row 2 and 3 times row 1 from row 3: both become $(0, 0, 1, 2)$. Subtract the new row 2 from row 3: zero. So $R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, rank $r = 2$, pivot columns 1 and 3. Doing the same to $I$ gives last row of $E$ equal to $(-3, 0, 1) - (-2, 1, 0) = (-1, -1, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="The four bases">
$C(B)$: columns 1 and 3 of $B$, $(1, 2, 3)$ and $(0, 1, 1)$; dimension 2. $C(B^T)$: $(1, 2, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 0, 1, 2)$; dimension 2. $N(B)$: special solutions $(-2, 1, 0, 0)$ and $(-1, 0, -2, 1)$; dimension $4 - 2 = 2$. $N(B^T)$: $(-1, -1, 1)$; dimension $3 - 2 = 1$. Check: $-1(1, 2, 0, 1) - (2, 4, 1, 4) + (3, 6, 1, 5) = (0, 0, 0, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check the counts">
$2 + 2 = 4 = n$ and $2 + 1 = 3 = m$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Four subspaces: $C(A)$ and $N(A^T)$ in $\mathrm{R}^m$; $C(A^T)$ and $N(A)$ in $\mathrm{R}^n$.
- Dimensions $r$, $m - r$, $r$, $n - r$: row rank equals column rank.
- Bases: pivot columns of $A$; first $r$ rows of $R$; special solutions; last $m - r$ rows of $E$, where $[A\;\;I] \to [R\;\;E]$.
- Row operations keep the row space and nullspace, and change the column space.
- Matrices themselves form vector spaces, with subspaces such as the diagonal matrices (dimension 3 for $3 \times 3$).
