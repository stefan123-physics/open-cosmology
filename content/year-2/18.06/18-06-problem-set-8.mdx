## What you need from the lessons

**Differential equations** (Lecture 23). Each eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ of $A$, with eigenvector $x_i$, gives a pure exponential solution of $du/dt = Au$, and the general solution combines them; the constants come from the start, $Sc = u(0)$, with the eigenvectors in the columns of $S$ (pp. 13–14 of the Lecture 23 transcript):

<Formula label="General solution" tex="u(t) = c_1e^{\lambda_1t}x_1 + c_2e^{\lambda_2t}x_2 + \cdots + c_ne^{\lambda_nt}x_n" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

Every solution goes to zero when every eigenvalue has a negative real part; one eigenvalue $0$ with the others of negative real part gives a steady state; a positive real part blows up (pp. 9–10). For a $2 \times 2$ matrix the eigenvalues add up to the trace and multiply to the determinant, so

<Formula label="Two by two stability" tex="\operatorname{trace} = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2 < 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \det A = \lambda_1\lambda_2 > 0" cite="reading1" at="pp. 12–13" />

The matrix exponential is defined by its series, which converges for every $A$ and $t$; with $n$ independent eigenvectors it is built from $S$ and the diagonal $\Lambda$, and $u(t) = e^{At}u(0)$:

<Formula label="The matrix exponential" tex="e^{At} = I + At + \frac{(At)^2}{2} + \frac{(At)^3}{6} + \cdots = Se^{\Lambda t}S^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 15–20" />

The geometric series converges only when every eigenvalue of $At$ is below 1 in size (p. 18):

<Formula label="Geometric series" tex="(I - At)^{-1} = I + At + (At)^2 + (At)^3 + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="pp. 17–18" />

A second-order equation $y'' + by' + ky = 0$ becomes a first-order system for $u = (y', y)$: the equation in the top row, the trivial equation $y' = y'$ below it. Higher orders work the same way, with ones just below the diagonal (p. 23):

<Formula label="First-order form" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix} y' \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -b & -k \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} y' \\ y \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 23" />

**Markov matrices** (Lecture 24). A Markov matrix has no negative entries and every column adds to 1. Then $\lambda = 1$ is an eigenvalue, the others have $|\lambda| \leq 1$ (in all but exceptional cases $|\lambda| < 1$), and the powers approach the steady state $c_1x_1$ (pp. 2–4 of the Lecture 24 transcript):

<Formula label="Approach to the steady state" tex="u_k = A^ku_0 = c_1\,1^k\,x_1 + c_2\lambda_2^kx_2 + \cdots \;\to\; c_1x_1" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

A matrix and its transpose have the same eigenvalues:

<Formula label="Same eigenvalues for the transpose" tex="\det(A - \lambda I) = \det(A^T - \lambda I)" cite="reading2" at="pp. 7–8" />

**Orthonormal bases and Fourier series** (Lecture 24). If $q_1, \dots, q_n$ are orthonormal and $v = x_1q_1 + \cdots + x_nq_n$, a dot product with $q_i$ picks out one coefficient (pp. 15–16):

<Formula label="Coefficient in an orthonormal basis" tex="x_i = q_i^Tv" cite="reading2" at="p. 16" />

Functions on $[0, 2\pi]$ have the inner product $\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)g(x)\,dx$, in which $1, \cos x, \sin x, \cos 2x, \sin 2x, \dots$ are orthogonal (pp. 19–20). In the Fourier series of $f$, the coefficient of $\cos x$ is found by multiplying by $\cos x$ and integrating:

<Formula label="Fourier series and its first coefficient" tex="f(x) = a_0 + a_1\cos x + b_1\sin x + a_2\cos 2x + b_2\sin 2x + \cdots, \qquad a_1 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos x\,dx" cite="reading2" at="pp. 18–21" />

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Solving du/dt = Au" points={16}>

Solve the system

<Formula label="The system of Problem 1" tex="\frac{du}{dt} = Au, \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} -3 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad u(0) = \begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own problem)" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the two eigenvalues of $A$.

<Hint>

The eigenvalues solve $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{trace})\lambda + \det A = 0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-big" label="The larger eigenvalue is" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-big" />

<Answer id="p1-a-small" label="The smaller eigenvalue is" value="-4" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-small" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a $2 \times 2$ matrix the trace and the determinant give the characteristic polynomial.

<Step title="The characteristic polynomial">

The trace is $-3 - 2 = -5$ and the determinant is $(-3)(-2) - (1)(2) = 4$:

<Formula label="Eigenvalues of A" tex="\lambda^2 + 5\lambda + 4 = (\lambda + 1)(\lambda + 4) = 0, \qquad \lambda_1 = -1,\ \lambda_2 = -4" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$-1 + (-4) = -5$ is the trace and $(-1)(-4) = 4$ the determinant. Both eigenvalues are negative, so every solution will decay to zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find eigenvectors $x_1$ for $\lambda_1$ and $x_2$ for $\lambda_2$, with first components equal to 1. Write $u(0) = c_1x_1 + c_2x_2$ and find $c_2$. Then give the second component $u_2(t)$ of the solution.

<Hint>

$x_1$ is in the nullspace of $A - \lambda_1I$: a matrix whose two rows are multiples of each other, so one row is enough.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$c_1x_1 + c_2x_2 = u(0)$ is two equations for $c_1$ and $c_2$. Then each $c_i x_i$ is multiplied by its own $e^{\lambda_it}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b-c2" label="$c_2 =$" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-b-c2" />

<Answer id="p1-b-u2" label="$u_2(t) =$" expr="2*exp(-t) - 2*exp(-4*t)"
  vars={{ t: [0.05, 4] }} names={{ t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*exp(-4*t) - 2*exp(-t)", hint: "The signs are reversed: this starts to go negative, but du₂/dt = 2u₁ − 2u₂ is 6 at t = 0, so u₂ must start to grow." },
    { expr: "4*exp(-t) - exp(-4*t)", hint: "This gives u₂(0) = 3 instead of 0: the two constants c₁ and c₂ have been swapped." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Eigenvectors from the nullspaces, constants from $u(0)$, and then each piece grows or decays with its own exponential.

<Step title="The eigenvectors">

$A + I = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$: the first row says $-2x + y = 0$, so $x_1 = (1, 2)$. $A + 4I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$: $x + y = 0$, so $x_2 = (1, -1)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The constants">

$c_1(1, 2) + c_2(1, -1) = (3, 0)$ means $c_1 + c_2 = 3$ and $2c_1 - c_2 = 0$. Adding, $3c_1 = 3$: so $c_1 = 1$ and $c_2 = 2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The solution">

<Formula label="Solution of Problem 1" tex="u(t) = e^{-t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} + 2e^{-4t}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} e^{-t} + 2e^{-4t} \\ 2e^{-t} - 2e^{-4t} \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5 (the general solution; the numbers are our own)" />

So $u_2(t) = 2e^{-t} - 2e^{-4t}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $t = 0$: $u = (1 + 2, 2 - 2) = (3, 0)$. And $du_2/dt = -2e^{-t} + 8e^{-4t}$ should equal $2u_1 - 2u_2 = 2e^{-t} + 4e^{-4t} - 4e^{-t} + 4e^{-4t} = -2e^{-t} + 8e^{-4t}$. It does.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

$u_2$ starts at 0, rises and falls back to 0. At which time is it largest, and what is its largest value?

<Hint>

Set $du_2/dt = 0$ and solve for $e^{3t}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c-t" label="The time of the maximum is $t =$" value="0.4621" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-c-t" />

<Answer id="p1-c-max" label="The largest value of $u_2$ is" value="0.9449" tolerance="0.005" calc="p1-c-max" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A maximum of a smooth function is where its derivative is zero.

<Step title="The time">

$du_2/dt = -2e^{-t} + 8e^{-4t} = 0$ gives $e^{3t} = 4$:

<Formula label="Time of the maximum" tex="t^* = \frac{\ln 4}{3} \approx 0.4621" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The value">

At $t^*$, $e^{-3t^*} = \frac14$, so $u_2 = 2e^{-t^*}(1 - e^{-3t^*}) = \frac32e^{-t^*} = \frac32 \cdot 4^{-1/3} \approx 0.9449$.

<Plot variable="t" domain={[0, 3]} range={[0, 3.2]} xLabel="t" yLabel="u"
  curves={[
    { expr: "exp(-t) + 2*exp(-4*t)" },
    { expr: "2*exp(-t) - 2*exp(-4*t)", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="The two components of the solution of Problem 1, computed from the formula: u₁ (solid) falls from 3, u₂ (dashed) rises from 0 to its largest value near t = 0.46 and then decays with u₁." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Before $t^*$ the term $8e^{-4t}$ wins and $u_2$ rises; after it, $-2e^{-t}$ wins and $u_2$ falls. So the stationary point is a maximum.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Show that $u_1(t) + u_2(t) = 3e^{-t}$, without using the solution, from the columns of $A$. (This part has no box.)

<Hint>

Add the two equations $du_1/dt = \dots$ and $du_2/dt = \dots$. What does each column of $A$ add up to?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The column sums of $A$ say how the total changes.

<Step title="Adding the equations">

$du_1/dt = -3u_1 + u_2$ and $du_2/dt = 2u_1 - 2u_2$. Their sum is $d(u_1 + u_2)/dt = -u_1 - u_2$, because each column of $A$ adds up to $-1$. So the total $s = u_1 + u_2$ solves $ds/dt = -s$ with $s(0) = 3$, which gives $s = 3e^{-t}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

From part (b): $(e^{-t} + 2e^{-4t}) + (2e^{-t} - 2e^{-4t}) = 3e^{-t}$. (In Lecture 23's example the columns add to zero, so there the total stays constant: Lecture 23 transcript, p. 1.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Stability from the trace and the determinant" points={16}>

For a real number $a$, take

<Formula label="The matrix of Problem 2" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & a \\ 1 & -3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \frac{du}{dt} = Au" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own problem)" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Every solution goes to zero exactly when $a$ is below a certain number. Find it.

<Hint>

The trace does not depend on $a$. What must the determinant do?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="Stable exactly when $a$ is below" value="6" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The $2 \times 2$ test: trace negative and determinant positive.

<Step title="The test">

The trace is $-5 < 0$ for every $a$. The determinant is $6 - a$, positive exactly when $a < 6$. So the system is stable for $a < 6$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $a = 6$ the determinant is zero, so one eigenvalue is $0$ and the other is the trace $-5$: a steady state, not decay. Above 6 the determinant is negative, the eigenvalues have opposite signs and the positive one blows up.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

For $a \geq 0$ the eigenvalues are real. Give the larger one as a formula in $a$.

<Hint>

$\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{trace})\lambda + \det A = 0$, solved with the quadratic formula. Simplify the term under the root.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="The larger eigenvalue is" expr="(-5 + sqrt(1 + 4*a))/2"
  vars={{ a: [0, 5] }} names={{ a: "a" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(5 + sqrt(1 + 4*a))/2", hint: "The eigenvalues add up to the trace, −5, not +5: this has the wrong sign in front of 5." },
    { expr: "-5 + sqrt(1 + 4*a)", hint: "Try a = 0: the matrix is then triangular with eigenvalues −2 and −3, but this gives −4. The factor 1/2 of the quadratic formula is missing." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The characteristic polynomial of a $2 \times 2$ matrix comes from its trace and determinant.

<Step title="The quadratic formula">

<Formula label="Eigenvalues of the matrix of Problem 2" tex="\lambda^2 + 5\lambda + (6 - a) = 0, \qquad \lambda = \frac{-5 \pm \sqrt{25 - 4(6 - a)}}{2} = \frac{-5 \pm \sqrt{1 + 4a}}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

The larger one takes the plus sign.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $a = 0$: $(-5 + 1)/2 = -2$, the larger diagonal entry of the triangular matrix. At $a = 6$: $(-5 + 5)/2 = 0$, as part (a) found.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Now take $a = -6.5$. Find the real part of the eigenvalues and the positive imaginary part, and say what the solutions do.

<Hint>

With $a = -6.5$ the term under the root is negative: its square root is $i$ times the root of the positive number.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The size of $e^{\lambda t}$ depends only on the real part of $\lambda$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c-re" label="The real part of the eigenvalues is" value="-2.5" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-c-re" />

<Answer id="p2-c-im" label="The positive imaginary part is" value="2.5" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-c-im" />

<Answer id="p2-c-what" label="Every solution other than zero"
  choices={["spirals out to infinity", "decays to zero without turning", "spirals in, shrinking to zero", "circles at a fixed size"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Complex eigenvalues of a real matrix come in a conjugate pair; their real part is half the trace.

<Step title="The eigenvalues">

$1 + 4a = 1 - 26 = -25$, so $\lambda = (-5 \pm 5i)/2 = -2.5 \pm 2.5i$.

</Step>

<Step title="The behaviour">

Each pure solution contains $e^{-2.5t}e^{\pm 2.5it}$. The factor $e^{\pm 2.5it} = \cos 2.5t \pm i\sin 2.5t$ has size 1 and turns; $e^{-2.5t}$ shrinks. So the solutions spiral in to zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The trace and determinant test agrees: trace $-5 < 0$ and determinant $6 + 6.5 = 12.5 > 0$. And $(-2.5)^2 + 2.5^2 = 12.5$, the product of the two conjugate eigenvalues.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

For comparison, take $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -3 \\ 3 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ and $du/dt = Bu$. Say what the solutions do. Then show that $\|u(t)\|^2 = u_1^2 + u_2^2$ never changes (this step has no box).

<Hint>

Trace $0$: the stability test is on its border. Find the eigenvalues.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiate $u_1^2 + u_2^2$ and use the two equations.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="Every solution other than zero"

<Solution>

**The idea.** Purely imaginary eigenvalues give pure oscillation: real part zero, so neither decay nor growth.

<Step title="The eigenvalues">

$\lambda^2 + 9 = 0$, so $\lambda = \pm 3i$. Since $|e^{3it}| = 1$, the solutions neither shrink nor grow: they circle at a fixed size.

</Step>

<Step title="The length">

$du_1/dt = -3u_2$ and $du_2/dt = 3u_1$, so

<Formula label="The length does not change" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(u_1^2 + u_2^2\right) = 2u_1(-3u_2) + 2u_2(3u_1) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$u(t) = (\cos 3t, \sin 3t)$ is a solution: its derivative $(-3\sin 3t, 3\cos 3t)$ equals $B u$. Its length is always 1.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

The $(1, 2)$ entry is $2e^{2t} - 2e^{3t}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$SS^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} -1 + 2 & 2 - 2 \\ -1 + 1 & 2 - 1 \end{pmatrix} = I$. Part (b) checks the result itself.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Check, from the series, that $e^{At}$ must equal $I$ at $t = 0$ and that its derivative at $t = 0$ must be $A$. Then check both on your matrix from part (a). (This part has no box.)

<Hint>

Differentiate the series term by term: what is left of each term at $t = 0$?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The first two terms of the series, $I + At$, decide the value and the slope at $t = 0$.

<Step title="From the series">

At $t = 0$ every term after $I$ is zero. Differentiating term by term gives $A + A^2t + \frac12A^3t^2 + \cdots$, which is $A$ at $t = 0$. (In fact the derivative is $Ae^{At}$, so $u = e^{At}u(0)$ solves $du/dt = Au$.)

</Step>

<Step title="On the matrix">

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

**The idea.** One entry of the product of part (a), at one time.

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Compare the two series of the lecture for this $A$ and $t > 0$. For which $t$ does the exponential series $I + At + (At)^2/2 + \cdots$ converge? And up to which $t$ does the geometric series $I + At + (At)^2 + \cdots$ converge?

<Hint>

The eigenvalues of $At$ are $t$ times the eigenvalues of $A$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-d-exp" label="The exponential series converges"
  choices={["only for t below 1/2", "for every t, however large", "only when every eigenvalue of A is negative", "only for t between −1 and 1"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p3-d-geo" label="The geometric series converges for $0 < t <$" value="0.3333" tolerance="0.005" calc="p3-d-geo" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Factorials make the exponential series converge always; the geometric series needs every eigenvalue of $At$ below 1 in size.

<Step title="The exponential series">

The $n$-th term is $(At)^n/n!$, and $n!$ grows faster than any power: the series converges for every $A$ and every $t$ (Lecture 23 transcript, p. 18).

</Step>

<Step title="The geometric series">

The eigenvalues of $At$ are $2t$ and $3t$. Both are below 1 when $3t < 1$, so the geometric series converges for $0 < t < \frac13 \approx 0.3333$. (Stopping at $2t < 1$, that is $t < \frac12$, forgets the larger eigenvalue.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $t = \frac13$ the matrix $I - At$ has the eigenvalue $1 - 3 \cdot \frac13 = 0$: it is singular, so $(I - At)^{-1}$ does not even exist there.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Exercise id="p4" title="A third-order equation as a system" points={12}>

Take the third-order equation

Here $y^{(3)}$ is the third derivative of $y$.

<Answer id="p4-a-13" label="$c_{13} =$" value="-2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p4-a-13" />

<Solution>

<Step title="The matrix">

<Formula label="The first-order form of Problem 4" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\begin{pmatrix} y'' \\ y' \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & -2 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} y'' \\ y' \\ y \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 23 (the method; the third-order matrix is our own)" />

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Show that $\det(C - \lambda I) = -(\lambda^3 - 2\lambda^2 - \lambda + 2)$ (this step has no box). Then find the largest eigenvalue of $C$.

<Hint>

Expand the determinant along the first row. For the roots, try small whole numbers.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="The largest eigenvalue of $C$ is" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p4-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The eigenvalues of this matrix are the roots of the equation's own polynomial: try $y = e^{\lambda t}$ and you get the same polynomial.

<Step title="The determinant">

<Formula label="Determinant along the first row" tex="\det\begin{pmatrix} 2 - \lambda & 1 & -2 \\ 1 & -\lambda & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = (2 - \lambda)\lambda^2 - 1\cdot(-\lambda) + (-2)\cdot 1 = -\lambda^3 + 2\lambda^2 + \lambda - 2" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The roots">

$\lambda^3 - 2\lambda^2 - \lambda + 2 = \lambda^2(\lambda - 2) - (\lambda - 2) = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 1)(\lambda + 1)$. The eigenvalues are $2$, $1$ and $-1$; the largest is $2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Their sum $2 + 1 - 1 = 2$ is the trace of $C$, and their product $-2$ is $\det C$ (put $\lambda = 0$ in the determinant).

</Step>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Two eigenvalues are positive, so most solutions blow up. Take $y(0) = 1$ and $y'(0) = -1$. For which value of $y''(0)$ does $y(t)$ go to zero as $t \to \infty$?

<Hint>

$y(t)$ is a combination of $e^{\lambda t}$ for the three eigenvalues of part (b): which amounts must be zero?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c" label="$y''(0) =$" value="1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p4-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only the pure solution with the negative eigenvalue decays; the start must contain nothing of the other two.

<Step title="The condition">

Write $y(t) = \alpha e^{2t} + \beta e^{t} + \gamma e^{-t}$. For $y \to 0$ we need $\alpha = \beta = 0$, so $y = \gamma e^{-t}$. Then $y(0) = \gamma = 1$, and $y'(0) = -1$ holds already. So $y = e^{-t}$ and $y''(0) = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

In general the three starting values give $\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 1$, $2\alpha + \beta - \gamma = -1$ and $4\alpha + \beta + \gamma = y''(0)$. The first and third give $3\alpha = y''(0) - 1$, and the first two give $3\alpha + 2\beta = 0$. Both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ vanish exactly when $y''(0) = 1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

Show that $(1, 1, 1)$ is an eigenvector of $A^T$, and explain why this makes $\lambda = 1$ an eigenvalue of $A$. (This part has no box.)

The rows of $A^T$ are the columns of $A$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Column sums of $A$ are row sums of $A^T$, and $A$ and $A^T$ share their eigenvalues.

<Step title="The eigenvector of the transpose">

Each entry of $A^T(1, 1, 1)$ is the sum of one column of $A$: $0.4 + 0.3 + 0.3$, $0.2 + 0.6 + 0.2$ and $0.2 + 0.1 + 0.7$, each equal to 1. So $A^T(1, 1, 1) = (1, 1, 1)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Back to A">

$\det(A - \lambda I) = \det(A^T - \lambda I)$, so $A$ has the same eigenvalue $1$, though with a different eigenvector (Lecture 24 transcript, pp. 7–8).

</Step>
