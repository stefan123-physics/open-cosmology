The singular value decomposition, the SVD, is "the final and best factorization of a matrix" (transcript, p. 1). Its factors are an orthogonal matrix, a diagonal matrix and another orthogonal matrix, and **any matrix whatsoever** has one (p. 1):

<Formula label="SVD" tex="A = U\Sigma V^T" cite="reading1" at="pp. 1, 5" />

It brings the course together (p. 1). For a [symmetric positive definite](/courses/18-06/symmetric-matrices-positive-definite) matrix it is $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$: orthonormal eigenvectors and positive eigenvalues, one $Q$ for both sides (p. 1). For other matrices the eigenvector matrix is not orthogonal, so two different orthogonal matrices are needed (pp. 1–2).

## The goal: orthogonal bases that stay orthogonal

Picture $A$ taking the row space to the column space (p. 2). A vector $v_1$ in the row space goes to $Av_1$ in the column space. Gram–Schmidt gives an orthogonal basis of the row space easily, but $A$ would generally not keep it orthogonal (p. 2). The SVD looks for the special orthonormal basis that **stays** orthogonal (pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="Row-space basis to column-space basis" tex="Av_1 = \sigma_1u_1, \quad Av_2 = \sigma_2u_2, \quad \dots, \quad Av_r = \sigma_ru_r" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–4" />

The $v$'s are orthonormal in the row space, the $u$'s orthonormal in the column space, and the stretching numbers are called $\sigma$, no longer $\lambda$ (p. 3). In matrix language (p. 4):

<Formula label="Matrix form" tex="A\begin{pmatrix} v_1 & \cdots & v_r \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} u_1 & \cdots & u_r \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \sigma_r \end{pmatrix}, \qquad AV = U\Sigma" cite="reading1" at="pp. 4–5" />

The nullspaces cause no difficulty: orthonormal bases $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$ of the nullspace and $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$ of the left nullspace complete $V$ and $U$ to square orthogonal matrices, and they meet zeros in $\Sigma$ (pp. 3, 5). (Strang says "$v_{r+1}$ down to $v_m$" and seems to name the two spaces $\mathbf{R}^m$ and $\mathbf{R}^n$ the other way round for a moment; the $v$'s run to $v_n$ in $\mathbf{R}^n$.) Since $V$ is square and orthogonal, $V^{-1} = V^T$ and $A = U\Sigma V^T$ (p. 5).

## Finding V and U

Two unknown orthogonal matrices at once is too many. To make $U$ disappear, use the great matrix $A^TA$, symmetric and positive (semi)definite (pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="AᵀA removes U" tex="A^TA = V\Sigma^TU^TU\Sigma V^T = V\begin{pmatrix} \sigma_1^2 & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \sigma_n^2 \end{pmatrix}V^T" cite="reading1" at="pp. 6–7" />

because $U^TU = I$ (p. 6); here $\sigma_{r+1} = \cdots = \sigma_n = 0$. This is exactly $Q\Lambda Q^T$ for $A^TA$: the $v$'s are its **eigenvectors** and the $\sigma^2$'s its **eigenvalues** (p. 7). In the other order the $V$'s drop out (pp. 7, 10):

<Formula label="AAᵀ removes V" tex="AA^T = U\Sigma\Sigma^TU^T" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

so the $u$'s are the eigenvectors of $AA^T$, and the $\sigma$'s are the positive square roots (p. 7).

## Strang's first example

<Formula label="Example matrix" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

It is invertible, rank 2, and not symmetric, so its own eigenvectors cannot be used (pp. 4–5). (In fact its eigenvalues are complex, $3.5 \pm i\sqrt{11.75}$: it has no real eigenvectors at all.) First (p. 8):

<Formula label="AᵀA for the example" tex="A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -3 \\ 4 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 25 & 7 \\ 7 & 25 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

Its eigenvectors are $(1, 1)$, with $A^TA(1, 1) = (32, 32)$, and $(1, -1)$, with eigenvalue $18$; normalised by $\sqrt2$, they are $v_1$ and $v_2$ (pp. 8–9). So $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{32}$ and $\sigma_2 = \sqrt{18}$ (p. 9). For $U$ (pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="AAᵀ for the example" tex="AA^T = \begin{pmatrix} 32 & 0 \\ 0 & 18 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 11" />

By luck it is diagonal, with eigenvectors $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$, and again eigenvalues 32 and 18: no surprise, since $AB$ and $BA$ always have the same eigenvalues (p. 11). Multiplying out, $\sqrt{32}/\sqrt2 = 4$ gives the first row $(4, 4)$ correctly, but the second row comes out $(3, -3)$ instead of $(-3, 3)$ (p. 12). Strang considers flipping the sign of an eigenvector but does not want a minus sign in $\Sigma$, and leaves the puzzle for later (pp. 12–13); the later transcripts do not return to it. Earlier he had remarked that $U$ could also be worked out from the other pieces (p. 10). (The page's own resolution follows that route: each eigenvector is fixed only up to sign, and the choices for $u$ and $v$ must match. Taking $u_2 = Av_2/\sigma_2 = (0, -6/\sqrt2)/\sqrt{18} = (0, -1)$ instead of $(0, 1)$ makes $U\Sigma V^T = A$ exactly, and $\Sigma$ keeps its positive entries: the sign sits in $U$.)

## A singular example

<Formula label="Rank-one example" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 8 & 6 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

Its row space is the line of multiples of $(4, 3)$, with the nullspace perpendicular; its column space is the line through $(4, 8)$, or $(1, 2)$, with the left nullspace perpendicular to it (pp. 13–14). With one-dimensional spaces the bases are forced: $v_1 = (0.8, 0.6)$ and $u_1 = (1, 2)/\sqrt5$ (pp. 14–15). From (p. 15)

<Formula label="AᵀA, rank one" tex="A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 80 & 60 \\ 60 & 45 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda = 125,\ 0, \qquad \sigma_1 = \sqrt{125}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 15–16" />

Strang states the eigenvalues 0 and 125 at once, the rank being one (p. 16); the trace $80 + 45$ confirms the nonzero one. Completing the bases with the nullspace directions, $v_2 = (0.6, -0.8)$ and $u_2 = (2, -1)/\sqrt5$ (p. 16):

<Formula label="Its SVD" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 8 & 6 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \sqrt{125} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0.8 & 0.6 \\ 0.6 & -0.8 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 16" />

$\sqrt{125}/\sqrt5 = 5$, and 5 times the products of $(1, 2)$ and $(0.8, 0.6)$ gives back whole numbers: $A$ (p. 16). The nullspace vectors are multiplied by the zero (p. 17).

## The right bases for the four subspaces

What the SVD really does is choose the right bases for the [four fundamental subspaces](/courses/18-06/four-fundamental-subspaces) (pp. 17–18):

- $v_1, \dots, v_r$: an orthonormal basis of the row space (dimension $r$);
- $u_1, \dots, u_r$: an orthonormal basis of the column space (dimension $r$);
- $v_{r+1}, \dots, v_n$: an orthonormal basis of the nullspace (dimension $n - r$);
- $u_{r+1}, \dots, u_m$: an orthonormal basis of the nullspace of $A^T$ (dimension $m - r$).

Gram–Schmidt could make orthonormal bases in chapter 4; the SVD needs eigenvalues because its bases also make the matrix **diagonal**: $A$ times each $v_i$ lies along the matching $u_i$, with no coupling (p. 17). The SVD returns in the last lectures of the course (p. 18).

<WorkedExample title="An SVD of your own">
<Problem>
(a) Find the SVD of $A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 5 \\ -1 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$, using $u_i = Av_i/\sigma_i$. (b) Find the SVD of the rank-one matrix $B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -4 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) AᵀA">
$A^TA = \begin{pmatrix} 25 + 1 & 25 - 7 \\ 25 - 7 & 25 + 49 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 26 & 18 \\ 18 & 74 \end{pmatrix}$: trace 100, determinant $1924 - 324 = 1600$, so eigenvalues $80$ and $20$. $A^TA - 80I = \begin{pmatrix} -54 & 18 \\ 18 & -6 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $v_1 = (1, 3)/\sqrt{10}$, and $v_2 = (3, -1)/\sqrt{10}$. Singular values $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{80} = 4\sqrt5$, $\sigma_2 = \sqrt{20} = 2\sqrt5$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) The u's">
$Av_1 = (20, 20)/\sqrt{10}$, so $u_1 = (20, 20)/(\sqrt{10} \cdot 4\sqrt5) = (1, 1)/\sqrt2$. $Av_2 = (10, -10)/\sqrt{10}$, so $u_2 = (1, -1)/\sqrt2$. They are orthonormal, and $\sigma_1\sigma_2 = 40 = \det A = 35 + 5$, as it should be for a $2 \times 2$ matrix with positive determinant.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Rank one">
The row space is the line through $(2, 1)$ and the column space the line through $(1, -2)$: $v_1 = (2, 1)/\sqrt5$, $u_1 = (1, -2)/\sqrt5$. $B^TB = \begin{pmatrix} 20 & 10 \\ 10 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$ has trace 25 and rank one, so $\sigma_1 = 5$. Check: $5u_1v_1^T = \frac55\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -4 & -2 \end{pmatrix} = B$. Complete with $v_2 = (1, -2)/\sqrt5$ (nullspace) and $u_2 = (2, 1)/\sqrt5$ (left nullspace).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Every $m \times n$ matrix: $A = U\Sigma V^T$, $U$ and $V$ orthogonal, $\Sigma$ diagonal with $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r > 0$.
- $Av_i = \sigma_iu_i$: an orthonormal basis of the row space goes to an orthogonal one of the column space.
- $V$: eigenvectors of $A^TA$; $U$: eigenvectors of $AA^T$ (signs matched by $u_i = Av_i/\sigma_i$); $\sigma_i^2$: their nonzero eigenvalues.
- The SVD gives orthonormal bases of all four fundamental subspaces that make $A$ diagonal.
