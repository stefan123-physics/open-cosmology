Two applications (transcript, p. 1). First **Markov matrices**, the eigenvalue application, where the important eigenvalue is 1. Then, if time allows, **Fourier series**, an application of [orthonormal bases](/courses/18-06/orthogonal-matrices-gram-schmidt) from the projection chapter.

## What a Markov matrix is

Strang invents a typical one, with columns $(0.1, 0.2, 0.7)$, $(0.01, 0.99, 0)$ and $(0.3, 0.3, 0.4)$ (p. 1):

<Formula label="Strang's Markov matrix" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.1 & 0.01 & 0.3 \\ 0.2 & 0.99 & 0.3 \\ 0.7 & 0 & 0.4 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Two properties make it Markov (p. 1):

1. every entry is $\geq 0$ (Markov matrices come from probabilities, which are never negative);
2. every column adds to $1$.

Both properties survive squaring, so all powers $A^k$ are Markov matrices too (pp. 1–2).

## Eigenvalue 1 and the steady state

For differential equations last time, the steady state came from $\lambda = 0$, because $e^{0t} = 1$ (p. 2). For powers, a zero eigenvalue dies at once; here the eigenvalue that matters is $\lambda = 1$ (p. 2). The key facts (pp. 2–4):

- $\lambda = 1$ is an eigenvalue, found without any determinant;
- every other eigenvalue has $|\lambda| \leq 1$, and in all but exceptional cases $|\lambda| < 1$; none is larger than 1;
- the eigenvector $x_1$ for $\lambda = 1$ has no negative components, when $\lambda = 1$ is not repeated (Strang does not prove this part, p. 9).

With $u_0 = c_1x_1 + c_2x_2 + \cdots$ (a complete set of eigenvectors assumed, as always) (p. 3):

<Formula label="Approach to the steady state" tex="u_k = A^ku_0 = c_1\,1^k\,x_1 + c_2\lambda_2^kx_2 + \cdots \;\to\; c_1x_1" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

All the terms with $|\lambda| < 1$ go to zero, and the steady state is the $x_1$ part of $u_0$ (p. 3).

**Why $\lambda = 1$** (pp. 4–6). Subtract $I$: the diagonal of the example becomes $-0.9$, $-0.01$, $-0.6$. Every column of $A - I$ now adds to zero, and the claim is that $A - I$ is singular (p. 4). Rather than a determinant, look at the rows: adding all three rows gives the zero row, so the rows are dependent and the square matrix is singular (pp. 5–6). In other words, $(1, 1, 1)$ is in the nullspace of $(A - I)^T$, the left nullspace (p. 6).

**$A$ and $A^T$ have the same eigenvalues** (pp. 7–8). The determinant of a matrix equals that of its transpose (property 10), and $(\lambda I)^T = \lambda I$:

<Formula label="Same eigenvalues for the transpose" tex="\det(A - \lambda I) = \det\big((A - \lambda I)^T\big) = \det(A^T - \lambda I)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 7–8" />

So $1$ is an eigenvalue of $A^T$, with eigenvector $(1, 1, 1)$, and therefore of $A$, but with a different eigenvector: the nullspace of $A - I$, not its left nullspace (p. 8). For the example Strang finds it on the spot: $0.6$ and $0.7$ make the last row zero ($0.42 - 0.42$), and then the first row, $-0.54 + 0.21 + 0.01y = 0$, needs $y = 33$ (pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Steady-state eigenvector" tex="x_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 \\ 33 \\ 0.7 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 8–9" />

All positive, as the theory predicts (pp. 8–9). (Strang first expects a component of 3300 and corrects himself to 33.)

## Movement between two states

Where do Markov matrices come from? Strang's example: the populations of California and Massachusetts, with the same fractions moving every year (pp. 9–10). The entries are probabilities of moving or staying, so they lie between 0 and 1, and each column adds to 1 because everybody is accounted for: the total population is conserved (p. 10). Each year $0.9$ of California stays and $0.1$ moves to Massachusetts; $0.8$ of Massachusetts stays and $0.2$ moves to California (pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="Population model" tex="\begin{pmatrix} u_{\text{Cal}} \\ u_{\text{Mass}} \end{pmatrix}_{k+1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.9 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.8 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} u_{\text{Cal}} \\ u_{\text{Mass}} \end{pmatrix}_k" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11" />

Strang notes the severe limitation: the same matrix acts at every step (p. 10). Start with $0$ in California and $1000$ in Massachusetts (p. 11). After one step the populations are $200$ and $800$, and the total stays $1000$ for ever (pp. 11–12).

The eigenvalues are $1$ and, from the trace $1.7$, $0.7$; the determinant $0.72 - 0.02 = 0.7$ agrees (pp. 12–13). $A - I$ gives $x_1 = (2, 1)$, positive, and $A - 0.7I$ gives $x_2 = (-1, 1)$ (pp. 13–14). Fitting $u_0 = (0, 1000)$ (p. 14):

<Formula label="Population after k years" tex="u_k = \frac{1000}{3}\,1^k\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + \frac{2000}{3}\,(0.7)^k\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 14" />

The $0.7^k$ part disappears, and the populations approach $\frac{2000}{3} \approx 667$ in California and $\frac{1000}{3} \approx 333$ in Massachusetts (pp. 13–14).

Many applications and other textbooks, for example in electrical engineering, use row vectors and multiply from the left. They work with the transpose, and then the **rows** add to one (p. 15).

## Expansion in an orthonormal basis

Before Fourier, a comment on projections with an orthonormal basis $q_1, \dots, q_n$ of $n$-dimensional space (p. 15). Any $v$ is a combination (pp. 15–16):

<Formula label="Expansion" tex="v = x_1q_1 + x_2q_2 + \cdots + x_nq_n" cite="reading1" at="pp. 15–16" />

To find $x_1$, take the dot product of everything with $q_1$: all terms but the first give zero, and $q_1^Tq_1 = 1$ (p. 16):

<Formula label="Coefficient in an orthonormal basis" tex="x_1 = q_1^Tv" cite="reading1" at="p. 16" />

In matrix language $Qx = v$, so $x = Q^{-1}v = Q^Tv$, and the first component is $q_1^Tv$ (p. 17).

## Fourier series

Fourier series are built on the same orthogonality (p. 18). A function is written as (p. 18)

<Formula label="Fourier series" tex="f(x) = a_0 + a_1\cos x + b_1\sin x + a_2\cos 2x + b_2\sin 2x + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="p. 18" />

Joseph Fourier's idea was to work in a space of functions: vectors become functions, and the basis $1, \cos x, \sin x, \cos 2x, \sin 2x, \dots$ is infinite, because the space is infinite-dimensional (p. 18). It works because these functions are orthogonal (p. 19).

For vectors, $v^Tw = v_1w_1 + \cdots + v_nw_n$. For functions, multiply $f(x)g(x)$ at every $x$ and, since there is a continuum of $x$'s, integrate instead of adding (pp. 19–20). The sines and cosines have period $2\pi$, so $f(x) = f(x + 2\pi)$ and the interval is $0$ to $2\pi$ (p. 20):

<Formula label="Inner product of functions" tex="f^Tg = \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\,g(x)\,dx" cite="reading1" at="p. 20" />

For example $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin x\cos x\,dx = \left[\tfrac12\sin^2x\right]_0^{2\pi} = 0$, and trigonometric identities give the same for every other pair (p. 20). (Strang calls the basis orthonormal; strictly it is orthogonal, since $\int\cos^2x\,dx = \pi$, not 1, which is why $\frac1\pi$ appears below; the constant function has $\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dx = 2\pi$, which is why $a_0$ is the average.) The constant $a_0$ is the average value of $f$ (p. 21). For $a_1$, do as for vectors: multiply by $\cos x$ and integrate. Everything vanishes except one term (p. 21):

<Formula label="First Fourier coefficient" tex="\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos x\,dx = a_1\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2x\,dx = \pi a_1, \qquad a_1 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos x\,dx" cite="reading1" at="p. 21" />

This is the classical formula for Fourier coefficients ("Euler's famous formula", or maybe Fourier's, as Strang puts it), and it is exactly an expansion in an orthogonal basis (p. 21). For more on Fourier series see 18.03's [introduction to Fourier series](/courses/18-03/fourier-series-introduction).

<WorkedExample title="A Markov chain and a Fourier coefficient">
<Problem>
(a) Each year $0.6$ of the members of club P stay and $0.4$ move to club Q; $0.7$ of club Q stay and $0.3$ move to P. Start with $700$ in P and none in Q. Find $u_k$ and the steady state. (b) Find $a_1$ for $f(x) = (1 + \cos x)^2$ by the formula, and check it by expanding $f$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Matrix and eigenvalues">
$A = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.7 \end{pmatrix}$: columns add to 1. The eigenvalues are $1$ and, from the trace $1.3$, $0.3$. $A - I = \begin{pmatrix} -0.4 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & -0.3 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $x_1 = (3, 4)$; $A - 0.3I = \begin{pmatrix} 0.3 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.4 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $x_2 = (1, -1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Solution">
$c_1(3, 4) + c_2(1, -1) = (700, 0)$: adding the equations, $7c_1 = 700$, so $c_1 = 100$ and $c_2 = 400$. Then $u_k = 100(3, 4) + 400(0.3)^k(1, -1)$. Check: $u_1 = (300 + 120, 400 - 120) = (420, 280)$, and directly $A(700, 0) = (420, 280)$. Steady state: $(300, 400)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Fourier coefficient">
$(1 + \cos x)^2\cos x = \cos x + 2\cos^2x + \cos^3x$. Over $[0, 2\pi]$ the integrals of $\cos x$ and $\cos^3x$ are zero and $\int 2\cos^2x\,dx = 2\pi$, so $a_1 = 2\pi/\pi = 2$. Check: $(1 + \cos x)^2 = 1 + 2\cos x + \cos^2x = \frac32 + 2\cos x + \frac12\cos 2x$, whose coefficient of $\cos x$ is indeed $2$ (and $a_0 = \frac32$ is the average).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Markov: entries $\geq 0$, columns add to 1. Then $\lambda = 1$ is an eigenvalue (rows of $A - I$ add to zero) and the others have $|\lambda| \leq 1$.
- $A^ku_0 \to c_1x_1$, the non-negative eigenvector for $\lambda = 1$; the total is conserved.
- $A$ and $A^T$ have the same eigenvalues.
- Orthonormal basis: $x_i = q_i^Tv$. Fourier: functions as vectors, $\int_0^{2\pi} fg\,dx$ as inner product, $a_1 = \frac1\pi\int_0^{2\pi} f\cos x\,dx$.
