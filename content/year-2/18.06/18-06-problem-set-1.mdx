## What you need from the lessons

**The two pictures** (Lecture 1). A system of linear equations is $Ax = b$. In the row picture each equation is a line (in two dimensions) or a plane (in three), and the solution is where they meet. In the column picture, $Ax$ is a combination of the columns of $A$, with the entries of $x$ as the amounts:

<Formula label="Matrix times vector, by columns" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = 1\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + 2\begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 12 \\ 7 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 19" />

If the combinations of the columns fill the whole space, $Ax = b$ can be solved for every $b$ and $A$ is invertible. If the columns lie in one plane (for a $3 \times 3$ matrix), only the right-hand sides in that plane can be reached, and $A$ is singular (pp. 15–16).

**Elimination** (Lecture 2). Subtract a multiple $\ell$ of a pivot row from each row below it, to get zeros under the pivot; the result is an upper triangular $U$, and the same steps turn $b$ into $c$. Then $Ux = c$ is solved by back substitution, from the bottom up (pp. 2–9). A zero in a pivot position is cured by a row exchange with a lower row that has a nonzero entry there; when no row below can help, the matrix is not invertible (pp. 5–7). The step "subtract $\ell$ times row 1 from row 2" is a multiplication by the elimination matrix $E_{21}$, the identity with $-\ell$ in row 2, column 1, and all steps together are (pp. 13–16)

<Formula label="Elimination in one line" tex="E_{32}(E_{21}A) = U = (E_{32}E_{21})A" cite="reading2" at="pp. 15–16" />

A row exchange is a multiplication by a permutation matrix $P$: do the exchange to the identity and use the result. On the left, a matrix acts on the rows; on the right, on the columns; and in general $AB \neq BA$ (pp. 17–18). The inverse of "subtract $\ell$ times row 1 from row 2" adds it back (p. 20).

**Matrix multiplication** (Lecture 3). For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ and an $n \times p$ matrix $B$, the entry in row $i$, column $j$ of $AB$ is row $i$ of $A$ times column $j$ of $B$ (pp. 1–4):

<Formula label="Entry of a product" tex="(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{ik}\,b_{kj}, \qquad (m \times n)(n \times p) = m \times p" cite="reading3" at="pp. 2–4" />

Each column of $AB$ is a combination of the columns of $A$, and each row of $AB$ a combination of the rows of $B$ (pp. 4–6). $AB$ is also a sum of columns times rows, and matching blocks multiply like entries (pp. 7–11):

<Formula label="Columns times rows" tex="AB = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (\text{column } k \text{ of } A)(\text{row } k \text{ of } B)" cite="reading3" at="pp. 8–9" />

**The inverse** (Lecture 3). A square matrix $A$ is invertible when some matrix $A^{-1}$ gives (p. 12)

<Formula label="The inverse" tex="A^{-1}A = I = AA^{-1}" cite="reading3" at="p. 12" />

If $Ax = 0$ for some nonzero $x$, then $A$ has no inverse (pp. 14–16).

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Two pictures of a 2 × 2 system" points={16}>

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Solve the system for $x$ and $y$.

<Hint>

Eliminate: subtract a multiple of the first equation from the second so that $x$ drops out. Then substitute back.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-x" label="$x =$" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-x" />

<Answer id="p1-a-y" label="$y =$" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-y" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the row picture the answer is where the two lines meet; elimination finds that point.

<Step title="Eliminate and substitute">

Subtract $\frac12$ of the first equation from the second: $(1 - 1)x + (-1 - \frac32)y = -3 - 2$, so $-\frac52y = -5$ and $y = 2$. Then $2x + 6 = 4$ gives $x = -1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Both equations: $2(-1) + 3(2) = 4$ and $-1 - 2 = -3$. In the column picture, $-1$ times the column $(2, 1)$ plus $2$ times the column $(3, -1)$ is $(-2 + 6, -1 - 2) = (4, -3)$, which is $b$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Keep the same left-hand sides. Which right-hand side $b = (b_1, b_2)$ has the solution $x = 2$, $y = 1$?

<Hint>

Read the system as $x$ times column 1 plus $y$ times column 2 of the matrix. Here you know the amounts.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b-1" label="$b_1 =$" value="7" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-b-1" />

<Answer id="p1-b-2" label="$b_2 =$" value="1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-b-2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $Ax$ is a combination of the columns of $A$: here, two of the first column and one of the second.

<Step title="By columns">

<Formula label="The combination" tex="2\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + 1\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 19 (multiplication by columns; the numbers are our own)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

By rows, the two dot products are $(2, 3)\cdot(2, 1) = 7$ and $(1, -1)\cdot(2, 1) = 1$: the same $b$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Now replace the coefficient $-1$ of $y$ in the second equation by a number $a$, so that the matrix becomes $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & a \end{pmatrix}$. For which $a$ is this matrix singular? For that $a$, and the right-hand side $(4, -3)$ of the original system, what does the row picture show?

<Hint>

The matrix is singular when its two columns lie on one line through the origin: when one column is a multiple of the other.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the row picture, compare the two equations: is the second one a multiple of the first, left side and right side?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c-a" label="The matrix is singular for $a =$" value="1.5" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-c-a" />

<Answer id="p1-c-pic" label="For that $a$ and the right-hand side $(4, -3)$, the row picture shows"
  choices={["two lines that cross at one point", "one line drawn twice: every point of it solves", "two parallel lines, so there is no solution", "two lines that cross at the origin"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Singular means the columns are parallel. Then the two lines of the row picture have the same slope, and the right-hand side decides whether they coincide.

<Step title="Parallel columns">

The column $(3, a)$ is a multiple of $(2, 1)$ only if it is $\frac32$ times it: $a = \frac32 = 1.5$. Then every combination of the columns lies on the line through $(2, 1)$, so most right-hand sides cannot be reached.

</Step>

<Step title="The row picture">

With $a = 1.5$ the equations are $2x + 3y = 4$ and $x + 1.5y = -3$. Twice the second is $2x + 3y = -6$: the same left side as the first equation with a different right side. The two lines are parallel and never meet, so there is no solution.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

In the column picture: $(4, -3)$ is not a multiple of $(2, 1)$, so it cannot be reached. A right-hand side such as $(4, 2)$, on the line through $(2, 1)$, would give one line drawn twice and infinitely many solutions.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Keep the right-hand side $(4, -3)$ and the matrix of part (c), with $a \neq 1.5$. Solve by elimination and give $y$ as a formula in $a$.

<Hint>

Subtract $\frac12$ of row 1 from row 2, on both sides. The second pivot then depends on $a$.

</Hint>

**The idea.** One elimination step leaves a triangular system; its second pivot is $a - \frac32$.

<Step title="Eliminate">

Row 2 minus $\frac12$ row 1 gives $(a - \frac32)y = -3 - 2 = -5$. So

<Formula label="y as a function of a" tex="y = \frac{-5}{a - \frac32} = -\frac{10}{2a - 3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $a = -1$: $y = -10/(-5) = 2$, as in part (a). As $a$ approaches $1.5$ the second pivot goes to zero and $y$ grows without bound: the two lines turn parallel and their crossing point runs away, as part (c) found.

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Columns in three dimensions" points={14}>

Take the matrix with columns $(1, 0, 1)$, $(2, 1, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 3)$:

<Formula label="A 3 × 3 matrix" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 9–12 (the column picture; the matrix is our own)" />

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the combination $x = (x_1, x_2, x_3)$ of the columns that gives $b = (5, 3, 4)$.

<Hint>

Write out the three equations: the second row has only $x_2$ and $x_3$, the third only $x_1$ and $x_3$. Express $x_1$ and $x_2$ through $x_3$ and put them into the first row.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a-1" label="$x_1 =$" value="1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-a-1" />

<Answer id="p2-a-2" label="$x_2 =$" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-a-2" />

<Answer id="p2-a-3" label="$x_3 =$" value="1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-a-3" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The column picture asks for the amounts of each column; the three equations say the same thing row by row.

<Step title="Solve">

The rows are $x_1 + 2x_2 = 5$, $x_2 + x_3 = 3$ and $x_1 + 3x_3 = 4$. From the last two, $x_2 = 3 - x_3$ and $x_1 = 4 - 3x_3$. The first row becomes $4 - 3x_3 + 6 - 2x_3 = 5$, so $5x_3 = 5$ and $x_3 = 1$. Then $x_2 = 2$ and $x_1 = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

<Formula label="The combination" tex="1\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} + 2\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + 1\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 12 (the column picture; the numbers are our own)" />

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Now replace the third column by $(4, 3, -2)$. Show that the new third column is a combination $\alpha\,(1, 0, 1) + \beta\,(2, 1, 0)$ of the first two, and give $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

<Hint>

The middle component of $\alpha(1, 0, 1) + \beta(2, 1, 0)$ involves only $\beta$, the last one only $\alpha$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b-alpha" label="$\alpha =$" value="-2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-b-alpha" />

<Answer id="p2-b-beta" label="$\beta =$" value="3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-b-beta" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Match the components one at a time, starting with those that contain only one unknown.

<Step title="Match components">

The middle components give $\beta = 3$, the last ones $\alpha = -2$. The first component is then $\alpha + 2\beta = -2 + 6 = 4$, which agrees with the first entry of $(4, 3, -2)$. So the new third column is $-2$ times column 1 plus $3$ times column 2.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$-2(1, 0, 1) + 3(2, 1, 0) = (-2 + 6, 3, -2) = (4, 3, -2)$. All three columns now lie in one plane through the origin: the new matrix is singular (Lecture 1 transcript, p. 16).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

With the new third column of part (b), the right-hand side $b = (5, 3, b_3)$ can be reached for only one value of $b_3$. Find it. How many solutions $x$ does $Ax = b$ then have?

<Hint>

Every combination of the three columns is a combination of the first two. Which $b_3$ makes $(5, 3, b_3)$ a combination of $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(2, 1, 0)$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the count: start from one solution and add a combination of the columns that gives the zero vector.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c-b3" label="$b_3 =$" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-c-b3" />

<Answer id="p2-c-count" label="For that $b_3$, the number of solutions is"
  choices={["none", "exactly one", "exactly three", "infinitely many"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The reachable right-hand sides form the plane of the first two columns; inside that plane there are many ways to reach a point, because the third column adds nothing new.

<Step title="The reachable b">

Of the first two columns, only $(2, 1, 0)$ has a middle component, so it is taken $3$ times to make the middle $3$. The first component then needs $5 - 6 = -1$ of $(1, 0, 1)$, and that fixes the last component: $b_3 = -1$. Any other $b_3$ lies off the plane.

</Step>

<Step title="How many solutions">

One solution is $x = (-1, 3, 0)$. By part (b), $x = (-2, 3, -1)$ gives $Ax = 0$, so adding any multiple of $(-2, 3, -1)$ to $(-1, 3, 0)$ gives another solution: infinitely many.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$-1(1, 0, 1) + 3(2, 1, 0) = (5, 3, -1)$. With $t = 1$: $x = (-3, 6, -1)$ gives $-3(1, 0, 1) + 6(2, 1, 0) - (4, 3, -2) = (-3 + 12 - 4, 6 - 3, -3 + 2) = (5, 3, -1)$ as well.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Elimination and back substitution" points={20}>

Solve by elimination:

<Formula label="The system" tex="2x + y + z = 3, \qquad 4x - y + 3z = 11, \qquad -2x + 5y + z = -5" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2 (the method; the system is our own)" />

Its matrix is $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & -1 & 3 \\ -2 & 5 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and its right-hand side $b = (3, 11, -5)$. The multiplier $\ell_{21}$ is the number of times row 1 is subtracted from row 2, and so on.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Find the three multipliers $\ell_{21}$, $\ell_{31}$, $\ell_{32}$ and the second and third pivots. (The first pivot is the $2$ in the top left.)

<Hint>

A multiplier is the entry to be removed divided by the pivot above it. A multiplier can be negative: subtracting a negative multiple of row 1 means adding.

</Hint>

<Hint>

After the first column is cleared, the second pivot is the new entry in row 2, column 2; $\ell_{32}$ uses that new entry, not the old $-1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a-l21" label="$\ell_{21} =$" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-a-l21" />

<Answer id="p3-a-l31" label="$\ell_{31} =$" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-a-l31" />

<Answer id="p3-a-l32" label="$\ell_{32} =$" value="-2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-a-l32" />

<Answer id="p3-a-p2" label="The second pivot is" value="-3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-a-p2" />

<Answer id="p3-a-p3" label="The third pivot is" value="4" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-a-p3" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Clear column 1 below the first pivot, then column 2 below the second pivot.

<Step title="Column 1">

$\ell_{21} = 4/2 = 2$: row 2 minus 2 times row 1 is $(0, -3, 1)$. $\ell_{31} = -2/2 = -1$: row 3 minus $(-1)$ times row 1, that is row 3 plus row 1, is $(0, 6, 2)$. The second pivot is $-3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Column 2">

$\ell_{32} = 6/(-3) = -2$: the new row 3 minus $(-2)$ times the new row 2 is $(0, 6 - 6, 2 + 2) = (0, 0, 4)$. The third pivot is $4$:

<Formula label="The upper triangular U" tex="A \to \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 & 1 \\ 0 & 6 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = U" cite="reading2" at="pp. 3–5 (the steps; the numbers are our own)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

No pivot is zero, so no row exchange was needed and $A$ is invertible. The lesson's rule that the product of the pivots is the determinant gives $2 \cdot (-3) \cdot 4 = -24$; a later set computes determinants directly.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Apply the same steps to the right-hand side to get $c$, with $Ux = c$. Give the last entry of $c$, then solve by back substitution.

<Hint>

Carry $b$ along as a fourth column (the augmented matrix): every row operation of part (a) acts on it too.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b-c3" label="The last entry of $c$ is" value="8" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-b-c3" />

<Answer id="p3-b-x" label="$x =$" value="1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-b-x" />

<Answer id="p3-b-y" label="$y =$" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-b-y" />

<Step title="The right-hand side">

$11 - 2 \cdot 3 = 5$ and $-5 + 3 = -2$ after column 1; then $-2 + 2 \cdot 5 = 8$ after column 2. So $c = (3, 5, 8)$ and

<Formula label="The triangular system" tex="2x + y + z = 3, \qquad -3y + z = 5, \qquad 4z = 8" cite="reading2" at="p. 9 (back substitution; the numbers are our own)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Back substitution">

$4z = 8$ gives $z = 2$. Then $-3y + 2 = 5$ gives $y = -1$. Then $2x - 1 + 2 = 3$ gives $x = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Put $(1, -1, 2)$ into the original equations: $2 - 1 + 2 = 3$, $4 + 1 + 6 = 11$, $-2 - 5 + 2 = -5$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={7}>

Two changes to $A$, one at a time. First change the entry in row 3, column 3 (the coefficient of $z$ in the third equation) from $1$ to a number $q$: for which $q$ does elimination break down for good? Then go back to $A$ and change the entry in row 2, column 2 from $-1$ to a number $s$: for which $s$ is there a zero in the second pivot position, and what happens then?

<Hint>

Redo part (a) with the letter in place of the number. Only some of the entries it produces change.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A zero in a pivot position is harmless if some row below has a nonzero entry in that column.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c-q" label="Elimination breaks down for good when $q =$" value="-3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-c-q" />

<Answer id="p3-c-s" label="The second pivot position holds a zero when $s =$" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-c-s" />

<Answer id="p3-c-what" label="For that $s$:"
  choices={["no row below can help: the matrix is singular", "a row exchange gives three nonzero pivots", "elimination goes on without any change", "only an exchange of two columns can help"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow the changed entry through the elimination steps and see which pivot it lands in.

<Step title="The entry q">

The steps of part (a) do not depend on the entry in row 3, column 3; they only add to it. After column 1 it is $q + 1$; after column 2 it is $q + 1 + 2 \cdot 1 = q + 3$. That is the third pivot, and nothing lies below it to exchange with: elimination fails for good at $q = -3$, and the matrix is then singular.

</Step>

<Step title="The entry s">

After column 1, row 2 is $(4, s, 3) - 2(2, 1, 1) = (0, s - 2, 1)$, while row 3 is still $(0, 6, 2)$. So $s = 2$ puts a zero in the second pivot position. Row 3 has a $6$ there, so exchange rows 2 and 3:

<Formula label="After the exchange" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 6 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 5–6 (a row exchange; the numbers are our own)" />

This is already upper triangular, with the pivots $2$, $6$, $1$: the changed matrix is invertible.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

With $q = -3$ the changed matrix sends $x = (-2, 1, 3)$ to $(-4 + 1 + 3, -8 - 1 + 9, 4 + 5 - 9) = (0, 0, 0)$: a nonzero $x$ with zero output, so that matrix has no inverse (Lecture 3 transcript, p. 16).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Elimination and permutation matrices" points={16}>

Use the matrix $A$ of Problem 3 and the elimination matrices $E_{21}$, $E_{31}$, $E_{32}$ of its elimination: each is the $3 \times 3$ identity with $-\ell$ in the place of its multiplier, so that $E_{32}E_{31}E_{21}A = U$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Multiply the three into one matrix $E = E_{32}E_{31}E_{21}$, with $EA = U$. What is the entry of $E$ in row 3, column 1?

<Hint>

Write the three matrices out first. Then compute $E_{31}E_{21}$, and let $E_{32}$ act on its rows: on the left, $E_{32}$ changes row 3 only.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The entry is not one of the multipliers. Row 3 of $E$ records everything that happened to row 3, including the copy of row 1 that came in with row 2.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="The entry of $E$ in row 3, column 1 is" value="-3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p4-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each elimination matrix is the identity with one changed entry, and a matrix on the left combines rows.

<Step title="The three matrices">

<Exercise id="p5" title="Four ways to multiply, and blocks" points={18}>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Let $P$ now be the permutation matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. Which matrix is its inverse?

<Hint>

Say in words what $P$ does to the rows of a matrix it multiplies on the left. How many times must you do it to get every row back in its place?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-d" label="The inverse of $P$ is"
  choices={["$P$ itself", "$P^2 = PP$", "$-P$", "none: $P$ is singular"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A permutation can always be undone by another permutation; here, by doing the same one twice more.

<Step title="What P does">

$PA$ has row 3 of $A$ on top, then row 1, then row 2: the rows move down one place, the last coming round to the top. Doing this three times brings every row back, so $P^3 = I$, which says $P^2 \cdot P = I = P \cdot P^2$. The inverse is $P^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$P^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, and $P^2P$: row 1 of $P^2$ picks row 2 of $P$, $(1, 0, 0)$; row 2 picks row 3, $(0, 1, 0)$; row 3 picks row 1, $(0, 0, 1)$. That is $I$. $P$ is not its own inverse, because $PP = P^2$ is not $I$.

</Step>

</Solution>
