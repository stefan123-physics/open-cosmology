The last lecture on determinants is about their applications: one number that gives formulas for things computed so far without formulas (transcript, p. 1). It continues [determinant formulas and cofactors](/courses/18-06/determinant-formulas-cofactors).

## The inverse from cofactors

The $2 \times 2$ inverse is known (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Two by two inverse" tex="\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The entries are cofactors: $d$ is the cofactor of $a$, and $-b$ is the cofactor of $c$ (the minus because $2 + 1$ is odd), sitting in the position of $b$: the cofactor matrix is **transposed** (pp. 1–3). In general (p. 2):

<Formula label="Inverse from cofactors" tex="A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\,C^T" cite="reading1" at="pp. 2–3" />

For $3 \times 3$, $\det A$ is a sum of products of three entries and each cofactor a product of two, such as $ei - fh$ for the corner entry $a$ of a matrix with rows $(a, b, c)$, $(d, e, f)$, $(g, h, i)$ (pp. 3–4). Gauss–Jordan was the better algorithm; this is the algebra behind it (p. 4).

**Why it is right** (pp. 4–9). It is enough to check (p. 5)

<Formula label="The identity behind the inverse" tex="AC^T = (\det A)\,I" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

On the diagonal, row $i$ of $A$ times column $i$ of $C^T$ (the cofactors of row $i$) is $a_{i1}C_{i1} + \cdots + a_{in}C_{in}$, the cofactor formula: $\det A$ (pp. 5–6). Off the diagonal, row 1 of $A$ times the cofactors of row 2 must give 0 (p. 7). The reason: it is the cofactor expansion of a matrix in which row 2 has been replaced by a copy of row 1, a matrix with **two equal rows**, so its determinant is zero (pp. 8–9). For $2 \times 2$: the matrix with rows $(a, b)$ and $(a, b)$ has determinant $a \cdot b + b \cdot (-a) = 0$ (pp. 8–9). The formula also shows how $A^{-1}$ responds when one entry of $A$ changes: through the determinant and the cofactors (pp. 9–10).

## Cramer's rule

The solution of $Ax = b$ is $x = A^{-1}b = C^Tb/\det A$ (transcript, p. 10). The products of cofactors with the numbers $b_1, \dots, b_n$ are themselves a determinant (pp. 11–13):

<Formula label="Cramer's rule" tex="x_j = \frac{\det B_j}{\det A}, \qquad B_j = A \text{ with column } j \text{ replaced by } b" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–13" />

Expanding $\det B_1$ by cofactors down its first column gives $b_1C_{11} + b_2C_{21} + \cdots$, exactly the first entry of $C^Tb$ (p. 12). Is the rule good in practice? It needs $n + 1$ determinants, and computing them takes "approximately forever": **a disastrous way to go**, while elimination produces $x$ in an instant (p. 13). Strang recalls a popular book from his childhood, *Mathematics for the Million*, that praised Cramer's rule, and jokes that it should have been "for the millionaire" (p. 13). (He names the author as Bell; the book of that title is by Lancelot Hogben.) The formulas are worth having for algebra, not for computation; MATLAB never uses them (p. 14).

## The determinant is a volume

Take the rows of a $3 \times 3$ matrix as three edges from the origin and complete the box, properly a **parallelepiped**, whose faces are parallelograms (transcript, pp. 14–16):

<Formula label="Determinant and volume" tex="|\det A| = \text{volume of the box with edges the rows of } A" cite="reading1" at="pp. 14–16" />

The sign of $\det A$ says whether the box is right-handed or left-handed; exchanging two edges flips it without changing the volume (p. 16). Since $\det A^T = \det A$, columns would do as well (p. 17).

**Checking it** (pp. 16–21). The elegant proof shows that volume has the three properties that define the determinant.

- $A = I$: the unit cube, volume 1 (pp. 16–17).
- $A = Q$, an orthogonal matrix: a rotated unit cube, volume 1 (pp. 17–18).
- Doubling one edge of a rectangular box doubles the volume (two copies fit together), and doubling a row doubles the determinant: property 3A (pp. 19–20).
- Exchanging two rows does not change the box: property 2 up to sign (pp. 20–21).

For the rotated cube the determinant agrees, by properties 9 and 10 (pp. 18–19):

<Formula label="Determinant of an orthogonal matrix" tex="Q^TQ = I \;\Rightarrow\; (\det Q^T)(\det Q) = (\det Q)^2 = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \det Q = \pm 1" cite="reading1" at="pp. 18–19" />

Property 3B, linearity in a row (for $2 \times 2$: the area for the row $(a + a', b + b')$ splits into the areas for $(a, b)$ and $(a', b')$), needs a figure; Strang leaves it to the book (pp. 21, 24).

**Areas in the plane** (pp. 21–24). The parallelogram with sides $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$, with fourth corner $(a + c, b + d)$, has area $|ad - bc|$ (p. 22). The usual way, base times height, brings square roots and worse; the determinant needs only the coordinates (p. 22). The triangle with corners $(0, 0)$, $(a, b)$, $(c, d)$ is half of it (p. 23). For a triangle with **any** three corners (p. 24):

<Formula label="Area of a triangle" tex="\text{area} = \frac{1}{2}\left|\det\begin{pmatrix} x_1 & y_1 & 1 \\ x_2 & y_2 & 1 \\ x_3 & y_3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\right|" cite="reading1" at="pp. 23–24" />

Subtracting row 1 from the other rows kills two of the ones and amounts to moving the triangle to start at the origin (p. 24). (Strang writes the determinant and says it is half of a parallelogram; the factor $\frac{1}{2}$ is written into the formula here.) Eigenvalues come next (p. 24).

<WorkedExample title="Cofactors, Cramer and area of your own">
<Problem>
(a) For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}$, find $A^{-1}$ from cofactors and solve $Ax = (8, 13)$ by Cramer's rule. (b) Find the area of the triangle with corners $(1, 1)$, $(4, 2)$, $(2, 5)$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Inverse">
$\det A = 15 - 8 = 7$. Cofactors: $C_{11} = 5$, $C_{12} = -4$, $C_{21} = -2$, $C_{22} = 3$. So $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{7}\begin{pmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ -4 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Cramer">
$x_1 = \det\begin{pmatrix} 8 & 2 \\ 13 & 5 \end{pmatrix}/7 = (40 - 26)/7 = 2$ and $x_2 = \det\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 8 \\ 4 & 13 \end{pmatrix}/7 = (39 - 32)/7 = 1$. Check: $3 \cdot 2 + 2 = 8$ and $4 \cdot 2 + 5 = 13$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Area">
$\det\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 5 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = 1(2 - 5) - 1(4 - 2) + 1(20 - 4) = 11$, so the area is $5.5$. Check by moving $(1, 1)$ to the origin: the sides $(3, 1)$ and $(1, 4)$ give $\frac{1}{2}(12 - 1) = 5.5$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $A^{-1} = C^T/\det A$, because $AC^T = (\det A)I$: own cofactors give $\det A$, another row's cofactors give 0.
- Cramer's rule: $x_j = \det B_j/\det A$. A fine formula, a poor algorithm.
- $|\det A|$ is the volume of the box spanned by the rows; $\det Q = \pm1$ for orthogonal $Q$.
- Parallelogram area $|ad - bc|$; triangle area half of that, or $\frac{1}{2}|\det|$ with a column of ones for any corners.
