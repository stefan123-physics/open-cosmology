This key lecture gives four words clear meanings: **independence**, **span**, **basis** and **dimension** (transcript, p. 1). Note what they apply to: a set of vectors is independent, spans, or is a basis; a space has a dimension. It continues [the complete solution of Ax = b](/courses/18-06/solving-ax-b-complete-solution).

## More unknowns than equations

First a fact (transcript, pp. 1–2). If $A$ is $m \times n$ with $n > m$, more unknowns than equations, then **$Ax = 0$ has nonzero solutions**. The reason is the algorithm: elimination finds at most $m$ pivots, so at least $n - m$ variables are free, and giving a free variable the value 1 produces a solution that is not all zeros (p. 2).

## Independence

<Formula label="Independence" tex="c_1x_1 + c_2x_2 + \cdots + c_nx_n \ne 0 \quad \text{unless } c_1 = c_2 = \cdots = c_n = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Vectors $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are **independent** if no combination gives the zero vector except the zero combination; if some other combination gives zero, they are **dependent** (p. 3). Examples in the plane (pp. 4–6):

- $v$ and $2v$ are dependent: $2v_1 - v_2 = 0$ (p. 4);
- a vector and the zero vector are dependent: take none of the first and, say, six of the zero vector. **If one vector is zero, independence is dead** (pp. 4–5);
- two vectors in different directions are independent, but **three vectors in the plane are always dependent** (pp. 5–6).

The last follows from the fact above. Put the vectors in the columns of a $2 \times 3$ matrix; there is a free variable, so some $c = (c_1, c_2, c_3) \ne 0$ has $Ac = 0$, a combination of the columns giving zero (p. 6).

**Columns of a matrix** (pp. 7–8). For vectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ in the columns of $A$, a combination is $Ac$, so:

<Formula label="Independence and the nullspace" tex="\text{independent} \;\text{ exactly when }\; N(A) = \{0\} \;\text{ exactly when }\; r = n \text{ (no free variables)}; \qquad \text{dependent} \;\text{ exactly when }\; Ac = 0 \text{ for some } c \ne 0 \;\text{ exactly when }\; r < n" cite="reading1" at="pp. 7–8" />

A free column is a combination of earlier columns, so with independent columns all $n$ are pivot columns (p. 7). The definition itself needs no matrix, and later the vectors need not be columns at all (p. 8).

## Span and basis

A set of vectors **spans** a space when the space consists of **all their combinations**; the columns of a matrix span its column space (pp. 8–9). The space they span is the smallest space containing them (p. 9). Spanning vectors may or may not be independent (p. 9).

A **basis** for a vector space is a sequence of vectors with two properties: **they are independent, and they span the space**. Enough vectors, and not too many (p. 10). A basis tells everything about a subspace (p. 10).

**Bases of $\mathrm{R}^3$** (pp. 11–14). The columns of the identity, along the $x$, $y$ and $z$ axes, are one basis: $c_1(1, 0, 0) + c_2(0, 1, 0) + c_3(0, 0, 1) = 0$ forces all $c$'s to be 0, as $N(I) = \{0\}$ (pp. 11–12). The pair $(1, 1, 2)$ and $(2, 2, 5)$ is independent but does not span $\mathrm{R}^3$ (p. 12). Adding their sum $(3, 3, 7)$ would be a goof: it lies in their plane (p. 13). For $n$ vectors in $\mathrm{R}^n$ the test is (pp. 13–14):

<Formula label="Basis of Rⁿ" tex="v_1, \dots, v_n \text{ are a basis of } \mathrm{R}^n \;\text{ exactly when }\; \text{the } n \times n \text{ matrix with these columns is invertible}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 13–14" />

(Strang tries $(3, 3, 8)$ as the third vector and says he believes it gives a basis, p. 13. It does not: all three vectors have equal first and second components, so they lie in the plane $x = y$; indeed $(3, 3, 8) = 2(2, 2, 5) - (1, 1, 2)$, and the matrix with these columns has two equal rows and is singular. The other vector he mentions, $(5, 7, -14)$, would work as the third vector: that matrix has determinant $-2$. Strang corrects the error at the start of Lecture 10.)

The pair $(1, 1, 2)$, $(2, 2, 5)$ is still a basis of something: the **plane** it spans (p. 14). Adding $(3, 3, 7)$ would still span the plane, but the three would no longer be independent (p. 14).

## Dimension

Bases are far from unique: the columns of any invertible $3 \times 3$ matrix are a basis of $\mathrm{R}^3$ (transcript, p. 15). But **every basis of a given space has the same number of vectors**, three for $\mathrm{R}^3$, $n$ for $\mathrm{R}^n$, and likewise for a column space, a nullspace, or any other space (pp. 15–16). That number is the **dimension** of the space (p. 16):

<Formula label="Dimension" tex="\text{dim}(\text{space}) = \text{number of vectors in any basis}" cite="reading1" at="p. 16" />

If a space has dimension 6, seven vectors are too many and five not enough (p. 16).

## Example: column space and nullspace

Take the matrix whose third column is the sum of the first two and whose fourth repeats the first (transcript, p. 17):

<Formula label="The example" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 17" />

The four columns span $C(A)$ by definition, but they are not independent: $(-1, -1, 1, 0)$ is in the nullspace (pp. 17–18). The natural basis of $C(A)$ is the **pivot columns**, 1 and 2; columns 3 and 4 add nothing (pp. 18–19). So (p. 19):

<Formula label="Rank and the column space" tex="\operatorname{rank}(A) = r = \text{number of pivot columns} = \text{dim}\, C(A) = 2" cite="reading1" at="pp. 19–20" />

A matrix has a rank; a space has a dimension; and the rank of a matrix is the dimension of its column space (pp. 19–20). Other bases: columns 1 and 3, 2 and 3, or 2 and 4, or vectors not among the columns at all, such as $(2, 2, 2)$ and $(7, 5, 7)$, the sum of all four columns (pp. 20–21). **When the dimension is known, the right number of independent vectors is automatically a basis**: if they failed to span, a further vector could be added independently, too many for the dimension (p. 21).

For the nullspace, $(-1, -1, 1, 0)$ alone does not span it (p. 22). Setting the free variables to $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ gives the two special solutions, which say how the columns depend on each other: column 3 is column 1 plus column 2, and column 4 equals column 1 (pp. 22–23). They are a basis of $N(A)$ (pp. 23–24):

<Formula label="Dimension of the nullspace" tex="N(A) \text{ has basis } \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}; \qquad \text{dim}\, N(A) = \text{number of free variables} = n - r" cite="reading1" at="pp. 22–24" />

<WorkedExample title="Testing independence and finding a basis">
<Problem>
(a) Find a basis and the dimension of the space spanned by $v_1 = (1, 0, 1, 2)$, $v_2 = (0, 1, 1, 1)$, $v_3 = (-1, 2, 1, 0)$, $v_4 = (2, 1, 3, 5)$, and a basis of the nullspace of the matrix with these columns. Is $(1, 2, 3, 4)$ in the span? (b) Are $(1, 1, 0)$, $(0, 1, 1)$, $(1, 0, 1)$ a basis of $\mathrm{R}^3$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Dependences">
$v_3 = 2v_2 - v_1$ and $v_4 = 2v_1 + v_2$, while $v_1$ and $v_2$ are not multiples of each other. So $v_1, v_2$ are a basis of the span, which has dimension 2: a plane in $\mathrm{R}^4$. The matrix is $4 \times 4$ of rank 2, so its nullspace has dimension $4 - 2 = 2$, with basis $(1, -2, 1, 0)$ and $(-2, -1, 0, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) A vector in the span?">
$av_1 + bv_2 = (a, b, a + b, 2a + b)$. Matching $(1, 2, 3, 4)$ needs $a = 1$, $b = 2$, and then $a + b = 3$ and $2a + b = 4$ both agree. So yes: $(1, 2, 3, 4) = v_1 + 2v_2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Three vectors in R³">
The matrix with these columns has determinant $1 \cdot (1 - 0) - 0 + 1 \cdot (1 - 0) = 2 \ne 0$, so it is invertible and the three vectors are a basis.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- More unknowns than equations: $Ax = 0$ has nonzero solutions.
- Independent: only the zero combination gives zero; for columns, $N(A) = \{0\}$ and $r = n$.
- Span: all combinations. Basis: independent and spanning. $n$ vectors are a basis of $\mathrm{R}^n$ exactly when the matrix of them is invertible.
- Every basis has the same number of vectors, the dimension. $\text{dim}\, C(A) = r$; $\text{dim}\, N(A) = n - r$.
