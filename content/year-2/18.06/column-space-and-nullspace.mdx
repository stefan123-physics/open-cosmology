This lecture is officially the start of vector spaces and subspaces, and of the two subspaces Strang cares most about: the **column space** and the **nullspace** of a matrix (transcript, p. 1). It continues [transposes, permutations and vector spaces](/courses/18-06/transposes-permutations-vector-spaces).

## Subspaces, unions and intersections

A vector space allows two operations, adding and multiplying by numbers; together they mean that **all linear combinations stay in the space** (transcript, p. 1):

<Formula label="Closed under linear combinations" tex="v, w \text{ in the space} \;\Rightarrow\; cv + dw \text{ in the space, for all numbers } c, d" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

$\mathrm{R}^3$ is one; inside it, a plane $P$ and a line $L$ through the origin are **subspaces**, vector spaces inside a vector space (p. 2). Every subspace contains the zero vector (p. 2).

Is the **union** of $P$ and $L$, all vectors in $P$ or $L$ or both, a subspace? **No**: adding a vector of $P$ to one of $L$ normally lands outside both (pp. 3–4). The **intersection**, the vectors in both, is a subspace; for a line not lying in the plane it is the zero vector alone, which is a subspace (pp. 4–5). This holds for any two subspaces $S$ and $T$ (pp. 5–6): if $v$ and $w$ are in both, then $v + w$ is in $S$ (as $S$ is a subspace) and in $T$ (as $T$ is), and so is $7v$. The intersection is usually smaller, but it is a subspace (p. 6).

## The column space

The example (transcript, p. 7):

<Formula label="The example matrix" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 4 & 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

Its columns have four components, so its **column space** $C(A)$ is a subspace of $\mathrm{R}^4$: the number of rows decides (pp. 7–8). Three vectors alone are not a subspace; $C(A)$ is **all their linear combinations**, the smallest space containing the columns (p. 8). Three vectors cannot fill all of four-dimensional space, as the class guesses, so it is something smaller (p. 9).

**Which $b$ can be reached?** $Ax = b$ is four equations in three unknowns, and for most $b$ there is no solution (p. 10):

<Formula label="Ax = b written out" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 4 & 1 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \\ b_4 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

But some right-hand sides work: $b = 0$ (take $x = 0$), $b = (1, 2, 3, 4)$ (take $x = (1, 0, 0)$, one of the first column), $b = (1, 1, 1, 1)$ (take $x = (0, 1, 0)$) (pp. 11–12). Choose any $x$ and see what $b$ comes out: the reachable $b$ are exactly the combinations of the columns (p. 12).

<Callout kind="key" title="When is Ax = b solvable?">
$Ax = b$ has a solution **exactly when $b$ is in the column space** $C(A)$. The coefficients of the combination that gives $b$ are the solution $x$ (pp. 12–13).
</Callout>

How big is $C(A)$ here? Column 3 is the sum of columns 1 and 2, so it adds nothing new: two columns give the same column space (pp. 13–14). Strang keeps the first columns that are not dependent and will call them **pivot columns**; column 1 could have been dropped instead (p. 14). So $C(A)$ is a **two-dimensional subspace of $\mathrm{R}^4$**, a plane (pp. 14–15).

## The nullspace

The **nullspace** $N(A)$ holds not right-hand sides but solutions: all $x$ with $Ax = 0$ (transcript, p. 15). Its vectors have three components, so for this example it lies in $\mathrm{R}^3$, while the column space was in $\mathrm{R}^4$. For an $m \times n$ matrix, $C(A)$ is in $\mathrm{R}^m$ and $N(A)$ in $\mathrm{R}^n$ (p. 16).

For a small example the nullspace can be found by eye (elimination will do it in general, p. 16). Zero is always in it (p. 17). So is $(1, 1, -1)$: column 1 plus column 2 minus column 3 gives zero (p. 17). So is $(2, 2, -2)$, and in fact every multiple (p. 18):

<Formula label="The nullspace of the example" tex="N(A) = \left\{\, c\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} : c \text{ any number} \,\right\}" cite="reading1" at="p. 18" />

a **line through the origin in $\mathrm{R}^3$** (p. 19).

**Why the nullspace is always a subspace** (pp. 19–20). If $Av = 0$ and $Aw = 0$, then by the distributive law of matrix multiplication (p. 20)

<Formula label="Closed under addition and multiples" tex="A(v + w) = Av + Aw = 0 + 0 = 0, \qquad A(12v) = 12\,Av = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 20" />

## Solutions of Ax = b do not form a subspace

Change the right-hand side to $b = (1, 2, 3, 4)$ (transcript, p. 21). There are solutions, $(1, 0, 0)$ and also $(0, -1, 1)$ (minus column 2 plus column 3) (pp. 21–22). But **they do not form a subspace: zero is not a solution** (p. 22). Strang says they form something like a line or plane that misses the origin, and leaves this case at "maybe a line" (pp. 22–23). (Not in the lecture: since the nullspace here is a line, so is this set, the line through $(1, 0, 0)$ along the nullspace direction; Lecture 8 shows this in general.) Every solution is $(1, 0, 0) + c(1, 1, -1)$; with $c = -1$ that gives $(0, -1, 1)$.

## Two ways to describe a subspace

The two subspaces of this lecture show the two natural ways (transcript, p. 23):

- **give some vectors and take all their combinations**, as for the column space;
- **give equations the vectors must satisfy**, as for the nullspace, which then has to be worked out.

The next lecture finds the nullspace systematically for matrices too big to see by eye (p. 23).

<WorkedExample title="Column space and nullspace of your own">
<Problem>
Let $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & -1 \\ 2 & 0 & 4 \\ 1 & 3 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$. (a) Find $N(B)$. (b) Solve $Bx = (3, 1, 4, 5)$ completely. (c) Is $(1, 0, 0, 0)$ in $C(B)$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A dependent column">
Column 3 is $2(\text{column } 1) - (\text{column } 2)$: $2(1, 0, 2, 1) - (1, 1, 0, 3) = (1, -1, 4, -1)$. So $x = (2, -1, -1)$ gives $Bx = 0$. Columns 1 and 2 are not multiples of each other, so this is the only dependence: $N(B)$ is the line of multiples of $(2, -1, -1)$, and $C(B)$ is a plane in $\mathrm{R}^4$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A particular solution plus the nullspace">
$(3, 1, 4, 5) = 2(1, 0, 2, 1) + (1, 1, 0, 3)$, so $x = (2, 1, 0)$ works. Adding any nullspace vector keeps $Bx$ the same: all solutions are $(2, 1, 0) + c(2, -1, -1)$, a line that misses the origin.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Not in the column space">
Any $b$ in $C(B)$ is $x_1(1, 0, 2, 1) + x_2(1, 1, 0, 3)$. A second component of 0 forces $x_2 = 0$, then a third component of 0 forces $x_1 = 0$, leaving $b = 0$, not $(1, 0, 0, 0)$. So $Bx = (1, 0, 0, 0)$ has no solution.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The intersection of two subspaces is a subspace; the union usually is not.
- $C(A)$, all combinations of the columns, lies in $\mathrm{R}^m$; $Ax = b$ is solvable exactly when $b$ is in $C(A)$.
- $N(A)$, all solutions of $Ax = 0$, lies in $\mathrm{R}^n$ and is a subspace by $A(v + w) = Av + Aw$ and $A(cv) = cAv$.
- The solutions of $Ax = b \ne 0$ are not a subspace; in the example they are one solution plus the nullspace line.
- Subspaces are described by vectors to combine or by equations to satisfy.
