This lecture finishes the chapter on elimination with permutations and transposes, and then starts what Strang calls real linear algebra: **spaces** of vectors (transcript, p. 1). It continues [the factorization A = LU](/courses/18-06/factorization-a-lu).

## Row exchanges: PA = LU

A perfectly good invertible matrix may still need row exchanges, when a zero turns up in a pivot position (transcript, p. 1). $A = LU$ assumed none (p. 2). MATLAB in fact exchanges rows more often than algebra requires: it avoids small pivots, which are bad for numerical accuracy (p. 2). Algebra needs an exchange only for a zero, and the general description of elimination is then (p. 2)

<Formula label="Elimination with row exchanges" tex="PA = LU" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

where $P$ puts the rows into an order in which no zero lands in a pivot position. This holds for every invertible $A$; for almost every one $P = I$, and it covers the few that need exchanges (p. 3).

A **permutation matrix** is the identity with its rows reordered, including the do-nothing order $I$ itself (p. 3). There are $n! = n(n - 1)\cdots(2)(1)$ of size $n \times n$: 24 of size 4, and $5! = 120$ of size 5 (p. 4). Every one is invertible, and its inverse is its transpose: in $P^TP$ the ones meet the ones (pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Inverse of a permutation" tex="P^TP = I, \qquad P^{-1} = P^T" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–4" />

Matrices with $Q^TQ = I$ will matter later; the permutations are a small group in the middle of them (p. 4).

## Transposes and symmetric matrices

Transposing turns columns into rows (transcript, pp. 4–5). The $3 \times 2$ matrix with columns $(1, 2, 4)$ and $(3, 3, 1)$ becomes a $2 \times 3$ matrix:

<Formula label="A transpose" tex="R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R^T = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 4–5" />

In symbols, the entry in row $i$ and column $j$ of $A^T$ is the entry in row $j$ and column $i$ of $A$: a flip across the main diagonal (p. 5):

<Formula label="Entries of the transpose" tex="(A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

A matrix is **symmetric** when transposing leaves it unchanged, $A^T = A$: whatever sits above the diagonal is mirrored below it (pp. 5–6). Symmetric matrices come up in a great many applications, and here is where (pp. 6–9): **for any rectangular $R$, $R^TR$ is symmetric.** Strang multiplies out the example and reads off 10, 11 and 7 in the first row, then the symmetric 11 and 7 below (p. 7). With $R$ the $3 \times 2$ matrix above, that $3 \times 3$ product is $RR^T$; the rest of it and the $2 \times 2$ product $R^TR$ are completed here:

<Formula label="A symmetric product (completed here)" tex="RR^T = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 11 & 7 \\ 11 & 13 & 11 \\ 7 & 11 & 17 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R^TR = \begin{pmatrix} 21 & 13 \\ 13 & 19 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

(On p. 7 Strang names the $3 \times 2$ factor $R^T$ rather than $R$, so he calls his product $R^TR$; the names swap between pp. 6 and 7, not the numbers. Both products are symmetric, which is his point.) The 11 in position (1, 2) and the 11 in position (2, 1) come from the same two rows multiplied in either order, so they must agree (p. 7). The proof without numbers uses the rule that transposes of a product come in reverse order, and that transposing twice gives back the matrix (pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Why RᵀR is symmetric" tex="(R^TR)^T = R^T(R^T)^T = R^TR" cite="reading1" at="pp. 8–9" />

## Vector spaces

What do we do with vectors? We **add** them and **multiply them by numbers** (scalars); so we form **linear combinations** (transcript, p. 9). A **vector space** is a set of vectors in which those operations are always possible without leaving the set, with some decent rules satisfied (p. 10). (The book lists eight rules; in practice it is the staying inside that is in doubt, not the rules, p. 13.)

The first example is $\mathrm{R}^2$: all vectors with two real components, such as $(3, 2)$, $(0, 0)$ and $(\pi, e)$ (p. 10). They add a component at a time, $(3, 2) + (\pi, e) = (3 + \pi, 2 + e)$, and the picture is the whole $xy$-plane (p. 11). The **zero vector** cannot be removed: multiplying $(3, 2)$ by 0, or adding $(3, 2)$ and $(-3, -2)$, gives it, so **every vector space contains zero** (p. 12). Likewise $\mathrm{R}^3$ holds all vectors with three real components; $(3, 2, 0)$ lives in $\mathrm{R}^3$, not $\mathrm{R}^2$ (p. 12). In general (p. 13):

<Formula label="The space Rⁿ" tex="\mathrm{R}^n = \text{all column vectors with } n \text{ real components}" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

**Not a vector space** (pp. 13–14). Take the quarter of the plane where both components are nonnegative. Sums stay inside, but multiplying $(3, 2)$ by $-5$ leaves it: it is not **closed** under multiplication by all real numbers (p. 14).

## Subspaces

A **subspace** is a vector space inside another one (transcript, p. 14). If a subspace of $\mathrm{R}^2$ holds one nonzero vector, it holds all its multiples, the whole line, and sums of points on the line stay on it (p. 15). But the line must pass through the origin: a line missing the origin loses the vector when it is multiplied by 0 (pp. 15–16). **Every subspace contains the zero vector** (p. 16).

The subspaces of $\mathrm{R}^2$ are (pp. 16–17):

- all of $\mathrm{R}^2$;
- any line through the origin (which looks like $\mathrm{R}^1$ but is not it: its vectors have two components);
- the zero vector alone (closed, since $0 + 0 = 0$ and $17 \cdot 0 = 0$).

In $\mathrm{R}^3$ they are $\mathrm{R}^3$, planes through the origin, lines through the origin, and the zero vector alone (p. 17).

## The column space

Subspaces come out of matrices (transcript, p. 18). Take $A$ with the columns $(1, 2, 4)$ and $(3, 3, 1)$, vectors in $\mathrm{R}^3$. A subspace containing them must contain their sum $(4, 5, 5)$, zero, and every combination of them (p. 18). All the linear combinations of the columns form the **column space** (p. 19):

<Formula label="Column space" tex="C(A) = \{\, x_1(\text{column } 1) + x_2(\text{column } 2) : x_1, x_2 \text{ any numbers} \,\}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 18–19" />

Geometrically, the two lines of multiples get filled in between, and $C(A)$ is a **plane through the origin** in $\mathrm{R}^3$ (pp. 19–20). Had the two columns been on one line, $C(A)$ would only have been that line (p. 20). Five vectors in $\mathrm{R}^{10}$ would fill some flat subspace through the origin, of dimension up to five, but not $\mathrm{R}^5$, since its vectors have ten components (p. 20). The next lecture asks what this says about $Ax = b$ (p. 21).

<WorkedExample title="Row exchanges, symmetry and subspaces">
<Problem>
(a) Find $P$, $L$ and $U$ with $PA = LU$ for $A = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$. (b) Compute $R^TR$ for $R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$. (c) Which of these subsets of $\mathrm{R}^3$ are subspaces: the vectors with $v_1 + v_2 + v_3 = 0$; those with $v_1 + v_2 + v_3 = 1$; the union of the lines through $(1, 2, 0)$ and $(2, -1, 0)$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Exchange, then eliminate">
The first pivot position holds 0, so exchange rows 1 and 2: $P = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, $PA = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 8 \end{pmatrix}$. Multiplier 2 for row 3: $(0, 2, 2)$; then multiplier 1: $(0, 0, 1)$. So $U = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. Check row 3 of $LU$: $2(1, 1, 3) + (0, 2, 1) + (0, 0, 1) = (2, 4, 8)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A symmetric product">
The entries are dot products of the columns $(1, 2, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 3)$: $R^TR = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 10 \end{pmatrix}$, symmetric as it must be.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Test for zero and closure">
The first set is a plane through the origin: sums and multiples keep the sum of components 0, so it is a subspace. The second does not contain zero, so it is not. The third contains zero and all multiples, but $(1, 2, 0) + (2, -1, 0) = (3, 1, 0)$ lies on neither line: not closed under addition, so not a subspace.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- With row exchanges, $PA = LU$. There are $n!$ permutation matrices of size $n$, and $P^{-1} = P^T$.
- $(A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji}$; symmetric means $A^T = A$; $R^TR$ is symmetric for every $R$.
- A vector space is closed under addition and scalar multiplication, so it contains every linear combination and the zero vector. $\mathrm{R}^n$ is the main example.
- Subspaces of $\mathrm{R}^2$: $\mathrm{R}^2$, lines through the origin, the zero vector. The column space $C(A)$ holds all combinations of the columns of $A$.
