Positive definite matrices bring the whole course together: pivots, determinants, eigenvalues, and something new, the expression $x^TAx$, "the guy to watch" (transcript, p. 1). Three goals: how to tell whether a matrix is positive definite, why it matters (minima), and the geometry (ellipses, not hyperbolas) (p. 1). All matrices here are symmetric. An earlier lecture (Lecture 25) made a [start](/courses/18-06/symmetric-matrices-positive-definite).

## Four tests for a 2 × 2 matrix

For $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{pmatrix}$, each of these is a complete test (pp. 1–2):

<Formula label="Tests for positive definiteness" tex="\text{(1) } \lambda_1 > 0,\ \lambda_2 > 0 \qquad \text{(2) } a > 0,\ ac - b^2 > 0 \qquad \text{(3) pivots } a > 0,\ \frac{ac - b^2}{a} > 0 \qquad \text{(4) } x^TAx > 0 \text{ for all } x \neq 0" cite="reading1" at="pp. 1–2" />

Test (2) uses the upper-left ("northwest") determinants; in (3) the second pivot is the determinant divided by $a$, since the product of the pivots is the determinant (p. 2). Test (4) gets the star: in most presentations it is the **definition**, and the other three are tests for it (p. 2).

**Practice** (pp. 2–3). Take $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & c \end{pmatrix}$. For positive definiteness $2c - 36 > 0$, so $c$ must be more than 18: with $c = 19$ the determinant is $38 - 36 = 2$. At $c = 18$ the matrix is on the borderline, **positive semidefinite**: it is singular (row 2 is three times row 1, rank one), so one eigenvalue is $0$ and the trace gives the other, $20$; and there is only one pivot, $2$ (p. 3).

## The quadratic form x^TAx

Multiply out with $x = (x_1, x_2)$ and $c = 18$ (p. 4):

<Formula label="The quadratic form" tex="x^TAx = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 & x_2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & 18 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{pmatrix} = 2x_1^2 + 12x_1x_2 + 18x_2^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

In general, with $x$ and $y$ for the components, it is $ax^2 + 2bxy + cy^2$: the diagonal entries multiply the squares, and the off-diagonal $b$ appears twice (p. 4). This is a **quadratic form**, purely second degree, with no linear or constant part (pp. 4–5). The definition: $A$ is positive definite if $x^TAx > 0$ for every $x$ except zero (p. 5).

**A failure** (pp. 5–8). Lower $c$ to $7$. The determinant is $14 - 36 = -22$, so the pivots are $2$ and $-11$ and one eigenvalue is negative (p. 5). At $x = (1, -1)$ the form is $2 - 12 + 7 = -3 < 0$ (p. 6). The graph of $f(x, y) = 2x^2 + 12xy + 7y^2$ goes up along the $x$ axis, along the $y$ axis and along $x = y$, but down along $x = -y$: it is a **saddle**, and the origin a saddle point, a maximum in some directions and a minimum in others; the eigenvector directions are the ones to look at (pp. 6–8).

**A success** (pp. 8–12). Raise $c$ to $20$. The determinant is $40 - 36 = 4 = \lambda_1\lambda_2$ and the trace is $22 = \lambda_1 + \lambda_2$; two numbers with positive product and positive sum are both positive (pp. 8–9). The graph of $2x^2 + 12xy + 20y^2$ is a **bowl** with its minimum at the origin: the pure squares overwhelm the cross term (p. 9). To see it, complete the square (pp. 11–12):

<Formula label="Completing the square" tex="2x^2 + 12xy + 20y^2 = 2(x + 3y)^2 + 2y^2" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–12" />

A sum of squares with positive coefficients cannot be negative. With $c = 18$ the leftover would be $0y^2$; with $c = 7$ it would be $-11y^2$ (p. 12). Cutting the bowl at height 1, $2x^2 + 12xy + 20y^2 = 1$, gives an **ellipse**; cutting a saddle gives a **hyperbola** (p. 12).

## Completing the square is elimination

The numbers 2, 3, 2 are no accident (p. 13). Eliminate on $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & 20 \end{pmatrix}$: the multiplier is $3$, and $20 - 3 \cdot 6 = 2$ (p. 13):

<Formula label="Elimination" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & 20 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = LU" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

The **pivots** stand outside the squares and the **multipliers** inside (pp. 13–14). So positive pivots give a sum of squares with positive weights, the graph goes up, and there is a minimum at the origin: "it's all connected together" (p. 14). Because elimination works for $n \times n$ matrices, so does this argument (p. 14).

## Minima: the matrix of second derivatives

In calculus a minimum needs $df/dx = 0$, and then the second derivative decides: positive means the slope increases through the point (p. 10). For a function of several variables, "positive" becomes "positive definite" for the matrix of second derivatives (pp. 10, 14):

<Formula label="Matrix of second derivatives" tex="\begin{pmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{yx} & f_{yy} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad f_{xy} = f_{yx}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 14–15" />

$f_{xx}$ and $f_{yy}$ must be positive, and also big enough to overcome the cross derivative (p. 14). The matrix is symmetric because mixed second derivatives can be taken in either order (p. 14). The 18.02 condition $f_{xx}f_{yy} > f_{xy}^2$ is just the $2 \times 2$ determinant test; linear algebra now gives the answer for any number of variables (p. 15).

## A 3 × 3 example and an ellipsoid

Strang's favourite matrix (pp. 15–17):

<Formula label="The second-difference matrix" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}: \quad \text{determinants } 2, 3, 4; \quad \text{pivots } 2, \tfrac32, \tfrac43; \quad \lambda = 2 - \sqrt2,\ 2,\ 2 + \sqrt2" cite="reading1" at="pp. 15–17" />

The determinants grow linearly, and the pivots are their ratios (p. 16). Strang recalls the eigenvalues from memory and checks them: they add to the trace $6$, and multiply to $2(4 - 2) = 4$, the determinant (pp. 16–17). Its quadratic form (p. 17):

<Formula label="Its quadratic form" tex="x^TAx = 2x_1^2 + 2x_2^2 + 2x_3^2 - 2x_1x_2 - 2x_2x_3" cite="reading1" at="p. 17" />

No $x_1x_3$ term appears, because those entries are zero. The graph, in four dimensions, is a bowl (a paraboloid), and completing squares would write the form as three squares weighted by the three positive pivots (pp. 17–18). Cutting at height 1, $x^TAx = 1$, gives an **ellipsoid**: a lopsided football (pp. 18–19). With all eigenvalues equal it would be a sphere (the identity); with two equal, a rugby ball; here, with three different eigenvalues, it has a major, a middle and a minor axis (pp. 18–19). The **principal axis theorem** $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$ says the axes point along the eigenvectors, and the eigenvalues set their lengths (p. 19). (Strang says "the lengths, or the half-lengths, or one over the eigenvalues"; for $x^TAx = 1$ the half-length along an eigenvector is $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$.)

<WorkedExample title="Tests of your own">
<Problem>
(a) For which $c$ is $A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 4 & c \end{pmatrix}$ positive definite? For $c = 6$, complete the square in $x^TAx$ and find the eigenvalues. (b) Is $B = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ positive definite?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The condition">
$a = 3 > 0$, and $3c - 16 > 0$ requires $c > 16/3$. At $c = 16/3$ the matrix is only semidefinite.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Completing the square, c = 6">
The multiplier is $4/3$: $3x^2 + 8xy + 6y^2 = 3\left(x + \tfrac43y\right)^2 + \left(6 - \tfrac{16}{3}\right)y^2 = 3\left(x + \tfrac43y\right)^2 + \tfrac23y^2$. Pivots $3$ and $\frac23$, product $2 = 18 - 16$, the determinant. Eigenvalues: trace $9$, determinant $2$, so $\lambda = (9 \pm \sqrt{73})/2 \approx 8.77$ and $0.228$, both positive.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Determinants and pivots">
Upper-left determinants: $4$; $12 - 4 = 8$; $\det B = 4(6 - 1) - 2(4 - 0) = 12$. All positive, so $B$ is positive definite. The pivots are $4$, $8/4 = 2$ and $12/8 = \frac32$, and $x^TBx = 4x_1^2 + 3x_2^2 + 2x_3^2 + 4x_1x_2 + 2x_2x_3 > 0$ for every $x \neq 0$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Symmetric $A$ is positive definite when $x^TAx > 0$ for all $x \neq 0$; equivalently all eigenvalues, all upper-left determinants or all pivots are positive.
- Completing the square is elimination: $x^TAx$ = pivots times squares of (multiplier combinations).
- Positive definite: bowl, minimum, ellipses. Indefinite: saddle, hyperbolas. Semidefinite: the borderline, a zero eigenvalue.
- A minimum of $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$: first derivatives zero and the matrix of second derivatives positive definite.
- $x^TAx = 1$ is an ellipsoid with axes along the eigenvectors of $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$.
