The last lecture ended with vector spaces whose "vectors" are matrices. This one carries on, then looks at rank-one matrices, and ends with a first look at graphs (transcript, pp. 1, 10, 18). It continues [the four fundamental subspaces](/courses/18-06/four-fundamental-subspaces).

## Matrix spaces

The space $M$ of all $3 \times 3$ matrices is a vector space: matrices can be added and multiplied by numbers. They can also be multiplied together, but that is not part of the vector space picture (transcript, p. 1). The symmetric matrices form a subspace $S$ (a sum of symmetric matrices is symmetric, though a product need not be), and so do the upper triangular matrices $U$ (pp. 1–2).

A basis of $M$: the nine matrices with a single 1 and zeros elsewhere. So $\text{dim}\, M = 9$; $M$ is practically $\mathrm{R}^9$ with the numbers written in a square (pp. 2–3). Only three of those nine are symmetric, so a basis of the big space need not contain one of a subspace (pp. 3–4). For $U$ it happens that six of them are a basis (p. 4). Counting free entries (pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Dimensions of matrix spaces" tex="\text{dim}\, M = 9, \qquad \text{dim}\, S = 6, \qquad \text{dim}\, U = 6" cite="reading1" at="pp. 2–4" />

A symmetric upper triangular matrix has zeros on both sides of the diagonal: the intersection $S \text{ intersect } U$ is the diagonal matrices $D$, of dimension 3 (pp. 4–5). The **union** of $S$ and $U$ is not a subspace, like two lines in the plane (p. 5). Instead take the **sum** $S + U$: all matrices $s + u$ with $s$ in $S$ and $u$ in $U$. Here it is all of $M$ (p. 6). The dimensions fit a nice formula (p. 7):

<Formula label="Dimensions of intersection and sum" tex="\text{dim}\, S + \text{dim}\, U = \text{dim}(S \text{ intersect } U) + \text{dim}(S + U): \qquad 6 + 6 = 3 + 9" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

(On p. 5 Strang briefly calls $S$ three-dimensional and corrects it to six.)

## Functions as vectors: a differential equation

One more space without column vectors: the solutions of (transcript, p. 7)

<Formula label="The differential equation" tex="\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + y = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

$\cos x$ and $\sin x$ are solutions, and so is $e^{ix}$, but it adds nothing new (pp. 7–8). The solutions form a vector space, a **nullspace** of the differential operator, with every solution a combination (p. 8):

<Formula label="The solution space" tex="y = c_1\cos x + c_2\sin x" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

$\cos x$ and $\sin x$ are a basis, like the special solutions of $Ax = 0$, and the dimension is 2, as for every linear homogeneous second-order equation; $e^{ix}$ and $e^{-ix}$, with complex coefficients allowed, are another basis (pp. 8–9). Strang jokes that this is five minutes of 18.06 taking care of [18.03](/courses/18-03/second-order-constant-coefficients) (p. 9). Functions are vectors here because they can be added and multiplied by constants (p. 9).

## Rank-one matrices

Rank one matrices are simple: every row is a multiple of one row (transcript, p. 10). With first row $(1, 4, 5)$ and second row $(2, 8, 10)$, the row space has basis $(1, 4, 5)$, the column space has dimension 1 too, and the matrix is its pivot column times that row (pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="A rank-one matrix" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 & 5 \\ 2 & 8 & 10 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11" />

**Every rank-one matrix is a column times a row**, $A = uv^T$ (p. 11). They are the building blocks: a $5 \times 17$ matrix of rank 4 is a combination of four rank-one matrices (pp. 11–12).

Do the matrices of rank 4 (or less) in the space of $5 \times 17$ matrices form a subspace? No: a sum of two can have rank 5 (pp. 12–13). In general (p. 13)

<Formula label="Rank of a sum" tex="\operatorname{rank}(A + B) \le \operatorname{rank}(A) + \operatorname{rank}(B)" cite="reading1" at="p. 13" />

and the rank-one matrices are not a subspace either: a sum of two usually has rank 2 (p. 13).

## A subspace of R⁴ and its four subspaces

Let $S$ be the vectors in $\mathrm{R}^4$ whose components add to zero (transcript, p. 13):

<Formula label="The subspace S" tex="S = \{\, v \text{ in } \mathrm{R}^4 : v_1 + v_2 + v_3 + v_4 = 0 \,\} = N(A), \qquad A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 13–15" />

It is a subspace: multiples and sums still have components adding to zero (p. 14). It is the nullspace of the $1 \times 4$ matrix of ones, which has rank 1, so $\text{dim}\, S = n - r = 4 - 1 = 3$ (pp. 14–15). (Strang calls the vector $b$ for a moment on p. 15; it is $v$.) The free variables are $v_2, v_3, v_4$, and the special solutions are a basis (p. 16):

<Formula label="Basis of S" tex="\begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \begin{pmatrix} -1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 16" />

The other three subspaces of $A$ (pp. 15–18): the row space is the line of multiples of $(1, 1, 1, 1)$, dimension 1; the column space is all of $\mathrm{R}^1$, dimension 1; and the left nullspace $N(A^T)$ contains only zero, since no nonzero combination of the single row is zero. Its dimension is 0 and its basis is the **empty set**. The counts check: $3 + 1 = 4 = n$ and $1 + 0 = 1 = m$ (pp. 17–18).

## Graphs and the small world

A **graph** here is not a curve: it is a set of **nodes** and **edges** connecting some of them, for example five nodes and six edges, which a matrix will describe next time (transcript, p. 18). (Strang calls it five by six; with one row per edge, as in Lecture 12, it is $6 \times 5$.) Take every person in the room as a node, with an edge between friends; or the whole country, some 260 million nodes in Strang's 1999 figure (pp. 18–19). How many steps separate the two people furthest apart? Strang counts his own distance to the president of the day as two, so his students' is at most three (pp. 19–21). "Six degrees of separation" is the popular answer, and a few shortcut edges bring distances down dramatically: a small world (p. 20). The web, with sites as nodes and links as edges, is another such graph (p. 21). The linear algebra of graphs is the next lecture (p. 20).

<WorkedExample title="Matrix spaces and rank one of your own">
<Problem>
(a) In the space of $2 \times 2$ matrices, let $T$ be the matrices with trace (sum of the diagonal entries) 0 and $S$ the symmetric matrices. Find the dimensions of $T$, $S$, $T \text{ intersect } S$ and $T + S$. (b) Write $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 & 3 \\ -4 & 2 & -6 \\ 6 & -3 & 9 \end{pmatrix}$ as $uv^T$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) T and S">
$T$: matrices $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & -a \end{pmatrix}$, three free numbers, so $\text{dim}\, T = 3$. $S$: $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & d \end{pmatrix}$, also 3.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Intersection and sum">
$T \text{ intersect } S$: $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & -a \end{pmatrix}$, dimension 2. Then $\text{dim}(T + S) = 3 + 3 - 2 = 4$, all $2 \times 2$ matrices.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Column times row">
Rows 2 and 3 are $-2$ and $3$ times row 1, so $u = (1, -2, 3)$ and $v = (2, -1, 3)$. The rank is 1.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Matrices and functions can be vectors: basis and dimension work the same way. $\text{dim}\, M = 9$ for $3 \times 3$ matrices, 6 for symmetric and for upper triangular ones, 3 for diagonal ones.
- $\text{dim}\, S + \text{dim}\, U = \text{dim}(S \text{ intersect } U) + \text{dim}(S + U)$.
- The solutions of $y'' + y = 0$ have basis $\cos x$, $\sin x$: dimension 2.
- Rank one means $A = uv^T$; rank-one matrices build all matrices but do not form a subspace.
- The zero subspace has dimension 0. Graphs of nodes and edges come next.
