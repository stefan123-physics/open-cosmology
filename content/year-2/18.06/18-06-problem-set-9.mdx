## What you need from the lessons

**Symmetric matrices** (Lecture 25). A real symmetric matrix, $A = A^T$, has real eigenvalues, and its eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal. Put them in the columns of $Q$; then $Q^{-1} = Q^T$ and

<Formula label="Spectral theorem" tex="A = Q\Lambda Q^{-1} = Q\Lambda Q^T" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–4" />

Multiplied out as columns times rows, this writes $A$ as a combination of projection matrices $q_iq_i^T$ onto perpendicular lines:

<Formula label="Spectral sum" tex="A = \lambda_1q_1q_1^T + \lambda_2q_2q_2^T + \cdots + \lambda_nq_nq_n^T" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–12" />

For a symmetric matrix the pivots and the eigenvalues have the same signs; shifting to $A - sI$ moves every eigenvalue down by $s$, so the pivots of $A - sI$ count the eigenvalues above $s$:

<Formula label="Pivots and eigenvalues" tex="\text{number of positive pivots} = \text{number of positive eigenvalues} \qquad (A = A^T)" cite="reading1" at="p. 14" />

The product of the pivots (without row exchanges) and the product of the eigenvalues are both $\det A$ (p. 14).

**Positive definite matrices** (Lectures 25 and 27). A symmetric matrix is positive definite when all its eigenvalues are positive, equivalently all its pivots, equivalently all its upper-left determinants (Lecture 25 transcript, pp. 15–18). For a $2 \times 2$ matrix the four tests read

<Formula label="Tests for positive definiteness" tex="\text{(1) } \lambda_1 > 0,\ \lambda_2 > 0 \qquad \text{(2) } a > 0,\ ac - b^2 > 0 \qquad \text{(3) pivots } a > 0,\ \frac{ac - b^2}{a} > 0 \qquad \text{(4) } x^TAx > 0 \text{ for all } x \neq 0" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2" />

for $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{pmatrix}$, whose quadratic form is $x^TAx = ax^2 + 2bxy + cy^2$ (p. 4 of the Lecture 27 transcript). Completing the square is elimination: the pivots stand outside the squares and the multipliers inside (pp. 11–14). Lecture 27's example:

<Formula label="Completing the square" tex="2x^2 + 12xy + 20y^2 = 2(x + 3y)^2 + 2y^2" cite="reading2" at="pp. 11–12" />

Cutting the graph of a positive definite form at height 1 gives an ellipse; cutting a saddle gives a hyperbola (p. 12). In $n$ dimensions $x^TAx = 1$ is an ellipsoid whose axes point along the eigenvectors, their lengths set by the eigenvalues (p. 19). (Putting $x = tq$ with a unit eigenvector $q$ gives $\lambda t^2 = 1$, so the half-length along $q$ is $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$.)

**Minima** (Lecture 27). At a point where the first derivatives of $f(x, y)$ are zero, $f$ has a minimum when the symmetric matrix of second derivatives is positive definite:

<Formula label="Matrix of second derivatives" tex="\begin{pmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{yx} & f_{yy} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad f_{xy} = f_{yx}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 14–15" />

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A spectral factorisation" points={14}>

Take the symmetric matrix

<Formula label="The matrix of Problem 1" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & -1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own problem)" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the two eigenvalues of $A$.

<Hint>

The eigenvalues solve $\lambda^2 - (\text{trace})\lambda + \det A = 0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Here the determinant is negative, so one root is positive and one is negative.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-big" label="The larger eigenvalue is" value="7" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-big" />

<Answer id="p1-a-small" label="The smaller eigenvalue is" value="-3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-small" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a $2 \times 2$ matrix, the trace and the determinant give the characteristic equation.

<Step title="The characteristic equation">

The trace is $5 + (-1) = 4$ and the determinant is $-5 - 16 = -21$:

<Formula label="Eigenvalues of A" tex="\lambda^2 - 4\lambda - 21 = (\lambda - 7)(\lambda + 3) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

So the eigenvalues are $7$ and $-3$, real, as they must be for a symmetric matrix.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$7 + (-3) = 4$ is the trace and $7 \cdot (-3) = -21$ is the determinant.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Let $q_1$ be the unit eigenvector for the larger eigenvalue, with positive components, and $P_1 = q_1q_1^T$. Give the entry $p_{12}$ of $P_1$. Then show that $A = \lambda_1P_1 + \lambda_2P_2$, with $P_2 = q_2q_2^T$ for a unit eigenvector $q_2$ of the other eigenvalue, and that $P_1 + P_2 = I$ (this last step has no box).

<Hint>

Solve $(A - \lambda I)x = 0$ for each eigenvalue: one row of $A - \lambda I$ is enough for a $2 \times 2$ matrix. Then divide by the length.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each entry of $q_1q_1^T$ is a product of two components of $q_1$: the entry in row 1, column 2 is the first component times the second. The two unit eigenvectors are perpendicular, so they are the columns of an orthogonal matrix $Q$, and $QQ^T = I$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$p_{12} =$" value="0.4" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Unit eigenvectors give the projections; the spectral sum puts them back together.

<Step title="The eigenvectors">

For $\lambda_1 = 7$: $A - 7I = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 4 \\ 4 & -8 \end{pmatrix}$, whose first row gives $x_1 = 2x_2$, so the eigenvector is along $(2, 1)$. For $\lambda_2 = -3$: $A + 3I = \begin{pmatrix} 8 & 4 \\ 4 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $(1, -2)$. They are perpendicular, $2 - 2 = 0$. Both have length $\sqrt5$, so $q_1 = (2, 1)/\sqrt5$ and $q_2 = (1, -2)/\sqrt5$.

</Step>

<Step title="The projections">

<Formula label="The two projection matrices" tex="P_1 = q_1q_1^T = \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad P_2 = q_2q_2^T = \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

So $p_{12} = 2/5 = 0.4$. Their sum is $\frac15\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix} = I$.

</Step>

<Step title="The spectral sum">

<Formula label="A as a sum of projections" tex="7P_1 - 3P_2 = \frac15\begin{pmatrix} 28 - 3 & 14 + 6 \\ 14 + 6 & 7 - 12 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ 4 & -1 \end{pmatrix} = A" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

This is the spectral sum of the lesson with $n = 2$ (Lecture 25 transcript, pp. 11–12).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$P_1^2 = P_1$: the $(1, 2)$ entry of $P_1^2$ is $\frac{1}{25}(4 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 1) = \frac{10}{25} = \frac25$, as it should be for a projection.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Use the spectral sum to find the entry in the first row, second column of $A^3$.

<Hint>

$P_1P_2 = 0$ and $P_i^2 = P_i$. What does that make $(\lambda_1P_1 + \lambda_2P_2)^3$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="The $(1, 2)$ entry of $A^3$ is" value="148" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Powers of $A$ only raise the eigenvalues to that power: $A^3 = Q\Lambda^3Q^T = \lambda_1^3P_1 + \lambda_2^3P_2$.

<Step title="The power">

The cross products vanish because $q_1^Tq_2 = 0$, and $P_i^2 = P_i$. So

<Formula label="The cube of A" tex="A^3 = 7^3P_1 + (-3)^3P_2, \qquad (A^3)_{12} = 343 \cdot \tfrac25 + (-27)\cdot\left(-\tfrac25\right) = 137.2 + 10.8 = 148" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Directly: $A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 41 & 16 \\ 16 & 17 \end{pmatrix}$, and the first row of $A^2$ times the second column of $A$ is $41 \cdot 4 + 16 \cdot (-1) = 148$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Find the second pivot of $A$ (no row exchanges), and say what kind of matrix $A$ is.

<Hint>

The product of the pivots is the determinant.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d-pivot" label="The second pivot is" value="-4.2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-d-pivot" />

<Answer id="p1-d-kind" label="$A$ is"
  choices={["positive definite", "negative definite", "indefinite", "positive semidefinite"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Elimination gives the pivots; their signs are the signs of the eigenvalues.

<Step title="Elimination">

Subtract $4/5$ of row 1 from row 2: the second pivot is $-1 - \frac45 \cdot 4 = -1 - \frac{16}{5} = -\frac{21}{5} = -4.2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The kind of matrix">

One pivot is positive and one negative, like the eigenvalues $7$ and $-3$: $x^TAx$ takes both signs, so $A$ is indefinite. Along $q_1$ the form is $7$ times a square, along $q_2$ it is $-3$ times a square: a saddle.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$5 \cdot (-4.2) = -21 = \det A = 7 \cdot (-3)$. At $x = (0, 1)$, $x^TAx = -1 < 0$, and at $x = (1, 0)$ it is $5 > 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors" points={12}>

Lecture 25 proves that the eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix are real and leaves the perpendicular eigenvectors to the book. Here both are done by hand for $2 \times 2$ matrices, and then for any size.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

For the symmetric matrix $\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{pmatrix}$, the eigenvalues are $\lambda = \tfrac12\left(a + c \pm \sqrt{\Delta}\right)$ with $\Delta = (\text{trace})^2 - 4\det$. Write $\Delta$ as a formula in $a$, $b$ and $c$.

<Hint>

The trace is $a + c$ and the determinant $ac - b^2$. Multiply out and collect.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$\Delta =$" expr="(a - c)^2 + 4*b^2"
  vars={{ a: [-3, 3], b: [0.5, 3], c: [-3, 3] }} names={{ a: "a", b: "b", c: "c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(a - c)^2 - 4*b^2", hint: "The determinant is ac − b², so −4 det adds +4b², it does not subtract it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The trace and the determinant give $\Delta$; then the cross term $2ac$ combines with $-4ac$.

<Step title="Multiplying out">

<Formula label="The discriminant" tex="\Delta = (a + c)^2 - 4(ac - b^2) = a^2 + 2ac + c^2 - 4ac + 4b^2 = (a - c)^2 + 4b^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $a = c$ and $b = 0$ (a multiple of $I$) it gives $0$: one repeated eigenvalue, as it should.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Use part (a) to show that the eigenvalues of a real symmetric $2 \times 2$ matrix are always real, and that they are equal only when the matrix is a multiple of the identity.

This part has no answer box. Write your argument, then open the solution and mark yourself.

<Hint>

A sum of squares of real numbers is never negative. When is it zero?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The number under the root is a sum of squares.

<Step title="Real roots">

$(a - c)^2 \geq 0$ and $4b^2 \geq 0$, so $\Delta \geq 0$ and $\sqrt{\Delta}$ is real: both eigenvalues are real.

</Step>

<Step title="Equal roots">

The eigenvalues are equal exactly when $\Delta = 0$, which needs both squares to vanish: $a = c$ and $b = 0$. Then the matrix is $aI$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A matrix that is not symmetric can fail: for $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ the trace is $0$ and the determinant $1$, so $\Delta = -4 < 0$, and the eigenvalues are not real.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Let $A$ be a real symmetric $n \times n$ matrix with $Ax = \lambda_1x$ and $Ay = \lambda_2y$, where $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$. Show that $y^Tx = 0$.

This part has no answer box. Write your argument, then open the solution and mark yourself.

<Hint>

Compute the number $y^TAx$ in two ways: once using $Ax = \lambda_1x$, once by moving $A$ onto $y$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** In $y^TAx$ the matrix can act on either vector, because $A^T = A$.

<Step title="Two ways">

On one side $y^TAx = y^T(\lambda_1x) = \lambda_1y^Tx$. On the other, $y^TA = y^TA^T = (Ay)^T = \lambda_2y^T$, so $y^TAx = \lambda_2y^Tx$:

<Formula label="Comparing the two" tex="\lambda_1\,y^Tx = \lambda_2\,y^Tx \quad\Rightarrow\quad (\lambda_1 - \lambda_2)\,y^Tx = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

Since $\lambda_1 \neq \lambda_2$, $y^Tx = 0$. This is the same device as Lecture 25's proof that the eigenvalues are real, which compares $\bar x^TAx$ computed two ways (transcript, pp. 6–7).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

In Problem 1 the eigenvectors $(2, 1)$ and $(1, -2)$ for $7$ and $-3$ have dot product $2 - 2 = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

The matrix $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ is not symmetric. Its eigenvalues are real (check it). Find an eigenvector for each, and give the absolute value of the cosine of the angle between them.

<Hint>

$\cos\theta = \dfrac{u^Tv}{\|u\|\,\|v\|}$. The sign depends on which way each eigenvector points, so the box asks for the absolute value.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="$|\cos\theta| =$" value="0.6" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Without symmetry the argument of part (c) breaks down, and the eigenvectors need not be perpendicular.

<Step title="Eigenvalues and eigenvectors">

The trace is $2$ and the determinant $1 - 4 = -3$: $\lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 3 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda + 1)$, real eigenvalues $3$ and $-1$. $B - 3I = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 4 \\ 1 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $u = (2, 1)$; $B + I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ gives $v = (2, -1)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The angle">

<Formula label="Angle between the eigenvectors" tex="\cos\theta = \frac{u^Tv}{\|u\|\,\|v\|} = \frac{4 - 1}{\sqrt5\,\sqrt5} = \frac35 = 0.6" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(d) (our own solution)" />

The angle is about $53°$, not $90°$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$Bu = (1 + 4, 2 + 1) = (6, 3) = 3u$ and $Bv = (2 - 4, 2 - 1) = (-2, 1) = -v$. Where the proof of (c) fails: $y^TB \neq (By)^T$ when $B^T \neq B$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Counting eigenvalues by pivots" points={16}>

Take the symmetric matrix

<Formula label="The matrix of Problem 3" tex="S = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 & -2 \\ 4 & 5 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own problem)" />

Do not compute its eigenvalues until the last part: the point is to learn about them from pivots alone.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the pivots of $S$ by elimination without row exchanges.

<Hint>

The first pivot is $2$. Clear the first column with multipliers $2$ and $-1$, then clear the second column of what is left.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a-2" label="The second pivot is" value="-3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-a-2" />

<Answer id="p3-a-3" label="The third pivot is" value="9.3333" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-a-3" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Plain elimination, one column at a time.

<Step title="First column">

Row 2 minus $2 \times$ row 1 gives $(0, -3, 5)$; row 3 plus row 1 gives $(0, 5, 1)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Second column">

The second pivot is $-3$. Row 3 minus $\left(-\frac53\right) \times$ row 2 leaves the third pivot

<Formula label="The third pivot" tex="1 + \tfrac53 \cdot 5 = 1 + \tfrac{25}{3} = \tfrac{28}{3} \approx 9.33" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

So the pivots are $2$, $-3$ and $28/3$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

How many eigenvalues of $S$ are positive? What is the product of all three eigenvalues?

<Hint>

$S$ is symmetric: the signs of its pivots are the signs of its eigenvalues.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b-count" label="Number of positive eigenvalues:" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-b-count" />

<Answer id="p3-b-prod" label="Product of the eigenvalues:" value="-56" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-b-prod" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a symmetric matrix the count of positive pivots is the count of positive eigenvalues, and both products equal the determinant.

<Step title="The count">

Two pivots ($2$ and $28/3$) are positive and one ($-3$) is negative, so two eigenvalues are positive and one is negative (Lecture 25 transcript, p. 14).

</Step>

<Step title="The product">

The product of the eigenvalues is $\det S$, which is the product of the pivots:

<Formula label="Determinant of S" tex="\det S = 2 \cdot (-3) \cdot \tfrac{28}{3} = -56" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

By cofactors along the first row: $2(15 - 1) - 4(12 + 2) + (-2)(4 + 10) = 28 - 56 - 28 = -56$. A negative determinant fits an odd number of negative eigenvalues.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

How many eigenvalues of $S$ are greater than $3$? Answer with the pivots of $S - 3I$.

<Hint>

The eigenvalues of $S - 3I$ are those of $S$ minus $3$. So positive eigenvalues of $S - 3I$ are eigenvalues of $S$ above $3$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="Number of eigenvalues of $S$ greater than $3$:" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Shift, then count positive pivots, as Lecture 25 suggests with $A - 7I$ (transcript, p. 14).

<Step title="Elimination on S − 3I">

$S - 3I = \begin{pmatrix} -1 & 4 & -2 \\ 4 & 2 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$. The first pivot is $-1$. Row 2 plus $4 \times$ row 1 gives $(0, 18, -7)$; row 3 minus $2 \times$ row 1 gives $(0, -7, 4)$. The second pivot is $18$, and the third is $4 - \frac{49}{18} = \frac{23}{18}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The count">

Pivots $-1$, $18$, $\frac{23}{18}$: two positive. So $S - 3I$ has two positive eigenvalues, and $S$ has two eigenvalues greater than $3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The product of the pivots, $-1 \cdot 18 \cdot \frac{23}{18} = -23$, must be $\det(S - 3I)$. Cofactors along the first row: $-1(0 - 1) - 4(0 + 2) + (-2)(4 + 4) = 1 - 8 - 16 = -23$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Elimination on $S + 2I$ gives the pivots $4$, $3$ and $1$. What does this say about $S + 2I$? Then compute the eigenvalues of $S$ to check parts (b) to (d) (this check has no box).

<Hint>

All pivots of a symmetric matrix positive: which test is that?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the check, use the characteristic polynomial $\lambda^3 - (\operatorname{trace} S)\lambda^2 + E\lambda - \det S$, where $E$ is the sum of the three $2 \times 2$ determinants on the diagonal of $S$. One of its roots is a whole number.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-d" label="$S + 2I$ is"
  choices={["indefinite", "positive definite", "positive semidefinite but singular", "negative definite"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Positive pivots of a symmetric matrix mean positive definite; for $S + 2I$ it says every eigenvalue of $S$ is above $-2$.

<Step title="The test">

$S + 2I$ is symmetric with all three pivots positive, so it is positive definite (Lecture 25 transcript, pp. 15–16). Its eigenvalues, those of $S$ plus $2$, are all positive: every eigenvalue of $S$ is greater than $-2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The eigenvalues of S">

The trace is $10$, the three $2 \times 2$ determinants on the diagonal add to $E = (10 - 16) + (6 - 4) + (15 - 1) = 10$, and $\det S = -56$:

<Formula label="Characteristic polynomial of S" tex="\lambda^3 - 10\lambda^2 + 10\lambda + 56 = (\lambda - 4)(\lambda^2 - 6\lambda - 14) = 0, \qquad \lambda = 4,\ 3 \pm \sqrt{23}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(d) (our own solution)" />

Numerically $\lambda \approx -1.80$, $4$ and $7.80$. The root $4$ has the eigenvector $(-1, 1, 3)$: $S(-1, 1, 3) = (-4, 4, 12)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Two eigenvalues are positive (part b); their product is $4 \cdot (9 - 23) = -56$ (part b); two of them, $4$ and $7.80$, are above $3$ (part c); all three are above $-2$ (part d).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Positive definite, up to a point" points={16}>

For a number $b > 0$ take the symmetric matrix

<Formula label="The matrix of Problem 4" tex="T = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & b & 0 \\ b & 3 & b \\ 0 & b & 3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own problem)" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the three upper-left determinants of $T$. Give the third one, $\det T$, as a formula in $b$.

<Hint>

The first two are $3$ and a $2 \times 2$ determinant. For the third, expand along the first row: the zero saves one term.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$\det T =$" expr="27 - 6*b^2"
  vars={{ b: [0.2, 3] }} names={{ b: "b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "27 - 3*b^2", hint: "That is only the first cofactor term, 3(9 − b²). The entry b in the first row adds a second term, −b(3b − 0)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>
