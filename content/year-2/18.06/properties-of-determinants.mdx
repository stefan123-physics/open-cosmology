This is the start of the second half of the course: square matrices, determinants and then eigenvalues, for which the determinant is needed (transcript, p. 1). It continues the elimination of [A = LU](/courses/18-06/factorization-a-lu) and the [permutation matrices](/courses/18-06/transposes-permutations-vector-spaces).

## Three properties that define the determinant

Every square matrix has a number, $\det A$ or $|A|$, that packs in as much information as one number can; above all, **$A$ is invertible exactly when $\det A \ne 0$** (transcript, p. 1). Rather than a big formula, start with three properties (pp. 1–4):

<Formula label="Property 1" tex="\det I = 1" cite="reading1" at="pp. 1–2" />

<Formula label="Property 2: exchanging rows" tex="\text{exchange two rows} \;\Rightarrow\; \det \text{ changes sign}; \qquad \det P = \pm 1" cite="reading1" at="pp. 2–3" />

A permutation matrix comes from $I$ by row exchanges, so its determinant is $+1$ or $-1$ for an even or odd number of exchanges (pp. 2–3). For $2 \times 2$ matrices the familiar formula $\det = ad - bc$ will check every property (p. 3). Property 3 is the key (pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Property 3: linear in each row" tex="\begin{vmatrix} ta & tb \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = t\begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix}, \qquad \begin{vmatrix} a + a' & b + b' \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} a' & b' \\ c & d \end{vmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–4" />

The determinant is a **linear function of one row when all the other rows stay the same** (p. 4). Strang stresses that this is **not** $\det(A + B) = \det A + \det B$, where all rows change at once (pp. 4–5). Linearity holds for every row, not only the first: exchange the row to the top, use property 3, and exchange back (p. 5).

## Consequences

**4. Two equal rows give $\det = 0$** (pp. 5–6). Exchanging the two equal rows leaves the matrix the same, yet property 2 says the sign changes; a number equal to its own negative is 0. This matches the fact that such a matrix has rank below $n$ (p. 6).

**5. Elimination does not change the determinant** (pp. 7–8). Subtract $\ell$ times row $i$ from row $k$. By property 3 the determinant splits into the original one plus $-\ell$ times a determinant with two equal rows, which is 0 by property 4 (p. 8):

<Formula label="Property 5 for 2 × 2" tex="\begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c - \ell a & d - \ell b \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} - \ell\begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ a & b \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

So $\det A = \det U$ for the upper triangular $U$ of elimination (p. 7).

**6. A row of zeros gives $\det = 0$** (pp. 8–10): take $t = 0$ in property 3. (Strang first multiplies the zero row by 5, then agrees with a student that $t = 0$ is simpler; he refers to it as rule 3B, but it is the factor rule 3A, pp. 9–10.)

**7. Triangular matrices** (pp. 10–12). For $U$ with diagonal $d_1, \dots, d_n$ and anything above:

<Formula label="Property 7" tex="\det U = d_1 d_2 \cdots d_n" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11" />

If no $d$ is zero, elimination upwards removes everything off the diagonal (property 5); factoring each $d_i$ out of its row (property 3) leaves $I$, whose determinant is 1 (property 1) (pp. 11–12). This is how MATLAB or any reasonable software computes a determinant: eliminate, then **multiply the pivots**, with a minus sign for an odd number of row exchanges (p. 11).

**8. $\det A = 0$ exactly when $A$ is singular** (pp. 12–13). A singular matrix reaches a row of zeros (property 6); an invertible one has a full set of nonzero pivots, whose product is not zero (p. 13). The $2 \times 2$ case from the pivots (p. 14):

<Formula label="Two by two from the pivots" tex="\begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = \begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & d - \frac{c}{a}b \end{vmatrix} = a\left(d - \frac{c}{a}b\right) = ad - bc" cite="reading1" at="p. 14" />

valid if $a \ne 0$; otherwise exchange rows first, and if that fails the matrix is singular (p. 14).

## Products, inverses and transposes

**9. The product rule** (pp. 15–18), which comes somewhat out of the blue:

<Formula label="Property 9" tex="\det AB = (\det A)(\det B)" cite="reading1" at="p. 15" />

From $A^{-1}A = I$ (pp. 15–16):

<Formula label="Consequences of property 9" tex="\det A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}, \qquad \det A^2 = (\det A)^2" cite="reading1" at="pp. 16–17" />

For the diagonal matrix with entries 2 and 3, the inverse has entries $\frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{1}{3}$: determinants 6 and $\frac{1}{6}$ (p. 16). Doubling a matrix is a different matter: by property 3, a factor 2 comes out of every row (pp. 17–18):

<Formula label="Doubling the matrix (property 3)" tex="\det 2A = 2^n\det A" cite="reading1" at="pp. 17–18" />

so $2^n$, not 2: like doubling the sides of a box, which multiplies its volume by 8 in three dimensions (p. 18). The formula for $A^{-1}$ fails exactly when $\det A = 0$, where $A^{-1}$ does not exist (p. 18).

**10. The transpose** (pp. 18–20):

<Formula label="Property 10" tex="\det A^T = \det A" cite="reading1" at="p. 18" />

So everything said about rows holds for columns: a zero column gives 0, exchanging two columns reverses the sign (pp. 18–19). Proof for the usual case $A = LU$: $\det A = \det L\,\det U$ and $\det A^T = \det U^T\det L^T$ by property 9. $L$ and $L^T$ are triangular with ones on the diagonal, so their determinants are 1, and $U$ and $U^T$ have the same diagonal (pp. 19–20).

**A hidden fact** (pp. 20–21). Property 2 would contradict itself if the same permutation could be reached by seven exchanges and also by ten. It cannot: every permutation is either **odd** or **even**, so the determinant is well defined by properties 1 to 3 (pp. 20–21).

<WorkedExample title="Determinants by the properties">
<Problem>
For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 7 \\ -2 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ find $\det A$ by elimination, then $\det 2A$, $\det A^{-1}$, $\det A^T$, and the determinant after exchanging rows 1 and 2.
</Problem>
<Step title="Elimination">
Row 2 minus 2 row 1: $(0, 3, 1)$. Row 3 plus row 1: $(0, 3, 4)$. Then row 3 minus row 2: $(0, 0, 3)$. No exchanges, pivots 2, 3, 3: $\det A = 18$. (Check by the $3 \times 3$ formula: $2(5 - 14) - (4 + 14) + 3(8 + 10) = 18$.)
</Step>
<Step title="The other determinants">
$\det 2A = 2^3 \cdot 18 = 144$; $\det A^{-1} = \frac{1}{18}$; $\det A^T = 18$; one exchange gives $-18$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Defined by: $\det I = 1$; a row exchange reverses the sign; linear in each row separately.
- Then: equal rows or a zero row give 0; elimination leaves the determinant unchanged; triangular means product of the diagonal; $\det A = \pm$ product of the pivots, and $\det A = 0$ exactly for singular $A$.
- $\det AB = \det A\,\det B$, $\det A^{-1} = 1/\det A$, $\det 2A = 2^n\det A$, $\det A^T = \det A$.
