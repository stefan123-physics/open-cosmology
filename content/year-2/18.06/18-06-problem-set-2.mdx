## What you need from the lessons

**Inverses** (Lecture 3). A square matrix $A$ is invertible when some matrix $A^{-1}$ gives

<Formula label="The inverse" tex="A^{-1}A = I = AA^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 12" />

If $Ax = 0$ for some nonzero $x$, there is no inverse (pp. 14–16). **Gauss–Jordan** finds $A^{-1}$ by eliminating on the augmented matrix $[A\;\; I]$: downwards as usual, then upwards, then dividing each row by its pivot, until the left half is $I$ (pp. 19–22). All the steps together are one matrix $E$, and $EA = I$ says $E = A^{-1}$:

<Formula label="Why Gauss–Jordan works" tex="E\,[A\;\; I] = [EA\;\; E] = [I\;\; A^{-1}]" cite="reading1" at="pp. 23–24" />

**Products, transposes and A = LU** (Lecture 4). Inverses and transposes of a product come in reverse order, and inverting and transposing can be done in either order:

<Formula label="Inverse of a product" tex="(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}, \qquad (A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–3" />

Elimination without row exchanges is $E_{32}E_{31}E_{21}A = U$, where $E_{21}$ subtracts the multiplier $\ell_{21}$ times row 1 from row 2, $E_{31}$ subtracts $\ell_{31}$ times row 1 from row 3, and $E_{32}$ subtracts $\ell_{32}$ times row 2 from row 3. Taking the inverses across, in reverse order, gives

<Formula label="A = LU" tex="A = E_{21}^{-1}E_{31}^{-1}E_{32}^{-1}\,U = LU" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

$L$ is lower triangular with ones on its diagonal and the multipliers below it, untouched; the forward product $E = E_{32}E_{31}E_{21}$ mixes them (pp. 9–12). $U$ has the pivots on its diagonal; dividing each row of $U$ by its pivot gives $A = LDU$, with the pivots in the diagonal matrix $D$ (pp. 6–7). Elimination on a full $n \times n$ matrix costs about

<Formula label="Cost of elimination" tex="n^2 + (n - 1)^2 + \cdots + 2^2 + 1^2 \approx \tfrac13n^3" cite="reading2" at="pp. 17–18" />

operations (a multiply and a subtract each), and each right-hand side afterwards about $n^2$ (p. 19).

**Permutations and transposes** (Lecture 5). A permutation matrix $P$ is the identity with its rows reordered; there are $n!$ of size $n \times n$ (p. 4). With row exchanges, elimination is $PA = LU$, and every permutation has $P^TP = I$:

<Formula label="Row exchanges and permutations" tex="PA = LU, \qquad P^{-1} = P^T" cite="reading3" at="pp. 2–4" />

The transpose flips a matrix across its diagonal, $(A^T)_{ij} = A_{ji}$; $A$ is **symmetric** when $A^T = A$, and $R^TR$ is symmetric for every rectangular $R$ (pp. 5–9).

**Vector spaces** (Lecture 5). A vector space is closed under addition and under multiplication by every real number, so every linear combination stays inside; every vector space and every subspace contains the zero vector (pp. 10–16). The subspaces of $\mathrm{R}^3$ are $\mathrm{R}^3$, the planes and the lines through the origin, and the zero vector alone (p. 17). The **column space** $C(A)$ is the set of all combinations of the columns of $A$ (pp. 18–20).

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Inverses by Gauss–Jordan" points={18}>

Take

<Formula label="A matrix to invert" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own problem)" />

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Find $A^{-1}$ by Gauss–Jordan elimination on $[A\;\; I]$. Give its $(1, 1)$, $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 3)$ entries.

<Hint>

Clear the first column below the first pivot, then the second column below the second pivot. Apply every row operation to all six columns of $[A\;\; I]$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

When the left half is upper triangular, go upwards: use row 3 to clear the entry above the third pivot, then row 2 to clear the entry above the second pivot. Here every pivot is $1$, so no division is needed at the end.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-11" label="The $(1, 1)$ entry of $A^{-1}$ is" value="4" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-11" />

<Answer id="p1-a-12" label="The $(1, 2)$ entry of $A^{-1}$ is" value="-2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-12" />

<Answer id="p1-a-23" label="The $(2, 3)$ entry of $A^{-1}$ is" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-a-23" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Elimination that turns $A$ into $I$ turns $I$ into $A^{-1}$, because all the steps together are $E = A^{-1}$.

<Step title="Downwards">

Subtract $2$ times row 1 from row 2 and $1$ times row 1 from row 3; then subtract row 2 from row 3:

<Formula label="Elimination downwards" tex="\left(\begin{array}{cccccc} 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 2 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right) \to \left(\begin{array}{cccccc} 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 2 & -1 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right) \to \left(\begin{array}{cccccc} 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & -1 & 1 \end{array}\right)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 19–21 (the method; the numbers are our own)" />

The pivots are $1$, $1$ and $1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Upwards">

Subtract row 3 from row 2, then row 2 from row 1:

<Formula label="Elimination upwards" tex="\to \left(\begin{array}{cccccc} 1 & 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -3 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & -1 & 1 \end{array}\right) \to \left(\begin{array}{cccccc} 1 & 0 & 0 & 4 & -2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & -3 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 & -1 & 1 \end{array}\right)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 21–22 (the method; the numbers are our own)" />

So

<Formula label="The inverse" tex="A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 & 1 \\ -3 & 2 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

The $(1, 1)$ entry is $4$, the $(1, 2)$ entry $-2$ and the $(2, 3)$ entry $-1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Multiply back. Row 1 of $A$ times the columns of $A^{-1}$: $4 - 3 = 1$, $-2 + 2 = 0$, $1 - 1 = 0$. Row 2: $8 - 9 + 1 = 0$, $-4 + 6 - 1 = 1$, $2 - 3 + 1 = 0$. Row 3: $4 - 6 + 2 = 0$, $-2 + 4 - 2 = 0$, $1 - 2 + 2 = 1$. So $AA^{-1} = I$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Use $A^{-1}$ to solve $Ax = b$ for $b = (1, 0, 2)$. Give $x_2$, the second component of $x$.

<Hint>

Multiply both sides of $Ax = b$ by $A^{-1}$ on the left. Only row 2 of $A^{-1}$ is needed for $x_2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$x_2 =$" value="-5" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $x = A^{-1}b$: each component of $x$ is a row of $A^{-1}$ times $b$.

<Step title="Multiply">

<Formula label="The solution by the inverse" tex="x = A^{-1}b = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 & 1 \\ -3 & 2 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 + 2 \\ -3 - 2 \\ 1 + 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 \\ -5 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$Ax = (6 - 5,\; 12 - 15 + 3,\; 6 - 10 + 6) = (1, 0, 2) = b$. For a single right-hand side, elimination on $[A\;\; b]$ is cheaper than finding $A^{-1}$ first; the inverse pays off only when it is already known.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

For a number $t$, take

<Formula label="A matrix with a parameter" tex="B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & t \\ 2 & 5 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own problem)" />

Run Gauss–Jordan on $[B\;\; I]$. For which $t$ does it break down, so that $B$ has no inverse? For the other values of $t$, give the $(1, 1)$ entry of $B^{-1}$ as a formula in $t$.

<Hint>

After the first step the second pivot is a formula in $t$. Gauss–Jordan breaks down when it is zero.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Divide row 2 by the second pivot, then subtract $t$ times row 2 from row 1. Put the two terms of the $(1, 1)$ entry over one denominator.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c-t" label="$B$ has no inverse for $t =$" value="2.5" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-c-t" />

<Answer id="p1-c-11" label="The $(1, 1)$ entry of $B^{-1}$ is" expr="5/(5 - 2*t)"
  vars={{ t: [0.5, 1.5] }} names={{ t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/(5 - 2*t)", hint: "That is the (2, 2) entry of B⁻¹. After row 2 is divided by its pivot, t times row 2 must still be taken off row 1." },
    { expr: "5/(5 + 2*t)", hint: "Row 2 minus 2 times row 1 leaves 5 − 2t in the pivot position, not 5 + 2t." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The second pivot decides everything: if it is zero there is no inverse, and otherwise Gauss–Jordan runs to the end.

<Step title="The second pivot">

Subtract $2$ times row 1 from row 2:

<Formula label="First step" tex="\left(\begin{array}{cccc} 1 & t & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 5 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right) \to \left(\begin{array}{cccc} 1 & t & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 - 2t & -2 & 1 \end{array}\right)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 21–22 (the method; the matrix is our own)" />

The second pivot $5 - 2t$ is zero for $t = 2.5$. Then the columns $(1, 2)$ and $(2.5, 5)$ lie on one line, and $B(2.5, -1) = (0, 0)$: a nonzero solution of $Bx = 0$, so $B$ has no inverse (Lecture 3 transcript, pp. 14–16).

</Step>

<Step title="Upwards">

For $t \neq 2.5$, divide row 2 by $5 - 2t$, then subtract $t$ times it from row 1:

<Formula label="The inverse with a parameter" tex="B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + \dfrac{2t}{5 - 2t} & \dfrac{-t}{5 - 2t} \\ \dfrac{-2}{5 - 2t} & \dfrac{1}{5 - 2t} \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{5 - 2t}\begin{pmatrix} 5 & -t \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

The $(1, 1)$ entry is $5/(5 - 2t)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $t = 0$ the matrix is lower triangular and the formula gives the $(1, 1)$ entry $5/5 = 1$, as it must. For $t = 2$: $B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & -2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and row 1 of $B$ times its columns gives $5 - 4 = 1$ and $-2 + 2 = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The inverse of a product" points={14}>

Take

<Formula label="Two matrices" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own problem)" />

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find $A^{-1}$ and $B^{-1}$, and from them $(AB)^{-1}$. Give its $(2, 1)$ and $(2, 2)$ entries.

<Hint>

$B$ is an elimination matrix: its inverse undoes its one row operation. $A^{-1}$ comes from Gauss–Jordan on a $2 \times 2$ matrix.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The order of the factors matters: undo the factor that acted last first.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a-21" label="The $(2, 1)$ entry of $(AB)^{-1}$ is" value="-4" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-a-21" />

<Answer id="p2-a-22" label="The $(2, 2)$ entry of $(AB)^{-1}$ is" value="5" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-a-22" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$, in reverse order.

<Step title="The two inverses">

$B$ adds $3$ times row 1 to row 2, so $B^{-1}$ subtracts it: $B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$. For $A$, Gauss–Jordan subtracts $\frac12$ of row 1 from row 2, which leaves the second pivot $\frac12$, and ends with

<Formula label="The inverse of A" tex="A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

Check: $AA^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 1 & -2 + 2 \\ 1 - 1 & -1 + 2 \end{pmatrix} = I$.

</Step>

<Step title="Reverse order">

<Formula label="The inverse of AB" tex="(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -4 & 5 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2 (the rule; the numbers are our own)" />

The $(2, 1)$ entry is $-4$ and the $(2, 2)$ entry $5$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$AB = \begin{pmatrix} 2 + 3 & 1 \\ 1 + 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $AB$ times our answer is $\begin{pmatrix} 5 - 4 & -5 + 5 \\ 4 - 4 & -4 + 5 \end{pmatrix} = I$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Now find $(BA)^{-1}$. Give its $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 1)$ entries.

<Hint>

Apply the same rule to $BA$: whose inverse comes first?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b-11" label="The $(1, 1)$ entry of $(BA)^{-1}$ is" value="4" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-b-11" />

<Answer id="p2-b-21" label="The $(2, 1)$ entry of $(BA)^{-1}$ is" value="-7" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-b-21" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $(BA)^{-1} = A^{-1}B^{-1}$: the matrix that a wrong order would give for $(AB)^{-1}$.

<Step title="Multiply">

<Formula label="The inverse of BA" tex="(BA)^{-1} = A^{-1}B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & -1 \\ -7 & 2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 1–2 (the rule; the numbers are our own)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$BA = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 6 + 1 & 3 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 7 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, and $BA$ times our answer is $\begin{pmatrix} 8 - 7 & -2 + 2 \\ 28 - 28 & -7 + 8 \end{pmatrix} = I$. $AB$ and $BA$ are different matrices, so their inverses are different too.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Show that if $A$, $B$ and $C$ are invertible $n \times n$ matrices, then $(ABC)^{-1} = C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}$. Show also that the inverse of an invertible symmetric matrix is symmetric.

This part has no answer box. Write your argument, then open the solution and mark yourself.

<Hint>

For the first claim, multiply $ABC$ by the proposed inverse and move the parentheses.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the second, use $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1}$ together with $A^T = A$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The inverse is checked by multiplying; the transpose rule reverses order just as the inverse rule does.

<Step title="Three factors">

<Formula label="Inverse of three factors" tex="(ABC)(C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}) = AB(CC^{-1})B^{-1}A^{-1} = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

The innermost pair cancels first, then the next. The product in the other order gives $I$ in the same way, so $C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}$ is the inverse.

</Step>

<Step title="Symmetric inverse">

Let $A^T = A$. Then $(A^{-1})^T = (A^T)^{-1} = A^{-1}$ (Lecture 4 transcript, p. 3): $A^{-1}$ equals its own transpose.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The $A$ of this problem is symmetric, and its inverse $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$ from part (a) is symmetric too. $B$ is not symmetric, and neither is $B^{-1}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A = LU for a 3 × 3 matrix" points={20}>

Take

<Formula label="A matrix to factor" tex="A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 4 & 5 & 3 \\ -2 & 5 & 5 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own problem)" />

No row exchanges are needed.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Eliminate to find $U$ and the multipliers $\ell_{21}$, $\ell_{31}$ and $\ell_{32}$, as in "What you need". Give $\ell_{31}$, $\ell_{32}$ and the third pivot.

<Hint>

A multiplier is the entry to be cleared divided by the pivot above it. A negative multiplier means a row is added, not subtracted.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Compute $\ell_{32}$ from the new row 2 and the new row 3, after the first column has been cleared, not from the original rows.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a-l31" label="$\ell_{31} =$" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-a-l31" />

<Answer id="p3-a-l32" label="$\ell_{32} =$" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-a-l32" />

<Answer id="p3-a-piv" label="The third pivot is" value="4" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-a-piv" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Clear column 1 with the first pivot, then column 2 with the second; the multipliers go straight into $L$.

<Step title="Column 1">

$\ell_{21} = 4/2 = 2$: row 2 minus $2$ times row 1 is $(0, 3, 1)$. $\ell_{31} = -2/2 = -1$: row 3 minus $(-1)$ times row 1, that is row 3 plus row 1, is $(0, 6, 6)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Column 2">

$\ell_{32} = 6/3 = 2$: $(0, 6, 6) - 2(0, 3, 1) = (0, 0, 4)$. So

<Formula label="The factors" tex="L = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 11–12 (the multipliers go into L; the numbers are our own)" />

The pivots are $2$, $3$ and $4$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Multiply back, a row at a time: row 2 of $LU$ is $2(2, 1, 1) + (0, 3, 1) = (4, 5, 3)$, and row 3 is $-(2, 1, 1) + 2(0, 3, 1) + (0, 0, 4) = (-2, 5, 5)$. Both are rows of $A$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

The forward product $E = E_{32}E_{31}E_{21}$ takes $A$ to $U$. Find its $(3, 1)$ entry for this $A$. Then find the $(3, 1)$ entry of $E$ for any multipliers $\ell_{21}$, $\ell_{31}$, $\ell_{32}$, as a formula.

<Hint>

Follow what happens to row 3: first $\ell_{31}$ times row 1 is taken off it, then $\ell_{32}$ times the new row 2, which itself is row 2 minus $\ell_{21}$ times row 1.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The $(3, 1)$ entry of $E$ is the coefficient of the original row 1 in the final row 3.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b-num" label="For this $A$, the $(3, 1)$ entry of $E$ is" value="5" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-b-num" />

<Answer id="p3-b-gen" label="In general, the $(3, 1)$ entry of $E$ is" expr="l21*l32 - l31"
  vars={{ l21: [0.5, 3], l31: [0.5, 3], l32: [0.5, 3] }} names={{ l21: "\\ell_{21}", l31: "\\ell_{31}", l32: "\\ell_{32}" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-l31", hint: "That is the (3, 1) entry of E only when ℓ₂₁ℓ₃₂ = 0: row 1 also reaches row 3 through the new row 2." },
    { expr: "l21*l32 + l31", hint: "E₃₁ subtracts ℓ₃₁ times row 1 from row 3, so ℓ₃₁ enters with a minus sign." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $E$ is $L^{-1}$, and in the forward order the multipliers interfere: row 1 reaches row 3 twice, directly and through row 2.

<Step title="Follow row 3">

After the three steps, row 3 is

<Formula label="The final row 3" tex="r_3 - \ell_{31}r_1 - \ell_{32}(r_2 - \ell_{21}r_1) = (\ell_{21}\ell_{32} - \ell_{31})\,r_1 - \ell_{32}\,r_2 + r_3" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

where $r_1, r_2, r_3$ are the rows of $A$. Its coefficients form row 3 of $E$. The $(3, 1)$ entry is $\ell_{21}\ell_{32} - \ell_{31}$, and for this $A$ it is $2 \cdot 2 - (-1) = 5$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

So $E = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 5 & -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, and $EL = I$: row 3 of $E$ times the columns of $L$ gives $5 - 4 - 1 = 0$, $-2 + 2 = 0$ and $1$. With $\ell_{31} = 0$ the formula gives $\ell_{21}\ell_{32}$: the $10$ that the lesson finds in $E$ for the multipliers $2$ and $5$ (Lecture 4 transcript, pp. 9–10).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Write $A = LDU'$, where $D$ is diagonal and $U'$ is upper triangular with ones on its diagonal. Give the $(2, 3)$ entry of $U'$.

<Hint>

$D$ holds the pivots. Divide each row of $U$ by its pivot.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="The $(2, 3)$ entry of $U'$ is" value="0.3333" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Pulling the pivots out of $U$ leaves ones on its diagonal.

<Step title="Divide by the pivots">
