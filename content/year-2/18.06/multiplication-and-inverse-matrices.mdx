Matrices have been multiplied already; this lecture states the rules, in several ways that all give the same answer and are all important, and then turns to inverses (transcript, p. 1). It continues [elimination with matrices](/courses/18-06/elimination-with-matrices).

## Entry by entry

In $AB = C$, the entry $c_{34}$ in row 3, column 4 comes from **row 3 of $A$** and **column 4 of $B$**: a dot product (transcript, pp. 1–2), which in index notation is (pp. 2–3)

<Formula label="Entry of a product" tex="c_{34} = a_{31}b_{14} + a_{32}b_{24} + \cdots = \sum_{k=1}^{n} a_{3k}\,b_{k4}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 2–3" />

For the product to exist, the number of columns of $A$ must match the number of rows of $B$ (p. 3):

<Formula label="Shapes" tex="(m \times n)(n \times p) = m \times p" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–4" />

## By columns, by rows, by columns times rows

**Columns** (pp. 4–6). Think of $B$ as $p$ columns side by side. $A$ times column $j$ of $B$ is column $j$ of $C$, so **every column of $AB$ is a combination of the columns of $A$**, with the coefficients taken from $B$ (pp. 5–6).

**Rows** (p. 6). Likewise, row $i$ of $A$ times $B$ is row $i$ of $C$: **every row of $AB$ is a combination of the rows of $B$** (p. 6).

**Columns times rows** (pp. 7–9). A row times a column is a number; a column times a row is a full matrix (p. 7). For example (pp. 7–8)

<Formula label="Column times row" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 12 \\ 3 & 18 \\ 4 & 24 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

a very special matrix: its columns are multiples of $(2, 3, 4)$, its rows multiples of $(1, 6)$ (p. 8). Its **row space**, the combinations of its rows, is just the line through $(1, 6)$, and its **column space** the line through $(2, 3, 4)$: a minimal matrix (pp. 9–10). The fourth way to multiply is then (p. 8)

<Formula label="Sum of columns times rows" tex="AB = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (\text{column } k \text{ of } A)(\text{row } k \text{ of } B)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 8–9" />

**Blocks** (pp. 10–11). Cut $A$ and $B$ into blocks that match, for example four $10 \times 10$ blocks each of a $20 \times 20$ matrix. The blocks multiply like entries, block rows times block columns (p. 11):

<Formula label="Block multiplication" tex="\begin{pmatrix} A_1 & A_2 \\ A_3 & A_4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} B_1 & B_2 \\ B_3 & B_4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} A_1B_1 + A_2B_3 & \cdots \\ \cdots & \cdots \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11" />

All these ways do the same multiplications, just grouped differently (p. 11).

## Inverses

For a square matrix, the key question is whether it is invertible (transcript, pp. 11–12). If it is (pp. 11–12),

<Formula label="Inverse" tex="A^{-1}A = I = AA^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 12" />

For square matrices a left inverse is automatically a right inverse, a fact that is not easy to prove; rectangular matrices will be different (p. 12). Invertible matrices are also called **non-singular** (p. 12).

**A singular matrix** (pp. 12–16). Take $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$. Its determinant is 0, but Strang gives other reasons, all of them important (pp. 13–14). The columns of any product $AB$ are combinations of the columns of $A$, which lie on one line, so $(1, 0)$, the first column of $I$, cannot be reached (p. 14). The reason Strang likes best (pp. 14–16):

<Formula label="A nonzero solution of Ax = 0" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 14–15" />

Three of column 1 minus one of column 2 is the zero column. If $A^{-1}$ existed, multiplying $Ax = 0$ by it would give $x = 0$; but $x$ is not 0 (pp. 15–16). **A square matrix with $Ax = 0$ for some nonzero $x$ has no inverse** (p. 16).

## Gauss–Jordan

Take the invertible $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 7 \end{pmatrix}$: its columns point in different directions (transcript, p. 17). Finding $A^{-1}$ is **solving systems**: $A$ times column $j$ of $A^{-1}$ is column $j$ of $I$ (p. 18). Here, two systems with right-hand sides $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ (pp. 18–19). Jordan's idea is to solve them together: augment $A$ by both columns, that is by $I$, and eliminate (pp. 19–20). Subtract 2 times row 1 from row 2; then, going upwards, which Gauss would not do, subtract 3 times row 2 from row 1 (pp. 21–22):

<Formula label="Gauss–Jordan" tex="\left(\begin{array}{cccc} 1 & 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 7 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right) \to \left(\begin{array}{cccc} 1 & 3 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 \end{array}\right) \to \left(\begin{array}{cccc} 1 & 0 & 7 & -3 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & 1 \end{array}\right)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 21–22" />

The left half is now $I$, and the right half is $A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$; check: $7 - 6 = 1$, $21 - 21 = 0$, $-2 + 2 = 0$, $-6 + 7 = 1$ (p. 22).

**Why it works** (pp. 23–24). All the elimination steps together are one matrix $E$, the product of the elimination matrices (dividing a row by its pivot is a multiplication by a diagonal matrix, so it belongs to $E$ too). It turned $A$ into $I$, so $EA = I$, which says $E = A^{-1}$; and on the right it turned $I$ into $EI = A^{-1}$ (p. 24):

<Formula label="Why Gauss–Jordan gives the inverse" tex="E\,[A\;\; I] = [EA\;\; E] = [I\;\; A^{-1}]" cite="reading1" at="pp. 23–24" />

<WorkedExample title="Products and inverses">
<Problem>
(a) For $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, compute $AB$ by entries and as a sum of columns times rows. (b) Invert $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ by Gauss–Jordan. (c) Show that $\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$ is not invertible.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Two ways">
By entries: $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 3 + 2 & 1 + 8 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 9 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$. As a sum: $\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 8 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$, the same.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Gauss–Jordan">
From $\left(\begin{array}{cccc} 2 & 5 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 3 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right)$, subtract $\frac{1}{2}$ of row 1 from row 2: $(0, \frac{1}{2})$ on the left and $(-\frac{1}{2}, 1)$ on the right. Subtract 10 times the new row 2 from row 1: $(2, 0)$ and $(6, -10)$. Divide the rows by the pivots 2 and $\frac{1}{2}$: $A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -5 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. Check: $2\cdot3 + 5\cdot(-1) = 1$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A singular matrix">
$x = (2, -1)$ gives $2\begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} = 0$, so there is no inverse.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $(AB)_{ij}$ = row $i$ of $A$ · column $j$ of $B$; shapes $(m \times n)(n \times p) = m \times p$.
- Columns of $AB$ combine columns of $A$; rows of $AB$ combine rows of $B$; $AB$ is a sum of columns times rows; blocks multiply like entries.
- $A^{-1}A = I = AA^{-1}$; no inverse if $Ax = 0$ for some $x \ne 0$.
- Gauss–Jordan: eliminate $[A\;\,I]$ to $[I\;\,A^{-1}]$.
