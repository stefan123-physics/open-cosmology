8.04 begins with the features that make quantum mechanics so different from classical physics: it is linear, it needs complex numbers, it gives up determinism, it allows superpositions, and it allows entanglement (notes p. 1). (MIT's material for this lecture is Barton Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 1; this page follows their order in its own words and marks what it adds. The notes write states in Dirac's \"ket\" notation, with a vertical bar and an angle bracket; this page labels them with subscripts instead: $\Psi_A$ for the state the notes call A, $\Psi_x$, $\Psi_y$ and $\Psi_\theta$ for photons polarized along $x$, along $y$ and at angle $\theta$, $\chi_{\text{up}}$ and $\chi_{\text{down}}$ for spin up and down along $z$, and $u(1)\,v(2)$ for the product of states of two particles, which the notes write with the tensor product symbol.) Quantum mechanics began in 1925 with Schrödinger and Heisenberg, building on Planck, Einstein, Bohr and de Broglie; applied to electromagnetism, light or gravity it becomes quantum electrodynamics, quantum optics or quantum gravity (notes p. 1).

## Linearity

A physical theory is a set of equations for its dynamical variables, and every solution is something the theory allows to happen; general relativity, for instance, has expanding universes among its solutions (notes p. 1). In a **linear** theory, two solutions can be added to give a third. Maxwell's electromagnetism is linear: if $(\vec E_1, \vec B_1, \rho_1, \vec J_1)$ and $(\vec E_2, \vec B_2, \rho_2, \vec J_2)$ are solutions, so is their sum, the *superposition* (notes p. 2). That is why countless radio, phone and internet waves can share the same air without disturbing each other.

In general, an equation $Lu = 0$ for an unknown $u$ is linear if the operator $L$ obeys

<Formula label="Linear operator" tex="L\left(u_1 + u_2\right) = Lu_1 + Lu_2, \qquad L\left(au\right) = aLu" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.4)" />

for any number $a$; then if $u_1$ and $u_2$ are solutions, so is $\alpha u_1 + \beta u_2$. A simple example is $du/dt + u/\tau = 0$, with $\tau$ a constant time (notes p. 2).

General relativity is nonlinear, and so is Newton's mechanics: for a particle in a potential $V(x)$,

<Formula label="Newton's second law in a potential" tex="m\frac{d^2x(t)}{dt^2} = -V'\left(x(t)\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.8)" />

and since $V'$ is in general not a linear function, the sum of two solutions is not a solution (notes p. 3). (The page's own answer to the notes' exercise: for $V = c_0 + c_1x + c_2x^2$ the equation is linear but, if $c_1 \ne 0$, inhomogeneous, and the sum of two solutions then fails, since the constant force $-c_1$ would be counted twice. Superposition works only for $V = c_0 + c_2x^2$, a spring.)

**Quantum mechanics is linear.** Its central equation, the Schrödinger equation, is linear in the wavefunction $\Psi$:

<Formula label="Schrödinger equation" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \hat H\Psi" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.11)" />

where the Hamiltonian $\hat H$, the energy operator, is a linear operator that does not depend on $\Psi$ (notes p. 4). So if $\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2$ are solutions, so is $\alpha\Psi_1 + \beta\Psi_2$ for any complex numbers $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Schrödinger found the equation at the end of 1925 (it was published in 1926) without knowing what $\Psi$ meant; months later Max Born proposed that it encodes probabilities, an idea Schrödinger disliked for the rest of his life (notes p. 3). In this sense quantum mechanics is simpler than classical mechanics.

## Complex numbers are essential

A complex number $z = a + ib$, with $a$ and $b$ real and $i^2 = -1$, has real part $a$, imaginary part $b$, complex conjugate $z^* = a - ib$, and norm (notes p. 4)

<Formula label="Norm of a complex number" tex="\left|z\right| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}, \qquad \left|z\right|^2 = z^*z" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eqs. (2.4)–(2.5)" />

Pictured as a vector in the complex plane, a number of length 1 at angle $\theta$ is $\cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, and Euler's identity writes it as a *pure phase* (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Euler's identity" tex="e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.6)" />

In classical physics complex numbers are a convenience; every measurable quantity is real. In quantum mechanics they are essential: the Schrödinger equation contains $i$, and the wavefunction itself is complex. Since no measurement gives a complex number, the link to experiment must be indirect: probabilities are computed from $\left|\Psi\right|^2$, as Born proposed. The mathematics needed is that of complex vector spaces, and it is often useful to think of a wavefunction as a vector (notes p. 5).

## Loss of determinism

By the end of the nineteenth century light was understood as an electromagnetic wave. To explain the photoelectric effect, Einstein proposed in 1905 that light's energy comes in packets, later called photons; Compton's experiments of 1923 convinced most sceptics (notes p. 5). A photon of frequency $\nu$ carries

<Formula label="Photon energy" tex="E = h\nu" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (3.1)" />

so all photons of one colour have the same energy, and a brighter beam has more of them (notes p. 6).

**A photon at a polarizer** (notes pp. 6–7). A polarizer passes light polarized along $\hat x$ and absorbs light polarized along $\hat y$. Light polarized at an angle $\theta$ to the $x$-axis has the field $\vec E = E_0\cos\theta\,\hat x + E_0\sin\theta\,\hat y$; the polarizer passes the $\hat x$ part, so a fraction $\cos^2\theta$ of the energy gets through, at the same frequency. Now send the light one photon at a time. A photon cannot pass in part, since part of a photon would have a lower frequency; so each photon either passes or is absorbed, and a fraction $\cos^2\theta$ of them pass. But the photons are identical. Why does one pass and the next not? Quantum mechanics answers that nobody can predict it: only the probability, $\cos^2\theta$, can be known. The polarizer is not to blame (it is not made of patches that treat photons differently, experiments show), and "hidden variables" that would decide each photon's fate have been ruled out in most forms by experiments based on the work of John Bell (notes p. 7).

In quantum language, the photon's state is a superposition of the two polarization states (notes p. 8),

<Formula label="Photon polarization state" tex="\Psi_\theta = \cos\theta\,\Psi_x + \sin\theta\,\Psi_y" cite="mit" at="notes p. 8, eq. (3.6)" />

and a photon that gets through is in the state $\Psi_x$, with no factor $\cos\theta$ left: for a single photon there is no room for one. The notes tell how, at the 1927 Solvay conference, Einstein objected that "God does not play dice" (a phrase he had already used in a 1926 letter to Max Born), while Bohr accepted the loss of determinism (notes p. 8).

## Quantum superpositions

A beam of light sent through a Mach–Zehnder interferometer, split into two paths and recombined, can be arranged to end up entirely in one detector, by interference. Sent one photon at a time it still does so. Two different photons cannot be what interferes, since destructive interference would turn two photons into none, against energy conservation; so each photon interferes with itself, travelling both paths at once, in a superposition of the two (notes pp. 8–9; the next lecture develops this).

**What a superposition means** (notes p. 10). Suppose a measurement of some quantity $Q$ always gives $a$ in the state $\Psi_A$ and always gives $b$ in the state $\Psi_B$. In the state

<Formula label="A superposition" tex="\Psi = \alpha\Psi_A + \beta\Psi_B, \qquad \alpha, \beta \text{ complex}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 10, eq. (4.1)" />

a measurement of $Q$ gives either $a$ or $b$, never a value in between, with probabilities

<Formula label="Probabilities of the two outcomes" tex="P(a) = \frac{\left|\alpha\right|^2}{\left|\alpha\right|^2 + \left|\beta\right|^2}, \qquad P(b) = \frac{\left|\beta\right|^2}{\left|\alpha\right|^2 + \left|\beta\right|^2}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 10, eq. (4.3)" />

After the measurement the state *is* $\Psi_A$ (if $a$ was found) or $\Psi_B$, so repeating the measurement at once gives the same result. Superposing a state with itself only multiplies it by a number, and multiplying by any nonzero number changes nothing physical: $\Psi_A$, $2\Psi_A$, $i\Psi_A$ and $-\Psi_A$ all describe the same state. That is why a photon's polarization, $\alpha\Psi_x + \beta\Psi_y$, has just two real parameters (the complex ratio $\beta/\alpha$), matching the shape and tilt of an elliptically polarized wave (notes pp. 10–11).

**Electron spin** (notes pp. 11–12). An electron's spin along any axis is found to be either up or down, spinning at the same fixed rate either way. The state $\chi_{\text{up}} + \chi_{\text{down}}$ (spin up plus spin down along $z$) gives up or down along $z$ with equal probabilities. A critic might say that a mixture of 50% up electrons and 50% down electrons gives the same results. It does not: measured along $x$, the mixture gives half up and half down, while every electron in the superposition is found up along $x$.

## Entanglement

For two particles, with states $u_i$ for the first and $v_j$ for the second, the simplest joint states are products $u_i(1)\,v_j(2)$; this product (the tensor product, in the notes' language) distributes over sums (notes p. 13). A state is **entangled** if it cannot be written as one such product, (a state of particle 1) times (a state of particle 2). For example, $u_1(1)\,v_1(2) + u_2(1)\,v_2(2)$ is entangled: matching it with $(\alpha_1u_1 + \alpha_2u_2)(\beta_1v_1 + \beta_2v_2)$ would need $\alpha_1\beta_1 = 1$, $\alpha_1\beta_2 = 0$, $\alpha_2\beta_1 = 0$ and $\alpha_2\beta_2 = 1$, which is impossible (notes p. 13).

For two electrons (notes p. 14):

<Formula label="An entangled pair" tex="\chi_{\text{up}}(1)\,\chi_{\text{down}}(2) + \chi_{\text{down}}(1)\,\chi_{\text{up}}(2)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 14, eq. (5.7)" />

Their spins along $z$ are always opposite. If Alice, on Earth, finds hers up, Bob's electron on the Moon is at once down, before any light signal could tell him. A conventional mixture of pairs could mimic this along one axis, but in 1964 John Bell showed that measurements along three directions distinguish the two, and experiments confirm the quantum correlations. They lead to no paradox: entanglement cannot be used to send information faster than light (notes p. 14).

<Problem>
(a) Photons polarized along $x$ meet a polarizer at $45°$ and then one at $90°$ (along $y$). What fraction gets through both? What if the $45°$ polarizer is removed? (b) Find the norm of $z = 3 + 4i$ and write it as $|z|e^{i\theta}$. (c) In the state $(1 + i)\Psi_A + 2\Psi_B$, what are the probabilities of $a$ and $b$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Two polarizers">
Each photon passes the first polarizer with probability $\cos^2 45° = 0.5$, and then emerges polarized at $45°$; it passes the second with probability $\cos^2 45° = 0.5$ again. So 0.25 of the photons get through. Without the middle polarizer, light polarized along $x$ meets a polarizer along $y$, and none gets through: adding a polarizer lets more light pass.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A complex number">
$|z| = \sqrt{9 + 16} = 5$, and $\theta = \arctan(4/3) \approx 0.927$ rad (about $53.1°$), so $z = 5e^{0.927i}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Probabilities">
$|1 + i|^2 = 2$ and $|2|^2 = 4$, so $P(a) = 2/6 = 1/3$ and $P(b) = 4/6 = 2/3$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Quantum mechanics is linear: the Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\partial\Psi/\partial t = \hat H\Psi$ allows superpositions of solutions.
- The wavefunction is complex; probabilities come from $|\Psi|^2$.
- Identical photons at a polarizer behave differently: only probabilities can be predicted.
- In a superposition $\alpha\Psi_A + \beta\Psi_B$ a measurement gives $a$ or $b$ with probabilities proportional to $|\alpha|^2$ and $|\beta|^2$; entangled particles share a state that cannot be split, with correlations no classical mixture can reproduce.
