So far the wavefunction has been a function of position. This lecture shows that its Fourier transform is an equally good description, a wavefunction in momentum space, and then defines the expected (average) values of observables and finds how they change in time (notes pp. 1–6). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 8; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example. As in the previous lecture, the Fourier integrals carry a factor $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$.)

## A delta function from Fourier's theorem

Time plays no role in this section, so it is left out (notes p. 1). Fourier's theorem pairs a wavefunction with its transform:

<Formula label="Fourier's theorem" tex="\Psi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(k)\,e^{ikx}dk, \qquad \Phi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi(x)\,e^{-ikx}dx" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.1)" />

$\Phi(k)$ carries all the information in $\Psi(x)$, and it is the weight with which plane waves of momentum $\hbar k$ are added up (notes p. 1). Substituting the second formula into the first (with $x'$ as the integration variable inside) and swapping the order of integration gives $\Psi(x) = \int dx'\,\Psi(x')\left[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int dk\,e^{ik(x - x')}\right]$. The bracket picks out the value of $\Psi$ at $x' = x$, which is exactly what the **delta function** does: $\int dx'\,f(x')\,\delta(x - x') = f(x)$ (notes p. 1, eqs. (1.2)–(1.3)). So:

<Formula label="Integral representation of the delta function" tex="\delta(x - x') = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\,e^{ik(x - x')}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.4)" />

Letting $k \to -k$ shows that $\delta$ is even, $\delta(x) = \delta(-x)$, and another useful property is $\delta(ax) = \delta(x)/|a|$ (notes p. 2, eqs. (1.5)–(1.6)).

## Parseval's theorem and momentum space

How does normalization look in terms of $\Phi$? Write both $\Psi^*$ and $\Psi$ as Fourier integrals, do the $x$ integral first, and recognize the delta function $\delta(k - k')$; the $k'$ integral then collapses (notes p. 2, eqs. (1.7)–(1.8)), leaving

<Formula label="Parseval's theorem" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\left|\Psi(x)\right|^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dk\left|\Phi(k)\right|^2" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.9)" />

This is **Parseval's theorem** (more generally, Plancherel's theorem). It hints that $|\Phi(k)|^2$, too, can be read as a probability (notes p. 2).

**In terms of momentum** (notes pp. 2–3). Changing variables to $p = \hbar k$, and rescaling $\Phi$ by a factor $1/\sqrt\hbar$ so that the formulas stay symmetric, gives a new function $\tilde\Phi(p)$ (eqs. (1.10)–(1.11)):

<Formula label="Fourier's theorem with momentum" tex="\Psi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\tilde\Phi(p)\,e^{ipx/\hbar}dp, \qquad \tilde\Phi(p) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi(x)\,e^{-ipx/\hbar}dx" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.11)" />

<Formula label="Parseval's theorem with momentum" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\left|\Psi(x)\right|^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp\left|\tilde\Phi(p)\right|^2" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.12)" />

Now $\tilde\Phi(p)$ is the weight of the momentum eigenstate $e^{ipx/\hbar}$, which has eigenvalue $p$ under $\hat p$. Just as $\Psi(x)$ is the wavefunction in position space, $\tilde\Phi(p)$ is the **wavefunction in momentum space**, and since a normalized $\Psi$ gives $\int dp\,|\tilde\Phi|^2 = 1$, we postulate (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Momentum probability" tex="\left|\tilde\Phi(p)\right|^2dp = \text{probability of a momentum between } p \text{ and } p + dp" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.13)" />

**In three dimensions** (notes p. 3) the factors become $(2\pi\hbar)^{-3/2}$ and the integrals run over $d^3p$ and $d^3x$ (eq. (1.14)). The same substitution gives the three-dimensional delta function, $\delta^3(\vec x - \vec x') = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}\int d^3k\,e^{i\vec k\cdot(\vec x - \vec x')}$, and Parseval's theorem $\int d^3x\,|\Psi(\vec x)|^2 = \int d^3p\,|\tilde\Phi(\vec p)|^2$, with $|\tilde\Phi(\vec p)|^2d^3p$ the probability of a momentum in $d^3p$ around $\vec p$ (eqs. (1.16)–(1.17)).

## Expectation values

For a random quantity $Q$ that takes the values $Q_1, \dots, Q_n$ with probabilities $p_1, \dots, p_n$, the **expectation value** is the average expected over many observations (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Expectation value of a random variable" tex="\langle Q\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{n}Q_i\,p_i" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.18)" />

Since $\Psi^*\Psi\,dx$ is the probability of finding the particle in $[x, x + dx]$ at time $t$, the expected position is (notes p. 4)

<Formula label="Expected position" tex="\langle\hat x\rangle \equiv \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\,\Psi^*(x, t)\,\Psi(x, t)\,dx" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.20)" />

It depends on time. Physically: prepare many identical copies of the system, measure the position in each at time $t$, and the average of the results approaches $\langle\hat x\rangle$ as the number of copies grows (notes p. 4).

**The expected momentum** is defined in the same way from the momentum probabilities, $\langle\hat p\rangle \equiv \int p\,\tilde\Phi^*(p, t)\,\tilde\Phi(p, t)\,dp$ (eq. (2.22)). To translate it into position space, write both $\tilde\Phi$'s as Fourier integrals of $\Psi$; the factor $p$ multiplying $e^{ipx/\hbar}$ can be produced by $\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$ acting on that exponential; and the remaining $p$ integral is again a delta function (notes pp. 4–5, eqs. (2.23)–(2.25)). Doing the last integral leaves (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Expected momentum in position space" tex="\langle\hat p\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\Psi^*(x, t)\,\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Psi(x, t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\Psi^*\,\hat p\,\Psi" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eqs. (2.26)–(2.27)" />

The operator acts on $\Psi$, with $\Psi^*$ to its left. This motivates the definition for **any operator** $\hat Q$ (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Expectation value of an operator" tex="\langle\hat Q\rangle \equiv \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\Psi^*(x, t)\,\hat Q\,\Psi(x, t)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.28)" />

**Kinetic energy** (notes p. 5). For $\hat T = \hat p^2/(2m) = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}$, the definition gives $\langle\hat T\rangle = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int dx\,\Psi^*\,\partial^2\Psi/\partial x^2$ (eq. (2.30)). Kinetic energy, the square of the momentum, should never be negative; integrating one derivative by parts (dropping boundary terms, which vanish) makes this manifest:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy, manifestly positive" tex="\langle\hat T\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\left|\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right|^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dp\,\frac{p^2}{2m}\left|\tilde\Phi(p, t)\right|^2" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eqs. (2.31)–(2.32)" />

the second form being the momentum-space calculation (notes p. 5). Expectation values of $\hat{\vec p} = \frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla$, of the potential $V(\hat x)$, and of the angular momentum $\hat{\vec L} = \hat{\vec r} \times \hat{\vec p}$, whose components are, for example, $\hat L_z = \hat x\hat p_y - \hat y\hat p_x = \frac{\hbar}{i}\left(x\frac{\partial}{\partial y} - y\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right)$, can be computed the same way (notes p. 5, eq. (2.33)).

## How expectation values change in time

Expectation values change because the wavefunction does. For an operator $\hat Q$ with no explicit time dependence, differentiate $\langle\hat Q\rangle = \int d^3x\,\Psi^*\hat Q\Psi$ under the integral and use the Schrödinger equation for $\partial\Psi/\partial t$ and its conjugate for $\partial\Psi^*/\partial t$ (notes p. 6, eq. (3.34)). The term with $(\hat H\Psi)^*$ can be rewritten using the Hermiticity of $\hat H$, which moves $\hat H$ onto the other function (eq. (3.35)); the two terms then combine into a commutator (notes p. 6):

<Formula label="Time dependence of an expectation value" tex="i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat Q\rangle = \left\langle\left[\hat Q, \hat H\right]\right\rangle" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eqs. (3.36)–(3.37)" />

So an observable that commutes with the Hamiltonian has a constant expectation value. Commutators obey a set of rules (a homework exercise in the course): they are antisymmetric, $[A, B] = -[B, A]$, so $[A, A] = 0$; linear, $[A, B + C] = [A, B] + [A, C]$; they act on products like derivatives,

<Formula label="Commutator of a product" tex="\left[A, BC\right] = \left[A, B\right]C + B\left[A, C\right], \qquad \left[AB, C\right] = A\left[B, C\right] + \left[A, C\right]B" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eqs. (3.41)–(3.42)" />

and they satisfy the Jacobi identity $[A, [B, C]] + [B, [C, A]] + [C, [A, B]] = 0$ (notes p. 6, eqs. (3.38)–(3.43)).

<WorkedExample title="A Gaussian, measured">
<Problem>
Take the normalized Gaussian $\Psi(x) = (2\pi a^2)^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/(4a^2)}$ at one instant. (a) Find $\langle\hat x\rangle$, $\langle\hat x^2\rangle$ and $\langle\hat p\rangle$. (b) Find $\langle\hat T\rangle$, and its value in eV for an electron with $a = 0.10$ nm ($\hbar c = 197.33$ eV nm, $m_ec^2 = 511{,}000$ eV). (c) What changes if $\Psi$ is multiplied by $e^{ik_0x}$? (d) Use the time-dependence formula to find $d\langle\hat x\rangle/dt$ for a free particle.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Position and momentum">
$|\Psi|^2$ is even, so $\langle\hat x\rangle = 0$; it is a normal distribution of standard deviation $a$, so $\langle\hat x^2\rangle = a^2$. $\Psi$ is real, so $\int \Psi\,\Psi'\,dx = \frac12\left[\Psi^2\right]_{-\infty}^{\infty} = 0$ and $\langle\hat p\rangle = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Kinetic energy">
$\partial\Psi/\partial x = -\frac{x}{2a^2}\Psi$, so $\int |\partial\Psi/\partial x|^2dx = \frac{\langle\hat x^2\rangle}{4a^4} = \frac{1}{4a^2}$ and $\langle\hat T\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{8ma^2}$. For the electron, $\frac{(\hbar c)^2}{8m_ec^2a^2} = \frac{197.33^2}{8 \times 511{,}000 \times 0.01} \approx 0.95$ eV.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A moving packet">
$|\Psi|^2$ is unchanged, so $\langle\hat x\rangle$ and $\langle\hat x^2\rangle$ are unchanged. The derivative gains a term $ik_0\Psi$, so $\langle\hat p\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{i}\,ik_0\int|\Psi|^2dx = \hbar k_0$: the packet now moves.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) The velocity of the average position">
With $\hat H = \hat p^2/(2m)$ and the product rule, $[\hat x, \hat p^2] = [\hat x, \hat p]\hat p + \hat p[\hat x, \hat p] = 2i\hbar\,\hat p$. So $i\hbar\,d\langle\hat x\rangle/dt = \langle 2i\hbar\,\hat p/(2m)\rangle$, which gives $d\langle\hat x\rangle/dt = \langle\hat p\rangle/m$: the average position moves like a classical particle. The result holds in any potential $V(x)$ too, since $\hat x$ commutes with $V(\hat x)$. (This application is the page's own; it is the first of the relations known as Ehrenfest's theorem.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Fourier's theorem gives $\delta(x - x') = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int dk\,e^{ik(x - x')}$, and Parseval's theorem $\int|\Psi(x)|^2dx = \int|\tilde\Phi(p)|^2dp$.
- $\tilde\Phi(p)$ is the wavefunction in momentum space: $|\tilde\Phi(p)|^2dp$ is the probability of a momentum in $[p, p + dp]$.
- The expectation value of an operator is $\langle\hat Q\rangle = \int\Psi^*\hat Q\Psi\,dx$; for momentum this follows from the momentum probabilities, and the kinetic energy's expectation value is manifestly positive.
- $i\hbar\,d\langle\hat Q\rangle/dt = \langle[\hat Q, \hat H]\rangle$: observables that commute with $\hat H$ are conserved on average.
