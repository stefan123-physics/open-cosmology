## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Bound states from imaginary wavenumber" points={5} mit="Problem 1">

Take the setting of the lesson on phase shifts: a particle on the half-line $x > 0$, with a hard wall at $x = 0$ and a potential that vanishes beyond $x = R$. For an energy $E = \hbar^2k^2/(2m) > 0$ the solution beyond the range of the potential is

<Formula label="Scattering solution outside the potential" tex="\psi(x) = e^{i\delta(k)}\,\sin\left(kx + \delta(k)\right), \qquad x > R" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Show that if the potential has a bound state, the scattering amplitude $A_s = e^{i\delta}\sin\delta$ has a pole (it becomes infinite) at $k = i\kappa$ for some $\kappa > 0$.

The choice box, our addition, checks the key step. Then open the solution and mark yourself.

<Hint>

Write the sine as two exponentials, an incoming wave $e^{-ikx}$ and an outgoing wave $e^{ikx}$. Which combination of $\delta$ multiplies the outgoing one, and how is it related to $A_s$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The equation $\psi'' = -k^2\psi$ for $x > R$ does not care whether $k$ is real. Put $k = i\kappa$. What is the energy then, and what do $e^{ikx}$ and $e^{-ikx}$ become? Which of the two can a normalizable state contain?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-term" label="At $k = i\kappa$ with $\kappa > 0$, a bound state needs" choices={["the term e^(−ikx) = e^(κx), which grows, to be absent next to e^(ikx) = e^(−κx): the factor e^(2iδ) in front of e^(ikx) must be infinite", "the term e^(ikx) = e^(κx), which grows, to be absent: the factor e^(2iδ) in front of it must vanish", "nothing special: both terms decay for imaginary k, so every κ gives a bound state", "both terms with equal size, as in a standing wave, so e^(2iδ) must be −1"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For real $k$ the outside solution is an incoming wave plus an outgoing wave, and the potential only decides the phase between them. For imaginary $k$ the same two terms are a growing and a decaying exponential, and the energy is negative. A bound state is a solution with the decaying term alone, so the factor that compares the two terms must be infinite there.

<Step title="Incoming and outgoing waves">

With $\sin y = \frac{1}{2i}\left(e^{iy} - e^{-iy}\right)$ the solution is

<Formula label="The solution as two exponentials" tex="\psi(x) = \frac{1}{2i}\left(-e^{-ikx} + e^{2i\delta(k)}\,e^{ikx}\right), \qquad x > R" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.8)" />

The factor of the outgoing wave is tied to the scattering amplitude. Since $2i\,e^{i\delta}\sin\delta = e^{i\delta}\left(e^{i\delta} - e^{-i\delta}\right) = e^{2i\delta} - 1$,

<Formula label="The outgoing factor and the scattering amplitude" tex="e^{2i\delta} = 1 + 2i\,A_s, \qquad A_s = e^{i\delta}\sin\delta = \frac{e^{2i\delta} - 1}{2i}" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.11), rewritten" />

So $A_s$ is infinite exactly where $e^{2i\delta}$ is.

</Step>

<Step title="Imaginary wavenumber">

For $x > R$ the Schrödinger equation is $\psi'' = -k^2\psi$ with $E = \hbar^2k^2/(2m)$. Both stay true for complex $k$: the two exponentials still solve the equation. Put $k = i\kappa$ with $\kappa > 0$. Then

<Formula label="The outside solution at imaginary wavenumber" tex="E = -\frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}{2m} < 0, \qquad \psi(x) = \frac{1}{2i}\left(-e^{\kappa x} + e^{2i\delta(i\kappa)}\,e^{-\kappa x}\right), \qquad x > R" cite="reading4" at="notes p. 9, eq. (3.15), with eq. (1.8) of the scattering notes continued to k = iκ" />

The incoming wave has become a growing exponential and the outgoing wave a decaying one.

</Step>

<Step title="What a bound state needs">

A bound state is a normalizable solution with $E < 0$. Call its energy $-\hbar^2\kappa_b^2/(2m)$. Beyond $R$ it must be the decaying exponential alone: $\psi_b \propto e^{-\kappa_bx}$.

At a given energy the solution that vanishes at the wall is unique up to an overall constant, because the wall fixes $\psi(0) = 0$ and the equation then fixes the rest once the slope at the wall is chosen. So at $k = i\kappa_b$ the continued scattering solution must be a multiple of $\psi_b$. Divide it by $e^{2i\delta}$ to compare:

<Formula label="The solution divided by the outgoing factor" tex="e^{-2i\delta(k)}\,\psi(x) = \frac{1}{2i}\left(e^{ikx} - e^{-2i\delta(k)}\,e^{-ikx}\right) \quad\to\quad \frac{1}{2i}\left(e^{-\kappa x} - e^{-2i\delta(i\kappa)}\,e^{\kappa x}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is the decaying exponential alone only if $e^{-2i\delta} = 0$ at $k = i\kappa_b$. So $e^{2i\delta(k)}$ is infinite there, and with it

<Formula label="The pole of the scattering amplitude" tex="A_s(k) = \frac{e^{2i\delta(k)} - 1}{2i} \to \infty \qquad \text{at } k = i\kappa_b, \quad \kappa_b = \frac{\sqrt{2m|E_b|}}{\hbar} > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The argument also runs backwards: where $A_s$ has a pole at $k = i\kappa$ with $\kappa > 0$, the solution that is regular at the wall is a pure decaying exponential outside, so it is normalizable and there is a bound state with energy $-\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Why $\kappa > 0$? At a pole with $\kappa < 0$ the surviving term $e^{ikx} = e^{-\kappa x}$ would grow with $x$, and that is no bound state. In the lesson's other form, $A_s = \frac{\tan\delta}{1 - i\tan\delta}$ (notes of Lecture 19, p. 8, eq. (3.11)), the pole sits where $\tan\delta = -i$, the same condition that gives the resonances just below the real axis. Bound states are its solutions on the positive imaginary axis. For real $k$ nothing of the kind can happen: $\delta$ is real there and $|A_s| = |\sin\delta| \le 1$. Problem 4(b) has an example to test this on: its last step finds the pole for a square well.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Simultaneous eigenfunctions" points={5} mit="Problem 2">

Two Hermitian operators $\hat A$ and $\hat B$ commute: $[\hat A, \hat B] = 0$. At least one of them, say $\hat A$, has no degeneracies in its spectrum: each of its eigenvalues belongs to one eigenfunction only, up to a constant factor. Show that every eigenfunction of $\hat A$ is also an eigenfunction of $\hat B$.

The choice box, our addition, asks what happens without the assumption on $\hat A$. Then open the solution and mark yourself.

<Hint>

Take $\hat A\psi = a\psi$ and look at the function $\hat B\psi$. What does $\hat A$ do to it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

You should find that $\hat B\psi$ is again an eigenfunction of $\hat A$ with the eigenvalue $a$ (or that it is zero). Now use that $a$ is not degenerate.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-deg" label="If $\hat A$ does have degeneracies, then" choices={["an eigenfunction of A need not be one of B, but inside each set of eigenfunctions that share an eigenvalue of A one can choose combinations that are", "nothing changes: every eigenfunction of A is still an eigenfunction of B", "A and B can no longer commute", "no function at all is an eigenfunction of both operators"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Because the operators commute, $\hat B$ cannot move an eigenfunction of $\hat A$ out of its eigenvalue. If only one function has that eigenvalue, $\hat B$ can do nothing but multiply it by a number.

<Step title="Acting with both operators">

Let $\psi$ be an eigenfunction of $\hat A$ with eigenvalue $a$. Then

<Formula label="B keeps the eigenvalue of A" tex="\hat A\left(\hat B\psi\right) = \hat B\left(\hat A\psi\right) = \hat B\left(a\psi\right) = a\left(\hat B\psi\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The first step is $\hat A\hat B = \hat B\hat A$. So the function $\hat B\psi$ satisfies the eigenvalue equation of $\hat A$ with the same eigenvalue $a$.

</Step>

<Step title="No degeneracy">

Either $\hat B\psi = 0$, and then $\psi$ is an eigenfunction of $\hat B$ with eigenvalue 0. Or $\hat B\psi$ is an eigenfunction of $\hat A$ with eigenvalue $a$. Since $a$ is not degenerate, every such function is a multiple of $\psi$:

<Formula label="An eigenfunction of A is an eigenfunction of B" tex="\hat B\psi = b\,\psi" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

for some number $b$. That is the claim. Because $\hat B$ is Hermitian, $b$ is real: $b\left(\psi, \psi\right) = \left(\psi, \hat B\psi\right) = \left(\hat B\psi, \psi\right) = b^*\left(\psi, \psi\right)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The assumption matters. $\hat L^2$ and $\hat L_z$ commute, but $\hat L^2$ is degenerate: for $l = 1$ three functions share the eigenvalue $2\hbar^2$. The function $\sin\theta\cos\phi$ (that is $x/r$, a combination of $Y_{1,1}$ and $Y_{1,-1}$) is an eigenfunction of $\hat L^2$, but $\hat L_z = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$ turns it into a multiple of $\sin\theta\sin\phi$, a different function. Inside the set of three one can still choose functions that are eigenfunctions of both: the spherical harmonics $Y_{1m}$. The proof above shows where the freedom comes from: $\hat B\psi$ must stay inside the set of eigenfunctions with eigenvalue $a$, and when that set holds one function only, nothing is left to choose. In the other direction, $\hat L_z$ on functions of $\phi$ alone has no degeneracy (one function $e^{im\phi}$ for each $m$), and indeed every $e^{im\phi}$ is an eigenfunction of $\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}$, which commutes with it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Expectation values in a particular wavefunction" points={10} mit="Problem 3">

A particle has the wavefunction

<Formula label="The wavefunction" tex="\psi(r, \theta, \phi) = \frac14\sqrt{\frac{5}{\pi}}\;\sin^2\theta\,\left(1 + \sqrt{14}\,\cos\theta\right)\cos 2\phi\;f(r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1" />

where $f(r)$ is a normalized radial wavefunction: $\int_0^\infty |f|^2r^2dr = 1$. You will need two spherical harmonics beyond those of the lesson. Built from the lesson's formulas, they are

<Formula label="Two more spherical harmonics" tex="Y_{2,\pm2}(\theta, \phi) = \frac14\sqrt{\frac{15}{2\pi}}\;\sin^2\theta\;e^{\pm2i\phi}, \qquad Y_{3,\pm2}(\theta, \phi) = \frac14\sqrt{\frac{105}{2\pi}}\;\sin^2\theta\,\cos\theta\;e^{\pm2i\phi}" cite="reading1" at="built from notes p. 9, eqs. (3.24) and (3.26), and p. 10, eq. (3.27) for m < 0 (the notes list only l = 0 and l = 1)" />

Throughout, $\Delta L^2$ means the uncertainty of the operator $\hat L^2$ (not the square of an uncertainty).

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Write the wavefunction in terms of spherical harmonics. Which values can a measurement of $L^2$ give, and which values a measurement of $L_z$? With which probabilities?

<Hint>

$\cos 2\phi = \frac12\left(e^{2i\phi} + e^{-2i\phi}\right)$. Which values of $m$ appear? The bracket has a term without $\cos\theta$ and a term with it: compare each with the two harmonics above.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Once $\psi = f(r)\sum c_{lm}Y_{lm}$, the orthonormality of the spherical harmonics makes $|c_{lm}|^2$ the probability of finding that $l$ and $m$. An outcome of $L^2$ collects every $m$ with the same $l$, and an outcome of $L_z$ every $l$ with the same $m$. Check that all the $|c_{lm}|^2$ add up to 1.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-out" label="Possible outcomes" choices={["L²: 6ħ² or 12ħ². Lz: 2ħ or −2ħ", "L²: 2ħ² or 3ħ². Lz: 2ħ or −2ħ", "L²: 6ħ² or 12ħ². Lz: 0, 2ħ or −2ħ", "L²: 4ħ² or 9ħ². Lz: 2ħ only"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p3-p12" label="Probability of the larger value of $L^2$:" value="0.667" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-p12" />

<Answer id="p3-pz" label="Probability of the largest value of $L_z$:" value="0.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-pz" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The spherical harmonics are the joint eigenfunctions of $\hat L^2$ and $\hat L_z$ and they are orthonormal. Once the angular part is written as a sum of them, the squares of the coefficients are the probabilities.

<Step title="The two pieces">

With $\cos2\phi = \frac12\left(e^{2i\phi} + e^{-2i\phi}\right)$, only $m = \pm2$ appear. From the two harmonics given:

<Formula label="The angular pieces as spherical harmonics" tex="\sin^2\theta\,\cos2\phi = 2\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{15}}\left(Y_{2,2} + Y_{2,-2}\right), \qquad \sin^2\theta\,\cos\theta\,\cos2\phi = 2\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{105}}\left(Y_{3,2} + Y_{3,-2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The coefficients">

Multiply by the constants of the wavefunction: $\frac14\sqrt{\frac5\pi}\cdot2\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{15}} = \frac12\sqrt{\frac23} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}$ and $\frac14\sqrt{\frac5\pi}\cdot\sqrt{14}\cdot2\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{105}} = \frac12\sqrt{\frac43} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$. So

<Formula label="The wavefunction in spherical harmonics" tex="\psi = f(r)\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\left(Y_{2,2} + Y_{2,-2}\right) + \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\left(Y_{3,2} + Y_{3,-2}\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Outcomes and probabilities">

The eigenvalue of $\hat L^2$ is $\hbar^2l(l + 1)$ (notes p. 10, eq. (3.28)): $6\hbar^2$ for $l = 2$ and $12\hbar^2$ for $l = 3$. The eigenvalue of $\hat L_z$ is $\hbar m$: $2\hbar$ or $-2\hbar$.

| Outcome | Terms | Probability |
|---|---|---|
| $L^2 = 6\hbar^2$ | $Y_{2,2}$, $Y_{2,-2}$ | $\frac16 + \frac16 = \frac13$ |
| $L^2 = 12\hbar^2$ | $Y_{3,2}$, $Y_{3,-2}$ | $\frac13 + \frac13 = \frac23$ |
| $L_z = 2\hbar$ | $Y_{2,2}$, $Y_{3,2}$ | $\frac16 + \frac13 = \frac12$ |
| $L_z = -2\hbar$ | $Y_{2,-2}$, $Y_{3,-2}$ | $\frac16 + \frac13 = \frac12$ |

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The four squared coefficients add up to $\frac16 + \frac16 + \frac13 + \frac13 = 1$, so the wavefunction is normalized, as it should be with a normalized $f$. The equal chances for $+2\hbar$ and $-2\hbar$ are no accident: the wavefunction is real, and a real wavefunction contains $e^{im\phi}$ and $e^{-im\phi}$ with equal weight.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

What are the expectation values of $L^2$ and of $L_z$?

<Hint>

An expectation value is the sum of each outcome times its probability.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-l2" label="$\langle L^2\rangle$ in units of $\hbar^2$:" value="10" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-l2" />

<Answer id="p3-lz" label="$\langle L_z\rangle$ in units of $\hbar$:" value="0" tolerance="0.01" calc="p3-lz" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Weigh the outcomes of part (a) with their probabilities.

<Step title="The two averages">

<Formula label="Expectation values of L² and Lz" tex="\langle L^2\rangle = \frac13\cdot6\hbar^2 + \frac23\cdot12\hbar^2 = 10\,\hbar^2, \qquad \langle L_z\rangle = \frac12\left(2\hbar\right) + \frac12\left(-2\hbar\right) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\langle L^2\rangle$ lies between its two outcomes, closer to the more likely one. $\langle L_z\rangle = 0$ also follows without any expansion: for a real wavefunction, $\int\psi\,\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\phi}\,d\phi = \frac{\hbar}{2i}\int\frac{\partial\left(\psi^2\right)}{\partial\phi}\,d\phi = 0$, because $\psi^2$ returns to its value after a full turn.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Find the uncertainties $\Delta L^2$ and $\Delta L_z$.

<Hint>

$\left(\Delta Q\right)^2 = \langle Q^2\rangle - \langle Q\rangle^2$. For $Q = L^2$ the outcomes of $Q^2$ are the squares of the outcomes of $L^2$, with the same probabilities.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-dl2" label="$\Delta L^2$ in units of $\hbar^2$:" value="2.83" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-dl2" />

<Answer id="p3-dlz" label="$\Delta L_z$ in units of $\hbar$:" value="2" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-dlz" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same probabilities give the averages of the squares.

<Step title="Uncertainty of L²">

<Formula label="Uncertainty of L²" tex="\left\langle\left(L^2\right)^2\right\rangle = \frac13\left(6\hbar^2\right)^2 + \frac23\left(12\hbar^2\right)^2 = 108\,\hbar^4, \qquad \Delta L^2 = \sqrt{108 - 10^2}\;\hbar^2 = 2\sqrt{2}\,\hbar^2 \approx 2.83\,\hbar^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Uncertainty of Lz">

<Formula label="Uncertainty of Lz" tex="\langle L_z^2\rangle = \frac12\left(2\hbar\right)^2 + \frac12\left(-2\hbar\right)^2 = 4\hbar^2, \qquad \Delta L_z = \sqrt{4\hbar^2 - 0} = 2\hbar" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For two outcomes $q_1$ and $q_2$ with probabilities $p$ and $1 - p$ the uncertainty is $|q_1 - q_2|\sqrt{p(1 - p)}$. Here that is $6\hbar^2\sqrt{\frac13\cdot\frac23} = 2\sqrt{2}\,\hbar^2$ and $4\hbar\sqrt{\frac12\cdot\frac12} = 2\hbar$. Neither uncertainty is zero, so the state is an eigenstate of neither operator, in agreement with part (a).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Spherical wells" points={10} mit="Problem 4">

A particle of mass $m$ moves in a central potential. The radial equation of the lesson holds for $u(r) = r\,R(r)$, and $u$ must vanish at $r = 0$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Consider the states with $l = 0$ in the infinite spherical well

<Formula label="The infinite spherical well" tex="V(r) = \begin{cases} 0, & r < a \\ \infty, & r > a \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 2" />

Solve the radial equation for $u(r)$ and find the possible energies $E_n$, $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ Then try to match this spectrum to that of a one-dimensional infinite well $V(x)$ (the choice box, our addition). In the formula box use `n`, `hbar`, `m`, `a` and `pi`.

<Hint>

For $l = 0$ there is no centrifugal term, and inside the well $V = 0$. The radial equation is then the one-dimensional free equation $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u'' = Eu$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

There are two conditions on $u$: it vanishes at $r = 0$ (always) and at $r = a$ (the infinite wall). The first one removes the cosine.

</Hint>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="The virial theorem and applications" points={15} mit="Problem 6">

For an operator $\hat\Omega$ that does not depend on time, the lesson's rule for the time derivative of an expectation value (notes of Lecture 8, p. 6, eq. (3.37)) reads

<Formula label="Time derivative of an expectation value" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat\Omega\rangle = \left\langle\left[\hat\Omega, \hat H\right]\right\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 3" />

where $\hat H$ is the Hamiltonian.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Explain carefully why the right-hand side vanishes if the system is in a stationary state.

<Hint>

A stationary state is an energy eigenstate, $\hat H\psi = E\psi$. Write the expectation value of the commutator as two inner products and let $\hat H$ act on the nearest $\psi$ in each. In one of them $\hat H$ first has to be moved to the other side of the inner product.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** In an energy eigenstate $\hat H$ can be replaced by the number $E$, whether it acts to the right or (being Hermitian) to the left. The two terms of the commutator then cancel.

<Step title="The two terms">

Let $\hat H\psi = E\psi$ with $\psi$ normalized. Then

<Formula label="The commutator with H in an energy eigenstate" tex="\left\langle\left[\hat\Omega, \hat H\right]\right\rangle = \left(\psi, \hat\Omega\hat H\psi\right) - \left(\psi, \hat H\hat\Omega\psi\right) = E\left(\psi, \hat\Omega\psi\right) - \left(\hat H\psi, \hat\Omega\psi\right) = \left(E - E^*\right)\left(\psi, \hat\Omega\psi\right) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

In the second term $\hat H$ was moved onto the left function, which is allowed because $\hat H$ is Hermitian. A number comes out of the left slot of an inner product complex conjugated, and $E^* = E$ because the eigenvalues of a Hermitian operator are real. Nothing was assumed about $\hat\Omega$: it need not be Hermitian.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The left-hand side agrees. The full stationary state is $\Psi = e^{-iEt/\hbar}\psi$, and in $\left(\Psi, \hat\Omega\Psi\right)$ the two phase factors cancel when $\hat\Omega$ has no time dependence of its own. So $\langle\hat\Omega\rangle$ is constant, which is why these states are called stationary. A superposition of two energies does not pass the test: the step $\hat H\psi = E\psi$ fails, and the lesson on [stationary states](/courses/8-04/stationary-states) shows the cross term that oscillates.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Step title="Commutator with the kinetic energy">

<Step title="The theorem">

Adding the two and using part (a):

<Formula label="The virial theorem from the commutator" tex="0 = \left\langle\left[\hat{\vec r}\cdot\hat{\vec p}, \hat H\right]\right\rangle = i\hbar\left(2\langle\hat T\rangle + \langle\hat V\rangle\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle\hat T\rangle = -\frac12\,\langle\hat V\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a general central potential the same steps give $2\langle\hat T\rangle = \left\langle r\frac{dV}{dr}\right\rangle$. For a power law $V \propto r^s$ that is $2\langle\hat T\rangle = s\langle\hat V\rangle$: $s = -1$ is the result here, and $s = 2$ gives $\langle\hat T\rangle = \langle\hat V\rangle$, the equal sharing of the harmonic oscillator. The ground state of hydrogen can be checked by hand with $\langle 1/r\rangle = 1/a_0$ (part (d)): $\langle\hat V\rangle = -e^2/a_0$, and $\langle\hat T\rangle = E_1 - \langle\hat V\rangle = -\frac{e^2}{2a_0} + \frac{e^2}{a_0} = \frac{e^2}{2a_0}$, which is $-\frac12\langle\hat V\rangle$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The virial theorem splits the known total energy into its kinetic and potential parts: the kinetic energy is minus the total energy.

<Step title="The kinetic energy">

From $\langle\hat T\rangle + \langle\hat V\rangle = E_n$ and $\langle\hat V\rangle = -2\langle\hat T\rangle$:

<Formula label="Kinetic and potential energy in the level n" tex="\langle\hat T\rangle = -E_n = \frac{e^2}{2a_0}\,\frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\alpha^2mc^2}{2n^2}, \qquad \langle\hat V\rangle = 2E_n = -\frac{e^2}{a_0}\,\frac{1}{n^2}" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 7, eq. (2.31), and p. 4, eq. (2.4), with the virial theorem of part (b)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The speed">

Setting $\frac12m\langle v^2\rangle = \frac{\alpha^2mc^2}{2n^2}$:

<Formula label="Root-mean-square speed of the electron" tex="\frac{\sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle}}{c} = \frac{\alpha}{n} \qquad\text{and, for the ground state with } Z \text{ protons,}\qquad \frac{\sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle}}{c} = Z\alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

With $Z$ protons the energies are $E_n = -\frac{Z^2e^2}{2a_0n^2}$ (notes p. 7, eq. (2.31)) and the potential $-Ze^2/r$ is still proportional to $1/r$, so the virial theorem holds unchanged and $\langle\hat T\rangle = Z^2\alpha^2mc^2/(2n^2)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Is it relativistic?">

In the ground state of hydrogen the speed is $\alpha c = 299{,}792/137.036 \approx 2188$ km/s, less than 1% of the speed of light, and higher levels are slower. Relativistic corrections are of relative order $(v/c)^2 = \alpha^2 \approx 5.3\times10^{-5}$: small, but measurable in the spectrum. So the electron of hydrogen is not relativistic, and the Schrödinger equation is a good description. For heavy nuclei that changes. The innermost electron of uranium ($Z = 92$) has $Z\alpha \approx 0.67$, and there a relativistic treatment is needed.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\alpha c/n$ is also the speed of the electron on the $n$-th orbit of Bohr's old model. The result does not depend on $l$ or $m$, only on $n$, because the energy does. The energies themselves confirm the number: $\frac12mc^2\alpha^2 = \frac12\times511{,}000\ \text{eV}/137.036^2 \approx 13.6$ eV.

</Step>

</Solution>
