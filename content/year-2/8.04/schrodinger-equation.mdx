The de Broglie wave $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ describes a free particle with definite momentum and energy. This lecture asks which operators extract those quantities from it, and so arrives at the momentum operator and the Schrödinger equation, first for a free particle, then in a potential and in three dimensions. Along the way it meets commutators, the Pauli matrices and Born's reading of the wavefunction (notes pp. 1–8). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 5; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## The momentum operator

The free particle's wavefunction is $\Psi(x, t) = e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$, with $p = \hbar k$, $E = \hbar\omega$ and $E = p^2/(2m)$ (notes p. 1, eqs. (1.1)–(1.2)). An $x$ derivative brings down a factor $ik$, so multiplying it by $\hbar/i$ brings down exactly the momentum:

<Formula label="Extracting the momentum" tex="\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Psi(x, t) = \frac{\hbar}{i}(ik)\Psi(x, t) = \hbar k\,\Psi(x, t) = p\,\Psi(x, t)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.3)" />

This identifies the **momentum operator** (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="Momentum operator" tex="\hat p \equiv \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \qquad \hat p\,\Psi = p\,\Psi" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eqs. (1.4)–(1.5)" />

An operator acts on a function of $x$ and $t$ and gives another. Because $\hat p$ acting on this $\Psi$ just multiplies it by the number $p$, $\Psi$ is an **eigenstate** of $\hat p$: a state of definite momentum (notes pp. 1–2). The analogy with matrices helps: operators are like matrices, wavefunctions like column vectors, and an eigenvector is a vector that a matrix only stretches, without turning it.

## The free Schrödinger equation

For the energy, a time derivative does the work (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Extracting the energy" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Psi = i\hbar(-i\omega)\Psi = \hbar\omega\,\Psi = E\,\Psi" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.6)" />

But for a free particle the energy is fixed by the momentum, so $E\Psi$ can also be written with momentum operators. Since $p$ is a constant, $E\Psi = \frac{p^2}{2m}\Psi = \frac{1}{2m}\,p\,(\hat p\Psi)$, and moving $p$ next to $\Psi$ and replacing it by $\hat p$ gives $E\Psi = \frac{1}{2m}\hat p\hat p\Psi$ (notes p. 2, eqs. (1.7)–(1.9)). This suggests the **energy operator**:

<Formula label="Free-particle energy operator" tex="\hat E \equiv \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.10)" />

Putting $\hat E$ in place of $E$ in the energy equation gives a differential equation that the de Broglie wave solves, the **free-particle Schrödinger equation** (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Free-particle Schrödinger equation" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.11)" />

Checking: for $\Psi = e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ the left side gives $\hbar\omega\Psi$ and the right side $\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\Psi$, so it is a solution exactly when $\hbar\omega = \hbar^2k^2/(2m)$, which is $E = p^2/(2m)$ (notes p. 3, eqs. (1.13)–(1.14)).

**More general solutions** (notes p. 3). The equation is linear, so sums of plane waves with different $k$ also solve it, for example $e^{i(k_1x - \omega_1t)} + e^{i(k_2x - \omega_2t)}$. But this is not a state of definite momentum: $\hat p$ multiplies the two terms by different numbers, $\hbar k_1$ and $\hbar k_2$, so the result is not a number times $\Psi$; nor is it a state of definite energy. The general solution is the most general superposition:

<Formula label="General free solution" tex="\Psi(x, t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dk\,\Phi(k)\,e^{i\left(kx - \omega(k)t\right)}, \qquad \omega(k) = \frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.17)" />

This solves the time evolution of any free packet: write the initial wavefunction as a Fourier integral, $\Psi(x, 0) = \int dk\,\Phi(k)e^{ikx}$, and then simply attach $e^{-i\omega(k)t}$ to each plane wave (notes p. 3, eq. (1.18)). The packet moves at the group velocity $d\omega/dk = dE/dp = p/m$, as a free particle should (notes p. 3, eq. (1.19)).

**Two features of the equation** (notes p. 4). It contains an explicit $i$, so for real $\Psi$ the right side would be real and the left side imaginary. (The notes conclude that there are no real solutions; strictly, both sides would have to vanish, which still allows real wavefunctions that never change, such as $\Psi = a + bx$ for a free particle. Any wavefunction that evolves in time must be complex.) Complex wavefunctions are forced on us. And it is not a conventional wave equation, $\partial^2\phi/\partial x^2 - \frac{1}{V^2}\partial^2\phi/\partial t^2 = 0$ (here $V$ is the wave's speed, not a potential), whose solutions $f(x \pm Vt)$ can be real (eq. (1.20)): the Schrödinger equation is first order in time.

## A particle in a potential

In a potential $V(x, t)$ the energy is kinetic plus potential, $E = p^2/(2m) + V(x, t)$, which suggests adding $V$ to the energy operator, where it simply multiplies the wavefunction (notes p. 4, eqs. (2.1)–(2.2)). The energy operator is usually called the **Hamiltonian**, and the postulated Schrödinger equation is (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Hamiltonian" tex="\hat H \equiv -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x, t)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.4)" />

<Formula label="Schrödinger equation" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x, t)\right)\Psi = \hat H\Psi" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eqs. (2.3) and (2.5)" />

**Position as an operator** (notes p. 5). The **position operator** $\hat x$ multiplies a function by $x$: $\hat xf(x) \equiv xf(x)$, and so $\hat x^kf(x) = x^kf(x)$. If the potential can be expanded in powers of $x$, then $V(\hat x, t)\Psi = V(x, t)\Psi$: the potential is an operator too (eqs. (2.6)–(2.8)).

## Linear operators and commutators

All these operators are **linear**: $\hat A(a\Psi) = a\hat A\Psi$ for a constant $a$, and $\hat A(\Psi_1 + \Psi_2) = \hat A\Psi_1 + \hat A\Psi_2$ (notes p. 5, eq. (2.9)). Linear operators can be added, and multiplied: $\hat A\hat B\Psi \equiv \hat A(\hat B\Psi)$, acting first with $\hat B$, the one nearest $\Psi$. The order can matter, and the difference is measured by the **commutator**:

<Formula label="Commutator" tex="\left[\hat A, \hat B\right] \equiv \hat A\hat B - \hat B\hat A" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.10)" />

Two operators whose commutator vanishes **commute**; every operator commutes with itself (notes p. 5). For position and momentum, act on an arbitrary function $\psi(x)$ and use the product rule (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Computing [x̂, p̂]" tex="\left[\hat x, \hat p\right]\psi = x\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d\psi}{dx} - \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d}{dx}\left(x\psi\right) = x\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d\psi}{dx} - \frac{\hbar}{i}\psi - x\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d\psi}{dx} = i\hbar\,\psi" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eqs. (2.11)–(2.12)" />

Since this holds for every $\psi$, it is an equality of operators: two operators are equal when they do the same thing to everything. This is the most fundamental commutation relation in quantum mechanics (notes p. 6):

<Formula label="Canonical commutation relation" tex="\left[\hat x, \hat p\right] = i\hbar" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.13)" />

The right side is a number, acting as an operator by multiplication. This relation can be used to prove Heisenberg's uncertainty principle: the product of the uncertainties in position and momentum is always at least $\hbar/2$ (notes p. 6). (The notes say "exceed"; the bound, stated in the Lecture 7 notes as $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2$ (eq. (1.15)), can be reached exactly, by Gaussian wave packets.)

**Matrices that do not commute** (notes p. 6). The correspondence is operators ↔ matrices, wavefunctions ↔ vectors, eigenstates ↔ eigenvectors, and quantum mechanics can in fact be written entirely with matrices. The **Pauli matrices**

<Formula label="Pauli matrices" tex="\sigma_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_2 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -i \\ i & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_3 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.15)" />

describe spin one-half: the spin operator has components $S_i = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_i$. Multiplying them in both orders gives $\sigma_1\sigma_2 = \text{diag}(i, -i)$ and $\sigma_2\sigma_1 = \text{diag}(-i, i)$, so (notes p. 6, eqs. (2.16)–(2.17))

<Formula label="Pauli commutators" tex="\left[\sigma_1, \sigma_2\right] = 2i\sigma_3, \qquad \left[\sigma_2, \sigma_3\right] = 2i\sigma_1, \qquad \left[\sigma_3, \sigma_1\right] = 2i\sigma_2" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.17) and the text after it" />

Matrix mechanics was worked out by Heisenberg in 1925 and clarified by Born and Jordan. Written as matrices, $\hat x$ and $\hat p$ would have to be infinite: no finite matrices can have a commutator equal to a number times the identity (a short reason, not given in the notes: the trace of any commutator of finite matrices is zero, while the trace of $i\hbar$ times the identity is not). That fits, since there are infinitely many independent wavefunctions on a line (notes p. 6).

## Two properties, and three dimensions

Two basic properties of the Schrödinger equation (notes pp. 6–7):

1. **First order in time.** Knowing $\Psi(x, t_0)$ for all $x$ is enough: the right side, which needs only $x$ derivatives and multiplications, can then be evaluated everywhere, giving $\partial\Psi/\partial t$ at every point and so the wavefunction a moment later, and so on.
2. **Linear.** If $\Psi_1$ and $\Psi_2$ are solutions, so is $\alpha\Psi_1 + \beta\Psi_2$ for any complex $\alpha$, $\beta$.

**In three dimensions** (notes pp. 7–8) the de Broglie wave is $e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega t)}$ with $\vec p = \hbar\vec k$, and the gradient $\nabla = \left(\partial/\partial x, \partial/\partial y, \partial/\partial z\right)$ extracts the whole momentum vector, so $\hat{\vec p} = \frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla$ (eqs. (2.18)–(2.21)). There are three position and three momentum operators; a position component commutes with every momentum component except its own:

<Formula label="Commutators in three dimensions" tex="\left[\hat x_i, \hat p_j\right] = i\hbar\,\delta_{ij}, \qquad \delta_{ij} = \begin{cases} 1 & i = j \\ 0 & i \ne j \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="notes pp. 7–8, eqs. (2.23)–(2.24)" />

With $\hat{\vec p}^{\,2} = -\hbar^2\nabla^2$ and the Laplacian $\nabla^2 = \partial^2/\partial x^2 + \partial^2/\partial y^2 + \partial^2/\partial z^2$ (eqs. (2.26)–(2.27)), the Schrödinger equation becomes (notes p. 8):

<Formula label="Schrödinger equation in three dimensions" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + V(\vec x, t)\right)\Psi(\vec x, t)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 8, eq. (2.28)" />

## What the wavefunction means

Schrödinger first thought the wavefunction described a particle that could spread out, with a fraction proportional to $|\Psi|^2$ found at each place (notes p. 8). Max Born saw the problem. Solving the Schrödinger equation for a particle scattered by a potential, he found a wavefunction spreading out in all directions and falling off as $1/r$; yet experiments never find fractions of particles going off in many directions, but whole particles. Born proposed instead that $\Psi$ gives the **probability** (more precisely, $|\Psi|^2$ gives the probability density) of finding the particle at a place when a measurement is made there (notes p. 8):

<Formula label="Born's rule" tex="dP = \left|\Psi(\vec x, t)\right|^2 d^3x" cite="mit" at="notes p. 8, eq. (3.1)" />

is the probability of finding the particle in a small volume $d^3x$ around $\vec x$ at time $t$. The particle must be somewhere, so (notes p. 8)

<Formula label="Normalization" tex="\int_{\text{all space}} d^3x\,\left|\Psi(\vec x, t)\right|^2 = 1" cite="mit" at="notes p. 8, eq. (3.2)" />

Whether this stays true as $\Psi$ evolves in time is the subject of the next lecture.

<WorkedExample title="Operators at work">
<Problem>
(a) An electron has the wavefunction $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ with $k = 20\ \text{nm}^{-1}$. Find its wavelength and, from the Schrödinger equation, its energy in eV (use $\hbar c = 197.33$ eV nm and $m_ec^2 = 511{,}000$ eV). (b) Find the commutator $[\hat x^2, \hat p]$ by acting on a function $\psi(x)$. (c) Check $[\sigma_2, \sigma_3] = 2i\sigma_1$ by multiplying the matrices.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A plane wave">
$\lambda = 2\pi/k = 2\pi/20 \approx 0.314$ nm. The equation requires $\hbar\omega = \hbar^2k^2/(2m)$, so $E = (\hbar c)^2k^2/(2m_ec^2) = 197.33^2 \times 400/1{,}022{,}000 \approx 15.2$ eV.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A commutator">
$\hat x^2\hat p\psi = x^2\frac{\hbar}{i}\psi'$, and $\hat p\hat x^2\psi = \frac{\hbar}{i}\left(2x\psi + x^2\psi'\right)$. The difference is $-\frac{\hbar}{i}2x\psi = 2i\hbar\,x\psi$, so $[\hat x^2, \hat p] = 2i\hbar\,\hat x$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Pauli matrices">
$\sigma_2\sigma_3$ has rows $(0, i)$ and $(i, 0)$; $\sigma_3\sigma_2$ has rows $(0, -i)$ and $(-i, 0)$. The difference has rows $(0, 2i)$ and $(2i, 0)$, which is $2i\sigma_1$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The momentum operator $\hat p = \frac{\hbar}{i}\partial/\partial x$ has the plane wave as eigenstate, with eigenvalue $\hbar k$; in three dimensions $\hat{\vec p} = \frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla$.
- The Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\partial\Psi/\partial t = \hat H\Psi$, with $\hat H = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial^2/\partial x^2 + V$, is linear, first order in time, and needs complex solutions.
- Operators need not commute: $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$, and the Pauli matrices obey $[\sigma_1, \sigma_2] = 2i\sigma_3$ and its cyclic versions.
- Born's rule: $|\Psi|^2 d^3x$ is the probability of finding the particle in $d^3x$, and the total probability is 1.
