This set practises three lessons: [the Mach–Zehnder interferometer and the Elitzur–Vaidman bombs](/courses/8-04/mach-zehnder-interferometer), [the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering and matter waves](/courses/8-04/photoelectric-compton-de-broglie), and [matter waves under a change of frame](/courses/8-04/de-broglie-waves-group-velocity). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 2, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

A few things differ from MIT's sheet. MIT gives the points of each problem; the split between parts is ours. MIT's hand-drawn sketches for Problem 4 are not reproduced: the beam splitter and the interferometer are described in words. In Problem 1(d) MIT asks when the slow-electron formula has "an error" of 10%; the sheet does not say relative to what, so the problem states our choice. In Problem 4 the answer boxes ask for results in terms of $R$ alone (MIT allows $R$ and $T$), because a box can only compare formulas whose letters are independent. A few boxes ask for a number where MIT asks only a question in words. The sheet's pages are numbered: "p. 1" to "p. 4" below are the printed page numbers.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within 1% of ours (the one box with a tighter limit says so), so keep four significant figures, and type it in the unit shown next to the box. Small and large numbers can be typed as `1.2e-3` or `1.2 × 10^-3`. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `l/sqrt(g^2 - 1)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them (`g` stands for $\gamma$, `l` for $\ell$, `hbar` for $\hbar$, `e2` for $e^2$).

Problem 3 and several other parts ask you to show something. They have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

Use these values (CODATA 2018, rounded): $\hbar c = 197.327$ MeV fm, so $hc = 2\pi\hbar c = 1239.84$ MeV fm $= 12{,}398.4$ eV Å; the rest energy of the electron $m_ec^2 = 0.510999$ MeV and of the proton $m_pc^2 = 938.272$ MeV; the fine-structure constant $\alpha = 1/137.036$. Here 1 Å (ångström) $= 10^{-10}$ m and 1 fm (femtometre) $= 10^{-15}$ m. Like MIT's sheet, the set uses Gaussian units for the charge: the energy of two charges $e$ a distance $r$ apart is $e^2/r$.

Two letters have two jobs, as on MIT's sheet. In Problem 1, $\gamma$ is the Lorentz factor of the electron; in Problem 5 it is an unknown constant. In Problem 4, $t$ is an amplitude of the beam splitter; in Problem 5 it is the time.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The de Broglie wavelength of an electron" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

An electron of momentum $p$ has the de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = h/p$ of the lesson (Lecture 3 notes, eq. (3.23)). This problem turns that into working formulas for a slow and for a fast electron, and finds where the slow formula stops being good.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

For an electron slow enough to ignore relativity (label "nr"), with kinetic energy $E_{\text{kin}}$, the wavelength can be written as

<Formula label="Slow electron: the wavelength in ångström" tex="\lambda_{\text{nr}} = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{E_{\text{kin}}}}\ \text{Å}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

Here the kinetic energy is put in as a bare number, its value in eV, and $\delta$ is a number without units. Find $\delta$.

<Hint>

Write the momentum of a slow electron in terms of its kinetic energy, $E_{\text{kin}} = p^2/(2m_e)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Multiply top and bottom of $h/p$ by $c$. Then only $hc$ (in eV Å) and $m_ec^2$ (in eV) appear, and no SI constants are needed.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-delta" label="$\delta =$" value="12.26" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-delta" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put $p = \sqrt{2m_eE_{\text{kin}}}$ into $\lambda = h/p$ and collect the constants into $hc$ and $m_ec^2$.

<Step title="The wavelength in terms of the kinetic energy">

<Formula label="Slow electron" tex="\lambda_{\text{nr}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_eE_{\text{kin}}}} = \frac{hc}{\sqrt{2\,m_ec^2\,E_{\text{kin}}}}" cite="reading1" at="notes p. 6, eq. (3.23), with p from the kinetic energy (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

With $hc = 12{,}398.4$ eV Å and $2m_ec^2 = 1{,}021{,}998$ eV, whose square root is $1010.9$ in units of $\sqrt{\text{eV}}$:

<Formula label="The constant" tex="\lambda_{\text{nr}} = \frac{12{,}398.4\ \text{eV Å}}{1010.9\,\sqrt{\text{eV}}\,\sqrt{E_{\text{kin}}}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \delta = \frac{12{,}398.4}{1010.9} = 12.26" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

An electron of 100 eV gets $\lambda_{\text{nr}} = 12.26/\sqrt{100} = 1.226$ Å $= 0.1226$ nm. The lesson's worked example found 0.123 nm for the same electron. A second route, with SI values of $h$, $m_e$ and the electronvolt, gives the same $\delta$ (it is in the page's calculation file).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

For an electron of any speed (label "r"), write its total energy as $E = \gamma m_ec^2$, with $\gamma$ the Lorentz factor. Its wavelength is then

<Formula label="Fast electron: the wavelength from the Lorentz factor" tex="\lambda_{\text{r}} = \frac{\ell}{\sqrt{\gamma^2 - 1}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

Find the length $\ell$ in fm. Then say which well-known length it is.

<Hint>

The relativistic link between energy and momentum is $E^2 - p^2c^2 = m_e^2c^4$ (Lecture 3 notes, eq. (2.13)). Solve it for $pc$ with $E = \gamma m_ec^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-ell" label="$\ell =$" value="2426" unit="fm" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-ell" />

<Answer id="p1-ell-name" choices={["The Compton wavelength of the electron", "The Compton wavelength of the electron divided by 2π", "The Bohr radius", "The classical electron radius"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The momentum follows from the energy, and $\lambda = h/p$ does the rest.

<Step title="The momentum">

<Formula label="Momentum from the Lorentz factor" tex="p^2c^2 = E^2 - m_e^2c^4 = \left(\gamma^2 - 1\right)m_e^2c^4 \quad\Rightarrow\quad p = m_ec\,\sqrt{\gamma^2 - 1}" cite="reading1" at="notes p. 3, eq. (2.13)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The wavelength">

<Formula label="The length in the formula" tex="\lambda_{\text{r}} = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{m_ec}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma^2 - 1}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \ell = \frac{h}{m_ec} = \frac{hc}{m_ec^2} = \frac{1239.84\ \text{MeV fm}}{0.510999\ \text{MeV}} = 2426\ \text{fm}" cite="reading1" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.10)" />

This is the Compton wavelength of the electron, $\lambda_C = h/(m_ec)$, of the lesson.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson quotes $\lambda_C = 0.002426$ nm for the electron, and 0.002426 nm is 2426 fm. The Compton wavelength divided by $2\pi$ is 386 fm (Problem 2), so a factor $2\pi$ lost by using $\hbar$ in place of $h$ would show at once.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Use $E_{\text{kin}} = (\gamma - 1)\,m_ec^2$ to write $\lambda_{\text{nr}}$ of part (a) in terms of $\ell$ and $\gamma$ (the box). Then show that $\lambda_{\text{r}} < \lambda_{\text{nr}}$ at every energy.

<Hint>

Start from $\lambda_{\text{nr}} = h/\sqrt{2m_eE_{\text{kin}}}$, not from the number $\delta$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the inequality, factor $\gamma^2 - 1 = (\gamma - 1)(\gamma + 1)$ and compare $\gamma + 1$ with 2.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-nr" label="$\lambda_{\text{nr}} =$" expr="l/sqrt(2*(g - 1))"
  vars={{ l: [1, 3], g: [1.2, 5] }}
  names={{ l: "\\ell", g: "\\gamma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "l/sqrt(g^2 - 1)", hint: "That is the relativistic wavelength of part (b). Put the kinetic energy (γ − 1) m c² into h/√(2 m E)." },
    { expr: "l/sqrt(g - 1)", hint: "A factor 2 is missing under the root: the slow-electron momentum is √(2 m E), from E = p²/(2m)." },
    { expr: "l/sqrt(2*g)", hint: "You used the total energy γ m c². The formula of part (a) takes the kinetic energy, (γ − 1) m c²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both wavelengths are $\ell$ divided by a root. Compare what is under the roots.

<Step title="The slow formula in the new letters">

<Formula label="Slow formula with the Lorentz factor" tex="\lambda_{\text{nr}} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e\,(\gamma - 1)\,m_ec^2}} = \frac{h}{m_ec}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\left(\gamma - 1\right)}} = \frac{\ell}{\sqrt{2\left(\gamma - 1\right)}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The comparison">

Since $\gamma^2 - 1 = (\gamma - 1)(\gamma + 1)$,

<Formula label="Ratio of the two wavelengths" tex="\frac{\lambda_{\text{nr}}}{\lambda_{\text{r}}} = \sqrt{\frac{\left(\gamma - 1\right)\left(\gamma + 1\right)}{2\left(\gamma - 1\right)}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma + 1}{2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

A moving electron has $\gamma > 1$, so $\gamma + 1 > 2$ and the ratio is larger than 1: $\lambda_{\text{r}} < \lambda_{\text{nr}}$. In words: at a given kinetic energy the true momentum is larger than $\sqrt{2m_eE_{\text{kin}}}$, so the true wavelength is shorter.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a slow electron $\gamma \to 1$, the ratio goes to 1 and the two formulas agree, as they must. For a very fast one the ratio grows like $\sqrt{\gamma/2}$, so the slow formula fails without limit.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={8}>

Three calculations with numbers.

**(i)** An electron has a de Broglie wavelength equal to its Compton wavelength. Find its total energy $E$ (rest energy included) in MeV, and its speed as a fraction of $c$. MIT asks: is it relativistic, in the sense that it moves faster than $0.2c$?

**(ii)** The de Broglie wavelength of a particle is a rough measure of the smallest distance it can explore in a collision. MIT's sheet of 2016 takes two machines: a proposed linear collider that would bring electrons to an energy of 1 TeV $= 1000$ GeV, and the Large Hadron Collider at Geneva with protons of 7 TeV. Find the two wavelengths in attometres (1 am $= 10^{-18}$ m $= 10^{-3}$ fm), and compare them.

**(iii)** Call the error of the slow formula $(\lambda_{\text{nr}} - \lambda_{\text{r}})/\lambda_{\text{r}}$: the difference as a fraction of the true wavelength (our choice; MIT's sheet does not define it). Find the largest kinetic energy, in keV, for which the error is at most 10%, and the electron's $\beta = v/c$ at that energy.

<Hint>

(i) Set $\lambda_{\text{r}} = \ell$ in the formula of part (b). The speed follows from $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

(ii) At these energies the rest energy is tiny next to the total energy, so $pc$ and $E$ are equal to many figures. Use $\lambda = hc/(pc)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

(iii) Part (c) gave the ratio $\lambda_{\text{nr}}/\lambda_{\text{r}}$ as a function of $\gamma$ alone. An error of 10% means that this ratio is 1.10.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e" label="(i) Total energy:" value="0.7227" unit="MeV" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-e" />

<Answer id="p1-beta1" label="(i) $v/c =$" value="0.7071" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-beta1" />

<Answer id="p1-lam-e" label="(ii) Electron at 1 TeV:" value="1.240" unit="am" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-lam-e" />

<Answer id="p1-lam-p" label="(ii) Proton at 7 TeV:" value="0.1771" unit="am" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-lam-p" />

<Answer id="p1-kmax" label="(iii) Largest kinetic energy:" value="214.6" unit="keV" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-kmax" />

The last box accepts 0.3% only, so that it tells this speed from the one of (i):

<Answer id="p1-beta3" label="(iii) $\beta =$" value="0.7100" tolerance="0.3%" calc="p1-beta3" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** All three use the formulas of parts (b) and (c), which depend on $\gamma$ alone.

<Step title="(i) Wavelength equal to the Compton wavelength">

$\lambda_{\text{r}} = \ell$ needs $\gamma^2 - 1 = 1$, so $\gamma = \sqrt 2$:

<Formula label="Energy and speed" tex="E = \sqrt 2\,m_ec^2 = 1.4142 \times 0.510999\ \text{MeV} = 0.7227\ \text{MeV}, \qquad \beta = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2} = 0.7071" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d)(i), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Its kinetic energy is $(\sqrt 2 - 1)\,m_ec^2 = 0.2117$ MeV. It moves at 71% of the speed of light, far above $0.2c$: it is relativistic. Another way to see it: $\lambda = \lambda_C$ means $p = m_ec$, a momentum that a slow electron ($p = m_ev$ with $v \ll c$) cannot have.

</Step>

<Step title="(ii) Two colliders">

For the electron, $\gamma = 10^{6}\ \text{MeV}/0.510999\ \text{MeV} = 1.957 \times 10^{6}$, so $\sqrt{\gamma^2 - 1}$ equals $\gamma$ to 13 figures and $pc = E$. The proton has $\gamma = 7 \times 10^{6}/938.272 = 7460$, and again $pc = E$ to better than one part in $10^{7}$. (For the same reason it does not matter whether the quoted energy is the total or the kinetic one.)

<Formula label="Wavelengths at the two colliders" tex="\lambda_e = \frac{hc}{E} = \frac{1239.84\ \text{MeV fm}}{10^{6}\ \text{MeV}} = 1.240 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{fm} = 1.240\ \text{am}, \qquad \lambda_p = \frac{1239.84\ \text{MeV fm}}{7 \times 10^{6}\ \text{MeV}} = 0.1771\ \text{am}" cite="reading1" at="notes p. 6, eq. (3.23)" />

At these energies the mass no longer matters and the wavelength is $hc/E$, like a photon's. So the proton's wavelength is 7 times shorter, simply because its energy is 7 times larger. Both are far below 1 fm, the rough size of a proton quoted in the lesson: the electron's by a factor of about 800.

</Step>

<Step title="(iii) Where the slow formula is 10% off">

The error is $\sqrt{(\gamma + 1)/2} - 1$, which grows with $\gamma$. It reaches $0.10$ when

<Formula label="The largest Lorentz factor" tex="\sqrt{\frac{\gamma + 1}{2}} = 1.10 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \gamma = 2 \times 1.10^2 - 1 = 1.42" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d)(iii), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Kinetic energy and speed" tex="E_{\text{kin}} = 0.42\,m_ec^2 = 0.2146\ \text{MeV} = 214.6\ \text{keV}, \qquad \beta = \sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{1.42^2}} = 0.7100" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d)(iii), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Put $\gamma = 1.42$ back: $\lambda_{\text{nr}}/\ell = 1/\sqrt{0.84} = 1.0911$ and $\lambda_{\text{r}}/\ell = 1/\sqrt{1.42^2 - 1} = 0.9919$, with ratio $1.100$. This electron is almost the one of (i), with $\gamma = 1.42$ against $1.414$: written with the kinetic energy, the slow formula is only 10% off even at 71% of the speed of light. If you measured the error as a fraction of $\lambda_{\text{nr}}$ instead, you found $\gamma = 2/0.81 - 1 = 1.469$ and 239.7 keV; that answers a different definition and the box does not accept it.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Bohr radius, Compton wavelength and classical electron radius" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

Three lengths belong to the electron. The **classical electron radius** $r_0$ is the radius of a charged ball whose electrostatic energy equals the electron's rest energy, with factors of order 1 left out:

<Formula label="Classical electron radius" tex="\frac{e^2}{r_0} = m_ec^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad r_0 = \frac{e^2}{m_ec^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

with $e$ the charge of the electron. The **reduced Compton wavelength** (MIT's sheet calls it the bar-Compton wavelength) is the Compton wavelength of the lesson divided by $2\pi$:

<Formula label="Reduced Compton wavelength" tex="\bar\lambda_C = \frac{\hbar}{m_ec}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

The third is the **Bohr radius** $a_0$, the size of the hydrogen atom, which this course reaches in [the lesson on the hydrogen atom](/courses/8-04/hydrogen-atom). The strength of the electromagnetic force is measured by the fine-structure constant, a pure number:

<Formula label="Fine-structure constant" tex="\alpha = \frac{e^2}{\hbar c} \simeq \frac{1}{137}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

<Part label="a" points={5}>

The Bohr radius is the one length that can be built from $e^2$, $\hbar$ and $m_e$ alone, with no numerical factor. Find its formula from the units. Then give its value in fm.

<Hint>

Find the units of $e^2$ from the fact that $e^2/r$ is an energy. The units of $\hbar$ follow from $E = \hbar\omega$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $a_0 = (e^2)^{n_1}\,\hbar^{n_2}\,m_e^{n_3}$ and demand that mass and time cancel while one power of length is left. For the number, rewrite the result with $\hbar c$, $m_ec^2$ and $\alpha$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a0" label="$a_0 =$" expr="hbar^2/(m*e2)"
  vars={{ hbar: [1, 2], m: [1, 3], e2: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", m: "m_e", e2: "e^2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*e2/hbar^2", hint: "That is upside down: it has the units of 1/length." },
    { expr: "e2/m", hint: "Without ħ the units cannot work: e²/m is a length times a speed squared. (Divided by c² it would be the classical electron radius, but c is not allowed here.)" },
    { expr: "hbar/(m*e2)", hint: "Check the units again: this is a time divided by a mass and a length. Two powers of ħ are needed." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-a0-fm" label="$a_0 =$" value="52918" unit="fm" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-a0-fm" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Three quantities and three base units (mass, length, time) leave exactly one combination that is a length.

<Step title="The units">

Write M, L and T for the units of mass, length and time. An energy is M L² T⁻². Then $e^2$ = energy × length = M L³ T⁻², $\hbar$ = energy × time = M L² T⁻¹, and $m_e$ = M.

</Step>

<Step title="The powers">

For $a_0 = (e^2)^{n_1}\,\hbar^{n_2}\,m_e^{n_3}$: time needs $-2n_1 - n_2 = 0$, length needs $3n_1 + 2n_2 = 1$, mass needs $n_1 + n_2 + n_3 = 0$. The first two give $n_1 = -1$, $n_2 = 2$, and then $n_3 = -1$:

<Formula label="Bohr radius" tex="a_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{m_e\,e^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The value">

Multiply top and bottom by $c^2$ and use $e^2 = \alpha\hbar c$:

<Formula label="Bohr radius in numbers" tex="a_0 = \frac{\hbar c}{\alpha\,m_ec^2} = \frac{197.327\ \text{MeV fm} \times 137.036}{0.510999\ \text{MeV}} = 52{,}918\ \text{fm}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

52,918 fm is 0.529 Å, the value the hydrogen lesson quotes. The formula is also the one found there from the physics of the atom, where it balances the kinetic energy $\hbar^2/(m_ea_0^2)$ against the potential energy $e^2/a_0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Show that the three lengths form a geometric sequence with ratio $\alpha$:

<Formula label="Three lengths, each smaller by the fine-structure constant" tex="a_0 : \bar\lambda_C : r_0 = 1 : \alpha : \alpha^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2" />

Use this to find $\bar\lambda_C$ and $r_0$ in fm.

<Hint>

Divide each length by the one before it and look for $e^2/(\hbar c)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-lc" label="$\bar\lambda_C =$" value="386.2" unit="fm" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-lc" />

<Answer id="p2-r0" label="$r_0 =$" value="2.818" unit="fm" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-r0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two ratios, each of which collapses to $\alpha$.

<Step title="The two ratios">

<Formula label="Ratios of neighbouring lengths" tex="\frac{\bar\lambda_C}{a_0} = \frac{\hbar}{m_ec}\,\frac{m_e\,e^2}{\hbar^2} = \frac{e^2}{\hbar c} = \alpha, \qquad \frac{r_0}{\bar\lambda_C} = \frac{e^2}{m_ec^2}\,\frac{m_ec}{\hbar} = \frac{e^2}{\hbar c} = \alpha" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So $\bar\lambda_C = \alpha\,a_0$ and $r_0 = \alpha\,\bar\lambda_C = \alpha^2a_0$, which is the sequence.

</Step>

<Step title="The values">

<Formula label="The two smaller lengths" tex="\bar\lambda_C = \frac{52{,}918\ \text{fm}}{137.036} = 386.2\ \text{fm}, \qquad r_0 = \frac{386.2\ \text{fm}}{137.036} = 2.818\ \text{fm}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Directly, $\bar\lambda_C = \hbar c/(m_ec^2) = 197.327/0.510999 = 386.2$ fm, without $\alpha$ at all. And $2\pi \times 386.16$ fm $= 2426$ fm, the Compton wavelength $\ell$ of Problem 1(b). The atom is about 137 times larger than the reduced Compton wavelength, the length at which confining an electron would make it relativistic, which is why hydrogen can be treated without relativity to a good first approximation.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Two-by-two matrices and linear devices" points={10} mit="Problem 3">

In the two-beam Mach–Zehnder interferometer of the lesson, a photon is described by a column of two amplitudes (the lesson calls them $\alpha$ and $\beta$; here they are $u_1$ and $u_2$):

<Formula label="A beam as a column vector" tex="u = \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \left|u_1\right|^2 + \left|u_2\right|^2 = 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

Any linear optical element turns an input $u$ into an output $u' = R\,u$, with $R$ a two-by-two matrix of complex numbers. A matrix is **unitary** if $R^\dagger R = \mathbf{1}$, where the dagger means: transpose the matrix and take the complex conjugate of every entry.

Show that if probability is conserved for every input $u$, then $R$ must be unitary.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Write conservation of probability as one equation of the form $u^\dagger M u = 0$, valid for every $u$, and give the matrix $M$ in terms of $R$. Which property does $M$ have, whatever $R$ is?

<Hint>

For a column $u$, the row $u^\dagger = (u_1^*, u_2^*)$ gives $u^\dagger u = \left|u_1\right|^2 + \left|u_2\right|^2$. What is the dagger of the product $R\,u$?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The total probability of a column is $u^\dagger u$, so conservation compares $u'^\dagger u'$ with $u^\dagger u$.

<Step title="The condition">

The dagger of a product reverses the order, $(Ru)^\dagger = u^\dagger R^\dagger$. The output is normalized when

<Formula label="Conservation of probability" tex="u'^\dagger u' = u^\dagger R^\dagger R\,u = u^\dagger u \quad\Rightarrow\quad u^\dagger M u = 0, \qquad M = R^\dagger R - \mathbf{1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

It must hold for every normalized $u$. It then holds for every column at all, because multiplying $u$ by a number multiplies both sides by the same factor.

</Step>

<Step title="A property of M">

$M$ is Hermitian, $M^\dagger = M$, because $(R^\dagger R)^\dagger = R^\dagger R$. So its diagonal entries are real and $M_{21} = M_{12}^*$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

Show that $u^\dagger M u = 0$ for every $u$ forces $M = 0$, that is $R^\dagger R = \mathbf{1}$.

<Hint>

"Every $u$" is a strong demand. Try simple columns first: what do $(1, 0)$ and $(0, 1)$ tell you about $M$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The off-diagonal entry $M_{12}$ is a complex number, so it takes two more test columns to kill it: one with real entries, such as $(1, 1)/\sqrt 2$, and one with a relative factor $i$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each test column picks out a combination of the entries of $M$. Four well-chosen columns pin down all of them.

<Step title="The diagonal">

For $u = (1, 0)$, $u^\dagger M u = M_{11}$, so $M_{11} = 0$. For $u = (0, 1)$ in the same way $M_{22} = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The off-diagonal entries">

With the diagonal gone, a general column gives

<Formula label="What is left of the condition" tex="u^\dagger M u = u_1^*\,M_{12}\,u_2 + u_2^*\,M_{21}\,u_1 = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

For $u = (1, 1)/\sqrt 2$ this reads $\tfrac12(M_{12} + M_{21}) = 0$. For $u = (1, i)/\sqrt 2$ it reads $\tfrac{i}{2}(M_{12} - M_{21}) = 0$. The sum and the difference both vanish, so $M_{12} = M_{21} = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The conclusion">

All four entries of $M$ are zero: $R^\dagger R = \mathbf{1}$, and $R$ is unitary. (With $M_{21} = M_{12}^*$ from part (a), the two test columns say that the real part and the imaginary part of $M_{12}$ vanish.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The converse is one line: if $R^\dagger R = \mathbf{1}$ then $u'^\dagger u' = u^\dagger u$ for every $u$, so unitary matrices are exactly the ones that conserve probability. A test with numbers: the lesson's balanced beam splitter, $\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, is real and symmetric, and its square is $\frac12\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \mathbf{1}$. The lesson's first guess, with all four entries $1/\sqrt 2$, has $R^\dagger R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, which is not the unit matrix, and indeed it sent $(1, 1)/\sqrt 2$ to a column of total probability 2.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Improving on bomb detection" points={15} mit="Problem 4">

The lesson's interferometer certifies one working Elitzur–Vaidman bomb in three without setting it off. This problem does better by changing the beam splitters.

Build a beam splitter with **reflectivity** $R$ and **transmissivity** $T$: a photon that arrives at it, from either side, is reflected with probability $R$ and transmitted with probability $T$, and $R + T = 1$. Let $r$ and $t$ be the positive square roots:

<Formula label="Amplitudes of the unbalanced beam splitter" tex="r \equiv \sqrt R, \qquad t \equiv \sqrt T" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 3" />

MIT's sheet shows the set-up in two sketches, described here in words. The beam splitter is a horizontal plate. As in the lesson, the upper entry of a column is the amplitude of the beam above the plate, and the lower entry that of the beam below it. A photon that hits the plate from above (input $(1, 0)$) is either reflected and leaves above the plate, or transmitted and leaves below it. A photon that hits from below (input $(0, 1)$) is either transmitted and leaves above, or reflected and leaves below.

The interferometer has two identical copies of this beam splitter. After the first one, a mirror in the upper branch and a mirror in the lower branch send the two beams to the second one, where the upper branch arrives from above and the lower branch from below. Detector D0 catches the beam that leaves the second beam splitter above the plate, and D1 the beam that leaves below it. The photon enters from above.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Write the two-by-two matrix $U$ of the beam splitter. To fix the signs, MIT asks that every entry is real and positive except the bottom right one (the 2,2 entry). Confirm that $U$ is unitary.

<Hint>

The first column of a matrix is what it does to $(1, 0)$, and the second column what it does to $(0, 1)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Which amplitude belongs to "leaves on the same side of the plate as it came from": $r$ or $t$? Then let unitarity decide the sign of the 2,2 entry.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-u" choices={["Top row (r, t), bottom row (t, −r)", "Top row (t, r), bottom row (r, −t)", "Top row (r, t), bottom row (t, r)", "Top row (r, −t), bottom row (t, r)"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Read the columns off the description, then use Problem 3: a device that conserves probability is a unitary matrix.

<Step title="The columns">

A photon from above stays above when reflected (amplitude of size $r$) and goes below when transmitted (size $t$): the first column is $(r, t)$. A photon from below goes above when transmitted and stays below when reflected: the second column is $(t, \pm r)$, with the sign still open.

</Step>

<Step title="The sign and the check">

<Formula label="The unbalanced beam splitter" tex="U = \begin{pmatrix} r & t \\ t & -r \end{pmatrix}, \qquad U^\dagger U = \begin{pmatrix} r & t \\ t & -r \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} r & t \\ t & -r \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} r^2 + t^2 & rt - tr \\ tr - rt & t^2 + r^2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

$U$ is real and symmetric, so $U^\dagger = U$, and $r^2 + t^2 = R + T = 1$. The minus sign is needed: with $+r$ in the corner the off-diagonal entries would be $2rt$, not zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $R = T = \tfrac12$, $U$ becomes $\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, the balanced beam splitter of the lesson (Lecture 2 notes, eq. (1.14)). The second choice in the box swaps $r$ and $t$: it is unitary too, but it describes a plate where a reflected photon changes side.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

A bomb is placed in the lower branch.

First, a **defective** bomb, which lets the photon pass as if nothing were there. What are the probabilities $P_0$ and $P_1$ that the photon reaches D0 and D1 (the choice box)?

Next, a **working** bomb, which explodes when the photon reaches it. Find the probability $P_{\text{boom}}$ of an explosion and the probabilities $P_0$ and $P_1$. In the three formula boxes, write the results with $R$ only (put $T = 1 - R$).

<Hint>

The state after the first beam splitter is $U$ acting on $(1, 0)$. With a defective bomb the second beam splitter acts on all of it.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A working bomb acts like the block in the lesson: the lower entry is removed from the state (its squared size is the chance of an explosion), and the second beam splitter acts on what is left, which is no longer normalized.

</Hint>

<Formula label="Output with a defective bomb" tex="U\begin{pmatrix} r \\ t \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} r^2 + t^2 \\ tr - rt \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} \quad\Rightarrow\quad P_0 = 1, \quad P_1 = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Working bomb">

The bomb explodes if it finds the photon in the lower branch:

<Formula label="Chance of an explosion" tex="P_{\text{boom}} = t^2 = T = 1 - R" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 7, table (2.25), for a general beam splitter (our own solution)" />

A bomb is tested with one photon after another, until you are reasonably sure that it is defective or you know that it works. What fraction $f$ of the working bombs is certified as working without being set off? Give $f$ in terms of $R$. Then give the largest value that $f$ can approach.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-f" label="$f =$" expr="R/(1 + R)"
  vars={{ R: [0.1, 0.9] }}
  mistakes={[

<Answer id="p4-fmax" label="$f$ stays below:" value="0.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-fmax" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A photon at D0 is what a defective bomb always gives, so it settles nothing and the test goes on. Only D1 or an explosion ends the test of a working bomb.

<Step title="Reading the outcomes">

A defective bomb sends every photon to D0. So one photon at D1 proves that the bomb works, and the photon that proves it went through the upper branch. A photon at D0 fits both kinds of bomb: test again. After $n$ photons in a row at D0, a working bomb would have survived with probability $P_0^{\,n} = R^{2n}$, which shrinks with every photon, so in the end you call the bomb defective.

</Step>

<Step title="The fraction">

A working bomb is certified at the first photon with probability $P_1$, at the second with $P_0P_1$, at the third with $P_0^2P_1$, and so on:

<Formula label="Fraction of working bombs certified" tex="f = P_1\left(1 + P_0 + P_0^2 + \dots\right) = \frac{P_1}{1 - P_0} = \frac{R\left(1 - R\right)}{1 - R^2} = \frac{R}{1 + R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The same follows without a series: the test ends at D1 or with an explosion, so $f = P_1/(P_1 + P_{\text{boom}}) = RT/(RT + T)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The largest value">

$f = 1 - 1/(1 + R)$ grows as $R$ grows, and $R$ is at most 1, so $f$ stays below $\tfrac12$ and approaches it for $R \to 1$. The value $\tfrac12$ itself is not reached: at $R = 1$ nothing enters the lower branch, $P_1 = 0$, and the photon cannot tell the bombs apart at all.

</Step>

For the balanced beam splitter, $R = \tfrac12$ gives $f = \tfrac13$, the result of the lesson's worked example. With $R = 0.9$, $f = 0.9/1.9 = 0.474$: nearly half of the working bombs are saved, where the balanced set-up saves a third. The price is patience. With $R$ close to 1 almost every photon lands at D0 and many photons are needed before the test ends.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Plane waves for matter particles" points={10} mit="Problem 5">

A matter particle moves along $x$ with momentum $p = \hbar k$. A reasonable first guess for its wave is a mixture of a cosine and a sine:

<Formula label="A trial matter wave" tex="\Psi(x, t) = \cos\left(kx - \omega t\right) + \gamma\,\sin\left(kx - \omega t\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4" />

with $\gamma$ a constant to be found (not the Lorentz factor of Problem 1). Physics adds a demand: moving the origin of $x$, or the zero of time, must not change the character of the wave. Such a move adds a constant $\epsilon$ to the phase $kx - \omega t$. So for every $\epsilon$ the shifted wave must be the old wave times a constant $a$, which may depend on $\epsilon$:

<Formula label="The demand on the shifted wave" tex="\cos\left(kx - \omega t + \epsilon\right) + \gamma\,\sin\left(kx - \omega t + \epsilon\right) = a\left[\cos\left(kx - \omega t\right) + \gamma\,\sin\left(kx - \omega t\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Write the equations for $\gamma$ and $a$ that follow from this demand.

<Hint>

Expand the left side with the addition formulas for $\cos(A + B)$ and $\sin(A + B)$, with $A = kx - \omega t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The equation must hold at every $x$ and $t$, and $\cos(kx - \omega t)$ and $\sin(kx - \omega t)$ are independent functions. Compare their coefficients on the two sides.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both sides are a cosine and a sine of the same phase. They agree everywhere only if the two coefficients agree.

<Step title="Expand the left side">

Write $\phi = kx - \omega t$. Then

<Formula label="The shifted wave, expanded" tex="\cos\left(\phi + \epsilon\right) + \gamma\,\sin\left(\phi + \epsilon\right) = \left(\cos\epsilon + \gamma\sin\epsilon\right)\cos\phi + \left(\gamma\cos\epsilon - \sin\epsilon\right)\sin\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compare coefficients">

The right side is $a\cos\phi + a\gamma\sin\phi$. So

<Formula label="The two conditions" tex="a = \cos\epsilon + \gamma\sin\epsilon, \qquad a\,\gamma = \gamma\cos\epsilon - \sin\epsilon" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Solve the equations: what is $\gamma^2$ (the number box)? There are two solutions for $\gamma$. For the one with the plus sign, which $a$ goes with it (the choice box)?

<Hint>

Put the first equation into the second to remove $a$. The result must hold for every $\epsilon$, also when $\sin\epsilon$ is not zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-g2" label="$\gamma^2 =$" value="-1" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-g2" />

<Answer id="p5-a" choices={["a = cos ε + i sin ε = e^(iε), a phase factor of size 1", "a = cos ε − i sin ε = e^(−iε), a phase factor of size 1", "a = cos ε, a real number that can vanish", "a = 1 for every ε"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Removing $a$ leaves one equation for $\gamma$ alone.

<Step title="Remove a">

Multiply the first condition by $\gamma$ and set it equal to the second:

<Formula label="The equation for the constant" tex="\gamma\cos\epsilon + \gamma^2\sin\epsilon = \gamma\cos\epsilon - \sin\epsilon \quad\Rightarrow\quad \left(\gamma^2 + 1\right)\sin\epsilon = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

It must hold for every shift $\epsilon$, so $\gamma^2 = -1$. No real $\gamma$ will do.

</Step>

<Step title="The two solutions">

<Formula label="Solutions" tex="\gamma = +i: \quad a = \cos\epsilon + i\sin\epsilon = e^{i\epsilon}; \qquad \gamma = -i: \quad a = \cos\epsilon - i\sin\epsilon = e^{-i\epsilon}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $\gamma = i$ the wave is $\cos\phi + i\sin\phi = e^{i\phi}$, and a shift gives $e^{i(\phi + \epsilon)} = e^{i\epsilon}e^{i\phi}$: the old wave times $a = e^{i\epsilon}$, as found. In both solutions $\left|a\right| = 1$, so the shift changes only an overall phase and the size $\left|\Psi\right|$ of the wave stays the same everywhere. A real mixture cannot do this: a shift by a quarter of a wavelength turns a pure cosine into a sine, a wave with its zeros in other places.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Which of the two solutions is the usual description of a matter wave with momentum $\hbar k$ along $+x$?

<Hint>

Look up the free-particle wavefunction at the end of the lesson on Lecture 4.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-conv" choices={["γ = +i, the wave e^(i(kx − ωt))", "γ = −i, the wave e^(−i(kx − ωt))", "Either one: physicists use both for the same particle", "γ = 0, the wave cos(kx − ωt)"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both solutions are acceptable waves. Which one stands for momentum $+\hbar k$ is a convention, and the lesson fixed it.

<Step title="The convention">

Its time dependence is $e^{-i\omega t}$. The other solution, $e^{-i(kx - \omega t)}$, has time dependence $e^{+i\omega t}$ and is not used.

</Step>

The lesson reached the same wave by another road: sums of right-moving and left-moving sines or cosines vanish everywhere at some instants, so they were ruled out, and of the two exponentials one had to be chosen. Here the demand that a shift changes nothing physical rules out the real waves and again leaves the two exponentials. The two cannot both describe the same particle, because their sum is $2\cos(kx - \omega t)$, a wave already ruled out.

</Step>

</Solution>
