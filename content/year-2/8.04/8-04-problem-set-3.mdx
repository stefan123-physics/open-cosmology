## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Exercises with commutators" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

Let $\hat A$, $\hat B$ and $\hat C$ be linear operators. The commutator of two operators is the one of the lesson:

<Formula label="Commutator" tex="\left[\hat A, \hat B\right] \equiv \hat A\hat B - \hat B\hat A" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.10)" />

<Part label="a" points={1}>

Show that a commutator with a product in its second slot splits as follows.

<Formula label="Product in the second slot" tex="\left[\hat A, \hat B\hat C\right] = \left[\hat A, \hat B\right]\hat C + \hat B\left[\hat A, \hat C\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

<Hint>

Write out both sides with the definition of the commutator. Operators may not be reordered, so keep every product in the order in which it stands.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Expand the right side with the definition. Two of its four terms cancel.

<Step title="Expand the right side">

<Formula label="The right side of (a), written out" tex="\left[\hat A, \hat B\right]\hat C + \hat B\left[\hat A, \hat C\right] = \hat A\hat B\hat C - \hat B\hat A\hat C + \hat B\hat A\hat C - \hat B\hat C\hat A = \hat A\hat B\hat C - \hat B\hat C\hat A" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The two middle terms cancel. What is left is $\hat A(\hat B\hat C) - (\hat B\hat C)\hat A = [\hat A, \hat B\hat C]$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The rule has the shape of the product rule for a derivative: "$[\hat A, \ \cdot\ ]$" acts on one factor at a time, and the factor $\hat B$ that is passed over stays on the left. If $\hat A$ commutes with $\hat B$ and with $\hat C$, both sides are zero, as they must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

Show the matching rule for a product in the first slot.

<Formula label="Product in the first slot" tex="\left[\hat A\hat B, \hat C\right] = \hat A\left[\hat B, \hat C\right] + \left[\hat A, \hat C\right]\hat B" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

<Hint>

Expand the right side as in part (a). Or use part (a) together with the fact that swapping the two slots of a commutator changes its sign.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same expansion as in part (a).

<Step title="Expand the right side">

<Formula label="The right side of (b), written out" tex="\hat A\left[\hat B, \hat C\right] + \left[\hat A, \hat C\right]\hat B = \hat A\hat B\hat C - \hat A\hat C\hat B + \hat A\hat C\hat B - \hat C\hat A\hat B = \hat A\hat B\hat C - \hat C\hat A\hat B" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is $(\hat A\hat B)\hat C - \hat C(\hat A\hat B) = [\hat A\hat B, \hat C]$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second route: $[\hat A\hat B, \hat C] = -[\hat C, \hat A\hat B]$, and part (a) gives $-[\hat C, \hat A]\hat B - \hat A[\hat C, \hat B] = [\hat A, \hat C]\hat B + \hat A[\hat B, \hat C]$. Both routes agree.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Show that the three nested commutators below add up to zero. (This is known as the Jacobi identity.)

<Formula label="Three nested commutators" tex="\left[\hat A, \left[\hat B, \hat C\right]\right] + \left[\hat B, \left[\hat C, \hat A\right]\right] + \left[\hat C, \left[\hat A, \hat B\right]\right] = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

<Hint>

Each nested commutator gives four products of three operators. There are only six different orders of $\hat A$, $\hat B$, $\hat C$, so each order must appear twice among the twelve terms.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write out all twelve terms. Each of the six orderings of the three operators appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign.

<Step title="Expand each term">

<Formula label="The first nested commutator" tex="\left[\hat A, \left[\hat B, \hat C\right]\right] = \hat A\hat B\hat C - \hat A\hat C\hat B - \hat B\hat C\hat A + \hat C\hat B\hat A" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The other two follow by moving the letters around in a circle ($\hat A \to \hat B \to \hat C \to \hat A$):

<Formula label="The second and third nested commutators" tex="\left[\hat B, \left[\hat C, \hat A\right]\right] = \hat B\hat C\hat A - \hat B\hat A\hat C - \hat C\hat A\hat B + \hat A\hat C\hat B, \qquad \left[\hat C, \left[\hat A, \hat B\right]\right] = \hat C\hat A\hat B - \hat C\hat B\hat A - \hat A\hat B\hat C + \hat B\hat A\hat C" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Add them">

Go through the six orderings. $\hat A\hat B\hat C$: plus in the first line, minus in the third. $\hat A\hat C\hat B$: minus in the first, plus in the second. $\hat B\hat C\hat A$: minus in the first, plus in the second. $\hat C\hat B\hat A$: plus in the first, minus in the third. $\hat B\hat A\hat C$: minus in the second, plus in the third. $\hat C\hat A\hat B$: minus in the second, plus in the third. Every ordering cancels, so the sum is zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Twelve terms, six orderings, each twice with opposite signs: nothing is left over. A quick test with matrices: for the Pauli matrices of the lesson, $[\sigma_2, \sigma_3] = 2i\sigma_1$, so the first term is $[\sigma_1, 2i\sigma_1] = 0$, and the other two vanish in the same way.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Calculate $[\hat x^n, \hat p]$ and $[\hat x, \hat p^n]$ for a whole number $n \ge 1$.

Each result is $i\hbar$ times an operator. Type that operator in the box, writing `x` for $\hat x$ and `p` for $\hat p$.

<Hint>

Start with $n = 2$: split $\hat x^2 = \hat x\,\hat x$ with the rule of part (b) and use $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$. Then do $n = 3$ and look for the pattern.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For general $n$, split off one factor: $\hat x^n = \hat x\,\hat x^{n-1}$ in part (b), and $\hat p^n = \hat p\,\hat p^{n-1}$ in part (a). Each gives a commutator with $n - 1$ in place of $n$, so the pattern can be proved step by step (induction).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-xn" label="$[\hat x^n, \hat p] = i\hbar \times$" expr="n*x^(n-1)" vars={{ n: [1, 6], x: [0.5, 2] }} names={{ x: "\\hat x" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x^(n-1)", hint: "Test it with n = 2: the rule of part (b) gives two terms, x[x, p] and [x, p]x, not one." },
    { expr: "-n*x^(n-1)", hint: "The size is right but the sign is that of [p, x^n], with the two slots swapped." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-pn" label="$[\hat x, \hat p^n] = i\hbar \times$" expr="n*p^(n-1)" vars={{ n: [1, 6], p: [0.5, 2] }} names={{ p: "\\hat p" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "p^(n-1)", hint: "Test it with n = 2: the rule of part (a) gives two terms, [x, p]p and p[x, p], not one." },
    { expr: "-n*p^(n-1)", hint: "The size is right but the sign is that of [p^n, x], with the two slots swapped." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Peel off one factor at a time with the rules of parts (a) and (b). Each factor of $\hat x$ (or $\hat p$) gives one term $i\hbar$ times the remaining factors, and there are $n$ of them.

<Step title="Powers of x">

Use part (b) with $\hat A = \hat x$, $\hat B = \hat x^{n-1}$ and $\hat C = \hat p$, and the canonical commutator of the lesson, $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$ (Lecture 5 notes, eq. (2.13)):

<Formula label="One step down for powers of x" tex="\left[\hat x^n, \hat p\right] = \hat x\left[\hat x^{n-1}, \hat p\right] + \left[\hat x, \hat p\right]\hat x^{n-1} = \hat x\left[\hat x^{n-1}, \hat p\right] + i\hbar\,\hat x^{n-1}" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.13), used in part (b)" />

For $n = 1$ the result is $i\hbar$. Suppose $[\hat x^{n-1}, \hat p] = i\hbar\,(n-1)\,\hat x^{n-2}$. Then the step above gives $i\hbar\,(n-1)\,\hat x^{n-1} + i\hbar\,\hat x^{n-1}$, so

<Formula label="Commutator of a power of x with p" tex="\left[\hat x^n, \hat p\right] = i\hbar\,n\,\hat x^{n-1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Powers of p">

Use part (a) with $\hat A = \hat x$, $\hat B = \hat p$ and $\hat C = \hat p^{n-1}$:

<Formula label="One step down for powers of p" tex="\left[\hat x, \hat p^n\right] = \left[\hat x, \hat p\right]\hat p^{n-1} + \hat p\left[\hat x, \hat p^{n-1}\right] = i\hbar\,\hat p^{n-1} + \hat p\left[\hat x, \hat p^{n-1}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The same induction gives

<Formula label="Commutator of x with a power of p" tex="\left[\hat x, \hat p^n\right] = i\hbar\,n\,\hat p^{n-1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Let the first commutator act on a function $\varphi(x)$, with $\hat p = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d}{dx}$: $\hat x^n\hat p\,\varphi - \hat p\,\hat x^n\varphi = \frac{\hbar}{i}\left(x^n\frac{d\varphi}{dx} - \frac{d}{dx}\left(x^n\varphi\right)\right) = -\frac{\hbar}{i}\,n\,x^{n-1}\varphi = i\hbar\,n\,x^{n-1}\varphi$. It agrees. Both results look like derivatives: $[\hat x^n, \hat p] = i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dx}x^n$ and $[\hat x, \hat p^n] = i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dp}p^n$. For $n = 1$ both reduce to $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

Calculate $[\hat x\hat p, \hat x^2]$ and $[\hat x\hat p, \hat p^2]$.

Again each result is $i\hbar$ times an operator: type that operator, with `x` for $\hat x$ and `p` for $\hat p$.

<Hint>

Split the product $\hat x\hat p$ in the first slot with the rule of part (b). One of the two commutators that appear is zero each time.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The commutators that are left are cases of part (d) with $n = 2$. Mind the order of the slots: $[\hat p, \hat x^2] = -[\hat x^2, \hat p]$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-xpx2" label="$[\hat x\hat p, \hat x^2] = i\hbar \times$" expr="-2*x^2" vars={{ x: [0.5, 2] }} names={{ x: "\\hat x" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "-2*x", hint: "That is [p, x^2] alone, divided by iħ. The factor x in front of it is still there." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-xpp2" label="$[\hat x\hat p, \hat p^2] = i\hbar \times$" expr="2*p^2" vars={{ p: [0.5, 2] }} names={{ p: "\\hat p" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-2*p^2", hint: "Check the sign: the commutator that is left is [x, p^2], with x in the first slot." },
    { expr: "2*p", hint: "That is [x, p^2] alone, divided by iħ. The factor p to the right of it is still there." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Part (b) splits $\hat x\hat p$. Then part (d) with $n = 2$ does the rest.

<Step title="The commutator with x squared">

<Formula label="Commutator of xp with x squared" tex="\left[\hat x\hat p, \hat x^2\right] = \hat x\left[\hat p, \hat x^2\right] + \left[\hat x, \hat x^2\right]\hat p = \hat x\left(-2i\hbar\,\hat x\right) + 0 = -2i\hbar\,\hat x^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Here $[\hat p, \hat x^2] = -[\hat x^2, \hat p] = -2i\hbar\,\hat x$ by part (d), and $\hat x$ commutes with $\hat x^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The commutator with p squared">

<Formula label="Commutator of xp with p squared" tex="\left[\hat x\hat p, \hat p^2\right] = \hat x\left[\hat p, \hat p^2\right] + \left[\hat x, \hat p^2\right]\hat p = 0 + \left(2i\hbar\,\hat p\right)\hat p = 2i\hbar\,\hat p^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\hat x\hat p$ has the units of $\hbar$, so $[\hat x\hat p, \hat x^2]$ must be $\hbar$ times a length squared and $[\hat x\hat p, \hat p^2]$ must be $\hbar$ times a momentum squared. Both results fit. Commuting with $\hat x\hat p$ gives back the same power, multiplied by $-2i\hbar$ for $\hat x^2$ and by $+2i\hbar$ for $\hat p^2$: the factor 2 is the power, and the sign tells $\hat x$ from $\hat p$.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Simple tests of the stationary phase approximation" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

Take a wavefunction at one moment, built from plane waves $e^{ikx}$ as in the lesson:

<Formula label="A packet of plane waves" tex="\Psi(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dk\,\Phi(k)\,e^{ikx}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

The function $\Phi(k)$ is sharply peaked around $k = k_0$. For each $\Phi(k)$ below, first use the stationary phase argument to predict where $|\Psi(x)|$ has its peak. Then work out the integral exactly, find $\Psi(x)$ and $|\Psi(x)|$, and see whether the prediction holds.

MIT's sheet gives one integral to use. It holds for complex constants $a$ and $b$, as long as the real part of $a$ is positive:

<Formula label="Gaussian integral" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-ax^2 + bx}\,dx = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{a}}\,\exp\left(\frac{b^2}{4a}\right), \qquad \text{Re}(a) > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={5}>

$\Phi(k) = e^{-L^2(k - k_0)^2}$, where $L$ is a constant with units of length.

Choose the predicted position of the peak, and type $|\Psi(x)|$ in the box.

<Hint>

The stationary phase argument of the lesson: write the integrand as a size times $e^{i\phi(k)}$. The integral is large only at those $x$ for which the phase $\phi(k)$ does not change with $k$ at $k = k_0$. Here $\Phi(k)$ is real and positive, so it adds nothing to the phase.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the exact integral, shift the variable to $q = k - k_0$. A factor $e^{ik_0x}$ comes out, and what is left is MIT's Gaussian integral with $a = L^2$ and $b = ix$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a-peak" choices={["At x = 0", "At x = k0 L²", "At x = L", "At x = 1/k0"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p2-a-abs" label="$|\Psi(x)| =$" expr="sqrt(pi)/L*exp(-x^2/(4*L^2))" vars={{ L: [0.5, 2], x: [0.3, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(pi)/L*exp(-x^2/L^2)", hint: "The exponent is b²/(4a) with a = L² and b = ix. A factor 4 is missing." },
    { expr: "exp(-x^2/(4*L^2))", hint: "The shape is right. The factor √(π/a) in front of the Gaussian integral is missing." },
    { expr: "sqrt(pi)/L*exp(x^2/(4*L^2))", hint: "This grows without limit far away. With b = ix, b² is negative." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With a real, positive $\Phi(k)$ the whole phase of the integrand is $kx$. It is stationary in $k$ only at $x = 0$, so the peak should be there. The exact integral is a Gaussian centred on $x = 0$.

<Step title="The prediction">

The phase of the integrand is $\phi(k) = kx$. The lesson's condition (Lecture 4 notes, eq. (2.24), at $t = 0$) is that the phase is stationary at $k_0$:

<Formula label="Stationary phase for a real profile" tex="\left.\frac{d\phi}{dk}\right|_{k_0} = x = 0" cite="reading1" at="notes p. 4, eqs. (2.23)–(2.24)" />

So $|\Psi(x)|$ should peak at $x = 0$. For any other $x$, the waves with $k$ near $k_0$ have phases that run through the circle and largely cancel.

</Step>

<Step title="The exact integral">

With $q = k - k_0$:

<Formula label="The packet of part (a)" tex="\Psi(x) = e^{ik_0x}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dq\,e^{-L^2q^2 + ixq} = e^{ik_0x}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{L^2}}\,\exp\left(\frac{(ix)^2}{4L^2}\right) = \frac{\sqrt\pi}{L}\,e^{ik_0x}\,e^{-x^2/(4L^2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The factor $e^{ik_0x}$ has size 1, so

<Formula label="Size of the packet of part (a)" tex="\left|\Psi(x)\right| = \frac{\sqrt\pi}{L}\,e^{-x^2/(4L^2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The size is a Gaussian with its peak at $x = 0$: the prediction holds. At $x = 0$ the integral is $\int e^{-L^2q^2}dq = \sqrt\pi/L$, the value of the formula there. Units: $dk$ has units 1/length and so has $\sqrt\pi/L$. The size falls to $1/e$ of its peak at $x = \pm 2L$, while $\Phi(k)$ falls to $1/e$ at $k - k_0 = \pm 1/L$: a narrow profile in $k$ makes a wide packet in $x$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

$\Phi(k) = e^{-L^2(k - k_0)^2}\,e^{-ikx_0}$, where $x_0$ and $L$ are constants with units of length.

Type the predicted position of the peak and $|\Psi(x)|$.

<Hint>

Now $\Phi(k)$ has a phase of its own, $-kx_0$. Add it to the phase $kx$ of the plane wave before asking where the total is stationary.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the exact integral, $e^{ikx}e^{-ikx_0} = e^{ik(x - x_0)}$. So the integral is the one of part (a) with $x - x_0$ in place of $x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b-peak" label="Peak of $|\Psi|$ at $x =$" expr="x0" vars={{ x0: [0.5, 3] }} names={{ x0: "x_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-x0", hint: "The total phase is k(x − x0). Its derivative with respect to k vanishes where x − x0 = 0." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-b-abs" label="$|\Psi(x)| =$" expr="sqrt(pi)/L*exp(-(x-x0)^2/(4*L^2))" vars={{ L: [0.5, 2], x: [0.3, 3], x0: [0.5, 2.5] }} names={{ x0: "x_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(pi)/L*exp(-(x+x0)^2/(4*L^2))", hint: "This peaks at x = −x0. The phases combine to k(x − x0), so the packet moves to +x0." },
    { expr: "sqrt(pi)/L*exp(-x^2/(4*L^2))", hint: "That is the packet of part (a). The phase e^(−ik x0) is not a constant: it depends on k and cannot be taken out of the integral." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A phase in $\Phi(k)$ that is linear in $k$ shifts the packet. Stationary phase predicts the shift, and the exact integral confirms it.

<Step title="The prediction">

The total phase is $\phi(k) = kx - kx_0$. It is stationary where

<Formula label="Stationary phase with a linear phase in the profile" tex="\left.\frac{d\phi}{dk}\right|_{k_0} = x - x_0 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = x_0" cite="reading1" at="notes p. 4, eqs. (2.23)–(2.24)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The exact integral">

The integrand is that of part (a) with $x \to x - x_0$:

<Formula label="The packet of part (b)" tex="\Psi(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dk\,e^{-L^2(k - k_0)^2}\,e^{ik(x - x_0)} = \frac{\sqrt\pi}{L}\,e^{ik_0(x - x_0)}\,e^{-(x - x_0)^2/(4L^2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Size of the packet of part (b)" tex="\left|\Psi(x)\right| = \frac{\sqrt\pi}{L}\,e^{-(x - x_0)^2/(4L^2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The Gaussian peaks at $x = x_0$, as predicted. For $x_0 = 0$ the result is that of part (a). The size $|\Phi(k)|$ is the same in both parts: only the phase of $\Phi$ differs, and that alone moves the packet. This is how the lesson's moving packet works too: there the phase $-\omega(k)t$ shifts the peak to $x = v_g t$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Galilean invariance of the free Schrödinger equation" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

<Formula label="The wavefunction of the moving observer" tex="\Psi'(x', t') = f(x, t)\,\Psi(x, t), \qquad i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi'}{\partial t'} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi'}{\partial x'^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

<Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Rewrite the primed derivatives in unprimed variables, insert $\Psi' = f\Psi$, and demand that the equation holds for every solution $\Psi$. That gives two conditions on $f$, and they fix it.

<Step title="The derivatives of the moving observer">

Because $x = x' + vt'$ and $t = t'$, the chain rule gives

<Formula label="Primed derivatives" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial x'} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}, \qquad \frac{\partial}{\partial t'} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + v\frac{\partial}{\partial x}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="Insert the product">

The primed equation for $\Psi' = f\Psi$ reads

<Formula label="The primed equation in unprimed variables" tex="i\hbar\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\Psi + f\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} + v\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Psi + vf\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right) = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}\Psi + 2\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} + f\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The term $i\hbar f\,\partial\Psi/\partial t$ on the left equals the last term on the right, because $\Psi$ obeys the unprimed equation. They cancel.

</Step>

<Step title="Two conditions">

What remains has terms with $\partial\Psi/\partial x$ and terms with $\Psi$. At one moment, $\Psi$ and its slope can be chosen freely, so each group must vanish by itself:

<Formula label="Conditions on f" tex="i\hbar\,v f = -\frac{\hbar^2}{m}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \qquad i\hbar\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + v\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right) = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The first condition says $\partial f/\partial x = -\frac{imv}{\hbar}f$, so $f = g(t)\,e^{-imvx/\hbar}$ with a function $g(t)$ still free. Then $\partial^2 f/\partial x^2 = -\frac{m^2v^2}{\hbar^2}f$, and the second condition becomes

<Formula label="The equation for the time factor" tex="i\hbar\frac{dg}{dt} + mv^2 g = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 g \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dg}{dt} = \frac{i\,mv^2}{2\hbar}\,g \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g(t) = e^{i mv^2 t/(2\hbar)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="The Galilean phase factor" tex="f(x, t) = \exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(mvx - \frac{1}{2}mv^2t\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="Check the result">

The exponent is $1/\hbar$ times a momentum times a length minus an energy times a time, so it has no units. $f$ contains only $x$, $t$, $\hbar$, $m$ and $v$, as required. ## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Current conservation in three dimensions" points={10} mit="Problem 4">

The lesson derived the probability current $J(x, t)$ in one dimension: starting from $\rho(x, t) = |\Psi(x, t)|^2$ and the one-dimensional Schrödinger equation, it arrived at

<Formula label="Conservation of probability in one dimension" tex="\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial J}{\partial x} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2" />

Repeat the steps in three dimensions. Start from $\rho(\vec x, t) = |\Psi(\vec x, t)|^2$, use the three-dimensional Schrödinger equation, and derive the form of the current $\vec J(\vec x, t)$ that belongs in

<Formula label="Conservation of probability in three dimensions" tex="\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2" />

This is a derivation, so there is no answer box: compare your steps with the solution and mark yourself.

<Hint>

Differentiate $\rho = \Psi^*\Psi$ with respect to time. Replace $\partial\Psi/\partial t$ with the Schrödinger equation and $\partial\Psi^*/\partial t$ with its complex conjugate. The potential is real.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The potential terms cancel. What is left contains $\Psi^*\nabla^2\Psi - \Psi\nabla^2\Psi^*$. Try to write it as the divergence of a vector: work out $\nabla\cdot(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi)$ with the product rule.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The time derivative of $\rho$ turns, with the Schrödinger equation, into a divergence. The vector under the divergence is minus the current.

<Step title="The equation and its conjugate">

The three-dimensional equation of the lesson (Lecture 5 notes, eq. (2.28)) and its complex conjugate, for a real potential $V$:

<Formula label="Time derivatives of the wavefunction and its conjugate" tex="\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi - \frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi, \qquad \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t} = -\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla^2\Psi^* + \frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi^*" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 8, eq. (2.28), solved for the time derivative" />

</Step>

<Step title="The time derivative of the density">

<Formula label="Rate of change of the density" tex="\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t}\Psi + \Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi^*\nabla^2\Psi - \Psi\nabla^2\Psi^*\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The two terms with $V$ are $+\frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi^*\Psi$ and $-\frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi^*\Psi$. They cancel because $V$ is real.

</Step>

<Step title="Write it as a divergence">

The product rule gives $\nabla\cdot(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi) = \nabla\Psi^*\cdot\nabla\Psi + \Psi^*\nabla^2\Psi$, and the same with $\Psi$ and $\Psi^*$ exchanged. In the difference the first terms cancel:

<Formula label="A total divergence" tex="\Psi^*\nabla^2\Psi - \Psi\nabla^2\Psi^* = \nabla\cdot\left(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi - \Psi\nabla\Psi^*\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Read off the current">

So $\partial\rho/\partial t = -\nabla\cdot\vec J$ with

<Formula label="Probability current in three dimensions" tex="\vec J(\vec x, t) = \frac{\hbar}{2im}\left(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi - \Psi\nabla\Psi^*\right) = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi\right)" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 7, eq. (4.1)" />

The sign works out because $\frac{i\hbar}{2m} = -\frac{\hbar}{2im}$. The last form uses $z - z^* = 2i\,\text{Im}\,z$ for $z = \Psi^*\nabla\Psi$. It is the result the lesson quoted without derivation.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For motion along $x$ only, $\nabla \to \partial/\partial x$ and $\vec J$ becomes the one-dimensional current of the lesson. For a plane wave $\Psi = A\,e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}$, $\nabla\Psi = i\vec k\,\Psi$ and $\vec J = |A|^2\,\hbar\vec k/m$: the density times the velocity $\vec p/m$, as a current of particles should be. Units: $|\Psi|^2$ is a probability per volume, $\nabla$ adds 1/length and $\hbar/m$ is a length squared per time, so $\vec J$ is a probability per area per time. A current is fixed by the conservation law only up to a vector with zero divergence. This is the natural choice, and the one of the lesson.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Time evolution of the overlap between two states" points={10} mit="Problem 5">

MIT notes that this problem comes from Merzbacher's textbook. At $t = 0$ a wavefunction is the sum of two narrow wave packets that are far apart:

<Formula label="Two packets" tex="\Psi(x, 0) = \Psi_1(x, 0) + \Psi_2(x, 0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3" />

Each packet can be normalized by itself. Both evolve with the same Schrödinger equation. The overlap integral of the two packets is defined as

<Formula label="Overlap integral" tex="\gamma(t) \equiv \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Psi_1^*(x, t)\,\Psi_2(x, t)\,dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3" />

At $t = 0$ the packets hardly touch, so $|\gamma(0)|$ is very small. As time goes on the packets move and spread. What happens to $|\gamma(t)|$? Does it grow when the packets come to overlap?

Choose an answer, and support it with a calculation of $d\gamma/dt$.

<Hint>

Differentiate $\gamma(t)$ under the integral, as the lesson did for the total probability $N(t)$. Each packet obeys $i\hbar\,\partial\Psi/\partial t = \hat H\Psi$ by itself, because the equation is linear.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You will find two integrals, one with $\hat H$ acting on $\Psi_1$ and one with $\hat H$ acting on $\Psi_2$. Which property of $\hat H$ relates them?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-overlap" choices={["It does not change at all: it stays as small as it was at t = 0", "It grows as the packets spread into each other, and stays large once they overlap", "It grows while the packets overlap and falls back when they have passed through each other", "It falls to exactly zero, because spreading packets become flatter and flatter"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The overlap integral is constant in time, for the same reason that the normalization is: the Hamiltonian is Hermitian.

<Step title="Differentiate the overlap">

With the lesson's forms of the Schrödinger equation and its conjugate (Lecture 6 notes, eqs. (2.8)–(2.9)), $\partial\Psi_2/\partial t = -\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat H\Psi_2$ and $\partial\Psi_1^*/\partial t = \frac{i}{\hbar}(\hat H\Psi_1)^*$:

<Formula label="Rate of change of the overlap" tex="\frac{d\gamma}{dt} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\partial\Psi_1^*}{\partial t}\Psi_2 + \Psi_1^*\frac{\partial\Psi_2}{\partial t}\right)dx = \frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\hat H\Psi_1\right)^*\Psi_2\,dx - \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi_1^*\left(\hat H\Psi_2\right)dx\right)" cite="reading3" at="notes pp. 3–4, eqs. (2.8)–(2.10), with two different wavefunctions" />

</Step>

<Step title="Use that the Hamiltonian is Hermitian">

The definition of a Hermitian operator (Lecture 6 notes, eq. (2.12)) is that these two integrals are equal for any two wavefunctions that vanish far away:

<Formula label="Hermitian Hamiltonian" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\hat H\Psi_1\right)^*\Psi_2\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi_1^*\left(\hat H\Psi_2\right)dx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d\gamma}{dt} = 0" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.12)" />

So $\gamma(t) = \gamma(0)$ at all times, and $|\gamma(t)|$ stays as small as it was at the start. It does not grow.

</Step>

<Step title="How can that be?">

When the packets have spread into each other, the product $|\Psi_1||\Psi_2|$ is no longer small. But $\gamma$ is the integral of $\Psi_1^*\Psi_2$, a complex function. Packets that started at different places arrive with phases that differ from point to point, so $\Psi_1^*\Psi_2$ turns around in the complex plane as $x$ changes, and its contributions cancel almost completely. The overlap of the two regions grows. The overlap integral does not.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $\Psi_2 = \Psi_1$: then $\gamma$ is the total probability $N$ of the lesson and the result is $dN/dt = 0$, which the lesson proved. A second check: the sum $\Psi = \Psi_1 + \Psi_2$ has $\int|\Psi|^2dx = N_1 + N_2 + 2\,\text{Re}\,\gamma$. The three totals $\int|\Psi|^2dx$, $N_1$ and $N_2$ are each conserved, so $\text{Re}\,\gamma$ must be constant. The same argument for $\Psi_1 + i\Psi_2$ shows that $\text{Im}\,\gamma$ is constant too.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each wave carries its own current, $\pm\frac{\hbar k}{m}$ times its density. The cross terms between the two waves do not contribute.

<Step title="The product">

$\partial\Psi/\partial x = ik\left(A\,e^{ikx} - B\,e^{-ikx}\right)$, so

<Formula label="The product for two opposite waves" tex="\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} = ik\left(|A|^2 - |B|^2\right) + ik\left(A B^* e^{2ikx} - A^* B\,e^{-2ikx}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The cross terms">

The second bracket is $z - z^*$ with $z = AB^*e^{2ikx}$, which is $2i\,\text{Im}\,z$. Multiplied by $ik$ it is $-2k\,\text{Im}\,z$: a real number. It has no imaginary part.

</Step>

<Step title="The current">

<Formula label="Current of two opposite waves" tex="J = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right) = \frac{\hbar k}{m}\left(|A|^2 - |B|^2\right)" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 5, eq. (3.6)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$J$ does not depend on $x$, although the density $|\Psi|^2$ has ripples from the cross terms. That fits the conservation law: both waves have the same energy and so the same frequency, the density then does not change in time, and $\partial J/\partial x = 0$. For $B = 0$ the current is $|A|^2\hbar k/m$, density times velocity. For $|B| = |A|$ it is zero: a standing wave carries nothing. With $|A| = 2$ and $|B| = 1$ the current is $3\hbar k/m$, three quarters of what the right-moving wave carries by itself.

</Step>
