Light is a wave, yet it also comes in particles; matter is made of particles, yet it also behaves as waves. This lecture gathers the evidence for both halves of that statement: the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, and de Broglie's matter waves (notes pp. 1–7). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 3; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## The photoelectric effect

Heinrich Hertz observed in 1887 that polished metal plates can emit electrons when light shines on them, producing a current (notes p. 1). Three facts stood out:

- There is a **threshold frequency** $\nu_0$: below it no current flows, however bright the light. Its value depends on the metal and on the state of its surface.
- The size of the current is proportional to the intensity of the light.
- The energy of the emitted electrons does not depend on the intensity at all.

Einstein explained all three in 1905 by proposing that the energy of light comes in quanta, later called photons, each carrying $h\nu$, with $h$ the constant Planck had introduced to fit the black-body spectrum (notes p. 1). Electrons are bound in the metal: the minimum energy needed to remove one is the **work function** $W$, hard to calculate (it comes from many electrons interacting with the atoms) but easy to measure. If one photon gives an electron more than $W$, the electron leaves with the difference (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Einstein's photoelectric equation" tex="E_{e^-} = \frac12mv^2 = h\nu - W" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.1)" />

<Formula label="Threshold frequency" tex="h\nu_0 = W" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.2)" />

Below $\nu_0$ no single photon has enough energy. Brighter light delivers more photons per second, so more electrons and a larger current, but each electron still gets one photon's worth of energy (notes p. 2). Einstein's equation predicts that the electrons' energy grows linearly with the frequency of the light. Millikan confirmed this in 1915 with careful measurements, and from the slope obtained Planck's constant to better than 1%; even so, many physicists were not yet convinced that light quanta were particles (notes p. 2).

For calculations without looking up constants, the notes recommend remembering

<Formula label="A useful combination" tex="\hbar c = 197.33\ \text{eV}\,\text{nm}, \qquad \hbar = \frac{h}{2\pi}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.3)" />

The notes write it as 197.33 MeV fm, with 1 MeV $= 10^6$ eV and 1 fm (femtometre) $= 10^{-15}$ m; the two forms are equal, since $10^6 \times 10^{-15} = 10^{-9}$ (notes p. 2). The notes' worked example sends ultraviolet light onto a metal and finds the electrons' kinetic energy and speed; the worked example below does the same with different numbers.

**The units of $h$.** From $E = h\nu$, $h$ has units of energy times time, which is the same as length times momentum: the units of angular momentum, $\vec r \times \vec p$ (notes pp. 2–3). Indeed the spin angular momentum of a spin one-half particle is $\hbar/2$. This gives every particle of mass $m$ a natural length, the **Compton wavelength**:

<Formula label="Compton wavelength" tex="\lambda_C = \frac{h}{mc}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.10)" />

It does not depend on how fast the particle moves, and it must not be confused with the de Broglie wavelength below, which uses the particle's actual momentum rather than $mc$ (notes p. 3). Its meaning: $\lambda_C$ is the wavelength of a photon whose energy equals the particle's rest energy, since $mc^2 = hc/\lambda$ gives $\lambda = h/(mc)$. To locate a particle with light you need a wavelength smaller than the accuracy you want; below $\lambda_C$ the photons carry enough energy to create new particles of mass $m$, and locating "the" particle stops making sense. The Compton wavelength marks the scale where relativistic quantum field theory is needed (notes p. 3). For the electron (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="The electron's Compton wavelength" tex="\lambda_C(e) = \frac{2\pi\,\hbar c}{m_ec^2} = \frac{2\pi \times 197.33\ \text{eV}\,\text{nm}}{511{,}000\ \text{eV}} = 0.002426\ \text{nm}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.12)" />

that is 2.426 picometres (pm), about 20 times smaller than the Bohr radius (53 pm) and about two thousand times the size of a proton (about 1 fm).

## Compton scattering

In 1916 Einstein proposed that a light quantum carries momentum as well as energy, which made it much more clearly a particle (notes p. 3). In relativity, a particle's energy, momentum and rest mass are related by

<Formula label="Relativistic energy and momentum" tex="E^2 - p^2c^2 = m^2c^4" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (2.13)" />

with $E = mc^2/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ and $p = mv/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ (notes p. 4, eq. (2.14)). A particle moving at the speed of light must have zero rest mass, or both would be infinite. Setting $m = 0$ gives $E = pc$ for the photon, and with $\nu = c/\lambda$ (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Photon momentum" tex="p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{h\nu}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eqs. (2.15)–(2.16)" />

**The classical expectation: Thomson scattering** (notes p. 4). An electromagnetic wave hitting a free electron shakes it at the wave's frequency, and the oscillating electron radiates at that same frequency. The scattered power per unit solid angle is described by the cross-section (in Gaussian units)

<Formula label="Thomson cross-section" tex="\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \left(\frac{e^2}{mc^2}\right)^2\frac12\left(1 + \cos^2\theta\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.17)" />

where $\theta$ is the angle between the incoming and scattered waves. A cross-section is an area: the area of the incoming wave that carries as much energy as the electron scatters. The length $e^2/(mc^2)$, the **classical electron radius**, is about 2.8 fm, not much bigger than a proton (notes p. 4). Classically, the frequency never changes.

**Photons instead** (notes pp. 4–5). Treated as photons, the process is a collision between a photon and an electron that is roughly at rest. Energy and momentum conservation show that the electron cannot simply absorb the photon (an exercise in the notes), and that the photon must lose energy, since the electron recoils: the final wavelength $\lambda_f$ is longer than the initial $\lambda_i$. Compton's experiments of 1923–1924, scattering X-rays (photon energies roughly 100 eV to 100 keV) off a carbon target, found exactly this, against the classical prediction. The calculation gives

<Formula label="Compton's formula" tex="\lambda_f = \lambda_i + \frac{h}{m_ec}\left(1 - \cos\theta\right) = \lambda_i + \lambda_C\left(1 - \cos\theta\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.18)" />

with the Compton wavelength of the electron, the particle the photon scatters off. The shift is largest at $\theta = 180°$, where it is $2\lambda_C$, and at $\theta = 90°$ it is exactly $\lambda_C$ (notes p. 5, eqs. (2.19)–(2.20)).

<Plot variable="th" domain={[0, 180]} range={[0, 6]} xLabel="scattering angle θ (degrees)" yLabel="shift (pm)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "2.426*(1 - cos(th*pi/180))", label: "λ_f − λ_i" }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 90, y: 2.426, label: "λ_C at 90°" }]}
  caption="Computed from Compton's formula with the electron's Compton wavelength, 2.426 pm: the shift grows from zero in the forward direction to 2λ_C ≈ 4.85 pm for a photon sent straight back." />

Compton used X-rays from molybdenum, with $E \approx 17.5$ keV and $\lambda_i = 0.0709$ nm, and placed the detector at $90°$ (notes p. 5, eq. (2.21)). The spectrum showed a peak at $\lambda_f = 0.0731$ nm, a shift of about 2.2 pm, close to the Compton wavelength of 2.4 pm. Since the photons carry about 17 keV and carbon's electrons are bound by only about 300 eV, treating them as free electrons is a fine approximation when the atom is ionised (notes pp. 5–6). But there was a second peak at the original wavelength. It comes from collisions in which the electron receives some momentum yet stays bound (not unlikely, since a bound electron's typical momentum is comparable to the photon's): then the whole atom recoils, and the Compton wavelength that matters is the atom's, about 20,000 times smaller than the electron's (a carbon atom has about $12 \times 1823 \approx 21{,}900$ electron masses; the notes say several thousand), giving no visible shift (notes p. 6).

## Matter waves

Light is both particle and wave: a photon has energy $E$ and momentum $p$, particle properties, and also a frequency $\nu$ and wavelength $\lambda$, wave properties, linked by $E = h\nu$ and $\lambda = h/p$ (notes p. 6). In 1924 Louis de Broglie proposed that this duality is universal: a particle of matter with momentum $p$ also has an associated plane wave, of wavelength

<Formula label="De Broglie wavelength" tex="\lambda = \frac{h}{p}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (3.23)" />

This is a purely quantum property: if $h$ went to zero, so would $\lambda$, and particles would show no wave behaviour (notes p. 6). The striking consequence is that matter can diffract and interfere. In the Davisson–Germer experiment (1927), electrons scattered off a metal came out strongly at certain angles, where waves reflected by the regular lattice of atoms interfere constructively. Two-slit interference has been seen with electrons sent one at a time, and in 2013 Eibenberger and collaborators reported interference of molecules of 810 atoms, with masses above 10,000 atomic mass units (notes p. 6).

**When do waves matter?** A particle of momentum $p$ meeting an object of size $\Delta x$ behaves classically when its wavelength is much smaller than the object, $\lambda/\Delta x \ll 1$ (notes p. 6). With $\lambda = h/p$ this reads

<Formula label="When wave effects are negligible" tex="\Delta x\,p \gg h" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (3.25)" />

and both sides have units of angular momentum (notes pp. 6–7). The classical limit is subtle, though: a classical electromagnetic wave needs a large number of photons, but a state with an exactly fixed number of photons is never classical, however large that number. Classical waves correspond to *coherent states*, in which the number of photons fluctuates (notes p. 7).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Scattering photons and the opaque early universe">
For its first few hundred thousand years the universe was filled with free electrons, and photons scattered off them again and again, Thomson scattering, the low-energy limit of Compton scattering in which the wavelength barely changes. Light could not travel far, so the universe was opaque. Only when the electrons joined nuclei to form neutral atoms did the photons fly free; they are the cosmic microwave background seen today. [8.286 follows the temperature down to decoupling](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling).
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Photons and electrons">
<Problem>
(a) Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on a metal with work function $W = 2.30$ eV. Find the photon energy, the electrons' kinetic energy and their speed. (Use $hc = 2\pi\hbar c \approx 1239.8$ eV nm and $m_ec^2 = 511{,}000$ eV.) (b) X-rays of wavelength 0.0500 nm scatter off electrons at $60°$. What is the scattered wavelength? (c) Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron with kinetic energy 100 eV and of a 0.145 kg ball at 40 m/s.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Photoelectrons">
The photon energy is $hc/\lambda = 1239.8/400 \approx 3.10$ eV, so the electrons leave with $3.10 - 2.30 = 0.80$ eV. Then $\frac12 m_ev^2 = 0.80$ eV gives $(v/c)^2 = 2 \times 0.80/511{,}000$, so $v/c \approx 1.77 \times 10^{-3}$ and $v \approx 530$ km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Compton shift">
$\lambda_C(1 - \cos 60°) = 2.426 \times 0.5 \approx 1.21$ pm $= 0.00121$ nm, so $\lambda_f \approx 0.0512$ nm.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Two de Broglie wavelengths">
For the electron, nonrelativistically $pc = \sqrt{2m_ec^2 K} = \sqrt{2 \times 511{,}000 \times 100} \approx 10{,}110$ eV, so $\lambda = hc/(pc) = 1239.8/10{,}110 \approx 0.123$ nm, comparable to the spacing of atoms in a crystal: electrons like these diffract. For the ball, $p = 0.145 \times 40 = 5.8$ kg m/s and $\lambda = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}/5.8 \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-34}$ m, absurdly small: no wave effects.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Photoelectric effect: $E_{e^-} = h\nu - W$; light's energy comes in quanta $h\nu$, so the electrons' energy depends on the frequency and the current on the intensity.
- $h$ has units of angular momentum; $\lambda_C = h/(mc)$ is a particle's Compton wavelength, 2.426 pm for the electron.
- Photons carry momentum $p = h/\lambda$; scattered off electrons, their wavelength grows by $\lambda_C(1 - \cos\theta)$, as Compton found.
- De Broglie: every particle has a wavelength $\lambda = h/p$; wave effects are negligible when $\Delta x\,p \gg h$.
