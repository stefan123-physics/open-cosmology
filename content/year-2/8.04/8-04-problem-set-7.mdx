## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `sqrt(hbar/(2*m*omega))*cos(omega*t)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer in the symbols it names. Write those symbols exactly as the box names them: `hbar` stands for $\hbar$, `omega` for $\omega$, `xi` for $\xi$. A box treats its symbols as independent of each other, so use only the symbols the problem asks for.

MIT gives the points per problem. The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show, sketch or explain something have no box, or only a choice box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons. A bound state of energy $E < 0$ decays as $e^{-\kappa|x|}$ with $\kappa = \sqrt{-2mE}/\hbar$. For the harmonic oscillator of frequency $\omega$, the energy eigenstates are $\psi_n$ with $E_n = \hbar\omega\left(n + \frac12\right)$, the ladder operators are $\hat a$ and $\hat a^\dagger$, and the oscillator length is $a = \sqrt{\hbar/(m\omega)}$, with the unit-free position $u = x/a$. Take care: in Problem 1 the letter $a$ is MIT's name for half the distance between the two wells, and from Problem 3 on it is the oscillator length.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Two delta functions" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

A particle of mass $m$ moves in one dimension in a double well made of two attractive delta functions, one at $x = a$ and one at $x = -a$:

<Formula label="The double delta-function well" tex="V(x) = -g\,\delta(x - a) - g\,\delta(x + a), \qquad g > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Find the transcendental equations that fix the energies of the bound states. Plot the energy levels, in units of $\hbar^2/(ma^2)$, against the unit-free strength

<Formula label="Unit-free strength of the wells" tex="\lambda \equiv \frac{m a g}{\hbar^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1" />

and explain the features of the plot.

The boxes are our additions. Write a bound-state energy as $E = -\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m)$ and put $\xi \equiv \kappa a$, so that $E = -\frac12\,\xi^2$ in units of $\hbar^2/(ma^2)$. Each condition can then be solved for $\lambda$: type the function of $\xi$ that $\lambda$ must equal, first for the even states and then for the odd states. Then give the smallest $\lambda$ above which there are two bound states, and the two energies at $\lambda = 1$, in units of $\hbar^2/(ma^2)$ (solve the conditions with any tool, or read them from your plot: these two boxes accept values within 2%).

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e-odd" label="$\lambda = 1$, odd state: $E\,/\,(\hbar^2/ma^2) =$" value="-0.317" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-e-odd" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Between and outside the delta functions the particle is free with negative energy, so the wavefunction is made of growing and decaying exponentials. Each delta function puts a kink in it. Evenness or oddness fixes the shape, and the size of the kink fixes the energy.

<Step title="The shape of the states">

With $E = -\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m)$ the equation away from $x = \pm a$ is $\psi'' = \kappa^2\psi$. Outside the wells the state must decay. Between them the even combination of the two exponentials is $\cosh\kappa x$ and the odd one is $\sinh\kappa x$. Making $\psi$ continuous at $x = \pm a$:

<Formula label="Even and odd bound states of the double well" tex="\psi_\text{even}(x) = \begin{cases} A\cosh\kappa x, & |x| < a \\ A\cosh\kappa a\;e^{-\kappa(|x| - a)}, & |x| > a \end{cases} \qquad \psi_\text{odd}(x) = \begin{cases} A\sinh\kappa x, & |x| < a \\ \pm A\sinh\kappa a\;e^{-\kappa(|x| - a)}, & |x| > a \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

(the lower sign is for $x < -a$).

</Step>

<Step title="The kink at a delta function">

Integrating the Schrödinger equation across $x = a$, as the lesson does at the origin, gives the jump in the slope:

<Formula label="Jump in the slope at x = a" tex="\psi'(a^+) - \psi'(a^-) = -\frac{2mg}{\hbar^2}\,\psi(a)" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 3, eqs. (1.13)–(1.14), with the delta function moved to x = a and α called g" />

For the even state the slope just outside is $-\kappa A\cosh\kappa a$ and the slope just inside is $\kappa A\sinh\kappa a$. For the odd state $\cosh$ and $\sinh$ change places. Dividing by $A\cosh\kappa a$ or $A\sinh\kappa a$:

<Formula label="Conditions for the even and the odd state" tex="\text{even:}\quad \kappa\left(1 + \tanh\kappa a\right) = \frac{2mg}{\hbar^2}, \qquad \text{odd:}\quad \kappa\left(1 + \coth\kappa a\right) = \frac{2mg}{\hbar^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The transcendental equations">

Multiply by $a$ and use $\xi = \kappa a$ and $\lambda = mag/\hbar^2$:

<Formula label="Transcendental equations for the double delta well" tex="\text{even:}\quad \xi\left(1 + \tanh\xi\right) = 2\lambda, \qquad \text{odd:}\quad \xi\left(1 + \coth\xi\right) = 2\lambda, \qquad \frac{E}{\hbar^2/(ma^2)} = -\frac{\xi^2}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Since $1 + \tanh\xi = 2/(1 + e^{-2\xi})$ and $1 + \coth\xi = 2/(1 - e^{-2\xi})$, the same two conditions can be written as

<Formula label="The same conditions with exponentials" tex="\text{even:}\quad \xi = \lambda\left(1 + e^{-2\xi}\right), \qquad \text{odd:}\quad \xi = \lambda\left(1 - e^{-2\xi}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The plot">

The curves below are drawn from these equations: for each $\xi$ the point $\left(\lambda(\xi), -\xi^2/2\right)$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 3]} range={[-5, 0.4]} xLabel="λ" yLabel="E in units of ħ²/(ma²)"
  curves={[
    { x: "s/(1 + exp(-2*s))", y: "-s^2/2", range: [0, 3.2], label: "even" },
    { x: "s/(1 - exp(-2*s))", y: "-s^2/2", range: [0.001, 3.2], label: "odd" },
    { expr: "-x^2/2", label: "one well", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="The bound-state energies of the double delta well against the strength λ, computed from the two transcendental equations (solid), and the energy −λ²/2 of a single well of strength g (dashed). The even state exists for every λ and lies below the dashed curve. The odd state starts at E = 0 when λ = 1/2 and lies above the dashed curve. For large λ both solid curves close in on the dashed one." />

</Step>

<Step title="The features of the plot">

- **Always one even state, never more than two states.** The function $\xi(1 + \tanh\xi)$ rises steadily from 0, so it equals $2\lambda$ exactly once for every $\lambda > 0$. The function $\xi(1 + \coth\xi)$ rises steadily from the value 1 (because $\xi\coth\xi \to 1$ as $\xi \to 0$), so it equals $2\lambda$ once if $2\lambda > 1$ and never otherwise. The odd state exists only for $\lambda > \frac12$, and it enters at $E = 0$.
- **Weak or close wells, $\lambda \ll 1$.** Then $\xi$ is small, $\tanh\xi \approx 0$, and $\xi \approx 2\lambda$: $E \approx -2\lambda^2\,\hbar^2/(ma^2) = -m(2g)^2/(2\hbar^2)$. This is the energy of a single delta well of strength $2g$. A state much wider than $2a$ cannot tell the two wells apart.
- **Strong or distant wells, $\lambda \gg 1$.** Then $e^{-2\xi}$ is tiny and both equations give $\xi \approx \lambda$: $E \approx -\frac12\lambda^2\,\hbar^2/(ma^2) = -mg^2/(2\hbar^2)$, the energy of one delta well of strength $g$. The particle sits in one well or the other, and the two levels are almost degenerate.
- **The even state is always the lower one,** as the node theorem demands: it has no node, and the odd state has one, at $x = 0$.

At $\lambda = 1$ the equations give $\xi = 1.109$ (even) and $\xi = 0.797$ (odd), so the energies are $-0.615$ and $-0.317$ in units of $\hbar^2/(ma^2)$, on either side of the single-well value $-0.5$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $g\,\delta(x)$ is an energy and $\delta(x)$ is one over a length, so $g$ is an energy times a length. Then $mag/\hbar^2$ has units $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}\cdot\text{J m}/(\text{J s})^2 = \text{kg m}^2/(\text{J s}^2) = 1$: a pure number. A second route to the even condition does not use the slopes: integrate $\psi'' - \kappa^2\psi = -(2mg/\hbar^2)\left[\delta(x - a) + \delta(x + a)\right]\psi$ over all $x$. The integral of $\psi''$ vanishes for a bound state, which leaves $\kappa^2\int\psi\,dx = (4mg/\hbar^2)\,\psi(a)$. With $\int\psi_\text{even}\,dx = 2Ae^{\kappa a}/\kappa$ this is $\xi e^{\xi} = 2\lambda\cosh\xi$, the even condition again.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Now let the wells be far apart: the separation $2a$ is large, which means $\lambda \gg 1$. Find a simple formula for the splitting $\Delta E = E_\text{odd} - E_\text{even}$ between the ground state and the first excited state. Give it in terms of $m$, $g$, $a$ and $\hbar$.

<Hint>

Use the forms of the two conditions with $e^{-2\xi}$. For large $\lambda$ the exponential is a small correction, so inside it you may replace $\xi$ by its leading value.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$E_\text{odd} - E_\text{even}$ is proportional to $\xi_\text{even}^2 - \xi_\text{odd}^2 = \left(\xi_\text{even} - \xi_\text{odd}\right)\left(\xi_\text{even} + \xi_\text{odd}\right)$.

</Hint>

    { expr: "m*g^2/hbar^2*exp(-2*m*a*g/hbar^2)", hint: "That is half the splitting: the distance of one level from the single-well energy. The even level moves down and the odd level moves up by the same amount." },

<Solution>

**The idea.** For distant wells each level is the single-well level plus a small correction from the tail of the wavefunction that reaches the other well. The correction has opposite signs for the even and the odd state.

<Step title="The two roots">

To leading order $\xi = \lambda$ in both conditions. Putting that into the small exponential:

<Formula label="The two roots for distant wells" tex="\xi_\text{even} \approx \lambda\left(1 + e^{-2\lambda}\right), \qquad \xi_\text{odd} \approx \lambda\left(1 - e^{-2\lambda}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The splitting">

<Formula label="Splitting of the two levels for distant wells" tex="\Delta E = \frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\left(\xi_\text{even}^2 - \xi_\text{odd}^2\right) \approx \frac{\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\times 4\lambda^2e^{-2\lambda} = \frac{2mg^2}{\hbar^2}\,\exp\left(-\frac{2mag}{\hbar^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

In words: $\Delta E = 4\left|E_1\right|e^{-2\kappa_1a}$, where $E_1 = -mg^2/(2\hbar^2)$ and $\kappa_1 = mg/\hbar^2$ belong to a single well. The factor $e^{-2\kappa_1a}$ is the size of one well's wavefunction at the position of the other well, a distance $2a$ away.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $mg^2/\hbar^2$ is $\text{kg}\,(\text{J m})^2/(\text{J s})^2 = \text{kg m}^2/\text{s}^2 = \text{J}$, an energy, and the exponent is the pure number $2\lambda$. Numbers, in units of $\hbar^2/(ma^2)$: at $\lambda = 2$ the exact roots give a splitting of $0.1475$ and the formula gives $0.1465$. At $\lambda = 3$ they give $0.04463$ and $0.04462$. The formula improves quickly as the wells move apart, and the levels become degenerate in the limit: a particle put in one well then stays there for a very long time.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Sketching wavefunctions (our own problem)" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, with the advice to work out by intuition what the solutions look like, and then to test that intuition with the shooting method and with the picture of the hydrogen molecule ion $\text{H}_2^+$ (one electron shared by two protons). The problem below is our own, on the same subject.

A particle of mass $m$ lives in a box with hard walls that is divided by a barrier. The barrier has height $V_0$ and width $d$, and on each side of it there is a well of width $w$:

<Formula label="A box divided by a barrier" tex="V(x) = \begin{cases} V_0, & |x| < \frac{d}{2} \\ 0, & \frac{d}{2} < |x| < \frac{d}{2} + w \\ \infty, & |x| > \frac{d}{2} + w \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

Think of each well as an atom and of $d$ as the distance between two atoms. In parts (a), (b), (d) and (e) the two lowest energies $E_1 < E_2$ are below the top of the barrier, $E_2 < V_0$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Sketch the ground state $\psi_1(x)$. Then choose the description of what it does inside the barrier, $|x| < d/2$.

<Hint>

The lesson's rule: where $E > V$ the wavefunction curves towards the axis, and where $E < V$ it curves away from it. How many nodes may a ground state have? And is it even or odd?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ground" label="Inside the barrier, the ground state" choices={["oscillates like a cosine, with a shorter wavelength than in the wells", "passes through zero at the centre and changes sign there", "curves away from the axis and has its lowest point at the centre, without reaching zero", "is exactly zero, because the particle cannot be there"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A ground state has no node, and in an even potential it is even. The sign of $E - V$ decides which way it curves.

<Step title="The sketch">

In each well $E_1 > V = 0$: the wavefunction curves towards the axis, as a piece of a sine wave that starts from zero at the hard wall. In the barrier $E_1 < V_0$: it curves away from the axis. It is even, so its slope at $x = 0$ is zero, and it has no node. Inside the barrier it is therefore a $\cosh$: positive, lowest at the centre, rising towards both wells. At $x = \pm d/2$ the potential makes a finite step, so $\psi$ and $\psi'$ are continuous there: the points $x = \pm d/2$ are turning points, where the curvature changes sign and the graph has an inflection.

<Formula label="Ground state of the divided box" tex="\psi_1(x) \propto \begin{cases} \cosh\kappa x, & |x| < \frac d2 \\ C\sin\left(k\left(\frac d2 + w - |x|\right)\right), & \frac d2 < |x| < \frac d2 + w \end{cases} \qquad k = \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}, \quad \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m\left(V_0 - E\right)}}{\hbar}" cite="reading1" at="notes p. 8, eq. (3.1), solved in each region (our own problem)" />

The graph under part (b) shows it for the numbers of part (e).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The wavefunction cannot vanish anywhere in the barrier. A zero would be a node, which a ground state does not have. At the centre there is a second reason: an even function has zero slope there, and with $\psi = 0$ and $\psi' = 0$ at one point the Schrödinger equation makes $\psi$ vanish everywhere (notes on the shape of wavefunctions, p. 9). For the same reason it cannot be identically zero inside the barrier.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Sketch the first excited state $\psi_2(x)$, and choose the description of what it does inside the barrier.

<Hint>

The node theorem gives the number of nodes. In an even potential, where must a single node be?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-excited" label="Inside the barrier, the first excited state" choices={["passes through zero at the centre, its only node, and curves away from the axis on both sides", "has two nodes, one near each edge of the barrier", "looks the same as the ground state, with a smaller value at the centre", "oscillates like a sine with one full wavelength inside the barrier"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The first excited state has exactly one node, and it is odd, so the node is at the centre.

<Step title="The sketch">

Inside the barrier the odd solution that curves away from the axis is $\sinh\kappa x$: zero at the centre, negative on one side and positive on the other. In the wells it is again a piece of a sine wave from each hard wall, but with opposite signs in the two wells. Outside the barrier its shape differs little from the ground state's.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-1.25, 1.25]} range={[-1.25, 1.25]} xLabel="x / w" yLabel="ψ (arbitrary units)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "abs(x) < 0.25 ? 0.2945*cosh(4.8344*x) : sin(2.5745*(1.25 - abs(x)))", label: "ground state" },

  ]}
  caption="The two lowest states of the divided box for the numbers of part (e): barrier of width d = w/2 (between x = −0.25w and 0.25w) and height 30 ħ²/(2mw²), computed from the formulas of the solutions. The ground state (solid) is even and dips to a positive minimum at the centre of the barrier. The first excited state (dashed) is odd and crosses zero there. In the wells both are arcs of a sine that vanish at the hard walls, x = ±1.25w." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The node theorem (lesson on the delta-function well) says the second bound state has one node. The hard walls are endpoints, not nodes. One node in an odd function sits at the origin, and $\sinh$ has no other zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Two limits can be done exactly. Find the ratio $E_2/E_1$ when the barrier is removed ($d \to 0$, with $w$ fixed), and when the barrier becomes infinitely high ($V_0 \to \infty$, with $d$ and $w$ fixed).

<Hint>

Without the barrier this is one box of width $2w$. With an infinitely high barrier it is two separate boxes of width $w$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ratio-open" label="$d \to 0$: $E_2/E_1 =$" value="4" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-ratio-open" />

<Answer id="p2-ratio-closed" label="$V_0 \to \infty$: $E_2/E_1 =$" value="1" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-ratio-closed" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In both limits the problem turns into the infinite square well, whose energies are $n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mL^2)$ for a well of width $L$.

<Step title="No barrier">

One well of width $2w$:

<Formula label="The two lowest energies without the barrier" tex="E_1 = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m\left(2w\right)^2}, \qquad E_2 = \frac{4\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m\left(2w\right)^2} = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mw^2}, \qquad \frac{E_2}{E_1} = 4" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Infinitely high barrier">

The wavefunction must vanish in the barrier, so each well is an infinite well of width $w$ on its own, with lowest energy $\pi^2\hbar^2/(2mw^2)$. The even and the odd combination of the two single-well ground states have the same energy:

<Formula label="The two lowest energies with an infinitely high barrier" tex="E_1 = E_2 = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mw^2}, \qquad \frac{E_2}{E_1} = 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The odd state has the same energy in both limits. That is as it should be: with no barrier it has a node at the centre, exactly where an infinitely thin, infinitely high barrier would force one. <Step title="The leading term">

<Formula label="The generating function as a product of two series" tex="e^{2s\xi}\,e^{-s^2} = \left(\sum_{j=0}^\infty\frac{(2\xi)^js^j}{j!}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^ks^{2k}}{k!}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The power $s^n$ comes from the pairs with $j + 2k = n$, and such a pair carries $\xi^j = \xi^{n - 2k}$. The highest power of $\xi$ is the one with $k = 0$ and $j = n$: the term $(2\xi)^ns^n/n!$. Comparing with $H_n\,s^n/n!$:

<Formula label="Leading term of the Hermite polynomial" tex="H_n(\xi) = (2\xi)^n + \left(\text{terms in } \xi^{n-2},\ \xi^{n-4},\ \ldots\right)" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 12, eq. (3.65), here derived from the generating function" />

So $H_n$ is a polynomial of degree $n$, with powers that go down in steps of two.

</Step>

<Step title="Three derivatives of the generating function">

With $G(s, \xi) = e^{-s^2 + 2s\xi}$:

<Formula label="Derivatives of the generating function" tex="\frac{\partial G}{\partial\xi} = 2s\,G, \qquad \frac{\partial^2G}{\partial\xi^2} = 4s^2\,G, \qquad \frac{\partial G}{\partial s} = \left(2\xi - 2s\right)G" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Therefore

<Formula label="A combination that vanishes" tex="\frac{\partial^2G}{\partial\xi^2} - 2\xi\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial\xi} + 2s\,\frac{\partial G}{\partial s} = \left(4s^2 - 4s\xi + 4s\xi - 4s^2\right)G = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Coefficient by coefficient">

Put the series for $G$ into this identity. The derivatives with respect to $\xi$ act on $H_n$, and $s\,\partial/\partial s$ multiplies $s^n$ by $n$:

<Formula label="The identity as a power series in s" tex="\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(H_n'' - 2\xi\,H_n' + 2n\,H_n\right)\frac{s^n}{n!} = 0 \quad \text{for all } s" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

A power series that vanishes for all $s$ has all its coefficients equal to zero, which is the Hermite equation for every $n$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Expanding the generating function by hand to order $s^3$ gives $1 + 2\xi s + \left(2\xi^2 - 1\right)s^2 + \left(\frac43\xi^3 - 2\xi\right)s^3$, so $H_0 = 1$, $H_1 = 2\xi$, $H_2 = 4\xi^2 - 2$ and $H_3 = 8\xi^3 - 12\xi$: the polynomials of the lesson (notes p. 12, eq. (3.67)). For example $H_2$ obeys the equation: $8 - 2\xi\times 8\xi + 4\left(4\xi^2 - 2\right) = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Harmonic oscillator and a wall (our own problem)" points={5} mit="Problem 5">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the same subject.

An atom is bound to a hard surface. It can move along the $x$ axis, at right angles to the surface, which is at $x = 0$. For $x > 0$ it feels the oscillator potential, and it cannot enter the surface:

<Formula label="An oscillator with a wall" tex="V(x) = \begin{cases} \frac12m\omega^2x^2, & x > 0 \\ \infty, & x < 0 \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the allowed energies. Number them with $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ from the ground state up, and give the energy of level $k$ in terms of $\hbar$, $\omega$ and $k$.

<Hint>

For $x > 0$ the Schrödinger equation is that of the full oscillator. What does the wall add to it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Which eigenstates $\psi_n$ of the full oscillator vanish at $x = 0$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-energy" label="Energy of level $k$:" expr="hbar*omega*(2*k + 3/2)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], omega: [0.5, 2], k: [0, 5] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "hbar*omega*(k + 1/2)", hint: "Those are the energies of the full oscillator. Its even states do not vanish at x = 0, so the wall rules them out." },
    { expr: "hbar*omega*(2*k + 1/2)", hint: "Those are the even states of the full oscillator, which are not zero at x = 0. The wall demands ψ(0) = 0: keep the odd ones." },
    { expr: "hbar*omega*(k + 3/2)", hint: "The lowest level is right, but the spacing is not. Only every second state of the full oscillator survives, so the levels are 2ħω apart." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the half line the equation is unchanged. The wall only adds the condition $\psi(0) = 0$, and the odd states of the full oscillator already satisfy it.

<Step title="The condition at the wall">

For $x > 0$ an energy eigenstate obeys the oscillator equation and must decay as $x \to \infty$. At the hard wall it must vanish, as at the walls of the infinite square well. Take such a state and continue it to $x < 0$ as an odd function, $\psi(-x) = -\psi(x)$. Because $\psi(0) = 0$, the continued function and its slope are continuous at the origin, and because the oscillator potential is even, it solves the oscillator equation on the whole line. It is normalizable, so it is one of the odd eigenstates $\psi_n$ of the full oscillator, with $n$ odd. The other way round, every $\psi_n$ with odd $n$ vanishes at $x = 0$, so its right half is a state of the oscillator with a wall. The states with even $n$ are of no use: they are even and do not vanish at the origin (an even solution with $\psi(0) = 0$ would also have $\psi'(0) = 0$, and then it would be zero everywhere).

</Step>

<Step title="The energies">

With $n = 2k + 1$:

<Formula label="Energies of the oscillator with a wall" tex="E_k = \hbar\omega\left(2k + 1 + \frac12\right) = \hbar\omega\left(2k + \frac32\right), \qquad k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 12, eq. (3.64), keeping the odd states (our own problem)" />

The ground state has energy $\frac32\hbar\omega$ and the levels are $2\hbar\omega$ apart.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The state of the full oscillator with $n = 2k + 1$ has $2k + 1$ nodes: one at the origin and $k$ on each side. On the half line that leaves $k$ nodes (the zero at the wall is an endpoint, not a node). So level $k$ has $k$ nodes, as the node theorem requires, and no level has been missed. The spacing fits the classical motion too: an atom that bounces off the wall completes a cycle in half the period of the full oscillator, so its frequency is $2\omega$, and the levels are spaced by $\hbar$ times that.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Write down the normalized ground state for $x > 0$, and find the expectation value $\langle\hat x\rangle$ in it: the average distance of the atom from the surface. Give it in terms of $\hbar$, $m$ and $\omega$.

<Hint>

The state is proportional to $\psi_1$ of the full oscillator, but it lives on half the line. How much of the norm of $\psi_1$ lies at $x > 0$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the unit-free position $u = x/a$ you need $\int_0^\infty u^3e^{-u^2}du$. Substitute $q = u^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-x" label="$\langle\hat x\rangle =$" expr="2*sqrt(hbar/(pi*m*omega))"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], omega: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(hbar/(pi*m*omega))", hint: "The state was not normalized on the half line. ψ₁ has only half of its norm at x > 0, so the wavefunction here is √2 ψ₁ and the density is twice as large." },
    { expr: "2*hbar/(pi*m*omega)", hint: "Check the units: ħ/(mω) is a length squared. The average position is 2/√π times the oscillator length √(ħ/mω)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The ground state is the first excited state of the full oscillator, cut in half and normalized again.

<Step title="The ground state">

$\psi_1$ is odd, so $|\psi_1|^2$ is even and half of its norm lies at $x > 0$. The normalized ground state of the oscillator with a wall is therefore $\sqrt2\,\psi_1$ for $x > 0$, and zero for $x < 0$. With $\psi_1 = \sqrt2\,u\,\psi_0$ and $u = x/a$:

<Formula label="Ground state of the oscillator with a wall" tex="\psi(x) = \sqrt2\,\psi_1(x) = \frac{2}{\pi^{1/4}\sqrt a}\,\frac{x}{a}\,e^{-x^2/(2a^2)} \quad (x > 0), \qquad a = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}}" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 3, eq. The density per unit of $u$ is $\frac{4}{\sqrt\pi}\,u^2e^{-u^2}$, and $\int_0^\infty u^3e^{-u^2}du = \frac12\int_0^\infty q\,e^{-q}\,dq = \frac12$:

<Formula label="Average distance from the wall in the ground state" tex="\langle\hat x\rangle = a\,\frac{4}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^\infty u^3e^{-u^2}du = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\,a = 2\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{\pi m\omega}} \approx 1.13\,a" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The norm: $\frac{4}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^\infty u^2e^{-u^2}du = \frac{4}{\sqrt\pi}\times\frac{\sqrt\pi}{4} = 1$. The average, $1.13\,a$, lies inside the classical range of this state, which ends at the turning point $\sqrt3\,a = 1.73\,a$ (Problem 3 with $n = 1$), and a little beyond the peak of the density at $x = a$. The wall pushes the atom outward: in the full oscillator the average position is zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Harmonic oscillator oscillating!" points={10} mit="Problem 6">

A particle of mass $m$ in a harmonic oscillator of frequency $\omega$ has, at time zero, the wavefunction

<Formula label="The state at time zero" tex="\Psi(x, 0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\psi_0(x) + \psi_1(x)\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 2" />

where $\psi_0$ and $\psi_1$ are the normalized eigenstates of the Hamiltonian with number eigenvalues zero and one. (MIT's sheet writes them with the letter phi.) Take them real, with the sign of the lesson: $\psi_1 = \hat a^\dagger\psi_0$, which is positive for $x > 0$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Write down $\Psi(x, t)$ and $|\Psi(x, t)|^2$. You may leave both in terms of $\psi_0$ and $\psi_1$.

<Hint>

Each energy eigenstate evolves with its own phase factor $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In $|\Psi|^2$ the two squared terms do not depend on time. The cross terms contain the two phase factors, one of them conjugated: only the difference of the two energies survives.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Stationary states only turn their phase, each at its own rate. The density of a sum of two of them feels the difference of the two rates.

<Step title="The wavefunction">

With $E_0 = \frac12\hbar\omega$ and $E_1 = \frac32\hbar\omega$:

<Formula label="The state at time t" tex="\Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\psi_0(x)\,e^{-i\omega t/2} + \psi_1(x)\,e^{-3i\omega t/2}\right) = \frac{e^{-i\omega t/2}}{\sqrt2}\left(\psi_0(x) + \psi_1(x)\,e^{-i\omega t}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The density">

The overall phase drops out of $|\Psi|^2$, and $e^{-i\omega t} + e^{i\omega t} = 2\cos\omega t$:

<Formula label="The probability density at time t" tex="\left|\Psi(x, t)\right|^2 = \frac12\left(\psi_0^2(x) + \psi_1^2(x)\right) + \psi_0(x)\,\psi_1(x)\cos\omega t" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ this is $\frac12\left(\psi_0 + \psi_1\right)^2$, the square of the starting state. Its integral over $x$ is $\frac12(1 + 1) + 0 = 1$ at all times, because $\psi_0$ and $\psi_1$ are orthonormal. The product $\psi_0\psi_1$ is odd in $x$, so the last term moves probability from one side to the other and back: at $t = 0$ the particle is more likely at $x > 0$, and half a period later at $x < 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find $\langle\hat x\rangle$ as a function of time, in terms of $\hbar$, $m$, $\omega$ and $t$. What is the amplitude of this oscillation, and what is its frequency?

<Hint>

Multiply the density of part (a) by $x$ and integrate. Two of the three terms are odd functions of $x$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

What is left is the integral of $\psi_0\,x\,\psi_1$. Write $\hat x$ with the ladder operators: $\hat x\,\psi_1$ holds a piece along $\psi_0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-x" label="$\langle\hat x\rangle(t) =$" expr="sqrt(hbar/(2*m*omega))*cos(omega*t)"

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only the interference term of the density is lopsided in $x$, and it swings at the frequency $\omega$.

<Step title="The integral">

$x\,\psi_0^2$ and $x\,\psi_1^2$ are odd functions, so they integrate to zero. That leaves

<Formula label="Expectation value of the position" tex="\langle\hat x\rangle = \cos\omega t\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_0\,x\,\psi_1\,dx = \cos\omega t\;\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\left(\psi_0, \left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)\psi_1\right)" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 2, eq. Since $\hat a\,\psi_1 = \psi_0$ and $\hat a^\dagger\psi_1 = \sqrt2\,\psi_2$, which is orthogonal to $\psi_0$, the inner product is 1.

</Step>

<Formula label="The position oscillates" tex="\langle\hat x\rangle(t) = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\,\cos\omega t" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The amplitude is $\sqrt{\hbar/(2m\omega)} = a/\sqrt2$, and the angular frequency is $\omega$: the frequency of the classical oscillator, and also $(E_1 - E_0)/\hbar$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$, a direct integration of $x\,|\Psi(x, 0)|^2$ with $\psi_1 = \sqrt2\,(x/a)\,\psi_0$ gives $\sqrt2/a$ times the ground state's $\langle\hat x^2\rangle = a^2/2$, which is $a/\sqrt2$, the same. The amplitude is smaller than the classical amplitudes $A_0 = a$ and $A_1 = \sqrt3\,a$ of the two states (Problem 3): the average position swings gently inside the well, while the density stays broad.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Formula label="Expectation value of the momentum" tex="\langle\hat p\rangle = \frac12\left[c_0^*c_1\left(-i\sqrt{\frac{m\omega\hbar}{2}}\right) + c_1^*c_0\left(i\sqrt{\frac{m\omega\hbar}{2}}\right)\right] = \frac{i}{2}\sqrt{\frac{m\omega\hbar}{2}}\left(e^{i\omega t} - e^{-i\omega t}\right) = -\sqrt{\frac{m\omega\hbar}{2}}\,\sin\omega t" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 2, eq. <Part label="d" points={2}>

Show that for any state of the harmonic oscillator, the probability density $|\Psi(x, t)|^2$ is equal to $|\Psi(x, t + T)|^2$ for $T = 2\pi/\omega$.

<Hint>

Expand the state in the energy eigenstates, $\Psi(x, 0) = \sum_nc_n\psi_n(x)$, and let each term evolve.

</Hint>

<Hint>

By what factor does the phase $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ of the state $\psi_n$ change when $t$ grows by $2\pi/\omega$? Does the factor depend on $n$?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** After one classical period every energy eigenstate has picked up the same phase factor, so the whole state has only changed by an overall factor.

<Step title="The expansion">

Any state can be written with the energy eigenstates, each evolving with its own phase:

<Formula label="A general oscillator state" tex="\Psi(x, t) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n\,\psi_n(x)\,e^{-i\left(n + \frac12\right)\omega t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="One period later">

Replacing $t$ by $t + 2\pi/\omega$ multiplies term $n$ by $e^{-i(2n + 1)\pi} = e^{-2\pi in}\,e^{-i\pi} = -1$, the same for every $n$:

<Formula label="The state after one period" tex="\Psi\left(x, t + \frac{2\pi}{\omega}\right) = -\Psi(x, t) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left|\Psi\left(x, t + \frac{2\pi}{\omega}\right)\right|^2 = \left|\Psi(x, t)\right|^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The state of this problem is an example: its density holds $\cos\omega t$, which repeats after $2\pi/\omega$, and its wavefunction of part (a) changes sign after that time. The proof rests on the even spacing of the levels: all energy differences are whole multiples of $\hbar\omega$. In a potential with unevenly spaced levels, such as the infinite square well with $E_n \propto n^2$, a general state does not repeat at the classical period.

</Step>
