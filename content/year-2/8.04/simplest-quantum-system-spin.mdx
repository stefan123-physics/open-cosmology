After a semester of wavefunctions on a line and in space, Zwiebach ends Lecture 24 by asking what the simplest possible quantum system would be, and finds that its mathematics is the spin of the electron. This page is the second half of the lecture; the first half is [hydrogen orbits and turning points](/courses/8-04/hydrogen-orbits-turning-points). (There are no lecture notes for this lecture on OCW; the page follows the transcripts of the three video segments, cited as reading1 to reading3, in its own words.)

## What is the simplest quantum system?

A particle in a box seems simple, but it has infinitely many states. A delta-function well has just one bound state, but infinitely many scattering states (reading1 p. 1). To simplify, list what quantum mechanics needs (reading1 p. 1). There is a Schrödinger equation, whose energy eigenstates are

<Formula label="Stationary states" tex="\Psi = e^{-iEt/\hbar}\,\psi, \qquad H\psi = E\psi" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 1, board" />

$H$ is an operator with units of energy that we are free to invent. There is an inner product $(\phi, \psi)$ that turns two states into a complex number, conjugating the first. And probability must be conserved, so $H$ must be **Hermitian**. The Hermitian conjugate $A^\dagger$ of any operator is defined through the inner product (reading1 p. 1):

<Formula label="Hermitian conjugate" tex="(\phi, A\psi) = (A^\dagger\phi, \psi), \qquad H^\dagger = H" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 1, board" />

That, Zwiebach says, is quantum mechanics: an equation, a Hamiltonian, an inner product and Hermitian operators. The course started from a particle on a line because classical mechanics suggested it, not because it is simplest.

## A particle at two points

Suppose the particle can be at only two points, $x_1$ and $x_2$ (reading1 p. 2). One point would be too simple: the particle is always there. With two, the wavefunction becomes two complex numbers,

<Formula label="Two-point wavefunction" tex="\psi(x) \;\to\; \begin{pmatrix}\alpha\\ \beta\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\psi(x_1)\\ \psi(x_2)\end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 2, board" />

with $|\alpha|^2$ the probability to be at $x_1$ and $|\beta|^2$ the probability to be at $x_2$ (reading1 p. 2). The two components need not be places. They could be the two arms of an interferometer, as in the [Mach–Zehnder interferometer](/courses/8-04/mach-zehnder-interferometer). They could be a molecule in the left or right half of a box with a partition. Or they could be a particle at rest that has something called spin, which can be up or down (reading1 p. 2).

The inner product of two functions, $\int\phi^*(x)\psi(x)\,dx$, multiplies the values at the same point after conjugating the first. For two-component vectors the same rule gives (reading1 p. 3)

<Formula label="Inner product of two-component states" tex="(\psi_1, \psi_2) = \alpha_1^*\alpha_2 + \beta_1^*\beta_2 = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1^* & \beta_1^*\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_2\\ \beta_2\end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3, board" />

where $\psi_1$ has components $\alpha_1, \beta_1$ and $\psi_2$ has $\alpha_2, \beta_2$: transpose and conjugate the first vector, then multiply. (In the transcript Zwiebach says the second value is conjugated too, and writes the first vector's components in the column; the rule he states in words, conjugating only the first, is the one written here.) In 8.05, an ordinary wavefunction is treated the same way, as an infinitely long column of its values at points $0, \pm\epsilon, \pm 2\epsilon, \dots$ (reading1 p. 3). With this inner product, the Hermitian conjugate of a matrix is its transpose with every entry conjugated, so a Hermitian matrix equals its own conjugate transpose (reading1 p. 3).

## The most general Hamiltonian

The Hamiltonian is now a $2 \times 2$ Hermitian matrix. Its diagonal entries must be real, and the entry below the diagonal must be the conjugate of the one above, so the most general one is (reading2 p. 1)

<Formula label="General 2 × 2 Hermitian matrix" tex="H = \begin{pmatrix}a_0 + a_3 & a_1 - ia_2\\ a_1 + ia_2 & a_0 - a_3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad a_0, a_1, a_2, a_3 \text{ real}" cite="reading2" at="transcript p. 1, board" />

This is the most general time-independent Hamiltonian of any system with two states: solving it once solves them all (reading2 p. 1). It is a real combination of four Hermitian matrices (reading2 pp. 1–2),

<Formula label="Pauli matrices" tex="H = a_0\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix} + a_1\sigma_1 + a_2\sigma_2 + a_3\sigma_3, \qquad \sigma_1 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_2 = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -i\\ i & 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad \sigma_3 = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="transcript pp. 1–2, board" />

the identity and the three **Pauli matrices**. The identity only adds a constant to every energy, like the $\frac12\hbar\omega$ of the harmonic oscillator, so it can be dropped (reading2 p. 2). Giving the other coefficients units of energy as frequencies times $\hbar/2$ (reading2 p. 2):

<Formula label="Hamiltonian with frequencies" tex="H = \omega_1\,\frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_1 + \omega_2\,\frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_2 + \omega_3\,\frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_3" cite="reading2" at="transcript p. 2, board" />

## Angular momentum appears

Each $\omega_i$ is a frequency, so each $\frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_i$ has the units of $\hbar$: angular momentum. And there are three of them. So name them (reading2 p. 2)

<Formula label="Spin operators" tex="S_x = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_1, \qquad S_y = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_2, \qquad S_z = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_3" cite="reading2" at="transcript p. 2, board" />

and test whether they behave like angular momentum. Multiplying the matrices, $\sigma_1\sigma_2 = \begin{pmatrix}i & 0\\ 0 & -i\end{pmatrix} = i\sigma_3$ and $\sigma_2\sigma_1 = -i\sigma_3$, so their commutator is $2i\sigma_3$ (reading2 p. 3) and

<Formula label="Commutator of spin operators" tex="[S_x, S_y] = \frac{\hbar}{2}\,\frac{\hbar}{2}\left(\sigma_1\sigma_2 - \sigma_2\sigma_1\right) = i\hbar\,S_z" cite="reading2" at="transcript p. 3, board" />

exactly the commutator of the orbital angular momenta $L_x$ and $L_y$. The others follow in the same way: $[S_y, S_z] = i\hbar S_x$ and $[S_z, S_x] = i\hbar S_y$ (reading2 p. 3). Orbital angular momentum was $\vec r \times \vec p$, built from derivatives; here two-by-two matrices have produced angular momentum out of nothing more than two states. This is **spin one-half**. Calling the two states spin up and spin down was physicists' interpretation, but the mathematics was there all along in the simplest quantum system. Zwiebach remarks that Schrödinger, had he been more mathematically inclined, might have found spin five minutes after his equation (reading2 p. 3). (Historically, spin was found from experiment instead, in the Stern–Gerlach experiment with which 8.05 begins.)

The Hamiltonian is then (reading2 p. 4)

<Formula label="Spin Hamiltonian" tex="H = \omega_1S_x + \omega_2S_y + \omega_3S_z = \vec\omega\cdot\vec S" cite="reading2" at="transcript p. 4, board" />

the Hamiltonian of a spin in a magnetic field. Solving its time evolution shows that the spin **precesses**, the effect behind nuclear magnetic resonance and the medical scanners based on it (reading2 p. 4).

## Spin states along z, x and y

As with orbital angular momentum, where $L^2$ and $L_z$ are measured, here $S^2$ and $S_z$ are measured. $S_z$ is already diagonal, so its eigenstates are the two columns (reading3 p. 1):

<Formula label="Spin up and down along z" tex="|{\uparrow}\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}1\\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \quad S_z|{\uparrow}\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2}|{\uparrow}\rangle; \qquad |{\downarrow}\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0\\ 1\end{pmatrix}, \quad S_z|{\downarrow}\rangle = -\frac{\hbar}{2}|{\downarrow}\rangle" cite="reading3" at="transcript p. 1, board" />

The $\frac12$ is not a choice: any other factor in front of the Pauli matrices would spoil the commutators, so two-by-two matrices force spin one-half (reading3 p. 1). For comparison, a photon has spin 1, with components $\pm\hbar$ for its two circular polarizations (reading3 p. 1).

But space has three directions and the vector space only two. The states $|{\uparrow}\rangle$ and $|{\downarrow}\rangle$ are orthogonal and already form a basis, every state being $a|{\uparrow}\rangle + b|{\downarrow}\rangle$ (reading3 p. 2). How can a spin point along $x$ or $y$? Try superpositions. $S_x = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_1$ swaps the two components, so the column $(1, 1)$ is unchanged and $(1, -1)$ changes sign (reading3 p. 2):

<Formula label="Spin along x" tex="|{\pm x}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|{\uparrow}\rangle \pm |{\downarrow}\rangle\right), \qquad S_x|{\pm x}\rangle = \pm\frac{\hbar}{2}|{\pm x}\rangle" cite="reading3" at="transcript p. 2, board" />

That seems to use up the possibilities, but it does not: the coefficient of $|{\downarrow}\rangle$ can be complex. $S_y$ acting on the column $(1, i)$ gives $\frac{\hbar}{2}(-i \cdot i, i) = \frac{\hbar}{2}(1, i)$ (reading3 p. 3):

<Formula label="Spin along y" tex="|{\pm y}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|{\uparrow}\rangle \pm i|{\downarrow}\rangle\right), \qquad S_y|{\pm y}\rangle = \pm\frac{\hbar}{2}|{\pm y}\rangle" cite="reading3" at="transcript p. 3, board" />

(The labels $|{\pm x}\rangle$ and $|{\pm y}\rangle$ are this page's; the lecture writes the combinations out.) Without complex numbers there would be no way to point a spin in every direction (reading3 p. 3). Spin is not a function of $x$, $\theta$ or $\phi$: it is an extra two-state world, with the units, commutators and eigenvalues of angular momentum. Two-state systems are the qubits of quantum computers, and the simplest setting for superposition, entanglement and Bell inequalities, topics of 8.05 (reading3 p. 3).

<WorkedExample title="Where does a spin point?">
<Problem>
A spin is in the state $\chi = \frac35|{\uparrow}\rangle + \frac45|{\downarrow}\rangle$. (a) Check that it is normalized. (b) Find the probabilities of $S_z = \pm\frac{\hbar}{2}$ and $\langle S_z\rangle$. (c) Find the probability of $S_x = +\frac{\hbar}{2}$ and $\langle S_x\rangle$. (d) Find $\langle S_y\rangle$, and the direction of the spin.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Normalization">
$(\chi, \chi) = \left(\frac35\right)^2 + \left(\frac45\right)^2 = \frac{9}{25} + \frac{16}{25} = 1$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Along z">
The probabilities are $0.36$ and $0.64$, so $\langle S_z\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2}(0.36 - 0.64) = -0.14\,\hbar$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Along x">
The overlap with $|{+x}\rangle$ is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\frac35 + \frac45\right) = \frac{7}{5\sqrt2}$, so the probability is $\frac{49}{50} = 0.98$; the probability of $-\frac{\hbar}{2}$ is $\frac{1}{50} = 0.02$. Then $\langle S_x\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2}(0.98 - 0.02) = 0.48\,\hbar$.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Along y, and the direction">
The overlap with $|{+y}\rangle$ is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\frac35 - \frac45 i\right)$, whose squared size is $\frac12$: both outcomes are equally likely and $\langle S_y\rangle = 0$, as for any state with real coefficients. The average spin is $\frac{\hbar}{2}(0.96, 0, -0.28)$, a vector of length $\frac{\hbar}{2}$ (since $0.96^2 + 0.28^2 = 1$) in the $x$–$z$ plane, tilted below the $x$ axis.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Spin and the 21 cm line">
The electron and the proton in a hydrogen atom both carry spin one-half. In the ground state, the energy is very slightly lower when their spins point in opposite directions, and a flip from the parallel to the opposite arrangement emits a radio photon of wavelength about 21 cm. Atomic hydrogen is the most common gas in the universe, so this line maps the gas in our Galaxy and in others; its Doppler shifts trace the [rotation of galaxies](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/rotation-curves), evidence for dark matter, and redshifted 21 cm emission is a way to study hydrogen in the early universe. [8.05 explains](/courses/8-05/hyperfine-21cm-adding-angular-momenta) where the splitting comes from.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The simplest quantum system has two states: its wavefunction is a pair of complex numbers $(\alpha, \beta)$, with inner product $\alpha_1^*\alpha_2 + \beta_1^*\beta_2$.
- Its most general Hamiltonian is a $2 \times 2$ Hermitian matrix, $a_0$ times the identity plus $a_1\sigma_1 + a_2\sigma_2 + a_3\sigma_3$, with four real numbers; without the constant it is $\vec\omega\cdot\vec S$.
- $S_i = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_i$ satisfy $[S_x, S_y] = i\hbar S_z$ and its cyclic partners: angular momentum appears from two states alone, and only with eigenvalues $\pm\frac{\hbar}{2}$. This is spin one-half.
- $|{\uparrow}\rangle$ and $|{\downarrow}\rangle$ point along $\pm z$; $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|{\uparrow}\rangle \pm |{\downarrow}\rangle)$ along $\pm x$; $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|{\uparrow}\rangle \pm i|{\downarrow}\rangle)$ along $\pm y$. Complex numbers are essential.
