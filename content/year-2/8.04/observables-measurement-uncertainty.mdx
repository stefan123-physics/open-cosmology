Observables, the quantities we can measure, are represented in quantum mechanics by Hermitian operators. This lecture proves the properties that make them suitable, states the measurement postulate that tells what a measurement gives and what it does to the state, and defines the uncertainty of an observable (notes pp. 1–7). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 9; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Hermitian operators, briefly written

A short notation for the integral of two functions helps (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="Inner product" tex="\left(\Psi_1, \Psi_2\right) \equiv \int dx\,\Psi_1^*(x)\,\Psi_2(x)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.2)" />

Constants come out of the second slot unchanged and out of the first slot conjugated: $(a\Psi_1, \Psi_2) = a^*(\Psi_1, \Psi_2)$ and $(\Psi_1, a\Psi_2) = a(\Psi_1, \Psi_2)$ (eq. (1.3)). Then $\hat Q$ is **Hermitian** if $(\Psi_1, \hat Q\Psi_2) = (\hat Q\Psi_1, \Psi_2)$ for all the wavefunctions we work with, and the expectation value in a normalized state is $\langle\hat Q\rangle = (\Psi, \hat Q\Psi)$ (notes p. 1, eqs. (1.4)–(1.5)).

**Claim 1: expectation values are real** (notes pp. 1–2). Conjugating the integral conjugates the integrand: $\langle\hat Q\rangle^* = \int dx\,(\hat Q\Psi)^*\Psi$. Hermiticity moves $\hat Q$ back onto the second $\Psi$, giving $\langle\hat Q\rangle$ again. So $\langle\hat Q\rangle^* = \langle\hat Q\rangle$.

**Claim 2: eigenvalues are real** (notes p. 2). If $\hat Q\psi_1 = q_1\psi_1$ with $\psi_1$ normalized, then

<Formula label="Expectation value in an eigenstate" tex="\left(\psi_1, \hat Q\psi_1\right) = \left(\psi_1, q_1\psi_1\right) = q_1\left(\psi_1, \psi_1\right) = q_1" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.9)" />

which is real by Claim 1. In an eigenstate, the expectation value is the eigenvalue.

**Claim 3: eigenfunctions can be chosen orthonormal** (notes p. 2). Label the eigenfunctions $\hat Q\psi_i = q_i\psi_i$. The claim is

<Formula label="Orthonormality" tex="\left(\psi_i, \psi_j\right) = \int dx\,\psi_i^*(x)\,\psi_j(x) = \delta_{ij}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.12)" />

For $i = j$ it is just normalization. For different eigenvalues, compute $(\psi_i, \hat Q\psi_j)$ in two ways: directly it is $q_j(\psi_i, \psi_j)$; moving $\hat Q$ to the left and using that $q_i$ is real, it is $q_i(\psi_i, \psi_j)$. Subtracting (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Different eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenfunctions" tex="\left(q_j - q_i\right)\left(\psi_i, \psi_j\right) = 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.15)" />

so $(\psi_i, \psi_j) = 0$ when $q_i \ne q_j$. When several eigenfunctions share an eigenvalue (a **degeneracy**) the argument says nothing about them, but suitable combinations of them can be chosen orthogonal; the proof comes in 8.05 (notes p. 2).

**Claim 4: eigenfunctions are complete** (notes p. 3). The eigenfunctions of $\hat Q$ are enough to build every well-behaved state: a result known as the spectral theorem, whose finite-dimensional version is proved in 8.05. In formulas,

<Formula label="Expansion in eigenfunctions" tex="\Psi(x) = \sum_i\alpha_i\,\psi_i(x), \qquad \alpha_i = \left(\psi_i, \Psi\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eqs. (1.16)–(1.17)" />

The coefficient formula follows by integrating $\psi_i^*$ against the sum and using orthonormality, which keeps only the $j = i$ term (eq. (1.18)). The same orthonormality turns the normalization of $\Psi$ into $\sum_i|\alpha_i|^2 = 1$ (notes p. 3, eqs. (1.19)–(1.20)).

## The measurement postulate

Now the rules for observables can be stated (notes p. 3):

> **Measurement postulate.** Measuring the Hermitian operator $\hat Q$ in the state $\Psi = \sum_i\alpha_i\psi_i$ gives one of its eigenvalues $q_1, q_2, \dots$. The probability of the outcome $q_i$ is $p_i = |\alpha_i|^2$. Once $q_i$ has been found, the system is left in the eigenstate $\psi_i$.

<Formula label="Probability of an outcome, and collapse" tex="p_i = \left|\alpha_i\right|^2, \qquad \Psi \to \psi_i \text{ after the outcome } q_i" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eqs. (1.21)–(1.22)" />

The jump of the state is the **collapse of the wavefunction**: an immediate second measurement gives $q_i$ again, with certainty (notes p. 3). If two eigenfunctions $\psi_i$ and $\psi_k$ share the eigenvalue $q$, the state after finding $q$ is the normalized sum of both pieces, $(\alpha_i\psi_i + \alpha_k\psi_k)/\sqrt{|\alpha_i|^2 + |\alpha_k|^2}$ (notes pp. 3–4, eqs. (1.23)–(1.24); the notes' denominator writes $\alpha_j$ for $\alpha_k$, a slip). The probabilities add up to 1, by $\sum|\alpha_i|^2 = 1$ (eq. (1.25)). This is the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics (notes p. 4).

The postulate uses the fact that a vector can be written as a sum of other vectors in infinitely many ways: to predict a measurement of $\hat Q_1$, expand the state in eigenstates of $\hat Q_1$; for $\hat Q_2$, in eigenstates of $\hat Q_2$; each expansion reveals the probabilities for its own observable (notes p. 4).

**A consistency check** (notes p. 4). With the expansion, the expectation value becomes

<Formula label="Expectation value from the probabilities" tex="\langle\hat Q\rangle = \sum_{i,j}\alpha_i^*\alpha_j\,q_j\left(\psi_i, \psi_j\right) = \sum_i\left|\alpha_i\right|^2q_i = \sum_i p_i\,q_i" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (1.26)" />

the possible outcomes weighted by their probabilities, exactly as for any random quantity.

## A particle on a circle

Identify the points $x = 0$ and $x = L$, so the particle lives on a circle of circumference $L$ and every wavefunction obeys $\Psi(x + L) = \Psi(x)$ (notes p. 4, eq. (1.27)). Two things change compared with the infinite line (notes p. 5). Momentum is **quantized**, since $e^{ikx}$ is periodic only if $e^{ikL} = 1$:

<Formula label="Quantized wavenumbers" tex="e^{ikL} = 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad kL = 2\pi m, \quad m \in \mathbb{Z}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (1.29)" />

and, the space being finite, the momentum eigenstates can be normalized: $\int_0^L|\psi_m|^2dx = N^2L = 1$ gives $N = 1/\sqrt L$ (eqs. (1.30)–(1.31)). So (notes p. 5)

<Formula label="Momentum eigenstates on a circle" tex="\psi_m(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt L}\,e^{2\pi imx/L}, \qquad \hat p\,\psi_m = \frac{2\pi\hbar m}{L}\,\psi_m" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eqs. (1.32)–(1.33)" />

with $m$ any integer, positive, negative or zero. To find the possible momenta of any state on the circle, rewrite its sines and cosines as exponentials, read off the coefficients of the $\psi_m$, and square them (notes p. 5, eqs. (1.34)–(1.36)); the notes do this for a mixture of $\sin(2\pi x/L)$ and $\cos(6\pi x/L)$, finding four momenta, and the worked example below does it for a different state.

## Uncertainty

For a random quantity with values $Q_i$ and probabilities $p_i$, the **variance** is the expected square of the deviation from the mean, $(\Delta Q)^2 \equiv \sum_ip_i(Q_i - \langle Q\rangle)^2$, and its square root, the standard deviation, is the uncertainty (notes p. 6, eqs. (2.37)–(2.38)). If $\Delta Q = 0$, every term vanishes and $Q$ always takes its mean value. Expanding the square and using $\sum p_i = 1$ (notes p. 6):

<Formula label="Variance" tex="\left(\Delta Q\right)^2 = \langle Q^2\rangle - \langle Q\rangle^2 \ge 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eqs. (2.40)–(2.41)" />

The quantum definition mimics this: the **uncertainty** of $\hat Q$ in the state $\Psi$ is the real number $\Delta Q$ with $(\Delta Q)^2 = \langle\hat Q^2\rangle - \langle\hat Q\rangle^2$ (notes p. 6, eqs. (2.42)–(2.43)). Two other forms are useful. Expanding the square shows (Claim 1, eqs. (2.44)–(2.45))

<Formula label="Uncertainty as an expected square" tex="\left(\Delta Q\right)^2 = \left\langle\left(\hat Q - \langle\hat Q\rangle\right)^2\right\rangle" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.44)" />

and, since $\hat Q$ is Hermitian and $\langle\hat Q\rangle$ is real, one of the two factors can be moved onto $\Psi^*$ (Claim 2, notes p. 7):

<Formula label="Uncertainty as an integral of a square" tex="\left(\Delta Q\right)^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\left|\left(\hat Q - \langle\hat Q\rangle\right)\Psi(x)\right|^2" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eqs. (2.46)–(2.48)" />

So if $\Delta Q = 0$ the integrand vanishes everywhere, $\hat Q\Psi = \langle\hat Q\rangle\Psi$, and $\Psi$ is an eigenstate; conversely an eigenstate has zero uncertainty. In short (notes p. 7):

<Formula label="Zero uncertainty means an eigenstate" tex="\Delta Q = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Psi \text{ is an eigenstate of } \hat Q" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eq. (2.50)" />

<WorkedExample title="Measuring momentum on a circle">
<Problem>
A particle on a circle of circumference $L$ is in the state $\Psi(x) = \sqrt{\frac2L}\left(0.6\cos\frac{2\pi x}{L} + 0.8i\sin\frac{4\pi x}{L}\right)$. (a) Check that it is normalized. (b) Find the possible momenta and their probabilities. (c) Find $\langle\hat p\rangle$ and $\Delta p$. (d) What is the state after a measurement finds $p = 4\pi\hbar/L$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Normalization">
The cosine and sine have different frequencies, so they are orthogonal on $[0, L]$, and $\frac2L\int_0^L\cos^2\frac{2\pi x}{L}dx = \frac2L\int_0^L\sin^2\frac{4\pi x}{L}dx = 1$. So the total is $0.36 + 0.64 = 1$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Expanding in momentum eigenstates">
$\cos\frac{2\pi x}{L} = \frac12\left(e^{2\pi ix/L} + e^{-2\pi ix/L}\right)$ and $\sin\frac{4\pi x}{L} = \frac{1}{2i}\left(e^{4\pi ix/L} - e^{-4\pi ix/L}\right)$. With $e^{2\pi imx/L} = \sqrt L\,\psi_m$, $\Psi = \frac{0.6}{\sqrt2}\left(\psi_1 + \psi_{-1}\right) + \frac{0.8}{\sqrt2}\left(\psi_2 - \psi_{-2}\right)$. So $p = \pm 2\pi\hbar/L$, each with probability $0.36/2 = 0.18$, and $p = \pm 4\pi\hbar/L$, each with probability $0.64/2 = 0.32$. The four add up to 1.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Mean and uncertainty">
The distribution is symmetric, so $\langle\hat p\rangle = 0$. Then $\langle\hat p^2\rangle = \left(\frac{2\pi\hbar}{L}\right)^2\left(0.36 \times 1 + 0.64 \times 4\right) = 2.92\left(\frac{2\pi\hbar}{L}\right)^2$, so $\Delta p = \sqrt{2.92}\,\frac{2\pi\hbar}{L} \approx 1.71\,\frac{2\pi\hbar}{L}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Collapse">
The state becomes $\psi_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt L}e^{4\pi ix/L}$, a momentum eigenstate: a second measurement gives $4\pi\hbar/L$ again, and $\Delta p = 0$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- For Hermitian $\hat Q$: expectation values and eigenvalues are real; eigenfunctions with different eigenvalues are orthogonal and can be chosen orthonormal; they are complete, $\Psi = \sum\alpha_i\psi_i$ with $\alpha_i = (\psi_i, \Psi)$.
- Measurement postulate: the outcome is an eigenvalue $q_i$, with probability $|\alpha_i|^2$, and the state collapses to $\psi_i$; then $\langle\hat Q\rangle = \sum p_iq_i$.
- On a circle of circumference $L$ momentum is quantized, $p = 2\pi\hbar m/L$, and its eigenstates are normalizable.
- The uncertainty $(\Delta Q)^2 = \langle\hat Q^2\rangle - \langle\hat Q\rangle^2 = \int|(\hat Q - \langle\hat Q\rangle)\Psi|^2dx$ vanishes exactly in eigenstates of $\hat Q$.
