The hydrogen atom is the problem quantum mechanics was built to solve. This lecture reduces the two-particle problem to a single particle in a central potential, sets its natural scales, and solves the radial equation as a series until the energy levels appear (notes pp. 1–7). (MIT's material for this lecture is the first part of Zwiebach's notes "Hydrogen Atom", titled "Lectures 21 and 22" in the PDF but serving Lectures 22 and 23 of MIT's calendar, which this site follows; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example. The notes use Gaussian units, in which the Coulomb energy is $e^2/r$; and as before, the script ℓ is written $l$.)

## Two bodies, one central problem

The proton and the electron have positions and momenta $\hat{\vec x}_p, \hat{\vec p}_p$ and $\hat{\vec x}_e, \hat{\vec p}_e$, each pair canonical, $[(\hat x_p)_i, (\hat p_p)_j] = i\hbar\delta_{ij}$ and likewise for the electron, with proton variables commuting with electron ones (notes p. 1, eq. (1.1)). The wavefunction depends on both positions, $|\Psi(\vec x_p, \vec x_e)|^2d^3x_pd^3x_e$ being the probability of finding the proton near $\vec x_p$ and the electron near $\vec x_e$ (eqs. (1.2)–(1.3)), and (notes p. 1)

<Formula label="Hydrogen Hamiltonian" tex="\hat H = \frac{\hat{\vec p}_p^{\,2}}{2m_p} + \frac{\hat{\vec p}_e^{\,2}}{2m_e} + V\left(\left|\hat{\vec x}_e - \hat{\vec x}_p\right|\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.4)" />

The potential depends only on the separation. Introduce new canonical pairs (notes pp. 1–3). The **centre of mass**: the total momentum and the mass-weighted position,

<Formula label="Centre-of-mass variables" tex="\hat{\vec P} = \hat{\vec p}_p + \hat{\vec p}_e, \qquad \hat{\vec X} = \frac{m_e\hat{\vec x}_e + m_p\hat{\vec x}_p}{m_e + m_p}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.5)" />

which are canonical, $[\hat X_i, \hat P_j] = i\hbar\delta_{ij}$ (eqs. (1.6)–(1.7)). The **relative** position $\hat{\vec x} = \hat{\vec x}_e - \hat{\vec x}_p$ commutes with both. For the relative momentum, try $\hat{\vec p} = \alpha\hat{\vec p}_e - \beta\hat{\vec p}_p$: being conjugate to $\hat{\vec x}$ requires $\alpha + \beta = 1$, and commuting with $\hat{\vec X}$ requires $m_e\alpha - m_p\beta = 0$ (eqs. (1.8)–(1.12)). With the **total mass** and the **reduced mass**

<Formula label="Total and reduced mass" tex="M = m_e + m_p, \qquad \mu = \frac{m_em_p}{m_e + m_p}, \qquad \hat{\vec p} = \frac{\mu}{m_e}\hat{\vec p}_e - \frac{\mu}{m_p}\hat{\vec p}_p" cite="mit" at="notes pp. 2–3, eqs. (1.13)–(1.15)" />

For very different masses $\mu$ is close to the lighter one. Classically $\vec p = \mu(\vec v_e - \vec v_p)$, which vanishes for pure centre-of-mass motion (notes p. 3).

Rewriting the kinetic energy in the new variables, the cross terms $\hat{\vec P}\cdot\hat{\vec p}$ cancel exactly (notes p. 3, eqs. (1.16)–(1.17)):

<Formula label="Separated Hamiltonian" tex="\hat H = \frac{\hat{\vec P}^{\,2}}{2M} + \frac{\hat{\vec p}^{\,2}}{2\mu} + V\left(|\hat{\vec x}|\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.18)" />

With $\hat{\vec P} = \frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla_X$ and $\hat{\vec p} = \frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla_x$, try $\Psi = \psi_{CM}(\vec X)\,\psi_{rel}(\vec x)$. Dividing the Schrödinger equation by $\Psi$ leaves one term depending only on $\vec X$ and one only on $\vec x$, each therefore constant (notes pp. 3–4, eqs. (1.19)–(1.25)): the centre of mass moves as a **free particle** of mass $M$ with unquantized energy $E_{CM}$; the relative motion is a particle of mass $\mu$ in the **central potential** $V$, with energy $E_{rel}$; and $E = E_{CM} + E_{rel}$. This is the two-body reduction promised in the previous lectures.

## The scales of hydrogen

The potential of a nucleus with $Z$ protons is (notes p. 4)

<Formula label="Coulomb potential" tex="V(r) = -\frac{Ze^2}{r}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.1)" />

with $Z = 1$ for hydrogen; larger $Z$ describes one electron around a heavier nucleus. The **fine-structure constant** is $\alpha = e^2/(\hbar c) \approx 1/137$ (notes p. 4). The **Bohr radius** comes from setting a typical kinetic energy equal to a typical potential energy, $\hbar^2/(ma_0^2) = e^2/a_0$, with $m$ the reduced mass, very nearly $m_e$ (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Bohr radius" tex="a_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{me^2} = \frac{\hbar c}{mc^2}\,\frac{1}{\alpha} \approx 0.529\ \text{Å} \approx 53\ \text{pm}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eqs. (2.2)–(2.3)" />

and the matching energy scale (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Atomic energy scale" tex="\frac{e^2}{a_0} = \frac{me^4}{\hbar^2} = \alpha^2\,mc^2 \approx \frac{511{,}000\ \text{eV}}{137^2} \approx 27.2\ \text{eV}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.4)" />

Smaller lengths follow by powers of $\alpha$ (notes p. 5, eq. (2.5)): $\alpha a_0 = \hbar/(mc) \approx 386$ fm (the notes call it the Compton wavelength and round to 390 fm; it is the Compton wavelength $h/(mc)$ of Lecture 3 divided by $2\pi$), and $\alpha^2a_0 = e^2/(mc^2) \approx 2.8$ fm, the classical electron radius.

## Solving the radial equation

For bound states, $E < 0$, the radial equation is (notes p. 5)

<Formula label="Radial equation for hydrogen" tex="-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} + \left(\frac{\hbar^2l(l + 1)}{2mr^2} - \frac{Ze^2}{r}\right)u = E\,u" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.6)" />

**Unit-free form** (notes p. 5). Write $r = \frac{a_0}{2Z}x$ (eq. (2.7)), which removes $Z$ and some factors of 2, and define the unit-free energy $\kappa$ by

<Formula label="Unit-free energy" tex="\kappa^2 = -\frac{E}{2Z^2e^2/a_0} > 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.11)" />

Then the equation is $-u'' + \frac{l(l + 1)}{x^2}u - \frac1xu = -\kappa^2u$ (eqs. (2.8)–(2.12)). Far out, $u'' \approx \kappa^2u$, so $u \sim e^{-\kappa x}$, which suggests the coordinate $\rho \equiv \kappa x = \frac{2Z\kappa}{a_0}r$ (notes pp. 5–6, eqs. (2.13)–(2.15)):

<Formula label="Equation in ρ" tex="-\frac{d^2u}{d\rho^2} + \frac{l(l + 1)}{\rho^2}u - \frac{1}{\kappa\rho}u = -u" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.15)" />

$\kappa$ survives in the equation, as it should: the equation must fix it.

**Peeling off the known behaviour** (notes p. 6). Near the origin $u \sim \rho^{l+1}$ and far out $u \sim e^{-\rho}$, so write (eq. (2.16))

<Formula label="Ansatz for u" tex="u(\rho) = \rho^{l+1}\,W(\rho)\,e^{-\rho}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.16)" />

and $W$ obeys (notes p. 6, eqs. (2.17)–(2.18))

<Formula label="Equation for W" tex="\rho\frac{d^2W}{d\rho^2} + 2\left(l + 1 - \rho\right)\frac{dW}{d\rho} + \left(\frac1\kappa - 2(l + 1)\right)W = 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.18)" />

With $W = \sum_ka_k\rho^k$, collecting powers of $\rho$ gives a one-step recursion (notes p. 6):

<Formula label="Recursion for the coefficients" tex="\frac{a_{k+1}}{a_k} = \frac{2(k + l + 1) - \frac1\kappa}{(k + 1)(k + 2l + 2)}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.21)" />

## Quantization

For large $k$ the ratio tends to $2/k$, which is larger than $2/(k + 1)$, so the series grows at least as fast as the one with $a_{k+1}/a_k = 2/(k + 1)$; that one has $a_k$ equal to $2^k/k!$ times its first coefficient, the series of $e^{2\rho}$ (notes p. 7, eqs. (2.22)–(2.25)). Then $u \sim \rho^{l+1}e^{2\rho}e^{-\rho}$ grows without bound: not normalizable. So the series **must stop**: $W$ is a polynomial of degree $N$, with $a_N \ne 0$ and $a_{N+1} = 0$ (eq. (2.26)), which by the recursion requires (notes p. 7)

<Formula label="Quantization condition" tex="\frac1\kappa = 2\left(N + l + 1\right), \qquad N, l = 0, 1, 2, \dots" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eq. (2.27)" />

Quantization has happened. Calling $n \equiv N + l + 1 = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ the **principal quantum number**, $\kappa = 1/(2n)$, and from the definition of $\kappa$ (notes p. 7, eqs. (2.28)–(2.31)):

<Formula label="Hydrogen energy levels" tex="E_n = -\frac{Z^2e^2}{2a_0}\,\frac{1}{n^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eq. (2.31)" />

For hydrogen, $\frac{e^2}{2a_0} \approx 13.6$ eV, so $E_n \approx -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$. The energies depend only on $n$, not separately on $l$ or $N$, a degeneracy explored on the next page.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Why the universe waited to become neutral">
Hydrogen holds its electron with 13.6 eV, yet the early universe became neutral only when it had cooled to about 4,000 K, where a typical photon carries roughly a third of an electronvolt. The reason is numbers: there are more than a billion photons for every atom, so even when the average photon is far too weak, the rare energetic ones keep breaking atoms apart (this explanation is the page's own summary). [8.286 follows the cooling to recombination and decoupling](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling).
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Reduced masses and a first excited state">
<Problem>
(a) Compute $a_0$ from $\hbar c = 197.327$ eV nm, $\alpha^{-1} = 137.036$ and $m_ec^2 = 511{,}000$ eV. (b) With the reduced mass ($m_p/m_e = 1836.15$), by how much is hydrogen's ground-state binding energy below $13.606$ eV? (c) Positronium binds an electron to a positron of equal mass: find its ground-state binding energy and its Bohr radius. (d) For $n = 2$, $l = 0$, build $W(\rho)$ from the recursion.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Bohr radius">
$a_0 = \frac{\hbar c}{\alpha\,m_ec^2} = \frac{197.327 \times 137.036}{511{,}000} \approx 0.0529$ nm $= 0.529$ Å.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Reduced mass of hydrogen">
$\mu/m_e = \frac{1836.15}{1837.15} \approx 0.999456$. Energies scale with the mass, so the binding is $13.606 \times 0.999456 \approx 13.599$ eV, lower by about $0.0074$ eV, a correction of one part in 1837.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Positronium">
$\mu = m_e/2$: the binding energy halves to about $6.80$ eV, and the Bohr radius, proportional to $1/\mu$, doubles to about $0.106$ nm.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) The 2s state">
$n = 2$, $l = 0$ means $N = 1$ and $1/\kappa = 4$. The recursion gives $a_1/a_0 = \frac{2(0 + 0 + 1) - 4}{1 \times 2} = -1$, and then $a_2 = 0$: $W$ is proportional to $1 - \rho$ (here $a_0$ is the first series coefficient, not the Bohr radius), with one node at $\rho = 1$, that is at twice the Bohr radius (since $\rho = Zr/(na_0)$ with $a_0$ the Bohr radius).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Hydrogen's two-body problem separates exactly: the centre of mass moves freely with mass $M$, the relative coordinate moves in $V(r)$ with the reduced mass $\mu \approx m_e$.
- Natural scales: $a_0 = \hbar^2/(me^2) \approx 0.529$ Å and $e^2/a_0 = \alpha^2mc^2 \approx 27.2$ eV, with $\alpha \approx 1/137$.
- With $u = \rho^{l+1}W(\rho)e^{-\rho}$, $W$ obeys a one-step recursion; a non-terminating series would grow like $e^{2\rho}$.
- Normalizability forces $W$ to be a polynomial of degree $N$, so $n = N + l + 1$ and $E_n = -\frac{Z^2e^2}{2a_0n^2}$, about $-13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$ for hydrogen.
