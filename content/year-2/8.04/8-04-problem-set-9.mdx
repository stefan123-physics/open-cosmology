## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A numerical test of stationary phase" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

Stationary phase told us where the peak of a wave packet is, and how it moves. The same idea can also give the value of an integral. Take a narrow Gaussian peaked at $x = 2$, multiplied by a phase factor:

<Formula label="A Gaussian integrated against a phase factor" tex="f(\lambda) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\;e^{-100(x - 2)^2}\,e^{i\phi(\lambda, x)}, \qquad \phi(\lambda, x) = 50\left(x - \frac{1}{32}\,\lambda\,x^4\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

with $\lambda$ a real number. The aim is to confirm that $|f(\lambda)|$ is largest near the value $\lambda_*$ that stationary phase selects, and to find the value of $f(\lambda_*)$ in three ways.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the width $\Delta$ of the Gaussian at half its maximum? That is: what is the largest $\Delta$ such that the Gaussian is at least half its maximum for all $x$ with $|x - 2| \le \frac12\Delta$? If you had to do the integral numerically, would it be safe to integrate only from $x = 1$ to $x = 3$? Explain.

<Hint>

Set $e^{-100(x - 2)^2} = \frac12$ and solve for $x - 2$. The width is twice that distance.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the second question, work out the size of the Gaussian at $x = 1$ and $x = 3$. The phase factor has size 1 everywhere.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-width" label="$\Delta =$" value="0.1665" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-width" />

<Answer id="p1-safe" label="Integrating from 1 to 3 is" choices={["safe: at x = 1 and x = 3 the Gaussian has fallen to about 10⁻⁴³ or less, and the phase factor has size 1", "safe only for λ = 0: for other λ the phase factor grows with x", "not safe: the range from 1 to 3 is only about twice the width Δ", "not safe: the phase oscillates faster and faster at large x, so the far tails add up"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The width follows from one logarithm. Whether the range is safe depends on how small the integrand is outside it.

<Step title="The width">

The Gaussian falls to half its maximum where $100(x - 2)^2 = \ln 2$, that is at a distance $\sqrt{\ln 2}/10 = 0.0833$ from the peak, on either side:

<Formula label="Width of the Gaussian at half maximum" tex="\Delta = 2\,\frac{\sqrt{\ln 2}}{10} = 0.1665" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The range from 1 to 3">

At $x = 1$ and at $x = 3$ the Gaussian is $e^{-100} \approx 3.7\times10^{-44}$, and it falls further outside. The phase factor $e^{i\phi}$ has size 1 for every real $x$ and $\lambda$, so it cannot make the tails larger. What is cut off is far below the rounding error of any computer: the range is safe. It could even be much shorter.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The range from 1 to 3 is about 12 times the width $\Delta$. The Gaussian is narrow, and that is what makes the problem work: only the behaviour of the phase close to $x = 2$ can matter.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Use stationary phase to find the critical value $\lambda_*$ of $\lambda$, for which $f(\lambda)$ should have the largest magnitude. For that value, write $\phi(\lambda_*, x)$ as a Taylor expansion around $x = 2$, up to and including the term quadratic in $(x - 2)$.

<Hint>

The Gaussian only lets the region near $x = 2$ contribute. The contributions add up best if the phase does not change there to first order: $\partial\phi/\partial x = 0$ at $x = 2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the expansion you need $\phi$, $\partial\phi/\partial x$ and $\partial^2\phi/\partial x^2$ at $x = 2$, with $\lambda = \lambda_*$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-lam" label="$\lambda_* =$" value="1" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-lam" />

<Answer id="p1-taylor" label="$\phi(\lambda_*, x) \simeq$" expr="75 - 37.5*(x - 2)^2"
  vars={{ x: [1.5, 2.5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "75 - 75*(x - 2)^2", hint: "The second derivative at x = 2 is −75, but the Taylor term is half of it times (x − 2)²." },
    { expr: "100 - 37.5*(x - 2)^2", hint: "Check the constant: it is the whole phase at x = 2, with both terms: 50(2 − 16/32)." },
    { expr: "75 + 37.5*(x - 2)^2", hint: "Check the sign: the term with x⁴ enters the phase with a minus sign, so the phase has a maximum at x = 2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** If the phase changes quickly across the Gaussian, the integrand oscillates and cancels itself. The magnitude is largest when the phase is stationary at the peak of the Gaussian.

<Step title="The critical value">

<Formula label="Stationary phase at the peak of the Gaussian" tex="\left.\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x}\right|_{x = 2} = 50\left(1 - \frac{\lambda\,x^3}{8}\right)_{x = 2} = 50\left(1 - \lambda\right) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda_* = 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The expansion">

With $\lambda = 1$: $\phi(1, 2) = 50\left(2 - \frac{16}{32}\right) = 75$, the first derivative vanishes, and $\partial^2\phi/\partial x^2 = -50\cdot\frac{12x^2}{32} = -75$ at $x = 2$. So

<Formula label="The phase near the peak, at the critical value" tex="\phi(\lambda_*, x) \simeq 75 - \frac{75}{2}\left(x - 2\right)^2 = 75 - 37.5\left(x - 2\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\lambda = 0$ the phase is $50x$, which runs through $50\Delta \approx 8.3$ radians across the width of the Gaussian, more than a full turn: strong cancellation. At $\lambda = 1$ the linear term is gone. Part (e) shows how much difference that makes.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

What is the excursion of the phase $\phi(\lambda_*, x)$ for $|x - 2| < \frac12\Delta$, that is, by how much does it change over the width of the Gaussian? Use the expansion of part (b), and give the result in units of $\pi$. It should be small enough that ignoring the variation of the phase at the critical $\lambda$ is a decent approximation. Do so, and perform the integral that is left. The result is a complex number: write it as a phase factor times a magnitude.

<Hint>

The quadratic term is largest at the edge, $|x - 2| = \frac12\Delta$. From part (a), $\left(\frac12\Delta\right)^2 = \ln 2/100$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With the phase frozen at its value at $x = 2$, the phase factor comes out of the integral, and what is left is $\int e^{-100(x - 2)^2}dx$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-exc" label="Excursion of the phase, in units of $\pi$:" value="0.0827" tolerance="5%" calc="p1-exc" />

<Answer id="p1-mag0" label="Magnitude with the phase frozen:" value="0.1772" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-mag0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Across the width of the Gaussian the phase moves by a small part of a half-turn, so as a first estimate it can be treated as constant.

<Step title="The excursion">

<Formula label="Change of the phase over the width of the Gaussian" tex="37.5\left(\tfrac12\Delta\right)^2 = 37.5\,\frac{\ln 2}{100} = 0.260 = 0.0827\,\pi" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is a twelfth of $\pi$. (With the full phase, not only its quadratic term, the change is 0.253 on one side of the peak and 0.267 on the other. The box accepts those values too.)

</Step>

<Step title="The integral with the phase frozen">

<Formula label="First estimate: constant phase" tex="f(\lambda_*) \simeq e^{75i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\;e^{-100(x - 2)^2} = e^{75i}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{100}} = 0.1772\;e^{75i}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The magnitude is the area of the Gaussian, the largest value $|f|$ could ever have: no cancellation at all. The phase of 75 radians is twelve full turns less 0.398 radians, so the phase factor may also be written $e^{-0.398i}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Now keep the quadratic term of the phase and perform the integral exactly. Write the result as a phase factor times a magnitude.

In the second box give the phase as the number to be added to 75, in radians.

<Hint>

The integral is still a Gaussian one, $\int e^{-c\,(x - 2)^2}dx = \sqrt{\pi/c}$, now with a complex number $c$. The formula holds as long as the real part of $c$ is positive.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $c$ in polar form, $c = |c|\,e^{i\gamma}$. Then $\sqrt{\pi/c} = \sqrt{\pi/|c|}\;e^{-i\gamma/2}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-mag2" label="Magnitude:" value="0.1715" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-mag2" />

<Answer id="p1-ph2" label="Phase $= 75\; +$" value="-0.179" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-ph2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A quadratic phase turns the real Gaussian into a Gaussian with a complex coefficient, which can still be integrated in closed form.

<Step title="The integral">

<Formula label="Second estimate: quadratic phase" tex="f(\lambda_*) \simeq e^{75i}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\;e^{-(100 + 37.5i)(x - 2)^2} = e^{75i}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{100 + 37.5i}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Phase and magnitude">

The complex coefficient has the size $\sqrt{100^2 + 37.5^2} = 106.8$ and the angle $\tan^{-1}0.375 = 0.359$. The square root halves the angle:

<Formula label="Second estimate as a phase times a magnitude" tex="f(\lambda_*) \simeq \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{106.8}}\;e^{i\left(75 - 0.179\right)} = 0.1715\;e^{74.82i}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

If the 37.5 is set to zero the result of part (c) comes back. The curvature of the phase lowers the magnitude by 3.2% and turns the phase back by 0.179 radians, about 10 degrees: small corrections, as the small excursion of part (c) promised.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

Perform the integral numerically as a function of $\lambda$ for $\lambda$ between 0 and 1, and plot $|f(\lambda)|$. What is the value of $f(\lambda)$ at the critical $\lambda$? Compare it with the two estimates. Which $\lambda$ gives the largest $|f(\lambda)|$? (Look a little beyond $\lambda = 1$ as well.)

<Hint>

Integrate the real part, $e^{-100(x - 2)^2}\cos\phi$, and the imaginary part, $e^{-100(x - 2)^2}\sin\phi$, separately. A simple sum with steps of 0.02 in $x$ from 1.6 to 2.4 is already very accurate, because the integrand is smooth and dies out at both ends.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the largest $|f|$, make a table in steps of 0.001 in $\lambda$ near the critical value.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-num" label="Numerically, $|f(\lambda_*)| =$" value="0.1715" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p1-num" />

<Answer id="p1-max" label="$|f|$ is largest at $\lambda =$" value="0.992" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p1-max" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The numerical integral keeps every term of the phase, so it tests both estimates, and it shows how sharply stationary phase picks out $\lambda_*$.

<Step title="The value at the critical λ">

Integrating real and imaginary parts numerically at $\lambda = 1$:

<Formula label="Numerical value at the critical λ" tex="f(\lambda_*) = 0.14367 - 0.09363\,i = 0.17149\;e^{-0.5776i}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The estimate of part (d), $0.1715\,e^{74.82i}$, is the same number to the digits shown: 74.82 radians is twelve full turns less 0.578. Its magnitude, 0.17151, differs from the numerical one by about one part in 10,000. The cruder estimate of part (c) is 3.4% too large in magnitude, and its phase is off by 0.18 radians.

</Step>

<Step title="The plot">

A fine table near the top gives the maximum at $\lambda = 0.992$, where $|f| = 0.17156$. That is less than 1% away from $\lambda_* = 1$, and the curve is so flat there that $|f(1)|$ is lower by only 0.04%. Stationary phase finds the peak.

</Step>

<Formula label="Magnitude with the phase expanded to second order" tex="\left|f(\lambda)\right| \simeq \frac{\sqrt\pi}{\left(100^2 + \left(37.5\,\lambda\right)^2\right)^{1/4}}\;\exp\left(-\frac{62500\left(1 - \lambda\right)^2}{100^2 + \left(37.5\,\lambda\right)^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Testing Levinson's theorem in an example" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

Take the lesson's attractive well against a wall: $V(x) = -V_0$ for $0 < x < a$, $V(x) = 0$ for $x > a$, and $V(x) = \infty$ for $x < 0$. Its phase shift $\delta(E)$ was found in the lecture:

<Formula label="Phase shift of the well against a wall" tex="\tan\delta = \frac{1 - \frac{k'}{k}\cot k'a\,\tan ka}{\tan ka + \frac{k'}{k}\cot k'a}, \qquad k^2 = \frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}, \quad k'^2 = \frac{2m\left(E + V_0\right)}{\hbar^2}, \quad z_0^2 = \frac{2mV_0a^2}{\hbar^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

(It is the lecture notes' eq. (1.31), p. 6, turned upside down.)

<Part label="a" points={2}>

As the energy $E$ goes to zero, $ka \to 0$. What happens to $k'a$? Show that $\tan\delta$ goes to zero, so that we may take $\delta \to 0$ as $ka \to 0$.

The second box is our addition: for small $ka$, $\tan\delta \simeq ka$ times a number that depends on $z_0$. Give that number.

<Hint>

$(k'a)^2 = (ka)^2 + z_0^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For small $ka$ use $\tan ka \simeq ka$. Which of the terms in the numerator and in the denominator stay finite, and which grow as $1/ka$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ka" label="$k'a \to$" expr="z0"
  vars={{ z0: [0.5, 5] }}
  names={{ z0: "z_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "0", hint: "Inside the well the particle keeps the kinetic energy V₀ even when E goes to zero." },
    { expr: "z0^2", hint: "z₀² is the limit of (k′a)². Take the square root." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-slope" label="$\tan\delta \simeq ka\;\times$" expr="tan(z0)/z0 - 1"
  vars={{ z0: [0.3, 1.3] }}
  names={{ z0: "z_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1 - tan(z0)/z0", hint: "Check the sign. For a shallow well tan z₀ is larger than z₀, and an attractive well pulls the wave in: δ is positive." },
    { expr: "z0/tan(z0) - 1", hint: "That is minus the numerator, 1 − z₀ cot z₀. It still has to be divided by the large term of the denominator, z₀ cot z₀ / ka." },
    { expr: "tan(z0)/z0", hint: "The numerator is 1 − z₀ cot z₀, not 1: the second term of the numerator stays finite when ka goes to zero." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At zero energy the wave outside the well becomes infinitely long, while inside the well it keeps a finite wavelength. The mismatch sends one term of the denominator to infinity.

<Step title="The wavenumber inside">

From the definitions, $(k'a)^2 = (ka)^2 + z_0^2$ (notes p. 6, eq. (1.33)), so $k'a \to z_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The limit of tan δ">

For small $ka$, $\tan ka \simeq ka$ and $\frac{k'}{k} = \frac{k'a}{ka} \simeq \frac{z_0}{ka}$. In the numerator the two factors of $ka$ cancel. In the denominator the second term grows as $1/ka$:

<Formula label="The phase shift at low energy" tex="\tan\delta \simeq \frac{1 - z_0\cot z_0}{ka + \dfrac{z_0\cot z_0}{ka}} \simeq ka\;\frac{1 - z_0\cot z_0}{z_0\cot z_0} = ka\left(\frac{\tan z_0}{z_0} - 1\right) \to 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

A phase shift is fixed only up to multiples of $\pi$ (notes p. 3, below eq. (1.8)). Since $\tan\delta \to 0$, we may choose $\delta = 0$ at zero energy, and follow $\delta$ continuously from there.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a very shallow well, $\tan z_0/z_0 - 1 \simeq z_0^2/3$ is small and positive: a weak attraction pulls the wave in a little, as the lesson says a positive $\delta$ does. The argument fails when $\cot z_0 = 0$, that is for $z_0 = \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}, \dots$: then the second term of the denominator is no longer large (it is about $-ka/2$), the denominator is about $ka/2$, and $\tan\delta \simeq 2/ka$ goes to infinity, so $\delta$ tends to $\frac{\pi}{2}$ rather than 0. Those are exactly the depths at which the well is about to gain a new bound state. Part (d) stays away from them.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

What is the limit of $\tan\delta$ as $E \to \infty$? Explain in detail.

<Hint>

At high energy $k'/k \to 1$. But $\tan ka$ and $\cot k'a$ keep oscillating, so do not look at the terms one by one. Multiply numerator and denominator by $ka\,\cos ka\,\sin k'a$ and collect sines and cosines of $k'a - ka$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

How large is $k'a - ka$ at high energy? Use $(k'a)^2 - (ka)^2 = z_0^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-inf" label="As $E \to \infty$, $\tan\delta$" choices={["goes to 0, falling off as z₀²/(2ka)", "goes to 0, falling off exponentially in ka", "has no limit: tan ka and cot k′a keep oscillating", "goes to −tan z₀"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At high energy the well is a small correction: the wave inside is almost the wave outside. The phase shift is the small extra phase collected while crossing the well.

<Step title="A form without poles">

Write $u = ka$ and $w = k'a$. Multiplying numerator and denominator by $u\cos u\,\sin w$:

<Formula label="The phase shift written with sines and cosines" tex="\tan\delta = \frac{u\cos u\,\sin w - w\sin u\,\cos w}{u\sin u\,\sin w + w\cos u\,\cos w} = \frac{u\sin\left(w - u\right) - \left(w - u\right)\sin u\,\cos w}{w\cos\left(w - u\right) - \left(w - u\right)\sin u\,\sin w}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The limit">

Since $w^2 - u^2 = z_0^2$, the difference $w - u = z_0^2/(w + u) \simeq z_0^2/(2u)$ goes to zero. Then the numerator goes to $u\cdot\frac{z_0^2}{2u} = \frac{z_0^2}{2}$, a fixed number (its second term dies out), while the denominator grows as $w \simeq u$:

<Formula label="The phase shift at high energy" tex="\tan\delta \simeq \frac{z_0^2}{2\,ka} \to 0 \qquad (E \to \infty)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

    { expr: "cos(phi)/r", hint: "The angle φ is measured around the z axis, so what counts is the distance from that axis, r sin θ, not r." },
    { expr: "1/(r*sin(theta)*cos(phi))", hint: "That is 1/x, which is the derivative of tan φ. Multiply by cos²φ to get the derivative of φ itself." },
    { expr: "-sin(phi)/(r*sin(theta))", hint: "That is ∂φ/∂x." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Differentiate the three relations that give $r$, $\theta$ and $\phi$ in terms of $x$, $y$ and $z$, then put the spherical expressions back in.

<Step title="The radius">

From $r^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2$: $\frac{\partial r}{\partial x} = \frac{x}{r}$, and likewise for $y$ and $z$.

</Step>

<Step title="The polar angle">

From $\cos\theta = z/r$: $-\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x} = -\frac{zx}{r^3}$, so $\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x} = \frac{zx}{r^3\sin\theta} = \frac{\cos\theta\cos\phi}{r}$, and the same with $y$. For $z$: $-\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial z} = \frac1r - \frac{z^2}{r^3} = \frac{\sin^2\theta}{r}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The angle around the z axis">

From $\tan\phi = y/x$: $\frac{1}{\cos^2\phi}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} = -\frac{y}{x^2}$ and $\frac{1}{\cos^2\phi}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} = \frac1x$, and $\phi$ does not depend on $z$.

</Step>

<Step title="All nine">

<Formula label="Derivatives of the spherical coordinates" tex="\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial r}{\partial x} &= \sin\theta\cos\phi, & \frac{\partial r}{\partial y} &= \sin\theta\sin\phi, & \frac{\partial r}{\partial z} &= \cos\theta, \\ \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x} &= \frac{\cos\theta\cos\phi}{r}, & \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial y} &= \frac{\cos\theta\sin\phi}{r}, & \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial z} &= -\frac{\sin\theta}{r}, \\ \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial x} &= -\frac{\sin\phi}{r\sin\theta}, & \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial y} &= \frac{\cos\phi}{r\sin\theta}, & \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z} &= 0 \end{aligned}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The three derivatives of $r$ are the components of the unit vector that points away from the origin, and their squares add up to 1. The derivatives of the angles carry $1/r$, as their units require. Moving along that unit vector changes neither angle: for example $\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}\sin\theta\cos\phi + \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial y}\sin\theta\sin\phi + \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial z}\cos\theta = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Use the results of part (a) to write $\hat L_x$, $\hat L_y$ and $\hat L_z$ as differential operators in spherical coordinates.

The boxes take $\hat L_x$: divide it by $i\hbar$ and give the function that stands in front of each derivative.

<Hint>

In position space $\hat L_x = \frac{\hbar}{i}\left(y\frac{\partial}{\partial z} - z\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)$. The chain rule writes $\frac{\partial}{\partial z}$ as $\frac{\partial r}{\partial z}\frac{\partial}{\partial r} + \frac{\partial\theta}{\partial z}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial z}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$, and the same for $x$ and $y$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Collect the terms in front of $\frac{\partial}{\partial r}$, $\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$ separately. The radial ones must cancel.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-lx-th" label="In $\hat L_x/(i\hbar)$, in front of $\partial/\partial\theta$:" expr="sin(phi)"
  vars={{ phi: [0.2, 1.3] }}
  names={{ phi: "\\phi" }}
  mistakes={[

  ]} />

<Answer id="p6-lx-ph" label="In $\hat L_x/(i\hbar)$, in front of $\partial/\partial\phi$:" expr="cos(phi)/tan(theta)"
  vars={{ theta: [0.3, 1.3], phi: [0.2, 1.3] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta", phi: "\\phi" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-cos(phi)/tan(theta)", hint: "That is the function for L̂x divided by ħ/i. Dividing by iħ instead changes the sign, because 1/i = −i." },
    { expr: "cos(phi)*tan(theta)", hint: "The ratio is upside down: −z ∂φ/∂y = −r cos θ · cos φ/(r sin θ)." },
    { expr: "sin(phi)/tan(theta)", hint: "That belongs to L̂y. For L̂x the derivative needed is ∂φ/∂y, which holds cos φ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each Cartesian derivative is a sum of three spherical ones, with the coefficients of part (a). In the combinations that make up angular momentum the radial derivatives cancel.

<Step title="The component along x">

In $y\frac{\partial}{\partial z} - z\frac{\partial}{\partial y}$ the three kinds of terms are:

<Formula label="Collecting the terms of Lx" tex="\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial r}&: \quad y\cos\theta - z\sin\theta\sin\phi = 0, \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}&: \quad -\frac{y\sin\theta}{r} - \frac{z\cos\theta\sin\phi}{r} = -\sin\phi, \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}&: \quad -\frac{z\cos\phi}{r\sin\theta} = -\cot\theta\cos\phi \end{aligned}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The other two">

For $\hat L_y$, in $z\frac{\partial}{\partial x} - x\frac{\partial}{\partial z}$: the radial terms cancel again, the terms with $\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}$ add up to $\cos\phi$, and the term with $\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$ is $-\cot\theta\sin\phi$. For $\hat L_z$, in $x\frac{\partial}{\partial y} - y\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$: the radial terms and the terms with $\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}$ cancel, and the terms with $\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$ add up to $\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\phi = 1$. Multiplying by $\frac{\hbar}{i} = -i\hbar$:

<Formula label="Angular momentum operators in spherical coordinates" tex="\hat L_x = i\hbar\left(\sin\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + \cot\theta\cos\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\right), \qquad \hat L_y = i\hbar\left(-\cos\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + \cot\theta\sin\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\right), \qquad \hat L_z = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\hat L_z$ is the lesson's result (notes p. 6, eq. (2.18)). No operator contains $r$ or $\frac{\partial}{\partial r}$: angular momentum acts on the angles only, which is why it commutes with any function of $r$, and so with a central potential. On the $x$ axis ($\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$, $\phi = 0$) the operator $\hat L_x$ vanishes, as it should: a rotation about the $x$ axis does not move a point on that axis.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Compute $\hat L_x^2$, $\hat L_y^2$ and $\hat L_z^2$ as differential operators in spherical coordinates, and use them to derive the expected form of $\hat L^2$.

The box is our addition, a check on the way: in $\hat L_x^2/(-\hbar^2)$, which function stands in front of the first derivative $\partial/\partial\theta$?

<Hint>

Let the operator act twice on a function, and remember that the first operator also differentiates the coefficients of the second: $\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}$ acts on $\cot\theta$, and $\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$ on $\sin\phi$ and $\cos\phi$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each square has five kinds of terms: $\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2}$, $\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}$, $\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta\,\partial\phi}$, $\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}$ and $\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$. In the sum the mixed terms and the terms with a single $\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$ cancel.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-lx2" label="In $\hat L_x^2/(-\hbar^2)$, in front of $\partial/\partial\theta$:" expr="cos(phi)^2/tan(theta)"
  vars={{ theta: [0.3, 1.3], phi: [0.2, 1.3] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta", phi: "\\phi" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "0", hint: "A first derivative does appear: in the second term of the operator, ∂/∂φ acts on the sin φ in front of ∂/∂θ." },
    { expr: "1/tan(theta)", hint: "That is the function in the sum L̂². In L̂x² alone it comes with cos²φ. The sin²φ comes from L̂y²." },
    { expr: "sin(phi)^2/tan(theta)", hint: "That one belongs to L̂y². In L̂x² the term comes from cot θ cos φ ∂/∂φ acting on sin φ, which gives another cos φ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Squaring a differential operator means applying it twice. The derivative in the first factor also hits the coefficients of the second, and that is where the terms with a single derivative come from.

<Step title="The squares">

By part (b), $\hat L_x/(i\hbar) = \sin\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + \cot\theta\cos\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$, and $\hat L_x^2$ is $-\hbar^2$ times this operator applied twice. Doing that, and using $\frac{d}{d\theta}\cot\theta = -\frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}$:

<Formula label="The square of Lx" tex="\frac{\hat L_x^2}{-\hbar^2} = \sin^2\phi\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2} + \cot^2\theta\cos^2\phi\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2} + 2\cot\theta\sin\phi\cos\phi\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta\,\partial\phi} + \cot\theta\cos^2\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} - \sin\phi\cos\phi\left(\frac{1}{\sin^2\theta} + \cot^2\theta\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

The same steps for $\hat L_y$, where $\sin\phi \to -\cos\phi$ and $\cos\phi \to \sin\phi$:

<Formula label="The square of Ly" tex="\frac{\hat L_y^2}{-\hbar^2} = \cos^2\phi\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2} + \cot^2\theta\sin^2\phi\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2} - 2\cot\theta\sin\phi\cos\phi\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta\,\partial\phi} + \cot\theta\sin^2\phi\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + \sin\phi\cos\phi\left(\frac{1}{\sin^2\theta} + \cot^2\theta\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

And $\hat L_z^2 = -\hbar^2\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The sum">

The mixed terms and the terms with a single $\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}$ cancel. The others combine with $\sin^2\phi + \cos^2\phi = 1$ and $\cot^2\theta + 1 = \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}$:

<Formula label="The square of the angular momentum in spherical coordinates" tex="\hat L^2 = \hat L_x^2 + \hat L_y^2 + \hat L_z^2 = -\hbar^2\left(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\theta^2} + \cot\theta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}\right) = -\hbar^2\left(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}\right)" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.19) (our own derivation)" />

This is the form the lesson quotes, and the angular part of the Laplacian (notes p. 3, eq. (1.10)).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Try it on the function $\cos\theta$, which is $z/r$: $\hat L^2\cos\theta = -\hbar^2\left(-\cos\theta - \cot\theta\sin\theta\right) = 2\hbar^2\cos\theta$. That is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue $\hbar^2\,l(l + 1)$ for $l = 1$, as the lesson's Legendre polynomial $P_1 = \cos\theta$ should be. And $\hat L_z\cos\theta = 0$: it is the state with $m = 0$.

</Step>
