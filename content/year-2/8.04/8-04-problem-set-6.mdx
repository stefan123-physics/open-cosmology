## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first (Problems 5 and 6 also need a computer). Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit shown. Unless a box asks for more digits, give 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `a/2 + a*cos(w*t)/pi^2`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Write the symbols of the box exactly as the box names them: `hbar` stands for $\hbar$, `w` for $\omega$, `xi` for $\xi$, `z0` for $z_0$, `Lx`, `Ly`, `nx` and `ny` for $L_x$, $L_y$, $n_x$ and $n_y$. The boxes know `sinh`, `cosh` and `tanh`, but not `coth`: write `cosh(xi)/sinh(xi)`.

MIT gives the points per problem. The splits between parts are ours. Several parts ask you to show something: those parts have no box, so open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons: $\Psi(x, t)$ for a wavefunction that depends on time, $\psi(x)$ for an energy eigenstate, $\langle x\rangle$ and $\langle p\rangle$ for expectation values, $k$ for the wavenumber where the particle is classically allowed and $\kappa$ for the decay constant where it is forbidden. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Particle in a square well" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A particle of mass $m$ moves in an infinite square well of width $a$, with walls at $x = 0$ and $x = a$. At time $t = 0$ its wavefunction is

<Formula label="The wavefunction at time zero" tex="\Psi(x, 0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\,\sqrt{\frac2a}\,\sin\frac{2\pi x}{a} + \sqrt{\frac23}\,\sqrt{\frac2a}\,\sin\frac{3\pi x}{a}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Is $\Psi$ an energy eigenstate? Find $\Psi(x, t)$.

<Hint>

Compare the two terms with the energy eigenstates $\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{2/a}\,\sin(n\pi x/a)$ of the lesson. Which values of $n$ appear?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each energy eigenstate evolves on its own, with its own phase factor $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-eigen" choices={["No: it is a sum of two eigenstates with different energies, so it has no single energy", "Yes: a sum of energy eigenstates is again an energy eigenstate", "Yes: its energy is the weighted average of the two energies", "No: it is not normalized, so it cannot be an eigenstate"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Read off the two eigenstates in the sum and give each its own phase.

<Step title="Not an eigenstate">

With the lesson's states and energies (lecture notes, Chapter 11, eq. (1.11)),

<Formula label="States and energies of the infinite square well" tex="\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac2a}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}, \qquad E_n = \frac{\hbar^2\pi^2n^2}{2ma^2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 3, eq. (1.11)" />

the wavefunction is $\Psi(x, 0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\psi_2 + \sqrt{\frac23}\,\psi_3$. Applying $\hat H$ gives $\frac{1}{\sqrt3}E_2\psi_2 + \sqrt{\frac23}E_3\psi_3$, which is not a number times $\Psi$, because $E_2 \ne E_3$. So $\Psi$ is not an energy eigenstate. It is normalized: $\frac13 + \frac23 = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="The wavefunction at time t">

Each stationary state carries the phase $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ (Chapter 10, eq. (1.15)):

<Formula label="The wavefunction at time t" tex="\Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\,\sqrt{\frac2a}\,\sin\frac{2\pi x}{a}\;e^{-iE_2t/\hbar} + \sqrt{\frac23}\,\sqrt{\frac2a}\,\sin\frac{3\pi x}{a}\;e^{-iE_3t/\hbar}, \qquad E_2 = \frac{4\hbar^2\pi^2}{2ma^2}, \quad E_3 = \frac{9\hbar^2\pi^2}{2ma^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3, eq. (1.15)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ both phases are 1 and the starting wavefunction comes back. The two phases turn at different rates, so $\Psi(x, t)$ is not one function of $x$ times one function of $t$: it is not stationary.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

The energy is measured at time $t$. What is the probability of finding each of the three values $\dfrac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2ma^2}$, $\dfrac{4\hbar^2\pi^2}{2ma^2}$ and $\dfrac{9\hbar^2\pi^2}{2ma^2}$? Type each as a decimal number.

<Hint>

The three values are $E_1$, $E_2$ and $E_3$. The probability of finding $E_n$ is the squared size of the coefficient of $\psi_n$ in $\Psi(x, t)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-prob1" label="Probability of $\frac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2ma^2}$:" value="0" tolerance="0.001" calc="p1-prob1" />

<Answer id="p1-prob2" label="Probability of $\frac{4\hbar^2\pi^2}{2ma^2}$:" value="0.3333" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-prob2" />

<Answer id="p1-prob3" label="Probability of $\frac{9\hbar^2\pi^2}{2ma^2}$:" value="0.6667" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-prob3" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The probability of an energy is the squared size of the coefficient of its eigenstate (the lesson on [observables and measurement](/courses/8-04/observables-measurement-uncertainty)).

<Step title="The three probabilities">

At time $t$ the coefficients are $c_1 = 0$, $c_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}$ and $c_3 = \sqrt{\frac23}\,e^{-iE_3t/\hbar}$. A phase has size 1, so

<Formula label="Probabilities of the three energies" tex="P(E_1) = 0, \qquad P(E_2) = \left|c_2\right|^2 = \frac13, \qquad P(E_3) = \left|c_3\right|^2 = \frac23" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The three add up to 1, and none depends on the time: the probabilities of energies never change, although the wavefunction does.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Find the expectation value $\langle x\rangle$ at time $t$. Write it with the frequency

<Formula label="The beat frequency of the two states" tex="\omega \equiv \frac{E_3 - E_2}{\hbar} = \frac{5\pi^2\hbar}{2ma^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (the name ω is ours)" />

so that your answer contains only $a$, $\omega$ and $t$.

<Hint>

$|\Psi|^2$ has three pieces: one from $\psi_2$ alone, one from $\psi_3$ alone, and a cross term that oscillates as $\cos\omega t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

By symmetry each state alone gives $\langle x\rangle = a/2$. For the cross term you need $\int_0^a x\,\psi_2\psi_3\,dx$. Turn the product of sines into a difference of cosines and integrate by parts.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-x" label="$\langle x\rangle =$" expr="a/2 - 32*sqrt(2)*a*cos(w*t)/(25*pi^2)"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 2], w: [0.5, 2], t: [0.2, 1.2] }}
  names={{ w: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "a/2 + 32*sqrt(2)*a*cos(w*t)/(25*pi^2)", hint: "Check the sign of the integral of x ψ₂ψ₃: it is negative, so the particle starts left of the centre." },
    { expr: "a/2 - 16*sqrt(2)*a*cos(w*t)/(25*pi^2)", hint: "There are two cross terms, complex conjugates of each other. Together they give twice the real part." },
    { expr: "a/2", hint: "That holds for a stationary state. Here the cross term between ψ₂ and ψ₃ does not vanish." },

<Solution>

**The idea.** Use the lesson's formula for the expectation value in a superposition of two stationary states: two constant terms and an oscillating cross term.

<Step title="The three terms">

With real $\psi_2$, $\psi_3$ and the coefficients of part (a), the formula of Chapter 10, eq. (1.18) reads

<Formula label="Expectation value of x in the superposition" tex="\langle x\rangle = \frac13\int_0^a x\,\psi_2^2\,dx + \frac23\int_0^a x\,\psi_3^2\,dx + 2\,\frac{\sqrt2}{3}\,\cos\omega t\int_0^a x\,\psi_2\psi_3\,dx" cite="reading1" at="p. 3, eq. (1.18)" />

Each $\psi_n^2$ is symmetric about the centre of the well, so the first two integrals are both $a/2$, and together the constant terms give $\left(\frac13 + \frac23\right)\frac a2 = \frac a2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The cross integral">

With $\sin A\sin B = \frac12\left[\cos(A - B) - \cos(A + B)\right]$,

<Formula label="The cross integral, step 1" tex="\int_0^a x\,\psi_2\psi_3\,dx = \frac1a\int_0^a x\left[\cos\frac{\pi x}{a} - \cos\frac{5\pi x}{a}\right]dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Integrating by parts, $\int_0^a x\cos(q\pi x/a)\,dx = \frac{a^2}{q^2\pi^2}\left[\cos q\pi - 1\right]$, which is $-\frac{2a^2}{q^2\pi^2}$ for odd $q$. So

<Formula label="The cross integral, result" tex="\int_0^a x\,\psi_2\psi_3\,dx = \frac1a\left[-\frac{2a^2}{\pi^2} + \frac{2a^2}{25\pi^2}\right] = -\frac{48\,a}{25\pi^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The result">

<Formula label="Expectation value of x at time t" tex="\langle x\rangle = \frac a2 - \frac{32\sqrt2}{25\pi^2}\,a\cos\omega t \approx a\left(0.5 - 0.183\cos\omega t\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The average position swings between $0.317a$ and $0.683a$: it stays inside the well, as it must. It swings about the centre, and with the frequency $(E_3 - E_2)/\hbar$ set by the difference of the two energies, as the lesson says. If one coefficient were zero the cross term would be gone and $\langle x\rangle = a/2$ at all times.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Find the expectation value $\langle p\rangle$ at time $t$. Write it with $\hbar$, $a$, $\omega$ and $t$ only (no $m$).

<Hint>

There is a quick way: how is $\langle p\rangle$ related to the rate of change of $\langle x\rangle$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\langle p\rangle = m\,d\langle x\rangle/dt$ (the lesson on [the time dependence of expectation values](/courses/8-04/momentum-space-expectation-values) shows it for a free particle; a potential $V(x)$ commutes with $\hat x$ and changes nothing). Afterwards remove $m$ with the definition of $\omega$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-p" label="$\langle p\rangle =$" expr="16*sqrt(2)*hbar*sin(w*t)/(5*a)"

  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", w: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[

<Solution>

**The idea.** The momentum is the mass times the velocity of the average position.

<Step title="Differentiate">

<Formula label="Expectation value of p at time t" tex="\langle p\rangle = m\,\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \frac{32\sqrt2}{25\pi^2}\,ma\,\omega\,\sin\omega t = \frac{32\sqrt2}{25\pi^2}\cdot\frac{5\pi^2\hbar}{2a}\,\sin\omega t = \frac{16\sqrt2}{5}\,\frac{\hbar}{a}\,\sin\omega t" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

In the third step $m\omega = 5\pi^2\hbar/(2a^2)$ was used.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Directly: each real state alone has $\langle p\rangle = 0$, and the cross term needs $\int_0^a\psi_2\left(-i\hbar\,\psi_3'\right)dx = -i\hbar\cdot\frac{6\pi}{a^2}\int_0^a\sin\frac{2\pi x}{a}\cos\frac{3\pi x}{a}\,dx = -i\hbar\cdot\frac{6\pi}{a^2}\cdot\left(-\frac{4a}{5\pi}\right) = \frac{24\,i\hbar}{5a}$. Twice the real part of $\frac{\sqrt2}{3}\,e^{-i\omega t}\cdot\frac{24\,i\hbar}{5a}$ is $\frac{16\sqrt2}{5}\frac{\hbar}{a}\sin\omega t$: the same. The units are those of $\hbar$ over a length, a momentum. The largest value, $4.53\,\hbar/a$, is smaller than the root-mean-square momentum $\sqrt{\langle p^2\rangle} = \sqrt{\frac13(2\pi)^2 + \frac23(3\pi)^2}\;\hbar/a = 8.51\,\hbar/a$, as the size of an average must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Non-degeneracy of bound states in one dimension" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

This is our own problem. MIT's sheet gives only the title and a pointer to a textbook, which is not used here.

Two different states are called degenerate when they have the same energy. The lesson states without proof that bound states in one dimension are never degenerate. Prove it here. Let $\psi_1(x)$ and $\psi_2(x)$ be two real solutions of the time-independent Schrödinger equation on the whole line, with the same potential $V(x)$ and the same energy $E$:

<Formula label="Two solutions with one energy" tex="-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\psi_1'' + V\psi_1 = E\psi_1, \qquad -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\psi_2'' + V\psi_2 = E\psi_2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Define the function $W(x) = \psi_1\psi_2' - \psi_2\psi_1'$ (it is called the Wronskian of the two solutions). Show that $W$ does not depend on $x$.

<Hint>

Multiply the first equation by $\psi_2$ and the second by $\psi_1$, and subtract.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Then compare what is left with the derivative $dW/dx$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The terms with $V$ and $E$ cancel between the two equations, and what is left is the derivative of $W$.

<Step title="Subtract the equations">

$\psi_2$ times the first equation minus $\psi_1$ times the second: the terms $V\psi_1\psi_2$ and $E\psi_1\psi_2$ appear in both and cancel. This leaves

<Formula label="What is left after subtracting" tex="-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\psi_2\psi_1'' - \psi_1\psi_2''\right) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Recognize the derivative">

<Formula label="The derivative of W" tex="\frac{dW}{dx} = \psi_1'\psi_2' + \psi_1\psi_2'' - \psi_2'\psi_1' - \psi_2\psi_1'' = \psi_1\psi_2'' - \psi_2\psi_1'' = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

So $W$ is the same number at every $x$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Nothing was assumed about the potential except that it is the same in both equations, and nothing about bound states. The result holds for any two solutions with equal energy. (At a delta function the slopes jump, but $\psi_1'$ and $\psi_2'$ jump in proportion to $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$, so $W$ does not jump.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Now let both solutions be bound states: $\psi_1 \to 0$ and $\psi_2 \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$, with slopes that stay finite. Show that $\psi_2 = c\,\psi_1$ for some constant $c$, so that the two are the same state.

<Hint>

What is the value of the constant $W$? Look far away.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$W = 0$ says $\psi_2'/\psi_2 = \psi_1'/\psi_1$ wherever neither function is zero. Both sides are derivatives of logarithms.

</Hint>

<Hint>

At a point where $\psi_1 = 0$, the slope $\psi_1'$ is not zero (otherwise $\psi_1$ would vanish everywhere). Use that to carry the constant $c$ across the zero.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The constant $W$ can be read off at infinity, where it is zero. A vanishing $W$ forces the two functions to be proportional.

<Step title="W is zero">

Far away both $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ go to zero while the slopes stay finite, so $W = \psi_1\psi_2' - \psi_2\psi_1' \to 0$. By part (a) $W$ is constant, so $W = 0$ at every $x$.

</Step>

<Step title="Between the zeros">

On a stretch where neither function vanishes, divide $\psi_1\psi_2' = \psi_2\psi_1'$ by $\psi_1\psi_2$:

<Formula label="Equal logarithmic derivatives" tex="\frac{\psi_2'}{\psi_2} = \frac{\psi_1'}{\psi_1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln\left|\psi_2\right| = \ln\left|\psi_1\right| + \text{constant} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \psi_2 = c\,\psi_1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Across a zero">

Let $\psi_1(x_0) = 0$. Then $\psi_1'(x_0) \ne 0$: a solution of a second-order equation with value and slope both zero at one point is zero everywhere. $W = 0$ at $x_0$ gives $\psi_2(x_0)\,\psi_1'(x_0) = 0$, so $\psi_2(x_0) = 0$ too: the two functions have the same zeros. Left and right of $x_0$ they are proportional, say with constants $c_-$ and $c_+$. The slopes $\psi_2' = c_\pm\psi_1'$ must agree at $x_0$, where $\psi_1' \ne 0$, so $c_- = c_+$. One constant $c$ serves for the whole line.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The infinite square well and the finite well of the lessons agree: every bound state there has its own energy. The proof used two things that part (c) and part (d) will take away in turn: that the wavefunctions vanish far away, and that a solution cannot vanish on a whole stretch.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

A free particle on a circle (the lesson on stationary states) does have degenerate states: for $k \ne 0$, $\psi_1 = \cos kx$ and $\psi_2 = \sin kx$ have the same energy. Compute their $W$, and say which step of the proof fails.

<Hint>

Put the two functions into $W = \psi_1\psi_2' - \psi_2\psi_1'$ and simplify.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-w" label="$W =$" expr="k"
  vars={{ k: [0.5, 3], x: [0.3, 1.2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-k", hint: "Check the order: W = ψ₁ψ₂′ − ψ₂ψ₁′, with ψ₁ = cos kx and ψ₂ = sin kx." },
    { expr: "k*cos(2*k*x)", hint: "A sign slip: the two terms add to k(cos² kx + sin² kx), not to k(cos² kx − sin² kx)." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "The derivative of cos kx is −k sin kx, so the second term is +k sin² kx and does not cancel the first." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On a circle nothing forces $W$ to vanish, because there is no "far away".

<Step title="Compute W">

<Formula label="W for the cosine and the sine" tex="W = \cos kx\cdot k\cos kx - \sin kx\cdot\left(-k\sin kx\right) = k\left(\cos^2kx + \sin^2kx\right) = k" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The step that fails">

$W$ is constant, as part (a) says it must be, but it is not zero. The first step of part (b) fails: on a circle the wavefunctions do not go to zero anywhere, so $W$ cannot be read off as zero. The two states are normalizable there for another reason (the circle has a finite length), and they are truly different states.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$W$ has the units of $\psi^2$ over a length, and $k$ is one over a length, which fits for these unit-free functions. For $k = 0$ the sine is zero everywhere and only one state is left: the lesson's one level without degeneracy.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Two infinite square wells of the same width lie side by side on the $x$ axis, with a stretch between them where the potential is infinite. A state that sits in the left well only and a state that sits in the right well only then have the same energy and both vanish far away. Which step of the proof in part (b) fails here?

<Hint>

Where is $\psi_1$ zero? Where is $\psi_2$ zero? Follow the proof through the stretch between the wells.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-two" choices={["The step across a zero: each state vanishes on a whole stretch, not only at single points, so the constant c may differ from one well to the other", "The step W = 0: these states do not vanish far away", "Part (a): W is not constant when the potential has walls", "No step fails: the two states are in fact the same state"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With an infinite barrier a solution may vanish on a whole interval, and the argument that carries $c$ across a zero breaks down.

<Step title="Follow the proof">

Let $\psi_1$ live in the left well and $\psi_2$ in the right well. Both vanish far away, and $W = 0$ everywhere: inside the left well $\psi_2$ and $\psi_2'$ are both zero, inside the right well $\psi_1$ and $\psi_1'$ are, and elsewhere all four are. So the first step of part (b) holds. In the left well $\psi_2 = c\,\psi_1$ holds with $c = 0$. In the right well it cannot hold for any $c$, since $\psi_1 = 0$ there and $\psi_2$ is not.

</Step>

<Step title="The step that fails">

The step across a zero assumed that $\psi_1' \ne 0$ wherever $\psi_1 = 0$. That rests on the equation having finite terms. Where $V$ is infinite the wavefunction is zero on a whole stretch, slope included, and the two wells are cut off from each other: the proportionality constant is free to change from one well to the other. So the theorem needs a potential that does not split the line into separate pieces.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Make the barrier between the wells high but finite and the degeneracy goes away: the states now leak into each other's well, the proof applies, and the two lowest states become one even and one odd state with slightly different energies. Problem 6 shows such a pair of wells.

</Step>

</Solution>

**The idea.** The square looks the same when $x$ and $y$ are swapped, so $(n_x, n_y)$ and $(n_y, n_x)$ share an energy. Anything beyond that is a coincidence of whole numbers.

<Step title="The degeneracy from symmetry">

For $n_x \ne n_y$ the states $\psi_{n_x, n_y}$ and $\psi_{n_y, n_x}$ are different functions with the same energy, since $n_x^2 + n_y^2$ does not care about the order. Swapping $x \leftrightarrow y$ is a reflection in the diagonal through the corner at the origin. It leaves the square and so the potential unchanged, and it turns one of the two states into the other. Examples: $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 1)$ with $n_x^2 + n_y^2 = 5$; $(1, 3)$ and $(3, 1)$ with 10. States with $n_x = n_y$, such as $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 2)$, have no partner of this kind. The reflections $x \to L - x$ and $y \to L - y$ and the half turn turn each state into itself, up to a sign. The quarter turns and the reflection in the other diagonal swap $(n_x, n_y)$ and $(n_y, n_x)$, up to a sign, so they explain the same degeneracy and no other.

</Step>

</Step>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Hydrogen ion using the square well model" points={10} mit="Problem 6">

In Problem Set 5 (its Problem 7) the hydrogen atom was imitated by a square well. The atom has the Bohr radius $a_0$ and the ground state energy $E_0$, in the units that MIT's sheet uses:

<Formula label="Bohr radius and ground state energy of hydrogen" tex="a_0 = \frac{\hbar^2}{me^2}, \qquad E_0 = -\frac{e^2}{2a_0} = -13.6\ \text{eV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 3" />

Here $e$ is the charge of the proton. Note that $|E_0| = \hbar^2/(2ma_0^2)$. The square well is $V(x) = -V_0$ for $|x| < a_0$ and zero outside, with the depth chosen so that its ground state lies at $E_0$. MIT quotes the result of that problem: $z_0 = 1.3192$ and

<Formula label="The well that imitates a hydrogen atom" tex="V_0 = z_0^2\left|E_0\right| = 1.7402\left|E_0\right| = 23.67\ \text{eV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 3" />

The well stands for the pull of the proton, and its ground state for the electron's energy.

To imitate the hydrogen molecular ion H₂⁺ (two protons, one electron), put two such wells side by side, a small distance $2\gamma a_0$ apart, where $\gamma$ is a small positive number:

<Formula label="The potential of the model ion" tex="V(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & |x| < \gamma a_0 \\ -V_0, & \gamma a_0 < |x| < (2 + \gamma)a_0 \\ 0, & |x| > (2 + \gamma)a_0 \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4" />

Work with $\gamma = 0.2$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Use the shooting method to find the energy of the lowest state: the energy of an electron shared by the two protons. Give it in eV. Plot the wavefunction.

<Hint>

Use $u = x/a_0$ and measure energies in units of $|E_0|$: $\varepsilon = E/|E_0|$. What does the equation become? Remember $|E_0| = \hbar^2/(2ma_0^2)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The potential is even and the lowest state has no nodes, so it is even: start with $\psi(0) = 1$, $\psi'(0) = 0$ and integrate outwards to about $u = 8$. A bound state has $-z_0^2 < \varepsilon < 0$. One atom alone has $\varepsilon = -1$: try values a little below that.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-e" label="Lowest energy:" value="-17.26" unit="eV" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-e" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Clean out the units with $a_0$ and $|E_0|$, then shoot for the even state without nodes.

<Step title="The unit-free equation">

With $x = a_0u$, and dividing by $|E_0| = \hbar^2/(2ma_0^2)$:

<Formula label="The model ion, unit-free" tex="-\frac{d^2\psi}{du^2} + \left(v(u) - \varepsilon\right)\psi = 0, \qquad v(u) = \begin{cases} -z_0^2 = -1.7403, & 0.2 < |u| < 2.2 \\ 0, & \text{elsewhere} \end{cases}, \qquad \varepsilon = \frac{E}{\left|E_0\right|}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Shoot">

Start with $\psi(0) = 1$, $\psi'(0) = 0$ (Chapter 12, eqs. (4.2)–(4.3)) and integrate to $u = 8$. At $\varepsilon = -1.30$ the solution runs off upwards, at $\varepsilon = -1.20$ downwards. Halving the interval:

<Formula label="The lowest state of the model ion" tex="\varepsilon = -1.2693, \qquad E = -1.2693 \times 13.6\ \text{eV} = -17.26\ \text{eV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

The solution at this energy has no zeros. No state was found below it: for every trial value of $\varepsilon$ between $-1.74$ and $-1.27$ the even solution runs off upwards.

  curves={[
    { expr: "abs(u) < 0.2 ? cosh(1.12663*u) : (abs(u) < 2.2 ? 1.02549*cos(0.68628*(abs(u) - 0.2)) + 0.37304*sin(0.68628*(abs(u) - 0.2)) : 0.56769*exp(-1.12663*(abs(u) - 2.2)))" }

<Step title="Check the result">

The pieces can be matched by hand, as for the finite well. With $\kappa = \sqrt{-\varepsilon}$ and $k = \sqrt{z_0^2 - \kappa^2}$, the even solution is $\cosh\kappa u$ between the wells, a shifted cosine inside a well, and $e^{-\kappa u}$ outside. Continuity of $\psi'/\psi$ at both edges of the well gives

<Formula label="Exact condition for the even states of the two wells" tex="2k = \arctan\frac{\kappa}{k} + \arctan\left(\frac{\kappa}{k}\tanh\kappa\gamma\right), \qquad k^2 + \kappa^2 = z_0^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

whose root for $\gamma = 0.2$ is $\kappa = 1.1266$, $k = 0.6863$: $\varepsilon = -\kappa^2 = -1.2693$, the same. For wells far apart ($\gamma \to \infty$) the condition becomes $k = \arctan(\kappa/k)$, or $\kappa = k\tan k$: one atom alone, with $\varepsilon = -1$. The shared electron lies lower than that, by $0.2693\times 13.6 = 3.66$ eV.

</Step>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

MIT's sheet says: the binding energy of the ion is found by adding the positive energy of the repulsion between the two protons to the energy of part (a). Take the protons at the centres of the two wells (our reading of the model), so that they are $2(1 + \gamma)a_0 = 2.4\,a_0$ apart. What sum do you get, in eV? Compare with the real ion, and answer the last question (our addition): in this model, is the ion safe against falling apart into a hydrogen atom and a proton far away?

<Hint>

Two protons a distance $R$ apart repel with the energy $e^2/R$. Write $e^2$ with $|E_0|$ and $a_0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the last question compare the sum with the energy of the pieces: a hydrogen atom has $-13.6$ eV, and a proton far away adds nothing.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-sum" label="Electron energy plus repulsion:" value="-5.93" unit="eV" tolerance="2%" calc="p6-sum" />

<Answer id="p6-bound" choices={["No: its total energy lies above that of a hydrogen atom with the second proton far away", "Yes: its total energy is negative, and that is enough", "Yes: the shared electron lies lower than in one atom, and that is enough", "It cannot be said without the mass of the proton"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Add the repulsion of the protons, then compare with the energy of the separated pieces.

<Step title="The repulsion">

From $E_0 = -e^2/(2a_0)$, $e^2 = 2|E_0|a_0$. With $R = 2.4\,a_0$:

<Formula label="Repulsion of the two protons" tex="\frac{e^2}{R} = \frac{2\left|E_0\right|a_0}{2.4\,a_0} = 0.8333\times 13.6\ \text{eV} = 11.33\ \text{eV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The sum">

<Formula label="Total energy of the model ion" tex="E_\text{total} = -17.26\ \text{eV} + 11.33\ \text{eV} = -5.93\ \text{eV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

This is the energy of the ion measured from two protons and an electron all at rest far apart.

</Step>

<Step title="Compare with the real ion">

The real ion needs about 2.65 eV to fall apart into a hydrogen atom and a proton (a calculated value from NIST's reference data, not on MIT's sheet). Measured from three particles far apart its energy is therefore about $-13.6 - 2.65 = -16.25$ eV. The model's $-5.93$ eV has the right sign but is far too high. Worse, $-5.93$ eV lies above the $-13.6$ eV of a hydrogen atom with a proton far away, by 7.67 eV: in this model the ion would fall apart. Sharing lowers the electron's energy by 3.66 eV (part (a)), but the protons' repulsion costs 11.33 eV.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The model gets one thing right: an electron shared between two wells lies lower than in one well. It cannot get the number right. In the real ion the pulls of the two protons add up between them and deepen the potential there, while in the model each well keeps its depth $V_0$ and the two do not even overlap. A wavefunction in one dimension is also a poor copy of an electron cloud in three. The lesson on [the Born–Oppenheimer approximation](/courses/8-06/born-oppenheimer-approximation) in 8.06 treats the real ion.

</Step>
