Physicists learn a great deal from scattering: Rutherford found the atomic nucleus by scattering alpha particles off gold foil (notes p. 1). This lecture sets up scattering in one dimension in a form that carries over to three: a finite-range potential in front of a hard wall, so that everything sent in comes back, and all the information is in the phase of what returns (notes pp. 1–7). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 18; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example. The notes' figures are not reproduced. MIT's calendar also lists Levinson's theorem for this lecture; it is in the next chapter of the notes and on the next page.)

## A potential of finite range

Scattering is **elastic** when the particles do not change type, which usually needs low energies; at high energies new particles are created and things get complicated (notes p. 1). Scattering off a fixed target, and even collisions of two particles in centre-of-mass coordinates, can be studied as a particle moving in a potential. Here (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="Finite-range potential with a wall" tex="V(x) = \begin{cases} \infty, & x < 0 \\ U(x), & 0 < x < R \\ 0, & x > R \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.1)" />

The interesting part $U$ (a script V in the notes) does not reach beyond $R$, and the wall at $x = 0$ reflects everything. The half-line $x > 0$ prepares for three dimensions, where the radius $r$ is positive too (notes pp. 1–2).

**No potential** (notes p. 2). With $U = 0$, the solutions with $k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2$ that vanish at the wall combine an incoming $e^{-ikx}$ and an outgoing $e^{ikx}$ (eqs. (1.2)–(1.4)):

<Formula label="Free solution with a wall" tex="\phi(x) = -\frac{e^{-ikx}}{2i} + \frac{e^{ikx}}{2i} = \sin kx" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.5)" />

The two waves carry equal and opposite currents.

**With the potential** (notes pp. 2–3). Beyond $R$, keep the same incoming wave $-e^{-ikx}/2i$. The outgoing wave must have the same $k$, to have the same energy, so it can differ only by a factor, written $e^{2i\delta}$ (eqs. (1.6)–(1.7)). The **phase shift** $\delta$ cannot depend on $x$ (a phase linear in $x$ would change the momentum), and it must be real, since the outgoing wave carries away all the incoming current. It depends on the energy and on the potential; it is natural to take it between 0 and $2\pi$, but more convenient to let it take any real value, so that it varies continuously with $E$ (notes p. 3). So (notes p. 3)

<Formula label="Canonical solution outside the range" tex="\psi(x) = \frac{1}{2i}\left(-e^{-ikx} + e^{ikx + 2i\delta}\right) = e^{i\delta}\sin\left(kx + \delta\right), \qquad x > R" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.8)" />

Compared with $\sin kx$, every feature has moved by $\delta/k$: a small $\delta > 0$ pulls the wave in (the potential attracts), a small $\delta < 0$ pushes it out (it repels). Shifting $\delta$ by $\pi$ changes nothing, as the first form shows (notes p. 3).

**The scattered wave** (notes p. 3). Define it as what the potential adds: $\psi = \phi + \psi_s$. The incoming waves cancel, leaving an outgoing wave (eqs. (1.9)–(1.10)):

<Formula label="Scattered wave and scattering amplitude" tex="\psi_s(x) = e^{i\delta}\sin\delta\;e^{ikx} = A_s\,e^{ikx}, \qquad \left|A_s\right|^2 = \sin^2\delta" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eqs. (1.11)–(1.12)" />

$A_s$ is the **scattering amplitude**, and $\sin^2\delta$ measures the probability of scattering (properly normalized statements need wave packets).

## Time delay

The phase shift determines how long the particle lingers (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Time delay" tex="\Delta t = 2\hbar\,\delta'(E)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (1.13)" />

evaluated at the packet's central energy. A negative delay means the particle spends less time near the origin: an attractive potential speeds it up, or a repulsive one turns it back early. A positive delay means it lingers, slowed down or temporarily trapped (notes p. 4).

**Derivation** (notes p. 4). Build an incoming packet $\int_0^\infty dk\,g(k)e^{-ikx}e^{-iE(k)t/\hbar}$ with $g$ real and peaked at $k_0$. Its reflection follows from the canonical solution: flip the sign of $k$ in the exponent, flip the overall sign, and multiply by $e^{2i\delta(k)}$ (eqs. (1.14)–(1.15)). Stationary phase at $k_0$ for $kx + 2\delta(k) - E(k)t/\hbar$ gives (notes p. 4)

<Formula label="Path of the reflected peak" tex="x = \frac{\hbar k_0}{m}\left(t - 2\hbar\left.\frac{d\delta}{dE}\right|_{E(k_0)}\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eqs. (1.16)–(1.17)" />

Without a phase shift the peak would follow $x = v_0t$, with $v_0 = \hbar k_0/m$, so the delay is $2\hbar\delta'(E)$ (eq. (1.18)).

**Compared with a natural time** (notes p. 5). Using $\frac1\hbar\frac{dE}{dk} = v_0$, the delay is $\Delta t = \frac{2}{v_0}\frac{d\delta}{dk}$, and dividing by the time a free particle needs to go in to $R$ and out again (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Delay relative to the free transit time" tex="\frac1R\frac{d\delta}{dk} = \frac{\Delta t}{2R/v_0}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (1.22)" />

## An attractive well

Take a well of depth $V_0$ and width $a$ against the wall (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Attractive well against a wall" tex="V(x) = \begin{cases} \infty, & x < 0 \\ -V_0, & 0 < x < a \\ 0, & x > a \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (1.23)" />

For $E > 0$ the solution is the canonical one outside and $A\sin k'x$ inside (to vanish at the wall), with $k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2$ and $k'^2 = 2m(E + V_0)/\hbar^2$ (eqs. (1.24)–(1.25)). Matching $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at $x = a$ and dividing (notes p. 6, eqs. (1.26)–(1.28)):

<Formula label="Matching condition" tex="k\cot\left(ka + \delta\right) = k'\cot k'a" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (1.28)" />

which can be solved for $\cot\delta$ with the identity for $\cot(A + B)$ (eqs. (1.29)–(1.31)), or equivalently written as $\tan(ka + \delta) = \frac{k}{k'}\tan k'a$. With the usual well strength $z_0^2 = 2mV_0a^2/\hbar^2$, $k'a = \sqrt{z_0^2 + (ka)^2}$ (eqs. (1.32)–(1.33)).

<Plot variable="q" domain={[0, 4]} range={[-3.3, 1.3]} xLabel="ka" yLabel="δ, sin²δ"
  curves={[

    { expr: "sin(atan((q/sqrt(3.4 + q^2))*tan(sqrt(3.4 + q^2))) - q)^2", label: "sin²δ", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="Computed from the matching condition for z₀² = 3.40, the notes' example. The phase starts at 0 and falls towards −π; the scattering probability sin²δ peaks where |δ| = π/2. The formula needs no branch correction on this range, since k′a stays between π/2 and 3π/2." />

For $z_0^2 = 3.40$ the notes plot the results (notes pp. 6–7). The phase runs from 0 at zero energy towards $-\pi$ at high energy; the total excursion $\pi$ reflects the single bound state such a well would hold, a connection taken up next lecture. The scattering probability $\sin^2\delta$ peaks where $|\delta| = \pi/2$. The unit-free delay $\frac1a\frac{d\delta}{dk}$ is **negative**: over the well the particle gains kinetic energy $V_0$, speeds up and bounces back sooner. At energies far above $V_0$ the particle hardly notices the well: $\delta \to -\pi$, equivalent to no shift, $\sin^2\delta \to 0$, no delay, and the amplitude inside, $|A|$, approaches its free value 1.

<WorkedExample title="A moved wall and a shallow well">
<Problem>
(a) Replace the potential by a hard wall at $x = a$ instead of $x = 0$. Find $\delta$ and the time delay, and evaluate it for a 1.0 eV electron with $a = 0.10$ nm ($v_0 \approx 593$ km/s). (b) For the well with $z_0^2 = 3.40$ at $ka = 0.50$, evaluate $\delta$, $\sin^2\delta$ and the unit-free delay $\frac1a\frac{d\delta}{dk} \approx -1.49$ (found numerically). Interpret.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A hard core">
Outside, $\psi \propto \sin(k(x - a)) = \sin(kx - ka)$, so $\delta = -ka$: the wave is pushed out by $a$, as for any repulsive core. Then $\frac{d\delta}{dk} = -a$ and $\Delta t = \frac{2}{v_0}\frac{d\delta}{dk} = -\frac{2a}{v_0}$: the packet comes back early by exactly the time it would have needed to cross the distance $a$ and back. Numerically $-\frac{2 \times 1.0 \times 10^{-10}}{5.93 \times 10^5} \approx -3.4 \times 10^{-16}$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The shallow well">
$k'a = \sqrt{3.40 + 0.25} \approx 1.910$, and $\delta = \tan^{-1}\left(\frac{0.50}{1.910}\tan 1.910\right) - 0.50 \approx -1.137$, so $\sin^2\delta \approx 0.82$: strong scattering. The delay ratio $-1.49$ says the packet returns early by about one and a half free transit times $2a/v_0$, as the particle races across the well.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- With a hard wall at the origin and a potential of range $R$, the solution beyond $R$ is $e^{i\delta}\sin(kx + \delta)$, with a real phase shift $\delta(E)$; $\delta > 0$ pulls the wave in, $\delta < 0$ pushes it out.
- The scattered wave is $A_se^{ikx}$ with $A_s = e^{i\delta}\sin\delta$; the scattering probability is $\sin^2\delta$.
- A reflected packet is delayed by $\Delta t = 2\hbar\,d\delta/dE$, or $\frac1R\frac{d\delta}{dk}$ free transit times.
- For an attractive well the phase falls from 0 towards $-\pi$ and the delay is negative: the particle speeds up over the well.
