Born's rule makes $|\Psi|^2$ a probability density, and probabilities must add up to 1. But the wavefunction changes in time according to the Schrödinger equation: will a wavefunction that starts normalized stay normalized? This lecture shows that it does, because the Hamiltonian is a Hermitian operator, and finds along the way a probability current that flows like electric charge (notes pp. 1–8). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 6; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Normalization

For a particle in one dimension, $dP = |\Psi(x, t)|^2dx$ is the probability of finding it in the interval $dx$ around $x$ at time $t$, so (notes p. 1, eqs. (1.3)–(1.4))

<Formula label="Normalization in one dimension" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \Psi^*(x, t)\,\Psi(x, t)\,dx = 1" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.4)" />

**Conditions at infinity** (notes pp. 1–2). If $\Psi$ had a nonzero limit as $x \to \pm\infty$, the integral would be infinite. So, assuming the limits exist (square-integrable functions without limits are possible but do not seem to arise in practice), $\Psi \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$. For the spatial derivative it will be enough that it stays bounded there (eqs. (1.5)–(1.6)).

**The overall factor carries no physics** (notes p. 2). If $\Psi$ satisfies the normalization, $2\Psi$ does not, which seems to contradict the earlier statement that the overall factor of a wavefunction is unphysical. The way out: if $\int |\Psi|^2 dx = N$ is finite but not 1, take

<Formula label="Probability with an unnormalized wavefunction" tex="dP = \frac{1}{N}\left|\Psi\right|^2dx, \qquad N = \int dx\left|\Psi\right|^2" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eqs. (1.7)–(1.8)" />

which adds up to 1 and does not change when $\Psi$ is multiplied by any number. A wavefunction with $N < \infty$ is **normalizable**, or square-integrable, and $\Psi/\sqrt N$ is normalized (eqs. (1.10)–(1.11)). Some useful wavefunctions are not normalizable: the plane wave $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ has $|\Psi|^2 = 1$ everywhere, so its integral is infinite, and on its own it cannot represent one particle. Pleasantly, superpositions of infinitely many such waves can be square-integrable (notes p. 2).

## Does normalization last?

Define the **probability density** and its integral over all space (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Probability density and total probability" tex="\rho(x, t) \equiv \Psi^*(x, t)\,\Psi(x, t) = \left|\Psi(x, t)\right|^2, \qquad N(t) \equiv \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \rho(x, t)\,dx" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eqs. (2.3)–(2.4)" />

If $N(t_0) = 1$, it stays 1 provided $dN/dt = 0$ at all times, **conservation of probability** (eq. (2.6)). Differentiating under the integral and using the Schrödinger equation and its complex conjugate,

<Formula label="The equation and its complex conjugate" tex="\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat H\Psi, \qquad \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\hat H\Psi\right)^*" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eqs. (2.8)–(2.9)" />

gives (notes p. 4)

<Formula label="Rate of change of the total probability" tex="\frac{dN}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\hat H\Psi\right)^*\Psi\,dx - \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi^*\left(\hat H\Psi\right)dx\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.10)" />

So probability is conserved if $\hat H$ can be moved from acting on the first function to acting on the second without changing the integral. That is the defining property of a **Hermitian operator** (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Hermitian operator" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\hat H\Psi_1\right)^*\Psi_2\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi_1^*\left(\hat H\Psi_2\right)dx" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.12)" />

for any two wavefunctions satisfying the conditions at infinity; with $\Psi_1 = \Psi_2 = \Psi$ it is exactly what is needed.

**Hermitian conjugate** (notes p. 4). For any linear operator $\hat T$, the Hermitian conjugate $\hat T^\dagger$ is the linear operator defined by moving the action to the first function:

<Formula label="Hermitian conjugate" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi_1^*\left(\hat T\Psi_2\right)dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\hat T^\dagger\Psi_1\right)^*\Psi_2\,dx" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.13)" />

and $\hat T$ is Hermitian if $\hat T^\dagger = \hat T$ (eq. (2.14)). Hermitian operators matter enormously: their eigenvalues are real, their eigenstates can be chosen to form an orthonormal basis, and every observable in quantum mechanics is represented by one, with its eigenvalues as the possible results of measurement (notes p. 4). The question of whether normalization lasts has become: is $\hat H$ Hermitian?

## The probability current

The integrand of $dN/dt$ is $\partial\rho/\partial t$. With the explicit Hamiltonian the potential terms cancel (for a real potential), leaving only second derivatives (notes p. 5, eqs. (3.1)–(3.2)). That combination turns out to be a total derivative:

<Formula label="A total derivative" tex="\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar}{2im}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} - \Psi\frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\right) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right)\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (3.3)" />

using $z - z^* = 2i\,\text{Im}\,z$. This has the form of a conservation law, like that of electric charge, $\partial\rho/\partial t + \partial J/\partial x = 0$, with the **probability current** (notes p. 5)

<Formula label="Probability current" tex="J(x, t) \equiv \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right), \qquad \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial J}{\partial x} = 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eqs. (3.4)–(3.6)" />

**Units** (notes p. 6). In one dimension $\int dx\,|\Psi|^2$ has no units, so $\Psi$ has units of $L^{-1/2}$ (in $d$ dimensions, $L^{-d/2}$). Then $\Psi^*\partial\Psi/\partial x$ has units $1/L^2$, and $\hbar/m$ has units $L^2/T$, so $J$ has units $1/T$: a probability per unit time (eqs. (3.7)–(3.8)).

**Conservation, at last** (notes p. 6). Integrating the conservation law over all $x$:

<Formula label="Total probability is conserved" tex="\frac{dN}{dt} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\,dx = -\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}\,dx = -\left(J(\infty, t) - J(-\infty, t)\right) = 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eqs. (3.9)–(3.11)" />

The current vanishes at infinity because $\Psi \to 0$ there while $\partial\Psi/\partial x$ stays bounded. So $dN/dt = 0$: the condition on $\hat H$ found above holds for these wavefunctions, and normalization lasts.

**Probability in a segment** (notes p. 6). The probability of finding the particle in $[a, b]$ is $P_{ab} = \int_a^b \rho\,dx$, and

<Formula label="Flow through the ends of a segment" tex="\frac{dP_{ab}}{dt} = -\int_a^b\frac{\partial J}{\partial x}\,dx = -J(b, t) + J(a, t)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (3.13)" />

The probability inside changes only by flow through the ends: a positive current at $b$ carries probability out, and a positive current at $a$ brings it in.

## Three dimensions, and the analogy with charge

In three dimensions the same route (left as homework in the course) gives (notes p. 7)

<Formula label="Density and current in three dimensions" tex="\rho = \left|\Psi\right|^2, \qquad \vec J = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\Psi^*\nabla\Psi\right), \qquad \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eqs. (4.1)–(4.2)" />

Now $\Psi$ has units $L^{-3/2}$ and $\vec J$ is a probability per unit time per unit area (eqs. (4.3)–(4.4)). For a fixed region $V$ with boundary surface $S$, the probability $Q_V = \int_V \rho\,d^3x$ of finding the particle inside changes as (notes p. 7)

<Formula label="Flux through the boundary" tex="\frac{dQ_V}{dt} = -\int_V \nabla\cdot\vec J\,d^3x = -\oint_S \vec J\cdot d\vec a" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eqs. (4.6)–(4.7)" />

by Gauss's theorem: probability leaves a region only by flowing across its boundary. This is a perfect analogy with electromagnetism, where charge density, charge in a volume and current density obey the same law; in quantum mechanics it is probability that flows (notes pp. 7–8).

<WorkedExample title="Normalizing and measuring flow">
<Problem>
(a) Normalize $\Psi(x) = Ce^{-|x|/a}$ and find the probability that the particle is within $a$ of the origin. (b) Find the current of the plane wave $Ae^{ikx}$, and its value for an electron with $|A|^2 = 1\ \text{m}^{-1}$ and $k = 1.0 \times 10^{10}\ \text{m}^{-1}$ ($\hbar = 1.0546 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg). (c) Find the current of $Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx}$ with $A$ and $B$ real.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Normalization">
$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-2|x|/a}dx = 2 \times \frac{a}{2} = a$, so $|C|^2a = 1$ and $C = 1/\sqrt a$. The probability within $a$ of the origin is $\frac{2}{a}\int_0^a e^{-2x/a}dx = 1 - e^{-2} \approx 0.865$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A plane wave">
$\Psi^*\partial\Psi/\partial x = ik|A|^2$, whose imaginary part is $k|A|^2$, so $J = \frac{\hbar k}{m}|A|^2$: the density times the velocity $p/m$, as for a flowing fluid. Here $\hbar k/m_e \approx 1.16 \times 10^6$ m/s, so $J \approx 1.16 \times 10^6$ per second.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Two waves">
$\Psi^*\partial\Psi/\partial x = ik\left(Ae^{-ikx} + Be^{ikx}\right)\left(Ae^{ikx} - Be^{-ikx}\right) = ik\left(A^2 - B^2\right) + ikAB\left(e^{2ikx} - e^{-2ikx}\right)$. The last term is $ik \times 2iAB\sin 2kx$, which is real, so $J = \frac{\hbar k}{m}\left(A^2 - B^2\right)$: the right-moving flow minus the left-moving flow. A standing wave, $A = B$, carries no current.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Wavefunctions must vanish at infinity; with $N = \int |\Psi|^2dx$ finite, $dP = |\Psi|^2dx/N$, and the overall factor carries no physics.
- Normalization is preserved in time because the Hamiltonian is Hermitian: $\int (\hat H\Psi_1)^*\Psi_2\,dx = \int \Psi_1^*\hat H\Psi_2\,dx$.
- Observables are Hermitian operators, with real eigenvalues.
- The probability current $J = \frac{\hbar}{m}\text{Im}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\right)$ obeys $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial J}{\partial x} = 0$; in three dimensions $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$, just like electric charge.
