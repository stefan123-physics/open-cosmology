Quantum mechanics now moves to three dimensions. When the potential depends only on the distance from a centre, the angular part of the problem is governed by angular momentum, a new observable with surprising properties. This page covers the notes' first three sections up to the Legendre polynomials (notes pp. 1–9); the next page continues with the associated Legendre functions and the radial equation. (MIT's material for Lectures 20 and 21 is one set of lecture notes by Zwiebach; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example. The notes' figure is not reproduced. The notes write the angular momentum number as a script ℓ; in formulas this page writes it as $l$, which read-aloud can say.)

## Momentum generates translations

Angular momentum will generate rotations, just as momentum generates translations. To see the latter, apply the exponential of $\hat p = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d}{dx}$ to a wavefunction and recognize the Taylor series (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="Momentum generates translations" tex="e^{i\hat pa/\hbar}\psi(x) = e^{a\frac{d}{dx}}\psi(x) = \psi(x) + a\psi'(x) + \frac{a^2}{2!}\psi''(x) + \cdots = \psi(x + a)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eqs. (1.1)–(1.3)" />

The operator shifts the whole wavefunction, by $-a$. Angular momentum comes in two kinds: **orbital**, from motion about a point, and **spin**, which even point particles carry; much of the mathematics is shared (notes p. 2).

## Central potentials

In three dimensions $\hat{\vec x} = (\hat x, \hat y, \hat z)$ and $\hat{\vec p} = \frac{\hbar}{i}\nabla$, with $[\hat x, \hat p_x] = [\hat y, \hat p_y] = [\hat z, \hat p_z] = i\hbar$ and all other commutators of coordinates and momenta zero (notes p. 2, eqs. (1.4)–(1.5)). A potential is **central** if it depends only on the distance $r$ from the origin, $V(\vec r) = V(r)$: it is spherically symmetric (notes p. 2). The equation $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V(r)\psi = E\psi$ then calls for spherical coordinates, in which (notes p. 2)

<Formula label="Laplacian in spherical coordinates" tex="\nabla^2 = \frac1r\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2}r + \frac{1}{r^2}\left(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.8)" />

The notes set two goals (notes p. 3). First, the angular bracket is $-\hat L^2/\hbar^2$, with $\hat L^2 = \hat L_x^2 + \hat L_y^2 + \hat L_z^2$, so that the equation becomes (eqs. (1.10)–(1.13))

<Formula label="Schrödinger equation in a central potential" tex="-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac1r\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2}\left(r\psi\right) + \frac{\hat L^2}{2mr^2}\psi + V(r)\psi = E\psi" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.13)" />

Second, the same equation describes two particles whose interaction depends only on their separation, $V(|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|)$, such as the proton and electron of hydrogen (eq. (1.14)); that reduction is not carried out in these notes.

## The angular momentum operators

Classically $\vec L = \vec r \times \vec p$. The quantum operators copy it (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Angular momentum operators" tex="\hat L_x = \hat y\hat p_z - \hat z\hat p_y, \qquad \hat L_y = \hat z\hat p_x - \hat x\hat p_z, \qquad \hat L_z = \hat x\hat p_y - \hat y\hat p_x" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (2.2)" />

There is no ordering ambiguity, since each product pairs a coordinate with a momentum along a different axis, and these commute. Each component is Hermitian: $(\hat y\hat p_z)^\dagger = \hat p_z\hat y = \hat y\hat p_z$, and so on, so all three are observables (notes p. 4, eqs. (2.3)–(2.5)).

**Commutators** (notes p. 4). In $[\hat L_x, \hat L_y]$ only $\hat z$ and $\hat p_z$ fail to commute, so only two terms survive: $\hat y[\hat p_z, \hat z]\hat p_x + \hat x[\hat z, \hat p_z]\hat p_y = i\hbar(\hat x\hat p_y - \hat y\hat p_x)$ (eqs. (2.6)–(2.7)). Cycling $x \to y \to z \to x$ gives the rest, the **algebra of angular momentum** (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Algebra of angular momentum" tex="\left[\hat L_x, \hat L_y\right] = i\hbar\hat L_z, \qquad \left[\hat L_y, \hat L_z\right] = i\hbar\hat L_x, \qquad \left[\hat L_z, \hat L_x\right] = i\hbar\hat L_y" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.9)" />

Commutators of angular momenta give angular momenta, with no coordinates or momenta left. Spin operators cannot be written with coordinates and momenta (their simplest form is two-by-two matrices), yet obey exactly the same algebra (notes p. 5, eq. (2.10)).

**No two components together** (notes pp. 5–6). Suppose $\psi_0$ were an eigenstate of both $\hat L_x$ and $\hat L_y$, with eigenvalues $\lambda_x$ and $\lambda_y$. The first commutator acting on it gives $i\hbar\hat L_z\psi_0 = (\lambda_x\lambda_y - \lambda_y\lambda_x)\psi_0 = 0$; then the other two give $\lambda_x = \lambda_y = 0$ (eqs. (2.11)–(2.13)). Only the trivial state annihilated by all three components escapes, so no two components can be measured together.

**$\hat L^2$ commutes with each component** (notes p. 6). Using $[A, BC] = [A, B]C + B[A, C]$,

<Formula label="L̂² commutes with L̂z" tex="\left[\hat L_z, \hat L^2\right] = \left[\hat L_z, \hat L_x\right]\hat L_x + \hat L_x\left[\hat L_z, \hat L_x\right] + \left[\hat L_z, \hat L_y\right]\hat L_y + \hat L_y\left[\hat L_z, \hat L_y\right] = 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.14)" />

since the four terms are $i\hbar(\hat L_y\hat L_x + \hat L_x\hat L_y - \hat L_x\hat L_y - \hat L_y\hat L_x)$. (In the last line of the notes' eq. (2.14) the fourth term has its two operators in the wrong order; the result, zero, stands.) $\hat L^2$ is a **Casimir operator**: it commutes with all three components. So $\hat L_z$ and $\hat L^2$ can share eigenstates.

**In spherical coordinates** (notes p. 6). With $x = r\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y = r\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z = r\cos\theta$ (eq. (2.15)), the chain rule gives $\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi} = x\frac{\partial}{\partial y} - y\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$, so (eqs. (2.16)–(2.18))

<Formula label="L̂z and L̂² in spherical coordinates" tex="\hat L_z = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}, \qquad \hat L^2 = -\hbar^2\left(\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta} + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eqs. (2.18)–(2.19)" />

$\hat L_z$ is like a momentum along the circle of $\phi$, confirming that it generates rotations about the $z$ axis; $\hat L^2$ is the angular part of the Laplacian, as promised.

## Eigenstates of L̂z and L̂²

Look for functions $\psi_{l m}(\theta, \phi)$ with (notes p. 7)

<Formula label="Joint eigenvalue equations" tex="\hat L_z\psi_{l m} = \hbar m\,\psi_{l m}, \qquad \hat L^2\psi_{l m} = \hbar^2l(l + 1)\,\psi_{l m}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eq. (3.1)" />

with $m$ and $l$ real and unit-free. The eigenvalues of $\hat L^2$ cannot be negative, since $(\psi, \hat L^2\psi) = (\hat L_x\psi, \hat L_x\psi) + (\hat L_y\psi, \hat L_y\psi) + (\hat L_z\psi, \hat L_z\psi) \ge 0$ (eqs. (3.2)–(3.3)), and $l(l + 1)$ covers all non-negative values as $l \ge 0$ (notes p. 7).

**The $\phi$ part** (notes p. 7). $\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\phi} = \hbar m\psi$ gives $\psi_{l m} = e^{im\phi}P_l^m(\theta)$. The angles $\phi$ and $\phi + 2\pi$ are the same point, so $e^{2\pi im} = 1$ and (eqs. (3.4)–(3.8))

<Formula label="m is an integer" tex="\psi_{l m}(\theta, \phi) = e^{im\phi}\,P_l^m(\theta), \qquad m \in \mathbb{Z}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eqs. (3.5) and (3.8)" />

(A footnote in the notes adds that requiring a sign change after $2\pi$ instead does not give a consistent set of states.)

**The $\theta$ part** (notes p. 8). Inserting $e^{im\phi}$ into the $\hat L^2$ equation and writing $x = \cos\theta$, so that $\sin\theta\frac{d}{d\theta} = -(1 - x^2)\frac{d}{dx}$, gives the equation for the **associated Legendre functions** (eqs. (3.9)–(3.15)):

<Formula label="Associated Legendre equation" tex="\frac{d}{dx}\left(\left(1 - x^2\right)\frac{dP_l^m}{dx}\right) + \left(l(l + 1) - \frac{m^2}{1 - x^2}\right)P_l^m(x) = 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 8, eq. (3.15)" />

## Legendre's equation and the quantization of ℓ

For $m = 0$ this is **Legendre's equation** for $P_l(x)$ (notes p. 8, eq. (3.16)). With a power series $\sum a_kx^k$, regular at $x = 0$, the coefficient of $x^k$ gives (notes p. 9)

<Formula label="Recursion for Legendre's equation" tex="a_{k+2} = -\frac{l(l + 1) - k(k + 1)}{(k + 1)(k + 2)}\,a_k" cite="mit" at="notes p. 9, eqs. (3.18)–(3.19)" />

Unless the series stops, it diverges at $x = \pm1$, that is at $\theta = 0$ or $\pi$. It stops if $l(l + 1) = k(k + 1)$ for some integer $k \ge 0$: take $l = k$, and $P_l$ is a polynomial of degree $l$. So (notes p. 9)

<Formula label="Quantization of ℓ" tex="l = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots" cite="mit" at="notes p. 9, eq. (3.20)" />

just as $m$ was quantized. The **Legendre polynomials** follow from Rodrigues' formula (the notes spell it "Rodriguez") or a generating function, and have definite parity (notes p. 9):

<Formula label="Rodrigues' formula and the first Legendre polynomials" tex="P_l(x) = \frac{1}{2^l\,l!}\frac{d^l}{dx^l}\left(x^2 - 1\right)^l; \qquad P_0 = 1, \quad P_1 = x, \quad P_2 = \frac12\left(3x^2 - 1\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 9, eqs. (3.21) and (3.23)" />

<WorkedExample title="Checking the Legendre machinery">
<Problem>
(a) Check that $P_2 = \frac12(3x^2 - 1)$ solves Legendre's equation with $l = 2$. (b) Build the $l = 2$ solution from the recursion starting with $a_0 = 1$. (c) Get $P_2$ from Rodrigues' formula. (d) For $l = 2$, compare $\sqrt{\langle\hat L^2\rangle}$ with the largest possible $L_z$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Direct check">
In expanded form the equation is $(1 - x^2)P'' - 2xP' + l(l + 1)P = 0$. With $P_2' = 3x$ and $P_2'' = 3$: $3(1 - x^2) - 6x^2 + 6 \cdot \frac12(3x^2 - 1) = 3 - 9x^2 + 9x^2 - 3 = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Recursion">
Taking $a_1 = 0$ (the even series), $a_2 = -\frac{6 - 0}{1 \times 2}a_0 = -3$, and $a_4 = -\frac{6 - 6}{3 \times 4}a_2 = 0$: the series stops at $1 - 3x^2 = -2P_2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Rodrigues">
$\frac{1}{8}\frac{d^2}{dx^2}(x^4 - 2x^2 + 1) = \frac18(12x^2 - 4) = \frac12(3x^2 - 1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Never along the axis">
$\hat L^2 = \hbar^2l(l + 1) = 6\hbar^2$, so the size is $\sqrt6\,\hbar \approx 2.45\hbar$, more than the largest $L_z = l\hbar = 2\hbar$ (the next page shows that $|m| \le l$). The angular momentum never lies exactly along $z$: that would make $L_x$ and $L_y$ both zero, which the algebra forbids.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Momentum generates translations, $e^{i\hat pa/\hbar}\psi(x) = \psi(x + a)$; angular momentum generates rotations.
- In a central potential the angular part of $\nabla^2$ is $-\hat L^2/(\hbar^2r^2)$.
- $[\hat L_x, \hat L_y] = i\hbar\hat L_z$ and cycles: no two components share nontrivial eigenstates, but $\hat L^2$ commutes with all three.
- Joint eigenstates of $\hat L_z$ and $\hat L^2$ are $e^{im\phi}P_l^m(\cos\theta)$ with integer $m$; regularity of Legendre's equation forces $l = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ and the Legendre polynomials.
