When the potential does not depend on time, the Schrödinger equation has an especially useful class of solutions, the stationary states. Finding them means solving an eigenvalue equation for the Hamiltonian, and this lecture works out the conditions its solutions must satisfy where the potential misbehaves, then solves it completely for a free particle on a circle (notes pp. 1–8). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 10; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example.)

## Separating space and time

With a time-independent potential $V(x)$ the Schrödinger equation is (notes p. 1)

<Formula label="Schrödinger equation with a time-independent potential" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} = \hat H\Psi = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x)\right)\Psi(x, t)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.1)" />

A **stationary state** is a solution in which position and time dependence factorize, $\Psi(x, t) = g(t)\,\psi(x)$ (eq. (1.2)). Substituting, $g$ passes through $\hat H$, and dividing by $g\psi$ gives (notes p. 1)

<Formula label="Separated equation" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{1}{g(t)}\frac{dg(t)}{dt} = \frac{1}{\psi(x)}\hat H\psi(x)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.4)" />

The left side depends only on $t$ and the right only on $x$ (a time-dependent potential would spoil this), so both must equal one constant $E$, with units of energy (notes p. 1). The time equation $i\hbar\,dg/dt = Eg$ gives $g = e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ (notes pp. 1–2, eqs. (1.5)–(1.6)), and the space equation is the **time-independent Schrödinger equation**, an eigenvalue equation for the Hermitian operator $\hat H$, so $E$ is real (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Time-independent Schrödinger equation" tex="\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2} + V(x)\right)\psi(x) = E\,\psi(x)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eqs. (1.7)–(1.8)" />

<Formula label="Stationary state" tex="\Psi(x, t) = e^{-iEt/\hbar}\,\psi(x), \qquad E \in \mathbb{R}, \quad \hat H\psi = E\psi" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.9)" />

The whole $\Psi$ is then an eigenstate of $\hat H$ too, since the time factor passes through (eq. (1.10)). A complex $E$ would also wreck normalization: $\int|\Psi|^2dx$ would carry a factor $e^{2\,\text{Im}(E)\,t/\hbar}$ that changes with time, so normalization forces $\text{Im}\,E = 0$, and then simply $\int|\psi|^2dx = 1$ (notes p. 2, eqs. (1.11)–(1.12)). In a stationary state $\langle\hat H\rangle = E$, and since it is an eigenstate of $\hat H$, the energy has zero uncertainty (notes p. 2, eq. (1.13)).

## What "stationary" means

Two observations (notes p. 3).

**(1)** For any operator $\hat Q$ with no explicit time dependence, the phases $e^{iEt/\hbar}$ from $\Psi^*$ and $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ from $\Psi$ cancel inside the integral, so $\langle\hat Q\rangle$ in a stationary state is the same at all times (eq. (1.14)). That is why these states are called stationary.

**(2)** A superposition of stationary states with different energies is not stationary: the two terms carry different time factors, so the sum cannot be factorized. For $\Psi = c_1e^{-iE_1t/\hbar}\psi_1 + c_2e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\psi_2$, expanding $\langle\hat Q\rangle$ gives two constant terms and two cross terms, and Hermiticity makes the cross terms complex conjugates of each other (notes p. 3, eqs. (1.15)–(1.17)):

<Formula label="Expectation value in a superposition" tex="\langle\hat Q\rangle = \left|c_1\right|^2\left(\psi_1, \hat Q\psi_1\right) + \left|c_2\right|^2\left(\psi_2, \hat Q\psi_2\right) + 2\,\text{Re}\left(c_1^*c_2\,e^{i(E_1 - E_2)t/\hbar}\int dx\,\psi_1^*\hat Q\psi_2\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.18)" />

It oscillates in time, at angular frequency $(E_1 - E_2)/\hbar$, whenever $E_1 \ne E_2$ and $(\psi_1, \hat Q\psi_2) \ne 0$; and it is real, as an expectation value of a Hermitian operator must be.

## Energy eigenstates and boundary conditions

The time-independent equation $\hat H\psi = E\psi$ usually allows only certain energies: like a matrix, it can have a discrete set of eigenvalues, though continuous ranges of energies also occur (notes pp. 3–4). Labelling the solutions $\psi_1, \psi_2, \dots$ with energies $E_1, E_2, \dots$, they can be chosen orthonormal and complete, $\int\psi_i^*\psi_j\,dx = \delta_{ij}$ (eqs. (2.20)–(2.21)). Written as (notes p. 4)

<Formula label="The equation for ψ''" tex="\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = -\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\left(E - V(x)\right)\psi" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.22)" />

it ties the curvature of $\psi$ to the potential. The potentials considered may be continuous, or piecewise continuous with jumps; may be unbounded; may contain delta functions (but not their powers or derivatives); and may become $+\infty$ beyond some points, which act as **hard walls** (notes p. 4).

**$\psi$ is continuous** (notes p. 4). If $\psi$ jumped, $\psi'$ would contain a delta function and $\psi''$ the derivative of one; then the right side, and so the potential, would need derivatives of delta functions, which are excluded.

**The slope** depends on the potential (notes pp. 4–5):

1. $V$ continuous: then $\psi''$ is continuous, and so is $\psi'$.
2. $V$ with finite jumps: $\psi''$ has finite jumps (continuous $\psi$ times a jumping $V$), so $\psi'$ is still continuous, though not smooth.
3. $V$ with delta functions: $\psi''$ contains delta functions, so $\psi'$ has finite jumps.
4. A hard wall at $x = a$ (finite $V$ to the left, infinite to the right): $\psi$ vanishes for $x \ge a$; its slope is finite just to the left and zero to the right, so it jumps.

In short (notes p. 5, eq. (2.23)): **$\psi$ and $\psi'$ are continuous, unless the potential has delta functions or hard walls, where $\psi'$ may have finite jumps.**

**A second argument for a finite step** (notes pp. 5–6). Integrate the equation from $a - \epsilon$ to $a + \epsilon$. The left side is a total derivative, so (eqs. (2.24)–(2.27))

<Formula label="Jump in the slope" tex="\lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\left(\left.\frac{d\psi}{dx}\right|_{a + \epsilon} - \left.\frac{d\psi}{dx}\right|_{a - \epsilon}\right) = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\int_{a - \epsilon}^{a + \epsilon}dx\left(V(x) - E\right)\psi(x)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.27)" />

For a finite step the integrand stays finite while the interval shrinks to nothing, so the jump is zero (eq. (2.28)). Integrating once more from below $a$ shows that $\psi$ itself has no jump either, since $\psi'$ is bounded (notes p. 6, eqs. (2.29)–(2.31)). (The page's remark: for a delta function in $V$ the same integral stays finite as $\epsilon \to 0$, which is exactly the slope jump of case 3.)

## A free particle on a circle

Take a circle of circumference $L$: the line with $x$ and $x + L$ identified, so $\psi(x + L) = \psi(x)$, and with it all derivatives are periodic (notes p. 6, eqs. (3.1)–(3.2)). The particle is free, $V = 0$.

**No negative energies** (notes pp. 6–7). Multiply $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' = E\psi$ by $\psi^*$, integrate over the circle (with $\psi$ normalized, the right side becomes $E$), and integrate by parts; the boundary terms at $0$ and $L$ cancel by periodicity, leaving

<Formula label="Energy on the circle is not negative" tex="E = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int_0^L dx\left|\frac{d\psi}{dx}\right|^2 \ge 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eq. (3.7)" />

with $E = 0$ only for a constant (nonzero) $\psi$.

**Solving** (notes pp. 7–8). With $k^2 \equiv 2mE/\hbar^2 \ge 0$, so that $E = \hbar^2k^2/(2m)$ and $p = \hbar k$ as usual, the equation is $\psi'' = -k^2\psi$ (eqs. (3.9)–(3.11)). The solution $e^{ikx}$ is also a momentum eigenstate, and periodicity demands $e^{ikL} = 1$, so $kL = 2\pi n$ with $n$ any integer: momentum is quantized because the wavenumber is (eqs. (3.12)–(3.14)). Normalizing over the circle (notes pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="Energy eigenstates on a circle" tex="\psi_n(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt L}\,e^{2\pi inx/L}, \qquad E_n = \frac{\hbar^2k_n^2}{2m} = \frac{2\pi^2\hbar^2n^2}{mL^2}, \qquad n \in \mathbb{Z}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 8, eqs. (3.17)–(3.18)" />

There are infinitely many, and they are **degenerate**: $E_n$ depends only on $|n|$, so $\psi_n$ and $\psi_{-n}$ share an energy. Only $\psi_0 = 1/\sqrt L$, the constant with zero energy, stands alone (notes p. 8). Degenerate states must differ in some other observable, and here it is momentum: $+2\pi\hbar n/L$ against $-2\pi\hbar n/L$. Any combination of degenerate eigenstates is again an eigenstate with the same energy (eqs. (3.19)–(3.20)), so $\cos k_nx$ and $\sin k_nx$ are real energy eigenstates as well, but not momentum eigenstates; only the exponentials are eigenstates of both $\hat H$ and $\hat p$ (eq. (3.21)).

The $\psi_n$ are orthonormal, $\int_0^L\psi_n^*\psi_m\,dx = \delta_{mn}$, automatically, since they are eigenstates of the Hermitian $\hat p$ with all different eigenvalues (eq. (3.22)); and they are complete: any periodic initial state is a Fourier series, which then evolves simply by attaching each energy's phase (notes p. 8):

<Formula label="Evolving a state on the circle" tex="\Psi(x, 0) = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}a_n\psi_n(x), \quad a_n = \int_0^L dx\,\psi_n^*(x)\Psi(x, 0), \quad \Psi(x, t) = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}a_n\psi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 8, eqs. (3.23)–(3.25)" />

<WorkedExample title="An electron on a ring">
<Problem>
An electron moves freely on a ring of circumference $L = 1.0$ nm. (a) Find $E_1$ and $E_2$ in eV ($\hbar c = 197.33$ eV nm, $m_ec^2 = 511{,}000$ eV). (b) The electron is in the state $(\psi_1 + \psi_2)/\sqrt2$ at $t = 0$. Find its probability density at time $t$, and the period with which it repeats ($\hbar = 6.582 \times 10^{-16}$ eV s).
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Energies">
$E_1 = \frac{2\pi^2(\hbar c)^2}{m_ec^2L^2} = \frac{2\pi^2 \times 197.33^2}{511{,}000 \times 1.0^2} \approx 1.50$ eV, and $E_2 = 4E_1 \approx 6.02$ eV.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A rotating density">
$\Psi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2L}}\left(e^{i(k_1x - E_1t/\hbar)} + e^{i(k_2x - E_2t/\hbar)}\right)$, so $|\Psi|^2 = \frac1L\left(1 + \cos\left((k_2 - k_1)x - (E_2 - E_1)t/\hbar\right)\right)$, with $k_2 - k_1 = 2\pi/L$. This is observation (2) at work: the density changes in time, and so does, for example, $\langle\cos(2\pi\hat x/L)\rangle$. The bump moves around the ring and returns after $T = 2\pi\hbar/(E_2 - E_1) = 2\pi \times 6.582 \times 10^{-16}/4.51 \approx 9.2 \times 10^{-16}$ s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- For a time-independent potential, stationary states $\Psi = e^{-iEt/\hbar}\psi(x)$ solve the Schrödinger equation when $\hat H\psi = E\psi$, with $E$ real; expectation values in them never change, while in superpositions of different energies they can oscillate at $(E_1 - E_2)/\hbar$.
- $\psi$ is always continuous; $\psi'$ is continuous except at delta functions and hard walls.
- On a circle the energy is never negative; the eigenstates $e^{2\pi inx/L}/\sqrt L$ have $E_n = 2\pi^2\hbar^2n^2/(mL^2)$, degenerate in pairs $\pm n$ that differ in momentum.
- Any periodic state is a Fourier series in these states and evolves by the phases $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$.
