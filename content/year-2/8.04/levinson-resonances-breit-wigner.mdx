This lecture draws two strong conclusions from the phase shift of the previous page: it counts bound states, and it reveals resonances, states that trap a particle for a while (notes pp. 1–9). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 19; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example. The notes' figures are not reproduced.)

## Levinson's theorem

The theorem relates the number $N_b$ of bound states to how far the phase shift falls as the energy runs from zero to infinity (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="Levinson's theorem" tex="N_b = \frac1\pi\left(\delta(0) - \delta(\infty)\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.1)" />

**Proof** (notes pp. 1–3). Take a potential of range $R$ with a wall at $x = 0$. Its bound states can be counted, but its scattering states form a continuum. To count them too, add a second wall far away, at $x = L$: a **regulator** that turns everything into bound states of a wide well, and whose effect fades as $L$ grows. Without the potential, the states are $\sin kx$ with $kL = n\pi$, so the number of states in $dk$ is (eqs. (1.2)–(1.3))

<Formula label="States per wavenumber without the potential" tex="dn = \frac{L}{\pi}\,dk" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.3)" />

With the potential, the positive-energy states are $e^{i\delta}\sin(kx + \delta)$ beyond $R$, and the far wall requires $kL + \delta(k) = n'\pi$, so $dn' = \frac{L}{\pi}dk + \frac1\pi\frac{d\delta}{dk}dk$ (eqs. (1.4)–(1.6)). Turning on the potential therefore removes $dn - dn' = -\frac1\pi\frac{d\delta}{dk}dk$ positive-energy states from each $dk$, and in all (notes p. 2)

<Formula label="Positive-energy states lost" tex="-\int_0^\infty\frac1\pi\frac{d\delta}{dk}\,dk = -\frac1\pi\left(\delta(\infty) - \delta(0)\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eqs. (1.7)–(1.8)" />

But as the potential is turned on continuously, every state can be followed: none disappears. The lost positive-energy states must have become negative-energy states, bound states, which proves the theorem (notes p. 3, eq. (1.9)).

## Resonances

**How negative can the delay be?** (notes p. 3). The delay is $\Delta t = 2\hbar\,\delta'(E)$. A large negative delay would mean the packet returns before it could have arrived, violating causality. The earliest return is perfect reflection at $x = R$, which saves the time $2R/v_0$ of going in and out (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Bound on the delay" tex="2\hbar\frac{d\delta}{dE} \gtrsim -\frac{2R}{v_0} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{d\delta}{dk} \gtrsim -R" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eqs. (2.1)–(2.3)" />

This is a heuristic argument; the notes add that the corrections to it become negligible for packets of high energy.

**How positive?** (notes p. 3). Without limit: a packet can be trapped in the potential for a long time, with a large amplitude inside. That is a **resonance**, a little like a bound state that eventually leaks out. An attractive well behind a barrier helps (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Well behind a barrier" tex="V(x) = \begin{cases} \infty, & x \le 0 \\ -V_0, & 0 < x < a \\ V_1, & a < x < 2a \\ 0, & x > 2a \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (2.4)" />

For energies between $0$ and $V_1$ the barrier region is forbidden. With $k'^2 = 2m(E + V_0)/\hbar^2$, $\kappa^2 = 2m(V_1 - E)/\hbar^2$ and $k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2$, the solution is $A\sin k'x$ in the well, a combination of $\cosh\kappa(x - a)$ and $\sinh\kappa(x - a)$ in the barrier (convenient for matching at $x = a$), and the canonical $e^{i\delta}\sin(kx + \delta)$ outside (notes p. 4, eqs. (2.5)–(2.7)). Matching gives (notes p. 4)

<Formula label="Phase shift of the well behind a barrier" tex="\tan\left(2ka + \delta\right) = \frac{k}{\kappa}\,\frac{\sin k'a\cosh\kappa a + \frac{k'}{\kappa}\cos k'a\sinh\kappa a}{\sin k'a\sinh\kappa a + \frac{k'}{\kappa}\cos k'a\cosh\kappa a}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.8)" />

With $z_0^2 = 2mV_0a^2/\hbar^2$, $z_1^2 = 2mV_1a^2/\hbar^2$ and $u = ka$, $(k'a)^2 = z_0^2 + u^2$ and $(\kappa a)^2 = z_1^2 - u^2$ (eqs. (2.9)–(2.10)). For $z_0^2 = 1$ and $z_1^2 = 5$ the notes plot the results (notes pp. 5–6). At first $\delta$ decreases linearly: a negative delay, as slow waves bounce off the outer edge of the barrier. Then $\delta$ crosses $-\pi/2$, so $\sin^2\delta = 1$, but nothing special happens inside: no resonance. Near $u = 1.8523$ ($E \approx 0.69V_1$), $\delta$ rises steeply and crosses $-\pi/2$ again: now the amplitude inside the well is very large and the time delay peaks at about 14 free transit times $4a/v_0$. That is a resonance.

## Modelling a resonance

Near a resonance the phase rises steeply by about $\pi$ (and $\delta$ matters only up to multiples of $\pi$). A simple model (notes p. 6):

<Formula label="Phase near a resonance" tex="\delta = \tan^{-1}\frac{\beta}{\alpha - k}, \qquad \alpha > 0, \; \beta > 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (3.1)" />

with $\alpha$ and $\beta$ wavenumbers; the phase climbs mainly between $\alpha - \beta$ and $\alpha + \beta$, sharply when $\beta \ll \alpha$. Then (notes p. 7)

<Formula label="Delay and scattering near the resonance" tex="\left.\frac{d\delta}{dk}\right|_{k = \alpha} = \frac1\beta, \qquad \left|A_s\right|^2 = \sin^2\delta = \frac{\beta^2}{\beta^2 + (\alpha - k)^2}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eq. (3.2)" />

so a small $\beta$ means a long delay. In terms of energy, near $k = \alpha$, $E - E_\alpha \approx \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(2\alpha)(k - \alpha)$ (eqs. (3.3)–(3.4)), which turns the scattering probability into the **Breit–Wigner distribution** (notes p. 7):

<Formula label="Breit–Wigner distribution" tex="\left|A_s\right|^2 \simeq \frac{\frac14\Gamma^2}{\left(E - E_\alpha\right)^2 + \frac14\Gamma^2}, \qquad \Gamma = \frac{2\hbar^2\alpha\beta}{m}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eqs. (3.5)–(3.7)" />

It peaks at 1 at $E = E_\alpha$ and falls to $\frac12$ at $E_\alpha \pm \Gamma/2$: $\Gamma$ is the **width at half maximum**, and a small $\Gamma$ is a narrow resonance.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-3, 3]} range={[0, 1.2]} xLabel="(E − E_α) / Γ" yLabel="|A_s|²"
  curves={[
    { expr: "1/(1 + 4*x^2)", label: "Breit–Wigner" }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 0.5, y: 0.5, label: "half maximum" }]}
  caption="The Breit–Wigner curve, computed from the formula above with energies measured from the resonance in units of the width Γ: the peak is 1 and the curve falls to one half at ±Γ/2." />

**Lifetime** (notes pp. 7–8). Define the resonance's lifetime $\tau \equiv \hbar/\Gamma = \frac{m}{2\hbar\alpha\beta}$ (eq. (3.8)). The time delay of a packet centred on the resonance is $\Delta t = 2\hbar\frac{d\delta}{dE} = \frac{2m}{\hbar\alpha\beta}$, so (notes p. 8, eqs. (3.9)–(3.10))

<Formula label="Lifetime and delay" tex="\tau = \frac{\hbar}{\Gamma} = \frac14\,\Delta t" cite="mit" at="notes p. 8, eq. (3.10)" />

Unstable particles are often called resonances. The notes' example is the Higgs boson, discovered in 2012, with a mass of 125 GeV, decaying for instance into two photons, two tau leptons or a bottom quark–antiquark pair, with a width of 4.07 MeV and so a lifetime of about $1.62 \times 10^{-22}$ s (notes p. 8). (The page's addition: 4.07 MeV is the width the Standard Model predicts; it is far too narrow to measure directly from the shape of the peak, and later indirect measurements, which assume the Higgs couples in the same way on and off its mass shell and are listed by the Particle Data Group, agree with it within their uncertainties.)

## Resonances in the complex k plane

Can resonances be found by an equation? Write the scattering amplitude as (notes p. 8)

<Formula label="Scattering amplitude through tan δ" tex="A_s = e^{i\delta}\sin\delta = \frac{\tan\delta}{1 - i\tan\delta}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 8, eq. (3.11)" />

At resonance $\delta = \pi/2$ and $A_s = i$. But the last form becomes **infinite** when $\tan\delta = -i$, which no real $\delta$ allows (eq. (3.12)). With the model phase, (notes p. 8)

<Formula label="The pole near a resonance" tex="A_s = \frac{\beta}{\left(\alpha - i\beta\right) - k}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 8, eq. (3.13)" />

which equals $i$ at $k = \alpha$ but has a **pole** at the complex wavenumber $k = \alpha - i\beta$: the real part gives the resonant energy, the imaginary part the width. The peak of $|A_s|$ on the real axis is the "shadow" of the infinity just below it (notes pp. 8–9). So resonances can be found by solving, for complex $k$ (notes p. 9),

<Formula label="Resonance condition" tex="\tan\delta(k) = -i" cite="mit" at="notes p. 9, eq. (3.14)" />

The complex plane has room for everything: for $k = i\kappa$ with $\kappa > 0$, $E = -\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m) < 0$, and bound states appear as poles of $A_s$ on the positive imaginary axis (notes p. 9, eq. (3.15)). Scattering states live on the real axis, resonances just below it, bound states on the imaginary axis.

The Higgs boson quoted above is the particle of the Higgs field, whose nonzero value in the vacuum gives other particles their masses. In the hot early universe that value is thought to have been zero, and the change to today's value is one of the phase transitions that cosmology studies. <Problem>
(a) On the previous page, the well with $z_0^2 = 3.40$ against a wall had a phase shift falling from 0 to $-\pi$. How many bound states does it have? Check directly. (b) Check the notes' Higgs lifetime from $\Gamma = 4.07$ MeV ($\hbar = 6.582 \times 10^{-22}$ MeV s), and find how far light travels in that time. (c) What is $|A_s|^2$ at $E = E_\alpha + \Gamma$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Levinson">
$N_b = \frac1\pi(0 + \pi) = 1$. Directly: with the wall, the bound states are the odd states of a symmetric well of half-width $a$, which need $z_0 > \pi/2$ for the first and $z_0 > 3\pi/2$ for the second; here $z_0 = \sqrt{3.40} \approx 1.84$, so exactly one.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The Higgs">
$\tau = \hbar/\Gamma = 6.582 \times 10^{-22}/4.07 \approx 1.62 \times 10^{-22}$ s. Light covers $c\tau \approx 3.0 \times 10^8 \times 1.62 \times 10^{-22} \approx 4.9 \times 10^{-14}$ m in that time, nearly sixty times the radius of a proton (about $0.84 \times 10^{-15}$ m).
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Off the peak">
$|A_s|^2 = \frac{1/4}{1 + 1/4} = 0.2$: one width away, the scattering probability is down to a fifth.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Levinson's theorem: $N_b = \frac1\pi(\delta(0) - \delta(\infty))$, proved by counting states between two walls.
- The time delay is bounded below by about $-2R/v_0$ (causality) but not above: long delays signal resonances, where a particle is trapped for a while.
- Near a resonance $|A_s|^2$ follows the Breit–Wigner curve with full width $\Gamma$ at half maximum, and the lifetime is $\tau = \hbar/\Gamma = \Delta t/4$.
- Resonances are poles of $A_s$ at complex $k = \alpha - i\beta$, solving $\tan\delta = -i$; bound states are poles on the positive imaginary axis.
