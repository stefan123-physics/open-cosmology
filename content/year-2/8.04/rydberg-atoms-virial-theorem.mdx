This page covers the last two parts of MIT's Lecture 23, after [the hydrogen spectrum and its wavefunctions](/courses/8-04/hydrogen-spectrum): the video segments "Rydberg atoms" (reading 1, transcript pp. 1–6) and "Orbits in the hydrogen atom" (reading 2, transcript pp. 1–3). Zwiebach's aim in both is intuition rather than new formalism: how big a highly excited atom is, and what its orbits look like. (These segments have no lecture notes on OCW, so the page follows the transcripts in its own words and draws its own figures; the transcripts have no printed page numbers, so pages are those of the PDFs. As on the earlier hydrogen pages, the script ℓ is written $l$ and the Coulomb energy is $-e^2/r$ in Gaussian units, with $E_n = -\frac{e^2}{2a_0}\frac{1}{n^2} \approx -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$.) The calculation of the turning points that completes the second part is on the next page, [hydrogen orbits: turning points and eccentricity](/courses/8-04/hydrogen-orbits-turning-points).

## What a Rydberg atom is

An atom is called a **Rydberg atom** when its outermost electron is in a state with a very large principal quantum number $n$ (reading 1, transcript p. 1). Large $n$ means an orbit far from the nucleus. Take a nucleus of charge $Ze$ with $Z$ electrons. The last electron, far outside, sees the charge $+Ze$ of the nucleus and the $Z - 1$ other electrons huddled close to it: a net charge of $+1$. To a good approximation it moves as the electron of a hydrogen atom (transcript p. 1).

So the first question is how large such an atom is. Size has no sharp definition for a wavefunction, but the expectation value of the distance $r$ from the nucleus is a sensible one. Looking at the wavefunctions, with their factor $e^{-r/(na_0)}$, one might guess a size of order $na_0$. Zwiebach admits that this is the guess he makes himself, and that it is completely wrong (transcript p. 1). The rest of the first part finds the right answer and then explains why the guess fails.

## The virial theorem

The key tool is the **virial theorem**. For any potential that goes as $1/r$, and so for hydrogen, the expectation values of the kinetic energy $T$ and the potential energy $V$ in a stationary state obey (transcript p. 2, board)

<Formula label="Virial theorem for a 1/r potential" tex="\langle V\rangle = -2\,\langle T\rangle" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 2, board" />

Zwiebach admits he never remembers where the factor 2 goes, and offers a picture instead. Draw the bound-state energy $E_n$ below zero on an energy axis. The kinetic energy is the same distance above zero: it is the binding energy with the opposite sign. This follows from the theorem, since $T + V = T - 2T = -T$, and $T + V$ is the total energy of the stationary state (transcript p. 2):

<Formula label="Kinetic and potential energy of a hydrogen state" tex="\langle T\rangle = -E_n, \qquad \langle V\rangle = -2\,\langle T\rangle = 2E_n" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 2, board" />

Both make sense: $E_n$ is negative, so the kinetic energy is positive and the potential energy negative. (In speaking, Zwiebach first says that the expectation value of $V$ is the energy; a student corrects him to $2E_n$, which he writes on the board.)

For the general case, which the lecture does not show (the page's own addition): for a potential proportional to $r^k$ the theorem reads $2\langle T\rangle = k\langle V\rangle$, and $k = -1$ gives the formula above. The same relation, for the time average of a classical system bound by gravity, is the [virial theorem of galaxies](/courses/8-902/virial-theorem), $2T + U = 0$.

Now apply this to hydrogen ($Z = 1$, as for a Rydberg atom). The potential is $V = -e^2/r$, and $2E_n = -\frac{e^2}{a_0}\frac{1}{n^2}$ (transcript pp. 2–3, board):

<Formula label="The expectation value of 1/r" tex="\left\langle -\frac{e^2}{r}\right\rangle = 2E_n = -\frac{e^2}{a_0}\,\frac{1}{n^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left\langle\frac{1}{r}\right\rangle = \frac{1}{n^2a_0}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 2–3, board" />

The factors 2, $e^2$ and the signs cancel. The virial theorem is exact and so is $E_n$, so this result is exact, and it does not depend on $l$. It points clearly to a typical radius of $n^2a_0$, not $na_0$ (transcript p. 3).

## The expected radius

The expectation value of $1/r$ is not the inverse of the expectation value of $r$: the average of the reciprocal of a random quantity is in general not the reciprocal of its average, and no theorem says otherwise (transcript p. 3). With much more effort one can compute $\langle r\rangle$ itself; Zwiebach quotes the result (transcript p. 3, board):

<Formula label="Expected radius of a hydrogen state" tex="\langle r\rangle = n^2a_0\left[1 + \frac12\left(1 - \frac{l(l + 1)}{n^2}\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3, board" />

Unlike $\langle 1/r\rangle$, this depends on $l$, though not strongly (transcript p. 3):

- For $l = 0$ the bracket is $\frac32$, so $\langle r\rangle = \frac32n^2a_0$. For the ground state, $n = 1$, this is the $\frac32a_0$ found on [the previous page](/courses/8-04/hydrogen-spectrum).
- For the largest value, $l = n - 1$, we have $\frac{l(l + 1)}{n^2} = 1 - \frac1n$, so the bracket is $1 + \frac{1}{2n}$ (the page's own step): $\langle r\rangle$ is $n^2a_0$ with a small correction when $n$ is large.

Either way the expected radius grows as $n^2a_0$. (A check of our own: since $1/r$ is a convex function, Jensen's inequality gives $\langle 1/r\rangle \ge 1/\langle r\rangle$, so $\langle r\rangle \ge n^2a_0$. The bracket is indeed never less than 1.)

## Why the guess na₀ fails

Zwiebach insists that when a guess turns out wrong, one should go back and find out why (transcript p. 3). The wavefunctions are (transcript p. 3, board)

<Formula label="Hydrogen eigenstates" tex="\psi_{nlm} = A\,r^l\,W_{nl}(r)\,e^{-r/(na_0)}\,Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi) = f_{nl}(r)\,Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3, board" />

where $W_{nl}$ is a polynomial of degree $n - l - 1$. (Zwiebach first says $n - l + 1$ and corrects it on p. 4; $n - l - 1$ is the degree found on the previous page.) The guess $na_0$ looked only at the exponential and forgot the polynomial.

To see how the probability is spread over $r$, ask for the probability $p(r)\,dr$ of finding the electron in the thin shell between $r$ and $r + dr$. It is $|\psi|^2$ times the volume element $r^2\,dr\,d\Omega$, integrated over all directions. The spherical harmonics are normalized, $\int |Y_{lm}|^2\,d\Omega = 1$, so the angles drop out (transcript p. 4, board):

<Formula label="Radial probability density" tex="p(r) = r^2\,|f_{nl}(r)|^2" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4, board" />

Now $r^l W_{nl}(r)$ is a polynomial that starts with $r^l$ and ends with $r^{n-1}$. At large radii the highest power wins. Keeping only it, $f_{nl} \propto r^{n-1}e^{-r/(na_0)}$, and (transcript pp. 4–5, board)

<Formula label="Approximate radial probability" tex="p(r) \propto r^{2n}\,e^{-2r/(na_0)}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 4–5, board" />

This is a fight between two factors. The exponential decays over a length of order $na_0$, but the power $r^{2n}$ takes off more and more slowly near the origin as $n$ grows, and pushes the peak far out. Setting $dp/dr = 0$ and dividing by $p$ gives the maximum (transcript p. 5, board):

<Formula label="Most probable radius" tex="\frac{2n}{r} - \frac{2}{na_0} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = n^2a_0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5, board" />

It is the interplay of the polynomial with the exponential that makes the atom so large (transcript p. 5). For the largest $l = n - 1$ the polynomial $W_{nl}$ has degree 0, so this "approximation" is exact (the page's own remark): the most probable radius of that state is exactly $n^2a_0$.

## Rydberg atoms in nature and in the laboratory

Rydberg atoms have been observed in interstellar gas, where protons capture electrons into states of very high $n$ (transcript p. 5). Zwiebach quotes $n = 350$ (transcript p. 5). The size is then $n^2a_0 \approx 350^2 \times 0.53\times10^{-10}$ m $\approx 6.5$ µm, comparable with a red blood cell, about 8 µm (transcript p. 5), and within a factor of about 5 to 10 of the thickness of a hair, about 50 µm, which the eye can just see (transcript p. 6). (The lecture says "almost a million times bigger" than usual; the factor $350^2$ is about 120,000.) The lecture also links this capture to the moment the universe cooled and formed hydrogen; the interstellar Rydberg atoms are made by the same process, but today, in gas that hot stars keep ionized.

Such atoms eventually spiral down, but slowly. An atom with $n = 5$ falls to $n = 1$ in about $10^{-7}$ s, while Rydberg atoms can last a millisecond, a tenth of a second, sometimes even a second (transcript p. 5). The reason is that the levels crowd together: with energies proportional to $1/n^2$, neighbouring levels are separated by an amount proportional to $1/n^3$ (transcript p. 6). Expanding for large $n$ (the page's own step):

<Formula label="Spacing of neighbouring levels at large n" tex="E_{n+1} - E_n = \frac{e^2}{2a_0}\left(\frac{1}{n^2} - \frac{1}{(n + 1)^2}\right) \approx \frac{e^2}{a_0}\,\frac{1}{n^3}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 6 (the 1/n³ law; the expansion is our own step)" />

In the laboratory, Rydberg atoms are now made with lasers. In an MIT laboratory three lasers do it: two lift the outermost electron to $n = 10$, the third to $n = 60$ (transcript p. 6). They are detected by ionizing them with an electric field. An ordinary atom would need a field of millions of volts per centimetre; a Rydberg atom, with its loosely bound electron, ionizes easily (transcript p. 6). These are almost semi-classical atoms, a laboratory in which Bohr's old picture of orbits, with photons of the right frequency emitted in jumps between them, works well (transcript p. 6).

**In the sky (the page's own addition).** Between neighbouring high levels the photons are radio waves. Radio astronomers observe these **radio recombination lines** from the ionized gas around hot young stars (H II regions). The line from $n = 110$ to $n = 109$, called H109α, lies at about 5.009 GHz, from $\nu = R_\text{H}c\left(\frac{1}{109^2} - \frac{1}{110^2}\right)$ with hydrogen's Rydberg frequency $R_\text{H}c = 3.2881\times10^{15}$ Hz (which includes the reduced mass). The same formula puts the line from $n = 351$ to $n = 350$ near 153 MHz.

## The shapes of the orbits

The second segment begins with a poll (reading 2, transcript p. 1). Take $n = 100$, so $l$ runs from 0 to 99. The semi-classical orbits change from circles to ellipses as $l$ changes. Which $l$ gives the most circular orbit, and which the most elongated? A little over half the class votes for $l = 0$ as the most circular, since $l = 0$ states are spherically symmetric. That is wrong: $l = 99$ gives the most circular orbit and $l = 0$ the most elongated (transcript p. 1). (A spherically symmetric probability cloud is not a circular orbit: it is the average over all the directions in which a very elongated orbit can point.)

The intuition comes from the effective potential of the radial equation (transcript p. 1, board):

<Formula label="Effective potential of hydrogen" tex="V_{\text{eff}}(r) = \frac{\hbar^2\,l(l + 1)}{2mr^2} - \frac{e^2}{r}" cite="reading2" at="transcript p. 1, board" />

Draw a high level $E_n$, just below zero, across this potential:

- For $l = 0$ there is no centrifugal term. The electron rushes in to $r = 0$, very fast, and back out to the turning point where $-e^2/r = E_n$ (transcript p. 1). For a planet that would be an orbit that reaches the sun at one end and swings far out at the other: the most elongated ellipse (transcript pp. 1–2).
- For $l = 1$ the term $1/r^2$ makes the potential rise near the origin. The radius now swings between a smallest value, no longer zero, and a largest one. Raising $l$ to 2, 3, … lifts the inner turning point and lowers the outer one: the ellipses become rounder (transcript p. 2).
- Finally the level just touches the bottom of the well. There the radius is fixed and the orbit is a circle; for a still larger $l$ there is no solution at all (transcript p. 2).

The same picture follows from angular momentum, $\vec L = \vec r \times \vec p$ (transcript p. 2). On a circular orbit $\vec r$ and $\vec p$ are perpendicular and the cross product is as large as it can be for the given size. On an orbit that dives almost straight at the proton, $\vec p$ is nearly along $\vec r$ and the angular momentum is small. So $l = 0$ is the most elongated orbit and the largest $l$ the most circular.

Why does the largest $l$ stop at $n - 1$? (A student asks about $l = 100$; Zwiebach answers only that it cannot be reached, transcript p. 1. The following is the page's own step.) Setting $dV_{\text{eff}}/dr = 0$ with $a_0 = \hbar^2/(me^2)$ puts the bottom of the well at

<Formula label="Bottom of the effective potential" tex="r = l(l + 1)\,a_0, \qquad V_{\text{eff}} = -\frac{e^2}{2a_0}\,\frac{1}{l(l + 1)}" cite="reading2" at="transcript p. 1 (V_eff); the minimum is our own step" />

The level $E_n = -\frac{e^2}{2a_0}\frac{1}{n^2}$ lies above this bottom only if $l(l + 1) \le n^2$. Since $(n - 1)n < n^2 < n(n + 1)$, the largest allowed value is $l = n - 1$, and even it does not sit exactly at the bottom. So the "perfectly circular" orbit of the lecture (transcript p. 2) is very nearly, not exactly, a circle; the next page finds its eccentricity, $1/\sqrt n$.

The turning points of these orbits, $r_+$ and $r_-$, are the subject of the next lecture. Zwiebach announces the result (transcript p. 2): their average, $\frac12(r_+ + r_-)$, is $n^2a_0$ for every $l$. All these orbits of different elongation then share a semi-major axis, and Kepler already knew that the period of a planet depends only on its semi-major axis. The quantum states with the same $n$ and different $l$ have the same energy in the same way: whatever makes all those classical periods equal is what makes the hydrogen levels degenerate (transcript p. 3). (Zwiebach calls it "the mystery of the quadratic potential" here; he means the $1/r$ potential, for which this is true.) The calculation itself is on [the next page](/courses/8-04/hydrogen-orbits-turning-points).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="From atoms to galaxies, and to the first atoms">
The virial theorem of this page, $2\langle T\rangle = -\langle V\rangle$ for a $1/r$ potential, holds for gravity too. Applied to the speeds of galaxies in a cluster, it gives the cluster's mass, and in the 1930s it showed Zwicky far more mass than the visible stars: one of the oldest pieces of evidence for [dark matter](/courses/8-902/virial-theorem). And the capture of electrons by protons that makes Rydberg atoms in interstellar gas today also happened once to the whole universe, when it [cooled enough to become neutral](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling). The electrons captured then cascaded down through the levels too. The faint lines they left on the cosmic microwave background are predicted but far too weak to have been detected so far (the page's own addition).
</Callout>

## Summary

- In a Rydberg atom the outermost electron has a very large $n$; it sees a net charge $+1$ and behaves like the electron of hydrogen.
- The virial theorem for a $1/r$ potential, $\langle V\rangle = -2\langle T\rangle$, gives $\langle T\rangle = -E_n$ and $\langle V\rangle = 2E_n$, and from that, exactly and for every $l$, $\langle 1/r\rangle = 1/(n^2a_0)$.
- $\langle r\rangle = n^2a_0\left[1 + \frac12\left(1 - l(l + 1)/n^2\right)\right]$: the size grows as $n^2a_0$, not $na_0$. The guess $na_0$ forgets the polynomial factor, whose top power $r^{n-1}$ pushes the peak of $p(r) = r^2|f_{nl}|^2$ out to $n^2a_0$.
- Rydberg atoms reach micrometres in size, and live long because their levels are only about $1/n^3$ apart; they are seen in interstellar gas and made with lasers in the laboratory.
- At fixed $n$, $l = 0$ gives the most elongated orbit and $l = n - 1$ the most nearly circular one, as the effective potential and $\vec L = \vec r \times \vec p$ show; the next page computes the turning points.
