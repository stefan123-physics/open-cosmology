## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `hbar*omega*(n + 1/2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. It treats those letters as independent, so where two of them are related (as $\xi$ and $\lambda$ are in Problem 2) the problem says which form to type. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `hbar` stands for $\hbar$, `omega` for $\omega$, `A` for $|\alpha|$, `ar` for $\text{Re}\,\alpha$, `xi` for $\xi$, `lam` for $\lambda$, `beta` for $\beta$, `Einf` for $E_\infty$, `z0` for $z_0$, `K0` for $K_0$, `kappa` for $\kappa$ and `V0` for $V_0$.

MIT gives the points per problem. The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons, with one change. The energy eigenstates of the oscillator, $\psi_n$ in the lesson, are called $\varphi_n$ here, as on MIT's sheet and in the lecture notes, to keep them apart from the states $\psi_\alpha$ of Problem 1. No constants of nature are needed: every answer is a formula or a pure number. Where a solution says only "notes", it means the lecture notes of the lesson that the problem practises: Chapters 14 and 15 in Problem 1 and Chapter 16 in Problem 4. Other chapters are named.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="States of the harmonic oscillator" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

A particle of mass $m$ is in a harmonic oscillator of frequency $\omega$, with ground state $\varphi_0$ and annihilation and creation operators $\hat a$ and $\hat a^\dagger$, as in the lesson. Consider the state

<Formula label="A state built with an exponential of the creation operator" tex="\psi_\alpha \equiv N\,\exp\left(\alpha\,\hat a^\dagger\right)\varphi_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

where $\alpha$ is any complex number and $N$ is a constant. The exponential of an operator is defined by its power series. For parts (a) and (b) it helps to write that series out.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the constant $N$ (real and positive) for which $\psi_\alpha$ is normalized. In the box, type `A` for $|\alpha|$.

<Hint>

Expand the exponential: $\psi_\alpha = N\sum_n \frac{\alpha^n}{n!}\left(\hat a^\dagger\right)^n\varphi_0$. The lesson tells you what $\left(\hat a^\dagger\right)^n\varphi_0$ is.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The states $\varphi_n$ are orthonormal, so the norm of a sum $\sum_n c_n\varphi_n$ is $\sum_n |c_n|^2$. Do you know the series that appears?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-n" label="$N =$" expr="exp(-A^2/2)"
  vars={{ A: [0.3, 2] }}
  names={{ A: "|\\alpha|" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-A^2)", hint: "That is N squared. The norm of the state holds N twice, so N² times the series must be 1." },
    { expr: "exp(A^2/2)", hint: "The series adds up to a number larger than 1, so N must be smaller than 1 to bring the norm back to 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The exponential spreads $\varphi_0$ over all the energy eigenstates. Their orthonormality turns the norm into a series for an exponential.

<Step title="The state as a sum over energy eigenstates">

The lesson's normalized eigenstates are $\varphi_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\left(\hat a^\dagger\right)^n\varphi_0$, so $\left(\hat a^\dagger\right)^n\varphi_0 = \sqrt{n!}\,\varphi_n$ and

<Formula label="The state as a sum over energy eigenstates" tex="\psi_\alpha = N\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^n}{n!}\left(\hat a^\dagger\right)^n\varphi_0 = N\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}\,\varphi_n" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.19), used in the series of the exponential" />

</Step>

<Step title="The norm">

Because $\left(\varphi_n, \varphi_m\right) = \delta_{mn}$ (notes p. 7, eq. (2.21)):

<Formula label="Norm of the state" tex="\left(\psi_\alpha, \psi_\alpha\right) = |N|^2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{|\alpha|^{2n}}{n!} = |N|^2\,e^{|\alpha|^2} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N = e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\alpha = 0$ the state is $\varphi_0$ itself and $N = 1$, as it must be. The sum also shows what the state is made of: the probability to find the energy $E_n$ is $|N|^2|\alpha|^{2n}/n! = e^{-|\alpha|^2}|\alpha|^{2n}/n!$. These probabilities add up to 1. They form the Poisson distribution with mean $|\alpha|^2$, which parts (c) and (d) will meet again.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Show that $\psi_\alpha$ is an eigenstate of the annihilation operator $\hat a$. What is the eigenvalue?

<Hint>

Act with $\hat a$ on the sum of part (a), term by term. The lesson's ladder relation says what $\hat a\,\varphi_n$ is.

</Hint>

<Hint>

After acting, rename the summation index so that the sum runs over $\varphi_{n-1}$ from $n = 1$, and pull one factor out.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-eig" label="The eigenvalue is" choices={["α", "α*, the complex conjugate of α", "|α|²", "√n, as for the energy eigenstate φₙ"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $\hat a$ lowers each $\varphi_n$ by one step and brings out $\sqrt n$. The coefficients $\alpha^n/\sqrt{n!}$ are built so that the lowered sum is the old sum times $\alpha$.

<Step title="Acting with the annihilation operator">

With $\hat a\,\varphi_n = \sqrt n\,\varphi_{n-1}$ and $\hat a\,\varphi_0 = 0$:

<Formula label="The annihilation operator on the state" tex="\hat a\,\psi_\alpha = N\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}}\,\sqrt n\,\varphi_{n-1} = N\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{(n-1)!}}\,\varphi_{n-1} = \alpha\,N\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{j}}{\sqrt{j!}}\,\varphi_{j} = \alpha\,\psi_\alpha" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 7, eq. (2.25), applied to the sum of part (a)" />

So $\psi_\alpha$ is an eigenstate of $\hat a$ with eigenvalue $\alpha$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second route uses a commutator of the notes, $\left[\hat a, \left(\hat a^\dagger\right)^k\right] = k\left(\hat a^\dagger\right)^{k-1}$ (notes p. 5, eq. (2.5)). Summed over the series it gives $\left[\hat a, e^{\alpha\hat a^\dagger}\right] = \alpha\,e^{\alpha\hat a^\dagger}$: the operator $\hat a$ acts on functions of $\hat a^\dagger$ like a derivative. Since $\hat a\,\varphi_0 = 0$, we get $\hat a\,e^{\alpha\hat a^\dagger}\varphi_0 = \left[\hat a, e^{\alpha\hat a^\dagger}\right]\varphi_0 = \alpha\,e^{\alpha\hat a^\dagger}\varphi_0$, the same result. The eigenvalue may be any complex number. That is allowed because $\hat a$ is not Hermitian. For $\alpha = 0$ the equation is $\hat a\,\varphi_0 = 0$, the lesson's equation for the ground state.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Find the expectation value of the Hamiltonian in the state $\psi_\alpha$.

<Hint>

Use the factorized Hamiltonian, $\hat H = \hbar\omega\left(\hat a^\dagger\hat a + \frac12\right)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Move $\hat a^\dagger$ to the other side of the inner product: $\left(\psi, \hat a^\dagger\hat a\,\psi\right) = \left(\hat a\psi, \hat a\psi\right)$. Then use part (b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-h" label="$\langle\hat H\rangle =$" expr="hbar*omega*(A^2 + 1/2)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], omega: [0.5, 2], A: [0.3, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", omega: "\\omega", A: "|\\alpha|" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "hbar*omega*A^2", hint: "The zero-point energy is missing: the Hamiltonian is ħω(N̂ + ½), not ħωN̂." },
    { expr: "hbar*omega*(A + 1/2)", hint: "The eigenvalue appears twice: once from â acting on the state on the right, once (complex conjugated) from â acting on the state on the left." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the factorized Hamiltonian the operator $\hat a$ stands next to the state, where part (b) replaces it by a number.

<Step title="The expectation value of the number operator">

<Formula label="Mean number in the state" tex="\langle\hat N\rangle = \left(\psi_\alpha, \hat a^\dagger\hat a\,\psi_\alpha\right) = \left(\hat a\,\psi_\alpha, \hat a\,\psi_\alpha\right) = \alpha^*\alpha\left(\psi_\alpha, \psi_\alpha\right) = |\alpha|^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The energy">

<Formula label="Mean energy in the state" tex="\langle\hat H\rangle = \hbar\omega\left(\langle\hat N\rangle + \frac12\right) = \hbar\omega\left(|\alpha|^2 + \frac12\right)" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.14), with the mean number above" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\alpha = 0$ this is the ground state energy $\frac12\hbar\omega$. The mean number $|\alpha|^2$ is the mean of the Poisson distribution found in part (a). It need not be a whole number: $\psi_\alpha$ is not an energy eigenstate, and $\langle\hat H\rangle$ is an average over many levels.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Find the uncertainty $\Delta E$ of the energy in the state $\psi_\alpha$.

<Hint>

$\Delta E = \hbar\omega\,\Delta N$, because the constant $\frac12\hbar\omega$ does not change an uncertainty. You need $\langle\hat N^2\rangle = \left(\psi_\alpha, \hat a^\dagger\hat a\,\hat a^\dagger\hat a\,\psi_\alpha\right)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The two operators in the middle stand in the wrong order for part (b). Swap them with $\left[\hat a, \hat a^\dagger\right] = 1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-de" label="$\Delta E =$" expr="hbar*omega*A"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], omega: [0.5, 2], A: [0.3, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", omega: "\\omega", A: "|\\alpha|" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "hbar*omega*A^2", hint: "That is ħω times the variance of N. The uncertainty is the square root of the variance." },
    { expr: "hbar*omega*sqrt(A^2 + 1/2)", hint: "The ½ in the Hamiltonian is a constant. It shifts every energy by the same amount and adds nothing to the spread." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "The state is an eigenstate of â, not of the Hamiltonian. It is a sum of many energy eigenstates, so its energy is uncertain." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Bring every $\hat a$ to the right and every $\hat a^\dagger$ to the left. Then each $\hat a$ gives $\alpha$ and each $\hat a^\dagger$ gives $\alpha^*$.

<Step title="The square of the number operator">

With $\hat a\,\hat a^\dagger = \hat a^\dagger\hat a + 1$:

<Formula label="Mean of the squared number" tex="\langle\hat N^2\rangle = \left(\psi_\alpha, \hat a^\dagger\left(\hat a^\dagger\hat a + 1\right)\hat a\,\psi_\alpha\right) = \left(\psi_\alpha, \hat a^\dagger\hat a^\dagger\hat a\,\hat a\,\psi_\alpha\right) + \left(\psi_\alpha, \hat a^\dagger\hat a\,\psi_\alpha\right) = |\alpha|^4 + |\alpha|^2" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.10), used to reorder the operators" />

</Step>

<Step title="The uncertainty">

<Formula label="Uncertainty of the energy" tex="\left(\Delta N\right)^2 = \langle\hat N^2\rangle - \langle\hat N\rangle^2 = |\alpha|^2, \qquad \Delta E = \hbar\omega\,\Delta N = \hbar\omega\,|\alpha|" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The variance of the number equals its mean, $|\alpha|^2$: the mark of the Poisson distribution of part (a). For $\alpha = 0$ the uncertainty vanishes, as it must for the energy eigenstate $\varphi_0$. For large $|\alpha|$ the ratio $\Delta E/\langle\hat H\rangle \approx 1/|\alpha|$ becomes small: the energy is then sharp compared with its size, as for a classical oscillator.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={4}>

Read the eigenvalue equation of part (b) as a differential equation, and use it to find the normalized wavefunction $\psi_\alpha(x)$ in explicit form. A constant phase factor may be left open.

The box is our addition, a check of your wavefunction: at which position $x$ does the probability density $|\psi_\alpha(x)|^2$ have its maximum? Type `ar` for the real part of $\alpha$.

<Hint>

The lesson writes $\hat a\,\varphi_0 = 0$ as a first-order differential equation, with $\hat p = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d}{dx}$. Do the same with $\alpha\,\psi_\alpha$ on the right side in place of zero.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The equation has the form $\psi' = \left(-b_1x + b_2\right)\psi$ with constants $b_1$ and $b_2$. Divide by $\psi$ and integrate.

</Hint>

<Hint>

To normalize, write $\alpha = \text{Re}\,\alpha + i\,\text{Im}\,\alpha$. Only the real part of the exponent enters $|\psi_\alpha|^2$. Complete the square in $x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-x0" label="$|\psi_\alpha|^2$ peaks at $x =$" expr="sqrt(2*hbar/(m*omega))*ar"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], omega: [0.5, 2], ar: [0.3, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", omega: "\\omega", ar: "\\text{Re}\\,\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(hbar/(2*m*omega))*ar", hint: "A factor 2 is lost. Complete the square again: the exponent of the density is −(mω/ħ)x² + 2√(2mω/ħ) Re α x." },
    { expr: "sqrt(2*m*omega/hbar)*ar", hint: "Check the units: this is one over a length. The length of the oscillator is √(ħ/mω)." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "That holds only when α is purely imaginary. The real part of α adds a term linear in x to the exponent, which shifts the Gaussian." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In position space $\hat a$ is a first-order differential operator. So $\hat a\,\psi_\alpha = \alpha\,\psi_\alpha$ is a first-order equation, solved by one integration, just as for the ground state.

<Step title="The differential equation">

The annihilation operator is $\hat a = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\left(x + \frac{\hbar}{m\omega}\frac{d}{dx}\right)$ in position space. The eigenvalue equation becomes

<Formula label="Eigenvalue equation in position space" tex="\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\left(x + \frac{\hbar}{m\omega}\frac{d}{dx}\right)\psi_\alpha = \alpha\,\psi_\alpha \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d\psi_\alpha}{dx} = \left(-\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}\,x + \sqrt{\frac{2m\omega}{\hbar}}\,\alpha\right)\psi_\alpha" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.20), with αψ on the right side" />

</Step>

<Step title="Its solution">

Dividing by $\psi_\alpha$ gives the derivative of $\ln\psi_\alpha$. One integration, with a constant $C$:

<Formula label="Solution of the eigenvalue equation" tex="\psi_\alpha(x) = C\,\exp\left(-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}\,x^2 + \sqrt{\frac{2m\omega}{\hbar}}\,\alpha\,x\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Normalization">

Only the real part of $\alpha$ enters the density. Completing the square, with the length $x_0 \equiv \sqrt{2\hbar/(m\omega)}\;\text{Re}\,\alpha$:

<Formula label="Probability density of the state" tex="\left|\psi_\alpha(x)\right|^2 = |C|^2\exp\left(-\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}\,x^2 + 2\sqrt{\frac{2m\omega}{\hbar}}\;\text{Re}\,\alpha\;x\right) = |C|^2\,e^{2\left(\text{Re}\,\alpha\right)^2}\exp\left(-\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}\left(x - x_0\right)^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The integral of the last Gaussian over all $x$ is $\sqrt{\pi\hbar/(m\omega)}$, so $|C| = \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}e^{-\left(\text{Re}\,\alpha\right)^2}$ and, up to a constant phase,

<Formula label="The normalized wavefunction" tex="\psi_\alpha(x) = \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}\exp\left(-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}\,x^2 + \sqrt{\frac{2m\omega}{\hbar}}\,\alpha\,x - \left(\text{Re}\,\alpha\right)^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The density is a Gaussian with its maximum at $x_0 = \sqrt{2\hbar/(m\omega)}\;\text{Re}\,\alpha$, the value in the box.

</Step>

<Step title="What the state looks like">

Write also $p_0 \equiv \sqrt{2m\omega\hbar}\;\text{Im}\,\alpha$. Then the exponent splits into a real Gaussian and a phase:

<Formula label="The state as a displaced ground state" tex="\psi_\alpha(x) = \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}\exp\left(-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}\left(x - x_0\right)^2\right)e^{ip_0x/\hbar}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is the ground state $\varphi_0$ of the lesson, moved to $x_0$ and given the momentum $p_0$. Such states are called coherent states.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\alpha = 0$ the formula is the lesson's $\varphi_0$ (notes p. 3, eq. (1.22)). The position of the peak agrees with operator algebra: $\hat x = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\left(\hat a + \hat a^\dagger\right)$ gives $\langle\hat x\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\left(\alpha + \alpha^*\right) = \sqrt{\frac{2\hbar}{m\omega}}\;\text{Re}\,\alpha$ (notes p. 2, eq. (1.12)). The constant phase (our addition) is fixed by the definition of $\psi_\alpha$: its overlap with $\varphi_0$ must be $N = e^{-|\alpha|^2/2}$, real and positive. Doing that Gaussian integral gives $C = \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}e^{-|\alpha|^2/2 - \alpha^2/2}$, which multiplies the last formula by the phase $e^{-ix_0p_0/(2\hbar)}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Two delta functions, again" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

A particle of mass $m$ moves in one dimension in a double well made of two attractive delta functions, a distance $2a$ apart:

<Formula label="The double delta-function well" tex="V(x) = -g\,\delta(x - a) - g\,\delta(x + a), \qquad g > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

This is the potential of Problem Set 7, Problem 1. Its result for the even bound state (the ground state) is restated on MIT's sheet. Write the energy as $E = -\hbar^2\kappa^2/(2m)$ and let $\xi \equiv \kappa a$. Then, in terms of $E_0 \equiv \hbar^2/(2ma^2)$:

<Formula label="Ground state of the double well" tex="\frac{E}{E_0} = -\xi^2, \qquad \frac{\xi}{1 + e^{-2\xi}} = \lambda, \qquad \lambda \equiv \frac{mag}{\hbar^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

(It follows from an even wavefunction, $\cosh\kappa x$ between the wells and $e^{-\kappa|x|}$ outside, with the lesson's jump in the slope at each delta function.) The pure number $\lambda$ measures the strength $g$ if $a$ is held fixed, or the separation if $g$ is held fixed. Here $g$ is fixed, and with it a natural length $a_0$ and the binding energy $E_\infty$ of a single delta function of strength $g$:

<Formula label="Natural length and energy of the problem" tex="\lambda = \frac{a}{a_0}, \qquad a_0 \equiv \frac{\hbar^2}{mg}, \qquad E_\infty \equiv \frac{mg^2}{2\hbar^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, pp. 1–2" />

Now read this as a model of a molecule of two atoms. The two delta functions are the two ions, a distance $2a$ apart, and the particle is an electron that both share. The ions repel each other. Let the energy of that repulsion, for ions a distance $x$ apart, be

<Formula label="Repulsion between the ions" tex="V_r(x) = \frac{\beta g}{x}, \qquad \beta > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

with $\beta$ a small number. The total potential energy of the molecule is the (negative) energy of the bound electron plus the (positive) repulsion:

<Formula label="Total potential energy of the molecule" tex="V_\text{tot} = E + V_r(2a)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Write the energy of the electron as $E = -E_\infty f(\xi, \lambda)$ and find the function $f$. Plot $E$ as a function of $a/a_0 = \lambda$, to see how the ground state energy changes with the distance between the ions. What are the values of $E$ for $a \to 0$ and for $a \to \infty$?

In the first box give $f$ written with both $\xi$ and $\lambda$, as it comes from changing the unit of energy. The second box is our addition: use the relation between $\xi$ and $\lambda$ to write $f$ with $\xi$ alone. The last two boxes take the two limits of $E$ in units of $E_\infty$ (exact numbers).

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the limits, look at $\xi/(1 + e^{-2\xi}) = \lambda$ when $\xi$ is very small, and when $\xi$ is very large.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-f" label="With $\xi$ and $\lambda$: $f =$" expr="xi^2/lam^2"

  names={{ xi: "\\xi", lam: "\\lambda" }}
  mistakes={[

**The idea.** In the forbidden region the wavefunction curves away from the axis, so its kinetic energy density is negative. The question is whether that negative amount stays finite, or even grows, when the tail is squeezed into the wall.

<Step title="The kinetic operator on the tail">

For $x > a$, $\psi = \frac{A}{\sqrt a}\,e^{-\kappa x}$ and $\hat K\psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' = -\frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}{2m}\,\psi$. With $A$ real:

<Formula label="The integral over the forbidden region" tex="K_\text{out} = -\frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}{2m}\,\frac{A^2}{a}\int_a^\infty e^{-2\kappa x}\,dx = -\frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}{2m}\,\frac{A^2}{a}\,\frac{e^{-2\kappa a}}{2\kappa} = -\frac{\hbar^2\,\kappa\,A^2\,e^{-2\kappa a}}{4ma}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Large z₀">

With $\kappa \simeq z_0/a$ and $A^2e^{-2\kappa a} \simeq \frac{\pi^2}{4z_0^2}\,e^{2z_0}e^{-2z_0} = \frac{\pi^2}{4z_0^2}$:

The kink that is left at the wall of the infinite well is the harmless remainder of a tail that has shrunk away. It carries no energy, and the energy of the infinite well comes from the inside alone.

</Step>

</Step>

Can $T = 1$ be reached for $E < V_0$? Then find the transmission coefficient for $E > V_0$ (MIT's sheet says: look it up, or work it out). At which energies above the barrier is $T = 1$? Give them as $E_n$, numbered by $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$

<Hint>

For the first question look at the sign of each term of $1/T$ in part (a).

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $E > V_0$ nothing in the matching changes except that $\kappa$ becomes imaginary: $\kappa = ik_2$ with $k_2^2 = 2m\left(E - V_0\right)/\hbar^2$. What does $\sinh$ of an imaginary number give?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-never" label="$T = 1$ for $E < V_0$?" choices={["No: for any barrier of finite width and height, sinh² is positive, so 1/T is larger than 1", "Yes, whenever 2κa is a whole multiple of π", "Yes, at E = V₀/2, where the factor in front of sinh² is smallest", "Yes, for every energy once the barrier is thick enough"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p5-res" label="$E_n =$" expr="V0 + n^2*pi^2*hbar^2/(8*m*a^2)"
  vars={{ V0: [0.5, 2], n: [1, 4], hbar: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ V0: "V_0", hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "n^2*pi^2*hbar^2/(8*m*a^2)", hint: "That is the kinetic energy above the barrier, E − V₀. The energy E itself is measured from the potential outside." },
    { expr: "V0 + n^2*pi^2*hbar^2/(2*m*a^2)", hint: "The barrier is 2a wide: a whole number of half wavelengths must fit into 2a, not into a." },
    { expr: "V0 - n^2*pi^2*hbar^2/(8*m*a^2)", hint: "Below the top of the barrier there is no perfect transmission. The energies lie above V₀." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** Below the top of the barrier nothing can cancel the reflection. Above it the waves inside can fit across the barrier, exactly as over the well of the lesson.

<Step title="Below the top">

In part (a), $V_0^2/\left(4E(V_0 - E)\right)$ is positive for $0 < E < V_0$, and $\sinh^2 2\kappa a > 0$ for every $\kappa a > 0$. So $1/T > 1$: there is always some reflection, and $T = 1$ is never reached. Part (b) shows the same at $E = V_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Above the top">

For $E > V_0$ put $\kappa = ik_2$ in part (a). Since $\sinh\left(2ik_2a\right) = i\sin\left(2k_2a\right)$, and $V_0 - E = -\left(E - V_0\right)$:

<Formula label="Transmission over a barrier, above its top" tex="\frac1T = 1 + \frac{V_0^2}{4E\left(E - V_0\right)}\,\sin^2\left(2k_2a\right), \qquad k_2 = \frac{\sqrt{2m\left(E - V_0\right)}}{\hbar}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Perfect transmission">

$T = 1$ when $\sin 2k_2a = 0$, that is $2k_2a = n\pi$ with $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ (not $n = 0$: that is $E = V_0$, where the factor in front is infinite and part (b) applies):

<Formula label="Energies of perfect transmission over the barrier" tex="E_n = V_0 + \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m\left(2a\right)^2} = V_0 + \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The condition $2k_2a = n\pi$ says that a whole number of half wavelengths fits across the barrier: $2a = n\lambda_2/2$. The kinetic energies $E_n - V_0$ are the levels of an infinite well of width $2a$. This is the resonant transmission of the lesson (lecture notes, Chapter 17, p. 3, eq. (1.14)), with the top of the barrier in the role of the bottom of the well. For very high energies the factor $V_0^2/\left(4E(E - V_0)\right)$ goes to zero and $T \to 1$ for every energy: a fast particle hardly notices the barrier.

</Step>

</Solution>
