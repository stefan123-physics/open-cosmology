The first lecture introduced the Mach–Zehnder interferometer in words. This one works it out with numbers: a photon's state as a column of two complex amplitudes, beam splitters as matrices, and then a startling use of the result, a way to test a bomb without setting it off (notes pp. 1–7). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 2; this page follows their order in its own words. The notes' figures are not reproduced; the set-up is described in the text.)

## A photon on two paths

The interferometer has two beam splitters, BS1 on the left and BS2 on the right, and two mirrors. Between the splitters a photon can travel along an upper or a lower branch; after BS2 the upper branch leads to a detector D0 and the lower one to a detector D1 (notes p. 1). At any vertical cut through the apparatus, the photon's state is given by two probability amplitudes, written as a column:

<Formula label="Photon state as a column" tex="\begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \left|\alpha\right|^2 + \left|\beta\right|^2 = 1" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eqs. (1.1)–(1.2)" />

Here $\alpha$ is the amplitude to be in the upper beam and $\beta$ the amplitude to be in the lower beam; $|\alpha|^2$ and $|\beta|^2$ are the probabilities of finding the photon there, and they add up to 1 because the photon is certainly in one of the two (notes p. 1). A photon certainly in the upper beam is the column $(1, 0)$, one certainly in the lower beam is $(0, 1)$, and the general state is a superposition of the two (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="A superposition of the two beams" tex="\begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix} = \alpha\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} + \beta\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.4)" />

**A phase shifter** is a piece of material that multiplies the amplitude passing through it by a fixed phase $e^{i\delta}$, with $\delta$ real. Since $\left|e^{i\delta}\right| = 1$, it does not change the probability of finding the photon in that beam. For $\delta = \pi$ it simply flips the sign of the amplitude, since $e^{i\pi} = -1$ (notes p. 2).

## Beam splitters as matrices

A photon hitting a beam splitter from above, $(1, 0)$, leaves as $(s, t)$: $s$ is the amplitude to be reflected and $t$ the amplitude to be transmitted. One hitting it from below, $(0, 1)$, leaves as $(u, v)$, with $v$ the reflection amplitude and $u$ the transmission amplitude (notes p. 2, eq. (1.5)). By linearity these four numbers fix what happens to any incoming state, and the beam splitter acts as a matrix:

<Formula label="A beam splitter acting on a state" tex="\begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix} \to \begin{pmatrix} s & u \\ t & v \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \alpha s + \beta u \\ \alpha t + \beta v \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="notes pp. 2–3, eqs. (1.6)–(1.8)" />

Probabilities must still add up to 1, so $|s|^2 + |t|^2 = 1$ and $|u|^2 + |v|^2 = 1$. The interferometer uses **balanced** beam splitters, which reflect and transmit with equal probability, so all four numbers have $|s|^2 = |t|^2 = |u|^2 = |v|^2 = 1/2$ (notes p. 3, eqs. (1.9)–(1.11)).

The first guess, all four equal to $1/\sqrt 2$, fails. It keeps $(1, 0)$ normalized, but it turns the normalized state $\left(1/\sqrt 2, 1/\sqrt 2\right)$ into $(1, 1)$, whose probabilities add up to 2. Changing the sign of $v$ repairs it (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="A balanced beam splitter" tex="\begin{pmatrix} s & u \\ t & v \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.14)" />

Acting on any normalized state $(\alpha, \beta)$, it gives $\left(\alpha + \beta, \alpha - \beta\right)/\sqrt 2$, and the probabilities add up to $\frac12\left(|\alpha + \beta|^2 + |\alpha - \beta|^2\right) = |\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$: the cross terms cancel (notes p. 4, eqs. (1.15)–(1.16)).

The minus sign means that a photon arriving from below has its amplitude flipped when it is reflected, a phase shift of $\pi$. Real beam splitters do this. A typical one is a glass plate with a reflective dielectric coating on one side, whose refractive index lies between those of glass and air. Light picks up a phase shift on reflection only when it meets a material of higher refractive index: so going from air into the coating it does, and going from glass into the coating it does not. The matrix above describes a splitter coated on its lower side; transmitted light is never shifted (notes p. 4). Coating the upper side instead gives a second matrix that also conserves probability:

<Formula label="The two beam splitters of the interferometer" tex="\text{BS1}: \ \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{BS2}: \ \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eqs. (1.17)–(1.18)" />

## The whole interferometer

A photon passes BS1 first and BS2 second, so the output is the BS2 matrix times the BS1 matrix times the input. The product is remarkably simple (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Input to output" tex="\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} \alpha \\ \beta \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \beta \\ -\alpha \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (1.19)" />

A photon sent in from below, $(0, 1)$, comes out as $(1, 0)$: it reaches D0 with probability 1 and D1 never (notes p. 4, table (1.20)). Inside the interferometer the photon has an amplitude in both branches at once; its two amplitudes for reaching D1 are equal and opposite, and cancel.

**Blocking the lower path** (notes pp. 5–6). Now put an absorbing block in the lower branch. After BS1 the state is $\left(1/\sqrt 2, 1/\sqrt 2\right)$. The lower half is absorbed, so with probability $1/2$ the photon ends in the block. Otherwise only the upper amplitude reaches BS2, as the column $\left(1/\sqrt 2, 0\right)$, and leaves as

<Formula label="Output with the lower path blocked" tex="\frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1/\sqrt 2 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 \\ 1/2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eqs. (1.21)–(1.22)" />

so the photon reaches D0 with probability $1/4$ and D1 with probability $1/4$ (notes p. 6, table (1.23)). Before the block, nothing reached D1. **Blocking a path made D1 possible**: with one path closed there is nothing left to cancel against.

## The Elitzur–Vaidman bombs

To see how strange this is, the notes turn to a thought experiment devised by Avshalom Elitzur and Lev Vaidman of Tel Aviv University (notes p. 6). Imagine bombs whose trigger is a photon detector inside a narrow tube that runs through the bomb. A photon sent down the tube is detected and the bomb explodes. But some bombs are defective: their detector does not work, and a photon passes straight through the tube without any effect. Suppose the detector cannot be examined without destroying the bomb. How can a working bomb be recognised without exploding it? Classically this is impossible: a photon sent into the tube either passes (defective) or sets the bomb off (working) (notes p. 6).

Quantum mechanics allows it. Place the bomb in the lower branch of the interferometer, with its tube along the beam, and send in a photon from below (notes pp. 6–7).

- **A defective bomb** is invisible to the photon: both paths are open, and every photon reaches D0. Nothing ever arrives at D1 and nothing explodes (table (2.24)).
- **A working bomb** acts like the block: its detector absorbs the lower half of the photon's state. So the bomb explodes with probability $1/2$; with probability $1/4$ the photon reaches D0, which tells us nothing; and with probability $1/4$ it reaches D1 (table (2.25)).

A photon at D1 is impossible with a defective bomb, so whenever one arrives there, the bomb is certainly working. Yet that photon went along the upper path: it never entered the bomb. This is an **interaction-free measurement**. Experiments, without bombs, have confirmed that such measurements work (notes p. 7). (The page's addition: the proposal was published in 1993, and a preliminary demonstration with single photons followed in 1995, whose authors also argued that a cleverer set-up can push the success rate from the one-in-four here as close to 1 as desired; both papers are listed below.)

<WorkedExample title="Phases and repeated tests">
<Problem>
(a) Check that BS1 keeps the state $\alpha = 0.6$, $\beta = 0.8i$ normalized. (b) A phase shifter with $\delta = \pi/3$ is placed in the lower branch, with both paths open and a photon entering from below. What are the probabilities at D0 and D1? (c) A working bomb is tested photon after photon, stopping as soon as a photon reaches D1 or the bomb explodes. What fraction of working bombs is identified without exploding?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Normalization">
BS1 gives $\left(-0.6 + 0.8i, \ 0.6 + 0.8i\right)/\sqrt 2$. Each entry has size squared $(0.36 + 0.64)/2 = 0.5$, and $0.5 + 0.5 = 1$, as it should.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A phase shifter">
After BS1 the state is $\left(1, 1\right)/\sqrt 2$; the shifter makes it $\left(1, e^{i\delta}\right)/\sqrt 2$, and BS2 gives $\left(1 + e^{i\delta}, 1 - e^{i\delta}\right)/2$. The probability at D0 is $\left|1 + e^{i\delta}\right|^2/4 = \cos^2(\delta/2) = \cos^2 30° = 0.75$, and at D1 it is $\sin^2 30° = 0.25$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Testing until the result is clear">
Each photon gives D1 (identified) with probability $1/4$, an explosion with probability $1/2$, and D0 (try again) with probability $1/4$. Only the first two end the test, so the fraction identified safely is $\frac{1/4}{1/4 + 1/2} = 1/3$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A photon in the interferometer is a column of two complex amplitudes $(\alpha, \beta)$, one for each beam, with $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$.
- Beam splitters act as two-by-two matrices; a balanced one has entries $\pm 1/\sqrt 2$, and one sign must be negative to conserve probability.
- With both paths open every photon from below reaches D0; blocking the lower path sends a quarter of them to D1.
- An Elitzur–Vaidman bomb in the lower path is identified as working whenever a photon reaches D1, although that photon never touched it: an interaction-free measurement.
