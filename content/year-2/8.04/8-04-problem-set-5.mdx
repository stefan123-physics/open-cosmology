## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit shown. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `hbar/(a*sqrt(2))`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Write the symbols of the box exactly as the box names them: `hbar` stands for $\hbar$, `D` for $|\Delta|$, `R` for $\text{Re}(\Delta^2)$, `phi` for $\phi_\Delta$, `tau` for $\tau$, `V0` for $V_0$, `kappa` for $\kappa$ and `xi` for $\xi$.

MIT gives the points per problem. The splits between parts are ours. Several parts ask you to show or to explain something: those have no box or only a choice box, so open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons: $\psi(x)$ for a wavefunction at one time and $\Psi(x, t)$ for one that depends on time, $\tilde\Phi(p)$ for the Fourier transform in the momentum $p$, $\langle\hat Q\rangle$ for an expectation value, and $\Delta x$, $\Delta p$ for the uncertainties, with $(\Delta Q)^2 = \langle\hat Q^2\rangle - \langle\hat Q\rangle^2$. Every particle has mass $m$ and moves in one dimension.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A Gaussian that saturates the uncertainty product" points={5} mit="Problem 1">

A particle has the Gaussian wavefunction

<Formula label="The real Gaussian" tex="\psi(x) = N\exp\left(-\frac12\,\frac{x^2}{a^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, eq. (1), p. 1" />

where $N$ is a real constant and $a$ is a real, positive constant with units of length. MIT's sheet gives two integrals. The first holds for complex $\alpha$ and $\beta$ as long as the real part of $\alpha$ is positive. The second gives the integral with an extra $x^2$ in terms of the one without:

<Formula label="Two Gaussian integrals" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,e^{-\alpha x^2 + \beta x} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{\alpha}}\,\exp\left(\frac{\beta^2}{4\alpha}\right), \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,x^2e^{-\alpha x^2} = \frac{1}{2\alpha}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,e^{-\alpha x^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Use the wavefunction in position space to find the uncertainties $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$. Confirm that the product sits exactly on the bound of the Heisenberg uncertainty relation, $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2$. MIT's sheet adds that the work is short when done the right way: with the second integral above you never need $N$, and for $\langle\hat p^2\rangle$ one of the two factors $\hat p$ can be moved onto $\psi^*$.

<Hint>

An expectation value is a ratio: $\langle\hat x^2\rangle = \int x^2|\psi|^2dx\,/\int|\psi|^2dx$. In that ratio $N$ cancels. What is $\alpha$ for $|\psi|^2$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\hat p$ is Hermitian, so $\langle\hat p^2\rangle = \int\left|\hat p\psi\right|^2dx = \hbar^2\int\left|\psi'\right|^2dx$, divided by $\int|\psi|^2dx$. The derivative of a Gaussian is $x$ times a Gaussian, so the integral is again the one for $\langle\hat x^2\rangle$.

</Hint>

  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}

<Solution>

**The idea.** Every expectation value is a ratio of two Gaussian integrals in which $N$ cancels, and both $\langle\hat x^2\rangle$ and $\langle\hat p^2\rangle$ lead to the same ratio.

<Step title="The uncertainty in position">

The density is $|\psi|^2 = N^2e^{-x^2/a^2}$: a Gaussian with $\alpha = 1/a^2$. It is even in $x$, so $\langle\hat x\rangle = 0$. The second integral of the sheet gives

<Formula label="Mean square position of the Gaussian" tex="\langle\hat x^2\rangle = \frac{\int x^2e^{-\alpha x^2}dx}{\int e^{-\alpha x^2}dx} = \frac{1}{2\alpha} = \frac{a^2}{2}, \qquad \Delta x = \frac{a}{\sqrt2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The uncertainty in momentum">

The wavefunction is real, so $\langle\hat p\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{i}\int\psi\,\psi'\,dx$ is $\frac{\hbar}{2i}$ times the integral of the derivative of $\psi^2$, which vanishes because $\psi \to 0$ at both ends. For $\langle\hat p^2\rangle$ move one $\hat p$ onto $\psi^*$, as in the lesson's form of the kinetic energy (Chapter 8 of the notes, p. 5, eq. (2.31)). With $\psi' = -(x/a^2)\,\psi$:

<Formula label="Mean square momentum of the Gaussian" tex="\langle\hat p^2\rangle = \hbar^2\,\frac{\int\left|\psi'\right|^2dx}{\int\left|\psi\right|^2dx} = \frac{\hbar^2}{a^4}\,\langle\hat x^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2a^2}, \qquad \Delta p = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt2\,a}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The product">

<Formula label="Uncertainty product of the real Gaussian" tex="\Delta x\,\Delta p = \frac{a}{\sqrt2}\times\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt2\,a} = \frac{\hbar}{2}" cite="reading1" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.15), for the bound" />

The product equals the bound for every $a$: the real Gaussian saturates the uncertainty relation.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $a$ is a length, and $\hbar/a$ is a momentum. A wider packet (larger $a$) has a larger $\Delta x$ and a smaller $\Delta p$, as it should. The packet of the lesson on wave packets has the exponent $-x^2/(4a^2)$, which is this one with $a$ replaced by $\sqrt2\,a$: then $\Delta x = a$ and $\Delta p = \hbar/(2a)$, the values found for it in Problem Set 4.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Find the Fourier transform $\tilde\Phi(p)$ of $\psi(x)$, with the lesson's convention

<Formula label="Fourier transform to momentum space" tex="\tilde\Phi(p) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi(x)\,e^{-ipx/\hbar}\,dx" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.11)" />

Confirm your result with Parseval's theorem, and then find $\Delta p$ again, now in momentum space. In the box, type $\tilde\Phi(p)$ divided by $N$.

<Hint>

The integral is the first one of the sheet. Read off $\alpha$ and $\beta$ from the exponent $-x^2/(2a^2) - ipx/\hbar$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the second half: $|\tilde\Phi(p)|^2$ is a Gaussian in $p$. Use the second integral of the sheet again, with $p$ in place of $x$ and a new $\alpha$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-phi" label="$\tilde\Phi(p)/N =$" expr="a/sqrt(hbar)*exp(-a^2*p^2/(2*hbar^2))"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 1.5], p: [0.2, 1.5], hbar: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2*pi)*a*exp(-a^2*p^2/(2*hbar^2))", hint: "That is the integral alone. The transform has the factor 1/√(2πħ) in front." },
    { expr: "a/sqrt(hbar)*exp(-a^2*p^2/(4*hbar^2))", hint: "Check β²/(4α): with α = 1/(2a²) and β = −ip/ħ it is −a²p²/(2ħ²)." },
    { expr: "a/sqrt(hbar)*exp(-p^2/(2*a^2*hbar^2))", hint: "A packet that is wide in x must be narrow in p: a² multiplies p² in the exponent. As written, the exponent is also not a pure number." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The Fourier transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian with the inverse width.

<Step title="The transform">

With $\alpha = 1/(2a^2)$ and $\beta = -ip/\hbar$ the first integral gives $\sqrt{\pi/\alpha} = \sqrt{2\pi}\,a$ and $\beta^2/(4\alpha) = -a^2p^2/(2\hbar^2)$:

<Formula label="The real Gaussian in momentum space" tex="\tilde\Phi(p) = \frac{N}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\,\sqrt{2\pi}\,a\,\exp\left(-\frac{a^2p^2}{2\hbar^2}\right) = \frac{N a}{\sqrt\hbar}\,\exp\left(-\frac{a^2p^2}{2\hbar^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Parseval's theorem">

The theorem (Chapter 8 of the notes, p. 3, eq. (1.12)) says that $\int|\psi|^2dx = \int|\tilde\Phi|^2dp$. On the left, $\alpha = 1/a^2$ gives $N^2\sqrt\pi\,a$. On the right, $|\tilde\Phi|^2 = (N^2a^2/\hbar)\,e^{-a^2p^2/\hbar^2}$ has $\alpha = a^2/\hbar^2$:

<Formula label="Parseval's theorem for the Gaussian" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\tilde\Phi(p)\right|^2dp = \frac{N^2a^2}{\hbar}\,\sqrt{\frac{\pi\hbar^2}{a^2}} = N^2\sqrt\pi\,a = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|\psi(x)\right|^2dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The two sides agree.

</Step>

<Step title="The momentum uncertainty again">

$|\tilde\Phi(p)|^2$ is even in $p$, so $\langle\hat p\rangle = 0$, and with $\alpha = a^2/\hbar^2$

<Formula label="Momentum uncertainty from momentum space" tex="\langle\hat p^2\rangle = \frac{\int p^2\left|\tilde\Phi\right|^2dp}{\int\left|\tilde\Phi\right|^2dp} = \frac{1}{2\alpha} = \frac{\hbar^2}{2a^2}, \qquad \Delta p = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt2\,a}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

the same as in part (a).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $|\tilde\Phi|^2dp$ must have the units of $|\psi|^2dx = N^2 \times$ length. Indeed $N^2a^2/\hbar$ times a momentum is $N^2a \times (a\,p/\hbar)$, and $a\,p/\hbar$ is a pure number. If $N$ is chosen to normalize $\psi$, $N^2 = 1/(\sqrt\pi\,a)$, then $\tilde\Phi$ is normalized too.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Complex Gaussians and the uncertainty product" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

Now let the width of the Gaussian be complex:

<Formula label="The complex Gaussian" tex="\psi(x) = N\exp\left(-\frac12\,\frac{x^2}{\Delta^2}\right), \qquad \Delta \in \mathbb{C}, \qquad \text{Re}\left(\Delta^2\right) > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, eq. (1), p. 1" />

$N$ is a real constant and $\Delta$ is a complex number that is not real. The integrals of Problem 1 help again. So does a relation that holds for every complex number $z$ other than zero, and that MIT's sheet asks you to prove:

<Formula label="Real part of an inverse" tex="\text{Re}\left(\frac1z\right) = \frac{\text{Re}(z)}{|z|^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Prove the relation for $\text{Re}(1/z)$. Then use the wavefunction in position space to find $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ in terms of $|\Delta|$ and $\text{Re}(\Delta^2)$. (MIT's sheet tells you what to expect: $\Delta x$ depends on both, and $\Delta p$ only on $\text{Re}(\Delta^2)$. A footnote adds that $\Delta x$ can be written with $\text{Re}(1/\Delta^2)$ alone.)

<Hint>

The density is $|\psi|^2 = \psi^*\psi$. The two exponents add to $-x^2\,\text{Re}(1/\Delta^2)$, so the density is a real Gaussian and Problem 1 applies with a new $\alpha$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the momentum: $\psi' = -(x/\Delta^2)\,\psi$, so $|\psi'|^2 = x^2|\psi|^2/|\Delta|^4$. Then use the relation with $z = \Delta^2$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The modulus squared of a complex Gaussian is a real Gaussian, whose width is set by $\text{Re}(1/\Delta^2)$.

<Step title="The relation">

Multiply the top and the bottom of $1/z$ by the complex conjugate $z^*$: $1/z = z^*/|z|^2$. The bottom is real, and $\text{Re}(z^*) = \text{Re}(z)$, which proves the relation.

</Step>

<Step title="The uncertainty in position">

$|\psi|^2 = N^2\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2}\left(\frac{1}{\Delta^2} + \frac{1}{(\Delta^*)^2}\right)\right) = N^2\exp\left(-x^2\,\text{Re}(1/\Delta^2)\right)$. This is the density of Problem 1 with $\alpha = \text{Re}(1/\Delta^2) = \text{Re}(\Delta^2)/|\Delta|^4$, which is positive, so the state can be normalized. The density is even, so $\langle\hat x\rangle = 0$ and

<Formula label="Position uncertainty of the complex Gaussian" tex="\langle\hat x^2\rangle = \frac{1}{2\,\text{Re}\left(1/\Delta^2\right)} = \frac{|\Delta|^4}{2\,\text{Re}\left(\Delta^2\right)}, \qquad \Delta x = \frac{|\Delta|^2}{\sqrt{2\,\text{Re}\left(\Delta^2\right)}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The uncertainty in momentum">

$\psi^*\hat p\,\psi = -\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{x}{\Delta^2}|\psi|^2$ is odd in $x$, so $\langle\hat p\rangle = 0$, although $\psi$ is not real. With $|\psi'|^2 = x^2|\psi|^2/|\Delta|^4$:

<Formula label="Momentum uncertainty of the complex Gaussian" tex="\langle\hat p^2\rangle = \hbar^2\,\frac{\int\left|\psi'\right|^2dx}{\int\left|\psi\right|^2dx} = \frac{\hbar^2}{|\Delta|^4}\,\langle\hat x^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\,\text{Re}\left(\Delta^2\right)}, \qquad \Delta p = \frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2\,\text{Re}\left(\Delta^2\right)}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a real $\Delta = a$ both $|\Delta|^2$ and $\text{Re}(\Delta^2)$ are $a^2$, and the results of Problem 1 come back. $\Delta x$ has units of length ($|\Delta|^2$ over $|\Delta|$) and $\Delta p$ of $\hbar$ over a length.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Step title="The transform">

With $\alpha = 1/(2\Delta^2)$ and $\beta = -ip/\hbar$:

<Formula label="The complex Gaussian in momentum space" tex="\tilde\Phi(p) = \frac{N}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\,\sqrt{2\pi\Delta^2}\,\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta^2p^2}{2\hbar^2}\right) = \frac{N\Delta}{\sqrt\hbar}\,\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta^2p^2}{2\hbar^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

(1.12), for the theorem" />

as in part (a).

</Step>

</Step>

<Formula label="Uncertainty product of the complex Gaussian" tex="\Delta x\,\Delta p = \frac{|\Delta|^2}{\sqrt{2\,\text{Re}\left(\Delta^2\right)}}\times\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt{2\,\text{Re}\left(\Delta^2\right)}} = \frac{\hbar}{2}\,\frac{|\Delta|^2}{\text{Re}\left(\Delta^2\right)} = \frac{\hbar}{2\cos 2\phi_\Delta}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The product has the units of $\hbar$, and it is even in $\phi_\Delta$: replacing $\Delta$ by $\Delta^*$ replaces $\psi$ by $\psi^*$, which changes neither density.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A hard wall" points={5} mit="Problem 4">

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

<Answer id="p4-energy" label="$E =$" expr="hbar^2*k^2/(2*m)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], k: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "hbar*k^2/(2*m)", hint: "That is the angular frequency ω = E/ħ, with units 1/s. The energy has one more factor ħ." },
    { expr: "hbar^2*k^2/m", hint: "The kinetic energy is p²/(2m) with p = ħk." },
    { expr: "hbar*k", hint: "That is the momentum p = ħk. The energy is p²/(2m)." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-spectrum" choices={["Every E > 0 is allowed, with two independent states for each, as for a free particle on the whole line", "Every E > 0 is allowed, with exactly one state for each", "Only a discrete list of energies, as in the box, because the wavefunction must vanish at the wall", "Every E, positive or negative, because the states need not be normalized"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One wall gives one condition, $\psi(0) = 0$. It removes one of the two free solutions at each energy and leaves the energy itself free.

<Step title="The equation and the wall">

For $x \le 0$ the wavefunction vanishes, and it is continuous, so $\psi(0) = 0$ (Chapter 10 of the notes, p. 5, case (4) above eq. (2.23)). For $x > 0$ the equation is $\psi'' = -\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\psi$.

</Step>

<Step title="Positive energies">

With $k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2$ and $k > 0$ the general solution is $A\sin kx + B\cos kx$, and $\psi(0) = 0$ removes the cosine:

<Formula label="Stationary states of the hard wall" tex="\psi_k(x) = \begin{cases} A\sin kx, & x > 0 \\ 0, & x \le 0 \end{cases} \qquad E = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}, \qquad k > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Nothing restricts $k$: there is no second wall. So every $E > 0$ is allowed, and for each there is one state (up to the constant $A$). $-k$ gives the same state with the other sign. The state cannot be normalized, since $\int_0^\infty\sin^2kx\,dx$ is infinite, like a plane wave.

</Step>

<Step title="Zero and negative energies">

For $E < 0$, with $\kappa^2 = -2mE/\hbar^2$, the solutions are $e^{\kappa x}$ and $e^{-\kappa x}$, and the only combination that vanishes at $x = 0$ is $\sinh\kappa x$, which grows without limit as $x \to \infty$. For $E = 0$ the solution that vanishes at the wall is $\psi = Ax$, which also grows without limit. A wavefunction that blows up at infinity is not acceptable, not even in the loose sense in which a sine wave is, so there are no states with $E \le 0$. (Problem 8 shows why no normalizable state can have $E$ below the lowest value of $V$.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\sin kx = \left(e^{ikx} - e^{-ikx}\right)/(2i)$: a wave running towards the wall and a reflected wave of the same size running away from it. The wall reflects everything, and the two running waves, which are two independent states for a free particle, are tied into one. The box of Problem 3 adds a second wall, a second condition, and with it the discrete list $k_n = n\pi/a$.

</Step>

<Answer id="p5-delta" label="$\delta$ for $E = V_0/4$:" value="60" unit="degrees" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-delta" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The step pushes the standing wave on the left by a phase that depends on the energy.

<Step title="Counting">

Part (b) left one free constant and no condition on $E$: every energy between 0 and $V_0$ has exactly one stationary state. Multiply the equation by $\psi$ and integrate over all $x$. With $\int\psi^2dx = 1$, and one integration by parts in the kinetic term (the boundary term vanishes because a normalizable $\psi$ goes to zero at both ends):

<Formula label="Energy of a real stationary state" tex="E = \langle\hat H\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left(\psi'\right)^2dx + \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}V(x)\,\psi^2\,dx" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.31), for the kinetic term" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two bounds">

The first term is positive: it is an integral of a square, and it is zero only if $\psi' = 0$ everywhere, which would make $\psi$ a constant that cannot be normalized. For the second, call the lowest value of the potential $V_\text{min}$. Since $\psi^2 \ge 0$,

<Formula label="The energy exceeds the minimum of the potential" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}V(x)\,\psi^2\,dx \ \ge\ V_\text{min}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi^2\,dx = V_\text{min} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad E > V_\text{min}" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The box of Problem 3 has $V_\text{min} = 0$ and its lowest energy is $\pi^2\hbar^2/(2ma^2) > 0$. In Problem 7 the level lies 10.1 eV above the bottom of the well. In both, the gap is the kinetic energy that confinement costs.

</Step>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Explain the claim in another way: try to sketch a wavefunction with $E < V(x)$ at every $x$, so that every point is in the region a classical particle could not reach, and see why it fails. The choice box asks for the key fact. Then compare your reasoning with the solution.

<Hint>

Write the equation as $\psi'' = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\left(V - E\right)\psi$. What is the sign of $\psi''$ where $\psi$ is positive? What does that mean for the shape of the graph?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-shape" choices={["ψ must be discontinuous wherever E is below V", "ψ″ has the opposite sign to ψ everywhere, so the graph oscillates for ever and never decays", "ψ″ is zero everywhere, so the graph is a straight line", "ψ″ has the sign of ψ everywhere, so the graph always bends away from the x axis and cannot come back to zero on both sides"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a forbidden region the wavefunction curves away from the axis. If every point is forbidden, it can never turn back.

<Step title="The shape">

With $E < V(x)$ everywhere, $\psi'' = \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(V - E)\,\psi$ has the same sign as $\psi$ at every point (the lesson's reading of Chapter 10, p. 4, eq. (2.22)). Where $\psi > 0$ the graph is curved upwards, like a bowl. Where $\psi < 0$ it is curved downwards.

</Step>

<Step title="The sketch fails">

Start the sketch at a point where $\psi > 0$ (if $\psi$ is negative there, flip its sign). If the slope there is zero or positive, then to the right the upward curvature makes the slope grow, $\psi$ stays positive and keeps rising, and it never returns to zero: the function blows up as $x \to \infty$. If the slope is negative, run the same argument to the left: going left the function rises, with ever steeper slope, and blows up as $x \to -\infty$. A function that decays on both sides would need a maximum in between where $\psi > 0$, and at a maximum $\psi'' \le 0$, which the equation forbids. So no normalizable wavefunction exists. A bound state needs a region with $E > V$, where the graph curves back towards the axis.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The pieces met in this set fit the picture. $e^{-\kappa x}$ in the step of Problem 5 is a forbidden-region solution that decays to the right, and it grows to the left, where it is saved only by joining an allowed region. $\sinh\kappa x$ in Problem 4 is the forbidden-region solution that vanishes at the wall, and it blows up on the other side.

</Step>
