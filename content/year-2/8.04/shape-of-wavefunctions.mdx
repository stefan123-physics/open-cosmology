Before solving more potentials, it pays to know what all their energy eigenstates have in common. This lecture proves that they can be taken real, and even or odd when the potential is, then uses classical intuition to predict their local wavelength and amplitude, and finally reads off their shapes from the sign of their curvature (notes pp. 1–11). (MIT's material for this lecture is the first three sections of Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 12; the last section, the shooting method, is the next page. This page follows the notes' order in its own words, with its own worked example; the notes' figures are not reproduced.)

## Two facts, and real wavefunctions

Two facts, proved as homework in the course (notes p. 1): for the equation

<Formula label="Time-independent Schrödinger equation" tex="-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + \left(V(x) - E\right)\psi = 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.1)" />

there are no energy eigenstates with $E$ below the minimum of $V$; and a one-dimensional potential on the whole line has **no degenerate bound states** (bound states being normalizable, so $\psi \to 0$ as $x \to \pm\infty$).

**Theorem 1: eigenstates can be chosen real** (notes pp. 1–2). Write the equation as $\psi'' + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E - V)\psi = 0$. Since $V$ and $E$ are real, conjugating shows that $\psi^*$ solves the same equation with the same energy. Then so do the real combinations

<Formula label="Real and imaginary parts" tex="\psi_r \equiv \frac12\left(\psi + \psi^*\right), \qquad \psi_{im} \equiv \frac{1}{2i}\left(\psi - \psi^*\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.4)" />

the real and imaginary parts of $\psi$. If $\psi^*$ is just a constant times $\psi$, the two give the same real solution; either way a real solution is available.

**Corollary 1** (notes p. 2). A bound state of a one-dimensional potential is real up to an overall constant phase. With no degeneracy, $\psi_{im} = c\,\psi_r$ with $c$ real, so $\psi = (1 + ic)\psi_r$, and $1 + ic = \sqrt{1 + c^2}\,e^{i\beta}$ is a constant times a phase.

**Theorem 2: an even potential allows even and odd eigenstates** (notes pp. 2–3). If $V(-x) = V(x)$, replacing $x$ by $-x$ in the equation shows (after checking that two derivatives of $\varphi(x) \equiv \psi(-x)$ give $\psi''(-x)$) that $\psi(-x)$ is a solution with the same energy (eqs. (1.6)–(1.9)). Their combinations are even and odd:

<Formula label="Even and odd combinations" tex="\psi_s(x) \equiv \frac12\left(\psi(x) + \psi(-x)\right), \qquad \psi_a(x) \equiv \frac12\left(\psi(x) - \psi(-x)\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.10)" />

The proof needs an even potential; for an odd potential nothing similar can be said.

**Corollary 2** (notes p. 3). Any bound state of a one-dimensional even potential is even or odd. With no degeneracy, $\psi(-x)$ and $\psi(x)$ are the same state, and taking $\psi$ real, $\psi(-x) = c\,\psi(x)$ with $c$ real (eq. (1.11)). Applying this twice gives $\psi(x) = c^2\psi(x)$, so $c = \pm 1$. This is why the finite square well's search could be split into even and odd states: no bound state can be neither.

## Bound states in slowly varying potentials

Classical physics gives useful hints about eigenstates: the **semiclassical approximation** (notes p. 3). The total energy $E = V + K$ is fixed, so where $V$ varies the kinetic energy $K$ varies too.

**Constant potential** (notes pp. 3–4). With $V$ constant and below $E$, $K$ and $p = \sqrt{2mK}$ are constant, and the equation $\psi'' = -\frac{p^2}{\hbar^2}\psi$ has real solutions such as

<Formula label="Wavelength from the classical momentum" tex="\psi \sim \cos\left(\frac{p}{\hbar}x\right) = \cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{h/p}\,x\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (2.2)" />

whose wavelength is the de Broglie wavelength of the classical momentum. A real $\psi$ is not a momentum eigenstate: it superposes momenta $p$ and $-p$, just as the classical $K$ fixes only $p^2$, not the sign of $p$.

**Slowly varying potential** (notes pp. 4–5). If $V$ changes slowly, $\psi$ looks locally like such a wave, with a **position-dependent de Broglie wavelength**

<Formula label="Local de Broglie wavelength" tex="\lambda(x) = \frac{h}{p(x)}, \qquad p(x) = \sqrt{2m\left(E - V(x)\right)}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.3)" />

so $\psi$ is some combination of $\cos(2\pi x/\lambda(x))$ and $\sin(2\pi x/\lambda(x))$ (eq. (2.4)). In a rising potential $K$ falls, $p$ falls and the wavelength grows. "Slowly" means that the change of $V$ over one local wavelength is small compared with $V$ itself (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Condition for the semiclassical approximation" tex="\lambda(x)\left|\frac{dV}{dx}\right| \ll \left|V(x)\right|" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.5)" />

This condition underlies the WKB approximation, developed in 8.06. (As written, the condition changes if a constant is added to $V$; the more usual form compares the change of $V$ over a wavelength with the kinetic energy $E - V$.)

**Turning points** (notes p. 5). A classical particle of energy $E$ can only be where $V(x) \le E$; where $V > E$ the region is **classically forbidden**. Between the **turning points** $x_L$ and $x_R$, where $V = E$, it oscillates back and forth, with a velocity $v(x)$ that depends on position.

**The amplitude** (notes p. 6). The particle spends more time where it moves slowly, and the semiclassical wavefunction is correspondingly larger there. Setting the probability of finding it in $dx$ equal to the fraction of a half-period $T$ it spends there, $dt/T = dx/(v(x)T)$, gives $|\psi|^2 \propto 1/p(x)$ (eqs. (2.6)–(2.7)). Since $|\psi|^2$ oscillates rapidly, this refers to its average over a few oscillations, the square of the wave's amplitude (notes p. 6):

<Formula label="Amplitude of the wave" tex="\text{Amp}\left(\psi(x)\right) \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{p(x)}} \propto \sqrt{\lambda(x)}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (2.8)" />

**The importance of averaging** (notes pp. 6–7). In an infinite well of width $a$, the classical particle bounces at constant speed, so it is equally likely to be anywhere: $P_{cl}(x) = 1/a$ (eq. (2.9)). The quantum density of a highly excited state, $P_{qm}(x) = \frac2a\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{a}$, oscillates wildly and has many zeros (eqs. (2.10)–(2.11)). But averaged over a stretch containing many oscillations, $\sin^2$ averages to $\frac12$ and (notes p. 7)

<Formula label="Quantum average equals classical probability" tex="\text{Average}\left(P_{qm}(x)\right) \simeq \frac2a \cdot \frac12 = \frac1a = P_{cl}(x)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eq. (2.12)" />

This is an instance of the loosely named **correspondence principle**: in some limits quantum states can be understood classically.

## Sketching wavefunctions

Dividing the equation by $\psi$ leaves no wavefunction on the right (notes p. 8):

<Formula label="Curvature relative to the function" tex="\frac{\psi''(x)}{\psi(x)} = -\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\left(E - V(x)\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 8, eq. (3.1)" />

- **Classically forbidden** ($E < V$): the right side is positive, so $\psi''$ has the same sign as $\psi$. The wavefunction is **convex towards the axis**: it bends away from it, and in a forbidden region reaching to $\pm\infty$ it must decay towards the axis (notes p. 8).
- **Classically allowed** ($E > V$): $\psi''$ has the opposite sign to $\psi$, so the wavefunction is **concave towards the axis**; over a long allowed region it oscillates like a sine or cosine (notes pp. 8–9).
- **Turning points** ($V(x_0) = E$): $\psi''(x_0) = 0$, an inflection point (notes p. 9, eq. (3.2)). Not every inflection point is a turning point: a node, $\psi = 0$, also makes $\psi'' = 0$. But $\psi$ and $\psi'$ can never both vanish at one point: the equation would then make every higher derivative vanish there too, and $\psi$ would be zero everywhere.

**Why only some energies work** (notes pp. 9–11). Take an even potential and integrate inwards from large $x$, starting with a decaying $\psi > 0$. At the origin the solution must join smoothly onto its even or its odd mirror image. Try increasing energies (slopes here are the usual $d\psi/dx$, so a function decaying to the right has a negative slope):

1. At a low energy the turning point is close in; $\psi$ bends towards the axis there but still has a negative slope at $x = 0$: it is still rising as one moves left. The even extension would have a kink, and the odd one a jump: no solution.
2. At one special energy the slope reaches exactly zero at $x = 0$: the even extension fits. This is the **ground state**.
3. A little higher, the slope at the origin has turned positive while $\psi$ is still positive: again no fit.
4. Higher still, $\psi$ reaches exactly zero at the origin, with a positive slope: the odd extension fits. This is the **first excited state**, odd, with one node at $x = 0$.

<WorkedExample title="Averages, wavelengths and amplitudes">
<Problem>
(a) For the $n = 50$ state of an infinite well of width $a$, find the quantum probability of being in $0 < x < 0.125a$, and compare with the classical value. (b) An electron of fixed energy moves through a slowly rising potential. At $x_1$ its kinetic energy is 4.0 eV, at $x_2$ it is 1.0 eV. Find its local de Broglie wavelengths ($hc = 1239.8$ eV nm, $m_ec^2 = 511{,}000$ eV) and the ratio of the amplitudes of $\psi$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Quantum against classical">
$\frac2a\int_0^L\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{a}dx = \frac{L}{a} - \frac{\sin(2n\pi L/a)}{2n\pi}$. With $L = 0.125a$ and $n = 50$, $\sin(12.5\pi) = 1$, so the probability is $0.125 - \frac{1}{100\pi} \approx 0.1218$, against the classical $0.125$: within 3%, and the difference shrinks as $n$ grows.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A slowing electron">
$\lambda = \frac{hc}{\sqrt{2m_ec^2K}}$: at $x_1$, $\frac{1239.8}{\sqrt{2 \times 511{,}000 \times 4.0}} \approx 0.613$ nm; at $x_2$, twice that, $\approx 1.23$ nm. The amplitude goes as $\sqrt\lambda$, so it is $\sqrt2 \approx 1.41$ times larger at $x_2$, where the electron moves at half the speed.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- In one dimension bound states are not degenerate; eigenstates can be chosen real, and bound states are real up to a phase; for an even potential they are even or odd.
- Semiclassically, $\psi$ has the local wavelength $h/p(x)$ and an amplitude $\propto 1/\sqrt{p(x)}$, valid when $V$ changes little over a wavelength; averaged over oscillations, $|\psi|^2$ reproduces the classical probability.
- $\psi''/\psi = -\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E - V)$: concave towards the axis where allowed, convex where forbidden, with inflection points at turning points; $\psi$ and $\psi'$ never vanish together.
- Matching an inward-integrated solution to its even or odd mirror image works only at special energies: the origin of quantization.
