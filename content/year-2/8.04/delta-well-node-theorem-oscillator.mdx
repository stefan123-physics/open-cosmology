Three topics: the simplest potential with a bound state, a theorem that orders all bound states by their nodes, and the first steps with the most important potential of all, the harmonic oscillator (notes pp. 1–12). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 13; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example. The notes' figures are not reproduced. Notation: the page writes $\gamma$ for the notes' unknown number #, $\eta$ for their small integration half-width $\epsilon$, and $\varepsilon$ for their unit-free oscillator energy, a script E.)

## The delta-function well

The potential vanishes everywhere except at the origin, where it is infinitely deep (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="Delta-function well" tex="V(x) = -\alpha\,\delta(x), \qquad \alpha > 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.1)" />

A bound state needs $E < 0$, so that every point but the origin is classically forbidden and the wavefunction can decay. Think of the delta function as the limit of ever narrower and deeper square wells of fixed area: the short middle region bends the wavefunction over, and in the limit it leaves a kink, a jump in $\psi'$ (notes pp. 1–2).

**Units first** (notes p. 2). A delta function has units of 1/length, so $\alpha$ has units of energy times length. With $\hbar$ and $m$, energy also has units of $\hbar^2/(mL^2)$. Eliminating $L$, the only energy that can be built is $m\alpha^2/\hbar^2$, so any bound-state energy must be (eqs. (1.2)–(1.5))

<Formula label="Bound-state energy from units" tex="E_b = -\gamma\,\frac{m\alpha^2}{\hbar^2}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.5)" />

with $\gamma$ a pure number (the notes write it as #). Reassuringly, a stronger delta function gives a deeper state.

**Solving** (notes p. 3). Away from the origin $\psi'' = \kappa^2\psi$ with $\kappa^2 = -2mE/\hbar^2 > 0$, so the solutions are $e^{\pm\kappa x}$ (eqs. (1.7)–(1.8)). The potential is even, so a ground state is even and nodeless, and an excited state would be odd, with a node at the origin. The only odd combination is $\sinh\kappa x$, which blows up at both ends: **there is at most one bound state**. Keeping the decaying exponential on each side and requiring continuity, $\psi = Ae^{-\kappa|x|}$ (eq. (1.9)).

**The kink** (notes pp. 3–4). Integrating the Schrödinger equation from $-\eta$ to $\eta$, the delta function contributes $-\alpha\psi(0)$, while the energy term vanishes as $\eta \to 0$. So the slope jumps by

<Formula label="Jump in the slope at a delta function" tex="\lim_{\eta \to 0}\left(\psi'(\eta) - \psi'(-\eta)\right) = -\frac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}\,\psi(0)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eqs. (1.13)–(1.14)" />

proportional to the wavefunction there; a delta function at a node would have no effect. For $Ae^{-\kappa|x|}$ the jump is $-2\kappa A$, so $\kappa = m\alpha/\hbar^2$ and (notes p. 4, eqs. (1.15)–(1.17))

<Formula label="The delta-function bound state" tex="E_b = -\frac{\hbar^2\kappa^2}{2m} = -\frac12\,\frac{m\alpha^2}{\hbar^2}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (1.17)" />

The number $\gamma$ that the units could not fix is $\frac12$.

## The node theorem

The infinite square well's states $\sqrt{2/a}\sin(n\pi x/a)$ have energies rising with $n$, and the $n$th has $n - 1$ nodes (the walls do not count) (notes p. 4, eqs. (2.18)–(2.20)). This is general:

> **Node theorem.** For a continuous potential with $V \to \infty$ as $|x| \to \infty$, the bound states $\psi_1, \psi_2, \psi_3, \dots$, ordered by energy, have $0, 1, 2, \dots$ nodes: $\psi_n$ has $n - 1$ (notes p. 5).

The notes give an intuitive, non-rigorous argument. Put the origin at a minimum of $V$ and define a **screened potential**: $V$ inside $|x| < a$, infinite walls outside (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Screened potential" tex="V_a(x) = \begin{cases} V(x), & |x| < a \\ \infty, & |x| > a \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.21)" />

Two plausible assumptions: as $a \to \infty$ the bound states of $V_a$ become those of $V$, and as $a$ grows the wavefunctions and their slopes deform continuously (notes p. 5). For tiny $a$, $V_a$ is a narrow well with a flat bottom (any minimum is locally flat), an infinite square well, for which the theorem holds. Now widen the screen. Could a node appear? (notes pp. 5–6)

- **At a wall.** A new node would have to enter through an endpoint, which needs $\psi'$ at that wall to change sign; in between, $\psi'$ would vanish there together with $\psi$, forcing $\psi \equiv 0$. Impossible.
- **In the middle.** Two new nodes could appear where the wavefunction dips down to cross the axis; but at the moment it first touches the axis, $\psi = \psi' = 0$ there. Impossible again.

So stretching the screen never changes the number of nodes. Nor can two neighbouring levels swap order, since at the swap they would be degenerate, which never happens for one-dimensional bound states. The ordering by nodes of the tiny well survives to $V$ itself (notes pp. 6–7).

## The harmonic oscillator: setting up

Classically, a mass $m$ on a spring of constant $k$ oscillates at $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, with energy (notes p. 7, eqs. (3.22)–(3.25))

<Formula label="Classical oscillator energy" tex="E = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac12m\omega^2x^2" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eq. (3.25)" />

The quadratic potential is everywhere in physics because near a minimum $x_0$ any potential is approximately $V(x_0) + \frac12V''(x_0)(x - x_0)^2$, an oscillator with $k = V''(x_0) > 0$ (notes p. 7, eqs. (3.26)–(3.27)). The **quantum harmonic oscillator** takes the same energy with operators (notes p. 7):

<Formula label="Oscillator Hamiltonian" tex="\hat H = \frac{\hat p^2}{2m} + \frac12m\omega^2\hat x^2, \qquad \left[\hat x, \hat p\right] = i\hbar" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eq. (3.28)" />

**Cleaning out the units** (notes p. 8). In $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + \frac12m\omega^2x^2\psi = E\psi$, the only energy available is $\hbar\omega$. Write $x = au$ with $u$ unit-free, choosing $a$ by equating a typical kinetic and potential energy, $\hbar^2/(ma^2) = m\omega^2a^2$ (notes p. 8, eqs. (3.31)–(3.32)):

<Formula label="Oscillator length" tex="a^2 = \frac{\hbar}{m\omega}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 8, eq. (3.32)" />

Then both terms carry $\hbar\omega$, and defining the unit-free energy $\varepsilon \equiv 2E/(\hbar\omega)$ gives the clean equation (notes pp. 8–9, eqs. (3.33)–(3.38)):

<Formula label="Unit-free oscillator equation" tex="\frac{d^2\psi}{du^2} = \left(u^2 - \varepsilon\right)\psi, \qquad \varepsilon = \frac{2E}{\hbar\omega}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 9, eq. (3.38)" />

## Why the energies are quantized

The equation has solutions for every $\varepsilon$ (a computer would happily integrate it); quantization must come from normalizability (notes p. 9). For large $|u|$, $\psi'' \approx u^2\psi$, which no polynomial solves (the degrees would not match). Trying $u^ke^{\beta u^2/2}$, the leading term of $\psi''$ is $\beta^2u^2\psi$, so $\beta = \pm 1$: solutions behave like $Au^ke^{-u^2/2} + Bu^ke^{u^2/2}$, and only the first is normalizable (eqs. (3.39)–(3.42)). So write, without loss of generality (notes p. 9),

<Formula label="Factoring out the Gaussian" tex="\psi(u) = h(u)\,e^{-u^2/2}, \qquad \frac{d^2h}{du^2} - 2u\frac{dh}{du} + \left(\varepsilon - 1\right)h = 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 9, eqs. (3.43)–(3.44)" />

**A quick look** (notes pp. 9–10). If $h$ is a polynomial of degree $j$, the $u^j$ terms in the equation give $-2j + \varepsilon - 1 = 0$, so $\varepsilon = 2j + 1$; the $u^{j-1}$ terms then force the next coefficient to vanish, so $h$ has only every other power, and $\psi$ is even or odd, as it must be for an even potential (eqs. (3.46)–(3.48)).

**Systematically** (notes pp. 10–11). With $h = \sum_k a_ku^k$, collecting the coefficient of $u^j$ gives the recursion

<Formula label="Recursion for the coefficients" tex="a_{j+2} = \frac{2j + 1 - \varepsilon}{(j + 2)(j + 1)}\,a_j" cite="mit" at="notes p. 10, eq. (3.52)" />

Choosing $a_0$ builds an even series and $a_1$ an odd one; together they are the general solution, as $a_0 = h(0)$ and $a_1 = h'(0)$ fix a second-order equation (notes pp. 10–11). (The notes call the even series's coefficients $a_2, a_4, \dots$ "odd"; they are the even-indexed ones, a slip.) If a series never stops, then $a_{j+2}/a_j \to 2/j$ for large $j$, just like the coefficients of $e^{u^2} = \sum u^{2n}/n!$; then $\psi \sim e^{u^2}e^{-u^2/2} = e^{u^2/2}$, the bad solution (notes p. 11, eqs. (3.55)–(3.58)). So the series must stop: $a_{j+2} = 0$ with $a_j \ne 0$, which needs $\varepsilon = 2j + 1$ (eq. (3.59)). For that energy the series of the other parity does not stop, so it is not an eigenstate (notes pp. 11–12). Calling the degree $n$ (notes p. 12):

<Formula label="Oscillator energies" tex="E_n = \hbar\omega\left(n + \frac12\right), \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots" cite="mit" at="notes p. 12, eq. (3.64)" />

The levels are **evenly spaced**, and the ground state has $E_0 = \hbar\omega/2$. The polynomials, normalized to start with $2^nu^n$, are the **Hermite polynomials**, solving (notes p. 12)

<Formula label="Hermite equation and the first Hermite polynomials" tex="\frac{d^2H_n}{du^2} - 2u\frac{dH_n}{du} + 2nH_n = 0; \quad H_0 = 1, \; H_1 = 2u, \; H_2 = 4u^2 - 2, \; H_3 = 8u^3 - 12u" cite="mit" at="notes p. 12, eqs. (3.66)–(3.67)" />

They can all be generated from $e^{-z^2 + 2zu} = \sum_n\frac{z^n}{n!}H_n(u)$ (eq. (3.68)). In terms of $x$ (notes p. 12):

<Formula label="Oscillator eigenstates" tex="\psi_n(x) = N_n\,H_n\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}}\,x\right)e^{-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}x^2}, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots" cite="mit" at="notes p. 12, eq. (3.70)" />

<WorkedExample title="One kink and evenly spaced levels">
<Problem>
(a) An electron sits in a delta well with $\alpha = 1.0$ eV nm. Find its binding energy and the decay length $1/\kappa$ ($\hbar c = 197.33$ eV nm, $m_ec^2 = 511{,}000$ eV), and the probability of finding it within $1/\kappa$ of the origin. (b) Check that $H_2 = 4u^2 - 2$ solves the Hermite equation with $n = 2$, and build $h$ for $\varepsilon = 5$ from the recursion starting with $a_0 = 1$. (c) A vibrating molecule has $\hbar\omega = 0.50$ eV. List its first three levels.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The delta well">
$|E_b| = \frac{m_ec^2\alpha^2}{2(\hbar c)^2} = \frac{511{,}000 \times 1.0^2}{2 \times 197.33^2} \approx 6.56$ eV, and $\frac1\kappa = \frac{(\hbar c)^2}{m_ec^2\alpha} \approx 0.0762$ nm. Normalized, $\psi = \sqrt\kappa\,e^{-\kappa|x|}$, and the probability within $1/\kappa$ is $2\kappa\int_0^{1/\kappa}e^{-2\kappa x}dx = 1 - e^{-2} \approx 0.865$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Hermite">
$H_2'' = 8$ and $-2uH_2' = -16u^2$, while $2 \times 2 \times H_2 = 16u^2 - 8$: the sum is zero. With $\varepsilon = 5$ the recursion gives $a_2 = \frac{1 - 5}{2}a_0 = -2$ and $a_4 = \frac{5 - 5}{12}a_2 = 0$, so $h = 1 - 2u^2 = -\frac12H_2$: the same state.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Vibrational levels">
$E_n = 0.50(n + \frac12)$ eV: $0.25$, $0.75$ and $1.25$ eV, spaced by $0.50$ eV.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The attractive delta well $-\alpha\delta(x)$ has exactly one bound state, $\psi \propto e^{-\kappa|x|}$ with $\kappa = m\alpha/\hbar^2$ and $E_b = -m\alpha^2/(2\hbar^2)$; its slope jumps by $-\frac{2m\alpha}{\hbar^2}\psi(0)$.
- Node theorem: the $n$th bound state has $n - 1$ nodes, because widening a screened potential can neither create nodes nor reorder levels.
- With $x = au$, $a^2 = \hbar/(m\omega)$, the oscillator equation is $\psi'' = (u^2 - \varepsilon)\psi$; normalizability forces $\psi = H_n(u)e^{-u^2/2}$ with Hermite polynomials and $E_n = \hbar\omega(n + \frac12)$.
