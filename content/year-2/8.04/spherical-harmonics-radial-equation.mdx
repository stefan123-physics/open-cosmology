The previous page found the joint eigenstates of $\hat L_z$ and $\hat L^2$ for $m = 0$. This page completes them for all $m$, normalizes them into spherical harmonics, and then separates the radial part of the Schrödinger equation, which turns out to be a one-dimensional problem with an extra repulsive term (notes pp. 9–12). (MIT's material is the second part of Zwiebach's combined notes for Lectures 20 and 21; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example. As on the previous page, the notes' script ℓ is written $l$. MIT's calendar also lists the two-body problem and the start of the hydrogen atom for this lecture; these notes only state, without derivation, that two particles interacting through their separation lead to the same central-potential equation, and the reduction is carried out on the next page, with hydrogen.)

## Associated Legendre functions

The equation for $P_l^m(x)$, with $x = \cos\theta$, involves only $m^2$, so $m$ and $-m$ share solutions. Taking $|m|$ derivatives of a Legendre polynomial gives one (notes p. 9):

<Formula label="Associated Legendre functions" tex="P_l^m(x) = \left(1 - x^2\right)^{|m|/2}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{|m|}P_l(x)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 9, eq. (3.24)" />

Since $P_l$ has degree $l$, this vanishes once $|m| > l$. So the solutions have (notes p. 9)

<Formula label="Allowed values of m" tex="-l \le m \le l" cite="mit" at="notes p. 9, eq. (3.25)" />

and no other solutions exist. For each $l$ there are $2l + 1$ values of $m$: $-l, -l + 1, \dots, l$. They are not polynomials in general, because of the square-root factor.

## Spherical harmonics

Normalized, the eigenfunctions are the **spherical harmonics**. For $m \ge 0$ (notes p. 9),

<Formula label="Spherical harmonics" tex="Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi) = N_{lm}\,(-1)^m\,e^{im\phi}\,P_l^m(\cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 9, eq. (3.26)" />

with the normalizing constant $N_{lm}$ equal to the square root of (2l + 1)/(4π) times (l − m)!/(l + m)!, and for $m < 0$, $Y_{l,m} = (-1)^m\left(Y_{l,-m}\right)^*$ (notes p. 10, eq. (3.27)). They satisfy $\hat L_zY_{lm} = \hbar mY_{lm}$ and $\hat L^2Y_{lm} = \hbar^2l(l + 1)Y_{lm}$ (eq. (3.28)). The first few (notes p. 10):

<Formula label="The first spherical harmonics" tex="Y_{00} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi}}, \qquad Y_{1,\pm1} = \mp\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}\,e^{\pm i\phi}\sin\theta, \qquad Y_{10} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\cos\theta" cite="mit" at="notes p. 10, eqs. (3.29)–(3.31)" />

In Cartesian form these are $\mp\sqrt{3/(8\pi)}\,(x \pm iy)/r$ and $\sqrt{3/(4\pi)}\,z/r$ (the notes print $z/r^2$ for the last, a typo). Being eigenfunctions of Hermitian operators with different eigenvalues, spherical harmonics with different $l$ or $m$ are orthogonal; the factor in front makes each one normalized. Integrating over the **solid angle**, $d\Omega = \sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$ (eq. (3.32)), they form an orthonormal set (notes p. 10):

<Formula label="Orthonormality of the spherical harmonics" tex="\int d\Omega\;Y_{lm}^*(\theta, \phi)\,Y_{l'm'}(\theta, \phi) = \delta_{ll'}\,\delta_{mm'}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 10, eq. (3.33)" />

## The radial equation

Now try a product of a radial function and a spherical harmonic (notes p. 10):

<Formula label="Separating the radial part" tex="\psi(r, \theta, \phi) = R_{El}(r)\,Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 10, eq. (4.1)" />

Inserted into the Schrödinger equation of the previous page, $\hat L^2$ acting on $Y_{lm}$ gives $\hbar^2l(l + 1)$, the spherical harmonic cancels, and only $r$ is left (eqs. (4.2)–(4.4)). With $u_{El}(r) \equiv rR_{El}(r)$ (eq. (4.5)), the result is the **radial equation** (notes p. 11):

<Formula label="Radial equation" tex="-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u_{El}}{dr^2} + \left(V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2l(l + 1)}{2mr^2}\right)u_{El} = E\,u_{El}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 11, eq. (4.6)" />

It is a one-dimensional Schrödinger equation with an **effective potential** (notes p. 11):

<Formula label="Effective potential" tex="V_{\text{eff}}(r) = V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2l(l + 1)}{2mr^2}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 11, eq. (4.7)" />

the original potential plus a **centrifugal** term, repulsive and proportional to the squared angular momentum. The equation differs for each $l$, but $m$ never appears: one radial solution serves all $2l + 1$ values of $m$ (notes p. 11). Hence the labels $E$ and $l$ on $R$ and $u$.

For l = 0 there is no barrier; for l ≥ 1 the effective potential rises steeply near the origin and keeps the particle away from it." />

**Normalization** (notes p. 11). With $\psi = \frac{u_{El}}{r}Y_{lm}$ and $d^3x = r^2dr\,d\Omega$, the factors of $r$ cancel and the angular integral gives 1 (eqs. (4.8)–(4.9)):

<Formula label="Normalization of u" tex="\int_0^\infty dr\,\left|u_{El}\right|^2 = 1" cite="mit" at="notes p. 11, eq. (4.10)" />

So $u$ is a one-dimensional wavefunction on the half-line $r > 0$, and its behaviour at $r = 0$ must be found.

**Near the origin** (notes pp. 11–12). Assume the centrifugal term dominates the potential as $r \to 0$. Then $u'' \simeq \frac{l(l + 1)}{r^2}u$, and $u = r^s$ works if $s(s - 1) = l(l + 1)$, that is $s = l + 1$ or $s = -l$ (eqs. (4.11)–(4.14)). For $l > 0$ the second blows up too fast to be normalized; for $l = 0$ it would give $R \sim 1/r$, which does not solve the Schrödinger equation at the origin. So, for all $l$ (notes p. 12),

<Formula label="Behaviour at the origin" tex="u_{El} \sim c\,r^{l+1}, \qquad R_{El} \sim c\,r^l \qquad \text{as } r \to 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 12, eqs. (4.15)–(4.16)" />

$u$ vanishes at the origin even for $l = 0$: effectively there is a wall at $r = 0$, as negative $r$ makes no sense. And $R$ can be nonzero at the origin only for $l = 0$: for $l > 0$ the angular momentum barrier keeps the particle away from the centre.

<Problem>
(a) Use the derivative formula to find $P_1^1$ and $P_2^1$ as functions of $\theta$. (b) Check that $Y_{10}$ is normalized. (c) For an electron with $l = 1$, find the centrifugal energy at $r = 0.10$ nm ($\hbar c = 197.33$ eV nm, $m_ec^2 = 511{,}000$ eV). (d) Check that $u = r^{l+1}$ and $u = r^{-l}$ satisfy $s(s - 1) = l(l + 1)$ for $l = 2$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Associated Legendre functions">
$P_1^1 = (1 - x^2)^{1/2}\frac{d}{dx}x = \sin\theta$, and $P_2^1 = (1 - x^2)^{1/2}\frac{d}{dx}\frac12(3x^2 - 1) = 3\cos\theta\sin\theta$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Normalization">
$\int d\Omega\,|Y_{10}|^2 = \frac{3}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \frac{3}{4\pi} \times 2\pi \times \frac23 = 1$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Centrifugal energy">
$\frac{\hbar^2l(l + 1)}{2m_er^2} = \frac{(\hbar c)^2 \times 2}{2m_ec^2r^2} = \frac{197.33^2 \times 2}{2 \times 511{,}000 \times 0.010} \approx 7.6$ eV: comparable with atomic energies, so the barrier matters at atomic distances.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) Exponents">
For $s = 3$: $3 \times 2 = 6 = 2 \times 3$. For $s = -2$: $(-2)(-3) = 6$. Both work; only $r^3$ is acceptable near the origin.
</Step>

## Summary

- $P_l^m = (1 - x^2)^{|m|/2}\frac{d^{|m|}}{dx^{|m|}}P_l$ exists only for $|m| \le l$: each $l$ has $2l + 1$ values of $m$.
- The spherical harmonics $Y_{lm} \propto e^{im\phi}P_l^m(\cos\theta)$ are orthonormal over the sphere and are the joint eigenstates of $\hat L_z$ and $\hat L^2$.
- With $\psi = \frac{u(r)}{r}Y_{lm}$, the radial equation is a one-dimensional Schrödinger equation with $V_{\text{eff}} = V + \hbar^2l(l + 1)/(2mr^2)$, independent of $m$, and $\int_0^\infty|u|^2dr = 1$.
- Near the origin $u \sim r^{l+1}$: an effective wall at $r = 0$, and only $l = 0$ states reach the centre.
