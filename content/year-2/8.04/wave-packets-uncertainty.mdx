A wave packet is a superposition of plane waves $e^{ikx}$ with a range of wavelengths. This lecture asks how its spread in position is related to its spread in wavenumber, and so in momentum: the origin of the uncertainty principle. It then estimates how long a free packet keeps its shape, and sets out the recipe for evolving any free packet in time (notes pp. 1–7). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 7; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example. Unlike the earlier pages, these notes put a factor $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ in front of the Fourier integrals, which makes the two directions symmetric.)

## A packet and its Fourier transform

At $t = 0$ a packet has the form (notes p. 1)

<Formula label="A wave packet and its Fourier transform" tex="\Psi(x, 0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(k)\,e^{ikx}dk, \qquad \Phi(k) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Psi(x, 0)\,e^{-ikx}dx" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eqs. (1.1)–(1.2)" />

By the Fourier inversion theorem, knowing either function fixes the other. Since a plane wave $e^{ikx}$ has momentum $\hbar k$, this is the packet written as a superposition of states of definite momentum (notes p. 1).

Take $\Phi(k)$ real, positive, peaked symmetrically at $k = k_0$ with width $\Delta k$. By stationary phase, $\Psi(x, 0)$ is then centred at $x = 0$, with some width $\Delta x$ (notes p. 1). It is not real, though:

<Formula label="When the packet is real" tex="\Psi(x, 0) \text{ is real exactly when } \Phi^*(-k) = \Phi(k)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.3)" />

To see it, conjugate $\Psi(x, 0)$ and let $k \to -k$ in the integral (the two sign flips, from $dk$ and from swapping the limits, cancel): $\Psi^* = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\Phi^*(-k)e^{ikx}dk$. If $\Phi^*(-k) = \Phi(k)$ this is $\Psi$. Conversely, if $\Psi$ is real, then $\int\left(\Phi^*(-k) - \Phi(k)\right)e^{ikx}dk = 0$ for all $x$, which says that the Fourier transform of the bracket vanishes, so the bracket itself vanishes (notes p. 2, eqs. (1.4)–(1.7)). A bump at $k_0$ with no partner at $-k_0$ fails the test, so $\Psi(x, 0)$ has real and imaginary parts, both centred on $x = 0$ (notes p. 2).

## Why Δx Δk ≈ 1

Write $k = k_0 + \tilde k$, with $\tilde k$ the distance from the peak (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Measuring from the peak" tex="\Psi(x, 0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,e^{ik_0x}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi\left(k_0 + \tilde k\right)e^{i\tilde kx}\,d\tilde k" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.9)" />

The integral gets its contributions from $\tilde k$ between $-\Delta k/2$ and $\Delta k/2$, where $\Phi$ is large. Across that range the phase $\tilde kx$ changes by $\Delta k\,|x|$ (notes p. 3, eqs. (1.10)–(1.11)). If that change is small, the contributions add up; if it is large, they cancel. So $\Psi(x, 0)$ is appreciable only for $|x|$ up to some $x_0$ with $\Delta k\,x_0 \approx 1$. Calling the width $\Delta x = 2x_0$, and dropping factors of 2 that this rough argument cannot fix (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Width in x times width in k" tex="\Delta x\,\Delta k \approx 1" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.12)" />

This is not yet quantum mechanics: it is a property of Fourier transforms, true of sound and radio signals too. The quantum step is $p = \hbar k$, so $\Delta p = \hbar\Delta k$ (eq. (1.13)), and multiplying by $\hbar$ gives the rough Heisenberg product $\Delta x\,\Delta p \approx \hbar$ (eq. (1.14)). With position and momentum uncertainties defined precisely, one can prove a lower bound (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Heisenberg uncertainty product" tex="\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.15)" />

**A flat momentum distribution** (notes p. 4). Let $\Phi(k)$ be a step: height $1/\sqrt{\Delta k}$ for $-\Delta k/2 < k < \Delta k/2$ and zero elsewhere. It satisfies the reality condition, so $\Psi(x, 0)$ must be real. Integrating the exponential between the two ends:

<Formula label="The packet of a flat distribution" tex="\Psi(x, 0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\Delta k}}\int_{-\Delta k/2}^{\Delta k/2}e^{ikx}dk = \sqrt{\frac{\Delta k}{2\pi}}\;\frac{\sin\left(\Delta k\,x/2\right)}{\Delta k\,x/2}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (1.16)" />

Its central peak ends at the first zeros, $x = \pm 2\pi/\Delta k$, so the peak's full width is $4\pi/\Delta k$. The notes take the distance from the centre to the first zero as the estimate, $\Delta x \approx 2\pi/\Delta k$, which gives $\Delta x\,\Delta k \approx 2\pi$ (notes p. 4, eq. (1.17)), a constant of order a few: exactly the kind of number the rough argument cannot pin down.

## How long a packet keeps its shape

The general free solution is $\Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int\Phi(k)e^{i(kx - \omega(k)t)}dk$ (notes p. 4, eq. (2.18)). Expanding $\omega(k)$ around the peak $k_0$ to second order (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Expanding the frequency" tex="\omega(k) = \omega(k_0) + (k - k_0)\left.\frac{d\omega}{dk}\right|_{k_0} + \frac12(k - k_0)^2\left.\frac{d^2\omega}{dk^2}\right|_{k_0}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.19)" />

For a free particle $\omega = \hbar k^2/(2m)$, so $d\omega/dk = \hbar k/m = p/m$ and $d^2\omega/dk^2 = \hbar/m$; all higher derivatives vanish, so this expansion is exact (eq. (2.20)). The linear term moves the packet at the group velocity. The quadratic term distorts it, through the phase factor $e^{-\frac{i}{2}(k - k_0)^2\hbar t/m}$ (eq. (2.21)). The shape barely changes while that phase is much less than one; since $k - k_0$ is of order $\Delta k$ and $\Delta p = \hbar\Delta k$, the condition is $(\Delta p)^2t/(\hbar m) \ll 1$ (notes p. 5, eqs. (2.22)–(2.23)). Using $\Delta x\,\Delta p \approx \hbar$:

<Formula label="Condition for little change of shape" tex="t \ll \frac{\hbar m}{\left(\Delta p\right)^2} \approx \frac{m\left(\Delta x\right)^2}{\hbar}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eqs. (2.24)–(2.25)" />

Equivalently, $\frac{\Delta p}{m}t \ll \Delta x$ (eq. (2.27)). This has a clear meaning: $\Delta p/m$ is the spread in the packet's velocity, and the shape changes once that spread, acting over time, produces a spread in position comparable with the packet's width (notes p. 5). With $\ll$ these are conditions for negligible change; with $\approx$ they estimate when the change becomes noticeable (notes p. 6).

The notes' exercise: an electron localized to $\Delta x = 10^{-10}$ m stays localized for only about $m(\Delta x)^2/\hbar \approx 10^{-16}$ s, while localized to $10^{-2}$ m it would stay so for about a second (notes p. 6, eq. (2.28)).

## Evolving a free packet in time

Given $\Psi(x, 0)$, the recipe for $\Psi(x, t)$ is (notes p. 6):

1. Compute the Fourier transform $\Phi(k)$ of $\Psi(x, 0)$.
2. Write $\Psi(x, 0)$ as its superposition of plane waves, its Fourier representation.
3. Each plane wave $e^{ikx}$ evolves into $e^{i(kx - \omega(k)t)}$ with $\omega = \hbar k^2/(2m)$, so by superposition

<Formula label="The free packet at time t" tex="\Psi(x, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\Phi(k)\,e^{i\left(kx - \omega(k)t\right)}dk, \qquad \omega(k) = \frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (3.3)" />

This is the answer: it solves the Schrödinger equation and reduces to $\Psi(x, 0)$ at $t = 0$.
4. Do the $k$ integral in closed form if possible; otherwise, numerically.

**A Gaussian packet** (notes pp. 6–7). The normalized Gaussian

<Formula label="A Gaussian packet" tex="\Psi_a(x, 0) = \frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{1/4}\sqrt a}\,e^{-x^2/(4a^2)}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 6, eq. (3.4)" />

has a width of order $a$, a length. The estimate above suggests it changes shape over a time of order $ma^2/\hbar$, and the full calculation (which the notes leave to the student) gives exactly twice that (notes p. 7):

<Formula label="Time scale of a Gaussian packet" tex="\tau = \frac{2ma^2}{\hbar}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eq. (3.6)" />

The probability density starts as $|\Psi_a(x, 0)|^2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,a}e^{-x^2/(2a^2)}$, and keeps that form with $a$ replaced by a width $a(t)$ that grows with time; finding $a(t)$, and how $\tau$ enters it, is the homework (notes p. 7, eqs. (3.7)–(3.8)).

<WorkedExample title="Widths and times">
<Problem>
(a) An electron is localized to $\Delta x = 1.0 \times 10^{-10}$ m. What is the least momentum uncertainty allowed, and the corresponding spread in velocity? ($\hbar = 1.0546 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.) (b) For how long, roughly, does a proton ($m_p = 1.6726 \times 10^{-27}$ kg) localized to the same $\Delta x$ keep its shape? (c) Find $\tau$ for an electron Gaussian with $a = 1.0$ nm. (d) A flat distribution has $\Delta k = 2.0 \times 10^{10}\ \text{m}^{-1}$. Where are the first zeros of $\Psi(x, 0)$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The uncertainty bound">
$\Delta p \ge \hbar/(2\Delta x) \approx 5.27 \times 10^{-25}$ kg m/s, so $\Delta v = \Delta p/m_e \ge 5.8 \times 10^5$ m/s: an electron confined to an atom's size has a velocity uncertain by hundreds of kilometres per second.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A heavier particle">
$m_p(\Delta x)^2/\hbar = 1.6726 \times 10^{-27} \times 10^{-20}/1.0546 \times 10^{-34} \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-13}$ s, nearly two thousand times longer than for an electron, because the proton is that much heavier.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A Gaussian">
$\tau = 2m_ea^2/\hbar = 2 \times 9.109 \times 10^{-31} \times 10^{-18}/1.0546 \times 10^{-34} \approx 1.7 \times 10^{-14}$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) The flat distribution">
At $x = \pm 2\pi/\Delta k = \pm 3.1 \times 10^{-10}$ m. With the notes' estimate (centre to first zero), $\Delta x \approx 3.1 \times 10^{-10}$ m and $\Delta x\,\Delta k \approx 2\pi$; the full width of the central peak is twice that.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A packet and its momentum distribution are Fourier transforms of each other; the packet is real only if $\Phi^*(-k) = \Phi(k)$.
- Fourier analysis gives $\Delta x\,\Delta k \approx 1$; with $p = \hbar k$ this is $\Delta x\,\Delta p \approx \hbar$, and precisely $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2$.
- A free packet changes shape because $d^2\omega/dk^2 = \hbar/m \ne 0$; it keeps its shape while $t \ll m(\Delta x)^2/\hbar$, and a Gaussian's time scale is $2ma^2/\hbar$.
- To evolve a free packet: Fourier transform, attach $e^{-i\omega(k)t}$ to each plane wave, and integrate back.
