This set practises the first three lessons of the course: [the key features of quantum mechanics](/courses/8-04/key-features-of-quantum-mechanics) (complex numbers, photons), [the Mach–Zehnder interferometer and the Elitzur–Vaidman bombs](/courses/8-04/mach-zehnder-interferometer), and [the photoelectric effect, Compton scattering and matter waves](/courses/8-04/photoelectric-compton-de-broglie). The first two problems look at an atom built with classical physics, and show why it needs quantum mechanics. The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 1, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Three things differ from MIT's sheet. Where MIT asks for an explanation or a comparison, this page often adds a choice box, so that you can check your reasoning. In Problem 6 MIT asks for formulas only: the number boxes there use an input state that we chose. And in Problem 3 the mass of the rubidium atom needs the atomic mass unit, which the sheet does not give: we state it below. The sheet's pages are numbered: "p. 1" to "p. 4" below are the printed page numbers.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within 2% of ours (1% where the box says so), so keep at least three significant figures, and type it in the unit shown next to the box. Small and large numbers can be typed as `1.5e-38` or `1.5 × 10^-38`. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `n^2*lam/alpha`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Write the symbols of the box exactly as the box names them (`b` stands for $\beta$, `lam` for $\lambda_e$, `Ee` for $E_e$).

MIT gives the points per problem. The splits between parts are ours. Several parts ask you to show or to explain something: they have no box, or only a choice box, so open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

Problems 1 and 2 use the units of MIT's sheet (Gaussian units), in which the potential energy of an electron at a distance $r$ from a proton is $V = -e^2/r$, the force between them has the size $e^2/r^2$, and the fine-structure constant is $\alpha = e^2/(\hbar c)$. You never need a value for $e$ itself: write $e^2 = \alpha\hbar c$. Take these values (CODATA 2018, rounded): $\hbar c = 197.327$ MeV fm, $m_ec^2 = 0.510999$ MeV, $\alpha = 1/137.036$ and $c = 2.998\times 10^{8}$ m/s. Lengths: 1 pm $= 10^{-12}$ m and 1 fm $= 10^{-15}$ m. Problem 3 uses MIT's rounded values $h = 6.6\times 10^{-34}$ J s and $k_B = 1.38\times 10^{-23}$ J/K, and from us the atomic mass unit $1.66\times 10^{-27}$ kg and 1 eV $= 1.602\times 10^{-19}$ J.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Radiative collapse of a classical atom" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

Try to build a hydrogen atom with classical physics: an electron of mass $m_e$ on a circular orbit of radius $r$ around a proton. An accelerated charge radiates. For a slow (non-relativistic) electron the radiated power is given by the Larmor formula,

<Formula label="Larmor formula" tex="\frac{dE}{dt} = -\frac{2}{3}\,\frac{e^2a^2}{c^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

where $a$ is the size of the electron's acceleration and $E$ its total energy. So the classical atom loses energy, and it may not be stable. This problem finds out how large the effect is.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the energy $\Delta E$ that the electron radiates in one revolution, divided by its kinetic energy $K$, in terms of $\beta = v/c$, where $v$ is the orbital speed. Your result shows that $\Delta E/K$ is small for a slow electron: the orbit can then be treated as a circle at each moment, although the electron slowly spirals inwards.

<Hint>

On a circular orbit the attraction $e^2/r^2$ supplies the centripetal force. That gives $a$, and it links $e^2/r$ to $m_ev^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The energy lost in one revolution is the Larmor power times the period $2\pi r/v$. Divide by $K = \frac12 m_ev^2$ and remove $e^2$ and $r$ with the force equation.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-ratio" label="$\Delta E/K =$" expr="(8*pi/3)*b^3" vars={{ b: [0.01, 0.3] }} names={{ b: "\\beta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(4*pi/3)*b^3", hint: "You divided by m v². The kinetic energy is half of that." },
    { expr: "(4/3)*b^3", hint: "That is the loss during the time r/v, one radian of the orbit. A full revolution takes 2πr/v." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Use Newton's second law for the circular orbit to write the acceleration and the kinetic energy in terms of $v$ and $r$. Then multiply the Larmor power by the period.

<Step title="The circular orbit">

The force $e^2/r^2$ keeps the electron on its circle:

<Formula label="Force on the circular orbit" tex="\frac{m_ev^2}{r} = \frac{e^2}{r^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad a = \frac{v^2}{r}, \qquad K = \frac12 m_ev^2 = \frac{e^2}{2r}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Energy lost in one revolution">

One revolution takes the time $2\pi r/v$. If the orbit hardly changes during that time, the energy radiated is

<Formula label="Loss per revolution" tex="\Delta E = \frac{2}{3}\,\frac{e^2}{c^3}\,\frac{v^4}{r^2}\cdot\frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{e^2}{r}\,\frac{v^3}{c^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

Divide by $K = e^2/(2r)$. The factor $e^2/r$ drops out:

<Formula label="Loss per revolution over kinetic energy" tex="\frac{\Delta E}{K} = \frac{8\pi}{3}\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^3 = \frac{8\pi}{3}\,\beta^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ratio is a pure number, as a ratio of two energies must be. For $\beta = 0.01$ it is $8.4\times 10^{-6}$: the electron loses a few millionths of its kinetic energy per turn. So the assumption made in the second step, an orbit that hardly changes in one revolution, holds as long as $\beta$ is small.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Take 50 pm as the size of a hydrogen atom and 1 fm as the size of its nucleus. Find the classical orbital speed as a fraction of the speed of light, $\beta = v/c$, for orbits of radius 50 pm, 1 pm and 1 fm. Compare each with 1.

<Hint>

From the force equation, $m_ev^2 = e^2/r$. Divide both sides by $m_ec^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $e^2 = \alpha\hbar c$. Then $\beta^2$ is a length divided by $r$, and the length is $\alpha\hbar c/(m_ec^2)$. Work it out in fm.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b50" label="$\beta$ at 50 pm:" value="0.00751" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-beta-50pm" />

<Answer id="p1-b1pm" label="$\beta$ at 1 pm:" value="0.0531" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-beta-1pm" />

<Answer id="p1-b1fm" label="$\beta$ at 1 fm:" value="1.68" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-beta-1fm" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The force equation gives $\beta^2$ as the ratio of one fixed length to the orbit radius.

<Step title="A length scale">

Dividing $m_ev^2 = e^2/r$ by $m_ec^2$ gives

<Formula label="Speed on a classical orbit" tex="\beta^2 = \frac{e^2}{m_ec^2\,r} = \frac{r_e}{r}, \qquad r_e = \frac{e^2}{m_ec^2} = \frac{\alpha\,\hbar c}{m_ec^2} = \frac{197.327\ \text{MeV fm}}{137.036 \times 0.510999\ \text{MeV}} = 2.818\ \text{fm}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The length $r_e$ is called the classical electron radius. The lesson on Compton scattering met it as "about 2.8 fm" (Lecture 3 notes, p. 4).

</Step>

<Step title="The three speeds">

With $r$ in fm (50 pm is 50,000 fm and 1 pm is 1000 fm):

<Formula label="The three orbital speeds" tex="\beta = \sqrt{\frac{2.818}{50{,}000}} = 0.00751\ \text{(50 pm)}, \qquad \sqrt{\frac{2.818}{1000}} = 0.0531\ \text{(1 pm)}, \qquad \sqrt{2.818} = 1.68\ \text{(1 fm)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compare with the speed of light">

At the size of the atom the electron moves at less than 1% of the speed of light, and at 1 pm at about 5%. Both are slow enough for non-relativistic mechanics. At 1 fm the formula gives a speed above $c$, which is impossible: long before the electron reaches the nucleus, the non-relativistic treatment has broken down. By part (a), the loss per revolution at 50 pm is $\frac{8\pi}{3}\beta^3 = 3.5\times 10^{-6}$ of the kinetic energy, and at 1 pm it is $1.3\times 10^{-3}$: still small, so the orbit is still nearly a circle there.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How long does the electron take to spiral from $r_i = 50$ pm down to $r_f = 1$ pm? Give the time in seconds. Are you right to ignore relativistic corrections on this stretch? And how would the non-relativistic answer change (the choice box) if the spiral went on from 50 pm to 1 fm?

<Hint>

By part (a) the orbit is nearly a circle at each moment. So the total energy is $E = K + V$ of a circular orbit of radius $r$, and $r$ slowly shrinks. Write $E$ as a function of $r$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Set $dE/dt = (dE/dr)(dr/dt)$ equal to the Larmor power, with $a = e^2/(m_er^2)$. This gives $dr/dt$ as a function of $r$: separate the variables and integrate from $r_i$ to $r_f$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-time" label="Time from 50 pm to 1 pm:" value="1.31e-11" unit="s" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-time" />

<Answer id="p1-rel" choices={["It roughly doubles, because the electron must travel much further in", "It grows by about 8 parts in a million, because the last stage is very fast", "It grows by about 0.3%, the square of the speed at 1 pm", "It becomes infinite, because the speed reaches the speed of light"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Energy conservation: the rate at which the orbit's energy falls equals the radiated power. This gives a differential equation for $r(t)$.

<Step title="Energy of a circular orbit">

With $K = e^2/(2r)$ from part (a) and $V = -e^2/r$:

<Formula label="Energy of the orbit" tex="E = K + V = -\frac{e^2}{2r}, \qquad \frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{e^2}{2r^2}\,\frac{dr}{dt}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The equation for the radius">

The acceleration is $a = e^2/(m_er^2)$. Setting $dE/dt$ equal to the Larmor power and using $r_e = e^2/(m_ec^2)$:

<Formula label="Shrinking of the orbit" tex="\frac{e^2}{2r^2}\,\frac{dr}{dt} = -\frac{2}{3}\,\frac{e^2}{c^3}\,\frac{e^4}{m_e^2r^4} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{dr}{dt} = -\frac{4}{3}\,\frac{r_e^2\,c}{r^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Integrate">

Separating, $r^2\,dr = -\frac43 r_e^2c\,dt$, and integrating from $r_i$ to $r_f$:

<Formula label="Time to spiral inwards" tex="t = \frac{r_i^3 - r_f^3}{4\,r_e^2\,c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

With $r_i = 5.0\times 10^{-11}$ m, $r_f = 1.0\times 10^{-12}$ m and $r_e = 2.818\times 10^{-15}$ m this gives $t = 1.31\times 10^{-11}$ s, about 13 picoseconds. A classical hydrogen atom would not last.

</Step>

<Step title="Relativistic corrections, and the last stage">

On this stretch the speed stays below $0.053c$ (part b). Relativistic corrections are of the order of $\beta^2$, which is at most 0.3% here, so ignoring them is justified. The radiated fraction per revolution also stays small, so the nearly circular orbit is justified too.

If the end point moves from 1 pm to 1 fm, the formula changes only through $r_f^3$, which is tiny next to $r_i^3$ in both cases. The time grows by the fraction $(1\ \text{pm}/50\ \text{pm})^3 = 8\times 10^{-6}$: the stretch below 1 pm takes about $10^{-16}$ s. The non-relativistic formula cannot be trusted near 1 fm, where it gives $\beta$ above 1 (part b), but that stretch adds almost nothing to the total: the electron spends nearly all of the 13 ps at large radii.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: a length cubed over a length squared times a speed is a time. A second route to the number: $r_f^3$ is negligible, so $t \approx (r_i/c)\,(r_i/r_e)^2/4$. Light crosses $r_i$ in $1.7\times 10^{-19}$ s, and $(r_i/r_e)^2/4 = 7.9\times 10^{7}$, which gives $1.3\times 10^{-11}$ s again.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={1}>

As the electron approaches the proton, what happens to its energy? Is there a lowest value that the energy can have?

<Hint>

Look at $E(r)$ for the circular orbit, from part (c), as $r$ goes to zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-energy" choices={["The energy rises towards zero as the electron falls in", "The energy falls to the lowest value, −e²/(2 r) at r = 50 pm, and stays there", "The energy falls without limit: classically there is no lowest energy", "The energy stays the same: what is radiated is taken from the potential energy only"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Read off $E = -e^2/(2r)$.

<Step title="No lowest energy">

As $r$ shrinks, the kinetic energy $e^2/(2r)$ grows, but the potential energy $-e^2/r$ falls twice as fast, so $E = -e^2/(2r)$ falls. For a point proton nothing stops it: $E \to -\infty$ as $r \to 0$. Classical physics gives the atom no lowest energy and no smallest size, and the electron keeps radiating all the way. (A real proton has a size of about 1 fm, but as part (b) showed, the model has failed before that.)

</Step>

<Step title="What this means">

Real hydrogen atoms are stable and all have the same size. Something must provide a lowest energy. Problem 2 shows what one extra rule, a quantized angular momentum, does.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Quantized energies" points={5} mit="Problem 2">

Model the hydrogen atom as an electron on a circular orbit around a fixed, heavy proton, with potential energy $V = -e^2/r$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

For a circular orbit, find how the kinetic energy $K$ and the potential energy $V$ of the electron are related to its total energy $E$. Type $K$ and $V$ in terms of `E`.

<Hint>

The force equation of Problem 1, $m_ev^2/r = e^2/r^2$, links $m_ev^2$ to $e^2/r$, and so $K$ to $V$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-k" label="$K =$" expr="-E" vars={{ E: [-5, -0.5] }} names={{ E: "E" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "E", hint: "The kinetic energy is positive, and the total energy of a bound orbit is negative." },
    { expr: "-E/2", hint: "K is half the size of V, not half the size of E. Add K and V first." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-v" label="$V =$" expr="2*E" vars={{ E: [-5, -0.5] }} names={{ E: "E" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-2*E", hint: "V and E are both negative for a bound orbit." },
    { expr: "E/2", hint: "It is the other way round: E is half of V." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The force equation fixes the kinetic energy at half the size of the potential energy.

<Step title="The relations">

From $m_ev^2 = e^2/r$:

<Formula label="Energies of a circular orbit" tex="K = \frac12 m_ev^2 = \frac{e^2}{2r} = -\frac12 V, \qquad E = K + V = \frac12 V = -K" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So $K = -E$ and $V = 2E$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$K + V = -E + 2E = E$. The total energy is negative: the electron is bound, and you must supply the energy $-E = K$ to free it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Now assume that the size $L$ of the electron's angular momentum can only take the values $n\hbar$, with $n$ a positive whole number. Find the allowed total energies $E_n$ and the radii $r_n$ of the orbits. Write them with $n$, the rest energy of the electron $E_e = m_ec^2$, the length $\lambda_e = \hbar/(m_ec)$ and the fine-structure constant $\alpha = e^2/(\hbar c)$.

(MIT's sheet calls $\hbar/(m_ec)$ the Compton wavelength of the electron and writes it $\lambda$. It is the Compton wavelength $\lambda_C = h/(m_ec)$ of the lesson divided by $2\pi$, often called the reduced Compton wavelength. We write $\lambda_e$ to keep the two apart.)

<Hint>

On a circular orbit $L = m_evr$. You have two equations, $m_evr = n\hbar$ and $m_ev^2 = e^2/r$, for the two unknowns $v$ and $r$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Divide the second equation by the first to get $v$. Its ratio to $c$ contains $e^2/(\hbar c) = \alpha$. Then $r_n$ follows from $L$, and $E_n$ from part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-en" label="$E_n =$" expr="-alpha^2*Ee/(2*n^2)" vars={{ alpha: [0.005, 0.01], Ee: [0.4, 0.6], n: [1, 5] }} names={{ alpha: "\\alpha", Ee: "E_e", n: "n" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-alpha^2*Ee/n^2", hint: "That is the potential energy of the orbit. The total energy is half of it." },
    { expr: "alpha^2*Ee/(2*n^2)", hint: "That is the kinetic energy. The total energy of a bound orbit is negative." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-rn" label="$r_n =$" expr="n^2*lam/alpha" vars={{ alpha: [0.005, 0.01], lam: [0.5, 2], n: [1, 5] }} names={{ alpha: "\\alpha", lam: "\\lambda_e", n: "n" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "n*lam/alpha", hint: "The speed depends on n as well, so n enters twice." },
    { expr: "n^2*lam*alpha", hint: "Check the place of α. It is a small number, and this radius would be smaller than λ_e." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The quantized angular momentum and the force equation together fix the speed and the radius of each orbit.

<Step title="The speed">

Divide $m_ev^2r = e^2$ by $m_evr = n\hbar$:

<Formula label="Speed on the n-th orbit" tex="v_n = \frac{e^2}{n\hbar} = \frac{\alpha c}{n}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The radius">

From $r = n\hbar/(m_ev)$:

<Formula label="Radius of the n-th orbit" tex="r_n = \frac{n\hbar}{m_e}\cdot\frac{n}{\alpha c} = \frac{n^2}{\alpha}\,\frac{\hbar}{m_ec} = \frac{n^2\lambda_e}{\alpha}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The energy">

By part (a), $E = -K = -\frac12 m_ev^2$:

<Formula label="Energy of the n-th orbit" tex="E_n = -\frac12 m_e\frac{\alpha^2c^2}{n^2} = -\frac{\alpha^2E_e}{2n^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Put in the numbers for $n = 1$. With $\lambda_e = \hbar c/(m_ec^2) = 386.16$ fm, the radius is $r_1 = 137.036 \times 386.16\ \text{fm} = 0.5292\times 10^{-10}$ m, and the energy is $E_1 = -0.510999\ \text{MeV}/(2\times 137.036^2) = -13.606$ eV. These are the Bohr radius and the ground-state energy that the full quantum theory gives much later in the course, in the lesson on [the hydrogen atom](/courses/8-04/hydrogen-atom). The speed $v_1 = \alpha c$ is $c/137$, so the non-relativistic treatment is consistent. And unlike in Problem 1, the energy now has a lowest value, $E_1$: the rule $L = n\hbar$ with $n \geq 1$ leaves no orbit below it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="De Broglie relations and the scale of quantum effects" points={10} mit="Problem 3">

<Part label="a" points={5}>

**Matter particles as waves.** If every moving particle has a wavelength, why do we not notice it in daily life? To answer, find the de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = h/p$, in metres, of each of these four objects. Use $h = 6.6\times 10^{-34}$ J s.

1. A car of mass 2000 kg moving at 22 m/s (50 miles per hour).
2. A marble of mass 10 g moving at 10 cm/s.
3. A smoke particle with a diameter of 100 nm and a mass of 1 fg ($10^{-18}$ kg), pushed about by air molecules at room temperature, $T = 300$ K. Assume its kinetic energy is the thermal average of the air molecules, $\frac32k_BT$, with $k_B = 1.38\times 10^{-23}$ J/K.
4. An atom of rubidium-87 (mass 87 atomic mass units) that has been laser cooled to a temperature of 100 microkelvin, $T = 1.0\times 10^{-4}$ K. Assume the kinetic energy $\frac32k_BT$ again.

<Hint>

For the car and the marble, $p = mv$ directly. Mind the units: grams to kilograms, centimetres to metres.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the smoke particle and the atom you know the kinetic energy and not the speed. Use $K = p^2/(2m)$ to get $p$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-car" label="Car:" value="1.5e-38" unit="m" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-car" />

<Answer id="p3-marble" label="Marble:" value="6.6e-31" unit="m" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-marble" />

<Answer id="p3-smoke" label="Smoke particle:" value="5.92e-15" unit="m" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-smoke" />

<Answer id="p3-rb" label="Rubidium atom:" value="2.70e-8" unit="m" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-rb" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the momentum of each object and divide $h$ by it. Then compare the wavelength with the size of the object and of anything it meets.

<Step title="Car and marble">

The momenta are $2000 \times 22 = 4.4\times 10^{4}$ kg m/s and $0.010 \times 0.10 = 1.0\times 10^{-3}$ kg m/s. With the de Broglie relation of the lesson:

<Formula label="Wavelengths of the car and the marble" tex="\lambda = \frac{h}{p}: \qquad \text{car: } \frac{6.6\times 10^{-34}}{4.4\times 10^{4}} = 1.5\times 10^{-38}\ \text{m}, \qquad \text{marble: } \frac{6.6\times 10^{-34}}{1.0\times 10^{-3}} = 6.6\times 10^{-31}\ \text{m}" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 6, eq. (3.23)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Smoke particle and atom">

From $K = p^2/(2m)$ with $K = \frac32k_BT$:

<Formula label="Thermal de Broglie wavelength" tex="p = \sqrt{2mK} = \sqrt{3mk_BT}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{3mk_BT}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Smoke particle: $K = 6.21\times 10^{-21}$ J, $p = 1.11\times 10^{-19}$ kg m/s, so $\lambda = 5.92\times 10^{-15}$ m. Rubidium atom: $m = 87 \times 1.66\times 10^{-27} = 1.444\times 10^{-25}$ kg, $K = 2.07\times 10^{-27}$ J, $p = 2.45\times 10^{-26}$ kg m/s, so $\lambda = 2.70\times 10^{-8}$ m, or 27 nm.

</Step>

<Step title="Why we do not notice">

Wave effects such as interference and diffraction show up when the wavelength is not negligible next to the sizes involved: the object, or the slits and obstacles it meets (the lesson's condition, Lecture 3 notes p. 6). The wavelengths of the car and the marble are more than fifteen powers of ten below the size of an atomic nucleus. Even the smoke particle, far too small to see with the naked eye, has a wavelength some $10^{7}$ times smaller than its own diameter of $10^{-7}$ m. So everyday objects behave as classical particles. The laser-cooled atom is different: its wavelength of 27 nm is about a hundred times larger than the atom itself (a few tenths of a nanometre). Cold atoms show their wave nature plainly.

</Step>

</Step>

<Exercise id="p5" title="Absorption?" points={5} mit="Problem 5">

<Hint>

Suppose it happened. Which two quantities must be the same before and after? Write both down in the frame in which the electron is at rest before the collision.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-why" choices={["Energy and momentum cannot both be conserved, unless the photon has no energy at all", "Electric charge would not be conserved", "The electron would have to move faster than light", "It can happen, but only for photons with more energy than the rest energy of the electron"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Test the process against the conservation of energy and of momentum. The lesson states the result without proof (Lecture 3 notes, p. 5).

<Step title="Before the collision">

Work in the frame in which the electron is at rest. The photon has energy $E_\gamma$ and, by the lesson's relation for a massless particle, momentum $E_\gamma/c$. So the totals are

<Formula label="Totals before the collision" tex="E_\text{total} = E_\gamma + m_ec^2, \qquad p_\text{total} = \frac{E_\gamma}{c}" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 4, eqs. (2.15)–(2.16)" />

</Step>

<Step title="After the supposed absorption">

Only the electron is left. It must carry all the energy and all the momentum, and for an electron these two are tied together:

<Formula label="Energy and momentum of the electron" tex="E^2 - p^2c^2 = m_e^2c^4" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 3, eq. (2.13)" />

Put in $E = E_\gamma + m_ec^2$ and $pc = E_\gamma$:

<Formula label="The condition for absorption" tex="\left(E_\gamma + m_ec^2\right)^2 - E_\gamma^2 = m_e^2c^4 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2E_\gamma\,m_ec^2 = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

The condition holds only for $E_\gamma = 0$: no photon at all. A photon with any energy would give the electron more energy than an electron with that momentum can have. Something else must carry away part of the energy and momentum: in Compton scattering it is a second photon.

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Now the phase shifter, with phase $\delta_u$, is in the upper leg, and the lower leg is free. Find $P_0$ and $P_1$ for a general input. Then give the number $P_0$ for our input with $\delta_u = \pi/3$.

<Hint>

Repeat part (a) with the phase on the upper amplitude. Or take the common factor $e^{i\delta_u}$ out of both output amplitudes: it does not change any probability.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-b" label="$P_0 =$" value="0.154" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only the difference between the phases of the two legs matters. A shift $\delta_u$ above acts like a shift $-\delta_u$ below.

<Step title="The amplitudes">

After BS1 the upper amplitude is multiplied by $e^{i\delta_u}$, and BS2 gives

<Formula label="Output amplitudes with a shifter in the upper leg" tex="\frac12\begin{pmatrix} e^{i\delta_u}(\beta - \alpha) + (\beta + \alpha) \\ e^{i\delta_u}(\beta - \alpha) - (\beta + \alpha) \end{pmatrix} = \frac{e^{i\delta_u}}{2}\begin{pmatrix} (\beta - \alpha) + e^{-i\delta_u}(\beta + \alpha) \\ (\beta - \alpha) - e^{-i\delta_u}(\beta + \alpha) \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The probabilities">

The factor in front has size 1. What remains is part (a) with $\delta_l$ replaced by $-\delta_u$:

<Formula label="Detection probabilities with a shifter in the upper leg" tex="P_0 = \frac12 + \frac12\left[\left(\left|\beta\right|^2 - \left|\alpha\right|^2\right)\cos\delta_u + 2\,\text{Im}\left(\alpha\beta^*\right)\sin\delta_u\right], \qquad P_1 = 1 - P_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

For our input and $\delta_u = \pi/3$: $P_0 = \frac12 + \frac12\left[0.14 - 0.831\right] = 0.154$, and $P_1 = 0.846$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For real $\alpha$ and $\beta$ the sine term vanishes, and it then makes no difference in which leg the shifter sits. Our input has a complex $\beta$, so the two legs give very different results: 0.986 against 0.154.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Both phase shifters are in place at once. Find $P_0$ and $P_1$ for a general input. Then give the number $P_0$ for our input with $\delta_l = \delta_u = \pi/3$.

<Hint>

Take the factor $e^{i\delta_u}$ out again. Which single phase is left on the lower amplitude?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-c" label="$P_0 =$" value="0.64" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A phase common to both legs drops out. The probabilities depend on $\delta_l - \delta_u$ only.

<Step title="The probabilities">

The amplitudes before BS2 are $e^{i\delta_u}(\beta - \alpha)/\sqrt2$ and $e^{i\delta_l}(\beta + \alpha)/\sqrt2$. Taking out $e^{i\delta_u}$ leaves the phase $e^{i(\delta_l - \delta_u)}$ on the lower one, so by part (a)

<Formula label="Detection probabilities with both shifters" tex="P_0 = \frac12 + \frac12\left[\left(\left|\beta\right|^2 - \left|\alpha\right|^2\right)\cos(\delta_l - \delta_u) - 2\,\text{Im}\left(\alpha\beta^*\right)\sin(\delta_l - \delta_u)\right], \qquad P_1 = 1 - P_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Our input">

With $\delta_l = \delta_u$ the difference is zero and $P_0 = \frac12 + \frac12 \times 0.28 = 0.64 = \left|\beta\right|^2$: two equal shifters do nothing at all, and the interferometer behaves as if it were empty.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Setting $\delta_u = 0$ gives part (a), and setting $\delta_l = 0$ gives part (b). In every case $P_0 + P_1 = 1$: the phase shifters and beam splitters lose no photons.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="Elitzur–Vaidman bombs" points={10} mit="Problem 7">

Use the bomb test of the lesson: the bomb sits in the lower leg of the interferometer, and one photon at a time is sent in from below. With a defective bomb the photon always reaches D0. With a working bomb the photon sets it off with probability $\frac12$, reaches D0 with probability $\frac14$ and reaches D1 with probability $\frac14$ (Lecture 2 notes, p. 7).

<Part label="a" points={5}>

You test each bomb again and again, until its state is certain beyond reasonable doubt. What fraction of the working bombs is certified as working without exploding?

<Hint>

For a working bomb, which outcomes of one photon end the test, and which one tells you nothing new, so that you send another photon?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Add the chances of being certified at the first photon, at the second, at the third, and so on: a geometric series. Or compare the two outcomes that end the test.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-a" label="Fraction certified:" value="0.333" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A photon at D0 leaves you where you were, so you try again. The test ends only at D1 (certified) or with an explosion.

<Step title="Sum over the attempts">

A working bomb is certified at the $k$-th photon if the first $k - 1$ photons all reached D0 and the $k$-th reaches D1:

<Formula label="Fraction of working bombs certified" tex="\frac14 + \frac14\cdot\frac14 + \left(\frac14\right)^2\frac14 + \dots = \frac{1/4}{1 - 1/4} = \frac13" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The other bombs">

In the same way a fraction $\frac{1/2}{1 - 1/4} = \frac23$ of the working bombs explodes. A defective bomb gives D0 every time; after $N$ photons at D0 in a row the odds that the bomb works after all have shrunk by a factor $4^{N}$, so a modest number of photons makes its state certain beyond reasonable doubt.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\frac13 + \frac23 = 1$: every working bomb is, in the end, certified or lost. The single-photon test certifies only a quarter of them; repeating raises this to a third. The lesson's worked example finds the same number by comparing the two outcomes that end the test.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Of the bombs in your store, 80% are defective. You pick one at random and test it with one photon. The photon is detected at D0. What is the probability that this bomb is defective?

<Hint>

Imagine 100 bombs, each tested with one photon. How many are defective and give D0? How many work and give D0?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-b" label="Probability that it is defective:" value="0.941" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A photon at D0 is possible with both kinds of bomb, but more likely with a defective one. Weigh the two ways of getting D0.

<Step title="Count the cases">

Of 100 bombs, 80 are defective, and all 80 give D0. The other 20 work, and a quarter of them, 5, give D0. So 85 tests end at D0, and in 80 of those the bomb is defective:

<Formula label="Probability that the bomb is defective, given D0" tex="P(\text{defective} \mid \text{D0}) = \frac{0.80 \times 1}{0.80 \times 1 + 0.20 \times \frac14} = \frac{0.80}{0.85} = \frac{16}{17} = 0.941" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Before the test the probability was 0.80. A photon at D0 is what a defective bomb always gives, so the probability must rise, and it does. It does not reach 1, because a working bomb also sends a quarter of its photons to D0.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
