A well, unlike a step, is crossed both ways: a wave coming in from the left can be reflected at either edge or pass right over. This lecture finds that at special energies the well becomes perfectly transparent, and uses it to explain a surprising experiment with electrons and noble gases (notes pp. 1–5). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 17; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own worked example. The notes' figures are not reproduced. MIT's calendar also lists scattering by finite-range potentials for this lecture; that topic is in the next chapter of the notes and on the next page.)

## Scattering over a square well

Take the finite square well of depth $V_0 > 0$ and width $2a$ (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="Finite square well" tex="V(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & |x| > a \\ -V_0, & |x| < a \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.1)" />

and a scattering state with $E > 0$, a wave coming in from the left. The ansatz has an incident and a reflected wave on the left, waves both ways inside, and only a transmitted wave on the right (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="Waves over the well" tex="\psi(x) = \begin{cases} Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx}, & x < -a \\ Ce^{ik_2x} + De^{-ik_2x}, & |x| < a \\ Fe^{ikx}, & x > a \end{cases}, \qquad k^2 = \frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}, \quad k_2^2 = \frac{2m(E + V_0)}{\hbar^2}" cite="mit" at="notes pp. 1–2, eqs. (1.2)–(1.3)" />

Although the potential is even, this state is neither even nor odd: the symmetry is broken by sending the wave in from one side (notes p. 2). Continuity of $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at $x = \pm a$ gives four equations, which fix $B$, $C$, $D$ and $F$ in terms of $A$. The same wavenumber $k$ appears on both sides, so here the coefficients are simply

<Formula label="Reflection and transmission" tex="R \equiv \frac{|B|^2}{|A|^2}, \qquad T \equiv \frac{|F|^2}{|A|^2}, \qquad R + T = 1" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eqs. (1.4)–(1.6)" />

$R + T = 1$ expresses equal currents on the two sides, $|A|^2 - |B|^2 = |F|^2$; it follows from the boundary conditions rather than adding to them (notes p. 2). Solving is straightforward but laborious; the notes quote the result (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Transmission coefficient" tex="\frac1T = 1 + \frac14\,\frac{V_0^2}{E\left(E + V_0\right)}\,\sin^2\left(2k_2a\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.7)" />

The second term is never negative, so $T \le 1$. As $E \to 0$ it blows up and $T \to 0$; as $E \to \infty$, $T \to 1$.

**Without units** (notes p. 2). With $e \equiv E/V_0$ and the well's strength $z_0^2 = 2ma^2V_0/\hbar^2$ (the same $z_0$ as for its bound states), $2k_2a = 2z_0\sqrt{1 + e}$ and (eqs. (1.8)–(1.10))

<Formula label="Transmission without units" tex="\frac1T = 1 + \frac{1}{4e(1 + e)}\,\sin^2\left(2z_0\sqrt{1 + e}\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.10)" />

## Perfect transparency

$T = 1$ exactly when the sine vanishes (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Condition for resonance" tex="2z_0\sqrt{1 + e} = n\pi, \qquad n \ge \frac{2z_0}{\pi}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eqs. (1.11)–(1.12)" />

where the inequality comes from $e > 0$ (strictly, $n > 2z_0/\pi$, since equality would mean $E = 0$). In unit-free form the resonances sit at $e_n + 1 = n^2\pi^2/(4z_0^2)$ (eq. (1.13)). For these $n$, the energies $E_n$ satisfy (notes p. 3)

<Formula label="Resonant energies" tex="E_n + V_0 = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m\left(2a\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.14)" />

The left side is the energy measured from the bottom of the well; the right side is the $n$th level of an **infinite** well of width $2a$. So the finite well is perfectly transparent exactly at those infinite-well levels that lie above zero. Those levels fit a whole number of half wavelengths across the well, and indeed $\sin(2k_2a) = 0$ means $k_2(2a) = n\pi$, that is $2a = n\lambda_2/2$ (notes p. 3, eq. (1.15)). The waves fit perfectly inside the well: **resonant transmission**.

The notes' example, $z_0 = 13\pi/4$, needs $n \ge 13/2$, so the first resonance is $n = 7$; three resonances fall below $E = V_0$, and they spread out as the energy grows (notes pp. 3–4).

## The Ramsauer–Townsend effect

Around 1921 (the year the notes give) Carl Ramsauer and John Sealy Townsend, separately, studied low-energy electrons scattering elastically off noble-gas atoms (notes pp. 3–4). These atoms have full electron shells: they are unreactive and hard to ionize. An incoming electron that penetrates the electron cloud feels the attraction of the nucleus, a roughly spherical attractive well. Most electrons that scatter bounce back, so the reflection coefficient is a stand-in for the scattering cross-section (notes p. 4).

What they found had no classical explanation. At very low energies the scattering was strong; as the energy rose it fell towards zero, then rose again. The dip is resonant transmission: $R \to 0$, $T \to 1$, and the electrons pass straight through the atoms. The first such resonance is near 1 eV, for electrons moving at about 600 km/s (notes p. 4). A one-dimensional well shows the effect but cannot match the data in detail; that needs a three-dimensional spherical well (notes p. 4).

<WorkedExample title="Resonances of a 10 eV well">
<Problem>
An electron passes over the square well of depth $V_0 = 10$ eV and width $2a = 0.40$ nm (the well whose three bound states were found on the square-well page; $z_0 \approx 3.24$, and an infinite well of this width has ground level $2.35$ eV). (a) Which $n$ give resonances? Find the first two resonant energies. (b) Find $T$ at $E = 5.0$ eV. (c) Check the notes' speed for a 1 eV electron ($m_ec^2 = 511{,}000$ eV).
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Resonances">
$n \ge 2z_0/\pi \approx 2.06$, so $n = 3, 4, \dots$. With infinite-well levels $2.35n^2$ eV: $E_3 = 9 \times 2.350 - 10 \approx 11.2$ eV and $E_4 = 16 \times 2.350 - 10 \approx 27.6$ eV. (The level $n = 2$, at $9.40$ eV from the bottom, lies below zero: it is close to the well's third, least bound state, at $-0.07$ eV.)
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Off resonance">
$e = 0.5$, so $2z_0\sqrt{1.5} \approx 7.94$ and $\sin^2 7.94 \approx 0.993$. Then $1/T = 1 + \frac{0.993}{4 \times 0.5 \times 1.5} \approx 1.331$, and $T \approx 0.75$: a quarter of the electrons are reflected.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A 1 eV electron">
$v = c\sqrt{2E/(m_ec^2)} = 3.0 \times 10^5\ \text{km/s} \times \sqrt{2/511{,}000} \approx 593$ km/s, about 600 km/s as the notes say.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Over a square well, $1/T = 1 + \frac{V_0^2}{4E(E + V_0)}\sin^2(2k_2a)$, with $R + T = 1$.
- The well is perfectly transparent when $2k_2a = n\pi$: a whole number of half wavelengths fits across it, at the levels of the infinite well of width $2a$ that lie above zero.
- The resonances grow farther apart as the energy rises.
- The Ramsauer–Townsend effect, slow electrons passing almost freely through noble-gas atoms near 1 eV, is resonant transmission.
