## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit shown. Small and large numbers can be typed as `1.6e-13` or `1.6 × 10^-13`. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `a*sqrt(1 + t^2/tau^2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. Write the symbols of the box exactly as the box names them: `hbar` stands for $\hbar$, `tau` for $\tau$, `th` for $\theta$, `a0` for $a_0$, `x0` and `p0` for $x_0$ and $p_0$.

MIT gives the points per problem. The splits between parts are ours. Much of this set asks you to show something: those parts have no box, so open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lessons: $\Psi$ for a wavefunction, $\Phi(k)$ for its Fourier transform in the wavenumber $k$, $\tilde\Phi(p)$ for the one in the momentum $p = \hbar k$, $\langle\hat Q\rangle$ for an expectation value and $\Delta x$, $\Delta p$ for uncertainties. Where numbers are needed, take the CODATA 2018 values $\hbar = 1.054572\times 10^{-34}$ J s and, for the proton, $m_p = 1.67262\times 10^{-27}$ kg.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Packets that change shape" points={5} mit="Problem 1">

Two short exercises on the lesson's estimate of how long a free wave packet keeps its shape.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

A free proton is localized within $\Delta x = 10^{-10}$ m, about the size of an atom. Estimate the time $t_s$ after which its packet has spread appreciably. Then do the same for a proton localized within 1 cm. Use the lesson's estimate $t_s \approx m(\Delta x)^2/\hbar$ and give both times in seconds. This is an estimate, so each box accepts values within 5%.

<Hint>

The lesson on wave packets has a condition for a packet to keep its shape: the time must be much shorter than a certain combination of $m$, $\Delta x$ and $\hbar$. Read "much shorter than" as "about equal to" to estimate when the change becomes noticeable.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The second case needs no new calculation. By what factor does $\Delta x$ grow from $10^{-10}$ m to $10^{-2}$ m, and how does $t_s$ depend on $\Delta x$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-atom" label="$\Delta x = 10^{-10}$ m: $t_s =$" value="1.59e-13" unit="s" tolerance="5%" calc="p1-atom" />

<Answer id="p1-cm" label="$\Delta x = 1$ cm: $t_s =$" value="1590" unit="s" tolerance="5%" calc="p1-cm" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A packet of width $\Delta x$ has a spread of velocities $\Delta p/m \approx \hbar/(m\,\Delta x)$. It has changed shape once that spread of velocities has moved its parts apart by about $\Delta x$.

<Step title="The estimate">

The lesson's condition for little change of shape, read as an estimate (notes p. 6), gives

<Formula label="Spreading time of a free packet" tex="t_s \approx \frac{m\left(\Delta x\right)^2}{\hbar}" cite="reading2" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.25)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two protons">

For the proton the size of an atom:

<Formula label="Proton localized within an atom's size" tex="t_s \approx \frac{1.67262\times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg} \times \left(10^{-10}\ \text{m}\right)^2}{1.054572\times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}} = 1.59\times 10^{-13}\ \text{s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

For $\Delta x = 1$ cm the width is $10^8$ times larger and $t_s$ grows with its square, a factor $10^{16}$:

<Formula label="Proton localized within a centimetre" tex="t_s \approx 1.59\times 10^{-13}\ \text{s} \times 10^{16} = 1.59\times 10^{3}\ \text{s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

That is about 26 minutes.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\text{kg m}^2/(\text{J s}) = \text{kg m}^2\,\text{s}^2/(\text{kg m}^2\,\text{s}) = \text{s}$. The lecture notes do the same estimate for an electron and find about $10^{-16}$ s and about a second (notes p. 6, eq. (2.28)). The proton is 1836 times heavier, and both our times are larger than the electron's by that factor: $8.6\times 10^{-17}\ \text{s} \times 1836 = 1.6\times 10^{-13}$ s. A proton pinned down to atomic size spreads within a fraction of a picosecond. Pinned down only to a centimetre it has a very small spread of velocities and holds its shape for about half an hour.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

A wave packet satisfies $\Delta x\,\Delta p \approx \hbar$ and moves with momentum $p$. Show that the condition $\Delta p \ll p$ guarantees that the packet does not spread appreciably in the time it takes to pass a fixed position.

As a start (the box is our addition): find the ratio of the time $t_\text{pass}$ the packet takes to pass a fixed point to the spreading time $t_s$ of part (a), in terms of $\Delta p$ and $p$. Type `dp` for $\Delta p$.

<Hint>

The packet has length $\Delta x$ and moves at the speed $p/m$. How long does it take to go by?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use $\Delta x\,\Delta p \approx \hbar$ to remove $\hbar$ from $t_s$, so that both times contain $m\,\Delta x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-ratio" label="$t_\text{pass}/t_s \approx$" expr="dp/p"
  vars={{ dp: [0.1, 1], p: [2, 9] }}
  names={{ dp: "\\Delta p" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "p/dp", hint: "That is the inverse ratio. It would make a packet with a sharp momentum (small Δp) take longest to pass, compared with its spreading time. But a small Δp means a slow spread." },
    { expr: "(dp/p)^2", hint: "The spreading time holds one power of Δp once ħ is replaced by Δx Δp, not two: m(Δx)²/ħ ≈ m Δx/Δp." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compare two times: the time the packet needs to go by, and the time it needs to spread.

<Step title="The two times">

The packet is about $\Delta x$ long and moves at the group velocity $p/m$, so it passes a fixed point in the time $t_\text{pass} \approx \Delta x/(p/m)$. In the spreading time, replace $\hbar$ by $\Delta x\,\Delta p$:

<Formula label="Passing time and spreading time" tex="t_\text{pass} \approx \frac{m\,\Delta x}{p}, \qquad t_s \approx \frac{m\left(\Delta x\right)^2}{\hbar} \approx \frac{m\,\Delta x}{\Delta p}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Their ratio">

<Formula label="Ratio of passing time to spreading time" tex="\frac{t_\text{pass}}{t_s} \approx \frac{\Delta p}{p}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

If $\Delta p \ll p$, then $t_\text{pass} \ll t_s$: the packet has gone by long before it has had time to change shape. This is the lesson's condition $t \ll m(\Delta x)^2/\hbar$ with $t = t_\text{pass}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ratio is a pure number, as a ratio of times must be. The lesson's other form of the condition says the same thing: the spread in velocity, $\Delta p/m$, acting for the time $t_\text{pass}$, moves the parts of the packet apart by $(\Delta p/m)\,t_\text{pass} = \Delta x\,\Delta p/p$, which is much less than $\Delta x$ when $\Delta p \ll p$ (notes p. 5, eq. (2.27)).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Probability current in three dimensions" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

When particles scatter elastically off a target in three dimensions, the wavefunction far from the target has the form

<Formula label="Wavefunction of elastic scattering, far from the target" tex="\psi(\vec x) = e^{ikz} + \frac{f(\theta)}{r}\,e^{ikr}, \qquad \text{valid for large } r" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

The time dependence is left out: it is the overall phase $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ with $E = \hbar^2k^2/(2m)$, and it plays no role here. The first term is the beam of incoming particles, moving in the $+z$ direction. The target sits at the origin $r = 0$, and the second term is the amplitude of the scattered particles, which move radially outward. Here $\theta$ is the polar angle, so $z = r\cos\theta$. The complex function $f(\theta)$ holds the information about the scattering. It depends on $\theta$ and is assumed not to depend on the azimuthal angle $\phi$.

The probability current of the lesson, $\vec J = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\psi^*\nabla\psi\right)$, then has three parts: $\vec J_1$ from the plane wave alone, $\vec J_2$ from the spherical wave alone, and $\vec J_{12}$ from the interference of the two:

<Formula label="The three parts of the current" tex="\vec J(\vec x) = \vec J_1(\vec x) + \vec J_2(\vec x) + \vec J_{12}(\vec x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the current $\vec J_1$. Then find the total flux of $\vec J_1$ through a large sphere of radius $R$ centred on the origin.

<Hint>

Only the $z$ derivative of $e^{ikz}$ is not zero. Compare with the current of a plane wave in one dimension, in the lesson's worked example.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the flux you need the component of $\vec J_1$ along the outward normal $\hat r$ of the sphere: $\hat z\cdot\hat r = \cos\theta$. Integrate it over the sphere, or argue from what enters and what leaves.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-j1" label="$\vec J_1 = (\ldots)\,\hat z$, with $(\ldots) =$" expr="hbar*k/m"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], k: [1, 3], m: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "hbar*k/(2*m)", hint: "The 1/2 belongs to the form with two terms, (ħ/2im)(ψ*∇ψ − ψ∇ψ*). With the imaginary part there is no 2." },
    { expr: "k/m", hint: "Check the units: a density times a velocity is needed, and k/m is not a velocity. The momentum is ħk." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-flux1" label="The flux of $\vec J_1$ through the sphere is" choices={["Zero for every R: what enters through one half of the sphere leaves through the other", "Positive, and it grows as R squared", "Negative, because the beam flows into the sphere", "Zero only in the limit of a very large sphere"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A plane wave carries a uniform current: the density times the velocity $\hbar k/m$. A uniform current brings as much into any closed surface as it takes out.

<Step title="The current">

With $\psi_1 = e^{ikz}$, the gradient is $\nabla\psi_1 = ik\,e^{ikz}\,\hat z$, so $\psi_1^*\nabla\psi_1 = ik\,\hat z$ and

<Formula label="Current of the plane wave" tex="\vec J_1 = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(ik\right)\hat z = \frac{\hbar k}{m}\,\hat z" cite="reading1" at="notes p. 7, eq. (4.1), applied to the plane wave" />

</Step>

<Step title="The flux">

On the sphere the outward normal is $\hat r$ and the element of area is $R^2\,d\Omega = R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$:

<Formula label="Flux of the plane-wave current through the sphere" tex="\oint \vec J_1\cdot\hat r\,da = \frac{\hbar k}{m}\,R^2\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\int_0^{\pi}\cos\theta\,\sin\theta\,d\theta = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The integral of $\cos\theta\sin\theta$ from 0 to $\pi$ vanishes: the hemisphere facing the beam ($\cos\theta < 0$) lets in what the far hemisphere lets out. This holds for every $R$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\vec J_1$ is constant, so $\nabla\cdot\vec J_1 = 0$, and by Gauss's theorem its flux through any closed surface is zero (notes p. 7, eqs. (4.6)–(4.7)). Units: $\psi_1$ is not normalized here, so $|\psi_1|^2 = 1$ counts as one particle per unit volume and $J_1$ is that density times the velocity $\hbar k/m$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find the radial component $\hat r\cdot\vec J_2$ of the current of the spherical wave, where $\hat r$ is the unit vector in the radial direction. In the box, type `F` for $|f(\theta)|^2$. Then find the flux of $\vec J_2$ through the sphere of radius $R$ in the limit $R \to \infty$. Leave it as an integral over the solid angle $d\Omega$, or over $d\theta$.

The second box is our addition: the flux in the special case that $|f(\theta)|$ has the same value $f_0$ in all directions.

<Hint>

The radial component of the gradient is the derivative with respect to $r$ at fixed angles. The function $f(\theta)$ is then a constant factor.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiate $e^{ikr}/r$ with respect to $r$. Two terms appear. Multiply by the complex conjugate of the wave: which of the two terms is imaginary?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-j2" label="$\hat r\cdot\vec J_2 =$" expr="hbar*k*F/(m*r^2)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], k: [1, 3], m: [0.5, 2], F: [0.5, 2], r: [2, 5] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", F: "|f|^2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "hbar*k*F/(m*r)", hint: "The amplitude of the spherical wave falls as 1/r, and the current holds the amplitude twice (once in ψ*, once in ∇ψ)." },
    { expr: "hbar*k*F/(m*r^2) - hbar*F/(m*r^3)", hint: "The term that comes from differentiating 1/r is real when multiplied by ψ*, so it has no imaginary part and drops out." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-flux2" label="Constant $|f| = f_0$: flux $=$" expr="4*pi*hbar*k*f0^2/m"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], k: [1, 3], m: [0.5, 2], f0: [0.5, 2], R: [2, 5] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", f0: "f_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "4*pi*hbar*k*f0^2*R^2/m", hint: "The R² of the sphere's area meets the 1/R² of the radial current: the flux cannot depend on R." },
    { expr: "hbar*k*f0^2/m", hint: "That is the flux per unit solid angle. The whole sphere has a solid angle of 4π." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A wave $e^{ikr}/r$ moves outward at the velocity $\hbar k/m$ with a density that thins out as $1/r^2$. So the same probability crosses every sphere each second.

<Step title="The radial derivative">

With $\psi_2 = f(\theta)\,e^{ikr}/r$:

<Formula label="Radial derivative of the spherical wave" tex="\frac{\partial\psi_2}{\partial r} = f(\theta)\left(\frac{ik}{r} - \frac{1}{r^2}\right)e^{ikr}, \qquad \psi_2^*\,\frac{\partial\psi_2}{\partial r} = \left|f(\theta)\right|^2\left(\frac{ik}{r^2} - \frac{1}{r^3}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The radial current">

Only the first term is imaginary:

<Formula label="Radial current of the scattered wave" tex="\hat r\cdot\vec J_2 = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\psi_2^*\,\frac{\partial\psi_2}{\partial r}\right) = \frac{\hbar k}{m}\,\frac{\left|f(\theta)\right|^2}{r^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

This is exact for the given $\psi_2$, with no terms of higher order in $1/r$.

</Step>

<Step title="The flux">

On the sphere $da = R^2\,d\Omega$, and the factors of $R$ cancel:

<Formula label="Flux of the scattered wave" tex="\oint \vec J_2\cdot\hat r\,da = \frac{\hbar k}{m}\int\left|f(\theta)\right|^2 d\Omega = \frac{2\pi\hbar k}{m}\int_0^{\pi}\left|f(\theta)\right|^2\sin\theta\,d\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

For a constant $|f| = f_0$ the integral over the solid angle is $4\pi f_0^2$ and the flux is $4\pi\hbar k f_0^2/m$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The flux does not depend on $R$: probability that crosses one sphere crosses every larger one, so none piles up between them. Units: the two terms of $\psi$ must have the same units, so $f$ is a length. Then $|f|^2\,d\Omega$ is an area, and the flux is the beam's current $\hbar k/m$ times an area: the area of the beam that the target scatters away. $\vec J_2$ also has a component along $\hat\theta$, equal to $\frac{\hbar}{m r^3}\,\text{Im}\left(f^*\,df/d\theta\right)$. It is tangent to the sphere, so it adds nothing to the flux.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the radial component of the interference term $\vec J_{12}$, keeping only the leading part in $1/r$ (drop the terms in $1/r^2$). Show that the result can be written as

<Formula label="Form of the interference current" tex="\hat r\cdot\vec J_{12} = \frac{\hbar k}{m r}\,\text{Im}\left[\,i\left(\ldots\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2" />

and find the bracket $(\ldots)$. It holds $f(\theta)$, $\cos\theta$, and exponentials with the product $kr$. (MIT's sheet adds that the flux of this term through the large sphere is delicate and is left for later: it leads to the optical theorem.)

The box is our addition, a check of your bracket: the value of $\hat r\cdot\vec J_{12}$ when $f$ is real and equal to $f_0$ at the angle $\theta$.

<Hint>

The interference current has two cross terms: $\vec J_{12} = \frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}\left(\psi_1^*\nabla\psi_2 + \psi_2^*\nabla\psi_1\right)$. Take the radial derivative in each.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the plane wave as $e^{ikr\cos\theta}$. Its radial derivative brings down $ik\cos\theta$. In the radial derivative of the spherical wave, keep only the $ik/r$ term.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the box: $\text{Im}(iw) = \text{Re}(w)$ for any complex number $w$, and a number and its complex conjugate have the same real part.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-j12" label="Real $f = f_0$: $\hat r\cdot\vec J_{12} =$" expr="hbar*k*f0*(1 + cos(th))*cos(k*r*(1 - cos(th)))/(m*r)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], k: [1, 2], m: [0.5, 2], f0: [0.5, 2], r: [3, 6], th: [0.4, 2.7] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", f0: "f_0", th: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*hbar*k*f0*cos(k*r*(1 - cos(th)))/(m*r)", hint: "That is right only in the forward direction, θ = 0. The radial derivative of the plane wave brings a factor cos θ, so one of the two cross terms carries cos θ." },
    { expr: "hbar*k*f0*(1 + cos(th))*cos(k*r)/(m*r)", hint: "The plane wave has a phase too, kz = kr cos θ. The two waves enter through the difference of their phases, kr − kr cos θ." },
    { expr: "hbar*k*f0*(1 - cos(th))*cos(k*r*(1 - cos(th)))/(m*r)", hint: "Both cross terms have the same sign: ik/r from the spherical wave and ik cos θ/r from the plane wave. This form would vanish in the forward direction, where the two waves travel together." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The cross terms contain one wave and the conjugate of the other, so they oscillate with the difference of the two phases, $kr - kz = kr(1 - \cos\theta)$, and fall as $1/r$.

<Step title="The two cross terms">

With $\psi_1 = e^{ikr\cos\theta}$ we have $\partial\psi_1/\partial r = ik\cos\theta\,\psi_1$. Keeping the $ik/r$ term of the derivative of $\psi_2$:

<Formula label="The two cross terms, to leading order in 1/r" tex="\psi_1^*\,\frac{\partial\psi_2}{\partial r} \simeq \frac{ik}{r}\,f(\theta)\,e^{ikr(1 - \cos\theta)}, \qquad \psi_2^*\,\frac{\partial\psi_1}{\partial r} = \frac{ik\cos\theta}{r}\,f^*(\theta)\,e^{-ikr(1 - \cos\theta)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The interference current">

Adding them and taking $\frac{\hbar}{m}\,\text{Im}$:

<Formula label="Radial interference current" tex="\hat r\cdot\vec J_{12} = \frac{\hbar k}{m r}\,\text{Im}\left[\,i\left(f(\theta)\,e^{ikr(1 - \cos\theta)} + \cos\theta\,f^*(\theta)\,e^{-ikr(1 - \cos\theta)}\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

This is MIT's form, and the bracket is the sum in round brackets.

</Step>

<Step title="A simpler form">

Since $\text{Im}(iw) = \text{Re}(w)$, and $f^*e^{-ikr(1 - \cos\theta)}$ is the complex conjugate of $f\,e^{ikr(1 - \cos\theta)}$ with the same real part:

<Formula label="Radial interference current, simplified" tex="\hat r\cdot\vec J_{12} = \frac{\hbar k}{m r}\left(1 + \cos\theta\right)\text{Re}\left(f(\theta)\,e^{ikr(1 - \cos\theta)}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

For a real $f = f_0$ this is $\frac{\hbar k}{mr}\,f_0\left(1 + \cos\theta\right)\cos\left(kr(1 - \cos\theta)\right)$, the value in the box.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The dropped term is $-\frac{\hbar}{m r^2}\,\text{Im}\left(f\,e^{ikr(1 - \cos\theta)}\right)$, from the $-1/r^2$ in the derivative of $\psi_2$. It is smaller than the kept terms by a factor of order $1/(kr)$. Backward, at $\theta = \pi$, the factor $1 + \cos\theta$ is zero: there the two waves run against each other, and the leading interference current vanishes. For large $kr$ the cosine oscillates quickly with $\theta$ everywhere except near $\theta = 0$, where the phase $kr(1 - \cos\theta)$ stops changing. That is why the flux of this term over the sphere is delicate, and why only the forward direction survives in it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Evolving the Gaussian wave packet" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

A particle of mass $m$ is in the normalized Gaussian wave packet of the lesson at $t = 0$:

<Formula label="The Gaussian packet at time zero" tex="\Psi_a(x, 0) = \frac{1}{\left(2\pi\right)^{1/4}\sqrt a}\,\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{4a^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2" />

The length $a$ sets the width of the packet at time zero. MIT's sheet gives one integral for this problem. It holds for complex constants $A$ and $b$ (the sheet calls the first one $a$) as long as the real part of $A$ is positive:

<Formula label="The Gaussian integral" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-Ax^2 + bx}\,dx = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{A}}\,\exp\left(\frac{b^2}{4A}\right), \qquad \text{Re}(A) > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 3" />

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Confirm that $\Psi_a(x, 0)$ is properly normalized.

<Hint>

Square the wavefunction (it is real) and use the Gaussian integral with $b = 0$. What is $A$?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The square of a Gaussian is a Gaussian, and its integral is known.

<Step title="The integral">

<Exercise id="p4" title="Parseval's identity in one and three dimensions" points={10} mit="Problem 4">

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The $x$ integral of two plane waves is a delta function, which forces the two momenta to be equal.

<Step title="One dimension">

<Step title="The commutator">

$[\hat p, \hat p^2] = 0$. On a test function $\varphi(x)$:

<Formula label="Commutator of momentum with the potential" tex="\left[\hat p, V(\hat x)\right]\varphi = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(V\varphi\right) - V\,\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x} = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\,\varphi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \left[\hat p, \hat H\right] = -i\hbar\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The rate of change">

<Formula label="Rate of change of the expected momentum" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat p\rangle = \left\langle\left[\hat p, \hat H\right]\right\rangle = -i\hbar\left\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\right\rangle \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat p\rangle = -\left\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\right\rangle" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 6, eqs. (3.36)–(3.37)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is Newton's second law for the averages: the rate of change of the momentum is the average force $-\partial V/\partial x$. For a constant force $F$, with $V = -Fx$, it reads $d\langle\hat p\rangle/dt = F$ exactly. For a free particle, $V = 0$, the average momentum is constant. Note what the equation does not say: the right side is the average of the force, which in general differs from the force at the average position, $-V'(\langle\hat x\rangle)$. The two agree when the force is a linear function of $x$ (no force, a constant force, the harmonic oscillator), or nearly so when the packet is narrow compared with the distance over which the force changes.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Momentum uncertainty of a free packet" points={5} mit="Problem 6">

Show that in a free-particle wave packet the momentum uncertainty $\Delta p$ does not change in time.

<Hint>

$(\Delta p)^2 = \langle\hat p^2\rangle - \langle\hat p\rangle^2$. What decides whether an expectation value changes in time?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For a free particle $\hat H = \hat p^2/(2m)$. Do $\hat p$ and $\hat p^2$ commute with it?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a free particle the Hamiltonian is a function of $\hat p$ alone, so every function of $\hat p$ commutes with it and has a constant expectation value.

<Step title="Two constant expectation values">

With $\hat H = \hat p^2/(2m)$ we have $[\hat p, \hat H] = 0$ and $[\hat p^2, \hat H] = 0$: powers of one operator commute with each other. The lesson's formula for the rate of change then gives

<Formula label="Constant momentum averages of a free particle" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat p\rangle = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\left\langle\left[\hat p, \hat H\right]\right\rangle = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\langle\hat p^2\rangle = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\left\langle\left[\hat p^2, \hat H\right]\right\rangle = 0" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 6, eqs. (3.36)–(3.37)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The uncertainty">

<Formula label="The momentum uncertainty is constant" tex="\left(\Delta p\right)^2 = \langle\hat p^2\rangle - \langle\hat p\rangle^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\Delta p\right)^2 = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second route, in momentum space: each plane wave of a free packet only gains a phase, so $\tilde\Phi(p, t) = \tilde\Phi(p, 0)\,e^{-ip^2t/(2m\hbar)}$ and the momentum distribution $|\tilde\Phi(p, t)|^2$ does not change at all. Every average over it, and so $\Delta p$, is constant. No force acts, so no momentum is exchanged. The Gaussian of Problem 3 shows it: $\Delta p = \hbar/(2a)$ at all times, while $\Delta x = a(t)$ grows. The product is $\Delta x\,\Delta p = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sqrt{1 + t^2/\tau^2}$, at the lower bound $\hbar/2$ only at $t = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="Finding meaning in the phase of the wavefunction" points={10} mit="Problem 7">

Let $\psi_0(x)$ be a normalized wavefunction with the expectation values $\langle\hat x\rangle = x_0$ and $\langle\hat p\rangle = p_0$, where $x_0$ and $p_0$ are constants. Define the boost operator $\hat B_q$ as the operator that multiplies any function of $x$ by a phase that depends on a real number $q$ (with the units of momentum):

<Formula label="The boost operator" tex="\hat B_q\,f(x) = e^{iqx/\hbar}\,f(x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 4" />

Boosting the first wavefunction gives a new one:

<Formula label="The boosted wavefunction" tex="\psi_\text{new}(x) = \hat B_q\,\psi_0(x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 4" />

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the expectation value $\langle\hat x\rangle$ in the state $\psi_\text{new}$?

<Hint>

What happens to the probability density $|\psi|^2$ when $\psi$ is multiplied by a phase, even one that depends on $x$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-x" label="$\langle\hat x\rangle_\text{new} =$" expr="x0"
  vars={{ x0: [1, 3], q: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ x0: "x_0", hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "x0 + q", hint: "Check the units: q is a momentum and cannot be added to a position." },
    { expr: "x0 + hbar/q", hint: "The phase has modulus 1, so the density |ψ|² is the same function of x as before: nothing in it can shift." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The position average depends only on $|\psi|^2$, and a phase factor does not change $|\psi|^2$.

<Step title="The average">

$|\psi_\text{new}|^2 = \left|e^{iqx/\hbar}\right|^2|\psi_0|^2 = |\psi_0|^2$, so

<Formula label="Position average after the boost" tex="\langle\hat x\rangle_\text{new} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\left|\psi_\text{new}(x)\right|^2dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x\left|\psi_0(x)\right|^2dx = x_0" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.20)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The same argument shows that $\psi_\text{new}$ is still normalized and that the average of every function of $x$, and so $\Delta x$, is unchanged.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the expectation value $\langle\hat p\rangle$ in the state $\psi_\text{new}$?

<Hint>

Let $\hat p = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}$ act on the product $e^{iqx/\hbar}\psi_0(x)$. Two terms appear.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-p" label="$\langle\hat p\rangle_\text{new} =$" expr="p0 + q"
  vars={{ p0: [1, 3], q: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ p0: "p_0", hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "p0", hint: "A phase that depends on x is not harmless for the momentum: the derivative in the momentum operator acts on it too." },
    { expr: "p0 - q", hint: "Check the sign: (ħ/i) times the derivative of e^(iqx/ħ) is +q times the exponential." },
    { expr: "p0 + q/hbar", hint: "The ħ of the momentum operator cancels the 1/ħ in the exponent. Check the units: q is already a momentum." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The momentum operator differentiates the phase factor, and that brings down $q$.

<Step title="The momentum operator on the boosted state">

<Formula label="Momentum operator acting on the boosted wavefunction" tex="\hat p\,\psi_\text{new} = \frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(e^{iqx/\hbar}\,\psi_0\right) = e^{iqx/\hbar}\left(q\,\psi_0 + \hat p\,\psi_0\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The average">

Multiplying by $\psi_\text{new}^* = e^{-iqx/\hbar}\psi_0^*$ removes the phase:

<Formula label="Momentum average after the boost" tex="\langle\hat p\rangle_\text{new} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\psi_0^*\left(q + \hat p\right)\psi_0\,dx = q + p_0" cite="reading3" at="notes p. 5, eqs. (2.26)–(2.27)" />

using the normalization of $\psi_0$ for the first term.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a plane wave $\psi_0 = e^{ip_0x/\hbar}$ the boost gives $e^{i(p_0 + q)x/\hbar}$, a plane wave of momentum $p_0 + q$. The lesson's example, a Gaussian multiplied by $e^{ik_0x}$, is the case $p_0 = 0$ and $q = \hbar k_0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Based on these results, what is the physical meaning of multiplying a wavefunction by the factor $e^{iqx/\hbar}$?

<Hint>

Compare the position distribution and the average momentum before and after.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-meaning" choices={["It leaves the position distribution alone and adds q to the particle's momentum", "It moves the packet a distance q along x and leaves its momentum alone", "Nothing: a phase factor never changes anything that can be measured", "It adds q to the momentum and moves the packet at the same time"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The factor gives the particle a kick.

<Step title="What changes and what does not">

The probability density in $x$ is the same function as before, so the particle is found in the same places with the same probabilities. Its average momentum has grown by $q$. So $e^{iqx/\hbar}$ adds the momentum $q$ to the particle, as a sudden push would: a boost. The momentum of a wavefunction is stored in the way its phase changes with $x$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In momentum space the statement is exact, not only for the average. The Fourier transform of $e^{iqx/\hbar}\psi_0$ is $\tilde\Phi_0(p - q)$: the whole momentum distribution is moved by $q$. A constant phase $e^{i\alpha}$, in contrast, changes nothing measurable. MIT's sheet calls $e^{iqx/\hbar}$ an overall factor, but it is a different phase at each point, and that is why it matters.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Compute the commutators $[\hat p, \hat B_q]$ and $[\hat x, \hat B_q]$. Each is a number times $\hat B_q$: give the two numbers.

<Hint>

Let each commutator act on a test function $\varphi(x)$. Part (b) has already worked out $\hat p$ acting on $e^{iqx/\hbar}\varphi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-comm-p" label="$[\hat p, \hat B_q] = (\ldots)\,\hat B_q$, with $(\ldots) =$" expr="q"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-q", hint: "That is the commutator in the other order, of B with p." },
    { expr: "q/hbar", hint: "The ħ of the momentum operator cancels the 1/ħ of the exponent. Check the units: p times B minus B times p must be a momentum times B." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p7-comm-x" label="$[\hat x, \hat B_q] = (\ldots)\,\hat B_q$, with $(\ldots) =$" expr="0"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "hbar/q", hint: "Both operators only multiply by a function of x, and two such multiplications can be done in either order." },
    { expr: "q", hint: "That is the number for the momentum. The position operator does not differentiate, so it does not notice the phase." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $\hat B_q$ is multiplication by a function of $x$. It commutes with $\hat x$, and $\hat p$ differentiates it.

<Step title="With the momentum">

From part (b), with any $\varphi$ in place of $\psi_0$: $\hat p\,\hat B_q\,\varphi = e^{iqx/\hbar}\left(q + \hat p\right)\varphi = q\,\hat B_q\,\varphi + \hat B_q\,\hat p\,\varphi$. So

<Formula label="Commutator of the momentum with the boost" tex="\left[\hat p, \hat B_q\right] = q\,\hat B_q" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="With the position">

$\hat x\,\hat B_q\,\varphi = x\,e^{iqx/\hbar}\varphi = e^{iqx/\hbar}\,x\,\varphi = \hat B_q\,\hat x\,\varphi$, so

<Formula label="Commutator of the position with the boost" tex="\left[\hat x, \hat B_q\right] = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The commutators give parts (a) and (b) again. The first says $\hat p\,\hat B_q = \hat B_q\left(\hat p + q\right)$. Since $\hat B_q^\dagger\hat B_q = 1$ (the conjugate phase undoes the phase), $\langle\hat p\rangle_\text{new} = \int\psi_0^*\,\hat B_q^\dagger\,\hat p\,\hat B_q\,\psi_0\,dx = \int\psi_0^*\left(\hat p + q\right)\psi_0\,dx = p_0 + q$, and in the same way $\langle\hat x\rangle_\text{new} = x_0$. An expansion in $q$ also agrees: to first order $\hat B_q = 1 + iq\hat x/\hbar$, and $[\hat p, iq\hat x/\hbar] = (iq/\hbar)(-i\hbar) = q$.

</Step>

</Solution>
