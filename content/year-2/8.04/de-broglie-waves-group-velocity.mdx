A free particle with momentum $p$ has a matter wave with de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = h/p$. But a wave of what? This lecture looks at how the wave behaves when the observer moves, and finds that it cannot be an ordinary, measurable wave. Then it justifies the second de Broglie relation, $E = \hbar\omega$, through the group velocity of a wave packet, and uses superposition to decide which complex wave describes a free particle (notes pp. 1–7). (MIT's material for this lecture is Zwiebach's lecture notes, Chapter 4; this page follows their order in its own words, with its own examples.)

## A wave of what?

The matter wave will turn out to be the **wavefunction**, which obeys the Schrödinger equation and carries information about probabilities (notes p. 1). Does it have a direction, like the polarization of an electromagnetic wave? Something similar exists, and it is spin; but in many situations (slow particles, no magnetic fields) spin hardly matters, so for now the wavefunction is a single complex number at each point and time (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="The wavefunction" tex="\Psi(x, t) \in \mathbb{C}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.1)" />

The de Broglie relation can be written with the wavenumber $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ and $\hbar = h/(2\pi)$ (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="Momentum and wavenumber" tex="p = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \frac{h}{2\pi}\frac{2\pi}{\lambda} = \hbar k" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.2)" />

## The de Broglie wavelength in two frames

Take two frames: S, and S′ moving along $+x$ at constant velocity $v$, with the origins together at time zero. They are related by a **Galilean transformation** (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="Galilean transformation" tex="x' = x - vt, \qquad t' = t" cite="mit" at="notes p. 1, eq. (1.3)" />

A particle of mass $m$ moving slowly with velocity $\tilde v$ in S (as in the notes, the tilde keeps it apart from the frames' relative velocity $v$) has, by differentiating, velocity $\tilde v - v$ in S′, so its momenta in the two frames differ by $mv$: $p' = p - mv$ (notes p. 2, eqs. (1.4)–(1.6)). The two observers therefore find different de Broglie wavelengths:

<Formula label="The wavelength changes" tex="\lambda' = \frac{h}{p'} = \frac{h}{p - mv} \ne \lambda" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.7)" />

That is strange. For an ordinary wave travelling through a medium, such as sound or water waves, the wavelength is the same for all Galilean observers and only the frequency changes (notes p. 2). Intuitively: a photograph of the wave at one instant shows its wavelength, and both observers agree on the photograph; but to measure the frequency each must wait for a full period to pass, and that takes different times for observers moving differently.

**The calculation for an ordinary wave** (notes pp. 2–3). The wave's value at a point and time, $\cos\phi$ or $\sin\phi$ or a mix, is observable, so both observers must agree on its **phase** $\phi = kx - \omega t$. In S,

<Formula label="The phase of a wave" tex="\phi = kx - \omega t = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\left(x - Vt\right), \qquad V = \frac{\omega}{k}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 2, eq. (1.8)" />

with $V$ the wave's velocity. The wavelength is read from the coefficient of $x$, and the frequency from minus the coefficient of $t$. Requiring $\phi'(x', t') = \phi(x, t)$ and substituting $x = x' + vt'$, $t = t'$:

<Formula label="The phase seen from S′" tex="\phi' = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\left(x' + vt' - Vt'\right) = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}x' - \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\left(V - v\right)t'" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.10)" />

<Formula label="Wavelength and frequency in S′" tex="\lambda' = \lambda, \qquad \omega' = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\left(V - v\right) = \omega\left(1 - \frac{v}{V}\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eqs. (1.11)–(1.12)" />

So for a wave in a medium, the wavelength is a Galilean invariant and the frequency changes (notes p. 3). Matter waves break this rule, and the conclusion is that **matter waves are not directly measurable**: their value is not something all Galilean observers must agree on, so $\Psi'(x', t')$ need not equal $\Psi(x, t)$ at the same point and time. (The correct relation between the two is a homework problem in the course.) (notes p. 3, eq. (1.13))

## The de Broglie relations

The wavelength is fixed by the momentum, $p = \hbar k$. De Broglie also proposed that the frequency is fixed by the energy (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Energy and frequency" tex="E = \hbar\omega" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.15)" />

with $E = p^2/(2m)$ for a nonrelativistic particle (eq. (1.16)). The notes give three reasons to believe it (notes pp. 3–4):

1. **Group velocity.** A wave packet built from matter waves moves at the group velocity $d\omega/dk$, and with $E = \hbar\omega$ this equals the particle's velocity:

<Formula label="The packet moves with the particle" tex="v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{dE}{dp} = \frac{d}{dp}\left(\frac{p^2}{2m}\right) = \frac{p}{m} = v" cite="mit" at="notes p. 3, eq. (1.17)" />

2. **Relativity.** $(E/c, \vec p)$ is a four-vector, and so is $(\omega/c, \vec k)$ for a wave whose phase is a relativistic invariant. Setting one equal to $\hbar$ times the other is consistent in every frame, and gives both relations at once (notes pp. 3–4, eqs. (1.18)–(1.20)).
3. **Photons.** For light, $E = \hbar\omega$ is Einstein's $E = h\nu$.

In summary, the **de Broglie relations**, valid for all particles (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="The de Broglie relations" tex="p = \hbar k, \qquad E = \hbar\omega" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (1.21)" />

## Phase and group velocities

To see why a packet moves at $d\omega/dk$, superpose plane waves with some function $\omega(k)$ (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="A wave packet" tex="\Psi(x, t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dk\,\Phi(k)\,e^{i\left(kx - \omega(k)t\right)}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.22)" />

where $\Phi(k)$ is peaked around $k = k_0$. Suppose first that $\Phi(k)$ is real, so the phase of the integrand is just $kx - \omega(k)t$. **Stationary phase**: only $k$ near $k_0$ can contribute, and if the phase still varied quickly there the contributions would cancel out. So the packet is large only where the phase has zero derivative at $k_0$ (notes pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="Stationary phase" tex="\left.\frac{d}{dk}\left(kx - \omega(k)t\right)\right|_{k_0} = x - \left.\frac{d\omega}{dk}\right|_{k_0}t = 0" cite="mit" at="notes p. 4, eq. (2.24)" />

so the packet is found at $x = \left(d\omega/dk\right)t$, moving with the **group velocity** (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Group velocity" tex="v_g = \left.\frac{d\omega}{dk}\right|_{k_0}" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.26)" />

(An exercise in the notes asks what changes if $\Phi$ is complex, with its own phase; the answer, worked out here rather than in the notes, is that the packet's position is offset but its velocity is unchanged.)

**A more careful version** (notes p. 5). At $t = 0$ the packet is $\Psi(x, 0) = \int dk\,\Phi(k)e^{ikx}$. Expanding $\omega(k) \approx \omega(k_0) + (k - k_0)\,v_g$, where $v_g$ is the slope $d\omega/dk$ at $k_0$, and dropping the terms of order $(k - k_0)^2$, the factors that do not depend on $k$ come out of the integral and what is left is the initial packet, shifted:

<Formula label="The packet slides along unchanged" tex="\Psi(x, t) = e^{-i\omega(k_0)t + ik_0v_gt}\,\Psi\left(x - v_gt, \ 0\right)" cite="mit" at="notes p. 5, eq. (2.31)" />

Taking the norm removes the phase factor in front: $\left|\Psi(x, t)\right| = \left|\Psi\left(x - v_gt, \ 0\right)\right|$. If the packet started with its peak at $x_0$, its peak is at $x_0 + v_gt$ later, moving at the group velocity (notes p. 5, eqs. (2.32)–(2.33)). The individual wave crests move instead at the **phase velocity** $\omega/k$, which for matter waves is different.

## The free particle's wavefunction

Which wave should describe a free particle with energy $E = \hbar\omega$ and momentum $p = \hbar k$, moving along $+x$? Candidates are $\sin(kx - \omega t)$, $\cos(kx - \omega t)$, $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ (time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$) and $e^{-i(kx - \omega t)}$ (time dependence $e^{+i\omega t}$) (notes p. 6). Superposition decides. A particle equally likely to move right or left is described by the sum of a right-moving and a left-moving wave, and a wavefunction that vanishes for all $x$ at some instant cannot describe a particle (notes pp. 6–7):

- With sines: $\sin(kx - \omega t) + \sin(kx + \omega t) = 2\sin kx\cos\omega t$, which vanishes everywhere whenever $\cos\omega t = 0$. Ruled out.
- With cosines: $\cos(kx - \omega t) + \cos(kx + \omega t) = 2\cos kx\cos\omega t$, which vanishes in the same way. Ruled out.
- With $e^{-i\omega t}$: $e^{i(kx - \omega t)} + e^{i(-kx - \omega t)} = 2\cos kx\,e^{-i\omega t}$, never zero for all $x$, since $e^{-i\omega t}$ is never zero. Allowed.
- With $e^{+i\omega t}$: likewise $2\cos kx\,e^{i\omega t}$. Allowed.

Could both of the last two describe the same right-moving particle? If so, their sum would describe it too (adding a state to itself changes nothing), but $e^{i(kx - \omega t)} + e^{-i(kx - \omega t)} = 2\cos(kx - \omega t)$, the cosine already ruled out. So one must choose, and the choice is a convention that all physicists share (notes p. 7):

<Formula label="Free-particle wavefunction" tex="\Psi(x, t) = e^{i\left(kx - \omega t\right)}, \qquad p = \hbar k, \quad E = \hbar\omega" cite="mit" at="notes p. 7, eqs. (3.12)–(3.13)" />

In three dimensions it is $e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega t)}$, with $\vec p = \hbar\vec k$ (notes p. 7, eqs. (3.14)–(3.15)).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Matter waves in an expanding universe">
In an expanding universe light is redshifted: its wavelength stretches in proportion to the scale factor $a(t)$. Free massive particles behave the same way. The standard result (an 8.286 homework problem, mentioned on the [geodesic equation page](/courses/8-286/christoffel-symbols)) is that their momentum falls as $1/a(t)$, and with $\lambda = h/p$ their de Broglie wavelength grows as $a(t)$, exactly like a photon's (this way of putting it is the page's own). [8.286 derives the redshift of light](/courses/8-286/cosmological-redshift) in Lecture 5.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Two frames and two velocities">
<Problem>
(a) An electron moves at $1.00 \times 10^6$ m/s in frame S. Find its de Broglie wavelength in S and in a frame S′ moving at $4.0 \times 10^5$ m/s in the same direction. ($h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.) (b) A 680 Hz sound wave travels through still air at 340 m/s. What wavelength and frequency does a cyclist riding along with it at 34 m/s measure? (c) Find the electron's phase and group velocities in S.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Matter wave">
In S, $p = m_ev = 9.109 \times 10^{-25}$ kg m/s and $\lambda = h/p \approx 7.27 \times 10^{-10}$ m $= 0.727$ nm. In S′ the electron moves at $6.0 \times 10^5$ m/s, so $\lambda' = h/(m_e \times 6.0 \times 10^5) \approx 1.21$ nm. The two observers disagree.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Sound wave">
In the air, $\lambda = 340/680 = 0.50$ m. The cyclist measures the same 0.50 m, and the frequency $680 \times \left(1 - 34/340\right) = 612$ Hz.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Phase and group velocity">
With $\omega = \hbar k^2/(2m)$, the phase velocity is $\omega/k = \hbar k/(2m) = v/2 = 5.0 \times 10^5$ m/s, and the group velocity is $d\omega/dk = \hbar k/m = v = 1.00 \times 10^6$ m/s: the packet keeps up with the electron, and its crests move at half the speed.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Observers in different Galilean frames find different de Broglie wavelengths, $\lambda' = h/(p - mv)$, whereas for sound or water waves the wavelength is invariant and only the frequency changes: matter waves are not directly measurable.
- The de Broglie relations are $p = \hbar k$ and $E = \hbar\omega$; with them, the group velocity $d\omega/dk$ of a packet equals the particle's velocity.
- By stationary phase, a packet peaked at $k_0$ moves at $v_g = d\omega/dk$ at $k_0$; its crests move at the phase velocity $\omega/k$.
- Superposition rules out real waves, and convention picks $\Psi = e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ as the free particle's wavefunction.
