Zwiebach returns to the diagram of energy levels and asks what the states look like in a classical picture: where the electron turns around, and how elongated its orbit is. ## The energy diagram of a central potential

A central potential $V(r)$ is solved one value of $l$ at a time. With $\psi = \frac{u(r)}{r}Y_{lm}(\theta, \phi)$, each $l$ has its own radial equation, a one-dimensional Schrödinger equation with an effective potential (transcript p. 1, board):

<Formula label="Radial equation" tex="-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} + V_{\text{eff}}(r)\,u = E\,u, \qquad V_{\text{eff}}(r) = V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2\,l(l + 1)}{2mr^2}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 1, board" />

The function $u$ does not know about $m$. The diagram of the spectrum therefore has a column for each $l = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ (not a continuous axis), with energy upwards (transcript p. 1). Solving the $l = 0$ equation gives a ladder of levels whose lowest is the ground state of that equation; then $l = 1$ gives another ladder, then $l = 2$, and so on. The lowest level of all the columns is the ground state of the whole system (transcript p. 2).

In a general central potential there is no reason for the levels of different columns to line up. In hydrogen they do: Zwiebach calls this a miracle, the result of a special symmetry of the $1/r$ potential (transcript p. 2). The diagram also hides a degeneracy: each level in the column $l$ stands for $2l + 1$ states, one for each $m$ from $-l$ to $l$, so each $l = 1$ level is three states and each $l = 2$ level five (transcript p. 5). These are usually not drawn, but they must be counted.

## Turning points

For hydrogen, $V = -e^2/r$, and the centrifugal term $\hbar^2l(l + 1)/(2mr^2)$ grows towards the origin. For $l > 0$ the effective potential is a well. At an energy $E_n$ a classical electron moves back and forth between two **turning points** $r_-$ and $r_+$, where $V_{\text{eff}} = E_n$ (transcript p. 2). The semi-classical picture is an ellipse with the proton at a focus, on which the distance to the proton swings between $r_-$ and $r_+$. It is roughly right for large quantum numbers. Quantum mechanically, the probability density is small outside the turning points and follows the time the classical electron spends at each radius (transcript p. 2).

The turning points solve (transcript p. 3, board)

<Formula label="Turning point condition" tex="\frac{\hbar^2\,l(l + 1)}{2mr^2} - \frac{e^2}{r} = -\frac{e^2}{2a_0}\,\frac{1}{n^2}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3, board" />

With $r = a_0x$ and $a_0 = \hbar^2/(me^2)$, the coefficient of the first term becomes $\frac{\hbar^2}{2ma_0^2} = \frac{e^2}{2a_0}$, the same factor as on the right. Dividing it out leaves a quadratic equation without units (transcript p. 3, board):

<Formula label="Turning points without units" tex="\frac{l(l + 1)}{x^2} - \frac{2}{x} + \frac{1}{n^2} = 0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3, board" />

It is quadratic in $1/x$, with solutions (transcript p. 4, board)

<Formula label="Solutions for 1/x" tex="\frac{1}{x} = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{1 - \frac{l(l + 1)}{n^2}}}{l(l + 1)}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4, board" />

Inverting, and multiplying top and bottom by $1 \mp \sqrt{\cdots}$, the denominator becomes $1 - \left(1 - \frac{l(l + 1)}{n^2}\right) = \frac{l(l + 1)}{n^2}$, and $l(l + 1)$ cancels (transcript p. 4, board):

<Formula label="Turning points of a hydrogen orbit" tex="r_\pm = n^2a_0\left(1 \pm \sqrt{1 - \frac{l(l + 1)}{n^2}}\right)" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4, board" />

Here $r_+$ is the outer and $r_-$ the inner turning point. (On the board the signs appear as $\mp$, carried over from the step for $x$; with $r_+$ the larger radius, as in the lecture's own example, they read $\pm$.)

## Shapes of the orbits

The square root decides the shape (transcript p. 4):

- For the largest $l = n - 1$, $\frac{l(l + 1)}{n^2} = 1 - \frac1n$ is close to 1 when $n$ is large. Then $r_+ \approx r_-$ and the orbit is nearly a circle.
- For $l = 0$ the square root is 1, so $r_- = 0$ and $r_+ = 2n^2a_0$: the most elongated orbit possible, which passes through the proton. The semi-classical picture is least reliable here; it works best for moderately large $l$.

Most striking is the average of the two (transcript p. 5):

<Formula label="Semi-major axis" tex="\frac{r_+ + r_-}{2} = n^2a_0" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 5, board" />

$r_+ + r_-$ is the long axis of the ellipse, so half of it is the distance from the centre of the ellipse (not the focus) to its far end: the **semi-major axis**. It does not depend on $l$. All the degenerate states of level $n$ correspond to ellipses with the same semi-major axis, from the circle at $l = n - 1$ to the line at $l = 0$ (transcript p. 5).

Why should such different ellipses have the same energy? Classically this was already in Kepler's third law: the period of an orbit depends only on its semi-major axis, and periods are related to energies. So the degeneracy in quantum mechanics is not unreasonable (transcript p. 5).

Since $r_\pm = n^2a_0(1 \pm \varepsilon)$ is exactly the form of the nearest and farthest distances of an ellipse with semi-major axis $n^2a_0$, the **eccentricity** is $\varepsilon = \sqrt{1 - l(l + 1)/n^2}$ (the page's own step): 1 for $l = 0$ and $1/\sqrt n$ for $l = n - 1$.

**A Rydberg atom.** Zwiebach's example is a highly excited atom with $n = 100$ and $l = 60$ (transcript p. 5). Then $n^2a_0 = 10{,}000\,a_0$ and the bracket is $1 \pm 0.80$, so $r_+ \approx 18{,}000\,a_0$ and $r_- \approx 2{,}000\,a_0$. With $a_0 = 0.0529$ nm, the semi-major axis is about 529 nm, about half a micrometre: some ten thousand times the size of the atom in its ground state.

<WorkedExample title="The ellipses of level n = 10">
<Problem>
Find the turning points, the semi-major axis and the eccentricity of the semi-classical orbits with $n = 10$ and $l = 0$, $5$ and $9$.
</Problem>
<Step title="l = 0">
The square root is 1: $r_- = 0$, $r_+ = 2 \times 100\,a_0 = 200\,a_0$, and $\varepsilon = 1$.
</Step>
<Step title="l = 5">
$\sqrt{1 - 30/100} = \sqrt{0.7} \approx 0.837$, so $r_+ \approx 183.7\,a_0$ and $r_- \approx 16.3\,a_0$, with $\varepsilon \approx 0.837$.
</Step>
<Step title="l = 9">
$\sqrt{1 - 90/100} = \sqrt{0.1} \approx 0.316$, so $r_+ \approx 131.6\,a_0$ and $r_- \approx 68.4\,a_0$, with $\varepsilon \approx 0.316$. Even the largest $l$ is not a circle at $n = 10$; at $n = 100$ it would have $\varepsilon = 1/\sqrt{100} = 0.1$.
</Step>
<Step title="Same semi-major axis">
In all three cases $\frac12(r_+ + r_-) = 100\,a_0$: the three orbits, and the $2l + 1$ states of each, all have the energy $E_{10} = -\frac{e^2}{2a_0}\,\frac{1}{100}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="From Kepler to dark matter">
The classical fact behind this page, that an orbit's period and energy are set by its size and the central mass, is how astronomers weigh things in space. Applied to stars and gas orbiting in galaxies, Kepler's laws predict orbital speeds that fall with distance beyond the visible matter. They are measured to stay flat instead, one of the main pieces of evidence for [dark matter](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/rotation-curves).
</Callout>

## Summary

- The spectrum of a central potential is drawn in columns, one radial equation for each $l$; each level stands for $2l + 1$ states. In hydrogen, levels of different $l$ coincide, thanks to a special symmetry of the $1/r$ potential.
- The classical turning points of hydrogen's effective potential are $r_\pm = n^2a_0\left(1 \pm \sqrt{1 - l(l + 1)/n^2}\right)$.
- $l = n - 1$ gives nearly circular orbits for large $n$; $l = 0$ gives $r_- = 0$, $r_+ = 2n^2a_0$; the eccentricity is $\sqrt{1 - l(l + 1)/n^2}$.
- Every state of level $n$ has the semi-major axis $n^2a_0$, the quantum counterpart of Kepler's third law.
