This set practises the lessons on [interference and soap bubbles](/courses/8-03/interference-soap-bubbles), on [many sources and phased radar](/courses/8-03/phased-radar-electron-interference) and on [diffraction](/courses/8-03/diffraction-resolution). Problem 1 also uses the [perfect conductor](/courses/8-03/electromagnetic-waves) of an earlier lesson, and Problem 3 the [radiation of an accelerated charge](/courses/8-03/wave-plates-radiation). The five problems follow MIT's Homework 10, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for its homeworks, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. All five problems count for the score (100 points). MIT's sheet prints the last problem as "Problem 5"; it is Problem 10.5.

Notation: as in the lessons, $\delta$ is the phase difference between two waves that meet, and $I_0$ is the intensity that one source (one slit, one dipole) gives on its own. The frequency in hertz is $\nu$, the wavelength in air is $\lambda = c/\nu$, and $n$ is a refractive index. The letter $L$ is the distance from the source to the conductor in Problem 1 and the distance from the slits to the screen in Problem 2. The letter $d$ is the distance between the centres of two neighbouring slits in Problems 2 and 5, and the thickness of the film in Problem 4. Where the lesson on thin films numbers the bright thicknesses with a capital N, MIT's sheet and this page write $m$.

MIT's figures are not copied here: each situation is described in words, and the one graph that a problem needs (Figure 5, in Problem 5) is drawn again from its formula.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within 1% of ours (2% for the small intensity in Problem 3(a), and a wider margin in Problem 5, where the values are read from a graph); the unit is named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `c/(2*nu)` or `cos(pi*sin(th)/2)^2`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the symbols the box names (`nu` stands for $\nu$, `th` for $\theta$, and `pi` for $\pi$).

MIT gives 20 points per problem and no split between parts. The split here is ours. Parts that ask for a sketch have no box for the sketch itself, only a short check: open the solution and compare your sketch with the graph there. A few boxes are checks of our own that MIT does not ask for; they are marked as such. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A point source in front of a mirror" points={20} mit="Problem 10.1">

A point source S sends out light of frequency $\nu$ in air ($n = 1$). It stands at a distance $L$ in front of a flat perfect conductor, which reflects all the light. An observer far away looks back at S along the line through S that is perpendicular to the conductor: the conductor, the source and the observer lie on one straight line, in that order (MIT's Figure 1). Two waves reach the observer, one straight from S and one by way of the conductor. The reflected wave passes S on its way; ignore any absorption by S.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

For which distances $L$ does the observer see a maximum of the interference pattern? Give the smallest such $L$, and the step from one such $L$ to the next, in terms of $c$ and $\nu$.

<Hint>

How much further does the reflected wave travel than the direct one? The observer is so far away that the two waves arrive with the same amplitude.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The path is not the whole story. The total electric field must be zero at the surface of a perfect conductor. What does that do to the sign of the reflected wave?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Add the phase from the extra path and the phase from the reflection. A maximum needs a total phase difference that is a whole number of times $2\pi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a-first" label="smallest $L$ for a maximum $=$" expr="c/(4*nu)"
  vars={{ c: [2, 5], nu: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ c: "c", nu: "\\nu" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "c/(2*nu)", hint: "With L equal to half a wavelength the reflected wave travels one whole wavelength further, which on its own would leave the two waves in step. But the reflection at a perfect conductor reverses the field, so here the two waves cancel." },
    { expr: "c/nu", hint: "With L equal to one wavelength the extra path is two wavelengths, and the reflection reverses the field: the two waves cancel at this distance." },
    { expr: "c/(8*nu)", hint: "The extra path is 2L, to the conductor and back. With this L it is a quarter of a wavelength, and together with the reversal at the conductor the phase difference is 3π/2: neither a maximum nor a minimum." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-a-step" label="step between such distances $=$" expr="c/(2*nu)"
  vars={{ c: [2, 5], nu: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ c: "c", nu: "\\nu" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "c/nu", hint: "The reflected wave goes to the conductor and back. When L grows by this much, the extra path grows by two wavelengths, so one maximum was skipped on the way." },
    { expr: "c/(4*nu)", hint: "That is the step from a maximum to the nearest minimum: the extra path changes by half a wavelength. To the next maximum it must change by a whole wavelength." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The observer receives two waves from the same source. They differ in phase for two reasons: the reflected wave travels further, and the conductor turns its field around.

<Step title="The reflection">

The charges of a perfect conductor cancel any electric field along its surface, so the total field of the incoming and the reflected wave is zero there:

<Formula label="Boundary condition at a perfect conductor" tex="\vec E = 0 \quad \text{at the surface}" cite="reading4" at="p. 7 (the boundary condition E = 0 at the conductor, and the reflected wave with a minus sign)" />

The reflected wave therefore leaves the surface with the opposite sign of the incoming one. A minus sign is a phase of $\pi$. The lesson on thin films found the same flip for a reflection off a medium with a larger refractive index (Lecture 20 notes, p. 2).

</Step>

<Step title="The phase difference">

The reflected wave first travels the distance $L$ to the conductor and then $L$ back to the place of the source. From there on the two waves travel together. The extra path is $2L$, which is $2L/\lambda$ wavelengths:

<Formula label="Phase difference at the observer" tex="\delta = \frac{2L}{\lambda}\,2\pi + \pi = \frac{4\pi\nu L}{c} + \pi" cite="mit" at="Problem 10.1(a), p. 1 (our own solution; the same form as the phase difference of the soap film, Lecture 20 notes, p. 3)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The maxima">

The waves add fully when $\delta = 2\pi m$ with $m$ a whole number (Lecture 20 notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Distances with a maximum" tex="2L = \left(m - \tfrac12\right)\lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad L = (2m - 1)\,\frac{\lambda}{4} = (2m - 1)\,\frac{c}{4\nu}, \qquad m = 1, 2, 3, \dots" cite="mit" at="Problem 10.1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The smallest distance is a quarter of a wavelength, $c/(4\nu)$, and the distances follow each other in steps of half a wavelength, $c/(2\nu)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The observer is far away compared with $L$, so the two waves have the same amplitude there. The lesson's average intensity for two waves of equal amplitude (Lecture 20 notes, p. 1) is $2I_0(1 + \cos\delta)$, where $I_0$ is the intensity of the source without the conductor. The $\pi$ of the reflection changes the sign of the cosine:

<Formula label="Intensity at the observer" tex="\langle I\rangle = 2I_0\left[1 - \cos\frac{4\pi\nu L}{c}\right] = 4I_0\sin^2\frac{2\pi\nu L}{c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 10.1, p. 1 (our own solution, from the average intensity of Lecture 20 notes, p. 1)" />

At $L = c/(4\nu)$ the sine is $\sin(\pi/2) = 1$: four times the intensity of the source alone, the most that two equal waves can give. A number of our own: for green light with $\lambda = 520$ nm the first three maxima are at $L = 130$ nm, $390$ nm and $650$ nm.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

For which distances $L$ does the observer see a minimum? Give the smallest such $L$ that is larger than zero.

<Hint>

A minimum needs a total phase difference that is an odd number of times $\pi$. One $\pi$ is already there.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="smallest $L > 0$ for a minimum $=$" expr="c/(2*nu)"
  vars={{ c: [2, 5], nu: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ c: "c", nu: "\\nu" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "c/(4*nu)", hint: "With L equal to a quarter of a wavelength the extra path is half a wavelength. Together with the reversal at the conductor the two waves are in step: a maximum." },
    { expr: "c/nu", hint: "This distance does give a minimum, but it is not the smallest one: a shorter distance already makes the extra path a whole number of wavelengths." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The reflection alone already puts the two waves in antiphase. They stay in antiphase when the extra path adds a whole number of wavelengths.

<Step title="The minima">

With the phase difference of part (a), $\delta = (2m + 1)\pi$ gives

<Formula label="Distances with a minimum" tex="2L = m\lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad L = m\,\frac{\lambda}{2} = m\,\frac{c}{2\nu}, \qquad m = 0, 1, 2, \dots" cite="mit" at="Problem 10.1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The smallest distance larger than zero is half a wavelength, $c/(2\nu)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The intensity of part (a), $4I_0\sin^2(2\pi\nu L/c)$, is zero when $2\pi\nu L/c = m\pi$: the same distances. Because the two amplitudes are equal the minimum is completely dark. The case $m = 0$ is a source that sits on the conductor: its light and the reflection cancel in this direction. It is the same effect as the very thin soap film of the lesson, which reflects nothing (Lecture 20 notes, p. 4). The minima lie halfway between the maxima of part (a).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={7}>

A plate of glass with refractive index $n$ is placed between the source and the conductor, so that the light crosses it on the way to the conductor and again on the way back. Assume that the surfaces of the glass reflect nothing. What is the smallest thickness $h$ of the plate that turns a maximum into a minimum and a minimum into a maximum, whatever $L$ is? Give $h$ in terms of $c$, $\nu$ and $n$.

<Hint>

In glass the wavelength is $\lambda/n$, so a thickness $h$ of glass holds more wavelengths than the same thickness of air. How many more?

</Hint>

<Hint>

To swap maxima and minima the phase difference must change by $\pi$. Count how often the reflected wave crosses the plate.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$h =$" expr="c/(4*nu*(n - 1))"
  vars={{ c: [2, 5], nu: [0.5, 2], n: [1.3, 1.9] }}
  names={{ c: "c", nu: "\\nu", n: "n" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "c/(4*nu*n)", hint: "The glass takes the place of air that was there before. Only the extra number of wavelengths counts, and that goes with n − 1, not with n." },
    { expr: "c/(2*nu*(n - 1))", hint: "The reflected wave crosses the plate twice, once on the way to the conductor and once on the way back. With this thickness the phase changes by 2π and nothing changes for the observer." },
    { expr: "c/(4*nu)", hint: "The thickness must depend on the glass: a plate with n close to 1 hardly differs from air and would have to be very thick." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The plate does not change the path in metres, but it changes the number of wavelengths on the path of the reflected wave. Half a wavelength more swaps maxima and minima.

<Step title="The extra phase">

A thickness $h$ holds $h/\lambda$ wavelengths in air and $nh/\lambda$ in glass, where the wavelength is $\lambda/n$ (Lecture 20 notes, p. 3). The reflected wave crosses the plate twice and the direct wave not at all, so the phase difference of part (a) grows by

<Formula label="Extra phase from the plate" tex="\Delta\delta = 2\,(n - 1)\,\frac{h}{\lambda}\,2\pi" cite="mit" at="Problem 10.1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The thickness">

Maxima and minima change places when $\Delta\delta$ is $\pi$ (or $3\pi$, $5\pi$, …). The smallest thickness is

<Formula label="Thinnest plate" tex="2\,(n - 1)\,\frac{h}{\lambda}\,2\pi = \pi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = \frac{\lambda}{4\,(n - 1)} = \frac{c}{4\nu\,(n - 1)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 10.1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The answer does not contain $L$: the plate adds the same phase wherever it stands between S and the conductor. For $n \to 1$ the plate becomes air and $h$ grows without limit, as it should. A number of our own: for $\lambda = 520$ nm and glass with $n = 1.5$, $h = 520/(4 \times 0.5) = 260$ nm.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Three narrow slits" points={20} mit="Problem 10.2">

Three long, narrow, parallel slits are lit by light of one wavelength, $\lambda = 500$ nm. Neighbouring slits are $d = 1$ mm apart, centre to centre. The light falls on a screen at a distance $L = 5$ m behind the slits (MIT's Figure 2). The slits are so narrow that the diffraction pattern of a single slit can be ignored: one slit alone would light the screen evenly with the intensity $I_0$.

On this page $x$ is the position on the screen, measured from the centre of the central bright fringe. All angles are small, so a point at $x$ lies in the direction $\sin\theta \approx x/L$.

First, the top slit is completely blocked.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

With two slits open, what is the distance on the screen between the central maximum and the first minimum, in millimetres?

<Hint>

The lesson gives the phase difference between the waves of two slits in the direction $\theta$. At the first minimum the two waves are in antiphase.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="distance $=$ (mm)" value="1.25" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two waves cancel where their paths differ by half a wavelength.

<Step title="The phase difference on the screen">

<Formula label="Phase difference of neighbouring slits" tex="\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,d\sin\theta \approx \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda L}\,x" cite="reading2" at="p. 2 (the two-slit phase difference; the small-angle form with x/L is written out by us)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The first minimum">

The first minimum is at $\delta = \pi$:

<Formula label="First minimum of two slits" tex="x = \frac{\lambda L}{2d} = \frac{(500 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m})(5\ \text{m})}{2 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}} = 1.25 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 1.25\ \text{mm}" cite="mit" at="Problem 10.2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: m × m / m = m. The angle is $x/L = 2.5 \times 10^{-4}$, so the small-angle form is safe. A remark on the figure: with the top slit blocked, the two open slits are the middle and the bottom one, and the centre of their pattern lies opposite the point halfway between them, 0.5 mm below the mark 0 of MIT's Figure 2. The distances between the fringes do not depend on that.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Sketch the intensity on the screen near the central fringe, with five bright fringes (the central one and two on each side). Label the axes. What is the largest intensity of each of the five fringes, in units of $I_0$?

<Hint>

At the centre of a bright fringe the two fields are in step and add. How does the intensity depend on the field?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="largest intensity of each fringe $=$ (in units of $I_0$)" value="4" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two equal fields in step give twice the field and four times the intensity.

<Step title="The pattern">

<Formula label="Two-slit intensity" tex="\langle I\rangle = 4I_0\cos^2\frac{\delta}{2} = 4I_0\cos^2\frac{\pi d\,x}{\lambda L}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2 (the intensity A cos²(δ/2); the value A = 4I₀ is written out by us)" />

The bright fringes are at $\delta = 0, \pm2\pi, \pm4\pi$, that is at $x = 0$, $\pm2.5$ mm and $\pm5$ mm, and every one of them reaches $4I_0$. The dark lines between them, at $\pm1.25$ mm and $\pm3.75$ mm, are completely dark.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-6.2, 6.2]} range={[0, 10]} xLabel="x (mm)" yLabel="intensity / I₀"
  curves={[
    { expr: "4*cos(pi*x/2.5)^2", label: "two slits" }
  ]}
  caption="The intensity on the screen for two slits, in units of the intensity of one slit, drawn from 4 cos²(πdx/λL) with λL/d = 2.5 mm. Five equal fringes of height 4, with a bright fringe every 2.5 mm. The vertical scale is the same as in the graphs of parts (d) and (h)." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Averaged over $x$ the intensity is $4I_0 \times \tfrac12 = 2I_0$, the sum of the two slits: interference moves the energy from the dark lines to the bright ones and does not make any.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

The top slit is now uncovered, so that three equal slits are open.

<Part label="c" points={2}>

What is now the distance between the central maximum and the first minimum, in millimetres?

<Hint>

Draw the three fields as three arrows of equal length, each turned by $\delta$ from the one before. For which $\delta$ do they first close up into a triangle?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="distance $=$ (mm)" value="0.833" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Three equal arrows add up to zero when they form a closed triangle: each is turned by a third of a full turn from the one before.

<Step title="The first minimum">

<Formula label="First minimum of three slits" tex="\delta = \frac{2\pi}{3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \frac{\lambda L}{3d} = \frac{2.5\ \text{mm}}{3} = 0.833\ \text{mm}" cite="reading2" at="p. 3 (δ = 2π/3 for three slits; the position on the screen is our own)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The central fringe is narrower than with two slits (0.833 mm against 1.25 mm): more slits make sharper fringes, as the lesson found for the radar.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Sketch the new intensity near the central fringe as a function of $x$, with five bright fringes (the central one and two on each side). Label the axes.

This part has no answer box: open the solution and compare your sketch with the graph. The heights of the fringes are asked for in part (e).

<Hint>

Use the lesson's result for many equal sources, with three sources. Between two large fringes it has two zeros, and something must lie between those two zeros.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Three slits give large fringes where all three fields are in step, at the same places as for two slits, and one small fringe between each pair of large ones.

<Step title="The pattern">

<Formula label="Three-slit intensity" tex="\langle I\rangle = I_0\left[\frac{\sin(3\delta/2)}{\sin(\delta/2)}\right]^2 = I_0\left(1 + 2\cos\delta\right)^2, \qquad \delta = \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda L}\,x" cite="reading2" at="p. 4 (the N-source intensity with N = 3; the form with 1 + 2 cos δ is our own)" />

The second form comes from adding the three fields with the middle slit as the reference: $e^{i\delta} + 1 + e^{-i\delta} = 1 + 2\cos\delta$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-3.1, 3.1]} range={[0, 10]} xLabel="x (mm)" yLabel="intensity / I₀"
  curves={[
    { expr: "(1 + 2*cos(2*pi*x/2.5))^2", label: "three slits" }
  ]}
  caption="The intensity on the screen for three equal slits, in units of the intensity of one slit, drawn from (1 + 2 cos δ)² with δ = 2πx/(2.5 mm). Large fringes at x = 0 and ±2.5 mm, a small fringe at ±1.25 mm, and zeros at ±0.833 mm and ±1.667 mm." />

</Step>

<Step title="Read the sketch">

Counting from the centre, the five neighbouring maxima are the central fringe, a small fringe at $x = \pm1.25$ mm, and a large fringe at $x = \pm2.5$ mm. The zeros are at $\delta = \pm2\pi/3$ and $\pm4\pi/3$, that is at $x = \pm0.833$ mm and $\pm1.667$ mm. This is the lesson's rule that the number of small maxima between two large ones is the number of sources minus two (Lecture 21 notes, p. 5): here one.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

What is the largest intensity of each of the five fringes of part (d), in units of $I_0$? Give the value for the central fringe (the second fringe on each side has the same value) and the value for the first fringe on each side.

<Hint>

At the central fringe three equal fields are in step. At the first fringe on each side neighbouring fields are in antiphase: add them one by one.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-e-main" label="central fringe, and second fringe on each side $=$ (in units of $I_0$)" value="9" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-e-main" />

<Answer id="p2-e-side" label="first fringe on each side $=$ (in units of $I_0$)" value="1" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-e-side" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The intensity goes with the square of the total field. Three fields in step give three times the field; with $\delta = \pi$ two of the three cancel and one is left.

<Step title="The heights">

<Formula label="Heights of the three-slit fringes" tex="\delta = 0, \pm2\pi:\ \ \langle I\rangle = (1 + 2)^2 I_0 = 9I_0; \qquad \delta = \pm\pi:\ \ \langle I\rangle = (1 - 2)^2 I_0 = I_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 10.2(e), p. 2 (our own solution, from the three-slit intensity of part d)" />

So the five fringes, from $x = -2.5$ mm to $x = +2.5$ mm, have the heights $9I_0$, $I_0$, $9I_0$, $I_0$, $9I_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson's form gives the same: at $\delta = \pi$, $\sin(3\pi/2)/\sin(\pi/2) = -1$, squared 1; and for $\delta \to 0$ the ratio of the sines tends to 3. The average of $(1 + 2\cos\delta)^2$ over $\delta$ is $1 + 4 \times \tfrac12 = 3$: three slits send three times the energy of one, as they must.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

The top slit is now covered with a material that lets through only half of the intensity of the light, and changes nothing else (not the phase and not the polarization).

<Part label="f" points={3}>

What is the largest intensity that a fringe can have now, in units of $I_0$? Which of the five fringes of part (d) have it?

<Hint>

Half the intensity is not half the field. By which factor does the field of the top slit drop?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The largest intensity is found where all three fields are in step. Add the three fields first, then square.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-f" label="largest intensity $=$ (in units of $I_0$)" value="7.33" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-f" />

<Answer id="p2-f-which" label="fringes with this intensity:" choices={[
  "Only the central fringe",
  "The central fringe and the second fringe on each side (x = 0 and ±2.5 mm)",
  "The first fringe on each side (x = ±1.25 mm)",
  "All five fringes"
]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The intensity goes with the square of the field, so half the intensity means a field that is smaller by the factor $1/\sqrt2$. Fields add, not intensities.

<Step title="The three fields in step">

Call the field of one open slit $E_0$, so that $I_0$ goes with $E_0^2$. The covered slit sends $E_0/\sqrt2$. Where the three are in step:

<Formula label="Largest intensity with one slit half covered" tex="\langle I\rangle = \left(1 + 1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\right)^2 I_0 = \left(\frac92 + 2\sqrt2\right) I_0 = 7.33\,I_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 10.2(f), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Where">

The cover does not change any phase, so the three fields are in step where they were before: at $\delta = 0$ and $\delta = \pm2\pi$. These are the central fringe and the second fringe on each side, at $x = 0$ and $x = \pm2.5$ mm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The value lies between the $4I_0$ of two slits and the $9I_0$ of three, as it should for a slit that is partly open. Adding intensities would give $2.5\,I_0$, and halving the field of the top slit would give $2.5^2 I_0 = 6.25\,I_0$: both are wrong.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Step title="The path difference at an angle">

For a ray that makes the angle $\theta$ with the normal inside the film, the condition for a maximum becomes

<Formula label="Bright wavelength at an angle" tex="2nd\cos\theta = \left(m - \tfrac12\right)\lambda \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda = \frac{4nd\cos\theta}{2m - 1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 10.4(c), p. 3 (MIT's reminder that the path difference goes with the cosine; the formula is our own solution, and for θ = 0 it is the brightest wavelength of Lecture 20 notes, p. 4)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The colour">

On the flat lake the observer looks along the normal: $\cos\theta = 1$, and $\lambda = 530$ nm, green. When a slope of the wave passes, the surface is tilted and $\cos\theta$ is smaller than 1, whichever way the slope leans. With $d$ and $m$ unchanged, the bright wavelength becomes shorter: the colour moves towards blue. On the crest and in the trough the surface is level again and the colour is back at green. So the colour swings between green and bluer shades, and never towards red.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A number of our own: light that arrives at $30^\circ$ to the normal in air is bent to $\sin\theta = 0.5/1.45$ in the oil, so $\cos\theta = 0.939$, and the bright wavelength drops from 530 nm to 497 nm, a blue-green. This is why an oil film or a soap bubble changes colour when you look at it from the side. (MIT's sheet has the flat film seen at nearly normal incidence, which is what makes the cosine largest there.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

The light falls on a far screen. The direction of a point on that screen makes the angle $\psi$ with the normal to the slits. The graph below shows the intensity on the far screen against $\sin\psi$. <Solution>

**The idea.** The fringes come from the two slits together and give the distance between their centres. The envelope comes from each slit alone and gives its width. A bright fringe that is missing marks a zero of the envelope.

<Step title="The pattern of two wide slits">

<Formula label="Two slits of finite width" tex="I = 4I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2\cos^2\frac{\delta}{2}, \qquad \beta = \frac{\pi a}{\lambda}\sin\psi, \qquad \delta = \frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\sin\psi" cite="reading3" at="p. 6 (with N = 2, where the multi-slit factor is 4 cos²(δ/2); the notes write D for the slit width and θ for the angle)" />

Here $I_0$ is the intensity that one slit alone sends straight ahead; on the scale of the graph $4I_0$ is 4. The cosine is zero at $\sin\psi = \left(m + \tfrac12\right)\lambda/d$ and largest at $\sin\psi = m\lambda/d$. The envelope is zero at $\sin\psi = m\lambda/a$ with $m \ne 0$ (Lecture 22 notes, p. 5).

</Step>

<Step title="The distance between the centres">

The first zero next to the central peak is at $\sin\psi = 0.1$. It is the first zero of the cosine:

<Formula label="Centre-to-centre distance" tex="\frac{\lambda}{2d} = 0.1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d = 5\lambda" cite="mit" at="Problem 10.5(a), p. 4 (our own solution, read from the graph)" />

The bright fringes should then be at $\sin\psi = 0, \pm0.2, \pm0.4, \pm0.6, \dots$ and the zeros of the cosine at $\pm0.1, \pm0.3, \pm0.5, \pm0.7$. The graph shows those zeros, and fringes near $\pm0.2$ and at $\pm0.6$.

</Step>

<Step title="The width of the slits">

The fringe at $\sin\psi = \pm0.4$ is missing: there the graph touches zero, with only very small bumps on either side. So the first zero of the envelope is at 0.4:

<Formula label="Slit width and the strip between the slits" tex="\frac{\lambda}{a} = 0.4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = 2.5\lambda, \qquad b = d - a = 2.5\lambda" cite="mit" at="Problem 10.5(a), p. 4 (our own solution, read from the graph)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The heights fit. At $\sin\psi = 0.2$, $\beta = \pi/2$ and the envelope is $(2/\pi)^2 = 0.405$ of the central value 4, which is 1.62; the fringe in the graph peaks a little higher and a little closer to the centre, because the envelope falls across the fringe. At $\sin\psi = 0.6$, $\beta = 3\pi/2$ and the envelope gives $4/(3\pi/2)^2 = 0.18$, the height of the low fringe. If $b$ were read as the distance between the centres of the slits, the answer would be $b = 5\lambda$; with the reading of Figure 4 used here it is the strip, $2.5\lambda$. An estimate within a tenth of these values is accepted.

</Step>
