## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Radio waves in the ionosphere" points={20} mit="Problem 9.1">

The ionosphere is a layer of ionised gas high in the atmosphere. For an electromagnetic wave of angular frequency $\omega$ it can be treated as a dielectric with the refractive index

<Formula label="Refractive index of the ionosphere" tex="n = \sqrt{1 - \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 9.1, p. 1" />

where the plasma frequency $\omega_p$ is taken to be a constant.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Plot the size $k$ of the wave vector against the frequency $\omega$ for an electromagnetic wave that travels through the ionosphere. For the box: give $k$ for $\omega > \omega_p$, in terms of $\omega$, $\omega_p$ and $c$.

<Hint>

In a medium of refractive index $n$ a wave of one frequency travels at the phase velocity $c/n$. How is the phase velocity related to $\omega$ and $k$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put the given $n$ into $k = n\omega/c$ and take $\omega$ inside the square root.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-k" label="$k =$" expr="sqrt(w^2 - wp^2)/c"
  vars={{ w: [2, 4], wp: [0.5, 1.5], c: [2, 5] }}
  names={{ w: "\\omega", wp: "\\omega_p", c: "c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "w/c", hint: "That is the wave number in vacuum. In the ionosphere the phase velocity is c/n, not c, so the refractive index must appear." },
    { expr: "sqrt(w^2 + wp^2)/c", hint: "Check the sign under the root: n² is 1 − ωp²/ω², so k vanishes at ω = ωp." },
    { expr: "w/(c*sqrt(1 - wp^2/w^2))", hint: "This divides by n. The phase velocity is ω/k = c/n, so k is n ω/c: n times the vacuum value, and here n is smaller than 1." },
    { expr: "sqrt(1 - wp^2/w^2)", hint: "That is n itself, a pure number. A wave number is one over a length: k = n ω/c." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The refractive index fixes the phase velocity, and the phase velocity is $\omega/k$. That gives $k$ as a function of $\omega$, which is the dispersion relation of the ionosphere.

<Step title="From n to k">

<Formula label="Wave number in a medium" tex="\frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{c}{n} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k = \frac{n\,\omega}{c}" cite="reading4" at="p. 3 (ω/k = c/n for a plane wave in matter)" />

With MIT's $n$:

<Formula label="Wave number in the ionosphere" tex="k = \frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{1 - \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2}} = \frac{\sqrt{\omega^2 - \omega_p^2}}{c} \qquad (\omega > \omega_p)" cite="mit" at="Problem 9.1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

In other words $\omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + c^2k^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The plot">

<Plot variable="u" domain={[0, 3]} range={[0, 3.2]} xLabel="ω / ω_p" yLabel="c k / ω_p"
  curves={[
    { expr: "u >= 1 ? sqrt(u^2 - 1) : NaN", label: "ionosphere" },
    { expr: "u", label: "vacuum", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="The size of the wave vector against the frequency, drawn from k = √(ω² − ω_p²)/c (solid) and, for comparison, from the vacuum value k = ω/c (dashed). Both axes are in units of the plasma frequency. Below ω = ω_p there is no real k (part c). The curve starts at k = 0 with a vertical tangent and comes closer and closer to the vacuum line from below." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: a frequency divided by a speed is one over a length. For $\omega \gg \omega_p$ the root tends to 1 and $k \to \omega/c$: a wave of very high frequency does not notice the ionosphere. At $\omega = \omega_p$ the wave number is zero, so the wavelength is infinite: the whole medium swings in step. For every $\omega > \omega_p$ the index is below 1, so $k$ is smaller, and the wavelength longer, than in vacuum.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

A radio wave travels through the ionosphere with the frequency $\omega = \sqrt2\,\omega_p$. Find its phase velocity $v_p$ and its group velocity $v_g$. Give each as a multiple of $c$.

<Hint>

The phase velocity is $\omega/k$ and the group velocity is $d\omega/dk$, both taken at the given frequency.

</Hint>

<Hint>

It is easier to differentiate $\omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + c^2k^2$ as it stands than to solve for $\omega$ first: $2\omega\,d\omega = 2c^2k\,dk$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-vp" label="$v_p/c =$" value="1.414" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-vp" />

<Answer id="p1-vg" label="$v_g/c =$" value="0.707" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-vg" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the graph of $\omega$ against $k$ the phase velocity is the slope of the line from the origin and the group velocity the slope of the tangent. Both follow from $\omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + c^2k^2$.

<Step title="The two velocities at any frequency">

<Formula label="Phase and group velocity" tex="v_p = \frac{\omega}{k}, \qquad v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The first is $c/n$. For the second, differentiate $\omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + c^2k^2$ with respect to $k$: $2\omega\,v_g = 2c^2k$, so

<Formula label="The two velocities in the ionosphere" tex="v_p = \frac{c}{n} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2}}, \qquad v_g = \frac{c^2k}{\omega} = c\,n = c\sqrt{1 - \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 9.1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="At the given frequency">

With $\omega = \sqrt2\,\omega_p$ the index is $n = \sqrt{1 - \tfrac12} = 1/\sqrt2$:

<Formula label="The two velocities at ω = √2 ω_p" tex="v_p = \sqrt2\,c \approx 1.414\,c, \qquad v_g = \frac{c}{\sqrt2} \approx 0.707\,c" cite="mit" at="Problem 9.1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The product is $v_pv_g = c^2$, as it is at every frequency above $\omega_p$ in this medium. The phase velocity is larger than $c$. That breaks no rule: as the lesson on dispersion explained, a phase velocity carries no information. A pulse, and with it the signal and the energy, travels at the group velocity, which is below $c$. The curve of $\omega$ against $k$ bends towards the line $\omega = ck$ from above, so its tangent is less steep than the line from the origin: $v_g < v_p$, the case in which the carrier runs forward through the envelope.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

What happens to an electromagnetic disturbance in the ionosphere when $\omega < \omega_p$?

<Hint>

What is the sign of $n^2$, and so of $k^2$, below the plasma frequency? What kind of function of $z$ has a second derivative that is a positive number times the function itself?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" choices={[
  "It travels on with its shape unchanged, at a speed below c",
  "It travels backwards, because the group velocity becomes negative",
  "It does not travel: the field oscillates in step at every depth and its size falls off exponentially with depth, so a wave that arrives from outside is reflected",
  "It travels on at the speed c, as in vacuum, because the ionosphere no longer responds"
]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Below the plasma frequency $n^2$ is negative. Then $k^2$ is negative too, and the cosine in space turns into an exponential.

<Step title="The wave number becomes imaginary">

For $\omega < \omega_p$,

<Formula label="Below the plasma frequency" tex="k^2 = \frac{\omega^2 - \omega_p^2}{c^2} < 0, \qquad k = \pm i\kappa, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{\omega_p^2 - \omega^2}}{c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 9.1(c), p. 1 (our own solution; κ is our name for the size of the imaginary wave number)" />

For a field along $x$ that depends on $z$ and $t$, the wave equation of the medium (Problem 3c, with $n^2$ in place of $\kappa_e$) reads $\partial^2E_x/\partial z^2 = (n^2/c^2)\,\partial^2E_x/\partial t^2$. With the time dependence $\cos(\omega t)$ the right-hand side is $-(n^2\omega^2/c^2)E_x = +\kappa^2E_x$, and the solutions are $\exp(-\kappa z)$ and $\exp(+\kappa z)$:

<Formula label="The field below the plasma frequency" tex="E_x(z, t) = E_0\,\exp(-\kappa z)\cos(\omega t)" cite="mit" at="Problem 9.1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

for a disturbance that is made at $z = 0$ and reaches into a thick layer at $z > 0$ (the growing solution $\exp(+\kappa z)$ is ruled out there).

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

Nothing travels. Every point swings with the same phase, and the size of the field falls off with depth, over the distance $1/\kappa$ (the penetration depth). With Faraday's law the magnetic field of this solution goes as $\sin(\omega t)$, a quarter period away from the electric field, so the Poynting vector averages to zero over a period: no energy is carried into the layer. A wave that arrives from below can only be sent back, so the ionosphere acts as a mirror for it. This is how radio signals of low enough frequency reach receivers beyond the horizon, while waves above the plasma frequency pass through into space (a remark of our own, not on MIT's sheet).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\kappa$ is a frequency over a speed, one over a length. At $\omega = \omega_p$ the decay constant $\kappa$ is zero and the solution joins the $k = 0$ of part (a). It grows as the frequency falls and tends to $\omega_p/c$ for $\omega \to 0$, so the penetration depth $1/\kappa$ is never shorter than $c/\omega_p$, and it grows without limit as $\omega$ approaches $\omega_p$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Two polarizers and a quarter-wave plate" points={20} mit="Problem 9.2">

A beam of light travels along the $z$ axis. It is linearly polarized along the $y$ direction and has the intensity $I_0$. Three devices can be put in the beam (MIT's Figure 1 shows each as a disc with its axes marked):

- two perfect polarizers, A and B. Each passes all of the light that is polarized along its easy axis and none of the light polarized perpendicular to it. Their easy axes make the angles $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$ with the $y$ direction;
- a quarter-wave plate C. It absorbs nothing and changes only the phase of the light: the part polarized along its slow axis leaves with its phase delayed by $\pi/2$ compared with the part polarized along its fast axis, which is perpendicular to the slow axis. The fast axis makes the angle $\theta_C$ with the $y$ axis.

The intensity is proportional to the time average of the square of the electric field, $I \propto \langle\vec E^2\rangle$. Every intensity asked for is to be given relative to $I_0$. In the formula boxes the angles are in radians.

First the two polarizers stand in the beam, A before B (MIT's Figure 2).

MIT's sheet has no part (c): the labels below are MIT's.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the intensity $I_a$ of the beam after polarizer A, in terms of $I_0$ and $\theta_A$.

<Hint>

Split the incoming field, which points along $y$, into a part along the easy axis of A and a part perpendicular to it. Which part passes?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The intensity goes with the square of the field.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$I_a =$" expr="I0*cos(tA)^2"
  vars={{ I0: [1, 3], tA: [0.2, 1.3] }}
  names={{ I0: "I_0", tA: "\\theta_A" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "I0*cos(tA)", hint: "That is how the field is reduced. The intensity goes with the square of the field." },
    { expr: "I0*sin(tA)^2", hint: "The angle θ_A is measured from the y direction, which is the direction of the incoming field. For θ_A = 0 all of the light must pass." },
    { expr: "I0/2", hint: "Half passes when the incoming light is unpolarized. This beam is polarized along y, so the result depends on the angle of the easy axis." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A polarizer keeps the part of the field along its easy axis. The intensity follows the square of the field.

<Step title="The field after A">

The incoming field has the amplitude $E_0$ along $y$, and the easy axis is at the angle $\theta_A$ from $y$. The part of the field along the easy axis is $E_0\cos\theta_A$, and the part perpendicular to it is stopped:

<Formula label="Intensity after a polarizer" tex="|\vec E_a| = E_0\cos\theta_A \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I_a = I_0\cos^2\theta_A" cite="reading2" at="p. 5 (the angle there is the angle between the field and the easy axis)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\theta_A = 0$ the easy axis is along the field and all the light passes. For $\theta_A = 90°$ nothing passes. After A the light is polarized along the easy axis of A, which matters for part (b).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the intensity $I_b$ of the beam after both polarizers, in terms of $I_0$, $\theta_A$ and $\theta_B$.

<Hint>

In which direction is the light polarized when it reaches B? The answer is not $y$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the rule of part (a) once more, with the angle between the easy axis of B and the polarization of the light that arrives at B.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$I_b =$" expr="I0*cos(tA)^2*cos(tB - tA)^2"
  vars={{ I0: [1, 3], tA: [0.2, 1.3], tB: [0.3, 1.4] }}
  names={{ I0: "I_0", tA: "\\theta_A", tB: "\\theta_B" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "I0*cos(tA)^2*cos(tB)^2", hint: "After A the light is polarized along the easy axis of A, not along y. What counts at B is the angle between the two easy axes." },
    { expr: "I0*cos(tB)^2", hint: "This leaves out polarizer A. Even when B is along y, the light first loses a part at A and leaves A polarized along its easy axis." },
    { expr: "I0*cos(tB - tA)^2", hint: "That is the loss at B alone. The light has already been reduced at A by the factor of part (a)." },
    { expr: "I0*cos(tA)*cos(tB - tA)", hint: "These are the factors for the field. The intensity goes with the square of the field." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each polarizer projects the field on its easy axis. After A the field lies along the easy axis of A, so B sees light at the angle $\theta_B - \theta_A$ to its own axis.

<Step title="The second projection">

<Formula label="Intensity after both polarizers" tex="|\vec E_b| = E_0\cos\theta_A\cos(\theta_B - \theta_A) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I_b = I_0\cos^2\theta_A\cos^2(\theta_B - \theta_A)" cite="mit" at="Problem 9.2(b), p. 2 (our own solution, with the rule of Lecture 17 notes, p. 5, used twice)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\theta_B = \theta_A$ polarizer B changes nothing and $I_b = I_a$. With B crossed to the incoming light ($\theta_B = 90°$) and A along $y$ or along $x$, nothing passes. But with A at $45°$ between them, $I_b = I_0\cos^2 45°\cos^2 45° = I_0/4$: the middle polarizer turns the polarization and lets light through, as in the lesson's demonstration with three polarizing sheets.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

Now polarizer A is taken out and the quarter-wave plate C is put in its place (MIT's Figure 3), so the beam passes C and then B.

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Find the intensity $I_c$ of the beam after the quarter-wave plate C, in terms of the given quantities.

<Hint>

Split the incoming field into a part along the fast axis and a part along the slow axis. What does the plate do to the size of each part?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="$I_c =$" expr="I0"
  vars={{ I0: [1, 3], tC: [0.2, 1.3] }}
  names={{ I0: "I_0", tC: "\\theta_C" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "I0*cos(tC)^2", hint: "That would be a polarizer with its easy axis along the fast axis. A wave plate stops neither part of the field: it only delays one of them." },
    { expr: "I0/2", hint: "A wave plate absorbs nothing. The factor ½ belongs to a polarizer in unpolarized or circularly polarized light." },
    { expr: "I0*(cos(tC)^4 + sin(tC)^4)", hint: "That is the intensity after a polarizer along y behind the plate (part e). Directly behind the plate both parts of the field are still there in full." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The plate shifts the phase of one part of the field against the other and leaves both sizes alone. The time average of the square of the field does not notice the shift.

<Step title="The two parts of the field">

Along the fast axis the incoming field has the amplitude $E_0\cos\theta_C$ and along the slow axis $E_0\sin\theta_C$. Write $\phi = kz - \omega t$ for the phase of the wave. Behind the plate the slow part is a quarter period behind the fast part:

<Formula label="The field behind the quarter-wave plate" tex="E_{\text{fast}} = E_0\cos\theta_C\cos\phi, \qquad E_{\text{slow}} = E_0\sin\theta_C\cos\left(\phi - \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = E_0\sin\theta_C\sin\phi" cite="reading3" at="pp. 1–2 (the quarter-wave plate: a phase difference of π/2 between the fast and the slow axis)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The intensity">

The two parts are perpendicular, so their squares add, and $\cos^2\phi$ and $\sin^2\phi$ both average to $\tfrac12$:

<Formula label="Intensity behind the plate" tex="\left\langle\vec E^2\right\rangle = \tfrac12E_0^2\cos^2\theta_C + \tfrac12E_0^2\sin^2\theta_C = \tfrac12E_0^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I_c = I_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 9.2(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

for every angle $\theta_C$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The intensity is unchanged, but the polarization is not. For $\theta_C = 0$ or $90°$ only one part exists and the light stays linearly polarized along $y$. For $\theta_C = 45°$ the two parts are equal and a quarter period apart: circular polarization. At any other angle the light is elliptically polarized, as the lesson found.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={7}>

In general the plate changes how much light passes polarizer B behind it. Let the easy axis of B be along the $y$ axis ($\theta_B = 0$). Find the intensity $I_b$ of the beam after polarizer B, in terms of $I_0$ and $\theta_C$.

<Hint>

Polarizer B keeps the $y$ component of the field. Each of the two parts of part (d) has a $y$ component: project each one back on the $y$ direction.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The two $y$ components are a quarter period apart in time, one going as $\cos\phi$ and the other as $\sin\phi$. For such a sum the time average of the square is half the sum of the squares of the two amplitudes: there is no cross term.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-e" label="$I_b =$" expr="I0*(cos(tC)^4 + sin(tC)^4)"
  vars={{ I0: [1, 3], tC: [0.2, 1.3] }}
  names={{ I0: "I_0", tC: "\\theta_C" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "I0", hint: "The plate absorbs nothing, but it changes the polarization. Unless its axes are along y and x, the light behind it has a part perpendicular to y, and B stops that part." },
    { expr: "I0*cos(tC)^2", hint: "This treats the plate as a polarizer along its fast axis, followed by B. The slow part of the field is not stopped: it also sends a component through B." },
    { expr: "I0*cos(tC)^4", hint: "This is the fast part alone, projected twice. The slow part passes the plate too and has a y component of amplitude E₀ sin²θ_C." },
    { expr: "I0/2", hint: "That holds only for θ_C = 45°, where the light behind the plate is circularly polarized. With the fast axis along y, for example, the light is unchanged and all of it passes B." },
    { expr: "I0*(cos(tC)^2 - sin(tC)^2)^2", hint: "This adds the two y components as if they were half a period apart, which is what a half-wave plate does. Here they are a quarter period apart, so their squares add." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Go from $y$ to the axes of the plate, apply the delay, and come back to $y$. Each part of the field is projected twice, so the fast part comes back with $\cos^2\theta_C$ and the slow part with $\sin^2\theta_C$, a quarter period apart.

<Step title="The y component behind the plate">

The fast axis makes the angle $\theta_C$ with $y$, so the fast part of part (d) has the $y$ component $E_{\text{fast}}\cos\theta_C$. The slow axis is perpendicular to it, and the slow part has a $y$ component of size $E_{\text{slow}}\sin\theta_C$. The sign is plus: whichever way the slow axis is drawn, the same direction was used to take the slow part of the incoming field, so the two signs multiply to $+1$. Polarizer B passes

<Formula label="The field after polarizer B" tex="E_y = E_0\left[\cos^2\theta_C\cos\phi + \sin^2\theta_C\sin\phi\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 9.2(e), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The intensity">

Over a period $\langle\cos^2\phi\rangle = \langle\sin^2\phi\rangle = \tfrac12$ and $\langle\cos\phi\sin\phi\rangle = 0$:

<Formula label="Intensity after the plate and polarizer B" tex="\left\langle E_y^2\right\rangle = \tfrac12E_0^2\left[\cos^4\theta_C + \sin^4\theta_C\right] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I_b = I_0\left[\cos^4\theta_C + \sin^4\theta_C\right] = I_0\left[1 - \tfrac12\sin^2(2\theta_C)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 9.2(e), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only the acceleration radiates, and the steady drift along $z$ has none. The acceleration vector runs round a circle in the $x$–$y$ plane. Each observer sees the shadow of that circle on the plane perpendicular to the line of sight: a circle, an ellipse or a line.

<Step title="The acceleration">

The term $\hat z\,\gamma t$ has no second derivative. The size of $\vec a$ is the constant $\alpha\beta^2$, and its direction turns with the angular frequency $\beta$.

</Step>

<Step title="The radiation field">

<Formula label="Radiation field of an accelerated charge" tex="\vec E_{\text{rad}}(\vec r, t) = \frac{-q\,\vec a_\perp(t - r/c)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2\,r}, \qquad \vec a_\perp = \vec a - (\vec a\cdot\hat r)\,\hat r, \qquad \vec B_{\text{rad}} = \frac1c\,\hat r\times\vec E_{\text{rad}}" cite="reading3" at="p. 5" />

The field at the astronomer follows the acceleration, so it swings with the angular frequency $\beta$ at every position, and it travels outwards along $\hat r$ at the speed $c$:

<Formula label="Frequency and wave vector" tex="\nu = \frac{\beta}{2\pi}, \qquad \vec k = \frac{\beta}{c}\,\hat r" cite="mit" at="Problem 9.4(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The three positions">

The acceleration is taken at the retarded time $t' = t - R/c$.

(i) $\hat r = \hat z$, so $\vec k = (\beta/c)\,\hat z$. The acceleration is already perpendicular to $\hat z$: $\vec a_\perp = \vec a$. Its two components are equal in size and a quarter period apart, so the light is **circularly polarized**. The field vector turns the same way as the electron: counter-clockwise for the astronomer, who looks back at the electron with $\hat x$ to the right and $\hat y$ up.

(ii) $\hat r = (\hat x + \hat z)/\sqrt2$, so $\vec k = (\beta/c)(\hat x + \hat z)/\sqrt2$. Here $\vec a\cdot\hat r = -\alpha\beta^2\cos(\beta t')/\sqrt2$, and

<Formula label="Perpendicular acceleration at position (ii)" tex="\vec a_\perp = -\alpha\beta^2\left[\frac{\cos(\beta t')}{2}\,\hat x + \sin(\beta t')\,\hat y - \frac{\cos(\beta t')}{2}\,\hat z\right] = -\alpha\beta^2\left[\frac{\cos(\beta t')}{\sqrt2}\,\frac{\hat x - \hat z}{\sqrt2} + \sin(\beta t')\,\hat y\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 9.4(a)(ii), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The two directions $(\hat x - \hat z)/\sqrt2$ and $\hat y$ are unit vectors perpendicular to $\hat r$ and to each other. The components along them are a quarter period apart and have the amplitudes $\alpha\beta^2/\sqrt2$ and $\alpha\beta^2$: the light is **elliptically polarized**, with the long axis along $\hat y$ and the short axis, $1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.707$ times as long, in the $x$–$z$ plane.

(iii) $\hat r = \hat x$, so $\vec k = (\beta/c)\,\hat x$. Now $\vec a_\perp = -\alpha\beta^2\sin(\beta t')\,\hat y$: only one component is left, and the light is **linearly polarized** along $\hat y$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

<Part label="c" points={7}>

Explain the direction of polarization of the reflected light from the acceleration of the charges in the glass. Why is the reflected light polarized parallel to the surface?

This part has no answer box: write your explanation in a few sentences, then open the solution and mark yourself.

<Hint>

The reflected wave is not light that bounces off the surface like a ball. It is radiated by the electrons in the glass, which the wave inside the glass shakes. Along which line do they move?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In which direction does an accelerated charge send no radiation? Compare that direction with the direction of the reflected ray at Brewster's angle.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The reflected light is radiated by the charges in the glass, and a charge does not radiate along the line of its own acceleration. At Brewster's angle the reflected ray lies exactly along that line for one of the two polarizations.

<Step title="The charges in the glass">

The electrons in a dielectric are bound to their atoms, and the electric field of the refracted wave shakes them back and forth along its own direction. These accelerated charges radiate. Their radiation, added up over the glass, makes the reflected wave in the air (and, with the incoming wave, the refracted wave in the glass).

</Step>

<Step title="The radiation of each polarization">

A far observer in the direction $\hat r$ receives a field in proportion to $\vec a_\perp = \vec a - (\vec a\cdot\hat r)\,\hat r$ (Lecture 18 notes, p. 5): nothing is radiated along the line of the acceleration.

- **Field in the plane of incidence.** Inside the glass this field is perpendicular to the refracted ray and lies in the plane of incidence. At Brewster's angle the reflected ray is perpendicular to the refracted ray and lies in the same plane, so it points exactly along the line on which the charges move. For an observer in that direction $\vec a_\perp = 0$: these charges send nothing into the reflected ray.
- **Field perpendicular to the plane of incidence.** The charges move along the normal to the plane of incidence. Every ray in the plane, the reflected one included, is perpendicular to that line, so $\vec a_\perp = \vec a$ and the charges radiate fully into the reflected direction.

</Step>

<Step title="The polarization of the reflected light">

So the reflected light comes only from charges that move perpendicular to the plane of incidence, and its field points along their motion. A direction perpendicular to the plane of incidence is perpendicular to the normal of the surface, which lies in that plane. So it is parallel to the surface: the reflected light is polarized parallel to the interface, for any polarization of the incoming light.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This agrees with part (a) and with the boundary conditions of the lesson, where the in-plane reflected amplitude vanishes at $\alpha = \beta$. The picture also explains why the right angle between the two rays defines Brewster's angle: it is the geometry in which the reflected direction lines up with the motion of the charges. It is the same rule as for the lesson's antenna, which sends nothing off its own end. A polarizer with its easy axis in the plane of incidence then removes the reflected light completely, which is the photographer's trick against glare.

</Step>
