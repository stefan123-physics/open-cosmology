The last lecture found the normal modes of an infinite chain from [translation symmetry](/courses/8-03/symmetry-infinite-oscillators). This one applies the same idea to beads on a string, makes the string finite by fixing two beads, and finally lets the beads merge into a continuous string: the result is the **wave equation** (transcript, pp. 1, 15).

## Translation symmetry, once more

The shift matrix moves each amplitude one place, $A'_j = A_{j+1}$; an eigenvector with $SA = \beta A$ has $A_j = \beta^jA_0$ (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 1–2). With $|\beta| \neq 1$ the amplitudes grow exponentially along the chain (in the lecture also called a power law: $\beta^j$ is geometric growth in $j$), so for oscillations take (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2)

<Formula label="Physical eigenvectors" tex="\beta = e^{ika}, \qquad A_j \propto e^{ijka}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Multiplying by $\beta$ then turns the complex amplitude round the unit circle; its projection on the real axis is a sine or a cosine (transcript, p. 2). Why write $ka$ rather than $\theta$? Because $a$ is the spacing, so $ja$ is the position $x$ of mass $j$, and $\cos(jka) = \cos kx$: $k$ is the **wave number** (pp. 2–3, 10). Any system with this symmetry and $[S, M^{-1}K] = 0$ has these eigenvectors; only $\omega$ remains, from one multiplication $M^{-1}KA$ (p. 3).

## Beads on a string

Point masses $m$, spaced $a$ apart, are joined by a massless string under constant tension $T$, from one edge of the universe to the other (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 4). Each mass moves only up and down, by a small $y_j \ll a$, so the angles $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$ of the string on either side of mass $j$ are small (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 4–5). The two tensions give (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 5)

<Formula label="Forces on bead j" tex="m\ddot x_j = -T\cos\theta_1 + T\cos\theta_2, \qquad m\ddot y_j = -T\sin\theta_1 - T\sin\theta_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

With $\cos\theta \approx 1$ the horizontal forces cancel: no horizontal motion (p. 5). With $\sin\theta_1 \approx (y_j - y_{j-1})/a$ and $\sin\theta_2 \approx (y_j - y_{j+1})/a$ (p. 6):

<Formula label="Equation of motion" tex="m\ddot y_j = \frac{T}{a}\left(y_{j-1} - 2y_j + y_{j+1}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

(A student corrects a sign on the board; p. 7.) The matrix $M^{-1}K$ has $2T/ma$ on the diagonal and $-T/ma$ beside it, the same pattern as the masses and springs (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 7).

## The dispersion relation

Put $A_j = e^{ijka}$ into row $j$ of $M^{-1}KA = \omega^2A$ and cancel $A_j$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="Dispersion relation of the beaded string" tex="\omega^2 = \frac{T}{ma}\left(2 - e^{ika} - e^{-ika}\right) = 2\omega_0^2(1 - \cos ka) = 4\omega_0^2\sin^2\frac{ka}{2}, \qquad \omega_0^2 = \frac{T}{ma}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Almost the same as for the masses and springs (notes, p. 3): comparing the two, $T/a$ plays the part of the spring constant. Fix the wave number, or the wavelength $2\pi/k$, and the frequency is fixed: $\omega = \omega(k)$, the **dispersion relation**, whose name will make sense in later lectures (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 8–9). The normal modes are standing waves; for an infinite string every $k$ is allowed (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 9).

## A finite string with fixed ends

To use the infinite solution for a finite string of $N$ beads tied to two walls, keep the infinite string but nail down bead 0 and bead $N + 1$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 10):

<Formula label="Fixed ends" tex="y_0 = 0, \qquad y_{N+1} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Since $\omega(k) = \omega(-k)$, any combination of $e^{ijka}$ and $e^{-ijka}$ is also a mode (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 11):

<Formula label="Trial solution" tex="y_j = \text{Re}\left[e^{i(\omega t + \phi)}\left(\alpha e^{ijka} + \beta e^{-ijka}\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

$y_0 = 0$ at all times needs $\alpha + \beta = 0$, so the shape is a sine. Then $y_{N+1} = 0$ needs $2i\alpha\sin((N + 1)ka) = 0$; $\alpha = 0$ would mean no motion at all, so (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 11–12)

<Formula label="Allowed wave numbers" tex="ka = \frac{n\pi}{N + 1}, \qquad n = 1, 2, \dots, N" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

The boundary conditions do two things: they fix the shape, a sine, and they make the allowed $k$ discrete, leaving exactly $N$ normal modes (transcript, p. 12). For $N = 4$ the first mode is one long arch and higher $n$ bring more wiggles, every bead still moving only up and down (p. 12). (The notes say $n$ is less than $N$; the lecture's $n = 1, \dots, N$ is right, $n = N$ included.) The notes also work out open ends ($y_1 = y_0$, $y_{N+1} = y_N$, giving $ka = n\pi/N$ and shapes $\cos(ka(j - \frac12))$) and a string driven at one end, whose amplitude $\sin(jk_da)/\sin((N + 1)k_da)$ blows up when the driving frequency matches a normal mode; there was no time for these in the lecture (notes, pp. 4–6; transcript, p. 12).

The method in five steps (notes, p. 6; transcript, pp. 12–13): symmetry and finite amplitudes give $\beta = e^{ika}$; the equation of motion comes from the forces; the two give $\omega(k)$; boundary conditions select the allowed $k$, and the general motion is a sum of the normal modes; initial conditions fix the rest.

## The continuum limit and the wave equation

Now make the string continuous (notes, p. 6; transcript, p. 13). Write the amplitude as a function of position, $A(x)$ with $x = ja$; row $j$ reads

<Formula label="Row j as a function of x" tex="\omega^2A(x) = \frac{T}{ma}\left[-A(x - a) + 2A(x) - A(x + a)\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Taylor-expand $A(x \pm a) = A(x) \pm aA'(x) + \frac12a^2A''(x) + \cdots$: the $A(x)$ and $A'(x)$ terms cancel (Lee corrects a sign here after a student's remark), leaving $-a^2A''(x)$ plus higher-order terms (the notes write order $a^3$; the odd powers cancel, so the first correction is of order $a^4$), negligible when $a$ is much smaller than the wavelength (notes, p. 6; transcript, pp. 13–14). Let $a \to 0$, but keep the **linear mass density** fixed, so the string does not become infinitely heavy (transcript, p. 14):

<Formula label="Linear mass density" tex="\rho_L = \frac{m}{a}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Then $M^{-1}K$ becomes the operator $-\frac{T}{\rho_L}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}$, and $\ddot y = -M^{-1}Ky$ becomes (notes, p. 6; transcript, pp. 14–15)

<Formula label="Wave equation" tex="\frac{\partial^2y(x, t)}{\partial t^2} = v_p^2\,\frac{\partial^2y(x, t)}{\partial x^2}, \qquad v_p = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho_L}}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 6–7" />

Putting in $e^{ikx}e^{i\omega t}$ gives the continuous dispersion relation $\omega^2 = (T/\rho_L)k^2$, so $\omega = v_pk$, where $v_p$ is the **phase velocity** (notes, p. 7). Lee calls this "probably the most important equation" learned so far, more important than $F = ma$: without it there would be no sound carrying the lecture to the listeners and, as a later lecture shows for light, nothing to see (transcript, p. 15). In a demonstration, a chain of 72 coupled oscillators, "as a physicist, that's actually equal to an infinite number", shows a pulse running back and forth until friction damps it and, driven steadily, standing waves (pp. 15–16).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Standing waves and the first light">
The fixed-end condition that turned a few beads into $N$ standing waves is the same counting that 8.286 uses for radiation in a box: only whole numbers of half wavelengths fit, each fitting wave is one mode, and giving each mode its quantum share of thermal energy yields the [black-body spectrum](/courses/8-286/black-body-radiation) of the early universe's radiation, which survives as the cosmic microwave background.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="A short beaded string of your own">
<Problem>
(a) Three beads ($N = 3$) on a string are fixed at both ends. Find the normal-mode frequencies in units of $\omega_0$ and the mode shapes. (b) A continuous string has $T = 50$ N and $\rho_L = 0.020$ kg/m. Find $v_p$ and the frequency of a wave of length $0.50$ m.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Frequencies">
$ka = n\pi/4$ for $n = 1, 2, 3$, so $\omega_n = 2\omega_0\sin(n\pi/8)$: $\omega_1 \approx 0.765\,\omega_0$, $\omega_2 = \sqrt2\,\omega_0 \approx 1.414\,\omega_0$, $\omega_3 \approx 1.848\,\omega_0$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Shapes">
$A_j = \sin(jn\pi/4)$ for $j = 1, 2, 3$. Mode 1: $(0.707, 1, 0.707)$, one arch. Mode 2: $(1, 0, -1)$, the middle bead still. Mode 3: $(0.707, -1, 0.707)$, neighbours opposite.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Continuous string">
$v_p = \sqrt{50/0.020} = \sqrt{2500} = 50$ m/s. With $k = 2\pi/0.50 \approx 12.6$ m⁻¹, $\omega = v_pk \approx 628$ rad/s, a frequency of $\omega/2\pi = 100$ Hz.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Translation symmetry: $A_j \propto e^{ijka}$, a wave with wave number $k$ ($x = ja$).
- Beaded string: $m\ddot y_j = \frac Ta(y_{j-1} - 2y_j + y_{j+1})$; $\omega^2 = 4\omega_0^2\sin^2\frac{ka}{2}$, $\omega_0^2 = T/ma$.
- Fixed ends: sine-shaped standing waves with $ka = n\pi/(N + 1)$, $n = 1, \dots, N$.
- Continuum limit ($a \to 0$, $\rho_L$ fixed): $\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial t^2} = v_p^2\frac{\partial^2y}{\partial x^2}$ with $v_p = \sqrt{T/\rho_L}$, and $\omega = v_pk$.
