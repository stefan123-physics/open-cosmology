The [last lecture](/courses/8-03/polarization-polarizers) made linearly polarized light with polarizers. This one makes circularly polarized light, then asks the question behind every electromagnetic wave so far: how is one produced at all (transcript, p. 1)?

## Wave plates

A **wave plate** is a sheet of material whose refractive index differs for light polarized along $x$ and along $y$: as for the bead arrays of [two-dimensional waves](/courses/8-03/two-dimensional-waves-snells-law), the dispersion relation can depend on direction, and the material can be engineered so (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 2–3 and 6–7). For light of angular frequency $\omega$ and a plate of thickness $l$, the wave numbers inside are (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 3–4)

<Formula label="Phase difference across a wave plate" tex="k_x = \frac{n_x}{c}\,\omega = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_x}, \quad k_y = \frac{n_y}{c}\,\omega = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_y}; \qquad \Delta\phi = \frac{2\pi l}{\lambda_x} - \frac{2\pi l}{\lambda_y} = \frac{n_x - n_y}{c}\,\omega l" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

so the two components leave with an extra phase difference $\Delta\phi$. The axis with the smaller phase is the **fast axis**, the other the **slow axis** (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 5). A **quarter-wave plate** has $\Delta\phi = \pi/2$. With the fast axis along $x$ (notes, pp. 1–2; transcript, pp. 4–6):

1. Light polarized along $x$ has no $y$ component to delay: it stays linear. (A student first guesses it turns to $y$.)
2. Light at $45°$ has equal components; delaying one by a quarter period gives **circular polarization**.
3. Light at another angle, say $30°$, gives **elliptical polarization**.

As a matrix (notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Quarter-wave plate matrices" tex="Q_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & i \end{pmatrix}, \qquad Q_\theta = \begin{pmatrix} \cos^2\theta + i\sin^2\theta & \cos\theta\sin\theta - i\sin\theta\cos\theta \\ \cos\theta\sin\theta - i\sin\theta\cos\theta & \sin^2\theta + i\cos^2\theta \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

with $\theta$ the angle of the fast axis from $x$.

**Demonstrations** (transcript, pp. 7–10, 21–22). On a projector, crossed polarizers go dark; a third polarizer at $45°$ between them lets some light through, and a quarter-wave plate in its place lets through noticeably more. Working out how much is left to the problem set; a separate recording shows both maxima at $45°$ (pp. 7–8, 21–22). Then a tube of supersaturated sugar water, lit with polarized light (pp. 8–10). Linearly polarized light is a sum of two circularly polarized waves turning opposite ways; sugar molecules differ from their mirror images (they are **chiral**), so the two circular components have different refractive indices and the polarization rotates along the tube. Since the indices also depend on frequency, each colour rotates by a different amount, so the colour changes along the tube; turning the final polarizer picks out different colours on the wall, and turning the first polarizer changes the colours of the whole tube, "a physics barber shop" (pp. 9–10).

## Making electromagnetic waves

Light from galaxies seen by the Hubble telescope has travelled billions of light years (transcript, p. 10). For a source to send power that far, the power through any sphere around it, the average Poynting flux times the area, must be the same for every sphere, with the [Poynting vector](/courses/8-03/electromagnetic-waves) $\vec S = \vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0$ (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 10–11). So $\langle S\rangle \propto 1/A \propto 1/r^2$, and **$E$ and $B$ must fall off as $1/r$**. Which charges manage that?

- **A charge at rest**: $E = q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2 \propto 1/r^2$ and $B = 0$, so $S = 0$ (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 11–12).
- **A charge moving at constant speed $u$**, with $\beta = u/c$ and $\theta$ the angle between $\hat r$ and $\vec u$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 12; Lee quotes the result without derivation):

<Formula label="Field of a uniformly moving charge" tex="\vec E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\,\frac{1 - \beta^2}{(1 - \beta^2\sin^2\theta)^{3/2}}\,\hat r, \qquad \vec B = \frac{\vec u\times\vec E}{c^2} \propto \frac{1}{r^2}, \qquad \vec S \propto \frac{1}{r^4}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

  The field lines crowd towards the plane perpendicular to the motion, but $S \propto 1/r^4$: no power reaches large distances. (More simply, in the charge's rest frame it is the first case.)

So the charge must be **accelerated**. Full derivations are in Georgi's textbook, pp. 355–360 (notes, p. 3; Lee says pp. 356–360, transcript, p. 13); the lecture gives a geometric argument instead.

## The kink

A charge at rest at A is accelerated at $a$ from $t = 0$ to $t = \Delta t$, reaching A′ with speed $u = a\Delta t \ll c$, and then coasts, reaching B at time $t$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 13–15). News of the change travels at $c$:

- outside the sphere of radius $ct$ the field is still that of a charge at rest at A;
- inside the sphere of radius $c(t - \Delta t)$ it is that of the charge moving uniformly, pointing from B;
- in the thin shell between, the field lines must join up, since there is no charge there.

The two straight sets of field lines, nearly parallel because $u \ll c$, are joined by a **kink**: a stretch of field with a component perpendicular to the direction it travels, an electromagnetic wave (transcript, pp. 14–15).

**Its size** (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 15–17). Zooming in, the shell has thickness $c\Delta t$, and the field line inside is displaced sideways by $u_\perp t$, the part of the charge's displacement perpendicular to the line of sight. By similar triangles, with $u_\perp = a_\perp\Delta t$ and $r = ct$:

<Formula label="Shape of the kink" tex="\frac{E_\perp}{E_\parallel} = \frac{-u_\perp t}{c\Delta t} = \frac{-a_\perp\Delta t\,t}{c\Delta t} = \frac{-a_\perp t}{c} = \frac{-a_\perp r}{c^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

the minus sign because the kink points against the acceleration (transcript, p. 17). **Gauss's law** fixes $E_\parallel$: a small pillbox across the outer surface encloses no charge; the transverse parts enter and leave through its sides and cancel, so $E_\parallel$ equals the static field outside, $q/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$ (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 18–19). Hence

<Formula label="Transverse field of the kink" tex="E_\parallel = E_{\text{out}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}, \qquad E_\perp = \frac{-q\,a_\perp}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r\,c^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

falling only as $1/r$. (The notes print $r^2$ in this denominator; the lecture and the notes' next page have $r$.) The field at $\vec r$ reflects the acceleration a time $r/c$ earlier, the **retarded time** (notes, p. 5; transcript, p. 19):

<Formula label="Radiation field" tex="\vec E_{\text{rad}}(\vec r, t) = \frac{-q\,\vec a_\perp(t - r/c)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2r}, \qquad \vec B_{\text{rad}}(\vec r, t) = \frac1c\,\hat r\times\vec E_{\text{rad}}(\vec r, t), \qquad \vec S_{\text{rad}} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec E_{\text{rad}}\times\vec B_{\text{rad}} \propto \frac{1}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

with $\vec a_\perp = \vec a - (\vec a\cdot\hat r)\hat r$, for $r$ much larger than the size of the charge's motion, so that the static parts have died away (notes, p. 5). Only the perpendicular acceleration counts, and "we are sending energy to the edge of the universe" (notes, p. 5; transcript, p. 19). The recipe: find $\vec a$, then $\vec a_\perp$ for the chosen $\vec r$, then $\vec E_{\text{rad}}$, $\vec B_{\text{rad}}$ and $\vec S_{\text{rad}}$; integrating $\vec S_{\text{rad}}$ over a sphere gives the total power, which the notes write as $q^2|a(t - r/c)|^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0c^3$ (notes, p. 5). (Integrating $\sin^2\theta$ over the sphere gives $8\pi/3$, so the total is $q^2a^2/6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3$, the Larmor formula; the notes' $4\pi$ is a slip.)

**Examples from the notes** (notes, pp. 6–7). A charge oscillating along $z$, $\vec x = \hat zd\cos\omega t$, has $\vec a = -\hat zd\omega^2\cos\omega t$.

- Along $z$, $\vec a$ is parallel to $\hat r$, so $\vec a_\perp = 0$: no radiation.
- Along $y$, at distance $R$: $\vec E_{\text{rad}} = \frac{qd\omega^2\cos(\omega(t - R/c))}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2R}\,\hat z$ and $\vec B_{\text{rad}} = \frac{qd\omega^2\cos(\omega(t - R/c))}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^3R}\,\hat x$: a harmonic wave, polarized along the motion, falling as $1/R$.
- At $30°$ from the $z$ axis the field is reduced to half, $\sin 30°$, and points along $\frac12\hat z - \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\hat y$. (The notes write $c^3$ in this field and in the next example; it is $c^2$.)

A charge on an ellipse, $x = \sqrt2A\cos\omega t$, $y = A\sin\omega t$, looks linearly polarized to observers on the $x$ and $y$ axes, which see only one component of $\vec a_\perp$, and elliptically polarized to one on the $z$ axis.

**Demonstration** (transcript, pp. 19–21, 22–24). A transmitting antenna drives electrons back and forth horizontally. A receiving rod with a small light bulb lights up only when held parallel to the transmitter, so the radiation is polarized along the motion; it dims with distance, and very close the bulb burns out (pp. 20–21). In a separate recording, the bulb never lights when the receiver is placed off the end of the transmitter, along the line of the charges' motion, and moving away across the room it fades, goes dark at certain points, which the demonstrator calls nodes, and lights again more weakly (pp. 23–24).

<WorkedExample title="A plate and an antenna of your own">
<Problem>
(a) A crystal has $n_x - n_y = 0.0090$. How thick must a quarter-wave plate for light of wavelength $600$ nm (in vacuum) be? (b) An electron is accelerated at $a = 1.0 \times 10^{15}$ m/s². Find the radiated field $1.0$ m away, perpendicular to the acceleration, and $30°$ from its direction.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The plate">
$\Delta\phi = (n_x - n_y)\omega l/c = 2\pi(n_x - n_y)l/\lambda = \pi/2$ gives $l = \lambda/4(n_x - n_y) = 600 \times 10^{-9}/0.036 \approx 1.7 \times 10^{-5}$ m, about 17 micrometres.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The field">
$E = qa/4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2r = (8.99 \times 10^9)(1.60 \times 10^{-19})(1.0 \times 10^{15})/(8.99 \times 10^{16} \times 1.0) \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-11}$ V/m. At $30°$ from the acceleration only $a_\perp = a\sin 30°$ counts: half as much, $8.0 \times 10^{-12}$ V/m.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Light scattered by free electrons">
An accelerated charge radiates, so a free electron shaken by a passing light wave sends light off in new directions. In the hot early universe, free electrons scattered light this way so often that the universe was opaque; only when they combined with nuclei into neutral atoms could light travel freely, and that light is the [cosmic microwave background](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling).
</Callout>

## Summary

- A wave plate delays one polarization: $\Delta\phi = (n_x - n_y)\omega l/c$; a quarter-wave plate turns $45°$ linear light into circular light.
- Radiation needs $E, B \propto 1/r$. Charges at rest or in uniform motion have $1/r^2$ fields and do not radiate.
- A brief acceleration makes a kink in the field lines; similar triangles and Gauss's law give $\vec E_{\text{rad}} = -q\vec a_\perp(t - r/c)/4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2r$.
- An oscillating charge radiates most perpendicular to its motion, nothing along it, with the field polarized along the motion.
