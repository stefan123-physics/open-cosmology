This set practises the lessons on [dispersion, phase velocity and group velocity](/courses/8-03/dispersion-phase-group-velocity), on [the Fourier transform](/courses/8-03/fourier-transform-am-radio) and on [the uncertainty principle](/courses/8-03/uncertainty-principle-2d-waves). The three problems follow MIT's Homework 7, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for its homeworks, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. All three problems count for the score (100 points).

Notation: the phase velocity is $v_p = \omega/k$ and the group velocity is $v_g = d\omega/dk$, as in the lessons. In Problem 1, $T$ is the surface tension of water (a force per unit length, in N/m), $\rho$ its density and $h$ its depth. In Problem 3, $T$ is the tension of a string (a force) and $\rho_L$ its mass per unit length. For the wavelength of Problem 1(a) MIT writes $\lambda$ with the subscript written out in full (critical); this page writes $\lambda_c$. In Problem 3 MIT's sheet points to a textbook equation for the Fourier transform. This site does not use that textbook: the problem states the transform in the form of the Lecture 14 notes.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Waves on the surface of water" points={40} mit="Problem 7.1">

MIT's sheet opens with a remark of Feynman's that the lecture also recalled: water waves are the waves everyone sees first, and yet they make a poor first example, because they have every complication a wave can have. This problem looks at the main one, their dispersion.

Take waves on the surface of water. Two things pull a deformed surface back: gravity, with the acceleration $g$, and the surface tension $T$ of the water (in newtons per metre). The water has the density $\rho$ and, when it is at rest, the depth $h$. If the viscosity is neglected, the phase velocity of a wave with wave number $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ can be shown to be

<Formula label="Phase velocity of water waves" tex="v_p^2(k) = \left(\frac{g}{k} + \frac{Tk}{\rho}\right)\tanh(kh)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.1, p. 1" />

Here $\tanh$ is the hyperbolic tangent. For water $\rho = 1$ g/cm³, which is $1000$ kg/m³. Where a number is asked, take $g = 9.8$ m/s² (MIT only says "on Earth").

In every part, find the angular frequency $\omega(k) = k\,v_p$ first: the group velocity is its derivative.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

The bracket has a gravity term and a surface-tension term. At about what wavelength $\lambda_c$ does surface tension become as important as gravity? Take the wavelength at which the two terms are equal, and give it in terms of $T$, $\rho$ and $g$.

For water at 20 °C, MIT gives $\lambda_c = 2$ cm. What surface tension $T$ does that correspond to, in N/m?

<Hint>

One term of the bracket grows with $k$ and the other falls. Set them equal and solve for $k$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The question asks for a wavelength, not a wave number: $\lambda = 2\pi/k$. For the number, turn your formula round to give $T$, and put $\lambda_c$ in metres and $\rho$ in kg/m³.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-tension" label="$T =$ (N/m)" value="0.0993" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-tension" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Gravity matters most for long waves (its term goes as $1/k$) and surface tension for short ones (its term goes as $k$). The crossover is where the two terms are equal.

<Step title="Equal terms">

<Formula label="The crossover" tex="\frac{g}{k_c} = \frac{Tk_c}{\rho} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k_c = \sqrt{\frac{\rho g}{T}}, \qquad \lambda_c = \frac{2\pi}{k_c} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho g}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The factor $\tanh(kh)$ multiplies both terms, so the depth does not enter.

</Step>

<Step title="The surface tension">

Solve for $T$ and put in $\lambda_c = 0.02$ m:

<Formula label="Surface tension from the crossover" tex="T = \frac{\rho g\lambda_c^2}{4\pi^2} = \frac{1000 \times 9.8 \times (0.02)^2}{4\pi^2}\ \text{N/m} \approx 0.099\ \text{N/m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: (kg/m³)(m/s²)(m²) = kg/s² = N/m. The answer is only as sharp as the question: "comparable" is not an exact condition, and MIT's 2 cm is a round number. (The surface tension of water found in tables is lower, about three quarters of this value: with it the two terms are equal at a slightly shorter wavelength than MIT's 2 cm.) What matters is the scale: waves much longer than a few centimetres are ruled by gravity, and ripples much shorter than that by surface tension. The ratio of the gravity term to the surface-tension term is $\rho g/(Tk^2) = (\lambda/\lambda_c)^2$, so the changeover is quick.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Let $\lambda \gg \lambda_c$, so that surface tension can be left out. Find the phase velocity and the group velocity for shallow water, where the wavelength is much larger than the depth ($\lambda \gg h$), in terms of $g$ and $h$. Are these waves dispersive?

<Hint>

For a small number $u$, $\tanh u \approx u$. Is $kh$ small here?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $\omega = k\,v_p$ and look at how it depends on $k$. The group velocity is the slope $d\omega/dk$.

</Hint>

  ]} />

<Answer id="p1b-vg" label="$v_g =$" expr="sqrt(g*h)"
  vars={{ g: [5, 15], h: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ g: "g", h: "h" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(g*h)/2", hint: "A factor ½ appears when ω goes as the square root of k, which is the deep-water case. Here ω is proportional to k itself." },
    { expr: "g*h", hint: "This is the square of a speed. The slope of ω = k √(gh) is √(gh)." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1b-disp" label="shallow-water waves are:" choices={[
  "Not dispersive: every wavelength has the same speed, and the group velocity equals the phase velocity",
  "Dispersive: longer waves travel faster, and the group velocity is smaller than the phase velocity",
  "Dispersive: shorter waves travel faster, and the group velocity is larger than the phase velocity",
  "Dispersive: longer waves travel faster, and the group velocity is larger than the phase velocity"
]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In shallow water $kh = 2\pi h/\lambda$ is small, and the hyperbolic tangent of a small number is that number. The wave number then drops out of the speed.

<Step title="The phase velocity">

Without the surface-tension term, and with $\tanh(kh) \approx kh$:

<Formula label="Shallow water" tex="v_p^2 \approx \frac{g}{k}\,kh = gh \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_p = \sqrt{gh}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The group velocity">

The dispersion relation is $\omega = k\,v_p = \sqrt{gh}\;k$, a straight line through the origin. With the definitions of the lecture,

<Formula label="Phase and group velocity" tex="v_p = \frac{\omega}{k}, \qquad v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1 (also Lecture 13 notes, p. 3)" />

the slope of the line is the same number: $v_g = \sqrt{gh} = v_p$. The waves are not dispersive: all wavelengths (as long as they stay much longer than $h$ and than $\lambda_c$) travel at one speed, and a pulse keeps its shape, as on the ideal string of the lesson.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: (m/s²)(m) = m²/s². In water 1 m deep the speed is $\sqrt{9.8 \times 1} \approx 3.1$ m/s. The result is the first term of a series: $\tanh u \approx u - u^3/3$ gives $v_p \approx \sqrt{gh}\left[1 - (kh)^2/6\right]$, so a little dispersion comes back when the waves are not long enough, with the longer waves the faster ones.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={10}>

Again let $\lambda \gg \lambda_c$, so that surface tension can be left out. Find the phase velocity and the group velocity for deep water ($\lambda \ll h$), in terms of $g$ and $k$. Are these waves dispersive?

<Hint>

For a large number $u$, $\tanh u$ is very close to 1.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\omega = k\,v_p$ now goes as a power of $k$ that is not 1. Differentiate it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1c-disp" label="deep-water waves are:" choices={[
  "Not dispersive: every wavelength has the same speed, and the group velocity equals the phase velocity",
  "Dispersive: longer waves travel faster, and the group velocity is smaller than the phase velocity",
  "Dispersive: shorter waves travel faster, and the group velocity is larger than the phase velocity",
  "Dispersive: longer waves travel faster, and the group velocity is larger than the phase velocity"
]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In deep water $kh$ is large and $\tanh(kh)$ is 1: the wave does not feel the bottom. What is left of the formula depends on $k$, so the waves are dispersive.

<Step title="The phase velocity">

<Formula label="Deep water" tex="v_p^2 \approx \frac{g}{k} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_p = \sqrt{\frac{g}{k}} = \sqrt{\frac{g\lambda}{2\pi}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The group velocity">

The dispersion relation is $\omega = k\,v_p = \sqrt{gk}$, and its slope is

<Formula label="Group velocity in deep water" tex="v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac12\sqrt{\frac{g}{k}} = \frac12\,v_p" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The waves are dispersive: the phase velocity grows with the wavelength, so long waves outrun short ones, and a group of waves moves at half the speed of its crests. The crests appear at the back of the group, run through it and die out at the front.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: (m/s²)/(1/m) = m²/s². A wave with $\lambda = 10$ m has $v_p \approx 3.9$ m/s and $v_g \approx 2.0$ m/s. The curve $\omega = \sqrt{gk}$ bends down, the case of the lesson in which $v_g < v_p$. "Deep" is reached soon: when the depth is half a wavelength, $\tanh(kh) = \tanh\pi \approx 0.996$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={10}>

Now let the water be deep ($\lambda \ll h$) and the wavelength so short that $\lambda \ll \lambda_c$: surface tension rules and gravity can be left out. Such waves are called capillary waves. Find their phase velocity and their group velocity, in terms of $T$, $\rho$ and $k$. Are capillary waves dispersive?

  "Not dispersive: every wavelength has the same speed, and the group velocity equals the phase velocity",
  "Dispersive: longer waves travel faster, and the group velocity is smaller than the phase velocity",
  "Dispersive: shorter waves travel faster, and the group velocity is larger than the phase velocity",
  "Dispersive: longer waves travel faster, and the group velocity is larger than the phase velocity"

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same steps as in part (c), with the surface-tension term in place of the gravity term. The speed now grows with $k$, so the dispersion has the opposite sense.

<Step title="The phase velocity">

<Formula label="Capillary waves" tex="v_p^2 \approx \frac{Tk}{\rho} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_p = \sqrt{\frac{Tk}{\rho}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi T}{\rho\lambda}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.1(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The group velocity">

The dispersion relation is $\omega = k\,v_p = \sqrt{T/\rho}\;k^{3/2}$, and its slope is

<Formula label="Group velocity of capillary waves" tex="v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac32\sqrt{\frac{Tk}{\rho}} = \frac32\,v_p" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.1(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Capillary waves are dispersive: the shorter the ripple, the faster it travels, and a group moves faster than its crests. The crests appear at the front of the group and fall back through it. The curve $\omega(k)$ bends up, as for the stiff string of the lesson.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: (N/m)(1/m)/(kg/m³) = m²/s². With the $T$ of part (a), ripples of $\lambda = 2$ mm have $v_p \approx 0.56$ m/s and $v_g \approx 0.84$ m/s; the gravity term left out is $(\lambda/\lambda_c)^2 = 0.01$ times the surface-tension term, so 1% of it.

Parts (c) and (d) fit together. In deep water, with both terms,

<Formula label="Deep water with both terms" tex="v_p^2 = \frac{g}{k} + \frac{Tk}{\rho}, \qquad v_g = v_p + k\,\frac{dv_p}{dk}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.1, p. 1 (the formula with tanh(kh) = 1; the second relation follows from ω = k v_p)" />

Long waves are fast and short ripples are fast, so in between the phase velocity has a smallest value. It lies where the two terms are equal, at the $\lambda_c$ of part (a), and there $dv_p/dk = 0$, so $v_g = v_p$. For water, with MIT's $\lambda_c = 2$ cm, that smallest phase velocity is $\sqrt{g\lambda_c/\pi} \approx 0.25$ m/s.

</Step>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A dispersion curve given as a graph" points={30} mit="Problem 7.2">

The curve starts at $\omega_0 = 1 \times 10^4$ s⁻¹ at $k = 0$.

<Part label="a" points={12}>

At about which angular frequency, or frequencies, are the phase velocity and the group velocity equal? There are two: give both, in units of $10^4$ s⁻¹.

<Hint>

On a graph of $\omega$ against $k$, which line has the slope $v_p$ and which line has the slope $v_g$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The two slopes are equal where the straight line from the origin to a point of the curve is also the tangent at that point. Lay a ruler through the origin and turn it until it just touches the curve. It can touch from below and from above.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-low" label="lower $\omega =$ ($10^4$ s⁻¹)" value="1.56" tolerance="15%" calc="p2-low" />

<Answer id="p2-high" label="higher $\omega =$ ($10^4$ s⁻¹)" value="5.82" tolerance="10%" calc="p2-high" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The phase velocity is the slope of the line from the origin to a point of the curve, and the group velocity is the slope of the tangent there (Lecture 13 notes, p. 3). They are equal where a line through the origin touches the curve.

<Step title="Two lines that touch">

A ruler through the origin touches the curve twice. A line of small slope touches the lower bend from below, near $k \approx 2.2$ per metre. A steeper line touches the upper bend from above, near $k \approx 4.3$ per metre. The frequencies there are

<Formula label="Equal phase and group velocity" tex="\omega \approx 1.6 \times 10^4\ \text{s}^{-1} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \omega \approx 5.8 \times 10^4\ \text{s}^{-1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.2(a), p. 2 (our own solution, read from the curve)" />

The common speed is the slope of each line: about $0.72 \times 10^4$ m/s at the lower point and about $1.35 \times 10^4$ m/s at the upper one.

  curves={[

  ]}

  caption="The dispersion curve with the two straight lines through the origin that touch it (dashed). At the points A and B the line from the origin is also the tangent, so the phase velocity equals the group velocity there. The curve is computed from the formula given in the solution; the lines have the slopes 0.72 and 1.35 (in units of 10⁴ m/s)." />

</Step>

<Step title="Between and outside">

Between A and B the curve is steeper than the line from the origin, so $v_g > v_p$. To the left of A and to the right of B the curve is flatter than that line, so $v_g < v_p$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The phase velocity $\omega/k$ changes with $k$ at the rate $(v_g - v_p)/k$, so the two velocities are equal exactly where $v_p$ has a smallest or a largest value. That fits the graph: $v_p$ is very large near $k = 0$ (the curve starts above the origin), falls to its smallest value at A, rises to a largest value at B, and falls again as the curve flattens.

You want to send a pulse through the medium at the highest possible speed. Around which mean angular frequency should the pulse be built (in units of $10^4$ s⁻¹), and how large is that speed (in units of $10^4$ m/s)?

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

<Answer id="p2-w" label="mean $\omega =$ ($10^4$ s⁻¹)" value="3.94" tolerance="10%" calc="p2-w" />

<Answer id="p2-v" label="speed $=$ ($10^4$ m/s)" value="3.09" tolerance="10%" calc="p2-v" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A pulse is an envelope, and an envelope travels at the group velocity $d\omega/dk$ taken at the mean frequency of the pulse. So look for the steepest point of the curve.

<Step title="The steepest point">

The curve is steepest halfway up, where it changes from bending up to bending down: near $k \approx 3.5$ per metre, at

<Formula label="Mean frequency of the fastest pulse" tex="\omega \approx 3.9 \times 10^4\ \text{s}^{-1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.2(b), p. 2 (our own solution, read from the curve)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The speed">

The tangent at that point passes close to the points $(3,\ 2.4)$ and $(4,\ 5.5)$ of the graph, so its slope is

<Formula label="Largest group velocity" tex="v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} \approx \frac{(5.5 - 2.4) \times 10^4\ \text{s}^{-1}}{(4 - 3)\ \text{m}^{-1}} = 3.1 \times 10^4\ \text{m/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.2(b), p. 2 (our own solution, read from the curve)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: (1/s)/(1/m) = m/s. The crests inside this pulse move at $v_p = \omega/k \approx 3.9/3.5 \approx 1.1 \times 10^4$ m/s, more slowly than the pulse: this point lies between A and B of part (a), where $v_g > v_p$. The choice has a second advantage. In the expansion of $\omega(k)$ around the carrier (Lecture 14 notes, pp. 4–5) the first term left out contains the second derivative of $\omega$, and at the steepest point that derivative is zero. So, to that order, the pulse built here does not spread.

</Step>

<Part label="c" points={8}>

How does the medium respond if you drive it at $\omega = \omega_0 = 1 \times 10^4$ s⁻¹? MIT's hint: what happens to $v_p$ and to $v_g$ as $\omega \to \omega_0$?

<Hint>

Read the wave number at $\omega_0$ from the graph. What wavelength is that?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $v_p$ look at the line from the origin to a point of the curve as the point slides to $k = 0$. For $v_g$ look at the tangent there.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" choices={[
  "A travelling wave of very short wavelength that moves at the highest speed the medium allows",
  "The whole medium oscillates in step, with no travelling wave: the wavelength and the phase velocity become infinite, the group velocity is zero, and no signal or energy moves along",
  "A travelling wave whose crests and whose envelope move together, since the two velocities are equal there",
  "A wave whose envelope runs backwards while its crests run forwards"
]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The frequency $\omega_0$ belongs to $k = 0$, the left end of the curve. Follow the two slopes as the point slides there.

<Step title="The two velocities">

As $\omega \to \omega_0$ the wave number goes to zero while $\omega$ stays at $\omega_0$, and the curve becomes horizontal:

<Formula label="The limit of the two velocities" tex="v_p = \frac{\omega}{k} \to \infty, \qquad v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} \to 0 \qquad (\omega \to \omega_0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.2(c), p. 2 (MIT's hint, worked out by us)" />

</Step>

<Step title="What the medium does">

$k = 0$ means an infinite wavelength: the motion $\cos(kx - \omega_0 t)$ becomes $\cos(\omega_0 t)$, the same at every $x$. All parts of the medium swing in step at the frequency $\omega_0$, like one large oscillator. Nothing travels: an envelope would move at $v_g = 0$, so no signal and no energy move along the medium. The infinite phase velocity only says that all places reach their largest displacement at the same instant.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

An infinite phase velocity breaks no rule, because a phase carries no information (the lesson's water wave meeting a beach). The curve has no point below $\omega_0$: there is no real $k$ for such a frequency, so a harmonic wave of that frequency does not exist in the medium. The Lecture 14 notes (pp. 1–2) describe a medium of this kind, an ionic crystal, whose curve also starts at a finite frequency at $k = 0$: radiation below that frequency does not propagate in it and is reflected.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A Gaussian pulse and its frequencies" points={30} mit="Problem 7.3">

An infinite string has the mass per unit length $\rho_L$ and the tension $T$. A pulse with the shape of a Gaussian travels along it. The displacement of the string is

<Formula label="The Gaussian pulse" tex="y(x, t) = \exp\left[-\frac12\left(\frac{x - vt}{\sigma}\right)^2\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.3, p. 2" />

in units of its largest value. The length $\sigma$ is the width of the pulse and $v$ is its speed.

A signal $f(t)$ is a sum of harmonic oscillations, and the amplitude of each frequency follows from the signal:

<Formula label="A signal and its distribution of frequencies" tex="f(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}C(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,d\omega, \qquad C(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)\,e^{i\omega t}\,dt" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3" />

(MIT's sheet points to a textbook for the second formula; this is the form of the lecture notes.) Take as the signal the motion of the point $x = 0$ of the string, $f(t) = y(0, t)$.

<Part label="a" points={12}>

Find the distribution of frequencies $C(\omega)$ of the pulse. It is again a Gaussian, $C(\omega) = C(0)\exp\left[-\tfrac12(\omega/\sigma_\omega)^2\right]$: find its width $\sigma_\omega$ as well. Give both in terms of $\sigma$, $v$ and $\omega$, with all constants right.

MIT calls this a very well-known integral and lets you look it up: for a real $\tau > 0$ and any complex number $b$,

<Formula label="The Gaussian integral" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\exp\left[-\frac{(t - b)^2}{2\tau^2}\right]dt = \tau\sqrt{2\pi}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.3(a), p. 2 (MIT allows looking the integral up; this form of it is ours)" />

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

  vars={{ s: [0.5, 2], v: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ s: "\\sigma", v: "v" }}

    { expr: "1/s", hint: "This is a width in wave number, in 1/m. A frequency is a wave number times the speed, ω = vk." },

<Solution>

**The idea.** At a fixed point the pulse is a Gaussian in time, of duration $\sigma/v$. The Fourier transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian: complete the square in the exponent and use the known integral.

<Step title="The signal">

At $x = 0$ the displacement is

<Formula label="The signal at x = 0" tex="f(t) = y(0, t) = \exp\left[-\frac{t^2}{2\tau^2}\right], \qquad \tau = \frac{\sigma}{v}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.3, p. 2 (the pulse at x = 0; the name τ is ours)" />

The time $\tau$ is the width of the pulse in time: a pulse of length $\sigma$ at the speed $v$ takes about $\sigma/v$ to pass.

</Step>

<Step title="Complete the square">

<Formula label="The exponent" tex="-\frac{t^2}{2\tau^2} + i\omega t = -\frac{(t - i\omega\tau^2)^2}{2\tau^2} - \frac{\omega^2\tau^2}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

(Multiply out the square to check: the cross term is $+i\omega t$, and the square of $i\omega\tau^2$ gives $+\omega^2\tau^2/2$, which the last term removes.)

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

The last term has no $t$ and comes out. What is left is the Gaussian integral with $b = i\omega\tau^2$:

<Formula label="Distribution of frequencies of the Gaussian pulse" tex="C(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\tau\sqrt{2\pi}\;e^{-\omega^2\tau^2/2} = \frac{\sigma}{v\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\left[-\frac{\sigma^2\omega^2}{2v^2}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.3(a), p. 2 (our own solution, with the formula for C(ω) of Lecture 14 notes, p. 3)" />

Compare with $\exp\left[-\tfrac12(\omega/\sigma_\omega)^2\right]$:

<Formula label="Width of the spectrum" tex="\sigma_\omega = \frac{1}{\tau} = \frac{v}{\sigma}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7.3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\sigma\omega/v$ is (m)(1/s)/(m/s), a pure number, and $C$ is a time, as it must be for $C\,d\omega$ to be a pure number like $y$. Put $t = 0$ in the first formula of the problem: the area under $C(\omega)$ must be $f(0) = 1$. It is: a Gaussian of height $\tau/\sqrt{2\pi}$ and width $1/\tau$ has the area $(\tau/\sqrt{2\pi}) \times \sqrt{2\pi}/\tau = 1$. $C(\omega)$ is real and even in $\omega$, as it must be for a real signal that is even in $t$.

At another point $x$ the signal is the same, only later by $x/v$, which multiplies $C(\omega)$ by the phase factor $e^{i\omega x/v}$ and leaves its size unchanged. That is the ideal string of the lesson, $\omega/k = v = \sqrt{T/\rho_L}$ for every frequency (Lecture 13 notes, p. 1): no dispersion, and the pulse keeps its shape.

</Step>

</Solution>
