The [last lecture](/courses/8-03/wave-plates-radiation) found how accelerated charges radiate. This one asks a practical question: how to take good photos (transcript, pp. 1–2).

## Polarized skies

A polarizing filter on a camera darkens the sky, making clouds stand out, and removes glare from windows and water (transcript, pp. 2–3). The first effect needs only what we know (pp. 2–3). Sunlight comes from countless uncorrelated emitters and is unpolarized. Light from the sky, well away from the sun, is sunlight scattered by small particles in the air (Lee speaks of dust and molecules) towards the camera. (Lee speaks of sky about 45° from the sun; the polarization is complete for scattering through 90°, the case the argument uses.) Since the field of a wave is transverse, light scattered through $90°$ towards you can keep only the part of the sun's field that is perpendicular to both the incoming and the outgoing direction: it is polarized, and a filter turned the right way removes most of it. The second effect, reflections, is "much more complicated" and needs light in matter (p. 3).

## Maxwell's equations in matter

Two kinds of material (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 3–4):

1. In a **perfect conductor** there is an unlimited supply of free charges, moving at no cost, and no field inside.
2. In a **dielectric** every charge is bound to an atom or molecule. A field can only shift them a little, displacing the electron cloud against the nucleus: the material becomes polarized, partly opposing the field.

To handle this as in vacuum, split the charge density into free and bound parts, $\rho = \rho_f + \rho_b$, and define the **electric displacement** (notes, pp. 1–2; transcript, pp. 4–5)

<Formula label="Displacement field" tex="\vec D \equiv \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P, \qquad -\nabla\cdot\vec P \equiv \rho_b, \qquad \varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E = \rho_f + \rho_b \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_f" cite="reading1" at="pp. 1–2" />

where $\vec P$ is the polarization (dipole moment per volume; for a pair of charges $\vec p = q\vec d$). $\vec D$ answers only to the free charge. Likewise the current has free, bound and polarization parts, $\vec J = \vec J_f + \vec J_b + \vec J_p$, with $\vec J_b = \nabla\times\vec M$ and $\vec J_p = \partial\vec P/\partial t$; defining $\vec H \equiv \vec B/\mu_0 - \vec M$, Ampère's law becomes (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 5–6)

<Formula label="Ampère's law in matter" tex="\nabla\times\vec H = \vec J_f + \frac{\partial\vec D}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

(Lee points out a typo in this equation on his slides, p. 7.) Bound currents round neighbouring atoms cancel, leaving a current at the surface, he adds in answer to a question (pp. 6–7).

**Without free charges or currents** (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 7–8), for small fields in a **linear, homogeneous, isotropic** material, $\vec P \propto \vec E$ and $\vec M \propto \vec B$, so $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$ and $\vec H = \vec B/\mu$, and

<Formula label="Maxwell's equations in a linear dielectric" tex="\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0, \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec B = 0, \qquad \nabla\times\vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla\times\vec B = \mu\varepsilon\,\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

the vacuum equations with $\mu_0\varepsilon_0$ replaced by $\mu\varepsilon$. (The notes print $\partial\vec B/\partial t$ in the last equation; it is $\partial\vec E/\partial t$.) The **permittivity** $\varepsilon = \kappa_e\varepsilon_0$ measures, roughly, the material's resistance to forming an electric field; the **permeability** $\mu$ is usually close to $\mu_0$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 8–9). So light travels at (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 8–9)

<Formula label="Speed of light in matter" tex="v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \frac{c}{n}, \qquad n = \frac{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

With $\mu \approx \mu_0$ and $\varepsilon > \varepsilon_0$, $n > 1$: light in matter is slower, with $\omega/k = c/\sqrt{\kappa_e}$ (notes, p. 3). Real materials are more complicated: $n$ depends on frequency, usually decreasing with wavelength (notes, p. 3). And the response itself depends on how fast the field varies: a slowly varying field can move ions, a fast one only the electron clouds (transcript, p. 9).

## Reflection and transmission

Light in air (medium 1) meets glass (medium 2) at the plane $z = 0$ (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 10–12). Write the incident, reflected and transmitted waves:

<Formula label="Three waves" tex="\vec E_I = \vec E_{0I}\cos(\vec k_I\cdot\vec r - \omega t), \quad \vec E_R = \vec E_{0R}\cos(\vec k_R\cdot\vec r - \omega t), \quad \vec E_T = \vec E_{0T}\cos(\vec k_T\cdot\vec r - \omega t); \qquad \vec B_I = \frac{1}{v_1}\hat k_I\times\vec E_I" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

and similarly for $\vec B_R$ and $\vec B_T$, with $\vec E_{0I}$ known. From [Lecture 16](/courses/8-03/two-dimensional-waves-snells-law), matching at $z = 0$ gives $\vec k_I\cdot\vec r = \vec k_R\cdot\vec r = \vec k_T\cdot\vec r$ there, $\theta_I = \theta_R$ and $n_1\sin\theta_I = n_2\sin\theta_T$; the cosines are then identical on the boundary and drop out (notes, pp. 4–5; transcript, p. 12). The amplitudes need more: Maxwell's equations in matter (notes, p. 5; transcript, pp. 13–15).

<Formula label="Boundary conditions" tex="\vec E^{(1)} = \vec E_{0I} + \vec E_{0R}, \quad \vec E^{(2)} = \vec E_{0T}; \qquad \varepsilon_1E_\perp^{(1)} = \varepsilon_2E_\perp^{(2)}, \qquad E_\parallel^{(1)} = E_\parallel^{(2)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

The first condition comes from $\nabla\cdot\vec D = 0$ applied to a flat pillbox across the surface, the second from Faraday's law around a thin rectangle across it; in both, shrinking the thickness to zero kills the side contributions and the enclosed flux.

**Polarization in the plane of incidence** (the $xz$-plane here; notes, p. 6; transcript, pp. 15–17). Projecting perpendicular and parallel to the surface:

<Formula label="Two equations for the amplitudes" tex="\varepsilon_1(-E_{0I}\sin\theta_I + E_{0R}\sin\theta_I) = -\varepsilon_2E_{0T}\sin\theta_T, \qquad E_{0I}\cos\theta_I + E_{0R}\cos\theta_I = E_{0T}\cos\theta_T" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

With Snell's law these read $E_{0I} - E_{0R} = \beta E_{0T}$ and $E_{0I} + E_{0R} = \alpha E_{0T}$ (notes, p. 6; transcript, p. 17), where

<Formula label="Reflected and transmitted amplitudes" tex="\beta = \frac{\varepsilon_2n_1}{\varepsilon_1n_2}, \quad \alpha = \frac{\cos\theta_T}{\cos\theta_I}; \qquad E_{0R} = \frac{\alpha - \beta}{\alpha + \beta}E_{0I}, \qquad E_{0T} = \frac{2}{\alpha + \beta}E_{0I}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

so $R = (\alpha - \beta)/(\alpha + \beta)$ and $T = 2/(\alpha + \beta)$. (In the notes the first step has $\sin\theta_I/\sin\theta_2$ where $\sin\theta_T/\sin\theta_I$ is meant; the result is the same.)

**Three cases** (notes, p. 7; transcript, pp. 17–19):

1. **Normal incidence**, $\theta_I = 0$ (Lee first says $90°$ and corrects himself): $\alpha = 1$. With $\mu_1 \approx \mu_2 \approx \mu_0$, $\varepsilon \propto n^2$, so $\beta = n_2/n_1$ and

<Formula label="Normal incidence" tex="R = \frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2}, \qquad T = \frac{2n_1}{n_1 + n_2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

   The more the indices differ, the more is reflected; Lee mentions diamond, whose large index (he says about 2.6; it is about 2.4) makes it sparkle (p. 18).
2. **Grazing incidence**, $\theta_I \to 90°$: $\alpha \to \infty$, $R \approx 1$, $T \approx 0$, which is why a distant lake or road with the sun beyond it shines so brightly (p. 19).
3. **Brewster's angle** $\theta_B$, where $\alpha = \beta$: $R = 0$ and the light is all transmitted. Then $\cos\theta_T/\cos\theta_B = n_2/n_1 = \sin\theta_B/\sin\theta_T$, so $\sin 2\theta_T = \sin 2\theta_B$; the solution other than $\theta_T = \theta_B$ is

<Formula label="Brewster's angle" tex="2\theta_B = \pi - 2\theta_T \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \theta_B + \theta_T = \frac{\pi}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

   the reflected and transmitted rays at right angles. (With Snell's law, $n_1\sin\theta_B = n_2\cos\theta_B$, that is $\tan\theta_B = n_2/n_1$.) Lee stresses that this comes from Maxwell's equations and is special to electromagnetic waves (p. 19).

**Polarized reflections** (notes, p. 8; transcript, pp. 19–21). Unpolarized light is a mixture of the polarization in the plane of incidence and the polarization perpendicular to it (along $y$). The notes state that the $y$ polarization has no Brewster angle: it is always partly reflected. So at Brewster's angle the reflected light is entirely polarized along the surface, and a polarizer can remove it: that is the photographer's trick. In the demonstration in class, light reflected from a glass plate at an arbitrary angle cannot be fully blocked by a polarizer at any orientation, but at Brewster's angle it can (p. 20). A separate recording shows the same in detail: away from Brewster's angle some orientations dim the reflected spot, and at Brewster's angle it vanishes completely for one orientation (pp. 20–21).

<WorkedExample title="Reflections of your own">
<Problem>
Light in air meets water ($n = 1.33$) and glass ($n = 1.50$). (a) Find Brewster's angle and the transmitted angle for glass. (b) Find Brewster's angle for water. (c) At normal incidence on glass, what fraction of the amplitude is reflected, and what fraction of the intensity?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Glass">
$\tan\theta_B = 1.50$, so $\theta_B \approx 56.3°$; then $\theta_T = 90° - 56.3° = 33.7°$. Check: $\sin 56.3° \approx 0.832$ and $1.50\sin 33.7° \approx 0.832$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Water">
$\tan\theta_B = 1.33$: $\theta_B \approx 53.1°$. A polarizing filter removes glare from a lake best when you look at the water about $37°$ below the horizontal.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Normal incidence">
$R = (1 - 1.50)/(1 + 1.50) = -0.20$: the reflected amplitude is a fifth of the incident one, with its sign reversed. Both waves are in air, so the reflected intensity is $R^2 = 0.04$ of the incident: a window reflects about 4% at each surface.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Light scattered through $90°$ is polarized: polarizing filters darken the sky.
- In matter, $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P$ and $\vec H = \vec B/\mu_0 - \vec M$ answer to free charges; in a linear medium Maxwell's equations are those of vacuum with $\mu\varepsilon$, so $v = c/n$, $n = \sqrt{\mu\varepsilon/\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$.
- At a boundary, $\varepsilon_1E_\perp^{(1)} = \varepsilon_2E_\perp^{(2)}$ and $E_\parallel^{(1)} = E_\parallel^{(2)}$; for polarization in the plane of incidence $R = (\alpha - \beta)/(\alpha + \beta)$, $T = 2/(\alpha + \beta)$.
- Normal incidence: $R = (n_1 - n_2)/(n_1 + n_2)$; grazing: $R \to 1$; Brewster's angle, $\theta_B + \theta_T = 90°$: no reflection of that polarization, so reflected light is polarized.
