summary: "A symmetry of a system is a matrix S such that if X(t) is a solution, so is SX(t); for the coupled pendulums it is the reflection S = (0 −1; −1 0). That holds exactly when S commutes with M⁻¹K, [S, M⁻¹K] = S M⁻¹K − M⁻¹K S = 0. Then, when the two mode frequencies differ, every normal-mode vector of M⁻¹K is an eigenvector of S, and conversely, if the eigenvalues of S are all different, every eigenvector of S is a normal mode: the much easier eigenvalue problem of S gives the mode shapes, and one multiplication M⁻¹KA gives ω². For the reflection, S² = I, so the eigenvalues are ±1 with A = (1, 1) and (1, −1), for every symmetric two-part system at once. Next, an infinite line of masses m joined by springs (constant K, natural length a): mẍⱼ = Kxⱼ₋₁ − 2Kxⱼ + Kxⱼ₊₁, an infinite matrix problem. Translation by one spacing is a symmetry; its matrix shifts every amplitude by one place, so its eigenvectors are Aⱼ = βʲ for any β: infinitely many modes. Putting them into M⁻¹K gives ω² = ω₀²(2 − β − 1/β) with ω₀² = K/m. Only |β| = 1 keeps the amplitudes finite at both ends, so β = e^(ika), and ω² = 2ω₀²(1 − cos ka), a dispersion relation. Combining β and 1/β (k and −k), which have the same ω, gives real amplitudes such as Aⱼ = sin(jka) or cos(jka): the normal modes are standing waves, sinusoidal because of translation symmetry. The highest frequency, ω = 2ω₀, comes at cos ka = −1, the masses alternating up and down."

Prof. Lee is back (transcript, p. 1). The last two lectures showed that a driving force can excite one normal mode and that the full solution is particular plus homogeneous; this one shows how **symmetry** finds the normal modes without solving the eigenvalue problem of $M^{-1}K$, and then moves to **infinitely many** coupled oscillators (p. 1).

## Symmetry as a matrix

Symmetry matters in physics, mathematics and art; detectors such as CMS at the Large Hadron Collider are built symmetric so that their data are easier to analyse (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2). For the two [coupled pendulums](/courses/8-03/driven-coupled-oscillators-symmetry) the symmetry is a reflection: swapping $x_1 \to -x_2$ and $x_2 \to -x_1$ leaves the physics unchanged (transcript, p. 2). As a matrix (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 3):

<Formula label="Reflection matrix" tex="\tilde X(t) = SX(t), \qquad S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

If $X$ solves $\ddot X = -M^{-1}KX$ and $\tilde X = SX$ is also a solution, then $S\ddot X = -M^{-1}KSX$; multiplying the original equation by $S$ gives $S\ddot X = -SM^{-1}KX$. Comparing the two (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Symmetry condition" tex="SM^{-1}K = M^{-1}KS, \qquad [S, M^{-1}K] \equiv SM^{-1}K - M^{-1}KS = 0" cite="reading1" at="pp. 1–2" />

The bracket is the **commutator**, $[A, B] = AB - BA$; the matrices **commute** when it is zero, and commutators will be everywhere in quantum physics (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 4).

## Symmetry gives the normal modes

**From modes to $S$** (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 4–5). Let $X = A^{(1)}\cos\omega_1t$ be a normal mode, and suppose the two mode frequencies differ, $\omega_1 \neq \omega_2$ (notes, p. 2; two identical uncoupled pendulums would be a counterexample otherwise). Swapping the pendulums does not change the frequency, so $\tilde X$ is the same normal mode, proportional to $A^{(1)}\cos\omega_1t$. Hence $SA^{(1)} = \beta_1A^{(1)}$: the mode vector is also an **eigenvector of $S$**, and likewise $A^{(2)}$.

**From $S$ to modes** (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 6–7). Run the logic backwards. Given $SA = \beta A$ and $[S, M^{-1}K] = 0$:

<Formula label="Reversing the logic" tex="S\,(M^{-1}KA) = M^{-1}K\,SA = \beta\,(M^{-1}KA)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

So $M^{-1}KA$ is an eigenvector of $S$ with the same eigenvalue $\beta$. If the eigenvalues of $S$ are all different, it must be a multiple of $A$: $M^{-1}KA = \omega^2A$ (p. 6). Solving the easy eigenvalue problem of $S$ gives the mode shapes, and one multiplication gives $\omega^2$, with no eigenvalue problem for $M^{-1}K$ (pp. 6–7).

For the reflection, $S^2 = I$, so $\beta^2 = 1$ and $\beta = \pm1$ (notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Reflection eigenvectors" tex="\beta_1 = -1:\ A^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \beta_2 = +1:\ A^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

And the remarkable consequence: every system with this symmetry, two pendulums, two masses between walls, more elaborate arrangements, has these same mode shapes. "Once you've solved one, you have solved them all" (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 7).

## An infinite chain

Infinitely many things joined at random would be hopeless; a physicist looks for the symmetry (transcript, pp. 7–8). The system: identical masses $m$ in a line from one edge of the universe to the other, joined by ideal springs of constant $K$ and natural length $a$, moving only along the line (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 8). Label them $j$, with displacements $x_j$. Two springs act on mass $j$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Equation of motion of mass j" tex="m\ddot x_j = -K(x_j - x_{j-1}) - K(x_j - x_{j+1}) = Kx_{j-1} - 2Kx_j + Kx_{j+1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

(The notes' text writes the spring constant as $k$, their figure $K$; here it is $K$, as Lee writes it, because $k$ will be the wave number. The same letter also names the stiffness matrix, whose entries are multiples of it.) With $x_j = A_j\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, the matrix $M^{-1}K$ is infinite, with $2K/m$ on the diagonal and $-K/m$ beside it, and solving its eigenvalue problem directly is not an option (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 9).

## Translation symmetry

Shift every mass one spacing $a$ along the chain: the system looks the same (notes, pp. 3–4; transcript, pp. 9–10). The shift matrix has ones just above the diagonal and moves each amplitude one place (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 10):

<Formula label="Shift matrix" tex="A' = SA, \qquad S = \begin{pmatrix} \ddots & & & \\ & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ & 0 & 0 & 1 \\ & & & \ddots \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A'_j = A_{j+1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

An eigenvector, $SA = \beta A$, therefore satisfies $A_{j+1} = \beta A_j$; with $A_0 = 1$ (zero would make everything zero) (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="Eigenvectors of translation" tex="A_j = \beta^j" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

for **any** nonzero $\beta$: infinitely many eigenvalues, as there should be for infinitely many degrees of freedom (transcript, p. 11). A row of coupled oscillators from Bell Labs and a long spring show the complicated waves and standing waves such a system makes (pp. 11–12).

## The dispersion relation

Now multiply by $M^{-1}K$. Only three terms touch row $j$ (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 12–13):

<Formula label="Row j" tex="\omega^2A_j = \omega_0^2(-A_{j-1} + 2A_j - A_{j+1}), \qquad \omega_0^2 = \frac{K}{m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

With $A_j = \beta^j$, dividing by $\beta^j$ (p. 13):

<Formula label="Frequency of each mode" tex="\omega^2 = \omega_0^2\left(2 - \beta - \frac{1}{\beta}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

**Which $\beta$?** (transcript, pp. 13–14). With $\beta = 2$, $A_j = 2^j$ explodes at the right-hand edge of the universe; with $|\beta| < 1$ it explodes at the left. Only $|\beta| = 1$ is physical, so $\beta = e^{ika}$, where $a$ is the spring's natural length and $k$ will turn out to be a **wave number** (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 14–15). Then $\beta + 1/\beta = 2\cos ka$ and (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 14)

<Formula label="Dispersion relation" tex="\omega^2 = 2\omega_0^2\,(1 - \cos ka)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

This relation between wave number and frequency is called a **dispersion relation** (notes, p. 5).

## Standing waves

What it means (notes, p. 5; transcript, pp. 14–17):

1. **Infinitely many normal modes**: each $k$ gives one.
2. **Their shape**: $\beta$ and $1/\beta$, that is $k$ and $-k$, give the same $\omega$, so any combination of $e^{ijka}$ and $e^{-ijka}$ is also a mode. The real choice is (notes, p. 5)

<Formula label="Mode shape" tex="A_j = \frac{1}{2i}\left(e^{ijka} - e^{-ijka}\right) = \sin(jka)" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

   so $x_j = \sin(jka)\cos(\omega t + \phi)$: a **standing wave**, sampled at the masses. The cosine combination is just as valid (transcript, p. 16). At $ka = 0$ or $\pi$, where $\beta = 1/\beta$, the sine vanishes at every mass and the mode is $A_j = 1$ or $A_j = (-1)^j$. The sinusoidal shape comes straight from translation symmetry (transcript, pp. 14–17).
3. **Maximum frequency**: $\cos ka = -1$ gives $\omega^2 = 4\omega_0^2$, $\omega = 2\omega_0$: neighbouring masses move in opposite directions, positive, negative, positive, stretching every spring to the full and giving the largest restoring force (notes, p. 5; transcript, p. 17). A demonstration with ten oscillators shows it (transcript, p. 17); in the demonstrations the masses move up and down, while the model above moves them along the line (p. 11).
4. **Minimum frequency**: $\cos ka = 1$ gives $\omega = 0$, all masses moving the same way (notes, p. 5).

Every possible motion is a superposition of these standing waves (notes, p. 5). Next: the continuous limit (transcript, p. 18).

<WorkedExample title="A chain of your own">
<Problem>
A chain has $\omega_0 = 10$ rad/s and spacing $a = 5.0$ cm. Find the frequency of the mode with wavelength $40$ cm ($k = 2\pi/\lambda$), compare it with $\omega_0ka$, and give the highest frequency and its wavelength.
</Problem>
<Step title="The 40 cm mode">
$k = 2\pi/0.40 \approx 15.7$ m⁻¹ and $ka = 15.7 \times 0.050 = \pi/4$. Then $\omega^2 = 2(100)(1 - \cos\frac{\pi}{4}) = 200(1 - 0.7071) \approx 58.6$, so $\omega \approx 7.65$ rad/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Long-wave estimate">
For small $ka$, $1 - \cos ka \approx \frac12(ka)^2$, so $\omega \approx \omega_0ka = 10 \times \pi/4 \approx 7.85$ rad/s, about 3% too high already at eight masses per wavelength.
</Step>
<Step title="Highest frequency">
$\cos ka = -1$ means $ka = \pi$, a wavelength $2a = 10$ cm: neighbours opposite. Then $\omega = 2\omega_0 = 20$ rad/s; no mode of this chain can oscillate faster.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A symmetry $S$ commutes with $M^{-1}K$; then normal modes with distinct frequencies are eigenvectors of $S$, and (with distinct eigenvalues of $S$) vice versa.
- Reflection: $S^2 = I$, modes $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ for every symmetric pair.
- Infinite chain: translation gives $A_j = \beta^j$; physical modes have $\beta = e^{ika}$.
- Dispersion relation $\omega^2 = 2\omega_0^2(1 - \cos ka)$; modes are standing waves $\sin(jka)$ or $\cos(jka)$, with $\omega \leq 2\omega_0$.
