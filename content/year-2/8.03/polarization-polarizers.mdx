summary: "Snell's law n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂ has no solution when light leaves a denser medium at a steep angle, (n₁/n₂) sin θ₁ > 1: then all the light is reflected (total internal reflection), as a laser in a tank of water shows, and as optical fibres use. Then the second way to add a dimension: let the direction of the electric field change. A wave travelling along z is E = Re[ψ₀e^(i(kz − ωt))] with ψ₀ = ψ₁x̂ + ψ₂ŷ, a superposition of an x-polarized and a y-polarized wave, written as the column Z = (ψ₁, ψ₂). Equal components in phase, Z = E₀(1, 1), oscillate along a line: linear polarization. Equal amplitudes a quarter period apart, Z = E₀(1, −i), turn the field round a circle (clockwise for the axes used, 'right-handed'; (1, i) goes the other way): circular polarization. Any phase difference other than 0 or π gives an ellipse, elliptical polarization, unless the amplitudes are equal and the phase difference is π/2 or 3π/2 (circular). Unpolarized light comes from many independent emitters at different times and slightly different frequencies. A grid of conducting wires is a polarizer: electrons moving along the wires cancel the field parallel to them and reflect it, while the field across them passes; that direction is the easy axis. As a matrix, P_θ = ((cos²θ, cos θ sin θ), (cos θ sin θ, sin²θ)), and linearly polarized light at angle θ to the easy axis keeps I = I₀cos²θ. Crossed polarizers block everything, but a third one at 45° between them lets light through, as a laptop screen shows; with single photons this shows that a photon behaves as a wave."

This lecture opens Part III, optics (from the course site). It finishes [Snell's law](/courses/8-03/two-dimensional-waves-snells-law) with an experiment, then takes the second way of going beyond one dimension: letting the direction of the electric field change (transcript, p. 1).

## Total internal reflection

Recall (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 1–3): all three waves at a boundary share the frequency and the component of $\vec k$ along it, so the reflected angle equals the incident angle and

<Formula label="Snell's law" tex="n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Going into a medium with larger $n$, the wave slows, its crests fall behind and the wave turns towards the normal (transcript, p. 3). Now go the other way, from $n_1 = 1.5$ into vacuum, $n_2 = 1$ (pp. 3–4; Lee first says "$n_1$ equal to $n_2$"). Then $\sin\theta_2 = 1.5\sin\theta_1$, which exceeds 1 once $\theta_1$ is large enough: no transmitted angle exists. Does the system explode? A laser shone through a tank of water ($n \approx 1.33$) at a steep angle shows what happens: no light comes out into the air; it is all reflected back into the water, bouncing along the tank (p. 4). This **total internal reflection** is how an **optical fibre** guides light; in a demonstration, the image of text on a card is carried through a fibre to a camera, though the transmission gave out midway, and such fibres carry signals under the sea from the US to Asia (pp. 4–5). The rainbow, also a consequence of Snell's law, is left to the problem set (p. 5).

## Polarization

Electromagnetic waves are predicted to be transverse, but, as Lee puts it, quoting Feynman, a theory that disagrees with experiment is wrong, however beautiful: how do we know light is not longitudinal (transcript, pp. 5–6)? Write a wave travelling along $z$ as (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 6–7)

<Formula label="Polarized plane wave" tex="\vec E(z, t) = \text{Re}\left[\vec\psi_0\,e^{i(kz - \omega t)}\right], \quad \vec\psi_0 = \psi_1\hat x + \psi_2\hat y, \quad \psi_1 = A_1e^{i\phi_1}, \ \psi_2 = A_2e^{i\phi_2}; \qquad E = \text{Re}\left[Z\,e^{i(kz - \omega t)}\right], \quad Z = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_1 \\ \psi_2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

a superposition of an $x$-polarized and a $y$-polarized wave. Following the field at $z = 0$ over time gives its **polarization** (notes, pp. 1–4; transcript, pp. 7–12):

1. **No phase difference**, $\vec E_1 = E_0\cos(kz - \omega t)\hat x$ and $\vec E_2 = E_0\cos(kz - \omega t)\hat y$: the field swings back and forth along a line at 45°, $Z = E_0\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$. This is **linear polarization**, like $E_0\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $E_0\begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}$ and in general $E_0\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \end{pmatrix}$ (notes, pp. 1–2; transcript, pp. 7–9). One could always rotate the axes to make it a single component, Lee admits, but the language will be useful (p. 9).
2. **Equal amplitudes, a quarter period apart** (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 9–10):

<Formula label="Circular polarization" tex="\vec E_1 = E_0\cos(kz - \omega t)\hat x, \quad \vec E_2 = E_0\sin(kz - \omega t)\hat y = E_0\cos\left(kz - \omega t - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\hat y; \qquad Z = E_0\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -i \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

   since $e^{-i\pi/2} = -i$. At $z = 0$ the field is $E_0(\cos\omega t, -\sin\omega t)$: constant length, turning round a circle. This is **circular polarization**; the notes call this sense, clockwise for $x$ to the right and $y$ up, "right-handed", and $E_0\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ i \end{pmatrix}$ turns counter-clockwise (notes, p. 2). (In the lecture the minus sign of the $y$ component is dropped and the rotation is described as counter-clockwise; p. 10.) The intensity, proportional to $E^2$, stays constant (p. 12).
3. **Unequal amplitudes**, $\vec E_1 = \frac{E_0}{2}\cos(kz - \omega t)\hat x$ with the same $\vec E_2$: $Z = E_0\begin{pmatrix} 1/2 \\ -i \end{pmatrix}$, a circle squashed along $x$: **elliptical polarization**, as are $E_0\begin{pmatrix} 1/2 \\ i \end{pmatrix}$, $E_0\begin{pmatrix} A \\ iB \end{pmatrix}$ and $E_0\begin{pmatrix} C \\ -iD \end{pmatrix}$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 10–11).
4. **Another phase difference**, $\vec E_2 = E_0\cos(kz - \omega t + \Delta\phi)\hat y$ with $\Delta\phi$ not 0, $\pi$, $\pi/2$ or $3\pi/2$: a tilted ellipse, starting from the 45° line as $\Delta\phi$ grows from zero (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 11–12). In general (notes, p. 4)

<Formula label="General polarization" tex="Z = \begin{pmatrix} \psi_1 \\ \psi_2 \end{pmatrix} = e^{i\phi}\begin{pmatrix} A\cos\theta - iB\sin\theta \\ A\sin\theta + iB\cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A \geq |B|" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

   an ellipse with axes $A$ and $|B|$ tilted by $\theta$.

So a rotating field needs nothing new: two linearly polarized waves with a phase difference (transcript, p. 12).

5. **Unpolarized light**, from a light bulb, say, comes from a huge number of uncorrelated emitters (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 14–15). Not fields in every direction at once, which would add up to zero, but waves emitted at different times with slightly different frequencies.

## Polarizers

A **perfect conductor** cancels the electric field at its surface and reflects the wave (transcript, p. 13). Make it a grid of parallel wires, here along $y$ (notes, p. 5; transcript, pp. 13–14):

1. A field along $\hat y$ drives electrons along the wires, which cancel it: the wave is reflected, as from a metal plate.
2. A field along $\hat x$ cannot move electrons across the wires: it passes.

The direction that passes, perpendicular to the wires, is the **easy axis**: "life is easy" for a field along it (p. 14). Drawn as a circle with an arrow, a polarizer acts on $Z$ as a matrix that keeps only the component along its easy axis (notes, p. 5; transcript, pp. 15–16):

<Formula label="Polarizer matrices" tex="P_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad P_{\pi/2} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \quad P_\theta = \begin{pmatrix} \cos^2\theta & \cos\theta\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta\sin\theta & \sin^2\theta \end{pmatrix}, \quad P_{\pi/4} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/2 & 1/2 \\ 1/2 & 1/2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

where $\theta$ is the angle of the easy axis from $x$. Linearly polarized light at $\theta$ to the easy axis leaves polarized along it with its field reduced by $\cos\theta$; since intensity goes as $\langle E^2\rangle$ (notes, p. 5; transcript, p. 16),

<Formula label="Intensity after a polarizer" tex="|\vec E_f| = |\vec E_0|\cos\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad I_f = I_0\cos^2\theta" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

and two polarizers at right angles pass nothing (notes, p. 6).

**Demonstrations** (transcript, pp. 16–18). A source emits vertically polarized microwaves towards a receiver read on an oscilloscope. Lee keeps saying "refract" for the reflected wave here and elsewhere (pp. 3, 13–17). Most of the class predicts, correctly, that a grid of vertical metal rods blocks the signal; turned through 90°, the grid lets it through (pp. 16–17). A 45° grid between crossed ones gives too small a signal, so Lee switches to his laptop: its screen emits light polarized up and down, and a polarizing sheet (a very fine version of the wires) with a horizontal easy axis blacks it out; turned 90°, the screen reappears (p. 17). With two crossed sheets nothing is seen, but a third at 45° slipped between them lets light through, most at 45° (pp. 17–18). (Lee calls the screen an LED screen; the polarized light comes from its liquid-crystal display.)

**Single photons** (transcript, pp. 18–19). If unpolarized light were simply half photons polarized along $x$ and half along $y$, Lee imagines Einstein arguing, then after an $x$ polarizer and a $y$ polarizer nothing would pass, fine; but what would a 45° polarizer do to a single $x$-polarized photon, which cannot be split? Sent through $x$ and then 45°, the light comes out at $I_0/4$: neither 0 nor $I_0/2$, but what the wave picture gives. A single photon behaves like a wave, a first link to quantum mechanics, and Lee's answer to Einstein's "God doesn't play dice" (pp. 18–19). Quarter-wave plates are left for later (p. 19).

<WorkedExample title="Polarizers and a fibre of your own">
<Problem>
(a) Unpolarized light of intensity $I_0$ passes three polarizers, the first with a vertical easy axis, the second at $30°$ to it, the third horizontal. What intensity comes out? Without the middle one? (b) A fibre of dense glass has $n = 1.60$ in air. Above what angle from the normal is light inside totally reflected?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Three polarizers">
The first passes half: $I_0/2$, vertically polarized. The second passes $\cos^2 30° = 0.75$ of that: $0.375\,I_0$, at $30°$. The third is $60°$ from the second: $\cos^2 60° = 0.25$, leaving $0.094\,I_0$. Without the middle polarizer the last two are crossed and nothing passes.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The fibre">
$\sin\theta_c = n_2/n_1 = 1/1.60$, so $\theta_c \approx 38.7°$. Light striking the wall at a larger angle from the normal stays inside.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Leaving a denser medium, light is totally reflected once $(n_1/n_2)\sin\theta_1 > 1$: optical fibres.
- $Z = (\psi_1, \psi_2)$ describes polarization: a phase difference of 0 or $\pi$, linear; equal amplitudes a quarter period apart, $E_0(1, \mp i)$, circular; any other case elliptical. Unpolarized light comes from uncorrelated emitters.
- A wire grid passes the field across the wires (the easy axis); $P_\theta$ projects onto it, and $I = I_0\cos^2\theta$.
- A $45°$ polarizer between crossed ones lets light through. Unpolarized light through polarizers at $x$ and then $45°$ keeps $I_0/4$, even sent one photon at a time.
