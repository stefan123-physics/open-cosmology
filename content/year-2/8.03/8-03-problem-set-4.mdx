A formula box takes plain typing, for example `n*pi/N` or `2*sqrt(T/(m*a))`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A beaded string that ends half a spacing from the walls" points={25} mit="Problem 4.1">

A massless string is stretched between two walls with a constant tension $T$. On it sit $N$ beads, each of mass $m$, numbered $1$ to $N$ from left to right. Neighbouring beads are a distance $a$ apart, but the first bead is only $a/2$ from the left-hand wall and the last bead only $a/2$ from the right-hand wall. The beads move only up and down, in the plane of the drawing, and by small amounts. Call $y_j$ the displacement of bead $j$. (MIT's Figure 1 shows the beads in a row between the two walls, with the spacings $a/2$, $a$, $a$, …, $a$, $a/2$.)

As in the lesson, a bead with a neighbour at distance $a$ on each side obeys

<Formula label="A bead between two neighbours" tex="m\ddot y_j = \frac{T}{a}\left(y_{j-1} - 2y_j + y_{j+1}\right)" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

and in a normal mode every bead moves with the same frequency, $y_j = A_j\cos(\omega t + \phi)$.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

First find the vertical force that the piece of string between the left-hand wall and bead 1 exerts on bead 1, in terms of $T$, $a$ and $y_1$. Then show that the left-hand wall can be replaced by an endless row of beads, all a distance $a$ apart, provided that the imaginary bead 0 always moves opposite to bead 1: in a normal mode, $A_0 = -A_1$. (MIT writes this condition as $A_1 = -A_0$.)

<Hint>

The piece of string between the wall and bead 1 is $a/2$ long, and its end on the wall stays at height zero. For small angles the vertical force of a string is the tension times its slope.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the endless row there is no wall, but a bead 0 at a distance $a$ to the left of bead 1. Write the vertical force of the piece of string between bead 0 and bead 1 on bead 1, and ask when it equals the force you found with the wall.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-wall" label="Force of the wall piece on bead 1 $=$" expr="-2*T*y1/a"
  vars={{ T: [1, 3], y1: [0.1, 0.5], a: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ y1: "y_1" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-T*y1/a", hint: "That is the force of a piece of string of length a. This piece is only a/2 long, so it is twice as steep." },
    { expr: "2*T*y1/a", hint: "The sign is wrong: when bead 1 is above the line, the string to the wall pulls it down, back toward the line." },
    { expr: "-T*y1/(2*a)", hint: "The slope is the height y₁ divided by the length a/2, which is 2y₁/a, not y₁/(2a)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A bead feels its neighbours only through the slope of the string on each side. If an imaginary bead gives the string next to bead 1 the same slope as the wall does, bead 1 cannot tell the difference.

<Step title="With the wall">

The string runs from height zero on the wall to height $y_1$ at bead 1, over a horizontal distance $a/2$. Its slope is $y_1/(a/2)$, and for small angles the vertical force on bead 1 is the tension times the slope, directed back toward the line:

<Formula label="Force of the wall piece on bead 1" tex="F_{\text{wall}} = -T\,\frac{y_1}{a/2} = -\frac{2T}{a}\,y_1" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="With an imaginary bead 0">

Take the wall away and continue the row of beads to the left without end, all a distance $a$ apart. Bead 0 is then a distance $a$ to the left of bead 1, and the string between them pulls on bead 1 with

<Formula label="Force of bead 0 on bead 1" tex="F_0 = -T\,\frac{y_1 - y_0}{a}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2 (one of the two terms of the bead equation)" />

The two forces are equal at all times exactly when $y_1 - y_0 = 2y_1$, that is when $y_0 = -y_1$. In a normal mode $y_j = A_j\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, so the condition reads

<Formula label="Boundary condition for the left-hand wall" tex="A_0 = -A_1" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.1(a), p. 1" />

Every real bead then obeys the equation of the endless row, and the modes of the endless row that satisfy this condition are the modes of the real system near its left-hand wall.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A picture confirms it. If bead 0 is at $-y_1$ and bead 1 at $+y_1$, the straight string between them crosses height zero halfway, a distance $a/2$ from bead 1. That is where the wall is. The point of the string at the wall never moves, so a wall can be put there without changing anything. As a second check, the equation of bead 1 in the endless row, with $y_0 = -y_1$, is $m\ddot y_1 = \frac{T}{a}(-3y_1 + y_2)$, the same as the sum of the wall force $-2Ty_1/a$ and the force $-T(y_1 - y_2)/a$ of the string to bead 2.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the condition that replaces the right-hand wall in the same way. Type the amplitude $A_{N+1}$ of the imaginary bead $N + 1$ in terms of $A_N$.

<Hint>

The right-hand wall is $a/2$ beyond bead $N$. Repeat the argument of part (a) with an imaginary bead $N + 1$ at a distance $a$ to the right of bead $N$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-bc" label="$A_{N+1} =$" expr="-AN"
  vars={{ AN: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ AN: "A_N" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "AN", hint: "Equal neighbours make the last piece of string flat, so it exerts no force: that describes a free end, not a wall. A wall holds the string at height zero halfway between bead N and bead N + 1." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "A bead that never moves stands for a wall at the place of that bead, a full spacing a beyond bead N. Here the wall is only a/2 beyond it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The right-hand end is the mirror image of the left-hand end.

<Step title="The condition">

The string from bead $N$ to the wall has the slope $-y_N/(a/2)$, so the wall piece pulls on bead $N$ with $-2Ty_N/a$. An imaginary bead $N + 1$ at a distance $a$ pulls with $-T(y_N - y_{N+1})/a$. They agree when $y_{N+1} = -y_N$:

<Formula label="Boundary condition for the right-hand wall" tex="A_{N+1} = -A_N" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Again the straight string between bead $N$ at $y_N$ and bead $N + 1$ at $-y_N$ passes through zero halfway, at the place of the wall. Compare the two other cases in the lecture notes: a wall at the place of bead $N + 1$ gives $y_{N+1} = 0$, and a free end gives $y_{N+1} = y_N$ (Lecture 8 notes, pp. 3 and 4).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={12}>

Find the normal modes and their frequencies. Use the result of the lesson for the endless row: its modes are $A_j \propto e^{\pm ijka}$, both signs with the same frequency, given by

<Formula label="Dispersion relation of the beaded string" tex="\omega^2 = 4\omega_0^2\sin^2\frac{ka}{2}, \qquad \omega_0^2 = \frac{T}{ma}" cite="reading2" at="p. 3" />

Number the modes $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ from the lowest frequency up. Type the allowed values of $ka$, the number of different modes, the angular frequency $\omega_n$ and the shape $A_j$ of mode $n$. Write the shape as a sine with amplitude 1, with the sign that makes bead 1 positive.

<Hint>

Because $e^{ijka}$ and $e^{-ijka}$ have the same frequency, any mix of them is a mode too. A real mix can be written as $A_j = \sin(jka + \alpha)$ with a phase $\alpha$ that is still free.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The condition at the left-hand wall fixes $\alpha$. Use $\sin u + \sin v = 2\sin\frac{u + v}{2}\cos\frac{u - v}{2}$ to bring $A_0 + A_1 = 0$ into a form you can solve.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With that phase, the condition $A_{N+1} + A_N = 0$ at the right-hand wall becomes a condition on $ka$ alone. The same sum formula helps again.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-ka" label="$ka =$" expr="n*pi/N"
  vars={{ n: [1, 4], N: [5, 9] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "n*pi/(N + 1)", hint: "That is the result for walls at the places of bead 0 and bead N + 1, a full spacing beyond the end beads. Here the two walls are only N spacings apart." },
    { expr: "2*n*pi/N", hint: "These values do satisfy the conditions, but they are only every second one: sin(Nka) = 0 also holds when Nka is an odd multiple of π." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-count" label="Number of modes $=$" expr="N"
  vars={{ N: [3, 9] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "N + 1", hint: "N beads have N degrees of freedom, so there are N modes. The value n = N + 1 gives the same pattern as n = N − 1." },
    { expr: "N - 1", hint: "n = N is a real mode: ka = π, and neighbouring beads move opposite to each other. The count equals the number of beads." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-omega" label="$\omega_n =$" expr="2*sqrt(T/(m*a))*sin(n*pi/(2*N))"
  vars={{ T: [1, 3], m: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 2], n: [1, 4], N: [5, 9] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*sqrt(T/(m*a))*sin(n*pi/(2*(N + 1)))", hint: "That belongs to walls a full spacing beyond the end beads, where ka = nπ/(N + 1). Here ka = nπ/N." },
    { expr: "sqrt(T/(m*a))*sin(n*pi/(2*N))", hint: "The dispersion relation has ω² = 4ω₀² sin²(ka/2), so its square root carries a factor 2." },
    { expr: "2*sqrt(T/(m*a))*sin(n*pi/N)", hint: "The sine in the dispersion relation contains half of ka, not ka itself." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-shape" label="$A_j =$" expr="sin((j - 1/2)*n*pi/N)"
  vars={{ j: [1, 5], n: [1, 3], N: [5, 8] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sin(j*n*pi/N)", hint: "This shape is zero at j = 0, so it puts the left-hand wall at the place of bead 0. The wall is halfway between bead 0 and bead 1." },
    { expr: "sin(j*n*pi/(N + 1))", hint: "That is the mode of a string with walls at the places of bead 0 and bead N + 1. Here both walls are half a spacing closer." },
    { expr: "cos((j - 1/2)*n*pi/N)", hint: "A cosine of (j − ½)ka is the same for bead 0 and bead 1: that is the shape for free ends. At a wall the two must be opposite." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The endless row gives the dispersion relation and the building blocks $e^{\pm ijka}$. The two wall conditions then pick the phase of the standing wave and the allowed values of $ka$.

<Step title="The left-hand wall fixes the phase">

Take $A_j = \sin(jka + \alpha)$. The condition $A_0 = -A_1$ is

<Formula label="Left-hand condition" tex="\sin\alpha + \sin(ka + \alpha) = 2\sin\left(\frac{ka}{2} + \alpha\right)\cos\frac{ka}{2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha = -\frac{ka}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

So $A_j = \sin\left[(j - \tfrac12)ka\right]$. This is a sine wave that is zero at $j = \tfrac12$, which is the place of the wall: with $x = (j - \tfrac12)a$ measured from the left-hand wall, the shape is simply $\sin kx$. (The other way to satisfy the condition, $\cos\frac{ka}{2} = 0$, is $ka = \pi$. It appears below as the mode $n = N$.)

</Step>

<Step title="The right-hand wall fixes ka">

The condition $A_{N+1} = -A_N$ becomes

<Formula label="Right-hand condition" tex="\sin\left[\left(N + \tfrac12\right)ka\right] + \sin\left[\left(N - \tfrac12\right)ka\right] = 2\sin(Nka)\cos\frac{ka}{2} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad ka = \frac{n\pi}{N}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The value $n = 0$ gives $A_j = 0$ for every bead, which is no motion at all. The values $n = 1, 2, \dots, N$ give $N$ different patterns. Beyond that nothing new appears: $n = N + 1$ gives the same pattern as $n = N - 1$, and so on. This is as it must be, because $N$ beads have $N$ degrees of freedom.

</Step>

<Step title="Modes and frequencies">

<Formula label="Normal modes of the beaded string between the two walls" tex="y_j^{(n)}(t) = C_n\,\sin\left[\left(j - \tfrac12\right)\frac{n\pi}{N}\right]\cos(\omega_n t + \phi_n), \qquad \omega_n = 2\sqrt{\frac{T}{ma}}\,\sin\frac{n\pi}{2N}, \qquad n = 1, 2, \dots, N" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.1(c), p. 1 (our own solution, with the dispersion relation of Lecture 8 notes p. 3)" />

The constants $C_n$ and $\phi_n$ are fixed by the initial conditions.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

One bead, $N = 1$: the bead sits between two pieces of string of length $a/2$, so $m\ddot y_1 = -4Ty_1/a$ and $\omega = 2\omega_0$. The formula gives $2\omega_0\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 2\omega_0$.

Two beads, $N = 2$: the equations are $m\ddot y_1 = \frac{T}{a}(-3y_1 + y_2)$ and $m\ddot y_2 = \frac{T}{a}(y_1 - 3y_2)$. Moving together ($y_1 = y_2$) gives $\omega^2 = 2\omega_0^2$, and moving opposite ($y_1 = -y_2$) gives $\omega^2 = 4\omega_0^2$. The formula gives $\omega_1 = 2\omega_0\sin\frac{\pi}{4} = 1.414\,\omega_0$ and $\omega_2 = 2\omega_0$, with the shapes $(\sin\frac{\pi}{4}, \sin\frac{3\pi}{4})$, two equal numbers, and $(\sin\frac{\pi}{2}, \sin\frac{3\pi}{2}) = (1, -1)$.

Three beads, $N = 3$: the formula gives $\omega^2/\omega_0^2 = 1$, $3$ and $4$. Their sum is 8 and their product is 12, which are the trace and the determinant of the matrix of the three equations of motion, with rows $(3, -1, 0)$, $(-1, 2, -1)$ and $(0, -1, 3)$.

For $n$ much smaller than $N$ the sine is nearly its argument, and $\omega_n \approx \frac{n\pi}{Na}\sqrt{\frac{Ta}{m}}$: the frequencies of a continuous string of length $Na$ with mass $m/a$ per unit length, fixed at both ends.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Two torsional pendulums and a reflection" points={25} mit="Problem 4.2">

A physicist studies two coupled torsional pendulums. She writes the equations of motion in terms of the angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ of the two pendulums from their equilibrium positions, in the matrix form $\ddot X = -M^{-1}K\,X$ of the lessons, where $X$ is the column with the entries $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$. She does not show us the matrix $M^{-1}K$. She tells us only that it commutes with the reflection matrix

<Formula label="The reflection matrix" tex="S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad [S, M^{-1}K] = SM^{-1}K - M^{-1}KS = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.2, p. 1" />

In a normal mode $X = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, where the column $A$ has the entries $A_1$ and $A_2$. Assume, as the lesson does, that the two normal-mode frequencies are different.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

How many normal modes does the system have?

<Hint>

Count the coordinates that are needed to say where everything is.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-count" label="Number of normal modes $=$" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-count" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A system of coupled oscillators has as many normal modes as it has degrees of freedom.

<Step title="Count">

Two angles, $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$, describe the system completely, so $M^{-1}K$ is a 2 × 2 matrix. It has two eigenvectors, and each is a normal mode: there are **two** normal modes.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The general motion needs four constants: two starting angles and two starting angular velocities. Two modes, each with an amplitude and a phase, supply exactly four.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={17}>

Find the amplitude ratio $A_1/A_2$ in each normal mode. Label the modes by the eigenvalue $\beta$ of $S$ that belongs to them ($SA = \beta A$).

<Hint>

When $S$ commutes with $M^{-1}K$ and the mode frequencies differ, every normal mode is also an eigenvector of $S$. So you can solve the easy problem $SA = \beta A$ in place of the eigenvalue problem of the unknown matrix.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Apply $S$ twice. What is $S^2$, and what does that say about $\beta^2$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For each value of $\beta$, write out the first row of $SA = \beta A$. It links $A_2$ to $A_1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-r1" label="For $\beta = -1$: $A_1/A_2 =$" value="1" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-r1" />

<Answer id="p2-r2" label="For $\beta = +1$: $A_1/A_2 =$" value="-1" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-r2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The symmetry does the work. The normal modes are the eigenvectors of $S$, whatever the springs and the moments of inertia are.

<Step title="Why the modes are eigenvectors of S">

Let $A$ be a normal mode, $M^{-1}KA = \omega^2A$. Because $S$ commutes with $M^{-1}K$,

<Formula label="S turns a mode into a mode of the same frequency" tex="M^{-1}K\,(SA) = S\,M^{-1}KA = \omega^2\,(SA)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

So $SA$ is a mode with the same frequency. The two frequencies differ, so only one direction belongs to each frequency, and $SA$ must be a multiple of $A$: $SA = \beta A$.

</Step>

<Step title="The eigenvalues of S">

Reflecting twice changes nothing: $S^2$ is the unit matrix. Then $S^2A = \beta^2A = A$, so $\beta = \pm 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="The eigenvectors">

The first row of $SA = \beta A$ reads $-A_2 = \beta A_1$, so $A_1/A_2 = -1/\beta$:

<Formula label="The two normal modes" tex="\beta = -1:\ \frac{A_1}{A_2} = 1, \quad A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}; \qquad \beta = +1:\ \frac{A_1}{A_2} = -1, \quad A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

In one mode the two pendulums turn by equal angles in the same sense. In the other they turn by equal angles in opposite senses. The symmetry alone cannot say which of the two has the higher frequency: for that the matrix $M^{-1}K$ itself is needed.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Work out what the condition says about the unknown matrix (this check is our own addition). Write $M^{-1}K$ with the rows $(p, q)$ and $(r, s)$. Then $SM^{-1}K$ has the rows $(-r, -s)$ and $(-p, -q)$, and $M^{-1}KS$ has the rows $(-q, -p)$ and $(-s, -r)$. They are equal when $r = q$ and $s = p$: the matrix has the rows $(p, q)$ and $(q, p)$. Acting on $(1, 1)$ it gives $(p + q)$ times $(1, 1)$, and on $(1, -1)$ it gives $(p - q)$ times $(1, -1)$. Both are eigenvectors, as found, with $\omega^2 = p + q$ and $p - q$. The frequencies differ as long as the coupling $q$ is not zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A string with a sliding ring at each end" points={25} mit="Problem 4.3">

A uniform thin string has length $L$ and mass $\mu$ per unit length. (The lessons write $\rho_L$ for this quantity.) Each end is tied to a massless ring that slides without friction on a vertical rod, and the tension in the string is $T$. Measure $x$ along the string at rest, from $x = 0$ at the left-hand rod to $x = L$ at the right-hand rod, and call $y(x, t)$ the small sideways (vertical) displacement. (MIT's Figure 2 shows the string stretched horizontally between the two vertical rods, with a ring at each end.)

<Part label="a" points={13}>

Find the normal modes and their frequencies for small transverse oscillations. Number the oscillating modes $m = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ from the lowest frequency up. Type the wave number $k_m$, the angular frequency $\omega_m$, and the shape of mode $m$ as a function of $x$, with amplitude 1 and positive at $x = 0$.

<Hint>

A normal mode of the wave equation has the form $\sin(\omega_m t + \beta_m)$ times a sine or cosine of $k_m x$, with $\omega_m = v_p k_m$ and $v_p = \sqrt{T/\mu}$. The boundary conditions pick the shape and the allowed $k_m$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

What can a frictionless rod do to a massless ring? It can push only sideways (horizontally), never up or down. A ring without mass cannot feel a net vertical force, so the string must not pull it up or down. What does that say about the slope of the string at the ring?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The slope $\partial y/\partial x$ must vanish at $x = 0$ and at $x = L$. Which of the two, sine or cosine, has zero slope at $x = 0$?

</Hint>

    { expr: "cos((2*m - 1)*pi*x/(2*L))", hint: "This shape is zero at x = L, which would be a fixed end. Both ends are free here." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A free end cannot be pulled up or down by the string, so the string arrives level at each rod. Between two such ends only cosines with a whole number of half wavelengths fit.

<Step title="The boundary conditions">

The rod is frictionless, so it pushes on the ring only horizontally. The only vertical force on the ring is the vertical part of the tension, $T$ times the slope of the string at the ring. The ring has no mass, so this force must be zero, or its acceleration would be infinite:

<Formula label="Free ends" tex="\frac{\partial y}{\partial x}(0, t) = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial y}{\partial x}(L, t) = 0" cite="reading3" at="p. 3 (the open end of the notes' example)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The shape">

A normal mode has the form of the lesson,

<Formula label="A normal mode of the string" tex="y_m(x, t) = A_m\sin(\omega_m t + \beta_m)\sin(k_m x + \alpha_m), \qquad \omega_m = v_p k_m, \quad v_p = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}" cite="reading3" at="p. 2" />

Its slope is proportional to $\cos(k_m x + \alpha_m)$. Zero slope at $x = 0$ gives $\alpha_m = \pi/2$, which turns the sine into $\cos k_m x$. Zero slope at $x = L$ then requires $\sin k_m L = 0$:

<Formula label="Normal modes with two free ends" tex="k_m = \frac{m\pi}{L}, \qquad \omega_m = \frac{m\pi}{L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}, \qquad y_m(x, t) = A_m\cos\frac{m\pi x}{L}\,\sin(\omega_m t + \beta_m), \qquad m = 1, 2, 3, \dots" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

There are infinitely many modes, one for each $m$. The amplitudes $A_m$ and the phases $\beta_m$ come from the initial conditions.

</Step>

<Step title="The case m = 0">

The value $k = 0$ also satisfies both conditions. Its shape is a constant and its frequency is zero: the whole string moves up or down along the rods as one piece, at a constant speed, because nothing holds it in place. It is a solution, but not an oscillation, so it is not counted among the oscillating modes. (This remark is our own addition.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\pi/L$ is 1/m and $\sqrt{T/\mu}$ is m/s, so $\omega_m$ is 1/s. The wavelength of mode $m$ is $2\pi/k_m = 2L/m$, so $m$ half wavelengths fit on the string, the same count as for a string with two fixed ends. The frequencies are the same as for fixed ends too. Only the shapes differ: here the ends are places of greatest motion, there they are places of no motion.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A string released from a zigzag" points={25} mit="Problem 4.4">

A string with mass $\mu$ per unit length is stretched with tension $T$ between two walls at $x = -L$ and $x = L$, where it is held fixed. It is pulled into a zigzag: up to the height $A$ at $x = -L/2$ and down to $-A$ at $x = L/2$, with straight pieces between. The height $A$ is very small compared with $L$. As a formula, the starting shape is

<Formula label="The starting shape" tex="y(x, 0) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{2A}{L}\,(x + L) & -L \le x \le -\dfrac{L}{2} \\[2mm] -\dfrac{2A}{L}\,x & -\dfrac{L}{2} \le x \le \dfrac{L}{2} \\[2mm] \dfrac{2A}{L}\,(x - L) & \dfrac{L}{2} \le x \le L \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.4, Figure 3, p. 2 (the shape of the figure, written as a formula)" />

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-1, 1]} range={[-1.3, 1.3]} xLabel="x / L" yLabel="y / A"
  curves={[
    { expr: "x < -0.5 ? 2*(x + 1) : (x < 0.5 ? -2*x : 2*(x - 1))" }
  ]}
  caption="The starting shape of the string, drawn from the formula above, with x in units of L and y in units of A. The height is greatly exaggerated." />

At $t = 0$ the string is let go from rest: $\partial y/\partial t = 0$ at $t = 0$ for every $x$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

How many normal modes does this system have?

<Hint>

Compare with the beaded string: $N$ beads have $N$ modes. How many "beads" does a continuous string have?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-count" choices={["Two, one for each half of the string", "Three, one for each straight piece", "Four", "Infinitely many"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The number of modes is the number of degrees of freedom, and it does not depend on how the string is started.

<Step title="Count">

A continuous string is the limit of a beaded string with more and more beads. Every point of it can move, so it has infinitely many degrees of freedom and **infinitely many** normal modes: one for each $m = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ in part (b).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The starting shape decides only how strongly each mode takes part. As part (b) shows, most modes are not excited at all by this zigzag, but they are still modes of the string.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={14}>

Number the modes $m = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ from the lowest frequency up, and write the shape of mode $m$ as $\sin[k_m(x + L)]$, which is zero at the left-hand wall and positive just to the right of it. Find the Fourier coefficients $A_m$ of the expansion of the starting shape in these modes, and write the full series $y(x, t)$ for $t > 0$. Type the angular frequency $\omega_m$, choose which modes take part, and give the two largest coefficients as numbers, $A_2/A$ and $A_6/A$.

<Hint>

The string is $2L$ long and fixed at both ends, so $\sin[k_m(x + L)]$ must also vanish at $x = L$. That gives $k_m$, and $\omega_m = v_p k_m$. Because the string starts from rest, each mode moves as $\cos\omega_m t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The coefficients follow from the orthogonality of the sines, as in the lesson. Over the length $2L$ the integral of $\sin^2[k_m(x + L)]$ is $L$, so $A_m = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} y(x, 0)\sin[k_m(x + L)]\,dx$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Symmetry saves most of the work. The starting shape is odd in $x$: $y(-x, 0) = -y(x, 0)$. Is the shape of mode $m$ odd or even in $x$? Treat odd $m$ and even $m$ apart.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-omega" label="$\omega_m =$" expr="m*pi/(2*L)*sqrt(T/mu)"
  vars={{ m: [1, 5], L: [0.5, 2], T: [1, 4], mu: [0.3, 2] }}
  names={{ mu: "\\mu" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*pi/L*sqrt(T/mu)", hint: "The string runs from −L to L, so its length is 2L, not L. That halves every wave number." },
    { expr: "m*pi/(2*L)*sqrt(mu/T)", hint: "Check the units: the square root of μ/T is one over a speed. The frequency is the wave number times √(T/μ)." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-which" choices={["Every mode", "Only odd m", "Every even m", "Only m = 2, 6, 10, …", "Only m = 4, 8, 12, …"]} answer={3} />

<Answer id="p4-a2" label="$A_2/A =$" value="0.8106" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-a2" />

<Answer id="p4-a6" label="$A_6/A =$" value="-0.09006" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-a6" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The modes of a string with fixed ends are sines. Projecting the starting shape on each of them gives its coefficient, and the symmetry of the zigzag makes most of them vanish.

<Step title="The modes">

The shape $\sin[k_m(x + L)]$ vanishes at $x = -L$. It vanishes at $x = L$ when $2k_mL = m\pi$:

<Formula label="Modes of the string of length 2L with fixed ends" tex="k_m = \frac{m\pi}{2L}, \qquad \omega_m = v_p k_m = \frac{m\pi}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}, \qquad m = 1, 2, 3, \dots" cite="reading3" at="p. 2 (ω_m = v_p k_m; the boundary conditions are those of this problem)" />

The general motion is a sum of modes $A_m\sin[k_m(x + L)]\sin(\omega_m t + \beta_m)$. Its velocity at $t = 0$ contains $\cos\beta_m$, and it is zero for every $x$ only if $\beta_m = \pi/2$ for every mode (or $-\pi/2$, which only flips the sign of $A_m$), as in the lesson. So each mode moves as $\cos\omega_m t$:

<Formula label="The expansion" tex="y(x, t) = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty} A_m\sin\left[k_m(x + L)\right]\cos\omega_m t, \qquad A_m = \frac{1}{L}\int_{-L}^{L} y(x, 0)\,\sin\left[k_m(x + L)\right]dx" cite="reading3" at="p. 5 (general solution and Fourier coefficients, here for a string of length 2L)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Symmetry">

Write $\sin[k_m(x + L)] = \sin\left(\frac{m\pi x}{2L} + \frac{m\pi}{2}\right)$. For odd $m$ this is plus or minus $\cos\frac{m\pi x}{2L}$, an even function of $x$. The starting shape is odd, so the integral over the symmetric range vanishes: $A_m = 0$ for every odd $m$.

For even $m = 2n$ the shape is $(-1)^n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}$, an odd function. Odd times odd is even, so the integral is twice the integral over the right-hand half:

<Formula label="Coefficients of the even modes" tex="A_{2n} = (-1)^n\,\frac{2}{L}\int_0^{L} y(x, 0)\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.4(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

On the right-hand half, $y(x, 0) = -A\,g(x)$, where $g$ is a triangle of height 1 with its top at $x = L/2$: $g = 2x/L$ up to the top and $g = 2(L - x)/L$ after it. Integrating by parts on the first piece, with $q = n\pi/L$:

<Formula label="First half of the triangle" tex="\int_0^{L/2}\frac{2x}{L}\sin qx\,dx = \frac{2}{L}\left[-\frac{x\cos qx}{q} + \frac{\sin qx}{q^2}\right]_0^{L/2} = -\frac{\cos\frac{n\pi}{2}}{q} + \frac{2\sin\frac{n\pi}{2}}{L\,q^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.4(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The second piece is the mirror image of the first about $x = L/2$, and under that mirror $\sin qx$ turns into $-(-1)^n\sin qx$. So for odd $n$ the two pieces are equal, and for even $n$ they cancel. For odd $n$ the cosine term is zero, and

<Formula label="The triangle projected on a sine" tex="\int_0^{L} g(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx = \frac{4L}{n^2\pi^2}\,\sin\frac{n\pi}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.4(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

which also gives the correct value, zero, for even $n$.

</Step>

<Step title="The coefficients">

Putting the pieces together, $A_{2n} = -(-1)^n\,\frac{8A}{n^2\pi^2}\sin\frac{n\pi}{2}$. It is zero for even $n$, and for odd $n$ the factor $-(-1)^n$ is $+1$:

<Formula label="Fourier coefficients of the zigzag" tex="A_m = \begin{cases} \dfrac{32A}{\pi^2m^2}\,\sin\dfrac{m\pi}{4} & m = 2, 6, 10, \dots \\[2mm] 0 & \text{every other } m \end{cases} \qquad A_2 = \frac{8A}{\pi^2} = 0.8106\,A, \quad A_6 = -\frac{8A}{9\pi^2} = -0.09006\,A" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.4(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Only the modes $m = 2, 6, 10, \dots$ take part. Their signs alternate and their sizes fall as $1/m^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The motion">

For $m = 2n$ with $n$ odd, $A_m\sin[k_m(x + L)] = -A_m\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}$, so the series can be written in two ways:

<Formula label="The motion of the string" tex="y(x, t) = \sum_{m = 2, 6, 10, \dots}\frac{32A}{\pi^2m^2}\sin\frac{m\pi}{4}\,\sin\left[\frac{m\pi(x + L)}{2L}\right]\cos\left(\frac{m\pi}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}\;t\right) = -\frac{8A}{\pi^2}\sum_{n = 1, 3, 5, \dots}\frac{\sin\frac{n\pi}{2}}{n^2}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\cos\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}}\;t\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4.4(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At the first corner, $x = -L/2$ and $t = 0$, the second form gives $\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L} = -\sin\frac{n\pi}{2}$, so every term is positive: $y = \frac{8A}{\pi^2}\left(1 + \frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{25} + \dots\right)$. The first three terms already give $0.9331\,A$, and the whole sum of $1/n^2$ over the odd numbers is $\pi^2/8$, so $y = A$, as it must be.

The zigzag is one full period of a triangle wave of period $2L$, which is why it contains only the sines $\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}$, and of those only the odd $n$. All the modes that take part have frequencies that are odd multiples of $\omega_2 = \frac{\pi}{L}\sqrt{T/\mu}$. So the motion repeats after the time $2\pi/\omega_2 = 2L/v_p$, and after half that time every cosine equals $-1$: the string is at rest again in the upside-down zigzag.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
