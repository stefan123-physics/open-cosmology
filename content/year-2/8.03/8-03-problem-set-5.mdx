This set practises the lessons on the [normal modes of a string and Fourier series](/courses/8-03/standing-waves-fourier-series) and on [travelling waves, energy and reflection](/courses/8-03/travelling-waves-reflection). The five problems follow MIT's Homework 5, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for its homeworks, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. MIT's sheet has a drawing for three of the problems (2, 4 and 5); here each situation is described in words, so make your own sketch first. All five problems count for the score (100 points).

Notation: the mass of a string per unit length is $\rho_L$ in every problem (MIT writes $\rho$ in Problem 3), and the wave speed is $v$ (the lessons write $v_p$). The displacement of the string is $\psi$ in Problems 1 and 4 (and, our choice, in Problem 2) and $y$ in Problems 3 and 5, as on MIT's sheet. In Problem 4 the transmission coefficient is called $\tau$, as in the lesson, because $T$ is the tension (MIT's sheet calls it $T$).

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The energy of a vibrating string" points={20} mit="Problem 5.1">

A string of length $L$ and total mass $M$ is stretched with tension $T$ between two fixed ends, at $x = 0$ and $x = L$. Its displacement $\psi(x, t)$ is small and transverse.

<Part label="a" points={12}>

The string vibrates in its $n$th normal mode with amplitude $A$ (the largest displacement of the string, reached at the antinodes). First, as a step of our own, give the angular frequency $\omega_n$ of that mode. Then find the total energy $E_n$ of the vibration. Write both in terms of $n$, $A$, $T$, $L$ and $M$ (not every symbol has to appear).

MIT's hint: look at the instant at which the string is straight. The vibration then has no potential energy, so all the energy is kinetic.

<Hint>

With both ends fixed the mode shape is a sine that vanishes at $x = 0$ and at $x = L$. Which wave numbers $k_n$ allow that? The frequency follows from $\omega_n = v k_n$, and the speed $v$ from the tension and the mass per unit length, $M/L$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write the mode as $\psi = A\sin(k_n x)\cos(\omega_n t)$. When the string is straight, every point moves at its largest speed, $A\omega_n\sin(k_n x)$ in size. Add up the kinetic energy $\tfrac12\,dm\,(\text{speed})^2$ of all the pieces $dm = (M/L)\,dx$ of the string.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-wn" label="$\omega_n =$" expr="n*pi*sqrt(T/(M*L))"
  vars={{ n: [1, 5], T: [1, 4], M: [0.5, 2], L: [1.5, 3] }}
  names={{ n: "n", T: "T", M: "M", L: "L" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "n*sqrt(T/(M*L))/2", hint: "That is the frequency in hertz, f = ω/2π. The box asks for the angular frequency." },
    { expr: "(2*n - 1)*pi*sqrt(T/(M*L))/2", hint: "That is the string of the lesson, with one end fixed and one end free. Here both ends are fixed, so the sine must vanish at x = L as well." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-e" label="$E_n =$" expr="n^2*pi^2*T*A^2/(4*L)"
  vars={{ n: [1, 5], T: [1, 4], A: [0.5, 2], L: [1.5, 3], M: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ n: "n", T: "T", A: "A", L: "L", M: "M" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "0.5*M*A^2*(n*pi*sqrt(T/(M*L)))^2", hint: "This is ½Mω²A², the energy if the whole mass moved with the full amplitude A. The speed follows the sine shape of the mode, and the square of a sine averages to one half over the string." },
    { expr: "pi^2*T*A^2/(4*L)", hint: "This is the energy of the lowest mode. Mode n has a frequency n times as high, and the kinetic energy goes with the square of the frequency." },
    { expr: "n^2*pi^2*T*A^2/(8*L)", hint: "This is the energy averaged over a cycle of only the kinetic part (or only the potential part). At the instant the string is straight, the kinetic energy is the whole energy, and it is twice this." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a normal mode every piece of the string is a harmonic oscillator with the same frequency and phase. When the string passes through its straight position, all the energy is kinetic, so it is enough to add up the kinetic energy at that instant.

<Step title="The mode and its frequency">

A normal mode has the form $A\sin(k x + \alpha)$ times a harmonic function of time (Lecture 9 notes, p. 2). The fixed end at $x = 0$ gives $\alpha = 0$, as in the lesson. The second fixed end needs $\sin(kL) = 0$:

<Formula label="Normal modes of a string with two fixed ends" tex="\psi_n(x, t) = A\sin(k_n x)\cos(\omega_n t), \qquad k_n = \frac{n\pi}{L}, \qquad \omega_n = v\,k_n, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.1(a), p. 1 (our own solution, with the method of Lecture 9 notes, pp. 2 and 3)" />

The wave speed is $v = \sqrt{T/\rho_L}$ (Lecture 9 notes, p. 1), and here $\rho_L = M/L$:

<Formula label="Frequency of mode n" tex="\omega_n = \frac{n\pi}{L}\sqrt{\frac{T L}{M}} = n\pi\sqrt{\frac{T}{M L}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The kinetic energy when the string is straight">

The velocity of the string is $\partial\psi/\partial t = -A\omega_n\sin(k_n x)\sin(\omega_n t)$. The string is straight when $\cos(\omega_n t) = 0$, and then $\sin(\omega_n t) = \pm 1$. The kinetic energy of a string is the integral of $\tfrac12\rho_L(\partial\psi/\partial t)^2$ along it:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy at that instant" tex="E_n = \int_0^L \frac{\rho_L}{2}\,A^2\omega_n^2\sin^2\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)dx = \frac{\rho_L}{2}\,A^2\omega_n^2\cdot\frac{L}{2} = \frac14\,M A^2\omega_n^2" cite="reading2" at="p. 3 (the kinetic energy of a string; the integral is our own)" />

The integral of $\sin^2$ over a whole number of half waves is half the length, $L/2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The result">

Put in $\omega_n^2 = n^2\pi^2 T/(ML)$:

<Formula label="Energy of mode n" tex="E_n = \frac14\,M A^2\omega_n^2 = \frac{n^2\pi^2\,T A^2}{4L}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: N m²/m = N m, a joule. The mass has dropped out, which makes sense at the other special instant: at the largest displacement the string is at rest and all the energy is potential, $\int_0^L \tfrac12 T(\partial\psi/\partial x)^2\,dx = \tfrac12 T A^2 k_n^2\cdot\tfrac{L}{2} = n^2\pi^2 TA^2/(4L)$. That is the same value, found from the tension and the shape alone. Compare also with one oscillator of mass $m$, whose energy is $\tfrac12 m\omega^2A^2$: the string counts as a mass $M/2$, because most of it moves less than the antinodes.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

The same string now vibrates in two modes at once:

<Formula label="Two modes together" tex="\psi(x, t) = A_1\sin\!\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right)\cos(\omega_1 t) + A_3\sin\!\left(\frac{3\pi x}{L}\right)\cos\!\left(\omega_3 t - \frac{\pi}{4}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.1(b), p. 1" />

Find the total energy of the vibration, in terms of $A_1$, $A_3$, $T$ and $L$. MIT lets you assume that it is the sum of the energies of the two modes taken separately. (The solution shows why that is allowed.)

<Hint>

Use the result of part (a) twice, once with $n = 1$ and once with $n = 3$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The energy of a mode does not change in time, so ask yourself whether the phase $\pi/4$ can matter.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e2" label="$E =$" expr="pi^2*T*(A1^2 + 9*A3^2)/(4*L)"
  vars={{ T: [1, 4], A1: [0.5, 2], A3: [0.5, 2], L: [1.5, 3] }}
  names={{ T: "T", A1: "A_1", A3: "A_3", L: "L" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi^2*T*(A1^2 + 3*A3^2)/(4*L)", hint: "The energy goes with the square of the mode number, so the third mode counts 3² = 9 times, not 3 times." },
    { expr: "pi^2*T*(A1 + 3*A3)^2/(4*L)", hint: "This adds the amplitudes before squaring, which brings in a cross term with A₁A₃. The cross term is an integral of the product of two different mode shapes along the string, and that integral is zero." },
    { expr: "pi^2*T*(A1^2 + 4.5*A3^2)/(4*L)", hint: "The phase π/4 only shifts the third mode in time. Its energy is constant in time, so the phase does not reduce it." },
    { expr: "pi^2*T*(A1^2 + A3^2)/(4*L)", hint: "The two modes have different frequencies: ω₃ = 3ω₁. For the same amplitude the faster mode has more energy." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each mode keeps its own energy, and for two different modes the energies simply add, because the mode shapes are orthogonal.

<Step title="Add the two energies">

With the result of part (a) for $n = 1$ and $n = 3$:

<Formula label="Energy of the two modes" tex="E = E_1 + E_3 = \frac{\pi^2 T A_1^2}{4L} + \frac{9\pi^2 T A_3^2}{4L} = \frac{\pi^2 T}{4L}\left(A_1^2 + 9A_3^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The phase $\pi/4$ does not appear: the energy of a mode is the same at every time, so shifting the mode in time changes nothing.

</Step>

<Step title="Why the energies add (our own addition)">

The kinetic energy contains $(\partial\psi/\partial t)^2$, the square of a sum of two terms. Besides the two squares it has a cross term proportional to

<Formula label="The cross term vanishes" tex="\int_0^L \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi x}{L}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{3\pi x}{L}\right)dx = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 5 (orthogonality of the sine functions, there for a string with one free end; the same holds for two fixed ends)" />

The potential energy contains $(\partial\psi/\partial x)^2$, whose cross term has the two cosines instead, and that integral is zero as well. So at every time the total energy is the sum of two parts, one for each mode, and each part is constant.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $A_3 = 0$ the result is $E_1$ of part (a), and for $A_1 = 0$ it is $E_3$. For equal amplitudes the third mode holds nine times the energy of the first: it has three times the frequency and three times the slope.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A square pulse released from rest" points={20} mit="Problem 5.2">

A string of length $L$, tension $T$ and mass per unit length $\rho_L$ is fixed at both ends, $x = 0$ and $x = L$. It is held in the shape of a square pulse: the piece between $x = 3L/8$ and $x = 5L/8$ is displaced by a height $h$, and the rest is not displaced. (MIT's Figure 1 shows this shape: a pulse of width $L/4$ centred on the middle of the string.) The string can move only up and down. At $t = 0$ it is released, with zero velocity everywhere.

Treat the square pulse as an ideal shape, as MIT does: a real string cannot have vertical edges.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

What is the speed $v$ of a travelling wave on this string?

<Hint>

The wave equation of the string has $v^2$ as the factor in front of $\partial^2\psi/\partial x^2$. Which two properties of the string does it contain?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-v" label="$v =$" expr="sqrt(T/rho)"
  vars={{ T: [1, 4], rho: [0.3, 0.8] }}
  names={{ T: "T", rho: "\\rho_L" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(rho/T)", hint: "Check the units: (kg/m)/N is s²/m², so this is one over a speed. A tighter string carries waves faster, not slower." },
    { expr: "T/rho", hint: "That is v². Its units are m²/s², not m/s." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The speed is fixed by the string alone: the tension pulls a displaced piece back, the mass per unit length resists.

<Step title="The speed">

<Formula label="Wave speed on a string" tex="v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho_L}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: N/(kg/m) = m²/s², and its square root is a speed. The two times of parts (b) and (c) are therefore $t = L/(4v)$ and $t = L/v$: the times a wave needs to travel a quarter of the string and the whole string.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

Draw to scale the shape of the string at

<Formula label="The first time" tex="t = \frac{L}{4}\sqrt{\frac{\rho_L}{T}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.2(b), p. 1" />

Then answer, as a check of our own, for that time: what is the height of the displaced pieces, in units of $h$, and where is the centre of the right-hand displaced piece, in units of $L$?

<Hint>

A shape released from rest is the sum of two travelling waves. What are their shapes, and in which directions do they move?

</Hint>

<Hint>

How far has each of them moved at this time? Check whether either has reached a wall yet.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b-height" label="height $/h =$" value="0.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-b-height" />

<Answer id="p2-b-centre" label="centre $/L =$" value="0.75" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-b-centre" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A string released from rest in a shape $f(x)$ moves as two copies of that shape at half the height, one travelling to the right and one to the left.

<Step title="Two half pulses">

<Formula label="A shape released from rest" tex="\psi(x, t) = \tfrac12 f(x - vt) + \tfrac12 f(x + vt)" cite="reading2" at="p. 4 (a stationary shape is two progressing waves; the factor ½ is written out by us)" />

At $t = 0$ this is $f(x)$, and its velocity is $-\tfrac12 v f' + \tfrac12 v f' = 0$, as required. The formula holds as it stands until a pulse reaches a wall.

</Step>

<Step title="Where they are">

At the given time each half pulse has moved a distance $vt = L/4$. The pulse going right, which started between $3L/8$ and $5L/8$, now lies between $5L/8$ and $7L/8$, with its centre at $3L/4$. The pulse going left lies between $L/8$ and $3L/8$, with its centre at $L/4$. Both have height $h/2$ and width $L/4$. They have just separated: the middle piece, between $3L/8$ and $5L/8$, is flat. Neither has reached a wall, since both are still $L/8$ away from it.

  ]}
  caption="The string at t = L/(4v) (solid) and at the start (dashed), drawn from the formula above: two pulses of height h/2 and width L/4, centred at L/4 and 3L/4." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The area between the string and its rest position was $h \times L/4$ at the start. Now it is $2 \times (h/2) \times (L/4)$, the same, as it must be while nothing has touched the walls. At the given instant the right-hand edge of the left pulse and the left-hand edge of the right pulse stand exactly where the edges of the starting pulse stood.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={8}>

Draw to scale the shape of the string at

<Formula label="The second time" tex="t = L\sqrt{\frac{\rho_L}{T}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.2(c), p. 1" />

Then answer, as a check of our own, for that time: what is the displacement of the middle of the string, $x = L/2$, in units of $h$? Mind the sign.

<Hint>

Each half pulse has now travelled a distance $L$. Follow the one going right: how far is it to the wall, what does a fixed end do to a pulse, and where is the pulse at the end?

</Hint>

<Hint>

A fixed end sends a pulse back upside down, with the same size. Do the same for the pulse going left, and add the two.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c-mid" label="$\psi(L/2)/h =$" value="-1" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-c-mid" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each half pulse reflects once from a fixed end, which turns it upside down, and at this time the two meet again in the middle.

<Step title="Follow the half pulses">

The centre of the pulse going right starts at $L/2$. It travels $L/2$ to the wall at $x = L$ and is reflected there. A fixed end reflects with $R = -1$ (Lecture 10 notes, p. 6): the pulse comes back upside down, with the same size and shape. It then travels the remaining $L/2$ back, so at $t = L/v$ its centre is at $L/2$ again, and it is moving to the left. By symmetry the pulse going left does the same at the wall $x = 0$ and is back at the centre, upside down and moving to the right.

</Step>

<Step title="Add them">

Two pulses of height $-h/2$, both between $3L/8$ and $5L/8$, add up to the starting shape upside down:

<Formula label="The string after one crossing time" tex="\psi\!\left(x, \frac{L}{v}\right) = -\,\psi(x, 0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.2(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The string is flat except between $3L/8$ and $5L/8$, where the displacement is $-h$. At this instant the string is at rest for a moment, because the two half pulses move in opposite directions and their velocities cancel, as at $t = 0$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 1]} range={[-1.2, 1.2]} xLabel="x / L" yLabel="ψ / h"
  curves={[
    { expr: "(x >= 0.375 and x <= 0.625) ? -1 : 0", label: "t = L/v" },
    { expr: "(x >= 0.375 and x <= 0.625) ? 1 : 0", label: "t = 0", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="The string at t = L/v (solid) and at the start (dashed), drawn from the result above: the starting pulse upside down." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The same follows from the normal modes. Mode $m$ of a string with two fixed ends has $\omega_m = m\pi v/L$, and a string released from rest has every mode proportional to $\cos(\omega_m t)$. At $t = L/v$ that factor is $\cos(m\pi)$: $-1$ for odd $m$ and $+1$ for even $m$. The starting shape is symmetric about the middle of the string, so it contains only the odd modes, and every one of them has changed sign. After twice this time, $2L/v$, all the factors are $+1$ and the starting shape is back: that is the period of the lowest mode.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The energy of a travelling pulse" points={20} mit="Problem 5.3">

A long string of mass per unit length $\rho_L$ is under tension $T$. A pulse travels along it, with the vertical displacement

<Formula label="A travelling Gaussian pulse" tex="y(x, t) = y_0\exp\!\left[-\frac12\left(\frac{x - vt}{\sigma}\right)^2\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.3, p. 1" />

Here $y_0$ is the height of the pulse, $\sigma$ sets its width and $v = \sqrt{T/\rho_L}$ is the wave speed. The energy of the wave is the kinetic energy of the moving string plus the potential energy of its deformation, each added up over the whole length of the string.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the kinetic energy per unit length of the string at time $t$, in terms of $\rho_L$, $v$, $y_0$, $\sigma$, $x$ and $t$. Sketch it as a function of $x$.

<Hint>

A piece $dx$ of the string has mass $\rho_L\,dx$ and moves up and down with velocity $\partial y/\partial t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiate the exponential with the chain rule: the inner function is $-\tfrac12(x - vt)^2/\sigma^2$. Then square the result. Squaring the exponential doubles its exponent.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-ke" label="kinetic energy per length $=$" expr="0.5*rho*v^2*y0^2*(x - v*t)^2/s^4*exp(-(x - v*t)^2/s^2)"
  vars={{ rho: [0.5, 2], v: [1, 2], y0: [0.5, 2], s: [1, 2], x: [2, 3], t: [0.1, 0.5] }}
  names={{ rho: "\\rho_L", v: "v", y0: "y_0", s: "\\sigma", x: "x", t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "0.5*rho*v^2*y0^2*(x - v*t)^2/s^4*exp(-(x - v*t)^2/(2*s^2))", hint: "The velocity contains the exponential once, so its square contains it twice: the factor ½ in the exponent goes away." },
    { expr: "0.5*rho*y0^2*(x - v*t)^2/s^4*exp(-(x - v*t)^2/s^2)", hint: "This uses the slope ∂y/∂x instead of the velocity ∂y/∂t. The time derivative of a function of x − vt carries an extra factor −v." },
    { expr: "rho*v^2*y0^2*(x - v*t)^2/s^4*exp(-(x - v*t)^2/s^2)", hint: "The kinetic energy of a mass is ½ m v². The factor ½ is missing." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The kinetic energy per unit length is $\tfrac12\rho_L$ times the square of the string's velocity, and the velocity follows from the chain rule.

<Step title="The velocity of the string">

Write $u = x - vt$ for the distance from the top of the pulse. Then $y = y_0 e^{-u^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ and

<Formula label="Velocity of the string" tex="\frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = \frac{dy}{du}\,\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \left(-\frac{u}{\sigma^2}\,y\right)(-v) = \frac{v\,u}{\sigma^2}\,y_0\,e^{-u^2/(2\sigma^2)}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1 (the chain rule for a function of x − vt; applied to the pulse by us)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The kinetic energy per unit length">

<Formula label="Kinetic energy density of the pulse" tex="\frac{\rho_L}{2}\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial t}\right)^2 = \frac{\rho_L v^2 y_0^2}{2\sigma^4}\,(x - vt)^2\,e^{-(x - vt)^2/\sigma^2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 3 (the kinetic energy of a string; applied to the pulse by us)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The sketch">

As a function of $x$ it has two humps. It is zero at the top of the pulse, $x = vt$, where the string is for a moment at rest. It is largest at $x = vt \pm \sigma$, on the two flanks, where the string moves fastest (down behind the top, up ahead of it). Far from the pulse it dies away. The whole pattern moves along with the pulse at speed $v$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-4, 4]} range={[0, 0.45]} xLabel="(x − vt) / σ" yLabel="energy density"
  curves={[
    { expr: "x^2*exp(-x^2)" }
  ]}
  caption="The kinetic energy per unit length of the pulse in units of ρ_L v² y₀²/(2σ²), computed from the formula above. It is zero at the top of the pulse and largest one σ on either side of it." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

<Solution>

**The idea.** The average power of a sinusoidal wave is $\tfrac12 Z\omega^2$ times its amplitude squared. The two boundary conditions, multiplied together, are exactly the energy balance.

<Step title="The power of a sinusoidal wave">

For $\psi = a\cos(kx - \omega t)$ the slope is $-ak\sin(kx - \omega t)$ and the velocity is $a\omega\sin(kx - \omega t)$, so

<Formula label="Average power of a travelling sine wave" tex="P = -T\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = T k\,\omega\,a^2\sin^2(kx - \omega t), \qquad \langle P\rangle = \tfrac12\,T k\,\omega\,a^2 = \tfrac12\,Z\,\omega^2 a^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.4(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The average of $\sin^2$ over a cycle is $\tfrac12$, and $Tk = \omega Z$.

</Step>

<Step title="The balance">

The incident wave brings in $\tfrac12 Z_L\omega^2A^2$. The reflected wave carries $\tfrac12 Z_L\omega^2R^2A^2$ back, and the transmitted wave carries $\tfrac12 Z_R\omega^2\tau^2A^2$ on. Energy is conserved if

<Formula label="Energy balance at the ring" tex="Z_L\left(1 - R^2\right) = Z_R\,\tau^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.4(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Write the left side as $Z_L(1 - R)(1 + R)$. By the force condition $Z_L(1 - R) = Z_R\tau$, and by continuity $1 + R = \tau$. The product is $Z_R\tau^2$, which is the right side. So the balance holds for every pair of tensions.

</Step>

<Step title="The example">

For $T_R = 4T_L$: $Z_R = 2Z_L$, so $R = (1 - 2)/(1 + 2) = -\tfrac13$ and $\tau = \tfrac23$. The transmitted fraction of the power is $(Z_R/Z_L)\,\tau^2 = 2 \times \tfrac49 = \tfrac89 = 0.889$, and the reflected fraction is $R^2 = \tfrac19 = 0.111$. Together they make 1.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The balance also holds at every instant, not only on average. The power arriving at the ring from the left is $-T_L(\partial\psi_L/\partial x)(\partial\psi_L/\partial t)$ and the power leaving to the right is $-T_R(\partial\psi_R/\partial x)(\partial\psi_R/\partial t)$, both at $x = 0$. The two pieces have the same velocity there (continuity) and the same $T\,\partial\psi/\partial x$ (the force condition), so the two powers are equal. A ring without mass and without friction can neither store nor use up energy. Note that the transmitted fraction is not $\tau^2$: the amplitude is smaller on the right, but the tighter string carries more power for the same amplitude.

</Step>

<Answer id="p4-small" label="$T_R \ll T_L$:" choices={["R → −1 and τ → 0", "R → +1 and τ → 2", "R → 0 and τ → 1", "R → −1 and τ → 2"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A very tight string on the right holds the ring still, like a wall. A very slack one leaves it free.

<Step title="Equal tensions">

For $T_R = T_L$ the formulas give $R = 0$ and $\tau = 1$. The two pieces are then one uniform string, and the ring, which has no mass and no friction, changes nothing: the wave passes without reflection.

</Step>

<Step title="A much larger tension on the right">

For $T_R \gg T_L$, $\sqrt{T_L}$ is negligible next to $\sqrt{T_R}$, so $R \to -1$ and $\tau \to 0$. A tiny tilt of the tight string already gives a large vertical force, so the ring hardly moves. For the left-hand piece this is a fixed end: the wave comes back upside down with its full size (Lecture 10 notes, p. 6, give $R = -1$ for a wall).

</Step>

<Step title="A much smaller tension on the right">

For $T_R \ll T_L$: $R \to +1$ and $\tau \to 2$. The slack string exerts almost no vertical force on the ring, so for the left-hand piece this is a free end. The wave comes back the same way up, and the ring moves with twice the incident amplitude (Lecture 10 notes, p. 6, give $R = 1$ and a transmission coefficient of 2 for a free end).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In both extreme cases all the power is reflected, since $R^2 \to 1$. In the last case the right-hand string does move, with amplitude $2A$, yet it carries almost no power: the transmitted fraction $(Z_R/Z_L)\tau^2$ goes to zero with $Z_R$. So the results agree with the energy balance of part (d).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A ring on a greased wire" points={20} mit="Problem 5.5">

A string of mass per unit length $\rho_L$ under tension $T$ ends on a massless ring. The ring slides on a vertical wire that is covered with a viscous grease. When the end of the string moves, the grease acts on the ring with a vertical drag force $F_y = -b\,\partial y/\partial t$, where $b$ is a positive constant. (MIT's drawing shows the string coming in from the left and the wire at its right-hand end.) Put the wire at $x = 0$, so that the string lies at $x < 0$, and call its displacement $y(x, t)$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Apply Newton's second law to the ring and find the boundary condition at the end of the string, as a relation between the partial derivatives of $y(x, t)$ at $x = 0$. MIT's hint: three forces act on the ring, the tension of the string, the normal force of the wire and the vertical drag force.

<Hint>

The ring has no mass, so the forces on it must add up to zero in each direction. The normal force of the wire is horizontal. Which two forces have a vertical part?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The string leaves the ring to the left. If it rises towards the left (a negative slope), it pulls the ring up. So the vertical part of its pull is $-T\,\partial y/\partial x$ for small slopes.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-bc" choices={[
  "T ∂y/∂x + b ∂y/∂t = 0 at x = 0",
  "T ∂y/∂x − b ∂y/∂t = 0 at x = 0",
  "T ∂y/∂x + b ∂²y/∂t² = 0 at x = 0",
  "∂y/∂x = 0 at x = 0, as for any massless ring"
]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A ring without mass cannot take a net force. Horizontally the wire balances the string. Vertically the string must balance the drag.

<Step title="The forces">

The string pulls the ring with the tension $T$, back along its own direction. Horizontally that is about $T$ to the left, and the normal force of the wire balances it. Vertically it is $-T\,\partial y/\partial x$: the sine of the small angle of the string is about its slope. The drag is $-b\,\partial y/\partial t$.

</Step>

<Step title="The boundary condition">

With zero mass, the sum of the vertical forces is zero:

<Formula label="Boundary condition at the greased wire" tex="-T\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} - b\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = 0 \quad\text{at } x = 0, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad T\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} + b\,\frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = 0" cite="reading2" at="p. 8 (Example 3: a massless ring with a drag force)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $b$ is a force per speed, N s/m, so $b\,\partial y/\partial t$ is a force, like $T$ times a slope. Without grease, $b = 0$, the condition is $\partial y/\partial x = 0$: the free end of the lesson. The sign makes sense too. If the ring moves up, the drag points down, so the string must pull up, which needs a negative slope at the ring.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

Show that the boundary condition can be satisfied by an incident pulse $f(t - x/v)$ together with a reflected pulse $g(t + x/v)$. Find $g$ in terms of $f$: the result has the form $g(t) = r\,f(t)$ with a constant $r$. Give $r$ in terms of $T$, $\rho_L$ and $b$.

<Hint>

Put $y = f(t - x/v) + g(t + x/v)$ into the boundary condition. By the chain rule, the $x$ derivative of $f(t - x/v)$ is $-f'/v$ and that of $g(t + x/v)$ is $+g'/v$, where the prime means the derivative of the function with respect to its argument.

</Hint>

<Hint>

At $x = 0$ you get a relation between $g'(t)$ and $f'(t)$ with a constant factor. Integrate it over time. Before the pulse arrives both functions are zero, which fixes the constant of integration.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-r" label="$r =$" expr="(sqrt(T*rho) - b)/(sqrt(T*rho) + b)"
  vars={{ T: [1, 4], rho: [1, 4], b: [0.2, 0.8] }}
  names={{ T: "T", rho: "\\rho_L", b: "b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(b - sqrt(T*rho))/(b + sqrt(T*rho))", hint: "Check the limit of no grease, b → 0. This gives −1, but a free end sends a pulse back the same way up." },
    { expr: "(T - b)/(T + b)", hint: "Check the units: b is a force per speed, T is a force. The slope of f(t − x/v) carries a factor 1/v, so b is compared with T/v." },
    { expr: "(sqrt(T/rho) - b)/(sqrt(T/rho) + b)", hint: "Check the units: √(T/ρ_L) is the wave speed, and b is a force per speed. The drag is compared with T/v = √(Tρ_L)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Any solution on the string is an incident plus a reflected travelling wave. The boundary condition at $x = 0$ ties the reflected one to the incident one.

<Step title="Put the two pulses in">

Take $y(x, t) = f(t - x/v) + g(t + x/v)$, with $v = \sqrt{T/\rho_L}$. The first term travels towards $+x$, to the ring, and the second travels away from it. Both solve the wave equation (Lecture 10 notes, p. 1). At $x = 0$:

<Formula label="Slope and velocity at the ring" tex="\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} = \frac{-f'(t) + g'(t)}{v}, \qquad \frac{\partial y}{\partial t} = f'(t) + g'(t) \quad\text{at } x = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.5(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Use the boundary condition">

With $Z = T/v = \sqrt{T\rho_L}$, the condition $T\,\partial y/\partial x + b\,\partial y/\partial t = 0$ becomes $Z(g' - f') + b(f' + g') = 0$, so

<Formula label="Relation between the two pulses" tex="g'(t) = \frac{Z - b}{Z + b}\,f'(t)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.5(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

Integrate over time. Long before the pulse arrives, $f$ and $g$ are both zero at the ring, so the constant of integration is zero:

<Formula label="The reflected pulse" tex="g(t) = \frac{Z - b}{Z + b}\,f(t), \qquad\text{so}\qquad g\!\left(t + \frac{x}{v}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{T\rho_L} - b}{\sqrt{T\rho_L} + b}\,f\!\left(t + \frac{x}{v}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.5(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

This $g$ satisfies the boundary condition at every time, whatever the shape of $f$. The reflected pulse is the incident pulse turned round, with its height multiplied by $r = (Z - b)/(Z + b)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$Z$ and $b$ have the same units: N/(m/s) = N s/m. The factor $r$ lies between $-1$ and $+1$, so the reflected pulse is never larger than the incident one. The energy balance works out as well. A pulse $f(t - x/v)$ carries the power $-T(\partial y/\partial x)(\partial y/\partial t) = Z f'^2$. The grease takes up $b(\partial y/\partial t)^2 = b(1 + r)^2 f'^2$, and $Z(1 - r^2) = b(1 + r)^2$ because $Z(1 - r) = b(1 + r)$. What is not reflected is turned into heat in the grease.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Show that your result behaves properly in the limits $b \to 0$ (the ring slips freely) and $b \to \infty$ (the string is clamped). Then, as two questions of our own: for which value of $b$, in terms of $T$ and $\rho_L$, is there no reflected pulse at all? And for $b = 3\sqrt{T\rho_L}$, what fraction of the energy of the incident pulse ends up in the grease?

<Hint>

For the two limits, decide which of $Z$ and $b$ can be neglected in $r$, and compare with what you know about a free end and a fixed end.

</Hint>

<Hint>

There is no reflection when $r = 0$. For the last question, the energy of a pulse goes with the square of its height, so the reflected pulse keeps the fraction $r^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-match" label="no reflection for $b =$" expr="sqrt(T*rho)"
  vars={{ T: [1, 4], rho: [1.5, 4] }}
  names={{ T: "T", rho: "\\rho_L" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(T/rho)", hint: "That is the wave speed, in m/s. The drag constant b is a force per speed, like T/v." },
    { expr: "T", hint: "Check the units: b is a force per speed, not a force. The condition is b = T/v." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p5-abs" label="fraction absorbed $=$" value="0.75" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-abs" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The grease acts like a second string whose "$Z$" is $b$. Very little grease is a free end, very thick grease is a clamp, and in between there is a value that swallows the pulse whole.

<Step title="No grease">

For $b \to 0$: $r \to Z/Z = 1$, so $g = f$. The pulse is reflected the same way up and with its full size. That is the free end of the lesson, with $R = 1$ (Lecture 10 notes, p. 6). The boundary condition becomes $\partial y/\partial x = 0$, as it should for a ring that slips freely.

</Step>

<Step title="Very thick grease">

For $b \to \infty$: $r \to -b/b = -1$, so $g = -f$. The pulse is reflected upside down with its full size, as from a fixed end, $R = -1$. The displacement of the ring is $f(t) + g(t) = (1 + r)f(t) \to 0$: the ring does not move, so the string is clamped.

</Step>

<Step title="No reflection (our own addition)">

The reflected pulse vanishes when $r = 0$:

<Formula label="The matched drag" tex="b = Z = \frac{T}{v} = \sqrt{T\rho_L}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5.5(c), p. 3 (our own addition)" />

The ring then moves exactly as a point in the middle of an endless string would, and the whole pulse is absorbed. For the string, the grease is then the same load as more string.

</Step>

<Step title="Three times the matched drag (our own addition)">

For $b = 3Z$: $r = (1 - 3)/(1 + 3) = -\tfrac12$. The reflected pulse is upside down at half the height, so it keeps $r^2 = \tfrac14$ of the energy. The grease takes the rest, $1 - r^2 = \tfrac34 = 0.75$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In both limits $r^2 \to 1$, so nothing is absorbed. For $b \to 0$ the drag force itself vanishes. For $b \to \infty$ the ring does not move, and a force on a point that does not move does no work. The absorbed fraction $1 - r^2 = 4Zb/(Z + b)^2$ is largest, equal to 1, at $b = Z$. It is the same for $b = 3Z$ and for $b = Z/3$, both give 0.75: only the sign of the reflected pulse differs.

</Step>

</Solution>
