summary: "When oscillators are coupled, the motion can look very complicated (a double pendulum, pendulums joined by a spring or a rod, the Wilberforce pendulum), but for small oscillations it is a superposition of simple harmonic motions. The key is the normal mode: a motion in which every part oscillates with the same frequency and the same phase (amplitudes may be zero or negative). For a mass 2m joined by two springs k to two masses m, both on the same side of it, one above the other, guessing from symmetry gives three modes: the 2m still and the small masses opposite (ω² = k/m), the 2m moving against both small masses (ω² = 4k/2m = 2k/m), and the whole system translating (ω = 0, x = c + vt). Three second-order equations need six constants, and the three modes supply exactly six, so their superposition is the general solution. The systematic method: write the equations as MẌ = −KX, where the mass matrix M says how the mass is distributed and the spring matrix K how the parts talk to each other; try X = Re[A e^(i(ωt+φ))], which gives (M⁻¹K − ω²I)A = 0, so det(M⁻¹K − ω²I) = 0. Here that is (ω₀² − ω²)ω²(ω² − 2ω₀²) = 0 with ω₀² = k/m: the same three frequencies, and putting each back gives the amplitude pattern."

So far a single object has met more and more forces: drag, then a [driving force](/courses/8-03/driven-oscillators-resonance) (transcript, p. 1). Now several objects are allowed to "talk to each other": **coupled oscillators** (p. 2). Two oscillators that do not interact just oscillate on their own; coupled ones do interesting things.

## Complicated motion, simple pieces

In general coupled systems are "super, super complicated" (transcript, p. 2; "extremely complicated" in the notes, p. 1). A double pendulum given a push moves almost like a living creature: the outer bob does all kinds of things, and only a computer could follow it (p. 2). But given only a small push, its motion becomes much easier to understand (p. 2). Other examples (pp. 2–3): two masses joined by a metal ruler, two pendulums joined by a spring or by a rod, and the **Wilberforce pendulum**, which starts out only rotating and then bobs up and down, because stretching its spring also twists it: one object, but rotation and vertical motion are coupled (p. 3). Ignoring drag and driving forces, Lee's claim is that all this complicated motion is an illusion: it is a superposition of harmonic motions (p. 3).

## Normal modes by guessing

The system: three masses on a frictionless table, all moving along one direction. A mass $2m$ on the left is joined by two springs of constant $k$ and natural length $l_0$ to two masses $m$ on its right, one above the other, each on its own spring (notes, pp. 1–2; transcript, pp. 4–5, 8). Call their displacements $x_1$ (the $2m$), $x_2$ and $x_3$ (p. 6). The motion can be anything, but stare at it long enough and special motions appear (p. 4):

<Formula label="Normal mode" tex="\text{normal mode: every part oscillates with the same frequency and the same phase}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The most general motion will turn out to be a superposition of the normal modes (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 4). Guided by symmetry, the class finds them (transcript, pp. 4–6; notes, p. 2):

- **The 2m still, small masses opposite.** Push one small mass in and pull the other out by the same amount. The two springs push and pull the $2m$ equally in opposite directions, so it stays put, as if each small mass were on a spring fixed to a wall: $F = -k\,\Delta x$, so $\omega^2 = k/m$ (pp. 5–6).
- **The 2m against both small masses.** Move both small masses one way and the $2m$ the other, by the same amount. The two small masses act like one mass $2m$, and the force on the $2m$ is $-2k \cdot 2\Delta x = -4k\,\Delta x$, so $\omega^2 = 4k/2m = 2k/m$ (pp. 4–5).
- **Translation.** Everything moves together; no spring is stretched, $F = 0$ and $\omega = 0$. It is an oscillation with zero frequency, $x = c + vt$: Lee pictures it as the limit of a very weak spring $k'$ to a wall, where $A\sin\omega_Ct \approx A\omega_Ct$, a constant velocity, as $\omega_C \to 0$ (pp. 6–7).

Lee raises the first objection himself, to "wake you up": the $2m$ is not moving, so is this a normal mode? It is: it oscillates with amplitude zero. A student raises the second: the small masses move in opposite directions. A minus sign in the amplitude keeps the phases equal (p. 5). (In the lecture the mode with $\omega^2 = 4k/2m$ is called A and the one with the $2m$ still is called B; the notes, followed here, label them the other way round.) With the notes' labels (notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="The three modes" tex="\begin{aligned} &\text{A: } x_1 = 0,\ x_2 = A\cos(\omega_At + \phi_A),\ x_3 = -A\cos(\omega_At + \phi_A), && \omega_A^2 = \tfrac{k}{m} \\ &\text{B: } x_1 = B\cos(\omega_Bt + \phi_B),\ x_2 = x_3 = -B\cos(\omega_Bt + \phi_B), && \omega_B^2 = \tfrac{2k}{m} \\ &\text{C: } x_1 = x_2 = x_3 = c + vt, && \omega_C = 0 \end{aligned}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

**Counting constants** (transcript, p. 7). The equations of motion will be three second-order equations, so the general solution has six constants. The three modes bring $A$, $\phi_A$, $B$, $\phi_B$, $c$ and $v$: six. So the general solution is their sum (notes, p. 2), "and I didn't even solve the differential equation" (transcript, p. 7). Make the $2m$ a $3m$ and the symmetry argument mostly fails; a general method is needed (p. 7).

## The equations of motion

Measure each displacement from equilibrium, where the springs have their natural length (transcript, p. 8). The forces on each mass give (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 8)

<Formula label="Equations of motion" tex="2m\ddot x_1 = k(x_2 - x_1) + k(x_3 - x_1), \qquad m\ddot x_2 = k(x_1 - x_2), \qquad m\ddot x_3 = k(x_1 - x_3)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The signs can be checked: increasing $x_1$ compresses the springs, which push it back (transcript, p. 8). The notes also derive these with explicit spring lengths $l_0$ and shifted coordinates $x_2' = x_2 - l_0$, $x_3' = x_3 - l_0$, reaching the same equations (notes, pp. 4–5).

## The matrix method

Collect terms and write (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 9)

<Formula label="Matrix form" tex="M\ddot X = -KX, \qquad M = \begin{pmatrix} 2m & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & m & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & m \end{pmatrix}, \quad X = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ x_3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad K = \begin{pmatrix} 2k & -k & -k \\ -k & k & 0 \\ -k & 0 & k \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

$M$ tells how the mass is distributed, and $K$ "how each component talks to the other components" (transcript, p. 9). Now use the definition of a normal mode: the same frequency and phase for every part, with amplitudes $A = (A_1, A_2, A_3)$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 9):

<Formula label="Normal-mode trial" tex="X = \text{Re}[Z], \qquad Z = Ae^{i(\omega t + \phi)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Then $\ddot Z = -\omega^2Z$, the exponential cancels, and multiplying by $M^{-1}$ on the left (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 10):

<Formula label="Eigenvalue problem" tex="M\omega^2A = KA \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (M^{-1}K - \omega^2I)A = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Every step mirrors the single oscillator, now with matrices (transcript, p. 10). A nonzero $A$ (otherwise nothing moves) requires (p. 11)

<Formula label="Frequency condition" tex="\det(M^{-1}K - \omega^2I) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

the eigenvalue problem of [linear algebra](/courses/18-06/eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors). With $\omega_0^2 = k/m$, $M^{-1}K - \omega^2I$ has rows $(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2, -\frac12\omega_0^2, -\frac12\omega_0^2)$, $(-\omega_0^2, \omega_0^2 - \omega^2, 0)$, $(-\omega_0^2, 0, \omega_0^2 - \omega^2)$, and its determinant factors (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 11–12):

<Formula label="Characteristic equation" tex="(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^3 - \omega_0^4(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2) = (\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)\,\omega^2\,(\omega^2 - 2\omega_0^2) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

So $\omega^2 = k/m$, $2k/m$ or $0$: exactly the three modes found by guessing (notes, p. 4); "the math also knows physics" (transcript, p. 13). Putting each frequency back into $(M^{-1}K - \omega^2I)A = 0$ gives the amplitude pattern. For $\omega_B^2 = 2k/m$: $A_1 = -A_2 = -A_3$, that is $X = B(1, -1, -1)\cos(\omega_Bt + \phi_B)$, "just the simple harmonic oscillator"; for $\omega_A^2 = k/m$: $A_1 = 0$, $A_2 = -A_3$ (notes, p. 4). The recipe works for any number of coupled objects (transcript, p. 13). Next time: beats, and driven coupled oscillators.

<WorkedExample title="Modes of your own">
<Problem>
A mass $m$ is attached to a wall by a spring $k$, and a mass $2m$ is attached to the first mass by another spring $k$ (wall, $m$, $2m$ in a line). Find the normal-mode frequencies and amplitude ratios, with $\omega_0^2 = k/m$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Equations and matrices">
$m\ddot x_1 = -kx_1 + k(x_2 - x_1)$ and $2m\ddot x_2 = -k(x_2 - x_1)$. So $M = \begin{pmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & 2m \end{pmatrix}$, $K = \begin{pmatrix} 2k & -k \\ -k & k \end{pmatrix}$ and $M^{-1}K = \omega_0^2\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -\frac12 & \frac12 \end{pmatrix}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Frequencies">
With $\omega^2 = s\,\omega_0^2$: $(2 - s)(\frac12 - s) - \frac12 = s^2 - \frac52s + \frac12 = 0$, so $s = \frac{5/2 \pm \sqrt{17/4}}{2} \approx 2.281$ or $0.219$. The frequencies are $\omega \approx 1.510\,\omega_0$ and $0.468\,\omega_0$. There is no zero mode: the wall holds the system in place.
</Step>
<Step title="Amplitudes">
The first row gives $(2 - s)A_1 = A_2$. Slow mode, $s \approx 0.219$: $A_2 \approx 1.78A_1$, both masses moving together, the heavy one more. Fast mode, $s \approx 2.281$: $A_2 \approx -0.28A_1$, the masses moving against each other, the light one more.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Small oscillations of coupled systems are superpositions of normal modes: every part at the same frequency and phase.
- Symmetry can reveal the modes; counting constants (two per mode) confirms that they give the general solution.
- In general: $M\ddot X = -KX$, try $Ae^{i(\omega t + \phi)}$, solve $\det(M^{-1}K - \omega^2I) = 0$, then find each $A$.
- A free system always has a zero-frequency mode, uniform translation.
