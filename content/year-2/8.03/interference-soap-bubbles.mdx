A soap bubble catches the light and shows bands of colour, although it is lit with ordinary white light (transcript, p. 3; Lee makes one by dipping two sticks in soap water and drawing them apart, succeeding about half the time). Everything needed to explain it is now in place: the [reflection coefficients](/courses/8-03/em-waves-in-matter-brewster) of the last lecture and one new idea, **interference** (transcript, pp. 1–3).

## Adding two waves

Take two waves of the same frequency and direction, polarized the same way, with different phases (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Two waves" tex="\vec E_1 = A_1\cos(\omega t - kz + \phi_1)\,\hat x, \qquad \vec E_2 = A_2\cos(\omega t - kz + \phi_2)\,\hat x, \qquad \vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The **intensity** is the size of the [Poynting vector](/courses/8-03/electromagnetic-waves). With $\vec B = \frac1v\hat k\times\vec E$ and $v = c/n$ (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="Intensity" tex="I = |\vec S| = \frac{1}{\mu_0}|\vec E\times\vec B| = \frac{n}{\mu_0c}|\vec E|^2 = cn\varepsilon_0|\vec E|^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Squaring $\vec E$ gives $A_1^2\cos^2(\ldots + \phi_1) + A_2^2\cos^2(\ldots + \phi_2)$ and a cross term, $2A_1A_2\cos(\ldots + \phi_1)\cos(\ldots + \phi_2) = A_1A_2[\cos(2\omega t - 2kz + \phi_1 + \phi_2) + \cos(\phi_1 - \phi_2)]$. Averaged over a period, each $\cos^2$ gives ½, the $\cos 2\omega t$ term gives 0, and the constant stays (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 5–7):

<Formula label="Average intensity" tex="\langle I\rangle = \frac1T\int_0^TI\,dt = cn\varepsilon_0\left[\frac{A_1^2}{2} + \frac{A_2^2}{2} + A_1A_2\cos\delta\right], \qquad \delta \equiv \phi_1 - \phi_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

(The notes label the left-hand side $|\vec E|^2$; it is $\langle I\rangle$.) As a function of the **phase difference** $\delta$ (notes, pp. 1–2; transcript, pp. 7–9):

- $\delta = 0, 2\pi, 4\pi, \ldots$: **constructive interference**, $\langle I\rangle/cn\varepsilon_0 = \frac12(A_1 + A_2)^2$;
- $\delta = \pi, 3\pi, \ldots$: **destructive interference**, $\frac12(A_1 - A_2)^2$.

With equal amplitudes the waves cancel completely at $\delta = \pi$ and give four times the intensity of one wave at $\delta = 0$ (transcript, pp. 8–9).

**Picture** (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 9–10). Write $\vec E_1 = \text{Re}[A_1e^{i\phi_1}e^{i(\omega t - kz)}]\hat x$ and likewise $\vec E_2$. At an instant when $\omega t - kz = 0$, the two amplitudes are arrows in the complex plane, of lengths $A_1$, $A_2$ at angles $\phi_1$, $\phi_2$; the field is the real part of their sum. In line, $\delta = 0$, they add fully; opposite, $\delta = \pi$, they cancel.

## Reflection flips the sign

How thick is a soap film that shows colours: 1 mm, the size of a pin's head; 100 μm, a hair; or 100 nm, a virus? Nobody votes for 1 mm (transcript, p. 10). At normal incidence, from the last lecture (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 11):

<Formula label="Normal-incidence coefficients" tex="R = \frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2}, \qquad T = \frac{2n_1}{n_1 + n_2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

$T$ is always positive: the transmitted wave never changes sign. If $n_1 > n_2$, $R > 0$ and the reflected wave keeps its sign; if $n_1 < n_2$, entering a slower medium, $R < 0$: the reflected amplitude **flips**, a phase difference of $\pi$; if $n_1 = n_2$ nothing is reflected, even between different materials (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 11–12).

**Air to soap water** (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 12–14), $n_1 = 1$, $n_2 = 1.5$, with $\langle I\rangle = cn\varepsilon_0A^2/2$:

<Formula label="Air to soap water" tex="R = \frac{1 - 1.5}{1 + 1.5} = -0.2, \quad T = \frac{2}{2.5} = 0.8; \qquad I_R = 0.04\,I_0, \quad I_T = 1.5 \cdot 0.8^2\,I_0 = 0.96\,I_0" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Only 4% of the intensity is reflected, with a sign flip, and 96% goes on; the factor 1.5 in $I_T$ is the $n$ in the intensity. A bubble looks bright anyway because the eye responds to intensity very nonlinearly, Lee adds (transcript, p. 13).

## The soap film

Now a film of index $n_2 = 1.5$ and thickness $d$ in air, lit at normal incidence (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 14–16):

1. At the front surface, 4% is reflected, flipped.
2. The 96% that enters meets the back surface going from $n = 1.5$ to $n = 1$: now $R = +0.2$, so 96% × 4% is reflected without a flip, and 96% × 96% leaves the film.
3. That reflection crosses the front surface again: 4% × 96% × 96% ≈ 3.7% comes out towards the eye (the notes round it to 3.8%), while a little bounces back and forth inside "forever", too faint to matter.

The eye sees the sum of reflections 1 and 3, of nearly equal strength. Their phase difference has two parts (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 16–17): the extra path $2d$ inside the film, where the wavelength is $\lambda/n_2$ (a student supplies the $n_2$), and the flip of reflection 1:

<Formula label="Phase difference in a soap film" tex="\delta = \frac{2d}{\lambda/n_2}\,2\pi + \pi" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Constructive interference needs $\delta = 2N\pi$, destructive $\delta = (2N + 1)\pi$ (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 17). So (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 17–18):

1. As $d \to 0$, $\delta \to \pi$: destructive. A very thin film reflects nothing and looks transparent.
2. Constructive interference at $d = (2N - 1)\lambda/4n_2$; destructive at $d = N\lambda/2n_2$.
3. For a fixed film, the brightest reflected wavelengths are

<Formula label="Brightest wavelengths" tex="\lambda_{\max} = \frac{4dn_2}{2N - 1}, \qquad N = 1, 2, 3, \ldots" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

**Thin: $d \approx 100$ nm** (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 18–19). $\lambda_{\max} = 600\text{ nm}/(2N - 1) = 600$, 200, 120 nm, … Visible light runs from about 400 nm (violet) to about 700 nm, with red around 650 nm, so only the first lies in it: the film looks coloured, here orange-red.

**Thick: $d \approx 100$ μm** (notes, pp. 4–5; transcript, pp. 19–20). $\lambda_{\max} = 600\ \mu\text{m}/(2N - 1)$: far too long for $N = 1$, but $N = 500$ gives 600.6 nm, $N = 501$ gives 599.4 nm, $N = 502$ gives 598.2 nm, and so on across the visible range. So many colours are reinforced that the brain sees white. (The transcript has "5000" for 500, p. 19.)

So colours need a film about 100 nm thick, the virus option; thick films look white; and a film thinning towards zero turns transparent (notes, p. 5; transcript, p. 20).

**Demonstration** (transcript, pp. 20–21). White light reflected from a soap film in a rotating holder is projected onto the wall. At first the image is white. Gravity drains the film, so it thickens at the bottom and thins at the top (upside down in the projection), and bands of colour appear and spread, like a rainbow, as different heights have different thicknesses. A separate recording shows the same (p. 21).

(a) Two waves have amplitudes $A_1 = 1.0$ and $A_2 = 0.50$ (in any units). Find the ratio of the largest to the smallest average intensity as $\delta$ varies. (b) A soap film with $n_2 = 1.33$ is $120$ nm thick. Which wavelengths does it reflect most strongly, and which not at all?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Contrast">
Largest: $\frac12(1.5)^2 = 1.125$; smallest: $\frac12(0.50)^2 = 0.125$ (in units of $cn\varepsilon_0$). The ratio is 9: unequal waves never cancel completely.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The film">
$4dn_2 = 4 \times 120 \times 1.33 \approx 638$ nm, so $\lambda_{\max} \approx 638$ nm for $N = 1$ (orange-red) and 213 nm for $N = 2$, ultraviolet. No reflection when $2dn_2 = N\lambda$: $\lambda = 2dn_2/N \approx 319$ nm, also ultraviolet. In visible light the film looks orange-red.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Two waves give $\langle I\rangle = cn\varepsilon_0[A_1^2/2 + A_2^2/2 + A_1A_2\cos\delta]$: constructive at $\delta = 2N\pi$, destructive at $(2N + 1)\pi$.
- Reflection into a higher index flips the sign ($R < 0$); transmission never does. Air to soap: 4% reflected, 96% transmitted.
- A film in air: $\delta = 2\pi\cdot2d/(\lambda/n_2) + \pi$, bright at $\lambda = 4dn_2/(2N - 1)$. Colours need $d \approx 100$ nm; thick films look white; $d \to 0$ reflects nothing.
