Work each part on paper first: draw the system, choose coordinates for the small displacements, write one equation of motion for each mass. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check.

A formula box takes plain typing, for example `w0^2*(1 + alpha)` or `T/(m*a)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. A number box accepts any value within 1% of ours, so keep at least three significant figures. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A double pendulum" points={20} mit="Problem 3.1">

Two masses hang one below the other. The mass $M_1$ is joined to a fixed point by a massless rigid rod of length $L$. The mass $M_2$ is joined to $M_1$ by a second massless rigid rod, also of length $L$. Both hinges are frictionless, gravity points down, and the whole system moves in one vertical plane. The upper rod makes the angle $\theta_1$ with the vertical and the lower rod makes the angle $\theta_2$ with the vertical (not with the upper rod). Then the horizontal positions are $X_1 = L\sin\theta_1$ and $X_2 = L\sin\theta_1 + L\sin\theta_2$. All angles are small, so you may drop every term of second order in $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$. Write $\omega_0^2 = g/L$ and $\alpha = M_2/M_1$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Write the equations of motion of the two masses. Give the angular accelerations $\ddot\theta_1$ and $\ddot\theta_2$ as formulas in $\theta_1$, $\theta_2$, $\omega_0$ and $\alpha$.

<Hint>

For small angles the two rods are almost vertical, so neither mass moves up or down. What does that say about the tension in the lower rod? And in the upper rod, which carries both masses?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Now take the horizontal force on each mass: the sideways part of each rod's tension is the tension times the sine of the angle, which is about the angle itself. For $M_2$ the horizontal acceleration is $\ddot X_2 = L(\ddot\theta_1 + \ddot\theta_2)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a1" label="$\ddot\theta_1 =$" expr="w0^2*(-(1 + alpha)*theta1 + alpha*theta2)"

  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-a2" label="$\ddot\theta_2 =$" expr="(1 + alpha)*w0^2*(theta1 - theta2)"
  vars={{ w0: [1, 3], alpha: [0.2, 4], theta1: [-0.3, 0.3], theta2: [-0.3, 0.3] }}
  names={{ w0: "\\omega_0", alpha: "\\alpha", theta1: "\\theta_1", theta2: "\\theta_2" }}

    { expr: "w0^2*(theta1 - theta2)", hint: "The second equation holds θ̈₁ + θ̈₂. When you take θ̈₁ from your first equation, all of it comes along, also the part with α." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** For small angles the two masses move only sideways. The vertical forces on each mass cancel, which fixes the two tensions, and the horizontal forces give two equations.

<Step title="The tensions">

Let $T_1$ be the tension in the upper rod and $T_2$ in the lower rod. To first order in the angles both $\cos\theta$ are 1. The mass $M_2$ has no vertical acceleration, so $T_2 = M_2 g$. The mass $M_1$ has none either, and it is pulled up by $T_1$, pulled down by its weight and by the lower rod: $T_1 = M_1 g + T_2 = (M_1 + M_2)g$.

</Step>

<Step title="The horizontal forces">

The sideways part of a tension is the tension times the sine of the angle, about the angle. The upper rod pulls $M_1$ back, towards the vertical through the hinge; the lower rod pulls $M_1$ towards $M_2$ and pulls $M_2$ back towards $M_1$:

<Formula label="Double pendulum: horizontal equations" tex="M_1 L\,\ddot\theta_1 = -(M_1 + M_2)g\,\theta_1 + M_2 g\,\theta_2, \qquad M_2 L\,(\ddot\theta_1 + \ddot\theta_2) = -M_2 g\,\theta_2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3.1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Divide the first by $M_1 L$ and the second by $M_2 L$:

<Formula label="Double pendulum: the equations in angles" tex="\ddot\theta_1 = -(1 + \alpha)\,\omega_0^2\,\theta_1 + \alpha\,\omega_0^2\,\theta_2, \qquad \ddot\theta_1 + \ddot\theta_2 = -\omega_0^2\,\theta_2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3.1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Put the first into the second, $\ddot\theta_2 = -\omega_0^2\theta_2 - \ddot\theta_1$:

<Formula label="Double pendulum: the second angle" tex="\ddot\theta_2 = (1 + \alpha)\,\omega_0^2\,(\theta_1 - \theta_2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3.1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

If $\alpha \to 0$ the lower mass is light, so the upper mass is a simple pendulum: the first equation becomes $\ddot\theta_1 = -\omega_0^2\theta_1$. Each term has the dimension of angle per second squared. For equal angles $\theta_1 = \theta_2$ the second equation gives $\ddot\theta_2 = 0$: if the two rods are parallel and tilted by the same angle, the lower rod does not start to turn relative to the vertical, because the whole pair is accelerated sideways together.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find the two normal-mode frequencies as functions of $\omega_0$ and $\alpha$. Give $\omega_1^2$ for the lower and $\omega_2^2$ for the higher frequency. You do not have to find the amplitude ratios.

<Hint>

Look for solutions in which both angles oscillate at the same frequency: $\theta_1 = A_1\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ and $\theta_2 = A_2\cos(\omega t + \phi)$. The two equations then turn into two linear equations for $A_1$ and $A_2$ with $\omega^2$ as a parameter.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A pair of linear equations has a solution that is not zero only when its determinant is zero. Write $\lambda = \omega^2/\omega_0^2$. The determinant is a quadratic equation for $\lambda$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b1" label="$\omega_1^2 =$" expr="w0^2*(1 + alpha - sqrt(alpha*(1 + alpha)))"
  vars={{ w0: [1, 3], alpha: [0.2, 4] }}
  names={{ w0: "\\omega_0", alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "w0^2*(1 + alpha - sqrt(alpha))", hint: "Under the root you need the product of the two off-diagonal entries of the matrix, α and (1 + α), not α alone." },
    { expr: "w0^2*(1 - sqrt(alpha*(1 + alpha)))", hint: "The diagonal entries of the matrix are (1 + α), not 1, so the centre of the two roots is at 1 + α." },
    { expr: "sqrt(w0^2*(1 + alpha - sqrt(alpha*(1 + alpha))))", hint: "This is a frequency ω₁. The box asks for its square, ω₁²." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-b2" label="$\omega_2^2 =$" expr="w0^2*(1 + alpha + sqrt(alpha*(1 + alpha)))"
  vars={{ w0: [1, 3], alpha: [0.2, 4] }}
  names={{ w0: "\\omega_0", alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "w0^2*(1 + alpha - sqrt(alpha*(1 + alpha)))", hint: "That is the lower frequency. Take the plus sign for the higher one." },
    { expr: "w0^2*(1 + alpha + sqrt(alpha))", hint: "Under the root you need the product of the two off-diagonal entries of the matrix, α and (1 + α), not α alone." },
    { expr: "w0^2*(1 + 2*alpha)", hint: "This is not a root of the determinant. With λ = ω²/ω₀², the determinant is a full quadratic in λ: solve it with the root formula." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equations of part (a) are linear and have constant coefficients, so each normal mode has both angles oscillating at one frequency. Putting such a trial into the equations gives a determinant, as in the lessons.

<Step title="The matrix">

Put the trial $\theta_1 = A_1\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, $\theta_2 = A_2\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ into the two equations of part (a). Each second derivative becomes $-\omega^2$ times the angle. With $\lambda = \omega^2/\omega_0^2$ the equations read

<Formula label="Double pendulum: the matrix" tex="\begin{pmatrix} 1 + \alpha - \lambda & -\alpha \\ -(1 + \alpha) & 1 + \alpha - \lambda \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} A_1 \\ A_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3.1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Amplitudes that are not both zero exist only when the determinant of this matrix is zero. This is the method of the notes: the frequencies come from the determinant ([Lecture 4 notes](/courses/8-03/coupled-oscillators-normal-modes), p. 3).

</Step>

<Step title="The determinant">

<Formula label="Double pendulum: the frequency condition" tex="(1 + \alpha - \lambda)^2 - \alpha(1 + \alpha) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3 (the method; this system is ours)" />

So $\lambda = (1 + \alpha) \pm \sqrt{\alpha(1 + \alpha)}$, and multiplying by $\omega_0^2$:

<Formula label="Double pendulum: normal-mode frequencies" tex="\omega_1^2 = \omega_0^2\left[\,1 + \alpha - \sqrt{\alpha(1 + \alpha)}\,\right], \qquad \omega_2^2 = \omega_0^2\left[\,1 + \alpha + \sqrt{\alpha(1 + \alpha)}\,\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 3.1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take equal masses, $\alpha = 1$: $\omega^2/\omega_0^2 = 2 \mp \sqrt2 = 0.586$ and $3.414$. Both are positive, and the lower one is below $\omega_0^2$, as it should be for the mode in which both rods swing together.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Let $\alpha \to \infty$: the upper mass is very light. What happens to the lower frequency? Give the value that $\omega_1^2/\omega_0^2$ approaches. Then say what happens to the higher frequency and whether the results make sense.

<Hint>

Write $\sqrt{\alpha(1 + \alpha)} = \alpha\sqrt{1 + 1/\alpha}$ and expand the square root for large $\alpha$, where $1/\alpha$ is small: the square root of one plus a small number is about one plus half that number.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$\omega_1^2/\omega_0^2 \to$" value="0.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-c-limit" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Expand the two roots for large $\alpha$, and then ask what the answer means for a light $M_1$.

<Step title="The lower frequency">

For large $\alpha$: $\sqrt{\alpha(1 + \alpha)} = \alpha\sqrt{1 + 1/\alpha} \approx \alpha + \tfrac12 - \tfrac{1}{8\alpha}$. Then

<Formula label="Double pendulum: very heavy lower mass" tex="\omega_1^2 \to \omega_0^2\left(1 + \alpha - \alpha - \tfrac12\right) = \tfrac12\,\omega_0^2, \qquad \omega_2^2 \approx 2\alpha\,\omega_0^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3.1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Do the results make sense?">

Yes. When $M_1$ is almost massless, nothing holds the joint between the rods in a bent position: the two rods line up and form one rod of length $2L$ carrying $M_2$. A pendulum of length $2L$ has $\omega^2 = g/(2L) = \omega_0^2/2$. That is the lower mode. The higher mode belongs to the light mass $M_1$ itself, which jiggles sideways between two stretched rods under the tension $M_2 g$. The restoring force on it is about $2M_2 g\,X_1/L$, so $\omega^2 \approx 2M_2 g/(M_1 L) = 2\alpha\omega_0^2$, which grows without limit as $M_1 \to 0$. Both limits agree with the formulas.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Now let $\alpha \to 0$: the lower mass is very light. What do the two frequencies become? Do the results make sense?

<Hint>

Put $\alpha = 0$ into your two formulas. Then think about what each mass does when the other one has almost no weight.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="As $\alpha \to 0$:" choices={["Both frequencies approach ω₀", "One frequency goes to zero and the other approaches ω₀", "One frequency approaches ω₀ and the other grows without limit", "Both frequencies approach 2ω₀"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put $\alpha = 0$ into both roots and compare with what the two light-mass pendulums would do on their own.

<Step title="The limit">

For $\alpha \to 0$ both roots tend to $\omega_0^2(1 \mp 0) = \omega_0^2$. For small $\alpha$ the roots are $\omega^2 \approx \omega_0^2(1 \mp \sqrt\alpha)$, so the two frequencies come close together as $\sqrt\alpha$, and the first correction does not cancel.

</Step>

<Step title="Do the results make sense?">

Yes. A very light $M_2$ cannot disturb $M_1$, which swings on its own as a simple pendulum of length $L$: frequency $\omega_0$. The light $M_2$ hangs from $M_1$ on a rod of length $L$, and it also swings with $\omega_0$ (the point of suspension moves, but its mass does not react on it). Two systems with the same frequency, weakly coupled: the two modes have almost equal frequencies, and the motion shows beats, as in the lesson on [coupled pendulums](/courses/8-03/coupled-pendulums-beats). For example, with $\alpha = 0.01$ the frequencies squared are $1.1105\,\omega_0^2$ and $0.9095\,\omega_0^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Two coupled LC circuits" points={20} mit="Problem 3.2">

Two identical circuits each contain an inductor $L$ and a capacitor $C$ in a loop. The two inductors sit close together, so that their mutual inductance $M$ couples the currents. Let $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ be the charges on the capacitors and $I_1 = \dot Q_1$, $I_2 = \dot Q_2$ the currents. Both currents are counted positive in the same sense round their loops, and $M$ is positive when two currents of the same sense strengthen each other's flux. (MIT does not fix these conventions. They are ours, and with the other sign for $M$ the roles of the two modes swap.) There is no resistance.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Find the frequencies of the two normal modes as functions of $L$, $M$ and $C$. Give $\omega_1^2$ for the mode with equal charges ($Q_1 = Q_2$) and $\omega_2^2$ for the mode with opposite charges.

<Hint>

Go once round loop 1. The voltage across the capacitor is $Q_1/C$. The inductor $L$ and the coupling both make an emf proportional to a rate of change of current. Which current does the coupling to loop 2 contain?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The two loops are identical, so you can guess the modes: $Q_2 = Q_1$ and $Q_2 = -Q_1$. Put each guess into the equation of loop 1 and read off the frequency.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a1" label="$\omega_1^2 =$" expr="1/((L + M)*C)"
  vars={{ L: [1, 2], M: [0.1, 0.8], C: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ L: "L", M: "M", C: "C" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/((L - M)*C)", hint: "With equal charges the two currents have the same sense, so the flux from loop 2 adds to that of loop 1: the effective inductance is larger than L, not smaller." },
    { expr: "1/(L*C)", hint: "That is the frequency of one circuit alone. The coupling changes the effective inductance in each mode." },
    { expr: "1/((L + 2*M)*C)", hint: "M enters once: the flux through loop 1 is L I₁ + M I₂, and in this mode I₂ = I₁." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-a2" label="$\omega_2^2 =$" expr="1/((L - M)*C)"
  vars={{ L: [1, 2], M: [0.1, 0.8], C: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ L: "L", M: "M", C: "C" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/((L + M)*C)", hint: "That is the mode with equal charges. With opposite charges I₂ = -I₁, and the coupling term changes sign." },
    { expr: "1/((L^2 - M^2)*C)", hint: "Check the units: L² C is henry squared times farad, not henry times farad. The two loops are coupled through one term, not a product." },
    { expr: "1/(L*C)", hint: "That is the frequency of one circuit alone. The coupling changes the effective inductance in each mode." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A loop equation says that the emfs add up to zero. The inductor of loop 1 makes an emf $-L\dot I_1$ and the coupled loop adds $-M\dot I_2$. By symmetry the two normal modes are $Q_2 = \pm Q_1$.

<Step title="The equations">

Going round loop 1, the capacitor voltage $Q_1/C$ balances the two induced emfs; loop 2 is the mirror image:

<Formula label="Coupled LC circuits: loop equations" tex="L\ddot Q_1 + M\ddot Q_2 + \frac{Q_1}{C} = 0, \qquad M\ddot Q_1 + L\ddot Q_2 + \frac{Q_2}{C} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3.2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The mutual inductance comes from the lessons on [inductance](/courses/8-02/transformers-and-magnetic-materials) (8.02); the analogy with a mass on a spring, with $L$ in the place of the mass and $1/C$ in the place of the spring constant, is the one the lesson on [free oscillators](/courses/8-03/damped-free-oscillators) draws.

</Step>

<Step title="The two modes">

Add the two equations: the sum $Q_1 + Q_2$ obeys $(L + M)(\ddot Q_1 + \ddot Q_2) + (Q_1 + Q_2)/C = 0$. Subtract them: the difference obeys $(L - M)(\ddot Q_1 - \ddot Q_2) + (Q_1 - Q_2)/C = 0$. Each is the equation of a harmonic oscillator. In the first mode the difference stays zero, in the second the sum:

<Formula label="Coupled LC circuits: normal-mode frequencies" tex="\omega_1^2 = \frac{1}{(L + M)\,C}\ \ (Q_1 = Q_2), \qquad \omega_2^2 = \frac{1}{(L - M)\,C}\ \ (Q_1 = -Q_2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3.2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $M = 0$ the circuits are independent and both frequencies become $1/\sqrt{LC}$. The frequencies are real as long as $M < L$, which holds for two identical coils: the flux one coil sends through the other cannot be more than the flux through itself. For $L = 1.0$, $M = 0.4$, $C = 1.0$ in some set of units: $\omega_1^2 = 0.714$ and $\omega_2^2 = 1.667$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

What current pattern belongs to each mode? The box asks about the lower-frequency one.

<Hint>

The lower frequency is the one with the larger effective inductance. In which mode do the two fluxes add?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="In the lower-frequency mode the currents are:" choices={["Equal and circulating the same way round", "Equal in size and circulating opposite ways round", "Different in size, but circulating the same way round", "Zero in one loop"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The mode with the larger effective inductance has the lower frequency.

<Step title="The pattern">

The lower frequency is $\omega_1$, with $Q_1 = Q_2$ and so $I_1 = I_2$: equal currents, going the same way round. Their fluxes add, so each loop has an effective inductance $L + M$. In the other mode the currents are equal and opposite, and the fluxes subtract.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

If $M$ is reversed in sign, the pairing of mode and frequency swaps. That is why we fixed the sign of $M$ in the statement.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={8}>

Could you have found the two modes from a symmetry argument alone? Say which symmetry, which matrix describes it, and what it tells you. (Self-marked.)

<Hint>

Swap the two circuits. Write the operation as a matrix that acts on $(Q_1, Q_2)$, and ask whether the swapped motion is also a solution.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Swapping the two identical circuits does not change the equations. A symmetry operation of this kind has its own eigenvectors, and the lessons show that they are the normal modes when the eigenvalues differ.

<Step title="The symmetry">

The operation is the swap matrix $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which turns $(Q_1, Q_2)$ into $(Q_2, Q_1)$. The two loop equations change places, so the swapped version of a solution is again a solution. In the words of [the lesson on symmetry](/courses/8-03/symmetry-infinite-oscillators): the symmetry matrix commutes with the matrix of the equations of motion. (There the mirror matrix has minus signs because the displacements of a mirrored pair point in opposite directions. Here the charges themselves are swapped.)

</Step>

<Step title="What it gives">

The eigenvalues of the swap matrix are $+1$ for $(1, 1)$ and $-1$ for $(1, -1)$. They differ, so these two vectors are the normal modes, and no differential equation had to be solved. Only the frequencies need the equations: putting the vectors into the loop equations gives the two $\omega^2$ of part (a).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Mark yourself correct if you named the swap, found the eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, and said that these are the equal-charge and opposite-charge modes of part (a).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Three masses on a ring" points={20} mit="Problem 3.3">

Three identical masses $m$ can slide without friction on a horizontal circle. Neighbouring masses are joined by identical springs, each with spring constant $k$, so the three springs form a closed ring. The circle is so large that its curvature does not matter: treat each spring as lying along the circle and ignore gravity. Let $x_1$, $x_2$, $x_3$ be the small displacements of the masses along the circle from their equilibrium positions, all counted in the same sense of rotation, and number the masses in that order round the ring, so that mass 1 is joined to masses 2 and 3.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Write the equation of motion of mass 1 as a formula for $\ddot x_1$ in $x_1$, $x_2$, $x_3$, $k$ and $m$. The other two follow by relabelling.

<Hint>

A spring pulls on each end with a force equal to $k$ times its change of length. What is the change of length of the spring between masses 1 and 2, in terms of $x_1$ and $x_2$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Mass 1 has two springs, one to each neighbour. Add the two forces, taking care of the sign: a spring that is stretched pulls mass 1 towards its neighbour.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$\ddot x_1 =$" expr="(k/m)*(x2 - 2*x1 + x3)"
  vars={{ k: [1, 3], m: [1, 3], x1: [-1, 1], x2: [-1, 1], x3: [-1, 1] }}
  names={{ k: "k", m: "m", x1: "x_1", x2: "x_2", x3: "x_3" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(k/m)*(x2 - x1)", hint: "The ring is closed, so mass 1 has a second spring, to mass 3. Its force is missing here." },

    { expr: "T/(m*a)", hint: "That is the mode with the beads moving together. Put y₂ = -y₁ into the first equation." },
    { expr: "2*T/(m*a)", hint: "That counts the two pieces of string next to bead 1 as if bead 2 stood still. With y₂ = -y₁ the middle piece is steeper than that." },
    { expr: "4*T/(m*a)", hint: "Put y₂ = -y₁ into the first equation again and add the terms with care: the factor is not 4." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both beads have the same mass and sit symmetrically, so the two modes are the in-phase and the out-of-phase motion.

<Step title="The modes">

Put $y_1 = A_1\cos(\omega t + \phi)$ and $y_2 = A_2\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, so $-\omega^2A_1 = -2\omega_0^2A_1 + \omega_0^2A_2$. For $A_2 = A_1$ one gets $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2$. For $A_2 = -A_1$ one gets $\omega^2 = 3\omega_0^2$. The second equation gives the same results:

<Formula label="Two beads: normal modes" tex="A = (1, 1):\ \omega_1^2 = \frac{T}{ma}, \qquad A = (1, -1):\ \omega_2^2 = \frac{3T}{ma}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3.5(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

Sketch for the first mode: both beads on the same side, the string a gentle arch with a flat middle piece. Second mode: one bead up and one down, the string an S-shape that crosses the straight line in the middle, with the steepest slope in the middle piece.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson on the [beaded string](/courses/8-03/beaded-string-wave-equation) gives for a string with two beads and fixed ends $\omega^2 = 4\omega_0^2\sin^2(n\pi/6)$ for the mode number $n = 1, 2$ (the phase step from bead to bead is $n\pi/3$): for $n = 1$, $4\sin^2(\pi/6) = 1$ and for $n = 2$, $4\sin^2(\pi/3) = 3$, so $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2$ and $3\omega_0^2$. The two methods agree.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="c" points={10}>

Now the right-hand end of the string is shaken sideways: its displacement is $Y(t) = \Delta\cos\omega_d t$, while the left end stays fixed. Find the steady-state amplitudes $B_1$ and $B_2$ of the two beads, $y_1 = B_1\cos\omega_d t$ and $y_2 = B_2\cos\omega_d t$, as formulas in $\omega_0$, $\omega_d$ and $\Delta$.

<Hint>

Only the equation of bead 2 changes: the piece of string to its right now has its end at $Y(t)$, not at zero. Write the new equation of motion and keep $\omega_0^2 = T/(ma)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put $y_1 = B_1\cos\omega_d t$ and $y_2 = B_2\cos\omega_d t$. The first equation gives $B_1$ in terms of $B_2$, and the second then gives $B_2$. You will need $(2\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)^2 - \omega_0^4 = (\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)(3\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-c1" label="$B_1 =$" expr="Delta*w0^4/((w0^2 - wd^2)*(3*w0^2 - wd^2))"
  vars={{ Delta: [0.5, 2], w0: [1, 1.5], wd: [3, 5] }}
  names={{ Delta: "\\Delta", w0: "\\omega_0", wd: "\\omega_d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Delta*w0^2*(2*w0^2 - wd^2)/((w0^2 - wd^2)*(3*w0^2 - wd^2))", hint: "That is the amplitude of bead 2, the one next to the shaken end. The first equation, which has no driving term, gives B₁ once B₂ is known." },
    { expr: "Delta*w0^2/((w0^2 - wd^2)*(3*w0^2 - wd^2))", hint: "Check the units: B₁ and Δ are lengths, so the ratio has no dimension. This ratio has the dimension of one over the frequency squared." },
    { expr: "Delta*w0^4/((w0^2 - wd^2)*(2*w0^2 - wd^2))", hint: "The denominator is the determinant (2ω₀² - ω_d²)² - ω₀⁴, which factorises as (ω₀² - ω_d²)(3ω₀² - ω_d²)." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p5-c2" label="$B_2 =$" expr="Delta*w0^2*(2*w0^2 - wd^2)/((w0^2 - wd^2)*(3*w0^2 - wd^2))"
  vars={{ Delta: [0.5, 2], w0: [1, 1.5], wd: [3, 5] }}
  names={{ Delta: "\\Delta", w0: "\\omega_0", wd: "\\omega_d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Delta*w0^4/((w0^2 - wd^2)*(3*w0^2 - wd^2))", hint: "That is the amplitude of bead 1. Bead 2 is the bead next to the shaken end, and its amplitude has one more factor that depends on ω_d." },
    { expr: "Delta*w0^2*(w0^2 - wd^2)/((w0^2 - wd^2)*(3*w0^2 - wd^2))", hint: "Try ω_d = 0, a very slow shake: the string is then a straight line to the displaced end, and bead 2 sits at two thirds of Δ. This formula gives one third." },
    { expr: "Delta*w0^2*(2*w0^2 - wd^2)/((2*w0^2 - wd^2)^2 - 2*w0^4)", hint: "The two off-diagonal entries are both ω₀², so the determinant is (2ω₀² - ω_d²)² - ω₀⁴." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The shaken end acts as a driving term in the equation of the bead next to it. As in the lesson on driven coupled oscillators, the steady state follows the drive and the two amplitudes solve two linear equations.

<Step title="The driven equation">

Bead 2 now has the string end at $Y(t)$ on its right:

<Formula label="Two beads: driven equations" tex="\ddot y_1 = \omega_0^2\left(y_2 - 2y_1\right), \qquad \ddot y_2 = \omega_0^2\left(y_1 - 2y_2\right) + \omega_0^2\,\Delta\cos\omega_d t" cite="mit" at="Problem 3.5(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

(We assume the shaking is so small that the tension stays $T$.)

</Step>

<Step title="The amplitudes">

Put in $y_1 = B_1\cos\omega_d t$ and $y_2 = B_2\cos\omega_d t$:

<Formula label="Two beads: amplitude equations" tex="(2\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)\,B_1 - \omega_0^2B_2 = 0, \qquad -\omega_0^2B_1 + (2\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)\,B_2 = \omega_0^2\Delta" cite="reading2" at="p. 2 (the method; this system is ours)" />

The first gives $B_1 = \omega_0^2B_2/(2\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)$. Into the second: $B_2\left[(2\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2) - \dfrac{\omega_0^4}{2\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2}\right] = \omega_0^2\Delta$, and the bracket times $(2\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)$ is $(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)(3\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)$. So

<Formula label="Two beads: steady-state amplitudes" tex="B_2 = \frac{\Delta\,\omega_0^2\,(2\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)(3\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)}, \qquad B_1 = \frac{\Delta\,\omega_0^4}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)(3\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3.5(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The denominator vanishes at $\omega_d^2 = \omega_0^2$ and $3\omega_0^2$, the two normal-mode frequencies of part (b): resonance. For $\omega_d \to 0$ the end moves very slowly, and the formulas give $B_1 = \Delta/3$ and $B_2 = 2\Delta/3$: the string is straight from the fixed left end to the displaced right end, so the beads sit at one third and two thirds of $\Delta$. That is the static shape.

</Step>

</Solution>
