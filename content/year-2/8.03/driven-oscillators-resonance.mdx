So far each oscillator was prepared, released and left alone. Today it is **driven** by an external force, and the questions are how it responds and at which frequency it moves (transcript, pp. 2–3).

## The quality factor

The [damped oscillator](/courses/8-03/damped-free-oscillators) had three regimes, set by how $\gamma$ compares with $\omega_0$ (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 1–2). One number sums this up (transcript, p. 2):

<Formula label="Quality factor" tex="Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\gamma}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

As $Q$ falls to $0.5$, that is $\gamma = 2\omega_0$, the system reaches the critical point and the oscillation disappears (p. 2).

## The equation of motion

Take the rod of the last lecture and add a driving torque (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2):

<Formula label="Driving torque" tex="\tau_{\text{drive}} = d_0\cos\omega_dt" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

with amplitude $d_0$ and driving angular frequency $\omega_d$. Dividing the total torque by the moment of inertia and writing $f_0 = d_0/I$ (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 3):

<Formula label="Driven damped oscillator" tex="\ddot\theta + \gamma\dot\theta + \omega_0^2\theta = f_0\cos\omega_dt, \qquad \gamma = \frac{3b}{ml^2}, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{3g}{2l}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Will the rod follow its own frequency, the driving frequency, or something in between (transcript, pp. 3–4)?

## The steady-state solution

Go complex: $\ddot z + \gamma\dot z + \omega_0^2z = f_0e^{i\omega_dt}$, and guess (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 4)

<Formula label="Trial solution" tex="z(t) = Ae^{i(\omega_dt - \delta)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The frequency $\omega_d$ is chosen to cancel $e^{i\omega_dt}$ on the right; $\delta$ allows the system to lag behind the drive, since it takes time to "feel" it (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 4). With $\dot z = i\omega_dz$ and $\ddot z = -\omega_d^2z$, the exponentials cancel and (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 5)

<Formula label="Complex equation" tex="(-\omega_d^2 + i\gamma\omega_d + \omega_0^2)A = f_0e^{i\delta} = f_0\cos\delta + if_0\sin\delta" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

One complex equation is two real ones, for the two unknowns $A$ and $\delta$ (transcript, p. 5):

<Formula label="Real and imaginary parts" tex="(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)A = f_0\cos\delta, \qquad \gamma\omega_dA = f_0\sin\delta" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Squaring and adding removes $\delta$; dividing removes $A$ (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="Amplitude and phase" tex="A(\omega_d) = \frac{f_0}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega_d^2}}, \qquad \tan\delta = \frac{\gamma\omega_d}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

and $\theta(t) = A(\omega_d)\cos(\omega_dt - \delta(\omega_d))$ (notes, eq. 1).

## The full solution: transients

This solution has **no free constant**: once $f_0$ and $\omega_d$ are given, everything is fixed (transcript, p. 6). A second-order equation needs two, and a student names what is missing: the homogeneous solution (p. 6). If the system is underdamped (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 7):

<Formula label="Full solution, underdamped case" tex="\theta(t) = A(\omega_d)\cos(\omega_dt - \delta) + Be^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega t + \alpha)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

$B$ and $\alpha$ come from the initial conditions; with no damping, critical damping or overdamping, the corresponding homogeneous solution is used instead: four kinds of solution in all (transcript, p. 7). Three omegas are now in play (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 7): $\omega_0$, the natural frequency without drag; $\omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$, the free frequency with drag; and $\omega_d$, the driving frequency.

The first term is the **steady state**, there for ever; the second decays (transcript, p. 8). At first the sum looks strange, the **transient**; wait, and only the oscillation at $\omega_d$ remains, as if the system, shaken long enough, accepts it (p. 8). On the air track, a cart driven by a motor slightly faster than its natural frequency stops and starts irregularly, then settles to the driving frequency (pp. 8–9). The same equation is solved in 18.03's [inhomogeneous equations](/courses/18-03/inhomogeneous-equations-and-stability).

## Driving a pendulum

A ball of mass $m$ on a string of length $l$; its top is moved sideways by (transcript, p. 9)

<Formula label="Moving the top" tex="d(t) = \Delta\sin\omega_dt" cite="reading2" at="p. 9" />

(In the lecture both are spoken "delta"; the notes write the drive amplitude $\Delta$ and the phase lag $\delta$, as here.) With $x$ horizontal and $y$ up, measured from the ball's rest position, three forces act: gravity $-mg\hat y$, drag $-b\dot x\hat x$ and the tension (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 10). For small angles $\sin\theta \approx (x - d)/l$ and $\cos\theta \approx 1$, so the tension is $-T\frac{x - d}{l}\hat x + T\hat y$ (p. 10). There is no vertical motion, so $T = mg$; horizontally (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 11–12)

<Formula label="Pendulum equation" tex="m\ddot x = -b\dot x - \frac{mg}{l}(x - d) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = f_0\sin\omega_dt" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

with $\gamma = b/m$, $\omega_0^2 = g/l$ and $f_0 = \omega_0^2\Delta$ (transcript, p. 12). The solution is already known; its meaning shows in the limits (pp. 12–13):

- **slow driving**, $\omega_d \to 0$: $A \to f_0/\omega_0^2 = \Delta$ and $\delta \to 0$. The ball follows the hand, in step;
- **fast driving**, $\omega_d \to \infty$: $A \to 0$ and $\delta \to \pi$. The ball barely moves, out of phase. (The notes and the lecture say that $\tan\delta$ goes to infinity here; in fact $\tan\delta \to 0$ from below, which gives $\delta \to \pi$. It is at $\omega_d = \omega_0$ that $\tan\delta$ is infinite.)

A toy pendulum made for Lee's son shows both: shaken slowly, the ball follows; shaken as fast as possible, it hardly moves (transcript, p. 13).

## Resonance

Driven near its natural frequency, the same pendulum swings hugely while the hand barely moves (transcript, p. 13). At $\omega_d = \omega_0$ (p. 14):

<Formula label="Amplitude at resonance" tex="A(\omega_0) = \frac{f_0}{\omega_0\gamma} = \frac{\omega_0^2\Delta}{\omega_0\gamma} = Q\,\Delta" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

Moving the top 1 cm and getting an amplitude of about 1 m would mean $Q \approx 100$ (transcript, p. 14). Plotted against $\omega_d$, the amplitude starts at $\Delta$, peaks near $\omega_0$ (the maximum is slightly below $\omega_0$, a homework exercise) and falls to zero; the phase rises from 0 through $\pi/2$ at $\omega_0$ to $\pi$ (notes, pp. 4–5; transcript, pp. 14–15). The Mexican-hat mass on a spring, driven by a motor, shows the same: small motion at low frequency, a large swing near $\omega_0$, and at high frequency small motion opposite to the drive (pp. 15–16).

Resonance is everywhere (transcript, pp. 16–17): a pipe in Lee's office air conditioner humming at one setting and whistling at another; an old washing machine that "walked" round the flat until its spin speed was changed; the Z boson, a resonance near 90 GeV in electron–positron collisions. A wine glass, rubbed with a wet finger, rings at about 683 Hz; driven by an amplified tone at that frequency and turned up, it shatters; Lee stresses that the glass must be of very high quality for the amplitude to grow so large, which in the language above means a large $Q$ (pp. 17–19). One particle has one resonance, two coupled particles have two, and the glass, made of countless particles, has infinitely many, of which rubbing excites the one it likes best (p. 17): the subject of the next lectures.

<WorkedExample title="A resonance curve of your own">
<Problem>
A mass on a spring has $\omega_0 = 10$ rad/s and $\gamma = 0.50$ s⁻¹, and is driven with $f_0 = 2.0$ m/s². Find $Q$, the static response $f_0/\omega_0^2$, and the amplitude and phase lag at $\omega_d = 8$, $10$ and $12$ rad/s.
</Problem>
<Step title="Q and the static response">
$Q = 10/0.50 = 20$. At very low driving frequency $A \to f_0/\omega_0^2 = 2.0/100 = 0.020$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="At and near resonance">
At $\omega_d = 10$: $A = f_0/(\omega_0\gamma) = 2.0/5.0 = 0.40$ m, $Q = 20$ times the static response, with $\delta = \pi/2$. At $\omega_d = 8$: $A = 2.0/\sqrt{36^2 + 0.25 \cdot 64} = 2.0/\sqrt{1312} \approx 0.055$ m, and $\tan\delta = 4/36$, $\delta \approx 0.11$ rad. At $\omega_d = 12$: $A = 2.0/\sqrt{44^2 + 0.25 \cdot 144} = 2.0/\sqrt{1972} \approx 0.045$ m, and $\tan\delta = 6/(-44)$, so $\delta \approx \pi - 0.136 \approx 3.01$ rad.
</Step>
<Step title="Reading the curve">
Twenty per cent off resonance the amplitude has dropped by a factor of about 7 to 9, and the phase has moved almost all the way to 0 below and to $\pi$ above: with $Q = 20$ the peak is narrow.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $Q = \omega_0/\gamma$; critical damping at $Q = 0.5$.
- Driven: $\ddot\theta + \gamma\dot\theta + \omega_0^2\theta = f_0\cos\omega_dt$; steady state at $\omega_d$ with $A(\omega_d)$ and $\tan\delta$ above.
- Full solution = steady state + decaying homogeneous solution (the transient).
- Slow: in step. Fast: tiny and out of phase. At $\omega_0$: amplitude $Q$ times the static one, lag $\pi/2$.
