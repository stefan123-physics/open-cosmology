This set practises the lessons on [the uncertainty principle and two-dimensional waves](/courses/8-03/uncertainty-principle-2d-waves), which first sets up the bead mesh, and on [waves in two and three dimensions and Snell's law](/courses/8-03/two-dimensional-waves-snells-law). The three problems follow MIT's Homework 8, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for its homeworks, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. MIT gives 25 points to each of the three problems, so the set has 75 points, and all three count for the score.

Notation: in Problem 1 MIT's sheet numbers the rows of beads with $j$ and the columns with $k$, so on this page $k$ is a column number, not a wave number. The modes are counted with the whole numbers $n_x$ and $n_y$ of the lesson. MIT writes the tensions $T_h$ and $T_v$ (the lesson writes the same two letters with capital subscripts). In Problem 2 the angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ belong to the light beam; in Problem 3 $\alpha$ is the angle of the rainbow and $\theta$ the angle of incidence on the drop, as on MIT's sheet. The angle of the ray inside the drop, $\phi$, is our own name. MIT's figures are described in words here.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Nine beads on a square mesh" points={25} mit="Problem 8.1">

A square frame with sides of length $L$ lies fixed in the plane $z = 0$, between $x = 0$ and $x = L$ and between $y = 0$ and $y = L$. Three strings run across it from left to right and three from bottom to top, evenly spaced, so they cross at nine points. At every crossing sits a bead of mass $m$: a grid of 3 × 3 beads. The ends of all strings are fixed to the frame. Every horizontal string has the tension $T_h$ and every vertical string the tension $T_v$. The beads move only along $z$, at right angles to the frame, and their displacements are small.

MIT numbers the rows $j = 1, 2, 3$ from the bottom to the top and the columns $k = 1, 2, 3$ from left to right. So the bead in row $j$ and column $k$ rests at $x = kL/4$, $y = jL/4$, and its displacement is $\psi_{j,k}(t)$. The strings themselves have no mass.

<Part label="a" points={13}>

Find the normal modes and their frequencies. Count the modes with two whole numbers: $n_x$ for the pattern along $x$ (along a row) and $n_y$ for the pattern along $y$ (along a column).

Give the shape of mode $(n_x, n_y)$: the displacement of bead $(j, k)$ at the instant of largest displacement, divided by the amplitude $A$ of the mode. Then give the square of its angular frequency, $\omega^2$, in terms of $T_h$, $T_v$, $m$, $L$, $n_x$ and $n_y$.

<Hint>

Look at one bead. Four pieces of string pull on it: two of a horizontal string and two of a vertical one. For small displacements each piece gives a force along $z$ equal to its tension times its slope. How long is each piece?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The lesson found the modes of a mesh inside fixed walls: a sine along $x$ times a sine along $y$, each zero on both walls. Here the walls are the frame, at $k = 0$ and $k = 4$, and at $j = 0$ and $j = 4$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put the product of sines into the equation of motion of one bead. The sum over the two neighbours in a row is $\sin(\kappa(k + 1)) + \sin(\kappa(k - 1)) = 2\sin(\kappa k)\cos\kappa$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each bead is pulled by four pieces of string. The pull is linear in the displacements, and the rows and the columns do not mix, so a mode is a mode of a row times a mode of a column: a sine times a sine, as in the lesson.

<Step title="The equation of motion of one bead">

Three beads divide each string into four pieces, so neighbouring beads are $a = L/4$ apart. A piece of a horizontal string between beads $(j, k)$ and $(j, k + 1)$ has the slope $(\psi_{j,k+1} - \psi_{j,k})/a$, and pulls on bead $(j, k)$ along $z$ with $T_h$ times this slope. Adding the four pieces:

<Formula label="Equation of motion of bead (j, k)" tex="m\,\frac{d^2\psi_{j,k}}{dt^2} = \frac{T_h}{a}\left(\psi_{j,k+1} + \psi_{j,k-1} - 2\psi_{j,k}\right) + \frac{T_v}{a}\left(\psi_{j+1,k} + \psi_{j-1,k} - 2\psi_{j,k}\right), \qquad a = \frac{L}{4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 8.1(a), p. 2 (our own solution: the forces of the four pieces of string on one bead)" />

The frame counts as beads that never move: $\psi_{j,k} = 0$ when $j$ or $k$ is 0 or 4.

</Step>

<Step title="The shapes">

Inside fixed walls the modes are products of sines that vanish on the walls (Lecture 16 notes, p. 3). With the walls at $k = 0, 4$ and $j = 0, 4$:

<Formula label="Normal modes of the mesh" tex="\psi_{j,k}(t) = A\,\sin\left(\frac{n_x\pi k}{4}\right)\sin\left(\frac{n_y\pi j}{4}\right)\cos(\omega t), \qquad n_x, n_y = 1, 2, 3" cite="reading1" at="p. 3 (the modes inside walls, here with x = kL/4, y = jL/4 and both pairs of walls a distance L apart)" />

A phase may be added to $\omega t$: that only resets the clock. The value $n_x = 4$ gives zero on every bead, and $n_x = 5$ gives the pattern of $n_x = 3$ again with the opposite sign, so there are three patterns along $x$, three along $y$, and $3 \times 3 = 9$ modes: as many as there are beads.

</Step>

<Step title="The frequencies">

Put the mode into the equation of motion. With $\kappa = n_x\pi/4$ the two neighbours in a row give $\sin(\kappa(k+1)) + \sin(\kappa(k-1)) = 2\sin(\kappa k)\cos\kappa$, so the first bracket is $-2(1 - \cos\kappa)$ times $\psi_{j,k}$, and the same holds along the column. With $1 - \cos(2u) = 2\sin^2 u$:

<Formula label="Frequencies of the nine modes" tex="\omega^2 = \frac{4T_h}{ma}\sin^2\left(\frac{n_x\pi}{8}\right) + \frac{4T_v}{ma}\sin^2\left(\frac{n_y\pi}{8}\right) = \frac{16}{mL}\left[T_h\sin^2\left(\frac{n_x\pi}{8}\right) + T_v\sin^2\left(\frac{n_y\pi}{8}\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 8.1(a), p. 2 (our own solution; the dispersion relation of Lecture 16 notes, p. 2, with k_x = n_x π/L, k_y = n_y π/L and a_H = a_V = L/4)" />

The three values of the squared sine are $\sin^2(\pi/8) = 0.146$, $\sin^2(2\pi/8) = 0.5$ and $\sin^2(3\pi/8) = 0.854$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: a tension divided by a mass and a length is N/(kg m) = 1/s². For $n_x = n_y = 1$ all beads move the same way and the frequency is the lowest; each extra half wave bends the strings more and raises it. The highest mode, $(3, 3)$, has $\omega$ equal to $\sin(3\pi/8)/\sin(\pi/8) = 1 + \sqrt2 = 2.414$ times that of the lowest mode, whatever the two tensions are. With equal tensions, $T_h = T_v = T$, the modes $(1, 3)$, $(3, 1)$ and $(2, 2)$ share one frequency, $\omega^2 = 16T/(mL)$, because $\sin^2(\pi/8) + \sin^2(3\pi/8) = 1 = 2\sin^2(2\pi/8)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={12}>

Now take $T_v = 1000\,T_h$. Draw nine diagrams, one for each normal mode, in the order of rising frequency. In each diagram mark every bead with $+$ (it moves up), $-$ (it moves down) or $0$ (it does not move), in three rows as the beads lie in the frame: row $j = 3$ on top.

Swapping all $+$ and $-$ signs of a diagram gives the same mode: it is the same motion half a period later, or the mode vector times $-1$. MIT gives two of the nine diagrams as examples. In the lowest mode all nine beads have a $+$. The fifth mode (the middle one of the nine, so the fifth from either end) has the top row $-\ 0\ +$, the middle row $0\ 0\ 0$ and the bottom row $+\ 0\ -$.

The diagrams have no answer box: draw them, then open the solution and mark yourself. As a check of our own, answer the three questions below. In the choices the rows are written from the top row to the bottom row, with a slash between rows.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The vertical strings are a thousand times tighter, so a bend along a column costs far more than a bend along a row. The number $n_y$ sorts the modes into three groups, and inside a group $n_x$ gives a small step.

<Step title="Order the modes">

With $T_v = 1000\,T_h$ the result of part (a) is

<Formula label="Frequencies for the tight vertical strings" tex="\omega^2 = \frac{16\,T_h}{mL}\left[\sin^2\left(\frac{n_x\pi}{8}\right) + 1000\,\sin^2\left(\frac{n_y\pi}{8}\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 8.1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The second term is at least 146 and the first at most 0.854, so every mode with $n_y = 1$ lies below every mode with $n_y = 2$, and those lie below every mode with $n_y = 3$. Inside each group the frequency rises with $n_x$. The order is $(n_x, n_y) = (1,1), (2,1), (3,1), (1,2), (2,2), (3,2), (1,3), (2,3), (3,3)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The signs">

Along a row, $\sin(n_x\pi k/4)$ for $k = 1, 2, 3$ has the signs $+\,+\,+$ for $n_x = 1$, $+\,0\,-$ for $n_x = 2$ and $+\,-\,+$ for $n_x = 3$. Along a column the same holds with $n_y$ and $j$. The sign of bead $(j, k)$ is the product of the two. In the table the top row is $j = 3$, and the last column gives $\omega$ divided by the lowest frequency $\omega_1$.

| Mode | $(n_x, n_y)$ | Top row | Middle row | Bottom row | $\omega/\omega_1$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | (1, 1) | + + + | + + + | + + + | 1 |
| 2 | (2, 1) | + 0 − | + 0 − | + 0 − | 1.0012 |
| 3 | (3, 1) | + − + | + − + | + − + | 1.0024 |
| 4 | (1, 2) | − − − | 0 0 0 | + + + | 1.8471 |
| 5 | (2, 2) | − 0 + | 0 0 0 | + 0 − | 1.8478 |
| 6 | (3, 2) | − + − | 0 0 0 | + − + | 1.8484 |
| 7 | (1, 3) | + + + | − − − | + + + | 2.4132 |
| 8 | (2, 3) | + 0 − | − 0 + | + 0 − | 2.4137 |
| 9 | (3, 3) | + − + | − + − | + − + | 2.4142 |

The lowest frequency is $\omega_1 = 48.43\sqrt{T_h/(mL)}$. The sizes of the displacements are not all equal: where two beads both have a $+$, the one nearer the middle of a half wave moves more (in mode 1 the centre bead moves twice as far as a corner bead).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Mode 1 is MIT's first example and mode 5 is its second. Inside a group each diagram has one more change of sign along a row than the one before; from one group to the next the columns get one more change of sign. The three groups lie near 1, 1.848 and 2.414 times $\omega_1$. These are the ratios $\sin(n_y\pi/8)/\sin(\pi/8)$ of a single string with three beads: with almost slack horizontal strings each column swings nearly as a three-bead string on its own, and the weak horizontal strings only split each of its three frequencies into three close ones.

</Step>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A beam through a plate" points={25} mit="Problem 8.2">

A beam of light travels in vacuum ($n_1 = 1$) and meets a flat transparent plate with the index of refraction $n_2$. In the vacuum the beam makes the angle $\alpha = 60°$ with the normal to the plate. The beam crosses the plate and enters, through the plate's second face, a third material with the index of refraction $n_3$. There it makes the angle $\beta = 30°$ with the normal. The two faces of the plate are parallel.

MIT's figure shows the three layers one above the other, with the normal as a dashed vertical line: the beam comes from below at $\alpha$ to the normal, runs steeply through the plate, and leaves upwards at $\beta$ to the normal.

<Part label="a" points={13}>

What range of values can $n_2$ have? Give the limit that the beam's path sets on $n_2$, and say on which side of it $n_2$ must lie.

Then, as a check of our own: for a plate with $n_2 = 1.5$, what angle does the beam make with the normal inside the plate, in degrees?

<Hint>

Write Snell's law for the first face. For which values of $n_2$ does it have a solution for the angle inside the plate?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The sine of an angle is at most 1. Does the second face add a condition on $n_2$? Look at what Snell's law keeps the same from layer to layer.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-limit" label="limit on $n_2$ $=$" value="0.866" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-limit" />

<Answer id="p2-side" label="$n_2$ must be:" choices={[
  "Smaller than this limit",
  "Larger than this limit",
  "Equal to this limit",
  "Larger than this limit and smaller than 1"
]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p2-angle" label="angle in the plate for $n_2 = 1.5$:" value="35.26" unit="degrees" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-angle" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Snell's law must have a solution at each face, or the beam does not get through. Only the first face limits $n_2$.

<Step title="Snell's law at both faces">

Call the angle to the normal inside the plate $\theta_2$. The faces are parallel, so the beam meets the second face at the same angle $\theta_2$:

<Formula label="Snell's law at the two faces" tex="n_1\sin\alpha = n_2\sin\theta_2 = n_3\sin\beta" cite="reading1" at="p. 7 (Snell's law, used once at each face)" />

The product $n\sin\theta$ is the same in all three layers.

</Step>

<Step title="The first face">

With $n_1 = 1$, $\sin\theta_2 = \sin\alpha/n_2$. A sine cannot be larger than 1, so there is a beam in the plate only if

<Formula label="Range of the plate's index" tex="n_2 > \sin\alpha = \sin 60° = \frac{\sqrt3}{2} = 0.866" cite="mit" at="Problem 8.2(a), p. 2 (our own solution, with the condition for no transmission of Lecture 16 notes, p. 7)" />

For a smaller $n_2$ the notes' condition $(n_1/n_2)\sin\alpha > 1$ holds and nothing is transmitted: the beam is reflected back into the vacuum. At $n_2 = \sin\alpha$ exactly the beam would run along the face and never reach the far side, so the limit itself is left out.

</Step>

<Step title="The second face">

There $\sin\beta = n_2\sin\theta_2/n_3 = \sin\alpha/n_3$: the plate's index has dropped out. Once the beam is in the plate, it leaves at $\beta = 30°$ whatever $n_2$ is, so the second face sets no further limit, and there is no upper limit on $n_2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The sample plate">

For $n_2 = 1.5$: $\sin\theta_2 = 0.866/1.5 = 0.577$, so $\theta_2 = 35.26°$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The limit 0.866 lies below 1. Ordinary transparent materials have an index of at least 1 for visible light, so for any ordinary plate the answer is: every value works. The limit matters only for a material with an index below 1. Two remarks on our reading of the question. First, the limit is our own answer: MIT gives none. Second, MIT's figure draws the beam steeper in the plate than in the third material. If the figure were drawn to scale, that would mean $\theta_2 < \beta$ and so $n_2 > n_3$; the text of the problem does not ask for this, and we have not used it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={12}>

What is the value of $n_3$?

<Hint>

Compare the first layer with the third directly. What does a plate with parallel faces do to the product $n\sin\theta$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-n3" label="$n_3 =$" value="1.732" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-n3" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The product $n\sin\theta$ is the same in every layer, so the plate in the middle does not matter.

<Step title="First and third layer">

From the chain of part (a), $n_1\sin\alpha = n_3\sin\beta$:

<Formula label="Index of the third material" tex="n_3 = \frac{n_1\sin\alpha}{\sin\beta} = \frac{\sin 60°}{\sin 30°} = \sqrt3 = 1.732" cite="mit" at="Problem 8.2(b), p. 2 (our own solution, with Snell's law of Lecture 16 notes, p. 7)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The beam is closer to the normal in the third material than in the vacuum ($30°$ against $60°$), so light is slower there and $n_3 > 1$, as found. The result does not contain $n_2$: a plate with parallel faces shifts a beam sideways but does not change the direction it has afterwards. With the sample plate of part (a), $1.5 \times \sin 35.26° = 0.866 = 1.732 \times \sin 30°$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The rainbow" points={25} mit="Problem 8.3">

Sunlight that travels almost horizontally enters the water drops of a dark cloud. Light that comes back out of the drops makes the rainbow, at an angle $\alpha$ of about 40 to 42 degrees.

MIT's figure shows one ray in a round drop. The ray arrives horizontally and meets the surface at the angle of incidence $\theta$ (the angle to the normal, which for a sphere is the line through the centre). It is refracted into the drop, reflected once at the back of the drop, and refracted out at the front. The outgoing ray runs back towards the side of the Sun. The angle $\alpha$ is the angle between the outgoing ray and the line of the incoming sunlight. The index of refraction of water is $n$, and that of air is taken as 1.

<Part label="a" points={12}>

Find $\alpha$ in terms of $\theta$ and $n$. Then plot $\alpha$ against $\theta$ for $0 \le \theta \le 90°$ with $n = 1.33$ (use any plotting tool), and find the largest value of $\alpha$ and the angle of incidence at which it is reached, both in degrees.

In the formula box use radians.

<Hint>

Call the angle of the ray to the normal inside the drop $\phi$. The drop is round, so the triangle made by the centre and the two ends of a straight piece of the path has two equal sides: the ray meets the surface at the same angle $\phi$ every time.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Add up how far the ray turns: at the entry, at the reflection and at the exit. A ray that turned by 180° in all would go straight back, with $\alpha = 0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the largest $\alpha$ set $d\alpha/d\theta = 0$. You need $d\phi/d\theta$: differentiate Snell's law, $\sin\theta = n\sin\phi$, on both sides.

</Hint>

**The idea.** Follow the direction of the ray through its three meetings with the surface and add up the turns. Then look for the angle of incidence at which $\alpha$ stops growing.

<Step title="The angles in the drop">

At the entry Snell's law gives the angle $\phi$ inside the drop:

<Formula label="Refraction into the drop" tex="\sin\theta = n\sin\phi" cite="reading1" at="p. 7 (Snell's law with n = 1 for air)" />

The centre of the drop and the two ends of each straight piece of the path form a triangle with two equal sides (two radii). So the ray arrives at the back of the drop at the angle $\phi$ to the normal, leaves it at $\phi$ (the law of reflection, Lecture 16 notes, p. 7), and arrives at the front again at $\phi$. By Snell's law it then leaves the drop at the angle $\theta$.

</Step>

<Step title="Add up the turns">

At the entry the ray turns by $\theta - \phi$, at the reflection by $180° - 2\phi$, and at the exit by $\theta - \phi$ again, all in the same sense. The total turn is $180° + 2\theta - 4\phi$. A turn of exactly $180°$ would send the ray straight back along the line of the sunlight. For water $4\phi$ is larger than $2\theta$ (see the plot below), so the turn is less than $180°$, and $\alpha$ is what is missing:

<Formula label="Angle of the outgoing ray" tex="\alpha = 4\phi - 2\theta = 4\arcsin\left(\frac{\sin\theta}{n}\right) - 2\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 8.3(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The largest angle">

Differentiating Snell's law gives $\cos\theta = n\cos\phi\,(d\phi/d\theta)$. Then $d\alpha/d\theta = 4\,d\phi/d\theta - 2 = 0$ when $2\cos\theta = n\cos\phi$. Square it and use $n^2\cos^2\phi = n^2 - \sin^2\theta$:

<Formula label="Angle of incidence for the largest α" tex="4\cos^2\theta = n^2 - 1 + \cos^2\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cos\theta = \sqrt{\frac{n^2 - 1}{3}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 8.3(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

For $n = 1.33$ this gives $\theta = 59.58°$. Then $\phi = 40.42°$ and $\alpha = 4 \times 40.42° - 2 \times 59.58° = 42.52°$.

<Plot variable="t" domain={[0, 90]} range={[0, 50]} xLabel="θ (degrees)" yLabel="α (degrees)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "(4*asin(sin(t*pi/180)/1.33) - 2*t*pi/180)*180/pi" }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 59.58, y: 42.52, label: "largest α" }]}
  caption="The angle α of the outgoing ray against the angle of incidence θ, drawn from α = 4 arcsin(sin θ/n) − 2θ with n = 1.33. The curve rises from zero, is flat at its top (θ = 59.58°, α = 42.52°) and falls to 15.0° at θ = 90°." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $\theta = 0$ the ray goes through the centre and comes straight back: $\alpha = 0$, as the formula gives. For small angles $\alpha \approx (4/n - 2)\theta$, which is positive because $n < 2$, so the curve starts upwards, and it is a maximum that it reaches. The value 42.52° is the upper end of the 40 to 42 degrees that MIT quotes for the rainbow.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Why does the rainbow appear at the largest value of $\alpha$?

Sunlight falls on every part of a drop, so all angles of incidence from 0 to 90° occur. Write down your explanation, then choose the statement that agrees with it.

<Hint>

Look at your plot. Take rays whose angles of incidence lie in a band a few degrees wide. Over how wide a range of $\alpha$ do they leave, when the band is near $\theta = 20°$ and when it is near the top of the curve?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-why" choices={[
  "Only rays with that one angle of incidence are reflected inside the drop",
  "Near the top of the curve α hardly changes with θ, so rays from a wide band of angles of incidence leave in almost the same direction and the light piles up there",
  "At that angle the light inside the drop is totally reflected, so none is lost",
  "The rays that leave at the largest α have travelled the shortest way through the water"
]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The drop sends light back at every angle from 0 up to the largest $\alpha$. What makes the bow is not that light goes there, but how much.

<Step title="Where the light piles up">

Rays strike the drop at all angles of incidence. Near the top of the curve of part (a), $d\alpha/d\theta = 0$: changing $\theta$ by a few degrees changes $\alpha$ very little (at $\theta = 55°$ and at $\theta = 64°$ the curve is still at 42.07° and 42.06°). So the rays of a wide band of $\theta$ leave the drop in almost the same direction, and the light is concentrated there. Away from the top, a band of the same width is spread over many degrees of $\alpha$ and gives only a faint glow.

</Step>

<Step title="The edge">

No ray with one reflection leaves at an angle larger than the largest $\alpha$. So an observer who looks at the cloud with the Sun behind sees a bright edge at the angle $\alpha_{\max}$ from the direction straight away from the Sun, a weaker glow inside it, and no such light outside it. All drops that lie at this angle from that direction send their concentrated light to the eye, and they form a circle around it: the bow.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The other statements fail. Every ray is partly reflected inside the drop, whatever its angle. The reflection at the back is not total: the angle inside, $\phi$, is the angle at which the ray came in by refraction, so by Snell's law the ray can always leave again. And the length of the path in the water plays no part in the argument.

</Step>

</Solution>

**The idea.** Each colour has its own index, so its own largest $\alpha$, and its bow sits there.

<Step title="How the largest angle depends on n">

Repeat part (a) with a slightly larger index. For $n = 1.34$: $\cos\theta = \sqrt{(1.34^2 - 1)/3}$ gives $\theta = 59.004°$, $\phi = 39.770°$ and

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-second" label="in the second bow:" choices={[
  "Red is on top again, and the second bow lies inside the first",
  "Red is on top again, and the second bow lies outside the first",
  "Blue is on top (red on the inside), and the second bow lies outside the first",
  "Blue is on top (red on the inside), and the second bow lies inside the first"

<Solution>

**The idea.** The second bow comes from light that is reflected twice inside the drop before it leaves. The extra reflection puts the bow at a larger angle and turns the order of the colours around.

<Step title="Two reflections">

At every meeting with the surface a part of the light is reflected and a part is refracted out. The light that is reflected a second time meets the surface at the angle $\phi$ once more and can leave after that. Its total turn is $2(\theta - \phi) + 2(180° - 2\phi) = 360° + 2\theta - 6\phi$. A turn of $360°$ changes nothing, so the ray has in effect turned back by $6\phi - 2\theta$ in the other sense, and its angle to the line of the sunlight is

<Formula label="Angle after two reflections" tex="\alpha_2 = 180° + 2\theta - 6\phi = 180° + 2\theta - 6\arcsin\left(\frac{\sin\theta}{n}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 8.3(d), p. 3 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Where the second bow sits">

This angle starts at $180°$ for $\theta = 0$, falls, and turns around at a smallest value, where again many rays leave in nearly the same direction. The same steps as in part (a) give $\cos\theta = \sqrt{(n^2 - 1)/8}$ there. For $n = 1.33$: $\theta = 71.94°$, $\phi = 45.63°$ and $\alpha_2 = 50.10°$. So the second bow is a circle of about 50°, outside the first bow at 42.5°. It is fainter, because at each reflection a part of the light leaves the drop.

  ]}

</Step>

<Step title="The colours">

For $n = 1.34$ the smallest $\alpha_2$ is 52.71°, against 50.10° for $n = 1.33$: here more bending gives a larger angle. Blue light, with the higher index, is therefore at the larger angle. In the second bow blue is on top (outside) and red is on the inside: the colours are in the reverse order, with the two red edges facing each other.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $\theta = 0$ the ray crosses the drop along a diameter, comes back, is reflected again at the front and leaves at the back in its first direction: $\alpha_2 = 180°$, as the formula gives. The band between 42.52° and 50.10° gets no light from one or two reflections, so the sky between the two bows looks darker than the sky inside the first bow.

</Step>
