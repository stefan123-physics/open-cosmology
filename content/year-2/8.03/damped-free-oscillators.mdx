Last time's mass on a spring obeyed Hooke's law; this lecture adds energy, more systems of the same kind, and then the force that was missing: drag (transcript, pp. 1–3). A reminder from office hours: if the minimum of the potential is at $x = L$ rather than $0$, the Taylor expansion of the [harmonic oscillator](/courses/8-03/harmonic-oscillator) page is made about $L$ (pp. 1–2).

## One equation, many systems

**An LC circuit** (notes, p. 1). Take the clockwise current as positive, $I = dQ/dt$, with $I(0) = I_{\text{initial}}$ and $Q(0) = 0$. The voltage drops across the inductor and the capacitor add to zero:

<Formula label="LC circuit" tex="L\frac{dI}{dt} + \frac{Q}{C} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot Q + \frac{1}{LC}\,Q = 0, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Its solution is $Q(t) = A\cos(\omega_0t + \phi)$, and the initial conditions give $\phi = -\pi/2$, $A = I_{\text{initial}}/\omega_0$, so $Q(t) = (I_{\text{initial}}/\omega_0)\sin\omega_0t$ (notes, p. 1). Comparing with the spring, $L$ plays the mass, $1/C$ the spring constant and $Q$ the coordinate. In general (notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Generalised oscillator" tex="\mu\,\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\kappa x" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

with a generalised mass $\mu$, coordinate $x$ and spring constant $\kappa$.

**Energy** (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 2–3). The kinetic and potential energies are $\frac12\mu\dot x^2$ and $\frac12\kappa x^2$. With $x = A\cos(\omega_0t + \phi)$ and $\omega_0^2 = \kappa/\mu$:

<Formula label="Total energy" tex="E = \tfrac12\mu A^2\omega_0^2\sin^2(\omega_0t + \phi) + \tfrac12\kappa A^2\cos^2(\omega_0t + \phi) = \tfrac12\kappa A^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, eq. 2" />

The energy is proportional to the square of the amplitude and to the spring constant, and it is **constant**: it flows back and forth between kinetic and potential form, but the sum never changes (transcript, pp. 2–3).

## A rod on a nail

A uniform rod of length $l$ hangs from a nail; its centre of mass is at $l/2$ (transcript, p. 3). Coordinates: $\theta$ from the downward vertical, anticlockwise positive; the initial conditions are $\theta(0) = \theta_{\text{initial}}$ and $\dot\theta(0) = 0$ (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 4). Gravity on the centre of mass gives the torque $\tau = -mg\frac{l}{2}\sin\theta$ (p. 4). (During the break students noted that the vector $\vec R$ should point from the nail to the centre of mass for this sign, which Lee corrected; p. 11.) With $I = \frac13ml^2$ for a uniform rod, $\tau = I\ddot\theta$ gives (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 5)

<Formula label="Rod equation of motion" tex="\ddot\theta = -\frac{3g}{2l}\sin\theta = -\omega_0^2\sin\theta, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{3g}{2l}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

This is exact, even for large angles, but "life is hard": it has no simple solution (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 5). A computer could solve it; here, take the **small-angle** case, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, which is very good (notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Small-angle accuracy" tex="\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 0.9999\ (1°), \qquad 0.999\ (5°), \qquad 0.995\ (10°)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

That is 99.99%, 99.9% and 99.5%. (In the lecture the 5° value is said as 99%; the notes give 99.9%, which is correct.) The equation becomes $\ddot\theta = -\omega_0^2\theta$, solved last time: $\theta(t) = \theta_{\text{initial}}\cos\omega_0t$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 6).

## Adding drag

Real oscillations die out: something is missing (transcript, pp. 6–7). Add a drag torque (notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Drag torque" tex="\tau_{\text{drag}}(t) = -b\,\dot\theta(t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The minus sign makes it oppose the motion; proportionality to $\dot\theta$ is chosen because it can be solved, and reality lies somewhere between $\dot\theta$ and $\dot\theta^2$, but this model works well for the demonstrations (transcript, p. 7). With the small-angle approximation (notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Damped equation of motion" tex="\ddot\theta + \gamma\dot\theta + \omega_0^2\theta = 0, \qquad \omega_0^2 = \frac{3g}{2l}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{3b}{ml^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

**A vote** (transcript, p. 8). Two equal masses, each on a spring, one wearing a "Mexican hat" that pushes the air. Does the hat leave the frequency unchanged (11 votes), lower it (17), or raise it (2)?

**Solving with complex numbers** (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 9). Write $\theta = \text{Re}[z]$ with $z = e^{i\alpha t}$, which survives differentiation. Then $(-\alpha^2 + i\gamma\alpha + \omega_0^2)e^{i\alpha t} = 0$, and $e^{i\alpha t}$ is never zero, so

<Formula label="Characteristic equation" tex="\alpha^2 - i\gamma\alpha - \omega_0^2 = 0, \qquad \alpha = \frac{i\gamma}{2} \pm \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \frac{\gamma^2}{4}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

There are three possibilities for the square root (notes, p. 4). The same equation is solved from the mathematics side in 18.03's [damped oscillations](/courses/18-03/damped-oscillations).

## Underdamped: ω₀² > γ²/4

Small drag. Define $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4$; then $z_\pm = e^{-\gamma t/2}e^{\pm i\omega t}$, and the real combinations $\frac12(z_+ + z_-)$ and $-\frac i2(z_+ - z_-)$ give $e^{-\gamma t/2}\cos\omega t$ and $e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin\omega t$ (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 10, 12):

<Formula label="Underdamped solution" tex="\theta(t) = e^{-\gamma t/2}(a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t) = Ae^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega t + \phi), \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \frac{\gamma^2}{4}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

Two constants, so by uniqueness this is the solution (transcript, p. 10). The initial conditions give $\tan\phi = -\gamma/2\omega$ and $A = \theta_{\text{initial}}/\cos\phi$ (notes, p. 5). Its predictions (transcript, pp. 10–12):

- the amplitude $Ae^{-\gamma t/2}$ decays exponentially, but never reaches zero;
- the motion still oscillates, crossing zero infinitely many times;
- the frequency $\omega$ is **smaller** than $\omega_0$.

The experiment agrees: the mass with the hat oscillates more slowly, as 17 of the class predicted (p. 11). In the complex plane the circle of last lecture becomes a **spiral**, rotating while it shrinks towards the origin (pp. 12–13).

## Critically damped: ω₀² = γ²/4

Now $\omega = 0$ and the system stops oscillating (notes, p. 5; transcript, p. 13). As $\omega \to 0$, $e^{-\gamma t/2}\cos\omega t \to e^{-\gamma t/2}$, but $e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin\omega t \to 0$, which is useless; dividing by $\omega$ first gives $e^{-\gamma t/2}\frac{\sin\omega t}{\omega} \to te^{-\gamma t/2}$ (pp. 13–14). So

<Formula label="Critically damped solution" tex="\theta(t) = (A + Bt)\,e^{-\gamma t/2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

There is no oscillation: the system crosses zero at most once, for instance after a strong initial push (transcript, p. 14). Door closers are designed this way, to return quickly to the closed position (notes, p. 6; transcript, pp. 14–15).

## Overdamped: ω₀² < γ²/4

Huge drag, as in water (transcript, p. 15). Now $\alpha = i\Gamma$, with two values $\Gamma_1$ and $\Gamma_2$ (notes, p. 6; the notes call them $\Gamma_\pm$, with $\Gamma_1 = \Gamma_+$, $\Gamma_2 = \Gamma_-$, and the amplitudes $A_\pm$ are here $A_1$, $A_2$):

<Formula label="Overdamped solution" tex="\Gamma_1 = \frac{\gamma}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{\gamma^2}{4} - \omega_0^2}, \quad \Gamma_2 = \frac{\gamma}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{\gamma^2}{4} - \omega_0^2}, \qquad \theta(t) = A_1e^{-\Gamma_1t} + A_2e^{-\Gamma_2t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Two decaying exponentials, no oscillation; again the system crosses zero at most once and then creeps back (transcript, pp. 15–16). A pendulum bob made of magnets shows it: the drag is colossal, it passes zero once and then approaches it slowly (p. 16).

**Two questions** (transcript, pp. 16–17). A torsion pendulum with magnetic braking oscillates a few times and stops: underdamped, because it crosses zero several times. And a car suspension? Underdamped keeps the car bobbing; heavily overdamped makes every bump jolt; **critically damped** is the right design (p. 17).

Without drag the energy is conserved; with the drag force $-bv$ it is not, since the hat passes energy to the air. Why do such similar-looking equations behave so differently? Lee leaves that to think about (pp. 17–18).

<WorkedExample title="A damped spring of your own">
<Problem>
A $0.50$ kg mass on a spring with $k = 50$ N/m feels a drag force $-bv$ with $b = 1.0$ kg/s, so $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0$ with $\gamma = b/m$. Find $\omega_0$, $\gamma$ and the damped frequency $\omega$; the time for the amplitude to halve; and the $b$ for critical damping.
</Problem>
<Step title="Frequencies">
$\omega_0 = \sqrt{50/0.50} = 10$ rad/s and $\gamma = 1.0/0.50 = 2.0$ s⁻¹. Since $\omega_0^2 = 100 > \gamma^2/4 = 1$, it is underdamped, with $\omega = \sqrt{100 - 1} = \sqrt{99} \approx 9.95$ rad/s: only half a percent below $\omega_0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Halving time">
The amplitude is $Ae^{-\gamma t/2}$, so it halves when $e^{-t} = \frac12$: $t = \ln 2 \approx 0.69$ s, about one full oscillation ($2\pi/\omega \approx 0.63$ s). The energy, proportional to the amplitude squared, falls roughly as $e^{-\gamma t}$, averaged over a cycle.
</Step>
<Step title="Critical damping">
Critical when $\gamma^2/4 = \omega_0^2$, that is $\gamma = 2\omega_0 = 20$ s⁻¹, so $b = m\gamma = 0.50 \times 20 = 10$ kg/s, ten times the present drag.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Spring, LC circuit and swinging rod share $\mu\ddot x = -\kappa x$; without drag the energy $\frac12\kappa A^2$ is constant.
- The rod obeys $\ddot\theta = -\omega_0^2\sin\theta$; for small angles $\sin\theta \approx \theta$.
- Drag $-b\dot\theta$: $\ddot\theta + \gamma\dot\theta + \omega_0^2\theta = 0$, solved with $e^{i\alpha t}$.
- Underdamped: decaying oscillation at $\omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$. Critical: $(A + Bt)e^{-\gamma t/2}$. Overdamped: two decaying exponentials.
