summary: "Besides standing waves, the wave equation has travelling-wave solutions ψ(x, t) = f(x − v_pt) for any smooth shape f: by the chain rule ∂²f/∂x² = f″ and ∂²f/∂t² = v_p²f″. The shape keeps its form and moves in the +x direction at speed v_p; f(x + v_pt) moves to −x, and f(kx ± ωt) with ω = v_pk also solves the equation. Nothing moves sideways: each point of the string only moves up and down, in such a way that the shape travels. Two equal and opposite pulses meeting on a string cancel for an instant, then pass through each other: the string remembers them in its velocity, which is not zero, so energy is conserved. A string carries kinetic energy ∫½ρ_L(∂ψ/∂t)²dx and potential energy ∫½T(∂ψ/∂x)²dx. Example: ψ = 1/(1 + (x − 3t)⁴) moves at speed 3. A shape held still and released splits into two half-height copies travelling apart, since f(x + v_pt) + f(x − v_pt) has zero initial velocity. Two strings with the same tension and densities ρ_L and 4ρ_L meet at x = 0; the string is continuous there and so is its slope (a massless junction cannot take a net force). An incident wave gives a reflected wave R = (v₂ − v₁)/(v₂ + v₁) times as large and a transmitted wave τ = 2v₂/(v₁ + v₂); here v₂ = v₁/2, so R = −⅓ and τ = ⅔. The frequency stays, the wavelength changes. A wall (v₂ → 0) reflects with R = −1; a free end (v₂ → ∞) with R = 1, τ = 2."

The [normal modes](/courses/8-03/standing-waves-fourier-series) of the wave equation are standing waves. This lecture finds a second kind of solution, the **travelling wave**, which can carry energy from one place to another, as sound carries the lecture from Lee's mouth to the listeners' ears (transcript, pp. 1–2).

## Travelling-wave solutions

Try (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2)

<Formula label="Travelling wave" tex="\psi(x, t) = f(x - v_pt)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

for any well-behaved function $f$. With $\tau = x - v_pt$, the chain rule gives $\partial f/\partial x = f'(\tau)$ and $\partial f/\partial t = -v_pf'(\tau)$, so (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 2–3)

<Formula label="It solves the wave equation" tex="\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2} = f''(\tau), \qquad \frac{\partial^2f}{\partial t^2} = v_p^2f''(\tau) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial^2f}{\partial t^2} = v_p^2\,\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

whatever the shape. So does $f(kx \pm \omega t)$, provided $\omega = v_pk$, as the normal modes already required (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 3).

**Which way does it move?** (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 3–5). At $t = 0$ the string has the shape $f(x)$. At a fixed $x$, increasing $t$ decreases $\tau$, so a later moment samples the shape further back: the whole shape has moved, unchanged, towards **$+x$**, at speed $v_p$. Likewise $f(x + v_pt)$ moves towards $-x$.

**What is moving?** (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 5). Every point of the string still moves only up and down. A Gaussian pulse travels because the points ahead of it rise and those behind it fall, together, so that the shape shifts along the string. (In the lecture the motion is called "up and down horizontally" and, on p. 14, "horizontal"; it is vertical. Lee also often says "refraction" where he means reflection, pp. 8 and 14–19.)

## Two pulses that cancel

The wave equation is linear, so two pulses meeting simply add: two equal pulses make one of twice the height (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 6). Now send an upward pulse from the left and an equal downward one from the right. When they overlap, the string is flat. What next: do they cancel for good, pass through each other, or do something creative? The class votes mostly for "pass", four students for "creative" (pp. 6–7).

If they cancelled, energy would vanish (p. 7). But how could a flat string "remember" the pulses? Through its **velocity**: unlike a string at rest, it is still moving, one part already heading down and the other up (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 7–8). So the pulses re-emerge and pass through each other; the energy was there all along, as kinetic energy (p. 8). On the Bell Labs wave machine a positive and a negative square pulse cancel for a moment and then continue (p. 8). In Lee's computer simulations of triangles, circles and "MIT waves", the point midway between two opposite pulses never moves (pp. 8–9).

## Energy on a string

A short piece $dx$ has mass $dm = \rho_L\,dx$ and moves with velocity $\partial\psi/\partial t$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 9):

<Formula label="Kinetic energy" tex="K = \int\frac12\rho_L\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}\right)^2dx" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Displaced, the piece is stretched from $dx$ to $\sqrt{dx^2 + d\psi^2}$, and the tension $T$ does work $dW = T\left(\sqrt{dx^2 + d\psi^2} - dx\right)$. For small slopes $\sqrt{1 + (\partial\psi/\partial x)^2} \approx 1 + \frac12(\partial\psi/\partial x)^2$, so (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 10–11)

<Formula label="Potential energy" tex="dW \approx \frac T2\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right)^2dx, \qquad U = \int\frac12T\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right)^2dx" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

**A quick check** (notes, pp. 3–4; transcript, p. 11): $\psi = 1/(1 + (x - 3t)^4)$ has the form $f(x - 3t)$, so it travels to the right at speed 3. Alternatively, the wave equation gives $v_p^2$ as the ratio of $\partial^2\psi/\partial t^2$ to $\partial^2\psi/\partial x^2$ (notes, p. 4).

## A shape released from rest

Hold the string still in a triangle and let go (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 12). The brute-force way is to decompose it into normal modes and evolve infinitely many terms (p. 12). Much simpler (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 12–13):

<Formula label="Two travelling halves" tex="g(x, t) = f(x + v_pt) + f(x - v_pt), \qquad \frac{\partial g}{\partial t}(x, 0) = v_pf' - v_pf' = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

Any shape at rest is two travelling waves of half its height moving apart: a triangle of height $h$ becomes two triangles of height $h/2$, and at time $t$ each has moved $v_pt$ (transcript, p. 13). The same holds for the normal modes, which are two travelling sine waves (notes, p. 4). On the wave machine, a shape made by hand and released splits in two exactly so (transcript, p. 13).

## Joining two strings

The left string has density $\rho_L$, the right one $4\rho_L$, both under the same tension $T$ (notes, pp. 4–5; transcript, p. 14):

<Formula label="Two speeds" tex="v_1 = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho_L}}, \qquad v_2 = \sqrt{\frac{T}{4\rho_L}} = \frac{v_1}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

A wave arriving from the left will be partly reflected and partly transmitted (transcript, p. 14). At the junction, $x = 0$ (notes, p. 5; transcript, pp. 14–15):

1. the string is continuous: $y_L(0^-, t) = y_R(0^+, t)$;
2. the slope is continuous: $\partial y_L/\partial x = \partial y_R/\partial x$ at $x = 0$. The junction is like a massless ring; unequal slopes would leave a net force on it and an infinite acceleration, which would send it off, Lee jokes, to Mars in a second (transcript, p. 15).

Write incident, reflected and transmitted waves with the same frequency (notes, p. 5; transcript, pp. 15–16):

<Formula label="Three waves" tex="y_L = f_i(-k_1x + \omega t) + f_r(k_1x + \omega t), \qquad y_R = f_t(-k_2x + \omega t), \qquad k_1 = \frac{\omega}{v_1},\ k_2 = \frac{\omega}{v_2}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 5–6" />

At $x = 0$ condition 1 gives $f_i + f_r = f_t$; condition 2 gives $-k_1f_i' + k_1f_r' = -k_2f_t'$, which, integrated over time and multiplied by $v_1v_2$, becomes $-v_2f_i + v_2f_r = -v_1f_t$ (notes, p. 6; transcript, pp. 16–17). Solving (notes, p. 6; transcript, pp. 17–18):

<Formula label="Reflection and transmission" tex="f_r = Rf_i,\quad R = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{v_2 + v_1}; \qquad f_t = \tau f_i,\quad \tau = \frac{2v_2}{v_1 + v_2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Here $v_2 = v_1/2$, so $R = -\frac13$ and $\tau = \frac23$: the reflected pulse is upside down, the transmitted one the right way up (notes, p. 6; transcript, p. 18). (Lee writes the transmission coefficient as $\tau$; the notes call it $T$.) The wave machine confirms it, and sending a pulse the other way, from heavy to light, gives a reflected pulse the right way up (pp. 18–19). The frequency is the same on both sides, but the wavelength changes, since $k \propto 1/v$; the amplitudes depend on the two media through their impedances, $Z = T/v$ for a string (notes, p. 6). Two extremes (notes, p. 6):

- a **wall** is an infinitely heavy string, $v_2 \to 0$: $R = -1$, $\tau = 0$, the wave returns upside down with the same size;
- a **free end**, attached to "air", is an infinitely light one, $v_2 \to \infty$: $R = 1$, $\tau = 2$.

The notes also set up boundary conditions for a string driven through a massless ring, with and without drag (notes, pp. 7–8).

<WorkedExample title="A junction of your own">
<Problem>
(a) Where does $\psi = 2e^{-(2x + 8t)^2}$ go, and how fast? (b) A wave passes from a heavy string to one with one-ninth of its density, at the same tension. Find $R$ and $\tau$, and check that energy is conserved, using the fact that the power carried by a sinusoidal wave of amplitude $A$ is proportional to $ZA^2$ with $Z = \sqrt{T\rho_L}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The pulse">
$2e^{-(2x + 8t)^2} = 2e^{-4(x + 4t)^2}$, a function of $x + 4t$: it travels towards $-x$ at speed 4, keeping its Gaussian shape.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Coefficients">
$v \propto 1/\sqrt{\rho_L}$, so $v_2 = 3v_1$. Then $R = (3 - 1)/(3 + 1) = \frac12$ and $\tau = 2 \cdot 3/(1 + 3) = \frac32$: both pulses the right way up, the transmitted one larger than the incident one.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Energy">
$Z = \sqrt{T\rho_L}$, so $Z_2 = Z_1/3$. Incident power $\propto Z_1A^2$; reflected $\propto Z_1(A/2)^2 = 0.25\,Z_1A^2$; transmitted $\propto \frac{Z_1}{3}\left(\frac{3A}{2}\right)^2 = 0.75\,Z_1A^2$. The sum is $Z_1A^2$: a larger amplitude on the lighter string carries no more than the energy that arrives.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- $f(x \mp v_pt)$ solves the wave equation for any shape and moves at $v_p$ towards $\pm x$; points of the string move only transversely.
- Opposite pulses pass through each other; the string keeps their memory in its velocity.
- Energy density: $\frac12\rho_L\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}\right)^2 + \frac12T\left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right)^2$.
- A shape released from rest splits into two half-height travelling copies.
- At a junction: $R = \frac{v_2 - v_1}{v_2 + v_1}$, $\tau = \frac{2v_2}{v_1 + v_2}$; wall $R = -1$, free end $R = 1$.
