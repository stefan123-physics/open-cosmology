This lecture finishes the [two-string problem](/courses/8-03/travelling-waves-reflection) and then turns to a new example of the wave equation: **sound** (transcript, p. 1).

## Two limits of the two-string result

With $R = (v_2 - v_1)/(v_2 + v_1)$ (transcript, pp. 1–2) and, from the last lecture, $\tau = 2v_2/(v_1 + v_2)$:

- **A string tied to a wall.** Treat the wall as a second string of infinite density, so $v_2 \to 0$: $R = -1$, $\tau = 0$. In this idealised case all the energy comes back, upside down (p. 2).
- **A string ending in a ring that slides freely.** Treat the air beyond as a second string of zero density, so $v_2 \to \infty$: $R = 1$, $\tau = 2$ (pp. 2–3). Has the energy crisis been solved by a transmitted wave twice as large? No: with zero density nothing carries any energy away. "Too bad. Go back to work" (p. 3).

A harmonic travelling wave is written $A\cos(kx - \omega t + \phi)$, among other equivalent forms (p. 3).

## Longitudinal waves

On a string the displacement is transverse. In a **longitudinal wave** it is along the direction of travel, like a push along a spring: a density wave (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 3–4). Sound is such a wave of air molecules moving back and forth. Plots of displacement or pressure against position look just like transverse waves, but the molecules are not moving up and down (transcript, p. 4).

**The setup** (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 4–5). A tube of cross-section $A$, in a room at pressure $P_0$. A thin slice of air between $x$ and $x + \Delta x$ is displaced: its left edge by $\psi(x, t)$, its right edge by $\psi(x + \Delta x, t)$. The pressure is $P_0 + \psi_P(x, t)$. Then (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="Volume and pressure changes" tex="\Delta V = A\left[\psi(x + \Delta x, t) - \psi(x, t)\right] \approx A\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\,\Delta x, \qquad -\psi_P(x + \Delta x, t) + \psi_P(x, t) \approx -\frac{\partial\psi_P}{\partial x}\,\Delta x" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

To go on, pressure must be related to volume (transcript, p. 6).

## Newton or Laplace?

Two views (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 6–7):

- **Newton**: heat spreads so fast that the temperature never changes, so $PV = nRT$ is constant and $V \propto 1/P$.
- **Laplace**: the oscillation is fast and the heat flow slow, so the compression is **adiabatic**: almost no heat flows in or out during a vibration, and

<Formula label="Adiabatic law" tex="PV^{\gamma} = \text{constant}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

($\gamma = 1$ gives Newton's case.) The class votes: about 15 for Newton, a majority for Laplace (transcript, p. 7).

**Small changes** (notes, pp. 1–2; transcript, pp. 7–9). Before the disturbance $P_0V_0^\gamma = C$; after it $(P_0 + \psi_P)(V_0 + \Delta V)^\gamma = C$. With $\psi_P \ll P_0$ and $\Delta V \ll V_0$, expand $(V_0 + \Delta V)^\gamma \approx V_0^\gamma(1 + \gamma\Delta V/V_0)$ and drop the product of two small quantities. The constants cancel, and with $\Delta V = A\,\Delta x\,\partial\psi/\partial x$ and $V_0 = A\,\Delta x$:

<Formula label="Pressure and displacement" tex="\psi_P = -\gamma P_0\,\frac{\Delta V}{V_0} = -\gamma P_0\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

**Newton's law** (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 10–11). The slice has mass $\rho A\,\Delta x$ and feels the net force $-A\,\Delta x\,\partial\psi_P/\partial x$, so $\rho\ddot\psi = -\partial\psi_P/\partial x$. (Lee's first version on the board lost a minus sign; he noticed, and a student showed where it went.) With the relation above:

<Formula label="Wave equation for sound" tex="\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} = \frac{\gamma P_0}{\rho}\,\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}, \qquad v_p = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P_0}{\rho}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The wave equation again, the same as for the string (transcript, p. 11).

**What is $\gamma$?** (notes, pp. 2–3; transcript, pp. 11–12). From the first law of thermodynamics, $dU + W = 0$ for an adiabatic process, with $W = P\,dV$ and $U = \alpha nRT = \alpha PV$, where $\alpha$ is the number of degrees of freedom divided by 2:

<Formula label="Adiabatic index" tex="\alpha(V\,dP + P\,dV) = -P\,dV \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dP}{P} = -\frac{\alpha + 1}{\alpha}\,\frac{dV}{V}, \qquad \gamma = \frac{\alpha + 1}{\alpha}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

A monatomic gas has 3 translational degrees of freedom: $\alpha = 3/2$, $\gamma = 5/3$. A diatomic gas adds 2 rotational ones (its vibration is not excited at ordinary temperatures): $\alpha = 5/2$, $\gamma = 7/5$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 11–12).

**The predictions** (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 12). With $P_0 \approx 10^5$ Pa and $\rho = 1.2$ kg/m³, Laplace's $\gamma = 1.4$ gives $v_p \approx 342$ m/s; Newton's $\gamma = 1$ gives about 289 m/s. (In the lecture Newton's value is said as 389 m/s; $\sqrt{10^5/1.2} \approx 289$.)

**The measurement** (transcript, pp. 12–14). A loudspeaker drives a tube at 1 kHz, setting up a standing wave, and a microphone on a rod shows the sound getting weak at the nodes. One node is at 64 cm, and after passing the next, the class calls "stop" at 30 cm: a wavelength of 34 cm, which gives

<Formula label="Measured speed" tex="v = f\lambda = 1000\ \text{Hz} \times 0.34\ \text{m} = 340\ \text{m/s}" cite="reading2" at="p. 13" />

within 1% of Laplace's prediction (p. 14). Newton was wrong because heat does not spread that fast: standing next to a fire, you do not feel its heat at once, while the air vibrates a thousand times a second (p. 14). The notes quote 343 m/s measured at 70 °F (notes, p. 3).

**Helium** (transcript, pp. 14–15). A monatomic gas has a larger $\gamma$, so the speed of sound rises; with the same throat and mouth, the frequencies rise. (The lecture and notes credit only $\gamma$. Helium's much lower density matters more: with the numbers of the worked example below, the larger $\gamma$ raises the speed by a factor of about 1.09 and the lower density by about 2.66.) After a breath from a helium balloon, Lee's voice comes out high-pitched (on the second try).

## Pipes: boundary conditions

A pulse reaching the open end of a tube does not simply leave as a narrow beam (transcript, pp. 15–16). Outside is an enormous reservoir of air, whose pressure is very hard to change, so the open end acts like a wall for the pressure: the pulse is mostly reflected, with its pressure amplitude reversed, and only a little leaks out, which is why the tube can be heard (p. 16). In terms of $\psi$ (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 16–17):

- **closed end**: the air at the wall cannot move, $\psi = 0$;
- **open end**: the pressure is the room pressure, $\psi_P = 0$, and since $\psi_P = -\gamma P_0\,\partial\psi/\partial x$, that means $\partial\psi/\partial x = 0$.

So a pipe closed at one end and open at the other has, for $\psi$, exactly the boundary conditions of a [string](/courses/8-03/standing-waves-fourier-series) fixed to a wall at one end and tied to a massless ring at the other (transcript, p. 17). For the pressure the picture is flipped: $\psi_P$ is largest at a closed end, where the air pushes on the wall without moving, and zero at an open end (pp. 17–18). The notes work out the normal modes for the three kinds of pipe (notes, pp. 4–5):

<Formula label="Pipe modes" tex="\text{closed–closed: } k_m = \frac{m\pi}{L}; \qquad \text{closed–open: } k_m = \frac{(m - \frac12)\pi}{L}; \qquad \text{open–open: } k_m = \frac{m\pi}{L}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 4–5" />

with $\omega_m = vk_m$. The notes also drive a pipe closed at $x = 0$ by moving the air at $x = L$, $\psi(L, t) = D\cos\omega_dt$, which gives $\psi = D\,\frac{\sin k_dx}{\sin k_dL}\cos\omega_dt$: it resonates when $\sin k_dL = 0$, at $k_d = m\pi/L$, the closed–closed modes, since the driven end is a node. (The notes write $(m - \frac12)\pi/L$ there, a slip; notes, p. 6.)

<WorkedExample title="Sound of your own">
<Problem>
(a) Find the speed of sound in helium at $P_0 = 1.0 \times 10^5$ Pa, taking its density as $0.17$ kg/m³. (b) An organ pipe of length $0.50$ m is closed at one end and open at the other. Find its three lowest frequencies in air ($v = 342$ m/s).
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Helium">
Helium is monatomic, $\gamma = 5/3$: $v = \sqrt{(5/3)(1.0 \times 10^5)/0.17} \approx 990$ m/s, almost three times the speed in air.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The pipe">
Closed–open: $k_m = (m - \frac12)\pi/L$, so $f_m = vk_m/2\pi = (2m - 1)v/4L$. With $v/4L = 342/2.0 = 171$ Hz: $f_1 = 171$ Hz, $f_2 = 513$ Hz, $f_3 = 855$ Hz, only the odd multiples, as for the fixed–free string.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Wall: $R = -1$; free end: $R = 1$ (the $\tau = 2$ wave carries no energy).
- Sound is a longitudinal wave; $\psi_P = -\gamma P_0\,\partial\psi/\partial x$ for adiabatic compressions.
- Wave equation with $v = \sqrt{\gamma P_0/\rho}$; $\gamma = (\alpha + 1)/\alpha$, 7/5 for air: about 342 m/s, confirmed by a 1 kHz standing wave (340 m/s). Newton's isothermal guess fails.
- Closed end $\psi = 0$, open end $\partial\psi/\partial x = 0$: pipes behave like strings with fixed and free ends.
