Lecture 5 was given by a substitute, Prof. Boleslaw Wyslouch, while Prof. Lee was at a conference (transcript, p. 1). It applies the systematic method of the [last lecture](/courses/8-03/coupled-oscillators-normal-modes) to two planned systems, two pendulums joined by a spring and two masses on a track; only the pendulums fit into this lecture, and the driven masses follow next time (pp. 1, 23). Along the way it shows the **beat phenomenon**. The pendulums with a spring will be the "workhorse" of the course, later generalised to infinitely many oscillators (p. 2), which will lead to waves.

## Two pendulums and a spring

Two identical pendulums, mass $m$ and length $l$, hang side by side, joined by a spring of constant $k$ that is relaxed when both hang straight down; small angles, no drag, ideal spring (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2). The coordinates $x_1$ and $x_2$ are the horizontal displacements from equilibrium, with $y$ needed briefly for the vertical forces (pp. 2–3).

**Forces on pendulum 1** (transcript, pp. 3–4). Tension $T_1$ along the string, gravity $-mg\hat y$, and the spring $k(x_2 - x_1)\hat x$:

<Formula label="Forces on pendulum 1" tex="m\ddot x_1 = -T_1\sin\theta_1 + k(x_2 - x_1), \qquad m\ddot y_1 = T_1\cos\theta_1 - mg" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

For small angles $\cos\theta_1 \approx 1$ and there is no vertical motion, so $T_1 = mg$; and $\sin\theta_1 \approx x_1/l$ (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 4). Pendulum 2 is the same with the labels swapped (notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Coupled equations" tex="m\ddot x_1 = -\left(k + \frac{mg}{l}\right)x_1 + kx_2, \qquad m\ddot x_2 = kx_1 - \left(k + \frac{mg}{l}\right)x_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Each mass is its own pendulum, but the spring term makes the motion of one depend on where the other is: that is the coupling (transcript, pp. 4–5).

## Normal modes

In matrix form, as last time (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="Matrices" tex="M = \begin{pmatrix} m & 0 \\ 0 & m \end{pmatrix}, \qquad K = \begin{pmatrix} k + mg/l & -k \\ -k & k + mg/l \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \ddot X = -M^{-1}KX" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The physics answer to "how do we solve it?" is to look for normal modes, in which everything moves at one frequency; the mathematical one is complex notation (transcript, pp. 6–7). With $Z = Ae^{i(\omega t + \phi)}$, $A$ real, the condition is again $\det(M^{-1}K - \omega^2I) = 0$ (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 7–8). The only thing that can be adjusted is $\omega^2$ (pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Frequency condition" tex="\left(\frac{g}{l} + \frac{k}{m} - \omega^2\right)^2 - \left(\frac{k}{m}\right)^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{g}{l} + \frac{k}{m} - \omega^2 = \pm\frac{k}{m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

<Formula label="Normal-mode frequencies" tex="\omega_1^2 = \frac{g}{l}, \qquad \omega_2^2 = \frac{g}{l} + \frac{2k}{m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The first does not depend on the spring at all: both pendulums swing together, the spring is never stretched, and cutting it would change nothing (transcript, pp. 9–10). In the second they swing in opposite directions, the spring is stretched from both ends, and the frequency is higher (p. 10). Putting each $\omega^2$ back into $(M^{-1}K - \omega^2I)A = 0$ gives the shapes (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 10–11):

<Formula label="Mode shapes" tex="\omega_1:\ A^{(1)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \omega_2:\ A^{(2)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

## The general motion

The general motion is a superposition, with four constants $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\phi_1$, $\phi_2$ for the initial conditions (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 12–13):

<Formula label="General solution" tex="x_1 = \alpha\cos(\omega_1t + \phi_1) + \beta\cos(\omega_2t + \phi_2), \qquad x_2 = \alpha\cos(\omega_1t + \phi_1) - \beta\cos(\omega_2t + \phi_2)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The frequencies and the shapes $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$ are fixed by the construction; only the amounts and phases are free (transcript, p. 12). Now hold pendulum 1 still at zero, pull pendulum 2 out by $x_0$, and release both from rest (p. 13). Then $\alpha = x_0/2$, $\beta = -x_0/2$, $\phi_1 = \phi_2 = 0$ (notes, p. 2):

<Formula label="Starting from one displaced pendulum" tex="x_1 = \frac{x_0}{2}(\cos\omega_1t - \cos\omega_2t), \qquad x_2 = \frac{x_0}{2}(\cos\omega_1t + \cos\omega_2t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The demonstration looks complicated: pendulum 2 slows down while pendulum 1 picks up, then the reverse. A computer animation shows each pendulum as the sum of the two normal modes (transcript, pp. 13–14). Choosing initial conditions with $\alpha$ or $\beta$ zero switches one mode off; then both pendulums swing with constant amplitude and no energy passes between them (pp. 14–15).

**Changing gravity** (transcript, pp. 15–17). Without touching the system, take it to Jupiter, in Wyslouch's MATLAB simulation, which raises $g$ from about 10 to 25 m/s²: larger $g$ raises both frequencies but brings them closer together, since the spring is unchanged, and the exchange of energy becomes slower. On the simulated Moon everything is slower but the frequencies are further apart, the exchange is faster, and the motion looks messy, much less obviously a sum of two cosines (pp. 16–17).

## Beats

Rewrite the solution with trigonometric identities, "zero physics" (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 18):

<Formula label="Carrier and envelope" tex="x_1 = -x_0\sin\frac{(\omega_1 + \omega_2)t}{2}\sin\frac{(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t}{2}, \qquad x_2 = x_0\cos\frac{(\omega_1 + \omega_2)t}{2}\cos\frac{(\omega_1 - \omega_2)t}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

When $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$ are close, for example $\omega_1 = 0.9\,\omega_2$, the first factor oscillates at about $0.95\,\omega_2$, nearly the pendulum's own frequency, while the second changes slowly, at $0.05\,\omega_2$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 18). So each pendulum oscillates fast (the **carrier**) inside a slowly changing amplitude (the **envelope**): its swing grows, falls to zero and grows again as the energy moves to the other pendulum and back. This is the **beat** phenomenon (transcript, pp. 18–19). The carrier has period $2\pi/\frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2}$. The envelope factor turns at half the difference, but the size of the swing, its absolute value, falls to zero twice in each of its cycles, so the swells (the beats) repeat with period $2\pi/|\omega_1 - \omega_2|$ (notes, p. 3). (When writing these on p. 18, Wyslouch says the slow factor of $x_2$ as "divided by $t$" and leaves the $t$ out of the fast factors; on p. 21 a student asks whether both should contain $t$, and he confirms that they do.)

Beats can be heard (transcript, pp. 19–21). Two loudspeakers at nearly the same frequency produce a sound whose loudness rises and falls; the closer the frequencies, the slower the wobble (pp. 19–20). Two tuning forks of nearly equal frequency are coupled through the air: strike one, stop it, and the other is now sounding (pp. 20–21). With identical frequencies there would be no beats, the beat period being infinite; a small weight on one fork detunes it and the beats become clearly audible (p. 21).

## Normal coordinates

A last trick (notes, pp. 3–4; transcript, pp. 22–23): describe the system by the sum and difference of the positions,

<Formula label="Normal coordinates" tex="U_1 = x_1 + x_2, \qquad U_2 = x_1 - x_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Adding and subtracting the two equations of motion removes the cross terms (notes, p. 4):

<Formula label="Decoupled equations" tex="m\ddot U_1 = -\frac{mg}{l}\,U_1, \qquad m\ddot U_2 = -\left(\frac{mg}{l} + 2k\right)U_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

Two independent harmonic oscillators, at $\omega_1$ and $\omega_2$, with no determinants and no matrices (transcript, pp. 22–23). For symmetric systems such variables can be guessed; in general they are found after the normal modes, but they always exist (p. 23). Next time: driven coupled oscillators.

<WorkedExample title="Beats of your own">
<Problem>
Two pendulums of length $0.50$ m and mass $0.20$ kg are joined by a spring with $k = 0.40$ N/m. Take $g = 9.8$ m/s². Pendulum 2 is pulled aside and pendulum 1 held at equilibrium, and both are released from rest. Find $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, the carrier frequency and the time for the energy to pass completely from pendulum 2 to pendulum 1. Repeat on the Moon, $g = 1.62$ m/s².
</Problem>
<Step title="On Earth">
$\omega_1^2 = 9.8/0.50 = 19.6$ and $\omega_2^2 = 19.6 + 2(0.40)/0.20 = 23.6$, so $\omega_1 \approx 4.43$ and $\omega_2 \approx 4.86$ rad/s. Carrier $(\omega_1 + \omega_2)/2 \approx 4.64$ rad/s. Pendulum 1 starts at rest; its amplitude $|x_0\sin\frac{(\omega_2 - \omega_1)t}{2}|$ is largest, and pendulum 2 still, when $\frac{(\omega_2 - \omega_1)t}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}$, at $t = \pi/(\omega_2 - \omega_1) \approx \pi/0.431 \approx 7.3$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="On the Moon">
$\omega_1^2 = 1.62/0.50 = 3.24$ and $\omega_2^2 = 3.24 + 4 = 7.24$, so $\omega_1 = 1.80$ and $\omega_2 \approx 2.69$ rad/s. The difference has grown to $0.89$ rad/s, and the transfer takes only $\pi/0.89 \approx 3.5$ s, although each swing is slower. The frequencies are no longer close, so the motion no longer looks like a clean carrier inside an envelope.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Two pendulums joined by a spring: $\omega_1^2 = g/l$ (together, spring idle) and $\omega_2^2 = g/l + 2k/m$ (opposite).
- The general motion is a superposition with four constants; starting with one pendulum displaced, energy passes back and forth.
- $\cos\omega_1t \pm \cos\omega_2t$ = fast carrier at the average frequency times a slow envelope at half the difference: beats.
- Normal coordinates $x_1 \pm x_2$ decouple the equations.
