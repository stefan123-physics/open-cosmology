Waves are "a group effort": many objects working together (transcript, p. 1). The last lecture went from finitely many oscillators to an infinite chain and then to a [continuous string](/courses/8-03/beaded-string-wave-equation), finding the wave equation. Today: its normal modes, and how boundary and initial conditions fix them (p. 2).

## The continuum limit, once more

The continuous limit means the spacing $a$ of the beads is much smaller than the wavelength, as for the coils of a long spring compared with a metre-long wave (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 1–2). Then $ka$ is small and the beaded string's dispersion relation becomes a straight line (notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Continuum dispersion relation" tex="\omega^2 = \frac{4T}{ma}\sin^2\frac{ka}{2} \approx \frac{4T}{ma}\left(\frac{ka}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{T}{\rho_L}k^2, \qquad v_p = \frac{\omega}{k} = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho_L}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The displacement is now called $\psi(x, t)$, the letter used for wave functions in 8.04 (transcript, p. 2):

<Formula label="Wave equation" tex="\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} = v_p^2\,\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

It is really infinitely many equations of motion at once: the label $j$ of the discrete beads has become the continuous position $x$ (transcript, p. 2).

## Normal modes by separation of variables

Look for solutions in which the shape and the time dependence are separate factors (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 3):

<Formula label="Separation" tex="\psi(x, t) = A(x)\,B(t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

$A(x)$ gives the shape, $B(t)$ how every point moves in time. Put it into the wave equation and divide by $AB\,v_p^2$ (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Separated equation" tex="\frac{1}{v_p^2B(t)}\frac{d^2B}{dt^2} = \frac{1}{A(x)}\frac{d^2A}{dx^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The left side depends only on $t$, the right only on $x$. Stay at one $x$ and let time run: the right side cannot change, so neither can the left; freeze time and move along the string: the same. So both equal a constant, given the "strange fancy name" $-k_m^2$ (transcript, p. 4). This is **separation of variables**, which comes back again and again in physics (p. 4). Each side is now the harmonic oscillator, "solved infinite number of times in this lecture" (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 5):

<Formula label="Time and space parts" tex="B(t) = B_m\sin(\omega_mt + \beta_m), \quad \omega_m = v_pk_m; \qquad A(x) = C_m\sin(k_mx + \alpha_m)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Combining $B_mC_m$ into one amplitude (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 6):

<Formula label="Normal mode" tex="\psi_m(x, t) = A_m\sin(\omega_mt + \beta_m)\sin(k_mx + \alpha_m)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Every point oscillates harmonically, only up and down, with the same frequency and phase, and the relative amplitude along the string is a sine, as for the discrete chain, because the system has the same translation symmetry (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 6–7, 13). Four unknowns: $k_m$ and $\alpha_m$ will be fixed by the boundary conditions, $A_m$ and $\beta_m$ by the initial conditions (transcript, p. 8).

**The Rijke tube** (transcript, pp. 7–8). A metal tube with a wire mesh inside: heat the mesh for a few seconds and the tube sounds a loud, single note. The heated air rises and expands, pushing on the air in the tube as a hand shakes a wave machine, and the system settles into a normal mode it likes: heat turned into sound.

## Boundary conditions: one end fixed, one end free

The example (notes, pp. 2–3; transcript, p. 9): a string of length $L$, tension $T$ and density $\rho_L$, nailed to a wall at $x = 0$ and tied at $x = L$ to a massless ring that slides up and down a rod; $\psi = 0$ is the equilibrium. Two boundary conditions (notes, p. 3):

<Formula label="Boundary conditions" tex="\psi(0, t) = 0, \qquad \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}(L, t) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The second, proposed by a student, has a physical reason: the rod's normal force can only push horizontally, so if the string met the ring at a slope, there would be a vertical force on a massless ring, and $F = ma$ with $m = 0$ would send it off with infinite acceleration "to the edge of the universe" (transcript, pp. 9–10).

The first gives $A_m\sin\alpha_m\sin(\omega_mt + \beta_m) = 0$ for all $t$; $A_m = 0$ would mean no motion, so $\sin\alpha_m = 0$ and we can choose $\alpha_m = 0$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 10–11). The second gives $A_mk_m\sin(\omega_mt + \beta_m)\cos k_mL = 0$ for all $t$, so $\cos k_mL = 0$ (p. 11):

<Formula label="Allowed wave numbers" tex="k_mL = \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}, \frac{5\pi}{2}, \dots \qquad k_m = \frac{(2m - 1)\pi}{2L}, \quad m = 1, 2, 3, \dots" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

With the dispersion relation, each $k_m$ fixes a frequency (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 12):

<Formula label="First modes" tex="k_1 = \frac{\pi}{2L},\ \lambda_1 = 4L,\ \omega_1 = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho_L}}\,\frac{\pi}{2L}; \qquad k_2 = \frac{3\pi}{2L},\ \lambda_2 = \frac{4L}{3}; \qquad k_3 = \frac{5\pi}{2L},\ \lambda_3 = \frac{4L}{5}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

The first mode is a quarter of a sine wave, rising from the wall to a maximum at the ring; each higher mode adds more wiggles and a higher frequency (transcript, pp. 12–14). The whole shape scales up and down in time; no point moves sideways, however much the moving shape suggests it (p. 13). With both ends fixed the modes are different, a homework exercise (p. 13). The general motion is the sum of all the modes (notes, p. 5; transcript, p. 13):

<Formula label="General solution" tex="\psi(x, t) = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty}A_m\sin(\omega_mt + \beta_m)\sin\frac{(2m - 1)\pi x}{2L}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

## Initial conditions and Fourier series

That leaves infinitely many $A_m$ and $\beta_m$ (transcript, p. 14). The mathematics department's answer is **Fourier decomposition**: a triangle, a square pulse, or even a drawing of an MIT dome can be built from sines, though sharp edges need very many terms; a prepared slide of partial sums uses 59 terms for the dome, and Lee's live computer program looks much like the dome after about 20 terms but still struggles with the sharp edges, which would need infinitely many (pp. 14–15). See 18.03's [introduction to Fourier series](/courses/18-03/fourier-series-introduction).

**An example** (notes, p. 5; transcript, pp. 16–18). At $t = 0$ the string is at rest, flat at height 0 from $x = 0$ to $L/2$ and at height $h$ from $L/2$ to $L$. Two initial conditions:

<Formula label="Initial conditions" tex="\dot\psi(x, 0) = 0, \qquad \psi(x, 0) \text{ given}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

The first: $\dot\psi(x, 0) = \sum A_m\omega_m\cos\beta_m\sin k_mx = 0$ for all $x$ requires $\cos\beta_m = 0$; choose $\beta_m = \pi/2$ ($-\pi/2$ would only flip the sign of $A_m$), and $\psi(x, 0) = \sum A_m\sin k_mx$ (notes, p. 5; transcript, pp. 16–17). For the second, use the **orthogonality** of the sines (notes, p. 5; transcript, p. 17):

<Formula label="Orthogonality" tex="\int_0^L\sin(k_mx)\sin(k_nx)\,dx = \begin{cases} L/2 & m = n \\ 0 & m \neq n \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

Multiplying the series by $\sin k_mx$ and integrating kills every term but one: a "mode picker" (transcript, pp. 17–18):

<Formula label="Fourier coefficients" tex="A_m = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L\psi(x, 0)\sin(k_mx)\,dx" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

For the step, only $L/2$ to $L$ contributes (notes, p. 5; transcript, p. 18):

<Formula label="Coefficients of the step" tex="A_m = \frac{2}{L}\int_{L/2}^{L}h\sin(k_mx)\,dx = -\frac{2h}{Lk_m}\left[\cos(k_mL) - \cos\frac{k_mL}{2}\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

with $\cos k_mL = 0$ here. In short: boundary conditions give $k_m$ and $\alpha_m$, initial conditions give $A_m$ and $\beta_m$, and the general solution sums all modes (transcript, pp. 18–19).

**A last remark** (transcript, p. 19). For the continuous string $\omega$ is proportional to $k$, a straight line; the beaded string's $\omega = 2\sqrt{T/ma}\,\sin(ka/2)$ bends away from it at large $k$. Measuring the frequencies of the normal modes could therefore reveal the hidden spacing $a$ of the masses.

<WorkedExample title="A plucked fixed–free string of your own">
<Problem>
A string of length $L = 1.0$ m is fixed at $x = 0$ and free (ring on a rod) at $x = L$, with $v_p = 40$ m/s. It is held in the straight line $\psi(x, 0) = hx/L$ and released from rest. Find the first three frequencies and the amplitudes $A_m$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Frequencies">
$f_m = \omega_m/2\pi = v_pk_m/2\pi = (2m - 1)v_p/4L$: $f_1 = 40/4 = 10$ Hz, $f_2 = 30$ Hz, $f_3 = 50$ Hz. Only odd multiples of the lowest frequency appear.
</Step>
<Step title="Amplitudes">
$A_m = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L\frac{hx}{L}\sin(k_mx)\,dx$. Integrating by parts, $\int_0^Lx\sin(k_mx)\,dx = \frac{\sin k_mL}{k_m^2} - \frac{L\cos k_mL}{k_m}$, and here $\cos k_mL = 0$, $\sin k_mL = (-1)^{m+1}$. So $A_m = \frac{2h(-1)^{m+1}}{L^2k_m^2} = \frac{8h(-1)^{m+1}}{(2m - 1)^2\pi^2}$: $A_1 \approx 0.811h$, $A_2 \approx -0.090h$, $A_3 \approx 0.032h$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
At the ring, $\psi(L, 0) = \sum A_m\sin k_mL = \frac{8h}{\pi^2}\left(1 + \frac19 + \frac1{25} + \cdots\right) = \frac{8h}{\pi^2} \cdot \frac{\pi^2}{8} = h$, as it should. The first mode alone already carries 81% of the starting height.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Continuum limit: $\omega = v_pk$, $v_p = \sqrt{T/\rho_L}$, and the wave equation for $\psi(x, t)$.
- Separation of variables: $\psi_m = A_m\sin(\omega_mt + \beta_m)\sin(k_mx + \alpha_m)$, standing waves with a sine shape.
- Boundary conditions fix $k_m$, $\alpha_m$: fixed end $\psi = 0$, free end $\partial\psi/\partial x = 0$; fixed–free gives $k_m = (2m - 1)\pi/2L$.
- Initial conditions fix $\beta_m$ and $A_m = \frac2L\int_0^L\psi(x, 0)\sin k_mx\,dx$, by orthogonality: a Fourier series.
