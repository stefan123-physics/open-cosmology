Why study vibrations and waves? Because we cannot even perceive the universe without them (transcript, p. 1). You hear a lecture through sound waves in the air, see through electromagnetic waves bouncing round the room, and quantum mechanics (8.04) describes matter by probability waves; gravitational waves, first detected in 2015 and announced in 2016, shortly before this lecture, may be passing through you unnoticed (p. 1). 8.03 builds on the mechanics of [8.01](/courses/8-01/tension-and-springs) and the electromagnetism of 8.02, and leads to optics and quantum mechanics (pp. 1–2). The plan: translate physical situations into mathematics, study one oscillator, then coupled oscillators, then infinitely many, which give waves, and then Fourier decomposition, boundary conditions, electromagnetic waves and optics (p. 2).

## A mass on a spring

The first system is a block on an ideal spring with spring constant $k$ and natural length $L_0$, on a frictionless (air) table (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2). Pull the block aside, hold it still and release it: what happens? (p. 2)

**Translate into mathematics** (pp. 2–3). Define a coordinate $x$ along the table, with $x = 0$ where the spring has its natural length: the equilibrium position. The initial conditions are $x(0) = x_{\text{initial}}$ and $\dot x(0) = 0$, and the question is: what is $x(t)$? (transcript, p. 3)

**Forces** (pp. 3–4). How many forces act? Three: the spring force, gravity and the normal force from the table (p. 3). The block does not leave the table, so the normal force cancels gravity, and the total force is the spring force (notes, eq. 1):

<Formula label="Total force" tex="\vec F = \vec F_S + \vec F_N + \vec F_g = \vec F_S = -k\,x(t)\,\hat x" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, eq. 1" />

Newton's law, with a dot meaning a time derivative, then gives the **equation of motion** (notes, eqs. 2–3):

<Formula label="Equation of motion" tex="m\ddot x = -kx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot x = -\frac{k}{m}\,x = -\omega^2x, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, eqs. 2–3" />

Defining $\omega$ makes life easier, and its meaning appears later (transcript, p. 4).

**Solve** (pp. 4–5). The mathematics department supplies the solution (18.03, the [second-order equation with constant coefficients](/courses/18-03/second-order-constant-coefficients)) (notes, eq. 4):

<Formula label="General solution" tex="x(t) = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, eq. 4" />

It satisfies the equation and has two arbitrary constants, so by the uniqueness theorem it is "the one and only one solution" (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 5). The initial conditions fix them: $x(0) = a = x_{\text{initial}}$ and $\dot x(0) = b\omega = 0$ (notes, eq. 5), so (eq. 6)

<Formula label="Released from rest" tex="x(t) = x_{\text{initial}}\cos(\omega t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, eq. 6" />

$x_{\text{initial}}$ is the amplitude and $\omega$ the angular frequency (transcript, pp. 5–6). In the demonstrations the air-track cart between two springs, and a mass hanging vertically, oscillate back and forth with an amplitude set by the starting point and a steady amplitude and frequency (p. 6); that the frequency does not depend on the amplitude, for small swings, shows in the pendulum demonstration below (p. 10). Lee finds it amazing that the same mathematics predicts this, the Higgs boson, the quark-gluon plasma of the early universe and gravitational waves; he quotes Descartes and Einstein ("The most incomprehensible thing about the universe is that it is comprehensible") (pp. 6–7; notes, p. 3).

## Why Hooke's law is everywhere

There is no exact Hooke's law: a force proportional to $x$ forever would let a stretched spring store unlimited energy, so it must break down (transcript, p. 7). Yet almost every system near equilibrium oscillates harmonically (p. 7). Lee shows a potential from Georgi's textbook that is far from $x^2$, which still looks like a parabola when you zoom in on its minimum (p. 7). In general, put the minimum of a smooth potential $V(x)$ at $x = 0$, so that the force there vanishes (notes, eq. 7), and expand (eq. 9):

<Formula label="Taylor expansion of the potential" tex="V(x) = V(0) + \frac{V'(0)}{1!}x + \frac{V''(0)}{2!}x^2 + \frac{1}{3!}\frac{d^3V}{dx^3}(0)\,x^3 + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="p. 3, eq. 9" />

The force is $F = -dV/dx = -V'(0) - V''(0)x - \frac12\frac{d^3V}{dx^3}(0)x^2 - \cdots$ (eq. 10), and $V'(0) = 0$ at the minimum. For small $x$ only the first remaining term matters (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 8):

<Formula label="Hooke's law near a minimum" tex="F(x) \approx -V''(0)\,x \qquad\text{when}\qquad |x\,\frac{d^3V}{dx^3}(0)| \ll V''(0)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

The condition comes from comparing the next term with this one (transcript, p. 8). So "Hooke's law" works for all systems with a smooth potential (and $V''(0) \neq 0$), for small oscillations about stable equilibrium; having solved the spring, "we have solved all those kinds of situations" (notes, p. 4). For large $x$ the non-linear terms grow; 8.03 mostly studies linear systems (notes, p. 4).

**A demonstration of non-linearity** (transcript, pp. 9–10). Two pendulums swung with small amplitudes reach their maxima together. Released at large amplitude, they start in phase but drift apart: their frequencies now depend on the amplitude, a sign of the higher-order terms.

## Linearity and time translation

The equation $\ddot x + \omega^2x = 0$ has two important properties (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 9):

1. **Linearity (superposition):** if $x_1(t)$ and $x_2(t)$ are solutions, so is $x_1(t) + x_2(t)$.
2. **Time-translation invariance:** if $x(t)$ is a solution, so is $x(t + a)$. By the chain rule, a derivative with respect to $t$ is the same as one with respect to $t' = t + a$, so moving the zero of time changes nothing. A spring constant that changed with time would break this symmetry.

## Three ways to write the solution

All three forms below are the same solution with two arbitrary constants (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 10):

<Formula label="Three forms" tex="x(t) = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t = A\cos(\omega t + \phi) = \text{Re}\left[Ae^{i(\omega t + \phi)}\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

The second expands as $(A\cos\phi)\cos\omega t - (A\sin\phi)\sin\omega t$, so it is the first with $a = A\cos\phi$ and $b = -A\sin\phi$ (notes, p. 4). The third is "a mathematical trick", Lee says (transcript, pp. 10–11). One could add any imaginary part $if(t)$ and take the real part; the clever choice $f(t) = A\sin(\omega t + \phi)$ turns the path in the complex plane into a **circle** (notes, eq. 11; transcript, p. 11):

<Formula label="The complex form" tex="x(t) = \text{Re}\left[A\cos(\omega t + \phi) + iA\sin(\omega t + \phi)\right] = \text{Re}\left[Ae^{i(\omega t + \phi)}\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 5, eq. 11" />

using $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$. The point moves round a circle of radius $A$ at angle $\omega t + \phi$, and the oscillation is its projection on the real axis: that is why $\omega$ is called an angular frequency (transcript, pp. 11–12). Lee calls the exponential a "phoenix function": differentiation cannot kill it, which turns differential equations into algebra (notes, p. 5; transcript, p. 12). And $e^{i\theta_1}e^{i\theta_2} = e^{i(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}$, so a time shift $t \to t + a$ is just a **rotation** in the complex plane (notes, eqs. 12–13; transcript, pp. 12–13).

What has been left out is friction: the pendulums and the air-track block slowly stop. The next lecture adds a drag force (transcript, pp. 13–14).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Oscillators in the early universe">
The harmonic oscillator comes back twice in 8.286. Each standing wave of the electromagnetic field in a box behaves as a harmonic oscillator, because the field equations are linear, and giving each one its quantum share of thermal energy leads to the [black-body law](/courses/8-286/black-body-radiation) for the radiation of the early universe. And at the end of inflation the field that drove it rolls down its potential and [oscillates about the minimum](/courses/8-286/exponential-expansion), handing its energy to other particles: there the Taylor argument above, that anything near the bottom of a smooth potential oscillates harmonically, applies directly.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="A spring of your own">
<Problem>
A $0.25$ kg block on a spring with $k = 100$ N/m is pulled $3.0$ cm from equilibrium and given a speed of $0.80$ m/s towards larger $x$. Find $\omega$, the period, $x(t)$ in the form $a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t$, and the amplitude $A$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Frequency and period">
$\omega = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{100/0.25} = \sqrt{400} = 20$ rad/s. Period $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi/20 \approx 0.314$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Constants">
$x(0) = a = 0.030$ m. $\dot x(0) = b\omega = 0.80$ m/s, so $b = 0.80/20 = 0.040$ m. Hence $x(t) = 0.030\cos 20t + 0.040\sin 20t$ (metres, seconds).
</Step>
<Step title="Amplitude">
$A = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{0.030^2 + 0.040^2} = 0.050$ m. The block swings 5.0 cm each way, further than where it started, because it was moving at $t = 0$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Mass on a spring: $m\ddot x = -kx$, $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, $x(t) = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t$, constants from $x(0)$ and $\dot x(0)$.
- Near any smooth minimum, $F \approx -V''(0)x$: Hooke's law holds for small oscillations; large ones bring non-linear terms.
- The equation is linear (superposition) and time-translation invariant.
- $x = \text{Re}[Ae^{i(\omega t + \phi)}]$: a circle in the complex plane projected on the real axis; time shifts are rotations.
