For slits a distance d apart and a distant screen, the paths to a point at angle θ differ by d sin θ, a phase difference δ = (2π/λ)d sin θ, and adding E₀e^(i(ωt − kr_A)) and E₀e^(i(ωt − kr_B)) gives ⟨I⟩ ∝ cos²(δ/2): bright where δ = 0, 2π, …, dark where δ = π, as two vibrators in a ripple tank show. After [thin films](/courses/8-03/interference-soap-bubbles), three more consequences of interference: the double slit, the phased radar (radio waves, from about 3 kHz to 300 GHz) and a first glimpse of quantum mechanics (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 1–2).

## Huygens's principle and two slits

**Huygens's principle**: every point on a wavefront acts as a source of spherical waves (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2). It can be derived from Maxwell's equations, and, Lee adds, it works because space has an odd number of dimensions and would fail in an even number (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2). (This concerns the strict principle, with sharp wavefronts; the wavelet picture used for interference also works for the two-dimensional ripple tank below.). Without it, light passing a slit would go straight on, and two slits could never send light to the same point (transcript, p. 4).

A plane wave of wavelength $\lambda$ meets a wall with slits A and B a distance $d$ apart; a screen is a distance $L \gg d$ away (notes, pp. 1–2; transcript, pp. 2–4). To a point P at angle $\theta$ the two paths are almost parallel, so

<Formula label="Two-slit phase difference" tex="r_B - r_A = d\sin\theta, \qquad \delta = \frac{d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\,2\pi = (d\sin\theta)\,k" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

With the field along $\hat z$ and equal amplitudes from both slits (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 5–6):

<Formula label="Two-slit field and intensity" tex="\vec E = E_0\left(e^{i(\omega t - kr_A)} + e^{i(\omega t - kr_B)}\right)\hat z = E_0e^{i(\omega t - kr_A)}e^{-i\delta/2}\left[e^{+i\delta/2} + e^{-i\delta/2}\right]\hat z, \qquad \langle I\rangle \propto \vec E\cdot\vec E^* \propto \cos^2\frac{\delta}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

so $\langle I\rangle = A\cos^2(\delta/2)$, with $A$ the intensity at $\delta = 0$ and $\sin\theta = \lambda\delta/2\pi d$ (notes, p. 2). At the centre, $\delta = 0$ by symmetry: bright. At $\delta = \pi$ the two arrows in the complex plane point opposite ways: dark. At $\delta = 2\pi$, bright again (transcript, pp. 6–7).

**Demonstrations** (transcript, pp. 8–9). In a ripple tank two vibrators make circular waves; a strobe light freezes them, showing bright and calm bands. Two overlaid transparencies of circles, projected, show that moving the centres further apart, larger $d$, brings the dark bands closer to the middle, as $\sin\theta \propto 1/d$ predicts.

## Three, four, N slits

A radar must send a narrow beam in one direction, but an oscillating dipole radiates over a broad range of angles, so echoes could come from anywhere (transcript, pp. 9–10). Interference can narrow it. With three equally spaced slits, neighbouring phase differences are equal, $\delta_{12} = \delta_{23} = \delta$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 10–11). The three fields cancel when their arrows form a closed triangle: $\delta = 2\pi/3$. With four, a closed square: $\delta = 2\pi/4 = \pi/2$; with five, $2\pi/5$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 11). The first zero moves in: the central peak narrows as sources are added.

For $N$ sources, with $r_1 \approx R$ (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 12–14), the sum is a geometric series, $\sum_{m=0}^{N-1}r^m = (1 - r^N)/(1 - r)$ with $r = e^{-i\delta}$:

<Formula label="N-source field" tex="E_{\text{total}} = E_0e^{i(\omega t - kR)}\left[1 + e^{-i\delta} + e^{-2i\delta} + \cdots + e^{-(N-1)i\delta}\right] = E_0e^{i(\omega t - kR)}\,\frac{1 - e^{-i\delta N}}{1 - e^{-i\delta}} = E_0e^{i(\omega t - kR)}e^{-i\delta(N-1)/2}\,\frac{\sin(N\delta/2)}{\sin(\delta/2)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

<Formula label="N-source intensity" tex="\langle I\rangle = I_0\left[\frac{\sin(N\delta/2)}{\sin(\delta/2)}\right]^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

**What it looks like** (notes, p. 5; transcript, pp. 14–15). At $\delta = 0$ all $N$ arrows line up: a **principal maximum**. As $\delta$ grows the chain of arrows curls up, and at $\delta = 2\pi/N$ it closes into a polygon: zero. Between principal maxima, at $\delta = 0, 2\pi, \ldots$, lie $N - 2$ small **secondary maxima**, and the principal maximum has width $4\pi/N$. Lee's way to remember the plots for $N = 3$ to 6: two tall parents and $N - 2$ children, the parents getting slimmer as the family grows.

## Phased radar

**Resolution** (notes, p. 6; transcript, pp. 15–16). The first zero, $\delta = 2\pi/N$, lies at

<Formula label="Width of the beam" tex="\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,d\sin\theta = \frac{2\pi}{N} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{Nd}" cite="reading2" at="p. 15" />

(The notes write $d\sin\theta/\lambda = 2\pi/N$ and $\sin\theta = 2\pi\lambda/Nd$, losing a $2\pi$; the lecture's $\lambda/Nd$ is right.) So for a narrow beam: a **small wavelength**, a **large spacing**, **many emitters**: a big, high-frequency array (notes, p. 6; transcript, p. 16).

**Sweeping** (notes, pp. 6–7; transcript, p. 16). The main beam points straight ahead. Give each emitter an extra phase, $0, \phi, 2\phi, \ldots, (N - 1)\phi$, and the phase difference becomes

<Formula label="Steered array" tex="\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,d\sin\theta - \phi" cite="reading2" at="p. 16" />

so the principal maximum, $\delta = 0$, moves to $\sin\theta = \lambda\phi/2\pi d$. Making $\phi$ grow with time, $\phi \to \phi t$ in the notes, sweeps the beam across the sky with nothing moving: a **phased radar** (notes, pp. 6–7; transcript, p. 16). (The notes' $\sin\theta = \phi t\lambda/2\pi$ is missing the $d$.) The same interference appears for light, water and sound (notes, p. 7).

## One electron at a time

Billiard balls fired at two slits pile up in two heaps behind them (transcript, pp. 16–17). Now an electron source that emits **one electron at a time** (notes, p. 8; transcript, p. 17): two heaps, or an interference pattern? Almost the whole class votes for interference. A video of an experiment by a Hitachi group shows single dots appearing on the detector, one per electron, and, as more and more accumulate, the bands of a two-slit interference pattern (transcript, pp. 18–19). Each electron seems to pass through both slits and **interfere with itself**, as quantum physics predicts (notes, p. 8). Stranger still: put a detector at the slits that reports which one each electron used, and the pattern becomes two heaps (transcript, p. 19).

**Looking ahead** (transcript, pp. 19–21; notes, pp. 9–10). Laser light through two slits gives a row of bright dots, but, unlike the formula, they fade away from the centre, and a single wide slit also gives a pattern. Both come from **diffraction**, the interference of infinitely many sources across each slit, the subject of the next lecture.

<WorkedExample title="An array and two slits of your own">
<Problem>
(a) A radar has $N = 8$ emitters spaced $d = \lambda/2$ apart. At what angle is the first zero beside the main beam? (b) What extra phase $\phi$ between neighbours steers the main beam to $30°$? (c) Red laser light, $\lambda = 633$ nm, passes two slits $0.25$ mm apart onto a screen $2.0$ m away. How far apart are the bright fringes?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Beam width">
$\sin\theta = \lambda/Nd = 1/(8 \times 0.5) = 0.25$, so $\theta \approx 14.5°$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Steering">
$\sin\theta = \lambda\phi/2\pi d = \phi/\pi$, so $\sin 30° = 0.5$ needs $\phi = \pi/2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Fringes">
Bright fringes at $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$; for small angles $y = L\sin\theta$, so the spacing is $L\lambda/d = 2.0 \times 633 \times 10^{-9}/2.5 \times 10^{-4} \approx 5.1 \times 10^{-3}$ m, about 5 mm.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Huygens's principle lets slits act as sources. Two slits: $\delta = (2\pi/\lambda)d\sin\theta$, $\langle I\rangle \propto \cos^2(\delta/2)$.
- $N$ sources: $\langle I\rangle = I_0[\sin(N\delta/2)/\sin(\delta/2)]^2$; first zero at $\delta = 2\pi/N$, so $\sin\theta = \lambda/Nd$.
- A phased radar narrows its beam with short $\lambda$, large $d$ and many emitters, and steers it with an extra phase $\phi$: $\sin\theta = \lambda\phi/2\pi d$.
- Single electrons build up an interference pattern; knowing which slit each used destroys it.
