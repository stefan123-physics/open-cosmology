The [last lecture](/courses/8-03/fourier-transform-am-radio) used the Fourier transform to follow a signal through a dispersive medium. This one shows what the transform says about how sharp a signal can be in time and in frequency at once, connects it to quantum mechanics, and starts on waves in two dimensions (transcript, pp. 1–2).

## A narrow pulse has a wide spectrum

The notes recall the narrow-band result $\psi(x, t) \approx \text{Re}[f_s(t - x/v_g)e^{-i(t - x/v_p)\omega_0}]$, with typical AM values $\omega_0$ from 0.3 to 30 MHz and $\Delta\omega$ about 5 kHz, so $\Delta\omega \ll \omega_0$ (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 1–2). Now take a pulse symmetric about $t = 0$ (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2):

<Formula label="Fourier transform of a two-sided exponential" tex="f(t) = e^{-\Gamma|t|}: \qquad C(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{0}e^{\Gamma t}e^{i\omega t}\,dt + \int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\Gamma t}e^{i\omega t}\,dt\right] = \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{2\Gamma}{\Gamma^2 + \omega^2} = \frac{\Gamma}{\pi(\Gamma^2 + \omega^2)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

(The notes write $e^{\Gamma|t|}$ at the top, which would not decay; the calculation uses $e^{-\Gamma|t|}$, as in the lecture. One step in the notes also has a stray $i$ in the second term.) Lee's plots, with $\Gamma$ raised step by step from 0.1 to 5, show the trade (transcript, pp. 2–3; notes, pp. 1–2): a small $\Gamma$ gives a wide $f(t)$ and a narrow $C(\omega)$; a large $\Gamma$ a narrow pulse and a wide spectrum. No choice makes both narrow. The problem set does the same for a Gaussian (p. 3).

## Measuring the spreads

To make this precise (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 3–4):

1. Take the **intensity** of the signal to be proportional to $|f(t)|^2$, as the energy of an electromagnetic wave goes as the square of its field.
2. Weight averages by it:

<Formula label="Average weighted by the intensity" tex="\langle g(t)\rangle = \frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(t)\,|f(t)|^2\,dt}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(t)|^2\,dt}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

3. Measure the spreads by the mean square deviations, like a standard deviation:

<Formula label="Spread in time and in frequency" tex="\Delta t^2 \equiv \langle(t - \langle t\rangle)^2\rangle = \frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left|(t - \langle t\rangle)f(t)\right|^2dt}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(t)|^2\,dt}, \qquad \Delta\omega^2 \equiv \langle(\omega - \langle\omega\rangle)^2\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The goal: $\Delta\omega\,\Delta t \geq \frac12$ (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 4).

**Frequency as a derivative** (notes, pp. 2–3; transcript, pp. 4–6). Differentiating under the integral pulls out a factor of $\omega$:

<Formula label="Frequency as i d/dt" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\omega\,C(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}\,d\omega = i\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}C(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}\,d\omega = i\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}f(t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

so $\langle\omega\rangle = \int f^*\,i\,\partial f/\partial t\,dt\,/\int|f|^2dt$ and $\Delta\omega^2 = \int|(i\,\partial/\partial t - \langle\omega\rangle)f|^2dt\,/\int|f|^2dt$. The take-home message: in such averages, replace $\omega$ by $i\,\partial/\partial t$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 5–6; Lee fixes where the derivative acts after a question, p. 6).

## The uncertainty principle

Now "the same trick" that proves Heisenberg's uncertainty principle (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 6–10). Write $T = t - \langle t\rangle$ and $\Omega = i\,\partial/\partial t - \langle\omega\rangle$, and for a free real parameter $\kappa$ consider

<Formula label="A function that cannot be negative" tex="\gamma(\kappa, t) \equiv (T - i\kappa\Omega)f(t), \qquad R(\kappa) = \frac{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\gamma(\kappa, t)|^2\,dt}{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(t)|^2\,dt} \geq 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Both integrals are of squares, so $R \geq 0$ (transcript, pp. 6–7). (Lee calls $\gamma$ a small $r$ and the ratio capital $R$; the notes write $R > 0$ where $R \geq 0$ is meant. If you cannot see where it comes from, he says, don't worry, and if you can, maybe you are Heisenberg; p. 6.) Expanding (the notes drop the $\kappa^2$ on the second term, which Lee adds aloud, p. 7), $|\gamma|^2 = |Tf|^2 + |\kappa\Omega f|^2 + i\kappa[Tf\,(\Omega f)^* - (\Omega f)\,Tf^*]$, and in the last term the $\langle\omega\rangle$ parts cancel, leaving $\kappa T\,\partial(ff^*)/\partial t$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 7–8). Integrating by parts, with $f$ vanishing at $t = \pm\infty$ and $\partial T/\partial t = 1$, that term contributes $-\kappa\int|f|^2dt$ (notes, pp. 3–4; transcript, pp. 8–9). The first two terms are exactly $\Delta t^2$ and $\kappa^2\Delta\omega^2$, so (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 9–10)

<Formula label="R as a function of kappa" tex="R(\kappa) = \Delta t^2 + \kappa^2\Delta\omega^2 - \kappa \geq 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

for every $\kappa$. Its minimum, at $\kappa = 1/2\Delta\omega^2$, is $\Delta t^2 - 1/4\Delta\omega^2 \geq 0$, that is (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 10)

<Formula label="Uncertainty principle for waves" tex="\Delta t\,\Delta\omega \geq \frac12" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

No assumption went in except the definitions: it is a mathematical property of waves (transcript, p. 10). A very short shake of the string (small $\Delta t$) necessarily contains a wide range of frequencies.

**AM radio** (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 10–11). A station's bandwidth $\Delta\omega = 2\pi\Delta\nu \approx 3 \times 10^4$ s⁻¹ means $\Delta t$ of a few times $10^{-5}$ s: two pulses much further apart than that can be told apart, closer ones cannot.

## Quantum mechanics

Multiply and divide by a speed $v$ (notes, pp. 4–5; transcript, p. 11): $v\Delta t \cdot \Delta\omega/v \geq \frac12$, that is $\Delta x\,\Delta k \geq \frac12$. In quantum mechanics a particle of momentum $p$ is a wave with $p = \hbar k$, where $\hbar = h/2\pi \approx 1 \times 10^{-34}$ J·s (the notes also give $6.6 \times 10^{-10}$ eV·s, a slip for $6.6 \times 10^{-16}$ eV·s), so

<Formula label="Heisenberg's uncertainty principle" tex="\Delta x\,\Delta p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

Position and momentum cannot both be specified sharply. The limit comes purely from the mathematics of waves; quantum mechanics enters only through $p = \hbar k$ (transcript, pp. 11–12).

## Does light have a mass?

From special relativity, $E^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4$; with $E = \hbar\omega$ and $p = \hbar k$, dividing by $\hbar^2$ gives (transcript, p. 12)

<Formula label="Dispersion relation of a massive photon" tex="\omega^2 = c^2k^2 + \omega_0^2, \qquad \omega_0 = \frac{mc^2}{\hbar}" cite="reading2" at="p. 12" />

a dispersion relation: if the photon had mass, light of different frequencies would travel through vacuum at different speeds. To test it you need a distant source of sharp pulses: a **millisecond pulsar**, a rapidly rotating neutron star spun up by matter taken from a companion, sweeping a beam of radio waves past the Earth (pp. 12–13). Lee quotes the pulsar B1937+21, which turns once every 1.6 ms, as about 16,000 light years away. Its pulses arrive at different times at different frequencies, which taken at face value means a photon mass of $1.3 \times 10^{-49}$ g, too large to fit other measurements, for example of galactic magnetic fields (pp. 13–14). The real explanation: the space in between is not quite empty but holds very dilute free electrons, which make light slightly dispersive, a topic for a later lecture (p. 14).

## Waves in two dimensions

A large plate, or a membrane, has a displacement $\psi(x, y)$ (transcript, p. 14). On an infinite plate, translation symmetry in $x$ and in $y$ fixes the normal modes (notes, p. 5; transcript, p. 15):

<Formula label="Two-dimensional normal mode" tex="\psi(x, y) = A\,e^{ik_xx}e^{ik_yy} = A\,e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}, \qquad \vec k = k_x\hat x + k_y\hat y, \quad \vec r = x\hat x + y\hat y" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

(the real part, with $e^{-i\omega t}$ for the time dependence). For a plate of finite size with fixed edges, the boundary conditions turn these into $\sin k_xx\,\sin k_yy$, zero at all four edges, and select the allowed $k_x$ and $k_y$; free edges give cosines instead (transcript, pp. 15–16).

**Chladni figures** (transcript, pp. 16–17). In a demonstration, a loudspeaker under a metal plate drives it at a chosen frequency. The vibration is too fast to see, so Lee pours sand on it. At a normal-mode frequency the sand is shaken off the moving parts and gathers on the **nodal lines**, the curves where the plate does not move: a two-dimensional version of the nodes of a string. At higher frequencies, higher modes appear with more and more lines, perhaps, Lee suggests, a pattern for a T-shirt.

The notes go further, with a mesh of beads joined by strings with tensions $T_H$ and $T_V$ and spacings $a_H$ and $a_V$ (notes, pp. 5–6). Translation symmetry again gives $e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}$ modes, now with

<Formula label="Beaded mesh" tex="\omega^2 = \frac{4T_H}{ma_H}\sin^2\frac{k_xa_H}{2} + \frac{4T_V}{ma_V}\sin^2\frac{k_ya_V}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

In one dimension a frequency allows only two modes, $e^{\pm ikx}$; in two, a fixed $\omega$ allows infinitely many, since a smaller $k_x$ can be balanced by a larger $k_y$ (notes, p. 6). With walls, $k_x = n_x\pi/L_H$, $k_y = n_y\pi/L_V$, and the modes are products $\sin(n_x\pi x/L_H)\sin(n_y\pi y/L_V)$ times an amplitude (notes, p. 6). The next lecture takes this up.

<WorkedExample title="Spreads of your own">
<Problem>
(a) For $f(t) = e^{-\Gamma|t|}$ find $\Delta t$ and $\Delta\omega$, and check the uncertainty relation. (b) A radio channel has a bandwidth of $\Delta\nu = 9$ kHz. What is the shortest time between pulses it can carry?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The two-sided exponential">
$|f|^2 = e^{-2\Gamma|t|}$ and $\langle t\rangle = 0$ by symmetry. $\int|f|^2dt = 1/\Gamma$ and $\int t^2|f|^2dt = 1/2\Gamma^3$, so $\Delta t^2 = 1/2\Gamma^2$. Since $f$ is real and even, $\langle\omega\rangle = 0$, and $|\partial f/\partial t| = \Gamma|f|$, so $\Delta\omega^2 = \Gamma^2$. Then $\Delta t\,\Delta\omega = 1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.71 \geq 0.5$, whatever $\Gamma$ is.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The channel">
$\Delta\omega = 2\pi \times 9000 \approx 5.7 \times 10^4$ s⁻¹, so $\Delta t \geq 1/(2\Delta\omega) \approx 8.8 \times 10^{-6}$ s: pulses must be roughly ten microseconds apart or more.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A narrow pulse has a wide spectrum: for $e^{-\Gamma|t|}$, $C(\omega) = \Gamma/\pi(\Gamma^2 + \omega^2)$.
- With spreads weighted by $|f|^2$ and $\omega$ replaced by $i\,\partial/\partial t$, the non-negative $R(\kappa) = \Delta t^2 + \kappa^2\Delta\omega^2 - \kappa$ gives $\Delta t\,\Delta\omega \geq \frac12$ for any wave.
- With $x = vt$ and $p = \hbar k$: $\Delta x\,\Delta p \geq \hbar/2$.
- A photon mass would make light dispersive; pulsar delays come from interstellar electrons instead.
- In two dimensions, translation symmetry gives modes $e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec r}$, and $\sin k_xx\sin k_yy$ on a plate with fixed edges; sand on a vibrating plate traces the nodal lines.
