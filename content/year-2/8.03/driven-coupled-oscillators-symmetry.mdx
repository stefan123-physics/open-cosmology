summary: "An arbitrary excitation of coupled oscillators is not harmonic, its amplitudes vary and its energy migrates between the parts; a normal-mode excitation is harmonic, with constant amplitude ratios and energy that stays put; and any motion is a sum of normal modes. Lectures 5 and 6 were given by Prof. Boleslaw Wyslouch (transcript, p. 1), substituting for Prof. Lee (Lecture 5 transcript, p. 1). Coupled oscillators are everywhere: pendulums joined by springs, masses on a track, one pendulum hanging from another, tuning forks coupled through the air, two LC circuits joined by a wire; more masses only make the mathematics longer (pp. 1–2).

## Arbitrary excitation and normal modes

Two kinds of motion of a coupled system can be contrasted (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 2–3):

| | Motion | Amplitude ratio | Energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arbitrary excitation | not harmonic | varies | migrates |
| Normal-mode excitation | harmonic | constant | stays |

In an arbitrary excitation the pendulums pass energy back and forth, as the [beats](/courses/8-03/coupled-pendulums-beats) showed; in a normal mode everything moves in sync, and the energy given to each part stays there (transcript, pp. 2–3). And any arbitrary excitation is a superposition of normal modes (p. 3). The number of normal-mode frequencies equals the number of masses (p. 4).

## Driving the pendulums

Take the two pendulums joined by a spring and push mass 1 with an external force (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 5):

<Formula label="Driving force" tex="F_d = F_0\cos(\omega_dt)\,\hat x" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The driving frequency $\omega_d$ is set from outside and has nothing to do with the normal-mode frequencies (transcript, p. 5). Damping is left out to keep the calculation manageable; if a result comes out infinite, damping will be called back (p. 5). The equations of motion gain one term, on mass 1 only; mass 2 feels the force only through the spring (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 6):

<Formula label="Driven equations" tex="m\ddot x_1 = -\left(k + \frac{mg}{l}\right)x_1 + kx_2 + F_0\cos(\omega_dt), \qquad m\ddot x_2 = kx_1 - \left(k + \frac{mg}{l}\right)x_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

In matrix form, with $M$ and $K$ as before and $F = (F_0, 0)$ (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 6):

<Formula label="Matrix form" tex="M\ddot X = -KX + F\cos(\omega_dt)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The undriven problem gave $\omega_1^2 = g/l$ with shape $(1, 1)$ and $\omega_2^2 = g/l + 2k/m$ with shape $(1, -1)$, and its determinant can be written compactly (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 6–7):

<Formula label="Compact determinant" tex="\det(M^{-1}K - \omega^2I) = (\omega^2 - \omega_1^2)(\omega^2 - \omega_2^2)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

## The steady state

Everything driven at $\omega_d$ moves at $\omega_d$ (transcript, p. 7). So try (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 8)

<Formula label="Trial solution" tex="Z = Be^{i\omega_dt}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} B_1 \\ B_2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The oscillating factors cancel, leaving two linear equations for the unknown amplitudes, everything else being given (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Equations for the amplitudes" tex="(M^{-1}K - \omega_d^2I)B = M^{-1}F: \qquad \begin{pmatrix} \frac km + \frac gl - \omega_d^2 & -\frac km \\ -\frac km & \frac km + \frac gl - \omega_d^2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} B_1 \\ B_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} F_0/m \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Two equations, two unknowns: they could be solved by elimination, but 8.03 likes **Cramer's rule** "for some historical reasons", while Wyslouch himself would use MATLAB or Mathematica (transcript, p. 9). Replace the first column of the matrix by the right-hand side for $B_1$, the second for $B_2$, and divide by the determinant (notes, pp. 2–3; transcript, pp. 10–11; see [Cramer's rule](/courses/18-06/cramers-rule-inverse-volume)):

<Formula label="Steady-state amplitudes" tex="B_1 = \frac{\frac{F_0}{m}\left(\frac km + \frac gl - \omega_d^2\right)}{(\omega_d^2 - \omega_1^2)(\omega_d^2 - \omega_2^2)}, \qquad B_2 = \frac{\frac{F_0}{m}\,\frac km}{(\omega_d^2 - \omega_1^2)(\omega_d^2 - \omega_2^2)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

## Resonance picks out the modes

The ratio (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 11–12):

<Formula label="Amplitude ratio" tex="\frac{B_1}{B_2} = \frac{k/m + g/l - \omega_d^2}{k/m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

At $\omega_d^2 = \omega_1^2 = g/l$ it is $+1$, the shape of the in-phase mode; at $\omega_d^2 = \omega_2^2 = g/l + 2k/m$ it is $-1$, the shape of the opposite-phase mode (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 12). Wyslouch then asks what is "fishy" about this and points out the catch himself: there both amplitudes are infinite, and infinity over infinity is a ratio of one. The answer is damping: a little of it keeps them large but finite (pp. 12–13). In Wyslouch's computed curves of $B_1$ and $B_2$ against $\omega_d$, both amplitudes are small at low frequency, where the masses move slowly together, and huge near each normal-mode frequency, with ratio $+1$ near the first and $-1$ near the second (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 13–14).

**Demonstrations** (transcript, pp. 14–17). Two coupled carts on a track behave the same way. Driven slowly, they barely move, together; turned up to the first resonance, they swing hugely together; moved to the second, the system "knows" to swing in opposition, while the motion left over from the random start is still mixed in (p. 14); with damping that homogeneous part dies away and the driven part remains (pp. 16–17). For three masses, Wyslouch first shows the three modes in a web applet from Colorado, a computer simulation (p. 15): all together; the middle at rest and the outer two opposite; and the middle moving twice as far, opposite to the outer two (ratio $-2$). His computed amplitude curves show three resonances (p. 16). Then the real three-cart system is driven: knob settings of about 32, 56 and 68 (no unit given; the last "according to my helpers") bring out each mode in turn (pp. 16–17). This "fishes out" normal modes: an earthquake that matches one building's normal frequency can bring it down while its neighbours stand, and an old car rattles when the road hits the frequency of one of its parts (pp. 14–15, 17).

**The full solution** is the homogeneous part plus the driven part (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 17):

<Formula label="Full solution" tex="x_1 = \alpha\cos(\omega_1t + \phi_1) + \beta\cos(\omega_2t + \phi_2) + B_1\cos(\omega_dt), \qquad x_2 = \alpha\cos(\omega_1t + \phi_1) - \beta\cos(\omega_2t + \phi_2) + B_2\cos(\omega_dt)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

With a little damping the homogeneous part dies away and the driven part, fed by the external force, stays; damping also shifts the $B$'s slightly (transcript, p. 17).

## Symmetry finds the normal modes

Many systems are symmetric, and then so are their normal modes (transcript, pp. 18–19). For the two pendulums the mirror symmetry is looking at the system from the other side: what one observer calls $x_1$, the other calls $-x_2$ (p. 19). As a matrix (pp. 19–20):

<Formula label="Mirror symmetry" tex="\tilde X(t) = SX(t), \qquad S = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 19–20" />

The physics is the same from either side, so if $X(t)$ is a solution, so is $\tilde X(t)$ (p. 19). In matrix language: when the system is symmetric, $S$ commutes with $M$ and $K$, $MS = SM$ and $KS = SK$; multiplying the equation of motion by $S$ then shows that $S\ddot X = -M^{-1}KSX$, so $\tilde X$ solves it too (p. 20). A normal mode oscillating at $\omega_1$, reshuffled by the constant matrix $S$, still oscillates at $\omega_1$, so it can only change by a factor: the normal-mode shapes are **eigenvectors of $S$** (pp. 21–22):

<Formula label="Eigenvectors of S" tex="S\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = -\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad S\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix} = +\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 21–22" />

(Wyslouch says the eigenvalues are "plus one" and "plus 2"; the calculation he has just done gives $-1$ and $+1$.) Finding the eigenvectors of a matrix of zeros and minus ones is "infinitely simpler" than working with all the $g$'s and $k$'s; he states without proof that they are the eigenvectors of $M^{-1}K$ as well (pp. 22–23).

<WorkedExample title="A driven pair of your own">
<Problem>
Use the pendulums of the beats page: $g/l = 19.6$ s⁻², $k/m = 2.0$ s⁻², so $\omega_1^2 = 19.6$ and $\omega_2^2 = 23.6$ s⁻². Drive mass 1 with $F_0/m = 0.10$ m/s². Find $B_1$ and $B_2$ at $\omega_d = 4.0$ rad/s, and at $\omega_d^2 = k/m + g/l = 21.6$ s⁻².
</Problem>
<Step title="Below both resonances">
$\omega_d^2 = 16$: the denominator is $(16 - 19.6)(16 - 23.6) = (-3.6)(-7.6) = 27.36$. Then $B_1 = 0.10(2.0 + 19.6 - 16)/27.36 = 0.56/27.36 \approx 0.020$ m and $B_2 = 0.10 \cdot 2.0/27.36 \approx 0.0073$ m. Both positive, in step with the force, and the driven mass moves about three times as much.
</Step>
<Step title="Between the resonances">
$\omega_d^2 = 21.6$: the denominator is $(2.0)(-2.0) = -4.0$, and the numerator of $B_1$ is $0.10(21.6 - 21.6) = 0$. So $B_1 = 0$: the mass that is pushed stays still, while $B_2 = 0.10 \cdot 2.0/(-4.0) = -0.050$ m. The second pendulum swings against the force so that its spring exactly cancels the drive on the first.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Arbitrary excitations wander; normal modes are harmonic with fixed shapes; any motion is a sum of normal modes.
- Driven: $(M^{-1}K - \omega_d^2I)B = M^{-1}F$, solved by Cramer's rule; resonance at each normal-mode frequency, with that mode's shape.
- Full motion = decaying homogeneous part + steady state $B\cos\omega_dt$.
- A symmetry $S$ that commutes with $M$ and $K$ has the normal-mode shapes as its eigenvectors.
