## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A ringing RLC circuit" points={20} mit="Problem 2.1">

A capacitor of $2\ \mu\text{F}$, a coil of $2$ mH and a resistor of $20\ \Omega$ are joined in one loop. Call $Q$ the charge on the capacitor's positive plate, so that the capacitor voltage is $V_C = Q/C$. The current $i$ leaves the positive plate, runs through the coil and the resistor and returns to the negative plate: it is the rate at which the capacitor loses charge, $i = -\dot Q$. (MIT's Figure 1 shows exactly this loop, with the arrow of $i$ pointing from the capacitor's positive plate through the coil toward the resistor.) At $t = 0$ the capacitor voltage is $V_C = 5$ V and the current is $i = 0.5$ A. Take $t > 0$ throughout.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Set up the equation of motion for $Q(t)$ and bring it to the standard form $\ddot Q + \gamma\dot Q + \omega_0^2 Q = 0$. What are the damping rate $\gamma$, in s⁻¹, and the natural angular frequency $\omega_0$, in rad/s?

<Hint>

Go once round the loop. The voltage across the capacitor must equal the sum of the voltages across the coil, $L\,di/dt$, and across the resistor, $R\,i$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Replace $i$ by $-\dot Q$ and $di/dt$ by $-\ddot Q$, then divide by $L$. The coefficient of $\dot Q$ is $\gamma$ and the coefficient of $Q$ is $\omega_0^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-gamma" label="$\gamma =$" value="10000" unit="s⁻¹" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-gamma" />

<Answer id="p1-omega0" label="$\omega_0 =$" value="15811" unit="rad/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-omega0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Kirchhoff's loop rule turns the circuit into a damped oscillator: charge plays the part of the displacement, the coil is the mass, the resistor is the drag and the capacitor is the spring.

<Step title="The loop rule">

The capacitor drives the current through the coil and the resistor, so its voltage equals their voltage drops:

<Formula label="The RLC loop" tex="\frac{Q}{C} = L\,\frac{di}{dt} + R\,i, \qquad i = -\dot Q \quad\Longrightarrow\quad L\ddot Q + R\dot Q + \frac{Q}{C} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2.1(a) (our own solution)" />

Without the resistor this is the LC circuit of the lesson, $\ddot Q + Q/(LC) = 0$ (Lecture 2 notes, p. 1). The resistor adds a term proportional to $\dot Q$, like a drag force.

</Step>

<Step title="The standard form">

Dividing by $L$ gives the damped oscillator of the lesson:

<Formula label="Standard form" tex="\ddot Q + \gamma\dot Q + \omega_0^2 Q = 0, \qquad \gamma = \frac{R}{L}, \qquad \omega_0^2 = \frac{1}{LC}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4 (the form of the damped equation)" />

With the numbers: $\gamma = 20/0.002 = 1.0\times 10^4\ \text{s}^{-1}$ and $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{0.002 \times 2\times 10^{-6}} = 1/\sqrt{4\times 10^{-9}} = 1.581\times 10^4$ rad/s.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: ohms divided by henries is a rate, 1/s; and henries times farads is seconds squared. Both are right. The damping rate is smaller than the natural frequency, so the circuit rings. Part (b) puts that into a precise statement.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

What kind of oscillator is this: undamped, underdamped, critically damped or overdamped? Explain. Then give the angular frequency $\omega$ with which the circuit actually oscillates, in rad/s.

<Hint>

The kind depends on how $\gamma/2$ compares with $\omega_0$. Which of the three solution types of the lesson has $\omega_0^2 > \gamma^2/4$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In that case the oscillation frequency is $\omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-kind" choices={["Undamped", "Underdamped", "Critically damped", "Overdamped"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1-omega" label="$\omega =$" value="15000" unit="rad/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-omega" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compare the damping with the natural frequency. The resistor is not zero, so the circuit is not undamped. The three damped cases are told apart by the sign of $\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4$.

<Step title="Compare">

Here $\gamma/2 = 5.0\times 10^3\ \text{s}^{-1}$, which is smaller than $\omega_0 = 1.581\times 10^4$ rad/s, so $\omega_0^2 > \gamma^2/4$: the circuit is **underdamped**. The charge oscillates with a decaying envelope and crosses zero again and again.

<Formula label="Underdamped frequency" tex="\omega = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \frac{\gamma^2}{4}} = \sqrt{2.5\times 10^{8} - 2.5\times 10^{7}} = 1.5\times 10^{4}\ \text{rad/s}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The same statement in terms of the resistor: the circuit is critically damped when $R = 2\sqrt{L/C} = 2\sqrt{10^3} = 63.2\ \Omega$, and overdamped above that. The actual resistor of $20\ \Omega$ is well below it, so underdamped is right. The frequency $\omega$ is only 5% below $\omega_0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={8}>

Find $V_C(t)$ for all $t > 0$, in volts, with $t$ in seconds. Use the initial values given above.

<Hint>

$V_C = Q/C$ and $C$ is constant, so $V_C$ obeys the same equation as $Q$. Write its underdamped solution with a cosine and a sine term and the envelope $e^{-\gamma t/2}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You need two conditions: the value $V_C(0)$ and the slope $\dot V_C(0) = \dot Q(0)/C$. The slope comes from the current, and the current leaves the positive plate.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-vc" label="$V_C(t) =$" expr="exp(-5000*t)*(5*cos(15000*t) - 15*sin(15000*t))"
  vars={{ t: [0, 0.0004] }}
  names={{ t: "t" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-5000*t)*(5*cos(15000*t) + 55/3*sin(15000*t))", hint: "The sign of the initial slope is wrong. The current leaves the positive plate, so the capacitor voltage is falling at t = 0, not rising." },
    { expr: "exp(-10000*t)*(5*cos(15000*t) - 15*sin(15000*t))", hint: "The envelope falls as e^(-γt/2), half the damping rate γ = R/L, not as e^(-γt)." },
    { expr: "5*exp(-5000*t)*cos(15000*t)", hint: "A cosine alone has zero slope at t = 0 apart from the envelope. The second initial condition, the current, fixes a sine term as well." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write the general underdamped solution for the voltage, with two constants, and fix them with the initial voltage and the initial slope.

<Step title="The general solution">

<Formula label="Underdamped solution for the capacitor voltage" tex="V_C(t) = e^{-\gamma t/2}\left(a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t\right), \qquad \frac{\gamma}{2} = 5000\ \text{s}^{-1}, \quad \omega = 15000\ \text{rad/s}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two conditions">

At $t = 0$ the voltage is $5$ V, so $a = 5$ V. For the slope, $\dot V_C = \dot Q/C = -i/C$, so

<Formula label="Initial slope of the voltage" tex="\dot V_C(0) = -\frac{i(0)}{C} = -\frac{0.5}{2\times 10^{-6}} = -2.5\times 10^{5}\ \text{V/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2.1(c) (our own solution)" />

Differentiating the general solution at $t = 0$ gives $\dot V_C(0) = -\tfrac{\gamma}{2}a + \omega b$. Hence $15000\,b = -2.5\times 10^5 + 5000 \times 5 = -2.25\times 10^5$, so $b = -15$ V.

</Step>

<Step title="The result">

<Formula label="The capacitor voltage" tex="V_C(t) = e^{-5000\,t}\left(5\cos 15000\,t - 15\sin 15000\,t\right)\ \text{V}, \qquad t \text{ in seconds}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2.1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$ it gives 5 V. Its slope at $t = 0$ is $-5000 \times 5 + 15000 \times (-15) = -2.5\times 10^5$ V/s, as required. The envelope has fallen to $1/e$ after $2/\gamma = 0.2$ ms, less than half a period $2\pi/\omega = 0.42$ ms, so the ringing dies out within a few periods (to 1% after about 0.9 ms, about two periods). The voltage even changes sign within the first fifth of a period, because the circuit starts out discharging at a high rate.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A crystal on four springs, shaken by its table" points={20} mit="Problem 2.2">

In a laboratory a delicate crystal of mass $M$ rests on four massless springs side by side, each with spring constant $k$. The whole set-up stands on a table, and moving the table makes the tabletop vibrate. In the frame of the tabletop, this acts as an extra vertical force $M A_0\cos\omega_d t$ on the crystal. Let $x$ be the vertical displacement of the crystal from its equilibrium position. The displacements are small, so the springs obey Hooke's law. In parts (a) to (c) there is no drag.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Write down the equation of motion. In the form $\ddot x + \omega_0^2 x = f_0\cos\omega_d t$, what is $\omega_0^2$?

<Hint>

Four springs side by side all stretch by the same amount, so their forces add. What single spring constant would do the same job?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-w0sq" label="$\omega_0^2 =$" expr="4*k/M"
  vars={{ k: [2, 5], M: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ M: "M" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "k/M", hint: "That is the value for one spring. The four springs all push on the crystal, so their constants add." },
    { expr: "sqrt(4*k/M)", hint: "That is ω₀ itself. The equation needs the coefficient of x, which is ω₀²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Springs in parallel add their spring constants. The table's vibration enters as a driving force.

<Step title="Equation of motion">

The restoring force is $-4kx$. The driving force is $MA_0\cos\omega_d t$. Newton's second law:

<Formula label="Equation of motion of the crystal" tex="M\ddot x = -4k\,x + M A_0\cos\omega_d t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot x + \omega_0^2 x = A_0\cos\omega_d t, \qquad \omega_0^2 = \frac{4k}{M}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2.2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So $f_0 = A_0$ in the notation of the lesson on driven oscillators.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $k/M$ is N/(m kg) = 1/s², as $\omega_0^2$ must be. A stiffer set of springs or a lighter crystal raises the natural frequency, as one would expect.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

In the steady state, with very little energy loss, find the amplitude of the crystal's vibration. Take $\omega_d$ below the natural frequency.

<Hint>

After the homogeneous solution has died out, the crystal moves at the driving frequency, $x = A\cos\omega_d t$ with no phase shift when there is no drag. Put it into the equation.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Then $\ddot x = -\omega_d^2 x$. Collect the terms in $A$ on one side.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-amp" label="$A =$" expr="A0/abs(4*k/M - wd^2)"
  vars={{ A0: [0.5, 2], k: [2, 5], M: [0.5, 1.5], wd: [0.2, 1.1] }}
  names={{ A0: "A_0", wd: "\\omega_d", M: "M" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "A0/abs(k/M - wd^2)", hint: "The restoring term is the sum of the four springs, 4k, not k." },
    { expr: "M*A0/abs(4*k/M - wd^2)", hint: "Check the units: a mass times an acceleration over a rate squared is not a length. The driving force per unit mass is A₀, so no extra factor M." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** After the transient the motion follows the drive, and the amplitude is the drive divided by how far the frequency is from resonance.

<Step title="Steady state">

Try $x = A\cos\omega_d t$ in the equation of motion. Then $\ddot x = -\omega_d^2 x$ and

<Formula label="Steady-state amplitude without drag" tex="(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)\,A = A_0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A = \frac{A_0}{\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2} = \frac{A_0}{4k/M - \omega_d^2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2 (the amplitude formula, with γ = 0)" />

For $\omega_d < \omega_0$ this is positive and the crystal moves in step with the table. Above resonance $A$ would be negative: the motion is then opposite to the drive, with an amplitude of the same form, $A_0/|4k/M - \omega_d^2|$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: an acceleration over a rate squared is a length. For $\omega_d \to 0$ the amplitude is $A_0 M/(4k)$, which is the static stretch of the springs under a constant extra force $MA_0$, as it must be. When $\omega_d \to \omega_0$ the amplitude grows without limit: that is resonance, which a little drag would tame.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Suppose the four springs are replaced by four new ones of the same wire and the same cross-section but a different length. The aim is to make the amplitude of part (b) ten times smaller. What is the new spring constant $k'$ in terms of $k$, $M$ and $\omega_d$? Then, assuming $k/M \gg \omega_d^2$, how long must the new springs be, compared with the old ones? Type the ratio of the new length to the old length.

<Hint>

The new amplitude is $A_0/(4k'/M - \omega_d^2)$. Set it equal to a tenth of the old one and solve for $k'$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For a spring of fixed material and cross-section, the spring constant is inversely proportional to the length. Now use the condition $k/M \gg \omega_d^2$ to simplify $k'$ and then compare the lengths.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-knew" label="$k' =$" expr="10*k - 9*M*wd^2/4"
  vars={{ k: [2, 5], M: [0.5, 1.5], wd: [0.2, 1.1] }}
  names={{ M: "M", wd: "\\omega_d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "10*k", hint: "That is only what is left when ω_d² is negligible next to k/M. The exact condition keeps the ω_d² term: the amplitude is A₀ over 4k/M minus ω_d²." },
    { expr: "k/10", hint: "A softer spring moves the natural frequency closer to the drive, so the crystal shakes more, not less. The new spring has to be stiffer." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-ratio" label="$\ell'/\ell =$" value="0.1" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Far below resonance the amplitude is inversely proportional to the spring constant, so ten times smaller amplitude needs ten times stiffer springs. A stiffer spring of the same wire is a shorter spring.

<Step title="The new spring constant">

The amplitude must become a tenth of the old one:

<Formula label="Condition for a tenfold smaller amplitude" tex="\frac{A_0}{4k'/M - \omega_d^2} = \frac{1}{10}\,\frac{A_0}{4k/M - \omega_d^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k' = 10\,k - \frac{9}{4}\,M\omega_d^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2.2(c) (our own solution)" />

For $k/M \gg \omega_d^2$ the second term is negligible: $k' \approx 10\,k$.

</Step>

<Step title="The length">

At fixed material and cross-section, $k \propto 1/\ell$. So $\ell'/\ell = k/k' \approx 1/10$: the new springs must be **ten times shorter**.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The shorter spring is stiffer, so the natural frequency, which is above the driving frequency already, moves further away from it: that is why the amplitude falls. Far below resonance the amplitude is about $A_0/\omega_0^2$, so a factor 10 in $\omega_0^2$, that is in $k$, gives a factor 10 in the amplitude.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={6}>

A better way is to put a soft, massless cushion between crystal and table, next to the springs, that gives a resistive force $-b\dot x$. Derive the condition that fixes $b$ so that the amplitude is again ten times smaller than the original one of part (b) (the springs are the old ones, with spring constant $k$). Give $b$ in terms of $k$, $M$ and $\omega_d$, and then for $k/M \gg \omega_d^2$ (keep the factor exact, for example with `sqrt(99)`).

<Hint>

The equation of motion now has a damping term. In the standard form it reads $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = A_0\cos\omega_dt$ with $\gamma = b/M$. The amplitude of the steady state is the one from the lesson on driven oscillators.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The amplitude has the square root of $(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega_d^2$ in the denominator. Square the condition "tenth of the old amplitude" and solve for $\gamma$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$b =$" expr="sqrt(99)*(4*k - M*wd^2)/wd"
  vars={{ k: [2, 5], M: [0.5, 1.5], wd: [0.2, 1.1] }}
  names={{ M: "M", wd: "\\omega_d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(99)*(4*k/M - wd^2)/wd", hint: "Check the units: this is a rate, not a mass per time. γ = b/M, so b = Mγ." },
    { expr: "99*(4*k - M*wd^2)/wd", hint: "The squared amplitudes differ by a factor 100, so (γω_d)² = 99 (ω₀² − ω_d²)². Take the square root: the factor is √99, not 99." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-b-approx" label="$b \approx$" expr="4*sqrt(99)*k/wd"
  vars={{ k: [2, 5], wd: [0.2, 1.1] }}
  names={{ wd: "\\omega_d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(99)*k/wd", hint: "The cushion works alongside four springs, so the stiffness is 4k, not k." },

    { expr: "40*k/wd", hint: "That makes the drag term ten times the spring term, but the two add as the squares under a root: the amplitude then drops by √101, which is about 10.05, not exactly 10." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** Drag adds a term to the denominator of the amplitude. The amplitude falls by ten when that extra term is large enough, not the spring term.

<Step title="Equation of motion with the cushion">

<Formula label="The crystal with a cushion" tex="M\ddot x = -4k\,x - b\dot x + MA_0\cos\omega_d t \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = A_0\cos\omega_d t, \quad \gamma = \frac{b}{M}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2 (the driven damped oscillator)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The amplitude with drag">

The steady-state amplitude of the lesson is

<Formula label="Amplitude of the driven damped oscillator" tex="A_b = \frac{A_0}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega_d^2}}" cite="reading2" at="p. 2" />

With no cushion, $A = A_0/(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)$. Setting $A_b = A/10$, squaring and cancelling $A_0^2$:

<Formula label="Condition for the cushion" tex="(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega_d^2 = 100\,(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \gamma\,\omega_d = \sqrt{99}\,(\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2.2(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for b">

With $\gamma = b/M$ and $\omega_0^2 = 4k/M$:

<Formula label="The cushion constant" tex="b = \sqrt{99}\,\frac{4k - M\omega_d^2}{\omega_d} \;\approx\; \frac{4\sqrt{99}\,k}{\omega_d} \approx \frac{39.8\,k}{\omega_d} \qquad (k/M \gg \omega_d^2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2.2(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $k/\omega_d$ is (N/m)·s = kg/s, a drag constant. The springs are not changed at all, yet the amplitude drops by ten. The drag term adds in quadrature to the spring term, so it must be $\sqrt{99} \approx 10$ times as large as the spring term $\omega_0^2 - \omega_d^2$. The price is that the cushion now dissipates energy, whereas the stiffer springs of part (c) do not.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The traffic light in the hurricane" points={20} mit="Problem 2.3">

In a hurricane a physicist watches a traffic signal hang from wires above an intersection. It bobs up and down like a mass on a damped spring, the wires playing the part of the spring. She sees that the oscillation amplitude falls to $1/e$ of its value in 4 s. Then the lower half of the signal breaks off and crashes to the ground. The remaining half settles at an equilibrium position 0.1 m higher than before. (MIT's drawing shows the signal before and after, with the 0.1 m marked.) Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, and assume that the wires are an ideal spring and that all the dissipation is in them.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

What is the natural frequency of the remaining half, with no damping, in hertz?

<Hint>

The equilibrium position is where the spring force balances the weight. How much does the wire stretch for a mass $m$, and how does that stretch change when the mass is halved?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The stretch is $mg/k$. The rise of 0.1 m is the difference between the two stretches. Find $k/m$ for the remaining half from it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-f0" label="$f_0 =$" value="1.576" unit="Hz" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-f0" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The wire's stretch at rest is $mg/k$, which carries the ratio $k/m$ and with it the natural frequency. The 0.1 m rise measures the change of that stretch when the mass halves.

<Step title="Stretch before and after">

With the whole signal of mass $M$ the wire is stretched by $Mg/k$. With half of it the stretch is $(M/2)g/k$. The signal rises by the difference:

<Formula label="Rise of the equilibrium position" tex="\frac{Mg}{k} - \frac{Mg}{2k} = \frac{Mg}{2k} = 0.1\ \text{m} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{(M/2)g}{k} = 0.1\ \text{m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2.3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So the stretch of the remaining half is 0.1 m, and $m'g/k = 0.1$ m with $m' = M/2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The natural frequency">

<Hint>

**The idea.** At equilibrium the springs balance gravity, so the displacements from equilibrium obey equations with no gravity term, written with the changes of length only.

</Step>

<Step title="The equations">

A spring stretched by $s$ pulls its mass toward the spring with force $K s$. So

<Formula label="Equations of motion of the two masses" tex="m_2\ddot y_2 = -K_A\,y_2 - K_B\,(y_2 - y_1), \qquad m_1\ddot y_1 = -K_C\,y_1 - K_B\,(y_1 - y_2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2.5(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

If $y_1 = y_2 = y$, spring B stays unstretched and each mass sees only its own outer spring: $m_2\ddot y = -K_A y$ and $m_1\ddot y = -K_C y$. If $K_B$ is very large the masses move together. Gravity would add $-m_1g$ and $-m_2g$, which cancel the constant parts of the spring forces at equilibrium and drop out of these equations.

</Step>

</Solution>

Write the equations of motion as $\ddot X = -(M^{-1}K)X$ with $X = (y_1, y_2)^T$ (the lower mass first), and give the four entries of the matrix $M^{-1}K$ in s⁻², with the numbers above.

<Hint>

Write $M\ddot X = -KX$ with $M = \text{diag}(m_1, m_2)$ and read the matrix $K$ off the equations of part (a): the coefficient of $y_1$ in the first row, and so on.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Divide the first row by $m_1$ and the second by $m_2$. The matrix is not symmetric.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-m11" label="$(M^{-1}K)_{11} =$" value="21" unit="s⁻²" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-m11" />

<Answer id="p5-m12" label="$(M^{-1}K)_{12} =$" value="-15" unit="s⁻²" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-m12" />

<Answer id="p5-m21" label="$(M^{-1}K)_{21} =$" value="-5" unit="s⁻²" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-m21" />

<Answer id="p5-m22" label="$(M^{-1}K)_{22} =$" value="11.5" unit="s⁻²" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-m22" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Collect the coefficients of $y_1$ and $y_2$ in each equation into the stiffness matrix $K$ and divide each row by its mass.

<Step title="The matrices">

From the equations of part (a), with the rows in the order $y_1$, $y_2$:

<Formula label="Matrix form" tex="M\ddot X = -KX, \quad M = \begin{pmatrix} m_1 & 0 \\ 0 & m_2 \end{pmatrix}, \quad K = \begin{pmatrix} K_B + K_C & -K_B \\ -K_B & K_A + K_B \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading3" at="p. 3 (the matrix form MẌ = −KX)" />

Then

<Formula label="The matrix M⁻¹K" tex="M^{-1}K = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{K_B + K_C}{m_1} & -\frac{K_B}{m_1} \\ -\frac{K_B}{m_2} & \frac{K_A + K_B}{m_2} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 21 & -15 \\ -5 & 11.5 \end{pmatrix}\ \text{s}^{-2}" cite="reading3" at="p. 3 (the matrix M⁻¹K)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: N/m divided by kg is 1/s². The matrix is not symmetric, but $M^{-1}K$ never needs to be, only $K$ is. In each row of $K$ the entries have sum $K_C$ (for $y_1$) and $K_A$ (for $y_2$), the outer springs: moving both masses together stretches only the outer springs.

</Step>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Find the normal modes and their angular frequencies. Give the angular frequencies $\omega_1 < \omega_2$ in rad/s, and for each mode the ratio $y_1/y_2$ of the amplitudes of the two masses.

<Hint>

Try $X = \text{Re}[A e^{i\omega t}]$. This gives $(M^{-1}K - \omega^2 I)A = 0$, which has a nonzero solution only if $\det(M^{-1}K - \omega^2 I) = 0$. It is a quadratic in $\omega^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For each $\omega^2$, put it back into the first row, $(21 - \omega^2)A_1 - 15A_2 = 0$, to find the ratio $A_1/A_2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-w1" label="$\omega_1 =$" value="2.524" unit="rad/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-w1" />

<Answer id="p5-w2" label="$\omega_2 =$" value="5.111" unit="rad/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-w2" />

<Answer id="p5-r1" label="$y_1/y_2$ (slow mode) $=$" value="1.025" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-r1" />

<Answer id="p5-r2" label="$y_1/y_2$ (fast mode) $=$" value="-2.925" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-r2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A normal mode is a motion in which both masses oscillate with the same frequency and phase. Putting such a motion into the matrix equation turns it into an eigenvalue problem.

<Step title="The frequencies">

<Formula label="Characteristic equation" tex="\det\begin{pmatrix} 21 - \lambda & -15 \\ -5 & 11.5 - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 - 32.5\,\lambda + 166.5 = 0, \qquad \lambda = \omega^2" cite="reading3" at="p. 3 (det(M⁻¹K − ω²I) = 0)" />

The roots are $\lambda = (32.5 \pm \sqrt{390.25})/2$, that is $\omega_1^2 = 6.3726\ \text{s}^{-2}$ and $\omega_2^2 = 26.1274\ \text{s}^{-2}$, so $\omega_1 = 2.524$ rad/s and $\omega_2 = 5.111$ rad/s.

</Step>

<Step title="The mode shapes">

From the first row, $A_1/A_2 = 15/(21 - \lambda)$:

<Formula label="Amplitude ratios of the two modes" tex="\left.\frac{A_1}{A_2}\right|_{\omega_1} = \frac{15}{21 - 6.3726} = 1.025, \qquad \left.\frac{A_1}{A_2}\right|_{\omega_2} = \frac{15}{21 - 26.1274} = -2.925" cite="mit" at="Problem 2.5(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

In the slow mode both masses move together in the same direction, the lower one slightly more. In the fast mode they move in opposite directions, the lower one about three times as far as the upper one.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The second row must give the same ratio: $-5A_1 + (11.5 - \lambda)A_2 = 0$ means $A_1/A_2 = (11.5 - \lambda)/5$, which is $1.025$ for $\lambda = 6.3726$ and $-2.925$ for $\lambda = 26.1274$, in agreement. The sum $\omega_1^2 + \omega_2^2 = 32.5$ is the trace of the matrix, and the product $166.5$ is its determinant.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="d" points={6}>

The lower mass $m_1$ is displaced 1 cm upward from its equilibrium position, the upper mass $m_2$ is held at its equilibrium position, and both are released from rest at $t = 0$. The motion is a sum of the two modes: $y_1(t) = \alpha_1\cos\omega_1 t + \alpha_2\cos\omega_2 t$ and $y_2(t) = \beta_1\cos\omega_1 t + \beta_2\cos\omega_2 t$. Give the coefficients $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$ and $\beta_1$, in centimetres. (Then $\beta_2 = -\beta_1$: see the solution.)

<Hint>

Released from rest means only cosines appear. In each mode $y_1$ and $y_2$ keep the ratio of part (c): $\alpha_1 = r_1\beta_1$ and $\alpha_2 = r_2\beta_2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The initial conditions are $y_1(0) = \alpha_1 + \alpha_2 = 1$ cm and $y_2(0) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 = 0$. Eliminate $\beta_2 = -\beta_1$ and solve for $\beta_1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a1" label="$\alpha_1 =$" value="0.2596" unit="cm" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-a1" />

<Answer id="p5-a2" label="$\alpha_2 =$" value="0.7404" unit="cm" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-a2" />

<Answer id="p5-b1" label="$\beta_1 =$" value="0.2531" unit="cm" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-b1" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The general motion is a sum of the two normal modes, each with its fixed amplitude ratio and its own frequency. The initial conditions fix the two mode amplitudes.

<Step title="Fix the amplitudes">

Released from rest, each mode is a cosine with no phase. The two modes have the amplitude ratios $r_1 = 1.0255$ and $r_2 = -2.9255$ of part (c), so $\alpha_1 = r_1\beta_1$ and $\alpha_2 = r_2\beta_2$. At $t = 0$:

<Formula label="Initial conditions" tex="y_2(0) = \beta_1 + \beta_2 = 0, \qquad y_1(0) = r_1\beta_1 + r_2\beta_2 = (r_1 - r_2)\,\beta_1 = 1\ \text{cm}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2.5(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

So $\beta_1 = 1/(r_1 - r_2) = 1/3.9510 = 0.2531$ cm, $\beta_2 = -0.2531$ cm, $\alpha_1 = 1.0255 \times 0.2531 = 0.2596$ cm and $\alpha_2 = -2.9255 \times (-0.2531) = 0.7404$ cm.

</Step>

<Step title="The motion">

<Formula label="Motion of the two masses" tex="\begin{aligned} y_1(t) &= 0.2596\cos(2.524\,t) + 0.7404\cos(5.111\,t)\ \text{cm} \\ y_2(t) &= 0.2531\cos(2.524\,t) - 0.2531\cos(5.111\,t)\ \text{cm} \end{aligned}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2.5(d), p. 4 (our own solution), t in seconds" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t = 0$: $y_1 = 0.2596 + 0.7404 = 1$ cm and $y_2 = 0$, as required. The slopes at $t = 0$ are zero, because only cosines appear. Most of the displacement of the lower mass goes into the fast mode, since the fast mode is the one in which the lower mass moves furthest.

</Step>

</Solution>
