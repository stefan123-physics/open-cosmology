The [last lecture](/courses/8-03/dispersion-phase-group-velocity) found that in a dispersive medium a pulse spreads out, and that two nearby waves make an envelope moving at $d\omega/dk$. This one asks two questions: what does the group velocity mean in general, and how can a signal be sent far through a dispersive medium (transcript, pp. 1–3)? Lee warns that the lecture has a lot of mathematics (p. 1).

## Dispersive media

In many media the speed depends on the wavelength: light in glass, deep water, a stiff string (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 1). A square pulse keeps its shape in a non-dispersive medium but spreads in a dispersive one, because its many components travel at different speeds, until a 0 can no longer be told from a 1 (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 1–2). Recall (notes, p. 1):

<Formula label="Phase and group velocity" tex="v_p = \frac{\omega}{k}, \qquad v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk}; \qquad \text{non-dispersive, } \omega = vk: \quad v_p = v = v_g" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

**An example from the notes: light in an ionic crystal** (notes, pp. 1–2). Its dispersion curve starts at a frequency $\omega_b$ when $k = 0$ and bends up towards the line $\omega = uk$. So, as functions of $k$, the phase velocity falls from very large values towards $u$ while the group velocity rises from 0 towards $u$; as functions of $\omega$, both exist only above $\omega_b$. Radiation striking the crystal below $\omega_b$ cannot propagate in it and, with no loss in the crystal, is totally reflected.

## Building a wave from a signal

Shake one end ($x = 0$) of a very long string so that its displacement is $f(t)$ (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 3–4). In a non-dispersive medium the result is simply

<Formula label="Non-dispersive string" tex="\psi(x, t) = f\left(t - \frac{x}{v}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

but in a dispersive one the different frequencies travel at different speeds, so decompose $f(t)$ into harmonic oscillations and follow each one: the **Fourier transform** (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 4–5). A friend from the mathematics department, Lee says, told him the problem was solved long ago (p. 5).

<Formula label="Fourier transform of the signal" tex="f(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}C(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,d\omega" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

$C(\omega)$ is the amplitude of the oscillation at $\omega$. Sines and cosines would do as well; the exponential is just easier, with any $i$ absorbed into $C(\omega)$ (transcript, p. 5). Each frequency becomes a travelling wave with the wave number $k(\omega)$ that the dispersion relation gives it (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 6):

<Formula label="Wave in a dispersive medium" tex="\psi(x, t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}C(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega t + ik(\omega)x}\,d\omega" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

**Check: no dispersion** (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 7). With $k(\omega) = \omega/v$, the exponent is $-i\omega(t - x/v)$, and the integral is $f$ evaluated at $t - x/v$: the result above, "which makes sense". (Lee drops the subscript of $v_p$ on the board and puts it back.)

## Finding the amplitudes

There are infinitely many $C(\omega)$ to find (transcript, p. 7). The tool is the **Dirac delta function** $\delta(x)$: zero except at $x = 0$, infinitely high there, with area 1, like a square pulse made narrower and narrower at fixed area (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 8). Three formulas are needed (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 8–9):

<Formula label="Delta function" tex="\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i(\omega - \omega')t}\,dt = \delta(\omega - \omega'), \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(x)\,dx = 1, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\delta(x - \alpha)f(\alpha)\,d\alpha = f(x)" cite="reading2" at="p. 8" />

(The notes write the first with a factor ½; it is $1/2\pi$, as in the lecture. The notes' derivation below also keeps a factor $1/2\pi$ in its last lines after the delta function has absorbed it; the version here follows the lecture.) Why the first is true, Lee says, would take 10 or 20 minutes: trust the mathematics department (p. 9). Then (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 9–10)

<Formula label="Extracting the amplitudes" tex="\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)\,e^{i\omega t}\,dt = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}C(\omega')\,d\omega'\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{i(\omega - \omega')t}\,dt = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}C(\omega')\,\delta(\omega - \omega')\,d\omega' = C(\omega)" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

This is the continuous version of the "mode picker" used for [Fourier series](/courses/8-03/standing-waves-fourier-series): there the orthogonality of the sines picked out one $A_m$, here the orthogonality of the exponentials picks out one $C(\omega)$ (transcript, p. 10). See also 18.03 on the [delta function](/courses/18-03/impulse-inputs-delta-function).

## AM radio

Sending the signal $f_s(t)$ directly through a dispersive medium fails: its components arrive at different times (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 11). The smart idea, which Lee dates to around the First World War and wide use in the Second, is **amplitude modulation** (p. 11). Send instead (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 11–12)

<Formula label="AM signal" tex="f(t) = f_s(t)\cos\omega_0t" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

where $f_s$ is slow (music, around 1 kHz) and the **carrier** $\cos\omega_0t$ is fast (AM radio, about 0.1 to 30 MHz) (notes, p. 4; Lee says "1.1 to 30 megahertz", transcript, p. 12). For a single tone $f_s = \cos\omega_st$ (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 12):

<Formula label="Product of two cosines" tex="\cos\omega_st\,\cos\omega_0t = \frac12\left[\cos(\omega_0 + \omega_s)t + \cos(\omega_0 - \omega_s)t\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

only two frequencies. In general $C(\omega)$ is sizeable only from $\omega_0 - \omega_s$ to $\omega_0 + \omega_s$, a narrow band since $\omega_s \ll \omega_0$ (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 12–13).

**Why that helps** (notes, pp. 4–5; transcript, pp. 13–14). However wild $\omega(k)$ is, in a narrow band around $\omega_0 = \omega(k_0)$ a Taylor expansion makes it nearly linear, and the group velocity appears by itself:

<Formula label="Dispersion relation near the carrier" tex="\omega = \omega(k) = \omega_0 + (k - k_0)\left.\frac{\partial\omega}{\partial k}\right|_{k = k_0} + \cdots \approx \omega_0 + (k - k_0)\,v_g" cite="reading1" at="pp. 4–5" />

So $\omega = v_gk + a$ with $a = \omega_0 - v_gk_0$, and $k = \omega/v_g + b$ with $b = k_0 - \omega_0/v_g$: linear, though not proportional (notes, p. 5; transcript, p. 14).

**The result** (notes, pp. 5–6; transcript, pp. 15–17). Write $f_s(t) = \int C(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}\,d\omega$ and use the complex carrier $e^{-i\omega_0t}$. Then $f(t) = \int C(\omega)e^{-i(\omega + \omega_0)t}\,d\omega = \int C(\omega - \omega_0)e^{-i\omega t}\,d\omega$, exactly. Send each frequency with its $k(\omega)$; then, approximately, put in $k = \omega/v_g + b$, shift the integration variable back by $\omega_0$ and collect terms, using $\omega_0/v_g + b = k_0$:

<Formula label="AM signal in a dispersive medium" tex="\psi(x, t) \approx \text{Re}\left[\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}C(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega(t - x/v_g)}\,d\omega\;e^{-i\omega_0t}\,e^{i(\omega_0/v_g + b)x}\right] = \text{Re}\left[f_s\left(t - \frac{x}{v_g}\right)e^{-i(\omega_0t - k_0x)}\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Two pieces, each moving rigidly: the **envelope** $f_s$, the signal itself, travelling at the **group velocity**, and the **carrier**, a harmonic wave at the phase velocity $\omega_0/k_0$ (notes, p. 6; transcript, pp. 15 and 17–18). Now the group velocity has a meaning: it is the speed of the signal. The envelope keeps its shape, which is how voices and music reach places thousands of miles away (notes, p. 6; transcript, p. 18). The notes list medium-frequency broadcasts, 300 kHz to 3 MHz, which can travel as "skywave" bent back by the atmosphere, and high frequencies, 3 to 30 MHz (notes, p. 6); Lee mentions radio waves bent by the atmosphere (transcript, p. 18).

**The simulation** (transcript, pp. 18–19). With stiffness $\alpha = 0.1$ a triangular pulse spreads and becomes unrecognisable; an amplitude-modulated signal, computed from many components in a narrow band, travels faster than in the non-dispersive case (blue), since $\alpha > 0$, but its envelope keeps its shape.

<WorkedExample title="A crystal of your own">
<Problem>
Take the curve described for the ionic crystal to be $\omega^2 = \omega_b^2 + u^2k^2$, with $u = 2.0 \times 10^8$ m/s and $\omega_b = 1.0 \times 10^{13}$ rad/s (this formula is not from MIT, whose notes give only a sketch of the curve, and the numbers are only an illustration). For $k = 1.0 \times 10^5$ rad/m find (a) $\omega$, (b) $v_p$ and $v_g$, and check that $v_pv_g = u^2$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The frequency">
$uk = 2.0 \times 10^{13}$ rad/s, so $\omega = \sqrt{1.0 + 4.0} \times 10^{13} \approx 2.24 \times 10^{13}$ rad/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The velocities">
$v_p = \omega/k \approx 2.24 \times 10^8$ m/s, above $u$. Differentiating, $2\omega\,d\omega = 2u^2k\,dk$, so $v_g = u^2k/\omega = (4.0 \times 10^{16} \times 1.0 \times 10^5)/(2.24 \times 10^{13}) \approx 1.79 \times 10^8$ m/s, below $u$. Their product is $u^2 = 4.0 \times 10^{16}$ m²/s², as the two curves in the notes suggest: one falls towards $u$, the other rises towards it.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Fourier transform: $f(t) = \int C(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}d\omega$ with $C(\omega) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int f(t)e^{i\omega t}dt$, from $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int e^{i(\omega - \omega')t}dt = \delta(\omega - \omega')$.
- A signal shaken into a medium becomes $\psi = \int C(\omega)e^{-i\omega t + ik(\omega)x}d\omega$; without dispersion, $f(t - x/v)$.
- AM radio sends $f_s(t)\cos\omega_0t$: a narrow band around $\omega_0$, where $\omega(k)$ is linear, so $\psi \approx \text{Re}[f_s(t - x/v_g)e^{-i(\omega_0t - k_0x)}]$: the signal travels undistorted at the group velocity.
