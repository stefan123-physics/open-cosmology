Light, a wave, also comes in quanta: in the photoelectric effect, explained by Einstein, electrons are knocked out only if the frequency is high enough, with maximum kinetic energy hν − φ. Electrons, particles, also behave as waves: through two slits they arrive one hit at a time yet build up an interference pattern, I₁₂ = |ψ₁ + ψ₂|² = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁I₂) cos δ, rather than I₁ + I₂. Second, gravitational waves: ripples in spacetime, predicted by general relativity, that stretch space one way and squeeze it the other. The last full lecture of the course recalls [diffraction](/courses/8-03/diffraction-resolution), a laser spot on the Moon "as large as the whole Missouri state", and the multi-slit pattern under its diffraction envelope (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 1). So far, two kinds of waves: waves of matter, like water waves and the Bell Labs machine of coupled oscillators, and waves of a vector field, light, which needs no matter at all. Together they describe nature very well. Two more remain: **probability waves** and **gravitational waves** (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2).

## Particles that are waves, waves that are particles

**Light as particles** (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 2–3). As Lee tells it, Hertz found that electrons are knocked out of a material more easily by high-frequency light than by low-frequency light, although a wave's intensity depends on its amplitude, not its frequency. Einstein explained this **photoelectric effect** with light in small quanta, now called photons, each with energy $E = h\nu$: the fastest electrons have kinetic energy $K_{\max} = h\nu - \phi$, where $\phi$ is the energy needed to free an electron, so below a threshold frequency nothing comes out (transcript, p. 3; Lee says "h bar" for $h$). It earned Einstein the Nobel Prize of 1921.

**Electrons as waves** (notes, pp. 1–2; transcript, pp. 4–5). Bullets fired through two slits pile up as $P_{12} = P_1 + P_2$. Electrons, [one at a time](/courses/8-03/phased-radar-electron-interference), do something else. With one slit open they give $I_1 = |\psi_1|^2$ or $I_2 = |\psi_2|^2$; with both open, and nobody checking which slit was used,

<Formula label="Electrons through two slits" tex="I_{12} = |\psi_1 + \psi_2|^2 = I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\delta" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

an interference pattern, with $\delta$ from the path difference. Each electron arrives like a particle, a single hit, yet interferes with itself like a wave. In reality it is neither (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 5).

## Watching the electrons

A sequence of thought experiments (notes, pp. 2–3; transcript, pp. 5–8):

1. **Shine a light at the slits.** Light scattered by a passing electron shows which slit it used. The pattern becomes $I_1 + I_2$, with no interference: the electrons behave like bullets, "disturbed".
2. **Dim the light** (fewer photons). Some electrons pass unseen: the result is a mixture of the two patterns, approaching full interference as the light gets very dim.
3. **Lower each photon's energy instead**, $E = h\nu = hc/\lambda$, keeping many photons but with a longer wavelength. Surprisingly, the interference returns: once $\lambda$ is longer than the distance between the slits, the scattered light could have come from either slit, and the path is unknown.

Conclusion: it is not possible to find the electron's position without disturbing it (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 8). This is **Heisenberg's uncertainty principle**, met earlier as a [property of waves](/courses/8-03/uncertainty-principle-2d-waves), $\Delta x\,\Delta p \geq \hbar/2$ (notes, p. 3).

**A single slit** (notes, pp. 3–4; transcript, pp. 8–9). Electrons through a slit of width $D$ make a diffraction pattern. The slit fixes $x$ to within $D$, and the spread of the pattern is a spread in $p_x$. A narrow slit, small $\Delta x$, gives a wide pattern, large $\Delta p_x$; a wide slit, a narrow pattern. In the limit (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 9)

<Formula label="Infinitely wide slit" tex="C(k_x) \propto \int_{-D/2}^{D/2}e^{-ik_xx}\,dx, \qquad D \to \infty \quad\Longrightarrow\quad C(k_x) \to \delta\text{ function}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

no knowledge of $x$ at all, and $k_x$, and so the sideways momentum $\hbar k_x$, exactly zero.

**Only probabilities** (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 9–11). The electron is described by a **wave function** $\psi$, and the probability of finding it is $P \propto |\psi|^2$, "one of the most crazy results in physics". Quantum mechanics predicts the evolution of $\psi$ exactly, but only the odds of each outcome. Could each electron have secretly decided its path in advance, a **hidden variable**? Not according to these experiments: whether the pattern appears depends on what we do at the slits after the electron has left the source (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 10). Whoever found a way around this, Lee says, would overturn quantum mechanics (p. 10). So a new kind of wave: a **probability wave**.

## A particle in a box

A particle confined between walls of infinite potential energy at $x = 0$ and $x = L$ (notes, p. 5; transcript, pp. 11–12). The walls force $\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0$, the boundary conditions of a [string fixed at both ends](/courses/8-03/standing-waves-fourier-series), so the normal modes are

<Formula label="Normal modes in a box" tex="\psi_m(x) = A_m\sin(k_mx), \quad k_m = \frac{m\pi}{L}; \qquad \psi_m(x, t) = A_m\sin(k_mx)\,e^{-i\omega_mt}, \quad m = 1, 2, 3, \ldots" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

The probability $|\psi_m|^2 \propto \sin^2(k_mx)$: for $m = 1$ the particle is most likely in the middle, for $m = 2$ never exactly there (transcript, pp. 11–12).

**The missing wave equation** (notes, pp. 5–6; transcript, pp. 12–13). Without a dispersion relation, $\omega_m$ is unknown. The wave equation of quantum mechanics is **Schrödinger's equation**, which, as Feynman remarked, cannot be derived from anything known before; it came out of Schrödinger's mind, and it works:

<Formula label="Schrödinger's equation" tex="i\hbar\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(x, t) = \left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} + V(x, t)\right]\psi(x, t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Inside the box $V = 0$; putting in $\psi_m$ (notes, p. 6; transcript, p. 13; $m$ in the equation is the particle's mass, not the mode number):

<Formula label="Dispersion relation of a free particle" tex="\hbar\omega_m\psi_m = \frac{\hbar^2k_m^2}{2m}\psi_m, \qquad \omega = \frac{\hbar k^2}{2m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

With **de Broglie's** matter waves, $p = \hbar k$, the [group velocity](/courses/8-03/dispersion-phase-group-velocity) is (notes, p. 6; transcript, p. 13)

<Formula label="Group velocity of a matter wave" tex="v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{\hbar k}{m} = \frac{p}{m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

the classical velocity. A particle is a **wave packet**, a superposition of many wavelengths, and it travels at the group velocity, just like the envelope of an [AM signal](/courses/8-03/fourier-transform-am-radio): no contradiction between the classical and the wave pictures (notes, p. 6; transcript, pp. 13–14).

**Living with probability** (transcript, pp. 14–15). The Standard Model, whose Lagrangian describes everything except gravity, is a quantum field theory, so particle physicists cannot predict the next collision either: they collide particles again and again until something interesting appears, such as a Higgs boson decaying into two Z bosons at the Large Hadron Collider. Lee's own research collides lead nuclei to make **quark-gluon plasma**, the matter that filled the universe about a microsecond after the Big Bang.

## Gravitational waves

General relativity predicts that moving masses send out ripples in spacetime, **gravitational waves**; everyone in the room is making them, Lee says, but far too weakly to detect, and Einstein himself thought detection might be impossible (transcript, pp. 15–16). A wave passing through a ring of particles stretches it in one direction and squeezes it in the perpendicular one, then the reverse (p. 16). A simulation by the LIGO collaboration shows two black holes of about 30 solar masses orbiting, radiating, and merging, after which the waves stop (p. 16).

**LIGO**, the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-wave Observatory, built by MIT, Caltech and many others, uses only what this course has covered (transcript, pp. 16–18). A laser beam is split in two, sent down perpendicular arms 4 km long to mirrors and recombined on a photodetector, set so that the two beams cancel completely. A passing gravitational wave changes the arm lengths, the cancellation is spoiled and light reaches the detector. Two such observatories, at Hanford and Livingston about 3,000 km apart, guard against local disturbances, from earthquakes to graduate students dancing nearby, Lee jokes. On 14 September 2015 both recorded the same signal, shifted slightly in time because of the distance between them, matching the calculated merger of black holes of 29 and 36 solar masses about 1.3 billion light years away; it was announced on 11 February 2016 (transcript, pp. 17–18). A new way to hear the universe; this is not the end of waves and vibrations, the notes conclude, but just the beginning (notes, p. 6; transcript, p. 18).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Hearing the early universe">
Light from the early universe reaches us only from the time the universe became transparent, as the [cosmic microwave background](/courses/8-286/cmb-spectrum); gravitational waves pass through matter almost freely and could carry news from much earlier. Many models of inflation predict a faint background of primordial gravitational waves, one of the signals cosmologists are searching for (a later development: none had been detected by the mid-2020s). And the quark-gluon plasma that Lee studies in collisions of lead nuclei is the state of matter the hot Big Bang passed through in its first microseconds.
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="An electron in a box of your own">
<Problem>
(a) An electron ($m = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ kg) is confined to a box $L = 1.0$ nm wide. Find the energy $\hbar\omega_1$ of the lowest mode, in joules and electronvolts, and the ratio $\omega_2/\omega_1$. (b) Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron moving at $1.0 \times 10^6$ m/s.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The box">
$k_1 = \pi/L$, so $\hbar\omega_1 = \hbar^2\pi^2/2mL^2 = (1.055 \times 10^{-34})^2\pi^2/(2 \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 10^{-18}) \approx 6.0 \times 10^{-20}$ J, about $0.38$ eV. Since $\omega \propto k^2$ and $k_2 = 2k_1$, $\omega_2/\omega_1 = 4$: unlike a string's, the modes are not equally spaced.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) De Broglie">
$p = mv = 9.11 \times 10^{-25}$ kg·m/s, and $\lambda = 2\pi/k = 2\pi\hbar/p = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}/9.11 \times 10^{-25} \approx 7.3 \times 10^{-10}$ m: comparable to the spacing of atoms, which is why crystals diffract electrons.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Light comes in quanta ($E = h\nu$); electrons interfere like waves, $I_{12} = |\psi_1 + \psi_2|^2$.
- Finding out which slit an electron used destroys the interference; long-wavelength light cannot tell, and the pattern returns. The wave function gives probabilities, $P \propto |\psi|^2$.
- A particle in a box has string-like modes $\sin(m\pi x/L)$; Schrödinger's equation gives $\omega = \hbar k^2/2m$, and with $p = \hbar k$ the group velocity is $p/m$.
- Gravitational waves stretch and squeeze space; LIGO's interferometers detected a black-hole merger in 2015.
