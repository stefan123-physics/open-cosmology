So far the wave equation has described strings, sound and [light](/courses/8-03/electromagnetic-waves), in each case with $\omega/k$ a constant. This lecture asks what happens when it is not, and why that matters for sending information (transcript, p. 1).

## Sending information

The notes recall Maxwell's equations in vacuum and the resulting wave equations, $\nabla^2\vec E = \frac{1}{c^2}\,\partial^2\vec E/\partial t^2$ and the same for $\vec B$, and a progressing wave of any shape, such as $\vec E = E_0\,\hat y\,f(z - vt)$, also solves them (notes, p. 1). A harmonic wave fills all of space and goes on forever, so the receiver cannot tell when a signal was sent: it carries no useful information (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 1–2). What works is a train of **pulses**, chunks of localised energy: a square pulse for 1, nothing for 0, and so on (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2).

On an ideal string, putting $\sin(kx - \omega t)$ into the wave equation gives (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 2–3)

<Formula label="Ideal string" tex="\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} = v^2\,\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}, \qquad \frac{\omega}{k} = v = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho_L}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

independent of $k$. A square pulse is a [Fourier sum](/courses/8-03/standing-waves-fourier-series) of infinitely many harmonic waves; here they all travel at $v$, so after a time $t$ the pulse has moved $vt$ with its shape unchanged. Such a medium is **non-dispersive**, and this is why $\omega(k)$ has been called a **dispersion relation** (notes, pp. 1–2; transcript, p. 3).

## A stiff string

A piano string resists bending even without tension: **stiffness**, a restoring effect separate from the tension (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 4). Will it make waves faster, slower or leave them alone? About five students vote faster, one the same, and the rest wait for the answer (p. 4). A simple model adds a fourth-derivative term (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 4):

<Formula label="Stiff-string wave equation" tex="\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial t^2} = v^2\left[\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} - \alpha\,\frac{\partial^4\psi}{\partial x^4}\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

with $\alpha > 0$. (An odd derivative such as $\partial^3\psi/\partial x^3$ would not be symmetric between bending one way and the other, Lee explains when asked later; the fourth-derivative term also appears as the next correction for the chain of coupled oscillators; p. 9.) Putting in $A\cos(kx - \omega t)$ (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 4–6; Lee's first attempt has sign errors, which students correct):

<Formula label="Dispersion relation of the stiff string" tex="\omega^2 = v^2(k^2 + \alpha k^4) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\omega}{k} = v\sqrt{1 + \alpha k^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

No longer a constant: the curve of $\omega$ against $k$ starts along the line $\omega = vk$ and bends upward (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 6). Large $k$ means a short wavelength, a string sharply bent, where tension and stiffness both pull it back: more restoring force, a higher speed (transcript, pp. 6–7). The voters for "faster" were right.

**Dispersion** (notes, p. 2; transcript, pp. 7–8). The components of a pulse now travel at different speeds $v_p = \omega(k)/k$, so the pulse spreads and loses its shape. In a demonstration, a sharp pulse sent along a real wave apparatus becomes wider and wider on each pass, leaving small wiggles behind it, until it disappears (transcript, pp. 7–8). A simulation then computes the Fourier components of a pulse and moves each at its own speed: a triangular pulse spreads with $\alpha = 0.02$ and spreads sooner and more with $\alpha = 0.2$, and a pulse shaped like the letters MIT soon becomes unrecognisable, ending up like a smooth bump (pp. 8–10). For sending signals, dispersion is "an enemy" (p. 9). In a material, it comes up in a partly inaudible exchange, molecules can likewise change the speed of different wavelengths differently (p. 9).

## Two waves: carrier and envelope

Infinitely many components are hard to follow; take two (notes, p. 2; transcript, p. 10):

<Formula label="Two travelling waves" tex="\psi_1(x, t) = A\sin(k_1x - \omega_1t), \quad v_1 = \frac{\omega_1}{k_1}; \qquad \psi_2(x, t) = A\sin(k_2x - \omega_2t), \quad v_2 = \frac{\omega_2}{k_2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

With $\sin A + \sin B = 2\sin\frac{A + B}{2}\cos\frac{A - B}{2}$, their sum is exactly (transcript, p. 11)

<Formula label="Sum of two waves" tex="\psi_1 + \psi_2 = 2A\sin\left(\frac{k_1 + k_2}{2}x - \frac{\omega_1 + \omega_2}{2}t\right)\cos\left(\frac{k_1 - k_2}{2}x - \frac{\omega_1 - \omega_2}{2}t\right)" cite="reading2" at="p. 11" />

(The notes write $\frac{A + B}{2}$, and sums, in the cosine as well; it is the difference, as in the lecture.) When $k_1 \approx k_2 \approx k$, and so $\omega_1 \approx \omega_2 \approx \omega$, the cosine varies slowly in space: it is the **envelope**. The sine, with the average wave number, is the fast **carrier**: the [beats](/courses/8-03/coupled-pendulums-beats) of Lecture 5, now in space as well as time (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 10–12). If $k_1$ and $k_2$ are far apart, a student notes, the sum still has the same form, but carrier and envelope are no longer easy to tell apart (p. 12).

The carrier moves at the **phase velocity** and the envelope at the **group velocity** (notes, p. 3; transcript, p. 12):

<Formula label="Phase and group velocity" tex="v_p = \frac{\omega}{k}, \qquad v_g = \frac{\omega_1 - \omega_2}{k_1 - k_2} \approx \frac{d\omega}{dk}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

**Reading them off the graph** of $\omega$ against $k$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 12–16): $v_p$ is the slope of the line from the origin to the point, $v_g$ the slope of the tangent there.

- **Non-dispersive** ($\omega \propto k$): the two lines coincide, $v_p = v_g$, and the whole pattern moves rigidly (pp. 12–13).
- **Curving up** (stiff string, $\alpha > 0$): the tangent is steeper, $v_g > v_p$: the envelope overtakes the carriers (pp. 13–14).
- **Curving down** (as for some strange medium, a plasma or a new material; written as $\alpha < 0$ in the lecture): $v_g < v_p$, the carriers run forward through the envelope (p. 14).
- **At a maximum** of $\omega(k)$: $v_g = 0$, the envelope stands still while the carriers move (pp. 14–15).
- **Beyond it**: $v_p > 0$ but $v_g < 0$: the carriers go forward and the envelope goes backwards, "doing the moonwalk" like Michael Jackson (p. 15).

A simulation shows the four cases side by side (pp. 15–16).

## A dispersive string between two walls

For a stiff string fixed at $x = 0$ and $x = L$ the normal modes are, as before (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 16),

<Formula label="Bounded stiff string" tex="\psi(x, t) = \sum_m A_m\sin(k_mx + \alpha_m)\sin(\omega_mt + \beta_m), \qquad k_m = \frac{m\pi}{L}, \quad \alpha_m = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

(here $\alpha_m$ is a phase, not the stiffness). Two lessons (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 16–17):

1. The boundary conditions set $k_m$ and the mode shapes; they do not depend on the dispersion relation.
2. The frequencies $\omega_m = \omega(k_m)$ do.

For an ideal string the points $(k_m, \omega_m)$ lie on a straight line, equally spaced, so every $\omega_m$ is a multiple of $\omega_1$ and any initial shape returns after $2\pi/\omega_1$. With dispersion the $\omega_m$ are not equally spaced, and the shape returns only after a common multiple of all the periods, if ever: for two modes, the least common multiple of $2\pi/\omega_1$ and $2\pi/\omega_2$ (notes, pp. 4–5; transcript, pp. 17–18; the notes' condition "$\omega_2 \neq \omega_1$" means $\omega_2 \neq 2\omega_1$, and the transcript's "is now the constant" means not constant). (Lee first says the spacing of the $k_m$ is $\pi/2$ and corrects it to $\pi/L$; p. 17.)

## Water waves and faster-than-light phases

Water waves, Lee recalls Feynman saying, are the easiest waves to see and the worst possible example, with every complication waves can have; the good news is that they are in the problem set (transcript, p. 18).

They also show that a phase velocity carries no information (pp. 18–19). A straight wave approaches a beach at a slant. The point where a crest meets the shoreline runs along the beach faster than the wave itself, and the more nearly parallel the crests are to the beach, the faster it runs: as the angle goes to zero, it goes to infinity, faster than light. Yet no water moves along the beach that fast; nothing is sent from one point to the other. A phase velocity can be as large as you like without sending anything faster than light.

<WorkedExample title="A stiff string of your own">
<Problem>
A stiff string has $v = 100$ m/s and $\alpha = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}$ m². (a) Find $v_p$ and $v_g$ for $k = 20$ rad/m. (b) The string is $1.0$ m long and fixed at both ends. Find $\omega_{10}/\omega_1$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Phase and group velocity">
$\alpha k^2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-4} \times 400 = 0.040$, so $v_p = 100\sqrt{1.040} \approx 102.0$ m/s. From $\omega = v\sqrt{k^2 + \alpha k^4}$, $v_g = d\omega/dk = v(1 + 2\alpha k^2)/\sqrt{1 + \alpha k^2} = 100 \times 1.080/1.0198 \approx 105.9$ m/s. The envelope is faster than the carrier, as the upward-curving graph says.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The modes">
$k_m = m\pi/L$, so $\omega_m = vk_m\sqrt{1 + \alpha k_m^2}$. For $m = 1$, $\alpha k_1^2 = 0.000987$; for $m = 10$, $\alpha k_{10}^2 = 0.0987$. Then $\omega_{10}/\omega_1 = 10\sqrt{1.0987/1.000987} \approx 10.48$, not 10: the overtones are sharp, which is why piano tuners stretch the octaves (a remark not in the lecture).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Information needs pulses; a pulse is many harmonic waves. If $\omega/k$ is the same for all $k$ (non-dispersive), the pulse keeps its shape.
- A stiff string has $\omega^2 = v^2(k^2 + \alpha k^4)$: short waves travel faster and pulses spread (dispersion).
- Two nearby waves make a carrier at $v_p = \omega/k$ inside an envelope at $v_g = d\omega/dk$; on the $\omega$–$k$ graph, the slope from the origin and the tangent slope. $v_g$ can exceed $v_p$, vanish or be negative.
- Between walls, dispersion leaves the mode shapes alone but makes the frequencies unequally spaced.
- Phase velocities can exceed $c$ without carrying information.
