The [last lecture](/courses/8-03/phased-radar-electron-interference) went from two sources to $N$. This one takes infinitely many, the sources filling a wide slit, and asks how sharp a laser beam, a phone screen or a telescope image can be (notes, p. 1; transcript, pp. 1–2).

## Infinitely many sources

A plane wave meets a slit of width $D$; by Huygens's principle every point in the opening is a source of spherical waves, and on a distant screen they all interfere (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2). This is **diffraction**, though it is the same physics as interference: Feynman remarked that nobody has ever defined the difference satisfactorily, and it is a matter of usage; the notes add that "interference" is used for a few sources and "diffraction" for many (notes, p. 1; transcript, p. 2).

**Method I** (notes, p. 2). Start from $N$ slits a distance $d$ apart, $\langle I\rangle \propto [\sin(N\delta/2)/\sin(\delta/2)]^2$ with $\delta = 2\pi d\sin\theta/\lambda$, and fill the slit: $d \to 0$, $N \to \infty$, $Nd = D$. Then $\delta \to 0$, $N\delta = 2\pi D\sin\theta/\lambda$ stays finite and $\sin(\delta/2) \approx \delta/2$, so, with the amplitude of each tiny source taken proportional to $1/N$,

<Formula label="Single-slit diffraction" tex="\beta = \frac{N\delta}{2} = \frac{\pi D\sin\theta}{\lambda}, \qquad \langle I\rangle \propto \left[\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right]^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

(The notes say each source's intensity is proportional to $N^{-2}$, which is the same thing.)

**Method II**, from Georgi's book (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 2–5). Add up the spherical waves from every point of the opening with a **two-dimensional Fourier transform**:

<Formula label="Aperture as a Fourier transform" tex="C(k_x, k_y) = \frac{1}{4\pi^2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy\,f(x, y)\,e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r(x, y)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Here $f(x, y)$ maps the sources, 1 in the opening and 0 on the wall; $e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r}$ is the wave from each point (the common $\omega t$ factored out); and $C(k_x, k_y)$ is proportional to the total field travelling in the direction $(k_x, k_y)$ (transcript, pp. 3–4). For a slit, $f = 1$ for $-D/2 \leq x \leq D/2$ and every $y$, zero otherwise. The $y$ integral is a [delta function](/courses/8-03/fourier-transform-am-radio), and the $x$ integral is elementary (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 5–7):

<Formula label="Field of a single slit" tex="C(k_x, k_y) = \frac{1}{4\pi^2}\int_{-D/2}^{D/2}e^{-ik_xx}\,dx\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-ik_yy}\,dy = \delta(k_y)\,\frac{1}{2\pi}\,\frac{2\sin(k_xD/2)}{k_x}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The field is zero unless $k_y = 0$: the slit is infinitely long in $y$, so along $y$ the sum of the wavelets is still a plane wavefront (transcript, pp. 7–8). For a distant screen, $\sin\theta = x/r = k_x/k = k_x\lambda/2\pi$, so $k_x = 2\pi\sin\theta/\lambda$, and $I \propto |C|^2 \propto \sin^2(k_xD/2)/k_x^2$ is again $(\sin\beta/\beta)^2$ (notes, p. 3; transcript, pp. 8–9).

## The single-slit pattern

The intensity has a central maximum $I_0$, zeros at $\sin\theta = \pm\lambda/D, \pm2\lambda/D, \ldots$, where all the sources across the slit cancel exactly, "a lot of work", and weaker maxima in between under an envelope falling as $1/\beta^2$ (notes, p. 4; transcript, pp. 9–10). The central maximum is twice as wide as the side ones (transcript, p. 13). Three observations (notes, pp. 4–5; transcript, pp. 10–11):

1. **A wider slit gives a narrower pattern.** Bullets through a wider gap spread wider; waves do the opposite.
2. The spacing of the zeros is proportional to $\lambda$: red light spreads more than blue, so the pattern can measure a wavelength.
3. The side maxima fade as $1/\beta^2$, quickly for a wide slit, slowly for a narrow one.

**Measuring a laser's wavelength** (transcript, pp. 11–13). A red laser passes a slit of $D = 0.16$ mm; with a volunteer, Lee measures the distance to the screen (7.5 m, then used as 7.6 m) and the gap between the two first minima, 7 cm, so the first minimum is $3.5$ cm from the centre. With $\sin\theta \approx 3.5\text{ cm}/r$:

<Formula label="Wavelength from the first minimum" tex="\lambda = D\sin\theta = \frac{D\,d}{r} = \frac{0.16 \times 10^{-3} \times 3.5 \times 10^{-2}}{7.6}\ \text{m} \approx 7.37 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 12–13" />

737 nm (a student works out the number on a smartphone), within the range of red light, about 620 to 750 nm, as Lee notes. (The notes record $r = 7.7$ m and 727 nm, notes, p. 4; here $d$ is the distance on the screen, not a slit spacing.)

## Real double slits

The double-slit dots of the last lecture faded away from the centre because real slits have a width (notes, p. 5; transcript, pp. 14–15). Each slit already sends out the single-slit pattern, so $N$ slits of width $D$, a distance $d$ apart, give the interference pattern **modulated** by the diffraction pattern (notes, pp. 5–6; transcript, p. 14):

<Formula label="Slits of finite width" tex="I = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2\left(\frac{\sin\frac{N\delta}{2}}{\sin\frac{\delta}{2}}\right)^2, \qquad \beta = \frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\sin\theta, \qquad \delta = kd\sin\theta = \frac{2\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

The fine fringes, at $\sin\theta = n\lambda/d$, come from the interference; the broad envelope, with zeros at $\sin\theta = n\lambda/D$, from diffraction (notes, p. 5).

## Resolution

To see fine detail, such as the "Soap Bubble" nebula, or a zebra rather than a blur that might be a lion, two nearby points must give separable peaks (transcript, p. 15). Lee compares his own photograph of the Ring Nebula, M57, about 2,500 light years away, taken with a 40 cm telescope, with the Hubble Space Telescope's, from a 240 cm mirror above the atmosphere (transcript, pp. 15–16).

**A circular aperture** of diameter $D$ gives a pattern of rings, by symmetry (notes, p. 6; transcript, pp. 16–17):

<Formula label="Circular aperture" tex="I(\theta) = I_0\left(\frac{J_1(\beta)}{\beta}\right)^2, \qquad J_1(x) = 0 \ \text{at}\ x \approx 3.83 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \beta = 3.83 = \frac{\pi D}{\lambda}\sin\theta, \quad \sin\theta \approx 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

with $J_1$ a Bessel function of the first kind. (Normalized to $I_0$ at the centre, the formula needs a factor 2, $(2J_1(\beta)/\beta)^2$; the zeros are unchanged.) Two points can be told apart when their peaks are separated by at least about this angle, the **resolution** $\Delta\theta \approx 1.22\lambda/D$ (notes, p. 6; transcript, pp. 16–17).

**The eye and the phone** (notes, pp. 6–7; transcript, pp. 17–18). The pupil is 2 to 4 mm across in bright light and 3 to 8 mm in dim light; with $D \approx 5$ mm and $\lambda \approx 500$ nm, the eye resolves about $1.22 \times (500 \times 10^{-9})/(5 \times 10^{-3}) \approx 1.22 \times 10^{-4}$. A phone screen with 401 pixels per inch has pixels $2.54\text{ cm}/401 \approx 6.3 \times 10^{-3}$ cm apart; held 20 cm away they are $3 \times 10^{-4}$ apart in angle, more than the limit, so the eye can still resolve them. (The notes divide by 10 cm in the formula and draw 20 cm.) A "jPhone" with 40,000 ppi, Lee jokes, would be pointless; around 2,000 ppi is roughly the useful limit, he says. With the numbers above, the limit is about 1,040 ppi at 20 cm and about 2,080 ppi at 10 cm; the notes put it as 2,000 pixels in 6 cm.

**A laser to the Moon** (notes, p. 7; transcript, pp. 18–19). A laser pointer is not a perfect pencil beam: its own aperture diffracts it. With $\lambda = 500$ nm and a $D = 1$ mm beam, $\theta \approx 1.22\lambda/D \approx 6 \times 10^{-4}$; over the $4 \times 10^8$ m to the Moon the central spot has a radius of about 240 km. (In class Lee puts it at 200 km, "the size of Missouri".) The *Enterprise*'s laser weapons would need much wider beams.

<WorkedExample title="Slits and stars of your own">
<Problem>
(a) Green light, $\lambda = 532$ nm, passes a slit $0.10$ mm wide onto a screen $3.0$ m away. How wide is the central bright band? (b) Two car headlights are $1.5$ m apart. Using the eye's limit of $1.22 \times 10^{-4}$, from how far away can they just be seen as two? (c) A telescope of diameter $2.4$ m observes at $500$ nm. What is its resolution?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The slit">
First zeros at $\sin\theta = \lambda/D = 532 \times 10^{-9}/1.0 \times 10^{-4} = 5.32 \times 10^{-3}$, that is $3.0 \times 5.32 \times 10^{-3} \approx 1.6$ cm from the centre on each side: a central band about 3.2 cm wide.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Headlights">
They merge when $1.5\text{ m}/L < 1.22 \times 10^{-4}$, so they can be told apart out to $L \approx 1.5/1.22 \times 10^{-4} \approx 1.2 \times 10^4$ m, about 12 km (in practice the atmosphere and the eye's own imperfections do worse).
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The telescope">
$1.22 \times 500 \times 10^{-9}/2.4 \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-7}$ rad, about 500 times finer than the eye.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="How sharp can a map of the sky be?">
The same limit, $\theta \approx 1.22\lambda/D$, sets how finely any telescope can map the universe, from galaxies to the [cosmic microwave background](/courses/8-286/cmb-spectrum): to see smaller details at a given wavelength, the mirror or antenna must be larger.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Diffraction is interference of infinitely many sources. A slit of width $D$: $I = I_0(\sin\beta/\beta)^2$, $\beta = \pi D\sin\theta/\lambda$, zeros at $\sin\theta = m\lambda/D$; wider slits give narrower patterns.
- The field is the Fourier transform of the aperture shape, $C(k_x, k_y) = \frac{1}{4\pi^2}\int\int fe^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec r}dx\,dy$.
- Real multi-slit patterns are interference fringes under a diffraction envelope.
- A circular aperture resolves angles down to $\sin\theta \approx 1.22\lambda/D$: about $10^{-4}$ for the eye, which sets useful screen resolutions and the spread of laser beams.
