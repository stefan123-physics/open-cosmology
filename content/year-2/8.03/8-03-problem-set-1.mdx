## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A mass hung from a spring" points={20} mit="Problem 1.1">

A block of mass $m$ hangs from an ideal spring of spring constant $k$. The top of the spring is fixed, and the block moves only up and down, in the earth's gravitational field. MIT asks you to show that the angular frequency of these vertical oscillations is the same as in the lesson, where the same spring pulls a block along a frictionless table:

<Formula label="Angular frequency of the spring (the result to recover)" tex="\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.1, p. 1 (the sheet cites the textbook for it; see the lesson, Eq. 3)" />

and to explain why gravity has no effect on this angular frequency. Measure the position $y$ of the block downward from the place where the spring has its natural length.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

This part is our own addition, a first step for the rest of the problem. How far below the natural-length position does the block hang at rest, in terms of $m$, $g$ and $k$?

<Hint>

At rest the block does not accelerate, so the total force on it is zero. Which two forces act on it, and which way does each point?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The spring force has the size $k$ times its stretch. Set it equal to the weight.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$y_0 =$" expr="m*g/k"
  vars={{ m: [0.1, 2], g: [9, 10], k: [10, 100] }}
  names={{ m: "m", g: "g", k: "k" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "k/(m*g)", hint: "Check the units: a stretch is a length, and this is not. The spring force is k times the stretch, so the stretch is the force divided by k." },
    { expr: "2*m*g/k", hint: "That is the lowest point of a block released from the natural-length position, not the place where it can rest. At rest the forces balance." },
    { expr: "m*g*k", hint: "Check the units: a stretch is a length. The spring force is k times the stretch, so the stretch is the force divided by k." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At rest the two forces on the block cancel: the weight pulls down and the stretched spring pulls up.

<Step title="Balance of forces">

With $y$ measured downward, the weight is $+mg$ and the spring force is $-ky$ (a stretch $y > 0$ pulls the block up). At rest the sum is zero:

<Formula label="Hanging at rest" tex="m g - k\,y_0 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad y_0 = \frac{m g}{k}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.1(a) (our own addition and solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Newtons divided by newtons per metre are metres. A stiffer spring (larger $k$) stretches less, and a heavier block stretches it more. With our own numbers, $m = 0.50$ kg and $k = 20$ N/m, the block hangs $0.50 \times 9.8/20 = 0.245$ m below the natural length.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Show that the vertical oscillations have the angular frequency $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, with the same $\omega$ as for the horizontal spring. (No box: open the solution and mark yourself.)

<Hint>

Write Newton's second law for the block at the position $y$, with both forces. Then measure the position from the hanging position of part (a) instead: $u = y - y_0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Substitute $y = u + y_0$ into the equation of motion. A constant that is left over cancels against the weight.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The weight is a constant force. A constant force only moves the place where the forces balance. Measured from that place, the equation of motion is the one of the lesson.

<Step title="The equation of motion in $y$">

Newton's second law for the block, with the weight and the spring force:

<Formula label="Vertical equation of motion" tex="m\ddot y = m g - k\,y" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.1(b) (our own solution)" />

This is not yet the equation of the lesson, because of the constant $mg$.

</Step>

<Step title="Measure from the hanging position">

Put $y = u + y_0$ with $y_0 = mg/k$ from part (a). The constant $y_0$ has no time derivative, so $\ddot y = \ddot u$, and

<Formula label="Equation of motion about the hanging position" tex="m\ddot u = m g - k\,u - k\,y_0 = -k\,u" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.1(b) (our own solution)" />

because $k\,y_0 = mg$ cancels the weight. This is the equation $m\ddot x = -kx$ of the lesson, with $u$ in place of $x$:

<Formula label="The same equation as for the horizontal spring" tex="\ddot u = -\omega^2 u, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, eq. 3" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\sqrt{k/m}$ is $\sqrt{(\text{N/m})/\text{kg}} = 1/\text{s}$. The solution is the general one of the lesson, $u(t) = a\cos\omega t + b\sin\omega t$ (notes, p. 2, eq. 4), now about the hanging position. With $m = 0.50$ kg and $k = 20$ N/m, $\omega = \sqrt{20/0.50} = 6.32$ rad/s, the same as for a horizontal block on that spring.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Explain why gravity has no effect on the angular frequency. (No box: open the solution and mark yourself.)

<Hint>

Compare the equation for $u$ with the equation for the horizontal block. Where did $g$ go?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Think of the potential energy of the block, spring plus gravity, as a function of $y$. What does a constant force do to its shape?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Gravity adds a force that does not depend on position. That moves the bottom of the potential, and leaves its curvature, and so the frequency, alone.

<Step title="In terms of forces">

In part (b) the weight only entered as a constant, and it was cancelled completely by the extra stretch $y_0$ of the spring. The restoring force on the displacement $u$ from the new equilibrium is $-ku$, whatever $g$ is. The value of $g$ decides where the block hangs, not how fast it swings about that place.

</Step>

<Step title="In terms of energy">

The potential energy of spring and gravity together is $V(y) = \tfrac12 k y^2 - m g y$. Completing the square gives $V = \tfrac12 k\,(y - mg/k)^2$: the same parabola as without gravity, moved by $y_0$ and lowered by a constant. The frequency of small oscillations depends only on the curvature $V''= k$ (the lesson's Taylor argument, notes, pp. 3–4), and the curvature did not change.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The statement is exact here only because the spring is ideal. For a spring that is not ideal, a heavier block stretches it into a region where the curvature differs, and gravity would then change $\omega$. The lesson's condition on the cubic term (notes, p. 4) is what measures that.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Real and complex representations of an oscillation" points={20} mit="Problem 1.2">

An oscillating quantity can be written as a real function, or as the real part of a complex one. In this problem the complex form has the time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$ (MIT's sign; the lesson writes $e^{+i\omega t}$, and the real part is the same cosine either way). Throughout, $\omega > 0$ and $\tau$ is a real constant with the unit of time. In the first three parts a motion is given by a complex representation of its displacement $X$, its velocity $\dot X$ or its acceleration $\ddot X$. Find a **real** representation of all three, in the form

<Formula label="The real form" tex="\eta(t) = c\cos\omega t + d\sin\omega t" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.2, p. 1" />

with $c$ and $d$ real and constant. The real quantity is the real part of the complex one. When you have to integrate, take the integration constants to be zero. The boxes check the three real functions, written out in full: type each as a function of `t`.

In the last three parts a motion is given by a real representation of its displacement, velocity or acceleration. Find the **complex** representation of all three, in the form

<Formula label="The complex form" tex="Q(t) = Q\,e^{-i(\omega t + \phi)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.2, p. 1" />

with $Q$ and $\phi$ real and constant ($\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ and $\delta$ are positive). The real quantity is $\text{Re}\,Q(t) = Q\cos(\omega t + \phi)$. Give each phase in the range $0 \le \phi < 2\pi$, in radians. For these parts the boxes check one of the three quantities in each case, the one that needs differentiating or integrating; the solution gives all three.

The split of the 20 points between the parts is ours.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Given: $X(t) = A\,e^{-i(\omega t - \pi/2)}$, with $A$ real. Find the real displacement $x$, velocity $v$ and acceleration $a$.

<Hint>

Split the exponential: $e^{-i(\omega t - \pi/2)} = e^{i\pi/2}\,e^{-i\omega t}$. What is $e^{i\pi/2}$? Then use $e^{-i\omega t} = \cos\omega t - i\sin\omega t$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The real part of the complex displacement is the real displacement. The velocity and the acceleration are its first and second time derivatives.

</Hint>

  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-a-a" label="$a(t) =$" expr="-A*w^2*sin(w*t)"
  vars={{ A: [0.5, 2], w: [1, 4], t: [0.2, 3] }}
  names={{ w: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "A*w^2*sin(w*t)", hint: "Differentiate twice: sine goes to cosine and cosine goes to minus sine, so the acceleration has the opposite sign to the displacement." },
    { expr: "-A*w*sin(w*t)", hint: "Two derivatives bring two factors of ω." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The real part of the complex displacement is the real displacement. Take it first, then differentiate the real function.

<Step title="Real part of the displacement">

Write $e^{-i(\omega t - \pi/2)} = e^{i\pi/2}e^{-i\omega t}$, with $e^{i\pi/2} = i$ (notes, p. 5, $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$):

<Formula label="Case (a): the complex displacement" tex="X(t) = A\,i\,(\cos\omega t - i\sin\omega t) = A\sin\omega t + i\,A\cos\omega t" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.2(a) (our own solution)" />

The real part is $x(t) = A\sin\omega t$: in the form of the problem, $c = 0$ and $d = A$.

</Step>

<Step title="Velocity and acceleration">

<Formula label="Case (a): real velocity and acceleration" tex="v = \dot x = A\omega\cos\omega t, \qquad a = \ddot x = -A\omega^2\sin\omega t" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.2(a) (our own solution)" />

So for the velocity $c = A\omega$, $d = 0$, and for the acceleration $c = 0$, $d = -A\omega^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The acceleration is $-\omega^2 x$, as it must be for an oscillation of angular frequency $\omega$ (the equation of motion of the lesson). The units are right: $A$ in metres gives $v$ in m/s and $a$ in m/s².

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Given: $\dot X(t) = B\,i\,\omega\tau\,e^{-i(\omega t + \pi/6)}$, with $B$ real, the complex **velocity**. Find the real displacement $x$, velocity $v$ and acceleration $a$.

<Hint>

Combine the factor $i$ with the phase: $i = e^{i\pi/2}$, so $i\,e^{-i\pi/6} = e^{i(\pi/2 - \pi/6)}$. Then take the real part to get the real velocity.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The real displacement is the integral of the real velocity, with no constant term. The acceleration is its derivative.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** First turn the complex velocity into a real one. Then integrate once for the displacement and differentiate once for the acceleration.

<Step title="Real velocity">

Combine $i$ with the phase, $i\,e^{-i\pi/6} = e^{i\pi/2}e^{-i\pi/6} = e^{i\pi/3}$:

<Formula label="Case (b): the complex velocity" tex="\dot X(t) = B\omega\tau\,e^{i\pi/3}e^{-i\omega t} = B\omega\tau\,e^{-i(\omega t - \pi/3)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.2(b) (our own solution)" />

Its real part is the real velocity:

<Formula label="Case (b): real velocity" tex="v(t) = B\omega\tau\cos\!\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{3}\right) = B\omega\tau\left[\tfrac12\cos\omega t + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\sin\omega t\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Displacement and acceleration">

Integrating with zero constant, and differentiating:

<Formula label="Case (b): real displacement and acceleration" tex="x(t) = B\tau\sin\!\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{3}\right), \qquad a(t) = -B\omega^2\tau\sin\!\left(\omega t - \frac{\pi}{3}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.2(b) (our own solution)" />

In the form of the problem, the velocity has $c = \tfrac12 B\omega\tau$ and $d = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}B\omega\tau$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The acceleration is $-\omega^2 x$ again, and $x$ has the unit of $B\tau$. If $B$ is a velocity-like constant with the unit of length per time, then $B\tau$ is a length, as a displacement should be. A second route to the velocity: $\text{Re}[i\,e^{-i\theta}] = \sin\theta$ with $\theta = \omega t + \pi/6$, so $v = B\omega\tau\sin(\omega t + \pi/6)$, which is the same function, because $\sin(\theta) = \cos(\theta - \pi/2)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A complex factor in front of the exponential has to be written as a real part and an imaginary part before the real part of the product is taken. Rationalise $1/(1 + i\omega\tau)$ first.

<Step title="The real acceleration">

Multiplying the top and bottom by the complex conjugate of the denominator:

<Formula label="Case (c): rationalised factor" tex="\frac{1}{1 + i\omega\tau} = \frac{1 - i\omega\tau}{1 + \omega^2\tau^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.2(c) (our own solution)" />

Then, with $e^{-i\omega t} = \cos\omega t - i\sin\omega t$, the real part of $(1 - i\omega\tau)(\cos\omega t - i\sin\omega t)$ is $\cos\omega t - \omega\tau\sin\omega t$:

<Formula label="Case (c): real acceleration" tex="a(t) = \frac{C}{1 + \omega^2\tau^2}\left(\cos\omega t - \omega\tau\sin\omega t\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.2(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Velocity and displacement">

Integrate once, with zero constant, using $\int\cos\omega t\,dt = \sin\omega t/\omega$ and $\int\sin\omega t\,dt = -\cos\omega t/\omega$:

<Formula label="Case (c): real velocity" tex="v(t) = \frac{C}{1 + \omega^2\tau^2}\left(\frac{\sin\omega t}{\omega} + \tau\cos\omega t\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.2(c) (our own solution)" />

And once more:

<Formula label="Case (c): real displacement" tex="x(t) = \frac{C}{1 + \omega^2\tau^2}\left(\frac{\tau\sin\omega t}{\omega} - \frac{\cos\omega t}{\omega^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.2(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiating $x$ twice returns the acceleration. A second route to the acceleration, in polar form: $1/(1 + i\omega\tau) = e^{-i\arctan(\omega\tau)}/\sqrt{1 + \omega^2\tau^2}$, so $a = C\cos(\omega t + \arctan(\omega\tau))/\sqrt{1 + \omega^2\tau^2}$, which expands to the same expression. For $\tau = 0$ the factor is 1, and $a = C\cos\omega t$, $v = (C/\omega)\sin\omega t$, $x = -(C/\omega^2)\cos\omega t$, as expected for a plain cosine.

</Step>

</Solution>

**The idea.** The force near a minimum is $-V''\xi - \tfrac12 V_3\xi^2 - \cdots$. The second term is small compared with the first when $|\xi V_3| \ll V''$ (notes, p. 4; transcript, p. 8). The displacement at which the two are of the same order is the limit.

<Step title="The third derivative">

<Formula label="Third derivative of the potential" tex="V_3(x) = \frac{E_0}{a^4}\left(24x + 24a\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.4(c) (our own solution)" />

At $x = a$ it is $48E_0/a^3$, and at $x = -4a$ it is $-72E_0/a^3$.

</Step>

<Step title="The limits">

<Formula label="Where the cubic term in V would matter" tex="\xi_c = \frac{V''(x_0)}{|V_3(x_0)|}: \qquad \xi_c = \frac{20E_0/a^2}{48E_0/a^3} = \frac{5a}{12}\quad (x = a), \qquad \xi_c = \frac{80E_0/a^2}{72E_0/a^3} = \frac{10a}{9}\quad (x = -4a)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4 (the condition); the numbers are our own solution" />

The oscillation about $x = a$ is harmonic only for amplitudes well below $0.4167a$; about $x = -4a$, well below $1.111a$. "Well below" means, for example, a tenth of $\xi_c$: there the quadratic term of the force, $\tfrac12 V_3\xi^2$, is 5% of the linear one.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The limits are in units of $a$ because $E_0$ cancels: a larger $E_0$ makes both $V''$ and $V_3$ larger in the same proportion. The valley at $x = -4a$ is wider and more symmetric ($|V_3|/V''$ is smaller there), so its harmonic region is larger. The quartic term in $V$ gives a force term $-\tfrac16 V_4\xi^3$ with the constant $V_4 = 24E_0/a^4$; it matters when $\xi^2 \sim 6V''/V_4$, that is $\xi \sim \sqrt5\,a$ at $x = a$, a larger distance than $\xi_c$, so the cubic term is the one that sets the limit.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="The pendulum" points={20} mit="Problem 1.5">

A point mass $m$ hangs from a massless string of length $L$, fixed at its upper end. It moves in a vertical plane, in the gravitational field $g$. Describe its position by the angle $\theta$ from the vertical, positive on the side you choose. In the later parts it is described by the horizontal coordinate $x$, measured from the lowest point, so that $x = L\sin\theta$ ($y$ is vertical).

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the exact equation of motion, using torque about the support, which equals the moment of inertia times the angular acceleration (MIT's route). Type the angular acceleration $\ddot\theta$ as a function of $\theta$.

<Hint>

The moment of inertia of a point mass at the distance $L$ about the support is $mL^2$. Which force has a torque about the support, and what is its lever arm?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Gravity acts at the mass, with the lever arm $L\sin\theta$ about the support. The torque restores the angle to zero. The tension has no torque about the support.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$\ddot\theta =$" expr="-g/L*sin(theta)"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], L: [0.5, 2], theta: [0.3, 2.5] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-g/L*theta", hint: "That is the small-angle form, which is part (b). The exact torque has sin θ in it." },
    { expr: "g/L*sin(theta)", hint: "Check the sign: the torque of gravity turns the pendulum back towards θ = 0, against the displacement." },
    { expr: "-g*sin(theta)", hint: "Check the units: an angular acceleration is in 1/s². Divide the torque mgL sin θ by the moment of inertia mL²." },
    { expr: "-g/L*cos(theta)", hint: "The lever arm of the weight about the support is L sin θ for θ measured from the vertical." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Torque about the fixed point: gravity has one, the tension does not (it passes through the point).

<Step title="Torque and moment of inertia">

The weight $mg$ has a lever arm $L\sin\theta$, and it turns the pendulum back towards the vertical. The moment of inertia of the point mass is $mL^2$:

<Formula label="Pendulum: exact equation of motion" tex="m L^2\,\ddot\theta = -\,m g\,L\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.5(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The mass drops out, so every pendulum of the same length moves in the same way. For small angles $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ gives $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\theta$, the next part. For $\theta = \pi$ (balanced upside down) the torque vanishes, as it must at an equilibrium.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

For small $\theta$, find the approximate equation $\ddot\theta = -\omega^2\theta$. Type the angular frequency $\omega$.

<Hint>

For small $\theta$ (in radians), $\sin\theta \approx \theta$. Compare with $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$ of the lesson.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$\omega =$" expr="sqrt(g/L)"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], L: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "g/L", hint: "That is ω²: the equation reads θ̈ = −ω²θ." },
    { expr: "sqrt(L/g)", hint: "Check the units: the root of L/g is a time, not a rate. A longer pendulum swings more slowly, so L belongs in the denominator." },
    { expr: "sqrt(g*L)", hint: "Check the units: the root of the product gL is a speed, not a rate." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Replace $\sin\theta$ by $\theta$. The equation is then the one of the lesson.

<Step title="The equation">

<Formula label="Pendulum: equation of motion for small angles" tex="\ddot\theta \approx -\frac{g}{L}\,\theta = -\omega^2\theta, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, eq. 3 (the same equation)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\sqrt{g/L}$ is in $\sqrt{(\text{m/s}^2)/\text{m}} = 1/\text{s}$. Longer pendulums are slower, and the mass does not enter. With $L = 1.0$ m and $g = 9.8$ m/s², $\omega = 3.13$ rad/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Justify the approximation, and find the period of the oscillations. Take the approximation $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ to be good while $\sin\theta$ differs from $\theta$ by less than 1% (our choice: MIT leaves the tolerance open). Give the largest angle $\theta_{\max}$ for which this holds, in degrees, and the period $T$.

<Hint>

The relative error of the approximation is $(\theta - \sin\theta)/\theta = 1 - \sin\theta/\theta$. Set it equal to 0.01, and solve it numerically or with the first Taylor correction $\sin\theta \approx \theta - \theta^3/6$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The period is the time of one full oscillation, $T = 2\pi/\omega$ with $\omega$ from part (b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-c-angle" label="$\theta_{\max} =$" value="14.06" unit="degrees" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-c-angle" />

<Answer id="p5-c-period" label="$T =$" expr="2*pi*sqrt(L/g)"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], L: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(L/g)", hint: "That is 1/ω. The period is 2π/ω." },
    { expr: "2*pi*sqrt(g/L)", hint: "That is 2πω. The period is 2π/ω: check the units, a period is a time." },
    { expr: "pi*sqrt(L/g)", hint: "That is half a period (a swing from one side to the other). One full oscillation is 2π/ω." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The error of replacing $\sin\theta$ by $\theta$ grows like $\theta^2/6$. Solve for the angle at which it reaches the chosen tolerance.

<Step title="The range of angles">

<Formula label="Relative error of the small-angle approximation" tex="\frac{\theta - \sin\theta}{\theta} = 1 - \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \approx \frac{\theta^2}{6} = 0.01" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.5(c) (our own solution)" />

The first-order estimate is $\theta = \sqrt{0.06} = 0.245$ rad. Solving $1 - \sin\theta/\theta = 0.01$ numerically gives $\theta_{\max} = 0.2453$ rad $= 14.06°$. So for swings up to about $14°$ on either side, the equation of motion differs from $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\theta$ by less than 1%.

</Step>

<Step title="The period">

<Formula label="Period of the pendulum for small swings" tex="T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, eq. 6 (the cosine has the period $2\pi/\omega$)" />

For small swings the period does not depend on the amplitude: that is what the demonstration of the lesson shows, and what fails at large amplitude (transcript, pp. 9–10).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\sqrt{L/g}$ is in seconds. A pendulum of length 1.0 m has the period $2\pi\sqrt{1.0/9.8} = 2.01$ s. At large amplitude the frequency does depend on the amplitude: the two pendulums in the lecture demonstration drift out of step because of it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d (i)" points={2}>

Find the exact potential energy $V(\theta)$ of the pendulum (gravity only). Choose the zero of potential energy at the lowest point of the swing.

<Hint>

How high above the lowest point is the mass when the string makes the angle $\theta$ with the vertical? The string has length $L$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The height of the support above the mass is $L\cos\theta$, so the mass is higher than the lowest point by $L - L\cos\theta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-d" label="$V(\theta) =$" expr="m*g*L*(1 - cos(theta))"
  vars={{ m: [0.5, 3], g: [9, 10], L: [0.5, 2], theta: [0.3, 2.5] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*g*L*theta^2/2", hint: "That is the small-angle form, which is what part (d)(ii) derives. The exact energy has a cosine." },
    { expr: "-m*g*L*cos(theta)", hint: "That is also a potential energy, but with the zero at the height of the support. With the zero at the lowest point, V = 0 at θ = 0." },
    { expr: "m*g*L*sin(theta)", hint: "The height of the mass above its lowest point is L - L cos θ, not L sin θ (that is its horizontal distance)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Gravity's potential energy is $mg$ times the height.

<Step title="The height">

At the angle $\theta$ the mass is a distance $L\cos\theta$ below the support, and at the lowest point $L$ below it. So it is $L(1 - \cos\theta)$ higher than at the bottom:

<Formula label="Pendulum: exact potential energy" tex="V(\theta) = m g L\,(1 - \cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.5(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$V(0) = 0$, and $V$ rises to its largest value $2mgL$ at $\theta = \pi$ (the mass straight above the support). Differentiating with respect to $\theta$ gives the torque $dV/d\theta = mgL\sin\theta$, which agrees with part (a): $mL^2\ddot\theta = -dV/d\theta$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d (ii)" points={2}>

Show that the Taylor expansion of $V(\theta)$ about $\theta = 0$ leads to the same equation of motion as in part (b). (No box: open the solution and mark yourself.)

<Hint>

Expand $\cos\theta = 1 - \theta^2/2 + \theta^4/24 - \cdots$. Keep the first term that is not constant.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The torque is $-dV/d\theta$, and the equation of motion is $mL^2\ddot\theta = -dV/d\theta$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Near the minimum a smooth potential is a parabola. A parabola in the angle is the potential of a spring (the lesson's Taylor argument).

<Step title="The expansion">

Use the Taylor series of the cosine, and $V(0) = 0$, $V'(0) = 0$:

<Formula label="Pendulum: Taylor expansion of the potential" tex="V(\theta) = m g L\left(\frac{\theta^2}{2} - \frac{\theta^4}{24} + \cdots\right) \approx \tfrac12\,(m g L)\,\theta^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 3, eq. 9 (here $V''(0) = mgL$)" />

The coefficient of $\theta^2/2$ is $V''(0) = mgL$.

</Step>

<Step title="Equation of motion">

With $mL^2\ddot\theta = -dV/d\theta \approx -mgL\,\theta$ the equation is $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\theta$, the same as part (b).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The next term, $-\theta^4/24$, is the first correction. Its ratio to the leading term is $\theta^2/12$, so the approximation is good for small $\theta$ in the same sense as in part (c).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

Describe the motion instead by the horizontal coordinate $x$, with the origin at the lowest point and the $x$ axis horizontal in the plane of the pendulum, $x = L\sin\theta$. Find the exact equation of motion from Newton's second law for the forces, $\vec F = m\vec a$, in terms of $x$ and $v = \dot x$ (type `v` for $\dot x$). Take $|x| < L$ and the mass on the lower half of the circle.

<Hint>

The forces on the mass are its weight and the tension of the string. The tension has the horizontal component $-T\sin\theta = -T x/L$. What is $T$ when the mass moves on a circle?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Along the string the mass moves on a circle, so $T - mg\cos\theta = m L\dot\theta^2$ (the radial equation). Then use $\cos\theta = \sqrt{1 - x^2/L^2}$ and $\dot\theta = v/(L\cos\theta)$, since $\dot x = L\cos\theta\,\dot\theta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-e" label="$\ddot x =$" expr="-g*x/L*sqrt(1 - x^2/L^2) - x*v^2/(L^2 - x^2)"
  vars={{ g: [9, 10], L: [1, 2], x: [0.1, 0.8], v: [0.5, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-g*x/L*sqrt(1 - x^2/L^2)", hint: "The velocity term is missing. The tension is larger than mgcos θ while the mass moves on its circle: the radial equation has the term mLθ̇²." },
    { expr: "-g*x/sqrt(L^2 - x^2)", hint: "That takes the vertical forces to balance, T cos θ = mg. The mass moves up and down along the circle, so they do not balance. Use the radial equation for T." },
    { expr: "-g*x/L", hint: "That is the approximate equation of part (f). The exact one has a square root and a term in ẋ²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Horizontal force $= m\ddot x$. The only horizontal force is the horizontal part of the tension. The tension is fixed by the motion along the string, which is a circle.

<Step title="The horizontal equation">

With the string at the angle $\theta$ from the vertical, the horizontal component of the tension is $-T\sin\theta = -T\,x/L$:

<Formula label="Pendulum: horizontal equation" tex="m\ddot x = -T\,\frac{x}{L}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.5(e) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The tension">

Along the string the mass is on a circle of radius $L$ and has the centripetal acceleration $L\dot\theta^2$ towards the support. The radial equation is $T - mg\cos\theta = mL\dot\theta^2$. Using $\cos\theta = \sqrt{1 - x^2/L^2}$ and $\dot\theta = \dot x/(L\cos\theta)$:

<Formula label="Pendulum: tension" tex="T = m g\sqrt{1 - \frac{x^2}{L^2}} + m\,\frac{\dot x^2}{L - x^2/L}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.5(e) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The exact equation in $x$">

Insert $T$ into the horizontal equation and divide by $m$:

<Formula label="Pendulum: exact equation of motion in x" tex="\ddot x = -\frac{g\,x}{L}\sqrt{1 - \frac{x^2}{L^2}} - \frac{x\,\dot x^2}{L^2 - x^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.5(e) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second route: differentiate $x = L\sin\theta$ twice, with $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\sin\theta$ from part (a): $\ddot x = L\cos\theta\,\ddot\theta - L\sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2 = -g\sin\theta\cos\theta - L\sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2$. With $\sin\theta = x/L$ this is the same equation. For $x \to 0$ both terms vanish and the equation reduces to $\ddot x \approx -(g/L)x$, which is part (f).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="f" points={2}>

Show that for small $x$ the equation of part (e) reduces to $\ddot x \approx -(g/L)\,x$, the same as for the angle. (No box: open the solution and mark yourself.)

<Hint>

Which terms of the exact equation are small when $x \ll L$? Compare each with the leading term.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Expand each factor in $x/L$ and keep the lowest order.

<Step title="The two corrections">

For small $x/L$ the square root is $\sqrt{1 - x^2/L^2} = 1 - x^2/(2L^2) + \cdots$, which differs from 1 only at second order in $x/L$. The velocity term $x\dot x^2/(L^2 - x^2)$ is of the third order in small quantities, since $x$ and $\dot x$ are both small, and $\dot x$ is of order $\omega x$ for a small swing. So

<Formula label="Pendulum: equation of motion in x for small x" tex="\ddot x \approx -\frac{g}{L}\,x" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, eq. 3 (the same equation)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The frequency is $\sqrt{g/L}$ again, as for the angle. For small swings $x \approx L\theta$, so the two descriptions are the same motion, as they should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="g" points={2}>

Justify the approximation of part (f). To make it quantitative, take it to be good while the square-root factor in your exact equation differs from 1 by less than 1% (our choice; the velocity term is discussed in the solution). Find the largest $|x|/L$.

<Hint>

Set $\sqrt{1 - x^2/L^2} = 0.99$ and solve for $x/L$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-g" label="$x_{\max}/L =$" value="0.1411" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-g-xmax" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The square root is the factor that makes the exact equation different from the linear one. Set it to 0.99.

<Step title="The range">

<Formula label="Pendulum: range of x for the small-x equation" tex="\sqrt{1 - \frac{x_{\max}^2}{L^2}} = 0.99 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{x_{\max}}{L} = \sqrt{1 - 0.99^2} = 0.1411" cite="mit" at="Problem 1.5(g) (our own solution)" />

So for $|x| < 0.141\,L$ (an angle of about $8.1°$) the square-root factor is within 1% of 1.

</Step>

<Step title="The velocity term">

For a swing of amplitude $x_0$ the speed satisfies $\dot x^2 \approx (g/L)(x_0^2 - x^2)$, so the velocity term is at most about $(x/L)(x_0^2/L^2)\,g$ in size, compared with the leading term $g\,x/L$ in the ratio $x_0^2/L^2$. For $x_0 = 0.141L$ that ratio is $0.0199$, about 2%, of the same size as the 1% correction from the square root.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The range is smaller than the $14°$ of part (c) because the two parts compare different quantities: part (c) compares $\sin\theta$ with $\theta$, and this part compares a square root with 1. The two ranges are not directly comparable. Both say the same thing: the approximation is good for swings of a few degrees to about ten degrees, and fails for large swings.

</Step>

</Solution>
