Turn on an electric stove ring and wait. At first nothing seems to happen, but hold your hand above it and you feel warmth. After a while the ring glows a dull red; a hotter one would glow orange, and the thin wire in an old light bulb glows almost white. The ring is giving off **electromagnetic radiation**, and what kind depends on how hot it is. This lesson is about that radiation, and about the puzzle it set for physics around 1900: the answer, Max Planck's idea that energy comes in packets, was the start of quantum physics.

## Every warm object glows

Lesson 6.12 showed that accelerating charges send out electromagnetic waves. Inside any object the atoms and their electrons are always jiggling, faster the hotter the object is (lesson 4.4), so every object sends out electromagnetic waves all the time. This is **thermal radiation**. Only at absolute zero, 0 K, would it stop.

At room temperature the radiation is almost all **infrared**, which our eyes cannot see. A thermal camera can: it shows people, animals and warm pipes glowing brightly in the dark. Heat an object to several hundred degrees Celsius and some of its radiation reaches the red end of the visible band; hotter still and more of the visible band is filled, so the glow turns orange, yellow and finally white.

Thermal radiation is the third way of moving heat, next to conduction and convection (lesson 4.7). Unlike them it needs no material at all: it crosses empty space at the speed of light. That is how the Sun's heat reaches the Earth through the near vacuum of space. An object also **absorbs** the radiation of everything around it; it warms up when it absorbs more than it emits, and cools down when it emits more.

## Black bodies

A surface that absorbs **all** the radiation falling on it, of every wavelength, is called a **black body**. It reflects nothing, so when it is cold it looks perfectly black. A good real example is a small hole in the side of a closed oven: light that goes in bounces around inside and is almost never reflected out again.

A black body is also the best possible **emitter**: no object at the same temperature can radiate more at any wavelength. And the spectrum it sends out, how much radiation there is at each wavelength, depends on **nothing but its temperature**: not on what it is made of or what shape it has (MIT's slides 5–6 show this with two ovens joined through a filter). That is what makes black bodies so useful. Many real objects, such as a stove ring, a star or the glowing gas of the early universe, come close to being black bodies, so measuring their spectrum tells us their temperature.

Three things stand out:

- At **very short wavelengths** a black body sends out almost nothing. The curve starts at zero and rises steeply.
- It has a **peak**: one wavelength at which the body radiates most strongly.
- At **long wavelengths** the curve falls away again, slowly, in a long tail.

As the temperature rises the whole curve grows, very quickly, and the peak moves to the left, towards shorter wavelengths. At 4500 K the peak lies at the red end of the visible band; at 7000 K the peak is in the visible band, near its violet end. This is why the stove ring changes colour as it heats up. (Lesson 7.6 uses the same curves to read the temperatures of stars from their colours.)

## Wien's displacement law

The wavelength of the peak, $\lambda_{\max}$, is inversely proportional to the absolute temperature: twice as hot, half the peak wavelength. This is **Wien's displacement law**:

<Formula label="Wien's displacement law" tex="\lambda_{\max}\,T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}" cite="wien" at="Problem 1(c): 0.29 cm K = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K, from the peak condition 5(1 − e^(−y)) = y" />

Here $T$ must be in kelvin and $\lambda_{\max}$ comes out in metres.

<WorkedExample title="Why a thermal camera works in the infrared">
<Problem>
The skin of your face is at about $34\ ^\circ\text{C}$. At what wavelength does it radiate most strongly?
</Problem>
<Step title="Temperature in kelvin">
$T = 34 + 273 = 307\ \text{K}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Use Wien's law">
$\lambda_{\max} = (2.9 \times 10^{-3})/307 = 9.4 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}$, about 9 micrometres. That is far into the infrared, more than ten times the longest wavelength we can see, which is why thermal cameras are built for this band.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="The glowing wire of a light bulb">
<Problem>
The tungsten wire in an old-fashioned light bulb is at about $2900\ \text{K}$. Where is the peak of its spectrum?
</Problem>
<Step title="Use Wien's law">
$\lambda_{\max} = (2.9 \times 10^{-3})/2900 = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m} = 1000\ \text{nm}$.
</Step>
<Step title="What it means">
The peak lies in the infrared, beyond the red end of the visible band at 700 nm. Most of the power of such a bulb comes out as invisible infrared: it heats the room more than it lights it, which is why these bulbs were replaced by LEDs.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The Stefan–Boltzmann law

The total power a black body radiates is the area under its curve, and that grows very fast with temperature: as the **fourth power** of the absolute temperature. For a black body with surface area $A$ this is the **Stefan–Boltzmann law**:

<Formula label="Stefan–Boltzmann law" tex="P = \sigma A T^4, \qquad \sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\,\text{K}^{-4}" cite="mit" at="slide 15" />

The constant $\sigma$ (sigma) is the Stefan–Boltzmann constant. Double the temperature and the power grows $2^4 = 16$ times. A real surface radiates a little less than a black body at the same temperature, so for real objects the law gives an upper limit.

<WorkedExample title="A hot plate">
<Problem>
The black top of a hot plate has an area of $0.020\ \text{m}^2$ and is at $600\ \text{K}$. Treat it as a black body. How much power does it radiate?
</Problem>
<Step title="Fourth power first">
$T^4 = 600^4 = 1.296 \times 10^{11}\ \text{K}^4$.
</Step>
<Step title="Use the Stefan–Boltzmann law">
$P = \sigma A T^4 = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 0.020 \times 1.296 \times 10^{11} = 147\ \text{W}$, about as much as a strong old light bulb, even though the plate does not glow visibly (Wien's law puts its peak at about 5 micrometres).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A disaster for classical physics

By the end of the 1800s the black-body curves had been measured carefully, and physicists tried to explain them with what they knew: Maxwell's electromagnetic waves and the rule that, in thermal equilibrium, energy is shared out evenly over all the ways something can move.

Picture the radiation inside a closed oven as standing waves between its walls, like the standing waves on a string in lesson 5.4. Each standing wave should get the same average share of energy. But there is no limit to how short a wavelength can be, so there are infinitely many possible standing waves, and more and more of them the shorter the wavelength. Give each one an equal share and the short-wavelength end of the spectrum, the ultraviolet and beyond, gets an endless amount of energy. The calculation predicted that any warm oven would contain an infinite amount of energy and pour out ultraviolet light and X-rays.

That is plainly wrong: a warm oven does no such thing, and the measured curves drop to zero at short wavelengths. The failure was later called the **ultraviolet catastrophe**. Classical physics matched the measurements at long wavelengths but not at short ones. (Guth tells this story in his 8.286 Lecture 16, transcript pp. 21–22.)

## Planck's packets of energy

In 1900 Max Planck found a formula that matched the measured curves exactly; it is the formula used to draw the graph above. To explain it he had to make a strange assumption: the energy of a wave of frequency $f$ can only be given or taken in whole packets, or **quanta**, each of size $hf$. The constant $h$ is now called **Planck's constant**:

<Formula label="Planck's constant" tex="h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 11.5" />

This rescues the short wavelengths. A short wavelength has a high frequency, so its packets are large. The jiggling particles of a warm object rarely have that much energy to spare, and a wave cannot take part of a packet: it gets a whole one or nothing. So the short-wavelength waves get almost nothing, and the spectrum drops to zero just as measured. At long wavelengths the packets are so small that the energy seems to flow smoothly, and the old classical result still works.

Planck himself thought of the packets as a trick of the calculation. In 1905 Albert Einstein went further: light **itself** travels in packets, which were later named **photons**. The strongest early evidence came from the photoelectric effect, the subject of the next lesson.

## The energy of a photon

Light of frequency $f$ is made of photons, each with energy

<Formula label="Energy of a photon" tex="E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 11.5" />

where the second form uses $c = f\lambda$ (lesson 5.8). The shorter the wavelength, the higher the frequency and the more energy each photon carries. A brighter light of the same colour has **more** photons, not bigger ones.

Photon energies in joules are tiny, so they are often given in **electronvolts** (lesson 6.4): $1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$.

<WorkedExample title="A green laser pointer">
<Problem>
A green laser pointer gives light with a wavelength of $532\ \text{nm}$ and a power of $1.0\ \text{mW}$. Find the energy of one photon, in joules and in electronvolts, and the number of photons it sends out each second.
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy of one photon">
$E = hc/\lambda = (6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8})/(532 \times 10^{-9}) = 3.74 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="In electronvolts">
$E = (3.74 \times 10^{-19})/(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 2.34\ \text{eV}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Photons per second">
The pointer sends out $1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ joules each second, so it emits $(1.0 \times 10^{-3})/(3.74 \times 10^{-19}) = 2.7 \times 10^{15}$ photons per second. With so many, the light looks perfectly smooth.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="A radio photon">
<Problem>
An FM radio station broadcasts at $100\ \text{MHz}$. What is the energy of one of its photons?
</Problem>
<Step title="Use E = hf">
$E = hf = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 1.00 \times 10^{8} = 6.63 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{J}$, or $4.1 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{eV}$: millions of times less than a photon of visible light.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The whole universe is a black body">
The universe is filled with radiation left over from the hot early universe: the **cosmic microwave background**. Its spectrum fits a black-body curve extremely accurately, at a temperature of about $2.7\ \text{K}$. Wien's law puts its peak at $(2.9 \times 10^{-3})/2.7 = 1.1 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}$, about a millimetre: in the microwave band, hence the name. In Guth's cosmology course the perfect black-body shape of this radiation is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that the universe was once hot and dense.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Every object above absolute zero gives off **thermal radiation**: electromagnetic waves from its jiggling charged particles. At room temperature it is mostly infrared; hotter objects glow red, then white. Radiation carries heat even across empty space.
- A **black body** absorbs all radiation that falls on it and is the best possible emitter; its spectrum depends only on its temperature.
- **Wien's displacement law**: $\lambda_{\max} T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}$. Hotter means a shorter peak wavelength.
- The **Stefan–Boltzmann law**: $P = \sigma A T^4$ with $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\,\text{K}^{-4}$.
- Classical physics predicted an infinite amount of short-wavelength radiation, the **ultraviolet catastrophe**. Planck solved it by letting energy come in packets $hf$; Einstein showed that light itself comes in such packets, **photons**.
- A photon has energy $E = hf = hc/\lambda$, with $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$; in electronvolts, divide the energy in joules by $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$.
