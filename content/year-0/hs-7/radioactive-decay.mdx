In 1896 Henri Becquerel left some uranium salts on a wrapped photographic plate and found the plate fogged, although no light had reached it: the uranium was giving out radiation of its own. Marie Curie soon found two new elements in uranium ore, polonium and radium, that were far more active still. The radiation comes from the nucleus, and this lesson is about where it comes from, what kinds there are, and how far each kind gets.

## Unstable nuclei

Lesson 7.7 built the nucleus from $Z$ protons and $N$ neutrons, $A = Z + N$ nucleons in all, written ${}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}$ with X the symbol of the element. The strong force holds the nucleons together against the electric repulsion of the protons, but only for the right mix. A nucleus with too many neutrons, too many protons, or simply too many nucleons is **unstable**: sooner or later it changes by itself into a more stable nucleus and gives out radiation as it does so. This is **radioactive decay**, and such nuclei are **radioactive**.

The nucleus that decays is called the **parent**, the nucleus left behind the **daughter**. Because the number of protons usually changes, the daughter is very often a different element. There are three common kinds of decay, named alpha, beta and gamma (after the first letters of the Greek alphabet, in the order they were found).

## Alpha decay

In **alpha decay** the nucleus throws out an **alpha particle**: two protons and two neutrons bound together, a helium-4 nucleus ${}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$. These are the particles Rutherford fired at gold foil (lesson 7.1). The daughter has two protons and four nucleons fewer:

<Formula label="Alpha decay" tex="{}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X} \;\to\; {}^{A-4}_{Z-2}\mathrm{Y} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}" cite="reading1" at="transcript pp. 1–2" />

Alpha decay happens mostly in heavy nuclei, such as uranium, radium and polonium, which hold so many protons that shedding a tightly bound lump of four nucleons makes them more stable. The alpha particles leave fast: most have a kinetic energy of about $4\ \text{MeV}$ or more (reading 1, p. 5).

<WorkedExample title="Radium becomes radon">
<Problem>
Radium-226, ${}^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra}$, the element Marie Curie isolated, decays by giving out an alpha particle. Find the daughter nucleus.
</Problem>
<Step title="Mass number">
The alpha particle takes 4 nucleons, so the daughter has $A = 226 - 4 = 222$.
</Step>
<Step title="Atomic number">
It takes 2 protons, so $Z = 88 - 2 = 86$. Element 86 is radon, so the daughter is radon-222:

${}^{226}_{88}\mathrm{Ra} \to {}^{222}_{86}\mathrm{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$.

Check: on the top, $222 + 4 = 226$; on the bottom, $86 + 2 = 88$. Radon is a gas, which is why it seeps out of rocks and soil that contain radium.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Beta-minus decay

A nucleus with too many neutrons can turn one of them into a proton. Charge must be conserved, so a negative charge has to appear as well: the nucleus gives out a fast **electron**, called a **beta particle** when it comes from a nucleus. A third, almost undetectable particle leaves too, an **antineutrino** $\bar\nu$ (see "The missing energy" below). What happens inside is

<Formula label="A neutron turns into a proton" tex="{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} \;\to\; {}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{p} + {}^{\;\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + \bar\nu" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 1.2.2, Beta particles" />

The electron is written ${}^{\;\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}$: it is not a nucleon, so its top number is 0, and its charge is $-1$ in units of $e$, which takes the place of the atomic number at the bottom. For the whole nucleus, the number of nucleons stays the same and the number of protons goes up by one:

<Formula label="Beta-minus decay" tex="{}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X} \;\to\; {}^{\;\;A}_{Z+1}\mathrm{Y} + {}^{\;\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + \bar\nu" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 2" />

<WorkedExample title="Strontium-90">
<Problem>
Strontium-90 (38 protons) is made in nuclear reactors and decays by beta-minus decay. Write the decay equation.
</Problem>
<Step title="The daughter">
The mass number stays $90$; the atomic number becomes $38 + 1 = 39$, which is yttrium:

${}^{90}_{38}\mathrm{Sr} \to {}^{90}_{39}\mathrm{Y} + {}^{\;\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + \bar\nu$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Top: $90 + 0 = 90$. Bottom: $39 + (-1) = 38$. Both sides balance.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

A famous beta emitter is carbon-14, which has 6 protons and 8 neutrons, two more neutrons than ordinary carbon-12. It becomes nitrogen-14 (7 protons, 7 neutrons): ${}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C} \to {}^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N} + {}^{\;\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + \bar\nu$. Lesson 7.9 shows how this decay is used to date wood and bone.

## Beta-plus decay

A nucleus with too many protons can do the opposite: turn a proton into a neutron. Now it gives out a **positron**, the antiparticle of the electron, with the same mass but charge $+e$, together with a **neutrino** $\nu$:

<Formula label="Beta-plus decay" tex="{}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X} \;\to\; {}^{\;\;A}_{Z-1}\mathrm{Y} + {}^{\;\;0}_{+1}\mathrm{e} + \nu" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 3" />

For example, carbon-11 (6 protons, 5 neutrons) becomes boron-11: ${}^{11}_{6}\mathrm{C} \to {}^{11}_{5}\mathrm{B} + {}^{\;\;0}_{+1}\mathrm{e} + \nu$. The atomic number goes down by one, from 6 to 5, and the mass number stays 11. Beta-plus decay needs enough spare energy to create the positron, so not every proton-rich nucleus can use it (reading 1, p. 3).

## Gamma decay

After an alpha or beta decay the daughter is often left in an **excited state**, with its nucleons arranged in a higher energy level, just as the electron of an atom can sit in a higher level (lesson 7.4). It drops to a lower level by giving out a photon. Nuclear energy levels are spaced by MeV rather than by eV (lesson 7.7), so these photons are **gamma rays**, the highest-frequency part of the electromagnetic spectrum (lesson 5.8). The excited nucleus is marked with a star:

<Formula label="Gamma decay" tex="{}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}^{*} \;\to\; {}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X} + \gamma" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4" />

A gamma ray has no mass and no charge, so neither $A$ nor $Z$ changes: the element stays the same and only loses energy. Gamma rays usually come straight after an alpha or beta decay, sometimes several in a row (reading 1, p. 6).

## The missing energy: the neutrino

All alpha particles from one kind of nucleus leave with the same few sharp energies, as energy conservation says they should: the parent has a definite amount of energy to give away. Beta particles do not. The electrons from one kind of nucleus come out with a whole spread of energies, from almost nothing up to a maximum, and almost none of them carry the full amount the nucleus gives up (reading 1, p. 10). The rest of the energy seemed simply to vanish.

In 1930 Wolfgang Pauli suggested a way out: a second, invisible particle leaves with the electron and takes the rest of the energy. It had to be uncharged, extremely light and very reluctant to interact with anything, since no one had seen it. It is now called the **neutrino**. Neutrinos pass through matter so easily that the first ones were caught only in 1956, by Frederick Reines and Clyde Cowan beside a nuclear reactor. Trillions of them, mostly from the Sun, pass through your body every second without effect.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A sea of neutrinos">
In the first seconds of the universe, neutrinos were made and destroyed constantly and were about as plentiful as photons. Left over from the Big Bang, they still fill all of space today, though they interact far too weakly to have been detected directly. MIT's cosmology course counts them as part of the hot radiation of the early universe (8.286 Lecture 17).
</Callout>

## Balancing decay equations

Every decay equation obeys two rules, which come from two conservation laws:

- **the mass numbers balance**: the top numbers add up to the same total on both sides, because the number of nucleons is conserved;
- **the atomic numbers balance**: the bottom numbers add up to the same total, because charge is conserved (lesson 6.1).

So if you know the parent and the daughter, you can tell which decay happened. An $A$ that drops by 4 means alpha; an unchanged $A$ with $Z$ one higher means beta-minus, one lower beta-plus; nothing changed means gamma.

<WorkedExample title="Which decay is it?">
<Problem>
In nature, bismuth-210 ($Z = 83$) becomes polonium-210 ($Z = 84$), and polonium-210 becomes lead-206 ($Z = 82$). Name the two decays.
</Problem>
<Step title="Bismuth to polonium">
$A$ stays $210$ and $Z$ goes up by $84 - 83 = 1$: a neutron has become a proton. This is beta-minus decay.
</Step>
<Step title="Polonium to lead">
$A$ drops by $210 - 206 = 4$ and $Z$ by $84 - 82 = 2$: the nucleus has lost an alpha particle.

${}^{210}_{84}\mathrm{Po} \to {}^{206}_{82}\mathrm{Pb} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## How far does the radiation get?

As the radiation passes through matter it knocks electrons out of the atoms in its way, leaving them as charged **ions** (lesson 6.1). This is why it is called **ionising radiation**, and why it can harm living cells. Each ionisation costs the radiation a little energy, so the more strongly a kind of radiation ionises, the sooner it runs out of energy and stops. Ionising power and penetrating power go opposite ways:

| | What it is | Charge | Ionising power | Stopped by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | helium nucleus | $+2e$ | strong | a sheet of paper, clothing, the dead outer layer of skin |
| Beta | electron or positron | $-e$ or $+e$ | medium | a thin sheet of aluminium, or water |
| Gamma | photon | none | weak | never completely; thick lead or concrete weakens it a lot |

An alpha particle is heavy, slow and doubly charged, so it pulls hard on every electron it passes and spends its energy within a very short distance. A beta particle is far lighter and has half the charge; it gets through paper and clothing but not a sheet of metal. A gamma ray has no charge at all, so it only ionises now and then, when it happens to hit an electron; most gamma rays go straight through a human body (reading 1, p. 17). Lead does not stop gamma rays sharply but cuts down their number more and more as it gets thicker.

Which is most dangerous depends on where the source is. Outside the body, alpha particles do not even get through the skin, while gamma rays reach every organ. Swallowed or breathed in, an alpha emitter is the worst: it sits next to living cells and dumps all its energy into a tiny region (reading 1, p. 17). Lesson 7.10 looks at radiation and health in detail.

## Random for one, predictable for many

Take one nucleus of carbon-14. When will it decay? Nobody can say. It might decay in the next second or in many thousands of years, and nothing you do to it, not heating it, squeezing it or binding it into a molecule, makes any noticeable difference. Every carbon-14 nucleus has the same chance of decaying in the next second, however long it has already existed. Radioactive decay is **random**.

For a large number of nuclei, though, the randomness averages out. Think of a sack of ordinary dice. You cannot say which die will show a six, but if you throw 6000 of them you can be sure that close to $6000/6 = 1000$ will. In the same way, the number of nuclei in a sample that decay each second is steady and predictable. It is called the **activity** of the sample and measured in **becquerels**: $1\ \text{Bq}$ is one decay per second (reading 4, p. 2).

<WorkedExample title="Counting decays">
<Problem>
Each nucleus of a certain isotope has a chance of 1 in 1000 of decaying in any one second. A sample holds $2.0 \times 10^{6}$ of these nuclei. About how many decay in the next second, and what is the activity?
</Problem>
<Step title="Expected number">
$2.0 \times 10^{6} \times \tfrac{1}{1000} = 2.0 \times 10^{3}$, so about 2000 nuclei decay in that second.
</Step>
<Step title="Activity">
2000 decays per second is an activity of about $2000\ \text{Bq}$. Count again a second later and you will get a slightly different number, a little above or below 2000, because each decay is a matter of chance. As nuclei decay, fewer are left, so the activity slowly falls: how quickly is the subject of lesson 7.9, on half-life.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Radiation all around us

A radiation counter clicks even with no source near it. This **background radiation** is natural, and most of it comes from:

- **radon gas**, made by the decay of radium in rocks and soil, which collects in badly ventilated rooms and cellars: about half of the background (reading 3, p. 5);
- **rocks, building materials and food**, which contain uranium, thorium and potassium-40; your own body contains enough potassium-40 for about 4400 of its nuclei to decay every second (reading 3, p. 12);
- **cosmic rays**, fast particles from space that hit the upper atmosphere and make showers of secondary radiation (reading 3, p. 14).

Medical X-rays and scans add a little more. Lesson 7.10 compares these sources and the doses they give.

## Summary

- An **unstable** (radioactive) nucleus changes by itself into a more stable one, the **daughter**, and gives out radiation.
- **Alpha decay**: a helium nucleus ${}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}$ leaves; $A$ goes down by 4 and $Z$ by 2. Example: radium-226 becomes radon-222.
- **Beta-minus decay**: a neutron turns into a proton; an electron and an antineutrino leave; $Z$ goes up by 1, $A$ stays. Example: carbon-14 becomes nitrogen-14. In **beta-plus decay** a proton turns into a neutron, and a positron and a neutrino leave; $Z$ goes down by 1.
- **Gamma decay**: an excited nucleus gives out a high-energy photon; $A$ and $Z$ stay the same.
- In every decay equation the mass numbers (top) and the atomic numbers (bottom) balance.
- The spread of energies of beta electrons led Pauli to propose the **neutrino**, found in 1956.
- Alpha ionises strongly and is stopped by paper; beta by a thin sheet of aluminium; gamma ionises weakly and is only weakened by thick lead or concrete.
- When a single nucleus decays is **random**; the **activity** of a large sample, in becquerels (decays per second), is predictable.
- **Background radiation** comes mostly from radon, rocks and food (including potassium-40 in our bodies) and cosmic rays.
