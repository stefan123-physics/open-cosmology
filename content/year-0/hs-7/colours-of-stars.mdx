On a clear winter night, look at the constellation Orion. Its top-left star, Betelgeuse, is clearly orange-red. Lower down and to the left, Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, shines a sharp blue-white. These colours are not an accident of the eye: they tell us how hot the surfaces of these stars are. This lesson uses the black-body laws of lesson 7.2 to read a star's temperature from its colour, then works out how much power a star sends out, and ends with the most important graph in the study of stars.

## Stars glow like black bodies

Lesson 7.2 showed that a hot object gives off thermal radiation, and that a **black body**, an object that absorbs everything that falls on it, gives off a spectrum that depends on nothing but its temperature. A star is a ball of hot, dense gas. Light from deep inside is absorbed and sent out again countless times before it escapes, so the gas at the visible surface behaves very much like a black body. A star's spectrum is close to a black-body curve, with the dark lines of lesson 7.5 cut into it.

The temperature found this way belongs to the star's visible **surface**. The inside is far hotter, millions of kelvin in the core where the star's energy is made, but that light never reaches us directly.

Two laws from lesson 7.2 do all the work. **Wien's law** gives the wavelength $\lambda_{\max}$ where the spectrum peaks:

<Formula label="Wien's displacement law" tex="\lambda_{\max}\,T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}" cite="reading3" at="slide 22 (where the spectrum has its maximum; the slide finds it per unit of frequency, and the same formula per unit of wavelength gives this constant)" />

and the **Stefan–Boltzmann law** says that each square metre of the surface radiates a power $\sigma T^4$, with $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\,\text{K}^{-4}$.

## Colour and temperature

A cool star has its peak in the infrared. Of the light it does give off in the visible band, much more is red than blue, so it looks red or orange. A hotter star has its peak in the visible band. A very hot star has its peak in the ultraviolet; in the visible band it then sends out more blue than red, so it looks blue-white. Move the slider and watch the share of red and blue light change.

<WorkedExample title="Betelgeuse and Sirius">
<Problem>
The surface of Betelgeuse is a little over $3300\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so about $3600\ \text{K}$. The surface of Sirius is about $10\,500\ \text{K}$. Where does the spectrum of each star peak, and does that fit their colours?
</Problem>
<Step title="Betelgeuse">
$\lambda_{\max} = (2.9 \times 10^{-3})/3600 = 8.1 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 810\ \text{nm}$. That is in the infrared, just past the red end of the visible band at 700 nm. In the visible band the curve is still rising towards red, so the star looks orange-red.
</Step>
<Step title="Sirius">
$\lambda_{\max} = (2.9 \times 10^{-3})/10\,500 = 2.8 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 280\ \text{nm}$, in the ultraviolet. In the visible band the curve is falling from blue towards red, so Sirius looks blue-white.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Why the Sun is not green">
<Problem>
The Sun's surface temperature is $5772\ \text{K}$, or about $5800\ \text{K}$. Where does its spectrum peak?
</Problem>
<Step title="Use Wien's law">
$\lambda_{\max} = (2.9 \times 10^{-3})/5772 = 5.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 500\ \text{nm}$, in the blue-green.
</Step>
<Step title="What we see">
The peak is broad and spreads over the whole visible band, so all colours arrive in similar amounts and our eyes see the mixture as white (the Earth's air scatters some blue away, which makes the Sun look a little yellow from the ground). The colour of a star is set by the **shape** of its whole visible spectrum, not by the colour at its peak.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## How much power a star sends out

The total power a star radiates, in all directions and at all wavelengths, is its **luminosity** $L$, in watts. A star is a sphere of radius $R$, so its surface area is $4\pi R^2$, and each square metre gives off $\sigma T^4$. Together:

<Formula label="Luminosity of a star" tex="L = 4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4" cite="reading2" at="p. 50, the Stefan–Boltzmann law above eq. (226)" />

Luminosity is a property of the star itself. How bright a star **looks** from Earth also depends on how far away it is: the same star placed further away looks fainter.

<WorkedExample title="The power of the Sun">
<Problem>
The Sun has a radius of $6.96 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}$ and a surface temperature of $5772\ \text{K}$. Find its luminosity and compare it with the measured value, $3.828 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Surface area">
$4\pi R^2 = 4\pi \times (6.96 \times 10^{8})^2 = 6.09 \times 10^{18}\ \text{m}^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Power per square metre">
$\sigma T^4 = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 5772^4 = 6.29 \times 10^{7}\ \text{W/m}^2$: every square metre of the Sun's surface gives off about 63 megawatts.
</Step>
<Step title="Luminosity">
$L = 6.09 \times 10^{18} \times 6.29 \times 10^{7} = 3.83 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}$, the measured value to within the rounding. The black-body picture of the Sun works.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

To compare two stars there is no need to work out each luminosity in full. Dividing the law for star 1 by the law for star 2, the $4\pi$ and $\sigma$ cancel:

<Formula label="Comparing two stars" tex="\frac{L_1}{L_2} = \left(\frac{R_1}{R_2}\right)^2 \left(\frac{T_1}{T_2}\right)^4" cite="reading2" at="p. 50, eq. (226) (the same law, written as a ratio instead of in logarithms)" />

<WorkedExample title="Sirius and its small companion">
<Problem>
Sirius has a faint companion, Sirius B. Sirius itself (Sirius A) has a diameter of $2.4 \times 10^{6}\ \text{km}$ and a surface temperature of $10\,500\ \text{K}$; Sirius B has a diameter of only $12\,000\ \text{km}$, about that of the Earth, but a surface temperature of $25\,200\ \text{K}$. How many times more luminous is Sirius A?
</Problem>
<Step title="The ratio of the radii">
The radii are in the same ratio as the diameters: $R_1/R_2 = (2.4 \times 10^{6})/(12\,000) = 200$, so $(R_1/R_2)^2 = 4.0 \times 10^{4}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The ratio of the temperatures">
$(T_1/T_2)^4 = (10\,500/25\,200)^4 = 0.030$.
</Step>
<Step title="Combine">
$L_1/L_2 = 4.0 \times 10^{4} \times 0.030 = 1.2 \times 10^{3}$. Sirius A gives off about 1200 times more power, even though each square metre of Sirius B radiates about 33 times more. Size wins.
</Step>
<Step title="In visible light">
Both stars have their peak in the ultraviolet, Sirius A at 280 nm and Sirius B at about 115 nm, but Sirius B lies much further out. Working out the area under each black-body curve between 400 and 700 nm shows that about 31 % of the power of Sirius A is visible light, but only about 6 % of the power of Sirius B. In visible light Sirius A is therefore about $1200 \times 31/6 \approx 6000$ times brighter. Real stars are not perfect black bodies, and NASA's Hubble news release gives "about 10,000 times fainter" for Sirius B: the same story, a much bigger difference in visible light than in total power.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Spectral classes

At the Harvard College Observatory, astronomers sorted stars by the dark lines in their spectra. Annie Jump Cannon, who classified the spectra of some half a million stars there, put the classes in the order used today:

**O B A F G K M**

The order turned out to be an order of **temperature**: the lines a star shows depend mostly on how hot its surface is (lesson 7.5 explains how lines arise). For stars on the main sequence (see below) the classes also run from biggest to smallest.

| Class | Colour | Example |
|---|---|---|
| O | blue | hottest, tens of thousands of kelvin |
| B | blue-white | |
| A | white | Sirius A (white, so class A), about 10 500 K |
| F | yellow-white | |
| G | yellow | the Sun, about 5800 K |
| K | orange | |
| M | red | coolest, down to about 2500 K; Betelgeuse (red, so class M), about 3600 K |

A traditional way to remember the order is "Oh Be A Fine Girl (or Guy), Kiss Me". Most stars in our galaxy are small, cool M stars, far too faint to see with the naked eye.

## The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram

In 1911 Ejnar Hertzsprung, and in 1913 Henry Norris Russell, each plotted stars by their luminosity against their temperature (or spectral class). The result, the **Hertzsprung–Russell diagram** or HR diagram, is the most important graph in the study of stars. Because luminosities range from far below to far above the Sun's, the luminosity axis counts powers of ten: a value of 2 means $10^2 = 100$ times the Sun's luminosity, a value of $-2$ one hundredth of it.

The law $L = 4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4$ tells us where stars of a given size lie. For a fixed radius, $L$ grows as $T^4$, which gives the lines in the graph below, each for one radius in units of the Sun's radius.

Plot real stars on such a diagram and they do not land just anywhere. They fall into a few groups:

- The **main sequence**: a band from hot, luminous stars to cool, faint ones, where most stars lie, the Sun among them. These are stars that are steadily fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores: joining hydrogen nuclei into helium nuclei, which releases the energy the star shines with (lesson 8.5 shows how). The Sun sits on it as a G star; Sirius A lies on it too, hotter and more luminous.
- **Giants and supergiants**: above the main sequence on the cool side. They are cool, yet very luminous, so by the Stefan–Boltzmann law they must be enormous. Betelgeuse, a red supergiant, is roughly 700 times the size of the Sun; placed where the Sun is, it would swallow the orbits of the inner planets.
- **White dwarfs**: below the main sequence on the hot side. They are hot but faint, so they must be tiny, about the size of the Earth. Sirius B is one.

The groups are stages in the life of a star. A star spends most of its life on the main sequence. When the hydrogen in its core runs out, its outer layers swell and it becomes a giant; a star like the Sun ends as a white dwarf that slowly cools. Where a star's energy comes from is the subject of lesson 8.5.

The same reasoning works on scales far larger than a star. ## Summary

- Stars glow almost like **black bodies**, so their spectra give their **surface temperatures**.
- By **Wien's law**, $\lambda_{\max} T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}$, a red star is cool (Betelgeuse, about 3600 K) and a blue-white star hot (Sirius, about 10 500 K); the Sun, at about 5800 K, peaks near 500 nm and looks white.
- A star's **luminosity**, the total power it radiates, is $L = 4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4$. For the Sun this gives $3.83 \times 10^{26}$ W, matching the measured value. For two stars, $L_1/L_2 = (R_1/R_2)^2 (T_1/T_2)^4$.
- The **spectral classes** O B A F G K M run from hottest (blue) to coolest (red); the Sun is a G star.
- The **Hertzsprung–Russell diagram** plots luminosity against temperature. Most stars lie on the **main sequence**; **giants** are cool but luminous, so huge; **white dwarfs** are hot but faint, so tiny.
