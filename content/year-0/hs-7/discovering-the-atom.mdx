Everything around you, the air, the water in a glass, your own hand, is built from atoms. Block 6 used electrons and protons as if we had always known about them. This lesson tells how they were found, and how one surprising experiment showed that an atom is almost entirely empty, with nearly all its mass packed into a tiny centre: the **nucleus**.

## Atoms: the building blocks

The idea is old. Greek thinkers of the fifth century BC suggested that you cannot cut matter into smaller and smaller pieces for ever: at some point you reach pieces that cannot be divided. They called them atoms, from the Greek for "uncuttable". For two thousand years this stayed an idea without evidence.

Around 1800 the English chemist John Dalton turned it into science. Chemical reactions always combine elements in fixed proportions, and Dalton explained this with atoms: every element is made of its own kind of tiny atom, and reactions only rearrange them. In Dalton's picture an atom was a small, solid, indivisible ball. Nobody yet suspected that it had parts inside.

Scientists describe atoms with **models**: simplified pictures that explain what has been measured. A model is kept as long as it fits the experiments and changed when a new experiment disagrees. The history of the atom is a chain of such changes.

## Thomson finds the electron

In the late nineteenth century physicists studied glass tubes with almost all the air pumped out and a high voltage between two metal plates inside. Rays came from the negative plate (the cathode) and made the glass glow where they struck it. These **cathode rays** were bent by electric and magnetic fields, in the direction that showed they carried negative charge.

In 1897 the English physicist J. J. Thomson measured how strongly the rays were bent and found their **charge-to-mass ratio** $e/m$. His trick was to use the two fields of Block 6 together. In an electric field $E$ the beam feels a force $eE$ (lesson 6.3); in a magnetic field $B$ at right angles it feels $evB$ (lesson 6.9). Set up so that the two forces point in opposite directions, they cancel for one particular speed, and the beam goes straight on:

<Formula label="Electric and magnetic forces balance" tex="eE = evB \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \frac{E}{B}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 8.6.1, Eq. (8.6.3)" />

Switch off the electric field, and the magnetic field alone bends the beam into a circle (lesson 6.9):

<Formula label="Radius of the circle in a magnetic field" tex="r = \frac{m_e v}{eB}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (8.5.2)" />

Knowing $v$, $B$ and the measured radius $r$ gives $e/m_e = v/(rB)$.

<WorkedExample title="Weighing the electron with two fields">
<Problem>
In a Thomson-style tube, a beam of electrons goes straight through crossed fields of $E = 2.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{V/m}$ and $B = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{T}$. With the electric field switched off, the beam bends into a circle of radius $11.4\ \text{cm}$. Find the speed of the electrons and their charge-to-mass ratio.
</Problem>
<Step title="Speed from the balanced forces">
$v = E/B = (2.0 \times 10^{4})/(1.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.0 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m/s}$, about 7% of the speed of light.
</Step>
<Step title="Charge-to-mass ratio from the circle">
$e/m_e = v/(rB) = (2.0 \times 10^{7})/(0.114 \times 1.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 1.75 \times 10^{11}\ \text{C/kg}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare with hydrogen">
A hydrogen ion (a proton) has the same size of charge but $e/m_p = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}/1.67 \times 10^{-27} = 9.6 \times 10^{7}\ \text{C/kg}$. The electron's ratio is about 1800 times larger: for the same charge, it is about 1800 times lighter.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Thomson found the same ratio whatever metal the cathode was made of and whatever gas was left in the tube. So these particles, soon called **electrons**, are part of every atom, and they are far lighter than any atom. (Their charge $e$ itself was measured separately a few years later; dividing gives $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$.)

That raised a new question. An atom is neutral, so it must also contain positive charge, and nearly all its mass. Where is it? In 1904 Thomson proposed the **plum-pudding model**: the atom is a ball of positive charge, spread evenly through its whole volume, with the tiny electrons stuck in it like plums in a pudding (or raisins in a cake).

## Rutherford's gold-foil experiment

The test came from the laboratory of Ernest Rutherford, a New Zealand physicist working in England, carried out by his assistants Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden. They used **alpha particles**: positively charged particles shot out at high speed by radioactive elements (they turned out to be helium nuclei, with charge $+2e$, lesson 7.8). An alpha particle is more than 7000 times as heavy as an electron, so an electron in its path hardly disturbs it, like a pebble in the path of a bowling ball.

A narrow beam of alpha particles was aimed at a gold foil only a few hundred atoms thick. Behind and around the foil was a screen of zinc sulfide, which gives a tiny flash of light wherever an alpha particle strikes it. Counting the flashes, in a darkened room, showed where the particles went.

**What the plum-pudding model predicts.** Inside a Thomson atom the positive charge is spread over the whole ball, so its electric field is weak everywhere, and the electrons are far too light to matter. A fast, heavy alpha particle should go through each atom with at most a tiny change of direction. Almost every particle should come out of the foil going nearly straight on.

**What was seen.** Most alpha particles did go straight through, as if the foil were hardly there. But a few were turned through large angles, and a very few, roughly one in many thousands, came bouncing back towards the source.

A bounce back means a very strong push. By Coulomb's law (lesson 6.2), the force grows as $1/r^2$ as the distance shrinks, so a very strong push needs the alpha particle to get very close to a large positive charge. Rutherford worked out in 1911 what the atom must look like:

- all the positive charge and nearly all the mass are packed into a tiny centre, the **nucleus**;
- the electrons are spread through the much larger space around it;
- most of the atom is **empty space**.

Most alpha particles never come near a nucleus, so they pass straight through. The rare one that heads almost straight at a nucleus is pushed back by its repulsion. Rutherford's picture, with light electrons going round a small heavy centre, is often called the **planetary model**, after planets going round the Sun.

## How close does an alpha particle get?

Energy gives an estimate of how small the nucleus must be. Fire an alpha particle straight at a nucleus. As it approaches, the repulsion slows it down: its kinetic energy $E_k$ turns into electric potential energy (lesson 6.4), until it stops for an instant and turns back. For two point charges $Q$ and $q$ a distance $r$ apart,

<Formula label="Potential energy of two point charges" tex="U = \frac{kQq}{r}" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (3.3.5), written with k = 1/(4πε₀)" />

At the turning point all the kinetic energy has become potential energy (conservation of energy), so the closest distance is

<Formula label="Distance of closest approach" tex="r = \frac{kQq}{E_k}" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (3.3.5) with conservation of energy" />

<WorkedExample title="An alpha particle against a gold nucleus">
<Problem>
A gold nucleus has charge $Q = 79e$ and an alpha particle $q = 2e$. An alpha particle with a kinetic energy of $5.0\ \text{MeV}$ heads straight for a gold nucleus. How close does it get?
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy in joules">
$E_k = 5.0 \times 10^{6} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 8.0 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$ (lesson 6.4: $1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$).
</Step>
<Step title="Closest distance">
$kQq = 9.0 \times 10^{9} \times 79 \times 2 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2 = 3.64 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{J m}$, so $r = 3.64 \times 10^{-26}/8.0 \times 10^{-13} = 4.5 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="What it tells us">
The alpha particle was turned back by an electric force alone, so it never touched the nucleus: the gold nucleus must be smaller than about $4.5 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{m}$. That is more than two thousand times smaller than the atom ($10^{-10}\ \text{m}$). In a Thomson atom the positive charge could never be this concentrated.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## How empty is an atom?

Later measurements give the sizes precisely. A hydrogen atom, the simplest one, is about $1.06 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ across, and its nucleus is a single proton about $1.68 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$ across. As rough values to remember: atoms are about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ across, nuclei about $10^{-15}$ to $10^{-14}\ \text{m}$.

<WorkedExample title="The emptiness of a hydrogen atom">
<Problem>
Use the diameters above. How many times larger is the atom than its nucleus? What fraction of the atom's volume does the nucleus fill? And if the nucleus were enlarged to the size of a $1\ \text{cm}$ blueberry, how wide would the atom be?
</Problem>
<Step title="Ratio of the diameters">
$1.06 \times 10^{-10}/1.68 \times 10^{-15} = 6.3 \times 10^{4}$: the atom is about 63 000 times wider than its nucleus.
</Step>
<Step title="Ratio of the volumes">
The volume of a sphere goes as the cube of its diameter, so the nucleus fills $1/(6.3 \times 10^{4})^3 = 4 \times 10^{-15}$ of the atom: four millionths of a billionth.
</Step>
<Step title="Scaled up">
A $1\ \text{cm}$ nucleus would sit at the centre of an atom $6.3 \times 10^{4}\ \text{cm} = 630\ \text{m}$ across, with nothing but a thin haze of electron in between.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Since almost all the mass is in the nucleus, nuclear matter is unimaginably dense. A proton has a mass of $1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$ and a radius of about $0.84 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$. Its volume is $\tfrac43\pi (0.84 \times 10^{-15})^3 = 2.5 \times 10^{-45}\ \text{m}^3$, so its density (lesson 4.1) is about $1.67 \times 10^{-27}/2.5 \times 10^{-45} = 7 \times 10^{17}\ \text{kg/m}^3$: some $7 \times 10^{14}$ times the density of water. Ordinary matter is light only because its atoms are almost entirely empty.

## A problem with the planetary atom

Rutherford's atom explained the scattering, but classical physics found two serious problems with it.

First, the electrons cannot be standing still, or the nucleus would simply pull them in. So they must move, for instance in circles around the nucleus, held by the electric attraction as planets are held by gravity. But an object moving in a circle is **accelerating** towards the centre (lesson 1.10), and lesson 6.12 showed that an accelerating charge sends out electromagnetic waves, carrying energy away. An orbiting electron should therefore lose energy all the time and spiral inwards. Worked out with classical physics, it would crash into the nucleus in a tiny fraction of a second. Every atom in the universe would collapse. They do not.

Second, a spiralling electron would go round faster and faster, so the light it gave off would change its frequency smoothly, giving a continuous spread of colours. But atoms of a gas, made to glow, give light of only a few sharp, fixed colours: their spectral lines.

Classical physics, which worked so well for planets, circuits and radio waves, fails inside the atom. The way out came in 1913 from the Danish physicist Niels Bohr, who allowed electrons only certain fixed orbits, with fixed energies: the start of quantum physics, and the subject of lesson 7.4. The nucleus itself turned out to have parts too, protons and neutrons, which lesson 7.7 describes.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="When the universe had no atoms">
Atoms did not always exist. In the hot early universe, matter was a glowing mix of bare nuclei and free electrons: any electron that joined a nucleus was knocked off again at once by the intense light. Only after a few hundred thousand years of expansion and cooling could nuclei hold on to their electrons and form neutral atoms. At that moment light could travel freely for the first time, and we still see that light today as the cosmic microwave background (Block 8).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Matter is made of **atoms**. Dalton (around 1800) used them to explain chemistry; they were pictured as solid, indivisible balls.
- In 1897 **Thomson** discovered the **electron**: balancing electric and magnetic forces ($v = E/B$) and bending the beam into a circle ($r = m_e v/(eB)$) gave $e/m_e \approx 1.76 \times 10^{11}\ \text{C/kg}$, about 1800 times that of a hydrogen ion. His **plum-pudding model** (1904) spread the positive charge through the whole atom.
- In the **gold-foil experiment** most alpha particles passed straight through, but a very few bounced back. **Rutherford** (1911) concluded that the positive charge and nearly all the mass sit in a tiny, dense **nucleus**, with electrons around it and mostly **empty space** between.
- From energy, $r = kQq/E_k$: a $5.0\ \text{MeV}$ alpha particle turns back $4.5 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{m}$ from a gold nucleus. Atoms are about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ across, nuclei about $10^{-15}$ to $10^{-14}\ \text{m}$; in hydrogen the nucleus fills only about $4 \times 10^{-15}$ of the volume, and nuclear matter has a density of about $7 \times 10^{17}\ \text{kg/m}^3$.
- In classical physics an orbiting electron accelerates, radiates, loses energy and spirals into the nucleus, and it would give a continuous spectrum. Real atoms are stable and give sharp lines: lesson 7.4 (Bohr) starts to explain why, and lesson 7.7 looks inside the nucleus.
