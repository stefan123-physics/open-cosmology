This set practises all eleven lessons of Block 7, from Rutherford's nucleus to the glow left over from the early universe. The problems are our own, of the kinds found in the exercises of Siyavula's chapters on the atom and on optical phenomena and in MIT's course notes and lectures; the hints and solutions are our own too. Use $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$ (so $1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$), $k = 9.0 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$, $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$, $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$, a proton mass of $m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, $1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, Wien's constant $2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}$ and $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\,\text{K}^{-4}$, unless a problem gives another value.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box wants the number only, in the unit printed after the box, for example `0.25` or `1.2e5`; small differences from rounding are accepted. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `(a + b)/c`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Alpha particles against copper" points={11}>

In a repeat of Rutherford's experiment, alpha particles with a kinetic energy of $4.0\ \text{MeV}$ are fired at a thin copper foil. A copper nucleus carries a charge of $+29e$ and an alpha particle $+2e$. The copper nucleus is so much heavier that you may treat it as fixed.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the kinetic energy of one alpha particle, in joules?

<Hint>

How many joules is one electronvolt? And how many electronvolts is one MeV?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$E_k =$" value="6.4e-13" unit="J" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p1-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The electronvolt is the energy a charge $e$ gains across one volt, so it converts to joules by multiplying with $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$.

<Step title="Convert">

<Formula label="The electronvolt" tex="1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}" cite="siy122" at="Sec. 12.2, Units of energy" />

$E_k = 4.0 \times 10^{6} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 6.4 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A tiny energy in everyday terms, but huge for one particle: it is four million times what an electron gains across one volt.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

An alpha particle heads straight for a copper nucleus. How close does it get before it stops and turns back?

<Hint>

At the turning point the alpha particle stands still for a moment. Where has all its kinetic energy gone?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$r =$" value="2.09e-14" unit="m" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p1-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As the alpha particle slows down, its kinetic energy turns into electric potential energy. At the closest point all of it has turned.

<Step title="Energy at the closest point">

<Formula label="Potential energy of two point charges" tex="U = \frac{kQq}{r}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.3.5), written with k = 1/(4πε₀)" />

<Formula label="Distance of closest approach" tex="r = \frac{kQq}{E_k}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.3.5) with conservation of energy, U = E_k at the turning point (our own step)" />

$r = 9.0 \times 10^9 \times (29 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}) \times (2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19})/(6.4 \times 10^{-13}) = 2.09 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{m}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Put the distance back in: $kQq = 9.0 \times 10^9 \times 4.64 \times 10^{-18} \times 3.2 \times 10^{-19} = 1.34 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{J m}$, and $U = 1.34 \times 10^{-26}/(2.09 \times 10^{-14}) = 6.4 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$, the kinetic energy of part (a). The distance is about $5000$ times smaller than an atom ($10^{-10}\ \text{m}$, lesson 7.1): only a tiny, heavy, charged centre could turn the alpha particle back this close.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the distance of closest approach $r$ for an alpha particle with kinetic energy $E_k$ aimed straight at a nucleus of charge $Ze$, in terms of $k$, $Z$, $e$ and $E_k$.

<Hint>

What are the charges of the two particles, written with $Z$ and $e$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put $Q = Ze$ and $q = 2e$ into $r = kQq/E_k$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$r =$" expr="2*k*Z*e^2/E"
  vars={{ k: [1, 10], Z: [1, 92], e: [1, 2], E: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ E: "E_k" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "k*Z*e^2/E", hint: "The alpha particle has two protons, so its charge is 2e, not e." },
    { expr: "2*k*Z*e/E", hint: "Both charges contain e: the nucleus has Ze and the alpha particle 2e, so e appears squared." },
    { expr: "E/(2*k*Z*e^2)", hint: "Upside down: a faster alpha particle should get closer, so E_k belongs below the line." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write both charges with $e$ and use the result of part (b).

<Step title="Insert the charges">

<Formula label="Closest approach of an alpha particle to a nucleus of charge Ze" tex="r = \frac{k\,(Ze)(2e)}{E_k} = \frac{2kZe^2}{E_k}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.3.5) with conservation of energy (our own step)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $Z = 29$ and $E_k = 6.4 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$ it gives the $2.09 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{m}$ of part (b). Twice the energy halves the distance; a nucleus with more charge keeps the alpha particle further away. Units: $(\text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2) \cdot \text{C}^2/\text{J} = \text{N m}^2/(\text{N m}) = \text{m}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

The copper foil is replaced by a gold foil (gold: $Z = 79$), with the same $4.0\ \text{MeV}$ alpha particles. What happens to the distance of closest approach?

<Answer id="p1-d" label="The distance of closest approach:"
  choices={[
    "Shrinks, because gold nuclei are heavier and pull harder",
    "Stays the same, because the alpha particles are the same",
    "Grows about 2.7 times",
    "Shrinks to about a third"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The distance is proportional to the charge of the nucleus, $r = 2kZe^2/E_k$.

<Step title="Compare the charges">

Gold has $79/29 = 2.72$ times the charge of copper, so the alpha particle turns back $2.72$ times further away: at $5.69 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{m}$. The nucleus pushes with its charge; its mass only keeps it from moving.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A glowing lava lake" points={11}>

The surface of a lava lake is at $1450\ \text{K}$. Treat it as a black body.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

At what wavelength does the lava radiate most strongly? Give it in micrometres.

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$\lambda_{\max} =$" value="2.0" unit="μm" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p2-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Wien's law: the hotter the body, the shorter the wavelength of its peak.

<Step title="Wien's law">

<Formula label="Wien's displacement law" tex="\lambda_{\max}\,T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}" cite="wien" at="Problem 1(c): 0.29 cm K = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K" />

$\lambda_{\max} = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}/1450 = 2.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m} = 2.0\ \text{μm}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

That is far beyond the red end of the visible spectrum ($700\ \text{nm} = 0.7\ \text{μm}$): the lava radiates mostly infrared. The dull orange-red glow we see is only the short-wavelength tail of its spectrum.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

How much power does each square metre of the lava's surface radiate? Give it in kilowatts.

<Hint>

Which law gives the power of a black body from its temperature and its area?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$P =$" value="251" unit="kW" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p2-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The power of a black body grows as the fourth power of its absolute temperature.

<Step title="Stefan–Boltzmann law">

<Formula label="Stefan–Boltzmann law" tex="P = \sigma A T^4" cite="reading2" at="slide 15" />

$P = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 1.00 \times 1450^4 = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 4.42 \times 10^{12} = 2.51 \times 10^{5}\ \text{W} = 251\ \text{kW}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\text{W m}^{-2}\,\text{K}^{-4} \cdot \text{m}^2 \cdot \text{K}^4 = \text{W}$. A quarter of a megawatt from one square metre: this is why you cannot stand close to a lava lake.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

What is the energy of a photon at the peak wavelength of part (a)? Give it in electronvolts.

<Hint>

Which formula gives a photon's energy from its wavelength?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$E =$" value="0.622" unit="eV" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p2-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A photon's energy is Planck's constant times its frequency, $hc/\lambda$.

<Step title="Photon energy">

<Formula label="Energy of a photon" tex="E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}" cite="siy115" at="Sec. 11.5" />

$E = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}/(2.0 \times 10^{-6}) = 9.95 \times 10^{-20}\ \text{J}$, and $9.95 \times 10^{-20}/(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 0.622\ \text{eV}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A red photon of $700\ \text{nm}$ carries $1.78\ \text{eV}$, almost three times as much. Longer wavelength, less energy per photon, as it must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

A crust forms on the lava, with a surface at $725\ \text{K}$, half the absolute temperature. How does the power radiated per square metre change?

<Answer id="p2-d" label="The power per square metre:"
  choices={[
    "Halves",
    "Falls to a quarter",
    "Falls to an eighth",
    "Falls to a sixteenth"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The power goes with $T^4$.

<Step title="Fourth power">

Halving $T$ multiplies $T^4$ by $(1/2)^4 = 1/16$: from $251\ \text{kW}$ to $5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 725^4 = 15.7\ \text{kW}$ per square metre. So halving the absolute temperature cuts the glow sixteenfold.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Measuring Planck's constant" points={13}>

In a school laboratory, light shines on a clean metal plate in a vacuum tube. For each colour the reverse voltage is raised until the current just stops. The results are:

| Wavelength of the light | Stopping voltage |
|---|---|
| $300\ \text{nm}$ | $1.84\ \text{V}$ |
| $450\ \text{nm}$ | $0.46\ \text{V}$ |

In this problem, do not use the given value of $h$ until part (d): the measurements will give it.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the frequency of the $450\ \text{nm}$ light?

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$f =$" value="6.67e14" unit="Hz" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p3-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Light travels at $c$, so frequency times wavelength is $c$.

<Step title="Frequency">

$f = c/\lambda = 3.00 \times 10^{8}/(450 \times 10^{-9}) = 6.67 \times 10^{14}\ \text{Hz}$. The same way, the $300\ \text{nm}$ light has $f = 3.00 \times 10^{8}/(300 \times 10^{-9}) = 1.00 \times 10^{15}\ \text{Hz}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The shorter wavelength has the higher frequency, by the factor $450/300 = 1.5$: $6.67 \times 10^{14} \times 1.5 = 1.00 \times 10^{15}\ \text{Hz}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Use both measurements to find Planck's constant $h$.

<Hint>

Write Einstein's photoelectric equation once for each colour. Which unknown appears in both, and how can you get rid of it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The fastest electrons have $E_{k,\max} = eV_s$. Subtract the two equations: the work function cancels.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$h =$" value="6.62e-34" unit="J s" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p3-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each photon gives its energy $hf$ to one electron: part of it pays the work function, the rest is the electron's kinetic energy, which the stopping voltage measures. Call the two frequencies $f_1$ ($300\ \text{nm}$) and $f_2$ ($450\ \text{nm}$), and their stopping voltages $V_1$ and $V_2$.

<Step title="Einstein's equation for each colour">

<Formula label="Einstein's photoelectric equation" tex="hf = W_0 + E_{k,\max}" cite="siy122" at="Sec. 12.2, Implications of Einstein's model" />

<Formula label="Stopping voltage" tex="eV_s = E_{k,\max}" cite="siy122" at="Sec. 12.2, Units of energy (E = q × V), applied to the stopping voltage" />

</Step>

<Step title="Subtract">

<Formula label="Planck's constant from two measurements" tex="hf_1 - hf_2 = eV_1 - eV_2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = \frac{e\,(V_1 - V_2)}{f_1 - f_2}" cite="siy122" at="Sec. 12.2, hf = W₀ + E_k,max written for two frequencies (our own step)" />

$h = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times (1.84 - 0.46)/(1.00 \times 10^{15} - 6.67 \times 10^{14}) = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.38/(3.33 \times 10^{14}) = 6.62 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\text{C} \cdot \text{V}/\text{Hz} = \text{J} \cdot \text{s}$. The result agrees with the accepted $6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$ to within the rounding of the measurements. This is how Millikan tested Einstein's equation: $h$ is the slope of the straight line of $eV_s$ against $f$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

What is the work function of the metal, in electronvolts?

<Answer id="p3-c" label="$W_0 =$" value="2.30" unit="eV" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p3-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $h$ known, Einstein's equation for either colour gives $W_0$.

<Step title="Work function">

For the $300\ \text{nm}$ light: $hf = 6.62 \times 10^{-34} \times 1.00 \times 10^{15} = 6.62 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} = 4.14\ \text{eV}$, so $W_0 = hf - eV_s = 4.14 - 1.84 = 2.30\ \text{eV}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The other colour must give the same: $hf = 6.62 \times 10^{-34} \times 6.67 \times 10^{14} = 4.42 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J} = 2.76\ \text{eV}$, and $2.76 - 0.46 = 2.30\ \text{eV}$. It does.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

What is the longest wavelength of light that can still knock electrons out of this metal? Now use $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$.

<Answer id="p3-d" label="$\lambda_0 =$" value="540" unit="nm" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p3-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At the threshold a photon has just enough energy to free an electron, with nothing left over.

<Step title="Threshold wavelength">

$hc/\lambda_0 = W_0$, so $\lambda_0 = hc/W_0 = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}/(2.30 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 5.40 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 540\ \text{nm}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$540\ \text{nm}$ is green light. Both colours used in the experiment, $300$ and $450\ \text{nm}$, have shorter wavelengths, so both could eject electrons, as they did. Red light would not.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

The $300\ \text{nm}$ lamp is replaced by one of the same colour that is twice as bright. What changes?

<Answer id="p3-e" label="With the brighter lamp:"
  choices={[
    "The stopping voltage doubles to 3.68 V",
    "The stopping voltage stays 1.84 V; twice as many electrons come out",
    "The stopping voltage and the current both stay the same",

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$v =$" value="49.4" unit="km/s" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p5-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A shift of a line by a fraction $\Delta\lambda/\lambda$ means a speed along the line of sight of that same fraction of $c$.

<Step title="Speed from the shift">

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\Delta\lambda/\lambda = 0.08/486.10 = 1.6 \times 10^{-4}$, much less than $1$, so the formula for slow motion is fine. The star swings back and forth: it orbits an unseen partner.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-c" label="$\lambda =$" value="656.41" unit="nm" tolerance="0.02" calc="hs7p-p5-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The speed of the star is the same for all its light, so every line shifts by the same fraction $v/c$.

<Step title="Same fraction, different shift">

$\Delta\lambda = 656.30 \times 0.08/486.10 = 0.108\ \text{nm}$, so the red line lies at $656.30 + 0.108 = 656.41\ \text{nm}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

From the speed: $656.30 \times 4.94 \times 10^{4}/(3.00 \times 10^{8}) = 0.108\ \text{nm}$, the same. </Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

How many alpha decays are there, written with $A_1$ and $A_2$? How much do they lower $Z$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-d" label="number of beta-minus decays $=$" expr="(A1 - A2)/2 - Z1 + Z2"
  vars={{ A1: [200, 240], A2: [200, 240], Z1: [80, 95], Z2: [80, 95] }}
  names={{ A1: "A_1", A2: "A_2", Z1: "Z_1", Z2: "Z_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(A1 - A2)/4", hint: "That is the number of alpha decays. Now find how much they lower Z, and compare with Z₂." },
    { expr: "Z1 - Z2 - (A1 - A2)/2", hint: "The sign is reversed: try it for the thorium chain, where the answer must be +4." },
    { expr: "(A1 - A2)/4 - Z1 + Z2", hint: "Each alpha decay lowers Z by 2, not by 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count the alpha decays from the mass numbers, then balance the atomic numbers with beta-minus decays.

<Step title="Balance A and Z">

<Formula label="Number of beta-minus decays in a decay chain" tex="n_\alpha = \frac{A_1 - A_2}{4}, \qquad Z_2 = Z_1 - 2n_\alpha + n_\beta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n_\beta = \frac{A_1 - A_2}{2} - Z_1 + Z_2" cite="reading7" at="transcript pp. 1–2, the alpha and beta-minus decay equations (our own step)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For thorium to lead: $(232 - 208)/2 - 90 + 82 = 12 - 8 = 4$, as in part (c). For uranium-238 ($Z = 92$) to lead-206: $(238 - 206)/2 - 92 + 82 = 16 - 10 = 6$ beta-minus decays, with $8$ alpha decays.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 9

<Exercise id="p9" title="An iodine tracer" points={11}>

Iodine-131 is a beta emitter with a half-life of $8.0\ \text{days}$, used in medicine. A hospital receives a sample with an activity of $400\ \text{MBq}$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is its activity $24\ \text{days}$ later?

<Answer id="p9-a" label="$A =$" value="50" unit="MBq" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p9-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count the half-lives; the activity halves in each.

<Step title="Three half-lives">

<Formula label="Radioactive decay law" tex="N = N_0\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/t_{1/2}}" cite="reading8" at="follows from transcript p. 24" />

$24/8.0 = 3$ half-lives, so the activity is $400 \times (1/2)^3 = 400/8 = 50\ \text{MBq}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Step by step: $400 \to 200 \to 100 \to 50\ \text{MBq}$. The activity falls in the same way as the number of nuclei, because $A = \lambda N$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the decay constant of iodine-131, in $\text{s}^{-1}$?

<Hint>

How is the decay constant related to the half-life? And how many seconds are $8.0\ \text{days}$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-b" label="$\lambda =$" value="1.00e-6" unit="s⁻¹" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p9-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The decay constant is the chance per second that one nucleus decays: $\ln 2$ divided by the half-life.

<Step title="Decay constant">

<Formula label="Decay constant and half-life" tex="\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}}" cite="reading8" at="transcript p. 24" />

$t_{1/2} = 8.0 \times 24 \times 3600 = 6.91 \times 10^{5}\ \text{s}$, so $\lambda = 0.693/(6.91 \times 10^{5}) = 1.00 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{s}^{-1}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Each nucleus has about a one-in-a-million chance of decaying in any one second. Units: $\ln 2$ is a pure number, so $\lambda$ is per second.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How many iodine-131 nuclei did the sample hold when it arrived?

<Hint>

$1\ \text{MBq}$ is $10^6$ decays per second. Which formula links the activity to the number of nuclei?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-c" label="$N_0 =$" value="3.99e14" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p9-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The activity is the number of nuclei times the chance per second that each decays.

<Step title="Number of nuclei">

<Formula label="Activity" tex="A = \lambda N" cite="reading8" at="transcript p. 23" />

$N_0 = A/\lambda = 4.00 \times 10^{8}/(1.00 \times 10^{-6}) = 3.99 \times 10^{14}$ nuclei (with the unrounded $\lambda = 1.003 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{s}^{-1}$).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Their mass: $3.99 \times 10^{14} \times 131 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 8.7 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{kg}$, less than a ten-millionth of a gram. A strong source can be an invisible speck of matter.

<Formula label="The atomic mass unit" tex="1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}" cite="siy43" at="How heavy is an atom?" />

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

How long after arrival has the activity fallen to $1.0\ \text{MBq}$?

<Hint>

By what factor must the activity fall? How many halvings is that?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-d" label="$t =$" value="69.2" unit="days" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p9-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The activity must fall by a factor $400$; find how many half-lives that takes with a logarithm.

<Step title="Number of half-lives">

<Formula label="Time from the fraction left" tex="\frac{t}{t_{1/2}} = \frac{\log\left(N/N_0\right)}{\log\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}" cite="reading8" at="follows from transcript p. 24 (our own step)" />

With $A/A_0 = 1.0/400$ in place of $N/N_0$: $t/t_{1/2} = \log(1/400)/\log(1/2) = 8.64$, so $t = 8.64 \times 8.0 = 69.2\ \text{days}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$2^8 = 256$ and $2^9 = 512$, so the factor $400$ needs between $8$ and $9$ half-lives, between $64$ and $72\ \text{days}$. $69.2\ \text{days}$ fits.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 10

<Exercise id="p10" title="A dose to a gland, and to a nurse" points={12}>

A patient is treated with iodine-131, which collects in the thyroid gland. Over the treatment its beta particles deposit $0.50\ \text{J}$ in the gland, which has a mass of $0.020\ \text{kg}$. The iodine also gives out gamma rays, which leave the body.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the absorbed dose in the gland?

<Answer id="p10-a" label="$D =$" value="25" unit="Gy" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p10-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The absorbed dose is the energy absorbed per kilogram of tissue.

<Step title="Absorbed dose">

<Formula label="Absorbed dose" tex="D = \frac{E}{m}" cite="reading9" at="p. 4" />

$D = 0.50/0.020 = 25\ \text{J/kg} = 25\ \text{Gy}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Half a joule would barely warm a cup of tea, but it is a large dose because it lands in a small gland. Here that is the purpose: the dose is aimed at one organ, and the rest of the body gets far less. Spread over the whole body, a dose this large would be deadly. Here it is given on purpose, under a doctor's control, to one small gland, to destroy thyroid tissue that is overactive or cancerous.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

What is the equivalent dose in the gland?

<Hint>

Which weighting factor belongs to beta particles?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-b" label="$H =$" value="25" unit="Sv" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p10-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The equivalent dose is the absorbed dose times the weighting factor of the kind of radiation.

<Step title="Equivalent dose">

<Formula label="Equivalent dose" tex="H = w_R\,D" cite="reading9" at="p. 4, with w_R = 1 for beta particles on p. 7" />

For beta particles $w_R = 1$, so $H = 1 \times 25 = 25\ \text{Sv}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Had the same energy come from alpha particles ($w_R = 20$), the equivalent dose would be $20 \times 25 = 500\ \text{Sv}$: per joule, alpha particles do far more harm. As in part (a), the $25\ \text{Sv}$ is a dose to one gland, planned by a doctor to destroy tissue; to the whole body a dose of that size would kill.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

A nurse standing $1.0\ \text{m}$ from the patient receives $12\ \text{μSv}$ per hour from the gamma rays. Treat the patient as a small source of gamma rays. What is the dose rate at $2.5\ \text{m}$?

<Hint>

How does the radiation from a small source spread out with distance?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-c" label="dose rate:" value="1.92" unit="μSv per hour" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p10-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The gamma rays spread over a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$, so the dose rate falls as $1/r^2$ (reading 10, p. 1).

<Step title="Inverse square">

$2.5$ times as far means $2.5^2 = 6.25$ times less: $12/6.25 = 1.92\ \text{μSv}$ per hour.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$(1.0/2.5)^2 = 0.16$, and $0.16 \times 12 = 1.92$. Stepping back a metre and a half cuts the dose rate by more than a factor $6$: distance is a cheap shield.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

The nurse spends a total of $8.0$ hours at $2.5\ \text{m}$ from the patient. What dose does the nurse receive? (The iodine's activity hardly changes in a day, as its half-life is $8.0\ \text{days}$.)

<Answer id="p10-d" label="dose:" value="15.4" unit="μSv" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p10-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Dose is dose rate times time.

<Step title="Dose">

$1.92 \times 8.0 = 15.4\ \text{μSv}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A natural background of about $2\ \text{mSv}$ a year (reading 13, p. 7) is $2000/365 = 5.5\ \text{μSv}$ a day, so the nurse gets about three days' worth of background ($15.4/5.5 = 2.8$).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

The nurse never touches the patient or anything the patient has used. After the shift, does the nurse give out radiation?

<Answer id="p10-e" label="After the shift the nurse:"
  choices={[
    "Gives out radiation for about 8 days, the half-life of iodine-131",
    "Gives out gamma rays, but no beta particles",
    "Gives out radiation until the next shower",
    "Gives out none: the nurse was irradiated, not contaminated"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Being hit by radiation does not make you radioactive; carrying the radioactive substance does.

<Step title="Irradiation against contamination">

The gamma rays passed through the nurse or were absorbed, and are gone. No iodine-131 got onto or into the nurse, so nothing is left to decay. The patient, who carries the iodine, is the one who gives out radiation for some weeks. Iodine also leaves the body in sweat, saliva and urine, which is why hospitals have rules about touching such a patient and the things the patient has used: they keep people from being contaminated.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 11

<Exercise id="p11" title="The cooling glow of the early universe" points={13}>

The cosmic microwave background reaches us from all directions as a nearly perfect black body at $2.725\ \text{K}$ (COBE FIRAS). It was sent out when the universe became transparent, by gas at about $3000\ \text{K}$. Since then every wavelength of this light has been stretched by the expansion of the universe.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

At what wavelength does the background radiation peak today? Give it in millimetres.

<Answer id="p11-a" label="$\lambda_{\max} =$" value="1.06" unit="mm" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p11-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The radiation is a black body, so Wien's law gives its peak.

<Step title="Wien's law">

$\lambda_{\max} = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}/2.725 = 1.06 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 1.06\ \text{mm}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A wavelength of about a millimetre: microwaves, hence the name. When it was sent out, at $3000\ \text{K}$, the peak lay at $2.9 \times 10^{-3}/3000 = 9.67 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 967\ \text{nm}$, in the near infrared.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the energy of a photon at today's peak wavelength, in electronvolts?

<Answer id="p11-b" label="$E =$" value="1.17e-3" unit="eV" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p11-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in Problem 2: $E = hc/\lambda$.

<Step title="Photon energy">

$E = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}/(1.06 \times 10^{-3}) = 1.87 \times 10^{-22}\ \text{J}$, and $1.87 \times 10^{-22}/(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 1.17 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{eV}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

About a thousandth of an electronvolt, far too little to ionise anything. A photon of the $967\ \text{nm}$ peak when the light was sent out had $1.29\ \text{eV}$, about $1100$ times more: stretching the wavelength lowers each photon's energy by the same factor.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

By what redshift $z$ has this light been stretched since it was sent out?

<Hint>

How do the temperature of a black body and the stretching of its wavelengths go together?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-c" label="$z =$" value="1100" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p11-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Stretching every wavelength by a factor $1 + z$ moves the peak by that factor, so the spectrum looks like a black body $1 + z$ times colder.

<Step title="Redshift">

<Formula label="Temperature of the background and redshift" tex="\frac{T_\text{then}}{T_\text{now}} = 1 + z" cite="reading11" at="p. 18 (the temperature falls as 1 over the scale factor), with p. 6 of Lecture 5 (1 + z is the ratio of the scale factors)" />

$1 + z = 3000/2.725 = 1101$, so $z = 1100$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The peaks of part (a) agree: $1.06\ \text{mm}/967\ \text{nm} = 1.064 \times 10^{-3}/(9.67 \times 10^{-7}) = 1101$. Since the light set out, the universe has grown about $1100$ times (reading 12, p. 6).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Light is sent out by a black body at temperature $T_\text{then}$ and reaches us with redshift $z$. Find the temperature $T_\text{now}$ of the black body we see, in terms of $T_\text{then}$ and $z$.

<Hint>

Is the light we see hotter or colder than when it was sent out? By what factor are its wavelengths longer?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-d" label="$T_\text{now} =$" expr="Tthen/(1 + z)"
  vars={{ Tthen: [1000, 10000], z: [0.1, 2000] }}
  names={{ Tthen: "T_\\text{then}" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Tthen*(1 + z)", hint: "Stretched light looks colder, not hotter: the peak moves to longer wavelengths." },
    { expr: "Tthen/z", hint: "The wavelengths are stretched by the factor 1 + z, not z. Try z = 0: nothing should change." },
    { expr: "Tthen/(1 + z)^4", hint: "The power per square metre falls with the fourth power, but the temperature itself falls with 1 + z." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Every wavelength is $1 + z$ times longer, so the peak is too, and by Wien's law the temperature is $1 + z$ times lower.

<Step title="Temperature now">

Every wavelength, the peak $\lambda_{\max}$ included, is $1 + z$ times longer now than when the light set out. By Wien's law the temperature is $2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}$ divided by $\lambda_{\max}$, so it is $1 + z$ times lower:

<Formula label="Temperature of redshifted black-body light" tex="T_\text{now} = \frac{T_\text{then}}{1 + z}" cite="wien" at="Problem 1(c), Wien's law applied before and after the stretching (our own step)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$3000/1101 = 2.725\ \text{K}$, the temperature measured today. With $z = 0$ (no stretching) $T_\text{now} = T_\text{then}$, as it should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

How many times more power did each square metre of the $3000\ \text{K}$ gas radiate than a black body at $2.725\ \text{K}$ does?

<Hint>

Which power of the temperature does the Stefan–Boltzmann law contain?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-e" label="ratio:" value="1.47e12" tolerance="3%" calc="hs7p-p11-e" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The power per square metre is $\sigma T^4$, so the ratio is the fourth power of the ratio of temperatures.

<Step title="Fourth power">

$(3000/2.725)^4 = 1101^4 = 1.47 \times 10^{12}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Directly: $\sigma \times 3000^4 = 4.59 \times 10^{6}\ \text{W/m}^2$, about twice as much as the surface of the red giant in Problem 6, against $\sigma \times 2.725^4 = 3.13 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{W/m}^2$ today. The ratio of the two is $1.47 \times 10^{12}$. The light that once filled the universe with an orange glow now arrives as a faint microwave hiss.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
