Everything astronomers know about the stars, they know from light. Nobody has ever visited a star, taken a sample of it or measured it with a ruler. And yet, as this block has shown, a beam of starlight spread out into a spectrum carries an astonishing amount of information. This last lesson of Block 7 brings it all together, then follows the same light out to the galaxies and to the oldest light in the universe.

## One beam, many messages

Point a telescope at a star, send its light through a spectrograph (lesson 7.5) and you get a rainbow crossed by dark lines. Here is what each part of it tells you, and which lesson showed how.

| What you measure in the light | What it tells you | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| where the continuous spectrum peaks | the surface temperature, by Wien's law $\lambda_{\max}T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}$ | 7.2, 7.6 |
| which dark lines appear | which elements the star contains, each with its own pattern of lines | 7.4, 7.5 |
| how far the lines have shifted | the speed along the line of sight, $\Delta\lambda/\lambda_\text{em} = v/c$ | 5.13, 7.5 |
| how bright it looks, plus its distance | its luminosity, and with the temperature its radius | 7.6, below |

The first three need nothing but the spectrum. The fourth needs one more thing: how far away the star is.

## From brightness to size

A star sends its power $L$ out equally in all directions. At a distance $d$ that power is spread over a sphere of area $4\pi d^2$, just like the sound of a loudspeaker in lesson 5.5. The power arriving on each square metre of a telescope, the **flux** $F$, is therefore

<Formula label="Flux at a distance d" tex="F = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2}" cite="mit" at="eqs. (3)–(4), p. 4: the flux falls as 1/r², because the same energy crosses every sphere around the source" />

Turn it round: if you measure the flux $F$ and know the distance $d$ (for nearby stars from parallax, Block 8), you know the luminosity, $L = 4\pi d^2 F$. And lesson 7.6 links luminosity, radius and temperature:

<Formula label="Luminosity of a star" tex="L = 4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4" cite="mit8902l8" at="p. 50, the Stefan–Boltzmann law above eq. (226)" />

The temperature comes from the colour. So the only unknown left is the radius:

<Formula label="Radius of a star from its luminosity and temperature" tex="R = \sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi\sigma T^4}}" cite="mit8902l8" at="p. 50, the Stefan–Boltzmann law above eq. (226), solved for R" />

<WorkedExample title="A whole star from one beam of light">
<Problem>
The spectrum of a star peaks at $725\ \text{nm}$. Its red hydrogen line, $656.3\ \text{nm}$ in the laboratory, is found at $656.19\ \text{nm}$. A telescope receives a flux of $2.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{W/m}^2$ from it, and its distance is $3.0 \times 10^{17}\ \text{m}$, about 32 light-years. Find its temperature, its motion, its luminosity and its radius.
</Problem>
<Step title="Temperature, from the peak">
$T = \dfrac{2.9 \times 10^{-3}}{725 \times 10^{-9}} = 4.0 \times 10^{3}\ \text{K}$: cooler than the Sun, so an orange star.
</Step>
<Step title="Motion, from the shifted line">
$\Delta\lambda = 656.19 - 656.3 = -0.11\ \text{nm}$, so $v = c\,\dfrac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_\text{em}} = 3.00 \times 10^{5}\ \text{km/s} \times \dfrac{-0.11}{656.3} = -50\ \text{km/s}$. The star comes towards us at about $50\ \text{km/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Luminosity, from brightness and distance">
$L = 4\pi d^2 F = 4\pi \times (3.0 \times 10^{17})^2 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-9} = 2.3 \times 10^{27}\ \text{W}$, about 6 times the Sun's $3.8 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Radius, from luminosity and temperature">
Each square metre gives off $\sigma T^4 = 5.67 \times 10^{-8} \times 4000^4 = 1.45 \times 10^{7}\ \text{W/m}^2$. Then $R = \sqrt{\dfrac{L}{4\pi\sigma T^4}} = 3.5 \times 10^{9}\ \text{m}$, about 5 times the radius of the Sun ($6.96 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}$). A star cooler than the Sun yet more luminous must be bigger: a small giant, above the main sequence in the HR diagram of lesson 7.6.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The same atoms everywhere

Look again at what made this work. The hydrogen line of that star sits, apart from a tiny shift, exactly where hydrogen puts it in a laboratory on Earth. The same is true for every star and every glowing cloud of gas that astronomers have examined, and for galaxies so far away that their light set out long before the Earth existed: the lines of hydrogen, helium, sodium and calcium all come in the same patterns, with the same spacings between them.

That is a discovery in its own right. The pattern of a hydrogen atom's lines follows from its energy levels (lesson 7.4), which are set by the charge and mass of the electron, Planck's constant and the laws of electricity. If any of those were different over there, the pattern would be different. It is not. Starlight shows that the atoms in the most distant galaxies are the same atoms as ours, obeying the same physics. That is what allows astronomers, and this course, to apply laws measured on Earth to the whole universe.

A galaxy's light is the light of its billions of stars added together: the hot blue ones, the cool red ones and everything in between, plus the glow of its gas clouds (MIT 8.902 notes, pp. 11–12). By taking the spectrum apart again, astronomers can tell what kinds of stars a galaxy contains.

## The galaxies' redshift

Lesson 5.13 introduced the redshift $z$ and showed that, for small $z$, it gives a speed. In the words of the university notes:

<Formula label="The redshift and the speed, for small z" tex="z = \frac{\lambda_\text{obs} - \lambda_\text{em}}{\lambda_\text{em}}, \qquad v \approx cz" cite="mit" at="eqs. (24) and (26), p. 11" />

The history of how this led to the expanding universe is worth telling carefully, because many people contributed. From 1912 Vesto Slipher, at the Lowell Observatory, measured the spectra of spiral "nebulae" and found that almost all of them were redshifted. In the 1920s Edwin Hubble showed that these nebulae are galaxies far beyond our own Milky Way and measured their distances. In 1927 the Belgian Georges Lemaître published, in French, a paper on an expanding universe, in which he knew from theory that speed should grow in proportion to distance and estimated the rate from other people's data. In 1929 Hubble, using mostly his own distances and velocities measured largely by Slipher, argued that the data themselves show this straight-line relation. It became known as **Hubble's law**:

<Formula label="Hubble's law" tex="v = H_0\,d" cite="mit" at="eqs. (25)–(26), p. 11 (for z much smaller than 1)" />

with $H_0$ close to $70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$ (lesson 5.13; one megaparsec is about 3.3 million light-years).

<WorkedExample title="A galaxy from two hydrogen lines">
<Problem>
In the spectrum of a galaxy the blue-green hydrogen line, $486.1\ \text{nm}$ in the laboratory, is found at $501.9\ \text{nm}$. Find its redshift and its speed, check where the red hydrogen line ($656.3\ \text{nm}$) should be, and estimate how far away the galaxy is with $H_0 = 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The redshift">
$z = \dfrac{501.9 - 486.1}{486.1} = \dfrac{15.8}{486.1} = 0.0325$.
</Step>
<Step title="The speed">
$z$ is small, so $v \approx cz = 0.0325 \times 3.00 \times 10^{5}\ \text{km/s} = 9750\ \text{km/s}$, away from us.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with the other line">
Every line is stretched by the same factor $1 + z$, so the red line should lie at $656.3 \times 1.0325 = 677.6\ \text{nm}$. If it is found there, the shift is real and the lines were identified correctly.
</Step>
<Step title="The distance">
$d = \dfrac{v}{H_0} = \dfrac{9750}{70}\ \text{Mpc} = 139\ \text{Mpc}$, or about 460 million light-years. The light we see left the galaxy roughly 460 million years ago: looking far away is looking back in time.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The oldest light

The farther we look, the earlier the light set out. The oldest light of all fills the whole sky: the **cosmic microwave background**. Its spectrum is a nearly perfect black body (lesson 7.2) with a temperature of $2.725\ \text{K}$, as measured by NASA's COBE satellite.

Where does it come from? As Guth explains in 8.286, the young universe was hot and filled with gas whose atoms were ionised: the free electrons kept scattering light, so the gas was opaque, like the inside of a star. As the universe expanded it cooled, and at about $3000\ \text{K}$, some 380 000 years after the Big Bang, the electrons were bound into atoms and the light could travel freely. We still receive that light today, so the background is an image of the universe at that moment.

Why is a $3000\ \text{K}$ glow now only $2.725\ \text{K}$? Because the universe has grown since. Lesson 5.13 showed that $1 + z$ is the factor by which the universe has grown while the light travelled, and that every wavelength is stretched by that factor. A black body stretched in this way stays a black body, with its peak moved to a longer wavelength, so by Wien's law it looks colder by the same factor:

<Formula label="Temperature of the background and redshift" tex="\frac{T_\text{then}}{T_\text{now}} = 1 + z" cite="reading2" at="p. 18 (the temperature falls as 1 over the scale factor), with p. 6 of Lecture 5 (1 + z is the ratio of the scale factors)" />

For the background, $1 + z = 3000/2.725 = 1100$. When it set out, its peak was at $(2.9 \times 10^{-3})/3000 = 970\ \text{nm}$, in the near infrared; stretched 1100 times, it now lies near $1.06\ \text{mm}$, in the microwave band, exactly where Wien's law puts the peak of a $2.725\ \text{K}$ black body.

## Hydrogen and helium everywhere

Spectra also tell **how much** of each element there is (lesson 7.5): from the strengths of the lines, once the temperature is known. Measured in the oldest stars and in clouds of gas in galaxies near and far, the answer is strikingly similar: the ordinary matter of the universe is about three quarters hydrogen and one quarter helium by mass, with only a little of everything else. Since a helium atom is about four times as heavy as a hydrogen atom (lesson 7.7), that means about 12 hydrogen atoms for every helium atom.

Remember that helium was first found in the light of the Sun (lesson 7.5). Now it turns out to be the second most common element in the whole universe. Where did it all come from? Stars do turn hydrogen into helium (Block 8), but that is not where most of it was made: Block 8 shows that most of the helium in the universe formed in the first few minutes after the Big Bang.

## Summary

- A star's spectrum gives its **temperature** (the peak, Wien's law), its **composition** (the lines) and its **speed along the line of sight** (the shift of the lines, $\Delta\lambda/\lambda_\text{em} = v/c$).
- With its distance, its brightness gives its **luminosity**: the flux is $F = L/(4\pi d^2)$. With the temperature, $L = 4\pi R^2\sigma T^4$ then gives its **radius**.
- The same line patterns appear in the nearest stars and the farthest galaxies: the **same atoms and the same physics** work everywhere.
- Galaxies are **redshifted**, the more so the farther away they are: Slipher measured the redshifts, Lemaître and Hubble linked them to distance, and Hubble argued from the data for $v = H_0 d$, with $v \approx cz$ for small $z$.
- The **cosmic microwave background** is the oldest light: a black body at $2.725\ \text{K}$, sent out at about $3000\ \text{K}$ some 380 000 years after the Big Bang and stretched by a factor $1 + z \approx 1100$ since.
- Spectra show that ordinary matter is about **three quarters hydrogen and one quarter helium** by mass, everywhere; Block 8 and the cosmology course 8.286 show how that helium was made in the first minutes.
