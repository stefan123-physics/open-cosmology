Lesson 7.8 found that radioactive nuclei give out alpha particles, beta particles and gamma rays, and that these radiations ionise the atoms in their way. That is why the word "radiation" makes people nervous. This lesson asks how much radiation does how much harm, how we measure it, where the radiation we all receive comes from, and how doctors put the same radiation to work. It is physics, not medical advice: questions about your own health belong with a doctor.

## Why ionising radiation harms cells

To ionise a hydrogen atom takes $13.6\ \text{eV}$ (lesson 7.4). An alpha particle from a radioactive nucleus carries a few MeV: one of $5\ \text{MeV}$ has some $370\,000$ times that energy. It cannot spend it all at once, so it ploughs on, knocking electrons out of atom after atom and leaving a trail of many thousands of ions, until it has nothing left. Beta particles, X-rays and gamma rays do the same, more thinly spread. Siyavula sums it up: ionising radiation breaks the bonds of molecules as it passes and leaves ions behind, and too much of it destroys living tissue (reading 5).

Not all radiation is ionising. Radio waves, microwaves, infrared and visible light carry too little energy per photon to free an electron (lesson 7.3). Ultraviolet light is at the border: it can still damage the DNA of skin cells, which is why too much sun can lead to skin cancer (reading 5). X-rays and gamma rays, with far more energy per photon, are fully ionising.

What matters in a living cell is its **DNA**, the long molecule that carries the instructions for making the cell and copying it. Radiation can damage it in two ways (reading 3, pp. 13–14):

- **directly**, by ionising the DNA molecule itself and breaking it;
- **indirectly**, by breaking up the water molecules around it. A cell is mostly water, so this is how most of the damage is done: the broken pieces of water are very reactive and attack the DNA nearby.

Cells repair most of this damage. If too much is broken, the cell dies. If the repair goes wrong but the cell survives, it may carry a changed instruction, a **mutation**, into every cell copied from it, and years later that can grow into a cancer (reading 1, p. 10). Cells that divide often, as in bone marrow, the gut, hair roots and growing children, are the most sensitive, because they copy their DNA most often (reading 1, pp. 9–10).

## Absorbed dose: the gray

The harm starts with energy taken in by the body. The **absorbed dose** $D$ is the energy $E$ absorbed per kilogram of tissue:

<Formula label="Absorbed dose" tex="D = \frac{E}{m}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4" />

Its unit is the **gray** ($\text{Gy}$): $1\ \text{Gy}$ is one joule absorbed per kilogram. Doses we meet in daily life are much smaller, so the milligray ($\text{mGy}$, a thousandth of a gray) is a common unit.

<WorkedExample title="Dose from a beam of gamma rays">
<Problem>
A person of mass $60\ \text{kg}$ stands in a broad beam of gamma rays, which deposit $0.012\ \text{J}$ in the whole body. What is the absorbed dose?
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy per kilogram">
$D = E/m = 0.012/60 = 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J/kg}$.
</Step>
<Step title="In gray">
That is $2.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{Gy}$, or $0.20\ \text{mGy}$. As the next sections show, it is about twice the dose of a chest X-ray.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

A gray is a tiny amount of energy as heat. The surprise is how much harm it can still do, because the energy is not spread evenly: it arrives in concentrated packets, each one strong enough to break molecules.

<WorkedExample title="Not a matter of heat">
<Problem>
A whole-body dose of about $3.5\ \text{Gy}$, received in a short time, kills about half of the people exposed if they are not treated (reading 4, p. 9). How much energy is that for a $60\ \text{kg}$ person, and how much would it warm the body if it all became heat? Take the body as water, which needs $4180\ \text{J}$ to warm $1\ \text{kg}$ by $1\ \text{K}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy">
$E = D\,m = 3.5 \times 60 = 210\ \text{J}$: the energy a $60\ \text{W}$ lamp uses in three and a half seconds.
</Step>
<Step title="Warming">
$210/(60 \times 4180) = 8.4 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{K}$, less than a thousandth of a degree. A sip of hot tea gives the body more heat. The harm comes from where the energy goes, into broken molecules, not from how much there is.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Equivalent dose: the sievert

The same absorbed dose does not always do the same harm. An alpha particle spends all its energy in a very short, dense track, so it breaks many molecules close together in the same cell, damage that is much harder to repair (reading 3, p. 13). Beta particles and gamma rays spread the same energy thinly. To compare risks, the absorbed dose is multiplied by a **radiation weighting factor** $w_R$, which gives the **equivalent dose** $H$:

<Formula label="Equivalent dose" tex="H = w_R\,D" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 4 and p. 10" />

Its unit is the **sievert** ($\text{Sv}$), with millisieverts ($\text{mSv}$) and microsieverts ($\text{μSv}$, a millionth of a sievert) for everyday doses. The weighting factors are rounded numbers, and the true values are uncertain (reading 1, p. 7):

| Radiation | Weighting factor $w_R$ |
|---|---|
| X-rays and gamma rays | 1 |
| beta particles (electrons and positrons) | 1 |
| alpha particles | 20 |

So for X-rays, gamma rays and beta particles, a dose of $1\ \text{Gy}$ is an equivalent dose of $1\ \text{Sv}$. For alpha particles, the same joule per kilogram counts 20 times as much (reading 1, p. 12; reading 2, p. 7). Neutrons, which you meet in nuclear reactors, have factors in between that depend on their energy (reading 1, p. 8).

<WorkedExample title="Alpha against gamma">
<Problem>
Radon gas (lesson 7.8) is breathed in, and its decay products stick in the lungs. Their alpha particles give a small piece of lung tissue an absorbed dose of $0.50\ \text{mGy}$. What is the equivalent dose? What would it be if the same energy had come from gamma rays?
</Problem>
<Step title="Alpha particles">
$H = w_R\,D = 20 \times 0.50 = 10\ \text{mSv}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Gamma rays">
$H = 1 \times 0.50 = 0.50\ \text{mSv}$. The same energy, taken in by the same tissue, counts 20 times as heavily when it comes as alpha particles.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The dose limits and the doses in the rest of this lesson are for the whole body. When only part of the body is hit, experts also weigh each organ by how sensitive it is and add the results into one **effective dose**, still in sieverts (reading 1, pp. 9–11). For a beginner the idea is enough: a sievert measures harm, not just energy.

## Radiation all around us

Everyone receives a dose from natural sources all the time, the **background radiation** of lesson 7.8. Its main parts (reading 2, pp. 5–14):

- **radon**, a radioactive gas from the decay of radium in rocks and soil, breathed in indoors, gives about half of the background. It collects in cellars and in houses on granite, so it varies a lot from place to place;
- **rocks and building materials**, which contain uranium, thorium and potassium-40 and give out gamma rays;
- **food and our own bodies**: each of us carries enough potassium-40 for about 4400 decays a second and enough carbon-14 for about 3700 (reading 2, p. 12);
- **cosmic rays**: fast particles from space that hit the upper atmosphere and make showers of secondary radiation. The atmosphere shields us, so the dose from them grows with height (reading 2, pp. 13–14).

Added up, this comes to a couple of millisieverts a year for a typical person, more in places with much radon (reading 2, p. 7). Medical scans add more for some people. The table gives a feel for the sizes; the values are rounded and vary from case to case.

| Source | Equivalent dose |
|---|---|
| a chest X-ray | about $0.1\ \text{mSv}$ (reading 2, p. 12) |
| natural background in a year | a couple of millisieverts (reading 2, p. 7) |
| a CT scan | about $10\ \text{mSv}$ (reading 2, p. 12) |
| limit for the public per year, not counting background and medicine | $1\ \text{mSv}$ (reading 2, pp. 3–4; EU directive, Art. 12) |
| limit for people who work with radiation, per year | $20$ to $50\ \text{mSv}$ depending on the country: $20$ in the EU (EU directive, Art. 9), $50$ in the US (reading 2, p. 3) |
| astronauts' doses (the range NASA gives) | $50$ to $2000\ \text{mSv}$ (NASA) |

<WorkedExample title="A long flight">
<Problem>
On the ground the dose rate from cosmic rays is about $0.03\ \text{μSv}$ per hour; on a long-distance flight it is about $3.7\ \text{μSv}$ per hour (reading 2, p. 13). Find the dose from a $10$-hour flight and compare it with ten hours on the ground and with a chest X-ray. A pilot flies $800$ hours a year: what dose is that?
</Problem>
<Step title="The flight">
$10 \times 3.7 = 37\ \text{μSv}$, or $0.037\ \text{mSv}$: about a third of a chest X-ray.
</Step>
<Step title="On the ground">
$10 \times 0.03 = 0.3\ \text{μSv}$. The flight gives $3.7/0.03 \approx 120$ times as much per hour, because there is far less air above the plane to shield it.
</Step>
<Step title="The pilot">
$800 \times 3.7 = 2960\ \text{μSv}$, about $3.0\ \text{mSv}$ a year: about as much as the natural background on the ground, but well below the limit for radiation workers, $20$ to $50\ \text{mSv}$ a year.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## How harmful is a dose?

For large doses received in a short time, the effects are well known from accidents and from the survivors of the atomic bombs (reading 4, p. 3). Below about a quarter of a gray, people notice almost nothing. Around $1\ \text{Gy}$ they become sick, with nausea and fewer blood cells, because the fast-dividing cells of the bone marrow and the gut are hit first. Around $3.5\ \text{Gy}$, about half of the people die without treatment (reading 4, pp. 6–9). With a background of about $2\ \text{mSv}$ a year, these doses are hundreds to a few thousand times the yearly background: a quarter of a gray of gamma rays is $250\ \text{mSv}$, about $125\ \text{years}$ of background, and $3.5\ \text{Gy}$ is about $1750\ \text{years}$.

For small doses, such as a few millisieverts, the main concern is a small extra chance of cancer many years later (reading 4, p. 6). That extra chance is so small that it cannot be measured, even in very large groups of people, because cancer is common anyway and the difference drowns in the statistics. Experts therefore disagree about how to estimate the risk of the smallest doses (reading 2, p. 5; reading 4, pp. 18–19). Radiation protection plays safe: it treats every dose as something to keep low, and sets limits far below the doses that are known to cause harm.

## Contamination and irradiation

Two words are often mixed up, but they mean very different things.

- You are **irradiated** when radiation from a source outside your body passes into you: an X-ray machine, a gamma source in a laboratory, cosmic rays. The dose stops as soon as the source is switched off, shielded or left behind. Being irradiated by X-rays or gamma rays does **not** make you radioactive.
- You are **contaminated** when radioactive material itself is on your skin or clothes, or inside you: dust breathed in, radon gas, food containing radioactive atoms. The material keeps giving out radiation wherever it goes, until it is washed off, leaves the body, or has decayed (lesson 7.9).

This is why food is sometimes deliberately irradiated with gamma rays from cobalt-60, to kill bacteria. The food is not made radioactive and gives out no radiation afterwards. Neutrons are never used, because they can turn nuclei in the food into radioactive ones (reading 7, pp. 9–12).

Contamination is what makes alpha emitters dangerous. Outside the body their alpha particles are stopped by the dead outer layer of the skin. Breathed in or swallowed, the same atoms sit right against living cells, and each alpha particle delivers all its energy, with a weighting factor of 20, into a tiny volume of tissue (reading 1, p. 9).

## Protection: time, distance, shielding

To keep a dose from a source outside the body low, there are three simple rules.

**Time.** The dose is the dose rate times the time spent near the source. Half the time, half the dose.

**Distance.** A small source sends its gamma rays out equally in all directions. At a distance $r$ they are spread over a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$, just like the sound from a small loudspeaker in lesson 5.5. So the dose rate falls with the square of the distance: twice as far gives a quarter, three times as far a ninth (reading 6, p. 1). Stepping back is one of the cheapest forms of protection. (Alpha and beta particles are stopped by the air itself within centimetres or metres, so for them distance helps even more.)

**Shielding.** Lesson 7.8 showed what stops each kind of radiation: paper or skin for alpha, a few millimetres of aluminium or plastic for beta, thick lead, concrete or water to weaken gamma rays. That is why an X-ray technician stands behind a lead-lined screen, and why stored radioactive sources sit in lead containers.

<WorkedExample title="Stepping back">
<Problem>
A technician works for $3.0$ hours at $0.50\ \text{m}$ from a small gamma source, where the dose rate is $40\ \text{μSv}$ per hour. What dose does she get? What would it be if she did the same work from $2.0\ \text{m}$, using long tongs?
</Problem>
<Step title="Close by">
$40 \times 3.0 = 120\ \text{μSv}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Four times as far">
$2.0\ \text{m}$ is four times as far, so the dose rate is $4^2 = 16$ times smaller: $40/16 = 2.5\ \text{μSv}$ per hour. In $3.0$ hours: $2.5 \times 3.0 = 7.5\ \text{μSv}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
The dose drops from $120$ to $7.5\ \text{μSv}$. Halving the working time as well would halve it again. A lead screen in between would lower it further.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Radiation in medicine

The same properties that make radiation harmful make it useful in hospitals.

- **X-ray images.** X-rays get through the body, so they can be used to look inside it (reading 5). They pass through skin and soft tissue more easily than through bone, so a picture of the X-rays that get through shows the bones. A chest X-ray gives about $0.1\ \text{mSv}$.
- **CT scans.** An X-ray tube turns around the patient and a computer builds up slices through the body. The pictures are far more detailed, and the dose is larger, about $10\ \text{mSv}$ for a scan of the body (reading 2, p. 12).
- **Tracers.** A small amount of a radioactive isotope, often technetium, is injected and collects in one organ or flows with the blood. Its gamma rays get out of the body, and a camera outside shows where it went and how the organ is working (reading 2, p. 13). A short half-life (lesson 7.9) means the activity soon dies away.
- **Radiotherapy.** Beams of high-energy X-rays, or of protons, are aimed at a tumour from several directions, so that the tumour gets a very large dose while the healthy tissue around it gets much less (reading 1, pp. 16–17). Cancer cells divide fast, so they are hit harder than most healthy cells (reading 3, p. 14).

Medical doses are not counted against the dose limits, because they are given for the patient's own benefit (reading 2, p. 4). A scan that finds a blood clot, a broken bone or a tumour today is worth a very small extra chance of harm many years from now. Doctors still weigh each scan against what it can show, and use no more dose than the picture needs. That weighing is a decision for the patient and the doctor, case by case.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Radiation from the galaxy">
On the ground, Earth's magnetic field and the thick layer of air above us shield us from most of the radiation in space. Astronauts have no such shelter. They are hit by particles trapped in Earth's magnetic field, by bursts of protons from the Sun, and by **galactic cosmic rays**: bare nuclei of every element from hydrogen to uranium, moving at nearly the speed of light, probably sped up in the remains of exploded stars (supernova remnants) in our own galaxy. They go almost straight through a spacecraft wall, and NASA gives the doses astronauts receive as $50$ to $2000\ \text{mSv}$ (NASA, Why Space Radiation Matters). Those same cosmic rays, reaching our atmosphere, make the carbon-14 used in dating (lesson 7.9; reading 2, p. 15).
</Callout>

<WorkedExample title="Astronaut doses in years of background">
<Problem>
Take a background of $2\ \text{mSv}$ a year. How many years of background equal the smallest and the largest astronaut doses in the table, $50\ \text{mSv}$ and $2000\ \text{mSv}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="Smallest">
$50/2 = 25\ \text{years}$ of background.
</Step>
<Step title="Largest">
$2000/2 = 1000\ \text{years}$. For comparison, the largest dose is $20\,000$ chest X-rays of $0.1\ \text{mSv}$, in line with NASA's comparison of about ten chest X-rays per millisievert.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Ionising radiation knocks electrons out of atoms and breaks molecules. Damage to **DNA**, directly or through broken-up water around it, can kill a cell or leave a mutation that may later lead to cancer; cells repair most damage, and fast-dividing cells are the most sensitive.
- The **absorbed dose** $D = E/m$ is the energy absorbed per kilogram, in **gray**: $1\ \text{Gy} = 1\ \text{J/kg}$. A lethal dose is only a few joules per kilogram: the harm is in the broken molecules, not in heat.
- The **equivalent dose** $H = w_R D$, in **sievert**, counts how harmful each radiation is: $w_R = 1$ for X-rays, gamma rays and beta particles, $20$ for alpha particles.
- **Background radiation** from radon (about half), rocks, food, our own bodies and cosmic rays gives a couple of millisieverts a year; a chest X-ray is about $0.1\ \text{mSv}$, a CT scan about $10\ \text{mSv}$, a long flight a few hundredths of a millisievert.
- Large doses (around a gray and more) cause radiation sickness; the risk from small doses is too small to measure, so protection keeps every dose low.
- **Irradiated** means exposed to radiation from outside, which stops when you leave; **contaminated** means radioactive material is on or in you. Alpha emitters are most dangerous inside the body.
- **Time, distance, shielding**: less time, more distance (for a small gamma source, the dose rate falls with the square of the distance) and the right material in between.
- Medicine uses radiation for X-ray images, CT scans, tracers and radiotherapy, where the benefit outweighs a small risk. Astronauts, outside the shield of air and magnetic field, receive doses of $50$ to $2000\ \text{mSv}$ from space radiation (the range NASA gives).
