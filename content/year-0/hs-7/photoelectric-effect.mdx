Shine ultraviolet light on a clean piece of zinc and the zinc gives off electrons. Shine red light on it, even a very bright red lamp, and nothing happens. This simple experiment, the **photoelectric effect**, did not fit the picture of light as a wave, and explaining it led Einstein to the idea that light comes in packets. The previous lesson met those packets, photons, each carrying an energy $E = hf$; this lesson shows the experiment that made physicists take them seriously.

## The experiment

Heinrich Hertz noticed in 1887 that ultraviolet light falling on a metal plate could cause sparks. The electron had not yet been discovered (that came in 1897, lesson 7.1). Around the turn of the twentieth century, experiments by several physicists, Thomson and Lenard among them, showed what was happening: light shone on a metal knocks electrons out of it. The electrons freed by light are called **photoelectrons**, and if they are collected they form an electric current, the **photocurrent**.

A modern version of the experiment uses a glass tube with the air pumped out. Inside is a metal plate, the one that is lit, and a second plate that collects the electrons. A meter in the circuit between them measures the current. You can change three things: the **frequency** (colour) of the light, its **intensity** (brightness), and the metal. A current flows even with no battery in the circuit: the freed electrons leave the metal with some kinetic energy, and some of them fly across to the collecting plate by themselves.

Inside a metal, some electrons are free to move around, but they cannot simply leave it: the metal holds them in, a little like gravity holds a ball on the Earth. To get one out you have to give it a minimum amount of energy. That minimum is the **work function** $W_0$ of the metal. It is different for different metals and also depends on how clean the surface is.

## What a wave would do, and what really happens

If light is a wave, its energy is spread out evenly over the wave front, and a brighter wave brings more energy per second to every part of the metal (lesson 6.12). That leads to three predictions:

- **Any colour should work**, given enough brightness or enough time: an electron can keep absorbing energy until it has collected $W_0$.
- **Dim light should need time.** With very faint light, an electron should take a while to soak up enough energy before it can escape.
- **Brighter light should give faster electrons**, because it delivers more energy to each one.

The experiments show none of this:

- **There is a threshold frequency** $f_0$. Below it no electrons come out at all, however bright the light and however long you wait. Above it they do, even in very dim light. The threshold depends on the metal.
- **There is no delay.** As soon as light above the threshold frequency arrives, electrons come out, even when the light is extremely dim.
- **The energy of the electrons depends on the frequency, not on the intensity.** Brighter light gives more electrons per second, a larger current, but each one leaves with the same largest kinetic energy. Raising the frequency raises that energy.

<WorkedExample title="How long would a wave make you wait?">
<Problem>
Dim light of intensity $1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{W/m}^2$ falls on a metal with a work function of $3.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$. Suppose the energy arrives as a smooth wave, and one electron can collect the energy falling on an area the size of an atom, about $1 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{m}^2$. Roughly how long would it take to collect enough energy to escape?
</Problem>
<Step title="Power arriving on the atom's area">
Intensity is power per square metre, so the power on that small area is $1.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 1 \times 10^{-19} = 1 \times 10^{-22}\ \text{W}$, that is $1 \times 10^{-22}$ joules every second.
</Step>
<Step title="Time to collect the work function">
$t = 3.6 \times 10^{-19} / 1 \times 10^{-22} = 3.6 \times 10^{3}\ \text{s}$, about an hour. In the real experiment the electrons appear at once. This is only a rough estimate, but no reasonable choice of numbers brings the wait down to zero.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Einstein's explanation: one photon, one electron

In 1905 Einstein proposed that light is not absorbed smoothly but in packets, each with energy

<Formula label="Energy of a photon" tex="E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 12.2, Implications of Einstein's model" />

where $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$ is Planck's constant. The key step is that **one photon gives all its energy to one electron**. Then everything falls into place:

- If $hf$ is less than $W_0$, no single photon can free an electron. More photons (brighter light) do not help, because an electron cannot add up the energy of several of them. That is the threshold.
- If $hf$ is more than $W_0$, the very first photon to arrive can free an electron. There is no waiting.
- Brighter light of the same colour means more photons per second, so more electrons per second; but each photon still carries the same $hf$, so each electron gets the same energy.

Energy is conserved in each single event. The photon's energy pays the work function, and what is left over becomes kinetic energy of the electron. Electrons that start a little deeper in the metal lose some extra energy on the way out, so the equation gives the **largest** kinetic energy, $E_{k,\max}$, that any electron can have:

<Formula label="Einstein's photoelectric equation" tex="hf = W_0 + E_{k,\max}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 12.2, Implications of Einstein's model" />

At the threshold the electrons only just get out, with no kinetic energy left: $E_{k,\max} = 0$. So the threshold frequency is set by the work function:

<Formula label="Threshold frequency" tex="W_0 = hf_0" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, eq. (1.2)" />

The longest wavelength that still works is then $\lambda_0 = c/f_0$. Einstein received the Nobel Prize in Physics for 1921 for this explanation.

<WorkedExample title="The threshold of a metal">
<Problem>
A metal has a work function of $3.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$. Find its threshold frequency and the longest wavelength that can eject electrons. Will red light of $650\ \text{nm}$ work? Will violet light of $400\ \text{nm}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="Threshold frequency">
$f_0 = W_0/h = 3.6 \times 10^{-19} / 6.63 \times 10^{-34} = 5.43 \times 10^{14}\ \text{Hz}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Longest wavelength">
$\lambda_0 = c/f_0 = 3.00 \times 10^{8} / 5.43 \times 10^{14} = 5.52 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 552\ \text{nm}$, green light.
</Step>
<Step title="Red and violet">
Red light at $650\ \text{nm}$ has a longer wavelength, so a lower frequency than the threshold: no electrons, however bright. Violet light at $400\ \text{nm}$ has a shorter wavelength, so a higher frequency: electrons come out.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="How fast are the electrons?">
<Problem>
The violet light of $400\ \text{nm}$ falls on the same metal ($W_0 = 3.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$). Find the largest kinetic energy and the largest speed of the photoelectrons.
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy of one photon">
$f = c/\lambda = 3.00 \times 10^{8} / 4.00 \times 10^{-7} = 7.5 \times 10^{14}\ \text{Hz}$, so $hf = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 7.5 \times 10^{14} = 4.97 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="What is left for the electron">
$E_{k,\max} = hf - W_0 = 4.97 \times 10^{-19} - 3.6 \times 10^{-19} = 1.37 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed">
From $E_{k,\max} = \tfrac12 m_e v^2$ with $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$: $v = \sqrt{2 \times 1.37 \times 10^{-19} / 9.11 \times 10^{-31}} = 5.5 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m/s}$, about 550 kilometres per second.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Energies in electronvolts

Energies of single photons and electrons are tiny in joules, so the **electronvolt** of lesson 6.4 is handier: $1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$. The work function of the metal above is $3.6 \times 10^{-19} / 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 2.25\ \text{eV}$, and the violet photons carry $4.97 \times 10^{-19} / 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3.1\ \text{eV}$. Work functions of metals are a few electronvolts, which is why visible and ultraviolet photons, of a few electronvolts each, are the ones that matter.

## Measuring the energy: the stopping voltage

How do we know how fast the electrons are? Connect a battery so that the collecting plate is **negative** compared with the lit metal. Now the electrons have to climb against the electric field to reach it. In lesson 6.4 a charge $e$ that moves through a voltage $V$ gains or loses an energy $eV$. Raise the battery voltage step by step: the current gets smaller, and at one voltage even the fastest electrons are just turned back and the current drops to zero. That voltage is the **stopping voltage** $V_s$. Applying "energy = charge × voltage" from lesson 6.4 to the fastest electrons, which just lose all their kinetic energy, gives

<Formula label="Stopping voltage" tex="eV_s = E_{k,\max}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 12.2, Units of energy (E = q × V); applied here by this page to the stopping voltage" />

Measured in electronvolts, the largest kinetic energy is simply the stopping voltage: if $V_s = 0.86\ \text{V}$, then $E_{k,\max} = 0.86\ \text{eV}$. For the violet light of the last example, $V_s = 1.37 \times 10^{-19} / 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 0.86\ \text{V}$.

<WorkedExample title="Finding the work function from a measurement">
<Problem>
Ultraviolet light of frequency $1.5 \times 10^{15}\ \text{Hz}$ falls on an unknown metal. The current stops when the reverse voltage reaches $2.4\ \text{V}$. Find the work function and the threshold frequency of the metal.
</Problem>
<Step title="Photon energy">
$hf = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 1.5 \times 10^{15} = 9.95 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$, or $9.95 \times 10^{-19}/1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 6.22\ \text{eV}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Kinetic energy from the stopping voltage">
$E_{k,\max} = eV_s = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.4 = 3.84 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$, that is $2.4\ \text{eV}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Work function and threshold">
$W_0 = hf - E_{k,\max} = 9.95 \times 10^{-19} - 3.84 \times 10^{-19} = 6.1 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$, about $3.8\ \text{eV}$. Then $f_0 = W_0/h = 6.1 \times 10^{-19} / 6.63 \times 10^{-34} = 9.2 \times 10^{14}\ \text{Hz}$, in the near ultraviolet.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The graph of energy against frequency

Rewrite the photoelectric equation as $E_{k,\max} = hf - W_0$. Plotted against the frequency, this is a **straight line**:

- its **slope** is Planck's constant $h$, the same for every metal;
- it crosses the frequency axis at the threshold frequency $f_0$;
- continued backwards (dashed below), it would cross the energy axis at $-W_0$.

Below $f_0$ there are no electrons, so the real data stop there. Change the work function with the slider: the line moves sideways but keeps its slope.

In 1915 Robert Millikan measured such lines carefully for several metals. He found them straight, as Einstein had predicted, and from their slope he obtained Planck's constant to better than one per cent. Even so, many physicists still doubted that light was really made of particles.

## Try it

Choose a metal and light it. First lower the frequency until the electrons stop, and note the threshold; then raise the intensity below that frequency and see that nothing changes. Above the threshold, change the intensity and watch the number of electrons change, then change the frequency and watch their speed change. The battery between the two plates can be set positive or negative: set it negative, watch the current against voltage graph, and find the voltage at which the current just drops to zero. That is the stopping voltage.

## Wave or particle?

Lesson 5.11 showed light making interference fringes, which only waves can do. The photoelectric effect shows light delivering its energy in separate packets, like particles. Both are true: light travels like a wave, spreading and interfering, but it gives up its energy one photon at a time. This double behaviour is called **wave–particle duality**. At university you will find that the same holds the other way round: electrons, which we think of as particles, also make interference patterns.

## Summary

- Light can knock electrons out of a metal: the **photoelectric effect**. Freeing one electron takes at least the **work function** $W_0$ of the metal.
- Three observations do not fit light as a wave: there is a **threshold frequency** below which nothing happens however bright the light; electrons come out **without delay**; their energy depends on the **frequency**, not on the intensity.
- Einstein: light comes in photons of energy $hf$, and one photon frees one electron. Energy conservation gives $hf = W_0 + E_{k,\max}$, and the threshold is $f_0 = W_0/h$.
- Brighter light means more photons, so more electrons per second; higher frequency means more energy per electron.
- A reverse **stopping voltage** $V_s$ just stops the fastest electrons: $eV_s = E_{k,\max}$.
- A graph of $E_{k,\max}$ against $f$ is a straight line with slope $h$ for every metal, crossing the frequency axis at $f_0$.
- Light behaves as a wave (interference) and as particles (the photoelectric effect): **wave–particle duality**.
