Nobody has ever been to a star or brought back a piece of one. Yet astronomers can tell you what the stars are made of, how hot their surfaces are and how fast they move. All of this comes from one thing: the light of the star, spread out into its wavelengths. Lesson 7.4 showed why an atom gives light only at certain wavelengths: its electrons can only jump between fixed energy levels. This lesson shows how astronomers turn that fact into a way of reading the light of the Sun and the stars.

## Spreading light out: the spectroscope

A **spectrum** is light sorted by wavelength: how much light there is at each wavelength. The instrument that makes it is a **spectroscope**. Light enters through a narrow **slit**, a lens makes the beam parallel, and a **prism** (lesson 5.9) or a **diffraction grating** (lesson 5.11) sends each wavelength off at its own angle. A second lens makes an image of the slit for every wavelength. Because the slit is narrow, each wavelength gives a thin line, and the images of all the wavelengths side by side form the spectrum.

When the spectrum is recorded, on a photograph in the past and with a camera or electronic detector today, the instrument is called a **spectrograph**. Astronomers put spectrographs behind their telescopes. The result is often drawn as a graph: brightness up, wavelength along the bottom. Bright lines become peaks, dark lines become dips.

The same idea works far outside visible light. Spectra are measured for radio waves, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays (lesson 5.8), each with its own kind of instrument.

## Three kinds of spectra

In 1859 the German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff summed up, in three rules, what kind of spectrum appears where.

1. A **hot solid, liquid or dense gas** gives light of all wavelengths: a **continuous spectrum**, a smooth rainbow. The glowing filament of an old light bulb and the hot, dense inside of a star are examples. Its shape depends on the temperature, as lesson 7.2 showed.
2. A **hot, thin gas** gives light only at a few sharp wavelengths: an **emission spectrum**, bright lines on a dark background. A discharge tube (lesson 7.4) and a glowing cloud of gas in space are examples.
3. A **cooler, thin gas in front of a hotter source** of continuous light takes out some wavelengths: an **absorption spectrum**, a rainbow crossed by dark lines.

Rules 2 and 3 belong together: as lesson 7.4 showed, a gas absorbs at the same wavelengths at which it emits. And each element has its own set of lines, like a fingerprint.

Which kind you see also depends on where you stand. Look at a cool cloud of gas against a bright star behind it, and you see dark lines. Look at the same cloud from the side, with no star behind it, and you see the light the atoms send out again after absorbing: bright lines on a dark sky.

## The dark lines in sunlight

In 1802 the English scientist William Wollaston passed sunlight through a prism and noticed a few dark lines in the rainbow. In 1817 the German optician Joseph von Fraunhofer found them again with much better instruments, measured hundreds of them and labelled the strongest with letters. They are still called the **Fraunhofer lines**.

Kirchhoff's rules explain them. The deep, dense layers of the Sun are very hot and give a continuous spectrum. Above them lies thinner, cooler gas. Its atoms absorb the wavelengths of their own lines, so those wavelengths are missing from the light that reaches us: the Sun shows an **absorption spectrum**.

That makes the dark lines a list of ingredients. Kirchhoff compared them with the bright lines of elements heated in a flame in the laboratory. A flame with a little table salt in it, for example, glows yellow at two wavelengths close together, $589.0$ and $589.6\ \text{nm}$: the lines of **sodium**. The Sun's spectrum has two dark lines at exactly those wavelengths (Fraunhofer's "D" lines), so the Sun contains sodium. In the same way iron and many other elements were found in the Sun.

## Helium: found in the Sun first

During a total eclipse of the Sun in 1868, the French astronomer Pierre Janssen pointed a spectroscope at the glowing gas at the edge of the hidden Sun. He saw bright lines, among them a yellow one at about $587.6\ \text{nm}$. At first sight it looked like the sodium lines, but it was not at their wavelengths: it lay about $1.4\ \text{nm}$ away from the nearer of the two. No element known on Earth gave a line there.

The British astronomer Norman Lockyer, who studied the same line, concluded that it came from an element not yet known on Earth, and it was named **helium**, after *helios*, the Greek word for the Sun. For years helium was known only from the Sun's spectrum. In 1895 the Scottish chemist William Ramsay found it on Earth, in gas given off by a uranium mineral, and its lines matched the yellow line of 1868. Block 8 shows where most of the helium in the universe was made: in its first few minutes.

## What else the spectrum tells

The lines tell which elements a star contains. The spectrum tells much more.

- **Temperature.** The continuous light of a star has the black-body shape of lesson 7.2, so where its peak lies gives the temperature of the star's surface (lesson 7.6 does this for the colours of stars). The lines tell it too: how strong each line is depends on how many atoms are in the right energy level to absorb it, and that depends on the temperature. In a very hot star much of the hydrogen is ionised, in a cool one most of its electrons sit in the ground state, and in both cases its visible lines are weak, because they are absorbed only by atoms whose electron is in the second level (lesson 7.4).
- **How much of each element.** A stronger line usually means more atoms of that element, once the temperature is taken into account.
- **Motion.** If the star moves towards us or away from us, all its lines shift (lesson 5.13). Lines that are broadened tell that the gas moves in different directions at once, as in a spinning star or a wind of gas blowing off it.
- **Other conditions.** The detailed shape of the lines also tells the density of the gas and the strength of magnetic fields, and lines that change over time show that the star itself is changing.

## Motion: the Doppler shift of lines

Lesson 5.13 showed that the light of a source moving away arrives with longer wavelengths (a redshift), and the light of one moving towards us with shorter wavelengths (a blueshift). Spectral lines make this measurable: you know where a line of hydrogen or helium should be from the laboratory, so you can see how far it has moved. Write $\lambda_\text{em}$ for the laboratory wavelength, $\lambda_\text{obs}$ for the wavelength we observe and $\Delta\lambda = \lambda_\text{obs} - \lambda_\text{em}$ for the shift. For a speed $v$ along the line of sight much smaller than the speed of light $c$,

<Formula label="Doppler shift of a spectral line, for v much smaller than c" tex="\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_\text{em}} = \frac{v}{c}" cite="mit3" at="pp. 3–5 (slow motion, z ≈ v/u) and p. 19 (the small-β approximation is our own step)" />

This is $z \approx v/c$ of lesson 5.13, since $z = \Delta\lambda/\lambda_\text{em}$. Here $v$ is positive for a source moving away (the line moves to longer wavelengths) and negative for one moving towards us. Turned round, it gives the speed:

<Formula label="Speed from the shift of a line" tex="v = c\,\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_\text{em}}" cite="mit3" at="pp. 3–5 and p. 19 (the small-β approximation is our own step)" />

Every line of the star shifts by the same fraction, so astronomers check the result with several lines at once. Only motion along the line of sight counts: a star moving sideways across the sky shows no shift. Move the slider of the graph above to see the whole pattern of dips slide; even at $9000\ \text{km/s}$, $3$ % of the speed of light, the red hydrogen line moves only about $20\ \text{nm}$.

<WorkedExample title="A star with an invisible partner">
<Problem>
In the laboratory the stronger sodium line lies at $589.0\ \text{nm}$. In the spectrum of a star it is measured at $589.12\ \text{nm}$ one night and at $588.88\ \text{nm}$ a few nights later. Some nights after that it is back at $589.12\ \text{nm}$, and the pattern repeats. What is happening?
</Problem>
<Step title="First night">
$\Delta\lambda = 589.12 - 589.0 = +0.12\ \text{nm}$, so $v = c\,\Delta\lambda/\lambda_\text{em} = 3.00 \times 10^{5}\ \text{km/s} \times 0.12/589.0 = +61\ \text{km/s}$. The star moves away from us at $61\ \text{km/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="A few nights later">
$\Delta\lambda = 588.88 - 589.0 = -0.12\ \text{nm}$, so $v = -61\ \text{km/s}$: now it moves towards us at the same speed.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with the other sodium line">
The line at $589.6\ \text{nm}$ must shift by the same fraction: $589.6 \times 0.12/589.0 = 0.12\ \text{nm}$, so it should be found at $589.72\ \text{nm}$ on the first night. Finding the same speed from both lines shows that the shift is real.
</Step>
<Step title="Interpretation">
A star that swings back and forth, $61\ \text{km/s}$ away and then $61\ \text{km/s}$ towards us, in a regular rhythm, is moving in an orbit. It must orbit a companion, even if the companion is too faint to see. The middle of the swing is $(589.12 + 588.88)/2 = 589.00\ \text{nm}$, exactly the laboratory value: the pair as a whole neither comes closer nor moves away. From the speed and the time of one swing, astronomers can work out the size of the orbit and the masses of the stars, with the laws of orbits of Block 2.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Shifts like these, a tenth of a nanometre or less, are measured routinely. The same method has found many planets around other stars: a planet pulls its star in a small orbit too, and the star's lines swing back and forth by a tiny amount.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Lines in the light of galaxies">
A galaxy is made of billions of stars and clouds of gas, and its light carries their lines: hydrogen, helium, calcium, sodium and more. In the light of almost every galaxy the whole pattern is shifted to longer wavelengths, the more so the farther away the galaxy is. Lesson 5.13 showed that this redshift was the first strong evidence that the universe is expanding, and lesson 7.11 brings together everything the light of stars and galaxies tells us.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A **spectroscope** spreads light into its wavelengths with a slit and a prism or grating; a **spectrograph** records the spectrum, behind a telescope.
- **Kirchhoff's rules**: a hot solid, liquid or dense gas gives a **continuous spectrum**; a hot thin gas gives bright **emission lines**; a cooler gas in front of a hotter source gives dark **absorption lines**, at the same wavelengths as that gas's emission lines.
- The dark **Fraunhofer lines** in sunlight come from cooler gas above the Sun's hot surface; matching them with laboratory lines (such as sodium's at $589.0$ and $589.6\ \text{nm}$) shows which elements the Sun contains.
- **Helium** was found in the Sun's spectrum in 1868, as a yellow line at $587.6\ \text{nm}$ that matched no known element, and on Earth only in 1895.
- A star's spectrum tells its **composition**, its **temperature** and its **motion**: the lines shift by $\Delta\lambda/\lambda_\text{em} = v/c$ for $v \ll c$, to longer wavelengths for a source moving away. A regular swing of the lines shows a star orbiting a companion.
