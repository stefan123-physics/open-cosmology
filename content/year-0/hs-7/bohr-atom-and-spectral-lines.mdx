Put hydrogen gas in a glass tube, pass an electric current through it, and the tube glows a pinkish red. Look at that glow through a prism or a diffraction grating (lesson 5.11) and the light does not spread into a rainbow: you see a few sharp, separate lines of colour on a dark background. Every element has its own pattern of lines. In lesson 7.1 the atom turned out to be a tiny nucleus with electrons around it; this lesson shows how Niels Bohr used the lines to find out how those electrons are arranged, with the photons of lesson 7.2.

## Line spectra: emission and absorption

A hot solid or a hot dense gas, such as the glowing filament of an old light bulb, gives off light of every colour: a **continuous spectrum**. A hot thin gas is different. In a **discharge tube**, a glass tube filled with a gas at low pressure, a high voltage between metal plates at the ends drives a current through the gas. The current gives the atoms energy, and the atoms give it back as light, but only at certain wavelengths. Spread out, that light is a set of bright lines: an **emission spectrum** (or line spectrum).

Now do the opposite. Shine white light, which contains every visible wavelength, through a cool gas and spread out what comes through. You see a rainbow crossed by thin dark lines: an **absorption spectrum**. The gas has taken out light at just those wavelengths. For any one element, the dark lines of its absorption spectrum sit at **exactly the same wavelengths** as the bright lines of its emission spectrum.

Because every element has its own pattern, a spectrum works like a fingerprint: the lines tell you which atoms are present, even in a gas far away. Lesson 7.5 shows how astronomers use this; here we ask where the lines come from.

## Why Rutherford's atom is not enough

In Rutherford's atom (lesson 7.1) the electrons orbit the nucleus like planets around the Sun. That picture has two problems.

- An orbiting electron moves in a circle, so it is accelerating, and an accelerating charge sends out electromagnetic waves (lesson 6.12). It would lose energy all the time and spiral into the nucleus in a tiny fraction of a second. Atoms would not be stable.
- While spiralling in, its orbit would shrink smoothly, so it would give off a smooth smear of all wavelengths, not sharp lines.

Real atoms are stable, and they give sharp lines. Something in the picture had to change.

## Bohr's postulates

Bohr kept the nucleus and the orbiting electron, but added rules that classical physics did not give. He simply **postulated** them (assumed them because they work):

1. **Allowed orbits.** The electron can only move in certain orbits around the nucleus, each with its own fixed energy. These energies are the atom's **energy levels**, numbered $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ from the lowest up. In an allowed orbit the electron does **not** radiate.
2. **Jumps.** The electron can jump from one allowed level to another. It **emits** a photon when it drops to a lower level, and it can **absorb** a photon to jump up to a higher level. In between, it is never found.
3. **Photon energy.** The photon carries exactly the energy difference between the two levels.

Combined with Planck's rule for the energy of one photon, $E = hf$ (lesson 7.2), the third postulate reads

<Formula label="Photon energy in a jump between two levels" tex="hf = E_\text{upper} - E_\text{lower}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 12.3, Emission spectra" />

Since $c = f\lambda$ (lesson 5.8), the wavelength of the photon is

<Formula label="Wavelength of the photon" tex="\lambda = \frac{c}{f} = \frac{hc}{E_\text{upper} - E_\text{lower}}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 12.3, worked example: E = hc/λ" />

This explains both kinds of spectrum at once. Only certain energy gaps exist, so only certain photon energies, and so only certain wavelengths, can be emitted: bright lines. A photon whose energy matches one of those gaps can be absorbed; a photon whose energy matches no gap passes through. So a cool gas removes the same wavelengths that the hot gas emits: dark lines in the same places.

The lowest level, $n = 1$, is the **ground state**; the atom normally sits there. A level above it is an **excited state**. An excited atom drops back down within a tiny fraction of a second, either straight to the ground state or in several smaller jumps, giving off a photon at each jump.

## The energy levels of hydrogen

Hydrogen, with one electron around one proton, is the simplest atom. Its energy levels are

<Formula label="Energy levels of hydrogen" tex="E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots" cite="reading2" at="p. 7, eq. (2.31), with p. 4, eq. (2.4)" />

Bohr obtained this formula by adding one more rule about which orbits are allowed (it fixes their size); we do not need that rule here. At university the same energies come out of quantum mechanics, as in MIT's 8.04 notes cited here, where $13.6\ \text{eV}$ is half of the energy scale $27.2\ \text{eV}$ of the hydrogen atom. The electronvolt (lesson 7.2) is $1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$.

The energies are **negative**. The zero of energy is chosen for an electron that has just escaped and is at rest far from the proton. A bound electron has less energy than that, so its energy is below zero, and the deeper it sits, the more energy it takes to pull it free. Working out the first levels:

| Level | Energy $E_n = -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$ |
|---|---|
| $n = 1$ (ground state) | $-13.6\ \text{eV}$ |
| $n = 2$ | $-13.6/4 = -3.40\ \text{eV}$ |
| $n = 3$ | $-13.6/9 = -1.51\ \text{eV}$ |
| $n = 4$ | $-13.6/16 = -0.85\ \text{eV}$ |
| $n = 5$ | $-13.6/25 = -0.54\ \text{eV}$ |
| $n = 6$ | $-13.6/36 = -0.38\ \text{eV}$ |
| very large $n$ | close to $0$ (the electron is free) |

An **energy-level diagram** draws each level as a horizontal line at its energy, with arrows for the jumps: a downward arrow for an emitted photon, an upward arrow for an absorbed one. The longer the arrow, the more energetic the photon. For hydrogen the gap from $n = 1$ to $n = 2$ is large ($10.2\ \text{eV}$), and the higher levels crowd closer and closer together just below zero.

## The Balmer lines: hydrogen's visible light

Hydrogen's lines fall into families, called **series**, according to the level the electron lands on.

- Jumps down to $n = 1$ (the **Lyman series**) have large energies, at least $10.2\ \text{eV}$: ultraviolet.
- Jumps down to $n = 2$ (the **Balmer series**) have energies of about $1.9$ to $3.4\ \text{eV}$: the first four are **visible light**.
- Jumps down to $n = 3$ (the **Paschen series**) have small energies: infrared.

<WorkedExample title="The red line of hydrogen">
<Problem>
An electron in a hydrogen atom drops from $n = 3$ to $n = 2$. Find the energy, frequency and wavelength of the photon, and its colour.
</Problem>
<Step title="The two levels">
$E_3 = -13.6/9 = -1.51\ \text{eV}$ and $E_2 = -13.6/4 = -3.40\ \text{eV}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Photon energy">
$hf = E_3 - E_2 = -1.51 - (-3.40) = 1.89\ \text{eV}$. In joules: $1.89 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3.02 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Frequency and wavelength">
$f = E/h = (3.02 \times 10^{-19})/(6.63 \times 10^{-34}) = 4.56 \times 10^{14}\ \text{Hz}$, and $\lambda = c/f = (3.00 \times 10^{8})/(4.56 \times 10^{14}) = 6.58 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 658\ \text{nm}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Colour">
$658\ \text{nm}$ is red light. This is the strongest visible line of hydrogen, called **H-alpha**; it gives the hydrogen tube its red glow. The measured wavelength is $656\ \text{nm}$; our answer is a little off only because $h$, $c$, the electronvolt and $13.6\ \text{eV}$ are rounded.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The same steps for the next jumps down to $n = 2$ give the other visible lines. The measured wavelengths are those shown on MIT's 8.033 slide (slide 17).

| Jump | Photon energy | Computed $\lambda$ | Measured $\lambda$ | Colour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $3 \to 2$ | $1.89\ \text{eV}$ | $658\ \text{nm}$ | $656\ \text{nm}$ | red |
| $4 \to 2$ | $2.55\ \text{eV}$ | $488\ \text{nm}$ | $486\ \text{nm}$ | blue-green |
| $5 \to 2$ | $2.86\ \text{eV}$ | $435\ \text{nm}$ | $434\ \text{nm}$ | blue-violet |
| $6 \to 2$ | $3.02\ \text{eV}$ | $411\ \text{nm}$ | $410\ \text{nm}$ | violet |

Jumps from even higher levels give lines closer and closer together, crowding towards the jump from "free" to $n = 2$, with $3.40\ \text{eV}$ and a wavelength of about $366\ \text{nm}$, just beyond the violet end of what we can see.

## Absorption and ionisation

Absorption follows the same rule in reverse: an atom in its ground state can take in a photon only if the photon's energy lifts the electron exactly to another level. There is one exception. If the photon has **more** energy than it takes to free the electron, it can always be absorbed: the electron leaves the atom, and the extra energy becomes its kinetic energy. The atom is then **ionised**: what is left is a positive ion, for hydrogen a bare proton.

The **ionisation energy** is the least energy that frees the electron from the ground state. For hydrogen it is the step from $E_1 = -13.6\ \text{eV}$ up to $0$: **$13.6\ \text{eV}$** (the value an MIT astrophysics problem set also quotes, 8.901 Problem Set 2, problem 2). From a higher level it is less, $13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$.

<WorkedExample title="Which photons can hydrogen absorb?">
<Problem>
Photons with energies of $10.2\ \text{eV}$, $11.0\ \text{eV}$, $12.75\ \text{eV}$ and $15.0\ \text{eV}$ pass through hydrogen gas whose atoms are all in the ground state. Which are absorbed, and what happens to the atom?
</Problem>
<Step title="List the gaps from the ground state">
$E_2 - E_1 = -3.40 - (-13.6) = 10.2\ \text{eV}$, $E_3 - E_1 = 12.09\ \text{eV}$, $E_4 - E_1 = -0.85 - (-13.6) = 12.75\ \text{eV}$, and freeing the electron takes $13.6\ \text{eV}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
$10.2\ \text{eV}$ matches the gap to $n = 2$: absorbed. $11.0\ \text{eV}$ lies between two gaps and matches none: it passes through. $12.75\ \text{eV}$ lifts the electron to $n = 4$: absorbed. $15.0\ \text{eV}$ is more than $13.6\ \text{eV}$: absorbed, and the atom is ionised; the electron leaves with $15.0 - 13.6 = 1.4\ \text{eV}$ of kinetic energy.
</Step>
<Step title="Afterwards">
An atom left in $n = 4$ falls back, directly or in steps. The possible jumps are $4 \to 3$, $4 \to 2$, $4 \to 1$, $3 \to 2$, $3 \to 1$ and $2 \to 1$, so the gas can now emit $6$ different lines, among them the blue-green and red Balmer lines.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="The photon that ionises hydrogen">
<Problem>
What is the longest wavelength a photon can have and still ionise a hydrogen atom in its ground state?
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy in joules">
$E = 13.6 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 2.176 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Wavelength">
$\lambda = hc/E = (6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8})/(2.176 \times 10^{-18}) = 9.14 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{m} = 91.4\ \text{nm}$: ultraviolet. Visible light cannot ionise hydrogen in its ground state; any photon of shorter wavelength can.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="13.6 eV and the early universe">
The young universe was so hot and so full of energetic photons that any hydrogen atom that formed was ionised again at once, so electrons and protons stayed apart and light could not travel far. Only once it had cooled enough could atoms form and stay together, and light has crossed the universe freely ever since: that light is the cosmic microwave background, met again in Block 8.
</Callout>

## The limits of Bohr's model

Bohr's model was a great success: from a few rules it gives hydrogen's spectral lines to within the rounding of the constants. But it is not the final answer.

- It only works for atoms or ions with **one electron**, such as hydrogen or helium that has lost one electron. For helium and every heavier atom the electrons push on each other, and Bohr's rules give the wrong lines.
- It does not say **how bright** each line is, or why some lines split into several close lines in a magnetic field or under a closer look.
- It does not explain **why** only certain orbits are allowed, or why an electron in them does not radiate: those are assumptions, not results.

Quantum mechanics, worked out in the 1920s by Schrödinger, Heisenberg, Born and others, answers these questions. In it an electron has no sharp orbit at all: it is described by a wave spread around the nucleus, and only certain wave patterns fit, each with its own energy. For hydrogen those energies are exactly $-13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$ again, so Bohr's energy levels survive, while his orbits do not. That is the picture you meet at university.

## Summary

- A hot thin gas gives an **emission spectrum** of bright lines; a cool gas in white light gives an **absorption spectrum** of dark lines at the same wavelengths. Each element has its own pattern.
- Bohr's postulates: the electron has only certain **energy levels**; it radiates only when it **jumps** between them; the photon's energy is the difference, $hf = E_\text{upper} - E_\text{lower}$, so $\lambda = hc/(E_\text{upper} - E_\text{lower})$.
- Hydrogen's levels are $E_n = -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$: $-13.6$, $-3.40$, $-1.51$, $-0.85\ \text{eV}$, crowding towards $0$.
- Jumps down to $n = 2$ give the visible **Balmer lines**, with H-alpha ($3 \to 2$) at $656\ \text{nm}$ in the red; jumps to $n = 1$ are ultraviolet, to $n = 3$ infrared.
- The **ionisation energy** of hydrogen is $13.6\ \text{eV}$; a photon needs at least that energy (a wavelength of at most $91.4\ \text{nm}$) to free the electron from the ground state.
- Bohr's model works only for one-electron atoms and cannot explain why its rules hold; quantum mechanics replaces his orbits but keeps the same energy levels.
