Lesson 7.8 ended with a puzzle: no one can say when a single radioactive nucleus will decay, yet a large sample decays at a steady, predictable rate. This lesson turns that into a law. It gives every radioactive isotope its own clock, the **half-life**, and that clock lets us read the age of a piece of charcoal, a rock, and the Earth itself.

## Many nuclei, one chance each

Recall from lesson 7.8 that every nucleus of a given isotope has the same chance of decaying in the next second, whatever its age and whatever happens around it. Call that chance per second $\lambda$, the **decay constant** of the isotope; its unit is $\text{s}^{-1}$ (per second). If a sample holds $N$ of these nuclei, then on average $\lambda N$ of them decay each second. That number is the **activity** $A$:

<Formula label="Activity" tex="A = \lambda N" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 23" />

Activity is measured in **becquerels** (Bq): one becquerel is one decay per second (reading 4, p. 2). Two things follow at once. A sample with twice as many nuclei is twice as active. And as nuclei decay, $N$ falls, so the activity falls with it: the sample decays more slowly as it gets older, not because each nucleus changes, but because fewer are left.

## The half-life

Because the number decaying each second is always the same *fraction* of what is left, the sample shrinks by the same factor in every equal stretch of time. The time in which half of the nuclei decay is the **half-life** $t_{1/2}$. After one half-life half the nuclei are left; after a second half-life half of that half, a quarter; after a third, an eighth:

| Time | $0$ | $t_{1/2}$ | $2t_{1/2}$ | $3t_{1/2}$ | $4t_{1/2}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nuclei left | $N_0$ | $\tfrac12 N_0$ | $\tfrac14 N_0$ | $\tfrac18 N_0$ | $\tfrac1{16} N_0$ |

Here $N_0$ is the number of nuclei at the start. This is decay by a fixed factor, the exponential decay of lesson 0.9. After a time $t$, the number of half-lives that have passed is $t/t_{1/2}$, and the factor $\tfrac12$ has been applied that many times:

<Formula label="Radioactive decay law" tex="N = N_0\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/t_{1/2}}" cite="reading1" at="follows from transcript p. 24" />

The lecture writes this law in the equivalent $e$ form of the next section (reading 1, p. 24); the version with $\tfrac12$ follows from it in one line.

The formula also works when $t$ is not a whole number of half-lives. And since $A = \lambda N$, the activity follows the same law: $A = A_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/t_{1/2}}$, with $A_0$ the activity at the start. Half-lives range from far less than a second to many billions of years, but for any one isotope the half-life is fixed: heating, pressure and chemistry do not change it.

<WorkedExample title="A short-lived sample">
<Problem>
A laboratory makes $6.4 \times 10^{9}$ nuclei of an isotope with a half-life of $8.0$ minutes. How many are left after (a) 40 minutes and (b) 20 minutes?
</Problem>
<Step title="Whole half-lives">
(a) 40 minutes is $40/8.0 = 5$ half-lives, so the number is halved five times: $N = 6.4 \times 10^{9} \times \left(\tfrac12\right)^{5} = 6.4 \times 10^{9} / 32 = 2.0 \times 10^{8}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Part of a half-life">
(b) 20 minutes is $20/8.0 = 2.5$ half-lives. With a calculator, $\left(\tfrac12\right)^{2.5} = 0.177$, so $N = 0.177 \times 6.4 \times 10^{9} = 1.1 \times 10^{9}$. That lies between the $1.6 \times 10^{9}$ left after two half-lives and the $8.0 \times 10^{8}$ left after three, as it should.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The exponential form

Lesson 0.9 introduced the number $e \approx 2.718$ and the natural logarithm $\ln$. Scientists usually write the decay law with $e$ as its base. Solving the "chance per second" picture with calculus (reading 1, p. 24) gives

<Formula label="Decay law with base e" tex="N = N_0\,e^{-\lambda t}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 24" />

This is the same curve as before. Setting $N = \tfrac12 N_0$ at $t = t_{1/2}$ gives $e^{-\lambda t_{1/2}} = \tfrac12$, so $\lambda t_{1/2} = \ln 2$:

<Formula label="Decay constant and half-life" tex="\lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}} \approx \frac{0.693}{t_{1/2}}" cite="reading1" at="transcript p. 24" />

So a large decay constant means a short half-life: the isotope decays fast. You do not need the $e$ form to work out how much is left (the $\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/t_{1/2}}$ form gives the same answers), but this link between $\lambda$ and $t_{1/2}$ is what turns a half-life into an activity.

<WorkedExample title="How active is the sample?">
<Problem>
Find the decay constant and the starting activity of the sample in the first example ($6.4 \times 10^{9}$ nuclei, half-life $8.0$ minutes), and its activity after 40 minutes.
</Problem>
<Step title="Decay constant">
Work in seconds, since the becquerel is per second: $t_{1/2} = 8.0 \times 60 = 480\ \text{s}$, so $\lambda = 0.693 / 480\ \text{s} = 1.44 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Each nucleus has a chance of about 1.4 in 1000 of decaying in any one second.
</Step>
<Step title="Activity">
$A_0 = \lambda N_0 = 1.44 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1} \times 6.4 \times 10^{9} = 9.2 \times 10^{6}\ \text{Bq}$, about nine million decays per second.
</Step>
<Step title="Later">
After 40 minutes, five half-lives, the activity has halved five times: $9.2 \times 10^{6} / 32 = 2.9 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Bq}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Working backwards: radioactive dating

The decay law can be run in reverse. If you know what fraction $N/N_0$ of a radioactive isotope is left, the number of half-lives that have passed is the power $x$ in $\left(\tfrac12\right)^{x} = N/N_0$. Rearranging the decay law with the logarithm rule of lesson 0.9, which finds an unknown power, gives:

<Formula label="Time from the fraction left" tex="\frac{t}{t_{1/2}} = \frac{\log\left(N/N_0\right)}{\log\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}" cite="reading1" at="follows from transcript p. 24 (rearranged as in lesson 0.9)" />

Either the LOG or the LN key works, as long as you use the same one on top and bottom. To date something you need an isotope with a suitable half-life, and a way to know $N_0$, the amount there was at the start.

## Carbon-14 dating

High in the atmosphere, cosmic rays (lesson 7.8) turn some nitrogen-14 nuclei into carbon-14, so the air always contains a small, nearly steady share of it (reading 3, pp. 15–16). Carbon-14 is a beta emitter with a half-life of **5730 years** (reading 2, p. 11). Plants take in carbon dioxide and animals eat plants, so every living thing carries the same tiny share of carbon-14 as the air. When an organism dies it stops taking in carbon, and from then on its carbon-14 decays without being replaced (reading 2, pp. 11–12). Measuring how much carbon-14 is left per gram of carbon, compared with living material, gives the time since death. (Newer measurements put the half-life closer to 5700 years (IAEA), the value a later lecture of the same MIT course uses (reading 3, p. 16); the difference does not matter at this level.)

<WorkedExample title="Charcoal from an old fireplace">
<Problem>
Charcoal from an ancient hearth has 30% of the carbon-14 per gram of carbon that living wood has. How long ago was the wood burnt?
</Problem>
<Step title="Number of half-lives">
$N/N_0 = 0.30$, so $\dfrac{t}{t_{1/2}} = \dfrac{\log 0.30}{\log 0.5} = 1.737$. A bit less than two half-lives, which fits: after two half-lives 25% would be left.
</Step>
<Step title="Time">
$t = 1.737 \times 5730$ years $\approx 9950$ years. The wood was burnt roughly ten thousand years ago.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Carbon dating has a limit. After many half-lives so little carbon-14 is left that its few decays are hard to tell apart from the background radiation, so the method works for ages up to some tens of thousands of years (reading 2, p. 11).

## Dating rocks and the age of the Earth

For rocks, the clock must run far more slowly. Two isotopes that do this are:

- **uranium-238**, which reaches stable lead-206 through the chain of decays in lesson 7.8, with a half-life of about $4.47 \times 10^{9}$ years (IAEA);
- **potassium-40**, which can decay to argon-40 (reading 1, p. 25), with a half-life of about $1.25 \times 10^{9}$ years (IAEA). Argon is a gas, so any argon is driven out while a rock is molten; argon found in it later was made after it set.

Here the starting amount comes from the daughter nuclei. If a crystal held no lead when it formed, every lead-206 nucleus in it now was once a uranium-238 nucleus, so $N_0$ is the uranium left plus the lead made.

<WorkedExample title="An old crystal">
<Problem>
A crystal that contained no lead when it formed now holds one lead-206 nucleus for every three uranium-238 nuclei. How old is it?
</Problem>
<Step title="Fraction left">
Out of every 4 uranium nuclei there were at the start, 3 are still uranium and 1 has become lead: $N/N_0 = 3/4 = 0.75$.
</Step>
<Step title="Age">
$\dfrac{t}{t_{1/2}} = \dfrac{\log 0.75}{\log 0.5} = 0.415$, so $t = 0.415 \times 4.47 \times 10^{9}$ years $= 1.86 \times 10^{9}$ years, about 1.9 billion years.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Dating of this kind on the oldest meteorites and rocks gives the Earth and the rest of the solar system an age of about **4.5 billion years** (NASA, *Facts About Earth*). That is almost exactly one half-life of uranium-238: about half of the uranium-238 the Earth was born with is still here.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A lower limit for the age of the universe">
The universe cannot be younger than the oldest things in it. Radioactive dating gives the Earth and the solar system an age of about 4.5 billion years, so any model of the expanding universe must allow at least that much time since the Big Bang. Comparing such ages with the age a model predicts is one of the checks the cosmology course makes.
</Callout>

## Short half-lives in medicine

In hospitals the opposite is wanted: a **tracer** that is very active for a short time. The most used is technetium-99m, which gives out $140\ \text{keV}$ gamma rays that a camera outside the body can pick up (reading 2, p. 12). It is injected and collects in the organ being studied, and the camera shows where it went. Its half-life is about **6 hours** (IAEA). (One MIT lecture puts it at about six days (reading 1, p. 25), a slip: the measured value is six hours. Another says "six days or so" (reading 2, p. 12), probably the same slip, though that sentence may mean the supply of molybdenum-99 that makes the technetium.) After two days, which is 8 half-lives, only $\left(\tfrac12\right)^{8} = \tfrac1{256}$ of it is left, less than half a percent. A short half-life means a high activity from a small amount, so the picture is clear, and the patient is soon free of it.

## Summary

- Every nucleus of an isotope has the same chance per second $\lambda$ of decaying (the **decay constant**), so a sample of $N$ nuclei has **activity** $A = \lambda N$, in becquerels (decays per second).
- The **half-life** $t_{1/2}$ is the time in which half of the nuclei decay. It is fixed for each isotope and does not depend on the size of the sample.
- After a time $t$: $N = N_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/t_{1/2}} = N_0 e^{-\lambda t}$, with $\lambda = \ln 2 / t_{1/2}$. The activity falls in the same way.
- Run backwards, $t/t_{1/2} = \log(N/N_0)/\log\tfrac12$ turns a measured fraction into an age.
- **Carbon-14** (half-life 5730 years) dates once-living material up to some tens of thousands of years.
- **Uranium-238** (about 4.5 billion years) and **potassium-40** (about 1.25 billion years) date rocks, by comparing the parent with the daughter it has made. The Earth is about 4.5 billion years old.
- **Medical tracers** such as technetium-99m (half-life about 6 hours) are very active for a short time and then die away.
