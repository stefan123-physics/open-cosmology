Lesson 7.1 found that nearly all the mass of an atom sits in a tiny, positive nucleus. This lesson looks inside it. A nucleus turns out to be a tight bundle of two kinds of particles, and the way they are counted names every atom in the universe. It also raises a puzzle: what stops a bundle of positive charges from flying apart?

## Protons and neutrons

The simplest nucleus, that of hydrogen, is a single **proton**: a particle with charge $+e = +1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$ and mass $1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, about 1800 times the mass of an electron. Heavier nuclei have more protons, but they are too heavy for their charge. A helium nucleus, for example, has the charge of two protons but the mass of about four.

Rutherford suggested in 1920 that the nucleus also holds a neutral particle, about as heavy as a proton. Neutral particles leave no tracks and are not bent by electric or magnetic fields, so it took until 1932 before James Chadwick found it: the **neutron**. Its mass is $1.675 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, about 0.1% more than the proton's, and it has no charge at all.

Protons and neutrons together are called **nucleons**. A neutral atom has as many electrons around its nucleus as there are protons inside it, so the charges cancel.

| particle | charge | mass (kg) | where |
|---|---|---|---|
| proton | $+e$ | $1.67 \times 10^{-27}$ | in the nucleus |
| neutron | $0$ | $1.675 \times 10^{-27}$ | in the nucleus |
| electron | $-e$ | $9.11 \times 10^{-31}$ | around the nucleus |

## Counting nucleons: Z, N and A

Three whole numbers describe a nucleus:

- the **atomic number** $Z$, the number of protons. It fixes the charge of the nucleus, so the number of electrons in the neutral atom, so its chemistry: every atom with $Z = 6$ is carbon, every atom with $Z = 79$ is gold;
- the **neutron number** $N$, the number of neutrons;
- the **mass number** $A$, the total number of nucleons.

<Formula label="Mass number" tex="A = Z + N" cite="reading4" at="Sec. 1.1.1" />

A nucleus is written with its chemical symbol, the mass number at the top left and the atomic number at the bottom left:

<Formula label="Notation for a nucleus" tex="{}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}" cite="mit" at="Atomic number and atomic mass number, standard notation" />

Since the symbol already tells you $Z$, the bottom number is often left out, and in words the mass number follows the name: uranium-238, or ²³⁸U.

<WorkedExample title="Inside a gold nucleus">
<Problem>
The gold in Rutherford's foil (lesson 7.1) is ${}^{197}_{79}\mathrm{Au}$. How many protons, neutrons and electrons does a neutral gold atom have?
</Problem>
<Step title="Protons">
The bottom number is $Z = 79$: 79 protons, so the nucleus has charge $+79e$, the charge used in lesson 7.1.
</Step>
<Step title="Neutrons">
$N = A - Z = 197 - 79 = 118$ neutrons.
</Step>
<Step title="Electrons">
The neutral atom has as many electrons as protons: 79. They decide how gold behaves in chemistry; the 118 neutrons add mass but no charge.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Isotopes

Change the number of neutrons and the charge of the nucleus stays the same, so the atom keeps its electrons, its chemistry and its place in the periodic table. Atoms of one element with different numbers of neutrons are called **isotopes**, from the Greek for "same place". They differ in mass, and some of their nuclei are stable while others are not.

Hydrogen has three isotopes, each with its own name:

- ordinary **hydrogen**, ${}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}$: one proton, no neutron;
- **deuterium**, ${}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H}$: one proton and one neutron;
- **tritium**, ${}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H}$: one proton and two neutrons. Its nucleus is unstable and changes by itself over the years (lesson 7.8).

Carbon gives another pair. Almost all carbon is carbon-12, with 6 protons and 6 neutrons; a tiny fraction is carbon-14, with 6 protons and 8 neutrons. Both burn, form carbon dioxide and build living things in exactly the same way, but carbon-14 is unstable, and that is what lets archaeologists date old wood and bone (lesson 7.9).

<WorkedExample title="Two isotopes of uranium">
<Problem>
Natural uranium ($Z = 92$) is mostly uranium-238, with a little uranium-235. How many neutrons does each have, and how much heavier is the larger nucleus?
</Problem>
<Step title="Neutrons">
Uranium-238: $N = 238 - 92 = 146$. Uranium-235: $N = 235 - 92 = 143$. Same element, three neutrons apart.
</Step>
<Step title="Mass">
Each nucleon has about the mass of a proton, so the difference is about $3 \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27} = 5.0 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$: about 1.3% of the whole nucleus. Chemically the two cannot be told apart; separating them takes machines that use this small difference in mass.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The size of a nucleus

Scattering experiments like Rutherford's, done with faster particles, measure the size of nuclei. Nuclear sizes are given in **femtometres**: $1\ \text{fm} = 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$. A nucleus has no sharp edge, but a useful radius follows a simple rule:

<Formula label="Radius of a nucleus" tex="R = R_0\,A^{1/3}, \qquad R_0 \approx 1.2\ \text{fm}" cite="reading4" at="Sec. 1.1.3, p. 6 (the notes use R₀ = 1.25 fm on p. 8)" />

The cube root says something simple. The volume of a sphere grows as $R^3$, and $R^3 = R_0^3A$: the volume is proportional to the number of nucleons. So nucleons pack together like marbles in a bag, each taking up about the same space, whatever the size of the nucleus.

## How dense is a nucleus?

If every nucleon takes up the same space, every nucleus should have the same density. Put the mass $A\,m_p$ (each nucleon weighs about one proton mass) into a sphere of radius $R_0A^{1/3}$, and $A$ cancels:

<Formula label="Density of nuclear matter" tex="\rho = \frac{A\,m_p}{\tfrac43\pi R_0^3 A} = \frac{3\,m_p}{4\pi R_0^3}" cite="reading4" at="Sec. 1.1.3, R = R₀A^(1/3), with density = mass/volume (lesson 4.1)" />

<WorkedExample title="The density of a uranium nucleus">
<Problem>
Find the radius, mass and density of a uranium-238 nucleus, and compare the density with that of water, $1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Radius">
$R = 1.2 \times 238^{1/3} = 1.2 \times 6.20 = 7.44\ \text{fm} = 7.44 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Mass and volume">
$m = 238 \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27} = 3.97 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{kg}$, and $V = \tfrac43\pi R^3 = \tfrac43\pi (7.44 \times 10^{-15})^3 = 1.73 \times 10^{-42}\ \text{m}^3$.
</Step>
<Step title="Density">
$\rho = m/V = 3.97 \times 10^{-25}/1.73 \times 10^{-42} = 2.3 \times 10^{17}\ \text{kg/m}^3$, the same as $3m_p/(4\pi R_0^3)$ gives for any nucleus. That is $2.3 \times 10^{14}$ times the density of water.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Lesson 7.1 found about $7 \times 10^{17}\ \text{kg/m}^3$ for a single proton, using the proton's own radius. Packed in a nucleus, nucleons sit a little further apart than that, so nuclear matter as a whole comes out a few times less dense. Both estimates agree on what matters: some $10^{17}\ \text{kg/m}^3$, a hundred million million times denser than water.

<WorkedExample title="The Earth as nuclear matter">
<Problem>
The Earth has a mass of $5.97 \times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}$ (NASA, Earth Fact Sheet). If all of it were squeezed to the density of a nucleus, $2.3 \times 10^{17}\ \text{kg/m}^3$, how large a ball would it make?
</Problem>
<Step title="Volume">
$V = m/\rho = 5.97 \times 10^{24}/2.3 \times 10^{17} = 2.6 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m}^3$.
</Step>
<Step title="Radius">
$R = (3V/(4\pi))^{1/3} = (6.2 \times 10^{6})^{1/3} = 180\ \text{m}$. The whole Earth would fit in a ball about 370 metres across: ordinary matter is almost all empty space.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## What holds a nucleus together?

Protons all carry positive charge, and like charges repel (lesson 6.1). In a nucleus they are crowded together at distances of about a femtometre, and Coulomb's law, $F = kQ_1Q_2/r^2$ (lesson 6.2), grows enormously at such small distances.

<WorkedExample title="Two protons in a nucleus">
<Problem>
Estimate the electric force between two protons $1.0\ \text{fm}$ apart, with $k = 9.0 \times 10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$. Compare it with their gravitational attraction ($G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$).
</Problem>
<Step title="Electric repulsion">
$F = ke^2/r^2 = 9.0 \times 10^{9} \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2/(1.0 \times 10^{-15})^2 = 230\ \text{N}$: about the weight of a 23 kg suitcase, acting on a particle of $1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$. On its own it would give each proton an acceleration of about $1.4 \times 10^{29}\ \text{m/s}^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Gravitational attraction">
$F_g = Gm_p^2/r^2 = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times (1.67 \times 10^{-27})^2/(1.0 \times 10^{-15})^2 = 1.9 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{N}$. The electric repulsion is about $10^{36}$ times larger: gravity is no help at all.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

So there must be a third force, far stronger than the electric force at these distances. It is called the **strong nuclear force** (or simply the nuclear force), and it has three properties that matter here (MIT 22.02 notes, Chapter 5, p. 69):

- **It attracts all nucleons.** Proton pulls proton, neutron pulls neutron, and proton pulls neutron. Charge plays no part.
- **It is very strong** at the distance between neighbouring nucleons, strong enough to beat the electric repulsion of 230 N between two protons.
- **It has a very short range.** It fades away within a few femtometres, so each nucleon feels only its nearest neighbours. That is why nothing of it is felt even at the size of an atom, while the electric force reaches across the whole nucleus.

This explains why neutrons are needed. Each neutron adds strong attraction without adding electric repulsion. In a large nucleus every proton is pushed by all the other protons, but pulled only by its neighbours, so heavier nuclei need a growing surplus of neutrons to stay together. Light stable nuclei have about as many neutrons as protons (carbon-12: 6 and 6; MIT 22.02 notes, Chapter 1, p. 9); uranium-238 has 146 neutrons to 92 protons. Too many or too few neutrons, and a nucleus is unstable: lesson 7.8.

## Atomic mass units and the missing mass

Kilograms are clumsy for nuclei, so physicists use the **atomic mass unit**, one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom:

<Formula label="The atomic mass unit" tex="1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}" cite="reading3" at="How heavy is an atom?" />

A proton has a mass of $1.00728\ \text{u}$ and a neutron $1.00866\ \text{u}$, so each nucleon is about 1 u: that is why the mass number is nearly the mass of an atom in u.

Weigh a nucleus very precisely, though, and you find something strange: it has slightly less mass than the protons and neutrons it is made of would have as free particles (MIT 22.02 notes, Chapter 1, p. 6). The difference is small, but it is real. The missing mass is linked to the energy that holds the nucleus together: to pull a nucleus apart into free nucleons you must do work against the strong force. How mass and energy are linked, and how much energy this is, needs Einstein's special relativity, so it waits for Block 8: lesson 8.4 works it out, and shows why it makes the Sun shine.

## Try it

Build nuclei from protons and neutrons. Start with one proton (hydrogen), add a neutron (deuterium) and another (tritium), and watch the element name stay the same while the mass number changes. Then build helium-4 and carbon-12, and see which combinations of protons and neutrons the simulation calls stable: too many protons, or too many neutrons, and the nucleus does not last. (What happens to an unstable nucleus is the subject of lesson 7.8.)

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The first nuclei">
In the first second after the Big Bang the universe was far too hot for nuclei: any proton and neutron that stuck together were knocked apart at once. A few minutes later it had cooled enough for deuterium to survive, and almost all of it was quickly built up into helium-4, an unusually tightly bound nucleus. ## Summary

- A nucleus is made of **protons** (charge $+e$) and **neutrons** (no charge, about 0.1% heavier, found by Chadwick in 1932): together, **nucleons**.
- The **atomic number** $Z$ (protons) fixes the element; the **mass number** $A = Z + N$ counts all nucleons. A nucleus is written ${}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}$, for example ${}^{197}_{79}\mathrm{Au}$.
- **Isotopes** of an element have the same $Z$ but different $N$: hydrogen, deuterium and tritium; carbon-12 and carbon-14. They have the same chemistry but different masses, and some are unstable.
- Nuclei have radius $R = R_0A^{1/3}$ with $R_0 \approx 1.2\ \text{fm}$, so all have about the same density, about $2 \times 10^{17}\ \text{kg/m}^3$: some $10^{14}$ times that of water.
- Two protons $1\ \text{fm}$ apart repel with about $230\ \text{N}$. The **strong nuclear force**, attractive between all nucleons, very strong but very short-ranged, holds the nucleus together; neutrons add attraction without repulsion.
- Masses of nuclei are given in **atomic mass units**, $1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$; a proton and a neutron are each about 1 u. A nucleus has slightly less mass than its separate parts: the missing mass is linked to the energy that holds it together (lesson 8.4).
