Lesson 4.8 showed that a gas that expands without taking in heat cools down: think of the air rushing out of a tyre valve, or a bubble of air rising until it forms a cloud. In this lesson you will look at that cooling once more, from the molecules' side, and then apply it to the largest gas there is: the whole universe, which has been expanding and cooling for 13.8 billion years.

## Why an expanding gas cools

Picture a gas in an insulated cylinder, closed by a piston that it pushes slowly outwards. The gas **does work** on the piston, so the work done **on** the gas, $W$, is negative. No heat flows in ($Q = 0$, an **adiabatic** change), so the first law of lesson 4.8 leaves only one source for that energy: the gas itself.

<Formula label="An adiabatic change" tex="Q = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \Delta U = W" cite="mit" at="slide 8 (dU = đQ + đW) with đQ = 0 (slide 20); slide 23: a slow expansion against a piston, W negative, ΔU negative" />

For an ideal monatomic gas the internal energy is $U = \tfrac32 N k_B T$ (lesson 4.8), so when $U$ falls, $T$ falls. **A gas that pays for its work out of its own energy cools down.** Since no heat flows in, the slow expansion also leaves the entropy of the gas unchanged ($\Delta S = Q/T = 0$, lesson 4.9); engineers call it **isentropic**. You will meet that idea again when we come to the universe.

Two words need care here. "No heat flows" can mean good insulation, or a change that is **quick** compared with the slow leaking of heat through the walls. But the change must still be **slow** compared with the molecules, so that the gas keeps pushing on the piston the whole time. If instead a thin wall bursts and the gas rushes into an empty space, it pushes nothing, does no work, and an ideal gas ends at the same temperature it started with (8.044 Lecture 6, slide 23). Only a gas that does work cools.

## The molecules' view: bouncing off a moving piston

Kinetic theory (lesson 4.4) shows the same thing one collision at a time. Think of a tennis ball hitting a racket. If the racket swings towards the ball, the ball flies back faster; if the racket is pulled back as the ball arrives, the ball comes off slower.

A molecule hitting a piston is just like that. Say a molecule flies straight at the piston with speed $v$ while the piston moves away at speed $u$. Seen from the piston (relative velocity, lesson 1.9), the molecule arrives with speed $v - u$ and bounces back with the same speed $v - u$. But the piston itself moves away at $u$, so in the cylinder the molecule comes back with speed $v - 2u$: slower than it arrived. A nitrogen molecule at $500\ \text{m/s}$ that hits a piston moving away at $10\ \text{m/s}$ comes back at $480\ \text{m/s}$.

Every molecule that hits the receding piston loses a little speed, so the average kinetic energy of the molecules goes down, and that average sets the temperature (lesson 4.4). The energy has not vanished: it went into pushing the piston. When the piston moves **in**, every molecule bounces off faster and the gas heats up. And when a gas rushes into empty space, there is no moving wall to bounce off, so the molecules keep their speeds.

## How much colder?

Lesson 4.8 gave the rule for a slow adiabatic change of an ideal monatomic gas: $T V^{2/3}$ stays the same, with $T$ in kelvin. Solved for the new temperature it reads:

<Formula label="Temperature after an adiabatic change" tex="T_2 = T_1 \left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{2/3}" cite="mit" at="slides 21–22 (T V^(γ−1) = constant, γ − 1 = 2/3 for a monatomic gas), rearranged" />

So the temperature goes as $V^{-2/3}$, a power law (lesson 0.6). Double the volume and the temperature becomes $2^{-2/3} = 0.63$ times as large; eight times the volume gives $8^{-2/3} = 1/4$ of the temperature. Air, whose molecules can also spin, follows the same kind of rule with the power $0.4$ instead of $2/3$: $T V^{0.4}$ stays constant (NASA Glenn, *Isentropic Compression or Expansion*). It still cools on expanding, just a little less steeply.

<WorkedExample title="Helium pushing a piston">
<Problem>
A cylinder holds $0.10\ \text{mol}$ of helium at $300\ \text{K}$ in a volume of $2.0\ \text{L}$. The cylinder is insulated, and the gas slowly pushes the piston out until the volume is $16.0\ \text{L}$. What is the temperature at the end, and how much work did the gas do on the piston? ($R = 8.31\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$)
</Problem>
<Step title="The new temperature">
The volume grows by a factor $16.0/2.0 = 8$, so $T_2 = 300 \times (2.0/16.0)^{2/3} = 300 \times \tfrac14 = 75\ \text{K}$. The helium has cooled from $27\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $-198\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check the rule">
$T_1 V_1^{2/3} = 300 \times 2.0^{2/3} = 476$ and $T_2 V_2^{2/3} = 75 \times 16.0^{2/3} = 476$ (volumes in litres). The two are equal, as they should be.
</Step>
<Step title="The work">
$U = \tfrac32 nRT$ (lesson 4.8), so $\Delta U = \tfrac32 \times 0.10 \times 8.31 \times (75 - 300) = -280\ \text{J}$. No heat flowed, so $W = \Delta U = -280\ \text{J}$: the gas did $280\ \text{J}$ of work on the piston, paid for entirely out of its internal energy.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The universe: a gas of light that expands

Now take the idea as far as it will go. The universe is expanding: the distances between galaxies grow all the time (you will meet the evidence in lessons 5.13 and 8.11). Running the film backwards, everything was once much closer together. Cosmologists describe this with the **scale factor** $a$: how large every distance in the universe was, compared with today. Today $a = 1$; when the universe was half its present size, $a = 0.5$.

The early universe was filled with a hot gas of light (and particles). As the universe expands, this gas expands with it. There is nothing outside it to take heat from, so the expansion is adiabatic, and it cools, just like the helium in the cylinder. Guth shows that, because the entropy of this gas stays constant, its temperature falls in the simplest possible way: inversely with the size of the universe, as long as the kinds of particle in the hot gas stay the same.

<Formula label="The temperature of the universe falls as it grows" tex="T \propto \frac{1}{a}" cite="reading1" at="p. 18 (entropy conserved: T falls as 1 over the scale factor while the kinds of particle stay the same)" />

There is a second way to see this. Light travelling through the universe is stretched along with it, in the same way as every distance between galaxies grows. Stretched light carries less energy: it looks like the glow of a cooler object. Lesson 5.13 explains the stretching, which astronomers call the cosmological redshift, and lesson 7.2 shows how the colour of a glow tells its temperature. Astronomers describe how much the universe has grown since some light set out with a number $z$, its **redshift**: when the light left, every distance was $1 + z$ times smaller than today. So the temperature then was $1 + z$ times higher:

<Formula label="Temperature then and now" tex="T_\text{then} = (1 + z)\,T_\text{now}" cite="reading1" at="p. 18 (T ∝ 1/a), with 1 + z the ratio of the scale factors now and then, reading2 pp. 5–6" />

The volume of a region grows as $a^3$, so $T \propto 1/a$ means $T V^{1/3}$ stays constant: light cools more slowly than helium, as the dashed curve of the graph showed. A gas of atoms that exchanged no heat with anything would follow our rule $T \propto V^{-2/3}$, which with $V \propto a^3$ gives $T \propto 1/a^2$ (our own step): it would cool faster than the light.

## The light we still see: decoupling

Today the universe is filled with light at a temperature of $2.725\ \text{K}$, measured by NASA's COBE satellite (*COBE FIRAS Overview*): the **cosmic microwave background**. Following the rule backwards tells us how hot the universe was in the past.

<WorkedExample title="Running the cooling backwards">
<Problem>
The background radiation has $T_\text{now} = 2.725\ \text{K}$. (a) What was its temperature when every distance was 1100 times smaller ($1 + z = 1100$)? (b) How much smaller than today was the universe when the radiation had the temperature of boiling water, $373\ \text{K}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) 1100 times smaller">
$T_\text{then} = 1100 \times 2.725\ \text{K} = 2997.5\ \text{K}$, about $3000\ \text{K}$: as hot as the filament of an old light bulb.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) As hot as boiling water">
$1 + z = T_\text{then}/T_\text{now} = 373/2.725 = 137$. Every distance in the universe was then 137 times smaller than today.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

That temperature of about $3000\ \text{K}$ is special. At higher temperatures the universe was a glowing fog: hydrogen could not hold on to its electrons, and the free electrons scattered the light again and again, so it could not travel far, just as light cannot pass through a fog. In Guth's account, by about $4000\ \text{K}$ half of the hydrogen had become neutral atoms, and by about $3000\ \text{K}$ so few free electrons were left that typical light never scattered again (the tiny bits of light, photons, come in lesson 7.2). This moment is called **decoupling**. Guth estimates that it came about 380 000 years after the start.

From then on the light travelled in straight lines, cooling as the universe grew, about 1100 times, until it reaches our telescopes as microwaves (lesson 5.8) at $2.725\ \text{K}$. When we look at the cosmic microwave background, we see an image of the universe at decoupling. Lesson 8.15 tells that story in full.

Going back further still, the universe was hotter still. At about a billion kelvin ($10^{9}\ \text{K}$), when distances were about $10^{9}/2.725 = 3.7 \times 10^{8}$ times smaller than today, a few minutes after the start, protons and neutrons could at last fuse into the first nuclei, helium above all (PDG review, Sec. 24.2). Lesson 8.14 explains how, and the university course works it out in detail.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
The whole history of the hot early universe is the story of a gas cooling as it expands. In MIT 8.286 Guth follows the temperature down from about ten billion kelvin, one second after the start, to the 3000 K of decoupling. He uses the rule of this lesson, the temperature falls as $1/a$, for every stretch of time in which the kinds of particle in the hot gas stay the same, and works out separately what happens when one kind disappears. The first law and entropy that you met in this block are exactly the tools he uses.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A gas that expands **adiabatically** (no heat in or out, $Q = 0$) while pushing a piston does work on its surroundings, so by the first law $\Delta U = W < 0$: its internal energy and its temperature fall. A gas that is compressed warms up. A gas that rushes into empty space does no work and, if ideal, does not cool.
- In molecules: each molecule bounces back slower off a piston that moves away, so the average kinetic energy, which sets the temperature, falls.
- For an ideal monatomic gas $T V^{2/3}$ stays constant (lesson 4.8): eight times the volume gives a quarter of the absolute temperature. Air follows $T V^{0.4} = \text{constant}$.
- The universe cools in the same way as it expands: its light is stretched and its temperature falls as $1/a$, so $T_\text{then} = (1 + z)\,T_\text{now}$.
- Today the background radiation is at $2.725\ \text{K}$. When the universe was about 1100 times smaller it was about $3000\ \text{K}$: atoms formed, the light stopped scattering (**decoupling**) and streamed free. That light is the cosmic microwave background. Earlier still, at about a billion kelvin, the first nuclei formed.
