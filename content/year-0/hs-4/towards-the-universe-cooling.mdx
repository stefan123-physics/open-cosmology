Pump up a bicycle tyre and the bottom of the pump turns warm. Let the air out again quickly and the valve feels cool. A deodorant spray can gets cold in your hand as you use it. High above a sunny field, a bubble of warm air rises, cools and turns into a cloud. All of these have one thing in common: a gas that is squeezed warms up, and a gas that expands cools down. In this lesson you will see why, with the first law of lesson 4.8 and the molecules of lesson 4.4. Then you will apply the same idea to the largest gas there is: the whole universe, which has been expanding and cooling for 13.8 billion years.

## Work done by an expanding gas

Picture a gas in a cylinder, closed by a piston that can slide. The molecules push on the piston (lesson 4.4). If you let the piston move outwards, the gas pushes it along: the gas **does work** on the piston, and on whatever the piston pushes. That energy has to come from somewhere.

The first law of thermodynamics (lesson 4.8) keeps track of it. The internal energy $U$ of the gas changes by the heat $Q$ that flows into it plus the work $W$ done on it:

<Formula label="The first law of thermodynamics" tex="\Delta U = Q + W" cite="mit" at="slide 8 (dU = đQ + đW)" />

When the gas expands by a small volume $\Delta V$ at pressure $p$, the work done **on** the gas is negative, because the gas pushes the piston away rather than being pushed:

<Formula label="Work done on a gas that expands a little" tex="W = -p\,\Delta V" cite="mit" at="slide 11 (đW = −P dV)" />

Now make the expansion **adiabatic**: no heat flows in or out, $Q = 0$. That is what happens when the cylinder is well insulated, or when the expansion is so quick that heat has no time to flow. Then $\Delta U = W = -p\,\Delta V$, which is negative: the internal energy falls. For an ideal gas of single atoms (a **monatomic** gas such as helium or argon) the internal energy is just the kinetic energy of all its atoms, $N$ times the average $\tfrac32 k_B T$ of lesson 4.4:

<Formula label="Internal energy of an ideal monatomic gas" tex="U = \tfrac{3}{2}\,N k_B T" cite="mit" at="slide 18 (U = (3/2)NkT for a monatomic gas)" />

So if $U$ falls, $T$ falls. **A gas that expands without taking in heat cools down.** Squeeze it instead and the work done on it is positive, so it warms up: that is the warm bicycle pump.

There is one more way to say this. A slow expansion with no heat flowing in leaves the entropy of the gas (lesson 4.9) unchanged; engineers call it an **isentropic** expansion. You will meet that idea again when we come to the universe.

## The molecules' view: bouncing off a moving piston

Kinetic theory (lesson 4.4) shows the same thing one collision at a time. Think of a tennis ball hitting a racket. If the racket swings towards the ball, the ball flies back faster; if the racket is pulled back as the ball arrives, the ball comes off slower.

A molecule hitting a piston is just like that. Say a molecule flies straight at the piston with speed $v$ while the piston moves away at speed $u$. Seen from the piston, the molecule arrives with speed $v - u$ and bounces back with the same speed $v - u$. But the piston itself moves away at $u$, so in the cylinder the molecule comes back with speed $v - 2u$: slower than it arrived. A nitrogen molecule at $500\ \text{m/s}$ that hits a piston moving away at $10\ \text{m/s}$ comes back at $480\ \text{m/s}$.

Every molecule that hits the receding piston loses a little speed, so the average kinetic energy of the molecules goes down, and that average **is** the temperature (lesson 4.4). The energy has not vanished: it went into pushing the piston. When the piston moves **in**, every molecule bounces off faster and the gas heats up.

## How much colder? A rule for an ideal gas

For an ideal monatomic gas, following the first law step by step through a slow adiabatic expansion gives a neat result. The university course (MIT 8.044) derives it with calculus; here we state it and check it with numbers. The quantity $T V^{\gamma - 1}$ stays the same, where $\gamma = 5/3$ for a monatomic gas, so $\gamma - 1 = 2/3$:

<Formula label="Adiabatic change of an ideal monatomic gas" tex="T\,V^{2/3} = \text{constant} \qquad\text{or}\qquad T_1 V_1^{2/3} = T_2 V_2^{2/3}" cite="mit" at="slides 19 and 21–22 (T V^(γ−1) = constant, γ = 5/3 for a monatomic gas; γ − 1 = 2/3 filled in)" />

Here $T$ must be the absolute temperature, in kelvin (lesson 4.2). Solving for the new temperature:

<Formula label="Temperature after an adiabatic change" tex="T_2 = T_1 \left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{2/3}" cite="mit" at="slides 21–22, rearranged" />

So the temperature goes as $V^{-2/3}$, a power law (lesson 0.6). Double the volume and the temperature becomes $2^{-2/3} = 0.63$ times as large; eight times the volume gives $8^{-2/3} = 1/4$ of the temperature. Compare this with Boyle's law (lesson 4.3), where the temperature was kept constant by letting heat flow in: here no heat flows in, so the gas pays for its work out of its own energy.

Air is not monatomic: its molecules are pairs of atoms, which can also spin. For air $\gamma = 1.4$ (NASA Glenn, *Isentropic Compression or Expansion*), so for air $T V^{0.4}$ stays constant. It still cools on expanding, just a little less steeply.

<WorkedExample title="Helium pushing a piston">
<Problem>
A cylinder holds $0.10\ \text{mol}$ of helium at $300\ \text{K}$ in a volume of $2.0\ \text{L}$. The cylinder is insulated, and the gas slowly pushes the piston out until the volume is $16.0\ \text{L}$. What is the temperature at the end, and how much work did the gas do on the piston? ($R = 8.31\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\,\text{K}^{-1}$)
</Problem>
<Step title="The new temperature">
The volume grows by a factor $16.0/2.0 = 8$, so $T_2 = 300 \times (2.0/16.0)^{2/3} = 300 \times \tfrac14 = 75\ \text{K}$. The helium has cooled from $27\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $-198\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check the rule">
$T_1 V_1^{2/3} = 300 \times 2.0^{2/3} = 476$ and $T_2 V_2^{2/3} = 75 \times 16.0^{2/3} = 476$ (volumes in litres). The two are equal, as they should be.
</Step>
<Step title="The work">
With $N k_B = nR$ (lesson 4.3), $U = \tfrac32 nRT$, so $\Delta U = \tfrac32 \times 0.10 \times 8.31 \times (75 - 300) = -280\ \text{J}$. No heat flowed, so $W = \Delta U = -280\ \text{J}$: the gas did $280\ \text{J}$ of work on the piston, paid for entirely out of its internal energy.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Cooling in everyday life

- **The bicycle pump.** Pushing the handle down compresses the air quickly, before much heat can leave, so it warms up. Much of the warmth you feel at the bottom of the pump comes from this, not only from friction.
- **The tyre valve.** Air rushing out of a tyre expands quickly, does work pushing the outside air away, and comes out cool.
- **The spray can.** As the gas inside expands it cools; most of the cold you feel, though, comes from the liquid inside evaporating, which takes latent heat (lesson 4.6).
- **Clouds.** Higher up the air pressure is lower (lesson 4.1). A rising bubble of air expands, does work on the air around it and cools, so fast that it hardly exchanges heat with its surroundings. When it is cold enough, its water vapour condenses into droplets: a cloud.
- **Engines.** In a car engine the hot gas pushes the piston down; as it expands it does work and cools (NASA Glenn, *Isentropic Compression or Expansion*). The work it does is exactly what drives the car.

## The universe: a gas of light that expands

Now take the idea as far as it will go. The universe is expanding: the distances between galaxies grow all the time (you will meet the evidence in lessons 5.13 and 8.11). Running the film backwards, everything was once much closer together. Cosmologists describe this with the **scale factor** $a$: how large every distance in the universe was, compared with today. Today $a = 1$; when the universe was half its present size, $a = 0.5$.

The early universe was filled with a hot gas of light (and particles). As the universe expands, this gas expands with it. There is nothing outside it to take heat from, so the expansion is adiabatic, and it cools, just like the helium in the cylinder. Guth shows that, because the entropy of this gas stays constant, its temperature falls in the simplest possible way: inversely with the size of the universe.

<Formula label="The temperature of the universe falls as it grows" tex="T \propto \frac{1}{a}" cite="reading1" at="p. 18 (entropy conserved: T falls as 1 over the scale factor while the kinds of particle stay the same)" />

There is a second way to see this. Light travelling through the universe is stretched with it: every wavelength grows by the same factor as every distance (lesson 5.13 calls this the cosmological redshift). Stretched black-body light is again black-body light, but cooler: its peak moves to longer wavelengths (lesson 7.2). Astronomers measure how much the universe has grown since light set out with the redshift $z$: when the light left, every distance was $1 + z$ times smaller than today. So the temperature then was $1 + z$ times higher:

<Formula label="Temperature then and now" tex="T_\text{then} = (1 + z)\,T_\text{now}" cite="reading1" at="p. 18 (T ∝ 1/a), with 1 + z the ratio of the scale factors now and then, reading2 p. 6" />

The volume of a region grows as $a^3$, so $T \propto 1/a$ means $T V^{1/3}$ stays constant: light cools more slowly than helium, as the dashed curve of the graph showed. A gas of atoms that exchanged no heat with anything would follow our rule $T \propto V^{-2/3}$, which with $V \propto a^3$ gives $T \propto 1/a^2$ (our own step): it would cool faster than the light.

## The light we still see: decoupling

Today the universe is filled with light at a temperature of $2.725\ \text{K}$, measured by NASA's COBE satellite (*COBE FIRAS Overview*): the **cosmic microwave background**. Following the rule backwards tells us how hot the universe was in the past.

<WorkedExample title="Running the cooling backwards">
<Problem>
The background radiation has $T_\text{now} = 2.725\ \text{K}$. (a) What was its temperature when every distance was 1100 times smaller ($1 + z = 1100$)? (b) How much smaller than today was the universe when the radiation had the temperature of boiling water, $373\ \text{K}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) 1100 times smaller">
$T_\text{then} = 1100 \times 2.725\ \text{K} = 2997.5\ \text{K}$, about $3000\ \text{K}$: as hot as the filament of an old light bulb.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) As hot as boiling water">
$1 + z = T_\text{then}/T_\text{now} = 373/2.725 = 137$. Every distance in the universe was then 137 times smaller than today.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

That temperature of about $3000\ \text{K}$ is special. At higher temperatures the universe was a glowing fog: hydrogen could not hold on to its electrons, and the free electrons scattered the light again and again, so it could not travel far, just as light cannot pass through a fog. In Guth's account, by about $4000\ \text{K}$ half of the hydrogen had become neutral atoms, and by about $3000\ \text{K}$ so few free electrons were left that a typical photon never scattered again. This moment is called **decoupling**. Guth estimates that it came about 380 000 years after the start.

From then on the light travelled in straight lines, cooling as the universe grew, about 1100 times, until it reaches our telescopes as microwaves at $2.725\ \text{K}$. When we look at the cosmic microwave background, we see an image of the universe at decoupling. Lesson 8.15 tells that story in full.

Going back further still, the universe was hotter still. At about a billion kelvin ($10^{9}\ \text{K}$), when distances were about $10^{9}/2.725 = 3.7 \times 10^{8}$ times smaller than today, a few minutes after the start, protons and neutrons could at last fuse into the first nuclei, helium above all (PDG review, Sec. 24.2). Lesson 8.14 explains how, and the university course works it out in detail.

The first law and entropy that you met in this block are exactly the tools he uses.

## Summary

- A gas that expands **adiabatically** (no heat in or out, $Q = 0$) does work on its surroundings, so by the first law $\Delta U = W < 0$: its internal energy and its temperature fall. A gas that is compressed warms up.
- In molecules: each molecule bounces back slower off a piston that moves away, so the average kinetic energy, the temperature, falls.
- For an ideal monatomic gas $T V^{2/3}$ stays constant ($\gamma = 5/3$): eight times the volume gives a quarter of the absolute temperature. Air ($\gamma = 1.4$) follows $T V^{0.4} = \text{constant}$.
- The universe cools in the same way as it expands: its light is stretched and its temperature falls as $1/a$, so $T_\text{then} = (1 + z)\,T_\text{now}$.
- Today the background radiation is at $2.725\ \text{K}$. When the universe was about 1100 times smaller it was about $3000\ \text{K}$: atoms formed, the light stopped scattering (**decoupling**) and streamed free. That light is the cosmic microwave background. Earlier still, at about a billion kelvin, the first nuclei formed.
