A room full of air looks perfectly still. Yet the gas in it is a swarm of about $2.5 \times 10^{25}$ molecules in every cubic metre, each racing along faster than a jet airliner, crashing into the others and into the walls billions of times a second. Lessons 4.1 to 4.3 described a gas by its pressure, volume and temperature. This lesson looks inside: it explains pressure as the drumming of molecules on the walls, and shows that temperature is nothing other than the energy of their motion.

## The kinetic model of a gas

The **kinetic theory** of matter starts from one idea: all matter is made of particles (atoms or molecules) that are always moving, and they move faster the hotter the matter is (reading 1, Sec. 3.2). In a solid the particles stay in place and vibrate about fixed points; in a liquid they slide past each other; in a gas they fly freely through the space between them.

For a gas the model is made precise with a few assumptions (Siyavula, Sec. 7.1, "The kinetic theory of gases"):

- The gas is made of a very large number of particles (molecules, or single atoms).
- The particles are tiny compared with the distances between them, so almost all of a gas is empty space.
- They move all the time, in straight lines, in random directions and with different speeds.
- The forces between them are very weak, except at the moment two of them collide.
- Collisions with each other and with the walls do not change the total kinetic energy: the molecules bounce like perfect billiard balls.

A gas that fits these assumptions exactly is an **ideal gas**. Real gases behave almost like it, except at very high pressure (when the molecules themselves take up a real share of the space) and at low temperature (when their attraction starts to matter and the gas is close to turning liquid) (Siyavula, Sec. 7.1, "Ideal gases and non-ideal gas behaviour"). Air at everyday pressure and temperature is very nearly ideal.

## Pressure from molecular impacts

Why does a gas push on its container? Every molecule that bounces off a wall gives the wall a tiny kick, and the kicks of many molecules add up to a steady force (NASA Glenn, "Kinetic Theory of Gases"). Lesson 3.6 has the tools to turn that into a formula.

Put $N$ molecules, each of mass $m$, in a cube with side $L$, so that the volume is $V = L^3$. Follow one molecule, and look only at its velocity component $v_x$ towards the right-hand wall.

**One hit.** The molecule arrives with momentum $+mv_x$ and bounces back with $-mv_x$. Its momentum changes by $-2mv_x$, so by Newton's third law the wall receives the opposite change, as for any object bouncing off a wall (lessons 2.4 and 3.6). The momentum given to the wall is minus the molecule's change:

<Formula label="Momentum given to the wall in one hit" tex="-\Delta(mv_x) = 2mv_x" cite="momentum" at="Change in momentum (ESCJ9), Case 1: object bouncing off a wall, Δp = p_f − p_i; with p_f = −p_i this is −2mv (this page's step)" />

**How often.** Between two hits on the same wall the molecule crosses to the far wall and back, a distance $2L$, which takes a time $\Delta t = 2L/v_x$. (Collisions with other molecules on the way do not change the result on average, because they only pass momentum on from one molecule to another.)

**Average force.** Force is momentum passed on per second (lesson 3.6), so this one molecule pushes on the wall with an average force $F = 2mv_x/(2L/v_x) = mv_x^2/L$.

**All the molecules.** The molecules have different speeds, so add up their forces: the total is $N$ times the average, $F = Nm\langle v_x^2\rangle/L$. The brackets $\langle\ \rangle$ mean "the average of": $\langle v_x^2\rangle$ is the average of the squares of $v_x$ over all molecules. Dividing by the area of the wall, $L^2$, gives the pressure (lesson 4.1): $p = Nm\langle v_x^2\rangle/L^3 = Nm\langle v_x^2\rangle/V$.

**Three directions.** By Pythagoras in three dimensions, $v^2 = v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2$ (use Pythagoras twice: first for $v_x$ and $v_y$, then for that result and $v_z$). The motion is random, with no direction preferred, so on average each of the three components has the same share: $\langle v_x^2\rangle = \tfrac13\langle v^2\rangle$. That gives

<Formula label="Pressure of a gas from molecular impacts" tex="p = \frac{N m \langle v^2\rangle}{3V}" cite="nasakinetic" at="the molecules give momentum to the walls, producing a force; the box derivation and formula are this page's own steps" />

This derivation is the page's own, built on the momentum change of a bounce (Siyavula Grade 12, Sec. 2.2) and the picture of molecules passing momentum to the walls (NASA Glenn). The university course reaches the same formula another way: in 8.044 the average squared momentum of an atom, $3mk_BT$ (slide 5), combined with $p = Nk_BT/V$ (slide 8) gives it too.

The pressure grows with the number of molecules per cubic metre, $N/V$, and with $m\langle v^2\rangle$: faster molecules hit the walls both more often and harder, which is why $v$ appears squared.

## Temperature is average kinetic energy

Lesson 4.3 also wrote the ideal gas law per molecule instead of per mole:

<Formula label="Ideal gas law, counting molecules" tex="pV = N k_B T" cite="reading2" at="slide 8: P = NkT/V" />

Here $k_B = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$ is **Boltzmann's constant**, the gas constant for a single molecule. This law comes from measurements on gases; by itself it says nothing about what molecules do. The kinetic model now explains it.

Now we have two expressions for $pV$: from the molecules, $pV = \tfrac13 Nm\langle v^2\rangle$, and from experiment, $pV = Nk_BT$. Setting them equal and dividing by $N$ gives $\tfrac13 m\langle v^2\rangle = k_BT$. Multiply both sides by $\tfrac32$: on the left stands $\tfrac12 m\langle v^2\rangle$, the average kinetic energy of one molecule (lesson 3.2).

<Formula label="Average kinetic energy of a molecule" tex="E_{k,\text{avg}} = \tfrac{3}{2}\,k_B T" cite="reading2" at="slide 5: the average energy of one atom of a monatomic gas is (3/2)kT; slide 8: E = (3/2)NkT for N atoms" />

<Callout kind="key">
The absolute temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the molecules: $E_{k,\text{avg}} = \tfrac32 k_B T$, with $k_B = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$ and $T$ in kelvin. Double the absolute temperature and you double the average kinetic energy.
</Callout>

This is what "temperature is motion" means (Siyavula, Sec. 7.1, "Temperature"). It also explains three things you met earlier:

- **Why the kelvin scale** (lesson 4.2). The formula only works with the absolute temperature. At $0\ \text{K}$ the average kinetic energy would be zero: in this model the molecules would stop. (Quantum physics says that a little motion always remains; this model is for ordinary temperatures.)
- **The mass does not appear.** At the same temperature, a light helium atom and a heavy carbon dioxide molecule have the same average kinetic energy. The light one simply moves faster.
- **Thermal equilibrium** (lesson 4.2). When fast molecules of a hot object collide with slower ones of a cold object, they share out their energy until the average is the same on both sides: equal temperatures.

The formula gives the energy of the motion from place to place. Molecules made of two or more atoms can also spin and vibrate, which stores extra energy; that is why the total energy of a gas of such molecules is more than $\tfrac32 Nk_BT$. The university course (8.044) works this out.

<WorkedExample title="The energy of one molecule of air">
<Problem>
What is the average kinetic energy of a molecule in a room at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="Temperature in kelvin">
$T = 20 + 273 = 293\ \text{K}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Average kinetic energy">
$E_{k,\text{avg}} = \tfrac32 k_B T = 1.5 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 293 = 6.07 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Is that a lot?">
For one molecule it is tiny: a raindrop of $0.1\ \text{g}$ falling at $1\ \text{m/s}$ has about $10^{16}$ times more kinetic energy. But a cubic metre of air holds about $2.5 \times 10^{25}$ molecules ($N/V = p/(k_BT)$), and together they carry $\tfrac32 pV = 1.5 \times 1.01 \times 10^5 \times 1 = 1.5 \times 10^5\ \text{J}$ of kinetic energy.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## How fast are the molecules?

Since $\tfrac12 m\langle v^2\rangle = \tfrac32 k_BT$, the average of $v^2$ is $3k_BT/m$. Its square root is a typical speed, called the **root-mean-square speed** or **rms speed**: take the square of every speed, average (the mean), then take the square root.

<Formula label="The rms speed" tex="v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}}" cite="reading2" at="slide 5: the average squared momentum is 3mkT; dividing by m² gives the average of v²" />

Hotter gases and lighter molecules are faster. Molecular masses are usually given in atomic mass units, $1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$ (NIST).

<WorkedExample title="Nitrogen at room temperature">
<Problem>
Air is mostly nitrogen, N₂ (78%, NASA Earth Fact Sheet), with a molecular mass of $28\ \text{u}$. Find the rms speed of the nitrogen molecules at $20\ ^\circ\text{C}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Mass of one molecule">
$m = 28 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 4.65 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Rms speed">
$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 293/(4.65 \times 10^{-26})} = \sqrt{2.61 \times 10^{5}} = 511\ \text{m/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
That is about 1.5 times the speed of sound in air, $343\ \text{m/s}$ (you will meet it in lesson 5.5). This is no coincidence: sound is passed on by the molecules themselves, so it cannot travel much faster than they move.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Hydrogen in the same room">
<Problem>
A balloon of hydrogen gas, H₂ ($2\ \text{u}$), is in the same room at $293\ \text{K}$. How fast are its molecules?
</Problem>
<Step title="Rms speed">
$m = 2 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 3.32 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, so $v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 293/(3.32 \times 10^{-27})} = 1.91 \times 10^3\ \text{m/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with a ratio">
At the same temperature $v_{\text{rms}} \propto 1/\sqrt m$, so hydrogen is $\sqrt{28/2} = \sqrt{14} = 3.74$ times faster than nitrogen: $3.74 \times 511 = 1.91 \times 10^3\ \text{m/s}$. Each hydrogen molecule has the same average kinetic energy as a nitrogen molecule, but a fourteenth of the mass.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Hydrogen atoms at the surface of the Sun">
<Problem>
The visible surface of the Sun has a temperature of $5772\ \text{K}$ (NASA Sun Fact Sheet), and most of its gas is hydrogen. Find the rms speed of a hydrogen atom ($m = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$) there.
</Problem>
<Step title="Rms speed">
$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 5772/(1.67 \times 10^{-27})} = \sqrt{1.43 \times 10^{8}} = 1.20 \times 10^4\ \text{m/s}$, about $12\ \text{km/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Can it escape?">
That is more than the $11.2\ \text{km/s}$ needed to escape from the Earth (lesson 3.11), but far less than the $618\ \text{km/s}$ needed to escape from the Sun (NASA Sun Fact Sheet). The Sun's enormous gravity holds on to its hot hydrogen.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A spread of speeds

The rms speed is only a typical value. In a real gas the collisions constantly speed molecules up and slow them down, so at any moment some are almost at rest and a few are very fast. (Some school books give every molecule of an ideal gas the same speed, to keep things simple; collisions in fact always spread the speeds out, ideal gas or not.) The share of molecules with a speed close to $v$ is given by the **Maxwell distribution**:

<Formula label="Maxwell distribution of speeds" tex="f(v) \propto v^2\, e^{-mv^2/(2k_BT)}" cite="reading3" at="p. 9, point ⑥" />

Two factors compete:

- The exponential factor (lesson 0.9) makes high speeds rare. The exponent is a kinetic energy, $\tfrac12 mv^2$, divided by $k_BT$: a molecule with much more energy than $k_BT$ is very unlikely, because it would need to collect energy from many collisions in a row.
- The factor $v^2$ makes very low speeds rare. A speed close to zero needs all three velocity components to be close to zero at the same time, while a larger speed can be reached with many combinations of directions.

So the curve starts at zero, rises to a peak and dies away in a long tail. The number in front of the formula (left out above) is chosen so that the total area under the curve is 1: all the molecules together (lesson 0.10). The peak, the most likely speed, lies at $\sqrt{2k_BT/m}$, which is $\sqrt{2/3} = 0.82$ times $v_{\text{rms}}$: for the nitrogen in the room, at about $417\ \text{m/s}$.

The tail matters. In the upper atmosphere a few molecules in the tail are fast enough to escape from the Earth. Over billions of years this has let the lightest gas, hydrogen, leak away almost completely, while heavier nitrogen and oxygen stay. The fast tail also explains why water evaporates below its boiling point: the fastest molecules at the surface can break free (lesson 4.6).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards the universe: a very hot gas">
The early universe was a hot gas. The higher the temperature, the harder the particles hit each other. In a hot enough gas the collisions break molecules apart; hotter still, they knock electrons off atoms; and at billions of kelvin even nuclei are smashed (Block 8). Lesson 4.10 shows the other half of the story: a gas that expands cools, its particles slow down, and the universe, which has been expanding ever since, has cooled from that hot start. The rule $E_{k,\text{avg}} = \tfrac32 k_BT$ is how cosmologists turn a temperature into an energy per particle (for particles much slower than light; for particles moving at nearly the speed of light the average energy is about $3k_BT$, the same size).
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Kinetic model:** a gas is a huge number of tiny molecules, far apart, in fast random motion, bouncing off each other and the walls without losing kinetic energy.
- **Pressure** is the total effect of molecules hitting the walls. Each hit gives the wall a momentum $2mv_x$, and adding all hits gives $p = Nm\langle v^2\rangle/(3V)$.
- With the ideal gas law $pV = Nk_BT$, this shows that **temperature measures the average kinetic energy** of a molecule: $E_{k,\text{avg}} = \tfrac32 k_BT$, with $k_B = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$, the same for every kind of molecule at the same temperature.
- **Rms speed:** $v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3k_BT/m}$, about $511\ \text{m/s}$ for nitrogen at room temperature; lighter molecules and hotter gases are faster.
- **Maxwell distribution:** speeds are spread out as $f(v) \propto v^2 e^{-mv^2/(2k_BT)}$, with a peak at $0.82\,v_{\text{rms}}$ and a tail of fast molecules; heating shifts the peak to higher speeds.
