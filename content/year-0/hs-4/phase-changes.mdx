Put a few ice cubes in a pan and heat it. The thermometer climbs to $0\ \text{°C}$ and then stops, for minutes, although the flame keeps burning. Only when the last bit of ice has melted does it start to rise again. Later, at $100\ \text{°C}$, it stops a second time, for even longer, while the water boils away. Lesson 4.5 said that heat makes things warmer. This lesson is about the heat that does not: the heat that changes a solid into a liquid and a liquid into a gas.

## Solid, liquid and gas

Matter comes in three **states** (or **phases**): solid, liquid and gas. A solid keeps its own shape and volume, a liquid takes the shape of its container, and a gas fills the whole container (Siyavula, Sec. 3.1). The particle model of lesson 4.4 explains the difference (reading 1, Table 3.1):

- In a **solid** the particles are packed close together and held by strong attractive forces. They cannot leave their places; they only vibrate about them.
- In a **liquid** the particles are still close together, but they have more energy. The forces still hold them together, yet they can slide past each other, so the liquid flows.
- In a **gas** the particles have so much energy that the forces can no longer hold them. They fly about freely, far apart, as in the kinetic model of lesson 4.4.

So two things decide the state: how strongly the particles attract each other, and how much energy of motion they have. From here on, the **bonds** between particles simply means these attractive forces that hold them together. Heating gives them more energy; cooling takes it away.

## The changes of state

Adding or removing heat moves a substance from one state to another. Each change has a name (Siyavula, Sec. 3.1, Figure 3.1):

- **Melting**: solid to liquid, at the **melting point**. The reverse is **freezing**, at the same temperature (the **freezing point**).
- **Evaporation**: liquid to gas at the surface, at any temperature below the boiling point. A puddle dries up without ever boiling.
- **Boiling**: liquid to gas everywhere in the liquid at once, with bubbles of vapour forming inside it, at the **boiling point**.
- **Condensation**: gas to liquid, as when your breath fogs a cold window.
- **Sublimation**: solid straight to gas, without becoming liquid first. Dry ice (frozen carbon dioxide) does this at normal air pressure. The reverse is **deposition**: frost on a winter morning is water vapour that turned straight into ice.

Going up the list (solid, liquid, gas) always takes in heat; going down gives heat out.

## The heating curve

Heat a block of ice steadily, from below its melting point, and plot its temperature. The graph is called a **heating curve**. It has sloping parts and flat parts (Siyavula, Sec. 3.1, "Heating and cooling curve of water"; reading 3, pp. 4–5):

1. The ice warms up from its starting temperature to $0\ \text{°C}$.
2. The temperature stays at $0\ \text{°C}$ while the ice melts. Ice and water are both present.
3. The water warms up from $0\ \text{°C}$ to $100\ \text{°C}$.
4. The temperature stays at $100\ \text{°C}$ while the water boils. Water and steam are both present.
5. Only when all the water has become steam can the steam get hotter.

On the sloping parts the heat makes the particles move faster: the temperature rises, as in lesson 4.5, by

<Formula label="Heat to warm a substance (lesson 4.5)" tex="Q = mc\,\Delta T" cite="reading3" at="p. 4: the heat added changes the temperature through the heat capacity, C_p = dq/dT" />

On the flat parts the heat is still flowing in, but it goes into **breaking the bonds** between the particles, not into faster motion (Siyavula, Sec. 3.1). Lesson 4.4 showed that temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles. During melting, that does not change: the heat is used to pull the molecules out of their fixed places, which raises their energy of position, not their speed. Only when the change of state is complete can the temperature rise again.

A cooling curve is the same graph run backwards. Steam that condenses, or water that freezes, gives out heat while its temperature stays constant, as the bonds form again (Siyavula, Sec. 3.1).

<Callout kind="key">
While a substance changes state, its temperature stays constant. All the heat goes into breaking the bonds between the particles (or comes out as they form again). This is why heating curves have flat parts.
</Callout>

People did not always know this. In the 1700s many thought that ice at its melting point needed only a tiny extra amount of heat to melt completely. The Scottish chemist Joseph Black noticed that snow does not vanish at once when a thaw sets in, and found by experiment that melting ice takes in far more heat than warming it by one degree (reading 3, p. 13). He called it **latent heat**: "latent" means hidden, because a thermometer does not show it.

## Latent heat: Q = mL

How much heat does a change of state take? Twice as much ice needs twice as much heat, so the heat is proportional to the mass. The heat per kilogram is called the **specific latent heat** $L$ (often just "latent heat"):

<Formula label="Heat for a change of state" tex="Q = mL" cite="reading3" at="p. 8: the enthalpy of melting (latent heat of fusion) is the heat stored on freezing and released on melting; written here per kilogram" />

Here $Q$ is the heat in joules, $m$ the mass in kilograms that changes state, and $L$ in J/kg. Each substance has two values: the **latent heat of fusion** $L_\text{f}$ for melting (and freezing) and the **latent heat of vaporisation** $L_\text{v}$ for boiling (and condensing). The temperature does not appear in $Q = mL$: there is no $\Delta T$, because the temperature does not change.

Tables of data often give latent heats per mole instead of per kilogram (lesson 4.3). A water molecule, H₂O, has a mass of $2 \times 1 + 16 = 18\ \text{u}$ (hydrogen $1\ \text{u}$, oxygen $16\ \text{u}$; lesson 4.4), so a mole of water, $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ molecules, has a mass of $6.02 \times 10^{23} \times 18 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 0.0180\ \text{kg}$, or $18.0\ \text{g}$. Melting a mole of ice takes $6008\ \text{J}$ (reading 4, p. 97), and boiling away a mole of water takes $40.65\ \text{kJ}$ (reading 2, "High heat of vaporisation"). Per kilogram:

<Formula label="Latent heat of fusion of water" tex="L_\text{f} = \frac{6008\ \text{J/mol}}{0.0180\ \text{kg/mol}} = 3.34 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J/kg}" cite="reading4" at="p. 97, data table: molar enthalpy of melting of water 6008 J/mol" />

<Formula label="Latent heat of vaporisation of water" tex="L_\text{v} = \frac{40\,650\ \text{J/mol}}{0.0180\ \text{kg/mol}} = 2.26 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J/kg}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 4.2, 'High heat of vaporisation': 40,65 kJ/mol" />

The same table gives the heat capacity of ice: $38.0\ \text{J}$ per mole per kelvin (reading 4, p. 97), so $c_\text{ice} = 38.0/0.0180 = 2.1 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J/(kg K)}$, about half that of liquid water, $c_\text{water} = 4180\ \text{J/(kg K)}$ (lesson 4.5).

Two things stand out. Boiling takes $2.26 \times 10^{6}/(3.34 \times 10^{5}) = 6.8$ times as much heat as melting. Melting only loosens the molecules so they can slide past each other; boiling has to pull them apart completely. And boiling a kilogram of water away takes more than five times the heat needed to warm it all the way from $0\ \text{°C}$ to $100\ \text{°C}$, $4180 \times 100 = 4.18 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$. That is why a pan takes a few minutes to come to the boil, but much longer to boil dry.

## From ice to steam

<WorkedExample title="A quarter of a kilogram of ice becomes steam">
<Problem>
A block of ice of $0.250\ \text{kg}$ comes out of a freezer at $-15\ \text{°C}$. How much heat does it take to turn it all into steam at $100\ \text{°C}$? Use $c_\text{ice} = 2100\ \text{J/(kg K)}$, $c_\text{water} = 4180\ \text{J/(kg K)}$, $L_\text{f} = 3.34 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J/kg}$ and $L_\text{v} = 2.26 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J/kg}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Warm the ice to 0 °C">
$Q_1 = mc_\text{ice}\Delta T = 0.250 \times 2100 \times 15 = 7.88 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Melt the ice">
$Q_2 = mL_\text{f} = 0.250 \times 3.34 \times 10^{5} = 8.35 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Warm the water to 100 °C">
$Q_3 = mc_\text{water}\Delta T = 0.250 \times 4180 \times 100 = 1.045 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Boil the water">
$Q_4 = mL_\text{v} = 0.250 \times 2.26 \times 10^{6} = 5.65 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Add up">
$Q = Q_1 + Q_2 + Q_3 + Q_4 = 7.61 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$. Boiling alone is 74% of it. On a $1.5\ \text{kW}$ hotplate that puts all its energy into the water (lesson 3.5), the whole job takes $7.61 \times 10^{5}/1500 = 507\ \text{s}$, about eight and a half minutes, of which the boiling takes $5.65 \times 10^{5}/1500 = 377\ \text{s}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The slope of each sloping part is $\Delta T/\Delta Q = 1/(mc)$ (lesson 0.5). Ice has about half the specific heat of water, so the ice part is about twice as steep as the water part: the same heat warms ice twice as much.

## Evaporation cools

A liquid can turn into gas well below its boiling point. Lesson 4.4 showed that the molecules of a substance do not all have the same speed: some are much faster than average. At the surface of a liquid, the fastest ones can break free from the pull of their neighbours and escape. This is **evaporation** (Siyavula, Sec. 3.1).

The molecules that leave are the fast ones, so the ones left behind are slower on average. Their average kinetic energy drops, and so does the temperature of the liquid (Siyavula, Sec. 3.1). Evaporation cools whatever it happens on. The heat it takes is about the same latent heat $L_\text{v}$ per kilogram, drawn from the liquid and from whatever the liquid touches.

How hard is it for a molecule to escape? Boiling away one mole takes $40\,650\ \text{J}$, and a mole holds $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ molecules (lesson 4.3), so one molecule needs $40\,650/(6.02 \times 10^{23}) = 6.75 \times 10^{-20}\ \text{J}$. At $100\ \text{°C}$ ($373\ \text{K}$) the average kinetic energy of a molecule is $\tfrac32 k_BT = 1.5 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 373 = 7.72 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J}$ (lesson 4.4). Escaping takes almost nine times the average energy. Only the few molecules far out in the fast tail of the speed distribution make it, which is why a puddle takes hours to dry.

<WorkedExample title="Cooling by sweat">
<Problem>
On a hot day a cyclist sweats out $0.80\ \text{kg}$ of water in an hour, and all of it evaporates from her skin. How much heat does that carry away, and at what rate? Take $L_\text{v} = 2.26 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J/kg}$. (At skin temperature the latent heat is a little larger than at $100\ \text{°C}$, so this is a slight underestimate.)
</Problem>
<Step title="Heat carried away">
$Q = mL_\text{v} = 0.80 \times 2.26 \times 10^{6} = 1.81 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Rate">
One hour is $3600\ \text{s}$, so the sweat removes $1.81 \times 10^{6}/3600 = 502\ \text{W}$ (lesson 3.5), about the power of five bright old-fashioned light bulbs.
</Step>
<Step title="Without sweating">
Suppose that heat stayed in her $75\ \text{kg}$ body instead. Taking the body to be mostly water, it would warm by $\Delta T = Q/(mc) = 1.81 \times 10^{6}/(75 \times 4180) = 5.8\ \text{K}$ in that hour: a dangerous rise. Evaporating a little water gets rid of a lot of heat.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="How much ice cools a drink?">
<Problem>
A glass holds $0.30\ \text{kg}$ of lemonade at $25\ \text{°C}$; treat it as water. How much ice at $0\ \text{°C}$ must melt to cool it to $0\ \text{°C}$? Ignore the glass and the air.
</Problem>
<Step title="Heat the lemonade must lose">
$Q = mc_\text{water}\Delta T = 0.30 \times 4180 \times 25 = 3.14 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Ice that this heat melts">
All of it goes into melting ice (conservation of energy, lesson 3.4): $m_\text{ice} = Q/L_\text{f} = 3.14 \times 10^{4}/(3.34 \times 10^{5}) = 0.094\ \text{kg}$, about $94\ \text{g}$, or a few ice cubes. Melting ice cools a drink far better than the same mass of cold water would, because the melting itself takes in so much heat.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Boiling and pressure

Over any liquid, some of its molecules are in the air as vapour, and they push like any gas. This **vapour pressure** grows quickly as the liquid gets hotter, because more molecules are fast enough to escape. A liquid **boils** when its vapour pressure equals the pressure pushing on it from outside (Siyavula, Sec. 3.1). Then bubbles of vapour can form inside the liquid without being squashed flat by the air above.

So the boiling point depends on the air pressure. Lesson 4.1 showed that the air pressure falls with height. On a mountain, water boils at a lower temperature than $100\ \text{°C}$ (reading 2, "Melting point and boiling point"), and pasta takes longer to cook, because the water simply cannot get hotter than its boiling point. A pressure cooker does the opposite: a sealed lid keeps the steam in, the pressure rises, and the water can reach a temperature above $100\ \text{°C}$ before it boils, so food cooks faster.

## Phase changes in space

Changes of state happen far from the Earth too. A comet is a lump of ice and dust. Far from the Sun it stays frozen, but as it comes closer, the Sun's heat turns its ices into gas: a cloud of gas and dust, the coma, grows around it (NASA, *Comets: Facts*). In the near-vacuum of space there is almost no pressure to keep the water liquid, so the ice sublimates, going straight from solid to gas.

Water is also unusual on the Earth. Its solid is less dense than its liquid, so ice floats (reading 2, "Less dense solid phase"). Lakes freeze from the top down, and the ice on top shields the water below from the cold air.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology: a cooling universe changes state">
As the universe expanded, it cooled (lesson 4.10), and like steam that cools into water, it went through changes of state on the way. Early on it was so hot that electrons and nuclei could not stay together: matter was a glowing gas of charged particles. Once it had cooled enough, electrons and nuclei joined into neutral atoms, much as water molecules join into droplets when steam condenses. Unlike boiling or condensing water, this did not happen at one fixed temperature with a flat step: it happened gradually, as the universe cooled from about $4000\ \text{K}$ to about $3000\ \text{K}$. Lesson 8.15 shows that this moment set free the light we still see today as the cosmic microwave background.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **States of matter:** in a solid the particles vibrate about fixed places, in a liquid they slide past each other, and in a gas they fly freely. What decides the state is how strongly they attract each other and how much energy they have.
- **Changes of state:** melting and freezing, evaporation, boiling and condensation, sublimation and deposition. Going from solid towards gas takes in heat; going back gives it out.
- **Heating curve:** while a substance melts or boils, the heat breaks bonds instead of speeding up the particles, so the temperature stays constant. The sloping parts follow $Q = mc\Delta T$.
- **Latent heat:** $Q = mL$, with $L_\text{f} = 3.34 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J/kg}$ for melting ice and $L_\text{v} = 2.26 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J/kg}$ for boiling water. Turning $0.250\ \text{kg}$ of ice at $-15\ \text{°C}$ into steam takes $7.61 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$, three quarters of it for the boiling.
- **Evaporation cools:** only the fastest molecules escape from the surface, so the liquid left behind is cooler. Sweating $0.80\ \text{kg}$ of water in an hour carries heat away at about $500\ \text{W}$.
- **Boiling point and pressure:** a liquid boils when its vapour pressure equals the outside pressure, so water boils below $100\ \text{°C}$ high on a mountain and above it in a pressure cooker.
