Why does a sharp knife cut better than a blunt one, why do your ears hurt at the bottom of a swimming pool, and why does a steel ship float while a steel nail sinks? The answers use two ideas: **density**, how tightly matter is packed, and **pressure**, how concentrated a force is. Both return again and again in this block, in gases, heat and in the stars and the universe of Block 8.

## Density

A cube of iron is far heavier than a cube of wood of the same size: the same volume, but more mass. The **density** of a material is its mass per unit volume (lesson 0.2 met its unit, $\text{kg/m}^3$):

<Formula label="Density" tex="\rho = \frac{m}{V}" cite="nasadensity" at="definition: the mass of an object divided by its volume" />

Here $\rho$ (the Greek letter rho) is the density, $m$ the mass in kilograms and $V$ the volume in cubic metres. Rearranged (lesson 0.4), $m = \rho V$ and $V = m/\rho$.

The density of a substance does not depend on how much of it you have: a teaspoon of water and a bathful have the same density, $1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$. Some values to remember: air near the ground has about $1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3$ (lesson 2.6; NASA, Earth Fact Sheet), and the Earth as a whole has an average density of $5513\ \text{kg/m}^3$ (NASA, Earth Fact Sheet), five and a half times that of water.

Why is air so much less dense than water, more than 800 times? In a solid the particles are tightly packed, in a liquid they are only a little further apart, but in a gas there are large empty spaces between them (Siyavula Grade 10, Table 3.1). For the same reason a solid or a liquid has a nearly fixed density, while the density of a gas changes a lot when it is squeezed or allowed to spread out (NASA, *Gas Density*).

<WorkedExample title="A paving slab and the air in a classroom">
<Problem>
A concrete paving slab measures $0.40\ \text{m}$ by $0.30\ \text{m}$ by $0.050\ \text{m}$ and has a mass of $14.4\ \text{kg}$. (a) What is the density of the concrete? (b) A classroom is $8.0\ \text{m}$ long, $6.0\ \text{m}$ wide and $3.0\ \text{m}$ high. What is the mass of the air in it?
</Problem>
<Step title="Volume of the slab">
$V = 0.40 \times 0.30 \times 0.050 = 6.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3$.
</Step>
<Step title="Density of the concrete">
$\rho = m/V = 14.4/(6.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.4 \times 10^{3}\ \text{kg/m}^3$, about 2.4 times the density of water.
</Step>
<Step title="Mass of the air">
The room has a volume of $8.0 \times 6.0 \times 3.0 = 144\ \text{m}^3$, so $m = \rho V = 1.2 \times 144 \approx 170\ \text{kg}$. The air in a classroom weighs more than two adults, even though you hardly notice it.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Pressure

Push a drawing pin into a board with your thumb. The force on the pin's head and on its point is the same, but at the point it is squeezed onto a tiny area, and that is what lets the pin go in. What matters is the force per area, the **pressure**:

<Formula label="Pressure" tex="p = \frac{F}{A}" cite="nasapressure" at="molecular and large-scale definitions: force divided by the area it acts on" />

Here $F$ is the size of the force pushing perpendicular to a surface and $A$ the area it is spread over. The SI unit of pressure is the **pascal**, $1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N/m}^2$ (Siyavula Grade 11, Table 7.1). One pascal is a small pressure: a sheet of paper lying on a table presses with a little less than that (about $0.8\ \text{Pa}$), so pressures are often given in kilopascals ($1\ \text{kPa} = 10^{3}\ \text{Pa}$).

The same force gives a large pressure on a small area and a small pressure on a large area. A knife, a needle and a nail have tiny areas at the tip so that a modest force makes a huge pressure. Snowshoes, the wide tracks of a tractor and the broad feet of a camel do the opposite: they spread a large weight over a large area, so the pressure is small and they do not sink in.

<WorkedExample title="A car on its tyres">
<Problem>
A car of mass $1200\ \text{kg}$ stands on four tyres. Each tyre touches the road on a patch of $0.015\ \text{m}^2$ ($150\ \text{cm}^2$). What pressure do the tyres exert on the road?
</Problem>
<Step title="The force">
The tyres carry the car's weight (lesson 2.5): $F = mg = 1200 \times 9.81 = 1.18 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The area">
Four patches: $A = 4 \times 0.015 = 0.060\ \text{m}^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="The pressure">
$p = F/A = 1.18 \times 10^{4}/0.060 = 2.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$, or $200\ \text{kPa}$. That is close to how much the air pressure inside a car tyre is raised above the air outside: a tyre flattens at the bottom until the patch is large enough for the air inside to carry the car (roughly: the sidewalls carry a small share of the load).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The pressure of the atmosphere

We live at the bottom of an ocean of air. Gravity holds the air to the Earth, and the air pressure at any place comes from the weight of all the air above it (NASA, *Earth Atmosphere Model*). At sea level it is about

<Formula label="Air pressure at sea level" tex="p_0 \approx 1.01 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}" cite="reading2" at="Table 7.1: 1 atm = 101 325 Pa" />

This value is also called one **atmosphere** (atm). Weather maps give it in hectopascals, about $1013\ \text{hPa}$, and some gauges in bar ($1\ \text{bar} = 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$).

That is a lot. On each square metre the air pushes with a force of $1.01 \times 10^{5}\ \text{N}$, the weight of $1.01 \times 10^{5}/9.81 = 1.03 \times 10^{4}\ \text{kg}$: the air above every square metre of ground has a mass of about ten tonnes. We are not crushed because the pressure acts in all directions at once (NASA, *Gas Pressure*), and the air and fluids inside our body push outwards just as hard. Pump the air out of an empty can and nothing pushes back from the inside: the air outside crushes it.

Higher up there is less air above you, so the pressure falls. The air also expands as the pressure drops, so its density falls too. The graph uses NASA's model of the lower atmosphere.

## Pressure in a liquid

A diver feels the pressure grow with every metre down. To see why, picture a column of water of area $A$ reaching from the surface down to a depth $h$. Its volume is $Ah$, so its mass is $m = \rho A h$ and its weight $mg = \rho A h g$. That weight rests on the area $A$ at the bottom, so it adds a pressure $\rho A h g/A = \rho g h$. On top of that comes the pressure $p_0$ of the air pressing on the surface:

<Formula label="Pressure at depth h in a liquid" tex="p = p_0 + \rho g h" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.53), P(z) = P₀ − ρgz for a fluid of constant density, with the depth h = −z below the surface" />

Here $\rho$ is the density of the liquid and $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. The extra part, $\rho g h$, is what many pressure gauges read: the pressure above that of the air.

Three things follow.

- The pressure grows in a straight line with depth (lesson 0.5): in water every $10\ \text{m}$ adds $1000 \times 9.81 \times 10 = 9.8 \times 10^{4}\ \text{Pa}$, nearly one atmosphere.
- At a given depth the pressure is the same in every direction: it pushes on the bottom, the sides and the top of anything under water.
- It depends only on the depth, not on the shape of the container or on how much liquid there is. A narrow tube and a wide lake have the same pressure at the same depth.

<WorkedExample title="A diver in a lake">
<Problem>
A diver swims $12\ \text{m}$ below the surface of a freshwater lake ($\rho = 1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$) on a day when the air pressure is $1.01 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$. What is the pressure on her?
</Problem>
<Step title="The water's part">
$\rho g h = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 12 = 1.18 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Add the air">
$p = 1.01 \times 10^{5} + 1.18 \times 10^{5} = 2.19 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$, a little more than twice the pressure at the surface. That is why divers must breathe air at the same raised pressure, and why the eardrums need to be "equalised" on the way down.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Floating and sinking

Because the pressure grows with depth, the water pushes harder on the bottom of a submerged object than on its top. The difference is an upward force, the **buoyant force** (or upthrust). Its size is given by **Archimedes' principle**: it equals the weight of the fluid that the object pushes aside.

<Formula label="Archimedes' principle" tex="F_B = \rho_\text{fluid}\, V g" cite="mit" at="p. 116, Example: the Archimedes principle (pressure times area at the bottom gives the volume displaced times ρg)" />

Here $V$ is the volume of the object that is under the surface and $\rho_\text{fluid}$ the density of the fluid. Compare this with the object's own weight, $F_g = \rho V g$ for an object fully under water (lesson 2.5). The $V$ and $g$ are the same, so only the densities count:

- If the object is **less dense** than the fluid, the upward push wins and it rises until only part of it is under the surface: it **floats**.
- If it is **denser**, it sinks.
- If the densities are equal, it stays wherever it is put, as a fish does.

A steel ship floats because it is not solid steel: the hull with all the air inside it has, on average, a lower density than water.

<WorkedExample title="A wooden block in water">
<Problem>
A block of wood has a volume of $2.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3$ and a density of $600\ \text{kg/m}^3$. It is held completely under water and then let go. What happens, and how much of it is under water when it comes to rest?
</Problem>
<Step title="The forces when it is under water">
Weight: $F_g = 600 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 9.81 = 11.8\ \text{N}$. Buoyant force: $F_B = 1000 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 9.81 = 19.6\ \text{N}$. The upward force is larger, so the block rises.
</Step>
<Step title="Floating at rest">
At rest the forces balance (lesson 2.2), so the block pushes aside water of the same weight as itself. Its mass is $600 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3} = 1.2\ \text{kg}$, and $1.2\ \text{kg}$ of water has a volume of $1.2 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3$. That is $1.2/2.0 = 0.60$, or 60% of the block, the same as $600/1000$: a floating object has the fraction $\rho/\rho_\text{fluid}$ of its volume under the surface.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Pressure in a gas

Where does the pressure of the air in a closed bottle or a bicycle tyre come from? The particles of a gas move all the time, in all directions, and keep colliding with each other and with the walls (Siyavula Grade 11, Sec. 7.1). Every hit gives the wall a tiny push. Billions upon billions of hits each second add up to a steady force, and that force divided by the area of the wall is the pressure (NASA, *Gas Pressure*).

This picture already says what changes the pressure. Squeeze the same gas into a smaller space and the particles hit the walls more often: the pressure rises. Heat the gas and the particles move faster and hit harder and more often: the pressure rises again. Lesson 4.3 puts this into one law, and lesson 4.4 works out the pressure from the motion of the particles.

## Summary

- **Density** is mass per volume, $\rho = m/V$, in $\text{kg/m}^3$. Water has $1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$ and air near the ground about $1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3$: the particles of a gas are far apart.
- **Pressure** is force per area, $p = F/A$, in pascals ($1\ \text{Pa} = 1\ \text{N/m}^2$). A small area gives a large pressure, a large area a small one.
- The atmosphere presses with about $p_0 = 1.01 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$ at sea level, the weight of the air above; it falls with height.
- In a liquid at rest $p = p_0 + \rho g h$: the pressure grows in a straight line with depth, acts in all directions and does not depend on the shape of the container. In water every $10\ \text{m}$ adds about one atmosphere.
- A fluid pushes up with the weight of the fluid pushed aside (Archimedes), $F_B = \rho_\text{fluid} V g$: an object less dense than the fluid floats.
- The pressure of a gas comes from its particles hitting the walls.
