This set practises the ten lessons of Block 4, from density and pressure to the cooling of the expanding universe. The problems, hints and solutions are our own; the methods are those of the lessons, which build on Siyavula's chapters on ideal gases and on the chemistry of water, and on MIT course notes and slides on fluids, statistical physics, heat, heat transfer and the early universe. Problems 1 to 10 count 10 points each, 100 in all; Problem 11 is optional. Throughout, take $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$, $R = 8.31\ \text{J/(mol K)}$, $k_B = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$, $N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}$ and $1\ \text{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$. For water take $c_\text{water} = 4180\ \text{J/(kg K)}$, $L_\text{f} = 3.34 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J/kg}$ and $L_\text{v} = 2.26 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J/kg}$, and for ice $c_\text{ice} = 2100\ \text{J/(kg K)}$, as in lessons 4.5 and 4.6.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first, starting with a sketch. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box wants the number only, in the unit printed in its label, for example `0.25` or `1.2e5`; small differences from rounding are accepted. Give a sign only where the problem asks for one; otherwise type the size. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `(a + b)/c`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A fishing float and a diver's camera" points={10}>

A fishing float has a volume of $1.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3$ and a mass of $0.36\ \text{kg}$. It floats in the sea, where the water has a density of $1025\ \text{kg/m}^3$. The air pressure at the surface is $1.01 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the density of the float?

<Answer id="p1-a" label="Density (kg/m³):" value="240" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p1-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Density is mass per volume.

<Step title="Density">

<Formula label="Density" tex="\rho = \frac{m}{V}" cite="nasadensity" at="definition: the mass of an object divided by its volume" />

$\rho = 0.36/(1.5 \times 10^{-3}) = 240\ \text{kg/m}^3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

That is less than a quarter of the density of water, so the float floats high. Dividing the volume by the mass instead would give $4.2 \times 10^{-3}$, far too small for any solid.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

What fraction of the float's volume is under the surface when it floats at rest?

<Hint>At rest the buoyant force equals the weight. Write both with densities.</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="Fraction under water:" value="0.234" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p1-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A floating object pushes aside its own weight of fluid.

<Step title="Forces in balance">

<Formula label="Archimedes' principle" tex="F_B = \rho_\text{fluid}\, V g" cite="reading1" at="p. 116, Example: the Archimedes principle (pressure times area at the bottom gives the volume displaced times ρg)" />

Here $V$ is the volume under the surface. The water pushes up with $\rho_\text{fluid}\,g$ times that volume, and the float weighs $\rho\,g$ times its whole volume. At rest the two are equal, so the fraction under water is $\rho/\rho_\text{fluid} = 240/1025 = 0.234$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

About 23% of the float is under water. In fresh water ($1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$) it would be $0.240$: a little more of it would sink in, because fresh water is less dense than seawater.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

A diver works $18\ \text{m}$ below the surface. What is the total pressure on her? Type a number like `1.2e5` if you like.

<Answer id="p1-c" label="Pressure (Pa):" value="2.82e5" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p1-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The pressure at depth is the air pressure on the surface plus the weight of the water column above each square metre.

<Step title="Pressure at depth">

<Formula label="Pressure at depth h in a liquid" tex="p = p_0 + \rho g h" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (6.53), P(z) = P₀ − ρgz for a fluid of constant density, with the depth h = −z below the surface" />

The water adds $\rho g h = 1025 \times 9.81 \times 18 = 1.81 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$, so $p = 1.01 \times 10^{5} + 1.81 \times 10^{5} = 2.82 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

That is about $2.8$ atmospheres: in water every $10\ \text{m}$ adds roughly one atmosphere, and $18\ \text{m}$ adds a little less than two.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

The diver's camera sits in a sealed housing with a flat window of area $0.0050\ \text{m}^2$. The air sealed inside the housing is at $1.01 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$. At $18\ \text{m}$ depth, what is the size of the net force that the water and the air inside together exert on the window?

<Hint>The air inside pushes outwards on the window, the water pushes inwards. Which pressure difference is left over?</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="Net force (N):" value="905" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p1-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Force is pressure times area; the two sides of the window push in opposite directions.

<Step title="The pressure difference">

<Formula label="Pressure" tex="p = \frac{F}{A}" cite="nasapressure" at="pressure force = pressure × area" />

The water pushes in with $2.82 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$, the air inside pushes out with $1.01 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$. The difference is just the water's part, $\rho g h = 1.81 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$, so $F = 1.81 \times 10^{5} \times 0.0050 = 905\ \text{N}$, inwards.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

That is the weight of about $92\ \text{kg}$ on a window of only $50\ \text{cm}^2$, smaller than a postcard, which is why the window must be thick. Using the total pressure and forgetting the air inside would give $1.41 \times 10^{3}\ \text{N}$, too much.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Liquid nitrogen and a pizza oven" points={10}>

A science teacher shows the class some liquid nitrogen, which boils at $77\ \text{K}$, and a block of dry ice (frozen carbon dioxide), which turns into gas at $-78.5\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. Use $T = \theta + 273.15$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the boiling point of liquid nitrogen in degrees Celsius? Give the sign.

<Answer id="p2-a" label="Boiling point (°C, with sign):" value="-196" tolerance="1%" calc="hs4p-p2-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The kelvin and Celsius scales have the same steps and differ only in where zero is.

<Step title="Kelvin to Celsius">

<Formula label="Celsius to kelvin" tex="T = \theta + 273.15" cite="nist" at="SI Units – Temperature: 0 °C is 273.15 K" />

$\theta = T - 273.15 = 77 - 273.15 = -196\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$77\ \text{K}$ is only $77$ steps above absolute zero, so in Celsius it must lie $77$ degrees above $-273.15\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, as it does.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

How many kelvin warmer than the liquid nitrogen is the dry ice?

<Hint>Change both temperatures to the same scale first.</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="Difference (K):" value="118" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p2-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A temperature difference is the same number in kelvin and in degrees Celsius.

<Step title="Same scale">

Dry ice: $T = -78.5 + 273.15 = 194.65\ \text{K}$. The difference is $194.65 - 77 = 118\ \text{K}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check by another route">

In Celsius: $-78.5 - (-196.15) = 117.65\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, which is the same difference, $118\ \text{K}$, because a step of one degree Celsius is a step of one kelvin.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

The class then visits a pizzeria. The air in the oven is heated from $20\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ to $320\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. By what factor does the average kinetic energy of the air molecules in the oven grow?

<Hint>The average kinetic energy is proportional to the temperature on one of the two scales. Which?</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="Factor:" value="2.02" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p2-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The average kinetic energy of the molecules is proportional to the absolute temperature, so ratios must be taken in kelvin.

<Step title="Temperatures in kelvin">

The oven goes from $20 + 273.15 = 293.15\ \text{K}$ to $320 + 273.15 = 593.15\ \text{K}$. The factor is $593.15/293.15 = 2.02$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The Celsius numbers would suggest a factor of $320/20 = 16$, which means nothing, because $0\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ is not the bottom of the scale. In kelvin the temperature only roughly doubles, and so does the average kinetic energy.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Back in the lab, two identical sealed flasks of air, both at $20\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ and at the same pressure, are joined by a wide tube to make one larger container. Which quantities of the air are doubled?

<Answer id="p2-d" label="Answer:" choices={["The volume and the thermal energy; the temperature and the pressure stay the same", "The temperature and the thermal energy; the volume stays the same", "All of them: the volume, the temperature, the pressure and the thermal energy", "Only the volume; the thermal energy is spread over twice the space, so it stays the same"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Temperature and pressure do not depend on how much gas there is; volume and thermal energy do.

<Step title="Intensive and extensive">

The joined container holds twice as many molecules in twice the volume, each moving as before. The temperature, which measures the average kinetic energy of a molecule, stays $20\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, and so does the pressure. The thermal energy is the total energy of all the molecules, so it doubles, and so does the volume.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is the same as pouring two cups of tea at $60\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ into one pot (lesson 4.2): the pot is still at $60\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ but holds twice the thermal energy.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A car tyre on a long drive" points={10}>

Early in the morning, at $8.0\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, a tyre gauge on a car tyre reads $2.20 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$. Like most gauges, it reads the pressure above that of the outside air, which is $1.01 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$. The air inside the tyre has a volume of $0.025\ \text{m}^3$; take this volume as fixed throughout.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

How many moles of air are in the tyre?

<Hint>The gas laws need the full pressure of the gas, measured from zero, and the temperature in kelvin.</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="Amount of air (mol):" value="3.44" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p3-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The ideal gas law gives the amount of gas from one state, in SI units.

<Step title="Pressure and temperature">

The pressure of the air inside is $p = 2.20 \times 10^{5} + 1.01 \times 10^{5} = 3.21 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$, and $T = 8.0 + 273 = 281\ \text{K}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The ideal gas law">

<Formula label="The ideal gas law" tex="pV = nRT" cite="mit" at="Sec. 7.2, The ideal gas equation" />

$n = pV/(RT) = 3.21 \times 10^{5} \times 0.025/(8.31 \times 281) = 3.44\ \text{mol}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Using the gauge reading alone would give $2.36\ \text{mol}$, and using $8.0$ instead of $281\ \text{K}$ would give about $121\ \text{mol}$: both slips are easy to make.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

After a long drive on a motorway, the air in the tyre has warmed to $38\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. What does the gauge read now? Type a number like `1.2e5` if you like.

<Answer id="p3-b" label="Gauge reading (Pa):" value="2.54e5" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p3-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At fixed volume the pressure is proportional to the absolute temperature; the gauge then leaves out the outside air.

<Step title="The pressure law">

<Formula label="Pressure law (fixed amount of gas, constant volume)" tex="\frac{p_1}{T_1} = \frac{p_2}{T_2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 7.2, Pressure-temperature relation" />

$T_2 = 38 + 273 = 311\ \text{K}$, so $p_2 = p_1 T_2/T_1 = 3.21 \times 10^{5} \times 311/281 = 3.55 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The gauge reading">

$3.553 \times 10^{5} - 1.01 \times 10^{5} = 2.54 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Applying the pressure law to the gauge reading itself would give $2.43 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$: too little, because the pressure law holds for the full pressure, not for the part above the outside air. That is why tyre pressures should be checked when the tyres are cold.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

While the tyre is still at $38\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, the driver lets some air out until the gauge reads $2.20 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$ again. What fraction of the air in the tyre does she let out?

<Hint>With the same volume and pressure, how does the amount of gas depend on the temperature?</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="Fraction let out:" value="0.0965" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p3-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At fixed pressure and volume, $n = pV/(RT)$: the amount of gas is inversely proportional to the absolute temperature.

<Step title="Amount left">

With the same $p$ and $V$ as in the morning, $n_2/n_1 = T_1/T_2 = 281/311 = 0.9035$. So $1 - 0.9035 = 0.0965$ of the air has left, almost a tenth.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

When the tyre cools to $8.0\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ again, the gauge will read less than the $2.20 \times 10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$ it should: letting air out of a hot tyre leaves it underinflated.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

How many molecules of air did she let out? Type a number like `1.2e23`.

<Answer id="p3-d" label="Number of molecules:" value="2.00e23" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p3-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Moles count particles: one mole holds $N_A$ of them.

<Step title="Count the molecules">

<Formula label="The Avogadro constant and the mole" tex="N_A = 6.02 \times 10^{23}\ \text{mol}^{-1}, \qquad N = n\,N_A" cite="nist-na" at="Avogadro constant" />

She let out $0.0965 \times 3.437 = 0.332\ \text{mol}$, so $N = 0.332 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 2.00 \times 10^{23}$ molecules.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The whole tyre holds $3.437 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 2.07 \times 10^{24}$ molecules, and a tenth of that is about $2 \times 10^{23}$, as found.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Neon in a glass bulb" points={10}>

A sealed glass bulb of volume $1.5 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3$ holds neon gas at $300\ \text{K}$ and a pressure of $2.0 \times 10^{3}\ \text{Pa}$. Neon is made of single atoms with a mass of $20\ \text{u}$ each.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the average kinetic energy of a neon atom? Type a number like `1.2e-21`.

<Answer id="p4-a" label="Average kinetic energy (J):" value="6.21e-21" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p4-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The absolute temperature fixes the average kinetic energy of a molecule, whatever its mass.

<Step title="Average kinetic energy">

<Formula label="Average kinetic energy of a molecule" tex="E_{k,\text{avg}} = \tfrac{3}{2}\,k_B T" cite="reading2" at="slide 5: the average energy of one atom of a monatomic gas is (3/2)kT" />

$E_{k,\text{avg}} = 1.5 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 300 = 6.21 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The mass of the atom was not needed: any gas at $300\ \text{K}$ has the same average kinetic energy per molecule. Forgetting the factor $\tfrac32$ would give $4.14 \times 10^{-21}\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the rms speed of the neon atoms?

<Hint>First find the mass of one atom in kilograms.</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="Rms speed (m/s):" value="612" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p4-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Setting $\tfrac12 m\langle v^2\rangle$ equal to $\tfrac32 k_B T$ gives the rms speed.

<Step title="Mass of an atom">

$m = 20 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 3.32 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Rms speed">

<Formula label="The rms speed" tex="v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}}" cite="reading2" at="slide 5: the average squared momentum is 3mkT; dividing by m² gives the average of v²" />

$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \times 300/(3.32 \times 10^{-26})} = \sqrt{3.74 \times 10^{5}} = 612\ \text{m/s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

<Exercise id="p7" title="A cool box at the beach" points={10}>

How much heat flows into the box each second?

<Answer id="p7-a" label="Heat flow (W):" value="32.1" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p7-a" />

<Solution>

<Step title="The conduction law">

</Step>

</Solution>

<Answer id="p7-b" label="Ice melted (kg):" value="0.346" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p7-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** All the heat that leaks in goes into melting ice, at constant temperature.

<Step title="Heat in one hour">

A new model of the cool box has, in addition, an outer shell of hard wood $1.0\ \text{cm}$ thick ($k = 0.16\ \text{W/(m K)}$) over the whole $0.80\ \text{m}^2$. The inner and outer faces are at $2\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ and $30\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ as before. What is the heat flow now?

<Hint>The heat has to pass through both layers, one after the other. Give each a thermal resistance.</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-c" label="Heat flow (W):" value="29.5" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p7-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For layers in series the thermal resistances add.

<Step title="The two resistances">

<Formula label="Thermal resistance of a layer" tex="R = \frac{d}{k A}, \qquad P = \frac{\Delta T}{R}" cite="reading6" at="Eq. (2.19), R = L/kA" />

<Formula label="Two layers in series" tex="P = \frac{\Delta T}{R_1 + R_2}" cite="reading6" at="Eq. (2.20), composite slab" />

</Step>

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

For a box full of drinks, where is the best place for the ice packs?

<Answer id="p7-d" label="Answer:" choices={["At the top: air cooled by the ice becomes denser and sinks, so a convection current carries the cold air down past the drinks", "At the bottom: cold air sinks, so it stays around the ice and keeps the bottom coldest", "It makes no difference: still air conducts so badly that only the walls matter"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Cold air is denser than warm air, so it sinks; that sets up a convection current.

<Step title="Convection in the box">

</Step>

</Solution>

</Step>

</Step>

</Solution>

</Step>

</Solution>

</Step>

</Solution>

<Step title="Heat to the river">

</Step>

<Step title="Entropy changes">

<Formula label="Entropy change from heat" tex="\Delta S = \frac{Q}{T}" cite="reading10" at="slide 8: dS = đQ/T for a system in equilibrium with a bath" />

Hot water: $-5.0 \times 10^{7}/450 = -1.111 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J/K}$. River: $+3.8 \times 10^{7}/290 = +1.310 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J/K}$. Total: $+1.99 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J/K}$ each second.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The total rises, as the second law requires. A perfect Carnot engine would leave it unchanged: it would give the river only $50 \times 290/450 = 32.2\ \text{MJ}$ each second.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Any heat engine that takes heat $Q_h$ from a reservoir at $T_h$ must give at least some heat to its cold reservoir at $T_c$. Write that least amount. Type `Q` for $Q_h$, `th` for $T_h$ and `tc` for $T_c$.

<Answer id="p9-d" label="Least $Q_c =$" expr="Q*tc/th" vars={{ Q: [10, 100], th: [400, 900], tc: [250, 350] }} names={{ Q: "Q_h", th: "T_h", tc: "T_c" }}
  mistakes={[{ expr: "Q*(1 - tc/th)", hint: "That is the most work the engine can do, not the heat it must give away." }, { expr: "Q*th/tc", hint: "Upside down: the heat given away is less than the heat taken in." }, { expr: "Q*tc", hint: "Divide by the hot temperature: the entropy taken from the hot side is Q_h/T_h." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The entropy given to the cold reservoir must at least make up for the entropy taken from the hot one.

<Step title="Entropy balance">

$Q_c/T_c \geq Q_h/T_h$, so $Q_c \geq Q_h\,\dfrac{T_c}{T_h}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For this station: $50 \times 290/450 = 32.2\ \text{MJ}$, as in part (c). The work left over is then at most $Q_h(1 - T_c/T_h)$, which gives the Carnot limit of part (a) again.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 10

<Exercise id="p10" title="An expanding gas and the cooling universe" points={10}>

An insulated cylinder holds $0.050\ \text{mol}$ of argon at $320\ \text{K}$ in a volume of $2.0\ \text{L}$. The gas slowly pushes the piston out until its volume is $6.75\ \text{L}$; no heat flows in or out. Treat argon as an ideal monatomic gas.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the temperature of the argon at the end?

<Hint>Without heat, $T V^{2/3}$ stays the same for a monatomic ideal gas. Note that $6.75/2.0 = 3.375 = 1.5^{3}$.</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-a" label="Temperature (K):" value="142" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p10-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** An expanding gas that takes in no heat pays for its work out of its internal energy, so it cools.

<Step title="Adiabatic rule">

<Formula label="Temperature after an adiabatic change" tex="T_2 = T_1 \left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{2/3}" cite="reading4" at="slides 21–22: T V^(γ−1) = constant, γ = 5/3 for a monatomic gas, rearranged" />

$V_1/V_2 = 2.0/6.75 = (1/1.5)^{3}$, so $(V_1/V_2)^{2/3} = (1/1.5)^{2} = 0.444$, and $T_2 = 320 \times 0.444 = 142\ \text{K}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The argon cools from $47\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ to about $-131\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. Using the power $1$ instead of $2/3$ would give $94.8\ \text{K}$, too cold.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

How much work does the argon do on the piston?

<Answer id="p10-b" label="Work done by the gas (J):" value="111" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p10-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $Q = 0$, the first law gives $\Delta U = W$: all the work comes out of the internal energy.

<Step title="Change in internal energy">

$\Delta U = \tfrac32 nR\,\Delta T = 1.5 \times 0.050 \times 8.31 \times (142.2 - 320) = -111\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Work">

$W = \Delta U = -111\ \text{J}$ is the work done on the gas, so the argon does $111\ \text{J}$ of work on the piston.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The internal energy falls from $\tfrac32 nRT_1 = 199\ \text{J}$ to $89\ \text{J}$, and the difference is the $111\ \text{J}$ of work, to rounding.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

The universe cools in the same way as it expands. The cosmic microwave background now has a temperature of $2.725\ \text{K}$. What was its temperature when every distance in the universe was 250 times smaller than today ($1 + z = 250$)?

<Answer id="p10-c" label="Temperature then (K):" value="681" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p10-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The temperature of the light in the universe goes as $1/a$: when every distance was $1 + z$ times smaller, it was $1 + z$ times hotter.

<Step title="Temperature then">

<Formula label="Temperature then and now" tex="T_\text{then} = (1 + z)\,T_\text{now}" cite="reading11" at="p. 18 (T ∝ 1/a), with 1 + z the ratio of the scale factors now and then" />

$T_\text{then} = 250 \times 2.725 = 681\ \text{K}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

That is hotter than an oven, but still well below the $3000\ \text{K}$ or so at which atoms formed (lesson 4.10), so at that time the light was already travelling freely.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

At what redshift $z$ did the background radiation have a temperature of $1000\ \text{K}$?

<Answer id="p10-d" label="Redshift $z$:" value="366" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p10-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Turn the rule round: $1 + z = T_\text{then}/T_\text{now}$.

<Step title="Redshift">

$1 + z = 1000/2.725 = 367$, so $z = 366$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$367 \times 2.725 = 1000\ \text{K}$. The difference between $z$ and $1 + z$ matters little here, but it is $1 + z$ that tells how many times smaller the universe was.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 11

<Exercise id="p11" title="Light and atoms in the expanding universe" points={10} optional>

This problem is optional: it does not count towards the score of the set.

Follow a region of the young universe after the light and the atoms in it have stopped exchanging energy. Take the light to cool as $1/a$, as in lesson 4.10, and the atoms, a monatomic gas that takes in no heat, to follow $T V^{2/3} = \text{constant}$, with the volume of the region growing as $a^{3}$. Today the light is at $2.725\ \text{K}$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What was the temperature of the light when $1 + z = 600$?

<Answer id="p11-a" label="Temperature (K):" value="1635" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p11-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $T_\text{then} = (1 + z)\,T_\text{now}$.

<Step title="Temperature">

$600 \times 2.725 = 1635\ \text{K}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

That is below the $3000\ \text{K}$ or so of decoupling (lesson 4.10), so by then the light was no longer scattering off free electrons.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

By the time $1 + z = 150$, by what factor has every distance grown since $1 + z = 600$, and what is the temperature of the light then?

<Answer id="p11-b" label="Temperature of the light (K):" value="409" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p11-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Distances grow by the ratio of the two values of $1 + z$; the light's temperature falls by the same factor.

<Step title="Growth and temperature">

Every distance has grown by $600/150 = 4$, so the light has cooled to $1635/4 = 409\ \text{K}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check by another route">

Directly from today: $150 \times 2.725 = 409\ \text{K}$, the same.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Suppose the atoms were also at $1635\ \text{K}$ when $1 + z = 600$, and from then on exchanged no heat with the light or anything else. What would their temperature be at $1 + z = 150$?

<Hint>The volume of the region grows as $a^{3}$. Put that into $T V^{2/3} = \text{constant}$.</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-c" label="Temperature of the atoms (K):" value="102" tolerance="3%" calc="hs4p-p11-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $V \propto a^{3}$, $T \propto V^{-2/3}$ becomes $T \propto 1/a^{2}$: the atoms cool faster than the light.

<Step title="Cooling of the atoms">

The volume grows by $4^{3} = 64$, so $T = 1635 \times 64^{-2/3} = 1635/16 = 102\ \text{K}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The light is then at $409\ \text{K}$, four times warmer. Left on their own, atoms cool as $1/a^{2}$ and light as $1/a$ (lesson 4.10): when the universe grows by a factor 4, the atoms cool by $4^{2} = 16$ and the light only by $4$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
