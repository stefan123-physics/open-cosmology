Put a pan of water on a stove. The metal of the pan is soon too hot to touch, while the water inside is still only lukewarm, although both have been on the same flame. Some materials need much more energy than others to warm up, and water needs more than almost anything else. This lesson makes "heat" precise, shows how to calculate the energy needed to warm something, and uses energy conservation to find the temperature when hot and cold things are mixed.

## Heat is energy on the move

Lesson 4.2 showed that energy flows by itself from a hotter object to a colder one until their temperatures are equal. That flowing energy is what physicists call **heat**:

<Callout kind="key">
**Heat** is energy that flows from one object to another because of a difference in temperature. It always flows from the higher temperature to the lower one (MIT 8.044 Lecture 6, slides 8 and 9).
</Callout>

Heat is something that happens, not something an object has. A cup of tea does not "contain heat". It contains **thermal energy** (also called internal energy): the energy of the random motion of its molecules (lesson 4.2). While the tea cools, heat flows out of it into the air and the table, and its thermal energy goes down by exactly that amount. This is the same distinction as between a bank balance and a payment: the tea has a balance of thermal energy, and heat is a payment from one object to another.

Because heat is energy, it is measured in **joules** (lesson 0.2), like work and kinetic energy. The MIT course 8.044 makes the link with work explicit (slides 7 and 8). The thermal energy of a substance can change in two ways: work can be done on it, or heat can flow into it. You can warm water by stirring it hard for a long time, which is work done by the spoon, or by putting it on a flame, which is heat. Both add energy, and energy is conserved. Lesson 4.8 turns this into the first law of thermodynamics.

What happens to the energy inside? Heating a substance gives its particles more energy, so they move faster (Siyavula Grade 10, 3.2). In a gas this shows up directly as a higher average kinetic energy of the molecules (lesson 4.4), and so as a higher temperature.

## Specific heat capacity

How much energy does it take to warm something? Three things matter:

- **The temperature rise.** Warming a pan of water by 20 degrees takes twice as long on the same flame as warming it by 10 degrees.
- **The mass.** Twice as much water takes twice as much energy for the same temperature rise.
- **The material.** A kilogram of water needs far more energy than a kilogram of iron for the same rise. This is why the pan gets hot so much sooner than the water in it (Siyavula Grade 11, 4.2).

The material enters through one number. The **specific heat capacity** $c$ of a substance (or just its specific heat) is the energy needed to raise the temperature of one kilogram of it by one degree (Siyavula Grade 11, 4.2):

<Formula label="Specific heat capacity" tex="c = \frac{Q}{m\,\Delta T}" cite="mit" at="4.2, The unique properties of water (ESBMT): definition of specific heat as the heat energy needed to raise the temperature of a unit mass by one degree" />

Here $Q$ is the heat that flows in, $m$ the mass and $\Delta T$ the rise in temperature. The unit of $c$ is joules per kilogram per kelvin, $\text{J/(kg K)}$. A step of one kelvin is a step of one degree Celsius (lesson 4.2), so $\Delta T$ is the same number on both scales, and the same $c$ works for both. Rearranged, the definition gives the energy needed to warm (or released in cooling) any amount of a substance:

<Formula label="Heat needed to change the temperature" tex="Q = m\,c\,\Delta T" cite="mit" at="4.2, The unique properties of water (ESBMT): the definition of specific heat, rearranged" />

When the object cools, $\Delta T$ is negative, and so is $Q$: heat flows out.

Sometimes you want the energy per degree for a whole object, not per kilogram. That is its **heat capacity** $C$, the heat that flows in divided by the rise in temperature. For an object made of one material, it is the mass times the specific heat:

<Formula label="Heat capacity of an object" tex="C = \frac{Q}{\Delta T} = m\,c" cite="reading1" at="slide 10 (heat capacities, the heat added per unit rise in temperature, at constant volume or pressure); C = mc follows from Q = mcΔT" />

A full kettle has a larger heat capacity than a half-full one, but the water in both has the same specific heat. The MIT course distinguishes heating at constant volume from heating at constant pressure (slide 10); for solids and liquids the difference is small, and this lesson does not need it.

### Some values

The table gives the specific heat of some common materials near room temperature. The values are worked out from the NIST Chemistry WebBook, which gives the heat capacity per mole; dividing by the mass of one mole gives it per kilogram. They are rounded.

| Material | $c$ in $\text{J/(kg K)}$ |
|---|---|
| Water (liquid) | 4180 |
| Ethanol (alcohol) | 2440 |
| Aluminium | 900 |
| Iron | 450 |
| Copper | 385 |
| Lead | 130 |

(Sources: NIST WebBook pages for water, ethanol, aluminium, iron, copper and lead; NIST gives 4184 for water at 25 °C, and this course uses 4180 throughout.)

Water stands out. One reason, given by Siyavula (Grade 11, 4.2), is the strong attraction between water molecules, the hydrogen bonds. Part of the energy that flows into water goes into pulling against these bonds rather than into making the molecules move faster, so the temperature rises less. The metals at the bottom of the table warm up quickly: that is why a metal spoon in hot soup soon becomes hot.

<WorkedExample title="The same energy, three materials">
<Problem>
A heater gives $5.0\ \text{kJ}$ of heat to each of three blocks of $0.50\ \text{kg}$: one of aluminium, one of copper, and one of water (in a thin bag). By how much does each warm up?
</Problem>
<Step title="The formula">
Solve $Q = mc\Delta T$ for the temperature rise: $\Delta T = Q/(mc)$, with $Q = 5000\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Aluminium and copper">
Aluminium: $\Delta T = 5000/(0.50 \times 900) = 11.1\ \text{K}$. Copper: $\Delta T = 5000/(0.50 \times 385) = 26\ \text{K}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Water">
$\Delta T = 5000/(0.50 \times 4180) = 2.4\ \text{K}$. The same energy that warms the copper by 26 degrees warms the water by less than 3.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Heating with a heater

An electric heater with power $P$ hands over the energy $P\,\Delta t$ in a time $\Delta t$ (lesson 3.5: power is energy per second). If all of it goes into the substance, then $Q = P\,\Delta t$, and

<Formula label="Temperature rise from a heater" tex="m\,c\,\Delta T = P\,\Delta t" cite="mit" at="4.2 (ESBMT): Q = mcΔT from the definition of specific heat, with the heat Q supplied at power P during Δt (power as energy per second, lesson 3.5)" />

So $\Delta T = P\,\Delta t/(mc)$: with a steady heater the temperature rises in a straight line with time, and the slope $P/(mc)$ is steeper for a stronger heater or a smaller mass.

<WorkedExample title="A kettle">
<Problem>
A $2.4\ \text{kW}$ kettle holds $0.60\ \text{kg}$ of water at $14\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. (a) How much energy is needed to bring the water to $100\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$? (b) How long does that take, if all the energy of the kettle goes into the water?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The energy">
$\Delta T = 100 - 14 = 86\ \text{K}$, so $Q = mc\Delta T = 0.60 \times 4180 \times 86 = 2.16 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The time">
$\Delta t = Q/P = 2.16 \times 10^{5}/2400 = 90\ \text{s}$, a minute and a half.
</Step>
<Step title="Reality check">
A real kettle takes a bit longer: some of the energy warms the kettle itself and leaks into the air. Lesson 6.8 uses a measured time like this to find how efficient a kettle is.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The same idea measures a specific heat in the laboratory: give a known amount of energy with a heater, measure the mass and the temperature rise, and work out $c = Q/(m\Delta T)$.

<WorkedExample title="Which metal is it?">
<Problem>
A $0.80\ \text{kg}$ block of an unknown metal has a $50\ \text{W}$ heater inside it. After the heater has been on for $4.0$ minutes, the block has warmed from $18.0\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ to $51.5\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. Assume no energy leaks away. Find the specific heat and use the table to guess the metal.
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy in">
$Q = P\,\Delta t = 50 \times 240 = 1.2 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}$ (4.0 minutes is 240 s).
</Step>
<Step title="Specific heat">
$\Delta T = 51.5 - 18.0 = 33.5\ \text{K}$, so $c = Q/(m\Delta T) = 1.2 \times 10^{4}/(0.80 \times 33.5) = 448\ \text{J/(kg K)}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The metal">
That is very close to iron, $450\ \text{J/(kg K)}$. If some energy had leaked into the air, the block would have warmed less, and the measured $c$ would come out a little too large.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Mixing hot and cold

Drop something hot into something cold, and heat flows from the hot one to the cold one until they reach the same temperature $\theta$ (lesson 4.2). If no energy leaks into the container or the air, energy conservation says that the heat lost by the hot object is exactly the heat gained by the cold one (MIT 8.044 Lecture 6, slide 8: energy is conserved). Write the hot object with the index 1 and the cold one with 2. Each loses or gains $Q = mc\Delta T$, so

<Formula label="Heat lost equals heat gained" tex="m_1 c_1 (\theta_1 - \theta) = m_2 c_2 (\theta - \theta_2)" cite="reading1" at="slide 8 (heat is a flow of energy; energy is conserved), with Q = mcΔT for each object" />

Both sides are positive: the hot object falls from $\theta_1$ to $\theta$, the cold one rises from $\theta_2$ to $\theta$. Solving for the final temperature gives a weighted average of the two starting temperatures, weighted by the heat capacities $m_1 c_1$ and $m_2 c_2$:

<Formula label="Final temperature of a mixture" tex="\theta = \frac{m_1 c_1 \theta_1 + m_2 c_2 \theta_2}{m_1 c_1 + m_2 c_2}" cite="reading1" at="slide 8 (energy is conserved); solved from heat lost = heat gained" />

The object with the larger heat capacity "wins": the final temperature ends up close to its starting temperature. Lesson 4.2 mixed equal masses of water and found the final temperature halfway; that is the special case $m_1 c_1 = m_2 c_2$.

<WorkedExample title="Unequal amounts of water">
<Problem>
You pour $0.20\ \text{kg}$ of water at $85\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ into a bowl with $0.50\ \text{kg}$ of water at $15\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. Ignore the bowl and the air. What is the final temperature?
</Problem>
<Step title="The specific heat cancels">
Both are water, so $c$ appears in every term of the formula and cancels: $\theta = (m_1 \theta_1 + m_2 \theta_2)/(m_1 + m_2)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Final temperature">
$\theta = (0.20 \times 85 + 0.50 \times 15)/(0.20 + 0.50) = 24.5/0.70 = 35\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Heat lost by the hot water: $0.20 \times 4180 \times (85 - 35) = 4.18 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}$. Heat gained by the cold water: $0.50 \times 4180 \times (35 - 15) = 4.18 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}$. They are equal, as they must be.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Hot copper in cold water">
<Problem>
A $0.30\ \text{kg}$ copper block is taken from boiling water at $95\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ and dropped into $0.25\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. What is the final temperature?
</Problem>
<Step title="Heat capacities">
Copper: $m_1 c_1 = 0.30 \times 385 = 115.5\ \text{J/K}$. Water: $m_2 c_2 = 0.25 \times 4180 = 1045\ \text{J/K}$, about nine times as much, although there is less water than copper.
</Step>
<Step title="Final temperature">
$\theta = (115.5 \times 95 + 1045 \times 20)/(115.5 + 1045) = 31\,873/1160.5 = 27.5\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$.
</Step>
<Step title="What it means">
The copper cools by $67.5$ degrees, while the water warms by only $7.5$. The water, with its much larger heat capacity, barely notices the hot block.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why water softens the climate

Water's large specific heat matters far beyond the kitchen. The same energy that warms a kilogram of iron by 9 degrees warms a kilogram of water by only 1. Seas and lakes therefore act as huge stores of energy (Siyavula Grade 11, 4.2). On a summer day, or during a whole summer, they absorb a great deal of energy from the Sun while warming only a little. At night and in winter they give that energy back to the air while cooling only a little. Land, with its smaller specific heat, warms and cools much faster.

That is why towns by the sea usually have milder weather than towns far inland: cooler summers, warmer winters and smaller differences between day and night (Siyavula Grade 11, 4.2). Water's large specific heat also helps living things: your body is mostly water, so it takes a lot of energy to change its temperature, and that helps keep it steady.

Put a block of iron or brick and a beaker of water on the burners, heat them, and watch their thermometers. Which warms faster? Then put a hot block into cold water and follow the energy as it flows from the hotter object to the colder one until both thermometers agree.

## Summary

- **Heat** is energy that flows from a hotter object to a colder one because of their difference in temperature. Objects contain thermal energy, not heat. Heat is measured in joules.
- The **specific heat capacity** $c$ is the energy needed to warm 1 kg of a substance by 1 K, in $\text{J/(kg K)}$. Water has $c = 4180\ \text{J/(kg K)}$, much more than metals such as iron (450) and copper (385).
- The heat needed to change the temperature of a mass $m$ by $\Delta T$ is $Q = mc\Delta T$; the heat capacity of a whole object is $C = mc$.
- A heater of power $P$ gives $Q = P\,\Delta t$, so the temperature rises in a straight line with time, by $\Delta T = P\,\Delta t/(mc)$.
- When hot and cold objects are mixed without energy leaking away, **heat lost = heat gained**. The final temperature is the average of the starting temperatures weighted by the heat capacities $mc$.
- Water's large specific heat lets seas and lakes store and release large amounts of energy with small temperature changes, which makes the climate near them milder.
