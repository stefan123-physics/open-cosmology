A cup of tea left on the table cools down, and a glass of cold lemonade warms up, until both are as warm as the room. In everyday speech "hot", "heat" and "temperature" are used loosely. In physics they mean different things. This lesson pins down what temperature is, how it is measured, why there is a lowest possible temperature, and why temperature is not the same as the energy something contains.

## What temperature measures

All matter is made of tiny particles (atoms or molecules) that never stop moving. You can see the effect without seeing the particles: a drop of food colouring in still water slowly spreads through the whole glass, because the water molecules keep knocking the dye molecules about. In 1828 Robert Brown watched tiny particles released from inside pollen grains dart about in water in an irregular way, and that **Brownian motion** is caused by the same collisions with moving water molecules (Siyavula Grade 10, 3.1, which shortens this to the pollen grains themselves; whole grains are too large to jiggle visibly).

How the particles move depends on the state of the matter (Siyavula Grade 10, 3.2). In a solid each particle vibrates around a fixed place. In a liquid the particles slide past each other. In a gas they fly freely through mostly empty space, colliding with each other and with the walls. In every case, warming the substance makes the particles move faster.

Lesson 3.4 called the energy of this random jiggling **thermal energy**: the kinetic energy of the moving particles plus the potential energy of the forces between them, as the last section of this lesson explains. Temperature tells you how vigorous the jiggling is:

<Callout kind="key">
The **temperature** of a substance is a measure of the average kinetic energy of its particles. The hotter it is, the faster its particles move on average (Siyavula Grade 11, 7.1).
</Callout>

Not every particle moves at the same speed: at any moment some are fast and some are slow, and they keep swapping energy in collisions. Temperature tracks the **average**. Lesson 4.4 turns this into a formula for a gas.

## Thermometers and thermal equilibrium

Drop a warm spoon into cold water. The fast-jiggling particles of the spoon bump into the slower water molecules and pass on some of their energy, so the spoon cools and the water warms. This goes on until the temperatures are equal. After that, nothing changes any more on the large scale, although the particles keep moving. The spoon and the water are then in **thermal equilibrium**. Two objects that are already at the same temperature are in thermal equilibrium the moment they touch: put them in contact and neither changes (MIT 8.044 notes, p. 3).

Energy always flows by itself from the hotter object to the colder one, never the other way. The energy that flows because of a temperature difference is called **heat**; lesson 4.5 shows how much is needed to warm something.

A **thermometer** uses this. You put it in contact with an object and wait until the two are in thermal equilibrium. The thermometer then reads its own temperature, which is now also the temperature of the object. That is why a fever thermometer needs a minute under your tongue. To show a number, a thermometer uses some property that changes with temperature. In a liquid-in-glass thermometer it is the volume of the liquid: most substances expand a little when warmed, because their faster particles push each other slightly further apart (**thermal expansion**), so the liquid rises up a narrow tube. A digital thermometer uses an electrical property instead.

Behind every thermometer is a rule so basic that it was given a name only after the first and second laws of thermodynamics (lessons 4.8 and 4.9) had been numbered, so it was put in front of them as the zeroth:

<Callout kind="note" title="The zeroth law of thermodynamics">
If object A is in thermal equilibrium with object C, and object B is in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other (MIT 8.044 Lecture 5, slide 5).
</Callout>

Let C be the thermometer. If it reads the same in A and in B, then A and B have the same temperature, and no energy will flow between them when they touch, even though they never touched the thermometer at the same time. Without this law, a thermometer reading would tell you nothing about what happens between other objects.

## Celsius and kelvin

The **Celsius** scale was first fixed by water: pure water freezes at $0\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ and boils at about $100\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ at normal air pressure (NIST). Lesson 0.2 introduced the SI unit of temperature, the **kelvin** (K). It has exactly the same step size as the Celsius degree, but it starts counting from the lowest temperature possible. This lesson writes $\theta$ (theta) for a temperature in degrees Celsius and $T$ for a temperature in kelvin. Since a step of one degree is the same on both scales, a temperature **change** is the same number on both:

<Formula label="A change in temperature" tex="\Delta T\ (\text{in K}) = \Delta\theta\ (\text{in }{}^{\circ}\text{C})" cite="nist" at="SI Units – Temperature: one Celsius degree is an interval of 1 K" />

The two scales differ only in where zero is. $0\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ is $273.15\ \text{K}$, so to convert, add 273.15:

<Formula label="Celsius to kelvin" tex="T = \theta + 273.15" cite="nist" at="SI Units – Temperature: 0 °C is 273.15 K" />

and the other way, $\theta = T - 273.15$. Siyavula rounds this to $T = \theta + 273$ (Grade 11, 7.2, Table 7.1), as lesson 0.2 did, and that is precise enough for nearly every calculation in this course. Temperatures in kelvin are written without a degree sign: "300 K", spoken "300 kelvin".

<Callout kind="note" title="Notation">
Many books write $t$ for a temperature in degrees Celsius. This lesson uses $\theta$, because $t$ already means time. Siyavula writes $T$ for both, with a small K or C below it to show the scale.
</Callout>

## Absolute zero

Cool a substance and its particles slow down. There is a limit: a temperature at which the particles have the least energy they can possibly have, so that nothing can take any more energy from them. That is **absolute zero**, $0\ \text{K}$, or $-273.15\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. Nothing can be colder, so temperatures in kelvin are never negative.

Gases give a striking way to find absolute zero, which lesson 4.3 uses. Cool a gas at constant pressure and its volume shrinks along a straight line. Extend that line to lower temperatures and it reaches zero volume at about $-273\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, whatever the gas (Siyavula Grade 11, 7.2, Charles' law). No real gas gets there, because it turns liquid first, but the line points to the same bottom of the scale every time.

The kelvin scale matters because it is the one in which temperature is proportional to the average kinetic energy of the particles (lesson 4.4). So "twice the temperature" only makes sense in kelvin: a gas at $600\ \text{K}$ has particles with twice the average kinetic energy of the same gas at $300\ \text{K}$. On the Celsius scale, $20\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ is not "twice as hot" as $10\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$.

<WorkedExample title="Converting temperatures">
<Problem>
(a) A freezer keeps food at $-18\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. What is that in kelvin? (b) A patient has a fever of $39.5\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. Give it in kelvin to one decimal place. (c) A gas is cooled to $90\ \text{K}$. What is that in degrees Celsius?
</Problem>
<Step title="Freezer">
$T = \theta + 273 = -18 + 273 = 255\ \text{K}$. Adding a negative number lowers the result: the freezer is 18 K below the freezing point of water, 273 K.
</Step>
<Step title="Fever">
To one decimal place we need the precise value: $T = 39.5 + 273.15 = 312.65\ \text{K}$, which is $312.7\ \text{K}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Cold gas">
$\theta = T - 273 = 90 - 273 = -183\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Changes and ratios">
<Problem>
Water is warmed from $15\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ to $75\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. (a) By how much does its temperature rise in kelvin? (b) Someone says "75 is five times 15, so the molecules now move five times as energetically". Check this claim in kelvin.
</Problem>
<Step title="The rise">
$\Delta\theta = 75 - 15 = 60\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, so $\Delta T = 60\ \text{K}$. Converting both ends gives the same: $348 - 288 = 60\ \text{K}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The ratio">
In kelvin the temperatures are $15 + 273 = 288\ \text{K}$ and $75 + 273 = 348\ \text{K}$. The ratio is $348/288 = 1.21$. The average kinetic energy of the molecules rises by about 21%, not by a factor of five. Ratios of temperatures must always be taken in kelvin.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## From the Sun to empty space

<WorkedExample title="The Sun and the sky">
<Problem>
The Sun's visible surface (the photosphere) is at about $5500\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, and its core at more than $1.5 \times 10^{7}\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, 15 million degrees (NASA, *Sun: Facts*). The faint microwave glow that fills all of space has a temperature of $2.725\ \text{K}$ (NASA, COBE FIRAS overview). (a) Give the surface temperature in kelvin and the glow in degrees Celsius. (b) Does it matter whether the Sun's core is quoted in °C or in K? (c) How many times higher in kelvin is the Sun's surface temperature than that of the glow?
</Problem>
<Step title="Converting">
Surface: $T = 5500 + 273.15 = 5773\ \text{K}$, about $5800\ \text{K}$. Glow: $\theta = 2.725 - 273.15 = -270.4\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, less than three degrees above absolute zero.
</Step>
<Step title="The core">
Adding 273 to 15 000 000 changes it by less than 0.002%, far less than the uncertainty in the number. At such high temperatures the two scales give practically the same number, about 15 million kelvin.
</Step>
<Step title="The ratio">
$5773/2.725 = 2119$, so about 2100 times. A ratio in degrees Celsius would make no sense here: $-270.4\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ is negative.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Thermal energy is not temperature

The particles of a substance have energy, and there are attractive forces between them (Siyavula Grade 10, 3.2). The **thermal energy** of an object, also called its **internal energy** (here the same thing; MIT 8.044 calls it $U$, notes p. 1), is the total energy of all its particles: the kinetic energy of their random motion, plus the potential energy of the attractive forces between them. Temperature and thermal energy are related, but they are not the same thing:

- **Temperature** does not depend on how much stuff there is. Pour two cups of tea at $60\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ into one pot, and the pot is still at $60\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$.
- **Thermal energy** does. The pot holds twice the thermal energy of one cup, because it has twice as many jiggling molecules.

The MIT course 8.044 calls the first kind of quantity **intensive** (independent of the number of particles, like temperature and pressure) and the second kind **extensive** (proportional to it, like volume and internal energy) (Lecture 5, slide 2).

So a small object can be very hot and still hold little thermal energy. A spark from a sparkler is at over a thousand degrees, yet when it lands on your skin it barely hurts: it contains so few atoms that its thermal energy is tiny. A lake at $15\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ is cool, yet its enormous number of molecules gives it vastly more thermal energy than a red-hot iron nail.

Which way energy flows is decided by **temperature**, not by thermal energy. Drop the red-hot nail into the lake: energy flows from the nail into the lake, although the lake has far more thermal energy, because the nail is hotter. The nail cools almost to $15\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, while the lake warms by an amount too small to measure.

<WorkedExample title="Mixing hot and cold water">
<Problem>
You pour $0.30\ \text{kg}$ of water at $70\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ into $0.30\ \text{kg}$ of water at $10\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ and stir. Assume no energy leaks into the jug or the air. What is the final temperature?
</Problem>
<Step title="Where the energy goes">
Energy flows from the hot water to the cold water until they reach thermal equilibrium. The energy the hot water loses is exactly the energy the cold water gains (lesson 3.4: energy is conserved).
</Step>
<Step title="Equal amounts">
The two portions are the same substance and the same mass, so warming one portion by a degree takes the same energy as cooling the other by a degree releases (lesson 4.5 shows why). The temperature of one must fall by as much as the other rises, so they meet halfway: $\theta = (70 + 10)/2 = 40\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check in kelvin">
$343\ \text{K}$ and $283\ \text{K}$ meet at $(343 + 283)/2 = 313\ \text{K}$, which is $313 - 273 = 40\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. Halfway is halfway on both scales, because they differ only by a shift.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

With unequal amounts, or two different substances, the final temperature is not halfway. Lesson 4.5 shows how to calculate it.

## Summary

- Matter is made of particles in constant random motion. **Temperature** measures the average kinetic energy of that motion: hotter means faster particles on average.
- Objects in contact exchange energy, always from hotter to colder, until their temperatures are equal: **thermal equilibrium**. A thermometer reads the temperature of whatever it is in equilibrium with.
- **Zeroth law:** two objects each in thermal equilibrium with a third are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
- Kelvin and Celsius have the same step size, so $\Delta T = \Delta\theta$, and $T = \theta + 273.15$ (273 in most calculations). Ratios of temperatures only make sense in kelvin.
- **Absolute zero**, $0\ \text{K} = -273.15\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, is the lowest temperature possible; kelvin temperatures are never negative.
- **Thermal (internal) energy** is the total kinetic and potential energy of all the particles and grows with the amount of matter; temperature does not. Temperature, not thermal energy, decides which way energy flows.
