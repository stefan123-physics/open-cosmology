Hold one end of a metal spoon in a cup of hot tea and the handle soon gets warm. Hold your hands above a radiator and you feel warm air rising. Stand in the sunshine and you feel warm although nothing touches you. Lesson 4.5 measured how much heat it takes to warm something up. This lesson asks how the heat gets there, and how fast.

## Three ways for heat to travel

Heat is the energy that flows from a hotter place to a colder one because of their temperature difference (lesson 4.2). It can travel in three ways (MIT 8.21, slide 8; MIT 16.050, Sec. 1.0):

- **Conduction**: the heat is passed on from particle to particle through matter that itself stays where it is. The spoon in the tea is an example.
- **Convection**: the heat is carried along by a liquid or a gas that flows from a hot place to a cold one. Warm air rising from a hot car park, or from a radiator, is an example.
- **Radiation**: the heat is carried by light, mostly infrared, which needs no matter at all and also crosses empty space. That is how the Sun warms the Earth. Radiation needs light and photons first, so it has a lesson of its own in Block 7 (lesson 7.2, thermal radiation).

In most real situations two or three of them work together. A warm house loses heat by conduction through its walls and windows, by convection when warm air leaks out, and by radiation from its outside surfaces.

## Conduction in the particle picture

Recall from lesson 4.4 that temperature is about motion: the hotter a substance, the faster its particles move. In a solid the particles cannot wander away; they vibrate about fixed places, tightly packed next to each other (Siyavula Grade 10, Table 3.1).

Now heat one end of a metal bar. The particles there vibrate more strongly. They push and jostle their neighbours, which start to vibrate more strongly too, and these pass the motion on to the next ones. Energy moves along the bar, from the hot end to the cold end, while every particle stays in its place. That is conduction. It goes on as long as there is a temperature difference, and it always runs from hot to cold.

Materials differ enormously in how well they conduct.

- **Metals** are the best conductors. Besides their vibrating atoms they contain many electrons that are free to move through the whole metal (they also carry electric current, Block 6). These fast electrons carry energy from the hot end to the cold end very quickly.
- **Non-metal solids** such as glass, brick, wood and plastic conduct far less well: only the vibrations are passed on.
- **Liquids** conduct poorly, and **gases** worst of all. In a gas the particles are far apart (Siyavula Grade 10, Table 3.1), so they meet only now and then to pass energy on.

## The conduction law

How much heat flows through a wall, a window or the bottom of a pan each second? Experiments show that the heat flow through a flat layer:

- is proportional to the **temperature difference** $\Delta T$ between its two faces (no difference, no flow);
- is proportional to its **area** $A$ (two equal windows side by side pass twice as much);
- is inversely proportional to its **thickness** $d$ (a layer twice as thick passes half as much).

Together:

<Formula label="Heat conduction through a layer" tex="P = \frac{k A\, \Delta T}{d}" cite="mit" at="slide 10: rate of heat transfer through a bar of length L and area A, qA = k(T_L − T_0)A/L" />

Here $P$ is the heat that flows through the layer per second, in watts (a power, lesson 3.5), $\Delta T$ the temperature difference across it, in kelvin, and $k$ the **thermal conductivity** of the material, in $\text{W/(m K)}$. This law was found by Joseph Fourier in 1807 (MIT 16.050, Sec. 2.0). A temperature *difference* is the same in kelvin and in degrees Celsius (lesson 4.2), so either may be used for $\Delta T$.

The thermal conductivity is a property of the material, measured and listed in tables. Some values at room temperature:

| Material | $k$ in $\text{W/(m K)}$ |
|---|---|
| silver | 420 |
| copper | 390 |
| aluminium | 200 |
| steel | about 50 |
| concrete | 1.4 |
| glass | 0.87 |
| water | 0.6 |
| hard wood | 0.16 |
| glass fibre insulation | 0.043 |
| air (still) | 0.026 |

The values for the metals, water and air are from MIT 16.050, Table 2.1; concrete, hard wood and glass fibre from MIT 8.21, slide 11; glass from MIT 4.42J, Table 2.1. Copper conducts about 15 000 times better than still air.

<WorkedExample title="Heat through a window pane">
<Problem>
A single pane of glass ($k = 0.87\ \text{W/(m K)}$) is $1.5\ \text{m}$ wide, $1.2\ \text{m}$ high and $4.0\ \text{mm}$ thick. On a winter evening its inner face is $0.50\ \text{K}$ warmer than its outer face. (a) How much heat flows through the glass each second? (b) The room is at $20\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ and the air outside at $0\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$. What would the heat flow be if this whole difference of $20\ \text{K}$ were across the glass itself?
</Problem>
<Step title="The area and the thickness in SI units">
$A = 1.5 \times 1.2 = 1.8\ \text{m}^2$ and $d = 4.0\ \text{mm} = 0.0040\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The heat flow">
$P = kA\,\Delta T/d = 0.87 \times 1.8 \times 0.50/0.0040 = 196\ \text{W}$, about as much as two old light bulbs, lost through one window.
</Step>
<Step title="If the glass took the whole 20 K">
$P = 0.87 \times 1.8 \times 20/0.0040 = 7.8 \times 10^{3}\ \text{W}$. That is far more than a real single window loses. The reason is that thin layers of almost still air cling to both faces of the glass (MIT 16.050, Sec. 3.0). Air conducts so badly that these films take most of the temperature difference; the glass itself is only a small part of the barrier (MIT 4.42J, window example).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Layers in series and insulation

A real wall is built of layers: brick or concrete, insulation, plaster. The same heat has to pass through each layer in turn. It helps to give each layer a **thermal resistance**, which says how hard it is for heat to get through:

<Formula label="Thermal resistance of a layer" tex="R = \frac{d}{k A}, \qquad P = \frac{\Delta T}{R}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (2.19), R = L/kA" />

A thick layer, a small area or a poor conductor gives a large resistance. For two layers one after the other the resistances simply add, as for electrical resistors in series (Block 6):

<Formula label="Two layers in series" tex="P = \frac{\Delta T}{R_1 + R_2}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (2.20), composite slab" />

Here $\Delta T$ is the temperature difference across the whole wall. The temperature drop across each layer is $P R_1$ and $P R_2$, so most of the drop is across the layer with the largest resistance.

<WorkedExample title="A concrete wall, with and without insulation">
<Problem>
An outside wall of a house has an area of $12\ \text{m}^2$ and is made of concrete $0.15\ \text{m}$ thick ($k = 1.4\ \text{W/(m K)}$). Inside it is $21\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$, outside $3\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$; take these as the temperatures of the two faces. (a) How much heat flows through the wall? (b) A layer of glass fibre $0.080\ \text{m}$ thick ($k = 0.043\ \text{W/(m K)}$) is added. What is the heat flow now?
</Problem>
<Step title="The bare wall">
$\Delta T = 21 - 3 = 18\ \text{K}$, so $P = kA\,\Delta T/d = 1.4 \times 12 \times 18/0.15 = 2.0 \times 10^{3}\ \text{W}$. Over a whole day that is $2.0\ \text{kW} \times 24\ \text{h} = 48\ \text{kWh}$ (lesson 3.5) through one wall.
</Step>
<Step title="The two resistances">
Concrete: $R_1 = 0.15/(1.4 \times 12) = 8.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{K/W}$. Glass fibre: $R_2 = 0.080/(0.043 \times 12) = 0.155\ \text{K/W}$. Together $R_1 + R_2 = 0.164\ \text{K/W}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The insulated wall">
$P = \Delta T/(R_1 + R_2) = 18/0.164 = 110\ \text{W}$, about 18 times less than before, or $2.6\ \text{kWh}$ a day.
</Step>
<Step title="Where the temperature drops">
Across the concrete the temperature falls by only $P R_1 = 110 \times 8.9 \times 10^{-3} = 0.98\ \text{K}$; across the glass fibre by $P R_2 = 110 \times 0.155 = 17.0\ \text{K}$. The thin insulation, not the thick concrete, holds back the heat.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why trapped air insulates

Still air is one of the poorest conductors there is, $0.026\ \text{W/(m K)}$. But air that is free to move does not stay still: warmed air rises and carries its heat away by convection (next section). Good insulators therefore trap air in tiny pockets where it cannot flow:

- glass fibre and mineral wool in walls and lofts, and foam plastics with their bubbles;
- wool, down feathers and fleece in clothes, and the fur of animals;
- double glazing, with a thin gap of air or another gas between two panes;
- snow, which is mostly air between the ice crystals.

The glass fibre in the table conducts almost as badly as the air it holds, $0.043$ against $0.026\ \text{W/(m K)}$. The fibres themselves are glass, which conducts twenty times better; their job is only to keep the air still. In the same way a jumper makes no heat of its own: it slows down the loss of the heat your body makes.

## Convection

In a liquid or a gas, heat can also travel by the flow of the fluid itself. It starts with density. Heat a gas at constant pressure and it expands: by Charles's law (lesson 4.3) its volume grows in proportion to its absolute temperature. The same mass then fills more space, so with $\rho = m/V$ (lesson 4.1):

<Formula label="Density of a gas at constant pressure" tex="\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} = \frac{T_1}{T_2}" cite="reading4" at="Charles's law, V/T constant at constant pressure (with ρ = m/V)" />

Most liquids also expand a little when warmed. The warmed fluid is less dense than the cooler fluid around it, so the upward push of the surrounding fluid (Archimedes, lesson 4.1) is larger than its weight: it rises. Cooler, denser fluid sinks and flows in to take its place, is warmed in turn, and rises too. A steady circulation forms, a **convection current**, which carries heat from the warm place to the cold one much faster than conduction through the still fluid could.

<WorkedExample title="A rising bubble of warm air">
<Problem>
Air near the ground at $20\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ ($293\ \text{K}$) has a density of $1.20\ \text{kg/m}^3$. Above a sunny car park a pocket of air is warmed to $35\ {}^{\circ}\text{C}$ ($308\ \text{K}$) at the same pressure. (a) What is its density? (b) What is the net upward force on each cubic metre of the warm air?
</Problem>
<Step title="The density of the warm air">
$\rho_2 = \rho_1 T_1/T_2 = 1.20 \times 293/308 = 1.14\ \text{kg/m}^3$, about 5% less than the air around it.
</Step>
<Step title="The net force">
A cubic metre of warm air pushes aside $1.20\ \text{kg}$ of cooler air, but its own mass is only $1.14\ \text{kg}$. The difference is $1.20 - 1.1416 = 0.058\ \text{kg}$, so the buoyant force beats the weight by $0.058 \times 9.81 = 0.57\ \text{N}$. That small push is enough to lift the warm air in a rising column, which gliders and birds use to climb.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Convection is everywhere:

- **A pan of water on the stove.** The water at the bottom is heated by conduction through the pan, expands and rises; cooler water from the top sinks to the bottom. Watch a few tea leaves or grains of rice and you can see the circulation. That is why a pan is heated from below and a freezer compartment sits at the top of an old fridge.
- **The sea breeze.** In the sunshine the land warms up faster than the sea. The air above the land warms, rises, and cooler air from over the sea flows inland to take its place (NASA Earth Observatory, *Deep Freeze and Sea Breeze*). At night the land cools faster and the flow can turn around.
- **Rooms and radiators.** A radiator warms the air next to it, which rises to the ceiling, spreads out, cools and sinks on the other side of the room. Despite its name, a radiator spreads much of its heat by convection.

In these examples the fluid moves by itself, because of density differences: **natural convection**. When a fan, a pump or the wind pushes the fluid along, it is called **forced convection**; that is why the wind makes you feel colder on a cold day.

## Convection in the Sun

The Sun has a convection zone too. Its energy is made in the core by nuclear fusion (lesson 8.5) and first travels outward as radiation. In the outer part of the Sun, where the temperature has dropped below about 2 million °C, the energy is carried the rest of the way by convection: huge bubbles of hot plasma (a gas of electrically charged particles) rise towards the visible surface, the photosphere, give off their heat there, and sink again (NASA, *Sun: Facts*).

## Summary

- Heat travels in three ways: **conduction** (passed on from particle to particle in matter that stays in place), **convection** (carried along by a flowing liquid or gas) and **radiation** (carried by light, lesson 7.2).
- In conduction the particles at the hot side vibrate more strongly and pass their motion on to their neighbours. Metals conduct best because of their free electrons; gases conduct worst because their particles are far apart.
- The heat flow through a layer is $P = kA\,\Delta T/d$, with $k$ the thermal conductivity: about $390\ \text{W/(m K)}$ for copper, $0.87$ for glass and $0.026$ for still air.
- Layers in series add their thermal resistances $R = d/(kA)$, so $P = \Delta T/(R_1 + R_2)$; a thin insulating layer can cut the heat loss of a wall many times over, and it takes most of the temperature drop.
- Insulators such as glass fibre, wool and double glazing work by trapping air, which conducts badly and cannot flow.
- In convection, warmed fluid expands, becomes less dense ($\rho_2/\rho_1 = T_1/T_2$ for a gas at constant pressure) and rises, while cooler fluid sinks: a pan of water, the sea breeze and the convection zone of the Sun all work this way.
