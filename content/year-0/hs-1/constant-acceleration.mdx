Lessons 1.4 and 1.5 described acceleration and the velocity–time graph. Many motions have an acceleration that stays the same for a while: a car pulling away from traffic lights, a train braking steadily, a ball rolling down a ramp, and (lesson 1.7) anything falling freely. For this **uniformly accelerated motion**, or motion at **constant acceleration**, four short equations connect everything there is to know. This lesson derives them, shows how to pick the right one, and finds what they say about the position–time graph.

## The five quantities

One stage of motion at constant acceleration along a straight line is described by five quantities:

- $v_i$, the **initial velocity**, at the start of the stage;
- $v_f$, the **final velocity**, at the end of the stage;
- $a$, the constant acceleration;
- $\Delta t$, the time the stage lasts;
- $\Delta x$, the displacement during the stage.

All of them except $\Delta t$ can be negative: as in lesson 1.2, a sign gives the direction along the line once you have chosen which way is positive. The key fact of this lesson is that **any three of these five quantities let you work out the other two**. The equations below are the tools that do it. Sometimes there are two possible answers (a ball rolled up a ramp passes the same point twice, as you will see below), and one special case gives too little information: with $a = 0$, knowing $v_i$ and $v_f$ tells you nothing new, because they are then equal.

Siyavula and lesson 1.5 write $t$ for the time since the start of the motion. This lesson writes $\Delta t$ instead, because a stage need not start at $t = 0$: the clock may already be running when the car starts braking.

## Equation 1: from the slope

At constant acceleration the velocity changes by the same amount every second, so the velocity–time graph is a straight line, and its slope is the acceleration (lesson 1.4: $a = \Delta v / \Delta t$). Write the change in velocity as $v_f - v_i$ and rearrange (lesson 0.4):

<Formula label="Velocity after a time Δt" tex="v_f = v_i + a\,\Delta t" cite="mit" at="Sec. 21.7, Equation (1); derived in Extension: Finding the equations of motion" />

In words: the final velocity is the starting velocity plus the acceleration times the time. The MIT course notes write the same line in their own notation (reading 1, Sec. 4.5, Eq. (4.4.2)). Here $a\,\Delta t$ is the change in velocity: an acceleration of 3.0 m/s² for 4.0 s adds $3.0 \times 4.0 = 12$ m/s to the velocity.

## Equation 2: from the area

Lesson 1.5 showed that the **area under a velocity–time graph is the displacement**. For a straight line from $v_i$ to $v_f$ over a time $\Delta t$, that area is a trapezium: a rectangle of height $v_i$ with a triangle of height $v_f - v_i$ on top. Its area is the width times the average of the two heights:

<Formula label="Displacement from the average velocity" tex="\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(v_i + v_f)\,\Delta t" cite="mit" at="Sec. 21.7, Equation (2); derived in Extension: Finding the equations of motion (Derivation of Equation 2)" />

So at constant acceleration (and, in general, only then) the average velocity is simply halfway between the initial and the final velocity.

## Equations 3 and 4: by algebra

Equations 1 and 2 are all the physics. The other two follow by removing one quantity.

**Remove $v_f$.** Put Equation 1 into Equation 2: $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_i + v_i + a\,\Delta t)\,\Delta t$. Multiplying out gives

<Formula label="Displacement after a time Δt" tex="\Delta x = v_i\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,\Delta t^{2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 21.7, Equation (3); Derivation of Equation 3" />

The first term is how far you would go at your starting velocity; the second is the extra (or, for negative $a$, the missing) displacement because the velocity changes. The MIT notes reach the same result from the area of the trapezium (reading 1, Sec. 4.5.2, Eq. (4.4.9)).

**Remove $\Delta t$.** From Equation 1, $\Delta t = (v_f - v_i)/a$. Put this into Equation 2:

$\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_f + v_i)\,\dfrac{v_f - v_i}{a} = \dfrac{v_f^{2} - v_i^{2}}{2a}$,

using $(v_f + v_i)(v_f - v_i) = v_f^{2} - v_i^{2}$. Multiplying both sides by $2a$ and rearranging:

<Formula label="Velocity after a displacement Δx" tex="v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2\,a\,\Delta x" cite="mit" at="Sec. 21.7, Equation (4); Derivation of Equation 4 (derived here by a shorter route)" />

This is the equation to use when the time does not matter, as in braking distances below.

## Choosing the right equation

Each equation contains four of the five quantities and leaves one out:

| Equation | Leaves out |
|---|---|
| $v_f = v_i + a\,\Delta t$ | $\Delta x$ |
| $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_i + v_f)\,\Delta t$ | $a$ |
| $\Delta x = v_i\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2} a\,\Delta t^{2}$ | $v_f$ |
| $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2a\,\Delta x$ | $\Delta t$ |

A method that always works:

1. Choose which direction is positive.
2. Write down the three quantities you know, with signs and SI units, and the one you want.
3. One of the five is neither given nor asked. Take the equation that leaves out exactly that one.
4. Rearrange, fill in, and check the units and the size of the answer.

"From rest" means $v_i = 0$; "comes to a stop" means $v_f = 0$. Look out for these hidden givens.

<WorkedExample title="A car pulling away">
<Problem>
A car waits at traffic lights. When they turn green it accelerates at a constant 3.0 m/s² for 6.0 s. What is its speed then, and how far has it gone?
</Problem>
<Step title="List the quantities">
Take the direction of travel as positive. Known: $v_i = 0$ (from rest), $a = +3.0$ m/s², $\Delta t = 6.0$ s. Wanted: $v_f$ and $\Delta x$.
</Step>
<Step title="Final speed">
Equation 1 leaves out $\Delta x$: $v_f = 0 + 3.0 \times 6.0 = 18$ m/s, which is $18 \times 3.6 \approx 65$ km/h.
</Step>
<Step title="Distance">
Equation 3 leaves out $v_f$, so it needs only the given numbers: $\Delta x = 0 \times 6.0 + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 3.0 \times 6.0^{2} = 54$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
Equation 2: $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(0 + 18) \times 6.0 = 54$ m, the same. Equation 4 agrees too: $v_f^{2} = 0 + 2 \times 3.0 \times 54 = 324$, and $\sqrt{324} = 18$ m/s. The units work out: m/s² times s gives m/s, and m/s² times s² gives m.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Signs and slowing down

The equations work for slowing down too, as long as every quantity carries its sign. If the positive direction is the direction of motion, a car that brakes has a **negative acceleration**: the acceleration points against the velocity. People often call this a **deceleration** and give it as a positive number ("a deceleration of 8.0 m/s²"); in the equations you must then write $a = -8.0$ m/s². Lesson 1.4 advised against using "deceleration" for a negative acceleration; here the word only names the size of a slowing-down acceleration, and the sign is written separately.

A negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down. An object moving in the negative direction with a negative acceleration speeds up. What matters is whether the velocity and the acceleration have the **same sign** (speeding up) or **opposite signs** (slowing down).

<WorkedExample title="Braking distance">
<Problem>
A car drives at 90 km/h on a dry road. The driver sees an obstacle, reacts after 0.80 s, and then brakes with a constant deceleration of 8.0 m/s². How far does the car travel before it stops? Compare with a speed of 45 km/h.
</Problem>
<Step title="Units and signs">
Take the direction of travel as positive. $v_i = 90 / 3.6 = 25$ m/s, $v_f = 0$, and $a = -8.0$ m/s² during braking.
</Step>
<Step title="Reaction distance">
During the reaction time the car does not brake yet: it moves at constant velocity (lesson 1.2), $25 \times 0.80 = 20$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Braking distance">
The time is neither given nor asked, so use Equation 4: $0 = 25^{2} + 2 \times (-8.0) \times \Delta x$, so $\Delta x = \dfrac{625}{16} \approx 39$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Stopping distance">
In total the car travels $20 + 39 = 59$ m before it stops.
</Step>
<Step title="At half the speed">
At 45 km/h, $v_i = 12.5$ m/s. Reaction distance $12.5 \times 0.80 = 10$ m; braking distance $\dfrac{12.5^{2}}{16} \approx 9.8$ m; together about 20 m. Half the speed gives half the reaction distance but only a **quarter** of the braking distance, because $v_i$ is squared.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
Equation 1 gives the braking time, $\Delta t = 25 / 8.0 \approx 3.1$ s, and Equation 2 then gives $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(25 + 0) \times 3.125 \approx 39$ m, the same. The minus signs work out: a negative $a$ and a positive $\Delta x$ give a positive braking distance.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The position–time graph is a parabola

Write the position at the end of the stage as $x = x_i + \Delta x$, where $x_i$ is the starting position. Equation 3 then gives the position at any time $\Delta t$ after the start:

<Formula label="Position at constant acceleration" tex="x = x_i + v_i\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2}\,a\,\Delta t^{2}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 4.5.2, Eq. (4.4.9) (written there as x(t) = x₀ + v₀t + ½at²)" />

The time appears squared, so the position is a **quadratic function of time**, and its graph is a **parabola** (lesson 0.6). For a positive acceleration it opens upwards: its slope is the velocity (lesson 1.3), which keeps increasing. Starting from rest or moving forwards, the graph gets steeper and steeper; moving backwards at first, it gets less steep, turns round and then gets steeper. For a negative acceleration it opens downwards. At constant velocity the $\Delta t^{2}$ term vanishes and the graph is the straight line of lesson 1.2.

From rest, $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2} a\,\Delta t^{2}$: the distance grows with the **square** of the time. Twice the time gives four times the distance; three times the time, nine times. Galileo was the first to find this law for accelerated motion (as Siyavula's section 21.7 notes). The car above covers $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 3.0 \times 2.0^{2} = 6.0$ m in the first 2.0 s and $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 3.0 \times 4.0^{2} = 24$ m in the first 4.0 s.

The same parabola turns up whenever the acceleration is constant: a ball thrown into the air (lessons 1.7 and 1.8), and, in Block 6, a charged particle in a uniform electric field.

## Solving for the time: the quadratic formula

When the time is the unknown in Equation 3, the equation is quadratic in $\Delta t$. Rearrange it to the standard form $A\,\Delta t^{2} + B\,\Delta t + C = 0$ and use the quadratic formula of lesson 0.6, with $A = \tfrac{1}{2}a$, $B = v_i$ and $C = -\Delta x$:

<Formula label="The time from the quadratic formula" tex="\Delta t = \frac{-v_i \pm \sqrt{v_i^{2} + 2\,a\,\Delta x}}{a}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 21.7, Equation (3); solved for Δt on this page with the quadratic formula of lesson 0.6, not in the source" />

There are two answers. Keep the one that makes physical sense: a time after the start, so not negative. Notice the square root: by Equation 4 it is the size of $v_f$ (the speed at the end, without its sign), which gives a useful check. The formula divides by $a$, so it does not work for $a = 0$: then simply use $\Delta t = \Delta x / v_i$.

<WorkedExample title="A cyclist rolling downhill">
<Problem>
A cyclist rides at 4.0 m/s onto a long slope and rolls down it with a constant acceleration of 0.50 m/s². How long does she take to cover the 60 m to the bottom?
</Problem>
<Step title="List the quantities">
Downhill is positive. Known: $v_i = 4.0$ m/s, $a = 0.50$ m/s², $\Delta x = 60$ m. Wanted: $\Delta t$. The final velocity is neither given nor asked, so use Equation 3.
</Step>
<Step title="Standard form">
$60 = 4.0\,\Delta t + 0.25\,\Delta t^{2}$, so $0.25\,\Delta t^{2} + 4.0\,\Delta t - 60 = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Quadratic formula">
$\Delta t = \dfrac{-4.0 \pm \sqrt{4.0^{2} + 4 \times 0.25 \times 60}}{2 \times 0.25} = \dfrac{-4.0 \pm \sqrt{76}}{0.50}$. With $\sqrt{76} \approx 8.72$ this gives $\Delta t \approx 9.4$ s or $\Delta t \approx -25$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Choose the answer">
A negative time would be before she reached the slope, so she takes about **9.4 s**.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Equation 4 gives her speed at the bottom, $\sqrt{4.0^{2} + 2 \times 0.50 \times 60} = \sqrt{76} \approx 8.7$ m/s. Then Equation 2 gives $\Delta t = 2\,\Delta x / (v_i + v_f) = 120 / 12.7 \approx 9.4$ s, the same.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Sometimes both answers make sense.

<WorkedExample title="Two answers: a ball rolled up a ramp">
<Problem>
A ball is rolled up a long ramp at 6.0 m/s. It slows down at 2.0 m/s², stops, and rolls back. When is it 5.0 m up the ramp?
</Problem>
<Step title="Signs">
Up the ramp is positive: $v_i = +6.0$ m/s, and the acceleration points down the ramp the whole time, $a = -2.0$ m/s², also while the ball rolls back.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve">
Equation 3: $5.0 = 6.0\,\Delta t - 1.0\,\Delta t^{2}$, so $\Delta t^{2} - 6.0\,\Delta t + 5.0 = 0$. This factors as $(\Delta t - 1.0)(\Delta t - 5.0) = 0$: $\Delta t = 1.0$ s or $\Delta t = 5.0$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Meaning">
Both are correct. At 1.0 s the ball passes the 5.0 m mark on the way up, with $v = 6.0 - 2.0 \times 1.0 = +4.0$ m/s; at 5.0 s it passes it on the way down, with $v = 6.0 - 2.0 \times 5.0 = -4.0$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Equation 4: $v_f^{2} = 6.0^{2} + 2 \times (-2.0) \times 5.0 = 16$, so $v_f = \pm 4.0$ m/s: the two signs are the two passes. The ball turns round when $v = 0$, after $6.0 / 2.0 = 3.0$ s, at $\dfrac{6.0^{2}}{2 \times 2.0} = 9.0$ m up the ramp.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## When the equations do not apply

The four equations hold only while the acceleration is **constant**. If the motion has stages (speeding up, then cruising, then braking), treat each stage on its own: the final velocity of one stage is the initial velocity of the next. If the acceleration changes all the time, as for a car in traffic or a falling leaf, the equations do not apply at all; then you need the slope and the area of the real graph, which is what calculus does at university.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Cosmologists ask the same question about the whole universe: is its expansion speeding up or slowing down? Gravity between all the matter in the universe should slow the expansion down. When astronomers measured distances to very distant exploding stars (supernovae), they found, to their surprise, that the expansion is speeding up today. The acceleration of the universe is not constant, so the equations of this lesson do not describe it over its whole history. But the way of thinking is the same: a speed that changes, a graph that bends, and a careful sign for "slowing down" versus "speeding up". The cosmology course works this out with the equations of general relativity.
</Callout>

## Summary

- At constant acceleration five quantities describe one stage of motion: $v_i$, $v_f$, $a$, $\Delta t$ and $\Delta x$. Any three let you work out the other two (sometimes with two possible answers).
- The slope of the straight velocity–time graph gives $v_f = v_i + a\,\Delta t$; the area under it gives $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_i + v_f)\,\Delta t$.
- Algebra gives the other two: $\Delta x = v_i\,\Delta t + \tfrac{1}{2} a\,\Delta t^{2}$ (no $v_f$) and $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2a\,\Delta x$ (no $\Delta t$).
- To choose: list the knowns and the unknown, and use the equation that leaves out the remaining quantity.
- Choose a positive direction and give every quantity its sign. Slowing down means $a$ and $v$ have opposite signs.
- Braking distance is $v_i^{2}/(2|a|)$: twice the speed, four times the braking distance.
- The position is quadratic in time, so the position–time graph is a parabola. Solving Equation 3 for the time needs the quadratic formula; keep the answers that make physical sense.
