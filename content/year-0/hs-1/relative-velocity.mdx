Lesson 1.1 showed that a position only means something once you have chosen a frame of reference: an origin and a positive direction. Velocity is no different. When a car's speedometer shows its speed, that is its speed *relative to the road*. Measured from another moving car, the same car may seem to crawl, or to rush past at twice the speed. This lesson shows how to go from one frame to another.

## Every velocity is relative

Imagine a girl standing still in the aisle of a train as it pulls out of a station, while you watch from the platform. To you she moves along with the train. To her, sitting in the frame of the train, she is not moving at all, and it is the platform with you on it that slides backwards. Both descriptions are correct. They simply use different frames of reference.

A **frame of reference** is a reference point (the origin) together with a set of directions, usually belonging to some observer: you on the platform, the girl in the train. Every velocity is measured relative to such a frame. Usually it is the ground, and then we do not say so. In this lesson we will always say so, because the whole point is that the velocity of one and the same object depends on the frame.

## The velocity of A relative to B

Take two objects, A and B, moving along the same straight line, and write $v_A$ and $v_B$ for their velocities relative to the ground (with signs, as in lesson 1.2). The **velocity of A relative to B** is the velocity that A seems to have to an observer who moves along with B:

<Formula label="Velocity of A relative to B" tex="v_{AB} = v_A - v_B" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 11.3, Eq. (11.3.2), written with subscripts: the observer's frame moves with B" />

Read the subscripts in order: **the first letter is the object, the second is the frame it is measured from**. So $v_{AB}$ is "A as seen from B". The minus sign makes sense: the observer on B carries B's own velocity along, so that velocity has to be taken off. Two cars side by side at the same velocity have $v_{AB} = 0$: seen from one, the other stands still.

Swapping the letters swaps the roles. B seen from A is $v_{BA} = v_B - v_A$, which is exactly the negative of $v_{AB}$:

<Formula label="Swapping object and frame" tex="v_{BA} = -v_{AB}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 11.3, Eq. (11.3.2), with the roles of A and B swapped" />

If A pulls away from B, then B falls back from A just as fast. The velocities $v_A$ and $v_B$ themselves are relative velocities too: they are measured relative to the ground, which we treat as the frame at rest.

<WorkedExample title="Overtaking on a motorway">
<Problem>
On a straight motorway, car A drives east at 32 m/s and a lorry B ahead of it drives east at 25 m/s. Coming the other way, on the other side, is a car C at 28 m/s west. Take east as positive. Find the velocity of A relative to the lorry, how long A needs to gain 35 m on the lorry, and the velocity of C relative to A.
</Problem>
<Step title="Write the velocities with signs">
Relative to the ground: $v_A = +32$ m/s, $v_B = +25$ m/s and $v_C = -28$ m/s (west is negative).
</Step>
<Step title="Car relative to lorry">
$v_{AB} = v_A - v_B = 32 - 25 = +7.0$ m/s. Seen from the lorry, the car creeps forward at only 7.0 m/s, which is why overtaking takes so long.
</Step>
<Step title="Time to gain 35 m">
In the lorry's frame the car moves at a constant 7.0 m/s, so gaining 35 m takes $35\ \text{m} / 7.0\ \text{m/s} = 5.0$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Oncoming car relative to A">
$v_{CA} = v_C - v_A = -28 - 32 = -60$ m/s. The driver of A sees car C rush towards her at 60 m/s, more than 200 km/h, although neither car goes faster than 32 m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Moving in the same direction, the relative speed is the difference of the speeds (7.0 m/s); in opposite directions it is their sum ($32 + 28 = 60$ m/s). The rule $v_A - v_B$ gives both cases automatically, as long as the signs are right.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Chaining relative velocities

Often you know a velocity relative to something that is itself moving: a passenger walking in a train, a boat moving through flowing water, a plane flying through the wind. Then the velocities **chain together**:

<Formula label="Adding relative velocities" tex="v_{AC} = v_{AB} + v_{BC}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 11.3, Eq. (11.3.2) rearranged to v = v′ + V" />

The velocity of A relative to C is the velocity of A relative to B plus that of B relative to C. A handy check is the order of the subscripts: the two inner letters (here B and B) match and drop out, leaving the outer ones, AC. The rule follows from the previous section: $v_{AB} + v_{BC} = (v_A - v_B) + (v_B - v_C) = v_A - v_C = v_{AC}$.

<WorkedExample title="Walking on a train">
<Problem>
A train T moves east along a straight track at 22.0 m/s relative to the ground G. You (Y) walk towards the back of the train at 1.5 m/s relative to the train. What is your velocity relative to the ground? Take east as positive.
</Problem>
<Step title="Write what is given">
$v_{TG} = +22.0$ m/s. You walk towards the back, which is west, so $v_{YT} = -1.5$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Chain the velocities">
$v_{YG} = v_{YT} + v_{TG} = -1.5 + 22.0 = +20.5$ m/s. The inner letters T match, the outer ones give YG.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Someone on the platform still sees you move east, only a little slower than the train. That is right: you cannot walk back fast enough to undo 22 m/s. Had you walked towards the front, you would have moved at $+23.5$ m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The same idea explains what a moving listener hears (lesson 5.6 on the Doppler effect uses it). Sound travels at about 340 m/s relative to the air it moves through (at room temperature). A listener who runs at 5.0 m/s straight towards the source meets the sound at $340 + 5.0 = 345$ m/s: relative to the listener, the sound comes in faster than relative to the air. In symbols, with the direction of the sound positive, the listener's velocity is $v_L = -5.0$ m/s, and the sound's velocity relative to the listener is $v_{\text{sound}} - v_L = 340 - (-5.0) = 345$ m/s.

## Relative velocity in two dimensions

When the motions are not along one line, the same rules hold with vectors:

<Formula label="Relative velocities as vectors" tex="\vec v_{AB} = \vec v_A - \vec v_B, \qquad \vec v_{AC} = \vec v_{AB} + \vec v_{BC}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 11.3, Eq. (11.3.2), and Sec. 11.4, Example 11.1" />

You add or subtract the vectors as in lesson 0.8: head to tail in a drawing, or component by component in a calculation. When the two vectors are at right angles, Pythagoras gives the size of the result and the tangent its direction:

<Formula label="Resultant of two perpendicular velocities" tex="v = \sqrt{v_x^{2} + v_y^{2}}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{v_y}{v_x}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 1.2, Magnitude of the resultant of vectors at right angles (Pythagoras) and Algebraic methods, Worked example 11 (direction)" />

Here $\theta$ is the angle between the result and the $x$-axis. (The boat example below measures its angle from north instead, so there the tangent is $v_x/v_y$.)

<WorkedExample title="A boat crossing a river">
<Problem>
A river 80 m wide flows east at 1.5 m/s relative to the bank. A boat B is steered straight across, due north, at 4.0 m/s relative to the water W. Find the boat's velocity relative to the ground G (the bank), how long the crossing takes, and how far downstream the boat lands.
</Problem>
<Step title="Chain the velocities">
$\vec v_{BG} = \vec v_{BW} + \vec v_{WG}$. With east as $x$ and north as $y$: $\vec v_{BW}$ has components $(0,\ 4.0)$ m/s and $\vec v_{WG}$ has $(1.5,\ 0)$ m/s, so $\vec v_{BG}$ has components $(1.5,\ 4.0)$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Size and direction">
The speed relative to the bank is $\sqrt{1.5^{2} + 4.0^{2}} = \sqrt{18.25} \approx 4.3$ m/s. Measured from north, $\tan\alpha = 1.5/4.0$, so $\alpha \approx 21°$: the boat moves at 21° east of north.
</Step>
<Step title="Time to cross">
Only the northward component carries the boat across: $80\ \text{m} / 4.0\ \text{m/s} = 20$ s. The current does not speed up or slow down the crossing.
</Step>
<Step title="Drift downstream">
During those 20 s the current carries the boat east: $1.5\ \text{m/s} \times 20\ \text{s} = 30$ m downstream.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The straight path from start to landing point is $\sqrt{30^{2} + 80^{2}} \approx 85$ m, and $85\ \text{m} / 20\ \text{s} \approx 4.3$ m/s: the same speed as before, found by another route.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

To land exactly opposite, the boat must instead be aimed partly upstream, so that the westward part of its velocity through the water cancels the current. A pilot in a crosswind does the same.

<WorkedExample title="A plane in a crosswind">
<Problem>
A pilot P wants to fly due north. Her plane flies at 75 m/s relative to the air A, and a crosswind moves the air due east at 20 m/s relative to the ground G. In which direction must she point the plane, and what is her speed relative to the ground?
</Problem>
<Step title="What must cancel">
$\vec v_{PG} = \vec v_{PA} + \vec v_{AG}$ must point due north, so its east component is zero. The wind gives $+20$ m/s east, so the plane's velocity through the air needs an east component of $-20$ m/s: it must point partly west.
</Step>
<Step title="Heading">
The plane's 75 m/s through the air is the long side of a right-angled triangle whose west side is 20 m/s. If $\beta$ is the angle west of north, $\sin\beta = 20/75$, so $\beta \approx 15°$ west of north.
</Step>
<Step title="Ground speed">
The north component that remains is $\sqrt{75^{2} - 20^{2}} = \sqrt{5225} \approx 72$ m/s. This is her speed relative to the ground, due north.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The ground speed is less than the airspeed, as it should be: part of the plane's effort goes into fighting the wind. Pythagoras the other way round, $\sqrt{72.3^{2} + 20^{2}} \approx 75$ m/s, returns the airspeed.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Galilean relativity

Is there a "true" frame, one that is really at rest? Galileo argued that you could never tell which one it is. In his *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* (1632; see the MacTutor biography of Galileo in the references) he asked the reader to shut himself in a cabin below deck on a ship that sails smoothly at constant velocity, and watch drops fall from a bottle, fish swim in a bowl, a ball being thrown: everything behaves exactly as when the ship lies in harbour. You cannot tell from inside whether the ship moves.

In modern words: **the laws of physics look the same in every frame of reference that moves at constant velocity relative to the ground (or to any other frame in which those laws hold)**. This is called the **principle of relativity**, or **Galilean relativity**. The rule of this lesson shows part of the reason. Going from one frame to another adds the same constant velocity to every velocity, so every *change* of velocity, and therefore every acceleration (lesson 1.4), is the same in both frames. Keys dropped in a smoothly moving train fall straight down, relative to the train, just as in a train standing still.

The principle holds only for frames that move at **constant** velocity. In a train that brakes or a bus that takes a sharp bend, you do feel the motion: things slide and you are thrown sideways.

<Callout kind="note" title="A later development: Einstein">
Simple adding and subtracting of velocities works extremely well at everyday speeds. It fails at speeds that are a sizeable fraction of the speed of light. Experiments show that light travels at the same speed relative to every observer, however fast that observer moves, which cannot be true if velocities simply add. In 1905 Einstein kept the principle of relativity but changed the rules for space and time, and with them the rule for adding velocities. His **special relativity** is part of the Year II relativity course; for anything slower than a few per cent of the speed of light the rules of this lesson are all you need.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Lesson 0.11 described the universe as expanding homogeneously: every distance between galaxies grows by the same factor, so a galaxy's speed away from us is proportional to its distance, Hubble's law:

<Formula label="Hubble's law" tex="v = H\,d" cite="guth4" at="slide 1" />

Relative velocities show why this does not put us at a centre. With the round example value $H$ = 70 km/s per megaparsec, take a galaxy A at 100 megaparsecs and a galaxy B at 200 megaparsecs, both in the same direction. Relative to us, A recedes at $v_A = 70 \times 100 = 7000$ km/s and B at $v_B = 70 \times 200 = 14\,000$ km/s. Now stand on galaxy A. Galaxy B, 100 megaparsecs further out, has $v_{BA} = v_B - v_A = 7000$ km/s, and we (O, our galaxy) have $v_{OA} = 0 - 7000 = -7000$ km/s: we too move away from A at 7000 km/s, in the other direction. An astronomer in A sees galaxies at 100 megaparsecs recede at 7000 km/s on both sides, exactly the same Hubble's law that we see. Guth makes this argument in 8.286 Lecture 4, changing frames with the same rule as this lesson. (At such large distances the cosmology course replaces this simple picture by a more careful one, but the conclusion stays: every galaxy sees the same pattern, and there is no centre.)
</Callout>

## Summary

- Every velocity is measured relative to a **frame of reference**; the same object can have different velocities in different frames.
- The velocity of A relative to B is $v_{AB} = v_A - v_B$: first letter the object, second the frame. Swapping the letters changes the sign, $v_{BA} = -v_{AB}$.
- Relative velocities chain: $v_{AC} = v_{AB} + v_{BC}$, with matching inner letters.
- Along a line, use signs: same direction gives the difference of the speeds, opposite directions the sum.
- In two dimensions, add the velocity vectors with components; for perpendicular velocities use Pythagoras and the tangent. A boat or plane must aim partly into a current or wind to travel straight.
- **Galilean relativity:** physics looks the same in every frame moving at constant velocity. At speeds near that of light, Einstein's relativity changes how velocities add.
