Physics describes motion with three ideas: where an object is, how fast it moves and where to, and how quickly that changes. This block takes them one at a time, and this first lesson is about the first: **position**, and the two different ways of saying how far something has gone, **distance** and **displacement**.

## Position needs a reference point

"I am 20 metres away" tells your friend very little. Twenty metres from what, and in which direction? To give a position you first need a **reference point**, a place everybody agrees on, such as a door, a lamppost or your home. In physics the reference point is called the **origin**.

Along a straight line, such as a street, you also need to say which way counts as **positive**. The other way is then negative. A reference point together with a chosen positive direction is called a **frame of reference**.

With a frame of reference, a **position** is simply a number with a unit, usually written $x$ and measured in metres. It is exactly a point on a number line, like the axis of a graph: the origin is at $x = 0$, points on the positive side have positive $x$, points on the other side negative $x$.

Take a straight street running east to west with a bus stop on it. Choose the bus stop as the origin and east as positive. A library 150 m east of the bus stop is at $x = +150$ m, and a park 60 m west of it is at $x = -60$ m.

Nothing forces us to choose the bus stop. With the library as the origin, the bus stop is at $x = -150$ m and the park at $x = -210$ m. The numbers change, but the street does not: the library and the park are still 210 m apart. A different frame of reference changes how we write positions, never what actually happens.

## Distance: the length of the path

Suppose you walk from the bus stop to the library and back again. How far have you walked? Your feet covered 150 m out and 150 m back. This length of the path is the **distance** travelled.

- Distance is the total length of the route, whatever twists and turns it takes.
- It has a size but no direction, so it is a **scalar** (lesson 0.8).
- It is never negative, and it can only grow as you keep moving.

A distance depends on the route. A winding path between two points is longer than the straight line between them.

## Displacement: the change in position

Back at the bus stop after your walk, you are exactly where you started. In that sense you have not gone anywhere. Physics has a separate quantity for this: the **displacement**, the change in position from the start to the end.

To write it, physicists use the Greek capital letter delta, $\Delta$, which means "change in" and always stands for **final value minus initial value**. With $x_i$ the initial position and $x_f$ the final position,

<Formula label="Displacement in one dimension" tex="\Delta x = x_f - x_i" cite="mit" at="Sec. 21.2, Displacement and distance" />

(Siyavula writes the arrows of vectors, $\Delta \vec x = \vec x_f - \vec x_i$. Along a single line the direction is carried by the sign, so this lesson drops the arrows in one dimension.)

For the walk to the library and back, $x_i = 0$ and $x_f = 0$, so $\Delta x = 0$: a distance of 300 m, but zero displacement.

Displacement differs from distance in three ways:

| Distance | Displacement |
|---|---|
| the length of the path | the change in position, from start to end |
| depends on the route | depends only on the start and the end |
| a scalar, never negative | a vector: it has a direction, and in one dimension a sign |

## Signs in one dimension

Along a line, a vector has only two possible directions, so a sign is enough to show which one:

- $\Delta x > 0$: the object ended up on the positive side of where it started;
- $\Delta x < 0$: it ended up on the negative side;
- $\Delta x = 0$: it ended where it started, however far it went in between.

A negative displacement is a perfectly good answer. It does not mean anything went wrong; it means "in the direction we did not call positive".

The size of the displacement can never be larger than the distance. The two are equal only when the object moves along a straight line without turning back.

<WorkedExample title="A walk with a turn-back">
<Problem>
Mila leaves home and walks 350 m east along a straight street to a postbox. There she turns round and walks 120 m back west to a bakery. Find the distance she walked and her displacement.
</Problem>
<Step title="Distance">
Add the lengths of the two parts of the route: $350\ \text{m} + 120\ \text{m} = 470\ \text{m}$. Distance has no direction, so that is the full answer.
</Step>
<Step title="Choose a frame of reference">
Take her home as the origin and east as positive. She starts at $x_i = 0$. The bakery is 120 m west of the postbox, which is 350 m east of home, so $x_f = +350 - 120 = +230$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Displacement">
$\Delta x = x_f - x_i = +230\ \text{m} - 0 = +230\ \text{m}$: she ends up 230 m east of home.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with another origin">
Put the origin at the postbox instead, still with east positive. Then home is at $x_i = -350$ m and the bakery at $x_f = -120$ m, and $\Delta x = -120 - (-350) = +230$ m. Same displacement, as it must be. The size of the displacement, 230 m, is smaller than the distance, 470 m, because she turned back.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Had we chosen west as positive, every sign would flip and we would find $\Delta x = -230$ m. That still means 230 m east: the motion is the same, only its description changed.

<WorkedExample title="Laps of a circular path">
<Problem>
Sam jogs on a circular path of radius $R$ = 35.0 m around a pond. Find the distance and the size of the displacement after half a lap and after one full lap.
</Problem>
<Step title="Half a lap">
The distance is half the circumference: $\pi R = \pi \times 35.0\ \text{m} = 110\ \text{m}$. Sam is now on the opposite side of the circle, so the straight line from start to end goes across the middle: its length is the diameter, $2R = 70.0\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="One full lap">
The distance is the whole circumference, $2\pi R = 220\ \text{m}$. Sam is back at the start, so the displacement is zero.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
After half a lap the size of the displacement (70.0 m) is less than the distance (110 m), as it must be for a curved path. Every extra lap adds 220 m of distance but leaves the displacement at zero.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Displacement in two dimensions

On a map, a displacement can point in any direction, so a sign is no longer enough. It is a vector in a plane, written $\Delta \vec r$, the arrow from the starting point straight to the end point (the "as the crow flies" line). You can handle it with the components of lesson 0.8: $\Delta x$ is the change in the east–west coordinate and $\Delta y$ the change in the north–south one. Each is final minus initial, exactly as in one dimension. The size and direction then follow from Pythagoras and the tangent:

<Formula label="Size and direction of a displacement in a plane" tex="|\Delta \vec r| = \sqrt{\Delta x^{2} + \Delta y^{2}}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 1.2, Algebraic methods, Worked examples 10 and 11" />

Here $\theta$ is the angle of the displacement measured from the positive $x$-axis. As in lesson 0.8, check the quadrant: if $\Delta x$ is negative, add 180° to the calculator's angle.

<WorkedExample title="A walk across town">
<Problem>
Noor walks 0.90 km east along one street, then turns the corner and walks 0.40 km north. Find the distance she walked and her displacement.
</Problem>
<Step title="Distance">
$0.90\ \text{km} + 0.40\ \text{km} = 1.3\ \text{km}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Components of the displacement">
With east as $+x$ and north as $+y$, and her start as the origin: $\Delta x = +0.90$ km and $\Delta y = +0.40$ km. In the diagram the two legs of her walk are $\vec A$ and $\vec B$, and her displacement is their sum.
</Step>
<Step title="Size and direction">
$|\Delta \vec r| = \sqrt{0.90^{2} + 0.40^{2}}\ \text{km} = 0.98\ \text{km}$, and $\tan\theta = 0.40/0.90$ gives $\theta = 24°$ north of east.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The straight line (0.98 km) is shorter than the route along the two streets (1.3 km) but longer than either street on its own, as the long side of a right-angled triangle must be.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why physics uses displacement

If distance is what your legs feel, why does physics bother with displacement?

- **It says where you ended up.** A distance of 470 m could leave Mila anywhere within 470 m of home; a displacement of +230 m tells you exactly where she is.
- **Displacements add as vectors.** The displacement of a whole trip is the vector sum of the displacements of its parts, so we can build up a complicated motion from simple pieces. Distances only add as plain numbers and lose the direction.
- **The rest of motion is built on it.** In the next lesson, velocity is displacement divided by time, and in later lessons acceleration is built from changes in velocity. Speed, which you probably know already, uses distance instead; the difference between the two matters as soon as something turns back or round a corner.

Distance is still useful: fuel use, the wear on your shoes and the time a journey takes all depend on the length of the path. Physics keeps both, and the skill is knowing which one a question asks for.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Astronomers give the position of a galaxy the way this lesson gives a position: with an origin, normally us (the Earth or the Sun), a direction on the sky and a distance. A galaxy's position is then a vector pointing from us to it. Choosing ourselves as the origin is a convention, not a claim that we are at the centre. In the university cosmology course you will see that in an expanding universe the pattern of galaxies moving apart looks the same seen from any galaxy, so any galaxy could serve as the origin, just as Mila's displacement came out the same whether we measured from her home or from the postbox.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A **position** needs a frame of reference: an origin and a positive direction. In one dimension it is a signed number, $x$, in metres.
- **Distance** is the length of the path travelled: a scalar, never negative, dependent on the route.
- **Displacement** is the change in position, $\Delta x = x_f - x_i$ (final minus initial): a vector, which in one dimension has a sign. It depends only on where the motion starts and ends, and is zero for a round trip.
- Changing the origin changes positions but not displacements; changing the positive direction flips the signs but not the motion.
- In two dimensions, a displacement has components $\Delta x$ and $\Delta y$, a size $\sqrt{\Delta x^{2} + \Delta y^{2}}$ and a direction from $\tan\theta = \Delta y/\Delta x$.
- The size of the displacement is never larger than the distance; they are equal only for straight-line motion without turning back.
