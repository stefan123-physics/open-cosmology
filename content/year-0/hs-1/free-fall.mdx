Lesson 1.4 met the acceleration of a falling stone, $g$; lesson 1.6 gave the four equations for motion at constant acceleration. This lesson puts the two together. Drop a stone, toss a ball straight up, or throw a pebble down from a cliff: if gravity is the only thing that acts, all of these are the same kind of motion, and the same four equations describe them.

## What free fall means

An object is in **free fall** when gravity is the only influence on its motion. That does not mean it has to move downwards: a ball thrown straight up is in free fall from the moment it leaves your hand, on the way up as well as on the way down. Siyavula calls such an object, launched and then left to gravity alone, a **projectile**.

In real life the air also pushes on a moving object, against its motion. This push is called **air resistance** (or drag). For a compact, heavy object such as a stone, a key or a ball at modest speed it is small, and in this lesson we ignore it. For a feather, a sheet of paper or a parachutist it is not small at all; Block 2 deals with it.

Near the Earth's surface, free fall has one simple property: **the acceleration is constant**, the same at every moment and for every object, and it points straight down. Its size is written $g$:

<Formula label="Acceleration of free fall near the Earth" tex="g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2" cite="nist-g" at="standard acceleration of gravity, 9.806 65 m/s², rounded to three figures" />

So a falling object gains 9.81 m/s of downward velocity every second. The value changes slightly from place to place on the Earth and becomes smaller far above the surface, but for everyday heights it is constant. Physicists have agreed on a standard value, 9.806 65 m/s² (NIST lists it); 9.81 m/s² is that value rounded to three figures, and this course uses it. Many books, Siyavula among them, round it further to 9.8 m/s².

## Choosing a positive direction

Free fall happens along a vertical line, so all of lesson 1.6 applies, with the acceleration replaced by gravity. The one thing to decide first is which direction is positive (lesson 1.2), and then to stick to it for the whole problem.

**Up as positive.** Then the acceleration is negative, $a = -g = -9.81$ m/s², whether the object is going up or coming down. The MIT course notes make the same choice (reading 2, Sec. 5.2):

<Formula label="Free fall with up as positive" tex="a = -g" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 5.2, Eq. (5.1.14)" />

and the equations of lesson 1.6 (in the MIT notes, reading 1, Eqs. (4.4.2) and (4.4.9), with $a = -g$) become

<Formula label="Velocity in free fall (up positive)" tex="v_f = v_i - g\,\Delta t" cite="mit" at="Sec. 3.2, Equations of motion (ESCJY), with the acceleration equal to g, pointing down" />

<Formula label="Displacement in free fall (up positive)" tex="\Delta x = v_i\,\Delta t - \tfrac{1}{2}\,g\,\Delta t^{2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 3.2, Equations of motion (ESCJY), with the acceleration equal to g, pointing down" />

<Formula label="Velocity after a height change (up positive)" tex="v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} - 2\,g\,\Delta x" cite="mit" at="Sec. 3.2, Equations of motion (ESCJY), with the acceleration equal to g, pointing down" />

Here $\Delta x$ is the change in height: positive if the object ends higher than it started, negative if it ends lower. The fourth equation, $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_i + v_f)\,\Delta t$, has no acceleration in it and stays as it was.

**Down as positive.** For an object that only falls, it is often easier to take down as positive. Then $a = +g$ and every quantity in the problem is positive. Both choices give the same physics; only the signs differ. This lesson uses up as positive unless it says otherwise.

## Dropping an object

"Dropped" means released from rest: $v_i = 0$. Take down as positive. After a time $\Delta t$ the object falls at $v = g\,\Delta t$ and has fallen a distance

<Formula label="Distance fallen from rest (down positive)" tex="\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}\,g\,\Delta t^{2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 3.2, Graphs of vertical projectile motion (ESCJZ), Cases 2 and 3 (an object falling from rest)" />

The speed grows in proportion to the time, the distance in proportion to the **square** of the time (lesson 1.6):

| Time after release | Speed | Distance fallen |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 s | 4.91 m/s | 1.23 m |
| 1.0 s | 9.81 m/s | 4.91 m |
| 1.5 s | 14.7 m/s | 11.0 m |
| 2.0 s | 19.6 m/s | 19.6 m |

Each half second adds the same 4.9 m/s to the speed, but more and more distance, because the object is moving faster all the time.

<WorkedExample title="How deep is the well?">
<Problem>
You drop a stone into a well and hear the splash 2.5 s later. Ignoring air resistance and the short time the sound takes to come back up, how deep is the water surface below you, and how fast does the stone hit it?
</Problem>
<Step title="Choose a direction and list the quantities">
The stone only falls, so take down as positive. Known: $v_i = 0$ (dropped), $a = +9.81$ m/s², $\Delta t = 2.5$ s. Wanted: $\Delta x$ and $v_f$.
</Step>
<Step title="Depth">
$\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 9.81 \times 2.5^{2} \approx 30.7$ m, so about 31 m.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed at the water">
$v_f = 0 + 9.81 \times 2.5 \approx 24.5$ m/s, about 25 m/s (roughly 88 km/h).
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
The average velocity is $\tfrac{1}{2}(0 + 24.5)$ m/s, so $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(0 + 24.5) \times 2.5 \approx 31$ m, the same. Units: m/s² times s² gives m. Because sound needs a little time to come back up, the real fall time is slightly shorter than 2.5 s, so the well is in fact a little less deep.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Throwing it down.** If you throw an object downwards instead of letting go, it starts with a velocity, and with down positive that $v_i$ is positive; nothing else changes. A pebble thrown down at 5.0 m/s from a bridge 20 m above a river reaches the water at $v_f = \sqrt{5.0^{2} + 2 \times 9.81 \times 20} \approx 20.4$ m/s, after $\Delta t = (20.4 - 5.0) / 9.81 \approx 1.57$ s. Simply dropped, it would take $\sqrt{2 \times 20 / 9.81} \approx 2.02$ s and arrive at 19.8 m/s.

## Throwing an object straight up

A ball thrown upwards leaves your hand with a positive velocity (up is positive). Gravity takes 9.81 m/s off that velocity every second. The ball slows down, stops for an instant at the top, and then falls back, its velocity now negative and growing in size.

At the top the **velocity is zero**, but the **acceleration is not**: it is still $-g$. If the acceleration were zero at the top, the velocity would stay zero and the ball would hang in the air. It is because the velocity keeps changing, from up to down, that the ball turns round.

Two results follow from $v_f = 0$ at the top. From $v_f = v_i - g\,\Delta t$, the **time to the top** is

<Formula label="Time to the highest point" tex="\Delta t = \frac{v_i}{g}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 5.2, Eqs. (5.1.21) and (5.1.22) (there for the vertical part of a throw at an angle)" />

and from $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} - 2g\,\Delta x$, the **maximum height** above the starting point is

<Formula label="Maximum height of a vertical throw" tex="\Delta x = \frac{v_i^{2}}{2\,g}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 3.2, Equations of motion, Textbook Exercise 3.1, question 1 (worked solution)" />

Twice the throwing speed gives twice the time to the top but four times the height.

**The flight is symmetric.** The acceleration is the same on the way up and on the way down, so the way down is the way up played backwards. The ball takes as long to fall from the top back to your hand as it took to rise, so the whole flight lasts $2v_i/g$. And at any height it has the same speed going down as it had going up, only in the opposite direction: it comes back to your hand at the speed you threw it with. Siyavula calls this **time symmetry**.

<WorkedExample title="A ball thrown straight up">
<Problem>
A ball is thrown straight up at 15 m/s. How long does it take to reach the top, how high does it rise, and when and how fast does it come back to the hand?
</Problem>
<Step title="Signs">
Up is positive: $v_i = +15$ m/s, $a = -9.81$ m/s². At the top $v_f = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Time to the top">
$\Delta t = \dfrac{15}{9.81} \approx 1.53$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Maximum height">
$\Delta x = \dfrac{15^{2}}{2 \times 9.81} \approx 11.5$ m above the hand.
</Step>
<Step title="Back at the hand">
By symmetry the ball is back after $2 \times 1.53 \approx 3.06$ s, moving at 15 m/s downwards: $v = -15$ m/s. Check with the first equation: $v_f = 15 - 9.81 \times 3.06 \approx -15$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
The average velocity on the way up is $\tfrac{1}{2}(15 + 0)$ m/s, so the height is $\tfrac{1}{2}(15 + 0) \times 1.53 \approx 11.5$ m, the same. Units: (m/s)² divided by m/s² gives m.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Read them together, as in lessons 1.3 and 1.5:

- The **position–time graph** is a parabola, because $\Delta x$ contains $\Delta t^{2}$. Its slope at each moment is the velocity.
- The **velocity–time graph** is a straight line sloping downwards. Its slope is the acceleration, $-g$, and the area under it is the displacement: the positive triangle (on the way up) and the negative triangle (on the way down) are equally big, so the ball ends where it started.
- The **acceleration–time graph** is flat. Nothing special happens to the acceleration at the top.

A dropped object gives a line with the same slope, starting at zero instead of $+15$ m/s; an object thrown downwards starts below zero. The slope is $-g$ in every case: what differs is only the starting velocity.

## Landing below the starting point

The equations do not care whether the object comes back to its starting height. When it lands lower, $\Delta x$ is simply negative, and the time often has to come from the quadratic formula of lesson 1.6.

<WorkedExample title="A stone thrown up from a cliff">
<Problem>
Standing at the edge of a cliff 25 m above a beach, you throw a stone straight up at 8.0 m/s. It rises, falls past the edge and lands on the beach. How long is it in the air, and how fast does it hit the beach?
</Problem>
<Step title="Signs">
Up is positive, with the origin at your hand: $v_i = +8.0$ m/s, $a = -9.81$ m/s², and the beach is below the start, so $\Delta x = -25$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Standard form">
$\Delta x = v_i\,\Delta t - \tfrac{1}{2} g\,\Delta t^{2}$ gives $-25 = 8.0\,\Delta t - 4.905\,\Delta t^{2}$, so $4.905\,\Delta t^{2} - 8.0\,\Delta t - 25 = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Quadratic formula">
$\Delta t = \dfrac{8.0 \pm \sqrt{8.0^{2} + 4 \times 4.905 \times 25}}{2 \times 4.905} = \dfrac{8.0 \pm \sqrt{554.5}}{9.81}$. With $\sqrt{554.5} \approx 23.5$: $\Delta t \approx 3.2$ s or $\Delta t \approx -1.6$ s. A negative time is before the throw, so the stone is in the air for about **3.2 s**.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed at the beach">
With the unrounded time, $v_f = 8.0 - 9.81 \times 3.216 \approx -23.5$ m/s: about 24 m/s, downwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
The third equation needs no time: $v_f^{2} = 8.0^{2} - 2 \times 9.81 \times (-25) = 554.5$, so the speed is $\sqrt{554.5} \approx 23.5$ m/s, the same. Symmetry gives another check: the stone passes the cliff edge on the way down after $2 \times 8.0 / 9.81 \approx 1.63$ s, at 8.0 m/s downwards, well before it lands.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Heavy and light objects fall alike

Drop a coin and a feather together and the coin lands first. It is easy to conclude that heavier things fall faster. Galileo concluded, hundreds of years ago, that the difference comes from the air: without air resistance, **all objects fall with the same acceleration, whatever their mass**. The MIT course notes call this Galileo's law of free falling bodies (reading 2, Sec. 5.2): the acceleration of a falling object near the Earth does not depend on its mass. Why gravity works this way, pulling harder on a heavier object but by exactly enough to give it the same acceleration, is a question for Block 2 (forces and Newton's laws).

The most famous demonstration was made on the Moon, which has almost no air. Viewers on Earth watched a feather and a hammer of 1.32 kg drop from about 1.6 m, side by side the whole way down, and land in the dust at the same instant. The astronaut letting them go was David Scott, commander of Apollo 15, as the crew's last moonwalk drew to a close; NASA's page "Apollo 15 Hammer-Feather Drop" tells the story.

## Free fall on the Moon

The Moon pulls more weakly than the Earth. NASA's Moon Fact Sheet gives its surface gravity:

<Formula label="Acceleration of free fall on the Moon" tex="g_{\text{Moon}} = 1.62\ \text{m/s}^2" cite="moon" at="Bulk parameters, surface gravity" />

That is $9.81 / 1.62 \approx 6.1$ times less than on the Earth, so everything happens in slow motion. The same equations apply, with $1.62$ m/s² in place of 9.81 m/s².

<WorkedExample title="The hammer drop, and a throw on the Moon">
<Problem>
(a) How long did the hammer and the feather take to fall the 1.6 m to the Moon's surface? How long would the hammer take on the Earth? (b) On the Moon, an astronaut throws a ball straight up at 15 m/s, the speed of the earlier example. How high does it rise?
</Problem>
<Step title="Fall time on the Moon">
Down is positive and $v_i = 0$, so $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2} g\,\Delta t^{2}$ gives $\Delta t = \sqrt{2\,\Delta x / g} = \sqrt{2 \times 1.6 / 1.62} \approx 1.4$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Fall time on the Earth">
$\Delta t = \sqrt{2 \times 1.6 / 9.81} \approx 0.57$ s: the same drop is over in less than half the time.
</Step>
<Step title="Height of the throw">
$\Delta x = \dfrac{15^{2}}{2 \times 1.62} \approx 69$ m, against 11.5 m on the Earth. The ball reaches the top after $15 / 1.62 \approx 9.3$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The height is inversely proportional to $g$, so it should be about 6.1 times the Earth value: $6.06 \times 11.47 \approx 69.5$ m, the same to within rounding. The fall times scale with the square root of that factor: $0.57 \times \sqrt{6.06} \approx 1.4$ s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
That every object falls with the same acceleration, whatever it is made of and however heavy it is, looks like a small fact about stones and feathers. Einstein took it as the starting point of his theory of gravity, general relativity, which is the theory cosmologists use to describe the expanding universe. Gravity is also the force that shapes the universe on the largest scales: it holds the Moon in orbit and galaxies together, and it pulls on the expansion of the universe as a whole. Block 2 gives Newton's law of gravitation, the first step on that road.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Free fall** is motion under gravity alone (air resistance ignored). It includes objects moving up as well as down.
- Near the Earth's surface every freely falling object has the same constant acceleration, $g = 9.81$ m/s², pointing down.
- Choose a positive direction first. With up positive, $a = -g$: $v_f = v_i - g\,\Delta t$, $\Delta x = v_i\,\Delta t - \tfrac{1}{2} g\,\Delta t^{2}$, $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} - 2g\,\Delta x$. For an object that only falls, down positive with $a = +g$ is often easier.
- Dropped from rest: $v = g\,\Delta t$ and $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2} g\,\Delta t^{2}$.
- Thrown straight up at $v_i$: the time to the top is $v_i/g$ and the maximum height $v_i^{2}/(2g)$. At the top the velocity is zero but the acceleration is still $g$, downwards.
- The flight is symmetric: the way down takes as long as the way up, and the object passes each height at the same speed in both directions.
- Graphs of a vertical throw: position a downward-opening parabola, velocity a straight line with slope $-g$, acceleration a flat line at $-g$.
- Without air, heavy and light objects fall together (Galileo; the Apollo 15 hammer and feather). On the Moon $g = 1.62$ m/s², about six times less than on the Earth.
