Lesson 0.11 met the light-year and the idea that a telescope looks back in time. This last lesson of Block 1 uses the motion tools of the block to make those ideas precise. Light is the simplest moving thing there is: it moves in a straight line at a constant speed, so the rule for constant velocity from lesson 1.2 is all we need. With it we can time signals to spacecraft, see why the Sun we see is eight minutes old, and find out why there is a limit to how far we can ever see.

## Light has a finite, constant speed

Light does not arrive instantly. It moves through empty space at a fixed speed, the **speed of light**, written $c$. The metre is defined so that this speed is exactly

$c = 299\,792\,458$ m/s,

an exact number with no uncertainty (it is listed as exact in the CODATA tables kept by NIST). To three significant figures (lesson 0.3) that is $c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s, the value this lesson uses: about 300 000 km every second, more than seven times around the Earth in one second.

Two facts make light easy to work with:

- In empty space light always moves at the same speed $c$, whatever its colour. Radio waves, microwaves and X-rays are forms of light too (Block 5 explains this), so a radio signal to a spacecraft also moves at $c$.
- Light in empty space moves in a straight line at that constant speed. Its acceleration is zero, so it is the simplest case of motion in this block: **constant velocity**.

## Distance = speed × time, for light

For motion at constant velocity, the displacement is the velocity times the time taken (lesson 1.2):

<Formula label="Displacement at constant velocity" tex="\Delta x = v\,\Delta t" cite="reading3" at="Motion at constant velocity (v = Δx/Δt, rearranged)" />

For light, $v = c$. Write $d$ for the distance the light covers and $t$ for the time it takes; then

<Formula label="Distance and travel time of light" tex="d = c\,t \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad t = \frac{d}{c}" cite="reading3" at="Motion at constant velocity (with v = c)" />

The second form, $t = d/c$, is the **light-travel time**: how long light, or any radio signal, needs to cover a distance $d$. Check the units: metres divided by metres per second gives seconds, as it should.

## Light-seconds, light-minutes and light-years

Because $c$ is fixed, a time of travel of light names a distance. Astronomers use this to make units of length:

| Unit | Light travels for | Distance |
|---|---|---|
| light-second | 1 s | $3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m |
| light-minute | 60 s | $1.80 \times 10^{10}$ m |
| light-hour | 3600 s | $1.08 \times 10^{12}$ m |
| light-year | 1 year | $9.46 \times 10^{15}$ m |

The **light-year** is the distance light travels in one year. It sounds like a time, but it is a **distance**. To find it in metres, first count the seconds in a year (taking a year as 365.25 days, the average including leap years): $365.25 \times 24 \times 3600 = 3.156 \times 10^{7}$ s. Then

<Formula label="The light-year" tex="1\ \text{light-year} = c \times 1\ \text{year} = 2.998 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s} \times 3.156 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s}" cite="reading1" at="opening paragraphs (definition in words; the multiplication is worked out here)" />

which gives $9.46 \times 10^{15}$ m, close to ten thousand billion kilometres. (Here $c$ is kept to four figures, as in lesson 0.2: the rounded $3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s would give $9.47 \times 10^{15}$ m, one unit too high in the last digit.) Light-seconds and light-minutes suit the solar system; light-years suit the stars and galaxies.

## Signal delays in the solar system

Here are three trips that light or a radio signal makes every day, worked out with $t = d/c$. You met the Moon and Sun figures in lesson 0.11; now they come from the formula.

**The Moon.** The Moon is on average 384 400 km away. Light needs $3.844 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m} / 3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s $= 1.28$ s to cover that: the Moon is about 1.3 light-seconds away. When astronauts on the Moon spoke to Earth, every answer came about $2 \times 1.28 \approx 2.6$ s after the question, the time for a signal there and back.

**The Sun.** The Sun is about 150 million km away. Its light takes $1.50 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m} / 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s} = 500$ s to reach us, about 8.3 minutes. The Sun is 8.3 light-minutes away, and the sunlight on your face left the Sun more than eight minutes ago.

**Mars.** Earth and Mars both orbit the Sun, so the distance between them keeps changing. The NASA article *Space Communications: 7 Things You Need to Know* gives about 35 million miles (56 million km) when the planets are closest and about 250 million miles (400 million km) when they are farthest apart. The one-way delay is then

- closest: $5.6 \times 10^{10}\ \text{m} / 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s} \approx 190$ s, a little over 3 minutes;
- farthest: $4.0 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m} / 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s} \approx 1300$ s, about 22 minutes.

(The same article rounds the range up to "four to 24 minutes"; its distances, divided by $c$, give the slightly shorter times above.) For comparison, Mars is on average 228 million km from the Sun, so sunlight needs about 13 minutes to reach it.

<WorkedExample title="A message to a Mars rover">
<Problem>
On a certain day Mars is 180 million km from Earth. A rover is driving slowly towards a rock at a steady 4.0 cm/s. A controller on Earth sees the rover, in the latest picture, just setting off, and sends the command "stop". How far has the rover driven by the time the command arrives? How long after the picture was taken does the controller learn that the rover has stopped?
</Problem>
<Step title="One-way delay">
$t = \dfrac{d}{c} = \dfrac{1.80 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}}{3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}} = 600$ s, which is 10.0 minutes.
</Step>
<Step title="The picture was already old">
The picture itself needed 600 s to reach Earth. If the controller sends the command at once, it needs another 600 s to reach Mars. So the rover keeps driving for $600 + 600 = 1200$ s after the moment shown in the picture.
</Step>
<Step title="Distance driven">
At constant velocity, $\Delta x = v\,\Delta t = 0.040\ \text{m/s} \times 1200\ \text{s} = 48$ m. A rover that was 30 m from the rock in the picture would have driven into it long before the "stop" arrived.
</Step>
<Step title="When the controller finds out">
The rover's reply, "I have stopped", needs another 600 s back to Earth: the controller learns of it $1200 + 600 = 1800$ s, or 30 minutes, after the picture was taken.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: m ÷ (m/s) gives s, and (m/s) × s gives m. Size: 10 minutes lies between the closest and farthest delays (3 and 22 minutes), as it should for a distance between 56 and 400 million km. This is why rovers are sent a whole day's plan at once and use their own cameras to avoid obstacles.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Looking back in time

Every view of the sky is a view of the past. Light that reaches your eye now left its source a light-travel time ago, so you see the source **as it was** when the light left, not as it is now. The Moon you see is 1.3 seconds old, the Sun 8.3 minutes old. The farther away the object, the longer its light has travelled, and the older the view.

With light-years this needs no calculation at all. An object $N$ light-years away is seen as it was $N$ years ago, because light covers one light-year per year. For stars in our own galaxy and for nearby galaxies, a distance in light-years **is** a look-back time in years. For the most distant galaxies this breaks down: space expands while their light is on its way, so their distance today is larger than the light-travel time suggests (see the horizon below).

<WorkedExample title="A star in the Middle Ages">
<Problem>
A star is 650 light-years away. How far is that in metres, and when did the light we see tonight leave it? A telescope shows that a second star, in the same direction, lies twice as far away. When did its light leave?
</Problem>
<Step title="Distance">
$650 \times 9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m} = 6.15 \times 10^{18}$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Look-back time">
Light covers one light-year per year, so the light left 650 years ago: seen from 2026, around the year 1376, in the 14th century. We see the star as it was then. If it had stopped shining since, we could not yet know.
</Step>
<Step title="The second star">
Twice the distance means twice the travel time at the same speed: 1300 light-years, so its light left 1300 years ago. Along one line of sight, nearer and farther objects are seen at different moments of the past.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Another route: $t = d/c = 6.15 \times 10^{18}\ \text{m} / 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s} = 2.05 \times 10^{10}$ s, and dividing by $3.156 \times 10^{7}$ s per year gives 650 years again.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The oldest light

How far back can we look? Not all the way. In its first few hundred thousand years the universe was filled with a hot, dense gas of charged particles (particles carrying electric charge, met properly in Block 6) in which light was scattered again and again, like light in thick fog: it could not travel far, and none of it reaches us directly. The NASA page *Universe: Overview* (its cosmic history) describes what changed. Around **380 000 years after the Big Bang** the gas had become cool enough for each atomic nucleus to hold on to its electrons (the light, negatively charged particles in the outer part of every atom; Blocks 6 and 7), making whole atoms with no net charge. With no free charged particles left to bounce off, light from that moment on could cross the universe unhindered.

That light still fills the universe. Stretched by the expansion of the universe into microwaves, it is called the **cosmic microwave background** (CMB), and it is the oldest light we can observe. It has been on its way for almost the whole age of the universe: 380 000 years is only $3.8 \times 10^{5} / 1.38 \times 10^{10} \approx 2.8 \times 10^{-5}$ of 13.8 billion years, about 0.003 percent. Seeing the CMB means seeing the universe as a baby. (The cosmology course estimates the 380 000 years from the temperature of the CMB.)

## A horizon to what we can see

The universe has an age, around **13.8 billion years**, and light has a finite speed. Together these set a limit. We can see an object only if its light has had time to reach us since the Big Bang. Just as a sailor at sea sees only as far as the horizon, there is a **horizon** to the universe we can see, and the part inside it is the **observable universe** of lesson 0.11.

A first estimate of how far the horizon lies is "speed × time" once more: in 13.8 billion years light covers 13.8 billion light-years, which is

$13.8 \times 10^{9} \times 9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m} \approx 1.31 \times 10^{26}$ m.

This is the same size as the Hubble distance of lesson 0.11, as it should be, since the Hubble time is close to the age of the universe.

That is a fair measure of how far the oldest light has *travelled*. But it is not the present distance of the objects that sent it. While the light was on its way, the universe kept expanding (lesson 0.11), so its source has moved much farther away since the light left. The distance today is therefore larger than 13.8 billion light-years. Working it out needs the expansion history. In the simplest model of the cosmology course (Guth, MIT 8.286 Lecture 8), a flat universe containing only matter, the present distance comes out at three times light's speed times the age; the real universe, with its mix of contents, gives a different factor. Here the honest conclusion is: we can see objects whose light has travelled for up to 13.8 billion years, and today they are even farther away than that.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
This whole lesson is a first step into cosmology. The university course (MIT 8.286, Lecture 8) defines the **horizon distance** as the present distance of the farthest objects whose light could have reached us, and calculates it by following a ray of light through an expanding universe: the same "distance = speed × time", but with a distance that is stretched while the light travels. Later it shows that the finite horizon raises a real puzzle: regions of the CMB on opposite sides of the sky were too far apart for light to have travelled between them, yet they have almost the same temperature. That puzzle is one of the reasons for the theory of inflation.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Light moves through empty space at a constant speed, $c = 299\,792\,458$ m/s exactly, or $3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s to three significant figures. Radio waves move at the same speed.
- Light moves at constant velocity, so $d = c\,t$ and the light-travel time is $t = d/c$.
- A light-second ($3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m), light-minute ($1.80 \times 10^{10}$ m) and light-year ($9.46 \times 10^{15}$ m) are distances: how far light goes in that time.
- Light takes about 1.3 s from the Moon and 8.3 minutes from the Sun; a signal to Mars takes between about 3 and 22 minutes, so rovers cannot be steered live.
- Looking far away is looking back in time: an object $N$ light-years away is seen as it was $N$ years ago.
- The oldest light we can see, the cosmic microwave background, was released about 380 000 years after the Big Bang.
- The age of the universe, about 13.8 billion years, sets a horizon: we only see objects whose light has had time to reach us. Because the universe expanded meanwhile, those objects are now farther away than 13.8 billion light-years.
