A car on a roundabout, the drum of a washing machine, a satellite round the Earth and the Moon round the Earth all go round in (nearly) circles. Lessons 1.1 to 1.9 described motion with a position, a velocity and an acceleration. This lesson applies the same ideas to an object going round a circle, and finds a surprise: an object can move at a perfectly steady speed and still accelerate.

## Steady speed, changing velocity

Think of a car going round a roundabout with its speedometer fixed at 30 km/h. Its **speed** does not change. But lesson 1.2 showed that **velocity** is a vector: a size (the speed) together with a direction. At every moment the car is heading in a different direction, so its velocity keeps changing. Lesson 1.4 defined acceleration as the rate of change of velocity, so the car is **accelerating**, even though it never goes faster or slower.

Motion round a circle at constant speed is called **uniform circular motion**. In this lesson the circle has radius $r$ and the speed is $v$.

Which way does the velocity point? Over a very short time the object moves along a tiny piece of the circle, and a tiny piece of a circle is almost a straight line touching the circle: a **tangent** (a line here, not the tangent of an angle from lesson 0.7). So the velocity always points **along the tangent** to the circle, at right angles to the radius (MIT notes, Sec. 6.2.1). You can see this when a stone whirled on a string is let go, or when mud flies off a spinning bicycle wheel: it leaves along the tangent, in the direction it was moving at that moment.

## Period and frequency

Uniform circular motion repeats itself. The time for one complete turn is the **period** $T$, in seconds. In one period the object travels once round the circle, a distance equal to the circumference $2\pi r$, so its speed is distance over time:

<Formula label="Speed on a circle from the period" tex="v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.4, Eqs. (6.4.3) and (6.4.4)" />

The **frequency** $f$ is the number of turns per second. If one turn takes $T$ seconds, there are $1/T$ turns in each second:

<Formula label="Frequency and period" tex="f = \frac{1}{T}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.4, Eq. (6.4.5)" />

The unit of frequency is "per second", s⁻¹, called the **hertz** (Hz). A drum turning 20 times a second has $f = 20$ Hz and $T = 1/20 = 0.050$ s. Engines and drums are often rated in revolutions (turns) per minute, rpm; divide by 60 to get hertz.

## Angles in radians and angular speed

Lesson 0.7 measured angles in **radians**: an angle $\theta$ that cuts off an arc of length $s$ on a circle of radius $r$ is $\theta = s/r$, and a full turn is $2\pi$ rad. Turned round, the arc is $s = r\theta$.

As the object goes round, the line from the centre to the object sweeps through an angle. If that angle grows by $\Delta\theta$ (in radians) in a time $\Delta t$, the **angular speed** is

<Formula label="Angular speed" tex="\omega = \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.2, Eq. (6.2.10) (written there for a very short interval)" />

The symbol $\omega$ is the Greek letter omega. Its unit is radians per second, rad/s. In uniform circular motion $\omega$ is constant. In one period the angle grows by a full turn, $2\pi$, so

<Formula label="Angular speed from period and frequency" tex="\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.4, Eqs. (6.4.4) and (6.4.5), solved for ω" />

Angular speed and ordinary speed are linked by the arc rule. In a time $\Delta t$ the object moves along an arc $s = r\,\Delta\theta$, so its speed is $v = s/\Delta t = r\,\Delta\theta/\Delta t$:

<Formula label="Speed and angular speed" tex="v = \omega r" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.2.1, Eq. (6.2.15)" />

This only works with $\omega$ in rad/s: the radian is the unit that makes arc = radius × angle true. All points of a turning wheel share the same $\omega$, but a point twice as far from the axle moves twice as fast.

<WorkedExample title="The minute hand of a clock">
<Problem>
The minute hand of a wall clock is 0.15 m long. What are its period, its angular speed and the speed of its tip?
</Problem>
<Step title="Period">
The minute hand goes round once an hour: $T = 60$ min $= 3600$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Angular speed">
$\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{3600\ \text{s}} \approx 1.75 \times 10^{-3}$ rad/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed of the tip">
$v = \omega r = 1.75 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{rad/s} \times 0.15\ \text{m} \approx 2.6 \times 10^{-4}$ m/s, about a quarter of a millimetre per second.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
In one hour the tip travels the circumference $2\pi \times 0.15 \approx 0.94$ m, and $0.94\ \text{m} / 3600\ \text{s} \approx 2.6 \times 10^{-4}$ m/s, the same. Far too slow to see, which matches watching a clock.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why the acceleration points to the centre

Now the key question: how large is the acceleration, and which way does it point?

Take two moments a short time $\Delta t$ apart. In that time the object moves round by a small angle $\Delta\theta$. The velocity is always at right angles to the radius, so when the radius turns through $\Delta\theta$, the velocity arrow turns through the **same angle** $\Delta\theta$. Its length stays $v$.

Lesson 0.8 added vectors head to tail. The first velocity plus the change gives the second, $\vec v_1 + \Delta\vec v = \vec v_2$, so if you draw the two velocity arrows from the same starting point, the change in velocity $\Delta\vec v$ is the arrow from the head of the first to the head of the second (final minus initial).

  extent={[-3, 2, -1, 4]}
  caption="Two velocity arrows of the same length, drawn from one point, for an object that has gone round by 30° (0.52 rad) between them. The change in velocity runs from the head of the first arrow to the head of the second. For a small turn it is nearly the arc of a circle of radius v, so its length is about v times the angle."
/>

The two heads lie on a circle of radius $v$, and $\Delta\vec v$ joins them. For a small angle the short straight arrow is almost the same as the arc between the heads, and the arc rule $s = r\theta$ (with radius $v$) gives

<Formula label="Size of the change in velocity for a small turn" tex="|\Delta \vec v| \approx v\,\Delta\theta" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.4.1, Eq. (6.5.2)" />

Divide by the time to get the size of the acceleration, $a = |\Delta\vec v|/\Delta t \approx v\,\Delta\theta/\Delta t = v\omega$, and the smaller $\Delta t$, the better the approximation:

<Formula label="Centripetal acceleration as v times ω" tex="a = v\,\omega" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.4.1, Eq. (6.5.3)" />

Using $\omega = v/r$, or $v = \omega r$, gives the two forms used most:

<Formula label="Centripetal acceleration" tex="a = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.4, Eqs. (6.4.6) and (6.4.7)" />

**Direction.** In the diagram $\Delta\vec v$ points almost straight back across the gap between the two arrows. For a very small turn it becomes exactly perpendicular to the velocity and points **inwards, towards the centre of the circle** (MIT notes, Sec. 6.4.1). This inward acceleration is called the **centripetal acceleration**; "centripetal" means "centre-seeking". It changes only the direction of the velocity, never its size.

Writing $v = 2\pi r/T$ gives one more useful form, handy when the period is known:

<Formula label="Centripetal acceleration from the period" tex="a = \frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.4, Eq. (6.4.9)" />

<WorkedExample title="A car on a roundabout">
<Problem>
A car drives round a roundabout of radius 20 m at a steady 36 km/h. Find the time for one full turn, its angular speed and its acceleration.
</Problem>
<Step title="Speed in m/s">
$36\ \text{km/h} = 36/3.6 = 10$ m/s (lesson 1.2).
</Step>
<Step title="Period and angular speed">
$T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{2\pi \times 20\ \text{m}}{10\ \text{m/s}} \approx 12.6$ s, and $\omega = \dfrac{v}{r} = \dfrac{10\ \text{m/s}}{20\ \text{m}} = 0.50$ rad/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Acceleration">
$a = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \dfrac{(10\ \text{m/s})^2}{20\ \text{m}} = 5.0\ \text{m/s}^2$, pointing to the centre of the roundabout.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The other form gives the same: $\omega^2 r = 0.50^2 \times 20 = 5.0$ m/s². Units: (m/s)²/m = m/s². The size, about half of $g$, is a firm but ordinary sideways push, as you feel in a car taking a roundabout briskly.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Because the speed is **squared**, it matters a lot. At 20 m/s on the same roundabout the acceleration would be $20^2/20 = 20$ m/s², four times as much, which is why roundabouts and sharp bends are taken slowly. A larger radius, on the other hand, means a smaller acceleration at the same speed.

<WorkedExample title="A washing-machine drum">
<Problem>
During the spin cycle a washing-machine drum of radius 0.25 m turns at 1200 revolutions per minute. Find its frequency, period and angular speed, the speed of the drum wall, and the acceleration of a sock pressed against the wall.
</Problem>
<Step title="Frequency and period">
$f = 1200/60 = 20$ Hz, so $T = 1/f = 1/20 = 0.050$ s per turn.
</Step>
<Step title="Angular speed and speed">
$\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi \times 20 \approx 126$ rad/s, and $v = \omega r \approx 126 \times 0.25 \approx 31$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Acceleration">
$a = \omega^2 r \approx 126^2 \times 0.25 \approx 3.9 \times 10^{3}$ m/s², towards the axis of the drum.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
With the period: $4\pi^2 r/T^2 = 4\pi^2 \times 0.25/0.050^2 \approx 3.9 \times 10^{3}$ m/s², the same. That is about 400 times $g$. The drum wall pushes the clothes inwards that hard, but the water can escape through the holes in the wall, and so it leaves the clothes.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The force needed to go round

Block 2 introduces **force**, a push or a pull, and Newton's second law, which says that an object of mass $m$ accelerates only when an overall (net) force acts on it, with force = mass × acceleration. Applied to circular motion, that law has a striking consequence: to keep an object on a circle, something must pull or push it **towards the centre**, with a force of size

<Formula label="The inward (centripetal) force" tex="F = \frac{m v^2}{r}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.1, Eq. (6.1.1) with Eq. (6.4.7)" />

This inward force is often called the **centripetal force**. It is not a new kind of force, but a job that some ordinary force must do: friction between the tyres and the road for the car on the roundabout, the drum wall for the sock, the tension in the string for a whirled stone, and gravity for the Moon. If that force disappears, the object carries on along the tangent. For the car above, with a mass of 1200 kg, the road must supply $1200 \times 5.0 = 6.0 \times 10^{3}$ N (newtons, the unit of force; Block 2). Lesson 2.9 returns to this force in detail.

## The Moon's orbit

The Moon's orbit round the Earth is nearly a circle. Its average distance from the Earth is 384 400 km, and it goes round once in 27.3 days, measured against the distant stars (NASA, Moon Fact Sheet).

<WorkedExample title="How fast does the Moon fall towards the Earth?">
<Problem>
Treat the Moon's orbit as a circle of radius $3.844 \times 10^{8}$ m with a period of 27.3 days. Find the Moon's speed and its centripetal acceleration.
</Problem>
<Step title="Period in seconds">
$T = 27.3 \times 24 \times 3600\ \text{s} \approx 2.36 \times 10^{6}$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed">
$v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T} = \dfrac{2\pi \times 3.844 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}}{2.36 \times 10^{6}\ \text{s}} \approx 1.02 \times 10^{3}$ m/s, about a kilometre per second.
</Step>
<Step title="Acceleration">
$a = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \dfrac{(1.02 \times 10^{3})^2}{3.844 \times 10^{8}} \approx 2.7 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s², towards the Earth.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The period form gives the same: $4\pi^2 r/T^2 \approx 2.7 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s². The speed agrees with the Moon's mean orbital velocity of about 1.02 km/s on the same NASA fact sheet.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The Moon is accelerating towards the Earth all the time: in a sense it is always falling, but it moves sideways fast enough to keep missing. The MIT notes (Sec. 6.1) tell how Newton realised this around 1666. The Moon's acceleration is about 3600 times smaller than $g$ at the Earth's surface. Block 2 shows how gravity, growing weaker with distance, explains about this number.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Circular motion reaches far beyond the Earth. The Sun, with all its planets, orbits the centre of our galaxy, the Milky Way, at an average speed of about 720 000 km/h, and takes about 230 million years for one orbit (NASA Science, Sun: Facts). That speed is $2.0 \times 10^{5}$ m/s; with $r = vT/(2\pi)$ the radius of the orbit comes out at about $2.3 \times 10^{20}$ m, and the Sun's centripetal acceleration $v^2/r$ at only about $1.7 \times 10^{-10}$ m/s². Tiny, but something has to supply it: mainly the pull of everything inside the orbit.

Astronomers measure such orbital speeds for stars and gas at many distances from the centres of galaxies. Far from the centre the speeds stay high, higher than the pull of the stars and gas we can see should allow for circles that large. Most astronomers take this as one of the main signs of **dark matter**, matter that does not shine but does pull. What dark matter is made of is still unknown, and some physicists instead test whether gravity itself behaves differently at such tiny accelerations. The graduate year of this site (8.902 and *Fundamentals of Dark Matter*) works through this evidence.
</Callout>

## Summary

- In **uniform circular motion** the speed is constant but the direction of the velocity keeps changing, so the object **accelerates**.
- The velocity points along the **tangent**, at right angles to the radius.
- **Period** $T$: time for one turn. **Frequency** $f = 1/T$, in hertz (Hz). Speed $v = 2\pi r/T$.
- **Angular speed** $\omega = \Delta\theta/\Delta t = 2\pi/T = 2\pi f$, with angles in radians, in rad/s; $v = \omega r$.
- In a short time the velocity arrow turns through the same angle as the radius, so $|\Delta\vec v| \approx v\,\Delta\theta$ and $a = v\omega$.
- **Centripetal acceleration**: $a = v^2/r = \omega^2 r = 4\pi^2 r/T^2$, pointing **to the centre**.
- An inward force $F = mv^2/r$ is needed, supplied by friction, tension, gravity or a wall; Block 2 explains forces.
- The Moon: $v \approx 1.02$ km/s and $a \approx 2.7 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s² towards the Earth.
