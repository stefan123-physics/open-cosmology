This set practises the eleven lessons of Block 1. The problems, hints and solutions are our own; the methods are those of the lessons, which build on Siyavula's chapters on motion in one dimension and vertical projectile motion and on MIT 8.01SC's course notes on motion in a plane, on circles and on reference frames, and on NASA's explanation of the light-year. Problems 1 to 10 count 10 points each, 100 in all; Problem 11 is optional. Throughout, take $g = 9.81$ m/s².

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. Number boxes accept a small rounding difference, so give your answer to two or three significant figures, in the unit shown. A velocity, displacement or acceleration along a line has a sign: type the minus sign when the answer points the negative way. Formula boxes take plain typing, for example `(a + b)/c`, and accept every correct way of writing the answer. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A dog fetching a stick" points={10}>

A dog runs along a straight beach, with positions measured in metres from a lifeguard post and positive towards the pier. It starts at $x = 6.0$ m, runs to $x = 21$ m to pick up a stick, and then runs back to $x = -3.0$ m, on the other side of the post, where its owner waits.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

What distance does the dog cover?

<Hint>Distance adds up the length of every leg, whatever its direction.</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="Distance (m):" value="39" tolerance="5%" calc="p1-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Add the legs">
Out: from 6.0 m to 21 m is 15 m. Back: from 21 m to −3.0 m is 24 m. The distance is $15 + 24 = 39$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Distance can never be negative, and it is at least as large as the size of the displacement found in part (b).
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

What is the dog's displacement?

<Hint>Displacement is the final position minus the starting position; the path in between does not matter.</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="Displacement (m):" value="-9" tolerance="5%" calc="p1-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Final minus initial">
$\Delta x = x_f - x_i = -3.0 - 6.0 = -9.0$ m: 9.0 m away from the pier.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Adding the legs with signs gives the same: $+15 + (-24) = -9$ m. The size, 9.0 m, is much less than the 39 m covered, because the dog doubled back.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A jog and a walk" points={10}>

A runner jogs 2.4 km due north along a straight road in 10 minutes. She then turns round and walks 0.60 km back south in 8.0 minutes.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

What is her average speed over the whole 18 minutes, in m/s?

<Hint>Average speed is the total distance divided by the total time. Work in metres and seconds.</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="Average speed (m/s):" value="2.78" tolerance="5%" calc="p2-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Total distance and time">
Distance: $2400 + 600 = 3000$ m. Time: 18 minutes is $18 \times 60 = 1080$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Divide">
Average speed $= 3000/1080 = 2.78$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
In km/h that is $2.78 \times 3.6 = 10$ km/h, between a fast walk and a jog, as the trip mixes both.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

What is her average velocity, taking north as positive?

<Hint>Average velocity is the displacement divided by the time. Find where she ends up compared with where she started.</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="Average velocity (m/s):" value="1.67" tolerance="5%" calc="p2-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Displacement">
$\Delta x = +2400 + (-600) = +1800$ m, so 1.8 km north.
</Step>
<Step title="Divide by the time">
$v_\text{ave} = 1800/1080 = 1.67$ m/s, pointing north.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The average velocity is smaller than the average speed because part of the trip undid the rest. The ratio $1800/3000 = 0.60$ is the same as $1.67/2.78$.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A toy train on a track" points={10}>

A toy train runs on a straight track. Its position–time graph is made of two straight pieces: from (0 s, 2.0 m) to (6.0 s, 14 m), and then from (6.0 s, 14 m) to (10 s, 5.0 m).

<Part label="a" points={4}>

What is the train's velocity during the second piece, from 6.0 s to 10 s?

<Hint>The velocity is the slope of the position–time graph: rise over run, with the sign of the rise.</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="Velocity (m/s):" value="-2.25" tolerance="5%" calc="p3-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Rise over run">
$v = \dfrac{5.0 - 14}{10 - 6.0} = \dfrac{-9.0}{4.0} = -2.25$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The graph slopes down, so the train moves back towards the start: a negative velocity fits. In the first piece the slope is $12/6.0 = 2.0$ m/s, so on the way back it runs a little faster.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the train's average velocity over the whole 10 s?

<Hint>Use only the first and the last point of the graph.</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="Average velocity (m/s):" value="0.3" tolerance="5%" calc="p3-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Secant from start to end">
$v_\text{ave} = \dfrac{5.0 - 2.0}{10 - 0} = 0.30$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Over the 10 s the train ends only 3.0 m beyond its start, so a small positive average velocity is right.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

What is its average speed over the whole 10 s?

<Hint>Add the distances of the two pieces, both counted as positive.</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="Average speed (m/s):" value="2.1" tolerance="5%" calc="p3-c" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Distance over time">
The train covers 12 m out and 9.0 m back, 21 m in all, so the average speed is $21/10 = 2.1$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
It lies between the two speeds of the pieces, 2.0 m/s and 2.25 m/s, as it must.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A ball against a wall" points={10}>

A tennis ball hits a wall at 8.0 m/s and bounces straight back at 6.0 m/s. It touches the wall for 0.040 s. Take the direction towards the wall as positive.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

What is the change in the ball's velocity?

<Hint>Write both velocities with their signs before you subtract.</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="Change in velocity (m/s):" value="-14" tolerance="5%" calc="p4-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Signs first">
Before: $v_i = +8.0$ m/s. After: $v_f = -6.0$ m/s, because the ball moves away from the wall.
</Step>
<Step title="Subtract">
$\Delta v = v_f - v_i = -6.0 - 8.0 = -14$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The ball first has to lose its 8.0 m/s and then gain 6.0 m/s the other way, so the change is larger than either speed. Ignoring the signs would give only 2 m/s, the common mistake.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

What is the ball's average acceleration while it touches the wall?

<Answer id="p4-b" label="Average acceleration (m/s²):" value="-350" tolerance="5%" calc="p4-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Change over time">
$a = \Delta v/\Delta t = -14/0.040 = -350$ m/s², pointing away from the wall.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: (m/s)/s = m/s². The size is about 36 times $g$, large but believable for a contact lasting only four hundredths of a second.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A skateboarder on a slope" points={10}>

A skateboarder coasts up a long straight slope without pushing. Her velocity, with up the slope as positive, falls steadily from $+6.0$ m/s at $t = 0$ to $-3.0$ m/s at $t = 6.0$ s: she slows, stops and rolls back down.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is her acceleration?

<Hint>The acceleration is the slope of the velocity–time graph, which here is a straight line.</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="Acceleration (m/s²):" value="-1.5" tolerance="5%" calc="p5-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Slope of the line">
$a = \dfrac{-3.0 - 6.0}{6.0 - 0} = \dfrac{-9.0}{6.0} = -1.5$ m/s², down the slope the whole time.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
She stops when $6.0 - 1.5\,t = 0$, at $t = 4.0$ s, which is inside the 6.0 s, as the problem says.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is her displacement over the 6.0 s?

<Hint>The displacement is the area between the velocity–time graph and the time axis, counting area below the axis as negative.</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="Displacement (m):" value="9" tolerance="5%" calc="p5-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Two triangles">
Above the axis, from 0 to 4.0 s: $\tfrac12 \times 4.0 \times 6.0 = 12$ m up the slope. Below the axis, from 4.0 s to 6.0 s: $\tfrac12 \times 2.0 \times 3.0 = 3.0$ m back down.
</Step>
<Step title="Add with signs">
$\Delta x = 12 - 3.0 = 9.0$ m up the slope.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
With constant acceleration, $\Delta x = \tfrac12(v_i + v_f)\,\Delta t = \tfrac12 \times (6.0 - 3.0) \times 6.0 = 9.0$ m.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

What distance does she cover in the 6.0 s?

<Answer id="p5-c" label="Distance (m):" value="15" tolerance="5%" calc="p5-c" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Add the sizes">
Up 12 m and down 3.0 m: the distance is $12 + 3.0 = 15$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The distance is larger than the displacement by twice the 3.0 m rolled back, $9.0 + 2 \times 3.0 = 15$ m.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="A train on a straight line" points={10}>

A train speeds up with constant acceleration along a straight track.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Use $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2\,a\,\Delta x$ to write the acceleration $a$ in terms of $v_i$, $v_f$ and $\Delta x$. Type `vi`, `vf` and `dx`.

<Hint>Move $v_i^{2}$ to the other side first, then divide.</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-a" label="$a =$" expr="(vf^2 - vi^2)/(2*dx)" vars={{ vf: [10, 40], vi: [2, 9], dx: [50, 500] }} names={{ vf: "v_f", vi: "v_i", dx: "\\Delta x" }}
  mistakes={[{ expr: "(vf^2 - vi^2)/dx", hint: "Divide by the whole of 2Δx, not by Δx alone." }, { expr: "(vi^2 - vf^2)/(2*dx)", hint: "Check the sign: subtract v_i² from both sides." }, { expr: "(vf - vi)^2/(2*dx)", hint: "v_f² − v_i² is not the same as (v_f − v_i)²." }]} />

<Solution>
<Step title="Subtract, then divide">
Subtract $v_i^{2}$ from both sides: $v_f^{2} - v_i^{2} = 2\,a\,\Delta x$. Divide by $2\,\Delta x$: $a = \dfrac{v_f^{2} - v_i^{2}}{2\,\Delta x}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with units">
(m/s)² divided by m gives m²/s² ÷ m = m/s², an acceleration. A train that speeds up ($v_f > v_i$) gets a positive $a$.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

The train speeds up from 12 m/s to 30 m/s over 420 m. What is its acceleration?

<Answer id="p6-b" label="Acceleration (m/s²):" value="0.9" tolerance="5%" calc="p6-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Use the rearranged formula">
$a = \dfrac{30^{2} - 12^{2}}{2 \times 420} = \dfrac{900 - 144}{840} = \dfrac{756}{840} = 0.90$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Put it back: $12^{2} + 2 \times 0.90 \times 420 = 144 + 756 = 900 = 30^{2}$.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How long does it take?

<Hint>The equation with the average velocity, $\Delta x = \tfrac12(v_i + v_f)\,\Delta t$, does not need the acceleration.</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-c" label="Time (s):" value="20" tolerance="5%" calc="p6-c" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Average velocity">
$\Delta t = \dfrac{2\,\Delta x}{v_i + v_f} = \dfrac{2 \times 420}{12 + 30} = \dfrac{840}{42} = 20$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
With the acceleration of part (b): $\Delta t = (v_f - v_i)/a = 18/0.90 = 20$ s as well.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="A ball thrown up from a balcony" points={10}>

A ball is thrown straight up at 11 m/s from a balcony 6.0 m above the ground. It just misses the balcony on the way down and lands on the ground below. Ignore air resistance and take up as positive.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

How high above the balcony does the ball rise?

<Hint>At the highest point the velocity is zero.</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-a" label="Height above the balcony (m):" value="6.17" tolerance="5%" calc="p7-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Velocity zero at the top">
$0 = v_i^{2} - 2\,g\,\Delta x$ gives $\Delta x = \dfrac{v_i^{2}}{2g} = \dfrac{121}{19.62} = 6.17$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The ball needs $11/9.81 = 1.12$ s to stop, at an average velocity of 5.5 m/s: $5.5 \times 1.121 = 6.17$ m.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

How long after the throw does it hit the ground?

<Hint>The ground is 6.0 m below the starting point, so the displacement at landing is $\Delta x = -6.0$ m.</Hint>
<Hint>$\Delta x = v_i\,\Delta t - \tfrac12\,g\,\Delta t^{2}$ becomes a quadratic equation in $\Delta t$. Keep the root that comes after the throw.</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-b" label="Time (s):" value="2.7" tolerance="5%" calc="p7-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="The quadratic equation">
$-6.0 = 11\,\Delta t - 4.905\,\Delta t^{2}$, or $4.905\,\Delta t^{2} - 11\,\Delta t - 6.0 = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Quadratic formula">
$\Delta t = \dfrac{11 \pm \sqrt{11^{2} + 4 \times 4.905 \times 6.0}}{2 \times 4.905} = \dfrac{11 \pm \sqrt{238.72}}{9.81} = \dfrac{11 \pm 15.45}{9.81}$. The plus sign gives 2.70 s; the minus sign gives −0.45 s, a time before the throw, which we drop.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
The top is $6.0 + 6.17 = 12.17$ m above the ground. Falling that far from rest takes $\sqrt{2 \times 12.17/9.81} = 1.58$ s, and $1.12 + 1.58 = 2.70$ s.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How fast is it moving when it hits the ground?

<Answer id="p7-c" label="Speed (m/s):" value="15.5" tolerance="5%" calc="p7-c" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Without the time">
$v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} - 2\,g\,\Delta x = 121 - 2 \times 9.81 \times (-6.0) = 238.72$, so the speed is 15.5 m/s (the velocity is −15.5 m/s, downwards).
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The same square root appeared in part (b). And from the time: $11 - 9.81 \times 2.696 = -15.4$ m/s, the same to rounding.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 8

<Exercise id="p8" title="A golf drive" points={10}>

A golf ball is hit from level ground at 30 m/s, at 25° above the horizontal. Ignore air resistance.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

How long is the ball in the air?

<Hint>Split the launch velocity into a horizontal and a vertical component.</Hint>
<Hint>Only the vertical component decides the flight time: the ball comes down when its vertical velocity has reversed.</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-a" label="Time of flight (s):" value="2.58" tolerance="5%" calc="p8-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Components">
$v_x = 30 \cos 25^{\circ} = 27.2$ m/s and $v_y = 30 \sin 25^{\circ} = 12.7$ m/s at launch.
</Step>
<Step title="Up and down">
The ball rises for $12.68/9.81 = 1.29$ s and takes as long to come down, so $t_\text{flight} = 2 \times 12.68/9.81 = 2.58$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: (m/s)/(m/s²) = s. A ball thrown straight up at the full 30 m/s would stay up about $2 \times 30/9.81 = 6.1$ s; at a low angle, less than half of that makes sense.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

How far away does it land?

<Hint>The horizontal component of the velocity does not change during the flight.</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-b" label="Range (m):" value="70.3" tolerance="5%" calc="p8-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Horizontal motion">
$R = v_x\,t_\text{flight} = 27.19 \times 2.585 = 70.3$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with the range formula">
$R = \dfrac{v_0^{2}\sin 2\theta}{g} = \dfrac{900 \times \sin 50^{\circ}}{9.81} = \dfrac{900 \times 0.766}{9.81} = 70.3$ m.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 9

<Exercise id="p9" title="Landing straight opposite" points={10}>

A small boat can move at 3.0 m/s relative to the water. The skipper wants to cross a river 48 m wide, which flows at 1.8 m/s relative to the banks, and land at the point straight opposite the start.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

At what angle to the straight-across direction must the skipper aim the boat upstream?

<Hint>Velocity of the boat relative to the bank = velocity relative to the water + velocity of the water relative to the bank.</Hint>
<Hint>To travel straight across, the upstream component of the boat's velocity through the water must cancel the current exactly.</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-a" label="Angle upstream (degrees):" value="36.9" tolerance="5%" calc="p9-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Cancel the current">
Split the 3.0 m/s through the water into a part straight across and a part upstream. Aimed at an angle $\theta$ upstream of straight across, the upstream part is $3.0 \sin\theta$, and it must equal the 1.8 m/s of the current: $\sin\theta = 1.8/3.0 = 0.60$.
</Step>
<Step title="The angle">
$\theta = 36.9^{\circ}$ upstream of straight across.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The upstream part can never be larger than the boat's own speed. A boat slower than the current could not land straight opposite at all: $\sin\theta$ would have to be larger than 1.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

How long does the crossing then take?

<Hint>Only the component of the velocity across the river carries the boat to the other bank.</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-b" label="Time (s):" value="20" tolerance="5%" calc="p9-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Component across">
$3.0 \cos 36.9^{\circ} = 2.4$ m/s. Pythagoras gives the same: $\sqrt{3.0^{2} - 1.8^{2}} = \sqrt{5.76} = 2.4$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Time to cross">
$\Delta t = 48/2.4 = 20$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Aimed straight across, the boat would cross in only $48/3.0 = 16$ s, but it would move at $\alpha = 31^{\circ}$ downstream of straight across ($\tan\alpha = 1.8/3.0$) and land $1.8 \times 16 = 28.8$ m downstream. Landing opposite costs 4 s more.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 10

<Exercise id="p10" title="A fairground ride" points={10}>

On a fairground ride, a chair moves in a level circle of radius 6.0 m and goes round once every 4.0 s.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the chair's speed?

<Hint>In one period the chair travels once round the circle.</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-a" label="Speed (m/s):" value="9.42" tolerance="5%" calc="p10-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Circumference over period">
$v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T} = \dfrac{2\pi \times 6.0}{4.0} = 9.42$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
That is about 34 km/h ($9.42 \times 3.6$), a fair speed for a ride.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is its angular speed?

<Answer id="p10-b" label="Angular speed (rad/s):" value="1.57" tolerance="5%" calc="p10-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="One turn is 2π radians">
$\omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} = \dfrac{2\pi}{4.0} = 1.57$ rad/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
$v = \omega r = 1.5708 \times 6.0 = 9.42$ m/s, the speed of part (a).
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

What is the size of its centripetal acceleration?

<Hint>The acceleration points to the centre and has size $v^{2}/r$, or $\omega^{2} r$.</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-c" label="Acceleration (m/s²):" value="14.8" tolerance="5%" calc="p10-c" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Speed squared over radius">
$a = \dfrac{v^{2}}{r} = \dfrac{9.425^{2}}{6.0} = 14.8$ m/s², towards the centre.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
$a = \omega^{2} r = 1.571^{2} \times 6.0 = 14.8$ m/s². That is about 1.5 times $g$.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 11

<Exercise id="p11" title="Messages and starlight" points={10} optional>

This problem is optional: it does not count towards the score of the set.

Take the speed of light as $3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s and one light-year as $9.46 \times 10^{15}$ m.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

A space probe is $2.25 \times 10^{11}$ m from the Earth. How many minutes does a radio signal from the probe take to reach us?

<Hint>Light and radio waves travel at constant speed, so time = distance / speed.</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-a" label="Time (minutes):" value="12.5" tolerance="5%" calc="p11-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Distance over speed">
$t = \dfrac{2.25 \times 10^{11}}{3.00 \times 10^{8}} = 750$ s, which is $750/60 = 12.5$ minutes.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The Sun, at $1.50 \times 10^{11}$ m, is 500 light-seconds (8.33 light-minutes) away; the probe is one and a half times as far, and $1.5 \times 8.33 = 12.5$ minutes.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

A star is 6.0 light-years away. How far is that in metres? Type a number like `1.2e5`.

<Answer id="p11-b" label="Distance (m):" value="5.68e16" tolerance="5%" calc="p11-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Multiply">
$6.0 \times 9.46 \times 10^{15} = 5.68 \times 10^{16}$ m. The light we see from it left the star 6.0 years ago.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The light of part (a) took only minutes; a light-year is about $9.46 \times 10^{15} / (2.25 \times 10^{11}) \approx 42\,000$ times the probe's distance, so the star's light takes years, not minutes.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>
