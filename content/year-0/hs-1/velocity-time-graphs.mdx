Lesson 1.3 drew position against time; lesson 1.4 introduced the acceleration, the rate at which the velocity changes. A **velocity–time graph** brings the two together. Time runs along the horizontal axis and the velocity along the vertical axis, so every point says how fast, and in which direction, the object moved at that moment. This one graph holds a surprising amount: its slope is the acceleration, and the area under it is the displacement. This lesson shows how to read both, and how to go from a velocity–time graph to a position–time graph and back.

## What the graph shows

Choose a positive direction, as in lesson 1.1. The velocity $v$ is then a number with a sign: positive when the object moves in the positive direction, negative when it moves the other way. On the graph, points above the time axis mean moving forwards, points below it mean moving backwards, and a point on the axis means at rest at that instant.

The most common mistake is to read a velocity–time graph as if it were a position–time graph. The height of the line is **how fast**, not **where**. A line that comes back down to the axis does not mean the object came home; it means the object came to a stop, wherever that was.

The basic shapes:

- **On the time axis** ($v = 0$ all the time): the object stands still.
- **Horizontal, above or below the axis:** a constant velocity, forwards or backwards (uniform motion).
- **A straight sloping line:** the velocity changes by the same amount every second, so the acceleration is constant.
- **Crossing the axis:** the velocity changes sign; the object stops for an instant and turns around.

Whether the object speeds up or slows down depends on the line moving **away from** or **towards** the time axis. Moving away from the axis, the speed grows: the velocity and the acceleration have the same sign. Moving towards it, the speed falls: they have opposite signs, as lesson 1.4 explained.

## The slope is the acceleration

On a position–time graph the slope was the velocity. On a velocity–time graph, rise over run is the change in velocity divided by the change in time, which is the average acceleration of lesson 1.4:

<Formula label="Acceleration as the slope of a velocity–time graph" tex="a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v_f - v_i}{t_f - t_i}" cite="mit" at="21.6, Motion at constant acceleration" />

Here $v_i$ is the velocity at the start time $t_i$ and $v_f$ the velocity at the final time $t_f$. The units are those of the vertical axis divided by those of the horizontal axis: (m/s)/s = m/s².

<WorkedExample title="A train leaving a station">
<Problem>
A train pulls out of a station along a straight track. Its velocity–time graph is a straight line from 5.0 m/s at $t = 4.0$ s to 14 m/s at $t = 16$ s. What is its acceleration?
</Problem>
<Step title="Rise and run">
Rise: $\Delta v = 14\ \text{m/s} - 5.0\ \text{m/s} = 9.0$ m/s. Run: $\Delta t = 16\ \text{s} - 4.0\ \text{s} = 12$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Slope">
$a = \dfrac{9.0\ \text{m/s}}{12\ \text{s}} = 0.75$ m/s², in the direction of motion.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The units are m/s², as an acceleration should be. Every second the train gains 0.75 m/s, so in 12 s it gains $0.75 \times 12 = 9.0$ m/s, which is the rise.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

A steeper line means a larger acceleration. A line that slopes down has a negative slope, so a negative acceleration. When the velocity–time graph curves, the acceleration changes; its value at one instant is the slope of the tangent, just as the tangent of a position–time graph gave the velocity in lesson 1.3:

<Formula label="Instantaneous acceleration" tex="a(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{v(t + \Delta t) - v(t)}{\Delta t}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 4.4.2, Eq. (4.3.25)" />

In this block the velocity graphs are mostly straight lines, so the slope is the same everywhere along each piece. The worked example at the end of the next section reads a tangent off a curved graph.

## The area is the displacement

Lesson 0.10 showed the other half of the story: the area between a velocity–time graph and the time axis is the displacement. For a constant velocity the area is a rectangle, velocity × time, and (m/s) × s = m confirms that it is a length:

<Formula label="Area under a velocity–time graph" tex="\Delta x = \text{area between the velocity–time graph and the time axis}" cite="mit" at="21.6, Motion at constant velocity" />

When the graph is made of straight pieces, split the area into rectangles and triangles: a rectangle has area width × height, a triangle $\tfrac12$ × base × height.

**Area below the axis.** A negative velocity means moving backwards, so area below the time axis counts as a **negative** displacement. That gives two different totals, the two quantities of lesson 1.1:

- the **displacement** is the area above the axis minus the area below it;
- the **distance** travelled adds all the areas as positive numbers.

<WorkedExample title="A remote-controlled car">
<Problem>
A remote-controlled car moves along a straight track; forwards is positive. Its velocity–time graph is shown above. From 0 to 4.0 s it speeds up steadily from rest to 2.0 m/s; from 4.0 s to 12 s it moves at 2.0 m/s; from 12 s to 16 s the graph is one straight line from 2.0 m/s to −2.0 m/s; from 16 s to 20 s it moves at −2.0 m/s; from 20 s to 22 s it slows steadily to rest. Find the acceleration from 12 s to 16 s, and the displacement and distance for the whole trip.
</Problem>
<Step title="Acceleration from 12 s to 16 s">
$a = \dfrac{-2.0\ \text{m/s} - 2.0\ \text{m/s}}{16\ \text{s} - 12\ \text{s}} = \dfrac{-4.0}{4.0} = -1.0$ m/s². The line is straight, so the acceleration is the same while the car slows down, stops at 14 s and starts back: at the moment it stops, the velocity is zero but the acceleration is not.
</Step>
<Step title="Areas above the axis">
0 to 4.0 s, a triangle: $\tfrac12 \times 4.0 \times 2.0 = 4.0$ m. 4.0 to 12 s, a rectangle: $8.0 \times 2.0 = 16$ m. 12 to 14 s, a triangle: $\tfrac12 \times 2.0 \times 2.0 = 2.0$ m. Together $4.0 + 16 + 2.0 = 22$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Areas below the axis">
14 to 16 s, a triangle of 2.0 m; 16 to 20 s, a rectangle of $4.0 \times 2.0 = 8.0$ m; 20 to 22 s, another triangle of 2.0 m. Together $2.0 + 8.0 + 2.0 = 12$ m, backwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Displacement and distance">
Displacement $= 22 - 12 = 10$ m forwards. Distance $= 22 + 12 = 34$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The distance is larger than the size of the displacement, as it must be when an object turns back. Every area is (m/s) × s, so every answer is in metres.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Curved graphs.** When the velocity–time graph curves, neither rule changes; only the reading becomes an estimate. For the slope at one moment, draw the tangent there by eye, pick two points on it far apart and work out rise over run. For the area, cut it into strips (lesson 0.10), treat each strip as a rectangle whose height is the velocity in the middle of the strip, and add them up.

/>

<WorkedExample title="An electric scooter pulling away">
<Problem>
A simple model of an electric scooter pulling away from rest gives the velocity–time graph above: it gains speed quickly at first and then more and more slowly. Estimate its acceleration at $t = 4.0$ s, and how far it travels in the first 8.0 s.
</Problem>
<Step title="Slope of the tangent">
The dashed tangent touches the curve at $t = 4.0$ s. Read two points on it far apart: 3.0 m/s at $t = 0$ and 9.0 m/s at $t = 8.0$ s. Its slope is $\dfrac{9.0 - 3.0}{8.0 - 0} = 0.75$ m/s², so at $t = 4.0$ s the scooter speeds up by about 0.75 m/s every second. (Earlier the tangent is steeper, later flatter: the acceleration falls as the scooter gets going.)
</Step>
<Step title="Area with strips">
Cut the first 8.0 s into four strips 2.0 s wide. In the middle of each strip, at 1.0, 3.0, 5.0 and 7.0 s, the graph reads about 3.0, 5.2, 6.7 and 7.9 m/s. The four rectangles together give $2.0 \times (3.0 + 5.2 + 6.7 + 7.9) \approx 46$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The answer is in metres, (m/s) × s. Thinner strips change the estimate only a little: with ever thinner strips the total settles at about 45 m, so four strips were already good to about one metre.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Constant acceleration: a straight line

With a constant acceleration $a$ the velocity changes by $a$ every second, so the graph is a straight line with slope $a$. Starting from $v_i$ at time 0, after a time $t$ the velocity is $v_f = v_i + a\,t$: the rearranged slope formula.

The area under that line is a **trapezium**. Split it into a rectangle of height $v_i$ and a triangle on top of height $v_f - v_i$, both of width $t$:

<Formula label="Displacement as the area of the trapezium" tex="\Delta x = v_i\,t + \tfrac12\,(v_f - v_i)\,t = \tfrac12\,(v_i + v_f)\,t" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 4.5.2, Eq. (4.4.5); second form Eq. (4.4.8), with v₀ + ½at written as ½(v_i + v_f)" />

The second form says: for constant acceleration, the displacement is the average of the start and end velocities times the time. Now replace the height of the triangle, $v_f - v_i$, by $a\,t$:

<Formula label="Displacement with constant acceleration" tex="\Delta x = v_i\,t + \tfrac12\,a\,t^{2}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 4.5.2, Eq. (4.4.6)" />

The term $v_i\,t$ is the rectangle, how far the object would go if it kept its starting velocity; the term $\tfrac12 a t^{2}$ is the triangle, the extra distance gained by speeding up. Lesson 1.6 collects these results with a few others into the equations for constant acceleration; here you have seen where they come from.

<WorkedExample title="A bus leaving a village">
<Problem>
A bus drives at 6.0 m/s as it passes the last house of a village. From there it speeds up steadily at 0.50 m/s² for 8.0 s. How fast is it going after 8.0 s, and how far has it gone?
</Problem>
<Step title="Final velocity from the slope">
$v_f = v_i + a\,t = 6.0 + 0.50 \times 8.0 = 10$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Distance from the trapezium">
$\Delta x = \tfrac12\,(6.0 + 10) \times 8.0 = 64$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by the other formula">
$v_i\,t + \tfrac12 a t^{2} = 6.0 \times 8.0 + \tfrac12 \times 0.50 \times 8.0^{2} = 48 + 16 = 64$ m. Both routes agree: 48 m for the rectangle and 16 m for the triangle on top.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Plot variable="t" domain={[0, 8]} range={[0, 12]} xLabel="time (s)" yLabel="velocity (m/s)"
  curves={[
    { expr: "6 + 0.5*t", label: "bus" },
    { expr: "6", label: "no speeding up", dash: true }
  ]}
  points={[{ x: 8, y: 10, label: "10 m/s" }]}
  caption="The bus, computed from v = 6.0 + 0.50t. The rectangle under the dashed line (6.0 m/s for 8.0 s) is 48 m; the triangle between the two lines adds 16 m, for 64 m in all."

**The acceleration–time graph.** One graph further, the same pattern repeats. With a constant acceleration, a graph of acceleration against time is a horizontal line, and the area under it (acceleration × time) is the change in velocity:

<Formula label="Area under an acceleration–time graph" tex="\Delta v = a\,t" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 4.5.1, Eq. (4.4.4)" />

For the bus: $0.50\ \text{m/s}^2 \times 8.0\ \text{s} = 4.0$ m/s, the rise from 6.0 m/s to 10 m/s.

## From velocity–time to position–time, and back

The two graphs describe the same motion, so each can be sketched from the other.

**From a velocity–time graph to a position–time graph.** The velocity is the slope of the position graph, and the area under the velocity graph is the change in position. So:

1. Where $v$ is positive the position rises; where $v$ is negative it falls; where $v = 0$ the position graph is flat for that instant (a turning point if the velocity changes sign there).
2. A horizontal piece of the velocity graph (constant velocity) gives a straight piece of the position graph.
3. A sloping straight piece (constant acceleration) gives a curved piece: bending upwards when $a$ is positive, downwards when $a$ is negative.
4. To get the heights, add up the area under the velocity graph from the start.

For the remote-controlled car, starting at $x = 0$: after 4.0 s it is at 4.0 m, after 12 s at $4.0 + 16 = 20$ m, and at 14 s, when it turns, at its farthest point, 22 m. It then comes back to 20 m at 16 s, to 12 m at 20 s and stops at 10 m, its displacement.

**From a position–time graph to a velocity–time graph.** Read the slope of each piece, as in lesson 1.3, and plot it. Take the lift of lesson 1.3: it waited, rose at 2.0 m/s for 6.0 s, waited again and came down at 3.0 m/s. Its velocity–time graph is four horizontal pieces: at 0, at +2.0 m/s, at 0 and at −3.0 m/s. (A real lift speeds up and slows down gradually, so the jumps between the pieces would really be short, steep slopes.) As a check, the areas give back the motion: $2.0 \times 6.0 = 12$ m up and $-3.0 \times 4.0 = -12$ m down, so the lift ends where it began.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Picture a distant galaxy and a graph of the speed at which it moves away from us, against time. The slope of that graph tells whether the expansion of the universe is slowing down or speeding up. For roughly the first eight billion years gravity slowed the expansion: the slope was negative. Observations of distant exploding stars (supernovae) showed that for the last several billion years the expansion has been speeding up instead, so the slope has turned positive. And the area under the graph, added up over billions of years, is how much the galaxy's distance has grown.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A velocity–time graph has time on the horizontal axis and velocity on the vertical axis; its height is how fast and in which direction, not where.
- Above the axis: moving forwards; below: moving backwards; crossing the axis: stopping for an instant and turning around.
- The slope is the acceleration, $a = \Delta v / \Delta t$; constant acceleration gives a straight line.
- The area between the graph and the time axis is the displacement, with area below the axis negative; the distance adds all areas as positive.
- For constant acceleration the trapezium gives $\Delta x = \tfrac12(v_i + v_f)\,t = v_i\,t + \tfrac12 a t^{2}$, the starting point for lesson 1.6.
- Sketch a position–time graph from a velocity–time graph by its slope and the area so far; go back by reading slopes.
