Lesson 1.2 described how fast and in which direction something moves: its velocity. But velocities rarely stay the same. A car pulls away from traffic lights, a cyclist brakes before a corner, a stone falls faster and faster. **Acceleration** describes how quickly the velocity changes. It is the third and last of the basic quantities of motion, after position and velocity, and it is the one that forces act on directly, as Block 2 will show.

## Average acceleration

Suppose an object has velocity $\vec v_i$ at time $t_i$ and velocity $\vec v_f$ at a later time $t_f$. The change in velocity is $\Delta \vec v = \vec v_f - \vec v_i$ (final minus initial, the same pattern as the displacement in lesson 1.1), and the time taken is $\Delta t = t_f - t_i$. The **average acceleration** is the change in velocity divided by the time taken:

<Formula label="Average acceleration" tex="\vec a_\text{ave} = \frac{\Delta \vec v}{\Delta t} = \frac{\vec v_f - \vec v_i}{t_f - t_i}" cite="mit" at="21.4, Average acceleration" />

Velocity is the rate of change of position; acceleration is the **rate of change of velocity**: how many metres per second the velocity changes by in each second. Its unit is therefore metres per second, per second:

<Formula label="The unit of acceleration" tex="1\ \frac{\text{m/s}}{\text{s}} = 1\ \text{m/s}^2" cite="mit" at="21.4, Average acceleration (unit metre per second squared)" />

An acceleration of 2 m/s² means that the velocity changes by 2 m/s every second.

<WorkedExample title="A tram pulling away">
<Problem>
A tram leaves a stop slowly, and on a straight stretch its velocity grows from 3.0 m/s to 15 m/s in 6.0 s. What is its average acceleration?
</Problem>
<Step title="Change in velocity and time taken">
Take the direction of travel as positive. $\Delta v = 15\ \text{m/s} - 3.0\ \text{m/s} = 12$ m/s and $\Delta t = 6.0$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Divide">
$a_\text{ave} = \dfrac{12\ \text{m/s}}{6.0\ \text{s}} = 2.0\ \text{m/s}^2$, in the direction of travel.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Unit: (m/s)/s = m/s², as it should be. Second by second, the velocity would go 3.0, 5.0, 7.0, 9.0, 11, 13 and 15 m/s: six steps of 2.0 m/s, which is indeed 12 m/s in 6.0 s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Notice what acceleration does **not** tell you. It says how the velocity changes, not how large the velocity is or which way the object is moving. A plane cruising steadily at 250 m/s has zero acceleration; a ball at the top of its flight has zero velocity for an instant but is still accelerating, because its velocity is changing from upwards to downwards.

## The sign of the acceleration

Acceleration is a vector, like velocity. Along a straight line, as in lesson 1.2, a sign gives its direction once you have chosen which way is positive. The sign of $a$ is the sign of $\Delta v$: positive when the velocity becomes more positive, negative when it becomes more negative.

Whether the object **speeds up** or **slows down** depends on the signs of the velocity and the acceleration together:

| velocity $v$ | acceleration $a$ | the object |
|---|---|---|
| positive | positive | speeds up (in the positive direction) |
| positive | negative | slows down |
| negative | negative | speeds up (in the negative direction) |
| negative | positive | slows down |

In short: **same sign, speeding up; opposite signs, slowing down.**

This is why physicists are careful with the everyday word **deceleration**. People use it for "slowing down", but a negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down: the third row of the table speeds up with a negative acceleration. Say "slowing down" when that is what you mean, and give the acceleration with its sign.

<WorkedExample title="A train braking while heading west">
<Problem>
Take east as the positive direction. A train is travelling west at 20 m/s and brakes steadily, so that 4.0 s later it travels west at 8.0 m/s. Find its acceleration and say whether it is speeding up or slowing down.
</Problem>
<Step title="Velocities with signs">
West is negative, so $v_i = -20$ m/s and $v_f = -8.0$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Acceleration">
$a = \dfrac{v_f - v_i}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{-8.0 - (-20)}{4.0}\ \text{m/s}^2 = \dfrac{12}{4.0}\ \text{m/s}^2 = +3.0\ \text{m/s}^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Speeding up or slowing down?">
The velocity is negative and the acceleration positive: opposite signs, so the train slows down, even though its acceleration is positive. The acceleration points east, against the motion.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The speed drops from 20 m/s to 8.0 m/s, so the train is indeed slowing down. Had we taken west as positive, both velocities would be positive and the acceleration $-3.0$ m/s²: the signs depend on the choice of direction, the physics does not.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Changing direction.** In two dimensions the velocity can also change by turning. A car going round a bend at a steady speed still has a changing velocity vector, because its direction changes, so it is accelerating. Lesson 1.10 works this out for motion in a circle.

## Constant acceleration

In many situations the velocity changes by the **same amount every second**, as for the tram above. The acceleration is then **constant**, and the average acceleration over any interval is the same number. Many school problems use constant acceleration; so does the first part of a car pulling away, and, to a good approximation, a falling stone.

With a constant acceleration $a$, the velocity after a time $\Delta t$ follows directly from the definition, by multiplying both sides by $\Delta t$ (lesson 0.4):

<Formula label="Velocity after a time Δt with constant acceleration" tex="v_f = v_i + a\,\Delta t" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 4.5, Eq. (4.4.2), with Δt for t" />

<WorkedExample title="A skateboarder on a slope">
<Problem>
A skateboarder rolls onto a straight downhill slope at 1.5 m/s and gains speed with a constant acceleration of 0.80 m/s² down the slope. How fast is she going 5.0 s later?
</Problem>
<Step title="Use the formula">
Take down the slope as positive. $v_f = 1.5\ \text{m/s} + 0.80\ \text{m/s}^2 \times 5.0\ \text{s} = 1.5 + 4.0 = 5.5$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: (m/s²) × s = m/s, which can be added to a velocity. Velocity and acceleration are both positive, so she should speed up, and 5.5 m/s is indeed more than 1.5 m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

A graph of velocity against time, a **velocity–time graph**, shows constant acceleration as a straight line: equal steps up (or down) in equal times. Its slope, rise over run, is $\Delta v / \Delta t$, the acceleration. Lesson 1.5 reads these graphs in full.

Under constant acceleration the *position* changes in a special way too. Lesson 1.6 shows that its position–time graph is a parabola. The same shape returns later: the path of a thrown ball (lesson 1.8) and of a charged particle in a uniform electric field (Block 6) are parabolas, because in each case the acceleration is constant.

## Acceleration at one instant

When the acceleration is not constant, the average over a long interval can hide a lot. As with velocity in lesson 1.3, we shrink the interval. The **instantaneous acceleration** is the average acceleration over a time interval that becomes shorter and shorter:

<Formula label="Instantaneous acceleration" tex="a(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{v(t + \Delta t) - v(t)}{\Delta t}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 4.4.2, Eq. (4.3.25)" />

Here $v(t)$ is the velocity at time $t$. On a velocity–time graph this is the slope of the **tangent** at that moment, just as the velocity is the slope of the tangent to a position–time graph. For constant acceleration the graph is straight, every tangent is the line itself, and the instantaneous and average accelerations are equal.

<WorkedExample title="A sprinter leaving the blocks">
<Problem>
A simple model of a sprinter's start gives her velocity in m/s as $v = \dfrac{11\,t}{t + 1.3}$, with $t$ in seconds after the start. Find her average acceleration over the first 2.0 s, and compare it with the slopes of the tangents at $t = 0$ and at $t = 2.0$ s, which are 8.5 m/s² and 1.3 m/s².
</Problem>
<Step title="Velocity after 2.0 s">
$v(2.0) = \dfrac{11 \times 2.0}{2.0 + 1.3} = \dfrac{22}{3.3} \approx 6.67$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Average acceleration">
She starts from rest, so $a_\text{ave} = \dfrac{6.67\ \text{m/s} - 0}{2.0\ \text{s}} \approx 3.3\ \text{m/s}^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
At the start her acceleration is large (8.5 m/s²); after 2.0 s it has dropped to 1.3 m/s², and it keeps falling as she nears her top speed. The average, 3.3 m/s², lies in between, and says nothing about the hard push out of the blocks.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Over a short interval the average approaches the tangent: $v(0.10) = \dfrac{1.1}{1.4} \approx 0.79$ m/s, so over the first 0.10 s the average acceleration is about 7.9 m/s², already close to 8.5 m/s².
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## How large are accelerations?

Some everyday values, worked out from the definition:

- **A car pulling away briskly** from 0 to 100 km/h in 10 s. In m/s, 100 km/h is $100 / 3.6 \approx 27.8$ m/s (lesson 1.2), so $a \approx 27.8 / 10 \approx 2.8$ m/s².
- **A car making an emergency stop** from 30 m/s to rest in 4.0 s: $a = (0 - 30)/4.0 = -7.5$ m/s², taking the direction of travel as positive.
- **A sprinter** leaving the blocks: in the model above, about 8.5 m/s² at the very start.
- **A falling stone**, when air resistance is small: its velocity grows by about 9.8 m/s every second. This acceleration of free fall near the Earth's surface is written $g$:

<Formula label="Acceleration of free fall" tex="g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 5.2 (value used in the projectile examples)" />

Lesson 1.7 is about free fall; from there on the High School uses the slightly more precise value $g = 9.81$ m/s². As a rough guide: a few m/s² is a comfortable ride, around $g$ is a hard brake or a sprint start, a few times $g$ is a rocket launch, and tens of $g$ is a crash.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
The universe is expanding: distant galaxies move away from us, and the farther they are, the faster they recede. For most of the twentieth century astronomers expected gravity to make that expansion slow down, a negative acceleration in the language of this lesson. In 1998 two teams measuring very distant exploding stars (supernovae) found the opposite: the expansion is speeding up, so the rate at which galaxies move apart is growing today. MIT's cosmology course tells this story in [8.286 Lecture 18](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-286-the-early-universe-fall-2013/fe429f4326945aa8e12f2eaf56219446_MKPswx4hjec.pdf) (transcript pp. 16 to 17). What drives the acceleration, called dark energy, is still one of the big open questions of physics.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity. Average acceleration: $\vec a_\text{ave} = \Delta \vec v / \Delta t$, a vector, in m/s².
- Along a line its sign gives the direction. Same sign as the velocity: speeding up. Opposite sign: slowing down. Avoid "deceleration" for a negative acceleration.
- Acceleration says how the velocity changes, not how large it is: an object can have zero velocity and still accelerate.
- A change of direction at constant speed is also an acceleration.
- Constant acceleration: the velocity changes by the same amount every second, $v_f = v_i + a\,\Delta t$, and the velocity–time graph is a straight line whose slope is the acceleration.
- The instantaneous acceleration is the slope of the tangent to the velocity–time graph.
- Typical sizes: a car pulling away, a few m/s²; free fall, $g \approx 9.8$ m/s².
