Lessons 1.1 and 1.2 described motion with numbers: a displacement, a velocity, a time. A **position–time graph** puts a whole trip into one picture. Time runs along the horizontal axis, the position along the vertical axis, and every point on the line says where the object was at that moment. Once you can read such a graph you can see at a glance when something stood still, when it moved fast or slowly, and when it turned back. This lesson shows how to read the graph, how to find the velocity from it, and how to go from a description in words to a graph and back again.

## What the graph shows

Motion along a straight line needs a **reference point** (the origin, where $x = 0$) and a choice of which direction counts as positive, as in lesson 1.1. The position $x$ is then a number with a sign: $x = 30$ m means 30 m from the origin in the positive direction, $x = -5$ m means 5 m from it the other way.

To draw the graph, put time $t$ on the horizontal axis and the position $x$ on the vertical axis. Each moment gets one point. Two things are worth remembering from the start:

- The graph is **not a picture of the path**. An object that walks out along a street and back again moves along one line, but its position–time graph goes up and then down, because time keeps running to the right.
- Time only goes forwards, so the graph never runs backwards or straight up. A vertical piece would mean being in two places at the same moment, or covering a distance in no time at all.

Some school experiments plot a **distance–time graph** instead, with the distance travelled so far on the vertical axis. That graph never goes down, even when the object turns back, because distance only adds up. A position–time graph does go down when the object moves in the negative direction, and that is what makes it show the direction as well as the speed.

## Standing still

The simplest motion is no motion. An object at rest keeps the same position, so its graph is a **horizontal line**. The height of the line tells you where the object is; the line being flat tells you it is not moving. A horizontal line has slope zero (lesson 0.5), and that zero is the velocity.

## Constant velocity: a straight line

An object moving at a constant velocity covers equal displacements in equal times, so its graph is a straight, sloping line. Its slope (rise over run: how far the position changed, divided by how long that took) is exactly the velocity of lesson 1.2:

<Formula label="Velocity as the slope of a position–time graph" tex="v = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t} = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{t_2 - t_1}" cite="mit" at="21.6, Motion at constant velocity" />

Here $(t_1, x_1)$ and $(t_2, x_2)$ are any two points on the line. This is lesson 1.2's $\Delta x / \Delta t$ with the start and end points numbered 1 and 2; because the motion is along one line, the velocity is written as a number with a sign instead of with a vector arrow. The units of the slope are those of the vertical axis divided by those of the horizontal axis: metres per second.

<WorkedExample title="A cyclist on a straight road">
<Problem>
A cyclist passes a road sign. Taking the sign as the origin and the direction of travel as positive, her position–time graph is a straight line from $x = 20$ m at $t = 0$ to $x = 110$ m at $t = 15$ s. What is her velocity?
</Problem>
<Step title="Rise and run">
Rise: $\Delta x = 110\ \text{m} - 20\ \text{m} = 90$ m. Run: $\Delta t = 15\ \text{s} - 0 = 15$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Slope">
$v = \dfrac{90\ \text{m}}{15\ \text{s}} = 6.0$ m/s, in the positive direction.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The units are m/s, as a velocity should be. A speed of 6.0 m/s is $6.0 \times 3.6 \approx 22$ km/h, a normal speed for a bicycle.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

A **steeper** line means a larger velocity: more metres in each second. A gentler line means a slower object.

**A negative slope.** When the line goes down, the position decreases: the object moves in the negative direction: towards the origin while $x$ is positive, and away from it on the other side once $x$ is negative. The slope, and so the velocity, is then negative. The sign of the slope gives the direction; its size gives the speed.

<WorkedExample title="Reading the walk">
<Problem>
Home is the origin and the direction of the post box is positive. Find the velocity in each part of the walk, then the displacement and the distance for the whole trip.
</Problem>
<Step title="Out to the post box (0 to 40 s)">
A straight rising line: $v = \dfrac{60\ \text{m} - 0}{40\ \text{s} - 0} = 1.5$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="At the post box (40 to 70 s)">
A horizontal line at 60 m: the walker stands still, $v = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Back home (70 to 100 s)">
A falling straight line: $v = \dfrac{0 - 60\ \text{m}}{100\ \text{s} - 70\ \text{s}} = -2.0$ m/s. The minus sign says she walks back towards home; the steeper line says she walks faster than on the way out.
</Step>
<Step title="Whole trip">
She ends where she started, so the displacement is zero. The distance walked is $60 + 60 = 120$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Velocity × time for each moving part gives back the change in position: $1.5 \times 40 = 60$ m out and $-2.0 \times 30 = -60$ m back, adding up to the zero displacement.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Changing velocity: a curved graph

If the velocity changes, the slope changes, and the graph **curves**. You can read the shape:

- a graph that gets **steeper** shows an object **speeding up**;
- a graph that gets **flatter** shows an object **slowing down**; where it becomes horizontal, the object has stopped, at least for an instant.

The rate at which the velocity changes is called the acceleration, the subject of lesson 1.4.

A curved graph has no single slope. Its velocity at one moment, the **instantaneous velocity** (the velocity over a very short time interval, $\Delta t$ close to zero), is the slope of the **tangent**: the straight line that just touches the curve at that moment, as in lesson 0.10. In symbols, it is the limit of the slope over shorter and shorter intervals:

<Formula label="Instantaneous velocity" tex="v(t) = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{x(t + \Delta t) - x(t)}{\Delta t}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 4.3.3, Eq. (4.3.5)" />

Here $x(t)$ (read "$x$ of $t$") is the position at time $t$.

<WorkedExample title="A car braking">
<Problem>
A car brakes on a straight road. Its position in metres, measured from where the driver starts braking, is $x = 20\,t - 2.0\,t^{2}$ with $t$ in seconds, from $t = 0$ until it stops at $t = 5.0$ s. Use a tangent to find its velocity at $t = 2.0$ s.
</Problem>
<Step title="The point on the graph">
$x(2.0) = 20 \times 2.0 - 2.0 \times 2.0^{2} = 32$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Read the tangent">
Draw the tangent at $(2.0\ \text{s}, 32\ \text{m})$ and read two points on it that are far apart, so that reading errors matter little. This tangent passes through $(0, 8\ \text{m})$ and $(4.0\ \text{s}, 56\ \text{m})$. Its slope is $\dfrac{56 - 8}{4.0 - 0} = 12$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with a short interval">
Without drawing: $x(1.9) = 30.78$ m and $x(2.1) = 33.18$ m, so over these 0.20 s the average velocity is $\dfrac{2.40}{0.20} = 12.0$ m/s. The short interval agrees with the tangent.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Average velocity: the slope of a secant

Over a longer interval, the **average velocity** is the displacement divided by the time taken. On the graph that is the slope of the **secant**, the straight line joining the point at the start of the interval to the point at its end:

<Formula label="Average velocity" tex="v_\text{ave} = \frac{x(t_2) - x(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 4.3.3, Eq. (4.3.4)" />

For the braking car between $t_1 = 1.0$ s and $t_2 = 4.0$ s: $x(1.0) = 18$ m and $x(4.0) = 48$ m, so

$v_\text{ave} = \dfrac{48 - 18}{4.0 - 1.0} = 10$ m/s.

The car went faster than 10 m/s at the start of the interval and slower at the end; the average lies in between. Somewhere inside the interval the tangent is parallel to the secant, and at that moment (here $t = 2.5$ s) the instantaneous velocity equals the average. (This is the mean value theorem; for a graph shaped like this one, a parabola, that moment always lies exactly halfway through the interval, as MIT's notes show in Example 4.2 of Sec. 4.3.)

## From words to a graph, and back

To draw a position–time graph from a description:

1. Choose the origin and the positive direction.
2. Split the motion into stages: standing still, moving steadily, speeding up or slowing down.
3. For each stage draw the matching shape: horizontal, a straight line with slope equal to the velocity, or a curve that steepens or flattens.
4. Join the stages without gaps: an object cannot jump from one place to another.

Reading a graph is the same list backwards: split it into pieces, and for each piece say whether the object stands still, moves steadily (how fast and which way) or speeds up or slows down.

<WorkedExample title="A lift">
<Problem>
A lift waits at the ground floor for 5.0 s, rises at a steady 2.0 m/s for 6.0 s, waits 4.0 s at that floor and then goes down at a steady 3.0 m/s to the ground floor. Draw its position–time graph.
</Problem>
<Step title="Origin and direction">
Measure the height $x$ above the ground floor, with up positive.
</Step>
<Step title="Stages">
0 to 5.0 s: horizontal at $x = 0$. From 5.0 s: rising for 6.0 s, by $2.0 \times 6.0 = 12$ m, so the line climbs to 12 m at $t = 11$ s. 11 to 15 s: horizontal at 12 m. Then down at 3.0 m/s: covering 12 m takes $12 / 3.0 = 4.0$ s, so the line falls to 0 at $t = 19$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The falling line is steeper than the rising one, as it should be: 3.0 m/s down against 2.0 m/s up. The lift ends where it began, so its displacement is zero.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Astronomers measure how fast distant galaxies move away from us. Imagine a graph of one galaxy's distance against time. Its slope is the speed at which the galaxy recedes, and Hubble's law says that slope is larger for galaxies that are farther away. If every galaxy had always kept the same speed, each graph would be a straight line, and tracing all the lines back would bring them to zero distance at about the same moment in the past: a first estimate of the age of the universe. In reality the expansion slowed down and later sped up, so the graphs are curves. Only the slope at the present moment can be measured (from the light of the galaxies, not by watching them move), and cosmologists work out the shape of the rest of each curve from how the expansion is thought to have changed. The speed measured today is the slope of the tangent at "now", exactly as in this lesson.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A position–time graph has time on the horizontal axis and position on the vertical axis; it is not a picture of the path.
- At rest: a horizontal line. Constant velocity: a straight sloping line.
- The slope is the velocity, $v = \Delta x / \Delta t$; a negative slope means moving in the negative direction.
- A curved graph means a changing velocity: steeper is speeding up, flatter is slowing down.
- The slope of the tangent is the instantaneous velocity; the slope of a secant is the average velocity over that interval.
- To draw a graph from words, choose an origin and a direction, split the motion into stages and draw each stage's shape.
