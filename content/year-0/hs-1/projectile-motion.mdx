Kick a football, throw a stone across a pond or squirt water from a garden hose: each of them flies along a curved path and comes back down. An object that moves only under the pull of gravity after it has been launched is called a **projectile**, and its path through the air is its **trajectory**. Lesson 1.7 treated objects that move straight up or down. This lesson adds a sideways motion, and finds that the two simply sit side by side. We ignore the push of the air (**air resistance**) until the end of the lesson: for a heavy, compact object that is not too fast, it hardly matters.

## Two motions at once

Set up axes as in lesson 0.8: $x$ along the ground in the direction of the throw, and $y$ straight up, with the origin where the object leaves your hand. Gravity pulls only downwards. So:

- **horizontally** nothing pushes or pulls: the horizontal velocity $v_x$ stays the same during the whole flight, as for the steady motion of lesson 1.2;
- **vertically** the object is in free fall (lesson 1.7): its vertical velocity $v_y$ changes by the same amount every second, with acceleration $g = 9.81$ m/s² downwards.

<Formula label="The acceleration of a projectile" tex="a_x = 0, \qquad a_y = -g" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.2, Eqs. (5.1.14) and (5.1.15)" />

The minus sign says the acceleration points down, because we chose up as positive. Near the Earth's surface $g$ is the same for every object, light or heavy (MIT notes, Sec. 5.2, just after Eqs. (5.1.14) and (5.1.15)). It is also the same whatever the direction or size of the velocity (reading 1, "Motion in a gravitational field").

The key idea of this lesson is that the two motions are **independent**: the sideways motion does not change the falling, and the falling does not change the sideways motion. They share only the clock. A simple test shows it. Push one coin off the edge of a table so that it flies off sideways, and at the same moment let a second coin drop straight down from the edge. You hear them land at the same moment: the sideways speed of the first coin did nothing to its fall.

## Splitting the launch velocity

A projectile is usually launched with a **speed** $v_0$ at an angle $\theta$ above the horizontal. Lesson 0.8 showed how to split such a vector into components with the sine and cosine of lesson 0.7: the horizontal side lies next to the angle, the vertical side opposite it.

<Formula label="Components of the launch velocity" tex="v_x = v_0\cos\theta, \qquad v_y = v_0\sin\theta \ \ \text{(at launch)}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.2, Eqs. (5.1.5) and (5.1.6)" />

A steep throw puts most of the speed into the vertical component and makes the projectile go high; a flat throw puts most into the horizontal component.

## Position and velocity at any moment

Now treat each direction with the tools you already have. Horizontally the velocity is constant, so the distance grows steadily with time. Vertically the acceleration is the constant $-g$, so the equations of lesson 1.6 apply with $a = -g$ and the initial velocity $v_0 \sin\theta$. With the clock started at launch:

<Formula label="Position of a projectile" tex="x = v_0\cos\theta\;t, \qquad y = v_0\sin\theta\;t - \tfrac{1}{2}\,g\,t^{2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.2, Eq. (5.1.16), with the launch point as origin" />

<Formula label="Velocity of a projectile" tex="v_x = v_0\cos\theta, \qquad v_y = v_0\sin\theta - g\,t" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.2, Eq. (5.1.17)" />

At any moment the velocity is the vector with these two components. Its size, the speed, follows from Pythagoras, $v = \sqrt{v_x^{2} + v_y^{2}}$ (lesson 0.8), and it always points along the trajectory.

## The highest point

On the way up $v_y$ shrinks; on the way down it grows again, now negative. At the top it passes through zero for an instant. The horizontal velocity does not: at the top the projectile still moves sideways at $v_x$, so its velocity there is horizontal, never zero (unless it was thrown straight up). Setting $v_y = 0$ gives the time to the top:

<Formula label="Time to the highest point" tex="t_{\text{top}} = \frac{v_0\sin\theta}{g}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.2, Example 5.1, Eqs. (5.1.21) and (5.1.22)" />

Putting this time into $y$ gives the greatest height above the launch point. Use $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2a\,\Delta x$ from lesson 1.6 for the vertical motion, with the vertical displacement $\Delta y$ in place of $\Delta x$, $v_f = 0$, $v_i = v_0 \sin\theta$ and $a = -g$, and you get the same result more quickly:

<Formula label="Maximum height above the launch point" tex="y_{\text{max}} = \frac{v_0^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{2g}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.2, Example 5.1, Eq. (5.1.23) (there with a launch height d added)" />

Here $\sin^{2}\theta$ means $(\sin\theta)^{2}$.

## Time of flight and range on level ground

When the projectile lands at the height it was launched from, $y = 0$ again. Setting $y = 0$ in the position formula gives $t\,(v_0\sin\theta - \tfrac{1}{2} g t) = 0$. One answer is $t = 0$, the launch; the other is the **time of flight**:

<Formula label="Time of flight on level ground" tex="t_{\text{flight}} = \frac{2\,v_0\sin\theta}{g}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.2, Eq. (5.1.16) with y = 0 (derived here)" />

That is exactly twice the time to the top: on level ground the way down takes as long as the way up, and the projectile lands with the same speed it was launched with, at the same angle below the horizontal.

The **range** $R$ is the horizontal distance to the landing point: the steady horizontal velocity times the time of flight, $R = v_0\cos\theta \times 2 v_0\sin\theta / g$. A rule from trigonometry, $2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin 2\theta$ (reading 2 shows where it comes from), makes this shorter:

<Formula label="Range on level ground" tex="R = \frac{v_0^{2}\sin 2\theta}{g}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.2, Eq. (5.1.16) with y = 0, combined with sin 2θ = 2 sin θ cos θ (reading 2, Sec. 4.3, Derivation of sin 2α); the range formula is derived here" />

Two things follow:

- $\sin 2\theta$ is largest, equal to 1, when $2\theta = 90^{\circ}$. So **without air resistance the range is greatest at 45°**, and then $R = v_0^{2}/g$.
- Two angles that add up to 90°, such as 30° and 60°, give the **same range**: their values of $2\theta$, 60° and 120°, have the same sine. The reason is the unit circle of lesson 0.7: the points at an angle $\alpha$ and at $180^{\circ} - \alpha$ are mirror images in the $y$-axis, so they are at the same height and have the same sine, $\sin(180^{\circ} - \alpha) = \sin\alpha$. The steeper throw goes higher and stays up longer, but moves sideways more slowly.

The range grows with the **square** of the launch speed: twice as fast lands four times as far away, just as the braking distance of lesson 1.6 grew with the square of the speed.

<WorkedExample title="A free kick">
<Problem>
A footballer kicks a ball from the ground at 18 m/s, 35° above the horizontal. Ignore air resistance. How long is the ball in the air, how high does it go, and where does it land?
</Problem>
<Step title="Components">
$v_x = 18\cos 35^{\circ} = 14.7$ m/s and, at launch, $v_y = 18\sin 35^{\circ} = 10.3$ m/s. Up is positive, $g = 9.81$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="The highest point">
$t_{\text{top}} = 10.3 / 9.81 = 1.05$ s. The height is $y_{\text{max}} = \dfrac{10.32^{2}}{2 \times 9.81} = 5.43$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Time of flight and range">
On level ground the flight lasts twice as long: $t_{\text{flight}} = 2 \times 1.052 = 2.10$ s. In that time the ball moves sideways $R = 14.74 \times 2.105 = 31.0$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with the range formula">
$R = \dfrac{18^{2} \times \sin 70^{\circ}}{9.81} = \dfrac{324 \times 0.940}{9.81} = 31.0$ m, the same. Units: (m/s)² divided by m/s² gives m. The size is sensible for a long kick.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The same numbers tell you where the ball is at any moment. After 1.5 s it is past the top: $v_y = 10.32 - 9.81 \times 1.5 = -4.39$ m/s (falling), at a height $y = 10.32 \times 1.5 - \tfrac{1}{2} \times 9.81 \times 1.5^{2} = 4.45$ m and $x = 14.74 \times 1.5 = 22.1$ m from the kick.

## The path is a parabola

The position formulas give $x$ and $y$ separately, both in terms of $t$. To find the shape of the path, remove $t$. From the horizontal motion, $t = x / (v_0\cos\theta)$. Put that into $y$:

<Formula label="The trajectory" tex="y = x\tan\theta - \frac{g}{2\,v_0^{2}\cos^{2}\theta}\,x^{2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.2.1, Orbit equation, Eq. (5.1.33), with Eqs. (5.1.5) and (5.1.6) put in" />

Here we used $\sin\theta / \cos\theta = \tan\theta$ (lesson 0.7). The height is a quadratic function of the horizontal distance, with a negative number in front of $x^{2}$: the graph is a **parabola** opening downwards (lesson 0.6). Near the launch the $x\tan\theta$ term wins and the path rises in the launch direction; further on the $x^{2}$ term takes over and pulls it down.

Why a parabola? Along $x$ the motion is steady, so $x$ is simply proportional to $t$: the horizontal axis is just a stretched clock. Along $y$ the acceleration is constant, and lesson 1.6 showed that constant acceleration makes the position quadratic in time. So the picture of $y$ against $x$ has the same shape as the parabola of $y$ against $t$. **Any constant acceleration** gives a parabola in this way, as long as the starting velocity is not parallel to it. In Block 6 you will meet an electron that enters the space between two charged plates: there the electric field gives it a constant sideways acceleration, and it bends along a parabola for exactly the same reason as a thrown ball.

## Launched horizontally

A special case: the projectile leaves a height $h$ moving horizontally, like a ball rolling off a table or a stone thrown flat from a cliff. Then $\theta = 0$: the horizontal velocity is the launch speed, and the vertical velocity starts at zero. The fall is the same as for an object simply dropped from that height (lesson 1.7): $h = \tfrac{1}{2} g t^{2}$. So:

<Formula label="Horizontal launch from a height h" tex="t = \sqrt{\frac{2h}{g}}, \qquad x = v_0\,t" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.2, Eq. (5.1.16) with θ = 0 and a launch height (the notes' y₀)" />

The time to land depends only on the height, never on the launch speed; a faster throw just lands further away.

<WorkedExample title="A stone from a cliff">
<Problem>
You throw a stone horizontally at 12 m/s from the top of a cliff 20 m above the sea. Ignore air resistance. How long does it take to reach the water, how far from the foot of the cliff does it land, and how fast is it moving then?
</Problem>
<Step title="Time of fall">
Only the vertical motion matters for the time: $t = \sqrt{2 \times 20 / 9.81} = 2.02$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Distance from the cliff">
The horizontal velocity stays 12 m/s, so $x = 12 \times 2.019 = 24.2$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Velocity on landing">
Horizontally still 12 m/s; vertically $9.81 \times 2.019 = 19.8$ m/s downwards. The speed is $\sqrt{12^{2} + 19.81^{2}} = 23.2$ m/s, and the stone hits the water at $\tan^{-1}(19.81 / 12) = 58.8^{\circ}$ below the horizontal (lesson 0.8, direction from components).
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
Lesson 1.6's $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2a\,\Delta x$ for the vertical motion alone, with $\Delta y$ in place of $\Delta x$, gives $v_y = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 20} = 19.8$ m/s, the same. Units: $\sqrt{\text{m}/(\text{m/s}^2)}$ is s. A dropped stone would take the same 2.02 s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Launched at an angle from a height

Often a projectile is launched at an angle from above the place where it lands: a shot put leaves the hand about 2 m up, a stone is thrown upwards from a roof. Then the flight is no longer symmetric, because the way down is longer than the way up. The method stays the same: write the height $y$ as a function of time, set it equal to the height of the landing point, and solve for $t$. That gives a quadratic equation, which the quadratic formula of lesson 0.6 solves. Of its two roots only the positive one is the landing; the negative one is a moment before the throw, when the stone was not flying yet.

<WorkedExample title="A stone from a roof">
<Problem>
You throw a stone from a flat roof 12 m above the ground at 15 m/s, 40° above the horizontal. Ignore air resistance. How long is the stone in the air, and how far from the wall does it land?
</Problem>
<Step title="Components">
$v_x = 15\cos 40^{\circ} = 11.5$ m/s and, at launch, $v_y = 15\sin 40^{\circ} = 9.64$ m/s. Up is positive and the origin is where the stone leaves your hand, so the ground is at $y = -12$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="The equation for the landing">
Set the height equal to that of the ground: $9.64\,t - \tfrac{1}{2} \times 9.81\,t^{2} = -12$, or $4.905\,t^{2} - 9.64\,t - 12 = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve it">
The quadratic formula gives $t = \dfrac{9.64 \pm \sqrt{9.64^{2} + 4 \times 4.905 \times 12}}{2 \times 4.905} = \dfrac{9.64 \pm 18.1}{9.81}$. The positive root is $t = 2.83$ s; the other, $-0.86$ s, lies before the throw. In 2.83 s the stone moves sideways $x = 11.49 \times 2.830 = 32.5$ m from the wall.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
On landing the vertical velocity is $9.64 - 9.81 \times 2.830 = -18.1$ m/s. Lesson 1.6's rule with $\Delta y = -12$ m gives the same size by another route: $\sqrt{9.64^{2} + 2 \times 9.81 \times 12} = 18.1$ m/s. The flight also takes longer than the 1.97 s it would last on level ground ($2 \times 9.64 / 9.81$), as it should, because the stone falls 12 m further.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Air resistance

Real projectiles push air out of the way, and the air pushes back against the motion. This **air resistance** grows with speed. It slows the horizontal motion as well as the vertical one, so a real ball:

- does not go as far or as high as the formulas say;
- has an **unsymmetrical** path: it rises along a long slope and comes down more steeply, closer to the vertical;
- reaches its greatest range at an angle a little **below** 45°.

For a heavy shot put the difference is small; for a table-tennis ball or a shuttlecock it is huge. Spin can also make the air lift or swerve a ball, as in a curling free kick. The MIT notes show a computed example of how much shorter the real path is (Sec. 5.2, Figure 5.1). Taking air resistance into account needs forces (Block 2) and, in general, a computer.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
The parabola is an approximation: it assumes that gravity is the same everywhere and always points the same way. That is true over a football pitch, not over a whole planet. Newton imagined a cannon on a high mountain firing faster and faster: each ball lands further away, because the curved ground drops away beneath it, until at about 8 km/s (ignoring the air) it never lands at all and circles the Earth. A projectile and an orbiting Moon are the same kind of motion; the orbit is what you get when the trajectory is long enough for the direction of gravity to change. ## Summary

- A projectile moves under gravity alone. Its horizontal and vertical motions are independent and share only the time.
- Horizontally the velocity is constant, $v_x = v_0\cos\theta$. Vertically the acceleration is $g = 9.81$ m/s² downwards, with $v_y = v_0\sin\theta$ at launch.
- At the top $v_y = 0$: $t_{\text{top}} = v_0\sin\theta / g$ and $y_{\text{max}} = v_0^{2}\sin^{2}\theta / (2g)$. The velocity there is horizontal; the acceleration is still $g$ downwards.
- On level ground $t_{\text{flight}} = 2v_0\sin\theta / g$ and the range is $R = v_0^{2}\sin 2\theta / g$: largest at 45° without air resistance, and equal for angles that add up to 90°.
- Launched horizontally from a height $h$, the time of fall is $\sqrt{2h/g}$, whatever the launch speed.
- Eliminating $t$ gives a height that is quadratic in $x$: the path is a parabola. Any constant acceleration gives one, including a charge in a uniform electric field (Block 6).
- Air resistance shortens and lowers the path, makes it steeper on the way down and moves the best angle below 45°.
