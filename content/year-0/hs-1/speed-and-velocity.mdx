Lesson 1.1 described *where* something is and how far it has moved. This lesson asks *how fast*. There are two answers to that question, and they are not the same: **speed** says only how quickly ground is covered, while **velocity** also says in which direction. The difference is exactly the difference between distance and displacement.

## Average speed

The **average speed** of a journey is the total distance travelled divided by the time it took:

<Formula label="Average speed" tex="\text{average speed} = \frac{\text{distance travelled}}{\text{time taken}}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 21.3, Average speed" />

Distance and time are both scalars (numbers without a direction, lesson 0.8), so speed is a scalar too. It is never negative. Its SI unit is the metre per second, m/s. The word "average" matters: on the way you may have stopped, sped up or slowed down, and the average speed says nothing about that. It only tells you the steady speed that would have covered the same distance in the same time.

## Average velocity

The **average velocity** is the displacement divided by the time it took. Recall from lesson 1.1 that the displacement is the change in position (final position minus initial position), written here with arrows as $\Delta \vec x = \vec x_f - \vec x_i$ to show that it is a vector: an arrow from where you started to where you ended. The time taken is $\Delta t = t_f - t_i$. Then

<Formula label="Average velocity" tex="\vec v_\text{ave} = \frac{\Delta \vec x}{\Delta t}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 21.3, Average velocity" />

Dividing a vector by a positive number (a time) changes its size but not its direction, so the average velocity is a vector that points the same way as the displacement. Velocity is the **rate of change of position**: how much the position changes per second.

Because distance and displacement can be very different, so can average speed and average velocity. A round trip is the extreme case.

<WorkedExample title="A train there and back">
<Problem>
A train runs along a straight line from one town to the next, 36 km away, in 30 min. It waits at the station for 20 min, then returns along the same track in 40 min. Find the average speed and the average velocity for the whole trip, and the velocity of each leg. Take the direction of the outward trip as positive.
</Problem>
<Step title="Distance and time">
The train covers 36 km out and 36 km back: a distance of 72 km. The trip takes $30 + 20 + 40 = 90$ min, which is 1.5 h or 5400 s.
</Step>
<Step title="Average speed">
Average speed $= \dfrac{72\ \text{km}}{1.5\ \text{h}} = 48$ km/h. In SI units: $\dfrac{72\,000\ \text{m}}{5400\ \text{s}} \approx 13$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Average velocity">
The train ends where it started, so its displacement is zero, and its average velocity for the whole trip is **zero**, although it was moving for more than an hour.
</Step>
<Step title="Each leg">
Outward: $\dfrac{+36\ \text{km}}{0.50\ \text{h}} = +72$ km/h, or $+20$ m/s. Back: $\dfrac{-36\ \text{km}}{40\ \text{min}} = \dfrac{-36\ \text{km}}{0.667\ \text{h}} = -54$ km/h, or $-15$ m/s. The return leg has a negative velocity because the train moves in the negative direction.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The units are a distance over a time in every line. The average speed (48 km/h) is lower than both leg speeds (54 and 72 km/h), as it should be, because it includes the 20 minutes of standing still.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## From km/h to m/s

Everyday speeds are given in kilometres per hour; physics uses metres per second. Lesson 0.2 showed how to change units by replacing each unit with its value: one kilometre is 1000 m and one hour is 3600 s, so

<Formula label="Kilometres per hour in metres per second" tex="1\ \text{km/h} = \frac{1000\ \text{m}}{3600\ \text{s}} = \frac{1}{3.6}\ \text{m/s}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 21.3, Worked example 1 (unit conversion) and Exercise 21.3 (km/h to m/s); the factor 1/3.6 is worked out here" />

So to go **from km/h to m/s, divide by 3.6**; to go back, multiply by 3.6. A sprinter running at 10 m/s moves at $10 \times 3.6 = 36$ km/h. A quick check: the number in m/s is always the smaller one, because a metre per second is the larger unit (it is 3.6 km/h).

## The sign of a velocity

Along a straight line, a vector has only two possible directions, so a sign is enough to give it. First choose which way is positive (for example east, or to the right, or up). Along a line we then write the velocity as a signed number $v$ without an arrow (strictly, it is the component $v_x$ of the velocity vector); it can be negative. In two dimensions, later in this lesson, $v$ without an arrow means the speed, the size of the vector, as in lesson 0.8. A **positive velocity** means moving in the positive direction and a **negative velocity** means moving the other way. The size of the velocity, without its sign, is the speed.

<WorkedExample title="A cyclist in two legs">
<Problem>
A cyclist rides 6.0 km east in 15 min, then turns and rides 2.0 km west in 10 min. Take east as positive. Find the velocity on each leg, and the average speed and average velocity for the whole ride, in m/s.
</Problem>
<Step title="Each leg">
East: $\dfrac{+6.0\ \text{km}}{0.25\ \text{h}} = +24$ km/h $\approx +6.7$ m/s. West: $\dfrac{-2.0\ \text{km}}{10\ \text{min}}$; ten minutes is $1/6$ h, so this is $-12$ km/h $\approx -3.3$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Whole ride: speed">
Distance $= 6.0 + 2.0 = 8.0$ km in $15 + 10 = 25$ min. Average speed $= \dfrac{8.0\ \text{km}}{25\ \text{min}} = 19.2$ km/h $\approx 5.3$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Whole ride: velocity">
Displacement $= +6.0 + (-2.0) = +4.0$ km, so the average velocity is $\dfrac{+4.0\ \text{km}}{25\ \text{min}} = +9.6$ km/h $\approx +2.7$ m/s, that is 2.7 m/s east.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The average velocity is smaller than the average speed, because part of the ride undid the rest. Notice also that it is **not** the plain average of the two leg velocities, $(24 + (-12))/2 = 6$ km/h: the cyclist spent longer on the first leg, so it counts for more.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Constant velocity

When the velocity stays the same all the time, the motion is called **uniform motion**, or motion at **constant velocity**: the position changes by the same amount every second. Then the average velocity over any part of the motion is that same constant $v$, and the definition $v = (x_f - x_i)/\Delta t$ can be rearranged to give the position after a time $\Delta t$:

<Formula label="Position at constant velocity" tex="x_f = x_i + v\,\Delta t" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 21.6, Motion at constant velocity (rearranged)" />

Here we write the one-dimensional position as a signed number $x$, as with the velocity. A tram that starts at $x = 50$ m and moves at a constant $+8.0$ m/s is at $50 + 8.0 \times 30 = 290$ m after 30 s. On a position–time graph, constant velocity gives a straight line whose slope is the velocity (lesson 0.5); lesson 1.3 studies these graphs in detail.

## Velocity at one instant

A car's speedometer does not show an average over the whole trip. It shows how fast the car is going *now*. This is the **instantaneous velocity**: the velocity at one moment in time, which can differ from the average velocity when the velocity changes along the way.

To find it, take the average velocity over a shorter and shorter time interval around that moment. This is the limit you met in lesson 0.10:

<Formula label="Instantaneous velocity" tex="v = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}" cite="reading3" at="Sec. 4.3.3, Eq. (4.3.5)" />

On a position–time graph it is the slope of the tangent at that moment. The **instantaneous speed** is the size of the instantaneous velocity, without its direction: this is what the speedometer shows.

Note the difference with averages. At each instant, speed is just the size of the velocity. Over a whole trip, though, the average speed is usually *larger* than the size of the average velocity, as the train and the cyclist showed. The two agree only for motion in one direction along a straight line.

## Velocity in two dimensions

Most motion is not along one line. A ball flies across a field, a ship crosses a bay. Here the components of lesson 0.8 do the work: we treat each direction separately, with its own displacement and its own velocity component.

<Formula label="Velocity components" tex="v_x = \frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}, \qquad v_y = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta t}" cite="reading4" at="Sec. 5.1, Eq. (5.1.2), applied to average velocities" />

The speed is then the size of the velocity vector, found with Pythagoras, and its direction with the tangent, exactly as for any vector in lesson 0.8.

<WorkedExample title="A drone">
<Problem>
A drone flies in a straight line at constant velocity. In 10 s it moves 36 m east and 48 m north. Take east as the $x$-direction and north as the $y$-direction. Find its velocity components, its speed and its direction.
</Problem>
<Step title="Components">
$v_x = \dfrac{36\ \text{m}}{10\ \text{s}} = 3.6$ m/s and $v_y = \dfrac{48\ \text{m}}{10\ \text{s}} = 4.8$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed">
$v = \sqrt{3.6^{2} + 4.8^{2}} = \sqrt{36} = 6.0$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Direction">
$\tan\theta = \dfrac{4.8}{3.6}$, so $\theta \approx 53°$ north of east. Both components are positive, so the drone flies into the north-east quarter, as the angle says.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The straight path is $\sqrt{36^{2} + 48^{2}} = 60$ m long, and $60\ \text{m} / 10\ \text{s} = 6.0$ m/s: the same speed found by another route.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Astronomers measure the velocities of galaxies, and in kilometres per second rather than metres per second, because they are so large. Hubble's law from lesson 0.11 gives the speed at which a galaxy moves away from us, with $d$ its distance (Guth uses the letter r for it):

<Formula label="Hubble's law" tex="v = H\,d" cite="guth4" at="slide 1" />

With the round example value $H$ = 70 km/s per megaparsec, a galaxy 100 megaparsecs away recedes at $70 \times 100 = 7000$ km/s, which is $7.0 \times 10^{6}$ m/s, about 2.3% of the speed of light. This is really a velocity, not only a speed: it points straight away from us, along the line from us to the galaxy. A galaxy at the same distance on the opposite side of the sky moves away just as fast in the opposite direction. In vector form, $\vec v = H\,\vec d$, with $\vec d$ the arrow from us to the galaxy. The cosmology course explains why this pattern means that all distances between galaxies grow, rather than that we sit at a centre.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Average speed = distance / time: a scalar, never negative.
- Average velocity = displacement / time, $\vec v_\text{ave} = \Delta \vec x / \Delta t$: a vector with the direction of the displacement. A round trip has zero average velocity.
- To convert km/h to m/s divide by 3.6; to convert back, multiply by 3.6.
- Along a line, the sign of the velocity gives its direction; its size is the speed.
- At constant velocity $x_f = x_i + v\,\Delta t$.
- The instantaneous velocity is the limit of the average velocity as the time interval shrinks to zero; its size is the instantaneous speed.
- In two dimensions, find the components $v_x$ and $v_y$ separately; the speed is $\sqrt{v_x^{2} + v_y^{2}}$.
