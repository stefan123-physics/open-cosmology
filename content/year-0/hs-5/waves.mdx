Drop a stone into a pond and rings spread out across the water. Flick one end of a long rope and a bump runs along it to the other end. In both cases something travels, but not the water or the rope: a floating leaf only bobs up and down as the ripples pass. What travels is a disturbance, carrying energy from one place to another. That is a **wave**. This lesson is about **mechanical waves**, which need a material to travel through; light, in lesson 5.8, is a wave that can also cross empty space.

## Pulses and the medium

The material a disturbance moves through is called the **medium**: the water, the rope, the air. A single disturbance, such as one flick of the rope, is a **pulse**. As it passes, each piece of the medium is pushed away from its rest position and comes back, but the medium as a whole stays where it is. The pulse needs the pieces of the medium to pull or push on their neighbours: that is how the disturbance is handed on.

A **wave** is a regular, continuing train of pulses, made by a source that keeps vibrating, like a hand moving a rope up and down in simple harmonic motion. Then every piece of the medium oscillates in the same way, each one a little later than the one before it.

## Transverse and longitudinal waves

There are two basic kinds of wave, depending on how the medium moves compared with the direction the wave travels.

- In a **transverse wave** the medium moves at right angles to the direction of travel. A wave on a rope or a guitar string is transverse. Its highest points are **crests** and its lowest points are **troughs**.
- In a **longitudinal wave** the medium moves back and forth along the direction of travel. Push and pull the end of a stretched slinky and you see regions where the coils are bunched together, **compressions**, and regions where they are spread out, **rarefactions**. Sound in air is a longitudinal wave of compressions and rarefactions.

Ripples on water are usually treated as transverse waves: the surface moves up and down as they pass.

## Amplitude, wavelength, period, frequency

Picture a transverse wave on a rope at one instant, like a photograph.

- The **amplitude** $A$ is the largest displacement of the medium from its rest position: the height of a crest, or the depth of a trough.
- The **wavelength** $\lambda$ (lambda) is the length of one complete wave: the distance between two neighbouring crests, or two troughs, or two compressions. More generally it is the distance between any two neighbouring points that are **in phase**, points that move in exactly the same way at the same moment.

Now watch one point of the rope as the wave goes by. It oscillates in simple harmonic motion, with the same **period** $T$ and **frequency** $f = 1/T$ as the source that makes the wave.

The plot shows the rope at time $t$. Here $x$ is the position along the rope and $y$ is how far the rope is displaced up or down at that position (in lesson 5.1, $x$ was the displacement of a single mass; along a rope we need both). Move the time slider and the whole pattern slides along, while each point of the rope only moves up and down.

## The wave equation

In one period, each point of the medium makes one complete oscillation, and in that same time the wave pattern moves on by exactly one wavelength: the crest that was here is replaced by the next one. Speed is distance over time, so the **wave speed** is

<Formula label="Wave speed" tex="v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = f\lambda" cite="mit" at="Sec. 8.7, the wave equation" />

School books often call this the **wave equation** (at university that name is used for a deeper equation that describes the whole wave). It connects three quantities, but they are not equally free:

- The **speed** is set by the medium: how stiff or tight it is and how heavy. A tighter rope carries waves faster, as the next lesson shows.
- The **frequency** is set by the source: how often it vibrates.
- The **wavelength** then follows: $\lambda = v/f$.

So a source that vibrates faster, in the same medium, makes shorter waves.

<WorkedExample title="Ripples in a tank">
<Problem>
A vibrating bar dips into a tank of water 12 times per second. The ripples it makes have crests $1.5\ \text{cm}$ apart. How fast do the ripples travel?
</Problem>
<Step title="Read off frequency and wavelength">
$f = 12\ \text{Hz}$ and $\lambda = 1.5\ \text{cm} = 0.015\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Use the wave equation">
$v = f\lambda = 12 \times 0.015 = 0.18\ \text{m/s}$: each crest moves 18 centimetres every second.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Ocean swell">
<Problem>
Out at sea, long waves called swell arrive at a buoy every $10\ \text{s}$, and their crests are $150\ \text{m}$ apart. Find their frequency and speed.
</Problem>
<Step title="Frequency">
$f = 1/T = 1/10 = 0.10\ \text{Hz}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed">
$v = \lambda/T = 150/10 = 15\ \text{m/s}$, about $54\ \text{km/h}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="The wavelength of a sound">
<Problem>
A tuning fork vibrates at $256\ \text{Hz}$. Sound travels through the air in the room at $340\ \text{m/s}$. What is the wavelength of the sound?
</Problem>
<Step title="Rearrange the wave equation">
$\lambda = v/f = 340/256 = 1.33\ \text{m}$. A higher note in the same air would have a shorter wavelength.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A mechanical wave is a travelling disturbance that carries energy through a **medium** without carrying the medium along. A single disturbance is a pulse; a wave is a regular train of pulses.
- In a **transverse** wave the medium moves at right angles to the direction of travel (crests and troughs); in a **longitudinal** wave it moves along it (compressions and rarefactions).
- The **wavelength** $\lambda$ is the distance between neighbouring points in phase; each point of the medium oscillates with the source's period $T$ and frequency $f$.
- The wave equation $v = f\lambda = \lambda/T$: the speed is set by the medium, the frequency by the source, and the wavelength follows.
