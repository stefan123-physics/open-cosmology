Hang a weight from a string and let it swing. Galileo noticed that a swinging lamp seems to keep the same rhythm whether it swings wide or narrow, and for three centuries the best clocks in the world were pendulum clocks. This lesson shows why the pendulum keeps time, and follows the energy of an oscillator as it moves.

## The pendulum is an oscillator too

A **simple pendulum** is a small heavy bob of mass $m$ on a light string of length $\ell$, swinging about a fixed pivot. Its position is given by the angle $\theta$ between the string and the vertical. At $\theta = 0$ the bob hangs straight down: that is its stable equilibrium.

When the bob is pulled aside, gravity has a part along the path that pulls it back towards the bottom. Measure the displacement along the arc: at angle $\theta$ (in radians) the bob has moved a distance $s = \ell\theta$ from the bottom. The part of the weight $mg$ along the path is $mg\sin\theta$, pointing back towards the bottom. That is a restoring force, but it is proportional to $\sin\theta$, not to the displacement itself, so it is not yet simple harmonic motion.

The step that makes it simple is the **small-angle approximation**. For a small angle measured in radians, $\sin\theta$ is almost equal to $\theta$ itself:

<Formula label="Small-angle approximation" tex="\sin\theta \approx \theta \qquad (\theta \text{ in radians, } |\theta| \ll 1)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (68)" />

At $10^\circ = 0.1745$ rad, $\sin\theta = 0.1736$: a difference of only 0.5%. Even at $30^\circ$ ($0.524$ rad, $\sin\theta = 0.500$) it is about 5%. With this approximation the restoring force is $mg\theta = (mg/\ell)\,s$: proportional to the displacement $s$, exactly like a spring with spring constant $mg/\ell$. So the pendulum oscillates in simple harmonic motion, and $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ from the previous lesson becomes $\sqrt{(mg/\ell)/m} = \sqrt{g/\ell}$. The MIT notes reach the same result from energy conservation:

<Formula label="Angular frequency of a pendulum (small angles)" tex="\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{\ell}}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (70)" />

<Formula label="Period of a pendulum (small angles)" tex="T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (84)" />

Three things are missing from this formula, and each one matters:

- **The mass.** A heavier bob needs more force to speed up, but gravity pulls it harder in exactly the same proportion. All pendulums of the same length keep the same time.
- **The amplitude.** As for the spring, a wider swing is also a faster swing. This is only true for small angles; for wide swings the period grows a little.
- **Anything except $\ell$ and $g$.** A longer pendulum is slower, and the same pendulum is slower where gravity is weaker.

<WorkedExample title="A clock pendulum">
<Problem>
A pendulum clock ticks once every second, so its pendulum has a period of $2.0\ \text{s}$ (one second each way). How long must the pendulum be? Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Rearrange the period formula">
Square $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$ to get $T^2 = 4\pi^2\ell/g$, so $\ell = gT^2/(4\pi^2)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Calculate">
$\ell = 9.81 \times 2.0^2/(4\pi^2) = 0.99\ \text{m}$. This is why tall "grandfather clocks" are about two metres high: the pendulum alone is nearly a metre long.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="The same pendulum on the Moon">
<Problem>
A $1.0\ \text{m}$ pendulum is taken to the Moon, where $g = 1.62\ \text{m/s}^2$ (NASA's Moon Fact Sheet). Compare its period there with its period on Earth.
</Problem>
<Step title="On Earth">
$T = 2\pi\sqrt{1.0/9.81} = 2.0\ \text{s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="On the Moon">
$T = 2\pi\sqrt{1.0/1.62} = 4.9\ \text{s}$. Weaker gravity gives a weaker restoring push, so the pendulum swings about two and a half times more slowly. Measuring the period of a pendulum is in fact a way to measure $g$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Energy in an oscillating spring

An oscillator keeps trading one kind of energy for another. For a mass on a spring, the moving mass has kinetic energy $\tfrac12 mv^2$, and a stretched or squeezed spring stores energy too. Stretching a spring by $x$ takes work. The force you need grows steadily from $0$ to $kx$ as you pull, so on average it is $\tfrac12 kx$, and the work is that average force times the distance $x$. (On a force–stretch graph it is the area of the triangle under the line.) The stored **spring energy** (elastic potential energy) is

<Formula label="Energy stored in a spring" tex="U = \tfrac12 k x^2" cite="mit" at="Eq. (55)" />

The total energy is the sum of the two:

<Formula label="Energy of a mass on a spring" tex="E = \tfrac12 m v^2 + \tfrac12 k x^2" cite="mit" at="Eq. (56), where the notes write the velocity v as ẋ" />

With no friction this total stays the same. At a turning point the mass is at rest and all the energy is in the spring: $E = \tfrac12 kA^2$. At equilibrium the spring is relaxed and all the energy is kinetic. In between it is shared, and the sum never changes:

<Formula label="Total energy of a simple harmonic oscillator" tex="E = \tfrac12 k A^2" cite="mit" at="Eq. (58)" />

So the energy grows with the square of the amplitude. Setting $\tfrac12 m v_\text{max}^2 = \tfrac12 kA^2$ gives $v_\text{max} = A\sqrt{k/m} = \omega A$, the same result as from the slope of the position graph in the previous lesson.

<WorkedExample title="Sharing the energy">
<Problem>
The block from the previous lesson ($m = 0.40\ \text{kg}$, $k = 25\ \text{N/m}$, amplitude $5.0\ \text{cm}$) is $3.0\ \text{cm}$ from equilibrium. How fast is it moving?
</Problem>
<Step title="Total energy">
$E = \tfrac12 kA^2 = \tfrac12 \times 25 \times 0.050^2 = 0.031\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy in the spring now">
$U = \tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12 \times 25 \times 0.030^2 = 0.011\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Kinetic energy and speed">
$K = E - U = 0.020\ \text{J}$, so $v = \sqrt{2K/m} = \sqrt{2 \times 0.020/0.40} = 0.32\ \text{m/s}$, a little less than the largest speed of $0.40\ \text{m/s}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Energy in a swinging pendulum

The pendulum does the same with gravity. At the end of its swing the bob is momentarily at rest and raised above its lowest point; at the bottom it is lowest and fastest. A bob on a string of length $\ell$, at angle $\theta$, is a height $h = \ell(1 - \cos\theta)$ above the bottom, and its energy is

<Formula label="Energy of a pendulum" tex="E = \tfrac12 m v^2 + m g \ell (1 - \cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (65), with v = ℓ dθ/dt" />

Released from rest at angle $\theta_0$, all its energy starts as gravitational potential energy, and at the bottom all of it is kinetic: $\tfrac12 mv^2 = mgh$, so $v = \sqrt{2gh}$. This holds for any angle, small or not.

<WorkedExample title="Speed at the bottom of the swing">
<Problem>
A pendulum $1.0\ \text{m}$ long is released from rest at $20^\circ$ from the vertical. How fast is the bob moving at the bottom?
</Problem>
<Step title="Height of the release point">
$h = \ell(1 - \cos\theta_0) = 1.0 \times (1 - \cos 20^\circ) = 0.060\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed">
$v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.060} = 1.09\ \text{m/s}$, whatever the mass of the bob.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Real oscillations die out

A real pendulum does not swing for ever. Air pushes against the bob and the pivot rubs, and these frictional forces turn the energy of the oscillation slowly into heat. The amplitude shrinks swing by swing until the pendulum hangs still. This is called a **damped** oscillation. A pendulum clock keeps going because a weight or a spring gives the pendulum a tiny push every swing, just enough to replace what friction takes.

<Callout kind="note">
Weak damping changes the amplitude much more than the period. A swing that slowly dies out still keeps very nearly the same rhythm.
</Callout>

## Summary

- For small angles (in radians) $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, and a pendulum is a simple harmonic oscillator with $\omega = \sqrt{g/\ell}$ and $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$.
- The period does not depend on the mass or (for small swings) on the amplitude; it measures $g$.
- A stretched spring stores $U = \tfrac12 kx^2$. The total energy of a mass on a spring, $E = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12 kA^2$, stays constant as it moves between spring and motion.
- A pendulum trades gravitational potential energy for kinetic energy: released from height $h$, it reaches $v = \sqrt{2gh}$ at the bottom.
- Friction drains the energy, so real oscillations are damped: the amplitude shrinks while the period stays nearly the same.
