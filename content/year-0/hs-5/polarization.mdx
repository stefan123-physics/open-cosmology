Hold a pair of polarizing sunglasses in front of a computer screen and turn them slowly. At one angle the screen looks normal; a quarter turn further it goes almost black. Nothing like that happens with ordinary tinted glasses. The screen's light has a hidden property, a direction, and these glasses can tell which direction it is. This lesson is about that property: **polarization**.

## A rope through a slot

Go back to the waves of lesson 5.3. In a **transverse** wave the pieces of the medium move at right angles to the direction the wave travels; in a **longitudinal** wave they move back and forth along it.

Now think of a transverse wave on a long rope that runs through a narrow vertical slot, for example between two bars of a fence. The wave travels along the rope, but "at right angles to the rope" still leaves many directions: you can shake the rope up and down, left and right, or along any slanting line in between. The direction in which the wave oscillates is its **polarization**.

- Shake the rope **up and down**: the rope moves along the slot, and the wave passes through.
- Shake it **left and right**: the bars stop the rope moving sideways, and the wave is blocked.
- Shake it along a **slanting line**: only the up-and-down part of the motion gets through, so a smaller wave comes out on the other side, now oscillating up and down.

A sound wave in air could not tell a slot from a hole: the air moves along the direction of travel, and every direction across it looks the same. **Only transverse waves can be polarized.** That makes polarization a test: if a wave shows it, the wave must be transverse.

## Light can be polarized

Light passes this test, which shows that light is a transverse wave, as lesson 5.8 said. What actually oscillates in a light wave is not a rope but something you meet in Block 6 (lesson 6.12 explains it). For now it is enough to speak of **the direction in which the light wave oscillates**, always at right angles to the direction the light travels.

Light from the Sun, a flame or a light bulb is **unpolarized**: it is sent out by a huge number of atoms independently of each other, so it is a jumble of waves oscillating in all directions across the beam, changing all the time. Light whose waves all oscillate along one line is **linearly polarized**.

A **polarizing filter** (or polarizer) works like the slot for light. It is a thin plastic sheet with a built-in direction, its **transmission direction** (the MIT notes call it the "easy axis"): light oscillating along that direction passes, light oscillating at right angles to it is absorbed. Whatever reaches it, what comes out is linearly polarized along the filter's direction.

When unpolarized light falls on one filter, the oscillations in all directions together count half along the filter's direction and half across it. So exactly half the intensity (lesson 5.5: the energy per square metre per second) gets through, however you turn the filter:

<Formula label="Unpolarized light through one filter" tex="I = \frac{I_0}{2}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 18–19, implied: after filters at 0° and 45° a quarter, I₀/4, is left, so the first filter passed I₀/2; NASA Goddard's Rayleigh scattering page (in the Sources list) says the same of unpolarized sunlight" />

Here $I_0$ is the intensity of the light falling on the filter and $I$ the intensity that passes. Turning a single filter in front of a lamp therefore changes nothing you can see: the light that comes through is always half as bright.

## A second filter: Malus's law

Now let the light that came out of the first filter, linearly polarized, fall on a second filter whose direction makes an angle $\theta$ with the direction of oscillation of the light. Like the slanting rope, the oscillation can be split into a part along the filter's direction and a part across it, just as a vector is split into components (lesson 0.8). If the oscillation has amplitude $A$, the part along the filter has amplitude $A\cos\theta$, and only that part gets through.

The intensity of a wave grows with the square of its amplitude (lesson 5.5 said so for sound, and it holds for light too). The amplitude is multiplied by $\cos\theta$, so the intensity is multiplied by $\cos^2\theta$:

<Formula label="Malus's law" tex="I = I_0\cos^2\theta" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

with $I_0$ now the intensity of the polarized light that reaches the second filter. This rule is called **Malus's law**. Check its two extremes:

- At $\theta = 0°$ the filter lines up with the light: $\cos 0° = 1$, so all of it passes.
- At $\theta = 90°$ the filters are **crossed**: $\cos 90° = 0$, so nothing passes. Two crossed filters held over each other look black.

<WorkedExample title="A lamp and two filters">
<Problem>
Unpolarized light with an intensity of $240\ \text{W/m}^2$ falls on a polarizing filter. Behind it is a second filter, turned $35°$ from the first. What intensity comes out of each filter? What comes out if the second filter is turned to $90°$?
</Problem>
<Step title="The first filter">
Unpolarized light loses half: $I_1 = 240/2 = 120\ \text{W/m}^2$, now polarized along the first filter.
</Step>
<Step title="The second filter">
Malus's law with $\theta = 35°$: $I_2 = 120 \times \cos^2 35° = 120 \times 0.671 = 80.5\ \text{W/m}^2$. Together the two filters pass about a third of the lamp's light.
</Step>
<Step title="Crossed">
At $90°$, $\cos^2 90° = 0$: nothing comes out of the second filter, although each filter on its own passes half.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Dimming to a tenth">
<Problem>
A photographer wants to dim polarized light to 10% of its intensity by turning a filter in front of it. At what angle between the filter and the light's direction of oscillation does that happen?
</Problem>
<Step title="Set up Malus's law">
$I/I_0 = \cos^2\theta = 0.10$, so $\cos\theta = \sqrt{0.10} = 0.316$.
</Step>
<Step title="Find the angle">
The angle whose cosine is $0.316$ (the cos⁻¹ key of a calculator, lesson 0.7) is $\theta = 71.6°$. Turning the filter on to $90°$ would dim the light completely.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Polarization in nature

Sunlight is unpolarized when it arrives, but two everyday effects polarize it.

**Reflection.** Light reflected from a flat, shiny surface such as water, a wet road or a window is partly polarized, oscillating mostly along the surface (horizontally, for a lake or a road). That reflected light is the **glare** that dazzles drivers and anglers. Polarizing sunglasses have their transmission direction vertical, so they block much of the glare while letting through half of the ordinary, unpolarized light from everything else. Tilt your head and the glare comes back. Why reflection polarizes light, and at which angle it does so completely, needs more about light in matter and is left to university.

**Scattering.** The sky is blue because the molecules of the air scatter sunlight in all directions, blue light much more than red. The scattered light is partly polarized as well, most strongly in the part of the sky $90°$ away from the Sun. You can see it yourself on a clear day: look at the sky through polarizing sunglasses and turn them, and the sky in that direction grows lighter and darker.

## Uses

- **Screens.** Flat screens of the liquid-crystal kind (LCD), on laptops, watches and calculators, send out polarized light: that is why a polarizing filter can black them out, as in the opening of this lesson. The screen itself uses polarizing filters to make each pixel light or dark.
- **Photography.** A polarizing filter on a camera lens removes reflections from windows and water, and darkens the blue sky so that white clouds stand out.
- **3D cinema.** The film is shown as two pictures at once, one for each eye, sent out with two different polarizations (in some systems at right angles to each other). Each lens of the glasses passes one of them and blocks the other, so each eye sees only its own picture, and the brain combines the two into depth.

Lesson 5.8 mentioned a faint microwave glow that fills the whole sky, the oldest light there is. This glow is polarized too, and NASA's WMAP satellite mapped its polarization over the full sky. Block 8 studies this microwave glow and what it tells us about the young universe.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Polarization** is the direction in which a transverse wave oscillates. Only transverse waves can be polarized; sound in air, a longitudinal wave, cannot.
- Light can be polarized, so it is a transverse wave. What oscillates in it comes in lesson 6.12.
- Light from the Sun or a lamp is **unpolarized**; a **polarizing filter** passes only the oscillation along its transmission direction, so it passes half of unpolarized light, $I = I_0/2$, as linearly polarized light.
- **Malus's law:** polarized light meeting a filter at angle $\theta$ keeps $I = I_0\cos^2\theta$. **Crossed** filters ($90°$) pass nothing.
- Reflection (glare) and scattering in the air (the blue sky) polarize sunlight. Polarization is used in sunglasses, LCD screens, camera filters and 3D cinema.
