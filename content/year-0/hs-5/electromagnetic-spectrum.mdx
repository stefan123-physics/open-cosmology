Sunlight reaches us across millions of kilometres of empty space. A sound could never make that trip: in lesson 5.3 a wave needed a medium, the rope or the air, to hand the disturbance on from one piece to the next. Light is different. It is a wave that needs no medium at all, and the light we see is only one small member of a large family of such waves.

## A wave without a medium

Light belongs to the **electromagnetic waves**, also called **electromagnetic radiation**. What is actually waving in an electromagnetic wave is not a material but something you meet in Block 6: electric and magnetic fields. Lesson 6.12 explains how they keep each other going. For now, two facts are enough:

- An electromagnetic wave can travel through empty space (a vacuum). It can also pass through some materials, such as air, water and glass.
- It is a transverse wave: what waves does so at right angles to the direction the light travels (lesson 5.12 uses this).

All electromagnetic waves travel through a vacuum at the same speed, the **speed of light**, written $c$. Its value is $299\,792\,458\ \text{m/s}$, and in calculations we round it to

<Formula label="The speed of light in vacuum" tex="c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 11.2" />

Nothing travels faster. In air light is very slightly slower, so the same value can be used there; in water and glass it is clearly slower, which lesson 5.9 uses to explain why light bends. Sound in air, at about $340\ \text{m/s}$, is almost 900 000 times slower than light: that is why you see lightning before you hear the thunder.

<WorkedExample title="A radio echo from the Moon">
<Problem>
A radio station on Earth sends a short pulse of radio waves to the Moon, which is $3.84 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}$ away (its average distance, from NASA's Moon Fact Sheet), and picks up the faint echo that bounces back. How long after sending does the echo arrive?
</Problem>
<Step title="Distance there and back">
The pulse travels to the Moon and back: $2d = 2 \times 3.84 \times 10^{8} = 7.68 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Time at the speed of light">
Time is distance over speed: $t = 2d/c = (7.68 \times 10^{8})/(3.00 \times 10^{8}) = 2.56\ \text{s}$. A radio conversation with someone on the Moon has a pause of two and a half seconds in it.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The wave equation for light

Light obeys the same rule as every wave in lesson 5.3: in one period the wave moves on by one wavelength, so its speed is frequency times wavelength. In a vacuum that speed is always $c$, so

<Formula label="The wave equation for light" tex="c = f\lambda" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 11.2" />

Because $c$ is fixed, frequency and wavelength are not independent: if one is known, the other follows. A **long wavelength means a low frequency, a short wavelength a high frequency.** Doubling the frequency halves the wavelength.

<WorkedExample title="The frequency of green light">
<Problem>
A green laser pointer gives light with a wavelength of $530\ \text{nm}$ (nanometres; $1\ \text{nm} = 10^{-9}\ \text{m}$). What is its frequency?
</Problem>
<Step title="Wavelength in metres">
$\lambda = 530\ \text{nm} = 530 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m} = 5.30 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Rearrange the wave equation">
$f = c/\lambda = (3.00 \times 10^{8})/(5.30 \times 10^{-7}) = 5.66 \times 10^{14}\ \text{Hz}$. The light wave goes through more than half a million billion oscillations every second.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="The wavelength of an FM station">
<Problem>
An FM radio station broadcasts at $96.0\ \text{MHz}$, that is $96.0 \times 10^{6}\ \text{Hz}$. What is the wavelength of its radio waves?
</Problem>
<Step title="Rearrange the wave equation">
$\lambda = c/f = (3.00 \times 10^{8})/(96.0 \times 10^{6}) \approx 3.1\ \text{m}$: a radio wave is about as long as a car, while green light was half a millionth of a metre.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The electromagnetic spectrum

Radio waves, light and X-rays are all the same kind of wave, travelling at the same speed $c$. What sets them apart is only their wavelength, and so their frequency. Sorted by wavelength, they form the **electromagnetic spectrum**. It is divided into seven bands, here from the longest wavelength (lowest frequency) to the shortest (highest frequency). The typical wavelength in the third column is one value picked inside each band; its frequency is worked out with $f = c/\lambda$.

| Band | Wavelengths | A typical wavelength | Its frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| radio waves | longer than $0.1\ \text{m}$ | $1\ \text{m}$ | $3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{Hz}$ |
| microwaves | $0.1\ \text{mm}$ to $0.1\ \text{m}$ | $1\ \text{cm}$ | $3.00 \times 10^{10}\ \text{Hz}$ |
| infrared | $700\ \text{nm}$ to $0.1\ \text{mm}$ | $10^{-5}\ \text{m}$ | $3.00 \times 10^{13}\ \text{Hz}$ |
| visible light | $400\ \text{nm}$ to $700\ \text{nm}$ | $500\ \text{nm}$ | $6.00 \times 10^{14}\ \text{Hz}$ |
| ultraviolet | $10\ \text{nm}$ to $400\ \text{nm}$ | $100\ \text{nm}$ | $3.00 \times 10^{15}\ \text{Hz}$ |
| X-rays | $1\ \text{nm}$ to $10\ \text{nm}$ | $5\ \text{nm}$ | $6.00 \times 10^{16}\ \text{Hz}$ |
| gamma rays | shorter than $1\ \text{nm}$ | $10^{-12}\ \text{m}$ | $3.00 \times 10^{20}\ \text{Hz}$ |

The borders between the bands are agreements, not sharp physical edges, and different books draw them in slightly different places (some count radio waves from $1\ \text{cm}$ upwards, for example). From one end of the table to the other, the wavelength shrinks from the size of a building or more to far less than the size of an atom.

## Visible light and colour

Our eyes respond to only one narrow band: wavelengths from about $400\ \text{nm}$ to $700\ \text{nm}$. Inside it, different wavelengths look like different **colours**. The longest, near $700\ \text{nm}$, look red; then come orange, yellow, green and blue, down to violet near $400\ \text{nm}$. Sunlight and the light of a lamp are a mix of all these wavelengths, which together look **white**; a prism or a raindrop spreads them out into a rainbow (lesson 5.9 explains how). Just beyond red lies infrared, which we feel as warmth but cannot see; just beyond violet lies ultraviolet, which bees can see but we cannot.

## Uses and dangers

Each band has its own uses, because waves of different wavelengths interact with matter in different ways.

- **Radio waves** carry radio and television broadcasts.
- **Microwaves** heat food in a microwave oven and are used in radar and by mobile phones.
- **Infrared** is given off by everything warm: night-vision cameras and heat sensors pick it up, and infrared lasers cut metal.
- **Visible light** is what we use to see the world.
- **Ultraviolet** from the Sun causes sunburn and can damage the skin and the eyes; sunscreen blocks part of it.
- **X-rays** pass through skin and soft tissue and are used to make images of bones.
- **Gamma rays** pass through the body easily; they are used to kill bacteria on medical equipment and food.

From ultraviolet upwards, the higher the frequency, the deeper the radiation goes into the body and the more harm it can do: ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays can break molecules apart and damage the DNA in cells. Why the high-frequency end is so much more harmful, even at low brightness, is a question for Block 7, where light turns out to have a second, surprising side.

## The sky in every band

Astronomers use the whole spectrum, because objects in space give off different bands depending on how hot and how dense they are.

- **Radio:** stars and the cool gas between them.
- **Microwaves:** the structure of nearby galaxies, and a faint glow that fills the whole sky, the oldest light there is (Block 8).
- **Infrared:** dust between the stars, and stars still forming inside dusty clouds.
- **Visible:** stars, and the gas and planets they light up.
- **Ultraviolet:** very hot objects, such as the hottest stars.
- **X-rays:** extremely hot gas, for example in clusters of galaxies.
- **Gamma rays:** the most violent events in the universe.

There is one problem: the **atmosphere** absorbs most bands before they reach the ground. Only radio waves and visible light come through well, with some infrared that can be caught from high, dry mountains. The ozone layer stops most of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet, which protects life but also hides the ultraviolet sky; X-rays and gamma rays from space never reach the ground at all. So radio telescopes are big dishes on the ground and visible-light telescopes sit on mountain tops, but for the other bands telescopes must go up: balloons reach about 35 km, rockets give a few minutes above the air, and for long observations telescopes are put into orbit. Examples are the Hubble Space Telescope (visible light), Spitzer (infrared), Planck (microwaves), Chandra (X-rays) and Fermi (gamma rays).

## Summary

- Light is an **electromagnetic wave**: it needs no medium and crosses a vacuum at the **speed of light**, $c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. What is waving (electric and magnetic fields) comes in lesson 6.12.
- For every electromagnetic wave $c = f\lambda$: a long wavelength means a low frequency, a short wavelength a high frequency.
- The **electromagnetic spectrum**, from long to short wavelength: radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays. The borders between the bands are agreements.
- **Visible light** runs from about $400\ \text{nm}$ (violet) to $700\ \text{nm}$ (red); white light is a mix of all colours.
- The atmosphere lets through radio waves, visible light, some infrared and a little ultraviolet; telescopes for the other bands must be above it.
