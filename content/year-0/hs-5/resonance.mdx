A child on a swing goes higher and higher with small pushes, as long as they come at the right moments. A singer holding exactly the right note can make a wine glass ring, and in rare cases shatter it. A footbridge can start to sway when a crowd walks across it in step. All three are the same effect: **resonance**, the large response of an oscillator that is pushed in rhythm with its own swing.

## Natural frequency

Pull a mass on a spring aside, or push a pendulum, and let go. It oscillates at a frequency that belongs to the system itself and not to how you started it: its **natural frequency**. From lessons 5.1 and 5.2 we know it for two cases. A mass $m$ on a spring with spring constant $k$ has

<Formula label="Natural angular frequency of a mass on a spring" tex="\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (4), below the equation" />

and a pendulum of length $\ell$ swinging through small angles has $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/\ell}$ (the swing of Eq. (2) in the notes). The small $0$ marks the oscillator's own frequency, to keep it apart from the frequency of a push that will come soon. As always, the natural frequency in hertz is $f_0 = \omega_0/2\pi$ and the natural period is $T = 1/f_0$.

Some objects have more than one natural frequency. A string fixed at both ends (lesson 5.4) can vibrate in any of its harmonics, $f_n = n\,v/2L$, so it has a whole list of natural frequencies. A wine glass, a bridge or a building also has several ways to vibrate, each with its own frequency.

<WorkedExample title="The rhythm of a mass on a spring">
<Problem>
A $0.50\ \text{kg}$ block is attached to a spring with $k = 50\ \text{N/m}$. Find its natural angular frequency, its natural frequency and its natural period.
</Problem>
<Step title="Angular frequency">
$\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{50/0.50} = 10\ \text{rad/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Frequency and period">
$f_0 = \omega_0/2\pi = 1.59\ \text{Hz}$ and $T = 2\pi/\omega_0 = 0.63\ \text{s}$. This block is used again below.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Driven oscillations

Now keep pushing the block back and forth with a force that repeats in time, for example with a motor and a crank. The MIT notes take the simplest such force, one that changes like a cosine:

<Formula label="Driving force" tex="F = F_0\cos(\omega t)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (3), Sec. 2" />

Here $F_0$ is the largest force and $\omega$ is the **driving angular frequency**, set by the motor, not by the spring. This is a **driven** (or **forced**) oscillation.

At first the motion is a messy mixture of the block's own swing and the swing the motor tries to impose. Friction makes the block's own swing die away, as for the damped pendulum of lesson 5.2, and what is left is a steady oscillation **at the driving frequency** (the notes' Eq. (43), valid "for long times"). The block moves in the rhythm of the motor. Its natural frequency still matters, because it decides how large the steady swing is:

- **Driven slowly** ($\omega$ much less than $\omega_0$), the block simply follows the force, which changes so slowly that the spring balances it at every moment: the amplitude is about $F_0/k$, as if the force were held still (notes, Sec. 3.2).
- **Driven very fast** ($\omega$ much more than $\omega_0$), the force reverses before the block can get going: it hardly moves at all.
- **Driven near its natural frequency**, the block swings far more than in either case. This is resonance.

## Resonance

Why is the natural frequency special? Think of the swing. If every push comes at the same point of the swing, and in the direction it moves, each push adds a little energy, and the swing grows cycle after cycle. Push at any other rhythm and some pushes come at the wrong moment and take energy back out.

Without any friction there would be no limit. The notes show that an oscillator driven exactly at its natural frequency, starting from rest, swings with an amplitude that grows steadily in time:

<Formula label="Undamped oscillator driven at resonance" tex="x(t) = \frac{F_0}{2m\omega_0}\,t\,\sin(\omega_0 t)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (24), Sec. 2.1" />

The factor in front of $\sin(\omega_0 t)$ is the amplitude, and it contains $t$: it keeps growing, as long as you keep driving. Real objects always lose energy to friction, and that sets a limit.

## Damping limits the peak

The friction on a moving object often grows with its speed, as air drag does at low speeds. The notes model it as a force opposite to the motion and proportional to the speed: the speed times a **friction constant** $b$ (in kg/s; not the same as the drag coefficient of lesson 2.6). They write the strength of the damping as $\gamma$ (gamma), the **damping constant**, in units of 1/s:

<Formula label="Damping constant" tex="\gamma = \frac{b}{2m}" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (26)–(27), with the drag force −bv" />

What does a value of $\gamma$ mean? Left alone, a weakly damped oscillator's swing shrinks to roughly a third of its size in every time $1/\gamma$, so after a few times $1/\gamma$ the starting motion has died away (notes, Eqs. (42)–(43): "for long times", meaning much longer than $1/\gamma$). A large $\gamma$ means the swing dies quickly, a small one that it rings on for a long time. The factor 2 in $b/2m$ is a choice that makes later formulas tidier. The university course 8.03 writes $\gamma$ for $b/m$ instead, twice this $\gamma$, so its formulas have $\gamma$ where the ones below have $2\gamma$.

With this friction the swing no longer grows for ever. Once the starting motion has died away, the block oscillates at the driving frequency with a fixed amplitude. The notes find it in their Eq. (43). Deriving it needs mathematics beyond this course, but the result is a formula you can use:

<Formula label="Amplitude of a driven, damped oscillator" tex="A = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\gamma^2\omega^2}}" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (40) and (43), where the notes write the amplitude as A_dr" />

Read it as a fraction with a variable bottom. The bottom is smallest, and the amplitude largest, when $\omega^2$ is close to $\omega_0^2$, because then the first term under the root almost vanishes. With weak damping the peak lies almost exactly at the natural frequency:

<Formula label="Frequency of largest amplitude" tex="\omega_\text{res} = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - 2\gamma^2} \approx \omega_0 \quad (\gamma \ll \omega_0)" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (46)–(47)" />

Putting $\omega = \omega_0$ into the amplitude formula leaves only the damping term under the root, $\sqrt{4\gamma^2\omega_0^2} = 2\gamma\omega_0$, so the height of the peak is

<Formula label="Amplitude at resonance (weak damping)" tex="A_\text{max} \approx \frac{F_0}{2m\gamma\omega_0}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (49)" />

Half the damping gives twice the peak. The graph shows the amplitude for the block of the worked example, driven with $F_0 = 0.20\ \text{N}$, for three strengths of damping.

Two things change as the damping grows. The peak becomes **lower**, because friction takes more energy away every cycle. And it becomes **wider**: a weakly damped oscillator responds strongly only in a narrow band of frequencies around $\omega_0$, while a heavily damped one responds a little over a broad range. Far from the peak, damping hardly matters.

<WorkedExample title="Driving the block">
<Problem>
The block of the first worked example ($m = 0.50\ \text{kg}$, $k = 50\ \text{N/m}$, $\omega_0 = 10\ \text{rad/s}$) is driven with $F_0 = 0.20\ \text{N}$. Its damping constant is $\gamma = 0.50\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Find the amplitude when it is driven very slowly, at resonance, and at $\omega = 20\ \text{rad/s}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Driven very slowly">
$A \approx F_0/k = 0.20/50 = 0.0040\ \text{m} = 4.0\ \text{mm}$.
</Step>
<Step title="At resonance">
$A_\text{max} \approx F_0/(2m\gamma\omega_0) = 0.20/(2 \times 0.50 \times 0.50 \times 10) = 0.040\ \text{m} = 4.0\ \text{cm}$: ten times as much, with the same force.
</Step>
<Step title="Driven at twice the natural frequency">
$F_0/m = 0.40\ \text{m/s}^2$, $\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 = 100 - 400 = -300$ and $4\gamma^2\omega^2 = 4 \times 0.25 \times 400 = 400$, so $A = 0.40/\sqrt{300^2 + 400} = 0.0013\ \text{m} = 1.3\ \text{mm}$, less than when driven slowly.
</Step>
<Step title="With four times the damping">
At $\gamma = 2.0\ \text{s}^{-1}$ the peak is $0.20/(2 \times 0.50 \times 2.0 \times 10) = 0.010\ \text{m} = 1.0\ \text{cm}$: a quarter of the height.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Pushing at the right moment

At resonance the force and the motion are not in step in the obvious way. The notes show (Eq. (49)) that the displacement lags a quarter of a period behind the force: the force is largest as the block rushes through its equilibrium position, and zero at the turning points.

The reason is energy. The work done by a force is force times distance moved. In a short moment the block covers the most distance where it moves fastest, at equilibrium, and almost none at the turning points, where it stops. So a push does the most work, and adds the most energy, when it comes as the oscillator moves fastest, in the direction it is moving.

For a swing this means: push once every swing, at its natural frequency, and in the direction it is going. The notes point out that, for a push of fixed strength lasting a fixed time, the best moment is near the bottom, where the swing moves fastest, and not at the top, where most people push because the swing stands still there and something moving fast is hard to push safely.

## Resonance everywhere

A string fixed at both ends has many natural frequencies, its harmonics. Shake one end with a vibrator and the string stays almost still at most frequencies. But when the driving frequency matches one of the harmonics, the small shaking builds up into a large standing wave with $n$ loops.

<WorkedExample title="A string driven by a vibrator">
<Problem>
A string $0.90\ \text{m}$ long is fixed at both ends, and waves travel along it at $72\ \text{m/s}$. A vibrator near one end shakes it at $120\ \text{Hz}$. Does a large standing wave form, and if so, how many loops does it have?
</Problem>
<Step title="Natural frequencies">
$f_1 = v/2L = 72/(2 \times 0.90) = 40\ \text{Hz}$, so the harmonics are $40$, $80$, $120$, $160\ \text{Hz}$, and so on.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
$120\ \text{Hz}$ is the third harmonic: the string resonates in a standing wave with three loops, each $0.30\ \text{m}$ long ($\lambda_3 = 2L/3 = 0.60\ \text{m}$). At $100\ \text{Hz}$, between two harmonics, it would only tremble.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The same idea explains many things you meet every day:

- **Musical instruments.** A guitar string alone is quiet. It drives the wooden body, which vibrates with it and moves much more air. Hold a struck tuning fork against a guitar and a string tuned to that note starts to vibrate by itself, while strings tuned to other notes stay almost still. (Through the air alone the effect is weak, and a string with a matching higher harmonic also responds, so musicians usually tune by ear.)
- **A singer and a glass.** Tap a wine glass and it rings at its natural frequency. Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave in the air (lesson 5.5), so a loud note at exactly that frequency pushes the glass in rhythm, and the glass rim swings further with every cycle. Glass has very little damping, so the peak is high and narrow: the note must be just right, and then the glass can break.
- **Bridges and buildings.** A bridge or a tall building has natural frequencies too. Wind, an earthquake or people walking in step can drive it near one of them. Engineers keep natural frequencies away from the expected rhythms, and add dampers: they make the peak lower, as in the graph.

Resonance can be useful (a swing, an instrument, and, in later blocks, tuning a radio to one station) or dangerous (a breaking glass or a swaying bridge). In every case the rule is the same: the response is large when the driving frequency matches a natural frequency, and damping decides how large.

## Summary

- Every oscillator has a **natural frequency**, the frequency at which it swings when left alone: $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ for a mass on a spring, $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/\ell}$ for a pendulum. A string has a whole list of them, its harmonics.
- A **driven** oscillator ends up moving at the frequency of the driving force, $F = F_0\cos(\omega t)$. Its amplitude is small when driven far below or far above $\omega_0$.
- **Resonance:** when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency, every push adds energy, and the amplitude becomes large. Without friction it would grow without limit.
- With damping $\gamma$ the amplitude is $A = (F_0/m)/\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\gamma^2\omega^2}$. For weak damping the peak lies at $\omega \approx \omega_0$ and is $A_\text{max} \approx F_0/(2m\gamma\omega_0)$ high. More damping gives a **lower and wider** peak.
- Pushes add the most energy when they come as the oscillator moves fastest, in the direction it moves.
- Resonance can be useful (swings, instruments) or dangerous (glasses, bridges, buildings).
