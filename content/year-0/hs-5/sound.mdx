Sound is so much a part of life that it is easy to forget it is a wave. A voice, a guitar, a passing train: each starts as something vibrating, and each reaches you as a tiny, fast change in the pressure of the air at your ear. This lesson puts the wave ideas of the last two lessons to work on sound: how fast it goes, what we hear as pitch and loudness, how sound weakens with distance, and how a column of air in a pipe can sing a note.

## Sound is a pressure wave

Strike a tuning fork and its prongs move back and forth many times a second. Each time a prong moves outward it pushes the air next to it together, making a **compression**: a thin region where the air is packed more tightly and its pressure is slightly above normal. When the prong moves back, it leaves a region where the air is spread out, a **rarefaction**, with the pressure slightly below normal. The air passes these pushes on to the air beyond it, and a train of compressions and rarefactions travels away from the fork.

The air itself only moves back and forth along the direction the sound travels, so sound is a **longitudinal wave**, like the pulses on the slinky in lesson 5.3. Because what changes from place to place is the pressure (lesson 4.1), sound is also called a **pressure wave**. When it reaches your ear, the changing pressure makes your eardrum vibrate, and those vibrations are passed on to the inner ear and from there to the brain.

A sound wave needs a **medium**, something made of particles that can push on each other: a gas, a liquid or a solid. In empty space there is nothing to compress, so sound cannot travel there at all.

## The speed of sound

Sound travels at very different speeds in different materials. The table gives a few values.

| Medium | Speed of sound |
|---|---|
| Air at 0 °C | 331 m/s |
| Air at 20 °C | 343 m/s |
| Sea water | 1531 m/s |
| Lead | 2160 m/s |
| Copper | 4760 m/s |
| Glass | 5100 m/s |
| Aluminium | 6420 m/s |

Two patterns stand out:

- Sound is fastest in solids, slower in liquids and slowest in gases. Solids and liquids are far harder to squeeze than a gas: they are much stiffer, so a push is handed on quickly from one part to the next. How heavy the material is matters too, as in lesson 5.3: of two materials equally stiff, the heavier one carries sound more slowly. That is why sound is slower in lead (2160 m/s), a very heavy metal, than in aluminium (6420 m/s), a light one.
- In a warmer gas sound is faster: its particles move faster, and the disturbance is passed on more quickly. Warming air from 0 °C to 20 °C raises the speed from 331 to 343 m/s.

In this lesson we use $343\ \text{m/s}$ for sound in air at room temperature. Light is so much faster that it reaches you practically at once, which is why you see a distant event before you hear it.

Sound is reflected by large surfaces such as a wall, a cliff or the sea floor: the reflection is an **echo**. If you know the speed of sound and time the echo, you know how far away the reflecting surface is. Ships measure the depth of the sea this way (**sonar**), and bats and dolphins find their way and their food by listening to the echoes of their own calls (**echolocation**). The sound goes there and back, so it covers the distance twice:

<Formula label="Distance from an echo" tex="\text{distance} = v \times \frac{t}{2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 10.2, SONAR and Worked example 1" />

Here $v$ is the speed of sound and $t$ the time between sending the sound and hearing its echo.

<WorkedExample title="How far away is the storm?">
<Problem>
You see a flash of lightning and hear the thunder $4.5\ \text{s}$ later. How far away did the lightning strike? Take the speed of sound as $343\ \text{m/s}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Light arrives at once">
The light of the flash reaches you practically instantly, so the $4.5\ \text{s}$ is the time the sound took to travel from the lightning to you (one way, not an echo).
</Step>
<Step title="Distance">
distance $= v \times 4.5 = 343 \times 4.5 = 1.5 \times 10^3\ \text{m}$: about one and a half kilometres. A handy rule: every three seconds is about a kilometre.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Pitch, loudness and what we can hear

Because sound is a wave, what we hear is tied to the properties of the wave from lesson 5.3.

- **Pitch** is how high or low a note sounds, and it is set by the **frequency**. A bird's chirp has a high frequency and a high pitch; a lion's roar a low frequency and a low pitch.
- **Loudness** is set mainly by the **amplitude**: the bigger the pressure changes, the louder the sound. A source that vibrates more strongly puts more energy into the air and makes a larger amplitude. The ear is also more sensitive to some frequencies than to others, so two sounds of the same amplitude but different pitch can seem unequally loud.

The human ear hears frequencies from about $20\ \text{Hz}$ to $20\,000\ \text{Hz}$ ($20\ \text{kHz}$). Sound below $20\ \text{Hz}$ is **infrasound**; sound above $20\ \text{kHz}$ is **ultrasound**. Many animals hear beyond our range: dogs up to about $45\ \text{kHz}$, bats up to about $120\ \text{kHz}$, while elephants hear infrasound down to about $5\ \text{Hz}$.

The wavelength of a sound follows from the wave equation of lesson 5.3:

<Formula label="Wavelength of a sound" tex="\lambda = \frac{v}{f}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 10.3, activity 'Range of wavelengths'" />

**Ultrasound** has many uses. When it travels through the body, part of it is reflected at every boundary between different materials, such as between muscle and fat or muscle and bone. The reflections are recorded and turned into a picture: this is how an unborn baby is scanned, without harming it. Ultrasound is also used to find flaws inside metal parts, to clean jewellery and instruments, and, focused, to break up kidney stones.

<WorkedExample title="The wavelength of ultrasound">
<Problem>
An ultrasound probe sends sound of frequency $1.5\ \text{MHz}$ ($1.5 \times 10^6\ \text{Hz}$) into sea water, where sound travels at $1531\ \text{m/s}$. Find the wavelength, and compare it with a $1000\ \text{Hz}$ tone in air.
</Problem>
<Step title="Ultrasound in water">
$\lambda = v/f = 1531/(1.5 \times 10^6) = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}$: about one millimetre.
</Step>
<Step title="An audible tone in air">
$\lambda = 343/1000 = 0.343\ \text{m}$, over three hundred times longer.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Intensity: how much energy arrives

A sound wave carries energy. How strong a sound is at some place is described by its **intensity** $I$: the energy that the wave carries through one square metre each second, at right angles to the direction it travels. Energy per second is power (lesson 3.5), so intensity is power per area, measured in watts per square metre, $\text{W/m}^2$:

<Formula label="Intensity" tex="I = \frac{P}{\text{area}}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 10.4, definition of intensity" />

A larger amplitude means a larger intensity: the intensity grows with the square of the pressure changes in the wave (OpenLearn, Box 3).

Now think of a small source, such as a bell, sending sound equally in all directions. At a distance $r$, the power $P$ of the source is spread over the surface of a sphere of radius $r$, whose area is $4\pi r^2$. So

<Formula label="Intensity at a distance from a point source" tex="I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2}" cite="nasa" at="The Inverse Square Law (stated there for electromagnetic energy; the geometry is the same for sound)" />

This is the **inverse square law**. Twice as far from the source, the same power covers four times the area, so the intensity is a quarter; three times as far, a ninth. It holds where nothing reflects or absorbs the sound, as in an open field; in a room, the walls reflect sound back and it falls off more slowly.

<WorkedExample title="A loudspeaker in a field">
<Problem>
A small loudspeaker in the middle of a field gives off $0.50\ \text{W}$ of sound, equally in all directions. Find the intensity $3.0\ \text{m}$ and $6.0\ \text{m}$ away.
</Problem>
<Step title="At 3.0 m">
$I = P/4\pi r^2 = 0.50/(4\pi \times 3.0^2) = 0.50/113 = 4.4 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{W/m}^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="At 6.0 m">
Twice as far, a quarter of the intensity: $4.4 \times 10^{-3}/4 = 1.1 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{W/m}^2$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The decibel scale

The ear copes with an enormous range. A sound loud enough to hurt is about $10^{12}$ times (a million million times) as intense as the faintest sound you can just hear. Numbers spread so widely are easier to handle on a logarithmic scale (lesson 0.9), and that is what the **decibel** scale is. The **sound level** $\beta$ (beta) of a sound with intensity $I$ is

<Formula label="Sound level in decibels" tex="\beta = 10\log\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{I}{I_0} = 10^{\beta/10}" cite="reading2" at="derived from Box 3 (every 10 dB is a factor 10 in intensity) together with Siyavula Sec. 10.4, Table 10.3 (0 dB at the threshold of hearing)" />

in decibels (dB), where $I_0$ is the intensity of the faintest sound a person can hear, and $\log$ is the base-10 logarithm. That faintest sound has $0\ \text{dB}$. Every $10\ \text{dB}$ more means **ten times** the intensity, $20\ \text{dB}$ more a hundred times, and so on. (Some books, Siyavula among them, loosely call the decibel value the "intensity"; strictly, intensity is in $\text{W/m}^2$ and the decibel value is the sound level.)

| Sound | Sound level | Intensity compared with the faintest audible sound |
|---|---|---|
| Whisper | 20 dB | $10^2$ times |
| Normal conversation | 60 dB | $10^6$ times |
| City traffic | 70 dB | $10^7$ times |
| Rock band | 110 dB | $10^{11}$ times |
| Threshold of pain | 120 dB | $10^{12}$ times |

Sounds of $80\ \text{dB}$ and above can damage your hearing if you are exposed to them for a long time; louder than the threshold of pain, damage can be immediate. Ear protection, shorter exposure and more distance from the source all help.

<WorkedExample title="Working with decibels">
<Problem>
(a) A lawnmower makes $90\ \text{dB}$ where you stand, a conversation $60\ \text{dB}$. How many times more intense is the lawnmower's sound? (b) A second, identical lawnmower starts next to the first. By how much does the sound level rise? (c) By how much does the level of a point source drop if you go twice as far away?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A difference of 30 dB">
$90 - 60 = 30\ \text{dB}$, three steps of $10\ \text{dB}$: $10^{30/10} = 10^3 = 1000$ times the intensity.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Twice the intensity">
Two equal sources give twice the intensity: the level rises by $10\log 2 = 3.0\ \text{dB}$, to $93\ \text{dB}$. Doubling the source adds only $3\ \text{dB}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Twice as far">
By the inverse square law the intensity becomes a quarter: $10\log(1/4) = -6.0\ \text{dB}$. Each doubling of the distance lowers the level by about $6\ \text{dB}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Standing waves in pipes

In lesson 5.4 a wave on a string was reflected at the fixed ends, and only standing waves that fitted the string could last: its harmonics. The same happens to sound in a tube, such as a flute, an organ pipe or a bottle you blow across. Sound is reflected at both ends of the tube, and the column of air inside vibrates in standing waves of its own. What changes is the rule at each end, which the MIT notes give for the back-and-forth movement of the air:

- At a **closed end** the air is stopped by the wall and cannot move: there is a **node** of the air's movement. (The pressure changes most there.)
- At an **open end** the pressure is held at the pressure of the room outside, and the air moves freely: there is an **antinode** of the air's movement.

A node and the next antinode are a quarter wavelength apart, half of one loop.

**A pipe open at both ends** has antinodes at both ends. Just as on a string, a whole number of half wavelengths must fit in the length $L$ of the pipe, and every harmonic is possible:

<Formula label="Pipe open at both ends" tex="\lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}, \qquad f_n = n\,\frac{v}{2L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots" cite="reading1" at="p. 5, case (C): k_m = mπ/L, with λ = 2π/k and f = v/λ" />

**A pipe closed at one end** has a node at the closed end and an antinode at the open end. Now the length must hold one quarter wavelength, or three quarters, or five quarters: an odd number of quarter wavelengths. Only the odd harmonics exist:

<Formula label="Pipe closed at one end" tex="\lambda_n = \frac{4L}{n}, \qquad f_n = n\,\frac{v}{4L}, \qquad n = 1, 3, 5, \dots" cite="reading1" at="pp. 4–5, case (B): k_m = (m − ½)π/L, so λ = 4L/(2m − 1); here n = 2m − 1" />

Here $v$ is the speed of sound in the air in the pipe. The fundamental of a pipe closed at one end, $f_1 = v/4L$, is half that of an open pipe of the same length: closing one end makes the note lower. A longer pipe plays lower notes, which is why organ pipes for the deepest notes are several metres long.

A flute and an organ pipe playing the same note still sound different. Each sounds its fundamental together with some higher harmonics (lesson 5.4), in different strengths, and this mix of harmonics gives a sound its **tone**, also called its **timbre**.

<WorkedExample title="Open or closed?">
<Problem>
A pipe is $0.60\ \text{m}$ long and the speed of sound in it is $343\ \text{m/s}$. Find its two lowest frequencies (a) when it is open at both ends, (b) when one end is closed.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Open at both ends">
$f_1 = v/2L = 343/(2 \times 0.60) = 286\ \text{Hz}$, and the next harmonic is $f_2 = 2f_1 = 572\ \text{Hz}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Closed at one end">
$f_1 = v/4L = 343/(4 \times 0.60) = 143\ \text{Hz}$. The second harmonic is missing; the next one is $f_3 = 3f_1 = 429\ \text{Hz}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Sound is a **longitudinal pressure wave** of compressions and rarefactions. It needs a medium and cannot cross empty space.
- Sound is fastest in solids and slowest in gases, and faster in warmer air: about $343\ \text{m/s}$ in air at 20 °C. An echo's delay $t$ gives the distance $v \times t/2$.
- **Pitch** is set by the frequency, **loudness** mainly by the amplitude. Humans hear about $20\ \text{Hz}$ to $20\ \text{kHz}$; above is **ultrasound**, used for scans, below is infrasound.
- **Intensity** is power per area, in $\text{W/m}^2$. From a point source $I = P/4\pi r^2$: twice as far, a quarter of the intensity.
- The **sound level** $\beta = 10\log(I/I_0)$ is in decibels: every $10\ \text{dB}$ is a factor of ten in intensity. Long exposure to $80\ \text{dB}$ or more can damage hearing.
- In pipes, a closed end is a node and an open end an antinode of the air's movement. Open at both ends: $\lambda_n = 2L/n$, all harmonics. Closed at one end: $\lambda_n = 4L/n$ with $n$ odd, only odd harmonics.
