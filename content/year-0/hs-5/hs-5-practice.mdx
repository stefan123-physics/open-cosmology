This set practises all thirteen lessons of Block 5, from the mass on a spring to the redshift of a galaxy. The problems are our own, of the kinds found in the exercises of Siyavula's chapters on waves, sound and light; the hints and solutions are our own too. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$, $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$ and a speed of sound of $343\ \text{m/s}$, unless a problem gives another value.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box wants the number only, in the unit printed after the box, for example `0.25` or `1.2e5`; small differences from rounding are accepted. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `(a + b)/c`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A spring and a pendulum in step" points={12}>

A block of mass $m = 0.30\ \text{kg}$ lies on a frictionless horizontal track and is attached to a spring with spring constant $k = 12\ \text{N/m}$. You pull the block $4.0\ \text{cm}$ from its equilibrium position and let go.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the period of the oscillation?

<Hint>

Which formula gives the period of a mass on a spring? Put the mass in kilograms.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$T =$" value="0.993" unit="s" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The period of a mass on a spring depends only on the mass and the spring constant, not on how far you pull.

<Step title="Angular frequency and period">

<Formula label="Period of a mass on a spring" tex="T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (19) and (49) combined" />

First $\omega = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{12/0.30} = \sqrt{40} = 6.32\ \text{rad/s}$, so $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi/6.32 = 0.993\ \text{s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units work out: $\sqrt{\text{kg}/(\text{N/m})} = \sqrt{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}/(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2)} = \text{s}$. About one second is what you would expect for a light block on a soft spring.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the largest speed of the block?

<Hint>

The amplitude is the distance you pulled the block. Where in the motion is the speed largest, and which formula gives it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$v_\text{max} =$" value="0.253" unit="m/s" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The speed is largest as the block passes the equilibrium position, and it equals the angular frequency times the amplitude.

<Step title="Largest speed">

<Formula label="Largest speed" tex="v_\text{max} = \omega A" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (58), second line" />

With $A = 0.040\ \text{m}$: $v_\text{max} = 6.32 \times 0.040 = 0.253\ \text{m/s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In one period the block travels $4A = 0.16\ \text{m}$ (out, back, out the other way and back), so its average speed is $0.16/0.993 = 0.161\ \text{m/s}$. The largest speed must be more than the average, and it is.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

A simple pendulum hangs next to the track. Find the length $\ell$ that makes the pendulum swing with exactly the same period as the block (small swings), in terms of $m$, $k$ and $g$.

<Hint>

Write down both periods and set them equal. The factor $2\pi$ appears on both sides.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$\ell =$" expr="m*g/k"
  vars={{ m: [0.1, 2], g: [1, 20], k: [1, 100] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "k*g/m", hint: "Check the units: a length in metres must come out. Which of m and k belongs above the line?" },
    { expr: "m/k", hint: "This has the unit s², not metres. Where does g come in?" },
    { expr: "k/(m*g)", hint: "Turned upside down: this has the unit 1/m, not metres. The pendulum's period has ℓ above the line, the spring's period has m above the line." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two periods are equal when the expressions under their square roots are equal.

<Step title="Set the periods equal">

<Formula label="Period of a pendulum (small angles)" tex="T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (84)" />

<Formula label="Pendulum in step with the spring" tex="2\pi\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ell = \frac{m g}{k}" cite="reading1" at="Eqs. (49) and (84), set equal (our own step)" />

For this block: $\ell = 0.30 \times 9.81/12 = 0.245\ \text{m}$, about $25\ \text{cm}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\text{kg} \cdot (\text{m/s}^2)/(\text{N/m}) = \text{N}/(\text{N/m}) = \text{m}$, a length as it should be. There is a nice meaning too: $mg/k$ is how far the spring would stretch if the block hung from it. A pendulum as long as that stretch keeps time with the spring.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

The block is replaced by one of twice the mass, $0.60\ \text{kg}$, and the pendulum's bob by one twice as heavy. What happens to the two periods?

<Answer id="p1-d" label="The periods:"
  choices={[
    "Both stay the same",
    "The block's period grows by a factor √2; the pendulum's period stays the same",
    "Both grow by a factor √2",
    "The block's period doubles; the pendulum's period stays the same"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Look for the mass in each formula.

<Step title="The spring">

$T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ grows with the square root of the mass: twice the mass gives $\sqrt{2}$ times the period, $0.993 \times \sqrt{2} = 1.40\ \text{s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The pendulum">

$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\ell/g}$ has no mass in it. A heavier bob feels a larger pull of gravity, but it is also harder to speed up, and the two effects cancel. The two oscillators are no longer in step.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Tuning a string" points={13}>

A string of a musical instrument is $0.70\ \text{m}$ long between its two fixed ends. It has a mass of $1.2\ \text{g}$ per metre and is stretched with a tension of $150\ \text{N}$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

How fast do waves travel along the string?

<Hint>

The wave speed on a string depends on the tension and on the mass per metre. Write the mass per metre in kilograms per metre.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$v =$" value="354" unit="m/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A tighter string pulls harder and a lighter string is easier to move: both make waves faster.

<Step title="Wave speed">

<Formula label="Wave speed on a string" tex="v = \sqrt{\frac{F}{\mu}}" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (15), where the notes write the tension as T" />

With $\mu = 1.2 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{kg/m}$: $v = \sqrt{150/(1.2 \times 10^{-3})} = \sqrt{1.25 \times 10^{5}} = 354\ \text{m/s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\sqrt{\text{N}/(\text{kg/m})} = \sqrt{(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2)\cdot\text{m}/\text{kg}} = \text{m/s}$. By chance this is close to the speed of sound in air.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the frequency of the fundamental (the lowest note of the string)?

<Hint>

In the fundamental, how many wavelengths fit between the fixed ends?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$f_1 =$" value="253" unit="Hz" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The fundamental is half a wavelength between the two fixed ends, so its wavelength is $2L$.

<Step title="Fundamental frequency">

<Formula label="Frequencies of a string fixed at both ends" tex="f_n = n\,\frac{v}{2L}" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (31), ω_n = nπv/L, divided by 2π" />

With $n = 1$: $f_1 = 354/(2 \times 0.70) = 354/1.40 = 253\ \text{Hz}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A wavelength of $1.40\ \text{m}$ at $253\ \text{Hz}$ gives $f\lambda = 253 \times 1.40 = 354\ \text{m/s}$, the wave speed of part (a). The note lies near the middle of a piano.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

The vibrating string makes a sound wave in the air with the same frequency as its fundamental. What is the wavelength of that sound in the air?

<Hint>

When a wave passes from the string into the air, which of frequency, speed and wavelength stays the same?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$\lambda =$" value="1.36" unit="m" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The air is pushed back and forth at the rhythm of the string, so the frequency stays the same. The speed is now the speed of sound, and the wavelength follows from $v = f\lambda$.

<Step title="Wavelength in air">

<Formula label="Wave speed" tex="v = f\lambda" cite="reading4" at="Sec. 8.7, the wave equation" />

$\lambda = v/f = 343/253 = 1.36\ \text{m}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The wavelength in air is a little shorter than the $1.40\ \text{m}$ on the string, because sound in air ($343\ \text{m/s}$) is a little slower than the wave on this string ($354\ \text{m/s}$). On a string with slower waves the two wavelengths would differ much more.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

The player wants the fundamental to have a chosen frequency $f$, by changing only the tension. Find the tension $F$ needed, in terms of $\mu$, $L$ and $f$.

<Hint>

Combine the formula for the fundamental with the formula for the wave speed, and solve for $F$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="$F =$" expr="4*mu*L^2*f^2"
  vars={{ mu: [0.0005, 0.01], L: [0.3, 1.2], f: [50, 1000] }}
  names={{ mu: "\\mu" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*mu*L*f", hint: "Nearly there, but the tension is not proportional to the frequency. Look at the square root in the wave speed." },
    { expr: "mu*L^2*f^2", hint: "Check the wavelength of the fundamental: is it L or 2L?" },
    { expr: "4*L^2*f^2/mu", hint: "From v = √(F/μ) the tension is F = μv². Is μ above or below the line?" }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The fundamental fixes the wave speed, $v = 2Lf$, and the wave speed fixes the tension.

<Step title="From frequency to tension">

<Formula label="Tension for a chosen fundamental" tex="f = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{F}{\mu}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F = \mu\,(2Lf)^2 = 4\mu L^2 f^2" cite="reading3" at="Eqs. (15) and (31) combined, solved for the tension (our own step)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $(\text{kg/m}) \cdot \text{m}^2 \cdot \text{s}^{-2} = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2 = \text{N}$. For this string and $f = 220\ \text{Hz}$: $F = 4 \times 1.2 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.70^2 \times 220^2 = 114\ \text{N}$, less than $150\ \text{N}$ because $220\ \text{Hz}$ is lower than $253\ \text{Hz}$. The tension grows with the square of the frequency: one octave higher (twice the frequency) needs four times the tension.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A pipe closed at one end" points={9}>

A narrow tube is closed at the bottom and open at the top. When you blow across the top, its lowest note has a frequency of $196\ \text{Hz}$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

How long is the air column in the tube?

<Hint>

At the closed end the air cannot move; at the open end it moves most. What fraction of a wavelength fits in the tube for the lowest note?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$L =$" value="0.438" unit="m" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For the lowest note, a quarter of a wavelength fits in a tube closed at one end, so $\lambda = 4L$.

<Step title="Length of the tube">

<Formula label="Pipe closed at one end" tex="f_n = n\,\frac{v}{4L}, \qquad n = 1, 3, 5, \dots" cite="mit803s11" at="pp. 4–5, case (B)" />

With $n = 1$: $L = v/(4f_1) = 343/(4 \times 196) = 343/784 = 0.438\ \text{m}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The wavelength is $4L = 1.75\ \text{m}$, and $196 \times 1.75 = 343\ \text{m/s}$, the speed of sound.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the next higher frequency at which this tube resonates?

<Hint>

Which harmonics can a tube closed at one end have?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$f_3 =$" value="588" unit="Hz" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p3-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A tube closed at one end has only the odd harmonics: the closed end must be a node and the open end an antinode.

<Step title="The next harmonic">

The second harmonic is missing, so the next one is the third: $f_3 = 3 \times 196 = 588\ \text{Hz}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

If you answered $392\ \text{Hz}$, you used the rule for a string or an open pipe. With $n = 3$ three quarters of a wavelength fit in the tube: still a node at the bottom and an antinode at the top.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Now the bottom is opened too, so the tube is open at both ends. What is its lowest frequency now?

<Answer id="p3-c" label="$f_1 =$" value="392" unit="Hz" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p3-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With two open ends there is an antinode at each end, and the lowest note fits half a wavelength in the tube.

<Step title="Open at both ends">

<Formula label="Pipe open at both ends" tex="f_n = n\,\frac{v}{2L}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots" cite="mit803s11" at="p. 5, case (C)" />

$f_1 = 343/(2 \times 0.438) = 392\ \text{Hz}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The wavelength is halved (from $4L$ to $2L$), so the frequency doubles: $2 \times 196 = 392\ \text{Hz}$, exactly one octave higher.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A warning siren" points={10}>

A warning siren on top of a tall mast sends out $8.0\ \text{W}$ of sound, equally in all directions. Ignore reflections from the ground and the absorption of sound by the air. The faintest sound a person can hear has an intensity of $I_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{W/m}^2$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the intensity of the sound $50\ \text{m}$ from the siren?

<Hint>

At a distance $r$ the sound power is spread over the surface of a sphere. How large is that surface?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$I =$" value="2.55e-4" unit="W/m²" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Intensity is power per square metre, and at distance $r$ the power is spread over a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$.

<Step title="Intensity">

<Formula label="Intensity at a distance from a point source" tex="I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2}" cite="nasaInverse" at="The Inverse Square Law (stated there for electromagnetic energy; the geometry is the same for sound)" />

$I = 8.0/(4\pi \times 50^2) = 8.0/31\,400 = 2.55 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{W/m}^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\text{W}/\text{m}^2$, as an intensity should be. The number is tiny compared with the siren's $8.0\ \text{W}$, because a sphere of radius $50\ \text{m}$ has a surface of more than $30\,000\ \text{m}^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the sound level there, in decibels?

<Hint>

Divide the intensity by $I_0$ first, then take the base-10 logarithm.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$\beta =$" value="84.1" unit="dB" tolerance="0.5" calc="p4-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The decibel scale counts factors of ten in intensity above the faintest audible sound.

<Step title="Sound level">

<Formula label="Sound level in decibels" tex="\beta = 10\log\left(\frac{I}{I_0}\right)" cite="openlearnDb" at="derived from Box 3 (every 10 dB is a factor 10 in intensity), with 0 dB at the threshold of hearing" />

$I/I_0 = 2.55 \times 10^{-4}/10^{-12} = 2.55 \times 10^{8}$, and $\log(2.55 \times 10^8) = 8.41$, so $\beta = 84.1\ \text{dB}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The intensity is between $10^8$ and $10^9$ times $I_0$, so the level must lie between $80$ and $90\ \text{dB}$: as loud as heavy traffic close by.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

How far from the siren has the sound level dropped to $60\ \text{dB}$, the level of a conversation? (This part takes two steps.)

<Hint>

Turn $60\ \text{dB}$ back into an intensity first: how many times $I_0$ is it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Then solve $I = P/(4\pi r^2)$ for $r$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c" label="$r =$" value="798" unit="m" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Go from decibels to intensity, then from intensity to distance.

<Step title="From decibels to intensity">

$60\ \text{dB}$ means $I/I_0 = 10^{60/10} = 10^6$, so $I = 10^6 \times 10^{-12} = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{W/m}^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="From intensity to distance">

<Formula label="Distance for a given intensity" tex="r = \sqrt{\frac{P}{4\pi I}}" cite="nasaInverse" at="The Inverse Square Law, solved for the distance (our own step)" />

$r = \sqrt{8.0/(4\pi \times 1.0 \times 10^{-6})} = \sqrt{6.37 \times 10^{5}} = 798\ \text{m}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

From $50\ \text{m}$ to $798\ \text{m}$ is a factor $16.0$ in distance, so a factor $16.0^2 = 255$ in intensity, and $10\log(255) = 24.1\ \text{dB}$. That is indeed the drop from $84.1$ to $60\ \text{dB}$. In practice the air and the ground absorb some sound, so the real distance is shorter.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A bat and a wall" points={10}>

A bat flies straight towards a wall at $v_b = 6.0\ \text{m/s}$ through still air. It sends out short calls at a frequency of $f_s = 40.0\ \text{kHz}$ and listens to the echo from the wall. Sound travels at $v = 343\ \text{m/s}$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

With what frequency does the call arrive at the wall?

<Hint>

For this step the wall is the listener, at rest, and the bat is a moving source. Does the source move towards the listener or away from it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="frequency at the wall:" value="40.7" unit="kHz" tolerance="0.2%" calc="p5-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A source moving towards a listener squeezes its waves together, so the listener receives a higher frequency.

<Step title="Moving source, listener at rest">

<Formula label="The Doppler formula for sound" tex="f_L = f_s\,\frac{v \pm v_L}{v \mp v_s}" cite="reading6" at="Sec. 6.2, the Doppler formula" />

Here $v_L = 0$ and the source moves towards the listener, so the upper sign (a minus) applies below the line: $f_L = 40.0 \times 343/(343 - 6.0) = 40.0 \times 343/337 = 40.7\ \text{kHz}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The frequency went up, as it should for a source coming closer, and only a little, because $6.0\ \text{m/s}$ is small compared with $343\ \text{m/s}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

The wall reflects the sound, and the bat hears the echo. Find the frequency $f_L$ of the echo that the bat hears, in terms of $f_s$, $v$ and $v_b$.

<Hint>

The wall sends back what it receives: it acts as a source at rest, with the frequency you found in part (a). Who moves now, and towards whom?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the Doppler formula twice: once from the bat to the wall, once from the wall back to the bat. Then multiply.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$f_L =$" expr="fs*(v + vb)/(v - vb)"
  vars={{ fs: [1000, 100000], v: [300, 400], vb: [1, 30] }}
  names={{ fs: "f_s", vb: "v_b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "fs*v/(v - vb)", hint: "That is what the wall receives. The bat is also moving towards the wall while it listens." },
    { expr: "fs*(v + vb)/v", hint: "That is only the second step. The call was already shifted on its way to the wall." },
    { expr: "fs*(v - vb)/(v + vb)", hint: "That is the echo of a bat flying away from the wall. Check the signs." },
    { expr: "fs*(v + 2*vb)/v", hint: "Adding the two shifts is only roughly right. Two Doppler factors in a row multiply." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The echo is shifted twice: once because the bat moves as a source, once because it moves as a listener.

<Step title="From the bat to the wall">

From part (a): the wall receives $f_s\,v/(v - v_b)$.

</Step>

<Step title="From the wall back to the bat">

The wall is now a source at rest that sends this frequency. The bat is a listener moving towards it, so the upper sign applies above the line, with $v_L = v_b$ and $v_s = 0$:

<Formula label="Echo heard by a moving bat" tex="f_L = \left(f_s\,\frac{v}{v - v_b}\right)\frac{v + v_b}{v} = f_s\,\frac{v + v_b}{v - v_b}" cite="reading6" at="Sec. 6.2, the Doppler formula applied twice (our own step)" />

For this bat: $f_L = 40.0 \times 349/337 = 41.4\ \text{kHz}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $v_b = 0$ the echo has the bat's own frequency, as it should. For a slow bat the shift is about $2v_b/v$ times $f_s$: $2 \times 6.0/343 \times 40.0 = 1.40\ \text{kHz}$, close to the exact $41.4 - 40.0$. Bats use this shift to tell how fast they approach an insect or a wall.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Later the bat flies straight away from the wall, again at $6.0\ \text{m/s}$, and still hears the echo of its calls. What frequency does it hear now?

<Answer id="p5-c" label="$f_L =$" value="38.6" unit="kHz" tolerance="0.2%" calc="p5-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both shifts now lower the frequency: the bat moves away from the wall as a source and as a listener.

<Step title="Change both signs">

$f_L = f_s\,(v - v_b)/(v + v_b) = 40.0 \times 337/349 = 38.6\ \text{kHz}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The shift is about as large as in part (b) but downwards: $40.0 - 38.6 = 1.4\ \text{kHz}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Bumps in the road" points={9}>

<Part label="a" points={3}>

<Answer id="p6-a" label="$f_0 =$" value="0.955" unit="Hz" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The body of the car on its springs is a mass on a spring.

<Step title="Natural frequency">

<Formula label="Natural angular frequency of a mass on a spring" tex="\omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (4), below the equation" />

$\omega_0 = \sqrt{3.6 \times 10^4/1000} = \sqrt{36} = 6.0\ \text{rad/s}$, so $f_0 = \omega_0/(2\pi) = 6.0/(2\pi) = 0.955\ \text{Hz}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

About one bounce per second, which is what a car feels like when you push down on it and let go.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

At what speed does the car bounce most?

<Hint>

The ridges push the car up at regular moments. How many ridges does the car cross per second at a given speed?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The bouncing is largest when the pushes come at the car's own rhythm.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-b" label="speed of the car:" value="11.5" unit="m/s" tolerance="2%" calc="p6-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The road drives the car's springs. The response is largest at resonance, when the driving frequency is close to the natural frequency.

<Step title="Resonance">

<Formula label="Frequency of largest amplitude" tex="\omega_\text{res} \approx \omega_0" cite="reading2" at="Eqs. (46)–(47), for weak damping" />

The car crosses one ridge every $12\ \text{m}$, so the number of ridges per second is its speed divided by $12\ \text{m}$. Setting that equal to $f_0$ gives the speed: $12 \times 0.955 = 11.5\ \text{m/s}$, about $41\ \text{km/h}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

The shock absorbers wear out, so the damping becomes even weaker. What changes?

<Answer id="p6-c" label="With weaker damping:"
  choices={[

    "The bouncing at the worst speed becomes smaller",
    "The worst speed moves to twice the value",
    "Nothing changes, because damping does not matter at resonance"
  ]}
  answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Damping takes energy away every cycle. Less damping gives a higher and narrower resonance peak.

<Step title="The resonance peak">

With weaker damping the amplitude at resonance grows, and the response falls off more sharply on either side of it: the worst speed stays near $11.5\ \text{m/s}$, but the bouncing there gets worse. That is why worn shock absorbers make a car unsafe on bumpy roads.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Exercise id="p7" title="Light in an optical fibre" points={12}>

What is the frequency of this light?

<Answer id="p7-a" label="$f =$" value="1.94e14" unit="Hz" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-a" />

<Solution>

<Step title="Frequency">

<Formula label="The wave equation for light" tex="c = f\lambda" cite="reading7" at="Sec. 11.2" />

$f = c/\lambda = 3.00 \times 10^8/(1550 \times 10^{-9}) = 1.94 \times 10^{14}\ \text{Hz}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Red light of $700\ \text{nm}$ has $4.3 \times 10^{14}\ \text{Hz}$. This light has a longer wavelength and a lower frequency: infrared, as stated.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

A ray in air hits the flat end of the fibre at an angle of incidence of $30.0^\circ$ (measured from the fibre's axis, which is the normal to the end face). At what angle to the axis does it travel inside the core?

Snell's law, with air as medium 1 and the core as medium 2.

</Hint>

<Solution>

<Step title="Snell's law">

$\sin\theta_2 = 1.00 \times \sin 30.0^\circ/1.50 = 0.500/1.50 = 0.333$, so $\theta_2 = 19.5^\circ$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$19.5^\circ$ is smaller than $30.0^\circ$: the ray bends towards the normal, as it must when it enters the optically denser core.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

What is the critical angle for light in the core that meets the boundary with the cladding?

<Answer id="p7-c" label="$\theta_c =$" value="69.0" unit="°" tolerance="0.3" calc="p7-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Total internal reflection is possible only from the larger to the smaller refractive index, here from the core into the cladding.

<Step title="Critical angle">

<Formula label="Critical angle" tex="\sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}" cite="reading8" at="Sec. 5.7, critical angle" />

Now medium 1 is the core and medium 2 the cladding: $\sin\theta_c = 1.40/1.50 = 0.933$, so $\theta_c = 69.0^\circ$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two indices are close, so the critical angle is large: only rays that meet the wall at a grazing angle are trapped. With the indices of real fibres it would be larger still.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Does the ray from part (b) stay inside the core when it meets the side wall?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-d" label="The ray:"
  choices={[

**The idea.** Compare the angle of incidence on the side wall with the critical angle.

<Step title="Angle on the side wall">

The ray makes $19.5^\circ$ with the axis, and the normal to the wall makes $90^\circ$ with the axis. Turned around: a ray stays trapped as long as it makes at most $90^\circ - 69.0^\circ = 21.0^\circ$ with the axis inside the core. Snell's law at the end face then allows an angle in air of up to $\sin^{-1}(1.50 \sin 21.0^\circ) = 32.6^\circ$, so the $30.0^\circ$ ray is just inside that limit.

**The idea.** Every wavelength is stretched by the same factor $1 + z$.

<Step title="Stretch the red line">

<Formula label="The redshift as a ratio of wavelengths" tex="1 + z = \frac{\lambda_\text{obs}}{\lambda_\text{em}}" cite="mit3" at="pp. 2–3" />

$\lambda_\text{obs} = 656.3 \times 1.0311 = 676.7\ \text{nm}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The red line moves by $20.4\ \text{nm}$, more than the $15.1\ \text{nm}$ of the blue-green line: the shift is the same fraction of each wavelength, so a longer wavelength moves further. This is how astronomers recognise the same pattern of lines in a shifted spectrum.
