Pull a mass on a spring a little to one side and let go. It slides back, overshoots, stops, comes back again, and keeps going back and forth. A child on a swing, a guitar string, the air in an organ pipe and the atoms in a crystal all do something similar. This lesson describes the simplest and most important kind of back-and-forth motion.

## Equilibrium and a restoring force

A mass $m$ on a frictionless table is attached to a spring. Where the spring is neither stretched nor squeezed, there is no net force: that is the **equilibrium position**. Measure the position $x$ from there. By Hooke's law, the spring pulls the mass back with a force proportional to how far it is displaced:

<Formula label="Hooke's law" tex="F = -kx" cite="mit" at="Eq. (15), with x measured from equilibrium" />

The minus sign is the whole story. Displace the mass to the right ($x > 0$) and the force points left; displace it to the left and the force points right. A force that always points back towards equilibrium is called a **restoring force**.

Newton's second law, $F = ma$, then gives the acceleration:

<Formula label="Acceleration under Hooke's law" tex="a = -\frac{k}{m}\,x" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (16)–(17)" />

When the acceleration is proportional to the displacement and points the opposite way, the motion is called **simple harmonic motion** (SHM). The notes call such a system a simple harmonic oscillator.

## Describing the motion: amplitude, period, frequency

Let the mass go from $x = A$ at time $t = 0$. It swings between $x = +A$ and $x = -A$ and repeats the same motion over and over.

- The **amplitude** $A$ is the largest displacement from equilibrium.
- The **period** $T$ is the time for one complete oscillation, there and back again.
- The **frequency** $f$ is the number of oscillations per second. Its unit is the **hertz** (Hz): $1\ \text{Hz} = 1\ \text{s}^{-1}$.

<Formula label="Frequency and period" tex="f = \frac{1}{T}" cite="siyavula10" at="Sec. 8.6, definitions of period and frequency" />

The position follows a cosine. One full cycle of a cosine is an angle of $2\pi$ radians, so the angle grows by $2\pi$ radians every period. That rate is the **angular frequency** $\omega$ (omega), in radians per second:

<Formula label="Angular frequency" tex="\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f" cite="mit" at="Eq. (49)" />

<Formula label="Position in simple harmonic motion" tex="x(t) = A\cos(\omega t)" cite="mit" at="Eq. (47) with phase φ = 0 (released from x = A at t = 0)" />

<Callout kind="note" title="Notation">
Dutch VWO textbooks often write $u$ for the displacement and $C$ for the spring constant, and start from a sine instead of a cosine. These are the same physics: a sine is a cosine shifted by a quarter period. This site uses $x$, $k$ and the cosine, as the university courses do.
</Callout>

## The period of a mass on a spring

Compare two formulas for the acceleration. The cosine motion has acceleration $a = -\omega^2 x$ (shown in the next section with a circle), and Hooke's law gives $a = -(k/m)x$. They agree when $\omega^2 = k/m$:

<Formula label="Angular frequency of a mass on a spring" tex="\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (19)" />

<Formula label="Period of a mass on a spring" tex="T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (19) and (49) combined" />

A heavier mass oscillates more slowly, and a stiffer spring makes it faster. The amplitude does not appear at all. Pull the mass twice as far and it has twice as far to go, but the spring pulls twice as hard, so it moves twice as fast and each oscillation takes exactly as long. This is why a spring, like the pendulum in the next lesson, can keep time.

<Callout kind="key">
In simple harmonic motion the period depends only on the system ($m$ and $k$), never on the amplitude.
</Callout>

## Velocity and acceleration: the shadow of a circle

The velocity is the slope of the position graph. The cosine is steepest where it crosses zero, so the mass moves fastest as it passes through equilibrium, and it is momentarily at rest at the turning points $x = \pm A$.

To find how fast, picture a point going round a circle of radius $A$ at constant angular speed $\omega$, as in uniform circular motion. After a time $t$ it has turned through the angle $\omega t$, so its $x$ coordinate is $A\cos(\omega t)$: exactly the position of the oscillating mass. Simple harmonic motion is the **shadow** of uniform circular motion on a line. That gives the velocity and acceleration at once:

- The point on the circle moves at speed $\omega A$ along the circle. Its shadow moves fastest when the point's velocity is parallel to the line, which happens as the shadow passes the centre: there the shadow's speed is the full $\omega A$.
- The point's acceleration has size $\omega^2 A$ and always points to the centre of the circle. Its part along the line is $-\omega^2 A\cos(\omega t) = -\omega^2 x$.

<Formula label="Largest speed" tex="v_\text{max} = \omega A" cite="mit" at="Eq. (58), second line: the velocity is −A₀ω₀ sin(ω₀t − φ), largest in size when the sine is ±1" />

<Formula label="Acceleration in simple harmonic motion" tex="a = -\omega^2 x, \qquad a_\text{max} = \omega^2 A" cite="mit" at="Eq. (20); a_max follows with x = A" />

The acceleration is largest at the turning points, where the spring is stretched or squeezed most, and zero at equilibrium.

<WorkedExample title="A block on a spring">
<Problem>
A $0.40\ \text{kg}$ block on a frictionless track is attached to a spring with $k = 25\ \text{N/m}$. It is pulled $5.0\ \text{cm}$ from equilibrium and released. Find the period, the frequency, the largest speed and the largest acceleration.
</Problem>
<Step title="Angular frequency">
$\omega = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{25/0.40} = \sqrt{62.5} = 7.91\ \text{rad/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Period and frequency">
$T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi/7.91 = 0.79\ \text{s}$, and $f = \omega/2\pi = 1.26\ \text{Hz}$ (dividing 1 by the rounded period, 0.79 s, would give 1.27 Hz: round only at the end).
</Step>
<Step title="Largest speed">
The amplitude is $A = 0.050\ \text{m}$, so $v_\text{max} = \omega A = 7.91 \times 0.050 = 0.40\ \text{m/s}$, reached as the block passes equilibrium.
</Step>
<Step title="Largest acceleration">
$a_\text{max} = \omega^2 A = 62.5 \times 0.050 = 3.1\ \text{m/s}^2$, at the turning points.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Measuring a spring constant with a stopwatch">
<Problem>
A $2.0\ \text{kg}$ mass hangs from a spring and bounces up and down. Ten oscillations take $5.0\ \text{s}$. What is the spring constant?
</Problem>
<Step title="Period">
$T = 5.0/10 = 0.50\ \text{s}$. Timing ten oscillations and dividing makes the result ten times less sensitive to your reaction time.
</Step>
<Step title="Rearrange the period formula">
Square $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ to get $T^2 = 4\pi^2 m/k$, so $k = 4\pi^2 m/T^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Calculate">
$k = 4\pi^2 \times 2.0/0.50^2 = 316\ \text{N/m}$, about $3.2 \times 10^2\ \text{N/m}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

A hanging spring oscillates about its new, stretched equilibrium position with the same period as on a table. Gravity adds the same constant force everywhere, so it only moves the point where the forces balance; measured from that new point, the net force is still $-kx$.

## Why it is everywhere

The MIT notes end with a striking result: almost any system that is pushed a little away from a **stable equilibrium**, a position it returns to when disturbed, feels a restoring force that is close to proportional to the displacement. So for small disturbances it oscillates in simple harmonic motion. A ball rolling in a bowl, a pendulum, a molecule whose atoms vibrate: all are simple harmonic oscillators as long as the swings stay small. That is why this one motion matters so much in physics.

## Try it

Hang different masses on the springs, change the spring constant, and time the oscillations. Check that the period does not change when you pull further, and that it grows with the square root of the mass.

## Summary

- A restoring force proportional to the displacement, $F = -kx$, produces **simple harmonic motion**: $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t)$.
- The amplitude $A$ is the largest displacement; the period $T$ is the time for one oscillation; the frequency is $f = 1/T$ in hertz; the angular frequency is $\omega = 2\pi f$.
- For a mass on a spring $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ and $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$, independent of the amplitude.
- The speed is largest at equilibrium, $v_\text{max} = \omega A$; the acceleration is $a = -\omega^2 x$, largest at the turning points.
- Small oscillations about any stable equilibrium are simple harmonic.
