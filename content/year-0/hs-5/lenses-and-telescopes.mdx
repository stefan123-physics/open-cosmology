A drop of water on a leaf makes the veins under it look larger. A pair of glasses lets someone read small print again. A camera, your own eye and every telescope that has looked at a distant galaxy work in the same way: a piece of curved glass, or a curved mirror, bends light so that it comes together again and forms an **image**. This lesson shows where that image lands and how big it is.

## Converging and diverging lenses

A **lens** is a piece of glass or clear plastic with curved surfaces. In lesson 5.9 you saw that light bends when it crosses from air into glass and back. At a curved surface, rays that hit different points meet the surface at different angles, so they bend by different amounts. The MIT notes call this positive power when the surfaces bend rays inwards, and negative power when they bend rays outwards.

- A **converging lens** is thicker in the middle than at the edges. Rays that arrive parallel to the line through the middle of the lens (the **optical axis**) are bent towards that line and all pass through one point behind the lens: the **focal point**.
- A **diverging lens** is thinner in the middle. It spreads parallel rays apart, as if they came from one point in front of the lens. That point is its focal point.

The distance from the centre of the lens to its focal point is the **focal length** $f$. A lens has a focal point on each side, at the same distance $f$ for a thin lens in air. The more strongly a lens bends light, the shorter its focal length. Opticians therefore describe a lens by its **strength** (also called its power), the inverse of the focal length in metres:

<Formula label="Strength of a lens" tex="P = \frac{1}{f}" cite="reading1" at="Lecture 4, slide 7: lens power in dioptres" />

The unit is the **dioptre**, one per metre. A converging lens has a positive focal length and a positive strength; for a diverging lens both are negative. Reading glasses of $+2.5$ dioptres are converging lenses with $f = 1/2.5 = 0.40\ \text{m}$; glasses of $-4.0$ dioptres are diverging lenses with $f = -0.25\ \text{m}$. Dutch books write the strength as S.

## Where the image forms

Put a small lamp, the **object**, in front of a converging lens. Every point of the lamp sends rays in all directions, and the lens bends the rays that hit it towards each other. Three rays from the top of the lamp are easy to follow:

1. A ray parallel to the axis is bent through the focal point behind the lens.
2. A ray through the focal point in front of the lens leaves the lens parallel to the axis.
3. A ray through the centre of a thin lens goes straight on.

Where these rays meet again, an **image** of the top of the lamp forms. Measure the **object distance** $d_o$ from the object to the lens and the **image distance** $d_i$ from the lens to the image. Similar triangles in this ray drawing lead to the **lens formula**, which the MIT slides call the imaging condition:

<Formula label="The lens formula" tex="\frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i}" cite="mit" at="Lecture 5, slide 2: imaging condition, written there with s_o and s_i" />

Dutch textbooks write the same formula with the letters v for the object distance (voorwerpsafstand) and b for the image distance (beeldafstand). To find $d_i$, first work out $1/d_i = 1/f - 1/d_o$, then take the inverse. A negative $d_i$ means that the image is on the same side of the lens as the object (more on this below). For a diverging lens, use a negative $f$.

The graph shows the lens formula at work for a converging lens. Move $f$ and watch the whole curve scale with it.

## Size, real and virtual images

The **magnification** $M$ compares the height of the image $h_i$ with the height of the object $h_o$. The ray through the centre of the lens shows that it equals the ratio of the distances:

<Formula label="Magnification" tex="M = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = -\frac{d_i}{d_o}" cite="mit" at="Lecture 5, slide 2: lateral magnification M_T" />

The size of $M$ tells you how many times larger the image is. The minus sign follows the MIT notes: a negative $M$ means that the image is upside down. Dutch books use the size only, as N = b/v.

The image can be of two kinds, as the MIT slides show for each case:

- A **real image** forms where the light really comes together, on the far side of the lens ($d_i > 0$). You can catch it on a screen or a camera sensor. It is always upside down. This happens when the object is further away than the focal point.
- A **virtual image** forms when the object is closer to a converging lens than its focal point ($d_i < 0$). The rays leaving the lens still spread apart and only seem to come from a point on the object's side. You cannot catch it on a screen, but your eye sees it when you look through the lens. It is upright and enlarged. A diverging lens always gives a virtual image, upright and smaller.

<WorkedExample title="Focusing a camera">
<Problem>
A camera has a lens with a focal length of $50\ \text{mm}$. It photographs a person $1.80\ \text{m}$ tall standing $3.0\ \text{m}$ away. Where must the sensor be, and how tall is the person on the sensor?
</Problem>
<Step title="Image distance">
$1/d_i = 1/f - 1/d_o = 1/0.050 - 1/3.0 = 20 - 0.33 = 19.67$ per metre, so $d_i = 0.0508\ \text{m} = 50.8\ \text{mm}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Magnification">
$M = -d_i/d_o = -0.0508/3.0 = -0.0169$. The image is upside down and $h_i = 0.0169 \times 1.80 = 0.0305\ \text{m} = 30.5\ \text{mm}$ tall, which fits on a sensor $36\ \text{mm}$ wide.
</Step>
<Step title="What focusing means">
For a very distant object the image would be at the focal point, $50.0\ \text{mm}$ behind the lens. To take a sharp photo of the person, the camera moves the lens about $0.85\ \text{mm}$ further from the sensor.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="A magnifying glass">
<Problem>
You look at a stamp through a magnifying glass of $+12.5$ dioptres, held $6.0\ \text{cm}$ above the stamp. Where is the image and how much larger does the stamp look?
</Problem>
<Step title="Focal length">
$f = 1/P = 1/12.5 = 0.080\ \text{m}$. The stamp is closer than the focal point, so expect a virtual image.
</Step>
<Step title="Image distance">
$1/d_i = 1/0.080 - 1/0.060 = 12.5 - 16.7 = -4.2$ per metre, so $d_i = -0.24\ \text{m}$: a virtual image $24\ \text{cm}$ from the lens, on the stamp's side.
</Step>
<Step title="Magnification">
$M = -d_i/d_o = -(-0.24)/0.060 = +4.0$: upright and four times as large.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The eye and the camera

In your **eye**, the curved front surface and the lens behind it together act as one converging lens. They form a real, upside-down image on the retina, the layer of light-sensitive cells at the back, and your brain turns it the right way round. The distance from lens to retina cannot change, so the eye focuses by changing its focal length instead: for a distant object the muscles around the lens are relaxed, and for a nearby object they contract, so that the lens becomes stronger (MIT Lecture 5, slide 12). Glasses add a lens of the right strength when the eye cannot do this well enough.

A **camera** works the other way round. Its lens has a fixed focal length and forms a real, upside-down, smaller image on the sensor; to focus on objects at different distances, the camera moves the lens, as in the worked example. An adjustable opening in the lens, the aperture, sets how much light is let through (Lecture 6, slide 3).

## Telescopes

A distant star or planet is so far away that its light arrives as parallel rays. A **refracting telescope** uses two converging lenses:

- The **objective**, a large lens with a long focal length $f_o$, forms a real image of the distant object at its focal point.
- The **eyepiece**, a small lens with a short focal length $f_e$, is used as a magnifying glass to look at that image. It is placed so that the image lies at its own focal point; the light then leaves the eyepiece as parallel rays that a relaxed eye can focus. The two lenses are $f_o + f_e$ apart.

A telescope does not make the Moon larger, it makes the **angle** it covers in your view larger. The ratio of that angle seen through the telescope to the angle seen with the naked eye is the **angular magnification**:

<Formula label="Angular magnification of a telescope" tex="M_a = \frac{f_o}{f_e}" cite="reading2" at="Lecture 8, slide 13: magnifying power, written there with a minus sign because the image is upside down" />

So a long objective and a short eyepiece give a large magnification. The image is upside down, which does not matter for looking at stars. The MIT slide therefore writes the magnifying power as $-f_o/f_e$: just as for $M$ above, the minus sign means that the image is upside down. This lesson uses only its size, as Dutch books do.

<WorkedExample title="A small refracting telescope">
<Problem>
A telescope has an objective with a focal length of $900\ \text{mm}$. You use an eyepiece with a focal length of $25\ \text{mm}$. What is the angular magnification, and how long is the telescope?
</Problem>
<Step title="Magnification">
$M_a = f_o/f_e = 0.900/0.025 = 36$: a crater on the Moon looks 36 times as wide as to the naked eye.
</Step>
<Step title="Length">
The lenses are $f_o + f_e = 0.900 + 0.025 = 0.925\ \text{m}$ apart. Swapping to a $10\ \text{mm}$ eyepiece gives $M_a = 90$ with almost the same tube.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Reflecting telescopes** replace the objective lens by a large curved mirror, the **primary mirror**. A hollow (concave) mirror brings parallel light together at a focal point just as a converging lens does. A smaller secondary mirror sends the light out to an eyepiece or a camera (Lecture 8, slide 14). Mirrors have a big advantage: the light does not pass through glass. In a lens, the amount of bending depends slightly on the colour of the light (dispersion, lesson 5.9), so the colours of a star come to a focus at slightly different places and the image gets coloured edges: chromatic aberration (Lecture 8, slide wk5-a-1). A mirror reflects every colour in the same way. All the largest telescopes are reflectors, including the Hubble Space Telescope, with a mirror $2.4\ \text{m}$ across, and the James Webb Space Telescope, whose mirror is built from segments whose positions can be adjusted.

## Why bigger is better

A telescope is above all a light bucket. All the light from a star that falls on the objective or the primary mirror ends up in the image, so the amount collected grows with the area of the opening, the **aperture**. A circle of diameter $D$ has an area of $\tfrac{1}{4}\pi D^2$, so the light collected is proportional to $D^2$: twice the width gives $2^2$ times the light. The symbol $\propto$ means "is proportional to":

<Formula label="Light collected by a telescope" tex="\text{light collected} \propto D^2" cite="webb" at="NASA: a larger mirror collects more light in a given time; the area of a circle grows with D²" />

A mirror twice as wide collects four times as much light. Faint, distant galaxies can only be seen with a big aperture. Webb's mirror has a collecting area of about $25\ \text{m}^2$, more than five times that of Hubble. Its mirror is made of hexagonal segments with a gap in the middle, so it is this area, not the width alone, that counts.

A wider aperture also shows **finer detail**: two stars very close together on the sky, or small features on a planet, stay separate instead of blurring into one. Why the size of the opening sets the sharpness has to do with light being a wave; it follows in lesson 5.11 and is worked out in "Where this leads".

<WorkedExample title="A telescope against your eye">
<Problem>
At night the pupil of your eye opens to about $7.0\ \text{mm}$. How much more light from a faint star does a telescope with an objective of $10\ \text{cm}$ collect?
</Problem>
<Step title="Ratio of the areas">
The light collected grows with $D^2$, so the ratio is $(0.10/0.0070)^2 = 14.3^2 = 204$. The telescope collects about 200 times as much light, so it shows stars far too faint for the naked eye.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A **converging lens** brings parallel light together at its **focal point**, a distance $f$ (the **focal length**) from the lens; a **diverging lens** spreads it out and has a negative $f$. The **strength** $P = 1/f$ is measured in dioptres.
- The **lens formula** $1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i$ gives the image distance (Dutch books: 1/f = 1/v + 1/b). The **magnification** is $M = h_i/h_o = -d_i/d_o$.
- An object beyond the focal point gives a **real**, upside-down image that can be caught on a screen; an object inside the focal point gives a **virtual**, upright, enlarged image (a magnifying glass).
- The eye focuses by changing the strength of its lens; a camera by moving its lens.
- A **refracting telescope** has an objective and an eyepiece, with angular magnification $M_a = f_o/f_e$. **Reflecting telescopes** use a curved mirror instead and avoid coloured edges.
- The light a telescope collects grows with $D^2$, and a wider aperture also sees finer detail: the reason telescopes keep getting bigger.
