Two ripples on a pond cross each other and carry on as if nothing happened. Two people talk at once and you still hear both voices. Waves can share the same space at the same time, and what happens where they overlap leads straight to the sound of every string and wind instrument.

## Superposition: waves add up

When two pulses or waves pass through the same point at the same time, the medium there is pushed by both at once. The rule is as simple as it could be: the displacements **add**. This is the **principle of superposition**. After they have passed through each other, each pulse continues in its original direction with its original shape, as if it had never met the other.

- When two crests (or two troughs) meet, the result is bigger than either: **constructive interference**. Two equal crests of height $A$ give a crest of $2A$ for an instant.
- When a crest meets a trough, they partly or wholly cancel: **destructive interference**. A crest and an equal trough give zero displacement at the moment they overlap.

<Formula label="Principle of superposition" tex="y = y_1 + y_2" cite="siyavula7" at="Sec. 7.3, principle of superposition" />

Superposition holds for waves on strings, sound and light, as long as the disturbances are not too large.

## Standing waves

Send a steady wave down a rope tied to a wall. At the wall it is reflected and comes back. Now two waves of the same frequency and amplitude run along the rope in opposite directions, and they add up everywhere. The result does not look like a travelling wave at all. The rope divides into loops that swing up and down in place:

- Some points never move at all. These are the **nodes**: there the two waves always cancel.
- Midway between them are points that swing with the largest amplitude, twice that of each wave. These are the **antinodes**.

This pattern is a **standing wave**. The MIT notes show that two equal waves travelling in opposite directions add up to exactly this: a fixed shape that grows and shrinks in time. Neighbouring nodes are half a wavelength apart, so each loop is $\lambda/2$ long.

To write this as a formula, describe a wave travelling to the right as $y = A\sin(kx - \omega t)$: at a fixed time it is a sine curve along the string, and as $t$ grows the pattern slides towards larger $x$. Here $\omega = 2\pi f$ as for an oscillation, and $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ does the same job in space: it is the **wavenumber**, the number of radians of the wave per metre. (This $k$ has nothing to do with the spring constant of lesson 5.1.) A wave travelling to the left has $kx + \omega t$ instead. Adding the two:

<Formula label="Standing wave from two travelling waves" tex="A\sin(kx - \omega t) + A\sin(kx + \omega t) = 2A\sin(kx)\cos(\omega t), \qquad k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 2.1, Eqs. (5)–(6); the notes use cosines with amplitude A/2 for each wave, here each wave has amplitude A, hence the factor 2" />

The factor $\sin(kx)$ fixes the shape and the nodes; the factor $\cos(\omega t)$ makes the whole shape swing up and down in time. You do not need to use this formula yourself: the plot shows the same thing.

## A string fixed at both ends

A guitar or violin string is held fixed at both ends, so both ends must be nodes. A standing wave only fits on the string if a whole number of loops, each half a wavelength long, fits exactly between the ends. With $L$ the length of the string, the allowed wavelengths are

<Formula label="Wavelengths of a string fixed at both ends" tex="\lambda_n = \frac{2L}{n}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots" cite="mit" at="Sec. 3.1.1, after Eq. (36)" />

With the wave equation $f = v/\lambda$ from the previous lesson, the allowed frequencies are

<Formula label="Frequencies of a string fixed at both ends" tex="f_n = n\,\frac{v}{2L}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (31), ω_n = nπv/L, divided by 2π" />

These special patterns are the **harmonics** (or normal modes) of the string. The lowest, $n = 1$, is the **fundamental**, also called the first harmonic: one loop, with $\lambda_1 = 2L$. The second harmonic has two loops and twice the frequency, the third has three loops and three times the frequency, and so on. A plucked string vibrates in several of its harmonics at once; their mixture gives each instrument its own sound.

The speed of waves on a string depends on how tight it is and how heavy it is. With the tension $F$ (in newtons) and the mass per metre $\mu$ (mu, in kg/m):

<Formula label="Wave speed on a string" tex="v = \sqrt{\frac{F}{\mu}}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (15), where the notes write the tension as T" />

A tighter string carries faster waves and plays a higher note; a thicker, heavier string carries slower waves and plays a lower one. Pressing a guitar string against a fret makes the vibrating length $L$ shorter, which raises every harmonic.

<WorkedExample title="The notes of a guitar string">
<Problem>
A guitar string is $0.65\ \text{m}$ long between its fixed ends, and waves travel along it at $286\ \text{m/s}$. Find the frequencies of its first three harmonics.
</Problem>
<Step title="Fundamental">
$f_1 = v/2L = 286/(2 \times 0.65) = 220\ \text{Hz}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Higher harmonics">
$f_2 = 2f_1 = 440\ \text{Hz}$ and $f_3 = 3f_1 = 660\ \text{Hz}$. The wavelengths are $\lambda_1 = 1.30\ \text{m}$, $\lambda_2 = 0.65\ \text{m}$ and $\lambda_3 = 0.43\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Tuning by tension">
<Problem>
A string with a mass of $5.0\ \text{g}$ per metre is stretched with a tension of $80\ \text{N}$ between two points $0.40\ \text{m}$ apart. What is its fundamental frequency?
</Problem>
<Step title="Wave speed">
$\mu = 0.0050\ \text{kg/m}$, so $v = \sqrt{F/\mu} = \sqrt{80/0.0050} = 126\ \text{m/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Fundamental">
$f_1 = v/2L = 126/(2 \times 0.40) = 158\ \text{Hz}$. To double the frequency with the same string and length you would need four times the tension, $320\ \text{N}$, because the speed grows only with the square root of the tension.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- **Superposition:** where waves overlap, their displacements add. Crest on crest gives constructive interference; crest on trough gives destructive interference. Afterwards each wave continues unchanged.
- Two equal waves travelling in opposite directions make a **standing wave**: **nodes** that never move and **antinodes** that move most, half a wavelength apart.
- On a string of length $L$ fixed at both ends, only whole numbers of half wavelengths fit: $\lambda_n = 2L/n$ and $f_n = n\,v/2L$. These are the harmonics; the lowest, $n = 1$, is the fundamental (the first harmonic).
- Standing waves of sound in air columns, as in flutes and organ pipes, follow in lesson 5.5.
- The wave speed on a string is $v = \sqrt{F/\mu}$: tighter or lighter strings give higher notes.
