In lesson 5.6 a siren sounded lower once the ambulance had passed and was driving away. Light is a wave too (lesson 5.8), so you might expect the same to happen with light: a lamp moving away should look a little "lower", that is, redder. It does. This small effect turned out to be one of the most important measurements in all of astronomy. It tells us how fast stars and galaxies move, and it gave the first clue that the whole universe is expanding.

## The Doppler effect for light

Think of a light source that sends out wave crests at a steady rate. If it moves **away** from you, each new crest starts a little farther away than the one before, so the crests arrive spread out: the wavelength you measure is **longer** than the one the source sent out, and the frequency is lower ($c = f\lambda$, lesson 5.8). For visible light a longer wavelength means a shift towards the red end of the spectrum, so this is called a **redshift**. A source moving **towards** you bunches its crests up: a shorter wavelength, shifted towards blue, a **blueshift**.

The word "redshift" is used for every part of the spectrum, not only for visible light. A radio wave or an X-ray that arrives with a longer wavelength is also "redshifted", even though nothing turns red.

Two differences with sound matter here. First, light needs no medium (lesson 5.8), so there is no air to measure motion against: only the motion of source and observer relative to each other counts. Second, only the part of the motion **along the line of sight**, straight towards or away from us, changes the wavelength. A star moving sideways across the sky shows (almost) no shift.

## Measuring the shift: the redshift z

Astronomers describe the shift with one number, the **redshift** $z$: the fraction by which the wavelength has grown. Let $\lambda_\text{em}$ be the wavelength the source emitted (as measured by someone moving with it) and $\lambda_\text{obs}$ the wavelength we observe. Then

<Formula label="The redshift" tex="z = \frac{\lambda_\text{obs} - \lambda_\text{em}}{\lambda_\text{em}}" cite="mit3" at="pp. 2–3" />

Adding 1 to both sides gives a form that is often handier, and that you will meet at university:

<Formula label="The redshift as a ratio of wavelengths" tex="1 + z = \frac{\lambda_\text{obs}}{\lambda_\text{em}}" cite="mit3" at="pp. 2–3" />

So $z = 0$ means no shift at all, a positive $z$ is a redshift, and a negative $z$ is a blueshift. Because $z$ is a fraction, it has no unit, and it is the same for every wavelength in the light of that source: if one wavelength is stretched by 1 %, they all are.

## From redshift to speed

How large is the shift for a given speed? For speeds much smaller than the speed of light, the answer is simple: the wavelength changes by the same fraction as the speed is of $c$.

<Formula label="Redshift and speed, for v much smaller than c" tex="z \approx \frac{v}{c}" cite="mit3" at="pp. 4–5 (slow motion, z ≈ v/u) and pp. 15–19 (relativistic formula, which for small β gives z ≈ β)" />

Here $v$ is the speed along the line of sight, positive away from us and negative towards us. For slow sources this is the same result you would get from the sound formula of lesson 5.6 with a moving source, with $c$ in place of the speed of sound. For fast sources the exact formula needs Einstein's special relativity (Block 8), and for light from very distant galaxies even that is not the whole story, as you will see below. In this lesson all speeds are small compared with $c = 3.00 \times 10^{5}\ \text{km/s}$, so the approximation is good.

## Fingerprints of elements: spectral lines

There is a catch. To measure a shift you need to know $\lambda_\text{em}$: what wavelength did the star actually send out? A star sends out a whole rainbow of wavelengths, so how can you tell which one moved where?

The answer is in **spectral lines**. A hot, thin gas of one element does not glow at all wavelengths. It gives light only at a few sharp wavelengths of its own, and each element has its own pattern of them, like a fingerprint. Hydrogen, for example, has visible lines at about $410$, $434$, $486$ and $656\ \text{nm}$. A cooler gas in front of a light source absorbs exactly the same wavelengths, so in starlight these lines usually appear as dark lines in the rainbow. Spread starlight into a spectrum with a prism or grating and these lines show up. Astronomers recognise the pattern of an element, compare it with the same lines measured in a laboratory and read off how far the whole pattern has moved. Why atoms give sharp lines, and what else they reveal about stars, is the subject of Block 7 (lessons 7.4 and 7.5).

<WorkedExample title="A star coming closer">
<Problem>
In the laboratory the red hydrogen line lies at $656.3\ \text{nm}$. In the spectrum of a star it is measured at $656.1\ \text{nm}$. How fast does the star move along our line of sight, and in which direction?
</Problem>
<Step title="The redshift">
$z = \dfrac{656.1 - 656.3}{656.3} = \dfrac{-0.2}{656.3} = -3.0 \times 10^{-4}$. It is negative: a blueshift.
</Step>
<Step title="The speed">
$v \approx zc = -3.0 \times 10^{-4} \times 3.00 \times 10^{5}\ \text{km/s} = -90\ \text{km/s}$. The star comes towards us at about $90\ \text{km/s}$, fast by everyday standards but tiny compared with $c$, so the approximation is excellent.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with another line">
The blue-green line at $486.1\ \text{nm}$ must move by the same fraction: $486.1 \times (1 - 3.0 \times 10^{-4}) = 485.95\ \text{nm}$, a shift of only $0.15\ \text{nm}$. Finding the same $z$ in every line is how astronomers know the shift is real.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Shifts like these are tiny, a fraction of a nanometre, but modern spectrographs measure them easily. They show stars orbiting each other, and the small wobble a planet gives its star.

## Hubble's discovery: the galaxies rush away

At the end of the 1920s Edwin Hubble compared the redshifts of galaxies with their distances. Almost all of them were redshifted: they move away from us. And the farther away a galaxy was, the larger its redshift. In a simple formula, the speed of a galaxy is proportional to its distance $d$:

<Formula label="Hubble's law" tex="v = H_0\,d" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 6.3, The expanding universe" />

The constant $H_0$ is the **Hubble constant**. Astronomers give distances to galaxies in **megaparsecs** (Mpc): one megaparsec is about $3.3$ million light-years (a light-year is the distance light travels in a year, lesson 1.11), so $H_0$ is written in km/s per Mpc. Measurements give values close to $70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$ (the Planck satellite's measurement of 2013 gave about $67\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$), and this lesson uses the round value $70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$: for every megaparsec of distance, a galaxy moves away about $70\ \text{km/s}$ faster.
<WorkedExample title="How far is the galaxy?">
<Problem>
In the spectrum of a galaxy the red hydrogen line ($656.3\ \text{nm}$) is found at $672.7\ \text{nm}$. How fast does the galaxy move away, and how far away is it? Take $H_0 = 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The redshift">
$z = \dfrac{672.7 - 656.3}{656.3} = \dfrac{16.4}{656.3} = 0.025$.
</Step>
<Step title="The speed">
$v \approx zc = 0.025 \times 3.00 \times 10^{5}\ \text{km/s} = 7500\ \text{km/s}$. (The exact formula of special relativity gives a speed only about 1 % lower: $z$ is still small.)
</Step>
<Step title="The distance">
From Hubble's law, $d = \dfrac{v}{H_0} = \dfrac{7500}{70}\ \text{Mpc} = 107\ \text{Mpc}$, or about $350$ million light-years.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Why would every galaxy run away from *us*? We are not at a special centre. Picture raisins in a rising loaf of bread: as the dough swells, every raisin moves away from every other one, and from any raisin the others seem to recede, faster the farther they are. The galaxies behave in the same way. Hubble's law is exactly what you see from any galaxy in a universe that is **expanding** as a whole.

## Stretched by space itself

For nearby galaxies, reading the redshift as a Doppler shift from motion works well. For distant galaxies there is a better way to see it. In an expanding universe, the space between the galaxies grows while the light is on its way to us, and the light wave is stretched along with it. Its crests are pulled apart, so it arrives with a longer wavelength. This is the **cosmological redshift**.

Seen this way, $1 + z$ has a beautiful meaning: it is the factor by which the universe has grown while the light travelled. Light from a galaxy at $z = 0.025$ left when all distances between galaxies were $1/1.025$ of today's, about 2.4 % smaller. A galaxy at $z = 2$ sent out the light we now see when distances between galaxies were a third of today's. Such a redshift cannot be read as a speed through space: $z \approx v/c$ would give twice the speed of light. The low-speed rule simply does not apply here; it is space that has stretched.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Where 8.286 starts">
The technical part of the university course on the early universe (MIT 8.286) begins here. It first derives the Doppler shift of light from special relativity, with the same definition $1 + z = \lambda_\text{obs}/\lambda_\text{em}$, and then shows that in an expanding universe every wavelength grows in step with the universe itself. Measuring redshifts, and comparing them with distances, is how cosmologists learn how fast the universe expands and how that expansion has changed over billions of years.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Light shows a Doppler effect: a source moving **away** gives longer wavelengths (**redshift**), one moving **towards** us shorter wavelengths (**blueshift**). Only the motion along the line of sight counts.
- The **redshift** is $z = (\lambda_\text{obs} - \lambda_\text{em})/\lambda_\text{em}$, or $1 + z = \lambda_\text{obs}/\lambda_\text{em}$; it has no unit and is the same for every wavelength of the source.
- For speeds much smaller than the speed of light, $z \approx v/c$, so a measured redshift gives the speed along the line of sight.
- Each element gives light at its own sharp wavelengths, its **spectral lines**. Astronomers recognise the pattern and measure how far it has shifted (more in Block 7).
- **Hubble's law**: distant galaxies are redshifted, the more so the farther away they are, $v = H_0\,d$ with $H_0 \approx 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$. This was the first strong evidence that the universe is expanding.
- For distant galaxies the redshift is best understood as the **stretching of space**: $1 + z$ is how much the universe has grown while the light travelled. That is where the university course 8.286 begins.
