You can hear someone talking in the next room through an open door, even when you cannot see them. Sound bends round the edge of the doorway; light, it seems, does not. In 1801 Thomas Young showed that light does the same thing as sound, only on a far smaller scale, and in doing so proved that light is a wave. This lesson explains the two effects behind his experiment: **diffraction** and **interference**.

## Wavefronts and Huygens' principle

Drop a stone in a pond and circles of crests spread out. A line along one crest, or along any set of points that are in step (in phase), is a **wavefront**. Near the stone the wavefronts are circles; far away, or behind a long straight paddle in a ripple tank, they are almost straight lines.

How does a wavefront move on? In the seventeenth century Christiaan Huygens gave a simple rule, **Huygens' principle**: every point of a wavefront acts as a tiny source that sends out its own small circular waves (wavelets). A moment later, the new wavefront is the line that touches all these wavelets. For a straight wavefront the wavelets along it together give a new straight wavefront a little further on, and the wave carries on in a straight line. The rule works just as well for curved wavefronts.

## Diffraction

Now put a barrier with a gap in the path of straight water waves. Only the part of the wavefront that reaches the gap gets through. The points in the gap still send out wavelets in all directions, but the wavelets that would have kept the edges of the wavefront straight have been blocked. So behind the gap the wavefront bends round into the shadow of the barrier and spreads out. A wave also bends round the edge of an obstacle in the same way. This spreading is called **diffraction**.

How much a wave spreads depends on the size of the gap compared with the wavelength. A gap much wider than the wavelength lets the wave through almost unchanged, with only a little bending at the edges. A gap about as wide as one wavelength, or narrower, turns the wave into almost circular wavefronts, as if the gap were a new point source. Siyavula sums this up as

<Formula label="Amount of diffraction" tex="\text{diffraction} \propto \frac{\lambda}{a}" cite="reading3" at="Sec. 6.5, Effect of slit width and wavelength (Siyavula writes the slit width as w)" />

with $a$ the width of the gap or slit. A longer wavelength or a narrower gap gives more diffraction.

This is why you hear round corners but do not see round them. The sound of a voice has wavelengths from tens of centimetres up to a few metres, not far from the width of a door, so it spreads out strongly. Visible light has wavelengths between about $400$ and $700\ \text{nm}$ (lesson 5.8), roughly a million times smaller than a door, so it hardly spreads at all and leaves a sharp shadow. To see light diffract you need a slit a fraction of a millimetre wide.

## Interference of waves from two sources

In lesson 5.4 you saw that where two waves overlap their displacements add up: crest on crest gives **constructive interference**, crest on trough gives **destructive interference**. Now take two sources in two dimensions, such as two dippers that tap the water of a ripple tank at the same frequency and in step, or two loudspeakers fed by the same signal.

At any point P, the wave from source 1 has travelled a distance $r_1$ and the wave from source 2 a distance $r_2$. What matters is the **path difference** $r_2 - r_1$:

- If it is a whole number of wavelengths, the two waves arrive in step: crest with crest, trough with trough. They reinforce each other.
- If it is an odd number of half wavelengths, a crest of one arrives together with a trough of the other. They cancel.

<Formula label="Constructive interference (sources in step)" tex="r_2 - r_1 = n\lambda, \qquad n = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots" cite="mit802" at="Sec. 14.2, Eq. (14.2.5), with the path difference δ = r₂ − r₁ (MIT writes the whole number as m)" />

<Formula label="Destructive interference (sources in step)" tex="r_2 - r_1 = \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\lambda, \qquad n = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots" cite="mit802" at="Sec. 14.2, Eq. (14.2.6)" />

In a ripple tank you see lines of calm water fanning out between lines of strong waves. With two loudspeakers you can walk across the room and hear the sound get louder and softer. For a steady pattern the two sources must have the same frequency and keep a fixed step with each other; the MIT notes call such sources **coherent**. Two separate light bulbs are not coherent: each sends out a jumble of short bursts of many wavelengths, so their light never shows a fixed pattern.

<WorkedExample title="Two loudspeakers">
<Problem>
Two loudspeakers play the same note of $686\ \text{Hz}$, in step. The speed of sound is $343\ \text{m/s}$. You stand $3.00\ \text{m}$ from one speaker and $3.75\ \text{m}$ from the other. Is the note loud or quiet where you stand?
</Problem>
<Step title="Wavelength">
$\lambda = v/f = 343/686 = 0.500\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Path difference">
$r_2 - r_1 = 3.75 - 3.00 = 0.75\ \text{m} = 1.5\lambda$: an odd number of half wavelengths. The waves arrive half a cycle out of step and largely cancel: the note is quiet. A step to a place where the difference is $1.00\ \text{m} = 2\lambda$ brings you to a loud spot.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Young's double slit: light is a wave

In Young's experiment, light from one narrow slit falls on two narrow slits close together. Because both slits are lit by the same wavefront, they act as two coherent sources. Today a laser does the job: its light has one wavelength and stays in step. Each slit diffracts the light, so the light from the two slits spreads out and overlaps. On a screen behind the slits you do not see two bright lines, as you would if light travelled only in straight lines, but a row of bright and dark stripes, called **fringes**. Dark fringes, places where light plus light gives darkness, can only be explained if light is a wave.

Let the slits be a distance $d$ apart and the screen a distance $L$ away, much further than $d$. Then the paths from the two slits to a point on the screen are almost parallel. Look at a point in the direction at an angle $\theta$ from straight ahead. A little right-angled triangle at the slits shows that the light from one slit travels an extra $d\sin\theta$ (lesson 0.7). Bright fringes appear where this extra path is a whole number of wavelengths:

<Formula label="Bright fringes of a double slit" tex="d\sin\theta = n\lambda, \qquad n = 0, \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots" cite="mit802" at="Sec. 14.2, Eqs. (14.2.4)–(14.2.5)" />

The central fringe, $n = 0$, is straight ahead; $n = \pm 1$ are the first fringes on either side, and so on. The angles are small, because $\lambda$ is much smaller than $d$. For small angles $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta = y/L$, where $y$ is the distance on the screen from the centre of the pattern. The bright fringes then lie at $y = n\lambda L/d$, evenly spaced, with a **fringe spacing**

<Formula label="Fringe spacing (small angles)" tex="\Delta y = \frac{\lambda L}{d}" cite="mit802" at="Sec. 14.2, Eqs. (14.2.7)–(14.2.8), y_b = mλL/d" />

A longer wavelength, a screen further away or slits closer together all spread the fringes out. Measuring $\Delta y$, $L$ and $d$ gives the wavelength of light, and shows that light waves are less than a thousandth of a millimetre long.

The brightness between the fringes changes smoothly. Adding the two waves, the MIT notes find the intensity $I$ (the power per square metre) at a distance $y$ from the centre of the screen:

<Formula label="Intensity of the double-slit pattern (small angles)" tex="I = I_0\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi d y}{\lambda L}\right)" cite="mit802" at="Sec. 14.3, Eq. (14.3.15)" />

Here $I_0$ is the intensity of the brightest fringes. You do not need to use this formula yourself; the plot shows what it means.

<WorkedExample title="Fringes from a laser pointer">
<Problem>
A red laser of wavelength $635\ \text{nm}$ shines on two slits $0.30\ \text{mm}$ apart. The screen is $2.5\ \text{m}$ away. Find the angle of the first bright fringe beside the centre, and the distance between neighbouring bright fringes.
</Problem>
<Step title="Angle of the first fringe">
With $n = 1$: $\sin\theta = \lambda/d = (635 \times 10^{-9})/(0.30 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.1 \times 10^{-3}$, so $\theta = 0.12^\circ$. A tiny angle, as expected.
</Step>
<Step title="Fringe spacing">
$\Delta y = \lambda L/d = (635 \times 10^{-9} \times 2.5)/(0.30 \times 10^{-3}) = 5.3 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 5.3\ \text{mm}$. Easy to see on a wall.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="Measuring a wavelength">
<Problem>
With slits $0.25\ \text{mm}$ apart and a screen $1.80\ \text{m}$ away, a student measures $39.6\ \text{mm}$ from the first to the eleventh bright fringe. What is the wavelength of the light?
</Problem>
<Step title="Fringe spacing">
From the first to the eleventh fringe there are 10 spacings: $\Delta y = 39.6/10 = 3.96\ \text{mm}$. Measuring across many fringes makes the result more precise.
</Step>
<Step title="Wavelength">
Rearranged: $\lambda = \Delta y\, d/L = (3.96 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.25 \times 10^{-3})/1.80 = 5.5 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 550\ \text{nm}$: yellowish green light.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## One slit and many slits

A single slit of width $a$ also gives a pattern of bright and dark bands, because the wavelets from different parts of the same slit interfere. Pair each point in the upper half of the slit with the point half a slit width below it. In the direction where those two paths differ by half a wavelength, every pair cancels, and the screen is dark. The MIT notes extend this argument to find all the dark places:

<Formula label="Dark places behind a single slit" tex="a\sin\theta = n\lambda, \qquad n = \pm 1, \pm 2, \dots" cite="mit802" at="Sec. 14.5, Eq. (14.5.4); Siyavula, Sec. 6.5 (extension), writes sin θ = mλ/w" />

Between the first dark places on either side lies a wide central bright band, with fainter bands beyond. A narrower slit pushes the dark places outwards: the light spreads more, just as $\lambda/a$ predicts.

<WorkedExample title="One narrow slit">
<Problem>
The same red laser, $635\ \text{nm}$, now shines through one slit $0.050\ \text{mm}$ wide onto a screen $2.0\ \text{m}$ away. How wide is the central bright band?
</Problem>
<Step title="First dark place">
$\sin\theta = \lambda/a = (635 \times 10^{-9})/(0.050 \times 10^{-3}) = 0.0127$. The angle is small, so the dark place is $y = L\sin\theta = 2.0 \times 0.0127 = 0.0254\ \text{m}$ from the centre.
</Step>
<Step title="Width of the band">
The band runs from the dark place on one side to the one on the other: $2 \times 2.54 = 5.1\ \text{cm}$ wide, a thousand times wider than the slit itself.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

A **diffraction grating** is a plate with hundreds of narrow, evenly spaced slits per millimetre. Light from neighbouring slits again reinforces where $d\sin\theta = n\lambda$, with $d$ now the distance between neighbouring slits (MIT notes, Eq. 14.8.1). With so many slits, the bright lines become very sharp and the dark gaps between them wide. Each colour comes out at its own angle, so a grating spreads light into a spectrum, and because its lines are so sharp it measures wavelengths precisely (MIT notes, Sec. 14.8). Astronomers use gratings to split the light of stars and galaxies into its wavelengths.

## Why telescopes have a limit

A telescope's opening is a round "gap" for the light that enters it, so the light of a star is diffracted too. Instead of a perfect point, the image of a star is a tiny bright disc with faint rings around it. Two stars that lie very close together on the sky give two discs that overlap and blur into one. The MIT notes find that a round opening of diameter $D$ can just separate two points when they are at least an angle $\theta_R$ apart, with

<Formula label="Smallest angle a round opening can resolve" tex="\sin\theta_R \approx 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D}" cite="mit803" at="p. 6, circular aperture" />

For these small angles $\sin\theta_R \approx \theta_R$ in radians. This is the reason promised in lesson 5.10: a wider mirror does not only collect more light, it also shows finer detail, because a larger $D$ gives a smaller $\theta_R$.

<WorkedExample title="Eye against telescope">
<Problem>
Compare the finest detail an eye with a pupil of $7.0\ \text{mm}$ and a telescope with an objective of $10\ \text{cm}$ can resolve, for yellow-green light of $550\ \text{nm}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The eye">
$\theta_R \approx 1.22 \times (550 \times 10^{-9})/(7.0 \times 10^{-3}) = 9.6 \times 10^{-5}$ radians.
</Step>
<Step title="The telescope">
$\theta_R \approx 1.22 \times (550 \times 10^{-9})/0.10 = 6.7 \times 10^{-6}$ radians: about 14 times finer, the same factor as the ratio of the diameters.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A **wavefront** joins points of a wave that are in phase. **Huygens' principle**: every point of a wavefront sends out small wavelets; the new wavefront touches them all.
- **Diffraction** is the spreading of a wave behind a gap or round an obstacle. It is strong when the gap is about as wide as the wavelength: diffraction $\propto \lambda/a$. Light, with its tiny wavelength, only shows it with very narrow slits.
- Two coherent sources give **interference**: loud or bright where the path difference is $n\lambda$, quiet or dark where it is $(n + \tfrac12)\lambda$.
- **Young's double slit**: bright fringes at $d\sin\theta = n\lambda$, spaced $\Delta y = \lambda L/d$ on a screen at distance $L$. The dark fringes prove that light is a wave, and the spacing measures its wavelength.
- A single slit of width $a$ has dark places at $a\sin\theta = n\lambda$; a grating sends each colour to its own angle.
- Diffraction limits every telescope: it can separate two points only about $1.22\lambda/D$ apart in angle, so a wider telescope sees sharper.
