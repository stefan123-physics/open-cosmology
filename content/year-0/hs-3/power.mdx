Lesson 3.1 defined **work**, $W = F\,\Delta x \cos\theta$, the energy a force transfers to an object as it moves, and lessons 3.2 to 3.4 followed that energy as kinetic energy $E_k$ and gravitational potential energy $E_p$. None of those lessons asked *how fast* the work was done. Carry a box of books up two floors in one minute or in five: the books end up at the same height with the same gain in $E_p$, so you have done the same work. But the quick trip leaves you out of breath and the slow one does not. The difference is the **power**: how much work is done each second.

## Power is the rate of doing work

**Power** is the rate at which work is done, or, which is the same thing, the rate at which energy is transferred from one object or form to another. If an amount of work $W$ is done in a time $\Delta t$, the power is

<Formula label="Power" tex="P = \frac{W}{\Delta t}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 13.7, Eq. (13.7.2), p. 13-18 (average power, the rate at which work is done, written there P_ave = W/Δt)" />

Like work and energy, power is a **scalar**: it has a size but no direction. It is positive when a force puts energy into an object and negative when a force takes energy out, as friction does (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 13.7, p. 13-18 and Example 13.9, Eq. (13.7.17), p. 13-21). Because work is an energy transfer, the same formula holds for any transfer of energy, also when nothing is pushed or lifted: the energy a heater gives off per second, or the light a lamp sends out per second, are powers too.

Two machines that do the same job differ in power only through the time they take. A machine with twice the power does the same work in half the time.

## The watt

With the work in joules and the time in seconds, power is measured in joules per second. This unit is called the **watt** (W), after the Scottish engineer James Watt, whose improved steam engines helped drive the Industrial Revolution:

<Formula label="The watt" tex="1\ \text{W} = 1\ \frac{\text{J}}{\text{s}} = 1\ \frac{\text{kg}\,\text{m}^{2}}{\text{s}^{3}}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 13.7, p. 13-18 (SI unit of power, [1 W] = [1 J·s⁻¹]); the base units follow from 1 J = 1 kg m²/s² (lesson 3.2)" />

The prefixes of lesson 0.1 work as usual: 1 kilowatt (kW) is 1000 W and 1 megawatt (MW) is $10^{6}$ W. An older unit, still used for car engines, is the **horsepower**, about 750 W (NIST Guide to the SI, Appendix B.9).

**Watch the letter W.** In this block $W$ in italics is a quantity, the work, while W upright is the unit watt. "$W = 600$ J" is a work; "$P = 600$ W" is a power. Reading the formulas aloud ("work", "watt") helps keep them apart.

<WorkedExample title="Running up the stairs">
<Problem>
A student of 55 kg climbs a staircase at a steady speed. The top is 4.2 m higher than the bottom, and the climb takes 6.0 s. How much work does she do against gravity, and what is her average power?
</Problem>
<Step title="The work">
Her speed is the same at the top as at the bottom, so all the work goes into gravitational potential energy (lesson 3.3): $W = m g h = 55 \times 9.81 \times 4.2 \approx 2.3 \times 10^{3}$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="The power">
$P = W/\Delta t = 2266/6.0 \approx 380$ W, about half a horsepower.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: J/s = W. Size: a few hundred watts is a sensible figure for a person working hard. Taking the same stairs slowly, in 15 s, needs the same work but only $2266/15 \approx 150$ W.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

This is only the power that goes into lifting her body. Her muscles turn food energy into work with a low efficiency (see below), so her body uses energy several times faster than this.

## Average and instantaneous power

The power in $P = W/\Delta t$ is the **average power** over the time $\Delta t$ (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 13.7, Eq. (13.7.2)). Often the rate changes along the way: a car's engine works harder while it speeds up than while it cruises. Just as lesson 0.10 went from an average rate of change to the rate at one moment, the **instantaneous power** is the average power over a time interval so short that the rate hardly changes inside it (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 13.7, Eq. (13.7.3)). On a graph of the work done against time (lesson 0.5), the average power is the slope of the line between two points, and the instantaneous power is the slope of the tangent at one point.

A falling ball shows the difference. Gravity pulls on the ball with a constant force $m g$ (lesson 2.5), but the ball falls faster and faster (lesson 1.7), so it covers more distance, and gravity does more work, in every new second. The power of gravity grows steadily with time, as the next section will show.

## Power and speed: P = Fv

Suppose a constant force $F$ acts along the direction of motion, so $\theta = 0$ and $\cos\theta = 1$. In a time $\Delta t$ the object moves $\Delta x$, and the force does work $W = F\,\Delta x$. Then

<Formula label="Power of a constant force along the motion" tex="P = \frac{W}{\Delta t} = \frac{F\,\Delta x}{\Delta t} = F\,v" cite="mit" at="Sec. 13.7, Eqs. (13.7.1) to (13.7.3), pp. 13-17 and 13-18 (W = F_x Δx for a force along the motion, so P_ave = F_x v_ave,x and P = F_x v_x)" />

Over a time interval, $\Delta x/\Delta t$ is the average velocity (lesson 1.2), so $F v$ with the average velocity gives the average power. Over a very short interval it is the velocity at that moment, so $F v$ with the instantaneous velocity gives the instantaneous power (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 13.7, Eqs. (13.7.2) and (13.7.3)). If the force points **against** the motion, its power is negative, $P = -F v$: it takes energy away from the object at that rate.

For the falling ball, the force is the weight $m g$ and the speed after a time $t$ from rest is $g t$ (lesson 1.7), so the power of gravity is $P = m g \times g t$: it grows in proportion to the time.

<WorkedExample title="A falling ball">
<Problem>
A ball of 0.50 kg is dropped from rest. Ignoring air resistance, what is the power of gravity 1.5 s after the release, and what was the average power of gravity over those 1.5 s?
</Problem>
<Step title="Instantaneous power">
The force is $m g = 0.50 \times 9.81 \approx 4.9$ N and the speed is $g t = 9.81 \times 1.5 \approx 14.7$ m/s, so $P = F v \approx 72$ W.
</Step>
<Step title="Average power">
In 1.5 s the ball falls $\tfrac{1}{2} g t^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 9.81 \times 1.5^{2} \approx 11$ m, so gravity does $W = m g \times 11.04 \approx 54$ J of work. The average power is $54.1/1.5 \approx 36$ W.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The speed grows steadily from zero, so the average speed is half the final speed, and the average power is half the final power: $72.2/2 = 36.1$ W. The work also equals the ball's kinetic energy at 1.5 s, $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.50 \times 14.7^{2}$, as conservation of energy (lesson 3.4) requires.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Driving at a steady speed

A car on a motorway or a cyclist on a flat road keeps a constant speed, yet the engine or the legs keep working. Why? At a constant velocity the net force is zero (lesson 2.2), so the driving force exactly balances the friction and air resistance against the motion (lesson 2.6). The driving force does positive work, the resistance does just as much negative work (the energy comes from the fuel or the rider's muscles), and the kinetic energy stays the same; the energy ends up as thermal energy in the air and the road (lesson 3.4). The power needed is the resisting force times the speed.

<WorkedExample title="Cycling at speed">
<Problem>
A cyclist rides at a steady 8.0 m/s. Air resistance and rolling friction together push back with 22 N. What power must her legs deliver to the wheels? Riding faster, at 12 m/s, the resistance rises to 40 N. What power does she need then?
</Problem>
<Step title="At 8.0 m/s">
At a steady speed the driving force equals the resistance, 22 N, and points along the motion: $P = F v = 22 \times 8.0 \approx 180$ W.
</Step>
<Step title="At 12 m/s">
$P = 40 \times 12 = 480$ W.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: N × m/s = (N m)/s = J/s = W. Only 1.5 times the speed needs $480/176 \approx 2.7$ times the power, because the force grows too. That is why going a little faster on a bicycle is so much harder: air resistance grows quickly with the speed (lesson 2.6).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The same rule works when lifting at a steady speed. The lifting force then equals the weight $m g$, so the power is $P = m g v$, with $v$ the upward speed.

<WorkedExample title="A lift going up">
<Problem>
A lift cabin with its passengers has a mass of 650 kg and rises at a steady 1.5 m/s. What power must the cable deliver?
</Problem>
<Step title="The force">
At a steady speed the tension in the cable balances the weight: $F = m g = 650 \times 9.81 \approx 6.4 \times 10^{3}$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="The power">
$P = F v = 6376.5 \times 1.5 \approx 9.6 \times 10^{3}$ W, or 9.6 kW.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
In 10 s the lift rises 15 m, so the cable does $W = m g h = 6376.5 \times 15 \approx 9.6 \times 10^{4}$ J of work, and $P = W/\Delta t$ gives the same 9.6 kW.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Efficiency

No machine turns all the energy it takes in into the job it is built for. An electric motor warms up, a car engine sends hot gases out of the exhaust, a lamp gets hot as well as giving light. Energy is not lost (lesson 3.4), but part of it ends up as thermal energy that does nothing useful. The **efficiency** says what fraction of the energy put in comes out as useful energy, usually written as a percentage. The Greek letter $\eta$ (eta) is often used for it:

<Formula label="Efficiency" tex="\eta = \frac{\text{useful energy out}}{\text{energy in}} = \frac{\text{useful power out}}{\text{power in}}" cite="openlearn" at="2.1 Some basic concepts (efficiency as the useful output divided by the input, given as a percentage); the power form follows by dividing both energies by the same time" />

Multiply the fraction by 100 to give it as a percentage: an efficiency of 0.25 is 25%. The two forms agree because both energies are transferred in the same time, and dividing both by that time gives powers. A real machine always has an efficiency below 100%; one above 100% would create energy, which never happens.

<WorkedExample title="How efficient is the hoist?">
<Problem>
An electric hoist takes 1.2 kW of electrical power from the mains. It lifts a load of 90 kg at a steady 0.95 m/s. What is its efficiency, and how much power is wasted?
</Problem>
<Step title="Useful power">
Lifting at a steady speed: $P = m g v = 90 \times 9.81 \times 0.95 \approx 840$ W.
</Step>
<Step title="Efficiency">
$\eta = 838.8/1200 \approx 0.70$, so the efficiency is 70%.
</Step>
<Step title="Wasted power">
$1200 - 838.8 \approx 360$ W does not go into lifting. It warms the motor, the gears and the air around them.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The efficiency lies between 0% and 100%, and the useful and wasted powers add up to the 1.2 kW put in.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The kilowatt-hour

If a machine works at a constant power $P$ for a time $\Delta t$, it transfers an energy $E = P\,\Delta t$ (the definition of power, turned round). For a household, the joule is a tiny unit, so electricity companies use a bigger one: the **kilowatt-hour** (kWh), the energy transferred by 1 kW in one hour. Because one hour is 3600 s,

<Formula label="The kilowatt-hour" tex="1\ \text{kWh} = 1000\ \text{W} \times 3600\ \text{s} = 3.6 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J}" cite="openlearn" at="2.1 Some basic concepts (one kilowatt-hour is the energy delivered at a power of 1 kW for one hour, 3600 kJ)" />

Despite the word "watt" in its name, the kilowatt-hour is a unit of **energy**, not of power: a power multiplied by a time. Your electricity bill counts kilowatt-hours used, not kilowatts (OpenLearn, 2.1).

<WorkedExample title="Boiling water for tea">
<Problem>
A 2.0 kW kettle is switched on for 3.0 minutes. How much electrical energy does it use, in joules and in kilowatt-hours? What does that cost at 0.30 euro per kWh?
</Problem>
<Step title="In joules">
$\Delta t = 3.0 \times 60 = 180$ s, so $E = P\,\Delta t = 2.0 \times 10^{3} \times 180 = 3.6 \times 10^{5}$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="In kilowatt-hours">
3.0 minutes is 0.050 h, so $E = 2.0 \text{ kW} \times 0.050 \text{ h} = 0.10$ kWh.
</Step>
<Step title="Cost and check">
$0.10 \times 0.30 = 0.03$ euro, three cents. Check: $3.6 \times 10^{5}$ J divided by $3.6 \times 10^{6}$ J per kWh is also 0.10 kWh.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Astronomers call the power that a star sends out as light its **luminosity**. The Sun's luminosity is about $3.8 \times 10^{26}$ W (NASA Sun Fact Sheet): every second it sends that many joules into space. How bright a star *looks* from Earth depends on its luminosity and on how far away it is, because its light spreads out over a larger and larger sphere. So if astronomers know a star's luminosity, measuring how bright it looks tells them its distance. Objects whose luminosity astronomers can work out without knowing their distance, such as certain pulsating stars (from how fast they pulse) and certain exploding stars, are used to measure the distances to other galaxies, and with them the expansion of the universe, as Block 8 will show.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Power** is the rate of doing work, or of transferring energy: $P = W/\Delta t$. It is a scalar, measured in **watts**: 1 W = 1 J/s. One horsepower is about 750 W.
- $W/\Delta t$ over a time interval is the **average power**; the power at one moment is the **instantaneous power**, the slope of the work–time graph.
- For a constant force along the motion, $P = F v$. At a steady speed the driving force balances the resistance, so the power needed is the resisting force times the speed; lifting at a steady speed takes $P = m g v$.
- **Efficiency** is the useful energy (or power) out divided by the energy (or power) in, as a percentage. It is always below 100%; the rest becomes mostly thermal energy.
- The **kilowatt-hour** is a unit of energy: $E = P\,\Delta t$, and 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.
