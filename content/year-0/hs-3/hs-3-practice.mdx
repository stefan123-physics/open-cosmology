This set practises the twelve lessons of Block 3, from work to an expanding ball of galaxies. The problems, hints and solutions are our own; the methods are those of the lessons, which build on Siyavula's chapters on work, mechanical energy, and momentum and impulse, and on MIT 8.01SC's course notes on kinetic energy, work and power, collisions, rockets, angular momentum and gravitational potential energy. Problems 1 to 10 count 9 points each and Problems 11 and 12 count 10 points each, 110 in all; Problem 13 is optional. Throughout, take $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ near the Earth's surface and $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$; Problem 13 also needs the Earth's mass, $5.97 \times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}$, and its radius, $6.371 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}$.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first, starting with a sketch. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box wants the number only, in the unit printed in its label, for example `0.25` or `1.2e5`; small differences from rounding are accepted. Give a sign only where the problem asks for one; otherwise type the size. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `(a + b)/c`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Mowing the lawn" points={9}>

A gardener pushes a lawnmower of mass $28\ \text{kg}$ across a level lawn. She pushes along the handle with a force of $85\ \text{N}$, and the handle points downwards at $50^{\circ}$ below the horizontal. The mower moves $12\ \text{m}$ in a straight line at a steady speed.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

How much work does her push do on the mower over the $12\ \text{m}$?

<Hint>Only the part of the push along the motion does work. What is the angle between the push and the displacement?</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="Work done by the push (J):" value="656" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p1-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Work is force times displacement times the cosine of the angle between them.

<Step title="Work of a constant force">

<Formula label="Work done by a constant force" tex="W = F\,\Delta x\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="5.2 Work, definition box (W in J, F in N, Δx in m, θ the angle between force and displacement)" />

The mower moves horizontally and the push points $50^{\circ}$ below the horizontal, so $\theta = 50^{\circ}$: $W = 85 \times 12 \times \cos 50^{\circ} = 656\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The push along the motion is $85\cos 50^{\circ} = 54.6\ \text{N}$; the rest of the push, straight down, only presses the mower into the grass and does no work. Without the angle the answer would be $85 \times 12 = 1020\ \text{J}$, too much.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

How much work does gravity do on the mower over the $12\ \text{m}$?

<Answer id="p1-b" label="Answer:" choices={["None: the weight is at right angles to the motion", "The weight times the distance, because gravity acts during the whole push", "A negative amount, because the weight points down while the mower moves forwards"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A force at right angles to the displacement does no work.

<Step title="The angle is 90°">

The weight points straight down and the mower moves horizontally, so $\theta = 90^{\circ}$ and $\cos 90^{\circ} = 0$: gravity does no work. The same is true of the normal force of the ground, which points straight up.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The mower stays at the same height, so it neither gains nor loses gravitational potential energy: there is nothing for gravity's work to change.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

The grass and the wheels resist the motion. How much work does this resistance do on the mower? Give the sign.

<Hint>The mower moves at a steady speed. What does that tell you about the net force, and so about the net work?</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="Work done by the resistance (J, with sign):" value="-656" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p1-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At a steady speed the net force is zero, so the net work done by all four forces is zero.

<Step title="Net work">

<Formula label="Net work from the net force" tex="W_{\text{net}} = F_{\text{net}}\,\Delta x\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="Net work, Approach 2 (work done by the resultant force)" />

With $F_{\text{net}} = 0$ the net work is zero. The weight and the normal force do no work, so the work of the push and the work of the resistance add up to zero: the resistance does $-656\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check by another route">

The resistance must balance the forward part of the push, $54.6\ \text{N}$, and it points backwards, so $\theta = 180^{\circ}$: $W = 54.6 \times 12 \times \cos 180^{\circ} = -655\ \text{J}$, the same to rounding. The minus sign means the resistance takes energy from the mower, as thermal energy in the grass and the wheels.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A curling stone" points={9}>

A curling stone of mass $19\ \text{kg}$ leaves the player's hand at $2.4\ \text{m/s}$ and slides $28\ \text{m}$ in a straight line over level ice before it stops. Take the friction of the ice on the stone as a constant force.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the kinetic energy of the stone as it leaves the hand?

<Answer id="p2-a" label="Kinetic energy (J):" value="54.7" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p2-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Kinetic energy depends on the mass and the square of the speed.

<Step title="Kinetic energy">

<Formula label="Kinetic energy" tex="E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v^{2}" cite="reading11" at="Kinetic energy (ESAHL), definition" />

$E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 19 \times 2.4^{2} = 54.7\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\text{kg} \times \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 = \text{J}$. Forgetting to square the speed would give $22.8\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

How large is the friction force on the stone?

<Hint>The work–energy theorem links the net work on the stone to its change in kinetic energy. Friction is the only force that does work here.</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="Friction force (N):" value="1.95" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p2-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Friction's work takes away all the kinetic energy over the $28\ \text{m}$.

<Step title="Work–energy theorem">

<Formula label="Work–energy theorem" tex="W_{\text{net}} = \Delta E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v_f^{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v_i^{2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 13.6, Eq. (13.6.1), p. 13-16 (written there W = ΔK = ½mv_f² − ½mv_i², with K for E_k)" />

The stone ends at rest, so $\Delta E_k = 0 - 54.72 = -54.72\ \text{J}$. Friction points backwards, so its work is $-F \times 28$. Then $F = 54.72/28 = 1.95\ \text{N}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The weight is $19 \times 9.81 = 186.4\ \text{N}$, so friction is only $1.954/186.4 = 0.0105$ of the weight: ice is very slippery, which is why the stone slides so far.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Write the friction force $F$ in terms of the mass $m$ of the stone, its starting speed $v$ and the distance $d$ it slides. Type `m`, `v` and `d`.

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$F =$" expr="m*v^2/(2*d)" vars={{ m: [1, 30], v: [0.5, 5], d: [1, 50] }} names={{ m: "m", v: "v", d: "d" }}
  mistakes={[{ expr: "m*v^2/d", hint: "Check the factor one half in the kinetic energy." }, { expr: "m*v/(2*d)", hint: "Kinetic energy grows with the square of the speed." }, { expr: "v^2/(2*d)", hint: "That is the size of the deceleration; a force also needs the mass." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Do part (b) with letters instead of numbers.

<Step title="Letters">

$F\,d = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v^{2}$, so $F = \dfrac{m\,v^{2}}{2\,d}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check by another route">

With constant deceleration, $0 = v^{2} - 2\,a\,d$ (lesson 1.6) gives $a = v^{2}/(2d)$, and $F = m\,a$ gives the same formula. With the numbers: $19 \times 2.4^{2}/(2 \times 28) = 1.95\ \text{N}$, as in part (b).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A mountain pass by bike" points={9}>

A cyclist, $68\ \text{kg}$ with her bike, rides from a village at $1850\ \text{m}$ above sea level up to a mountain pass at $2640\ \text{m}$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

By how much does her gravitational potential energy increase? Type a number like `1.2e5` if you like.

<Answer id="p3-a" label="Increase in potential energy (J):" value="5.27e5" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p3-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only the change in height matters, not the height above sea level.

<Step title="Change in potential energy">

<Formula label="Gravitational potential energy near the Earth's surface" tex="E_p = m\,g\,h" cite="reading2" at="Gravitational potential energy (E_P = mgh, with h the perpendicular height from the reference point)" />

The climb is $2640 - 1850 = 790\ \text{m}$, so $\Delta E_p = 68 \times 9.81 \times 790 = 5.27 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At this height $g$ is still very close to $9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$: $2.6\ \text{km}$ is tiny next to the Earth's radius of $6371\ \text{km}$, so $m g h$ works well here.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Her road winds up the mountainside in hairpin bends, $11\ \text{km}$ long. A cable car to the pass would go much more steeply, along a shorter line. How does the work done against gravity on the long road compare with that on the cable car's route?

<Answer id="p3-b" label="Answer:" choices={["The same for both routes", "Larger on the long road, because it is longer", "Smaller on the long road, because its slope is gentler"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The work done against gravity depends only on the start and end heights, not on the path.

<Step title="The path does not matter">

On a gentle slope the force needed against gravity is smaller, but it acts over a longer distance; on a steep line the force is larger over a shorter distance. The two factors cancel: on both routes the work against gravity is $m g \times 790\ \text{m}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The long road still tires her more, but for other reasons: her muscles use energy with every turn of the pedals even on level ground, friction and air resistance take their share, and the trip takes longer. That energy does not end up as potential energy.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

On the way back she takes another road, which passes a lake $120\ \text{m}$ below the village. With the village as the zero level, what is the gravitational potential energy of her $0.60\ \text{kg}$ water bottle at the lake? Give the sign.

<Hint>Measure the height from the chosen zero level. Which way does it point?</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="Potential energy at the lake (J, with sign):" value="-706" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p3-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Below the zero level the height is negative, and so is the potential energy.

<Step title="Negative height">

$h = -120\ \text{m}$, so $E_p = 0.60 \times 9.81 \times (-120) = -706\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The value depends on the chosen zero level: with the lake as the zero level it would be $0\ \text{J}$. What does not depend on the choice is the change: going from the village to the lake, the bottle's potential energy falls by $706\ \text{J}$ whichever zero level is used.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A roller coaster" points={9}>

A roller-coaster car is pulled up to the top of the first hill, $32\ \text{m}$ above the lowest point of the track, and released from rest. In parts (a) and (b), ignore friction and air resistance.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

How fast does the car go at the lowest point of the track?

<Answer id="p4-a" label="Speed (m/s):" value="25.1" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p4-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Without friction, mechanical energy is conserved: the potential energy lost becomes kinetic energy.

<Step title="Mechanical energy at two points">

<Formula label="Mechanical energy at two points" tex="\tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v_i^{2} + m\,g\,h_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v_f^{2} + m\,g\,h_f" cite="reading3" at="Using the law of conservation of energy (ESAHP), E_M1 = E_M2 written out as mgh + ½mv² on both sides" />

With $v_i = 0$, $h_i = 32\ \text{m}$ and $h_f = 0$, the mass cancels: $v_f = \sqrt{2 g h_i} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 32} = 25.1\ \text{m/s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

That is $25.1 \times 3.6 = 90\ \text{km/h}$, a believable top speed for a large roller coaster. The mass of the car was not needed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

The track then climbs over a second hill, whose top is $18\ \text{m}$ above the lowest point. How fast does the car go over its top?

<Hint>Compare the top of the second hill with the starting point, not with the lowest point.</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="Speed (m/s):" value="16.6" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p4-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Between the start and the second hilltop the car has dropped $32 - 18 = 14\ \text{m}$ in all.

<Step title="Height lost">

$v = \sqrt{2 g \times 14} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 14} = 16.6\ \text{m/s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The speed is lower than at the lowest point, as it must be, but not in proportion to the height: speed goes as the square root of the height dropped.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

On the real ride the car with its riders has a mass of $520\ \text{kg}$ and reaches the lowest point at $23.5\ \text{m/s}$. How much thermal energy did friction and air resistance produce on the way down? Type a number like `1.2e5` if you like.

<Hint>Find the mechanical energy at the top and at the lowest point. Where did the difference go?</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c" label="Thermal energy (J):" value="1.97e4" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p4-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The non-conservative forces take away mechanical energy; the amount they take becomes thermal energy.

<Step title="Mechanical energy lost">

<Formula label="Work of non-conservative forces" tex="W_{\text{nc}} = \Delta E_k + \Delta E_p = \Delta E_{\text{mech}}" cite="reading10" at="Sec. 14.7, the section's Eqs. (14.6) and (14.7): W_nc = ΔK + ΔU = ΔE_m" />

At the top: $E_p = 520 \times 9.81 \times 32 = 1.632 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$, with no kinetic energy. At the lowest point: $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 520 \times 23.5^{2} = 1.436 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$, with no potential energy. The difference is $1.97 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

That is about 12% of the starting energy: the real speed, $23.5\ \text{m/s}$, is a little below the frictionless $25.1\ \text{m/s}$ of part (a). The total energy is still conserved; it has only changed form.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

At the end of the ride, on level track, the car ($520\ \text{kg}$) runs at $8.0\ \text{m/s}$ into a buffer spring with spring constant $k = 2.6 \times 10^{5}\ \text{N/m}$. How far is the spring squeezed when the car stops for a moment? Ignore friction during the squeeze.

<Answer id="p4-d" label="Squeeze (m):" value="0.358" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p4-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The car's kinetic energy becomes elastic potential energy in the spring.

<Step title="Energy into the spring">

<Formula label="Elastic potential energy of a spring" tex="E_{\text{el}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,k\,x^{2}" cite="reading10" at="Sec. 14.4.2, Eq. (14.12), written there U_s(x) = ½kx²" />

$\tfrac{1}{2} \times 520 \times 8.0^{2} = 1.664 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,k\,x^{2}$, so $x = \sqrt{2 \times 1.664 \times 10^{4}/(2.6 \times 10^{5})} = 0.358\ \text{m}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In letters, $x = v\sqrt{m/k} = 8.0 \times \sqrt{520/(2.6 \times 10^{5})} = 0.358\ \text{m}$. A stiffer spring would stop the car in a shorter distance, but with a larger force.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A pump fills a water tank" points={9}>

An electric pump lifts water from a well into a tank on a tower. In $4.0$ minutes it raises $1500\ \text{kg}$ of water through a height of $24\ \text{m}$. The water starts and ends at rest.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What useful power does the pump deliver to the water?

<Hint>The useful work is the gain in potential energy of the water. Power is work divided by time, with the time in seconds.</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="Useful power (W):" value="1470" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p5-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Power is the rate of doing work.

<Step title="Useful work">

$W = m g h = 1500 \times 9.81 \times 24 = 3.53 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Divide by the time">

<Formula label="Power" tex="P = \frac{W}{\Delta t}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 13.7, Eq. (13.7.2), p. 13-18 (average power, the rate at which work is done, written there P_ave = W/Δt)" />

<Step title="Check the result">

Forgetting to change the minutes into seconds would give a power 60 times too large. About $1.5\ \text{kW}$ is the power of a small household pump.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

The pump takes $2.1\ \text{kW}$ of electrical power from the mains while it runs. What is its efficiency, in percent?

<Answer id="p5-b" label="Efficiency (%):" value="70" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p5-b" />

<Solution>

<Step title="Efficiency">

<Formula label="Efficiency" tex="\eta = \frac{\text{useful power out}}{\text{power in}}" cite="openlearn" at="2.1 Some basic concepts (efficiency as the useful output divided by the input, given as a percentage)" />

$\eta = 1471.5/2100 = 0.70$, or 70%.

The efficiency is less than 100%, as it must be. The other 30% becomes thermal energy in the motor and in the water's friction with the pipes.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How much electrical energy does one filling take, in kilowatt-hours?

<Step title="Check in joules">

<Exercise id="p6" title="A hockey puck" points={9}>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the size of the average force of the stick on the puck?

<Answer id="p6-b" label="Average force (N):" value="523" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p6-b" />

<Solution>

The puck's weight is only $0.165 \times 9.81 = 1.62\ \text{N}$, so the stick pushes with more than 300 times the weight. During the hit, the weight and the ice hardly matter.

</Solution>

</Solution>

<Answer id="p7-c" label="Answer:" choices={["Zero", "The momentum of the thrown ball, in the direction of the throw", "Twice the momentum of the thrown ball, because both skaters now move"]} answer={0} />

<Step title="Thrust">

Units: $\text{kg/s} \times \text{m/s} = \text{kg m/s}^2 = \text{N}$. Remember to change $2.2\ \text{km/s}$ into $2200\ \text{m/s}$.

</Solution>

What is the rocket's acceleration just after lift-off?

<Answer id="p9-b" label="Acceleration (m/s²):" value="3.94" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p9-b" />

<Step title="Net force">

The weight is $2400 \times 9.81 = 2.354 \times 10^{4}\ \text{N}$, so the net force is $33\,000 - 23\,544 = 9456\ \text{N}$, upwards, and $a = 9456/2400 = 3.94\ \text{m/s}^2$.

**The idea.** Gravity takes away $g$ metres per second of speed every second of the burn.

<Step title="Burn time">

The fuel lasts $1500/15 = 100\ \text{s}$.

<Step title="Rocket climbing in uniform gravity">

<Formula label="Rocket climbing straight up in uniform gravity" tex="\Delta v = u \ln \frac{m_i}{m_f} - g\,t" cite="reading8" at="Sec. 12.3.2, Eqs. (12.3.100) and (12.3.101), for a burn of time t starting from rest" />

</Solution>

## Problem 10

<Exercise id="p10" title="A spinning stool" points={9}>

A student sits on a stool that turns freely about a vertical axis. She holds a $2.0\ \text{kg}$ dumbbell in each hand, with her arms stretched out so that each dumbbell is $0.75\ \text{m}$ from the axis, and she turns at $1.2\ \text{rad/s}$. Take the moment of inertia of the student and the stool alone (without the dumbbells) as $2.4\ \text{kg m}^2$ throughout. Treat the dumbbells as point masses.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the angular momentum of the student, the stool and the dumbbells together?

<Hint>First find the total moment of inertia: add $m r^{2}$ for each dumbbell to that of the student and stool.</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-a" label="Angular momentum (kg m²/s):" value="5.58" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p10-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Moments of inertia add; angular momentum is the moment of inertia times the angular speed.

<Step title="Moment of inertia">

<Formula label="Moment of inertia" tex="I = \sum m\,r^{2}" cite="reading9" at="Sec. 19.7, Eqs. (19.5.24) and (19.5.26) (written there as an integral over the body)" />

$I_i = 2.4 + 2 \times 2.0 \times 0.75^{2} = 2.4 + 2.25 = 4.65\ \text{kg m}^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Angular momentum">

<Formula label="Angular momentum of a spinning body" tex="L = I\,\omega" cite="reading9" at="Sec. 19.7, Eq. (19.5.27)" />

$L = 4.65 \times 1.2 = 5.58\ \text{kg m}^2/\text{s}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

She pulls the dumbbells in until each is $0.20\ \text{m}$ from the axis. How fast does she turn now?

<Answer id="p10-b" label="Angular speed (rad/s):" value="2.18" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p10-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** No outside torque acts about the axis, so the angular momentum stays the same while the moment of inertia shrinks.

<Step title="New moment of inertia">

$I_f = 2.4 + 2 \times 2.0 \times 0.20^{2} = 2.4 + 0.16 = 2.56\ \text{kg m}^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Conservation of angular momentum">

<Formula label="Changing the shape of a spinning body" tex="I_i\,\omega_i = I_f\,\omega_f" cite="reading9" at="Sec. 19.8, Eq. (19.5.36) with L = Iω from Sec. 19.7, Eq. (19.5.27)" />

$\omega_f = 5.58/2.56 = 2.18\ \text{rad/s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

She spins faster, as a skater does when she pulls in her arms. The speed does not grow as much as the dumbbells' $m r^{2}$ shrinks, because most of the moment of inertia at the end is her own and the stool's.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

What is the rotational kinetic energy after she has pulled the dumbbells in?

<Answer id="p10-c" label="Rotational kinetic energy (J):" value="6.08" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p10-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Rotational kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2} I \omega^{2}$, the turning version of $\tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}$.

<Step title="Kinetic energy after">

<Formula label="Rotational kinetic energy" tex="E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}\,I\,\omega^{2}" cite="reading12" at="Sec. 16.3.1, Eq. (16.2.4)" />

$E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2.56 \times 2.180^{2} = 6.08\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Before, it was $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 4.65 \times 1.2^{2} = 3.35\ \text{J}$: the kinetic energy has grown, although the angular momentum has not.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Where does the extra kinetic energy come from?

<Answer id="p10-d" label="Answer:" choices={["From the work her arms do pulling the dumbbells inwards", "From the angular momentum, part of which turns into kinetic energy", "From nowhere: kinetic energy is conserved, and the difference is rounding"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Energy is conserved as a whole, but not each kind of energy separately.

<Step title="Work of her arms">

To keep a dumbbell on its circle, her hand must pull it towards the axis. Pulling it further in, she moves it along that pull, so her arms do positive work on it: about $6.08 - 3.35 = 2.73\ \text{J}$ in all. That energy comes from her muscles.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Angular momentum is not a kind of energy (its unit is $\text{kg m}^2/\text{s}$, not $\text{J}$), and it is the same before and after. If she lets her arms out again, the dumbbells pull her hands outwards and do work on her arms instead, and the kinetic energy falls back to $3.35\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Hint>Write the total energy per kilogram at the surface, with $E_p = -GMm/r$. At the highest point the probe is at rest for a moment.</Hint>
<Hint>Gravity weakens as the probe rises, so $h = v^{2}/(2g)$ gives a wrong answer here.</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-b" label="Height (km):" value="1890" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p11-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Conservation of energy with the potential energy $-GMm/r$, because the probe climbs a large fraction of the moon's radius.

<Step title="Energy at the surface">

<Formula label="Mechanical energy far from a planet" tex="E_{\text{mech}} = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2} - \frac{G M m}{r}" cite="reading10" at="Example 14.2, Eq. (14.2) of the example (written there for the surface, r = R)" />

Per kilogram: $GM = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 4.80 \times 10^{22} = 3.204 \times 10^{12}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$, so $\tfrac{1}{2}(1500)^{2} - 3.204 \times 10^{12}/(1.56 \times 10^{6}) = 1.125 \times 10^{6} - 2.054 \times 10^{6} = -9.29 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J/kg}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Highest point">

There all of it is potential energy: $-GM/r_{\text{max}} = -9.29 \times 10^{5}$, so $r_{\text{max}} = 3.204 \times 10^{12}/(9.285 \times 10^{5}) = 3.45 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}$. The height is $3.45 \times 10^{6} - 1.56 \times 10^{6} = 1.89 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}$, or $1890\ \text{km}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The total energy is negative, so the probe is bound and must come back, as it should below the escape speed. With a constant surface gravity, $GM/R^{2} = 1.32\ \text{m/s}^2$, the estimate $v^{2}/(2g)$ would give only $855\ \text{km}$: the weakening gravity lets the probe climb more than twice as high.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Write the greatest distance $r_{\text{max}}$ from the moon's centre in terms of $G$, $M$, $R$ and the launch speed $v$. Type `G`, `M`, `R` and `v`.

<Answer id="p11-c" label="$r_{\text{max}} =$" expr="2*G*M*R/(2*G*M - R*v^2)" vars={{ G: [1, 2], M: [10, 20], R: [1, 2], v: [0.5, 2] }} names={{ G: "G", M: "M", R: "R", v: "v" }}
  mistakes={[{ expr: "R + R^2*v^2/(2*G*M)", hint: "That treats gravity as constant all the way up, like h = v²/2g." }, { expr: "2*G*M/v^2", hint: "That leaves out the potential energy at the surface." }, { expr: "2*G*M*R/(2*G*M + R*v^2)", hint: "Check the sign: the probe must rise above the surface, so r_max is larger than R." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Do part (b) with letters.

<Step title="Letters">

$\tfrac{1}{2} v^{2} - \dfrac{G M}{R} = -\dfrac{G M}{r_{\text{max}}}$. Multiply by $2/(GM)$: $\dfrac{2}{r_{\text{max}}} = \dfrac{2}{R} - \dfrac{v^{2}}{G M}$, so $r_{\text{max}} = \dfrac{2 G M R}{2 G M - R v^{2}}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $v = 0$ it gives $r_{\text{max}} = R$: the probe never leaves the ground. When $R v^{2}$ approaches $2GM$, that is when $v$ approaches the escape speed, the denominator goes to zero and $r_{\text{max}}$ grows without limit.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

A second probe is fired straight up at $2.20\ \text{km/s}$. What happens to it?

<Answer id="p11-d" label="Answer:" choices={["It escapes, still moving when it is very far away", "It rises very high, stops and falls back", "It goes into a circular orbit around the moon"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The sign of the total energy decides whether the probe is bound.

<Step title="Total energy">

$2.20\ \text{km/s}$ is more than the escape speed of $2.03\ \text{km/s}$. Per kilogram, $\tfrac{1}{2}(2200)^{2} - 2.054 \times 10^{6} = 2.42 \times 10^{6} - 2.054 \times 10^{6}$, which is above zero: the probe is unbound.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Far away its potential energy is zero, so it keeps kinetic energy: it moves on at $\sqrt{2 \times 3.66 \times 10^{5}} = 856\ \text{m/s}$. A probe fired straight up cannot go into a circular orbit: that needs a sideways speed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 12

<Exercise id="p13" title="Will this ball of galaxies stop?" points={10}>

A model universe is filled evenly with galaxies and expands by Hubble's law, $v = H r$, with an expansion rate $H = 2.0 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Its density is $\rho = 1.2 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{kg/m}^3$. As in lesson 3.12, draw a ball of any radius in it and follow a galaxy on its edge, with gravity as the only force.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the critical density of this model universe? Type a number like `3.5e-27`.

<Hint>The critical density is the density at which a galaxy on the edge has exactly zero energy per kilogram. The radius of the ball cancels.</Hint>

<Answer id="p13-a" label="Critical density (kg/m³):" value="7.15e-27" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p13-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $v = H r$ and $M = \tfrac43\pi r^{3}\rho$, zero energy for the edge galaxy fixes one density, whatever the size of the ball.

<Step title="Energy of the edge galaxy">

<Formula label="Energy per kilogram of a galaxy on the edge" tex="\frac{E_{\text{mech}}}{m} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,v^{2} - \frac{G M}{r} = \text{constant}" cite="reading14" at="pp. 28–29 (board): ½mv² − GmM/r for a test particle on the boundary, divided here by m" />

<Formula label="Mass of an evenly filled ball" tex="M = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}\rho" cite="reading14" at="p. 18: M = (4π/3) r_i³ ρ_i" />

Setting $\tfrac12 H^{2} r^{2} = \tfrac43\pi G\rho\,r^{2}$ and solving for the density gives the critical density.

</Step>

<Step title="Critical density">

<Formula label="Critical density" tex="\rho_c = \frac{3 H^{2}}{8\pi G}" cite="reading13" at="p. 20 (board)" />

$\rho_c = 3 \times (2.0 \times 10^{-18})^{2}/(8\pi \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11}) = 1.2 \times 10^{-35}/(1.677 \times 10^{-9}) = 7.15 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg/m}^3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\text{s}^{-2}/(\text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2) = \text{kg/m}^3$. In hydrogen atoms of $1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$ that is $7.15 \times 10^{-27}/(1.67 \times 10^{-27}) = 4.3$ atoms per cubic metre. Forgetting to square $H$ gives a result that is not even in kilograms per cubic metre: check the units.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

What happens to this ball of galaxies?

<Answer id="p13-b" label="Answer:" choices={["It reaches a largest size, stops and falls back together", "It expands for ever, slowing down but never stopping", "It expands faster and faster", "It depends on the radius of the ball you draw"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compare the density with the critical density.

<Step title="Denser than critical">

$\rho/\rho_c = 1.2 \times 10^{-26}/(7.15 \times 10^{-27}) = 1.68$. The ball is denser than critical, so the energy of a galaxy on its edge is negative: it is bound, like a probe fired below the escape speed (lesson 3.11). The ball stops and falls back.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The radius cancelled in part (a), so every ball drawn in this universe, large or small, gives the same answer. Expanding faster and faster would need something other than gravity, such as dark energy.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

By the time the ball has grown to $1.5$ times its starting radius, what fraction of its starting speed does a galaxy on the edge still have?

<Hint>Use the speed of the edge as the ball grows, with $\rho/\rho_c$ from part (b).</Hint>

<Answer id="p13-c" label="Speed as a fraction of the starting speed:" value="0.664" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p13-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Energy conservation for the edge galaxy, written with the density compared with the critical density.

<Step title="Speed of the edge">

<Formula label="Speed of the edge as the ball grows" tex="v^{2} = v_0^{2}\left[1 - \frac{\rho}{\rho_c}\left(1 - \frac{r_0}{r}\right)\right]" cite="reading13" at="p. 17 (board), written there ȧ² = 2E + (8π/3)Gρ_i/a; this form with ρ/ρ_c is lesson 3.12's" />

With $r_0/r = 1/1.5$: $v^{2}/v_0^{2} = 1 - 1.677 \times (1 - 0.667) = 1 - 0.559 = 0.441$, so $v/v_0 = 0.664$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A ball with exactly the critical density would still have $\sqrt{r_0/r} = \sqrt{0.667} = 0.816$ of its starting speed at this size. The denser ball has slowed down more, as it should.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Write the largest radius of the ball, as a multiple of its starting radius ($r_\text{max}/r_0$), in terms of $q = \rho/\rho_c$, for $q$ larger than 1. Type `q`.

<Answer id="p13-d" label="$r_\text{max}/r_0 =$" expr="q/(q - 1)" vars={{ q: [1.1, 5] }} names={{ q: "q" }}
  mistakes={[{ expr: "(q - 1)/q", hint: "Upside down: the ball grows, so the answer must be larger than 1." }, { expr: "1/(q - 1)", hint: "Check the top: at q = 2 the ball should grow to twice its starting radius." }, { expr: "q", hint: "Set the speed to zero and solve for r₀/r first." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At the largest radius the edge galaxy stops for a moment: $v = 0$.

<Step title="Speed zero">

$1 - q\,(1 - r_0/r) = 0$ gives $1 - r_0/r = 1/q$, so $r_0/r = (q - 1)/q$ and $r_\text{max}/r_0 = \dfrac{q}{q - 1}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For this universe, $1.677/0.677 = 2.48$: the ball grows to about two and a half times its starting radius. For $q = 2$ the formula gives $2$, and as $q$ comes down towards $1$ the largest radius grows without limit: at exactly the critical density the ball never stops.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 13

<Exercise id="p12" title="A meteoroid from far away" points={10} optional>

This problem is optional: it does not count towards the score of the set.

A meteoroid of mass $2.0\ \text{kg}$ starts at rest very far from the Earth and falls straight towards it, pulled only by the Earth's gravity.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

How fast is it moving when it reaches the top of the atmosphere, $100\ \text{km}$ above the surface?

<Hint>Far away, both its kinetic and its potential energy are zero. What is its total energy then, and later?</Hint>

<Answer id="p12-a" label="Speed (m/s):" value="11100" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p12-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The total energy stays zero, so the meteoroid arrives at the escape speed for that distance from the centre: falling in from rest far away is escape run backwards.

<Step title="Energy conservation">

$\tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2} - \dfrac{G M m}{r} = 0$ with $r = 6.371 \times 10^{6} + 1.0 \times 10^{5} = 6.471 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m}$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24}}{6.471 \times 10^{6}}} = 1.11 \times 10^{4}\ \text{m/s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

That is a little below the escape speed from the surface itself, $11.2\ \text{km/s}$ (lesson 3.11), because the meteoroid is still $100\ \text{km}$ up. The mass was not needed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is its kinetic energy there? Type a number like `1.2e5` if you like.

<Answer id="p12-b" label="Kinetic energy (J):" value="1.23e8" tolerance="3%" calc="hs3p-p12-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Kinetic energy from the mass and the speed.

<Step title="Kinetic energy">

$E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2.0 \times (1.110 \times 10^{4})^{2} = 1.23 \times 10^{8}\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

It also equals the potential energy lost, $G M m/r = 1.23 \times 10^{8}\ \text{J}$: a stone of $2\ \text{kg}$ carries as much energy as it takes to lift a $1000\ \text{kg}$ car more than $12\ \text{km}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
