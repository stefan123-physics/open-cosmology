So far, Block 3 has described motion with energy. Lesson 3.2 gave a moving object kinetic energy, $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}$, which depends on the speed but not on the direction. This lesson introduces a second quantity built from mass and velocity, **momentum**, which does keep track of direction. It leads to a new way of writing Newton's second law (lesson 2.3) and to **impulse**: what a force does when it acts for a certain time. That turns out to explain why airbags, crumple zones and soft landings save lives.

## Momentum

Stopping a rolling football is easy. Stopping a lorry rolling at the same speed is not, and neither is stopping a bullet, even though it is tiny. What makes something hard to stop depends on both its mass and its velocity. Physicists combine them into one quantity. The **momentum** of an object of mass $m$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ is

<Formula label="Momentum" tex="\vec p = m\,\vec v" cite="mit" at="2.2 Momentum (ESCJ7), definition box" />

The symbol for momentum is $p$. The MIT course notes call it the "quantity of motion", a name that goes back to Newton (reading 4, Sec. 10.2, Eq. (10.2.1), and the Principia quotation that opens Ch. 10). Momentum is proportional to the mass and to the velocity: twice the mass at the same velocity, or twice the velocity with the same mass, gives twice the momentum.

**The unit.** With mass in kg and velocity in m/s, momentum is measured in **kg·m/s**. This unit has no name of its own. Since $1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^{2}$ (lesson 2.3), a newton times a second is $1\ \text{N·s} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}$, so momentum can also be given in N·s. That will be useful for impulse below.

Some examples. A hockey puck of 0.17 kg sliding at 30 m/s has $p = 0.17 \times 30 = 5.1$ kg·m/s. A lorry of 12 000 kg at 15 m/s has $p = 12\,000 \times 15 = 1.8 \times 10^{5}$ kg·m/s, about 35 000 times as much. Different objects can have the same momentum: a bus of 9000 kg creeping along at 2.0 m/s has $9000 \times 2.0 = 1.8 \times 10^{4}$ kg·m/s, and so does a car of 1200 kg at 15 m/s, because $1200 \times 15 = 1.8 \times 10^{4}$ kg·m/s.

**Momentum is not kinetic energy.** The bus and the car above have the same momentum, but not the same kinetic energy. The bus has $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 9000 \times 2.0^{2} = 1.8 \times 10^{4}$ J and the car $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times 15^{2} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{5}$ J, 7.5 times as much. Kinetic energy grows with the square of the speed and is a scalar; momentum grows in proportion to the speed and is a vector.

## Momentum is a vector

Mass is a scalar, and multiplying a vector by a positive scalar keeps its direction (lesson 0.8). So **momentum points the same way as the velocity**. Whenever you give a momentum, give its direction too: "$1.8 \times 10^{5}$ kg·m/s east", not just a number.

For motion along a straight line we do the same as in Block 1: choose one direction as positive, and let the sign show the direction. A trolley of 0.50 kg rolling to the left at 4.0 m/s, with right chosen as positive, has velocity $-4.0$ m/s and momentum

$p = 0.50 \times (-4.0) = -2.0$ kg·m/s, which means 2.0 kg·m/s to the left.

A negative momentum is not "less than nothing": it is momentum in the negative direction. Two identical trolleys rolling towards each other at the same speed have momenta of the same size and opposite signs, so together their momentum adds up to zero, even though both are moving and both have kinetic energy. Lesson 3.7 will show why that total is so important.

Like velocity, momentum depends on the frame of reference (lesson 1.9): a bag on a moving train has no momentum relative to the train, but a lot relative to the ground (reading 4, Sec. 10.2). As with kinetic energy, pick one frame and keep it.

## Change in momentum

When something hits, pushes or pulls an object, its velocity usually changes, and with it its momentum. The **change in momentum** is the final momentum minus the initial momentum:

<Formula label="Change in momentum" tex="\Delta \vec p = \vec p_f - \vec p_i = m\,\vec v_f - m\,\vec v_i" cite="mit" at="Change in momentum (ESCJ9), Case 1: object bouncing off a wall" />

For a constant mass this is $\Delta \vec p = m\,\Delta \vec v$. Along a line, put in the velocities with their signs. Then the sign of $\Delta p$ gives its direction, and the sign matters a great deal:

- **An object that stops** loses all its momentum: $\Delta p = 0 - p_i = -p_i$.
- **An object that slows down** without turning round still moves forwards, but its change in momentum points backwards, against the motion.
- **An object that bounces back** has the largest change: its momentum must first be brought to zero and then be built up again in the other direction.

<WorkedExample title="A football bouncing off a wall">
<Problem>
A football of 0.45 kg is kicked straight at a wall. It hits the wall at 12 m/s and bounces straight back at 9.0 m/s. What is its change in momentum?
</Problem>
<Step title="Choose a positive direction">
Take "towards the wall" as positive. Then $v_i = +12$ m/s and $v_f = -9.0$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Momentum before and after">
$p_i = 0.45 \times 12 = 5.4$ kg·m/s and $p_f = 0.45 \times (-9.0) \approx -4.1$ kg·m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Change in momentum">
$\Delta p = p_f - p_i = 0.45 \times (-9.0 - 12) \approx -9.5$ kg·m/s: about 9.5 kg·m/s directed away from the wall.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The change is larger than either momentum, as it must be for a bounce: the wall first removes the 5.4 kg·m/s and then gives the ball about 4.1 kg·m/s the other way, and $5.4 + 4.05 = 9.45$. Had the ball stopped dead against the wall, the change would only have been $-5.4$ kg·m/s. Units: kg × m/s = kg·m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

A common mistake is to subtract the two speeds, $12 - 9.0 = 3.0$ m/s, and forget that the ball has turned round. Always work with signed velocities.

## Newton's second law in terms of momentum

What changes an object's momentum? A net force. Newton's second law (lesson 2.3) says $\vec F_{\text{net}} = m\vec a$, and lesson 1.4 defined the average acceleration as $\vec a = \Delta \vec v / \Delta t$. For an object whose mass does not change, put the two together:

$\vec F_{\text{net}} = m\,\dfrac{\Delta \vec v}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{m\,\Delta \vec v}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{\Delta \vec p}{\Delta t}.$

Newton himself stated his second law in terms of the "change of motion" (reading 4, the Principia quotation at the start of Ch. 10). In words: *the net force on an object equals the rate of change of its momentum.*

<Formula label="Newton's second law in terms of momentum" tex="\vec F_{\text{net}} = \frac{\Delta \vec p}{\Delta t}" cite="reading1" at="2.3 Newton's Second Law revisited (ESCJB), definition box and the derivation from F_net = ma" />

For a constant mass this says nothing new: it is $F = ma$ written differently. But the momentum form is more general. It also applies to a whole system of objects, such as a rocket together with the gas it throws out (lesson 3.9), and it is the form that survives in Einstein's relativity. For a force that varies during $\Delta t$, it gives the **average** net force over that time; the MIT notes write it for one instant, with the rate of change of momentum at that instant (lesson 0.10) in place of $\Delta \vec p / \Delta t$ (reading 4, Sec. 10.2, Eqs. (10.2.2) and (10.2.3)).

<WorkedExample title="A car joining the motorway">
<Problem>
A car of 1100 kg speeds up from 5.0 m/s to 20 m/s in 6.0 s, in a straight line. What is the average net force on it?
</Problem>
<Step title="Change in momentum">
Take the direction of motion as positive: $\Delta p = 1100 \times (20 - 5.0) = 1.65 \times 10^{4}$ kg·m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Net force">
$F_{\text{net}} = \Delta p / \Delta t = 1.65 \times 10^{4} / 6.0 \approx 2.8 \times 10^{3}$ N, forwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
The acceleration is $a = (20 - 5.0)/6.0 = 2.5$ m/s², and $F_{\text{net}} = m a = 1100 \times 2.5 = 2750$ N, the same. Units: (kg·m/s)/s = kg·m/s² = N.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Impulse

Multiply both sides of $\vec F_{\text{net}} = \Delta \vec p / \Delta t$ by $\Delta t$. The product of the net force and the time for which it acts is called the **impulse**, written $\vec J$ here (the MIT notes write $\vec I$; do not confuse the symbol with the joule, J, a unit of energy). It equals the change in momentum, a rule known as the **impulse–momentum theorem**:

<Formula label="Impulse–momentum theorem" tex="\vec J = \vec F_{\text{net}}\,\Delta t = \Delta \vec p" cite="reading2" at="2.5 Impulse (ESCJK), definition box and the impulse-momentum theorem" />

Impulse is a vector in the direction of the net force, and its unit is N·s, which is the same as kg·m/s, the unit of momentum. (The MIT notes print the unit as "N·m" just after Eq. (10.2.4); that is a slip, as the kg·m/s next to it shows.)

The theorem says that what counts for the change in momentum is force **and** time together. The same impulse of 20 N·s can come from a force of 2.0 N acting for 10 s or from a force of 2000 N acting for only 0.010 s. A small force acting for a long time can change the momentum just as much as a large force acting briefly.

<WorkedExample title="Hitting a golf ball">
<Problem>
A golf ball of 0.046 kg lies at rest on the tee. The club is in contact with it for 0.50 ms (milliseconds), and the ball leaves at 60 m/s. What impulse did the club give the ball, and what was the average force?
</Problem>
<Step title="Impulse">
The impulse equals the change in momentum: $J = \Delta p = 0.046 \times (60 - 0) \approx 2.8$ N·s, in the direction the ball flies.
</Step>
<Step title="Average force">
With $\Delta t = 0.50\ \text{ms} = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}$ s: $F = J / \Delta t = 2.76 / (5.0 \times 10^{-4}) \approx 5.5 \times 10^{3}$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
By lesson 2.3: $a = 60 / (5.0 \times 10^{-4}) = 1.2 \times 10^{5}$ m/s², and $m a = 0.046 \times 1.2 \times 10^{5} = 5520$ N, the same. The ball's weight is only $0.046 \times 9.81 \approx 0.45$ N, so during the hit the club pushes with about $1.2 \times 10^{4}$ times the ball's weight. That is why we may ignore gravity during such a short collision.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Impulse on a force–time graph

In a real kick or collision the force is not constant. It builds up as the two objects squash into each other, reaches a peak, and falls back to zero as they separate. How do we find the impulse then?

Lessons 0.10 and 1.5 showed that the area under a velocity–time graph gives the displacement, because each thin strip of the area is (velocity) × (short time). In the same way each thin strip under a **force–time graph** is (force) × (short time), a small impulse. Adding all the strips gives the total: **the impulse is the area under the force–time graph** (reading 2, 2.5; reading 4, Example 10.1). Area below the time axis, where the force points the negative way, counts as negative impulse.

The **average force** over the collision is the constant force that would give the same impulse in the same time: $F_{\text{ave}} = J / \Delta t$ (reading 4, Eqs. (10.2.6) to (10.2.8)). On the graph it is the height of a rectangle with the same area and the same width.

<WorkedExample title="Reading the impulse of a kick from the graph">
<Problem>
The graph above shows the force of a boot on a football of 0.45 kg that was at rest. Find the impulse, the speed of the ball afterwards, and the average force.
</Problem>
<Step title="Impulse as an area">
The area under the graph is a triangle with base 10 ms = 0.010 s and height 900 N: $J = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.010 \times 900 = 4.5$ N·s.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed of the ball">
The ball starts at rest, so $J = \Delta p = m v_f$ and $v_f = 4.5 / 0.45 = 10$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Average force">
$F_{\text{ave}} = J / \Delta t = 4.5 / 0.010 = 450$ N, half the peak force, as for any triangle.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by splitting the area">
The rising part is a triangle of base 0.0040 s: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.0040 \times 900 = 1.8$ N·s. The falling part has base 0.0060 s: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.0060 \times 900 = 2.7$ N·s. Together $1.8 + 2.7 = 4.5$ N·s. Units: N × s = N·s = kg·m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Safety: make the stop last longer

When something must be stopped, its change in momentum is fixed by its mass and speed. Rearranging the impulse–momentum theorem gives the average force needed:

<Formula label="Average force for a given change in momentum" tex="F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}" cite="reading3" at="Air-bags in motor vehicles (ESCJN)" />

The longer the stop lasts, the smaller the force. Many safety devices work on exactly this idea (reading 3):

- **Airbags and seat belts.** In a crash the driver keeps moving forwards. An airbag and a seat belt that stretches a little stop the driver over a longer time than a hard steering wheel would.
- **Crumple zones.** The front and back of a car are built to fold up in a crash. The crushing takes time, so the people inside are stopped more gently. A car that crumples also does not bounce back, and a bounce would make the change in momentum, and so the impulse on the people, even larger.
- **Soft landings.** Bending your knees when you land after a jump, crash mats in gymnastics, padded gloves for catching a hard ball and soft gravel beds where runaway lorries can stop all make the stopping time longer.

<WorkedExample title="An airbag in a crash">
<Problem>
A driver of 70 kg is in a car that crashes into a wall at 15 m/s (54 km/h). How large is the average force on the driver if she is stopped in 0.010 s by the steering wheel, and if she is stopped in 0.15 s by a seat belt and airbag?
</Problem>
<Step title="Change in momentum">
Either way she goes from 15 m/s to rest: the size of her change in momentum is $70 \times 15 = 1.05 \times 10^{3}$ kg·m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Steering wheel">
$F = 1.05 \times 10^{3} / 0.010 \approx 1.1 \times 10^{5}$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Seat belt and airbag">
$F = 1.05 \times 10^{3} / 0.15 = 7.0 \times 10^{3}$ N, fifteen times smaller ($0.15 / 0.010 = 15$).
</Step>
<Step title="Check the size">
Her weight is $70 \times 9.81 \approx 690$ N. The airbag pushes with about 10 times her weight, which a body can survive; the steering wheel would push with about 150 times her weight, concentrated on a small area. The impulse, about $1.05 \times 10^{3}$ N·s, is the same in both cases: only the time differs.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The bounce matters too. A car of 1500 kg that hits a wall at 14 m/s and stops has $\Delta p = 1500 \times 14 = 2.1 \times 10^{4}$ kg·m/s. If it bounced back at 2.0 m/s, the change would be $1500 \times (14 + 2.0) = 2.4 \times 10^{4}$ kg·m/s, about 14% more, and the people inside would feel a larger impulse. Crumple zones avoid both a short stop and a bounce.

The same idea works the other way round in sport. A player who **follows through** keeps the bat or racket on the ball for longer, so the same force acts for a longer time, gives a larger impulse, and sends the ball off faster.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Momentum is not only for things with mass. Light has no mass, yet it carries momentum: when light is absorbed or reflected, it hands over momentum and gives a small push, which is how a solar sail can push a spacecraft. In the hot early universe, space was filled with so much light that its pressure mattered for the whole universe. Not as a push: a pressure that is the same everywhere pushes equally in all directions. Instead, as the university cosmology course (8.286) shows, pressure adds to the pull of gravity and makes the expansion slow down faster.
</Callout>

Lesson 3.7 takes the next step: when two objects push on each other, Newton's third law (lesson 2.4) says they get equal and opposite impulses, and that leads to one of the great conservation laws of physics.

## Summary

- **Momentum** is mass times velocity: $\vec p = m\vec v$. It is a vector in the direction of the velocity, measured in kg·m/s = N·s. Along a line, choose a positive direction; the sign of $p$ gives the direction.
- Momentum and kinetic energy are different: objects with the same momentum can have very different kinetic energies.
- The **change in momentum** is $\Delta \vec p = \vec p_f - \vec p_i = m\,\Delta \vec v$. Use signed velocities: a bounce gives a larger change than a stop.
- **Newton's second law** in terms of momentum: $\vec F_{\text{net}} = \Delta \vec p / \Delta t$. For a constant mass it is the same as $\vec F_{\text{net}} = m\vec a$.
- **Impulse** is $\vec J = \vec F_{\text{net}}\,\Delta t = \Delta \vec p$. For a changing force it is the area under the force–time graph, and the average force is $J / \Delta t$.
- For a given change in momentum, a longer stopping time means a smaller force. Airbags, seat belts, crumple zones and bent knees all use this.
