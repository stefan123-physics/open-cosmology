A rocket is the clearest example of momentum at work. Lesson 2.4 explained why a rocket moves at all: its engine pushes gas out backwards, and the gas pushes the rocket forwards. This lesson makes that precise with conservation of momentum (lesson 3.7). It answers three questions: how much faster does a rocket go when it throws out a little gas, how large is the push of its engine, and how much fuel does it need to reach a given speed? The answers explain why launch rockets are tall towers that are almost all fuel, and why they drop pieces of themselves on the way up.

## Pushing on its own exhaust

Inside a rocket engine, fuel burns with an oxidizer (a substance, such as liquid oxygen, that the fuel burns with). The hot gas that forms is pushed out through the nozzle at the back at high speed. To speed the gas up backwards, the rocket must push on it; by Newton's third law (lesson 2.4), the gas pushes on the rocket with a force of the same size in the opposite direction, forwards (MIT notes, Sec. 12.3). That forward force is the rocket's **thrust**.

The rocket does not push on the air or on the ground. It pushes on its own exhaust, which it carries with it as fuel. Because a rocket also carries its own oxidizer, it needs no air at all, and it works in the vacuum of space, where an aeroplane engine or a propeller could not (NASA Glenn, Rocket Thrust).

The **exhaust speed** $u$ is the speed of the gas **relative to the rocket**, as lesson 1.9 defines relative velocity: the speed at which the gas leaves the nozzle as seen by someone riding on the rocket. It is set by the engine and its fuel, not by how fast the rocket is already moving (MIT notes, Sec. 12.3).

## Choosing the system

The mass of a rocket changes all the time: every second its engine throws out part of its fuel. That makes it tricky to use Newton's second law for the rocket alone. Lesson 3.6 wrote the second law as $\vec F_{\text{net}} = \Delta \vec p / \Delta t$, but that form is for an object whose mass stays the same; applied to a body that is losing mass, it gives wrong answers. The MIT notes warn that mass flow needs care (Sec. 12.1) and keep the mass of the system fixed (Sec. 12.3, Eqs. (12.3.71) and (12.3.72)): the trick is to choose a system whose mass does **not** change.

So choose as the system the rocket **together with** the gas that it throws out during the time we study, as lesson 3.7 taught (reading 1, Sec. 10.3). The push of the rocket on the gas and the push of the gas on the rocket are then internal forces. If no external force acts (a rocket far out in space, with no gravity or air to speak of), the system is isolated and its total momentum stays the same (reading 1, Sec. 10.7). The rocket gains forward momentum, and the gas gains exactly as much backward momentum.

## One small burn step

Follow the rocket for one short moment, in which it throws out a small mass $\Delta m$ of gas. Take the direction of motion as positive.

- **Before:** the rocket, of mass $m$ (including the fuel still on board, so also the bit $\Delta m$ about to leave), moves at velocity $v$. The total momentum is $m v$.
- **After:** the rocket, now of mass $m - \Delta m$, moves at $v + \Delta v$. The gas leaves backwards at speed $u$ relative to the rocket, so its velocity relative to the ground is $v + \Delta v - u$ (lesson 1.9).

The total momentum after the step is therefore

$(m - \Delta m)(v + \Delta v) + \Delta m\,(v + \Delta v - u).$

Multiply out the brackets: the terms $-\Delta m\,v$ and $-\Delta m\,\Delta v$ from the first product cancel against $+\Delta m\,v$ and $+\Delta m\,\Delta v$ from the second, leaving $m v + m\,\Delta v - u\,\Delta m$. This is the momentum of the system at the end of the step in the MIT notes (Eq. (12.3.73)). Conservation of momentum sets it equal to the momentum before:

$m v = m v + m\,\Delta v - u\,\Delta m, \quad\text{so}\quad m\,\Delta v = u\,\Delta m.$

<Formula label="Speed gained in one small burn step" tex="\Delta v = \frac{u\,\Delta m}{m}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 12.3, Eqs. (12.3.70) to (12.3.74): momentum of the system at t and t + Δt (the notes then let Δt shrink to zero)" />

Three things stand out.

- The speed $v$ that the rocket already has drops out. A burn gives the same gain in speed whether the rocket starts at rest or already moves at 7 km/s. Seen from a frame moving with the rocket, nothing about the burn has changed (lesson 1.9).
- A faster exhaust gives more speed for the same fuel. Engine builders work hard to make $u$ as large as possible.
- The same $\Delta m$ helps more when the rocket is lighter, because $m$ is in the denominator. A kilogram of gas thrown out near the end of the burn, when most of the fuel is gone, adds more speed than a kilogram thrown out at the start.

<WorkedExample title="A short burst from a space probe">
<Problem>
A space probe of 1200 kg, fuel included, drifts far from any planet. A small thruster fires a burst that throws out 2.0 kg of gas backwards at 2400 m/s relative to the probe. How much faster does the probe go?
</Problem>
<Step title="Choose the system">
The probe and the 2.0 kg of gas together; the pushes between them are internal and no external force acts. Forwards is positive.
</Step>
<Step title="Apply the step formula">
$\Delta v = u\,\Delta m / m = 2400 \times 2.0 / 1200 = 4.0$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: (m/s) × kg / kg = m/s. Size: the gas carries off momentum $2.0 \times 2400 = 4800$ kg·m/s backwards relative to the probe, so the probe must gain about 4800 kg·m/s forwards; divided by its mass of about 1200 kg that is 4.0 m/s. The gain is small because only one six-hundredth of the mass was thrown out.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Thrust

How large is the force of the gas on the rocket? Look at the gas thrown out in one step, a fixed lump of mass $\Delta m$, so lesson 3.6 may be used for it. Before the step it moved with the rocket; afterwards it moves backwards at $u$ relative to the rocket. Its velocity has changed by very nearly $-u$ (strictly by $\Delta v - u$, but the tiny $\Delta v$ of a short step can be ignored), so its momentum by about $-u\,\Delta m$. By the impulse–momentum theorem (lesson 3.6), the rocket gave it an impulse of about $-u\,\Delta m$, a push backwards. By Newton's third law, the gas gave the rocket an equal impulse forwards, about $+u\,\Delta m$, during the same time $\Delta t$. Dividing by $\Delta t$ gives the force on the rocket, the **thrust**:

<Formula label="Thrust" tex="F_{\text{thrust}} = \frac{\Delta m}{\Delta t}\,u" cite="mit" at="Sec. 12.3, Eq. (12.3.79), written there with the rate at which the fuel is thrown out" />

The thrust is the **mass thrown out per second** times the **exhaust speed**. Its unit is (kg/s) × (m/s) = kg·m/s², the newton. The MIT notes stress that thrust is not an extra force from outside: it is the forward recoil of the rocket as it throws its fuel out (Sec. 12.3). Real engines get a little extra thrust (or lose a little) because the gas pressure at the end of the nozzle differs from the pressure of the air outside; NASA Glenn's thrust equation includes that term, which we leave out (NASA Glenn, Rocket Thrust).

Once the thrust is known, Newton's second law tells how the rocket's speed changes at any instant: the rocket's acceleration is the thrust plus the external forces, divided by the rocket's mass at that instant (MIT notes, Eq. (12.3.78)). For a rocket climbing straight up, the external force is its weight $m g$, pulling down.

<WorkedExample title="Can it lift off?">
<Problem>
A small launch rocket has a mass of $5.0 \times 10^{4}$ kg on the launch pad. Its engines throw out 250 kg of gas per second at 2.8 km/s relative to the rocket. Find the thrust, and the rocket's acceleration just after the engines start, straight up.
</Problem>
<Step title="Thrust">
$F_{\text{thrust}} = 250 \times 2800 = 7.0 \times 10^{5}$ N, upwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Weight">
$F_g = m g = 5.0 \times 10^{4} \times 9.81 \approx 4.9 \times 10^{5}$ N, downwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Acceleration">
Up is positive. The net force is $7.0 \times 10^{5} - 4.9 \times 10^{5} \approx 2.1 \times 10^{5}$ N upwards, so $a = F_{\text{net}} / m \approx 2.1 \times 10^{5} / 5.0 \times 10^{4} \approx 4.2$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: N / kg = m/s². The thrust is bigger than the weight, so the rocket can rise; had the weight been bigger, it would have stayed on the pad with its engines roaring. The acceleration grows during the climb: the thrust stays about the same while the mass, and so the weight, drops as the fuel burns.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Real rockets work the same way. NASA's Saturn V, the rocket of the Apollo Moon flights, had a mass of about 2.8 million kg when fully fuelled, and it gave 34.5 million newtons of thrust at launch (NASA, What Was the Saturn V?). Its weight was about $2.8 \times 10^{6} \times 9.81 \approx 2.75 \times 10^{7}$ N, so the thrust was only about a quarter more than the weight, and the rocket rose slowly at first, at about $(3.45 \times 10^{7} - 2.75 \times 10^{7}) / 2.8 \times 10^{6} \approx 2.5$ m/s².

## Adding up many steps

A real burn is a long string of small steps, and in each one the rocket is a little lighter than in the step before. So each step adds a little more speed than the one before it.

<WorkedExample title="A burn in six steps">
<Problem>
A rocket of 1000 kg in empty space has an exhaust speed of 2000 m/s. It throws out 600 kg of fuel in six bursts of 100 kg. How much speed does it gain?
</Problem>
<Step title="Step by step">
Use $\Delta v = u\,\Delta m / m$ with the mass before each burst: 1000, 900, 800, 700, 600 and 500 kg. The six gains are $2000 \times 100 / 1000 = 200$ m/s, then about 222, 250, 286, 333 and 400 m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Add them">
The total is about 1690 m/s. The rocket ends at 400 kg.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Each gain is larger than the one before, as it should be, because the rocket keeps getting lighter. If all 600 kg had been thrown out in one burst at the start, the formula would have given $2000 \times 600 / 1000 = 1200$ m/s: smaller, because each kilogram would have had to push the full mass of 1000 kg.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

A real engine throws its fuel out smoothly, which is like taking more and more, smaller and smaller bursts. Each kilogram then leaves when the rocket is a bit lighter than in the six-burst version, so the total rises: with very many tiny steps it approaches about 1830 m/s. Adding up a quantity in thinner and thinner strips is the idea of lesson 0.10 (the area under a graph). The MIT notes do this adding with calculus (Sec. 12.3.1, Eqs. (12.3.80) to (12.3.84)), and the result is the **rocket equation**, also called the ideal rocket equation (NASA Glenn, Ideal Rocket Equation):

<Formula label="The rocket equation" tex="\Delta v = u \ln \frac{m_i}{m_f}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 12.3.1, Eq. (12.3.84)" />

Here $m_i$ is the mass of the rocket at the start of the burn, $m_f$ its mass at the end, and $\ln$ is the natural logarithm of lesson 0.9 (the LN key on a calculator). The ratio $R = m_i / m_f$ is the **mass ratio** (MIT notes, Eq. (12.3.86); the notes use it for a burn that uses up all the fuel, so that $m_f$ is the empty, or dry, mass). It holds when no external force acts, so it ignores gravity and air.

For the burn above, $m_i / m_f = 1000 / 400 = 2.5$ and $\Delta v = 2000 \times \ln 2.5 \approx 1.83 \times 10^{3}$ m/s, the value the many-step total approaches. Because the rocket only loses mass, $m_i / m_f$ is larger than 1, its logarithm is positive, and the rocket speeds up. (The MIT notes write that the logarithm is larger than 1; larger than 0 is what is meant.)

Notice what the rocket equation does **not** contain: the rate at which the fuel burns, or how long the burn takes. In empty space, only the exhaust speed and the mass ratio count.

## Why rockets are mostly fuel

The logarithm grows slowly. Doubling the mass ratio adds only $u \ln 2 \approx 0.69\,u$ to the final speed, however large the ratio already is. To gain a speed several times larger than the exhaust speed, a rocket needs an enormous mass ratio. Turn the rocket equation round, using $e^{\ln x} = x$ (lesson 0.9):

<WorkedExample title="How much of a rocket must be fuel?">
<Problem>
A rocket must reach 7.7 km/s, about the speed of a low orbit round the Earth (lesson 2.12 found 7.7 km/s for the International Space Station). Its exhaust speed is 3.2 km/s. Ignoring gravity and air, what mass ratio does it need, and what fraction of its starting mass must be fuel?
</Problem>
<Step title="Mass ratio">
$m_i / m_f = e^{7.7 / 3.2} = e^{2.41} \approx 11$.
</Step>
<Step title="Fuel fraction">
At the end only $1/11 \approx 0.09$ of the starting mass is left: tanks, engines and payload (the cargo it carries, such as a satellite) together. About 91% of the rocket at launch must be fuel.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Put the result back in: $3.2 \times \ln 11.1 \approx 3.2 \times 2.41 \approx 7.7$ km/s. A mass ratio of about 11 means that the empty rocket, payload included, has less than a tenth of the mass of the fuelled one: the tanks must be very thin and light, and still carry the engines and the payload.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

And this is the best case. In reality a rocket loses speed to air drag on the way up, and to gravity: for a rocket climbing straight up, the MIT notes find that gravity takes away $g$ times the burn time (Sec. 12.3.2, Eqs. (12.3.100) and (12.3.101)):

<Formula label="Rocket climbing straight up in uniform gravity" tex="\Delta v = u \ln \frac{m_i}{m_f} - g\,t" cite="mit" at="Sec. 12.3.2, Eqs. (12.3.100) and (12.3.101), for a burn of time t starting from rest" />

Gravity pulls on the rocket and its gas during the whole burn, and over a burn of 120 s it takes away up to $9.81 \times 120 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{3}$ m/s. That is why the MIT notes conclude that the fuel should be burned as fast as possible (Sec. 12.3.2). So a real launch to orbit needs more than 7.7 km/s from its engines, and an even larger mass ratio. Leaving the Earth altogether needs about 11.2 km/s, the escape speed (lesson 2.12; lesson 3.11 works it out from energy), and with the same exhaust speed a mass ratio of about $e^{11.2/3.2} = e^{3.5} \approx 33$, before any losses. The Saturn V at launch had about 24 times the mass of what it could put into Earth orbit (about 118 000 kg) and about 64 times the mass of what it could send to the Moon (about 43 500 kg) (NASA, What Was the Saturn V?). These numbers compare the launch mass with the payload, not $m_i/m_f$ of one burn, so they cannot be set against the mass ratios above: only staging makes them possible.

## Stages

A rocket of one piece has a problem: once half its fuel is gone, it still carries the large, half-empty tanks and all its engines, and every later burst of gas must speed up that dead weight too. The answer is to build the rocket in **stages**: separate sections stacked on top of each other, each with its own tanks and engines. When a stage's fuel is used up, the stage is dropped and the next one fires. Each stage then starts lighter, its mass ratio is higher, and the same fuel gives more speed (MIT notes, Sec. 12.3.1, after Eq. (12.3.87)). The Saturn V had three stages: the first lifted it to about 68 km, the second carried it almost into orbit, and the third reached orbit and later pushed the spacecraft towards the Moon (NASA, What Was the Saturn V?).

<WorkedExample title="One stage or two?">
<Problem>
A rocket of 100 t (100 000 kg) has an exhaust speed of 2.8 km/s and carries a payload of 1.0 t. It holds 90 t of fuel in tanks and engines of 9.0 t. Compare (a) one stage, burning all 90 t, with (b) two stages: a first stage of 70 t of fuel in 7.0 t of tanks and engines, dropped when empty, and a second stage of 20 t of fuel in 2.0 t of tanks and engines. Ignore gravity and air.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) One stage">
$m_i = 100$ t and, with all fuel burned, $m_f = 9.0 + 1.0 = 10$ t. So $\Delta v = 2.8 \ln 10 \approx 2.8 \times 2.30 \approx 6.4$ km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) First stage">
The first stage burns 70 t: from 100 t down to 30 t. $\Delta v_1 = 2.8 \ln (100/30) \approx 2.8 \times 1.20 \approx 3.37$ km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Second stage">
Drop the empty first stage (7.0 t): 23 t is left. The second stage burns 20 t, down to 3.0 t (its tanks and engines plus the payload). $\Delta v_2 = 2.8 \ln (23/3.0) \approx 2.8 \times 2.04 \approx 5.70$ km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
Two stages give $3.37 + 5.70 \approx 9.1$ km/s, against 6.4 km/s for one stage, with the same fuel, the same engines and the same payload. Only dropping 7.0 t of empty hardware at the right moment made the difference.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The masses add up: $70 + 7.0 + 20 + 2.0 + 1.0 = 100$ t. The speeds of the two stages may simply be added because each step's gain does not depend on the speed the rocket already has. Units: km/s times a pure number (a logarithm has no unit) gives km/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A rocket pushes gas out backwards; by Newton's third law the gas pushes the rocket forwards. It needs no air to push on, so it works in space.
- Never use $F = \Delta p / \Delta t$ for a body whose mass changes. Take the rocket **and** the gas it throws out as the system: then the total momentum is conserved when no external force acts.
- One small burn step: throwing out a mass $\Delta m$ at speed $u$ relative to the rocket gives $\Delta v = u\,\Delta m / m$, whatever speed the rocket already has.
- **Thrust** is the mass thrown out per second times the exhaust speed, $F_{\text{thrust}} = (\Delta m / \Delta t)\,u$. With the weight, it gives the rocket's acceleration at each instant.
- Adding many steps gives the **rocket equation**, $\Delta v = u \ln (m_i / m_f)$, with $m_i / m_f$ the mass ratio. Because the logarithm grows slowly, a speed of a few times $u$ needs a rocket that is mostly fuel: a mass ratio of about 11 for low orbit, more with gravity and drag, and about 33 to escape.
- **Stages** drop empty tanks and engines on the way, so each burn has a better mass ratio and the same fuel gives more speed.
