Block 2 described motion with forces. Block 3 adds a second way of looking at the same motion: through energy. The bridge between the two is **work**. In everyday speech, "work" is anything that makes you tired. In physics it has a precise meaning: a force does work on an object when the object moves along or against the direction of the force, and the work measures how much energy the force hands over. This lesson defines work, shows when it is positive, negative or zero, and finds it from a graph. Lesson 3.2 then shows what the energy handed over does: it changes the object's speed.

## Force times displacement

Push a supermarket trolley along a straight aisle. The harder you push, and the further you push it, the more you have done. Work captures both. For a constant force acting along the direction of motion, the work is the force times the displacement. In general the force may point at an angle to the motion, and then only the part of the force along the displacement counts (Siyavula 5.2; MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 13.8.2):

<Formula label="Work done by a constant force" tex="W = F\,\Delta x\cos\theta" cite="mit" at="5.2 Work, definition box (W in J, F in N, Δx in m, θ the angle between force and displacement)" />

Here $F$ is the size of the force, $\Delta x$ the size of the displacement of the object (lesson 1.1), and $\theta$ the angle between the force and the displacement. The factor $F\cos\theta$ is the component of the force along the displacement (lesson 0.8). The other component, at right angles to the motion, does no work.

Three things to notice:

- **Work is a scalar.** It has a size and a sign, but no direction. The force and the displacement are vectors; their combination is just a number with a unit.
- **Both are needed.** No displacement, no work: pushing hard against a wall that does not move does no work on the wall, however tired you get. No force along the motion, no work either.
- **Work is done by a particular force on a particular object.** Always say which: "the work done by friction on the box".

The unit of work is a newton times a metre. It is called the **joule** (J), after James Joule:

<Formula label="The joule" tex="1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N·m} = 1\ \text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}^2" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 13.4, Eq. (13.4.3)" />

One joule is the work done by a force of one newton acting along a displacement of one metre. Lifting a 100 g apple from the floor to a shelf 1.0 m high takes about $0.10 \times 9.81 \times 1.0 \approx 1$ J (the reason is explained below, in "Lifting against gravity").

<WorkedExample title="Pushing a trolley">
<Problem>
You push a supermarket trolley 12 m along a straight aisle with a steady horizontal force of 45 N in the direction it moves. How much work do you do on the trolley?
</Problem>
<Step title="Angle">
The force and the displacement point the same way, so $\theta = 0°$ and $\cos 0° = 1$.
</Step>
<Step title="Work">
$W = F\Delta x\cos\theta = 45 \times 12 \times 1 = 540$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: N × m = J. Size: about 45 N is the weight of a 4.6 kg bag, and 12 m is a short aisle, so a few hundred joules is reasonable.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A force at an angle

When you pull a sledge with a rope, the rope points up as well as forwards. Only the forward part of the pull moves the sledge along; the upward part only lifts a little of its weight off the snow.

<WorkedExample title="Pulling a sledge">
<Problem>
A child pulls a sledge 15 m across flat snow. The rope makes an angle of 35° with the ground, and the pull in the rope is 80 N. How much work does the rope do on the sledge?
</Problem>
<Step title="The component along the motion">
The sledge moves horizontally, so $\theta = 35°$. The forward component is $F\cos\theta = 80 \times \cos 35° = 80 \times 0.819 = 65.5$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Work">
$W = F\Delta x\cos\theta = 80 \times 15 \times \cos 35° \approx 983$ J, about $9.8 \times 10^{2}$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Pulling along the ground ($\theta = 0°$) with the same 80 N would give $80 \times 15 = 1200$ J; at an angle the work must be less, and it is. The upward component (45.9 N) is at 90° to the motion and does none of the work.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Positive, negative and zero work

The sign of the work comes from $\cos\theta$ (lesson 0.7):

| Angle between force and displacement | $\cos\theta$ | Work |
|---|---|---|
| $0° \le \theta < 90°$ (force helps the motion) | positive | positive |
| $\theta = 90°$ (force at right angles to the motion) | zero | zero |
| $90° < \theta \le 180°$ (force opposes the motion) | negative | negative |

The sign is not a direction (work has none). It says which way the energy goes: a force that does **positive work** gives energy to the object; a force that does **negative work** takes energy from it (Siyavula 5.2). A force at $\theta = 180°$, pointing straight against the motion, does $W = -F\Delta x$.

**Negative work.** Throw a ball of 0.15 kg straight up. While it rises 4.0 m, gravity pulls down and the ball moves up, so $\theta = 180°$, and gravity does $W = -mg\,\Delta x = -0.15 \times 9.81 \times 4.0 \approx -5.9$ J on the ball. That is why the ball slows down. On the way down, gravity and the motion point the same way and gravity does $+5.9$ J: the ball speeds up again (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 13.4, Example 13.5). Kinetic friction on a sliding object and the force of brakes on a car also do negative work.

**Zero work.** A force at right angles to the motion does no work at all. Three important cases:

- **The normal force** (lesson 2.5) on an object sliding along a level floor pushes straight up, while the object moves sideways. The weight, pulling straight down, also does no work on it.
- **Carrying a bag** across a level room at a steady speed: your hand pushes up on the bag, the bag moves horizontally, so your hand does no work on the bag. Your arm still gets tired, because your muscles use energy just to keep pushing, but none of that energy goes into the bag (Siyavula 5.2 makes the same point with a strongman carrying heavy loads).
- **A centripetal force** (lesson 2.9) points to the centre of the circle, while the object moves along the circle, at 90° to the radius. So the tension in the string of a ball whirled in a circle, or the pull of gravity on a satellite in a circular orbit, does no work. That is exactly why these objects keep the same speed: the force changes the direction of the velocity but never hands over energy.

## Work done by several forces

Usually several forces act on an object at once. Each does its own work, and the **net work** (or total work) is the sum of them all, with their signs. There are two equivalent ways to find it (Siyavula 5.2, "Net work"):

1. Find the work done by each force separately and add the results.
2. Find the net force $F_{\text{net}}$ first (lesson 2.3), and work out the work it does along the displacement:

<Formula label="Net work from the net force" tex="W_{\text{net}} = F_{\text{net}}\,\Delta x\cos\theta" cite="mit" at="Net work, Approach 2 (work done by the resultant force)" />

The net work is what decides whether the object speeds up or slows down. Lesson 3.2 turns this into the work–energy theorem.

<WorkedExample title="Pushing a box against friction">
<Problem>
You push a 20 kg box 4.0 m across a level floor with a horizontal force of 100 N. The coefficient of kinetic friction between box and floor is $\mu_k = 0.25$ (lesson 2.6). Find the work done by each force and the net work.
</Problem>
<Step title="The forces">
Four forces act: your push (100 N forwards), kinetic friction (backwards), the weight (down) and the normal force (up). The floor is level and the push is horizontal, so $F_N = mg = 20 \times 9.81 = 196.2$ N, and the friction is $F_f = \mu_k F_N = 0.25 \times 196.2 = 49.05$ N, about 49 N.
</Step>
<Step title="Work done by each force">
Your push: $100 \times 4.0 \times \cos 0° = 400$ J. Friction: $49.05 \times 4.0 \times \cos 180° \approx -196$ J. The weight and the normal force are at 90° to the motion: 0 J each.
</Step>
<Step title="Net work, first way">
$W_{\text{net}} = 400 - 196 + 0 + 0 \approx 204$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Net work, second way">
The net force is $F_{\text{net}} = 100 - 49.05 = 50.95$ N forwards, along the motion, so $W_{\text{net}} = 50.95 \times 4.0 \approx 204$ J. The two ways agree, as they must.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The net work is positive, so the box gains energy and speeds up: the push is larger than the friction, which matches Newton's second law. Had you pushed with a force equal to the friction (49.05 N) on a box that was already sliding, it would keep a constant speed and the net work would be zero.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Work against friction

For an object sliding a distance $\Delta x$, kinetic friction always points straight against the motion ($\theta = 180°$), so its work is always negative:

<Formula label="Work done by kinetic friction" tex="W = -\mu_k F_N\,\Delta x" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 13.4, Eq. (13.4.5), there with F_N = mg on a level surface" />

The energy that friction takes from a sliding object does not vanish: the surfaces get warmer. Rub your hands together and you feel it. Lesson 3.4 follows that energy. The longer the path, the more work friction does: a box dragged along a winding route loses more energy than one dragged straight.

## Lifting against gravity

To lift an object of mass $m$ at a steady speed, you must push up with a force equal to its weight, $mg$ (lesson 2.3: zero acceleration, zero net force). Lift it through a height $h$ and your force and the displacement both point up, so you do work

<Formula label="Work done lifting an object at steady speed" tex="W = m\,g\,h" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 13.4, Example 13.5, Eq. (13.4.16): gravity does −mgΔy on a rising object; the lifting force does the opposite" />

At the same time gravity does $-mgh$, so the net work is zero and the speed does not change. Lesson 3.3 shows that the work you did is not lost: it is stored as **gravitational potential energy**, and you get it back when the object falls.

<WorkedExample title="Loading a crate onto a truck">
<Problem>
A crate of apples with a mass of 8.0 kg is lifted 1.5 m from the ground onto the back of a truck, at a steady speed. (a) How much work is done on the crate by the person lifting it? (b) Instead, the crate is pushed at a steady speed up a smooth ramp that is 3.0 m long and rises 1.5 m. How much work is done now?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Straight up">
$W = mgh = 8.0 \times 9.81 \times 1.5 \approx 118$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Up the ramp">
The ramp rises 1.5 m over 3.0 m, so its angle with the ground is 30° ($\sin\theta = 1.5/3.0$, lesson 0.7). On a smooth ramp, the push needed along the slope is the component of the weight along the slope, $mg\sin\theta$ (lesson 2.8): $8.0 \times 9.81 \times 0.5 = 39.2$ N. That is only half the weight, but it acts over twice the distance: $W = 39.2 \times 3.0 \approx 118$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The ramp makes the job feel easier (a smaller force), not smaller: the work is the same, $mgh$, because the crate ends at the same height. Without friction, the work needed to lift something depends only on how high it goes.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Work as the area under a graph

Draw a graph of the force along the motion against the position. For a constant force it is a horizontal line, and the work $F\Delta x$ is the **area of the rectangle** under it: height times width. This is the same idea as the area under a velocity–time graph in lesson 0.10, where the area gave a distance.

When the force changes along the way, the formula $W = F\Delta x\cos\theta$ no longer applies directly, but the area still gives the work. Cut the area into thin strips: across each strip the force is almost constant, so each strip is almost a rectangle of area (force) × (short distance), and the strips add up to the work (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 13.5):

<Formula label="Work from a force–displacement graph" tex="W = \text{area under the force–displacement graph}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 13.5, Eq. (13.5.3) and Figure 13.5" />

For an object moving forwards, area below the horizontal axis (a force pointing backwards) counts as negative work.

## Stretching a spring

A spring is the most important force that changes along the way. To stretch a spring slowly from its natural length, your hand must pull with a force equal to the spring's pull back, $F = kx$ (Hooke's law, lesson 2.7), where $x$ is the extension. So the force grows from zero at the start to $kx$ at the end, and its graph is a straight line through the origin. The area under it is a **triangle** with base $x$ and height $kx$:

<Formula label="Work done stretching a spring from its natural length" tex="W = \tfrac12\,k\,x^2" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 13.5, Example 13.6, Eq. (13.5.5) with the spring starting at its natural length (the spring itself does the same work with a minus sign)" />

The same work is needed to compress a spring by $x$. While you stretch the spring, the spring itself pulls back, against the motion, and does $-\tfrac12 kx^2$. Lesson 3.3 gives this stored energy its name, **elastic potential energy**, and lesson 3.4 puts it to full use: the work you did is stored in the spring, ready to be given back.

<WorkedExample title="Stretching an exercise spring">
<Problem>
A spring of an exercise device has $k = 250$ N/m. (a) How much work does it take to stretch it 0.12 m from its natural length? (b) How much more to stretch it from 0.12 m to 0.24 m?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The first 12 cm">
$W = \tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12 \times 250 \times 0.12^2 = 1.8$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The next 12 cm">
Up to 0.24 m: $\tfrac12 \times 250 \times 0.24^2 = 7.2$ J. So the second 12 cm takes $7.2 - 1.8 = 5.4$ J, three times as much as the first, because the spring pulls back harder the further it is stretched.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Another route: over the first 12 cm the force grows steadily from 0 to 30 N, so on average it is 15 N, and $15 \times 0.12 = 1.8$ J. Doubling the extension multiplies the work by four, because $x$ is squared.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
The definition of work as force times distance reaches all the way to the expanding universe. A gas presses on the walls of its container (its pressure, Block 4, is the force on each square metre of wall). When the gas expands and pushes a wall outwards, it does work and, when no heat flows in, loses that much energy. In the university cosmology course, Guth applies exactly this to the light that fills the universe: as the universe expands, the light's pressure does work, and that work is why the energy of radiation falls faster than that of matter (the same loss shows up as the stretching of each light wave; MIT 8.286, Lecture 15). Lesson 3.12 uses energy, built from work, to ask whether an expanding ball of matter will ever stop.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A constant force does **work** $W = F\Delta x\cos\theta$ on an object, with $\theta$ the angle between the force and the displacement. Only the component of the force along the motion counts.
- Work is a **scalar**, measured in **joules**: 1 J = 1 N·m = 1 kg·m²/s².
- Work is **positive** when the force helps the motion (energy goes into the object), **negative** when it opposes it, and **zero** when the force is at right angles to the motion or nothing moves. The normal force on a sliding object and a centripetal force do no work.
- The **net work** is the sum of the works of all the forces, or the work of the net force.
- Kinetic friction does negative work, $-\mu_k F_N\Delta x$; lifting an object at a steady speed takes work $mgh$.
- The work is the **area under the force–displacement graph**; for stretching a spring by $x$ it is the triangle $\tfrac12 kx^2$.
