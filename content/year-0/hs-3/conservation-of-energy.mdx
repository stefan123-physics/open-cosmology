A roller coaster has no engine on the way down. A chain pulls the train up the first hill, and from then on it races through dips and over smaller hills on its own, fastest at the lowest points and slowest at the tops. Where does its speed come from, and why can no later hill be higher than the first one? The answer is one of the most useful rules in all of physics: energy is never made or destroyed, only changed from one form into another. This lesson uses it to find speeds and heights without knowing any forces along the way.

## Mechanical energy

Lessons 3.2 and 3.3 met two kinds of energy. **Kinetic energy**, $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}$, is the energy of motion. **Gravitational potential energy**, $E_p = m g h$, is the energy stored by height above a chosen zero level. Their sum is called the **mechanical energy** (Siyavula 22.4):

<Formula label="Mechanical energy" tex="E_{\text{mech}} = E_k + E_p = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v^{2} + m\,g\,h" cite="reading1" at="Mechanical energy (ESAHN), definition box, written there E_M = E_P + E_K = mgh + ½mv²" />

Like its two parts, mechanical energy is a scalar, measured in joules. Its value depends on the zero level chosen for $E_p$, so keep the same zero level for the whole problem; as in lesson 3.3, only changes have a physical meaning.

<WorkedExample title="A delivery drone">
<Problem>
A delivery drone of mass 1.2 kg flies at 8.0 m/s, 25 m above the ground. Find its mechanical energy, with the ground as the zero level.
</Problem>
<Step title="Kinetic energy">
$E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1.2 \times 8.0^{2} = 38.4$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Potential energy">
$E_p = m g h = 1.2 \times 9.81 \times 25 = 294.3$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Add them">
$E_{\text{mech}} = 38.4 + 294.3 = 332.7$ J, about 330 J.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Both parts are in joules, so they may be added. Most of the energy is potential: 25 m of height is worth much more than 8.0 m/s of speed. To fall freely from rest through 25 m, a drone would reach about 22 m/s, far more than 8.0 m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Energy is conserved

Every observation so far agrees with one law (Siyavula 22.5; MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 14.1):

**The law of conservation of energy:** energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only change from one form into another, or pass from one object to another.

Besides kinetic and potential energy there are many other forms: **thermal energy** (the random jiggling of the molecules, the tiny particles a substance is made of (Block 4), in a warm object), chemical energy (stored in food and fuel), sound, light and electrical energy. A system that no energy enters or leaves is called **isolated** (Siyavula calls this a closed system; MIT's notes use "closed system" for one that matter cannot leave, and add "no energy entering or leaving" separately); its total energy stays exactly the same, however the energy inside it changes form.

## When mechanical energy is conserved

Within that general law there is a simpler rule that is often enough. Think of an object on which **only gravity does work**. By the work–energy theorem of lesson 3.2, the work done by gravity is the gain in kinetic energy, $\Delta E_k$. By lesson 3.3, the work done by gravity is also minus the change in potential energy, $-\Delta E_p$. So $\Delta E_k = -\Delta E_p$, or

<Formula label="Conservation of mechanical energy" tex="\Delta E_k + \Delta E_p = 0" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 14.5, Eq. (14.18), written there ΔE_m = ΔK_sys + ΔU_sys = 0" />

Whatever potential energy the object loses, it gains as kinetic energy, and the other way round. The sum $E_k + E_p$ does not change: the **mechanical energy is conserved** (Siyavula 22.5). Comparing any starting point with any later point:

<Formula label="Mechanical energy at two points" tex="\tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v_i^{2} + m\,g\,h_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v_f^{2} + m\,g\,h_f" cite="mit" at="Using the law of conservation of energy (ESAHP), E_M1 = E_M2 written out as mgh + ½mv² on both sides" />

Here $v_i$ and $h_i$ are the speed and height at the start, $v_f$ and $h_f$ at the end. The same equation is Eq. (14.25) of MIT's notes (Sec. 14.5.1).

When does "only gravity does work" hold? Other forces may act, as long as they do **no work**. A force at right angles to the motion does no work (lesson 3.1). So:

- the **normal force** of a smooth track, slide or slope does no work, because it is always at right angles to the surface along which the object moves;
- the **tension** in a pendulum's string does no work, because the bob moves along a circle and the string always points to its centre, at right angles to the motion.

What does spoil the rule is **friction** or **air resistance**, which act against the motion; they are the subject of the last part of this lesson. Until then every problem says "ignore friction" or "smooth".

Two things make this rule powerful. First, the mass cancels: divide every term by $m$ and it is gone, so a heavy and a light object reach the same speed (lesson 1.7 found the same for free fall). Second, **the path does not matter** (Siyavula 22.5): only the heights and speeds at the two points appear. A curved track, a straight slope and a sheer drop of the same height give the same final speed, although they take different times.

<WorkedExample title="A sledge on an icy hill">
<Problem>
A sledge is pushed off the top of an icy hill at 3.0 m/s. The bottom of the hill is 12 m lower. Ignoring friction and air resistance, how fast is the sledge going at the bottom?
</Problem>
<Step title="Choose the zero level">
Take the bottom of the hill as the zero level: $h_i = 12$ m, $h_f = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Conserve mechanical energy">
$\tfrac{1}{2} m v_i^{2} + m g h_i = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_f^{2} + 0$. Divide by $m$ and multiply by 2: $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2 g h_i = 3.0^{2} + 2 \times 9.81 \times 12 = 244.4$ m²/s².
</Step>
<Step title="The speed">
$v_f = \sqrt{244.4} = 15.6$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: m²/s² under the root gives m/s. From rest the sledge would reach $\sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 12} = 15.3$ m/s; the push adds only a little, because energies add, not speeds: $3.0 + 15.3$ would be far too much. The shape of the hill and the mass of the sledge were never needed.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Following the energy on the way down

Watch a ball of 0.40 kg dropped from rest from 6.0 m, with air resistance ignored. With the ground as the zero level it starts with $E_p = 0.40 \times 9.81 \times 6.0 = 23.5$ J and no kinetic energy. As it falls, $E_p$ drops in proportion to the height and $E_k$ grows by exactly the same amount, so the total stays at 23.5 J all the way down. Halfway down, at 3.0 m, each part is 11.8 J; just before it lands, all 23.5 J is kinetic.

## A swing: the pendulum

A child's swing is a pendulum: a seat on ropes that swings along part of a circle. The ropes do no work, so mechanical energy is conserved, and the speed anywhere depends only on how far the seat is below its highest point.

<WorkedExample title="How fast is the swing?">
<Problem>
A swing is pulled back until its seat is 0.80 m higher than its lowest point, and let go from rest. Ignoring air resistance, find the speed of the seat at the lowest point, and the height at which it moves at half that speed.
</Problem>
<Step title="Lowest point">
Take the lowest point as the zero level. All the potential energy at the start becomes kinetic energy there: $m g h_i = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_f^{2}$, so $v_f = \sqrt{2 g h_i} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.80} = 3.96$ m/s, about 4.0 m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Half the speed">
Half the speed means a quarter of the kinetic energy ($E_k$ goes with $v^{2}$, lesson 3.2). So only a quarter of the starting potential energy has been turned into kinetic energy, and three quarters is still potential: the seat is at $\tfrac{3}{4} \times 0.80 = 0.60$ m above the lowest point.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
At 0.60 m the seat has dropped 0.20 m: $v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.20} = 1.98$ m/s, which is indeed half of 3.96 m/s. The length of the ropes and the mass of the child were never needed.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Adding a spring

Lesson 3.3 showed that a stretched or squeezed spring stores **elastic potential energy**, measured from the spring's natural length:

<Formula label="Elastic potential energy of a spring" tex="E_{\text{el}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,k\,x^{2}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 14.4.2, Eq. (14.12), written there U_s(x) = ½kx² with U_s(0) = 0" />

Here $k$ is the spring constant in N/m (lesson 2.7) and $x$ the extension or compression in metres. A spring, like gravity, gives back all the work done on it: the work it does depends only on where it starts and ends. Forces with this property are called **conservative forces**, and for each of them a potential energy can be defined (MIT 8.01SC notes, Secs. 14.2 and 14.3). So the rule widens: **when only gravity and springs do work, the mechanical energy, now including the spring's energy, is conserved**:

<Formula label="Mechanical energy with a spring" tex="E_{\text{mech}} = E_k + E_p + E_{\text{el}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v^{2} + m\,g\,h + \tfrac{1}{2}\,k\,x^{2} = \text{constant}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 14.5, Eq. (14.18), with the spring's U_s = ½kx² of Sec. 14.4.2 in ΔU_sys (as in Example 14.3, where the starting energy is ½kx²)" />

<WorkedExample title="A spring launcher">
<Problem>
A toy launcher has a spring with $k = 400$ N/m. It is squeezed by 0.050 m and fires a ball of 0.020 kg. (a) The launcher lies flat on a smooth table: how fast does the ball leave? (b) The launcher points straight up: how high does the ball rise above the point where it was released? Ignore friction and air resistance.
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy in the spring">
$E_{\text{el}} = \tfrac{1}{2} k x^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 400 \times 0.050^{2} = 0.50$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Flat on the table">
No height changes, so all 0.50 J becomes kinetic energy: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.020 \times v^{2} = 0.50$, so $v = \sqrt{2 \times 0.50 / 0.020} = 7.1$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Straight up">
At the top of its flight the ball is at rest for a moment, so all 0.50 J is gravitational potential energy: $m g h = 0.50$ J and $h = 0.50 / (0.020 \times 9.81) = 2.5$ m. (Strictly, the ball also rises by the 0.050 m of the spring's stroke before it leaves; measuring from the release point includes that.)
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: $\text{J} / (\text{kg} \times \text{m/s}^{2}) = \text{N m} / \text{N} = \text{m}$, a height, as it should be. A heavier ball would rise less: the same 0.50 J buys less height for more weight.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Lesson 3.3 left one picture open: a mass hanging on a spring, which has gravitational and elastic energy at the same time. Here is what happens when it is let go.

<WorkedExample title="A mass let go on a spring">
<Problem>
A mass of 0.50 kg is hooked onto the end of a spring with $k = 50$ N/m while the spring is at its natural length, and let go from rest. How far does it drop before it stops for a moment?
</Problem>
<Step title="Set up the energies">
Let it drop a distance $x$. Take the starting point as the zero level of height: at the bottom $h = -x$ and the speed is zero again. At the start all three energies are zero, so at the bottom $0 + m g\,(-x) + \tfrac{1}{2} k x^{2} = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve">
Divide by $x$: $\tfrac{1}{2} k x = m g$, so $x = 2 m g / k = 2 \times 0.50 \times 9.81 / 50 = 0.196$ m, about 0.20 m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
At that point gravity has given up $0.50 \times 9.81 \times 0.196 = 0.962$ J and the spring holds $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 50 \times 0.196^{2} = 0.962$ J. The answer is twice the stretch at which the mass would hang at rest, $m g / k = 0.098$ m (lesson 2.7): the mass overshoots that point and then bounces back up. It keeps oscillating around it, and only friction in the spring and air resistance slowly bring it to rest there.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Where friction's energy goes

Slide a book across a table and it stops: its kinetic energy is gone, and its height has not changed. Has energy been destroyed? No. Rub your hands together and they get warm; the book and the table get warmer in the same way, only by too little to feel. Friction turns the ordered motion of the whole book into the random motion of the molecules in the book and the table: into **thermal energy** (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 14.8). A little also leaves as sound.

Friction is a **non-conservative force**: the work it does depends on the path, and a longer path means more work (MIT 8.01SC notes, Secs. 14.2 and 14.7; lesson 3.1). Air resistance is another. For such forces no potential energy can be defined, and the mechanical energy changes. Repeat the argument of the start of the lesson, but now let the net work be the work of gravity plus the work $W_{\text{nc}}$ of the non-conservative forces: $\Delta E_k = -\Delta E_p + W_{\text{nc}}$, so

<Formula label="Work of non-conservative forces" tex="W_{\text{nc}} = \Delta E_k + \Delta E_p = \Delta E_{\text{mech}}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 14.7, the section's Eqs. (14.6) and (14.7): W_nc = ΔK + ΔU = ΔE_m" />

Friction acts against the motion, so its work is negative and the mechanical energy **decreases**. For kinetic friction of constant size $F_f$ over a distance $\Delta x$ (lesson 2.6), the work is $-F_f\,\Delta x$, and that much mechanical energy becomes thermal energy, shared between the object and the surface:

<Formula label="Thermal energy produced by sliding friction" tex="\text{thermal energy produced} = F_f\,\Delta x" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 14.8 (the kinetic energy lost to friction becomes thermal energy of the object and the surface), with the work of friction from Sec. 14.2, Eq. (14.8), W_f = −μ_k N Δx, and W_nc = ΔE_m from Sec. 14.7, Eq. (14.7)" />

The energy is not lost from the universe, but it cannot be collected again to push the book back: MIT's notes call such processes **irreversible** (Sec. 14.7). Total energy is still conserved, as the law says; only the mechanical part has gone down.

A non-conservative force can also **add** mechanical energy. When you push a swing, or a car's engine drives it uphill, the work done is positive and the mechanical energy rises. That energy comes from somewhere else: from the chemical energy of food in your muscles, or of fuel in the engine (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 14.8.1).

<WorkedExample title="Down a playground slide">
<Problem>
A child of 25 kg starts from rest at the top of a slide 2.5 m high and 4.0 m long, and reaches the bottom at 4.0 m/s. How much mechanical energy has become thermal energy, and what is the average friction force along the slide?
</Problem>
<Step title="Potential energy lost">
With the bottom as the zero level: $E_p = m g h = 25 \times 9.81 \times 2.5 = 613$ J at the top, zero at the bottom.
</Step>
<Step title="Kinetic energy gained">
$E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 25 \times 4.0^{2} = 200$ J at the bottom.
</Step>
<Step title="Thermal energy">
$W_{\text{nc}} = \Delta E_k + \Delta E_p = 200 - 613 = -413$ J. So about 413 J, roughly two thirds of the starting energy, became thermal energy in the child's clothes and the slide.
</Step>
<Step title="Average friction force">
The friction acted along the 4.0 m of the slide: $F_f = 413 / 4.0 = 103$ N, about 100 N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Without friction the child would arrive at $\sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 2.5} = 7.0$ m/s. The real speed, 4.0 m/s, is lower, as it must be. Energy accounts: 613 J at the start = 200 J of motion + 413 J of heat.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Try it

In the skate park, drag the skater to the top of the ramp and let go. Switch on the bar graph or the pie chart and watch the kinetic and potential energy trade places while their total stays the same. Then turn on friction: the total mechanical energy now shrinks and the thermal energy grows, until the skater comes to rest at the bottom. Try a ramp of another shape with the same height, and check that the speed at the bottom does not change.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Conservation of energy is the first tool of the university cosmology course. In MIT's 8.286, Alan Guth follows a particle on the edge of a huge, evenly expanding ball of matter. He first finds its motion from Newton's law of gravity, and then finds that its kinetic energy plus its gravitational potential energy stays constant as the ball grows (Lecture 6), exactly as for the sledge on the hill, but with the far-from-Earth form of potential energy that lesson 3.11 introduces. Written in terms of the expansion, that constant sum becomes, in this simple Newtonian picture, the Friedmann equation for how the universe expands (Lecture 7); lesson 3.12 works it through. Later courses show that for the universe as a whole, in Einstein's general relativity, the bookkeeping of energy is subtler than for a sledge, so the rule is used there with care.

## Summary

- **Law of conservation of energy:** energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form into another or passed between objects. An isolated system keeps its total energy.
- **Mechanical energy** is $E_{\text{mech}} = E_k + E_p = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2} + m g h$, in joules.
- When **only gravity and springs do work** (forces at right angles to the motion, such as a smooth normal force or a pendulum string, do none), mechanical energy is conserved: $\tfrac{1}{2} m v_i^{2} + m g h_i = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_f^{2} + m g h_f$. The mass cancels and the path does not matter.
- A spring adds its **elastic potential energy** $\tfrac{1}{2} k x^{2}$ to the sum. Gravity and springs are **conservative forces**.
- **Friction and air resistance** are non-conservative: $W_{\text{nc}} = \Delta E_{\text{mech}}$. Their negative work turns mechanical energy into **thermal energy** (and sound), $F_f\,\Delta x$ for sliding friction. Total energy is still conserved, but the process cannot be undone.
- A push or an engine does positive work and adds mechanical energy, taken from another form such as chemical energy.
