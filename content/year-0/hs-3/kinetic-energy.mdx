Lesson 3.1 defined **work**: a constant force $F$ that acts on an object while it moves through a displacement $\Delta x$ does work $W = F\,\Delta x \cos\theta$, measured in joules, where $\theta$ is the angle between the force and the displacement. The **net work** is the total work done by all the forces on the object together. But what does work actually *do*? Push a trolley along a smooth floor and it speeds up; let friction act on a sliding book and it slows down. Work changes how fast things move. This lesson gives a name to the energy of motion, **kinetic energy**, and finds the exact rule that links it to work: the **work–energy theorem**.

## Kinetic energy

**Kinetic energy** is the energy an object has because it is moving (the word comes from the Greek for "moving"; MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 13.2). An object of mass $m$ moving at speed $v$ has kinetic energy

<Formula label="Kinetic energy" tex="E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v^{2}" cite="reading1" at="Kinetic energy (ESAHL), definition" />

Some books write $K$ or KE instead of $E_k$; the MIT course notes use $K$ (Sec. 13.2, Eq. (13.2.1)). A few things to notice:

- **It is a scalar.** Kinetic energy has a size but no direction (lesson 0.8). Only the speed counts: a car moving north at 20 m/s has exactly the same kinetic energy as the same car moving south at 20 m/s.
- **It is never negative.** The mass is positive and $v^{2}$ is positive or zero, so $E_k \geq 0$. An object at rest has no kinetic energy.
- **It grows with the square of the speed** but only in proportion to the mass. Twice the mass gives twice the kinetic energy; twice the speed gives four times as much.

**The unit.** With the mass in kg and the speed in m/s, the unit is kg m²/s². Split it as (kg m/s²) × m: a newton (lesson 2.3: 1 N = 1 kg m/s²) times a metre, which is a joule, the unit of work from lesson 3.1. So kinetic energy and work are measured in the same unit, as they must be if one can turn into the other.

For example, a car of 1500 kg at 20 m/s has $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1500 \times 20^{2} = 3.0 \times 10^{5}$ J. A cyclist and bicycle of 90 kg together at 6.0 m/s have $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 90 \times 6.0^{2} \approx 1.6 \times 10^{3}$ J, so the car has about 190 times as much. That difference is a large part of why a crash with a car is so much worse than a crash with a bicycle.

## Where ½mv² comes from

The factor $\tfrac{1}{2}$ and the square are not guesses: they follow from Newton's second law and the equations of motion. Take an object of mass $m$ moving along a straight line, with a constant net force $F_{\text{net}}$ pointing along the motion. While the object moves through a displacement $\Delta x$ the net force does work

$W_{\text{net}} = F_{\text{net}}\,\Delta x$ (the angle is $0$, so $\cos\theta = 1$).

By Newton's second law (lesson 2.3) the net force gives the object a constant acceleration, $F_{\text{net}} = m a$, so $W_{\text{net}} = m\,a\,\Delta x$. Lesson 1.6 gave the equation of motion that links $a\,\Delta x$ to the speeds, without the time:

<Formula label="Velocity after a displacement Δx" tex="v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2\,a\,\Delta x" cite="mit" at="Sec. 13.3.1, Eqs. (13.3.1)–(13.3.5) (derived there from the position and velocity at constant acceleration, and written as a_x Δx = ½v_x,f² − ½v_x,i²)" />

Rearranged: $a\,\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_f^{2} - v_i^{2})$. Multiply by $m$:

<Formula label="Work done by a constant net force" tex="W_{\text{net}} = F_{\text{net}}\,\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v_f^{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v_i^{2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 13.3.1, Eqs. (13.3.6) and (13.3.7)" />

The quantity $\tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}$ appears by itself: the work done by the net force equals the change in $\tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}$. That is why it is worth giving that quantity a name.

## The work–energy theorem

The result of the last section, written with kinetic energy, is the **work–energy theorem**: *the net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy.*

<Formula label="Work–energy theorem" tex="W_{\text{net}} = \Delta E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v_f^{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\,m\,v_i^{2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 13.6, Eq. (13.6.1), p. 13-16 (written there W = ΔK = ½mv_f² − ½mv_i², with K for E_k)" />

The theorem says three things at once:

- **Positive net work** speeds the object up: its kinetic energy increases.
- **Negative net work** slows it down: its kinetic energy decreases. This happens when the net force points against the motion, like friction on a sliding box or the brakes of a car ($\theta = 180°$, $\cos\theta = -1$).
- **Zero net work** leaves the speed unchanged. In uniform circular motion (lessons 1.10 and 2.9) the net force points to the centre, always at 90° to the motion, so it does no work and the speed stays the same, even though the direction keeps changing.

Two remarks. First, "net work" is the total work of all the forces, which is the same as the work done by the net force: you may add the works of the separate forces, or find the net force first; both give the same answer. Second, we derived the theorem for a constant force, but it also holds when the force changes along the way; proving that needs calculus, which the university course does (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 13.6, Eq. (13.6.1)).

<WorkedExample title="Speeding up a car">
<Problem>
A car of mass 1200 kg joins a motorway. It speeds up from 10 m/s to 25 m/s over a distance of 150 m. What is the average net force on it along the road?
</Problem>
<Step title="Kinetic energy before and after">
$E_{k} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times 10^{2} = 6.0 \times 10^{4}$ J at the start and $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times 25^{2} \approx 3.8 \times 10^{5}$ J at the end.
</Step>
<Step title="Work–energy theorem">
The change in kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times (25^{2} - 10^{2}) = 3.15 \times 10^{5}$ J. This is the net work, so the net force must do $3.15 \times 10^{5}$ J of work over 150 m.
</Step>
<Step title="The force">
The net force points along the motion ($\cos\theta = 1$): $F_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{net}} / \Delta x = 3.15 \times 10^{5} / 150 \approx 2.1 \times 10^{3}$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
Newton's second law gives the acceleration, $a = 2100 / 1200 = 1.75$ m/s². Lesson 1.6 then gives $v_f^{2} = 10^{2} + 2 \times 1.75 \times 150 = 625$, and $\sqrt{625} = 25$ m/s, the speed we started from. Units: J/m = N m/m = N.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Twice the speed, four times the energy

Because the speed is squared, kinetic energy rises faster and faster as the speed goes up. For the 1200 kg car above, getting from rest to 10 m/s takes $6.0 \times 10^{4}$ J of net work. Getting from 10 m/s to 20 m/s, the same gain in speed, takes $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1200 \times (20^{2} - 10^{2}) = 1.8 \times 10^{5}$ J: three times as much. From rest to 20 m/s therefore takes $2.4 \times 10^{5}$ J, four times as much as to 10 m/s. This is a power law of lesson 0.6, $y \propto x^{2}$, where $\propto$ means "is proportional to": doubling $x$ multiplies $y$ by $2^{2} = 4$, and tripling it multiplies $y$ by $3^{2} = 9$.

## Braking distance

When a car brakes on a level road, the road pushes back on the tyres with a friction force that points against the motion. Suppose the braking force $F$ is constant and is the only horizontal force. Over the braking distance $d$ it does work $W = F\,d\cos 180° = -F\,d$, and the car ends at rest, so by the work–energy theorem $-F\,d = 0 - \tfrac{1}{2} m v_i^{2}$:

<Formula label="Braking distance" tex="d = \frac{m\,v_i^{2}}{2F}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 13.3.1, Eq. (13.3.7), p. 13-7 (F_x Δx = K_f − K_i), with Sec. 13.6, p. 13-16 (negative work slows the object); here F_x = −F, Δx = d and K_f = 0, rearranged for d (the rearrangement is ours, not in the source)" />

The brakes have to remove all the kinetic energy, and they remove $F$ joules for every metre. So the braking distance is proportional to the kinetic energy: twice the speed, four times the braking distance. Lesson 1.6 found the same with the equations of motion; the energy view shows *why*. A heavier vehicle with the same braking force also needs longer, in proportion to its mass.

<WorkedExample title="Braking from 72 km/h and from 108 km/h">
<Problem>
A car of 1600 kg brakes on a level road with a constant braking force of $1.0 \times 10^{4}$ N. How far does it travel while braking from 72 km/h to rest? And from 108 km/h?
</Problem>
<Step title="Speeds in SI units">
$72 / 3.6 = 20$ m/s and $108 / 3.6 = 30$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Kinetic energy">
At 20 m/s: $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1600 \times 20^{2} = 3.2 \times 10^{5}$ J. At 30 m/s: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1600 \times 30^{2} = 7.2 \times 10^{5}$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Braking distance">
The brakes remove $1.0 \times 10^{4}$ J per metre: $d = 3.2 \times 10^{5} / 1.0 \times 10^{4} = 32$ m from 72 km/h, and $7.2 \times 10^{5} / 1.0 \times 10^{4} = 72$ m from 108 km/h.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
1.5 times the speed should give $1.5^{2} = 2.25$ times the distance, and $32 \times 2.25 = 72$ m. With Newton's second law and lesson 1.6 instead: $a = 1.0 \times 10^{4} / 1600 = 6.25$ m/s², and $d = v_i^{2}/(2a) = 400 / 12.5 = 32$ m, the same.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The real stopping distance is longer: the car first travels at full speed during the driver's reaction time (lesson 1.6), and on a wet road the braking force is smaller.

## A ball thrown upwards

Throw a ball straight up and, ignoring air resistance, the only force on it is its weight $m g$ (lesson 2.5), pointing down. On the way up the displacement points up, so gravity does negative work: rising a height $h$, the ball has work $-m g h$ done on it ($\theta = 180°$). The ball slows down until it stops at the top. The work–energy theorem gives the height:

$-m g h = 0 - \tfrac{1}{2} m v_i^{2}$, so

<Formula label="Height reached by a ball thrown upwards" tex="h = \frac{v_i^{2}}{2g}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 13.6, Example 13.7, Eq. (13.6.2) (work of gravity with the work–kinetic energy theorem; the height is rearranged here, not in the source)" />

The mass cancels: a heavy ball and a light ball thrown at the same speed rise equally high, just as all objects fall alike (lesson 1.7).

<WorkedExample title="How high does the ball go?">
<Problem>
A ball of 0.15 kg is thrown straight up at 12 m/s. Ignoring air resistance, how high does it rise, and how much work does gravity do on it on the way up?
</Problem>
<Step title="Kinetic energy at the start">
$E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.15 \times 12^{2} \approx 11$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Height">
At the top the kinetic energy is zero, so gravity has done about $-11$ J of work: $h = \dfrac{12^{2}}{2 \times 9.81} \approx 7.3$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="On the way down">
Falling back the same height, gravity points along the motion and does about $+11$ J of work. The ball returns to the hand with the same kinetic energy, so at 12 m/s again (now moving down).
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
Lesson 1.7 with $v_f = 0$ and $a = -9.81$ m/s²: $0 = 12^{2} - 2 \times 9.81 \times h$ gives the same $h$. Also $m g h = 0.15 \times 9.81 \times 7.3 \approx 11$ J, the kinetic energy we started with.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

With air resistance, drag (lesson 2.6) points against the motion both on the way up and on the way down, so it does negative work on both legs: the ball rises less high and comes back slower. Lesson 3.3 will give the quantity $m g h$ its own name, gravitational potential energy.

## Kinetic energy depends on the frame

Lesson 1.9 showed that a velocity is always measured relative to a frame of reference. Kinetic energy is built from the speed, so it depends on the frame too. A bag of 2.0 kg lies on the luggage rack of a train moving at 25 m/s. Relative to the train the bag is at rest: $E_k = 0$. Relative to the ground it moves at 25 m/s: $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 2.0 \times 25^{2} \approx 630$ J. Both answers are correct; they belong to different frames.

The work–energy theorem holds in every frame that moves at constant velocity, but the work and the change in kinetic energy both take different values in different frames (the displacements differ too). The MIT course notes give an example: two cars that gain the same speed show different changes in kinetic energy, and the ratio between them changes when you watch from a moving frame (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 13.2, Example 13.1). The rule is simple: **choose one frame and use it for the whole calculation**. For things on Earth this is usually the ground.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Kinetic energy is one half of how the university cosmology course (8.286) first finds the equation for the expansion of the universe. Picture a huge ball of galaxies, all moving apart. A galaxy at the edge has kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}$ because of the expansion, while the gravity of all the matter inside pulls it back and does negative work on it as it moves out, just like the ball thrown upwards. If the kinetic energy is large enough, the galaxy keeps moving away for ever; if not, it stops and falls back. Written out with the energy of gravity (lesson 3.3, then lessons 3.11 and 3.12 at the end of this block), this energy balance becomes the Friedmann equation, the backbone of cosmology. For matter alone it already sorts universes into those that expand for ever and those that recollapse, although the dark energy found later changes what actually happens to ours.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Kinetic energy** is the energy of motion: $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}$. It is a scalar, never negative, and measured in joules (1 J = 1 kg m²/s² = 1 N m).
- For a constant net force, $W_{\text{net}} = m a\,\Delta x$ and $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2a\,\Delta x$ together give $W_{\text{net}} = \tfrac{1}{2} m v_f^{2} - \tfrac{1}{2} m v_i^{2}$.
- **Work–energy theorem:** $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta E_k$. Positive net work speeds an object up, negative net work slows it down, zero net work keeps the speed constant. It also holds for changing forces.
- Kinetic energy grows with the square of the speed: twice the speed, four times the energy. So the braking distance, $d = m v_i^{2}/(2F)$, also grows with the square of the speed.
- A ball thrown up at speed $v_i$ rises $h = v_i^{2}/(2g)$, whatever its mass, because gravity does work $-m g h$ on the way up.
- Kinetic energy depends on the frame of reference. Pick one frame and keep it.
