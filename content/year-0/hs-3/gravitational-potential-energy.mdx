Lift a book onto a high shelf and let go of it there: nothing happens. Push it off the edge and it falls, speeds up and hits the floor with a bang. The book on the shelf was not moving, yet it was ready to move: the work you did when you lifted it was not lost but stored. Energy stored in this way, by position, is called **potential energy**. This lesson is about the most familiar kind, the energy stored by lifting something against gravity.

## Energy stored by position

**Potential energy** is the energy an object has because of where it is, or because of its state (Siyavula 22.2). A book on a shelf, water behind a dam and a skier at the top of a slope all have it because of their height. A stretched spring or a drawn bow has it because of its shape; you will meet that kind at the end of this lesson. The energy stored by height is called **gravitational potential energy**, written $E_p$. (Strictly, this energy belongs to the object and the Earth together, since it comes from the pull between them; university courses say so, but in this course we speak of the object's energy.) In this course "potential energy" on its own means the gravitational kind, unless the lesson says otherwise.

The word "potential" means "possible": the energy is not doing anything yet, but it can be turned into motion. When the book falls, its potential energy becomes kinetic energy, the energy of motion of lesson 3.2.

## Lifting against gravity

How much energy is stored? Find it from the work you do. Lift an object of mass $m$ straight up through a height $h$, at a **steady speed**. Its weight $m g$ pulls it down (lesson 2.5), so to keep it moving steadily you must push up with a force of the same size, $F = m g$: then the net force is zero and the speed does not change (lesson 2.2). Your force points up and the object moves up, so by lesson 3.1 the work you do is force times displacement:

<Formula label="Work done against gravity" tex="W = F\,h = m\,g\,h" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 14.4.1, Eq. (14.3), with the sign reversed: the force that lifts is opposite to the weight" />

Because the object arrives at the top with the same speed it started with, this work has not gone into kinetic energy. It is stored, and that stored energy is the gravitational potential energy:

<Formula label="Gravitational potential energy near the Earth's surface" tex="E_p = m\,g\,h" cite="mit" at="Gravitational potential energy (E_P = mgh, with h the perpendicular height from the reference point)" />

Here $m$ is the mass in kilograms, $g = 9.81$ m/s² is the gravitational acceleration and $h$ is the height in metres above a chosen **zero level** (or reference level), such as the floor or the ground. The unit is the same as that of work: kg × m/s² × m = N × m = J, the **joule** (lesson 3.1). MIT's notes call the same quantity U and write it U = mgy, with y the height. Lifting an object of weight 1 N through 1 m stores 1 J.

While you lift, gravity does work too: its force $m g$ points down while the object moves up, so gravity does **negative** work, $-m g h$. The gain in potential energy is exactly minus the work done by gravity (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 14.4.1, Eq. 14.4). That is a useful second way to say it: potential energy goes up when gravity does negative work, and down when gravity does positive work.

<WorkedExample title="A box of books on a shelf">
<Problem>
A box of books has a mass of 12 kg. You lift it from the floor onto a shelf 1.8 m high, at a steady speed. How much work do you do, and how much gravitational potential energy does the box gain?
</Problem>
<Step title="The force you need">
At a steady speed your upward force equals the weight: $F = m g = 12 \times 9.81 = 117.7$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="The work">
$W = F h = 117.7 \times 1.8 = 212$ J, about $2.1 \times 10^{2}$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="The potential energy">
All of this work is stored: with the floor as the zero level, the box on the shelf has $E_p = m g h = 12 \times 9.81 \times 1.8 = 212$ J, about 210 J.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: kg × m/s² × m = N·m = J. Size: the weight is a little under 120 N, and lifting 120 N through about 2 m should store a little over 200 J. It does.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Only changes matter: choosing the zero level

The height $h$ in $m g h$ is measured from a zero level that **you** choose. For a book in a room the floor is natural; for a cable car in the mountains, sea level; for a ball thrown from a balcony, perhaps the street. A different choice gives a different value of $E_p$. Does that make the answer wrong? No, because only **changes** in potential energy have a physical meaning (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 14.4.1). When an object moves from a height $h_i$ to a height $h_f$, its potential energy changes by

<Formula label="Change in gravitational potential energy" tex="\Delta E_p = m\,g\,h_f - m\,g\,h_i = m\,g\,\Delta h" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 14.4.1, Eq. (14.4), written there ΔU = mgΔy = mgy_f − mgy_i" />

and the height difference $\Delta h = h_f - h_i$ is the same whatever zero level you chose. Moving the zero level up or down adds the same amount to every value of $E_p$ and so drops out of every change. In a calculation, pick the zero level that makes the numbers easiest, say which one you picked, and keep it for the whole problem.

An object **below** the zero level has a negative height and so a **negative** potential energy. That is not strange: it only means the object has less potential energy than it would have at the zero level.

<WorkedExample title="A plant pot and two zero levels">
<Problem>
A plant pot of 2.0 kg stands on a shelf 1.5 m above the floor of a flat. The floor is 6.0 m above the street. The pot falls off the shelf onto the floor. Find the change in its potential energy, first with the floor as the zero level, then with the street.
</Problem>
<Step title="Floor as the zero level">
On the shelf $h = 1.5$ m: $E_p = 2.0 \times 9.81 \times 1.5 = 29.43$ J. On the floor $h = 0$, so $E_p = 0$. The change is $0 - 29.43 = -29.43$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Street as the zero level">
On the shelf $h = 6.0 + 1.5 = 7.5$ m: $E_p = 2.0 \times 9.81 \times 7.5 = 147.15$ J. On the floor $h = 6.0$ m: $E_p = 2.0 \times 9.81 \times 6.0 = 117.72$ J. The change is $117.72 - 147.15 = -29.43$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
The values of $E_p$ differ completely, but the change is the same: about $-29$ J. It is negative because the pot ends lower. Directly: $\Delta E_p = m g\,\Delta h = 2.0 \times 9.81 \times (-1.5) = -29$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Below the zero level">
Had the pot been carried down into a cellar 2.0 m below the floor, then with the floor as the zero level its potential energy there would be $2.0 \times 9.81 \times (-2.0) = -39$ J: negative, because it is below the chosen zero.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The path does not matter

Suppose you need to get a heavy crate onto a loading platform. You can lift it straight up, or push it up a ramp. Which needs more work against gravity?

On a smooth ramp at an angle $\theta$, the part of the weight that pulls the crate back down the slope is $m g \sin\theta$ (lesson 2.8). To push the crate up at a steady speed you need a force of just that size along the ramp, which is smaller than the whole weight. But you push it over the whole length $L$ of the ramp, which is longer than the height $h$. Since $\sin\theta = h/L$ (lesson 0.7), the work is

<Formula label="Work along a smooth ramp" tex="W = m\,g\sin\theta \times L = m\,g\,\frac{h}{L} \times L = m\,g\,h" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 14.2, Eqs. (14.3)–(14.7): the work done by gravity along two different paths between the same two points is the same, mgy_i (the ramp form mg sin θ × L is derived here from lesson 2.8, not in MIT's notes)" />

exactly the same as lifting straight up. A ramp makes the job easier by needing a smaller force, not by needing less energy: what you save in force you pay back in distance.

The same holds for any path. Carry the crate up a staircase: on each flat step you move sideways, at right angles to the weight, and the weight does no work there (lesson 3.1: the angle is 90° and $\cos 90^\circ = 0$); on each rise you lift it. Only the vertical parts count, and they add up to the total height. MIT's notes compare two paths between the same two points, one straight down a slant and one down and then sideways, and find the same work done by gravity along both (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 14.2). So **the change in gravitational potential energy depends only on the change in height, not on the path taken.** That is what makes potential energy a useful idea at all: a quantity that depends only on position. Lesson 3.4 gives forces with this property a name.

Friction is different. Dragging a box along a rough floor and back takes work each way, and a longer path takes more work. With friction on the ramp you would have to do more work than $m g h$, but the extra is not stored as potential energy: it warms the ramp and the crate and cannot be got back (lesson 3.4).

<WorkedExample title="Up the ramp or straight up?">
<Problem>
A crate of 40 kg is pushed up a smooth ramp 5.0 m long onto a platform 1.2 m high, at a steady speed. Find the force needed along the ramp and the work done. Compare with lifting the crate straight up.
</Problem>
<Step title="The angle of the ramp">
$\sin\theta = h/L = 1.2/5.0 = 0.24$.
</Step>
<Step title="Force along the ramp">
$F = m g \sin\theta = 40 \times 9.81 \times 0.24 = 94.18$ N, about 94 N.
</Step>
<Step title="Work along the ramp">
$W = F L = 94.18 \times 5.0 = 470.9$ J, about 470 J.
</Step>
<Step title="Straight up">
The force is the whole weight, $40 \times 9.81 = 392.4$ N, over 1.2 m: $W = 392.4 \times 1.2 = 470.9$ J. The same.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Both equal $m g h = 40 \times 9.81 \times 1.2$. On the ramp the force is 0.24 times the weight, about a quarter, and the distance $5.0/1.2 = 4.2$ times the height, about four times: the factors cancel.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## When the object falls

On the way down the story runs backwards. A falling object moves in the direction of its weight, so gravity does **positive** work on it, $m g h$ for a fall through a height $h$, and the potential energy drops by the same amount. Where does that energy go? By the work–energy theorem of lesson 3.2, the net work on an object equals its gain in kinetic energy, $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta E_k$. If gravity is the only force doing work (no air resistance), the potential energy lost becomes kinetic energy gained.

<WorkedExample title="A dropped ball">
<Problem>
A ball of 0.50 kg is dropped from rest from a height of 3.2 m. Ignore air resistance. How much potential energy does it lose, and how fast is it moving just before it lands?
</Problem>
<Step title="Potential energy lost">
With the ground as the zero level, $E_p$ falls from $0.50 \times 9.81 \times 3.2 = 15.7$ J to zero.
</Step>
<Step title="Kinetic energy gained">
Gravity does $+15.7$ J of work on the ball and is the only force doing work, so the ball gains $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2} = 15.7$ J. Then $v = \sqrt{2 \times 15.7 / 0.50} = 7.92$ m/s, about 7.9 m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with free fall">
Lesson 1.7 gives the same from the equations of motion: $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} - 2 g\,\Delta x = 0 - 2 \times 9.81 \times (-3.2) = 62.8$ m²/s², so $v_f = 7.9$ m/s downwards. Two routes, one answer.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Notice that the mass cancels: $m g h = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}$ gives the same speed for a heavy ball as for a light one, just as lesson 1.7 found that all objects fall with the same acceleration. Turning this exchange between potential and kinetic energy into a rule for every kind of motion is the subject of the next lesson, conservation of energy.

## A first look: energy in a stretched spring

Gravity is not the only force that can store energy. Stretch a spring and let go, and it snaps back: the work you did while stretching it was stored in the spring. This is **elastic potential energy**. Here the force is not constant: by Hooke's law (lesson 2.7) it grows from zero to $k x$ as the extension grows from zero to $x$. On average it is half the final force, $\tfrac{1}{2} k x$, and it acts over the distance $x$, so the stored energy is

<Formula label="Elastic potential energy of a spring" tex="E_{\text{el}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,k\,x^{2}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 14.4.2, Eq. (14.12), written there U_s(x) = ½kx², zero at the natural length" />

For the toy spring of lesson 2.7, with $k = 200$ N/m, a stretch of 0.10 m needs a final force of 20 N, an average force of 10 N, and stores $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 200 \times 0.10^{2} = 1.0$ J. For a spring the natural zero level is its natural length, where it stores nothing (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 14.4.2). A mass hanging on a spring has both kinds at once: when it moves down while the spring is stretched, its gravitational potential energy falls and the spring's elastic energy rises. Lesson 3.4 puts the two together.

## Far from the Earth, mgh fails

The formula $E_p = m g h$ rests on one assumption: that the force of gravity, $m g$, is the same at every height. Near the ground that is true to a very good approximation. But lesson 2.11 showed that gravity weakens with distance from the Earth's centre, $g = G M/r^{2}$. At the height of the space station, about 400 km up, $g$ is only about 8.7 m/s², about 11.5% less than on the ground.

So $m g h$ works only for heights much smaller than the Earth's radius of 6371 km. Use it for a whole climb to 400 km and it overestimates: for a mass of 1.0 kg it gives $1.0 \times 9.81 \times 400\,000 = 3.9 \times 10^{6}$ J, while the correct value, from a formula that allows for the weakening of gravity, is about $3.7 \times 10^{6}$ J. That formula, with the zero level placed infinitely far away, is $E_p = -G M m/r$ (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 14.4.3); lesson 3.11 uses it to find how fast a rocket must go to escape from the Earth.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
The same bookkeeping of energy, kinetic energy plus gravitational potential energy, is where the study of the expanding universe begins. In MIT's cosmology course, Alan Guth imagines a huge ball of matter that is expanding evenly, and follows one particle on its edge (MIT 8.286, Lecture 6). As the ball grows, the particle moves outwards and slows down, trading kinetic energy for potential energy, with the far-from-Earth form $-G M m/r$ rather than $m g h$, because the distances are huge. Whether its total energy is positive or negative decides whether the expansion goes on for ever or stops and turns around (Lecture 7). Lesson 3.12 works this out step by step, and in this simple Newtonian model it leads to the equation that describes how the universe expands.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Potential energy** is energy stored by position or state. **Gravitational potential energy** is stored by lifting an object against gravity.
- Lifting a mass $m$ through a height $h$ at a steady speed takes work $m g h$, which is stored: $E_p = m g h$, in joules, with $h$ measured from a chosen **zero level**.
- Only **changes** matter: $\Delta E_p = m g\,\Delta h$ is the same for every zero level. Below the zero level $E_p$ is negative.
- The change in $E_p$ depends only on the change in height, **not on the path**: a smooth ramp needs a smaller force over a longer distance, and the same work $m g h$.
- When an object falls, gravity does positive work and $E_p$ turns into kinetic energy; without air resistance the potential energy lost equals the kinetic energy gained.
- A stretched spring stores **elastic potential energy** $\tfrac{1}{2} k x^{2}$ (used in lesson 3.4).
- $m g h$ holds only for heights much smaller than the Earth's radius; far away $g$ weakens and the full formula $-G M m/r$ (lesson 3.11) is needed.
