Block 1 described going round in a circle with an angular speed $\omega$ (lesson 1.10), lesson 2.10 showed that a force can turn an object through its **torque**, and lessons 3.6 and 3.7 introduced momentum and its conservation. This lesson brings these together. Turning things have a momentum of their own, **angular momentum**, and it obeys a conservation law just as momentum does. That law explains why a spinning skater speeds up when she pulls in her arms, and why a planet moves fastest when it is close to the Sun, as Kepler found (lesson 2.12).

## Moving and turning side by side

Every idea for motion along a line has a partner for turning about an axis. The pairs below are the ones this lesson uses; the right-hand column is built step by step in the sections that follow.

| Moving along a line | Turning about an axis |
|---|---|
| velocity $v$, in m/s | angular speed $\omega$, in rad/s (lesson 1.10) |
| mass $m$: how hard it is to speed up | moment of inertia $I$: how hard it is to spin up |
| force $F$ | torque $\tau$ (lesson 2.10) |
| momentum $p = mv$ | angular momentum $L = I\omega$ |
| kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}$ | rotational kinetic energy $\tfrac{1}{2} I \omega^{2}$ |

The MIT course notes open their chapter on angular momentum with the rotational partner of the rule that a force changes momentum: a torque changes the angular momentum (Sec. 19.1). Later they call angular impulse the "rotational analog" of impulse (Sec. 19.5).

## Angular momentum of a mass going round

Take a small object of mass $m$ going round a circle of radius $r$ at speed $v$. Its momentum is $p = mv$ (lesson 3.6), along the circle, at right angles to the radius. Its **angular momentum** about the centre of the circle is the momentum times the radius:

<Formula label="Angular momentum of a mass on a circle" tex="L = m\,v\,r" cite="mit" at="Example 19.2 (the magnitude Rmv about the centre of the circle); Sec. 19.2.1, Eq. (19.2.4)" />

The symbol for angular momentum is $L$. Its unit is kg·m/s times m, that is **kg·m²/s** (Sec. 19.2.1, below Eq. (19.2.1)). With $v = \omega r$ from lesson 1.10, the same quantity can be written with the angular speed:

<Formula label="Angular momentum from the angular speed" tex="L = m\,r^{2}\,\omega" cite="mit" at="Example 19.2, the result mR²ω_z" />

Compare this with the torque of lesson 2.10, which is the force times its lever arm. Angular momentum is the momentum times its lever arm. For a mass on a circle the lever arm is just the radius, because the momentum is at right angles to it. An object that moves in some other direction uses the perpendicular distance from the centre to the line of its momentum instead, exactly as for torque (Sec. 19.2.1, Eqs. (19.2.3) and (19.2.4)). So even an object moving in a straight line has angular momentum about a point that does not lie on its path (Example 19.1).

Angular momentum has a direction too: the turning is either anticlockwise or clockwise. As for torque in lesson 2.10, we can give one sense a plus sign and the other a minus sign. (The MIT notes make $L$ a vector along the axis, fixed by a right-hand rule, Sec. 19.2.2; you will meet that at university.)

<WorkedExample title="A stone on a string">
<Problem>
A stone of 0.15 kg is whirled round in a horizontal circle on a string of 0.50 m, making 2.0 turns per second. Find its angular speed, its speed and its angular momentum about the centre.
</Problem>
<Step title="Angular speed and speed">
Two turns per second is $\omega = 2\pi \times 2.0 \approx 12.6$ rad/s (lesson 1.10). The speed is $v = \omega r = 12.57 \times 0.50 \approx 6.3$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Angular momentum">
$L = m v r = 0.15 \times 6.28 \times 0.50 \approx 0.47$ kg·m²/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by the other formula">
$L = m r^{2} \omega = 0.15 \times 0.50^{2} \times 12.57 \approx 0.47$ kg·m²/s, the same. Units: kg × m² × rad/s = kg·m²/s, since the radian has no unit of its own.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A spinning body and its moment of inertia

A wheel, a spinning top or a skater is not one small mass but very many. Think of the body as made of small pieces with masses $m_1, m_2, \ldots$ at distances $r_1, r_2, \ldots$ from the axis. When the body spins, every piece goes round its own circle, all with the same angular speed $\omega$. Each piece has angular momentum $m r^{2} \omega$, and adding them up gives $(m_1 r_1^{2} + m_2 r_2^{2} + \cdots)\,\omega$ (Sec. 19.7, Eq. (19.5.24)). The sum in brackets is called the **moment of inertia** $I$ of the body about that axis:

<Formula label="Moment of inertia" tex="I = \sum m\,r^{2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 19.7, Eqs. (19.5.24) and (19.5.26) (written there as an integral over the body)" />

Here $\sum$ (the Greek capital sigma) means "add up over all the pieces". The unit of $I$ is kg·m² (reading 1, Sec. 16.3.1). With it, the angular momentum of a spinning body takes the same form as $p = mv$:

<Formula label="Angular momentum of a spinning body" tex="L = I\,\omega" cite="mit" at="Sec. 19.7, Eq. (19.5.27); Sec. 19.1, Eq. (19.1.5)" />

The moment of inertia depends on the mass and on **where** the mass is. Because each distance is squared, mass far from the axis counts much more than mass close to it: the same piece at twice the distance adds four times as much. A body therefore has a different moment of inertia about different axes. The MIT notes work out $I$ for a few simple shapes (with an integral, which is a sum of very many very small pieces, lesson 0.10):

| Shape, axis | Moment of inertia |
|---|---|
| small mass $m$ at distance $r$ from the axis | $m r^{2}$ (Example 19.2) |
| thin ring or hoop, mass $M$, radius $R$, about its centre | $M R^{2}$ (Sec. 19.7, Eq. (19.5.31)) |
| uniform disc or solid cylinder (the same for any thickness), mass $M$, radius $R$, about its axis | $\tfrac{1}{2} M R^{2}$ (reading 1, Example 16.2, Eq. (16.2.11)) |
| thin uniform rod, mass $M$, length $\ell$, about its middle | $\tfrac{1}{12} M \ell^{2}$ (reading 1, Sec. 16.3.2, Eq. (16.2.5)) |

All of the ring is at the full radius $R$, while much of a disc is close to its axis, so a disc has half the moment of inertia of a ring of the same mass and radius.

<WorkedExample title="Two weights on a rod">
<Problem>
Two weights of 2.0 kg each sit on a light rod, one on each side, 0.30 m from an axis through the middle. The rod spins about that axis at 4.0 rad/s. Find the moment of inertia and the angular momentum. Then the weights are slid in to 0.15 m from the axis: what is the moment of inertia now?
</Problem>
<Step title="Moment of inertia">
The light rod adds almost nothing, so $I = 2 \times 2.0 \times 0.30^{2} = 0.36$ kg·m².
</Step>
<Step title="Angular momentum">
$L = I\omega = 0.36 \times 4.0 \approx 1.4$ kg·m²/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Weights closer in">
$I = 2 \times 2.0 \times 0.15^{2} = 0.090$ kg·m²: half the distance gives a quarter of the moment of inertia.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with the single masses">
Each weight moves at $v = \omega r = 4.0 \times 0.30 = 1.2$ m/s and has $m v r = 2.0 \times 1.2 \times 0.30 = 0.72$ kg·m²/s; the two together have $2 \times 0.72 = 1.44$ kg·m²/s, as above. Units: kg·m² × rad/s = kg·m²/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Rotational kinetic energy

A spinning wheel stays in one place, yet it clearly has kinetic energy: all its pieces are moving. Each piece of mass $m$ at distance $r$ moves at $v = \omega r$ and has $\tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} m r^{2} \omega^{2}$ (lesson 3.2). Adding the pieces gives $\tfrac{1}{2}(\sum m r^{2})\,\omega^{2}$, and the sum is again the moment of inertia (reading 1, Sec. 16.3.1, Eqs. (16.2.1) and (16.2.2)):

<Formula label="Rotational kinetic energy" tex="E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}\,I\,\omega^{2}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 16.3.1, Eq. (16.2.4) (written there for an axis through the centre of mass)" />

This is not a new kind of energy: it is the ordinary kinetic energy of all the pieces, written in a handier way. It counts in the conservation of energy (lesson 3.4) like any other kinetic energy.

<WorkedExample title="A potter's wheel">
<Problem>
The top of a potter's wheel is a uniform disc of 5.0 kg with a radius of 0.20 m. It turns at 1.5 turns per second. Find its moment of inertia, its angular momentum and its kinetic energy.
</Problem>
<Step title="Moment of inertia">
For a uniform disc, $I = \tfrac{1}{2} M R^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 5.0 \times 0.20^{2} = 0.10$ kg·m².
</Step>
<Step title="Angular speed and angular momentum">
$\omega = 2\pi \times 1.5 \approx 9.4$ rad/s, so $L = I\omega = 0.10 \times 9.42 \approx 0.94$ kg·m²/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Kinetic energy">
$E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} I \omega^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.10 \times 9.42^{2} \approx 4.4$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
Since $\tfrac{1}{2} I \omega^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} (I\omega)\,\omega$, the energy is also $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.942 \times 9.42 \approx 4.4$ J. Units: kg·m² × (rad/s)² = kg·m²/s² = J. A ring of the same mass and radius would have twice the moment of inertia, and twice the angular momentum and energy at the same speed.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Torque changes angular momentum

Lesson 3.6 showed that a net force acting for a time $\Delta t$ changes the momentum: $\Delta p = F\,\Delta t$. In the same way, a net torque acting for a time $\Delta t$ changes the angular momentum. The product of torque and time is called the **angular impulse**, and it equals the change in angular momentum:

<Formula label="Angular impulse" tex="\Delta L = \tau\,\Delta t" cite="mit" at="Sec. 19.5, Eq. (19.4.2) (written there with an integral for a torque that changes; here the torque is constant)" />

So $\tau = \Delta L / \Delta t$: the net torque equals the rate of change of the angular momentum (Sec. 19.3, Eq. (19.3.9), written there for one instant). For a body whose moment of inertia stays the same, a torque makes it spin faster or slower. A torque in the same sense as the spin speeds it up; a torque against the spin, such as friction in an axle, slows it down.

For such a body $\Delta L = I\,\Delta\omega$, so the torque equals the moment of inertia times $\Delta\omega / \Delta t$, the rate at which the angular speed changes. That rate is called the **angular acceleration**, in rad/s², and the result is the rotating version of Newton's second law, force equals mass times acceleration (lesson 2.3), with $I$ in place of the mass:

<Formula label="Torque and angular acceleration" tex="\tau = I\,\frac{\Delta\omega}{\Delta t}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 19.1, Eq. (19.1.6) (written there as τ = Iα, with the angular acceleration α = dω/dt at one instant; here the torque is constant)" />

A large moment of inertia makes a body hard to spin up or slow down, just as a large mass makes it hard to speed up.

<WorkedExample title="Pushing a playground roundabout">
<Problem>
A playground roundabout has a moment of inertia of 240 kg·m² and is at rest. A child pushes along the edge, at right angles to the radius, with a steady force of 50 N at 1.5 m from the axle, for 4.0 s. Ignore friction. How fast does the roundabout turn afterwards?
</Problem>
<Step title="Torque">
The force is at right angles to the radius, so (lesson 2.10) $\tau = r F = 1.5 \times 50 = 75$ N·m.
</Step>
<Step title="Change in angular momentum">
$\Delta L = \tau\,\Delta t = 75 \times 4.0 = 300$ kg·m²/s. It started at rest, so $L = 300$ kg·m²/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Angular speed">
$\omega = L / I = 300 / 240 \approx 1.3$ rad/s, about one turn every 5 s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: N·m × s = kg·m²/s² × s = kg·m²/s, the unit of angular momentum. The edge then moves at $v = \omega r = 1.25 \times 1.5 \approx 1.9$ m/s, a fast walking pace: reasonable for a few seconds of pushing.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Conservation of angular momentum

If no net torque acts from outside, then $\Delta L = 0$: the angular momentum cannot change. The forces between the parts of a system come in third-law pairs (lesson 2.4). Each pair acts along the line joining the two parts, so the two forces have the same lever arm, and their torques cancel in pairs just as their impulses do in lesson 3.7. The MIT notes add that no isolated system has ever been found whose angular momentum changes, so this cancelling can be taken as a fact of experiment (Sec. 19.6, Sec. 19.8). This is the **law of conservation of angular momentum**: *if the net external torque on a system is zero, its angular momentum stays the same.*

<Formula label="Conservation of angular momentum" tex="L_i = L_f" cite="mit" at="Sec. 19.8, Principle of Conservation of Angular Momentum, Eqs. (19.5.35) and (19.5.36)" />

The interesting part is that a body is not rigid: it can change its shape, and so its moment of inertia. With $L = I\omega$ fixed,

<Formula label="Changing the shape of a spinning body" tex="I_i\,\omega_i = I_f\,\omega_f" cite="mit" at="Sec. 19.8, Eq. (19.5.36) with L = Iω from Sec. 19.7, Eq. (19.5.27) (the step is the page's own)" />

If the body pulls its mass inwards, $I$ goes down, so $\omega$ must go up: it spins faster. If it spreads its mass outwards, it spins more slowly. You can try this on an office chair that turns easily: hold a heavy book in each hand with your arms stretched out, get someone to start you turning, then pull the books in to your chest. You speed up. Push them out again, and you slow down.

<WorkedExample title="A skater pulls in her arms">
<Problem>
A figure skater spins on the tip of one skate with her arms stretched out. Her moment of inertia is then about 3.6 kg·m², and she makes 1.5 turns per second. She pulls her arms in close to her body, which lowers her moment of inertia to 1.2 kg·m². Friction from the ice is small. How fast does she spin now, and what happens to her kinetic energy?
</Problem>
<Step title="Angular momentum">
At the start $\omega_i = 2\pi \times 1.5 \approx 9.42$ rad/s, so $L = I_i \omega_i = 3.6 \times 9.42 \approx 34$ kg·m²/s.
</Step>
<Step title="New angular speed">
No outside torque acts, so $L$ stays the same: $\omega_f = L / I_f = 33.9 / 1.2 \approx 28.3$ rad/s. That is three times as fast, 4.5 turns per second.
</Step>
<Step title="Kinetic energy before and after">
Before: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 3.6 \times 9.42^{2} \approx 160$ J. After: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1.2 \times 28.3^{2} \approx 480$ J. Her kinetic energy has tripled, by $480 - 160 = 320$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The moment of inertia went down by a factor $3.6 / 1.2 = 3$, so $\omega$ goes up by 3, and $\tfrac{1}{2} I \omega^{2}$ changes by $\tfrac{1}{3} \times 3^{2} = 3$: $3 \times 160 = 480$ J. Units: kg·m² × (rad/s)² = J.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Where does the extra energy come from?** Angular momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is not, just as in lesson 3.7, where carts pushed apart by a spring gained kinetic energy. Each arm goes round a circle and needs an inward force to do so (lesson 2.9). To pull her arms in, the skater must pull at least that hard (a little harder to start them moving), and her hands then move inwards in the direction of her pull. Her muscles do work on her arms (lesson 3.1), and that work, 320 J in the example, becomes the extra kinetic energy. It comes from the chemical energy in her muscles. When she lets her arms out again, she slows down, and her muscles have to hold her arms back; the kinetic energy she loses ends up mostly as thermal energy in her muscles (lesson 3.4).

You can also see this in a formula. With $\omega = L/I$, the kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2} I \omega^{2} = L^{2} / (2I)$: for a fixed $L$, a smaller moment of inertia means more kinetic energy.

## Kepler's second law

A planet going round the Sun is a mass going round a centre, so it has angular momentum about the Sun. The only force on it (ignoring the other planets) is the Sun's gravity, which points straight at the Sun. A force that points at the Sun has no lever arm about the Sun, so its torque about the Sun is zero (lesson 2.10). The planet's angular momentum about the Sun therefore stays the same all the way round its orbit (reading 2, Sec. 25.3). The MIT notes use the same argument for a meteor that swings past the Earth (Sec. 19.4, Example 19.4). As a bonus, the orbit stays in one flat plane (reading 2, Sec. 25.3).

At the closest point of an ellipse (perihelion, distance $r_p$, speed $v_p$) and at the furthest point (aphelion, $r_a$, $v_a$), the velocity is at right angles to the line to the Sun, so there $L = m v r$:

<Formula label="Angular momentum at perihelion and aphelion" tex="m\,r_p\,v_p = m\,r_a\,v_a" cite="reading2" at="Example 25.1, Eq. (25.6.2)" />

The mass cancels: a planet must move faster when it is closer. This is the rule $r_p v_p = r_a v_a$ that lesson 2.12 used. It is also Kepler's second law. In a short time $\Delta t$ the line from the Sun sweeps out a thin triangle of area $\tfrac{1}{2}\,r\,v_\perp\,\Delta t$ (lesson 2.12), where $v_\perp$ is the part of the velocity at right angles to that line. Its angular momentum is $L = m\,r\,v_\perp$, so the area swept out per unit time is $L/(2m)$. A constant angular momentum means equal areas in equal times (reading 2, Sec. 25.6.2, Eqs. (25.5.4) to (25.5.6)). Lesson 2.12 promised this reason; now you have it.

<WorkedExample title="A comet on a long orbit">
<Problem>
A comet passes closest to the Sun at $9.0 \times 10^{10}$ m from it, moving at 54 km/s. The far end of its orbit is $4.5 \times 10^{12}$ m from the Sun. How fast does the comet move there?
</Problem>
<Step title="Why angular momentum is conserved">
The Sun's gravity points at the Sun, so it has no torque about the Sun. At both ends of the orbit the velocity is at right angles to the line to the Sun, so $r_p v_p = r_a v_a$.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve">
$v_a = \dfrac{v_p\,r_p}{r_a} = \dfrac{54 \times 9.0 \times 10^{10}}{4.5 \times 10^{12}} \approx 1.1$ km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The far end is $\dfrac{4.5 \times 10^{12}}{9.0 \times 10^{10}} = 50$ times as far, and $54 / 50 = 1.08$, so the speed there is 50 times smaller. Both products $r v$ are $4.86 \times 10^{15}$ m²/s. The comet's mass was not needed, as the formula promised.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Conservation of angular momentum shapes much of what we see in the sky. Every real cloud of gas in space turns at least a little. When gravity pulls it together, little outside torque acts on it, so its angular momentum stays roughly the same, and like the skater it spins faster as it shrinks. The spin resists the collapse in the plane of rotation, while along the axis the cloud can still fall together, so a spinning cloud flattens into a disc. Astronomers think the Sun and planets formed from such a disc, which is why the planets orbit in nearly the same plane and in the same direction, and disc galaxies such as the Milky Way are thought to owe their shape to gas that kept its spin as it settled. At the extreme, when the core of a massive star collapses into a neutron star only about 20 kilometres across, it is left spinning fast: pulsars, which are rotating neutron stars, send out pulses at intervals from milliseconds to seconds (NASA, Imagine the Universe!: Neutron Stars). As a simple estimate, if a ball kept its shape and its angular momentum while its radius shrank 1000 times, its moment of inertia would fall $10^{6}$ times, and it would spin $10^{6}$ times faster. The graduate course (8.902) shows how the spin of a rotating disc of stars or gas helps it resist its own gravity.

## Summary

- **Angular momentum** is the momentum of turning. For a mass going round a circle of radius $r$ about the centre, $L = m v r = m r^{2} \omega$, in kg·m²/s: the momentum times its lever arm.
- A spinning body has $L = I\omega$, where the **moment of inertia** $I = \sum m r^{2}$ (in kg·m²) depends on how far the mass is from the axis. A hoop has $I = MR^{2}$, a solid disc $\tfrac{1}{2} M R^{2}$.
- A spinning body has **rotational kinetic energy** $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} I \omega^{2}$: the kinetic energy of all its pieces together.
- A torque acting for a time changes the angular momentum: $\Delta L = \tau\,\Delta t$.
- **Conservation of angular momentum:** with no net outside torque, $L$ stays the same, so $I_i \omega_i = I_f \omega_f$. Pulling mass inwards makes a body spin faster; its kinetic energy rises, paid for by the work done in pulling.
- Gravity has no torque about the Sun, so a planet's angular momentum is constant: $r_p v_p = r_a v_a$, and the line to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times (Kepler's second law).
