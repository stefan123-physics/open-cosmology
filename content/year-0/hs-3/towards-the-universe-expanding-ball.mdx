Throw a ball up and it comes back, unless you throw it faster than the escape speed (lesson 3.11). Now ask the same question about the whole universe. The galaxies are all moving away from each other (lesson 0.11; lesson 8.11 shows how we know). Their gravity pulls them back together. Will that pull one day stop the expansion and make everything fall back, or will the galaxies fly apart for ever? With nothing more than the energy of Block 3 and Newton's law of gravity, you can follow the answer that Alan Guth derives near the start of MIT's cosmology course.

## A ball of galaxies

Galaxies are spread fairly evenly through space when you look on large enough scales. So, following Guth (MIT 8.286, Lecture 6), picture a huge ball filled evenly with galaxies, like a cloud of dust, with a **density** $\rho$ (mass per cubic metre). Put yourself at its centre. The ball is expanding in a special way: every galaxy moves straight away from the centre, with a speed proportional to its distance. Twice as far away means twice as fast. This is **Hubble's law**:

<Formula label="Hubble's law" tex="v = H\,r" cite="reading2" at="slide 8 (written there as a vector, v_i = H_i r, at the starting time)" />

Here $r$ is the galaxy's distance from the centre and $H$ is the **Hubble constant**, the same number for every galaxy at a given moment. Today it is about $70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$: a galaxy one megaparsec (lesson 0.11) further away recedes about 70 km/s faster. Lesson 0.11 wrote this law as v = Hd, with d for the distance; here the distance is the radius r of the ball. Lesson 8.11 explains the measurements; here we only need the pattern.

Doesn't putting yourself at the centre make you special? No. Lesson 1.9 showed that an observer in any other galaxy sees exactly the same pattern, everything rushing away from *them*. The ball is a tool for the calculation: draw it around any point you like.

The matter inside a ball of radius $r$ has a mass $M$:

<Formula label="Mass of an evenly filled ball" tex="M = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}\rho" cite="reading4" at="slide 2 (written there M(r_i) = (4π/3) r_i³ ρ_i)" />

## Only the mass inside pulls

Take one galaxy on the edge of the ball. All the other galaxies pull on it. Newton proved two results for a thin, evenly filled round shell of matter (Lecture 7, transcript p. 2; we use them without proof, as Guth does). Lesson 2.11 used the first one, and lesson 2.13 the second:

- outside the shell, the shell pulls exactly as if all its mass sat at its centre;
- inside the shell, the pulls from all sides cancel: no net force at all.

Build the universe from shells. The shells inside our galaxy's distance pull it towards the centre, together like one point mass $M$. The shells further out, around it, have no effect. So the edge galaxy feels the force of Newton's law of gravitation (lesson 2.11) from the mass inside only. Its acceleration is $G M/r^{2}$, towards the centre (Lecture 6, transcript p. 19).

As the ball grows, the galaxies inside stay inside: they all move outwards, the nearer ones more slowly, and no shell of galaxies overtakes another (Guth checks this carefully, Lecture 6, transcript pp. 17–18). So $M$ stays the same, even though $r$ grows and $\rho$ falls (transcript p. 18). Our galaxy behaves like a probe fired up from a planet of mass $M$, a planet that is itself expanding beneath it.

## The energy of a galaxy on the edge

Only gravity does work on the edge galaxy, so by conservation of energy (lesson 3.4) its mechanical energy stays the same. Far from a mass, the potential energy is $-G M m/r$ (lesson 3.11). Divide everything by the galaxy's mass $m$, which drops out anyway, and you get the energy per kilogram:

<Formula label="Energy per kilogram of a galaxy on the edge" tex="\frac{E_{\text{mech}}}{m} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,v^{2} - \frac{G M}{r} = \text{constant}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 28–29 (board): Guth's conserved quantity, times m r_i², is ½mv² − GmM/r for a test particle on the boundary, written there with ȧ r_i for v" />

Guth first finds from the equation of motion that a certain combination stays constant, and then shows that it is exactly this energy, kinetic plus (negative) potential, of a particle on the boundary (transcript pp. 28–29). As the ball grows, $r$ grows, the potential energy rises towards zero and the kinetic energy falls: the galaxies slow down.

A galaxy deep inside the ball is no different. The matter outside it does not pull on it, so it moves as if it sat on the edge of a smaller ball. (For a particle inside, $-G M m/r$ is not its full potential energy, Guth points out, but the outer matter has no effect on its motion, so the argument still works; Lecture 6, transcript pp. 29–30.)

## The escape-speed question again

This is exactly the question of lesson 3.11, with the ball as the planet. The escape speed from the edge of the ball is $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2 G M/r}$, and the sign of the energy decides:

- $v > v_{\text{esc}}$, energy positive: the ball **expands for ever**, and its edge keeps some speed even when the ball is huge.
- $v = v_{\text{esc}}$, energy zero: it **just** expands for ever, slowing down towards zero speed.
- $v < v_{\text{esc}}$, energy negative: the ball is **bound**. It reaches a largest size, stops and falls back together.

Guth works out the same three cases from his equation (Lecture 7, transcript pp. 17–20). He calls the first an open universe and the last a closed universe, which collapses all the way to what is often called the **Big Crunch**, the opposite of the Big Bang.

It is the probes of lesson 3.11 again. Fired straight up at 8.0 km/s, less than the escape speed of 11.2 km/s, a probe climbs about 6700 km and falls back. At 12.0 km/s it escapes and keeps a little speed for ever. For the universe, the "planet" and the "probe" are the same ball of galaxies.

## The critical density

Now put in the two facts about the ball: the speed of the edge is $v = H r$, and the mass inside is $M = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}\rho$. Then $G M/r = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi G \rho\, r^{2}$. The ball just escapes when its energy is zero:

<Formula label="Energy zero: the dividing line" tex="\tfrac{1}{2}\,H^{2} r^{2} = \frac{4}{3}\pi G \rho\, r^{2}" cite="reading3" at="p. 20 (board): the critical case E = 0; this form with v = Hr and the mass of the ball is the page's own" />

Both sides contain $r^{2}$, so the radius cancels. Solve for the density. The density at which the ball is exactly on the dividing line is the **critical density** $\rho_c$:

<Formula label="Critical density" tex="\rho_c = \frac{3 H^{2}}{8\pi G}" cite="reading3" at="p. 20 (board)" />

A ball **denser** than $\rho_c$ has negative energy: its gravity wins, and it stops and falls back. A ball **less dense** than $\rho_c$ expands for ever (transcript p. 20).

That the radius has cancelled is the most important point of this lesson. Draw any ball you like in the evenly filled universe, small or enormous, centred anywhere: it gives the same critical density, because the kinetic and the potential energy of a kilogram on its edge both grow as $r^{2}$. Whether the expansion stops depends only on two numbers that are the same everywhere, the expansion rate $H$ and the density $\rho$. So the answer belongs to the universe, not to the ball we happened to draw.

<WorkedExample title="The critical density today">
<Problem>
Take $H_0 = 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$ and $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ N m²/kg². What is the critical density? A hydrogen atom has a mass of about $1.67 \times 10^{-27}$ kg (almost all of it the proton). How many hydrogen atoms per cubic metre is that?
</Problem>
<Step title="The Hubble constant in SI units">
One megaparsec is a million parsecs (lesson 0.11), $3.086 \times 10^{22}$ m, and $70\ \text{km/s} = 7.0 \times 10^{4}$ m/s. So $H_0 = 7.0 \times 10^{4}/(3.086 \times 10^{22}) = 2.268 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. A speed divided by a distance is one over a time.
</Step>
<Step title="The critical density">
$H_0^{2} = 5.145 \times 10^{-36}\ \text{s}^{-2}$ and $8\pi G = 1.677 \times 10^{-9}$ N m²/kg². Then $\rho_c = 3 \times 5.145 \times 10^{-36}/(1.677 \times 10^{-9}) = 9.20 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg/m}^{3}$.
</Step>
<Step title="In hydrogen atoms">
$9.20 \times 10^{-27}/(1.67 \times 10^{-27}) = 5.5$. The critical density is about **five or six hydrogen atoms in every cubic metre**.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: $\text{s}^{-2}/(\text{N m}^{2}/\text{kg}^{2}) = \text{s}^{-2}\,\text{kg}^{2}/(\text{kg m}^{3}\,\text{s}^{-2}) = \text{kg/m}^{3}$, a density. Guth's lecture finds $8.4 \times 10^{-30}$ g/cm³ for the Planck satellite's 2013 Hubble constant of about $67\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$, that is $8.4 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg/m}^{3}$, "about 5 protons per cubic meter" (transcript p. 22). Our $H$ is a little larger, so our density is a little larger: $9.20 \times (67/70)^{2} = 8.4$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

That is an astonishingly small density. Water has 1000 kg/m³, so the critical density is about $10^{-29}$ times the density of water, as Guth points out (transcript p. 22). Space between the galaxies is a far better vacuum than any machine on Earth can make. Yet matter spread this thinly through the enormous volume of the universe has enough gravity to decide its fate: a little more of it and the expansion would stop.

## How fast does the edge slow down?

Write the density as a multiple of the critical density. Let the ball start with radius $r_0$ and edge speed $v_0 = H r_0$. For a ball with exactly $\rho_c$ the zero-energy line above says $G M/r_0 = \tfrac{1}{2} v_0^{2}$; for any other density, $G M/r_0$ is $\rho/\rho_c$ times that. Since $G M/r = (G M/r_0)(r_0/r)$, conservation of energy, $\tfrac12 v^{2} - G M/r = \tfrac12 v_0^{2} - G M/r_0$, gives the speed of the edge at every later radius:

<Formula label="Speed of the edge as the ball grows" tex="v^{2} = v_0^{2}\left[1 - \frac{\rho}{\rho_c}\left(1 - \frac{r_0}{r}\right)\right]" cite="reading3" at="p. 17 (board), written there ȧ² = 2E + (8π/3)Gρ_i/a; this form with the density compared with ρ_c is the page's own" />

Here $\rho$ is the density at the start, when the radius is $r_0$ and the expansion rate $H$.

<WorkedExample title="A ball twice as dense as critical">
<Problem>
A ball of galaxies starts with twice the critical density, $\rho = 2\rho_c$. How large is it when it stops, compared with its starting size?
</Problem>
<Step title="Where the speed is zero">
With $\rho/\rho_c = 2$ the speed is zero when $1 - 2\,(1 - r_0/r) = 0$, so $1 - r_0/r = \tfrac12$ and $r_0/r = \tfrac12$.
</Step>
<Step title="The largest size">
$r = 2 r_0$: the ball grows to **twice** its starting radius, stops, and then falls back together. The plot shows it: with the slider at 2, the curve reaches zero at $r/r_0 = 2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
A density just above critical should stop much later. For $\rho/\rho_c = 1.25$: $1 - 1.25\,(1 - r_0/r) = 0$ gives $r_0/r = 0.2$, so $r = 5 r_0$. The closer the density is to critical, the larger the ball grows before it turns round, and at exactly $\rho_c$ it never turns round at all.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## What Einstein's theory says

Is a Newtonian picture good enough for the whole universe? Guth stresses that it is: this calculation gives exactly the answer of Einstein's general relativity (Lecture 6, transcript p. 6). Roughly, the reason is this: because the universe looks the same everywhere, a small ball already tells you everything, and inside a small ball Newton's gravity is accurate.

Divide the energy per kilogram, $\tfrac12 H^{2} r^{2} - \tfrac{4}{3}\pi G\rho\, r^{2}$, by $\tfrac12 r^{2}$ and you get an equation for the expansion rate:

<Formula label="The expansion rate from the energy" tex="H^{2} = \frac{8\pi}{3}\,G\rho + \frac{2}{r^{2}}\cdot\frac{E_{\text{mech}}}{m}" cite="reading3" at="p. 9 (board): the conservation law multiplied by 2/a², (ȧ/a)² − (8π/3)Gρ = 2E/a²; written here with H for ȧ/a and the energy per kilogram of the edge" />

This is the **Friedmann equation**, the central equation of cosmology, in Newtonian form. Alexander Friedmann first derived it in 1922 from Einstein's theory (transcript p. 9). In general relativity the energy term turns out to describe how space itself is curved. With zero energy it gives back $\rho = \rho_c$. The university course makes all of this precise (see Where this leads).

## The real universe

How dense is the real universe? In 2013 Guth said that, as far as we know, its density equals the critical density, to within about half a percent (transcript p. 22). The surprise is what that density is made of. Ordinary matter and dark matter (lesson 2.13) together make up only about a third of it, about 0.3 of the critical density, Guth said in Lecture 7 (transcript p. 23). The rest, about 0.7, is **dark energy**, which in Lecture 6 he put at "about 60% or 70% of the total" (transcript p. 15).

In 1998 astronomers measuring distant supernovae found that the expansion is not slowing down but **speeding up** (lesson 8.17). In our ball model that cannot happen: gravity only ever pulls back, so the edge can only slow down. Something else must be at work, and dark energy is the name for that unknown cause. So the simple question of this lesson, "will it stop?", is not decided by matter alone. If dark energy stays as it is, the expansion goes on for ever, faster and faster; whether it does stay so is still not known (lesson 8.17).

The ball model is still the right starting point. It shows where the equation of the expanding universe comes from and why there is a critical density, and adding dark energy to it is exactly what the university course does later.

## Summary

- Model the universe as a huge ball filled evenly with galaxies, expanding by **Hubble's law** $v = H r$. Any point can be the centre.
- By Newton's shell results, a galaxy on the edge feels only the pull of the mass inside, $M = \tfrac43\pi r^{3}\rho$, as if it sat at the centre. The matter outside has no effect.
- Only gravity does work, so the edge galaxy's energy per kilogram, $\tfrac12 v^{2} - G M/r$, stays constant. Positive or zero: the ball **expands for ever**. Negative: it stops at a largest size and **falls back**, as with the escape speed of lesson 3.11.
- With $v = H r$ the radius cancels: the dividing line is the **critical density** $\rho_c = 3 H^{2}/(8\pi G)$, the same for every ball. Denser balls stop; less dense ones do not.
- For $H_0 = 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc} = 2.27 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}$, $\rho_c \approx 9.2 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg/m}^{3}$: five or six hydrogen atoms per cubic metre.
- The same equation, the **Friedmann equation**, follows from Einstein's general relativity. Since 1998 we know the expansion is speeding up because of **dark energy**, so matter alone does not decide whether the universe stops.
