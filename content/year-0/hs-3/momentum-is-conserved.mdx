Lesson 3.6 introduced momentum, $\vec p = m\vec v$, and showed that a net force changes it: $\vec F_{\text{net}} = \Delta \vec p / \Delta t$. This lesson asks what happens when two or more objects push on **each other**. The answer is one of the most powerful laws in physics: whatever happens between the objects, their **total momentum** stays the same, as long as nothing outside them interferes. To say that precisely, we first need the idea of a system.

## Systems, internal and external forces

A **system** is the object or group of objects that we choose to study, for example two trolleys on a track, a person and a boat, or two colliding cars. Everything else is the **surroundings** (reading 1, Sec. 10.3). The choice is ours, and a good choice makes a problem easy; the MIT notes make choosing the system the first step of every problem (reading 1, Sec. 10.9.1).

Once the system is chosen, every force on its parts belongs to one of two kinds:

- An **internal force** is exerted by one part of the system on another part of the system: the push of one skater on the other, or the force of the spring between two trolleys.
- An **external force** comes from something outside the system: the Earth's gravity, the normal force of the floor, friction, air drag.

The **net external force** on the system is the vector sum of all the external forces (reading 1, Eq. (10.3.1)). When it is zero, the system is called an **isolated system** (reading 1, Sec. 10.7). Siyavula's definition is stricter: no external force acts on an isolated system at all, and it exchanges no matter with its surroundings, so nothing enters or leaves it. The MIT form, a net external force of zero, is the one we use.

Real systems are rarely isolated completely, but often nearly. For two trolleys on a level track, gravity pulls each trolley down and the track pushes it up with the normal force; these two external forces cancel. Friction with the track and the push of the air are external too, but often so small that we may ignore them. Treating a system as isolated is a choice we make when the external forces cancel or are too small to matter for the result.

## Why the total momentum cannot change

Take two objects, A and B, that push on each other for a short time $\Delta t$, for example two trolleys pushed apart by a spring. Newton's third law (lesson 2.4) says that the force of A on B and the force of B on A are equal in size and opposite in direction:

$\vec F_{AB} = -\vec F_{BA}.$

Both forces act for the same time $\Delta t$, because they last exactly as long as the contact. By lesson 3.6 each force changes the momentum of the object it acts on: the momentum of B changes by $\Delta \vec p_B = \vec F_{AB}\,\Delta t$, and that of A by $\Delta \vec p_A = \vec F_{BA}\,\Delta t$. The two impulses are therefore equal and opposite, and adding the changes gives

<Formula label="Momentum changes in an interaction" tex="\Delta \vec p_A = -\Delta \vec p_B \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta \vec p_A + \Delta \vec p_B = 0" cite="mit" at="Conservation of momentum (ESCJF), derivation for two billiard balls from F_net = Δp/Δt and Newton's third law" />

Whatever momentum A gains, B loses, and the other way round. The forces do not even need to be constant: at every instant they are equal and opposite, so the areas under their force–time graphs (lesson 3.6) are equal and opposite too.

The same works for any number of objects. Every internal force is one half of a third-law pair, and the two halves change the total momentum by equal and opposite amounts, so **all internal forces cancel in pairs** (reading 1, Sec. 10.4, Eqs. (10.4.3) to (10.4.5)). Only external forces can change the total momentum of a system. Over a time $\Delta t$:

<Formula label="Change in the total momentum of a system" tex="\Delta \vec p_{\text{sys}} = \vec F_{\text{ext}}\,\Delta t" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 10.9.1, Eq. (10.9.2) (average external impulse); Sec. 10.4, Eq. (10.4.10)" />

Here $\vec F_{\text{ext}}$ is the net external force (on average over $\Delta t$). This is lesson 3.6's impulse–momentum theorem, now for a whole system.

## The law of conservation of momentum

The **total momentum** of a system is the vector sum of the momenta of all its parts:

<Formula label="Total momentum of a system" tex="\vec p_{\text{sys}} = \vec p_1 + \vec p_2 + \cdots + \vec p_n" cite="mit" at="Conservation of momentum (ESCJF), sum below the definition box (written there as p_T)" />

For an isolated system $\vec F_{\text{ext}} = 0$, so $\Delta \vec p_{\text{sys}} = 0$. This is the **law of conservation of momentum**: *the total momentum of an isolated system is constant.* Whatever happens inside the system, a push, a collision or an explosion, the total momentum just before equals the total momentum just after:

<Formula label="Conservation of momentum" tex="\vec p_{\text{sys},i} = \vec p_{\text{sys},f}" cite="mit" at="Collisions (ESCJG), total momentum before and after" />

The MIT notes write the same equation as Eq. (10.7.5) (reading 1, Sec. 10.7). For two objects, labelled 1 and 2, moving along a line, write the initial velocities as $v_{1,i}$ and $v_{2,i}$ and the final ones as $v_{1,f}$ and $v_{2,f}$, each with its sign:

<Formula label="Conservation of momentum for two objects along a line" tex="m_1 v_{1,i} + m_2 v_{2,i} = m_1 v_{1,f} + m_2 v_{2,f}" cite="mit" at="Collisions (ESCJG), the equation for the conservation of momentum in a collision of two objects" />

(Siyavula writes the subscripts the other way round, with the i or f before the object's number; the order here follows the MIT notes, Eq. (10.7.3).)

The momentum of **each** object can change a great deal; only the total stays fixed. Remember too that the total is a vector sum. Two trolleys rolling towards each other, one of 0.50 kg at 2.0 m/s to the right and one of 0.80 kg at 1.5 m/s to the left, have, with right as positive, a total momentum of

$p_{\text{sys}} = 0.50 \times 2.0 + 0.80 \times (-1.5) = -0.20$ kg·m/s,

that is 0.20 kg·m/s to the left. Whatever the two trolleys then do to each other, their total momentum stays 0.20 kg·m/s to the left until an external force acts.

**A recipe.** Based on the MIT notes' problem-solving strategy (reading 1, Sec. 10.9.1), these steps work for every problem of this kind:

1. Choose the system, and check that the external forces cancel or are small during the event.
2. Choose a positive direction.
3. Write the total momentum just before and just after, with signs.
4. Set the two equal and solve for the unknown.
5. Check: units, size, and whether the total really is the same before and after.

## Pushing apart: carts, boats and cannons

The simplest case is a system that starts at rest. Its total momentum is zero, so it must still be zero afterwards: if one part moves off one way, another part must move off the other way.

<WorkedExample title="Two carts pushed apart by a spring">
<Problem>
A cart of 0.80 kg and a cart of 1.2 kg stand at rest on a level track, with a compressed spring between them. The spring is released, and the heavier cart rolls away to the right at 0.60 m/s. What is the velocity of the lighter cart?
</Problem>
<Step title="System and direction">
The system is the two carts with the spring; the spring's push is internal. Gravity and the normal force cancel, and friction is small. Take right as positive. Call the light cart 1 and the heavy cart 2.
</Step>
<Step title="Before and after">
Before, both are at rest: the total momentum is zero. After: $m_1 v_{1,f} + m_2 v_{2,f} = 0.80\,v_{1,f} + 1.2 \times 0.60$.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve">
$0 = 0.80\,v_{1,f} + 0.72$, so $v_{1,f} = -0.72 / 0.80 = -0.90$ m/s: 0.90 m/s to the left.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The two carts have momenta of 0.72 kg·m/s each, in opposite directions, which add to zero. The lighter cart moves faster, by the factor $1.2 / 0.80 = 1.5$, the inverse ratio of the masses: $0.90 / 0.60 = 1.5$. Units: kg·m/s divided by kg gives m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Siyavula describes an experiment like this one: two spring-loaded trolleys released between two barriers, with the distances they travel in the same time as a measure of their speeds (Siyavula, Conservation of momentum (ESCJF)).

<WorkedExample title="Stepping off a small boat">
<Problem>
A person of 60 kg stands in a small boat of 140 kg that floats at rest next to a jetty. The person jumps horizontally towards the jetty at 2.5 m/s, measured relative to the water. What happens to the boat?
</Problem>
<Step title="System and direction">
The system is the person and the boat; the push of the feet on the boat is internal. The water pushes on the boat sideways a little, but during the short jump its impulse is small. Take "towards the jetty" as positive. Call the person 1 and the boat 2.
</Step>
<Step title="Before and after">
Before: total momentum zero. After: $60 \times 2.5 + 140\,v_{2,f} = 0$, so $140\,v_{2,f} = -150$ kg·m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve">
$v_{2,f} = -150 / 140 \approx -1.1$ m/s: the boat moves away from the jetty at about 1.1 m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The person carries 150 kg·m/s towards the jetty and the boat $140 \times 1.07 \approx 150$ kg·m/s the other way. This is why stepping off a light boat is tricky: as you push off, the boat slides away from under you, and you do not get the push you expected.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The same reasoning explains **recoil**. When a gun fires, the expanding gases push the bullet forwards and the gun backwards. The gases themselves also rush forwards out of the barrel, so strictly the gun gets as much backward momentum as the bullet and the gases together get forward momentum. If we ignore the small mass of the gases, as the cannon example below does, the gun gets (nearly) as much backward momentum as the bullet gets forward momentum. Because the gun is much heavier, its backward speed is much smaller, but it is the "kick" a shooter feels in the shoulder. The jet and missile in Siyavula's Worked example 9 work the same way.

<WorkedExample title="The recoil of a cannon">
<Problem>
An old cannon of 1500 kg stands on wheels, at rest. It fires a ball of 12 kg horizontally at 180 m/s relative to the ground. With what speed does the cannon roll back? Ignore the mass of the gunpowder gases and the friction of the wheels during the shot.
</Problem>
<Step title="Before and after">
Take the direction of the ball as positive. Call the ball 1 and the cannon 2. The total momentum is zero before the shot, so afterwards $12 \times 180 + 1500\,v_{2,f} = 0$, which gives $1500\,v_{2,f} = -2160$ kg·m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve">
$v_{2,f} = -2160 / 1500 = -1.44$ m/s: the cannon rolls back at about 1.4 m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Ball and cannon have momenta of the same size, 2160 kg·m/s, in opposite directions. The speeds are in the inverse ratio of the masses: $180 / 1.44 = 125$, and $1500 / 12 = 125$. Units: m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Sticking together

Momentum conservation also works when objects collide and stay together. Afterwards they move as one object with one common velocity, and the mass of that object is the sum of the masses.

<WorkedExample title="Two railway wagons couple">
<Problem>
A railway wagon of $2.0 \times 10^{4}$ kg rolls at 3.0 m/s along a level track into a wagon of $3.0 \times 10^{4}$ kg at rest. The couplings lock, and the two wagons roll on together. How fast do they move?
</Problem>
<Step title="Before">
Take the direction of motion as positive. The total momentum before is $2.0 \times 10^{4} \times 3.0 + 0 = 6.0 \times 10^{4}$ kg·m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="After">
The coupled wagons have a mass of $5.0 \times 10^{4}$ kg and one common velocity $v_f$: $5.0 \times 10^{4}\,v_f = 6.0 \times 10^{4}$, so $v_f = 1.2$ m/s, forwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The common speed lies between the two starting speeds (0 and 3.0 m/s), as it must. The moving wagon has two-fifths of the total mass, and $\tfrac{2}{5} \times 3.0 = 1.2$ m/s. Units: kg·m/s divided by kg gives m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## What conservation of momentum does not say

**It does not say that kinetic energy is conserved.** Compare the kinetic energies in the examples above, using $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}$ (lesson 3.2):

- **Carts pushed apart.** Before, nothing moves: no kinetic energy. After, the carts have $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.80 \times 0.90^{2} \approx 0.32$ J and $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1.2 \times 0.60^{2} \approx 0.22$ J, together about 0.54 J. The kinetic energy came from the energy stored in the compressed spring (lesson 3.4).
- **The cannon.** The ball gets $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 12 \times 180^{2} \approx 1.9 \times 10^{5}$ J and the cannon only about $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1500 \times 1.44^{2} \approx 1.6 \times 10^{3}$ J. The two share the momentum equally, but the light ball takes 125 times as much kinetic energy, the same factor as the ratio of the masses. The energy came from the gunpowder.
- **The wagons.** Before: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 2.0 \times 10^{4} \times 3.0^{2} = 9.0 \times 10^{4}$ J. After: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 5.0 \times 10^{4} \times 1.2^{2} = 3.6 \times 10^{4}$ J. So 60% of the kinetic energy, $5.4 \times 10^{4}$ J, has gone into heat, sound and the squashing of the couplings.

So in an isolated system momentum is **always** conserved, while kinetic energy can grow (when stored energy is released) or shrink (when it turns into heat and sound). The **total** energy is still conserved, as lesson 3.4 showed; only its form changes. Lesson 3.8 sorts collisions by what happens to the kinetic energy: in an elastic collision it is the same afterwards, in an inelastic one some is lost, and objects that stick together lose the most.

**It does not say that each object keeps its momentum.** Only the total is fixed: in every example above, each object's momentum changed.

**It does not hold when a net external force acts.** A ball of 0.50 kg dropped from rest gains momentum as it falls: after 1.0 s it has $0.50 \times 9.81 \times 1.0 \approx 4.9$ kg·m/s downwards. The Earth's gravity is an external force on the ball, so the ball's momentum is not conserved. But choose the ball **and the Earth** as the system, and the gravitational forces become an internal third-law pair: the Earth is pulled up towards the ball and gains 4.9 kg·m/s upwards. With the Earth's mass of $5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg (lesson 2.11; NASA, Earth Fact Sheet), that is a speed of only about $8 \times 10^{-25}$ m/s, far too small to notice, but the total momentum of ball plus Earth stays zero (reading 1, Sec. 10.8).

**Short events help.** External forces often act during a collision or an explosion, for example the friction of the road on two crashing cars. But the internal forces of the impact are huge and the event is short, so the impulse of the external forces, force × time, is small compared with the momenta involved (reading 1, Sec. 10.9.1, Eq. (10.9.2)). That is why we compare the total momentum **just before** and **just after** the event. Long afterwards, friction will have stopped the wreck.

## Momentum in two dimensions

Momentum is a vector, so conservation of momentum is a vector law: the total momentum keeps both its size and its direction. In a plane, split every momentum into $x$- and $y$-components (lesson 0.8). Then each component is conserved on its own: the total $p_x$ before equals the total $p_x$ after, and the same for $p_y$. The MIT notes go further: if the external force is zero in one direction only, the momentum component in that direction is still conserved (reading 1, Sec. 10.9.1).

<Formula label="Conservation of one component" tex="p_{\text{sys},x,i} = p_{\text{sys},x,f}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 10.9.1, Eq. (10.9.4)" />

<WorkedExample title="A collision at an icy crossing">
<Problem>
A car of 1200 kg drives east at 10 m/s and a van of 1800 kg drives north at 8.0 m/s. They collide on an icy crossing and slide on locked together. Find their velocity just after the collision.
</Problem>
<Step title="Components before">
Take east as $+x$ and north as $+y$. Before, only the car moves east: $p_x = 1200 \times 10 = 1.2 \times 10^{4}$ kg·m/s. Only the van moves north: $p_y = 1800 \times 8.0 = 1.44 \times 10^{4}$ kg·m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Components after">
The wreck has a mass of 3000 kg and keeps both components: $v_x = 1.2 \times 10^{4} / 3000 = 4.0$ m/s and $v_y = 1.44 \times 10^{4} / 3000 = 4.8$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Size and direction">
Speed: $\sqrt{4.0^{2} + 4.8^{2}} \approx 6.2$ m/s. Direction: $\tan^{-1}(4.8 / 4.0) \approx 50^\circ$ north of east.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The direction lies between east and north, a little closer to north, because the van carried slightly more momentum than the car. The total momentum has size $\sqrt{(1.2 \times 10^{4})^{2} + (1.44 \times 10^{4})^{2}} \approx 1.9 \times 10^{4}$ kg·m/s, and with the unrounded speed of 6.25 m/s, $3000 \times 6.25 \approx 1.9 \times 10^{4}$ kg·m/s, the same.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Conservation of momentum is one of the laws physicists trust most. When Einstein's special relativity arrived, it turned out that Newton's momentum $mv$, added up for the objects in a collision, would be conserved in one frame of reference but not in another moving frame. Rather than give up the conservation law, physicists redefined momentum and energy (for fast objects) so that both are conserved in every frame; at everyday speeds the new momentum is just $mv$. The university cosmology course (8.286) uses these relativistic energy and momentum to describe light and fast particles in the early universe.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A **system** is the group of objects you choose to study. **Internal forces** act between its parts; **external forces** come from outside. A system with no net external force is **isolated**.
- By Newton's third law, two interacting objects get equal and opposite impulses: $\Delta \vec p_A = -\Delta \vec p_B$. So internal forces cancel in pairs, and only an external force can change the total momentum: $\Delta \vec p_{\text{sys}} = \vec F_{\text{ext}}\,\Delta t$.
- **Conservation of momentum:** the total momentum of an isolated system is constant, $\vec p_{\text{sys},i} = \vec p_{\text{sys},f}$; for two objects along a line, $m_1 v_{1,i} + m_2 v_{2,i} = m_1 v_{1,f} + m_2 v_{2,f}$.
- A system at rest that pushes itself apart (carts, a person and a boat, a gun or cannon) still has zero total momentum: the parts move off in opposite directions with speeds in the inverse ratio of their masses. Objects that stick together move on with the total momentum divided by the total mass.
- Kinetic energy need not be conserved: it can come from stored energy or turn into heat and sound. Lesson 3.8 sorts collisions by this.
- During short collisions and explosions external forces usually matter little, so compare the moments just before and just after. In two dimensions each component of the momentum is conserved separately.
