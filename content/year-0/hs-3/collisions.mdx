Lesson 3.7 showed that the total momentum of an isolated system is the same just before and just after a collision. It also showed that kinetic energy can change: two railway wagons that coupled lost 60% of theirs. This lesson sorts collisions by what happens to the kinetic energy, and shows what we can predict when we know which kind of collision it is. It ends with explosions, which are collisions run backwards, and with glancing collisions in a plane.

## Two quantities to check

In every collision we can compare two totals before and after: the total momentum and the total kinetic energy, $E_k = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2}$ for each object (lesson 3.2). For an isolated system the momentum always passes the test. The kinetic energy decides the kind of collision (Siyavula, Collisions (ESCJG); reading 1, Sec. 15.3):

- In an **elastic collision** the total kinetic energy after the collision is the same as before. Billiard balls and steel balls come close to this.
- In an **inelastic collision** the total kinetic energy after the collision is less than before. Most collisions of everyday objects are of this kind.
- In a **perfectly inelastic collision** the objects stick together and move on as one. This loses the most kinetic energy of all (see below). The MIT notes call it "totally inelastic"; Siyavula has no separate name for it and simply calls it inelastic.

For two objects moving along a line, an elastic collision obeys two conditions at once. The momentum condition is the one from lesson 3.7,

<Formula label="Momentum is conserved in every collision" tex="m_1 v_{1,i} + m_2 v_{2,i} = m_1 v_{1,f} + m_2 v_{2,f}" cite="mit" at="Collisions (ESCJG), the equation for the conservation of momentum in a collision of two objects" />

and the energy condition adds the kinetic energies of both objects:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy is conserved in an elastic collision" tex="\tfrac{1}{2} m_1 v_{1,i}^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} m_2 v_{2,i}^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} m_1 v_{1,f}^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} m_2 v_{2,f}^{2}" cite="mit" at="Elastic collisions (ESCJH), Worked example 11, the condition that energy is conserved" />

The MIT notes write the same two conditions as Eqs. (15.4.1) and (15.4.2) (reading 1, Sec. 15.4.1). Note the difference: momentum uses the velocities with their signs, kinetic energy uses their squares, which are never negative. A kinetic energy does not care about direction.

## Checking a collision

To find out what kind of collision took place, use momentum to find any unknown velocity, then add up the kinetic energies before and after.

<WorkedExample title="Is this collision elastic?">
<Problem>
A ball of 0.50 kg rolls along a straight track at 4.0 m/s and hits a block of 1.5 kg at rest. After the collision the ball rolls back at 1.0 m/s. Find the velocity of the block, and decide what kind of collision this is.
</Problem>
<Step title="Momentum">
Take the ball's starting direction as positive, and call the ball 1 and the block 2. Before: $0.50 \times 4.0 + 0 = 2.0$ kg·m/s. After: $0.50 \times (-1.0) + 1.5\,v_{2,f}$. Setting them equal gives $1.5\,v_{2,f} = 2.5$ kg·m/s, so $v_{2,f} = 2.5 / 1.5 \approx 1.7$ m/s, forwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Kinetic energy before and after">
Before: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.50 \times 4.0^{2} = 4.0$ J. After: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.50 \times 1.0^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1.5 \times 1.67^{2} \approx 0.25 + 2.08 \approx 2.3$ J.
</Step>
<Step title="Decide">
The kinetic energy has dropped by about 1.7 J, some 42% of the starting 4.0 J. The ball and block did not stick, so this is an inelastic collision.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Momentum after: $-0.50 + 1.5 \times 1.667 = 2.0$ kg·m/s, the same as before. The block moves forwards, the way it was pushed. Units: kg·m/s divided by kg gives m/s; kg × (m/s)² gives J.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Where the lost kinetic energy goes

Energy is never destroyed (lesson 3.4), so the 1.7 J must have gone somewhere. During a collision the objects squash into each other for a moment, like a ball that flattens against the floor. In an elastic collision they spring back completely, and all the energy stored in the squashing returns as kinetic energy (Siyavula, Elastic collisions (ESCJH)). In an inelastic collision they do not spring back fully, and the missing kinetic energy turns into other forms:

- **Thermal energy**: the objects get slightly warmer, just as friction warms a sliding book (lesson 3.4).
- **Sound**: the bang or click of the impact carries a little energy away.
- **Deformation**: a permanent change of shape, such as a dent in a car or a squashed lump of clay. Bending metal or clay out of shape takes energy (Siyavula, Inelastic collisions (ESCJJ)).

Siyavula's example is an asteroid that hits the Moon: most of its kinetic energy becomes heat, enough to melt rock and form a crater. A car crash is another: the crumpled metal shows where much of the kinetic energy went, and the crumple zones of lesson 3.6 are designed to absorb it. The total energy is conserved in every collision; only the **kinetic** energy can change.

A ball that bounces on the floor shows the difference too. A ball that bounced back to the height it was dropped from would have had an elastic collision with the floor. A real ball bounces back lower, so each bounce is a little inelastic, and the "missing" height is energy turned into heat and sound.

## Sticking together: perfectly inelastic collisions

When two objects stick together they have one common velocity $v_f$ afterwards, so the momentum condition becomes

<Formula label="Objects that stick together" tex="m_1 v_{1,i} + m_2 v_{2,i} = (m_1 + m_2)\,v_f" cite="mit" at="Inelastic collisions (ESCJJ), Worked example 14, the momentum equation for objects that stick together" />

This kind of collision loses the largest share of the kinetic energy that momentum conservation allows. The reason is that the total momentum must survive the collision, so the joined objects must keep moving at $v_f$ and keep the kinetic energy of that motion; all the rest of the kinetic energy is lost (reading 1, Sec. 15.3, Eq. (15.3.4), where the final relative velocity is zero). Two identical cars that crash head-on at the same speed and lock together have a total momentum of zero, so the wreck stops dead: all the kinetic energy is lost.

When the target is at rest, the fraction of the kinetic energy that is lost depends only on the masses (reading 1, Example 15.2):

<Formula label="Fraction of kinetic energy lost when sticking to a target at rest" tex="\text{fraction of } E_k \text{ lost} = \frac{m_2}{m_1 + m_2}" cite="reading1" at="Example 15.2, Eq. (15.5.5) (written there as ΔK/K_initial = −m₂/(m₁ + m₂), the minus sign showing a loss)" />

Here $m_1$ is the moving object and $m_2$ the target. A light object that sticks to a heavy target loses nearly all its kinetic energy; a heavy object that picks up a light one loses little.

<WorkedExample title="A lump of clay hits a trolley">
<Problem>
A lump of clay of 0.20 kg flies horizontally at 5.0 m/s into the back of a trolley of 0.80 kg at rest, and sticks to it. How fast does the trolley move off, and how much kinetic energy is lost?
</Problem>
<Step title="Common velocity">
Take the clay's direction as positive. Momentum before: $0.20 \times 5.0 = 1.0$ kg·m/s. After: $(0.20 + 0.80)\,v_f = 1.0$, so $v_f = 1.0$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Kinetic energy">
Before: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.20 \times 5.0^{2} = 2.5$ J. After: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1.00 \times 1.0^{2} = 0.50$ J. So 2.0 J is lost, 80% of the starting kinetic energy.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with the rule">
The fraction lost should be $m_2 / (m_1 + m_2) = 0.80 / 1.00 = 0.80$, that is 80%, as found. The common speed, 1.0 m/s, lies between the two starting speeds, 0 and 5.0 m/s, as it must.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Elastic head-on collisions

If a collision along a line is elastic, the two conditions (momentum and kinetic energy) are two equations for the two unknown final velocities, so both can be found. Solving them takes some algebra (Siyavula's Worked example 12 and reading 1, Sec. 15.4.1, do it in two different ways). The MIT notes first combine the two conditions into a surprisingly simple rule:

<Formula label="Relative velocity in a head-on elastic collision" tex="v_{1,i} - v_{2,i} = v_{2,f} - v_{1,f}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 15.4.1, Eq. (15.4.7)" />

The left side is the velocity of object 1 relative to object 2 before the collision (lesson 1.9); the right side is the same relative velocity after it, with the sign reversed. In words: **in a head-on elastic collision the objects separate as fast as they approached** (reading 1, Eq. (15.4.9)). Combining this with momentum gives the final velocities:

<Formula label="Final velocity of object 1 in a head-on elastic collision" tex="v_{1,f} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{1,i} + \frac{2 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{2,i}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 15.4.1, Eq. (15.4.14)" />

<Formula label="Final velocity of object 2 in a head-on elastic collision" tex="v_{2,f} = \frac{m_2 - m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{2,i} + \frac{2 m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{1,i}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 15.4.1, Eq. (15.4.17)" />

You do not need to learn these by heart; what matters is what they say in three special cases.

**Equal masses swap velocities.** With $m_1 = m_2$ the first fractions become zero and the second ones become 1:

<Formula label="Head-on elastic collision of equal masses" tex="v_{1,f} = v_{2,i}, \qquad v_{2,f} = v_{1,i}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 15.4.1, Eq. (15.4.21)" />

The two objects simply exchange velocities. A snooker ball that hits an identical ball at rest head-on stops dead, and the other ball rolls off with its velocity (Siyavula's Worked example 11 is a case of this). The balls of a Newton's cradle pass the motion along in the same way. It also works when both are moving: two gliders of 0.25 kg on an air track, one at +2.5 m/s and one at −1.5 m/s, bounce apart at −1.5 m/s and +2.5 m/s. Total momentum before and after: $0.25 \times (2.5 - 1.5) = 0.25$ kg·m/s.

**A target at rest.** With $v_{2,i} = 0$ only one term of each formula is left: the incoming object ends with $\frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{1,i}$, the target with $\frac{2 m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{1,i}$. The graph shows both for every mass ratio.

- **Light object hits a heavy one** ($m_1 \ll m_2$): the factor $\frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$ is close to $-1$, so the light object bounces back with nearly its old speed, and the heavy one barely moves. A ball bouncing off a wall is the extreme case (reading 1, Eq. (15.4.20) and Example 15.3).
- **Equal masses:** the incoming object stops, the target takes over its velocity.
- **Heavy object hits a light one** ($m_1 \gg m_2$): the heavy object keeps nearly its velocity, and the light target flies off at nearly **twice** that velocity (reading 1, Eq. (15.4.19)). A bowling ball of 7.0 kg rolling at 2.0 m/s that hits a table-tennis ball of 0.0027 kg elastically sends it off at $\frac{2 \times 7.0}{7.0027} \times 2.0$, just under 4.0 m/s.

<WorkedExample title="Two trolleys with spring bumpers">
<Problem>
A trolley of 0.60 kg rolls at 3.0 m/s into a trolley of 0.40 kg at rest. Both have springy bumpers, so the collision is elastic. Find both velocities afterwards.
</Problem>
<Step title="Use the formulas">
With $v_{2,i} = 0$ and $m_1 + m_2 = 1.00$ kg: $v_{1,f} = \frac{0.60 - 0.40}{1.00} \times 3.0 = 0.60$ m/s, and $v_{2,f} = \frac{2 \times 0.60}{1.00} \times 3.0 = 3.6$ m/s. Both move forwards; the lighter trolley moves faster.
</Step>
<Step title="Check the momentum">
Before: $0.60 \times 3.0 = 1.8$ kg·m/s. After: $0.60 \times 0.60 + 0.40 \times 3.6 = 0.36 + 1.44 = 1.8$ kg·m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check the kinetic energy">
Before: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.60 \times 3.0^{2} = 2.7$ J. After: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.60 \times 0.60^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.40 \times 3.6^{2} = 0.108 + 2.592 = 2.7$ J. Elastic, as assumed.
</Step>
<Step title="Check the relative speed">
They approached at $3.0 - 0 = 3.0$ m/s and separate at $3.6 - 0.60 = 3.0$ m/s, as the rule for elastic collisions demands.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Compare the ball and block of the first worked example. Had that collision been elastic, the formulas would have sent the ball back at $\frac{0.50 - 1.5}{2.0} \times 4.0 = -2.0$ m/s and the block forwards at $\frac{2 \times 0.50}{2.0} \times 4.0 = 2.0$ m/s. The real ball came back at only 1.0 m/s: the relative speed dropped from 4.0 m/s before to $1.67 + 1.0 \approx 2.7$ m/s after, another sign of an inelastic collision.

## Explosions: sticking together in reverse

Imagine a film of the clay sticking to the trolley, played backwards. One object (clay and trolley together) flies apart into two pieces. That is an **explosion**: a collision run in reverse. Momentum is conserved just as before, because the forces that push the pieces apart are internal. But now the kinetic energy **increases**: it comes from stored energy, such as the chemical energy of an explosive or a compressed spring (lesson 3.7's carts). The MIT notes call a collision in which the kinetic energy increases "superelastic" (reading 1, Sec. 15.3, Eq. (15.3.5)).

<WorkedExample title="A firework shell bursts">
<Problem>
A firework shell of 1.2 kg moves horizontally at 15 m/s at the top of its flight when it bursts into two pieces. A piece of 0.50 kg flies straight on at 30 m/s. Find the velocity of the other piece and the kinetic energy released.
</Problem>
<Step title="Momentum">
Gravity is an external force, but it acts downwards, and the burst is very short, so the horizontal momentum is conserved. Take the direction of flight as positive. Before: $1.2 \times 15 = 18$ kg·m/s. After: $0.50 \times 30 + 0.70\,v = 15 + 0.70\,v$. So $0.70\,v = 3.0$ kg·m/s and $v = 3.0 / 0.70 \approx 4.3$ m/s, forwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Kinetic energy">
Before: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1.2 \times 15^{2} = 135$ J. After: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.50 \times 30^{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.70 \times 4.29^{2} \approx 225 + 6.4 \approx 231$ J. The burst released about 96 J of chemical energy as kinetic energy.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by running it backwards">
If the two pieces, at 30 m/s and 4.29 m/s, collided and stuck together, they would move on at $(15 + 3.0) / 1.2 = 15$ m/s, the shell's original velocity, and lose the same 96 J. The explosion is exactly the perfectly inelastic collision in reverse.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Glancing collisions in two dimensions

Most collisions are not head-on. When two pucks on an air table or two billiard balls hit off-centre, they fly apart at angles. Momentum is a vector, so, as lesson 3.7 showed, each component of the total momentum is conserved on its own (reading 1, Sec. 15.6.1, Eqs. (15.6.3) and (15.6.4)). Split every momentum into $x$- and $y$-components (lesson 0.8) and apply conservation to each.

<WorkedExample title="Two pucks on an air table">
<Problem>
A puck of 0.20 kg slides along the $x$-axis at 4.0 m/s and strikes a puck of 0.30 kg at rest off-centre. Afterwards the first puck moves with velocity components $v_x = 1.0$ m/s and $v_y = 1.5$ m/s. Find the velocity of the second puck, and decide whether the collision is elastic.
</Problem>
<Step title="The x-components">
Before: $p_x = 0.20 \times 4.0 = 0.80$ kg·m/s. After: $0.20 \times 1.0 + 0.30\,v_x = 0.80$, so the second puck has $v_x = 0.60 / 0.30 = 2.0$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="The y-components">
Before: nothing moves along $y$, so $p_y = 0$. After: $0.20 \times 1.5 + 0.30\,v_y = 0$, so the second puck has $v_y = -0.30 / 0.30 = -1.0$ m/s. It moves off on the other side of the $x$-axis.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed and direction">
Second puck: speed $\sqrt{2.0^{2} + 1.0^{2}} \approx 2.2$ m/s, at $\tan^{-1}(1.0 / 2.0) \approx 27^\circ$ below the $x$-axis. First puck: speed $\sqrt{1.0^{2} + 1.5^{2}} \approx 1.8$ m/s, at $\tan^{-1}(1.5 / 1.0) \approx 56^\circ$ above it.
</Step>
<Step title="Kinetic energy">
Before: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.20 \times 4.0^{2} = 1.6$ J. After: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.20 \times (1.0^{2} + 1.5^{2}) + \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.30 \times (2.0^{2} + 1.0^{2}) = 0.325 + 0.75 \approx 1.1$ J. About a third of the kinetic energy is lost, so the collision is inelastic.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The $y$-momenta after the collision are $+0.30$ and $-0.30$ kg·m/s, which cancel, as they must. For the kinetic energy we used the squared speed, $v^{2} = v_x^{2} + v_y^{2}$ (lesson 0.8); kinetic energy has no components.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Try it

Open the **Explore 1D** screen of the simulation. Switch on the kinetic energy display, set the **elasticity** slider to 100% and let the two balls collide: the total kinetic energy is the same before and after. Untick **Reflecting Border** first, so that the balls do not bounce off the edges: a ball that hits the edge has its momentum changed by the wall. Now lower the elasticity to 50% and then to 0%, and compare. At 0% the balls stick together and the most kinetic energy disappears, while the total momentum of the two balls never changes. Then make the two masses equal at 100% elasticity, tick **Values** (or open **More Data**) to read the velocities, and check that the balls swap velocities. The elasticity measures how much of the relative speed survives the collision; the MIT notes call this ratio the coefficient of restitution (reading 1, Eq. (15.3.1)).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
The early universe was filled with light and with free electrons, and the two kept colliding. Light that meets a free electron bounces off in a new direction, but when the light has far too little energy to make the electron recoil noticeably, as was the case then, it keeps almost all its energy: very like a light ball bouncing off a heavy one in an elastic collision. Because the light was scattered again and again, it could not travel far in a straight line, and the universe was opaque, like fog. When the universe had cooled enough for the electrons to be captured by nuclei into atoms, after a few hundred thousand years, the collisions stopped and the light has travelled freely ever since. The university cosmology course (8.286) works out when this happened; that light reaches us today as the cosmic microwave background.

## Summary

- Momentum is conserved in every collision of an isolated system. The **kinetic energy** decides the kind of collision.
- **Elastic**: the total kinetic energy is the same before and after. **Inelastic**: some kinetic energy becomes thermal energy, sound and deformation. **Perfectly inelastic**: the objects stick together, $m_1 v_{1,i} + m_2 v_{2,i} = (m_1 + m_2)\,v_f$, and lose the most kinetic energy; for a target at rest the fraction lost is $m_2/(m_1 + m_2)$. The total energy is always conserved.
- To classify a collision, find the unknown velocity from momentum, then compare the total kinetic energy before and after.
- In a head-on **elastic** collision the objects separate as fast as they approached. **Equal masses swap velocities**; a light object bounces back off a heavy one; a heavy object sends a light target off at nearly twice its own velocity.
- An **explosion** is a perfectly inelastic collision in reverse: the momentum is unchanged, but the kinetic energy grows, released from stored energy.
- In a **glancing collision** each component of the total momentum is conserved separately; kinetic energy is computed from the squared speeds.
