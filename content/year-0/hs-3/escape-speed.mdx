Throw a ball straight up and it comes back down. Throw it harder and it goes higher before it turns round. Is there a speed so large that it never comes back at all? Lesson 3.3 could not answer that, because $m g h$ assumes that gravity is equally strong at every height. Lesson 2.11 showed that it is not: the pull of a planet weakens with distance. This lesson finds the potential energy that allows for that weakening, and with it the speed needed to escape from the Earth, the Moon or the Sun.

## Why mgh is not enough

The formula $E_p = m g h$ of lesson 3.3 treats the force of gravity on an object as a constant $m g$. By Newton's law of gravitation (lesson 2.11), the force between a planet of mass $M$ and an object of mass $m$ at a distance $r$ from the planet's centre is

<Formula label="Newton's law of gravitation" tex="F = \frac{G M m}{r^{2}}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 14.4.3, Eq. (14.13) (written there as a vector, with m₁ and m₂)" />

with $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ N m²/kg². Double the distance from the centre and the force drops to a quarter. Near the ground, where $r$ hardly changes, $m g h$ works well. But for a rocket that climbs thousands of kilometres, the force keeps getting smaller, and $m g h$ overestimates the energy needed. To go further we need the work done against a force that changes along the way.

## Work against a force that weakens

Lesson 3.1 showed that the work done by a force that changes is the **area under the force–displacement graph**, found by cutting it into thin strips (as for the area under a velocity–time graph in lesson 0.10). So the work needed to lift an object from the surface, at $r = R$, out to a distance $r$ is the area under the curve $G M m/r^{2}$ between those two distances.

That area can be found without calculus, by a neat trick. Take one thin strip, from a distance $r_1$ to a slightly larger distance $r_2$. Across the strip the force falls from $G M m/r_1^{2}$ to $G M m/r_2^{2}$. A value in between is $G M m/(r_1 r_2)$: it is smaller than the first and larger than the second. Use it as the height of the strip. Its area is then

<Formula label="Area of one thin strip" tex="\frac{G M m}{r_1 r_2}\,(r_2 - r_1) = G M m \left(\frac{1}{r_1} - \frac{1}{r_2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Sec. 14.4.3, Eq. (14.15) does the same sum with an integral; this strip-by-strip form is the page's own" />

Now add the strips one after another, from $R$ to $r_1$, from $r_1$ to $r_2$, from $r_2$ to $r_3$, and so on up to $r$. Every middle term appears once with a plus sign and once with a minus sign, so they all cancel, and only the first and the last are left:

<Formula label="Work to lift an object from R to r" tex="W = G M m \left(\frac{1}{R} - \frac{1}{r}\right)" cite="mit" at="Sec. 14.4.3, Eq. (14.15) (ΔU from r_i = R to r_f = r; the work done against gravity)" />

The thinner the strips, the less it matters which in-between value we chose for each one, so in the limit of very thin strips this result is exact. It is the gain in potential energy, $\Delta E_p$, between $R$ and $r$.

Two checks. First, lifting a 1.0 kg object from the Earth's surface to $r = 2R$, twice as far from the centre, takes $G M m/(2R) = 3.13 \times 10^{7}$ J, while $m g h$ with $h = R = 6.371 \times 10^{6}$ m would give $6.25 \times 10^{7}$ J, twice as much. Second, for a small height $h$, with $r = R + h$, the work becomes $G M m\,h/(R(R + h))$, and when $h$ is tiny compared with $R$ that is $m\,(G M/R^{2})\,h$. Since $G M/R^{2} = g$ (lesson 2.11), it is just $m g h$. So the new result contains the old one.

## Potential energy with the zero at infinity

In lesson 3.3 you could put the zero level anywhere. Far from a planet, the most useful choice is a place where gravity has no effect at all: infinitely far away. Let $r$ in the formula for $W$ grow without end. Then $1/r$ shrinks towards zero, and the work to take the object from $R$ all the way out to infinity is $G M m/R$: a **finite** amount, even though the distance is endless. That is because the force dies away so quickly.

If the potential energy is zero at infinity, and getting there from a distance $r$ takes an amount $G M m/r$ of work, then at $r$ the object must have $G M m/r$ less than zero:

<Formula label="Gravitational potential energy, zero at infinity" tex="E_p = -\frac{G M m}{r}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 14.4.3, Eq. (14.16), written there U_G(r) = −Gm₁m₂/r with U_G(∞) ≡ 0" />

Here $r$ is the distance from the **centre** of the planet, not the height above its surface. The minus sign often surprises people, but it says something simple: an object near a planet is trapped in a "well", and you must add energy to pull it out to where the energy is zero. The closer to the planet, the deeper in the well and the more negative $E_p$. Negative values were already allowed in lesson 3.3 for an object below the chosen zero level; here **every** point is below it. As before, only changes have a meaning, and the changes are exactly those of the formula for $W$ above.

A satellite of 1200 kg is to be raised from the ground to a height of 1000 km. How much does its gravitational potential energy increase? Compare with $m g h$.
</Problem>

## Escape speed

Now the question from the start. Launch an object from the surface of a planet at a speed $v$, and ignore air resistance. Only gravity does work, so by lesson 3.4 its mechanical energy stays the same:

<Formula label="Mechanical energy far from a planet" tex="E_{\text{mech}} = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2} - \frac{G M m}{r}" cite="mit" at="Example 14.2, Eq. (14.2) of the example (written there for the surface, r = R)" />

As the object climbs, $r$ grows, the potential energy rises towards zero and the kinetic energy falls. If it is to reach infinity, its kinetic energy must still be zero or more when its potential energy has reached zero. The smallest launch speed that does this leaves the object with exactly zero speed "at infinity", so its mechanical energy is exactly zero all the way. Setting $\tfrac{1}{2} m v^{2} - G M m/R = 0$ and solving for $v$ gives the **escape speed**:

<Formula label="Escape speed from the surface" tex="v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M}{R}}" cite="mit" at="Example 14.2, Eq. (14.6) of the example (written there v_esc, for the asteroid Toro)" />

MIT's notes call it the escape velocity, but only its size matters: as long as the object does not hit the planet or its air, any direction away from the ground will do. The mass $m$ of the object has cancelled, just as in free fall: a pebble and a spacecraft need the same speed. For the Earth, $G M/R = 6.256 \times 10^{7}$ J/kg, so

$v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2 \times 6.256 \times 10^{7}} = 1.12 \times 10^{4}$ m/s, about **11.2 km/s** or about 40 000 km/h.

The Earth Fact Sheet lists 11.186 km/s. In energy terms, every kilogram that is to escape from the ground needs 62.6 MJ. Real rockets do not reach 11.2 km/s at the ground: they speed up gradually above the dense air, keeping their engines on. But the energy the spacecraft itself must gain is the same (the fuel burns much more), because only the start and the end of the path matter.

Escaping from the Earth does not mean escaping from the Sun. An object that just escapes from the Earth still goes round the Sun, roughly in the Earth's orbit; leaving the solar system takes more (see the last section).

<WorkedExample title="How small a moon could you jump off?">
<Problem>
Someone who can jump 0.50 m high on Earth leaves the ground at $\sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.50} = 3.1$ m/s (lesson 3.4). Take a small moon of rock and loose rubble, with a density (mass per cubic metre, lesson 0.2) of 2000 kg/m³, the page's own round value. What is the largest radius such a moon could have for this jump to carry the jumper away for good?
</Problem>
<Step title="Write the mass with the radius">
The volume of a sphere is $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}$, so the moon's mass is $M = 2000 \times \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}$ kg (with $R$ in metres). Put this into the escape speed: $v_{\text{esc}}^{2} = 2GM/R = 2G \times 2000 \times \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^{2}$. The radius no longer cancels: at the same density, a moon twice as wide has twice the escape speed.
</Step>
<Step title="Solve for the radius">
The number in front is $2 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 2000 \times \tfrac{4}{3}\pi = 1.12 \times 10^{-6}$ per second squared. The jump gives $v^{2} = 2 \times 9.81 \times 0.50 = 9.81$ m²/s². Setting $v_{\text{esc}}^{2} = v^{2}$: $R^{2} = 9.81/(1.12 \times 10^{-6}) = 8.76 \times 10^{6}$ m², so $R = 2.96 \times 10^{3}$ m, about **3 km**. A moon of this kind with a radius under about 3 km could be left with a jump; on a larger one the jumper comes back down, slowly.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: $(\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2)/(1/\text{s}^2) = \text{m}^2$, so $R$ comes out in metres. Put the radius back: $M = 2000 \times \tfrac{4}{3}\pi \times (2.96 \times 10^{3})^{3} = 2.17 \times 10^{14}$ kg, and $\sqrt{2 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 2.17 \times 10^{14}/2.96 \times 10^{3}} = 3.13$ m/s, the jump speed again. For a real moon of this kind: Phobos, the larger moon of Mars, has a mass of $1.06 \times 10^{16}$ kg (Mars Fact Sheet) and radii of 13.0, 11.4 and 9.1 km. As a sphere of radius 11 km, about their average, its density is $1.06 \times 10^{16}/(\tfrac{4}{3}\pi \times (1.1 \times 10^{4})^{3}) = 1.9 \times 10^{3}$ kg/m³, close to 2000, and its escape speed is $\sqrt{2 \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.06 \times 10^{16}/1.1 \times 10^{4}} = 11$ m/s. Phobos is almost four times wider than the 3 km moon, so a jumper stays on it, as the step above predicts. (Its rough shape makes the true value differ from place to place.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The low escape speed of the Moon is one reason it has almost no air: gas molecules there, warmed by sunlight, slowly leak away into space.

## Bound or unbound: the sign of the total energy

The escape speed is the dividing line between two kinds of motion, and the total mechanical energy $E_{\text{mech}} = E_k + E_p$ tells you which side an object is on. Since $E_k$ is never negative and $E_p$ rises to zero only at infinity:

- $E_{\text{mech}} < 0$: the object is **bound**. It can never reach infinity, because there its energy would be at least zero. It rises until its kinetic energy is used up and falls back, or it moves round the planet on a closed orbit, an ellipse (MIT 8.01SC notes, Ch. 25, Sec. 25.4.2).
- $E_{\text{mech}} = 0$: it **just escapes**, slowing down towards zero speed as it gets ever further away (Sec. 25.4.3).
- $E_{\text{mech}} > 0$: it is **unbound** and escapes with speed to spare: far away it still has kinetic energy $E_{\text{mech}}$ left (Sec. 25.4.4).

<WorkedExample title="Two probes fired straight up">
<Problem>
Two probes are fired straight up from the ground, one at 8.0 km/s and one at 12.0 km/s. Ignore the air and the Earth's rotation. How high does the first one get? How fast is the second one moving when it is very far away?
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy per kilogram">
Divide everything by $m$, which cancels anyway. At the ground $G M/R = 6.256 \times 10^{7}$ J/kg. The first probe has $\tfrac{1}{2} v^{2} = 3.2 \times 10^{7}$ J/kg, so its total is $3.2 \times 10^{7} - 6.256 \times 10^{7} = -3.056 \times 10^{7}$ J/kg: negative, so it is bound.
</Step>
<Step title="The highest point">
At the top its speed is zero, so all of its total is potential energy: $-G M/r_{\text{max}} = -3.056 \times 10^{7}$ J/kg, which gives $r_{\text{max}} = 3.986 \times 10^{14}/3.056 \times 10^{7} = 1.304 \times 10^{7}$ m. That is a height of $1.304 \times 10^{7} - 6.371 \times 10^{6} = 6.67 \times 10^{6}$ m, about 6700 km.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare with mgh">
With a constant $g$, $\tfrac{1}{2} v^{2} = g h$ would give $h = v^{2}/(2g) = 3.3 \times 10^{6}$ m, only about half as high. Because gravity weakens on the way up, the probe gets much further.
</Step>
<Step title="The faster probe">
At 12.0 km/s, $\tfrac{1}{2} v^{2} = 7.2 \times 10^{7}$ J/kg, more than $6.256 \times 10^{7}$, so the total is positive and the probe escapes. Far away all of its energy is kinetic: $\tfrac{1}{2} v_f^{2} = \tfrac{1}{2} v^{2} - G M/R$, so $v_f = \sqrt{v^{2} - v_{\text{esc}}^{2}} = \sqrt{(12.0 \times 10^{3})^{2} - (11.19 \times 10^{3})^{2}} = 4.3 \times 10^{3}$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: J/kg = m²/s², so $\sqrt{\text{J/kg}}$ is a speed and $(\text{m}^3\text{/s}^2)/(\text{J/kg}) = $ m. Both answers lie on the right side of the dividing line: 8.0 km/s is below 11.2 km/s and turns back, 12.0 km/s is above it and keeps a little speed.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Orbit speed and escape speed

A satellite on a circular orbit is bound too. Lesson 2.12 found its speed from $G M m/r^{2} = m v^{2}/r$:

<Formula label="Speed in a circular orbit" tex="v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 25.4.1, Eq. (25.4.15) (μ there is m when M ≫ m), solved for the speed (the step is the page's own)" />

Multiply $m v^{2} = G M m/r$ by one half: the kinetic energy of the satellite is $\tfrac{1}{2} G M m/r$, exactly half the size of its potential energy $-G M m/r$. So its total energy is

<Formula label="Total energy of a circular orbit" tex="E_{\text{mech}} = \frac{G M m}{2 r} - \frac{G M m}{r} = -\frac{G M m}{2 r}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 25.4.1 (the circular orbit has the lowest energy for a given angular momentum); this form for E is the page's own step from Eq. (25.4.15)" />

negative, as it must be for a bound orbit. To escape from the same distance, the total must be raised to zero: the kinetic energy must grow from $\tfrac{1}{2} G M m/r$ to $G M m/r$, that is, **double**. Doubling the kinetic energy means multiplying the speed by $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$. So at every distance the escape speed $\sqrt{2 G M/r}$ is $\sqrt{2}$ times the circular orbit speed $\sqrt{G M/r}$, the factor that lesson 2.12 announced without proof.

Just above the ground the circular speed is 7.91 km/s (lesson 2.9) and the escape speed 11.2 km/s. For the International Space Station, at $r = 6.791 \times 10^{6}$ m, lesson 2.12 found 7.66 km/s; from that height an escape needs $\sqrt{2} \times 7.66 = 10.8$ km/s.

The same rule works for the Earth going round the Sun. Its mean speed is 29.78 km/s (Earth Fact Sheet). The speed needed to escape from the Sun, starting from the Earth's distance of $1.496 \times 10^{11}$ m, is $\sqrt{2 G M/r} = 42.1$ km/s, now with $M$ the Sun's mass, $1.9884 \times 10^{30}$ kg. The ratio is $42.1/29.78 = 1.41$, as expected (the Earth's orbit is almost a circle). This is why leaving the solar system is so much harder than leaving the Earth.

## Try it

In the simulation, set up a sun and a planet and change the planet's starting speed. At one speed the path is a circle; a little faster and it becomes a longer ellipse; at about $\sqrt{2}$ times the circle speed and above, the planet no longer comes back. <Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
The question "will it come back or escape?" returns on the largest scale of all. In MIT's cosmology course, Alan Guth pictures the universe, in a simple Newtonian model, as a huge ball of matter that is expanding evenly, and follows a particle on its edge (MIT 8.286, Lecture 6). That particle is like a ball thrown up from a planet: the matter inside the ball pulls it back, so it slows down. If its total energy is negative, the expansion eventually stops and turns into a collapse; if it is zero or positive, the expansion goes on for ever. Guth then turns this energy into the equation for the expansion of the universe (Lecture 7). Lesson 3.12 works through this model step by step. In the full theory, general relativity, the same sign is tied to the geometry of space, and the real universe also contains dark energy, which makes today's expansion speed up; so the simple energy argument is a starting point, not the whole story.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Far from a planet, gravity weakens as $1/r^{2}$, so $m g h$ no longer works. The work to lift an object from $R$ to $r$ is the area under the force graph: $G M m\,(1/R - 1/r)$.
- With the zero level at infinity, the gravitational potential energy is $E_p = -G M m/r$, with $r$ measured from the centre. It is negative because pulling an object out to infinity takes positive work. Near the ground its changes reduce to $m g\,\Delta h$.
- Energy conservation gives the **escape speed** $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2 G M/R}$, the least launch speed that reaches infinity. It does not depend on the object's mass: 11.2 km/s for the Earth, 2.38 km/s for the Moon, 618 km/s for the Sun.
- The sign of the total energy $E_{\text{mech}} = E_k + E_p$ decides: negative means **bound**, zero means it just escapes, positive means **unbound** with speed to spare.
- A circular orbit has $E_{\text{mech}} = -G M m/(2r)$. Escaping from it means doubling the kinetic energy, so the escape speed is $\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$ times the orbit speed at the same distance.
- In a simple Newtonian model of the universe, the sign of the energy of an expanding ball of matter decides whether the expansion stops (lesson 3.12).
