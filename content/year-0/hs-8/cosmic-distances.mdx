How far away is a star? You cannot pace it out or stretch a tape measure to it, and its light tells you nothing about distance on its own: a faint point of light could be a dim star nearby or a brilliant one far away. Yet astronomers quote distances to galaxies billions of light-years away. They get there step by step, with a set of methods called the **cosmic distance ladder**. Each method works over a certain range of distances, and each is checked against the one below it, where the ranges overlap.

## The ladder at a glance

| Rung | Method | Works out to about |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | radar echoes | the planets of the Solar System |
| 2 | parallax | nearby stars in our own galaxy |
| 3 | Cepheid variables | nearby galaxies |
| 4 | Type Ia supernovae | galaxies billions of light-years away |
| 5 | redshift | the most distant galaxies (lesson 8.11) |

The first two rungs are geometry: they need nothing but angles, times and the speed of light. The upper rungs use brightness, and so depend on the rungs below to tell how bright their objects really are. A mistake low on the ladder carries all the way up, which is why astronomers work so hard on its bottom rungs (NASA's illustration "Three Steps to the Hubble Constant" shows the upper three).

## Radar in the Solar System

Inside the Solar System we can bounce a signal off a planet. A radio telescope sends a short radar pulse towards Venus and times how long the echo takes to come back. Radio waves travel at the speed of light $c$ (lesson 1.11), and the pulse covers the distance twice, there and back, so

<Formula label="Distance from a radar echo" tex="d = \frac{c\,t}{2}" cite="radar" at="radar signals sent to Venus and back, 1961" />

where $t$ is the time between sending the pulse and receiving the echo. In 1961 engineers at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory did exactly this with Venus, and from the result they found the **astronomical unit** (AU), the average distance between the Earth and the Sun, far more precisely than before. Today it is fixed at $1\ \text{AU} = 1.496 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$ (149 597 870.7 km). The AU is the measuring rod for the next rung.

<WorkedExample title="An echo from Venus">
<Problem>
A radar pulse sent towards Venus comes back after $276\ \text{s}$ (4.6 minutes). How far away is Venus at that moment?
</Problem>
<Step title="Halve the round trip">
$d = c\,t/2 = 3.00 \times 10^{8} \times 276/2 = 4.14 \times 10^{10}\ \text{m}$, about 41 million kilometres.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Radar does not reach the stars: even the nearest star is so far away that an echo would be hopelessly weak, and it would take years to come back.

## Parallax

Hold a finger at arm's length and look at it with one eye, then the other. The finger seems to jump against the background. That apparent shift, caused by looking from two different places, is **parallax**. Bring the finger closer and the jump gets bigger: the size of the shift tells the distance.

Astronomers use the Earth's orbit as the two "eyes". In January and in July the Earth is on opposite sides of the Sun, 2 AU apart. A nearby star, photographed six months apart, seems to shift a little against much more distant stars. The **parallax angle** $p$ is half of that shift: the angle under which the radius of the Earth's orbit, 1 AU, would be seen from the star.

<ParallaxDiagram caption="Generated diagram: as the Earth goes round the Sun, a nearby star seems to move back and forth against the distant stars. The parallax angle p is half of that yearly shift: the angle that 1 AU makes as seen from the star." />

The triangle Sun–Earth–star has a right angle at the Sun, so $\tan p = 1\ \text{AU}/d$. Stellar parallaxes are tiny, and for a small angle in radians $\tan p \approx p$ (lesson 0.7). So

<Formula label="Distance from parallax" tex="d = \frac{1\ \text{AU}}{p} \qquad (p \text{ in radians})" cite="mit" at="p. 8: the parsec defined by a triangle whose base is 1 AU (board)" />

Parallaxes are so small that they are measured in **arcseconds**. A degree is split into 60 arcminutes, and an arcminute into 60 arcseconds, so one arcsecond is $1/3600$ of a degree. In radians,

<Formula label="One arcsecond in radians" tex="1\ \text{arcsec} = \frac{1^{\circ}}{3600} = \frac{\pi}{180 \times 3600}\ \text{rad} = 4.848 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{rad}" cite="reading2" at="entry Parsec (1 arcsecond)" />

That is about the angle of a 2 cm coin seen from 4 kilometres away.

## The parsec

Astronomers turn this into a unit. The **parsec** (pc, from *parallax second*) is the distance at which a star has a parallax of exactly one arcsecond, that is, the distance from which 1 AU is seen under an angle of 1 arcsecond:

<Formula label="The parsec" tex="1\ \text{pc} = \frac{1\ \text{AU}}{4.848 \times 10^{-6}} = \frac{1.496 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}}{4.848 \times 10^{-6}} = 3.086 \times 10^{16}\ \text{m}" cite="mit" at="p. 8 (board): the distance at which 1 AU subtends one second of arc" />

With a light-year of $9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m}$ this is $3.086 \times 10^{16}/9.46 \times 10^{15} = 3.26$ light-years, so one parsec is about $3.09 \times 10^{16}\ \text{m}$ or 3.26 light-years. Guth's lecture gives 3.2616 light-years. For larger distances there are the kiloparsec (kpc, 1000 pc) and the megaparsec (Mpc, a million parsecs), the unit of lesson 5.13.

Because the distance is $1/p$, a star with half the parallax is twice as far away. With $p$ in arcseconds the distance comes out directly in parsecs:

<Formula label="Distance in parsecs" tex="d\ (\text{pc}) = \frac{1}{p\ (\text{arcsec})}" cite="reading2" at="entry Parsec: the distance at which a star shows a parallax of 1 arcsecond" />

<WorkedExample title="A star with a parallax of 0.125 arcseconds">
<Problem>
A star photographed six months apart shows a parallax of $p = 0.125$ arcseconds. Find its distance in parsecs, metres and light-years.
</Problem>
<Step title="Parsecs">
$d = 1/p = 1/0.125 = 8.0\ \text{pc}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Metres">
$d = 8.0 \times 3.09 \times 10^{16} = 2.47 \times 10^{17}\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Light-years">
$d = 2.47 \times 10^{17}/9.46 \times 10^{15} = 26.1$ light-years. The light we see from it left the star 26 years ago.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Even the nearest stars show a parallax of less than one arcsecond, so every star is more than a parsec away. For a long time parallax worked reliably only out to a few hundred light-years. In a later development, in 2014, the Hubble Space Telescope measured the parallax of Cepheid stars about 7500 light-years away.

<WorkedExample title="How small is that angle?">
<Problem>
What parallax does a star 7500 light-years away have?
</Problem>
<Step title="Distance in parsecs">
$d = 7500/3.26 = 2300\ \text{pc}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Parallax">
$p = 1/d = 1/2300 = 4.3 \times 10^{-4}$ arcseconds: less than a two-thousandth of an arcsecond.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Other galaxies are millions of light-years away. Their parallaxes are far too small to measure, so beyond this point a different idea is needed.

## Brightness and the inverse square law

Lesson 7.11 showed how the light of a star spreads out. A star sends out its luminosity $L$, its total power, equally in all directions. At a distance $d$ that power is spread over a sphere of area $4\pi d^2$, so the power arriving on each square metre, the **flux** $F$, is

<Formula label="Flux at a distance d" tex="F = \frac{L}{4\pi d^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 8, eq. (15); the 1/r² fall-off from eqs. (3)–(4), p. 4" />

This is the inverse square law of lessons 0.6 and 5.5. Now turn it round. If you know how luminous an object really is, measuring its flux gives its distance:

<Formula label="Distance from luminosity and flux" tex="d = \sqrt{\frac{L}{4\pi F}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 8, eq. (15), solved for D" />

An object whose luminosity is known is called a **standard candle**, after the old idea of a candle of fixed brightness. Often you do not even need $L$ itself: two standard candles of the same kind have the same luminosity, so the ratio of their fluxes gives the ratio of their distances,

<Formula label="Comparing two identical candles" tex="\frac{F_1}{F_2} = \left(\frac{d_2}{d_1}\right)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d_2 = d_1\sqrt{\frac{F_1}{F_2}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (4), p. 4" />

The method is only as good as the luminosity. The upper rungs of the ladder are kinds of objects whose luminosity can be found from something we can measure.

## Cepheid variables

**Cepheid variables** are stars that pulse: they swell and shrink in a regular rhythm, and their brightness rises and falls with it. The time of one cycle is the star's **period**. In 1912 Henrietta Leavitt, studying variable stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud (a small galaxy next to our own), found that the brighter Cepheids have longer periods. All the stars of that cloud are at nearly the same distance from us, so the brighter-looking ones really are the more luminous ones. This is the **period–luminosity relation**: the longer a Cepheid's period, the more luminous it is.

That makes a Cepheid a standard candle with a built-in label. Measure how long it takes to pulse and you know its luminosity; measure its flux and the inverse square law gives the distance. First, though, the relation must be calibrated: the luminosity of some Cepheids must be found from their distances, measured by parallax. That is why Hubble's parallaxes of Cepheids, above, matter so much. Cepheids are bright enough to be seen in other galaxies, and in Lecture 4 Guth calls them crucial to the cosmological distance scale.

<WorkedExample title="Two Cepheids with the same period">
<Problem>
A Cepheid in our galaxy is known from parallax to be $2000\ \text{pc}$ away. A Cepheid in another galaxy has exactly the same period, but its flux is a million times smaller. How far away is that galaxy?
</Problem>
<Step title="Same period, same luminosity">
The two stars have the same luminosity, so $d_2 = d_1\sqrt{F_1/F_2} = 2000 \times \sqrt{10^{6}} = 2000 \times 1000 = 2.0 \times 10^{6}\ \text{pc}$.
</Step>
<Step title="In other units">
That is $2.0\ \text{Mpc}$, or $2.0 \times 10^{6} \times 3.26 = 6.5$ million light-years.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Type Ia supernovae

Even a bright Cepheid is just one star among billions in its galaxy, and in very distant galaxies single Cepheids are too faint to pick out. The next rung needs something far brighter.

A **white dwarf** is the small, dense remnant left when a star like the Sun has used up its fuel (lessons 7.6 and 8.5): about a Sun's worth of mass squeezed to the size of the Earth. A white dwarf in a pair of stars can pull gas from its companion, or merge with it. When it passes a limit it can no longer support itself and explodes completely. That explosion is a **Type Ia supernova**. For a few weeks it can outshine its whole galaxy, and because these explosions happen in such similar conditions, they all reach roughly the same peak luminosity. Type Ia supernovae are therefore standard candles that can be seen across billions of light-years.

They are calibrated in the same way as everything else on the ladder: in nearby galaxies that contain both Cepheids and a Type Ia supernova, the Cepheids give the distance, and so the supernova's luminosity.

<WorkedExample title="A supernova in a faraway galaxy">
<Problem>
Take the peak luminosity of a Type Ia supernova to be $1.0 \times 10^{36}\ \text{W}$ for this example (about 2.6 billion times the Sun's $3.828 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}$). At its peak, a telescope measures a flux of $2.0 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{W/m}^2$ from one. How far away is it?
</Problem>
<Step title="Use the inverse square law">
$d = \sqrt{L/4\pi F} = \sqrt{1.0 \times 10^{36}/(4\pi \times 2.0 \times 10^{-15})} = \sqrt{4.0 \times 10^{49}} = 6.3 \times 10^{24}\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="In megaparsecs and light-years">
$6.3 \times 10^{24}/3.09 \times 10^{22} = 204\ \text{Mpc}$, or $6.3 \times 10^{24}/9.46 \times 10^{15} = 6.7 \times 10^{8}$ light-years: the light left the galaxy about 670 million years ago.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The top rung: redshift

Lesson 5.13 showed that the light of distant galaxies is redshifted, and that the redshift grows with distance. Once the supernovae have given the distances of many galaxies, the link between redshift and distance can be measured. After that, the redshift of a galaxy, which is easy to measure from its spectrum, is a distance indicator in its own right, out to the most distant galaxies we can see. Comparing distance and redshift also gives the expansion rate of the universe. That is the subject of the next lesson.

At these distances there is a catch. While the light travels, the universe expands, so "distance" no longer has a single meaning. The university course takes that up (see "Where this leads").

## Summary

- Distances in astronomy are found with a **distance ladder**: each method covers a range of distances and is calibrated by the one below.
- **Radar**: $d = ct/2$ from the time of an echo. It measured the distance to Venus and fixed the astronomical unit, $1\ \text{AU} = 1.496 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$.
- **Parallax**: a nearby star seems to shift as the Earth orbits the Sun. With the parallax $p$ in arcseconds, $d\ (\text{pc}) = 1/p$; one parsec is $3.09 \times 10^{16}\ \text{m}$, or 3.26 light-years.
- **Standard candles**: if the luminosity $L$ is known, the measured flux gives the distance through $F = L/(4\pi d^2)$.
- **Cepheid variables** pulse with a period that tells their luminosity (Leavitt, 1912); they reach nearby galaxies.
- **Type Ia supernovae**, exploding white dwarfs with nearly the same peak luminosity, reach billions of light-years.
- At the top, the **redshift** of galaxies, calibrated with these candles, measures the largest distances and the expansion of the universe.
