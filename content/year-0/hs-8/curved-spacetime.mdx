Block 2 gave a law of gravity that has worked for more than three centuries: every two masses attract with $F = Gm_1m_2/r^2$. Special relativity (lessons 8.1 and 8.2) then changed our ideas of space and time, but it said nothing about gravity. This lesson shows how Einstein brought the two together. The idea at the heart of it is so simple that you have already met it, in a lift.

## Newton's gravity in one minute

Newton's law gives the force of a body of mass $M$ on a mass $m$ at distance $r$. Put it into Newton's second law and the mass of the falling body drops out (MIT 8.033, slide 16):

<Formula label="Acceleration in a gravitational field" tex="a = \frac{G M}{r^2}" cite="mit" at="slide 16 (F = ma combined with F = GmM/r²)" />

A feather and a hammer fall together, as long as the air does not get in the way. That only works because the $m$ in $F = ma$ (how hard a body is to speed up) is the same as the $m$ in Newton's law of gravity (how strongly gravity pulls on it). Nothing in Newton's theory says the two must be equal. Galileo found it roughly, and Eötvös in 1890 and many later experimenters found with high precision that the acceleration depends neither on the mass of a body nor on what it is made of (slide 17). By 2000 the tests reached a few parts in $10^{13}$ (slide 18). Einstein took this as a clue: if every body falls in exactly the same way, the fall may have nothing to do with the body and everything to do with space and time themselves.

## The equivalence principle

In lesson 2.5 you met apparent weight in a lift: a scale reads more while the lift speeds up going upwards, and nothing at all if the cable snaps and the lift falls freely. Now push this further.

- **A rocket in empty space.** Far from every planet, a rocket accelerates at $9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$. You stand on its floor and feel your normal weight. A ball you let go of "falls" to the floor at $9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$: really the floor is speeding up towards the ball.
- **A falling lift.** On Earth, a lift falls freely. You, the lift and a ball you let go of all fall together, so the ball floats in front of you. Astronauts in the International Space Station float for the same reason: the station and everything in it are falling round the Earth together (lesson 2.9).

Einstein's step, from 1911, was to say that these are not just look-alikes. The **equivalence principle**: no experiment in a small closed laboratory can tell a uniform gravitational field from an acceleration of the same size (slide 13). The stronger form adds that inside a freely falling lab, gravity is gone and all the laws of special relativity hold, wherever the lab is. Two conditions matter: the lab must be small and the experiment short. In a very tall falling lift you could notice that the bottom falls a little faster than the top, because gravity weakens with height (lesson 2.11). That leftover effect is real gravity that no change of frame can remove.

## Light bends in gravity

Send a flash of light straight across an accelerating rocket. While the light crosses, the rocket speeds up, so the light reaches the far wall a little lower than where it started: inside the rocket, its path is a curve bending towards the floor. By the equivalence principle the same must happen in a lab standing on the Earth. **Gravity bends light** (slides 19 and 20; the rocket reasoning is the page's own).

<WorkedExample title="Light across a lift">
<Problem>
A lift is $3.0\ \text{m}$ wide. How far does a light beam crossing it from wall to wall drop on the way, in a lift standing on the Earth?
</Problem>
<Step title="Time to cross">
$t = 3.0/(3.00 \times 10^8) = 1.0 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The drop">
In an accelerating rocket the floor gains $\tfrac12 g t^2$ on the light, so the light drops $\tfrac12 \times 9.81 \times (1.0 \times 10^{-8})^2 = 4.9 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{m}$. By the equivalence principle that is also the drop on Earth.
</Step>
<Step title="What it means">
An atom is about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ across (lesson 7.1), so the drop is some $200\,000$ times smaller than an atom: far too small to see in a lift. To see light bend you need a much bigger mass and a much longer path, such as starlight passing the Sun.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Clocks run slower lower down

The same rocket shows something about time. Let a lamp on the floor send light up to a detector on the ceiling, a height $h$ higher. The light needs a time of about $h/c$ to get there. In that time the rocket, and so the detector, has gained a speed $v = g h/c$ compared with the moment the light left. The detector is moving away from where the light came from, so it sees the light redshifted, as in lesson 5.13: $z \approx v/c$. By the equivalence principle, light climbing a height $h$ in a gravitational field is redshifted in the same way:

<Formula label="Gravitational redshift over a height h" tex="z \approx \frac{g h}{c^2}" cite="mit" at="slides 21–22 (gravitational redshift); the rocket reasoning is worked out on this page" />

Think of the wave crests as the ticks of a clock on the floor. The detector on the ceiling receives them further apart than they were sent, so the floor clock seems to tick slowly. This is not a trick of the signal: the top clock really records more time. A clock that sits a height $h$ higher gains on the lower one, over a time $t$,

<Formula label="Time gained by the higher clock" tex="\Delta t \approx \frac{g h}{c^2}\,t" cite="mit" at="slide 30 (Newtonian metric: a clock lower in the gravitational potential runs slow; NASA's GPS presentation says the same on its slide 27); the form gh/c² for a small height is worked out on this page" />

This is **gravitational time dilation**: clocks run slower lower down in a gravitational field. Pound and Rebka measured the redshift in 1960 by sending gamma rays up and down a tower at Harvard (slide 22). Unlike the time dilation of lesson 8.2 this effect is not symmetric: the two clocks agree that the lower one runs slow.

<WorkedExample title="A clock at the top of a skyscraper">
<Problem>
An atomic clock is placed at the top of a skyscraper, $500\ \text{m}$ above an identical clock at the foot. How much faster does it run, and how much does it gain in a year of $3.156 \times 10^7\ \text{s}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="The fraction">
$gh/c^2 = 9.81 \times 500/(3.00 \times 10^8)^2 = 5.45 \times 10^{-14}$.
</Step>
<Step title="One year">
$\Delta t = 5.45 \times 10^{-14} \times 3.156 \times 10^7 = 1.7 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{s}$: the top clock is about $1.7$ microseconds ahead after a year. Tiny, but GPS needs clocks that agree to far better than a microsecond, as the next section shows.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Far from the ground: GPS clocks

For a satellite the formula $gh/c^2$ is not enough, because $g$ weakens with height. In lesson 3.11 you found the work needed to lift a mass $m$ from the surface (distance $R$ from the centre) to a distance $r$: $W = GMm(1/R - 1/r)$. The rate of a clock depends on that same quantity: the work per kilogram, divided by $c^2$. That is what the "Newtonian metric" of the university course says (slide 30, with the potential of slide 12):

<Formula label="Clock rates at two distances from a planet" tex="\frac{\Delta t}{t} \approx \frac{G M}{c^2}\left(\frac{1}{R} - \frac{1}{r}\right)" cite="mit" at="slide 30 (Newtonian metric) with φ = −GM/r from slide 12; the form for slow clocks and weak gravity is worked out on this page" />

Close to the surface, with $r = R + h$ and $h$ small, the bracket is almost $h/R^2$, and since $GM/R^2 = g$ this is $gh/c^2$ again.

<WorkedExample title="How much do GPS clocks gain from gravity?">
<Problem>
GPS satellites orbit about $20\,200\ \text{km}$ up. Use $GM = 3.986 \times 10^{14}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$ for the Earth (NASA's Earth Fact Sheet) and $R = 6371\ \text{km}$. How much does a satellite clock gain on a ground clock per day because of gravity?
</Problem>
<Step title="The two distances">
$R = 6.371 \times 10^6\ \text{m}$ and $r = 6371 + 20\,200 = 26\,571\ \text{km} = 2.6571 \times 10^7\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The fraction">
$GM/c^2 = 3.986 \times 10^{14}/(9.00 \times 10^{16}) = 4.43 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}$, and $1/R - 1/r = 1.193 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{-1}$, so $\Delta t/t = 5.28 \times 10^{-10}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Per day">
$5.28 \times 10^{-10} \times 86\,400 = 4.6 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{s}$: about $46$ microseconds a day ahead. (With $gh/c^2$ and $g$ = 9.81 m/s² all the way up you would get about $190$ microseconds, four times too much.)
</Step>
<Step title="Together with the speed effect">
Lesson 8.2 found that the satellite's speed makes its clock lose about $7$ microseconds a day. With the unrounded values the net gain is $45.7 - 7.2 = 38.5$ microseconds a day. A 2012 NASA Goddard presentation on GPS gives about $38$ microseconds a day, close to our rough estimate (which ignores, for example, the spin of the Earth). Uncorrected, that would make the measured distance to each satellite wrong by about $11\ \text{km}$ after one day.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Spacetime is curved

Special relativity already joined space and time into one four-dimensional whole, **spacetime**: three directions of space and one of time, in which every event has a place and a moment (slide 11). Galileo and Eötvös showed that every body falls in the same way, whatever it is made of. For Einstein this meant that falling is a property of space and time, not of the falling thing: once you know where a freely falling body is and how fast it moves, its path is fixed (slide 17). The MIT slides sum up general relativity in one line (slide 11): matter curves spacetime, and a body moving as straight as it can through curved spacetime seems to us to be pulled off course. That apparent pull is what we call gravity. A planet orbiting the Sun is not being tugged by a force; it is following the straightest possible path through spacetime that the Sun has curved.

The common picture is a **rubber sheet**: a heavy ball (the Sun) makes a dip in a stretched sheet, and a marble rolled past it curves round the dip. It helps, but it has limits:

- It explains gravity with gravity: the marble rolls into the dip only because the Earth pulls it down.
- It shows two curved dimensions of space, while real spacetime has three of space and one of time.
- Most importantly, for slow objects near a planet the main effect is the curving of **time**, which a sheet cannot show. The Newtonian metric on slide 30 changes only the time part of spacetime, and the different clock rates of the previous section are exactly that. A falling apple "follows" the slower clocks downwards.

## Gravitational lensing and the 1919 eclipse

If gravity bends light, a star seen close to the Sun should appear slightly shifted from its usual place. Normally the Sun's glare hides such stars, but not during a total eclipse. The British astronomers Arthur Eddington, Frank Dyson and Andrew Crommelin tested this during the total solar eclipse of 29 May 1919, with two expeditions, to Príncipe off West Africa and to Sobral in Brazil (NASA, "Hubble Gravitational Lenses"). They compared photographs of the stars near the eclipsed Sun with photographs of the same stars taken at night, and found that the starlight had been bent. Some astronomers doubted whether the measurements were precise enough, but later observations confirmed the bending.

Much bigger masses bend light much more. A galaxy or a cluster of galaxies acts as a **gravitational lens**: it bends the light of more distant objects behind it into arcs, into rings or into several images of the same object. The first double image of a quasar, the very bright centre of a distant galaxy, was seen in 1979 (NASA, "10 Things Einstein Got Right"). Lensing also magnifies: behind the cluster Abell 370, Hubble sees galaxies too faint to see without the lens, and how the background images are distorted shows where the cluster's mass is, most of it invisible **dark matter** (NASA's Hubble page; dark matter returns at the end of this block). Gravity even delays light: radio signals from spacecraft passing behind the Sun take longer to arrive, as Mariner, Viking and Cassini measured (NASA, "10 Things").

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Lenses as telescopes">
A massive galaxy cluster works as a natural telescope that magnifies the galaxies behind it. Astronomers use these lenses to see some of the faintest and most distant galaxies known, and to weigh clusters, dark matter included, from the way they bend light.
</Callout>

## Ripples in spacetime

If spacetime can curve, it can also vibrate (slides 7 and 8). Masses that move violently, such as two black holes spiralling into each other, send out **gravitational waves**: ripples in which space is stretched one way and squeezed the other way, travelling at the speed of light. They are a prediction of Einstein's general theory of relativity of 1915, but they are so weak that it took a century to detect them (NASA JPL, 2016). On 14 September 2015 both detectors of LIGO in the United States caught such a wave, from two black holes of about 29 and 36 times the mass of the Sun that had merged about 1.3 billion years earlier (NASA JPL, 2016). The 4-km arms of the detectors changed in length by far less than the width of a proton. Black holes are the next lesson. In the same spirit, NASA's satellite Gravity Probe B (launched 2004) showed that the spinning Earth drags spacetime round with it (NASA, "10 Things").

## Summary

- All bodies fall with the same acceleration $a = GM/r^2$, whatever their mass or material; Einstein took this as a sign that gravity belongs to space and time.
- **Equivalence principle:** no experiment in a small closed lab can tell gravity from an equal acceleration; in a freely falling lab gravity disappears and special relativity holds.
- It follows that light bends in gravity, and that clocks lower down run slow: over a height $h$ the higher clock runs faster by the fraction $gh/c^2$, and light climbing $h$ is redshifted by $z \approx gh/c^2$.
- Far from the ground use $\Delta t/t \approx (GM/c^2)(1/R - 1/r)$: GPS clocks gain about $46$ μs a day from gravity and lose about $7$ μs from speed.
- Gravity is curved spacetime: bodies move as straight as they can through it. The rubber sheet helps, but it borrows gravity and shows no curving of time.
- Gravitational lensing, first tested on starlight passing the Sun in the 1919 eclipse, bends, multiplies and magnifies distant light; gravitational waves, ripples of spacetime, were first detected in 2015.
