Throw a ball forwards from a moving bicycle and it flies off faster than if you had stood still: the bicycle's speed is added to the throw. Now switch on the bicycle's headlight. Does its light also leave at the speed of light plus the speed of the bicycle? Common sense says yes. Experiment says no. This lesson follows the puzzle that led Albert Einstein to a new theory of space and time, **special relativity**, and ends with its first surprise: whether two things happen "at the same time" depends on who is watching.

## Velocities add in everyday life

Lesson 1.9 showed that every velocity is measured relative to a frame of reference, and that velocities chain together. Seen from the road, a passenger who walks forwards through a train moves at her walking speed plus the speed of the train. In the language of the relativity course: if an object moves at velocity $u$ in one frame, then in a second frame that moves at velocity $v$ along the same line it moves at

<Formula label="Changing frames the everyday (Galilean) way" tex="u' = u - v" cite="reading1" at="slide 12" />

Here $u'$ is the velocity measured in the second frame. This is called the **Galilean** rule, after Galileo's idea (lesson 1.9) that physics looks the same in every frame moving at constant velocity. It works perfectly for trains, planes and planets. The same course slide then asks the question of this lesson: what about the light from a torch on a train?

## Inertial frames

The frames in which Galileo's idea holds have a name. An **inertial frame** is a frame of reference that is not accelerating: in it, Newton's first law (lesson 2.2) holds, so an object with no resultant force on it stays at rest or keeps moving at constant velocity (reading 1, slide 10). The ground is (very nearly) an inertial frame; so is a train moving smoothly along a straight track at constant speed, or a spaceship coasting with its engines off. A train that brakes, or a car taking a bend, is not: there, objects slide about although nothing pushes them.

Any frame that moves at constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is itself inertial. There is no single "true" one: from inside a closed, smoothly moving cabin, no mechanics experiment tells you whether you move.

## Light names no frame

Lesson 6.12 found the speed of an electromagnetic wave from two constants measured on a lab bench, the magnetic constant $\mu_0$ and the electric constant $\varepsilon_0$:

<Formula label="The speed of light from electricity and magnetism" tex="c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}}" cite="mit802" at="Sec. 13.4, Eq. (13.4.15), p. 9" />

This gives $c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s. Look at what the formula does **not** contain: any speed of the lab, of the source or of the observer. With the rule $u' = u - v$, light could only have the speed $c$ in one special frame, and would move at $c - v$ or $c + v$ in others. Relative to what, then, does light travel at $c$?

## The ether

Every wave in the earlier blocks travels through something: sound through air, ripples through water, a pulse along a rope. Its speed is measured relative to that medium (lesson 5.3). So nineteenth-century physicists assumed that light, too, is a wave in a medium, which they called the **ether** (or aether): something invisible that fills all of space, even the vacuum between the stars. The speed $c$ would be the speed of light relative to the ether, and the ether would be the one frame truly at rest.

Earth moves round the Sun at about 30 km/s, so it should rush through the ether, with an "ether wind" blowing past the lab. Light sent along the wind and back should take a different time from light sent across it. That difference was what Albert Michelson and Edward Morley set out to measure (source, slides 15–17).

## A race across a river

The idea is easiest to see with a boat on a river, where velocities really do add.

<WorkedExample title="Two rowers, one river">
<Problem>
A river flows at $0.50$ m/s. Two rowers who both row at $1.30$ m/s relative to the water race over the same distance: $60$ m and back. Rower A goes $60$ m upstream and returns; rower B crosses to the other bank, $60$ m away, and returns. Who wins?
</Problem>
<Step title="Along the current">
Upstream, A's speed relative to the bank is $1.30 - 0.50 = 0.80$ m/s, which takes $60/0.80 = 75.0$ s. Back downstream it is $1.30 + 0.50 = 1.80$ m/s, which takes $60/1.80 = 33.3$ s. Altogether $108.3$ s: the slow leg costs more time than the fast leg saves.
</Step>
<Step title="Across the current">
To go straight across, B must aim partly upstream, as the plane in a crosswind did in lesson 1.9. The part of B's velocity that goes across is $\sqrt{1.30^2 - 0.50^2} = \sqrt{1.44} = 1.20$ m/s, on the way there and on the way back. The round trip of $120$ m takes $120/1.20 = 100$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Result">
B wins by about $8.3$ s. With a current, a trip along it and back always takes longer than a trip across it and back; without a current the two would tie.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Replace the rowers by light, at speed $c$, and the river by the ether wind, at speed $v$. For two arms of lengths $L_\parallel$ (along the wind) and $L_\perp$ (across it), the same reasoning gives the round-trip times

<Formula label="Round trips through an ether wind" tex="t_\parallel = \frac{L_\parallel}{c - v} + \frac{L_\parallel}{c + v}, \qquad t_\perp = \frac{2L_\perp}{\sqrt{c^2 - v^2}}" cite="mit" at="slide 16 (the slide writes t⊥ with the factor γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²))" />

## The Michelson–Morley experiment

Michelson's **interferometer** splits one beam of light in two. One half travels along one arm, the other half along an arm at right angles; mirrors send both back, and they meet again and interfere (lesson 5.11). Where the two waves arrive in step the light is bright, where they arrive out of step it is dark: you see a pattern of bright and dark **fringes**. If one beam is delayed by a whole period of the light wave, the pattern looks the same; a delay of a fraction of a period moves the fringes by that fraction.

You cannot know in advance which way the ether wind blows, so they turned the whole apparatus through 90°, which swaps the roles of the two arms. Because $v$ is tiny compared with $c$, the change in the time difference works out to be, to a very good approximation (source, slide 17),

<Formula label="Expected change on turning the interferometer" tex="\Delta t \approx \frac{L_\parallel + L_\perp}{c}\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2" cite="mit" at="slide 17 (written there as Δt′ − Δt)" />

Dividing by the period of the light, $\lambda/c$, gives the expected shift of the fringes, as a fraction of one fringe:

<Formula label="Expected fringe shift" tex="N \approx \frac{L_\parallel + L_\perp}{\lambda}\left(\frac{v}{c}\right)^2" cite="mit" at="slide 17 (the arm length counted in wavelengths, times (v/c)²)" />

The factor $(v/c)^2$ is very small, but the arms are millions of wavelengths long, and the two together give a shift you can see.

<WorkedExample title="How big is the effect?">
<Problem>
A student builds an interferometer with two arms of $2.0$ m each and green light of wavelength $550$ nm. If the lab moved through an ether at Earth's orbital speed, $30$ km/s, what change in time difference and what fringe shift would turning it through 90° give?
</Problem>
<Step title="The ratio v/c">
$v/c = 3.0 \times 10^{4} / 3.00 \times 10^{8} = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}$, so $(v/c)^2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-8}$: one part in a hundred million.
</Step>
<Step title="The time difference">
$\Delta t \approx (4.0 / 3.00 \times 10^{8}) \times 1.0 \times 10^{-8} = 1.3 \times 10^{-16}$ s. No stopwatch can measure that.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare with the light wave">
One period of the green light lasts $\lambda/c = 550 \times 10^{-9} / 3.00 \times 10^{8} = 1.8 \times 10^{-15}$ s. The delay is $1.3 \times 10^{-16} / 1.8 \times 10^{-15} \approx 0.07$ of a period, so the fringes would move by about $0.07$ of a fringe. The second formula gives the same: $N \approx (4.0 / 550 \times 10^{-9}) \times 10^{-8} = 0.07$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

By the course slide's count, Michelson and Morley had 11 m of light path in all, both arms together, roughly $2 \times 10^{7}$ wavelengths. By the same count they expected a shift of about $0.2$ of a fringe, and they could see shifts as small as $0.01$ (source, slide 17).

They saw **no shift**, apart from tiny amounts within their measuring error, whichever way the apparatus pointed. Turning the apparatus did not change the difference between the travel times along the two arms: the speed of light did not depend on how the lab moved (source, slide 17). Other tests agreed. Light from double stars, which move towards us and away from us as they orbit, arrives in good order, so the speed of light does not depend on the motion of the source either (source, slide 14). Physicists tried to rescue the ether, for example by supposing that Earth drags its ether along, but other observations and experiments ruled out each of these rescues (source, slide 20).

## Einstein's two postulates

Einstein turned the problem around. Instead of explaining away the result, he took it as a law of nature and built physics on two starting rules, or **postulates**, in his 1905 paper (reading 2, slides 4 and 16):

1. **The principle of relativity:** the laws of physics are the same in every inertial frame. This is Galileo's idea, now for all of physics, light and electricity included.
2. **The constancy of the speed of light:** the speed of light in vacuum has the same value, $c$, in every inertial frame, whatever the motion of the source or of the observer.

The second really follows from the first, if the speed of light counts as a law of physics (reading 2, slide 4): Maxwell's $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$ is built from constants that are the same in every lab, so every inertial observer must find the same $c$. There is then no need for an ether, and no frame is special.

Both postulates are simple; together they are startling. A spaceship racing towards a star at half the speed of light still measures the starlight passing it at exactly $c$, not $1.5c$. So the rule $u' = u - v$ cannot be exactly right. It is a very good approximation at everyday speeds, but something about our ideas of time and distance must change. "Special" refers to the inertial frames: the later "general" relativity also covers accelerating frames and gravity (reading 2, slide 4).

## Simultaneity is relative

The first thing that has to go is the idea that "at the same time" means the same for everyone (reading 2, slide 5). Here is a thought experiment.

A train carriage moves smoothly along a platform. A lamp hangs in the exact middle of the carriage, and it flashes once. Light from the flash runs towards the front door and towards the back door.

- **For a passenger in the carriage**, the carriage is at rest. The lamp is equally far from both doors and the light moves at $c$ both ways, so it reaches the front and back doors **at the same moment**.
- **For someone on the platform**, the light also moves at $c$ in both directions: that is the second postulate, and it does not matter that the lamp is on a moving train. But while the light is under way, the back door moves forwards to meet it and the front door moves away from it. The light has a shorter way to go to the back door, so it reaches the back door **first**.

Both observers are right. Two events at different places (light reaching the front door, light reaching the back door) that happen at the same time in one inertial frame happen at different times in another frame moving relative to it. This is the **relativity of simultaneity** (reading 3, slide 9). With the everyday rule the platform observer would have said the light moves at $c + v$ forwards and $c - v$ backwards, and the two arrivals would again have coincided; it is the constant speed of light that breaks the tie.

Notice what is not relative: everyone agrees that the flash came before both arrivals. Only the timing of separate events far apart depends on the frame. At everyday speeds the difference is far too small to notice, which is why nobody suspected it. The next lesson shows how the same reasoning makes moving clocks run slow and moving objects shorter.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Because light has the same speed for every observer, astronomers can turn light travel times into distances without asking how fast Earth or a distant galaxy is moving: light from a galaxy a million light-years away has been on its way for a million years, whatever its source was doing. And the ether is gone for good: the universe has no medium at rest that all motion is measured against. Both ideas return when Block 8 measures the distances and speeds of galaxies.
</Callout>

## Summary

- At everyday speeds velocities add (the Galilean rule $u' = u - v$), and the laws of mechanics are the same in every **inertial frame**: a frame that does not accelerate, in which Newton's first law holds.
- Maxwell's speed of light, $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 3.00 \times 10^8$ m/s, names no frame. Physicists thought light travelled through an **ether**, the one frame at rest.
- Michelson and Morley's interferometer should have shown Earth's motion through the ether as a fringe shift of about $0.2$ when turned (by the course slide's numbers); it showed none.
- **Einstein's postulates (1905):** the laws of physics are the same in every inertial frame, and the speed of light in vacuum is the same, $c$, in every inertial frame.
- **Simultaneity is relative:** events at different places that happen at the same time for one observer can happen at different times for another observer moving relative to the first.
