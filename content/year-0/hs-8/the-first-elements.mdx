Lesson 8.13 traced the universe back to a hot, dense beginning, and lesson 8.5 showed how stars make helium out of hydrogen. This lesson combines the two. For a few minutes after the Big Bang, the whole universe was hotter than the centre of the Sun, and it was a nuclear reactor. What did it make? Following the reasoning of the MIT course 8.902 (Lecture 20), we can work out the answer with the physics of lessons 7.7 to 8.5, and then compare it with what astronomers measure.

## A reactor the size of the universe

Run the expansion backwards and the universe gets hotter and denser. One second after the start its temperature was about $10^{10}$ K (PDG review, Sec. 24.2). Atoms, and even nuclei, could not exist: the universe was a hot soup of free protons, free neutrons, electrons, positrons, neutrinos and a huge number of photons. Over the next few minutes, protons and neutrons fused into the lightest nuclei. The process is called **Big Bang nucleosynthesis** (8.902 notes, p. 109).

It differs from the fusion in a star (lesson 8.5) in three ways:

- **Neutrons were available.** A star has to turn protons into neutrons one by one, the slow first step of the proton–proton chain. The early universe already had free neutrons.
- **The clock was running.** The universe expanded and cooled all the time. The reactor was hot enough for fusion for only a few minutes, and by the time the first nuclei formed, its density had dropped to about that of air (PDG review, Sec. 24.2).
- **Free neutrons do not last.** A neutron on its own decays, so the neutrons had to be locked into nuclei before too many were lost.

To find out what came out of the reactor, we need two numbers: how many neutrons there were for every proton, and when the fusion could start.

## Neutrons and protons

At the very high temperatures of the first second, collisions with electrons, positrons and neutrinos kept turning protons into neutrons and neutrons back into protons (8.902 notes, eqs. (477)–(479)). These changes are driven by the weak force of lesson 8.7. But the two particles are not equal: the neutron is a little heavier.

<Formula label="Extra rest energy of a neutron" tex="\Delta E = (m_n - m_p)\,c^2 \approx 1.29\ \text{MeV}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (481), p. 110 (the 1.29 MeV in the neutron-to-proton ratio); the masses are written out by the page" />

<WorkedExample title="How much more is a neutron?">
<Problem>
A neutron has a mass of $1.00866\ \text{u}$ and a proton $1.00728\ \text{u}$ (NIST values, rounded). Find the extra rest energy of the neutron in MeV and in joules, and the temperature at which $k_B T$, the typical energy per particle in a hot gas, is as large ($k_B = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$, lesson 8.4).
</Problem>
<Step title="Mass difference">
$1.00866 - 1.00728 = 0.00138\ \text{u}$, and with $1\ \text{u} = 931.5\ \text{MeV}$ (lesson 8.4): $0.00138 \times 931.5 = 1.29\ \text{MeV}$.
</Step>
<Step title="In joules">
$1.29 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-13} = 2.06 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Matching temperature">
$T = 2.06 \times 10^{-13}/(1.38 \times 10^{-23}) = 1.5 \times 10^{10}\ \text{K}$: fifteen billion kelvin.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Turning a proton into a neutron costs those 1.29 MeV. While the universe was far hotter than $1.5 \times 10^{10}$ K, collisions supplied that energy easily, and there were almost as many neutrons as protons. As it cooled, making a neutron got harder than unmaking one, and the balance shifted towards protons (8.902 notes, p. 110).

The weak reactions also got slower as the universe thinned out. At about one second, near $10^{10}$ K, they stopped altogether: the ratio **froze out**. At that moment there was about **one neutron for every 6 protons** (PDG review, Sec. 24.2; the simpler estimate in the 8.902 notes gives 1 to 5).

## Free neutrons decay

After freeze-out, nothing turned protons back into neutrons, but the neutrons themselves were unstable. Lesson 7.8 met the decay of a free neutron:

<Formula label="Decay of a free neutron" tex="{}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} \;\to\; {}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{p} + {}^{\;\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + \bar\nu" cite="reading1" at="p. 110 (neutrons β-decay before they can fuse into deuterium); the decay equation is written out as in lesson 7.8" />

A free neutron has a mean life of $878.4\ \text{s}$ (PDG neutron listing), so its half-life is $878.4 \times \ln 2 = 609\ \text{s}$: **about 10 minutes**. Lesson 7.9's decay law tells us how many are left after a time $t$:

<Formula label="Decay law" tex="N = N_0\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/t_{1/2}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (491), p. 111 (the neutrons lost by decay in the first 200 s); the decay law itself is that of lesson 7.9" />

<WorkedExample title="Three minutes of decay">
<Problem>
At freeze-out a sample of the early universe holds 700 nucleons: 100 neutrons and 600 protons, the 1-to-6 ratio. How many neutrons are left 3 minutes later, and what is the ratio of neutrons to protons then? Use $t_{1/2} = 609\ \text{s}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Fraction left">
$t/t_{1/2} = 180/609 = 0.296$, and $\left(\tfrac12\right)^{0.296} = 0.815$.
</Step>
<Step title="Neutrons and protons">
$N = 100 \times 0.815 = 81.5$ neutrons. The other $18.5$ have become protons: $600 + 18.5 = 618.5$ protons. The total is still 700.
</Step>
<Step title="Ratio">
$81.5/618.5 = 0.132$, about 1 neutron for every 7.6 protons.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

This simple estimate treats freeze-out as one sudden moment, which it is not. Detailed calculations give **about one neutron for every 7 protons** by the time the nuclear reactions began (PDG review, Sec. 24.2). Either way, the waiting cost a fair share of the neutrons. Why did the universe have to wait at all?

## The deuterium bottleneck

Every heavier nucleus has to be built step by step, and the first step is joining a proton and a neutron into a deuterium nucleus (lesson 7.7: hydrogen-2, written D):

<Formula label="Forming deuterium" tex="{}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{p} + {}^{1}_{0}\mathrm{n} \;\rightleftharpoons\; {}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H} + \gamma" cite="reading1" at="eq. (482), p. 110" />

The double arrow means the reaction runs both ways. Forming deuterium releases its binding energy, $2.22\ \text{MeV}$, as a gamma ray (lesson 8.4 found 1.11 MeV per nucleon, for 2 nucleons). But a photon with at least 2.22 MeV can also hit a deuterium nucleus and break it apart again. Deuterium is by far the most loosely bound of the light nuclei, so it is the weak link.

<WorkedExample title="How strong were the photons?">
<Problem>
Use Wien's law of lesson 7.2, $\lambda_{\max} T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}$, and $E = hc/\lambda$ to find the energy of a photon at the peak of the spectrum when the universe was at $2 \times 10^{9}$ K. At what temperature would a peak photon carry exactly the 2.22 MeV needed to break up deuterium?
</Problem>
<Step title="Peak wavelength">
$\lambda_{\max} = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}/(2 \times 10^{9}) = 1.45 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{m}$, smaller than an atom: gamma rays.
</Step>
<Step title="Peak photon energy">
$E = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}/(1.45 \times 10^{-12}) = 1.37 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$, which is $1.37 \times 10^{-13}/(1.6 \times 10^{-13}) = 0.86\ \text{MeV}$: well below 2.22 MeV.
</Step>
<Step title="Temperature for 2.22 MeV">
$E = 2.22 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-13} = 3.55 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$ belongs to $\lambda = hc/E = 5.6 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{m}$, and Wien's law gives $T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}/(5.6 \times 10^{-13}) = 5.2 \times 10^{9}\ \text{K}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

So from about $5 \times 10^9$ K downwards, a typical photon was too weak to split deuterium. Yet deuterium still could not survive (8.902 notes, p. 110). The reason is the sheer number of photons: for every proton or neutron there were more than a billion of them (the measured ratio, in the PDG review, is one nucleon per $1.7 \times 10^9$ photons). A black-body spectrum has a long tail of photons with far more than the peak energy (lesson 7.2). Only a tiny fraction of all photons are in that tail, but a tiny fraction of more than a billion per nucleon is still enough to break up every deuterium nucleus as soon as it forms.

Deuterium could only build up once the universe had cooled to about $10^{9}$ K (PDG review, Sec. 24.2; the 8.902 notes find $8 \times 10^{8}$ K, eq. (490)), about **three minutes** after the start (the 8.902 notes give 200 s). This wait is called the **deuterium bottleneck**, and during it the neutrons kept decaying.

## Building helium-4

Once deuterium survived, the next steps went quickly (8.902 notes, p. 110). A deuterium nucleus captures a proton to make helium-3, and helium-3 meets another deuterium nucleus to make helium-4 (PDG review, Sec. 24.2):

<Formula label="From deuterium to helium-3" tex="{}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H} + {}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{p} \to {}^{3}_{2}\mathrm{He} + \gamma" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 24.2, p. 1: D(p,γ)³He" />

<Formula label="From helium-3 to helium-4" tex="{}^{3}_{2}\mathrm{He} + {}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H} \to {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} + {}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{p}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 24.2, p. 1: ³He(D,p)⁴He" />

Check the counting rule of lesson 7.8: in the second reaction the nucleons give $3 + 2 = 4 + 1$ and the charges $2 + 1 = 2 + 1$. Helium-4 is the most tightly bound of all the light nuclei (lesson 8.4, about 7.1 MeV per nucleon), so once the chain gets going, almost every neutron ends up there and stays (PDG review, Sec. 24.2). The protons left over, with no neutron to pair with, stay single: they become the nuclei of hydrogen atoms.

## How much helium?

Astronomers describe the result by the **helium mass fraction** $Y$: the fraction of the mass of ordinary matter that is helium. Every helium-4 nucleus holds 2 neutrons and 2 protons, so each neutron takes one proton with it into helium. If there are $n$ neutrons for every $p$ protons, the helium holds $2n$ nucleons out of $n + p$, and since nearly all the mass of an atom is in its nucleons (lesson 7.7):

<Formula label="Helium mass fraction" tex="Y = \frac{2n}{n + p} = \frac{2\,(n/p)}{1 + n/p}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (492)–(493), pp. 111–112; also PDG review eq. (24.1)" />

In words, $Y$ is twice the fraction of all nucleons that are neutrons.

<WorkedExample title="A quarter helium">
<Problem>
(a) When the fusion starts there is one neutron for every 7 protons. Count what is made from a group of 16 nucleons, and check the answer with the formula. (b) Suppose instead the neutrons had not decayed at all during the wait, so the ratio was still 1 to 6. What would $Y$ be?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Counting">
16 nucleons in the ratio 1 to 7 are 2 neutrons and 14 protons. The 2 neutrons and 2 of the protons make one helium-4 nucleus; the other 12 protons stay as hydrogen. Helium has 4 of the 16 units of mass: $Y = 4/16 = 0.25$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Formula">
$Y = 2 \times \tfrac17/(1 + \tfrac17) = \tfrac27 \times \tfrac78 = 0.25$. A quarter of the mass is helium-4, three quarters hydrogen.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) No decay">
14 nucleons in the ratio 1 to 6 are 2 neutrons and 12 protons, giving one helium-4 nucleus and 10 hydrogen nuclei: $Y = 4/14 = 0.29$. The formula agrees: $2 \times \tfrac16/(1 + \tfrac16) = \tfrac27 = 0.29$. The neutrons lost during the bottleneck lower the helium from 29% to 25%.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The curve shows why the waiting matters: the fewer neutrons are left when deuterium can finally form, the less helium there is. If the bottleneck had lasted much longer than the neutron's half-life, there would be almost no helium at all.

## Traces, and why nothing heavier

Not quite every neutron reached helium-4. When the reactions died out, a little deuterium and helium-3 was left over, a few atoms for every $10^5$ hydrogen atoms, and a tiny amount of lithium-7, about one atom for every $10^{10}$ hydrogen atoms (PDG review, Sec. 24.2). That is all. Why no carbon, oxygen or iron?

- **A gap at 5 and at 8.** No stable nucleus has 5 or 8 nucleons. Helium-4 plus a proton or a neutron makes 5, and two helium-4 nuclei make 8: all of these fall apart at once (PDG review, Sec. 24.2). The road from helium to heavier nuclei is blocked.
- **The electric barrier.** Getting round the gap needs reactions between charged nuclei such as helium-3 and helium-4, and their electric repulsion (lesson 8.4) is larger than between two hydrogen nuclei. Only a few such reactions happened, which is where the lithium-7 comes from.
- **Falling temperature and density.** The universe kept expanding. Within minutes it was too cool and too thin for the slow reactions that could have bridged the gap.

Stars do get past the gap, but much later: a star's core stays hot and dense for millions or billions of years, long enough to fuse helium into carbon (lesson 8.5).

## Checking against the sky

The prediction is clear: about 25% helium by mass, a few parts in $10^5$ deuterium and helium-3, a trace of lithium. Astronomers can test it, but they must look at matter that stars have hardly changed, because stars have since made more helium and destroyed deuterium. Recent measurements, as summarised by the Particle Data Group in 2025 (later developments, not part of the MIT notes), give:

- **Helium-4:** $Y = 0.245$, from the glowing gas in small galaxies that contain very few heavy elements, so little of their gas has been through stars (PDG review, eq. (24.3)). That is about one helium atom for every 12 hydrogen atoms. The Sun, made later from recycled gas, has about 28% (lesson 8.5).
- **Deuterium:** $2.5 \times 10^{-5}$ deuterium atoms per hydrogen atom, measured in distant gas clouds that absorb the light of quasars behind them (PDG review, eq. (24.2)).
- **Lithium-7:** about $1.5 \times 10^{-10}$ per hydrogen atom in the oldest stars, about 3 times less than predicted ($4.7 \times 10^{-10}$). This "lithium problem" is still not solved (PDG review, eqs. (24.4) and (24.10)).

These amounts span nine orders of magnitude, from helium down to lithium, and except for lithium they fit the prediction with one single value of the density of ordinary matter (PDG review, Sec. 24.1). Few things show as directly that the universe really was hot and dense in its first minutes: a cold or always-thin universe would not have made a quarter of its mass into helium.

<Callout kind="note" title="Heavier elements came from stars">
Everything beyond lithium, the carbon in your body, the oxygen you breathe and the iron in your blood, was made much later inside stars (lesson 8.5) and spread through space when stars died. The first stars formed from gas that was only hydrogen and helium with a trace of lithium.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Weighing the universe with deuterium">
The leftover deuterium depends strongly on how many protons and neutrons there were for every photon: with more of them, more deuterium is burned on to helium. So measuring deuterium measures the amount of ordinary matter in the universe (8.902 notes, p. 112). The university lecture works this out with the Saha equation, and the answer agrees with an independent measurement from the cosmic microwave background, the subject of lesson 8.15.
</Callout>

## Summary

- For a few minutes after the Big Bang the whole universe was a nuclear reactor: at one second about $10^{10}$ K, and by the time the first nuclei formed as thin as air.
- A neutron has $1.29\ \text{MeV}$ more rest energy than a proton. As the universe cooled, neutrons became rarer; at about one second the weak reactions stopped, leaving about **one neutron for every 6 protons**.
- Free neutrons decay with a half-life of about 10 minutes, so the ratio fell to **about 1 to 7** before fusion could start.
- **The deuterium bottleneck:** deuterium (binding energy 2.22 MeV) was broken apart by the high-energy tail of more than a billion photons per nucleon until the universe cooled to about $10^9$ K, some three minutes after the start.
- Then almost every neutron went into helium-4: $Y = 2(n/p)/(1 + n/p) = 0.25$ for $n/p = 1/7$. Traces of deuterium and helium-3 (a few in $10^5$) and lithium-7 (about one in $10^{10}$) were left.
- Nothing heavier formed: there are no stable nuclei with 5 or 8 nucleons, and the universe cooled and thinned too fast to bridge the gap.
- Measurements agree ($Y = 0.245$, D/H $= 2.5 \times 10^{-5}$; lithium is about 3 times too rare, an open problem): strong evidence for a hot Big Bang. The heavier elements were made later, in stars.
