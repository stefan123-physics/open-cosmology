Lesson 3.11 asked how fast a stone must be thrown to escape from a planet. The heavier and smaller the planet, the higher that escape speed. Now push this to the limit: squeeze a mass into so small a ball that the escape speed reaches the speed of light. Then nothing can get out, not even light. Such an object is a **black hole**, and the universe is full of them.

## How a black hole forms

Lesson 8.5 followed a star to the end of its life. A star like the Sun ends as a white dwarf, held up against its own gravity by its tightly packed electrons (lesson 8.6). A much heavier star fuses its core all the way to iron, then runs out of fuel; its iron core collapses and the star explodes as a supernova (NASA, *Black Holes*).

What is left of the core depends on its mass. Stars of more than about 8 solar masses can end their lives this way; for stars just above that limit the core becomes a **neutron star**, a ball made almost entirely of neutrons, packed as tightly as the protons and neutrons in an atomic nucleus. Stars of about 20 solar masses or more leave a **stellar-mass black hole** of a few to perhaps hundreds of solar masses (NASA, *Black Hole Types*). The reason is that even neutrons can only resist so much: the 2006 slides of the MIT relativity course put the mass of a neutron star at most about 3 solar masses (Lecture 24, slide 30), an upper limit; later estimates are somewhat lower. A collapsing core heavier than that has nothing left that can stop it. It keeps shrinking, and its gravity at its surface keeps growing, until light itself cannot escape.

## Escape speed and the Schwarzschild radius

The first topic that slide 8 of the MIT lecture lists is the escape velocity (Lecture 23, slide 8). As in lesson 3.11, an object at distance $r$ from the centre of a mass $M$ just escapes when its kinetic energy $\tfrac12 mv^2$ equals the work $GMm/r$ needed to pull it out to infinity. The mass $m$ cancels:

<Formula label="Escape speed at distance r" tex="v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 G M}{r}}" cite="mit" at="slide 8 (escape velocity, the first topic listed); derived in lesson 3.11" />

The smaller $r$, the larger the escape speed. Set it equal to the speed of light, $v_{\text{esc}} = c$, and solve for $r$: $c^2 = 2GM/r$, so

<Formula label="Schwarzschild radius" tex="r_s = \frac{2 G M}{c^2}" cite="mit" at="slides 8 and 10–11 (escape velocity; a black hole is an object contained within its own event horizon); setting the escape speed equal to c is worked out on this page" />

This is the **Schwarzschild radius**, named after Karl Schwarzschild (1873 to 1916, whose portrait opens the MIT lecture on slide 1), who first worked out the exact shape of spacetime round a ball of mass in general relativity. A mass squeezed inside its own Schwarzschild radius is a black hole. The sphere of radius $r_s$ round it is the **event horizon**: nothing about what happens inside it can ever reach an observer outside (NASA, *Black Hole Anatomy*). A black hole is an object contained within its own event horizon (slide 11).

Our argument used Newton's gravity and treated light like a thrown stone, which is not really right: near a black hole gravity is far too strong for Newton, and light always moves at $c$. The correct calculation needs general relativity, and it is what the university course does. By a lucky coincidence it gives exactly the same radius, $2GM/c^2$. It also shows that the escape speed formula has a useful second form: since $2GM = r_s c^2$,

<WorkedExample title="A black hole of 10 solar masses">
<Problem>
A collapsing star leaves a black hole of 10 solar masses. How large is its event horizon? Use $M_\odot = 1.99 \times 10^{30}\ \text{kg}$, $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$ and $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The mass">
$M = 10 \times 1.99 \times 10^{30} = 1.99 \times 10^{31}\ \text{kg}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The radius">
$r_s = 2 \times 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.99 \times 10^{31}/(3.00 \times 10^8)^2 = 2.95 \times 10^4\ \text{m}$, or $29.5\ \text{km}$. Ten Suns' worth of matter inside a sphere about $60\ \text{km}$ across, the size of a large city.
</Step>
<Step title="A rule of thumb">
Since $r_s$ is proportional to $M$, every solar mass adds $2.95\ \text{km}$. A black hole of 1000 solar masses would have $r_s = 2950\ \text{km}$.
</Step>
<Step title="How dense?">
The average density inside the horizon is $M/(\tfrac43\pi r_s^3) = 1.99 \times 10^{31}/(\tfrac43\pi \times (2.95 \times 10^4)^3) = 1.9 \times 10^{17}\ \text{kg/m}^3$: about $2 \times 10^{14}$ times the density of water.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Near the horizon: time and light

The event horizon is not a surface you could stand on. It is a boundary in space: inside it, the speed needed to escape is larger than the speed of light (NASA, *Black Hole Anatomy*). General relativity predicts that everything inside falls on to a point of infinite density at the centre, the **singularity**. Nobody knows what really happens there: the prediction probably marks where general relativity itself stops working (NASA, *Black Hole Anatomy*).

Lesson 8.8 showed that clocks run slower lower down in gravity and that light climbing out is redshifted, by $gh/c^2$ near the Earth. Near a black hole these effects stop being tiny:

- A clock hovering just outside the horizon ticks very slowly compared with a clock far away. The closer it hovers, the slower it runs, and at the horizon itself it would seem to stop.
- Light sent out from near the horizon arrives far away strongly redshifted, and fainter. Light from the horizon itself never arrives at all.
- So a friend watching you fall in from far away (Lecture 24, slide 8) sees you slow down, turn red and fade, and never quite sees you cross the horizon. On your own watch, though, you cross it after a short time, and for a large black hole you would notice nothing special at the moment you cross (Lecture 23, slide 24). Both are true: they are times on different clocks, as in lesson 8.2.

Before you reach the horizon of a small black hole, however, its gravity pulls much harder on your feet than on your head. You are stretched along the direction of the fall and squeezed from the sides: astronomers call it spaghettification (NASA, *Black Holes*). Far from a black hole, on the other hand, nothing unusual happens: if the Sun were replaced by a black hole of the same mass, the planets would keep their orbits (NASA, *Black Holes*), only it would be very cold.

## Finding something invisible

A black hole gives out no light of its own, so astronomers find black holes through what they do to their surroundings (NASA, *Black Holes*).

**X-ray binaries.** Nearly all the stellar-mass black holes seen with telescopes were found because they orbit an ordinary star (NASA, *Black Hole Types*); gravitational waves, below, now find black holes too. Gas pulled off the star does not fall straight in but swirls round the black hole in a disk, the **accretion disk**. Rubbing within the disk makes the gas so hot that it shines in X-rays (lesson 5.8; NASA, *Black Hole Types*). The orbit of the visible star, as in lesson 2.13, weighs the invisible partner. If it is heavier than 3 solar masses and still invisible, it can be neither an ordinary star (which would shine) nor a neutron star. The 2006 MIT slides gave V404 Cygni as the best example, whose invisible partner had $12 \pm 2$ solar masses by the measurements of that time, and Cygnus X-1 (Lecture 24, slide 30). About 50 such black holes are known in our Galaxy (NASA, *Black Hole Types*).

**Gravitational waves.** When two black holes orbit each other closely, they spiral together and merge, and send out ripples in spacetime. Lesson 8.8 described the first such signal, caught by LIGO in 2015 from black holes of about 29 and 36 solar masses: the first direct sign of two black holes merging. As a later development, long after the 2006 MIT slides, the detectors have since heard many more such mergers; black holes grow both by swallowing matter and by merging with other black holes (NASA, *Black Hole Anatomy*).

**Stars orbiting the centre of the Galaxy.** This is the clearest case of all, and the next section works it out.

## Supermassive black holes

Almost every large galaxy has a black hole at its centre of hundreds of thousands to billions of solar masses: a **supermassive black hole** (NASA, *Black Hole Types*). The MIT slides list the evidence of 2006: very bright galaxy centres called quasars, with huge jets of gas, and the motions of stars and gas that point to central masses of $10^6$ to $10^9$ solar masses in a tiny space (Lecture 24, slides 11 and 13). The heaviest known, TON 618, has about 66 billion solar masses (NASA, *Black Holes*). How such giants grew so large, some within the first billion years after the Big Bang, is still an open question.

Our own Galaxy has one too, called **Sagittarius A\*** (Sgr A\*), about 27 000 light-years away. For about 30 years, teams led by Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez tracked the stars that circle it; with Kepler's third law (lessons 2.12 and 2.13) the orbits give its mass, about 4 million solar masses, in a region far too small for any group of stars. Their work shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2020 (ESO, 2022). One star, S2, goes round in 16 years. In May 2018 it passed less than 20 billion kilometres from the black hole at more than 25 million km/h, and its light showed exactly the gravitational redshift that general relativity predicts (ESO, 2018).

<WorkedExample title="How close did the star S2 come?">
<Problem>
Sgr A\* has a mass of about $4 \times 10^6$ solar masses. Find its Schwarzschild radius, and compare it with S2's closest approach. ESO says S2 came within about 1500 Schwarzschild radii. Is that consistent with "less than 20 billion km"? And how does the speed of S2 there compare with the escape speed?
</Problem>
<Step title="The Schwarzschild radius">
$M = 4 \times 10^6 \times 1.99 \times 10^{30} = 7.96 \times 10^{36}\ \text{kg}$, so $r_s = 2 \times 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 7.96 \times 10^{36}/(3.00 \times 10^8)^2 = 1.18 \times 10^{10}\ \text{m}$: about 12 million kilometres, or $0.08$ times the distance from the Earth to the Sun ($1.496 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$, lesson 3.11).
</Step>
<Step title="The closest approach">
$1500 \times 1.18 \times 10^{10}\ \text{m} = 1.8 \times 10^{13}\ \text{m}$, or 18 billion kilometres: indeed less than 20 billion. That is about 120 times the distance from the Earth to the Sun.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed against escape speed">
$25 \times 10^6\ \text{km/h} = 2.5 \times 10^{10}\ \text{m}/3600\ \text{s} = 6.9 \times 10^6\ \text{m/s}$, which is 2.3% of $c$. The escape speed at $r = 1500\,r_s$ is $c\sqrt{1/1500} = 0.026\,c$, or 2.6% of $c$. The two are nearly equal; ESO even gives the speed as almost 3% of $c$, and 25 million km/h is only a lower bound, just as 20 billion km is only an upper bound on the distance. So these rounded numbers cannot tell us which is larger. That is no surprise: S2's orbit is a very long, thin ellipse, and a star on such an orbit passes its closest point at just under the escape speed. We know S2 is bound because we see it come back every 16 years.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A picture of the shadow

Later developments, after the MIT slides of 2006, gave the first pictures. The **Event Horizon Telescope** links eight radio observatories across the Earth into one telescope the size of the planet (ESO, 2022). In 2019 it showed the black hole in the centre of the galaxy Messier 87, and in 2022 Sgr A\*, from observations made in April 2017. Both images show a dark region surrounded by a bright ring: glowing gas whose light is bent round the black hole. The dark **shadow** is about twice as large as the event horizon itself (NASA, *Black Hole Anatomy*), and the size of the ring matched what general relativity predicts (ESO, 2022).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Black holes and galaxies">
A supermassive black hole sits in the centre of nearly every large galaxy, and some were already huge less than a billion years after the Big Bang. How galaxies and their black holes grew up together is one of the questions the university course on structure formation takes up.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A star of about 20 solar masses or more ends in a supernova, and its collapsing core, heavier than any neutron star can be (at most about 3 solar masses in the 2006 slides; later estimates are somewhat lower), becomes a **black hole**.
- The escape speed at distance $r$ is $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2GM/r}$. It equals $c$ at the **Schwarzschild radius** $r_s = 2GM/c^2$, about $2.95$ km per solar mass: $29.5$ km for 10 solar masses.
- The **event horizon** at $r_s$ is a boundary, not a surface: nothing from inside escapes. Near it clocks run very slowly and light climbing out is strongly redshifted, so seen from far away a falling object never quite crosses it.
- Black holes are found through stars orbiting an invisible mass, X-ray binaries (heated gas from a companion star), gravitational waves from mergers, and Event Horizon Telescope images of their shadow.
- **Supermassive black holes**, of up to billions of solar masses, sit in the centres of galaxies. Ours, Sagittarius A\*, has about 4 million solar masses and $r_s \approx 1.2 \times 10^{10}$ m, weighed from the orbits of stars such as S2.
