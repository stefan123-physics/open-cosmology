Lesson 8.12 ran the expansion of the universe backwards and found a start about 13.8 billion years ago. This lesson asks what was there at that start, what the name "Big Bang" really means, how hot the young universe was, and in what order things happened as it cooled.

## What the Big Bang theory says

In his opening lecture of 8.286, Guth sums up the standard theory like this (transcript pp. 1–2): the universe as we know it began about 13.8 billion years ago in a state that was extremely **hot**, extremely **dense** and almost perfectly **uniform**, a soup of particles and light that filled space and was already expanding. Everything since then follows from that start: the theory computes how fast the universe expanded, how hot and how dense it was at every moment, and how the first chemical elements formed.

The popular picture is very different, and wrong. It shows a small, dense lump of matter that explodes at one point and sprays its pieces out into empty space. If that were so, the sky would look different in the direction of the explosion than in the opposite direction. It does not: on average the universe looks the same in every direction (pp. 2–3). So the Big Bang did not happen at one place. It happened **everywhere at once**: every bit of space was hot and dense, and every distance between bits of matter has been growing ever since. Lesson 8.11 showed the same thing with the raisin bread and the balloon: no galaxy is the centre, and the galaxies are not flying through space, space between them is growing.

What the theory does **not** say is just as important.

- It starts with a universe that is already hot and already expanding. It does not say what made it expand, or where all the matter came from. Guth calls it a theory of what happened after a bang, not of the bang itself (pp. 4–5).
- It does not say what happened "before". The words "as we know it" matter: whether anything came before the Big Bang is an open question. Lesson 8.18 meets inflation, an idea for the moments just before.
- It is not a theory of a point. The hot soup filled all of space. The part of the universe we can see today was then very small, but it was not a lump sitting in empty space.

## The scale factor: a ruler that grows

Because the expansion is the same everywhere, one number is enough to describe it. Imagine a ruler laid between two distant galaxies. As the universe expands, the ruler stretches, and every other ruler between every other pair of galaxies stretches by the same factor at the same time. That factor is the **scale factor** $a(t)$. Guth compares it to the scale on a map: instead of drawing a new map every day, you keep the map and only change the line "1 cm on the map is so many kilometres" (Lecture 4 p. 20). We set $a = 1$ today, so the distance $d(t)$ between two galaxies at time $t$ is

<Formula label="Distances grow with the scale factor" tex="d(t) = a(t)\, d_\text{now}" cite="reading2" at="p. 21, where Guth multiplies a coordinate distance (in notches) by the scale factor; here the coordinate distance is today's distance, so a = 1 now" />

where $d_\text{now}$ is the distance today. When $a = 1/2$, every distance between galaxies was half of what it is now; when $a = 1/1000$, a thousand times smaller. The galaxies themselves did not shrink; when $a$ was very small, they did not exist yet at all. What was smaller was the spacing of everything.

A smaller spacing means more crowding. If every length is halved, every volume becomes $(1/2)^3 = 1/8$ as large, so the same matter is packed **8 times** as densely. In general the density of matter goes as $1/a^3$ (Guth, Lecture 17 p. 18). This is the "dense" in "hot and dense".

Lesson 5.13 and lesson 7.11 already met the scale factor in disguise: light that set out when the universe had scale factor $a$ arrives stretched by the factor $1 + z = 1/a$.

## Cooling as space expands

The universe is filled with light, and that light is stretched along with space: every wavelength grows in proportion to $a$ (lesson 5.13). The light of the young universe was black-body radiation (lesson 7.2), and Wien's law says that the peak wavelength and the temperature of a black body always give the same product, $\lambda_{\max} T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}$. If $\lambda_{\max}$ grows like $a$, the temperature must fall like $1/a$. Guth reaches the same result in Lecture 17 by a different route, from how the radiation thins out as the volume grows:

<Formula label="Radiation cools as the universe grows" tex="T \times a = \text{constant} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = \frac{T_\text{now}}{a}" cite="reading4" at="p. 18: the temperature falls as 1 over the scale factor (as long as the kinds of particle in the radiation stay the same)" />

Today $a = 1$ and the radiation is the cosmic microwave background at $T_\text{now} = 2.725\ \text{K}$ (measured by NASA's COBE satellite; NASA, *COBE FIRAS Overview*). So the formula tells us how hot the universe was at any earlier size. Lesson 7.11 used it for the moment the background light set out; here we use it to look much further back. It is the same cooling as that of an expanding gas (lesson 4.10): the universe is the largest expanding gas there is.

<WorkedExample title="How hot was a smaller universe?">
<Problem>
The cosmic background radiation now has $T_\text{now} = 2.725\ \text{K}$. (a) How hot was it when every distance was $1/100$ of today's? (b) And at $1/1000$? Where was the peak of its spectrum then? (c) How much smaller than today was the universe when its temperature was $1.0 \times 10^{10}\ \text{K}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) A hundred times smaller">
$a = 1/100 = 0.01$, so $T = 2.725/0.01 = 272.5\ \text{K}$. That is about $-0.7\ ^\circ\text{C}$, just below the freezing point of water: the whole sky was as warm as a cold winter day.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A thousand times smaller">
$T = 2.725/0.001 = 2725\ \text{K}$, as hot as the filament of a dim light bulb. Wien's law puts the peak at $\lambda_{\max} = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}/2725 = 1.06 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}$, in the near infrared, just beyond red light. This is a little below the $3000\ \text{K}$ at which the light was set free (lesson 7.11), so the universe had only just become transparent.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Ten billion kelvin">
Turn the formula round: $a = T_\text{now}/T = 2.725/(1.0 \times 10^{10}) = 2.7 \times 10^{-10}$. Every distance was about $1/(2.7 \times 10^{-10}) = 3.7 \times 10^{9}$ times smaller than today: almost four billion times.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A temperature clock

How fast did the young universe cool? That depends on how fast $a$ grew, which the university course works out from the Friedmann equation (8.286 Lecture 7). For roughly its first ten thousand to a hundred thousand years (8.902 Lecture 19 notes, p. 109) the energy of the universe was mostly radiation, and for a universe filled with radiation the result, which we take as given, is

<Formula label="Growth of a universe filled with radiation" tex="a(t) \propto \sqrt{t}" cite="reading3" at="p. 14 (board)" />

with $t$ the time since the start. Put this into $T \times a = \text{constant}$ and the temperature falls as $1/\sqrt{t}$: four times as long, half as hot. Guth works out the number in front for the era around one second, when the radiation consists of light, neutrinos and pairs of electrons and positrons (Lecture 17 p. 17):

<Formula label="Temperature of the early universe against time" tex="T = \frac{9.98 \times 10^{9}\ \text{K}}{\sqrt{t\ [\text{s}]}}" cite="reading4" at="p. 17" />

So one second after the start the universe was at about ten billion kelvin, $10^{10}\ \text{K}$: a hot soup of light and particles (NASA, *Universe: Overview*). The rule is only for the era around one second. As the universe cools, kinds of particle disappear from the radiation, and soon after one second the electron–positron pairs are gone; then the number in front no longer holds (Guth, p. 17). So the formula is a good guide to the size of the temperature near one second, not an exact clock for all times. Recall from lesson 4.4 what such a temperature means: the particles move so fast that every collision smashes atoms, and even nuclei, apart.

<WorkedExample title="Reading the temperature clock">
<Problem>
Use the rounded rule $T = 1.0 \times 10^{10}\ \text{K}/\sqrt{t}$, with $t$ in seconds. (a) How hot was the universe at $t = 0.01\ \text{s}$? (b) At $t = 25\ \text{s}$? (c) When had it cooled to $1.0 \times 10^{9}\ \text{K}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) One hundredth of a second">
$\sqrt{0.01} = 0.1$, so $T = 1.0 \times 10^{10}/0.1 = 1.0 \times 10^{11}\ \text{K}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Twenty-five seconds">
$\sqrt{25} = 5$, so $T = 1.0 \times 10^{10}/5 = 2.0 \times 10^{9}\ \text{K}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) One billion kelvin">
$\sqrt{t} = 1.0 \times 10^{10}/(1.0 \times 10^{9}) = 10$, so $t = 100\ \text{s}$ by the rule. But the rule only holds while the electron–positron pairs are still around (Guth, Lecture 17 p. 17), and they disappear soon after one second. The real universe reached about $10^{9}\ \text{K}$, about three minutes after the start (lesson 8.14). That is the era in which protons and neutrons fused into the first nuclei.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A short history of the universe

Putting the scale factor and the temperature together gives the history of the universe in order. The times are rounded; the temperatures fall all the way.

| When | How hot | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| The first tiny fraction of a second | unknown | Physics we have not tested; perhaps inflation (lesson 8.18). |
| About $10^{-5}\ \text{s}$ | one to a few trillion K | Quarks and gluons are bound into protons and neutrons (lesson 8.7). |
| About $1\ \text{s}$ | about $10^{10}\ \text{K}$ | A soup of light, neutrinos, electrons and positrons, with a sprinkling of protons and neutrons. |
| The first minutes | about $10^{9}\ \text{K}$, about three minutes after the start | Protons and neutrons fuse into nuclei of hydrogen, helium and a little lithium; most of today's helium exists by about five minutes (lesson 8.14). |
| About 380 000 years | about $3000\ \text{K}$ | Nuclei capture electrons into atoms; the fog clears and the light is set free: the cosmic microwave background (lesson 8.15). |
| Up to a few hundred million years | cooling further | The "dark ages": no stars yet. Then the first stars, 30 to 300 times as massive as the Sun, light up and gather into the first galaxies. |
| Today, 13.8 billion years | $2.725\ \text{K}$ | Galaxies, stars, planets, us. |

The early times come from MIT 8.902's timeline of the hot universe (Lecture 19 notes, p. 109) and Guth's Lecture 17; the dark ages and the first stars from NASA (*Universe: Overview* and *The Big Bang*). The 8.902 notes give the temperature at $10^{-5}\ \text{s}$ as an energy per particle of about 100 to 300 MeV (p. 109), which is about $1.2 \times 10^{12}$ to $3.5 \times 10^{12}\ \text{K}$: one to a few trillion kelvin. The clock of the previous section does not hold that early, because many more kinds of particle were then part of the radiation.

Before 380 000 years the universe was opaque, like the inside of a star: free electrons kept scattering the light (lesson 7.11). So no telescope can ever see further back than the microwave background with light. Everything before it we know by calculation, checked by what it left behind.

## Three pieces of evidence

A theory about the first minutes of the universe needs evidence we can measure today. There are three main pieces, each the subject of its own lesson.

1. **The expansion.** Distant galaxies move away from us at speeds in proportion to their distance, $v = H_0 d$ (lesson 8.11). Run backwards, every distance shrinks towards zero about 13.8 billion years ago (lesson 8.12): the universe was once far denser.
2. **The light elements.** The theory predicts how much hydrogen, helium and lithium the first minutes made. Guth points out that the predicted amounts of about five kinds of nuclei agree well with what is measured, one of the major confirmations of the theory (Lecture 1 p. 4). Lesson 8.14 shows how.
3. **The cosmic microwave background.** If the universe was once hot and has cooled as it expanded, its old light should still fill all of space, stretched into a cold black-body glow. It does: a black body at $2.725\ \text{K}$ from every direction. Guth calls it an image of the universe as it was when the light was set free (Lecture 17 pp. 19–20). Lesson 8.15 looks at it closely.

Each piece on its own might perhaps be explained in another way. Together, from a few simple assumptions (a hot gas, expanding, pulled back by gravity), the theory accounts for all three with the same numbers. That is why it is the standard theory of the early universe.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="From a growing ruler to the Friedmann equation">
In MIT 8.286, Guth turns the scale factor of this lesson into a calculation. Lecture 7 derives the **Friedmann equation**, which says how fast $a(t)$ grows given what the universe contains. Lecture 16 solves it for a universe filled with radiation, finding $a \propto \sqrt{t}$, and shows that black-body radiation cools as $T \propto 1/a$; Lecture 17 combines them into the temperature clock $T = 9.98 \times 10^{9}\ \text{K}/\sqrt{t}$ and follows the universe down to $3000\ \text{K}$, when the microwave background was set free.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The **Big Bang theory** says that about 13.8 billion years ago the universe was extremely hot, dense and uniform, and that space has expanded and cooled ever since, **everywhere at once**. It was not an explosion from one point into empty space, and the theory says nothing about what caused the expansion or what came before.
- One **scale factor** $a(t)$ describes the expansion: $d(t) = a(t)\,d_\text{now}$, with $a = 1$ today. Densities of matter go as $1/a^3$.
- The radiation cools as space grows: $T \times a$ stays constant, so $T = T_\text{now}/a$ with $T_\text{now} = 2.725\ \text{K}$. At $a = 1/100$ the sky was at $272.5\ \text{K}$, at $a = 1/1000$ at $2725\ \text{K}$.
- With radiation in charge, $a \propto \sqrt{t}$ and $T \propto 1/\sqrt{t}$: about $10^{10}\ \text{K}$ one second after the start.
- Timeline: protons and neutrons at about $10^{-5}\ \text{s}$; the light nuclei in the first minutes; atoms and the microwave background at about 380 000 years; the first stars and galaxies after a few hundred million years.
- Three pieces of evidence carry the theory: the expansion of the universe, the amounts of the light elements, and the cosmic microwave background.
