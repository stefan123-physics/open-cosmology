Lesson 8.11 found that the galaxies are moving away from us, and that the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it goes: Hubble's law. A film of moving galaxies can be played backwards. Played backwards, the galaxies come closer together, and at some moment in the past they must all have been on top of each other. This lesson finds when that was: the age of the universe.

## Playing the film backwards

Hubble's law says that a galaxy at distance $d$ moves away from us at a speed proportional to that distance:

<Formula label="Hubble's law" tex="v = H_0\, d" cite="reading2" at="p. 7, where Guth writes the distance as r" />

$H_0$ is the Hubble constant, its value today. Now ask how long the galaxy has needed to get from right here to where it is now. If it always kept its present speed, the time is distance divided by speed (lesson 1.2):

<Formula label="Time since all galaxies were together (constant speeds)" tex="t = \frac{d}{v} = \frac{d}{H_0\, d} = \frac{1}{H_0}" cite="reading2" at="p. 14, in words: running the expansion backwards, every age is proportional to 1/H; the step t = d/v is ours" />

The distance cancels. A galaxy twice as far away moves twice as fast, so it needed exactly the same time. Every galaxy, near or far, was at our position (and at every other galaxy's position) at the same moment, a time $1/H_0$ ago. This time is called the **Hubble time**. It is the first estimate of the age of the universe.

## From km/s/Mpc to years

Astronomers give $H_0$ in kilometres per second per megaparsec: the speed of recession, in km/s, for every megaparsec of distance. A kilometre and a megaparsec are both lengths, so in the end $H_0$ is "one divided by a time", like a frequency. To turn it into a time we put everything in SI units. As lesson 8.10 showed, a parsec is 3.26 light-years (Guth gives 3.2616, Lecture 4 p. 8), and a megaparsec (Mpc) is a million parsecs.

<WorkedExample title="The Hubble time in seconds and in years">
<Problem>
Take $H_0 = 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$, the round value used for examples. Find the Hubble time $1/H_0$ in seconds and in years. Use $1\ \text{light-year} = 9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m}$ and $1\ \text{year} = 3.156 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="One megaparsec in metres">
$1\ \text{Mpc} = 3.26 \times 10^{6}\ \text{light-years} = 3.26 \times 10^{6} \times 9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m} = 3.08 \times 10^{22}\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The Hubble constant in SI units">
$70\ \text{km/s} = 7.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{m/s}$ for every $3.08 \times 10^{22}\ \text{m}$, so $H_0 = 7.0 \times 10^{4}/(3.08 \times 10^{22}) = 2.27 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The Hubble time">
$1/H_0 = 1/(2.27 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}) = 4.41 \times 10^{17}\ \text{s}$. In years: $4.41 \times 10^{17}/(3.156 \times 10^{7}) = 1.40 \times 10^{10}\ \text{years}$, about 14 billion years.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Doing this every time is tiresome, so cosmologists remember one conversion, which Guth writes on the board:

<Formula label="Converting the Hubble constant to years" tex="\frac{1}{10^{10}\ \text{years}} = 97.8\ \text{km/s/Mpc}" cite="reading2" at="p. 8 (board)" />

So $1/H_0 = (97.8/H_0) \times 10^{10}\ \text{years}$, or $(977.8/H_0)$ billion years, with $H_0$ in km/s/Mpc. For $H_0 = 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$ that gives $977.8/70 = 14.0$ billion years, the same as the long way.

<WorkedExample title="Near and far galaxies agree">
<Problem>
With $H_0 = 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$, one galaxy is $150\ \text{Mpc}$ away and another $450\ \text{Mpc}$. How fast does each move away, and how long did each need to reach its distance at that speed?
</Problem>
<Step title="Speeds from Hubble's law">
$v = H_0 d = 70 \times 150 = 10\,500\ \text{km/s}$ for the near galaxy and $70 \times 450 = 31\,500\ \text{km/s}$ for the far one.
</Step>
<Step title="Times in SI units">
Near: $d = 150 \times 3.08 \times 10^{22} = 4.62 \times 10^{24}\ \text{m}$ and $v = 1.05 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m/s}$, so $t = d/v = 4.40 \times 10^{17}\ \text{s}$. Far: $d = 1.39 \times 10^{25}\ \text{m}$ and $v = 3.15 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m/s}$, so $t = 4.40 \times 10^{17}\ \text{s}$ as well.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Both equal the Hubble time from the previous example (the last digit differs only by rounding). Three times as far, three times as fast: the same start.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why the real age is not exactly 1/H₀

The Hubble time assumes that every galaxy always kept today's speed. That is not quite true, for two reasons.

- **Gravity slows the expansion.** All the matter in the universe pulls on all the rest (lesson 2.11), like a ball thrown upwards that slows as it rises. So in the past the galaxies moved apart faster than now, and they covered their distances in less time than $1/H_0$. Gravity makes the universe younger than the Hubble time.
- **Dark energy speeds it up.** Since 1998 astronomers have known that the expansion is now getting faster, driven by what is called dark energy (lesson 8.17). Over the last few billion years the galaxies moved more slowly than today, which makes the universe older than a matter-only universe would be.

For a universe that contains only matter and is exactly "flat" (just balanced between expanding for ever and falling back, which our universe is very close to), the university course works out the expansion with gravity included. The result, which we take as given, is two-thirds of the Hubble time:

<Formula label="Age of a flat universe of matter alone" tex="t = \frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{H_0}" cite="reading1" at="p. 11 (board)" />

The plot compares the two cases, scaled so that both have the same size and the same expansion rate today. Running backwards, the universe of matter alone reaches zero size sooner, because it was expanding faster in the past.

<WorkedExample title="Too young for its stars">
<Problem>
With $H_0 = 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$, how old would a flat universe of matter alone be? Compare it with M15, a **globular cluster**: a dense, ball-shaped swarm of very old stars. M15 is one of the oldest known, about 12 billion years old (NASA Science, *Messier 15*).
</Problem>
<Step title="Age of the matter-only universe">
$t = \tfrac23 \times 14.0 = 9.3$ billion years.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
M15's stars would be older than the universe they live in, which is impossible. So the universe is not made of matter alone.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

This is a real piece of history. In his 2013 lecture Guth explains that before 1998 the universe was thought to contain matter alone, and with today's $H_0$ such a universe comes out near 10 billion years old (Lecture 8 p. 12). The oldest stars were hard to fit into so short a time. Guth quotes a later study of globular clusters by Krauss and Chaboyer (published in 2001) which says the universe must be at least 11.2 billion years old (Lecture 8 p. 13). The discovery of dark energy in 1998 settled the question: with dark energy included, the computed age rises to about **13.8 billion years**, older than every star (Lecture 8 p. 13). The same problem had hit the Big Bang once before. Hubble's own value of $H_0$ was about seven times too large, which gave ages near 2 billion years, younger than the rocks of the Earth (Lecture 8 pp. 11–12).

By chance the slowing and the speeding up almost cancel. With the Planck satellite's 2013 value that Guth uses, $H_0 = 67.3\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$ (Lecture 8 p. 12; the year: Lecture 4 p. 12), the Hubble time is $977.8/67.3 = 14.5$ billion years, and the age of 13.8 billion years is about 95% of it.

## How the age is measured

The best measurement does not time a single galaxy. It uses the **cosmic microwave background**: light released about 380 000 years after the universe began, which still fills the whole sky (lesson 7.11; more in lesson 8.15). NASA's WMAP satellite (launched 2001, observing until 2010) mapped tiny differences in its temperature across the whole sky. Typical warmer and cooler spots differ by only about 1 part in 100 000 (Lecture 4 p. 12); one example on NASA's page shows two patches whose temperatures differ by two ten-thousandths of a kelvin. The pattern of those spots tells how much ordinary matter, dark matter and dark energy the universe contains and how fast it expands. From those amounts the whole expansion history can be computed and run backwards to the start, as in the plot above but with all the ingredients. WMAP's results fixed the age to within half a percent (NASA Science, *WMAP Overview*). The Planck satellite measured the same light again. Guth quotes Planck's 2013 value of the Hubble constant, 67.3 km/s/Mpc (Lecture 4 p. 12, Lecture 8 p. 12), and gives the age as about 13.8 billion years (Lecture 8 p. 13).

## Cross-checks: nothing may be older than the universe

An age found from the expansion can be tested against clocks that have nothing to do with it.

- **The oldest stars.** A globular cluster, as in the worked example above, is a dense, ball-shaped swarm of very old stars. From how far its stars have got in using up their fuel (lesson 8.5), astronomers find an age of about 12 billion years for M15, one of the oldest (NASA Science, *Messier 15*). That is younger than 13.8 billion years, with room for the first stars to form.
- **The Earth.** Radioactive dating (lesson 7.9) compares a long-lived parent nucleus, such as uranium-238, with the daughter nuclei it has made since a rock formed. It gives the Earth an age of about 4.5 billion years (NASA Science, *Facts About Earth*), a third of the age of the universe.

Three completely different methods, the expansion of space, the burning of stars and the decay of nuclei, give ages that fit together. That agreement is one of the strongest reasons to trust the answer.

## Summary

- Running Hubble's law $v = H_0 d$ backwards at constant speeds, every galaxy took the same time $t = d/v = 1/H_0$ to reach its distance: all were together one **Hubble time** ago.
- To convert, put $H_0$ in SI units: $1\ \text{Mpc} = 3.08 \times 10^{22}\ \text{m}$, so $H_0 = 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$ is $2.27 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ and $1/H_0$ is about 14 billion years. Quick rule: $1/H_0 = (977.8/H_0)$ billion years.
- Gravity slowed the expansion, which makes the universe younger than $1/H_0$: matter alone would give $t = \tfrac23 \cdot 1/H_0$, too young for the oldest stars. Dark energy speeds the expansion up and makes it older again.
- Maps of the cosmic microwave background (WMAP, then Planck) give an age of about 13.8 billion years.
- The oldest stars (about 12 billion years) and the Earth (about 4.5 billion years, from radioactive dating) are younger than the universe, as they must be.
