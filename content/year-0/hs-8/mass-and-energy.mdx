Relativity changed time and length (lesson 8.2). It also changes energy, and it leads to a startling conclusion: a body that gives off energy loses mass. Turned round, every mass is a store of energy, and a very large one. This lesson works out what that means, from a single electron to the Sun.

## Mass is a form of energy

A mass $m$ at rest holds an energy called its **rest energy**:

<Formula label="Rest energy" tex="E_0 = mc^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 1: the energy of a particle at rest" />

Here $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$ is the speed of light. Because $c^2 = 9.0 \times 10^{16}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2$ is such a huge number, a small mass stands for an enormous energy.

This is not a new kind of energy added to the list of lesson 3.4. It says that mass itself belongs on the list. Energy can change form, and mass is one of the forms. When a system gives off energy (as light, as heat, or as the kinetic energy of fragments), its mass goes down by $\Delta m = \Delta E/c^2$. When it takes in energy, its mass goes up. Energy is still conserved; what changes is our idea of what counts as energy.

<WorkedExample title="The energy in one gram">
<Problem>
How much rest energy is there in $1.0\ \text{g}$ of matter, about the mass of a paperclip? How long could a large power station with an output of $1.0\ \text{GW}$ run on that energy?
</Problem>
<Step title="Rest energy">
$E_0 = mc^2 = 1.0 \times 10^{-3} \times (3.00 \times 10^{8})^2 = 9.0 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Running time">
Power is energy per second (lesson 3.5), so $t = E/P = 9.0 \times 10^{13}/(1.0 \times 10^{9}) = 9.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{s}$, which is $25$ hours. One gram of mass, turned completely into other forms of energy, would run a big power station for a whole day.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

No ordinary process comes close to turning all of a mass into other energy. Burning, heating and chemistry turn only a tiny fraction of it, as the last section shows.

## Energy in motion: E = γmc²

A body that moves has more energy than one at rest. In relativity its total energy is its rest energy multiplied by the Lorentz factor $\gamma$ of lesson 8.2:

<Formula label="Total energy of a moving body" tex="E = \gamma mc^2, \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1: the energy is mc² divided by √(1 − u²/c²)" />

At rest $v = 0$, so $\gamma = 1$ and $E = mc^2$ again. As the speed grows, $\gamma$ grows, slowly at first and then very fast: at $v = 0.9c$ it is $2.29$, at $0.99c$ it is $7.09$, and as $v$ gets close to $c$ the factor $\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ goes to zero, so $\gamma$ and the energy grow without limit.

That is why **nothing with mass can reach the speed of light**. Every push adds energy, but near $c$ each extra joule buys a smaller and smaller gain in speed. Reaching $c$ itself would take an infinite amount of energy. The speed of light is not a barrier that something pushes against; it is a limit that can be approached but never reached.

## Kinetic energy, old and new

The part of the energy that comes from the motion is the **kinetic energy**: the total energy minus the rest energy.

<Formula label="Relativistic kinetic energy" tex="E_k = E - E_0 = (\gamma - 1)\,mc^2" cite="mit" at="p. 1: the kinetic energy is γ − 1 times m₀c²" />

In lesson 3.2 the kinetic energy was $\tfrac12 mv^2$. Both cannot be right in general. The MIT lecture shows that at speeds well below $c$ the new formula becomes the old one:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy at low speed" tex="E_k \approx \tfrac12 mv^2 \quad \text{when } v \ll c" cite="mit" at="p. 1: evaluated for small v, the kinetic energy is ½m₀v²" />

You can check this with numbers. The table gives both kinetic energies as multiples of the rest energy $mc^2$, so it holds for any mass.

| $v/c$ | $\gamma$ | $(\gamma - 1)mc^2$ (relativity) | $\tfrac12 mv^2$ (Newton) | relativity ÷ Newton |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | 1.00005 | $5.0 \times 10^{-5}\,mc^2$ | $5.0 \times 10^{-5}\,mc^2$ | 1.0001 |
| 0.1 | 1.005 | $0.00504\,mc^2$ | $0.00500\,mc^2$ | 1.008 |
| 0.5 | 1.155 | $0.155\,mc^2$ | $0.125\,mc^2$ | 1.24 |
| 0.9 | 2.29 | $1.29\,mc^2$ | $0.405\,mc^2$ | 3.2 |
| 0.99 | 7.09 | $6.09\,mc^2$ | $0.490\,mc^2$ | 12 |

At one percent of the speed of light, which is $3000\ \text{km/s}$ and far faster than any rocket, the two formulas still agree to one part in ten thousand. That is why Newton's $\tfrac12 mv^2$ works perfectly for cars, planets and spacecraft. Only near $c$ do they part: Newton's kinetic energy never gets past $\tfrac12 mc^2$, while the true kinetic energy climbs without limit.

<WorkedExample title="A fast electron">
<Problem>
A small accelerator gives an electron a speed of $0.80c$. Its rest energy is $0.511\ \text{MeV}$. Find its total energy and its kinetic energy, and compare with Newton's formula.
</Problem>
<Step title="Lorentz factor">
$\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.80^2} = 1/\sqrt{0.36} = 1/0.60 = 5/3$, about $1.67$.
</Step>
<Step title="Total and kinetic energy">
$E = \gamma mc^2 = (5/3) \times 0.511 = 0.852\ \text{MeV}$, so $E_k = (\gamma - 1)mc^2 = (2/3) \times 0.511 = 0.341\ \text{MeV}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Newton's answer">
$\tfrac12 mv^2 = \tfrac12 \times 0.80^2 \times mc^2 = 0.32 \times 0.511 = 0.164\ \text{MeV}$: less than half the true value. At this speed Newton's formula is badly wrong.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Particle energies in MeV

For single particles the joule is an awkward unit. Physicists use the electronvolt of lesson 6.4, $1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$, and the **mega-electronvolt**, $1\ \text{MeV} = 10^6\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$.

With the rounded masses used on this site, the electron's rest energy is $m_e c^2 = 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times (3.00 \times 10^8)^2 = 8.20 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{J}$, which is $0.512\ \text{MeV}$. The proton's is $m_p c^2 = 1.67 \times 10^{-27} \times (3.00 \times 10^8)^2 = 1.50 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{J}$, or $939\ \text{MeV}$. The precise values, from the measured constants, are:

- electron: $m_e c^2 = 0.511\ \text{MeV}$ (NIST, 0.510 999 MeV);
- proton: $m_p c^2 = 938\ \text{MeV}$ (NIST, 938.272 MeV), almost a thousand MeV, about 1836 times the electron's.

The small differences from our results come only from rounding $c$, $m_e$, $m_p$ and the electronvolt. Particle physicists often quote masses directly as energies: "the electron has a mass of $0.511\ \text{MeV}$" means $0.511\ \text{MeV}/c^2$.

These numbers set the scale of nuclear and particle physics. Nuclear energies are measured in MeV too: an alpha particle leaves a radioactive nucleus with about $4\ \text{MeV}$ (lesson 7.8). That is still small next to the rest energy of the nucleons involved, but no longer a negligible part of it. Lesson 8.4 shows how such energies power nuclear reactors and the stars.

## Light has energy but no rest mass

A photon of frequency $f$ carries energy (lesson 7.2):

<Formula label="Energy of a photon" tex="E = hf" cite="photon" at="Sec. 11.5" />

Yet a photon has **no rest mass**: $m = 0$. It never sits still; it always moves at $c$. The formula $E = \gamma mc^2$ cannot describe it ($\gamma$ would be infinite and $m$ zero), and the MIT lecture gives the rule for a massless particle instead: its energy is its momentum times $c$.

<Formula label="Energy of a massless particle" tex="E = pc" cite="reading1" at="p. 1: for massless particles such as the photon, the energy is the momentum times c" />

So light carries energy, which is why sunlight warms what it falls on, and it carries momentum too. And because a body that gives off light loses energy, it also loses mass, by $\Delta m = E/c^2$. A green photon (wavelength $540\ \text{nm}$) carries $hc/\lambda = 2.3\ \text{eV}$, tiny next to the electron's $511\ 000\ \text{eV}$, but a star sends out an enormous number of them.

## The Sun is getting lighter

The Sun radiates a power, its **luminosity**, of $L = 3.828 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}$ (NASA Sun Fact Sheet). Every second that much energy leaves it as light and other radiation, so every second its mass drops by $L/c^2$:

<Formula label="Mass lost per second by a shining body" tex="\frac{\Delta m}{\Delta t} = \frac{L}{c^2}" cite="reading2" at="slide 16 (E = mc² applied to the luminosity; the formula is the page's own)" />

<WorkedExample title="How much mass does the Sun lose?">
<Problem>
Use the Sun's luminosity to find the mass it loses every second, and the fraction of its mass, $1.99 \times 10^{30}\ \text{kg}$, it would lose if it shone at today's rate for 10 billion years.
</Problem>
<Step title="Mass per second">
$\Delta m/\Delta t = L/c^2 = 3.828 \times 10^{26}/(3.00 \times 10^{8})^2 = 4.25 \times 10^{9}\ \text{kg/s}$. A tonne is $1000\ \text{kg}$, so this is about $4.3$ million tonnes every second. NASA's fact sheet lists the same "mass conversion rate", $4.26 \times 10^{9}\ \text{kg/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Over 10 billion years">
A year is $3.156 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s}$, so 10 billion years is $3.156 \times 10^{17}\ \text{s}$. The mass lost is $4.25 \times 10^{9} \times 3.156 \times 10^{17} = 1.34 \times 10^{27}\ \text{kg}$, which is $1.34 \times 10^{27}/(1.99 \times 10^{30}) = 6.7 \times 10^{-4}$ of the Sun's mass: less than 0.07%.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Four million tonnes a second sounds like a lot, but the Sun is so massive that it hardly notices. Where does the energy come from? Deep in the Sun's core, hydrogen nuclei join to make helium, and a helium nucleus has slightly less mass than the parts it was made from. Lesson 8.4 works out how much.

## Why chemistry never notices

If every release of energy lowers a mass, why do chemists never see it? Weigh the substances before and after any chemical reaction in a closed container, and the total mass stays the same as far as any balance can tell. The answer is the size of $c^2$.

<WorkedExample title="A hot-water bottle gets heavier">
<Problem>
You fill a hot-water bottle with $1.5\ \text{kg}$ of water and heat it from $20\ \text{°C}$ to $70\ \text{°C}$. Water needs $4180\ \text{J}$ to warm $1\ \text{kg}$ by one degree (lesson 4.5). By how much does the mass of the water increase?
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy added">
$Q = 1.5 \times 4180 \times 50 = 3.14 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Extra mass">
$\Delta m = Q/c^2 = 3.14 \times 10^{5}/(9.0 \times 10^{16}) = 3.5 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{kg}$, which is $3.5 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{g}$. A good school balance reads to $0.01\ \text{g}$: the change is about three million times too small for it to show.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Chemistry is no different. When a proton and an electron join to form a hydrogen atom, $13.6\ \text{eV}$ leaves as light (lesson 7.4). Compared with the atom's rest energy of about $939\ \text{MeV}$, that is $13.6/(939 \times 10^{6}) = 1.4 \times 10^{-8}$: the atom is lighter than its two parts by about one part in a hundred million. Chemical reactions involve energies of the same size, a few electronvolts per molecule. Burning carbon, C + O₂ → CO₂, gives off $393\ \text{kJ}$ for every mole of carbon dioxide formed, and a mole is $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ molecules. So each molecule formed releases $393 \times 10^{3}/(6.02 \times 10^{23}) = 6.5 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$, about $4.1\ \text{eV}$. Reactions like this change the mass by a similarly tiny fraction. The mass is not exactly conserved, but no one has ever weighed the change in a chemical reaction. In nuclear reactions, with millions of electronvolts per nucleus, the change is a million times larger and easy to measure.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Light that weighs">
E = mc² also works the other way round: anything with energy has a mass and feels and produces gravity, including light itself. In Guth's cosmology course this is the starting point for the radiation that fills the universe. Today it is a tiny part of the cosmic energy budget, but in the first tens of thousands of years after the Big Bang, radiation outweighed all the matter and controlled how fast the universe expanded.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Mass is a form of energy: a mass at rest holds the **rest energy** $E_0 = mc^2$. One gram holds $9.0 \times 10^{13}\ \text{J}$. A body that gives off energy $\Delta E$ loses mass $\Delta E/c^2$.
- A moving body has total energy $E = \gamma mc^2$ with $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$. It grows without limit as $v$ approaches $c$, so nothing with mass can reach the speed of light.
- The **kinetic energy** is $E_k = (\gamma - 1)mc^2$. At speeds well below $c$ it equals Newton's $\tfrac12 mv^2$; near $c$ it is much larger.
- Particle energies are given in MeV: the electron's rest energy is $0.511\ \text{MeV}$, the proton's $938\ \text{MeV}$.
- A photon has energy $E = hf$ (and momentum, $E = pc$) but no rest mass, and always moves at $c$.
- The Sun radiates $3.828 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}$ and so loses $L/c^2 \approx 4.3$ million tonnes of mass every second. In heating and chemistry the change of mass is far too small to measure; in nuclear reactions (lesson 8.4) it is not.
