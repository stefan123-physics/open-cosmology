Light travels as a wave and arrives as photons (lessons 5.11 and 7.3). In 1924 Louis de Broglie turned this round and proposed that the same holds for matter: an electron, which we picture as a tiny particle, should also travel as a wave. Experiments soon showed that he was right. This lesson finds the wavelength of a particle, shows why you never notice the wave of a football, and follows the idea to one of the most famous results of physics, the uncertainty principle.

## The wavelength of a particle

Start with light. A photon of wavelength $\lambda$ has energy $E = hf = hc/\lambda$ (lesson 7.2), and a massless particle has $E = pc$ (lesson 8.3). Together they give the momentum of a photon:

<Formula label="Momentum of a photon" tex="p = \frac{E}{c} = \frac{h}{\lambda}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 3, Eq. (3.22), quoting Eq. (2.16)" />

De Broglie's idea was that this link between a wave property, the wavelength, and a particle property, the momentum, is not special to light. Any particle with momentum $p$ comes with a wave whose wavelength is

<Formula label="de Broglie wavelength" tex="\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{mv}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 3, Eq. (3.23); p = mv for a particle of mass m and speed v (lesson 3.6) is added by this page" />

Here $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$ is Planck's constant and $p = mv$ is the momentum of lesson 3.6. A faster or heavier particle has a **shorter** wavelength. The MIT notes point out that this is a purely quantum effect: if Planck's constant were zero, every wavelength would be zero and particles would show no wave behaviour at all.

<WorkedExample title="An electron and a tennis ball">
<Problem>
Find the de Broglie wavelength of an electron moving at $2.0 \times 10^{6}\ \text{m/s}$, and of a tennis ball of mass $0.058\ \text{kg}$ served at $50\ \text{m/s}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The electron">
$p = m_e v = 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 2.0 \times 10^{6} = 1.82 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{kg m/s}$, so $\lambda = h/p = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}/(1.82 \times 10^{-24}) = 3.6 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$, or $0.36\ \text{nm}$. That is a few times the size of an atom.
</Step>
<Step title="The tennis ball">
$p = 0.058 \times 50 = 2.9\ \text{kg m/s}$, so $\lambda = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}/2.9 = 2.3 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{m}$. A proton is about $10^{-15}\ \text{m}$ across (lesson 7.1): the ball's wavelength is more than $10^{18}$ times smaller still.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why a football does not diffract

Lesson 5.11 showed that a wave spreads out noticeably behind a gap or an obstacle only when its wavelength is not much smaller than the size of the gap. The same rule tells us when the wave of a particle matters. With $a$ the size of the gap or obstacle that the particle meets, its wave nature can be ignored when

<Formula label="When wave effects can be ignored" tex="\lambda \ll a" cite="mit" at="Sec. 3, Eq. (3.24), λ/x ≪ 1 (the notes write the size of the object as x)" />

For the tennis ball, $\lambda$ is about $10^{-34}\ \text{m}$ and every doorway, net or racket is enormously larger, so the ball moves exactly as Newton's laws say. That is why nobody noticed matter waves before the twentieth century. For an electron, with a wavelength of a fraction of a nanometre, the "gaps" must be about the size of atoms. Nature provides them: in a crystal the atoms sit in neat rows about $0.1$ to $0.4\ \text{nm}$ apart.

## Electrons that interfere

In 1927 Davisson and Germer fired a beam of electrons at the surface of a metal crystal. The scattered electrons did not spread out evenly: at certain angles many more of them came back than at others. These peaks are the same constructive interference that a diffraction grating gives for light (lesson 5.11), now from electron waves scattered by the rows of atoms. They showed that electrons really do behave as waves.

Electrons can also be sent through two very narrow slits. Behind them, on a screen, they make bright and dark fringes, exactly like light in Young's experiment. The experiment even works when the electrons are sent **one at a time**. Each electron arrives at one spot, as a particle would, but the spots pile up into fringes, as only a wave could make. Where an electron lands is a matter of chance; the wave decides where it is likely to land. This is wave–particle duality again (lesson 7.3), now for matter.

The fringe spacing of lesson 5.11 is still proportional to the wavelength, so faster electrons, with shorter wavelengths, give fringes closer together. Interference is not limited to electrons: in an experiment reported in 2013, which the MIT notes describe, even molecules of 810 atoms, with a mass of more than 10 000 atomic mass units, were made to interfere.

## Electron microscopes

Lesson 5.11 showed that a telescope cannot separate two points closer than an angle of about the wavelength divided by the width of its opening: diffraction blurs them. A light microscope has the same limit. It cannot show details much smaller than the wavelength of visible light, a few hundred nanometres, which is why a virus, about $100\ \text{nm}$ across, is invisible in it.

An **electron microscope** uses a beam of electrons instead of light, focused by magnetic fields instead of glass lenses. The electrons are accelerated through a voltage $V$, so each gains a kinetic energy $E_k = eV$ (lesson 6.4). Since $E_k = \tfrac12 mv^2 = p^2/2m$, the momentum is $p = \sqrt{2mE_k}$ and the wavelength is

<Formula label="Wavelength of electrons accelerated through a voltage V" tex="\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 3, Eq. (3.23), combined by this page with E_k = eV and E_k = p²/2m" />

<WorkedExample title="The wavelength in an electron microscope">
<Problem>
In an electron microscope, electrons are accelerated from rest through $10\ \text{kV}$. Find their wavelength and compare it with green light of $500\ \text{nm}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Kinetic energy">
$E_k = eV = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.0 \times 10^{4} = 1.6 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Momentum and wavelength">
$p = \sqrt{2 m_e E_k} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-15}} = 5.40 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{kg m/s}$, so $\lambda = h/p = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}/(5.40 \times 10^{-23}) = 1.23 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{m}$, about $0.012\ \text{nm}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Comparison">
$500\ \text{nm}/0.0123\ \text{nm} \approx 41\ 000$: the electron wave is about forty thousand times shorter than green light. (The electrons' kinetic energy, $10\ \text{keV}$, is only 2% of their rest energy of $511\ \text{keV}$, so Newton's $\tfrac12 mv^2$ is good enough here; lesson 8.3 showed when it is not.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

In practice the magnetic lenses are much less perfect than glass ones, so an electron microscope does not reach the detail its wavelength alone would allow. Even so, it shows viruses, the inside of cells and, in the best instruments, rows of single atoms.

## A wave in a short packet

A wave with one exact wavelength, like the sine waves of lesson 5.3, goes on and on with the same crests forever. As a description of a particle, it tells you the momentum exactly, $p = h/\lambda$, but nothing at all about where the particle is: it is equally likely to be anywhere.

To describe a particle that is in a definite region, we need a **wave packet**: a wave that is large in one place and zero elsewhere. By the principle of superposition (lesson 5.4) such a packet can be built by adding waves with slightly different wavelengths. Where they are in step, they add up; further away they drift out of step and cancel. The shorter the packet, the wider the range of wavelengths needed to cancel it everywhere outside.

The MIT notes make this exact. Write each wave with its wavenumber $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ (lesson 5.4). If a packet is made of waves whose wavenumbers spread over a range $\Delta k$, its length $\Delta x$ obeys

<Formula label="Width of a wave packet" tex="\Delta x\,\Delta k \approx 1" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 1, Eq. (1.12)" />

This is a fact about waves of every kind, not about quantum physics: a short burst of sound or a short radio pulse obeys it too. Quantum physics enters only through de Broglie's rule. Since $p = h/\lambda = (h/2\pi)\,k$, the momentum is $p = \hbar k$, with the **reduced Planck constant** $\hbar = h/2\pi = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$. A spread $\Delta k$ of wavenumbers is then a spread $\Delta p = \hbar\,\Delta k$ of momenta.

## The uncertainty principle

Multiplying the rule for wave packets by $\hbar$ gives $\Delta x\,\Delta p \approx \hbar$. With $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ defined precisely, as the spreads in many repeated measurements, the product can never be smaller than $\hbar/2$:

<Formula label="Heisenberg's uncertainty principle" tex="\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \frac{h}{4\pi} = \frac{\hbar}{2}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 1, Eq. (1.15), written there as Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2" />

This is Heisenberg's **uncertainty principle**. The more sharply a particle's position is fixed (small $\Delta x$), the more spread out its momentum must be (large $\Delta p$), and the other way round. It is not a complaint about poor instruments. A particle is described by a wave, and a wave simply cannot have a sharp position and a sharp wavelength at the same time.

For everyday objects the limit is far too small to matter. A speck of dust of mass $1.0 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{kg}$ whose position is known to $1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}$ needs a spread in speed of only $h/(4\pi m\,\Delta x) = 5.3 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{m/s}$: at that speed it would take about 600 years to drift one millimetre. For an electron, which is $10^{15}$ times lighter, the story is very different.

<WorkedExample title="An electron held inside an atom">
<Problem>
An electron is held inside an atom, so its position is known to about the size of the atom, $\Delta x = 1.0 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ (lesson 7.1). Find the smallest spread in its momentum and in its speed, and estimate its kinetic energy.
</Problem>
<Step title="Spread in momentum">
$\Delta p \ge h/(4\pi\,\Delta x) = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}/(4\pi \times 1.0 \times 10^{-10}) = 5.3 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{kg m/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Spread in speed">
$\Delta v = \Delta p/m_e = 5.3 \times 10^{-25}/(9.11 \times 10^{-31}) = 5.8 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m/s}$, nearly 600 kilometres per second.
</Step>
<Step title="Kinetic energy">
A momentum spread this large means the momentum itself is at least about this large, so $E_k \approx p^2/2m_e = (5.3 \times 10^{-25})^2/(2 \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31}) = 1.5 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$, about $1\ \text{eV}$. This is only a rough estimate, but it has the right size: the energies of hydrogen in lesson 7.4 are a few electronvolts, up to $13.6\ \text{eV}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

So an electron in an atom can never sit still. This also answers a puzzle from lesson 7.1, why the electron does not fall into the nucleus. Squeezing the electron into a smaller space would raise its kinetic energy (as $1/\Delta x^2$) faster than the electric attraction can supply it, so the atom settles at a size where the two balance, about $10^{-10}\ \text{m}$. Bohr's rule of lesson 7.4 has a wave picture too: the allowed orbits are those around which a whole number of electron wavelengths fits, a standing wave bent into a circle (lesson 5.4).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Uncertainty in the stars and the early universe">
Matter waves matter on the largest scales too. When a star like the Sun has used up its fuel, its core shrinks into a white dwarf, so dense that its electrons are squeezed very close together. By the uncertainty principle they then have large momenta, and the pressure this gives holds the dead star up against its own gravity. And in the very early universe, as lesson 8.18 describes, tiny quantum ripples of the same uncertain kind are thought to have been stretched to enormous size, becoming the seeds of the galaxies.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Every particle with momentum $p$ behaves as a wave with the **de Broglie wavelength** $\lambda = h/p = h/mv$. For a photon this is the usual link between momentum and wavelength.
- Wave effects show only when $\lambda$ is not much smaller than the gap or obstacle. For everyday objects $\lambda$ is absurdly small (about $10^{-34}\ \text{m}$ for a tennis ball), so they follow Newton's laws.
- Electrons diffract from the rows of atoms in a crystal (Davisson and Germer, 1927) and make fringes behind two slits, even one at a time: wave–particle duality holds for matter as well as light.
- **Electron microscopes** use electrons of wavelength $h/\sqrt{2meV}$, about $0.012\ \text{nm}$ at $10\ \text{kV}$, to see far finer detail than light allows.
- A particle in a definite region is a **wave packet**, built from a spread of wavelengths: $\Delta x\,\Delta k \approx 1$. With $p = \hbar k$ this gives Heisenberg's **uncertainty principle**, $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge h/4\pi = \hbar/2$.
- An electron confined to an atom has $\Delta p \ge 5 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{kg m/s}$ and a kinetic energy of about an electronvolt, the scale of atomic energies; it can never be at rest.
