Lesson 2.13 met the puzzle and its history in short. Stars and gas far out in spiral galaxies orbit faster than the visible matter can explain; Zwicky and Rubin found the first signs, the missing mass was called **dark matter**, and some physicists try changing the law of gravity instead. This lesson uses what Block 8 has added since: light bent by mass, particles, and the cosmic microwave background. How is a galaxy's dark matter arranged? How does bent light weigh it, and what does the Bullet Cluster show? Which particles could it be, and why must it be slow? How much of it is there? And why do cosmologists think that without it, there would be no galaxies at all, and so no us?

## Weighing a galaxy, ring by ring

Recall the main result of lesson 2.13. A star or gas cloud of mass $m$ on a circular orbit of radius $r$ needs a centripetal force $mv^2/r$ (lesson 2.9), and gravity supplies it (lesson 2.11):

<Formula label="Gravity supplies the centripetal force" tex="\frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{G\,M(r)\,m}{r^2}" cite="mit" at="eq. (22), p. 10 (written there with M)" />

There is one new detail in the notation. A galaxy's mass is spread out, and (as lesson 2.13 explained) only the mass **inside** the orbit counts. So we write it as $M(r)$: the mass inside radius $r$, which may be different for every orbit. The mass $m$ cancels, and the formula can be read two ways round:

<Formula label="Orbital speed from the mass inside" tex="v = \sqrt{\frac{G\,M(r)}{r}}" cite="mit" at="eq. (22), p. 10, solved for the speed" />

<Formula label="Mass inside an orbit from its speed" tex="M(r) = \frac{v^2\, r}{G}" cite="mit" at="eqs. (22)–(23), p. 10, solved for the mass" />

This lesson uses $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$, a light-year of $9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m}$ and a Sun mass of $1.99 \times 10^{30}\ \text{kg}$.

Now look at a spiral galaxy. Most of its light comes from the inner part. If most of its **mass** were there too, then an orbit outside that part would enclose the same $M$ as one just inside the edge, and the first formula gives $v \propto 1/\sqrt{r}$: four times farther out, half the speed. This is the **Kepler fall-off**, the same pattern as the planets, where Neptune crawls and Mercury races.

The measurements say otherwise. Astronomers measure the speeds of stars, and farther out of gas clouds, from the Doppler shift of their spectral lines (lesson 7.5). In spiral galaxies the circular speeds are typically about $200 \pm 50\ \text{km/s}$, and far out they stay **constant** instead of falling (MIT 8.902, lecture 1, p. 10). By the third formula, a constant $v$ means $M(r) \propto r$: the mass inside keeps growing in step with the radius, even beyond the stars.

<Problem>
In a spiral galaxy the rotation curve is flat at 160 km/s out to the farthest gas clouds that can be measured, 60 000 light-years from the centre. Counting the light of all its stars and its gas gives a visible mass of $2.0 \times 10^{10}$ Sun masses. (a) What mass lies inside 60 000 light-years? (b) What fraction of it is not visible? (c) How fast would the gas at 60 000 light-years move if the visible matter were all there is?
</Problem>
<Step title="Radius and speed in SI units">
$r = 60\,000 \times 9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m} = 5.68 \times 10^{20}\ \text{m}$ and $v = 160\ \text{km/s} = 1.6 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) The mass inside">
$M = \dfrac{v^2 r}{G} = \dfrac{(1.6 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m/s})^2 \times 5.68 \times 10^{20}\ \text{m}}{6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2} = 2.2 \times 10^{41}\ \text{kg}$. Divided by the Sun's mass: $2.2 \times 10^{41} / 1.99 \times 10^{30} = 1.1 \times 10^{11}$ Sun masses.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The dark fraction">
Visible: $2.0 \times 10^{10}$ of the $1.1 \times 10^{11}$ Sun masses. The rest, $1 - 2.0 \times 10^{10}/1.1 \times 10^{11}$, is about 82%: more than four fifths of the mass inside the orbit gives off no light we can see.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The speed without dark matter">
$v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM_\text{vis}}{r}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 2.0 \times 10^{10} \times 1.99 \times 10^{30}}{5.68 \times 10^{20}}} = 6.8 \times 10^{4}\ \text{m/s}$, about 68 km/s. The gas really moves more than twice as fast.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: $(\text{m/s})^2 \times \text{m} / (\text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2) = \text{m}^3\,\text{s}^{-2} \times \text{kg}^2 / (\text{kg m}^3\,\text{s}^{-2}) = \text{kg}$. And (c) agrees with (a): the speed squared scales with the mass, and $(68/160)^2 = 0.18$, the visible 18%.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Masses add, so speeds squared add

How does a galaxy's dark matter have to be arranged to give a flat curve? Split the mass inside radius $r$ into the visible part and the dark part. Because masses simply add, the squares of the speeds add too:

<Formula label="Visible and dark mass together" tex="v^2 = \frac{G\,(M_\text{vis} + M_\text{dark})}{r}" cite="mit" at="eq. (22), p. 10, with the mass inside split into two parts (this lesson's own step)" />

The graph below builds a model galaxy this way. The visible matter is a ball of stars and gas 8 000 light-years in radius; inside it the speed rises in a straight line, outside it falls as $1/\sqrt{r}$. Around it lies a large, round cloud of dark matter, a **dark halo**: little of it near the centre, and farther out a mass that grows in step with the radius.

Near the centre, the visible matter does most of the pulling. Far out, the halo takes over, and the two together keep the curve almost flat. The model is our own and kept simple, but real galaxies are fitted in the same spirit: whatever the light does not explain is put into the halo. The 8.902 notes (eq. (23), p. 10) show what a perfectly flat curve needs: a halo whose density falls off as $1/r^2$ to large radii.

## The history: clusters and rotation curves

Rotation curves were not the first sign. In 1933 the Swiss-born astronomer **Fritz Zwicky** studied the Coma Cluster, a large cluster of galaxies (NASA Science, *Dark Matter*). He found that its galaxies move so fast that the gravity of the visible matter could not hold the cluster together: it should have flown apart long ago. He proposed that the cluster holds a large amount of unseen matter, which he called *dunkle Materie*, dark matter.

In the 1970s the American astronomer **Vera Rubin** measured how fast stars move in the outer parts of spiral galaxies, and found them moving too fast to be held by the matter that can be seen (NASA Science, *Dark Matter*). She concluded that unseen matter must hold them in place. Her rotation curves, and those of others, made the problem hard to ignore. The 8.902 notes (p. 10) call the flat rotation curves one of the first hints of dark matter.

## Bending light: lensing and the Bullet Cluster

Rotation curves and cluster speeds both use orbits. A completely different method uses light. Lesson 8.8 showed that mass curves spacetime, so that light passing a galaxy or cluster bends: a **gravitational lens** turns the background galaxies into arcs, rings and multiple images. How strongly a lens bends light depends on how much mass it has and how that mass is spread across the sky (and on the distances to the lens and to the background galaxies), but not on whether the mass shines. And it does not matter how that mass moves, so lensing weighs a cluster without assuming anything about orbits (Ferreras, Sec. 2.6). Lensing maps of galaxy clusters give far more mass than their stars and gas, in agreement with the orbits.

The most striking case is the **Bullet Cluster**, about 3.8 billion light-years away, whose mass map was published in 2006 (NASA Science, *Dark Matter*). It is two clusters of galaxies that have just passed through each other. Most of the ordinary matter in a cluster is not in its galaxies but in hot gas between them, so hot that it shines in X-rays. In the collision the two gas clouds ran into each other and were slowed down, so they lag behind. The galaxies, with lots of space between them, passed through almost without touching.

Lensing then showed where the mass is: not with the gas, where most of the ordinary matter is, but with the galaxies, ahead of the gas on both sides (Ferreras, Sec. 2.6). Most of the mass passed through the collision unhindered. That is exactly what matter that feels gravity but hardly collides with anything should do. For many astronomers this is the most direct evidence that dark matter is a real substance, and not only a sign that our law of gravity fails far out in galaxies.

## What dark matter is not

Everything we know about dark matter comes from its gravity (Ferreras, Sec. 1.1). It does not absorb, reflect or give off light (NASA Science, *Dark Matter*). "Dark" here means invisible, not dark-coloured. That rules out a lot:

- **Not ordinary cold gas.** Gas that does not shine still absorbs light of particular colours from the stars behind it, so it would show up as absorption lines in their spectra (lesson 7.5), and it does not (MIT 8.902, lecture 1, p. 10). Hot gas in clusters is ordinary matter too, and it is seen, in X-rays.
- **Not, for the most part, dim stars or dark compact objects** such as burnt-out stars, planets or the black holes left by dying stars (lesson 8.9). Such objects in our galaxy's halo would act as tiny lenses, briefly brightening a star behind them. Searches that watched millions of stars for years found far too few such events (MIT 8.902, lecture 1, p. 10; Ferreras, Sec. 2.6).
- **Not neutrinos**, the very light particles of lesson 8.7. They exist, but they move so close to the speed of light that they would have smoothed out the small clumps that galaxies grew from (MIT 8.902, lecture 1, p. 10). Dark matter must be **cold**: slow-moving.
- **Not dark energy**, the subject of the next lesson. Dark energy is spread evenly through space and drives the expansion faster; dark matter clumps around galaxies and pulls.

## What might it be?

The most popular idea is that dark matter is a new kind of particle, not one of those in lesson 8.7. Physicists have proposed many candidates; these are among the leading ones (NASA Science, *Dark Matter*; Ferreras, Secs. 6.4-6.5):

- **WIMPs**, weakly interacting massive particles: heavy, slow particles that would feel gravity and perhaps the weak force, but not the electromagnetic force, so they give off no light. Telescopes look for the gamma rays that WIMPs might give off when two of them meet and destroy each other. None has been detected so far (MIT 8.902, lecture 1, p. 11).
- **Axions**: very light particles, first proposed in 1977 to solve a separate puzzle about the strong force. Telescopes have searched for them too.
- **Primordial black holes**: black holes that might have formed in the very early universe, not from stars, and so might escape the limits above.

And some physicists keep testing a different answer: that there is no dark matter, and gravity itself works differently at the tiny accelerations far out in galaxies (modified Newtonian dynamics, MOND; MIT 8.902, lecture 1, p. 11). The Bullet Cluster is one of the hardest tests for this idea (Ferreras, Ch. 7). What dark matter is remains one of the biggest open questions in physics.

## How much is there?

Measured from the whole universe, mainly from the cosmic microwave background of lesson 8.15, the shares are roughly: ordinary matter about 5%, dark matter about 27%, and dark energy about 68% (NASA Science, *Dark Matter*). Lesson 8.15 gave about 25% and 70% from WMAP's earlier maps; later, sharper measurements moved them to these values. So there is about $27/5 = 5.4$ times as much dark matter as ordinary matter: of all matter, $27/(27 + 5) = 0.84$, or 84%, is dark. Everything we have ever seen, every star, planet and gas cloud, is the small remainder. The galaxy of the worked example, 82% dark inside its orbits, fits this pattern well.

Cosmologists write the share of all matter, dark plus ordinary, as the **density parameter** $\Omega_m$. The Planck satellite's 2018 results give $\Omega_m = 0.315$ (MIT 8.902, lecture 10, p. 69), a later development than the first evidence above. That is NASA's 27% + 5% = 32% again, to within rounding.

## Dark matter built the galaxies

Dark matter does more than hold galaxies together: it is probably why they exist. When the cosmic microwave background was released (lesson 8.15), matter was spread out almost perfectly evenly: the ripples in it were only about 1 part in 100 000 ($10^{-5}$) (MIT 8.902, lecture 13, p. 86). Gravity makes such lumps grow, because a denser region pulls in more matter. But ordinary matter could only start to clump once the light had let go of it, and since then, the 8.902 notes estimate (lecture 13, p. 86), its lumps can have grown by only about a factor of 1000:

$10^{-5} \times 10^{3} = 10^{-2}$.

Lumps only one per cent denser than average are far less than what we see around us today, where galaxies and clusters are much denser than the universe on average. Ordinary matter alone could not have built them in time.

Dark matter solves this. It feels no push from light, so its lumps could start growing earlier, while the ordinary matter was still held back. When the light let go, the ordinary gas fell into the dark lumps that were already waiting, the **halos**, cooled, and formed stars and galaxies (MIT 8.902, lecture 13, p. 86). In computer simulations of the universe with cold dark matter, the structures that form look and move like the ones telescopes see; with fast-moving (hot) dark matter they do not hold together as observed (NASA Science, *Dark Matter*). Hot dark matter streams out of small clumps and smooths them away: large structures can still form, but the small clumps come too late, and so do the galaxies (Ferreras, Sec. 6.2). Nearly every galaxy, the Milky Way included, sits inside its own dark halo.

## Summary

- For a circular orbit, $v = \sqrt{GM(r)/r}$, with $M(r)$ the mass inside the orbit. Turned round, $M(r) = v^2 r/G$.
- If the mass sat where the light is, speeds far out would fall as $1/\sqrt{r}$ (the Kepler fall-off). Measured rotation curves of spiral galaxies stay **flat**, so $M(r)$ keeps growing in step with $r$ where there is little light.
- Masses add, so speeds squared add: $v^2 = G(M_\text{vis} + M_\text{dark})/r$. A dark **halo** around the visible galaxy makes the total curve flat.
- Zwicky (1933) found the galaxies of the Coma Cluster moving too fast; Rubin (1970s) found the same for stars far out in spiral galaxies.
- **Gravitational lensing** weighs clusters by bending light and finds the same extra mass. In the **Bullet Cluster**, most of the mass passed through a collision with the galaxies while the hot gas was slowed.
- Dark matter is not ordinary gas, mostly not dim stars or other compact objects, not neutrinos and not dark energy. Candidates include WIMPs, axions and primordial black holes; none has been found, and modified gravity is still tested.
- The universe is about 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter and 68% dark energy: about five times as much dark as ordinary matter.
- Dark matter clumped first, and the ordinary gas fell into its halos to form the galaxies.
