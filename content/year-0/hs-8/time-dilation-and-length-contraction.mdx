The previous lesson ended with two rules that look harmless: the laws of physics are the same for everyone moving steadily, and light always travels at $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$, whoever measures it. This lesson follows them to their first surprising result. Moving clocks tick slowly and moving objects are short, and both effects are measured every day: in particles from space and in the satellites that tell your phone where it is.

## A clock made of light

The simplest clock to think about is a **light clock**: two mirrors facing each other a distance $d$ apart, with a pulse of light bouncing between them. Every time the pulse returns to the bottom mirror, the clock ticks. The slides of MIT's relativity course point to a light-clock movie to explain time dilation (8.033 Lecture 5, slides 5 and 6); the triangle below is worked out on this page.

For someone holding the clock, the light goes straight up and straight down, a distance $2d$ at speed $c$. One tick takes

<Formula label="Tick of a light clock at rest" tex="\Delta t_0 = \frac{2d}{c}" cite="mit" at="slides 5–6 (light clock); worked out on this page" />

Now put the clock on a spaceship that moves sideways at speed $v$ past you. In your frame the mirrors move while the light is on its way, so the pulse runs along a slanted path: up to where the top mirror has got to, and down to where the bottom mirror has got to. The slanted path is longer than $2d$. By the second postulate the light still travels at $c$ in your frame, so a longer path takes a longer time.

## Pythagoras gives the factor

Call the time between two ticks in your frame $\Delta t$. In half a tick, $\Delta t/2$, three distances form a right-angled triangle:

- the light travels along the slanted side, a distance $c\,\Delta t/2$ (the hypotenuse);
- the mirrors move sideways a distance $v\,\Delta t/2$;
- the gap between the mirrors, $d$, is at right angles to the motion and is the same for both of you.

Pythagoras' theorem (lesson 0.7) gives

<Formula label="The light clock seen moving" tex="\left(\frac{c\,\Delta t}{2}\right)^2 = d^2 + \left(\frac{v\,\Delta t}{2}\right)^2" cite="mit" at="slides 5–6 (light clock); worked out on this page" />

Multiply by 4 and collect the terms with $\Delta t$: $(c^2 - v^2)\,\Delta t^2 = 4d^2$. Divide by $c^2$ and take the square root: $\Delta t = (2d/c)/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$. The top is the tick $\Delta t_0$ of the clock at rest. The factor that is left over is so important that it has its own name, the **Lorentz factor** $\gamma$ (gamma):

<Formula label="Lorentz factor" tex="\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{v^2}{c^2}}}" cite="reading1" at="slide 15" />

<Formula label="Time dilation" tex="\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t_0" cite="mit" at="slide 4, written there as Δt′ = γΔt" />

Because $v$ is less than $c$, the square root is less than 1 and $\gamma$ is more than 1. The moving clock ticks **slowly**: this is **time dilation**. It is not a fault of light clocks. By the first postulate every clock on the ship, a wristwatch, a heartbeat, an atom, must keep step with the light clock, otherwise the crew could tell that they were moving. Time itself runs slowly on the moving ship, as seen from your frame.

<WorkedExample title="A light clock on a fast ship">
<Problem>
A light clock has its mirrors $0.90\ \text{m}$ apart. It is on a spaceship that flies past the Earth at $0.60c$. How long is one tick on the ship, and as measured from the Earth?
</Problem>
<Step title="On the ship">
$\Delta t_0 = 2d/c = 2 \times 0.90/(3.00 \times 10^8) = 6.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{s}$, or 6.0 nanoseconds.
</Step>
<Step title="The Lorentz factor">
$\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.60^2} = 1/\sqrt{0.64} = 1/0.80 = 1.25$.
</Step>
<Step title="From the Earth">
$\Delta t = \gamma\Delta t_0 = 1.25 \times 6.0 = 7.5$ nanoseconds. Check with the triangle: in half a tick (3.75 ns) the light travels $1.125\ \text{m}$ and the ship moves $0.675\ \text{m}$, and indeed $\sqrt{0.675^2 + 0.90^2} = 1.125$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Proper time

Which of the two times is the "real" one? Both are, each in its own frame. But one of them is special. The **proper time** $\Delta t_0$ between two events is the time measured by a single clock that is present at both of them, so a clock for which the two events happen in the same place. The astronaut's clock is at both ticks of the light clock; you need two clocks at different places on Earth to time them. The proper time is shorter than the time between the same two events measured in any other inertial frame, where they happen at different places, and every observer agrees on its value (MIT 8.033 Lecture 5, slides 21 and 22). To use $\Delta t = \gamma\Delta t_0$ correctly, first ask: which clock is at both events?

At everyday speeds $\gamma$ is extremely close to 1. For speeds much smaller than $c$ it is almost exactly $1 + v^2/(2c^2)$. At $0.1c$ both give $1.005$. For a jet aircraft at $250\ \text{m/s}$ the difference from 1 is about $3.5 \times 10^{-13}$: no wonder nobody noticed before 1905. Only near the speed of light does the effect become large: $\gamma = 1.15$ at $0.5c$, $2.29$ at $0.9c$, and at $0.998c$ it is $15.8$.

## Muons from space

Cosmic rays, fast particles from space, hit the top of the atmosphere and make showers of new particles. Among them are **muons**, heavy cousins of the electron. A muon is unstable: its mean lifetime is $2.2\ \text{μs}$ (Particle Data Group). The mean lifetime is 1 divided by the decay constant of lesson 7.9, so the half-life is $\ln 2$ times the mean lifetime: $2.2 \times \ln 2 = 1.52\ \text{μs}$. MIT's slides pose the puzzle (Lecture 5, slide 6): even at the speed of light, a muon would cover only about half a kilometre in one half-life, yet many muons made high in the atmosphere reach the ground.

<WorkedExample title="Muons reaching the ground">
<Problem>
A batch of muons is made $12\ \text{km}$ above the ground and comes straight down at $0.998c$. What fraction is left at the ground, (a) if their clocks ran at the normal rate and (b) with time dilation?
</Problem>
<Step title="Travel time in the Earth's frame">
$t = 12\,000/(0.998 \times 3.00 \times 10^8) = 4.01 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{s}$, or $40.1\ \text{μs}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Without time dilation">
With one more digit kept (the answer is very sensitive to it), that is $40.08/1.523 = 26.3$ half-lives, and $(\tfrac12)^{26.3} = 1.2 \times 10^{-8}$: about 1 muon in 84 million would survive. Practically none.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) With time dilation">
$\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.998^2} = 15.8$, so measured from the ground the muon's half-life is $15.8 \times 1.52 = 24.0\ \text{μs}$. The trip is $40.1/24.0 = 1.67$ half-lives, and $(\tfrac12)^{1.67} = 0.31$: about a third arrive. Detectors on the ground do find muons in large numbers, as relativity predicts.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Length contraction: the muon's view

Now look at the same trip from the muon's side. In its own frame the muon is at rest and lives its normal half-life of $1.52\ \text{μs}$; it is the Earth and its air that rush upwards at $0.998c$. How can it reach the ground in only $1.67$ of its own half-lives, $2.54\ \text{μs}$? In that time the ground moves towards it only about $0.76\ \text{km}$.

Both frames must agree on what happens: either the muon reaches the ground or it does not. So for the muon the atmosphere must be only about $0.76\ \text{km}$ thick. A moving object is shorter along its direction of motion, by the same factor $\gamma$:

<Formula label="Length contraction" tex="L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma}" cite="mit" at="slides 12–13" />

Here $L_0$ is the **rest length**, measured in the frame where the object is at rest (the 12 km of air, measured on Earth), and $L$ is its length measured by someone it moves past. For the muon, $L = 12/15.8 = 0.76\ \text{km}$. Lengths at right angles to the motion do not change: that is why the gap $d$ of the light clock was the same for both observers. The ground observer explains the survival by slow clocks, the muon by a short atmosphere, and both get the same answer. (The university course shows why the two descriptions fit together: the frames disagree about which events happen at the same time.)

## GPS needs relativity

The satellites of the Global Positioning System carry atomic clocks, and your phone finds its position from the times their signals take to arrive. Light travels $300\ \text{m}$ in a microsecond, so a clock error of one microsecond already means an error of hundreds of metres. A 2012 NASA Goddard presentation on GPS (Moreau) describes circular orbits about $20\,200\ \text{km}$ above the ground, each taking $11$ hours $58$ minutes.

<WorkedExample title="How slow is a GPS clock?">
<Problem>
Using the Earth's radius of $6371\ \text{km}$, estimate the speed of a GPS satellite and how much its clock falls behind in one day because of special relativity.
</Problem>
<Step title="Speed on the orbit">
$r = 6371 + 20\,200 = 26\,571\ \text{km}$ and $T = 11 \times 3600 + 58 \times 60 = 43\,080\ \text{s}$, so $v = 2\pi r/T = 2\pi \times 2.6571 \times 10^7/43\,080 = 3.88 \times 10^3\ \text{m/s}$ (lesson 1.10).
</Step>
<Step title="The Lorentz factor">
$v/c = 1.29 \times 10^{-5}$, far too small for a calculator to show $\gamma$ directly. Use $\gamma \approx 1 + v^2/(2c^2)$: $\gamma - 1 = 8.3 \times 10^{-11}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Lost time per day">
In one day ($86\,400\ \text{s}$) the satellite clock falls behind by $8.3 \times 10^{-11} \times 86\,400 = 7.2 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{s}$, about $7\ \text{μs}$. Uncorrected, that alone would add a ranging error of about $7.2 \times 10^{-6} \times 3.00 \times 10^8 = 2.2\ \text{km}$ every day.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Special relativity is not the whole story. Gravity also changes the rate of clocks, an effect of general relativity (lesson 8.8): a clock higher up, where gravity is weaker, runs fast. For GPS satellites this wins. The same presentation gives the size of the net effect: without a correction, their clocks would gain about $38\ \text{μs}$ per day on clocks on the ground, so the gravity effect must be about $38 + 7 = 45\ \text{μs}$ per day the other way. The satellite clocks are therefore set to tick slightly slowly before launch. NASA's "10 Things Einstein Got Right" puts it simply: without relativity built in, your phone would send you kilometres off course.

## The twin paradox

Time dilation leads to the most famous puzzle in relativity, which MIT's slide 6 mentions straight after the muons. One of two twins flies to a distant star at high speed and comes back; the other stays at home. The traveller's clocks, including her body, run slowly as seen from Earth, so she comes back younger. For example, a trip at $0.60c$ that takes $20$ years on Earth takes $20/1.25 = 16$ years for the traveller.

The paradox: from the rocket, the Earth is the one moving, so should the twin at home not be the younger one? No, because the two twins are not in the same situation. The twin at home stays in one steady frame the whole time. The traveller does not: to come back she must slow down, turn round and speed up again, changing from the outgoing frame to the returning one. The postulates only say that steady frames are equivalent, and only the stay-at-home twin is in one throughout. Careful working out in either frame gives the same answer: the traveller is younger. The fast muons of this lesson are a small-scale version of the same effect: their clocks, too, record less time than the clocks they pass.

## Summary

- A light clock shows with Pythagoras that a clock moving at speed $v$ ticks slowly: $\Delta t = \gamma\Delta t_0$, with the Lorentz factor $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$.
- The **proper time** $\Delta t_0$ is measured by one clock present at both events; in every other inertial frame the time between the same two events is longer.
- $\gamma$ is almost exactly 1 at everyday speeds and grows without limit as $v$ approaches $c$.
- Cosmic-ray muons reach the ground because their clocks run slow as seen from the Earth; seen by the muon, the atmosphere is contracted: $L = L_0/\gamma$, with $L_0$ the rest length. Lengths at right angles to the motion do not change.
- GPS satellite clocks run slow by about $7\ \text{μs}$ a day from their speed and fast by more from weaker gravity; without correction, positions would drift by kilometres a day.
- In the twin paradox the traveller ages less, because only she changes frames to turn round.
