Lesson 7.7 ended with a puzzle: weigh a nucleus very precisely and it has slightly less mass than the protons and neutrons it is made of. Lesson 8.3 gave the key: mass is a form of energy, and a mass $m$ holds a rest energy $E = mc^2$. This lesson puts the two together. The missing mass tells us how tightly each nucleus is held together, and one graph of that tightness explains both how nuclear power stations work and why the Sun shines.

## Mass defect and binding energy

Take a nucleus with $Z$ protons and $N$ neutrons, and compare its mass $M$ with the mass its parts would have as free particles. The difference is the **mass defect**:

<Formula label="Mass defect" tex="\Delta m = Z\,m_p + N\,m_n - M" cite="mit" at="Sec. 1.2.1, p. 6" />

Here $M$ is the mass of the bare nucleus, without its electrons. (Tables often give the mass of the whole atom; then the mass of the $Z$ electrons must be taken off first.)

The mass defect is always positive for a nucleus that holds together. To pull the nucleus apart into free nucleons you have to do work against the strong force, and that work becomes the extra rest energy of the free particles. Turn it around: when free nucleons come together into a nucleus, this energy is given out, and the nucleus is left lighter. The energy is the **binding energy**:

<Formula label="Binding energy" tex="E_b = \Delta m\,c^2" cite="mit" at="Sec. 1.2.1, p. 6" />

## A handy unit: MeV per u

Nuclear masses are given in atomic mass units (lesson 7.7), and nuclear energies in mega-electronvolts: $1\ \text{MeV} = 10^6\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$ (lesson 8.3). One conversion factor links the two:

<Formula label="Energy of one atomic mass unit" tex="1\ \text{u} \times c^2 = 931.5\ \text{MeV}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 1.1.2, p. 6 (the notes give 931.502 MeV)" />

You can check it with $E = mc^2$: $1.66 \times 10^{-27} \times (3.00 \times 10^8)^2 = 1.49 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{J}$, and dividing by $1.6 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$ gives $934\ \text{MeV}$. The small difference comes from rounding the three constants; with their precise values the answer is $931.5\ \text{MeV}$. So a mass defect in u, times 931.5, is a binding energy in MeV.

<WorkedExample title="The binding energy of helium-4">
<Problem>
A helium-4 nucleus (2 protons, 2 neutrons) has a mass of $4.00151\ \text{u}$. A free proton has $1.00728\ \text{u}$ and a free neutron $1.00866\ \text{u}$ (NIST values, rounded). Find the mass defect, the binding energy, and the binding energy per nucleon.
</Problem>
<Step title="Mass of the parts">
$2 \times 1.00728 + 2 \times 1.00866 = 4.03188\ \text{u}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Mass defect">
$\Delta m = 4.03188 - 4.00151 = 0.03037\ \text{u}$: the nucleus is 0.75% lighter than its parts.
</Step>
<Step title="Binding energy">
$E_b = 0.03037 \times 931.5 = 28.3\ \text{MeV}$, or $4.5 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Per nucleon">
$28.3/4 = 7.07\ \text{MeV}$ per nucleon. This is an average over the four nucleons: removing just one of them from helium-4 costs a different amount.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Compare this with the atom. Freeing the electron of hydrogen takes $13.6\ \text{eV}$ (lesson 7.5); pulling helium-4 apart takes about 2 million times as much. That ratio is the difference between chemistry, which rearranges electrons, and nuclear physics.

## The binding energy curve

The curve has a simple explanation if you picture a nucleus as a drop of liquid (MIT 22.02 notes, Sec. 1.2.2, pp. 7–8):

- **Rising part.** The strong force reaches only the nearest neighbours (lesson 7.7). A nucleon inside a nucleus is pulled from all sides, but one at the surface has fewer neighbours. In a small nucleus a large share of the nucleons sit at the surface, so it is loosely bound per nucleon. Helium-4 is a special case: its two protons and two neutrons fit together unusually well, which is why it sits high above the curve.
- **The peak.** Around iron-56 and nickel-62, at almost $8.8\ \text{MeV}$ per nucleon, nuclei are as tightly bound as they can be.
- **Falling part.** Every proton repels every other proton, not just its neighbours. The repulsion grows roughly as $Z^2$, while the strong attraction grows only as $A$ (MIT 22.02 notes, Sec. 1.3.1, p. 11), so in heavy nuclei it eats more and more of the binding.

## Two ways to release energy

Any reaction that makes the products *more* tightly bound releases energy. The extra binding energy appears as kinetic energy of the products, and since energy and mass go together, the products have less mass than what went in. The energy released is called $Q$:

<Formula label="Energy released by a nuclear reaction" tex="Q = \left(m_{\text{before}} - m_{\text{after}}\right)c^2" cite="reading4" at="p. 2" />

Equivalently, $Q$ is the total binding energy after the reaction minus the total binding energy before it. The curve shows two ways to gain binding energy, both moving towards the peak (8.033 Lecture 13, slide 30; 22.01 Lecture 2, p. 8):

- **Fission**: split a heavy nucleus, on the falling right-hand side, into two medium-sized ones near the peak.
- **Fusion**: join light nuclei, on the steep left-hand side, into a heavier one.

Near iron neither works: splitting iron or joining iron nuclei both cost energy. That is why iron is the end of the line for the nuclear fires inside stars.

## Fission of uranium-235

Uranium-235 splits when it absorbs a neutron. The neutron joins the nucleus to make a very short-lived, wobbling uranium-236, which breaks into two medium-sized nuclei and gives off two or three neutrons, on average about 2.4 (MIT 22.01 Lecture 20, pp. 1–4). Many different pairs of fragments are possible; one of them is

<Formula label="One way uranium-235 can split" tex="\mathrm{n} + {}^{235}_{92}\mathrm{U} \to {}^{140}_{54}\mathrm{Xe} + {}^{94}_{38}\mathrm{Sr} + 2\,\mathrm{n}" cite="reading6" at="pp. 1–2 (the general fission reaction; this particular split is the page's own example)" />

Check the counting: the numbers of nucleons ($1 + 235 = 140 + 94 + 2$) and of protons ($92 = 54 + 38$) are the same on both sides. Slow neutrons, with energies of only about $0.025\ \text{eV}$, are the most likely to cause fission (MIT 22.01 Lecture 7, p. 12).

<WorkedExample title="Energy from one fission">
<Problem>
Use the table of binding energies per nucleon to estimate the energy released when uranium-235 splits into xenon-140, strontium-94 and two neutrons.
</Problem>
<Step title="Binding energy before">
A free neutron has no binding energy, so before the split there is only uranium-235: $235 \times 7.59 = 1783.7\ \text{MeV}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Binding energy after">
Xenon-140: $140 \times 8.29 = 1160.6\ \text{MeV}$. Strontium-94: $94 \times 8.59 = 807.5\ \text{MeV}$. Together $1968.1\ \text{MeV}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy released">
$Q = 1968.1 - 1783.7 = 184\ \text{MeV}$, mostly as kinetic energy of the two fragments, which fly apart and heat whatever they hit.
</Step>
<Step title="And afterwards">
Both fragments have too many neutrons to be stable, so they go on to decay (lesson 7.8), adding more energy. Altogether one fission gives about $200\ \text{MeV}$ (MIT 22.01 Lecture 7, p. 12): some 15 million times the $13.6\ \text{eV}$ of the hydrogen atom.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Chain reactions and reactors

The neutrons freed by one fission can split more uranium-235 nuclei, and theirs can split more again: a **chain reaction**. What happens depends on how many of the new neutrons, on average, cause a further fission:

- more than one: the number of fissions grows rapidly, generation after generation;
- exactly one: the reaction runs at a steady rate;
- less than one: it dies out.

A nuclear reactor keeps this number at exactly one (MIT 22.01 Lectures 3, 7 and 16):

- **Fuel.** Natural uranium is mostly uranium-238 (lesson 7.7), so most reactors use fuel enriched in uranium-235.
- **Moderator.** Water slows the fast new neutrons down: a neutron bouncing off the hydrogen nuclei in water loses energy quickly, and slow neutrons are the best at causing fission.
- **Control rods.** Rods containing boron, which absorbs neutrons, are pushed in or pulled out to take away the spare neutrons and keep the reaction steady.
- **Turning heat into electricity.** The fission fragments heat the fuel, the heat boils water, and the steam spins a turbine that drives a generator (lesson 6.11).

The fragments left in the used fuel are strongly radioactive (lesson 7.8), which is why it must be stored safely for a long time.

<WorkedExample title="How much fuel does a reactor use?">
<Problem>
A reactor core produces $3.0\ \text{GW}$ of heat, and each fission gives $200\ \text{MeV}$. How many fissions happen each second, and what mass of uranium-235 is used up in a day?
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy per fission in joules">
$200\ \text{MeV} = 200 \times 10^6 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3.2 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Fissions per second">
$3.0 \times 10^9 / 3.2 \times 10^{-11} = 9.4 \times 10^{19}$ fissions per second.
</Step>
<Step title="Mass per day">
A day has $86\,400\ \text{s}$, and each uranium-235 nucleus has a mass of about $235 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 3.90 \times 10^{-25}\ \text{kg}$. So $9.4 \times 10^{19} \times 86\,400 \times 3.90 \times 10^{-25} = 3.2\ \text{kg}$ a day. A coal-fired plant giving the same heat would burn millions of times more fuel: in MIT 22.01 Lecture 16, a teaching assistant puts fission fuel at about 3.5 million times the energy of the same mass of coal.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Fusion of hydrogen isotopes

At the other end of the curve, light nuclei release energy by joining. The reaction planned for fusion power stations joins deuterium and tritium, the two heavy isotopes of hydrogen (lesson 7.7), into helium-4 and a neutron (MIT 22.01 Lecture 2, p. 7):

<Formula label="Deuterium–tritium fusion" tex="{}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H} + {}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H} \to {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} + \mathrm{n}" cite="reading2" at="p. 7" />

<WorkedExample title="Energy from one deuterium–tritium fusion">
<Problem>
The nuclear masses are: deuterium $2.01355\ \text{u}$, tritium $3.01550\ \text{u}$, helium-4 $4.00151\ \text{u}$, neutron $1.00866\ \text{u}$ (NIST values, rounded). How much energy does one fusion release? Compare it, per unit of mass of fuel, with fission.
</Problem>
<Step title="Mass before and after">
Before: $2.01355 + 3.01550 = 5.02905\ \text{u}$. After: $4.00151 + 1.00866 = 5.01017\ \text{u}$. The mass lost is $0.01888\ \text{u}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy released">
$Q = 0.01888 \times 931.5 = 17.6\ \text{MeV}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Per unit of mass">
Fusion: $17.6\ \text{MeV}$ from $5\ \text{u}$ of fuel, about $3.5\ \text{MeV}$ per u. Fission: $200\ \text{MeV}$ from $236\ \text{u}$ (uranium-235 plus its neutron), about $0.85\ \text{MeV}$ per u. Kilogram for kilogram, this fusion gives about 4 times as much energy as fission.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why fusion needs a fierce heat

Fission is started by a neutron, which has no charge and slips into a nucleus easily. Fusion is harder: both nuclei are positive, and they repel each other (lesson 6.2). They must come within a few femtometres, close enough for the strong force to grab them, and the electric potential energy at that distance is $U = ke^2/r$ (lesson 6.4):

<Formula label="Electric potential energy of two charges e at distance r" tex="U = \frac{k e^2}{r}" cite="mit802ch3" at="Eq. (3.3.5), with both charges equal to e" />

<WorkedExample title="The electric barrier for deuterium and tritium">
<Problem>
Using $R = R_0A^{1/3}$ with $R_0 = 1.2\ \text{fm}$ (lesson 7.7), estimate how close deuterium and tritium nuclei must come to touch, the electric energy they need for that, and the temperature at which a typical nucleus in a gas has that much kinetic energy, $\tfrac32 k_B T$ (lesson 4.4, with $k_B = 1.38 \times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$).
</Problem>
<Step title="Touching distance">
Deuterium: $1.2 \times 2^{1/3} = 1.51\ \text{fm}$. Tritium: $1.2 \times 3^{1/3} = 1.73\ \text{fm}$. Touching, their centres are $r = 3.24\ \text{fm}$ apart.
</Step>
<Step title="Electric energy">
$U = 9.0 \times 10^9 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2 / 3.24 \times 10^{-15} = 7.1 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{J}$, about $0.44\ \text{MeV}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Temperature">
$\tfrac32 k_B T = U$ gives $T = 2U/(3k_B) = 2 \times 7.1 \times 10^{-14}/(3 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23}) = 3.4 \times 10^9\ \text{K}$: billions of kelvin.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

In practice fusion starts at lower temperatures than this estimate. A gas always has some nuclei moving much faster than the average, and quantum physics lets two nuclei now and then slip through the electric barrier without quite enough energy. Even so, the fuel of a fusion reactor is a plasma (bare nuclei and free electrons) at many millions of kelvin, far too hot to touch any wall, so it is held in a ring-shaped vacuum chamber by strong magnetic fields (MIT 22.01 Lecture 2, pp. 7 and 24). Nature found an easier way: in the core of a star, gravity squeezes and heats a huge ball of hydrogen, and the fusion that results is the subject of lesson 8.5, how stars shine.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Where the elements come from">
In the first few minutes after the Big Bang, the whole universe was hot and dense enough for fusion. Protons and neutrons fused into deuterium and then into helium-4, the tightly bound nucleus high on the left of the curve, but the universe cooled and thinned too fast to go much further. Heavier elements were made much later, by fusion inside stars, which can build nuclei up the curve as far as the iron peak but no further (MIT 22.01 Lecture 2, p. 8). The same curve that runs power stations tells us where the atoms around us were made.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A nucleus has less mass than its free nucleons. The **mass defect** is $\Delta m = Z m_p + N m_n - M$, and the **binding energy** $E_b = \Delta m\,c^2$ is the energy needed to pull the nucleus apart.
- $1\ \text{u}$ of mass is worth $931.5\ \text{MeV}$. Helium-4: $\Delta m = 0.03037\ \text{u}$, $E_b = 28.3\ \text{MeV}$, $7.07\ \text{MeV}$ per nucleon, millions of times the energies of chemistry.
- The **binding energy per nucleon** rises steeply for light nuclei (surface nucleons have fewer neighbours), peaks near iron-56 and nickel-62 at almost $8.8\ \text{MeV}$, and falls slowly for heavy nuclei (the repulsion of the protons).
- Reactions that move towards the peak release energy $Q = (m_{\text{before}} - m_{\text{after}})c^2$: **fission** of heavy nuclei and **fusion** of light ones.
- **Fission**: a slow neutron splits uranium-235 into two fragments and 2 or 3 neutrons, releasing about $200\ \text{MeV}$. The neutrons can start a **chain reaction**; a reactor keeps it steady with a moderator and control rods and turns the heat into electricity.
- **Fusion**: ${}^{2}\mathrm{H} + {}^{3}\mathrm{H} \to {}^{4}\mathrm{He} + \mathrm{n}$ releases $17.6\ \text{MeV}$, about 4 times more per kilogram than fission, but the nuclei must overcome their electric repulsion (about $0.44\ \text{MeV}$), which needs a plasma at many millions of kelvin.
