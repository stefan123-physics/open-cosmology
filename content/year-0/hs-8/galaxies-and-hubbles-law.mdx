In lesson 5.13 you met Hubble's law as a one-line result: distant galaxies are redshifted, and the farther away, the faster they recede. Lesson 7.11 showed how that redshift is read from the lines in a galaxy's light, and lesson 8.10 how astronomers measure the distances. This lesson puts the pieces together. First: what is a galaxy, and how did we learn that there are others besides our own? Then: how is a Hubble diagram built, what does its straight line tell us, and why does it **not** mean that we sit at the centre of the universe?

## Our galaxy, the Milky Way

On a dark night, far from city lights, a faint band of light crosses the sky: the **Milky Way**. Through a telescope it breaks up into countless stars. It is our own **galaxy**, seen from the inside: a huge system of stars, gas and dust, held together by gravity.

The Milky Way is a **spiral galaxy**. Most of its stars lie in a flat disk more than 100,000 light-years across, with spiral arms winding out from a central bulge (NASA Science, *Galaxies*). The Sun lies in that disk, about halfway out from the centre along one of the arms. Seen from inside the disk, the many distant stars in it merge into the band across the sky. Like a planet around the Sun, the Sun orbits the centre of the galaxy, and one orbit takes about 240 million years.

<WorkedExample title="How fast does the Sun go round the galaxy?">
<Problem>
Take the disk to be about $100\,000$ light-years across, so the Sun, about halfway out, is roughly $25\,000$ light-years from the centre. One orbit takes about $240$ million years. Estimate the Sun's speed around the centre of the galaxy. (A light-year is $9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m}$, a year $3.16 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s}$.)
</Problem>
<Step title="The length of one orbit">
For a circle (lesson 1.10) the distance once round is $2\pi r = 2\pi \times 25\,000 \times 9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m} = 1.49 \times 10^{21}\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The time">
$240 \times 10^{6} \times 3.16 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s} = 7.6 \times 10^{15}\ \text{s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The speed">
$v = \dfrac{1.49 \times 10^{21}\ \text{m}}{7.6 \times 10^{15}\ \text{s}} = 2.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m/s}$, about $200\ \text{km/s}$. We all race through the galaxy at roughly that speed, without feeling a thing.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Keep that number in mind: galaxies, and the stars in them, move at a few hundred kilometres per second through their surroundings. It will matter below.

The Milky Way is not alone. With more than 50 smaller and larger galaxies around it, it forms the **Local Group**. The largest of its neighbours is the **Andromeda galaxy**, about 2.5 million light-years away (NASA Science, *Messier 31*), which you can just see with the naked eye as a faint smudge. Beyond the Local Group, galaxies come in groups, in **clusters** of up to thousands of galaxies, and clusters gather into even larger **superclusters**.

## Spirals, ellipticals and irregulars

Galaxies range from dwarfs of a few thousand stars, a few hundred light-years across, to giants of trillions of stars that span more than a million light-years (NASA Science, *Galaxies*). By their shape, astronomers sort them into a few main types (MIT 8.902, lecture 1, p. 9; NASA Science, *Galaxy Types*):

- **Spiral galaxies** have a flat, rotating disk of stars and gas with spiral arms, and a bulge in the middle. New stars keep forming from the gas in the arms, so spirals contain many young, hot stars and look bluish (lesson 7.6 showed that hot stars are blue). Many spirals, the Milky Way and Andromeda among them, have a **bar** of stars across the centre.
- **Elliptical galaxies** are round to oval balls of stars, with hardly any gas or dust left to make new stars. Their stars are mostly old and cool, so they look reddish. Their stars do not move in one neat disk but swarm in all directions. They are common in the crowded centres of clusters, and may form when galaxies collide and merge.
- **Lenticular galaxies** sit in between: a disk and a bulge like a spiral, but no arms and little gas, like an elliptical.
- **Irregular galaxies** have no regular shape at all, often because a neighbour has pulled them out of shape. They usually contain gas, dust and both young and old stars.

So the colour of a galaxy, read with the tools of Block 7, tells you a lot about its history: blue means stars are still being born, red means that star formation stopped long ago.

## Island universes

A hundred years ago, nobody knew whether other galaxies existed at all. Telescopes showed many faint, fuzzy patches of light, the **nebulae**, some of them with a spiral shape. Were they clouds of gas inside the Milky Way, or whole "island universes" like our own, far away? Most astronomers then took the Milky Way to be the whole universe. In 1920 Harlow Shapley and Heber Curtis argued the question in a famous public debate, without settling it (NASA Science, *Hubble Views the Star that Changed the Universe*).

The answer needed a distance, and lesson 8.10 showed how to get one: a **Cepheid** variable star, whose period of pulsation gives its true brightness. On the night of 5 October 1923, Edwin Hubble photographed the Andromeda nebula with the 100-inch Hooker telescope on Mount Wilson in California. On the plate he found a star that changed in brightness, now called V1. Its brightness rose and fell with a period of 31.4 days: a Cepheid. From its period and how faint it looked, Hubble found a distance of about 1 million light-years.

That is about ten times the size of the whole disk of the Milky Way, so Andromeda had to lie far outside it: a galaxy of its own. (Modern measurements put Andromeda even farther away, at 2.5 million light-years, but the conclusion stands.) Hubble went on to find Cepheids in other nebulae. The universe was suddenly far larger than anyone had thought, and filled with galaxies.

## The Hubble diagram

Lesson 7.11 told how this led to the expanding universe: from 1912 Vesto Slipher found that almost all spiral nebulae are redshifted, and in 1927 Lemaître and in 1929 Hubble linked their speeds to their distances. Here we look at how such a result is actually obtained. For each galaxy you need two separate measurements.

The **speed** comes from the spectrum. The lines of hydrogen, calcium and other elements are found at longer wavelengths than in the laboratory, all stretched by the same fraction, the redshift $z$ (lesson 5.13):

<Formula label="The redshift" tex="z = \frac{\lambda_\text{obs} - \lambda_\text{em}}{\lambda_\text{em}}" cite="reading3" at="eq. (24), p. 11" />

For speeds much smaller than the speed of light, $v \approx cz$.

The **distance** comes from a standard candle such as a Cepheid (lesson 8.10). It has nothing to do with the spectrum, which is what makes the comparison a real test.

Now plot every galaxy as a point: distance along the horizontal axis, speed up the vertical axis. This is a **Hubble diagram**. On average the points lie along a straight line through the origin (MIT 8.286, lecture 4, transcript p. 7):

<Formula label="Hubble's law" tex="v = H_0\,d" cite="reading1" at="slide 1 (written there as v = Hr)" />

The slope of the line is the **Hubble constant** $H_0$. Astronomers measure speeds in km/s and distances in megaparsecs (lesson 8.10; $1\ \text{Mpc}$ is about $3.26$ million light-years), so $H_0$ comes in km/s per Mpc. Today's measurements give values close to $70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$ (MIT 8.902, lecture 10, p. 62), the round value this course uses: for every megaparsec of distance, a galaxy recedes about $70\ \text{km/s}$ faster.

<WorkedExample title="From one spectrum to a distance">
<Problem>
In the spectrum of a faint galaxy the red hydrogen line, $656.3\ \text{nm}$ in the laboratory, is found at $667.8\ \text{nm}$. Its distance is too large to see single Cepheids. How far away is it? Take $H_0 = 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The redshift">
$z = \dfrac{667.8 - 656.3}{656.3} = \dfrac{11.5}{656.3} = 0.0175$.
</Step>
<Step title="The speed">
$z$ is small, so $v \approx cz = 0.0175 \times 3.00 \times 10^{5}\ \text{km/s} = 5250\ \text{km/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The distance">
Turn Hubble's law round: $d = \dfrac{v}{H_0} = \dfrac{5250}{70}\ \text{Mpc} = 75\ \text{Mpc}$, or about $245$ million light-years.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

This is how Hubble's law is used every day: once $H_0$ is known from galaxies whose distances were measured, one spectrum is enough to find the distance of any far galaxy. That is why it is often called the last step of the cosmic distance ladder.

## On average, not exactly

Hubble's own diagram of 1929 looked far less tidy than the line above. As Guth points out in MIT 8.286 (transcript pp. 8–9), Hubble's speeds only reached about $1000\ \text{km/s}$, the points were scattered, and he found a slope of $500\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$. That is about seven times today's value. Over the following decades the measured values came down steadily, and only around 2001, when the Hubble Space Telescope found Cepheids in more distant galaxies and so calibrated the distances much better, did they become precise (transcript p. 10).

Why the scatter? Every galaxy has its own motion on top of the expansion. The Sun goes round the galaxy at about $200\ \text{km/s}$, as you worked out above, and the Milky Way itself moves too. Hubble and Lemaître both had to estimate the Sun's own motion and subtract it, about $300\ \text{km/s}$ in their estimates, a large correction when the fastest galaxies went at only $1000\ \text{km/s}$. Galaxies pull on their neighbours by gravity, which gives them speeds of a few hundred km/s in every direction.

The nearest big galaxy shows this most clearly. Andromeda is not moving away at all: it comes **towards** us at about $110\ \text{km/s}$ (NASA Science, *Milky Way is Destined for Head-On Collision*), a blueshift. Its distance, 2.5 million light-years, is only $0.77\ \text{Mpc}$, so Hubble's law would give it just $70 \times 0.77 = 54\ \text{km/s}$ away from us. The pull between the two galaxies wins easily. In about 4 billion years they will collide, and the pair is expected to end up as one large elliptical galaxy.

Far away it is the other way round. For the galaxy of the worked example, $75\ \text{Mpc}$ away, the Hubble speed is $5250\ \text{km/s}$, and an extra $300\ \text{km/s}$ of its own motion changes it by only about 6 %. So Hubble's law describes galaxies **on average**, and it shows best at large distances.

## Every galaxy sees the same law

Everything seems to rush away from us. Does that put the Milky Way at the centre of the universe? Guth calls this the naive reading of Hubble's law (transcript p. 16), and it is wrong. Velocity is always measured relative to something (lesson 1.9), and "at rest" just means "at rest relative to me". An astronomer in another galaxy has every right to call their own galaxy at rest. Let us see what they find.

<WorkedExample title="Hubble's law from another galaxy">
<Problem>
Put four galaxies on one line, with distances from the Milky Way (positive to the right) and velocities from Hubble's law, $v = 70\ \text{km/s/Mpc} \times d$: galaxy R at $-30\ \text{Mpc}$ ($-2100\ \text{km/s}$, moving to the left), the Milky Way at $0$, galaxy P at $20\ \text{Mpc}$ ($1400\ \text{km/s}$) and galaxy Q at $50\ \text{Mpc}$ ($3500\ \text{km/s}$). What does an astronomer in galaxy P measure?
</Problem>
<Step title="Change to P's point of view">
To make P stand still, subtract P's velocity, $1400\ \text{km/s}$, from every velocity (lesson 1.9), and measure every distance from P, so subtract $20\ \text{Mpc}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Galaxy Q">
Distance $50 - 20 = 30\ \text{Mpc}$, velocity $3500 - 1400 = 2100\ \text{km/s}$, moving away. And $70 \times 30 = 2100$: Hubble's law.
</Step>
<Step title="The Milky Way and galaxy R">
The Milky Way: $-20\ \text{Mpc}$ and $0 - 1400 = -1400\ \text{km/s}$, so $20\ \text{Mpc}$ away on the left and moving further left, away from P, at $70 \times 20$. Galaxy R: $-30 - 20 = -50\ \text{Mpc}$ and $-2100 - 1400 = -3500\ \text{km/s}$, again $70 \times 50$ and moving away.
</Step>
<Step title="The conclusion">
From P every galaxy recedes, at a speed in proportion to its distance from P, with the same $H_0$. The astronomer in P sees exactly what we see, and could just as well think that P is the centre.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

This works only because the law is a straight line through the origin. Suppose instead the speed grew with the **square** of the distance, say $400\ \text{km/s}$ at $20\ \text{Mpc}$ and $2500\ \text{km/s}$ at $50\ \text{Mpc}$. From P, galaxy Q would then be $30\ \text{Mpc}$ away and recede at $2500 - 400 = 2100\ \text{km/s}$, while the same law used around P would predict only $900\ \text{km/s}$ at that distance. In such a universe, the law would look different from every galaxy, and one place really would be special. The university notes turn this around (MIT 8.902, lecture 10, p. 62): if the universe looks the same from every galaxy, the expansion **must** follow a straight-line law, $v = H_0 d$.

## A rising loaf of raisin bread

A good picture of this uniform expansion is a loaf of raisin bread rising in the oven. As the dough swells, every distance between raisins grows by the same factor. Two raisins that were 1 cm apart end up 2 cm apart; two that were 3 cm apart end up 6 cm apart. So in the same time, the farther raisin has moved away three times as far: three times as fast. Every raisin sees all the others recede at speeds in proportion to their distance, and no raisin is at the centre of the pattern.

Guth describes the same idea as a photographic blow-up (transcript pp. 16–17): a picture of a region of the universe at a later time looks like an enlarged copy of the earlier one. With one important exception: the galaxies themselves do **not** grow. Like the raisins, they keep their size, because gravity holds each galaxy together. Only the distances between galaxies (in fact between groups and clusters, which are also held together) increase.

A second picture uses a balloon with dots drawn on it. Blow it up and every dot moves away from every other dot, and no dot on the surface is the centre. The balloon also shows two things the loaf cannot: there is no edge to fall off, and the expansion is not into some empty space around it. The universe is not a bomb exploding outwards from one point into empty space. Every distance between galaxies grows, everywhere at once.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="One number for the whole universe">
Uniform expansion means that every distance between galaxies is multiplied by the same factor as time goes on. MIT 8.286 (lecture 4) writes that factor as one function of time, the **scale factor** $a(t)$, and shows that Hubble's law follows from it automatically; the redshift of lesson 5.13 then measures how much $a(t)$ has grown while the light travelled. MIT 8.902 (lecture 10) proves that a universe that looks the same everywhere and in every direction must expand according to $v = H_0 d$. The next lesson uses $H_0$ to estimate how old the universe is.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A **galaxy** is a huge system of stars, gas and dust held together by gravity. Ours, the **Milky Way**, is a barred spiral more than 100,000 light-years across; the Sun orbits its centre at about $200\ \text{km/s}$, once every 240 million years.
- The main types are **spirals** (rotating disk with arms, gas and young blue stars), **ellipticals** (round to oval, little gas, old red stars), **lenticulars** in between, and **irregulars**.
- In 1923 Hubble found a Cepheid in the Andromeda "nebula" and showed that it lies far outside the Milky Way: the spiral nebulae are galaxies of their own.
- A **Hubble diagram** plots each galaxy's speed, from its redshift ($v \approx cz$), against its distance, from standard candles. On average the points lie on the straight line $v = H_0\,d$, with $H_0$ close to $70\ \text{km/s/Mpc}$.
- Galaxies also have their own motions of a few hundred km/s, so the law holds on average and shows best far away; nearby Andromeda even approaches us.
- Seen from any galaxy, the others recede in the same way, so Hubble's law puts no galaxy at the centre. The universe expands as a whole, like a rising raisin loaf, while the galaxies themselves keep their size.
