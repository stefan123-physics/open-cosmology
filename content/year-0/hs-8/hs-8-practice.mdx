This set practises all nineteen lessons of Block 8, from Einstein's postulates to inflation. The problems are our own, of the kinds found in MIT's relativity, quantum, nuclear and cosmology courses and, for photons and electronvolts, in the exercises of Siyavula's chapters; the hints and solutions are our own too. Use $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$, $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$ (so $1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$), $h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$, $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$, $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$, $m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, $1\ \text{u} \times c^2 = 931.5\ \text{MeV}$, the Sun's mass $1.99 \times 10^{30}\ \text{kg}$, one light-year $9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m}$, one parsec $3.086 \times 10^{16}\ \text{m}$ and one year $3.156 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s}$, unless a problem gives another value.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box wants the number only, in the unit printed after the box, for example `0.25` or `1.2e5`; small differences from rounding are accepted. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `(a + b)/c`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A fast space probe" points={12}>

A robot probe flies past the Earth in a straight line at $v = 0.96c$. Before launch, at rest, it was $50.0\ \text{m}$ long.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

The probe shines a laser beam straight ahead. How fast does an observer on the Earth measure that light to travel?

<Answer id="p1-a" label="The light travels at:"
  choices={[
    "1.96c: the speed of the probe plus the speed of light",
    "c, the same as for any other source",
    "0.04c: the speed of light minus the speed of the probe",
    "Slightly more than c, because the laser is moving"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Einstein's second postulate: light travels at $c$ in every inertial frame, whatever the motion of its source.

<Step title="The speed of light does not add">

The everyday rule of adding velocities fails for light. Every observer measures the beam at $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$, the observer on the Earth as much as a passenger on the probe. It is exactly this postulate that makes moving clocks slow and moving rods short in the parts below.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

What is the Lorentz factor $\gamma$ of the probe?

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$\gamma =$" value="3.57" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p1-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The Lorentz factor depends only on $v/c$.

<Step title="Lorentz factor">

<Formula label="Lorentz factor" tex="\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{v^2}{c^2}}}" cite="reading1" at="slide 15" />

$v^2/c^2 = 0.96^2 = 0.9216$, so $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.9216} = 1/\sqrt{0.0784} = 1/0.28 = 3.57$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\gamma$ is larger than $1$, as it must be for anything that moves. At $0.96c$ the effects are large: a factor of more than three.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

The probe's own clock shows $3.00\ \text{h}$ between passing the Earth and passing a space station further along its path. How long is that, measured with clocks on the Earth and the station?

<Hint>

Which clock measures the proper time: the one that is present at both events?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$\Delta t =$" value="10.7" unit="h" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p1-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The probe's clock is at both events (passing the Earth, passing the station), so it measures the proper time $\Delta t_0$. Clocks that see it move measure a longer time.

<Step title="Time dilation">

<Formula label="Time dilation" tex="\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t_0" cite="mit" at="slide 4, written there as Δt′ = γΔt" />

$\Delta t = 3.57 \times 3.00 = 10.7\ \text{h}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In those $10.7\ \text{h}$ the probe covers, as seen from the Earth, $0.96 \times 3.00 \times 10^{8} \times 10.7 \times 3600 = 1.11 \times 10^{13}\ \text{m}$. On the probe that stretch of space is contracted by $\gamma$, to $1.11 \times 10^{13}/3.57 = 3.11 \times 10^{12}\ \text{m}$, which the probe covers at $0.96c$ in $3.00\ \text{h}$. Both views agree.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

How long is the probe, measured from the Earth as it flies past?

<Answer id="p1-d" label="$L =$" value="14.0" unit="m" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p1-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A moving object is shorter along its direction of motion by the factor $\gamma$.

<Step title="Length contraction">

<Formula label="Length contraction" tex="L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma}" cite="mit" at="slides 12–13" />

$L = 50.0/3.57 = 14.0\ \text{m}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The rest length $50.0\ \text{m}$ is the longest length anyone measures; every moving observer finds less. On board, the crew's own instruments still show $50.0\ \text{m}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

At what speed $v$ does a moving clock run $\gamma$ times slow? Find $v$ in terms of $c$ and $\gamma$.

<Hint>

Start from $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$. Square both sides, then solve for $v^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e" label="$v =$" expr="c*sqrt(1 - 1/g^2)"
  vars={{ c: [1, 10], g: [1.01, 20] }}
  names={{ g: "\\gamma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "c*(1 - 1/g^2)", hint: "You solved for v²/c², not for v/c. Take the square root." },
    { expr: "c/g", hint: "That would make a clock at rest (γ = 1) move at c. Solve γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) for v step by step." },
    { expr: "c*sqrt(1 - 1/g)", hint: "γ appears squared once you square the formula: 1/γ² = 1 − v²/c²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Turn the formula for $\gamma$ round.

<Step title="Solve for v">

<Formula label="Speed from the Lorentz factor" tex="\frac{1}{\gamma^2} = 1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = c\,\sqrt{1 - \frac{1}{\gamma^2}}" cite="reading1" at="slide 15, solved for v (our own step)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\gamma = 3.57$ gives $v = c\sqrt{1 - 1/12.76} = c\sqrt{0.9216} = 0.96c$, the probe of this problem. $\gamma = 1$ gives $v = 0$, and however large $\gamma$ becomes, $v$ stays below $c$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="An electron from an accelerator" points={13}>

A small linear accelerator speeds electrons up from rest through a voltage of $1.53 \times 10^{6}\ \text{V}$, so each electron gains a kinetic energy of $1.53\ \text{MeV}$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the rest energy $m_ec^2$ of an electron, in joules?

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$E_0 =$" value="8.20e-14" unit="J" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p2-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Mass is a form of energy: a mass $m$ at rest holds $mc^2$.

<Step title="Rest energy">

<Formula label="Rest energy" tex="E_0 = mc^2" cite="reading2" at="p. 1: the energy of a particle at rest" />

$E_0 = 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times (3.00 \times 10^{8})^2 = 8.20 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In electronvolts: $8.20 \times 10^{-14}/(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 5.12 \times 10^{5}\ \text{eV} = 0.512\ \text{MeV}$. The measured value is $0.511\ \text{MeV}$ (NIST); the small difference comes from the rounded constants. The rest of this problem uses $0.511\ \text{MeV}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the Lorentz factor $\gamma$ of an electron leaving the accelerator?

<Hint>

What is the total energy of the electron: its rest energy plus what?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$\gamma =$" value="3.99" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p2-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The total energy is $\gamma mc^2$, and it is the rest energy plus the kinetic energy.

<Step title="Total energy">

<Formula label="Total energy of a moving body" tex="E = \gamma mc^2" cite="reading2" at="p. 1" />

<Formula label="Relativistic kinetic energy" tex="E_k = E - E_0 = (\gamma - 1)\,mc^2" cite="reading3" at="p. 1" />

$E = 0.511 + 1.53 = 2.04\ \text{MeV}$, so $\gamma = E/E_0 = 2.041/0.511 = 3.99$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$(\gamma - 1)\,mc^2 = 2.99 \times 0.511 = 1.53\ \text{MeV}$, the kinetic energy given. The kinetic energy is three times the rest energy, so $\gamma$ is close to $4$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How fast does the electron move? Give $v/c$.

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Where did the spectrum of the background peak at that time? Give it in millimetres.

<Answer id="p11-b" label="$\lambda_{\max} =$" value="0.152" unit="mm" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p11-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The background is a black body at every moment, so Wien's law gives its peak.

<Step title="Wien's law">

<Formula label="Wien's displacement law" tex="\lambda_{\max}\,T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}" cite="wien" at="Problem 1(c) (0.29 cm K, per unit of wavelength)" />

$\lambda_{\max} = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}/19.1 = 1.52 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m} = 0.152\ \text{mm}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Today's peak is $2.9 \times 10^{-3}/2.725 = 1.06\ \text{mm}$, and $1.06/7.0 = 0.152\ \text{mm}$: seven times shorter, as the stretching factor says.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

At what redshift did the background radiation have a temperature of $4000\ \text{K}$, when about half of the hydrogen was neutral?

<Answer id="p11-c" label="$z =$" value="1467" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p11-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Turn the formula of part (a) round: $1 + z = T_\text{then}/T_\text{now}$.

<Step title="Redshift">

$1 + z = 4000/2.725 = 1468$, so $z = 1467$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Decoupling, at about $3000\ \text{K}$ (reading 16, p. 19), came a little later, at $z = 3000/2.725 - 1 = 1100$. A higher temperature means an earlier, smaller universe and a larger redshift.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Could a telescope ever see the universe as it was at $z = 1467$?

<Answer id="p11-d" label="Answer:"
  choices={[
    "No: the light from that time has all been absorbed by dust in our Galaxy",
    "No: until decoupling the universe was a fog of free electrons that scattered light, so no light travelled freely from that time",
    "No: the universe did not yet exist at that redshift",
    "Yes, with a large enough telescope: the light from that time is only very faint and very redshifted",
    "No: the light is too blue for any detector"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Light reaches us only from after decoupling.

<Step title="The fog">

At $4000\ \text{K}$ about half of the hydrogen was still ionised, and free electrons scattered the photons again and again, like light in a fog. Only after decoupling, at about $3000\ \text{K}$ and $380\,000$ years, could the light travel in straight lines. The microwave background is the glow of that last scattering: the earliest light we can see (lesson 8.15).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

Find the energy $E$ of a photon at the peak of a black-body spectrum at temperature $T$, in terms of $h$, $c$, $T$ and Wien's constant $b$ (so $\lambda_{\max}T = b$).

<Hint>

## Problem 12

<Exercise id="p12" title="A galaxy with a flat rotation curve" points={12}>

The gas clouds of a spiral galaxy orbit its centre at $180\ \text{km/s}$, the same at $20\,000$ light-years as at $80\,000$ light-years from the centre: the rotation curve is flat. All the stars and gas that can be seen add up to $4.0 \times 10^{10}$ solar masses, nearly all of it within $20\,000$ light-years.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

How many metres is $80\,000$ light-years?

<Answer id="p12-a" label="$r =$" value="7.57e20" unit="m" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p12-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Multiply by the length of one light-year.

<Step title="In metres">

$r = 80\,000 \times 9.46 \times 10^{15} = 7.57 \times 10^{20}\ \text{m}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What mass lies inside the orbit at $80\,000$ light-years?

<Hint>

For a circular orbit, gravity supplies the centripetal force. Only the mass inside the orbit counts.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p12-b" label="$M =$" value="3.68e41" unit="kg" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p12-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The orbital speed of a cloud measures the mass inside its orbit.

<Step title="Mass inside">

<Formula label="Gravity supplies the centripetal force" tex="\frac{m v^2}{r} = \frac{G\,M(r)\,m}{r^2}" cite="reading12" at="eq. (22), p. 10 (written there with M)" />

<Formula label="Mass inside an orbit from its speed" tex="M(r) = \frac{v^2\, r}{G}" cite="reading12" at="eqs. (22)–(23), p. 10, solved for the mass" />

$M = (1.80 \times 10^{5})^2 \times 7.57 \times 10^{20}/(6.67 \times 10^{-11}) = 3.68 \times 10^{41}\ \text{kg}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In solar masses: $3.68 \times 10^{41}/(1.99 \times 10^{30}) = 1.85 \times 10^{11}$. Units: $(\text{m/s})^2 \cdot \text{m}/(\text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2) = \text{m}^3\text{s}^{-2}/(\text{m}^3\text{kg}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}) = \text{kg}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

How many times more mass lies inside $80\,000$ light-years than inside $20\,000$ light-years?

<Answer id="p12-c" label="ratio of the masses:" value="4" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p12-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With the same $v$ at both radii, $M = v^2r/G$ grows in step with $r$.

<Step title="Ratio">

$M(80\,000)/M(20\,000) = 80\,000/20\,000 = 4$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Three quarters of the mass inside $80\,000$ light-years lies outside $20\,000$ light-years, where there are hardly any stars: a flat rotation curve means mass that gives no light.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

How much dark mass lies inside $80\,000$ light-years? Give it in solar masses.

<Hint>

The dark mass is everything the orbits demand minus what can be seen.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p12-d" label="dark mass:" value="1.45e11" unit="solar masses" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p12-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Subtract the visible mass from the mass inside the orbit, both in solar masses.

<Step title="Dark mass">

$1.85 \times 10^{11} - 4.0 \times 10^{10} = 1.45 \times 10^{11}$ solar masses.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Inside $20\,000$ light-years the orbits demand $1.85 \times 10^{11}/4 = 4.62 \times 10^{10}$ solar masses, only a little more than the visible $4.0 \times 10^{10}$: near the centre the stars and gas explain most of the motion. Nearly all the dark mass lies farther out, where there is hardly any light.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

Suppose the rotation curve stays flat at $180\ \text{km/s}$ farther out. Out to what distance must it stay flat for the galaxy to hold $5.4$ times as much dark mass as visible mass, the ratio for the universe as a whole (lesson 8.16)?

<Hint>

First find the total mass needed. On a flat curve the mass inside grows in step with the distance, as in part (c).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p12-e" label="$r =$" value="1.11e5" unit="light-years" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p12-e" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the total mass, then scale the distance up from $80\,000$ light-years in the same proportion.

<Step title="Total mass needed">

Visible plus dark: $(1 + 5.4) \times 4.0 \times 10^{10} = 2.56 \times 10^{11}$ solar masses.

</Step>

<Step title="Distance">

$r = 80\,000 \times 2.56 \times 10^{11}/(1.85 \times 10^{11}) = 1.11 \times 10^{5}$ light-years.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Then the dark share of all the mass is $5.4/6.4 = 0.84$; inside $80\,000$ light-years it is $1.45/1.85 = 0.78$. The farther the curve stays flat, the larger the dark share.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 13

<Exercise id="p13" title="Dark energy and inflation" points={14}>

Take a flat universe with $\Omega_m = 0.31$ and $\Omega_\text{vac} = 0.69$ today, close to the Planck 2018 values (reading 19, eq. (300)). Matter thins out as $1/a^3$ while the density of vacuum energy stays the same.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Light from a galaxy at $z = 3$ left when the universe was a quarter of its present size. How many times denser than the vacuum energy was the matter then?

<Answer id="p13-a" label="$\rho_m/\rho_\text{vac} =$" value="28.8" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p13-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Today the ratio is $\Omega_m/\Omega_\text{vac}$; going back, the matter density grows as $1/a^3$.

<Step title="Ratio of densities">

<Formula label="Matter compared with vacuum energy" tex="\frac{\rho_m}{\rho_\text{vac}} = \frac{\Omega_m}{\Omega_\text{vac}} \cdot \frac{1}{a^3}" cite="reading19" at="eqs. (298)–(299), p. 68, divided (as in lesson 8.17)" />

$a = 1/(1 + z) = 1/4 = 0.25$, so $\rho_m/\rho_\text{vac} = (0.31/0.69) \times 4^3 = 0.449 \times 64 = 28.8$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Was the expansion speeding up or slowing down when that light left?

<Answer id="p13-b" label="The expansion was:"
  choices={[
    "Speeding up, because vacuum energy always drives acceleration",
    "Slowing down, because the matter density was more than twice the vacuum energy density",
    "Neither: it was constant",
    "Speeding up, because the universe was smaller"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Vacuum energy pushes with twice its density, matter pulls with its density.

<Step title="The acceleration equation">

<Formula label="Acceleration of the expansion" tex="\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho_m - 2\rho_\text{vac}\right)" cite="reading20" at="p. 21 (board), with radiation left out and zero pressure for matter" />

With $\rho_m = 28.8\,\rho_\text{vac}$, the bracket is positive, so $\ddot a$ is negative: gravity was slowing the expansion. The speeding up came later, once the matter had thinned out.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Find the redshift $z$ at which the expansion changed from slowing down to speeding up, in terms of $\Omega_m$ and $\Omega_\text{vac}$.

<Hint>

The change comes where $\rho_m = 2\rho_\text{vac}$. Solve for $a$, then use $1 + z = 1/a$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p13-c" label="$z =$" expr="(2*Ov/Om)^(1/3) - 1"
  vars={{ Om: [0.1, 0.5], Ov: [0.5, 0.9] }}
  names={{ Om: "\\Omega_m", Ov: "\\Omega_\\text{vac}" }}

    { expr: "(Om/(2*Ov))^(1/3)", hint: "That is the scale factor a at the change. The redshift is 1/a − 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Set the bracket of the acceleration equation to zero.

<Step title="Solve for a and z">

<Formula label="Redshift at which the acceleration began" tex="\frac{\Omega_m}{\Omega_\text{vac}} \cdot \frac{1}{a^3} = 2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1 + z = \frac{1}{a} = \left(\frac{2\,\Omega_\text{vac}}{\Omega_m}\right)^{1/3}" cite="reading20" at="p. 21 (board), with reading 19, eqs. </Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $\Omega_m = 0.31$ and $\Omega_\text{vac} = 0.69$: $(2 \times 0.69/0.31)^{1/3} - 1 = 4.45^{1/3} - 1 = 1.645 - 1 = 0.645$. The galaxy of part (a), at $z = 3$, lies well before this, as part (b) found.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Suppose inflation had to stretch a patch of space by a factor of $10^{30}$. How many doublings is that?

<Hint>

$2^n = 10^{30}$. Take the logarithm of both sides.

<Answer id="p13-d" label="$n =$" value="99.7" tolerance="3%" calc="hs8p-p13-d" />
