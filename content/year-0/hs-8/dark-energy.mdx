Lesson 8.12 met a surprise without explaining it. A universe of matter alone would be too young for its oldest stars, and the age of 13.8 billion years only comes out if something called **dark energy** is included. Lesson 8.16 then counted the universe and found dark energy to be its largest part, about 68%. This lesson asks what that "something" is, or rather what we know about it. How was it found? Why does it make the expansion speed up? And why did it take over only in the last few billion years?

## What gravity should do

Since Hubble (lesson 8.11) we know that the galaxies move apart. But every galaxy also pulls on every other one (lesson 2.11). So everyone expected the expansion to **slow down**, like a ball thrown upwards that keeps rising but more and more slowly (NASA Science, *What is Dark Energy?*). The only open question seemed to be how strongly it slows: enough to fall back one day, or not. Guth remembers that the supernova teams set out just to measure how fast the universe was slowing down (Lecture 18, p. 17).

How can you see whether the expansion slows down? Light from far away is old light. Light that has travelled for 5 billion years shows its galaxy as it was 5 billion years ago. So by measuring distances and redshifts of galaxies very far away, astronomers can compare how fast the universe expanded then with how fast it expands now. For that they need distances to objects billions of light-years away, and the brightest standard candles we have.

## Supernovae that were too faint

Lesson 8.10 introduced **Type Ia supernovae**: exploding white dwarfs that all reach roughly the same peak luminosity, bright enough to be seen across billions of light-years. Measure the flux of one, and the inverse square law $F = L/(4\pi d^2)$ gives its distance. Measure the redshift of its galaxy, and you know how much the universe has stretched since the light left.

In 1998 two teams did this for distant supernovae: the **High-Z Supernova Search Team** and the **Supernova Cosmology Project**, based in Berkeley and led by Saul Perlmutter (Lecture 18, pp. 16–17). Together they were something like 52 astronomers (Lecture 18, p. 16). Both found the same thing. At a given redshift, the distant supernovae were **fainter** than expected (NASA Science, *What is Dark Energy?*), by about 20% to 30% compared with models without dark energy (Lecture 20, pp. 8–9). Fainter means farther away. The supernovae were farther than a slowing universe allows.

<WorkedExample title="How much farther is a fainter supernova?">
<Problem>
A model universe slowed by gravity predicts that a supernova at a certain redshift lies 2000 Mpc away. It is measured to be 25% fainter than that model predicts: its flux is 0.75 times the predicted flux. Same supernova, same luminosity: how far away is it really? (The numbers are chosen for this example.)
</Problem>
<Step title="Flux and distance">
For the same luminosity, $F \propto 1/d^2$, so $d \propto 1/\sqrt{F}$. A flux of 0.75 times the prediction means a distance of $1/\sqrt{0.75} = 1.155$ times the prediction.
</Step>
<Step title="The distance">
$d = 2000\ \text{Mpc} \times 1.155 = 2310\ \text{Mpc}$: about 15% farther than the model predicted.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
At 2310 Mpc the flux is $(2000/2310)^2 = 0.75$ of the flux at 2000 Mpc, as measured. A difference in brightness that the eye could hardly notice means a sizeable difference in distance.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why fainter means speeding up

Why does "farther than expected" mean that the expansion is speeding up? Guth admits that this is not obvious, and that it is easiest to think of it the other way round (Lecture 20, pp. 8–9). Take a supernova at a fixed distance. Its redshift tells us how fast its galaxy was moving away when the light left (lesson 8.11). If the expansion has **sped up** to reach today's rate, then it was **slower** in the past. The galaxy was moving away more slowly, and its redshift is smaller. So in a speeding-up universe a given redshift belongs to a farther, fainter supernova: just what the teams saw.

So the expansion of the universe is not slowing down but speeding up. Guth puts it this way: the universe expands faster today than about five billion years ago, and that came as a shock, because everyone had expected gravity to slow it (Lecture 18, p. 17).

Such a dramatic claim needs checks. Could dust have dimmed the supernovae? Dust usually blocks blue light more than red, so it makes things look redder, and the distant supernovae showed no such reddening. Dust in their own galaxies would also dim some supernovae much more than others, and that extra spread was not seen (Lecture 20, pp. 9–10). Were supernovae different long ago? The distant ones looked just like nearby ones in every property that could be compared (Lecture 20, p. 11). Then a completely independent measurement agreed: the pattern of ripples in the cosmic microwave background (lesson 8.15) needs the same amount of dark energy as the supernovae (Lecture 20, pp. 11–12). Adam Riess, Saul Perlmutter and Brian Schmidt, who led the teams, shared the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery (NASA Science, *What is Dark Energy?*).

## A name for what we do not know

Something makes the expansion speed up. Astronomers call it **dark energy**. That is a name, not an explanation: NASA's page says plainly that we do not know what dark energy is (NASA Science, *What is Dark Energy?*). We only know what it does, and roughly how much of it there is: about 68% of everything in the universe (NASA Science, *Dark Matter*).

Do not confuse it with dark matter (lesson 8.16). Dark matter clumps around galaxies and **pulls**, like ordinary matter. Dark energy is spread evenly through all of space and makes the expansion **speed up**. What the two have in common is mainly that we do not know what either of them is.

## The simplest idea: energy of empty space

The simplest explanation, and the one most cosmologists take seriously, is a **vacuum energy**: an energy that belongs to empty space itself (Lecture 18, p. 17). To older physics that sounds strange, since a vacuum is just nothing. But in quantum physics (lesson 8.6) even empty space is never completely still. Its fields keep fluctuating, as the uncertainty principle demands, so there is no reason why the lowest possible energy of space should be exactly zero (Lecture 18, pp. 19–20).

The idea has an older history. In 1917 Einstein applied his new theory of gravity (lesson 8.8) to the whole universe. Like everyone at the time, he thought the universe was static. His equations said that a static universe of matter would collapse, so he added an extra term with a new constant, the **cosmological constant** $\Lambda$ (Greek capital lambda), which acts as a kind of universal repulsion (Lecture 18, pp. 18–19). When Hubble showed that the universe expands, Einstein dropped it; the physicist George Gamow reported that Einstein called it his "biggest blunder" (NASA Science, *What is Dark Energy?*). From a modern point of view it is natural to treat the cosmological constant simply as a vacuum energy (Lecture 18, p. 20).

## Matter thins out, the vacuum does not

The key property of vacuum energy is what happens when space grows. Lesson 8.13 described the expansion with the scale factor $a$: every distance between galaxies is multiplied by $a$, with $a = 1$ today. The same matter then fills a volume $a^3$ times as large, so its density falls as $1/a^3$. But a region of empty space that expands is still empty space; what else could it become? So its energy density stays **exactly the same** (Lecture 18, p. 20):

<Formula label="Matter thins out, the vacuum energy does not" tex="\rho_m \propto \frac{1}{a^3}, \qquad \rho_\text{vac} = \text{constant}" cite="reading3" at="p. 64 and eqs. (298)–(299), p. 68" />

Here $\rho_m$ is the density of all matter, dark and ordinary, and $\rho_\text{vac}$ the density of the vacuum energy, written as a mass density with Einstein's E = mc² (lesson 8.3), so that the two can be compared. (Radiation thins out even faster, as $1/a^4$, and today hardly counts.)

Cosmologists describe the contents of the universe today with the **density parameters** of lesson 8.16: $\Omega_m$ is the share of matter in today's total, and $\Omega_\text{vac}$ the share of vacuum energy. Round values are $\Omega_m = 0.3$ and $\Omega_\text{vac} = 0.7$: roughly 30% matter and 70% dark energy (MIT 8.902, lecture 10, p. 64). Since $\rho_m$ falls as $1/a^3$ and $\rho_\text{vac}$ stays the same, the ratio of the two at any scale factor is

<Formula label="Matter compared with vacuum energy" tex="\frac{\rho_m}{\rho_\text{vac}} = \frac{\Omega_m}{\Omega_\text{vac}} \cdot \frac{1}{a^3}" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (298)–(299), p. 68, divided (this lesson's own step)" />

Today ($a = 1$) the vacuum energy is $0.7/0.3 = 2.3$ times as dense as matter. In the past, when $a$ was smaller, matter was packed more tightly and won easily. In the future, matter keeps thinning while the vacuum energy stays: the vacuum wins more and more.

## Pulling and pushing

Why does vacuum energy make the expansion speed up instead of slowing it down? The full answer needs Einstein's theory: a constant energy density turns out to come with a **negative pressure**, and in general relativity pressure, too, affects gravity (Lecture 18, pp. 20–21). The result can be written as one equation for how the expansion speeds up or slows down. For matter and vacuum energy it is

<Formula label="Acceleration of the expansion" tex="\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho_m - 2\rho_\text{vac}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 21 (board), with radiation left out and zero pressure for matter" />

Here $\ddot a$ ("a double dot") is the acceleration of the scale factor: positive when the expansion speeds up, negative when it slows down. Matter, with the minus sign, slows the expansion, just as everyone expected. The vacuum energy enters with a plus sign and a factor 2: it pushes twice as hard as the same density of matter pulls (Lecture 18, p. 21). We take this equation as given; the university course derives it. It means that the expansion slows down while $\rho_m > 2\rho_\text{vac}$ and speeds up once $\rho_m < 2\rho_\text{vac}$.

<WorkedExample title="When did the vacuum energy take over?">
<Problem>
Take $\Omega_m = 0.3$ and $\Omega_\text{vac} = 0.7$. (a) At what scale factor were the densities of matter and vacuum energy equal? Which redshift does that belong to? (b) At what scale factor did the expansion start to speed up?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Equal densities">
Set the ratio equal to 1: $\dfrac{0.3}{0.7} \cdot \dfrac{1}{a^3} = 1$, so $a^3 = 0.3/0.7 = 0.429$ and $a = 0.429^{1/3} = 0.75$. With $1 + z = 1/a$ (lesson 8.13): $z = 1/0.75 - 1 = 0.33$. Light from galaxies at that redshift left them when the two densities were equal.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The start of the speeding up">
Now matter must have exactly twice the vacuum density: $\dfrac{0.3}{0.7} \cdot \dfrac{1}{a^3} = 2$, so $a^3 = 0.3/1.4 = 0.214$ and $a = 0.60$, or $z = 1/0.60 - 1 = 0.67$. Before that the expansion slowed down; since then it has sped up.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
At $a = 0.60$, matter was $1/0.60^3 = 4.6$ times as dense as today: $0.3 \times 4.6 = 1.4$, which is twice the vacuum's $0.7$. Speeding up started (b) before the densities were equal (a), because the vacuum pushes twice as hard.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The Planck satellite's 2018 values, $\Omega_m = 0.315$ and $\Omega_\text{vac} = 0.6847$ (MIT 8.902, lecture 10, p. 69; a later development), give almost the same answer: equal densities at $a = (0.315/0.6847)^{1/3} = 0.77$. In cosmic terms dark energy took over only recently, in the last few billion years.

What happens next? As matter keeps thinning, the vacuum energy becomes the only thing that counts, and the scale factor then grows exponentially (Lecture 18, p. 23; lesson 0.9): every distance between faraway galaxies doubles again and again in equal steps of time, like money at a fixed interest rate.

## The cosmic budget and the age of the universe

Put together, the universe today is about 68% dark energy, 27% dark matter and 5% ordinary matter (NASA Science, *Dark Matter*). Lesson 8.16 explained the matter part. Everything made of atoms, all the stars, planets, gas and people, is the smallest slice.

Dark energy also solves the puzzle of lesson 8.12. With $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc a flat universe of matter alone would be only $\tfrac23 \times 977.8/70 = 9.3$ billion years old, younger than its oldest stars. A universe that slowed down less in its early life and then sped up needed more time to reach today's expansion rate. With dark energy included, the computed age becomes about 13.8 billion years (lesson 8.12), comfortably older than every star. So three separate lines of evidence fit together: the faint supernovae, the ripples in the cosmic microwave background, and stars that are older than a universe of matter alone could be.

## Open questions

Dark energy is the largest part of the universe, and physicists know least about it. Some of the open questions:

- **Why is the vacuum energy so small?** Quantum physics suggests that empty space should have energy, but a simple estimate of how much comes out larger than the measured value by a factor of about $10^{120}$: a 1 with 120 zeros. Guth calls the vacuum energy the least understood issue in physics (Lecture 20, pp. 13 and 16). NASA's page calls this the cosmological constant problem (NASA Science, *What is Dark Energy?*).
- **Is it really constant?** Another idea, called **quintessence**, is a field that fills space and changes very slowly, so that it only looks constant for now. Guth called it the minority view in 2013, but "up for grabs" (Lecture 20, p. 12).
- **Is gravity different on the largest scales?** Some physicists ask whether general relativity itself needs changing, so that there is no dark energy at all (NASA Science, *What is Dark Energy?*).

New telescopes, such as ESA's Euclid satellite, NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, are mapping billions of galaxies and many more supernovae to measure whether dark energy changes over time (NASA Science, *What is Dark Energy?*). These are later developments: the answer is not known yet.

## Summary

- Gravity should slow the expansion of the universe. In 1998 two teams found that distant **Type Ia supernovae** are fainter, and so farther away, than a slowing universe allows: the expansion has been **speeding up**. Riess, Perlmutter and Schmidt shared the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics.
- Fainter means speeding up: a universe that has sped up expanded more slowly in the past, so at a given redshift a supernova is farther away.
- Dust and changes in supernovae were ruled out, and the cosmic microwave background independently needs the same dark energy.
- **Dark energy** is the name for the unknown cause. The simplest idea is **vacuum energy**, Einstein's **cosmological constant**: an energy density of empty space that stays the same as space grows.
- Matter thins out as $\rho_m \propto 1/a^3$, while $\rho_\text{vac}$ stays constant, so the vacuum wins at late times. It pushes twice as hard as matter pulls: the expansion speeds up once $\rho_m < 2\rho_\text{vac}$. With $\Omega_m = 0.3$ and $\Omega_\text{vac} = 0.7$ that happened at $a = 0.60$, and the densities were equal at $a = 0.75$.
- The universe is about 68% dark energy, 27% dark matter and 5% ordinary matter. Dark energy makes the age of 13.8 billion years possible.
- What dark energy is, why it is so small, and whether it changes over time are open questions.
