Lesson 8.13 ran the expansion of the universe back to a hot, dense, evenly filled start, and lesson 8.15 showed the light of that start, the microwave background. The hot Big Bang explains a great deal, but Guth calls it a theory of the aftermath of a bang: it starts with everything already hot, already expanding and already very even, and does not say why. This lesson looks at two puzzles hidden in those starting conditions, and at **inflation**, the idea proposed to solve them.

## Two puzzles in the starting conditions

The Big Bang theory works: it predicts the light elements (lesson 8.14) and the microwave background (lesson 8.15). But to get those answers you must put in, by hand, a universe that was **extremely uniform** and **extremely close to flat**. You are allowed to simply assume this. Guth's point is that an assumption is not an explanation, and in the Big Bang without inflation the two facts cannot be explained at all.

## The horizon problem

The microwave background is the same in every direction to about **one part in 100 000** (lesson 8.15). Two photons arriving from opposite sides of the sky have the same temperature, so the places that sent them out, about 380 000 years after the start, had the same temperature.

Equal temperatures are normally no mystery. A hot drink cools to room temperature because energy flows between the drink and the air until they match. But that takes contact, and nothing, not even light, travels faster than $3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. The largest distance light could have covered since the Big Bang is called the **horizon distance**: two places farther apart than that have never been able to affect each other.

In MIT's cosmology course, Guth works out where the two emission points were when the light set out. Each was about 23 horizon distances from the place that would become our home, so the two were about **46 horizon distances** apart. Neither could have known anything about the other. Yet they glowed at the same temperature. In his opening lecture Guth puts it another way: evening out the young universe in time would have needed matter and energy to be moved at about a hundred times the speed of light.

You could say that the universe simply started out uniform. That is allowed, but it explains nothing. This is the **horizon problem**.

## The flatness problem

General relativity allows space to be **curved**. "Flat" here does not mean thin like a pancake: it means that the geometry of school, Euclid's geometry, holds, so that the angles of a triangle add up to exactly 180°. Which geometry the universe has depends on how much it contains, measured by a number Ω (capital omega):

<Formula label="The density parameter" tex="\Omega = \frac{\text{actual density}}{\text{critical density}}" cite="reading2" at="slide 10" />

The critical density depends on how fast the universe expands. If $\Omega = 1$ space is flat; above 1 it closes up on itself like the surface of a ball; below 1 it is open, curved like a saddle. All ordinary matter, dark matter (lesson 8.16) and dark energy (lesson 8.17) count in Ω.

The trouble is that $\Omega = 1$ is an **unstable balance**. Picture a ball resting on the sharp ridge of a roof, or on a knife-edge: perfectly placed, it stays; a hair to one side and it rolls off, faster and faster. Guth uses a pencil balanced on its tip. In the expanding universe, if Ω starts a tiny bit above 1, it grows and the universe soon falls back together; a tiny bit below 1, and Ω quickly drops towards 0 and the universe empties out. Either way, no galaxies would form.

Yet today Ω is measured to be very close to 1. Running the expansion back, Guth finds that one second after the Big Bang Ω must have been equal to 1 to about **15 decimal places** with the measurements of around 1980, and to about **18 decimal places** with those of 2013. It is like finding a pencil still upright after 14 billion years. The Big Bang theory alone gives no reason why Ω started so finely balanced. This is the **flatness problem**.

## The idea of inflation

Inflation adds a very short first chapter, before the hot Big Bang. Guth proposed it around 1980, and he is also the lecturer of the MIT course that this lesson follows. It rests on a surprising fact from general relativity: gravity can **push**. In Einstein's theory not only mass and energy but also pressure produces gravity, and a material with a **negative pressure** produces gravitational repulsion. Lesson 8.17 met a weak form of this today: the energy of empty space, which makes the expansion speed up. Inflation needs a vacuum-like energy of the same kind, but enormously denser.

In Guth's sample picture:

- A tiny patch of this repulsive material, perhaps only about $10^{-28}\ \text{cm}$ across, sits in the early universe. Its repulsion makes it expand **exponentially**: in each fixed stretch of time its size doubles, then doubles again, and again. Guth's sample doubling time is about $10^{-37}\ \text{s}$.
- Strangely, the density of this material does **not** go down as it expands, so the push does not weaken while it lasts.
- After about **100 doublings**, a growth of at least $10^{28}$ times, in about $10^{-35}\ \text{s}$, the patch that will become everything we can now see is about the size of a marble, roughly 1 cm across.
- The material is unstable. It decays, rather like a radioactive substance, and its energy turns into ordinary particles: the hot, dense soup that the Big Bang theory starts from. The expansion then coasts on, slowed by ordinary gravity.

These numbers are only samples: Guth stresses that nobody knows at what energy inflation happened, so the starting size, the doubling time and the number of doublings could all be different. More inflation than needed does no harm: it only means that the uniform region we live in is larger than the part we can see.

Guth's first version had a flaw: it could not come to a smooth end. Andrei Linde, and independently Andreas Albrecht and Paul Steinhardt, then proposed a changed version, called new inflation, in which it ends gracefully; later versions work in a similar way.

## Counting doublings

Exponential growth (lesson 0.9) is growth by the same factor in each step. After $n$ doublings a starting size $L_0$ has become

<Formula label="Size after n doublings" tex="L = L_0 \times 2^{n}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7 (in words: wait one doubling time and the size doubles, wait the same time again and it doubles again)" />

To find how many doublings give a growth factor $L/L_0$, take logarithms, as in lesson 0.9:

<Formula label="Number of doublings" tex="n = \log_{2}\frac{L}{L_0} = \frac{\log (L/L_0)}{\log 2}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 7–8 (a factor of about 10²⁸ takes about 100 doublings); the logarithm form is the change of base of lesson 0.9" />

<WorkedExample title="Inflating by a factor of 10²⁶">
<Problem>
Suppose inflation had to stretch a patch by a factor of $10^{26}$, and each doubling took $1 \times 10^{-37}\ \text{s}$. How many doublings are needed, and how long does it take? How much bigger would ten extra doublings make the patch?
</Problem>
<Step title="Doublings">
$n = \log 10^{26} / \log 2 = 26/0.301 = 86.4$. Since 86 doublings fall just short, it takes **87 doublings**.
</Step>
<Step title="Time">
$87 \times 1 \times 10^{-37}\ \text{s} = 8.7 \times 10^{-36}\ \text{s}$. Light needs about $(10^{-15}\ \text{m})/(3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}) = 3 \times 10^{-24}\ \text{s}$ just to cross a proton, so the whole growth is over in less than a billionth of that time.
</Step>
<Step title="Ten more doublings">
$2^{10} = 1024$: ten extra doublings make the patch about **a thousand times** larger, in only another $10^{-36}\ \text{s}$. With exponential growth, a little more time gives a lot more size. That is why inflation easily makes "more than enough".
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## How inflation solves the two puzzles

**The horizon problem.** Before inflation, the region that became our whole observable universe was incredibly small, much smaller than the horizon distance at that time. There was plenty of time for it to even out, just as the air in a room spreads out evenly instead of staying in one corner. Inflation then stretched this already uniform patch until it contained everything we can see. So the two places on opposite sides of the sky were once neighbours and did share their heat; inflation only moved them apart, faster than any signal could follow.

**The flatness problem.** Blow up a balloon to the size of the Earth, and a patch of it the size of a football pitch looks perfectly flat. Inflation does the same to space. During inflation the density stays constant, while the effect of curvature shrinks as the square of the growth: Guth shows that

<Formula label="Inflation flattens space" tex="|\Omega - 1| \propto \frac{1}{a^{2}}" cite="reading4" at="p. 21: during inflation the curvature term of the Friedmann equation falls as 1/a² while the density term stays fixed (strictly this is (Ω − 1)/Ω, which is almost the same once Ω is near 1)" />

where $a$ is the scale factor of lesson 8.13. So every doubling of the size divides $|\Omega - 1|$ by 4. Instead of rolling away from the knife-edge, the ball is pushed onto it.

<WorkedExample title="Ironing out the curvature">
<Problem>
Suppose that just before inflation the universe was 10% too dense, so $|\Omega - 1| = 0.1$. How close to 1 is Ω after the 87 doublings of the previous example?
</Problem>
<Step title="The shrink factor">
The size grows by $2^{87}$, so $|\Omega - 1|$ is divided by $(2^{87})^2 = 2^{174} = 2.4 \times 10^{52}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The result">
$|\Omega - 1| = 0.1/(2.4 \times 10^{52}) = 4 \times 10^{-54}$. That is far closer to 1 than the $10^{-18}$ needed at one second. After inflation $|\Omega - 1|$ grows again, as in the flatness problem, but from such a small start it is still tiny today.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Inflation therefore predicts that Ω should be 1 today. For years this looked wrong: before 1998 astronomers found only Ω ≈ 0.2 to 0.3. The missing part was dark energy (lesson 8.17), discovered in 1998. Including it, the measurements give Ω = 1 to about half a percent, as Guth quoted in 2013, and WMAP's results put space within 0.4% of flat.

Inflation solves a third puzzle as well. Theories that join the forces into one (lesson 8.7) predict that the hot early universe made far too many **magnetic monopoles**, particles with a single magnetic pole (if those theories are right). If they formed before most of the inflation, it spread them out over a volume $(10^{28})^3 = 10^{84}$ times larger, in Guth's sample numbers, so thinly that we would not expect to meet one.

## Ripples from quantum jitters

Inflation smooths everything out so well that its early workers worried it would leave a perfectly smooth universe, with no seeds for galaxies. Quantum physics rescued it. The uncertainty principle (lesson 8.6) makes it impossible for anything to be perfectly uniform: on the tiniest scales the energy density jitters, a little higher here and a little lower there. Inflation stretched these quantum jitters to enormous sizes. They became the ripples of about one part in 100 000 seen in the microwave background, and gravity, helped by dark matter (lesson 8.16), made them grow into galaxies and clusters of galaxies. In this picture, every galaxy began as a quantum fluctuation.

Inflation cannot predict how strong the ripples are, because that depends on unknown details of the physics. But it does predict how their strength changes with their size, and Guth shows that the measurements of WMAP and Planck follow that prediction beautifully. An alternative idea, that the ripples came from so-called cosmic strings, predicted a very different pattern and was ruled out.

## How sure are we?

Inflation explains the uniformity, the flatness and the ripples with one idea, and its predictions have passed real tests: Ω = 1, which seemed wrong until 1998, and the detailed pattern of the ripples. NASA's WMAP pages say that its results support inflation. That makes it the leading explanation of the first moment.

But it is **not proven**. Nobody knows which field drove inflation, at what energy it happened, or what came before it; NASA's pages say scientists are not sure what powered it. Guth adds that there is still no real theory of how the universe started, with inflation or without. The numbers in this lesson, $10^{-37}\ \text{s}$ and 100 doublings, are sample values, not measurements. Inflation remains an extrapolation far beyond any physics tested in a laboratory, and each new measurement of the background tests it again.

## Summary

- The hot Big Bang needs a start that was extremely uniform and extremely close to flat, but cannot explain either.
- **Horizon problem:** the microwave background has the same temperature in opposite directions to one part in 100 000, yet the places that sent it out were about 46 horizon distances apart, too far for anything to have evened them out.
- **Flatness problem:** Ω = 1 is an unstable balance, like a ball on a knife-edge; to be near 1 today, Ω had to equal 1 to about 15 to 18 decimal places at one second.
- **Inflation** (Guth, around 1980): a very brief first moment in which a repulsive, vacuum-like energy made a tiny patch double in size again and again, in Guth's sample numbers every $10^{-37}\ \text{s}$, about 100 times, before decaying into the hot Big Bang.
- Growth after $n$ doublings: $L = L_0 \times 2^{n}$; a factor $L/L_0$ needs $n = \log(L/L_0)/\log 2$ doublings. Ten doublings multiply by about a thousand.
- Inflation solves both puzzles: our observable universe came from a patch small enough to even out, and stretching pushed Ω towards 1, since $|\Omega - 1| \propto 1/a^{2}$ during inflation. Stretched quantum jitters became the seeds of the galaxies.
- Its predictions fit the measurements well, but what drove it is unknown: inflation is strongly supported, not proven.
