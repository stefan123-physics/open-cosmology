The nucleus of lesson 7.7 is made of protons and neutrons, held together by the strong force. Beta decay (lesson 7.8) showed that a neutron can turn into a proton, giving out an electron and an antineutrino. And lesson 8.3 showed that mass is a form of energy. This lesson puts these pieces together into the modern picture of matter: a few kinds of elementary particles, four forces between them, and a way of seeing each force as particles passed to and fro. It ends with why this matters for the first moments of the universe.

## The building blocks

Protons and neutrons are not elementary: each is made of three smaller particles called **quarks**. Ordinary matter needs only two kinds, the **up quark** and the **down quark**. Their charges are fractions of the elementary charge $e$ (Particle Data Group): an up quark has $+\tfrac23 e$, a down quark $-\tfrac13 e$. A proton is up, up, down, and a neutron is up, down, down (PDG). Adding the charges gives the right answers:

<Formula label="Charge of a proton from its quarks" tex="\tfrac23 e + \tfrac23 e + \left(-\tfrac13 e\right) = +e" cite="pdgq" at="quark summary: charges of u and d; N baryons: p = uud" />

For the neutron, $\tfrac23 e - \tfrac13 e - \tfrac13 e = 0$, as it must be.

Quarks are never seen on their own. Try to pull one out of a proton and the strong force between them does not weaken with distance; long before the quark gets free, the energy you put in makes a new quark and antiquark instead (MIT 8.286 Lecture 21, p. 24). Quarks are **confined** inside particles such as protons and neutrons.

The second family of building blocks are the **leptons**, particles that feel no strong force at all (8.286 Lecture 21, p. 22). The **electron** is a lepton, and so is the **neutrino** of lesson 7.8. Together:

| Particle | Family | Charge | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|---|
| up quark | quark | $+\tfrac23 e$ | two in every proton, one in every neutron |
| down quark | quark | $-\tfrac13 e$ | one in every proton, two in every neutron |
| electron | lepton | $-e$ | in every atom, around the nucleus |
| electron neutrino | lepton | $0$ | given out in beta decay and by the Sun |

These four are all that everyday matter is made of. Nature also has heavier copies of each: the quarks called strange, charm, bottom and top, and the leptons called the muon and the tau, each with a neutrino of its own (8.286 Lecture 21, pp. 21–22). The heavier quarks and the muon and tau decay within a tiny fraction of a second, so they are found only in cosmic rays and in particle accelerators.

Seen at this level, beta decay is simple. Inside a neutron (up, down, down) one down quark turns into an up quark, giving out an electron and an antineutrino; the neutron has become a proton (up, up, down). Check the charge: $-\tfrac13 e$ before, and $+\tfrac23 e - e + 0 = -\tfrac13 e$ after.

## Antimatter

Lesson 7.8 met the **positron**: the same mass as an electron, but charge $+e$. It is the electron's **antiparticle**. In the same way, every particle has an antiparticle with the same mass and the opposite charge, a standard result of particle physics: the antiproton has charge $-e$, the antineutrino of beta decay is the neutrino's partner, and there are antiquarks too. A few neutral particles, such as the photon, are their own antiparticle. Matter made of antiparticles is called **antimatter**.

When a particle meets its own antiparticle, they **annihilate**: both disappear, and all of their rest energy (lesson 8.3) comes out in another form. An electron and a positron that meet at rest turn into gamma rays (NASA, "NASA's Fermi Catches Thunderstorms Hurling Antimatter into Space"):

<Formula label="Electron–positron annihilation" tex="{}^{\;\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + {}^{\;\;0}_{+1}\mathrm{e} \;\to\; \gamma + \gamma" cite="nasaFermi" at="when antimatter meets normal matter, both are destroyed and converted into gamma rays" />

Why two photons and not one? Before the meeting the total momentum is zero. A single photon always carries momentum (lesson 8.3), so it cannot be the only product. Two photons flying off in opposite directions have a total momentum of zero, and they share the energy equally.

From $E = hf = hc/\lambda$ (lesson 7.2), $\lambda = hc/E = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}/(8.18 \times 10^{-14}) = 2.43 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{m}$. The process also runs backwards. A gamma-ray photon with enough energy, passing close to a nucleus, can turn into an electron and a positron: energy becomes mass, as $E = mc^2$ allows (MIT 8.701 Lecture 1.1, slide 2: particles are created and annihilated in reactions). The photon must supply at least the two rest energies, $2 \times 0.511 = 1.022\ \text{MeV}$. Annihilation and pair creation are the clearest proof that mass and energy are two forms of one thing.

## Four forces

Every push and pull in nature comes from just four basic forces. You have met all of them already:

- **Gravity** (lesson 2.11) attracts everything with mass, and reaches across any distance, getting weaker as $1/r^2$.
- **The electromagnetic force** (lesson 6.2) acts on charges. It also reaches across any distance as $1/r^2$, and it holds atoms and molecules together.
- **The strong force** (lesson 7.7) acts between quarks, and so holds protons, neutrons and nuclei together. Outside a nucleus it is not felt at all.
- **The weak force** changes one kind of quark or lepton into another. It causes beta decay (lesson 7.8), and it is the only force apart from gravity that a neutrino feels (8.286 Lecture 21, p. 22).

How strong are they? Comparing forces is tricky, because they depend on distance in different ways. A rough comparison for two particles at the distances and energies inside a nucleus is enough here, with the strong force set to 1:

| Force | Acts on | Range | Rough strength inside a nucleus |
|---|---|---|---|
| strong | quarks | about $10^{-15}\ \text{m}$ | $1$ |
| electromagnetic | charges | unlimited | about $1/137 \approx 10^{-2}$ |
| weak | quarks and leptons | about $10^{-18}\ \text{m}$ | about $10^{-5}$ |
| gravity | all mass and energy | unlimited | about $6 \times 10^{-39}$ |

The strong force is "of order 1" in MIT's 8.701 course (Lecture 1.3); the value $1/137$ for electromagnetism is the fine-structure constant (PDG). The weak value is our own estimate from the measured strength of the weak force (PDG's Fermi constant times the square of the proton's rest energy). Its basic charge is in fact about as strong as the electric one; the weak force is so feeble only because its carriers are so heavy (8.701 Lecture 1.3). For gravity, lesson 6.13 found that between two protons it is $1.24 \times 10^{36}$ times weaker than the electric force, and $(1/137)/(1.24 \times 10^{36}) = 5.9 \times 10^{-39}$. The exact numbers depend on how you compare, but the order is always the same: strong, electromagnetic, weak, gravity.

Gravity is by far the weakest, yet it rules the universe on large scales. Lesson 6.13 showed why: large bodies are electrically neutral, so the electric forces cancel, while gravity only attracts and always adds up. The strong and weak forces cannot compete at all beyond the size of a nucleus.

## Forces as exchanged particles

In the modern picture, a force acts because the two particles **emit and absorb** a third particle, a force carrier (MIT 8.701 Lecture 1.1, slide 5). Picture two skaters on ice throwing a heavy ball to and fro: each throw and each catch pushes them apart, although they never touch. The analogy only shows a repulsion, and real force carriers are quantum particles, not balls, but it gives the idea: a force is a stream of particles passed between the particles of matter.

Each force has its own carriers (8.286 Lecture 21, p. 22; 8.701 Lecture 1.3):

| Force | Carrier | Rest energy of the carrier (PDG) |
|---|---|---|
| electromagnetic | photon | $0$ |
| strong | gluon | $0$ |
| weak | W⁺, W⁻ and Z bosons | $80.4\ \text{GeV}$ (W) and $91.2\ \text{GeV}$ (Z) |
| gravity | graviton (hypothetical, never detected) | $0$ expected |

Here $1\ \text{GeV} = 10^9\ \text{eV} = 1000\ \text{MeV}$, so a W boson is about 86 times heavier than a proton (938 MeV). For the photon and the graviton the PDG tables give only upper limits on the mass: if it is not zero, it is too small to measure.

The mass of the carrier sets the **range** of the force. A massless carrier, like the photon, gives a force that reaches across any distance. A heavy carrier gives a force that dies away quickly: the MIT 8.701 lecture shows that adding a mass to the force particle makes the force fade much faster with distance, and the heavier the particle, the shorter the range. A rough estimate of the range is

<Formula label="Range of a force carried by a particle of mass m" tex="r \approx \frac{h}{2\pi m c}" cite="reading2" at="p. 1: a massive force carrier gives a Yukawa potential whose range shrinks as the mass grows (the estimate is the page's own)" />

The strong force is a puzzle here: its gluons are massless, yet the force does not reach beyond a nucleus. Part of the answer is confinement again. Gluons, like quarks, cannot travel freely, so the strong force between quarks stays locked inside protons and neutrons (8.286 Lecture 21, p. 24). The force between whole nucleons, which lesson 7.7 found holds each nucleon only to its nearest neighbours, is a leftover of it, carried mainly by **pions**, the lightest particles made of a quark and an antiquark. A pion has a rest energy of about $140\ \text{MeV}$ (PDG: 139.57 MeV for a charged pion), so $mc^2 = 140 \times 10^{6} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 2.24 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{J}$ and $r \approx hc/(2\pi mc^2) = 6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}/(2\pi \times 2.24 \times 10^{-11}) = 1.4 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{m}$: just the range of the nuclear force.

## The Higgs boson

Why does the W boson have a mass at all, when the photon has none? And where do the masses of the quarks and the electron come from? The answer of the Standard Model, the theory of all the particles and forces in this lesson apart from gravity, is the **Higgs field**: a field that fills all of space. Particles that interact with it get a rest mass; particles that do not, like the photon, stay massless (MIT 8.286 Lecture 22, pp. 7 and 12–13). In the Standard Model the neutrinos get no mass from it either; their tiny masses need something extra (8.286 Lecture 22, p. 12). A field also has its own particle, and for the Higgs field it is the **Higgs boson**. Its discovery was announced on 4 July 2012 by the ATLAS and CMS experiments at CERN's Large Hadron Collider (US Department of Energy, Office of Science), and today's measured rest energy is $125\ \text{GeV}$ (PDG: 125.20 GeV). Even so, the Higgs field accounts only for the masses of the elementary particles. The up and down quarks in a proton add up to just $2 \times 2.16 + 4.70 = 9.0\ \text{MeV}$ (PDG), about 1% of the proton's $938\ \text{MeV}$; almost all the rest is the energy of the quarks' motion and of the strong force that binds them, which counts as mass through $E = mc^2$.

## Forces that were once one

The strengths in the table are measured at the energies we can reach. At higher energies they change, and they become more alike. The Higgs field is what makes the weak force and electromagnetism look so different today: at very high energies the two would be governed by one symmetry, which the Higgs field breaks as the universe cools (8.286 Lecture 22, pp. 6–7). Our own rough estimate of where this happens is the rest energy of the W and Z: at energies of about $100\ \text{GeV}$ and above, the two act as a single **electroweak** force.

Physicists have measured the strengths of the strong, weak and electromagnetic forces near $100\ \text{GeV}$ and worked out how they should change at higher energies. To good accuracy, the three meet at about $10^{16}\ \text{GeV}$:

<Formula label="Energy at which the three forces would meet" tex="E_\text{GUT} \approx 10^{16}\ \text{GeV}" cite="reading4" at="p. 16: the three interaction strengths, extrapolated from about 100 GeV, meet near 10¹⁶ GeV" />

This is the basis of the **grand unified theories**: above this energy the strong, weak and electromagnetic forces would be one force, which looks like three at lower energies (8.286 Lecture 23, p. 16). Grand unification is attractive but has not been confirmed. Gravity is left out altogether: no tested theory yet brings it into the same scheme (8.286 Lecture 21, p. 23).

No accelerator comes anywhere near $10^{16}\ \text{GeV}$. But the early universe did. In a hot gas a typical particle has a kinetic energy of about $\tfrac32 k_B T$ (lesson 4.4). That rule is for particles much slower than light; at these energies they move at nearly the speed of light, and it gives only the order of magnitude of the temperature, which is all we need here: $T \approx 2E/(3k_B)$.

</Step>

$E = 10^{16} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-10} = 1.6 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J}$ for a single particle, so $T = 2 \times 1.6 \times 10^{6}/(3 \times 1.38 \times 10^{-23}) = 7.7 \times 10^{28}\ \text{K}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Temperatures like these existed only in the first tiny fraction of a second after the Big Bang (lesson 8.13). As the universe expanded and cooled, the Higgs field settled into its present value and the strong, weak and electromagnetic forces separated into the three we see today (8.286 Lecture 22, p. 7).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The universe as a particle accelerator">
To understand the first moments of the universe, cosmologists need particle physics at energies no laboratory can reach. In MIT's cosmology course, Guth sketches the Standard Model and grand unified theories for exactly this reason: if they are right, the hot early universe should have made magnetic monopoles, far too many of them, which is one of the puzzles that led to the theory of inflation (lesson 8.18).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Ordinary matter is made of **up quarks** ($+\tfrac23 e$) and **down quarks** ($-\tfrac13 e$), which make protons (up, up, down) and neutrons (up, down, down), and of **leptons**: the electron and the neutrino. Quarks are confined: they are never seen alone.
- Every particle has an **antiparticle** with the same mass and opposite charge. A particle and its antiparticle **annihilate** into energy; an electron and a positron at rest give two gamma-ray photons of $0.511\ \text{MeV}$ each. A photon with at least $1.022\ \text{MeV}$ can create an electron–positron pair.
- There are four forces: **strong** (range about $10^{-15}\ \text{m}$), **electromagnetic** (unlimited), **weak** (about $10^{-18}\ \text{m}$) and **gravity** (unlimited). Inside a nucleus their rough strengths are $1$, $10^{-2}$, $10^{-5}$ and $6 \times 10^{-39}$.
- Forces act by the exchange of carrier particles: gluons, photons, the W and Z bosons, and perhaps gravitons. A heavy carrier gives a short range, $r \approx h/(2\pi mc)$; the W gives about $2.5 \times 10^{-18}\ \text{m}$.
- The **Higgs field** fills space and gives the quarks, the electron and the W and Z their rest mass; its particle, the Higgs boson ($125\ \text{GeV}$), was announced in 2012.
- At high energies the forces become alike: electroweak unification above about $100\ \text{GeV}$, and possibly grand unification near $10^{16}\ \text{GeV}$, energies reached only in the very early universe.
