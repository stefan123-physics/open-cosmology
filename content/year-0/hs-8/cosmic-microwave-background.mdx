Lesson 8.13 ran the expansion backwards to a hot, dense start, and lesson 8.14 showed that the first few minutes made the nuclei of hydrogen and helium. If the young universe was that hot, it must have glowed, like the hot objects of lesson 7.2. This lesson shows that its glow is still here. It fills the whole sky, it arrives as microwaves, and it is the oldest light anyone can see.

## A glowing fog clears

In the early universe the temperature was far too high for atoms to exist. The nuclei and the electrons moved about separately: the matter was **ionised**, as in the hot gas of a star. Free electrons are very good at scattering light. A photon could travel only a short way before an electron knocked it in a new direction, so light zigzagged about and the universe was **opaque**, like a thick fog that glows from inside.

As the universe expanded it cooled. In Guth's account, by about 4000 K about half of the hydrogen had become neutral: electrons were caught by nuclei into atoms, as in the Bohr model of lesson 7.4. This is called **recombination**, even though, as Guth points out, the electrons and nuclei had never been combined before. A neutral atom hardly scatters light at all, unless the light has just the right energy for one of its spectral lines. So by about **3000 K** there were too few free electrons left, and a typical photon never scattered again. This is called **decoupling**. Guth estimates that it happened about **380 000 years** after the Big Bang (NASA's WMAP pages give about 375 000 years). From then on the universe was **transparent**.

Since decoupling these photons have flown in straight lines. When we catch one, we see where it last scattered, just as you see a face by the light that travels straight from it to your eye. Looking out in any direction, we therefore look back to a moment about 380 000 years after the start, at a great distance. That sphere of last scatterings all around us is called the **surface of last scattering**. Behind it, the fog hides everything earlier.

## A hiss from every direction

The radiation was found in 1965 by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, who were not looking for it. Their large radio antenna picked up a faint hiss that would not go away. As Guth tells it, they measured it at only one frequency, and convincing themselves that it was not noise in their own equipment was a real achievement: they compared it with a reference cooled by liquid helium, and the hiss was the same in every direction they pointed the antenna. Something that is equally bright everywhere cannot be one star or our own galaxy: it must fill the whole universe. Their paper was printed alongside a theory paper by Dicke, Peebles, Roll and Wilkinson, who explained the hiss as radiation left over from the hot beginning.

## A black body, cooled by the expansion

The fog before decoupling was dense and in **thermal equilibrium**, so its glow was black-body radiation (lesson 7.2): a spectrum fixed by the temperature alone. Its peak follows Wien's law, with the same constant as in lessons 7.2 and 7.6:

<Formula label="Wien's displacement law" tex="\lambda_{\max}\,T = 2.9 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m K}" cite="wien" at="Problem 1(c) (0.29 cm K, per unit of wavelength)" />

Since then the universe has grown, and every wavelength has grown with it: this is the cosmological redshift of lesson 5.13, with $1 + z = \lambda_\text{obs}/\lambda_\text{em}$ equal to the factor by which the universe has grown. Stretching every wavelength of a black-body spectrum by the same factor gives a black-body spectrum again, at a lower temperature. Guth writes the rule as

<Formula label="The radiation cools as the universe grows" tex="T \propto \frac{1}{a}" cite="reading2" at="p. 18 (board)" />

where $a$ is the scale factor of lesson 8.13, which already met this rule: twice the size, half the temperature. Written with the redshift (our own step), radiation that reaches us with redshift $z$ was hotter when it set out:

<Formula label="Temperature then and now" tex="T_\text{then} = (1 + z)\,T_\text{now}" cite="reading2" at="p. 18 (board): T ∝ 1/a, combined with 1 + z = (size now)/(size then) from lesson 5.13" />

Today the radiation has a temperature of $2.725\ \text{K}$, as measured by NASA's COBE satellite ($2.725 \pm 0.002\ \text{K}$; NASA, *COBE FIRAS Overview*).

<WorkedExample title="The oldest light, then and now">
<Problem>
Lesson 7.11 found where the spectrum of the background peaks now and where it peaked at decoupling, and how much the universe has grown since. Recall those results, then go one step further: how many times more power did each square metre of the glowing fog radiate at decoupling than a black body at $2.725\ \text{K}$ radiates now?
</Problem>
<Step title="What lesson 7.11 found">
Now: $\lambda_{\max} = (2.9 \times 10^{-3})/2.725 = 1.06 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 1.06\ \text{mm}$, in the microwave band (lesson 5.8), hence the name **cosmic microwave background**, or CMB. At decoupling: $\lambda_{\max} = (2.9 \times 10^{-3})/3000 = 9.7 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 970\ \text{nm}$, near infrared, just beyond the red end of the visible band at 700 nm. The stretch: $1 + z = T_\text{then}/T_\text{now} = 3000/2.725 = 1100$, the same as the ratio of the peaks, $(1.06 \times 10^{-3})/(9.7 \times 10^{-7}) = 1100$. Since decoupling every distance in the universe has grown about 1100 times.
</Step>
<Step title="What it looked like">
Like the light bulb of lesson 7.2, the young universe glowed mostly in the infrared. Only a small share of its power was visible light, but to an eye inside the fog it would have looked like the warm white of an old light bulb, shining from every direction.
</Step>
<Step title="How much brighter">
By the Stefan–Boltzmann law of lesson 7.2 the power per square metre grows as $T^4$, so the ratio is $(3000/2.725)^4 = 1100^4 = 1.5 \times 10^{12}$. The expansion explains this huge factor: each photon's wavelength has grown 1100 times, so its energy has dropped 1100 times, and the photons are spread through a volume $1100^3 = 1.3 \times 10^9$ times larger. Together that is $1100 \times 1100^3 = 1100^4$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## COBE: a perfect black body

For 25 years the measurements were too scattered to prove that the spectrum really had the black-body shape. NASA's **COBE** satellite (Cosmic Background Explorer), launched on 18 November 1989, settled it. In January 1990 its team showed a spectrum that, in Guth's words, was a fantastically good fit to a black body. So precise a black body can only come from radiation that was in thermal equilibrium with matter, and that needs a very dense early universe: it is strong evidence for the hot Big Bang. John Mather and George Smoot shared the 2006 Nobel Prize in physics for the COBE results.

## Ripples: the seeds of galaxies

Once the small effect of our own motion through the radiation is removed, the background is the same in every direction to about one part in 100 000. COBE first found the tiny differences in 1992. NASA's **WMAP** (Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe, launched in 2001, observing until 2010) mapped them across the whole sky in much finer detail, and ESA's **Planck** satellite (launched in 2009) mapped them again even more sharply. These differences are called **anisotropies**.

<WorkedExample title="How small is one part in 100 000?">
<Problem>
A typical ripple in the background is about one part in 100 000 of its temperature. How large is that in kelvin?
</Problem>
<Step title="Divide">
$2.725/100\,000 = 2.7 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{K}$, about 27 millionths of a kelvin. Two patches of WMAP's map can differ by only about two ten-thousandths of a kelvin. Measuring that from space, against a sky at under 3 K, is why these missions took decades to build.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The ripples show where matter was a little denser or a little thinner at decoupling. The MIT 8.902 notes explain that dark matter (lesson 8.16) had already begun to gather into clumps by then, and its gravity made the light from those places slightly cooler or warmer. Gravity kept pulling more matter into the denser regions. Over billions of years the clumps grew into the galaxies and clusters of galaxies we see today, which is why the ripples are called the **seeds of galaxies**. The notes add that without dark matter, ripples about a hundred times larger would have been needed to grow today's galaxies in time.

The pattern of hot and cold spots, how large they typically are and how strong they are at each size, depends on what the universe contains. From WMAP's map cosmologists found that ordinary atoms make up about 5% of the universe, dark matter about 25% and dark energy about 70%, that space is very nearly flat, and that the universe is about 13.8 billion years old (lesson 8.12), with WMAP's final results fixing the age to within half a percent. Planck's maps gave the same picture more sharply. The oldest light in the universe has become its most precise measuring instrument.

## Summary

- The early universe was ionised: free electrons scattered light, so it was an opaque, glowing fog.
- As it cooled, electrons were caught into neutral atoms (**recombination**). By about **3000 K**, some **380 000 years** after the start, the photons stopped scattering (**decoupling**) and the universe became transparent. The **surface of last scattering** is where the photons we receive scattered for the last time.
- Penzias and Wilson found the radiation in 1965 as a hiss that was the same in every direction.
- It is black-body radiation, cooled as the universe grew: $T \propto 1/a$, so $T_\text{then} = (1 + z)\,T_\text{now}$. Since decoupling the universe has grown about **1100** times.
- Today it has $2.725\ \text{K}$; by Wien's law its peak is at $1.06\ \text{mm}$, in the microwave band. At decoupling the peak was near 970 nm.
- **COBE** showed a near-perfect black-body spectrum and first found ripples of about **one part in 100 000**; **WMAP** and **Planck** mapped them in detail. The ripples are the seeds of the galaxies, and their pattern gives the age (about 13.8 billion years) and the contents of the universe.
