The High School began with powers of ten and a ladder of sizes from the proton to the observable universe (lesson 0.11). It ends here, at the edge of what anyone knows. This last lesson looks back at the road you have travelled, lists the great questions that are still open, and shows where the university programme picks up each thread.

Put together, these lessons tell one story. The universe is expanding (lesson 8.11), and running the expansion backwards gives an age of about 13.8 billion years (lesson 8.12). It began hot, dense and uniform (lesson 8.13), made its hydrogen and helium in the first few minutes (lesson 8.14) and released the cosmic microwave background, now at 2.725 K, about 380 000 years later (lesson 8.15; NASA LAMBDA, *COBE FIRAS Overview*). Measured in this way, the universe is about 5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter and 68% dark energy (NASA Science, *Dark Matter*).

That last sentence is also where the open questions begin: the part of the universe that physics understands well is the 5%.

## What is dark matter?

Lesson 8.16 showed that galaxies and clusters hold far more mass than their stars and gas: something pulls by gravity but gives off no light. It is not ordinary gas, and for the most part not dim stars or other dark objects (lesson 8.16). The leading ideas are new kinds of particle, such as WIMPs and axions, and some have suggested black holes formed in the very early universe (NASA Science, *Dark Matter*). Experiments are searching for them, but so far none of these candidates has been found. Dark matter is about five times as common as the matter we are made of, and nobody knows what it is.

## What is dark energy?

Since 1998, when two teams found that distant Type Ia supernovae are dimmer than expected, astronomers have known that the expansion of the universe is speeding up (lesson 8.17; NASA Science, *Dark Energy*). Whatever causes this is called **dark energy**. As Guth puts it in his first 8.286 lecture, dark energy is by definition whatever causes the acceleration, and asked what it really is, the honest answer is that nobody knows (Lecture 1, p. 25).

The most plausible candidate, Guth says, is the energy of empty space itself, the **vacuum energy**. Because of the uncertainty principle (lesson 8.6), quantum physics says that even empty space is never completely still: its fields keep fluctuating, so it is no surprise that it has some energy (Lecture 1, pp. 26–27). The surprise is how little. A simple quantum estimate, cut off at the tiny length where quantum gravity should take over (about $10^{-33}\ \text{cm}$), comes out too large by about 120 orders of magnitude: a factor of 1 followed by 120 zeros (Lecture 1, p. 27). This mismatch is known as the cosmological constant problem. Other ideas for dark energy have been suggested too: a new field that changes over time, or a flaw in general relativity itself (NASA Science, *Dark Energy*).

## What drove inflation, and what came before?

Lesson 8.18 explained inflation: in a tiny fraction of a second the young universe grew enormously, which explains why it is so uniform and so flat, and stretched quantum ripples into the seeds of galaxies. But what powered it? Scientists are not sure what came before inflation or what drove it (NASA Science, *Universe: Overview*).

Guth, one of the inventors of inflation, is just as careful about the Big Bang itself. We have a very good picture of the Big Bang, he says, but we do not really know that the universe *began* with it: whether anything came before is, in his words, completely open (Lecture 1, p. 2). In many models inflation, once started, never stops everywhere: it goes on forever into the future, though not into the past, making one "pocket universe" after another (Lecture 1, p. 24). Whether our universe is one of many, a **multiverse**, is one of the boldest open questions in physics, and 8.286 discusses it in its first two lectures.

## Why is there matter at all?

Lesson 8.7 showed that every particle has an antiparticle, and that the two annihilate into energy when they meet. In the hot early universe particles and antiparticles were made in pairs and destroyed in pairs. As it cooled, the pairs annihilated. If there had been exactly as much matter as antimatter, nothing but light would have been left: no atoms, no stars, no us (Ferreras, Sec. 6.1, pp. 135–136).

Something must have tipped the balance. The annihilations made the photons that still fill the universe, and there are about a billion of them for every proton or neutron. So the excess of matter was tiny: for every billion or so particles that met an antiparticle, about one was left over. Every atom in your body is made from that small surplus. The laws of physics as we know them do not explain where it came from, and finding out is one of the great open problems of particle physics (Ferreras, Sec. 6.1, pp. 135–136).

## Why do two ways of measuring the expansion disagree?

Lesson 8.11 measured the expansion rate, the Hubble constant, with a ladder of distances: Cepheids and Type Ia supernovae (lesson 8.10). It can also be worked out in another way, from the pattern of ripples in the cosmic microwave background (lesson 8.15), together with a model of how the universe has grown since. The two ought to agree. As a later development (after Guth's 2013 lectures), they do not: the background radiation gives about 67 to 68 km/s/Mpc, while space-telescope measurements of Cepheids and supernovae give about 70 to 76 km/s/Mpc (NASA Science, *Hubble Constant and Tension*).

This disagreement is called the **Hubble tension**. In 2024 a team using the Webb telescope checked the Cepheid measurements of the Hubble telescope and confirmed them, so a simple measurement error now looks very unlikely (NASA Science, *NASA's Webb, Hubble Telescopes Affirm Universe's Expansion Rate, Puzzle Persists*). Perhaps something is missing from our picture of the early universe: new particles, a change to gravity, or an early form of dark energy have all been suggested (NASA Science, *Hubble Constant and Tension*). Nobody knows yet.

## The singularity and quantum gravity

Lesson 8.9 met the **singularity** at the centre of a black hole: general relativity predicts that everything falls on to a point of infinite density. Nobody knows what really happens there; the prediction probably marks the place where general relativity itself stops working (NASA Science, *Black Hole Anatomy*). The very first instant of the universe raises the same problem: the further back you go, the denser and hotter it gets, until the known laws can no longer say what happened (Lecture 8, p. 20).

The two great theories of the twentieth century, general relativity for gravity (lesson 8.8) and quantum physics for everything small (lessons 8.6 and 8.7), have never been joined into one theory of **quantum gravity**. It should matter at lengths of about $10^{-33}\ \text{cm}$ (Lecture 1, p. 27), far smaller than anything an experiment can reach. Without it, nobody can say what really happens inside a black hole or at the first instant.

The universe will most likely keep expanding forever (NASA Science, *Universe: Overview*). Guth, asked about the ultimate future, first says that nobody really knows. In the models he describes, our part of the universe thins out until life becomes impossible, because the matter is spread too thin, while the whole goes on expanding, as far as anyone can tell, forever (Lecture 2, p. 8). If dark energy is not constant after all, the story could change, which is one more reason why measuring it is so important.

## How far can we see?

Here is one last estimate, tying the first lessons of the High School to the last ones. Lesson 1.11 showed that light covers a distance $d = ct$ in a time $t$. Light from the most distant sources has been travelling for almost the whole age of the universe. But while it travelled, space went on expanding behind it, so its source is now much farther away than $ct$. For a universe that contains only matter, Guth works out in 8.286 how much farther:

<Formula label="Distance today to the source of the oldest light (matter only)" tex="d = 3\,c\,t_0" cite="reading3" at="p. 19 (board): the horizon distance of a flat universe of matter only" />

where $t_0$ is the age of the universe. In practice the oldest light we can see is the cosmic microwave background, which set out about 380 000 years after the Big Bang (lesson 8.15); its source is only a little closer than $3ct_0$.

<WorkedExample title="The size of the visible universe">
<Problem>
Take the age of the universe as $t_0 = 13.8$ billion years and one light-year as $9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m}$. How far does light travel in that time? And how far away, in a universe of matter only, is the source of that light today?
</Problem>
<Step title="The light's own journey">
In 13.8 billion years light travels 13.8 billion light-years: $1.38 \times 10^{10} \times 9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m} = 1.31 \times 10^{26}\ \text{m}$. That is the "about $10^{26}$ m" of lesson 0.11.
</Step>
<Step title="Where the source is now">
$d = 3ct_0 = 3 \times 13.8 = 41.4$ billion light-years, or $3.92 \times 10^{26}\ \text{m}$: three times as far as the light itself travelled.
</Step>
<Step title="What it means">
The visible universe is far bigger than "13.8 billion light-years away" suggests. Our real universe is not made of matter only, and its dark energy changes the number somewhat; 8.286 shows how to work it out. Beyond that distance there is almost certainly more universe, whose light has not reached us yet.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The High School built one story: an expanding universe about **13.8 billion years** old that began hot and dense, made hydrogen and helium in its first minutes and left the cosmic microwave background, now at 2.725 K.
- The universe is about **5% ordinary matter, 27% dark matter and 68% dark energy**: most of it is not understood.
- Open questions: what **dark matter** is; what **dark energy** is (the simplest quantum estimate of the energy of empty space is about 120 orders of magnitude too large); what powered **inflation** and whether anything came **before**; why a tiny excess of **matter over antimatter** survived; why two measurements of the expansion rate disagree (the **Hubble tension**, a later development); and what happens at a **singularity**, which needs a theory of **quantum gravity**.
- With constant dark energy the universe most likely **expands forever**, faster and faster, while matter thins out; but nobody really knows.
- Light that travelled for a time $t_0$ covered $ct_0$, but its source is now farther away: $3ct_0$ in a universe of matter only, about 41 billion light-years for $t_0 = 13.8$ billion years.
- The university programme starts in Year I with **8.01 mechanics** and **18.01 calculus** and leads, through relativity, quantum and statistical physics, to the cosmology of **8.286**.
