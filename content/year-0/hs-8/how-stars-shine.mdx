Lesson 7.6 measured the Sun's luminosity, and lesson 8.3 showed that by shining, the Sun loses about 4 million tonnes of mass every second. Lesson 8.4 found where such energy can come from: joining light nuclei. This lesson puts the pieces together. It asks what keeps the Sun shining, and for so long, and follows the energy from the core, where it is made, to the surface, where it leaves as sunlight.

## The puzzle: so much power, for so long

The Sun radiates $L = 3.828 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}$ (NASA Sun Fact Sheet). It has a mass of $M = 1.99 \times 10^{30}\ \text{kg}$ and a radius of $R = 6.96 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}$, and it is about 4.5 billion years old (NASA, *Sun: Facts*). The MIT lecture asks the questions that were still open within living memory: what is the Sun, why does it shine, and why for so long (slide 10)?

Any store of energy $E$ that is spent at the rate $L$ lasts a time

<Formula label="How long a store of energy lasts" tex="t = \frac{E}{L}" cite="mit" at="the definition of power, P = E/t (lesson 3.5), rearranged; slide 10 asks why the Sun shines for so long" />

So the question becomes: which store of energy in the Sun is big enough to give $t$ of billions of years? We use $1\ \text{year} = 3.156 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s}$ throughout.

<WorkedExample title="Could the Sun be burning?">
<Problem>
Burning carbon, C + O₂ → CO₂, gives off $393\ \text{kJ}$ for every mole of carbon (Siyavula, Grade 11, Sec. 12.2), and a mole of carbon has a mass of $12.0\ \text{g}$. Suppose, very generously, that the whole Sun were carbon and the oxygen came free. How long could it shine?
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy per kilogram">
$393 \times 10^{3}\ \text{J}/0.0120\ \text{kg} = 3.3 \times 10^{7}\ \text{J/kg}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The whole Sun">
$E = 3.3 \times 10^{7} \times 1.99 \times 10^{30} = 6.5 \times 10^{37}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Time">
$t = E/L = 6.5 \times 10^{37}/(3.828 \times 10^{26}) = 1.7 \times 10^{11}\ \text{s}$, which is about 5400 years: roughly as long as people have been writing. The Sun has already shone almost a million times longer.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

A second idea is gravity. When a ball of gas shrinks, its parts fall closer together and lose gravitational potential energy, which heats the gas, and hot gas glows (lesson 7.2). Lesson 3.11 gave the potential energy of two masses at distance $r$, $E_p = -GMm/r$. For a whole star the masses are all of the order of $M$ and the distances of the order of $R$, so the energy released in pulling the Sun together from a thin, spread-out cloud is roughly

<Formula label="Gravitational energy of a star, order of magnitude" tex="E \approx \frac{G M^2}{R}" cite="mit801ch14" at="Sec. 14.4.3, Eq. (14.16) of that section, U = −Gm₁m₂/r with both masses of the order of M and r of the order of R; the estimate is the page's own" />

<WorkedExample title="Could the Sun run on its own weight?">
<Problem>
Estimate how long gravity alone could keep the Sun shining at today's rate. Use $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Gravitational energy">
$E \approx GM^2/R = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times (1.99 \times 10^{30})^2/(6.96 \times 10^{8}) = 3.8 \times 10^{41}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Time">
$t = E/L = 3.8 \times 10^{41}/(3.828 \times 10^{26}) = 9.9 \times 10^{14}\ \text{s}$, which is $3.1 \times 10^{7}$ years, about 30 million years.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The answer: nuclear fusion

Lesson 8.4 showed that joining light nuclei into helium releases millions of times more energy per kilogram than any chemical reaction. The core of the Sun is the place where this can happen (NASA, *Sun: Facts*). Its centre is at about $1.6 \times 10^{7}\ \text{K}$ (NASA Sun Fact Sheet), and at such temperatures nuclei move fast enough to come close despite their electric repulsion, so the short-range strong force can join them (MIT slide 12; lesson 8.4). Even so, only the very fastest nuclei get close enough, helped by a quantum effect called tunnelling that lesson 8.6 meets. The Sun is mostly hydrogen: about 70% of its mass, with 28% helium and small amounts of oxygen, carbon and iron (slides 8 and 9, read from the dark lines in its spectrum, lesson 7.5). So the fuel is hydrogen nuclei: protons.

Two protons cannot simply stick together to make helium-4, which has two protons *and* two neutrons. On the way, protons must turn into neutrons. Lesson 7.8 met the process: in beta-plus decay a proton turns into a neutron, giving out a positron $\mathrm{e}^+$ and a neutrino $\nu$ (MIT slide 14). The Sun's main route, the **proton–proton chain**, takes three steps (slide 15):

<Formula label="Step 1: two protons make deuterium" tex="{}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H} + {}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H} \to {}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H} + {}^{\;\;0}_{+1}\mathrm{e} + \nu" cite="mit" at="slide 15 (the chain as a diagram; slide 14 for the proton that turns into a neutron); the equations are written out by the page" />

<Formula label="Step 2: deuterium and a proton make helium-3" tex="{}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H} + {}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H} \to {}^{3}_{2}\mathrm{He} + \gamma" cite="mit" at="slide 15" />

<Formula label="Step 3: two helium-3 nuclei make helium-4" tex="{}^{3}_{2}\mathrm{He} + {}^{3}_{2}\mathrm{He} \to {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He} + {}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H} + {}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}" cite="mit" at="slide 15" />

Steps 1 and 2 must each happen twice to make the two helium-3 nuclei for step 3, and step 3 gives two protons back. In all, six protons go in and two come out again, so four protons have become one helium-4 nucleus (slide 15). The two positrons each meet an electron of the hot gas, and both are destroyed and turn into gamma rays (slide 15 counts the two electrons going in; lesson 8.7 explains this annihilation). The first step is by far the slowest, because a proton turning into a neutron is an extremely rare, slow process; lesson 8.7 meets the force behind it, the weak force. That is lucky: it is why the Sun burns its fuel slowly instead of all at once.

Each step obeys the counting rule of lesson 7.8. In step 1, for example, the nucleon numbers are $1 + 1 = 2 + 0$ and the charges $1 + 1 = 1 + 1$.

## How much energy, and how much fuel?

Count the whole chain: four protons and the two electrons that later meet the positrons go in, one helium-4 nucleus comes out, along with gamma rays and two neutrinos, which have almost no mass. The energy released is the mass that goes missing, times $c^2$ (lesson 8.4):

<Formula label="Energy from one proton–proton chain" tex="Q = \left(4m_p + 2m_e - m_{\text{He}}\right)c^2" cite="mit" at="slide 16 (the rest mass of He is 99.3% of 4 × the rest mass of H; the missing 0.7% becomes other forms of energy); writing the masses of the parts out is the page's own" />

<WorkedExample title="Energy from one helium nucleus">
<Problem>
A proton has a mass of $1.00728\ \text{u}$, an electron $0.00055\ \text{u}$ and a helium-4 nucleus $4.00151\ \text{u}$ (NIST values, rounded). Find the energy released by one chain, in MeV and in joules, and the fraction of the mass that turns into energy.
</Problem>
<Step title="Mass before and after">
Before: $4 \times 1.00728 + 2 \times 0.00055 = 4.03022\ \text{u}$. After: $4.00151\ \text{u}$. The mass lost is $0.02871\ \text{u}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy">
$Q = 0.02871 \times 931.5 = 26.7\ \text{MeV}$. With $1\ \text{MeV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-13}\ \text{J}$ this is $4.3 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Fraction">
$0.02871/4.03022 = 0.0071$: 0.7% of the mass of the hydrogen becomes energy, just as the MIT slide says. A kilogram of hydrogen gives $0.0071 \times (3.00 \times 10^{8})^2 = 6.4 \times 10^{14}\ \text{J}$, about 20 million times as much as a kilogram of burning carbon.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="The Sun's fuel bill">
<Problem>
How many helium nuclei must the Sun make every second to shine with $L = 3.828 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}$, and how much hydrogen does that use up? Use $m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Chains per second">
$L/Q = 3.828 \times 10^{26}/(4.28 \times 10^{-12}) = 8.94 \times 10^{37}$ helium nuclei every second.
</Step>
<Step title="Hydrogen used">
Each one uses four protons: $4 \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27} \times 8.94 \times 10^{37} = 6.0 \times 10^{11}\ \text{kg}$ every second, about 600 million tonnes.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with lesson 8.3">
Only 0.71% of that is lost as mass: $0.0071 \times 6.0 \times 10^{11} = 4.3 \times 10^{9}\ \text{kg/s}$, the 4 million tonnes per second that lesson 8.3 found from $L/c^2$. The rest is still there, now as helium.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The neutrinos made in step 1 hardly interact with anything. They fly straight out of the Sun and reach the Earth, $1.5 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}$ away (NASA, *Sun: Facts*), about 500 seconds later. Detecting them is a way to look directly into the core, which light cannot do (slide 18).

## Gravity against pressure

Why does the Sun not collapse under its own weight, or blow itself apart? Every layer is pulled inward by the gravity of everything below it, and it presses on the layers beneath. Deeper down, more weight presses from above, so the pressure grows with depth, as in the sea (lesson 4.1). In the centre it reaches about $2.5 \times 10^{16}\ \text{Pa}$, roughly $2.5 \times 10^{11}$ times the air pressure at the Earth's surface, and the gas is squeezed to $1.6 \times 10^{5}\ \text{kg/m}^3$, some 160 times the density of water (NASA Sun Fact Sheet).

What holds the layers up is the pressure of the hot gas pushing outward (NASA, *Sun: Facts*). By the ideal gas law (lesson 4.3), the pressure of a gas grows with its temperature, and fusion keeps the core hot. In a steady star the two balance in every layer: gravity pulls in, pressure pushes out.

The balance also works as a **thermostat**. If the fusion runs a little too fast, the core heats up, its pressure rises and it expands. A gas that expands cools, because it does work pushing its surroundings outward and that energy comes from its heat (lesson 4.8), and in cooler gas fewer nuclei are fast enough to fuse, so the fusion slows again. If it runs too slow, the core shrinks, heats up and the fusion speeds up. That is why the Sun has shone so steadily for billions of years.

## From the core to the surface

The energy made in the core, which reaches out to about $1.4 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}$ from the centre, a fifth of the radius, must still get out (NASA, *Sun: Facts*). It travels in two very different ways.

- **The radiation zone.** Just outside the core the energy travels as radiation: light, mostly X-rays at these temperatures (Wien's law, lesson 7.2). But the gas is so dense that a photon gets only a short way before it is absorbed. The energy is given out again in a random direction, absorbed again, and so on, countless times. It zigzags outward on an enormously long path. In empty space, light would cross the Sun's radius in $R/c = 6.96 \times 10^{8}/(3.00 \times 10^{8}) = 2.3\ \text{s}$; inside the Sun the energy needs about 170 000 years to reach the top of the convection zone (NASA, *Sun: Facts*).
- **The convection zone.** In the outer part of the Sun, below about 2 million kelvin, the gas is cool and opaque enough that radiation can no longer carry all the energy. Instead the gas itself moves: big bubbles of hot gas rise, give off their heat near the top and sink again, like water boiling in a pan (lesson 4.7).
- **The photosphere.** At the top, the gas becomes thin enough for light to escape straight into space. This thin layer is what we see as the Sun's surface, at about 5800 K (lesson 7.6), and from here most of the sunlight leaves (NASA, *Sun: Facts*).

<Callout kind="note" title="The light we see is old energy">
The sunlight reaching your eyes left the photosphere about 8 minutes ago, but the energy it carries was made in the core long before that, during its slow trip outward. The neutrinos from the same reactions arrive straight from the core.
</Callout>

## How long will the Sun shine?

The Sun is a little less than halfway through its life, with about 5 billion years still to go (NASA, *Sun: Facts*): a total of about 10 billion years. Is there enough hydrogen for that?

<WorkedExample title="Ten billion years of sunshine">
<Problem>
How much hydrogen does the Sun use in 10 billion years at today's rate of $6.0 \times 10^{11}\ \text{kg/s}$? What fraction of its mass, and of its hydrogen, is that?
</Problem>
<Step title="Time in seconds">
$10 \times 10^{9} \times 3.156 \times 10^{7} = 3.156 \times 10^{17}\ \text{s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Hydrogen used">
$6.0 \times 10^{11} \times 3.156 \times 10^{17} = 1.9 \times 10^{29}\ \text{kg}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Fractions">
$1.9 \times 10^{29}/(1.99 \times 10^{30}) = 0.095$: about a tenth of the Sun's mass. Since 70% of the Sun is hydrogen, this is about 14% of its hydrogen.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

So the Sun never burns most of its hydrogen. Only the core is hot and dense enough for fusion, and the gas in the core does not mix with the outer layers. When the core has turned its hydrogen into helium, the Sun's main-sequence life (lesson 7.6) ends. The core then shrinks and heats up, the outer layers swell, and the Sun becomes a red giant, large enough to swallow Mercury and Venus and perhaps the Earth (NASA, *Sun: Facts*). In its hotter core it fuses helium into carbon, and at the end its outer layers drift away and the core is left as a white dwarf (NASA, *Stars*).

## Heavier stars, heavier elements

The lifetime of a star is its fuel divided by its luminosity. A star with more mass has more fuel, but its stronger gravity squeezes its core harder and hotter, and it shines far more brightly. It uses its fuel so much faster that the heaviest stars live only a few million years, while the lightest, dim red stars may shine for trillions of years (NASA, *Stars*).

In a massive star the core gets hot enough for more steps. After helium has become carbon, carbon fuses into oxygen, neon and magnesium, and in the largest stars silicon fuses into iron. Once silicon fuses into iron, the star runs out of fuel within days (NASA, *Stars*). Then it stops, because iron sits at the peak of the binding energy curve of lesson 8.4: fusing iron would take energy instead of releasing it. The iron core collapses, and the star explodes as a supernova that scatters the new elements into space, where they become part of later stars and planets (NASA, *Stars*).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Stars and the first elements">
Stars are not where the first helium came from. As lesson 8.4 mentioned, the whole universe was hot and dense enough for fusion in the first few minutes after the Big Bang, and lesson 8.14 works out what it made then. Stars came much later: they have added more helium since, and built the carbon, oxygen and iron that later stars and planets, and we ourselves, are made of.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The Sun radiates $3.828 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}$ and is about 4.5 billion years old. A store of energy $E$ lasts $t = E/L$.
- Chemical burning would keep the Sun shining for only about 5400 years; shrinking under gravity, $E \approx GM^2/R$, for about 30 million years. Neither is enough: the Sun's energy is nuclear.
- In the core, at about 16 million kelvin, the **proton–proton chain** turns four protons into one helium-4 nucleus, with positrons, neutrinos and gamma rays. It releases $Q = (4m_p + 2m_e - m_{\text{He}})c^2 = 26.7\ \text{MeV}$: 0.7% of the mass of the hydrogen.
- To shine, the Sun makes $8.94 \times 10^{37}$ helium nuclei per second and uses $6.0 \times 10^{11}\ \text{kg}$ of hydrogen per second, of which $4.3 \times 10^{9}\ \text{kg}$ is lost as mass.
- Gravity pulls every layer inward and the pressure of the hot gas pushes it out. The balance acts as a thermostat that keeps the fusion steady.
- Energy leaves the core through the **radiation zone** (absorbed and given out again in random directions, about 170 000 years) and the **convection zone** (rising bubbles of hot gas), and escapes as light from the photosphere.
- The Sun will shine for about 10 billion years in all, fusing about a tenth of its mass. Heavier stars are far brighter and live shorter lives (the heaviest only a few million years), and they fuse heavier elements, up to iron.
