For thousands of years people watched the planets wander across the night sky, and for most of that time astronomers described their paths with circles on circles. Then, early in the 17th century, Johannes Kepler found three simple rules that fit the planets far better. He could not explain **why** they hold. Newton did: lesson 2.11 gave his law of gravitation, and in this lesson you will see how that law, together with the centripetal force of lesson 2.9, explains Kepler's rules. With them you can work out how fast the International Space Station moves, how high a television satellite must fly to hang above one spot on the Earth, and how long a year lasts on Mars.

## From Tycho's observations to Kepler's laws

Kepler's laws grew out of the best measurements of the sky made before the telescope. The Danish astronomer **Tycho Brahe** (1546 to 1601) built an observatory called Uraniborg on the island of Hven, with very large instruments, and measured the positions of the planets night after night for about twenty years (mactutorbrahe). A modern study of his instruments, quoted by the MacTutor history archive, found that most of his errors were between half and one arcminute, where an arcminute is a sixtieth of a degree: remarkable for observations made by eye.

In 1599 Tycho moved to Prague, and the young mathematician Johannes Kepler joined him there as an assistant (mactutorbrahe). Kepler was soon working on the hard problem of the orbit of Mars (mactutorkepler). Tycho died in 1601, and Kepler, with Tycho's observations in hand, kept going. According to his MacTutor biography, nearly a thousand pages of his calculations survive, and he called the work his "war with Mars" (mactutorkepler). He found that the orbit of Mars is not a circle but an ellipse, and that Mars moves faster when it is closer to the Sun. He published these first two laws in his *Astronomia nova* (New Astronomy) in 1609 and found the third, which links the periods of the planets to the sizes of their orbits, while his *Harmony of the World* of 1619 was already being printed (mactutorkepler).

The MIT course notes state the three laws as follows (Sec. 25.1), and the rest of this lesson takes them one at a time:

1. Each planet moves on an **ellipse** with the Sun at one **focus**.
2. The line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out **equal areas in equal times**.
3. The square of a planet's period is proportional to the cube of the **semi-major axis** of its ellipse, with the same constant for every planet.

## The first law: ellipses

An **ellipse** is a stretched circle. You can draw one with two drawing pins, a loop of string and a pencil: push the pins into a board, put the loop round them, pull it tight with the pencil and move the pencil round. Each pin marks a **focus** (plural: **foci**) of the ellipse. Because the string keeps the same length, the distances from any point of the ellipse to the two foci always add up to the same total. The further apart the pins, the flatter the ellipse; with both pins in the same hole you draw a circle.

Three numbers describe an ellipse:

- the **semi-major axis** $a$: half of its longest width, the major axis, which runs through both foci;
- the **semi-minor axis** $b$: half of its shortest width;
- the **eccentricity** $e$, which says how flat it is: the distance from the centre to a focus is $e\,a$. A circle has $e = 0$; the flatter the ellipse, the closer $e$ comes to 1.

Kepler's first law puts the Sun at one focus. The other focus is empty: nothing is there. So the planet's distance from the Sun changes as it goes round. Its closest point is called the **perihelion** and its furthest point the **aphelion**; both lie at the ends of the major axis. The MIT notes give these distances, and the semi-minor axis, in terms of $a$ and $e$ (there written with the Greek letter ε):

<Formula label="Distance at perihelion and at aphelion" tex="r_p = a\,(1 - e), \qquad r_a = a\,(1 + e)" cite="mit" at="Appendix 25B.2, Eqs. (25.B.7) and (25.B.8) (written there with ε)" />

<Formula label="Semi-minor axis of an ellipse" tex="b = a\sqrt{1 - e^2}" cite="mit" at="Appendix 25C, Eq. (25.C.5) (written there with ε)" />

Adding the two distances gives $r_p + r_a = 2a$: the semi-major axis is the average of the closest and furthest distances. Astronomers therefore often call $a$ the planet's mean distance from the Sun.

The orbits of the planets are only slightly squashed. The Earth's eccentricity is 0.0167 and that of Mars 0.0935 (NASA, Earth and Mars Fact Sheets): drawn to scale, both look like circles, and you can only tell that the Sun is off-centre. That is why astronomers before Kepler could get so far with circles, and why it took Tycho's careful data to tell the difference.

<WorkedExample title="How far is Mars from the Sun?">
<Problem>
The orbit of Mars has a semi-major axis of $227.956 \times 10^{6}$ km and an eccentricity of 0.0935 (NASA, Mars Fact Sheet). Find its distances from the Sun at perihelion and at aphelion, and how many times further away it is at aphelion.
</Problem>
<Step title="Perihelion">
$r_p = a(1 - e) = 227.956 \times 10^{6} \times (1 - 0.0935) \approx 206.6 \times 10^{6}$ km.
</Step>
<Step title="Aphelion">
$r_a = a(1 + e) = 227.956 \times 10^{6} \times 1.0935 \approx 249.3 \times 10^{6}$ km.
</Step>
<Step title="Ratio">
$r_a/r_p = 249.3/206.6 \approx 1.21$: Mars is about 21% further from the Sun at aphelion than at perihelion.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The two distances add up to $455.9 \times 10^{6}$ km, twice the semi-major axis, as they must. The ratio also follows from $e$ alone: $(1 + e)/(1 - e) = 1.0935/0.9065 \approx 1.21$, the same.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The second law: equal areas in equal times

Draw a line from the Sun to a planet. As the planet moves, this line sweeps across the inside of the ellipse like the hand of a clock, but a clock whose hand changes length. Kepler's second law says that in equal times the line sweeps out **equal areas**: in one week near perihelion it covers the same area as in one week near aphelion. Near the Sun the line is short, so to sweep the same area the planet must move further along its orbit. A planet therefore moves **fastest at perihelion and slowest at aphelion**.

You can put this into a formula. In a short time $\Delta t$ the planet moves a small distance, and the line sweeps out a thin triangle with the Sun at its tip. Its long side is the distance $r$; its height is how far the planet moves at right angles to the line, $v_\perp\,\Delta t$, where $v_\perp$ is the part of the velocity perpendicular to the line to the Sun. The area of a triangle is half its base times its height, so

<Formula label="Area swept out in a short time" tex="\Delta A = \tfrac{1}{2}\,r\,v_\perp\,\Delta t" cite="mit" at="Sec. 25.6.2, Eqs. (25.5.2) to (25.5.4) (numbered 25.5.x in the notes; written there as dA/dt = ½ r² dθ/dt, and r dθ/dt = v⊥)" />

Equal areas in equal times means that $r\,v_\perp$ stays the same all the way round. At perihelion and at aphelion the velocity is exactly at right angles to the line to the Sun, so there $v_\perp$ is the whole speed, and

<Formula label="Speeds at perihelion and aphelion" tex="r_p\,v_p = r_a\,v_a" cite="mit" at="Sec. 25.7, Example 25.1, Eq. (25.6.2) (the satellite's mass cancelled)" />

**Why** does $r\,v_\perp$ stay the same? The torque of lesson 2.10 gives the answer, and the MIT notes make this step in Sec. 25.3. The pull of the Sun points straight at the Sun, so about the Sun it has no lever arm and no torque. Nothing twists the planet's motion round the Sun: gravity can speed the planet up as it falls inwards and slow it down as it climbs outwards, but always in a way that keeps $r\,v_\perp$ fixed. Multiplied by the planet's mass, $m\,r\,v_\perp$ is called the **angular momentum** of the planet about the Sun, and the MIT notes derive the second law from the fact that it does not change (Sec. 25.6.2). You will meet angular momentum properly at university.

## The third law: periods and sizes

Kepler's third law compares different planets. The **period** $T$ of a planet is the time for one orbit (lesson 1.10), and the further a planet is from the Sun, the longer its period. But not in proportion: the third law says that the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, a power law like those of lesson 0.6,

<Formula label="Kepler's third law" tex="T^2 \propto a^3" cite="mit" at="Sec. 25.1, law III (written there as T² = k a³, with k the same for all planets), and Sec. 25.6.3" />

So the ratio $T^2/a^3$ has the same value for every planet of the Sun. Astronomers use a handy unit for distances in the Solar System: the **astronomical unit** (au), very nearly the semi-major axis of the Earth's orbit, $149.598 \times 10^{6}$ km or about $1.496 \times 10^{11}$ m (NASA, Earth Fact Sheet). Measure $a$ in au and $T$ in years, and for the Earth $T^2/a^3 = 1$. Then for every planet of the Sun

<Formula label="Kepler's third law in years and astronomical units" tex="\left(\frac{T}{1\ \text{year}}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{a}{1\ \text{au}}\right)^3" cite="mit" at="Sec. 25.1, law III, with k fixed by the Earth's orbit (T = 1 year for a = 1 au; the units are the page's own choice)" />

## Kepler's third law from Newton

Kepler found his laws in the data. Newton showed where they come from. Take the simplest case, a circular orbit of radius $r$ round a heavy central body of mass $M$, such as a planet round the Sun or a satellite round the Earth. The only force on the orbiting body, of mass $m$, is gravity, $F = G M m/r^2$ (lesson 2.11), and it points to the centre. So gravity alone must supply the centripetal force $m v^2/r$ of lesson 2.9:

<Formula label="Gravity supplies the centripetal force" tex="\frac{G M m}{r^2} = \frac{m v^2}{r}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 25.4.1, Eq. (25.4.15) (a circular orbit; the reduced mass μ there is m when M is much larger than m)" />

The mass $m$ of the orbiting body cancels: a pebble and a space station on the same orbit move at the same speed. Multiply both sides by $r/m$ and take the square root:

<Formula label="Speed in a circular orbit" tex="v = \sqrt{\frac{G M}{r}}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 25.4.1, Eq. (25.4.15), solved for the speed (the step is the page's own)" />

The further out the orbit, the slower the body moves. Now use the period: one orbit is the circumference $2\pi r$, so $T = 2\pi r/v$ (lesson 1.10). Squaring and putting in $v^2 = G M/r$ gives

<Formula label="Kepler's third law for a circular orbit" tex="T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G M}\,r^3" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 9.2.2, Eq. (9.2.7) (written there for the Moon round the Earth)" />

This is Kepler's third law: $T^2$ is proportional to $r^3$, and Newton's law tells us what the constant is. It depends only on the central mass $M$, which is why it is the same for every planet of the Sun. The MIT notes point out that this agreement with Kepler's law was strong evidence for Newton that his law of gravitation was right (reading 1, Sec. 9.2.2).

Newton also showed, with more mathematics than this course uses, that gravity falling off as $1/r^2$ gives elliptical orbits with the Sun at a focus (the first law), and that for an ellipse the same rule holds with the semi-major axis $a$ in place of $r$. Strictly, the two bodies both move round their common centre, and the rule becomes

<Formula label="Kepler's third law for an ellipse (Newton's form)" tex="T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G\,(M + m)}\,a^3" cite="mit" at="Sec. 25.6.3, Eq. (25.5.14) (numbered 25.5.x in the notes; masses m₁ and m₂ there)" />

For a planet $m$ is tiny next to the Sun's $M$ (even Jupiter's mass is about a thousandth of the Sun's), so $M + m$ is almost exactly $M$. For Jupiter this changes $T^2$ by about 0.1%, most of the gap you found; the rest comes from small pulls of the other planets and from rounding in the tables.

<Problem>

</Step>

A television satellite should stay above the same point on the equator, so that dishes on the ground can point at it without moving. It must go round once in the time the Earth takes to turn once relative to the stars, one **sidereal day** of 23 h 56 min 4 s (NASA, Earth Fact Sheet: 23.9345 h), not the 24 h day from noon to noon. Such an orbit is called **geostationary**. The MIT notes work out its size from the third law (reading 1, Example 9.1) and find a radius of $4.22 \times 10^{7}$ m, about 42 200 km. That puts the satellite $42\,200 - 6371 \approx 35\,800$ km above the ground, more than five Earth radii up, where it moves at only about 3 km/s.

<Problem>
A radio relay for explorers on Mars should stay above one point of the Martian equator, just as a geostationary satellite does for the Earth. Mars has a mass of $0.64169 \times 10^{24}$ kg and a mean radius of 3389.5 km, and it turns once relative to the stars in 24.6229 h (NASA, Mars Fact Sheet). Find the radius of the orbit and its height above the ground.
</Problem>
<Step title="Rearrange the third law">
From $T^2 = 4\pi^2 r^3/(G M)$: $r^3 = G M T^2/(4\pi^2)$, so $r = \sqrt[3]{G M T^2/(4\pi^2)}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
The period is $T = 24.6229 \times 3600 \approx 88\,642$ s, and $G M = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 6.4169 \times 10^{23} \approx 4.283 \times 10^{13}$ m³/s². Then $r^3 = 4.283 \times 10^{13} \times 88\,642^2/(4\pi^2) \approx 8.52 \times 10^{21}$ m³, so $r \approx 2.04 \times 10^{7}$ m, about 20 400 km.
</Step>
<Step title="Height">
Subtract the radius of Mars: $20\,430 - 3390 \approx 17\,000$ km above the ground, about five Mars radii up.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The Mars Fact Sheet also gives $G M$ directly, $0.042828 \times 10^{6}$ km³/s² $= 4.2828 \times 10^{13}$ m³/s², the same as our product. The speed by two routes: $2\pi r/T = 2\pi \times 2.043 \times 10^{7}/88\,642 \approx 1.45$ km/s, and $\sqrt{G M/r} = \sqrt{4.283 \times 10^{13}/2.043 \times 10^{7}} \approx 1.45 \times 10^{3}$ m/s, the same. Units: $\text{m}^3\text{/s}^2 \times \text{s}^2 = \text{m}^3$. The orbit is much smaller than the geostationary one because Mars has only about a tenth of the Earth's mass.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Faster, slower, and escaping

What happens if a satellite at some distance $r$ does not move at exactly the circular speed $\sqrt{G M/r}$, but still moves at right angles to the line to the Earth? Newton's law answers it: a little faster, and gravity bends its path less than the circle, so it swings out on an ellipse with its closest point where it started. A little slower, and gravity bends its path more, so it falls inwards on an ellipse with its furthest point where it started. The simulation below lets you try both.

Faster still, and the ellipse becomes longer and longer, until at a speed of $\sqrt{2}$ times the circular speed the body never comes back: it **escapes**. Just above the ground this escape speed is $\sqrt{2} \times 7.91 \approx 11.2$ km/s. Why the factor is exactly $\sqrt{2}$ needs the idea of energy, which comes in Block 3; the MIT notes treat these open orbits, which are parabolas and hyperbolas, with energy too (Sec. 25.4.3 and 25.4.4).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Turn Kepler's third law round and it **weighs** the central body: $M = 4\pi^2 a^3/(G T^2)$. Measure the size and the period of any orbit, and you know the mass inside it, without ever touching it. Lesson 2.13 uses the Earth's year to weigh the Sun, and the Sun's orbit round the centre of the Milky Way to weigh the Galaxy. The MIT notes do the same for a star called S0-2, which astronomers have followed round the centre of our Galaxy (Sec. 25.7, Example 25.2): its orbit shows a mass of millions of Suns packed into a tiny space, which astronomers take to be a giant black hole. Applied to stars and gas far out in galaxies, the same reasoning finds more mass than the visible stars and gas can account for, one of the main reasons most astronomers think galaxies contain **dark matter**.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **First law:** every planet moves on an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. The semi-major axis $a$ and the eccentricity $e$ give the closest and furthest distances, $r_p = a(1 - e)$ and $r_a = a(1 + e)$. The planets' orbits are nearly circles.
- **Second law:** the line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, so $r\,v_\perp$ stays the same and $r_p v_p = r_a v_a$: fastest at perihelion, slowest at aphelion. It holds because gravity points at the Sun and has no torque about it.
- **Third law:** $T^2 \propto a^3$ for all planets of the Sun; in years and astronomical units, $T^2 = a^3$.
- **Newton explains it:** for a circular orbit gravity supplies the centripetal force, $G M m/r^2 = m v^2/r$, so $v = \sqrt{G M/r}$ and $T^2 = (4\pi^2/G M)\,r^3$. The orbiting mass cancels; only the central mass counts.
- The International Space Station, about 420 km up, moves at about 7.7 km/s and goes round in about 93 minutes; a geostationary satellite circles 35 800 km up, once per sidereal day.
- Faster than $\sqrt{2}$ times the circular speed, a body escapes; Block 3 explains why with energy.
