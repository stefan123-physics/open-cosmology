Lesson 2.2 showed what happens when the forces on an object cancel: nothing changes, and the object stays at rest or keeps moving in a straight line at a constant speed. This lesson asks what happens when they do **not** cancel. The answer is Newton's second law, the most used formula in all of mechanics. It links the forces on an object to its acceleration, and so to everything lessons 1.4 to 1.8 taught you about motion.

## Force, mass and acceleration

Usually several forces act on an object at once. Lesson 2.1 called their vector sum the **net force** (or resultant force), $\vec F_{\text{net}}$. Experiments with a trolley on a smooth track, pulled by a steady force, show two things.

- With the same trolley, **twice the net force gives twice the acceleration**. The acceleration is proportional to the net force.
- With the same net force, a trolley of **twice the mass gets half the acceleration**. The acceleration is inversely proportional to the mass.

The table shows the pattern with simple numbers.

| Net force | Mass | Acceleration |
|---|---|---|
| 4.0 N | 2.0 kg | 2.0 m/s² |
| 8.0 N | 2.0 kg | 4.0 m/s² |
| 4.0 N | 4.0 kg | 1.0 m/s² |

Both rules are summed up in one: the acceleration equals the net force divided by the mass. Written the usual way round, with arrows because force and acceleration are both vectors (lesson 0.8), it is **Newton's second law**:

<Formula label="Newton's second law" tex="\vec F_{\text{net}} = m\,\vec a" cite="mit" at="Sec. 2.3, Newton's second law of motion (the definition box)" />

In words: **a net force on an object makes it accelerate in the direction of the net force; the acceleration is proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass**. The MIT course notes give the same law as their Eq. (7.3.5) (reading 1, Sec. 7.3) and call it the equation of motion.

Three points to take from it straight away:

- **The acceleration points the same way as the net force.** Not necessarily the same way as the velocity: a braking car moves forwards, but the net force on it, and its acceleration, point backwards (lesson 1.4).
- **Only the net force counts.** If a box is pushed forwards with 50 N and friction (lesson 2.1; more in lesson 2.6) pulls it back with 50 N, the net force is zero and so is the acceleration, as lesson 2.2 said.
- **Mass measures inertia.** The mass in this law tells you how hard it is to change an object's motion. The MIT notes (reading 1, Sec. 7.1.1) even use this to measure mass: push two objects with the same force and compare their accelerations; the one that accelerates half as much has twice the mass.

The graph shows the second rule for a fixed net force: double the mass, half the acceleration.

## The newton

Lesson 2.1 gave the newton in SI base units; the second law shows why it is this combination. Put $m = 1$ kg and $a = 1$ m/s² into $F_{\text{net}} = m a$: one newton is the net force that gives a mass of one kilogram an acceleration of one metre per second squared.

<Formula label="The newton" tex="1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg} \times 1\ \text{m/s}^{2} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^{2}" cite="si-units" at="1.3, Combinations of SI base units" />

Always put masses in kilograms and accelerations in m/s² before using the law; then the force comes out in newtons. A quick unit check, $\text{N}/\text{kg} = \text{m/s}^{2}$, catches many slips.

## From forces to motion

The first way to use the law: **you know the forces, and you want the motion**. Add the forces to find the net force, divide by the mass to find the acceleration, and then use the equations of motion of lesson 1.6.

<WorkedExample title="Pulling a sledge">
<Problem>
A child pulls a sledge of 12 kg over flat snow with a horizontal rope, with a force of 60 N. Friction between the runners and the snow is 18 N. The sledge starts from rest. Find its acceleration, and its speed and the distance it has gone after 2.0 s.
</Problem>
<Step title="Choose a direction and add the forces">
Take the direction of the pull as positive. Along the snow there are two forces: the pull, $+60$ N, and friction, $-18$ N. The weight and the upward push of the snow are vertical and cancel (the sledge does not sink or fly up), so they do not affect the motion along the snow. The net force is $60 - 18 = 42$ N forwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Acceleration">
$a = F_{\text{net}}/m = 42/12 = 3.5$ m/s², in the direction of the pull.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed and distance">
The forces stay the same, so the acceleration is constant and the equations of lesson 1.6 apply. With $v_i = 0$: $v_f = 3.5 \times 2.0 = 7.0$ m/s, and $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 3.5 \times 2.0^{2} = 7.0$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: N/kg = (kg·m/s²)/kg = m/s². Another route to the distance: the average velocity is $\tfrac{1}{2}(0 + 7.0) = 3.5$ m/s, and $3.5 \times 2.0 = 7.0$ m, the same.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## From motion to forces

The second way works backwards: **you know the motion, and you want the force**. Find the acceleration from the motion (lessons 1.4 and 1.6), then multiply by the mass. This is how engineers find the forces in a crash or the grip a car's tyres need.

<WorkedExample title="An emergency stop">
<Problem>
A car of 1500 kg drives at 20 m/s and brakes steadily to a stop in 40 m. What net force acts on it while it brakes?
</Problem>
<Step title="Acceleration from the motion">
Take the direction of travel as positive. The time is neither given nor asked, so use $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2a\,\Delta x$: $0 = 20^{2} + 2a \times 40$, so $a = -400/80 = -5.0$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Force">
$F_{\text{net}} = m a = 1500 \times (-5.0) = -7.5 \times 10^{3}$ N. The minus sign says the net force points backwards, against the motion: it is mainly the grip of the road on the braking tyres.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The stop takes $\Delta t = 20/5.0 = 4.0$ s, and $\tfrac{1}{2}(20 + 0) \times 4.0 = 40$ m, as given. The direction makes sense too: the car slows down, so the acceleration and the net force point against the velocity.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Weight: mass and gravity

Lesson 1.7 showed that every object falling freely near the Earth has the same acceleration, $g = 9.81$ m/s² downwards. The only force on a freely falling object is the pull of the Earth, its **weight** $\vec F_g$. So Newton's second law, with $\vec a = \vec g$, gives the weight that lesson 2.1 stated:

<Formula label="Weight" tex="\vec F_g = m\,\vec g" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 2.4, Weight and mass" />

The weight points down, towards the centre of the Earth. Its size is $F_g = m g$. The value of $g$ also tells you how many newtons of weight each kilogram has: $g = 9.81$ N/kg, which is the same as 9.81 m/s². An object does not have to be falling to have this weight: a book on a table has it too, but there the table pushes up and the net force is zero.

Lesson 2.1 warned that mass (in kg) and weight (in N) are different things. The formula shows how they are linked: the mass stays the same everywhere, but $g$, and so the weight, depends on where the object is. A mass of 60.0 kg has a weight of $60.0 \times 9.81 \approx 589$ N on the Earth. On the Moon, where the surface gravity is $g_{\text{Moon}} = 1.62$ m/s² (lesson 1.7), the same person still has a mass of 60.0 kg but a weight of only $60.0 \times 1.62 \approx 97.2$ N.

Bathroom scales really measure a force and divide it by 9.81 to show kilograms. Taken to the Moon, they would show $97.2 / 9.81 \approx 9.91$ kg for the same person: the reading changes, the person does not.

<WorkedExample title="Hauling up a bucket">
<Problem>
A builder hauls a bucket of sand with a mass of 8.0 kg straight up on a rope, speeding it up at 1.5 m/s². How hard must the rope pull? How hard does it pull when the bucket is held still?
</Problem>
<Step title="Forces and direction">
Take up as positive. Two forces act on the bucket: the pull of the rope, $F_{\text{rope}}$, upwards, and the weight, $m g = 8.0 \times 9.81 \approx 78$ N, downwards. (Lesson 2.1 called the pull of a rope its tension; lesson 2.5 returns to it.)
</Step>
<Step title="Apply the second law">
$F_{\text{net}} = F_{\text{rope}} - m g = m a$, so $F_{\text{rope}} = m(g + a) = 8.0 \times (9.81 + 1.5) \approx 90$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Held still">
Now $a = 0$, so $F_{\text{rope}} = m g \approx 78$ N: the rope only balances the weight. Speeding the bucket up costs an extra $8.0 \times 1.5 = 12$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
$78 + 12 = 90$ N, and the answer is larger than the weight, as it must be for the bucket to speed up upwards. The same rope pulling with less than 78 N would let the bucket accelerate downwards.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## What F = ma does not say

The second law is short, and so it is easy to read more into it than it says.

**It does not say that heavier objects fall faster.** A heavier object has a larger weight, but it also has a larger mass to accelerate, and the two grow together. Put $F_g = m g$ into the second law: $a = F_g/m = m g/m = g$. The mass cancels. A shot of 5.0 kg has a weight of about 49 N, five times that of a 1.0 kg ball, but five times the mass too, so its acceleration is $49/5.0 \approx 9.8$ m/s², the same. This is the explanation promised in lesson 1.7 of why all objects fall alike. In air, a feather does fall more slowly, but that is because of a different force, air resistance (lesson 2.6), which is large compared with the feather's tiny weight.

**It does not say that motion needs a force.** A force is needed to **change** the velocity, not to keep it. An object moving at a constant velocity has zero net force on it (lesson 2.2).

**It does not say which way an object moves.** The net force fixes the direction of the acceleration, not of the velocity. A ball thrown upwards moves up while the net force on it, its weight, points down.

**It counts only the forces acting on the object.** The forces that the object itself exerts on other things do not belong in its $\vec F_{\text{net}}$. Lesson 2.4 shows why this matters.

## Forces in two dimensions

Force and acceleration are vectors, so the law holds for each direction separately. Split every force into components (lesson 0.8), add the components along each axis, and apply the second law to each axis on its own:

<Formula label="The second law in components" tex="F_{\text{net},x} = m\,a_x, \qquad F_{\text{net},y} = m\,a_y" cite="mit" at="Sec. 2.3, Newton's second law of motion (applied to the x- and y-directions separately)" />

A force along $x$ has no effect on the motion along $y$, and the other way round. This is the same independence of directions that made projectile motion simple (lesson 1.8).

<WorkedExample title="Two pushes on a puck">
<Problem>
A puck of 0.50 kg lies on an air-hockey table, where it floats without friction. Two players push it at once, seen from above: one with 3.0 N towards the east ($+x$), the other with 4.0 N towards the north ($+y$). Find the puck's acceleration.
</Problem>
<Step title="Components of the net force">
The weight and the upward push of the air cushion cancel, so only the two pushes count. Along $x$: $F_{\text{net},x} = 3.0$ N. Along $y$: $F_{\text{net},y} = 4.0$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Acceleration along each axis">
$a_x = 3.0/0.50 = 6.0$ m/s² and $a_y = 4.0/0.50 = 8.0$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Size and direction">
$a = \sqrt{6.0^{2} + 8.0^{2}} = 10$ m/s², at an angle $\tan^{-1}(8.0/6.0) \approx 53^\circ$ north of east.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by the other route">
Add the forces first: the net force has size $\sqrt{3.0^{2} + 4.0^{2}} = 5.0$ N, at the same angle, since $4.0/3.0 = 8.0/6.0$. Then $5.0/0.50 = 10$ m/s², the same. The acceleration points along the net force, as the law says.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

When a rope pulls at an angle, as in quiz question 3, only its component along the ground speeds the object up along the ground; the other component pulls partly upwards and changes how hard the ground pushes back.

## Objects that move together

When two objects move together, with the same acceleration, you can apply the second law to the **whole group**, or to **each object** on its own. Using both is the standard method for connected objects, such as a car and its trailer or two boxes pushed together.

<WorkedExample title="Two boxes pushed together">
<Problem>
On a smooth floor, a box A of 4.0 kg touches a box B of 2.0 kg. You push A with 30 N, so that A pushes B ahead of it. Find the acceleration and the force with which A pushes on B. (Ignore friction.)
</Problem>
<Step title="The group">
Treat A and B together as one object of $4.0 + 2.0 = 6.0$ kg. The only horizontal force on the group from outside is your push, so $a = 30/6.0 = 5.0$ m/s². The push between A and B acts inside the group and does not appear.
</Step>
<Step title="Box B on its own">
The only horizontal force on B is the push of A. B accelerates at 5.0 m/s² too, so that push is $2.0 \times 5.0 = 10$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with box A">
On A there is your push, 30 N forwards, and B pushes back on A with 10 N (an equal and opposite push: Newton's third law, lesson 2.4). The net force on A is $30 - 10 = 20$ N, and $4.0 \times 5.0 = 20$ N: it fits. Notice that B is pushed with only 10 N, not the full 30 N: most of your push goes into accelerating A.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
The same law, with gravity as the force, describes the universe as a whole. Imagine a huge sphere of galaxies, all moving apart. A galaxy at the edge is pulled back by the gravity of all the matter inside the sphere, and $F_{\text{net}} = m a$ then tells how quickly the expansion slows down. The university cosmology course (8.286) derives the equation for the expansion of the universe in exactly this Newtonian way; the full treatment with general relativity comes in the graduate course on general relativity (8.962). The fact that the mass cancels, as for falling objects in this lesson, is why the answer does not depend on the mass of the galaxy you follow. Einstein later took that same fact, that all objects fall alike, as the starting point of general relativity.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Newton's second law:** $\vec F_{\text{net}} = m\,\vec a$. A net force makes an object accelerate in the direction of the net force; the acceleration is proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass.
- **One newton** is the net force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s²: $1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^{2}$.
- **Forces to motion:** add the forces, divide by the mass, then use the equations of motion. **Motion to forces:** find the acceleration from the motion, then multiply by the mass.
- **Weight** is the force of gravity, $F_g = m g$ with $g = 9.81$ m/s² on the Earth and 1.62 m/s² on the Moon. Mass (kg) is the same everywhere; weight (N) is not.
- Heavy and light objects fall with the same acceleration: $a = m g/m = g$.
- In two dimensions, apply the law to each component separately: $F_{\text{net},x} = m a_x$ and $F_{\text{net},y} = m a_y$.
- For objects moving together, apply the law to the whole group and to each object on its own.
