A box pushed across a floor stops when you stop pushing; a ball rolling on grass slows down; a feather drifts down instead of falling like a stone. Lesson 2.2 said that all of these are slowed by forces. This lesson looks at the two forces responsible: **friction**, the force between two surfaces in contact, and **drag**, the force of air or water on an object moving through it. Both oppose motion, but they behave quite differently. Friction hardly depends on speed; drag grows quickly as the speed rises.

## Where friction comes from

Rub your hands together hard and they get warm: two surfaces are sliding over each other, and something resists the motion. That something is **friction**. Even surfaces that feel smooth are rough on a very small scale, and where the tiny bumps of two surfaces press into each other they catch and stick. Underneath, as lesson 2.1 said, these are electric forces between the atoms of the two surfaces.

Lesson 2.1 named friction $\vec F_f$ as one of the contact forces. Three things are true of it:

- **It acts along the surface**, parallel to it. The normal force $\vec F_N$ (lesson 2.5) is the part of the push of a surface that is perpendicular to it; friction is the part along it.
- **It opposes sliding**, or an attempt to slide, of one surface over the other.
- **It comes in pairs.** When a box slides across a floor, the floor exerts friction on the box (backwards) and the box exerts friction on the floor (forwards). This is Newton's third law (lesson 2.4).

There are two kinds of friction, depending on whether the surfaces are sliding over each other or not.

## Static friction

Push gently against a heavy crate and it does not move. The crate stays at rest, so by the first law (lesson 2.2) the net force on it is zero: the floor must push back along the surface with a force exactly as large as your push. This is **static friction**, the friction between two surfaces that are **not** sliding over each other.

Push a little harder and the crate still does not move: static friction has grown to match. Static friction **adjusts itself** to whatever is needed to stop sliding, from zero (when nothing pushes sideways) up to a maximum. Push harder than that maximum and the crate breaks loose and starts to slide.

Experiments show that the maximum is proportional to the normal force: press the surfaces together twice as hard and they can resist twice as much. The constant of proportionality is the **coefficient of static friction**, $\mu_s$ (the Greek letter mu, with s for static):

<Formula label="Static friction" tex="F_f \le \mu_s\,F_N" cite="mit" at="Friction forces (maximum static friction, written there as f_s^max = μ_s N)" />

Read it carefully: this is not a formula for static friction itself but a **limit**. The actual friction is whatever keeps the surfaces from sliding, as long as it is not more than $\mu_s F_N$. The MIT course notes state the same limit, with static friction anywhere from zero up to its maximum (reading 1, Sec. 8.3.2, Eqs. (8.3.6) and (8.3.7)). The direction of static friction is always against the way the object would slide if there were no friction.

## Kinetic friction

Once the crate slides, there is still friction, and you have to keep pushing to keep it moving. The friction on a sliding object is **kinetic friction** (kinetic means "to do with motion"; it is also called sliding friction). It is again proportional to the normal force, with the **coefficient of kinetic friction** $\mu_k$:

<Formula label="Kinetic friction" tex="F_f = \mu_k\,F_N" cite="mit" at="Friction forces (kinetic friction, written there as f_k = μ_k N)" />

This time it **is** a formula for the friction itself: the size of kinetic friction is fixed by the normal force and the surfaces, whatever the push. Its direction is against the direction of sliding. The MIT notes give the same law as Eq. (8.3.5) and add two surprising facts found by experiment (reading 1, Sec. 8.3.2): kinetic friction is, to a good approximation, **independent of the area of contact** (a brick slides just as easily on its narrow side as on its wide side), and **independent of the speed** at ordinary sliding speeds. The first was found by Leonardo da Vinci around 1500 and rediscovered and published by Guillaume Amontons in 1699; the second by Charles-Augustin Coulomb.

For most pairs of surfaces $\mu_k$ is somewhat smaller than $\mu_s$: it is harder to get something sliding than to keep it sliding. You feel this when a heavy cupboard suddenly "gives" and then moves more easily.

**The coefficients have no unit.** A coefficient of friction is a force divided by a force, $\mu = F_f/F_N$, so the newtons cancel: it is a pure number, a **dimensionless** quantity. Its size says how "grippy" a pair of surfaces is. The table gives rough values of our own, typical of those quoted for these surfaces; they are not taken from the sources of this lesson. Real measurements vary a lot with how clean, dry and worn the surfaces are, so treat them as illustrations only.

| Surfaces | $\mu_s$ (roughly) | $\mu_k$ (roughly) |
|---|---|---|
| rubber tyre on dry asphalt | 0.9 | 0.7 |
| rubber tyre on wet asphalt | 0.5 | 0.4 |
| wood on wood | 0.4 | 0.3 |
| steel on steel, oiled | 0.1 | 0.05 |
| ice on ice | 0.1 | 0.03 |

A coefficient tells you about a **pair** of surfaces, never one surface alone: "the friction coefficient of rubber" means nothing until you say what the rubber slides on.

## Pushing a crate

<WorkedExample title="Getting a crate moving">
<Problem>
A crate of 40 kg stands on a flat warehouse floor. Between crate and floor $\mu_s = 0.50$ and $\mu_k = 0.35$. A worker pushes it horizontally, first with 150 N, then with 220 N. What happens each time? If the crate slides, find its acceleration.
</Problem>
<Step title="Normal force">
The floor is flat and the push is horizontal, so vertically only the weight and the normal force act, and they balance (the crate neither sinks nor lifts off): $F_N = F_g = m g = 40 \times 9.81 \approx 392$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="The most static friction can give">
$\mu_s F_N = 0.50 \times 392 \approx 196$ N. A push smaller than this cannot start the crate sliding.
</Step>
<Step title="Pushing with 150 N">
150 N is less than 196 N, so the crate stays put. Static friction is then exactly 150 N, backwards: just enough to make the net force zero. It is **not** 196 N; that is only the limit.
</Step>
<Step title="Pushing with 220 N">
220 N is more than 196 N, so the crate breaks loose and slides. Now kinetic friction acts: $F_f = \mu_k F_N = 0.35 \times 392 \approx 137$ N, backwards. The net force is $F_{\text{net}} = 220 - 137 \approx 83$ N forwards, and by the second law (lesson 2.3) $a = F_{\text{net}}/m = 83/40 \approx 2.1$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: N/kg = m/s². Size: the acceleration is well below $g$, as it should be for a push smaller than the crate's weight. And once it slides, a push of only 137 N would keep the crate moving at a constant speed, less than the 196 N needed to start it: harder to start than to keep going.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Friction is useful

Friction is often treated as a nuisance, but without it you could not walk, write, drive or even keep a ladder against a wall.

**Walking.** When you take a step, your shoe pushes backwards on the ground. The shoe does not slip, so this is static friction. By Newton's third law (lesson 2.4) the ground pushes your shoe forwards with an equal force, and that forward push is what speeds you up. On ice, where the maximum static friction is small, your foot slips backwards instead and you cannot get going.

**Cars.** A car moves and brakes through the friction of the road on its tyres. A rolling tyre does not slide over the road: the bit of tyre touching the road is momentarily at rest on it. So a car that drives or brakes normally uses **static** friction. If the brakes lock the wheels, the tyres skid and **kinetic** friction takes over. Because $\mu_k$ is smaller than $\mu_s$, a skidding car usually needs a longer distance to stop, and it cannot be steered. This is why many cars have an anti-lock braking system, which keeps the wheels turning while braking hard. A rolling wheel also feels a small **rolling resistance**, because tyre and road squash a little where they touch; on a good road it is a roughly constant force, much smaller than sliding friction.

<WorkedExample title="Skidding to a stop">
<Problem>
A car drives at 20 m/s on a level road. The driver brakes so hard that the wheels lock and the car skids. Find the stopping distance on a dry road ($\mu_k = 0.70$) and on a wet road ($\mu_k = 0.40$).
</Problem>
<Step title="Acceleration from friction">
On a level road $F_N = m g$, so the kinetic friction is $F_f = \mu_k m g$. It is the only horizontal force, so $a = F_f/m = \mu_k g$. The mass cancels: a heavy car and a light car skid in the same way. Dry: $a = 0.70 \times 9.81 \approx 6.9$ m/s². Wet: $a = 0.40 \times 9.81 \approx 3.9$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Stopping distance">
The friction is constant, so the acceleration is constant and $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2a\,\Delta x$ (lesson 1.6) applies with $v_f = 0$ and the acceleration against the motion: $\Delta x = v_i^{2}/(2a)$. Dry: $\Delta x = 20^{2}/(2 \times 6.87) \approx 29$ m. Wet: $\Delta x = 20^{2}/(2 \times 3.92) \approx 51$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
On the dry road the stop takes $20/6.87 \approx 2.9$ s at an average speed of 10 m/s, which gives $10 \times 2.91 \approx 29$ m again. The wet road needs almost twice the distance, as expected from the ratio $0.70/0.40 = 1.75$ of the coefficients.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Drag: friction in air and water

An object moving through a **fluid** (a liquid or a gas) feels a force from the fluid that opposes its motion: **drag**. In air it is usually called **air resistance**. Unlike friction between surfaces, drag depends strongly on speed: a cyclist feels hardly any at walking pace and a strong push back at 40 km/h.

Drag in general is complicated. It depends on the size, shape and speed of the object, and on the density, the "thickness" (viscosity) and other properties of the fluid. For objects moving fast through air, such as balls, cars, cyclists and skydivers, it follows a simple rule well enough. The size of the drag force is

<Formula label="Air drag at high speed" tex="F_D = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\rho\,C_D\,A\,v^{2}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 8.6, Eq. (8.6.1)" />

pointing against the velocity. The letters mean:

- $v$: the speed of the object through the air.
- $\rho$ (the Greek letter rho): the **density** of the air, its mass per cubic metre, in kg/m³. Near the ground air has a density of about 1.2 kg/m³. (Lesson 4.1 treats density in full.)
- $A$: the **cross-sectional area** of the object, the area it shows to the oncoming air, in m². A skydiver lying flat has a much larger $A$ than one diving head first.
- $C_D$: the **drag coefficient**, a number that depends on the shape. The MIT notes call it "a dimensionless number that is a property of the object" (reading 1, Sec. 8.6).

**The drag coefficient has no unit.** Check it with the units: $\rho A v^{2}$ has units $\text{kg/m}^{3} \times \text{m}^{2} \times \text{m}^{2}/\text{s}^{2} = \text{kg·m/s}^{2} = \text{N}$. That is already a force, so $C_D$ (and the ½) must be pure numbers. The MIT notes list some values (reading 1, Table 8.1): about 0.47 for a sphere, 1.05 for a cube, and only 0.04 for a smooth, streamlined (teardrop) shape. Streamlining a shape lowers $C_D$; that is why fast cars, fish and aircraft are shaped as they are.

The most important feature is the $v^{2}$: **twice the speed gives four times the drag**. For a car with $C_D = 0.30$ and $A = 2.2$ m², at 30 m/s (108 km/h) the drag is $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1.2 \times 0.30 \times 2.2 \times 30^{2} \approx 360$ N; at half the speed, 15 m/s, it is a quarter of that, about 89 N. This is why driving fast costs so much more fuel.

At very low speeds, such as a tiny droplet sinking through oil, drag behaves differently and is proportional to $v$ instead of $v^{2}$ (reading 1, Sec. 8.6). The university lesson treats both cases; here we stay with air drag.

## Terminal velocity

Drop a stone and, at first, there is almost no drag: the stone falls freely with acceleration $g$ (lesson 1.7). As it speeds up, the upward drag grows, the net force $F_{\text{net}} = m g - F_D$ shrinks, and so does the acceleration. Eventually the drag has grown as large as the weight. Then the net force is zero, and by the first law the object falls on at a steady speed: its **terminal velocity** $v_t$.

Setting $\tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_D A v_t^{2} = m g$ and solving for $v_t$ gives

<Formula label="Terminal velocity" tex="v_t = \sqrt{\frac{2\,m\,g}{\rho\,C_D\,A}}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 8.7, Example 8.12, Eq. (8.6.111)" />

A heavy, compact object (large $m$, small $A$) has a high terminal velocity; a light, spread-out one (a feather, a leaf, a parachute) has a low one. This is why a feather falls slowly in air while, without air (lesson 2.3), it would fall just like a hammer.

<WorkedExample title="A skydiver">
<Problem>
A skydiver with her gear has a mass of 90 kg. Falling belly down she has $C_D = 1.0$ and $A = 0.75$ m²; the air has $\rho = 1.2$ kg/m³. Find her terminal velocity. Then her parachute opens, giving $C_D = 1.4$ and $A = 30$ m². What is her new terminal velocity?
</Problem>
<Step title="Belly down">
$v_t = \sqrt{2 m g/(\rho C_D A)} = \sqrt{2 \times 90 \times 9.81/(1.2 \times 1.0 \times 0.75)} = \sqrt{1766/0.90} \approx 44$ m/s, about 160 km/h.
</Step>
<Step title="Under the parachute">
$v_t = \sqrt{1766/(1.2 \times 1.4 \times 30)} = \sqrt{1766/50.4} \approx 5.9$ m/s, a speed at which she can land safely.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
At 44.3 m/s the drag is $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1.2 \times 1.0 \times 0.75 \times 44.3^{2} \approx 883$ N, and her weight is $90 \times 9.81 \approx 883$ N: they balance, as they must. Units: $\sqrt{\text{kg·m/s}^{2}/(\text{kg/m}^{3} \times \text{m}^{2})} = \sqrt{\text{m}^{2}/\text{s}^{2}} = $ m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

How does the speed approach $v_t$? Solving Newton's second law with this drag force gives, for an object dropped from rest (reading 1, Example 8.12, Eq. (8.6.117)):

<Formula label="Speed of a falling object with air drag" tex="v(t) = v_t \tanh\!\left(\frac{g\,t}{v_t}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 8.7, Example 8.12, Eq. (8.6.117) (written there with v_∞β/m, which equals g/v_∞)" />

You do not need to derive this; the university lesson does. The function $\tanh$ (the hyperbolic tangent, on most calculators) grows like its input when the input is small and then levels off at 1. So at first $v \approx g t$, free fall, and after a while $v$ levels off at $v_t$. The time scale is $v_t/g$, about 4.5 s for the skydiver.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Friction and drag are everyday forces, but the idea of a terminal velocity turns up in the early universe. In the theory of inflation, a field (a quantity spread through all of space) "rolls" down a very gentle hill of energy. The expansion of the universe acts on that field rather like a drag force, which cosmologists often call Hubble friction. Just as drag holds a skydiver to a steady speed, this effect soon holds the field to a slow, steady roll (Hubble friction grows in proportion to the speed of the field, like the low-speed drag above, not like air drag with its $v^{2}$), and that "slow roll" lets inflation last long enough to do its work. The comparison is a picture, not a proof: the field is not a falling object, and the friction term comes from the equations of general relativity. The university cosmology course meets this slow roll in its last lectures, and the graduate astrophysics course writes down the Hubble friction term itself.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Friction** $\vec F_f$ acts along the surface between two objects in contact and opposes sliding, or an attempt to slide. It comes in third-law pairs.
- **Static friction** acts when the surfaces do not slide. It adjusts to whatever is needed, from zero up to a maximum: $F_f \le \mu_s F_N$.
- **Kinetic friction** acts when they slide: $F_f = \mu_k F_N$, against the sliding. It hardly depends on speed or contact area; usually $\mu_k < \mu_s$.
- The coefficients $\mu_s$ and $\mu_k$ are **dimensionless** (a force over a force) and belong to a pair of surfaces.
- Static friction lets us walk and lets rolling tyres drive and brake; a skidding car uses the weaker kinetic friction, and its braking acceleration is $\mu_k g$.
- **Drag** in air at high speed: $F_D = \tfrac{1}{2}\rho C_D A v^{2}$, against the velocity, with the **dimensionless drag coefficient** $C_D$ set by the shape. Twice the speed gives four times the drag.
- A falling object speeds up until drag equals weight; then it falls at its **terminal velocity** $v_t = \sqrt{2 m g/(\rho C_D A)}$, approached as $v = v_t \tanh(g t/v_t)$.
