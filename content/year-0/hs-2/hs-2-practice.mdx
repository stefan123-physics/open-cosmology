This set practises the thirteen lessons of Block 2. The problems, hints and solutions are our own; the methods are those of the lessons, which build on Siyavula's chapter on Newton's laws (force, Newton's three laws and the forces between masses) and on MIT 8.01SC's course notes on Newton's laws and their applications, circular motion, torque and equilibrium, and celestial mechanics. Problems 1 to 4 count 9 points each and Problems 5 to 12 count 8 points each, 100 in all; Problem 13 is optional. Throughout, take $g = 9.81$ m/s² near the Earth's surface, $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ N m²/kg², the Earth's mass $5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg and its radius $6.371 \times 10^{6}$ m.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first, starting with a sketch and a free-body diagram. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. Number boxes accept a small rounding difference, so give your answer to two or three significant figures, in the unit shown. A force or acceleration along a line has a sign only where the problem says so; otherwise type its size. Formula boxes take plain typing, for example `(a + b)/c`, and accept every correct way of writing the answer. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Two tugboats and a barge" points={9}>

Two tugboats pull a barge with horizontal ropes. Take east as the $x$-direction and north as the $y$-direction. One tug pulls with $F_1 = 2400$ N at 30° north of east; the other pulls with $F_2 = 1800$ N at 20° south of east.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the $x$-component (east) of the resultant force of the two tugs?

<Hint>Split each pull into an east part and a north part. The east part of a force at an angle $\theta$ to east is $F\cos\theta$.</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="East component (N):" value="3770" tolerance="5%" calc="p1-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="East parts">
$F_{1,x} = 2400 \cos 30^{\circ} = 2078$ N and $F_{2,x} = 1800 \cos 20^{\circ} = 1691$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Add">
$F_{\text{net},x} = 2078 + 1691 = 3770$ N, to the east.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The resultant's east part is less than $2400 + 1800 = 4200$ N, the sum of the two sizes, because neither tug pulls straight east.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the size of the resultant force?

<Hint>Find the north component in the same way, with a minus sign for the part that points south. Then combine the two components with Pythagoras.</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="Size of the resultant (N):" value="3810" tolerance="5%" calc="p1-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="North parts">
$F_{1,y} = 2400 \sin 30^{\circ} = +1200$ N and $F_{2,y} = -1800 \sin 20^{\circ} = -615.6$ N, so $F_{\text{net},y} = 1200 - 615.6 = 584.4$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Pythagoras">
$F_\text{net} = \sqrt{3770^{2} + 584.4^{2}} = 3815$ N, or about 3810 N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The resultant is only a little larger than its east component, because the two north–south parts nearly cancel.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

At what angle north of east does the resultant point?

<Hint>The tangent of the angle is the north component divided by the east component.</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="Angle north of east (degrees):" value="8.8" tolerance="5%" calc="p1-c" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Inverse tangent">
$\tan\theta = 584.4/3770 = 0.155$, so $\theta = 8.8^{\circ}$ north of east.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The angle lies between the two ropes' directions, 20° south and 30° north of east, and leans to the north because the stronger tug pulls that way.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A balloon rising steadily" points={9}>

A hot-air balloon, with its basket and passengers, has a total mass of 450 kg. It rises at a constant velocity of 1.5 m/s. The air resists the motion with a force of 300 N.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

What is the resultant force on the balloon?

<Answer id="p2-a" label="The resultant force is:" choices={["zero", "upwards, because the balloon moves up", "downwards, because gravity is the largest force"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>
<Step title="Newton's first law">
The velocity does not change, so the acceleration is zero. By Newton's first law the resultant force is then zero: the forces are in equilibrium.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
A resultant force upwards would make the balloon rise faster and faster; moving up does not need an upward resultant, only an upward velocity.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

How large is the upward lift force of the hot air?

<Hint>Draw the three forces: the lift up, the weight down, and the air resistance, which points against the motion.</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="Lift force (N):" value="4710" tolerance="5%" calc="p2-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Weight">
$F_g = m\,g = 450 \times 9.81 = 4414.5$ N, downwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Balance">
The balloon moves up, so the air resistance points down. Equilibrium needs lift = weight + air resistance $= 4414.5 + 300 = 4714.5$ N, about $4.71 \times 10^{3}$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
If the balloon were sinking at the same speed, the air resistance would point up and the lift would be $4414.5 - 300$ N instead. The 300 N is small next to the weight, so the lift is only about 7% more than the weight.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="An electric scooter" points={9}>

A rider on an electric scooter has a total mass, with the scooter, of 95 kg. Starting from rest on a level road, the motor gives a forward driving force of 210 N, while friction and air resistance together push back with 58 N. Take both forces as constant.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the acceleration?

<Hint>Find the resultant force first; only that part of the push accelerates the scooter.</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="Acceleration (m/s²):" value="1.6" tolerance="5%" calc="p3-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Resultant force">
$F_\text{net} = 210 - 58 = 152$ N, forwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Second law">
$a = F_\text{net}/m = 152/95 = 1.6$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: N/kg = (kg·m/s²)/kg = m/s². Using the 210 N alone would give 2.2 m/s², too large, because it ignores the resistance.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

How fast is the scooter going after 4.5 s?

<Answer id="p3-b" label="Speed (m/s):" value="7.2" tolerance="5%" calc="p3-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Constant acceleration">
$v_f = v_i + a\,\Delta t = 0 + 1.6 \times 4.5 = 7.2$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
That is $7.2 \times 3.6 = 25.9$ km/h, a believable speed for a scooter.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How far does it travel in those 4.5 s?

<Answer id="p3-c" label="Distance (m):" value="16.2" tolerance="5%" calc="p3-c" />

<Solution>
<Step title="From rest">
$\Delta x = \tfrac12\,a\,\Delta t^{2} = \tfrac12 \times 1.6 \times 4.5^{2} = 16.2$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
The average velocity is $\tfrac12(0 + 7.2) = 3.6$ m/s, and $3.6 \times 4.5 = 16.2$ m.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="An astronaut pushes off" points={9}>

During a spacewalk, an astronaut of mass 95 kg (with her suit) floats next to a satellite of mass 380 kg. Both are at rest relative to each other. She pushes the satellite with a force of 76 N for 0.50 s. Ignore every other force.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the size of the astronaut's acceleration while she pushes?

<Hint>Newton's third law tells you how hard the satellite pushes back on her.</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="Astronaut's acceleration (m/s²):" value="0.8" tolerance="5%" calc="p4-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="The reaction force">
By Newton's third law the satellite pushes on the astronaut with 76 N, in the opposite direction.
</Step>
<Step title="Second law for her">
$a = 76/95 = 0.80$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
She gets an acceleration even though she is the one pushing: the force on her comes from the satellite.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the size of the satellite's acceleration?

<Answer id="p4-b" label="Satellite's acceleration (m/s²):" value="0.2" tolerance="5%" calc="p4-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Same force, larger mass">
$a = 76/380 = 0.20$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The satellite has four times her mass ($380/95 = 4.0$), so with the same force it gets a quarter of her acceleration: $0.80/4.0 = 0.20$ m/s².
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How fast are they moving apart when the push ends?

<Hint>Find each speed after 0.50 s. They move in opposite directions.</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c" label="Speed apart (m/s):" value="0.5" tolerance="5%" calc="p4-c" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Each speed">
Astronaut: $0.80 \times 0.50 = 0.40$ m/s. Satellite: $0.20 \times 0.50 = 0.10$ m/s, the other way.
</Step>
<Step title="Add">
They separate at $0.40 + 0.10 = 0.50$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Mass times speed is the same for both, $95 \times 0.40 = 38$ and $380 \times 0.10 = 38$ kg·m/s, as it must be when equal and opposite forces act for the same time.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Two masses over a pulley" points={8}>

A light string runs over a light, frictionless pulley. A mass $m_1 = 0.70$ kg hangs from one end and a mass $m_2 = 0.45$ kg from the other. The masses are released from rest.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the size of the acceleration of the masses?

<Hint>Write Newton's second law for each mass separately. The string has the same tension $T$ on both sides, and both masses have the same size of acceleration.</Hint>
<Hint>Adding the two equations makes $T$ drop out.</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="Acceleration (m/s²):" value="2.13" tolerance="5%" calc="p5-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Two equations">
The heavier mass moves down: $m_1 g - T = m_1 a$. The lighter one moves up: $T - m_2 g = m_2 a$.
</Step>
<Step title="Add them">
$(m_1 - m_2)\,g = (m_1 + m_2)\,a$, so $a = \dfrac{0.25 \times 9.81}{1.15} = 2.13$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The acceleration is less than $g$, as it must be: only the difference in weight drives both masses.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

What is the tension in the string?

<Answer id="p5-b" label="Tension (N):" value="5.37" tolerance="5%" calc="p5-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Lighter mass">
$T = m_2\,(g + a) = 0.45 \times (9.81 + 2.133) = 5.37$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with the other mass">
$T = m_1\,(g - a) = 0.70 \times (9.81 - 2.133) = 5.37$ N as well. It lies between the two weights, $0.45 \times 9.81 = 4.41$ N and $0.70 \times 9.81 = 6.87$ N.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Show that the tension can be written with the masses and $g$ alone. Write $T$ in terms of $m_1$, $m_2$ and $g$. Type `m1`, `m2` and `g`.

<Hint>Use the acceleration you found in part (a), written as a formula, in $T = m_2\,(g + a)$.</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-c" label="$T =$" expr="2*m1*m2*g/(m1 + m2)" vars={{ m1: [2, 5], m2: [0.1, 1.9], g: [1, 20] }} names={{ m1: "m_1", m2: "m_2", g: "g" }}
  mistakes={[{ expr: "m1*m2*g/(m1 + m2)", hint: "Check the factor 2: add g and a over the common denominator m₁ + m₂." }, { expr: "(m1 - m2)*g/(m1 + m2)", hint: "That is the acceleration, not the tension." }, { expr: "m2*g", hint: "That is the weight of the lighter mass; the tension is larger because that mass accelerates upwards." }]} />

<Solution>
<Step title="Substitute the acceleration">
$T = m_2\left(g + \dfrac{(m_1 - m_2)\,g}{m_1 + m_2}\right) = m_2\,g\,\dfrac{(m_1 + m_2) + (m_1 - m_2)}{m_1 + m_2} = \dfrac{2\,m_1 m_2\,g}{m_1 + m_2}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
For equal masses, $m_1 = m_2 = m$, it gives $T = 2m^{2}g/(2m) = m\,g$: nothing accelerates and the string holds each weight. The formula is the same with $m_1$ and $m_2$ swapped, as it should be, since the string pulls equally on both.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Friction and drag: a bookcase and a cyclist" points={8}>

Parts (a) and (b) are about a bookcase, part (c) about a cyclist. A bookcase of mass 32 kg stands on a level floor. Between the bookcase and the floor, $\mu_s = 0.45$ and $\mu_k = 0.32$. You push horizontally.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the smallest push that gets the bookcase moving?

<Hint>On a level floor with a horizontal push, the normal force equals the weight. Static friction can grow up to $\mu_s F_N$ and no further.</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-a" label="Push (N):" value="141" tolerance="5%" calc="p6-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Normal force">
$F_N = m\,g = 32 \times 9.81 = 313.9$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Maximum static friction">
$\mu_s F_N = 0.45 \times 313.9 = 141$ N. A push just larger than this starts the bookcase moving.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
A push of 100 N would not move it: static friction would then be exactly 100 N, not 141 N, because static friction only matches what is needed.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Once it slides, you keep pushing with the force you found in (a), rounded to three significant figures. What is the acceleration of the bookcase?

<Hint>A sliding object feels kinetic friction, which is smaller than the largest static friction.</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-b" label="Acceleration (m/s²):" value="1.27" tolerance="5%" calc="p6-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Kinetic friction">
$F_f = \mu_k F_N = 0.32 \times 313.9 = 100.5$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Second law">
$a = \dfrac{141 - 100.5}{32} = 1.27$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
With the unrounded push $\mu_s F_N$ the mass cancels and $a = (\mu_s - \mu_k)\,g = 0.13 \times 9.81 = 1.28$ m/s²: the same to rounding, because the push of 141 N was rounded down.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Now a separate situation. A cyclist feels an air drag of 18 N when riding at 6.0 m/s. Estimate the drag at 9.0 m/s, in the same position and the same air.

<Hint>At these speeds the drag force grows with the square of the speed.</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-c" label="Drag (N):" value="40.5" tolerance="5%" calc="p6-c" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Scale with the speed squared">
The speed is $9.0/6.0 = 1.5$ times as large, so the drag is $1.5^{2} = 2.25$ times as large: $18 \times 2.25 = 40.5$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Everything else in $F_D = \tfrac12\,\rho\,C_D\,A\,v^{2}$ stays the same, so only $v^{2}$ changes. A guess of $18 \times 1.5 = 27$ N would treat drag as proportional to the speed.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="Calibrating a spring" points={8}>

A spring hangs from a hook; its length is 18.0 cm. With a 0.80 kg mass hanging at rest from it, its length is 24.4 cm.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

What is the spring constant $k$?

<Hint>Use the extension, not the length. At rest, the spring's upward force balances the weight.</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-a" label="Spring constant (N/m):" value="123" tolerance="5%" calc="p7-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Extension and force">
$x = 24.4 - 18.0 = 6.4$ cm $= 0.064$ m. The force is the weight, $0.80 \times 9.81 = 7.85$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Divide">
$k = F/x = 7.848/0.064 = 123$ N/m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: N/m. Dividing by the whole length, 0.244 m, would give a much smaller and wrong value.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

What is the length of the spring with a 1.3 kg mass hanging from it?

<Answer id="p7-b" label="Length (cm):" value="28.4" tolerance="5%" calc="p7-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="New extension">
$x = m\,g/k = 1.3 \times 9.81/122.6 = 0.104$ m $= 10.4$ cm.
</Step>
<Step title="Add the natural length">
$18.0 + 10.4 = 28.4$ cm.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by proportion">
Hooke's law makes the extension proportional to the load: $6.4 \times 1.3/0.80 = 10.4$ cm.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 8

<Exercise id="p8" title="A box down a ramp" points={8}>

A 6.0 kg box is released from rest at the top of a ramp 4.0 m long, inclined at 32° to the horizontal. The coefficient of kinetic friction between box and ramp is $\mu_k = 0.20$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the normal force on the box?

<Hint>Split the weight into a part along the slope and a part into the slope. Only the part into the slope is balanced by the normal force.</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-a" label="Normal force (N):" value="49.9" tolerance="5%" calc="p8-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Component into the slope">
$F_N = m\,g\cos\theta = 58.86 \times \cos 32^{\circ} = 49.9$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
It is less than the full weight, 58.9 N, as it must be on a slope.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the acceleration of the box down the ramp?

<Hint>Along the slope, the weight's parallel part pulls down and kinetic friction pulls up the slope.</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-b" label="Acceleration (m/s²):" value="3.53" tolerance="5%" calc="p8-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Forces along the slope">
Down the slope: $m\,g\sin\theta = 58.86 \times \sin 32^{\circ} = 31.2$ N. Friction up the slope: $\mu_k F_N = 0.20 \times 49.92 = 9.98$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Second law">
$a = \dfrac{31.19 - 9.98}{6.0} = 3.53$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Check with the slope formula">
$a = g\,(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta) = 9.81 \times (0.530 - 0.20 \times 0.848) = 3.54$ m/s², the same to rounding. Without friction it would be $g\sin 32^{\circ} = 5.20$ m/s².
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How fast is the box moving at the bottom of the ramp?

<Answer id="p8-c" label="Speed (m/s):" value="5.32" tolerance="5%" calc="p8-c" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Without the time">
$v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2\,a\,\Delta x = 0 + 2 \times 3.535 \times 4.0 = 28.28$, so $v_f = 5.32$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The box does slide: $\tan 32^{\circ} = 0.62$ is larger than 0.20, so the slope beats friction. The trip takes $5.32/3.535 = 1.50$ s, and the average speed times the time, $2.66 \times 1.50 = 4.0$ m, is the length of the ramp.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 9

<Exercise id="p9" title="Round the bend and over the top" points={8}>

<Part label="a" points={4}>

A cyclist rides round a flat bend of radius 22 m. The coefficient of static friction between tyres and road is $\mu_s = 0.60$. What is the highest speed at which she can take the bend without sliding outwards?

<Hint>Friction is the only sideways force, so it must supply the whole centripetal force $m v^{2}/r$. At the top speed, static friction is at its maximum.</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-a" label="Top speed (m/s):" value="11.4" tolerance="5%" calc="p9-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Friction at its limit">
$\mu_s\,m\,g = \dfrac{m v^{2}}{r}$. The mass cancels: $v^{2} = \mu_s\,g\,r = 0.60 \times 9.81 \times 22 = 129.5$ m²/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Square root">
$v = \sqrt{129.5} = 11.4$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
That is $11.4 \times 3.6 = 41$ km/h, fast for a bike on a tight bend, and the answer does not depend on how heavy the rider is.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

A bucket of water is swung in a vertical circle of radius 0.95 m. What is the smallest speed at the top of the circle for the water to stay in the bucket?

<Hint>At the top, both the weight of the water and the push of the bucket point down, towards the centre. At the smallest speed the bucket no longer pushes.</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-b" label="Least speed (m/s):" value="3.05" tolerance="5%" calc="p9-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Weight alone at the top">
With no push from the bucket, gravity alone gives the centripetal force: $m\,g = m v^{2}/r$, so $v = \sqrt{g\,r} = \sqrt{9.81 \times 0.95} = 3.05$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: $\sqrt{\text{m/s}^2 \times \text{m}} = $ m/s. Slower than this, gravity pulls the water inwards more than the circle needs, and the water falls out of its circular path.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 10

<Exercise id="p10" title="Lifting a wheelbarrow" points={8}>

A wheelbarrow of mass 15 kg carries a load of 60 kg of sand. Measured horizontally from the wheel's axle, the centre of gravity of the barrow is 0.60 m away, that of the sand 0.40 m, and the handles 1.40 m. The gardener holds the handles still, with the legs just off the ground, and pulls straight up.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

What upward force must the gardener apply to the handles?

<Hint>Take torques about the axle. Then the unknown force of the ground on the wheel has no torque.</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-a" label="Force on the handles (N):" value="231" tolerance="5%" calc="p10-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Clockwise torques of the weights">
Sand: $60 \times 9.81 \times 0.40 = 235.4$ N·m. Barrow: $15 \times 9.81 \times 0.60 = 88.3$ N·m. Together: 323.7 N·m.
</Step>
<Step title="Balance">
The lift on the handles turns the other way: $F \times 1.40 = 323.7$, so $F = 231$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The total weight is $75 \times 9.81 = 735.8$ N. The gardener lifts less than a third of it because the load sits much closer to the axle than the handles do.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

How large is the upward force of the ground on the wheel?

<Hint>The barrow is in equilibrium, so the upward forces add up to the total weight.</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-b" label="Force on the wheel (N):" value="505" tolerance="5%" calc="p10-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Forces add to zero">
$735.8 - 231.2 = 505$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with torques about the handles">
About the handles, the wheel's force has a moment arm of 1.40 m, the sand 1.00 m and the barrow 0.80 m: force on the wheel $= \dfrac{60 \times 9.81 \times 1.00 + 15 \times 9.81 \times 0.80}{1.40} = 505$ N.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 11

<Exercise id="p11" title="A satellite and a weaker gravity" points={8}>

A weather satellite of mass 1200 kg orbits 2000 km above the Earth's surface.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

What is the gravitational force of the Earth on the satellite? Type a number like `1.2e5` if you like.

<Hint>The distance in Newton's law of gravitation is measured from the centre of the Earth, not from its surface.</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-a" label="Force (N):" value="6820" tolerance="5%" calc="p11-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Distance from the centre">
$r = 6.371 \times 10^{6} + 2.0 \times 10^{6} = 8.371 \times 10^{6}$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Newton's law">
$F = \dfrac{G M m}{r^{2}} = \dfrac{6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24} \times 1200}{(8.371 \times 10^{6})^{2}} = 6.82 \times 10^{3}$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
On the ground the satellite weighs $1200 \times 9.81 = 1.18 \times 10^{4}$ N. The ratio $6823/11772 = 0.58$ matches $(6.371/8.371)^{2} = 0.58$.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

At what height above the surface is the gravitational field strength half its value at the surface? Give the height in km.

<Hint>The field strength goes as $1/r^{2}$. How must $r$ change for the field to fall to half its value?</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-b" label="Height (km):" value="2640" tolerance="5%" calc="p11-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Distance from the centre">
$\dfrac{G M}{r^{2}} = \dfrac12 \cdot \dfrac{G M}{R^{2}}$ gives $r = \sqrt{2}\,R$.
</Step>
<Step title="Height">
$h = r - R = (\sqrt{2} - 1)\,R = 0.414 \times 6371 = 2640$ km.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
At that distance, $G M/r^{2} = 4.91$ N/kg, half of the $9.82$ N/kg that $G M/R^{2}$ gives at the surface. A common slip is to double the radius: at $h = R$ the field is only a quarter.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 12

<Exercise id="p12" title="An asteroid's orbit" points={8}>

An asteroid's closest distance to the Sun is 1.9 au and its farthest is 3.3 au.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the semi-major axis of its orbit?

<Hint>The closest and farthest points lie at the two ends of the long axis of the ellipse.</Hint>

<Answer id="p12-a" label="Semi-major axis (au):" value="2.6" tolerance="5%" calc="p12-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Half the long axis">
The long axis is $1.9 + 3.3 = 5.2$ au, so $a = 2.6$ au.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
It lies between the closest and farthest distances, as it must.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is its orbital period, in years?

<Hint>For orbits around the Sun, Kepler's third law in years and astronomical units has no constant in it.</Hint>

<Answer id="p12-b" label="Period (years):" value="4.19" tolerance="5%" calc="p12-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Third law">
$T^{2} = a^{3} = 2.6^{3} = 17.58$, so $T = 4.19$ years.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
$4.19^{2} = 17.6$, the same as $a^{3}$. The orbit is larger than the Earth's, so the period is longer than one year, as the third law says it must be.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

How many times faster does it move at its closest point than at its farthest?

<Hint>Kepler's second law: near the Sun the line to the asteroid is short, so it must move faster to sweep out the same area. At these two points the velocity is at right angles to that line.</Hint>

<Answer id="p12-c" label="Speed ratio:" value="1.74" tolerance="5%" calc="p12-c" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Equal areas">
$r_p\,v_p = r_a\,v_a$, so $v_p/v_a = r_a/r_p = 3.3/1.9 = 1.74$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The ratio is larger than 1, so it moves fastest near the Sun. For a circle both distances would be equal and the ratio would be exactly 1.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 13

<Exercise id="p13" title="Weighing a star and a galaxy" points={10} optional>

This problem is optional: it does not count towards the score of the set.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Astronomers find a planet that circles its star once every 48 days at a distance of $3.5 \times 10^{10}$ m. Assume the orbit is a circle and the planet's mass is tiny next to the star's. What is the mass of the star? Type a number like `1.2e5`.

<Hint>Gravity supplies the centripetal force. With $v = 2\pi r/T$ this gives Kepler's third law with the central mass in it.</Hint>

<Answer id="p13-a" label="Mass of the star (kg):" value="1.47e30" tolerance="5%" calc="p13-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Period in seconds">
$T = 48 \times 24 \times 3600 = 4.147 \times 10^{6}$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Kepler's third law for the mass">
$M = \dfrac{4\pi^{2} r^{3}}{G\,T^{2}} = \dfrac{4\pi^{2} \times (3.5 \times 10^{10})^{3}}{6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times (4.147 \times 10^{6})^{2}} = 1.47 \times 10^{30}$ kg.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by another route">
The planet's speed is $v = 2\pi r/T = 5.30 \times 10^{4}$ m/s, and $M = v^{2} r/G$ gives the same $1.47 \times 10^{30}$ kg. That is about 0.74 of the Sun's mass: an ordinary star, a little lighter than the Sun.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

A star in a spiral galaxy moves in a circle of radius $3.0 \times 10^{20}$ m around the galaxy's centre at $2.1 \times 10^{5}$ m/s. Treating the mass inside its orbit as if it sat at the centre, how much mass lies inside the orbit?

<Answer id="p13-b" label="Mass (kg):" value="1.98e41" tolerance="5%" calc="p13-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Speed and radius">
$M = \dfrac{v^{2} r}{G} = \dfrac{(2.1 \times 10^{5})^{2} \times 3.0 \times 10^{20}}{6.674 \times 10^{-11}} = 1.98 \times 10^{41}$ kg.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The centripetal acceleration $v^{2}/r = 1.47 \times 10^{-10}$ m/s² is tiny, yet over such a large radius it needs an enormous mass. Comparing masses found this way with the mass of the stars we can see is one of the main pieces of evidence for dark matter, as in the last lesson of this block.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
