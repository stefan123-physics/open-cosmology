Lesson 2.1 measured forces with a spring balance and promised to explain why its scale has equal steps. This lesson keeps that promise. A spring is the simplest object whose force you can predict from a measurement of length alone: stretch it twice as far and it pulls twice as hard. That rule, Hooke's law, turns a spring into a measuring instrument, a shock absorber in a car and, in Block 5, the model for everything that vibrates.

## Stretching and squeezing a spring

Lay a spring on a smooth table, fix one end to a wall and leave the other end free. With no force on it, the spring takes its **natural length** $L_0$: it is neither stretched nor squeezed. Pull the free end and the spring gets longer; push it towards the wall and the spring gets shorter. Either way, you have to keep exerting a force to hold it there (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 8.1.1).

What matters is not the length $L$ of the spring itself, but how much it differs from the natural length. That difference is the **extension**:

<Formula label="Extension of a spring" tex="x = L - L_0" cite="mit" at="Sec. 8.1.1, Figure 8.1 (x measured from the end of the spring at its equilibrium length l₀)" />

A stretched spring has a positive extension. A squeezed (compressed) spring is shorter than $L_0$, so its extension is negative; its size is then called the **compression**. In this lesson $x$ always points along the spring, positive in the direction in which the spring gets longer.

## Hooke's law

Hang different weights on a spring and measure each extension, and you find a simple pattern: as long as the spring is not stretched too far, the force needed is **proportional to the extension**. Twice the extension needs twice the force; three times the extension, three times the force. Robert Hooke described this in the seventeenth century, and it is called **Hooke's law**. For the size of the force:

<Formula label="Size of the spring force" tex="F = k\,x" cite="mit" at="Sec. 8.1.1, Eq. (8.1.1)" />

Here $x$ is the size of the extension (or compression), and $k$ is a number that belongs to the spring: its **spring constant**. By Newton's third law (lesson 2.4), the force of your hand on the spring and the force of the spring on your hand are equally large and opposite, so this one formula gives both.

Dutch VWO textbooks and Binas often write $C$ for the spring constant and $u$ for the extension, so that Hooke's law reads $F = C\cdot u$, with $C$ in N/m. It is the same law; this site uses $k$ and $x$, as the university courses do (and as lesson 5.1 does).

Now the direction. A stretched spring pulls the free end back towards the wall; a compressed spring pushes it away from the wall. In both cases the spring force points **back towards the natural length**, opposite to the extension. A force that always points back towards the equilibrium position (the position where the forces balance, lesson 2.2) is called a **restoring force**. With signs, Hooke's law reads:

<Formula label="Hooke's law" tex="F = -k\,x" cite="mit" at="Sec. 8.1.1, Eq. (8.1.2), there written F_x = −kx" />

Here $F$ is the spring's force on the object at its free end, with the same positive direction as $x$. The minus sign carries the whole idea of a restoring force:

- stretched, $x > 0$: then $F < 0$, the spring pulls back;
- compressed, $x < 0$: then $F > 0$, the spring pushes back out;
- at the natural length, $x = 0$: no force at all.

Hooke's law is not a fundamental law of nature, like Newton's law of gravitation (lesson 2.11) or the law for the electric force between charges, which hold for all matter everywhere. It is a **model** found by experiment: many springs were tested, each behaved this way over a range of stretches, and the rule was then trusted for all ideal springs (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 8.1.1). Its power is that it predicts the force from a length, without measuring the force.

## The spring constant

The spring constant says how stiff a spring is. From $F = kx$, it is the force per metre of extension:

<Formula label="Spring constant" tex="k = \frac{F}{x}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 8.1.1, Eq. (8.1.1) solved for k (k in N·m⁻¹)" />

So its unit is the newton per metre, **N/m**. A stiff spring, like the one under a car, needs a large force for a small stretch and has a large $k$. A soft spring, like the one in a ballpoint pen, has a small $k$. Every spring has its own value, and it does not depend on how far you happen to stretch it (below the limit of the next section).

<WorkedExample title="A spring in a toy">
<Problem>
A spring in a toy has a natural length of 12.0 cm. A force of 7.0 N stretches it to 15.5 cm. (a) What is its spring constant? (b) What force stretches it by 5.0 cm?
</Problem>
<Step title="Find the extension">
$x = L - L_0 = 15.5 - 12.0 = 3.5$ cm $= 0.035$ m. Always convert to metres before using $k$ in N/m.
</Step>
<Step title="Spring constant">
$k = F/x = 7.0/0.035 = 200$ N/m: every centimetre of stretch needs 2.0 N.
</Step>
<Step title="Force for 5.0 cm">
$F = kx = 200 \times 0.050 = 10$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: N/m times m gives N. Proportion: 5.0 cm is a bit less than one and a half times 3.5 cm, and 10 N is a bit less than one and a half times 7.0 N. The force grows in the same ratio as the extension, as it must.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The force–extension graph

Plot the force on a spring against its extension and Hooke's law gives a straight line through the origin: the force is directly proportional to the extension (lesson 0.5). Its **slope is the spring constant** $k$: the rise in newtons divided by the run in metres.

## The elastic limit

Hooke's law only holds over a limited range (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 8.1.1). Stretch a spring further and further, and two things happen in turn:

- beyond the **limit of proportionality**, the graph starts to curve: the force is no longer proportional to the extension;
- beyond the **elastic limit**, the spring no longer returns to its natural length when you let go. It stays permanently longer, and its old spring constant is no longer valid.

For most metal springs the two limits lie close together. Below the elastic limit a spring is **elastic**: it always springs back. Every calculation in this lesson assumes the spring stays well inside that range. A slinky that has been pulled out too far and hangs limp is a spring that went past its elastic limit.

Some springs, such as those in a garage door or a trampoline, are wound so tightly that the coils press on each other. They need a starting force before they begin to stretch at all (the notes call it a pre-tension, Sec. 8.1.1, Eq. 8.1.4). Their force–extension line does not pass through the origin, but its slope is still the spring constant.

## Measuring a spring constant

To find $k$ in the lab, hang a spring from a stand, measure its natural length, then hang on it weights of known size one after another and measure each new length. The table shows a set of such measurements, with weights of 0.50 N each.

| Load | Extension |
|---|---|
| 0.50 N | 2.1 cm |
| 1.00 N | 3.9 cm |
| 1.50 N | 6.2 cm |
| 2.00 N | 7.9 cm |
| 2.50 N | 10.1 cm |

Real measurements scatter a little, so do not divide each load by its extension and pick one answer. Plot the points, draw the **line of best fit** through them and the origin (lesson 0.5), and find its slope.

<Problem>
Find the spring constant from the line of best fit in the graph above.
</Problem>
<Step title="Pick two points on the line, far apart">

## The spring balance

Now the spring balance of lesson 2.1 makes sense. Its pointer moves by the extension $x$ of the spring inside, and $x = F/k$ is proportional to the force on the hook. So equal steps of force give equal steps along the scale, and the scale can be marked with evenly spaced lines once a single known weight has fixed the spacing.

Suppose a spring balance contains a spring with $k = 400$ N/m and has room for 10 cm of stretch. Its largest reading is $F = kx = 400 \times 0.10 = 40$ N, and each newton on the scale is $1/400$ m $= 2.5$ mm apart. A balance for weighing letters needs a much softer spring, so that small forces still move the pointer a readable distance. And a balance should never be loaded past its largest reading: beyond the elastic limit the spring stays stretched and the scale is wrong from then on.

## A mass hanging at rest

Hang an object of mass $m$ from a spring and let it come to rest. Two forces act on the object: its weight $mg$ downwards and the spring force $kx$ upwards. It is at rest, so by Newton's first law (lesson 2.2) the forces balance:

<Formula label="A mass hanging at rest on a spring" tex="k\,x = m\,g" cite="mit" at="Sec. 8.1.1, Eq. (8.1.1) for the size of the spring force, set equal to the weight of Sec. 8.2.3, Eq. (8.2.2)" />

So the spring stretches by $x = mg/k$: twice the mass, twice the stretch. This is exactly how the hanging weights in the table above stretched the lab spring.

<WorkedExample title="A bird feeder on a spring">
<Problem>
A bird feeder of mass 0.25 kg hangs from a spring with $k = 40$ N/m. How far is the spring stretched when the feeder hangs still? And how far when a bird of 0.050 kg sits on it?
</Problem>
<Step title="Forces in balance">
The weight is $mg = 0.25 \times 9.81 = 2.45$ N. At rest the spring pulls up just as hard, so $kx = 2.45$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Extension">
$x = mg/k = 2.45/40 = 0.061$ m, about 6.1 cm.
</Step>
<Step title="With the bird">
The total mass is 0.30 kg, so $x = 0.30 \times 9.81/40 = 0.074$ m, about 7.4 cm. The bird alone adds $0.050 \times 9.81/40 = 0.012$ m: it pulls the feeder 1.2 cm further down. (Subtracting the rounded 6.1 cm from 7.4 cm gives 1.3 cm; the unrounded stretches, 6.13 and 7.36 cm, differ by 1.2 cm.)
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: (kg × N/kg)/(N/m) gives m. The bird adds a fifth to the mass and should add a fifth to the stretch: a fifth of 6.1 cm is 1.2 cm. It does.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Springs in series and in parallel

Two springs can work together in two ways. Take a spring with $k_1 = 60$ N/m and one with $k_2 = 30$ N/m, and hang a load of 3.0 N from them. Assume the springs themselves are so light that their own weight can be ignored.

**In parallel** (side by side, both holding a short bar from which the load hangs, so that the bar stays level; for unequal springs the load must hang nearer the stiffer spring): both springs stretch by the same amount $x$ and share the load. Together they pull up with $k_1 x + k_2 x = (k_1 + k_2)\,x$, so the pair acts like one spring with $k = k_1 + k_2 = 90$ N/m. Under 3.0 N it stretches $3.0/90 = 0.033$ m, about 3.3 cm. Parallel springs are stiffer than either one alone.

**In series** (one hooked below the other): each spring carries the **whole** load. The lower spring holds the load; the upper spring holds the lower spring, which pulls on it with the full 3.0 N (Newton's third law, as with the tension in a rope, lesson 2.5). So each spring stretches as much as it would alone, and the stretches add: $3.0/60 + 3.0/30 = 0.050 + 0.10 = 0.15$ m. The pair acts like one spring with $k = 3.0/0.15 = 20$ N/m. In general $1/k = 1/k_1 + 1/k_2$, and springs in series are softer than either one alone.

A quick check: two identical springs in parallel are twice as stiff as one; in series they are half as stiff. Cutting a spring in half does the reverse of putting two in series: each half has twice the spring constant of the whole spring.

## A first look at oscillations

Hooke's law and Newton's second law together predict more than a resting position. Put an object of 0.50 kg on a smooth table, attached to the toy spring of $k = 200$ N/m, pull it 4.0 cm from the natural length and let go. At that instant the spring pulls back with $F = kx = 200 \times 0.040 = 8.0$ N, so the object starts with an acceleration $a = F/m = 8.0/0.50 = 16$ m/s² towards the natural length (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 8.1.1, Eq. 8.1.3).

What happens next? The object speeds up until it reaches the natural length. There the force is zero, but the object is moving and, by the first law, keeps going. Now the spring is compressed and pushes back, slowing the object down until it stops on the other side, and the whole thing runs in reverse. With no friction the object moves back and forth for ever: it **oscillates** (moves to and fro about its equilibrium position, again and again).

This is the motion that Block 5 (lesson 5.1) studies as **simple harmonic motion**: the restoring force $F = -kx$ makes the position change with time like a sine (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 23.2). The same back-and-forth of springs is what Hooke and others used to make watches keep time (Sec. 23.1).

## Why springs obey Hooke's law

Why should a coil of steel behave so neatly? A spring is made of atoms held in place by electric forces between their electrons and nuclei. Pull the atoms slightly apart and these forces pull them back; push them slightly together and the forces push them apart. For small shifts the restoring force is proportional to the shift, so the whole spring, made of countless such links, obeys Hooke's law too. A full explanation needs quantum mechanics (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 8.2). Pull too hard and atoms slide past each other into new positions: that is the elastic limit, seen from the inside.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Almost anything pushed a little away from a stable position feels a restoring force roughly proportional to the push (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 23.7), so Hooke's law and the oscillations it causes turn up all over physics, from atoms in a crystal to the air in a sound wave (Block 5). Cosmologists find traces of them in the cosmic microwave background, the oldest light we can see, and in how galaxies are spread across the sky. The university courses build up to this one step at a time.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The **extension** of a spring is $x = L - L_0$, measured from its natural length $L_0$; a compression is a negative extension.
- **Hooke's law:** the spring force is proportional to the extension and points back towards the natural length, $F = -kx$. Its size is $F = kx$. It is a **restoring force**.
- The **spring constant** $k = F/x$ is in N/m: large for a stiff spring, small for a soft one.
- A force–extension graph is a straight line through the origin whose **slope is $k$**; find $k$ from the line of best fit through measured points.
- Hooke's law holds only up to the limit of proportionality; past the **elastic limit** a spring stays permanently stretched.
- A spring balance has an evenly spaced scale because its stretch is proportional to the force.
- A mass hanging at rest stretches a spring by $x = mg/k$, from $kx = mg$.
- In parallel $k = k_1 + k_2$ (stiffer); in series $1/k = 1/k_1 + 1/k_2$ (softer).
- A mass on a spring, pulled and released, **oscillates** about its equilibrium position: simple harmonic motion, Block 5.
