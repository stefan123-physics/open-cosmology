Kick a ball and it flies off. Squeeze a sponge and it changes shape. Lean on a door and it swings open. Each time something pushes or pulls on something else. Block 1 described motion: where an object is, how fast it goes and how its velocity changes. This block asks **why** the motion changes, and the answer is always a force. This lesson introduces forces themselves: what they are, how they are measured, which kinds there are and how several forces on one object combine.

## Pushes and pulls

A **force** is a push or a pull that one object exerts on another. A force can do three things to an object:

- start it moving, stop it, or make it faster or slower;
- change the direction in which it moves;
- change its shape, as when you stretch a spring or squash clay.

The first two together mean: a force can change an object's **velocity**, and so give it an **acceleration** (lesson 1.4). Lesson 2.3 turns that into Newton's second law.

A force always has a size and a direction. A push of the same strength to the left or to the right has opposite effects, so a force is a **vector** (lesson 0.8). It is written $\vec F$, and its size, the **magnitude** $F$, is never negative. Every force also has two objects involved: the object that **exerts** it and the object it **acts on**. Name forces that way: "the push of the hand on the door", "the pull of the Earth on the ball". In this lesson we always look at the forces acting **on** one chosen object.

## The newton

The SI unit of force is the **newton**, symbol N, named after Isaac Newton. It is not a new base unit: lesson 0.2 already showed it is built from the kilogram, the metre and the second.

<Formula label="The newton in SI base units" tex="1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^{2}" cite="si-units" at="1.3, Combinations of SI base units" />

The combination comes from the way physicists measure a force: by the acceleration it gives a known mass (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 7.1). A force of **1 N gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s²**: if nothing else pushes or pulls, its velocity grows by 1 m/s every second. Lesson 2.3 makes this exact.

To get a feeling for the size: a small apple of mass 0.10 kg is pulled down by the Earth with a force of about 1 N (the section on weight below shows how to work this out), and holding up a full 1.5-litre bottle of water, with a mass of 1.5 kg, takes about 15 N.

## Kinds of force

Forces come in two groups.

A **contact force** acts only while the two objects touch.

- **Applied force** $\vec F_A$: a push or pull by a person or a machine, such as your hand on a shopping trolley.
- **Normal force** $\vec F_N$: the push of a surface on an object resting against it. "Normal" means perpendicular: this force is always at right angles to the surface. A table holds up a book with an upward normal force; a wall you lean on pushes back horizontally.
- **Friction** $\vec F_f$: the force of a surface along the surface, which opposes sliding (or an attempt to slide) between the two surfaces. It is why a sliding box comes to a stop.
- **Tension**: the pull of a rope, string, chain or cable on the object tied to it. A rope can only pull, never push, and it pulls along its own length.
- **Drag** (air resistance): the force of air or water on an object moving through it, opposing the motion.

A **non-contact force** acts across a distance, even through empty space.

- **Gravity**: every object with mass attracts every other. The pull of the Earth on an object is called its **weight**. The Earth also pulls on the Moon this way, with no rope in between.
- **Electric force**: between electric charges. A rubbed balloon attracts small bits of paper; an electron and a proton attract each other (Block 6).
- **Magnetic force**: between magnets, and between a magnet and iron, such as a magnet pulling a paperclip (Block 6).

The split is useful in practice, but it is not deep. Look closely at "contact" and the atoms of your hand never quite touch the atoms of the table: when they come very close, the electrons in them repel each other. The normal force is, underneath, an electric force between atoms (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 8.3.1), and so, at bottom, is friction.

## Weight

Near the Earth's surface every object falls with the same acceleration $g = 9.81$ m/s² if air resistance can be neglected (lesson 1.7). The force that causes this fall is the object's weight, and it is proportional to the mass:

<Formula label="Weight near the Earth's surface" tex="F_g = m\,g" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 8.2.3, Eq. (8.2.2)" />

Here $m$ is the mass in kilograms and $g = 9.81$ N/kg. That is the same $g$ as in lesson 1.7, written with a different unit: $1\ \text{N/kg} = 1\ (\text{kg·m/s}^{2})/\text{kg} = 1\ \text{m/s}^{2}$. Lesson 2.3 explains why the weight is exactly $m$ times the acceleration of free fall. The weight points straight down, towards the centre of the Earth. Some books write it as $W$ instead of $F_g$; this course uses $F_g$.

For the apple of the section on the newton, $F_g = 0.10 \times 9.81 = 0.981$ N, about 1 N. A bag of potatoes of 2.5 kg weighs $2.5 \times 9.81 = 24.5$ N, about 25 N.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Mass is not weight">
In everyday speech "weight" often means mass ("I weigh 60 kilos"). In physics they differ. **Mass**, in kilograms, says how much matter an object has and is the same everywhere. **Weight**, in newtons, is the force of gravity on it, and depends on where the object is: on the Moon the same bag of potatoes would weigh much less, though its mass is still 2.5 kg.
</Callout>

## Measuring a force

A force is usually measured with a **spring balance**, also called a newton meter: a spring in a tube, with a hook at the bottom and a scale marked in newtons. The larger the pull on the hook, the more the spring stretches (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 8.1.1), so the pointer moves further down the scale. The scale is made by hanging known weights on the spring. Lesson 2.7 shows that for a good spring the stretch is proportional to the force, which is why the scale has equal steps.

<WorkedExample title="Reading a spring balance">
<Problem>
A bag of sugar with a mass of 0.40 kg hangs at rest from a spring balance. What does the balance read?
</Problem>
<Step title="Find the weight">
The bag is pulled down by its weight, $F_g = m g = 0.40 \times 9.81 = 3.92$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="What the spring does">
The bag hangs still, so the spring must pull it up with a force just as large as its weight (the next sections explain why). The balance shows the size of that pull: about 3.9 N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: kg times N/kg gives N. Size: 0.40 kg is a bit less than half a kilogram, and half a kilogram weighs about 4.9 N, so 3.9 N is sensible.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Free-body diagrams

Most objects feel several forces at once. To keep track of them, physicists draw a **free-body diagram**: the object is drawn as a single dot, and every force acting **on** it is an arrow that starts at the dot. The rules:

- draw only the forces on the chosen object, never the forces it exerts on other things;
- the direction of each arrow is the direction of the force;
- the length shows the size, so equal forces get equal arrows;
- label every arrow, for example $\vec F_g$, $\vec F_N$, $\vec F_A$, $\vec F_f$.

A book of 1.2 kg lies on a table. Two forces act on it: its weight, $1.2 \times 9.81 = 11.8$ N downwards, and the normal force of the table upwards. The book stays put, so the two arrows are equally long.

## The resultant force

All the forces on an object together have the same effect as one single force: their vector sum. This is the **resultant force**, or **net force**:

<Formula label="Resultant (net) force" tex="\vec F_\text{net} = \vec F_1 + \vec F_2 + \cdots" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 8.4.1, Eq. (8.4.1)" />

That forces really add like the arrows of lesson 0.8 is an experimental fact: two forces applied together give the same acceleration as their vector sum applied alone (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 7.1, Eq. 7.1.6). All the forces must act on the **same** object.

**Forces along one line.** Choose one direction as positive and add the forces as signed numbers. A negative answer means the resultant points the other way. For the crate, vertically $+245 + (-245) = 0$: the weight and the normal force cancel. Horizontally, with forwards positive, $+120 + (-80) = +40$ N. The resultant force on the crate is 40 N forwards.

<WorkedExample title="A tug of war">
<Problem>
In a tug of war, team Red pulls the rope to the left with 820 N and team Blue pulls it to the right with 760 N. What is the resultant force on the rope?
</Problem>
<Step title="Choose a positive direction">
Take right as positive. Blue's pull is $+760$ N and Red's pull is $-820$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Add">
$F_\text{net} = +760 + (-820) = -60$ N. The minus sign means to the left: the resultant force is 60 N to the left, so the rope starts to move towards team Red.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The resultant must point towards the stronger team and be the difference of the two pulls: $820 - 760 = 60$ N. It is.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Forces in different directions.** Use components, as in lesson 0.8: split every force into its $x$- and $y$-components, add the $x$-components and add the $y$-components, then find the size and direction of the result.

<Formula label="Components of the net force" tex="F_{\text{net},x} = F_{1,x} + F_{2,x} + \cdots, \qquad F_{\text{net},y} = F_{1,y} + F_{2,y} + \cdots" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 8.4.1, Eq. (8.4.3)" />

<WorkedExample title="A sledge pulled by two ropes">
<Problem>
Two children pull a sledge over flat snow, seen from above. One rope pulls with 150 N at 25° to the left of the forward direction, the other with 120 N at 40° to the right. Take forward as the $+x$ direction and left as $+y$. What is the resultant of the two pulls?
</Problem>
<Step title="Components of each pull">
First rope: $150\cos 25° = 136$ N forwards and $150\sin 25° = 63.4$ N to the left.
Second rope (40° to the right, so a negative $y$-component): $120\cos 40° = 91.9$ N forwards and $-120\sin 40° = -77.1$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Add the components">
$F_{\text{net},x} = 136 + 91.9 = 228$ N and $F_{\text{net},y} = 63.4 + (-77.1) = -13.7$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Size and direction">
$F_\text{net} = \sqrt{228^{2} + 13.7^{2}} = 228$ N. The $y$-component is negative and small, so the resultant points a little to the right of straight ahead: $\tan^{-1}(13.74/227.9) = 3.5°$ to the right. (Keep the extra digits until the end, as in lesson 0.3: the rounded values $13.7/228$ would give 3.4°.)
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The resultant (228 N) is less than the two pulls added (270 N), because the ropes are not parallel. The rope at 40° is the weaker one but pulls more sideways ($77.1$ N against $63.4$ N), so the resultant leans slightly towards its side, as found.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Balanced and unbalanced forces

When the resultant force on an object is zero, the forces on it are **balanced**. The book on the table is an example: weight and normal force cancel. When the resultant is not zero, the forces are **unbalanced**, like the horizontal forces on the crate (40 N forwards) or on the tug-of-war rope.

What do these two cases do to the motion? Experiments, and lessons 2.2 and 2.3, give a short answer:

- **Balanced forces:** the velocity does not change. An object at rest stays at rest, and a moving object keeps the same speed in the same direction.
- **Unbalanced forces:** the velocity changes, in the direction of the resultant force. The object accelerates.

Be careful with the first point: balanced forces do **not** mean that nothing moves. A car driving at a steady speed on a straight road has balanced forces on it; the forward push of the road on the driving wheels just cancels the backward forces of the air and the road. Conversely, an object can be standing still for an instant while the forces on it are unbalanced: a ball at the top of its flight (lesson 1.7) is pulled down by its weight alone.

The single force that would balance a set of forces is the opposite of their resultant: same size, opposite direction. To keep the sledge from moving, a third child would have to pull back with 228 N, 3.5° to the left of straight backwards.

Push a crate or a fridge and watch friction push back; in the tug of war, add pullers on both sides and see when the forces are balanced and when the cart starts to roll.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Physicists today trace every force in this lesson to a few fundamental interactions: gravity, the electromagnetic force (electric and magnetic forces together) and two nuclear forces that act only over distances no larger than an atomic nucleus. The electric force between two charged particles is enormously stronger than the gravitational pull between them, so why does gravity rule the motion of planets, stars and galaxies? Electric charges come in two kinds, positive and negative, and large bodies hold almost equal amounts of both, so their electric forces nearly cancel. Gravity only attracts (in Newton's theory, which this course uses), so it adds up with every extra kilogram. On the scale of planets and galaxies, and in the expanding universe as a whole, gravity is therefore the force that matters most. The university cosmology course (MIT 8.286) starts from exactly that: Newton's gravity acting on a uniform, expanding cloud of matter.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A force is a push or a pull of one object on another; it can change an object's velocity or shape. It is a vector, $\vec F$, with a magnitude $F$ and a direction.
- The SI unit is the newton: $1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^{2}$, the force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s².
- Contact forces: applied force, normal force (perpendicular to the surface), friction (along the surface), tension, drag. Non-contact forces: gravity, electric and magnetic forces.
- Weight near the Earth is $F_g = m g$, with $g = 9.81$ N/kg, pointing down. Mass (kg) is not weight (N).
- A spring balance measures a force by the stretch of a spring.
- A free-body diagram shows the object as a dot and every force on it as a labelled arrow from the dot.
- The resultant (net) force is the vector sum of all forces on the object: along a line add signed numbers, otherwise add components.
- Balanced forces (resultant zero): the velocity stays the same. Unbalanced forces: the object accelerates.
