Push against a wall and you feel the wall push back on your hands. Step off a small boat onto a jetty and the boat slides away behind you. Let go of an inflated balloon and it shoots off as the air rushes out. In each case one object pushes on another, and the other pushes back.

Newton's third law says that this always happens. No force ever acts alone: forces come in **pairs**, and the two forces of a pair are exactly equal in size. This lesson is about recognising those pairs and about the most common mistakes people make with them.

## Forces come in pairs

A force is always one object pushing or pulling on another. Newton noticed that the second object always pushes or pulls back. Calling the objects A and B, the law reads (Siyavula states it in almost the same words):

> **Newton's third law.** If body A exerts a force on body B, then body B exerts a force of the same size on body A, in the opposite direction.

Write $\vec F_{AB}$ for the force that A exerts on B, and $\vec F_{BA}$ for the force that B exerts on A. The law then says:

<Formula label="Newton's third law" tex="\vec F_{AB} = -\vec F_{BA}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 7.4, Eq. (7.4.1), with A and B for bodies 1 and 2" />

The minus sign means "the same size, the opposite direction". The two forces together are called an **interaction pair**, or an **action-reaction pair**. Every such pair has three properties:

1. The two forces have the **same size** and point in **opposite directions**.
2. They are the **same type** of force: if one is gravity, so is the other; if one is a contact push, so is the other.
3. They act on **two different objects**: one on A, the other on B.

The names "action" and "reaction" suggest that one force comes first and causes the other. It does not. The two forces exist at the same moment, start together and stop together. Either one may be called the action.

## Finding the partner of a force

To find the partner of a force, first say the force in full as "X pushes (or pulls) Y". Then swap the two names. The partner is "Y pushes (or pulls) X", of the same type and in the opposite direction.

- Your foot kicks a ball forwards. Partner: the ball pushes your foot backwards.
- A rope pulls a sledge forwards. Partner: the sledge pulls the rope backwards.
- The Earth pulls an apple down (gravity). Partner: the apple pulls the Earth up (gravity too).

The last example shows that the law also holds for forces that act at a distance, such as gravity and the pull between magnets, not only for contact forces.

A useful habit: never write just "gravity" or "the push". Write who exerts the force and on whom, such as "the gravitational pull of the Earth on the apple". Then the partner can be read off at once.

## A book on a table

A book lies at rest on a table. Two forces act on the book:

- its **weight**: the Earth pulls the book down by gravity;
- the **normal force**: the table pushes the book up. ("Normal" means "at right angles to the surface"; Lesson 2.5 treats this force in detail.)

These two forces are equal in size and opposite in direction. It is tempting to call them a third-law pair, but they are not: both act on the **same** object, the book, and they are of **different types**, one gravity and one a contact push.

The true partners are found by swapping names:

| Force on the book | Its third-law partner |
|---|---|
| Earth pulls book down (gravity) | book pulls Earth up (gravity) |
| table pushes book up (contact) | book pushes table down (contact) |

So why are the weight and the normal force equal? Because the book does not accelerate. By the second law the total force on it must then be zero, so the table pushes up exactly as hard as the Earth pulls down. That is a different law. In a lift that is speeding up on its way upwards, the floor pushes up on a person harder than their weight, so these two forces are no longer equal. The third-law partners, floor on feet and feet on floor, stay equal all the time.

## Why the pair never cancels

If every force has an equal and opposite partner, how can anything ever start to move? Do the two forces not add up to zero?

They would, if they acted on the same object. But the two forces of a pair act on **different** objects, and an object only feels the forces that act on it. To decide how one object moves, list only the forces **on** that object and add those; its own push on other things does not count.

Think of a dog pulling a sledge across snow. The dog pulls the sledge forwards, and the sledge pulls the dog backwards with the same force. The sledge feels only the forwards pull (and some friction from the snow), so it can speed up. The dog moves forwards because its paws push backwards on the ground, and the ground pushes the dog forwards. As long as the ground's push on the dog is larger than the sledge's pull on the dog, the dog speeds up too.

## Walking, swimming and rockets

Almost every way of getting moving uses the third law. To go forwards, you push something **else** backwards.

- **Walking and running.** Your foot pushes backwards on the ground. The ground pushes your foot forwards, and that forwards push is what moves you. On smooth ice the ground cannot grip your foot, so this push stays small, which is why walking on ice is so hard.
- **Swimming.** Your hands and feet push water backwards. The water pushes you forwards.
- **A car.** The engine turns the wheels; the tyres push backwards on the road, and the road pushes the car forwards.
- **A rocket.** The engine burns fuel and pushes the hot exhaust gas out backwards at high speed. The gas pushes the rocket forwards with a force of the same size. The rocket does not need air to push against: it pushes on its own exhaust. That is why rockets work in the vacuum of space, where there is no air at all.

## Equal forces, different accelerations

The two forces of a pair are always equal, but the objects need not move in the same way. By Newton's second law, $a = F/m$. When both bodies feel the same size of force $F$, each accelerates in its own way:

<Formula label="Accelerations of the two bodies of a pair" tex="a_A = \frac{F}{m_A}, \qquad a_B = \frac{F}{m_B}, \qquad \frac{a_A}{a_B} = \frac{m_B}{m_A}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 7.3, Eq. (7.3.5), applied to both bodies, with Sec. 7.4, Eq. (7.4.1) for the equal forces" />

(These formulas hold when the pair is the only force on each body, or the other forces cancel.) The lighter body gets the larger acceleration, in proportion: half the mass, twice the acceleration.

<WorkedExample title="Two trolleys colliding">
<Problem>
Two trolleys roll towards each other on a smooth, level track and collide. Trolley A has a mass of 0.50 kg, trolley B a mass of 1.5 kg. At one moment during the collision, A pushes on B with a force of 6.0 N. What is the force on A at that moment, and what are the two accelerations? (Friction is small enough to ignore.)
</Problem>
<Step title="The force on A">
By the third law, B pushes on A with 6.0 N, in the opposite direction. The size of the trolleys makes no difference.
</Step>
<Step title="The two accelerations">
$a_A = \dfrac{6.0\ \text{N}}{0.50\ \text{kg}} = 12\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $a_B = \dfrac{6.0\ \text{N}}{1.5\ \text{kg}} = 4.0\ \text{m/s}^2$, in opposite directions.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
B has three times the mass of A, so it should get a third of A's acceleration: $12/3 = 4.0$ m/s². Units: $1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^2$, so N/kg is m/s².
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The same idea works on a huge scale. In Lesson 1.10 you found that the Moon, going round the Earth, has a centripetal acceleration of about $2.7 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s² towards the Earth. Something must pull it: the Earth's gravity. By the third law, the Moon pulls the Earth just as hard.

<WorkedExample title="The Earth and the Moon pull each other">
<Problem>
The Moon has a mass of $7.35 \times 10^{22}$ kg and the Earth $5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg (NASA fact sheets). Use the Moon's acceleration of $2.7 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s² to find the force between them, and the acceleration this gives the Earth.
</Problem>
<Step title="The force on the Moon">
$F = m a = 7.35 \times 10^{22}\ \text{kg} \times 2.7 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m/s}^2 \approx 2.0 \times 10^{20}$ N, towards the Earth.
</Step>
<Step title="The force on the Earth">
The third law: the Moon pulls the Earth with the same $2.0 \times 10^{20}$ N, towards the Moon.
</Step>
<Step title="The Earth's acceleration">
$a_\text{Earth} = \dfrac{2.0 \times 10^{20}\ \text{N}}{5.97 \times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{-5}$ m/s². (Here the force before rounding, 1.98 × 10²⁰ N, was divided, so that rounding twice does not shift the last digit.)
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The Earth is about 81 times as massive as the Moon, so its acceleration should be about 81 times smaller: $2.7 \times 10^{-3}/81 \approx 3.3 \times 10^{-5}$ m/s², the same answer. (Strictly, the Moon's acceleration from Lesson 1.10 treats the Earth as fixed; taking the Earth's motion into account changes the force by only about 1 %.) Tiny, but not zero: the Earth too moves in a small monthly circle, about a point inside the Earth that lies on the line towards the Moon.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Common mistakes

- **"The bigger object pushes harder."** When a lorry hits a small car, the force of the lorry on the car is exactly as large as the force of the car on the lorry. The car is damaged more because its smaller mass gives it a much larger acceleration, not because it feels a larger force.
- **"The reaction comes after the action."** The two forces appear and vanish at the same moment.
- **"The pair cancels, so nothing can move."** The forces act on different objects, so they never cancel. Add only the forces on one object.
- **"The normal force is the reaction to the weight."** Both act on the same object and they are different types of force, so they cannot be a pair. Their partners are the book pushing the table and the book pulling the Earth.
- **"The third law only works for things that touch."** It holds for gravity, and for the forces between magnets and between electric charges at rest too: the Earth pulls the Moon and the Moon pulls the Earth.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
In space, the third law means that nothing simply orbits something else: both bodies move. The Moon pulls the Earth as hard as the Earth pulls the Moon, and its pull on the Earth's oceans is the main cause of the tides (why there are two high tides a day, not one, comes from the way gravity weakens with distance, which is beyond this lesson). A planet pulls its star as hard as the star pulls the planet, so the star moves in a small circle of its own. Astronomers have found many planets around other stars by measuring this tiny wobble of the star (NASA Science, *How We Find and Characterize*). On the largest scales, galaxies pull each other in pairs in the same way: our Milky Way and the Andromeda galaxy pull on each other with equal forces, and the two are moving towards each other (NASA Science, *NASA's Hubble Shows Milky Way is Destined for Head-On Collision*).
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Newton's third law:** if A exerts a force on B, then B exerts a force of the same size on A in the opposite direction: $\vec F_{AB} = -\vec F_{BA}$.
- The two forces of a pair are of the **same type**, act **at the same moment**, and act on **different objects**, so they never cancel.
- To find a partner, say the force as "X on Y" and swap the names. A book's weight pairs with the book pulling the Earth, not with the normal force.
- Walking, swimming, driving and rockets all work by pushing something backwards and being pushed forwards.
- Equal forces can give very different accelerations: by $a = F/m$, the lighter body accelerates more, $a_A/a_B = m_B/m_A$.
