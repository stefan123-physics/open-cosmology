Lesson 2.1 named the everyday forces, and lessons 2.3 and 2.4 gave the two laws that connect them to motion. This lesson takes a closer look at three of those forces: the **weight** of an object, the **normal force** of a surface and the **tension** in a string or rope. Each looks simple, and each hides a trap that catches many students. Together they are enough to understand a lift, a floating astronaut and two masses hanging over a pulley.

## Weight and the field strength g

Lesson 2.1 introduced the weight $\vec F_g$, the pull of the Earth on an object, and lesson 2.3 showed why it equals the mass times the acceleration of free fall. Written as sizes:

<Formula label="Weight and gravitational field strength" tex="F_g = m\,g, \qquad g = \frac{F_g}{m}" cite="reading3" at="Sec. 2.4, Weight and mass (F_g = m g)" />

The second form gives $g$ a second meaning. It is the **gravitational field strength**: the weight of each kilogram, in newtons per kilogram. Near the Earth's surface $g = 9.81$ N/kg, so every kilogram is pulled down with 9.81 N wherever it is, resting, moving or falling. You met the same number in lesson 1.7 as an acceleration; the units agree, since $1\ \text{N/kg} = 1\ \text{m/s}^{2}$.

The field strength depends on where you are. On the Moon it is 1.62 N/kg (the Moon's surface gravity, NASA Moon Fact Sheet). A suitcase of 12 kg has a weight of $12 \times 9.81 \approx 118$ N on the Earth and only $12 \times 1.62 \approx 19.4$ N on the Moon. Its mass is 12 kg in both places.

Two facts about weight matter for the rest of this lesson.

- **Weight acts all the time.** It does not switch off when an object rests on a table, hangs on a rope or floats in a space station.
- **You never feel your weight directly.** What you feel is the push of the floor on your feet or of a chair on your body. Gravity pulls equally on every kilogram of you; it is the surfaces holding you up that you notice. This is why the normal force deserves its own section.

## The normal force

The **normal force** $\vec F_N$ is the push of a surface on an object that touches it. "Normal" means perpendicular: the normal force always points straight out of the surface, at right angles to it (reading 1, The normal force). A floor pushes up, a wall pushes sideways, and a sloping surface pushes at a slant (lesson 2.8).

Where does it come from? When an object presses on a surface, the atoms of the surface are squeezed a tiny bit closer together, and their electrons push back (reading 2, Sec. 8.3.1). The harder the object presses, the harder the surface pushes back, up to the point where the surface breaks.

That gives the normal force its most important property: **it has no formula of its own. It takes whatever size is needed to stop the object moving into the surface.** You find it from Newton's second law. For an object resting on a level floor, with no other vertical forces, the vertical acceleration is zero, so the normal force must balance the weight:

<Formula label="Normal force when only weight acts vertically" tex="F_N = m\,g" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 8.3.1, Example 8.2, Eq. (8.3.4)" />

This is true **only in that situation**. The MIT notes stress the same point: the normal force and the weight are two different forces that happen to be equal here because the object is at rest (reading 2, Example 8.2). They are not a third-law pair either (lesson 2.4): the partner of the floor's push on the box is the box's push on the floor.

Take a crate of 6.0 kg on a level floor. Its weight is $6.0 \times 9.81 \approx 58.9$ N, so at rest the floor pushes up with 58.9 N. Now change one thing at a time, while the crate stays on the floor.

- **Push straight down on the lid with 25 N.** Two forces now point down. The crate still does not move, so the floor pushes up with $58.9 + 25 = 83.9$ N.
- **Pull straight up on a handle with 25 N.** Now the handle helps to hold the crate up, and the floor only has to supply $58.9 - 25 = 33.9$ N.
- **Pull up with 58.9 N.** The floor has nothing left to do: $F_N = 0$. Pull any harder and the crate leaves the floor. A surface can only push, never pull, so the normal force cannot become negative.

The same happens when a rope pulls at an angle, as lesson 2.3 hinted: the upward part of the pull takes over part of the floor's job.

<WorkedExample title="Pulling a suitcase on wheels">
<Problem>
A traveller pulls a suitcase of 15 kg along a level station floor. The handle pulls with 60 N at 30° above the horizontal. The wheels roll so easily that friction can be ignored. Find the normal force of the floor and the acceleration of the suitcase.
</Problem>
<Step title="Forces and components">
Three forces act: the weight $F_g = 15 \times 9.81 \approx 147$ N down, the normal force $F_N$ up, and the pull $F_A = 60$ N at 30°. Split the pull into components (lessons 0.7 and 0.8): vertically $60 \sin 30^\circ = 30$ N up, horizontally $60 \cos 30^\circ \approx 52.0$ N forwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Vertical: the normal force">
The suitcase does not move up or down, so the vertical forces cancel: $F_N + 30 - 147 = 0$, so $F_N \approx 117$ N. That is less than the weight, because the handle carries 30 N of it.
</Step>
<Step title="Horizontal: the acceleration">
Only the horizontal part of the pull speeds the suitcase up: $a = F_{\text{net}}/m = 52.0/15 \approx 3.46$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The normal force is positive, so the suitcase stays on the floor, as it should. It would only leave the floor if the upward part of the pull reached the full weight, which at 30° needs a pull of $147/\sin 30^\circ \approx 294$ N, far more than 60 N. Units: N/kg = m/s².
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Apparent weight in a lift

Bathroom scales do not measure your weight. They measure how hard they have to push up on your feet, which is the normal force, and then divide by 9.81 to show kilograms. Normally that push equals your weight. In a lift that speeds up or slows down, it does not (reading 3 and the main source, Apparent weight).

Take up as positive and apply Newton's second law to you, standing on the scales. Two forces act: the normal force $F_N$ up and your weight $m g$ down. So $F_N - m g = m a$, where $a$ is the acceleration of the lift (and of you):

<Formula label="Scale reading in an accelerating lift" tex="F_N = m\,(g + a)" cite="mit" at="Sec. 2.3, Lifts and rockets (T − F_g = m a in each stage, here applied to the person on the scales) and Apparent weight" />

The reading $F_N$ is called your **apparent weight**. Your real weight $m g$ does not change at all.

- **Lift at rest, or moving at a constant speed** ($a = 0$): the scales read your weight. Moving does not matter, only accelerating does.
- **Accelerating upwards** (starting to go up, or stopping on the way down): $a > 0$, and the scales read more. Your legs have to push harder, as if you had grown heavier.
- **Accelerating downwards** (starting to go down, or stopping on the way up): $a < 0$, and the scales read less. For a moment you seem to weigh less.

<WorkedExample title="Scales in a lift">
<Problem>
A student of 55 kg stands on bathroom scales in a lift. What do the scales read, in newtons and in "kilograms", when the lift (a) moves at a steady speed, (b) accelerates upwards at 1.8 m/s², (c) accelerates downwards at 1.8 m/s²?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Steady speed">
$a = 0$, so $F_N = m g = 55 \times 9.81 \approx 540$ N, and the scales show 55 kg.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Accelerating upwards">
$a = +1.8$ m/s²: $F_N = 55 \times (9.81 + 1.8) \approx 639$ N. Divided by 9.81, the scales show about 65.1 kg.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Accelerating downwards">
$a = -1.8$ m/s²: $F_N = 55 \times (9.81 - 1.8) \approx 441$ N, and the scales show about 44.9 kg (dividing the unrounded 440.55 N by 9.81).
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The changes up and down are equal and opposite: $55 \times 1.8 = 99$ N either side of 540 N. In "kilograms" the reading moves by $55 \times 1.8/9.81 \approx 10.1$ kg either way. Nothing happened to the student's mass or weight.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Weightlessness

Follow the graph to its left end. If the lift cable snapped and the lift fell freely (ignore the safety brakes), its acceleration would be $a = -g$, and the formula above gives a reading of $F_N = m\,(g - g) = 0$. The scales fall just as fast as you do, so they cannot push on your feet. You would feel **weightless**, and a pen let go of in front of you would hang in the air beside you, because it falls at the same rate too.

**Weightless does not mean without weight.** Gravity still pulls on you with $m g$; it is the only force on you, and it is what makes you fall. What has gone is the normal force, and with it the feeling of weight (reading 3, Weight and mass). The MIT notes point out the everyday version: at the top of a jump you feel weightless for a moment, though nobody would claim gravity has switched off (reading 2, Example 8.2).

Astronauts in the International Space Station are in exactly this state. At the station's height the Earth's gravity is still about 90 % of its strength at the surface (NASA, "What Is Microgravity?"). An astronaut of 70 kg still has a weight of about $0.9 \times 70 \times 9.81 \approx 620$ N up there. The station and everyone in it are falling towards the Earth all the time; they also move sideways so fast that, as they fall, the curved surface of the Earth falls away beneath them. They keep falling around the Earth, in orbit, and because they all fall together, nothing pushes on anything. Lesson 2.9 shows what sideways speed this needs.

## Tension in a string

A rope, string, cable or chain can only **pull**, and it pulls along its own length. The size of that pull is the **tension** $T$ in the string (reading 1, Tension; reading 2, Sec. 8.5.1; lesson 2.1). A string pulls on **both** objects tied to its ends, each time towards the string. Lesson 2.4 explains why: the string pulls the object at one end, so the object pulls back on the string, and the same holds at the other end.

In this course a string is usually **light**: its mass is so small compared with the objects it pulls that it can be ignored. Then the tension is the same all along the string. The reason is Newton's second law applied to the string itself: with almost no mass, any difference between the pulls at its two ends would give it an enormous acceleration. So the two pulls must be equal (reading 2, Sec. 8.5.1, Eq. (8.5.1)). A light string simply passes the pull on from one end to the other (reading 2, Eq. (8.5.3)).

A lamp of 2.5 kg hanging at rest from a light cord shows the simplest case. Two forces act on the lamp: its weight $2.5 \times 9.81 \approx 24.5$ N down and the tension up. It is at rest, so $T = 24.5$ N. The cord pulls just as hard on the hook in the ceiling.

**Pulleys.** A pulley is a wheel that turns the direction of a string. If the pulley is light and turns without friction, the string has the **same tension on both sides** (reading 2, Example 8.9): the pulley only changes the direction of the pull, not its size. To hold up a sack of 20 kg with a rope over such a pulley, you pull down on the free end with the sack's weight, $20 \times 9.81 \approx 196$ N. The pulley itself is pulled down by both stretches of rope, so its support must hold $2 \times 196 \approx 392$ N.

**Two strings at an angle.** A string that pulls at a slant has to be tighter than one that pulls straight up, because only its upward part holds the load. A bird feeder of 3.0 kg hangs from the middle of a washing line, and each half of the line makes 20° with the horizontal. The weight is $3.0 \times 9.81 \approx 29.4$ N. The horizontal parts of the two pulls cancel, since the two halves are mirror images, so both halves have the same tension $T$. Vertically, each half pulls up with $T \sin 20^\circ$, and together they hold the weight: $2\,T \sin 20^\circ = 29.4$ N, so $T \approx 43.0$ N. Each half pulls harder than the whole weight of the feeder. Check: $2 \times 43.0 \times \sin 20^\circ \approx 29.4$ N. Had the line hung steeply, at 70° to the horizontal, each half would only need about 15.7 N; pulling a line perfectly straight with a load on it is impossible, because the tension would have to be infinite.

Careful: the tension equals the weight of the hanging object **only if that object does not accelerate**. A rope that speeds a bucket upwards pulls harder than the bucket's weight (lesson 2.3, "Hauling up a bucket"); a rope that lets it speed up downwards pulls less.

## Two masses joined by a string

The tension really earns its place in problems with two objects joined by a string. The method is the one of lesson 2.3 for objects that move together, with the same acceleration and the same tension for both (main source, Worked example 10, two crates joined by a rope).

1. Draw a free-body diagram for **each** object separately.
2. Use the fact that a taut string does not stretch: both objects move with the **same size of acceleration** $a$.
3. Use the fact that a light string over a light, frictionless pulley has the **same tension** $T$ at both ends.
4. For each object, choose as positive the direction in which it moves, and write $F_{\text{net}} = m a$.
5. Add the equations: the tension drops out and gives $a$. Then put $a$ back into one equation to find $T$.

<WorkedExample title="Two masses over a pulley">
<Problem>
A light string runs over a light, frictionless pulley. A mass $m_1 = 3.0$ kg hangs from one end and a mass $m_2 = 2.0$ kg from the other. They are let go from rest. Find the acceleration, the tension, and the speed after 1.5 s.
</Problem>
<Step title="Which way, and the free-body diagrams">
The heavier mass $m_1$ goes down and $m_2$ goes up. On $m_1$: its weight $m_1 g$ down and the tension $T$ up. On $m_2$: its weight $m_2 g$ down and the same $T$ up.
</Step>
<Step title="Second law for each mass">
For $m_1$, with down positive: $m_1 g - T = m_1 a$. For $m_2$, with up positive: $T - m_2 g = m_2 a$.
</Step>
<Step title="Add the two equations">
The tension cancels: $(m_1 - m_2)\,g = (m_1 + m_2)\,a$, so

<Formula label="Acceleration of two masses over a pulley" tex="a = \frac{(m_1 - m_2)\,g}{m_1 + m_2}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 8.6, Example 8.9, Eqs. (8.6.52) to (8.6.54) (the second law m g − T = m a for each hanging block, with the same tension on both sides of a light, frictionless pulley); the two-mass result is derived on this page" />

Here $a = (3.0 - 2.0) \times 9.81/(3.0 + 2.0) \approx 1.96$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Tension and speed">
From the equation for $m_2$: $T = m_2(g + a) = 2.0 \times (9.81 + 1.96) \approx 23.5$ N. The acceleration is constant, so after 1.5 s the speed is $v_f = 1.96 \times 1.5 \approx 2.94$ m/s (lesson 1.6).
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The other equation gives the same tension: $3.0 \times (9.81 - 1.96) \approx 23.5$ N. The tension lies between the two weights, 19.6 N and 29.4 N, as it must: it is more than enough to lift $m_2$ but not enough to hold up $m_1$. Two extreme cases fit the formula too: equal masses give $a = 0$, and a vanishing $m_2$ gives $a = g$, free fall.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The net force that drives the pair is only the **difference** of the two weights, while it has to accelerate the **total** mass. That is why the acceleration is much smaller than $g$. Machines that move heavy loads use the same trick: a lift car hangs on one side of its cable and a counterweight on the other, so the motor only has to supply the difference.

The same method works when one object slides along a table and the other hangs over the edge, as in the experiment with a cart and a falling block in the MIT notes (reading 2, Example 8.8).

<WorkedExample title="A cart pulled by a hanging mass">
<Problem>
A cart of 1.5 kg stands on a level table, where it rolls without friction. A light string runs from the cart over a light, frictionless pulley at the edge of the table to a mass of 0.50 kg that hangs down. Find the acceleration, the tension and the normal force of the table on the cart.
</Problem>
<Step title="Second law for each object">
The hanging mass moves down: $0.50\,g - T = 0.50\,a$. The cart moves along the table, and the only horizontal force on it is the tension: $T = 1.5\,a$.
</Step>
<Step title="Acceleration and tension">
Adding gives $0.50\,g = 2.0\,a$, so $a = 0.50 \times 9.81/2.0 \approx 2.45$ m/s². Then $T = 1.5 \times 2.45 \approx 3.68$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Normal force on the cart">
Vertically the cart does not move, and only its weight and the normal force act vertically, so $F_N = 1.5 \times 9.81 \approx 14.7$ N. The string pulls horizontally and does not change it.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
For the hanging mass: $0.50 \times (9.81 - 2.45) \approx 3.68$ N, the same tension. The tension is less than the hanging mass's weight, $0.50 \times 9.81 \approx 4.91$ N, as it must be for that mass to speed up downwards.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Common mistakes

- **"The normal force always equals $m g$."** Only when the surface is level and nothing else pushes or pulls vertically, and nothing accelerates vertically. Pushing down, pulling up, a lift that accelerates or a slope all change it.
- **"Weightless means there is no gravity."** In free fall gravity is the only force acting. What is missing is the normal force.
- **"The tension equals the weight of the hanging mass."** Only if the mass does not accelerate. In the pulley example the tension, 23.5 N, differs from both weights.
- **"The pulley adds the two tensions."** On each object, only the string tied to it pulls. The pulley's support does carry both sides of the string.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Everything in a freely falling lift floats together because everything falls with the same acceleration $g$. That rests on a remarkable fact: the mass that sets how hard gravity pulls on an object is the same as the mass that sets how hard it is to accelerate. The MIT notes call this the principle of equivalence (reading 2, Sec. 8.2.2). So a person in a freely falling lift cannot tell from inside whether there is any gravity at all: everything floats, just as it would far out in space. Einstein took this seriously and made it a principle of its own: in a small enough region, free fall in a gravitational field cannot be told apart from floating with no gravity. It was his starting point for general relativity, the theory of gravity in which space and time themselves can bend and stretch. General relativity is the framework that cosmologists use to describe the expanding universe, and the university course builds up to it step by step, starting from Newton's laws.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Weight:** $F_g = m g$, where $g$ is the gravitational field strength, the weight per kilogram: 9.81 N/kg on the Earth, 1.62 N/kg on the Moon. Weight acts all the time.
- **Normal force** $\vec F_N$: the push of a surface, perpendicular to it. It takes whatever size is needed to stop the object moving into the surface. It equals $m g$ only on a level surface with no other vertical forces and no vertical acceleration; it can never be negative.
- **Apparent weight:** scales read the normal force, $F_N = m(g + a)$ with up positive: more when accelerating upwards, less when accelerating downwards.
- **Weightlessness** is free fall: $a = -g$ and $F_N = 0$. Gravity still acts; astronauts in orbit are falling around the Earth.
- **Tension** $T$: a string pulls along itself on both objects at its ends. A light string has the same tension all along, also over a light, frictionless pulley.
- **Two objects joined by a string:** one free-body diagram and one equation $F_{\text{net}} = m a$ for each, with the same $a$ and the same $T$; add the equations to find $a$, then $T$.
