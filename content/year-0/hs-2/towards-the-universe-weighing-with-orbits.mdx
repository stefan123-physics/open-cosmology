You cannot put the Sun on a scale. Yet astronomers quote its mass to five figures, and they do the same for Jupiter, for stars and even for whole galaxies. This last lesson of Block 2 shows how: everything that orbits is a scale. Lesson 2.9 showed that an object on a circle needs an inward force $mv^2/r$; lesson 2.11 showed that gravity between two masses is $GMm/r^2$. Setting the two equal turns an orbit into a measurement of mass. Used on the Milky Way and on other galaxies, the same method gives one of the biggest surprises in modern astronomy.

## An orbit is a scale

Take a small body of mass $m$ (a planet, a moon, a star) moving on a circle of radius $r$ around a much heavier body of mass $M$. Newton's law of gravitation (lesson 2.11) gives the pull between them:

<Formula label="Newton's law of universal gravitation" tex="F = \frac{GMm}{r^2}" cite="mit" at="Newton's law of universal gravitation" />

Here $G$ is the gravitational constant. This lesson uses $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}$ N m²/kg², the value the Siyavula page gives (CODATA, kept by NIST, lists $6.674\,30 \times 10^{-11}$). Siyavula writes the same law with m₁ and m₂ for the two masses and d for their distance; here $M$ is the central body, $m$ the orbiting one and $r$ the radius of the orbit.

That pull is the only force on the small body, and it points to the centre. So it **is** the centripetal force that keeps the body on its circle (lesson 2.9). Newton's second law then says:

<Formula label="Gravity supplies the centripetal force" tex="\frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{GMm}{r^2}" cite="reading2" at="Velocity structure of galaxies, eq. (22), p. 10" />

The mass $m$ of the orbiting body is on both sides and cancels. Multiply both sides by $r/G$ and the mass of the central body is left on its own:

<Formula label="Mass from orbital speed and radius" tex="M = \frac{v^2 r}{G}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (22), p. 10, solved for M" />

Often the period $T$ is easier to measure than the speed. One trip round the circle is a distance $2\pi r$ in a time $T$, so $v = 2\pi r/T$ (lesson 1.10). Putting this in gives Kepler's third law of lesson 2.12, here turned round to give the mass:

<Formula label="Kepler's third law for a circular orbit" tex="T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{GM} \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad M = \frac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G\,T^2}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 9.2.2, Eq. (9.2.7), p. 9-6 (solved for the central mass)" />

Two things make this a good scale. You only need a distance and a time, and those astronomers can measure. And the orbiting body's mass does not matter: a pebble and a planet on the same orbit move at the same speed. What you weigh is the central body, never the one that orbits it.

## Weighing the Sun

<WorkedExample title="The Earth's orbit weighs the Sun">
<Problem>
The Earth circles the Sun at a distance of $1.50 \times 10^{11}$ m and takes one year ($3.156 \times 10^{7}$ s) for one orbit. Treat the orbit as a circle and find the Sun's mass.
</Problem>
<Step title="Choose the formula">
We know $r$ and $T$, so use $M = 4\pi^2 r^3/(G T^2)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Put in the numbers">
$r^3 = (1.50 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m})^3 = 3.375 \times 10^{33}$ m³ and $T^2 = (3.156 \times 10^{7}\ \text{s})^2 = 9.960 \times 10^{14}$ s². Then

$M = \dfrac{4\pi^2 \times 3.375 \times 10^{33}}{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 9.960 \times 10^{14}} = 2.01 \times 10^{30}$ kg.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
The NASA *Sun Fact Sheet* lists $1.9884 \times 10^{30}$ kg. Our rough circle, with rounded inputs, lands within 1%: the Sun is about 330 000 times heavier than the Earth.
</Step>
<Step title="Check by the other route">
The Earth's speed is $v = 2\pi r/T = 2\pi \times 1.50 \times 10^{11} / 3.156 \times 10^{7} = 2.99 \times 10^{4}$ m/s, close to the 29.78 km/s of the NASA *Earth Fact Sheet*. Then $M = v^2 r/G = (2.99 \times 10^{4})^2 \times 1.50 \times 10^{11} / 6.67 \times 10^{-11}$ gives $2.0 \times 10^{30}$ kg again. Units: m³ / (N m²/kg² × s²) = m³ kg² / (kg m s⁻² m² s²) = kg.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Weighing Jupiter

The same trick works for any planet that has a moon. Jupiter has dozens, and the orbits of the four large ones are measured very precisely.

<WorkedExample title="Ganymede weighs Jupiter">
<Problem>
Ganymede, the largest moon of Jupiter, orbits at $1.07 \times 10^{9}$ m from Jupiter's centre and takes 7.15 days per orbit (NASA *Jovian Satellite Fact Sheet*, rounded). Find Jupiter's mass, and compare it with the Sun's.
</Problem>
<Step title="Period in seconds">
$T = 7.15 \times 24 \times 3600\ \text{s} = 6.18 \times 10^{5}$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="The mass">
$M = \dfrac{4\pi^2 r^3}{G T^2} = \dfrac{4\pi^2 \times (1.07 \times 10^{9})^3}{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times (6.18 \times 10^{5})^2} = 1.90 \times 10^{27}$ kg.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
The NASA *Jupiter Fact Sheet* lists $1898.13 \times 10^{24}$ kg, the same to three figures. Jupiter, the heaviest planet, is still about 1050 times lighter than the Sun.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Ganymede's speed is $v = \sqrt{GM/r} = \sqrt{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.90 \times 10^{27} / 1.07 \times 10^{9}} \approx 1.09 \times 10^{4}$ m/s, about 11 km/s: slower than the Earth round the Sun, as it should be for a much lighter central body at a much smaller distance.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

You can try this yourself in the simulation below. Change the mass of the star or planet and watch how the orbit and the speed change; a heavier centre needs a faster orbit at the same distance.

## Weighing the Milky Way

The Sun itself is on an orbit. Lesson 1.10 met it: the Sun, with all its planets, circles the centre of our galaxy, the Milky Way, at an average speed of about 720 000 km/h and takes about 230 million years per trip (NASA Science, *Sun: Facts*). That orbit is a scale too, this time for the galaxy.

<WorkedExample title="The Sun's orbit weighs the Galaxy">
<Problem>
Use the Sun's galactic speed, 720 000 km/h, and period, 230 million years (one year is $3.156 \times 10^{7}$ s), to find the radius of the Sun's orbit and the mass that keeps it there.
</Problem>
<Step title="Speed in SI">
$720\,000\ \text{km/h} = 720\,000\,000\ \text{m} / 3600\ \text{s} = 2.0 \times 10^{5}$ m/s, or 200 km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Radius of the orbit">
From $v = 2\pi r/T$: $r = \dfrac{vT}{2\pi} = \dfrac{2.0 \times 10^{5} \times 230 \times 10^{6} \times 3.156 \times 10^{7}}{2\pi} = 2.3 \times 10^{20}$ m. In light-years (lesson 1.11) that is $2.3 \times 10^{20} / 9.46 \times 10^{15} \approx 24\,000$ light-years. This is only what NASA's rounded speed and period give, not a measured distance; careful measurements put the Sun a little farther from the centre.
</Step>
<Step title="The mass inside">
$M = \dfrac{v^2 r}{G} = \dfrac{(2.0 \times 10^{5})^2 \times 2.3 \times 10^{20}}{6.67 \times 10^{-11}} = 1.4 \times 10^{41}$ kg. Divided by the Sun's $1.99 \times 10^{30}$ kg, that is about $7 \times 10^{10}$ Sun masses: roughly $10^{11}$, a hundred billion Suns.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units as before give kg. Size: the centripetal acceleration $v^2/r$ is only about $1.7 \times 10^{-10}$ m/s², the same tiny value as in lesson 1.10, so a huge mass is needed even for this gentle pull at such a large distance.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Take this result as rough, for two reasons. First, NASA's figures are rounded averages, and the orbit is treated as a perfect circle, so the answer gives the size (the power of ten) rather than an exact value. Second, and more important, this is **only the mass inside the Sun's orbit**. Unlike the Sun at the centre of the solar system, the Galaxy's mass is spread out, in a disc of stars and gas. For a round (spherical) spread of mass, Newton also showed (we state the result here without proof) that the pulls of the matter outside an orbit cancel: a thin round shell of matter exerts no net force on anything inside it, because the nearer side has less mass but is closer. So the orbit measures exactly the mass inside it; for a flat disc this is only roughly true. Either way, the Sun's orbit says nothing about the Galaxy beyond it. To weigh the outer Galaxy, we need orbits farther out.

## Rotation curves: the speeds far out

Astronomers measure the speeds of stars and gas clouds at many distances from the centre of a galaxy, using the Doppler effect (Block 5 explains it: light from something moving towards us is slightly bluer, from something moving away slightly redder). A graph of orbital speed against distance from the centre is called a **rotation curve**.

What should it look like? In a spiral galaxy most of the light comes from the bright central part. Suppose most of the **mass** is there too. Then, beyond that bright part, a farther orbit encloses hardly any extra mass: $M$ stays about the same, and

<Formula label="Speed around a central mass" tex="v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}, \qquad v \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (22), p. 10 (v_c ∝ r^(−1/2) for a central mass)" />

Lesson 2.12 found this rule for the planets round the Sun: the closest planet, Mercury, moves fastest, and the farthest, Neptune, slowest. Four times farther out, half the speed. The speed should fall away like the planets' speeds do.

That is not what is seen. For spiral galaxies, the measured speeds rise near the centre and then stay **roughly flat**, about as fast at the edge of the visible disc as farther in, and they stay flat even beyond it, where only thin gas is left to measure.

Solid: a schematic flat curve, v = 200 km/s, the typical shape measured for many spiral galaxies, not the Milky Way's own measured curve. The gap between the two grows with distance."

A flat curve means the mass keeps growing. If $v$ stays the same, then $M = v^2 r/G$ is proportional to $r$: twice as far out, twice as much mass inside the orbit. But out there the light has almost run out. Something is pulling that we cannot see.

<WorkedExample title="How much is missing?">
<Problem>
In a spiral galaxy, a star 20 000 light-years from the centre orbits at 220 km/s. A gas cloud 80 000 light-years out, beyond almost all the stars, also orbits at 220 km/s. (a) How fast should the cloud move if all the mass were inside the star's orbit? (b) How does the mass inside the cloud's orbit compare with the mass inside the star's orbit? (c) Find the mass inside the cloud's orbit.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The prediction">
With the same mass inside, $v \propto 1/\sqrt{r}$. Four times the radius gives half the speed: $220/2 = 110$ km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The comparison">
Both move at 220 km/s, so $M = v^2 r/G$ is simply proportional to $r$: four times the radius, four times the mass. Three quarters of the mass inside the cloud's orbit lies between the two orbits, where there is little light.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The mass">
$r = 80\,000 \times 9.46 \times 10^{15}\ \text{m} = 7.57 \times 10^{20}$ m and $v = 2.2 \times 10^{5}$ m/s, so $M = \dfrac{(2.2 \times 10^{5})^2 \times 7.57 \times 10^{20}}{6.67 \times 10^{-11}} = 5.5 \times 10^{41}$ kg, about $2.8 \times 10^{11}$ Sun masses.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Another route for (b): the mass inside the star's orbit is $(2.2 \times 10^{5})^2 \times 1.89 \times 10^{20} / 6.67 \times 10^{-11} = 1.4 \times 10^{41}$ kg, and four times that is $5.5 \times 10^{41}$ kg, as in (c).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Dark matter

Most astronomers take flat rotation curves as one of the main pieces of evidence for **dark matter**: matter that pulls by gravity like ordinary matter but gives off no light that our telescopes detect. The NASA Science page *Dark Matter* tells the history in short. In 1933 the Swiss-born astronomer Fritz Zwicky found that the galaxies in the Coma Cluster, a large group of galaxies, move too fast for the pull of their visible matter to hold them together, and proposed an unseen kind of matter. In the 1970s the American astronomer Vera Rubin measured how fast stars on the outer edges of spiral galaxies move, and found them too fast for the matter that can be seen. Her measurements, together with those of others, did much to convince astronomers that the problem was real.

Some care is needed with what is known and what is not:

- **Known:** in galaxy after galaxy, the speeds far out are higher than the visible stars and gas can explain with Newton's law of gravitation.
- **Not known:** what dark matter is. Ordinary gas that does not shine is ruled out, because it would block light of particular colours and show up that way; many particle candidates have been proposed, but none has been detected (the MIT 8.902 notes, pp. 10–11, and the NASA page list some).
- **Another idea:** instead of extra matter, gravity itself might behave differently at the tiny accelerations found at the edges of galaxies. This idea, modified Newtonian dynamics, is a minority view but is still tested (the 8.902 notes list it among the possibilities).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
The method of this lesson, an orbit as a scale, is how dark matter was found in galaxies and how it is still measured. The graduate year of this site takes it much further. *Fundamentals of Dark Matter* starts by weighing the Sun with all eight planets, then works through rotation curves, galaxy clusters and the bending of light by mass, and asks what dark matter could be made of, including the alternative of modified gravity. MIT 8.902 shows why the cosmic web of galaxies could not have grown in the time available without dark matter. In the cosmology of the whole universe, dark matter turns out to outweigh ordinary matter several times over.
</Callout>

## Summary

- In a circular orbit, gravity supplies the centripetal force: $mv^2/r = GMm/r^2$. The orbiting mass cancels.
- So an orbit weighs the central body: $M = v^2 r/G$, or with the period, $M = 4\pi^2 r^3/(GT^2)$ (Kepler's third law turned round).
- The Earth's orbit ($r = 1.50 \times 10^{11}$ m, $T = 1$ year) gives the Sun's mass, about $2.0 \times 10^{30}$ kg; Ganymede's orbit gives Jupiter's, about $1.9 \times 10^{27}$ kg.
- The Sun's orbit around the Milky Way (about 200 km/s, 230 million years) gives about $10^{41}$ kg, roughly $10^{11}$ Sun masses, **inside** the Sun's orbit only.
- A **rotation curve** plots orbital speed against distance from a galaxy's centre. If the mass sat in the centre, speeds would fall as $1/\sqrt{r}$; measured curves stay roughly flat, so the mass inside keeps growing in proportion to $r$ where there is little light.
- This is one of the main pieces of evidence for **dark matter** (Zwicky 1933, Rubin 1970s). What it is remains unknown, and modified gravity is still tested as an alternative.
