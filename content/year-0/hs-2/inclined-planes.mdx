A ramp into a lorry, a playground slide, a ski slope and a road up a hill are all **inclined planes**: flat surfaces that make an angle with the horizontal. Lessons 2.5 and 2.6 gave you every force you need here: the weight, the normal force, friction and tension. What is new is the direction. On a slope the forces no longer line up with "horizontal" and "vertical", and the trick of this lesson is to pick axes that make the problem simple again.

## Choosing axes along the slope

Call the angle between the slope and the horizontal $\theta$ (the Greek letter theta). A block resting on the slope feels three forces:

- its **weight** $\vec F_g$, straight down, of size $m g$;
- the **normal force** $\vec F_N$ of the slope, perpendicular to the surface (lesson 2.5), so tilted away from the vertical by the angle $\theta$;
- **friction** $\vec F_f$ along the surface (lesson 2.6), if the surface is not smooth.

With ordinary horizontal and vertical axes, two of these three forces point at awkward angles. But the block can only move **along** the slope: the surface stops it moving into the slope, and nothing lifts it off. So choose the axes to fit the slope (reading 1, Resolving forces into components):

- the $x$-axis **along the slope**, with up the slope as positive;
- the $y$-axis **perpendicular to the slope**, with out of the slope as positive.

Any coordinate system is allowed, and lining the $x$-axis up with the slope is the simplest choice (reading 1, Resolving forces into components). Newton's second law can then be written for each axis separately (reading 2, Sec. 8.4.1). With these axes the normal force lies along $y$ and friction along $x$. Only the weight has to be split into components (lesson 0.8). The acceleration is along $x$ only, so along $y$ the forces must cancel.

## Splitting the weight

The weight points straight down. The geometry of the slope gives the angle between the weight and the $y$-axis: it is the slope angle $\theta$ itself. (Turn a level surface through $\theta$, and the line perpendicular to it turns through $\theta$ as well, away from the vertical.) The weight is then the long side of a right-angled triangle whose other two sides are its components (lesson 0.7):

<Formula label="Components of the weight on a slope" tex="F_{g\parallel} = m\,g\,\sin\theta, \qquad F_{g\perp} = m\,g\,\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="Resolving forces into components (written there as F_gx = F_g sin θ and F_gy = F_g cos θ)" />

Here $F_{g\parallel}$ is the part of the weight **parallel to the slope**, pointing down the slope, and $F_{g\perp}$ is the part **perpendicular to the slope**, pointing into it.

Two quick checks make the formulas easy to remember.

- **A level surface**, $\theta = 0$: $\sin 0 = 0$ and $\cos 0 = 1$. Nothing pulls the block along the surface, and all of the weight presses into it, as on a floor.
- **A vertical wall**, $\theta = 90^\circ$: $\sin 90^\circ = 1$ and $\cos 90^\circ = 0$. All of the weight points along the "slope", and nothing presses the block against it: it simply falls.

Mixing up sine and cosine is the most common mistake on slopes. If you are unsure, imagine the slope almost flat: the part along the slope must then be tiny, so it is the sine.

## The normal force on a slope

Along the $y$-axis the block does not move: it neither sinks into the slope nor lifts off. So the forces along $y$ cancel. If only the weight and the normal force act in that direction, the normal force balances the perpendicular part of the weight (main source, Object on an inclined plane and Worked example 15):

<Formula label="Normal force on a slope" tex="F_N = m\,g\,\cos\theta" cite="mit" at="Object on an inclined plane; Worked example 15 (N − F_g cos θ = 0, so N = F_g cos θ)" />

This is the normal force that "adjusts to what is needed" of lesson 2.5 at work again. On a slope it is **less than the weight**, and the steeper the slope, the smaller it gets. Like lesson 2.5, this holds only when nothing else pushes or pulls perpendicular to the slope; a rope pulled at an angle to the slope, or a hand pressing the block down, changes it.

## Sliding down a smooth slope

Start with a slope so smooth that friction can be ignored, such as a trolley with good wheels or an ice ramp. Along the $x$-axis only one force acts: the part of the weight along the slope, $m g \sin\theta$, pointing down the slope. Newton's second law along the slope, $F_{\text{net}} = m a$ (lesson 2.3), with down the slope as positive, gives $m g\sin\theta = m a$, so

<Formula label="Acceleration down a frictionless slope" tex="a = g\,\sin\theta" cite="mit" at="Object on an inclined plane (the component of the force of gravity parallel to the slope accelerates the object down the slope); the formula itself is derived on this page from F_net = m a" />

The mass cancels, just as in free fall (lesson 1.7): a heavy trolley and a light one roll down the same smooth ramp together. The acceleration runs from 0 on a level surface to $g$ for a vertical drop. A slope "dilutes" gravity: the same pull of the Earth gives a smaller acceleration that is easier to watch and to time.

<WorkedExample title="A trolley on a smooth ramp">
<Problem>
A toy trolley of 2.0 kg is let go from rest on a ramp that makes 20° with the horizontal. It has light wheels and rolls without friction, so we can treat it as sliding on a smooth slope. Find the two components of its weight, the normal force, the acceleration, and the speed after it has rolled 1.5 m down the ramp.
</Problem>
<Step title="Weight and its components">
The weight is $F_g = m g = 2.0 \times 9.81 = 19.62$ N. Along the slope: $19.62 \sin 20^\circ \approx 6.71$ N, down the slope. Into the slope: $19.62 \cos 20^\circ \approx 18.4$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Normal force">
Perpendicular to the ramp the trolley does not move, so the normal force balances the part into the slope: $F_N \approx 18.4$ N, perpendicular to the ramp, outwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Acceleration">
The only force along the ramp is the 6.71 N, so $a = 6.71/2.0 \approx 3.36$ m/s². The formula gives the same: $a = g\sin\theta = 9.81 \times \sin 20^\circ \approx 3.36$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Speed after 1.5 m">
The acceleration is constant, so $v_f^{2} = v_i^{2} + 2 a\,\Delta x$ (lesson 1.6) with $v_i = 0$: $v_f = \sqrt{2 \times 3.355 \times 1.5} \approx 3.17$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The two components and the weight form a right-angled triangle: $\sqrt{6.71^{2} + 18.44^{2}} \approx 19.6$ N, the weight again. The acceleration is between 0 and $g$, as it must be. Units: N/kg = m/s², and $\sqrt{\text{m/s}^{2} \times \text{m}} = $ m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Friction: when does an object stay put?

Put a book on a plank and slowly lift one end. At first the book stays where it is. Then, at a certain angle, it suddenly starts to slide (main source, Object on an inclined plane). Lesson 2.6 explains why.

While the book is at rest, the forces along the slope cancel. The part of the weight down the slope, $m g\sin\theta$, is balanced by **static friction up the slope** (main source, Worked example 14; reading 2, Sec. 8.3.2):

<Formula label="Static friction on an object at rest on a slope" tex="F_f = m\,g\,\sin\theta" cite="mit" at="Worked example 14 (F_f − F_g sin θ = 0, so F_f = F_g sin θ)" />

Friction points up the slope because without it the book would slide down. As the slope gets steeper, $m g\sin\theta$ grows, so the static friction that is needed grows too. At the same time the normal force $m g\cos\theta$ shrinks, and with it the **maximum** static friction, $\mu_s F_N = \mu_s\,m g\cos\theta$ (lesson 2.6). The book stays put as long as the friction needed is not more than the maximum:

$m g\sin\theta \le \mu_s\,m g\cos\theta$

Divide both sides by $m g\cos\theta$, and use $\sin\theta/\cos\theta = \tan\theta$:

<Formula label="Condition for an object to stay at rest on a slope" tex="\tan\theta \le \mu_s" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 8.3.2, Eq. (8.3.7) and the just-slipping condition, applied to a block on an inclined plane (combined here with F_f = F_g sin θ and N = F_g cos θ of the main source, Worked examples 14 and 15)" />

Two things stand out. The **mass has cancelled**: a full and an empty box of the same material start to slide at the same angle. And the condition gives an easy way to **measure** $\mu_s$. Tilt the surface slowly until the object just starts to slide, and read off that angle, the **critical angle** $\theta_c$. At that moment static friction has reached its maximum, so the condition holds with an equals sign:

<Formula label="Measuring the coefficient of static friction" tex="\mu_s = \tan\theta_c" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 8.3.2 (just-slipping condition: static friction at its maximum, (f_s)max = μ_s N, Eq. (8.3.7)); worked out here for a slope" />

You can try it at home with a book, a coin and a protractor, or with a phone app that measures angles.

<WorkedExample title="Measuring the grip of a plank">
<Problem>
A wooden block lies on a plank. One end of the plank is slowly lifted, and the block starts to slide when the plank makes 27° with the horizontal. (a) Find $\mu_s$ between block and plank. (b) The block has a mass of 0.40 kg. The plank is lowered to 15°. How large is the friction on the block now, and how large could it be at most?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The coefficient">
At the critical angle, $\mu_s = \tan\theta_c = \tan 27^\circ \approx 0.51$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The friction at 15°">
At 15° we have $\tan 15^\circ \approx 0.27$, less than 0.51, so the block stays put. Static friction then balances the part of the weight along the slope: $F_f = m g\sin\theta = 0.40 \times 9.81 \times \sin 15^\circ \approx 1.02$ N, up the slope.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The maximum">
The normal force is $F_N = 0.40 \times 9.81 \times \cos 15^\circ \approx 3.79$ N, so static friction could reach $\mu_s F_N \approx 0.510 \times 3.79 \approx 1.93$ N. Only about half of that is used.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The ratio of the friction used to the maximum is $\tan 15^\circ/\tan 27^\circ \approx 0.53$, which matches $1.02/1.93$. The answer to (a) needs no mass at all, as the mass cancelled; $\mu_s$ has no unit.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Sliding down with friction

Once the object slides, **kinetic friction** takes over (lesson 2.6). It has the fixed size $\mu_k F_N = \mu_k\,m g\cos\theta$ and points **up the slope**, against the sliding. Along the slope, with down as positive, the net force is $m g\sin\theta - \mu_k\,m g\cos\theta$. Divide by $m$:

<Formula label="Acceleration of an object sliding down a slope with friction" tex="a = g\,(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="Object on an inclined plane (the parallel component of gravity against friction) with the kinetic friction f_k = μ_k N of 2.2 Force (reading 1, Friction forces) and N = F_g cos θ (Worked example 15); the formula itself is derived on this page" />

The mass cancels once more. Three cases follow from the sign of the bracket:

- $\tan\theta > \mu_k$: the bracket is positive and the object speeds up down the slope, more slowly than without friction;
- $\tan\theta = \mu_k$: the acceleration is zero, so a sliding object goes on at a **constant speed** (lesson 2.2);
- $\tan\theta < \mu_k$: the bracket is negative, so an object that was given a push down the slope **slows down** and stops.

Since $\mu_k$ is usually smaller than $\mu_s$, there is a range of angles where an object at rest stays at rest, but the same object, once nudged, keeps sliding down: the reason a tap on a stuck box on a ramp can set it off.

<WorkedExample title="A playground slide">
<Problem>
A child slides from rest down a straight slide that makes 35° with the horizontal. The coefficient of kinetic friction between her clothes and the slide is 0.30, and the slide is 3.2 m long. Find her acceleration, her speed at the bottom, and how long the ride takes.
</Problem>
<Step title="Acceleration">
$a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta) = 9.81 \times (\sin 35^\circ - 0.30 \cos 35^\circ) = 9.81 \times (0.574 - 0.246) \approx 3.22$ m/s², down the slide.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed at the bottom">
From rest over $\Delta x = 3.2$ m: $v_f = \sqrt{2 a\,\Delta x} = \sqrt{2 \times 3.216 \times 3.2} \approx 4.54$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Time">
With constant acceleration from rest, $v_f = a\,\Delta t$, so $\Delta t = 4.54/3.216 \approx 1.41$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Without friction she would accelerate at $g\sin 35^\circ \approx 5.63$ m/s², so friction takes away a good part, as expected. The answer does not depend on her mass, so a parent on the same slide in the same clothes would reach the bottom just as fast (if the friction coefficient is the same). Other route for the distance: average speed times time, $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 4.54 \times 1.41 \approx 3.2$ m.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Pushing or pulling up a slope

To get a load **up** a slope you have to supply a force $\vec F_A$ yourself, for example with a rope parallel to the slope. Now two forces point down the slope: the part of the weight, $m g\sin\theta$, and kinetic friction, $\mu_k\,m g\cos\theta$, because friction always opposes the sliding, and the load slides up. Along the slope, with up as positive:

<Formula label="Pulling a load up a slope with a force parallel to it" tex="F_A - m\,g\,\sin\theta - \mu_k\,m\,g\,\cos\theta = m\,a" cite="mit" at="Worked example 15 (a force T up the incline balances F_g sin θ) with Newton's second law and kinetic friction against the motion (2.2 Force, Friction forces)" />

**The direction of friction depends on the motion.** When a load slides down, friction points up the slope; when it is pulled up, friction points down the slope. For static friction it depends on which way the load would slide without friction. Always decide it again for each problem.

<WorkedExample title="Hauling a crate up a ramp">
<Problem>
A worker pulls a crate of 45 kg up a loading ramp that makes 15° with the horizontal. Her rope is parallel to the ramp and pulls with 250 N. Between crate and ramp $\mu_k = 0.25$. The crate is already moving up the ramp. Find the normal force, the friction and the acceleration. What pull would keep the crate moving at a constant speed?
</Problem>
<Step title="Weight and its components">
$F_g = 45 \times 9.81 \approx 441$ N. Along the ramp: $441.45 \sin 15^\circ \approx 114$ N, down the ramp. Into the ramp: $441.45 \cos 15^\circ \approx 426$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Normal force and friction">
The rope pulls along the ramp, so it does not change the forces perpendicular to it: $F_N \approx 426$ N. The crate slides up, so kinetic friction points down the ramp: $F_f = \mu_k F_N = 0.25 \times 426.4 \approx 107$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Acceleration">
$F_{\text{net}} = 250 - 114.3 - 106.6 \approx 29.1$ N up the ramp, so $a = 29.1/45 \approx 0.65$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Constant speed">
For zero acceleration the pull must exactly balance the two forces down the ramp: $F_A = 114.3 + 106.6 \approx 221$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Lifting the crate straight up at a constant speed would take its full weight, 441 N. The ramp halves the force needed, even with friction; the price is that the crate has to travel a longer way to reach the same height. Units: N/kg = m/s².
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A block on a slope tied to a hanging mass

Lesson 2.5 solved problems with two objects joined by a string over a pulley. The same five steps work when one of the objects rests on a slope. The only new thing is that the slope gives that object the components you met above. The main source solves problems with two objects joined by a rope or a tow bar (main source, Worked examples 10 and 13), without a pulley; the same method carries over.

<WorkedExample title="A block pulled up a slope by a falling mass">
<Problem>
A block of $m_1 = 2.0$ kg lies on a slope of 30°. A light string runs from the block up the slope, over a light, frictionless pulley at the top, to a mass of $m_2 = 1.5$ kg that hangs freely. Between block and slope $\mu_s = 0.25$ and $\mu_k = 0.20$. The masses are let go from rest. Does the block move? If so, find the acceleration and the tension in the string.
</Problem>
<Step title="Which way, if at all?">
The hanging mass pulls the string with its weight, $m_2 g = 1.5 \times 9.81 \approx 14.7$ N, trying to drag the block up the slope. Down the slope pulls the part of the block's weight $m_1 g\sin 30^\circ = 2.0 \times 9.81 \times 0.5 \approx 9.81$ N. Static friction can add at most $\mu_s\,m_1 g\cos 30^\circ = 0.25 \times 2.0 \times 9.81 \times \cos 30^\circ \approx 4.25$ N to hold the block. Together that is about 14.06 N, less than 14.7 N, so the block starts to slide up the slope and the hanging mass goes down.
</Step>
<Step title="Free-body diagrams and the second law">
For the block, with up the slope positive: the tension $T$ up the slope, and the part of its weight and kinetic friction, $F_f = \mu_k\,m_1 g\cos 30^\circ \approx 3.40$ N, both down the slope. So $T - m_1 g\sin\theta - F_f = m_1 a$. For the hanging mass, with down positive: $m_2 g - T = m_2 a$. The string does not stretch, so both have the same size of acceleration $a$, and the same tension $T$ at both ends.
</Step>
<Step title="Add the equations">
The tension drops out:

<Formula label="Acceleration of a block on a slope tied to a hanging mass" tex="a = \frac{m_2\,g - m_1\,g\,\sin\theta - \mu_k\,m_1\,g\,\cos\theta}{m_1 + m_2}" cite="mit" at="Worked example 10 (two objects joined by a rope: the second law for each, the same acceleration and tension, solved simultaneously), with the forces along the slope from Object on an inclined plane; the method is the source's, the formula is derived on this page" />

Here $a = (14.72 - 9.81 - 3.40)/3.5 \approx 0.430$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Tension">
From the hanging mass: $T = m_2(g - a) = 1.5 \times (9.81 - 0.430) \approx 14.1$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The block's equation gives the same tension: $2.0 \times 0.430 + 9.81 + 3.40 \approx 14.1$ N. ## Common mistakes

- **"The normal force on a slope equals $m g$."** It equals $m g\cos\theta$, which is less, and even that only when nothing else pushes or pulls perpendicular to the slope.
- **Swapping sine and cosine.** The part along the slope is $m g\sin\theta$. Test with a nearly flat slope: then it must be almost zero.
- **Friction always points up the slope.** Only when the object slides, or tends to slide, down. When it is pulled up, friction points down the slope.
- **Using $F_f = \mu_s F_N$ for an object at rest.** That is only the maximum. At rest, static friction is whatever balances the other forces along the slope, here $m g\sin\theta$.
- **Counting the weight and its components together.** Use either the weight or its two components in a sum of forces, never both.

## Summary

- On a slope of angle $\theta$, choose the $x$-axis **along** the slope and the $y$-axis **perpendicular** to it. Then only the weight needs splitting.
- The weight splits into $m g\sin\theta$ **down the slope** and $m g\cos\theta$ **into the slope**.
- If nothing else acts perpendicular to the slope, the **normal force** is $F_N = m g\cos\theta$.
- On a **smooth slope** the acceleration is $a = g\sin\theta$, whatever the mass.
- An object **stays at rest** on a slope as long as $\tan\theta \le \mu_s$; static friction is then $m g\sin\theta$, up the slope. The angle at which it starts to slide measures $\mu_s = \tan\theta_c$.
- An object **sliding down** with friction has $a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)$: it speeds up, keeps a constant speed or slows down as $\tan\theta$ is more than, equal to or less than $\mu_k$.
- **Pulled up** a slope, both $m g\sin\theta$ and kinetic friction point down the slope: $F_A - m g\sin\theta - \mu_k m g\cos\theta = m a$.
- With a **string over a pulley**, write $F_{\text{net}} = m a$ for each object along its own direction of motion, with the same $a$ and $T$, and add the equations.
