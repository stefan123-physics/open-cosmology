Try to open a heavy door by pushing right next to the hinges. It hardly moves, however hard you push. Push at the handle, on the far side, and the same door swings open easily. So the effect of a force depends not only on how large it is and which way it points, but also on **where** it acts. Lessons 2.1 to 2.9 treated every object as a single point, where only the size and direction of a force mattered. Real objects have a size, and a force on them can make them turn. This lesson is about that turning effect, and about what it takes for an object such as a seesaw, a plank or a bookcase to stay still.

## The turning effect of a force

An object that can turn about a fixed point or axis, such as a door on its hinges or a seesaw on its stand, turns about its **pivot**. Draw the force as an arrow and extend it in both directions into a long straight line: this is the force's **line of action** (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 17.3.1). The perpendicular distance from the pivot to that line is the **lever arm** $d$ of the force (MIT calls it the moment arm).

The turning effect of the force is the force times its lever arm. It is called the **torque**, written $\tau$ (the Greek letter tau); in many school books it is called the **moment** of the force:

<Formula label="Torque (moment) of a force" tex="\tau = F\,d" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 17.3.1, Eq. (17.2.30), there written τ = r⊥F with r⊥ the moment arm" />

Its unit is the newton metre, **N·m** (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 17.3.1). A force of 1 N with a lever arm of 1 m gives a torque of 1 N·m. In Block 3 you will meet energy, whose unit, the joule, is also a newton times a metre; a torque is never written in joules, to keep the two apart.

The formula explains the door. The handle is far from the hinges, so a push there has a long lever arm and a large torque. A push next to the hinges has a short lever arm and needs a much larger force for the same torque.

<WorkedExample title="Opening a door">
<Problem>
A door handle is 0.80 m from the hinges. You push on it with 15 N, at right angles to the door. (a) What torque do you exert about the hinges? (b) How hard must you push at 0.20 m from the hinges to get the same torque?
</Problem>
<Step title="Lever arm">
The force is perpendicular to the door, so its line of action is at right angles to the door and the lever arm is the full distance from the hinges: $d = 0.80$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Torque">
$\tau = F d = 15 \times 0.80 = 12$ N·m.
</Step>
<Step title="Pushing close to the hinges">
The same torque with $d = 0.20$ m needs $F = \tau/d = 12/0.20 = 60$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: N times m gives N·m, and N·m divided by m gives N. Size: the lever arm is four times shorter, so the force must be four times larger, and $4 \times 15 = 60$ N.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A force at an angle

Often a force does not act at right angles to the object. Let $r$ be the distance from the pivot to the point where the force acts, and $\theta$ the angle between the force and the line from the pivot to that point. Then the lever arm is $d = r \sin\theta$ (lesson 0.7), and the torque is:

<Formula label="Torque of a force at an angle" tex="\tau = r\,F\sin\theta" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 17.3.1, Eq. (17.2.29); the moment arm r⊥ = r sin θ in Eq. (17.2.30)" />

There is a second way to read the same formula. Split the force into two components (lesson 0.8): one along the line from the pivot, $F\cos\theta$, and one perpendicular to it, $F\sin\theta$. The component along the line points straight at (or away from) the pivot and cannot turn anything. Only the perpendicular component turns, with the full distance $r$ as its lever arm: $\tau = r \times F\sin\theta$ (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 17.3.1, and Sec. 18.3, Eq. 18.3.9).

Three cases are worth remembering:

- at $\theta = 90^\circ$ the force is perpendicular, $\sin\theta = 1$ and the torque is largest, $\tau = rF$;
- at $\theta = 0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$ the force points straight towards or away from the pivot, $\sin\theta = 0$ and there is **no torque at all** (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 17.3.1);
- in between, the torque is smaller than $rF$.

  curves={[
    { expr: "0.80*15*sin(x*pi/180)", label: "τ = rF sin θ" }

/>

For example, if you push on the handle with the same 15 N but at $30^\circ$ to the door, the torque is $0.80 \times 15 \times \sin 30^\circ = 6.0$ N·m: only half as much, because only half of your push is perpendicular to the door.

## Clockwise and anticlockwise

A torque can turn an object one way or the other: **clockwise** (the way the hands of a clock move) or **anticlockwise** (the other way; American books say counterclockwise). To add torques you give them signs. The usual choice, also in the MIT notes, is to count anticlockwise torques as positive and clockwise torques as negative (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 17.3.2 and Sec. 18.2). The **resultant torque** is then the sum of all the torques with their signs.

Which way is clockwise depends on which side you look from: a door seen from above turns clockwise, seen from below anticlockwise (Sec. 17.3.2). So always choose one viewpoint for a whole problem and keep it.

## The principle of moments

Put a long, uniform plank on a pivot under its middle: a seesaw. A child on the left gives an anticlockwise torque, a child on the right a clockwise torque. The seesaw balances when the two torques are equally large, so that they cancel. With weights $F_1$ and $F_2$ at distances $d_1$ and $d_2$ from the pivot:

<Formula label="Lever law" tex="F_1\,d_1 = F_2\,d_2" cite="mit" at="Sec. 18.2, Eq. (18.2.3), there written d₁N₁ = d₂N₂" />

With more forces the same idea becomes the **principle of moments**: an object is in balance when the sum of the clockwise moments about a point equals the sum of the anticlockwise moments about that point. With signs, this says that the resultant torque is zero (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 18.1, Eq. 18.1.2). For an object in balance it holds about **every** point you choose, not only the pivot (Appendix 18A of the notes).

Notice what is missing from the lever law: the weight of the plank itself, and the upward push of the pivot. Both act at the pivot, so their lever arms are zero and they have no torque about it (Sec. 18.2).

<WorkedExample title="Where to sit on a seesaw">
<Problem>
Mila (24 kg) sits 1.5 m from the pivot of a uniform seesaw that is pivoted at its middle. Where must her father (80 kg) sit to balance her? The seesaw has a mass of 30 kg. How hard does the pivot push up?
</Problem>
<Step title="Torques about the pivot">
Mila's weight $24 g$ has lever arm 1.5 m; her father's weight $80 g$ has the unknown lever arm $d$. The seesaw's own weight acts at its middle, at the pivot, so it has no torque about the pivot.
</Step>
<Step title="Principle of moments">
$24 g \times 1.5 = 80 g \times d$. The $g$ cancels, so $d = 24 \times 1.5/80 = 0.45$ m. Her father sits 0.45 m from the pivot, on the other side.
</Step>
<Step title="The pivot force">
The seesaw does not move up or down, so the forces balance too: the pivot pushes up with the total weight, $(24 + 80 + 30) \times 9.81 = 134 \times 9.81 \approx 1.31 \times 10^{3}$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Both torques: $24 \times 9.81 \times 1.5 \approx 353$ N·m anticlockwise and $80 \times 9.81 \times 0.45 \approx 353$ N·m clockwise. They cancel. The father is $80/24 \approx 3.3$ times heavier and sits $1.5/0.45 \approx 3.3$ times closer: heavier means closer to the pivot.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Equilibrium of an extended object

Lesson 2.2 said that an object is in equilibrium when the resultant force on it is zero. For an object you can treat as a point, that is enough. An **extended object** (one whose size matters, such as a plank, a ladder or a bridge) needs more. Hold a steering wheel and push up on its left side and down, just as hard, on its right side. The two forces are equal and opposite, so the resultant force is zero and the wheel does not move off; but both forces turn it the same way, and it spins. So an extended object is in **equilibrium** (at rest, and staying at rest) only when two conditions hold together (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 18.1):

<Formula label="Conditions for equilibrium of an extended object" tex="\sum F = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum \tau = 0" cite="mit" at="Sec. 18.1, Eqs. (18.1.1) and (18.1.2)" />

The first says the object does not start to move off; the second, that it does not start to turn. The torques may be taken about any point you like (Appendix 18A). A smart choice saves work: take torques about the point where an **unknown** force acts. That force then has no lever arm and drops out of the torque equation (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 18.4, Example 18.2, the alternative solution).

## The centre of gravity

Gravity pulls on every bit of a plank, not on one point. Yet for torques the plank behaves as if its whole weight acted at a single point, its **centre of gravity** (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 17.4.2). Where the pull of gravity is the same everywhere, as it is across any object on Earth, this point is also the **centre of mass**, the average position of all the object's mass (Sec. 17.4.2, Eq. 17.3.27). For a uniform plank, rod or block, it lies in the middle.

You can find a centre of gravity by experiment. Balance a ruler on one finger: the finger ends up under the middle, because only there does the weight have no lever arm about it. Hang a flat card from a pin and let it settle: the centre of gravity comes to rest straight below the pin. Hang it from a second hole, and the two vertical lines cross at the centre of gravity. For some shapes, like a ring or a boomerang, that point is not even on the object.

<WorkedExample title="A painter on a plank">
<Problem>
A uniform plank of 20 kg and 4.0 m long rests on two supports, A at its left end and B at its right end. A painter of 70 kg stands 1.0 m from A. How hard does each support push up?
</Problem>
<Step title="The forces">
Four forces act on the plank, all vertical: its own weight $20 \times 9.81 = 196.2$ N at its centre of gravity, 2.0 m from A; the push of the painter's feet, equal to his weight $70 \times 9.81 = 686.7$ N (lesson 2.5), 1.0 m from A; and the upward forces $F_A$ and $F_B$ of the supports.
</Step>
<Step title="Torques about A">
Take torques about A, so that the unknown $F_A$ drops out. Clockwise: $196.2 \times 2.0 + 686.7 \times 1.0 = 1079$ N·m. Anticlockwise: $F_B \times 4.0$. Balance gives $F_B = 1079/4.0 \approx 270$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Forces">
The resultant force is zero: $F_A + F_B = 196.2 + 686.7 = 882.9$ N, so $F_A = 882.9 - 269.8 \approx 613$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Take torques about B instead: $F_A \times 4.0 = 196.2 \times 2.0 + 686.7 \times 3.0$, which gives $F_A \approx 613$ N again. The painter stands nearer to A, so A carries more of the load, as it should.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Stability: why things tip over

Stand a box on the floor and tilt it a little on one bottom edge, then let go. It falls back. The box now rests on that edge only, which acts as the pivot, and its weight acts at the centre of gravity. As long as the centre of gravity is on the inner side of the edge, the weight's torque about the edge turns the box back onto its base.

Tilt it further and the centre of gravity moves over the edge. Once the vertical line through the centre of gravity passes **outside the base**, the weight's torque turns the other way and the box falls over. While the box is tilted on its edge, the floor pushes on it only at that edge, so the edge is the pivot. The MIT notes find the same thing for a person standing on a slope with one foot higher up than the other: as the feet are brought closer together, the push of the ground on the upper (uphill) foot drops to zero just as the person starts to topple, turning about the lower foot, the way the box turns about its edge (MIT 8.01SC notes, Sec. 18.4, Example 18.3). In the limit their centre of gravity is straight above the lower foot, the same rule as for the box.

The box tips when its centre of gravity is straight above the edge. If the centre of gravity is a height $h$ above the floor and a distance $b$ (half the width, for a uniform box) from the edge, that happens at the tilt angle $\theta$ with $\tan\theta = b/h$ (lesson 0.7).

<WorkedExample title="A bookcase and a crate">
<Problem>
A uniform bookcase is 0.60 m wide and 1.8 m tall. A uniform crate is also 0.60 m wide, but only 0.60 m tall. How far can each be tilted before it tips over?
</Problem>
<Step title="Bookcase">
Its centre of gravity is in the middle: 0.30 m from the edge and 0.90 m up. So $\tan\theta = 0.30/0.90 = 0.333$, and $\theta \approx 18^\circ$.
</Step>
<Step title="Crate">
Its centre of gravity is 0.30 m from the edge and 0.30 m up: $\tan\theta = 1.0$, so $\theta = 45^\circ$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The tall bookcase tips at a much smaller tilt than the low crate, as experience says. Both angles lie between 0° and 90°, and a ratio of 1 gives 45°, as it must.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

So an object is **stable** when its centre of gravity is low and its base is wide. Racing cars are built low and wide. Bookcases are fixed to the wall because a child climbing on a front shelf puts weight in front of the bookcase and raises the centre of gravity of the whole, so the vertical line through it can move towards, and past, the front edge. You widen your base too: stand with your feet apart on a moving train.

## Levers: why a long spanner helps

A **lever** is a rigid bar that turns about a pivot (often called the fulcrum). You push with an effort force at one place, and the lever exerts a force on a load at another. In balance the principle of moments holds: effort × its lever arm = load × its lever arm. With a long lever arm for your effort and a short one for the load, a small effort holds or moves a large load. A crowbar, a wheelbarrow, scissors, a bottle opener and pliers all use this.

A spanner is a lever whose pivot is the bolt. To loosen a stuck bolt you need a certain torque, and $F = \tau/d$: the longer the spanner, the smaller the force.

<WorkedExample title="A stuck wheel nut">
<Problem>
A wheel nut of a car needs a torque of 120 N·m to come loose. How hard must you push, at right angles, at the end of (a) a spanner 0.20 m long and (b) a wheel brace 0.50 m long?
</Problem>
<Step title="Short spanner">
$F = \tau/d = 120/0.20 = 600$ N: about the weight of a 61 kg person, hard to manage with your arms.
</Step>
<Step title="Long wheel brace">
$F = 120/0.50 = 240$ N: much easier.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The brace is 2.5 times longer and needs 2.5 times less force: $600/2.5 = 240$ N. Units: N·m divided by m gives N.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Nothing comes for free. For the same turn of the nut, the end of the long brace moves along a larger circle, so your hand moves further. Block 3 shows that the work you do (force times distance) stays the same. Your forearm is a lever used the other way round: the muscle attaches close to the elbow and the load sits in your hand far away, so the muscle must pull much harder than the load, but your hand moves far and fast.

## Try it

Put bricks on both sides of the plank and move them until it balances. Check the principle of moments: the mass times the distance on one side should equal the mass times the distance on the other. Then try the game, where you predict which way the plank will tip.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
The pull of the Sun on a planet points straight at the Sun. Take the Sun as the pivot: the line of that force passes right through it, so its lever arm and its torque about the Sun are zero. Nothing twists the planet's motion around the Sun, and that is why a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times (Kepler's second law, lesson 2.12). The university course 8.01 turns this into the conservation of angular momentum, which also explains why a slowly turning cloud of gas spins faster as gravity pulls it together, and why such clouds flatten into spinning discs: the discs in which planets form and, astronomers think, the discs of spiral galaxies.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The **torque** (moment) of a force is its turning effect about a pivot: $\tau = F d$, with $d$ the **lever arm**, the perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force. Unit: N·m.
- For a force at an angle $\theta$ to the line from the pivot, $\tau = r F \sin\theta$: largest at $90^\circ$, zero when the force points through the pivot.
- Torques turn **clockwise** or **anticlockwise**; give them opposite signs and add them to get the resultant torque.
- **Principle of moments:** in balance, the clockwise moments about any point add up to the anticlockwise moments. For two forces: $F_1 d_1 = F_2 d_2$.
- An extended object is in **equilibrium** when the resultant force is zero **and** the resultant torque is zero. Take torques about an unknown force to remove it.
- The weight acts at the **centre of gravity**, the middle of a uniform object (the centre of mass, where gravity is uniform).
- An object **tips over** when the vertical line through its centre of gravity passes outside its base: a low centre of gravity and a wide base make it stable.
- A **lever** trades force for distance: a long spanner gives the same torque with a smaller force.
