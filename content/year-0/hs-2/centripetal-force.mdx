Lesson 1.10 showed that an object going round a circle at a steady speed $v$ is still accelerating: its velocity keeps turning, and the acceleration $a = v^2/r$ points to the centre. It ended with a promise: something must pull or push the object inwards, with a force of size $m v^2/r$. Now that lessons 2.1 to 2.7 have introduced forces and Newton's laws, this lesson keeps that promise. It shows where the inward force comes from in everyday cases, how fast a car can take a bend, why a roller coaster does not fall out of a loop, and why there is no outward force throwing you to the side in a turning car.

## Newton's second law on a circle

Newton's second law (lesson 2.3) says that the net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration, $\vec F_{\text{net}} = m\,\vec a$, and that the two point the same way. In uniform circular motion the acceleration points to the centre and has size $v^2/r = \omega^2 r$ (lesson 1.10, with $\omega$ the angular speed in rad/s). So the net force must point **to the centre** too, with size

<Formula label="Newton's second law for uniform circular motion" tex="F_{\text{net}} = \frac{m v^2}{r} = m\,\omega^2 r" cite="mit" at="Sec. 9.1, Eq. (9.1.2) (the inward, radial part of the second law, with dθ/dt = ω), and v = rω from Ch. 6, Eq. (6.2.15)" />

The MIT notes split the second law into two parts: one along the radius, which is this equation, and one along the circle (Sec. 9.1, Eqs. (9.1.3) and (9.1.4)). In uniform circular motion the speed does not change, so the part of the net force **along** the circle is zero: all of the net force points inwards. If some force also acted along the circle, the object would speed up or slow down as it went round.

A net force pointing to the centre of a circle is called a **centripetal force**, from the Latin for "centre-seeking", like the centripetal acceleration it causes. The MIT notes tell how Newton reached this idea around 1666 by thinking about the Moon: by the first law the Moon would carry on in a straight line, so some force must keep turning it towards the Earth (reading 1, Sec. 6.1). As the lecturer in the MIT video puts it, the force side of the equation is the "why" of the motion and the acceleration side is the "how" (reading 2): a circle is only possible if the forces supply exactly $m v^2/r$.

## A job, not a new kind of force

The word "centripetal force" misleads many people into thinking it is one more force on the list of lesson 2.1, next to weight, friction and tension. It is not. It is a **job** that ordinary forces must do. Ask, for each circle: which real force, or which combination of real forces, points to the centre?

- A ball whirled on a string on a smooth table: the **tension** in the string.
- A car on a flat bend: **static friction** of the road on the tyres, sideways.
- A sock against the wall of a spinning washing-machine drum (lesson 1.10): the **normal force** of the wall.
- The Moon or a satellite going round the Earth: **gravity**.
- A roller coaster upside down at the top of a loop: **gravity and the normal force** of the track together.

When you draw a force diagram (lesson 2.1) for an object going round, draw only these real forces. Never add an extra arrow labelled "centripetal force": that would count the same force twice. Instead, add up the real forces and set their net inward part equal to $m v^2/r$.

<WorkedExample title="A ball on a string">
<Problem>
A 0.25 kg ball is tied to a light string and whirled in a horizontal circle of radius 0.50 m on a smooth, flat table, at a steady 3.0 m/s. How large is the tension in the string? The string breaks when the tension reaches 20 N. What is the highest speed it can stand?
</Problem>
<Step title="Forces">
Vertically the weight and the normal force of the table balance (the ball does not lift off or sink). Horizontally only the tension $T$ acts, along the string towards the centre. So the tension does the whole job: $T = m v^2/r$.
</Step>
<Step title="Tension">
$T = \dfrac{0.25 \times 3.0^2}{0.50} = 4.5$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Highest speed">
At the breaking point $20 = m v^2/r$, so $v = \sqrt{20 \times 0.50/0.25} = \sqrt{40} \approx 6.3$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
By the other route: $\omega = v/r = 3.0/0.50 = 6.0$ rad/s and $m\omega^2 r = 0.25 \times 6.0^2 \times 0.50 = 4.5$ N, the same. Units: kg·(m/s)²/m = kg·m/s² = N. Doubling the speed from 3.0 m/s would need four times the tension, 18 N, just below the limit, which agrees with a top speed a little over 6 m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The table matters here. Whirl the ball overhead without a table and the string cannot stay horizontal: it slopes down, so that part of the tension can hold the ball up against its weight. The idea is the same, but the sums need the components of lesson 2.3.

## A car on a flat bend

On a flat road the weight of a car points down and the normal force of the road points up. Neither points sideways, so neither can turn the car. The only sideways force is **friction** between the tyres and the road. A rolling tyre does not slide sideways over the road (lesson 2.6), so this is **static** friction, pointing towards the centre of the bend.

Static friction adjusts itself to what is needed, but only up to its maximum $\mu_s F_N$ (lesson 2.6), and on a flat road $F_N = m g$. The car can go round as long as the friction needed, $m v^2/r$, is not more than that maximum. At the top speed the two are equal:

<Formula label="Friction at its limit on a flat bend" tex="\mu_s\,m\,g = \frac{m v_{\text{max}}^2}{r}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 9.3, Example 9.6, Eq. (9.3.20) (a coin on a turntable, written there with μ for μ_s and with ω_max; v = ωr)" />

The mass cancels, and solving for the speed gives

<Formula label="Top speed on a flat bend" tex="v_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\mu_s\,g\,r}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 9.3, Example 9.6, Eq. (9.3.21) (ω_max = √(μg/R) with μ for μ_s, multiplied by the radius)" />

The MIT notes find the same limit for a coin on a turning record player, which slides off once the turntable spins too fast (Example 9.6). Above $v_{\text{max}}$ friction cannot supply enough inward force, the tyres slide, and the car carries on in a straighter line than the bend: it skids outwards, off the road.

<WorkedExample title="How fast can a car take a bend?">
<Problem>
A flat bend has a radius of 50 m. Find the top speed without skidding on a dry road ($\mu_s = 0.9$) and on a wet road ($\mu_s = 0.5$), using the rough values of lesson 2.6.
</Problem>
<Step title="Dry road">
$v_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{0.9 \times 9.81 \times 50} = \sqrt{441} \approx 21.0$ m/s, about 76 km/h.
</Step>
<Step title="Wet road">
$v_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{0.5 \times 9.81 \times 50} = \sqrt{245} \approx 15.7$ m/s, about 56 km/h.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
For a car of 1000 kg at 21.0 m/s, the inward force needed is $1000 \times 21.0^2/50 \approx 8.8 \times 10^{3}$ N, and the most friction can give is $0.9 \times 1000 \times 9.81 \approx 8.8 \times 10^{3}$ N: equal, as they must be at the limit. Units: $\sqrt{\text{m/s}^2 \times \text{m}} = $ m/s. A lighter or heavier car gets the same answer, because the mass cancelled.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Banked bends.** Racing tracks and many motorway exits are tilted, or **banked**, towards the inside of the bend. Then the normal force of the road is tilted too, and its sideways part pushes the car towards the centre, so less friction is needed. The MIT video works out the case of a bend with no friction at all, banked at an angle $\theta$: there is exactly one speed at which a car goes round without sliding up or down the slope (reading 3, p. 3, there written with the angle φ),

<Formula label="Speed on a frictionless banked bend" tex="v = \sqrt{r\,g\,\tan\theta}" cite="reading3" at="transcript p. 3 (written there with the bank angle φ)" />

For a bend of radius 50 m banked at 15°, this is $\sqrt{50 \times 9.81 \times \tan 15^\circ} \approx 11.5$ m/s, about 41 km/h. Lesson 2.8 shows how to split forces on a tilted surface into parts along and across it; the university lesson does the banked bend in full.

## Over the top of a loop

A roller-coaster car runs round a vertical loop, upside down at the top. Why does it not fall out? At the top, the centre of the loop is **below** the car. Two forces act on the car there, and both point down, towards the centre: its weight $m g$ and the push of the track $F_N$, a normal force (the track is above the car and pushes it down). Together they must supply the inward force:

<Formula label="At the top of a vertical circle" tex="F_N + m\,g = \frac{m v^2}{r}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 9.1, Eq. (9.1.2), applied at the top of a vertical circle (the notes do not work out a loop; the setting is the page's own, like the whirled bucket of segment 11.3, seg113)" />

A track can push but not pull, so $F_N$ cannot be less than zero. The slower the car, the smaller $F_N$, until at the **least speed** the track does not push at all and gravity alone does the job: $m g = m v^2/r$, so

<Formula label="Least speed at the top of a vertical circle" tex="v = \sqrt{g\,r}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 9.1, Eq. (9.1.2) with F_N = 0 at the top (the result is the page's own)" />

Any slower, and gravity pulls the car down more than the circle bends: it would leave the track and fall inwards across the loop (real roller coasters have extra wheels under the rails that stop this). A bucket of water swung over your head in a vertical circle works in the same way. Swing it fast enough and the water stays in, even upside down; MIT shows this with ping-pong balls in a whirled bucket (segment 11.3). The water is not "held up": it is falling, but the bucket is pulled down with at least the acceleration $g$ that the water would have on its own, so the water stays inside.

<WorkedExample title="A roller-coaster loop">
<Problem>
The top of a roller-coaster loop is part of a circle of radius 8.0 m. Find the least speed at the top. A 60 kg rider passes the top at 12 m/s. How hard does the seat push on her there?
</Problem>
<Step title="Least speed">
$v = \sqrt{g r} = \sqrt{9.81 \times 8.0} \approx 8.9$ m/s, about 32 km/h.
</Step>
<Step title="Push of the seat at 12 m/s">
At the top the seat is above her head and pushes her down, like the track on the car. The inward force needed is $m v^2/r = 60 \times 12^2/8.0 = 1080$ N, and her weight is $m g = 60 \times 9.81 \approx 589$ N. The seat supplies the rest: $F_N = 1080 - 589 \approx 491$ N, downwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
In symbols, $F_N = m(v^2/r - g) = 60 \times (18 - 9.81) \approx 491$ N, the same. It is positive because 12 m/s is more than the least speed of 8.9 m/s. It is less than her weight, so upside down at the top she feels lighter than usual, like the apparent weight in a lift of lesson 2.5.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## At the bottom of a dip and over a hump

At the **bottom** of a vertical circle the centre is **above** the object: the lowest point of a swing, the dip in a road, the bottom of a loop. Now the weight points down, away from the centre, and the push of the seat or road points up, towards it. The push must beat the weight by the inward force:

<Formula label="At the bottom of a vertical circle" tex="F_N - m\,g = \frac{m v^2}{r}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 9.1, Eq. (9.1.2), applied at the bottom of a vertical circle (the notes do not work out this case; the setting is the page's own)" />

So $F_N = m g + m v^2/r$: the seat pushes harder than your weight, and you feel **heavier** at the bottom of a swing or a dip.

A car driving over the crest of a **hump** in the road is at the top of a circle, but now the road is below the car and can only push up, away from the centre. The weight must beat that push:

<Formula label="Over the crest of a hump" tex="m\,g - F_N = \frac{m v^2}{r}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 9.1, Eq. (9.1.2), applied at the crest of a hump (the setting is the page's own)" />

So $F_N = m g - m v^2/r$: the car presses less on the road and feels lighter. The faster it goes, the smaller $F_N$, until at $v^2 = g r$ the road no longer pushes at all. Any faster, and gravity cannot bend the car's path as sharply as the road curves: the car leaves the road.

<WorkedExample title="A swing and a hump">
<Problem>
A 30 kg child on a swing with 2.5 m long chains passes the lowest point at 4.0 m/s. How hard does the seat push on her? A car drives over a hump whose crest is part of a circle of radius 40 m. Above what speed does it leave the road?
</Problem>
<Step title="Swing">
Needed inward: $m v^2/r = 30 \times 4.0^2/2.5 = 192$ N; her weight: $30 \times 9.81 \approx 294$ N. So $F_N = 294 + 192 \approx 486$ N, upwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Hump">
The car leaves the road when $v^2 > g r$, so above $v = \sqrt{9.81 \times 40} \approx 19.8$ m/s, about 71 km/h, whatever its mass.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The seat pushes $486/294 \approx 1.65$ times her weight: more than her weight, as it must be at the bottom. For the hump, a car of 1200 kg at 15 m/s, below the limit, gets $F_N = 1200 \times (9.81 - 15^2/40) \approx 5.0 \times 10^{3}$ N: positive, so it stays on the road, but pressing less than half as hard as on a flat road.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Satellites: when gravity does the job

Throw a stone sideways and it falls to the ground in a curve (lesson 1.8). Throw it faster and it lands further away. Now imagine throwing it so fast that, while it falls, the round Earth curves away beneath it just as fast: it keeps falling but never lands. It is in **orbit**. The only force on it is gravity, so gravity alone supplies the inward force. The MIT notes treat the Moon in this way: its orbit is nearly a circle, so by the second law some force towards the Earth must cause its inward acceleration, and that force is gravity (Sec. 9.2, Eq. (9.2.1) and the text after it).

For an orbit just above the ground, where gravity on a mass $m$ is still $m g$, the condition is the same as for the least speed at the top of the loop: $m g = m v^2/r$, so $v = \sqrt{g r}$, now with $r$ the radius of the Earth.

<WorkedExample title="An orbit skimming the Earth">
<Problem>
Imagine a satellite going round the Earth just above the ground, with no air and no mountains in the way. The Earth's mean radius is 6371 km (NASA, Earth Fact Sheet). How fast must it go, and how long does one orbit take?
</Problem>
<Step title="Speed">
$r = 6.371 \times 10^{6}$ m, so $v = \sqrt{g r} = \sqrt{9.81 \times 6.371 \times 10^{6}} \approx 7.91 \times 10^{3}$ m/s, nearly 8 km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Time for one orbit">
One orbit is the circumference $2\pi r$, so it takes $2\pi \times 6.371 \times 10^{6}/7.91 \times 10^{3} \approx 5.06 \times 10^{3}$ s, about 84 minutes.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The inward acceleration is $v^2/r = (7.91 \times 10^{3})^2/6.371 \times 10^{6} \approx 9.8$ m/s², equal to $g$, as it must be when gravity is the only force. The mass of the satellite cancelled: a pebble and a space station on the same orbit need the same speed.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Real satellites fly a few hundred kilometres up, above most of the air. There gravity is a little weaker than $g$ at the ground, so they move a little slower and take a little longer. Lesson 2.11 gives the law that says how gravity weakens with distance, and with it the speed of any circular orbit. The astronauts on board feel weightless (lesson 2.5) not because gravity is gone, but because they and their spacecraft fall round the Earth together.

## There is no centrifugal force

Sit in a car that turns sharply to the left and you seem to be thrown to the right, against the door. It feels like a force pushing you **outwards**, away from the centre of the bend. People often call it the **centrifugal force** ("centre-fleeing"). Seen from the road, there is no such force on you.

Look at it from the road, which is an **inertial frame** (lesson 2.2: a point of view that is not accelerating, in which the first law holds). Before the bend you and the car move together at the same velocity. Then the road pushes the car's tyres sideways and the car turns. Nothing has pushed **you** sideways yet, so by the first law you carry on in a straight line, while the car turns under you. The door, moving left with the car, comes towards you, and then it pushes you inwards, to the left, round the bend. The force you feel from the door points **inwards**, not outwards. The Siyavula textbook uses the same example of passengers in a turning car to illustrate the first law (reading 4, Worked example 8).

The same goes for a stone on a string. If the string breaks, the stone does not fly straight outwards, away from the centre: it carries on along the tangent, in the direction it was moving (lesson 1.10). There is a real outward force in the picture, but it does not act on the stone. By Newton's third law (lesson 2.4) the stone pulls on the string as hard as the string pulls on the stone, so the string pulls outwards on your **hand**. You feel that pull, and it is easy to mistake it for a force on the stone.

The feeling of being pressed outwards comes from describing the motion from **inside** the turning car, which is not an inertial frame: it accelerates towards the centre of the bend. Physicists sometimes add a "centrifugal force" by hand to make Newton's laws work in such a turning frame, but it is a bookkeeping correction, not a push or pull by any object. In this course we always describe motion from an inertial frame, and then there is no centrifugal force.

## Beyond gravity and friction

The rule "the net inward force equals $m v^2/r$" works for any force that can do the job. In Block 6 you will meet a charged particle, such as an electron, moving through a magnetic field. The magnetic force on it always acts at right angles to its velocity, so it turns the particle without changing its speed, and the particle goes round in a circle. Setting the magnetic force equal to $m v^2/r$ then gives the radius of that circle, exactly as in this lesson.

## Try it

In the simulation, set the Sun and the Earth going. Switch on the gravity force and velocity arrows and watch: the force on the Earth always points to the Sun, at right angles to its velocity, and the Earth goes round in a (nearly) circular orbit. Then change the Earth's starting speed. A little slower and gravity bends its path more than the circle; a little faster and the path opens out. Switch off gravity and the planet flies off in a straight line, along the tangent.

The rule of this lesson is one of astronomy's best tools for weighing things. If gravity alone keeps a planet or a star on a circle, then the gravity pulling it inwards must equal $m v^2/r$. Measure the speed and the radius of the orbit, and you know how strong the pull is, and with the law of gravitation of lesson 2.11, how much mass is pulling. Lesson 2.13 uses this to weigh the Sun from the Earth's orbit and the Milky Way from the Sun's orbit. Astronomers have done the same for stars and gas far out in many galaxies, and found more pull than the visible stars and gas can provide (NASA's *Dark Matter* page tells how Vera Rubin found this for stars on the outer edges of spiral galaxies in the 1970s): one of the main reasons most astronomers think that galaxies contain **dark matter**, matter that does not shine. The graduate year of this site follows that evidence in detail.

## Summary

- In uniform circular motion Newton's second law requires a net force **towards the centre** of size $F_{\text{net}} = m v^2/r = m\omega^2 r$, and no net force along the circle.
- This **centripetal force** is not a new kind of force but a job done by real forces: tension, friction, a normal force, gravity, or several together. Never draw it as an extra arrow in a force diagram.
- A ball on a string on a smooth table: the tension is $T = m v^2/r$.
- A car on a flat bend is turned by static friction; it skids above $v_{\text{max}} = \sqrt{\mu_s g r}$, whatever its mass. A banked bend lets the normal force help.
- At the top of a vertical loop, $F_N + m g = m v^2/r$; because a track can only push, the speed there must be at least $\sqrt{g r}$.
- At the bottom of a swing or dip, $F_N - m g = m v^2/r$ (you feel heavier); over a hump, $m g - F_N = m v^2/r$, and a car leaves the road when $v^2 > g r$.
- A satellite is in free fall, with gravity alone as the inward force; just above the ground it would need $\sqrt{g r} \approx 7.9$ km/s.
- Seen from an inertial frame there is **no centrifugal force**: objects tend to go straight on, and something pushes them inwards. The outward pull you feel on a string acts on your hand (the third law).
