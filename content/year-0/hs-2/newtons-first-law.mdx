Lesson 2.1 introduced forces: pushes and pulls, measured in newtons, which add up as vectors to a single resultant force. Block 1 described motion without asking what causes it. This lesson starts the link between the two with Newton's first law, which says what happens to an object when the resultant force on it is zero. The answer surprised people for a very long time: nothing happens to its velocity at all.

## Does motion need a force?

Push a book across a table and let go: it stops almost at once. Stop pedalling a bicycle and it slowly rolls to a halt. Everyday experience seems to say that a moving object needs something to keep it moving, and that motion dies away by itself once the push stops. The physics of Aristotle, taught in Europe for many centuries, is commonly summarised in a similar way for "forced" motion, such as a cart that is pushed: it moves only as long as something keeps pushing it. (A stone falling, which Aristotle counted as natural motion, was a separate case.) Galileo studied motion for much of his life, from the unpublished essays *De Motu* (about 1590) to experiments with balls rolling on inclined planes around 1602 to 1604 (dates from the MacTutor biography of Galileo in the references).

Here is an argument in Galileo's spirit, a thought experiment rather than a measurement. Let a ball roll down one smooth ramp and up a second one. It climbs to almost the height it started from. Make the second ramp less steep and the ball rolls further along it to reach that height. Make the second ramp flat, and the ball can never reach its starting height, so it should roll on and on. What stops a real ball is not the lack of a push but friction and air resistance, which are forces. The idea that an object keeps moving by itself is the **principle of inertia**; the MIT course notes (Sec. 7.2) say it was first realised by Galileo.

## Newton's first law

Isaac Newton made the principle the first of his three laws of motion, published in his *Principia* in 1687. In modern words:

**Newton's first law:** an object stays at rest, or keeps moving at constant velocity, unless a resultant force acts on it.

"Constant velocity" means the same speed **and** the same direction (lesson 1.2): motion in a straight line at a steady speed. Newton's own Latin, in the English translation quoted in the MIT notes (Sec. 7.2), speaks of "uniform motion in a right line", that is, in a straight line. With $\vec F_{\text{net}}$ for the resultant force (lesson 2.1) and $\vec v$ for the velocity:

<Formula label="Newton's first law" tex="\vec F_{\text{net}} = \vec 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \vec v \text{ stays constant}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 2.3, Newton's first law (definition box)" />

Rest is simply the special case $\vec v = \vec 0$. The law also works the other way round. If the velocity of an object changes, in size, in direction or both, then it has an acceleration (lesson 1.4) and the resultant force on it cannot be zero. So a force is not what keeps things moving; **a resultant force is what changes their velocity**. How large the change is for a given force is the subject of the second law, in lesson 2.3.

## Inertia and mass

The property that the first law describes, an object's tendency to keep its present state of motion, is called **inertia**. Everything has it: a parked car tends to stay parked, and a moving car tends to keep moving.

Some objects have more inertia than others. An empty wheelbarrow is easy to set moving and easy to stop; the same wheelbarrow full of wet sand is hard to start, hard to stop and hard to steer round a corner. What makes the difference is the amount of matter in it, its **mass**, measured in kilograms. **Mass is the measure of inertia**: the more mass an object has, the harder it is to change its velocity. Newton called mass the "quantity of matter", and the MIT notes call mass measured in this way the **inertial mass**. Lesson 2.3 turns "harder to change" into a number.

Inertia has nothing to do with gravity or with weight. Far out in space, where a loaded wheelbarrow would weigh almost nothing, it would still be just as hard to set moving: its mass, and so its inertia, is the same everywhere.

## The first law in everyday life

**Seatbelts.** In a moving car, the passengers move at the same velocity as the car. In a crash the car is stopped by a large force from whatever it hits, but that force acts on the car, not on the passengers. By the first law an unbelted passenger keeps moving forwards at the old speed until something else, the dashboard or the windscreen, stops them. A seatbelt is there to supply that force sooner and spread it over the strong parts of the body.

<WorkedExample title="Why a passenger hits the dashboard">
<Problem>
A car moving at 15 m/s (54 km/h) hits a wall and is brought to rest in 0.10 s, slowing down steadily. A passenger without a seatbelt is not held by anything during this time. How far does the car move while it stops, how far does the passenger move, and how far forward does the passenger move relative to the car?
</Problem>
<Step title="The car">
The car slows steadily from 15 m/s to 0, so its average velocity is $\tfrac{1}{2}(15 + 0) = 7.5$ m/s (lesson 1.6). In 0.10 s it moves $7.5 \times 0.10 = 0.75$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="The passenger">
No resultant force acts on the passenger, so by the first law the velocity stays 15 m/s. In 0.10 s the passenger moves $15 \times 0.10 = 1.5$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Relative to the car">
The passenger moves $1.5 - 0.75 = 0.75$ m further than the car. Seen from inside the car, the passenger is flung forward by 0.75 m, more than enough to hit the dashboard or windscreen.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The passenger is not "thrown" forward by any force: seen from the road, the passenger just keeps going while the car stops. The forward movement exists only relative to the car, which is slowing down (lesson 1.9). The numbers are sensible too: 0.75 m is about the distance from the front seat to the windscreen.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The same reasoning explains why you lean outwards when a car takes a sharp bend. The car turns because the road pushes sideways on its tyres; your body tends to keep going in a straight line until the seat, the belt or the door pushes you round with the car.

**The tablecloth trick.** A cloth is whipped out from under a table full of plates and glasses, and they stay almost where they were. Friction from the moving cloth does pull on the plates, but if the cloth is pulled fast it acts for only a fraction of a second. Because of their inertia, the plates hardly change their state of rest in so short a time. Pull the cloth slowly and the same friction acts for long enough to drag everything onto the floor.

**A puck on ice.** An ice hockey puck slides a very long way after it is hit, because the ice exerts only a tiny friction force on it. On a rough concrete floor the same puck stops after a short distance. The graph shows both cases.

<WorkedExample title="A puck that coasts">
<Problem>
A puck leaves a hockey stick at 8.0 m/s along very smooth ice. Ignore friction and air resistance. Where is it 2.5 s later, and what is its velocity then?
</Problem>
<Step title="Use the first law">
The downward pull of gravity on the puck is balanced by the upward push of the ice, and we ignore friction and air resistance, so the resultant force is zero. The puck keeps its velocity: 8.0 m/s in the same direction.
</Step>
<Step title="Distance">
At constant velocity the displacement is velocity times time (lesson 1.2): $8.0\ \text{m/s} \times 2.5\ \text{s} = 20$ m along its original direction.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
On the graph, the area under the horizontal line from 0 to 2.5 s is a rectangle of $8.0 \times 2.5 = 20$ m. Real ice has a little friction, so a real puck would be slightly slower and slightly closer; the first law gives the limit with no friction at all.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Equilibrium: forces that balance

The first law speaks of the *resultant* force. An object can have several forces acting on it and still keep a constant velocity, as long as they cancel. This is the rule for balanced forces of lesson 2.1; the first law is what tells us that balanced forces mean an unchanged velocity. An object on which the resultant force is zero is said to be in **equilibrium**:

<Formula label="Equilibrium" tex="\vec F_{\text{net}} = \vec F_1 + \vec F_2 + \vec F_3 + \dots = \vec 0" cite="mit" at="Sec. 2.3, Forces in equilibrium (stated in words)" />

By the first law an object in equilibrium is either at rest or moving at constant velocity, and an object that is at rest or moving at constant velocity is in equilibrium. A book lying on a table and a car cruising at a steady speed along a straight motorway are both in equilibrium: for the car, the forward push of the road on the driving wheels exactly balances air resistance and other friction.

<WorkedExample title="A parachutist at constant speed">
<Problem>
A parachutist and her parachute together have a force of gravity of 780 N pulling them down. With the parachute open she falls straight down at a steady 5.5 m/s. How large is the force of the air on her and the parachute, and in which direction does it act?
</Problem>
<Step title="Is she in equilibrium?">
Her velocity is constant: same speed, same direction. So by the first law the resultant force on her is zero.
</Step>
<Step title="Balance the forces">
Only two forces act: gravity, 780 N down, and the push of the air. For the resultant to be zero the air must push 780 N up.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Suppose the air pushed less, say 700 N. Then the resultant would be $780 - 700 = 80$ N downwards, not zero, and she would speed up. She does not, so 780 N is right. Note that she *moves* downwards with *no* resultant force: motion needs no force, only a change of motion does.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Coasting through space

Far from planets and stars there is no air and almost no friction, so the first law shows itself very clearly. A spacecraft fires its rockets to change its velocity; once the engines are switched off, it coasts on at constant velocity, and the crew must fire an engine again to speed up, slow down or turn.

The spacecraft Voyager 1, launched in 1977, flew past Jupiter and Saturn and in August 2012 became the first spacecraft to cross into interstellar space. On 21 August 2024 it was moving at 17.0 km/s relative to the Sun, with no engine pushing it along (figures from NASA's page "Voyager 1: The Farthest Spacecraft" in the references). It is not exactly a first-law object: the Sun's gravity still pulls it back, very weakly at that distance, and slows it a little. But to a good approximation it simply coasts. At 17.0 km/s it covers about $5.4 \times 10^{8}$ km every year.

## The first law and frames of reference

Lesson 1.9 showed that velocities depend on the frame of reference. So does the first law hold in every frame? Think of a ball lying on the smooth floor of a bus. When the bus brakes, the ball rolls towards the front, although nothing pushes it forwards. Seen from the road, nothing strange happens: the ball keeps its velocity, as the first law says, while the bus slows down beneath it. Seen from inside the braking bus, the ball starts moving with no force acting, and the first law seems to fail.

A frame of reference in which the first law does hold is called an **inertial frame of reference**. For everyday motion the ground is, to a good approximation, an inertial frame. Every frame that moves at **constant velocity** relative to an inertial frame is inertial too, because adding a constant velocity to every velocity (lesson 1.9) does not change whether a velocity is constant. That is why physics in a smoothly moving train is the same as on the platform: this is the principle of relativity of lesson 1.9, and the MIT notes (Ch. 11, Sec. 11.2.1) read the first law as a form of this principle: if no resultant force acts on an object in one inertial frame, none acts on it in the other. A frame that speeds up, slows down or turns, such as the braking bus or a car taking a bend, is not inertial. In it, objects seem to start moving with no force acting. In this course we always describe motion from an inertial frame.

## Summary

- **Newton's first law:** an object stays at rest, or keeps moving at constant velocity, unless a resultant force acts on it.
- A force is not needed to keep something moving; a **resultant force changes velocity**. Things that slow down on their own are slowed by friction or air resistance. Galileo was the first to see this clearly; Newton made it his first law (1687).
- **Inertia** is the tendency to keep the present state of motion. **Mass** is its measure: more mass, more inertia. Inertia does not depend on gravity.
- Seatbelts, the tablecloth trick, a puck on ice and a spacecraft coasting through space all show the first law at work.
- An object with zero resultant force is in **equilibrium**: at rest or at constant velocity, with the forces on it balancing.
- The first law holds in **inertial frames of reference**; every frame moving at constant velocity relative to one is inertial too, while a braking or turning vehicle is not.
