An apple falls from a tree. The Moon goes round the Earth. The Earth goes round the Sun. Newton's great step was to see that these are the same thing: one force, **gravity**, pulling every mass towards every other mass. Lessons 2.1 to 2.5 used the weight $F_g = m g$, with $g$ a fixed 9.81 N/kg near the ground. This lesson gives the law behind it, which works at any distance, from two people in a room to the planets.

## The law of universal gravitation

Newton's law of universal gravitation says that every two masses attract each other. The force acts along the line joining them, it is always attractive, and its size depends on only two things: the masses and the distance between them.

> **Newton's law of gravitation.** Any two point masses pull on each other, each one straight towards the other. How hard they pull grows in step with the two masses multiplied together, and shrinks with the square of how far apart they are.

For masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ at a distance $r$ apart, the size of the force is

<Formula label="Newton's law of gravitation" tex="F = G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}" cite="mit" at="Newton's law of universal gravitation (the law in the definition box, written there with d for the distance)" />

Here $F$ is in newtons, the masses in kilograms and $r$ in metres. $G$ is a fixed number, the **gravitational constant**, which the next section explains.

Some points to notice:

- **A point mass** is an object so small compared with the distance that its size does not matter. Real bodies such as the Earth are not small. Newton showed that a sphere whose mass is spread evenly (or in even shells) pulls on things outside it exactly as if all its mass sat at its centre (MIT notes, Sec. 9.2.1). So for planets, stars and balls, **$r$ is the distance between the centres**, never from the surface.
- **The force is a pair.** The Earth pulls you down with the same size of force as you pull the Earth up: the two forces of Newton's third law (lesson 2.4), both given by the same formula.
- **Inverse square.** The distance appears squared in the bottom of the fraction. Lesson 0.6 called this an **inverse square law**: twice the distance gives a quarter of the force, three times the distance a ninth.
- **Proportional to each mass.** Double one of the masses and the force doubles; double both and it becomes four times as large.

The MIT notes write the same law with a **unit vector** (an arrow of length 1 that only points along the line between the two masses) to show its direction (Sec. 8.2.1, Eq. (8.2.1)). In this course we find the size from the formula and then say the direction in words: towards the other body.

## The gravitational constant G

The constant $G$ turns "proportional to" into an equation. Its measured value (CODATA, kept by NIST) is

<Formula label="The gravitational constant" tex="G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N·m}^2\text{/kg}^2" cite="nistg" at="Numerical value 6.674 30 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻²" />

Many textbooks round it to $6.67 \times 10^{-11}$, which is fine for school problems. Its unit makes the formula come out in newtons: $\text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2 \times \text{kg} \times \text{kg}/\text{m}^2 = \text{N}$.

The power $10^{-11}$ means that $G$ is tiny. Between everyday objects the pull of gravity is far too weak to feel.

<WorkedExample title="Two students on a bench">
<Problem>
Two students with masses of 60 kg and 75 kg sit side by side on a bench, with their centres 0.50 m apart. Treat them as point masses. How large is the gravitational pull between them, and how does it compare with the weight of the lighter student?
</Problem>
<Step title="The force between them">
$F = G\,\dfrac{m_1 m_2}{r^2} = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times \dfrac{60 \times 75}{0.50^2} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{N}.$
</Step>
<Step title="Compare with a weight">
The lighter student's weight is $F_g = m g = 60 \times 9.81 \approx 589$ N. The pull of the other student is about $2.0 \times 10^{-9}$ times that: about two billionths.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: $\text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2 \times \text{kg}^2/\text{m}^2 = \text{N}$. Size: a millionth of a newton is roughly the weight of a single grain of sand, so it makes sense that nobody notices being pulled towards a neighbour. (Treating people as points is rough, but it gives the right size.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Gravity only becomes large when at least one of the masses is enormous, such as a planet. That is why we feel the Earth's pull so strongly and the pull of everything else around us not at all.

## Measuring G in a laboratory

Because the force between ordinary objects is so small, measuring $G$ is hard. The first laboratory experiment that could do it was carried out by Henry Cavendish in 1797 and 1798, with an instrument designed by John Michell. A light rod with a small lead ball at each end hung from a thin wire. Two large lead balls were brought close to the small ones, and their gravitational pull twisted the wire by a very small angle. From the twist, Cavendish found the tiny force between the balls.

Cavendish's own aim, as the title of his paper says, was to find the **density** of the Earth (its mass per cubic metre), and that is the result he reported. The value of $G$ as a separate constant was worked out from results like his only later, in the nineteenth century, even though many textbooks say that Cavendish "measured G" (Persson 2025, arXiv). Either way, his experiment was the first to measure gravity between objects in a laboratory, and it is the reason we know $G$ and, as the last section shows, the mass of the Earth. Even today $G$ is known less precisely than almost any other constant of nature: only to about five significant figures.

## The gravitational field strength g

Put any mass $m$ near a planet of mass $M$, at a distance $r$ from the planet's centre. The planet pulls it with $F = G M m/r^2$. Divide by $m$ to get the force **per kilogram**:

<Formula label="Gravitational field strength" tex="g = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{G M}{r^2}" cite="mit" at="Newton's law of universal gravitation (the force on an object of mass m₀ divided by m₀, a₀ = G M/d²)" />

The mass $m$ of the object has cancelled. So $g$ depends only on the planet and on where you are. We call it the **gravitational field strength** at that place. (Siyavula calls the same quantity the gravitational acceleration; the MIT notes give it as the force on a standard kilogram, in newtons per kilogram, Sec. 8.2.3.) The idea of a **field** is this: the planet changes the space around it, so that at every point there is a value of $g$, ready to act on any mass placed there. A mass $m$ placed at that point feels the force

<Formula label="Force on a mass in a gravitational field" tex="F_g = m\,g" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 8.2.3, Eq. (8.2.2)" />

which is the weight of lessons 2.1 and 2.5, now with $g$ worked out from the planet instead of measured. (In Block 6 you will meet the electric field, built on exactly the same idea: a force per unit of charge instead of per kilogram.)

The unit of $g$ is the newton per kilogram. Since $1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^2$, a newton per kilogram is the same as a metre per second squared: $g$ is also the acceleration of anything falling freely at that place. Because $m$ cancelled, a heavy and a light object fall with the same acceleration, as lesson 2.3 found.

## g on the Earth, the Moon and Mars

At the surface of a planet, $r$ is the planet's radius $R$, so

<Formula label="Field strength at the surface of a planet" tex="g = \frac{G M}{R^2}" cite="mit" at="Newton's law of universal gravitation (a₀ = G M_Earth/d²_Earth, with d the radius of the planet)" />

<WorkedExample title="g at the surface of the Earth">
<Problem>
The Earth has a mass of $5.9722 \times 10^{24}$ kg and a mean radius of 6371 km (NASA, Earth Fact Sheet). Find the gravitational field strength at its surface.
</Problem>
<Step title="Put in the numbers">
$R = 6.371 \times 10^{6}$ m, so $g = \dfrac{6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.9722 \times 10^{24}}{(6.371 \times 10^{6})^2} \approx 9.82\ \text{N/kg}.$
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The NASA fact sheet lists a mean surface gravity of 9.820 m/s², the same value. The 9.81 m/s² that this course uses is a little smaller because the Earth spins and is slightly flattened: the measured $g$ is about 9.78 m/s² at the equator and 9.83 m/s² at the poles (MIT notes, Sec. 8.2.3). Units: $\text{N·m}^2/\text{kg}^2 \times \text{kg}/\text{m}^2 = \text{N/kg}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The same formula works for any round body. With the masses and radii from NASA's fact sheets:

| Body | Mass (kg) | Mean radius (km) | $g = GM/R^2$ (N/kg) | Fact sheet (m/s²) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Earth | $5.9722 \times 10^{24}$ | 6371 | 9.82 | 9.820 |
| Moon | $7.346 \times 10^{22}$ | 1737.4 | 1.624 | 1.62 |
| Mars | $6.4169 \times 10^{23}$ | 3389.5 | 3.728 | 3.73 |

The Moon has about 1/81 of the Earth's mass, but it is also much smaller, so you are closer to its centre: its field strength ends up about a sixth of the Earth's. Mars has about a tenth of the Earth's mass and about half its radius, giving a bit more than a third of the Earth's $g$.

<WorkedExample title="A toolbox on three worlds">
<Problem>
A toolbox has a mass of 12 kg. What does it weigh on the Earth, on the Moon and on Mars?
</Problem>
<Step title="Weight is mass times field strength">
Earth: $12 \times 9.81 \approx 118$ N. Moon: $12 \times 1.62 \approx 19.4$ N. Mars: $12 \times 3.73 \approx 44.8$ N.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The mass is 12 kg everywhere; only the pull changes. The Moon's weight is about a sixth of the Earth's, and Mars's a bit more than a third, as the field strengths say.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Gravity weakens with height

Go up, and $r$ grows, so $g$ falls. At a height $h$ above the surface, the distance to the centre is $r = R + h$:

<Formula label="Field strength at a height h above a planet" tex="g = \frac{G M}{(R + h)^2}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 8.2.1 (far from the surface the force is no longer constant; the distance is measured from the centre of the Earth)" />

A few kilometres up a mountain, $R + h$ is hardly different from $R$, so $g$ barely changes: that is why the constant $g$ of lesson 1.7 works so well near the ground. Far away, the inverse square law takes over. At a distance $2R$ from the centre, one Earth radius above the ground, $g$ is a quarter of its surface value: about 2.45 N/kg.

<WorkedExample title="Gravity at the space station">
<Problem>
The International Space Station orbits about 400 km above the ground (NASA's page *What Is Microgravity?* gives 200 to 250 miles; 250 miles is about 400 km). Find the Earth's field strength there, and compare it with the surface value.
</Problem>
<Step title="Distance from the centre">
$r = R + h = 6371\ \text{km} + 400\ \text{km} = 6771\ \text{km} = 6.771 \times 10^{6}$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Field strength">
$g = \dfrac{6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.9722 \times 10^{24}}{(6.771 \times 10^{6})^2} \approx 8.69\ \text{N/kg}.$
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Compared with the surface: $8.69/9.82 \approx 0.885$, about 89 %. That matches NASA's statement that gravity at the station is about 90 percent as strong as on the ground. So the astronauts float not because gravity is gone, but because they and the station fall round the Earth together (lesson 2.5).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Newton's Moon test

The law of gravitation passed its first great test with the Moon. Newton's question was: is the force that makes an apple fall the same force that keeps the Moon on its curved path? If it is, and if it weakens as one over the distance squared, then the Moon's acceleration towards the Earth must be $g$ divided by the square of the Moon's distance measured in Earth radii.

<WorkedExample title="Does the Moon fall as the law predicts?">
<Problem>
The Moon's mean distance from the Earth is 384 400 km (NASA, Moon Fact Sheet); the Earth's radius is 6371 km. Predict the Moon's acceleration towards the Earth from the inverse square law, and compare it with the centripetal acceleration of its orbit found in lesson 1.10.
</Problem>
<Step title="How many Earth radii away?">
$384\,400/6371 \approx 60.3$, so the Moon is about 60 Earth radii from the Earth's centre.
</Step>
<Step title="The predicted acceleration">
Gravity there is $60^2 = 3600$ times weaker than at the surface, so the Moon should fall towards the Earth with $9.81/3600 \approx 2.7 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s².
</Step>
<Step title="Compare with the orbit">
Lesson 1.10 found the Moon's centripetal acceleration from its distance and its period of 27.3 days alone, without any law of gravity: also about $2.7 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s². The two agree.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Directly from the field strength: $GM/r^2 = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.9722 \times 10^{24}/(3.844 \times 10^{8})^2 \approx 2.70 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s², the same answer.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The pull that drops an apple, weakened by the inverse square of the distance, is exactly what keeps the Moon on its orbit. The MIT notes tell that Newton first explained the Moon's motion in this way in 1666, long before he published the law (Sec. 9.2.1). That the same law holds in a garden and in the sky is why it is called **universal**.

## Weighing the Earth

Turn $g = GM/R^2$ round and you get the mass of the Earth from things that can be measured on the ground: $g$ from a falling object, $R$ from the size of the Earth, and $G$ from a laboratory experiment.

<Formula label="The mass of the Earth from g, R and G" tex="M = \frac{g R^2}{G}" cite="mit" at="Newton's law of universal gravitation (a₀ = G M_Earth/d²_Earth, solved for the mass)" />

<WorkedExample title="The mass of the Earth">
<Problem>
Use $g = 9.81$ N/kg, $R = 6.371 \times 10^{6}$ m and $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ N m²/kg² to find the mass of the Earth. Then find its average density, its mass divided by its volume $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The mass">
$M = \dfrac{9.81 \times (6.371 \times 10^{6})^2}{6.674 \times 10^{-11}} \approx 5.97 \times 10^{24}$ kg.
</Step>
<Step title="The density">
Volume $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi (6.371 \times 10^{6})^3 \approx 1.08 \times 10^{21}$ m³, so the density is about $5.5 \times 10^{3}$ kg/m³, five and a half times that of water (1000 kg/m³). The Earth Fact Sheet gives a mean density of 5513 kg/m³.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
NASA's value for the Earth's mass, $5.9722 \times 10^{24}$ kg, agrees to three figures. (Ours is a little lower in the fourth figure because 9.81 m/s² includes the effect of the Earth's spin.) Ordinary rock at the surface is much less dense than this average, so the inside of the Earth must be denser still.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

This is why Cavendish's experiment is often called "weighing the Earth": once the pull between lead balls in a room is known, the Earth's pull on everything tells you its mass. The same trick, with orbits instead of falling apples, weighs the Sun and whole galaxies (lessons 2.12 and 2.13).

## Try it

In the simulation, two masses pull on each other. Change either mass and watch both force arrows change together: they always stay equal, as the third law says. Double one mass: the force doubles. Then move the masses apart. Double the distance between their centres and the force falls to a quarter.

## Common mistakes

- **Measuring $r$ from the surface.** For planets and moons, $r$ is the distance from the **centre**. At a height of one Earth radius, $r = 2R$ and $g$ is a quarter, not a half.
- **Mixing up $g$ and $G$.** $G$ is one universal constant, $6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ N m²/kg². $g$ is the field strength of one body at one place: 9.81 N/kg at the Earth's surface, less higher up, different on other planets.
- **"Twice as far, half the force."** It is an inverse **square** law: twice as far gives a quarter.
- **"There is no gravity in space."** At the space station gravity is still about 90 % as strong as on the ground. Gravity grows weaker with distance but never reaches zero.
- **"The heavier body pulls harder."** The Earth pulls an apple exactly as hard as the apple pulls the Earth. The apple moves much more because its mass is so much smaller.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Gravity is the force that shapes the universe on the largest scales. It holds stars together in galaxies and galaxies together in clusters, and astronomers use the law of this lesson, through orbits, to weigh them; doing so is one of the main reasons most astronomers think galaxies contain dark matter (lesson 2.13). Gravity also acts on the universe as a whole: it pulls all matter towards all other matter, and so tends to slow the expansion of the universe (today another effect, often called dark energy, makes the expansion speed up instead; Year IV explains it). In the cosmology course of Year IV (MIT 8.286), Guth takes a uniform ball of matter that is expanding, applies Newton's law of gravitation to it, and arrives at the Friedmann equation, the central equation for how the universe expands. Strictly the universe needs Einstein's general relativity, but for this case the Newtonian argument gives the same equation.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Newton's law of gravitation:** every two masses attract each other along the line between them with $F = G m_1 m_2/r^2$, with $r$ the distance between their centres. Spheres act as if their mass were at the centre.
- **The gravitational constant** $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ N m²/kg² is tiny, so gravity between everyday objects is far too weak to feel. Cavendish's torsion balance (1797 to 1798) first measured gravity between objects in a laboratory, to find the density of the Earth; $G$ itself was worked out from such measurements later.
- **The gravitational field strength** is the force per kilogram, $g = F/m = GM/r^2$, in N/kg, which is the same as m/s². A mass $m$ in the field feels $F_g = m g$.
- At the surface $g = GM/R^2$: about 9.8 N/kg on the Earth, 1.62 N/kg on the Moon and 3.73 N/kg on Mars.
- $g$ weakens with height as an inverse square: at twice the distance from the centre it is a quarter as strong.
- **Newton's Moon test:** at about 60 Earth radii, gravity is 3600 times weaker, giving the Moon's acceleration of about $2.7 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s².
- **Weighing the Earth:** $M = gR^2/G \approx 6 \times 10^{24}$ kg.
