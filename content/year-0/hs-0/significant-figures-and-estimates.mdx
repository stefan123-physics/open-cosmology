Every measurement has a limit. A school ruler is marked in millimetres, so a length read from it is good to about a millimetre and no better. If you measure a pencil as 17.3 cm, writing it as 17.300 cm would claim a precision you do not have.

This lesson is about saying honestly how well a number is known, through **significant figures** and rounding, and about the opposite skill: getting a useful answer quickly when you know very little, through **estimates**.

## Rounding

To **round** a number to a given place, look at the first digit you are dropping:

- If it is 5 or more, increase the last digit you keep by one.
- If it is less than 5, leave the last digit you keep as it is.

So 7.3486 rounded to two decimal places is 7.35 (the dropped digit is 8), and to one decimal place it is 7.3 (the dropped digit is 4). When a 9 is rounded up it becomes 0 and the digit before it goes up: 2.497 to two decimal places is 2.50.

Rounding throws information away, so do it **only at the end** of a calculation. If you round at every step, the small errors add up. For example, $2.46 \times 3.46 = 8.5116$, which rounds to 8.5 at one decimal place. Rounding each factor first gives $2.5 \times 3.5 = 8.75$, which rounds to 8.8: the early rounding has moved the answer.

## Significant figures

None of this lesson's sources sets out rules for significant figures, so the ones below are the standard rules used in science teaching, in our own words.

The **significant figures** of a measured number are the digits that carry real information: all the digits you are sure of, plus the first uncertain one. A length of 17.3 cm has three significant figures; the 3 is the uncertain one.

To count them:

- Non-zero digits are always significant: 482 has three.
- Zeros **between** non-zero digits are significant: 30.05 has four.
- **Leading** zeros are not significant. They only place the decimal point: 0.0047 has two, the same as $4.7 \times 10^{-3}$.
- **Trailing** zeros after a decimal point are significant. They say the measurement was made to that place: 2.50 has three, and 2.500 has four.
- Trailing zeros in a whole number, as in 1500, are unclear: they could be measured or could just fill the places. Scientific notation removes the doubt: $1.5 \times 10^{3}$ has two significant figures and $1.500 \times 10^{3}$ has four.

That last point is one more reason to use scientific notation: the number in front shows exactly the digits you trust. Siyavula's own example writes the speed of light as $3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s, rounded to two decimal places in the number in front.

## Calculating with measured numbers

A calculated result cannot be more precise than the measurements it comes from. Two working rules:

1. **Multiplying and dividing:** give the result as many significant figures as the factor with the **fewest**.
2. **Adding and subtracting:** give the result as many **decimal places** as the term with the fewest.

<WorkedExample title="A table top">
<Problem>
A table top measures 1.25 m by 0.80 m. Find its area, and its perimeter.
</Problem>
<Step title="Area: a product">
$1.25 \times 0.80 = 1.0000$, but 0.80 has only two significant figures, so the area is $1.0$ m². The trailing zero matters: it says the area is known to about a tenth of a square metre.
</Step>
<Step title="Perimeter: a sum">
$1.25 + 0.80 + 1.25 + 0.80 = 4.10$ m. Both lengths have two decimal places, so the sum keeps two: 4.10 m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: metres times metres gives m², and a sum of metres gives metres. Size: a table about a metre long and almost a metre wide should have an area near 1 m² and a perimeter near 4 m, as found. The area has two significant figures, the same as the less precise side, 0.80 m, which limits it.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The rule for sums can be surprising. $12.11\ \text{m} + 0.3\ \text{m} = 12.41$ m, but 0.3 m is known only to a tenth of a metre, so the sum is 12.4 m: the extra hundredths of the first number are lost.

These rules are a quick guide, not exact laws. Physicists who need to be careful give each measurement an **uncertainty**, as in $17.3 \pm 0.1$ cm, and work out how the uncertainties combine. You will meet that at university. For this course, significant figures are enough.

A last point: **exact numbers** have no uncertainty. The 2 in "twice the length", or the 60 in "60 seconds per minute", is exact and never limits the significant figures of an answer.

## Estimates

Often a rough answer is all you need, or all you can get. How much water does a city use per year? How many seconds in a lifetime? An **order-of-magnitude estimate** finds the power of ten, without worrying about the digits.

The key method is **divide and conquer**: split a quantity you cannot guess into factors you can. Each factor may be off, but as Mahajan's MIT course notes point out, errors in several independent guesses tend partly to cancel, so the product is often better than you would expect.

Two tricks keep the arithmetic easy:

- Handle the **powers of ten first**, then the numbers in front.
- Round the numbers in front boldly. Mahajan suggests treating every number as either 1 or "few", where a few is about 3, so that few × few ≈ 10.

<WorkedExample title="Heartbeats in a lifetime">
<Problem>
About how many times does a human heart beat in a lifetime?
</Problem>
<Step title="Divide">
Beats in a lifetime = (beats per minute) × (minutes per hour) × (hours per day) × (days per year) × (years in a life).
</Step>
<Step title="Guess each factor">
A resting heart beats about 70 times a minute. There are 60 minutes in an hour, 24 hours in a day and 365 days in a year. Take a life of 80 years.
</Step>
<Step title="Multiply, powers of ten first">
In scientific notation the factors are $7 \times 10^{1}$, $6 \times 10^{1}$, $2.4 \times 10^{1}$, $3.65 \times 10^{2}$ and $8 \times 10^{1}$. The powers give $10^{1+1+1+2+1} = 10^{6}$. The numbers in front give $7 \times 6 \times 2.4 \times 3.65 \times 8 \approx 2900$, or $2.9 \times 10^{3}$. Together: about $2.9 \times 10^{9}$ beats.
</Step>
<Step title="State it honestly">
The guesses are rough (the heart beats faster during exercise, and lives differ), so the answer is "about three billion", or of order $10^{9}$. Writing 2 943 360 000 would claim a precision the estimate does not have.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Check with a second method** when you can. If two quite different routes give the same order of magnitude, you can trust it. For example, the number of seconds in a year: $3600 \times 24 \times 365$ has powers $10^{3} \times 10^{1} \times 10^{2} = 10^{6}$ and numbers in front $3.6 \times 2.4 \times 3.65 \approx 32$, so about $3.2 \times 10^{7}$ s. A handy memory aid gives the same: a year is close to $\pi \times 10^{7}$ seconds.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Cosmology uses both skills. Some numbers are known extremely precisely: the temperature of the cosmic microwave background, the faint glow left over from the early universe, was determined in 2009, by combining earlier measurements, as 2.72548 K with an uncertainty of about 0.00057 K. By this lesson's rule (the trusted digits plus the first uncertain one) that is 2.7255 K: five significant figures, the uncertainty being in the fourth decimal place. For quick estimates, cosmologists round it to 2.7 K. Other quantities, such as how much of the universe is made of the unseen "dark matter", were long known only to an order of magnitude, and estimates like the ones in this lesson were how physicists first saw that something was missing.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Round by looking at the first dropped digit (5 or more: round up), and round only at the end.
- Significant figures are the trusted digits plus the first uncertain one. Leading zeros never count; trailing zeros after a decimal point do; scientific notation removes doubt.
- Products and quotients keep the fewest significant figures; sums and differences keep the fewest decimal places. Exact numbers do not limit the result.
- An order-of-magnitude estimate splits a quantity into guessable factors, multiplies them with the powers of ten handled first, and is checked by a second method where possible.
