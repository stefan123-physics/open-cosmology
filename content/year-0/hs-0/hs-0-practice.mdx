This set practises the eleven lessons of Block 0. The problems, hints and solutions are our own; the methods are those of the lessons, which build on Siyavula's *Skills for science* chapter. Problems 1 to 10 count 10 points each, 100 in all; Problem 11 is optional.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. Number boxes accept a small rounding difference, so give your answer to two or three significant figures, in the unit shown. Formula boxes take plain typing, for example `(a + b)/c`, and accept every correct way of writing the answer. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each problem ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Cells in a row" points={10}>

A typical red blood cell is about $8.0 \times 10^{-6}$ m across. How many such cells, laid side by side, make a row 1.0 cm long?

<Hint>Write 1.0 cm in metres first, using a power of ten.</Hint>
<Hint>Divide the length of the row by the width of one cell, handling the numbers in front and the powers of ten separately.</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-n" label="Number of cells:" value="1250" tolerance="5%" calc="p1-n" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Same unit">
1.0 cm $= 1.0 \times 10^{-2}$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Divide">
$\dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{-2}}{8.0 \times 10^{-6}} = 0.125 \times 10^{4} = 1.25 \times 10^{3}$, so about 1250 cells, or $1.3 \times 10^{3}$ to two significant figures.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
A centimetre is ten thousand micrometres and a cell is 8 micrometres, so the answer should be a bit more than a thousand. It is.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Filling a bucket" points={10}>

A tap fills a 12-litre bucket in 40 s.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

What is the flow rate in m³/s? (One litre is $10^{-3}$ m³.)

<Hint>Flow rate is volume divided by time.</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="Flow rate (m³/s):" value="0.0003" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Convert, then divide">
12 L $= 12 \times 10^{-3}$ m³, so the flow rate is $12 \times 10^{-3} / 40 = 3.0 \times 10^{-4}$ m³/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: m³ divided by s gives m³/s. Size: a cubic metre is a thousand litres, and this tap would take $1/(3.0 \times 10^{-4}) \approx 3300$ s, nearly an hour, to fill one. That fits a household tap.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

What is the same flow rate in litres per minute?

<Hint>Multiply by a conversion factor equal to one: 60 s per minute.</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="Flow rate (L/min):" value="18" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Conversion factor">
$\dfrac{12\ \text{L}}{40\ \text{s}} \times \dfrac{60\ \text{s}}{1\ \text{min}} = 18$ L/min.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
40 s is two thirds of a minute, so a full minute fills one and a half buckets: $1.5 \times 12 = 18$ L.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Water in a lifetime" points={10}>

Estimate how many litres of water a person drinks in a lifetime. Assume 2 litres a day for 80 years, ignoring leap days.

<Hint>Split the estimate into factors: litres per day, days per year, years.</Hint>
<Hint>Multiply the powers of ten and the numbers in front separately.</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-v" label="Litres:" value="58400" tolerance="5%" calc="p3-v" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Multiply the factors">
$2 \times 365 \times 80 = 58\,400$ litres.
</Step>
<Step title="State it honestly">
The inputs are rough, so the useful answer is "about $6 \times 10^{4}$ litres". Its order of magnitude is $10^{4}$ by the power in its scientific notation, or $10^{5}$ if you round 5.8 up to the nearer power: somewhere between ten thousand and a hundred thousand litres, a small swimming pool's worth.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Per year that is $2 \times 365 = 730$ litres, less than a cubic metre; eighty such years give about $6 \times 10^{4}$ litres.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Rearranging a motion formula" points={10}>

A runner on a straight track starts at position $x_0$ and runs at a steady speed $v$. After a time $t$ her position is $x = x_0 + v\,t$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Make $v$ the subject of the formula.

<Hint>Undo the addition first, then the multiplication.</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$v =$" expr="(x - x0)/t" vars={{ x: [10, 100], x0: [0, 9], t: [1, 20] }} names={{ x0: "x_0" }}
  mistakes={[{ expr: "x/t - x0", hint: "Divide the whole of x minus x₀ by t, not just x." }, { expr: "(x + x0)/t", hint: "To remove x₀ from the right side, subtract it from both sides." }, { expr: "x - x0/t", hint: "Divide the whole of x minus x₀ by t, not just x₀." }]} />

<Solution>
<Step title="Subtract, then divide">
Subtract $x_0$ from both sides: $x - x_0 = v\,t$. Divide both sides by $t$: $v = (x - x_0)/t$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with units">
Metres divided by seconds gives m/s, a speed.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

She starts at the 15 m mark and passes the 60 m mark 9.0 s later. What is her speed?

<Answer id="p4-b" label="Speed (m/s):" value="5" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Use the rearranged formula">
$v = (60 - 15)/9.0 = 45/9.0 = 5.0$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Put it back: $15 + 5.0 \times 9.0 = 60$ m. A speed of 5 m/s is a steady jog.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Reading a straight line" points={10}>

A cart moves along a straight track. Its position–time graph is a straight line through the points (1.0 s, 4.0 m) and (5.0 s, 16 m).

<Part label="a" points={4}>

What is the cart's speed?

<Hint>Speed is the slope of the position–time graph: rise over run.</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="Speed (m/s):" value="3" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Rise over run">
$\dfrac{16 - 4.0}{5.0 - 1.0} = \dfrac{12}{4.0} = 3.0$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: metres over seconds. In the 4.0 s between the points the cart covers $3.0 \times 4.0 = 12$ m, which is the rise.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Where was the cart at time 0?

<Answer id="p5-b" label="Position at 0 s (m):" value="1" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="The intercept">
Go back 1.0 s from the first point at 3.0 m/s: $4.0 - 3.0 \times 1.0 = 1.0$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
From the second point: going back 5.0 s at 3.0 m/s gives $16 - 15 = 1.0$ m as well.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Where is it at 8.0 s, if it keeps going the same way?

<Answer id="p5-c" label="Position at 8.0 s (m):" value="25" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-c" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Use the equation of the line">
Position $= 1.0 + 3.0 \times 8.0 = 25$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
From the second point: 3.0 s later at 3.0 m/s is 9.0 m further, $16 + 9.0 = 25$ m.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="A dropped stone" points={10}>

A stone is dropped from a bridge 20 m above the water. Its height above the water $t$ seconds later is $h = 20 - 4.9\,t^{2}$ (in metres).

<Part label="a" points={6}>

When does it reach the water?

<Hint>Set $h = 0$ and solve for $t^{2}$ first.</Hint>
<Hint>Keep only the solution that makes physical sense.</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-a" label="Time (s):" value="2.02" tolerance="2%" calc="p6-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Solve by square roots">
$0 = 20 - 4.9\,t^{2}$ gives $t^{2} = 20/4.9 = 4.08$, so $t = \pm 2.02$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Physical solution">
The stone is dropped at $t = 0$, so the negative root is not physical: $t \approx 2.0$ s.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

The fall time is proportional to the square root of the height. From a bridge four times as high (80 m), how long does the fall take?

<Hint>A power law with power $\tfrac12$: multiplying the height by 4 multiplies the time by $4^{1/2}$.</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-b" label="Time (s):" value="4.04" tolerance="2%" calc="p6-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Scaling">
$4^{1/2} = 2$, so the time doubles: $2 \times 2.02 = 4.04$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Directly: $t = \sqrt{80/4.9} = 4.04$ s.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="A kite" points={10}>

A kite flies on a straight string 50 m long that makes an angle of 35° with the level ground.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

How high is the kite above the hand holding the string?

<Hint>The string is the hypotenuse; the height is the side opposite the 35° angle.</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-a" label="Height (m):" value="28.7" tolerance="2%" calc="p7-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Sine">
Height $= 50 \sin 35^{\circ} = 50 \times 0.574 = 28.7$ m, about 29 m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The height must be less than the 50 m string, and less than half of it only if the angle were below 30°. At 35°, a little more than half is right.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

How far away is the kite horizontally?

<Answer id="p7-b" label="Horizontal distance (m):" value="41" tolerance="2%" calc="p7-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Cosine">
Distance $= 50 \cos 35^{\circ} = 50 \times 0.819 = 41$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with Pythagoras">
$28.7^{2} + 41.0^{2} = 824 + 1681 = 2505 \approx 50^{2} = 2500$.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 8

<Exercise id="p8" title="A walk in two legs" points={10}>

A hiker walks 250 m due east, then 600 m due north.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

How far is she from her starting point?

<Hint>The two legs are at right angles.</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-a" label="Distance (m):" value="650" tolerance="2%" calc="p8-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Pythagoras">
$\sqrt{250^{2} + 600^{2}} = \sqrt{62\,500 + 360\,000} = \sqrt{422\,500} = 650$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The result is longer than either leg (600 m) and shorter than both together (850 m), as it must be.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

At what angle north of east is her displacement?

<Hint>Use the components: $R_x$ = 250 m east, $R_y$ = 600 m north.</Hint>

<Answer id="p8-b" label="Angle (degrees):" value="67.4" tolerance="2%" calc="p8-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Tangent">
$\tan\theta = 600/250 = 2.4$, so $\theta = \tan^{-1} 2.4 = 67.4°$ north of east. Both components are positive, so the calculator's angle is the right one.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 9

<Exercise id="p9" title="Doubling bacteria" points={10}>

A culture of 1000 bacteria doubles every 20 minutes. How long does it take to reach a million bacteria?

<Hint>After $n$ doublings there are $1000 \times 2^{n}$ bacteria. Set that equal to $10^{6}$.</Hint>
<Hint>Take the logarithm of both sides to bring $n$ down.</Hint>

<Answer id="p9-t" label="Time (minutes):" value="199" tolerance="2%" calc="p9-t" />

<Solution>
<Step title="The equation">
$1000 \times 2^{n} = 10^{6}$, so $2^{n} = 1000$.
</Step>
<Step title="Logarithms">
$n \log 2 = \log 1000 = 3$, so $n = 3/0.301 = 9.97$ doublings.
</Step>
<Step title="Time">
$9.97 \times 20 = 199$ minutes, about 3.3 hours.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Ten doublings multiply by $2^{10} = 1024$, just over a thousand, so just under 10 doublings is right.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 10

<Exercise id="p10" title="A lift ride" points={10}>

A lift starts from rest and speeds up steadily to 2.0 m/s in 2.0 s. It then rises at a steady 2.0 m/s for 10 s, and finally slows steadily to rest in 2.0 s.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

How far does the lift rise?

<Hint>Draw the velocity–time graph: a triangle, a rectangle and another triangle.</Hint>
<Hint>The distance is the total area under the graph.</Hint>

<Answer id="p10-a" label="Distance (m):" value="24" tolerance="2%" calc="p10-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Areas">
Speeding up: $\tfrac12 \times 2.0 \times 2.0 = 2.0$ m. Steady: $2.0 \times 10 = 20$ m. Slowing: 2.0 m again.
</Step>
<Step title="Total">
$2.0 + 20 + 2.0 = 24$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: (m/s) × s = m. At the top speed for the whole 14 s the lift would rise 28 m; the slow start and stop cost 4 m, leaving 24 m.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

What is its average speed over the whole ride?

<Answer id="p10-b" label="Average speed (m/s):" value="1.71" tolerance="2%" calc="p10-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Distance over time">
The ride lasts $2.0 + 10 + 2.0 = 14$ s, so the average speed is $24/14 = 1.71$ m/s, a little less than the top speed, as expected.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 11

<Exercise id="p11" title="A receding galaxy" points={10} optional>

This problem is optional: it does not count towards the score of the set.

Hubble's law says a galaxy at distance $d$ moves away at speed $v = H\,d$. Take $H$ = 70 km/s per megaparsec (Mpc).

<Part label="a" points={5}>

How fast does a galaxy 200 Mpc away recede?

<Answer id="p11-a" label="Speed (km/s):" value="14000" tolerance="2%" calc="p11-a" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Multiply">
$v = 70 \times 200 = 14\,000$ km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Units: (km/s per Mpc) × Mpc = km/s. That is under 5% of the speed of light (300 000 km/s), so the simple law is a fair guide.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Another galaxy recedes at 3500 km/s. How far away is it?

<Hint>Rearrange Hubble's law for $d$.</Hint>

<Answer id="p11-b" label="Distance (Mpc):" value="50" tolerance="2%" calc="p11-b" />

<Solution>
<Step title="Rearrange">
$d = v/H = 3500/70 = 50$ Mpc.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Half the distance of a galaxy at 100 Mpc (7000 km/s), so half the speed: consistent.
</Step>
</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
