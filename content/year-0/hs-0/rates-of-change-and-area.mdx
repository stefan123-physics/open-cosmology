Everything changes, and physics asks how fast. Lesson 0.5 found the slope of a straight line, a rate that is the same everywhere along it. But most graphs in physics curve: a falling stone gains speed, a cooling cup loses heat ever more slowly. This lesson extends the idea of slope to curves and introduces its partner, the **area under a graph**. Together they are the two central ideas of calculus, which you will study properly at university.

## Average rate of change

Take any quantity that depends on time, say a position $x(t)$ (read "$x$ of $t$": the position at time $t$). Between two moments $t = a$ and $t = b$, its **average rate of change** is the change in the quantity divided by the change in time:

<Formula label="Average rate of change" tex="\text{average rate} = \frac{x(b) - x(a)}{b - a}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.2, Average gradient" />

On the graph this is the slope of the straight line through the two points, a line called a **secant**. For a position this average rate is the **average velocity**.

<WorkedExample title="A ball rolling down a ramp">
<Problem>
A ball starts from rest and rolls down a ramp. Its distance from the start, in metres, is $x(t) = 0.80\,t^{2}$ with $t$ in seconds. What is its average velocity between $t = 1.0$ s and $t = 3.0$ s?
</Problem>
<Step title="Positions">
$x(1.0) = 0.80 \times 1.0^{2} = 0.80$ m and $x(3.0) = 0.80 \times 3.0^{2} = 7.2$ m.
</Step>
<Step title="Rate">
Average velocity $= \dfrac{7.2 - 0.80}{3.0 - 1.0} = \dfrac{6.4}{2.0} = 3.2$ m/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The rate at one instant

A speedometer shows the speed at this moment, not an average. To find it from a graph, shrink the interval: keep the first point at time $a$, move the second point to $a + h$, and let the gap $h$ become smaller and smaller. The secant through the two points then turns towards a line that just touches the curve at $a$: the **tangent**. Its slope is the **rate of change at the instant** $a$:

<Formula label="Rate of change at one instant" tex="\text{rate at } a = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x(a + h) - x(a)}{h}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 6.1, Gradient at a point" />

The symbol $\lim_{h \to 0}$ ("the **limit** as $h$ goes to zero") means: the value the fraction gets closer and closer to as $h$ shrinks. You cannot simply put $h = 0$, because that gives $0/0$; you watch what happens as $h$ approaches 0.

For the ball at $t = 2.0$ s:

| Gap $h$ (s) | $x(2.0 + h)$ (m) | Average velocity (m/s) |
|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | 7.2 | 4.0 |
| 0.1 | 3.528 | 3.28 |
| 0.01 | 3.23208 | 3.208 |
| 0.001 | 3.2032008 | 3.2008 |

with $x(2.0) = 3.2$ m. The averages close in on **3.2 m/s**: that is the ball's velocity at the moment $t = 2.0$ s. The same algebra works at any moment $t$: the average velocity from $t$ to $t + h$ is $\dfrac{0.80(t + h)^{2} - 0.80\,t^{2}}{h} = 1.6\,t + 0.80\,h$, which tends to $1.6\,t$ as $h$ goes to zero. (At $t = 2.0$ s and $h = 0.1$ s it gives $3.2 + 0.08 = 3.28$ m/s, as in the table.) So the ball's velocity at any moment is $v = 1.6\,t$ m/s.

On a position–time graph the slope of the tangent is the velocity. On a velocity–time graph it is the **acceleration**, the rate at which the velocity changes (Block 1 treats it in full).

## Area under a velocity–time graph

Now the reverse question. Suppose you know the velocity at every moment. How far has the object travelled?

If the velocity is constant, distance = velocity × time. On a velocity–time graph that product is the **area** of a rectangle: height (velocity) times width (time). The units confirm it: (m/s) × s = m. This holds in general:

<Formula label="Area under a velocity–time graph" tex="\text{displacement} = \text{area under the velocity–time graph}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 21.6, Motion at constant velocity" />

When the velocity changes steadily, the area is a triangle or a trapezium. The rolling ball's velocity grows steadily as $v = 1.6\,t$ m/s, as found above. In the first 3.0 s it rises from 0 to 4.8 m/s, and the area under it is the triangle

$\tfrac12 \times 3.0\ \text{s} \times 4.8\ \text{m/s} = 7.2$ m,

exactly the position $x(3.0) = 7.2$ m found earlier. The area under the velocity graph gave back the position graph.

**Below the axis.** A negative velocity means moving backwards. Area below the time axis then counts as negative: the displacement is the area above the axis minus the area below it. The total distance travelled adds both areas as positive numbers. A trolley that rolls forward at 3.0 m/s for 4.0 s (area $+12$ m) and then back at 2.0 m/s for 3.0 s (area $-6.0$ m) ends $12 - 6.0 = 6.0$ m from its start, but has travelled $12 + 6.0 = 18$ m.

When the graph curves, the area is no longer a simple shape. Then cut it into thin vertical strips. Each strip is almost a rectangle, so its area is almost (velocity) × (short time). Adding the strips gives an estimate, and thinner strips give a better one. In the limit of infinitely thin strips the estimate becomes exact: that limit is what calculus calls an **integral**.

## Two sides of one idea

Slope and area undo each other. The slope of the position graph gives the velocity graph; the area under the velocity graph gives back the change in position. This link, the fundamental theorem of calculus, is one of the most useful facts in all of science. In the blocks on motion you will use both directions without needing calculus: slopes and areas of straight-line graphs are enough there.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Cosmology is about a rate of change: how fast the universe expands. Picture a graph of the size of the universe against time. Its slope at the present moment, divided by the present size, is the **Hubble constant** from lessons 0.4 and 0.5: the expansion rate today. Reading the history of the universe means working out how that slope changed over billions of years, and adding up time along the way gives the age of the universe.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The average rate of change between two moments is the slope of the secant: $(x(b) - x(a))/(b - a)$.
- The rate at one instant is the slope of the tangent, the limit of the secant's slope as the interval shrinks to zero.
- On a position–time graph the tangent's slope is the velocity; on a velocity–time graph it is the acceleration.
- The area under a velocity–time graph is the displacement; for curved graphs, add thin strips.
- Slope and area undo each other: the core of calculus.
