Angles are everywhere in physics: the slope of a hill, the direction of a push, the position of a planet in the sky. This lesson gives you two ways to measure an angle, degrees and radians, and the three functions that connect angles to lengths: **sine**, **cosine** and **tangent**. Together they are called **trigonometry**, "triangle measuring".

## Degrees and radians

An angle measures an amount of turning. In **degrees**, a full turn is 360°, a half turn 180° and a right angle 90°. The number 360 is a choice made long ago; nothing in nature picks it out.

Physicists and mathematicians usually prefer **radians**, which come from the circle itself. Draw a circle of radius $r$ around the corner of the angle. The angle cuts off an arc of length $s$. The angle in radians is the ratio of the two:

<Formula label="Radian measure" tex="\theta = \frac{s}{r}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 10.1, The radian measure of θ, p. 701" />

So an angle of 1 radian cuts off an arc exactly as long as the radius. The ratio does not depend on the size of the circle: a bigger circle has a longer arc and a longer radius in the same proportion. Because $s$ and $r$ are both lengths, an angle in radians is a pure number. The word "radian" (rad) is written only to make clear what is meant.

A full turn cuts off the whole circumference, $2\pi r$ (the distance around a circle of radius $r$), so a full turn is $2\pi r / r = 2\pi$ radians. That gives the conversion:

<Formula label="Degrees and radians" tex="2\pi\ \text{rad} = 360^{\circ}, \qquad 1\ \text{rad} = \frac{180^{\circ}}{\pi} \approx 57.3^{\circ}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 10.1, Equation 10.1, p. 704" />

A half turn is $\pi$ rad, a right angle $\pi/2$ rad, and 60° is $60 \times \pi/180 = \pi/3$ rad. Turned around, $\theta = s/r$ gives the length of an arc (Stitz and Zeager, p. 707): $s = r\,\theta$, with $\theta$ in radians. A wheel of radius 0.30 m that turns through 2.0 rad rolls $0.30 \times 2.0 = 0.60$ m.

**Calculator warning.** Every calculator has a degree mode and a radian mode. $\sin 30$ is 0.5 in degree mode but about $-0.99$ in radian mode, where it means 30 radians. Check the mode before every calculation.

## Sine, cosine and tangent

Take a right-angled triangle and pick one of its other two angles, $\theta$. Name the sides as seen from $\theta$: the **hypotenuse** is the longest side, opposite the right angle; the **opposite** side faces $\theta$; the **adjacent** side lies along $\theta$ and is not the hypotenuse. Then

<Formula label="The trigonometric ratios" tex="\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.3, Defining the trigonometric ratios" />

These ratios depend only on the angle, not on the size of the triangle: all right-angled triangles with the same angle $\theta$ are scaled copies of each other (they are **similar**), so their side ratios agree. Dividing the first ratio by the second also shows that $\tan\theta = \sin\theta / \cos\theta$.

The sides also obey **Pythagoras' theorem**: (opposite)² + (adjacent)² = (hypotenuse)². Divided by (hypotenuse)², that becomes $\sin^{2}\theta + \cos^{2}\theta = 1$ for every angle.

<WorkedExample title="A ramp">
<Problem>
A ramp 4.0 m long rises at 20° to the horizontal. How high does it rise, and how far does it reach horizontally?
</Problem>
<Step title="Name the sides">
The ramp is the hypotenuse, 4.0 m. The rise is opposite the 20° angle; the horizontal reach is adjacent to it.
</Step>
<Step title="Rearrange the ratios">
From $\sin\theta$ = opposite/hypotenuse: opposite = hypotenuse × $\sin\theta$ = $4.0 \times \sin 20^{\circ} = 4.0 \times 0.342 = 1.37$ m. In the same way, adjacent = $4.0 \times \cos 20^{\circ} = 4.0 \times 0.940 = 3.76$ m. The ramp's length is known to two significant figures, so the answers are 1.4 m and 3.8 m (lesson 0.3); the extra digit is kept only for the check.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with Pythagoras">
$1.37^{2} + 3.76^{2} = 1.88 + 14.14 = 16.0$, and $4.0^{2} = 16$. The sides fit.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Finding an angle.** If you know two sides, the inverse functions, written $\sin^{-1}$, $\cos^{-1}$ and $\tan^{-1}$ on a calculator, give the angle. A path that climbs 3.0 m over a horizontal distance of 4.0 m rises at $\theta = \tan^{-1}(3.0/4.0) = 36.9^{\circ}$, or 37° to two significant figures.

## Special angles

For a few angles the ratios have exact values, worth knowing without a calculator. They come from two simple triangles: half a square (angles 45°, 45°, 90°) and half an equilateral triangle (30°, 60°, 90°).

| Angle | sin | cos | tan |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0° | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 30° ($\pi/6$) | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}$ |
| 45° ($\pi/4$) | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ | $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$ | 1 |
| 60° ($\pi/3$) | $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ | $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | $\sqrt{3}$ |
| 90° ($\pi/2$) | 1 | 0 | undefined |

Notice that sine rises from 0 to 1 as the angle grows from 0° to 90°, while cosine falls from 1 to 0.

## Beyond 90°: the unit circle

A triangle cannot have two angles of 90° or more, but physics needs angles of any size: a wheel turns through many full turns. The way out is a circle of radius 1, the **unit circle**, centred on the origin. Turn from the positive $x$-axis anticlockwise through an angle $\theta$. The point you reach on the circle has coordinates

<Formula label="Sine and cosine on the unit circle" tex="(x, y) = (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)" cite="reading3" at="Sec. 5.8, Defining ratios in the Cartesian plane" />

This is the case $r = 1$ of Siyavula's definition for a point at distance $r$ from the origin, $\sin\theta = y/r$ and $\cos\theta = x/r$. For angles between 0° and 90° it agrees with the triangle definition (the hypotenuse is the radius, 1). For larger angles it defines sine and cosine anew, and they can be negative: at 180° the point is $(-1, 0)$, so $\cos 180^{\circ} = -1$ and $\sin 180^{\circ} = 0$. After a full turn everything repeats. Sine and cosine are **periodic**, with period $2\pi$ rad (360°), and their graphs are waves:

## Small angles

For small angles there is a useful shortcut. A thin slice of circle looks almost like a thin right-angled triangle, so the arc $s$ is nearly the same as the opposite side. In radians this gives

<Formula label="Small angles" tex="\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta \qquad (\text{small } \theta \text{, in radians})" cite="mit1801" at="Lecture 9 notes, sin x ≈ x near 0" />

At 0.10 rad (5.7°) the error is less than half a percent: $\sin 0.10 = 0.0998$. The rule only works in radians, which is one of the main reasons physicists use them.

It turns angles into sizes and distances. An object of size $D$ at a large distance $d$ fills an angle $\theta \approx D/d$. A person 1.8 m tall seen from 100 m fills $1.8/100 = 0.018$ rad, about 1.0°.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Astronomers cannot pace out the distance to a galaxy, but they can measure angles on the sky very precisely. If they know how big an object really is, the small-angle rule gives its distance: $d \approx D/\theta$. A galaxy about 100 000 light-years across that fills an angle of 0.001 rad must be about $10^{8}$ light-years away. The same idea, with the hot and cold spots in the oldest light in the universe as the "object", is one of the ways cosmologists measure the shape of space.
</Callout>

## Summary

- An angle in radians is arc over radius, $\theta = s/r$; a full turn is $2\pi$ rad = 360°, and $s = r\theta$.
- In a right-angled triangle, $\sin\theta$ = opposite/hypotenuse, $\cos\theta$ = adjacent/hypotenuse, $\tan\theta$ = opposite/adjacent; $\sin^{2}\theta + \cos^{2}\theta = 1$.
- On the unit circle, the point at angle $\theta$ is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$; sine and cosine are waves with period $2\pi$.
- For small angles in radians, $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta$, so an object of size $D$ at distance $d$ fills an angle $D/d$.
- Check your calculator's degree or radian mode.
