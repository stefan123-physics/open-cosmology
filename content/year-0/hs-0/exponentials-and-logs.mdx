Some quantities grow by **adding** the same amount in each step: a car at steady speed covers the same distance every second. Others grow by **multiplying** by the same factor: a population of cells that doubles every hour, money earning interest on its interest. The second kind is **exponential growth**, and it soon outruns any steady increase. Its reverse, **exponential decay**, describes radioactive atoms and much else. **Logarithms** are the tool for asking how many steps such growth or decay takes.

## Growth and decay by a factor

If a quantity starts at $a$ and is multiplied by a factor $b$ in each step, then after $x$ steps it is

<Formula label="Exponential function" tex="y = a\,b^{x}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 6.5, Functions of the form y = ab^x + q (with q = 0)" />

If $b > 1$ the quantity grows; if $b$ is between 0 and 1 it decays. A growth of 30% per step means a factor $b = 1.30$; a loss of 20% per step means $b = 0.80$. This is the same idea as Siyavula's growth formula: final value = initial value × (1 + growth rate)^(number of periods).

<WorkedExample title="Algae on a pond">
<Problem>
A patch of algae covers 50 m² of a pond and grows by 30% each week. How large is it after 6 weeks?
</Problem>
<Step title="The factor">
Growing by 30% means multiplying by 1.30 each week.
</Step>
<Step title="Six steps">
$50 \times 1.30^{6} = 50 \times 4.83 = 241$ m². The area has almost quintupled.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare with steady growth">
If the patch instead grew by a fixed 15 m² each week (30% of the starting 50 m²), it would cover only $50 + 6 \times 15 = 140$ m². Exponential growth pulls further ahead every week.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Doubling time and half-life.** For exponential growth, the time to double is always the same, wherever you start: that is a quick test for exponential behaviour. For decay, the time to halve is the **half-life**. After one half-life half is left, after two a quarter, after $n$ half-lives $(\tfrac12)^{n}$.

## Logarithms

Exponential growth raises the question: how many steps until the algae cover 1000 m²? That means solving $1.30^{x} = 20$ for an **exponent**, and the rules of earlier lessons cannot do it. The **logarithm** is defined exactly for this:

<Formula label="Definition of the logarithm" tex="y = \log_{b} x \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x = b^{y} \qquad (b > 0,\ b \ne 1,\ x > 0)" cite="mit" at="Sec. 2.6, Logarithms" />

In words: $\log_{b} x$ is the power you must raise $b$ to, to get $x$. So $\log_{2} 8 = 3$ because $2^{3} = 8$. The logarithm undoes the exponential, as a square root undoes squaring.

The most common base is 10, written just $\log$. The logarithm base 10 of a number is its power of ten from lesson 0.1: $\log 1000 = 3$, $\log 1 = 0$, $\log 0.01 = -2$. For numbers between the powers, it gives a decimal: $\log 2 \approx 0.301$, because $10^{0.301} \approx 2$. Exponents need not be whole numbers: you met $10^{1/2} = \sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$ in lesson 0.6, and a calculator gives any decimal power, such as $10^{0.301}$, in the same way. A decimal power lies between the whole powers on either side of it. Only positive numbers have a logarithm, since no power of a positive base is zero or negative.

## The laws of logarithms

Because logarithms are powers, the laws of exponents become laws of logarithms. Multiplying numbers adds their powers, so:

<Formula label="Laws of logarithms" tex="\log(xy) = \log x + \log y, \qquad \log\frac{x}{y} = \log x - \log y, \qquad \log\left(x^{n}\right) = n \log x" cite="reading3" at="Sec. 2.8, Laws of logarithms (the power law is in Sec. 2.6)" />

They hold for any base, as long as all logarithms use the same one. (Siyavula treats the first two as enrichment; they follow from $b^{p}\,b^{q} = b^{p+q}$ and $b^{p}/b^{q} = b^{p-q}$.) The third law is the one that solves for an exponent: it brings the exponent down in front.

<WorkedExample title="When does the algae cover 1000 m²?">
<Problem>
Starting from 50 m² and growing by 30% per week, when does the algae cover 1000 m²?
</Problem>
<Step title="Set up the equation">
$50 \times 1.30^{x} = 1000$, so $1.30^{x} = 20$.
</Step>
<Step title="Take the logarithm of both sides">
$\log(1.30^{x}) = \log 20$, and by the third law $x \log 1.30 = \log 20$.
</Step>
<Step title="Divide">
$x = \dfrac{\log 20}{\log 1.30} = \dfrac{1.301}{0.1139} = 11.4$ weeks.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
$1.30^{11.4} \approx 20$, and the graph above passes 1000 m² between weeks 11 and 12. (The same method gives a half-life: for decay by 20% per hour, $0.80^{x} = 0.5$ gives $x = \log 0.5 / \log 0.80 \approx 3.1$ hours.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Dividing in the last step of that example, $\log 20 / \log 1.30$, is an instance of the **change-of-base rule**: a logarithm in any base can be computed from logarithms in base 10.

<Formula label="Change of base" tex="\log_{b} x = \frac{\log x}{\log b}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 2.6, Laws of logarithms (change of base)" />

## The graph of the logarithm

Since the logarithm undoes the exponential, its graph is the graph of $10^{x}$ mirrored in the line $y = x$: every point $(p, q)$ on one becomes $(q, p)$ on the other. The logarithm grows ever more slowly, passes through $(1, 0)$ because $\log 1 = 0$, and does not exist for $x \le 0$.

## The number e and natural logarithms

One base appears everywhere in science: the number $e \approx 2.718$. The logarithm with base $e$ is the **natural logarithm**, written $\ln x$, so $\ln x = \log_{e} x$. Calculators have both a LOG key (base 10) and an LN key (base $e$); either works for solving equations like the one above, as long as you use the same one on both sides.

Why $e$? Growth that happens smoothly all the time, rather than in steps, is written most simply as a power of $e$. You will see in calculus why $e$ is the natural choice: the curve $y = e^{x}$ rises, at every point, at a rate equal to its own height.

## Logarithmic scales

When quantities range over many powers of ten, scientists often plot their logarithms instead. On a **logarithmic scale**, equal steps along the axis multiply the value by the same factor: each step from one mark to the next might be a factor of 10. An exponential curve becomes a straight line when you plot the logarithm of $y$ against $x$, because $\log(a\,b^{x}) = \log a + x \log b$ is linear in $x$ with slope $\log b$. And a power law $y = kx^{n}$ (lesson 0.6) becomes a straight line when you plot $\log y$ against $\log x$, with slope $n$. That is how physicists find the power in a power law from measurements.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
In the theory of **inflation**, the very early universe grew exponentially, doubling in size again and again in a tiny fraction of a second. In his first lecture of the cosmology course, Guth illustrates it with a patch of about $10^{-28}$ cm growing to the size of a marble, about 1 cm: a factor of $10^{28}$. How many doublings is that? Solve $2^{x} = 10^{28}$: $x = 28/\log 2 = 28/0.301 \approx 93$ doublings. Logarithms turn a mind-boggling factor into a modest count.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Exponential change multiplies by the same factor each step: $y = a\,b^{x}$, growth for $b > 1$ and decay for $0 < b < 1$. Doubling time and half-life stay the same throughout.
- $\log_{b} x$ is the power that turns $b$ into $x$: $y = \log_{b} x$ means $x = b^{y}$. $\log$ is base 10, $\ln$ is base $e \approx 2.718$.
- $\log(xy) = \log x + \log y$, $\log(x/y) = \log x - \log y$, $\log(x^{n}) = n\log x$.
- To solve for an exponent, take the logarithm of both sides and bring the exponent down.
- On logarithmic axes, exponentials and power laws become straight lines.
