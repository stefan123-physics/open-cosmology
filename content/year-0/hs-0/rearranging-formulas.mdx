Physics is full of formulas: distance equals speed times time, density equals mass divided by volume. A formula is written to give one quantity, but the question often asks for another. Then you have to **rearrange** it. This lesson gives you one rule that covers every case, and shows how to use it on equations with numbers and on formulas with letters.

## An equation is a balance

An **equation** says that two expressions are equal, such as $2x + 3 = 11$. Think of it as a balance with the two sides in the two pans. The balance stays level as long as you do **the same thing to both sides**. Two moves are always safe:

<Formula label="Moves that keep an equation true" tex="\text{if } P = Q \text{ then } P + C = Q + C, \quad \text{and, for } C \ne 0, \quad C\,P = C\,Q" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 0.3, Procedures which generate equivalent equations, p. 38" />

Here $P$ and $Q$ stand for the two sides and $C$ for any number. Adding includes subtracting (add a negative number), and multiplying includes dividing (multiply by $1/C$). The one forbidden move is multiplying or dividing by zero. Multiplying both sides by zero gives $0 = 0$, which is true but has lost all the information; dividing by zero has no meaning at all.

Solving an equation means using these moves until the unknown stands alone on one side. Each move gives an equation with the same solution, so the last one, $x = \ldots$, gives the answer to the first.

## Solving a linear equation

An equation is **linear** in $x$ when it can be rearranged into the form (number) × $x$ = (number), with a number in front of $x$ that is not zero. In practice: once brackets are multiplied out and fractions cleared, $x$ appears only to the first power, with no $x^{2}$ left over and no $x$ under a root or in a denominator. Stitz and Zeager give a two-step strategy for it:

1. Move all terms with $x$ to one side and all other terms to the other side.
2. Take $x$ out as a common factor, then divide both sides by the number in front of it (its **coefficient**).

<WorkedExample title="Unknowns on both sides">
<Problem>
Solve $5x - 7 = 2x + 8$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Collect the x terms">
Subtract $2x$ from both sides: $3x - 7 = 8$. Add 7 to both sides: $3x = 15$.
</Step>
<Step title="Divide by the coefficient">
Divide both sides by 3: $x = 5$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Put $x = 5$ back into the original equation: the left side is $5 \cdot 5 - 7 = 18$ and the right side is $2 \cdot 5 + 8 = 18$. Both sides agree.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Brackets and fractions need one step first: multiply out the brackets, and clear the fractions by multiplying both sides by the denominator.

<WorkedExample title="A fraction">
<Problem>
Solve $\dfrac{x + 6}{4} = x - 3$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Clear the fraction">
Multiply both sides by 4. On the right, the 4 multiplies both terms: $x + 6 = 4x - 12$.
</Step>
<Step title="Collect and divide">
Subtract $x$ and add 12 on both sides: $18 = 3x$. Divide by 3: $x = 6$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Left: $(6 + 6)/4 = 3$. Right: $6 - 3 = 3$. They agree.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Changing the subject of a formula

A formula is an equation with letters for quantities. The quantity on its own on the left is its **subject**. To make another quantity the subject, use exactly the same moves, treating the other letters as if they were numbers.

A useful way to see what to do: look at how the quantity you want has been built up, then **undo the steps in reverse order**. Siyavula's method for physics formulas is the same: multiply both sides to remove a quantity from a denominator, then divide both sides by whatever is still attached to the quantity you want.

<WorkedExample title="Time from distance and speed">
<Problem>
The average speed is $v = d/t$, the distance $d$ divided by the time $t$. Make $t$ the subject, and find the time to cover 450 km at 90 km/h.
</Problem>
<Step title="Remove t from the denominator">
Multiply both sides by $t$: $v\,t = d$.
</Step>
<Step title="Divide by what is attached to t">
Divide both sides by $v$: $t = \dfrac{d}{v}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Use it">
$t = \dfrac{450\ \text{km}}{90\ \text{km/h}} = 5.0$ h.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with units">
Kilometres divided by kilometres per hour gives hours, a time. The units would have caught the wrong answer $t = v\,d$, which comes out in km²/h.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The quantity you want may also sit inside a sum. A cyclist who starts at position $x_0$ and rides at a steady speed $v$ is, after a time $t$, at position $x = x_0 + v\,t$.

<WorkedExample title="When does the cyclist get there?">
<Problem>
Make $t$ the subject of $x = x_0 + v\,t$, and find when a cyclist who starts at 20 m and rides at 6.0 m/s reaches 80 m.
</Problem>
<Step title="Undo the addition">
$t$ was first multiplied by $v$, then $x_0$ was added. Undo in reverse: subtract $x_0$ from both sides, $x - x_0 = v\,t$.
</Step>
<Step title="Undo the multiplication">
Divide both sides by $v$: $t = \dfrac{x - x_0}{v}$. The whole of $x - x_0$ is divided.
</Step>
<Step title="Use it and check">
$t = (80 - 20)\ \text{m} / 6.0\ \text{m/s} = 10$ s. Check: $20 + 6.0 \times 10 = 80$ m. The units work too: metres over metres per second gives seconds.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

A formula can need several steps. The area of a circle is $A = \pi r^{2}$. Here $r$ was first squared and then multiplied by $\pi$, so undo in reverse: divide by $\pi$, then take the square root.

$A = \pi r^{2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r^{2} = \dfrac{A}{\pi} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \sqrt{\dfrac{A}{\pi}}$.

The **square root** $\sqrt{a}$ of a positive number $a$ is the positive number whose square is $a$: $\sqrt{25} = 5$, because $5^{2} = 25$. Taking the square root undoes squaring. But $(-5)^{2} = 25$ as well, so an equation such as $r^{2} = 25$ has two solutions, 5 and $-5$. (Quadratics come back in lesson 0.6.)

Strictly, then, $r^{2} = A/\pi$ has two solutions, a positive and a negative square root. A radius is a length, so only the positive one makes sense. Always ask which solutions are physical.

## Common mistakes

- **Doing a step to only part of a side.** In $y = 3x + 6$, subtracting 6 gives $3x = y - 6$, and dividing by 3 must divide all of $y - 6$: $x = (y - 6)/3$, not $y - 6/3$. Brackets help.
- **Losing a sign.** Moving a term to the other side changes its sign, because you subtract it from both sides.
- **Dividing by something that can be zero.** If you divide by a letter, check that it cannot be zero in the problem.
- **Not checking.** Substitute your answer back in, or check the units: both sides of a correct formula have the same units (lesson 0.2).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
In the 1920s astronomers found that distant galaxies move away from us, faster the farther they are. **Hubble's law** says the speed $v$ is the distance $d$ times a number $H$, the Hubble constant: $v = H\,d$. Speeds are easy to measure, distances are hard. Rearranged, $d = v/H$ turns a measured speed into a distance. With $H$ = 70 km/s/Mpc, a round value close to the measured ones (km/s per megaparsec, where a megaparsec is about 3.26 million light-years), a galaxy moving away at 7000 km/s is $d = 7000/70 = 100$ Mpc away. You will meet this law again in the cosmology course.
</Callout>

## Summary

- An equation stays true when you add, subtract, multiply or divide both sides by the same thing (never multiply or divide by zero).
- To solve a linear equation, collect the unknown on one side, then divide by its coefficient; check by substituting.
- To change the subject of a formula, undo what was done to the wanted quantity in reverse order, on both sides.
- Watch the whole side, the signs and possible division by zero, keep only physical solutions, and check the units.
