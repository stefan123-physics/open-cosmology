Some quantities are completely described by a number and a unit: a mass of 2.0 kg, a time of 30 s, a temperature of 293 K. These are **scalars**. Others also need a direction. Walking 3 km north takes you somewhere quite different from walking 3 km east, and a push to the left has the opposite effect of a push to the right. Quantities with a size and a direction are **vectors**. **Displacement**, the change of position together with its direction (3 km north), is the simplest example; velocity, acceleration and force are all vectors, so almost every later block uses this lesson.

## Arrows and notation

A vector is drawn as an **arrow**. Its length shows the size of the vector, called its **magnitude**, and the arrowhead shows the direction. The start of the arrow is its **tail**, the end its **head**.

In writing, a vector gets a small arrow over its symbol: $\vec F$ for a force, $\vec v$ for a velocity. Its magnitude is written without the arrow, $F$, and is never negative.

Directions can be given in several ways: relative words (left, up), compass directions (north, 30° east of north), or, as in this course, an **angle measured anticlockwise from the positive $x$-axis**, as on the unit circle of lesson 0.7.

The two axes divide the plane into four **quadrants**, numbered anticlockwise: the first quadrant has $x$ and $y$ both positive (angles 0° to 90°), the second $x$ negative and $y$ positive (90° to 180°), the third both negative (180° to 270°) and the fourth $x$ positive and $y$ negative (270° to 360°).

Two vectors are **equal** if they have the same magnitude and the same direction, wherever they are drawn. The **negative** of a vector, $-\vec A$, has the same magnitude and points the opposite way.

## Adding vectors

The sum of two or more vectors is called their **resultant**. To add vectors by drawing, use the **head-to-tail** method: draw the first arrow, put the tail of the second at the head of the first, and so on. The resultant is the arrow from the tail of the first to the head of the last.

The order does not matter: $\vec A + \vec B = \vec B + \vec A$. Drawing both orders from the same starting point gives a parallelogram whose diagonal is the resultant, which is why this is also called the **parallelogram method**.

Two special cases need no drawing:

- **Same line.** Choose one direction as positive. Then vectors along the line add like signed numbers: 7 N to the right plus 3 N to the left is $+7 + (-3) = +4$ N, that is 4 N to the right.
- **At right angles.** The two vectors and their resultant form a right-angled triangle, so Pythagoras gives the magnitude and trigonometry the angle.

**Subtracting** a vector means adding its negative: $\vec A - \vec B = \vec A + (-\vec B)$. **Multiplying** a vector by a number $k$ stretches its length by $k$; a negative number also turns it around.

## Components

Drawing is slow and not very precise. The precise method splits every vector into two perpendicular parts, along the $x$- and $y$-axes. These parts are its **components**. A vector $\vec R$ with magnitude $R$ at an angle $\theta$ from the positive $x$-axis is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle whose other sides are its components:

<Formula label="Components of a vector" tex="R_x = R\cos\theta, \qquad R_y = R\sin\theta" cite="mit" at="Sec. 1.3, Components of vectors" />

<VectorDiagram
  vectors={[{ x: 5, y: 2.5, label: "\\vec R" }]}
  components={[0]}
  angle={0}
  caption="A vector and its two components (dashed), drawn from the numbers: here Rx = 5.0, Ry = 2.5, so R ≈ 5.6 and θ ≈ 26.6°."
/>

Components carry signs: a vector pointing partly to the left has a negative $R_x$, one pointing partly down a negative $R_y$. With the angle measured from the positive $x$-axis, the cosine and sine supply these signs automatically.

Going back, Pythagoras gives the magnitude and the tangent gives the angle:

<Formula label="Magnitude and direction from components" tex="R = \sqrt{R_x^{2} + R_y^{2}}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{R_y}{R_x}" cite="reading4" at="Sec. 1.2, Magnitude of the resultant of vectors at right angles" />

<Callout kind="warning" title="Check the quadrant">
A calculator's $\tan^{-1}$ only gives angles between $-90°$ and $+90°$. The vectors $(5, 2)$ and $(-5, -2)$ have the same ratio $R_y/R_x$ but point in opposite directions. Always look at the signs of the components: if $R_x$ is negative, add 180° to the calculator's answer.
</Callout>

## Adding vectors with components

Here is the reason components are so useful: **adding vectors is the same as adding their components separately**. The $x$-components add up to the $x$-component of the resultant, and the same for $y$. Arrows become arithmetic.

<WorkedExample title="A sailing trip">
<Problem>
A boat sails 8.0 km in a direction 20° from the positive $x$-axis (east), then turns and sails 5.0 km at 150°. How far is it from its starting point, and in which direction?
</Problem>
<Step title="Components of each leg">
First leg: $8.0\cos 20° = 7.52$ km and $8.0\sin 20° = 2.74$ km.
Second leg: $5.0\cos 150° = -4.33$ km and $5.0\sin 150° = 2.50$ km. The negative $x$-component means the second leg heads partly west.
</Step>
<Step title="Add component by component">
$R_x = 7.52 + (-4.33) = 3.19$ km and $R_y = 2.74 + 2.50 = 5.24$ km.
</Step>
<Step title="Magnitude and direction">
$R = \sqrt{3.19^{2} + 5.24^{2}} = 6.13$ km. Both components are positive, so the angle is in the first quadrant: $\theta = \tan^{-1}(5.24/3.19) = 58.7°$. The distances are given to two significant figures, so the boat ends about 6.1 km from its start, in a direction 59° from east.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
The resultant is shorter than the two legs together (13 km), as it must be when the boat turns back partly. A rough sketch shows the end point up and to the right of the start, matching 58.7°.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
When astronomers measure how fast a distant galaxy moves away, their telescope is riding on the Earth, which circles the Sun, which circles the centre of our galaxy, which itself moves through space. Each of these motions is a velocity vector, and our total velocity is their vector sum. The light from a galaxy reveals only its speed along the line of sight, so astronomers correct it for the component of our own total velocity along that same line: the components of this lesson, with the line of sight as the axis.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Scalars have only a size; vectors have a size and a direction and are drawn as arrows, written $\vec F$ with magnitude $F$.
- Vectors add head to tail; the order does not matter. Subtracting means adding the negative.
- A vector of size $R$ at angle $\theta$ has components $R_x = R\cos\theta$ and $R_y = R\sin\theta$.
- To add vectors, add their components; then $R = \sqrt{R_x^{2} + R_y^{2}}$ and $\tan\theta = R_y/R_x$, checking the quadrant from the signs.
