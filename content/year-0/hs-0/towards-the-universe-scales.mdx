Every lesson in this block was a tool: powers of ten, units, estimates, formulas, graphs, angles, vectors, logarithms, rates. This last lesson of the block puts them to work on the biggest question there is: how large is the universe, and what is it doing? You will meet each of these ideas again in the cosmology course at the end of the programme.

## A ladder of sizes

Here are some lengths in the universe, each in metres and in scientific notation. Moving down the table the lengths grow, often by huge factors.

| Object | Size or distance | In metres |
|---|---|---|
| A proton (radius) | 0.84 femtometres (1 fm = $10^{-15}$ m) | $8.4 \times 10^{-16}$ |
| A hydrogen atom (the Bohr radius) | about 0.053 nanometres | $5.3 \times 10^{-11}$ |
| A person (height) | about 1.7 m | $1.7 \times 10^{0}$ |
| The Earth (diameter) | 12 756 km | $1.28 \times 10^{7}$ |
| Earth to Moon | 384 400 km | $3.84 \times 10^{8}$ |
| The Sun (diameter) | about 1.4 million km | $1.4 \times 10^{9}$ |
| Earth to Sun | about 150 million km | $1.50 \times 10^{11}$ |
| The nearest star, Proxima Centauri | 4.24 light-years | $4.01 \times 10^{16}$ |
| The Milky Way's disk of stars | more than 100 000 light-years | $9.5 \times 10^{20}$ |
| The Andromeda galaxy | 2.5 million light-years | $2.4 \times 10^{22}$ |
| The Hubble distance (see below) | about 14 billion light-years | $1.3 \times 10^{26}$ |

The proton and atom sizes are CODATA values from NIST; the planets, Sun, stars and galaxies are NASA's figures. From the proton to the last row is a factor of about $10^{26}/10^{-15} = 10^{41}$: forty-one powers of ten. No ruler covers that range. Scientific notation (lesson 0.1) and orders of magnitude (lesson 0.3) are the only way to think about it.

Notice also how empty the universe is. The Sun is 1.4 million km across, but the nearest star is about 30 million times that distance away. Galaxies, by contrast, are packed closer: Andromeda is only about 25 Milky Way widths from us.

## The light-year and looking back in time

Beyond the solar system, astronomers measure distance in **light-years**: the distance light travels in one year, $9.46 \times 10^{15}$ m (lesson 0.2). A light-year is a distance, but it also says how long the light was on its way. That makes every telescope a time machine:

- Moonlight left the Moon $3.84 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m} / 3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s ≈ 1.3 s ago.
- Sunlight left the Sun about 500 s, or 8.3 minutes, ago (lesson 0.1).
- The light of Proxima Centauri left it 4.24 years ago.
- The light of the Andromeda galaxy left it 2.5 million years ago, before there were any modern humans to see it.

The farther we look, the further back in time we see. The most distant light we can receive left its source not long after the universe began. The universe is around 13.8 billion years old. That sets a horizon: we cannot see anything whose light has not had time to reach us. The part of the universe within that horizon is the **observable universe**.

## A scale model

Numbers this large are hard to picture, so shrink everything by the same factor.

<WorkedExample title="The solar system on a football field, and beyond">
<Problem>
Shrink the solar system so that the Sun becomes a ball 1.0 m across. How large is the Earth, how far is it from the Sun, and how far away is Proxima Centauri?
</Problem>
<Step title="The scale factor">
The real Sun is $1.4 \times 10^{9}$ m across, so every length must be divided by $1.4 \times 10^{9}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The Earth">
Its diameter becomes $1.28 \times 10^{7} / 1.4 \times 10^{9} = 9.1 \times 10^{-3}$ m, about 9 mm: a peanut. Its distance from the Sun becomes $1.50 \times 10^{11} / 1.4 \times 10^{9} \approx 107$ m, about the length of a football field.
</Step>
<Step title="The nearest star">
Proxima Centauri's distance becomes $4.01 \times 10^{16} / 1.4 \times 10^{9} = 2.9 \times 10^{7}$ m, about 29 000 km. That is almost three quarters of the way around the real Earth, whose circumference is about 40 000 km.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Proxima is $4.01 \times 10^{16} / 1.50 \times 10^{11} \approx 2.7 \times 10^{5}$ times farther away than the Sun, and $2.9 \times 10^{7}/107 \approx 2.7 \times 10^{5}$ as well. A scale model keeps all ratios, as it should.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The expanding universe

In the 1920s astronomers measured the speeds of galaxies from their light and found something unexpected: nearly all of them move away from us, and the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it recedes. This is **Hubble's law**, which you have used in lessons 0.4 and 0.5:

<Formula label="Hubble's law" tex="v = H\,d" cite="guth4" at="slide 1" />

Here $v$ is the speed at which a galaxy moves away, $d$ its distance (Guth writes $r$), and $H$ the Hubble constant, about 70 km/s per megaparsec (a megaparsec is about 3.26 million light-years).

Does this mean we are at the centre of an explosion? No. Imagine that every distance between galaxies is stretched by the same factor, say doubled, in a certain time. A galaxy that was 1 unit away is now 2 units away: it moved 1 unit. One that was 3 units away is now 6 units away: it moved 3 units in the same time, so three times as fast. Speed proportional to distance is just what uniform stretching gives. And it looks the same from **every** galaxy: each sees all the others moving away according to the same law. There is no centre. This is the **homogeneous expansion** that the cosmology course starts from.

The Hubble constant also gives a time. Dividing a distance by its speed, $d/v = 1/H$, is how long the galaxies would have taken to get where they are at their present speeds. For $H$ = 70 km/s/Mpc,

$\dfrac{1}{H} \approx 4.4 \times 10^{17}\ \text{s} \approx 14$ billion years,

the **Hubble time**. It is close to the age of the universe, 13.8 billion years. That the two are of the same size is expected: running the expansion backwards brings all galaxies together at about that time in the past. That they agree as closely as they do today is partly a coincidence of our era, because the expansion has not always gone at the same rate; the cosmology course examines it. Light travelling for one Hubble time covers $c/H \approx 1.3 \times 10^{26}$ m, where $c$ is the usual symbol for the speed of light, the **Hubble distance** in the table, a rough measure of the size of the observable universe. (The exact size is larger, because space kept expanding while the light was on its way; the cosmology course works it out.)

## Summary

- Lengths in the universe span more than forty powers of ten, from the proton (about $10^{-15}$ m) to the observable universe (about $10^{26}$ m).
- A light-year is a distance, $9.46 \times 10^{15}$ m; looking far away means looking back in time. The universe is about 13.8 billion years old, which sets the edge of the observable universe.
- A scale model divides every length by the same factor and keeps all ratios.
- Hubble's law, $v = H\,d$: galaxies recede at speeds proportional to their distance, because every distance grows by the same factor. There is no centre.
- The Hubble time $1/H \approx 14$ billion years is close to the age of the universe.
