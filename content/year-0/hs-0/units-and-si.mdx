A number on its own says little. "The table is 2 long" could mean 2 metres or 2 kilometres. A physical quantity is always **a number times a unit**: 2 m, 3.5 kg, 60 s. The unit says what was counted, and the number says how many.

This lesson is about the units science uses, the **SI system**, and how to change from one unit to another without mistakes.

## Why units matter

Units are not a formality. In 1999 NASA lost its Mars Climate Orbiter, a probe that had flown to Mars for about ten months. The software of one team gave the pushes of the probe's small steering thrusters in an imperial unit, based on the pound-force, while the navigation software that used the numbers expected SI units, based on the newton. (Force, a push or a pull, and its unit the newton come in Block 2.) One pound-force is about 4.45 newtons, so every push was misjudged by more than a factor of four. The probe came in too low over Mars and was lost.

The lesson: always write the unit next to the number, and carry the units through every step of a calculation.

## The SI base units

To avoid such mix-ups, scientists everywhere use one agreed set of units, the **International System of Units**, or **SI** (from its French name, *Système international d'unités*). It is built on seven **base units**:

| Quantity | Unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| length | metre | m |
| mass | kilogram | kg |
| time | second | s |
| electric current | ampere | A |
| temperature | kelvin | K |
| amount of substance | mole | mol |
| luminous intensity | candela | cd |

Every other unit in physics is built from these seven. Two things to notice:

- The base unit of mass is the **kilogram**, not the gram. It is the only base unit with a prefix in its name.
- Unit symbols are written in lower case, except for units named after people: A (after André-Marie Ampère) and K (after Lord Kelvin), and the same goes for the newton, N, below. The unit names themselves are written in lower case: ampere, kelvin.

In this High School you will mostly need the first five. The mole appears with gases, and the candela not at all.

## Units built from base units

Most quantities are combinations. Speed is a distance divided by a time, so its unit is metres per second, m/s (also written m·s⁻¹, where the dot means "times"). Area is a length times a length, m². A volume is m³, and density, mass per volume, is kg/m³.

Some combinations are used so often that they have their own name:

<Formula label="Named SI units" tex="1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg·m/s}^{2}, \qquad 1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{kg·m}^{2}\text{/s}^{2}, \qquad 1\ \text{Hz} = 1\ \text{s}^{-1}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 1.3, Combinations of SI base units, Table 1.2" />

The newton (N) is the unit of force, the joule (J) of energy and work, and the hertz (Hz) of frequency: how many times per second something happens. You will meet each in its own lesson. For now, the point is that each is just base units in disguise.

The prefixes from the previous lesson work on every unit: 5 kN is $5 \times 10^{3}$ N, and 2 MHz is $2 \times 10^{6}$ Hz.

## Converting units

To change a unit, multiply by a **conversion factor**: a fraction whose top and bottom are the same quantity in different units. Since 1 km and 1000 m are equal,

<Formula label="A conversion factor" tex="\frac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}} = 1" cite="mit" at="Sec. 1.3, How to change units" />

Multiplying by 1 never changes a quantity, only how it is written. (Siyavula shows metric conversions as steps of ×1000 or ÷1000 on a diagram. The factor method used here gives the same results and also works for mixed units such as km/h.) Choose the fraction that way up which makes the old unit cancel, just as numbers cancel in a fraction:

$2.5\ \text{km} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}} = 2500\ \text{m}$.

The km on top and the km below cancel, leaving metres. If you pick the fraction the wrong way up, the units come out as km²/m: that tells you at once to turn it over.

<WorkedExample title="Motorway speed in metres per second">
<Problem>
A car drives at 90 km/h. What is that in m/s?
</Problem>
<Step title="Two factors, one for each unit">
Kilometres must become metres and hours must become seconds. One hour is $60 \times 60 = 3600$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Multiply and cancel">
$90\ \dfrac{\text{km}}{\text{h}} \times \dfrac{1000\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{km}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{h}}{3600\ \text{s}} = \dfrac{90 \times 1000}{3600}\ \dfrac{\text{m}}{\text{s}} = 25\ \text{m/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Since $1000/3600 = 1/3.6$, any speed in km/h becomes m/s by dividing by 3.6, and $90/3.6 = 25$. A speed in m/s is always the smaller number, because $1000/3600$ is less than 1: a metre is 1000 times shorter than a kilometre, but a second is 3600 times shorter than an hour.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Squares and cubes.** When a unit is squared, its conversion factor is squared too. One metre is 100 cm, so

$1\ \text{m}^{2} = (100\ \text{cm})^{2} = 10^{4}\ \text{cm}^{2}, \qquad 1\ \text{m}^{3} = (100\ \text{cm})^{3} = 10^{6}\ \text{cm}^{3}$.

A floor of 12 m² is therefore $12 \times 10^{4} = 1.2 \times 10^{5}$ cm², not 1200 cm². Forgetting to square the factor is one of the most common unit mistakes.

<WorkedExample title="The density of water in SI units">
<Problem>
Water has a density of about 1.0 g/cm³. Write this in kg/m³.
</Problem>
<Step title="Grams to kilograms">
$1.0\ \dfrac{\text{g}}{\text{cm}^{3}} \times \dfrac{1\ \text{kg}}{1000\ \text{g}} = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \dfrac{\text{kg}}{\text{cm}^{3}}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Cubic centimetres to cubic metres">
There are $10^{6}$ cm³ in one m³, so multiply by $10^{6}\ \text{cm}^{3}/1\ \text{m}^{3}$:
$1.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 10^{6}\ \text{kg/m}^{3} = 1.0 \times 10^{3}$ kg/m³.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
A cube of water one metre on each side holds 1000 litres and has a mass of about 1000 kg, a tonne. That matches.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Temperature

The SI unit of temperature is the **kelvin** (K). One kelvin is the same step as one degree Celsius, but the kelvin scale starts at **absolute zero**, the lowest temperature possible, which is about −273 °C. To convert:

<Formula label="Celsius to kelvin" tex="\text{temperature in K} = \text{temperature in }{}^{\circ}\text{C} + 273" cite="mit" at="Sec. 1.3, How to change units" />

A room at 20 °C is at $20 + 273 = 293$ K. The exact number to add is 273.15, but 273 is accurate enough for nearly all calculations in this course. Temperatures in kelvin are written without a degree sign, and they are never negative.

## Checking a formula with units

Units also catch mistakes in formulas. Two rules:

1. You can only add, subtract or compare quantities with the **same units**. Adding 3 m to 5 s means nothing.
2. Both sides of a correct formula have the **same units**.

So if you are unsure whether a distance is (speed) × (time) or (speed) × (time)², check the units. The first gives (m/s) × s = m, a length. The second gives (m/s) × s² = m·s, which is not a length at all, so it cannot be right. A unit check cannot prove a formula correct (a missing factor 2 has no unit), but it can prove one wrong, and it is quick.

## Other units you will meet

SI is not the only system:

- **Imperial units** (miles, pounds, gallons) are still used in the United States and partly in the United Kingdom. Convert them to SI before calculating.
- The **centimetre-gram-second (CGS)** system uses the centimetre and the gram instead of the metre and the kilogram. Some astronomy and atomic physics books still use it.
- In **natural units**, physicists set certain constants, such as the speed of light, equal to 1. This simplifies the equations of particle physics and of the early universe. You will meet them at university.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Astronomical distances are so large that astronomers also use their own units. A **light-year** is the distance light travels in one year: about $2.998 \times 10^{8}$ m/s times $3.156 \times 10^{7}$ s (one year in seconds), which is $9.46 \times 10^{15}$ m. It is a distance, not a time. Cosmologists measure the distances between galaxies in units of millions of light-years or more, and they often convert back to metres or seconds to calculate, using exactly the method of this lesson.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A quantity is a number times a unit; always write and carry the unit.
- SI has seven base units: m, kg, s, A, K, mol and cd. All other units, such as m/s, N, J and Hz, are built from them.
- To convert, multiply by factors equal to one, chosen so the old unit cancels. Square or cube the factor when the unit is squared or cubed.
- km/h to m/s: divide by 3.6. Celsius to kelvin: add 273.
- Both sides of a correct formula have the same units; a unit mismatch proves a formula wrong.
