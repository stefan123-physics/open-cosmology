Physics deals with numbers of every size. Sunlight travels about 300 000 000 metres in one second. A grain of fine dust is perhaps 0.000 01 metres across. Writing out all those zeros is slow, and it is easy to lose one. Lose a single zero and the answer is wrong by a factor of ten.

This lesson gives you a short way to write such numbers, **scientific notation**, and shows how to calculate with it. Every later lesson uses it, and in cosmology, where distances in metres run to more than twenty digits, nothing else would work.

## Powers of ten

A **power of ten** is 10 multiplied by itself a number of times. The number being multiplied, here 10, is called the **base**. The small raised number, the **exponent** (or power), says how many tens are multiplied:

- $10^{1} = 10$
- $10^{2} = 10 \times 10 = 100$
- $10^{3} = 10 \times 10 \times 10 = 1000$
- $10^{6} = 1\,000\,000$ (a one followed by six zeros)

For a positive exponent there is a quick rule: $10^{n}$ is a 1 followed by $n$ zeros.

Going down the list, each step divides by 10. Keep going past $10^{1}$ and the pattern gives the meaning of zero and negative exponents:

- $10^{0} = 10^{1} / 10 = 1$
- $10^{-1} = 1 / 10 = 0.1$
- $10^{-2} = 0.1 / 10 = 0.01$
- $10^{-3} = 0.001$

So $10^{0} = 1$, and a negative exponent means "one divided by": $10^{-n} = 1/10^{n}$. A negative power of ten is a small number, not a negative one. $10^{-3}$ is 0.001, which is still greater than zero.

## The rules for exponents

Powers with the same base follow a few rules. Siyavula's mathematics book lists them for any positive base $a$ and $b$:

<Formula label="Laws of exponents" tex="a^{m} \times a^{n} = a^{m+n}, \qquad \frac{a^{m}}{a^{n}} = a^{m-n}, \qquad \left(a^{m}\right)^{n} = a^{mn}, \qquad (ab)^{n} = a^{n} b^{n}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 2.2, list of exponent laws" />

You do not need to memorise them blindly. With base 10 they are just counting tens. In $10^{2} \times 10^{3}$ there are two tens times three tens, five tens in all, so $10^{2} \times 10^{3} = 10^{5}$. In $10^{5} / 10^{2}$, two of the five tens cancel, leaving $10^{3}$. The rules work for negative exponents too: $10^{4} \times 10^{-6} = 10^{4 + (-6)} = 10^{-2}$.

The division rule also shows again why $10^{0} = 1$: any number divided by itself is 1, and $10^{3} / 10^{3} = 10^{3-3} = 10^{0}$.

## Scientific notation

A number in **scientific notation** is written as a number between 1 and 10, times a power of ten:

<Formula label="Scientific notation" tex="a \times 10^{n}, \qquad 1 \le a < 10, \quad n \text{ a whole number}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 1.2, Scientific notation" />

The exponent $n$ can be positive, negative or zero. The number $a$ in front carries the digits; the power of ten carries the size. A negative number keeps its minus sign in front: $-4\,200 = -4.2 \times 10^{3}$.

(Siyavula's general description says only that the number in front lies between 0 and 10, but its method of conversion moves the point until the number is between 1 and 10. That stricter form, $1 \le a < 10$, is the usual standard and the one used here.)

**To write a number in scientific notation**, move the decimal point until exactly one non-zero digit stands in front of it. Count the places you moved it:

- If the original number was **large** (you moved the point to the left), $n$ is that count, positive.
- If the original number was **small**, less than 1 (you moved the point to the right), $n$ is minus that count.

To go back, move the point the other way: right for a positive $n$, left for a negative one.

(Siyavula, a South African book, writes a decimal comma: 3,24 rather than 3.24, as is also usual in the Netherlands. This site uses the decimal point, as most science writing in English does.)

<WorkedExample title="Converting both ways">
<Problem>
Write 52 000 000 and 0.000 078 in scientific notation, and write $9.1 \times 10^{-3}$ as an ordinary decimal.
</Problem>
<Step title="A large number">
In 52 000 000 the decimal point sits after the last zero. Moving it 7 places to the left gives 5.2, so $52\,000\,000 = 5.2 \times 10^{7}$.
</Step>
<Step title="A small number">
In 0.000 078 the point moves 5 places to the right to give 7.8, so $0.000\,078 = 7.8 \times 10^{-5}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Back to a decimal">
The power is $-3$, so the point in 9.1 moves 3 places to the left: $9.1 \times 10^{-3} = 0.0091$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
A positive power gave a number bigger than 10 and a negative power a number smaller than 1, as they should. Counting digits once more: 5.2 followed by 7 places is 52 000 000.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Multiplying and dividing

Multiplication and division are where scientific notation pays off. Deal with the numbers in front and the powers of ten separately:

<Formula label="Multiplying and dividing in scientific notation" tex="(a \times 10^{m}) \times (b \times 10^{n}) = (a \times b) \times 10^{m+n}, \qquad \frac{a \times 10^{m}}{b \times 10^{n}} = \frac{a}{b} \times 10^{m-n}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 1.2, Scientific notation, Worked example 2" />

The product $a \times b$ may come out bigger than 10, and the quotient $a/b$ may come out smaller than 1. Then rewrite the result so the number in front is between 1 and 10 again, changing the power to match. For example, $(3.0 \times 10^{4}) \times (5.0 \times 10^{2}) = 15 \times 10^{6} = 1.5 \times 10^{7}$, because $15 = 1.5 \times 10^{1}$.

<WorkedExample title="How old is sunlight?">
<Problem>
The Sun is about $1.50 \times 10^{11}$ m from Earth, and light travels at $3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s. How long does sunlight take to reach us?
</Problem>
<Step title="Set up the division">
The speed of light, $3.00 \times 10^{8}$ m/s (metres per second), says how many metres the light covers in each second; units like this are the subject of the next lesson. At a steady speed, the time taken is the distance divided by the speed:

$t = \dfrac{1.50 \times 10^{11}\ \text{m}}{3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers and powers separately">
$1.50 / 3.00 = 0.500$ and $10^{11} / 10^{8} = 10^{11 - 8} = 10^{3}$, so $t = 0.500 \times 10^{3}$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Back into scientific notation">
$0.500 = 5.00 \times 10^{-1}$, so $t = 5.00 \times 10^{-1} \times 10^{3} = 5.00 \times 10^{2}$ s, which is 500 seconds, or about 8.3 minutes.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
Metres divided by metres per second leaves seconds, a time, as it should. The light you see from the Sun left it about 8 minutes ago.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The distance is the rounded value of 150 million kilometres on NASA's Sun facts page; the speed of light is exactly 299 792 458 m/s, rounded here to three digits.

## Adding and subtracting

Adding needs one extra step. You can only add the numbers in front when the powers of ten are the **same**, just as you can only add tens to tens and hundreds to hundreds. So first rewrite one of the numbers:

$4.2 \times 10^{5} + 3.1 \times 10^{4} = 4.2 \times 10^{5} + 0.31 \times 10^{5} = 4.51 \times 10^{5}$.

Making the number in front ten times smaller (3.1 to 0.31) and the power ten times bigger ($10^{4}$ to $10^{5}$) leaves the value unchanged. Subtracting works the same way.

Notice what happens when the powers are far apart. In $5.0 \times 10^{8} + 2.0 \times 10^{2}$, the second number is less than a millionth of the first, and adding it changes nothing you would write down with a few digits. In physics, adding a very small quantity to a very large one often means the small one can be ignored.

## Orders of magnitude

The **order of magnitude** of a number is, roughly, the power of ten it is closest to. A simple way to find it: write the number in scientific notation and look at the power of ten. A school day of 6 hours lasts 21 600 s, which is $2.16 \times 10^{4}$ s: of order $10^{4}$ seconds, or "tens of thousands of seconds". (When the number in front is above about 3, some people round up to the next power instead. Either way, an order of magnitude is only a rough size.) Two quantities differ by an order of magnitude when one is about ten times the other, and by three orders of magnitude when the ratio is about a thousand.

Orders of magnitude are how physicists compare sizes quickly. Siyavula's chapter on the atom pictures it like this: if an atom were as big as a sports stadium, its nucleus would be about the size of a pea. A stadium is roughly $10^{2}$ m across and a pea roughly $10^{-2}$ m. Dividing,

$\dfrac{10^{2}}{10^{-2}} = 10^{2-(-2)} = 10^{4}$,

so in this picture the atom is about ten thousand times wider than its nucleus: four orders of magnitude. (Measurements put the real ratio between about $10^{4}$ and $10^{5}$, depending on the atom, so the picture gets the order of magnitude about right.)

## Prefixes

For everyday use, scientists put a **prefix** in front of a unit instead of writing a power of ten. The prefix stands for the power:

| Prefix | Symbol | Power of ten |
|---|---|---|
| tera | T | $10^{12}$ |
| giga | G | $10^{9}$ |
| mega | M | $10^{6}$ |
| kilo | k | $10^{3}$ |
| hecto | h | $10^{2}$ |
| deca | da | $10^{1}$ |
| deci | d | $10^{-1}$ |
| centi | c | $10^{-2}$ |
| milli | m | $10^{-3}$ |
| micro | μ | $10^{-6}$ |
| nano | n | $10^{-9}$ |
| pico | p | $10^{-12}$ |

Siyavula's units section lists these and more; the ones from giga down to nano are the ones to know by heart. A few things to watch:

- **Capitals matter.** M is mega ($10^{6}$), m is milli ($10^{-3}$): a factor of $10^{9}$ apart.
- The prefix is written straight against the unit symbol, with no space or dot: km, ms, μm.
- To convert, replace the prefix by its power of ten. So 32 km $= 32 \times 10^{3}$ m $= 3.2 \times 10^{4}$ m, and 4.5 ms $= 4.5 \times 10^{-3}$ s.

The units themselves, metres, seconds, kilograms and the rest, are the subject of the next lesson.

## On a calculator

Calculators and computers show powers of ten with the letter E (for "exponent"): `3.00E8` means $3.00 \times 10^{8}$, and `5.2E-5` means $5.2 \times 10^{-5}$. To enter $1.50 \times 10^{11}$, type 1.50, then the key marked EXP, EE or ×10ˣ, then 11. Do not type "× 10" and then the exponent key as well: that multiplies by ten twice, and the answer comes out ten times too big.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Cosmology deals with the largest and smallest numbers in science. In the university course on the early universe you will meet the flatness problem. The **Big Bang** is the hot, dense beginning of the universe, and **density** is how much mass is packed into each cubic metre. For the universe to look as it does today, its density one second after the Big Bang had to match a particular critical value to about 18 decimal places, within about one part in $10^{18}$. No one could think about a number like that by writing out its zeros. Powers of ten make it a number you can work with.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $10^{n}$ is 1 followed by $n$ zeros; $10^{0} = 1$ and $10^{-n} = 1/10^{n}$, a small positive number.
- Scientific notation writes a number as $a \times 10^{n}$ with $1 \le a < 10$.
- To multiply, multiply the numbers in front and add the powers; to divide, divide them and subtract the powers. Then bring the number in front back between 1 and 10.
- To add or subtract, first give both numbers the same power of ten.
- The order of magnitude is the nearest power of ten. Prefixes such as k ($10^{3}$), M ($10^{6}$), m ($10^{-3}$) and μ ($10^{-6}$) stand for powers of ten in front of a unit.
