Straight lines describe things that change at a steady rate. Many things in physics do not. A falling stone covers more ground each second than the second before; the pull of gravity weakens rapidly as you move away from a planet. This lesson is about the next simplest kinds of relation: **quadratics**, where a quantity is squared, and **power laws**, where it is raised to some other power.

## The parabola

The simplest quadratic is $y = x^{2}$. Its graph is a U-shaped curve called a **parabola**. Multiplying by a number $a$ and adding a number $q$ gives the family

<Formula label="Parabola" tex="y = a\,x^{2} + q" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.3, Functions of the form y = ax² + q" />

The two constants each change the graph in one way:

- $a$ sets the shape. If $a$ is positive the parabola opens upward (a valley); if $a$ is negative it opens downward (a hill). The larger the size of $a$, the narrower the curve.
- $q$ moves the curve up or down. The **turning point**, the lowest or highest point, is at $(0, q)$.

The parabola is symmetric about the vertical line through its turning point: $x$ and $-x$ give the same $y$, because $(-x)^{2} = x^{2}$.

## Solving quadratic equations

A **quadratic equation** is one that can be written as

<Formula label="Quadratic equation" tex="a\,x^{2} + b\,x + c = 0, \qquad a \ne 0" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 0.7, Definition 0.15, p. 83" />

Solving it means finding where the parabola $y = ax^{2} + bx + c$ crosses the $x$-axis (the horizontal axis, where $y = 0$). Unlike a linear equation, it can have two solutions, one, or none. There are three methods; use the simplest that works.

**1. Square roots.** If there is no $x$ term ($b = 0$), isolate $x^{2}$ and take the square root, remembering both signs. From $3x^{2} - 48 = 0$: $x^{2} = 16$, so $x = 4$ or $x = -4$, written $x = \pm 4$. If $x^{2}$ comes out negative, there is no real solution. A **real number** is any number on the number line: positive, negative or zero, whole or not, including roots such as $\sqrt{2}$. The square of a real number is never negative. (Mathematicians also use "complex" numbers, whose squares can be negative; you will meet them at university.)

**2. Factoring.** If the left side can be written as a product of two brackets, use the **zero product property**: a product is zero only when at least one of its factors is zero.

<Formula label="Zero product property" tex="A\,B = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A = 0 \ \text{ or } \ B = 0" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 0.6.1, The Zero Product Property of Real Numbers, p. 77" />

To factor $x^{2} + bx + c$, look for two numbers that multiply to $c$ and add up to $b$. For $x^{2} + 2x - 15$ they are 5 and $-3$ (since $5 \times -3 = -15$ and $5 + (-3) = 2$), so $x^{2} + 2x - 15 = (x + 5)(x - 3)$, and $x^{2} + 2x - 15 = 0$ when $x = -5$ or $x = 3$. You can check a factorisation by multiplying the brackets out again.

**3. The quadratic formula.** It always works, even when the equation does not factor nicely:

<Formula label="The quadratic formula" tex="x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^{2} - 4ac}}{2a}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 0.7, Theorem 0.9, p. 86" />

The $\pm$ gives the two solutions: one with the plus sign, one with the minus sign. The expression under the root, $b^{2} - 4ac$, is called the **discriminant**, and it tells you how many real solutions there are before you calculate them:

- positive: two solutions (the parabola crosses the $x$-axis twice);
- zero: one solution (it just touches the axis);
- negative: no real solution (it misses the axis).

<WorkedExample title="A ball thrown upward">
<Problem>
A ball is thrown upward from a balcony. Its height above the ground, in metres, $t$ seconds later is $h = 1.5 + 14t - 4.9t^{2}$. (You will see where such a formula comes from in Block 1.) When does it hit the ground?
</Problem>
<Step title="Write it as a quadratic equation">
The ball is on the ground when $h = 0$: $-4.9t^{2} + 14t + 1.5 = 0$. So $a = -4.9$, $b = 14$, $c = 1.5$.
</Step>
<Step title="The discriminant">
$b^{2} - 4ac = 196 - 4(-4.9)(1.5) = 196 + 29.4 = 225.4$. It is positive, so there are two solutions.
</Step>
<Step title="The formula">
$\sqrt{225.4} \approx 15.01$, so $t = \dfrac{-14 \pm 15.01}{-9.8}$. The minus sign gives $t = 29.01/9.8 \approx 2.96$ s; the plus sign gives $t = 1.01/(-9.8) \approx -0.10$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Which solution is physical?">
The ball was thrown at $t = 0$, so a negative time makes no sense here: the ball hits the ground after about 2.96 s. (The negative solution is where the parabola, extended backwards, would have crossed the ground before the throw.)
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
At $t = 2.96$ s: $1.5 + 14(2.96) - 4.9(2.96)^{2} = 1.5 + 41.44 - 42.93 \approx 0$. It works, to the precision of the rounded time.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Power laws

A quadratic $y = kx^{2}$ is one example of a **power law**, $y = k\,x^{n}$, where $k$ is a constant and the power $n$ can be any number. Some you will meet often:

| Power $n$ | Example |
|---|---|
| 1 | direct proportion: distance at a steady speed |
| 2 | the area of a square or a circle |
| 3 | the volume of a cube or a sphere |
| $-1$ | inverse proportion, $y = k/x$ |
| $-2$ | the inverse square law of gravity and of light |
| $\tfrac{1}{2}$ | a square root: $x^{1/2} = \sqrt{x}$ |

A power of $-1$ means "one divided by", just as for powers of ten: $x^{-1} = 1/x$. Its graph, $y = k/x$, is a **hyperbola**, a curve that comes closer and closer to both axes without ever touching them. A power $1/n$ is a root: $x^{1/2} = \sqrt{x}$, because $(x^{1/2})^{2} = x^{1} = x$ by the laws of exponents of lesson 0.1.

The most useful fact about power laws is how they **scale**. If you multiply $x$ by some factor $f$, then $y$ is multiplied by $f^{n}$:

$k\,(f x)^{n} = f^{n} \cdot k\,x^{n}$.

So doubling the side of a cube multiplies its volume by $2^{3} = 8$, and doubling the distance from a lamp divides the brightness by $2^{2} = 4$. You do not need to know $k$ to use this.

**The inverse square law.** Light from a small lamp spreads out equally in all directions. At a distance $r$ it is spread over the surface of a sphere of radius $r$, whose area is $4\pi r^{2}$. The same light over a larger area means less light per square metre: the brightness is proportional to $1/r^{2}$. Gravity follows the same law, as you will see in Block 2.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Towards cosmology">
Power laws run through cosmology. As the universe expands, the matter in it is spread over a larger volume. Volume grows as size cubed, so when every distance in the universe doubles, the density of matter falls by $2^{3} = 8$: a power law with $n = -3$. The energy density of light falls even faster, as size to the power $-4$, because each bit of light is also stretched to longer wavelengths. That difference decides which of the two dominated the young universe.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The parabola $y = ax^{2} + q$ opens upward for positive $a$ and downward for negative $a$; its turning point is $(0, q)$.
- A quadratic equation $ax^{2} + bx + c = 0$ is solved by square roots ($b = 0$), by factoring with the zero product property, or by the quadratic formula $x = (-b \pm \sqrt{b^{2} - 4ac})/2a$.
- The discriminant $b^{2} - 4ac$ gives two, one or no real solutions; in physics, keep only the solutions that make sense.
- A power law $y = kx^{n}$ scales as $f^{n}$ when $x$ is multiplied by $f$. The inverse square law ($n = -2$) describes light and gravity.
