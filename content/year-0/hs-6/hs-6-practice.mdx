This set practises all thirteen lessons of Block 6, from charged spheres to the reason gravity, not electricity, shapes the universe. The problems are our own, of the kinds found in the exercises of Siyavula's chapters on electrostatics, electromagnetism and electrodynamics and in MIT's 8.02T course notes; the hints and solutions are our own too. Use $k = 9.0 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$, $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}$, $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$, an electron mass of $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$, a proton mass of $m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$, $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $c = 3.00 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s}$, unless a problem gives another value.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box wants the number only, in the unit printed after the box, for example `0.25` or `1.2e5`; small differences from rounding are accepted. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `(a + b)/c`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Two spheres that touch" points={12}>

Two identical small metal spheres stand on plastic stands. Sphere A carries $+8.0\ \text{nC}$ and sphere B carries $-2.4\ \text{nC}$. Their centres are $4.0\ \text{cm}$ apart, far enough to treat them as point charges.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

How many extra electrons does sphere B carry?

<Hint>

Every extra electron adds a charge of $-e$. Write the charge in coulombs first: $1\ \text{nC} = 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="number of extra electrons:" value="1.5e10" tolerance="3%" calc="hs6p-p1-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Charge comes in whole numbers of the elementary charge, so the number of electrons is the charge divided by $e$.

<Step title="Count the electrons">

<Formula label="Charge is quantized" tex="Q = n e" cite="siy164" at="Sec. 16.4, Unit of charge" />

$n = Q/e = 2.4 \times 10^{-9}/(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 1.5 \times 10^{10}$ extra electrons.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Fifteen billion electrons: a huge number, yet the charge is only a few nanocoulombs, because each electron carries so little. The answer is a whole number of electrons, here $15\,000\,000\,000$ exactly.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

How large is the force between the two spheres? Is it an attraction or a repulsion?

<Hint>

Use only the sizes of the charges in Coulomb's law, and the distance in metres. The signs tell you the direction.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$F =$" value="1.08e-4" unit="N" tolerance="3%" calc="hs6p-p1-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Coulomb's law gives the size of the force; unlike charges attract.

<Step title="Coulomb's law">

<Formula label="Coulomb's law" tex="F = k\,\frac{Q_1 Q_2}{r^2}" cite="siy92" at="Sec. 9.2, Coulomb's law" />

$F = 9.0 \times 10^9 \times (8.0 \times 10^{-9})(2.4 \times 10^{-9})/0.040^2 = 1.08 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{N}$, an attraction, because A is positive and B negative.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $(\text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2) \cdot \text{C}^2/\text{m}^2 = \text{N}$. The force is small, about the weight of $11$ milligrams, as you would expect for charges of a few nanocoulombs.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

The spheres are made to touch and are then put back with their centres $4.0\ \text{cm}$ apart. How large is the force between them now?

<Hint>

Charge is conserved. Two identical conductors that touch share the total charge equally.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$F =$" value="4.41e-5" unit="N" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p1-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** First find the new charges, then use Coulomb's law again.

<Step title="Share the charge">

<Formula label="Charge shared by two identical conductors" tex="Q = \frac{Q_1 + Q_2}{2}" cite="siy163" at="Sec. 16.3, Arrangement of charge" />

$Q = (+8.0 - 2.4)/2 = +2.8\ \text{nC}$ on each sphere.

</Step>

<Step title="The new force">

$F = 9.0 \times 10^9 \times (2.8 \times 10^{-9})^2/0.040^2 = 4.41 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{N}$, now a repulsion: both spheres are positive.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The total charge is $+5.6\ \text{nC}$ before and after. The force is about two and a half times weaker than before ($1.08 \times 10^{-4}/4.41 \times 10^{-5} = 2.45$), because the product of the charges fell from $19.2$ to $7.84$ (in $\text{nC}^2$).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Two other identical spheres carry charges $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ of the same sign. They touch and are put back at the same distance. Find the force after touching divided by the force before, in terms of $Q_1$ and $Q_2$.

<Hint>

Write both forces with Coulomb's law, using the shared charge $(Q_1 + Q_2)/2$ for the force after touching. Then divide: $k$ and $r$ cancel.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="$F_\text{after}/F_\text{before} =$" expr="(Q1 + Q2)^2/(4*Q1*Q2)"
  vars={{ Q1: [1, 10], Q2: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ Q1: "Q_1", Q2: "Q_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(Q1 + Q2)^2/(Q1*Q2)", hint: "Each sphere gets half of the total charge, not all of it. Where does the factor 1/2 go when you square it?" },
    { expr: "(Q1 + Q2)/(2*sqrt(Q1*Q2))", hint: "Close, but the force depends on the product of the two charges, so the shared charge appears squared, not to the first power." },
    { expr: "4*Q1*Q2/(Q1 + Q2)^2", hint: "That is the force before divided by the force after. Turn it over." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Before touching the product of the charges is $Q_1 Q_2$; afterwards it is the square of the shared charge.

<Step title="Divide the two forces">

<Formula label="Force after touching divided by force before" tex="\frac{F_\text{after}}{F_\text{before}} = \frac{k\left(\frac{Q_1 + Q_2}{2}\right)^2/r^2}{k\,Q_1 Q_2/r^2} = \frac{(Q_1 + Q_2)^2}{4 Q_1 Q_2}" cite="siy92" at="Sec. 9.2, Coulomb's law, with the shared charge of Sec. 16.3 (our own step)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $Q_1 = Q_2$ the ratio is $4Q_1^2/4Q_1^2 = 1$: nothing changes, as it should, because no charge moves. With $Q_1 = 1$ and $Q_2 = 3$ (in any unit) it is $16/12 = 1.33$. The ratio is never smaller than $1$, because $(Q_1 + Q_2)^2 - 4Q_1Q_2 = (Q_1 - Q_2)^2$ is never negative: for charges of the same sign, sharing never weakens the push. Part (c) was different, because there the charges had opposite signs and partly cancelled.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Where the field vanishes" points={13}>

Two small spheres are $0.30\ \text{m}$ apart. Sphere A carries $+2.0\ \text{nC}$ and sphere B carries $+8.0\ \text{nC}$. Point P lies on the straight line between them, $0.10\ \text{m}$ from A.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

How strong is the electric field at P due to sphere A alone?

<Hint>

Treat the sphere as a point charge. Which formula gives the field at a distance $r$ from it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$E_A =$" value="1800" unit="N/C" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p2-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The field of a point charge falls off with the square of the distance.

<Step title="Field of A">

<Formula label="Field of a point charge" tex="E = \frac{kQ}{r^2}" cite="siy93" at="Sec. 9.3, electric field strength" />

$E_A = 9.0 \times 10^9 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-9}/0.10^2 = 18/0.010 = 1800\ \text{N/C}$, pointing away from A, so towards B.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $(\text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2) \cdot \text{C}/\text{m}^2 = \text{N/C}$. A positive charge's field points away from it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

How strong is the field at P due to sphere B alone? What is the total field at P?

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$E_B =$" value="1800" unit="N/C" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p2-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same formula, with B's charge and B's distance to P.

<Step title="Field of B">

P is $0.30 - 0.10 = 0.20\ \text{m}$ from B, so $E_B = 9.0 \times 10^9 \times 8.0 \times 10^{-9}/0.20^2 = 72/0.040 = 1800\ \text{N/C}$, pointing away from B, so towards A.

</Step>

<Step title="Add the two fields">

The two fields at P are equally strong and point in opposite directions, so they cancel: the total field at P is zero. A charge placed at P feels no force.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

B has four times the charge of A but is twice as far from P, and the field falls as $1/r^2$: $4/2^2 = 1$. That is why the two fields are equal.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Now take any two charges $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ of the same sign, a distance $d$ apart. Find the distance $x$ from $Q_1$ to the point between them where the total field is zero, in terms of $d$, $Q_1$ and $Q_2$.

<Hint>

How strong must the two fields be at a point where they cancel? How far is that point from each charge, written with $x$ and $d$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

At that point the two fields are equally strong: $kQ_1/x^2 = kQ_2/(d - x)^2$. Take the square root of both sides.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$x =$" expr="d/(1 + sqrt(Q2/Q1))"
  vars={{ d: [0.1, 2], Q1: [1, 10], Q2: [1, 10] }}
  names={{ Q1: "Q_1", Q2: "Q_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "d/(1 + Q2/Q1)", hint: "The fields fall off as 1/r², so the distances go with the square roots of the charges." },
    { expr: "d/(1 + sqrt(Q1/Q2))", hint: "That is the distance from the other charge. The zero point lies closer to the smaller charge." },
    { expr: "d*sqrt(Q1/Q2)", hint: "Try two equal charges: the zero point must then lie halfway, at d/2. Does your formula give that?" }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two fields cancel where they are equally strong, and between two charges of the same sign they point in opposite directions.

<Step title="Equal fields">

<Formula label="The point where the field is zero" tex="\frac{kQ_1}{x^2} = \frac{kQ_2}{(d - x)^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d - x}{x} = \sqrt{\frac{Q_2}{Q_1}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \frac{d}{1 + \sqrt{Q_2/Q_1}}" cite="siy93" at="Sec. 9.3, E = kQ/r² for each charge, set equal (our own step)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For this problem: $x = 0.30/(1 + \sqrt{4}) = 0.30/3 = 0.10\ \text{m}$, point P. For two equal charges $x = d/2$, halfway, as symmetry demands. The answer has the unit of $d$, a length.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

An electron is placed at the midpoint between A and B. What is the size of its acceleration at that moment? Ignore gravity.

<Hint>

At the midpoint both spheres are $0.15\ \text{m}$ away and the two fields point in opposite directions. Find the total field first, then the force $eE$, then Newton's second law.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="$a =$" value="4.22e14" unit="m/s²" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p2-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The total field gives the force on the electron, and the force gives its acceleration.

<Step title="Total field at the midpoint">

$E_A = 9.0 \times 10^9 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-9}/0.15^2 = 800\ \text{N/C}$ towards B and $E_B = 9.0 \times 10^9 \times 8.0 \times 10^{-9}/0.15^2 = 3200\ \text{N/C}$ towards A. The total is $3200 - 800 = 2400\ \text{N/C}$, pointing towards A.

</Step>

<Step title="Force and acceleration">

<Formula label="Force on a charge in a field" tex="F = qE" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 1.6" />

$F = eE = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2400 = 3.84 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{N}$, pointing towards B, against the field, because the electron is negative. Then $a = F/m_e = 3.84 \times 10^{-16}/(9.11 \times 10^{-31}) = 4.22 \times 10^{14}\ \text{m/s}^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The acceleration is enormous, more than $10^{13}$ times $g$, which is why gravity could be ignored. The electron is pulled towards B, the larger positive charge, as you would expect.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A floating oil drop" points={13}>

In an experiment like Millikan's, two horizontal metal plates are $1.2\ \text{cm}$ apart, with a voltage of $1200\ \text{V}$ between them; the upper plate is positive. A tiny oil drop with a mass of $4.9 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{kg}$ hangs still between the plates.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

How strong is the electric field between the plates?

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$E =$" value="1.0e5" unit="V/m" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p3-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Between parallel plates the field is uniform, and its strength is the voltage per metre of gap.

<Step title="Field">

<Formula label="Voltage in a uniform field" tex="V = Ed" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (3.2.1), its size" />

$E = V/d = 1200/0.012 = 1.0 \times 10^{5}\ \text{V/m}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$1\ \text{V/m} = 1\ \text{N/C}$, so a charge of one coulomb here would feel $10^5\ \text{N}$. The field points downwards, from the positive upper plate to the negative lower one.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What is the size of the charge on the drop? Is it positive or negative?

<Hint>

The drop does not move, so the forces on it balance. Which two forces act on it?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="size of the charge:" value="4.81e-19" unit="C" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p3-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The electric force upwards balances the weight of the drop.

<Step title="Balance the forces">

<Formula label="Electric force balancing the weight" tex="qE = mg" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 1.6, F = qE, set equal to the weight (our own step)" />

$q = mg/E = 4.9 \times 10^{-15} \times 9.81/(1.0 \times 10^{5}) = 4.81 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The sign">

The field points down, but the force on the drop must point up. A force against the field means a negative charge: the drop carries extra electrons and is pulled towards the positive upper plate.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\text{kg} \cdot (\text{m/s}^2)/(\text{N/C}) = \text{N}/(\text{N/C}) = \text{C}$. The charge is a few times $e$, as it must be for a drop that has picked up a few electrons.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

How many extra electrons does the drop carry?

<Answer id="p3-c" label="number of extra electrons:" value="3" tolerance="0.2" calc="hs6p-p3-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The charge must be a whole number of elementary charges.

<Step title="Divide by e">

$n = q/e = 4.81 \times 10^{-19}/(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 3.0$, so $3$ extra electrons.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result comes out very close to a whole number, as Millikan found with his drops: charge is quantized (lesson 6.1).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

The drop drifts slowly upwards, from the lower plate to the upper plate. How much work does the electric field do on it, in electronvolts?

<Hint>

A charge of size $e$ moved through one volt gains one electronvolt. How large is this charge, in units of $e$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-d" label="$W =$" value="3600" unit="eV" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p3-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The work done by the field is the charge times the voltage it crosses.

<Step title="Work in electronvolts">

<Formula label="The electronvolt" tex="1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (3.1.12)" />

A charge of $3e$ crossing $1200\ \text{V}$ gains $3 \times 1200 = 3600\ \text{eV}$, or $3600 \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 5.76 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{J}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

While the drop rises by $0.012\ \text{m}$, gravity takes energy back: $mgd = 4.9 \times 10^{-15} \times 9.81 \times 0.012 = 5.77 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{J}$, the same amount. That fits: the two forces balance, so the drop gains no kinetic energy on the way.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={4}>

Find the voltage $V$ needed to hold a drop of mass $m$ still, if it carries $n$ extra electrons and the plates are a distance $d$ apart, in terms of $m$, $g$, $d$, $n$ and $e$.

<Answer id="p3-e" label="$V =$" expr="m*g*d/(n*e)"
  vars={{ m: [1e-15, 1e-13], g: [9, 10], d: [0.005, 0.02], n: [1, 10], e: [1.5e-19, 1.7e-19] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*g/(n*e)", hint: "That is the field strength, in V/m. How do you get from the field to the voltage between the plates?" },
    { expr: "m*g/(n*e*d)", hint: "Check the units: a voltage is a field times a distance, not a field divided by a distance." },
    { expr: "m*g*d/e", hint: "This drop carries n electrons, not one. Where does n go?" }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Combine the balance of forces with the field between the plates.

<Step title="Solve for the voltage">

**The idea.** The fields cancel where they are equally strong, and that point lies closer to the weaker current.

<Step title="Equal fields">

With $x$ the distance from the $20\ \text{A}$ wire: $\mu_0 \times 20/(2\pi x) = \mu_0 \times 30/(2\pi (0.060 - x))$, so $20(0.060 - x) = 30x$, $1.2 = 50x$ (in metres and amperes) and $x = 0.024\ \text{m} = 2.4\ \text{cm}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $2.4\ \text{cm}$ from one wire and $3.6\ \text{cm}$ from the other: $20/2.4 = 30/3.6 = 8.33$, so the fields are equal. Compare Problem 2: there the field falls as $1/r^2$, here as $1/r$, so here the distances go with the currents themselves, not with their square roots.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

What is the force on each metre of the $30\ \text{A}$ wire? Do the wires attract or repel each other?

<Answer id="p9-c" label="force per metre:" value="2.0e-3" unit="N/m" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p9-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each wire lies in the field of the other and carries a current, so it feels a force.

<Step title="Force per metre">

<Formula label="Force per metre between two parallel currents" tex="\frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d}" cite="reading7" at="Eq. (9.2.1)" />

$F/L = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 20 \times 30/(2\pi \times 0.060) = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{N/m}$. Currents in the same direction attract.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The $20\ \text{A}$ wire feels exactly the same force per metre, towards the other wire, as Newton's third law demands. The formula is symmetric in $I_1$ and $I_2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

A solenoid $15\ \text{cm}$ long must give a field of $1.0 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{T}$ inside it with a current of $2.5\ \text{A}$. How many turns does it need?

<Answer id="p9-d" label="$N$ (about how many) $=$" value="477" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p9-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Inside a long solenoid the field depends on the number of turns per metre and the current.

<Step title="Turns per metre">

<Formula label="Field inside a long solenoid" tex="B = \mu_0 n I, \qquad n = \frac{N}{L}" cite="reading7" at="Eq. (9.4.3)" />

$n = B/(\mu_0 I) = 1.0 \times 10^{-2}/(4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 2.5) = 3180$ turns per metre.

</Step>

<Step title="Number of turns">

$N = nL = 3180 \times 0.15 = 477$ turns.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Back again: $4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times (477/0.15) \times 2.5 = 1.0 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{T}$, about $200$ times the Earth's field.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 10

<Exercise id="p10" title="A coil pulled out of a magnet" points={12}>

A square coil of $200$ turns, each $5.0\ \text{cm}$ by $5.0\ \text{cm}$, sits face on between the poles of a large magnet, in a uniform field of $0.25\ \text{T}$. The ends of the coil are joined, and the coil has a resistance of $5.0\ \text{Ω}$. In $0.10\ \text{s}$ the coil is pulled steadily out of the field, to a place where there is no field.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the magnetic flux through one turn of the coil at the start?

<Answer id="p10-a" label="$\Phi =$" value="6.25e-4" unit="Wb" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p10-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Face on, the whole area of the turn is crossed by the field.

<Step title="Flux">

<Formula label="Magnetic flux" tex="\Phi = BA\cos\theta" cite="siy103" at="Sec. 10.3, Magnetic flux" />

$A = 0.050 \times 0.050 = 2.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$ and $\theta = 0$, so $\Phi = 0.25 \times 2.5 \times 10^{-3} = 6.25 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{Wb}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\text{T}\,\text{m}^2 = \text{Wb}$. Face on gives the largest flux; turned edge on it would be zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

What emf is induced in the coil while it is pulled out?

<Answer id="p10-b" label="$\varepsilon =$" value="1.25" unit="V" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p10-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A changing flux through a coil induces an emf, $N$ times as large as for one turn.

<Step title="Faraday's law">

<Formula label="Faraday's law of induction" tex="\varepsilon = -N\,\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}" cite="siy103" at="Sec. 10.3, Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction" />

The flux falls from $6.25 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{Wb}$ to zero, so $\Delta\Phi = -6.25 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{Wb}$, and the size of the emf is $200 \times 6.25 \times 10^{-4}/0.10 = 1.25\ \text{V}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\text{Wb/s} = \text{T}\,\text{m}^2/\text{s} = \text{V}$. Pulling the coil out twice as fast would give twice the emf for half as long.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

What current flows in the coil meanwhile?

<Answer id="p10-c" label="$I =$" value="0.25" unit="A" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p10-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The induced emf drives a current through the coil's own resistance.

<Step title="Current">

$I = \varepsilon/R = 1.25/5.0 = 0.25\ \text{A}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The current flows only while the flux changes: before and after the pull it is zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

What does the induced current do while the coil is being pulled out?

<Answer id="p10-d" label="The induced current:"
  choices={[
    "It has no magnetic effect, because the coil is leaving the field",
    "It makes its own field along the magnet's field, so you must pull harder",
    "It makes its own field opposite to the magnet's field, so it helps the coil out of the field",
    "It flows only after the coil has left the field"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change that causes it.

<Step title="Opposing the change">

The change is a loss of flux through the coil. The induced current tries to keep that flux, so its own field through the coil points the same way as the magnet's field. Seen as forces, the magnet pulls the coil back: you must do work to pull it out, and that work becomes the heat made by the current in the coil's resistance.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

A phone charger has a transformer that makes $5.0\ \text{V}$ from the $230\ \text{V}$ mains. Its primary coil has $1840$ turns. How many turns does the secondary coil need?

<Answer id="p10-e" label="$N_s =$" value="40" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p10-e" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In an ideal transformer the voltages are in the same ratio as the numbers of turns.

<Step title="Turns ratio">

<Formula label="The transformer" tex="\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p}" cite="reading9" at="slide 26" />

$N_s = N_p\,V_s/V_p = 1840 \times 5.0/230 = 40$ turns.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$230/5.0 = 46$ and $1840/40 = 46$: the same ratio. A transformer works only with alternating current, because only a changing current gives a changing flux through the secondary coil.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 11

<Exercise id="p11" title="A signal from a satellite" points={9}>

A television satellite hangs about $3.6 \times 10^7\ \text{m}$ above the equator and sends its signal at a frequency of $12.0\ \text{GHz}$ ($1.20 \times 10^{10}\ \text{Hz}$).

<Part label="a" points={2}>

What is the wavelength of the signal, in centimetres?

<Answer id="p11-a" label="$\lambda =$" value="2.50" unit="cm" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p11-a" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Every electromagnetic wave travels at $c$ in vacuum (and almost exactly at $c$ in air).

<Step title="Wavelength">

<Formula label="The wave equation for light" tex="c = f\lambda" cite="siy112" at="Sec. 11.2" />

$\lambda = c/f = 3.00 \times 10^8/(1.20 \times 10^{10}) = 0.0250\ \text{m} = 2.50\ \text{cm}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A few centimetres: microwaves, between radio waves and infrared, as lesson 5.8 places them.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

At a dish on the ground the electric field of the signal swings with an amplitude of $3.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{V/m}$. What is the amplitude of its magnetic field?

<Answer id="p11-b" label="$B =$" value="1.0e-12" unit="T" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p11-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In an electromagnetic wave the two fields keep a fixed ratio, the speed of light.

<Step title="Use E = cB">

<Formula label="The two fields of the wave" tex="E = cB" cite="reading8" at="Eq. (13.4.27)" />

$B = E/c = 3.0 \times 10^{-4}/(3.00 \times 10^8) = 1.0 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{T}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $(\text{V/m})/(\text{m/s}) = \text{V}\,\text{s}/\text{m}^2 = \text{Wb}/\text{m}^2 = \text{T}$. The field is tens of millions of times weaker than the Earth's magnetic field, yet the dish collects enough of the wave to pick up the signal.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

How long does the signal take to travel from the satellite straight down to the dish?

<Answer id="p11-c" label="$t =$" value="0.12" unit="s" tolerance="1%" calc="hs6p-p11-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The signal travels at the speed of light.

<Step title="Travel time">

$t = 3.6 \times 10^7/(3.00 \times 10^8) = 0.12\ \text{s}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

About a tenth of a second. A live interview by satellite goes up and down twice (question and answer), which is why the replies seem to come a little late.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

The wave travels straight down. In which directions do its electric and magnetic fields swing?

<Answer id="p11-d" label="The fields:"
  choices={[
    "Both swing vertically, along the direction of travel",
    "The electric field swings vertically and the magnetic field horizontally",
    "Both swing in the same horizontal direction",
    "Both swing horizontally, at right angles to each other"
  ]}
  answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** An electromagnetic wave is transverse: both fields are at right angles to the direction of travel and to each other.

<Step title="Directions">

The wave moves vertically, so both fields lie in the horizontal plane, at right angles to each other. Which horizontal direction the electric field takes is the polarization of the signal (lesson 5.12).

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 12

<Exercise id="p12" title="Charged dust between the stars" points={12}>

Between the stars of the Milky Way float tiny grains of dust. Starlight and collisions with gas leave such grains with a small electric charge. Take two equal grains, each of mass $1.0 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{kg}$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Each of two equal bodies of mass $m$ carries $N$ extra electrons. Find the number $N$ for which their electric repulsion is exactly as strong as their gravitational attraction, in terms of $m$, $e$, $G$ and $k$.

<Hint>

Which two forces must be equally strong? How does each depend on the distance $r$ between the bodies?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Both forces fall off as $1/r^2$, so the distance cancels. Set $k(Ne)^2 = Gm^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p12-a" label="$N =$" expr="m/e*sqrt(G/k)"
  vars={{ m: [1e-6, 1000], e: [1.5e-19, 1.7e-19], G: [6e-11, 7e-11], k: [8e9, 1e10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m/e*sqrt(k/G)", hint: "Upside down: a heavier body needs more charge, and stronger gravity (larger G) too. Which of G and k belongs above the line?" },
    { expr: "m^2*G/(e^2*k)", hint: "That is N². Take the square root." },
    { expr: "m*e*sqrt(G/k)", hint: "Ne is the charge, so N is the charge divided by e, not multiplied." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The repulsion and the attraction are both inverse-square laws; set them equal and solve for the charge.

<Step title="Set the forces equal">

<Formula label="Coulomb's law" tex="F_e = k\,\frac{Q_1 Q_2}{r^2}" cite="siy92" at="Sec. 9.2, Coulomb's law" />

<Formula label="Newton's law of gravitation" tex="F_g = G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}" cite="siy24" at="Sec. 2.4, Newton's law of universal gravitation" />

<Formula label="Extra electrons for which repulsion balances gravity" tex="k\,\frac{(Ne)^2}{r^2} = G\,\frac{m^2}{r^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad N = \frac{m}{e}\sqrt{\frac{G}{k}}" cite="siy92" at="Sec. 9.2, Coulomb's law set equal to Newton's law of Sec. 2.4 (our own step)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\sqrt{G/k}$ has the unit $\sqrt{(\text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2)/(\text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2)} = \text{C/kg}$, so $m\sqrt{G/k}$ is a charge and $N$ a pure number, as a count must be. For these grains $N = 1.0 \times 10^{-17} \times \sqrt{6.67 \times 10^{-11}/9.0 \times 10^9}/(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 5.4 \times 10^{-9}$: far less than one electron.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Each grain carries just one extra electron. How many times stronger is their electric repulsion than their gravitational attraction?

<Answer id="p12-b" label="$F_e/F_g =$" value="3.45e16" tolerance="3%" calc="hs6p-p12-b" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Divide Coulomb's law by Newton's law: the distance cancels.

<Step title="The ratio">

$\dfrac{F_e}{F_g} = \dfrac{k e^2}{G m^2} = \dfrac{9.0 \times 10^9 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2}{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times (1.0 \times 10^{-17})^2} = 3.45 \times 10^{16}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

From part (a): one electron is $1/(5.4 \times 10^{-9})$ times the balancing number, and the forces go with its square: $(1/5.38 \times 10^{-9})^2 = 3.45 \times 10^{16}$. For a dust grain, a single electron beats gravity many millions of billions of times over.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Now take two stars, each with a mass of $2.0 \times 10^{30}\ \text{kg}$ (about the mass of the Sun). What charge on each star, of the same sign, would make their electric repulsion balance their gravitational attraction?

<Answer id="p12-c" label="$Q =$" value="1.72e20" unit="C" tolerance="3%" calc="hs6p-p12-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The charge in part (a) is $Ne = m\sqrt{G/k}$, for any mass.

<Step title="The charge">

$Q = m\sqrt{G/k} = 2.0 \times 10^{30} \times \sqrt{6.67 \times 10^{-11}/(9.0 \times 10^{9})} = 2.0 \times 10^{30} \times 8.61 \times 10^{-11} = 1.72 \times 10^{20}\ \text{C}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

That looks like a vast charge, but compare it with what is in the star. It contains about $2.0 \times 10^{30}/(1.67 \times 10^{-27}) = 1.20 \times 10^{57}$ nucleons, and the charge is $1.72 \times 10^{20}/(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 1.08 \times 10^{39}$ elementary charges: only one extra charge for every $1.1 \times 10^{18}$ nucleons, the balance point found in lesson 6.13.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

So why does gravity, and not electricity, shape the clouds of gas and dust, the stars and the galaxies?

<Answer id="p12-d" label="Gravity rules because:"
  choices={[
    "The electric force is weaker than gravity between single particles",
    "Electric forces do not act across the empty space between the stars",
    "Large bodies stay almost exactly neutral, but gravity only attracts and adds up",
    "Charge is not conserved over long times, so it slowly leaks away from stars and gas clouds"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Strength alone does not decide which force matters on large scales; cancellation does.

<Step title="Cancellation against adding up">

Between single particles the electric force wins by an enormous factor (part (b)). But it comes with two signs, and matter neutralises itself: an excess of one sign attracts the other from its surroundings until the excess is gone. Gravity has only one sign, so the attraction of every bit of mass adds up. On the scale of stars and galaxies, nothing is left of the electric force, while gravity keeps growing with the mass.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
