Rub a balloon on your hair and it lifts the hairs towards it; bring two rubbed balloons together and they push each other away. The previous lesson showed that charged objects attract or repel. This lesson answers the next question: **how strong** is that force, and how does it change with the charges and the distance between them?

## The force between two point charges

In the 1780s the French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb measured the tiny forces between small charged spheres with a very sensitive twisting balance. He found a simple rule. It holds for **point charges**: charged objects that are small compared with the distance between them. (A charged sphere with its charge spread evenly over it, seen from outside, acts as if its charge were all at its centre. Any small charged object, seen from far enough away, acts as a point charge.)

For two point charges $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ a distance $r$ apart, the size of the force each one exerts on the other is

<Formula label="Coulomb's law" tex="F = k\,\frac{Q_1 Q_2}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 9.2, Coulomb's law" />

Here the charges are in coulombs (C), $r$ in metres and $F$ in newtons. In this formula we use the **sizes** of the charges, without their signs, so $F$ is never negative. The direction comes separately, from the signs (next section).

The number $k$ is the **Coulomb constant** (Siyavula calls it the electrostatic constant). In this block we use

<Formula label="The Coulomb constant" tex="k = 9.0 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 9.2, Coulomb's law (the electrostatic constant)" />

A more precise value is $8.99 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$, and university books write $k$ as $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$. The size of $k$ tells you that one coulomb is an enormous charge: two charges of $1\ \text{C}$ a metre apart would push on each other with about $9 \times 10^9\ \text{N}$, the weight of nearly a million tonnes. Everyday charges are measured in microcoulombs ($1\ \mu\text{C} = 10^{-6}\ \text{C}$) or nanocoulombs ($1\ \text{nC} = 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$).

Two things in the formula are worth reading slowly:

- **The force grows with each charge.** Double one charge and the force doubles; double both and it becomes four times as large. The force depends on the product $Q_1 Q_2$.
- **The force falls with the square of the distance.** Twice as far apart gives a quarter of the force, three times as far a ninth. This is an **inverse-square law**.

<WorkedExample title="Two charged beads">
<Problem>
A small bead carries a charge of $+8.0\ \text{nC}$ and another a charge of $-5.0\ \text{nC}$. Their centres are $5.0\ \text{cm}$ apart. How large is the force between them, and is it an attraction or a repulsion?
</Problem>
<Step title="Write everything in SI units">
$Q_1 = 8.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$, $Q_2 = 5.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$ (sizes only), $r = 0.050\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Use Coulomb's law">
$F = kQ_1Q_2/r^2 = 9.0 \times 10^9 \times (8.0 \times 10^{-9})(5.0 \times 10^{-9})/0.050^2 = 1.4 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{N}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Direction">
One charge is positive and one negative, so the beads **attract**: each is pulled towards the other with a force of $1.4 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{N}$. That is the weight of a mass of about $15\ \text{mg}$: small, but enough to swing a light bead hanging from a thread.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Attraction, repulsion and Newton's third law

The direction of the force always lies along the line joining the two charges:

- **Like charges repel.** Two positive charges, or two negative ones, push each other apart.
- **Opposite charges attract.** A positive and a negative charge pull towards each other.

The force on $Q_1$ and the force on $Q_2$ are equally large and point in opposite directions, even when one charge is much bigger than the other. This is Newton's third law at work: the two forces are an action–reaction pair. A large charge pushes on a small one exactly as hard as the small one pushes back. The small one may simply move more, if it is also lighter: by Newton's second law it gets the larger acceleration.

## Compared with Newton's law of gravitation

Coulomb's law looks almost the same as Newton's law of gravitation for two masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ a distance $r$ apart:

<Formula label="Newton's law of gravitation" tex="F_g = G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 9.2, similarity of Coulomb's law to Newton's universal law of gravitation" />

with $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$. The two laws share a lot:

- both forces act at a distance, through empty space, along the line between the two objects;
- both are proportional to a product: of the charges, or of the masses;
- both fall with the square of the distance.

There are two big differences. **Gravity only attracts**, because mass has one sign, while the electric force attracts or repels. And between small particles **the electric force is far stronger**, as the next example shows.

<WorkedExample title="Electricity against gravity in a hydrogen atom">
<Problem>
In a hydrogen atom an electron is held near a proton. Take their distance as $5.0 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{m}$. Each has a charge of size $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$; the proton's mass is $m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$ and the electron's $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$. Compare the electric and the gravitational force between them.
</Problem>
<Step title="The electric force">
$F_e = ke^2/r^2 = 9.0 \times 10^9 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2/(5.0 \times 10^{-11})^2 = 9.22 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{N}$, an attraction.
</Step>
<Step title="The gravitational force">
$F_g = Gm_pm_e/r^2 = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.67 \times 10^{-27} \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31}/(5.0 \times 10^{-11})^2 = 4.06 \times 10^{-47}\ \text{N}$, also an attraction.
</Step>
<Step title="The ratio">
$F_e/F_g = 9.22 \times 10^{-8}/4.06 \times 10^{-47} = 2.3 \times 10^{39}$. The electric force is more than a thousand billion billion billion billion times stronger. Gravity plays no noticeable part inside an atom.
</Step>
<Step title="What the force does">
By Newton's second law the electric force alone would give the light electron an acceleration $a = F_e/m_e = 9.22 \times 10^{-8}/9.11 \times 10^{-31} = 1.0 \times 10^{23}\ \text{m/s}^2$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Because both forces fall as $1/r^2$, the distance cancels in the ratio:

<Formula label="Electric against gravitational force, proton and electron" tex="\frac{F_e}{F_g} = \frac{k e^2}{G\, m_p m_e}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 9.2, Worked example 2 (the same comparison for two electrons)" />

So the ratio is about $2.3 \times 10^{39}$ at **every** distance. For two protons it is smaller, because the proton is heavier, but still about $1.2 \times 10^{36}$.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Then why does gravity rule the sky?">
If the electric force is so much stronger, why are planets, stars and galaxies held together by gravity? Because big objects are almost perfectly neutral: their positive and negative charges are nearly equal in number, so the electric attractions and repulsions cancel. Mass has no negative kind, so gravity from every particle adds up. The last lesson of this block follows this idea out to the whole universe.
</Callout>

## Adding forces from several charges

Force is a vector: it has a size and a direction. When a charge feels forces from two or more other charges, each force is worked out with Coulomb's law **as if the others were not there**, and then the forces are added as vectors. The result is the **net force**.

When all the charges lie on one straight line, every force points along that line, and adding vectors becomes adding numbers with signs. Choose a positive direction (say to the right), give each force a $+$ sign if it points that way and a $-$ sign if it points the other way, and add:

<Formula label="Net force along a line" tex="F_\text{net} = F_1 + F_2 + \dots" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 9.2, choosing a positive direction (forces in one dimension)" />

<WorkedExample title="Three charges in a row">
<Problem>
Three small charges lie on a straight line. Charge A, $+3.0\ \mu\text{C}$, is at the left end. Charge B, $-2.0\ \mu\text{C}$, is $0.20\ \text{m}$ to the right of A. Charge C, $+5.0\ \mu\text{C}$, is $0.30\ \text{m}$ to the right of B. Find the net force on B.
</Problem>
<Step title="Force of A on B">
B is negative and A positive, so A pulls B towards itself: to the left. Its size is $9.0 \times 10^9 \times (3.0 \times 10^{-6})(2.0 \times 10^{-6})/0.20^2 = 1.35\ \text{N}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Force of C on B">
C is also positive, so it pulls B towards itself: to the right. Its size is $9.0 \times 10^9 \times (5.0 \times 10^{-6})(2.0 \times 10^{-6})/0.30^2 = 1.00\ \text{N}$. C has the larger charge but is farther away, and the distance counts squared.
</Step>
<Step title="Add with signs">
Take right as positive: $F_\text{net} = -1.35 + 1.00 = -0.35\ \text{N}$. The net force on B is $0.35\ \text{N}$ to the left, towards A.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

When the charges do not lie on one line, the forces point in different directions. Then you split each force into components, as in the lesson on vectors, add the components, and find the size and direction of the result with Pythagoras and trigonometry. The idea is the same: each pair of charges is handled on its own, and the forces add.

<WorkedExample title="Two forces at right angles">
<Problem>
Charge B, $+2.0\ \mu\text{C}$, sits at the corner of a right angle. Charge A, $+4.0\ \mu\text{C}$, is $0.30\ \text{m}$ to the left of B, and charge C, $-3.0\ \mu\text{C}$, is $0.30\ \text{m}$ straight above B. Find the net force on B.
</Problem>
<Step title="The two forces">
A and B are both positive, so A pushes B away: to the right, with $9.0 \times 10^9 \times (4.0 \times 10^{-6})(2.0 \times 10^{-6})/0.30^2 = 0.80\ \text{N}$. C is negative, so it pulls B towards itself: upwards, with $9.0 \times 10^9 \times (3.0 \times 10^{-6})(2.0 \times 10^{-6})/0.30^2 = 0.60\ \text{N}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Add them as vectors">
The two forces are at right angles, so they are already the components of the net force. Its size is $\sqrt{0.80^2 + 0.60^2} = 1.0\ \text{N}$, and it points up from the right-hand direction at an angle of $\tan^{-1}(0.60/0.80) = 37^\circ$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Try it

Move the two charges closer and farther apart and watch the force arrows. Check that halving the distance makes the force four times as large, that doubling one charge doubles it, and that the arrows on the two charges are always equally long, even when the charges are different.

## Summary

- Two point charges exert forces on each other of size $F = kQ_1Q_2/r^2$, with $k = 9.0 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$ and the charges taken without their signs.
- Like charges repel and opposite charges attract; the force acts along the line between them, and the two forces are equal and opposite.
- The force is proportional to the product of the charges and falls with the square of the distance: an inverse-square law, like Newton's law of gravitation.
- Gravity only attracts, but the electric force can also repel, and between a proton and an electron it is about $2.3 \times 10^{39}$ times stronger than gravity.
- Forces from several charges add as vectors; along a line, choose a positive direction and add the forces with their signs.
