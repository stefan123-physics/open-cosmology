Every appliance in a house carries a small label: "230 V, 1800 W" on a kettle, "8 W" on an LED lamp. Every month or year the electricity company sends a bill that counts "kWh". This lesson explains both numbers. The voltage tells how much energy each coulomb of charge carries, the current how many coulombs pass each second, and together they tell how fast an appliance uses energy and what it costs.

## Energy carried by a current

In lesson 6.4 the voltage between two points was defined as the energy per coulomb: a charge $Q$ that moves through a voltage $V$ transfers an energy $QV$. In a circuit the battery or the socket gives this energy to the charge, and the lamp, heater or motor takes it out again. The MIT notes follow one charge around a circuit: the battery raises its potential energy, the resistor takes it away, and when the charge is back where it started its energy is the same as before.

<Formula label="Energy transferred by a charge passing through a voltage" tex="E = QV" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.3, ΔU = Δq ΔV" />

In lesson 6.6 the current was the charge that passes per second, $I = Q/t$, so in a time $t$ a charge $Q = It$ passes. Put that in:

<Formula label="Energy transferred by a current in a time t" tex="E = VIt" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.3, ΔU = Δq ΔV with Δq = I Δt (Eq. 6.3.1)" />

Here $E$ stands for energy, in joules. (In lessons 6.3 and 6.4 the same letter was the electric field; on this page there is no field, only energy.)

<WorkedExample title="A car headlamp">
<Problem>
A car headlamp on a $12\ \text{V}$ battery draws a current of $4.5\ \text{A}$. The lamp is on for $10$ minutes. How much charge passes through it, and how much energy does the battery give it?
</Problem>
<Step title="Charge">
$t = 10 \times 60 = 600\ \text{s}$, so $Q = It = 4.5 \times 600 = 2700\ \text{C}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy">
$E = QV = 2700 \times 12 = 3.2 \times 10^{4}\ \text{J}$. Each of the $2700$ coulombs delivers $12$ joules.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Electrical power

Lesson 3.5 defined **power** as the energy transferred per second, $P = E/t$, measured in **watts**: $1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}$. Divide $E = VIt$ by $t$:

<Formula label="Electrical power" tex="P = VI" cite="mit" at="Sec. 6.3, Eq. (6.3.1)" />

The MIT notes reach the same result as the rate at which a resistor takes energy from the charges that pass through it:

<Formula label="Power as energy per second" tex="P = \frac{E}{t} = \frac{Q}{t}\,V = IV" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.3.1)" />

This is exactly the power the battery supplies: what the battery puts in each second, the rest of the circuit takes out. Two things make an appliance powerful: a large voltage (much energy per coulomb) or a large current (many coulombs per second). Large powers are given in kilowatts: $1\ \text{kW} = 1000\ \text{W}$.

<WorkedExample title="A hair dryer">
<Problem>
A hair dryer on a $230\ \text{V}$ socket draws $8.0\ \text{A}$. What is its power, and how much energy does it use in $5.0$ minutes?
</Problem>
<Step title="Power">
$P = VI = 230 \times 8.0 = 1840\ \text{W}$, about $1.8\ \text{kW}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy">
$E = Pt = 1840 \times 300 = 5.5 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Power in a resistor: heating

When a current flows through a resistor, the moving electrons keep bumping into the atoms of the material. Each bump hands some energy to the atoms, which vibrate harder: the resistor warms up. All the electrical energy a resistor takes becomes thermal energy. This is the **heating effect** of a current. It is what a kettle, a toaster, an oven and the old filament lamp are built on, and it is why a phone charger, a laptop and a long extension cable feel warm.

For a resistor, Ohm's law $V = IR$ (lesson 6.6) gives two more forms of $P = VI$. Replace $V$ by $IR$, or replace $I$ by $V/R$:

<Formula label="Power in a resistor" tex="P = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.3.2)" />

The heat made this way is often called **Joule heating**, after the English physicist James Prescott Joule. Which form is handiest depends on what you know:

- In a **series** circuit (lesson 6.7) every resistor carries the same current, so $P = I^2R$ shows that the **largest** resistance gets the most power.
- In a **parallel** circuit every branch has the same voltage, so $P = V^2/R$ shows that the **smallest** resistance gets the most power. That is why a low-resistance short circuit across a socket is dangerous: an enormous current and an enormous power, until the fuse melts.

<WorkedExample title="The heated rear window of a car">
<Problem>
The heating wires in the rear window of a car have a total resistance of $1.2\ \text{Ω}$ and are connected to the $12\ \text{V}$ battery. Find the current and the heating power, and check the power with $I^2R$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Current">
$I = V/R = 12/1.2 = 10\ \text{A}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Power">
$P = V^2/R = 12^2/1.2 = 120\ \text{W}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
$P = I^2R = 10^2 \times 1.2 = 120\ \text{W}$, and $P = VI = 12 \times 10 = 120\ \text{W}$: all three forms agree, as they must.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Power ratings of appliances

The power on a label, the **power rating**, is the power the appliance uses when it gets the voltage written next to it. A lamp labelled "230 V, 60 W" uses $60\ \text{W}$ on a $230\ \text{V}$ socket. From the two numbers you can work out its current and its resistance when it is on.

<WorkedExample title="What the label tells you">
<Problem>
A lamp is labelled "230 V, 60 W". Find the current through it and its resistance when it is on.
</Problem>
<Step title="Current">
$I = P/V = 60/230 = 0.26\ \text{A}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Resistance">
$R = V^2/P = 230^2/60 = 880\ \text{Ω}$. (Or $R = V/I$, with the unrounded current.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Used at another voltage, an appliance does not keep its rated power. If its resistance stays the same, $P = V^2/R$ changes with the **square** of the voltage: a heater rated $2000\ \text{W}$ at $230\ \text{V}$ gives only a quarter, $500\ \text{W}$, on $115\ \text{V}$. (The filament of a lamp is less simple: its resistance grows as it heats up.) Labels also help with safety. A socket circuit is protected by a fuse or circuit breaker of, say, $16\ \text{A}$: at $230\ \text{V}$ that allows at most $230 \times 16 = 3680\ \text{W}$, so a $2000\ \text{W}$ heater, a $2000\ \text{W}$ kettle and a $1000\ \text{W}$ toaster together on one circuit, $5000\ \text{W}$ or $5000/230 = 22\ \text{A}$, are far more than it is made for, and the breaker switches the circuit off.

One more thing about the socket: its voltage is not steady like a battery's. The mains voltage **alternates**: it swings one way and the other $50$ times a second, and the current swings with it. This is **alternating current** (AC); a battery gives direct current. The "230 V" on a label is the **effective** value of the swinging voltage (Siyavula calls it the rms value): the steady voltage that would give a heater the same average power. That is why $P = VI$ and $P = V^2/R$ work with $230\ \text{V}$ just as they do with a battery. Lesson 6.11 shows how an alternating voltage is made.

## Kilowatt-hours and the electricity bill

For an electricity company the joule is far too small a unit. A $2\ \text{kW}$ kettle uses more than a hundred thousand joules every minute: $2000 \times 60 = 1.2 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$. Bills count energy in **kilowatt-hours** instead: the energy used by a power of $1\ \text{kW}$ in one hour.

<Formula label="The kilowatt-hour" tex="1\ \text{kWh} = 1000\ \text{W} \times 3600\ \text{s} = 3.6 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (6.3.1), power as energy per unit time; 1000 W × 3600 s is our own step" />

The handy rule: energy in kWh is the power in **kilowatts** times the time in **hours**. The bill is then simply the number of kilowatt-hours times the price of one kilowatt-hour. The price depends on the country, the year and the contract. The examples below take €0.30 per kWh.

<WorkedExample title="Boiling water for a month">
<Problem>
A $2.0\ \text{kW}$ kettle is on for $15$ minutes a day, every day for $30\ \text{days}$. How much energy does it use, and what does that cost at €0.30 per kWh?
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy in kWh">
$15$ minutes is $0.25\ \text{h}$, so each day $E = 2.0 \times 0.25 = 0.50\ \text{kWh}$, and in $30\ \text{days}$ $15\ \text{kWh}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Cost">
$15 \times 0.30 = 4.50$, so €4.50. In joules that energy is $15 \times 3.6 \times 10^{6} = 5.4 \times 10^{7}\ \text{J}$, a number no bill would want to print.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="An old lamp or an LED?">
<Problem>
An old $60\ \text{W}$ filament lamp and an $8\ \text{W}$ LED lamp give about the same light. Each is on for $5$ hours a day for a year ($365\ \text{days}$). Compare their yearly costs at €0.30 per kWh.
</Problem>
<Step title="Filament lamp">
$E = 0.060\ \text{kW} \times 5 \times 365\ \text{h} = 110\ \text{kWh}$ (exactly $109.5$), costing $109.5 \times 0.30 = 32.85$, so €32.85.
</Step>
<Step title="LED lamp">
$E = 0.008 \times 5 \times 365 = 14.6\ \text{kWh}$, costing $14.6 \times 0.30 = 4.38$, so €4.38.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
The LED saves about €28 a year on a single lamp. Both give the same light, so the old lamp must turn most of its energy into heat: it is far less **efficient**.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Efficiency

Energy is never lost (lesson 3.4), but it does not all come out in the form we want. A lamp should give light, yet it also gives heat; a motor should turn, yet it warms up and hums. The **efficiency** of a device is the fraction of the energy put in that comes out in the useful form. The Siyavula text describes it in words; written as a ratio it is

<Formula label="Efficiency" tex="\eta = \frac{E_\text{out}}{E_\text{in}} = \frac{P_\text{out}}{P_\text{in}}" cite="siy-eff" at="15.1, Useful and wasted energy" />

where "out" means the useful output. The Greek letter $\eta$ is "eta". Efficiency has no unit and is often given as a percentage. It is always less than $1$ (100%): the rest leaves as heat (and sound), and the heat spreads out into the surroundings where it can no longer do anything useful. A heater is the one exception in a sense: heat is the useful output, so nearly all of its energy counts.

<WorkedExample title="How efficient is a kettle?">
<Problem>
A $1.8\ \text{kW}$ kettle takes $3.5$ minutes to heat $1.0\ \text{kg}$ of water (one litre) from 20 °C to the boil at 100 °C. Water needs $4180\ \text{J}$ to warm $1\ \text{kg}$ by one degree (its specific heat, lesson 4.5). What is the efficiency of the kettle?
</Problem>
<Step title="Useful energy">
The water warms by $80$ degrees, so $E_\text{out} = 4180 \times 1.0 \times 80 = 3.34 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$, about $3.3 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy put in">
$3.5$ minutes is $210\ \text{s}$, so $E_\text{in} = Pt = 1800 \times 210 = 3.78 \times 10^{5}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Efficiency">
$\eta = 3.34 \times 10^{5}/3.78 \times 10^{5} = 0.88$, or 88%. The other 12% warms the kettle itself and the air around it.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why power lines use high voltages

Power stations send electricity over long cables, and the cables have resistance. They waste $P = I^2R$ as heat. For a given power $P = VI$, a higher voltage means a smaller current, and the loss falls with the **square** of the current.

<WorkedExample title="Ten times the voltage">
<Problem>
A town takes $1.0\ \text{MW}$ ($10^{6}\ \text{W}$) through cables with a total resistance of $5.0\ \text{Ω}$. Compare the power lost in the cables when the power is sent at $10\ \text{kV}$ and at $100\ \text{kV}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="At 10 kV">
$I = P/V = 10^{6}/10^{4} = 100\ \text{A}$, so the loss is $I^2R = 100^2 \times 5.0 = 5.0 \times 10^{4}\ \text{W}$: 5% of the power.
</Step>
<Step title="At 100 kV">
$I = 10\ \text{A}$, so the loss is $10^2 \times 5.0 = 500\ \text{W}$: only 0.05%. Ten times the voltage gives a hundred times less loss. This is why the overhead lines across the country run at hundreds of kilovolts, and transformers (lesson 6.11) near the houses bring the voltage down again.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The power of a star">
Astronomers describe a star by its power, the energy it sends out every second, which they call its **luminosity**. The Sun's is about $3.8 \times 10^{26}\ \text{W}$ (NASA's Sun Fact Sheet), as much as about $2 \times 10^{23}$ two-kilowatt kettles. Comparing how bright a star looks with the power it really gives off is one of the ways to measure how far away it is.
</Callout>

## Try it

Build a circuit with a battery, a switch and a light bulb. Put an ammeter in series and a voltmeter across the bulb, read the current and the voltage, and work out the bulb's power with $P = VI$. Then add a second bulb, first in series and then in parallel, and see how the brightness (the power) of each bulb changes. Try a wire straight across the battery and watch what happens to the current.

## Summary

- A charge $Q$ passing through a voltage $V$ transfers an energy $E = QV$; with $Q = It$ that is $E = VIt$.
- Electrical power is the energy per second: $P = VI$, in watts ($1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}$). For a resistor $P = I^2R = V^2/R$, and all of it becomes heat (Joule heating).
- In series the largest resistance takes the most power, in parallel the smallest. An appliance's power rating is its power at the voltage on its label.
- The electricity bill counts energy in kilowatt-hours: $1\ \text{kWh} = 3.6 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J}$, and the cost is the number of kWh times the price per kWh.
- Efficiency is the useful fraction of the energy put in, $\eta = E_\text{out}/E_\text{in} = P_\text{out}/P_\text{in}$, always less than 100%. High-voltage power lines keep the $I^2R$ loss small.
