A torch battery says $1.5\ \text{V}$, a car battery $12\ \text{V}$, a wall socket in Europe $230\ \text{V}$. Everyone uses the word "voltage", but what does the number measure? In the previous lesson an electric field pushed on a charge with a force. This lesson follows the energy: how much work the field does on a charge that moves through it. The voltage turns out to be exactly that work, counted per coulomb.

## The field does work, like gravity

Think back to gravity (lessons 3.1 and 3.3). Near the ground the gravitational field is uniform: it pulls every mass $m$ down with the same force $mg$. When a stone falls through a height $h$, gravity does work $W = mgh$ on it, and the stone's gravitational potential energy drops by the same amount. Lift it back up and you put that energy back.

An electric field between two large parallel plates, one charged $+$ and one $-$, is uniform in the same way: in the gap it has the same strength $E$ and direction everywhere, pointing from the positive plate to the negative one. A positive charge $q$ in the gap feels a force $qE$ along the field. If it moves a distance $d$ along the field, the field does work

<Formula label="Work done by a uniform field on a charge moving along it" tex="W = qEd" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 3.2, from ΔU = −qE₀d" />

just as gravity does work $mgh$ on a falling stone. The electric force is like gravity in one more important way: the work it does depends only on where the charge starts and ends, not on the path in between. So the charge has an **electric potential energy** $U$ that depends only on where it is, and the field's work comes out of it:

<Formula label="Change in potential energy" tex="\Delta U = -W" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (3.1.6)" />

The minus sign says that when the field does positive work, the potential energy goes down. A positive charge "falls" along the field and loses potential energy, as a stone falls down and loses height. A negative charge feels the force the other way: it falls towards the positive plate, against the field. If the charge is free to move, the lost potential energy becomes kinetic energy, by conservation of energy.

## Voltage: energy per coulomb

The work $W = qEd$ is proportional to the charge: a charge twice as large gets twice the work. So divide it out. The work done per coulomb is a property of the two points alone, not of the charge you happen to move. It is called the **potential difference** between the two points, or the **voltage**:

<Formula label="Potential difference (voltage)" tex="V = \frac{W}{q}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 17.1" />

(Textbooks often say it the other way round: the work that something else, such as a battery or your hand, must do per coulomb to push the charge back against the field, from the lower point to the higher one. It is the same amount.)

Its unit is the **volt**, after Alessandro Volta, who built the first battery:

<Formula label="The volt" tex="1\ \text{V} = 1\ \text{J/C}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 17.1" />

So "$12\ \text{V}$" on a car battery means that every coulomb of charge that the battery moves from one terminal to the other is given $12$ joules of energy. Turned round, $W = qV$: the energy that a charge $q$ gains or loses between two points is its charge times the voltage between them.

Only differences of potential matter, just as only differences in height matter for gravitational potential energy. Often one place is chosen as zero, such as the negative plate, or the Earth itself (that is why the zero of a circuit is called "earth" or "ground"). The **potential** of any other point is then the voltage between that point and the zero. Field lines always point from high potential to low potential, the way water runs downhill.

<WorkedExample title="Work and voltage">
<Problem>
A small charge of $2.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}$ moves from one plate to the other, and the field does $3.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}$ of work on it. What is the voltage between the plates? How much work would the field do on a charge three times as large?
</Problem>
<Step title="Voltage">
$V = W/q = 3.0 \times 10^{-4}/2.0 \times 10^{-6} = 150\ \text{V}$.
</Step>
<Step title="A larger charge">
The voltage belongs to the plates, so it stays $150\ \text{V}$. Three times the charge gets three times the work: $W = qV = 6.0 \times 10^{-6} \times 150 = 9.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Voltage in a uniform field

Combine $W = qEd$ with $V = W/q$ and the charge drops out. Between two points a distance $d$ apart along a uniform field, the voltage is

<Formula label="Voltage in a uniform field" tex="V = Ed" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (3.2.1)" />

So between parallel plates the potential falls steadily, in a straight line, from the positive plate to the negative plate. (Here $V$ is the size of the voltage. The university course writes the same result as a drop, $\Delta V = -Ed$: the potential at the end minus the potential at the start.) Turned round, $E = V/d$: a strong field means a large voltage over a short distance. This also gives the field a second unit. Since $V = Ed$, the field can be written in volts per metre, and $1\ \text{V/m}$ is the same as $1\ \text{N/C}$ (both are $1\ \text{J/(C m)}$).

<WorkedExample title="An electron between two plates">
<Problem>
Two parallel plates are $2.5\ \text{cm}$ apart with a voltage of $400\ \text{V}$ between them. Find the field between the plates and the force on an electron there. The electron's charge has size $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The field">
$E = V/d = 400/0.025 = 1.6 \times 10^{4}\ \text{V/m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The force">
$F = eE = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.6 \times 10^{4} = 2.6 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{N}$, towards the positive plate, because the electron is negative.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with energy">
Crossing the whole gap, the field does work $Fd = 2.56 \times 10^{-15} \times 0.025 = 6.4 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J}$ on the electron. That is the same as $eV = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 400 = 6.4 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J}$, as it must be.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The electronvolt

For a single electron the joule is an awkwardly large unit, as the last example shows. Atomic and particle physicists use a much smaller one: the energy that an electron (or anything else with a charge of size $e$) gains when it is accelerated through a voltage of one volt. This is the **electronvolt**, eV:

<Formula label="The electronvolt" tex="1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 12.2, Units of energy" />

It is simply $W = qV$ with $q = e$ and $V = 1\ \text{V}$. The nice thing is that a particle with charge $e$ accelerated through $V$ volts gains exactly $V$ electronvolts: through $400\ \text{V}$, $400\ \text{eV}$. Larger energies use the usual prefixes: a keV is $10^3\ \text{eV}$ and an MeV $10^6\ \text{eV}$.

A charged particle that starts at rest and is accelerated through a voltage $V$ turns all the potential energy it loses into kinetic energy. By conservation of energy

<Formula label="Speed gained by a charged particle accelerated from rest" tex="qV = \tfrac12 m v^2" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (3.1.10), with the kinetic energy ½mv²" />

This is how an **electron gun** works. A hot metal wire gives off electrons, and a voltage between the wire and a metal plate with a small hole in it pulls them forward. They shoot through the hole as a thin, fast beam. The picture tube of an old television was built around one: the beam was steered across the screen and made it glow wherever it hit. Electron microscopes and the X-ray tubes in hospitals still use electron guns today.

<WorkedExample title="An electron gun">
<Problem>
In a school electron tube the electrons are accelerated from rest through $2000\ \text{V}$ ($2.0\ \text{kV}$). How much energy does each gain, and how fast does it leave the gun? The electron's mass is $m_e = 9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy">
$2.0\ \text{keV}$, which in joules is $eV = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2000 = 3.2 \times 10^{-16}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed">
From $eV = \tfrac12 m_e v^2$: $v = \sqrt{2eV/m_e} = \sqrt{2 \times 3.2 \times 10^{-16}/9.11 \times 10^{-31}} = 2.7 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m/s}$. That is about 9% of the speed of light ($3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$), reached in a few centimetres.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="A proton through the same kind of voltage">
<Problem>
A proton ($m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$) and an electron both start at rest and are accelerated through $500\ \text{V}$. Compare their speeds.
</Problem>
<Step title="Same energy">
Both charges have size $e$, so both gain $500\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 500 = 8.0 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J}$. (The proton is pushed towards the negative plate, the electron towards the positive one.)
</Step>
<Step title="Speeds">
Proton: $v = \sqrt{2 \times 8.0 \times 10^{-17}/1.67 \times 10^{-27}} = 3.1 \times 10^{5}\ \text{m/s}$. Electron: $v = \sqrt{2 \times 8.0 \times 10^{-17}/9.11 \times 10^{-31}} = 1.3 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
The proton is about 1840 times heavier, so with the same kinetic energy it is $\sqrt{1840} \approx 43$ times slower.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Energies in electronvolts">
Particle physicists and astronomers give the energy of a single particle in electronvolts, or in keV, MeV and larger units for faster particles. When later lessons describe the hot early universe, its temperature is often given as the typical energy of one particle in it, in eV.
</Callout>

## The potential of a point charge

Between two plates the field is the same everywhere. Around a single point charge $Q$ it is not: it gets weaker further out, as $kQ/r^2$ (lesson 6.3). So the potential no longer falls in a straight line. There is also no plate to call zero. The usual choice is to put the zero very far away ("at infinity"), where the field of the charge has died out. The potential at a distance $r$ from the charge is then

<Formula label="Potential of a point charge, zero far away" tex="V = \frac{kQ}{r}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (3.3.3), written with k = 1/(4πε₀)" />

with Coulomb's constant $k = 9.0 \times 10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2\ \text{C}^{-2}$. Here $Q$ keeps its sign. Around a positive charge the potential is positive and falls as you move outwards, along the field lines; around a negative charge it is negative and rises towards zero. Half the distance means twice the potential: it falls off as $1/r$, more gently than the field.

A second charge $q$ at that place has potential energy $U = qV$, so the potential energy of two point charges a distance $r$ apart, again counted from zero when they are far apart, is

<Formula label="Potential energy of two point charges, zero far apart" tex="U = \frac{kQq}{r}" cite="reading2" at="Eq. (3.3.5), written with k = 1/(4πε₀)" />

If the two charges have the same sign, $U$ is positive: work had to be done to push them together, and if you let go they fly apart and that energy becomes kinetic energy. If the signs are opposite, $U$ is negative, just as the potential energy of a mass and a planet is negative when it is counted from zero far away: you must put energy in to pull the two apart.

<WorkedExample title="Near a small charged sphere">
<Problem>
A small charged sphere carries $Q = +2.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$ and acts like a point charge. Find the potential at $3.0\ \text{cm}$ and at $6.0\ \text{cm}$ from its centre. A proton is let go at $3.0\ \text{cm}$. How much energy has it gained when it passes $6.0\ \text{cm}$, and when it is very far away?
</Problem>
<Step title="The potentials">
At $r = 0.030\ \text{m}$: $V = kQ/r = 9.0 \times 10^{9} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-9}/0.030 = 600\ \text{V}$. At $r = 0.060\ \text{m}$, twice as far, half as much: $300\ \text{V}$.
</Step>
<Step title="From 3 cm to 6 cm">
The proton falls through $600 - 300 = 300\ \text{V}$, so it gains $300\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 300 = 4.8 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J}$ of kinetic energy.
</Step>
<Step title="All the way out">
Far away the potential is zero, so in total the proton falls through $600\ \text{V}$ and gains $600\ \text{eV}$. That is all the potential energy it had at the start: $U = kQe/r = 9.0 \times 10^{9} \times 2.0 \times 10^{-9} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}/0.030 = 9.6 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Equipotentials

Join up all the points that have the same potential and you get an **equipotential**: a line on a flat drawing, a surface in space. They work like the contour lines on a map of a hilly landscape, which join points of the same height.

- Moving a charge along an equipotential takes **no work**: the voltage between its start and end is zero, so $W = qV = 0$.
- So the field has no part along an equipotential: **field lines cross equipotentials at right angles**, pointing from higher to lower potential, just as the steepest way down a hill crosses its contour lines at right angles.
- Between parallel plates the equipotentials are flat planes parallel to the plates, equally spaced for equal steps of voltage, because the potential falls steadily. Around a single point charge they are spheres (circles on a drawing) centred on the charge, crowded close together near the charge where the field is strong: since $V = kQ/r$, equal steps of voltage lie ever closer together as $r$ gets smaller.

<Callout kind="note">
Do not confuse potential with potential energy. The potential $V$ (in volts) belongs to a place in the field; the potential energy $U$ (in joules) belongs to a charge at that place, and equals its charge times the potential there.
</Callout>

Check that the equipotentials cross the field arrows at right angles, and that between a row of positive charges and a row of negative charges the potential falls evenly from one to the other.

## Summary

- An electric field does work on a charge that moves through it, as gravity does on a falling mass. In a uniform field, a charge $q$ moving a distance $d$ along the field gets $W = qEd$, and its electric potential energy drops by that amount: $\Delta U = -W$.
- The voltage (potential difference) between two points is the work per coulomb, $V = W/q$, measured in volts: $1\ \text{V} = 1\ \text{J/C}$. A charge $q$ gains or loses $qV$.
- In a uniform field $V = Ed$, so the field can be given in V/m ($1\ \text{V/m} = 1\ \text{N/C}$). Field lines point from high to low potential.
- One electronvolt is the energy gained by a charge $e$ through $1\ \text{V}$: $1\ \text{eV} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{J}$. A particle accelerated from rest through $V$ reaches $\tfrac12 mv^2 = qV$, as in an electron gun.
- With the zero far away, the potential at a distance $r$ from a point charge $Q$ is $V = kQ/r$, and two point charges have potential energy $U = kQq/r$: positive for like charges, negative for unlike ones.
- Equipotentials join points of equal potential. They cross the field lines at right angles, and moving a charge along one takes no work.
