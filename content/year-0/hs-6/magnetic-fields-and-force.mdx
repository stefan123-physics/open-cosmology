Hold a compass near a fridge magnet and the needle swings round to face it. Hold it anywhere else and it settles pointing roughly north. Both effects are magnetism, a force that, like the electric force, acts across empty space. This lesson describes magnets and their fields, and then the surprising rule at the heart of electromagnetism: a magnetic field pushes on an electric charge, but only while the charge is moving. That rule explains why a current-carrying wire is pushed sideways near a magnet, and so how an electric motor works.

## Magnets and their poles

A **magnet** attracts iron, and it attracts or repels other magnets. The pull is strongest near its ends, the **poles**. Hang a bar magnet from a thread and it turns until one end points north: that end is called the **north pole** (N), the other the **south pole** (S). Two magnets show a simple rule, much like the rule for charges in lesson 6.1:

- **like poles repel**: north pushes north away, and south pushes south away;
- **unlike poles attract**: a north pole and a south pole pull towards each other.

There is one big difference with electric charge. A positive charge can exist on its own, but a magnetic pole cannot. Cut a bar magnet in half and you do not get a separate north and south pole: each half is a complete new magnet with a north pole at one end and a south pole at the other. Cut again, and again: every piece, down to a single atom, is still a magnet with two poles. A lone pole, a **magnetic monopole**, has never been found.

Only a few materials, mainly **iron**, **nickel** and **cobalt**, are strongly magnetic; they are called **ferromagnetic**. Where does their magnetism come from? The electrons in every atom move, and moving charges make magnetic fields. In most materials these tiny fields point every which way and cancel. In iron, the fields of neighbouring atoms line up with each other in small regions called **domains**. In an ordinary iron nail the domains point in many directions and the nail is not a magnet; stroke it a few times in one direction with a magnet and many domains turn the same way: the nail is **magnetised**. That is also why a magnet attracts an unmagnetised iron nail with either pole: the magnet lines up domains in the nail so that the near end becomes an opposite pole.

## Magnetic fields and field lines

Around every magnet there is a **magnetic field**: a region in which another magnet, or a piece of iron, feels a magnetic force. As for the electric field in lesson 6.3, the field is drawn with **field lines**. A small compass is the probe: its needle is itself a tiny magnet that can turn freely, and its north end points along the field. Move it around a bar magnet, draw the direction at each point, and join the arrows into lines. Sprinkling iron filings on a sheet of paper over a magnet shows the same pattern at once: each filing becomes a tiny magnet and lines up with the field.

The rules for magnetic field lines:

- outside a magnet the lines run **from the north pole to the south pole**; the arrows show the way the north end of a compass points;
- field lines **never cross**;
- the field is **strongest where the lines are closest together**, near the poles, and weaker where they spread out.

Between a north pole and a south pole facing each other, the lines run straight across from one to the other. Between two north poles facing each other, the lines bend away from each other and leave a spot in the middle where there is almost no field.

## The Earth is a magnet

A compass works anywhere on Earth because the Earth itself has a magnetic field, shaped much like the field of a giant bar magnet inside it. The field is made by electric currents in the hot, liquid iron of the Earth's outer core. The Earth's magnetic poles lie near its geographic poles, the points where its axis of rotation meets the surface, but not exactly at them, and they slowly wander over the years.

Here is a puzzle. The north end of a compass needle points to the Arctic, and unlike poles attract. So the magnetic pole in the Arctic must be a **south pole** of the Earth's magnet. It is still called the "magnetic north pole" because it lies in the north. The field lines of the Earth come out of the ground in the southern hemisphere and go back into it in the northern hemisphere.

The Earth's field is also a shield. Far above the atmosphere, in a region called the **magnetosphere**, it deflects most of the fast charged particles (mostly protons and electrons) that stream out from the Sun, the **solar wind**. Some of them are steered along the field lines down towards the polar regions. There they hit atoms of the upper air and make them glow green and red: the **aurorae**, the northern and southern lights. Why a magnetic field steers moving charges is the subject of the rest of this lesson.

## The force on a moving charge

How strong is a magnetic field, and what does it do to a charge? Experiments show four things. Place a charge $q$ in a magnetic field $B$:

- if the charge is **at rest**, the field does not push on it at all;
- if it **moves along the field lines**, there is still no force;
- if it moves **across** the field lines, there is a force, and it is proportional to the charge, to its speed $v$ and to the strength of the field;
- the force is always **at right angles** to both the velocity and the field, and it reverses if the charge is negative instead of positive.

For a charge moving at right angles to the field, the force is

<Formula label="Magnetic force on a moving charge (at right angles to the field)" tex="F = q v B" cite="reading3" at="Sec. 11.2, Electric motors" />

If the velocity makes an angle $\theta$ with the field, only the part of the motion across the field counts:

<Formula label="Magnetic force at an angle θ to the field" tex="F = q v B \sin\theta" cite="reading4" at="Eq. (8.2.2)" />

At $\theta = 90°$, $\sin\theta = 1$ and the force is the largest, $qvB$. At $\theta = 0°$ (moving along the field) or $180°$ (against it) $\sin\theta = 0$ and there is no force. In this lesson $q$, $v$ and $B$ stand for sizes: the direction comes from a rule of its own, below.

This formula also **defines** the strength of the magnetic field. Its unit is the **tesla** (T), after the Serbian-American engineer Nikola Tesla: a field of $1\ \text{T}$ pushes with $1\ \text{N}$ on a charge of $1\ \text{C}$ moving at $1\ \text{m/s}$ at right angles to it. Since a coulomb per second is an ampere,

<Formula label="The tesla" tex="1\ \text{T} = 1\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{C} \cdot \text{m/s}} = 1\ \frac{\text{N}}{\text{A} \cdot \text{m}}" cite="reading4" at="Sec. 8.2, after Eq. (8.2.2)" />

A tesla is a strong field. The Earth's field at its surface is only about $5 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{T}$, the value the MIT notes use. An older unit is the **gauss**, with $1\ \text{T} = 10^4$ gauss, so the Earth's field is about half a gauss.

Note the contrast with the electric force of lesson 6.3. An electric field pushes on a charge whether it moves or not, and along the field lines. A magnetic field ignores a charge at rest and pushes a moving one sideways, across the field lines.

<WorkedExample title="A proton crossing a field">
<Problem>
A proton (charge $1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$) moves at $2.0 \times 10^5\ \text{m/s}$ through a uniform magnetic field of $0.30\ \text{T}$. Find the magnetic force on it when it moves (a) at right angles to the field, (b) at $30°$ to the field, (c) along the field.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) At right angles">
$F = qvB = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.0 \times 10^5 \times 0.30 = 9.6 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{N}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) At 30°">
$F = qvB\sin\theta = 9.6 \times 10^{-15} \times \sin 30° = 9.6 \times 10^{-15} \times 0.50 = 4.8 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{N}$: half as much.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Along the field">
$\sin 0° = 0$, so $F = 0$: the proton carries straight on.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Which way? The right-hand rule

The force is at right angles to both the velocity and the field, but that still leaves two opposite directions. A hand rule picks the right one. Hold your **right hand** with the thumb up, the first finger pointing straight ahead and the second finger bent at right angles to it:

- point the **first finger** along the velocity of a positive charge (which is also the direction of the current it makes);
- point the **second finger** along the field;
- the **thumb** then points along the force.

For a **negative** charge, such as an electron, the force is the other way: point the first finger against its velocity, or work out the force for a positive charge and reverse it.

An example: a positive charge moves east through a field that points north. First finger east, second finger north: the thumb points up, so the force is upward. An electron moving east in the same field is pushed down. Other books use other hand rules (some use the left hand, with the fingers given other roles); correctly used, they all give the same answer, so learn one and stick to it.

Because three directions at right angles are hard to draw on flat paper, a field pointing **into** the page is drawn as a grid of crosses (×, the tail of an arrow flying away from you) and a field pointing **out of** the page as a grid of dots (•, the tip of an arrow coming towards you).

## Circles in a uniform field

The magnetic force is always at right angles to the velocity. So it never pushes a charge forward or holds it back: it does no work (lesson 3.1), and the charge's speed and kinetic energy stay the same. It only changes the direction of motion. A force of constant size that always acts at right angles to the velocity is exactly what keeps an object going round a circle (lessons 1.10 and 2.9). So a charge moving at right angles to a uniform magnetic field goes round in a **circle**, with the magnetic force as the centripetal force:

<Formula label="Magnetic force as the centripetal force" tex="q v B = \frac{m v^2}{r}" cite="reading4" at="Eq. (8.5.1)" />

Divide both sides by $v$ and solve for the radius:

<Formula label="Radius of the circle" tex="r = \frac{m v}{q B}" cite="reading4" at="Eq. (8.5.2)" />

A faster or heavier particle moves in a wider circle; a stronger field or a larger charge bends it into a tighter one. The time for one turn is the length of the circle divided by the speed:

<Formula label="Time for one turn" tex="T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi m}{q B}" cite="reading4" at="Eq. (8.5.3)" />

The speed has dropped out. A faster particle goes round a bigger circle, but it takes exactly the same time per turn as a slower one. Machines that speed up particles in a circle (cyclotrons) are built on this fact.

If the charge also moves partly along the field, that part of its motion is not affected. It then goes round and round while drifting along the field lines: a spiral, or **helix**. That is how particles from the Sun spiral along the Earth's field lines down towards the poles and make the aurorae.

<WorkedExample title="An electron going round">
<Problem>
An electron (mass $9.11 \times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$, charge of size $1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$) moves at $4.0 \times 10^6\ \text{m/s}$ at right angles to a uniform field of $1.5\ \text{mT}$ ($1.5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{T}$). Find the radius of its circle and the time for one turn.
</Problem>
<Step title="Radius">
$r = mv/(qB) = 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 4.0 \times 10^6/(1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.5 \times 10^{-3}) = 0.015\ \text{m}$: a circle with a radius of about $1.5\ \text{cm}$, small enough to see in a glass tube.
</Step>
<Step title="Time for one turn">
$T = 2\pi m/(qB) = 2\pi \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31}/(1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.5 \times 10^{-3}) = 2.4 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{s}$: about 42 million turns a second.
</Step>
<Step title="A faster electron">
At twice the speed the radius doubles to $0.030\ \text{m}$, but the time for one turn is still $2.4 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{s}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The force on a current-carrying wire

A current in a wire is a stream of moving charges (lesson 6.6). Put the wire in a magnetic field, across the field lines, and every one of those charges is pushed sideways, so the wire as a whole is pushed sideways. How large is the force on a straight piece of wire of length $L$?

In lesson 6.6 the current was $I = nev_dA$: $n$ free electrons per cubic metre, each with charge of size $e$, drifting at speed $v_d$ through a wire of cross-sectional area $A$. A length $L$ of wire contains $N = nAL$ free electrons, and each feels a force $ev_dB$. Together:

$F = nAL \times ev_dB = (nev_dA) \times LB = BIL$.

<Formula label="Force on a current-carrying wire (at right angles to the field)" tex="F = B I L" cite="reading4" at="Eq. (8.3.1), magnitude" />

If the wire makes an angle $\theta$ with the field, the force is $F = BIL\sin\theta$: largest at right angles, zero when the wire lies along the field. The direction comes from the same right-hand rule, with the first finger along the current. Reverse the current, or turn the magnet round, and the force reverses.

The formula gives a way to measure a field in the laboratory: hang a straight piece of wire across the gap of a magnet, pass a known current through it, and measure the extra force on it with a sensitive balance. The field is $B = F/(IL)$.

<WorkedExample title="Measuring a magnet with a balance">
<Problem>
A straight wire crosses the gap between the poles of a magnet at right angles to the field. A length of $5.0\ \text{cm}$ of the wire is in the field. With a current of $2.0\ \text{A}$, a balance shows that the wire is pushed with a force of $4.0\ \text{mN}$. (a) How strong is the field? (b) What force does a current of $3.5\ \text{A}$ give?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The field">
$B = F/(IL) = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}/(2.0 \times 0.050) = 0.040\ \text{T}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A larger current">
$F = BIL = 0.040 \times 3.5 \times 0.050 = 7.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{N} = 7.0\ \text{mN}$. The force is proportional to the current.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The motor principle

Bend a wire into a rectangular loop, put it between the poles of a magnet, and send a current through it. On the two sides of the loop that lie across the field, the current flows in opposite directions, one side "out" and the other "back". So the forces on those two sides point in opposite directions, one up and one down. Two equal, opposite forces that do not act along the same line make the loop turn about its axis, like the two hands on a steering wheel (a turning effect, or torque, lesson 2.10).

On its own the loop would only turn until it faced across the field, rock back and forth, and stop. In a direct-current **electric motor**, a **split-ring commutator** solves this: two half-rings on the axle, touched by sliding contacts, reverse the current in the loop every half turn. The forces then keep turning the loop the same way, round and round. Real motors use coils of many turns, which multiply the force, and they turn electrical energy into the motion of fans, drills, electric cars and the vibrating motor in a phone.

## Summary

- Every magnet has a **north** and a **south pole**: like poles repel, unlike poles attract. Cutting a magnet gives two complete magnets; a lone pole has never been found. Iron, nickel and cobalt are **ferromagnetic**: their **domains** can be lined up to make a magnet.
- **Field lines** run from north to south outside a magnet, never cross, and are closest where the field is strongest. A compass needle lines up along them.
- The **Earth** is a magnet, with its field made by currents in the liquid outer core; the magnetic pole in the Arctic is a south pole of that magnet. The field shields the Earth from the solar wind and steers particles into the aurorae.
- A magnetic field pushes only on a **moving** charge: $F = qvB\sin\theta$, largest ($qvB$) at right angles to the field and zero along it. The force is at right angles to both $v$ and $B$; its direction follows from the **right-hand rule** (first finger velocity of a positive charge, second finger field, thumb force) and reverses for a negative charge.
- The field is measured in **tesla**, $1\ \text{T} = 1\ \text{N/(A m)}$; the Earth's field is about $5 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{T}$.
- The magnetic force does no work, so a charge moving at right angles to a uniform field goes round a **circle** of radius $r = mv/(qB)$, taking a time $T = 2\pi m/(qB)$ per turn, whatever its speed.
- A straight wire carrying a current $I$ feels $F = BIL\sin\theta$. On a loop in a magnet, the forces on opposite sides turn it: the principle of the **electric motor**, kept turning by a split-ring commutator.
