The flash of a camera lights up a whole room for a thousandth of a second, but the small battery that drives it could never deliver that much power at once. Between the battery and the flash tube sits a component that saves the energy up for a few seconds and then lets it all go in one burst: a **capacitor**. This lesson shows what a capacitor is, how much charge and energy it holds, and why it is built the way it is. It puts together the field between two plates (lesson 6.3) and the voltage across them (lesson 6.4).

## Two plates and a gap

The simplest capacitor is two flat metal plates facing each other, with a narrow gap of air or another insulator between them. Connect the plates to the two terminals of a battery. Electrons are pulled off one plate and pushed onto the other, until the voltage between the plates equals the voltage of the battery. One plate now carries a charge $+Q$ and the other $-Q$. That is a **charged capacitor**.

Three things are worth noticing.

- Charge has only been **moved**, from one plate to the other through the wires and the battery. The capacitor as a whole stays neutral: $+Q$ and $-Q$ add up to zero. When we say a capacitor "holds a charge $Q$", we mean the charge on one plate.
- The insulator in the gap stops the charge from crossing straight back. Disconnect the battery and the charges stay on the plates, held by their attraction across the gap.
- Connect the two plates with a wire (or a lamp) and the electrons flow back. The capacitor **discharges** and gives back what it stored.

Any two conductors separated by an insulator form a capacitor; flat plates are just the easiest to understand. In a circuit diagram a capacitor is drawn as two short parallel lines, a picture of the two plates.

## Capacitance: charge per volt

Charge a capacitor to a higher voltage and it holds more charge. Experiments show that the charge is exactly proportional to the voltage between the plates: twice the voltage, twice the charge. The constant of proportionality is the **capacitance** $C$:

<Formula label="Charge on a capacitor" tex="Q = CV" cite="mit" at="Eq. (5.1.1)" />

(The MIT notes write the voltage as $|\Delta V|$, to stress that it is the size of a potential difference; here it is $V$, as in lesson 6.4.) The capacitance says how much charge the capacitor stores for every volt. Its unit is the coulomb per volt, called the **farad** (F) after Michael Faraday:

<Formula label="The farad" tex="1\ \text{F} = 1\ \text{C/V}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 5.1" />

One farad is a very large capacitance, as the next section shows. Most capacitors in electronics are measured in microfarads ($1\ \mu\text{F} = 10^{-6}\ \text{F}$), nanofarads ($10^{-9}\ \text{F}$) or picofarads ($1\ \text{pF} = 10^{-12}\ \text{F}$).

<WorkedExample title="Charge on a capacitor">
<Problem>
A capacitor marked $470\ \mu\text{F}$ is connected to a $9.0\ \text{V}$ battery. How much charge does it store? How much would it store on a $4.5\ \text{V}$ battery?
</Problem>
<Step title="Charge at 9.0 V">
$Q = CV = 470 \times 10^{-6} \times 9.0 = 4.2 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{C}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Half the voltage">
The capacitance belongs to the capacitor and does not change. Half the voltage gives half the charge: $Q = 470 \times 10^{-6} \times 4.5 = 2.1 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{C}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The parallel-plate capacitor

What decides the capacitance? For two flat plates, each of area $A$, a distance $d$ apart, the answer follows from two facts about the field between them.

First, from lesson 6.4: between two parallel plates the field is uniform, and the voltage across the gap is $V = Ed$. So the field is the voltage divided by the gap:

<Formula label="Field between the plates" tex="E = \frac{V}{d}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (3.2.1), its size rearranged" />

Second, the field depends on how densely the charge is packed on the plates. Field lines start on the charges of the positive plate and end on the negative plate (lesson 6.3), so the more charge there is on each square metre, the more crowded the lines and the stronger the field. In fact the field is proportional to the charge per square metre, $Q/A$:

<Formula label="Field of the charged plates" tex="E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (5.2.1), with the charge per square metre written as Q/A" />

Here $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}$ is the **electric constant**, the same constant that sits inside Coulomb's constant, $k = 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$. (Proving this formula needs Gauss's law, which comes at university; here we take it as given.) Put the two expressions for $E$ equal: $V/d = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)$, so $Q = (\varepsilon_0 A/d)\,V$. Comparing with $Q = CV$ gives the capacitance:

<Formula label="Capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor" tex="C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (5.2.4)" />

The capacitance depends only on the shape of the capacitor, not on the charge or the voltage.

- **Larger plates** give a larger capacitance: at the same voltage the charge has more room to spread out, so more of it fits on.
- **A narrower gap** gives a larger capacitance: a given charge makes a given field, and across a narrow gap that field adds up to only a small voltage. So for each volt, more charge is needed.

This is why real capacitors use very large plates (long strips of metal foil, rolled up) with a very thin insulator between them.

<WorkedExample title="A home-made capacitor">
<Problem>
Two square sheets of aluminium, $20\ \text{cm}$ by $20\ \text{cm}$, are held $1.0\ \text{mm}$ apart by small plastic spacers, with air between them. Find the capacitance. Then connect a $12\ \text{V}$ battery: find the charge on each plate and the field in the gap.
</Problem>
<Step title="Capacitance">
The area is $A = 0.20 \times 0.20 = 0.040\ \text{m}^2$, so $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d = 8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 0.040/0.0010 = 3.5 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{F}$, about $350\ \text{pF}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Charge">
$Q = CV = 3.54 \times 10^{-10} \times 12 = 4.2 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$: $+4.2\ \text{nC}$ on one plate and $-4.2\ \text{nC}$ on the other.
</Step>
<Step title="Field">
$E = V/d = 12/0.0010 = 1.2 \times 10^{4}\ \text{V/m}$. As a check, $Q/(\varepsilon_0 A) = 4.25 \times 10^{-9}/(8.85 \times 10^{-12} \times 0.040) = 1.2 \times 10^{4}\ \text{V/m}$ too.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample title="How large is one farad?">
<Problem>
How large would the plates of a one-farad capacitor have to be, with an air gap of $1.0\ \text{mm}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="Rearrange">
From $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$: $A = Cd/\varepsilon_0 = 1.0 \times 0.0010/(8.85 \times 10^{-12}) = 1.1 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m}^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Picture it">
That is more than $100\ \text{km}^2$: a square about $11\ \text{km}$ on each side, big enough to cover a city. A farad really is a huge unit.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Energy stored in a capacitor

Charging a capacitor takes work. Each new bit of charge has to be carried from the negative plate to the positive plate, against the push of the charge already there. At the start the plates are uncharged, the voltage is zero and the first bit of charge goes across for free. As the charge grows, the voltage $V = Q/C$ grows with it, so each later bit needs more work. The voltage grows in step with the charge already moved, from $0$ to its final value $V$, so on average each bit of charge is carried across $\tfrac12 V$. The work, which is stored as energy in the capacitor, is the charge times this average voltage:

<Formula label="Energy stored in a capacitor" tex="U = \tfrac12 QV = \tfrac12 CV^2 = \frac{Q^2}{2C}" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (5.4.1) and (5.4.2)" />

The three forms say the same thing; use $Q = CV$ to go from one to another. (On a graph of voltage against charge, a straight line through zero, the stored energy is the area of the triangle under the line. A stretched spring stores half of its final force times the stretch, for the same reason.)

Notice the factor $\tfrac12$. A battery that pushes a charge $Q$ through a voltage $V$ does work $QV$, but the capacitor ends up with only half of it. The other half is turned into heat in the wires while the current flows.

<WorkedExample title="A camera flash">
<Problem>
The flash capacitor of a camera has a capacitance of $220\ \mu\text{F}$ and is charged to $330\ \text{V}$. How much energy does it store? If the flash empties it in $1.0\ \text{ms}$, at what average power is the energy released?
</Problem>
<Step title="Energy">
$U = \tfrac12 CV^2 = \tfrac12 \times 220 \times 10^{-6} \times 330^2 = 12\ \text{J}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Power">
Power is energy per second: $12\ \text{J}/0.0010\ \text{s} = 1.2 \times 10^{4}\ \text{W}$, or $12\ \text{kW}$. The battery fills the capacitor slowly over a few seconds, and the capacitor gives the energy back thousands of times faster.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Energy in the field itself">
Where is a capacitor's energy kept? For flat plates, put $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$ and $V = Ed$ into $U = \tfrac12 CV^2$: you get $U = \tfrac12 \varepsilon_0 E^2 \times Ad$. Here $Ad$ is the volume of the gap, so every cubic metre of field holds the same energy, $\tfrac12 \varepsilon_0 E^2$ (MIT notes, Eqs. 5.4.3 and 5.4.4). Energy belongs to the field, not to the plates. Light is an electric and magnetic field travelling through space (lesson 6.12), and it carries energy in just this way. For the first tens of thousands of years of the universe, most of its energy was in light of this kind.
</Callout>

## Dielectrics

In most real capacitors the gap is not filled with air but with an insulating material: paper, plastic film, glass, ceramic or a thin layer of oxide. Such an insulator between the plates is called a **dielectric**. It does three useful jobs. It keeps the plates apart, even when they are rolled up tightly; it can stand a stronger field than air before a spark jumps across; and it makes the capacitance larger.

Why larger? The field between the plates **polarizes** the dielectric, as a charged balloon polarizes a wall (lesson 6.1). In every molecule the electrons shift a little towards the positive plate. Near the positive plate this leaves a thin layer of negative charge on the face of the dielectric, and near the negative plate a layer of positive charge. These layers partly cancel the charge on the plates, so the field in the gap is weaker. For the same charge the voltage $V = Ed$ is lower, and so $C = Q/V$ is larger. The factor by which the capacitance grows is the **dielectric constant** $\kappa$ (the Greek letter kappa) of the material:

<Formula label="Capacitance with a dielectric" tex="C = \kappa C_0" cite="mit" at="Eq. (5.5.1), where the notes write κ as κₑ" />

Here $C_0$ is the capacitance with empty space between the plates, so for parallel plates filled with a dielectric $C = \kappa\varepsilon_0 A/d$. Every material has $\kappa > 1$. For air it is so close to 1 (1.0006) that air counts as empty space; the MIT notes give about 3.7 for paper, 4 to 6 for glass and 80 for water.

<WorkedExample title="Filling the gap with paper">
<Problem>
The gap of the home-made capacitor above ($350\ \text{pF}$ with air) is filled with paper of the same thickness, $\kappa = 3.7$. What is its capacitance now, and how much charge does the same $12\ \text{V}$ battery put on it?
</Problem>
<Step title="Capacitance">
$C = \kappa C_0 = 3.7 \times 3.54 \times 10^{-10} = 1.3 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{F}$, or $1.3\ \text{nF}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Charge">
$Q = CV = 1.31 \times 10^{-9} \times 12 = 1.6 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{C}$, $3.7$ times as much as with air.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Capacitors in everyday life

Almost every electronic device contains capacitors, from a few to many millions.

- **Flashes and defibrillators.** A capacitor is filled slowly and emptied in an instant, as in the camera flash above. A defibrillator in a hospital or a public place stores a few hundred joules this way and sends them through a patient's chest in a few milliseconds.
- **Smoothing.** A power adapter turns the alternating voltage of the wall socket into a steady one. A large capacitor charges up whenever the voltage is high and gives charge back when it dips, smoothing out the ripples.
- **Memory.** A computer's working memory stores each bit as a tiny charge on one of billions of microscopic capacitors: charged means 1, empty means 0.
- **Touch screens and keys.** Your finger is a conductor. Brought close to a pattern of electrodes in a phone's screen, it changes their capacitance a little, and the phone measures where.
- **Tuning.** Together with a coil, a capacitor makes an electric circuit that oscillates at one natural frequency, like a pendulum. Changing the capacitance tunes a radio to a station.

## Try it

Charge the capacitor with the battery and watch the charges gather on the plates. Change the plate area and the gap, and read the capacitance: check that halving the gap doubles it. Then disconnect the battery and change the gap again: the charge cannot change now, so watch what happens to the voltage and the stored energy. Finally connect a light bulb and let the capacitor discharge.

## Summary

- A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator. Charging it moves charge from one plate to the other, leaving $+Q$ and $-Q$; the capacitor as a whole stays neutral.
- The charge is proportional to the voltage: $Q = CV$. The capacitance $C$ is measured in farads, $1\ \text{F} = 1\ \text{C/V}$; real capacitors are usually in the pF to mF range.
- For two parallel plates of area $A$ a distance $d$ apart, $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$, with $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}$. The field between the plates is uniform, $E = V/d$.
- A charged capacitor stores an energy $U = \tfrac12 QV = \tfrac12 CV^2 = Q^2/(2C)$, and this energy sits in the field between the plates.
- A dielectric between the plates is polarized and weakens the field, so the capacitance grows: $C = \kappa C_0$ with $\kappa > 1$.
- Capacitors store energy for flashes and defibrillators, smooth power supplies, hold the bits of computer memory, sense touches and tune radios.
