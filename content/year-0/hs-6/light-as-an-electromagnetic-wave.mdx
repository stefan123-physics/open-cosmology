When you switch on a radio, it picks up a signal sent from a mast many kilometres away, with no wire between the two. Sunlight reaches us across 150 million kilometres of almost empty space. Lesson 5.8 said that both are electromagnetic waves, and that what waves in them is an electric and a magnetic field. This lesson shows how two fields can make a wave that needs no medium at all, how fast it must go, how it is made, and what it carries.

## Two fields that make each other

Two ideas from this block fit together like the two halves of a puzzle.

- **A changing magnetic field makes an electric field.** That is Faraday's law from lesson 6.11: when the magnetic flux through a loop changes, a voltage is induced around the loop. The electric field that drives the charges round is there even when there is no wire: the wire only shows it.
- **A changing electric field makes a magnetic field.** This was the idea of the Scottish physicist James Clerk Maxwell. Lesson 6.10 showed that currents make magnetic fields. Maxwell argued that a changing electric field must do the same, even where no charge flows. Think of a capacitor being charged (lesson 6.5): current flows in the wires, but no charge crosses the gap between the plates. Still, the field between the plates grows, and Maxwell's idea says that this growing field makes a magnetic field around the gap, just as if the current carried on straight through. At university this extra source of magnetic field is called the displacement current.

Together the two rules say something striking. Nature treats electric and magnetic fields almost the same way: each one, when it changes, makes the other.

## A wave that keeps itself going

Now imagine an electric field somewhere in empty space that changes. The change makes a magnetic field around it. That magnetic field is itself changing, so it makes an electric field a little further on. That electric field is changing too, so it makes a magnetic field further on still, and so on. The pair of fields hands itself on through space, with no material needed to carry it. That is an **electromagnetic wave**.

Worked out in full, the two rules fix how the wave looks:

- The electric field $E$ and the magnetic field $B$ are **at right angles to each other**.
- Both are **at right angles to the direction of travel**. An electromagnetic wave is a transverse wave, like the wave on a rope in lesson 5.3, which is why light can be polarized (lesson 5.12).
- The two fields rise and fall **together**: where $E$ is largest, $B$ is largest too. They are in phase.

To picture it, let the wave travel along a table away from you. If its electric field points up and down, its magnetic field points left and right, and both fields flip direction every half wavelength.

## Maxwell's prediction: the speed of light

When Maxwell worked out how fast such a wave must travel, the answer contained nothing but two constants you already know: the permeability of free space $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$ from magnetism (lesson 6.10) and the electric constant $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}$ from electricity (lesson 6.5).

<Formula label="The speed of an electromagnetic wave" tex="c = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0 \varepsilon_0}}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (13.4.15), p. 9" />

Both constants can be measured on a lab bench, with currents, magnets and charged plates, without ever looking at light.

<WorkedExample title="Working out the speed of light from electricity and magnetism">
<Problem>
Use $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$ and $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}$ to find the speed of an electromagnetic wave in vacuum.
</Problem>
<Step title="Multiply the two constants">
$\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 8.85 \times 10^{-12} = 1.11 \times 10^{-17}\ \text{s}^2/\text{m}^2$. (The units work out to one over a speed squared.)
</Step>
<Step title="Take the square root and invert">
$\sqrt{1.11 \times 10^{-17}} = 3.33 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{s/m}$, so $c = 1/(3.33 \times 10^{-9}) = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
This is the speed of light of lesson 5.8, to three figures. Two constants from experiments with magnets and capacitors give the speed of light.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Maxwell saw that the speed he had calculated matched the measured speed of light, and he drew the conclusion: **light is an electromagnetic wave.** Optics, electricity and magnetism, three subjects that had seemed separate, turned out to be one. Every member of the spectrum of lesson 5.8, from radio waves to gamma rays, is the same kind of wave with a different wavelength.

The fields also fix each other's size. In vacuum the electric field and the magnetic field of the wave are always in the same ratio, and that ratio is the speed of light:

<Formula label="The two fields of the wave" tex="E = cB" cite="mit" at="Eq. (13.4.27), p. 11" />

Because $c$ is so large, the magnetic field of a wave is tiny in teslas even when the electric field is easy to measure.

<WorkedExample title="The fields of a radio signal">
<Problem>
Near a house a few kilometres from a radio mast, the electric field of the radio wave swings with an amplitude of $0.030\ \text{V/m}$. What is the amplitude of its magnetic field?
</Problem>
<Step title="Use E = cB">
$B = E/c = 0.030/(3.00 \times 10^{8}) = 1.0 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{T}$. That is half a million times weaker than the Earth's magnetic field ($5 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{T}$, lesson 6.10), yet a radio can still pick up the wave, for example through a rod aerial, whose electrons are pushed back and forth by the electric field.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## How electromagnetic waves are made

A charge at rest has a steady electric field around it. A steady current has a steady magnetic field. Neither of these changes, so nothing travels away. To make a wave, the fields must change, and that happens when charges **accelerate**: speed up, slow down or change direction. Accelerating charges send out electromagnetic waves.

The simplest source is an **antenna**. An alternating voltage pushes the electrons in a metal rod up and down, so one end becomes positive and the other negative, then the other way round, again and again. The changing electric field near the rod, and the changing magnetic field of the current in it, break away and travel outwards as a wave. The wave has the same frequency as the electrons' motion, and its wavelength follows from $\lambda = c/f$. A common design is the half-wave antenna: two rods in a line, each a quarter of a wavelength long.

<WorkedExample title="An antenna for a television channel">
<Problem>
A television channel broadcasts at $600\ \text{MHz} = 6.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{Hz}$. How long should each rod of a half-wave antenna for this channel be?
</Problem>
<Step title="Wavelength">
$\lambda = c/f = (3.00 \times 10^{8})/(6.00 \times 10^{8}) = 0.500\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Rod length">
Each rod is a quarter wavelength: $0.500/4 = 0.125\ \text{m}$, about $12.5\ \text{cm}$, so the whole antenna is $25\ \text{cm}$ long. A station with a lower frequency needs a longer antenna, which is why masts for long radio waves are so tall.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The test of Maxwell's idea came from the German physicist Heinrich Hertz in 1887. He made sparks jump across a small gap between two metal rods: each spark makes charges rush back and forth very fast, so the rods act as an antenna. A few metres away, a loop of wire with a tiny gap of its own showed small sparks too, although nothing connected it to the first circuit. Something had crossed the room. Hertz also showed that these invisible waves could be reflected, just like light. Radio, television, mobile phones and Wi-Fi all grew out of his sparks.

Light itself is made the same way, by charges that move back and forth very fast: the electrons in atoms and in hot matter. How atoms do this is the subject of Block 7.

## Electromagnetic waves carry energy

Sunlight warms your skin and a microwave oven heats your food: electromagnetic waves deliver energy. Lesson 6.5 showed that an electric field stores energy, $\tfrac12 \varepsilon_0 E^2$ in every cubic metre. A magnetic field stores energy too. Together, the energy in each cubic metre of the wave is

<Formula label="Energy per cubic metre of the fields" tex="u = \tfrac12 \varepsilon_0 E^2 + \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}" cite="mit" at="Eqs. (13.6.1) and (13.6.2), p. 15" />

In an electromagnetic wave, with $E = cB$, the two parts turn out to be exactly equal: half of the energy is in the electric field and half in the magnetic field. The wave carries this energy away from its source at the speed of light.

As for sound (lesson 5.5), the **intensity** $I$ of the wave is the power it carries through each square metre at right angles to its direction, in W/m². A small source that sends a power $P$ out equally in all directions spreads it over a sphere, so

<Formula label="Intensity from a point source" tex="I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^2}" cite="mit" at="Eq. (13.6.13), p. 17" />

Twice as far away, the intensity is four times smaller.

<WorkedExample title="How much power reaches a radio">
<Problem>
A radio transmitter sends out $50\ \text{kW}$, which we assume spreads equally in all directions. What is the intensity of its waves $10\ \text{km}$ away?
</Problem>
<Step title="Spread the power over a sphere">
$I = P/(4\pi r^2) = (5.0 \times 10^{4})/(4\pi \times (1.0 \times 10^{4})^2) = 4.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{W/m}^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="What it means">
Each square metre receives only 40 millionths of a watt, but a radio needs far less than that to pick up the signal.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A **changing magnetic field makes an electric field** (Faraday, lesson 6.11) and a **changing electric field makes a magnetic field** (Maxwell's idea). The two keep making each other, so they can travel through empty space as an **electromagnetic wave**.
- In the wave, $E$ and $B$ are at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel (the wave is transverse), rise and fall together, and in vacuum $E = cB$.
- Maxwell found the speed $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} \approx 3.00 \times 10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$ from two constants of electricity and magnetism: the speed of light. **Light is an electromagnetic wave**, and so is every band of the spectrum (lesson 5.8).
- **Accelerating charges** make electromagnetic waves, for example electrons pushed back and forth in an antenna; Hertz made and detected such waves with sparks in 1887.
- The waves carry **energy**, shared equally between the electric and the magnetic field; the intensity from a point source is $I = P/(4\pi r^2)$.
