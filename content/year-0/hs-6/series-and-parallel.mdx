A torch has one bulb and a battery in a single loop. A house has dozens of lamps, a fridge and a phone charger, each switched on and off on its own. A string of old fairy lights goes dark when one bulb breaks; the lights in your kitchen do not go out when one bulb fails. The difference is in how the parts are connected: one after the other (**in series**) or side by side (**in parallel**). In the previous lesson one resistor was connected to a battery and $V = IR$ gave the current. This lesson connects several, and finds the rules that govern any circuit.

## Circuit diagrams

Physicists draw circuits with standard symbols, like a road map, rather than as pictures. The lines stand for wires. Their resistance is so small that we ignore it, so the potential hardly changes along a wire: it changes only where the current passes through a component.

| Component | Symbol | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| cell | a long thin line and a short thick line, side by side | gives energy to the charge; the long line is the positive terminal |
| battery | two or more cells joined | the same, with a larger voltage |
| resistor | a small rectangle (or a zigzag) | turns electrical energy into heat |
| light bulb | a circle with a cross | glows when a current flows through it |
| switch | a break in the line with a hinged piece | opens or closes the circuit |
| ammeter | a circle with A | measures the current through it |
| voltmeter | a circle with V | measures the voltage between its two ends |

A current flows only around a **closed** circuit: open a switch anywhere in the loop and it stops everywhere in that loop. If two points are joined by a wire with almost no resistance, by accident, nearly all the current takes that easy path instead of going through the components. That is a **short circuit**, and the large current it causes can overheat the wires; a fuse in series breaks the circuit when that happens.

As in the previous lesson, an ammeter is connected in series with the component whose current it measures, and a voltmeter in parallel, across the component.

## Kirchhoff's two rules

Two conservation laws decide how current and voltage are shared in every circuit. They are called Kirchhoff's rules, after the German physicist Gustav Kirchhoff.

**The junction rule.** A **junction** is a point where wires meet. Charge cannot be made or destroyed (lesson 6.1), and in a steady current it does not pile up anywhere. So as much charge flows out of a junction each second as flows in: the current into a junction equals the current out. If a current $I$ splits into two branches,

<Formula label="Junction rule (charge is conserved)" tex="I = I_1 + I_2" cite="mit" at="Sec. 7.4, Eq. (7.4.1); two-branch form Eq. (7.3.4)" />

**The loop rule.** Voltage is energy per coulomb (lesson 6.4). Follow one coulomb of charge all the way round a closed loop. The battery gives it energy; each component it passes through takes some away. When it is back at its starting point it is at the same potential as before, so the energy it gained must equal the energy it lost: around any closed loop, the voltage gains add up to the voltage drops. Counting each gain as a positive change in potential $\Delta V$ and each drop as a negative one, the changes around the loop add up to zero:

<Formula label="Loop rule (energy is conserved)" tex="\sum \Delta V = 0" cite="mit" at="Sec. 7.4, Eq. (7.4.2)" />

In a single loop with one battery, the battery's voltage is shared among the components.

These two rules, together with $V = IR$ for each resistor, are all you need. Series and parallel are the two simplest cases.

## Resistors in series

Resistors are **in series** when they are joined end to end, so the current passes through each one in turn without branching off. Then:

- **Each resistor carries the same current.** There are no branches, and charge does not pile up (the junction rule), so an ammeter placed before, between or after the resistors gives the same reading.
- **The voltage drops add up to the battery's voltage.** Going once round the loop (the loop rule):

<Formula label="Voltages in series" tex="V = V_1 + V_2 + \ldots" cite="mit" at="Sec. 7.3, Eq. (7.3.1)" />

Each resistor obeys $V_1 = IR_1$, $V_2 = IR_2$, with the same current $I$. So $V = IR_1 + IR_2 = I(R_1 + R_2)$, and the pair acts exactly like one resistor of resistance $R_1 + R_2$:

<Formula label="Resistors in series" tex="R_\text{s} = R_1 + R_2 + \ldots" cite="mit" at="Sec. 7.3, Eqs. (7.3.2) and (7.3.3)" />

Every extra resistor in series raises the total resistance, so the same battery drives a smaller current. Each resistor takes a share of the battery's voltage in proportion to its resistance, so the largest resistor gets the largest share. A chain of resistors used to split a voltage like this is called a **voltage divider**.

<WorkedExample title="Two resistors in series">
<Problem>
A $10\ \text{Ω}$ and a $20\ \text{Ω}$ resistor are connected in series to a $6.0\ \text{V}$ battery. Find the current and the voltage across each resistor.
</Problem>
<Step title="Total resistance">
$R_\text{s} = 10 + 20 = 30\ \text{Ω}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Current">
$I = V/R_\text{s} = 6.0/30 = 0.20\ \text{A}$, through both resistors and the battery.
</Step>
<Step title="Voltage across each">
$V_1 = IR_1 = 0.20 \times 10 = 2.0\ \text{V}$ and $V_2 = IR_2 = 0.20 \times 20 = 4.0\ \text{V}$. Check with the loop rule: $2.0 + 4.0 = 6.0\ \text{V}$, the battery's voltage. The resistor that is twice as large takes twice the voltage.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The weakness of a series circuit shows in the old fairy lights: if one bulb breaks, the only path is broken and every bulb goes out.

## Resistors in parallel

Resistors are **in parallel** when they sit side by side, each connected across the same two points, so the current divides between several paths. Then:

- **Each has the same voltage across it.** Both ends of every branch are joined by wires to the same two points, and the potential difference between two points has only one value.
- **The currents add up.** By the junction rule the current $I$ from the battery splits between the branches, $I = I_1 + I_2$, and joins up again on the other side.

Each branch obeys $I_1 = V/R_1$ and $I_2 = V/R_2$ with the same $V$. So $I = V/R_1 + V/R_2 = V(1/R_1 + 1/R_2)$. One resistor $R_\text{p}$ that draws the same current, $I = V/R_\text{p}$, must therefore have

<Formula label="Resistors in parallel" tex="\frac{1}{R_\text{p}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \ldots" cite="mit" at="Sec. 7.3, Eqs. (7.3.5) and (7.3.6)" />

For just two resistors, adding the fractions gives a handy form, "product over sum":

<Formula label="Two resistors in parallel" tex="R_\text{p} = \frac{R_1 R_2}{R_1 + R_2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 7.3, unnumbered equation after Eq. (7.3.6)" />

Adding a resistor in parallel opens one more path for the charge, so the total resistance goes **down** and the battery delivers **more** current. The total is always smaller than the smallest resistor in the group, and the smallest resistor carries the largest current. A short circuit is the extreme case: a path of almost zero resistance in parallel takes almost all the current.

<WorkedExample title="Two resistors in parallel">
<Problem>
A $20\ \text{Ω}$ and a $30\ \text{Ω}$ resistor are connected in parallel to a $12\ \text{V}$ battery. Find the current in each resistor, the current from the battery and the total resistance.
</Problem>
<Step title="Current in each branch">
Each resistor has the full $12\ \text{V}$ across it: $I_1 = 12/20 = 0.60\ \text{A}$ and $I_2 = 12/30 = 0.40\ \text{A}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Current from the battery">
Junction rule: $I = 0.60 + 0.40 = 1.0\ \text{A}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Total resistance">
$R_\text{p} = 20 \times 30/(20 + 30) = 12\ \text{Ω}$, smaller than either resistor. Check: $I = V/R_\text{p} = 12/12 = 1.0\ \text{A}$, as before.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

This is how a house is wired. Every lamp and appliance is connected in parallel across the mains, so each gets the full voltage, each has its own switch, and one broken bulb leaves all the others burning.

## Combinations

Many circuits mix the two. Wherever some resistors are clearly in series, or clearly in parallel, swap them for the single resistor that is equivalent to them, and keep doing this until only one resistor is left. Then retrace the steps to find each current and voltage.

<WorkedExample title="A resistor in series with a parallel pair">
<Problem>
A $12\ \text{V}$ battery drives a current through a $4.0\ \text{Ω}$ resistor, which is in series with a parallel pair of $10\ \text{Ω}$ and $15\ \text{Ω}$. Find the current from the battery and the current in each resistor of the pair.
</Problem>
<Step title="Replace the parallel pair">
$R_\text{p} = 10 \times 15/(10 + 15) = 6.0\ \text{Ω}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Total resistance and current">
The circuit is now $4.0\ \text{Ω}$ and $6.0\ \text{Ω}$ in series: $R = 4.0 + 6.0 = 10\ \text{Ω}$, so $I = 12/10 = 1.2\ \text{A}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Voltages">
Across the $4.0\ \text{Ω}$ resistor: $1.2 \times 4.0 = 4.8\ \text{V}$. Across the pair: $1.2 \times 6.0 = 7.2\ \text{V}$. Loop rule: $4.8 + 7.2 = 12\ \text{V}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Currents in the pair">
Both have $7.2\ \text{V}$ across them: $7.2/10 = 0.72\ \text{A}$ and $7.2/15 = 0.48\ \text{A}$. Junction rule: $0.72 + 0.48 = 1.2\ \text{A}$, the current from the battery.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Real batteries: emf and internal resistance

So far the battery's voltage was fixed. A real battery behaves a little differently. Inside it, a chemical reaction acts like a pump: it does work on the charge, lifting each coulomb from the low-potential terminal to the high one. The work it does on each coulomb is called its **emf** (short for "electromotive force", though it is not a force), written $\varepsilon$ and measured in volts.

A real battery is not a perfect source, though. The current also has to pass through the battery itself, which has a small resistance of its own, the **internal resistance** $r$. Part of the emf, $Ir$, is used up there and warms the battery. What is left for the outside circuit, the **load**, is the terminal voltage:

<Formula label="Terminal voltage of a real battery" tex="V = \varepsilon - Ir" cite="mit" at="Sec. 7.2, Eq. (7.2.5)" />

The internal resistance is simply one more resistor in series with the load $R$. The loop rule then gives $\varepsilon = IR + Ir$, so

<Formula label="Current from a battery with internal resistance" tex="I = \frac{\varepsilon}{R + r}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 7.2, Eq. (7.2.7)" />

With no current the terminal voltage equals the emf; the more current the load draws, the lower the terminal voltage. The voltage $Ir$ used up inside is often nicknamed the "lost volts", but its energy has not vanished: it is what warms the battery. This is why a car's headlights dim for a moment when the starter motor draws its large current.

<WorkedExample title="A battery with internal resistance">
<Problem>
A battery has an emf of $6.0\ \text{V}$ and an internal resistance of $0.40\ \text{Ω}$. It is connected to a $2.6\ \text{Ω}$ resistor. Find the current and the terminal voltage. What current would flow if the terminals were short-circuited by a thick wire?
</Problem>
<Step title="Current">
$I = \varepsilon/(R + r) = 6.0/(2.6 + 0.40) = 2.0\ \text{A}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Terminal voltage">
$V = \varepsilon - Ir = 6.0 - 2.0 \times 0.40 = 5.2\ \text{V}$. Check: $V = IR = 2.0 \times 2.6 = 5.2\ \text{V}$ across the load.
</Step>
<Step title="Short circuit">
With $R \approx 0$, only the internal resistance limits the current: $I = \varepsilon/r = 6.0/0.40 = 15\ \text{A}$, and all the energy heats the battery. Short-circuiting a battery is dangerous for exactly this reason.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Circuit diagrams use standard symbols. An ammeter goes in series with a component, a voltmeter in parallel across it.
- Kirchhoff's **junction rule**: the current into a junction equals the current out (charge is conserved). The **loop rule**: around any closed loop the voltage gains equal the voltage drops (energy is conserved).
- **Series**: the same current through each resistor, the voltages add, and $R_\text{s} = R_1 + R_2 + \ldots$ is larger than any of them.
- **Parallel**: the same voltage across each resistor, the currents add, and $1/R_\text{p} = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2 + \ldots$ is smaller than the smallest one; for two, $R_\text{p} = R_1R_2/(R_1 + R_2)$.
- Combinations are solved by replacing series and parallel groups step by step.
- A real battery has an emf $\varepsilon$ and an internal resistance $r$: its terminal voltage $V = \varepsilon - Ir$ drops as the current grows.
