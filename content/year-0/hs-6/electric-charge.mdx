Pull a jumper over your head on a dry winter day and you hear it crackle; touch a door handle after walking across a carpet and you feel a small spark. Rub a balloon on your hair and it picks up scraps of paper and sticks to the wall. The ancient Greeks noticed the same thing with amber, a fossil resin: rubbed with cloth, it attracted bits of straw. The word "electron" comes from the Greek word for amber. All of these are signs of **electric charge**, and this block is about what charge does, at rest and in motion. This first lesson is about charge that sits still: **electrostatics**.

## Two kinds of charge

Experiments with rubbed objects show that there are exactly two kinds of charge. They were named **positive** ($+$) and **negative** ($-$). Rub two glass rods with silk and hang one of them on a thread: bring the other near and it swings away. Rub a plastic rod with fur and bring it near the hanging glass rod: it swings towards it. So the glass and the plastic carry different kinds of charge, and

- **like charges repel**: two positive or two negative objects push each other apart;
- **unlike charges attract**: a positive and a negative object pull towards each other.

These pushes and pulls are forces, the **electrostatic force**. Like every force they come in pairs (lesson 2.4): the glass rod pushes the hanging rod away, and the hanging rod pushes back just as hard. The closer the two charged objects are, the stronger the force. How much stronger is the subject of the next lesson.

All ordinary matter contains huge amounts of both kinds of charge. Everything is built from atoms, and every atom contains tiny, light particles called **electrons**, which carry negative charge, held by a heavier part that carries positive charge (lesson 7.1 tells how this was discovered). When an object has exactly as much positive as negative charge, the two cancel and the object is **neutral**. It is **charged** when one kind is in excess, and in practice that always happens because some electrons have moved:

- an object with **extra electrons** is negatively charged;
- an object that has **lost electrons** is positively charged.

The positive charge stays where it is: in solids, only electrons are moved around.

## Charging by rubbing

When two different materials are rubbed together, electrons move from one to the other. Which way they go depends on the materials: some hold on to their electrons more tightly than others. Rub a balloon on dry hair and electrons move from the hair to the balloon. The balloon becomes negative, the hair positive. A list that orders materials by how easily they give up electrons is called a **triboelectric series** (from the Greek for "rubbing"): glass and human hair sit near the giving end, while rubber lies towards the taking end, and plastics such as polyester and Teflon sit right at it. Rubbing two materials from the list, the one nearer the taking end ends up negative.

Rubbing does not make charge: it only separates it. Before rubbing, the hair and the balloon were both neutral. Afterwards the balloon has, say, a billion extra electrons and the hair is missing exactly a billion. Add the two charges and the total is still zero. This is one of the most basic laws of physics:

<Callout kind="key" title="Conservation of charge">
The total charge of an isolated system never changes. Charge can move from one object to another, but it is never created and never destroyed.
</Callout>

It is a conservation law like the conservation of energy in lesson 3.4, and as far as anyone has measured, it holds without exception.

## Conductors and insulators

In some materials electrons can move freely from atom to atom. These are **conductors**: metals, the human body, tap water and the ground. In others the electrons stay bound to their own atoms and cannot travel through the material. These are **insulators**: plastic, glass, rubber, dry wood, dry air.

This makes a big difference to where extra charge ends up.

- Put extra charge on an **insulator** and it stays where it was put. A rubbed balloon is charged only on the patch that was rubbed.
- Put extra charge on a **conductor** and it spreads out at once. The extra charges all repel each other, so they move as far apart as they can: over the whole **outside surface**. On a round metal ball they spread evenly; on a pointed object they crowd together at the points.

That is why you can charge a plastic rod by rubbing it while holding it in your hand, but not a metal rod: charge on the metal rod would flow straight through the rod and your body into the ground.

## The elementary charge and the coulomb

The SI unit of charge is the **coulomb** (C). The symbol for a charge is $Q$. One coulomb is an enormous amount of charge for electrostatics. Rubbed objects carry only tiny fractions of a coulomb, so we often use the **microcoulomb** (μC, a millionth of a coulomb, $10^{-6}\ \text{C}$) and the **nanocoulomb** ($1\ \text{nC} = 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$), with the prefixes from lessons 0.1 and 0.2.

Every electron carries exactly the same amount of charge. Its size is the **elementary charge**:

<Formula label="The elementary charge" tex="e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}" cite="reading3" at="Sec. 16.4, Unit of charge" />

An electron has charge $-e$. Since charge moves around only by whole electrons, every charge you can find is a whole number of elementary charges. Charge is **quantized**: it comes in steps, like money that comes in cents, with no half cent.

<Formula label="Charge is quantized" tex="Q = n e" cite="reading3" at="Sec. 16.4, Unit of charge" />

Here $n$ is a whole number: positive for an object that has lost $n$ electrons, negative for one that has $n$ extra electrons. Turned around, $n = Q/e$ counts the electrons. One coulomb is $1/(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 6.25 \times 10^{18}$ elementary charges, more than six billion billion.

The steps are far too small to notice in everyday life: a rubbed balloon has many billions of extra electrons, and adding one more changes its charge by a tiny fraction. The American physicists Robert Millikan and Harvey Fletcher measured $e$ in 1909, by watching tiny charged oil drops float between two charged metal plates.

<WorkedExample title="Counting the electrons on a comb">
<Problem>
After being pulled through dry hair, a plastic comb has a charge of $-4.0\ \text{nC}$. How many extra electrons does it carry?
</Problem>
<Step title="Convert to coulombs">
$Q = -4.0\ \text{nC} = -4.0 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Divide by the elementary charge">
The size of the charge divided by the charge of one electron: $4.0 \times 10^{-9} / 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 2.5 \times 10^{10}$. The comb carries $2.5 \times 10^{10}$ extra electrons, twenty-five billion, and the hair is missing exactly as many.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Charging by contact

Touch a charged object against a neutral conductor and some of the charge flows across: both end up with the same kind of charge. When two **identical** conductors touch, they act for a moment as one larger conductor, and the total charge spreads evenly over both. Pulled apart again, each keeps half of the total:

<Formula label="Charge shared by two identical conductors" tex="Q = \frac{Q_1 + Q_2}{2}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 16.3, Arrangement of charge" />

Here $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are the charges before contact (with their signs) and $Q$ is the charge on each sphere afterwards. The rule needs identical spheres: conductors of different shapes or sizes share unequally. Insulators do not share at all, since their charge cannot move.

<WorkedExample title="Two metal spheres touch">
<Problem>
Two identical metal spheres stand on plastic stands. Sphere A carries $+7.0\ \text{nC}$ and sphere B carries $-3.0\ \text{nC}$. They are made to touch and then separated. What is the charge on each sphere, and how many electrons moved, in which direction?
</Problem>
<Step title="The total charge is conserved">
$Q_1 + Q_2 = +7.0 + (-3.0) = +4.0\ \text{nC}$, before and after.
</Step>
<Step title="Share it equally">
$Q = +4.0/2 = +2.0\ \text{nC}$ on each sphere.
</Step>
<Step title="Which electrons moved?">
Sphere A went from $+7.0\ \text{nC}$ to $+2.0\ \text{nC}$: it gained $5.0\ \text{nC}$ of negative charge, so electrons flowed from B to A. Their number is $5.0 \times 10^{-9} / 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3.1 \times 10^{10}$. Check with B: from $-3.0\ \text{nC}$ to $+2.0\ \text{nC}$ is a loss of $5.0\ \text{nC}$ of negative charge, the same electrons.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Earthing

The Earth is a huge conductor. Connect a charged conductor to the ground, by a metal wire or simply by touching it with your hand (your body is a conductor too), and its extra charge flows away until it is neutral. A negatively charged object sends its extra electrons into the ground; a positively charged one draws electrons up from the ground. The Earth is so large that this changes it by nothing you could notice. Connecting to the ground like this is called **earthing** (or **grounding**). The spark you feel at a door handle is exactly this: the charge you picked up from the carpet flows through the metal.

Earthing is also used for safety. A building collects charge from the air around it, and a pointed metal lightning rod on the roof, connected to the ground, lets some of that charge leak away, which may make a strike a little less likely. Its main job, though, is to give lightning a safe path: if lightning does strike, the rod and its thick wire carry the current down into the ground instead of through the building.

## Charging by induction

A charged object can also charge a conductor **without touching it**. Take a neutral metal sphere on a plastic stand and bring a negatively charged rod near it.

1. The rod repels the free electrons in the sphere, which crowd to the far side. The near side is left positive. The sphere as a whole is still neutral: its charge has only been rearranged.
2. Touch the far side with your finger. The pushed-away electrons now have a way out, and some flow through you into the ground.
3. Take your finger away, then remove the rod. The electrons that left cannot come back, so the sphere is left **positively charged**, opposite to the rod.

This is **charging by induction**. The rod loses nothing: it never touched the sphere. A sensitive instrument called an **electroscope** uses the first step to detect charge: two thin metal leaves hang from a metal rod, and a charged object brought near the top moves electrons in the metal: a negative object pushes electrons down into the leaves, a positive one pulls electrons up out of them. Either way both leaves get the same kind of charge as the object, so they repel and spread apart.

## Polarization: why a balloon sticks to a wall

A rubbed balloon sticks to a wall, but the wall is an insulator and is not charged. How can a charged object attract a neutral one?

In an insulator the electrons cannot leave their atoms, but they can shift a little within them. A negative balloon pushes the electrons in each atom near the wall's surface slightly away, so every atom gets a slightly positive side facing the balloon and a slightly negative side facing away. This is **polarization**. The positive sides are a little closer to the balloon than the negative sides, and the closer charges are, the stronger the force, so the attraction is slightly larger than the repulsion. Added over billions of atoms, the net pull is enough to hold a light balloon. The wall stays neutral throughout: no charge has been added or removed.

The same effect explains why a charged comb picks up small scraps of paper, and why a thin stream of water from a tap bends towards a charged balloon.

## Summary

- There are two kinds of charge, **positive** and **negative**. **Like charges repel, unlike charges attract**, more strongly the closer they are. A neutral object has equal amounts of both.
- Objects become charged when **electrons move**: extra electrons make an object negative, missing electrons make it positive. Rubbing two materials moves electrons from one to the other.
- **Conservation of charge:** the total charge of an isolated system never changes.
- In **conductors** charge moves freely and spreads over the outside surface; in **insulators** it stays put.
- Charge is **quantized**: $Q = ne$, with $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$ and $n$ a whole number. The unit is the **coulomb**.
- Two identical conductors that touch share their total charge equally, $Q = (Q_1 + Q_2)/2$. **Earthing** lets charge flow to or from the ground; **induction** charges a conductor without contact; **polarization** lets a charged object attract a neutral one.
