In lesson 6.10 an electric current made a magnetic field. Michael Faraday asked the reverse question: can a magnetic field make a current? In 1831 he found that it can, but only while something **changes**. This discovery, electromagnetic induction, is how almost all the world's electricity is made: in every power station a magnet and a coil turn past each other. It also explains transformers, induction hobs, electric guitars and the card readers that work by a tap.

## A magnet, a coil and a meter

Connect a coil of wire to a sensitive ammeter and push a bar magnet into it. While the magnet moves, the meter shows a current. Hold the magnet still inside the coil and the current is zero, however strong the magnet. Pull it out and the meter swings the other way. Move it faster and the current is larger.

The same happens if you hold the magnet still and move the coil: only the relative motion counts. And with no magnet at all, a second coil whose current is switched on or off does the same thing, since lesson 6.10 showed that a current in a coil makes a field like a bar magnet's.

In each case the coil acts as if a battery had been put in it, but only while the magnetic field through the coil is changing. The voltage that drives this current is called an **induced emf**, written $\varepsilon$, and measured in volts, like the emf of a battery in lesson 6.7.

## Magnetic flux

To say exactly what has to change, we need a measure of how much magnetic field passes **through** a loop. Picture the field lines: the more lines go through the loop, the larger this amount. It is called the **magnetic flux**, $\Phi$ (capital phi). For a flat loop of area $A$ in a uniform field $B$,

<Formula label="Magnetic flux" tex="\Phi = BA\cos\theta" cite="mit" at="Sec. 10.3, Magnetic flux" />

Here $\theta$ is the angle between the field and the **normal** to the loop, the line that stands at right angles to the loop's surface. When the loop faces the field squarely, $\theta = 0$ and $\Phi = BA$, the largest value. When the field runs along the plane of the loop, $\theta = 90^\circ$ and no field lines pass through it: $\Phi = 0$. In between, only the part of the field at right angles to the loop, $B\cos\theta$, counts.

The unit of flux is the **weber** (Wb):

<Formula label="The weber" tex="1\ \text{Wb} = 1\ \text{T m}^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, below Eq. (10.1.2)" />

So a field of $1\ \text{T}$ through an area of $1\ \text{m}^2$, face on, is a flux of one weber. The flux can therefore change in three ways: the field gets stronger or weaker, the area inside the field changes, or the loop turns so that $\theta$ changes.

<WorkedExample title="Flux through a tilted loop">
<Problem>
A rectangular loop of wire measures $8.0\ \text{cm}$ by $5.0\ \text{cm}$. It is in a uniform field of $0.40\ \text{T}$. Find the flux through it when (a) it faces the field, (b) its normal makes an angle of $60^\circ$ with the field, (c) its plane lies along the field.
</Problem>
<Step title="The area">
$A = 0.080 \times 0.050 = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Face on">
$\Phi = BA = 0.40 \times 4.0 \times 10^{-3} = 1.6 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{Wb}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Tilted">
$\Phi = BA\cos 60^\circ = 1.6 \times 10^{-3} \times 0.50 = 8.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{Wb}$: half as much.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Along the field">
$\cos 90^\circ = 0$, so $\Phi = 0$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Faraday's law

Faraday's experiments, and many since, show that the induced emf depends on how **fast** the flux changes. For a coil of $N$ turns, each with the same change of flux $\Delta\Phi$ in a time $\Delta t$,

<Formula label="Faraday's law of induction" tex="\varepsilon = -N\,\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 10.3, Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction" />

Each turn of the coil gets the same emf, and the turns are in series, so their emfs add: that is why $N$ appears. A steady flux, however large, gives no emf. Doubling the speed of the change doubles the emf. The minus sign tells the direction, and has a section of its own below.

If the coil is part of a closed circuit with resistance $R$, the emf drives a current $I = \varepsilon/R$, by Ohm's law (lesson 6.6).

<WorkedExample title="Switching off an electromagnet">
<Problem>
A small coil of $150$ turns, each of area $2.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$, sits face on in the field of an electromagnet, $0.30\ \text{T}$. The electromagnet is switched off and its field falls steadily to zero in $0.050\ \text{s}$. What emf is induced in the coil while the field falls?
</Problem>
<Step title="The change of flux through one turn">
At the start $\Phi = BA = 0.30 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-3} = 6.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{Wb}$; at the end it is zero. So $\Delta\Phi = 0 - 6.0 \times 10^{-4} = -6.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{Wb}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Faraday's law">
$\varepsilon = -N\,\dfrac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t} = -150 \times \dfrac{-6.0 \times 10^{-4}}{0.050} = 1.8\ \text{V}$. The emf lasts only as long as the field is falling. Once it is zero, the emf is gone.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Lenz's law: the minus sign

Which way does the induced current flow? The rule is named after the physicist Emil Lenz:

> The induced current flows in the direction whose magnetic field **opposes the change** in flux that causes it.

Push the north pole of a magnet towards a coil: the flux through the coil grows. The induced current makes the near end of the coil a **north** pole too, which pushes the magnet back. Pull the magnet away again: now the flux falls, and the current reverses, so that the near end becomes a **south** pole, which pulls the magnet back. Either way, the coil resists the motion. With the grip rule of lesson 6.10 (fingers along the current, thumb towards the north face), you can then find which way the current goes round.

Lenz's law is no extra law of nature: it is **conservation of energy** (lesson 3.4). The current in the coil heats the wire, and that energy must come from somewhere. Because the coil pushes back, you have to do work to move the magnet, and that work becomes the electrical energy. If the induced current helped the change instead, the coil would pull the magnet in faster, which would make a larger current, which would pull harder still: energy from nothing, which never happens.

## A rod moving through a field

The simplest generator is a metal rod of length $L$ moving with speed $v$ at right angles to a field $B$. Lesson 6.9 showed that each free charge in the rod feels a force $qvB$ along the rod, so charges pile up at one end until an emf builds up between the ends.

Faraday's law gives its size. Let the rod slide along two metal rails that are joined at one end, so that rod, rails and join make a loop. In a time $\Delta t$ the rod moves $v\,\Delta t$, so the area of the loop grows by $Lv\,\Delta t$ and the flux by $BLv\,\Delta t$. Dividing by $\Delta t$:

<WorkedExample title="A rod on rails">
<Problem>
A metal rod $0.25\ \text{m}$ long slides at a steady $4.0\ \text{m/s}$ along two rails, at right angles to a field of $0.60\ \text{T}$. The rails are joined by a $2.0$ Ω resistor (the rod and rails have almost no resistance). Find the emf, the current, the force needed to keep the rod moving, and compare the power you put in with the heat made in the resistor.
</Problem>
<Step title="Emf and current">
$\varepsilon = BLv = 0.60 \times 0.25 \times 4.0 = 0.60\ \text{V}$, and $I = \varepsilon/R = 0.60/2.0 = 0.30\ \text{A}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The braking force">
The current in the rod feels the force of lesson 6.9, $F = BIL = 0.60 \times 0.30 \times 0.25 = 0.045\ \text{N}$. By Lenz's law it points backwards, against the motion, so you must push with $0.045\ \text{N}$ to keep the speed steady.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy check">
Power you put in: $Fv = 0.045 \times 4.0 = 0.18\ \text{W}$. Heat in the resistor: $I^2R = 0.30^2 \times 2.0 = 0.18\ \text{W}$ (lesson 6.8). They are equal: every joule of work you do becomes a joule of heat.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Generators and alternating current

A **generator** turns mechanical energy into electrical energy. In the simplest kind, a coil of $N$ turns and area $A$ spins at a steady rate in a uniform field. As it turns, the angle $\theta$ between the field and its normal keeps growing, so the flux $BA\cos\theta$ rises and falls over and over. Turning at the angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$ of lesson 5.1, with $f$ turns per second, the coil has $\theta = \omega t$, and Faraday's law gives the emf

<Formula label="Emf of a coil turning in a field" tex="\varepsilon = NBA\omega\sin(\omega t)" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (10.4.3), p. 12" />

(The step from $\cos$ to $\sin$ needs calculus; the result is that the emf is largest when the flux is changing fastest, as the coil passes through $\theta = 90^\circ$, where the flux itself is zero.) The emf swings between $+NBA\omega$ and $-NBA\omega$, $f$ times a second: it is an **alternating** emf, and the current it drives is **alternating current** (AC). Turning the coil faster raises both the frequency and the peak emf.

The ends of the spinning coil are joined to two **slip rings** that turn with it, and fixed carbon **brushes** press on the rings to carry the current out. In a **DC generator** the slip rings are replaced by one ring split in two (a split-ring commutator, as in the motor of lesson 6.9), which swaps the connections every half turn, so that the current outside always flows the same way, though it still rises and falls. A generator is the motor of lesson 6.9 run backwards: a motor turns electrical energy into motion, a generator turns motion into electrical energy. In big power stations the magnet spins and the coils stand still, so that the large currents need no sliding contacts.

The mains in Europe and South Africa is made by generators that turn $50$ times a second, so it alternates at $50\ \text{Hz}$, as lesson 6.8 said. The "$230\ \text{V}$" of lesson 6.8 is the effective (rms) value; the peak of the mains voltage is larger by a factor $\sqrt{2}$, about $\sqrt{2} \times 230 \approx 325\ \text{V}$.

<WorkedExample title="A small generator">
<Problem>
A generator coil has $100$ turns of area $0.010\ \text{m}^2$ and turns $50$ times a second in a field of $0.20\ \text{T}$. What is its peak emf?
</Problem>
<Step title="Angular frequency">
$\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi \times 50 = 314\ \text{rad/s}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Peak emf">
$NBA\omega = 100 \times 0.20 \times 0.010 \times 314 = 63\ \text{V}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Transformers

A **transformer** changes the size of an alternating voltage. Two coils are wound on one iron core: the **primary**, with $N_p$ turns, connected to the supply, and the **secondary**, with $N_s$ turns, connected to the appliance. The alternating current in the primary makes an alternating field in the iron (lesson 6.10), and the iron leads the same changing flux through every turn of both coils. Each turn of either coil therefore gets the same emf, so the voltages are in the ratio of the turns:

<Formula label="The transformer" tex="\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p}" cite="reading3" at="slide 26" />

With more turns on the secondary ($N_s > N_p$) it is a **step-up** transformer; with fewer, a **step-down** transformer, like the one in a phone charger. A transformer needs a **changing** flux, so it only works with alternating current: on a battery it gives nothing, except a brief pulse when you switch on or off. This is one of the main reasons the mains is AC.

A good transformer wastes very little energy, and an **ideal** transformer wastes none. Then, by conservation of energy, the power that goes into the primary comes out of the secondary: $V_pI_p = V_sI_s$. Stepping the voltage up by some factor steps the current down by the same factor.

<WorkedExample title="A transformer for a 12 V lamp">
<Problem>
A garden lamp needs $12\ \text{V}$ and draws $1.5\ \text{A}$. Its transformer works from the $230\ \text{V}$ mains and has $1150$ turns on the primary. (a) How many turns does the secondary need? (b) Taking the transformer as ideal, what current does it take from the mains?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The turns ratio">
$N_s = N_p\,\dfrac{V_s}{V_p} = 1150 \times \dfrac{12}{230} = 60$ turns.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The power balance">
The lamp uses $P = V_sI_s = 12 \times 1.5 = 18\ \text{W}$, so $I_p = P/V_p = 18/230 = 0.078\ \text{A}$: a much smaller current at the much higher voltage.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

### Why power lines use high voltage

A power station must send a power $P = VI$ (lesson 6.8) along lines that have some resistance $R$. The current heats the lines at the rate $I^2R$, and that energy is lost. For the same power, a higher voltage means a smaller current, and the loss falls with the **square** of the current. So electricity is stepped up to hundreds of thousands of volts for the long lines and stepped down again, in stages, near the homes that use it.

<WorkedExample title="Losses in a power line">
<Problem>
A power station sends $10\ \text{MW}$ along lines with a total resistance of $5.0$ Ω. Find the power lost in the lines if it is sent (a) at $10\ \text{kV}$, (b) at $400\ \text{kV}$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) At 10 kV">
$I = P/V = 10 \times 10^6/(10 \times 10^3) = 1000\ \text{A}$, and the loss is $I^2R = 1000^2 \times 5.0 = 5.0 \times 10^6\ \text{W}$: half of everything sent.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) At 400 kV">
$I = 10 \times 10^6/(400 \times 10^3) = 25\ \text{A}$, and the loss is $25^2 \times 5.0 = 3125\ \text{W}$, about $3.1\ \text{kW}$, or 0.031%. Forty times the voltage gives $40^2 = 1600$ times less loss.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The **magnetic flux** through a loop is $\Phi = BA\cos\theta$, with $\theta$ the angle between the field and the normal to the loop. Its unit is the weber, $1\ \text{Wb} = 1\ \text{T m}^2$.
- **Faraday's law:** a changing flux through a coil of $N$ turns induces an emf $\varepsilon = -N\,\Delta\Phi/\Delta t$. A steady flux induces nothing; a faster change induces more.
- **Lenz's law** (the minus sign): the induced current opposes the change that causes it. It follows from conservation of energy: the work done against it becomes electrical energy.
- A rod moving through a field has an emf $\varepsilon = BLv$. A coil turning in a field gives an alternating emf $\varepsilon = NBA\omega\sin(\omega t)$: this is the **generator**, which makes the $50\ \text{Hz}$ mains.
- A **transformer** changes an alternating voltage in the ratio of its turns, $V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p$; an ideal one keeps the power, $V_pI_p = V_sI_s$. Power lines use high voltage because a smaller current loses far less as heat, $I^2R$.
