This block has been about electric and magnetic forces. They hold atoms and molecules together, make your hair stand up near a balloon, drive every motor and carry light across space. Particle for particle they are far stronger than gravity: lesson 6.2 found that a proton pulls on an electron about $2.3 \times 10^{39}$ times harder electrically than gravitationally. And yet, when you look at the sky, it is gravity that keeps the Moon around the Earth, the Earth around the Sun and the Sun in its orbit through the galaxy. This last lesson of the block asks why the weakest force wins on the largest scales, and what that means for the universe as a whole.

## Two inverse-square laws, side by side

Coulomb's law (lesson 6.2) and Newton's law of gravitation (lesson 2.11) have the same shape. For two point charges $Q_1$ and $Q_2$, or two masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, a distance $r$ apart:

<Formula label="Coulomb's law" tex="F_e = k\,\frac{Q_1 Q_2}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 9.2, Coulomb's law" />

<Formula label="Newton's law of gravitation" tex="F_g = G\,\frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 2.4, Newton's law of universal gravitation" />

In this block $k = 9.0 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$ and $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$. Both forces act across empty space, both fall with the square of the distance. Because of that, if you divide one by the other, the $r^2$ cancels: **the ratio of the two forces between two particles is the same at every distance**. Moving the particles apart weakens both forces by exactly the same factor.

For two protons, each with charge $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$ and mass $m_p = 1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, the ratio is

<Formula label="Electric against gravitational force, two protons" tex="\frac{F_e}{F_g} = \frac{k e^2}{G\, m_p^2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 2.13.1 (c), the same ratio for an electron and a proton, independent of r" />

<WorkedExample title="Two protons">
<Problem>
Two protons repel each other electrically and attract each other gravitationally. Which force wins, and by how much? How heavy would a particle with charge $e$ have to be for the two forces between two such particles to balance?
</Problem>
<Step title="The ratio">
$\dfrac{F_e}{F_g} = \dfrac{9.0 \times 10^9 \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19})^2}{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times (1.67 \times 10^{-27})^2} = 1.2 \times 10^{36}$. The electric push is about a million million million million million million times stronger than the gravitational pull. Two lone protons fly apart.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare with the hydrogen atom">
Lesson 6.2 found $2.3 \times 10^{39}$ for an electron and a proton. The ratio is larger there because the electron is about 1800 times lighter than a proton, so its gravity is weaker still, while its charge is just as large.
</Step>
<Step title="A particle heavy enough to balance">
The forces balance when $Gm^2 = ke^2$, that is $m = e\sqrt{k/G} = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times \sqrt{9.0 \times 10^9/6.67 \times 10^{-11}} = 1.9 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{kg}$. That is about 2 micrograms, a speck of dust, and $1.1 \times 10^{18}$ times the mass of a proton. No particle carrying a single elementary charge comes anywhere near that.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

So between the building blocks of matter, gravity does not stand a chance. Inside atoms, molecules and everyday objects the electric force does everything; gravity only matters because the Earth is so big.

## Why big things are neutral

Here is the catch. The electric force comes in two kinds, attraction and repulsion, and ordinary matter contains both kinds of charge in almost exactly equal amounts (lesson 6.1). Every atom has as many electrons as protons, and the charge of an electron is exactly as large as that of a proton, only negative. Experiments show the two charges are equal in size to an astonishing precision (MIT 8.02T notes, Sec. 2.13.1).

Think of the Earth and the Moon. Every proton in the Moon is pushed away by every proton in the Earth, but pulled just as hard by every electron in the Earth. Every electron in the Moon feels the opposite. Add up all these pushes and pulls and they cancel: two neutral bodies exert no electric force on each other at all.

Matter also keeps itself neutral. If a body somehow gets an excess of one kind of charge, it attracts the opposite kind from everything around it and pushes its own kind away, until the excess is gone. A cloud of gas in space that has lost electrons pulls electrons back in from its surroundings. The stronger the electric force, the faster any imbalance is undone. That is why the very strength of the electric force is also why we hardly ever notice it on large scales.

## How small an imbalance would matter?

How neutral do bodies have to be for gravity to win? Suppose two bodies of masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ each carry a tiny excess of the same sign: $f$ extra elementary charges for every nucleon (proton or neutron) they contain. A nucleon has a mass of about $m_p$, so a body of mass $m_1$ contains about $m_1/m_p$ nucleons and carries a charge $Q_1 = f\,e\,m_1/m_p$, and the same for the second body. Put these charges into Coulomb's law and divide by Newton's law: the masses and the distance cancel, and only the fraction $f$ is left.

<Formula label="Repulsion against gravity for two slightly charged bodies" tex="\frac{F_e}{F_g} = f^2\,\frac{k e^2}{G\, m_p^2}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 2.13.1 (c) and (d): the ratio for single particles and the neutrality of planets, combined here for a small imbalance" />

The fraction $ke^2/(Gm_p^2)$ is the two-proton ratio from the worked example, $1.24 \times 10^{36}$ before rounding. The electric repulsion equals the gravitational attraction when $f^2 \times 1.24 \times 10^{36} = 1$, so when $f = 9.0 \times 10^{-19}$: **one extra elementary charge for every $1.1 \times 10^{18}$ nucleons** (the same number as in the worked example above, and for the same reason). That holds for two grains of sand as much as for two stars.

<WorkedExample title="A Moon with a whisper of charge">
<Problem>
Suppose the Earth ($5.97 \times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}$) and the Moon ($7.35 \times 10^{22}\ \text{kg}$) each had one electron missing for every $10^{15}$ nucleons, so $f = 10^{-15}$. Their distance is $3.84 \times 10^8\ \text{m}$. Compare the electric force between them with their gravitational attraction.
</Problem>
<Step title="The charges">
The Earth contains about $5.97 \times 10^{24}/1.67 \times 10^{-27} = 3.57 \times 10^{51}$ nucleons, so its charge would be $10^{-15} \times 3.57 \times 10^{51} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C} = 5.72 \times 10^{17}\ \text{C}$. The Moon, with $4.40 \times 10^{49}$ nucleons, would carry $7.04 \times 10^{15}\ \text{C}$.
</Step>
<Step title="The two forces">
Electric: $F_e = 9.0 \times 10^9 \times 5.72 \times 10^{17} \times 7.04 \times 10^{15}/(3.84 \times 10^8)^2 = 2.5 \times 10^{26}\ \text{N}$, a repulsion. Gravity: $F_g = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24} \times 7.35 \times 10^{22}/(3.84 \times 10^8)^2 = 2.0 \times 10^{20}\ \text{N}$, an attraction.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
$F_e/F_g = 1.2 \times 10^{6}$, the same as $f^2 \times 1.24 \times 10^{36} = 10^{-30} \times 1.24 \times 10^{36}$. An imbalance of one charge in a million billion nucleons would push the Moon away a million times harder than gravity holds it. Since the Moon stays in its orbit, the Earth and the Moon must be far more neutral than that.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Gravity only adds up

Gravity has no such escape. Mass comes in one kind only: there is no negative mass that could cancel it. So gravity **only attracts**, and the pull of every particle in a body adds to the pull of all the others. A pebble has a feeble pull; the Earth, made of about $3.6 \times 10^{51}$ nucleons all pulling the same way, holds you to the ground. A star adds up even more, and a galaxy of countless stars more still.

So the two forces trade places as things get bigger:

- **atoms, molecules, everyday objects:** the electric force rules, gravity is negligible between the particles;
- **mountains, planets, stars, galaxies:** the electric forces cancel because the bodies are neutral, and gravity, which never cancels, takes over.

This is why the motion of planets, the shape of stars and the orbits of stars in galaxies are all problems of gravity, the subject of Block 2 and of most of the cosmology course.

## What about magnetism?

Space is full of magnetic fields. The Earth has one, made by currents in its liquid core, which steers the charged particles of the solar wind into the aurorae (lesson 6.9). The Sun has strong, tangled fields. Even whole galaxies have them: the Planck satellite mapped the direction of the Milky Way's field from the polarised glow of its dust, and the airborne observatory SOFIA traced the field along the spiral arms of the Whirlpool Galaxy in the same way.

Yet magnetic fields do not hold planets, stars or galaxies together, for two reasons:

- A magnetic field pushes only on **moving charges**. A star or planet as a whole carries almost no net charge, so the field has almost no grip on it.
- Every magnet has a north and a south pole, and no lone pole has ever been found. Magnetic effects of the two poles partly cancel, so a magnet's pull dies away faster with distance than an inverse-square force.

What magnetic fields do shape is thin, partly charged gas: the solar wind, the glowing loops above the Sun's surface, and the gas and dust between the stars of a galaxy. They matter for how stars form from that gas, but the paths of the stars themselves are set by gravity.

## Gravity and the whole universe

If gravity rules the large scales, what does it do to the universe as a whole? Newton asked exactly this. In letters to the theologian Richard Bentley in 1692 and 1693 (shown on the slides of MIT 8.286, Lecture 5) he reasoned that a finite cloud of stars would fall together into one great mass in the middle. So he imagined the stars spread evenly through an **infinite** space. Then, he argued, each star is pulled equally in every direction, does not know which way to go, and stays at rest: a **static** universe, unchanging for ever.

The trouble is that gravity only attracts. A universe evenly filled with matter that never moves would need no gravitational pull anywhere, yet everywhere there is matter to pull. In the university course Guth shows, with a reformulation of Newton's own law (Gauss's law of gravity), that this is a contradiction: an evenly filled universe cannot stand still. As Guth tells it, Newton's symmetry argument went unchallenged for more than two hundred years, until Einstein, working with his new theory of gravity, realised that such a universe would collapse.

Things are even worse for a static universe than that. Any region that happens to be a little denser than average pulls a little harder on its surroundings, gathers more matter and becomes denser still. Newton himself guessed that matter would clump in this way into many separate masses. With electric charge such clumping is undone, because like charges repel. With gravity it only grows. A universe ruled by gravity cannot simply sit still: it must be expanding or collapsing.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Where 8.286 picks this up">
Hubble's redshifts (lesson 5.13) show that our universe is expanding. Lecture 5 of MIT 8.286, The Early Universe, starts the dynamics of that expansion exactly here: with Newton's static universe and why gravity rules it out. From there the course works out how gravity slows the expansion of an evenly filled universe, the Friedmann equations that describe it, and how the small denser regions grow into galaxies.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The electric force and gravity both fall as $1/r^2$, so the ratio of the two between two particles does not depend on their distance: about $1.2 \times 10^{36}$ for two protons and $2.3 \times 10^{39}$ for an electron and a proton.
- Large bodies are almost perfectly **neutral**: positive and negative charges cancel, and any imbalance is quickly undone by attracting opposite charge.
- Two bodies with one extra elementary charge per about $10^{18}$ nucleons would already repel each other as strongly as gravity attracts them; with $f$ extra charges per nucleon, $F_e/F_g = f^2\,ke^2/(Gm_p^2)$.
- **Gravity only attracts** and adds up over every particle, so it rules planets, stars and galaxies.
- Magnetic fields exist in planets, stars and galaxies, but they push only on moving charges: they shape thin charged gas, not the motion of whole stars.
- Newton hoped an infinite, evenly filled universe could stay at rest. It cannot: because gravity only attracts, a universe full of matter must expand or collapse. That is where the cosmology course 8.286 begins its study of the expanding universe.
