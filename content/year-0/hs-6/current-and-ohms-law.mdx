Press a switch and the room lights up at once, although the power station may be a hundred kilometres away. In the previous lessons charges sat still, or flew through the empty gap between two plates. This lesson is about charges that move through a material: an **electric current**. It asks what a current is, why a voltage is needed to keep one going, and why some things let charge through easily and others hardly at all.

## Electric current

In a metal wire, some electrons are not tied to any one atom: these **free electrons** wander through the whole metal (that is why a metal is a conductor, lesson 6.1). Connect the ends of the wire to a battery and the voltage sets up an electric field along the wire, which pushes on every free electron. Charge starts to flow along the wire. The **current** is how much charge flows past a point of the wire each second:

<Formula label="Electric current" tex="I = \frac{Q}{t}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 17.2, Flow of charge" />

Here $Q$ is the charge that passes the point in a time $t$. Current is measured in **amperes** (A), after the French physicist André-Marie Ampère:

<Formula label="The ampere" tex="1\ \text{A} = 1\ \text{C/s}" cite="mit" at="Sec. 17.2, Flow of charge" />

Currents in daily life span a huge range. A small indicator light uses a few thousandths of an ampere (milliamperes, mA); a lamp takes a fraction of an ampere; an electric kettle about ten amperes. The MIT notes mention currents as small as nano-amperes (billionths of an ampere) in your nerves, and far larger ones than a kettle's in a lightning stroke.

Since every electron carries a charge of size $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, the charge $Q$ that passes is a whole number $N$ of electron charges, $Q = Ne$, as in lesson 6.1 (here with a capital $N$, because $n$ is used below for something else). Even a small current is carried by an enormous number of electrons.

<WorkedExample title="Counting the electrons through a bulb">
<Problem>
A torch bulb carries a current of $0.15\ \text{A}$ for $4.0$ minutes. How much charge flows through it, and how many electrons is that?
</Problem>
<Step title="Charge">
$t = 4.0 \times 60 = 240\ \text{s}$, so $Q = It = 0.15 \times 240 = 36\ \text{C}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Electrons">
$N = Q/e = 36/(1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 2.3 \times 10^{20}$ electrons, more than a hundred billion billion.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Which way does a current flow?

Long before anyone knew about electrons, Benjamin Franklin guessed that something flowed from the object he called "positive" to the one he called "negative". So a current was drawn as flowing out of the **positive** terminal of a battery, round the circuit, and back into the **negative** terminal. This is still the rule. It is called the **conventional current**: the direction in which positive charge would flow.

We now know that in a metal the charges that actually move are electrons, which are negative. They drift the other way, from the negative terminal round to the positive one. For the current it makes no difference: negative charge moving one way carries the same current as positive charge moving the other way. In salt water, or in the gas of a neon lamp, positive and negative charges both move, in opposite directions, and both add to the current.

<Callout kind="note">
In circuit diagrams and in every formula of this block, arrows show the conventional current, from $+$ to $-$ outside the battery. Electrons move against those arrows.
</Callout>

## Electrons drift slowly

How fast do the electrons move along a wire? Picture a wire with cross-sectional area $A$ that contains $n$ free electrons in every cubic metre. If they all drift along the wire at an average speed $v_d$, then in a time $t$ every electron within a length $v_d t$ of a point passes it: that is $n A v_d t$ electrons, with charge $n e A v_d t$. Dividing by $t$ gives the current:

<Formula label="Current and drift speed" tex="I = n e v_d A" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (6.1.4), with charge e for each carrier" />

The average speed $v_d$ is called the **drift speed**. Each electron actually zigzags about at high speed, bouncing off the atoms of the metal; the field only adds a slow, steady drift along the wire on top of that random motion. In copper there are about $n = 8.5 \times 10^{28}$ free electrons per cubic metre (one or so for every atom), so a large current needs only a small drift speed.

<WorkedExample title="How fast do the electrons drift?">
<Problem>
A copper wire with a cross-sectional area of $1.5\ \text{mm}^2$ carries a current of $3.0\ \text{A}$. Find the drift speed of its electrons, and how long an electron takes to drift one metre.
</Problem>
<Step title="Area in square metres">
$1\ \text{mm}^2 = (10^{-3}\ \text{m})^2 = 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2$, so $A = 1.5 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Drift speed">
$v_d = I/(neA) = 3.0/(8.5 \times 10^{28} \times 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.5 \times 10^{-6}) = 1.5 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m/s}$, about a seventh of a millimetre per second.
</Step>
<Step title="Time for one metre">
$t = 1.0/(1.47 \times 10^{-4}) = 6.8 \times 10^{3}\ \text{s}$, almost two hours.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

So why does a lamp light up the moment you press the switch? Because the wire is already full of free electrons. Think of a garden hose that is already full of water: open the tap and water comes out of the far end at once, although the water that went in at the tap needs much longer to get there. When the switch closes, the push travels along the wire very fast, at a large part of the speed of light, and the electrons everywhere in the circuit, including those inside the lamp, start drifting at almost the same moment.

## Resistance

Connect the same battery to a short thick copper wire and to the thin wire of a lamp, and very different currents flow. The **resistance** of a component tells how hard it is to drive a current through it: the voltage needed per ampere of current.

<Formula label="Resistance" tex="R = \frac{V}{I}" cite="reading2" at="Sec. 11.2, Ohm's Law" />

Here $V$ is the voltage across the component and $I$ the current through it. Resistance is measured in **ohms** ($\text{Ω}$), after the German physicist Georg Simon Ohm:

<Formula label="The ohm" tex="1\ \text{Ω} = 1\ \text{V/A}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 17.3, Resistance" />

Where does resistance come from? On their way along the wire, the drifting electrons keep colliding with the atoms of the metal, which vibrate about their places. In each collision an electron hands over some of the kinetic energy that the field gave it, and the atoms vibrate harder: the wire gets warmer. This is why a phone charger feels warm, and why the very thin wire (the **filament**) of an old-fashioned light bulb gets so hot that it glows white. The thick wires leading to the bulb carry the same current but hardly warm up, because their resistance is much smaller. (How much energy a resistance turns into heat each second is the subject of lesson 6.8.)

A **resistor** is a component made to have a chosen resistance. Resistors set the currents in every electronic device, from a phone to a television.

## Ohm's law

Ohm found that, for a metal wire, the current is proportional to the voltage across it: double the voltage and the current doubles. For a metal kept at the same temperature, then, the resistance $R = V/I$ is the same whatever the voltage. This is **Ohm's law**, usually written

<Formula label="Ohm's law" tex="V = I R" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (6.2.7)" />

and also used as $I = V/R$ or $R = V/I$. Components that obey it, such as metal wires and ordinary resistors, are called **ohmic**. Read $I = V/R$ as a rule of thumb: a larger voltage drives a larger current, and a larger resistance lets less through.

<WorkedExample title="Using Ohm's law">
<Problem>
(a) A $330\ \text{Ω}$ resistor is connected to a $9.0\ \text{V}$ battery. What current flows through it? (b) In another experiment, a component carries $0.12\ \text{A}$ when the voltage across it is $3.0\ \text{V}$. What is its resistance?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Current">
$I = V/R = 9.0/330 = 0.027\ \text{A}$, or 27 milliamperes.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Resistance">
$R = V/I = 3.0/0.12 = 25\ \text{Ω}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Ohmic and non-ohmic conductors

The best test of Ohm's law is a graph. Set the voltage across a component to a series of values, measure the current each time, and plot the current $I$ against the voltage $V$: an **$I$–$V$ graph** (also called a characteristic). For an ohmic conductor the points lie on a straight line through the origin. Its slope is $I/V = 1/R$: the steeper the line, the smaller the resistance.

Many components do not obey Ohm's law. A light bulb is the best-known example. As the voltage rises, the current heats the filament from room temperature to well over a thousand degrees, and a hot metal resists more than a cold one. So each extra volt adds less current than the one before, and the graph bends over. A **diode**, used in every electronic device, is even more extreme: it lets almost no current through in one direction and conducts easily in the other. Such components are called **non-ohmic**. For them $R = V/I$ still gives the resistance at one moment, but the value depends on the voltage.

<WorkedExample title="The resistance of a lamp">
<Problem>
A small lamp carries $0.10\ \text{A}$ at $1.0\ \text{V}$, when it barely glows, and $0.30\ \text{A}$ at $6.0\ \text{V}$, when it is fully lit. Find its resistance in each case.
</Problem>
<Step title="Barely glowing">
$R = V/I = 1.0/0.10 = 10\ \text{Ω}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Fully lit">
$R = 6.0/0.30 = 20\ \text{Ω}$. Six times the voltage gives only three times the current: the hot filament has twice the resistance of the warm one, so the lamp is non-ohmic.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

Two more non-ohmic components are made on purpose to change their resistance, so that they can work as sensors. An **NTC thermistor** (NTC stands for "negative temperature coefficient") is a small piece of a ceramic material whose resistance falls as it gets warmer: the opposite of a metal. It is used to measure temperature. A **light-dependent resistor** (LDR) has a resistance that falls as more light shines on it, so it is used in light sensors. In a circuit, a falling resistance means a larger current at the same voltage, and that change in current or voltage is what the sensor reports.

The slope of an $I$–$V$ graph, $I/V = 1/R$, has a name of its own: the **conductance** $G$ of the component, which says how easily it lets a current through.

<Formula label="Conductance" tex="G = \frac{1}{R} = \frac{I}{V}" cite="mit6071kirchhoff" at="p. 5, Eq. (1.6)" />

Conductance is measured in **siemens** (S), with $1\ \text{S} = 1\ \text{A/V}$, the inverse of an ohm. The $25\ \text{Ω}$ component in the worked example on Ohm's law has $G = 1/25 = 0.040\ \text{S}$.

## Resistivity

What decides the resistance of a wire? Three things:

- **Length.** A wire twice as long is like two equal wires one after the other: twice the resistance.
- **Thickness.** A wire with twice the cross-sectional area is like two equal wires side by side, offering two paths: half the resistance.
- **Material.** Some materials let electrons through far more easily than others.

Together these give

<Formula label="Resistance of a wire" tex="R = \frac{\rho L}{A}" cite="reading3" at="Eq. (6.2.10), with the length written L instead of l" />

where $L$ is the length of the wire, $A$ its cross-sectional area and $\rho$ (the Greek letter rho) the **resistivity** of the material. The resistivity is the resistance between two opposite faces of a cube of the material one metre on each side, and its unit is the ohm metre ($\text{Ω}\,\text{m}$). Values at room temperature, from the MIT notes:

- silver $1.6 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{Ω}\,\text{m}$, copper $1.7 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{Ω}\,\text{m}$ and aluminium $2.8 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{Ω}\,\text{m}$: the best conductors, used for wires and cables;
- nichrome, an alloy used in toasters and heaters, $1.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{Ω}\,\text{m}$, about 60 times copper;
- glass, between $10^{10}$ and $10^{14}\ \text{Ω}\,\text{m}$: an insulator.

The range is enormous: a glass rod resists at least $10^{17}$ times as much as a copper rod of the same shape.

<WorkedExample title="A household cable and a heating wire">
<Problem>
(a) A copper wire in a household cable is $20\ \text{m}$ long with a cross-sectional area of $1.5\ \text{mm}^2$. What is its resistance? (b) What length of nichrome wire $0.40\ \text{mm}$ thick (in diameter) has a resistance of $10\ \text{Ω}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The copper wire">
$R = \rho L/A = 1.7 \times 10^{-8} \times 20/(1.5 \times 10^{-6}) = 0.23\ \text{Ω}$: small, so the wire wastes little energy.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Cross-section of the nichrome wire">
The radius is $0.20\ \text{mm}$, so $A = \pi r^2 = \pi \times (0.20 \times 10^{-3})^2 = 1.26 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Length">
From $R = \rho L/A$: $L = RA/\rho = 10 \times 1.26 \times 10^{-7}/(1.0 \times 10^{-6}) = 1.3\ \text{m}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The resistivity of a metal grows as it gets hotter, because the atoms vibrate harder and get in the electrons' way more often. That is why the lamp above is non-ohmic. Some materials, cooled far below room temperature, lose their resistance completely: they become **superconductors**. At ordinary pressure, even the best ones known today only do this far below the freezing point of water: the Siyavula text on resistance (reading 1) names a copper oxide that becomes a superconductor at about $-140\ \text{°C}$ as the record.

## Measuring current and voltage

Two meters measure what Ohm's law connects.

- An **ammeter** measures the current through a component. The current has to flow through the meter, so it is connected **in series**: you break the circuit and put the ammeter in the gap. A good ammeter has a very small resistance, so that it hardly changes the current it measures.
- A **voltmeter** measures the voltage across a component: the difference in potential between its two ends. It is connected **in parallel**: its two leads touch the two ends of the component, and the circuit is not broken. A good voltmeter has a very large resistance, so that hardly any current is drawn off through it.

To measure the resistance of a component, then, put it in a circuit with a battery and an ammeter in series, connect a voltmeter across the component, and divide the voltmeter reading by the ammeter reading. Repeating this for several voltages gives the points of an $I$–$V$ graph.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Never put an ammeter across a battery">
Because an ammeter has almost no resistance, connecting it in parallel, straight across a battery or a power supply, lets a very large current flow. That can blow the meter's fuse or damage it. If a meter has several ranges, start on the largest one.
</Callout>

## Summary

- An electric current is the charge flowing past a point per second, $I = Q/t$, in amperes: $1\ \text{A} = 1\ \text{C/s}$. The conventional current flows from $+$ to $-$ outside the battery; in a metal the electrons drift the other way.
- The free electrons in a wire drift slowly, $I = nev_dA$, typically a fraction of a millimetre per second; a lamp lights at once because the whole circuit is already full of electrons that start moving together.
- Resistance is the voltage needed per ampere, $R = V/I$, in ohms ($1\ \text{Ω} = 1\ \text{V/A}$). Collisions of the electrons with the atoms cause it and warm the conductor.
- Ohm's law: for an ohmic conductor at constant temperature $R$ is constant, so $V = IR$ and the $I$–$V$ graph is a straight line through the origin. A lamp filament and a diode are non-ohmic, and so are an NTC thermistor and an LDR, whose resistance falls as they get warmer or are lit. The conductance $G = 1/R$, in siemens, is the slope of the $I$–$V$ graph.
- The resistance of a wire is $R = \rho L/A$: it grows with length, falls with thickness and depends on the material through the resistivity $\rho$.
- An ammeter (very small resistance) is connected in series; a voltmeter (very large resistance) in parallel, across the component.
