Rub a balloon on your hair and hold it near a few small pieces of paper: they jump towards it before the balloon even touches them. Lesson 6.2 gave the size of such a force, Coulomb's law. But how does one charge "know" that another is there, across empty space? This lesson describes the answer physicists use: a charge changes the space around it, and that change, the **electric field**, pushes or pulls any other charge you put there.

## A field: force per unit charge

Put a charge $Q$ somewhere and bring a second, small charge $q$ to a point near it. The small charge feels a force. Move it to another point and the force changes in size and direction. So at every point around $Q$ there is a force waiting for any charge that arrives: the region around $Q$ has an **electric field**.

To measure the field without disturbing it, use a **test charge**: a small positive charge, small enough that it does not push the other charges out of place. The force on it depends on how big the test charge is (twice the charge, twice the force), so divide that out. The **electric field strength** $E$ at a point is the force per unit of test charge:

<Formula label="Electric field strength" tex="E = \frac{F}{q}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 9.3, electric field strength" />

Its unit is the newton per coulomb, $\text{N/C}$. The field is a vector: its direction is the direction of the force on a **positive** test charge. Once you know the field at a point, turn the formula round to find the force on any charge $q$ placed there:

<Formula label="Force on a charge in a field" tex="F = qE" cite="mit802ch1" at="Sec. 1.6, after Eq. (1.6.2)" />

A positive charge is pushed along the field; a negative charge, such as an electron, is pushed the opposite way.

<WorkedExample title="Measuring a field, then using it">
<Problem>
A test charge of $+4.0\ \text{nC}$ placed at a point P feels a force of $6.0 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{N}$ pointing east. (a) What is the electric field at P? (b) The test charge is taken away and an electron (charge $-1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$) is put at P. What force does it feel?
</Problem>
<Step title="The field">
$E = F/q = (6.0 \times 10^{-4})/(4.0 \times 10^{-9}) = 1.5 \times 10^5\ \text{N/C}$, pointing east, the same way as the force on the positive test charge.
</Step>
<Step title="The force on the electron">
The field is still there after the test charge is gone. The size of the force is $F = qE = 1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.5 \times 10^5 = 2.4 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{N}$. The electron is negative, so the force points west.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The field of a point charge

For a small charged object, a **point charge** $Q$, Coulomb's law gives the force on a test charge $q$ at distance $r$: $F = kQq/r^2$, with $k = 9.0 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$. Divide by $q$ and the test charge drops out:

<Formula label="Field of a point charge" tex="E = \frac{kQ}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="Sec. 9.3, electric field strength (from Coulomb's law, Sec. 9.2)" />

The field depends only on the charge $Q$ that makes it and on the distance $r$, not on whatever charge you use to probe it. It points straight away from a positive charge and straight towards a negative one. Like the intensity of sound from a point source in lesson 5.5, it falls off with the square of the distance: twice as far, a quarter of the field; ten times as far, a hundredth.

<WorkedExample title="Near a charged sphere">
<Problem>
A small metal sphere carries a charge of $+2.0 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{C}$ (2.0 microcoulombs). (a) Find the electric field $15\ \text{cm}$ from its centre. (b) A small bead with a charge of $-1.5\ \text{nC}$ is placed there. Find the force on the bead.
</Problem>
<Step title="The field">
$E = kQ/r^2 = (9.0 \times 10^9 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-6})/0.15^2 = 8.0 \times 10^5\ \text{N/C}$, pointing away from the sphere, because the sphere is positive.
</Step>
<Step title="The force on the bead">
$F = qE = 1.5 \times 10^{-9} \times 8.0 \times 10^5 = 1.2 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{N}$. The bead is negative, so the force points towards the sphere: the bead is attracted, as Coulomb's law says for unlike charges.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Field lines

A field has a size and a direction at every point, which is hard to draw. **Field lines** are a way to picture it:

- At each point the field points along the line (along its tangent where the line curves); an arrow on the line shows which way.
- Field lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges, or go off to very far away (or come in from very far away) when there is no charge of the other sign to end on. Around a single positive charge they point straight outwards like the spokes of a wheel; around a single negative charge, straight inwards.
- Where the lines are close together the field is strong; where they spread out it is weak.
- Field lines leave or arrive at a charge, or a charged surface such as a metal plate, at right angles to it.
- Field lines never cross: at a crossing the field would have two directions at once.
- The number of lines drawn leaving (or arriving at) a charge is proportional to its charge: draw twice as many lines for twice the charge.

Field lines are a drawing, not something real; the field is there between them too. The density rule also explains the inverse square. The lines leaving a point charge cross every sphere around it, and a sphere of radius $r$ has area $4\pi r^2$. At twice the distance the same lines are spread over four times the area, so they are four times less dense: a quarter of the field.

## Two charges

When there are two charges, the field at any point is the sum of the two separate fields, added as vectors (lesson 0.8): $\vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2$.

- **Opposite charges of equal size** (a **dipole**): the lines leave the positive charge and curve round to end on the negative one. Between the charges both fields point the same way, from $+$ to $-$, so the field there is strong. Far away the two charges nearly cancel and the field is weak.
- **Two equal positive charges**: no line goes from one charge to the other; the lines bend away from each other. Exactly halfway between them the two fields are equal and opposite and cancel: the field is zero there. Two equal negative charges give the same pattern with all the arrows reversed.
- **Charges of different size**: more lines leave the bigger charge, and close to each charge its own field wins. Very far away the pair looks like a single charge equal to their sum.

<WorkedExample title="Halfway between two charges">
<Problem>
A charge of $+12\ \text{nC}$ and a charge of $-2.0\ \text{nC}$ are $0.60\ \text{m}$ apart. Find the electric field exactly halfway between them.
</Problem>
<Step title="Each field on its own">
Both are $0.30\ \text{m}$ from the midpoint. From the positive charge: $E_1 = (9.0 \times 10^9 \times 12 \times 10^{-9})/0.30^2 = 1200\ \text{N/C}$, pointing away from it, towards the negative charge. From the negative charge: $E_2 = (9.0 \times 10^9 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-9})/0.30^2 = 200\ \text{N/C}$, pointing towards the negative charge.
</Step>
<Step title="Add them">
Both point the same way, so the sizes add: $E = 1200 + 200 = 1.4 \times 10^3\ \text{N/C}$, towards the negative charge. (At a point outside the pair the two fields would point in opposite directions and you would subtract.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A uniform field between two plates

Take two large, flat metal plates parallel to each other, a small distance apart, and give one a positive and the other an equal negative charge. Between the plates the field lines run straight from the positive plate to the negative plate, at right angles to the plates, parallel and evenly spaced. Evenly spaced lines mean the same strength everywhere, and parallel lines the same direction: the field is **uniform**. Only near the edges of the plates do the lines bulge outwards and the field weakens. How strong the field is depends on how much charge the plates carry; lesson 6.4 shows how to find it from the voltage across the plates.

A uniform field is useful because the force on a charge $q$ is the same wherever it is between the plates. By Newton's second law the charge then has a constant acceleration:

<Formula label="Acceleration of a charge in a field" tex="a = \frac{qE}{m}" cite="mit802" at="Eq. (2.6.2)" />

The motion is constant acceleration, as in Block 1. A charge released from rest that crosses a gap $d$ reaches the speed given by $v^2 = 2ad$:

<Formula label="Speed after crossing the gap from rest" tex="v = \sqrt{\frac{2qEd}{m}}" cite="mit802" at="Eq. (2.6.3)" />

<WorkedExample title="A proton between two plates">
<Problem>
A proton (charge $1.6 \times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, mass $1.67 \times 10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$) is released from rest next to the positive plate. The uniform field between the plates is $2.5 \times 10^4\ \text{N/C}$ and the plates are $4.0\ \text{cm}$ apart. Find its acceleration, the speed with which it hits the negative plate, and how long the trip takes.
</Problem>
<Step title="Acceleration">
$a = qE/m = (1.6 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.5 \times 10^4)/(1.67 \times 10^{-27}) = 2.4 \times 10^{12}\ \text{m/s}^2$, towards the negative plate. That is more than $10^{11}$ times the acceleration of free fall.
</Step>
<Step title="Final speed">
$v = \sqrt{2ad} = \sqrt{2 \times 2.4 \times 10^{12} \times 0.040} = 4.4 \times 10^5\ \text{m/s}$, about 440 kilometres per second.
</Step>
<Step title="Time">
$t = v/a = (4.4 \times 10^5)/(2.4 \times 10^{12}) = 1.8 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{s}$: less than a millionth of a second.
</Step>
<Step title="Can we ignore gravity?">
The weight of the proton is $mg = 1.67 \times 10^{-27} \times 9.81 = 1.6 \times 10^{-26}\ \text{N}$, while the electric force is $qE = 4.0 \times 10^{-15}\ \text{N}$, more than $10^{11}$ times larger. For particles like protons and electrons, gravity hardly ever matters.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

A charge that enters the field sideways, moving parallel to the plates, keeps its sideways speed and is pushed towards one plate at the same time: it follows a parabola, just like a ball thrown horizontally in lesson 1.8. This is how the tubes of old oscilloscopes steered their beams of electrons. (Old television tubes did the same job with magnetic fields instead, which lesson 6.9 describes.)

## Comparing with the gravitational field

The idea of a field was not new: lesson 2.11 already described gravity this way, even if it did not use the word. The Earth fills the space around it with a **gravitational field** $g$, the gravitational force per unit mass:

<Formula label="Gravitational field strength" tex="g = \frac{F}{m}" cite="mit802ch1" at="Eq. (1.5.2), field as force per unit mass" />

Its unit is $\text{N/kg}$, which is the same as $\text{m/s}^2$. Near the Earth's surface $g = 9.81\ \text{N/kg}$, the familiar $9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$; a mass $m$ there feels its weight $F = mg$, just as a charge feels $F = qE$. Further out, use Newton's law of gravitation: a mass $m$ at distance $r$ from a mass $M$ feels the force $F = GMm/r^2$, where $G$ is the gravitational constant. Divide by $m$, as we divided by $q$ for the electric field, and the field of the mass $M$ is $g = GM/r^2$: an inverse square, exactly like $E = kQ/r^2$.

| | Gravitational field | Electric field |
|---|---|---|
| Made by, and acts on | mass | charge |
| Definition | $g = F/m$, in $\text{N/kg}$ | $E = F/q$, in $\text{N/C}$ |
| Of a point source | $g = GM/r^2$ | $E = kQ/r^2$ |
| Direction | always towards the mass | away from $+$, towards $-$ |
| Acceleration of an object in it | $g$, the same for every object | $qE/m$, depends on charge and mass |

There are two big differences. Mass comes in one kind only, so gravity always attracts and its field lines always point inwards; charge comes in two kinds, so electric forces can push or pull. And in a gravitational field everything falls with the same acceleration, whatever its mass, while in an electric field the acceleration $qE/m$ depends on how much charge the object carries for its mass. An electron and a proton in the same field feel forces of the same size, but the electron, about 1800 times lighter, accelerates about 1800 times faster, and in the opposite direction.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Two inverse-square fields">
On the scale of atoms the electric force between two particles is enormously stronger than gravity, as lesson 6.2 showed. Yet the motion of planets, stars and galaxies is ruled by gravity. The reason lies in the table above: large objects carry almost exactly equal amounts of positive and negative charge, so their electric fields cancel, while gravitational fields never cancel. The last lesson of this block returns to this.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A charge creates an **electric field** around it: any other charge placed there feels a force. The field strength is the force per unit positive test charge, $E = F/q$, in $\text{N/C}$; the force on a charge $q$ is $F = qE$, along the field for a positive charge and against it for a negative one.
- The field of a point charge is $E = kQ/r^2$: away from a positive charge, towards a negative one, and four times weaker at twice the distance.
- **Field lines** run from $+$ to $-$, never cross, and are closest where the field is strongest. Fields of several charges add as vectors; halfway between two equal like charges the field is zero.
- Between two oppositely charged parallel plates the field is **uniform**. A charge there has a constant acceleration $a = qE/m$, and from rest it crosses a gap $d$ with speed $v = \sqrt{2qEd/m}$.
- The gravitational field $g = F/m$ (in $\text{N/kg}$) is the gravity version: $g = GM/r^2$ is also an inverse square, but it always attracts, and every object falls with the same acceleration.
