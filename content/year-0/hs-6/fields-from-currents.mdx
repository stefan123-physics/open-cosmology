In lesson 6.9 a magnet's field pushed on a current. This lesson turns that round: a current makes a magnetic field of its own. Every wire that carries a current is surrounded by a magnetic field, and by winding wire into coils you can make magnets that switch on and off at the press of a button. That is how electric bells, door locks, loudspeakers and the cranes that lift scrap cars work.

## Oersted's discovery

For a long time electricity and magnetism seemed to be two separate subjects. In 1820 the Danish physicist Hans Christian Oersted showed in a lecture that they are linked. He ran a current through a wire held over a compass, and the compass needle turned. When the current stopped, the needle swung back; when the current was reversed, it turned the other way.

A compass needle lines up with the magnetic field, so the experiment shows that:

- a current makes a magnetic field around it;
- with no current there is no field;
- reversing the current reverses the field.

You can repeat it with a battery, a piece of wire and a compass. Only close the circuit for a few seconds at a time: a bare wire has very little resistance, so the current is large and the battery soon runs flat and the wire gets hot.

## The field around a straight wire

Sprinkle iron filings on a card with a vertical wire through it, switch on a large current, and the filings form **circles** centred on the wire. The field lines of a long straight wire are closed circles in planes at right angles to the wire. They have no beginning and no end, unlike the field lines of electric charges, which start and stop on charges.

To draw such fields on flat paper, a current or field coming **out of** the page is shown as a dot, ⊙ (the point of an arrow coming towards you), and one going **into** the page as a cross, ⊗ (the feathers at the tail of an arrow flying away from you).

The direction of the circles follows from the **right-hand grip rule**: grip the wire with your right hand, with your thumb pointing along the current. Your fingers then curl the way the field lines go. As in the rest of this block, "current" means conventional current, from + to −. The rule only works with the right hand; the left hand always gives the wrong direction.

So a current coming out of the page has field lines going anticlockwise around it, and a current going into the page has them going clockwise.

### How strong is the field?

The field is strongest close to the wire and weaker further away. For a long straight wire carrying a current $I$, the field strength at a distance $r$ from the wire is

<Formula label="Field of a long straight wire" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.1.7), p. 5" />

Here $\mu_0$ (mu nought) is a constant of nature, the **permeability of free space**, $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$. It plays the same part for magnetic fields that $k$ played for electric ones. Note that $B$ falls as $1/r$, not $1/r^2$: twice as far from the wire, the field is half as strong. The field of a point charge falls faster because it spreads out in all directions; the field of a long wire only spreads out sideways.

For comparison, the Earth's magnetic field at its surface is about $5 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{T}$ ($50\ \mu\text{T}$).

<WorkedExample title="A compass next to a cable">
<Problem>
A long straight cable carries a steady current of $12\ \text{A}$. (a) What is the magnetic field $3.0\ \text{cm}$ from the cable? (b) How far from the cable is its field as strong as the Earth's, $5 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{T}$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Use the formula for a straight wire">
$B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} = \dfrac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 12}{2\pi \times 0.030} = 8.0 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{T}$, or $80\ \mu\text{T}$: more than the Earth's field, so a compass this close turns noticeably.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Solve for the distance">
$r = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi B} = \dfrac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 12}{2\pi \times 5 \times 10^{-5}} = 0.048\ \text{m}$, about $5\ \text{cm}$. Further away, the cable's field is weaker than the Earth's.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The field of a loop

Bend the wire into a circular loop. Apply the grip rule to each piece of the loop and you find that, inside the loop, the fields of all the pieces point the same way: they add up. The field lines pass through the loop, spread out around the outside and come back in at the other face.

A loop therefore acts as a flat magnet with a north face and a south face. There is a second form of the right-hand rule for it: curl the fingers of your right hand round the loop in the direction of the current, and your thumb points out of the **north** face, the side where the field lines come out.

At the centre of a circular loop of radius $R$ the field is

<Formula label="Field at the centre of a circular loop" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2R}" cite="reading1" at="p. 9, B₀ below Eq. (9.1.15)" />

A flat coil of $N$ turns wound close together acts like $N$ loops on top of each other, so the field at its centre is $N$ times as large.

## The solenoid

Now wind the wire into a long coil, with many turns side by side along a tube. Such a coil is a **solenoid**. The fields of all the loops add up. Inside a long solenoid the field lines run parallel to the axis and are evenly spaced: the field is **uniform**, the same at every point inside, away from the ends. Outside, the field is weak and has the same shape as the field of a bar magnet, with a north pole at one end and a south pole at the other. The grip rule for a loop tells you which end is north.

The field inside a long solenoid is

<Formula label="Field inside a long solenoid" tex="B = \mu_0 n I, \qquad n = \frac{N}{L}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.4.3), p. 20" />

where $N$ is the number of turns, $L$ the length of the solenoid and $n$ the number of turns **per metre**. Neither the width of the coil nor the position inside it appears: only how tightly the turns are packed and how much current flows.

<WorkedExample title="A coil for a physics lab">
<Problem>
A solenoid is $20\ \text{cm}$ long and has $500$ turns. It carries a current of $2.0\ \text{A}$. Find the field inside it and compare it with the Earth's field.
</Problem>
<Step title="Turns per metre">
$n = N/L = 500/0.20 = 2500$ turns per metre.
</Step>
<Step title="The field">
$B = \mu_0 n I = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 2500 \times 2.0 = 6.3 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{T}$, or $6.3\ \text{mT}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
$6.3 \times 10^{-3} / (5 \times 10^{-5}) \approx 130$: the field inside the coil is about 130 times the Earth's field.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Electromagnets and iron cores

A solenoid with a current is a magnet that you can switch on and off, make stronger by turning up the current or adding turns, and turn round by reversing the current. Put a bar of **iron** inside it and the field becomes much stronger. That combination is an **electromagnet**.

Iron is **ferromagnetic**: it is made of tiny regions, called domains, in which the atoms' own magnetic fields already point the same way, but different domains point in different directions, so a plain piece of iron is not a magnet. The coil's field lines the domains up, and then their fields add to the coil's. Inside the iron the field can become $10^3$ to $10^4$ times as strong as the coil's field alone. The iron used in an electromagnet is chosen so that its domains fall back out of line when the current stops; in a permanent magnet they stay lined up.

You can test this at home with a battery, insulated wire and an iron nail: wind the wire round the nail, connect the battery, and the nail picks up paper clips. More turns pick up more clips, and the clips drop when you disconnect.

Electromagnets are used wherever a force has to be switched by electricity: scrapyard cranes that lift and drop cars, electric bells, relays (switches worked by a small current), loudspeakers and magnetic locks on doors. Many electric motors use them too.

## Force between two parallel currents

A current makes a magnetic field, and a current in a magnetic field feels a force. So two currents push or pull on each other. Take two long straight wires, a distance $d$ apart, with currents $I_1$ and $I_2$. Wire 1 makes a field $B = \mu_0 I_1/(2\pi d)$ at wire 2, at right angles to wire 2. A length $L$ of wire 2 then feels the force $F = BI_2L$ of lesson 6.9, so the force per metre of wire is

<Formula label="Force per metre between two parallel currents" tex="\frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d}" cite="reading1" at="Eq. (9.2.1), p. 13" />

Wire 1 feels the same force from wire 2, in the opposite direction, as Newton's third law requires. The grip rule and the direction rule of lesson 6.9 give the result:

- currents in the **same** direction **attract**;
- currents in **opposite** directions **repel**.

<WorkedExample title="The two wires of a power cord">
<Problem>
A power cord feeds a heater. Its two wires are $4.0\ \text{mm}$ apart and carry $10\ \text{A}$ each, in opposite directions (to the heater and back). What is the force between $2.0\ \text{m}$ of the two wires, and is it a push or a pull?
</Problem>
<Step title="Force per metre">
$\dfrac{F}{L} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d} = \dfrac{4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 10 \times 10}{2\pi \times 0.0040} = 5.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{N/m}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Force on the length of cord">
$F = 5.0 \times 10^{-3} \times 2.0 = 1.0 \times 10^{-2}\ \text{N}$. The currents are opposite, so the wires repel, but only with about the weight of a one-gram mass: the insulation holds them easily.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

### The old definition of the ampere

This force was used to define the unit of current. From 1948 until 2019 the ampere was defined as the steady current that, flowing in two long straight parallel wires $1\ \text{m}$ apart in a vacuum, makes them push or pull on each other with a force of $2 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{N}$ per metre of length. Check it with the formula: $F/L = 4\pi \times 10^{-7} \times 1 \times 1/(2\pi \times 1) = 2 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{N/m}$. That definition is the reason why $\mu_0$ has the tidy value $4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$. Infinitely long wires cannot be built, so since 2019 the ampere has been based instead on the charge of one electron, $e$.

## Summary

- A current makes a magnetic field (Oersted, 1820). Around a long straight wire the field lines are circles; the **right-hand grip rule** (thumb along the current, fingers curl with the field) gives their direction.
- The field of a long straight wire is $B = \mu_0 I/(2\pi r)$, with $\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$: it falls as $1/r$.
- A loop of wire acts as a flat magnet, with $B = \mu_0 I/(2R)$ at its centre. A long coil, a **solenoid**, has a uniform field $B = \mu_0 n I$ inside, with $n$ turns per metre, and a bar-magnet field outside.
- An iron core lines up its domains with the coil's field and makes an **electromagnet** that is much stronger and can be switched on and off.
- Two parallel currents attract (same direction) or repel (opposite directions) with a force per metre $F/L = \mu_0 I_1 I_2/(2\pi d)$. From 1948 to 2019 this force defined the ampere.
