The Bose gas of Chapter VII has its counterpart in the **Fermi gas**: identical particles of half-integer spin, no two of which may share a single-particle state. This page follows the first half of Wen's handwritten Chapter VIII (notes pp. 76–81): the grand partition function of free fermions, the Fermi–Dirac distribution, an exact equation of state, and the two limits, a hot gas that behaves classically and a cold gas held up by the Pauli principle alone. The [next page](/courses/8-08/degenerate-fermi-gas) turns to the cold gas in detail. The grand canonical ensemble comes from [8.044](/courses/8-044/grand-canonical-ensemble), the Fermi sphere from [the free Fermi gas of 8.044](/courses/8-044/free-fermi-gas), and the thermal wavelength $\lambda$ from [Chapter I](/courses/8-08/information-equipartition-ideal-gas). (Wen writes the grand partition function as $Q_G$ and calls $\Omega$ the "thermopotential"; it is the grand potential. The formulas here, like his, count one spin state per wave vector unless we say otherwise.)

## Free fermions and their occupation numbers

Put the particles in a cubic box of side $L$ with periodic boundary conditions. A single particle then has plane-wave states $|\vec k\rangle$ with

<Formula label="Single-particle states" tex="\vec k = \frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x, n_y, n_z), \qquad \epsilon_k = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 76, point ①" />

where $n_x$, $n_y$, $n_z$ are whole numbers (notes p. 76). A state of many identical particles is fixed completely by saying how many particles occupy each single-particle state: the set of **occupation numbers** $\{n_k\}$. For fermions the Pauli principle allows only $n_k = 0$ or $1$. The particles do not interact, so the energy of the many-particle state is just the sum of the occupied energies:

<Formula label="Energy of a many-fermion state" tex="E(\{n_k\}) = \sum_k \epsilon_k n_k, \qquad N = \sum_k n_k" cite="reading1" at="p. 76, point ①" />

## The grand partition function

At fixed $N$ the occupation numbers are tied together by $\sum_k n_k = N$, and the sum over states is awkward. In the grand canonical ensemble $N$ is free: each set $\{n_k\}$ is weighted by $e^{-\beta(E - \mu N)}$ with $\beta = 1/k_BT$, and since the exponent is itself a sum over states, $-\beta\sum_k(\epsilon_k - \mu)n_k$, the sum over all sets splits into a product of independent sums, one for each state (notes p. 76):

<Formula label="Grand partition function of free fermions" tex="Q_G = \sum_{\{n_k\}} e^{-\beta\sum_k(\epsilon_k - \mu)n_k} = \prod_k\sum_{n_k = 0, 1} e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)n_k} = \prod_k\left[1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)}\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 76" />

The logarithm turns the product into a sum, and the **grand potential** is

<Formula label="Grand potential" tex="\Omega = -k_BT\ln Q_G = -k_BT\sum_k\ln\left[1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)}\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 76" />

Everything else follows by differentiating $\Omega(T, V, \mu)$.

## The Fermi–Dirac distribution

The mean number of particles is minus the derivative with respect to $\mu$ (notes p. 77):

<Formula label="Number of particles" tex="N = -\left.\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\mu}\right|_{V,T} = \sum_k\frac{e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)}}{1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)}} = \sum_k\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)} + 1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 77" />

Each term is the mean occupation of one state, the **Fermi–Dirac distribution**:

<Formula label="Fermi–Dirac distribution" tex="n_k = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)} + 1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 77" />

It always lies between 0 and 1, as the Pauli principle demands; it is exactly $\frac12$ at $\epsilon_k = \mu$, close to 1 for states well below $\mu$ and close to 0 well above. For a large box the sum over states becomes an integral. The allowed $\vec k$ form a grid with one point in each cube of volume $(2\pi/L)^3$, so a region $d^3k$ holds $V\,d^3k/(2\pi)^3$ states (notes p. 77), and the density $n = N/V$ is

<Formula label="Density of the Fermi gas" tex="n = \frac NV = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)} + 1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 77" />

Given $n$ and $T$, this equation fixes the chemical potential $\mu(n, T)$.

## An exact equation of state

The pressure is $P = -\partial\Omega/\partial V$ at fixed $\mu$ and $T$ (notes p. 77). The volume enters only through the energies: with $\vec k = (2\pi/L)(n_x, n_y, n_z)$ and $L = V^{1/3}$,

<Formula label="How the energies depend on the volume" tex="\epsilon_k = \frac{\hbar^2(2\pi)^2(n_x^2 + n_y^2 + n_z^2)}{2m\,V^{2/3}}, \qquad \frac{\partial\epsilon_k}{\partial V} = -\frac23\,\frac{\epsilon_k}{V}" cite="reading1" at="p. 77" />

so differentiating each logarithm in $\Omega$ gives

<Formula label="Pressure of the ideal Fermi gas" tex="P = -\sum_k\frac{\partial\epsilon_k}{\partial V}\,n_k = \frac23\,\frac1V\sum_k\epsilon_kn_k = \frac23\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\,\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)} + 1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 77" />

The middle expression is two thirds of the energy per volume. With the internal energy $U = V\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\epsilon_kn_k$ (notes p. 77):

<Formula label="Pressure and energy" tex="PV = \frac23\,U" cite="reading1" at="p. 77" />

This holds at **every** temperature and density, for any free non-relativistic particles in three dimensions, since nothing in the derivation used the form of $n_k$. To get the equation of state in the usual variables one solves the density equation for $\mu(N, T, V)$ and puts it into $U(T, V, \mu)$, so that $PV = \frac23U(T, V, \mu(T, V, N))$ (notes p. 77). The two limits below can be done by hand.

## The high-temperature limit: a classical gas

Keep $n$ fixed and raise $T$ (notes p. 78). The states within reach of the thermal energy fill a region of $k$-space whose radius grows as $T^{1/2}$ and whose volume grows as $T^{3/2}$; if $\mu$ stayed put, more and more of them would be occupied and $n$ would grow. To keep $n$ fixed the occupations must shrink: $\mu \to -\infty$ and every $n_k \ll 1$. Then the $+1$ in the denominator is negligible, $n_k \approx e^{\beta\mu}e^{-\beta\epsilon_k}$, and

<Formula label="Pressure and density at high temperature" tex="P = \frac23\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\,e^{-\beta\epsilon_k}\,e^{\beta\mu}, \qquad n = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,e^{-\beta\epsilon_k}\,e^{\beta\mu}" cite="reading1" at="p. 78" />

(The notes say the occupations fall as $1/T$; the typical occupation is $n\lambda^3$, which falls as $T^{-3/2}$. Either way they go to zero.) The factor $e^{\beta\mu}$ cancels in the ratio, which is two thirds of a Boltzmann average energy. Writing $I(\beta) = \int d^3k\,e^{-\beta\epsilon_k} \propto \beta^{-3/2}$:

<Formula label="The classical limit" tex="\frac Pn = \frac23\,\frac{\int d^3k\,\epsilon_k e^{-\beta\epsilon_k}}{\int d^3k\,e^{-\beta\epsilon_k}} = \frac23\langle\epsilon_k\rangle = \frac23\,\frac{-\partial I/\partial\beta}{I} = \frac1\beta = k_BT" cite="reading1" at="p. 78" />

So $PV = Nk_BT$, the classical ideal gas, with $U = N\langle\epsilon_k\rangle = \frac32Nk_BT$ consistent with $PV = \frac23U$. Wen's boxed conclusion: at high temperature a Fermi gas is a classical gas. (His margin sketch on p. 78 is a generic one: the occupation against energy, close to 1 well below $\mu$ and falling to 0 over a few $k_BT$ around it.) In the classical limit $\mu$ lies far below the lowest state, every level is nearly empty, the particles almost never compete for a state, and the Pauli principle has nothing to do.

## The zero-temperature limit: the Fermi sea

At $T = 0$ the Fermi–Dirac function becomes a step: $n_k = 1$ for $\epsilon_k < \mu$ and $n_k = 0$ for $\epsilon_k > \mu$ (notes p. 78). The value of $\mu$ at $T = 0$ is the **Fermi energy** $\epsilon_F$, and the occupied states fill a sphere in $k$-space, the **Fermi sea**, of radius $k_F = \sqrt{2m\epsilon_F}/\hbar$. Counting the states in it,

<Formula label="Fermi wave vector, one spin state" tex="n = \int_{k < k_F}\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3} = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{4\pi}{3}k_F^3 = \frac{k_F^3}{6\pi^2}, \qquad k_F = (6\pi^2n)^{1/3}, \qquad \epsilon_F = \frac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 79" />

(For spin-½ particles with both spin states filled, $n = k_F^3/3\pi^2$; the next page uses that.) The pressure integral is then elementary (notes p. 78):

<Formula label="Pressure at zero temperature" tex="P = \frac23\int_{k < k_F}\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} = \frac23\,\frac{\hbar^2}{2m(2\pi)^3}\int_0^{k_F}4\pi k^4\,dk = \frac{\hbar^2k_F^5}{30\pi^2m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 78" />

and in terms of the density (notes p. 79),

<Formula label="Fermi pressure" tex="P = \frac{(6\pi^2)^{5/3}}{15\pi^2}\,\frac{\hbar^2n^{2/3}}{2m}\,n = \frac25\,n\epsilon_F, \qquad V^{5/3}P = \frac{(6\pi^2)^{5/3}\hbar^2}{30\pi^2m}\,N^{5/3} = \text{const.}" cite="reading1" at="p. 79" />

A classical gas at $T = 0$ would exert no pressure at all. The Fermi gas does, and as it is squeezed its zero-temperature pressure rises as $V^{-5/3}$, more steeply than the $V^{-1}$ of a classical isotherm (Wen's sketch on p. 79).

## Quantum limit and classical limit

Wen compares the two limits by a rough estimate (notes p. 79). At $T = 0$ the pressure is of the order of an energy per volume, that is, the energy per particle times $n$. Each particle is confined, by the others, to a region of size about $n^{-1/3}$, so its kinetic energy is about $\hbar^2n^{2/3}/2m$, and

<Formula label="The Fermi pressure, estimated" tex="P \sim \frac{\hbar^2n^{2/3}}{2m}\times n" cite="reading1" at="p. 79" />

This is the **Fermi pressure**, a pressure of quantum origin: the Pauli principle forbids two fermions to share the same small region in the same state, and confinement costs kinetic energy. In a hot gas the thermal energy $k_BT$ is much larger than this confinement energy, and the classical picture holds. The condition is

<Formula label="Condition for the classical limit" tex="\frac{\sqrt{2mk_BT}}{\hbar} \gg n^{1/3}, \quad\text{or}\quad k_BT \gg \frac{\hbar^2k_F^2}{2m} = \epsilon_F" cite="reading1" at="p. 79" />

(The notes write the first inequality the other way round, $\ll$; the second form, which they give alongside, shows that $\gg$ is meant.) In words: the typical thermal wave number is much larger than $n^{1/3}$, so the thermal wavelength is much smaller than the spacing between particles, the condition $n\lambda^3 \ll 1$ of Chapter I. For one spin state the two forms are related exactly by $n\lambda^3 = \frac{4}{3\sqrt\pi}\,(T_F/T)^{3/2}$, with $T_F = \epsilon_F/k_B$ the **Fermi temperature** (this relation is the page's addition).

## The first quantum correction

How does the classical gas turn into a quantum one as it cools? Wen expands about the classical limit (notes pp. 80–81). Write the **fugacity** $z = e^{\beta\mu}$, which is small at high temperature, and use the Gaussian integral of Chapter I:

<Formula label="Grand potential and free energy at high temperature" tex="\Omega = -k_BTV\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\ln\left(1 + ze^{-\beta\epsilon_k}\right), \qquad A = \Omega + \mu N, \qquad \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,e^{-\beta\epsilon_k} = \lambda^{-3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 80" />

with $\lambda = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT}$ and $\mu$ in $A$ expressed through $V$, $N$ and $T$. Since $\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}e^{-2\beta\epsilon_k} = 2^{-3/2}\lambda^{-3}$, expanding the occupation $ze^{-\beta\epsilon_k}/(1 + ze^{-\beta\epsilon_k})$ to second order in $z$ gives

<Formula label="Density to second order in the fugacity" tex="N = V\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\left(ze^{-\beta\epsilon_k} - z^2e^{-2\beta\epsilon_k}\right) = V\left(z - \frac{z^2}{2^{3/2}}\right)\lambda^{-3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 80" />

so $n\lambda^3 = z - z^2/2^{3/2}$, which inverts, to the same order, to

<Formula label="Fugacity in terms of the density" tex="z = n\lambda^3 + \frac{(n\lambda^3)^2}{2^{3/2}}, \qquad \mu = k_BT\ln z" cite="reading1" at="p. 80" />

The fugacity is slightly larger than its classical value $n\lambda^3$: to hold the same density, a Fermi gas must be pushed to a higher chemical potential, because the Pauli principle keeps the lowest states from being crowded. Now expand $\ln(1 + x) \approx x - \frac12x^2$ in $\Omega$, add $\mu N = Nk_BT\ln z$ with $\ln z \approx \ln n\lambda^3 + n\lambda^3/2^{3/2}$, and collect terms to first order in $n\lambda^3$ (notes p. 81):

<Formula label="Free energy with the first quantum correction" tex="A = Nk_BT\left(\ln n\lambda^3 - 1 + \frac12\,\frac{n\lambda^3}{2^{3/2}}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 81" />

The first two terms are the classical ideal gas of Chapter I; the last is the **quantum correction**, small when $n\lambda^3 \ll 1$. It depends on the volume through $n = N/V$, so it adds to the pressure:

<Formula label="Pressure with the quantum correction" tex="P = -\frac{\partial A}{\partial V} = \frac{Nk_BT}{V} + k_BT\,\frac{N^2\lambda^3}{2^{5/2}V^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 81" />

(The notes' first term reads $k_BT/V$, with the factor $N$ left out.) As a **virial expansion** (notes p. 81),

<Formula label="Virial expansion of the Fermi gas" tex="\frac{PV}{Nk_BT} = 1 + \frac{n\lambda^3}{2^{5/2}} = 1 + \frac{C_2}{V} + \cdots, \qquad C_2 = \frac{N\lambda^3}{2^{5/2}} > 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 81" />

(On the left the notes write $PV/k_BT$, again without the $N$.) This is the second virial coefficient quoted on [the van der Waals page](/courses/8-08/van-der-waals-equation): positive, as for a gas with a hard-core repulsion. The extra pressure is the Fermi pressure beginning to show: the fermions keep out of each other's way within about a thermal wavelength.

<WorkedExample title="A cold gas of potassium-40 atoms">
<Problem>
A trapped gas of potassium-40 atoms, all in the same spin state, so that Wen's one-spin formulas apply exactly, has density $n = 1.0 \times 10^{19}$ m⁻³ (mass $m = 6.636 \times 10^{-26}$ kg). (a) Find $k_F$ and the Fermi temperature $T_F$. (b) At $T = 2.0$ μK, find $\lambda$, $n\lambda^3$ and the quantum correction to $PV/(Nk_BT)$. (c) What would the pressure be at $T = 0$?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The Fermi sphere">
$k_F = (6\pi^2 \times 1.0 \times 10^{19}\ \text{m}^{-3})^{1/3} \approx 8.40 \times 10^6$ m⁻¹. Then $\epsilon_F = \hbar^2k_F^2/2m \approx 5.91 \times 10^{-30}$ J, and $T_F = \epsilon_F/k_B \approx 0.43$ μK.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) At 2 μK">
$\lambda = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT} \approx 0.195$ μm, so $n\lambda^3 \approx 0.074$. The check $\frac{4}{3\sqrt\pi}(T_F/T)^{3/2} = 0.752 \times (0.428/2.0)^{3/2}$, with $T_F$ unrounded, gives the same. The correction is $n\lambda^3/2^{5/2} \approx 0.013$: the gas pushes about 1.3% harder than a classical gas at the same density and temperature. With $T$ almost five times $T_F$ the gas is nearly classical, but not quite.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) At zero temperature">
$P = \frac25n\epsilon_F \approx 2.4 \times 10^{-11}$ Pa. That is tiny in everyday terms, because the gas is millions of times thinner than air, but it is not zero: cooled to absolute zero, a classical gas would exert no pressure at all.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Fermions in the early universe">
In the hot early universe, electrons, positrons and neutrinos were Fermi gases in thermal equilibrium, with chemical potentials close to zero and so hot that they moved at nearly the speed of light. The occupation number of this page, with $\mu = 0$ and $\epsilon = \hbar ck$, then fixes how much energy each species carries. Compared with the Bose–Einstein occupation $1/(e^x - 1)$ of photons, the Fermi–Dirac one gives exactly $7/8$ as much energy per spin state, the factor that [MIT 8.286 Lecture 17](/courses/8-286/neutrinos-and-particle-counting) uses when it counts the particles that fill the early universe.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Free fermions are labelled by occupation numbers $n_k = 0, 1$, with $E = \sum_k\epsilon_kn_k$. In the grand ensemble $Q_G = \prod_k[1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)}]$ and $\Omega = -k_BT\sum_k\ln[1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)}]$.
- $N = -\partial\Omega/\partial\mu$ gives the **Fermi–Dirac distribution** $n_k = 1/(e^{\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)} + 1)$; a region $d^3k$ holds $V\,d^3k/(2\pi)^3$ states.
- Because $\epsilon_k \propto V^{-2/3}$, $P = -\partial\Omega/\partial V$ gives **$PV = \frac23U$** at every temperature.
- **High temperature** ($n\lambda^3 \ll 1$, $k_BT \gg \epsilon_F$): $\mu \to -\infty$, $n_k \ll 1$ and $PV = Nk_BT$: a classical gas.
- **Zero temperature**: a Fermi sea of radius $k_F = (6\pi^2n)^{1/3}$ (one spin state), with the Fermi pressure $P = \frac25n\epsilon_F \propto n^{5/3}$.
- Expanding in the fugacity $z = e^{\beta\mu}$: $A = Nk_BT(\ln n\lambda^3 - 1 + n\lambda^3/2^{5/2})$ and $PV/(Nk_BT) = 1 + n\lambda^3/2^{5/2}$, a positive second virial coefficient.
