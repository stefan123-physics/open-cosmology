A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it; type decimals, for example `0.25` or `-1.5`. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `sqrt(c*x/(d + y))^3`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `a0` stands for $a_0$, `Tc` for $T_c$, `xi` for $\xi$, `kB` for $k_B$, `eF` for $\epsilon_F$, `hbar` for $\hbar$, `mn` for $m_n$, `pi` is π, `sqrt` the square root, `exp` the exponential and `log` the natural logarithm; the other letters (`a`, `b`, `m`, `T`, `N`, `S`, `n`, `G`, `M`) are typed as they are.

MIT's sheet gives no points. The points are ours: 20 for Problem 1, 15 each for Problems 2 and 3, and 20 for Problem 4, 70 points in all; every split between parts is ours too. Parts that ask you to show something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes, with MIT's letters in Problem 1. $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature, $\beta = 1/k_BT$ and $\mu$ the chemical potential. In Problem 1, $\psi(x)$ is the amplitude of the condensed bosons, $A$ the Ginzburg–Landau free energy, $a(T)$, $a_0$ and $b$ its coefficients, $m$ the mass of a boson, $U(x)$ the external potential, $T_c$ the transition temperature, $\xi$ a length and $\nu$ an exponent; MIT's free energy is written in units with $\hbar = 1$, and we keep them. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A condensate next to a hard wall" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

Bosons that interact with each other move along a line. Their condensate is described by the Ginzburg–Landau free energy

<Formula label="Ginzburg–Landau free energy in one dimension" tex="A = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}dx\left[\frac{1}{2m}\left|\partial_x\psi\right|^2 + \left(\frac{a(T)}{2} + U(x)\right)|\psi|^2 + \frac b4|\psi|^4\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

where $\psi$ is the amplitude of the condensed bosons (the order parameter) and, close to $T_c$,

<Formula label="The quadratic coefficient" tex="a(T) = a_0\left(\frac{T}{T_c} - 1\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

with constants $a_0$, $b$ and $m$; we take $a_0 > 0$ and $b > 0$ (our assumption, which MIT's question needs). A hard wall fills the left half of the line:

<Formula label="The wall" tex="U(x) = +\infty\ \ \text{for } x < 0, \qquad U(x) = 0\ \ \text{for } x > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

(The notes' free energy, reading 1, p. 74, is written in three dimensions with $\hbar^2/2m$, $a(T)$ and $\frac12U_0$ in place of MIT's $1/2m$, $a/2$ and $b/4$; the physics is the same.)

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Show that the bosons condense below $T_c$, and find the size $|\psi(+\infty)|$ of the condensate amplitude far from the wall.

<Hint>

Far from the wall $U = 0$ and the amplitude stops changing, so only the terms without a derivative matter. Write them as a function of $\rho = |\psi|^2 \geq 0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A free energy per length of the form $\frac a2\rho + \frac b4\rho^2$ has its smallest value at $\rho = 0$ when $a > 0$. What happens when $a < 0$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-amp" label="$|\psi(+\infty)|$ for $T < T_c$:" expr="sqrt(a0*(1 - T/Tc)/b)"
  vars={{ a0: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2], T: [0.1, 0.9], Tc: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ a0: "a_0", b: "b", T: "T", Tc: "T_c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2*a0*(1 - T/Tc)/b)", hint: "That sets a/2 + (b/4)|ψ|² to zero. The derivative of (b/4)ρ² with respect to ρ = |ψ|² is (b/2)ρ, not (b/4)ρ." },
    { expr: "a0*(1 - T/Tc)/b", hint: "That is the condensate density |ψ|², not the amplitude. The box asks for |ψ(+∞)|, its square root." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Far from the wall the condensate is uniform, so it simply sits at the minimum of the potential part of the free energy.

<Step title="The uniform free energy">

For $x \to +\infty$ the potential is zero and $\partial_x\psi \to 0$, so the free energy per length is $f(\rho) = \frac a2\rho + \frac b4\rho^2$ with $\rho = |\psi|^2$. Its slope is $f'(\rho) = \frac a2 + \frac b2\rho$.

</Step>

<Step title="Above and below the transition">

Above $T_c$, $a > 0$ and $f$ grows for every $\rho > 0$: the minimum is $\rho = 0$, no condensate. Below $T_c$, $a < 0$ and $f$ first falls: the minimum moves to $f'(\rho) = 0$, a nonzero density of condensed bosons. That is the condensation.

<Formula label="Condensate far from the wall" tex="|\psi(+\infty)|^2 = -\frac ab = \frac{a_0}{b}\left(1 - \frac{T}{T_c}\right), \qquad |\psi(+\infty)| = \sqrt{\frac{a_0}{b}\left(1 - \frac{T}{T_c}\right)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Only the size is fixed: $\psi(+\infty) = |\psi(+\infty)|\,e^{i\theta}$ with any phase $\theta$, the symmetry $\psi \to e^{i\theta}\psi$ of the notes (reading 1, p. 74).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The amplitude grows from zero as $(T_c - T)^{1/2}$, the mean-field law of an order parameter. The notes find the same density with their letters, $|\psi|^2 = -a/U_0$ (reading 1, p. 75a): MIT's $\frac a2$ and $\frac b4$ are the notes' $a$ and $\frac{U_0}{2}$ halved, and the ratio is unchanged. At the minimum, $f = -a^2/4b$ per length, which parts (b) and (c) use.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Near $x = 0$ the wall pushes the condensate away. To estimate how far, MIT takes a trial form for $\psi$, linear up to a length $\xi$ and constant after it:

<Formula label="Trial profile of the condensate" tex="\psi(x) = 0\ \ (x < 0), \qquad \psi(x) = \frac x\xi\,\psi(+\infty)\ \ (0 < x < \xi), \qquad \psi(x) = \psi(+\infty)\ \ (\xi < x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

with $\psi(+\infty)$ from part (a). Find how the free energy depends on $\xi$; part (c) then finds the $\xi$ that makes it smallest. Write $a$ for $a(T)$, which is negative here. Give $\Delta A(\xi)$, the free energy of the trial profile minus that of a condensate with $\psi = \psi(+\infty)$ everywhere in $x > 0$.

<Hint>

Split the line into three pieces. For $x < 0$, $\psi = 0$ and nothing contributes. For $x > \xi$ the profile is the uniform condensate, with $-a^2/4b$ per length. Only the strip $0 < x < \xi$ needs an integral.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the strip, $|\partial_x\psi|^2 = |\psi(+\infty)|^2/\xi^2$, and $\int_0^\xi (x/\xi)^2\,dx = \xi/3$, $\int_0^\xi (x/\xi)^4\,dx = \xi/5$. Compare the strip with the uniform condensate over the same length $\xi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-da" label="$\Delta A(\xi) =$" expr="-a/(2*m*b*xi) + 2*a^2*xi/(15*b)"
  vars={{ a: [-2, -0.5], b: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], xi: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ a: "a", b: "b", m: "m", xi: "\\xi" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-a/(2*m*b*xi) - 7*a^2*xi/(60*b)", hint: "That is the free energy of the strip 0 < x < ξ alone. A wider strip also shortens the uniform region beyond ξ, which would have had −a²/4b per length: compare with the uniform condensate over the same length." },
    { expr: "-a/(2*m*b*xi) + a^2*xi/(4*b)", hint: "That treats the strip as if it held no condensate at all, keeping only the gradient term and the lost −a²/4b per length. The linear ramp still has some condensate: add the a/2 and b/4 terms of the strip." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The wall forces the amplitude to zero. Rising quickly costs gradient energy; rising slowly leaves a wide strip with too little condensate. The trial length $\xi$ balances the two.

<Step title="The strip">

With $\rho_\infty = |\psi(+\infty)|^2 = -a/b$, the three terms over $0 < x < \xi$ are

<Formula label="Free energy of the strip" tex="\int_0^\xi dx\left[\frac{\rho_\infty}{2m\xi^2} + \frac a2\rho_\infty\frac{x^2}{\xi^2} + \frac b4\rho_\infty^2\frac{x^4}{\xi^4}\right] = \frac{\rho_\infty}{2m\xi} + \frac{a\rho_\infty\xi}{6} + \frac{b\rho_\infty^2\xi}{20} = -\frac{a}{2mb\xi} - \frac{7a^2\xi}{60b}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compared with the uniform condensate">

A uniform condensate over the same length has $-a^2\xi/4b$. The difference is

<Formula label="Free energy of the trial profile, measured from the uniform condensate" tex="\Delta A(\xi) = -\frac{a}{2mb\,\xi} - \frac{7a^2\xi}{60b} + \frac{a^2\xi}{4b} = \frac{|a|}{2mb\,\xi} + \frac{2a^2}{15b}\,\xi" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The first term, the cost of bending, falls with $\xi$; the second, the cost of the missing condensate, grows with it.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Both terms are positive: the wall always costs free energy, as it must, since the uniform state is the lowest. As $\xi \to 0$ the cost of the sudden jump diverges; as $\xi \to \infty$ the strip without enough condensate grows without limit.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

(MIT's part (b), continued.) Find the length $\xi$ that makes $\Delta A$ of part (b) smallest.

<Hint>

Set $d\Delta A/d\xi = 0$; remember that $|a| = -a$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-xi" label="$\xi$ at the minimum:" expr="sqrt(-15/(4*m*a))"
  vars={{ a: [-2, -0.5], m: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ a: "a", m: "m" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-15/(4*m*a)", hint: "That is ξ², the value where the two terms' slopes cancel. ξ is its square root, a length." },
    { expr: "sqrt(-1/(m*a))", hint: "That is the length set by the units alone. Keep the numbers: the minimum of |a|/(2mbξ) + 2a²ξ/(15b) carries a factor 15/4." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A sum $\alpha/\xi + \gamma\xi$ is smallest at $\xi = \sqrt{\alpha/\gamma}$.

<Step title="The minimum">

$d\Delta A/d\xi = -|a|/(2mb\xi^2) + 2a^2/(15b) = 0$ gives

<Formula label="The best trial length" tex="\xi^2 = \frac{15}{4m|a|}, \qquad \xi = \sqrt{\frac{15}{4m|a(T)|}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

($b$ cancels), and the smallest cost is $\Delta A_{\min} = 2\sqrt{\alpha\gamma} = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt{15}}\,\dfrac{|a|^{3/2}}{b\sqrt m} = 0.516\,\dfrac{|a|^{3/2}}{b\sqrt m}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check against the exact profile (our addition)">

Setting the variation of $A$ to zero gives $\frac{1}{2m}\psi'' = \frac a2\psi + \frac b2|\psi|^2\psi$, whose solution with $\psi(0) = 0$ is $\psi = \psi(+\infty)\tanh(x/w)$ with $w = \sqrt{2/(m|a|)}$. Its cost is $\dfrac{\sqrt2}{3}\,\dfrac{|a|^{3/2}}{b\sqrt m} = 0.471\,\dfrac{|a|^{3/2}}{b\sqrt m}$. The trial profile costs a factor $\sqrt{6/5} = 1.095$ more, as a trial function must, and its $\xi$ is $1.37$ times $w$: the same length scale, with the same dependence on $m$ and $a$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

(MIT's part (c).) Show that close to $T_c$ the length $\xi$ diverges as

<Formula label="Divergence of the length" tex="\xi \propto |T_c - T|^{\nu}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

and find the critical exponent $\nu$, as defined by this formula (so a length that diverges has $\nu < 0$; many books put the sign in the formula instead and write $\xi \propto |T - T_c|^{-\nu}$). The length $\xi$ is the coherence length of the superfluid; MIT notes that it is, for example, the size of the core of a vortex.

<Answer id="p1-nu" label="$\nu$ (as a decimal):" value="-0.5" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-nu" mistakes={[{ value: "0.5", tolerance: "0.01", calc: "p1-nu-books", hint: "Right size, wrong sign for this page's definition: with ξ ∝ |T_c − T|^ν, a length that diverges at T_c needs ν < 0. The +½ belongs to the books' form |T − T_c|^(−ν)." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put $a(T)$ into the result of part (c).

<Step title="Near the transition">

With $|a| = a_0(1 - T/T_c) = a_0|T_c - T|/T_c$,

<Formula label="The coherence length near the transition" tex="\xi = \sqrt{\frac{15\,T_c}{4ma_0}}\,|T_c - T|^{-1/2}, \qquad \nu = -\frac12" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The exact width $w = \sqrt{2/(m|a|)}$ of part (c) diverges with the same power, so the exponent does not depend on the trial form: it comes from $|a| \propto |T_c - T|$ alone, the mean-field value. In the other convention this is the familiar $\nu = \frac12$ of Ginzburg–Landau theory. As $T \to T_c$ the condensate becomes so weak that it needs an ever longer distance to recover from the wall.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The chemical potential of a warm Fermi gas (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the degenerate Fermi gas of Chapter VIII. The notes find the heat capacity without computing how the chemical potential moves; here you compute it.

Take $N$ free spin-½ fermions in a volume $V$ in three dimensions, with a density of states $D(\epsilon) \propto \epsilon^{1/2}$ for $\epsilon > 0$ (both spin states counted: twice the notes' one-spin $D$, reading 2, p. 82). At zero temperature they fill the levels up to $\epsilon_F$; we take $k_BT \ll \epsilon_F$ (our assumption).

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Show the Sommerfeld expansion: for a function $\phi(\epsilon)$ that is smooth near $\mu$ and grows no faster than a power,

<Formula label="The Sommerfeld expansion" tex="\int_0^\infty\phi(\epsilon)\,n_F(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon = \int_0^\mu\phi(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon + \frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_BT)^2\phi'(\mu) + \ldots" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

where the dots are of order $T^4$ (and terms of order $e^{-\mu/k_BT}$).

<Hint>

Let $\Phi(\epsilon) = \int_0^\epsilon\phi$. Integrate by parts: the integral becomes $\int\Phi(\epsilon)\,(-\partial n_F/\partial\epsilon)\,d\epsilon$, and $-\partial n_F/\partial\epsilon$ is a narrow peak at $\mu$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Expand $\Phi$ about $\mu$ to second order. With $t = \beta(\epsilon - \mu)$, the integral $\int t^2e^t/(1 + e^t)^2\,dt$ over the whole line is the one the notes evaluate for the heat capacity (reading 2, p. 87).

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** At low temperature $n_F$ differs from a step only within a few $k_BT$ of $\mu$; the expansion collects that difference.

<Step title="By parts">

With $\Phi' = \phi$ and $\Phi(0) = 0$, and $n_F \to 0$ at large $\epsilon$, $\int_0^\infty\phi\,n_F\,d\epsilon = \int_0^\infty\Phi(\epsilon)\left(-\partial n_F/\partial\epsilon\right)d\epsilon$. The weight $-\partial n_F/\partial\epsilon = \beta e^t/(1 + e^t)^2$ has unit area and is even in $t = \beta(\epsilon - \mu)$. Extending the lower limit to $-\infty$ costs only terms of order $e^{-\beta\mu}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Expand">

With $\Phi(\epsilon) = \Phi(\mu) + (\epsilon - \mu)\phi(\mu) + \frac12(\epsilon - \mu)^2\phi'(\mu) + \ldots$, the first term gives $\Phi(\mu)$, the second vanishes because the weight is even, and the third gives $\frac12\phi'(\mu)(k_BT)^2\int t^2e^t/(1 + e^t)^2\,dt = \frac12\phi'(\mu)(k_BT)^2\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{3}$. That is the result. The next even term is of order $(k_BT)^4$ times the third derivative of $\phi$ at $\mu$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $\phi = 1$ (with a cut-off far above $\mu$) the correction vanishes, as it must: the area under $n_F$ is shifted symmetrically about $\mu$, which is the picture of the notes (reading 2, p. 87, and the lesson's discussion of why $\mu$ moves only as $T^2$).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Keep $N$ fixed and find $\mu(T)$ to order $T^2$.

<Hint>

Use part (a) with $\phi = D$: $N = \int_0^\mu D\,d\epsilon + \frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_BT)^2D'(\mu)$ must equal $\int_0^{\epsilon_F}D\,d\epsilon$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

To the order needed, $\int_\mu^{\epsilon_F}D\,d\epsilon \approx (\epsilon_F - \mu)D(\epsilon_F)$, and in the small term $D'(\mu)$ may be taken at $\epsilon_F$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-mu" label="$\mu(T) =$" expr="eF*(1 - pi^2/12*(kB*T/eF)^2)"
  vars={{ eF: [1, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.02, 0.2] }}
  names={{ eF: "\\epsilon_F", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "eF*(1 + pi^2/12*(kB*T/eF)^2)", hint: "The sign: in three dimensions D grows with ε, so warming would add particles above μ faster than it removes them below; μ must fall to keep N fixed." },
    { expr: "eF*(1 - pi^2/6*(kB*T/eF)^2)", hint: "That is the shift for D ∝ ε, where D′/D = 1/ε. In three dimensions D ∝ ε^(1/2), so D′/D = 1/(2ε) and the shift is half as large." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Warming spreads the occupation symmetrically about $\mu$; because $D$ is larger above $\mu$ than below, that would add particles, so $\mu$ must drop.

<Step title="Fixed number">

From part (a) with $\phi = D$, and $\int_\mu^{\epsilon_F}D \approx (\epsilon_F - \mu)D(\epsilon_F)$,

<Formula label="Shift of the chemical potential" tex="\mu - \epsilon_F = -\frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_BT)^2\,\frac{D'(\epsilon_F)}{D(\epsilon_F)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Three dimensions">

With $D \propto \epsilon^{1/2}$, $D'/D = 1/(2\epsilon_F)$:

<Formula label="Chemical potential of the warm 3D Fermi gas" tex="\mu(T) = \epsilon_F\left[1 - \frac{\pi^2}{12}\left(\frac{k_BT}{\epsilon_F}\right)^2\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The shift is of order $T^2$, as the notes argue (reading 2, p. 87), and negative, as the lesson says for the 3D density of states. A numerical solution of $N = \int D\,n_F$ at $k_BT = 0.05\,\epsilon_F$ gives $\mu = 0.99794\,\epsilon_F$; the formula gives $0.99794\,\epsilon_F$ too.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the energy $E(T)$ of the gas to order $T^2$, in terms of $N$, $\epsilon_F$ and $k_BT$.

<Hint>

Use part (a) with $\phi = \epsilon D(\epsilon)$, then replace $\int_0^\mu$ by $\int_0^{\epsilon_F}$ plus a correction that uses the shift of part (b).

</Hint>

<Hint>

In three dimensions $D(\epsilon_F) = 3N/2\epsilon_F$ and $E(0) = \frac35N\epsilon_F$ (reading 2, p. 83).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-u" label="$E(T) =$" expr="3/5*N*eF + pi^2/4*N*(kB*T)^2/eF"
  vars={{ N: [1, 100], eF: [1, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.02, 0.2] }}
  names={{ N: "N", eF: "\\epsilon_F", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3/5*N*eF + 3*pi^2/8*N*(kB*T)^2/eF", hint: "That keeps the T² term of the expansion with μ left at ε_F. The chemical potential has moved down by the shift of part (b), which removes ε_F D times that shift: the D′ part cancels." },
    { expr: "3/5*N*eF + pi^2/2*N*(kB*T)^2/eF", hint: "That is T times the heat capacity, C·T = (π²/2)N k_B² T²/ε_F. The energy is the integral of C dT, which brings a factor 1/2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same expansion, now with the energy-weighted density of states, and the shift of $\mu$ taken into account.

<Step title="The expansion">

$E = \int_0^\mu\epsilon D\,d\epsilon + \frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_BT)^2\left[D + \epsilon D'\right]_{\epsilon_F}$. The first term is $E(0) - (\epsilon_F - \mu)\epsilon_FD(\epsilon_F) = E(0) - \frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_BT)^2\epsilon_FD'(\epsilon_F)$ by part (b). The $D'$ parts cancel and

<Formula label="Energy of the warm Fermi gas" tex="E = E(0) + \frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_BT)^2D(\epsilon_F) = \frac35N\epsilon_F + \frac{\pi^2}{4}\,\frac{N(k_BT)^2}{\epsilon_F}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Differentiating, $C = dE/dT = \frac{\pi^2}{3}k_B^2T\,D(\epsilon_F) = \frac{\pi^2}{2}Nk_B\,\frac{k_BT}{\epsilon_F}$, the heat capacity of the notes (reading 2, p. 87), found there by another route.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

(Our numbers.) From your result of part (b), how far has $\mu$ fallen below $\epsilon_F$ at $T = 0.1\,T_F$? Give $(\epsilon_F - \mu)/\epsilon_F$ as a decimal (tolerance 1%).

<Answer id="p2-shift" label="$(\epsilon_F - \mu)/\epsilon_F$:" value="0.00822" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-shift" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put $k_BT/\epsilon_F = 0.1$ into part (b).

<Step title="The number">

$(\epsilon_F - \mu)/\epsilon_F = \frac{\pi^2}{12}\times 0.01 = 0.00822$: less than one percent, even at a tenth of the Fermi temperature. For the electrons of a metal at room temperature, $T/T_F$ is a few thousandths and the shift is far smaller still.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The exact numerical solution at $T = 0.1\,T_F$ gives $0.00836$; the difference, about 1.6% of the shift, is mostly the term of order $T^4$ that the expansion drops.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="An ideal Fermi gas in two dimensions (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the ideal Fermi gas of Chapter VIII, in two dimensions, where the notes' formulas can be carried through exactly.

$N$ free spin-½ fermions of mass $m$ move on a flat area $S$ (for example electrons in a thin layer; our model), with $\epsilon_k = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ and $\vec k = \frac{2\pi}{L}(n_x, n_y)$ as in the notes (reading 2, p. 76), in two dimensions.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the density of states $D(\epsilon)$, counting both spin states.

<Hint>

Count the states with wave number below $k$: in two dimensions each state takes an area $(2\pi)^2/S$ of the $k$-plane, and each $\vec k$ holds two fermions.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-dos" label="$D(\epsilon) =$" expr="S*m/(pi*hbar^2)"
  vars={{ S: [1, 10], m: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ S: "S", m: "m", hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "S*m/(2*pi*hbar^2)", hint: "That counts one spin state. Spin-½ fermions have two states for each wave vector." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count, then differentiate the count, as the notes do in three dimensions (reading 2, p. 82).

<Step title="The count">

The number of states below $\epsilon$ is $2\times\dfrac{S}{(2\pi)^2}\,\pi k^2 = \dfrac{Sk^2}{2\pi}$ with $k^2 = 2m\epsilon/\hbar^2$, so it is $\dfrac{Sm\epsilon}{\pi\hbar^2}$.

<Formula label="Density of states in two dimensions" tex="D(\epsilon) = \frac{d}{d\epsilon}\,\frac{Sm\epsilon}{\pi\hbar^2} = \frac{Sm}{\pi\hbar^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

It does not depend on $\epsilon$: in two dimensions the growing circumference of the circle $2\pi k$ is exactly cancelled by $dk/d\epsilon \propto 1/k$. In three dimensions the extra factor $k$ gives $D \propto \epsilon^{1/2}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find the Fermi energy $\epsilon_F$ in terms of the density $n = N/S$.

<Answer id="p3-ef" label="$\epsilon_F =$" expr="pi*hbar^2*n/m"
  vars={{ n: [1, 10], m: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ n: "n", m: "m", hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*pi*hbar^2*n/m", hint: "That puts one fermion in each state. With spin ½ two fermions share each wave vector, so the levels fill only half as high." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At zero temperature the $N$ fermions fill every state up to $\epsilon_F$.

<Step title="Filling">

$N = \int_0^{\epsilon_F}D\,d\epsilon = \dfrac{Sm}{\pi\hbar^2}\,\epsilon_F$, so

<Formula label="Fermi energy in two dimensions" tex="\epsilon_F = \frac{\pi\hbar^2n}{m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$\epsilon_F \propto n$ in two dimensions, against $n^{2/3}$ in three (reading 2, p. 79): in both, $\epsilon_F \sim \hbar^2/(m\,\ell^2)$, with $\ell$ the spacing between the particles.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the chemical potential $\mu(T)$ at fixed $N$, exactly, at any temperature.

<Hint>

$N = D\int_0^\infty d\epsilon/(e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)} + 1)$ with a constant $D$. The integrand is $-\frac1\beta\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\ln\left(1 + e^{-\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}\right)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-mu" label="$\mu(T) =$" expr="kB*T*log(exp(eF/(kB*T)) - 1)"
  vars={{ eF: [1, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ eF: "\\epsilon_F", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "kB*T*log(eF/(kB*T))", hint: "That is the classical gas, μ = k_BT ln(nλ²/2) with two spin states, which holds only when k_BT ≫ ε_F. The integral can be done exactly; it needs no approximation." },
    { expr: "eF", hint: "That is the value at zero temperature. In two dimensions μ stays very close to ε_F at low temperature, but not at every temperature: try k_BT = ε_F." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With a constant density of states the number integral is elementary.

<Step title="The integral">

$N = D\,k_BT\ln\left(1 + e^{\beta\mu}\right)$, and $N = D\epsilon_F$. So $1 + e^{\beta\mu} = e^{\beta\epsilon_F}$ and

<Formula label="Exact chemical potential in two dimensions" tex="\mu(T) = k_BT\ln\left(e^{\epsilon_F/k_BT} - 1\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At high temperature $e^{\epsilon_F/k_BT} - 1 \approx \epsilon_F/k_BT$ and $\mu \approx k_BT\ln(\epsilon_F/k_BT)$, which is large and negative: the classical gas, with $\epsilon_F/k_BT = n\lambda^2/2$ for $\lambda = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT}$. At low temperature $\mu \to \epsilon_F$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

(Our numbers.) Give $\mu/\epsilon_F$ at $T = T_F$ as a decimal (tolerance 1%).

<Answer id="p3-num" label="$\mu/\epsilon_F$ at $T = T_F$:" value="0.5413" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put $k_BT = \epsilon_F$ into part (c).

<Step title="The number">

$\mu/\epsilon_F = \ln(e - 1) = 0.5413$. The chemical potential passes through zero at $k_BT = \epsilon_F/\ln 2 = 1.443\,\epsilon_F$, roughly where the gas stops being degenerate.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

At low temperature, $k_BT \ll \epsilon_F$, how does $\mu$ differ from $\epsilon_F$ in two dimensions?

<Answer id="p3-low" label="For k_BT ≪ ε_F, ε_F − μ is" choices={["proportional to T², as in three dimensions", "proportional to T, from the width k_BT of the step", "exponentially small, about k_BT e^(−ε_F/k_BT)", "negative: μ rises above ε_F as the gas warms"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Expand the exact result, and compare with the rule of Problem 2.

<Step title="The expansion">

$\mu = \epsilon_F + k_BT\ln\left(1 - e^{-\epsilon_F/k_BT}\right) \approx \epsilon_F - k_BT\,e^{-\epsilon_F/k_BT}$. The correction is smaller than any power of $T$.

</Step>

<Step title="Why">

Problem 2(b) found $\mu - \epsilon_F = -\frac{\pi^2}{6}(k_BT)^2D'/D$. In two dimensions $D' = 0$, so the $T^2$ term vanishes, and so do all higher terms of the expansion, which bring higher derivatives of $D$. What remains comes from the bottom of the band at $\epsilon = 0$, which the Sommerfeld expansion leaves out. The heat capacity is still $\frac{\pi^2}{3}k_B^2T\,D$ at low temperature, as the notes say in any dimension (reading 2, p. 87).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A neutron star (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 4">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the compact stars at the end of the degenerate Fermi gas (reading 2, p. 88). The notes estimate the radius of a neutron star without numerical factors; here the factors are kept, and the star's few protons are counted too.

Our model: a ball of $N = M/m_n$ neutrons of uniform density, radius $R$ and mass $M$, at zero temperature. The neutrons are a nonrelativistic spin-½ Fermi gas with $k_n = (3\pi^2n_n)^{1/3}$ (two spin states, as in reading 2, p. 84), and the gravitational energy of a uniform ball is $-\frac35GM^2/R$ (standard result, stated by us). Use $\hbar = 1.0546 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, $G = 6.6743 \times 10^{-11}$ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², $m_n = 1.6749 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, $c = 2.9979 \times 10^8$ m/s and the solar mass $M_\odot = 1.9884 \times 10^{30}$ kg (CODATA 2018 and the IAU; our values).

<Part label="a" points={5}>

The energy of the star is the kinetic energy of the neutron Fermi sea plus the gravitational energy. Find the radius $R$ that makes it smallest, in terms of $\hbar$, $G$, $m_n$ and $M$.

<Hint>

The kinetic energy is $\frac35N\epsilon_F$ with $\epsilon_F = \hbar^2k_n^2/2m_n$ (reading 2, p. 83), and $n_n = N/(\frac43\pi R^3)$. It falls as $1/R^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The total has the form $\alpha/R^2 - \gamma/R$, whose minimum is at $R = 2\alpha/\gamma$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-r" label="$R =$" expr="(9*pi/4)^(2/3)*hbar^2/(G*mn^(8/3)*M^(1/3))"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 1.5], G: [0.5, 1.5], mn: [0.5, 1.5], M: [1, 10] }}

<Solution>

**The idea.** Squeezing raises the Fermi energy as $1/R^2$; gravity gains only as $1/R$. The balance is a minimum.

<Step title="The kinetic energy">

With $k_n = (3\pi^2n_n)^{1/3} = (9\pi N/4)^{1/3}/R$,

<Formula label="Energy of the neutron Fermi sea" tex="E_K = \frac35N\,\frac{\hbar^2k_n^2}{2m_n} = \frac{3}{10}\left(\frac{9\pi}{4}\right)^{2/3}\frac{\hbar^2N^{5/3}}{m_nR^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

$E = E_K - \frac35GM^2/R$ is smallest at $R = 2\alpha/\gamma$ with $\alpha = E_KR^2$ and $\gamma = \frac35GM^2$. With $N = M/m_n$,

<Step title="The number">

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Compare $R$ with the Schwarzschild radius $2GM/c^2$ of the same mass. Which statement is right?

<Answer id="p4-gr" label="The star of part (b)" choices={["lies inside its Schwarzschild radius, so it is a black hole", "is about 2.7 times its Schwarzschild radius, so relativity matters", "is a thousand times its Schwarzschild radius, so Newton's gravity is exact", "is so dense that its neutrons must move faster than light"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A body is close to forming a black hole when its radius is a few Schwarzschild radii.

<Step title="The numbers">

<Part label="d" points={3}>

A free neutron decays into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino. Inside the star the antineutrinos escape (our assumption), so their chemical potential is zero. Show that in equilibrium

<Formula label="Equilibrium of neutrons, protons and electrons" tex="\mu_n = \mu_p + \mu_e" cite="reading3" at="p. 34 (the condition Σνᵢμᵢ = 0, applied by us to this reaction)" />

and that the star must hold as many electrons as protons.

<Hint>

Write the decay as a reaction with $\sum_i\nu_iX_i$, as in the notes' Chapter IV (reading 3, p. 34).

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A reaction that can run both ways is in equilibrium when the free energy no longer changes as it runs.

<Step title="The reaction">

For the reaction proton + electron − neutron = 0 (the antineutrino leaves and carries no chemical potential), the notes' condition $\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i = 0$ (reading 3, p. 34) gives $\mu_p + \mu_e - \mu_n = 0$. Each chemical potential includes the particle's rest energy.

</Step>

<Step title="Charge">

A star with a net charge would push its own charge out with a force far larger than gravity, so $n_p = n_e$: the protons and electrons have the same density, and as both have spin ½, the same Fermi wave number $k_p$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={7}>

(Our numbers.) Take a neutron density $n_n = 0.16$ fm⁻³ (our value), the electrons relativistic with $\epsilon = \sqrt{(\hbar ck)^2 + (m_ec^2)^2}$, the neutrons and protons nonrelativistic with $\mu = mc^2 + \hbar^2k^2/2m$, and zero temperature. Use $m_nc^2 = 939.565$ MeV, $m_pc^2 = 938.272$ MeV, $m_ec^2 = 0.511$ MeV and $\hbar c = 197.327$ MeV fm (our values, CODATA 2018). Find the ratio $n_p/n_n$ of protons to neutrons (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

First find the neutrons' Fermi wave number and their $\mu_n$. Then solve $\mu_p(k_p) + \mu_e(k_p) = \mu_n$ for $k_p$; it is a quadratic equation if you treat the electrons as ultrarelativistic, $\mu_e \approx \hbar ck_p$, and you may check afterwards that this is accurate enough.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The ratio of densities is $(k_p/k_n)^3$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-x" label="$n_p/n_n$:" value="0.00535" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-x" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A neutron turns into a proton and an electron only if that lowers the energy. The electrons, being light, have a high Fermi energy at a small density, and that blocks the decay after very few protons have formed.

<Step title="The neutrons">

$k_n = (3\pi^2 \times 0.16)^{1/3}$ fm⁻¹ $= 1.6795$ fm⁻¹, and $\hbar^2k_n^2/2m_n = (197.327 \times 1.6795)^2/(2 \times 939.565)$ MeV $= 58.45$ MeV. So $\mu_n - m_pc^2 = 1.293 + 58.45 = 59.74$ MeV.

</Step>

<Step title="The protons and electrons">

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

$n_p/n_n = (k_p/k_n)^3 = (0.2937/1.6795)^3 = 0.00535$: about one proton (and one electron) for every 190 neutrons.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Real neutron-star matter is shaped also by the nuclear forces, which this model leaves out.

</Step>
