The notation is that of the lecture notes. $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature, $\beta = 1/k_BT$, $m$ the mass of a particle, $n$ the number of particles per unit volume, $P$ the pressure, $\sigma$ the scattering cross-section and $\lambda = 1/(\sigma n)$ the mean free path. ## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A gas effusing from a vessel (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

A vessel of volume $V$ holds a gas of atoms of mass $m$ at temperature $T$. A small hole of area $A$ in its wall opens onto a vacuum. We take the pressure low enough that the mean free path is much larger than the hole, so the gas effuses through it, and the walls keep the gas at the temperature $T$ while it leaks out (our assumptions). The notes give the flux of effusion, the number of atoms that pass through a unit area of the hole per second:

<Formula label="Flux of effusion" tex="I = n\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{2\pi m}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 94" />

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Show that the number $N$ of atoms in the vessel falls exponentially with time, and find the time $t_e$ in which it falls by a factor $e$.

<Hint>

The density in the vessel is $n = N/V$, and atoms leave through the area $A$. Write $dN/dt$ in terms of $N$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-te" label="$t_e =$" expr="V/A*sqrt(2*pi*m/(kB*T))"
  vars={{ V: [0.5, 2], A: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 2], T: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ V: "V", A: "A", m: "m", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "V/A*sqrt(m/(kB*T))", hint: "That takes the flux as the density times a typical speed √(k_BT/m). The exact flux has the extra factor 1/√(2π): only atoms moving toward the hole cross it, at their component v_x." },
    { expr: "V/A*sqrt(m/(3*kB*T))", hint: "That takes the flux as n times the root-mean-square speed √(3k_BT/m), as if every atom headed straight for the hole at that speed. Only the component v_x > 0 carries atoms through." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The number leaving per second is the flux times the area, and the flux is proportional to the density, so the loss rate is proportional to $N$.

<Step title="The rate equation">

$\dfrac{dN}{dt} = -IA = -\dfrac{N}{V}\,A\sqrt{\dfrac{k_BT}{2\pi m}}$. With $T$ fixed, the right side is a constant times $N$, so $N(t) = N(0)\,e^{-t/t_e}$, and the pressure $P = Nk_BT/V$ falls in the same way.

</Step>

<Step title="The time constant">

$t_e = \dfrac{V}{A}\sqrt{\dfrac{2\pi m}{k_BT}}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$V/A$ is a length and $\sqrt{m/k_BT}$ is an inverse speed, so $t_e$ is a time. A bigger vessel or a smaller hole empties more slowly, and so does a heavier gas, as $\sqrt m$. The result does not depend on the pressure, as long as the mean free path stays larger than the hole.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

The vessel holds 2.0 litres of neon (atomic mass 20.180 u) at 300 K, and the hole is a circle 50 μm across (our values). Find $t_e$ in hours.

<Hint>

The area is $\pi(25\ \mu\text{m})^2$. Work in SI units: $1\ \text{u} = 1.66054 \times 10^{-27}$ kg.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-num" label="$t_e$ in hours:" value="2.02" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into the result of part (a).

<Step title="The pieces">

$A = \pi(25 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{m})^2 = 1.963 \times 10^{-9}$ m², so $V/A = 2.0 \times 10^{-3}/1.963 \times 10^{-9} = 1.019 \times 10^{6}$ m. With $m = 20.180 \times 1.66054 \times 10^{-27} = 3.351 \times 10^{-26}$ kg and $k_BT = 4.142 \times 10^{-21}$ J, $\sqrt{2\pi m/k_BT} = 7.130 \times 10^{-3}$ s/m.

</Step>

<Step title="The time">

$t_e = 1.019 \times 10^6 \times 7.130 \times 10^{-3}$ s $= 7.26 \times 10^3$ s, about 2.02 hours.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A hole of this size lets out about $1/7260$ of the gas per second. For the hole to be smaller than the mean free path, the neon must be at a low pressure, about 10 Pa or less: there the mean free path is about 1.7 mm (our estimate, with the hard-ball cross-section of the notes and a neon radius of about 0.14 nm).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Find the mean kinetic energy $\epsilon_\text{out}$ that one escaping atom carries away.

<Hint>

The escaping atoms are not a fair sample of the gas: an atom crosses the hole at a rate proportional to its $v_x$. Average the energy with the weight $v_x f(\vec p)$ over $v_x > 0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Split the energy into the parts $p_x^2/2m$, $p_y^2/2m$ and $p_z^2/2m$. The weight $v_x$ does not touch the $y$ and $z$ motion.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e" label="$\epsilon_\text{out} =$" expr="2*kB*T"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 2], T: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3/2*kB*T", hint: "That is the mean kinetic energy of an atom inside the vessel. Fast atoms reach the hole more often than slow ones, so the atoms that escape carry more." },
    { expr: "kB*T", hint: "That is only the energy of the motion along x, toward the hole, averaged with the weight v_x. The motion along y and z adds k_BT/2 each, unweighted." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Weight each atom by how often it crosses the hole, which is proportional to $v_x$.

<Step title="The weighted average">

With the Boltzmann distribution $f \propto e^{-\beta p^2/2m}$ of the notes (reading 1, p. 94), $\epsilon_\text{out} = \dfrac{\int_{v_x > 0} v_x\,\epsilon\,f\,d^3p}{\int_{v_x > 0} v_x\,f\,d^3p}$, with $\epsilon = (p_x^2 + p_y^2 + p_z^2)/2m$.

</Step>

<Step title="The motion along the hole">

For the $y$ and $z$ parts the weight $v_x$ cancels between top and bottom, and each gives its ordinary mean $\frac12k_BT$. For the $x$ part, with $w = \beta p_x^2/2m$ and $p_x\,dp_x \propto dw$: the mean of $p_x^2/2m$ over the escaping atoms is $k_BT\,\dfrac{\int_0^\infty w\,e^{-w}\,dw}{\int_0^\infty e^{-w}\,dw} = k_BT$.

</Step>

<Step title="The total">

$\epsilon_\text{out} = k_BT + \frac12k_BT + \frac12k_BT = 2k_BT$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$2k_BT$ is more than the mean $\frac32k_BT$ inside, as it must be for a sample biased toward fast atoms. The energy leaves at the rate $2k_BT$ times the rate at which atoms leave, so the walls must supply heat to keep $T$ fixed, as our assumption says they do.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Now let the vessel be thermally insulated instead, so that no heat reaches the gas (our variant). As the gas effuses, the temperature of the gas left in the vessel

<Answer id="p1-cool" label="The temperature of the gas left behind" choices={["rises, because the atoms left behind gain the room of those that leave", "falls, because each escaping atom takes away more than the mean energy", "stays the same, because the hole does not touch the atoms left behind", "stays the same, because an ideal gas expanding into vacuum keeps its temperature"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compare the energy that leaves per atom with the mean energy per atom that stays.

<Step title="The energy balance">

The gas inside has energy $E = \frac32Nk_BT$. Each atom that leaves takes $2k_BT$ (part (c)), more than the mean $\frac32k_BT$, so $E/N$ falls with every escape: $\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{E}{N}\right) = \frac{1}{N}\frac{dN}{dt}\left(2k_BT - \frac32k_BT\right) < 0$, since $dN/dt < 0$. The gas left behind cools.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

An ideal gas that expands freely into a vacuum keeps its temperature only when the whole gas, inside and outside, shares its energy again. Here the escaped atoms never come back, and the fast ones have gone.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={5}>

Effusion sorts atoms by mass, which was once used to enrich uranium. Uranium hexafluoride gas holds ²³⁵UF₆ molecules of mass 349.034 u and ²³⁸UF₆ molecules of mass 352.041 u (added up by us from the atomic masses of the IAEA's Atomic Mass Evaluation 2020). Take each stage to let through only a small part of the gas it receives, so that the gas passed on has the ratio of ²³⁵UF₆ to ²³⁸UF₆ of the two effusion fluxes (our idealization), and each stage feeds the next. About how many stages raise the share of ²³⁵U from 0.72% (natural uranium) to 3.0% (a typical reactor fuel)? (Our values.)

<Hint>

From the flux of effusion, the ratio of the two fluxes at the same temperature is the ratio of the densities times a factor $\alpha$ that depends only on the two masses.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The stages multiply the ratio of ²³⁵U to ²³⁸U, not the share of ²³⁵U in all the uranium. Turn both shares into ratios first.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-stages" label="Number of stages (about how many):" value="338" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-stages" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each stage multiplies the ratio of the isotopes by the same factor, so the number of stages is a ratio of logarithms.

<Step title="The factor of one stage">

$I \propto n/\sqrt m$ at fixed $T$, so in the gas passed on the ratio of ²³⁵U to ²³⁸U is larger by $\alpha = \sqrt{\dfrac{352.041}{349.034}} = 1.004298$.

</Step>

<Step title="The two ratios">

At the start, the ratio of ²³⁵U to ²³⁸U is $0.0072/0.9928 = 0.007252$; at the end, $0.030/0.970 = 0.030928$. The ratio must grow by a factor $4.2646$.

</Step>

<Step title="The number of stages">

Each stage multiplies the ratio by $\alpha$, so the number of stages is $\ln 4.2646/\ln 1.004298 = 1.45035/0.0042892 = 338$: about 338 stages (338 give 2.998%, which rounds to 3.0%).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Each stage gains only 0.43%, because the two molecules differ in mass by less than 1% and the flux goes as the square root of the mass. Working with the shares instead of the ratios gives $\ln(3.0/0.72)/\ln\alpha = 333$, a few stages short. Our idealized stage passes only a trickle of gas; a real stage passes about half of it, gains less, and a real plant needs many more stages.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Sound in a gas of molecules that rotate (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

The notes find the speed of sound in a monatomic gas from the adiabatic condition, the statement that a piece of the flowing gas keeps its entropy per particle (reading 1, p. 96):

<Formula label="Speed of sound" tex="c_s = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P_0}{\rho_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma k_BT}{m}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 97" />

with $\gamma = 5/3$ for a monatomic gas, and $P_0$ and $\rho_0$ the pressure and the mass density of the gas at rest (the notes write $c$ for the speed; we write $c_s$). For a gas of molecules whose heat capacity per molecule at constant volume is $c_v$, the entropy per molecule is $s = c_v\ln T - k_B\ln n + \text{constant}$ (a standard result for an ideal gas, stated by us; for $c_v = \frac32k_B$ it is the notes' entropy, reading 1, p. 96). Everything in the derivation of the sound wave stays the same except the value of $\gamma$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the exponent $\gamma$ for which constant $s$ means constant $P\rho^{-\gamma}$, in terms of $c_v$ and $k_B$.

<Hint>

Constant $s$ means constant $T^{c_v/k_B}/n$. Use $P = nk_BT$ to replace $T$ by $P/n$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-gamma" label="$\gamma =$" expr="1 + kB/cv"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 2], cv: [1, 4] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", cv: "c_v" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1 + cv/kB", hint: "The ratio is upside down: for a monatomic gas, c_v = (3/2)k_B, this gives 5/2 instead of Wen's 5/3." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write the conserved combination in terms of $P$ and $n$ and read off the power.

<Step title="Eliminating the temperature">

$s/k_B = (c_v/k_B)\ln T - \ln n + \text{constant}$, so $T^{c_v/k_B}n^{-1}$ is conserved. With $T = P/(nk_B)$ this is $P^{c_v/k_B}n^{-c_v/k_B - 1}$, up to a constant.

</Step>

<Step title="The exponent">

Raise it to the power $k_B/c_v$: $P\,n^{-(1 + k_B/c_v)}$ is conserved, and since $\rho = mn$, so is $P\rho^{-\gamma}$ with $\gamma = 1 + \dfrac{k_B}{c_v}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $c_v = \frac32k_B$, $\gamma = 1 + \frac23 = \frac53$, the notes' value. Since the heat capacity per molecule at constant pressure of an ideal gas is $c_v + k_B$, $\gamma$ is also the ratio of the two heat capacities, its usual definition.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

At room temperature a nitrogen molecule rotates but does not vibrate, so $c_v = \frac52k_B$ (our assumption). Find the ratio of the speed of sound $c_s$ to the root-mean-square speed $\sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle} = \sqrt{3k_BT/m}$ of the molecules, as a decimal.

<Answer id="p2-ratio" label="$c_s/\sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle}$:" value="0.683" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both speeds are $\sqrt{k_BT/m}$ times a number, so their ratio depends on $\gamma$ only.

<Step title="The ratio">

$\gamma = 1 + \frac25 = \frac75$ from part (a), so $c_s/\sqrt{\langle v^2\rangle} = \sqrt{\gamma/3} = \sqrt{7/15} = 0.683$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For a monatomic gas the ratio is $\sqrt{5/9} = 0.745$: sound is always a little slower than the molecules, of the same order, as the notes say (reading 1, p. 97). Molecules that rotate store part of the energy of a compression in their rotation, so the pressure rises less and sound is slower.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Hydrogen molecules (mass 2.016 u) also have $c_v = \frac52k_B$ at room temperature (our assumption). Find the speed of sound in hydrogen gas at 300 K, in m/s.

<Answer id="p2-h2" label="$c_s$ in hydrogen, in m/s:" value="1316" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-h2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put $\gamma = 7/5$ and the mass of the molecule into the notes' formula.

<Step title="The number">

$m = 2.016 \times 1.66054 \times 10^{-27} = 3.348 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, so $c_s = \sqrt{1.4 \times 4.142 \times 10^{-21}/3.348 \times 10^{-27}}$ m/s $= 1316$ m/s.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Nitrogen (28.014 u), about 14 times heavier, gives about $\sqrt{14}$ times less, 353 m/s. The speed does not depend on the pressure, only on $T$ and the kind of molecule.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

An organ pipe is tuned in a church at 15 °C and played at 25 °C (our values). The air in the pipe has the temperature of the church, and the wavelength of each note is fixed by the length of the pipe. By how many percent is its pitch higher at 25 °C?

<Hint>

At a fixed wavelength the frequency is proportional to $c_s$. Use the absolute temperature.

<Answer id="p2-pipe" scale="0.01" label="Rise in pitch, in percent:" value="1.72" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-pipe" />

<Step title="The ratio">

The frequency grows by the factor $\sqrt{298.15/288.15} = 1.01720$: the pitch is 1.72% higher.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

In musical terms that is about 30 cents, almost a third of a semitone, which a trained ear hears. For a small change, the relative change of the pitch is half that of the temperature: $10/(2 \times 288.15) = 0.0174$, close to the exact value.

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Diffusion and mobility from the Boltzmann equation (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 3">

A dilute classical gas of particles of mass $m$ is at a uniform temperature $T$. The notes describe it by the occupation $g(\vec r, \vec k)$ of the levels $\epsilon_k = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, with velocity $\vec v = \hbar\vec k/m$, and in the relaxation-time approximation the steady, linearized solution of the Boltzmann equation is

<Formula label="Linearized solution of the Boltzmann equation" tex="\delta g = -\tau\vec v_k\cdot\frac{\partial g_0}{\partial\vec r} - \frac{\tau}{\hbar}\vec F\cdot\frac{\partial g_0}{\partial\vec k}" cite="reading1" at="p. 109" />

with the classical equilibrium occupation

<Formula label="Equilibrium occupation of a classical gas" tex="g_0 = e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 108" />

The density and the particle current are $n = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,g$ and $j_x = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,v_x\,g$ (one level for each $\vec k$: we leave out spin, our choice, which changes nothing below). Take $\tau$ the same for every level (our assumption).

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Let the density vary slowly along $x$, through a chemical potential $\mu(x)$, with no force. Show that the current has the form $j_x = -D\,\partial n/\partial x$ and find $D$.

<Hint>

$g_0$ depends on $x$ only through $\mu(x)$. Compare $\partial g_0/\partial x$ with $\partial n/\partial x$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$g_0$ itself carries no current, since it is even in $\vec k$. The current of $\delta g$ is an average of $v_x^2$ over the Boltzmann distribution, which equipartition gives.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-d" label="$D =$" expr="tau*kB*T/m"
  vars={{ tau: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 2], T: [1, 3], m: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ tau: "\\tau", kB: "k_B", T: "T", m: "m" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*tau*kB*T/m", hint: "That uses the mean square of the whole speed, ⟨v²⟩ = 3k_BT/m. The current along x picks out v_x², whose mean is a third of it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The density gradient shifts the equilibrium occupation; $\delta g$ is odd in $v_x$ and carries the current.

<Step title="The gradients">

$\partial g_0/\partial x = \beta g_0\,\partial\mu/\partial x$, and so $\partial n/\partial x = \beta n\,\partial\mu/\partial x$. Then $\delta g = -\tau v_x\,\beta g_0\,\partial\mu/\partial x$.

</Step>

<Step title="The current">

$j_x = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,v_x\,\delta g = -\tau\beta\,\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial x}\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,v_x^2\,g_0 = -\tau\beta\,\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial x}\,n\langle v_x^2\rangle$. With $\beta n\,\partial\mu/\partial x = \partial n/\partial x$ and $\langle v_x^2\rangle = k_BT/m$, $j_x = -\dfrac{\tau k_BT}{m}\,\dfrac{\partial n}{\partial x}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The diffusion constant">

$D = \dfrac{\tau k_BT}{m}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$k_BT/m$ is a speed squared and $\tau$ a time, so $D$ is in m²/s, as it should be. The current flows down the gradient, from high to low density.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Now let the density be uniform, and let each particle feel a weak constant force $F$ along $x$. The gas then drifts with a mean velocity $u = j_x/n = \eta F$. Find the mobility $\eta$.

<Hint>

$\partial g_0/\partial k_x = -\beta\,(\partial\epsilon_k/\partial k_x)\,g_0$, and $\partial\epsilon_k/\partial k_x = \hbar v_x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-eta" label="$\eta =$" expr="tau/m"
  vars={{ tau: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ tau: "\\tau", m: "m" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m/tau", hint: "That is the friction coefficient: a drift u costs a drag force (m/τ)u. The mobility is the drift per unit force, its inverse." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The force term of $\delta g$ tilts the distribution toward positive $v_x$.

<Step title="The deviation">

$\partial g_0/\partial k_x = -\beta\hbar v_x\,g_0$, so $\delta g = -\dfrac{\tau}{\hbar}F\,(-\beta\hbar v_x\,g_0) = \tau\beta F\,v_x\,g_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The drift">

$j_x = \tau\beta F\,n\langle v_x^2\rangle = \tau\beta F\,n\,\dfrac{k_BT}{m} = \dfrac{\tau F}{m}\,n$, so $u = \dfrac{\tau}{m}F$ and $\eta = \dfrac{\tau}{m}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

This is the drift of the Drude model, $d\vec u/dt = -\vec u/\tau + \vec F/m$ in a steady state (reading 1, p. 106, written there for the force $e\vec E$ on an electron). The temperature has dropped out.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Let the gas sit in a potential energy $U(x)$, so $F = -dU/dx$, with both a density gradient and the force acting. Show that the current vanishes only if $n \propto e^{-U/k_BT}$, and that the results of (a) and (b) satisfy the Einstein relation of the notes,

<Formula label="Einstein relation" tex="D = \eta k_BT" cite="reading1" at="p. 115" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the linearized solution the two terms of $\delta g$ add, so the two currents add.

<Step title="The total current">

$j_x = -D\,\dfrac{\partial n}{\partial x} + \eta Fn = -\dfrac{\tau k_BT}{m}\dfrac{\partial n}{\partial x} - \dfrac{\tau}{m}\dfrac{dU}{dx}\,n$.

</Step>

<Step title="Equilibrium">

$j_x = 0$ means $\dfrac{1}{n}\dfrac{\partial n}{\partial x} = -\dfrac{1}{k_BT}\dfrac{dU}{dx}$, so $n \propto e^{-U/k_BT}$: the Boltzmann distribution in the potential, as it must be.

</Step>

<Step title="The Einstein relation">

$D/\eta = (\tau k_BT/m)/(\tau/m) = k_BT$. The relaxation time and the mass cancel.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The notes reach the Einstein relation the other way round: they demand the Boltzmann distribution and deduce $D = \eta k_BT$ (reading 1, p. 115). Here both $D$ and $\eta$ come from one equation, and the relation holds whatever $\tau$ is, because the same collisions cause both the diffusion and the friction.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

The notes estimate the diffusion constant as $D \simeq \lambda\bar v/6$ (reading 1, p. 98), with $\bar v = \sqrt{2k_BT/m}$ as in their formulas for viscosity and heat conduction (reading 1, pp. 102 and 104). Take the mean free path to be $\lambda = \bar v\tau$ (our identification). By what factor is $D$ of part (a) larger than the notes' estimate? Give a decimal.

<Answer id="p3-ratio" label="$D$ of part (a) divided by the notes' estimate:" value="3" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-ratio" mistakes={[{ value: "2.36", tolerance: "1%", calc: "p3-meanspeed", hint: "That uses the mean speed √(8k_BT/πm). The question takes the notes' v̄ = √(2k_BT/m), also in λ = v̄τ." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write both in terms of $\tau$ and $k_BT/m$.

<Step title="The estimate">

$\lambda\bar v/6 = \bar v^2\tau/6 = (2k_BT/m)\,\tau/6 = \tau k_BT/3m$.

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

$D$ of part (a) divided by the estimate is $(\tau k_BT/m)/(\tau k_BT/3m) = 3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Both are rough: the notes' six-direction picture and our single relaxation time are models of the collisions, and they agree on the form $D \propto \tau k_BT/m$, not on the number in front. With the mean speed $\sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m}$ in place of $\bar v$ the factor would be $3\pi/4 = 2.36$. The order of magnitude is what such estimates are for.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Krypton in a double-glazed window (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 4">

A double-glazed window has two panes $d = 12$ mm apart, and the gap is filled with krypton at 300 K and 101 325 Pa (our values; we take one temperature for the whole gas, although the panes differ a little). Krypton atoms have mass 83.798 u; treat them as hard balls of radius $r = 0.20$ nm (our value). Heat crosses the gap by conduction only: we ignore convection and radiation (our assumption). As MIT's sheet says of its Problem 4, you need to estimate the mean free path first. The notes give

<Formula label="Mean free path and cross-section" tex="\lambda = \frac{1}{\sigma n}, \qquad \sigma = \pi(2r)^2 = 4\pi r^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 93" />

<Formula label="Heat conductivity of a gas" tex="K = \frac{1}{6} n\bar v\lambda c_v = \frac{c_v}{6\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 104" />

(The notes' box on p. 104 writes $3\sigma$; with $K = \frac16 n\bar v\lambda c_v$ and $n\lambda = 1/\sigma$ it is $6\sigma$, as the lesson notes.)

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the mean free path of the krypton atoms, in nm.

<Hint>

First find the number density from $P = nk_BT$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-lam" label="$\lambda$ in nm:" value="81.3" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-lam" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Density from the ideal-gas law, cross-section from the radius.

<Step title="Density and cross-section">

$n = P/k_BT = 101\,325/4.142 \times 10^{-21} = 2.446 \times 10^{25}$ m⁻³, and $\sigma = 4\pi(0.20 \times 10^{-9})^2 = 5.027 \times 10^{-19}$ m².

</Step>

<Step title="The mean free path">

$\lambda = 1/(\sigma n) = 1/(5.027 \times 10^{-19} \times 2.446 \times 10^{25})$ m $= 8.13 \times 10^{-8}$ m $= 81.3$ nm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The spacing between atoms is $n^{-1/3} = 3.44$ nm, so an atom passes about 24 neighbours' spacings between collisions, as in a dilute gas it should. The gap is some $1.5 \times 10^5$ mean free paths wide, so the gas in it is far into the hydrodynamic limit, and Fourier's law applies.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the heat conductivity $K$ of the krypton from the notes' formula, with $c_v = \frac32k_B$, in mW/(m K).

<Answer id="p4-k" label="$K$ in mW/(m K):" value="1.68" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-k" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into $K = \frac16 n\bar v\lambda c_v$.

<Step title="The speed">

$m = 83.798 \times 1.66054 \times 10^{-27} = 1.3915 \times 10^{-25}$ kg, so $\bar v = \sqrt{2k_BT/m} = 244$ m/s.

</Step>

<Step title="The conductivity">

$n\lambda = 1/\sigma$, so $K = \dfrac{c_v\bar v}{6\sigma} = \dfrac{1.5 \times 1.3806 \times 10^{-23} \times 244}{6 \times 5.027 \times 10^{-19}}$ W/(m K) $= 1.68 \times 10^{-3}$ W/(m K), that is 1.68 mW/(m K).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The units: J/K times m/s over m² is W/(m K). The estimate is rough: the lesson found the simple formula low by a factor of about 2.4 for the viscosity of argon, and the radius 0.20 nm is only our value. The heavy, large krypton atom is a poor carrier of heat, which is why it is used in windows.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

With the panes 20 K apart (our value), find the heat flux through the gap, in W/m², from your $K$.

<Answer id="p4-q" label="$q$ in W/m²:" value="2.79" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-q" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Fourier's law across a uniform gap: $q = K\Delta T/d$.

<Step title="The flux">

$q = 1.675 \times 10^{-3} \times 20/0.012$ W/m² $= 2.79$ W/m².

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A square metre of window loses about 2.8 W through the gas alone in this model. In a real window convection and radiation add to it, which our assumption leaves out.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

$K$ does not depend on the pressure, as long as the mean free path is much shorter than the gap. Below which pressure of krypton at 300 K does the mean free path become longer than $d = 12$ mm? Give it in Pa.

<Hint>

Write $\lambda$ in terms of $P$ and solve $\lambda = d$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-p" label="Pressure in Pa:" value="0.687" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-p" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $\lambda = k_BT/(\sigma P)$ grows as the pressure falls.

<Step title="The pressure">

$\lambda = d$ at $P = \dfrac{k_BT}{\sigma d} = \dfrac{4.142 \times 10^{-21}}{5.027 \times 10^{-19} \times 0.012}$ Pa $= 0.687$ Pa.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

That is $1.5 \times 10^5$ times below atmospheric pressure, the same factor as the gap is wider than $\lambda$ in part (a), because $\lambda \propto 1/P$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

As the pressure is lowered from 101 325 Pa to well above the pressure of part (d), the conductivity of the gas in the gap

<Answer id="p4-trend" label="The conductivity" choices={["falls in proportion to the pressure, because there are fewer atoms to carry heat", "grows as the pressure falls, because each atom flies farther between collisions", "stays about the same, because fewer atoms each carry heat a longer way", "falls as the square root of the pressure, like the effusion flux"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In $K = \frac16 n\bar v\lambda c_v$ the density enters twice, once directly and once through $\lambda = 1/\sigma n$.

<Step title="The cancellation">

$n\lambda = 1/\sigma$ does not depend on the pressure: half the atoms each carry their energy twice as far. So $K$ stays the same, as the notes point out for the viscosity and the conductivity (reading 1, pp. 102 and 104).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Once $\lambda$ exceeds the gap (below about 0.7 Pa), an atom flies from pane to pane without a collision; the distance it carries heat is then fixed at $d$, and the heat flux falls in proportion to the number of atoms, that is, to the pressure. That is why the wall of a vacuum flask must be pumped to a pressure well below this, not merely to a "low" pressure.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Light escaping from the Sun (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 5">

Light made in the Sun does not fly straight out: it is scattered again and again, and works its way out by a random walk. Our model (our assumptions throughout): the Sun is a ball of radius $R = 6.957 \times 10^8$ m and mass $M_\odot = 1.988 \times 10^{30}$ kg (the IAU's nominal GM⊙ divided by G) with uniform density, made of fully ionized hydrogen, so that there is one free electron for every proton mass. A photon scatters only off free electrons, with the Thomson cross-section $\sigma_T = 6.652 \times 10^{-29}$ m² (CODATA 2018), flies at the speed of light $c$ between scatterings, and leaves each scattering in a random direction. The notes treat a random walk of steps of length $\lambda$ (reading 1, p. 100):

<Formula label="Random walk" tex="\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle = \Delta\vec x_1^{\,2} + \Delta\vec x_2^{\,2} + \cdots = N\lambda^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 100" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the mean free path of a photon in this model, in cm.

<Answer id="p5-lam" label="$\lambda$ in cm:" value="1.78" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-lam" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The mean density gives the number of electrons per volume, and $\lambda = 1/(\sigma_Tn_e)$.

<Step title="The electrons">

$\bar\rho = M_\odot/(\frac43\pi R^3) = 1410$ kg/m³, so $n_e = \bar\rho/m_p = 1410/1.6726 \times 10^{-27} = 8.43 \times 10^{29}$ m⁻³.

</Step>

<Step title="The mean free path">

$\lambda = 1/(6.652 \times 10^{-29} \times 8.43 \times 10^{29})$ m $= 0.0178$ m, about 1.78 cm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$\sigma_Tn_e \approx 56$ per metre: a photon meets an electron every couple of centimetres, so the Sun is utterly opaque.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the time $t$ it takes a photon, starting at the centre, to random-walk a distance $R$, in terms of $R$, $\lambda$ and $c$.

<Hint>

How many steps of length $\lambda$ make $\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle = R^2$? Each step takes the time $\lambda/c$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-t" label="$t =$" expr="R^2/(lam*c)"
  vars={{ R: [1, 3], lam: [0.1, 1], c: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ R: "R", lam: "\\lambda", c: "c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*R^2/(lam*c)", hint: "That uses the one-dimensional spreading ⟨x²⟩ = 2Dt with the three-dimensional D = λc/6. The distance from the centre spreads in all three directions: ⟨x²⟩ = 6Dt." },
    { expr: "R/c", hint: "That is the time of a straight flight out. Between scatterings the photon moves only λ in a random direction, and the steps partly undo each other." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The distance grows as the square root of the number of steps.

<Step title="The number of steps">

$N\lambda^2 = R^2$ gives $N = (R/\lambda)^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The time">

Each step takes $\lambda/c$, so $t = N\lambda/c = \dfrac{R^2}{\lambda c}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The same follows from diffusion: with $D = \lambda c/6$ in place of $\lambda\bar v/6$ (reading 1, p. 98) and $\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle = 6Dt$ (reading 1, p. 100), $R^2 = \lambda ct$. Halving the mean free path doubles the time.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find this time in years (one year = 365.25 days).

<Answer id="p5-yr" label="$t$ in years:" value="2870" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-yr" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers of part (a) into part (b).

<Step title="The time">

$t = (6.957 \times 10^8)^2/(0.01783 \times 2.998 \times 10^8)$ s $= 9.05 \times 10^{10}$ s. One year is $3.156 \times 10^7$ s, so $t \approx 2870$ years.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The number of steps is $(R/\lambda)^2 = 1.5 \times 10^{21}$. In the real Sun the centre is far denser than the mean, and light is also absorbed and re-emitted by ions and by electrons passing close to them, which adds to the scattering; so the mean free path is shorter and the escape slower than in our model. The random-walk law $t = R^2/\lambda c$ is the part that carries over.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

How many times longer is $t$ than the time $R/c$ of a straight flight out of the Sun? Give the factor in units of $10^{10}$.

<Answer id="p5-factor" scale="1e10" label="$t/(R/c)$ in units of $10^{10}$:" value="3.90" tolerance="2%" calc="p5-factor" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Divide the results of parts (b) and (c).

<Step title="The factor">

$t/(R/c) = R/\lambda = 6.957 \times 10^8/0.01783 = 3.90 \times 10^{10}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The straight flight takes $R/c = 2.32$ s; $2.32 \times 3.90 \times 10^{10}$ s is $9.05 \times 10^{10}$ s, the time of part (c).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The same random walk before decoupling">
Before decoupling, the photons of the early universe random-walked in just this way between scatterings off free electrons. In a time $t$ a photon wanders a distance of about $\sqrt{\lambda ct}$, and on scales smaller than that the photons smear out the hot and cold patches of the plasma: this is the Silk damping of [MIT 8.902 Lecture 17](/courses/8-902/cmb-anisotropies). Once the electrons combined into atoms, the mean free path grew longer than the distance light could travel in the age of the universe, and the light has flown freely ever since: that is decoupling, dated in [MIT 8.286 Lecture 17](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling).
</Callout>
