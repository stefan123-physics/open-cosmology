The notation is that of the lecture notes. $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature, $\beta = 1/k_BT$, $\mu$ the chemical potential and $n_F(\epsilon) = 1/(e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)} + 1)$ the Fermi–Dirac occupation of a level of energy $\epsilon$. $N$ is a number of particles, $\epsilon_F$ the Fermi energy and $T_F = \epsilon_F/k_B$ the Fermi temperature. In Problems 1 and 3, $D(\epsilon)$ is the density of states of **one** spin state, as in the notes (reading 1, p. 82); in Problem 2 the heat capacity formula needs $D$ counting both spin states, and the statement says so. In Problem 1, $\mu_B$ is the Bohr magneton, $B$ the magnetic field, $N_\uparrow$ and $N_\downarrow$ the numbers of electrons whose moment points along and against the field, $M$ the magnetic moment and $\chi = M/B$ the susceptibility. In Problem 2, $n$ is the number of atoms (and of conduction electrons) per unit volume, $T_D$ the Debye temperature and $C$ a heat capacity. In Problem 3, $\omega$ is the angular frequency of the trap, $m$ the mass of an atom and $E_0$ the energy at zero temperature. In Problem 4, $N_D$ and $N_A$ are the densities of donors and acceptors, $n_e$ and $n_h$ those of electrons and holes, $n_i$ the density of each in the pure crystal, $\epsilon_B$ the barrier, $V$ the voltage and $I$ the current.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Electron spins in a magnetic field, at any temperature and any field (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the spin susceptibility of a metal (reading 1, p. 85). The notes find it at zero temperature in a weak field; here you follow it to high temperature and to a strong field.

Take $N$ free electrons in a volume $V$. Each carries a magnetic moment of size $\mu_B$, along or against a field $B$, so its energy is $\epsilon_k \mp \mu_BB$ with $\epsilon_k = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, and the moment of the gas is $M = \mu_B(N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow)$ (reading 1, p. 85). Only the spin moments count (our assumption: the orbital motion of the electrons in the field, which gives a smaller diamagnetism, is left out). $D(\epsilon) \propto \epsilon^{1/2}$ is the density of states of one spin band at $B = 0$, and $\epsilon_F$ the Fermi energy at $B = 0$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Show that, at any temperature, in a weak field the susceptibility is

<Formula label="Spin susceptibility at any temperature" tex="\chi = \frac MB = 2\mu_B^2\int_0^\infty D(\epsilon)\left(-\frac{\partial n_F}{\partial\epsilon}\right)d\epsilon" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

with $n_F$ taken at $B = 0$, and that at $T = 0$ it is the notes' $\chi = 2\mu_B^2D(\epsilon_F)$.

<Hint>

Each spin band is a set of levels shifted by $\mp\mu_BB$: $N_\uparrow = \int D(\epsilon)\,n_F(\epsilon - \mu_BB)\,d\epsilon$, and the same with $+\mu_BB$ for $N_\downarrow$. Expand both to first order in $B$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Does $\mu$ change to first order in $B$? Compare $N_\uparrow + N_\downarrow$ with its value at $B = 0$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A weak field moves the two spin bands in opposite directions by the same small amount; the response is the derivative of the occupation.

<Step title="The two bands">

Measured from the bottom of its own band, each band has the levels of $B = 0$, so $N_\uparrow = \int D(\epsilon)\,n_F(\epsilon - \mu_BB)\,d\epsilon$, and $N_\downarrow$ is the same with $+\mu_BB$. To first order, $n_F(\epsilon \mp \mu_BB) = n_F(\epsilon) \mp \mu_BB\,\partial n_F/\partial\epsilon$. The total $N_\uparrow + N_\downarrow$ has no term of first order, so $\mu$, fixed by $N$, moves only at order $B^2$ and may be kept at its value for $B = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The difference">

$N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow = -2\mu_BB\int D\,\partial n_F/\partial\epsilon\,d\epsilon$, and $M = \mu_B(N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow)$ gives the formula. Its weight $-\partial n_F/\partial\epsilon$ is a peak of unit area around $\mu$, of width a few $k_BT$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $T = 0$, $n_F$ is a step at $\epsilon_F$ and $-\partial n_F/\partial\epsilon = \delta(\epsilon - \epsilon_F)$, so $\chi = 2\mu_B^2D(\epsilon_F)$, the notes' result (reading 1, p. 85). As long as $k_BT \ll \epsilon_F$ the peak is narrow and $D$ hardly changes across it, so $\chi$ stays at this value: the Pauli susceptibility does not depend on temperature.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Now take the gas hot and dilute, $k_BT \gg \epsilon_F$, so that it is classical. Find $\chi$ in terms of $N$, $\mu_B$, $k_B$ and $T$.

<Hint>

In a classical gas every occupation is small: $n_F \approx e^{-\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}$. What is $-\partial n_F/\partial\epsilon$ then?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Both spin bands together hold all the electrons: $2\int D\,n_F\,d\epsilon = N$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-curie" label="$\chi$ for $k_BT \gg \epsilon_F$:" expr="N*muB^2/(kB*T)"
  vars={{ N: [1, 100], muB: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ N: "N", muB: "\\mu_B", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*N*muB^2/(kB*T)", hint: "That takes ∫D n_F dε = N, as if each spin band held N electrons. N counts both bands: 2∫D n_F dε = N." },
    { expr: "N*muB^2/(3*kB*T)", hint: "That is the classical result for a moment that can point in any direction, where the average of μ_z² is μ²/3. An electron's spin has only two orientations along B, each with the full component μ_B." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Without the Pauli principle in play, each electron turns its moment freely, and the result is Curie's law.

<Step title="The classical occupation">

With $n_F \approx e^{-\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}$, $-\partial n_F/\partial\epsilon = \beta n_F$. Then part (a) gives $\chi = 2\mu_B^2\beta\int D\,n_F\,d\epsilon = \mu_B^2\beta N$:

<Formula label="Curie's law for free electrons" tex="\chi = \frac{N\mu_B^2}{k_BT}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

This is the Curie law of free moments, $\chi = N\mu_B^2/k_BT$, the comparison the lesson makes. Directly: one electron has $\langle\mu_z\rangle = \mu_B\tanh(\beta\mu_BB) \approx \mu_B^2B/k_BT$. The ratio of the degenerate value $\frac32N\mu_B^2/\epsilon_F$ (the lesson's form of $2\mu_B^2D(\epsilon_F)$) to this one is $\frac32k_BT/\epsilon_F$, small in a metal: only the electrons near the Fermi surface can turn.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Back at $T = 0$, make the field strong. As $B$ grows, electrons move from the raised band to the lowered one, until at some field $B_p$ the raised band is empty and every moment points along the field. Find $B_p$ in terms of $\epsilon_F$ and $\mu_B$.

<Hint>

At $T = 0$ both bands fill to the same $\mu$. The lowered band has its bottom at $-\mu_BB$, the raised one at $+\mu_BB$. The raised band is just empty when $\mu = \mu_BB$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In one band the number of electrons below an energy $x$ above its bottom grows as $x^{3/2}$. At $B = 0$, each band holds $N/2$ up to $\epsilon_F$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-bp" label="$B_p =$" expr="eF/(2^(1/3)*muB)"
  vars={{ eF: [1, 5], muB: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ eF: "\\epsilon_F", muB: "\\mu_B" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "eF/muB", hint: "That keeps μ at ε_F, and asks when the bottom of the raised band reaches it. But μ moves: when the raised band empties, the lowered one holds all N electrons in a single spin state, and its Fermi sea is deeper." },
    { expr: "eF/(2*muB)", hint: "That sets the splitting 2μ_BB equal to ε_F. A single spin band holding all N electrons fills to 2^(2/3)ε_F above its bottom, not to ε_F." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Fully polarized, all $N$ electrons sit in one spin band, which must then be filled twice as full as at $B = 0$.

<Step title="One band, all the electrons">

The number in one band filled to a height $x$ above its bottom is $\frac N2(x/\epsilon_F)^{3/2}$, since at $B = 0$ it is $N/2$ at $x = \epsilon_F$. When the raised band is just empty, $\mu = \mu_BB_p$, and the lowered band, with its bottom at $-\mu_BB_p$, is filled to $x = 2\mu_BB_p$ and holds all $N$:

<Formula label="The field of full polarization" tex="\frac N2\left(\frac{2\mu_BB_p}{\epsilon_F}\right)^{3/2} = N \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mu_BB_p = 2^{-1/3}\,\epsilon_F = 0.794\,\epsilon_F" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The single band holds $N$ electrons in one spin state, so its Fermi energy is $2^{2/3}\epsilon_F$, the value for spinless fermions at the same density ($k_F$ grows by $2^{1/3}$). In a weak field the linear law of part (a), $N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow = 2\mu_BB\,D(\epsilon_F) = \frac32N\mu_BB/\epsilon_F$, would reach $N$ already at $\mu_BB = \frac23\epsilon_F$; the true magnetization bends below the straight line and gets there later, at $0.794\,\epsilon_F$, since $D$ shrinks towards the bottom of the emptying band.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

(Our numbers.) Take $\epsilon_F = 3.24$ eV (our value, close to the free-electron value for sodium) and $\mu_B = 5.788 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/T (CODATA 2018). Give $B_p$ in units of $10^4$ T, as a decimal (tolerance 1%).

<Answer id="p1-bpnum" scale="1e4" label="$B_p$ in $10^4$ T:" value="4.44" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-bpnum" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into part (c).

<Step title="The number">

$B_p = 0.794 \times 3.24\ \text{eV}/(5.788 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{eV/T}) = 4.44 \times 10^4$ T.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

That is more than forty thousand tesla, far beyond any laboratory magnet. In every field one can apply, the spins of a metal respond in the weak-field regime of part (a), and the Pauli susceptibility is all one needs.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Electrons and lattice vibrations in the heat capacity of a metal (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the heat capacity of the degenerate electron gas (reading 1, p. 87), set against that of the lattice vibrations of Chapter VII.

Our model of a simple metal: one conduction electron per atom, $n = 1.40 \times 10^{28}$ atoms (and electrons) per m³, a Debye temperature $T_D = 91$ K (our values, close to those of potassium), and free electrons with the electron mass $m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg and two spin states. Use $\hbar = 1.0546 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and $k_B = 1.3806 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K (CODATA 2018; our values). The electrons have the heat capacity of the notes,

<Formula label="Heat capacity of a degenerate Fermi gas" tex="C_{\text{el}} = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\,k_B^2T\,D(\epsilon_F)" cite="reading1" at="p. 87" />

where here $D$ counts **both** spin states, and far below $T_D$ the lattice has the Debye heat capacity

<Formula label="Debye heat capacity far below the Debye temperature" tex="C_{\text{ph}} = \frac{12\pi^4}{5}\,Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{T_D}\right)^3" cite="reading2" at="p. 54 (the limit T ≪ T_D of the Debye function, written as the lesson writes it)" />

for $N$ atoms.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the Fermi temperature $T_F$ of the conduction electrons, in kelvin (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

With two spin states, $k_F = (3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$ (reading 1, p. 84), and $\epsilon_F = \hbar^2k_F^2/2m_e$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-tf" label="$T_F$ in K:" value="24600" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-tf" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The density fixes the Fermi wave number, and that fixes the Fermi energy.

<Step title="The numbers">

$k_F = (3\pi^2 \times 1.40 \times 10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3})^{1/3} = 7.46 \times 10^9$ m⁻¹, $\epsilon_F = \hbar^2k_F^2/2m_e = 3.39 \times 10^{-19}$ J $= 2.12$ eV, and $T_F = \epsilon_F/k_B = 2.46 \times 10^4$ K.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A Fermi energy of a couple of electronvolts, as the notes quote for metals (reading 1, p. 83); room temperature is less than an eightieth of $T_F$, so the electrons are deeply degenerate.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

At very low temperature the electrons' heat capacity, linear in $T$, wins over the lattice's $T^3$. Find the temperature $T^*$ at which the two are equal, in terms of $T_F$ and $T_D$.

<Hint>

First write $D(\epsilon_F)$, counting both spin states, in terms of $N$ and $\epsilon_F$: in three dimensions $N \propto \epsilon_F^{3/2}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-tstar" label="$T^* =$" expr="sqrt(5*TD^3/(24*pi^2*TF))"
  vars={{ TD: [50, 500], TF: [5000, 50000] }}
  names={{ TD: "T_D", TF: "T_F" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(5*TD^3/(48*pi^2*TF))", hint: "That puts the density of states of one spin band, 3N/(4ε_F), into the heat capacity formula. The formula needs D counting both spin states: D(ε_F) = 3N/(2ε_F)." },
    { expr: "sqrt(5*TD^3/(6*pi^2*TF))", hint: "That uses the coefficient 3π⁴/5 of the phonon energy U/(Nk_BT⁴/T_D³). The heat capacity is the derivative of U ∝ T⁴, four times larger: 12π⁴/5." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A linear law and a cubic law cross once; below the crossing the electrons carry most of the heat.

<Step title="The electrons">

$N = \int_0^{\epsilon_F}D\,d\epsilon$ with $D \propto \epsilon^{1/2}$ gives $D(\epsilon_F) = 3N/2\epsilon_F$, so $C_{\text{el}} = \frac{\pi^2}{2}Nk_B\,T/T_F$, the notes' three-dimensional form (reading 1, p. 87).

</Step>

<Step title="The crossing">

$\frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{T}{T_F} = \frac{12\pi^4}{5}\frac{T^3}{T_D^3}$ gives

<Formula label="Where the two heat capacities are equal" tex="T^* = \sqrt{\frac{5\,T_D^3}{24\pi^2\,T_F}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A stiffer lattice (larger $T_D$) freezes its vibrations out sooner, so the electrons win up to a higher $T^*$; a larger $T_F$ means fewer electrons near the Fermi surface, and a lower $T^*$. Plotting $C/T$ against $T^2$ gives a straight line whose intercept is the electrons' coefficient, the way the two parts are told apart in measurements.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

(Our numbers.) Give $T^*$ for the metal of this problem, in kelvin (tolerance 1%).

<Answer id="p2-tstarnum" label="$T^*$ in K:" value="0.804" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-tstarnum" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put $T_D = 91$ K and $T_F$ of part (a) into part (b).

<Step title="The number">

$T^* = \sqrt{5 \times 91^3/(24\pi^2 \times 24\,580)}$ K $= 0.804$ K. Below about a kelvin the conduction electrons carry most of the heat of this metal; at room temperature they carry very little.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

(Our numbers.) The electronic heat capacity of one mole is written $\gamma T$. Find $\gamma$ in mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻², with $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ atoms in a mole (the Avogadro constant, CODATA 2018; our value) (tolerance 1%).

<Answer id="p2-gamma" label="$\gamma$ in mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻²:" value="1.67" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-gamma" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One mole holds $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ conduction electrons, so its heat capacity is part (b)'s $\frac{\pi^2}{2}Nk_B\,T/T_F$ with that $N$.

<Step title="The number">

$\gamma = \frac{\pi^2}{2}Nk_B/T_F = 4.935 \times 8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}/24\,580\ \text{K} = 1.67 \times 10^{-3}$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻², that is $1.67$ mJ mol⁻¹ K⁻².

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At 300 K this gives $0.50$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, two percent of the lattice's classical $3Nk_B = 24.9$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ for one mole. The free-electron model leaves out the electrons' interactions with each other and with the lattice, which change $\gamma$ in a real metal, though not the law $C \propto T$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A cloud of fermionic atoms in a harmonic trap (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 3">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the degenerate Fermi gas (reading 1, pp. 82–87) held not in a box but in a trap, as in experiments with cold atoms.

Our model: $N$ identical fermionic atoms of mass $m$, all in the same spin state (a spin-polarized cloud), in an isotropic harmonic trap of angular frequency $\omega$. Identical fermions in one spin state hardly collide when they are cold, so we treat them as an ideal gas (our assumption). A single atom has the levels $\epsilon = \hbar\omega(n_x + n_y + n_z)$ with $n_x, n_y, n_z = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ (energies measured from the zero-point energy, our choice). $N$ is large, so $\epsilon_F \gg \hbar\omega$ and sums over levels may be replaced by integrals.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the density of states $D(\epsilon)$ for $\epsilon \gg \hbar\omega$.

<Hint>

Count the levels below $\epsilon$: they are the points $(n_x, n_y, n_z)$ with $n_x + n_y + n_z < \epsilon/\hbar\omega$ and all three non-negative. What shape do they fill?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-dos" label="$D(\epsilon) =$" expr="eps^2/(2*(hbar*w)^3)"
  vars={{ eps: [1, 10], hbar: [0.5, 1.5], w: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ eps: "\\epsilon", hbar: "\\hbar", w: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "eps^3/(6*(hbar*w)^3)", hint: "That is the number of levels below ε. The density of states is its derivative with respect to ε." },
    { expr: "eps^2/(hbar*w)^3", hint: "That is the derivative of (ε/ħω)³/3. The levels with n_x + n_y + n_z < ε/ħω fill a corner of a cube cut off by a plane, of volume (ε/ħω)³/6." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count, then differentiate the count, as the notes do for the box (reading 1, p. 82).

<Step title="The count">

The points with $n_x + n_y + n_z < s$, $s = \epsilon/\hbar\omega$, fill the corner of the positive octant cut off by a plane, a pyramid of volume $s^3/6$. So the number of levels below $\epsilon$ is $\epsilon^3/6(\hbar\omega)^3$, and

<Formula label="Density of states in a harmonic trap" tex="D(\epsilon) = \frac{d}{d\epsilon}\,\frac{\epsilon^3}{6(\hbar\omega)^3} = \frac{\epsilon^2}{2(\hbar\omega)^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Exactly, the level $\hbar\omega s$ holds $(s + 1)(s + 2)/2$ states, which for large $s$ is $s^2/2$ per step $\hbar\omega$: the same $D$. It grows as $\epsilon^2$, faster than the $\epsilon^{1/2}$ of the box, because the trap lets the atoms spread out as their energy rises.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Find the Fermi energy $\epsilon_F$ in terms of $N$, $\hbar$ and $\omega$.

<Answer id="p3-ef" label="$\epsilon_F =$" expr="(6*N)^(1/3)*hbar*w"
  vars={{ N: [10, 1000], hbar: [0.5, 1.5], w: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ N: "N", hbar: "\\hbar", w: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(3*N)^(1/3)*hbar*w", hint: "That puts two atoms in each level, as for electrons with two spin states. Here all atoms are in one spin state, so each level holds one." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At zero temperature the $N$ atoms fill every level up to $\epsilon_F$, one atom per level.

<Step title="Filling">

$N = \epsilon_F^3/6(\hbar\omega)^3$ from part (a), so

<Formula label="Fermi energy in a harmonic trap" tex="\epsilon_F = (6N)^{1/3}\,\hbar\omega" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$\epsilon_F/\hbar\omega = (6N)^{1/3}$ is large for large $N$, as the integrals assumed: for a million atoms, about 180 levels of the oscillator are filled along each direction.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="The potential barrier of a p–n junction (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 4">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the diode at the end of Chapter VIII (reading 1, p. 92), where the notes draw the barrier without computing its height.

Our model: a semiconductor whose electrons and holes form classical gases, so the density of conduction electrons is $n_e = N_c\,e^{-(\epsilon_c - \mu)/k_BT}$, with $\epsilon_c$ the energy of the bottom of the conduction band and $N_c$ a constant at fixed $T$ (twice $\lambda^{-3}$, for two spin states, reading 1, pp. 90–91; p. 90 writes λ³ for λ⁻³ and leaves out the spin factor). In the pure crystal, $n_e = n_h = n_i$, and the law of mass action $n_en_h = n_i^2$ holds whatever the doping (reading 1, p. 91). On the n side the crystal holds $N_D$ donors per unit volume, on the p side $N_A$ acceptors, all ionized, with $N_D, N_A \gg n_i$ (our assumptions). So on the n side $n_e = N_D$, and on the p side $n_h = N_A$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

In equilibrium the chemical potential is the same on both sides (reading 1, p. 92), so the band edges must shift. Find the height $\epsilon_B = \epsilon_c(\text{p}) - \epsilon_c(\text{n})$ of the barrier that the electrons on the n side face, in terms of $k_B$, $T$, $N_D$, $N_A$ and $n_i$.

<Hint>

On the p side, how many conduction electrons are there? Use the law of mass action.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $n_e = N_c\,e^{-(\epsilon_c - \mu)/k_BT}$ on each side with the same $\mu$, and divide.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-eb" label="$\epsilon_B =$" expr="kB*T*log(ND*NA/ni^2)"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2], ND: [10, 100], NA: [10, 100], ni: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T", ND: "N_D", NA: "N_A", ni: "n_i" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "kB*T*log(ND/ni)", hint: "That is only how far μ on the n side lies above its value in the pure crystal. On the p side μ lies below that value by k_BT ln(N_A/n_i): the barrier is the sum of the two." },
    { expr: "kB*T*log(ni^2/(ND*NA))", hint: "That has the wrong sign. On the p side conduction electrons are scarce, so the band edge there lies higher, and the electrons from the n side must climb." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same $\mu$ with very different electron densities on the two sides is only possible if the band edges sit at different heights.

<Step title="The two sides">

On the n side $n_e = N_D$. On the p side the electrons are the minority: $n_e = n_i^2/N_A$. With the same $\mu$ and the same $N_c$,

<Formula label="Ratio of the electron densities" tex="\frac{n_e(\text{n})}{n_e(\text{p})} = \frac{N_DN_A}{n_i^2} = e^{[\epsilon_c(\text{p}) - \epsilon_c(\text{n})]/k_BT}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The barrier">

<Formula label="Height of the barrier" tex="\epsilon_B = k_BT\,\ln\frac{N_DN_A}{n_i^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The same holds for the holes, which see the barrier from the other side: $n_h(\text{p})/n_h(\text{n}) = N_A/(n_i^2/N_D)$, the same ratio. The barrier is the Boltzmann factor that keeps the many electrons of the n side in balance with the few of the p side: as many climb over it per second as fall back, which is why no current flows without a voltage.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

(Our numbers.) Take $N_D = 10^{22}$ m⁻³, $N_A = 10^{23}$ m⁻³, $n_i = 1.0 \times 10^{16}$ m⁻³ and $T = 300$ K (our values, of the order of those for silicon), with $k_BT = 0.025852$ eV. Give $\epsilon_B$ in eV (tolerance 1%).

<Answer id="p4-ebnum" label="$\epsilon_B$ in eV:" value="0.774" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-ebnum" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into part (a).

<Step title="The number">

$N_DN_A/n_i^2 = 10^{45}/10^{32} = 10^{13}$, $\ln 10^{13} = 29.93$, and $\epsilon_B = 29.93 \times 0.025852$ eV $= 0.774$ eV: the junction holds a built-in voltage of about $0.77$ V.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The barrier is smaller than the gap of a silicon-like crystal (1.12 eV in the lesson's example), as it must be: on each side $\mu$ lies inside the gap, and part (d) shows that $\epsilon_B < \Delta$ in general.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

(Our numbers.) At $T = 300$ K, find the ratio of the current at $V = +0.2$ V to the size of the current at $V = -0.2$ V (tolerance 1%).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

The pure crystal's density obeys $n_i^2 \propto T^3e^{-\Delta/k_BT}$, with $\Delta$ the gap (reading 1, p. 91). Keep the doping fixed and warm the junction. In the classical regime of this problem, the barrier $\epsilon_B$

<Answer id="p4-temp" label="As T rises, ε_B" choices={["grows in proportion to T, from the factor k_BT in front", "falls as T rises, from close to Δ when the crystal is cold", "stays the same, because the dopant densities do not change", "rises towards Δ, as more pairs are excited across the gap"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write $n_i^2$ out in the result of part (a).

<Step title="The barrier and the gap">

With $n_i^2 = AT^3e^{-\Delta/k_BT}$ ($A$ a constant), $\epsilon_B = \Delta - k_BT\ln\left(AT^3/N_DN_A\right)$. </Step>

</Step>

</Solution>
