The [previous page](/courses/8-08/photon-and-phonon-gases) dealt with bosons that can be created and destroyed freely. Atoms of helium-4 or sodium are bosons too, but their number is fixed, and that changes everything: the chemical potential returns, and below a critical temperature it can no longer do its job, so a finite fraction of all the atoms falls into one single quantum state. This is Wen's point ③, **boson condensation** (notes pp. 55–61). The 8.044 page on [Bose-Einstein condensation](/courses/8-044/bose-einstein-condensation) found the critical temperature and the condensate fraction; Wen goes on to the order parameter, finite systems, the equation of state and the heat capacity, using the [grand canonical ensemble](/courses/8-044/grand-canonical-ensemble).

## The Bose distribution with a conserved number

For bosons with a conserved number, the grand partition function sums over all occupation numbers $\{n_k\}$ with the weight $e^{-\beta(E - \mu N)}$, where $N = \sum_k n_k$ (notes p. 55). It factorises just as for photons:

<Formula label="Grand partition function of the ideal Bose gas" tex="Q_G = \sum_{\{n_k\}} e^{-\beta\left(\sum_k n_k\varepsilon_k - \mu N\right)} = \prod_k \sum_{n_k = 0}^{\infty} e^{-\beta n_k(\varepsilon_k - \mu)} = \prod_k \frac{1}{1 - e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_k - \mu)}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 55, point ③" />

(The notes drop the minus sign in the last exponent.) Each factor is the partition function of one oscillator with $\hbar\omega = \varepsilon_k - \mu$: putting a boson into level $k$ costs the energy $\varepsilon_k$ but brings in $\mu$ from the reservoir, a net **energy cost** $\varepsilon_k - \mu$. The probability that level $k$ holds $n_k$ bosons is $e^{-\beta n_k(\varepsilon_k - \mu)}(1 - e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_k - \mu)})$, and the mean is the **Bose distribution**:

<Formula label="Bose distribution" tex="\langle n_k\rangle = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon_k - \mu)} - 1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 55" />

(Wen's boxed version has $-\beta$ in the exponent, a sign slip; with it the occupation would be negative.) Every cost $\varepsilon_k - \mu$ must be positive, otherwise the geometric series diverges, so $\mu$ must lie below the lowest level: with $\varepsilon_0 = 0$ for $\vec k = 0$, $\mu \le 0$.

## Fixing the chemical potential

The chemical potential is whatever makes the total come out right (notes p. 56):

<Formula label="Number of bosons fixes the chemical potential" tex="N = \sum_k\langle n_k\rangle = V\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon_k - \mu)} - 1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 56" />

with $\varepsilon_k = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ for free bosons of mass $m$.

At high temperature $\mu \to -\infty$ and every occupation is small, so the $-1$ can be dropped. The integral is then a Gaussian, and with the thermal wavelength $\lambda$ it gives the classical gas of [Chapter I](/courses/8-08/information-equipartition-ideal-gas):

<Formula label="Classical limit" tex="N = V\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_k - \mu)} = V\,\frac{e^{\beta\mu}}{\lambda^3}, \qquad n\lambda^3 = e^{\beta\mu} = z, \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_BT}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 56" />

with $z = e^{\beta\mu}$, the **fugacity**. In general, expand $1/(z^{-1}e^{\beta\varepsilon} - 1) = \sum_{m=1}^\infty z^me^{-m\beta\varepsilon}$; each term is again a Gaussian integral, $\int d^3k\,e^{-m\beta\varepsilon_k}/(2\pi)^3 = m^{-3/2}\lambda^{-3}$, and the density obeys

<Formula label="Density of an ideal Bose gas" tex="n\lambda^3 = \sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^m}{m^{3/2}} = g_{3/2}(z)" cite="reading1" at="p. 56" />

The function $g_{3/2}(z)$ grows from 0 at $z = 0$ to a finite value at $z = 1$, where it is $\zeta(3/2)$, with infinite slope there. At fixed density, lowering $T$ raises $n\lambda^3$, and the solution $z$ moves towards 1 (Wen's sketch on p. 56 shows two temperatures $T_1 > T_2$).

## Condensation: the chemical potential gets stuck

Since $z = e^{\beta\mu}$ cannot exceed 1, the equation has **no solution** when $n\lambda^3 > g_{3/2}(1) = 2.612$ (notes p. 57). This happens below a temperature, or above a density,

<Formula label="Condensation temperature and density" tex="k_BT_c = \frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m}\left(\frac{n}{g_{3/2}(1)}\right)^{2/3}, \qquad n_c = g_{3/2}(1)\left(\frac{mk_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 57" />

What happens then? As $\mu \to 0$ the excited levels hold most of the bosons; adding more, we cannot raise $\mu$ further. But at $\mu = 0$ the $\vec k = 0$ level can hold **any number** of bosons. Wen's picture: think of $\mu$ as the energy level of a particle reservoir. Moving a boson from the reservoir into the $\vec k = 0$ level costs $\varepsilon_0 - \mu = -\mu$. At $\mu = 0$ this costs nothing, so the free energy does not care how many bosons sit there. If $\mu$ were above zero, every transfer would lower the free energy, without limit: the system would be unstable. So the extra bosons all go into the lowest level.

Wen also sketches $\mu(T)$ at fixed density for the three gases. For fermions it starts at the Fermi energy at $T = 0$ and falls; for a classical gas, $\mu = k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3)$ starts at zero at $T = 0$, rises to a positive maximum and then falls through zero (at low temperature the classical formula no longer applies, which is the point of the comparison); for bosons it rises with falling $T$ and reaches zero at $T_c$, then stays there. The corner at $T_c$ is a **singularity**, the sign of a phase transition.

## The condensate and the order parameter

Below $T_c$ (or above $n_c$), the excited levels hold as many bosons as they can at $z = 1$, and the rest, $N_0$, sit in the $\vec k = 0$ level (notes p. 58):

<Formula label="Bosons in the condensate" tex="N = N_0 + \frac{V}{\lambda^3}\,g_{3/2}(1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{N_0}{N} = 1 - \frac{g_{3/2}(1)}{n\lambda^3} = 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{3/2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 58" />

using $n\lambda_c^3 = g_{3/2}(1)$ and $\lambda \propto T^{-1/2}$. In the condensed phase $N_0/N \neq 0$; in the gas phase it is zero. So $N_0/N$ is an **order parameter**, like the magnetisation of the [Ising model](/courses/8-08/ising-model-mean-field). Wen sketches the energy distribution of the bosons, $D(\varepsilon)/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)} - 1)$: above $T_c$ a broad hump; below, a smaller hump of bosons in excited levels plus a spike of $N_0$ at $\varepsilon = 0$.

## No phase transition in a finite system

In a box of finite size the sum over levels can be done exactly, and nothing is singular (notes p. 58). The lowest level alone holds

<Formula label="Occupation of the lowest level" tex="N = \sum_k\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon_k - \mu)} - 1} = \sum_k\frac{1}{z^{-1}e^{\beta\varepsilon_k} - 1}, \qquad N_0 = \frac{1}{z^{-1} - 1} = \frac{z}{1 - z}" cite="reading1" at="p. 58" />

(The notes write the sum with $z\,e^{\beta\varepsilon_k}$ where $z^{-1}e^{\beta\varepsilon_k}$ belongs, and hence $N_0 = 1/(z - 1)$, which has the wrong sign. For $z$ near 1, $N_0 \approx 1/(1 - z)$.) For $N_0$ to be a finite fraction of $N$, $1 - z$ must be of order $1/N$: as $1 - z$ goes from $2/N$ to $1/N$, $N_0$ goes from $N/2$ to $N$. So in a finite system, density against $z$ is a smooth curve that turns sharply upwards within about $1/N$ of $z = 1$, and $z$ against $N$ at fixed $T$ bends over smoothly towards 1. Only in the limit $V \to \infty$ (at fixed density) does the turn become a true corner, a singularity, and the condensation a true phase transition. The same holds for every phase transition: strictly, they exist only in infinite systems.

## Equation of state

The thermodynamics comes from the grand potential, $\Omega = -k_BT\ln Q_G$ (notes p. 59):

<Formula label="Grand potential of the ideal Bose gas" tex="\Omega = -k_BT\ln Q_G = k_BT\sum_k\ln\left[1 - e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_k - \mu)}\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 59" />

As for photons, the pressure comes from how the levels move with the volume: now $\varepsilon_k = \hbar^2k^2/2m \propto V^{-2/3}$, so $\partial\varepsilon_k/\partial V = -\frac23\varepsilon_k/V$ and

<Formula label="Pressure and energy" tex="P = -\left.\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial V}\right|_{\mu, T} = \sum_k\langle n_k\rangle\left(-\frac{\partial\varepsilon_k}{\partial V}\right) = \frac{2}{3V}\sum_k\langle n_k\rangle\varepsilon_k \quad\Longrightarrow\quad PV = \frac23U" cite="reading1" at="p. 59" />

The energy uses the same expansion in powers of $z$ and the Gaussian integral. Writing $\varepsilon e^{-m\beta\varepsilon}$ as $-\frac1m\,\partial e^{-m\beta\varepsilon}/\partial\beta$ (at fixed $z$), each term becomes a derivative of $m^{-3/2}\lambda^{-3}$, and $\partial\lambda^{-3}/\partial\beta = -\frac32\lambda^{-3}/\beta$ because $\lambda^{-3} \propto \beta^{-3/2}$:

<Formula label="Energy of the ideal Bose gas" tex="U = V\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\varepsilon_k\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}z^me^{-m\beta\varepsilon_k} = -V\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^m}{m^{5/2}}\,\frac{\partial\lambda^{-3}}{\partial\beta} = \frac32\,V\lambda^{-3}g_{5/2}(z)\,k_BT" cite="reading1" at="p. 59" />

with the family $g_\nu(z) = \sum_m z^m/m^\nu$ (Wen notes that integrating $g_\nu(z)/z$ from 0 to $z$ gives $g_{\nu + 1}(z)$, so each is the next one's derivative times $z$; his box writes the $1/z$ outside the integral, a slip). Together, Wen boxes the three equations of the ideal Bose gas (notes p. 60):

<Formula label="Equations of state of the ideal Bose gas" tex="\frac{U}{V} = \frac32\,k_BT\,\frac{g_{5/2}(z)}{\lambda^3}, \qquad P = \frac23\,\frac{U}{V} = k_BT\,\frac{g_{5/2}(z)}{\lambda^3}, \qquad n\lambda^3 = g_{3/2}(z) \ \text{ or } \ z = 1" cite="reading1" at="p. 60" />

The last one fixes $z(n, T)$: from $g_{3/2}(z) = n\lambda^3$ in the gas phase, and $z = 1$ in the condensed phase. There the pressure, $k_BTg_{5/2}(1)/\lambda^3 \propto T^{5/2}$, depends on the temperature only: the condensate carries no momentum and exerts no pressure, and the density of bosons in excited levels is fixed by $T$ alone. So Wen's isotherms in the $P$–$V$ plane are **flat** at small volume, like the coexistence region of a liquid and its vapour, and fall as $V$ grows once the volume is large enough for the gas phase; the corner where they meet is a singularity.

## Heat capacity

In the condensed phase, $z = 1$ and $U$ depends on $T$ through $\lambda^{-3} \propto T^{3/2}$ only (notes p. 60):

<Formula label="Heat capacity below the transition" tex="U = \frac32\,k_BT\,V\,\frac{g_{5/2}(1)}{\lambda^3} \propto T^{5/2}, \qquad C_V = \left.\frac{\partial U}{\partial T}\right|_{V,N} = \frac{15}{4}\,k_B\,\frac{g_{5/2}(1)}{\lambda^3}\,V" cite="reading1" at="p. 60" />

So $C_V \propto T^{3/2}$, with $g_{5/2}(1) = \zeta(5/2) = 1.341$. Wen sets this against the other low-temperature laws:

- $C_V \propto T^{3/2}$: conserved free bosons with $\varepsilon_k \propto k^2$ (this page);
- $C_V \propto T$: conserved fermions, whatever $\varepsilon_k$ (the next chapter);
- $C_V \propto T^3$: non-conserved free bosons with $\varepsilon_k \propto k$ (photons and phonons, previous page);
- $C_V \propto T^3$: conserved **interacting** bosons, whatever $\varepsilon_k$ (the [next page](/courses/8-08/interacting-bosons-gross-pitaevskii)).

Real liquid helium-4 follows the last line, not the first: its heat capacity rises as $T^3$ at low temperature and has a sharp peak at 2.17 K, as Wen's sketch shows. In the gas phase, $z$ changes with $T$, and differentiating at fixed $N$ gives

<Formula label="Heat capacity above the transition" tex="C_V = k_B\,\frac{V}{\lambda^3}\left[\frac{15}{4}\,g_{5/2}(z) - \frac94\,\frac{g_{3/2}(z)^2}{g_{1/2}(z)}\right], \qquad g_{1/2}(1) = \infty" cite="reading1" at="p. 60" />

(The notes write the second term in the bracket as $\frac94\,g_{3/2}(z)/g_{1/2}(z)$, which lacks a factor $g_{3/2}(z)/\lambda^3$ and so has the wrong units. Per boson, dividing by $Nk_B = k_BVg_{3/2}(z)/\lambda^3$, the corrected result reads $C_V/Nk_B = \frac{15}{4}g_{5/2}/g_{3/2} - \frac94g_{3/2}/g_{1/2}$.) At $T_c$ the second term vanishes because $g_{1/2}(1)$ is infinite, so $C_V$ is **continuous**, reaching $\frac{15}{4}\times 1.341/2.612 = 1.93$ times $Nk_B$; but its slope jumps, so the curve has a **cusp**. At high temperature it falls to the classical $\frac32Nk_B$.

## Condensates in the laboratory

The transition temperature depends only on the density and the mass (notes p. 61). Wen gives two estimates. For **sodium atoms** ($Z = 11$, $A = 23$) in a trap at $n = 10^{14}$ cm⁻³, $T_c = 1.5$ μK: this is why the first atomic condensates needed laser and evaporative cooling to millionths of a kelvin. For **helium-4** ($Z = 2$, $A = 4$) at a liquid density of 0.12 g/cm³, $n = 1.8 \times 10^{22}$ cm⁻³ and the formula gives about 2.8 K. (The notes print $T_c = 1.8$ K, which does not follow from their numbers.) The actual transition of liquid helium, to a superfluid, is at 2.17 K, remarkably close for a liquid of strongly interacting atoms.

How is condensation seen? Wen describes the **momentum distribution**, the number of bosons with wave vector between $k$ and $k + dk$:

<Formula label="Momentum distribution" tex="N(k)\,dk = V\,\frac{4\pi k^2\,dk}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon_k - \mu)} - 1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 61" />

(the notes leave out the $(2\pi)^3$ here). Above $T_c$ it is a broad hump; below, the same hump of bosons in excited levels sits beside the $N_0 = N - (V/\lambda^3)g_{3/2}(1)$ bosons at $k = 0$. In the **time-of-flight** method the trap is switched off and the cloud allowed to fly apart: atoms with large momenta spread far, and a picture taken later is a picture of the momentum distribution. Above $T_c$ it shows a broad smooth cloud; below, a sharp narrow peak, the condensate, stands on top of it. A second test is **interference**: two condensates released side by side overlap and show stripes, as two waves do, a sign that the condensate is one coherent wave, which is the starting point of the next page.

<Step title="Half the transition temperature">
$N_0/N = 1 - 0.5^{1.5} = 0.646$, so about 64,600 atoms are in the lowest level. From $N_0 \approx 1/(1 - z)$, $1 - z = 1.5 \times 10^{-5}$ and $\mu = k_BT\ln z \approx -k_BT/N_0$: $\mu/k_B \approx -4 \times 10^{-12}$ K, zero for every practical purpose, as the infinite-system picture says. The heat capacity there is $1.93 \times 0.354 = 0.68$ times $Nk_B$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Bosons in the hot early universe">
The bosons of the early universe's radiation are photons, whose number is not conserved: their chemical potential is zero and they never condense, and their energy density follows from the Bose distribution of this chapter. In [MIT 8.286 Lecture 17](/courses/8-286/neutrinos-and-particle-counting) Guth counts every effectively massless species in the black-body energy density, fermions with a factor $7/8$ per spin state, which gives $g = 10\frac34$ for photons, neutrinos and electron-positron pairs.
</Callout>

## Summary

- With the number of bosons conserved, each level is an oscillator with $\hbar\omega = \varepsilon_k - \mu$: $Q_G = \prod_k[1 - e^{-\beta(\varepsilon_k - \mu)}]^{-1}$ and $\langle n_k\rangle = 1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon_k - \mu)} - 1)$, so $\mu \le 0$.
- The density fixes $z = e^{\beta\mu}$ through $n\lambda^3 = g_{3/2}(z)$; classically $n\lambda^3 = z$.
- Since $g_{3/2}(z) \le 2.612$, there is no solution below $k_BT_c = (2\pi\hbar^2/m)(n/2.612)^{2/3}$: $\mu$ sticks at 0 and $N_0/N = 1 - (T/T_c)^{3/2}$ bosons collect in $\vec k = 0$. $N_0/N$ is the order parameter; in a finite system $N_0 = z/(1 - z)$ and there is no true singularity.
- $PV = \frac23U$, $P = k_BTg_{5/2}(z)/\lambda^3$: isotherms are flat in the condensed phase.
- $C_V = \frac{15}{4}k_BVg_{5/2}(1)/\lambda^3 \propto T^{3/2}$ below $T_c$, continuous with a cusp of $1.93Nk_B$ at $T_c$. Sodium atoms at $10^{14}$ cm⁻³ condense at 1.5 μK; the ideal-gas estimate for helium is a few kelvin, against the real 2.17 K.
