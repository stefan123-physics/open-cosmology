The [previous page](/courses/8-08/van-der-waals-equation) derived the free energy of the van der Waals gas,

<Formula label="Free energy of the van der Waals model" tex="A(T, V) = Nk_BT\left(\ln\frac{N\lambda^3}{V - b} - 1\right) - \frac{a}{V}" cite="reading1" at="p. 14, point ②" />

with $b$ the volume excluded by the hard cores and $a > 0$ the strength of the attraction. This page follows points ③ to ⑦ of Wen's Chapter II (notes pp. 15–20): how a phase transition hides in this formula, the **Gibbs potential** that reveals it, the **critical point**, the **Clapeyron equation**, and the behaviour near the critical point, where the notes meet the idea of **universality**. The thermodynamic potentials and their differentials are those of [8.044's Maxwell relations](/courses/8-044/maxwell-relations). (Everything here is in our own words, with our own examples and plots.)

## Where is the phase transition?

Knowing the free energy $A(T, V)$ means knowing everything about the gas in equilibrium (notes p. 15, point ③). So does it describe a phase transition, the condensation of a gas into a liquid? Wen's criterion: a phase transition is a **singularity** of the free energy, a point where it, or one of its derivatives, fails to be smooth. The van der Waals $A(T, V)$ is a perfectly smooth function of $T$ and $V$ for $V > b$. Wen's first conclusion, that there is then no phase transition, is marked in the notes with a cross: the conclusion is wrong, and the transition is found by examining the gas at fixed pressure instead, through the Gibbs potential $G(T, P)$.

(Why $A$ misleads, in the page's words: below a certain temperature the van der Waals $A(T, V)$ has a range of volumes where it curves the wrong way, where $\partial P/\partial V > 0$ and the gas would expand when pushed. A uniform gas there is unstable and splits into two phases, so the true free energy differs from the formula; the Gibbs potential does that bookkeeping automatically.)

## The Gibbs potential

To hold the pressure fixed, Wen attaches the gas to a piston pushed by a spring (notes p. 16, point ④). With the spring set to push with pressure $P$, its energy is $PV$ plus a constant, and the Gibbs potential of the gas is the **free energy of the combined system**, gas plus spring. In each state $n$ the gas has energy $E_n$ and volume $V_n$, so

<Formula label="Partition function of gas plus spring" tex="Q_{\text{com}} = \sum_n e^{-\beta(E_n + V_nP)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 16, point ④" />

and its free energy is $A_{\text{com}} = -k_BT\ln Q_{\text{com}}$. The notes evaluate this in two ways. First, since the volume of a large system hardly fluctuates, $V_n$ can be replaced by its typical value $V^*$, so $Q_{\text{com}} = Q_{\text{sys}}e^{-\beta PV^*}$ and $A_{\text{com}} = A_{\text{sys}} + PV^* = G$ (notes p. 16). Second, more carefully, sort the states by their volume:

<Formula label="Summing over volumes" tex="Q_{\text{com}} = \int dV\sum_n\delta(V - V_n)\,e^{-\beta(E_n + VP)} = \int dV\,e^{-\beta[A(T, V) + VP]} \approx e^{-\beta[A(T, V^*) + V^*P]}" cite="reading1" at="p. 16, point ④" />

The sum over the states of volume $V$ is the partition function of the gas at that volume, $e^{-\beta A(T, V)}$. The remaining integrand is proportional to the **probability distribution of the volume**, and for a large system it is so sharply peaked that the integral is dominated by the volume $V^*$ where $A(T, V) + PV$ is smallest. (The notes write $\bar V$ for $V^*$; we keep the bar for the reduced variables below.) Hence the rule boxed in the notes (p. 16):

- at fixed $T$ and $V$, a system minimizes its free energy $A$;
- at fixed $T$ and $P$, it minimizes its Gibbs potential $G = A + PV$.

From $dA = -S\,dT - P\,dV$, the Gibbs potential changes as

<Formula label="Differential of the Gibbs potential" tex="dG = dA + d(PV) = -S\,dT + V\,dP, \qquad V = \frac{\partial G(T, P)}{\partial P}, \quad S = -\frac{\partial G(T, P)}{\partial T}" cite="reading1" at="p. 16, point ④" />

## The van der Waals gas at constant pressure

Now the question becomes concrete (notes p. 17, point ⑤): given the pressure, what is the volume? It is the volume that minimizes

<Formula label="Gibbs potential as a function of volume" tex="G = Nk_BT\left(\ln\frac{N\lambda^3}{V - b} - 1\right) - \frac{a}{V} + PV = -Nk_BT\ln(V - b) - \frac{a}{V} + PV + \text{terms independent of } V" cite="reading1" at="p. 17, point ⑤" />

Its slope is (notes p. 18)

<Formula label="Slope of the Gibbs potential" tex="\frac{\partial G}{\partial V} = -\frac{Nk_BT}{V - b} + \frac{a}{V^2} + P" cite="reading1" at="p. 18" />

which vanishes exactly when $P$ equals the van der Waals pressure $Nk_BT/(V - b) - a/V^2$ at that volume: the extrema of $G$ are the points where the horizontal line at height $P$ meets the isotherm. Wen's sketches show three behaviours (notes pp. 17–18), with the critical isotherm as the boundary between the other two. At **high temperature** the isotherm falls steadily, there is one solution, and $G(V)$ has a single minimum. At **low temperature** the isotherm has a wiggle, a horizontal line can cross it three times, and $G(V)$ has two minima, a small volume (the liquid) and a large one (the gas), with a maximum between them.

Which of the two minima wins? At low pressure the gas minimum is the deeper one, at high pressure the liquid; at one pressure $P_0(T)$ they are equally deep. As $P$ passes $P_0$ the system jumps from one minimum to the other, and the volume, $\partial G/\partial P$, jumps with it: the gas condenses. $G(T, P)$ itself stays continuous but gets a **kink** at $P_0$, the singularity that marks the transition (notes p. 17; at high temperature Wen's sketch of $G(P)$ is smooth; the sketch labels the kink $P_c$, but it is the coexistence pressure $P_0$, not the critical pressure). The points $(T, P_0(T))$ form the liquid–gas line of the phase diagram, liquid above it, gas below. (Since $G(V_2) - G(V_1)$ is the integral of $\partial G/\partial V = P - P_{\text{vdW}}(V)$ between the two volumes, equal depth means the line at $P_0$ cuts off equal areas of the wiggle above and below it: this is Maxwell's equal-area rule, the page's addition.)

The line ends. As the temperature rises the two minima approach each other and the barrier between them shrinks, until at the **critical point** they merge: above it there is no distinction between liquid and gas (Wen's sketch of the phase diagram, notes p. 17).

## The critical point

The volumes where $\partial G/\partial V = 0$ are the roots of the equation of state written as a cubic in $V$ (notes p. 17):

<Formula label="Equation of state as a cubic" tex="V^3 - \left(b + \frac{Nk_BT}{P}\right)V^2 + \frac{a}{P}\,V - \frac{ab}{P} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 17, point ⑤" />

Below the critical temperature it has three real roots for a range of pressures, above it only one. At the critical point the three roots coincide, and the cubic must be $(V - V_c)^3$ (notes p. 17). Comparing coefficients gives three conditions, $3V_c = b + Nk_BT_c/P_c$, $3V_c^2 = a/P_c$ and $V_c^3 = ab/P_c$, with the solution

<Formula label="Critical point of the van der Waals gas" tex="V_c = 3b, \qquad P_c = \frac{a}{27b^2}, \qquad k_BT_c = \frac{8a}{27bN}" cite="reading1" at="p. 17, point ⑤" />

(Dividing the third condition by the second gives $V_c = 3b$; the second then gives $P_c$ and the first $T_c$. The notes write $T_c = 8a/27bR$, with $R = Nk_B$.) A combination with neither $a$ nor $b$ in it is $P_cV_c/(Nk_BT_c) = 3/8$, the same for every van der Waals gas.

<WorkedExample title="Fitting carbon dioxide">
<Problem>
Carbon dioxide has its critical point at $T_c \approx 304.13$ K and $P_c \approx 7.38$ MPa, with a molar volume there of about 94.1 cm³ (NIST). Choose the van der Waals constants per mole that reproduce $T_c$ and $P_c$, and compare the predicted critical volume with the measured one.
</Problem>
<Step title="Solve for b and a">
From the two formulas, $b = Nk_BT_c/(8P_c)$ and $a = 27(Nk_BT_c)^2/(64P_c)$. For one mole $Nk_B = R = 8.314$ J/K, so $Nk_BT_c \approx 2529$ J and $b \approx 2529/(8 \times 7.377 \times 10^6)$ m³ $\approx 4.28 \times 10^{-5}$ m³, about 42.8 cm³; $a \approx 27 \times 2529^2/(64 \times 7.377 \times 10^6) \approx 0.366$ Pa m⁶ per mole squared.
</Step>
<Step title="The critical volume">
The model predicts $V_c = 3b \approx 128.5$ cm³ per mole, about 37% more than the measured 94.1 cm³. Put differently, the model's $P_cV_c/RT_c = 0.375$, while carbon dioxide's is $7.377 \times 10^6 \times 94.1 \times 10^{-6}/2529 \approx 0.274$.
</Step>
<Step title="What this says">
The van der Waals equation gets the existence and the rough location of the critical point right with two constants, but not the details. Most real gases have $P_cV_c/RT_c$ between about 0.23 and 0.31, all well below $3/8$: the mean-field treatment is too crude close to the critical point.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why the attraction matters

What makes the wiggle? The notes compare the signs of the constants (p. 18). With **attraction**, $a > 0$, and a hard core, $b > 0$, the slope $\partial G/\partial V$ crosses zero once at large $T$, where the $Nk_BT/(V - b)$ term dominates, but three times at small $T$, where the $a/V^2$ term can win over a range of volumes: two phases become possible. With **repulsion** instead, $a < 0$, both terms push the same way, $\partial G/\partial V$ rises steadily and crosses zero once at every temperature: no liquid. A liquid needs atoms that attract one another. Wen closes the point with the shape of a real interaction, a hard core (giving $b > 0$) and an attractive well outside it (giving $a > 0$), as plotted on the [previous page](/courses/8-08/van-der-waals-equation).

## The Clapeyron equation

Along the transition line the two phases coexist, so their Gibbs potentials are equal, $G_l(T, P) = G_g(T, P)$ (subscripts l for the liquid, g for the gas) (notes p. 19, point ⑥). Moving along the line, both change by the same amount:

<Formula label="Equal changes along the line" tex="dG_l = V_l\,dP - S_l\,dT = dG_g = V_g\,dP - S_g\,dT" cite="reading1" at="p. 19, point ⑥" />

Solving for the slope of the line gives the **Clapeyron equation**:

<Formula label="Clapeyron equation" tex="\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{\Delta S}{\Delta V} = \frac{L}{T\,\Delta V}, \qquad L = T\,\Delta S" cite="reading1" at="p. 19, point ⑥" />

where $\Delta S$ and $\Delta V$ are the jumps in entropy and volume between the phases and $L$ is the **latent heat**, the heat absorbed in the transition at temperature $T$. The same reasoning holds for any two phases. Wen applies it to **melting** (notes p. 19): for a solid–liquid transition $\Delta V \approx 0$, so the melting line is nearly vertical in the $P$–$T$ plane. Going from solid to liquid has $\Delta S > 0$, so the line slopes forwards when the liquid takes more room ($\Delta V > 0$, the usual case) and backwards when it takes less ($\Delta V < 0$), as for water: ice melts under pressure.

<WorkedExample title="Boiling water and melting ice">
<Problem>
(a) Water boils at 100 °C (373.15 K) and 101325 Pa with a latent heat of about 40.66 kJ per mole. Treating the vapour as an ideal gas and neglecting the volume of the liquid, how fast does the boiling pressure rise with temperature? (b) Ice at 0 °C melts with a latent heat of about 6.01 kJ per mole; a mole of water fills 18.02 cm³ and a mole of ice 19.65 cm³. What is the slope of the melting line?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Boiling">
The vapour fills $RT/P = 8.314 \times 373.15/101325 \approx 0.0306$ m³ per mole, and $\Delta V$ is nearly all of it. So $dP/dT \approx 40660/(373.15 \times 0.0306) \approx 3.56 \times 10^3$ Pa/K: the boiling pressure rises by about 3.6 kPa, roughly 3.5% of an atmosphere, per kelvin.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Melting">
$\Delta V = 18.02 - 19.65 = -1.63$ cm³ per mole. So $dP/dT = 6010/(273.15 \times (-1.63 \times 10^{-6}))$ Pa/K $\approx -1.35 \times 10^7$ Pa/K. The melting line slopes backwards and is steep: about 13.5 MPa, some 130 atmospheres, lowers the melting point by one kelvin.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Scaling near the critical point and universality

Measure pressure, volume and temperature in units of their critical values (notes p. 20, point ⑦):

<Formula label="Reduced variables" tex="\bar P = \frac{P}{P_c}, \qquad \bar V = \frac{V}{V_c}, \qquad \bar T = \frac{T}{T_c}" cite="reading1" at="p. 20, point ⑦" />

Dividing the van der Waals equation by $P_cV_c$ and using $V_c = 3b$, $a/(P_cV_c^2) = 3$ and $Nk_BT_c/(P_cV_c) = 8/3$, the constants $a$ and $b$ disappear:

<Formula label="Reduced van der Waals equation" tex="\left(\bar V - \frac13\right)\left(\bar P + \frac{3}{\bar V^2}\right) = \frac83\,\bar T" cite="reading1" at="p. 20, point ⑦" />

The same equation for every gas: in reduced variables, all van der Waals gases behave alike near their critical points. Wen calls this **universality** (notes p. 20). (Classically this is the law of corresponding states.)

Close to the critical point the shape of $G$ is simple. Write $t$ for the distance from the critical temperature (for example $t = T/T_c - 1$) and $V_0$ for the volume in the middle of the two phases. Near the critical point, and at the pressure where the phases coexist, the Gibbs potential is even about $V_0$, and its leading terms are (notes p. 20)

<Formula label="Gibbs potential near the critical point" tex="G(t, V) = C_1\,t\,(V - V_0)^2 + C_2\,(V - V_0)^4 + \text{const}" cite="reading1" at="p. 20, point ⑦" />

with $C_1$ and $C_2$ positive. For $t > 0$ there is one minimum, at $V_0$; for $t < 0$ the quadratic term turns negative and the minimum splits in two. Setting the slope to zero,

<Formula label="The two minima" tex="\frac{\partial G}{\partial V} = 2C_1t\,(V - V_0) + 4C_2\,(V - V_0)^3 = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad V - V_0 = \pm\sqrt{-\frac{C_1t}{2C_2}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 20, point ⑦" />

so the volume jump between liquid and gas is

<Formula label="Volume jump" tex="\Delta V = \sqrt{-\frac{2C_1t}{C_2}} \propto |t|^{1/2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 20, point ⑦" />

For the van der Waals gas itself (this calculation is the page's addition), expanding the reduced equation of state about the critical point to lowest orders in $t$ and $\phi = \bar V - 1$ gives $\bar P \approx 1 + 4t - 6t\phi - \frac32\phi^3$. In units of $P_cV_c$ that makes $C_1 = 3$ and $C_2 = \frac38$, and the jump in reduced volume is $\Delta\bar V \approx 4\sqrt{-t}$. At $\bar T = 0.99$ this predicts $0.40$, close to the exact coexisting volumes of the van der Waals gas, $0.831$ and $1.243$, which differ by $0.41$.

The notes then quote experiment (p. 20): the measured jump grows as

<Formula label="Measured scaling" tex="\Delta V \propto |t|^\beta, \qquad \beta \approx \frac13" cite="reading1" at="p. 20, point ⑦" />

and, strikingly, with the same $\beta$ for every gas: the exponent does not depend on what atoms the gas is made of. This is universality in a deeper sense than the reduced equation. (The comparison that follows is ours.) Experiment confirms the power law but not the mean-field exponent: the theory predicts that there should be a universal exponent, but gets its value wrong ($\frac12$ instead of about $\frac13$; today's best value is close to 0.326). Getting it right needs the fluctuations that the mean field throws away, the subject of the theory of critical phenomena. The next chapter, on the Ising model, studies a magnet with the same kind of mean-field theory.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Phase transitions in the early universe">
The picture of this page, a free energy with two minima and a system that must pass from one to the other, is how cosmologists think about the early universe. As the universe cooled it passed through phase transitions in which a Higgs field took on a non-zero value ([MIT 8.286 Lecture 22](/courses/8-286/higgs-field)). In Guth's original model of inflation the universe sat in a local minimum, the **false vacuum**, a supercooled state of the old phase, and the transition was to happen by bubbles of the new phase nucleating and growing, which Guth compared to water boiling; their messy collisions were the model's downfall ([Lecture 23](/courses/8-286/false-vacuum)).
</Callout>

## Summary

- A phase transition is a singularity of the free energy. The van der Waals $A(T, V)$ hides it; the **Gibbs potential** reveals it.
- $G$ is the free energy of the gas together with a piston of energy $PV$: $Q_{\text{com}} = \int dV\,e^{-\beta[A(T, V) + PV]} \approx e^{-\beta G}$. At fixed $T$ and $P$ the volume minimizes $A + PV$, and $dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP$.
- Below $T_c$, $G(V)$ has two minima, liquid and gas; where they are equally deep the volume jumps and $G(T, P)$ has a kink. A liquid needs an attraction, $a > 0$.
- The critical point, where the three roots of the cubic merge: $V_c = 3b$, $P_c = a/(27b^2)$, $k_BT_c = 8a/(27bN)$; $P_cV_c/Nk_BT_c = 3/8$ for the model, about 0.23 to 0.31 for real gases.
- **Clapeyron**: $dP/dT = L/(T\Delta V)$ along a coexistence line; ice, which shrinks on melting, has a backward-sloping melting line.
- In reduced variables $(\bar V - \frac13)(\bar P + 3/\bar V^2) = \frac83\bar T$ for every gas. Near $T_c$ the volume jump grows as $|t|^{1/2}$ in mean-field theory; experiments give $|t|^\beta$ with $\beta \approx \frac13$ for all gases: universality.
