8.08 is the second course in statistical physics, and Xiao-Gang Wen's handwritten notes begin with the ensemble that most calculations use: a system held at a fixed temperature by a large heat bath. The 8.044 pages on [the canonical ensemble](/courses/8-044/canonical-ensemble) and [its examples](/courses/8-044/canonical-examples) derived it by expanding the bath's entropy to first order and wrote it as a classical density in phase space; Wen idealises the bath so that the Boltzmann factor is exact, works with discrete quantum states, and then shows why the **free energy** is the quantity a system at fixed temperature minimises (notes pp. 1–4). Notation follows the notes, which follow Huang's textbook: the partition function is $Q$ (8.044 wrote *Z*), the Helmholtz free energy is $A$ (8.044's *F*), and $\Gamma$ counts states. (MIT's material is eleven scanned pages numbered 1 to 12 by hand, with no page 8; this page covers points ① to ④ on pp. 1–4 and the [next page](/courses/8-08/information-equipartition-ideal-gas) points ⑤ to ⑦. Everything here is in our own words, with our own examples.)

## A system and its heat bath

Put a small system in thermal contact with a much larger **heat reservoir**, or bath, and wrap the two together in an insulating box (notes p. 1, point ①). The pair is isolated, so it is a microcanonical ensemble; the small system alone is the **canonical ensemble**. The question is: what is the probability that the system is in a particular state, of energy $E_n$?

Wen's first step is to idealise the bath. An **ideal heat bath** is one whose temperature does not depend on its energy, however much it gives or takes. Since temperature is defined by $1/T = \partial S/\partial E$, a constant $T$ means the bath's entropy grows linearly with its energy (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="Entropy of an ideal heat bath" tex="\frac1T = \frac{\partial S(E)}{\partial E} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad S_{\text{bath}}(E) = \frac{E}{T}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1, point ①" />

(up to a constant, which drops out of every probability). With $S = k_B\ln\Gamma$, the number of states of the bath is then an exponential:

<Formula label="Number of states of an ideal bath" tex="\Gamma_{\text{bath}}(E) = e^{E/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1, point ①" />

A real bath is not exactly ideal, but it is close: expanding $\ln\Gamma_{\text{bath}}$ about the total energy, the first-order term is exactly $-E_n/k_BT$, and the next one is smaller by the ratio of the system's energy to the bath's, which a large enough bath makes as small as we like. (This remark is ours; the notes take the ideal bath as given.)

## The Boltzmann distribution

Now let the system have energy levels $E_n$, $n = 1, 2, \ldots$ (notes p. 1, point ②). The total energy $E_{\text{tot}}$ of system plus bath is fixed, and in the microcanonical ensemble every state of the whole with that energy is equally likely (notes p. 2). If the system is in its $n$th state, there is $1$ way to arrange the system and $\Gamma_{\text{bath}}(E_{\text{tot}} - E_n)$ ways to arrange the bath, so the number of states of the whole is

<Formula label="States of the whole with the system in state n" tex="1\cdot\Gamma_{\text{bath}}(E_{\text{tot}} - E_n) = e^{(E_{\text{tot}} - E_n)/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, point ②" />

The factor $e^{E_{\text{tot}}/k_BT}$ is the same for every $n$, so the probability of each state of the system is proportional to the **Boltzmann factor** (notes p. 2, boxed):

<Formula label="Boltzmann distribution" tex="P_n \propto e^{-E_n/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, point ②" />

Nothing about the system was used: it can be a single atom or a large body, quantum or classical. Everything comes from how fast the bath's number of states falls when the system takes energy from it.

## The partition function

The constant of proportionality is fixed by requiring the probabilities to add up to one. Their sum before normalising is the **partition function**, the "total probability" in Wen's words (notes p. 2, point ③):

<Formula label="Partition function" tex="Q(T, V) \equiv \sum_n e^{-E_n/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, point ③" />

so that $P_n = e^{-E_n/k_BT}/Q$. The energies $E_n$ depend on the volume and anything else that sets the system's levels, hence $Q(T, V)$. This form, a sum over quantum states, is the quantum one; for a classical system the sum becomes an integral over phase space, as on the [next page](/courses/8-08/information-equipartition-ideal-gas).

Wen then rewrites $Q$ as an integral over energy. Insert $1 = \int dE\,\delta(E - E_n)$ into each term; the sum of delta functions counts the system's states at energy $E$, which is its density of states $\Gamma_{\text{sys}}(E)$, and that is $e^{S(E)/k_B}$, with $S(E)$ the entropy the system would have alone at energy $E$ (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Partition function as an integral over energy" tex="Q = \int dE\,\sum_n\delta(E - E_n)\,e^{-E/k_BT} = \int dE\,\Gamma_{\text{sys}}(E)\,e^{-E/k_BT} = \int dE\,e^{-\frac{1}{k_BT}\left(E - S(E)\,T\right)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, point ③" />

The integrand is proportional to the probability that the system has energy $E$, whichever of its states it is in. Two factors compete in it: the Boltzmann factor falls with $E$, and the number of states $\Gamma_{\text{sys}}(E)$ rises with it.

## The free energy and why the peak is sharp

The exponent is $-A(E)/k_BT$, with

<Formula label="The free energy as a function of energy" tex="A = E - S(E)\,T" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

so the most probable energy is the one that makes $A$ **smallest**. (The notes' first line says "maximize" and calls the extreme value the maximum; a few lines later they say the system minimises the free energy, which is right, since $A$ appears with a minus sign in the exponent.) At the minimum the derivative of $A$ vanishes, so the equilibrium energy $\bar E$ satisfies (notes p. 3)

<Formula label="Condition for the equilibrium energy" tex="\frac{1}{T} = \left.\frac{dS(E)}{dE}\right|_{E = \bar E}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The left side is $1/T_{\text{bath}}$, the right side $1/T_{\text{sys}}$: the system settles at the energy where its own temperature equals the bath's. $A = E - ST$ at that energy is the **free energy**, or Helmholtz free energy (notes p. 3). Wen draws two conclusions:

1. $e^{-A/k_BT}$ is proportional to the probability distribution of the canonical ensemble's energy. A system in contact with a bath "wants" to minimise its free energy, which is what reaching equilibrium means (notes p. 3, ⓐ).
2. That distribution is **sharply peaked**, with a width $\Delta E$ small compared with $\bar E$ (notes p. 3, ⓑ). So the integral for $Q$ is, up to a factor much less important than the exponential, the height of the peak:

<Formula label="Free energy from the partition function" tex="Q = \int dE\,e^{-A/k_BT} \sim e^{-A_{\min}/k_BT}, \qquad A = -k_BT\ln Q" cite="reading1" at="p. 3, point ⓑ" />

How sharp is the peak? Take a system whose number of states grows as $E^N$, which is roughly true for $N$ independent degrees of freedom such as classical oscillators. Then $S = Nk_B\ln E$ plus a constant, $A(E) = E - Nk_BT\ln E$ is smallest at $\bar E = Nk_BT$, and with $x = E/\bar E$ the energy distribution is $e^{-(A(E) - A_{\min})/k_BT} = (x\,e^{1 - x})^N$. (This model is our illustration; the notes only sketch a narrow peak.)

The relative width falls as $1/\sqrt N$. When we take the logarithm of $Q$, the width contributes only a term of order $\ln N$, while $A_{\min}/k_BT$ is of order $N$; for a macroscopic body the difference is utterly negligible, and $A = -k_BT\ln Q$ is exact for all practical purposes.

## Comparison with the microcanonical ensemble

Wen sets the two ensembles side by side (notes p. 4). In the microcanonical ensemble every state of energy $E$ is equally likely, so the weight of energy $E$ is just the number of states, $e^{S/k_B}$, and the system maximises its entropy. In the canonical ensemble each of those states also carries a Boltzmann factor:

<Formula label="Weights of an energy in the two ensembles" tex="\text{microcanonical: } \Gamma(E) = e^{S/k_B}, \qquad \text{canonical: } \Gamma(E)\,e^{-E/k_BT} = e^{-(E - ST)/k_BT} = e^{-A/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

So the rule "maximise $S$" for an isolated system becomes "minimise $A = E - ST$" for a system at fixed temperature. The new ingredient is the Boltzmann factor, which is the bath's contribution: energy $E$ taken by the system lowers the bath's entropy by $E/T$. So the system and bath together maximise $S - E/T$, and multiplying by $-T$, that is the same as minimising $E - ST = A$.

## Thermodynamics from the free energy

Point ④ derives the thermodynamic quantities from $A(T)$ (notes p. 4). Define the function of two variables

<Formula label="The free energy before minimising" tex="\tilde A(E, T) = E - S(E)\,T" cite="reading1" at="p. 4, point ④" />

The free energy is its minimum over $E$:

<Formula label="Free energy as the minimum" tex="A(T) = \tilde A(\bar E(T), T), \qquad \left.\frac{\partial\tilde A(E, T)}{\partial E}\right|_{E = \bar E} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 4, point ④" />

**Entropy.** Differentiating with the chain rule, the term through $\bar E(T)$ vanishes because $\tilde A$ is stationary there, and only the explicit $T$ remains (notes p. 4, ⓐ):

<Formula label="Entropy from the free energy" tex="\frac{\partial A(T)}{\partial T} = \left.\frac{\partial\tilde A}{\partial E}\right|_{E = \bar E}\frac{\partial\bar E(T)}{\partial T} + \left.\frac{\partial\tilde A}{\partial T}\right|_{E = \bar E} = 0 + (-S) = -S" cite="reading1" at="p. 4, point ⓐ" />

The heat absorbed per unit rise in temperature is the **heat capacity** (Wen calls it the specific heat), here at fixed volume:

<Formula label="Heat capacity" tex="C = T\,\frac{dS}{dT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4, point ⓐ" />

**Energy.** From $A = \bar E - ST$ (notes p. 4, ⓑ):

<Formula label="Mean energy from the free energy" tex="\bar E = A + S\,T = A - T\,\frac{\partial A}{\partial T}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4, point ⓑ" />

Wen checks this a second way, straight from the definition of the average. With $\beta = 1/k_BT$,

<Formula label="Mean energy from the partition function" tex="\bar E = \langle E\rangle = \frac{\sum_n E_n\,e^{-\beta E_n}}{\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\ln Q = k_BT^2\,\frac{\partial}{\partial T}\left(-\frac{A}{k_BT}\right) = A - T\,\frac{\partial A}{\partial T}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4, point ⓑ" />

using $\partial/\partial\beta = -k_BT^2\,\partial/\partial T$ and $\ln Q = -A/k_BT$. The two routes agree, as they must. So once $Q$ is known, $A = -k_BT\ln Q$ gives the entropy, the energy and the heat capacity by differentiation; the [next page](/courses/8-08/information-equipartition-ideal-gas) adds the pressure.

<WorkedExample title="A molecule with three levels">
<Problem>
A molecule has three states, with energies 0, 0.020 eV and 0.050 eV, and is in a gas at 300 K, which acts as its bath. Find its partition function, the probability of each state, its mean energy, its free energy and its entropy, and check the entropy against $-k_B\sum_n P_n\ln P_n$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The Boltzmann factors">
At 300 K, $k_BT = 0.025852$ eV. The factors are $1$, $e^{-0.020/0.025852} = e^{-0.7736} = 0.4613$ and $e^{-0.050/0.025852} = e^{-1.9341} = 0.1446$, so $Q = 1.6059$.
</Step>
<Step title="Probabilities and mean energy">
$P_n = 0.623$, $0.287$ and $0.090$. The mean energy is $\bar E = (0.020 \times 0.4613 + 0.050 \times 0.1446)/1.6059$ eV $= 0.01025$ eV, about $0.40\,k_BT$.
</Step>
<Step title="Free energy and entropy">
$A = -k_BT\ln Q = -0.025852 \times \ln 1.6059$ eV $= -0.01225$ eV. Then $S = (\bar E - A)/T$, which in units of $k_B$ is $(0.01025 + 0.01225)/0.025852 = 0.870$.
</Step>
<Step title="The check">
$-\sum_n P_n\ln P_n = -(0.623\ln 0.623 + 0.287\ln 0.287 + 0.090\ln 0.090) = 0.870$, the same. The free energy is lower than the lowest level: the molecule's freedom to occupy three states is worth $TS = 0.0225$ eV.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Boltzmann factors in the early universe">
The hot early universe is a gas in thermal equilibrium, and the factor $e^{-E/k_BT}$ decides what it contains. While $k_BT$ is far above a particle's rest energy, the particle is as common as photons; once $k_BT$ falls well below it, its numbers are suppressed by a Boltzmann factor and it annihilates away. The same factor, applied to the binding energy of hydrogen, sets when electrons and protons combine and the cosmic microwave background is released. In 8.286 Guth quotes the resulting energy density of black-body radiation; its derivation is a Boltzmann-weighted sum over the states of the photon gas, done in this course's chapter on the Bose gas.
</Callout>

## Summary

- An **ideal heat bath** keeps its temperature whatever energy it exchanges, so $S_{\text{bath}} = E/T$ and $\Gamma_{\text{bath}}(E) = e^{E/k_BT}$.
- System plus bath is microcanonical. With the system in state $n$, the bath has $e^{(E_{\text{tot}} - E_n)/k_BT}$ states, so $P_n \propto e^{-E_n/k_BT}$, normalised by the **partition function** $Q = \sum_n e^{-E_n/k_BT}$.
- Rewritten as an integral over energy, $Q = \int dE\,e^{-(E - S(E)T)/k_BT}$: the energy distribution is $e^{-A/k_BT}$ with $A = E - ST$. The system sits at the minimum of $A$, where its temperature equals the bath's.
- The peak has a relative width of order $1/\sqrt N$, so $Q \approx e^{-A_{\min}/k_BT}$ and $A = -k_BT\ln Q$.
- From $A(T)$: $S = -\partial A/\partial T$, $C = T\,dS/dT$ and $\bar E = A - T\,\partial A/\partial T = -\partial\ln Q/\partial\beta$.
