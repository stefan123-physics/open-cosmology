The [first page of Chapter IX](/courses/8-08/mean-free-path-sound-diffusion) described diffusion as a random walk, and the [second](/courses/8-08/viscosity-conduction-boltzmann-equation) found friction (viscosity) in a gas. This last page of the chapter, Wen's notes pp. 114–126, shows that the two are linked. A particle that diffuses must also feel friction, in a fixed ratio set by the temperature: the **Einstein relation**. The **Langevin equation** then describes the motion of a Brownian particle in time, and the same ideas, put in the language of Fourier transforms, explain **noise**: the random fluctuations of a current or a voltage. The Fourier transform comes from [8.03](/courses/8-03/fourier-transform-am-radio) and the RLC circuit from [8.02](/courses/8-02/lc-and-rlc-circuits). Everything here is in our own words, with our own examples.

## The Einstein relation

Take one particle in a gas, held by a spring potential $V = \frac{1}{2}\kappa x^2$ (notes p. 114; Wen writes the spring constant as $K$). In equilibrium its position follows the Boltzmann distribution $P(x) \propto e^{-\beta\kappa x^2/2}$, and by equipartition $\langle\frac{1}{2}\kappa x^2\rangle = \frac{1}{2}k_BT$:

<Formula label="Spread in a spring potential" tex="\langle x^2\rangle = \frac{k_BT}{\kappa}" cite="reading1" at="p. 114" />

Now look at the same particle from the point of view of diffusion. Without the spring, its probability distribution $P(x, t)$ obeys the diffusion equation $\partial P/\partial t - D\,\partial^2P/\partial x^2 = 0$, and $\langle x^2\rangle = 2Dt$ in one dimension, $2dDt$ in $d$ dimensions, which for a random walk equals the number of steps times the square of the step length (notes p. 114; the notes leave out the square). Spread like this grows for ever. With the spring the particle feels a force $F$, and a force in a fluid causes a **drift** at a speed proportional to it,

<Formula label="Mobility" tex="u = \eta F" cite="reading1" at="p. 114" />

where $\eta$ is the **mobility**. The drift pulls the particle back and keeps $\langle x^2\rangle$ finite as $t \to \infty$. Requiring that it settles at exactly $k_BT/\kappa$ links the friction to the diffusion (notes p. 114).

To see how, write the particle current with both parts, $\vec j = n\vec u - D\nabla n$, where $n = NP$ for $N$ such particles, and put it in the continuity equation $\partial n/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\vec j = 0$ with $\vec u = \eta\vec F$ (notes p. 115):

<Formula label="Diffusion with a force" tex="\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} + \eta\nabla\cdot(\vec Fn) - D\nabla^2n = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 115" />

In the steady state, which is equilibrium, $\partial n/\partial t = 0$ and the two currents cancel, $\eta\vec Fn = D\nabla n$. For a force from a potential, $\vec F = -\nabla V$, this says $\nabla\ln n = -(\eta/D)\nabla V$. But in equilibrium $n \propto e^{-\beta V}$, so $\nabla\ln n = -\beta\nabla V$, and therefore $\eta/D = \beta$ (notes p. 115):

<Formula label="Einstein relation" tex="D = \eta k_BT" cite="reading1" at="p. 115" />

Wen sums it up as **fluctuation ↔ dissipation**: the diffusion constant $D$, which measures the random spreading $\langle x^2\rangle = 2dDt$, is fixed by the mobility $\eta$, which measures the friction that dissipates the drift.

## Brownian motion in water

For a sphere of radius $a$ moving through a liquid of viscosity $\nu$, a rough estimate of the drag is a viscous stress $\nu\,du/dx \sim \nu u/a$ acting over an area $a^2$, so $F \sim a\nu u$ (notes p. 116). The exact result for slow flow is **Stokes's law**, valid when $a$ is much larger than the liquid's mean free path:

<Formula label="Stokes's law and the mobility" tex="\vec F = 6\pi a\nu\vec u \quad\Rightarrow\quad \eta = \frac{1}{6\pi a\nu}" cite="reading1" at="p. 116" />

With the Einstein relation, the mean square distance travelled in three dimensions is (notes p. 116)

<Formula label="Brownian motion of a sphere" tex="\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle = 6Dt = 6k_BT\eta t = \frac{k_BTt}{\pi a\nu}, \qquad \sqrt{\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle} = 8.9\ \mu\text{m}\,\sqrt{\frac{t}{1\ \text{min}}\bigg/\frac{a}{1\ \mu\text{m}}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 116" />

for water, whose viscosity at 20 °C is about 0.01 poise, that is $10^{-3}$ Pa s (notes p. 116; the 8.9 μm is for 300 K, and at 20 °C it is 8.8 μm). Turned round, the formula measures Boltzmann's constant, $k_B = \langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle\pi a\nu/Tt$, and with the gas constant $R = k_BN_A$ Avogadro's number (notes p. 116). (This is how Jean Perrin counted atoms around 1908, following Einstein's 1905 paper; the history is the page's addition.)

For a particle in a gas, Wen notes that the viscosity is $\nu \sim \bar p/\sigma$, an average momentum over a cross-section (the previous page's $m\bar v/6\sigma$), so that $\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle \approx k_BTt\,\sigma/(a\bar p) \approx \sigma\bar vt/a$ (notes p. 116).

<WorkedExample title="Measuring Boltzmann's constant with a microscope">
<Problem>
A plastic bead of radius $a = 0.5$ μm and density 1050 kg/m³ floats in water at 20 °C ($T = 293$ K, $\nu = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}$ Pa s). (a) Find its mobility, its diffusion constant and its rms displacement in 60 s. (b) A student tracks many such beads and finds $\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle = 1.5 \times 10^{-10}$ m² after 60 s. What values of $k_B$ and $N_A$ does that give? (c) How long does the bead keep its velocity, and how far does it fly in that time?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Mobility and diffusion">
$\eta = 1/(6\pi a\nu) = 1/(6\pi \times 0.5 \times 10^{-6} \times 10^{-3}) \approx 1.06 \times 10^8$ m/(N s). Then $D = \eta k_BT \approx 1.06 \times 10^8 \times 4.05 \times 10^{-21} \approx 4.3 \times 10^{-13}$ m²/s, and $\sqrt{6Dt} \approx 12.4$ μm in a minute, in line with $8.8\ \mu\text{m} \times \sqrt{1/0.5}$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Boltzmann's constant and Avogadro's number">
$k_B = \langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle\pi a\nu/(Tt) = 1.5 \times 10^{-10} \times \pi \times 0.5 \times 10^{-6} \times 10^{-3}/(293 \times 60) \approx 1.34 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K, and $N_A = R/k_B = 8.314/1.34 \times 10^{-23} \approx 6.2 \times 10^{23}$ per mole: within 3% of the true values, from a few hours with a microscope.
</Step>

Its thermal speed is $\sqrt{k_BT/m} \approx 2.7$ mm/s, so it flies freely for only about $1.6 \times 10^{-10}$ m, the size of an atom, before the water randomizes its motion. Under a microscope only the random walk can be seen.
</Step>

## The Langevin equation

The random walk hides how the particle moves in time. The **Langevin equation** describes it (notes p. 117). The particle obeys Newton's law with an external force $F_\text{ex}$ and the collision force $F'$ of the fluid: $dv/dt = F_\text{ex}/m + F'/m$. If the collision force averaged to zero, the average velocity would grow without limit under a constant $F_\text{ex}$; but there is friction, so the average collision force is $\langle F'\rangle = -m\gamma v$ with a **damping coefficient** $\gamma$. (The notes write $-\gamma v$ here and include the $m$ from the next line on.) Splitting off the average leaves a random force $\tilde F$ with $\langle\tilde F\rangle = 0$:

<Formula label="Langevin equation" tex="\frac{dv}{dt} + \gamma v = \frac{F_\text{ex}}{m} + \frac{\tilde F}{m}, \qquad \langle\tilde F\rangle = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 117" />

In the steady state $\langle v\rangle = F_\text{ex}/m\gamma$, so the mobility is $\eta = 1/m\gamma$ (notes p. 117). This equation describes the true motion of a Brownian particle.

For $\langle x^2\rangle$, set $F_\text{ex} = 0$, multiply by $x$ and average. The random force is not correlated with the position, $\langle\tilde Fx\rangle = 0$, and $x\ddot x = \frac{1}{2}\frac{d^2}{dt^2}x^2 - \dot x^2$, while equipartition gives $\langle\dot x^2\rangle = k_BT/m$. With $Y = \langle x^2\rangle$ (notes p. 117):

<Formula label="Equation for the mean square displacement" tex="\frac{d^2Y}{dt^2} = -\gamma\frac{dY}{dt} + \frac{2k_BT}{m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 117" />

Wen reads it as the motion of a particle of unit mass with friction $\gamma$ under a constant force $2k_BT/m$: at long times it reaches the terminal speed $dY/dt = 2k_BT/m\gamma$, so $\langle x^2\rangle = (2k_BT/m\gamma)t = 2Dt$ with $D = k_BT/m\gamma = k_BT\eta$, the Einstein relation again (notes p. 117). The full solution with $\langle x^2\rangle = 0$ and $d\langle x^2\rangle/dt = 0$ at $t = 0$ is (notes p. 118)

<Formula label="Mean square displacement of a Brownian particle" tex="\langle x^2\rangle = \frac{2k_BT}{m\gamma}\left[t - \frac{1}{\gamma}\left(1 - e^{-\gamma t}\right)\right] = 2k_BT\eta\left[t - m\eta\left(1 - e^{-t/m\eta}\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 118" />

At long times it is a random walk shifted by the crossover time, $\langle x^2\rangle \to 2k_BT\eta(t - m\eta)$. (The notes write $2k_BT\eta t - m\eta$, without the factor $2k_BT\eta$ on the second term.) At short times, expanding the exponential to second order, $\langle x^2\rangle = k_BTt^2/m$: the particle flies freely with $\frac{1}{2}m\langle v^2\rangle = \frac{1}{2}k_BT$ (notes p. 118).

## Fluctuation and friction

The velocity tells the same story more directly (notes pp. 118–119). Multiplying the Langevin equation by $v$ gives $\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\langle v^2\rangle + \gamma\langle v^2\rangle = \langle v\tilde F\rangle/m$, and now $\langle v\tilde F\rangle \neq 0$, because the random force itself generates part of the velocity:

<Formula label="Velocity driven by the random force" tex="v(t) = v_0e^{-\gamma t} + \int_{-\infty}^t e^{-\gamma(t - t')}\,\frac{\tilde F(t')}{m}\,dt'" cite="reading1" at="p. 118" />

so, with the force correlation function $K(s) = \langle\tilde F(t + s)\tilde F(t)\rangle$,

<Formula label="Correlation of velocity and force" tex="\langle v\tilde F\rangle = \frac{1}{m}\int_{-\infty}^t\langle\tilde F(t)\tilde F(t')\rangle e^{-\gamma(t - t')}\,dt' = \frac{1}{m}\int_0^\infty K(s)\,e^{-\gamma s}\,ds = \frac{K_\gamma}{m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 118" />

Then $\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\langle v^2\rangle + \gamma\langle v^2\rangle = K_\gamma/m^2$, whose solution relaxes as $Ae^{-2\gamma t}$ to $\langle v^2\rangle = K_\gamma/m^2\gamma$ (notes p. 119). That must be the equipartition value $k_BT/m$, which fixes the friction:

<Formula label="Friction from the random force" tex="m\gamma = \frac{1}{k_BT}K_\gamma, \qquad \frac{1}{\eta} = \frac{1}{k_BT}\int_0^\infty\langle F(s)F(0)\rangle\,e^{-s/m\eta}\,ds" cite="reading1" at="p. 119" />

In Wen's words, the correlation of the random force determines the friction (notes p. 119).

## Friction on a disc in a gas

Wen tests the formula on a disc of area $a^2$ and mass $M$ moving through a gas of molecules of mass $m$ (notes p. 119). The force on it is a series of short kicks, each of height $F_0$ and duration $t_0$, arriving at random with an average spacing $\tau^*$. Two moments more than $t_0$ apart are uncorrelated, so $K(s) = 0$ for $s > t_0$, while $K(0) = F_0^2t_0/\tau^*$; in between $K(s)$ falls off linearly, a triangle. If the kicks are much shorter than the relaxation time, $t_0 \ll M\eta$, the exponential is 1 over the triangle and

<Formula label="Integral of the force correlation" tex="\int_0^\infty K(s)\,e^{-s/M\eta}\,ds = \frac{F_0^2t_0^2}{2\tau^*} = \frac{(2mv_x)^2}{2\tau^*} = \frac{2mk_BT}{\tau^*}" cite="reading1" at="p. 119" />

using that each kick transfers a momentum $F_0t_0 = 2mv_x$ and $m\langle v_x^2\rangle = k_BT$. With the collision rate $1/\tau^* \sim na^2\bar v$:

<Formula label="Friction of a small disc" tex="\frac{1}{\eta} = \frac{2m}{\tau^*} \approx 2mna^2\bar v \qquad (a \ll \lambda)" cite="reading1" at="p. 119" />

For a body much larger than the mean free path, hydrodynamics applies instead: $1/\eta = 6\pi\nu a$ with $\nu = \frac{1}{6} n\bar vm\lambda$ gives $1/\eta = \pi nm\bar v\,a\lambda$ (notes p. 119). The two agree in form, with $a^2$ replaced by $a\lambda$: a large body feels the gas as a viscous fluid, a small one as a hail of separate molecules. (The notes write the mass of the disc as $m$ too; we call it $M$.)

## Noise and the power spectrum

A random signal $A(t)$, such as a fluctuating current or voltage, is **noise** (notes p. 120). It can be thought of as a mixture of harmonics of all frequencies, and the question is how much power sits at each frequency. Power goes as the square of the amplitude, as for light ($P \propto E^2$) or a voltage across a resistor ($P = V^2/R$). With the Fourier transform $A(\omega) = \int dt\,A(t)e^{i\omega t}$, the total energy in a long time $t_\infty$ is (notes p. 120)

<Formula label="Energy in the frequency domain" tex="E = \int_0^{t_\infty}dt\,A^2(t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,|A(\omega)|^2 = \int_0^\infty\frac{d\omega}{\pi}\,|A(\omega)|^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 120" />

since $A(-\omega) = A(\omega)^*$ for a real signal. The power in $[\omega, \omega + d\omega]$ is therefore $|A(\omega)|^2d\omega/\pi t_\infty$, and $|A(\omega)|^2/\pi t_\infty$ is the **power spectrum** (notes p. 120).

For a random signal, $A(\omega)$ itself is random, but its average square is not (notes p. 121). Writing out both transforms, the average involves only $\langle A(t)A(t')\rangle$, which depends only on $t - t'$; one of the two time integrals then just gives a factor $t_\infty$:

<Formula label="Power spectrum and correlation function" tex="\left\langle\frac{|A(\omega)|^2}{\pi t_\infty}\right\rangle = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty dt\,G(t)\,e^{i\omega t} = \frac{S(\omega)}{\pi}, \qquad G(t) = \langle A(t)A(0)\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 121" />

The power spectrum is the Fourier transform of the **correlation function** $G(t)$ (the Wiener–Khinchin theorem; the name is the page's addition). For example, a signal that remembers itself for a correlation time $1/\gamma$, $G(t) = c\,e^{-\gamma|t|}$, has

<Formula label="Spectrum of an exponential correlation" tex="S(\omega) = \frac{2c\gamma}{\omega^2 + \gamma^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 122" />

flat up to about $\omega \sim \gamma$ and falling beyond. (The notes write $(c\gamma/\pi)/(\omega^2 + \gamma^2)$; the integral of $ce^{-\gamma|t|}e^{i\omega t}$ is $2c\gamma/(\omega^2 + \gamma^2)$, and the power spectrum $S/\pi$ is $2c\gamma/\pi(\omega^2 + \gamma^2)$.) As the correlation time goes to zero, $S(\omega)$ becomes a constant: **white noise**, in which $A(t)$ and $A(t + \Delta t)$ have no correlation at all (notes p. 122).

## Shot noise

A current is made of discrete charges (notes p. 123). Divide time into short intervals $\Delta t$; in each, one electron passes ($s_i = 1$) with probability $p$, or none ($s_i = 0$). The current in interval $i$ is $I_i = es_i/\Delta t$ and its average is $I = pe/\Delta t$. Different intervals are independent, while in the same interval $s_i^2 = s_i$:

<Formula label="Current correlations" tex="\langle I_iI_j\rangle = \begin{cases} (pe/\Delta t)^2 = I^2 & i \neq j \\ (e/\Delta t)^2p = eI/\Delta t & i = j \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="p. 123" />

The excess correlation sits in a single interval and sums to $\sum_j(\langle I_iI_j\rangle - I^2) = eI/\Delta t$ (for $p \ll 1$; exactly, there is a factor $1 - p$), so $\int dt\,[\langle I(t)I(0)\rangle - I^2] = eI$ (notes p. 123, which leave out the $-I^2$ in this integral). In the limit of short intervals,

<Formula label="Correlation function of a current of charges" tex="G(t) = \langle I(t)I(0)\rangle = I^2 + eI\,\delta(t)" cite="reading1" at="p. 123" />

and the power spectrum is (notes p. 123)

<Formula label="Shot noise" tex="\frac{S(\omega)}{\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left[2\pi I^2\delta(\omega) + eI\right] = 2I^2\delta(\omega) + \frac{eI}{\pi}" cite="reading1" at="p. 123" />

The first term is the steady current, at zero frequency. The second is **shot noise**: white, independent of $\omega$, and proportional to the charge $e$ of the carriers, so measuring it measures the charge (notes p. 123). Per unit ordinary frequency $f = \omega/2\pi$ it is $2eI$, Schottky's formula (the name is the page's addition). For a current of 1 mA, $2eI \approx 3.2 \times 10^{-22}$ A² per hertz, so over a bandwidth of 1 MHz the current fluctuates by about 18 nA rms.

## Velocity noise of a Brownian particle

Back to the Langevin equation with a random force, $dv/dt + \gamma v = F(t)/m$, $\langle F\rangle = 0$ (notes p. 124). In frequency space it is algebraic, $-i\omega v(\omega) + \gamma v(\omega) = F(\omega)/m$, so $v(\omega) = F(\omega)/m(\gamma - i\omega)$. The averages $\langle v(\omega)v(-\omega)\rangle$ and $\langle F(\omega)F(-\omega)\rangle$ are each $t_\infty$ times the Fourier transform of the corresponding correlation function, and the velocity spectrum is (notes p. 124)

<Formula label="Velocity noise spectrum" tex="\frac{S(\omega)}{\pi} = \frac{1}{\pi}\,\frac{\langle v(\omega)v(-\omega)\rangle}{t_\infty} = \frac{1}{\pi}\,\frac{K(\omega)}{m^2(\gamma^2 + \omega^2)}, \qquad K(\omega) = \int d\tau\,\langle F(\tau)F(0)\rangle e^{i\omega\tau}" cite="reading1" at="p. 124" />

If the force is white noise, $\langle F(t_1)F(t_2)\rangle = 2\pi K\,\delta(t_1 - t_2)$ with a constant $K$, then $K(\omega) = 2\pi K$ (notes p. 125). The friction formula of the previous section fixes $K$: $K_\gamma = \int_0^\infty 2\pi K\delta(\tau)e^{-\gamma\tau}d\tau = \pi K$ (half the delta function lies at $\tau > 0$), so $m\gamma = \pi K/k_BT$. Then (notes p. 125)

<Formula label="Power spectrum of the velocity" tex="P^v(\omega) = \frac{S(\omega)}{\pi} = \frac{2k_BTm\gamma}{\pi m^2(\gamma^2 + \omega^2)} = \frac{2k_BT\gamma}{\pi m(\gamma^2 + \omega^2)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 125" />

As a check, the total over all frequencies gives back equipartition, $\langle v^2\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,\frac{\langle v(\omega)v(-\omega)\rangle}{t_\infty} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega\,\frac{k_BT\gamma}{\pi m(\gamma^2 + \omega^2)} = \frac{k_BT}{m}$ (notes p. 125). The power spectrum of the velocity is determined by the friction $\gamma$, the mass $m$ and the temperature $T$.

## Johnson noise in a circuit

A circuit is a Brownian particle too (notes p. 126). Take an inductor $L$ in parallel with a capacitor $C$ and a resistor $R$, with charge $q$ on the capacitor, current $I$ through the inductor and voltage $V$ across all three. Then $V = q/C = L\,dI/dt$ and $I = -dq/dt - V/R$. With $x = LI$, so that $\dot x = V$:

<Formula label="Circuit as an oscillator" tex="C\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\frac{x}{L} - \frac{1}{R}\frac{dx}{dt}, \qquad E = \frac{1}{2}LI^2 + \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \frac{1}{2L}x^2 + \frac{1}{2}C\dot x^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 126" />

This is a particle of "mass" $C$ in the potential $x^2/2L$, with friction $m\gamma = 1/R$, that is $\gamma = 1/RC$ (notes p. 126). The thermal agitation of the electrons in the resistor is the random force. Let $L \to \infty$, leaving just $R$ and $C$; the power spectrum of $V = \dot x$ is the velocity spectrum of the previous section with $m = C$ and $\gamma = 1/RC$ (notes p. 126):

<Formula label="Johnson noise" tex="P^V(\omega) = \frac{2k_BT/RC}{\pi C\left[(1/RC)^2 + \omega^2\right]} = \frac{2k_BTR}{\pi(1 + \omega^2R^2C^2)}, \qquad P^V(f) = 2\pi P^V(\omega) = \frac{4k_BTR}{1 + \omega^2R^2C^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 126" />

At low frequency, per unit ordinary frequency $f$, every resistor shows a voltage noise $4k_BTR$, whatever it is made of. This is **Johnson–Nyquist noise** (the names are the page's addition). The capacitance cuts it off above $\omega \sim 1/RC$, and the total over all frequencies is $\langle V^2\rangle = k_BT/C$, equipartition for the energy $\frac{1}{2}CV^2$.

  caption="Computed from P^V(f) = 4k_BTR/(1 + ω²R²C²): flat, white noise of 4k_BTR at low frequency, cut off above ω = 1/RC by the capacitance. The area under the curve, 4k_BTR × 1/4RC, is the equipartition value ⟨V²⟩ = k_BT/C." />

For example, a 1 MΩ resistor at 300 K measured over a bandwidth of 10 kHz shows $\sqrt{4k_BTR\Delta f} \approx 13$ μV rms, if the capacitance is ignored. With a stray capacitance of 10 pF the cutoff $1/2\pi RC$ is at about 16 kHz, so the 10 kHz band is not quite flat and holds about 12 μV, and the noise over all frequencies is $\sqrt{k_BT/C} \approx 20$ μV.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Noise and the cosmic microwave background">
Thermal noise is what radio astronomers measure against. When Penzias and Wilson found the cosmic microwave background in 1965, the signal was a hiss in their receiver that they had to tell apart from electrical noise, which they did partly by comparing it with a load cooled by liquid helium, as told in [MIT 8.286 Lecture 18](/courses/8-286/cmb-spectrum). The physics of this page explains why that comparison works: a resistor at temperature $T$ produces noise in proportion to $T$, whatever it is made of, so a noise power can be quoted as a temperature, and the sky's extra hiss corresponded to about 3.5 K. (The connection is the page's own; the lecture tells the history.)
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Einstein relation**: a particle with mobility $\eta$ ($u = \eta F$) diffuses with $D = \eta k_BT$, required for the drift-diffusion equation to give the Boltzmann distribution. Fluctuation (diffusion) and dissipation (friction) go together.
- With Stokes's law $\eta = 1/6\pi a\nu$, a sphere in water wanders $\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle = k_BTt/\pi a\nu$, about 8.9 μm in a minute for $a = 1$ μm; measuring it gives $k_B$ and $N_A$.
- **Langevin equation** $dv/dt + \gamma v = F_\text{ex}/m + \tilde F/m$: $\langle x^2\rangle = (2k_BT/m\gamma)[t - (1 - e^{-\gamma t})/\gamma]$, ballistic ($k_BTt^2/m$) at short times and diffusive ($2Dt$) at long times. The friction is fixed by the force correlations, $m\gamma = \frac{1}{k_BT}\int_0^\infty K(s)e^{-\gamma s}ds$.
- The **power spectrum** is the Fourier transform of the correlation function, $S(\omega) = \int dt\,G(t)e^{i\omega t}$; uncorrelated noise is **white**.
- **Shot noise**: $G = I^2 + eI\delta(t)$, a white spectrum $eI/\pi$ proportional to the charge. **Johnson noise**: $P^V(f) = 4k_BTR/(1 + \omega^2R^2C^2)$, set only by the temperature and the resistance.
