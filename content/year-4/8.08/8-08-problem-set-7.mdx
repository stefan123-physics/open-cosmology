This set practises the Bose gases of Chapter VII of Wen's notes: the [ideal Bose gas and its condensation](/courses/8-08/ideal-bose-gas-condensation), the [photon and phonon gases](/courses/8-08/photon-and-phonon-gases) with the Debye model of a solid, and the sound waves of a [condensate of interacting bosons](/courses/8-08/interacting-bosons-gross-pitaevskii). It goes with MIT's Problem Set 7 (dated March 16, 2005). MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

**Every problem on this page is our own.** MIT's sheet has four problems, and each of them gives only the number of a problem in a textbook (K. Huang, *Statistical Mechanics*), with no text. The textbook is not used here. In its place, each of MIT's four numbers gets an original problem on a subject of Chapter VII, the chapter the set belongs to by its date: an ideal Bose gas of rubidium atoms at and below its condensation (Problem 1), an ideal Bose gas on a surface (Problem 2), the Debye model of a solid at high temperature (Problem 3), and the sound quanta of liquid helium (Problem 4). The models, the numbers and the questions are all ours, and they avoid the problems of the earlier sets: Problem Set 6 already had the fluctuations of a Bose level and the photon gas of an expanding universe.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it; for very large or small numbers type a power of ten as `3.1e-12`. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `a*(b*T)^4/(c + d)^2`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `kB` stands for $k_B$, `hbar` for $\hbar$, `Tc` for $T_c$, `TD` for $T_D$, `lam` for $\lambda$, `pi` is π, `sqrt` the square root and `exp` the exponential; the other letters (`n`, `T`, `v`) are typed as they are.

MIT's sheet gives no points. The points are ours: 20 for Problem 1, 15 for Problem 2, 20 for Problem 3 and 15 for Problem 4, 70 points in all; every split between parts is ours too. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes. $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature, $\beta = 1/k_BT$, $\mu$ the chemical potential, $z = e^{\beta\mu}$ the fugacity, $\lambda = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT}$ the thermal wavelength of a boson of mass $m$, $n$ the number of bosons per unit volume (per unit area in Problem 2), and $g_\nu(z) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty z^j/j^\nu$, with $g_{3/2}(1) = 2.612$ and $g_{5/2}(1) = 1.341$. $U$ is the internal energy, $P$ the pressure, $S$ the entropy and $C_V$ the heat capacity at fixed volume. In Problems 3 and 4, $v$ is a speed of sound and $T_D$ the Debye temperature. Masses are in atomic mass units, $1$ u $= 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}$ kg.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A rubidium gas at its condensation (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the condensation, energy, pressure and entropy of the ideal Bose gas (notes pp. 55–60).

A gas of rubidium-87 atoms (our values: mass $86.909$ u, density $n = 1.0 \times 10^{20}$ m⁻³) is held at a fixed density in a large box. Treat it as an ideal Bose gas of spinless bosons with $\varepsilon_k = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ (our assumption: real atoms in a trap interact weakly and sit in a non-uniform potential). From the notes, the gas obeys

<Formula label="Equations of state of the ideal Bose gas" tex="\frac{U}{V} = \frac32\,k_BT\,\frac{g_{5/2}(z)}{\lambda^3}, \qquad P = \frac23\,\frac{U}{V}, \qquad n\lambda^3 = g_{3/2}(z) \ \text{ or } \ z = 1" cite="reading1" at="p. 60" />

where $z = 1$ holds in the condensed phase, below the condensation temperature $T_c$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find $T_c$, in nanokelvin (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

At $T_c$ the gas phase just reaches $z = 1$, so $n\lambda^3 = g_{3/2}(1)$. Solve that for $T$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-tc" label="$T_c$ in nK:" value="398" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-tc" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Condensation begins when the density of the gas phase cannot grow any further, at $z = 1$.

<Step title="The formula">

With $\lambda^3 = (2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT)^{3/2}$, the condition $n\lambda^3 = g_{3/2}(1)$ gives

<Formula label="Condensation temperature" tex="k_BT_c = \frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m}\left(\frac{n}{g_{3/2}(1)}\right)^{2/3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 57" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$m = 86.909 \times 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}$ kg $= 1.443 \times 10^{-25}$ kg, so $2\pi\hbar^2/(mk_B) = 3.507 \times 10^{-20}$ K m², and $(n/2.612)^{2/3} = 1.136 \times 10^{13}$ m⁻². Then $T_c = 3.98 \times 10^{-7}$ K $= 398$ nK.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $T_c$ the thermal wavelength is $\lambda = (2.612/n)^{1/3} = 0.297$ μm, a little more than the spacing between atoms, $n^{-1/3} = 0.215$ μm: the waves of neighbouring atoms overlap. The notes' own example (reading 1, p. 61), sodium atoms at $10^{14}$ cm⁻³, condenses near $1.5$ μK: a denser gas of lighter atoms condenses at a higher temperature, as $T_c \propto n^{2/3}/m$ says.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the energy per atom at $T = T_c$, as the decimal $U/(Nk_BT_c)$ (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

Divide $U/V$ by $n$, and write $n$ with the third equation at $z = 1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-u" label="$U/(Nk_BT_c)$:" value="0.770" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-u" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At $T_c$ both $U/V$ and $n$ are fixed by $z = 1$, so their ratio is a pure number times $k_BT_c$.

<Step title="The ratio">

$\dfrac{U}{N} = \dfrac{\frac32k_BT\,g_{5/2}(1)/\lambda^3}{g_{3/2}(1)/\lambda^3} = \dfrac32\,\dfrac{g_{5/2}(1)}{g_{3/2}(1)}\,k_BT_c = \dfrac32 \times \dfrac{1.341}{2.612}\,k_BT_c = 0.770\,k_BT_c$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A classical gas has $\frac32k_BT$ per atom. The Bose gas has about half of that: bosons crowd into the low levels, which the Bose distribution fills more than the Boltzmann factor does. At high temperature, where $g_{5/2}(z) \approx g_{3/2}(z) \approx z$, the ratio returns to $\frac32$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the pressure at $T = T_c$, as the decimal $P/(nk_BT_c)$ (tolerance 1%). Then give $P$ itself, in pascals (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

$P = \frac23U/V$: use part (b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-p" label="$P/(nk_BT_c)$:" value="0.514" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-p" />

<Answer id="p1-pa" label="$P$ in Pa:" value="2.82e-10" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-pa" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The pressure is two thirds of the energy density, so the result of part (b) carries over with $\frac23$.

<Step title="The ratio">

$P/(nk_BT_c) = \frac23 \times 0.770 = g_{5/2}(1)/g_{3/2}(1) = 1.341/2.612 = 0.514$.

</Step>

<Step title="The pressure">

$P = 0.514 \times 1.0 \times 10^{20} \times 1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 3.98 \times 10^{-7}$ Pa $= 2.82 \times 10^{-10}$ Pa.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

An ideal classical gas at the same density and temperature would push with $nk_BT_c = 5.50 \times 10^{-10}$ Pa, about twice as much. The pressure is tiny because both the density and the temperature are tiny. Such gases are held by magnetic or optical traps rather than by walls, because any wall would be far hotter than the gas.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

(Our addition.) Below $T_c$, find the entropy as a fraction of its value at $T_c$: $S(T)/S(T_c)$, for $T < T_c$ at the same density.

<Hint>

At $\mu = 0$ the entropy follows from $TS = U + PV - \mu N$, a relation of the grand canonical ensemble (stated by us). Write $S$ with $U$ alone.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the condensed phase $z = 1$, so $U$ depends on $T$ only through $T/\lambda^3$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-s" label="$S(T)/S(T_c) =$" expr="(T/Tc)^(3/2)"
  vars={{ T: [0.2, 0.9], Tc: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ T: "T", Tc: "T_c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(T/Tc)^(5/2)", hint: "That is how the energy U falls below T_c. The entropy is S = (5/3)U/T, which has one power of T fewer." },
    { expr: "1", hint: "That would keep the entropy at its value at T_c. Below T_c the atoms in excited levels, which carry all the entropy, become fewer as the gas cools." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $\mu = 0$, $TS = U + PV$, and $PV = \frac23U$, so the entropy is $\frac53U/T$.

<Step title="The entropy">

<Formula label="Entropy of the condensed Bose gas" tex="S = \frac{U + PV}{T} = \frac53\,\frac{U}{T} = \frac52\,k_BV\,\frac{g_{5/2}(1)}{\lambda^3} \propto T^{3/2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

since $\lambda^{-3} \propto T^{3/2}$. So $S(T)/S(T_c) = (T/T_c)^{3/2}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Per atom, at $T_c$, $S/(Nk_B) = \frac52 \times 1.341/2.612 = 1.28$. Below $T_c$, the entropy is proportional to $1 - N_0/N = (T/T_c)^{3/2}$, the fraction of atoms that are not in the condensate: each excited atom carries the same entropy, $1.28\,k_B$, and the condensate carries none. It is one quantum state, with one way of being arranged. As $T \to 0$, $S \to 0$, as the third law requires.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={4}>

(Our addition.) The gas is below $T_c$. It is now compressed slowly to half its volume at a fixed temperature, and it stays below $T_c$. What happens to its pressure?

<Answer id="p1-iso" label="During the compression, the pressure" choices={["doubles, as for any ideal gas at a fixed temperature", "rises as the density to the power 5/3", "stays the same: extra atoms join the condensate", "falls, because the condensate pulls the atoms together"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the condensed phase $z = 1$, and then the pressure depends on the temperature alone.

<Step title="The pressure below T_c">

With $z = 1$, $P = k_BT\,g_{5/2}(1)/\lambda^3$, which contains $T$ but not the density (notes p. 60). Compressing at a fixed temperature leaves the density of atoms in excited levels at $g_{3/2}(1)/\lambda^3$, and every extra atom per unit volume goes into the $\vec k = 0$ level, which has no momentum and pushes on nothing.

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

The isotherm is flat, like the coexistence region of a liquid and its vapour, as in Wen's sketch of the isotherms. The condensate is the "liquid", with no volume of its own in this ideal model. Halving the volume at $T = 0.5\,T_c$, for example, raises $N_0/N$ from $1 - 0.5^{3/2} = 0.65$ to $1 - 0.5^{3/2}/2 = 0.82$ without changing $P$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Bosons on a surface (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own: Wen's way of fixing the chemical potential (notes p. 56), repeated in two dimensions.

$N$ bosons of mass $m$ move freely on a flat square surface of side $L$, with $\varepsilon_k = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ and a two-dimensional wave vector $\vec k$ (our model; atoms held on a surface by a strong force across it). They form an ideal Bose gas at the temperature $T$, with the density $n = N/L^2$ per unit area. As in three dimensions, the sum over levels becomes an integral, now $L^2\int d^2k/(2\pi)^2$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the fugacity $z$ in terms of $n\lambda^2$.

<Hint>

Write $N = L^2\int\frac{d^2k}{(2\pi)^2}\,\frac{1}{z^{-1}e^{\beta\varepsilon_k} - 1}$ and change from $k$ to $\varepsilon$: in two dimensions $d^2k = 2\pi k\,dk$, and $k\,dk = m\,d\varepsilon/\hbar^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The integral $\int_0^\infty dx\,\frac{1}{z^{-1}e^x - 1}$ can be done in closed form: multiply the top and bottom by $ze^{-x}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-z" label="$z =$" expr="1 - exp(-n*lam^2)"
  vars={{ n: [0.5, 2], lam: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ n: "n", lam: "\\lambda" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "n*lam^2", hint: "That is the classical limit, where the −1 in the Bose distribution is dropped. It holds only for nλ² ≪ 1; for nλ² > 1 it would even give z > 1, which a Bose gas cannot have." },
    { expr: "exp(-n*lam^2)", hint: "That is 1 − z, the distance of the fugacity from 1, not z itself." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In two dimensions the density of states of free particles is constant, and the integral that fixes $z$ becomes elementary.

<Step title="Change to energies">

With $d^2k = 2\pi k\,dk$ and $k\,dk = m\,d\varepsilon/\hbar^2$,

<Formula label="Number of bosons on a surface" tex="N = \frac{L^2}{2\pi}\int_0^\infty k\,dk\,\frac{1}{z^{-1}e^{\beta\varepsilon_k} - 1} = \frac{L^2m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int_0^\infty\frac{d\varepsilon}{z^{-1}e^{\beta\varepsilon} - 1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The number of levels per unit energy, $L^2m/2\pi\hbar^2$, does not depend on $\varepsilon$.

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

With $x = \beta\varepsilon$, $\int_0^\infty\frac{ze^{-x}\,dx}{1 - ze^{-x}} = \Big[\ln(1 - ze^{-x})\Big]_0^\infty = -\ln(1 - z)$. Since $mk_BT/2\pi\hbar^2 = 1/\lambda^2$,

<Formula label="Density of a Bose gas on a surface" tex="n\lambda^2 = -\ln(1 - z) = g_1(z), \qquad z = 1 - e^{-n\lambda^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $n\lambda^2 \ll 1$, $z \approx n\lambda^2$: the classical gas, the two-dimensional form of the notes' $n\lambda^3 = z$ (p. 56). The series $-\ln(1 - z) = z + z^2/2 + z^3/3 + \cdots$ is $g_1(z)$, the member $\nu = 1$ of the family $g_\nu$ (notes p. 59), as $g_{3/2}$ is in three dimensions.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Does this gas condense: is there a temperature below which the equation for $z$ has no solution, as in three dimensions?

<Answer id="p2-bec" label="The two-dimensional ideal Bose gas" choices={["condenses below the temperature where nλ² = 2.612", "condenses below the temperature where nλ² = 1", "condenses at every temperature, since the density of states is constant", "never condenses: some z below 1 always solves it"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In three dimensions condensation happens because $g_{3/2}(z)$ stays finite as $z \to 1$. Ask whether $g_1(z)$ does.

<Step title="The function">

$g_1(z) = -\ln(1 - z)$ grows without bound as $z \to 1$. So for any density and any temperature, however low, $z = 1 - e^{-n\lambda^2}$ is a solution below 1. The excited levels can hold any number of bosons, and no finite fraction is ever forced into the lowest level.

</Step>

<Step title="Why">

At small energies the Bose distribution is about $k_BT/(\varepsilon - \mu)$. In three dimensions the density of states goes to zero as $\sqrt\varepsilon$, and the integral stays finite at $\mu = 0$; in two dimensions it is constant, and $\int d\varepsilon/\varepsilon$ diverges at the lower end. The low levels, filled more and more as $\mu \to 0$, can always take the extra bosons.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

(Our numbers.) Take $n\lambda^2 = 4$. Give the chemical potential as the decimal $\mu/k_BT$ (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

$\mu = k_BT\ln z$, with $z$ from part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-mu" label="$\mu/k_BT$:" value="-0.01849" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-mu" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A high density makes $z$ close to 1 and $\mu$ close to zero, but never zero.

<Step title="The numbers">

$1 - z = e^{-4} = 0.01832$, so $z = 0.98168$ and $\mu/k_BT = \ln 0.98168 = -0.01849$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The lowest level holds $z/(1 - z) = e^4 - 1 = 53.6$ bosons on average (the occupation of the $\varepsilon = 0$ level, as in the notes' finite system on p. 58). In a sample of, say, $10^5$ bosons that is a tiny fraction: nothing like a condensate. At this density a three-dimensional gas, with $n\lambda^3$ above 2.612, would hold a finite fraction of its bosons in the lowest level.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The Debye solid when it is warm (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 3">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the Debye model of a solid (notes pp. 53–54), at temperatures above the Debye temperature.

In the Debye model, a solid of $N$ atoms has the energy per atom

<Formula label="Energy per atom in the Debye model" tex="\frac{U}{N} = 3k_BT\,\mathcal D(u), \qquad \mathcal D(u) = \frac{3}{u^3}\int_0^u dt\,\frac{t^3}{e^t - 1}, \qquad u = \frac{T_D}{T}" cite="reading1" at="p. 54" />

where $U$ is the energy of the vibrations as the notes define it and $T_D$ is the Debye temperature.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

For $T \gg T_D$, find the first two terms of $U/N$: the leading term and the first correction to it.

<Hint>

For small $t$, $\frac{t^3}{e^t - 1} = t^2 - \frac{t^3}{2} + \frac{t^4}{12} - \cdots$ (a standard series, stated by us). Integrate term by term from $0$ to $u$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-u" label="$U/N \approx$" expr="3*kB*T - 9*kB*TD/8"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [5, 10], TD: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T", TD: "T_D" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*kB*T - 3*kB*TD/8", hint: "That multiplies 3u/8 by k_BT instead of 3k_BT: 3k_BT × 3u/8 = (9/8)k_BT_D." },
    { expr: "3*kB*T", hint: "That is only the leading term, the classical value of equipartition. The question asks for the next term too: keep the term linear in u in 𝒟(u)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Expand the integrand for small $t$, since at high temperature the upper limit $u$ is small.

<Step title="The expansion">

$\int_0^u(t^2 - t^3/2 + t^4/12)\,dt = u^3/3 - u^4/8 + u^5/60$, so $\mathcal D(u) = 1 - \frac38u + \frac{1}{20}u^2 - \cdots$. The first two terms are the ones in the notes (p. 54).

</Step>

<Step title="The energy">

<Formula label="Energy per atom at high temperature" tex="\frac{U}{N} = 3k_BT\left(1 - \frac38\,\frac{T_D}{T} + \cdots\right) = 3k_BT - \frac98\,k_BT_D + \cdots" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Units: both terms are energies. The correction does not depend on $T$, so it is a fixed offset of the energy and adds nothing to the heat capacity, which is still $3Nk_B$ to this order: the law of Dulong and Petit. Part (d) asks what the offset is.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the first correction to the law of Dulong and Petit: $C_V/(3Nk_B)$ to second order in $T_D/T$.

<Hint>

Keep the term in $u^2$ of $\mathcal D(u)$ from part (a), multiply by $3k_BT$, and differentiate with respect to $T$ at a fixed $T_D$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="$C_V/(3Nk_B) \approx$" expr="1 - TD^2/(20*T^2)"
  vars={{ TD: [1, 2], T: [5, 10] }}
  names={{ TD: "T_D", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1 - 3*TD/(8*T)", hint: "That is 𝒟(u) to first order, which gives U/(3Nk_BT), not the heat capacity. The term linear in u becomes a constant in U and carries no heat." },
    { expr: "1 + TD^2/(20*T^2)", hint: "The sign is wrong. The term (3/20)k_BT_D²/T in U/N falls as T rises, so its derivative is negative: quantum effects make a solid hold less heat than the classical 3Nk_B, never more." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only the terms of $U$ that change with $T$ contribute to $C_V$.

<Step title="The energy to the next order">

$\dfrac{U}{N} = 3k_BT\left(1 - \dfrac38u + \dfrac{1}{20}u^2\right) = 3k_BT - \dfrac98k_BT_D + \dfrac{3}{20}\,\dfrac{k_BT_D^2}{T}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The heat capacity">

<Formula label="Heat capacity of a warm Debye solid" tex="\frac{C_V}{N} = 3k_B - \frac{3}{20}\,k_B\,\frac{T_D^2}{T^2}, \qquad \frac{C_V}{3Nk_B} = 1 - \frac{1}{20}\left(\frac{T_D}{T}\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $T = T_D$ this gives $0.950$; the exact Debye heat capacity there, $\frac{3}{u^3}\int_0^u t^4e^t\,dt/(e^t - 1)^2$ at $u = 1$, is $0.952$. The heat capacity approaches $3Nk_B$ from below, as Wen's sketch of $C_V/3Nk_B$ shows.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

(Our addition.) According to part (b), above what temperature is $C_V$ within 1% of $3Nk_B$? Give $T/T_D$ as a decimal (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

Set the correction of part (b) equal to $0.01$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-t99" label="$T/T_D$:" value="2.236" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-t99" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The correction falls as the square of $T_D/T$, so it is small already a little above $T_D$.

<Step title="The temperature">

$\frac{1}{20}(T_D/T)^2 = 0.01$ gives $(T/T_D)^2 = 5$ and $T/T_D = \sqrt5 = 2.236$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The exact Debye heat capacity at $T = 2.236\,T_D$ is $0.9901 \times 3Nk_B$, so the approximation of part (b) is good here. For a solid with $T_D$ near room temperature or below, $C_V$ is within about 5 per cent of $3Nk_B$ at room temperature, which is why the law of Dulong and Petit was found long before quantum mechanics.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

(Our addition.) What is the energy $\frac98k_BT_D$ of part (a)?

<Answer id="p3-zp" label="The energy (9/8)k_BT_D per atom is" choices={["the zero-point energy of the vibrations, per atom", "the average energy of one phonon at the temperature T = T_D", "the binding energy of one atom", "the heat needed to warm the solid from absolute zero to T_D"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The classical result $3k_BT$ counts the full energy of each oscillator. The $U$ of the notes leaves out the zero-point energy $\frac12\varepsilon$ of every mode (notes p. 49). So $U$ must fall short of $3k_BT$ by the zero-point energy.

<Step title="The zero-point energy">

The Debye density of states, three polarisations included, is $D(\varepsilon) = 9N\varepsilon^2/\varepsilon_{\text{max}}^3$ for $\varepsilon < \varepsilon_{\text{max}} = k_BT_D$ (notes p. 54, written with $\int D\,d\varepsilon = 3N$). The zero-point energy is

<Formula label="Zero-point energy of a Debye solid" tex="E_0 = \int_0^{\varepsilon_{\text{max}}} d\varepsilon\,D(\varepsilon)\,\frac{\varepsilon}{2} = \frac{9N}{2\varepsilon_{\text{max}}^3}\,\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{max}}^4}{4} = \frac98\,Nk_BT_D" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

exactly the offset of part (a).

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

At high temperature each mode holds an energy close to $k_BT$ in all, zero-point energy included, as equipartition says; the notes' $U$, which starts from zero at $T = 0$, is that total minus $E_0$. The other choices are not this energy: a phonon at $T_D$ has an energy of order $k_BT_D$, not a fixed $\frac98k_BT_D$ per atom; the binding energy of a crystal is not in the Debye model at all; and the heat needed to warm the solid to $T_D$ is $U$ at $T_D$, $3Nk_BT_D\,\mathcal D(1) = 2.02\,Nk_BT_D$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Sound quanta in liquid helium (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 4">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the heat capacity of a condensate of interacting bosons, whose lowest excitations are sound waves (notes pp. 60 and 69), counted like the phonons of a solid (pp. 53–54).

Liquid helium-4 near absolute zero (our values: mass density $0.145$ g/cm³, so $n = 2.18 \times 10^{28}$ atoms per m³ with a mass of $4.0026$ u each, and speed of sound $v = 238$ m/s) has as its lowest excitations quantised sound waves with $\varepsilon_k = \hbar v|\vec k|$. A liquid has no stiffness against shear, so it carries only longitudinal sound: one polarisation (our assumption, true of a liquid). Take the temperature low enough that the sound waves are the only excitations that matter and the largest wave vector plays no part (our assumption).

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the energy per unit volume $U/V$ of the sound quanta at the temperature $T$.

<Hint>

Count as for photons: $U = V\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{\varepsilon_k}{e^{\beta\varepsilon_k} - 1}$, with one polarisation and $v$ in place of $c$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With $x = \beta\hbar vk$ the integral becomes $\int_0^\infty x^3\,dx/(e^x - 1) = \pi^4/15$ (notes p. 51).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-u" label="$U/V =$" expr="pi^2*(kB*T)^4/(30*(hbar*v)^3)"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 1.5], v: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T", hbar: "\\hbar", v: "v" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi^2*(kB*T)^4/(10*(hbar*v)^3)", hint: "That counts three polarisations, as for the phonons of a solid. A liquid cannot carry transverse sound: only the longitudinal wave is left." },
    { expr: "pi^2*(kB*T)^4/(15*(hbar*v)^3)", hint: "That counts two polarisations, as for photons, the notes' σT⁴ with v in place of c. Sound in a liquid has only one." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A gas of sound quanta is a photon gas with a slower speed and a different count of polarisations.

<Step title="The integral">

With $d^3k = 4\pi k^2\,dk$ and $x = \beta\hbar vk$,

<Formula label="Energy of the sound quanta of a liquid" tex="\frac{U}{V} = \frac{4\pi}{(2\pi)^3}\int_0^\infty dk\,\frac{\hbar vk^3}{e^{\beta\hbar vk} - 1} = \frac{(k_BT)^4}{2\pi^2(\hbar v)^3}\int_0^\infty dx\,\frac{x^3}{e^x - 1} = \frac{\pi^2}{30}\,\frac{(k_BT)^4}{(\hbar v)^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Two polarisations and $v = c$ give twice this, $\frac{\pi^2}{15}(k_BT)^4/(\hbar c)^3 = \sigma T^4$, the energy density of the photon gas (notes p. 51). Three polarisations give the low-temperature Debye result: with $k_BT_D = \hbar v(6\pi^2n)^{1/3}$, $3 \times \frac{\pi^2}{30}(k_BT)^4/(\hbar v)^3 = \frac{3\pi^4}{5}\,nk_BT^4/T_D^3$ (notes p. 54).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

(Our numbers.) At $T = 0.30$ K, give the heat capacity per atom, $C_V/(Nk_B)$ (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

$C_V = \partial U/\partial T$ at a fixed volume, and $U \propto T^4$. Divide by $Nk_B = nVk_B$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c" label="$C_V/(Nk_B)$:" value="2.71e-4" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Differentiate the $T^4$ law, then count per atom.

<Step title="The heat capacity">

$C_V/V = 4U/(VT) = \frac{2\pi^2}{15}\,k_B\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar v}\right)^3$, so $\dfrac{C_V}{Nk_B} = \dfrac{2\pi^2}{15n}\left(\dfrac{k_BT}{\hbar v}\right)^3$.

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$k_BT/\hbar v = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 0.30/(1.0546 \times 10^{-34} \times 238)$ m⁻¹ $= 1.650 \times 10^8$ m⁻¹; cubed, $4.494 \times 10^{24}$ m⁻³. Times $2\pi^2/15 = 1.316$ and divided by $n = 2.18 \times 10^{28}$ m⁻³: $C_V/(Nk_B) = 2.71 \times 10^{-4}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The thermal wave vector $1.65 \times 10^8$ m⁻¹ is 66 times smaller than the largest wave vector the atoms allow, $(6\pi^2n)^{1/3} = 1.09 \times 10^{10}$ m⁻¹, so leaving out the cutoff was right. The heat capacity is tiny: at $0.30$ K only long sound waves are excited, with wavelengths of order $2\pi/(1.65 \times 10^8\ \text{m}^{-1}) = 38$ nm, about a hundred times the spacing between atoms, $n^{-1/3} = 0.36$ nm.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

(Our addition.) Compare an ideal Bose gas of atoms of the same mass and density. Give its heat capacity per atom $C_V/(Nk_B)$ at $T = 0.30$ K (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

First find its condensation temperature, as in Problem 1. Then use the notes' heat capacity of the condensed phase, $C_V = \frac{15}{4}k_BV\,g_{5/2}(1)/\lambda^3$, and write it per atom with $n\lambda_c^3 = g_{3/2}(1)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-cid" label="$C_V/(Nk_B)$ of the ideal Bose gas:" value="0.0570" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-cid" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Below $T_c$ the ideal gas has $C_V \propto T^{3/2}$, which falls much more slowly than the $T^3$ of sound waves.

<Step title="The condensation temperature">

$m = 4.0026 \times 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}$ kg $= 6.646 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, and $k_BT_c = (2\pi\hbar^2/m)(n/2.612)^{2/3}$ gives $T_c = 3.13$ K. (At the smaller density $0.12$ g/cm³ of the notes, p. 61, the same formula gives about $2.8$ K; the notes print 1.8 K there, a slip.) So $0.30$ K is far below $T_c$.

</Step>

<Step title="The heat capacity">

Per atom, with $\lambda^{-3} = \lambda_c^{-3}(T/T_c)^{3/2}$ and $n\lambda_c^3 = g_{3/2}(1)$,

<Formula label="Heat capacity per boson below the transition" tex="\frac{C_V}{Nk_B} = \frac{15}{4}\,\frac{g_{5/2}(1)}{g_{3/2}(1)}\left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{3/2} = 1.926\left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{3/2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 60 (written per boson by us)" />

and $(0.30/3.13)^{3/2} = 0.0296$, so $C_V/(Nk_B) = 0.0570$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The ideal gas would hold about 210 times more heat per degree than the sound waves at $0.30$ K. Measured liquid helium at such temperatures follows the $T^3$ law of parts (a) and (b), not the $T^{3/2}$ of the ideal gas: the repulsion between the atoms turns the lowest excitations from free particles, $\hbar^2k^2/2m$, into sound, $\hbar v|\vec k|$, as Wen's interacting condensate shows (notes p. 69), and the last line of his table of low-temperature laws says (p. 60).

</Step>

</Solution>
