The [previous page](/courses/8-08/grand-partition-function) built the grand canonical ensemble and the thermodynamic potential $\Omega = A - \mu N$. Point ④ of Xiao-Gang Wen's notes (pp. 31–35) puts it to work on four problems that look unrelated: a gas spread over two regions, atoms sticking to a surface, a chemical reaction, and the one-dimensional Ising magnet. All four are solved by one rule: **systems that exchange particles are in equilibrium when their chemical potentials are equal**, and a reaction is in equilibrium when the chemical potentials, counted with the reaction's coefficients, add up to zero. (Everything here is in our own words, with our own examples; Wen's numbers are quoted as his.)

## Two gases at different potential energies

Two regions of a classical gas at the same temperature exchange molecules, but in region 2 each molecule has an extra potential energy $V_0$, for instance because it is higher up in a gravitational field (notes p. 31, point ④ⓐ; Wen's sketch shows the potential as a step). How are the densities $n_1$ and $n_2$ related?

Wen's trick is to let each region exchange particles with a common reservoir of chemical potential $\mu$. For region 1, with Chapter I's free energy of the ideal gas,

<Formula label="Ω of region 1" tex="\Omega_1 = A_1(V, T, N_1) - \mu N_1 = N_1k_BT\left(\ln n_1\lambda^3 - 1\right) - \mu N_1" cite="reading1" at="p. 31, point ④ⓐ" />

with $\lambda = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT}$. The probability of $N_1$ is proportional to $e^{-\Omega_1/k_BT}$, so the equilibrium $N_1$ minimises $\Omega_1$:

<Formula label="Chemical potential of an ideal gas" tex="\left.\frac{\partial A_1}{\partial N_1}\right|_{V,T} = \mu = k_BT\left[\ln(n_1\lambda^3) - 1\right] + k_BT \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mu = k_BT\ln(n_1\lambda^3), \qquad n_1 = \frac{1}{\lambda^3}\,e^{\mu/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 31, point ④ⓐ" />

This is the **chemical potential of the ideal gas**. Since a classical gas has $n\lambda^3 \ll 1$, $\mu$ is negative, and large in magnitude compared with $k_BT$. Putting it back gives

<Formula label="Ω of the ideal gas" tex="\Omega = Nk_BT\left(\frac{\mu}{k_BT} - 1\right) - \mu N = -Nk_BT = -k_BT\,\frac{V}{\lambda^3}\,e^{\mu/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 31, point ④ⓐ" />

which agrees with $\Omega = -PV$ and $P = nk_BT$, and with the exact sum on the previous page. (In the notes the last form of this line, and the boxed version beside it, leave out the factor $k_BT$; the middle step $-Nk_BT$ has it.)

In region 2 the energy of every state is shifted by $N_2V_0$, and since $A = E - ST$, so is the free energy (notes p. 31):

<Formula label="Region 2" tex="\Omega_2 = N_2k_BT\left[\ln(n_2\lambda^3) - 1\right] + N_2V_0 - \mu N_2, \qquad \mu = \frac{\partial A_2}{\partial N_2} = k_BT\ln(n_2\lambda^3) + V_0, \qquad n_2 = \frac{1}{\lambda^3}\,e^{(\mu - V_0)/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 31, point ④ⓐ" />

The two densities are linked because both regions have the same $\mu$ (notes p. 32, boxed: "chemical potentials are equal in the equilibrium state"):

<Formula label="Equal chemical potentials" tex="\frac{\partial A_1}{\partial N_1} = \mu = \frac{\partial A_2}{\partial N_2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{n_2}{n_1} = e^{-V_0/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 32, point ④ⓐ" />

using $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2$ (same molecules, same temperature). (The notes' box has $\partial N_1$ in both denominators; the second must be $\partial N_2$.) Note that the **pressures** $n_ik_BT$ are not equal across the step: it is $\mu$, not $P$, that equalises when the potential energy differs.

**The atmosphere.** With $V_0 = mgh$ this is the barometric formula of an isothermal atmosphere. Wen's numbers for oxygen at 300 K: $gm_{\text{O}_2}/k_B(300\text{ K}) = 1.3 \times 10^{-4}$ per metre, or 0.13 per km, so 10 km up the density of oxygen is $e^{-1.3} = 0.27$ of its value at the ground (notes p. 32). (Unrounded, the rate is $1.26 \times 10^{-4}$ per metre and the ratio 0.28. The real atmosphere is colder at 10 km, so this is a model.) Wen leaves a question in a box: at the ground $n_{\text{O}_2}/n_{\text{N}_2} \approx 1/4$; what is the ratio at 10 km? Each gas has its own $\mu$ and its own exponential, so the ratio falls by $e^{-(m_{\text{O}_2} - m_{\text{N}_2})gh/k_BT}$. With a mass difference of 4 u that factor is 0.85 at 10 km, giving 0.21. (In the real atmosphere wind and turbulence keep the air mixed up to about 100 km, so the measured ratio stays near 1/4; the separation by mass takes over only above that.)

<WorkedExample title="Separating uranium isotopes in a centrifuge">
<Problem>
Uranium hexafluoride gas at 300 K spins in a centrifuge whose wall moves at $v = 600$ m/s. In the rotating frame a molecule at radius $r$ has potential energy $-\tfrac12m\omega^2r^2$, which is $-\tfrac12mv^2$ at the wall. The two isotopes give molecules of 349 u (²³⁵UF₆) and 352 u (²³⁸UF₆). By what factor is the ratio of light to heavy molecules larger at the axis than at the wall? ($1\text{ u} = 1.66054 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, $k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K.)
</Problem>
<Step title="Each isotope separately">
Each kind of molecule has its own chemical potential, the same everywhere in the drum, so each follows $n(r) \propto e^{-V_0(r)/k_BT} = e^{m\omega^2r^2/2k_BT}$. For the heavy molecule, $mv^2/2k_BT = 352 \times 1.66054 \times 10^{-27} \times 600^2/(2 \times 4.142 \times 10^{-21}) = 25.4$: its density at the wall is $e^{25.4} \approx 10^{11}$ times that at the axis. The gas is pressed into a thin layer at the wall.
</Step>
<Step title="The ratio">
Dividing the two exponentials, the light-to-heavy ratio changes between axis and wall by $e^{\Delta m\,v^2/2k_BT}$ with $\Delta m = 3$ u: $\Delta m\,v^2/2k_BT = 0.216$, so the factor is $e^{0.216} = 1.24$. The axis is enriched in ²³⁵U by 24% relative to the wall. One centrifuge does little; practical enrichment chains many of them in a cascade.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Adsorption on a surface

Now a gas at temperature $T$ and pressure $P$ (system 1) meets a surface with a grid of sites (system 2), each of which is either empty or holds one atom; an atom on a site has energy $\epsilon_0$, which Wen calls the "energy cost" and which is negative when the surface binds (notes p. 32, point ④ⓑ). Atoms move between gas and sites, so the two systems have the same $\mu$, the gas's:

<Formula label="Chemical potential of the gas in terms of the pressure" tex="\mu = k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3) = k_BT\ln\left(\frac{P}{k_BT}\,\lambda^3\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 32, point ④ⓑ" />

using $P = nk_BT$. Each site is a tiny grand canonical system of its own, and each of its states has the weight $e^{-(E - \mu N)/k_BT}$ (notes p. 32, boxed). The occupied site has $E = \epsilon_0$ and $N = 1$, the empty one $E = 0$ and $N = 0$:

<Formula label="Weights of a filled and an empty site" tex="P(\text{filled}) \propto e^{-(\epsilon_0 - \mu)/k_BT}, \qquad P(\text{empty}) \propto e^{0} = 1" cite="reading1" at="p. 32, point ④ⓑ" />

So the average number of atoms per site, the fraction of sites filled, is (notes pp. 32–33, boxed)

<Formula label="Fraction of sites filled" tex="p = \frac{e^{-(\epsilon_0 - \mu)/k_BT}}{1 + e^{-(\epsilon_0 - \mu)/k_BT}} = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(\epsilon_0 - \mu)/k_BT}} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{k_BT}{\lambda^3P}\,e^{\epsilon_0/k_BT}}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 32–33, point ④ⓑ" />

Wen checks the limits (notes p. 33): $p \to 1$ as $P \to \infty$, every site full, and $p \to 0$ as $P \to 0$. At low coverage the 1 in the denominator dominates and

<Formula label="Low coverage" tex="p \approx \frac{P\lambda^3}{k_BT}\,e^{-\epsilon_0/k_BT} = n\lambda^3\,e^{-\epsilon_0/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 33, point ④ⓑ" />

proportional to the gas density, as in the 8.044 model of a freely moving adsorbed layer (where $\epsilon_0$ has the opposite sign: a positive binding energy). Two remarks of ours. Writing $P_0(T) = (k_BT/\lambda^3)e^{\epsilon_0/k_BT}$, the result is $p = P/(P + P_0)$, known as the Langmuir isotherm: half the sites are filled at $P = P_0$, which rises steeply with temperature. And $1/(1 + e^{(\epsilon_0 - \mu)/k_BT})$ has the form of the Fermi-Dirac occupation, for the same reason: a site, like a fermion state, holds zero or one.

## Chemical reactions and the law of mass action

Point ④ⓒ (notes pp. 33–35) takes the reaction in which two hydrogen atoms form a molecule and release a binding energy $\epsilon_0$:

<Formula label="The reaction" tex="2\text{H} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2 + \epsilon_0 \quad\text{or}\quad 2\text{H} - \text{H}_2 = \epsilon_0 \quad\text{or}\quad \sum_i\nu_iX_i = \epsilon_0" cite="reading1" at="p. 33, point ④ⓒ" />

with $X_1 = \text{H}$, $\nu_1 = 2$ and $X_2 = \text{H}_2$, $\nu_2 = -1$. At given $T$ and $P$, what is $n_{\text{H}}/n_{\text{H}_2}$? When the reaction runs, the numbers change in step with the coefficients:

<Formula label="Changes of the particle numbers" tex="\frac{\delta N_1}{\nu_1} = \frac{\delta N_2}{\nu_2} = \cdots = \frac{\delta N_i}{\nu_i}" cite="reading1" at="p. 33, point ④ⓒ" />

The reaction proceeds until it minimises the total free energy $A_{\text{tot}} = \sum_iA_i(V, T, N_i)$. At the minimum a small step of the reaction changes nothing (notes p. 34):

<Formula label="Equilibrium of a reaction" tex="\delta A_{\text{tot}} = \sum_i\delta N_i\,\frac{\partial A_i}{\partial N_i} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sum_i\nu_i\mu_i = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 34, point ④ⓒ" />

one condition for each independent reaction. The equal chemical potentials of the first two examples are the simplest case, the "reaction" $X_1 - X_2 = 0$ of a particle moving from one place to the other.

Now the chemical potentials. Give each species an internal energy, $\epsilon_{\text{H}}$ and $\epsilon_{\text{H}_2}$, with $2\epsilon_{\text{H}} - \epsilon_{\text{H}_2} = \epsilon_0$. The internal energy adds $N\epsilon$ to the free energy, as $V_0$ did above (notes p. 34):

<Formula label="Chemical potentials of the two species" tex="A_{\text{H}} = N_{\text{H}}k_BT\left(\ln n_{\text{H}}\lambda_{\text{H}}^3 - 1\right) + N_{\text{H}}\epsilon_{\text{H}}, \qquad \mu_{\text{H}} = k_BT\ln(n_{\text{H}}\lambda_{\text{H}}^3) + \epsilon_{\text{H}}, \qquad \mu_{\text{H}_2} = k_BT\ln(n_{\text{H}_2}\lambda_{\text{H}_2}^3) + \epsilon_{\text{H}_2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 34, point ④ⓒ" />

The condition $2\mu_{\text{H}} - \mu_{\text{H}_2} = 0$ becomes

<Formula label="Equilibrium condition for hydrogen" tex="\frac{(n_{\text{H}}\lambda_{\text{H}}^3)^2}{n_{\text{H}_2}\lambda_{\text{H}_2}^3} = e^{-(2\epsilon_{\text{H}} - \epsilon_{\text{H}_2})/k_BT} = e^{-\epsilon_0/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 34, point ④ⓒ" />

The molecule is twice as heavy, so $\lambda_{\text{H}_2} = \lambda_{\text{H}}/\sqrt2$, and with $m$ the mass of the atom (notes p. 34, boxed):

<Formula label="Atoms and molecules of hydrogen" tex="\frac{n_{\text{H}}^2}{n_{\text{H}_2}} = \frac{1}{\left(2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT\right)^{3/2}}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{3/2}}\cdot e^{-\epsilon_0/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 34, point ④ⓒ" />

and for any reaction $\sum_i\nu_iX_i = \epsilon_0$ among classical gases, the **law of mass action**:

<Formula label="Law of mass action" tex="\prod_i\left(\lambda_i^3n_i\right)^{\nu_i} = e^{-\epsilon_0/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 34, point ④ⓒ" />

Each $\lambda_i^3n_i$ is a species' density measured in units of one particle per thermal volume; the Boltzmann factor of the energy released decides the balance. (Our remark: the model treats atoms and molecules as point particles. Real H₂ also rotates and vibrates, and both species have spin states; each of these multiplies the right side by a ratio of internal partition functions, which changes the numbers but not the form.)

**Wen's numbers** (notes p. 35), in cgs units: the binding energy is 436 kJ/mol, so $\epsilon_0 = 7.24 \times 10^{-12}$ erg $= 4.5$ eV per molecule. At 1 atm ($10.1$ N/cm², about $10^6$ dyn/cm²) and 300 K, nearly all the gas is molecules, so $n_{\text{H}_2} = P/k_BT = 2.4 \times 10^{19}$ cm⁻³. With $\epsilon_0/k_BT = 175$ and $m_p = 1.68 \times 10^{-24}$ g for the atom's mass,

<Formula label="Hydrogen atoms at room temperature" tex="\frac{n_{\text{H}}^2}{n_{\text{H}_2}} = 3.5\times10^{23}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\,e^{-175} = 3.5\times10^{-53}\ \text{cm}^{-3}, \qquad n_{\text{H}} = \sqrt{2.4\times10^{19}\times3.5\times10^{-53}}\ \text{cm}^{-3} = 2.9\times10^{-17}\ \text{cm}^{-3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 35, point ④ⓒ" />

That is one free atom in about $3.4 \times 10^{16}$ cm³, a cube more than 3 km on a side. (Rounding $\epsilon_0/k_BT = 174.9$ to 175 makes the result 11% smaller than the unrounded $3.9 \times 10^{-53}$ cm⁻³; $n_{\text{H}}$ becomes $3.1 \times 10^{-17}$ cm⁻³. The conclusion is the same.)

<WorkedExample title="When does hydrogen become neutral? (the Saha equation)">
<Problem>
A hydrogen gas holds $n = 10^9$ protons per cubic metre, free or bound in atoms (our example). The reaction is $\text{e} + \text{p} \rightleftharpoons \text{H} + 13.6$ eV. Using the law of mass action, find the fraction $x$ of the protons that are free at 3,500 K and at 4,000 K, and the temperature at which half the hydrogen is ionised. ($m_e = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.)
</Problem>
<Step title="The law of mass action for ionisation">
Written as $\text{e} + \text{p} - \text{H} = \epsilon_0$ with $\epsilon_0 = 13.6$ eV, the law gives $(\lambda_e^3n_e)(\lambda_p^3n_p)/(\lambda_{\text{H}}^3n_{\text{H}}) = e^{-\epsilon_0/k_BT}$. The proton and the atom have almost the same mass, so $\lambda_p = \lambda_{\text{H}}$ and only the electron's thermal wavelength survives. (The spin states also cancel: 2 for the electron times 2 for the proton, against 4 for the atom's ground state. Excited states of the atom are ignored.) With $n_e = n_p = xn$ and $n_{\text{H}} = (1 - x)n$, this is $x^2/(1 - x) = e^{-\epsilon_0/k_BT}/(n\lambda_e^3)$, with $1/\lambda_e^3 = (m_ek_BT/2\pi\hbar^2)^{3/2}$. This is the **Saha equation** (the name is not in the notes).
</Step>
<Step title="The numbers">
At 3,500 K the right side is 0.0131, so $x = 0.11$; at 4,000 K it is 4.47 and $x = 0.84$. Half the hydrogen is ionised at about 3,800 K. There $\epsilon_0/k_BT = 41.6$: the gas turns neutral only when $k_BT$ is some 40 times smaller than the binding energy. The reason is the large factor $1/(n\lambda_e^3)$, about $6 \times 10^{17}$ at 3,800 K: a free electron has so many more states available than a bound one that ionisation wins until the Boltzmann factor is tiny. A denser gas would turn neutral at a higher temperature.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## No order in the one-dimensional Ising model

The last application (notes p. 35, point ④ⓓ) turns the law of mass action on a magnet. In the Ising chain, with energy $E = -J\sum_is_is_{i+1}$, an ordered stretch of spins can be interrupted by a **domain wall**: a link where the spins disagree. Wen calls a wall from up to down A and one from down to up B. Each costs $2J$, since one bond goes from $-J$ to $+J$. Along the chain the walls must alternate, so they come in pairs, and Wen treats their creation as a reaction:

<Formula label="Domain walls as a reaction" tex="\text{A} + \text{B} = \epsilon_{\text{A}} + \epsilon_{\text{B}} = \epsilon_0 = 4J \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (\lambda_{\text{A}}n_{\text{A}})(\lambda_{\text{B}}n_{\text{B}}) = e^{-\epsilon_0/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 35, point ④ⓓ" />

with one power of $\lambda$ for each wall, not three, because the chain is one-dimensional. What are $\lambda_{\text{A}}$ and $\lambda_{\text{B}}$? Wen reads the meaning of $\lambda^3n$ backwards: $\lambda^3n = \lambda^3/v$, with $v$ the volume per particle, is one over the number of states available per particle. A wall can sit on any link, so a wall whose density is $n_{\text{A}}$ per link has about $1/n_{\text{A}}$ links, and so states, to choose from: in units of links, $\lambda_{\text{A}} = \lambda_{\text{B}} \approx 1$. With $n_{\text{A}} = n_{\text{B}}$,

<Formula label="Density of domain walls" tex="n_{\text{A}} = n_{\text{B}} \approx e^{-2J/k_BT}\ \text{per link}" cite="reading1" at="p. 35, point ④ⓓ" />

so a domain is about $e^{2J/k_BT}$ links long. However low the temperature, it is finite: the walls never die out, and a long enough chain contains many of them, with as many spins up as down on average. **The one-dimensional Ising model has no spontaneous order at any $T > 0$**, in contrast to the mean-field prediction $T_c = zJ/k_B = 2J/k_B$ for a chain ($z = 2$) found in [Chapter III](/courses/8-08/ising-model-mean-field).

Our check: in zero field the open chain can be solved exactly, because each link independently has a wall (energy $+J$) or not (energy $-J$). A link is then a wall with probability $1/(1 + e^{2J/k_BT})$, which approaches Wen's $e^{-2J/k_BT}$ at low temperature; the difference is the "1 +", the same exclusion as in the adsorption formula, since a link holds at most one wall.

## Summary

- Systems that exchange particles are in equilibrium when their chemical potentials are equal, $\partial A_1/\partial N_1 = \mu = \partial A_2/\partial N_2$.
- For a classical gas $\mu = k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3)$, negative; with an extra energy $V_0$ per molecule, $n_2/n_1 = e^{-V_0/k_BT}$ (the barometric formula; a centrifuge works the same way).
- Sites that hold one atom each are filled to the fraction $p = 1/(1 + (k_BT/\lambda^3P)e^{\epsilon_0/k_BT})$: proportional to $P$ at low pressure, saturating at 1.
- A reaction $\sum_i\nu_iX_i = \epsilon_0$ is in equilibrium when $\sum_i\nu_i\mu_i = 0$, which for classical gases is the law of mass action $\prod_i(\lambda_i^3n_i)^{\nu_i} = e^{-\epsilon_0/k_BT}$: about $3 \times 10^{-17}$ free hydrogen atoms per cm³ at room temperature, ionised hydrogen at a few thousand kelvin (the Saha equation).
- Domain walls in the Ising chain behave like a dilute gas with density $e^{-2J/k_BT}$ per link, never zero above $T = 0$, so the one-dimensional Ising model never orders.
