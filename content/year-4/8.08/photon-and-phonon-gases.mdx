Chapter VII of Wen's notes is about gases of bosons, and it starts with the two that are easiest because their number is not fixed: photons, the quanta of light in a cavity, and phonons, the quanta of sound in a crystal (notes pp. 48–54, points ① and ②). Both are collections of independent harmonic oscillators, one for each wave vector, so everything follows from the canonical ensemble of the [first chapter](/courses/8-08/canonical-ensemble-free-energy). The 8.044 pages on [identical particles](/courses/8-044/identical-particles) and [thermal radiation](/courses/8-044/thermal-radiation) reached Planck's law by a different road, through thermodynamics and the modes of a cavity; here we follow Wen's, which starts from how to label a state of many bosons. (Wen's handwritten page numbers continue from earlier chapters; this chapter is pp. 48–69, and this page covers pp. 48–54.)

## Labelling the states of many bosons

Wen lists two facts about photons (notes p. 48, point ①): they are **bosons**, and their **number is not conserved**, since the walls of a cavity absorb and emit them freely. With no fixed number there is no need for the grand canonical ensemble: the canonical ensemble, with the photon number free, already gives the equilibrium.

In a cubic box of side $L$ with periodic boundary conditions, one photon has a wave vector from a grid (polarisation aside for now):

<Formula label="Allowed wave vectors" tex="\vec k = \frac{2\pi}{L}\,(n_x, n_y, n_z)" cite="reading1" at="p. 48, point ①" />

with $n_x$, $n_y$ and $n_z$ whole numbers. Two bosons in levels $\vec k_1$ and $\vec k_2$ are in the state $|\vec k_1, \vec k_2\rangle$, and because they are identical this is the same state as $|\vec k_2, \vec k_1\rangle$; two bosons may also share a level, $\vec k_1 = \vec k_2$. So the list $|\vec k_1, \ldots, \vec k_N\rangle$ over-counts: only how many bosons sit in each level matters. A better label is the set of **occupation numbers** $|n_{\vec k_1}, n_{\vec k_2}, \ldots\rangle$, where each $n_{\vec k}$ runs over $0, 1, 2, \ldots$ independently of the others. (For fermions each $n_{\vec k}$ would be 0 or 1.)

With the photon energies $\varepsilon_k$, the energy of a state is $\sum_k n_k\varepsilon_k$, and because the $n_k$ are independent the sum over states factorises (notes p. 48):

<Formula label="Partition function of the photon gas" tex="Q = \sum_{n_{\vec k_1} = 0}^{\infty}\,\sum_{n_{\vec k_2} = 0}^{\infty}\cdots\, e^{-\beta\sum_k n_k\varepsilon_k} = \prod_k \sum_{n_k = 0}^{\infty} e^{-\beta n_k\varepsilon_k} = \prod_k \frac{1}{1 - e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 48" />

## A collection of oscillators

This $Q$ counts one polarisation. Light has two, each with its own set of oscillators, so the full partition function is $Q^2$ and the free energy, a sum over levels, gets a factor 2 (notes p. 49):

<Formula label="Free energy of the photon gas" tex="A = -k_BT\ln Q^2 = 2k_BT\sum_k \ln\left(1 - e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 49" />

Wen reads this as a statement about the system: it is a sum of **independent subsystems**, one for each level $\vec k$, whose state is labelled by $n_k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ with energy $n_k\varepsilon_k$. Each subsystem has partition function $Q_k = 1/(1 - e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k})$ and free energy $A_k = k_BT\ln(1 - e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k})$, and those are exactly the partition function and free energy of a **harmonic oscillator** with $\hbar\omega = \varepsilon_k$, whose energies are $n_k\hbar\omega + \frac12\hbar\omega$ (the constant zero-point energy is dropped). (Summing the $A_k$, the notes leave out the factor 2 for the polarisations that they wrote one line earlier.) So a photon gas is a collection of oscillators labelled by $\vec k$, and "$n_k$ photons in level $\vec k$" means "oscillator $\vec k$ in its $n_k$-th excited state". The probability of that is

<Formula label="Probability of n photons in a level" tex="P(n_k) = \frac{e^{-\beta n_k\varepsilon_k}}{\sum_{n_k} e^{-\beta n_k\varepsilon_k}} = e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k n_k}\left(1 - e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 49" />

a geometric distribution: each extra photon costs a factor $e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k}$.

## The Bose distribution

The mean number of photons in a level (of one polarisation) follows by differentiating the geometric series with respect to $\beta\varepsilon_k$ (notes p. 50):

<Formula label="Mean occupation of a level" tex="\langle n_k\rangle = \sum_{n_k} P(n_k)\,n_k = \left(1 - e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k}\right)\left(-\frac{\partial}{\partial(\beta\varepsilon_k)}\right)\frac{1}{1 - e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k}} = \frac{1}{e^{\beta\varepsilon_k} - 1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 50" />

Wen boxes it next to its fermion counterpart, $\langle n_k\rangle = 1/(e^{\beta\varepsilon_k} + 1)$. The difference of one sign matters most at low energy: for $\varepsilon_k \ll k_BT$ a boson level holds about $k_BT/\varepsilon_k$ particles, many more than one, while a fermion level is at most half full. At high energy both fall to the classical Boltzmann factor $e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k}$.

## Photon density

To add up the levels, note that each $\vec k$ occupies a volume $(2\pi/L)^3$ of $k$-space, so a sum over levels becomes an integral (notes p. 50):

<Formula label="From a sum over levels to an integral" tex="\sum_{\vec k} \;\to\; V\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 50" />

With $\varepsilon_k = \hbar\omega_k = \hbar c|\vec k|$, the total number of photons, two polarisations included, is $N = 2\sum_k\langle n_k\rangle$, and the photon density $n = N/V$ is

<Formula label="Photon number density" tex="n = \frac{2}{(2\pi)^3}\int_0^\infty dk\,\frac{4\pi k^2}{e^{\beta\hbar ck} - 1} = \frac{1}{\pi^2c^3}\int_0^\infty d\omega\,\frac{\omega^2}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1} = \kappa\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar c}\right)^3" cite="reading1" at="p. 50" />

with the number

<Formula label="The constant in the photon density" tex="\kappa = \frac{1}{\pi^2}\int_0^\infty dx\,\frac{x^2}{e^x - 1} = \frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2} = 0.2436" cite="reading1" at="p. 50" />

(The notes write $0.23\ldots$; the integral is $2\zeta(3) = 2.404$, and the value is 0.2436. Here $\zeta(3) = 1 + 1/2^3 + 1/3^3 + \cdots = 1.202$.)

Wen's **physical picture** (notes p. 51) explains the $T^3$ without any integral: a typical photon has energy of order $k_BT$, so a typical wave vector $k \sim k_BT/\hbar c$ and a thermal wavelength of order $\hbar c/k_BT$. There is about one photon per cube of that wavelength (a mode with $\varepsilon \sim k_BT$ holds of order one photon), so $n \sim (k_BT/\hbar c)^3$. Each carries about $k_BT$, so the energy density is of order $(k_BT/\hbar c)^3k_BT \propto T^4$.

## Energy density and Planck's law

The exact energy is $U = 2\sum_k\varepsilon_k\langle n_k\rangle$ (notes p. 51):

<Formula label="Energy of the photon gas" tex="U = \frac{8\pi\hbar cV}{(2\pi)^3}\int_0^\infty dk\,\frac{k^3}{e^{\beta\hbar ck} - 1} = \frac{V\hbar}{\pi^2c^3}\int_0^\infty d\omega\,\frac{\omega^3}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 51" />

The integral over $x = \beta\hbar\omega$ is $\int_0^\infty x^3\,dx/(e^x - 1) = \pi^4/15$, which gives **Stefan's law**:

<Formula label="Stefan's law" tex="\frac{U}{V} = \sigma T^4, \qquad \sigma = \frac{\pi^2k_B^4}{15(\hbar c)^3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 51" />

(Wen calls $\sigma$ Stefan's constant. It is the coefficient of the energy density, often called the radiation constant, $7.566 \times 10^{-16}$ J m⁻³ K⁻⁴; the constant usually called the Stefan-Boltzmann constant is $c\sigma/4$, which appears in the next section.) Dividing $U$ by the photon number gives a mean energy per photon of $(\pi^4/15)/(2\zeta(3))\,k_BT = 2.70\,k_BT$, close to the "typical $k_BT$" of the picture.

Wen contrasts this with the **classical** answer: equipartition would give each mode $k_BT$, so $U = 2\sum_k k_BT = (2Vk_BT/(2\pi)^3)\int 4\pi k^2\,dk$, which is infinite. Quantum mechanics cures it because modes with $\hbar\omega \gg k_BT$ are frozen, holding only $e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}$ photons each.

Writing $U/V = \int_0^\infty d\omega\,u(\omega, T)$, the energy density per unit angular frequency, the **spectral density**, is Planck's distribution (notes p. 51):

<Formula label="Planck distribution" tex="u(\omega, T) = \frac{\hbar}{\pi^2c^3}\,\frac{\omega^3}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 51" />

so that $u\,d\omega$ is the energy density in frequencies between $\omega$ and $\omega + d\omega$. Wen sketches it for $T = 2.73$ K, the temperature of the cosmic microwave background.

## Black-body radiation and radiation pressure

Make a small hole of area $A$ in the wall of a cavity (notes p. 52). The radiation that leaves through it is **black-body radiation**: a hole absorbs everything that falls on it, so what comes out is the cavity's equilibrium radiation. Photons move at speed $c$ in all directions; half of them move towards the wall, and a photon at angle $\theta$ to the normal crosses the hole at the rate $c\cos\theta$ per unit area and density. The power per unit area is then the energy density times $c$, times $\frac12$, times the average of $\cos\theta$ over the outward hemisphere:

<Formula label="Average of the angle factor" tex="\langle\cos\theta\rangle = \frac{\int_0^{\pi/2} d\theta\,\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\int_0^{\pi/2} d\theta\,\sin\theta} = \frac{1/2}{1} = \frac12" cite="reading1" at="p. 52" />

(The factors $2\pi$ from the azimuth cancel.) So the power radiated per unit area is (Wen writes it $W/A$)

<Formula label="Power radiated per unit area" tex="\frac{W}{A} = \frac{U}{V}\,\frac12\,c\,\langle\cos\theta\rangle = \frac14\,\frac{U}{V}\,c = \frac{c}{4}\,\sigma T^4" cite="reading1" at="p. 52" />

and, frequency by frequency, the **power spectrum** per unit area is $\frac{c}{4}\,u(\omega, T)$. With Wen's $\sigma$, $c\sigma/4 = 5.670 \times 10^{-8}$ W m⁻² K⁻⁴.

The **pressure** follows from the free energy (notes p. 52). The allowed wave vectors scale as $1/L$, so every mode energy scales as $\varepsilon_k \propto V^{-1/3}$ and $\partial\varepsilon_k/\partial V = -\varepsilon_k/3V$. Then

<Formula label="Radiation pressure" tex="P = -\frac{\partial A}{\partial V} = -2\sum_k \frac{\partial\varepsilon_k}{\partial V}\,\frac{e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k}}{1 - e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k}} = \frac{1}{3V}\,2\sum_k\frac{\varepsilon_k\,e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k}}{1 - e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k}} = \frac{U}{3V}" cite="reading1" at="p. 52" />

using $e^{-\beta\varepsilon}/(1 - e^{-\beta\varepsilon}) = \langle n_k\rangle$. The result $P = \frac13U/V$ holds for any gas whose particle energy is proportional to $|\vec k|$; for the non-relativistic gas of the next page it will be $\frac23U/V$.

## Phonons: the same gas in a crystal

The vibrations of a crystal lattice are quantised too, into **phonons** (notes p. 53, point ②). Phonons are "just like photons": each vibration mode with wave vector $\vec k$ is an oscillator, its number of quanta is not conserved, and the Bose distribution with no chemical potential applies. Wen lists three differences:

1. There are **three polarisations** in three dimensions (two transverse and one longitudinal), not two.
2. The speed is the speed of sound, $v \ll c$.
3. There is an **upper bound on $\vec k$**. A wave on a lattice is defined only at the atoms, and on a chain of atoms a wave shorter than twice the spacing looks, at the atoms, exactly like a longer one; Wen's sketch of a chain of atoms shows waves of decreasing wavelength down to that limit. The rule is that the number of $\vec k$-levels (for each polarisation) equals the number of lattice sites.

Counting levels in a sphere of radius $k_{\text{max}}$ in $k$-space:

<Formula label="Number of k-levels" tex="\sum_{\vec k} 1 = V\int_{|\vec k| < k_{\text{max}}}\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3} = \frac{V}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{4\pi}{3}\,k_{\text{max}}^3 = N_{\text{site}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 53" />

which gives

<Formula label="Largest phonon wave vector" tex="k_{\text{max}} = \left(\frac{6\pi^2N_{\text{site}}}{V}\right)^{1/3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 53" />

(The notes leave out the $(2\pi)^3$ in the counting, and the boxed result, $k_{\text{max}} = (3N_{\text{site}}/V)^{1/3}$, also drops the $4\pi$ of the line above it; with both, the factor is $6\pi^2$.)

Two simple models keep this structure (notes p. 53). The **Debye model** keeps the allowed $\vec k = (2\pi/L)(n_x, n_y, n_z)$ with $|\vec k| < k_{\text{max}}$ and gives each the energy of a sound wave, $\varepsilon_k = \hbar v|\vec k|$. The **Einstein model** keeps the same number of levels, $N_{\text{site}}$ for each polarisation, but gives them all one energy, $\varepsilon_k = \varepsilon_0$: every atom an independent oscillator.

## The Debye density of states

To turn sums over levels into integrals over energy, define the **density of states** $D(\varepsilon)$, the number of levels per unit energy (notes p. 54). For the Debye model, three polarisations included,

<Formula label="Debye density of states" tex="D(\varepsilon) = 3V\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\delta(\varepsilon - \hbar vk) = \frac{3}{2\pi^2}\,\frac{V}{(\hbar v)^3}\int_0^{\varepsilon_{\text{max}}} d\varepsilon'\,\delta(\varepsilon - \varepsilon')\,\varepsilon'^2 = \begin{cases} \dfrac{3}{2\pi^2}\,\dfrac{V}{(\hbar v)^3}\,\varepsilon^2, & \varepsilon < \varepsilon_{\text{max}} = \hbar vk_{\text{max}} \\ 0, & \varepsilon > \varepsilon_{\text{max}} \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="p. 54" />

(In the last line the notes write $3/(2\pi)$; it is $3/(2\pi^2)$, as on the line before.) The total is $\int D\,d\varepsilon = 3N_{\text{site}}$, three modes per atom. A **real material** differs in both respects: the allowed $\vec k$ fill a cube (the Brillouin zone) rather than a sphere, and the energy is a periodic function of $\vec k$, of the type $\varepsilon_k \propto \sqrt{\sin^2k_xa + \sin^2k_ya + \sin^2k_za}$ for a simple cubic lattice of spacing $a$. Its $D(\varepsilon)$, which Wen sketches, starts as $\varepsilon^2$ like Debye's and then has sharp peaks and kinks where the energy surfaces touch the zone boundary.

## The Debye heat capacity

The internal energy per atom is the energy of the phonons divided by the number of atoms, $N = \frac13\int D\,d\varepsilon$ (notes p. 54). With $D \propto \varepsilon^2$ the prefactors cancel:

<Formula label="Energy per atom in the Debye model" tex="\frac{U}{N} = \frac{\int d\varepsilon\,D(\varepsilon)\,\varepsilon\left(e^{\beta\varepsilon} - 1\right)^{-1}}{\frac{1}{3}\int d\varepsilon\,D(\varepsilon)} = 3\,\frac{\int_0^{\varepsilon_{\text{max}}} d\varepsilon\,\varepsilon^3\left(e^{\beta\varepsilon} - 1\right)^{-1}}{\int_0^{\varepsilon_{\text{max}}} d\varepsilon\,\varepsilon^2} = 9\,\varepsilon_{\text{max}}^{-3}\int_0^{\varepsilon_{\text{max}}} d\varepsilon\,\frac{\varepsilon^3}{e^{\beta\varepsilon} - 1} = 3k_BT\,\mathcal D(u)" cite="reading1" at="p. 54" />

Here the **Debye temperature** $T_D = \varepsilon_{\text{max}}/k_B$, $u = T_D/T$, and the Debye function, after the substitution $t = \beta\varepsilon$, is

<Formula label="The Debye function and its limits" tex="\mathcal D(u) = \frac{3}{u^3}\int_0^u dt\,\frac{t^3}{e^t - 1} = \begin{cases} 1 - \frac38u + \cdots, & u \ll 1\ (T \gg T_D) \\ \dfrac{\pi^4}{5u^3}, & u \gg 1\ (T \ll T_D) \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="p. 54" />

(Wen writes this function as $D(u)$; it is written $\mathcal D$ here to keep it apart from the density of states.) At high temperature $U/N \to 3k_BT$: every one of the three modes per atom has its classical $k_BT$, and $C_V \to 3Nk_B$, the law of Dulong and Petit. At low temperature the cutoff no longer matters, phonons behave like photons, and

<Formula label="Low-temperature heat capacity" tex="U = \frac{3\pi^4}{5}\,Nk_B\,\frac{T^4}{T_D^3}, \qquad C_V = \frac{\partial U}{\partial T} = \frac{12\pi^4}{5}\,Nk_B\left(\frac{T}{T_D}\right)^3" cite="reading1" at="p. 54" />

Wen's sketch of $C_V/3Nk_B$ shows the resulting curve: $T^3$ at the start, rising through $T \approx T_D$ and levelling off at 1. (The two forms of $U$ and $C_V$ are this page's steps from his limits.) The Einstein model, with every mode at $\varepsilon_0$, gets the high-temperature limit right but at low temperature gives a heat capacity that falls roughly as $e^{-\varepsilon_0/k_BT}$, much faster than measured (this comparison is not in the notes): the soft, long-wavelength sound waves that Debye keeps are what carry the heat at low temperature.

<WorkedExample title="Copper in the Debye model">
<Problem>
Copper has $8.49 \times 10^{28}$ atoms per m³ and a Debye temperature of about 343 K. Find $k_{\text{max}}$, the sound speed $v$ the Debye model implies, and the heat capacity of one mole at 10 K and at room temperature.
</Problem>
<Step title="The cutoff">
$k_{\text{max}} = (6\pi^2 \times 8.49 \times 10^{28})^{1/3}$ m⁻¹ $= 1.71 \times 10^{10}$ m⁻¹, a shortest wavelength $2\pi/k_{\text{max}} = 0.37$ nm, of the order of the spacing between atoms. (Wen's boxed $(3N/V)^{1/3}$ would give $6.3 \times 10^9$ m⁻¹.)
</Step>
<Step title="The implied sound speed">
$k_BT_D = \hbar vk_{\text{max}}$, so $v = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 343/(1.0546 \times 10^{-34} \times 1.71 \times 10^{10})$ m/s $= 2.62 \times 10^3$ m/s: an average over the three polarisations, of the order of the measured speeds of sound in copper.
</Step>
<Step title="Heat capacity at 10 K">
$T/T_D = 0.0292$, and $(12\pi^4/5)R(T/T_D)^3 = 233.8 \times 8.314 \times 2.48 \times 10^{-5}$ J/(mol K) $= 0.048$ J/(mol K). At room temperature, $T \approx T_D$, it is close to the classical $3R = 24.9$ J/(mol K), some 500 times more. (At 10 K a real metal adds a further heat capacity, proportional to $T$, from its conduction electrons, a Fermi-gas effect of a later chapter.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The photon gas of the universe">
The cosmic microwave background is the photon gas of this page at $T = 2.725$ K. Wen's formulas give it about $4.1 \times 10^8$ photons per m³ (411 per cm³) and an energy density of $4.17 \times 10^{-14}$ J/m³. In [MIT 8.286 Lecture 16](/courses/8-286/black-body-radiation) Guth uses the same $T^4$ and $T^3$ laws, with a factor counting the spin states of each kind of particle, to work out how much radiation the early universe held; the Planck curve of this page is the spectrum that the [CMB measurements of 8.286 Lecture 18](/courses/8-286/cmb-spectrum) found the background to follow.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Photons are bosons whose number is not conserved, so the canonical ensemble suffices. States are labelled by occupation numbers $n_k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$, and each level $\vec k$ is an independent oscillator with $\hbar\omega = \varepsilon_k$: $Q = \prod_k 1/(1 - e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k})$, $A = 2k_BT\sum_k\ln(1 - e^{-\beta\varepsilon_k})$.
- The mean occupation is the Bose distribution $\langle n_k\rangle = 1/(e^{\beta\varepsilon_k} - 1)$ (fermions: $+1$).
- With $\sum_k \to V\int d^3k/(2\pi)^3$: $n = 0.2436\,(k_BT/\hbar c)^3$, $U/V = \sigma T^4$ with Wen's $\sigma = \pi^2k_B^4/15(\hbar c)^3$, and Planck's spectrum $u(\omega, T) = (\hbar/\pi^2c^3)\,\omega^3/(e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1)$; the classical answer is infinite.
- A hole in a cavity radiates $\frac{c}{4}\sigma T^4$ per unit area; the radiation pressure is $P = U/3V$, because $\varepsilon_k \propto V^{-1/3}$.
- Phonons are the same gas with three polarisations, speed $v \ll c$ and $k_{\text{max}} = (6\pi^2N/V)^{1/3}$. The Debye model gives $D(\varepsilon) \propto \varepsilon^2$ up to $k_BT_D$, $U/N = 3k_BT\,\mathcal D(T_D/T)$, $C_V \to 3Nk_B$ at high $T$ and $C_V = \frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B(T/T_D)^3$ at low $T$.
