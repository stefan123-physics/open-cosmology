This set practises the [grand canonical ensemble](/courses/8-08/grand-partition-function) and the rule that systems which exchange particles have [equal chemical potentials](/courses/8-08/equal-chemical-potentials), from Chapter IV of Wen's notes, together with the [van der Waals gas](/courses/8-08/van-der-waals-equation) of Chapter II. The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 4 (dated February 23, 2005), in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its dates are left out. MIT's Problem 1 is only a pointer to a problem in a textbook (Huang's *Statistical Mechanics*), named by number. The textbook is not used here: **Problem 1 is our own problem**, on a subject of Chapter IV (the law of mass action, for hydrogen molecules that split into atoms at a fixed pressure). MIT's Problem 2 is stated in full on the sheet and is given here as MIT sets it, in our words, with "absorbed" read as "adsorbed". Our additions to it are marked "(Our addition.)" where they stand: part (a)(iii), on the fluctuation of the number of atoms, and part (c), which puts the van der Waals gas of part (b) in contact with the surface of part (a).

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `exp(-a/(kB*T))/(1 + x^2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `kB` stands for $k_B$, `mu` for $\mu$, `lam` for $\lambda$, `e0` for $\epsilon_0$, `Ns` for $N_s$, `v0` for $v_0$ and `vb` for $\bar v$, `sqrt` is the square root, `exp` the exponential and `log` the natural logarithm; the other letters (`T`, `P`, `K`, `n`) are typed as they are.

MIT's sheet gives no points. The points (20 for Problem 1 and 30 for Problem 2, 50 in all) and every split between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes, with MIT's letters where the sheet has its own. $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature and $\beta = 1/k_BT$. $\mu$ is a chemical potential and $z = e^{\beta\mu}$ the fugacity. $\lambda = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT}$ is the thermal wavelength of a particle of mass $m$, $n$ a number density and $P$ a pressure. In Problem 1, $\epsilon_0$ is the binding energy of the hydrogen molecule, $K$ the equilibrium constant and $\alpha$ the fraction of molecules split. In Problem 2, $N_s$ is the number of sites, $p_0$, $p_1$ and $p_2$ are the probabilities that a site holds zero, one or two atoms, and $v_0$ and $\bar v$ are the two constants of the van der Waals free energy $A$.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Hydrogen molecules that split into atoms (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on a subject of Chapter IV of the notes: the law of mass action.

A container holds hydrogen at the temperature $T$ and the fixed pressure $P$. Some molecules split, $\text{H}_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\text{H}$, and the reverse reaction releases the binding energy $\epsilon_0$. As in the notes, we treat atoms and molecules as point particles (our assumption: rotations, vibrations and spins are left out), so that each species $i$ is a classical ideal gas with the chemical potential

<Formula label="Chemical potential of each species" tex="\mu_i = k_BT\ln(n_i\lambda_i^3) + \epsilon_i, \qquad 2\epsilon_{\text{H}} - \epsilon_{\text{H}_2} = \epsilon_0" cite="reading1" at="p. 34, point ④ⓒ" />

where $\epsilon_i$ is the internal energy of one particle of species $i$. Write $\lambda$ for the thermal wavelength of the atom.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

The ratio $K(T) = n_{\text{H}}^2/n_{\text{H}_2}$ is fixed by the temperature alone. Find it in terms of $\lambda$, $\epsilon_0$ and $k_BT$.

<Hint>

The reaction is in equilibrium when the chemical potentials, counted with the reaction's coefficients, add up to zero. Which combination of $\mu_{\text{H}}$ and $\mu_{\text{H}_2}$ is that here?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The molecule has twice the mass of the atom. How does its thermal wavelength compare with $\lambda$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-k" label="$K(T) = n_{\text{H}}^2/n_{\text{H}_2} =$" expr="exp(-e0/(kB*T))/(2^(3/2)*lam^3)"
  vars={{ e0: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2], lam: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ e0: "\\epsilon_0", kB: "k_B", T: "T", lam: "\\lambda" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-e0/(kB*T))/lam^3", hint: "This uses the atom's thermal wavelength for the molecule too. The molecule is twice as heavy, so its λ is smaller by √2, and its λ³ by 2^(3/2)." },
    { expr: "2^(3/2)*exp(-e0/(kB*T))/lam^3", hint: "The factor 2^(3/2) is upside down: this takes the molecule's thermal wavelength √2 times larger than the atom's. Since λ ∝ 1/√m, the heavier molecule has the smaller λ." },
    { expr: "exp(e0/(kB*T))/(2^(3/2)*lam^3)", hint: "The sign of the exponent is reversed. Splitting a molecule costs the energy ε₀, so atoms become rare at low temperature: K must vanish as T goes to zero." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Equal chemical potentials, counted with the reaction's coefficients: two atoms have the same free energy per step of the reaction as one molecule.

<Step title="The equilibrium condition">

One step of the reaction removes one molecule and adds two atoms, so the total free energy is stationary when

<Formula label="Equilibrium of the dissociation" tex="2\mu_{\text{H}} - \mu_{\text{H}_2} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 34, point ④ⓒ" />

With the chemical potentials above, $2k_BT\ln(n_{\text{H}}\lambda^3) - k_BT\ln(n_{\text{H}_2}\lambda_{\text{H}_2}^3) = -(2\epsilon_{\text{H}} - \epsilon_{\text{H}_2}) = -\epsilon_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The thermal wavelength of the molecule">

$\lambda \propto 1/\sqrt m$ and the molecule has mass $2m$, so $\lambda_{\text{H}_2} = \lambda/\sqrt2$ and $\lambda_{\text{H}_2}^3 = \lambda^3/2^{3/2}$. Then

<Formula label="Equilibrium constant" tex="K(T) = \frac{n_{\text{H}}^2}{n_{\text{H}_2}} = \frac{\lambda_{\text{H}_2}^3}{\lambda^6}\,e^{-\epsilon_0/k_BT} = \frac{e^{-\epsilon_0/k_BT}}{2^{3/2}\lambda^3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 34, point ④ⓒ (boxed)" />

the boxed result of the notes (p. 34), written with the atom's $\lambda$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$K$ has the units of a density, as $n_{\text{H}}^2/n_{\text{H}_2}$ must. At low temperature the Boltzmann factor makes it vanish (all molecules); at high temperature it grows as $T^{3/2}$ and the atoms win.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Start from $N_0$ molecules and let a fraction $\alpha$ of them split, so that the container holds $2\alpha N_0$ atoms and $(1 - \alpha)N_0$ molecules. The pressure $P$ is held fixed (the volume adjusts). Find $\alpha$ in terms of $P$, $k_BT$ and $K$.

<Hint>

Each species is an ideal gas at the same temperature, and the pressure is the sum of the two partial pressures, $P = (n_{\text{H}} + n_{\text{H}_2})k_BT$. What fraction of all the particles are atoms?

</Hint>

<Hint>

$n_{\text{H}}$ and $n_{\text{H}_2}$ are $P/k_BT$ times the fractions of atoms and of molecules among all $(1 + \alpha)N_0$ particles. Put them into $K = n_{\text{H}}^2/n_{\text{H}_2}$ and solve for $\alpha$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-alpha" label="$\alpha =$" expr="1/sqrt(1 + 4*P/(K*kB*T))"
  vars={{ P: [0.5, 2], K: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ P: "P", K: "K", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/sqrt(1 + P/(K*kB*T))", hint: "This solves α²/(1 − α²) = Kk_BT/P: the factor 4 is missing. Each molecule that splits gives two atoms, so n_H is proportional to 2α, and n_H² to 4α²." },
    { expr: "sqrt(K*kB*T/(4*P))", hint: "That is the limit of a small fraction split, α ≪ 1, where 1 − α² ≈ 1. It is not the full answer: it grows without bound and passes 1 when Kk_BT > 4P." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count the particles, turn the counts into partial densities at the fixed pressure, and put them into the law of mass action.

<Step title="The densities">

The container holds $(1 + \alpha)N_0$ particles, all at the same $T$, so $n_{\text{H}} + n_{\text{H}_2} = P/k_BT$ and each density is $P/k_BT$ times its share:

<Formula label="Partial densities at fixed pressure" tex="n_{\text{H}} = \frac{2\alpha}{1 + \alpha}\,\frac{P}{k_BT}, \qquad n_{\text{H}_2} = \frac{1 - \alpha}{1 + \alpha}\,\frac{P}{k_BT}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own problem and solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The law of mass action">

<Formula label="Fraction of molecules split" tex="\frac{n_{\text{H}}^2}{n_{\text{H}_2}} = \frac{4\alpha^2}{(1 + \alpha)(1 - \alpha)}\,\frac{P}{k_BT} = K \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha^2 = \frac{Kk_BT}{Kk_BT + 4P}, \qquad \alpha = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + 4P/(Kk_BT)}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own problem and solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$0 < \alpha < 1$ for every $P$ and $K$. As $K \to \infty$ (high temperature) $\alpha \to 1$, every molecule split; as $K \to 0$, $\alpha \to 0$. For small $\alpha$, $\alpha \approx \sqrt{Kk_BT/4P}$, the square-root law of a weakly split gas.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

(Our numbers.) Take Wen's binding energy, 436 kJ/mol, that is $\epsilon_0 = 7.240 \times 10^{-19}$ J per molecule (notes p. 35), the atom's mass $m = 1.6735 \times 10^{-27}$ kg and $P = 101325$ Pa ($k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K, $\hbar = 1.054572 \times 10^{-34}$ J s). In this model, find the fraction $\alpha$ split at $3000$ K, and the temperature in kelvin at which half the molecules are split (tolerance 1% each).

<Hint>

Work out $\lambda$, $\epsilon_0/k_BT$ and then $Kk_BT$, a pressure, at 3000 K. Compare it with $4P$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\alpha = \frac12$ when $Kk_BT = \frac43P$. Since $K$ grows steeply with $T$, find that temperature by trying a few values and narrowing down, or by bisection.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-num" label="$\alpha$ at 3000 K:" value="0.1677" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-num" />

<Answer id="p1-half" label="$T$ in K at which $\alpha = 1/2$:" value="3414" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-half" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Everything follows from $Kk_BT$, which is a pressure: the gas is half split when it is $\frac43$ of $P$.

<Step title="At 3000 K">

$k_BT = 4.142 \times 10^{-20}$ J, so $\epsilon_0/k_BT = 17.48$, and $\lambda = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT} = 3.175 \times 10^{-11}$ m. Then $K = e^{-17.48}/(2^{3/2}\lambda^3) = 2.831 \times 10^{23}$ m⁻³ and $Kk_BT = 1.173 \times 10^4$ Pa. So $4P/Kk_BT = 34.57$ and $\alpha = 1/\sqrt{35.57} = 0.1677$: about one molecule in six is split.

</Step>

<Step title="Half split">

$\alpha = \frac12$ needs $Kk_BT = \frac43 P = 1.351 \times 10^5$ Pa, about 11.5 times its value at 3000 K. Bisection gives $T = 3414$ K, where $\epsilon_0/k_BT = 15.36$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At 3414 K the gas is half split although $k_BT$ is only $\frac{1}{15}$ of the binding energy: as in the Saha example of the lesson, the atoms win because two free atoms have far more states than one molecule. The small-$\alpha$ formula of part (b) gives 0.170 at 3000 K, close to 0.1677, as it should be where $\alpha$ is small. (Real hydrogen molecules also rotate and vibrate, which multiplies $K$ by ratios of internal partition functions and changes these numbers, as the lesson remarks, but not the form of the result.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

At a fixed temperature, the pressure is raised. What happens to the fraction $\alpha$ of the molecules that are split?

<Answer id="p1-press" label="When P is raised at fixed T," choices={["α grows, because the atoms are pressed closer together and so collide and split molecules more often", "α stays the same, because K depends on T alone", "α falls, because joining atoms into molecules lowers the number of particles", "α first grows and then falls"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $K$ depends on $T$ alone, but $\alpha$ depends on $P/(Kk_BT)$.

<Step title="The pressure dependence">

$\alpha = 1/\sqrt{1 + 4P/(Kk_BT)}$ falls as $P$ grows. The reason: $n_{\text{H}}^2/n_{\text{H}_2} = K$ is fixed, and raising both densities by the same factor raises $n_{\text{H}}^2$ more than $n_{\text{H}_2}$; the balance is restored only by moving atoms into molecules. In the language of Le Chatelier's principle, the system answers the compression by reducing its number of particles. For a weakly split gas, $\alpha \propto P^{-1/2}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Sites that hold two atoms, and a van der Waals gas" points={30} mit="Problem 2">

A surface has $N_s$ sites. Each site can be empty, or adsorb one atom or two atoms, and adsorbing one or two atoms costs no energy. The surface is in contact with a gas of these atoms.

<Part label="a (i)" points={8}>

The gas has the chemical potential $\mu$ and the temperature $T$. Find the probabilities $p_0$, $p_1$ and $p_2$ that a site is empty, holds one atom, or holds two atoms. (Our reading of MIT's model: a site has exactly one state with no atom, one with one atom and one with two atoms.)

<Hint>

Each site is a small grand canonical system of its own, exchanging atoms with the gas, as in the adsorption example of the notes. A state with energy $E$ and $N$ atoms has the weight $e^{-(E - \mu N)/k_BT}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Here $E = 0$ in all three states, so the weights are $1$, $z$ and $z^2$ with $z = e^{\mu/k_BT}$. Each number of atoms is one state of the site.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-p0" label="$p_0 =$" expr="1/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)) + exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))"
  vars={{ mu: [-2, 1], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu: "\\mu", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[

  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-p1" label="$p_1 =$" expr="exp(mu/(kB*T))/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)) + exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))"
  vars={{ mu: [-2, 1], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu: "\\mu", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(mu/(kB*T))/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)))", hint: "That is the occupied fraction of a site that holds at most one atom. The doubly occupied state adds z² to the denominator." },
    { expr: "2*exp(mu/(kB*T))/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)))^2", hint: "This treats the site as two separate places, each empty or full, so one atom can sit in either place: the singly occupied state is counted twice, in the numerator and in (1 + z)². That describes a site with two distinct places, which has two one-atom states; the site here has one state for each number of atoms, so the sum is 1 + z + z²." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-p2" label="$p_2 =$" expr="exp(2*mu/(kB*T))/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)) + exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))"
  vars={{ mu: [-2, 1], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu: "\\mu", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(2*mu/(kB*T))/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)))^2", hint: "This treats the site as two separate places, each empty or full, so the singly occupied state is counted twice in (1 + z)². That describes a site with two distinct places, which has two one-atom states; the site here has one state for each number of atoms, so the sum is 1 + z + z²." },
    { expr: "exp(mu/(kB*T))/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)) + exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))", hint: "That is p₁. Two atoms each bring a factor e^(μ/k_BT), so their weight is e^(2μ/k_BT)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each site is a three-state system that exchanges atoms with the gas, with the weights $1$, $z$ and $z^2$.

<Step title="One site">

A state with $N$ atoms and energy $E$ has the weight $e^{-(E - \mu N)/k_BT}$ (notes p. 32, boxed). Here $E = 0$ for $N = 0$, $1$ and $2$, so with $z = e^{\mu/k_BT}$

<Formula label="Grand partition function of one site" tex="Q_G^{\text{site}} = 1 + z + z^2, \qquad p_0 = \frac{1}{1 + z + z^2}, \quad p_1 = \frac{z}{1 + z + z^2}, \quad p_2 = \frac{z^2}{1 + z + z^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a)(i), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The three probabilities add up to 1. At $z \to 0$ (a very dilute gas) the site is empty; at $z \to \infty$ it holds two atoms. At $z = 1$ the three states are equally likely, since none costs energy and none is favoured by the gas.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="a (ii)" points={5}>

Find the average number $\langle N\rangle$ of atoms adsorbed on the whole surface.

<Hint>

The sites are independent: find the average number of atoms on one site, $0\cdot p_0 + 1\cdot p_1 + 2\cdot p_2$, and multiply by $N_s$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-n" label="$\langle N\rangle =$" expr="Ns*(exp(mu/(kB*T)) + 2*exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)) + exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))"
  vars={{ Ns: [10, 100], mu: [-2, 1], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ Ns: "N_s", mu: "\\mu", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Ns*(exp(mu/(kB*T)) + exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)) + exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))", hint: "That is the average number of occupied sites, N_s(p₁ + p₂). A doubly occupied site holds two atoms, so p₂ counts twice." },
    { expr: "Ns*exp(mu/(kB*T))/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)))", hint: "That is the number of atoms on sites that hold at most one atom. Here a site can hold two, which changes both the weights and the count." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The sites do not interact, so the grand partition function of the surface is that of one site to the power $N_s$, and the mean number is $N_s$ times that of one site.

<Step title="The mean number">

<Formula label="Mean number of atoms adsorbed" tex="\langle N\rangle = N_s\,(p_1 + 2p_2) = N_s\,\frac{z + 2z^2}{1 + z + z^2} = N_sk_BT\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\ln Q_G^{\text{site}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a)(ii), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The last form is $\langle N\rangle = -\partial\Omega/\partial\mu$ with $\Omega = -N_sk_BT\ln(1 + z + z^2)$, the route of the lesson.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $z \to \infty$ every site holds two atoms and $\langle N\rangle \to 2N_s$; at $z = 1$, $\langle N\rangle = N_s$, the middle of the range 0 to $2N_s$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="a (iii)" points={5}>

(Our addition.) Find the variance $\langle(N - \langle N\rangle)^2\rangle$ of the number of atoms on the surface.

<Hint>

The lesson's rule $\langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle = k_BT\,\partial\langle N\rangle/\partial\mu$ holds for the whole surface. Write $\langle N\rangle$ with $z$ and use $k_BT\,\partial/\partial\mu = z\,\partial/\partial z$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-var" label="$\langle(N - \langle N\rangle)^2\rangle =$" expr="Ns*exp(mu/(kB*T))*(1 + 4*exp(mu/(kB*T)) + exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)) + exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))^2"
  vars={{ Ns: [10, 100], mu: [-2, 1], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ Ns: "N_s", mu: "\\mu", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Ns*(exp(mu/(kB*T)) + 2*exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)) + exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))", hint: "That is ⟨N⟩ itself, the variance of a Poisson distribution, which holds for the ideal gas in an open volume. A site that holds at most two atoms is not a Poisson source: use k_BT ∂⟨N⟩/∂μ." },
    { expr: "Ns*(exp(mu/(kB*T)) + 4*exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))/(1 + exp(mu/(kB*T)) + exp(2*mu/(kB*T)))", hint: "That is N_s times the mean square of the number of atoms on one site, without subtracting the square of its mean." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Differentiate the mean number with respect to $\mu$, as in the lesson on the grand partition function.

<Step title="The fluctuation">

$k_BT\,\partial/\partial\mu = z\,d/dz$, and $z\,\frac{d}{dz}\frac{z + 2z^2}{1 + z + z^2} = z\,\frac{(1 + 4z)(1 + z + z^2) - (z + 2z^2)(1 + 2z)}{(1 + z + z^2)^2}$. The numerator simplifies to $1 + 4z + z^2$, so

<Formula label="Variance of the number adsorbed" tex="\left\langle(N - \langle N\rangle)^2\right\rangle = k_BT\,\frac{\partial\langle N\rangle}{\partial\mu} = N_s\,\frac{z\,(1 + 4z + z^2)}{(1 + z + z^2)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our addition and solution)" />

The same follows from one site directly: the mean square of its number of atoms, $(z + 4z^2)/(1 + z + z^2)$, minus the square of its mean, $(z + 2z^2)^2/(1 + z + z^2)^2$, has the same numerator over $(1 + z + z^2)^2$; the sites are independent, so their variances add.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $z \to \infty$ the variance falls to zero, as it must for a full surface, and at $z \to 0$ it tends to $N_sz = \langle N\rangle$, the Poisson value of rare, independent atoms. At $z = 1$ it is $\frac23N_s$: per site, the variance of a number spread evenly over 0, 1 and 2.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={7}>

Now the gas is described by the van der Waals model, with the free energy

<Formula label="Van der Waals free energy of the gas" tex="A = Nk_BT\left[\ln\left(\frac{N\lambda^3}{V - Nv_0}\right) - 1\right] + \frac{N^2\bar v}{V}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1" />

where $v_0$ and $\bar v$ are constants, $N$ is the number of atoms in the gas, $V$ its volume and $\lambda = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT}$. (Our remark: the sheet's $\bar v$ is half the $\bar v$ of the [Chapter II lesson](/courses/8-08/van-der-waals-equation), whose last term is $\frac12N^2\bar v/V$; either way an attraction makes $\bar v$ negative, and $Nv_0$ is the excluded volume $b$.) Find the chemical potential of the gas as a function of $T$ and the density $n = N/V$ (you may keep $\lambda$, which depends on $T$).

<Hint>

The chemical potential is the free energy of one more particle: $\mu = (\partial A/\partial N)$ at fixed $V$ and $T$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$N$ appears three times: in front, inside the logarithm (in the numerator and in $V - Nv_0$), and in $N^2$. Differentiate each, then write $N/V = n$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-mu" label="$\mu =$" expr="kB*T*log(n*lam^3/(1 - n*v0)) + kB*T*n*v0/(1 - n*v0) + 2*n*vb"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2], n: [0.1, 0.5], lam: [0.5, 1.5], v0: [0.2, 1], vb: [-2, -0.3] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T", n: "n", lam: "\\lambda", v0: "v_0", vb: "\\bar v" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "kB*T*log(n*lam^3/(1 - n*v0)) + 2*n*vb", hint: "The hard-core term is missing. N also appears in V − Nv₀ inside the logarithm; its derivative gives Nk_BT·v₀/(V − Nv₀) = k_BT·nv₀/(1 − nv₀)." },
    { expr: "kB*T*log(n*lam^3/(1 - n*v0)) + kB*T*n*v0/(1 - n*v0) + n*vb", hint: "The derivative of N²v̄/V with respect to N is 2Nv̄/V = 2nv̄, not nv̄." },
    { expr: "kB*T*(log(n*lam^3/(1 - n*v0)) - 1) + n*vb", hint: "That is A/N, the free energy per particle. The chemical potential is the derivative ∂A/∂N, which is not the same thing when A is not simply proportional to N at fixed V." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in the lesson on equal chemical potentials, $\mu = \partial A/\partial N$ at fixed $V$ and $T$ (notes p. 31).

<Step title="Differentiate">

Write the logarithm as $\ln N + \ln\lambda^3 - \ln(V - Nv_0)$. Then

<Formula label="Derivative of the van der Waals free energy" tex="\frac{\partial A}{\partial N} = k_BT\left[\ln\frac{N\lambda^3}{V - Nv_0} - 1\right] + Nk_BT\left[\frac1N + \frac{v_0}{V - Nv_0}\right] + \frac{2N\bar v}{V}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="In terms of the density">

The $-1$ and the $+1$ cancel, and $N/(V - Nv_0) = n/(1 - nv_0)$:

<Formula label="Chemical potential of the van der Waals gas" tex="\mu = k_BT\ln\frac{n\lambda^3}{1 - nv_0} + k_BT\,\frac{nv_0}{1 - nv_0} + 2n\bar v" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

With $v_0 = 0$ and $\bar v = 0$ it is the ideal gas, $\mu = k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3)$, of the lesson. The hard cores raise $\mu$ (both their terms are positive: less room makes each particle costlier), and an attraction ($\bar v < 0$) lowers it. A second check: the Gibbs–Duhem relation $n = \partial P/\partial\mu$ at fixed $T$ of the lesson holds with $P = -\partial A/\partial V = nk_BT/(1 - nv_0) + n^2\bar v$, since $\partial P/\partial n = k_BT/(1 - nv_0)^2 + 2n\bar v$ and $\partial\mu/\partial n = k_BT/[n(1 - nv_0)^2] + 2\bar v$ differ by exactly the factor $n$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

(Our addition, with our numbers.) Now let the van der Waals gas of part (b) be the gas in contact with the surface of part (a). Take $n\lambda^3 = 0.0500$, $nv_0 = 0.300$ and $n\bar v = -0.600\,k_BT$. Find the average number of atoms per site, $\langle N\rangle/N_s$, to three significant figures (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

The surface and the gas exchange atoms, so the surface has the gas's chemical potential. Find $z = e^{\mu/k_BT}$ from part (b), then use part (a)(ii).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-cover" label="$\langle N\rangle/N_s =$" value="0.0340" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-cover" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Equal chemical potentials: the sites see only $\mu$, so the gas enters through $z$ alone.

<Step title="The fugacity">

From part (b), $z = \dfrac{n\lambda^3}{1 - nv_0}\exp\left(\dfrac{nv_0}{1 - nv_0} + \dfrac{2n\bar v}{k_BT}\right) = \dfrac{0.0500}{0.700}\,e^{0.4286 - 1.200} = 0.07143 \times 0.4624 = 0.03303$.

</Step>

<Step title="The number per site">

$\langle N\rangle/N_s = (z + 2z^2)/(1 + z + z^2) = 0.03521/1.03412 = 0.0340$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

An ideal gas at the same density and temperature has $z = n\lambda^3 = 0.0500$ and gives $0.0523$. The attraction lowers the chemical potential of the gas more than the hard cores raise it, so the surface takes up about a third fewer atoms: an atom held by its neighbours in the gas is less ready to leave for the surface.

</Step>

</Solution>
