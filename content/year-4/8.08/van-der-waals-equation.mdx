Chapter I of Wen's notes treated gases whose atoms do not interact, so that the partition function splits into a product of one-atom factors. Real atoms repel each other strongly when they touch and attract each other weakly at a distance, and that is what makes gases condense into liquids. This page follows the first two numbered points of Wen's handwritten Chapter II (notes pp. 13–14): an approximate partition function for an interacting gas, the free energy it gives, and from it the **van der Waals equation of state**. The [next page](/courses/8-08/van-der-waals-phase-transitions) uses that free energy to find the liquid–gas transition. The ideal gas and its thermal wavelength come from [the canonical-ensemble examples of 8.044](/courses/8-044/canonical-examples), and the free energies from [Maxwell relations and thermodynamic potentials](/courses/8-044/maxwell-relations). (Wen writes the partition function as $Q$ and the Helmholtz free energy as $A$, as in Huang's textbook; 8.044 calls them Z and F. Everything here is in our own words, with our own example.)

## The partition function of an interacting gas

Take $N$ identical atoms of mass $m$ in a box of volume $V$, with kinetic energies $\varepsilon(\vec k) = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ and a potential energy $U(\vec q_1, \dots, \vec q_N)$ that depends on their positions. The classical partition function is an integral over all positions $\vec q_i$ and wave vectors $\vec k_i$ (notes p. 13, point ①):

<Formula label="Partition function of an interacting gas" tex="Q = \frac{1}{N!}\int\frac{d^{3N}k\,d^{3N}q}{(2\pi)^{3N}}\;e^{-\beta\left[\sum_i\varepsilon(\vec k_i) + U(\vec q_1, \dots, \vec q_N)\right]}" cite="reading1" at="p. 13, point ①" />

with $\beta = 1/k_BT$. Since $\vec p = \hbar\vec k$, the measure $d^3k\,d^3q/(2\pi)^3$ is the familiar $d^3p\,d^3q/h^3$, one state per cell of size $h^3$ in phase space, and the $1/N!$ is the correct counting for identical atoms (notes p. 13). (Wen writes the potential energy as $V(q_i)$; we call it $U$ to keep $V$ for the volume.)

The kinetic part still factorizes, atom by atom. The potential energy does not: it is a sum over pairs,

<Formula label="Pair interactions" tex="U = \frac12\sum_{i \neq j} v(\vec q_i - \vec q_j)" cite="reading1" at="p. 13, point ①" />

where $v(\vec q)$ is the interaction of two atoms a distance $\vec q$ apart and the $\frac12$ stops each pair being counted twice. Every atom's position is coupled to every other's, and the integral over $3N$ coordinates can no longer be done exactly.

## The mean-field approximation

Wen's way out is to replace $U$ by its **average**, the **mean field** (notes p. 13). If the atoms are spread uniformly with density $n = N/V$, the chance of finding one atom in $d^3q$ and another in $d^3q'$ is $n^2\,d^3q\,d^3q'$, so

<Formula label="Mean-field interaction energy" tex="U \approx \langle U\rangle = \frac12\int d^3q\,d^3q'\,n^2\,v(\vec q - \vec q\,') = \frac12 N n\,\bar v, \qquad \bar v = \int d^3q\,v(\vec q)" cite="reading1" at="p. 13, point ①" />

where $\bar v$ is the **average potential**, the interaction integrated over all separations. (The step from the double integral is $\frac12 V n^2\bar v = \frac12 N n\bar v$; the notes' intermediate line drops one factor of $n$ but end with the same result.) The mean field is a number, not a function of the positions, so it comes straight out of the integral.

That is fine for the weak attraction at a distance, but not for the hard core: at short range $v$ is huge (for hard balls, infinite), and its average would be meaningless. The hard core is taken into account differently, in the box at the right of the notes: each atom cannot enter the space already taken up by the others, so the volume available to it is not $V$ but $V - b$, where $b$ is the **excluded volume**. For hard balls whose centres cannot come closer than about $r_0$ (the hard-core size) it is of order $N\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}r_0^3$ (notes p. 13). (The notes define $\bar v = \int d^3q\,v(\vec q)$ without saying where to integrate; reading it as the integral over the attractive part outside the core, with the hard core entering only through $b$, is ours, and it is what makes the two approximations fit together.)

With both approximations the position integrals each give $V - b$, the wave-vector integrals each give one over the cube of the thermal wavelength, and

<Formula label="Mean-field partition function" tex="Q = \frac{1}{N!}\left[\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,e^{-\beta\varepsilon(\vec k)}\right]^N(V - b)^N\,e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}Nn\bar v} = \frac{1}{N!}\left(\frac{V - b}{\lambda^3}\right)^N e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}Nn\bar v}" cite="reading1" at="p. 13, point ①" />

where $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}$ is the thermal de Broglie wavelength (the Gaussian integral over $\vec k$ is $(m k_BT/2\pi\hbar^2)^{3/2} = 1/\lambda^3$). Without interactions, $b = 0$ and $\bar v = 0$, this is the ideal gas.

## The free energy

The Helmholtz free energy is $A = -k_BT\ln Q$. With Stirling's approximation $\ln N! \approx N\ln N - N$ (a step the notes take without naming it, p. 13):

<Formula label="Free energy in the mean-field approximation" tex="A = Nk_BT\left(\ln\frac{N\lambda^3}{V - b} - 1\right) + \frac12 N n\bar v = Nk_BT\left(\ln\frac{N\lambda^3}{V - b} - 1\right) + \frac12\,\frac{N^2\bar v}{V}" cite="reading1" at="p. 13, point ①" />

The first term is the ideal gas's free energy with the volume reduced by $b$; the second is the mean interaction energy. Because $n = N/V$, the interaction term depends on the volume, and that is the source of all the new physics.

## The van der Waals equation of state

The pressure is minus the derivative of the free energy with respect to volume (notes p. 14, point ②):

<Formula label="Pressure" tex="P = -\frac{\partial A}{\partial V} = \frac{Nk_BT}{V - b} + \frac12\,\frac{N^2\bar v}{V^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 14, point ②" />

Moving the second term to the left and multiplying by $V - b$ gives the equation of state. Wen names the constant in front of $1/V^2$ (notes p. 14):

<Formula label="The van der Waals equation of state" tex="(V - b)\left(P + \frac{a}{V^2}\right) = Nk_BT, \qquad a = -\frac12N^2\bar v" cite="reading1" at="p. 14, point ②" />

and in the same constants the free energy of the van der Waals model is

<Formula label="Free energy of the van der Waals model" tex="A = Nk_BT\left(\ln\frac{N\lambda^3}{V - b} - 1\right) - \frac{a}{V}" cite="reading1" at="p. 14, point ②" />

(The notes write $R$ for $Nk_B$, which is the gas constant when $N$ is one mole; here we keep $Nk_B$.) Both constants have a plain meaning. The hard core makes $b > 0$ and raises the pressure: the atoms have less room. An attraction makes $\bar v < 0$, hence $a > 0$, and lowers the pressure: an atom about to hit the wall is pulled back by the others behind it. Since $b$ and $a$ grow as $N$ and $N^2$, it is the ratios $b/V$ and $a/V^2$ that matter, as they should for an intensive pressure. (In tables, $a$ and $b$ are usually given per mole, that is, for $N$ equal to Avogadro's number.)

A real interaction potential has both parts: a steep repulsive core and a shallow attractive well (Wen sketches one on notes p. 18). The plot shows a common smooth model of that shape.

## The virial expansion

At low density every gas is nearly ideal, and the corrections can be written as a power series in $1/V$, the **virial expansion** (notes p. 14). For the van der Waals gas, expanding $P = Nk_BT/(V - b) - a/V^2$ in $b/V$ gives

<Formula label="Virial expansion" tex="\frac{PV}{Nk_BT} = 1 + \frac{C_2(T)}{V} + \frac{C_3(T)}{V^2} + \cdots, \qquad C_2 = b - \frac{a}{Nk_BT}, \quad C_3 = b^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 14, point ②" />

(The notes write the third term as $C_3/V^3$; it is $C_3/V^2$, one power of $1/V$ more than the second.) The **second virial coefficient** $C_2$ is the first correction to the ideal gas. If we ignore the attraction, it is simply the excluded volume, $C_2 = b$ (notes p. 14). With the attraction, $C_2$ is negative at low temperature, where the attraction wins, and positive at high temperature, where the hard core wins. It passes through zero at the **Boyle temperature** $T_B = a/(bNk_B)$, where the gas behaves almost ideally over a wide range of densities. (The Boyle temperature is the page's addition.)

Near T = T_B the first correction vanishes and only the b² term remains." />

The second virial coefficient can be measured, and Wen compares two simple gases (notes p. 14). For **hard balls**, which only repel, $C_2$ is a positive volume of order $N\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}r_0^3$. (For hard spheres whose centres cannot come closer than $r_0$, the exact value is $N\cdot\frac{2\pi}{3}r_0^3$; the notes' expression gives the order of magnitude, as their estimate of $b$ did.) For an **ideal Fermi gas**, whose atoms do not interact at all, the result for one spin state is

<Formula label="Second virial coefficient of an ideal Fermi gas" tex="C_2 = N\,\frac{\lambda^3}{2^{5/2}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 14, point ②" />

which is positive too. Wen's margin note draws the conclusion: a Fermi gas behaves like a gas of hard balls whose size is of order $\lambda$. Two identical fermions cannot be in the same state, so they keep out of each other's way within about a thermal wavelength, and the gas pushes on the walls a little harder than a classical ideal gas. Setting $N\frac{2\pi}{3}r_0^3$ equal to $N\lambda^3/2^{5/2}$ gives an effective hard-core distance $r_0 \approx 0.44\,\lambda$. (For bosons the same coefficient is negative, $-N\lambda^3/2^{5/2}$, an effective attraction; this, and the factor for several spin states, which divides $C_2$ by their number, are the page's additions. The quantum gases themselves are the subject of Chapters VII and VIII.)

<WorkedExample title="A model gas: hard cores and a shallow well">
<Problem>
A model gas has hard cores whose centres cannot come closer than $r_0 = 0.34$ nm, and an attraction of constant depth $\varepsilon = k_B \times 120$ K for separations from $r_0$ to $1.5\,r_0$, with no interaction beyond. For one mole, find $b$ (using the exact hard-sphere value $\frac{2\pi}{3}r_0^3$ per atom), $\bar v$ and $a$, the Boyle temperature, and $C_2$ at 150 K and 600 K. At 150 K and atmospheric pressure, by how much does the gas fall short of ideal?
</Problem>
<Step title="The excluded volume">
$b = N_A\cdot\frac{2\pi}{3}r_0^3 = 6.022 \times 10^{23} \times 2.094 \times (3.4 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{m})^3 \approx 4.96 \times 10^{-5}$ m³, about 49.6 cm³ per mole.
</Step>
<Step title="The average potential and a">
The well fills a shell from $r_0$ to $1.5\,r_0$, of volume $\frac{4\pi}{3}(1.5^3 - 1)r_0^3$, so $\bar v = -\varepsilon \cdot \frac{4\pi}{3}(1.5^3 - 1)r_0^3$. Then $a = -\frac12N_A^2\bar v = \frac12N_A^2\,\varepsilon\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}\times 2.375\,r_0^3 \approx 0.117$ Pa m⁶ per mole squared.
</Step>
<Step title="The Boyle temperature">
$T_B = a/(bN_Ak_B)$. The factors of $r_0^3$ and $N_A$ cancel and leave $T_B = 2.375\,\varepsilon/k_B = 285$ K.
</Step>
<Step title="The second virial coefficient">
$C_2 = b\,(1 - T_B/T)$: at 600 K, $49.6 \times (1 - 0.475) \approx 26.0$ cm³ per mole, positive; at 150 K, $49.6 \times (1 - 1.9) \approx -44.6$ cm³ per mole, negative.
</Step>
<Step title="The deviation from ideal">
At 150 K and 101325 Pa, one mole of ideal gas fills $N_Ak_BT/P \approx 0.0123$ m³. The correction $C_2/V$ is then $-44.6 \times 10^{-6}/0.0123 \approx -0.0036$, or −0.36%: the product $PV$ is about 0.36% below $Nk_BT$, small but easily measured.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

(The mean field is the first term of an expansion in $\varepsilon/k_BT$, so at 150 K, where $\varepsilon/k_BT = 0.8$, the model's own exact second virial coefficient differs a lot: computed without the mean-field approximation it is about $-95$ cm³ per mole, more than twice as negative, and the exact Boyle temperature is about 341 K. At 600 K, where $\varepsilon/k_BT = 0.2$, the two agree better, 23.5 against 26.0 cm³ per mole. The mean field gives the right sizes and signs, not precise values. The [next page](/courses/8-08/van-der-waals-phase-transitions) finds that these values of $a$ and $b$ put the critical temperature at $8a/(27bN_Ak_B) \approx 84$ K.)

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Ideal gases in the early universe">
The gases of the hot early universe (photons, neutrinos, electrons, positrons and nuclei) are treated as ideal, and the virial expansion says why that is safe: the corrections are of order $C_2/V$, an interaction volume per particle times the number density. What does not go away is the "statistical interaction" of identical particles that gave the Fermi gas its positive $C_2$. In the early universe the particles are relativistic and their thermal wavelength is comparable to their spacing, so this is no small correction: it is why each fermion species contributes $7/8$ as much to the energy density as a boson species with the same number of spin states, a factor that runs through the counting of particles in [MIT 8.286 Lecture 17](/courses/8-286/neutrinos-and-particle-counting).
</Callout>

## Summary

- Interactions spoil the factorization of the partition function. In the **mean-field approximation** the pair energy $\frac12\sum v(\vec q_i - \vec q_j)$ is replaced by its average $\frac12Nn\bar v$, with $\bar v = \int d^3q\,v(\vec q)$ over the attractive part, and the hard core reduces the volume to $V - b$.
- Then $Q = (1/N!)[(V - b)/\lambda^3]^N e^{-\beta Nn\bar v/2}$ and $A = Nk_BT[\ln(N\lambda^3/(V - b)) - 1] - a/V$, with $a = -\frac12N^2\bar v$.
- $P = -\partial A/\partial V$ gives the **van der Waals equation** $(V - b)(P + a/V^2) = Nk_BT$: the hard core raises the pressure, the attraction ($a > 0$) lowers it.
- Its **virial expansion** has $C_2 = b - a/(Nk_BT)$ and $C_3 = b^2$; $C_2$ changes sign at the Boyle temperature $a/(bNk_B)$.
- Hard balls have $C_2$ of order $N\frac{4\pi}{3}r_0^3$; an ideal Fermi gas has $C_2 = N\lambda^3/2^{5/2}$, as if the Pauli principle gave the atoms a hard core of size about $\lambda$.
