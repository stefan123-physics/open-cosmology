Mean-field theory, on the [previous page](/courses/8-08/ising-model-mean-field), solved the Ising model approximately and found a transition at $T_c = zJ/k_B$. Wen now steps back (notes pp. 24–27, points ③ to ⑤): close to $T_c$ the order parameter is small, and that alone fixes the shape of the free energy. The result is **Ginzburg–Landau theory** (Wen's name for what is also called Landau theory), which predicts how the order parameter, the heat capacity and the susceptibility behave near the transition, draws the phase diagram in a field, and applies unchanged to the critical point of water. (Citations use the handwritten page numbers 21 to 27 at the top right of the scanned notes, and Wen's circled point numbers. $N$ is the number of spins, $\langle s\rangle$ the average spin and $h$ the field times the spin's moment, as on the previous page. On this page $\beta$, $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ are critical exponents; temperatures are written out as $k_BT$.)

## Expanding the free energy near the transition

Near the transition $\langle s\rangle$ is small, so expand the free energy at $h = 0$ in powers of it (notes p. 24, point ③). Only even powers appear, because flipping every spin, $\langle s\rangle \to -\langle s\rangle$, does not change the energy. Wen's boxed expansion is

<Formula label="Ginzburg–Landau free energy" tex="A = \frac{N}{2}\,a\langle s\rangle^2 + \frac{N}{4}\,b\langle s\rangle^4 + A_0(T), \qquad A_0(T) = -k_BT\ln 2\;N" cite="reading1" at="p. 24, point ③ (boxed)" />

where $a$ and $b$ are functions of $T$. The constant $A_0$ is the free energy of the disordered state; $-k_BT\ln 2$ per spin is the free energy of a spin with two equally likely states. The equilibrium $\langle s\rangle$ makes $A$ smallest (notes p. 24):

<Formula label="Condition for the minimum" tex="\frac{\partial A}{\partial\langle s\rangle} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad a\langle s\rangle + b\langle s\rangle^3 = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 24, point ③ (boxed)" />

For the transition to happen at $T_c$, the coefficient $a$ must change sign there, while $b$ stays positive so that $A$ is bounded below: $a(T_c) = 0$ and $b(T_c) > 0$. Near $T_c$ we may write (notes p. 24)

<Formula label="The coefficient a near the transition" tex="a = a_0\left(\frac{T}{T_c} - 1\right), \qquad a_0 > 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 24, point ③" />

The mean-field free energy of the previous page shows where these coefficients come from (this step is ours, not in the notes). At $h = 0$ it is $N[-k_BT\ln(2\cosh(zJ\langle s\rangle/k_BT)) + \frac12 zJ\langle s\rangle^2]$. With $\ln\cosh x = \frac12 x^2 - \frac{1}{12}x^4 + \ldots$ and $zJ = k_BT_c$, it becomes $A_0(T)$, exactly Wen's, plus

<Formula label="Coefficients of the mean-field Ising model" tex="a = k_BT_c\left(1 - \frac{T_c}{T}\right) \approx k_BT_c\left(\frac{T}{T_c} - 1\right), \qquad b = \frac{k_BT_c}{3}\left(\frac{T_c}{T}\right)^3 \approx \frac{k_BT_c}{3}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 22 and 24 (worked out here)" />

so $a_0 = k_BT_c$ and $b(T_c) = k_BT_c/3$. Ginzburg–Landau theory does not need these values: any system whose free energy can be expanded like this behaves in the same way near $T_c$, whatever the coefficients.

## The order parameter: the exponent β

The condition $a\langle s\rangle + b\langle s\rangle^3 = 0$ has the root $\langle s\rangle = 0$ always, and the roots $\langle s\rangle^2 = -a/b$ when $a < 0$. So (notes p. 24)

<Formula label="Order parameter in Ginzburg–Landau theory" tex="\langle s\rangle = \begin{cases} 0, & T > T_c \\ \pm\sqrt{\dfrac{a_0}{b}}\sqrt{1 - \dfrac{T}{T_c}} = \pm\sqrt{\dfrac{-a}{b}}, & T < T_c \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="p. 24, point ③" />

Below $T_c$ the non-zero roots are the minima and $\langle s\rangle = 0$ is a maximum, as in the sketches of the previous page. With the reduced temperature $t = 1 - T/T_c$, the order parameter grows as a power of $t$ (notes p. 24):

<Formula label="The exponent β" tex="t \equiv 1 - \frac{T}{T_c}, \qquad \langle s\rangle \propto t^{\beta}, \qquad \beta = \frac12" cite="reading1" at="p. 24, point ③" />

Wen sketches this curve: zero above $T_c$, rising steeply just below it. $\beta$ is a **critical exponent**: it describes how the order parameter (here the magnetization) vanishes at $T_c$. The square root makes the fall to zero infinitely steep at $T_c$.

## The free energy and the heat capacity: the exponent α

Putting the solution back into $A$ (notes p. 24): above $T_c$, $A = A_0(T)$; below it, $\frac12 Na\langle s\rangle^2 + \frac14 Nb\langle s\rangle^4 = -\frac12 Na^2/b + \frac14 Na^2/b$, so

<Formula label="Free energy in equilibrium" tex="A = \begin{cases} A_0(T), & T > T_c \\ -\dfrac{N}{4}\,\dfrac{a_0^2t^2}{b} + A_0(T), & T < T_c \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="p. 24, point ③" />

The ordered phase has the lower free energy, as it should. The extra term is of order $t^2$, so $A$ and its first derivative, minus the entropy $S = -\partial A/\partial T$, are continuous at $T_c$: there is no latent heat. Wen sketches $S$ rising linearly below $T_c$ and joining the $-\partial A_0/\partial T$ of the disordered phase at $T_c$ with a kink. The second derivative jumps. The heat capacity is (notes p. 24)

<Formula label="Heat capacity and the exponent α" tex="C_V = -T\frac{\partial^2A}{\partial T^2} = T\frac{\partial S}{\partial T} \propto |t|^{-\alpha}, \qquad \alpha = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 24, point ③" />

An exponent of zero means no divergence: $C_V$ has a finite jump, larger below $T_c$ than above, which is the jump in $\partial^2A/\partial T^2$ of Wen's p. 21. Its size follows from the $t^2$ term (our step): $\partial^2/\partial T^2$ of $-Na_0^2(1 - T/T_c)^2/4b$ is $-Na_0^2/(2bT_c^2)$, so at $T_c$

<Formula label="Size of the heat-capacity jump" tex="\Delta C_V = \frac{N a_0^2}{2\,b\,T_c} = \frac32 Nk_B \quad\text{(mean field)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 24 (worked out here)" />

with the mean-field values of $a_0$ and $b$. (Wen's sketch of $A(T)$ on this page again rises with $T$; with $\partial A/\partial T = -S < 0$ it in fact falls, a point that does not affect the argument.)

## Response to a field: the exponent γ

A field adds the energy $-h\sum_i s_i = -Nh\langle s\rangle$ (notes p. 25):

<Formula label="Ginzburg–Landau free energy in a field" tex="A = A_0(T) + \frac{N}{2}\,a\langle s\rangle^2 + \frac{N}{4}\,b\langle s\rangle^4 - N\langle s\rangle h" cite="reading1" at="p. 25" />

and the free energy as a function of $T$, $N$ and $h$ is its minimum over $\langle s\rangle$, $A(T, N, h) = A_{\text{min}}$. Above $T_c$, with $a > 0$ and a small field, $\langle s\rangle$ stays small and the quartic term can be dropped. Then $\partial A/\partial\langle s\rangle = Na\langle s\rangle - Nh = 0$ gives $\langle s\rangle = h/a$ and (notes p. 25)

<Formula label="Free energy above T_c in a weak field" tex="A_{\text{min}} = -\frac12\,\frac{h^2}{a}\,N + O(h^4)" cite="reading1" at="p. 25 (boxed)" />

The magnetization is minus the derivative of the free energy with respect to the field, and here it is simply the total spin (notes p. 25):

<Formula label="Magnetization and susceptibility above T_c" tex="M = -\frac{\partial A(T, N, h)}{\partial h} = -\frac{\partial A_{\text{min}}}{\partial h} = \sum_i s_i = \frac{N}{a}\,h, \qquad \chi = \frac{1}{V}\frac{\partial M}{\partial h} = \frac{N}{aV}" cite="reading1" at="p. 25" />

with $V$ the volume of the sample. Wen's "quick way" (p. 25): $\chi$ is $N/V$ divided by the coefficient of $\frac12 N\langle s\rangle^2$ in $A$, that is, by the curvature of the free energy at its minimum. A flat minimum is easy to push. Since $a \propto T - T_c$, the susceptibility diverges at the transition (notes p. 25):

<Formula label="The exponent γ" tex="\chi \propto |t|^{-\gamma}, \qquad \gamma = 1" cite="reading1" at="p. 25 (boxed)" />

Below $T_c$, expand about one of the minima, $\langle s\rangle = s_{\text{min}} + \delta\langle s\rangle$ with $s_{\text{min}} = \sqrt{-a/b}$ (the notes write this position as $\langle s\rangle$ with the subscript "min"; below $T_c$, $a < 0$, so the root is real). The second derivative of $\frac12 a\langle s\rangle^2 + \frac14 b\langle s\rangle^4$ at the minimum is $a + 3bs_{\text{min}}^2 = a - 3a = -2a$, so (notes p. 25)

<Formula label="Expansion about the ordered minimum" tex="A = \text{const} + N\,\frac12\left(a + 3bs_{\text{min}}^2\right)\left(\delta\langle s\rangle\right)^2 - Nh\,\delta\langle s\rangle, \qquad \chi = \frac{N}{-2aV} \propto |t|^{-\gamma}, \quad \gamma = 1" cite="reading1" at="p. 25" />

The same exponent, but at the same distance from $T_c$ the susceptibility below is half that above: the ordered state sits in a steeper well. (The factor of 2 is our remark; it is a standard prediction of the theory.)

  curves={[
    { expr: "x > 1 ? 1/(x - 1) : NaN", label: "above T_c" },
    { expr: "x < 1 ? 1/(2*(1 - x)) : NaN", label: "below T_c", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="The susceptibility of Ginzburg–Landau theory near T_c, computed from χ = N/(aV) above and χ = N/(−2aV) below, with a = a₀(T/T_c − 1). Both branches diverge as 1/|T − T_c| (γ = 1); at the same distance from T_c the branch below is half as high, so at T/T_c = 1.1 the value is 10 and at 0.9 it is 5." />

## The phase diagram in a field

Wen next asks what happens away from $h = 0$ (notes p. 26, point ④). The free energy in a field is a tilted double well: the term $-Nh\langle s\rangle$ lowers the side with the sign of $h$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-1.6, 1.6]} range={[-0.7, 0.6]} xLabel="⟨s⟩" yLabel="(A − A₀)/(N a₀)" samples={300}
  curves={[
    { expr: "0.5*(r - 1)*x^2 + 0.25*x^4 - h*x", label: "A(⟨s⟩)" }
  ]}
  params={{ r: { value: 0.6, min: 0.2, max: 1.6, step: 0.05, label: "T/T_c" }, h: { value: 0, min: -0.3, max: 0.3, step: 0.01, label: "h/a_0" } }}
  caption="The Ginzburg–Landau free energy per spin in a field, computed from ½a⟨s⟩² + ¼b⟨s⟩⁴ − h⟨s⟩ with a = a₀(T/T_c − 1) and, for drawing, b = a₀. Above T_c (first slider above 1) there is one minimum, pushed sideways by h. Below T_c there are two; at h = 0 they are equally deep, and a small h makes one the lower: as h passes through zero the deepest minimum jumps from one side to the other, which is the first-order transition. The higher minimum, while it lasts, is a metastable state (a remark not in the notes)." />

- **At fixed $h \neq 0$**, $A_{\text{min}}(T)$ is a smooth function of $T$ (notes p. 26). The field picks one side at every temperature, so as $T$ falls the minimum moves smoothly from small $\langle s\rangle$ to large; nothing singular happens and there is **no phase transition**.
- **At fixed $T > T_c$**, sweeping $h$ from negative to positive moves the single minimum smoothly through zero.
- **At fixed $T < T_c$**, the same sweep makes the deepest minimum jump from $-\sqrt{-a/b}$ to $+\sqrt{-a/b}$ as $h$ passes through zero. $A(h, T)$ has a kink there, and $M$ jumps: a **first-order** transition.

In the $(T, h)$ plane, then, the first-order transitions form a line along $h = 0$ from $T = 0$ to $T_c$, with $M = +N\sqrt{-a/b}$ just above it and $M = -N\sqrt{-a/b}$ just below (notes p. 26). The line ends at $T_c$, where the jump has shrunk to zero: there the transition, met along $h = 0$, is **second order**, continuous in $M$ with a singularity only in derivatives. Wen marks the free energies of the two kinds of transition point: a double well with equal minima on the line, a single flat-bottomed well at the end point.

Across the first-order line the magnetization jumps by (notes p. 26)

<Formula label="Jump in magnetization" tex="\Delta M = 2N\sqrt{\frac{-a}{b}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 26, point ④" />

(the notes write $2\sqrt{-a/b}$, the jump per spin). It shrinks to zero as $T \to T_c$, where the second-order transition has $\Delta M = 0$.

Is there a **latent heat**, $T\Delta S$? On the line the two minima have the same free energy (notes p. 26),

<Formula label="Free energies of the two states" tex="A^{(1)}_{\text{min}} = A^{(2)}_{\text{min}} = -\frac{N a^2}{4b} + A_0, \qquad S_1 = -\frac{\partial A^{(1)}_{\text{min}}}{\partial T} = S_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 26, point ④" />

and so the same entropy: $\Delta S = 0$ and the latent heat is zero. (The notes write $-\frac12 Na^2/b$ here, a slip against the $-\frac14$ of p. 24; the conclusion does not depend on it. The notes also leave out $A_0$, which is the same on both sides, and write the entropy with a minus sign in front of $\frac{N}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial T}(a^2/b)$ where a plus belongs.) The reason is the symmetry $s_i \to -s_i$, which maps one state onto the other. Wen adds that in general, without such a symmetry, $\Delta S \neq 0$: a first-order transition then has a latent heat, as boiling does.

## Application: the critical point of water

Ginzburg–Landau theory is not only about magnets (notes p. 27, point ⑤). Take the phase diagram of water and its vapour in the $(T, P)$ plane: the boiling curve, where liquid and vapour coexist, ends at a **critical point**, beyond which there is no difference between them. Near it, write the density as

<Formula label="Density near the critical point" tex="n = n_c(P, T) + \delta n" cite="reading1" at="p. 27, point ⑤" />

where $\delta n$ plays the part of the order parameter. The Gibbs free energy, as a function of the density, can again be expanded in powers of $\delta n$. Here there is no up-down symmetry, so odd powers appear; but the reference density $n_c(P, T)$ can be chosen to make the cubic term vanish, leaving (notes p. 27, boxed)

<Formula label="Ginzburg–Landau theory of the liquid–gas transition" tex="G(T, P, n) = h(T, P)\,\delta n + r_0(T, P)\,\delta n^2 + u_0\,\delta n^4" cite="reading1" at="p. 27, point ⑤ (boxed)" />

(Wen's sketch marks the double wells of the two coexisting phases along the boiling curve and the critical point where $h = r_0 = 0$.) This is the magnet's free energy with new names: $h(T, P)$ acts as the field, $r_0$ as $a/2$ and $u_0$ as $b/4$. So the first-order line, the boiling curve near the critical point, is where the two wells are equally deep, $h(T, P) = 0$; and the critical point, where the double well turns into a single flat one, is where both coefficients vanish (notes p. 27):

<Formula label="The critical point" tex="h(P_c, T_c) = 0, \qquad r_0(P_c, T_c) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 27, point ⑤" />

two equations that fix the two numbers $P_c$ and $T_c$. Crossing the boiling curve, the density jumps (liquid to vapour), like $M$ crossing $h = 0$; going round the end of the curve, it changes smoothly. Near $(P_c, T_c)$, Wen concludes, the critical point of water is the same as that of the Ising model, because both are described by the same Ginzburg–Landau theory: an example of **universality**.

(A remark not in the notes. Universality holds even better than this suggests. Near a critical point fluctuations matter, and the true exponents in three dimensions are not the mean-field ones: measurements on fluids such as water, whose critical point lies near 647 K and 22.1 MPa, give $\beta \approx 0.33$ rather than $\frac12$. But the liquid–gas critical point and the three-dimensional Ising magnet share the same corrected exponents, which is universality in its full form.)

<WorkedExample title="Ginzburg–Landau theory for the mean-field magnet">
<Problem>
Take the square-lattice magnet of the previous page in mean-field theory, with $T_c = 200$ K, so that $a_0 = k_BT_c$ and $b = k_BT_c/3$. Find $\langle s\rangle$ from Ginzburg–Landau theory at 190 K and at 150 K and compare with the full self-consistent equation. Then find the jump in the heat capacity per mole, the ratio of the susceptibilities at 190 K and 210 K, and the jump in magnetization per spin across $h = 0$ at 190 K.
</Problem>
<Step title="The order parameter">
$\langle s\rangle = \sqrt{a_0/b}\,\sqrt{t} = \sqrt{3t}$. At 190 K, $t = 0.05$ and $\langle s\rangle = \sqrt{0.15} \approx 0.387$; the full equation $\langle s\rangle = \tanh(200\langle s\rangle/190)$ gives 0.379, so the expansion is 2% high. At 150 K, $t = 0.25$ and $\sqrt{0.75} \approx 0.866$, against the full 0.776 of the previous page: 12% high. The expansion works only while $\langle s\rangle$ is small.
</Step>
<Step title="The heat capacity">
$\Delta C_V = Na_0^2/(2bT_c) = N(k_BT_c)^2/(2T_c \cdot k_BT_c/3) = \frac32 Nk_B$. For a mole, $\frac32 R = 1.5 \times 8.314$ J/(mol K) $\approx 12.5$ J/(mol K), whatever $T_c$ is.
</Step>
<Step title="The susceptibility">
At 210 K, $a = a_0 \times 0.05$ and $\chi = N/(0.05\,a_0V)$. At 190 K, $a = -a_0 \times 0.05$ and $\chi = N/(2 \times 0.05\,a_0V)$. The ratio $\chi(190\ \text{K})/\chi(210\ \text{K})$ is $0.5$.
</Step>
<Step title="The jump in magnetization">
$\Delta M/N = 2\sqrt{-a/b} = 2\sqrt{3t} = 2 \times 0.387 \approx 0.775$: reversing a weak field at 190 K turns about 39% of the spins, since $0.775/2 = 0.387$ of them must flip to change $\langle s\rangle$ from $+0.387$ to $-0.387$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="False vacua and phase transitions in the early universe">
The tilted double well above is the picture behind inflation. In Guth's [false vacuum lecture](/courses/8-286/false-vacuum), a scalar field sits in a minimum of its potential energy that is not the lowest one, just like the magnet held in the higher well after the field has been reversed. Such a state can last a long time, and while it does its energy density stays constant and drives exponential expansion. Leaving it is a first-order transition: bubbles of the true vacuum form and grow, as droplets do in a supercooled vapour. Guth's original model failed because those bubbles never merged smoothly; the [Higgs field page](/courses/8-286/higgs-field) draws the second-order picture of a single well turning into a ring of minima.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Near $T_c$ the order parameter is small and the free energy can be expanded: $A = A_0 + \frac12 Na\langle s\rangle^2 + \frac14 Nb\langle s\rangle^4 - Nh\langle s\rangle$, with $a = a_0(T/T_c - 1)$ and $b > 0$. Mean-field theory gives $a_0 = k_BT_c$, $b = k_BT_c/3$, but the conclusions do not depend on the values.
- **Critical exponents** (with $t = 1 - T/T_c$): $\langle s\rangle \propto t^{\beta}$, $\beta = \frac12$; $C_V \propto |t|^{-\alpha}$, $\alpha = 0$, a finite jump ($\frac32 Nk_B$ in mean field); $\chi \propto |t|^{-\gamma}$, $\gamma = 1$, with $\chi$ below $T_c$ half that above.
- In a field: at fixed $h \neq 0$ no transition; at fixed $T < T_c$, sweeping $h$ through zero makes $M$ jump by $2N\sqrt{-a/b}$, a **first-order** transition with no latent heat because of the up-down symmetry. The line of first-order transitions ends at $T_c$ in a **second-order** transition.
- For water, $\delta n = n - n_c$ is the order parameter and $G = h\,\delta n + r_0\delta n^2 + u_0\delta n^4$: the boiling curve is $h = 0$, the critical point $h = r_0 = 0$. Same theory, same critical behaviour: **universality**.
