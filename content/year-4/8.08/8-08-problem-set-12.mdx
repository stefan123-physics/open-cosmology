This set practises Wen's Chapter IX, transport: the [mean free path, effusion and diffusion](/courses/8-08/mean-free-path-sound-diffusion), [viscosity, heat conduction and the Drude model](/courses/8-08/viscosity-conduction-boltzmann-equation), and [Brownian motion, the Einstein relation and noise](/courses/8-08/einstein-relation-langevin-noise). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 12 (dated April 27, 2005), the last set of the course, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its dates are left out. MIT's Problems 2, 3 and 4 are stated in full on the sheet, with MIT's numbers, and are given here in our words with all their parts. They ask for estimates, and an estimate depends on the formulas chosen, so each of them states the route we assume ("our reading"), and its answer boxes accept that route within a few percent; the solutions say what another reasonable route would give. Where we split one of MIT's parts into two boxes, the part says so. MIT's Problem 1 is only a pointer to a problem in a textbook (K. Huang, *Statistical Mechanics*), named by number, and the textbook is not used here: **Problem 1 is our own problem**, on a subject of Chapter IX: the heat carried by the conduction electrons of a metal, which joins the notes' heat conduction to their Drude model and gives the Wiedemann–Franz law. MIT's Problem 5 points to another problem of the same textbook, but adds two changes of its own: the correlation function has a Gaussian form, and the power spectrum is to be found at all frequencies. Problem 5 keeps MIT's two changes, in our words, and **its physical setup and its other parts are ours**: a Brownian particle pushed by a random force with MIT's correlation function. Our assumptions and values are marked where they stand.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it; type decimals, for example `0.25` or `-1.5`, in the unit the box names. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `sqrt(c*x/(d + y))^3`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `kB` stands for $k_B$, `gamma` for $\gamma$, `tau` for $\tau$, `omega` for $\omega$, `pi` is π, `sqrt` the square root and `exp` the exponential; the other letters (`n`, `m`, `T`, `e`, `A`) are typed as they are.

MIT's sheet gives no points. The points are ours: 15 each for Problems 1, 2 and 3, and 20 each for Problems 4 and 5, 85 points in all; every split between parts is ours too. Parts that ask you to show something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes. $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature, $m$ the mass of one particle, $n$ a number density, $\lambda$ a mean free path and $\bar v$ a typical speed. In Problem 1, $K$ is the thermal conductivity of the electrons, $\sigma$ their electrical conductivity, $c_v$ the heat capacity per electron, $\epsilon_F$ the Fermi energy, $v_F$ the Fermi speed, $\tau$ the relaxation time and $L$ the Lorenz number. In Problem 2, $K$ is the thermal conductivity, $C$ the heat capacity per unit volume, $c_s$ the speed of sound and $n_a$ the number of atoms per unit volume. In Problem 3, $\rho$ is the resistivity, $\tau$ the relaxation time of the electrons, $e$ and $m$ their charge and mass and $v_F$ their Fermi speed. In Problems 2 and 3, $\rho_m$ is the mass density. In Problem 4, $\sigma$ is the cross-section of a molecule, $\nu$ the viscosity (Wen's letter), $a$ the radius of the ball, $\eta$ its mobility and $D$ its diffusion constant. In Problem 5, $\tilde F$ is the random force, $\varphi(t)$ its correlation function with MIT's constants $A$ and $\tau$, $\gamma$ the damping rate, $\omega$ the angular frequency, and $P^F(\omega)$ and $P^v(\omega)$ the power spectra of the force and of the velocity.

Constants (CODATA 2018): $k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K, $e = 1.602177 \times 10^{-19}$ C, $m_e = 9.109384 \times 10^{-31}$ kg, $\hbar = 1.054572 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and the atomic mass unit $1\ \text{u} = 1.660539 \times 10^{-27}$ kg.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Heat carried by the electrons of a metal: the Wiedemann–Franz law (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on heat conduction and the Drude model of Chapter IX, applied together to the conduction electrons of a metal.

Our model: the conduction electrons of a metal are a free, degenerate Fermi gas of density $n$, electron mass $m$ and Fermi energy $\epsilon_F = \frac12mv_F^2$, at a temperature $k_BT \ll \epsilon_F$, scattered with a relaxation time $\tau$. They carry heat as the molecules of a gas do, with $K = \frac13 n\bar v\lambda c_v$ (standard kinetic theory with the angle average done in full, stated by us; the notes' $\frac16$, reading 1 p. 104, gives half), with the speed $\bar v = v_F$ and the mean free path $\lambda = v_F\tau$ (the speed and the path of Problem 3), and with $c_v$ the heat capacity per electron of the degenerate Fermi gas, from the notes' Chapter VIII (reading 2, p. 87):

<Formula label="Heat capacity per electron" tex="c_v = \frac{\pi^2}{2}\,k_B\,\frac{k_BT}{\epsilon_F}" cite="reading2" at="p. 87" />

The same electrons conduct electricity with the Drude conductivity $\sigma = e^2n\tau/m$ (reading 1, p. 106). The lattice vibrations also carry some heat; we leave them out (our assumption).

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the electrons' thermal conductivity $K$ in terms of $n$, $\tau$, $m$, $k_B$ and $T$.

<Hint>

Put the three ingredients into the formula, and write $\epsilon_F$ in terms of $v_F$: the factors of $v_F$ cancel.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-k" label="$K =$" expr="pi^2*n*tau*kB^2*T/(3*m)"
  vars={{ n: [1, 10], tau: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ n: "n", tau: "\\tau", kB: "k_B", T: "T", m: "m" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*n*tau*kB^2*T/(2*m)", hint: "That treats the electrons as a classical gas, each taking up (3/2)k_B of heat capacity and moving at a thermal speed with v̄² = 3k_BT/m. In a degenerate Fermi gas only the electrons within about k_BT of the Fermi energy take up heat, and they move at v_F." },
    { expr: "pi^2*n*tau*kB^2*T/(6*m)", hint: "That is half the result: it uses the notes' factor 1/6 in K = (1/6) n v̄ λ c_v. The model here takes the standard 1/3." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Few electrons take up heat (a fraction of order $k_BT/\epsilon_F$), but those few move very fast. The two effects meet in a $K$ that does not depend on $v_F$ at all.

<Step title="Putting it together">

$K = \frac13\,n\,v_F\,(v_F\tau)\,\frac{\pi^2}{2}k_B\frac{k_BT}{\epsilon_F}$, and $v_F^2/\epsilon_F = 2/m$:

<Formula label="Thermal conductivity of the electrons" tex="K = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\,\frac{n\tau k_B^2T}{m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own problem and solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The units: m⁻³ × s × J² K⁻² × K/kg $=$ J² s/(m³ kg K), and J $=$ kg m²/s², so this is J/(m s K) $=$ W/(m K), a thermal conductivity. Like $\sigma$, $K$ is proportional to $n\tau/m$: both are carried by the same electrons and limited by the same collisions.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the ratio $K/(\sigma T)$ in this model. It is called the Lorenz number, $L$.

<Hint>

Divide part (a) by $\sigma T = e^2n\tau T/m$. What is left over?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-l" label="$L = K/(\sigma T) =$" expr="pi^2*kB^2/(3*e^2)"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 1.5], e: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", e: "e" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi^2*kB^2/(6*e^2)", hint: "That is half the result: it comes from the notes' factor 1/6 in the kinetic formula instead of the standard 1/3." },
    { expr: "3*kB^2/(2*e^2)", hint: "That is the ratio for a classical electron gas, the first mistake listed in part (a). The heat capacity of a degenerate Fermi gas brings π²/2 times k_BT/ε_F instead of 3/2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Everything that depends on the particular metal, $n$, $\tau$ and $m$, cancels in the ratio.

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Lorenz number of the free-electron model" tex="L = \frac{K}{\sigma T} = \frac{\pi^2n\tau k_B^2T/3m}{e^2n\tau T/m} = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\left(\frac{k_B}{e}\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

This is the **Wiedemann–Franz law** (the name is our addition): good conductors of electricity are good conductors of heat, in the same proportion, at a given temperature. It holds because the same electrons carry both and the same collisions stop both; $\tau$ cancels whatever scatters the electrons, as long as a collision stops the flow of heat as well as it stops the flow of charge. (The full calculation from the Boltzmann equation, with the heat current beside the notes' electric current, gives the same value, $\frac{\pi^2}{3}(k_B/e)^2$. With the notes' $\frac16$ the ratio would be half of it, the same factor 2 as in Problem 2.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Give $L$ of part (b) as a number, in units of $10^{-8}$ W Ω/K² (tolerance 2%).

<Answer id="p1-lnum" scale="1e-8" label="$L$ in $10^{-8}$ W Ω/K²:" value="2.44" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-lnum" mistakes={[{ value: "1.22", tolerance: "2%", calc: "p1-lnum-half", hint: "That is half the result: it comes from the notes' factor 1/6 in the kinetic formula instead of the standard 1/3." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put in $k_B$ and $e$.

<Step title="The number">

$k_B/e = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}$ V/K, so $(k_B/e)^2 = 7.426 \times 10^{-9}$ V²/K², and $L = 3.290 \times 7.426 \times 10^{-9} = 2.44 \times 10^{-8}$ W Ω/K² (a V² is a W Ω).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

With the notes' $\frac16$ it would be half, $1.22 \times 10^{-8}$ W Ω/K². Measured Lorenz numbers of copper, silver and gold near room temperature are all about 2.3 in these units (our values): close to the model's value, and nearly the same for all three, as the law says.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

(Our numbers.) Aluminium has a resistivity of $2.65 \times 10^{-8}$ Ω m at 293 K (our value). With the $L$ of part (c), estimate its thermal conductivity, in W/(m K) (tolerance 2%).

<Answer id="p1-al" label="$K$ in W/(m K):" value="270" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-al" mistakes={[{ value: "135", tolerance: "2%", calc: "p1-al-half", hint: "That is half the estimate: it uses the Lorenz number with the notes' factor 1/6 instead of the standard 1/3." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The law turns an electrical measurement into a thermal one: $K = L\sigma T = LT/\rho$.

<Step title="The number">

$K = 2.44 \times 10^{-8} \times 293/(2.65 \times 10^{-8})$ W/(m K) $= 270$ W/(m K).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The measured value is about 237 W/(m K) (our value): the estimate is 14% above it, partly because real metals have a Lorenz number a little below the model's. With the notes' $\frac16$ the estimate would be 135 W/(m K), 1.75 times too small. The law explains why metals that conduct electricity well, such as aluminium and copper, are also the ones used to carry heat away, as in cooking pans and heat sinks.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The mean free path of phonons in silicon" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

Solid silicon has a mass density $\rho_m = 2.3$ g/cm³ and a speed of sound $c_s = 2200$ m/s. At $T = 300$ K its thermal conductivity is $K = 1.5$ W/(cm K). Assume that the heat is carried by phonons, and estimate their mean free path. MIT's hint: you may need to estimate the density of phonons first.

Our reading of MIT's model: the phonons carry heat as the molecules of a gas do in the notes,

<Formula label="Heat conductivity of a gas" tex="K = \frac16 n\bar v\lambda c_v" cite="reading1" at="p. 104" />

with $nc_v$ replaced by the heat capacity per unit volume $C$ of the lattice, and every phonon moving at $\bar v = c_s$. For $C$ we count the "density of phonons" as the density of vibration modes, three per atom, each holding $k_B$ of heat capacity (equipartition). The atomic mass of silicon is 28.0855 u (our value). (MIT's speed of sound is lower than the measured speeds of sound in silicon, which are several kilometres per second; we keep MIT's value.)

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the number of atoms per unit volume, $n_a$, and from it $C$. Give $C$ in units of $10^6$ J/(m³ K) (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

$n_a = \rho_m/(28.0855\ \text{u})$, in SI units. Then $C = 3n_ak_B$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" scale="1e6" label="$C$ in $10^6$ J/(m³ K):" value="2.04" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At high enough temperature every vibration mode of the lattice holds $k_BT$ of energy, so the heat capacity is just a count of modes.

<Step title="The density of atoms">

$n_a = 2300/(28.0855 \times 1.660539 \times 10^{-27})$ m⁻³ $= 4.93 \times 10^{28}$ m⁻³.

</Step>

<Step title="The heat capacity">

$C = 3n_ak_B = 3 \times 4.93 \times 10^{28} \times 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/(m³ K) $= 2.04 \times 10^6$ J/(m³ K).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Is 300 K high enough? With MIT's speed of sound, the Debye temperature $\hbar c_s(6\pi^2n_a)^{1/3}/k_B$ is 240 K, below 300 K, so equipartition is a fair estimate (our check). This is the law of Dulong and Petit: per mole, $3N_Ak_B = 24.9$ J/(mol K).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={9}>

Estimate the mean free path $\lambda$ of the phonons, in nanometres (tolerance 3%).

<Hint>

Solve the conductivity formula for $\lambda$, with $nc_v \to C$ and $\bar v \to c_s$. Convert $K$ to W/(m K) first.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-lam" label="$\lambda$ in nm:" value="200" tolerance="3%" calc="p2-lam" mistakes={[{ value: "100", tolerance: "3%", calc: "p2-third", hint: "That is half: it comes from K = (1/3)Cvλ, as many books write it. This problem uses the notes' K = (1/6) n v̄ λ c_v." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The conductivity is the heat capacity times a speed times a distance; we know the first two.

<Step title="The number">

$K = 1.5$ W/(cm K) $= 150$ W/(m K). Then

<Formula label="Mean free path of the phonons" tex="\lambda = \frac{6K}{Cc_s} = \frac{6 \times 150}{2.04 \times 10^6 \times 2200}\ \text{m} = 2.0 \times 10^{-7}\ \text{m}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

about 200 nm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

That is some 730 times the spacing between atoms, $n_a^{-1/3} = 0.27$ nm: phonons in a good crystal travel far between collisions. Many books write $K = \frac13Cv\lambda$ instead of the notes' $\frac16$ (it counts the distance from the last collision differently); that route gives half, 100 nm. Either way the answer is a few hundred lattice spacings, which is what an estimate can claim.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Electrons in copper, in the Drude model" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

Solid copper has a mass density $\rho_m = 8.9$ g/cm³ and an electrical resistivity $\rho = 1.7 \times 10^{-6}$ Ω cm. Take the density of the conduction electrons equal to the density of copper atoms, and the electron mass equal to its mass in vacuum. From the Drude model, estimate the relaxation time $\tau$ of the electrons and their mean free path $\lambda$.

Our reading: the Drude conductivity of the notes, in SI units,

<Formula label="Drude conductivity" tex="\frac{1}{\rho} = \frac{e^2n\tau}{m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 106 (the notes write the conductivity as σ = e²nτ/m; here σ = 1/ρ)" />

and $\lambda = v_F\tau$, where the speed is that of the electrons at the Fermi surface, as the notes argue (reading 1, p. 106), with the free-electron value $v_F = \hbar(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}/m$ for two spin states (our choice of the exact form). The atomic mass of copper is 63.546 u (our value). We split MIT's question into three boxes.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the density $n$ of conduction electrons, in units of $10^{28}$ m⁻³ (tolerance 1%).

<Answer id="p3-n" scale="1e28" label="$n$ in $10^{28}$ m⁻³:" value="8.43" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-n" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One conduction electron per atom.

<Step title="The number">

$n = 8900/(63.546 \times 1.660539 \times 10^{-27})$ m⁻³ $= 8.43 \times 10^{28}$ m⁻³.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the relaxation time $\tau$, in units of $10^{-14}$ s (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

Convert the resistivity to Ω m first: 1 Ω cm is $10^{-2}$ Ω m.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-tau" scale="1e-14" label="$\tau$ in $10^{-14}$ s:" value="2.47" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-tau" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Solve the Drude formula for $\tau$.

<Step title="The number">

$\rho = 1.7 \times 10^{-8}$ Ω m, and

<Formula label="Relaxation time in copper" tex="\tau = \frac{m}{\rho e^2n} = \frac{9.109 \times 10^{-31}}{1.7 \times 10^{-8} \times (1.602 \times 10^{-19})^2 \times 8.43 \times 10^{28}}\ \text{s} = 2.47 \times 10^{-14}\ \text{s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The units: kg/(Ω m × C² × m⁻³) $=$ kg m²/(Ω C²), and Ω C² $=$ (V/A) C² $=$ J s, so the result is kg m²/(J s) $=$ s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Find the mean free path $\lambda$, in nanometres (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

First $k_F = (3\pi^2n)^{1/3}$, then $v_F = \hbar k_F/m$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-lam" label="$\lambda$ in nm:" value="38.9" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-lam" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The current is carried by electrons at the Fermi surface, which move at the Fermi speed whatever the temperature.

<Step title="The Fermi speed">

$k_F = (3\pi^2 \times 8.43 \times 10^{28})^{1/3}$ m⁻¹ $= 1.36 \times 10^{10}$ m⁻¹, and $v_F = \hbar k_F/m = 1.57 \times 10^6$ m/s (a Fermi energy of 7.0 eV).

</Step>

<Step title="The mean free path">

$\lambda = v_F\tau = 1.57 \times 10^6 \times 2.47 \times 10^{-14}$ m $= 3.89 \times 10^{-8}$ m, about 39 nm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

That is about 170 times the spacing between atoms, $n^{-1/3} = 0.23$ nm. Drude's original model used the classical thermal speed instead, $\sqrt{3k_BT/m} = 1.2 \times 10^5$ m/s at 300 K, which gives a path of only 2.9 nm: thirteen times shorter, and wrong, as the notes point out by marking the classical speed with a cross (reading 1, p. 106). The notes' own rough speed, $\sqrt{\epsilon_F/m}$ (reading 1, p. 106), is $v_F/\sqrt2$ and gives 27.5 nm; $\hbar n^{1/3}/m$ gives 12.6 nm: the same order. The values are close to those of the worked example on [the lesson](/courses/8-08/viscosity-conduction-boltzmann-equation), which used slightly different data for copper.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Air, and a small ball that floats in it" points={20} mit="Problem 4">

(a) Estimate the mean free path of the molecules of air at 300 K and 1 atm, and estimate the viscosity of air. (b) A ball of radius $a = 0.1$ mm moves through the air. Estimate its friction coefficient. (c) Let the ball float in the air and move by Brownian motion. Estimate how long it takes to drift 1 mm.

Our reading: air is one gas of hard-ball molecules of mass $m = 28.97$ u and diameter $d = 0.37$ nm (our values), so $\sigma = \pi d^2$ (the notes' $4\pi r^2$ with $r = d/2$), at 1 atm $= 101\,325$ Pa. We use the notes' estimates without extra factors:

<Formula label="Mean free path and viscosity of a gas" tex="\lambda = \frac{1}{\sigma n}, \qquad \nu = \frac16 n\bar vm\lambda, \qquad \bar v = \sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m}}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 93, 102 and 104" />

The friction coefficient is the force per unit velocity, $F/u = 1/\eta$, the inverse of the notes' mobility; "drift 1 mm" we read as a root-mean-square distance of 1 mm in three dimensions. We split MIT's part (a) into our parts (a) and (b), so MIT's (b) and (c) are our (c) and (d).

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the mean free path $\lambda$ of the molecules, in nanometres (tolerance 2%).

<Answer id="p4-lam" label="$\lambda$ in nm:" value="95.0" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-lam" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The density follows from the ideal-gas law, the cross-section from the size of a molecule.

<Step title="The numbers">

$n = P/k_BT = 101\,325/(1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 300)$ m⁻³ $= 2.45 \times 10^{25}$ m⁻³ and $\sigma = \pi(0.37 \times 10^{-9})^2$ m² $= 4.30 \times 10^{-19}$ m², so $\lambda = 1/(\sigma n) = 9.50 \times 10^{-8}$ m, 95 nm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The spacing between molecules is $n^{-1/3} = 3.4$ nm, so a molecule passes some 28 neighbours' distances between collisions. A more careful count, which lets the partners move too, divides $\lambda$ by $\sqrt2$, giving 67 nm.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

(MIT's part (a), continued.) Estimate the viscosity $\nu$ of air, in units of $10^{-6}$ Pa s (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

$n\lambda = 1/\sigma$, so $\nu = m\bar v/6\sigma$: the density drops out.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-nu" scale="1e-6" label="$\nu$ in $10^{-6}$ Pa s:" value="7.74" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-nu" mistakes={[{ value: "15.5", tolerance: "2%", calc: "p4-nu-notes", hint: "That is twice the answer, as in the notes' box on p. 102, which has 3σ. With ν = (1/6) n v̄ m λ and nλ = 1/σ the denominator is 6σ." }]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Viscosity is momentum carried a mean free path by diffusing molecules.

<Step title="The number">

$m = 28.97 \times 1.660539 \times 10^{-27}$ kg $= 4.81 \times 10^{-26}$ kg and $\bar v = \sqrt{2k_BT/m} = 415$ m/s, so

<Formula label="Estimated viscosity of air" tex="\nu = \frac{m\bar v}{6\sigma} = \frac{4.81 \times 10^{-26} \times 415}{6 \times 4.30 \times 10^{-19}}\ \text{Pa s} = 7.7 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{Pa s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

(The notes' box on p. 102 writes $\sqrt{2mk_BT}/(3\sigma)$; with $\nu = \frac16 n\bar vm\lambda$ and $n\lambda = 1/\sigma$ it is $\sqrt{2mk_BT}/(6\sigma)$.)

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The measured viscosity of air at 300 K is about $1.85 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa s (our value), 2.4 times larger: the same factor by which the estimate misses for argon in [the lesson](/courses/8-08/viscosity-conduction-boltzmann-equation). The six-direction argument gets the order of magnitude, not the number.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

(MIT's part (b).) Estimate the friction coefficient $1/\eta$ of the ball, with your $\nu$ of part (b), in units of $10^{-8}$ kg/s (tolerance 3%).

<Hint>

Compare the radius of the ball with the mean free path of part (a). Which of the notes' two friction laws applies?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-fric" scale="1e-8" label="$1/\eta$ in $10^{-8}$ kg/s:" value="1.46" tolerance="3%" calc="p4-fric" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The ball is a thousand times larger than the mean free path, so the air flows round it as a viscous fluid and Stokes's law applies.

<Step title="Stokes's law">

The notes give $F = 6\pi a\nu u$ for a sphere much larger than the mean free path (reading 1, p. 116), so

<Formula label="Friction coefficient of the ball" tex="\frac{1}{\eta} = 6\pi a\nu = 6\pi \times 10^{-4} \times 7.74 \times 10^{-6}\ \text{kg/s} = 1.46 \times 10^{-8}\ \text{kg/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Stokes's law also needs slow flow, a small Reynolds number (the density of the air times the radius and the speed of the ball, over $\nu$); for the slow Brownian motion of part (d) that holds easily. With the measured viscosity the coefficient would be $3.5 \times 10^{-8}$ kg/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

(MIT's part (c).) How long does the ball take to drift 1 mm? Give the time in units of $10^5$ s (tolerance 3%).

<Hint>

The Einstein relation gives the diffusion constant from the mobility. In three dimensions, $\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle = 6Dt$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-t" scale="1e5" label="$t$ in $10^5$ s:" value="5.87" tolerance="3%" calc="p4-t" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Fluctuation and dissipation are linked: the friction of part (c) fixes how fast the ball wanders.

<Step title="The diffusion constant">

By the Einstein relation (reading 1, p. 115), $D = \eta k_BT = 4.142 \times 10^{-21}/1.458 \times 10^{-8}$ m²/s $= 2.84 \times 10^{-13}$ m²/s.

</Step>

<Step title="The time">

$t = \langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle/6D = (10^{-3})^2/(6 \times 2.84 \times 10^{-13})$ s $= 5.87 \times 10^5$ s, almost a week (6.8 days).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Reading "drift 1 mm" along one direction instead, $\langle x^2\rangle = 2Dt$, gives three times longer; with the measured viscosity it is 2.4 times longer again. The ball forgets its velocity within a time $M\eta$, with $M$ its mass: about 0.3 s for a ball of density 1000 kg/m³ (our value), so on the scale of days it is deep in the random-walk regime of the Langevin equation. In real air a ball this size falls far faster than it diffuses; MIT's "floating" sets gravity aside, and the estimate is of the random motion alone (our remark).

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Noise with a Gaussian correlation (setup ours, MIT's changes kept)" points={20} mit="Problem 5">

MIT's sheet points to a textbook problem here and changes it in two ways: the correlation function has the Gaussian form below, and the power spectrum is to be found for all frequencies. The setup is ours, chosen so that MIT's two changes apply, and so are parts (b), (c) and (e).

Our setup: a particle of mass $m$ moves along a line in a fluid and obeys the Langevin equation of the notes, without an external force,

<Formula label="Langevin equation" tex="\frac{dv}{dt} + \gamma v = \frac{\tilde F}{m}, \qquad \langle\tilde F\rangle = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 117 (with no external force)" />

and the random force $\tilde F(t)$ has MIT's correlation function (reading it as the force's correlation is ours),

<Formula label="Gaussian correlation function" tex="\varphi(t) = \langle\tilde F(t)\tilde F(0)\rangle = Ae^{-t^2/\tau^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 1" />

with constants $A > 0$ and $\tau > 0$, the correlation time of the force. As in the notes, the power spectrum of a signal is the Fourier transform of its correlation function divided by $\pi$, for $\omega \geq 0$, so that its integral over $0 \leq \omega < \infty$ is the mean square of the signal:

<Formula label="Power spectrum from the correlation function" tex="P(\omega) = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\infty}^\infty dt\,G(t)\,e^{i\omega t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 121" />

<Part label="a" points={5}>

(MIT's part (b), for the force.) Find the power spectrum $P^F(\omega)$ of the random force, for all frequencies.

<Hint>

Complete the square in the exponent: $-t^2/\tau^2 + i\omega t = -(t/\tau - i\omega\tau/2)^2 - \dots$

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-u^2/\tau^2}du = \sqrt\pi\,\tau$, and the shift of $u$ into the complex plane does not change the integral.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-pf" label="$P^F(\omega) =$" expr="A*tau/sqrt(pi)*exp(-omega^2*tau^2/4)"
  vars={{ A: [0.5, 2], tau: [0.5, 2], omega: [0.2, 2] }}
  names={{ A: "A", tau: "\\tau", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "A*tau*sqrt(pi)*exp(-omega^2*tau^2/4)", hint: "That is the Fourier transform of φ itself. The notes' power spectrum is that transform divided by π." },
    { expr: "A*tau/sqrt(pi)*exp(-omega^2*tau^2/2)", hint: "Check the exponent: completing the square in −t²/τ² + iωt leaves −(ωτ)²/4, not −(ωτ)²/2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The Fourier transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian: a force correlated over a time $\tau$ has its power spread up to frequencies of about $1/\tau$.

<Step title="The transform">

With $-t^2/\tau^2 + i\omega t = -(t/\tau - i\omega\tau/2)^2 - \omega^2\tau^2/4$, the integral is $A\sqrt\pi\,\tau\,e^{-\omega^2\tau^2/4}$, and dividing by $\pi$,

<Formula label="Power spectrum of the force" tex="P^F(\omega) = \frac{A\tau}{\sqrt\pi}\,e^{-\omega^2\tau^2/4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The total is $\int_0^\infty P^F\,d\omega = \frac{A\tau}{\sqrt\pi}\cdot\frac{\sqrt\pi}{\tau} = A = \varphi(0) = \langle\tilde F^2\rangle$, as it must be. As $\tau \to 0$ with $A\tau$ fixed the spectrum becomes flat: white noise, the case of the notes (reading 1, p. 122).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

At what frequency has $P^F$ fallen to half its value at $\omega = 0$? Give $\omega\tau$ at that frequency as a decimal (tolerance 1%).

<Answer id="p5-half" label="$\omega\tau$ at half power:" value="1.665" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-half" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Set the Gaussian factor equal to one half.

<Step title="The number">

$e^{-\omega^2\tau^2/4} = \frac12$ gives $\omega\tau = 2\sqrt{\ln 2} = 1.665$. A force that stays correlated for a time $\tau$ has no power left at frequencies much above $1/\tau$; above $\omega\tau \approx 4$ the spectrum is already down by a factor $e^{-4} \approx 0.02$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

The notes fix the friction by the random force (reading 1, p. 119):

<Formula label="Friction from the random force" tex="m\gamma = \frac{1}{k_BT}\int_0^\infty\varphi(s)\,e^{-\gamma s}\,ds" cite="reading1" at="p. 119" />

Find $m\gamma$ when the force correlation time is short, $\gamma\tau \ll 1$.

<Hint>

For $\gamma\tau \ll 1$ the factor $e^{-\gamma s}$ is close to 1 wherever $\varphi(s)$ is not small. Mind the lower limit of the integral.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-fric" label="$m\gamma =$" expr="sqrt(pi)*A*tau/(2*kB*T)"
  vars={{ A: [0.5, 2], tau: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ A: "A", tau: "\\tau", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(pi)*A*tau/(kB*T)", hint: "That integrates φ over the whole time axis. The notes' integral starts at s = 0, which takes half of the even Gaussian." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a short correlation time only the area under the correlation function matters.

<Step title="The integral">

With $e^{-\gamma s} \approx 1$, $\int_0^\infty Ae^{-s^2/\tau^2}ds = \frac12\sqrt\pi\,A\tau$, so

<Formula label="Friction for a short correlation time" tex="m\gamma = \frac{\sqrt\pi\,A\tau}{2k_BT}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 1 (our own problem and solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Exactly, $\int_0^\infty e^{-s^2/\tau^2 - \gamma s}ds = \frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\,\tau\,e^{\gamma^2\tau^2/4}\operatorname{erfc}(\gamma\tau/2)$, which tends to $\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2}\tau$ as $\gamma\tau \to 0$; at $\gamma\tau = 0.1$ it is 5.4% smaller. A stronger or longer-lasting random force means more friction, at a given temperature: fluctuation and dissipation again.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

(MIT's part (b), for the velocity.) The notes give the power spectrum of the velocity as $P^v(\omega) = \frac{1}{\pi}\,\frac{K(\omega)}{m^2(\gamma^2 + \omega^2)}$, with $K(\omega)$ the Fourier transform of the force correlation function (reading 1, p. 124). Find $P^v(\omega)$ for all frequencies, eliminating $A$ with part (c), in terms of $k_B$, $T$, $m$, $\gamma$, $\tau$ and $\omega$.

<Hint>

$K(\omega)$ is $\pi$ times the $P^F(\omega)$ of part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-pv" label="$P^v(\omega) =$" expr="2*kB*T*gamma*exp(-omega^2*tau^2/4)/(pi*m*(gamma^2 + omega^2))"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], gamma: [0.5, 2], tau: [0.3, 1], omega: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T", m: "m", gamma: "\\gamma", tau: "\\tau", omega: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*kB*T*gamma/(pi*m*(gamma^2 + omega^2))", hint: "That is the white-noise spectrum of the notes. Here the force is correlated over τ, and its own spectrum falls off for ωτ of order 1 and above." },
    { expr: "kB*T*gamma*exp(-omega^2*tau^2/4)/(pi*m*(gamma^2 + omega^2))", hint: "That is half the spectrum: it belongs to a friction twice as large as the notes' formula gives, as when φ is integrated over the whole time axis instead of from 0." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The fluid filters the force: the velocity responds to frequencies below $\gamma$, and the force supplies nothing above about $1/\tau$.

<Step title="Putting it together">

$K(\omega) = \sqrt\pi\,A\tau\,e^{-\omega^2\tau^2/4}$, and part (c) gives $\sqrt\pi\,A\tau = 2k_BTm\gamma$. So

<Formula label="Power spectrum of the velocity" tex="P^v(\omega) = \frac{2k_BT\gamma}{\pi m\left(\gamma^2 + \omega^2\right)}\,e^{-\omega^2\tau^2/4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

As $\tau \to 0$ it becomes the notes' white-noise result $2k_BT\gamma/\pi m(\gamma^2 + \omega^2)$ (reading 1, p. 125). With $\gamma\tau \ll 1$ the two differ only at $\omega \gtrsim 1/\tau$, where the spectrum is already down by a factor of order $(\gamma\tau)^2$: there the Gaussian cuts off the slow $1/\omega^2$ tail of the white-noise spectrum.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

Show that if the friction is taken from the full formula of part (c), with $e^{-\gamma s}$ kept, then $\int_0^\infty P^v(\omega)\,d\omega = k_BT/m$ exactly, for any $\tau$: equipartition holds whatever the correlation time.

<Hint>

Write $K(\omega) = \int ds\,\varphi(s)e^{i\omega s}$ and do the $\omega$ integral first, over the whole line (the integrand is even in $\omega$). You need $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{e^{i\omega s}}{\gamma^2 + \omega^2}\,d\omega = \frac{\pi}{\gamma}\,e^{-\gamma|s|}$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The total velocity noise and the friction are the same integral of $\varphi$, weighted by $e^{-\gamma|s|}$.

<Step title="The integral">

$\int_0^\infty P^v\,d\omega = \frac{1}{2\pi m^2}\int ds\,\varphi(s)\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{e^{i\omega s}d\omega}{\gamma^2 + \omega^2} = \frac{1}{2\pi m^2}\cdot\frac{\pi}{\gamma}\int_{-\infty}^\infty\varphi(s)e^{-\gamma|s|}ds = \frac{1}{m^2\gamma}\int_0^\infty\varphi(s)e^{-\gamma s}ds$, using that $\varphi$ is even.

</Step>

<Step title="Equipartition">

By the friction formula, the last integral is $k_BTm\gamma$, so $\int_0^\infty P^v\,d\omega = k_BT/m = \langle v^2\rangle$: equipartition, for any $\tau$. Nothing used the Gaussian form; the notes' check (reading 1, p. 125) is the white-noise case of the same identity.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

With the approximate friction of part (c) instead, the total comes out as $(k_BT/m)\,e^{\gamma^2\tau^2/4}\operatorname{erfc}(\gamma\tau/2)$, which differs from $k_BT/m$ by terms of order $\gamma\tau$: the approximation and the exact identity agree in the limit where the approximation holds.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
