The second supplement of 8.08 is not a set of handwritten lecture notes but a typed draft chapter of a book by Wen, chapter 4, "Boson systems" (pp. 28–54). It builds a quantum theory of many bosons in a way that leads straight into quantum field theory: first free bosons, then the vacuum and the Casimir effect, then interacting bosons, a superfluid and its sound waves. We split it into three pages. This one covers sections 4.1 to 4.5 (pp. 28–37): what makes particles identical, how free bosons become a set of harmonic oscillators, and how those oscillators are the vibrations of a string. Wen sets $\hbar = 1$ (he says so on p. 35), so an energy and an angular frequency are the same thing; we do the same in formulas and put $\hbar$ back in numbers. (Everything is in our own words, with our own examples; citations give Wen's section, printed page and equation numbers.)

Wen opens (p. 28) with a bold remark: bosons are the simplest many-particle systems, yet they show superfluidity, magnetism and crystals, and he suggests that a boson system may even be the deep structure from which photons and electrons emerge, a programme of his own research. The chapter's method is to find a classical field that describes the bosons, study its vibrations, and then quantize them.

## One particle, then two identical ones

Start with no particles: the **vacuum** $|0\rangle$, with energy zero (section 4.1, p. 28). States with one particle form a space spanned by $|x\rangle$, a particle at position $x$ on a line, normalised as $\langle x|x'\rangle = \delta(x - x')$; a general one-particle state is $\int dx\,\psi(x)|x\rangle$. Wen takes the particle to be relativistic:

<Formula label="One-particle Hamiltonian and its energies" tex="\hat H_1 = \sqrt{-c^2\partial_x^2 + m^2c^4}, \qquad \epsilon_k = \sqrt{c^2k^2 + m^2c^4}" cite="reading1" at="§4.1, pp. 29–30, eqns (4.1.1) and (4.1.5)" />

Its eigenstates are plane waves $|k\rangle = \int dx\,e^{ikx}|x\rangle$. A massless particle has $\epsilon_k = c|k|$; in three dimensions $k$ becomes a vector and $k^2$ its squared length. For one particle, Wen notes, there is nothing to say about statistics yet.

Two particles are where identity comes in (p. 29). The two-particle states are spanned by $|x_1x_2\rangle$, and Wen declares that

<Formula label="Two names for one state" tex="|x_1x_2\rangle = |x_2x_1\rangle" cite="reading1" at="§4.1, p. 29, eqn (4.1.2)" />

Being "identical" means exactly this, and nothing more: a particle at $x_1$ together with a particle at $x_2$ is a **single** state, whichever name we give it. Had the two kets named two states, we could tell the particles apart. A general state is then $\int_{x_1 \le x_2}dx_1\,dx_2\,\psi(x_1, x_2)|x_1x_2\rangle$, with the integral over half the plane only, so nothing is counted twice. Since the other half holds the same states under other names, $\psi$ can be extended to it by $\psi(x_1, x_2) = \psi(x_2, x_1)$, and the integral over the whole plane gets a factor $\frac12$. The conclusion is surprising at first: the wave function of two identical particles is symmetric, and nothing in the argument said whether they were bosons or fermions.

## Statistics comes from the Hamiltonian

What then makes particles bosons? Wen's answer (pp. 30–31) is the Hamiltonian that acts on these symmetric wave functions. If it is just the sum of two one-particle Hamiltonians,

<Formula label="Free bosons: the n-particle Hamiltonian" tex="\hat H_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{-c^2\partial_{x_i}^2 + m^2c^4}" cite="reading1" at="§4.1, p. 30, eqns (4.1.4) and (4.1.6)" />

(with $n = 2$ for two particles), the particles are **bosons**, and they do not interact. Any Hamiltonian for identical particles must be unchanged when $x_1$ and $x_2$ are swapped, or it would turn an allowed (symmetric) wave function into a forbidden one. The eigenstates here are symmetric plane waves, $e^{i(k_1x_1 + k_2x_2)} + e^{i(k_1x_2 + k_2x_1)}$, with energy $\epsilon_{k_1} + \epsilon_{k_2}$; again $|k_1k_2\rangle = |k_2k_1\rangle$, and for $n$ bosons every ordering of $k_1 \dots k_n$ names the same state.

Section 4.2 (which Wen marks with two asterisks) shows a Hamiltonian that makes the same symmetric wave functions describe **fermions**. In two dimensions, for non-relativistic particles of mass $m$ at $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$, take

<Formula label="Two fermions described by a symmetric wave function" tex="\hat H_2^{\text{ferm}} = -\frac{(\partial_{x_1} + ia_x)^2 + (\partial_{y_1} + ia_y)^2}{2m} - \frac{(\partial_{x_2} - ia_x)^2 + (\partial_{y_2} - ia_y)^2}{2m}, \quad a_x = \frac{y_1 - y_2}{r_{12}^2}, \quad a_y = -\frac{x_1 - x_2}{r_{12}^2}" cite="reading1" at="§4.2, p. 31, eqn (4.2.1)" />

with $r_{12}^2 = (x_1 - x_2)^2 + (y_1 - y_2)^2$. It is still unchanged by swapping the particles. To see why it describes fermions, let $\Theta(x, y)$ be the polar angle of the point $(x, y)$ and write $\psi = e^{i\Theta}\tilde\psi$, with $\Theta$ evaluated at the relative position $(x_1 - x_2, y_1 - y_2)$:

<Formula label="Removing the extra terms by a phase" tex="\psi = e^{i\Theta(x_1 - x_2,\,y_1 - y_2)}\,\tilde\psi, \qquad \hat H_2^{\text{ferm}} = e^{i\Theta}\,\tilde H_2^{\text{ferm}}\,e^{-i\Theta}" cite="reading1" at="§4.2, p. 31, eqn (4.2.2)" />

The derivative of the angle is exactly what the extra terms contain: $e^{i\Theta}\partial_x e^{-i\Theta} = \partial_x + iy/(x^2 + y^2)$ and $e^{i\Theta}\partial_y e^{-i\Theta} = \partial_y - ix/(x^2 + y^2)$. (The notes print only the added terms on the right.) So $\tilde H_2^{\text{ferm}}$ is the plain kinetic energy of two free particles. But the phase changes sign when the particles are swapped, $e^{i\Theta(-x, -y)} = -e^{i\Theta(x, y)}$, because turning a vector round adds $\pi$ to its angle. A symmetric $\psi$ therefore means an **antisymmetric** $\tilde\psi$: two free fermions in the usual description.

Wen draws a strong conclusion (pp. 31–32). Fermions can be described by symmetric wave functions with a complicated Hamiltonian, or by antisymmetric ones with a simple Hamiltonian, and he regards the first as closer to the physics. In a short imagined dialogue he argues that a minus sign under exchange is a strange thing to build statistics on, since an overall phase of a wave function has no meaning; the usual defence, that only the phases $0$ and $\pi$ keep the many-particle wave function continuous, fails for particles on a lattice, which still have well-defined statistics. In his view statistics is a **dynamical** property of the Hamiltonian: it can be changed, and in two dimensions scaling $a_x$ and $a_y$ by $\theta/\pi$ gives particles with **fractional statistics**, where the statistical angle $\theta = 0$ means bosons and $\theta = \pi$ fermions (Problem 4.2.1, p. 32).

## Occupation numbers

Wen now asks (section 4.3, p. 33) where identical particles come from at all. If particles are actors placed on an empty stage, it seems we could always follow their paths and tell the two states before and after an exchange apart. His answer will come from a different picture of the vacuum, built in three steps.

First, put the bosons on a ring of length $L$. The wave vectors are then quantized, $\kappa_n = 2\pi n/L$ with $n$ an integer, and the space of all states, with any number of bosons, is the sum of the zero-, one-, two-boson spaces and so on. Instead of listing each boson's wave vector, $|k_1k_2k_3\rangle$, list how many bosons sit in each level: the **occupation numbers** $n_{\kappa_n} = 0, 1, 2, \dots$ (p. 34, Fig. 4.3). The vacuum has all of them zero, and every state is labelled once and only once. A list of wave vectors in any order gives the same occupation numbers, so this labelling has the identity of the particles built in. For free bosons such a state has the energy

<Formula label="Energy of free bosons from occupation numbers" tex="E_{\text{tot}} = \sum_n n_{\kappa_n}\epsilon_{\kappa_n}" cite="reading1" at="§4.3, p. 34, eqn (4.3.1)" />

Second, compare with a set of harmonic oscillators, one for every $\kappa_n$ (p. 34). An oscillator in its $n_{\kappa_n}$th excited state has energy $(n_{\kappa_n} + \frac12)\hbar\omega_{\kappa_n}$. If each oscillator's frequency is chosen so that $\hbar\omega_{\kappa_n} = \epsilon_{\kappa_n}$, the total energy of the oscillators is the boson energy plus a constant $\frac12\sum_n\epsilon_{\kappa_n}$, and the states match one to one: a state of the oscillators **is** a state of the bosons. Free bosons are a collection of harmonic oscillators.

## The vibrating string

Third, find a classical system whose normal modes are exactly these oscillators (section 4.4, pp. 34–36). It is a string forming a loop of length $L$, with displacement $h(x, t)$ obeying

<Formula label="The string's wave equation" tex="\ddot h(x, t) = c^2\partial_x^2h(x, t) - m^2c^4h(x, t), \qquad h(x + L, t) = h(x, t)" cite="reading1" at="§4.4, pp. 34–35, eqn (4.4.1)" />

(the $m^2c^4$ term is a restoring force on every piece of the string, as if each were also tied to its rest position by a spring). Wen splits this second-order equation into two first-order ones, with $p$ playing the part of a momentum, and combines them into a complex field:

<Formula label="Phase-space form and the complex field" tex="\dot h = \sqrt{-c^2\partial_x^2 + m^2c^4}\;p, \quad -\dot p = \sqrt{-c^2\partial_x^2 + m^2c^4}\;h, \qquad \phi = \frac{h + ip}{\sqrt2}" cite="reading1" at="§4.4, p. 35, eqns (4.4.2) and (4.4.3)" />

In terms of $\phi$ the two equations become one, which has the form of a Schrödinger equation:

<Formula label="The field equation" tex="i\dot\phi(x, t) = \sqrt{-c^2\partial_x^2 + m^2c^4}\;\phi(x, t)" cite="reading1" at="§4.4, p. 35, eqn (4.4.4)" />

Now expand in modes, $\phi(x, t) = \sum_{\kappa_n}\phi_{\kappa_n}(t)L^{-1/2}e^{i\kappa_nx}$. Each mode obeys $i\dot\phi_\kappa = \epsilon_\kappa\phi_\kappa$. Writing $\phi_\kappa = (X_\kappa + iP_\kappa)/\sqrt2$ gives

<Formula label="Each mode is an oscillator" tex="\dot X_{\kappa} = \epsilon_{\kappa}P_{\kappa}, \qquad \dot P_{\kappa} = -\epsilon_{\kappa}X_{\kappa}, \qquad M_\kappa = \frac{1}{\epsilon_\kappa}, \quad K_\kappa = \epsilon_\kappa" cite="reading1" at="§4.4, p. 35, eqns (4.4.5) and (4.4.6)" />

These are Hamilton's equations of an oscillator with coordinate $X_\kappa$, momentum $P_\kappa$, mass $M_\kappa$ and spring constant $K_\kappa$, so its angular frequency is $\omega_\kappa = \sqrt{K_\kappa/M_\kappa} = \epsilon_\kappa$, exactly what the bosons require (with $\hbar = 1$). (The notes write $\phi_\kappa = X_\kappa + iP_\kappa$ in section 4.4 but $(X_\kappa + iP_\kappa)/\sqrt2$ in section 4.5; only the second makes the two forms of the energy below agree, so we use it throughout.) The total energy can be written three ways:

<Formula label="Energy of the string" tex="E_{\text{tot}} = \sum_\kappa\left(\frac{P_\kappa^2}{2M_\kappa} + \frac12K_\kappa X_\kappa^2\right) = \int dx\,\phi^*\sqrt{-c^2\partial_x^2 + m^2c^4}\;\phi = \int dx\left(\frac12\dot h\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{-c^2\partial_x^2 + m^2c^4}}\,\dot h + \frac12h\sqrt{-c^2\partial_x^2 + m^2c^4}\;h\right)" cite="reading1" at="§4.4, p. 35, eqn (4.4.7)" />

What we have built is a one-dimensional **classical field theory**: the field is the string displacement $h$, or equivalently the complex $\phi$. Wen calls eqn (4.4.1) a coordinate-space equation of motion (second order in time) and eqn (4.4.4) a phase-space one (first order). His conclusion (p. 36): the quantum theory of this string is the same as the quantum theory of the free bosons of eqn (4.1.6). A gas of bosons can be pictured as particles (Fig. 4.5a) or as a vibrating string (Fig. 4.5b); in two dimensions the string becomes a membrane, and in $d$ dimensions a vibrating "$d$-brane".

## Quantizing the string: second quantization

The string so far is classical (section 4.5, pp. 36–37). To quantize it, replace each pair $(X_\kappa, P_\kappa)$ by operators with $[\hat X_\kappa, \hat P_\kappa] = i$. The modes and the field become operators too,

<Formula label="Field operators" tex="\hat\phi_\kappa = \frac{\hat X_\kappa + i\hat P_\kappa}{\sqrt2}, \qquad \hat\phi(x) = \sum_{\kappa}L^{-1/2}e^{i\kappa x}\hat\phi_\kappa" cite="reading1" at="§4.5, p. 37" />

and they obey

<Formula label="Algebra of the field operators" tex="[\hat\phi_\kappa, \hat\phi^\dagger_{\kappa'}] = \delta_{\kappa\kappa'}, \quad [\hat\phi_\kappa, \hat\phi_{\kappa'}] = 0; \qquad [\hat\phi(x), \hat\phi^\dagger(x')] = \delta(x - x'), \quad [\hat\phi(x), \hat\phi(x')] = 0" cite="reading1" at="§4.5, p. 37, eqn (4.5.1) and the line below it" />

(The notes write the field as a sum over $\hat\psi_\kappa$, a typing slip for $\hat\phi_\kappa$.) The classical energy $\sum_\kappa\frac12\epsilon_\kappa(P_\kappa^2 + X_\kappa^2)$ becomes the Hamiltonian

<Formula label="Second-quantized Hamiltonian of free bosons" tex="\hat H_0 = \sum_\kappa\frac12\epsilon_\kappa\left(\hat P_\kappa^2 + \hat X_\kappa^2\right) = \sum_\kappa\epsilon_\kappa\left(\hat\phi^\dagger_\kappa\hat\phi_\kappa + \frac12\right)" cite="reading1" at="§4.5, p. 37, eqn (4.5.2)" />

which is the familiar [oscillator in terms of raising and lowering operators](/courses/8-04/creation-annihilation-operators), once for every mode. The eigenvalues $n_\kappa$ of $\hat\phi^\dagger_\kappa\hat\phi_\kappa$ are the occupation numbers, so the energy is $\sum n_\kappa\epsilon_\kappa$ plus the constant, exactly as for the bosons. This is the **second-quantized** description. The total number of bosons and its density are

<Formula label="Number operator" tex="\hat N = \sum_\kappa\hat\phi^\dagger_\kappa\hat\phi_\kappa = \int dx\,\hat\phi^\dagger(x)\hat\phi(x), \qquad [\hat N, \hat\phi^\dagger] = \hat\phi^\dagger, \quad [\hat N, \hat\phi] = -\hat\phi" cite="reading1" at="§4.5, p. 37" />

so $\hat\phi^\dagger(x)\hat\phi(x)$ is the density operator, $\hat\phi^\dagger$ adds a boson (the **creation operator**) and $\hat\phi$ removes one (the **annihilation operator**). So one operator now does what the particle picture needed a whole family for: there was a separate $\hat H_n$ (eqn 4.1.6) for every number of particles $n$, whereas $\hat H_0$ handles zero, one or many bosons alike.

<WorkedExample title="Three bosons on a ring, three ways">
<Problem>
Massless bosons live on a ring of length $L = 2$ cm. Two bosons are in the level $n = -2$ and one is in $n = 5$. Give the state in the wave-vector, occupation and oscillator pictures, and find its energy and momentum. Then count how many different three-boson states can be made from the three levels $n = -1, 0, 1$, and compare with three distinguishable particles.
</Problem>
<Step title="Three names for one state">
In wave vectors it is $|\kappa_{-2}\kappa_{-2}\kappa_5\rangle$, and the orderings $|\kappa_{-2}\kappa_5\kappa_{-2}\rangle$ and $|\kappa_5\kappa_{-2}\kappa_{-2}\rangle$ are the same state. In occupation numbers, $n_{\kappa_{-2}} = 2$, $n_{\kappa_5} = 1$ and all others zero. In the oscillator picture, oscillator $\kappa_{-2}$ is in its second excited state, oscillator $\kappa_5$ in its first, and all the rest in their ground states; with creation operators the state is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\hat\phi^\dagger_{\kappa_{-2}})^2\hat\phi^\dagger_{\kappa_5}|0\rangle$, where the $1/\sqrt2$ normalises the doubly occupied level.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy and momentum">
A level holds bosons of energy $\hbar c|\kappa_n| = |n|\,(2\pi\hbar c/L)$. With $2\pi\hbar c = 1.986 \times 10^{-25}$ J m, one unit is $9.93 \times 10^{-24}$ J, or $6.20 \times 10^{-5}$ eV. The state has $2 + 2 + 5 = 9$ units, $E = 5.58 \times 10^{-4}$ eV above the vacuum. The momentum adds the wave vectors with their signs: $(-2 - 2 + 5)(2\pi\hbar/L) = h/L = 3.31 \times 10^{-32}$ kg m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Counting states">
Identical bosons: a state is fixed by three occupation numbers that add up to 3, and there are $\binom{5}{2} = 10$ ways to choose them. Distinguishable particles: each of the three can be in any of the three levels, $3^3 = 27$ states. The difference is the ordering: each of the 3 states with all bosons in one level matches one arrangement of distinguishable particles, each of the 6 states like $(2, 1, 0)$ matches 3, and the state $(1, 1, 1)$ matches 6, which gives $3 + 18 + 6 = 27$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Particles as the vibrations of a field">
Wen's lesson that a particle is a quantum of vibration of a field, not a thing placed in empty space, is the language of modern cosmology. Guth opens his [lecture on the false vacuum](/courses/8-286/false-vacuum) with it: every particle is a quantum excitation of a field, and a scalar field, a single number at each point of space like the string's displacement $h$, describes a spinless particle. The field that drives inflation is such a scalar field. The [black-body radiation](/courses/8-286/black-body-radiation) of the early universe is counted the same way, as one harmonic oscillator per mode of the electromagnetic field.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Identical particles**: there is only one state with a particle at $x_1$ and one at $x_2$, $|x_1x_2\rangle = |x_2x_1\rangle$, so the wave function can be taken symmetric for bosons and fermions alike.
- **Statistics** is set by the Hamiltonian acting on symmetric wave functions: a sum of one-particle Hamiltonians gives bosons; the two-dimensional Hamiltonian (4.2.1) gives fermions, which the phase $e^{i\Theta}$ turns into the usual antisymmetric description. Wen sees statistics as dynamical; in two dimensions it can be fractional.
- On a ring, wave vectors are $\kappa_n = 2\pi n/L$, and states are labelled by **occupation numbers**, with energy $\sum n_\kappa\epsilon_\kappa$.
- Free bosons are a set of **harmonic oscillators**, one per wave vector, with $\hbar\omega_\kappa = \epsilon_\kappa$; the zero-point energies add a constant $\frac12\sum\epsilon_\kappa$.
- These oscillators are the modes of a **vibrating string**, $\ddot h = c^2\partial_x^2h - m^2c^4h$, or in phase-space form $i\dot\phi = \sqrt{-c^2\partial_x^2 + m^2c^4}\,\phi$ with $\phi = (h + ip)/\sqrt2$: a classical field theory.
- Quantizing the string gives **second quantization**: $\hat H_0 = \sum\epsilon_\kappa(\hat\phi^\dagger_\kappa\hat\phi_\kappa + \frac12)$, with creation and annihilation operators and $\hat N = \int\hat\phi^\dagger\hat\phi\,dx$, one Hamiltonian for any number of bosons.
