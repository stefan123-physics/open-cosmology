The [previous page](/courses/8-08/ideal-fermi-gas) found the Fermi sea at zero temperature. Most Fermi gases of interest are close to it: the electrons in a metal have Fermi temperatures of tens of thousands of kelvin, and those in a white dwarf far more. This page follows the second part of Wen's Chapter VIII (notes pp. 82–88): the density of states, the ground-state energy and pressure, then three properties of metals (compressibility, spin susceptibility and heat capacity), and finally white dwarfs and neutron stars. Much of it revisits [the free Fermi gas of 8.044](/courses/8-044/free-fermi-gas) at the level of a second course: the heat capacity is derived rather than estimated, and the size of a compact star is estimated directly from the balance of pressure and weight.

## The density of states

At low temperature everything happens near the Fermi energy, so it pays to count states by energy rather than by wave vector. The **density of states** $D(\epsilon)$ is defined by: $D(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon$ is the number of single-particle states with energy between $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon + d\epsilon$ (notes p. 82). In three dimensions

<Formula label="Density of states" tex="D(\epsilon) = V\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\delta(\epsilon_k - \epsilon)" cite="reading1" at="p. 82" />

The easiest way to evaluate it is to count first. The number of states below an energy $\epsilon_F$ fills the sphere $k < k_F$ with $k_F = \sqrt{2m\epsilon_F}/\hbar$ (notes p. 82):

<Formula label="Number of states below the Fermi energy" tex="N(\epsilon_F) = \int_0^{\epsilon_F}D(\epsilon)\,d\epsilon = V\int_{k < k_F}\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3} = \frac{V}{6\pi^2}\,k_F^3 = \frac{V}{6\pi^2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2m}}{\hbar}\right)^3\epsilon_F^{3/2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 82" />

For spinless fermions, one per state, this is the number of fermions in the Fermi sea; for spin-½ fermions each state holds two, so $N = 2 \times Vk_F^3/6\pi^2$ (notes p. 82). Either way $k_F^3$ measures the number density. Differentiating the count with respect to $\epsilon_F$ gives the density of states of one spin state:

<Formula label="Density of states in three dimensions" tex="D(\epsilon) = \frac{\partial N(\epsilon)}{\partial\epsilon} = \frac{\sqrt2}{2\pi^2}\,\frac{m^{3/2}}{\hbar^3}\,\epsilon^{1/2}\,V" cite="reading1" at="p. 82" />

(The boxed formula in the notes leaves out the factor $V$; p. 83 restores it.) It grows as the square root of the energy.

## The ground state and its pressure

At $T = 0$ the states below $\epsilon_F$ are full. Their energy and number are (notes p. 83)

<Formula label="Ground-state energy and number" tex="U_0 = \int_0^{\epsilon_F}d\epsilon\,\epsilon D(\epsilon) = \frac{\sqrt2}{2\pi^2}\,\frac{m^{3/2}}{\hbar^3}\,V\,\frac25\epsilon_F^{5/2}, \qquad N = \int_0^{\epsilon_F}d\epsilon\,D(\epsilon) = \frac{\sqrt2}{2\pi^2}\,\frac{m^{3/2}}{\hbar^3}\,V\,\frac23\epsilon_F^{3/2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 83" />

and their ratio is the energy per particle:

<Formula label="Ground-state energy" tex="U_0 = \frac35N\epsilon_F" cite="reading1" at="p. 83" />

so each particle has on average an energy of the order of $\epsilon_F$, even at absolute zero. The relation $PV = \frac23U$ of the previous page then gives the pressure (notes p. 83):

<Formula label="Pressure of the Fermi sea" tex="P_0 = \frac23\,\frac{U_0}{V} = \frac25\,n\epsilon_F" cite="reading1" at="p. 83" />

with $\epsilon_F$ fixed by the density through $k_F$. For the conduction electrons of a metal Wen quotes $n \sim 10^{22}$ cm⁻³ and $\epsilon_F$ of a few eV, far larger than $k_BT \approx \frac1{40}$ eV at room temperature, and a pressure $P_0$ of order $10^4$ atm (notes p. 83). At room temperature the electrons of a metal are therefore very nearly in their ground state: a **degenerate** Fermi gas.

## The compressibility of a metal

The electron gas pushes outwards; what holds a metal together is the attraction between the electrons and the positive ions. Wen's model keeps the first exactly and replaces the second by a constant pressure $P_0$ squeezing the metal (notes p. 84). With two spin states, $n_e = 2k_F^3/6\pi^2$, so $k_F = (3\pi^2n_e)^{1/3}$ and $\epsilon_F = \hbar^2(3\pi^2)^{2/3}n_e^{2/3}/2m$, and the electrons' ground-state energy is

<Formula label="Electron energy as a function of volume" tex="U_0 = \frac35N\epsilon_F = CNn_e^{2/3} = \frac{CN^{5/3}}{V^{2/3}}, \qquad C = \frac35\,\frac{\hbar^2(3\pi^2)^{2/3}}{2m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 84" />

The model energy adds the work done against the squeezing pressure:

<Formula label="Model for a metal" tex="U_{\text{tot}} = \frac{CN^{5/3}}{V^{2/3}} + P_0V" cite="reading1" at="p. 84" />

The first term falls as the metal expands, the second rises; the metal settles at the minimum (Wen's sketch on p. 84), where $\partial U_{\text{tot}}/\partial V = 0$:

<Formula label="Equilibrium volume" tex="P_0 = \frac23\,\frac{CN^{5/3}}{V_0^{5/3}}, \qquad V_0 = \left(\frac{3P_0}{2CN^{5/3}}\right)^{-3/5}" cite="reading1" at="p. 84" />

The first equation just says that the electrons' Fermi pressure $\frac25n\epsilon_F$ balances $P_0$. (In the notes the exponent of $N$ in these two expressions looks like $5/2$, and the outer exponent is written $+3/5$; solving the first for $V_0$ gives $N^{5/3}$ and $-3/5$.) The **compressibility** measures how much the volume gives under extra pressure, and near the minimum it is set by the curvature of $U_{\text{tot}}$:

<Formula label="Compressibility of the electron gas" tex="\chi = -\frac1V\frac{dV}{dP} = \frac{1}{V\,\partial^2U_{\text{tot}}/\partial V^2} = \frac{1}{V\cdot\frac23\cdot\frac53\,CN^{5/3}/V^{8/3}} = \frac{9}{10}\,\frac{1}{Cn_e^{5/3}} = \frac{3}{2\,n_e\epsilon_F}" cite="reading1" at="p. 84" />

(The last line of the notes gives $\frac92$ in place of $\frac32$, a slip of a factor 3 that enters one line earlier, where substituting $C$ gives $3m/[\hbar^2(3\pi^2)^{2/3}n_e^{5/3}]$ and the notes write 9 for the 3: $\frac{9}{10}\cdot\frac53 = \frac32$.) Its inverse, the bulk modulus $\frac23n_e\epsilon_F = \frac53P_0$, is enormous. In the same model, halving the volume raises the Fermi pressure by $2^{5/3}$, so the extra pressure needed is $(2^{5/3} - 1)P_0 \approx 2.2\,P_0$, of order $10^4$ atm for Wen's numbers. Wen puts that in everyday terms: one atmosphere is the weight of about 10 m of water, so this is the pressure at the bottom of a column of water some $10^5$ m, a hundred kilometres, deep. (He also quotes a compressibility of order $1/(10^4$ atm$)$, which fits his own formula; corrected, his $n$ and $\epsilon_F$ give a bulk modulus about three times larger, of order $3 \times 10^4$ atm, still consistent with the halving pressure.)

## The spin susceptibility of a metal

An electron carries a magnetic moment of size $\mu_B$, the Bohr magneton, along or against a magnetic field $B$; label the two cases $\uparrow$ and $\downarrow$ (notes p. 85). In the field their energies are

<Formula label="Energies in a magnetic field" tex="\epsilon_\uparrow = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} - \mu_BB, \qquad \epsilon_\downarrow = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} + \mu_BB, \qquad M = \mu_B(N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow)" cite="reading1" at="p. 85" />

where $M$ is the induced magnetic moment. Wen's picture is two parabolas, one per spin, shifted down and up by $\mu_BB$. Both are filled to the same chemical potential, so electrons pour from the raised band into the lowered one. Only those near the Fermi energy move: the lowered band gains $\mu_BB\,D(\epsilon_F)$ electrons and the raised band loses as many, where $D$ is the density of states of one spin state. The imbalance is

<Formula label="Spin imbalance" tex="\Delta N = N_\uparrow - N_\downarrow = 2\mu_BB\,D(\epsilon_F) = 2\mu_BB\,\frac{\sqrt2}{2\pi^2}\,\frac{m^{3/2}}{\hbar^3}\,\epsilon_F^{1/2}\,V" cite="reading1" at="p. 85" />

With both spin states counted, $n = 2 \times \frac{1}{6\pi^2}(\sqrt{2m}/\hbar)^3\epsilon_F^{3/2}$, so $\epsilon_F \propto n^{2/3}$ and $\Delta N/V \propto n^{1/3}$ (notes p. 85). The **Pauli spin susceptibility** is

<Formula label="Pauli spin susceptibility" tex="\chi = \frac MB = 2\mu_B^2\,\frac{\sqrt2}{2\pi^2}\,\frac{m^{3/2}}{\hbar^3}\,\epsilon_F^{1/2}\,V = \mu_B^2\,\frac{3^{1/3}}{\pi^{4/3}}\,\frac{m}{\hbar^2}\,n^{1/3}\,V" cite="reading1" at="p. 85" />

(The notes write $(2\pi)^2$ for $2\pi^2$ in the first form and $\sqrt2\,3^{1/3}/\pi$ for the constant in the second; with $\epsilon_F^{1/2} = \hbar(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}/\sqrt{2m}$ the constant is $3^{1/3}/\pi^{4/3}$.) Equivalently $\chi = \frac32N\mu_B^2/\epsilon_F$. It does not depend on temperature, unlike the Curie law $\chi = N\mu_B^2/k_BT$ of [free moments](/courses/8-044/paramagnets), and it is smaller than the Curie value by a factor $\frac32k_BT/\epsilon_F$: of all the electrons, only the few near the Fermi surface are free to turn their moments. (The comparison with Curie's law is the page's addition.)

## The heat capacity at low temperature

Now let the temperature rise a little above zero (notes p. 86). With the Fermi–Dirac function $n_F(\epsilon) = 1/(e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)} + 1)$, the energy and number are

<Formula label="Energy and number at finite temperature" tex="U = \int d\epsilon\,D(\epsilon)\,\epsilon\,n_F(\epsilon), \qquad N = \int d\epsilon\,D(\epsilon)\,n_F(\epsilon)" cite="reading1" at="p. 86" />

The second equation fixes $\mu(N, V, T)$, which is put into the first to give $U$ as a function of $N$, $V$ and $T$. The heat capacity is $C = \partial U/\partial T$ at fixed $N$ and $V$. Wen's trick is to note that $N$ does not change, so

<Formula label="Fixed particle number" tex="\left.\frac{\partial N}{\partial T}\right|_{N,V} = \int d\epsilon\,D(\epsilon)\left.\frac{\partial n_F}{\partial T}\right|_{N,V} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \int d\epsilon\,\mu\,D(\epsilon)\left.\frac{\partial n_F}{\partial T}\right|_{N,V} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 86" />

and this zero may be subtracted from the heat capacity:

<Formula label="Heat capacity" tex="C = \int d\epsilon\,D(\epsilon)\,\epsilon\left.\frac{\partial n_F}{\partial T}\right|_{N,V} = \int d\epsilon\,D(\epsilon)\,(\epsilon - \mu)\left.\frac{\partial n_F}{\partial T}\right|_{N,V}" cite="reading1" at="p. 86" />

The derivative of $n_F$ has two parts, one from the explicit $T$ and one through $\mu(T)$:

<Formula label="Temperature derivative of the occupation" tex="\left.\frac{\partial n_F}{\partial T}\right|_{N,V} = \frac{\epsilon - \mu}{k_BT^2}\,\frac{e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}}{\left[1 + e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}\right]^2} + \frac{1}{k_BT}\left.\frac{\partial\mu}{\partial T}\right|_{N,V}\frac{e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}}{\left[1 + e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}\right]^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 86" />

Both are peaked within a few $k_BT$ of $\mu$. In the first, $\epsilon - \mu \sim k_BT$, so it is of order $T^{-1}$. In the second, $\partial\mu/\partial T$ turns out to be proportional to $T$ (shown below), so it is of order $T^0$, smaller by a factor $T$, and Wen drops it. Since $D(\epsilon)$ hardly changes over the few $k_BT$ that matter, it can be taken out at $\epsilon = \mu$, and with $t = \beta(\epsilon - \mu)$ (notes p. 87):

<Formula label="Heat capacity, evaluated" tex="C = \frac{D(\mu)}{k_BT^2}\int d\epsilon\,(\epsilon - \mu)^2\,\frac{e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}}{\left[1 + e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}\right]^2} = \frac{D(\mu)}{k_BT^2}\,k_B^3T^3\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,\frac{t^2e^t}{(1 + e^t)^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 87" />

The remaining integral is a pure number. Wen defines $I_n = 2\int_0^\infty dt\,t^ne^t/(1 + e^t)^2$, with $I_0 = 1$ and $I_2 = \pi^2/3$ (notes p. 87), and since the integrand is even, the integral above is $I_2$:

<Formula label="Low-temperature heat capacity" tex="C = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\,k_B^2T\,D(\mu)" cite="reading1" at="p. 87" />

This holds in any number of dimensions, as Wen notes: only the density of states at the Fermi energy enters. Here $D$ must count every spin state the particles can occupy; for electrons it is twice the one-spin $D$ defined above. In three dimensions, $D \propto \epsilon^{1/2}$ and $N \propto \epsilon_F^{3/2}$ give $D(\epsilon_F) = 3N/2\epsilon_F$ (with $D$ and $N$ counting the same spin states), and with $\mu \approx \epsilon_F$:

<Formula label="Heat capacity of the 3D Fermi gas" tex="C = \frac{\pi^2}{2}\,k_B\,\frac{k_BT}{\epsilon_F}\,N = \frac32k_B\,\frac{\frac{\pi^2}{3}k_BT}{\epsilon_F}\,N" cite="reading1" at="p. 87" />

The second form invites a comparison with a classical gas, whose $U = \frac32Nk_BT$ gives $C = \frac32Nk_B$: the Fermi gas behaves as if only a fraction $\frac{\pi^2}{3}k_BT/\epsilon_F$ of its particles were classical. Wen's sketch of $C(T)$ rises linearly from zero and levels off at $\frac32k_B$ per particle when $k_BT$ is well above $\epsilon_F$.

Why is $\partial\mu/\partial T$ proportional to $T$ (notes p. 87)? Wen's sketches show the occupation $n_F$: as $T$ rises, the step is rounded over a width $k_BT$, and as many states are emptied just below $\mu$ as are filled just above it, because $n_F - \frac12$ is odd about $\epsilon = \mu$. To first order in $T$ the count is unchanged. Only because $D(\epsilon)$ is not constant across the rounded region does $N$ change, and then at second order, $N = N_0 + (\text{const})\,T^2$. Keeping $N$ fixed therefore shifts $\mu$ by an amount of order $T^2$, $\mu = \mu_0 + (\text{const})\,T^2$, and $\partial\mu/\partial T \propto T$. (For the 3D density of states the constant is negative: $\mu$ falls slightly as the gas warms.)

## White dwarfs and neutron stars

The same Fermi pressure holds up stars that have stopped burning (notes p. 88). Wen estimates their size by dropping all numerical factors. The Fermi pressure of particles of mass $m$ at density $n$ is of order $\hbar^2n^{5/3}/m$. A white dwarf contains electrons and protons in equal numbers, so both gases have the same density, and the protons, being 1836 times heavier, add a negligible pressure:

<Formula label="Pressure inside a white dwarf" tex="P_{\text{tot}} = P_e + P_p \approx P_e \approx \frac{\hbar^2n^{5/3}}{m_e}" cite="reading1" at="p. 88" />

This pressure must carry the weight of the matter above. Take a column of unit cross-section reaching from the centre to the surface of a star of mass $M$ and radius $R$ (Wen's sketch): its mass is $m \approx nm_pR$, and its weight is of order $GMm/R^2$. Balancing,

<Formula label="Balance of pressure and weight" tex="P_{\text{tot}} \approx G\,\frac{Mm}{R^2} = G\,\frac{M}{R^2}\,(nm_pR) = \frac{GMm_pn}{R} = \frac{\hbar^2n^{5/3}}{m_e}" cite="reading1" at="p. 88" />

With $n \approx M/(m_pR^3)$ this becomes $GMm_pm_e/R = \hbar^2M^{2/3}/(m_p^{2/3}R^2)$, and solving for $R$:

<Formula label="Radius of a white dwarf" tex="R_{\text{WD}} = M^{-1/3}\,\frac{\hbar^2}{m_em_p^{5/3}}\,G^{-1} = \left(\frac{M_\odot}{M}\right)^{1/3}\frac{\hbar^2}{m_em_p^{5/3}M_\odot^{1/3}G} \approx \left(\frac{M_\odot}{M}\right)^{1/3}\,6200\ \text{km}" cite="reading1" at="p. 88" />

Wen checks the units in the margin: since $Gm^2/L$ is an energy $E$ for a mass $m$ and a length $L$, the combination $\hbar^2/(m^3G)$ has the units of $\hbar^2/(mLE)$, which is a length. The striking result is that a **heavier white dwarf is smaller**, $R \propto M^{-1/3}$: more mass needs a higher pressure, hence a higher density. A white dwarf of one solar mass is about the size of the Earth.

A **neutron star** is held up by the Fermi pressure of neutrons, which also carry the mass. Replacing $m_e$ by the neutron mass, close to $m_p$, divides the radius by $m_p/m_e \approx 1836$ (notes p. 88; Wen writes $m_p$, which gives the same 3.4 km):

<Formula label="Radius of a neutron star" tex="R_{\text{NS}} \approx \left(\frac{M_\odot}{M}\right)^{1/3}\,3.4\ \text{km}" cite="reading1" at="p. 88" />

These are estimates without the numerical factors, and real neutron stars, which are also strongly affected by general relativity and nuclear forces, have radii of about ten kilometres; but the estimate gets the scale right, a city-sized ball of nuclear matter. Wen's margin also lists $m_n = 939.565$ MeV, $m_p = 938.271$ MeV and $m_e = 0.51$ MeV: the neutron outweighs the proton by $1.3$ MeV, more than the electron's mass. (Why that matters, which the notes leave unsaid, is our addition: a neutron is heavier than a proton and an electron together, so it decays, unless the electrons are so densely packed that their Fermi energy blocks the decay and instead drives electrons into protons. That happens in the collapse to a neutron star.) A real white dwarf, made of helium, carbon or oxygen, also has about two nucleons per electron rather than one, which changes the numbers but not the scaling.

<WorkedExample title="The conduction electrons of lithium">
<Problem>
Lithium has one conduction electron per atom, $n = 4.70 \times 10^{28}$ m⁻³, with two spin states. Treat them as a free Fermi gas of electrons ($m = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg). (a) Find $k_F$, $\epsilon_F$ and the Fermi temperature. (b) Find the pressure $P_0$, the bulk modulus $1/\chi = \frac23n\epsilon_F$, and the extra pressure that would halve the volume in Wen's model, all in atmospheres. (c) At 300 K, compare the electronic heat capacity with the classical $\frac32Nk_B$, and the Pauli susceptibility with the Curie value $N\mu_B^2/k_BT$.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The Fermi sea">
$k_F = (3\pi^2 \times 4.70 \times 10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3})^{1/3} \approx 1.12 \times 10^{10}$ m⁻¹. Then $\epsilon_F = \hbar^2k_F^2/2m \approx 7.61 \times 10^{-19}$ J $\approx 4.75$ eV, and $T_F = \epsilon_F/k_B \approx 55\,000$ K.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Pressure and stiffness">
$P_0 = \frac25n\epsilon_F \approx 1.43 \times 10^{10}$ Pa, about $1.4 \times 10^5$ atm. The bulk modulus is $\frac53P_0 \approx 2.38 \times 10^{10}$ Pa, about $2.4 \times 10^5$ atm. Halving the volume needs an extra $(2^{5/3} - 1)P_0 = 2.17\,P_0 \approx 3.1 \times 10^{10}$ Pa, some $3 \times 10^5$ atm.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Heat capacity and susceptibility">
$C/(\frac32Nk_B) = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\,k_BT/\epsilon_F = \frac{\pi^2}{3} \times 300/55\,000 \approx 0.018$: under 2% of the classical value. The Pauli susceptibility is smaller than the Curie value by $\frac32\,k_BT/\epsilon_F \approx 0.0082$. Both say the same thing: at room temperature fewer than one electron in fifty takes part.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Degenerate stars and the cosmic distance scale">
White dwarfs matter to cosmology through the type Ia supernovae that [MIT 8.286 Lecture 20](/courses/8-286/supernova-evidence) uses as standard candles to show that the expansion is accelerating. The lecture takes their equal brightness as an empirical assumption and does not discuss what they are; the standard picture, beyond the lecture, is a white dwarf pushed towards the maximum mass that electron Fermi pressure can support, the Chandrasekhar limit treated in [8.044](/courses/8-044/white-dwarfs).
</Callout>

## Summary

- The **density of states** $D(\epsilon) = V\int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\delta(\epsilon_k - \epsilon) = V\frac{\sqrt2\,m^{3/2}}{2\pi^2\hbar^3}\epsilon^{1/2}$ per spin state in 3D.
- At $T = 0$: $U_0 = \frac35N\epsilon_F$ and $P_0 = \frac25n\epsilon_F$. In a metal $\epsilon_F$ is a few eV, far above $k_BT$: the electrons are degenerate.
- **Compressibility** (Wen's model $U_{\text{tot}} = CN^{5/3}/V^{2/3} + P_0V$): $\chi = 3/(2n_e\epsilon_F)$.
- **Pauli susceptibility**: $\chi = 2\mu_B^2D(\epsilon_F) = \frac32N\mu_B^2/\epsilon_F$, independent of $T$.
- **Heat capacity**: $C = \frac{\pi^2}{3}k_B^2T\,D(\epsilon_F)$ in any dimension ($D$ counting all spin states), $\frac{\pi^2}{2}Nk_B(k_BT/\epsilon_F)$ in 3D; $\mu$ moves only as $T^2$.
- **Compact stars**: $\hbar^2n^{5/3}/m_e \sim GMm_pn/R$ gives $R_{\text{WD}} \approx 6200\ \text{km}\,(M_\odot/M)^{1/3}$; with neutrons, $R_{\text{NS}} \approx 3.4\ \text{km}\,(M_\odot/M)^{1/3}$.
