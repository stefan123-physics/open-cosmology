The [previous page](/courses/8-08/degenerate-fermi-gas) treated the electrons of a metal as free. The last pages of Wen's Chapter VIII (notes pp. 89–92) ask what the lattice of ions changes: it organizes the electron states into **bands**, which explains why some solids conduct and others do not, and it makes possible the **semiconductor**, whose carriers are a dilute classical gas living on top of a filled Fermi sea. The page ends with the **diode**, the p–n junction. The intrinsic semiconductor was met briefly in [8.044](/courses/8-044/free-fermi-gas); here Wen derives it two ways. (Between pp. 88 and 89 the scan contains an unnumbered page with sketches for a homework problem, Problem 12.6, presumably from Huang's textbook, about a free energy with a phase transition. It is not part of the Fermi gas and is not covered here.)

## Bands on a lattice

A free particle in one dimension has plane-wave states $\psi_k(x) = e^{ikx}$ with energy $\epsilon_k = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ (notes p. 89). Now let the particle live on a lattice, able to sit only at the sites $x = na$, $n = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots$, a spacing $a$ apart. The wave function becomes

<Formula label="A plane wave on a lattice" tex="\psi_k(n) = e^{ikna}, \qquad \psi_{k + K}(n) = \psi_k(n) \quad\text{if}\quad K = \frac{2\pi}{a}" cite="reading1" at="p. 89" />

since $e^{i2\pi n} = 1$ at every site. Two wave numbers that differ by $2\pi/a$ describe the same state, so every distinct state has $k$ in one interval of length $2\pi/a$, the **Brillouin zone**, $-\pi/a < k \le \pi/a$. The energy must then be a periodic function of $k$. Wen's example is

<Formula label="A band on a lattice" tex="\epsilon_k = \frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2}\left[1 - \cos(ka)\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 89" />

which for small $k$, where $1 - \cos ka \approx \frac12k^2a^2$, is the free-particle $\hbar^2k^2/2m$ again (notes p. 89). Near the edges of the zone the band bends over and flattens instead of rising for ever. (This cosine form is what one gets for a particle that hops between neighbouring sites; the notes give it without derivation.)

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-3.1416, 3.1416]} range={[0, 3]} xLabel="ka" yLabel="ε/(ħ²/ma²)" samples={300}
  curves={[
    { expr: "1 - cos(x)", label: "lattice band" },
    { expr: "x^2/2", label: "free particle", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="Computed from ε_k = (ħ²/ma²)(1 − cos ka) and from the free-particle ε_k = ħ²k²/2m, across the Brillouin zone −π < ka ≤ π. The two agree for small k; towards the zone edges the lattice band levels off at its maximum, 2ħ²/ma²." />

A real crystal has several such bands, one above another, separated by gaps (Wen's band-structure sketches, p. 89). Because the distinct values of $k$ are those in one Brillouin zone, and a crystal of $N_c$ unit cells allows $N_c$ of them, **each band holds as many levels as there are unit cells** (per spin state). Filling the levels from the bottom with the available electrons gives two possibilities:

- **Metal**: the chemical potential falls inside a band, which is only partly filled. There are empty levels just above the occupied ones, a Fermi surface, and the electrons can respond to the smallest push.
- **Insulator**: the electrons exactly fill the lowest bands, and $\mu$ falls in the gap between the last full band and the first empty one. A full band cannot carry a current, and to move an electron into the empty band costs the whole gap.

A **semiconductor** is an insulator whose gap $\Delta$ is small enough that, at ordinary temperatures, a few electrons are thermally excited across it.

## Electrons and holes: counting with Fermi–Dirac occupations

Near the bottom of the upper band (the **conduction band**) the energy is $\epsilon_e = \hbar^2k^2/2m_e$ above the band edge, and near the top of the lower band (the **valence band**) it is $\epsilon_h = \hbar^2k^2/2m_h$ below the edge, with effective masses $m_e$ and $m_h$ set by the curvature of each band. Measure energies from the middle of the gap, so that the band edges are at $\pm\Delta/2$ (Wen's sketch, p. 90). An empty level in the valence band is a **hole**, and the probability that a level is empty is one minus its Fermi–Dirac occupation. Wen's first method simply counts (notes p. 90, Method 1):

<Formula label="Densities of electrons and holes" tex="n_e = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_e + \frac{\Delta}{2} - \mu)} + 1}, \qquad n_h = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_h + \frac{\Delta}{2} + \mu)} + 1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 90" />

(These count one spin state; spin doubles both.) In a pure semiconductor every conduction electron has come from the valence band, so $\mu$ must be adjusted to make $n_e = n_h$. If $m_e = m_h$ the two integrals are mirror images and $\mu = 0$: the chemical potential sits exactly in the middle of the gap. Then

<Formula label="Equal masses" tex="n_e = n_h = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon + \frac{\Delta}{2})} + 1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 90" />

and two limits are easy (notes p. 90). For **large** $T$, $k_BT \gg \Delta$, the gap hardly matters:

<Formula label="Carrier density when the gap is negligible" tex="n_e \approx \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{1}{e^{\beta\epsilon} + 1} = \frac{1}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2mk_BT}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\int_0^\infty dt\,\frac{t^2}{e^{t^2} + 1} \sim \left(\frac{mk_BT}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} \sim \frac{1}{\lambda^3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 90" />

about one carrier per thermal wavelength cubed ($n_e\lambda^3 \approx 0.77$ per spin state): no longer a classical gas but at the crossover to the quantum regime, since with $\mu = 0$ no level is more than half filled. (Here $t = \hbar k/\sqrt{2mk_BT}$; the notes write $e^t$ in the denominator, which belongs to the substitution $t = \epsilon/k_BT$ with a different power of $t$ in front.) For **small** $T$, $k_BT \ll \Delta$, the occupation is tiny, the $+1$ can be dropped, and

<Formula label="Carrier density when the gap is large" tex="n_e \approx \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,e^{-\beta\epsilon}\,e^{-\beta\Delta/2} = \lambda^{-3}\,e^{-\beta\Delta/2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 90" />

a classical gas, with the Boltzmann cost of half the gap. Half, because creating a carrier pair costs $\Delta$, but the cost is shared between the electron and the hole.

## Electrons and holes as a chemical reaction

Wen's second method (notes p. 91, Method 2, which labels the two masses by the conduction and valence bands) treats the creation of an electron–hole pair as a chemical reaction,

<Formula label="The reaction" tex="(\text{electron}) + (\text{hole}) = \Delta" cite="reading1" at="p. 91" />

meaning that a pair costs the energy $\Delta$ and can appear or disappear in equilibrium. For $k_BT \ll \Delta$ the conduction levels have $n_k \ll 1$ and the valence levels have $1 - n_k \ll 1$, so the electrons and the holes both form **classical gases**. For classical gases a reaction $\nu_1X_1 + \nu_2X_2 + \dots$ that costs an energy $\epsilon_0$ is in equilibrium when the **law of mass action** holds:

<Formula label="Law of mass action" tex="\nu_1X_1 + \nu_2X_2 + \dots = \epsilon_0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (\lambda_1^3n_1)^{\nu_1}(\lambda_2^3n_2)^{\nu_2}\cdots = e^{-\epsilon_0/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 91" />

with $\lambda_i = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/m_ik_BT}$ the thermal wavelength of each species. (It follows from the classical result $n_i = \lambda_i^{-3}e^{\beta(\mu_i - \epsilon_i)}$ for each species and the equilibrium condition that the chemical potentials, weighted by the $\nu_i$, add up to zero; Method 1 in its small-$T$ limit is a special case.) Applied to electrons and holes:

<Formula label="Mass action for electrons and holes" tex="\lambda_e^3n_e\,\lambda_h^3n_h = e^{-\Delta/k_BT}, \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 91" />

The chemical potential has dropped out: the product $n_en_h$ is fixed by temperature and gap alone, whatever impurities do to $\mu$. In a pure semiconductor $n_e = n_h$, and with two spin states for each carrier (which multiplies the right-hand side by $2 \times 2 = 4$; the electron–hole law in the notes, written below the boxed general law, leaves the spin factors out, and the 2 reappears in their result) the square root gives

<Formula label="Carrier density of an undoped semiconductor" tex="n_e = n_h = 2\,e^{-\Delta/2k_BT}\left(\frac{\sqrt{m_em_h}\,k_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2} \propto T^{3/2}\,e^{-\frac{\Delta}{2k_BT}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 91" />

Wen's boxed reminder (p. 91) states when this is valid: $\lambda^3n \ll 1$ is the classical regime, $\lambda^3n \gtrsim 1$ the quantum one. If the masses differ, $n_e = n_h$ puts the chemical potential at $\mu = \frac34k_BT\ln(m_h/m_e)$, slightly off the middle of the gap towards the band with the lighter carriers (this follows from Method 1 and is the page's addition; it is the 8.044 result).

## Conductivity of a semiconductor

Both electrons and holes carry current, so the conductivity of an undoped semiconductor is proportional to the number of carriers (notes p. 91):

<Formula label="Conductivity of an undoped semiconductor" tex="\sigma \propto n_e \sim n_h \propto T^{3/2}\,e^{-\frac{\Delta}{2k_BT}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 91" />

(This assumes that how easily each carrier moves changes only slowly with temperature; how carriers move is the subject of Chapter IX.) The exponential dominates: a semiconductor conducts better and better as it warms, the opposite of a metal, whose number of conduction electrons is fixed and whose conductivity falls as the lattice vibrates more.

<WorkedExample title="Carriers in a silicon-like semiconductor">
<Problem>
Take a semiconductor with gap $\Delta = 1.12$ eV and effective masses $m_e = 1.08\,m$ and $m_h = 0.81\,m$ ($m$ the free-electron mass; values close to those of silicon), two spin states for each carrier, and no doping. (a) Find $n_e$ at 300 K. (b) Check that the carriers form a classical gas. (c) By what factor does the conductivity grow between 300 K and 350 K, if the gap and the mobility stay the same?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The carrier density">
At 300 K, $k_BT = 0.0259$ eV, so $\Delta/2k_BT \approx 21.7$ and $e^{-21.7}$ is about $4 \times 10^{-10}$. The prefactor $2(\sqrt{m_em_h}\,k_BT/2\pi\hbar^2)^{3/2}$ is about $2.3 \times 10^{25}$ m⁻³, and $n_e = n_h \approx 8.9 \times 10^{15}$ m⁻³. A solid has about $5 \times 10^{28}$ atoms per cubic metre, so only one electron in several trillion is free.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Classical?">
The electrons' thermal wavelength is $\lambda_e = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/m_ek_BT} \approx 4.1$ nm, so $n_e\lambda_e^3 \approx 6 \times 10^{-10}$: far below 1, as the method assumed.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Warming by 50 K">
$n_e(350)/n_e(300) = (350/300)^{3/2}\,e^{(\Delta/2k_B)(1/300 - 1/350)} \approx 1.26 \times 22.1 \approx 28$. A 17% rise in temperature makes the semiconductor conduct about 28 times better.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The diode: a p–n junction

Impurities change the picture (notes p. 92). Atoms that give up an extra electron make **n-type** material, with extra electrons and the chemical potential close to the conduction band; atoms that capture one make **p-type** material, with extra holes and $\mu$ close to the valence band. Wen's sketches then join the two. In equilibrium the chemical potential must be the same throughout, so electrons flow from the n side to the p side until a charge layer builds up; the bands bend across the junction, leaving a **potential barrier** of height $\epsilon_B$ that electrons on the n side must climb to reach the p side, and an electric field $\vec E$ in the junction.

A voltage across the diode separates the chemical potentials, $\mu_1$ on the n side and $\mu_2$ on the p side. With **positive** (forward) bias the n side is raised: the barrier becomes small and electrons flow over it from n to p. With **negative** (reverse) bias the barrier becomes large and only a small flow of electrons remains, in the opposite direction, from p to n (Wen's arrow). Only electrons in the Boltzmann tail can climb the barrier, so Wen writes the current as

<Formula label="Current through the junction" tex="I \sim V\,e^{-\epsilon_B/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 92" />

with the barrier $\epsilon_B$ itself depending on the bias. Since the barrier sits in an exponential, a small change of bias changes the current enormously, and Wen's sketch of the current against voltage shows the result: a steep rise on one side, almost nothing on the other. The junction is a one-way valve for current, a **rectifier**. (A standard form, the page's addition, makes this explicit: if a forward voltage $V$ lowers the barrier by $eV$, the current is $I = I_s(e^{eV/k_BT} - 1)$, where the $-1$ is the small reverse flow that does not depend on the barrier.)

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The same law of mass action in the early universe">
The equilibrium of electrons and holes, $\lambda_e^3n_e\,\lambda_h^3n_h \propto e^{-\Delta/k_BT}$, is a classical law of mass action, and the early universe used the same law. In [MIT 8.902 Lecture 20](/courses/8-902/big-bang-nucleosynthesis), the abundance of deuterium, made when a proton and a neutron combine and emit a photon, follows the **Saha equation**, in which thermal-wavelength factors for each species multiply a Boltzmann factor of the binding energy, $2.22$ MeV. In both, the exponential decides: pair creation across the gap dies out as the semiconductor cools, and deuterium survives only once the universe is cool enough that photons can no longer break it apart.
</Callout>

## Summary

- On a lattice of spacing $a$, $k$ and $k + 2\pi/a$ are the same state; the distinct states form a **Brillouin zone**, and the energy is periodic, as in $\epsilon_k = \frac{\hbar^2}{ma^2}(1 - \cos ka) \approx \hbar^2k^2/2m$ for small $k$.
- Each **band** holds one level per unit cell. **Metal**: $\mu$ inside a band. **Insulator**: $\mu$ in a gap. **Semiconductor**: a small gap $\Delta$.
- Counting with Fermi–Dirac occupations (equal masses): $\mu$ sits mid-gap, and $n_e \approx \lambda^{-3}e^{-\Delta/2k_BT}$ for $k_BT \ll \Delta$.
- As a reaction, electron + hole $= \Delta$: the **law of mass action** $\lambda_e^3n_e\,\lambda_h^3n_h = 4e^{-\Delta/k_BT}$ (with spin), so $n_e = n_h \propto T^{3/2}e^{-\Delta/2k_BT}$, and so is the conductivity of an undoped semiconductor.
- A **p–n junction** equalizes $\mu$ by building a barrier $\epsilon_B$; the current $\propto e^{-\epsilon_B/k_BT}$ grows steeply under forward bias and is tiny under reverse bias: a diode.
