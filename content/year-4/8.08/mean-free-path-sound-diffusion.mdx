Chapter IX of Wen's notes, the last long chapter of the course, is about **transport**: how particles, momentum, energy and charge move through a gas or a metal when it is not quite in equilibrium. This page follows the first nine pages of the handwritten notes (pp. 93–101): the mean free path, the two limits it separates, effusion through a small hole, the equations of a gas that flows as a fluid, sound waves, diffusion and the random walk, and the damping of sound by diffusion. The [next page](/courses/8-08/viscosity-conduction-boltzmann-equation) applies the same reasoning to viscosity, heat conduction and electrons in metals, and the [third page](/courses/8-08/einstein-relation-langevin-noise) to Brownian motion and noise. The Boltzmann distribution comes from [the canonical-ensemble examples of 8.044](/courses/8-044/canonical-examples), the entropy of the ideal gas from [Chapter I](/courses/8-08/information-equipartition-ideal-gas), and the diffusion equation from [18.02](/courses/18-02/diffusion-equation). Everything here is in our own words, with our own example.

## The mean free path and the two limits

A particle moving through a gas travels in a straight line until it hits another one. If each collision centre presents a target of area $\sigma$, the **scattering cross-section**, then a particle sweeping out a cylinder of cross-section $\sigma$ and length $\lambda$ meets, on average, one collision centre when that cylinder contains exactly one of them (notes p. 93). With $n$ collision centres per unit volume, $\sigma\lambda n = 1$:

<Formula label="Mean free path" tex="\lambda = \frac{1}{\sigma n}" cite="reading1" at="p. 93" />

This is the **mean free path**. For two hard balls of radius $r$, a collision happens whenever their centres pass within $2r$ of each other, so the target is a disc of radius $2r$ (notes p. 93):

<Formula label="Cross-section of two hard balls" tex="\sigma = \pi(2r)^2 = 4\pi r^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 93" />

(A more careful count, which allows for the motion of the collision partners, divides $\lambda$ by $\sqrt2$; the page keeps Wen's simpler formula.) Wen's first point is that transport has **two limits**, depending on how the mean free path compares with the scale we look at. If a gas leaks through a hole of diameter $d$ smaller than $\lambda$, the particles that reach the hole fly straight through without meeting anyone: this is **effusion**, the **collisionless** limit. If the hole is much larger than $\lambda$, the particles collide many times on their way and the gas streams through as a fluid: this is **hydrodynamic flow** (notes p. 93, with a sketch of each).

## Effusion

Effusion needs only the distribution of momenta in the gas. Write $f(\vec p)\,d^3p$ for the density of particles with momentum in $d^3p$; in equilibrium it is the **Boltzmann distribution** (notes p. 94):

<Formula label="Boltzmann distribution of momenta" tex="f(\vec p) = C\,e^{-\beta\vec p^{\,2}/2m}, \qquad C = n\,(2\pi mk_BT)^{-3/2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 94" />

with $\beta = 1/k_BT$; the constant $C$ is fixed by $\int d^3p\,f(\vec p) = n$, the total density. A hole in the wall at $x = 0$, with gas on one side and vacuum on the other, lets through every particle that arrives with $v_x > 0$. The **flux of effusion**, the number of particles per unit area per second, is then

<Formula label="Flux of effusion" tex="I = \int_{v_x > 0} v_x\,f(\vec p)\,d^3p = C\int_{p_x > 0}\frac{p_x}{m}\,e^{-\beta(p_x^2 + p_y^2 + p_z^2)/2m}\,dp_x\,dp_y\,dp_z = n\sqrt{\frac{k_BT}{2\pi m}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 94" />

So $I \propto n\,T^{1/2}m^{-1/2}$. Wen asks for the physical picture (notes p. 94): the flux is the density times a typical speed, and a typical speed is $\sqrt{k_BT/m}$. (Written with the mean speed $\langle|\vec v|\rangle = \sqrt{8k_BT/\pi m}$ it is the textbook $\frac{1}{4} n\langle|\vec v|\rangle$; that form is the page's addition.) Because lighter particles get through faster, effusion separates isotopes, a little at each pass.

## Non-viscous hydrodynamics: three equations

In the opposite limit, $\lambda/\ell \to 0$ with $\ell$ the length scale of interest, each particle is "caged" by its neighbours (notes p. 95). A small piece of the gas then keeps its particles and its heat as it moves, and the gas can be described by smooth fields: the **mass density** $\rho(\vec x, t) = m\,n(\vec x, t)$ and the **mass current** $\rho\vec u$, where $\vec u$ is the average velocity of the particles, the velocity of the fluid. Three equations govern them (notes pp. 95–96).

**① Mass conservation.** Mass flows but is not created, which is the **continuity equation**:

<Formula label="Continuity equation" tex="\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(\rho\vec u) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 95, point ①" />

In integral form, the mass in a volume $V$ changes only by the flux through its surface, $\frac{d}{dt}\int_V d^3x\,\rho + \oint_{\partial V} d\vec S\cdot\rho\vec u = 0$.

**② Newton's law.** If we follow one piece of fluid, $d\vec u/dt = \vec f/m$ per particle. But $\vec u(\vec x, t)$ is a velocity field, and following the piece means moving with it, so the time derivative is the **material derivative** (notes p. 95):

<Formula label="Material derivative" tex="\frac{d\vec u}{dt} = \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \vec u\cdot\nabla\right)\vec u" cite="reading1" at="p. 95, point ②" />

The force comes from the pressure. On a slab of area $A$ and thickness $\Delta x$ the net force is $F_x = A[P(x) - P(x + \Delta x)] = -\Delta x\,A\,\partial P/\partial x$, shared among the $n\,A\,\Delta x$ particles in it, so each feels $-(\partial P/\partial x)/n$ (notes p. 96). Adding an external force $\vec f_\text{ex}$ per particle and multiplying by $mn = \rho$ gives **Euler's equation**:

<Formula label="Euler's equation" tex="\rho\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \vec u\cdot\nabla\right)\vec u + \nabla P = n\vec f_\text{ex}" cite="reading1" at="p. 96, point ②" />

**③ The adiabatic condition.** Since a piece of fluid exchanges no heat with its neighbours, its entropy per particle $s = S/N$ does not change as it flows (notes p. 96). For an ideal monatomic gas

<Formula label="Entropy of the ideal gas" tex="S = Nk_B\left[\frac{5}{2} - \ln(n\lambda_T^3)\right], \qquad \lambda_T = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_BT}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 96, point ③" />

where $\lambda_T$ is the thermal wavelength (Wen writes it $\lambda$ too; we add the subscript to keep it apart from the mean free path). Constant $s$ means constant $n\lambda_T^3$, so $nT^{-3/2}$ is conserved. With $P = nk_BT$, $T \propto P/n$, and $n(n/P)^{3/2} = n^{5/2}P^{-3/2}$ is conserved, which is the same as $P\rho^{-5/3}$. Along the flow:

<Formula label="Adiabatic condition" tex="\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \vec u\cdot\nabla\right)\left(P\rho^{-\gamma}\right) = 0, \qquad \gamma = \frac{5}{3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 96, point ③" />

That makes five equations (one for mass, three components of Euler's, one adiabatic) for five unknowns, $\rho$, $P$ and the three components of $\vec u$ (notes p. 96).

## Sound waves

A gas at rest with density $\rho_0$ and pressure $P_0$ is a solution. Small disturbances, $\rho = \rho_0 + \delta\rho$, $P = P_0 + \delta P$ and a small $\vec u$, obey the equations with every product of two small quantities dropped (notes p. 97):

<Formula label="Linearized equations" tex="\frac{\partial\,\delta\rho}{\partial t} + \rho_0\nabla\cdot\vec u = 0, \qquad \rho_0\frac{\partial\vec u}{\partial t} + \nabla\delta P = 0, \qquad \frac{\delta P}{P_0} = \gamma\,\frac{\delta\rho}{\rho_0}" cite="reading1" at="p. 97" />

The third comes from the adiabatic condition, $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(P\rho^{-\gamma}) = \rho_0^{-\gamma}\partial\,\delta P/\partial t - \gamma P_0\rho_0^{-\gamma - 1}\partial\,\delta\rho/\partial t = 0$. Taking the time derivative of the first equation and the divergence of the second eliminates $\vec u$: $\partial^2\delta\rho/\partial t^2 - \nabla^2\delta P = 0$, and with $\delta P = \gamma(P_0/\rho_0)\delta\rho$ this is a **wave equation**:

<Formula label="Sound waves" tex="\frac{\partial^2\delta\rho}{\partial t^2} - c^2\nabla^2\delta\rho = 0, \qquad c = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P_0}{\rho_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma k_BT}{m}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 97" />

The speed of sound is of the order of the thermal speed of the particles (notes p. 97). (A rule of thumb of our own for why: a pressure disturbance is passed on by the moving particles, so its speed is set by theirs.) The same result, with $\gamma = 7/5$ for air, is the subject of [the 8.03 page on sound waves](/courses/8-03/sound-waves).

## Diffusion

The hydrodynamic equations treat the gas as if $\lambda = 0$. With a finite mean free path, particles also wander from one place to another on their own, even when the fluid is at rest, and this is **diffusion** (notes p. 98). Wen's estimate: think of the particles as moving along the six directions $\pm x$, $\pm y$, $\pm z$, a sixth of them each way, with the same mean speed $\bar v$. Across a plane at $x$, a sixth of the particles from the left cross to the right and a sixth from the right cross to the left, so the net flux is $j_x = \frac{1}{6}\bar v(n_1 - n_2)$. The particles crossing have come about one mean free path since their last collision, so $n_1$ and $n_2$ are the densities a distance $\lambda$ apart, and $n_1 - n_2 = -\lambda\,\partial n/\partial x$. Then (notes p. 98)

<Formula label="Diffusion flux" tex="j_x = -\frac{1}{6}\bar v\lambda\,\partial n/\partial x, \qquad \vec j = -D\nabla n, \qquad D \simeq \frac{\lambda\bar v}{6}" cite="reading1" at="p. 98" />

The **diffusion constant** $D$ vanishes in the hydrodynamic limit $\lambda \to 0$. Adding the diffusion flux to the flow, $\vec j = n\vec u - D\nabla n$, the continuity equation becomes (notes p. 99)

<Formula label="Continuity equation with diffusion" tex="\frac{\partial n}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot(n\vec u) - D\nabla^2n = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 99" />

or the same with $\rho$ in place of $n$. In a fluid at rest, $\vec u = 0$, it is the **diffusion equation** $\partial n/\partial t - D\nabla^2n = 0$.

## Spreading from a point

Put $N$ particles at the origin at $t = 0$, $n(0, \vec x) = N\delta(\vec x)$ (notes p. 99). In Fourier space, $\tilde n(t, \vec k) = \int d^3x\,e^{-i\vec k\cdot\vec x}n(t, \vec x)$, the diffusion equation becomes $\partial\tilde n/\partial t + Dk^2\tilde n = 0$, so each Fourier mode decays on its own, $\tilde n = N e^{-Dk^2t}$. Transforming back means a Gaussian integral, done by completing the square in the exponent, $-Dt\,k^2 + i\vec k\cdot\vec x = -Dt(\vec k - \frac{i}{2}\frac{\vec x}{Dt})^2 - \frac{\vec x^{\,2}}{4Dt}$ (notes p. 99; the notes leave out the factor $N$ in this step and restore it in the result). The result is

<Formula label="Point source spreading" tex="n(\vec x, t) = \frac{N}{(4\pi Dt)^{3/2}}\,e^{-|\vec x|^2/4Dt}" cite="reading1" at="p. 100" />

a Gaussian that widens with time. Its mean square distance from the origin, with $\int dx\,x^2e^{-x^2} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt\pi$, is (notes p. 100)

<Formula label="Mean square displacement" tex="\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle = \int d^3x\,\vec x^{\,2}\,\frac{e^{-|\vec x|^2/4Dt}}{(4\pi Dt)^{3/2}} = 4Dt\times\frac{3}{2} = 6Dt, \qquad \bar x \equiv \sqrt{\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle} = \sqrt{6Dt}" cite="reading1" at="p. 100" />

The typical distance grows only as $\sqrt t$. The plot shows how the particles are spread over distance, $4\pi r^2n$, which peaks at $r = 2\sqrt{Dt}$ and moves out ever more slowly.

## The random walk

The same law comes out of a **random walk** (notes p. 100). Let each step have length $\lambda$ in a random direction. After $N$ steps the position is $\vec x = \Delta\vec x_1 + \Delta\vec x_2 + \cdots$, and because different steps are independent, the cross terms average to zero:

<Formula label="Random walk" tex="\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle = \Delta\vec x_1^{\,2} + \Delta\vec x_2^{\,2} + \cdots = N\lambda^2, \qquad \bar x = \lambda\sqrt N" cite="reading1" at="p. 100" />

By the central limit theorem (see [8.044](/courses/8-044/sums-central-limit-theorem)) the position after many steps has a Gaussian distribution, $n(\vec x, N) = (\pi\alpha)^{-3/2}e^{-\vec x^{\,2}/\alpha}$. Its mean square is $\frac{3}{2}\alpha$, which must equal $N\lambda^2$, so $\alpha = \frac{2}{3}N\lambda^2$. If each step takes a time $\tau$, then $N = t/\tau$, and comparing with the point-source solution, where $\alpha = 4Dt$, gives (notes p. 100)

<Formula label="Diffusion constant of a random walk" tex="D = \frac{\lambda^2}{6\tau} = \frac{\lambda\bar v}{6}" cite="reading1" at="p. 100" />

with $\bar v = \lambda/\tau$. The random walk reproduces exactly the estimate made with the six directions: diffusion is the random walk of each particle from collision to collision.

## The damping of sound by diffusion

Diffusion also damps sound (notes p. 101). With the diffusion term in the continuity equation, the linearized equations are

<Formula label="Linearized equations with diffusion" tex="\frac{\partial\,\delta\rho}{\partial t} + \rho_0\nabla\cdot\vec u - D\nabla^2\delta\rho = 0, \qquad \rho_0\frac{\partial\vec u}{\partial t} + \nabla\delta P = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 101" />

and eliminating $\vec u$ as before,

<Formula label="Damped wave equation" tex="\frac{\partial^2\delta\rho}{\partial t^2} - c^2\nabla^2\delta\rho - D\nabla^2\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\delta\rho = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 101" />

A plane wave $\delta\rho = Ae^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ solves it if $-\omega^2 + c^2k^2 - i\omega Dk^2 = 0$. For small $D$, put $\omega \approx ck$ in the last term: $\omega^2 = c^2k^2(1 - iDk/c)$, and so (notes p. 101)

<Formula label="Dispersion relation with damping" tex="\omega = ck\left(1 - \frac{i}{2}\,\frac{Dk}{c}\right), \qquad \delta\rho = Ae^{i(kx - ckt)}\,e^{-\Gamma t}, \qquad \Gamma = \frac{Dk^2}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 101" />

The wave travels at the speed of sound and dies away at the **damping rate** $\Gamma$ (Wen writes both the decay time $\tau = 2/Dk^2$ and the damping rate $1/\tau = Dk^2/2$). With $D = \lambda\bar v/6$, $\Gamma = \lambda\bar vk^2/12$, and since $\bar v$ is of the order of $c$, the decay time $12/(\lambda\bar vk^2)$ is about $1/(\lambda k)$ times the inverse frequency $1/\omega$ (notes p. 101). A sound wave oscillates about $1/(\lambda k)$ times before it dies: long waves, many mean free paths long, are hardly damped at all, and short ones die fast.

(A remark of the page's own: Wen's diffusion term in the continuity equation is a model, not the real mechanism. In a gas of one kind of atom, $\vec u$ is by definition the average velocity of the atoms, so the mass current is exactly $\rho\vec u$, with no diffusion term. In a full treatment the damping of sound comes from viscosity and heat conduction, the coefficients of the next page. They are of the same size, $\lambda\bar v$ times the density, and give the same $k^2$ law, which is what Wen's diffusion model captures.)

<WorkedExample title="Argon at room temperature">
<Problem>
Argon atoms (mass 39.95 u) behave roughly as hard balls of radius $r = 0.18$ nm. For argon at 300 K and atmospheric pressure (101 325 Pa), find the density, the cross-section and the mean free path; the speed $\bar v = \sqrt{2k_BT/m}$, the diffusion constant and the speed of sound; how far a puff of argon atoms spreads in one second and in one hour; and how quickly diffusion damps sound at 1 kHz and at 100 kHz.
</Problem>
<Step title="Density, cross-section and mean free path">
$n = P/k_BT = 101\,325/(1.3806 \times 10^{-23} \times 300) \approx 2.45 \times 10^{25}$ m⁻³. $\sigma = 4\pi r^2 = 4\pi(0.18 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{m})^2 \approx 4.07 \times 10^{-19}$ m². So $\lambda = 1/(\sigma n) \approx 1.00 \times 10^{-7}$ m, about 100 nm, some 30 times the spacing between atoms. A hole smaller than this lets the gas effuse; the effusion flux $n\sqrt{k_BT/2\pi m}$ is about $2.44 \times 10^{27}$ atoms per m² per second.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed, diffusion constant and sound">
$m = 39.95 \times 1.6605 \times 10^{-27} \approx 6.63 \times 10^{-26}$ kg, so $\bar v = \sqrt{2k_BT/m} \approx 353$ m/s and $D = \lambda\bar v/6 \approx 5.9 \times 10^{-6}$ m²/s. The speed of sound is $c = \sqrt{5k_BT/3m} \approx 323$ m/s, close to $\bar v$ as expected.
</Step>
<Step title="Spreading">
$\sqrt{6Dt}$ is about 6.0 mm after one second and 36 cm after an hour (3600 times the time, 60 times the distance). Diffusion alone is slow: smells cross a room by air currents, not by diffusion.
</Step>
<Step title="Damping of sound">
At 1 kHz, $k = 2\pi f/c \approx 19.5$ m⁻¹ and $\Gamma = Dk^2/2 \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-3}$ s⁻¹: the wave would take about 890 s to decay, in which it travels some 290 km. At 100 kHz, $k$ is 100 times larger and $\Gamma$ is $10^4$ times larger, about 11 s⁻¹: the wave dies within about 29 m. There $\lambda k \approx 2 \times 10^{-4}$, so the wave still oscillates thousands of times first.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

(The estimate $D = \lambda\bar v/6$ uses only rough averages; it gives the right order of magnitude, not a precise value. With the $\sqrt2$ for moving partners the mean free path would be about 71 nm. Real argon damps sound several times faster than these numbers say, because viscosity and heat conduction, the true mechanisms, are larger than Wen's diffusion term; in air the slow exchange of energy with molecular vibrations adds more. The model shows the essential law: damping grows as the square of the frequency.)

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Mean free paths in the universe">
Both of Wen's limits appear in cosmology. Before decoupling, the photons of the early universe had a short mean free path, set by Thomson scattering off free electrons, and the photons and the ionized gas moved together as one fluid that carried sound waves. As the electrons combined into atoms, the photons stopped scattering and the universe became transparent: that is decoupling, dated in [MIT 8.286 Lecture 17](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling). Even before then the coupling was not perfect: photons random-walked out of the smallest hot and cold patches and smeared them out, the **Silk damping** described in [MIT 8.902 Lecture 17](/courses/8-902/cmb-anisotropies), a cousin of the diffusive damping of sound on this page. At the other extreme, stars in a galaxy almost never meet, which makes a galaxy a collisionless system, as [8.902 Lecture 3](/courses/8-902/relaxation-times) shows.

## Summary

- The **mean free path** is $\lambda = 1/(\sigma n)$; for hard balls of radius $r$, $\sigma = 4\pi r^2$. On scales below $\lambda$ a gas is **collisionless** (effusion); on scales far above it, **hydrodynamic**.
- From the Boltzmann distribution $f = n(2\pi mk_BT)^{-3/2}e^{-\beta p^2/2m}$, the **effusion flux** is $I = n\sqrt{k_BT/2\pi m}$, proportional to $nT^{1/2}m^{-1/2}$.
- **Non-viscous hydrodynamics**: the continuity equation, Euler's equation $\rho(\partial/\partial t + \vec u\cdot\nabla)\vec u + \nabla P = n\vec f_\text{ex}$, and the adiabatic condition $(\partial/\partial t + \vec u\cdot\nabla)(P\rho^{-5/3}) = 0$: five equations for $\rho$, $P$, $\vec u$.
- Linearized, they give **sound** with $c = \sqrt{\gamma P_0/\rho_0} = \sqrt{\gamma k_BT/m}$, of the order of the thermal speed.
- **Diffusion**: $\vec j = -D\nabla n$ with $D \simeq \lambda\bar v/6 = \lambda^2/6\tau$. A point cloud spreads as a Gaussian with $\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle = 6Dt$, as does a random walk of steps $\lambda$, $\langle\vec x^{\,2}\rangle = N\lambda^2$.
- Diffusion **damps sound** at the rate $\Gamma = Dk^2/2$: a wave oscillates about $1/(\lambda k)$ times before it dies.
