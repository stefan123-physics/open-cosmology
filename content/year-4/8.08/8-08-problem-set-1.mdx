This set practises the first week of the course: counting states and the entropy $S = k_B\ln\Gamma$ of the [statistical ensembles supplement](/courses/8-08/counting-states-spins-ideal-gas), and the [canonical ensemble and the free energy](/courses/8-08/canonical-ensemble-free-energy) of Chapter I, with its [stretched chain and ideal gas](/courses/8-08/information-equipartition-ideal-gas). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 1 of February 2, 2005, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its due date is left out. MIT's Problems 1 and 4 are only pointers to problems in a textbook (Huang's *Statistical Mechanics*), named by number, with no title. The textbook is not used here: **Problems 1 and 4 are our own problems**, on topics of the first week (counting the states of an Einstein solid, and the canonical ensemble of a gas in a gravitational field). MIT's Problems 2 and 3 also point to a textbook problem, but MIT replaces its questions with its own, parts (a) to (d), about a hanging chain of $N$ links of length $a$. **The setup of the chain in Problems 2 and 3 is ours**, built so that MIT's questions apply; the questions are MIT's, rewritten. Our additions to them are marked where they stand: the worked numbers of Problem 2(a), part (e) of Problem 2, and the polymer numbers and number boxes of Problems 3(c) and 3(d). The choice boxes of Problems 3(a) and 3(d) are also ours.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it; give it as a decimal. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `q*log(N) - N`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `kB` stands for $k_B$, `lam` for $\lambda$, `sigma` for $\sigma$, and `log` for the natural logarithm; the other letters (`N`, `q`, `y`, `L`, `a`, `m`, `g`, `T`) are typed as they are.

MIT's sheet gives no points, so the points here are ours: 25 for each problem, 100 points in all. Parts that ask you to show something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of Wen's notes. $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature and $\beta = 1/k_BT$. $\Gamma$ is a number of states, $S = k_B\ln\Gamma$ the entropy, $Q$ the partition function and $A$ the free energy. Two results of the notes are used throughout: the temperature of an isolated system follows from its entropy as a function of its energy,

<Formula label="Temperature from the entropy" tex="\frac1T = \frac{\partial S(E)}{\partial E}" cite="reading2" at="Section 1.2.2, pp. 3–4, Eqs. (1.2.12)–(1.2.13)" />

and a system in a heat bath has the free energy and mean energy

<Formula label="Free energy and mean energy from the partition function" tex="Q = \sum_n e^{-E_n/k_BT}, \qquad A = -k_BT\ln Q, \qquad \bar E = -\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\ln Q" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, point ③; p. 3, point ⓑ; p. 4, point ⓑ" />

For large numbers Stirling's formula, $\ln n! \approx n\ln n - n$, is enough (the notes give the next terms in Supplement 1, Eq. (1.2.5)).

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Counting the states of an Einstein solid (our own problem)" points={25} mit="Problem 1">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on counting states and the temperature of an isolated system.

A simple model of a solid (Einstein's) treats it as $N$ independent quantum harmonic oscillators, all with the same angular frequency $\omega$. An oscillator in its $n$-th level has the energy $(n + \frac12)\hbar\omega$, with $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ The solid is isolated, with total energy

<Formula label="Energy of the Einstein solid" tex="E = \left(q + \frac N2\right)\hbar\omega, \qquad q = n_1 + n_2 + \cdots + n_N" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

so that $q$ quanta of energy $\hbar\omega$ are shared among the oscillators. A state of the solid is a list $(n_1, n_2, \ldots, n_N)$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Take a tiny solid of $N = 3$ oscillators sharing $q = 4$ quanta. How many states does it have?

<Hint>

List the states by the value of $n_1$. If $n_1 = 0$, the other two oscillators share 4 quanta: in how many ways?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Two oscillators sharing $k$ quanta can do it in $k + 1$ ways. Add these up for $n_1 = 0$ to $4$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-count" label="Number of states:" value="15" tolerance="0" calc="p1-count" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count the lists of three whole numbers that add up to 4.

<Step title="Count by the first oscillator">

If $n_1 = k$, the other two share $4 - k$ quanta, which they can do in $5 - k$ ways ($n_2 = 0, 1, \ldots, 4 - k$). So the number of states is $5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second count: write the 4 quanta as 4 dots and separate the three oscillators by 2 bars, so that two dots, a bar, one dot, a bar and one dot stand for $(2, 1, 1)$. Every state is one arrangement of 4 dots and 2 bars in a row of 6, and there are $\binom{6}{2} = 15$ of them.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Now take any $N$ and $q$. How many states $\Gamma(q, N)$ does the solid have?

<Hint>

Use the second count of part (a): $q$ dots and $N - 1$ bars in a row. Which positions do the bars take?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-gamma" label="$\Gamma(q, N) =$" choices={["N^q, since each quantum can go to any of the N oscillators", "(q + N)!/(q! N!)", "(q + N − 1)!/[q! (N − 1)!]", "q!/[N! (q − N)!]"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Quanta are not labelled: a state says only how many quanta each oscillator has. So count arrangements of identical dots and bars.

<Step title="Dots and bars">

The $q$ quanta and the $N - 1$ walls between neighbouring oscillators make a row of $q + N - 1$ places. A state is a choice of the $N - 1$ places that hold walls:

<Formula label="Number of states of the Einstein solid" tex="\Gamma(q, N) = \binom{q + N - 1}{q} = \frac{(q + N - 1)!}{q!\,(N - 1)!}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $N = 3$, $q = 4$ it gives $6!/(4!\,2!) = 15$, as in part (a). The other choices give 81, 35 and 4. $N^q$ counts the quanta as if they were labelled; $(q + N)!/(q!\,N!)$ has one wall too many.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Let $q$ and $N$ both be large. Use Stirling's formula, and replace $q + N - 1$ by $q + N$ and $N - 1$ by $N$, to find the entropy $S/k_B$ as a function of $q$ and $N$.

<Hint>

Take the logarithm of $\Gamma$: $\ln(q + N)! - \ln q! - \ln N!$. Apply $\ln n! \approx n\ln n - n$ to each term.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The terms $-n$ cancel, because $(q + N) - q - N = 0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-s" label="$S/k_B =$" expr="(q + N)*log(q + N) - q*log(q) - N*log(N)"
  vars={{ q: [50, 500], N: [50, 500] }}
  names={{ q: "q", N: "N" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "q*log(q) + N*log(N) - (q + N)*log(q + N)", hint: "That is minus the entropy: it is negative, but the logarithm of a number of states larger than 1 is positive. The factorial in the numerator, (q + N)!, enters with a plus sign." },
    { expr: "q*log((q + N)/q)", hint: "That is only one of two parts. The full result can be written q ln((q + N)/q) + N ln((q + N)/N): the second term is missing." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The entropy is the logarithm of the count of part (b); Stirling's formula turns the factorials into smooth functions.

<Step title="Stirling's formula">

With the replacements, $S/k_B = \ln(q + N)! - \ln q! - \ln N!$. Each term is $n\ln n - n$, and the three $-n$ cancel:

<Formula label="Entropy of the Einstein solid" tex="\frac{S}{k_B} = (q + N)\ln(q + N) - q\ln q - N\ln N = q\ln\frac{q + N}{q} + N\ln\frac{q + N}{N}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both terms of the second form are positive, and the expression is symmetric in $q$ and $N$, as the count with $q$ dots and $N$ bars is. For $q = N = 1000$ it gives $2000\ln 2 = 1386.3$, against the exact $\ln[1999!/(1000!\,999!)] = 1381.6$: the difference, about 5, grows only like $\ln N$, while the entropy grows like $N$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={6}>

Use $1/T = \partial S/\partial E$ to find the mean number of quanta per oscillator, $q/N$, as a function of $y = \hbar\omega/k_BT$.

<Hint>

By the energy formula, $dE = \hbar\omega\,dq$ at fixed $N$. So $1/T = (1/\hbar\omega)\,\partial S/\partial q$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The derivative of $S/k_B$ with respect to $q$ is a single logarithm. Set it equal to $y$ and solve for $q/N$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-nbar" label="$q/N =$" expr="1/(exp(y) - 1)"
  vars={{ y: [0.3, 3] }}
  names={{ y: "y" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(-y)", hint: "That is the Boltzmann factor of one quantum. It is close to the answer only at low temperature, y much greater than 1, where q/N is small; at high temperature it stays below 1, while the number of quanta per oscillator grows without limit." },
    { expr: "1/y", hint: "That is the high-temperature limit, y much less than 1, where each oscillator holds about k_BT of energy (equipartition). Solve ln((q + N)/q) = y exactly instead." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The temperature measures how fast the entropy grows as quanta are added.

<Step title="The derivative">

From part (c), $\partial(S/k_B)/\partial q = \ln(q + N) + 1 - \ln q - 1 = \ln\frac{q + N}{q}$. So

<Formula label="Temperature of the Einstein solid" tex="\frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\,\frac{\partial S}{\partial q} = \frac{k_B}{\hbar\omega}\ln\frac{q + N}{q}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for q/N">

$\ln(1 + N/q) = y$ gives $N/q = e^y - 1$, so

<Formula label="Mean number of quanta per oscillator" tex="\frac qN = \frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT} - 1}, \qquad E = N\hbar\omega\left(\frac12 + \frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT} - 1}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is Planck's mean occupation number, found here from counting alone. The canonical ensemble gives the same result for one oscillator in a heat bath.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At high temperature, $y \ll 1$, $e^y - 1 \approx y$ and $q/N \approx k_BT/\hbar\omega$: each oscillator has energy $k_BT$, the classical value for a kinetic and a potential quadratic term. At low temperature, $y \gg 1$, $q/N \approx e^{-y}$: the quanta die out exponentially.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={4}>

For a solid at the temperature where $\hbar\omega = 0.8\,k_BT$ (our number), what is the mean number of quanta per oscillator? Give it to three decimals.

<Hint>

Put $y = 0.8$ into part (d). Do not use either of the two limits: $y = 0.8$ is neither large nor small.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-num" label="$q/N =$" value="0.816" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Evaluate the exact result of part (d).

<Step title="The number">

$e^{0.8} = 2.2255$, so $q/N = 1/1.2255 = 0.816$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two limits give $1/y = 1.25$ and $e^{-y} = 0.449$; the true value lies between them, as it must.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A hanging chain with a weight, as an isolated system" points={25} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet points to a textbook problem here but asks its own four questions, (a) to (d), about a hanging chain. The setup below is ours, so that MIT's questions apply; the questions are MIT's.

A chain of $N$ links, each of length $a$, hangs from a hook in the ceiling. The joints are free and the chain is one-dimensional: each link points either straight down or straight up. The links have no mass. A weight of mass $m$ hangs from the lower end. If $N_d$ links point down and $N_u$ point up, the length of the chain, measured downward from the hook to the weight, is

<Formula label="Length of the hanging chain" tex="L = (N_d - N_u)\,a, \qquad N_d + N_u = N" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own setup)" />

Take the potential energy of the weight to be zero when it is at the level of the hook, and neglect its motion: the energy of the chain with its weight is the weight's potential energy, which depends on $L$ only. The chain and the weight form an isolated system. As MIT asks, assume that the ceiling does not block the chain, so links may point up past the hook.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

How many states does the chain have when its length is $L$? Write it with $N$, $L$ and $a$. Then, as a check (our numbers): how many states are there for $N = 12$ and $L = 4a$?

<Hint>

A length fixes $N_d$ and $N_u$. Solve the two equations of the setup for them.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Then count the ways to choose which $N_u$ of the $N$ links point up.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-count" label="Number of states for N = 12, L = 4a:" value="495" tolerance="0" calc="p2-count" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A state is a list of $N$ directions. The length fixes how many of each there are, but not which links they are.

<Step title="Count">

From $N_d + N_u = N$ and $N_d - N_u = L/a$: $N_d = \frac12(N + L/a)$ and $N_u = \frac12(N - L/a)$. The number of states is the number of ways to pick the $N_u$ upward links:

<Formula label="Number of states of the chain" tex="\Gamma(L) = \binom{N}{N_u}, \qquad N_d = \frac12\left(N + \frac La\right), \quad N_u = \frac12\left(N - \frac La\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This binomial coefficient is $N!$ divided by the factorials of $N_d$ and $N_u$. It is zero unless $N + L/a$ is an even whole number between $0$ and $2N$.

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

For $N = 12$, $L = 4a$: $N_d = 8$, $N_u = 4$ and $\Gamma = 12!/(8!\,4!) = (12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10 \cdot 9)/24 = 495$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Summed over all lengths, $\Gamma(L)$ must give $2^N$, the number of all lists of directions, and it does by the binomial theorem. The largest $\Gamma$ is at $L = 0$; the fully stretched chain, $L = Na$, has a single state.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find the entropy and the energy of the chain when its length is $L$. Take $N$ large and $L \ll Na$, as MIT does, and keep the entropy to order $L^2$.

<Hint>

Write $N_d = \frac N2(1 + x)$ and $N_u = \frac N2(1 - x)$ with $x = L/Na$, and apply Stirling's formula to $\ln\Gamma$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You should reach $S/k_B = N\ln 2 - \frac N2\left[(1 + x)\ln(1 + x) + (1 - x)\ln(1 - x)\right]$. Expand the bracket for small $x$, using $\ln(1 + x) \approx x - x^2/2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-s" label="$S/k_B =$" expr="N*log(2) - L^2/(2*N*a^2)"
  vars={{ N: [50, 200], L: [1, 5], a: [0.5, 1] }}
  names={{ N: "N", L: "L", a: "a" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "N*log(2) - L^2/(N*a^2)", hint: "The drop is twice too large. The bracket (1 + x) ln(1 + x) + (1 − x) ln(1 − x) is about x², and it is multiplied by N/2, not by N." },
    { expr: "N*log(2) - L^2/(2*N^2*a^2)", hint: "That drop, x²/2 with x = L/Na, is the drop per link. There are N links, so the total is N x²/2." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-e" label="$E =$" expr="-m*g*L"
  vars={{ m: [0.5, 2], g: [5, 15], L: [1, 5] }}
  names={{ m: "m", g: "g", L: "L" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "m*g*L", hint: "Check the sign: L is measured downward, so a longer chain puts the weight lower, and its potential energy falls as L grows." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The entropy is $k_B$ times the logarithm of the count of part (a). Nearly all states have a length near zero, so the entropy is close to $Nk_B\ln 2$ and falls quadratically as the chain is stretched.

<Step title="Stirling's formula">

With $\ln n! \approx n\ln n - n$ and $N_d = \frac N2(1 + x)$, $N_u = \frac N2(1 - x)$:

<Formula label="Entropy of the chain" tex="\frac{S}{k_B} = \ln\Gamma \approx N\ln 2 - \frac N2\left[(1 + x)\ln(1 + x) + (1 - x)\ln(1 - x)\right], \qquad x = \frac{L}{Na}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Small extension">

$(1 \pm x)\ln(1 \pm x) \approx \pm x + x^2/2$, so the bracket is $x^2$ and

<Formula label="Entropy of a slightly stretched chain" tex="S(L) \approx k_B\left(N\ln 2 - \frac{L^2}{2Na^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The energy">

The weight is a distance $L$ below the hook, so

<Formula label="Energy of the chain with its weight" tex="E(L) = -mgL" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $L = 0$ the entropy is $Nk_B\ln 2$, the logarithm of all $2^N$ states, as in Wen's chain (Chapter I, p. 5): to leading order, fixing the length at zero costs no entropy. The quadratic drop is the Gaussian shape of the binomial distribution, $\Gamma(L) \approx 2^N e^{-L^2/2Na^2}$ up to a slowly varying factor.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Find the temperature $T$ of the chain when its length is $L$.

<Hint>

Both $S$ and $E$ depend on $L$ only. Use the chain rule: $\partial S/\partial E = (dS/dL)/(dE/dL)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-t" label="$T =$" expr="N*m*g*a^2/(kB*L)"
  vars={{ N: [50, 200], m: [0.5, 2], g: [5, 15], a: [0.5, 1], kB: [0.5, 2], L: [1, 5] }}
  names={{ N: "N", m: "m", g: "g", a: "a", kB: "k_B", L: "L" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-N*m*g*a^2/(kB*L)", hint: "The sign is off. Both dS/dL and dE/dL are negative for L > 0, so their ratio, 1/T, is positive." },
    { expr: "N*m*g*a^2/(2*kB*L)", hint: "That is half the answer, what the entropy drop L²/(Na²) gives. The drop is L²/(2Na²), whose derivative is L/(Na²)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The temperature of an isolated system is set by how its entropy changes with its energy. Here both change only through the length.

<Step title="The chain rule">

$dS/dL = -k_BL/(Na^2)$ and $dE/dL = -mg$, so

<Formula label="Temperature of the hanging chain" tex="\frac1T = \frac{dS/dL}{dE/dL} = \frac{k_BL}{Nmga^2}, \qquad T = \frac{Nmga^2}{k_BL}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: kg (m/s²) m²/(J/K · m) = K. A short chain is hot: at a high temperature the entropy wins over the weight, and the chain stays crumpled near $L = 0$. Because the ceiling does not block the chain, $L$ may also be negative (the weight above the hook); then $T < 0$, the negative temperature of a system whose number of states falls as its energy rises, as for the spins of the [statistical ensembles supplement](/courses/8-08/counting-states-spins-ideal-gas).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

Find the length of the chain in terms of $T$. Show that, for a fixed $T$ and a small force $mg$, the length is proportional to the force.

<Hint>

Solve part (c) for $L$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-l" label="$L =$ (to the order of part (b))" expr="N*m*g*a^2/(kB*T)"
  vars={{ N: [50, 200], m: [0.5, 2], g: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 1], kB: [0.5, 2], T: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ N: "N", m: "m", g: "g", a: "a", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "N*a*tanh(m*g*a/(kB*T))", hint: "That is the exact result without the small-extension approximation, the subject of part (e). This box asks for the form that follows from part (b): expand the tanh for a small argument." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Invert the temperature. The chain then behaves as a spring whose stiffness is set by the temperature.

<Step title="Solve for the length">

<Formula label="Length of the chain at temperature T" tex="L = \frac{Na^2}{k_BT}\,mg" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

At fixed $T$ this is proportional to the force $mg$: Hooke's law, with a spring constant $k_BT/(Na^2)$ that grows with the temperature.

</Step>

<Step title="When it holds">

The result came from the small-extension entropy of part (b), so it needs $L \ll Na$, which is $mga \ll k_BT$: the work the force does on one link is small compared with the thermal energy. That is what MIT's "small force" means here.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: m² N/J = m. A hotter chain is shorter for the same weight, because the entropy, which favours $L = 0$, weighs more against the energy.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={4}>

(Our addition.) Keep the full entropy of part (b), before its expansion, and find the exact length. For $N = 100$ and $mga = 0.5\,k_BT$, what is $L/a$? Give it to two decimals.

<Hint>

Differentiate the bracket of the full entropy with respect to $x$. You should get a logarithm of $(1 + x)/(1 - x)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\frac12\ln\frac{1 + x}{1 - x}$ is the inverse of the hyperbolic tangent.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-exact" label="$L/a =$" value="46.21" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-exact" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same chain rule as part (c), with the entropy kept to all orders in $x$.

<Step title="The exact temperature">

From the full entropy, $dS/dx = -\frac{Nk_B}{2}\ln\frac{1 + x}{1 - x} = -Nk_B\tanh^{-1}x$, and $dL = Na\,dx$, so $dS/dL = -(k_B/a)\tanh^{-1}x$. With $dE/dL = -mg$:

<Formula label="Exact length of the hanging chain" tex="\frac1T = \frac{k_B}{mga}\tanh^{-1}\frac{L}{Na}, \qquad L = Na\tanh\frac{mga}{k_BT}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(e), our addition (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$L/a = 100\tanh 0.5 = 100 \times 0.46212 = 46.21$. The linear law of part (d) would give $50$, 8% too long.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $mga \ll k_BT$, the tanh is close to its small argument, which gives part (d). For $mga \gg k_BT$, $L \to Na$: the chain hangs straight, and no force can make it longer. This is the same tanh as for the magnetic moment of spins in a field ([statistical ensembles supplement](/courses/8-08/counting-states-spins-ideal-gas), Curie's law).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The chain without a weight in a heat bath" points={25} mit="Problem 3">

MIT's sheet points to the same textbook problem, now with $m = 0$, and asks its own four questions, (a) to (d). We use the chain of Problem 2 without the weight. Its links have no energy of any kind, so every state of the chain has the energy zero. The chain is in contact with a heat bath at temperature $T$, and its ends are held so that its length is $L$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the partition function $Q(L)$ of the chain at length $L$.

<Hint>

$Q$ is the sum of $e^{-E_n/k_BT}$ over the states the chain can be in. Which states are those at a fixed length, and what is $E_n$ for each?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-q" label="$Q(L)$ equals" choices={["2^N, the number of all states", "1, since every state has the energy zero", "Γ(L)/2^N, the probability of the length L", "Γ(L), the number of states of length L"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At fixed length the sum runs only over the states with that length, and each contributes $e^0 = 1$.

<Step title="The sum">

<Formula label="Partition function of the chain at fixed length" tex="Q(L) = \sum_{n:\,\text{length } L} e^{-E_n/k_BT} = \sum_{n:\,\text{length } L} 1 = \Gamma(L) = \binom{N}{N_u}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

with $N_d$ and $N_u$ as in Problem 2(a). It does not depend on $T$: with every $E_n = 0$, the Boltzmann factors are all 1.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

If the length were free as well, the sum would run over all states and give $2^N$; $\Gamma(L)/2^N$ is then the probability of the length $L$ in the bath.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find the free energy $A(T, L)$ of the chain, for large $N$ and $L \ll Na$, to order $L^2$.

<Hint>

$A = -k_BT\ln Q$, and you found $\ln\Gamma(L)$ for these conditions in Problem 2(b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$A =$" expr="-kB*T*(N*log(2) - L^2/(2*N*a^2))"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 2], T: [1, 3], N: [50, 200], L: [1, 5], a: [0.5, 1] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T", N: "N", L: "L", a: "a" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "kB*T*(N*log(2) - L^2/(2*N*a^2))", hint: "The sign is off: A = −k_BT ln Q. With ln Q positive, the free energy is negative." },
    { expr: "-kB*T*N*log(2)", hint: "That is −k_BT ln 2^N, the free energy when the length is free. At fixed length Q is Γ(L), which falls as L grows, so A must depend on L." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With no energy, the free energy $A = E - ST$ is $-ST$: all of it is entropy.

<Step title="The free energy">

<Formula label="Free energy of the chain" tex="A(T, L) = -k_BT\ln\Gamma(L) \approx -Nk_BT\ln 2 + \frac{k_BT\,L^2}{2Na^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$A = -TS$ with the entropy of Problem 2(b), as it must be for $E = 0$. It rises with $L$: stretching the chain costs free energy, though not energy, as in Wen's chain (Chapter I, pp. 5–6).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={7}>

The tension of the chain is $\tau = \partial A/\partial L$, as MIT defines it. Find $\tau$, and show that for a small tension the length is proportional to $\tau$. Then (our addition) take a polymer of $N = 1000$ segments of length $a = 0.5$ nm at $T = 300$ K, held at $L = 50$ nm: what is the tension, in piconewtons?

<Formula label="Tension of the chain" tex="\tau = \frac{\partial A}{\partial L}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c), p. 1" />

<Hint>

Differentiate the result of part (b). Only the $L^2$ term depends on $L$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the numbers, $k_BT$ at 300 K is about $4.14 \times 10^{-21}$ J. Keep every length in metres.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-tau" label="$\tau =$" expr="kB*T*L/(N*a^2)"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 2], T: [1, 3], N: [50, 200], L: [1, 5], a: [0.5, 1] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T", N: "N", L: "L", a: "a" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-kB*T*L/(N*a^2)", hint: "The sign is off. A rises with L, so ∂A/∂L is positive: you must pull to hold the chain stretched." },
    { expr: "kB*T*L/(2*N*a^2)", hint: "That is half the answer. The derivative of L²/2 is L: the 2 cancels." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-num" label="$\tau$ in pN:" value="0.828" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The tension is the slope of the free energy. Since the free energy is all entropy, the force is an entropic force, as in a rubber band.

<Step title="The tension">

<Formula label="Tension of the chain at small extension" tex="\tau = \frac{k_BT}{Na^2}\,L, \qquad L = \frac{Na^2}{k_BT}\,\tau" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

So $L \propto \tau$ for small $\tau$, with the same spring constant $k_BT/(Na^2)$ as Problem 2(d), where $mg$ played the part of $\tau$. The exact slope of the free energy is $\tau = (k_BT/a)\tanh^{-1}(L/Na)$, which is linear while $L \ll Na$, that is while $\tau a \ll k_BT$.

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$k_BT = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 300$ J $= 4.142 \times 10^{-21}$ J and $Na^2 = 1000 \times (0.5 \times 10^{-9})^2$ m² $= 2.5 \times 10^{-16}$ m², so $\tau = 4.142 \times 10^{-21} \times 50 \times 10^{-9}/(2.5 \times 10^{-16})$ N $= 8.28 \times 10^{-13}$ N $= 0.828$ pN.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The extension is $L/Na = 0.1$, small enough for the linear law (the exact tanh law differs by 0.3%). Forces of a fraction of a piconewton are what optical tweezers measure on single long molecules, where such entropic springs are seen.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={7}>

When we pull the chain we do work on it, yet its internal energy is always $U = 0$. Where does the energy go? Is energy still conserved? Then (our addition): how much heat goes into the bath when the polymer of part (c) is pulled slowly from $L = 0$ to $L = 50$ nm, in units of $k_BT$?

<Hint>

The chain is in a heat bath. As it is stretched its entropy falls. What must happen to the bath's entropy, and what does that take?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the number, integrate $\tau\,dL$ from $0$ to $L$ with the tension of part (c).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-where" label="The work done on the chain" choices={["flows into the heat bath as heat", "is stored in the links as potential energy", "is lost: an entropic force does not conserve energy", "speeds up the links"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p3-heat" label="Heat to the bath in units of $k_BT$:" value="5.00" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-heat" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The first law holds for the chain as for anything else. With $U$ fixed at zero, every joule of work must leave as heat, and the bath takes it.

<Step title="Energy conservation">

For a slow stretch at fixed $T$, $dU = \delta W - \delta Q_{\text{out}}$ with $\delta W = \tau\,dL$ the work done on the chain and $\delta Q_{\text{out}}$ the heat it gives to the bath. Since $U = 0$ always,

<Formula label="Work and heat for the chain" tex="\delta Q_{\text{out}} = \tau\,dL = dA = -T\,dS" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(d), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The work goes into the bath as heat. The chain's entropy falls by $-dS$, the bath's rises by $\delta Q_{\text{out}}/T = -dS$: total entropy is unchanged in a slow stretch, and energy is conserved. Wen makes the same argument for the fully stretched chain (Chapter I, p. 6). If the chain is allowed to shrink back slowly, doing work on whatever holds its end, it takes the same heat back from the bath. (A real rubber band that contracts quickly, with no time to take heat in, cools instead.)

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$W = \int_0^L\tau\,dL' = \frac{k_BT\,L^2}{2Na^2}$, so $W/k_BT = (50 \times 10^{-9})^2/(2 \times 2.5 \times 10^{-16}) = 2.5 \times 10^{-15}/(5 \times 10^{-16}) = 5.00$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

It equals $T$ times the entropy drop of Problem 2(b), $k_B L^2/(2Na^2)$, in units of $k_BT$: $5.00$. It is small compared with $Nk_BT\ln 2 = 693\,k_BT$, the cost of pulling the chain all the way straight.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="An ideal gas in a gravitational field (our own problem)" points={25} mit="Problem 4">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the canonical ensemble and the ideal gas of Chapter I.

$N$ identical molecules of mass $m$ fill a very tall vertical cylinder of cross-section area $\sigma$, in contact with a heat bath at temperature $T$. Gravity $g$ points down, and a molecule at height $z \ge 0$ above the floor has the potential energy $mgz$. Take the cylinder as infinitely tall, treat the gas as classical and ideal, and ignore the molecules' internal motions. As in Chapter I, the partition function of $N$ identical, non-interacting molecules is

<Formula label="Partition function of N identical molecules" tex="Q = \frac{Q_1^N}{N!}, \qquad Q_1 = \int\frac{d^3p\,d^3r}{h^3}\,e^{-\beta\varepsilon(\vec p, \vec r)}, \qquad \int\frac{dp}{h}\,e^{-\beta p^2/2m} = \frac{1}{\lambda}" cite="reading1" at="p. 11, point ⑦ (written here for one molecule with the energy ε)" />

where $\varepsilon$ is the energy of one molecule and $\lambda = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT}$ the thermal wavelength.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Find the one-molecule partition function $Q_1$, in terms of $\sigma$, $\lambda$, $k_B$, $T$, $m$ and $g$.

<Hint>

The energy is $\vec p^{\,2}/2m + mgz$. The integral splits into three momentum integrals, two integrals across the cylinder and one over the height.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The integral of $e^{-\beta mgz}$ from $z = 0$ to infinity is a length. Which one?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-q1" label="$Q_1 =$" expr="sigma*kB*T/(m*g*lam^3)"
  vars={{ sigma: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 2], T: [1, 3], m: [0.5, 2], g: [0.5, 2], lam: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ sigma: "\\sigma", kB: "k_B", T: "T", m: "m", g: "g", lam: "\\lambda" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sigma*m*g/(kB*T*lam^3)", hint: "The height integral is upside down: the integral of e^(−mgz/k_BT) from 0 to infinity is k_BT/(mg), a length that grows with the temperature." },
    { expr: "sigma*kB*T/(m*g*lam^2)", hint: "One momentum integral is missing. Each of the three momentum components gives a factor 1/λ, including the vertical one." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The energy is a sum of terms in separate variables, so the integral is a product of one-dimensional integrals.

<Step title="The integrals">

The three momentum integrals give $1/\lambda^3$, the two across the cylinder give $\sigma$, and the height gives

<Formula label="The height integral" tex="\int_0^\infty dz\,e^{-mgz/k_BT} = \frac{k_BT}{mg} \equiv \ell" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

so

<Formula label="One-molecule partition function in a gravitational field" tex="Q_1 = \frac{\sigma\,\ell}{\lambda^3} = \frac{\sigma\,k_BT}{mg\,\lambda^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Compare a box of volume $V$, where $Q_1 = V/\lambda^3$ (Chapter I, p. 11): the tall cylinder acts as a box of height $\ell$. $Q_1$ is a pure number, as it must be: $\sigma\ell$ is a volume.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find the mean energy per molecule, $\bar E/N$, and from it the heat capacity of the gas.

<Hint>

Write $\ln Q$ with every factor of $\beta$ shown: $\lambda^3 \propto \beta^{3/2}$ and $\ell \propto 1/\beta$. Only these matter for $-\partial\ln Q/\partial\beta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-e" label="$\bar E/N =$" expr="5/2*kB*T"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 2], T: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3/2*kB*T", hint: "That is the kinetic energy alone, the result for a gas in a box. The potential energy mgz adds its own average." },
    { expr: "2*kB*T", hint: "That treats the potential energy as a quadratic term worth k_BT/2. The energy mgz is linear in z, and over an infinite column its mean is k_BT." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each power of $1/\beta$ in $Q_1$ adds $k_BT$ per molecule to the mean energy, through the logarithm.

<Step title="Differentiate">

$Q_1 \propto \beta^{-3/2}\cdot\beta^{-1} = \beta^{-5/2}$, so $\ln Q = N\ln Q_1 - \ln N! = -\frac52N\ln\beta + (\text{terms without }\beta)$ and

<Formula label="Mean energy of the gas in a gravitational field" tex="\bar E = -\frac{\partial\ln Q}{\partial\beta} = \frac52\,\frac{N}{\beta} = \frac52Nk_BT, \qquad C = \frac{d\bar E}{dT} = \frac52Nk_B" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Split it: $\frac32k_BT$ is the kinetic energy, by equipartition, and the rest, $k_BT = mg\langle z\rangle$, is the mean potential energy, as part (c) confirms. The gravitational field raises the heat capacity from $\frac32Nk_B$ to $\frac52Nk_B$: heating the gas also lifts it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Find the mean height $\langle z\rangle$ of a molecule. Then (our numbers) evaluate it for nitrogen molecules, $m = 28.014$ u, at $T = 300$ K with $g = 9.81$ m/s², in kilometres. (Earth's atmosphere is neither isothermal nor in a uniform field, so this is only the model.)

<Hint>

The probability of a height between $z$ and $z + dz$ is proportional to the Boltzmann factor $e^{-mgz/k_BT}$. Normalise it and take the mean.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The unified atomic mass unit is $1.66054 \times 10^{-27}$ kg.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-z" label="$\langle z\rangle$ in km:" value="9.08" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-z" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The heights follow an exponential distribution with the scale $\ell$ of part (a).

<Step title="The mean">

<Formula label="Mean height of a molecule" tex="\langle z\rangle = \frac{\int_0^\infty z\,e^{-z/\ell}\,dz}{\int_0^\infty e^{-z/\ell}\,dz} = \ell = \frac{k_BT}{mg}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$m = 28.014 \times 1.66054 \times 10^{-27}$ kg $= 4.6518 \times 10^{-26}$ kg, so $\langle z\rangle = 4.1419 \times 10^{-21}/(4.6518 \times 10^{-26} \times 9.81)$ m $= 9.08 \times 10^3$ m $= 9.08$ km.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$mg\langle z\rangle = k_BT$, the mean potential energy found in part (b). Lighter molecules spread higher: for helium (4.003 u) the same formula gives about 64 km.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={6}>

Show that the pressure on the floor equals the weight of the gas divided by the area, $Nmg/\sigma$.

<Hint>

Find the number density $n(z)$ from the distribution of heights of part (c), with $N$ molecules in all. Near the floor the gas is an ideal gas, so its pressure is $n(0)k_BT$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The density falls exponentially with height. The pressure at the floor must hold up all the gas above it.

<Step title="The density">

Normalising $N$ molecules over the column, $n(z) = \frac{N}{\sigma\ell}\,e^{-z/\ell}$: the barometric formula.

</Step>

<Step title="The pressure">

<Formula label="Pressure on the floor" tex="P(0) = n(0)\,k_BT = \frac{N}{\sigma\ell}\,k_BT = \frac{N}{\sigma}\,\frac{k_BT}{k_BT/mg} = \frac{Nmg}{\sigma}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is the weight of the gas, $Nmg$, spread over the floor.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A second route: a thin layer of gas between $z$ and $z + dz$ is at rest, so the pressure below it exceeds the pressure above it by its weight per area, $dP/dz = -mg\,n(z)$. With $n = P/k_BT$ this gives $P \propto e^{-z/\ell}$, and integrating $mg\,n(z)$ over the whole column gives $P(0) = Nmg/\sigma$ again. The result does not depend on $T$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
