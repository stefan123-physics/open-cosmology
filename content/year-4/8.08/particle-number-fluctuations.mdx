Statistical physics predicts averages, but a real system never sits exactly at its average: the number of atoms in a region, its volume, its energy all jitter about their mean values. Chapter VI of Wen's notes asks how big these **thermal fluctuations** are, and finds that their size is fixed by quantities we can measure in the laboratory, such as the compressibility. This page follows point ① (notes pp. 41–44): the fluctuating number of particles in a small volume, first by counting and then from the free energy, and what it means near the liquid–gas critical point. The [next page](/courses/8-08/volume-energy-fluctuations) treats the fluctuations of the volume and the energy, and the general link between fluctuation and response. (Citations use the handwritten page numbers 41 to 47 at the top right of the scanned notes, and Wen's circled point numbers. As in Wen's notes, $A$ is the Helmholtz free energy, called $F$ in 8.044. The text, the worked example and the plot are our own.)

## Counting atoms in a small region

Take a box of volume $V_0$ holding $N_0$ atoms of an ideal gas, and look at a smaller region of volume $V$ inside it (notes p. 41, point ①; Wen's sketch shows the small region in a corner, the rest of the box acting as a **particle reservoir**). The atoms move independently, so each one is in the small region with probability

<Formula label="Probability that one atom is in the region" tex="p = \frac{V}{V_0}" cite="reading1" at="p. 41, point ①" />

(Wen writes $P(1)$ for this probability and $P(0) = 1 - P(1)$ for the chance that the atom is elsewhere.) Give each atom a label $x_i$, equal to 1 if atom $i$ is in the region and 0 if not. The number of atoms in the region is then

<Formula label="The number of atoms in the region" tex="N = \sum_{i=1}^{N_0} x_i" cite="reading1" at="p. 41, point ①" />

Its average is a sum of $N_0$ equal terms, each $\langle x_i\rangle = p$. For the average of $N^2$, split the double sum into the terms with $i = j$ and those with $i \neq j$. Since $x_i^2 = x_i$, the first kind each give $p$; since different atoms are independent, the second kind each give $\langle x_i\rangle\langle x_j\rangle = p^2$, and there are $N_0^2 - N_0$ of them (notes p. 41):

<Formula label="The first two moments" tex="\langle N\rangle = N_0\,p, \qquad \langle N^2\rangle = \sum_i\langle x_i^2\rangle + \sum_{i\neq j}\langle x_ix_j\rangle = N_0\,p + (N_0^2 - N_0)\,p^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 41, point ①" />

(The notes write the first sum as $\sum_i\langle x_i\rangle^2$; it is the average of $x_i^2$, which is $p$, as the next line of the notes uses.) The second line is $\langle N\rangle + \langle N\rangle^2 - \langle N\rangle p$, so the spread of $N$ about its mean is

<Formula label="The spread of the number of atoms" tex="\Delta N = \sqrt{\langle(N - \langle N\rangle)^2\rangle} = \sqrt{\langle N^2\rangle - \langle N\rangle^2} = \sqrt{\langle N\rangle(1 - p)} \;\to\; \sqrt{\langle N\rangle} \quad\text{as } V/V_0 \to 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 41, point ①" />

This is the binomial distribution, which becomes the Poisson distribution when the region is a small part of the box. The relative fluctuation is the result to remember (notes p. 41, boxed):

<Formula label="The relative fluctuation" tex="\frac{\Delta N}{\langle N\rangle} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 41, point ①" />

with $N$ standing for the typical number $\langle N\rangle$. A region holding a hundred atoms fluctuates by about 10%; a cubic centimetre of air, with some $10^{19}$ molecules, by a few parts in $10^{10}$. This is why thermodynamics, which ignores fluctuations, works so well for macroscopic bodies.

## From thermodynamic functions to probabilities

Counting worked because the atoms were independent. For a generic system, with interactions, Wen turns the thermodynamic functions themselves into probabilities (notes p. 42). He lists four of them:

<Formula label="Thermodynamic functions as probabilities" tex="P \propto e^{S/k_B}, \qquad P \propto e^{-\beta A}, \qquad P \propto e^{-\beta\Omega}, \qquad P \propto e^{-\beta G}" cite="reading1" at="p. 42" />

with $\beta = 1/k_BT$. (The notes list the four without saying when each applies; the conditions that follow are our gloss.) For an isolated system, $e^{S/k_B}$ is the number of microscopic states, and every state is equally likely. At fixed temperature, $e^{-\beta A}$ is the total probability of all the states that make up a given macroscopic state (Wen's margin note "total prob."), since $A = -k_BT\ln Q$ sums the Boltzmann factors of those states. With a particle reservoir the grand potential $\Omega$ plays the same part, and at fixed pressure the Gibbs potential $G$. The most probable macroscopic state maximizes $S$, or minimizes $A$, $\Omega$ or $G$, and **the curvature at the minimum sets the size of the fluctuations**.

Wen first shows what goes wrong without a reservoir. The free energy of an ideal gas (from [Chapter I](/courses/8-08/information-equipartition-ideal-gas)) is

<Formula label="Free energy of an ideal gas" tex="A(T, V, N) = Nk_BT\left[\ln(n\lambda^3) - 1\right], \qquad n = \frac{N}{V}" cite="reading1" at="p. 42" />

with $\lambda$ the thermal wavelength. Taken alone, $e^{-\beta A}$ as a function of $N$ peaks where $\partial A/\partial N = k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3)$ vanishes, at $n\lambda^3 = 1$: a density that has nothing to do with the gas actually in the box. (The notes write $A$ as $Nk_BT\ln N$ plus a constant and put the peak at $N$ of order one half; the part of the "constant" that grows with $N$ moves the peak, but the point is the same.) The free energy of the small region alone is not the whole story, because atoms that enter it must leave the reservoir.

## Number fluctuations with a reservoir

With the reservoir included, adding an atom to the region costs the free energy $\mu$ it had in the reservoir, so the probability of finding $N$ atoms in the region goes as $e^{-\beta A_{\text{tot}}}$ with (notes p. 42)

<Formula label="Free energy of region and reservoir" tex="A_{\text{tot}}(T, V, N) = A - \mu N = \text{const} + \frac12\,\left.\frac{\partial^2A}{\partial N^2}\right|_{\bar N}(N - \bar N)^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 42" />

The most probable number $\bar N$ minimizes $A_{\text{tot}}$, which is the condition $\partial A/\partial N = \mu$; near it, $A_{\text{tot}}$ is a parabola, and the linear term $-\mu N$ does not change its curvature. The probability is therefore a Gaussian,

<Formula label="Number fluctuations from the free energy" tex="P(N) \propto e^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\frac{\partial^2A}{\partial N^2}(N - \bar N)^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle = \frac{k_BT}{\partial^2A/\partial N^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 42" />

since a Gaussian $e^{-(N - \bar N)^2/2\sigma^2}$ has variance $\sigma^2$. For the ideal gas, $\partial A/\partial N = k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3)$ (the notes' form, $k_BT\ln N + k_BT$, has the same second derivative) and

<Formula label="The ideal gas again" tex="\frac{\partial^2A}{\partial N^2} = \frac{k_BT}{N} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle = \bar N" cite="reading1" at="p. 42" />

the counting result of the first section, now found without counting. The new method works for any system whose free energy we know.

## The link to the compressibility

What is $\partial^2A/\partial N^2$ for a general fluid? It is the derivative $\partial\mu/\partial N$ at fixed $V$ and $T$, and Wen shows that it is set by how easily the fluid is squeezed (notes p. 43). The free energy is extensive, so it can be written as $N$ times a function of $T$ and the volume per particle $v$:

<Formula label="Free energy per particle" tex="A(V, T, N) = N\,a\!\left(T, \frac{V}{N}\right) = \frac{V}{v}\,a(T, v), \qquad v = \frac{V}{N}" cite="reading1" at="p. 43" />

Changing variables from $(V, T, N)$ to $(V, T, v)$, at fixed $V$ a change of $N$ is a change of $v$:

<Formula label="Changing variables" tex="\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial N}\right)_{V,T} = \left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial N}\right)_{V,T}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\right)_{V,T} = -\frac{V}{N^2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\right)_{V,T} = -\frac{v^2}{V}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\right)_{V,T}" cite="reading1" at="p. 43" />

The pressure is $P = -\partial A/\partial V$ at fixed $T$ and $N$, and since $A = N a(T, v)$ that is $-\partial a/\partial v$ at fixed $T$. The first derivative is then

<Formula label="The chemical potential" tex="\frac{\partial A}{\partial N} = -\frac{v^2}{V}\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\left[\frac{V}{v}\,a(v, T)\right]_{V,T} = a(v, T) - v\left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial v}\right)_T = a(v, T) + v\,P(v, T)" cite="reading1" at="p. 43" />

the free energy plus $Pv$ per particle, which is indeed the chemical potential. (The notes write $\partial a/\partial v = \partial A/\partial V = +P$ and so $a - vP$; the sign is minus, $P = -\partial A/\partial V$. With the notes' sign the next step does give a negative value, $\partial^2A/\partial N^2 = -v^2/(VK_T)$, impossible at a minimum, and the boxed result then drops that minus sign: two slips that cancel, so the box below is right.) Differentiating once more, the two terms in $P$ cancel and only the change of pressure with volume is left:

<Formula label="The second derivative" tex="\frac{\partial^2A}{\partial N^2} = -\frac{v^2}{V}\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\Big[a(v, T) + v\,P(v, T)\Big]_{V,T} = -\frac{v^3}{V}\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial v}\right)_T = \frac{v^2}{V}\,\frac{1}{K_T}" cite="reading1" at="p. 43" />

where $K_T$ is the **isothermal compressibility**, the fractional change of volume per unit pressure (notes p. 43, boxed):

<Formula label="Isothermal compressibility" tex="K_T = -\frac{1}{v}\left(\frac{\partial v}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T" cite="reading1" at="p. 43" />

(8.044 writes it $\kappa_T$.) Putting this into the variance gives the main result of the section:

<Formula label="Number fluctuations and the compressibility" tex="\langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle = k_BT\,\frac{V K_T}{v^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 43" />

For an ideal gas $K_T = 1/P$ and $k_BTV/(Pv^2) = N$, the result we had. Wen's comment is the lesson of the whole chapter: the left side is a **fluctuation**, something the system does on its own at equilibrium; $K_T$ is a **response**, how the system reacts when we push on it. The two are closely related, because both are set by the same curvature of the free energy.

## Large fluctuations near the critical point

Divide by $N$ to get the relative fluctuation of the density $n = N/V$ in a region of volume $V$ (notes p. 44):

<Formula label="Relative density fluctuation" tex="\frac{\delta n}{n} = \frac{\sqrt{\langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle}}{N} = \sqrt{k_BT\,\frac{K_T}{v}}\;\frac{1}{\sqrt N} = \sqrt{k_BTK_T}\;\frac{1}{\sqrt V} = \sqrt{\frac{V_c}{V}}, \qquad V_c = k_BT\,K_T" cite="reading1" at="p. 44" />

$V_c$ is a volume (energy times inverse pressure) that measures how soft the fluid is. (Wen's $V_c$ is this fluctuation volume, not the critical volume of the van der Waals gas.) Two conclusions follow (notes p. 44). First, the density in a smaller volume fluctuates more, and the fluctuations become of order one when $V$ shrinks to about $V_c$. Second, $V_c$ depends strongly on where the fluid sits in its phase diagram. For ordinary water it is about an ångström cubed, so the density is steady on any scale larger than a molecule. But at the **critical point**, where liquid and gas become one (Wen sketches the $P$–$T$ diagram and the isotherms of the [van der Waals gas](/courses/8-08/van-der-waals-phase-transitions)), the critical isotherm is flat, $\partial P/\partial V = 0$, so $K_T$ and $V_c$ grow without limit. When $V_c$ reaches roughly $(5000\ \text{Å})^3$, the cube of a wavelength of visible light, regions of that size differ in density, scatter light strongly, and the clear fluid turns milky. This is **critical opalescence**.

The van der Waals gas shows how fast the fluctuations grow. Along the critical density, $v = v_c = 3b/N$, its reduced equation of state $P/P_c = 8(T/T_c)/(3v/v_c - 1) - 3(v_c/v)^2$ gives $\partial(P/P_c)/\partial(v/v_c) = -6(T/T_c - 1)$, and with $k_BT_c = \frac83 P_cv_c$ the variance per particle is $\langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle/\bar N = k_BTK_T/v = \frac49\,(T/T_c)/(T/T_c - 1)$ (this application is ours; the notes only sketch the isotherms). Far above $T_c$ it tends to $\frac49$, below the ideal gas's 1, because at this density the hard cores make the gas stiffer; close to $T_c$ it grows as $1/(T - T_c)$, the mean-field behaviour that the susceptibility of the [Ginzburg–Landau theory](/courses/8-08/ginzburg-landau-theory) also showed.

<WorkedExample title="Water, air and a 10 nm box">
<Problem>
Water at 25 °C has an isothermal compressibility of about $4.52 \times 10^{-10}$ Pa⁻¹ and a number density of $3.33 \times 10^{28}$ molecules per m³. Find Wen's fluctuation volume $V_c$ for water, compare its density fluctuations with those of an ideal gas at the same density, and find the relative density fluctuation in a cube of water 10 nm on a side. Do the same for air at 25 °C and atmospheric pressure (101 325 Pa). How large a cube of water fluctuates in density by 1%?
</Problem>
<Step title="The fluctuation volume of water">
$k_BT = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 298.15 \approx 4.116 \times 10^{-21}$ J, so $V_c = k_BTK_T \approx 4.116 \times 10^{-21} \times 4.52 \times 10^{-10} \approx 1.86 \times 10^{-30}$ m³, or 1.86 Å³: Wen's "about an ångström cubed".
</Step>
<Step title="Compared with an ideal gas">
The variance per molecule is $\langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle/\bar N = nV_c \approx 3.33 \times 10^{28} \times 1.86 \times 10^{-30} \approx 0.062$. Water's number fluctuations are only about 6% of those of an ideal gas at the same density: the liquid is stiff, and the molecules cannot crowd together or spread apart freely.
</Step>
<Step title="The 10 nm cube of water">
$V = (10^{-8}\ \text{m})^3 = 10^{-24}$ m³, so $\delta n/n = \sqrt{1.86 \times 10^{-30}/10^{-24}} \approx 1.36 \times 10^{-3}$, about 0.14%, although the cube holds some 33 000 molecules.
</Step>
<Step title="The same cube of air">
For an ideal gas $K_T = 1/P$, so $V_c = k_BT/P \approx 4.116 \times 10^{-21}/101\,325 \approx 4.06 \times 10^{-26}$ m³, about 40.6 nm³, the volume per molecule. The cube holds only $10^{-24}/4.06 \times 10^{-26} \approx 24.6$ molecules on average, and $\delta n/n = \sqrt{4.06 \times 10^{-26}/10^{-24}} \approx 0.20$: a 20% fluctuation, the same as $1/\sqrt{24.6}$.
</Step>
<Step title="A 1% fluctuation in water">
$\delta n/n = 0.01$ needs $V = V_c/10^{-4} \approx 1.86 \times 10^{-26}$ m³, a cube of side about 2.65 nm, holding some 620 molecules. An ideal gas with that many molecules would fluctuate by $1/\sqrt{620} \approx 0.04$, or 4%.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Counting galaxies in cells">
Cosmologists count galaxies the way the first section counted atoms: divide a galaxy survey into cells and record how many galaxies each holds. If galaxies were scattered at random and independently, like the atoms of an ideal gas, the counts would fluctuate with variance equal to the mean, $\Delta N = \sqrt{\langle N\rangle}$, the Poisson spread derived above. Real galaxies cluster, and their counts fluctuate more. The [MIT 8.902 page on the power spectrum](/courses/8-902/power-spectrum) (Lectures 14 and 15) measures that clustering with the two-point correlation function $\xi(r)$, positive when pairs at separation $r$ are more common than chance; by its definition it is zero for a random (Poisson) scattering. (Relating the extra variance of the counts to an integral of $\xi$ over the cell is a standard step that the page adds; it plays the part that the compressibility plays here, an excess over the ideal gas's $\Delta N^2 = \langle N\rangle$.)
</Callout>

## Summary

- The number of independent atoms in a region that is a fraction $p$ of the box is a binomial count: $\langle N\rangle = N_0p$, $\Delta N = \sqrt{\langle N\rangle(1 - p)} \to \sqrt{\langle N\rangle}$, so $\Delta N/\langle N\rangle = 1/\sqrt N$.
- Thermodynamic functions are probabilities: $P \propto e^{S/k_B}$, $e^{-\beta A}$, $e^{-\beta\Omega}$ or $e^{-\beta G}$, depending on what the system exchanges with its surroundings. The most probable state maximizes $S$ or minimizes $A$, $\Omega$, $G$; the curvature there sets the fluctuations.
- With a particle reservoir, $A_{\text{tot}} = A - \mu N$ and $\langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle = k_BT/(\partial^2A/\partial N^2)$; for the ideal gas this is $\bar N$.
- Writing $A = N a(T, v)$ gives $\partial^2A/\partial N^2 = v^2/(VK_T)$, so $\langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle = k_BTVK_T/v^2$: the fluctuation is fixed by the response, the compressibility $K_T$.
- The density fluctuates by $\delta n/n = \sqrt{V_c/V}$ with $V_c = k_BTK_T$: about 1 Å³ for water, but unbounded at the critical point, where fluctuations the size of a light wavelength cause critical opalescence.
