The [previous page](/courses/8-08/vacuum-casimir-interacting-bosons) wrote interacting bosons as a second-quantized Hamiltonian, a set of anharmonic oscillators whose eigenstates nobody can find in general. This page covers the rest of Wen's chapter, sections 4.9 to 4.14 (pp. 44–54). His strategy is to go back to a classical field, find its ground state and its small vibrations, and only then quantize those vibrations. On the way he meets a quantum phase transition, Landau's link between continuous transitions and broken symmetry, sound waves in a superfluid, and the phonons that carry them. Wen sets $\hbar = 1$; we restore it in numbers.

## A classical field for interacting bosons

What classical field theory belongs to the interacting Hamiltonian? Wen first identifies the density (section 4.9, p. 44). Raising the potential everywhere by $\delta U$ raises the energy of $N$ bosons by $N\delta U$; in the free field theory of the previous page it raises $E_{\text{tot}}$ by $\int d^dx\,\phi^*\phi\,\delta U$. So the number of bosons is $\int d^dx\,|\phi|^2$, and the density is $n(x) = \phi^*(x)\phi(x)$. Since the pair energy is a double integral over the density, the natural guess for the energy and the Lagrangian of interacting bosons is

<Formula label="Energy of the interacting classical field" tex="E_{\text{tot}} = \int d^dx\,\phi^*\left(-\frac{\partial_x^2}{2m} + U\right)\phi + \int d^dx\,d^dx'\,|\phi(x)|^2\,\frac{V(x - x')}{2}\,|\phi(x')|^2, \qquad L = \int d^dx\,i\phi^*\dot\phi - E_{\text{tot}}" cite="reading1" at="§4.9, p. 44, eqns (4.9.2) and (4.9.3)" />

Varying $\phi^*$ gives the equation of motion

<Formula label="Nonlinear field equation" tex="i\dot\phi(x, t) = \left(-\frac{1}{2m}\partial_x^2 + U_{\text{eff}}(x)\right)\phi(x, t), \qquad U_{\text{eff}}(x) = U + \int d^dx'\,V(x - x')|\phi(x')|^2" cite="reading1" at="§4.9, p. 44, eqn (4.9.4)" />

Each boson feels the potential $U$ plus the potential made by all the others. The equation is **nonlinear**: interacting bosons are an anharmonic field theory. Wen's problems (p. 45) ask the reader to fill in the reasoning: the Lagrangian is unchanged by $\phi \to e^{i\theta}\phi$, and this U(1) symmetry makes $\int d^dx\,|\phi|^2$, the number of bosons, constant in time; adding a term proportional to $\int(\phi + \phi^*)$ would break both. He also notes that the "guess" can be derived: take the operator equation of motion $\partial_t\hat\phi = i[\hat H, \hat\phi]$ for the Hamiltonian (4.8.7) and replace the operators by their averages, $\langle\hat\phi\rangle = \phi$.

## A quantum phase transition

A classical state of this theory is a complex function $\phi(x)$, which holds both coordinates and momenta, so the classical ground state is the function that minimizes $E_{\text{tot}}$ (section 4.10, p. 45). The kinetic term $\int|\partial_x\phi|^2/2m$ is smallest for a constant $\phi$, and if the interaction is not too strong the minimum is a constant. The energy per volume is then

<Formula label="Energy density of a uniform field" tex="u = U|\phi|^2 + \frac12\bar V|\phi|^4, \qquad \bar V = \int d^dx\,V(x)" cite="reading1" at="§4.10, p. 45" />

If $\bar V < 0$ (attraction on balance), $u$ has no lower bound and there is no ground state at all. (The notes say the ground state of an attractive gas "is well defined", a slip for "is not well defined"; Wen's footnote asks what really happens to such a gas.) If $\bar V > 0$, minimizing over $|\phi|^2$ gives

<Formula label="Classical ground state and its energy" tex="\phi_{\text{grnd}} = \begin{cases} 0, & U > 0 \\ \sqrt{-U/\bar V}, & U < 0 \end{cases} \qquad u = \begin{cases} 0, & U > 0 \\ -\frac12U^2/\bar V, & U < 0 \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="§4.10, p. 45, eqns (4.10.1) and (4.10.2)" />

  curves={[

  caption="The energy density u = U|φ|² + ½V̄|φ|⁴ along the real axis of φ, computed with V̄ = 1. For U > 0 the only minimum is φ = 0. The ground state is the state at zero temperature, so a singularity in the ground-state energy as a parameter changes is a **quantum phase transition**, one driven by a parameter, not by temperature (p. 46). Here $u$ and $\partial u/\partial U$ are both continuous at $U = 0$, while $\partial^2u/\partial U^2$ jumps from $0$ to $-1/\bar V$: a second-order transition. For $U > 0$ adding a boson costs energy, so the ground state has none. For $U < 0$ each boson lowers the energy by $|U|$, but crowding them costs interaction energy, and the balance is the density $n = -U/\bar V$. The phase with $\phi \neq 0$ is the **boson-condensed** phase, which Wen later identifies as a superfluid. (The classical field only approximates the quantum ground state. According to Wen, a more careful study finds the same kind of singularity in two or more dimensions; in one dimension there is a singularity at $U = 0$ but of a different form.)

## Continuous transitions break a symmetry

When can the minimum of a smooth energy depend on its parameters in a singular way? Wen takes a toy energy function of one variable (section 4.11, pp. 46–48):

<Formula label="A toy energy function" tex="E_{abcd}(x) = ax + bx^2 + cx^3 + dx^4, \qquad d > 0" cite="reading1" at="§4.11, p. 46, eqn (4.11.1)" />

He finds two ways. In **minimum switching** (Fig. 4.9, with $b = -1$, $c = 0$, $d = 1$), the function has two local minima, and as $a$ passes zero the lower one becomes the higher one. The global minimum jumps from one to the other, its slope $\partial E_0/\partial a$ jumps, and the transition is **first order**. In **minimum splitting** (Fig. 4.10, $a = c = 0$, $d = 1$), the function $bx^2 + x^4$ is unchanged by $x \to -x$. For $b > 0$ its one minimum sits at the symmetric point $x = 0$; as $b$ goes below zero it splits into two, at $\pm\sqrt{-b/2}$, which move apart continuously (Fig. 4.11). This gives a **continuous** transition, and it needs the symmetry: even a small term $ax$ turns it into a smooth crossover or a first-order jump.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-1.3, 1.3]} range={[-0.6, 0.8]} xLabel="x" yLabel="E(x)" samples={300}
  curves={[
    { expr: "a*x + b*x^2 + x^4", label: "E(x) = ax + bx² + x⁴" }

  caption="Wen's two mechanisms, computed from E(x) = ax + bx² + x⁴. With b = −1, move a through zero: the deeper of the two minima changes side abruptly (minimum switching, first order). In the split phase the energy function still has the $x \to -x$ symmetry, but the ground state does not: the symmetry turns one ground state into the other. A ground state with less symmetry than its energy is **spontaneous symmetry breaking**, and a continuous transition of this kind is a symmetry-breaking transition. This is Landau's insight [Landau 1937]: one can describe phases by an **order parameter** that changes under the symmetry, such as $x$ (or $x^3$) here, zero in the symmetric phase and non-zero in the broken one. It is the same idea as the [Ginzburg–Landau theory of Chapter III](/courses/8-08/ginzburg-landau-theory), there with the magnetization. Wen adds that for a long time all continuous transitions were believed to be of this kind, and challenges the reader: a third mechanism would be a new kind of transition beyond Landau's theory.

The boson field fits the pattern (p. 48). Its energy has a U(1) symmetry, $\phi \to e^{i\theta}\phi$, and a translation symmetry, $x \to x + a$. The state $\phi = 0$ keeps both. The state $\phi = \sqrt{-U/\bar V}$ keeps translations but not U(1): a phase rotation turns it into $e^{i\theta}\sqrt{-U/\bar V}$, another of a whole circle of degenerate ground states (Fig. 4.12, the bottom of what is often called a Mexican-hat shape). Since the two phases have different symmetries, Landau's theory says they must be separated by a phase transition. His problems ask the reader to show what explicit breaking does: a term $h\int(\phi + \phi^*)$ in the energy turns the transition into a smooth crossover however small $h$ is, while $h_2\int(\phi^2 + \phi^{*2})$ leaves a $\phi \to -\phi$ symmetry that still allows a continuous transition. (Explicit breaking changes the energy function; spontaneous breaking changes only the ground state, a distinction Wen stresses in a footnote.)

## Sound waves

How do small disturbances of the ground state move (section 4.12, pp. 49–50)? Take a short-range interaction, $V(x - x') = g\,\delta(x - x')$, so $\bar V = g$ and the field equation and the Lagrangian become

<Formula label="Field equation with a contact interaction" tex="i\dot\phi = \left(-\frac{1}{2m}\partial_x^2 + U + g|\phi|^2\right)\phi, \qquad L = \int d^dx\left(i\phi^*\dot\phi - \phi^*\left(-\frac{\partial_x^2}{2m} + U\right)\phi - \frac g2|\phi|^4\right)" cite="reading1" at="§4.12, p. 49, eqns (4.12.1) and (4.12.3)" />

In the condensed phase, put $\phi = \sqrt{-U/g} + \delta\phi$ and keep only terms linear in $\delta\phi$. Since $g|\phi_{\text{grnd}}|^2 = -U$, the result is

<Formula label="Linearized equation in the condensed phase" tex="i\,\delta\dot\phi = \left(-\frac{1}{2m}\partial_x^2 - U\right)\delta\phi - U\,\delta\phi^*" cite="reading1" at="§4.12, p. 49" />

The term in $\delta\phi^*$ mixes the field with its conjugate. Splitting $\delta\phi = (\delta h + i\,\delta p)/\sqrt2$ into real parts gives $\delta\dot h = -\frac{1}{2m}\partial_x^2\delta p$ and $-\delta\dot p = (-\frac{1}{2m}\partial_x^2 - 2U)\delta h$, and so a wave equation for $\delta h$ alone:

<Formula label="Wave equation for small vibrations" tex="\delta\ddot h = \frac{1}{2m}\partial_x^2\left(-\frac{1}{2m}\partial_x^2 - 2U\right)\delta h" cite="reading1" at="§4.12, p. 50, eqn (4.12.4)" />

A plane wave $\delta h = \text{Re}(Ce^{ik\cdot x - i\omega_kt})$ solves it if

<Formula label="Dispersion of sound in the condensate" tex="\omega_k = \sqrt{\frac{|k|^2}{2m}\left(\frac{|k|^2}{2m} - 2U\right)} = \sqrt{\frac{|k|^2}{2m}\left(\frac{|k|^2}{2m} + 2gn_0\right)}, \qquad \omega_k \approx v|k|, \quad v = \sqrt{\frac{-U}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{gn_0}{m}}" cite="reading1" at="§4.12, p. 50, eqn (4.12.5)" />

with $n_0 = |\phi_{\text{grnd}}|^2$ the density. At small $k$ the frequency grows linearly with $|k|$: this is **sound** in the superfluid, with speed $v$. At large $k$ it approaches $k^2/2m + gn_0$, a free particle pushed up by the mean interaction. In the symmetric phase, where $\phi_{\text{grnd}} = 0$ and $U > 0$, the linear equation is just $i\,\delta\dot\phi = (-\frac{1}{2m}\partial_x^2 + U)\delta\phi$, with

<Formula label="Dispersion in the symmetric phase" tex="\omega_k = \frac{|k|^2}{2m} + U" cite="reading1" at="§4.12, p. 50, eqn (4.12.6)" />

which never goes below $U$: those excitations have an energy **gap**.

## Phonons: new particles from old

A wave is a collection of oscillators, one for every allowed wave vector $k = \frac{2\pi}{L}(n_1, n_2, \dots)$, with frequency $\omega_k$ (section 4.13, pp. 50–52). Quantizing them, as for the string, gives states labelled by whole numbers $\{n^{\text{ph}}_k\}$ with energy $\sum_kn^{\text{ph}}_k\omega_k$ above the ground state. So the low-lying states of the hopelessly complicated interacting system are those of **free** bosons with dispersion $\omega_k$. Wen calls them emergent bosons.

What are they? In the symmetric phase they are just the original bosons: the ground state has none, $n^{\text{ph}}_k$ counts them, and a few of them form a dilute gas whose interactions can be ignored; their dispersion $k^2/2m + U$ is the original one. In the condensed phase they are something else. Their dispersion is linear, the original bosons are dense and interact strongly, and the energy eigenstates do not even have definite numbers of original bosons in each level, though they do have definite $n^{\text{ph}}_k$. These new bosons are **phonons**, quanta of sound, and Wen presents them as the simplest example of entirely new particles emerging from collective motion.

Phonons are **gapless**: their energy goes to zero at long wavelength. Gapless excitations are rare, Wen remarks (p. 52), so they need a reason. Here the reason is the broken continuous symmetry. The energy is unchanged by $\phi \to e^{i\theta}\phi$, but the ground state is not, so there is a continuous circle of degenerate ground states. A slow variation of $\phi$ along that circle costs almost no energy (Fig. 4.13). The general statement is the theorem of Nambu and Goldstone [Nambu 1960; Goldstone 1961]: a phase that spontaneously breaks a continuous symmetry has gapless excitations, the **Nambu–Goldstone modes**. The superfluid phonon is one.

<WorkedExample title="Sound in an atomic condensate">
<Problem>
A condensate of rubidium-87 atoms (mass $m = 1.443 \times 10^{-25}$ kg) has mean interaction energy $gn_0 = k_B \times 100$ nK. Find (a) the speed of sound, (b) the wave number and wavelength where the dispersion crosses over from sound to particle-like, and (c) the phonon energy and frequency there.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Speed of sound">
$gn_0 = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K $\times 10^{-7}$ K $= 1.381 \times 10^{-30}$ J. Then $v = \sqrt{gn_0/m} = \sqrt{1.381 \times 10^{-30}/1.443 \times 10^{-25}}$ m/s $= 3.09 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s, about 3 mm/s, some hundred thousand times slower than sound in air.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The crossover">
With $\hbar$ restored, the kinetic energy $\hbar^2k^2/2m$ equals the interaction energy $gn_0$ when at $k = \sqrt{2mgn_0}/\hbar = 5.99 \times 10^6$ m⁻¹. The wavelength is $2\pi/k = 1.05$ μm: on longer scales the condensate carries sound, on shorter ones its excitations look like single atoms.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Energy at the crossover">
There $\hbar\omega = \sqrt{gn_0(gn_0 + 2gn_0)} = \sqrt3\,gn_0$, equal to $k_B \times 173$ nK, or $2.39 \times 10^{-30}$ J. Divided by Planck's constant this is a frequency of $3.61$ kHz.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Bogoliubov: the oscillators made explicit

Section 4.14 (pp. 52–54, marked by Wen as advanced) shows directly how the sound waves become independent oscillators. To make the U(1) symmetry visible, write the field near the condensate in terms of a density and a phase:

<Formula label="Density and phase variables" tex="\phi(x, t) = \sqrt{n_0 + \delta n(x, t)}\;e^{i\theta(x, t)}, \qquad n_0 = -U/g" cite="reading1" at="§4.14, p. 52" />

To second order in $\delta n$ and $\theta$, the Lagrangian (4.12.3) becomes

<Formula label="Lagrangian for sound waves" tex="L = \int d^dx\left[-(n_0 + \delta n)\,\partial_t\theta - \frac{n_0(\partial_x\theta)^2}{2m} - \frac{(\partial_x\delta n)^2}{8mn_0} - \frac g2\delta n^2\right]" cite="reading1" at="§4.14, p. 52, eqn (4.14.1)" />

Under a phase rotation $\phi \to e^{i\alpha}\phi$ the phase shifts, $\theta \to \theta + \alpha$, and the Lagrangian must not change, which is why it contains derivatives of $\theta$ but no $\theta^2$ term. That absence is what will make the excitations gapless. Now combine the two real variables into one complex one, equal to $\delta\phi$ to first order:

<Formula label="Complex fluctuation and its Lagrangian" tex="\delta\varphi = \frac{\delta n}{2\sqrt{n_0}} + i\sqrt{n_0}\,\theta, \qquad L = \int d^dx\left(i\,\delta\varphi^*\delta\dot\varphi - \delta\varphi^*\left(-\frac{\partial_x^2}{2m} + gn_0\right)\delta\varphi - gn_0\,\text{Re}(\delta\varphi^2)\right)" cite="reading1" at="§4.14, p. 52, eqn (4.14.2)" />

(dropping total time derivatives). The term $gn_0\text{Re}(\delta\varphi^2)$ has the same coefficient as $gn_0|\delta\varphi|^2$, again because of the U(1) symmetry: in $\theta$ they cancel, and in $\delta n$ they add up to $\frac g2\delta n^2$. In modes, $\delta\varphi(x) = \sum_k\mathcal{V}^{-1/2}e^{ik\cdot x}\delta\varphi_k$, this reads

<Formula label="Coupled modes" tex="L = \sum_k\left(i\,\delta\varphi_k^*\delta\dot\varphi_k - \delta\varphi_k^*\left(\frac{|k|^2}{2m} + gn_0\right)\delta\varphi_k - \frac{gn_0}{2}\left(\delta\varphi_{-k}\delta\varphi_k + \delta\varphi^*_{-k}\delta\varphi^*_k\right)\right)" cite="reading1" at="§4.14, p. 53, eqn (4.14.3)" />

(In the sentence after it the notes write the coupling as $\frac g2(\dots)$, dropping the $n_0$.) Without the last term each mode would be a separate oscillator; the last term couples $k$ to $-k$. The cure is the **Bogoliubov transformation**, a new variable that mixes each mode with the conjugate of its partner:

<Formula label="Bogoliubov transformation" tex="a_k = u_k\,\delta\varphi_k + v_k\,\delta\varphi^*_{-k}, \qquad |u_k|^2 - |v_k|^2 = 1" cite="reading1" at="§4.14, p. 53 (the definition is unnumbered; the condition is eqn 4.14.5)" />

(The notes write $v_k\delta\varphi_{-k}$ without the conjugate, a slip: only the conjugate makes the condition $|u_k|^2 - |v_k|^2 = 1$ keep the time-derivative term in the form $\sum_ka_k^*\dot a_k = \sum_k\delta\varphi^*_k\delta\dot\varphi_k$, up to a total derivative. The notes also state a second condition, $u_k^*v_k - u_{-k}v^*_{-k} = 0$, which holds for the real $u_k = u_{-k}$, $v_k = v_{-k}$ below.) With the choice

<Formula label="The coefficients and the oscillator energy" tex="u_k = \sqrt{\frac{\frac{|k|^2}{2m} + gn_0}{2E_k} + \frac12}, \qquad v_k = \sqrt{\frac{\frac{|k|^2}{2m} + gn_0}{2E_k} - \frac12}, \qquad E_k = \sqrt{\left(\frac{|k|^2}{2m} + gn_0\right)^2 - (gn_0)^2}" cite="reading1" at="§4.14, p. 53, eqns (4.14.7), with (4.14.6) the resulting energy" />

the energy terms of (4.14.3) become $\sum_kE_ka_k^*a_k$, and the Lagrangian is a sum of independent oscillators, $L = \sum_k(ia_k^*\dot a_k - E_ka_k^*a_k)$. Writing $a_k = (X_k + iP_k)/\sqrt2$ turns each term into $P_k\dot X_k - P_k^2/2M_k - K_kX_k^2/2$, an oscillator of mass $M_k = 1/E_k$ and spring constant $K_k = E_k$, with frequency $\omega_k = \sqrt{K_k/M_k} = E_k$. Multiplying out, $E_k^2 = \frac{|k|^2}{2m}\left(\frac{|k|^2}{2m} + 2gn_0\right)$: the same dispersion as eqn (4.12.5).

The formula for $E_k$ shows where the gap went (p. 54). The first $gn_0$ comes from the coefficient of $|\delta\varphi|^2$, the second from that of $\text{Re}(\delta\varphi^2)$. Because they are equal, $E_k \to 0$ as $k \to 0$. The U(1) symmetry protects the gapless phonon; Wen's last problem shows that breaking it down to $\phi \to -\phi$ opens a gap in both phases, a gap that closes only at the transition.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The same shape in the Higgs field">
The energy $u = U|\phi|^2 + \frac12\bar V|\phi|^4$ with $U < 0$ has the shape described for the [Higgs field](/courses/8-286/higgs-field) in 8.286 Lecture 22 (given by a substitute lecturer): an energy that depends only on the size of the field, with its minimum away from zero, so the field must settle on one of infinitely many equivalent values, spontaneously breaking a symmetry as it does so. That lecture also relates a particle's rest mass to the lowest frequency of its field's vibrations, $m = \hbar\omega_{\text{min}}/c^2$: the gap $U$ of the symmetric phase here is a mass in that sense, and the gapless phonon a massless excitation. (In the electroweak theory the would-be Goldstone modes are absorbed into the massive W and Z bosons, so no massless particle appears; that step is in neither Wen's chapter nor the 8.286 lecture.)

## Summary

- Interacting bosons have a classical field theory with energy $\int\phi^*(-\partial_x^2/2m + U)\phi + \frac12\int\int|\phi|^2V|\phi|^2$ and the nonlinear equation $i\dot\phi = (-\partial_x^2/2m + U_{\text{eff}})\phi$; the density is $|\phi|^2$.
- For a uniform field, $u = U|\phi|^2 + \frac12\bar V|\phi|^4$. With $\bar V > 0$ the ground state goes from $\phi = 0$ to $|\phi|^2 = -U/\bar V$ at $U = 0$, with $u = -U^2/2\bar V$: a second-order **quantum phase transition** into the boson-condensed (superfluid) phase.
- **Minimum switching** gives first-order transitions; **minimum splitting**, which needs a symmetry, gives continuous ones that break the symmetry spontaneously (Landau). The condensate breaks the U(1) symmetry $\phi \to e^{i\theta}\phi$; its ground states form a circle.
- Small vibrations of the condensate: $\omega_k = \sqrt{(k^2/2m)(k^2/2m + 2gn_0)}$, linear at small $k$ with sound speed $v = \sqrt{gn_0/m}$. In the symmetric phase $\omega_k = k^2/2m + U$, with a gap.
- Quantized, the vibrations are free emergent bosons. In the condensate they are **phonons**, quite unlike the original bosons, and **gapless** because a continuous symmetry is broken (Nambu–Goldstone).
- The **Bogoliubov transformation** $a_k = u_k\delta\varphi_k + v_k\delta\varphi^*_{-k}$ decouples $k$ and $-k$ and gives $E_k = \sqrt{(k^2/2m + gn_0)^2 - (gn_0)^2}$; the equal coefficients forced by U(1) make $E_k \to 0$ as $k \to 0$.
