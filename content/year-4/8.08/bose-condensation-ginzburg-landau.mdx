The [previous page](/courses/8-08/superfluidity-vortices) used the condensate field $\psi$ at zero temperature to explain superflow and vortices. Wen closes Chapter VII (notes pp. 73–75) by looking again at what that field means, and then writing a **Ginzburg–Landau theory** for it at finite temperature, in the same spirit as the one for the Ising magnet in Chapter III. It gives the heat capacity near the transition, which Wen compares with real helium-4 and with free bosons. The chapter ends by describing an ordinary solid in the same language: a crystal as a "condensate" of density waves. (Citations give the handwritten page numbers at the top right of the scan. The number 75 is used twice: "p. 75a" is the sixth page of the PDF, "p. 75b" the seventh. Wen writes the density as $n$; this page writes it $\rho$ and keeps $n$ for whole numbers.)

## The condensate as one wavefunction

In a Bose condensate all bosons occupy one single-particle state $\psi_0(x)$ (notes p. 73, remark). The state of $N$ bosons is then a product:

<Formula label="N-boson wavefunction of a condensate" tex="\Psi(x_1, \ldots, x_N) = \psi_0(x_1)\,\psi_0(x_2)\cdots\psi_0(x_N)" cite="reading1" at="p. 73" />

Wen compares the densities. For one particle in a box of length $L$, $|\psi_0|^2$ is a plateau of height $1/L$; Wen calls it $n_0$ (the value $1/L$, from normalization, is ours). In the $N$-boson state each particle adds the same plateau, so the density is $N$ times larger, $Nn_0$, with the same shape (notes p. 73). So the single-particle wavefunction of the condensate, multiplied by $N$, is the density of the fluid itself (our remark).

**Collective excitations.** The simplest way to excite the condensate is to change the single state that every boson occupies, $\psi_0 \to \psi_1$, for all of them at once (notes p. 73):

<Formula label="A collective excitation" tex="\Psi_1(x_1, \ldots, x_N) = \psi_1(x_1)\cdots\psi_1(x_N)" cite="reading1" at="p. 73" />

$\psi_1$ need not be orthogonal to $\psi_0$. With a uniform ground state, $\psi_0 = $ const, Wen lists two kinds (notes p. 73):

<Formula label="Two kinds of collective mode" tex="\text{density wave: } \psi_1 = \psi_0 + \alpha e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}; \qquad \text{vortex: } \psi_1 = f(r)\,e^{i\theta}\psi_0, \quad n\text{-vortex: } \psi_1 = f(r)\,e^{in\theta}\psi_0" cite="reading1" at="p. 73" />

with $(r, \theta)$ polar coordinates (the previous page wrote the angle with a different letter, as Wen does). The density wave makes the density ripple: with $\psi_0$ and $\alpha$ real, $|\psi_1|^2 = \psi_0^2 + 2\psi_0\alpha\cos(\vec k\cdot\vec x) + \alpha^2$. The vortex with $n = 1$ is the minimum vortex; the $n$-vortex carries $n$ quanta of circulation.

## The free energy of the condensate

The amplitude of the condensed bosons is the **order parameter** (notes p. 74). At zero temperature the free energy (here simply the energy) of interacting bosons in an external potential $U(x)$ is the functional of the previous page (written $\Omega$ there), with $U$ added:

<Formula label="Free energy at zero temperature" tex="A = \int d^3x\left[\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^2 + (U - \mu)|\psi|^2 + \frac{U_0}{2}|\psi|^4\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 74" />

At finite temperature not all bosons are condensed. Wen's picture contrasts the condensed bosons, piled into the lowest level, with the non-condensed ones spread over the excited levels with a chemical potential $\mu(T)$. Keep $\psi$ as the amplitude of the condensed bosons only, and let the rest contribute a smooth background $A_0(T)$. The coefficient of $|\psi|^2$ now depends on temperature (notes p. 74):

<Formula label="Ginzburg–Landau free energy of Bose condensation" tex="A = \int d^3x\left[\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^2 + a(T)|\psi|^2 + \frac{U_0}{2}|\psi|^4\right] + A_0(T)" cite="reading1" at="p. 74" />

- If $a(T) > 0$, the minimum is at $\psi = 0$: **no condensation**.
- If $a(T) < 0$, the minimum is at $\psi \neq 0$: **a finite condensate**.

The free energy has a **U(1) symmetry** (notes p. 74): multiplying $\psi$ by a phase does not change it,

<Formula label="U(1) symmetry" tex="\psi \to e^{i\theta}\psi, \qquad A \to A" cite="reading1" at="p. 74" />

so for $a < 0$ the minima form a circle in the complex $\psi$ plane, the bottom of the "Mexican hat" that Wen sketches. The condensate picks one point on the circle. (The physical origin of the symmetry, our remark: the number of bosons is conserved, and a phase change $e^{i\theta}$ is what that conservation law generates in quantum mechanics.)

## The condensate density, entropy and heat capacity

For a uniform condensate the gradient term vanishes, and we minimize $a|\psi|^2 + \frac{U_0}{2}|\psi|^4$ over $|\psi|^2$ (notes p. 75a):

<Formula label="Condition for the minimum" tex="a(T) + U_0|\psi|^2 = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 75a" />

so below the transition the condensate density is $|\psi|^2 = -a/U_0$. Putting it back,

<Formula label="Free energy of the condensed phase" tex="\frac{A}{V} = \frac{A_0}{V} + \begin{cases} 0, & a(T) > 0\\ -\dfrac12\,\dfrac{a^2}{U_0}, & a(T) < 0\end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="p. 75a" />

Wen's sketch shows $A/V$ falling with $T$, with the condensed branch below the continuation of the normal one. The entropy and heat capacity follow as usual (notes p. 75a):

<Formula label="Entropy and heat capacity" tex="S = -\frac{\partial A}{\partial T}, \qquad C = T\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}" cite="reading1" at="p. 75a" />

To see their shape, take $a = a_0(T/T_c - 1)$ near the transition, as for the Ising model in Chapter III (this step is ours). Below $T_c$ the condensate density is $|\psi|^2 = (a_0/U_0)(1 - T/T_c)$, growing linearly from zero; $|\psi|$ itself grows as $(1 - T/T_c)^{1/2}$, the exponent $\beta = \frac12$ of mean-field theory. The extra free energy $-a_0^2(1 - T/T_c)^2/(2U_0)$ per volume gives an extra entropy, negative (the condensate is ordered), that vanishes at $T_c$, and an extra heat capacity:

<Formula label="Heat-capacity jump" tex="\frac{\Delta C}{V} = \frac{a_0^2}{U_0T_c^2}\,T \quad (T < T_c), \qquad \text{a jump of } \frac{a_0^2}{U_0T_c} \text{ at } T_c" cite="reading1" at="p. 75a (sketch of C; worked out here)" />

Wen's sketches show exactly this: the entropy below $T_c$ falling under the normal curve $-\partial A_0/\partial T$ and joining it at $T_c$ with a kink, and the heat capacity jumping down at $T_c$. At low temperatures his sketch rises as $T^3$, which is not part of Ginzburg–Landau theory: it comes from the sound-wave excitations of the interacting condensate found at the end of the first part of the chapter (notes p. 69).

**Real helium and free bosons.** Wen ends p. 75a with two measured or computed curves. In real helium-4, $C$ rises as $T^3$ at low temperature and has a sharp, tall peak at the transition, 2.17 K, the **lambda point** (named for the shape of the curve). For free bosons, $C$ rises as $T^{3/2}$ to a cusp at $T_c$ and then falls, continuous at the transition (the ideal Bose gas of [8.044](/courses/8-044/bose-einstein-condensation)). The finite jump of Ginzburg–Landau theory lies between the two: like all mean-field theories, it misses the fluctuations of the order parameter that make the real peak so sharp (our remark).

For the ideal Bose gas the condensate fraction below $T_c$ is $1 - (T/T_c)^{3/2}$. Show that near $T_c$ it vanishes linearly, as Ginzburg–Landau theory says $|\psi|^2$ should, and find the coefficient. Then compare the two at $T/T_c = 0.95$, $0.8$ and $0.5$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Near the transition">
Write $T/T_c = 1 - \tau$ with $\tau$ small. Then $(1 - \tau)^{3/2} \approx 1 - \frac32\tau$, so the fraction is $\approx \frac32\tau = \frac32(1 - T/T_c)$: linear in $1 - T/T_c$, the form $|\psi|^2 = (a_0/U_0)(1 - T/T_c)$ with $a_0/U_0$ equal to $\frac32$ times the total density.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
At $T/T_c = 0.95$: exact $1 - 0.95^{3/2} = 0.0741$, linear $0.075$, 1% apart. At $0.8$: $0.284$ against $0.30$, 5% apart. At $0.5$: $0.646$ against $0.75$, 16% apart. </Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A solid as a "condensate": density waves

Wen's last page (notes p. 75b) applies the same ideas to an unexpected case, the **solid**. A liquid has a uniform density; a solid has a density that oscillates in space. Take one wave vector, in one dimension. What is the order parameter? Wen writes the density as

<Formula label="Density of a density wave" tex="\rho(x) = \rho_0 + \alpha\cos(kx + \phi) = \rho_0 + \mathrm{Re}\left(\psi\,e^{ikx}\right), \qquad \psi = \alpha e^{i\phi}" cite="reading1" at="p. 75b" />

The complex number $\psi$ is the order parameter of a solid, which Wen calls a **charge density wave** (CDW). If $\psi = 0$ there is no density wave: the liquid. The **phase** of $\psi$ gives the **position** of the wave: shifting the wave by a distance $\ell$ changes $\phi$ by $-k\ell$.

Now the symmetry argument. Space is uniform, so the energy cannot depend on where the density wave sits; therefore the free energy cannot depend on the phase of $\psi$ (notes p. 75b). That is the same U(1) symmetry as for the Bose condensate, and the Ginzburg–Landau free energy has the same form:

<Formula label="Ginzburg–Landau theory of a density wave" tex="A = \int d^dx\left[a(T)|\psi|^2 + b(T)|\psi|^4 + \ldots\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 75b" />

in $d$ dimensions. Terms such as $\psi\psi + \psi^*\psi^*$ or $\psi\psi\psi^*$ are absent, because they change under $\psi \to e^{i\theta}\psi$ and so would break the symmetry (notes p. 75b). Wen's conclusion: **a density wave is a Boson condensation**, mathematically. (Our remark: in this picture the density wave that appears as a liquid freezes plays the part of the condensate. Real melting in three dimensions is a first-order transition, which this simple expansion does not capture.)

**What does a vortex look like in a solid?** If $\psi$ is a condensate field, it can have a vortex, $\psi(\vec x) = f(r)e^{i\theta}\psi_0$, and Wen asks what the density $\rho = \rho_0 + \mathrm{Re}(\psi(\vec x)e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x})$ looks like then (notes p. 75b). His answer is a sketch: rows of crests, one of which stops at a point. Take $\vec k$ along $y$ and $\psi_0$ real. The crests of the wave are where $ky + \theta = 2\pi j$, with $j$ a whole number. Far to the right of the vortex, where $\theta \approx 0$, they lie at $ky = 2\pi j$; far to the left, where $\theta \approx \pm\pi$, at $ky = \pm\pi, \pm 3\pi, \ldots$. Between $ky = -2\pi$ and $2\pi$ there are three crests on the right but only two on the left: one extra row of atoms ends at the core. This is an **edge dislocation**, the defect by which crystals deform plastically (the name is ours; Wen leaves the answer as a picture).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A circle of minima">
The Mexican-hat free energy of this page, with a whole circle of equivalent minima of which the condensate picks one, is the kind of potential Guth describes in Lecture 22 of 8.286 for the Higgs field: a field whose potential is lowest away from zero, so that as the universe cools it settles into one of many equivalent values and the symmetry is spontaneously broken. (The Higgs field is a complex doublet, so its minima form a 3-sphere rather than a circle, and its symmetry is a gauge symmetry; the picture is the same.)
</Callout>

## Summary

- In a Bose condensate every boson is in one state, $\Psi = \psi_0(x_1)\cdots\psi_0(x_N)$. Collective excitations change that state for all at once: density waves $\psi_0 + \alpha e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}$ and vortices $f(r)e^{in\theta}\psi_0$.
- Ginzburg–Landau theory: $A = \int[\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^2 + a(T)|\psi|^2 + \frac{U_0}{2}|\psi|^4] + A_0(T)$, symmetric under $\psi \to e^{i\theta}\psi$. For $a < 0$, $|\psi|^2 = -a/U_0$ and $A/V$ drops by $a^2/(2U_0)$.
- With $a = a_0(T/T_c - 1)$ the heat capacity jumps by $a_0^2/(U_0T_c)$ at $T_c$. Real helium-4 has a sharp lambda peak at 2.17 K and $C \propto T^3$ at low $T$; free bosons have $C \propto T^{3/2}$ and a cusp.
- A solid's density wave $\rho_0 + \mathrm{Re}(\psi e^{ikx})$ has a complex order parameter whose phase is the wave's position. Translation symmetry gives the same U(1)-symmetric Ginzburg–Landau theory, and a vortex in $\psi$ is a dislocation.
