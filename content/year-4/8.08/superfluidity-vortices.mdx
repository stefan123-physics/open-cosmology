The first part of Chapter VII described interacting bosons by a single complex field $\psi$, found its ground state $\psi_0$ by minimizing a grand potential, and showed that its low-energy excitations are sound waves rather than free particles. Wen now uses that field to explain **superfluidity** (notes pp. 70–72): why a condensate can flow around a ring for ever, what a vortex is, and how fast a superfluid may flow before friction sets in. (Citations give the handwritten page numbers at the top right of the scanned notes, pp. 70–75 for this part of the chapter; "notes p. 69" refers to the last page of the first part, which carries on the same numbering. The notes write the density as $\rho$ on p. 70 and as $n$ on p. 71; this page keeps $\rho$ for the density and uses $n$ only for the whole number of turns of the phase.)

## Flow around a ring

Take the bosons in a ring-shaped container (a torus) of radius $R$, and let $x$ be the distance measured along the ring (notes p. 70). At zero temperature the state of the condensate makes the grand potential smallest:

<Formula label="Grand potential of the condensate" tex="\Omega = \int d^3x\left[\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^2 - \mu|\psi|^2 + \frac12 U_0|\psi|^4\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 70" />

Here $m$ is the mass of a boson, $\mu$ the chemical potential and $U_0$ the strength of the contact repulsion between bosons. For $\mu > 0$ the potential $-\mu|\psi|^2 + \frac12 U_0|\psi|^4$ has its minimum away from zero, at $|\psi_0|^2 = \mu/U_0$ (notes p. 68): this is the condensate, sketched by Wen as the familiar double well along a cut through $\psi$.

How does a condensate flow? The particle current is the probability current of quantum mechanics, multiplied by the number of particles (notes p. 70):

<Formula label="Density current" tex="J_x = v_x\rho = \frac{1}{m}\,\mathrm{Re}\left(\psi^*\,\frac{\hbar}{i}\,\partial_x\psi\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 70" />

with $\rho = |\psi|^2$ the density and $v_x$ the flow velocity. If $\psi = \psi_0$ is constant, $J_x = 0$: no flow. A flowing state has a phase that changes along the ring. Going once around, $x$ grows by $2\pi R$ and $\psi$ must come back to the same value, so the phase can only wind by a whole number of turns (notes p. 70):

<Formula label="A flowing state and its current" tex="\psi = \psi_0\,e^{i\frac{n}{R}x}, \quad n \text{ an integer}, \qquad J_x = \frac{\hbar n}{mR}\,|\psi_0|^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 70" />

The flow velocity is $\hbar n/(mR)$. (The notes write the current as $\frac{n}{\hbar Rm}|\psi_0|^2$, a slip: $\partial_x\psi = (in/R)\psi$ gives $\hbar n/(mR)$, which is a velocity times a density.)

Wen's key point: $\psi_0 e^{inx/R}$ satisfies the equation of motion, and it minimizes $\Omega$ (among all states with the same winding number, a qualification that is ours). The flow could only stop if the winding number went to zero, and a whole number cannot shrink gradually: the fluid keeps flowing: it is a **superfluid**. Wen sketches $\Omega$ against $n$ as a row of wells at the integers, each separated from the next by a barrier, with a state trapped in the well at $n = 2$.

The bottoms of the wells follow from the kinetic term of $\Omega$ (this step is ours): $|\nabla\psi|^2 = (n/R)^2|\psi_0|^2$, while the other two terms do not depend on the phase, so for a fluid of volume $V$, keeping the amplitude at $|\psi_0|$ (letting it relax lowers the result slightly, a negligible correction for a large ring)

<Formula label="Energy of the winding states" tex="\Omega_n - \Omega_0 = \frac{\hbar^2 n^2}{2mR^2}\,|\psi_0|^2\,V = n^2E_1" cite="reading1" at="p. 70 (sketch of Ω against n; worked out here)" />

where $E_1$ is the extra energy of one turn. A state with $n \neq 0$ is not the ground state, only a local minimum: it would like to fall to $n = 0$, but it cannot get there smoothly.

## Why the winding cannot unwind

Why is there a barrier? To go from $n = 1$ to $n = 0$, that is from $\psi = e^{ix/R}\psi_0$ to $\psi_0$, the phase must unwind (notes p. 71). Picture $\psi$ at each point of the ring as a point in the complex plane: for $n = 1$ the values go once around a circle of radius $|\psi_0|$ near the bottom of the potential; for $n = 0$ they all sit at one point. A loop that goes once around the origin cannot be shrunk to a point without crossing the origin. So somewhere along the way **$\psi$ must pass through zero**, where the potential is higher: Wen's sketch shows the loop of values being dragged over the central hump of the potential. This is a potential barrier, and $n$ cannot change.

The only way out is local: **a vortex** can cross the fluid and change $n$ by one (notes p. 71).

## What a vortex is

In a plane, with polar coordinates $x = r\cos\varphi$, $y = r\sin\varphi$, a vortex is the state (notes p. 71)

<Formula label="A vortex" tex="\psi = f(r)\,\psi_0\,e^{i\varphi}" cite="reading1" at="p. 71" />

The phase turns once as one goes around the origin. Since the phase is not defined at $r = 0$, the amplitude must vanish there: $f(0) = 0$, and $f(r)$ rises to 1 over a distance Wen calls the size of the vortex core. (Balancing the gradient energy $\hbar^2/(2m\xi^2)$ against $\mu$ gives a core size $\xi$ of order $\hbar/\sqrt{2m\mu}$; this estimate is ours.) Outside the core the fluid moves with the superfluid velocity, and carries the current (notes p. 71)

<Formula label="Superfluid velocity and current" tex="\vec v_s = \frac{\hbar}{m}\nabla\varphi(x, y), \qquad \vec J = \rho\,\vec v_s" cite="reading1" at="p. 71" />

For the vortex, $|\nabla\varphi| = 1/r$, so the fluid circles the core with speed $v_s = \hbar/(mr)$, fastest close to the core. Because the phase can change by a whole number of turns only, the circulation around any closed loop is **quantized** (notes p. 71):

<Formula label="Quantization of circulation" tex="\oint d\vec s\cdot\vec v_s = \frac{\hbar}{m}\cdot 2\pi\cdot\text{integer} = \frac{h}{m}\times\text{integer}" cite="reading1" at="p. 71" />

(The notes write the middle expression with $\hbar/2m$, a slip: with $\hbar/m$ it gives the result the notes state, $h/m$ times an integer.)

In a boxed remark Wen draws the consequence: **a superfluid cannot rotate uniformly**. Solid-body rotation, $\vec v(\vec r) = \vec\omega \times \vec r$, has curl $2\vec\omega$, but $\vec v_s$ is a gradient, whose curl vanishes wherever $\psi \neq 0$. A rotating superfluid therefore fills with vortices, as Wen sketches. The number needed follows from the circulation (this count is ours, an argument due to Feynman and Onsager): around an area $A$, solid rotation has circulation $2\omega A$, and $N_v$ vortices give $N_vh/m$, so their density is

<Formula label="Vortex density in a rotating superfluid" tex="\frac{N_v}{A} = \frac{2m\omega}{h}" cite="reading1" at="p. 71, boxed remark (count worked out here)" />

**Tunnelling of a vortex.** Wen's last sketch on p. 71 shows how the flow in the ring can change after all. Look at the ring from above: the phase winds by $2\pi n$ around the inner hole. If a vortex sits in the fluid between the inner and outer walls, the phase winds by $2\pi$ around the vortex alone and by $2\pi(n + 1)$ around the outer edge, which encloses both. A vortex that moves across the fluid from one wall to the other therefore changes the winding around the hole by one. Along the way $\psi$ vanishes only inside the core of the vortex, not over a whole cross-section of the ring, so this is the cheapest route over the barrier. Wen calls the process vortex tunnelling; we add that it can also be thermally activated, and that it is rare.

## Excitations on a flowing superfluid

Why does a superfluid flow without friction past the walls of its container, and up to what speed? Wen answers by comparing the excitations of the fluid at rest and in motion (notes p. 72). At rest, $\psi = \psi_0$, the low-energy excitations are the sound waves of the first part of the chapter, $\omega_k = v|k|$ with $v$ the sound speed. (On p. 72 $v$ is the sound speed and $v_s$ the flow speed; on p. 69 the notes called the sound speed $v_s$.) For small $k$ this is the limit of the full dispersion of the interacting gas (notes p. 69):

<Formula label="Excitations of the condensate at rest" tex="\omega_k^2 = \left(\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} + 2\mu\right)\frac{k^2}{2m}, \qquad \omega_k \approx v|k| \;\text{ for small } k, \qquad v = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{m}}" cite="reading2" at="p. 69 (boxed)" />

Now let the superfluid flow: $\psi = \psi_0e^{iKx}$, with velocity $v_s = \hbar K/m$. An excitation travelling with the flow is carried along and has its frequency raised; one travelling against it has its frequency lowered (notes p. 72):

<Formula label="Excitations on a flowing superfluid" tex="\omega_k = \begin{cases}(v + v_s)|k|, & k > 0\\ (v - v_s)|k|, & k < 0\end{cases} \;=\; v|k| + v_sk" cite="reading1" at="p. 72" />

This is the Doppler shift. If $v_s < v$, every excitation still costs positive energy. The flowing state cannot lower its energy by creating excitations, so nothing slows it: **stable flow, no friction**. If $v_s > v$, the excitations with $k < 0$ have negative energy; the fluid lowers its energy by making them, and the flow is **unstable**.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[-3, 3]} range={[-2, 6]} xLabel="k in units of mv/ħ" yLabel="ħω_k/(mv²)" samples={400}
  curves={[

    { expr: "x^2/2 + u*x", label: "free", dash: true }

  caption="Excitation energies on a superfluid flowing at speed v_s, computed from ħω_k = √(ε_k(ε_k + 2μ)) + ħv_s k with ε_k = ħ²k²/2m (solid) and, for free bosons, from ε_k + ħv_s k (dashed); here μ = mv². With the slider below 1 the solid curve stays above zero everywhere except at k = 0: the flow is stable. Above 1 it dips below zero for small negative k. The dashed curve dips below zero for any v_s > 0: free bosons have no superflow." />

**The critical velocity.** In real helium-4 the excitation curve is not a straight line: after rising as $v|k|$ it bends over and has a minimum at finite $k$, the roton minimum, which Wen sketches (notes p. 72). Tilting the curve by $v_sk$, the first point to touch zero is where the line $v_ck$ from the origin just grazes the curve. That defines the **critical velocity of superfluid flow**, which we can write as (Wen gives the construction as a sketch)

<Formula label="Critical velocity" tex="v_c = \min_k\frac{\omega_k}{|k|}" cite="reading1" at="p. 72 (sketch of v_ck; written as a formula here)" />

Below $v_c$ the flow is frictionless; at $v_s = v_c$ the tilted curve touches zero at the roton minimum. For the dispersion of the interacting Bose gas, $\omega_k/|k|$ is smallest at $k \to 0$, so $v_c = v$. (For real helium-4 the roton minimum gives a Landau critical velocity near 60 m/s, well below the sound speed of about 240 m/s; in experiments flow usually breaks down at far lower speeds still, because vortices are created at the walls. These figures are not in the notes.)

## No superflow for free bosons

Wen ends with a warning (notes p. 72): a condensate of **free** bosons is not a superfluid. Their excitations are free particles, $\omega_k = \hbar k^2/2m$, so $\omega_k/|k|$ goes to zero at small $k$, and $v_c = 0$. On a ring the single-particle states are $e^{inx/R}$ with energies (the notes call the integer $m$ here)

<Formula label="Single-particle levels on a ring" tex="\psi = e^{i n x/R}, \qquad \epsilon_n = \frac{\hbar^2n^2}{2mR^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 72 (energies worked out here from Wen's sketch)" />

Put all $N$ bosons in the state $n = 1$: a flowing condensate. Without interactions nothing stops one boson from dropping into $n = 0$, lowering the energy, then another, and another. Each step is a single-particle transition, with no barrier, since the others are not affected: the flow decays one particle at a time. It is the repulsion $U_0$ that ties the bosons together into one field $\psi$, so that the winding can change only for all of them at once, through $\psi = 0$. Superfluidity needs interactions.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Line defects in the early universe">
A vortex is a **topological defect**: the field vanishes along a line, and the phase winds by a whole number of turns around it, a number that no smooth change can undo. In Lecture 22 of 8.286, Guth's course lists the defects that may form when a Higgs field settles into different values in different regions of the cooling universe. Cosmic strings, one of them, are one-dimensional lines of trapped field energy that some grand unified theories predict. Superfluid vortices are the laboratory example of such a line defect.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A condensate of interacting bosons is one complex field $\psi$, found by minimizing $\Omega = \int[\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^2 - \mu|\psi|^2 + \frac12U_0|\psi|^4]$. Its current is $J_x = \frac1m\mathrm{Re}(\psi^*\frac{\hbar}{i}\partial_x\psi)$.
- In a ring, $\psi = \psi_0e^{inx/R}$ with $n$ an integer carries $J_x = \hbar n|\psi_0|^2/(mR)$. Changing $n$ requires $\psi$ to pass through zero: a barrier, so the flow persists. Only a vortex crossing the fluid can change $n$.
- A vortex is $\psi = f(r)\psi_0e^{i\varphi}$ with a core where $f \to 0$. The superfluid velocity $\frac{\hbar}{m}\nabla\varphi$ has quantized circulation $h/m$ times an integer, and no curl, so a rotating superfluid forms $2m\omega/h$ vortices per unit area.
- On a flowing superfluid, $\omega_k \to v|k| + v_sk$. The flow is stable below the critical velocity $v_c = \min\omega_k/|k|$: the sound speed for phonons, set by the roton minimum in real helium.
- Free bosons have $v_c = 0$ and their flow decays one particle at a time: superfluidity needs interactions.
