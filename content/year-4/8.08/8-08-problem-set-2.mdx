This set practises the canonical ensemble of [Chapter I of Wen's notes](/courses/8-08/canonical-ensemble-free-energy): the Boltzmann factor, the partition function and the free energy, [equipartition and the classical ideal gas](/courses/8-08/information-equipartition-ideal-gas), and the spin entropy and Curie's law of the [first supplement](/courses/8-08/counting-states-spins-ideal-gas). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 2 (February 9, 2005), in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its due date is left out. MIT's Problems 2, 4 and 5 point to problems in the course textbook (K. Huang, *Statistical Mechanics*) by number only. The textbook is not used here. **Problem 4 is our own problem**: the sheet gives only the pointer, and our problem is on the Maxwell distribution of speeds from the same chapter of Wen's notes. In Problem 2 MIT adds a part (c) of its own, the equipartition of kinetic and potential energy for particles in a harmonic potential: **the setup and parts (a) and (b) of Problem 2 are ours**, built so that MIT's part (c) applies, and part (c) is MIT's, rewritten. In Problem 5 MIT adds three remarks (the air is an ideal gas, the combination $(\gamma - 1)/\gamma$ is a constant whose value is to be found, and the entropy per particle does not depend on the height): **the setup and the questions of Problem 5 are ours**, an atmosphere in which the entropy per molecule is the same at every height, built so that MIT's remarks apply. Problems 1 and 3 are stated in full on MIT's sheet and are given here as MIT sets them. Our additions to them are marked: part (d) of Problem 1 (a number), the entropy box and the field $B_f$ of Problem 3(a), and the reading of Problem 3(b) with a monatomic gas at a fixed volume. The numbers of Problems 4 and 5 are ours too.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it; type decimals, not fractions. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `sqrt(m*g)/kB`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `muB` stands for $\mu_B$, `kB` for $k_B$, `hbar` for $\hbar$, `w` for $\omega$, `gam` for $\gamma$, `Bf` for $B_f$ and `pi` for $\pi$; the other letters (`g`, `B`, `T`, `N`, `m`, `x`) are typed as they are.

MIT's sheet gives no points, so the points are ours: 20 for Problem 1, 20 for Problem 2, 25 for Problem 3, 15 for Problem 4 and 20 for Problem 5, 100 points in all, split between the parts as shown. Parts that ask you to show something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of Wen's notes. $T$ is the temperature, $k_B$ Boltzmann's constant and $\beta = 1/k_BT$. $Q$ is the partition function, $A = -k_BT\ln Q$ the free energy, $E$ the mean energy and $S$ the entropy. In Problems 1 and 3, $g$ is the g-factor of the spin, $\mu_B$ the Bohr magneton and $B$ the magnetic field; in Problem 5, $g$ is the acceleration of gravity. $P(\pm\frac12)$ is a probability in Problem 1 and $P$ the pressure in Problem 5. Angle brackets $\langle\ \rangle$ are averages in the canonical ensemble.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Curie's law from the canonical ensemble" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

One spin 1/2 sits in a magnetic field $B$ along $z$. Its two states have the energies

<Formula label="The two levels of the spin" tex="E\!\left(S_z = +\tfrac12\right) = +\frac{g\mu_B B}{2}, \qquad E\!\left(S_z = -\tfrac12\right) = -\frac{g\mu_B B}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

so the state with the spin along the field has the higher energy (for an electron the magnetic moment points against the spin). The spin is in contact with a heat bath at the temperature $T$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the probability $P(\frac12)$ that the spin is in the state $S_z = +\frac12$, and the probability $P(-\frac12)$ that it is in the state $S_z = -\frac12$.

<Hint>

In the canonical ensemble each state has a weight $e^{-E_n/k_BT}$. The probabilities are the weights divided by their sum.

</Hint>

<Hint>

There are only two states here. Divide the numerator and the denominator by the weight of the state you want, so that each probability has the form $1/(1 + e^{\ldots})$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-up" label="$P(+\tfrac12) =$" expr="1/(exp(g*muB*B/(kB*T)) + 1)"
  vars={{ g: [1.5, 2.5], muB: [0.5, 1.5], B: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ g: "g", muB: "\\mu_B", B: "B", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/(exp(-g*muB*B/(kB*T)) + 1)", hint: "That is the probability of the other state, S_z = −1/2. The state S_z = +1/2 has the higher energy, so it must be the less likely one: its probability is below one half." },
    { expr: "1/(exp(g*muB*B/(2*kB*T)) + 1)", hint: "The exponent is half too small. The ratio of the two weights is e to the power of minus the energy difference over k_B T, and the two levels differ by g μ_B B, not g μ_B B/2." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-down" label="$P(-\tfrac12) =$" expr="1/(exp(-g*muB*B/(kB*T)) + 1)"
  vars={{ g: [1.5, 2.5], muB: [0.5, 1.5], B: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ g: "g", muB: "\\mu_B", B: "B", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(g*muB*B/(2*kB*T))", hint: "That is only the Boltzmann weight of the state, not its probability: it is larger than 1. Divide by the sum of the two weights." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A system in contact with a heat bath is found in a state of energy $E_n$ with a probability proportional to the Boltzmann factor. With two states the sum is easy.

<Step title="The Boltzmann weights">

The probability of a state is its weight divided by the partition function,

<Formula label="The Boltzmann distribution" tex="P_n \propto e^{-E_n/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2, point ②" />

Here the two weights are $e^{-g\mu_BB/2k_BT}$ and $e^{+g\mu_BB/2k_BT}$, and their sum is the partition function of one spin, $Q_1 = 2\cosh(g\mu_BB/2k_BT)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The two probabilities">

Dividing each weight by $Q_1$:

<Formula label="The probabilities of the two states" tex="P\!\left(\tfrac12\right) = \frac{e^{-g\mu_BB/2k_BT}}{e^{-g\mu_BB/2k_BT} + e^{g\mu_BB/2k_BT}} = \frac{1}{e^{g\mu_BB/k_BT} + 1}, \qquad P\!\left(-\tfrac12\right) = \frac{1}{e^{-g\mu_BB/k_BT} + 1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two add up to 1, since $\frac{1}{e^{y} + 1} + \frac{1}{e^{-y} + 1} = \frac{1}{e^{y} + 1} + \frac{e^{y}}{1 + e^{y}} = 1$. At $B = 0$ both are $\frac12$; at $T \to 0$ the spin sits in the lower state, $P(-\frac12) \to 1$; at very high temperature both tend to $\frac12$ again.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the average spin $\langle S_z\rangle$.

<Hint>

$\langle S_z\rangle = (+\frac12)\,P(\frac12) + (-\frac12)\,P(-\frac12)$. Write both probabilities with the weights $e^{\mp y/2}$, where $y = g\mu_BB/k_BT$: then they have the same denominator, $e^{-y/2} + e^{y/2}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A difference of two exponentials over their sum is a hyperbolic tangent. Watch the sign: which state is more likely?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-sz" label="$\langle S_z\rangle =$" expr="-tanh(g*muB*B/(2*kB*T))/2"
  vars={{ g: [1.5, 2.5], muB: [0.5, 1.5], B: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ g: "g", muB: "\\mu_B", B: "B", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "tanh(g*muB*B/(2*kB*T))/2", hint: "The sign is wrong. The lower level is S_z = −1/2, so that state is the more likely one and the average spin is negative." },
    { expr: "-tanh(g*muB*B/(kB*T))/2", hint: "The argument of tanh is twice too large. Each level lies g μ_B B/2 from zero, so the argument is g μ_B B/(2 k_B T)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Average $S_z$ over the two states with the probabilities of part (a).

<Step title="The average">

With the weights $e^{\mp g\mu_BB/2k_BT}$,

<Formula label="The average spin" tex="\langle S_z\rangle = \frac12\,\frac{e^{-g\mu_BB/2k_BT} - e^{g\mu_BB/2k_BT}}{e^{-g\mu_BB/2k_BT} + e^{g\mu_BB/2k_BT}} = -\frac12\tanh\frac{g\mu_BB}{2k_BT}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The size of $\langle S_z\rangle$ never exceeds $\frac12$, the value of a fully aligned spin, which it reaches when $g\mu_BB \gg k_BT$. The spin points against the field on average, so the magnetic moment $-g\mu_B\langle S_z\rangle$ points along it. Supplement 1 finds the same tanh law for the moment of $N$ spins by counting states at fixed energy (Section 1.2.3, p. 4).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

The spin susceptibility is defined on MIT's sheet as

<Formula label="The spin susceptibility" tex="\chi = \left.\frac{\langle S_z\rangle}{B}\right|_{B \to 0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1" />

Find $\chi$.

<Hint>

For a small argument, $\tanh y \approx y$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-chi" label="$\chi =$" expr="-g*muB/(4*kB*T)"
  vars={{ g: [1.5, 2.5], muB: [0.5, 1.5], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ g: "g", muB: "\\mu_B", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "g*muB/(4*kB*T)", hint: "The size is right, but the sign is not: the average spin of part (b) is negative for a positive field, so the susceptibility defined with S_z is negative." },
    { expr: "-g*muB/(2*kB*T)", hint: "This is twice too large. Expand −(1/2) tanh(g μ_B B/2k_BT) for small B: both the 1/2 in front and the 2 in the argument stay." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a weak field the tanh is linear in $B$, so $\langle S_z\rangle/B$ has a finite limit.

<Step title="The weak-field limit">

With $\tanh y \approx y$ for small $y$:

<Formula label="Curie's law for one spin" tex="\langle S_z\rangle \approx -\frac12\,\frac{g\mu_BB}{2k_BT}, \qquad \chi = -\frac{g\mu_B}{4k_BT}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The susceptibility falls as $1/T$: this is Curie's law.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The moment of one spin is $-g\mu_B\langle S_z\rangle \approx g^2\mu_B^2B/4k_BT$. For $N$ spins that is the moment Supplement 1 finds by counting states,

<Formula label="Curie's law for N spins" tex="\mathcal M = \frac{g^2\mu_B^2N}{4k_BT}\,B" cite="reading2" at="Section 1.2.3, p. 5" />

Units: $\mu_B$ is in J/T, so $\chi$ is in 1/T, as a spin (a pure number) per field must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

(Our addition.) An electron spin ($g = 2.00$) sits in a field $B = 1.00$ T at $T = 1.00$ K. The Bohr magneton is $\mu_B = 9.274\times10^{-24}$ J/T and $k_B = 1.3806\times10^{-23}$ J/K. What is the size $|\langle S_z\rangle|$ of the average spin? Give a decimal.

<Hint>

First the ratio $g\mu_BB/2k_BT$. Then use the exact result of part (b), not the weak-field one: check how small the ratio is.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-num" label="$|\langle S_z\rangle| =$" value="0.2931" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into the tanh of part (b).

<Step title="The numbers">

The ratio is $g\mu_BB/2k_BT = \mu_BB/k_BT = 9.274\times10^{-24}/1.3806\times10^{-23} = 0.6717$, and

<Formula label="The average spin at 1 T and 1 K" tex="|\langle S_z\rangle| = \frac12\tanh 0.6717 = 0.2931" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own numbers)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The weak-field law of part (c) would give $\frac12 \times 0.6717 = 0.3359$, 15% too high: at one kelvin a field of one tesla is no longer weak, and the spin is already 59% polarised ($0.2931/0.5$).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Equipartition in a harmonic trap (setup and parts (a) and (b) ours)" points={20} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet points here to a textbook problem and adds a part (c) of its own, on the equipartition of kinetic and potential energy in a harmonic potential. The setup and parts (a) and (b) below are ours, built so that MIT's part (c) applies.

$N$ identical atoms of mass $m$ move in one isotropic harmonic trap of angular frequency $\omega$. They do not interact with each other, and they are treated as classical particles. Label their $3N$ coordinates $q_i$ and momenta $p_i$, with $i$ running from 1 to $3N$; the energy is the sum of $p_i^2/2m + \frac12 m\omega^2 q_i^2$ over all $i$ (our setup). The gas is in contact with a heat bath at the temperature $T$.

<Part label="a" points={7}>

Find the classical partition function $Q_1$ of one atom, with the phase-space measure $d^3p\,d^3q/h^3$ of Wen's notes and $h = 2\pi\hbar$. (For the $N$ identical atoms, $Q = Q_1^N/N!$.)

<Hint>

The integral factorises into six Gaussian integrals: three over momenta and three over coordinates.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-a x^2}\,dx = \sqrt{\pi/a}$. Use it with $a = \beta/2m$ for a momentum and $a = \beta m\omega^2/2$ for a coordinate; the masses cancel in the product.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-q1" label="$Q_1 =$" expr="(kB*T/(hbar*w))^3"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 1.5], w: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T", hbar: "\\hbar", w: "\\omega" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(2*pi*kB*T/(hbar*w))^3", hint: "This is (2π)³ too large: it divides the phase-space volume by ħ³ instead of h³ = (2πħ)³." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a classical partition function the momenta and the coordinates are integrated separately, and for a quadratic energy every integral is a Gaussian.

<Step title="The six integrals">

For one direction, the momentum integral gives $\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}$ and the coordinate integral $\sqrt{2\pi k_BT/m\omega^2}$; their product is $2\pi k_BT/\omega$. Three directions and the measure $1/h^3$ give

<Formula label="The partition function of one atom in the trap" tex="Q_1 = \frac{1}{h^3}\left(\frac{2\pi k_BT}{\omega}\right)^3 = \left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar\omega}\right)^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$k_BT/\hbar\omega$ is a pure number, as a partition function must be. It is the number of oscillator levels, spaced by $\hbar\omega$, that lie below $k_BT$: the classical treatment needs $k_BT \gg \hbar\omega$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

From $Q = Q_1^N/N!$, find the mean energy $E$ of the gas and its heat capacity $C$.

<Hint>

$E = -\partial\ln Q/\partial\beta$. Only the power of $T$ in $Q$ matters: $\ln Q = 3N\ln T + (\text{terms without } T)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-e" label="$E =$" expr="3*N*kB*T"
  vars={{ N: [1, 10], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ N: "N", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*N*kB*T/2", hint: "That is the kinetic energy alone, the energy of a free gas. In the trap each of the 3N coordinates also carries potential energy." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$C =$" expr="3*N*kB"
  vars={{ N: [1, 10], kB: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ N: "N", kB: "k_B" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*N*kB/2", hint: "That is the heat capacity of a free monatomic gas. The potential energy of the trap doubles it." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The mean energy is minus the derivative of $\ln Q$ with respect to $\beta$; $Q$ is a power of $T$.

<Step title="The mean energy">

$\ln Q = 3N\ln(1/\beta\hbar\omega) - \ln N!$, so, with the relation of Chapter I,

<Formula label="Mean energy from the partition function" tex="E = -\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}\ln Q" cite="reading1" at="p. 4, point ⓑ" />

we get

<Formula label="Energy and heat capacity of the trapped gas" tex="E = \frac{3N}{\beta} = 3Nk_BT, \qquad C = \frac{dE}{dT} = 3Nk_B" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Each of the $6N$ quadratic terms in the energy carries $\frac12k_BT$, which part (c) shows. A free monatomic gas has only the $3N$ kinetic terms and the heat capacity $\frac32Nk_B$ (Chapter I, p. 9). The factor $1/N!$ changes the entropy, not the energy.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={7}>

(MIT's part.) Show the equipartition of the energy: for every $i$,

<Formula label="Equipartition of kinetic and potential energy" tex="\left\langle\frac{p_i^2}{2m}\right\rangle = \left\langle\frac12m\omega^2q_i^2\right\rangle = \frac12k_BT" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 1" />

<Hint>

The Boltzmann weight is a product of one factor for each $p_i$ and one for each $q_i$. In the average of $p_i^2/2m$ all the other factors cancel between numerator and denominator.

</Hint>

<Hint>

What is left is a ratio of two one-dimensional integrals. Write it as minus the $\beta$ derivative of the logarithm of $\int dp_i\,e^{-\beta p_i^2/2m}$, as Wen does for a free particle.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each quadratic term in the energy has its own Gaussian factor in the Boltzmann weight, and the mean of a quadratic term over a Gaussian does not depend on its coefficient.

<Step title="The kinetic energy">

All factors other than the one of $p_i$ cancel, so

<Formula label="Mean kinetic energy of one component" tex="\left\langle\frac{p_i^2}{2m}\right\rangle = \frac{\int dp_i\,\frac{p_i^2}{2m}\,e^{-\beta p_i^2/2m}}{\int dp_i\,e^{-\beta p_i^2/2m}} = -\frac{d}{d\beta}\ln\sqrt{\frac{2\pi m}{\beta}} = \frac{1}{2\beta} = \frac{k_BT}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 9, point ⑥" />

</Step>

<Step title="The potential energy">

The same steps with $q_i$ in place of $p_i$ and $m\omega^2$ in place of $1/m$:

<Formula label="Mean potential energy of one coordinate" tex="\left\langle\frac12m\omega^2q_i^2\right\rangle = -\frac{d}{d\beta}\ln\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\beta m\omega^2}} = \frac{1}{2\beta} = \frac{k_BT}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The $6N$ averages add up to $3Nk_BT$, the energy of part (b). Neither $m$ nor $\omega$ appears: a stiffer trap squeezes the atoms closer to the centre but leaves their mean potential energy unchanged.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Cooling by adiabatic demagnetization" points={25} mit="Problem 3">

$N$ spins 1/2, each with the two levels $\pm g\mu_BB/2$ of Problem 1, sit in a magnetic field $B$. They do not interact with each other.

<Part label="a" points={12}>

At first the spin system has the temperature $T$. The field is then lowered slowly to zero, with the spins isolated from everything else. What temperature does the spin system reach? MIT's hint: in this slow (adiabatic) process the entropy does not change.

To find out, first write the entropy per spin, $S/Nk_B$, as a function of $x = g\mu_BB/2k_BT$ alone (our addition). Then find the temperature $T_f$ the spins reach when the field is lowered from $B$ to a smaller value $B_f$ (our addition), and let $B_f$ go to zero.

<Hint>

For $N$ independent spins, $Q = Q_1^N$ with $Q_1 = 2\cosh x$. Then $A = -Nk_BT\ln Q_1$ and $S = (E - A)/T$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

If the entropy depends on $B$ and $T$ only through $x$, what has to stay fixed while $B$ changes?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-s" label="$S/Nk_B =$" expr="log(2*cosh(x)) - x*tanh(x)"
  vars={{ x: [0.2, 3] }}
  names={{ x: "x" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "log(2*cosh(x))", hint: "That is ln Q₁, which is −A/(N k_B T). The entropy is (E − A)/T, so the mean energy E = −N k_B T x tanh x has to be added too." },
    { expr: "log(2*cosh(x)) + x*tanh(x)", hint: "The sign of the energy term is wrong. The lower level is the more occupied one, so the mean energy is negative; with a plus sign the entropy would exceed N k_B ln 2, more than two states per spin allow." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-tf" label="$T_f =$" expr="T*Bf/B"
  vars={{ T: [0.5, 2], B: [1, 2], Bf: [0.1, 0.9] }}
  names={{ T: "T", B: "B", Bf: "B_f" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "T*B/Bf", hint: "This is upside down: lowering the field would then heat the spins. Keeping x = g μ_B B/2k_BT fixed means T falls in proportion to B." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The entropy of independent spins depends on the field and the temperature only through their ratio. An adiabatic change keeps the entropy, so it keeps $B/T$.

<Step title="The entropy per spin">

With $Q_1 = 2\cosh x$, the mean energy is $E = -N\,\partial\ln Q_1/\partial\beta = -Nk_BT\,x\tanh x$ and $A = -Nk_BT\ln(2\cosh x)$. So

<Formula label="Entropy of N spins in a field" tex="\frac{S}{Nk_B} = \frac{E - A}{Nk_BT} = \ln(2\cosh x) - x\tanh x, \qquad x = \frac{g\mu_BB}{2k_BT}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Constant entropy">

$S/Nk_B$ falls steadily from $\ln 2$ at $x = 0$ towards 0 as $x$ grows, so a fixed entropy means a fixed $x$, that is a fixed $B/T$. Lowering the field from $B$ to $B_f$ gives

<Formula label="Temperature after lowering the field" tex="T_f = T\,\frac{B_f}{B}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

and as $B_f \to 0$ the temperature of the spins goes to zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $x = 0$ the entropy is $\ln 2$ per spin, two equally likely states, and for large $x$ it goes to 0, all spins in the lower level. In a real paramagnetic salt the spins feel a small field $b$ from their neighbours even at $B = 0$, so the temperature stops near $T\,b/B$ instead of reaching zero. That is how this method reaches millikelvin temperatures.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={13}>

Now the $N$ spins in the field $B$ are in thermal contact with a classical ideal gas of $N$ particles in a volume $V$. We take the particles monatomic and $V$ fixed (our reading; MIT's sheet does not say). At first both have the temperature $T$, and $g\mu_BB \gg k_BT$. The field is lowered slowly to zero, and spins and gas stay at a common temperature. What temperature does the gas reach? Give $T_f/T$ as a decimal.

<Hint>

Now the total entropy, spins plus gas, is constant. What is the spin entropy at the start, when $g\mu_BB \gg k_BT$, and at the end, when $B = 0$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The gas entropy at fixed $V$ and $N$ depends on the temperature through $\frac32Nk_B\ln T$ (Chapter I, p. 11). The gas has to give up exactly the entropy the spins gain.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-gas" label="$T_f/T =$" value="0.630" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-gas" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The spins start almost perfectly ordered and end completely disordered, so they gain the entropy $Nk_B\ln 2$. In a slow process the total entropy stays the same, so the gas loses $Nk_B\ln 2$, and the gas can only lose entropy by cooling.

<Step title="The spin entropy">

At the start $x = g\mu_BB/2k_BT \gg 1$, and the entropy of part (a) is close to 0: nearly every spin is in the lower level. At the end $B = 0$, both levels have the same energy, and the spin entropy is $Nk_B\ln 2$ at any temperature.

</Step>

<Step title="The gas entropy">

The entropy of the ideal gas is

<Formula label="Entropy of the ideal gas" tex="S = Nk_B\left(\frac52 + \ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 11, point ⑦" />

with $\lambda \propto T^{-1/2}$, so at fixed $V$ and $N$ it changes by $\frac32Nk_B\ln(T_f/T)$. Constant total entropy gives

<Formula label="The gas pays for the spin entropy" tex="\frac32Nk_B\ln\frac{T_f}{T} = -Nk_B\ln 2, \qquad \frac{T_f}{T} = 2^{-2/3} = 0.630" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The ratio does not depend on $N$, $V$ or the first temperature, only on the counts: two states per spin against three momentum directions per atom. A gas at fixed pressure instead of fixed volume would have $\frac52Nk_B\ln T$ and end at $2^{-2/5} = 0.758$ of its first temperature. The first condition matters: with a weaker field the spins would start with some entropy already and the cooling would be smaller.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="The Maxwell distribution of speeds (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 4">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the Maxwell distribution of Chapter I of Wen's notes.

In a classical ideal gas at the temperature $T$, the fraction of molecules with a speed between $v$ and $v + dv$ is proportional to

<Formula label="Maxwell distribution of speeds" tex="e^{-\beta m v^2/2}\,v^2\,dv" cite="reading1" at="p. 9, point ⑥" />

where $m$ is the mass of a molecule.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the most probable speed $v_p$, where this distribution has its maximum.

<Hint>

Set the derivative of $v^2e^{-mv^2/2k_BT}$ with respect to $v$ to zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-vp" label="$v_p =$" expr="sqrt(2*kB*T/m)"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T", m: "m" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(kB*T/m)", hint: "That is the maximum of v e^(−mv²/2k_BT), with v to the first power, as for a gas moving in a plane. In three dimensions the factor is v²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The factor $v^2$ grows and the exponential falls; the maximum is where their logarithmic slopes cancel.

<Step title="The maximum">

$\frac{d}{dv}\ln\left(v^2e^{-mv^2/2k_BT}\right) = \frac{2}{v} - \frac{mv}{k_BT} = 0$ gives

<Formula label="The most probable speed" tex="v_p = \sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: J/kg = m²/s², so $v_p$ is a speed. It grows with the temperature and falls with the mass.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the mean speed $\langle v\rangle$.

<Hint>

Normalise first: $\langle v\rangle = \int_0^\infty v^3e^{-av^2}dv \big/ \int_0^\infty v^2e^{-av^2}dv$ with $a = m/2k_BT$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\int_0^\infty v^3e^{-av^2}dv = 1/2a^2$ and $\int_0^\infty v^2e^{-av^2}dv = \frac14\sqrt{\pi/a^3}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-mean" label="$\langle v\rangle =$" expr="sqrt(8*kB*T/(pi*m))"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T", m: "m" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(3*kB*T/m)", hint: "That is the root mean square speed, the square root of ⟨v²⟩, which equipartition gives at once. The mean of v itself is smaller." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Average $v$ over the normalised distribution; both integrals are standard Gaussian moments.

<Step title="The two integrals">

With $a = m/2k_BT$,

<Formula label="The mean speed" tex="\langle v\rangle = \frac{1/2a^2}{\frac14\sqrt{\pi/a^3}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi a}} = \sqrt{\frac{8k_BT}{\pi m}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\sqrt{8/\pi} = 1.596$ lies between $\sqrt2 = 1.414$ of $v_p$ and $\sqrt3 = 1.732$ of the root mean square speed: the long tail of fast molecules pulls the mean above the peak.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Nitrogen molecules have the mass $m = 28.0$ u, with $1\ \text{u} = 1.6605\times10^{-27}$ kg. What is their mean speed at $T = 300$ K, in m/s? Use $k_B = 1.3806\times10^{-23}$ J/K.

<Hint>

Use the result of part (b), with the mass in kilograms.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-num" label="$\langle v\rangle$ in m/s:" value="476.3" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into part (b).

<Step title="The numbers">

$m = 28.0 \times 1.6605\times10^{-27} = 4.649\times10^{-26}$ kg, and

<Formula label="Mean speed of nitrogen at 300 K" tex="\langle v\rangle = \sqrt{\frac{8 \times 1.3806\times10^{-23} \times 300}{\pi \times 4.649\times10^{-26}}}\ \text{m/s} = 476.3\ \text{m/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own numbers)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The most probable speed is $422.1$ m/s and the root mean square speed $517.0$ m/s, in the ratio $\sqrt2 : \sqrt{8/\pi} : \sqrt3$ of part (b). All three are comparable to the speed of sound in air, about 350 m/s at this temperature, as they should be: sound is carried by the molecules' own motion.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

What fraction of the molecules move faster than $2v_p$, twice the most probable speed? Give a decimal. You may use the complementary error function, $\operatorname{erfc}(s) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_s^\infty e^{-t^2}dt$, with $\operatorname{erfc}(2) = 0.004678$.

<Hint>

Change to the variable $t = v/v_p$, so the distribution becomes $\frac{4}{\sqrt\pi}\,t^2e^{-t^2}dt$. Check that it is normalised.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate $t^2e^{-t^2}$ from 2 to infinity by parts, with $t\,e^{-t^2}$ as the part you integrate.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-frac" label="fraction faster than $2v_p$:" value="0.0460" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-frac" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the variable $t = v/v_p$ the distribution has no parameters left, so the fraction is a pure number, the same for every gas at every temperature.

<Step title="Integrate by parts">

$\int_s^\infty t^2e^{-t^2}dt = \frac12 s\,e^{-s^2} + \frac12\int_s^\infty e^{-t^2}dt$, so the fraction above $s$ is

<Formula label="The fraction of molecules faster than s times the most probable speed" tex="F(s) = \operatorname{erfc}(s) + \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\,s\,e^{-s^2}, \qquad F(2) = 0.004678 + 0.041334 = 0.0460" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$F(0) = \operatorname{erfc}(0) = 1$: every molecule is faster than zero, so the distribution is normalised. About 4.6% of the molecules move faster than twice the most probable speed, and the tail falls off like $e^{-s^2}$ beyond.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="An atmosphere with the same entropy per molecule at every height (setup and questions ours)" points={20} mit="Problem 5">

MIT's sheet points here to a textbook problem and adds three remarks: the air is an ideal gas, the combination $(\gamma - 1)/\gamma$ is just a constant whose value is to be found, and the entropy per particle does not depend on the height $z$. MIT also suggests doing part (b) before part (a). The setup and the questions below are ours, built so that these remarks apply.

Air near the ground is stirred by convection: a parcel of air that rises expands slowly without exchanging heat, so the entropy per molecule is the same at every height. Treat the air as a classical ideal gas of molecules of mass $m$ in the uniform gravity $g$, with $P = nk_BT$, where $n$ is the number of molecules per volume. Each molecule has the heat capacity $c_V$ at fixed volume, constant over the range of temperatures here, and $\gamma = c_P/c_V$ with $c_P = c_V + k_B$. The entropy per molecule is $s = c_V\ln T - k_B\ln n + \text{const}$ (our statement; for a monatomic gas it is Wen's ideal-gas entropy of Chapter I, p. 11, with $c_V = \frac32k_B$). The air is at rest, so its pressure falls with the height as $dP/dz = -n m g$ (the standard balance of forces on a thin layer, stated by us).

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the temperature gradient $dT/dz$ in terms of $\gamma$, $m$, $g$ and $k_B$.

<Hint>

Follow MIT's suggestion and do part (b) first: it gives how $P$ and $T$ change together.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Divide $dP/dz = -nmg$ by $P = nk_BT$, so that $d\ln P/dz = -mg/k_BT$. Then replace $d\ln P$ by a multiple of $d\ln T$, using part (b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-grad" label="$dT/dz =$" expr="-(gam-1)*m*g/(gam*kB)"
  vars={{ gam: [1.2, 1.7], m: [0.5, 2], g: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ gam: "\\gamma", m: "m", g: "g", kB: "k_B" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(gam-1)*m*g/(gam*kB)", hint: "The sign is wrong: the pressure falls with height, and along a curve of constant entropy the temperature falls with it." },
    { expr: "-gam*m*g/((gam-1)*kB)", hint: "This uses the exponent of part (b) upside down, as if T went as P to the power γ/(γ − 1). It is P that goes as T to that power." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Hydrostatic balance fixes how fast the pressure falls; constant entropy ties the temperature to the pressure. Together they give how fast the temperature falls.

<Step title="Pressure against height">

Dividing the balance by $P = nk_BT$:

<Formula label="Logarithmic pressure gradient" tex="\frac{d\ln P}{dz} = -\frac{mg}{k_BT}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Use part (b)">

With $P \propto T^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}$, $d\ln P = \frac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1}\,d\ln T$, so $\frac{\gamma}{\gamma - 1}\,\frac{1}{T}\frac{dT}{dz} = -\frac{mg}{k_BT}$, and

<Formula label="The temperature gradient of the atmosphere" tex="\frac{dT}{dz} = -\frac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma}\,\frac{mg}{k_B}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The right side does not depend on $T$ or $z$: the temperature falls linearly with the height, which is why MIT calls $(\gamma - 1)/\gamma$ "just a constant". Units: $mg/k_B$ is (kg m/s²)/(J/K) = K/m, a temperature per length. For $\gamma \to 1$ (a gas with a very large heat capacity) the atmosphere becomes isothermal.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Use the hint of MIT's sheet, that the entropy per particle does not depend on the height: along the atmosphere $P$ is proportional to a power of $T$. Find the exponent $\nu$ in $P \propto T^\nu$, in terms of $\gamma$.

<Hint>

Keep $s$ fixed and eliminate $n$ with $n = P/k_BT$: then $c_V\ln T - k_B\ln P + k_B\ln T$ is constant.

</Hint>

<Hint>

That is $c_P\ln T - k_B\ln P = \text{const}$. Write $c_P/k_B$ in terms of $\gamma$, using $c_P = c_V + k_B$ and $\gamma = c_P/c_V$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-nu" label="$\nu =$" expr="gam/(gam-1)"
  vars={{ gam: [1.2, 1.7] }}
  names={{ gam: "\\gamma" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(gam-1)/gam", hint: "That is the exponent of the same law turned around, T ∝ P^((γ − 1)/γ). The question asks for P as a power of T." },
    { expr: "1/(gam-1)", hint: "That is the exponent for the number density, n ∝ T^(1/(γ − 1)). The pressure P = n k_B T has one more factor of T." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Constant entropy per molecule is one relation between $n$ and $T$; the ideal-gas law turns it into a relation between $P$ and $T$.

<Step title="Constant entropy">

With $n = P/k_BT$, $s = c_V\ln T - k_B\ln P + k_B\ln T + \text{const} = c_P\ln T - k_B\ln P + \text{const}$. Fixed $s$ means $P \propto T^{c_P/k_B}$, and from $c_P - c_V = k_B$ and $c_P = \gamma c_V$ we get $c_P/k_B = \gamma/(\gamma - 1)$:

<Formula label="Pressure and temperature at constant entropy per molecule" tex="P \propto T^{\gamma/(\gamma - 1)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a monatomic gas $\gamma = 5/3$ and $\nu = 5/2$; then $P/T^{5/2}$ fixed is $V/(N\lambda^3)$ fixed with $V/N = k_BT/P$, the condition for constant entropy from Wen's formula. It is also the familiar adiabatic law $PV^\gamma = \text{const}$ written with $T$ instead of $V$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Find the value of the constant $(\gamma - 1)/\gamma$ for air. Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, molecules of two atoms; at the temperatures of the atmosphere they rotate freely, but their vibrations are not excited. Give a decimal.

<Hint>

Count the quadratic terms in the energy of one molecule that are active: three for its motion, and the rotations about the two axes across the molecule. Each gives $\frac12k_B$ to $c_V$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Then $c_P = c_V + k_B$, and $(\gamma - 1)/\gamma = (c_P - c_V)/c_P = k_B/c_P$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-const" label="$(\gamma - 1)/\gamma =$" value="0.2857" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-const" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Equipartition gives $c_V$ from the active degrees of freedom, and the constant is $k_B/c_P$.

<Step title="Count the degrees of freedom">

Three directions of motion and two rotations give five quadratic terms, so $c_V = \frac52k_B$ and $c_P = \frac72k_B$; $\gamma = 7/5$. Then

<Formula label="The constant for air" tex="\frac{\gamma - 1}{\gamma} = \frac{k_B}{c_P} = \frac27 = 0.2857" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The rotation about the axis of the molecule does not count: its energy levels are far above $k_BT$, as Chapter I explains for rotations whose level spacing exceeds $k_BT$ (p. 10). (Wen's p. 10 writes $c_V \approx \frac72 k_B$ per molecule for air; that is $c_P$. Three translations and two rotations give $c_V = \frac52 k_B$, as his table of rotation and vibration temperatures on the same page implies.) A monatomic gas would give $2/5 = 0.4$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

(Our numbers.) Take the mean mass of an air molecule as $m = 29.0$ u, with $1\ \text{u} = 1.6605\times10^{-27}$ kg, $g = 9.81$ m/s² and $k_B = 1.3806\times10^{-23}$ J/K. By how many kelvin per kilometre does the temperature fall with the height?

<Hint>

Use part (a) with the constant of part (c), and convert from kelvin per metre to kelvin per kilometre.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-lapse" label="$-dT/dz$ in K/km:" value="9.78" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-lapse" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into part (a).

<Step title="The numbers">

$mg/k_B = 29.0 \times 1.6605\times10^{-27} \times 9.81/1.3806\times10^{-23} = 0.03422$ K/m, and

<Formula label="The temperature gradient of dry air" tex="-\frac{dT}{dz} = \frac27 \times 0.03422\ \text{K/m} = 9.78\ \text{K/km}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 1 (our own numbers)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is what meteorologists call the dry adiabatic lapse rate, the rate for rising dry air. The average measured fall in the lower atmosphere is smaller, because rising moist air releases heat when its water vapour condenses, which this ideal-gas model leaves out.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
