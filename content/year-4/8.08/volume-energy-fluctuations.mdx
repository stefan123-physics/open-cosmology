The [previous page](/courses/8-08/particle-number-fluctuations) found the fluctuations of the number of particles in a small volume and tied them to the compressibility. This page follows the rest of Wen's Chapter VI (notes pp. 45–47, points ② to ④): the fluctuations of the **volume** of a gas held at fixed pressure, found in two ways; the fluctuations of the **energy** of a body in contact with a heat bath; and the general rule behind all of them, that the size of a fluctuation is set by the size of a response. (Citations use Wen's handwritten page numbers, top right, and his circled point numbers. $A$ is the Helmholtz free energy, $G$ the Gibbs potential, $K_T$ the isothermal compressibility. The text, examples and plots are our own.)

## Volume fluctuations at fixed pressure

Put a gas in a cylinder closed by a piston on which the outside presses with a fixed pressure $P$ (notes p. 45, point ②). The volume is now free to change. When the gas pushes the piston out by $dV$ it does work $P\,dV$ on the outside, so the free energy of gas and surroundings together is $A(V, T) + PV$. Following the previous page, the probability of a volume $V$ is the exponential of minus this over $k_BT$, and near its maximum it is a Gaussian:

<Formula label="Probability of a volume at fixed pressure" tex="e^{-\beta\left(A(V, T) + VP\right)} = C\,e^{-\frac12\beta\frac{\partial^2A}{\partial V^2}(V - \bar V)^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 45, point ②" />

where $C$ is a constant (the value at the maximum; Wen writes #) and $\bar V$ is the volume at which $-\partial A/\partial V$ equals the outside pressure. The curvature is again a compressibility:

<Formula label="Curvature of the free energy in V" tex="\left(\frac{\partial^2A}{\partial V^2}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial V}\right)_T = -\frac{1}{(\partial V/\partial P)_T} = \frac{1}{VK_T}, \qquad K_T = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T" cite="reading1" at="p. 45, point ②" />

so the variance of the volume is

<Formula label="Volume fluctuations" tex="\langle(V - \bar V)^2\rangle = \frac{k_BT}{\partial^2A/\partial V^2} = k_BT\,K_T\,V" cite="reading1" at="p. 45, point ②" />

For an ideal gas, $V = Nk_BT/P$ and $VK_T = Nk_BT/P^2 = V/P = V^2/(Nk_BT)$, which gives (notes p. 45, boxed)

<Formula label="Volume fluctuations of an ideal gas" tex="\langle(V - \bar V)^2\rangle = \frac{V^2}{N}, \qquad \delta V = \sqrt{\langle(V - \bar V)^2\rangle} = \frac{V}{\sqrt N}" cite="reading1" at="p. 45, point ②" />

This fits the previous page. There the volume was fixed and the number of particles fluctuated by $\delta N/N = \sqrt{k_BTK_T/V}$; here the number is fixed and the volume fluctuates by $\delta V/V = \sqrt{k_BTK_T/V}$, the same amount. Either way it is the density $N/V$ that fluctuates, by the same relative amount.

## A second way: the Gibbs potential

The same result comes out of the Gibbs potential (notes p. 45, "second way"). Define it as the logarithm of the sum over all volumes of the weight above,

<Formula label="Gibbs potential as a sum over volumes" tex="G(P, T) = -k_BT\ln\int dV\,e^{-\beta\left[A(V, T) + VP\right]}" cite="reading1" at="p. 45, point ②" />

(the notes use $G$ in this sense without writing the definition out; it is implied by their next line). Differentiating with respect to $P$ brings down $-\beta V$ inside the integral, and the $-k_BT$ in front turns it into an average:

<Formula label="The volume as a derivative of G" tex="\frac{\partial G}{\partial P} = -k_BT\,\frac{\int dV\,(-\beta)V\,e^{-\beta[A(V, T) + VP]}}{\int dV\,e^{-\beta[A(V, T) + VP]}} = \langle V\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 45, point ②" />

the familiar thermodynamic rule $\partial G(P, T)/\partial P = V$. Differentiating again, the derivative of the numerator gives $-\beta\langle V^2\rangle$ and that of the denominator $+\beta\langle V\rangle^2$:

<Formula label="The second derivative of G" tex="\frac{\partial^2G}{\partial P^2} = -\beta\left[\langle V^2\rangle - \langle V\rangle^2\right] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle(V - \bar V)^2\rangle = -k_BT\,\frac{\partial^2G}{\partial P^2} = k_BT\,VK_T" cite="reading1" at="p. 45, point ②" />

the last step because $\partial^2G/\partial P^2 = \partial V/\partial P = -VK_T$. Wen's margin box sums up the pattern: $P = -\partial A/\partial V$ and $V = \partial G/\partial P$, so $V$ and $P$ are a **conjugate pair**, and the variance of $V$ can be read either from $A(V, T)$, as $k_BT$ over its curvature, or from $G(P, T)$, as $-k_BT$ times its curvature. (The margin writes $G(P, V)$; the Gibbs potential is a function of $P$ and $T$.)

## Energy fluctuations

Now let a body exchange energy, not volume, with a large heat bath at temperature $T$ (notes p. 46, point ③). When the body has energy $E$, the bath has given up $E$, and its entropy is lower by $E/T$. So, up to a constant, the total entropy of body and bath is

<Formula label="Total entropy of body and bath" tex="S_{\text{tot}} = S(E) - \frac{E}{T}" cite="reading1" at="p. 46" />

and the probability of the energy $E$ is $e^{S_{\text{tot}}/k_B}$, proportional to the number of states of body and bath together. $S_{\text{tot}}$ is largest at $\bar E$, where $dS/dE = 1/T$; expanding to second order, the $-E/T$ term drops out of the curvature:

<Formula label="Probability of an energy" tex="P(E) \propto e^{\frac12\frac{d^2}{dE^2}\left[\frac{1}{k_B}\left(S(E) - \frac{E}{T}\right)\right](E - \bar E)^2} = e^{\frac12k_B^{-1}\frac{d^2S}{dE^2}(E - \bar E)^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 46, point ③" />

$d^2S/dE^2$ is negative (the entropy is concave), so this is a Gaussian, with variance $-k_B/(d^2S/dE^2)$. The curvature of the entropy is a heat capacity: since $dS/dE = 1/T$,

<Formula label="Curvature of the entropy" tex="-\frac{\partial^2S}{\partial E^2} = \frac{1}{T^2}\frac{\partial T}{\partial E} = \frac{1}{T^2C_V}" cite="reading1" at="p. 46, point ③" />

and the energy fluctuations are

<Formula label="Energy fluctuations" tex="\langle(E - \bar E)^2\rangle = -\frac{k_B}{d^2S/dE^2} = k_BT^2C_V" cite="reading1" at="p. 46, point ③" />

How do they grow with the size of the body? Write the entropy and energy per particle, $s = S/N$ and $\epsilon = E/N$ (the notes allow per unit volume too; the argument is the same). Then $d^2S/dE^2 = (1/N)\,d^2s/d\epsilon^2$, and $\langle(E - \bar E)^2\rangle = -Nk_B/(d^2s/d\epsilon^2)$ grows as $N$ (notes p. 46). The spread $\delta E$ grows as $\sqrt N$ while the energy itself grows as $N$:

<Formula label="Relative energy fluctuation" tex="\delta E = \sqrt{\langle(E - \bar E)^2\rangle} = \sqrt{k_BT^2C_V} \sim \sqrt N, \qquad \frac{\delta E}{\bar E} \sim \frac{1}{\sqrt N}" cite="reading1" at="p. 46, point ③" />

so a macroscopic body in a heat bath has, for all practical purposes, a definite energy. (This is also why the canonical ensemble, with its fluctuating energy, gives the same thermodynamics as the microcanonical one with its fixed energy: a remark of ours, not the notes'.)

For a monatomic ideal gas, Wen takes the entropy per particle $s = k_B\ln[v(2m\epsilon)^{3/2}/h^3]$ (up to an added constant, which does not change the derivatives). Only the $\frac32k_B\ln\epsilon$ part depends on $\epsilon$, so

<Formula label="Curvature for the ideal gas" tex="\frac{d^2s}{d\epsilon^2} = -\frac32\,\frac{k_B}{\epsilon^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 46, point ③" />

(The notes leave out the minus sign; it is needed for the variance to be positive.) Then

<Formula label="Energy fluctuation of an ideal gas" tex="\delta E = \sqrt N\,\sqrt{\frac23}\,\epsilon = \sqrt{\frac23}\,\frac{E}{\sqrt N}" cite="reading1" at="p. 46, point ③" />

the same as $\sqrt{k_BT^2C_V}$ with $C_V = \frac32Nk_B$ and $E = \frac32Nk_BT$. The plot shows how the distribution of the energy narrows as the gas grows.

## Fluctuation and response

All these results are one rule (notes p. 47, point ④). Let $x$ be any parameter of a system that can fluctuate, and $A(x)$ the free energy of the system with that value of $x$ (including whatever $x$ exchanges with). The probability of $x$ is $e^{-\beta A(x)}$, and near its minimum $\bar x$ the free energy is a parabola:

<Formula label="Fluctuation of any parameter" tex="P \propto e^{-\beta A(x)}, \qquad A = \text{const} + \frac12\,\frac{\partial^2A}{\partial x^2}(x - \bar x)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \langle(x - \bar x)^2\rangle = \frac{k_BT}{\left.\partial^2A/\partial x^2\right|_{\bar x}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 47, point ④" />

Wen's example is again the volume. A gas pushes on a piston pressed by a fixed outside pressure $P_0$ (Wen draws it as a spring, but writes only the term $P_0V$); the free energy of gas plus piston is $A_{\text{tot}} = A + P_0V$, and since $P_0V$ is linear in $V$ it does not change the curvature:

<Formula label="Volume fluctuations as a response" tex="\langle(V - \bar V)^2\rangle = \frac{k_BT}{\partial^2A_{\text{tot}}/\partial V^2} = \frac{k_BT}{\partial^2A/\partial V^2} = \frac{k_BT}{(-\partial P/\partial V)_T} = k_BT\left(-\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T" cite="reading1" at="p. 47, point ④" />

For the ideal gas $PV = k_BTN$, $-\partial P/\partial V = k_BTN/V^2$, and the variance is $V^2/N$ again, $\Delta V/V = 1/\sqrt N$ (notes p. 47). The left side is a **fluctuation**; the right side contains $-\partial V/\partial P$, a **response**: how much the volume gives when the pressure, which plays the part of a "force" on the volume, is changed.

The bottom of Wen's page draws the general picture. Without a force, $x$ sits in the well of $A(x)$; the thermal energy $k_BT$ lets it climb a little way up the walls, so it wanders over a range $\Delta x$ that is wide when the well is shallow. Now apply a force $F$ conjugate to $x$: the free energy becomes $A - xF$, the parabola tilts, and its minimum moves to

<Formula label="Response to a force" tex="\bar x = K\,F, \qquad K = \frac{1}{\partial^2A/\partial x^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 47, point ④" />

A shallow well both lets $x$ wander far and lets a force move it far. The same number $K$ therefore measures both (notes p. 47, boxed; there, and in the boxes on p. 45 and at the top of p. 47, the square is written outside the average, $\langle(x - \bar x)\rangle^2$, where the average of the square is meant):

<Formula label="Fluctuation equals k_BT times response" tex="\langle\Delta x^2\rangle = k_BT\,K" cite="reading1" at="p. 47, point ④" />

This is the static form of what is usually called the **fluctuation–dissipation theorem** (the name is not in the notes). It works in both directions: from a measured response we can predict the noise, and from measured noise we can find the response without pushing at all.

<WorkedExample title="Thermal noise of an AFM cantilever">
<Problem>
An atomic force microscope senses surfaces with a tiny cantilever, a spring of stiffness $k_s$. (a) For $k_s = 0.1$ N/m at 300 K, find the root-mean-square thermal jitter of its tip, and compare it with the deflection caused by a force of 1 pN. (b) A second cantilever, at the same temperature, is seen to jitter with a root-mean-square of 0.5 nm. What is its stiffness? (c) For comparison, how big are the energy fluctuations of 1 cm³ of helium at 273.15 K and 101 325 Pa, held in a heat bath?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The jitter">
The tip's free energy is $A(x) = \frac12k_sx^2$, so $\partial^2A/\partial x^2 = k_s$ and $K = 1/k_s$. With $k_BT = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 300 \approx 4.142 \times 10^{-21}$ J, $\langle x^2\rangle = k_BT/k_s \approx 4.142 \times 10^{-20}$ m², a root-mean-square of $2.04 \times 10^{-10}$ m, about 0.20 nm.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) The response">
A force of $10^{-12}$ N moves the tip by $KF = 10^{-12}/0.1 = 10^{-11}$ m, 0.01 nm: some 20 times smaller than the thermal jitter. A single reading cannot see such a force; averaging over many readings, or a stiffer or colder cantilever, is needed.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Stiffness from the noise">
Turn the rule round: $k_s = k_BT/\langle x^2\rangle = 4.142 \times 10^{-21}/(0.5 \times 10^{-9})^2 \approx 0.0166$ N/m. Measuring the thermal noise is a common way of finding a cantilever's stiffness without pushing on it.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) A macroscopic gas">
$N = PV/k_BT = 101\,325 \times 10^{-6}/(1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 273.15) \approx 2.69 \times 10^{19}$ atoms, so $\delta E/\bar E = \sqrt{2/(3N)} \approx 1.6 \times 10^{-10}$. A gas of only 1500 atoms, by contrast, fluctuates by about 2.1%.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Thermal noise in the cosmic background">
The photons of the cosmic microwave background are a gas in thermal equilibrium, and the energy rule above tells how much their energy in a region fluctuates if the region exchanges energy with its surroundings. For black-body radiation $E = uV$ with $u \propto T^4$ ([MIT 8.286 Lecture 16](/courses/8-286/black-body-radiation)), so $C_V = 4E/T$ and $\delta E/\bar E = \sqrt{k_BT^2C_V}/E = \sqrt{4k_BT/(uV)}$. Today, at $T = 2.725$ K with $u = 4.17 \times 10^{-14}$ J/m³, a cubic centimetre fluctuates by about 6% (it holds only some 400 photons), but a cube one megaparsec on a side by about $10^{-38}$. The ripples of one part in 100 000 that the CMB shows on far larger scales cannot be thermal noise. [MIT 8.286 Lecture 23](/courses/8-286/inflation-solutions) explains how inflation produces them instead, from quantum fluctuations.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A gas at fixed pressure has probability $\propto e^{-\beta(A + PV)}$ for volume $V$; with $\partial^2A/\partial V^2 = 1/(VK_T)$, $\langle(V - \bar V)^2\rangle = k_BTVK_T$, which is $V^2/N$ for an ideal gas.
- The same follows from $G(P, T) = -k_BT\ln\int dV\,e^{-\beta(A + PV)}$: $\partial G/\partial P = \langle V\rangle$ and $\langle(V - \bar V)^2\rangle = -k_BT\,\partial^2G/\partial P^2$. $V$ and $P$ are conjugate.
- In contact with a heat bath, the total entropy is $S(E) - E/T$, and $\langle(E - \bar E)^2\rangle = k_BT^2C_V$: $\delta E \sim \sqrt N$, $\delta E/\bar E \sim 1/\sqrt N$. For an ideal gas $\delta E = \sqrt{2/3}\,E/\sqrt N$.
- For any fluctuating parameter $x$ with free energy $A(x)$, $\langle(x - \bar x)^2\rangle = k_BT/(\partial^2A/\partial x^2)$. A force $F$ shifts $x$ by $KF$, with $K = 1/(\partial^2A/\partial x^2)$, so $\langle\Delta x^2\rangle = k_BTK$: **fluctuation equals $k_BT$ times response**.
