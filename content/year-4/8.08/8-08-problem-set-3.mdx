This set practises phase transitions: the [Ising model and its mean-field theory](/courses/8-08/ising-model-mean-field) and the [Ginzburg–Landau theory](/courses/8-08/ginzburg-landau-theory) of Chapter III of Wen's notes, which ends with the critical point of water, the end of the [liquid–gas transition](/courses/8-08/van-der-waals-phase-transitions) of Chapter II. The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 3 (dated February 16, 2005), in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its dates are left out. MIT's Problems 1 and 2 are stated in full on the sheet and are given here as MIT sets them, in our words; MIT spells the name "Ginzberg", and we write Ginzburg. Our additions to them are marked where they stand: in Problem 1 the small-field box of part (b), the number box of part (d) and the choice box of part (e); in Problem 2 the choice box of part (b) and the number box of part (d). MIT's Problem 3 is only a pointer to a problem in a textbook (Huang's *Statistical Mechanics*), named by number, with one remark: call the order parameter $m$ rather than $S$, so that it is not confused with the entropy. The textbook is not used here: **Problem 3 is our own problem**, on a subject of Chapter III (a Landau free energy whose transition is first order, with its latent heat), with MIT's 20 points, and it keeps MIT's remark: the order parameter is $m$, and $s$ is an entropy.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `exp(-a1*T)/(1 + h2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. MIT's sheet gives points only for Problem 3, 20 points. The 20 points each of Problems 1 and 2, and every split between parts, are ours: 60 points in all. Parts that ask you to show something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes, with MIT's letters where the sheet has its own. $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature and $\beta = 1/k_BT$. In Problem 1, $S$ is the value of a spin 1 (not an entropy), $h$ is the magnetic field times the magnetic moment, an energy, and $J > 0$ is the coupling between neighbours. In Problem 2, $G$ is the Gibbs potential, $P$ the pressure, $n$ the number density of water molecules and $\delta n$ its difference from the density at the critical point. In Problem 3, $m$ is the order parameter, $f$ the free energy per unit volume and $s$ the entropy per unit volume. A subscript $c$ marks the critical point (Problems 1 and 2) or the transition (Problem 3).

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A chain of spin-1 spins in mean-field theory" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

A spin 1 has three states: its value is $S = +1$, $0$ or $-1$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

One spin 1 sits in a magnetic field, with the energy $E = -hS$, in contact with a heat bath at the temperature $T$. Find its average value $\langle S\rangle$.

<Hint>

Use the canonical ensemble: each of the three states has the probability $e^{-\beta E}/Z$. Write down the three energies first.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The state $S = 0$ has the energy zero, so its Boltzmann factor is 1. It adds nothing to the numerator of $\langle S\rangle$, but it does add to $Z$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-avg" label="$\langle S\rangle =$" expr="2*sinh(h/(kB*T))/(1 + 2*cosh(h/(kB*T)))"
  vars={{ h: [0.3, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ h: "h", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "tanh(h/(kB*T))", hint: "That is the average of a two-state spin, S = ±1 only. A spin 1 also has the state S = 0, whose Boltzmann factor 1 adds to the partition function." },
    { expr: "-2*sinh(h/(kB*T))/(1 + 2*cosh(h/(kB*T)))", hint: "The sign is reversed. With E = −hS, the state S = +1 has the lower energy when h > 0, so it is the more likely one and ⟨S⟩ is positive." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Three states with the energies $-h$, $0$ and $+h$: weight each by its Boltzmann factor.

<Step title="The partition function">

<Formula label="Partition function of one spin 1" tex="Z_1 = e^{\beta h} + 1 + e^{-\beta h} = 1 + 2\cosh\beta h" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The average">

<Formula label="Average of a spin 1 in a field" tex="\langle S\rangle = \frac{(+1)\,e^{\beta h} + 0 + (-1)\,e^{-\beta h}}{Z_1} = \frac{2\sinh\beta h}{1 + 2\cosh\beta h}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the limits">

In a strong field, $\beta h \to \infty$, the ratio tends to $e^{\beta h}/e^{\beta h} = 1$: the spin sits in $S = +1$. In zero field it is 0, as the symmetry $S \to -S$ requires. And at $\beta h = 1$ it is $2 \times 1.1752/(1 + 2 \times 1.5431) = 0.575$, less than the $\tanh 1 = 0.762$ of a two-state spin: the state $S = 0$ pulls the average down.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

(Our addition.) In a weak field, $\beta h \ll 1$, the average is proportional to $h$. Find the ratio $\langle S\rangle/h$ in this limit. You will need it in part (c).

<Hint>

Expand $\sinh x \approx x$ and $\cosh x \approx 1$ to the lowest order in $x = \beta h$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-curie" label="$\langle S\rangle/h \approx$" expr="2/(3*kB*T)"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/(kB*T)", hint: "That is the slope of tanh(βh), the two-state spin. For a spin 1 the denominator 1 + 2cosh βh is 3 at small field, not 2." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Keep only the first order in $\beta h$.

<Step title="Expand">

$\langle S\rangle \approx 2\beta h/(1 + 2) = \frac23\beta h$, so $\langle S\rangle/h \approx 2/(3k_BT)$: a Curie law, with $\frac23$ of the slope of a two-state spin. The factor $\frac23$ is the average of $S^2$ over the three states, $(1 + 0 + 1)/3$, as the general rule $\langle S\rangle \approx \beta h\,\overline{S^2}$ for a weak field says.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={7}>

Now take a one-dimensional Ising model of $N$ spins 1, $S_i = 0, \pm 1$, with the energy

<Formula label="Energy of the spin-1 Ising chain" tex="E = -J\sum_{i=1}^{N} S_iS_{i+1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

and $J > 0$ (our assumption, which the sum running up to the spin number N + 1 suggests: the last spin is coupled back to the first, so the chain is closed into a ring, which changes nothing for large $N$). Use mean-field theory to find the critical temperature $T_c$ below which the chain magnetizes by itself, with $\langle S\rangle \neq 0$ at zero field. Type $k_B$ as `kB`.

<Hint>

Replace each product by its mean-field form, as in the lesson: $S_iS_{i+1} \approx \langle S\rangle S_i + \langle S\rangle S_{i+1} - \langle S\rangle^2$. How many terms of the sum contain a given spin $S_i$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each spin then sits in an effective field $h_{\text{eff}}$ proportional to $\langle S\rangle$, and its average is the result of part (a) with $h \to h_{\text{eff}}$. A non-zero solution of this self-consistent equation appears when the slope of its right-hand side at $\langle S\rangle = 0$ passes 1: use part (b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-tc" label="$T_c =$" expr="4*J/(3*kB)"
  vars={{ J: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ J: "J", kB: "k_B" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*J/kB", hint: "That is the mean-field T_c of a chain of two-state spins S = ±1, where the slope of tanh is 1. For a spin 1 the slope of ⟨S⟩ at small field is only 2/3 of that, so T_c is lower by the same factor." },
    { expr: "2*J/(3*kB)", hint: "That gives each spin one neighbour. In the sum over i, the spin S_i appears twice, in S_(i−1)S_i and in S_iS_(i+1), so its effective field is 2J⟨S⟩." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In mean-field theory each spin feels only the average of its two neighbours. That turns the chain into independent spins 1 in an effective field, and $\langle S\rangle$ must reproduce itself.

<Step title="The mean-field energy">

The approximation drops only the product of the two fluctuations:

<Formula label="The mean-field approximation" tex="S_iS_{i+1} \approx \langle S\rangle S_i + \langle S\rangle S_{i+1} - \langle S\rangle^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 22, point ② (written there for s = ±1)" />

On a ring each spin appears in two terms of the sum, so the coordination number is $z = 2$, and

<Formula label="Mean-field energy of the chain" tex="E_{\text{mean}} = -2J\langle S\rangle\sum_{i=1}^{N} S_i + NJ\langle S\rangle^2, \qquad h_{\text{eff}} = 2J\langle S\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The self-consistent equation">

Each spin is now a spin 1 in the field $h_{\text{eff}}$, so part (a) gives

<Formula label="Self-consistent equation for spin 1" tex="\langle S\rangle = \frac{2\sinh(2\beta J\langle S\rangle)}{1 + 2\cosh(2\beta J\langle S\rangle)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Where a non-zero solution appears">

The right-hand side starts at zero with the slope $\frac23 \times 2\beta J = \frac43\beta J$ (part (b)) and then bends over towards 1. As for the tanh of the lesson, a second crossing with the line $\langle S\rangle$ exists when this slope is more than 1. The transition is where $\frac43 J/k_BT = 1$:

<Formula label="Mean-field critical temperature of the spin-1 chain" tex="T_c = \frac{4J}{3k_B}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The magnetization rises continuously from zero below $T_c$: expanding the right-hand side to third order in $x = 2\beta J\langle S\rangle$ gives $\frac23(x - x^3/6)$, and the negative cubic term bends the curve below the line again, as tanh does.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The general mean-field rule is $k_BT_c = zJ\,\overline{S^2}$: $z = 2$ and $\overline{S^2} = \frac23$ give $\frac43 J$, and for two-state spins, $\overline{S^2} = 1$, it gives the $zJ$ of the lesson.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

(Our addition.) Solve the self-consistent equation of part (c) numerically at $T = T_c/2$. Give the positive solution $\langle S\rangle$ to three decimals (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

At $T = T_c/2$ the argument is $2\beta J\langle S\rangle = 3\langle S\rangle$. Iterate $\langle S\rangle \to 2\sinh(3\langle S\rangle)/[1 + 2\cosh(3\langle S\rangle)]$ starting from 1, or find the crossing by bisection.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-num" label="$\langle S\rangle$ at $T_c/2$:" value="0.937" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write the argument in units of $T_c$ and solve by iteration.

<Step title="The argument">

$2\beta J = 2J/k_BT = \frac32\,T_c/T$, which is 3 at $T = T_c/2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Iterate">

From $\langle S\rangle = 1$: $2\sinh 3/(1 + 2\cosh 3) = 20.036/21.135 = 0.948$, then $0.939$, and the iteration settles at $\langle S\rangle = 0.937$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A chain of two-state spins ($S = \pm 1$ only) at half its own $T_c$ solves $\langle S\rangle = \tanh 2\langle S\rangle$, which gives 0.958: the spin 1 is a little less ordered at the same fraction of its $T_c$, because the state $S = 0$ is still occupied now and then.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={3}>

(Our addition.) Mean-field theory says the chain orders below $T_c$. What does the exact statistical mechanics of this chain (one dimension, coupling only between neighbours) say at any temperature $T > 0$ and zero field?

<Answer id="p1-exact" label="The exact chain" choices={["orders below 4J/(3k_B), as mean-field theory says", "orders as well, but below a lower temperature", "never orders: ⟨S⟩ = 0 at any temperature T > 0", "orders at every temperature, however high"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A break in the order costs a fixed energy, but it can sit anywhere along the chain, so its entropy grows with the length. At any $T > 0$ the entropy wins.

<Step title="The cost of a break">

Take a long chain with all spins $+1$ and let the spins from some site onwards be $-1$. Only the one bond at the break changes, from $-J$ to $+J$: the energy rises by $2J$, whatever $N$ is. The break can sit at any of about $N$ places, so the entropy rises by $k_B\ln N$. The free energy changes by $2J - k_BT\ln N$, which is negative for any $T > 0$ once $N$ is large enough: breaks always appear, many of them, and they cut the chain into domains of both signs. The average is zero. (A step from $+1$ to $0$ costs only $J$, which helps disorder even more.)

</Step>

<Step title="The exact statement">

Exactly, the partition function of the ring is the trace of the $N$th power of a $3 \times 3$ transfer matrix, whose entries are the Boltzmann factors of one bond for the nine pairs of neighbouring values. All its entries are positive, so its largest eigenvalue is single and depends smoothly on $T$, and so does the free energy per spin: no phase transition at any $T > 0$. Mean-field theory fails here because a spin with only two neighbours is far from their average; this is what the lesson says about the two-state chain, and it holds for spin 1 as well.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The critical point of water in Ginzburg–Landau theory" points={20} mit="Problem 2">

Near its critical point, the transition between water and vapour can be described by a Ginzburg–Landau free energy, or more precisely a Gibbs potential, as a function of the density:

<Formula label="Ginzburg–Landau Gibbs potential of water" tex="G = (h_1T - h_2P)\,\delta n + (-a_1 + a_2T + a_3P)\,\delta n^2 + b\,\delta n^4" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1" />

All the constants $h_1$, $h_2$, $a_1$, $a_2$, $a_3$ and $b$ are positive. Here $\delta n = n - n_c$, where $n$ is the number density of water molecules and $n_c$ the density at the critical point $(T_c, P_c)$. At given $T$ and $P$, the equilibrium density is the one that minimizes $G$.

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Find the temperature $T_c$ and the pressure $P_c$ of the critical point in terms of the constants. (MIT's sheet asks for them from $h_1$, $h_2$, $a_1$, $a_2$ and $b$; in fact $a_3$ enters as well and $b$ does not, as the solution shows.)

<Hint>

Compare with the magnet of the lesson: the first term plays the part of the "field" and the second that of the quadratic coefficient. At the critical point both must vanish.

</Hint>

<Hint>

That gives two linear equations, $h_1T = h_2P$ and $a_2T + a_3P = a_1$. Use the first to eliminate $P$ from the second.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-tc" label="$T_c =$" expr="a1*h2/(a2*h2 + a3*h1)"
  vars={{ a1: [0.5, 2], a2: [0.5, 2], a3: [0.5, 2], h1: [0.5, 2], h2: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ a1: "a_1", a2: "a_2", a3: "a_3", h1: "h_1", h2: "h_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "a1/a2", hint: "That leaves out the a₃P term. At the critical point the pressure is P_c, not zero, so the pressure part of the δn² coefficient counts too." },
    { expr: "a1*h1/(a2*h2 + a3*h1)", hint: "That is P_c, not T_c. At the critical point h₁T = h₂P, so T_c = (h₂/h₁)P_c." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-pc" label="$P_c =$" expr="a1*h1/(a2*h2 + a3*h1)"
  vars={{ a1: [0.5, 2], a2: [0.5, 2], a3: [0.5, 2], h1: [0.5, 2], h2: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ a1: "a_1", a2: "a_2", a3: "a_3", h1: "h_1", h2: "h_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "a1/a3", hint: "That leaves out the a₂T term. At the critical point the temperature is T_c, not zero, so the temperature part of the δn² coefficient counts too." },
    { expr: "a1*h2/(a2*h2 + a3*h1)", hint: "That is T_c, not P_c. At the critical point h₁T = h₂P, so P_c = (h₁/h₂)T_c." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in the lesson's water example, the critical point is where the double well of $G(\delta n)$ turns into a single flat one: the linear coefficient (the "field") and the quadratic coefficient both vanish there.

<Step title="Two conditions">

<Formula label="Conditions for the critical point" tex="h(P_c, T_c) = 0, \qquad r_0(P_c, T_c) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 27, point ⑤" />

Here $h(T, P) = h_1T - h_2P$ and $r_0(T, P) = -a_1 + a_2T + a_3P$, so

<Formula label="The critical point" tex="h_1T_c = h_2P_c, \qquad a_2T_c + a_3P_c = a_1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

Put $P_c = (h_1/h_2)T_c$ into the second equation: $T_c(a_2 + a_3h_1/h_2) = a_1$, so

<Formula label="Critical temperature and pressure" tex="T_c = \frac{a_1h_2}{a_2h_2 + a_3h_1}, \qquad P_c = \frac{a_1h_1}{a_2h_2 + a_3h_1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The quartic coefficient $b$ does not enter: it only has to be positive, so that $G$ is bounded below.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Put both back: $h_1T_c - h_2P_c = (a_1h_1h_2 - a_1h_1h_2)/(\ldots) = 0$, and $a_2T_c + a_3P_c = a_1(a_2h_2 + a_3h_1)/(a_2h_2 + a_3h_1) = a_1$. Both are positive, as a critical temperature and pressure must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the line of first-order transitions between water and vapour in the $T$–$P$ plane, and sketch it. Give the pressure on the line as a function of $T$, and say where the line ends.

<Hint>

On the line the two phases coexist: $G(\delta n)$ has two minima that are equally deep. When is a function $r_0\,\delta n^2 + b\,\delta n^4 + h\,\delta n$ with two minima symmetric between them?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Two minima need $r_0 < 0$; equally deep ones need the odd term to vanish.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-line" label="On the transition line, $P =$" expr="h1*T/h2"
  vars={{ h1: [0.5, 2], h2: [0.5, 2], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ h1: "h_1", h2: "h_2", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "h2*T/h1", hint: "The ratio is upside down. The line is where h₁T − h₂P = 0, so P = (h₁/h₂)T." }
  ]} />

(Our addition.) Just above the line, at a slightly higher pressure than on it at the same temperature, which phase is stable?

<Answer id="p2-side" label="Just above the line, the stable phase is" choices={["vapour, because there the minimum has δn < 0", "water, because there the minimum has δn > 0", "both: water and vapour coexist there", "neither: the fluid there is above its critical point"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The magnet of the lesson has its first-order line where the field vanishes below $T_c$. Here the "field" is $h_1T - h_2P$, and "below $T_c$" means a negative $\delta n^2$ coefficient.

<Step title="The line">

With $r_0 < 0$, $G(\delta n)$ is a double well. With $h_1T - h_2P = 0$ it is even in $\delta n$, and its two minima $\pm\delta n_0$ are equally deep: water and vapour coexist. With $h_1T - h_2P \neq 0$ the odd term tilts the well and one phase wins. So the first-order line is

<Formula label="The first-order line" tex="P = \frac{h_1}{h_2}\,T, \qquad T < T_c" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

On this line $r_0 = -a_1 + (a_2 + a_3h_1/h_2)T$, which is negative exactly when $T < T_c$. The line ends at the critical point $(T_c, P_c)$, which lies on it, since $P_c/T_c = h_1/h_2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Which side is water">

Above the line, $h_1T - h_2P < 0$, so the linear term $(h_1T - h_2P)\,\delta n$ is lowered by a positive $\delta n$: the deeper minimum has $\delta n > 0$, the denser phase, water. Below the line the vapour wins. Raising the pressure condenses the vapour, as it should.

</Step>

<Step title="The sketch">

In units of the critical point the line is $P/P_c = T/T_c$, a straight line ending at $(1, 1)$:

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0.8, 1.15]} range={[0.75, 1.2]} xLabel="T/T_c" yLabel="P/P_c" samples={200}
  curves={[

  ]}
  points={[{ x: 1, y: 1, label: "critical point" }]}

The straight line is only the model's description near the critical point: the real boiling curve of water bends." />

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Find the change of the density across the first-order line, $\Delta n$, the density of the water minus that of the vapour, between the two phases that coexist at a temperature $T < T_c$ on the line. Write it with $T$, $T_c$, $b$, $a_2$, $a_3$, $h_1$ and $h_2$: use $T_c$ in place of $a_1$, and the line to eliminate $P$.

<Hint>

On the line $G = r_0\,\delta n^2 + b\,\delta n^4$ with $r_0 < 0$. Set $dG/d\,\delta n = 0$ to find the two minima.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The minima are at $\pm\delta n_0$, so $\Delta n = 2\delta n_0$. Along the line $P = (h_1/h_2)T$, so $r_0$ is a multiple of $T - T_c$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-dn" label="$\Delta n =$" expr="sqrt(2*(a2 + a3*h1/h2)*(Tc - T)/b)"
  vars={{ a2: [0.5, 2], a3: [0.5, 2], h1: [0.5, 2], h2: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2], Tc: [2, 3], T: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  names={{ a2: "a_2", a3: "a_3", h1: "h_1", h2: "h_2", b: "b", Tc: "T_c", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt((a2 + a3*h1/h2)*(Tc - T)/(2*b))", hint: "That is δn₀, the distance of one phase from n_c. The density jumps from −δn₀ to +δn₀, twice as far." },

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the line the potential is a symmetric double well, and the jump is the distance between its two minima.

<Step title="The minima">

$dG/d\,\delta n = 2r_0\,\delta n + 4b\,\delta n^3 = 0$ gives $\delta n = 0$ (the maximum) or $\delta n_0^2 = -r_0/(2b)$. The water has $+\delta n_0$ and the vapour $-\delta n_0$, so

<Formula label="Density jump" tex="\Delta n = 2\delta n_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2\,(a_1 - a_2T - a_3P)}{b}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Along the line">

With $P = (h_1/h_2)T$ and $a_1 = (a_2 + a_3h_1/h_2)T_c$ from part (a), $a_1 - a_2T - a_3P = (a_2 + a_3h_1/h_2)(T_c - T)$:

<Formula label="Density jump along the line" tex="\Delta n = \sqrt{\frac{2}{b}\left(a_2 + \frac{a_3h_1}{h_2}\right)(T_c - T)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$\Delta n$ falls to zero at $T_c$, where water and vapour become the same fluid, and is real only below it. It has the form of the lesson's jump in magnetization per spin: twice the square root of minus the quadratic coefficient over the quartic one, once the factors $\frac12$ and $\frac14$ of the lesson's free energy are allowed for.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

(Our addition.) Close to the critical point $\Delta n \propto (T_c - T)^{\beta}$. Give the exponent $\beta$ as a decimal (tolerance 1%).

<Answer id="p2-beta" label="$\beta =$" value="0.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-beta" />

Fluctuations, left out by Ginzburg–Landau theory, change the exponent, but in the same way for the magnet and the fluid.)

</Step>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A first-order transition in Landau theory (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 3">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, with the remark that the order parameter is better called $m$ than $S$, so that it is not confused with the entropy. The problem below is our own, on a subject of Chapter III of the notes, and it follows that remark.

When the fourth-order coefficient of a Ginzburg–Landau free energy is negative, a sixth-order term is needed to keep the free energy bounded, and the transition becomes first order. Take the free energy per unit volume, as a function of the order parameter $m$ and the temperature $T$,
$f(m, T) = f_0(T) + \frac12 a_0(T - T_0)\,m^2 - \frac14 b\,m^4 + \frac16 c\,m^6$
(our own model, stated by us), with constants $a_0 > 0$, $b > 0$ and $c > 0$, and a smooth $f_0(T)$ that does not depend on $m$. The equilibrium state minimizes $f$ at each $T$. The entropy per unit volume is $s = -\partial f/\partial T$, taken at the equilibrium value of $m$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the stationary points of $f$ in $m$. Above which temperature $T_1$ is $m = 0$ the only one, so that no ordered state, stable or metastable, exists?

<Hint>

$\partial f/\partial m = m\,[a_0(T - T_0) - b\,m^2 + c\,m^4]$. Besides $m = 0$, the bracket is a quadratic equation in $m^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The quadratic in $m^2$ has real roots only while its discriminant is positive.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-t1" label="$T_1 =$" expr="T0 + b^2/(4*a0*c)"
  vars={{ T0: [1, 3], a0: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2], c: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ T0: "T_0", a0: "a_0", b: "b", c: "c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "T0", hint: "T₀ is where m = 0 stops being a minimum, because the m² coefficient changes sign there. The ordered minima, held up by the negative m⁴ term, exist above T₀ as well." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The non-zero stationary points are the roots of a quadratic in $m^2$; they exist as long as its discriminant is positive.

<Step title="The stationary points">

Write $\alpha = a_0(T - T_0)$. Then $\partial f/\partial m = m(\alpha - b\,m^2 + c\,m^4) = 0$ gives $m = 0$ or

<Formula label="Non-zero stationary points" tex="m^2 = \frac{b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4\alpha c}}{2c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The $+$ root is a minimum of $f$ (the ordered state) and the $-$ root, where it is positive, a maximum between it and $m = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The highest temperature">

The roots are real while $b^2 > 4\alpha c$, that is, below

<Formula label="Upper limit of the ordered state" tex="T_1 = T_0 + \frac{b^2}{4a_0c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

At $T_1$ the minimum and the maximum merge at $m^2 = b/(2c)$ and disappear.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $T_0 < T < T_1$ both $\alpha > 0$ (so $m = 0$ is a local minimum) and the ordered minimum exist: two minima, as a first-order transition needs. Below $T_0$, $m = 0$ is a maximum and only the ordered state is left.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

The transition happens at the temperature $T_c$ where the ordered minimum is exactly as deep as $f$ at $m = 0$. Find $T_c$, and the value $m_c > 0$ to which the order parameter jumps there.

<Hint>

At $T_c$ two conditions hold at $m_c$: $f(m_c) = f(0)$ and $\partial f/\partial m = 0$. Divide the first by $m^2$ and the second by $m$, and eliminate $\alpha = a_0(T - T_0)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The two equations are $\frac12\alpha - \frac14 b\,m^2 + \frac16 c\,m^4 = 0$ and $\alpha - b\,m^2 + c\,m^4 = 0$. Subtract half the second from the first.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-tc" label="$T_c =$" expr="T0 + 3*b^2/(16*a0*c)"
  vars={{ T0: [1, 3], a0: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2], c: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ T0: "T_0", a0: "a_0", b: "b", c: "c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "T0 + b^2/(4*a0*c)", hint: "That is T₁, where the ordered minimum first appears. There it is still higher than f at m = 0; it becomes as deep only at a lower temperature." },
    { expr: "T0", hint: "At T₀ the state m = 0 stops being a minimum, but the ordered state has already been the deeper one for a while above T₀." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-mc" label="$m_c =$" expr="sqrt(3*b/(4*c))"
  vars={{ b: [0.5, 2], c: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ b: "b", c: "c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(b/(2*c))", hint: "That is the value of m at T₁, where the minimum and the maximum merge. At T_c the minimum lies further out." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two equal minima: the ordered one must be stationary and as deep as $f(0) = f_0$.

<Step title="Two conditions">

With $\alpha = a_0(T - T_0)$,

<Formula label="Conditions at the transition" tex="\tfrac12\alpha - \tfrac14 b\,m_c^2 + \tfrac16 c\,m_c^4 = 0, \qquad \alpha - b\,m_c^2 + c\,m_c^4 = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Subtracting half the second from the first leaves $\frac14 b\,m_c^2 - \frac13 c\,m_c^4 = 0$, so $m_c^2 = 3b/(4c)$. Then $\alpha = b\,m_c^2 - c\,m_c^4 = \frac{3b^2}{4c} - \frac{9b^2}{16c} = \frac{3b^2}{16c}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The results">

<Formula label="Transition temperature and jump of the order parameter" tex="T_c = T_0 + \frac{3b^2}{16a_0c}, \qquad m_c = \sqrt{\frac{3b}{4c}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$T_0 < T_c < T_1$, since $\frac{3}{16} < \frac14$, as the two minima of part (a) require; and $m_c^2 = \frac{3b}{4c}$ is larger than the $\frac{b}{2c}$ at $T_1$. The figure shows the three temperatures.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Find the latent heat per unit volume, $L = T_c\,\Delta s$, absorbed when the ordered phase turns into the disordered one at $T_c$. Write it with $a_0$, $b$, $c$ and $T_c$.

<Hint>

At fixed $m$, $\partial f/\partial T = f_0'(T) + \frac12 a_0 m^2$. Since $\partial f/\partial m = 0$ in each phase, this is also the total derivative of the equilibrium $f$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The two phases differ only by the $m^2$ term: $\Delta s = s(m = 0) - s(m_c)$. Use $m_c^2$ from part (b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-l" label="$L =$" expr="3*a0*b*Tc/(8*c)"
  vars={{ a0: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2], c: [0.5, 2], Tc: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ a0: "a_0", b: "b", c: "c", Tc: "T_c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*a0*b*Tc/(4*c)", hint: "That uses a₀m_c² for the entropy jump. The m² term of f is ½a₀(T − T₀)m², so its T-derivative gives Δs = ½a₀m_c²." },
    { expr: "3*a0*b/(8*c)", hint: "That is the entropy jump Δs per unit volume. The latent heat is T_c Δs." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The latent heat is $T\Delta S$ (notes p. 26, point ④, where the symmetric case gives zero). Here the two phases are not related by a symmetry, and the ordered one has a lower entropy.

<Step title="The entropies">

In each phase $\partial f/\partial m = 0$, so differentiating the equilibrium $f$ with respect to $T$ needs only the explicit $T$: $s = -f_0'(T) - \frac12 a_0 m^2$. The disordered phase has $m = 0$; the ordered phase at $T_c$ has $m = m_c$:

<Formula label="Entropy jump and latent heat" tex="\Delta s = \tfrac12 a_0m_c^2 = \frac{3a_0b}{8c}, \qquad L = T_c\,\Delta s = \frac{3a_0bT_c}{8c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$L > 0$: heat is absorbed when the ordered phase disorders on heating, as when ice melts. As $b \to 0$, $m_c \to 0$ and $L \to 0$: the transition becomes continuous, the case of the lesson, which has no latent heat.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

(Our numbers.) Take $T_0 = 300$ K and $b^2/(a_0c) = 40.0$ K. Give, in kelvin, how far above $T_0$ the transition lies, $T_c - T_0$, and how far above $T_0$ the ordered state can still exist as a metastable state when the sample is heated, $T_1 - T_0$ (tolerance 1% each).

<Hint>

Put the numbers into parts (a) and (b). Only the combination $b^2/(a_0c)$ is needed.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-num-tc" label="$T_c - T_0$ in K:" value="7.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-num-tc" />

<Answer id="p3-num-t1" label="$T_1 - T_0$ in K:" value="10.0" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-num-t1" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two temperatures are fixed fractions of $b^2/(a_0c)$.

<Step title="The numbers">

$T_c - T_0 = \frac{3}{16} \times 40.0$ K $= 7.50$ K and $T_1 - T_0 = \frac14 \times 40.0$ K $= 10.0$ K. So the transition is at $307.5$ K. A sample heated without any seeds of the disordered phase can stay ordered up to $310.0$ K; one cooled without seeds of the ordered phase can stay disordered down to $T_0 = 300$ K, where $m = 0$ stops being a minimum. That is a window of hysteresis 10 K wide.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

Make $b$ smaller and smaller, $b \to 0^+$, with $a_0$ and $c$ fixed. What happens to the transition?

<Answer id="p3-limit" label="As b goes to zero," choices={["T_c moves down to T₀, and the jump in m and the latent heat both go to zero", "T_c moves up, away from T₀, and the jump in m and the latent heat both grow", "T_c moves down to T₀, but the jump in m stays finite", "nothing: T_c, the jump and the latent heat do not depend on b"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Read the $b$-dependence off parts (b) and (c).

<Step title="The limit">

$T_c - T_0 \propto b^2$, $m_c \propto \sqrt b$ and $L \propto b$ all go to zero. At $b = 0$ the free energy is $\frac12 a_0(T - T_0)m^2 + \frac16 c\,m^6$, and $m$ grows continuously from zero below $T_0$: the first-order transition has turned into a continuous one at $T_0$. (The point $b = 0$, where the two kinds of transition meet, is called a tricritical point.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
