The [first page of Chapter IX](/courses/8-08/mean-free-path-sound-diffusion) found that particles diffuse with $D \simeq \lambda\bar v/6$. The same argument works for anything the particles carry. Carried momentum is **viscosity**, carried energy is **heat conduction**, carried charge is **electric current**. This page follows Wen's notes pp. 102–113: viscosity and the Navier–Stokes equation, the Reynolds number, heat conduction and the speed at which water freezes, then electrons in a metal, first in the simple Drude model, with the Hall effect, and then with the Boltzmann equation, which gives the conductivity again and the thermopower besides. The free electron gas, with its Fermi energy and Fermi function, comes from [8.044](/courses/8-044/free-fermi-gas). (The handwritten page numbers jump from 111 to 113; nothing seems to be missing from the argument.) Everything here is in our own words, with our own examples.

## Viscosity: momentum carried by diffusion

Take a gas flowing in the $x$ direction with a speed $u_x$ that changes with height $y$ (notes p. 102). Particles crossing a plane of constant $y$ carry their $x$ momentum $mu_x$ with them; those from below carry the momentum of the layer below, those from above that of the layer above. The flux of $x$ momentum across the plane is a force per unit area on it, and by the six-direction argument of the diffusion estimate,

<Formula label="Flux of x momentum" tex="F_x = \frac{1}{6} n\bar v\,\left[mu_x(y - \lambda) - mu_x(y + \lambda)\right] = -\frac{1}{6} nm\bar v\lambda\,\frac{\partial u_x}{\partial y}" cite="reading1" at="p. 102" />

where $u_x(y - \lambda)$ and $u_x(y + \lambda)$ are the flow speeds one mean free path below and above. This is the definition of the **viscosity** $\nu$: the force in the $x$ direction on a surface normal to $y$ is $F_x = -\nu\,\partial u_x/\partial y$, with (notes p. 102)

<Formula label="Viscosity of a gas" tex="\nu = \frac{1}{6} n\bar vm\lambda = \frac{m\bar v}{6\sigma} = \frac{\sqrt{2mk_BT}}{6\sigma}" cite="reading1" at="p. 102" />

using $\lambda = 1/(\sigma n)$ and $\bar v = \sqrt{2k_BT/m}$. (The notes write $3\sigma$ in the denominator; with $\nu = \frac{1}{6} n\bar vm\lambda$ and $n\lambda = 1/\sigma$ it is $6\sigma$. Wen writes the viscosity as $\nu$, where many books use the letter eta or mu.) The striking result, noted by Wen, is that $\nu$ is **independent of the density**: a denser gas has more particles to carry momentum, but each carries it a shorter distance. For the argon of the previous page, $\nu \approx 9.6 \times 10^{-6}$ Pa s; the measured value is about $2.3 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa s, so the simple estimate is good to a factor of about 2.4.

In general a force on a small surface has three components and the surface has three orientations, so the forces form a **pressure tensor** $P_{ij}$: the force on a surface of area $A$ normal to the $i$ axis has components $AP_{ij}$, $j = 1, 2, 3$ (notes p. 102). It splits into the usual pressure and a traceless viscous part:

<Formula label="Pressure tensor" tex="P_{ij} = \delta_{ij}P + P'_{ij}, \qquad P'_{ij} = -\nu\left(\frac{\partial u_i}{\partial x_j} + \frac{\partial u_j}{\partial x_i} - \frac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\nabla\cdot\vec u\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 102" />

For the shear flow above, the component of $P'_{ij}$ with one index $x$ and the other $y$ is $-\nu\,\partial u_x/\partial y$, the force we found.

## The Navier–Stokes equation and the Reynolds number

The mass $\Delta V\rho$ of a small box, times its acceleration, equals the net force from the pressure tensor on its faces, which per unit volume is $-\partial P_{ij}/\partial x_j$ (notes p. 103). So $\rho(\partial/\partial t + \vec u\cdot\nabla)u_i + \partial_jP_{ij} = 0$, and with the viscous stress written out, plus the external force, this is the **Navier–Stokes equation**:

<Formula label="Navier–Stokes equation" tex="\rho\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \vec u\cdot\nabla\right)\vec u + \nabla\left(P - \frac{\nu}{3}\nabla\cdot\vec u\right) - \nu\nabla^2\vec u = n\vec f_\text{ex}" cite="reading1" at="p. 103" />

Together with the continuity equation and the adiabatic condition of the previous page it determines the flow, for example of a gas through a pipe of width $L$ entering at speed $u_0$ (notes p. 103). The inputs are $u_0$, $L$, $\nu$ and $\rho$. Since any quantity can be set to 1 by a choice of units, only a dimensionless combination of them can decide what the flow looks like. The viscosity is a force per area divided by a velocity per length, so its units are mass/(time × length), and the one dimensionless combination is the **Reynolds number** (notes p. 103):

<Formula label="Reynolds number" tex="R = \frac{\rho Lu_0}{\nu}" cite="reading1" at="p. 103" />

For $R \gg 1$ the flow is **turbulent**; for $R \ll 1$ it is smooth **streamline** flow, dominated by viscosity (notes p. 103). Honey crawling down a spoon has a tiny $R$; air round a moving car has $R$ in the millions.

## Heat conduction

Heat conduction is energy carried without a net transport of particles (notes p. 104). In a gas with a temperature gradient the densities and speeds on the two sides adjust so that the particle flux vanishes, $j_x = \frac{1}{6}(n_1\bar v_1 - n_2\bar v_2) = 0$, but the particles from the hot side carry more energy than those from the cold side. Each carries a mean energy $c_vT$, with $c_v$ the heat capacity per particle ($\frac{3}{2}k_B$ for a point particle with three degrees of freedom), so the heat flux is

<Formula label="Heat flux" tex="q_x = \frac{1}{6} n\bar v\,c_v(T_1 - T_2) = -\frac{1}{6}\lambda n\bar vc_v\,\partial T/\partial x" cite="reading1" at="p. 104" />

with $T_1 - T_2 = -\lambda\,\partial T/\partial x$ again across one mean free path. (Wen writes the energy as $l\,k_BT/2$ for $l$ degrees of freedom, so $c_v = lk_B/2$; we write $c_v$ throughout.) This is **Fourier's law** $\vec q = -K\nabla T$, with the **heat conductivity** (notes p. 104)

<Formula label="Heat conductivity of a gas" tex="K = \frac{1}{6} n\bar v\lambda c_v = \frac{c_v}{6\sigma}\sqrt{\frac{2k_BT}{m}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 104" />

again independent of $n$. (The notes write $3\sigma$ here too, for the same reason as for $\nu$.) Energy conservation, $\partial\mathcal U/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\vec q = 0$ with energy density $\mathcal U = nc_vT$, then gives the **heat equation**, and with a heat source $W$ per unit volume and time its general form (notes p. 104):

<Formula label="Heat equation" tex="\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} - \frac{K}{nc_v}\nabla^2T = 0, \qquad nc_v\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} - K\nabla^2T = W" cite="reading1" at="p. 104" />

It is a diffusion equation for temperature, with diffusion constant $K/nc_v = \lambda\bar v/6$, the same as for particles.

## How fast a pond freezes

Wen applies heat conduction to a sheet of ice on water (notes p. 105). The water is at the freezing point $T_0 = 273$ K, the air at $T_0 - \Delta T$, and the ice between them has grown to a thickness $x$. Heat flows up through the ice at the rate $q = K\Delta T/x$ per unit area. That heat comes from freezing: with $\ell = \Delta S\,T_0$ the latent heat per molecule ($\Delta S$ the entropy lost per molecule on freezing) and $n$ the number density, growing the ice by $dx$ releases $n\ell\,dx$ per unit area. So

<Formula label="Growth of the ice" tex="\frac{dx}{dt}\,n\ell = K\frac{\Delta T}{x} \quad\Rightarrow\quad x^2 = \frac{2K\Delta T}{n\ell}\,t, \qquad x = \sqrt{\frac{2K\Delta T}{n\ell}}\,\sqrt t" cite="reading1" at="p. 105" />

The ice slows its own growth: the thicker it is, the worse it lets the heat out, and $x$ grows as $\sqrt t$, like a diffusion distance.

Then Wen estimates the numbers (notes p. 105). For the entropy he uses the ideal-gas form $S = k_B[\frac{5}{2} + \ln(v/\lambda_T^3)]$ per molecule, with $v$ the volume per molecule, and assumes the volume per molecule in water is $e$ times that in ice; then $\Delta S = k_B$ and $\ell = k_BT_0$. (This is only for the estimate: water is in fact denser than ice, and the measured entropy of fusion is about $2.6\,k_B$ per molecule.) For the conductivity he takes the gas formula with $c_v \sim k_B$, giving $K \sim (k_B/\sigma)\sqrt{k_BT/m}$, and for a solid sets $\sigma \sim a^2$ and $n \sim 1/a^3$, with $a$ the lattice constant. All together:

<Formula label="Wen's estimate of the freezing speed" tex="x \sim \sqrt{a\bar v\,\frac{\Delta T}{T_0}}\,\sqrt t = 0.3\ \text{mm}\,\sqrt{\frac{\Delta T}{T_0}}\,\sqrt{\frac{t}{1\ \text{s}}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 105" />

with $a \sim 3$ Å and $\bar v = 300$ m/s, so that $\sqrt{a\bar v} = 0.3$ mm per square root of a second.

## Electrons in a metal: the Drude model

The current in a metal is carried by electrons that scatter off impurities (and lattice vibrations). The **Drude model** describes them by their average velocity $\vec u$ (notes p. 106). In a short time $\Delta t$ a fraction $\Delta t/\tau$ of the electrons collide, and a collision leaves an electron with zero average velocity, so $\langle\vec u\rangle(t + \Delta t) = (1 - \Delta t/\tau)\langle\vec u\rangle(t)$ (the notes write the left side as $\Delta\langle\vec u\rangle$, but it is the new value, not the change) and $d\vec u/dt = -\vec u/\tau$: without a field, the average velocity decays as $u_0e^{-t/\tau}$. The time $\tau$ is the mean free time, $\tau = \lambda/\bar v$. With an electric field $\vec E$ the electrons are also accelerated:

<Formula label="Drude equation of motion" tex="\frac{d\vec u}{dt} = -\frac{1}{\tau}\vec u + \frac{e}{m}\vec E" cite="reading1" at="p. 106" />

With the charge current density $\vec j = en\vec u$, this becomes (notes p. 106)

<Formula label="Drude current" tex="\vec j + \tau\frac{d\vec j}{dt} = \frac{e^2n\tau}{m}\vec E" cite="reading1" at="p. 106" />

and in the steady state it is **Ohm's law**:

<Formula label="Ohm's law and the Drude conductivity" tex="\vec j = \sigma\vec E, \qquad \sigma = \frac{e^2n\tau}{m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 106" />

How does $\sigma$ depend on temperature? Only $\tau \approx \lambda/\bar v$ can, and the impurities fix $\lambda$ (notes p. 106). If the electrons moved like a classical gas, $\bar v \approx \sqrt{k_BT/m}$, we would get $\tau \propto T^{-1/2}$. Wen crosses that out: the electrons that carry the current are those at the Fermi surface, and they move at about $\sqrt{E_F/m} \sim \hbar n^{1/3}/m$, independent of $T$ as long as $k_BT \ll E_F$. So $\sigma \propto T^0$: the resistance of an impure metal levels off at low temperature. (At higher temperatures, scattering off lattice vibrations adds a resistance that grows with $T$; that is the page's addition.)

## The Hall effect

In a magnetic field the electrons also feel the Lorentz force (notes p. 107, in Gaussian units):

<Formula label="Drude model in a magnetic field" tex="\frac{d\vec u}{dt} = -\frac{1}{\tau}\vec u + \frac{e}{m}\vec E + \frac{e}{mc}\vec u\times\vec B \quad\Rightarrow\quad \vec j = \frac{e^2n\tau}{m}\vec E + \frac{e\tau}{mc}\vec j\times\vec B" cite="reading1" at="p. 107" />

in the steady state. With $\vec B = (0, 0, B)$, $\vec j\times\vec B = (j_yB, -j_xB, 0)$, and with $\sigma_0 = e^2n\tau/m$ and the cyclotron frequency $\omega_c = eB/mc$ the equations for $j_x$ and $j_y$ can be inverted (notes p. 107):

<Formula label="Resistivity tensor" tex="\begin{pmatrix}E_x\\E_y\end{pmatrix} = \sigma_0^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1 & -\omega_c\tau\\ \omega_c\tau & 1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}j_x\\j_y\end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 107" />

The matrix is the **resistivity**. Its off-diagonal elements $\rho_{xy}$ and $\rho_{yx}$, the **Hall resistivity**, do not depend on $\tau$ at all:

<Formula label="Hall resistivity and Hall coefficient" tex="|\rho_{xy}| = |\rho_{yx}| = \frac{\omega_c\tau}{\sigma_0} = \frac{eB\tau}{mc}\,\frac{m}{e^2n\tau} = \frac{B}{enc} = R_HB, \qquad R_H = \frac{1}{enc}" cite="reading1" at="p. 107" />

Its meaning: drive a current $j_x$ along a strip with $j_y = 0$, and a transverse field $E_y = \rho_{yx}j_x$ of size $R_HBj_x$ builds up (notes p. 107, which write $\rho_{xy}$ here; the matrix gives $\rho_{yx}$, of the same size). The deflected electrons pile up on one edge until their field balances the magnetic force. Measuring it gives the density of the carriers and, from its sign, the sign of their charge. (In SI units drop the $c$: $R_H = 1/(ne)$. Note also that the diagonal resistivity $1/\sigma_0$ does not change with $B$: the simple Drude model has no magnetoresistance. Both remarks are the page's.)

## The Boltzmann equation

The Drude model follows only an average velocity. The **Boltzmann equation** follows the whole distribution (notes p. 108). Let $g(\vec r, \vec k, t)$ be the occupation of the single-particle level with wave vector $\vec k$ at position $\vec r$, so that the number of particles in $d^3r\,d^3k$ is $dN = g\,d^3r\,d^3k/(2\pi)^3$. In equilibrium $g$ is one of the familiar distributions,

<Formula label="Equilibrium distributions" tex="g_0 = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)} + 1}\ \text{(fermions)}, \qquad g_0 = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)} - 1}\ \text{(bosons)}, \qquad g_0 = e^{-\beta(\epsilon_k - \mu)}\ \text{(classical gas)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 108" />

and a $g \neq g_0$ describes a gas out of equilibrium.

Without collisions, each particle just moves: in a time $dt$ its position changes by $\vec v_k\,dt$ and its wave vector by $\vec F\,dt/\hbar$, where $\vec F$ is the force and $\vec v_k = \partial\epsilon_k/\hbar\partial\vec k$ the velocity. The particles now in $d^3r\,d^3k$ are the ones that were in the cell they came from a moment ago, and by **Liouville's theorem** that cell had the same volume, $d^3r\,d^3k = d^3r'\,d^3k'$. So the occupation is carried along unchanged (notes pp. 108–109): $g(\vec r, \vec k, t) = g(\vec r - \vec v_k\,dt, \vec k - \vec F\,dt/\hbar, t - dt)$, which to first order in $dt$ is

<Formula label="Collisionless Boltzmann equation" tex="\frac{\partial g}{\partial t} + \vec v\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\vec r} + \frac{1}{\hbar}\vec F\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\vec k} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 109" />

Collisions knock particles out of a level and into others. In the **relaxation-time approximation** they simply drive $g$ back towards equilibrium at a rate $1/\tau$: the "out" term $-g/\tau$ and the "in" term $+g_0/\tau$ (notes p. 109):

<Formula label="Boltzmann equation, relaxation-time approximation" tex="\frac{\partial g}{\partial t} + \vec v\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\vec r} + \frac{1}{\hbar}\vec F\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\vec k} = -\frac{1}{\tau}(g - g_0)" cite="reading1" at="p. 109" />

In a steady state, write $\delta g = g - g_0$. If the gradients of $g_0$ and the force are small, $\delta g$ is small too, of first order in them, and the terms with $\partial\delta g/\partial\vec r$ and $\vec F\cdot\partial\delta g/\partial\vec k$ are of second order. Dropping them (notes p. 109):

<Formula label="Linearized solution" tex="\delta g = -\tau\vec v_k\cdot\frac{\partial g_0}{\partial\vec r} - \frac{\tau}{\hbar}\vec F\cdot\frac{\partial g_0}{\partial\vec k}" cite="reading1" at="p. 109" />

Here $g_0$ is the equilibrium distribution with a temperature $T(\vec r)$ and a chemical potential $\mu(\vec r)$ that may vary slowly in space.

## Currents from the Boltzmann equation

For fermions $g_0 = f = 1/(e^{\beta(\vec r)[\epsilon_k - \mu(\vec r)]} + 1)$ (notes p. 110). Its gradient in space comes through $\beta(\epsilon - \mu)$: $\partial f/\partial\vec r = -(\partial f/\partial\epsilon)[\nabla\mu + \frac{\epsilon - \mu}{T}\nabla T]$, and its gradient in $\vec k$ is $\partial f/\partial\vec k = \hbar\vec v_k\,\partial f/\partial\epsilon$. Putting these in (notes p. 110):

<Formula label="Deviation from equilibrium" tex="\delta g(\vec r, \vec k) = \left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial\epsilon}\right)\tau\,\vec v_k\cdot\left[-\nabla\mu(\vec r) + \vec F(\vec r, \vec k) - \frac{\epsilon_k - \mu(\vec r)}{T(\vec r)}\nabla T\right], \qquad \vec F = e\vec E + \frac{e}{c}\vec v_k\times\vec B" cite="reading1" at="p. 110" />

Once we know $\vec E$, $\vec B$, $T$ and $\mu$ everywhere, we know $g = g_0 + \delta g$, and from it we can calculate any current (notes p. 110). The same works for a Bose gas with $1/(e^{\beta(\epsilon - \mu)} - 1)$ in place of $f$, or a classical gas with $e^{-\beta(\epsilon - \mu)}$.

Take the **electric current** with $\vec B = 0$ (notes p. 111). An electric field and a gradient of the chemical potential push the electrons in the same way, so they combine into one effective field $\vec{\mathcal E} = \vec E - \nabla\mu/e$. The current is $j_x = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,ev_x\,\delta g$ (the equilibrium part carries none), and it has two parts, one driven by the field and one by the temperature gradient:

<Formula label="Electric current" tex="j_x = L^{11}\mathcal E_x + L^{12}(-\partial T/\partial x), \qquad L^{11} = \int\frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3}\,\tau e^2\left(-\frac{\partial f}{\partial\epsilon}\right)v_x^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 111" />

with $L^{12}$ the same integral with $e\tau\frac{\epsilon - \mu}{T}$ in place of $e^2\tau$. At low temperature, $-\partial f/\partial\epsilon$ is a narrow peak at the Fermi energy with $\int d\epsilon\,(-\partial f/\partial\epsilon) = 1$. Averaging over directions, $v_x^2 \to v^2/3$; with $d^3k = 4\pi k^2dk = 2\pi k\,(2m/\hbar^2)\,d\epsilon$ and $n = \frac{4\pi}{3}k_F^3/(2\pi)^3$ (notes p. 111):

<Formula label="The conductivity from the Boltzmann equation" tex="L^{11} = \frac{1}{3}\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}\,2\pi k_F\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\,\tau e^2v_F^2 = \frac{1}{3}\,\frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}\,\frac{4\pi}{m}\,\tau e^2k_F^3 = \frac{e^2\tau n}{m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 111" />

the Drude result (notes p. 111). Only the electrons near the Fermi surface contribute, which is why $\bar v$ in the Drude estimate had to be the Fermi speed. (This counts a single spin state, as $n = k_F^3/6\pi^2$ shows; with two spin states both $L^{11}$ and $n$ double, and $L^{11} = e^2\tau n/m$ still holds.)

## Thermopower

If no current is allowed to flow, a temperature gradient along a wire builds up an electric field (notes p. 113). Setting $j = 0$ in the current gives

<Formula label="Thermopower" tex="\vec{\mathcal E} = Q\,\nabla T, \qquad Q = \frac{L^{12}}{L^{11}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 113" />

The coefficient $Q$ is the **thermopower** (or Seebeck coefficient). A voltmeter connected across a wire whose ends are at $T_1$ and $T_0$ needs two different metals to see it, as in Wen's sketch of a thermocouple, since otherwise the two leads' voltages cancel. Wen estimates $L^{12}$ as $L^{11}$ with one factor $e$ replaced by $k_B$ and a factor $k_BT/E_F$ for the narrow window of energies $\epsilon - \mu \sim k_BT$ that contribute (notes p. 113):

<Formula label="Size of the thermopower" tex="Q \sim \frac{n^{1/3}e\tau k_B^2T}{\hbar^2}\bigg/\frac{e^2\tau n}{m} = \frac{k_B}{e}\,\frac{mk_BT}{n^{2/3}\hbar^2} \sim \frac{k_B}{e}\,\frac{1}{(n^{1/3}\lambda_T)^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 113" />

with $\lambda_T$ the thermal wavelength: $Q$ is $k_B/e$ times the square of the ratio of the spacing between electrons to $\lambda_T$, which is the ratio $k_BT/E_F$ up to a number. Writing $Q = Q_0\,k_B/e$, a wire of length $L$ develops $eL\mathcal E = Q_0k_B(T_1 - T_0)$. Since $k_B/e = 1/11\,605$ V/K (1 eV corresponds to about 11 600 K), the field in volts per metre is $Q_0/11\,605$ times the gradient in kelvin per metre. (The notes write 1160 here; the conversion is 1 eV ↔ 11 605 K.) For a free electron gas with a relaxation time that does not depend on energy, the Sommerfeld expansion turns the estimate into $Q = \frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{k_B}{e}\frac{k_BT}{E_F}$; that formula is the page's addition.

<WorkedExample title="Electrons in copper">
<Problem>
Copper has $n = 8.47 \times 10^{28}$ conduction electrons per m³ and a resistivity of $1.68 \times 10^{-8}$ Ω m at room temperature. Using the Drude formula (in SI units, $\sigma = e^2n\tau/m$), find the mean free time $\tau$; the Fermi speed $v_F = \hbar(3\pi^2n)^{1/3}/m$ and the mean free path $v_F\tau$; $\omega_c\tau$ in a field of 1 T; the Hall voltage across a strip 0.1 mm thick carrying 1 A in 1 T; and the free-electron thermopower at 300 K.
</Problem>
<Step title="Mean free time">
$\tau = m/(\rho e^2n) = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}/(1.68 \times 10^{-8} \times (1.602 \times 10^{-19})^2 \times 8.47 \times 10^{28}) \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-14}$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Fermi speed and mean free path">
$k_F = (3\pi^2n)^{1/3} \approx 1.36 \times 10^{10}$ m⁻¹, so $v_F = \hbar k_F/m \approx 1.57 \times 10^6$ m/s and $E_F = \hbar^2k_F^2/2m \approx 7.0$ eV. The mean free path is $v_F\tau \approx 39$ nm, about 170 times the spacing between atoms, $n^{-1/3} \approx 0.23$ nm (copper has one conduction electron per atom): the electrons sail through the perfect parts of the lattice.
</Step>
<Step title="Cyclotron frequency">
$\omega_c = eB/m \approx 1.76 \times 10^{11}$ s⁻¹ in 1 T, so $\omega_c\tau \approx 0.0044$. An electron is scattered long before it completes an orbit, and the Hall angle is small.
</Step>
<Step title="Hall voltage">
In SI units $R_H = 1/(ne) \approx 7.4 \times 10^{-11}$ m³/C. For a strip of thickness $d$ the Hall voltage is $IB/(ned) = 1 \times 1/(8.47 \times 10^{28} \times 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 10^{-4}) \approx 0.74$ μV: small, because a good metal has so many carriers. In a semiconductor with a million times fewer carriers it would be a million times larger.
</Step>
<Step title="Thermopower">
$k_BT/E_F = 0.02585/7.03 \approx 0.0037$ at 300 K, so $Q = \frac{\pi^2}{2} \times 86.2\ \mu\text{V/K} \times 0.0037 \approx 1.6$ μV/K. The measured thermopower of copper has this size, about 2 μV/K, though with the opposite sign: the real Fermi surface of copper is not a sphere, and the sign of $Q$ is sensitive to that.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The Boltzmann equation for stars">
Wen's collisionless Boltzmann equation says that the occupation of phase space is carried along unchanged by the motion. The stars of a galaxy obey exactly that equation, with the force $-m\nabla\phi$ of the galaxy's own gravity and no collision term, since stars almost never meet: it is the **collisionless Boltzmann equation** of [MIT 8.902 Lecture 6](/courses/8-902/jeans-equations), from which the Jeans equations for the motions of stars in galaxies follow.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Viscosity** is momentum carried by diffusing particles: $F_x = -\nu\,\partial u_x/\partial y$ with $\nu = \frac{1}{6} n\bar vm\lambda = m\bar v/6\sigma$, independent of the density.
- The viscous stress $P'_{ij} = -\nu(\partial_iu_j + \partial_ju_i - \frac{2}{3}\delta_{ij}\nabla\cdot\vec u)$ turns Euler's equation into the **Navier–Stokes equation**. The **Reynolds number** $R = \rho Lu_0/\nu$ separates turbulent ($R \gg 1$) from streamline ($R \ll 1$) flow.
- **Heat conduction**: $\vec q = -K\nabla T$ with $K = \frac{1}{6} n\bar v\lambda c_v$, also independent of $n$; the heat equation $\partial T/\partial t = (K/nc_v)\nabla^2T$. Ice on a pond grows as $x = \sqrt{2K\Delta T t/n\ell}$.
- **Drude model**: $d\vec u/dt = -\vec u/\tau + e\vec E/m$ gives $\sigma = e^2n\tau/m$, nearly independent of $T$ for impurity scattering since the electrons move at the Fermi speed. **Hall effect**: $|\rho_{xy}| = B/(nec)$, $R_H = 1/(nec)$, independent of $\tau$.
- **Boltzmann equation** in the relaxation-time approximation: $\partial g/\partial t + \vec v\cdot\partial g/\partial\vec r + \frac{1}{\hbar}\vec F\cdot\partial g/\partial\vec k = -(g - g_0)/\tau$. Its linear solution gives $j_x = L^{11}\mathcal E_x + L^{12}(-\partial T/\partial x)$, with $L^{11} = e^2\tau n/m$ (Drude again) and the **thermopower** $Q = L^{12}/L^{11} \sim (k_B/e)(k_BT/E_F)$.
