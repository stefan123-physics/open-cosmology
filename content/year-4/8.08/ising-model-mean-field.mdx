A magnet is many small magnetic moments that push each other to line up. On their own, as in [the paramagnet of 8.044](/courses/8-044/paramagnets), the moments point every which way unless a field aligns them; with an interaction between neighbours, they can line up by themselves below a certain temperature. Chapter III of Wen's notes (pp. 21–27, headed "Ising model: phase transition and Ginzburg–Landau theory") studies the simplest model that does this. This page covers its first two points (pp. 21–23): the model itself and its mean-field solution. The [next page](/courses/8-08/ginzburg-landau-theory) covers the rest: Ginzburg–Landau theory, critical exponents and the phase diagram. (MIT's material for this chapter is seven scanned handwritten pages, numbered 21 to 27 at the top right; citations use those numbers and Wen's circled point numbers. Everything here is in our own words, with our own examples. We write $N$ for the number of spins, which the notes write with the subscript "spin".)

## The Ising model

Put a spin on every site of a lattice: a chain in one dimension, a square grid in two. Each spin can point only up or down, so it is a number $s_i = +1$ or $s_i = -1$ (notes p. 21, point ①). A **spin configuration** $\{s_i\}$ is a list of these numbers, one per site, and its energy is

<Formula label="Energy of the Ising model" tex="E(\{s_i\}) = -J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle} s_is_j - h\sum_i s_i" cite="reading1" at="p. 21, point ①" />

The first sum runs over nearest-neighbour pairs $\langle ij\rangle$, each pair counted once (Wen says so on p. 22). The coupling $J$ is an energy: each aligned pair ($s_is_j = +1$) contributes $-J$ and each opposite pair $+J$. The second term is the energy of the spins in an external magnetic field; $h$ is that field times the spin's magnetic moment, so it too is an energy, and $h > 0$ favours $s_i = +1$.

The ground states at $h = 0$ follow from the sign of $J$ (notes p. 21):

- $J > 0$: every pair wants to be aligned, so the ground state has all spins up, or all spins down. This is a **ferromagnet**.
- $J < 0$: every pair wants to be opposite, so the spins alternate, up, down, up, down. This is an **antiferromagnet**.

Note that for $J > 0$ there are two ground states with exactly the same energy. The energy does not change if every spin is flipped, $s_i \to -s_i$, as long as $h = 0$: the model has an up-down symmetry. This is the symmetry that will be broken below.

## Finite temperature and what a phase transition is

At temperature $T$ the probabilities follow the canonical ensemble (see [the canonical ensemble of 8.044](/courses/8-044/canonical-ensemble)), with the partition function and the free energy (notes p. 21)

<Formula label="Partition function and free energy" tex="Z = \sum_{\{s_i\}} e^{-\beta E}, \qquad A = -k_BT\ln Z" cite="reading1" at="p. 21, point ①" />

with $\beta = 1/k_BT$ and $E = E(\{s_i\})$. The sum runs over all $2^N$ configurations of $N$ spins. Everything else, the energy, the entropy and the magnetization, follows from $A(T, h)$.

What does it mean for this system to have a **phase transition**? Wen's answer (p. 21) is a sketch of the free energy at $h = 0$ for a two-dimensional lattice in the limit of infinitely many spins: $A(T)$ is continuous, but at one temperature $T_c$ it changes its curve abruptly, and the second derivative $\partial^2A/\partial T^2$ has a jump there. For any finite number of spins the same derivative goes smoothly from one value to the other. The reason is simple: each term $e^{-\beta E}$ of $Z$ is a smooth, positive function of $T$, and a finite sum of such terms is smooth and positive, so its logarithm is smooth too. Sharp behaviour can only appear in the limit $N \to \infty$, where the sum has infinitely many terms. Since the heat capacity is $C = -T\,\partial^2A/\partial T^2$ (it appears on p. 24), a jump in $\partial^2A/\partial T^2$ is a jump in the heat capacity.

(Some context not in the notes. Ising solved the chain in 1925 and found no transition at any $T > 0$. Onsager solved the square lattice exactly in 1944: it has a transition at $T_c = 2J/[k_B\ln(1 + \sqrt 2)] \approx 2.27J/k_B$, where the heat capacity in fact grows without bound, like $-\ln|T - T_c|$, rather than jumping; the jump is the mean-field behaviour found on the next page. Wen's sketch of $A(T)$ also rises with $T$; since $\partial A/\partial T = -S$ is negative, $A$ in fact falls, and what the sketch is about is the break in its curve at $T_c$. Likewise the sketch of $\partial^2A/\partial T^2$ is drawn positive, which is really the shape of $-\partial^2A/\partial T^2$, the heat capacity over $T$.)

## Mean-field theory

The sum over $2^N$ configurations cannot be done in general, so Wen turns to an approximation (notes p. 22, point ②). Write each spin as its average plus a fluctuation. The product of two spins is then

<Formula label="The mean-field approximation" tex="s_is_j \approx \langle s_i\rangle s_j + s_i\langle s_j\rangle - \langle s_i\rangle\langle s_j\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 22, point ② (box at top right)" />

which drops only the product of the two fluctuations, $(s_i - \langle s_i\rangle)(s_j - \langle s_j\rangle)$. The approximation keeps the right average, since averaging both sides gives $\langle s_i\rangle\langle s_j\rangle \approx \langle s_is_j\rangle$: it assumes the two spins fluctuate independently. In a uniform state $\langle s_i\rangle = \langle s\rangle$ does not depend on $i$.

Now count. Each site has $z$ nearest neighbours, the **coordination number**: $z = 2$ on a chain, $z = 4$ on a square lattice. So there are $zN/2$ pairs, and each spin $s_i$ appears in $z$ of them. Putting the approximation into the energy gives the **mean-field energy** (notes p. 22)

<Formula label="Mean-field energy" tex="E_{\text{mean}} = -(h + zJ\langle s\rangle)\sum_i s_i + \tfrac12 zNJ\langle s\rangle^2, \qquad h_{\text{eff}} = h + zJ\langle s\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 22, point ②" />

Each spin now feels only an **effective field** $h_{\text{eff}}$: the external field plus the average pull of its $z$ neighbours. The spins have become independent, like those of a paramagnet, and the constant last term corrects for counting each pair's average twice. The partition function is now a product of one-spin sums (notes p. 22):

<Formula label="Mean-field partition function" tex="Z \approx \sum_{\{s_i\}} e^{-\beta E_{\text{mean}}} = \left(e^{-\beta h_{\text{eff}}} + e^{\beta h_{\text{eff}}}\right)^N e^{-\frac12\beta zNJ\langle s\rangle^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 22, point ②" />

and its logarithm is the **mean-field free energy**, boxed in the notes:

<Formula label="Mean-field free energy" tex="A = \left[-k_BT\ln\left(e^{-\beta h_{\text{eff}}} + e^{\beta h_{\text{eff}}}\right) + \tfrac12 zJ\langle s\rangle^2\right]N" cite="reading1" at="p. 22, point ② (boxed)" />

## The self-consistent equation

But what is $\langle s\rangle$? Wen computes it from the same mean-field energy (notes p. 22). Within the approximation each spin is independent in the field $h_{\text{eff}}$, so its average is that of a single two-state spin, as for the spin-½ paramagnet:

<Formula label="Average spin in the effective field" tex="\langle s\rangle = \frac{e^{\beta h_{\text{eff}}} - e^{-\beta h_{\text{eff}}}}{e^{\beta h_{\text{eff}}} + e^{-\beta h_{\text{eff}}}} = \tanh(\beta h_{\text{eff}})" cite="reading1" at="p. 22, point ②" />

The average appears on both sides, since $h_{\text{eff}}$ contains it. This is the **self-consistent equation** (boxed on p. 23):

<Formula label="Self-consistent equation" tex="\langle s\rangle = \tanh\big[\beta(h + zJ\langle s\rangle)\big]" cite="reading1" at="p. 23 (boxed)" />

Its solution gives $\langle s\rangle$ as a function $f(T, h)$, and putting it into $A(T, h, \langle s\rangle)$ gives the mean-field free energy as a function of $T$ and $h$ alone.

Wen's second way to the same equation (p. 23): treat $\langle s\rangle$ in $A(T, h, \langle s\rangle)$ as a free parameter and **minimize** $A$ with respect to it. Differentiating the boxed free energy,

<Formula label="Minimizing the free energy" tex="\frac{1}{N}\frac{\partial A}{\partial\langle s\rangle} = -\frac{e^{\beta h_{\text{eff}}} - e^{-\beta h_{\text{eff}}}}{e^{\beta h_{\text{eff}}} + e^{-\beta h_{\text{eff}}}}\,zJ + zJ\langle s\rangle = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \langle s\rangle = \tanh(\beta h_{\text{eff}})" cite="reading1" at="p. 23" />

the same as the self-consistent equation. (The notes write a minus sign in the denominator of the fraction, a slip: it is the derivative of the logarithm of $e^{-\beta h_{\text{eff}}} + e^{\beta h_{\text{eff}}}$, so the denominator is that sum, and the fraction is the tanh. The notes also leave out the factor $N$ here.) The second way is the more useful one: it shows which solution to take when there are several, the one with the lowest $A$.

## Spontaneous symmetry breaking at h = 0

Without a field the free energy per spin depends on $\langle s\rangle$ only through $\langle s\rangle^2$, because flipping all spins changes nothing. Wen sketches its shape at three temperatures (p. 23): above $T_c$ it has one minimum, at $\langle s\rangle = 0$; at $T_c$ the bottom is very flat; below $T_c$ the point $\langle s\rangle = 0$ has become a maximum, with two equal minima on either side.

If the slope is less than 1, the curve stays below the line for positive $\langle s\rangle$, and the only crossing is at zero. If the slope is more than 1, the curve starts above the line and must cross it again: there are three solutions, $0$ and $\pm s_0$.

The two outer solutions are the minima of $A$, the stable states; the middle one is the maximum. The two minima have exactly the same free energy, so nothing in the equations decides between them. A real magnet cooled through $T_c$ ends up in one of them, chosen by a tiny stray field or a chance fluctuation, and stays there: the state has less symmetry than the energy. Wen calls this **symmetry breaking** (p. 23), and $\langle s\rangle$ is the **order parameter**: zero in the disordered phase above $T_c$, non-zero in the ordered phase below it, reaching 1 at $T = 0$.

The transition is where the slope at the origin passes 1. For small $\langle s\rangle$, $\tanh(\beta zJ\langle s\rangle) \approx \beta zJ\langle s\rangle$, so the condition is $\beta zJ = 1$ (notes p. 23):

## How good is mean-field theory?

(This section goes beyond the notes.) The approximation ignores the fluctuations of the neighbours, which is worst when a spin has few of them. So mean-field theory gets the existence of ordering right in two and more dimensions, but its $T_c$ is always too high. On a chain it predicts $T_c = 2J/k_B$, yet the exact chain has no transition at all: a single break in a long row of aligned spins costs only $2J$, and there are so many places for it that at any $T > 0$ breaks always appear and destroy the order. On a square lattice it predicts $4J/k_B$ against Onsager's exact $2.27J/k_B$. In three dimensions the error is smaller, and the more neighbours a spin has, the better the average describes them.

<WorkedExample title="A square-lattice magnet in mean-field theory">
<Problem>
An Ising magnet on a square lattice has $J/k_B = 50$ K. Find its mean-field critical temperature and compare it with Onsager's exact value. Then find $\langle s\rangle$ at 150 K at zero field, the effective field each spin feels, and the mean-field energy per spin.
</Problem>
<Step title="The critical temperature">
With $z = 4$, $T_c = zJ/k_B = 4 \times 50$ K $= 200$ K. Onsager's exact result is $2 \times 50$ K$/\ln(1 + \sqrt 2) \approx 113.5$ K: mean-field theory overestimates $T_c$ by more than 75%.
</Step>
<Step title="The order parameter at 150 K">
Here $\beta zJ = T_c/T = 200/150 = 4/3$, so we must solve $\langle s\rangle = \tanh(4\langle s\rangle/3)$. Iterate from $\langle s\rangle = 1$: $\tanh(4/3) = 0.870$, then $\tanh(4 \times 0.870/3) = 0.821$, then $0.799$, $0.787$, $0.782$, …, settling at $\langle s\rangle \approx 0.776$. On average 88.8% of the spins point the majority way, since $(1 + 0.776)/2 = 0.888$.
</Step>
<Step title="The effective field and the energy">
Each spin feels $h_{\text{eff}} = zJ\langle s\rangle = 4 \times 50$ K $\times 0.776 \times k_B \approx 155$ K $\times\,k_B$, without any external field. The mean-field energy is $\langle E_{\text{mean}}\rangle = -h_{\text{eff}}N\langle s\rangle + \frac12 zNJ\langle s\rangle^2 = -\frac12 zNJ\langle s\rangle^2$, so per spin $-2 \times 50$ K $\times 0.776^2 \times k_B \approx -60.1$ K $\times\,k_B$, about $-8.30 \times 10^{-22}$ J.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Symmetry breaking in the early universe">
The picture of this page, a free energy with one minimum at high temperature that turns into a set of equivalent minima below a critical temperature, is how particle physics describes the hot early universe. In Guth's [Higgs field lecture](/courses/8-286/higgs-field), the Higgs field plays the part of $\langle s\rangle$: when the universe was hot its minimum was at zero and the electroweak symmetry was intact; as the universe cooled, the minimum moved away from zero and the field had to pick one of infinitely many equivalent values, just as the magnet picks up or down. Separate regions pick independently, and where they disagree, defects are left behind, like the walls between up and down domains of a magnet; in grand unified theories these include the [magnetic monopoles](/courses/8-286/magnetic-monopoles) whose overproduction inflation was first proposed to solve.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The **Ising model**: spins $s_i = \pm 1$ on a lattice with $E = -J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j - h\sum_i s_i$. For $J > 0$ the ground states are all up or all down (ferromagnet), for $J < 0$ alternating (antiferromagnet).
- $Z = \sum e^{-\beta E}$ and $A = -k_BT\ln Z$. A finite system has a smooth $A$; a sharp phase transition, such as a jump in $\partial^2A/\partial T^2$, needs infinitely many spins.
- **Mean-field theory** drops the product of two neighbours' fluctuations. Each spin then feels the effective field $h_{\text{eff}} = h + zJ\langle s\rangle$, with $z$ the coordination number, and the free energy is $A = N[-k_BT\ln(2\cosh\beta h_{\text{eff}}) + \frac12 zJ\langle s\rangle^2]$.
- $\langle s\rangle$ follows from the **self-consistent equation** $\langle s\rangle = \tanh[\beta(h + zJ\langle s\rangle)]$, which is also the condition for $A$ to be stationary in $\langle s\rangle$; the minimum is the equilibrium.
- At $h = 0$ a non-zero solution exists below $T_c = zJ/k_B$: the system picks one of two equivalent states, **spontaneous symmetry breaking**, with $\langle s\rangle$ as the order parameter. Mean-field $T_c$ is too high (no transition at all on a chain; $4J/k_B$ against the exact $2.27J/k_B$ on a square lattice).
