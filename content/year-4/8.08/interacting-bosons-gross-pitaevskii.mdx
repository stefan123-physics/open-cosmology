The [ideal Bose gas](/courses/8-08/ideal-bose-gas-condensation) condenses, but its condensate is a strange object: the pressure does not depend on the density, and the heat capacity grows as $T^{3/2}$, while real liquid helium shows $T^3$. Wen's point ④, **interacting bosons and superfluidity** (notes pp. 62–69), repairs this by describing the condensate as a classical field, the shared wavefunction of all the bosons, and adding a repulsion between them. The method is the one of [Ginzburg-Landau theory](/courses/8-08/ginzburg-landau-theory): write down a functional, find its minimum, then study small oscillations about it. (This page ends where the first of the two Chapter VII files ends, on p. 69; the second file, on superfluid flow, starts on p. 70.)

## All bosons in one state

At $T = 0$ all bosons condense: they are all in the same single-particle state (notes p. 62). Start with one boson, obeying the Schrödinger equation in a potential $U(\vec x)$:

<Formula label="One boson" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + U\right)\psi \equiv H\psi" cite="reading1" at="p. 62, point ④" />

Its state is normalised, $\int d^3x\,\psi^*\psi = 1$, and its energy is

<Formula label="Energy of one boson" tex="E_1 = \int d^3x\,\psi^*H\psi = \int d^3x\left(\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^2 + U|\psi|^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 62" />

after an integration by parts; $|\nabla\psi|^2$ is the sum of the squared sizes of the three partial derivatives of $\psi$. For $N$ bosons all described by the same wavefunction, Wen rescales $\psi$ so that it counts all of them:

<Formula label="N bosons in one state" tex="\int d^3x\,|\psi|^2 = N, \qquad |\psi|^2 = \rho, \qquad \int d^3x\left(\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^2 + U|\psi|^2\right) = E = NE_1" cite="reading1" at="p. 62" />

so $|\psi|^2$ is the **density** of bosons. Wen adds that this is "not quite right": the true state of $N$ bosons is a wavefunction of $N$ positions, $\Psi(\vec x_1, \ldots, \vec x_N) = \prod_i\psi(\vec x_i)$ (with $\psi$ normalised to 1). The single field normalised to $N$ is a shorthand that captures its density and energy, and it is what makes the rest of the chapter simple.

## The grand potential and the equation of motion

The ground state is the $\psi_0$ that minimises $E$ subject to $\int|\psi_0|^2 = N$ (notes p. 63). For $U = 0$, the kinetic term is smallest for a constant, so $\psi_0 = \sqrt n\,e^{i\theta}$ with $n = N/V$ and **any phase** $\theta$. As in statistical mechanics, a constraint is easier to handle with a chemical potential. Let $N$ float and minimise instead the "grand canonical" combination

<Formula label="Grand potential of the condensate" tex="\Omega = E - \mu N = \int d^3x\left[\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^2 + (U - \mu)|\psi|^2\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 63" />

with no other constraint. In the same spirit, energies are measured from $\mu$ in the equation of motion:

<Formula label="Equation of motion with the chemical potential" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + U - \mu\right)\psi" cite="reading1" at="p. 63" />

which only multiplies $\psi$ by the phase $e^{i\mu t/\hbar}$. The two are linked. Varying $\psi$ by $\delta\psi$ changes $\Omega$ by

<Formula label="Variation of the grand potential" tex="\delta\Omega = \int d^3x\left[\delta\psi^*\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + U - \mu\right)\psi + \delta\psi\left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + U - \mu\right)\psi^*\right] + O(\delta\psi^2)" cite="reading1" at="p. 63" />

after integrating the gradient terms by parts. For a minimum the first-order change must vanish for every $\delta\psi$, so

<Formula label="Condition for the ground state" tex="\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + (U - \mu)\right]\psi_0 = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 63" />

which is exactly the right-hand side of the equation of motion. A minimum of $\Omega$ is therefore a solution that does not change in time.

## Free bosons: the chemical potential is fixed

Wen's recipe (notes p. 64): pick a $\mu$; find a $\psi_0$ that satisfies the condition; check whether $\int|\psi_0|^2 = N$; if not, find another $\psi_0$ or pick a new $\mu$. Try it for a constant $U$:

1. **$\mu < U$.** The operator $-\hbar^2\nabla^2/2m + (U - \mu)$ is positive, so the only solution is $\psi_0 = 0$: no bosons.
2. **$\mu = U$.** Then $-\hbar^2\nabla^2\psi_0/2m = 0$ has the solutions $\psi_0 = c\,e^{i\theta}$ for any numbers $c$ and $\theta$: **any number** of bosons.
3. **$\mu > U$.** There are solutions $\psi_0 = c\,e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x}$, plane waves with

<Formula label="Plane-wave solutions for a chemical potential above U" tex="\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} = \mu - U" cite="reading1" at="p. 64" />

But they are not the ground state. On any solution of the condition, $\Omega = \int\psi_0^*(\ldots)\psi_0 = 0$, while the constant $\psi = \sqrt n$ gives $\Omega = \int d^3x\,n(U - \mu) < 0$, lower still. So the plane wave is not a minimum (Wen calls it a maximum; strictly it is a stationary point that is not a minimum). Indeed, with $\mu > U$ the grand potential has no minimum at all: it falls without limit as $n$ grows, the instability of the [previous page](/courses/8-08/ideal-bose-gas-condensation) in a new form.

So to get an $N$-boson state we must set $\mu = U$ and **choose** $\psi_0 = \sqrt n\,e^{i\theta}$ with $n = N/V$ (notes p. 65). For free bosons, $\mu$ alone does not fix $N$. In general, Wen notes, there is only one solution $\psi_0$ for each $\mu$, and $\mu$ is tuned until $\int|\psi_0|^2 = N$.

## Excitations of the free condensate

Disturb the ground state: $\psi = \psi_0 + \delta\psi$ (notes p. 65). Since $\psi_0$ makes the right-hand side vanish, it has no time dependence, and because the equation is linear the disturbance obeys the same equation:

<Formula label="Equation of motion for excitations" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\,\delta\psi}{\partial t} = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + U - \mu\right)\delta\psi" cite="reading1" at="p. 65" />

For constant $U$ and $\mu = U$, the solutions are plane waves $\delta\psi \propto e^{i\vec k\cdot\vec x - i\omega_kt}$ with

<Formula label="Excitation energy of free bosons" tex="\hbar\omega_k = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 65" />

the energy of one boson lifted out of the condensate with momentum $\hbar\vec k$. More generally (notes p. 66) a field $\psi = c_0e^{i\theta} + c_1e^{i\vec k_1\cdot\vec x - i\omega_1t} + c_2e^{i\vec k_2\cdot\vec x - i\omega_2t} + \cdots$ describes a condensate of density $|c_0|^2$ in the $\vec k = 0$ level, with $|c_1|^2$ bosons per unit volume in the level $\vec k_1$, each carrying momentum $\hbar\vec k_1$ and energy $\hbar\omega_1$, and so on. Excitations with $\omega \propto k^2$ are what gave the ideal gas its $T^{3/2}$.

## Interacting bosons

Now let the bosons interact through a potential $V(\vec x, \vec x')$ between a boson at $\vec x$ and one at $\vec x'$ (notes p. 67). With the density $|\psi|^2$, the interaction energy is the usual double sum over pairs:

<Formula label="Grand potential with interactions" tex="\Omega = \int d^3x\left[\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^2 + U|\psi|^2\right] + \int d^3x\,d^3x'\,\frac12\,V(\vec x, \vec x')\,|\psi(\vec x)|^2\,|\psi(\vec x')|^2 - \int d^3x\,\mu|\psi|^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 67" />

The equation of motion is a **non-linear** Schrödinger equation, in which each boson feels the outside potential plus the mean potential of all the others:

<Formula label="Non-linear Schrödinger equation" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + U_{\text{eff}} - \mu\right)\psi, \qquad U_{\text{eff}}(\vec x) = U(\vec x) + \int d^3x'\,V(\vec x, \vec x')\,|\psi(\vec x')|^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 67" />

The ground state again minimises $\Omega$, $[-\hbar^2\nabla^2/2m + U_{\text{eff}}(\vec x, \psi_0) - \mu]\psi_0 = 0$. For a **short-range** interaction, $V(\vec x, \vec x') = v_0\,\delta^3(\vec x - \vec x')$, the double integral collapses and

<Formula label="Gross-Pitaevskii grand potential and equation" tex="\Omega = \int d^3x\left[\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}|\nabla\psi|^2 + (U - \mu)|\psi|^2 + \frac{v_0}{2}|\psi|^4\right], \qquad i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + U - \mu + v_0|\psi|^2\right)\psi" cite="reading1" at="p. 67" />

the **Gross-Pitaevskii equation**. Writing $\psi = \psi_0 + \delta\psi$ with a time-independent $\psi_0(\vec x)$ and keeping first order, $|\psi|^2\psi$ gives $2|\psi_0|^2\delta\psi + \psi_0^2\delta\psi^*$, so the collective excitations obey

<Formula label="Equation of motion for collective excitations" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\,\delta\psi}{\partial t} = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + U - \mu + 2v_0|\psi_0|^2\right)\delta\psi + v_0\,\psi_0^2\,\delta\psi^* + O(\delta\psi^2)" cite="reading1" at="p. 67" />

(The notes write $v_0|\psi|^2\delta\psi^*$; it is $v_0\psi_0^2$, which equals $v_0|\psi_0|^2$ when $\psi_0$ is real, as in the example below.) Unlike the free case, $\delta\psi$ is now coupled to its complex conjugate.

## The uniform condensate and its broken symmetry

Take $U = 0$ (notes p. 68). Then $\Omega = \int d^3x[(\hbar^2/2m)|\nabla\psi|^2 + V(\psi)]$, with a potential $V(\psi) = -\mu|\psi|^2 + \frac12v_0|\psi|^4$, the same shape as the [Ginzburg-Landau free energy](/courses/8-08/ginzburg-landau-theory), whose square term changes sign at the transition; here it is the coefficient $-\mu$ that changes sign. A uniform $\psi_0 = \sqrt n\,e^{i\theta}$ satisfies the ground-state condition when

<Formula label="Uniform ground state with repulsion" tex="\left(-\mu + v_0n\right)\sqrt n\,e^{i\theta} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = \frac{\mu}{v_0} \ \text{ or } \ n = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 68" />

1. **$\mu < 0$**: $n = \mu/v_0$ would be negative, which is not allowed, so $\psi_0 = 0$: no bosons.
2. **$\mu > 0$**: $\psi_0 = \sqrt{\mu/v_0}\,e^{i\theta}$, while $n = 0$ is now a maximum of $V(\psi)$. The minima form a ring in the complex $\psi$ plane, all with the same $|\psi_0|$ but different phases, and the condensate must pick one: **symmetry breaking**, like the ball in Wen's sketch that sits at one point in the trough of a wine-bottle bottom.

For an $N$-boson system the density fixes the chemical potential: $N/V = \mu/v_0$, or $\mu = v_0n$. The interaction has given $\mu$ a job again. The grand potential per volume is

<Formula label="Grand potential of the uniform condensate" tex="\frac{\Omega}{V} = \begin{cases} 0, & \mu < 0,\ \psi_0 = 0 \\ -\dfrac{\mu^2}{2v_0}, & \mu > 0,\ \psi_0 \neq 0 \end{cases}" cite="reading1" at="p. 68" />

It is continuous at $\mu = 0$ but its second derivative jumps: a singularity, a phase transition between "no bosons" and "bosons condensed", with $\psi_0$ as the order parameter. It happens at zero temperature as $\mu$ is varied, a **quantum phase transition**.

## Sound in the condensate

Now the collective excitations of the uniform condensate, with $\psi_0 = \sqrt n$ real and $\mu = v_0n$ (notes p. 68). The equation becomes

<Formula label="Excitations of the uniform condensate" tex="i\hbar\frac{\partial\,\delta\psi}{\partial t} = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + \mu\right)\delta\psi + \mu\,\delta\psi^*" cite="reading1" at="p. 68" />

Split $\delta\psi = \delta\psi_1 + i\,\delta\psi_2$ into real and imaginary parts. Then $\delta\psi_1$ (a change in the size of $\psi$, so in the density) and $\delta\psi_2$ (a change in its phase) drive each other:

<Formula label="Real and imaginary parts" tex="-\hbar\frac{\partial\,\delta\psi_2}{\partial t} = \left(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + 2\mu\right)\delta\psi_1, \qquad \hbar\frac{\partial\,\delta\psi_1}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\,\delta\psi_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 68" />

Differentiating the first in time and using the second gives a wave equation, $-\hbar^2\,\partial^2\delta\psi_2/\partial t^2 = (-\hbar^2\nabla^2/2m + 2\mu)(-\hbar^2\nabla^2/2m)\delta\psi_2$, solved by (notes p. 69)

<Formula label="Collective mode" tex="\delta\psi_2 = C\sin(\vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega_kt + \theta), \qquad \delta\psi_1 = \frac{\hbar k^2}{2m\omega_k}\,C\cos(\vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega_kt + \theta)" cite="reading1" at="p. 69" />

with the dispersion relation

<Formula label="Dispersion of the collective mode" tex="\omega_k^2 = \left(\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}k^2 + 2\mu\right)\frac{k^2}{2m} = \frac{k^2}{2m}\left(\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}k^2 + 2v_0n\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 69" />

For small $k$ this is **sound**, $\omega_k \approx v_s|\vec k|$, and for large $k$ it returns to the free-particle $\omega_k \approx \hbar k^2/2m$:

<Formula label="Speed of sound in the condensate" tex="v_s = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{v_0n}{m}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 69" />

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 2.5]} range={[0, 7]} xLabel="ħk/√(2mμ)" yLabel="ħω/μ" samples={300}

  caption="The energy of a collective mode, computed from ħω = √(ε_k(ε_k + 2μ)) with ε_k = ħ²k²/2m, in units where ħk/√(2mμ) is the variable and μ the energy unit. At small k it follows the sound line ħv_s k (slope √2 in these units); around ħk ≈ √(4mμ), where ħ²k²/2m = 2μ, it bends over to the free-particle parabola. Without interactions (μ = 0) only the parabola is left." />

The **low-energy excitations** are therefore $\hbar\omega_k = \hbar v_s|\vec k|$ (the notes drop the $\hbar$ on the right), not $\hbar^2k^2/2m$ as for free bosons. They are counted exactly like phonons, so at low temperature $C_V \propto T^3$, not $T^{3/2}$: the last line of the table on the previous page, and what liquid helium shows. Wen's last sketch explains why the mode costs so little: the ring of minima means the phase $\theta$ of the condensate can turn at no cost. A long-wavelength wave in which the phase turns slowly from place to place (his row of arrows) costs almost nothing, so the mode has no gap. **Symmetry breaking** comes with a **gapless mode**.

<WorkedExample title="Sound in a sodium condensate">
<Problem>
A sodium condensate (mass 22.99 u) has density $1.0 \times 10^{20}$ m⁻³ and contact strength $v_0 = 1.0 \times 10^{-50}$ J m³. (This value corresponds to sodium's measured scattering length of about 2.75 nm through $v_0 = 4\pi\hbar^2a/m$, a relation not in the notes.) Find $\mu$, the speed of sound, and the wavelength at which the modes turn from sound into free particles.
</Problem>
<Step title="Chemical potential">
$\mu = v_0n = 1.0 \times 10^{-50} \times 10^{20}$ J $= 1.0 \times 10^{-30}$ J, or $\mu/k_B = 72$ nK: well below the microkelvin transition temperatures of the previous page.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed of sound">
$m = 22.99 \times 1.6605 \times 10^{-27}$ kg $= 3.82 \times 10^{-26}$ kg, so $v_s = \sqrt{\mu/m} = \sqrt{2.62 \times 10^{-5}}$ m/s $= 5.1 \times 10^{-3}$ m/s: about 5 millimetres per second, about 67,000 times slower than sound in air (343 m/s).
</Step>
<Step title="The crossover">
The two terms in $\omega_k^2$ are equal when $\hbar^2k^2/2m = 2\mu$, at $k = \sqrt{4m\mu}/\hbar = 3.7 \times 10^6$ m⁻¹, a wavelength $2\pi/k = 1.7$ μm. Waves longer than that are sound; shorter ones are single atoms kicked out of the condensate.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A field that picks a phase">
The structure of this page, a complex field whose energy depends only on $|\psi|^2$ and is lowest at $|\psi| \neq 0$, so that the ground state must pick one of infinitely many equivalent values, is the structure of the Higgs field. In [MIT 8.286 Lecture 22](/courses/8-286/higgs-field) (taught by a stand-in lecturer) the Higgs field settles at random into one of its equivalent minima as the universe cools, which breaks the symmetry; the [next page there](/courses/8-286/magnetic-monopoles) shows how regions that chose differently leave defects behind, the magnetic monopoles of grand unified theories.
</Callout>

## Summary

- At $T = 0$ all bosons share one wavefunction, normalised to $N$, so $|\psi|^2$ is the density and $E = \int[(\hbar^2/2m)|\nabla\psi|^2 + U|\psi|^2]$.
- The ground state minimises $\Omega = E - \mu N$; the condition $[-\hbar^2\nabla^2/2m + U - \mu]\psi_0 = 0$ is the right-hand side of the equation of motion, so $\psi_0$ is time-independent.
- Free bosons in a constant $U$ need $\mu = U$, and then any $\psi_0 = \sqrt n\,e^{i\theta}$ works; excitations are free particles, $\hbar\omega = \hbar^2k^2/2m$.
- A contact repulsion adds $\frac{v_0}{2}|\psi|^4$ and gives the Gross-Pitaevskii equation. With $U = 0$: $\psi_0 = 0$ for $\mu < 0$; $\psi_0 = \sqrt{\mu/v_0}\,e^{i\theta}$ with a chosen phase for $\mu > 0$, $\mu = v_0n$, $\Omega/V = -\mu^2/2v_0$: a quantum phase transition.
- Collective modes: $\omega_k^2 = (\hbar^2k^2/2m + 2\mu)k^2/2m$, sound with $v_s = \sqrt{v_0n/m}$ at long wavelength, so $C_V \propto T^3$. The broken phase symmetry is what makes the mode gapless.
