This set practises the thermal fluctuations of [Chapter VI of Wen's notes](/courses/8-08/volume-energy-fluctuations) (the fluctuation of any parameter from the curvature of the free energy, and the [fluctuations of energy and of particle number](/courses/8-08/particle-number-fluctuations)) and the Bose gases of Chapter VII: the [photon gas](/courses/8-08/photon-and-phonon-gases) and the [ideal Bose gas with its condensate](/courses/8-08/ideal-bose-gas-condensation). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 6 (dated March 9, 2005), in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its dates are left out. MIT's Problem 1 is stated in full on the sheet and is given here as MIT sets it, in our words; MIT's small sketch of it is described in words instead of reproduced. Our additions to it are marked where they stand: the box for the curvature of the free energy in part (a), the numbers of part (c) and the choices of part (d). MIT's Problems 2, 3 and 4 point to problems in a textbook (K. Huang, *Statistical Mechanics*) by number only. The textbook is not used here. **Problems 2 and 3 are our own problems**: energy fluctuations of a collection of two-level atoms (Chapter VI), and the fluctuations of the number of bosons in one level, for photons and for a condensate (Chapters VI and VII). In Problem 4 MIT adds a question of its own: by what factor does the total number of photons in the universe change after the expansion? **The setup of Problem 4 and its parts (a), (b), (d) and (e) are ours**, a photon gas in a slowly expanding universe, built so that MIT's question applies; part (c) is MIT's question, rewritten.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `a*exp(-b/(kB*T))/(1 + c)^2`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `kB` stands for $k_B$, `eps` for $\varepsilon$, `nbar` for $\bar n$, `Ti` for $T_i$, `sqrt` is the square root and `exp` the exponential; the other letters (`N`, `L`, `T`, `s`) are typed as they are.

MIT's sheet gives no points. The points are ours: 20 for Problem 1, 15 each for Problems 2 and 3, and 20 for Problem 4, 70 points in all; every split between parts is ours too. Parts that ask you to show something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes. $k_B$ is Boltzmann's constant, $T$ the temperature, $\beta = 1/k_BT$, $A$ the free energy and $C_V$ the heat capacity at fixed volume. A bar or angle brackets mark an average. In Problem 1, $x$ is the position of the wall and $N$ the number of molecules on each side. In Problem 2, $\varepsilon$ is the energy of the excited state of one atom and $N$ the number of atoms. In Problem 3, $n$ is the number of bosons in one level and $\bar n$ its average. In Problem 4, $\sigma = \pi^2k_B^4/15(\hbar c)^3$ is the constant of the notes in $U/V = \sigma T^4$ (an energy density constant, not the Stefan–Boltzmann constant of a radiating surface), and the subscripts i (initial) and f (final) mark the start and the end of the expansion.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The jitter of a sliding wall between two gases" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

A closed box of size $L \times L \times 2L$ is split into two halves by a wall, so that each half is a cube $L \times L \times L$. The wall can slide freely along the long direction of the box. Each half holds an ideal gas of $N$ molecules, and the whole box sits in a heat bath that keeps both gases at the temperature $T$. (MIT's sheet has a small sketch: the long box seen from the side, the upright wall in the middle with a double arrow showing that it slides left and right, and an axis $x$ along the box.) Let $x$ be the position of the wall, measured from the left end of the box (our choice of origin), so that its average is $\bar x = L$. The size of the wall's thermal jitter is

<Formula label="The fluctuation of the wall's position" tex="\Delta x = \sqrt{\langle(x - \bar x)^2\rangle}, \qquad \bar x = \langle x\rangle" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={5}>

(Our addition.) The probability of a wall position $x$ is proportional to $e^{-\beta A_{\text{tot}}(x)}$, where $A_{\text{tot}}(x)$ is the free energy of the two gases together when the wall is at $x$. Find the curvature $\partial^2A_{\text{tot}}/\partial x^2$ at $x = \bar x = L$. It plays the part of the stiffness of a spring that holds the wall in the middle.

<Hint>

When the wall is at $x$, the left gas has the volume $V_1 = L^2x$ and the right gas $V_2 = L^2(2L - x)$. Use $\partial A/\partial V = -P$ for each gas.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\partial A_{\text{tot}}/\partial x = L^2(-P_1 + P_2)$, minus the net force of the two gases on the wall. Differentiate once more with $P = Nk_BT/V$, and remember that $dV_1/dx = L^2$ and $dV_2/dx = -L^2$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-curv" label="$\partial^2A_{\text{tot}}/\partial x^2$ at $x = L$:" expr="2*N*kB*T/L^2"
  vars={{ N: [1, 100], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2], L: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ N: "N", kB: "k_B", T: "T", L: "L" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "N*kB*T/L^2", hint: "That is the stiffness from one gas only. Moving the wall squeezes one gas and lets the other expand, and both push it back towards the middle: their curvatures add." },
    { expr: "2*N*kB*T/L^6", hint: "That is the curvature in the volume, the sum of ∂²A/∂V² = Nk_BT/V² of the two gases. The wall moves by x, and the volumes change by L² dx, so the curvature in x is L⁴ times larger." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The wall is a parameter that can fluctuate, with the free energy of the two gases as its potential. Expand that free energy to second order about the middle.

<Step title="The force on the wall">

For each gas $\partial A/\partial V = -P$. With $V_1 = L^2x$ and $V_2 = L^2(2L - x)$,

<Formula label="Slope of the free energy of the two gases" tex="\frac{\partial A_{\text{tot}}}{\partial x} = L^2\left(-P_1 + P_2\right) = -Nk_BT\left(\frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{2L - x}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

which is zero at $x = L$, where the two pressures are equal: the middle is the equilibrium position.

</Step>

<Step title="The curvature">

<Formula label="Curvature of the free energy of the two gases" tex="\frac{\partial^2A_{\text{tot}}}{\partial x^2} = Nk_BT\left(\frac{1}{x^2} + \frac{1}{(2L - x)^2}\right) = \frac{2Nk_BT}{L^2} \quad\text{at } x = L" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is the same curvature as in the notes' ideal gas, $-\partial P/\partial V = Nk_BT/V^2$ (reading 1, p. 47), once for each gas and multiplied by $L^4$ because $dV = \pm L^2\,dx$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Units: $Nk_BT$ is an energy, and an energy over a length squared is a force per length, the unit of a spring constant. In terms of the pressure $P = Nk_BT/L^3$ of each gas, the stiffness is $2PL$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the fluctuation $\Delta x$ of the position of the wall.

<Hint>

Use the general rule of the notes for a parameter that can fluctuate: the mean square deviation is $k_BT$ divided by the curvature of the free energy.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-dx" label="$\Delta x =$" expr="L/sqrt(2*N)"
  vars={{ N: [1, 100], L: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ N: "N", L: "L" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "L/sqrt(N)", hint: "That keeps only one gas. Both gases push the wall back, so the curvature is twice that of one gas and the mean square deviation half as large." },
    { expr: "L^2/(2*N)", hint: "That is the mean square deviation ⟨(x − x̄)²⟩. The fluctuation Δx is its square root, a length." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Fluctuation is $k_BT$ times the response: the softer the spring, the larger the jitter.

<Step title="The rule">

<Formula label="Fluctuation of a parameter" tex="\langle(x - \bar x)^2\rangle = \frac{k_BT}{\left.\partial^2A/\partial x^2\right|_{\bar x}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 47, point ④" />

</Step>

<Step title="The wall">

With the curvature of part (a),

<Formula label="Fluctuation of the wall" tex="\langle(x - \bar x)^2\rangle = \frac{k_BT\,L^2}{2Nk_BT} = \frac{L^2}{2N}, \qquad \Delta x = \frac{L}{\sqrt{2N}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The temperature drops out: a hotter gas pushes harder, which stiffens the spring exactly as much as the extra thermal energy shakes it.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The relative jitter $\Delta x/L = 1/\sqrt{2N}$ is the $1/\sqrt N$ of the notes' volume fluctuations, $\Delta V/V = 1/\sqrt N$ (reading 1, p. 47), with $2N$ molecules pushing on the wall in place of $N$. Exactly, $e^{-\beta A_{\text{tot}}}$ is proportional to $x^N(2L - x)^N$, and its variance is $L^2/(2N + 3)$ (for $N = 5$, $L^2/13$); for a large $N$ this is $L^2/(2N)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

(Our numbers.) Take $L = 10.0$ cm, with air at $1.00$ atm ($101\,325$ Pa) and $300$ K in each half, treated as an ideal gas. Give $\Delta x$ in picometres (1 pm $= 10^{-12}$ m; tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

First find $N$ from $PV = Nk_BT$ with $V = L^3$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-num" label="$\Delta x$ in pm:" value="0.452" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count the molecules, then use part (b).

<Step title="The number of molecules">

$N = PL^3/k_BT = 101\,325 \times 10^{-3}/(1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 300) = 2.45 \times 10^{22}$ in each half.

</Step>

<Step title="The jitter">

$\Delta x = 0.100/\sqrt{2 \times 2.45 \times 10^{22}}$ m $= 4.52 \times 10^{-13}$ m $= 0.452$ pm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

This is a few hundred times smaller than an atom: the jitter of a macroscopic wall is far too small to see, which is why a piston in a cylinder seems to stand still. The stiffness of part (a) is $2PL = 2.03 \times 10^4$ N/m, a stiff spring indeed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

(MIT's question; the choices are ours.) Does your result depend on the mass of the wall?

<Answer id="p1-mass" label="The fluctuation of the wall's position" choices={["is smaller for a heavier wall, as Δx ∝ 1/√M", "does not depend on the mass: only the wall's speeds do", "is larger for a heavier wall, which the gas cannot stop", "grows with the mass, as Δx ∝ √M"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In classical statistical mechanics the Boltzmann factor of the wall splits into a kinetic part and a part that depends on the position.

<Step title="Position and momentum separate">

The energy of the wall is $p^2/2M$ plus the free energy of the gases, which depends on $x$ only. The probability of a position and a momentum is then the product of $e^{-\beta p^2/2M}$ and $e^{-\beta A_{\text{tot}}(x)}$. Integrating over $p$ leaves a factor that does not depend on $x$, so the distribution of positions, and $\Delta x$, does not contain $M$.

</Step>

<Step title="What the mass does">

The mass sets the speed of the wall, $\langle p^2\rangle/M = k_BT$ (equipartition), and so how fast it wanders: a heavy wall drifts slowly to and fro, a light one quickly. Both cover the same range of positions. The answer does not depend on the mass, as long as the wall moves classically: when $k_BT$ is small next to $\hbar$ times the frequency of its oscillation, quantum mechanics changes this, but for a macroscopic wall that does not happen at any ordinary temperature.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Energy fluctuations of two-level atoms (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on energy fluctuations, a subject of Chapter VI of the notes.

Take $N$ atoms, each with two states only: a ground state of energy $0$ and an excited state of energy $\varepsilon > 0$ (our model). The atoms do not interact, and they are held at the temperature $T$ by a heat bath, so that their total energy $E$ fluctuates.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the average energy $\bar E$.

<Hint>

Write the partition function of one atom first, with its two states; the atoms are independent, so their average energies add.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-e" label="$\bar E =$" expr="N*eps/(exp(eps/(kB*T)) + 1)"
  vars={{ N: [1, 100], eps: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ N: "N", eps: "\\varepsilon", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "N*eps*exp(-eps/(kB*T))", hint: "That uses the Boltzmann factor e^(−βε) as if it were the probability. The probabilities of the two states must add to 1, so divide by the partition function of one atom, 1 + e^(−βε)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each atom contributes $\varepsilon$ times its probability of being excited.

<Step title="One atom">

The partition function of one atom is $1 + e^{-\beta\varepsilon}$, so the probability of the excited state is $p = e^{-\beta\varepsilon}/(1 + e^{-\beta\varepsilon}) = 1/(e^{\beta\varepsilon} + 1)$.

</Step>

<Step title="All atoms">

<Formula label="Average energy of two-level atoms" tex="\bar E = N\varepsilon p = \frac{N\varepsilon}{e^{\beta\varepsilon} + 1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At low temperature $\bar E \to 0$: every atom is in its ground state. At high temperature $\bar E \to N\varepsilon/2$: the two states become equally likely, and no temperature can excite more than half the atoms.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the mean square fluctuation $\langle(E - \bar E)^2\rangle$ directly from the probabilities of the states.

<Hint>

For one atom, $\langle E_1^2\rangle = \varepsilon^2p$ and $\langle E_1\rangle = \varepsilon p$, where $E_1$ is its energy. Then use that the atoms are independent.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The variances of independent quantities add: the total is $N$ times the variance of one atom.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-var" label="$\langle(E - \bar E)^2\rangle =$" expr="N*eps^2*exp(eps/(kB*T))/(exp(eps/(kB*T)) + 1)^2"
  vars={{ N: [1, 100], eps: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ N: "N", eps: "\\varepsilon", kB: "k_B", T: "T" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "N*eps^2/(exp(eps/(kB*T)) + 1)", hint: "That is N⟨E₁²⟩ = Nε²p, the sum of the mean squares of the atoms' energies, not the spread of the total. Subtract the square of the mean for each atom: ε²p − ε²p² = ε²p(1 − p)." },
    { expr: "N^2*eps^2*exp(eps/(kB*T))/(exp(eps/(kB*T)) + 1)^2", hint: "That grows as N², as if all the atoms were excited or not together. They are independent, so their variances add and the total grows only as N." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One atom is a two-valued random variable; the total energy is a sum of $N$ independent copies.

<Step title="One atom">

The energy $E_1$ of one atom is $\varepsilon$ with the probability $p$ and $0$ with $1 - p$, so $\langle E_1^2\rangle - \langle E_1\rangle^2 = \varepsilon^2p - \varepsilon^2p^2 = \varepsilon^2p(1 - p)$.

</Step>

<Step title="All atoms">

<Formula label="Energy fluctuation of two-level atoms" tex="\langle(E - \bar E)^2\rangle = N\varepsilon^2p(1 - p) = \frac{N\varepsilon^2e^{\beta\varepsilon}}{\left(e^{\beta\varepsilon} + 1\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

using $1 - p = e^{\beta\varepsilon}/(e^{\beta\varepsilon} + 1)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At high temperature $p = \frac12$ and the result is $N\varepsilon^2/4$: $N$ coin flips of size $\varepsilon$. At low temperature it vanishes, as $N\varepsilon^2e^{-\beta\varepsilon}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Show that your result equals $k_BT^2C_V$, with $C_V = d\bar E/dT$, as the notes' general rule for energy fluctuations says.

<Hint>

Differentiate $\bar E$ of part (a) with respect to $T$, using $d(\beta\varepsilon)/dT = -\beta\varepsilon/T$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two routes, one answer: the direct count of part (b) and the curvature of the entropy behind the rule of the notes.

<Step title="The rule">

<Formula label="Energy fluctuations" tex="\langle(E - \bar E)^2\rangle = k_BT^2C_V" cite="reading1" at="p. 46, point ③" />

</Step>

<Step title="The heat capacity">

$C_V = \dfrac{d\bar E}{dT} = N\varepsilon\,\dfrac{e^{\beta\varepsilon}}{(e^{\beta\varepsilon} + 1)^2}\,\dfrac{\varepsilon}{k_BT^2}$, the Schottky heat capacity of two-level atoms. So $k_BT^2C_V = N\varepsilon^2e^{\beta\varepsilon}/(e^{\beta\varepsilon} + 1)^2$, the result of part (b).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The rule says a large heat capacity means large energy fluctuations. Here $C_V$ has its peak near $k_BT \approx 0.42\,\varepsilon$, and so does $\langle(E - \bar E)^2\rangle/T^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

(Our numbers.) Take $N = 1000$ atoms with $\varepsilon = 5k_BT$. Give the relative fluctuation $\delta E/\bar E$, with $\delta E = \sqrt{\langle(E - \bar E)^2\rangle}$, as a decimal (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

Write the ratio with $p$: $\delta E/\bar E = \sqrt{N\varepsilon^2p(1 - p)}/(N\varepsilon p)$. Then put in $p = 1/(e^5 + 1)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-num" label="$\delta E/\bar E$:" value="0.385" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The ratio depends only on $N$ and $p$.

<Step title="The numbers">

$p = 1/(e^5 + 1) = 6.69 \times 10^{-3}$, so on average $Np = 6.69$ atoms are excited, and $\delta E/\bar E = \sqrt{(1 - p)/(Np)} = \sqrt{0.9933/6.69} = 0.385$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The notes' estimate $\delta E/\bar E \sim 1/\sqrt N$ would give $0.032$ for $1000$ atoms: the fluctuation here is twelve times larger.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

(Our addition.) What happens to $\delta E/\bar E$ at low temperature, $k_BT \ll \varepsilon$, for a fixed $N$?

<Answer id="p2-low" label="At low temperature, δE/Ē" choices={["goes to zero, because the energy no longer changes", "stays near 1/√N, as for any large system", "becomes √(2/(3N)), the value of an ideal gas", "grows, as 1/√(average number of excited atoms)"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The energy is $\varepsilon$ times the number of excited atoms, a count that becomes small.

<Step title="The limit">

For $p \ll 1$, $\delta E/\bar E = \sqrt{(1 - p)/(Np)} \approx 1/\sqrt{Np}$, and $Np$ is the average number of excited atoms. When it falls to a few, the energy fluctuates by a large fraction of itself; at $Np < 1$ the fluctuation exceeds the mean. The rule $\delta E/\bar E \sim 1/\sqrt N$ assumes that every particle shares the energy; here only the excited ones carry it, so it is their number that counts. Part (d) is an example.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="How much the number of bosons in one level fluctuates (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the fluctuations (Chapter VI) of the Bose gases of Chapter VII.

In a gas of bosons that do not interact, each level is an independent subsystem. The probability that a level of energy $\varepsilon$ holds $n$ bosons is

<Formula label="Probability of n bosons in one level" tex="P(n) = e^{-\beta n(\varepsilon - \mu)}\left(1 - e^{-\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)}\right), \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots" cite="reading2" at="p. 55, point ③" />

where $\mu$ is the chemical potential; for photons $\mu = 0$ (reading 2, p. 49). Its average is $\bar n = 1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon - \mu)} - 1)$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the mean square fluctuation $\langle(n - \bar n)^2\rangle$ of the number of bosons in the level, written with $\bar n$ alone.

<Hint>

Write $y = \beta(\varepsilon - \mu)$. The average is $\bar n = -\partial\ln Q/\partial y$ with $Q = 1/(1 - e^{-y})$; one more derivative gives the variance.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\langle(n - \bar n)^2\rangle = \partial^2\ln Q/\partial y^2 = -\partial\bar n/\partial y$. Write the result in terms of $\bar n$ using $e^y = 1 + 1/\bar n$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-var" label="$\langle(n - \bar n)^2\rangle =$" expr="nbar*(1 + nbar)"
  vars={{ nbar: [0.1, 5] }}
  names={{ nbar: "\\bar n" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "nbar", hint: "That is the variance of a Poisson count, the number of independent classical particles in a small region. Bosons in one level are not independent of each other: their distribution is geometric, not Poisson, and its spread is larger." },
    { expr: "nbar^2", hint: "That is only the part that dominates when the level is crowded, n̄ ≫ 1. For a nearly empty level the variance must tend to n̄, as for rare independent events: keep the term linear in n̄ as well." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a distribution of the form $e^{-yn}/Q$, the derivatives of $\ln Q$ in $y$ give the average and the variance.

<Step title="Average and variance">

With $Q = \sum_n e^{-yn} = 1/(1 - e^{-y})$, $\bar n = -\partial\ln Q/\partial y$ and $\langle n^2\rangle - \bar n^2 = \partial^2\ln Q/\partial y^2 = -\partial\bar n/\partial y$. With $\bar n = 1/(e^y - 1)$,

<Formula label="Variance of the number of bosons in one level" tex="\langle(n - \bar n)^2\rangle = \frac{e^y}{\left(e^y - 1\right)^2} = \bar n\,(1 + \bar n)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

since $e^y/(e^y - 1) = 1 + \bar n$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For a nearly empty level, $\bar n \ll 1$, the variance is $\bar n$, the Poisson value of rare, independent arrivals. For a crowded level, $\bar n \gg 1$, it is $\bar n^2$: the number fluctuates by as much as its own size. At $y = 1$: $\bar n = 0.582$ and $\bar n(1 + \bar n) = 0.921$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Now take a whole black-body gas of photons in a box, at the temperature $T$. Its total number of photons $N$ fluctuates. Find the ratio $\langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle/\bar N$ as a decimal (tolerance 1%). You may use $\int_0^\infty x\,dx/(e^x - 1) = \pi^2/6$ and $\int_0^\infty x^2\,dx/(e^x - 1) = 2\zeta(3) = 2.404$.

<Hint>

The levels are independent, so the variances add: $\langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle = \sum\bar n(1 + \bar n)$ over all levels and both polarisations. Turn both sums into integrals over $x = \beta\hbar ck$, as for the photon density in the notes.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The common factors cancel in the ratio, which becomes $\int x^2e^x/(e^x - 1)^2\,dx$ over $\int x^2/(e^x - 1)\,dx$. Integrate the numerator by parts, using $\frac{d}{dx}\left[-\frac{1}{e^x - 1}\right] = \frac{e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-ratio" label="$\langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle/\bar N$:" value="1.368" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Sum the variance of part (a) over every level, exactly as the average is summed to give the photon density.

<Step title="Sum over levels">

With $\mu = 0$ and $x = \beta\hbar ck$, each level has $\bar n = 1/(e^x - 1)$ and $\bar n(1 + \bar n) = e^x/(e^x - 1)^2$. The sum over levels and polarisations is the same integral $2V\int d^3k/(2\pi)^3$ for both (reading 2, p. 50), and its prefactor cancels in the ratio:

<Formula label="Photon-number fluctuation of a black body" tex="\frac{\langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle}{\bar N} = \frac{\int_0^\infty dx\,x^2e^x/(e^x - 1)^2}{\int_0^\infty dx\,x^2/(e^x - 1)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numerator">

By parts, $\int_0^\infty x^2\frac{e^x}{(e^x - 1)^2}dx = \left[-\frac{x^2}{e^x - 1}\right]_0^\infty + \int_0^\infty\frac{2x}{e^x - 1}dx = 0 + 2 \times \frac{\pi^2}{6} = \frac{\pi^2}{3} = 3.290$. (The boundary term vanishes at both ends: $x^2/(e^x - 1) \approx x$ near zero.)

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

$\dfrac{\pi^2/3}{2\zeta(3)} = \dfrac{3.290}{2.404} = 1.368$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The ratio is more than 1: the photon number of a black body fluctuates more than a Poisson count of the same mean, because of the $\bar n^2$ term of the crowded low-energy levels. It is not much more, because most photons sit in levels with $\bar n$ below 1. The relative fluctuation is $\sqrt{1.368/\bar N}$, which falls as $1/\sqrt{\bar N}$, as the notes' rule for large systems says.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

(Our addition.) Below the condensation temperature of an ideal Bose gas, in the grand canonical ensemble of the notes, the lowest level holds $N_0$ bosons on average, a large number. Take $N_0 = 10^6$. According to part (a), how large is the fluctuation $\Delta N_0 = \sqrt{\langle(n - N_0)^2\rangle}$ of the number of bosons in that level?

<Answer id="p3-cond" label="The grand canonical ensemble gives" choices={["ΔN₀ about 10³, so ΔN₀/N₀ about 10⁻³", "ΔN₀ about 1, a single boson more or less", "ΔN₀ about 10⁶, about as large as N₀ itself", "ΔN₀ = 0, since the condensate is one quantum state"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The condensate is one level with a huge $\bar n$, so the $\bar n^2$ term of part (a) wins.

<Step title="The size">

With $\bar n = N_0 = 10^6$, $\langle(n - N_0)^2\rangle = N_0(1 + N_0)$ and $\Delta N_0 = \sqrt{N_0(N_0 + 1)} \approx N_0 = 10^6$: the number in the condensate would fluctuate by 100%. (With $\varepsilon = 0$ the average is $N_0 = z/(1 - z)$, $z = e^{\beta\mu}$. The notes, p. 58, write $N_0 = 1/(z - 1)$, from a sum written with $z\,e^{\beta\varepsilon}$ where $z^{-1}e^{\beta\varepsilon}$ is meant: a slip, since their own graph there takes $z = e^{\beta\mu} < 1$.)

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

This is not what a real condensate does. The grand canonical ensemble lets the gas trade bosons freely with a reservoir, and the cheapest place to put or take them is the level at $\mu \approx 0$. A trapped gas of atoms has a fixed total number, and then the condensate can only change by trading atoms with the excited levels, which makes its fluctuation far smaller than $N_0$. The averages of the grand canonical ensemble are still right; its fluctuation of the condensate is not.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Photons in an expanding universe (setup ours, part c MIT's)" points={20} mit="Problem 4">

MIT's sheet gives the number of a textbook problem here and adds one question of its own, part (c) below: by what factor does the total number of photons in the universe grow or shrink after the expansion? The setup and the other parts are ours, built so that MIT's question applies.

Treat the radiation that fills the universe as a black-body photon gas in a large box of volume $V$ whose walls move outwards as the universe expands, so that every length grows by the same factor. The expansion is slow, so the gas stays in equilibrium, and no heat flows into the box from outside (our assumptions). From the notes, the photon gas has

<Formula label="Energy, pressure and number of a photon gas" tex="U = \sigma VT^4, \qquad P = \frac{U}{3V}, \qquad N = \kappa V\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar c}\right)^3, \quad \kappa = \frac{1}{\pi^2}\int_0^\infty dx\,\frac{x^2}{e^x - 1} = 0.2436" cite="reading2" at="pp. 50–52" />

(the notes print $\kappa = 0.23\ldots$ on p. 50, where the integral gives $0.2436$). During the expansion every length grows by the factor $s > 1$, so $V_f = s^3V_i$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the final temperature $T_f$ in terms of the initial temperature $T_i$ and $s$.

<Hint>

No heat flows in, so the energy changes only by the work the gas does on the moving walls: $dU = -P\,dV$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put $U = \sigma VT^4$ and $P = \sigma T^4/3$ into $dU = -P\,dV$ and separate the variables: you get a relation between $dT/T$ and $dV/V$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-t" label="$T_f =$" expr="Ti/s"
  vars={{ Ti: [1, 10], s: [1.5, 5] }}
  names={{ Ti: "T_i", s: "s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Ti/s^3", hint: "That makes T fall as 1/V. Work it through: dU = −P dV with U = σVT⁴ gives dT/T = −(1/3) dV/V, so T falls only as V^(−1/3)." },
    { expr: "Ti*s^(-3/4)", hint: "That keeps U = σVT⁴ fixed, as if the radiation did no work. It pushes on the moving walls with P = U/3V and loses the energy P dV." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A slow expansion without heat is a reversible adiabatic one: energy goes only into work.

<Step title="Energy balance">

$d(\sigma VT^4) = -\frac13\sigma T^4\,dV$ gives $4\sigma VT^3\,dT + \sigma T^4\,dV = -\frac13\sigma T^4\,dV$, that is

<Formula label="Cooling of radiation in a slow expansion" tex="\frac{dT}{T} = -\frac13\,\frac{dV}{V} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad VT^3 = \text{const}, \qquad T_f = T_i\left(\frac{V_i}{V_f}\right)^{1/3} = \frac{T_i}{s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check with single photons">

Each level's energy is $\varepsilon_k = \hbar c|\vec k| \propto V^{-1/3}$ (reading 2, p. 52): when every length grows by $s$, every wavelength grows by $s$ and every photon energy falls by $s$. A Planck spectrum at $T_i$ with every energy divided by $s$ is a Planck spectrum at $T_i/s$. This is the cooling of the cosmic background radiation as the universe expands, in inverse proportion to its scale factor, met in [8.286](/courses/8-286/black-body-radiation).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the ratio $U_f/U_i$ of the final to the initial energy of the photon gas.

<Hint>

Use $U = \sigma VT^4$ with part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-u" label="$U_f/U_i =$" expr="1/s"
  vars={{ s: [1.5, 5] }}
  names={{ s: "s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1", hint: "Energy would be conserved only if the gas did no work. It pushes on the walls as they move outwards, with the pressure U/3V, and so loses energy." },
    { expr: "s^(-4)", hint: "That is the ratio of the energy densities, U/V ∝ T⁴. The total energy also has the factor V, which grows as s³." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Combine $V \propto s^3$ with $T^4 \propto s^{-4}$.

<Step title="The ratio">

$U_f/U_i = (V_f/V_i)(T_f/T_i)^4 = s^3 \times s^{-4} = 1/s$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The lost energy is the work done on the walls: $\int P\,dV = \int\frac13\sigma T^4\,dV$ with $T = T_i(V_i/V)^{1/3}$ gives $\sigma V_iT_i^4(1 - 1/s) = U_i - U_f$. Each photon keeps existing but loses energy as its wavelength stretches.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

(MIT's question.) By what factor does the total number of photons change in this expansion? Give $N_f/N_i$ as a decimal (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

$N$ is proportional to $VT^3$. What did part (a) say about that product?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-n" label="$N_f/N_i$:" value="1" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-n" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The number of photons is $\kappa(k_B/\hbar c)^3$ times $VT^3$, and part (a) found $VT^3$ unchanged.

<Step title="The factor">

$N_f/N_i = (V_f/V_i)(T_f/T_i)^3 = s^3 \times s^{-3} = 1$. The number of photons neither grows nor shrinks: the box holds just as many photons, each with $1/s$ of its former energy, spread over $s^3$ times the volume, so the number density falls as $1/s^3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

In the picture of single photons, a slow expansion stretches every level without moving photons between levels: each occupation number $n_k$ stays what it was, so their sum stays the same. Nothing in a photon gas conserves the number of photons in general (walls absorb and emit them), but a slow expansion of the gas alone does.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

</Solution>

</Part>
