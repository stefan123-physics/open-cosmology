Statistical physics never predicts what one molecule does; it predicts probabilities, and averages over them. Chapter V of Wen's notes (pp. 36–40) collects the probability that the rest of the course needs: one random variable, two random variables and what it means for them to be independent, the binomial distribution, and the central limit theorem. 8.044 built the same tools for continuous variables with probability densities, in [one random variable](/courses/8-044/one-random-variable), [two random variables](/courses/8-044/two-random-variables) and [sums and the central limit theorem](/courses/8-044/sums-central-limit-theorem); this page follows Wen's shorter route, which works with discrete variables and sums instead of integrals, and links to those pages for the parts they treat at length. (MIT's material for this chapter is five scanned handwritten pages, numbered 36 to 40 at the top right; citations use those numbers and Wen's circled point numbers. Everything here is in our own words, with our own examples.)

## One random variable

A **random variable** $s$ is a quantity whose value is not fixed but comes with a probability: $P(s)$ is the probability that it takes the value $s$ (notes p. 36, point ①). The probabilities of all possible values add up to one:

<Formula label="Normalization" tex="\sum_s P(s) = 1" cite="reading1" at="p. 36, point ①" />

The distribution $P(s)$ says everything there is to know about $s$. Two kinds of number summarize it. The **average** and, more generally, the **moments** are

<Formula label="Average and moments" tex="\langle s\rangle = \sum_s s\,P(s), \qquad \langle s^n\rangle = \sum_s s^n P(s)" cite="reading1" at="p. 36, point ①" />

and the **fluctuation** $\Delta s$, the typical distance of $s$ from its average, is the square root of the average squared distance:

<Formula label="Fluctuation" tex="\Delta s = \sqrt{\langle(s - \langle s\rangle)^2\rangle} = \sqrt{\langle s^2\rangle - \langle s\rangle^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 36, point ①" />

The second form follows by expanding the square: $\langle(s - \langle s\rangle)^2\rangle = \langle s^2\rangle - 2\langle s\rangle\langle s\rangle + \langle s\rangle^2$, since $\langle s\rangle$ is a fixed number that comes out of the average. The square $(\Delta s)^2$ is the **variance**. For example, if $s$ is 1 with probability $p$ and 0 otherwise, then $\langle s\rangle = p$ and, since $s^2 = s$, also $\langle s^2\rangle = p$; so $(\Delta s)^2 = p - p^2 = p(1 - p)$. This two-valued variable is the building block of the rest of the chapter. (For continuous variables the sums become integrals over a probability density, as on the [8.044 page](/courses/8-044/one-random-variable).)

## Two random variables and their joint probability

Now take two random variables $s_1$ and $s_2$, each of which can be 0 or 1 (notes p. 36, point ②). Each has its own distribution: $P_1(1)$ is the probability that $s_1 = 1$, and $P_1(0) = 1 - P_1(1)$; likewise $P_2(1)$ and $P_2(0) = 1 - P_2(1)$ for $s_2$.

What is the probability that $s_1 = 1$ **and** $s_2 = 1$? It is tempting to answer $P_1(1)P_2(1)$, and Wen writes exactly that with a question mark, then marks it wrong with a cross: in general it is wrong. Knowing the two separate distributions is not enough, because they say nothing about whether the two variables tend to go together. The complete description is the **joint probability** $P_{12}(s_1, s_2)$, one number for each pair of values, boxed in the notes. Its four values add up to one:

<Formula label="Joint probability" tex="P_{12}(1,1) + P_{12}(1,0) + P_{12}(0,1) + P_{12}(0,0) = 1" cite="reading1" at="p. 36, point ②" />

The joint probability contains the separate distributions. The probability that $s_1 = 1$, whatever $s_2$ is, is the sum over both values of $s_2$:

<Formula label="Probability of one variable alone" tex="P_1(1) = P_{12}(1,1) + P_{12}(1,0)" cite="reading1" at="p. 36, point ②" />

In general $P_1(s_1) = \sum_{s_2}P_{12}(s_1, s_2)$: summing out one variable leaves the distribution of the other (in 8.044 this is the "marginal" density, obtained by integrating instead of summing). The reverse is not possible: $P_1$ and $P_2$ together have two free numbers, $P_{12}$ has three.

## Conditional probability

Suppose we are told that $s_2 = 1$. What is now the probability of each value of $s_1$ (notes p. 37)? Only the outcomes with $s_2 = 1$ are left, so the new probability is proportional to $P_{12}(s_1, 1)$. Dividing by the sum over $s_1$ makes it add up to one again. This is the **conditional probability**, which Wen writes "P if s₂ = 1" of $s_1$ and we write in the usual way with a bar, read "given":

<Formula label="Conditional probability" tex="P(s_1 \mid s_2 = 1) = \frac{P_{12}(s_1, 1)}{\sum_{s_1 = 0,1}P_{12}(s_1, 1)}, \qquad P(s_1 \mid s_2 = 0) = \frac{P_{12}(s_1, 0)}{\sum_{s_1 = 0,1}P_{12}(s_1, 0)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 37" />

The denominator of the first is $P_2(1)$ and that of the second $P_2(0)$, by the rule for one variable alone. If the two conditional probabilities are the same, knowing $s_2$ tells us nothing about $s_1$.

## Two dice examples

Wen makes this concrete with a fair die (notes pp. 37–38). Let $m = 1, \ldots, 6$ be the number it shows (the notes call it $n$; we keep $n$ for the number of variables below), each with probability $1/6$, and define two random variables from the same roll.

**Example I** (p. 37): $s_1 = 1$ if $m$ is even, $s_2 = 1$ if $m$ is a multiple of 3, and 0 otherwise. Then $P_1(1) = 1/2$ and $P_2(1) = 1/3$. The joint probabilities come from listing the faces: $s_1 = s_2 = 1$ only for $m = 6$, and so on.

| | $s_2 = 1$ | $s_2 = 0$ |
|---|---|---|
| $s_1 = 1$ | $1/6$ (6) | $1/3$ (2, 4) |
| $s_1 = 0$ | $1/6$ (3) | $1/3$ (1, 5) |

Given $s_2 = 1$, the probabilities of $s_1 = 1$ and $s_1 = 0$ are $\frac{1/6}{1/6 + 1/6} = \frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{1}{2}$; given $s_2 = 0$ they are $\frac{1/3}{2/3} = \frac{1}{2}$ and $\frac{1}{2}$ again. In the other direction, given $s_1 = 1$ the probabilities of $s_2 = 1$ and $s_2 = 0$ are $\frac{1}{3}$ and $\frac{2}{3}$, and given $s_1 = 0$ the same. Knowing one variable never changes the odds of the other: Wen concludes that $s_1$ and $s_2$ are **independent random variables**. Indeed every entry of the table is the product of the separate probabilities, for example $P_{12}(1,1) = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6}$.

**Example II** (p. 38): now $s_1 = 1$ if $m = 1, 2, 3$ and 0 if $m = 4, 5, 6$, while $s_2 = 1$ if $m$ is even and 0 if it is odd. (The notes write "$s_2 = 1$" in both halves of the definition; the second must be $s_2 = 0$, which is what the table in the notes uses.) Each variable alone is 1 or 0 with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, but the joint table is lopsided:

| | $s_2 = 1$ | $s_2 = 0$ |
|---|---|---|
| $s_1 = 1$ | $1/6$ (2) | $1/3$ (1, 3) |
| $s_1 = 0$ | $1/3$ (4, 6) | $1/6$ (5) |

Given $s_2 = 1$, the probabilities of $s_1 = 1$ and $s_1 = 0$ are $\frac{1/6}{1/2} = \frac{1}{3}$ and $\frac{2}{3}$; given $s_2 = 0$ they are $\frac{2}{3}$ and $\frac{1}{3}$. These are not equal: an even roll makes a low roll less likely, because only one of the three even faces is low. The variables are **not independent**, although each alone is a fair coin.

## Independence and correlation

If $s_1$ and $s_2$ are independent, the joint probability can be built from the separate ones (notes p. 38, boxed):

<Formula label="Independent random variables" tex="P_{12}(s_1, s_2) = P_1(s_1)\,P_2(s_2)" cite="reading1" at="p. 38 (boxed)" />

This is the same statement as before: with a product, the conditional probability $P_1(s_1)P_2(1)/\sum P_1(s_1')P_2(1) = P_1(s_1)$ does not depend on $s_2$. One consequence is that the average of the product is the product of the averages, because the double sum splits:

<Formula label="Average of a product of independent variables" tex="\langle s_1s_2\rangle = \sum_{s_1,s_2}s_1s_2\,P_1(s_1)P_2(s_2) = \Big(\sum_{s_1}s_1P_1(s_1)\Big)\Big(\sum_{s_2}s_2P_2(s_2)\Big) = \langle s_1\rangle\langle s_2\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 38" />

For **correlated** random variables this fails, and the difference measures how strongly they go together. Wen defines (p. 38)

<Formula label="Correlation" tex="\langle s_1s_2\rangle - \langle s_1\rangle\langle s_2\rangle \equiv \text{correlation between } s_1 \text{ and } s_2" cite="reading1" at="p. 38 (bottom)" />

It is positive when the two tend to be large together, negative when one tends to be large while the other is small, and zero for independent variables. In Example II, $s_1s_2 = 1$ only for $m = 2$, so $\langle s_1s_2\rangle = \frac{1}{6}$, while $\langle s_1\rangle\langle s_2\rangle = \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$: the correlation is $\frac{1}{6} - \frac{1}{4} = -\frac{1}{12}$, negative because an even roll tends to be a high one. (The number is ours; the notes stop at the definition.) A zero correlation does not in general prove independence, since it tests only one average; for two variables that are each 0 or 1 it does, because then the single number $\langle s_1s_2\rangle = P_{12}(1,1)$ fixes the whole table once $P_1$ and $P_2$ are known.

Wen adds a box on a **random force** $F(t)$, such as the kicks a dust grain gets from the molecules around it (p. 38; the notes call the time $s$). Its value at one time and at a later time are two random variables. The average of their product, $\langle F(t)F(0)\rangle$, is the correlation of the force with itself; for a long time difference the two values become independent, so it splits into $\langle F(t)\rangle\langle F(0)\rangle$, which is zero for a force that averages to zero:

<Formula label="Correlation of a random force" tex="\langle F(t)F(0)\rangle \to \langle F(t)\rangle\langle F(0)\rangle = 0 \quad\text{as } t \to \infty" cite="reading1" at="p. 38 (box)" />

How quickly this correlation dies away is a property of the surroundings, and it will matter whenever fluctuating forces drive a system.

## The binomial distribution

Take $n$ independent random variables $s_1, \ldots, s_n$, each 1 with probability $p$ and 0 with probability $1 - p$ (notes p. 39, point ③): $n$ tosses of a biased coin, or $n$ spins each up with probability $p$. Because they are independent, the probability of one particular set of values is the product $P(s_1)P(s_2)\cdots P(s_n)$. A set with $k$ ones and $n - k$ zeros has probability $p^k(1 - p)^{n-k}$, and there are $\binom{n}{k} = n!/[k!(n - k)!]$ such sets, one for each choice of which $k$ variables are 1. So the probability that $\sum_i s_i = k$ is the **binomial distribution**

<Formula label="Binomial distribution" tex="B(k; n, p) = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n - k)!}\,p^k(1 - p)^{n-k} = \binom{n}{k}p^k(1 - p)^{n-k}" cite="reading1" at="p. 39, point ③" />

Its average and second moment are (notes p. 39)

<Formula label="Mean and second moment of the binomial distribution" tex="\langle k\rangle = \sum_k k\,B(k; n, p) = np, \qquad \langle k^2\rangle = n^2p^2 + np(1 - p)" cite="reading1" at="p. 39, point ③" />

so the variance is $\langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle^2 = np(1 - p)$, which is $n$ times the variance $p(1 - p)$ of a single two-valued variable. The mean is intuitive, a fraction $p$ of the $n$ variables; the fluctuation $\Delta k = \sqrt{np(1 - p)}$ grows only like $\sqrt n$, so the **relative** fluctuation $\Delta k/\langle k\rangle = \sqrt{(1 - p)/(np)}$ shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$.

## Wen's trick for the averages

The sums over $k$ can be done without any algebra on factorials (notes p. 39). Set $x = p/(1 - p)$ and define

<Formula label="Generating function" tex="Z = (1 + x)^n = 1 + nx + \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}x^2 + \cdots + \binom{n}{k}x^k + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="p. 39, point ③" />

(The notes write the third term as $\frac{n(n + 1)}{2}x^2$, a slip: the coefficient of $x^2$ is $\binom{n}{2} = \frac{n(n - 1)}{2}$.) The letter $Z$ is suggestive: each term $\binom{n}{k} x^k$, divided by $Z$, is exactly the binomial probability,

<Formula label="Each term is a probability" tex="\frac{1}{Z}\binom{n}{k}x^k = \binom{n}{k}\frac{(p/(1 - p))^k}{(1 + p/(1 - p))^n} = \binom{n}{k}\frac{p^k(1 - p)^{n-k}}{(1 - p + p)^n} = B(k; n, p)" cite="reading1" at="p. 39, point ③" />

so $Z$ plays the part of a partition function, with $x$ in the role of a Boltzmann factor for each variable that is 1. As with a partition function, differentiating $\ln Z$ brings down the variable we want to average. The operator $x\,\frac{d}{dx}$ turns $x^k$ into $kx^k$, so

<Formula label="Average from the generating function" tex="x\frac{d}{dx}\ln Z = \frac{1}{Z}\sum_k k\binom{n}{k}x^k = \langle k\rangle, \qquad \langle k\rangle = x\,\frac{n}{1 + x} = np" cite="reading1" at="p. 39, point ③ (bottom)" />

where the last step uses $x/(1 + x) = p$. Wen ticks this result. The second moment follows the same way (this step is ours; the notes state only the result): applying $x\,d/dx$ once more to $\ln Z$ gives the variance,

<Formula label="Variance from the generating function" tex="\langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle^2 = x\frac{d}{dx}\Big(x\frac{d}{dx}\ln Z\Big) = x\frac{d}{dx}\frac{nx}{1 + x} = \frac{nx}{(1 + x)^2} = np(1 - p)" cite="reading1" at="p. 39, point ③ (variance not derived in the notes)" />

since $1/(1 + x) = 1 - p$; adding $\langle k\rangle^2 = n^2p^2$ gives Wen's $\langle k^2\rangle$. This is the same pattern as in the canonical ensemble, where one derivative of $\ln Z$ with respect to the inverse temperature gives the average energy and a second derivative gives its fluctuation.

## The central limit theorem

The last point of the chapter (notes p. 40, point ④) explains why the binomial distribution in the graph looks so much like a Gaussian. Take $n$ independent random variables $x_1, \ldots, x_n$, all with the same distribution $P(x)$, and form their mean

<Formula label="Mean of n variables" tex="X = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i" cite="reading1" at="p. 40, point ④" />

The **central limit theorem** says that for $n \to \infty$ the distribution of $X$ is Gaussian, whatever $P(x)$ was (as long as its variance is finite; see the [8.044 page](/courses/8-044/sums-central-limit-theorem) for the conditions of the theorem and the counterexample of the Lorentzian):

<Formula label="Gaussian distribution of the mean" tex="P_G(X) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\,e^{-(X - \langle X\rangle)^2/2\sigma^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 40, point ④" />

(Wen writes $\sigma$ for the variance itself, so his formula has $\sigma$ where this one has $\sigma^2$; we keep the usual convention that $\sigma$ is the standard deviation.) The Gaussian is fixed by two numbers, and Wen finds both from the properties it must have: it is normalized, $\int dX\,P_G(X) = 1$; its average is that of $X$; and its variance is that of $X$. The average is easy. Each $x_i$ has the same average $\langle x\rangle = \int dx\,x\,P(x)$, so the mean of $n$ of them has it too, $\langle X\rangle = \langle x\rangle$. For the variance, write $X - \langle X\rangle = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i(x_i - \langle x\rangle)$ and square:

<Formula label="Variance of the mean" tex="\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n^2}\Big\langle\Big(\sum_i(x_i - \langle x\rangle)\Big)^2\Big\rangle = \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_i\big\langle(x_i - \langle x\rangle)^2\big\rangle + \frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i \ne j}\big\langle(x_i - \langle x\rangle)(x_j - \langle x\rangle)\big\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 40, point ④" />

The cross terms vanish: for $i \ne j$ the two variables are independent, so the average of the product is the product of the averages, and each average $\langle x_i - \langle x\rangle\rangle$ is zero. What is left is $n$ equal terms (notes p. 40, boxed):

<Formula label="Width of the Gaussian" tex="\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\big\langle(x - \langle x\rangle)^2\big\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 40 (boxed)" />

The mean of $n$ variables scatters about the true average with a standard deviation $1/\sqrt n$ times that of a single variable. The binomial distribution is the special case where each $x_i = s_i$ is 0 or 1: $k/n$ is the mean $X$, its variance is $p(1 - p)/n$, and so $k$ itself is close to a Gaussian with mean $np$ and variance $np(1 - p)$, which is the dashed curve in the graph. Note that the argument for $\sigma^2$ used only independence, and holds for every $n$; what needs $n \to \infty$ is the Gaussian shape.

## Why large systems hardly fluctuate

(This section goes beyond the notes.) This $1/\sqrt n$ is the reason thermodynamics works. A macroscopic quantity, such as the energy or the magnetization of a body, is a sum over some $10^{22}$ nearly independent parts. Its average grows like the number of parts and its fluctuation only like the square root, so the fluctuation relative to the average is of order $10^{-11}$, far below anything a thermometer or a magnetometer can see. The averages of statistical physics are then simply the measured values. The next chapter makes this precise for systems in thermal equilibrium.

<Problem>
(a) Roll a fair twelve-sided die showing $m = 1, \ldots, 12$. Let $s_1 = 1$ if $m \le 6$ and $s_2 = 1$ if $m$ is a prime number, each 0 otherwise. Find the joint probabilities, the probability of $s_1 = 1$ given $s_2 = 1$ and given $s_2 = 0$, and the correlation. Are the variables independent?

(b) A paramagnet has $n = 400$ independent spins, each pointing up with probability $p = 0.6$. Find the average number of up spins, its fluctuation, and the probability (in the Gaussian approximation) that 260 or more spins point up. How large is the relative fluctuation for $10^{22}$ spins?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The joint table">
The primes up to 12 are 2, 3, 5, 7 and 11, so $P_2(1) = 5/12$, and $P_1(1) = 6/12 = 1/2$. Counting faces: $P_{12}(1,1) = 3/12$ (2, 3, 5), $P_{12}(1,0) = 3/12$ (1, 4, 6), $P_{12}(0,1) = 2/12$ (7, 11) and $P_{12}(0,0) = 4/12$ (8, 9, 10, 12). They add up to $12/12 = 1$.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Conditional probabilities and correlation">
Given a prime, $P(s_1 = 1 \mid s_2 = 1) = \frac{3/12}{5/12} = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6$; given a non-prime, $P(s_1 = 1 \mid s_2 = 0) = \frac{3/12}{7/12} = \frac{3}{7} \approx 0.429$. These differ, so the variables are not independent: primes are commoner among the low faces. The correlation is $\langle s_1s_2\rangle - \langle s_1\rangle\langle s_2\rangle = \frac{3}{12} - \frac{1}{2} \times \frac{5}{12} = \frac{1}{24} \approx 0.042$, positive, as it should be.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Mean and fluctuation">
The number $k$ of up spins has the binomial distribution, so $\langle k\rangle = np = 400 \times 0.6 = 240$ and $(\Delta k)^2 = np(1 - p) = 240 \times 0.4 = 96$, giving $\Delta k \approx 9.80$. The relative fluctuation is $9.80/240 \approx 0.041$, about 4%.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A large excess">
In the Gaussian approximation, "260 or more" means $k > 259.5$ (the step of $k = 260$ starts half a unit below it), which is $(259.5 - 240)/9.80 \approx 1.99$ standard deviations above the mean. The Gaussian tail beyond that is $\frac{1}{2}\,\text{erfc}(1.99/\sqrt2) \approx 0.023$: about one chance in 43.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) A macroscopic magnet">
For $n = 10^{22}$ spins, $\Delta k/\langle k\rangle = \sqrt{(1 - p)/(np)} = \sqrt{0.4/(0.6 \times 10^{22})} \approx 8.2 \times 10^{-12}$. The number of up spins is fixed to about eleven significant figures.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Random variables in the clustering of matter">
Cosmology cannot predict where a particular galaxy will form, only the statistics of the density field, so it uses exactly these tools. In 8.902's [power spectrum lecture](/courses/8-902/power-spectrum), the clustering of matter is measured by the two-point correlation function: the average of the product of the density contrast (the fractional excess of density over the mean) at two points a given distance apart, which, like the random force above, is non-zero when the two values are correlated. In the [Press–Schechter lecture](/courses/8-902/press-schechter), the density contrast smoothed over a region is treated as a Gaussian random variable with zero mean, and the fraction of matter in halos above a given mass is (up to a famous factor of 2) the probability that this variable lies above a collapse threshold: the same kind of Gaussian tail as in the worked example.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A random variable $s$ is fixed by its distribution $P(s)$, with $\sum_s P(s) = 1$. Average $\langle s\rangle = \sum sP(s)$, moments $\langle s^n\rangle$, fluctuation $\Delta s = \sqrt{\langle s^2\rangle - \langle s\rangle^2}$.
- Two random variables need a **joint probability** $P_{12}(s_1, s_2)$; summing over one gives the distribution of the other, and dividing a slice by its sum gives the **conditional probability** $P(s_1 \mid s_2)$.
- They are **independent** when $P_{12} = P_1P_2$, equivalently when the conditional probability of one does not depend on the other. Then $\langle s_1s_2\rangle = \langle s_1\rangle\langle s_2\rangle$; the **correlation** $\langle s_1s_2\rangle - \langle s_1\rangle\langle s_2\rangle$ measures the departure.
- The number of ones among $n$ independent variables that are 1 with probability $p$ has the **binomial distribution** $B(k; n, p) = \binom{n}{k} p^k(1 - p)^{n-k}$, with $\langle k\rangle = np$ and variance $np(1 - p)$; Wen gets them from $Z = (1 + x)^n$ with $x = p/(1 - p)$, like averages from a partition function.
- **Central limit theorem**: the mean $X$ of $n$ independent, identically distributed variables becomes Gaussian for large $n$, with average $\langle x\rangle$ and variance $\frac{1}{n}\langle(x - \langle x\rangle)^2\rangle$. Relative fluctuations fall like $1/\sqrt n$, which is why macroscopic quantities are sharp.
