Chapter I held the number of particles fixed and let the system exchange energy with a heat bath. Many systems also exchange **particles**: a gas in contact with a liquid, molecules sticking to a surface, atoms that combine into molecules, a small volume inside a larger gas. Xiao-Gang Wen's fourth chapter builds the ensemble for them, the **grand canonical ensemble**, by the same route as Chapter I: an idealised reservoir, then a sum over states, then a free energy that the system minimises (notes pp. 28–30, points ① to ③). The 8.044 page on [the grand canonical ensemble](/courses/8-044/grand-canonical-ensemble) derived it by expanding the reservoir's entropy and went on to Fermi and Bose occupations; Wen instead adds a particle reservoir to the canonical ensemble and spends the chapter on what equal chemical potentials imply. Notation follows the notes (which, like Huang's textbook, write $Q$ for partition functions and $A$ for the free energy): the grand partition function is $Q_G$ (8.044 wrote Ξ) and the **thermodynamic potential** is $\Omega$ (8.044's grand potential $\Phi_G$). (MIT's material for this chapter is eight scanned pages numbered 28 to 35 by hand; in the PDF, p. 30 comes before p. 29. This page covers pp. 28–30 and the [next page](/courses/8-08/equal-chemical-potentials) the applications on pp. 31–35. Everything here is in our own words, with our own examples.)

## Three nested ensembles

Wen's opening sketch (notes p. 28) puts the three ensembles inside one another. The system alone, with fixed energy and particle number, is the **microcanonical** ensemble. Put it in contact with a heat bath at temperature $T$ and the system is **canonical**. Now let it also exchange particles with a **particle reservoir** characterised by a number $\mu$, the chemical potential, and the system becomes **grand canonical**, described by $T$ and $\mu$ instead of a fixed energy and a fixed particle number.

## The grand partition function

Wen's reservoir is idealised in the same spirit as the ideal heat bath of [Chapter I](/courses/8-08/canonical-ensemble-free-energy): it has energy $\mu$ per particle it holds, so with $N_{\text{res}}$ particles its energy is $N_{\text{res}}\mu$ (notes p. 28, point ①). If the system is in its $n$th state with $N$ particles, of energy $E_{n,N}$, the energy of system plus reservoir is

<Formula label="Energy of system plus particle reservoir" tex="E_{n,N} + N_{\text{res}}\,\mu = E_{n,N} - N\mu + N_{\text{tot}}\,\mu" cite="reading1" at="p. 28, point ①" />

because the total number of particles $N_{\text{tot}} = N + N_{\text{res}}$ is fixed. The last term is a constant and is dropped. System plus reservoir sits in the heat bath, so it is an ordinary canonical ensemble, and its partition function, which Wen calls the **grand partition function** of the system, is a sum over both the state and the particle number (notes p. 28):

<Formula label="Grand partition function" tex="Q_G = \sum_{n,N}e^{-\beta(E_{n,N} - N\mu)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 28, point ①" />

with $\beta = 1/k_BT$. Summing first over the states $n$ at fixed $N$ gives the canonical partition function $Q_N(V, T)$ of $N$ particles, so

<Formula label="Grand partition function as a sum over N" tex="Q_G(\mu, V, T) = \sum_N e^{\beta\mu N}\,Q_N(V, T) = \sum_N z^N\,Q_N(V, T), \qquad z = e^{\beta\mu}" cite="reading1" at="p. 28, point ①" />

where $z$ is the **fugacity**. Every particle the system takes from the reservoir costs the reservoir energy $\mu$, and that is the only way the reservoir enters. (Our remark: a real reservoir is not exactly like this, but just as for the heat bath, what matters is its first-order response. Its free energy changes by $\mu = \partial A_{\text{res}}/\partial N_{\text{res}}$ per particle, and that gives the same weight $e^{\beta\mu N}$.)

## The average number of particles

Each term $e^{-\beta(E_{n,N} - N\mu)}$ is proportional to the probability that the system has $N$ particles and is in its $n$th state; summed over $n$, the term $e^{\beta\mu N}Q_N$ is proportional to the probability that it has $N$ particles, whatever its state (notes p. 29, point ②). The mean particle number is therefore

<Formula label="Mean particle number" tex="\langle N\rangle = \frac{\sum_N N\,e^{\beta\mu N}Q_N}{\sum_N e^{\beta\mu N}Q_N} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial\mu}\Omega(\mu, V, T)" cite="reading1" at="p. 29, point ②" />

where the **thermodynamic potential** is defined from the grand partition function as the free energy was from $Q$ (notes p. 29, boxed):

<Formula label="Thermodynamic potential" tex="\Omega \equiv -k_BT\ln Q_G" cite="reading1" at="p. 29, point ②" />

Differentiating $-k_BT\ln Q_G$ with respect to $\mu$ brings down a factor $\beta N$ in each term, which is the average above.

## Ω = A − μN, and what the system minimises

As in Chapter I, the sum is dominated by its largest term. Write $Q_N = e^{-\beta A_N}$, with $A_N$ the free energy of $N$ particles. The weight of $N$ particles is then

<Formula label="Weight of N particles" tex="e^{\beta\mu N}\,Q_N = e^{\beta\mu N}\,e^{-\beta A_N} = e^{-\beta(A_N - \mu N)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 29, point ②" />

Let $\bar N$ be the particle number that maximises it. For a large system the peak is sharp, so (notes p. 29)

<Formula label="Grand partition function from its largest term" tex="Q_G \approx e^{\beta\mu\bar N}\,Q_{\bar N} = e^{-\beta(A_{\bar N} - \mu\bar N)} = e^{-\beta\Omega} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Omega = A - \mu N, \qquad \bar N = \langle N\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 29, point ②" />

Wen's margin note draws the lesson: the system "wants" to maximise $e^{\beta\mu N}Q_N$, which is to say it minimises $\Omega = A - \mu N$. A system in a heat bath minimises its free energy $A$; a system that can also trade particles minimises $A - \mu N$. Setting the derivative with respect to $N$ to zero gives the condition for the peak, $\partial A/\partial N = \mu$: the system takes particles until the free energy of one more particle equals the reservoir's price for it. (That condition is written out on the [next page](/courses/8-08/equal-chemical-potentials), where the notes use it.)

## Fluctuations of the particle number

How far does $N$ wander from $\bar N$? Wen gives two ways (notes p. 29). First, expand the exponent of the weight to second order about its maximum; the first-order term vanishes there, so the weight is a Gaussian:

<Formula label="Gaussian distribution of N" tex="e^{\beta\mu N}\,e^{-\beta A_N} \approx \text{const}\times e^{-\frac12\beta\left.\frac{\partial^2A_N}{\partial N^2}\right|_{N = \bar N}(N - \bar N)^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 29, point ②" />

whose variance is $k_BT/(\partial^2A/\partial N^2)$. Second, differentiate $\Omega$ twice with respect to $\mu$, exactly, without any approximation (in the notes the last step drops the bracket around $\langle N^2\rangle - \langle N\rangle^2$, restored here):

<Formula label="Second derivative of Ω" tex="-\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\mu^2}\Omega = \beta\,\frac{\sum_N N^2 e^{\beta\mu N}Q_N}{\sum_N e^{\beta\mu N}Q_N} - \beta\,\frac{\left(\sum_N N e^{\beta\mu N}Q_N\right)^2}{\left(\sum_N e^{\beta\mu N}Q_N\right)^2} = \beta\left(\langle N^2\rangle - \langle N\rangle^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 29, point ②" />

so (notes p. 29, boxed)

<Formula label="Fluctuation of the particle number" tex="\left\langle(N - \bar N)^2\right\rangle = -k_BT\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\mu^2}\Omega(\mu, V, T)" cite="reading1" at="p. 29, point ②" />

The two agree. (Our check: since $\langle N\rangle = -\partial\Omega/\partial\mu$, the right side is $k_BT\,\partial\langle N\rangle/\partial\mu$; and since $\mu = \partial A/\partial N$ at the peak, $\partial N/\partial\mu = 1/(\partial^2A/\partial N^2)$, the variance of the Gaussian.) Both $\Omega$ and $N$ are proportional to the size of the system, so the variance is too, and the **relative** fluctuation falls as $1/\sqrt{\bar N}$: large systems have a sharp particle number even when it is not fixed.

## Pressure, entropy and particle number from Ω

Point ③ derives the thermodynamic quantities (notes p. 30). **Pressure.** Differentiating $\ln Q_G$ with respect to the volume brings down a factor $-\beta\,\partial E_{n,N}/\partial V$ in each term, and $-\partial E_{n,N}/\partial V$ is the force on the walls per unit area:

<Formula label="Volume derivative of ln Q_G" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial V}\ln Q_G = \frac{\sum_{n,N}\left(-\beta\,\frac{\partial E_{n,N}}{\partial V}\right)e^{-\beta(E_{n,N} - N\mu)}}{\sum_{n,N}e^{-\beta(E_{n,N} - \mu N)}} = -\beta\left\langle\frac{\partial E_{n,N}}{\partial V}\right\rangle = \beta P" cite="reading1" at="p. 30, point ③" />

<Formula label="Pressure from Ω" tex="P = -\left.\frac{\partial\Omega(V, T, \mu)}{\partial V}\right|_{\mu,T}" cite="reading1" at="p. 30, point ③" />

**Ω = −PV.** Of the three variables $V$, $T$ and $\mu$, only $V$ is extensive, and $\Omega$ is extensive, so it must be proportional to $V$ (notes p. 30):

<Formula label="Ω is proportional to the volume" tex="\Omega = V\,\omega(T, \mu), \qquad \omega(T, \mu) = -P(T, \mu), \qquad \Omega = -PV" cite="reading1" at="p. 30, point ③" />

where $\omega$ is intensive. The middle step is the pressure formula applied to $V\omega$.

**Entropy.** Write $\Omega = A(V, T, N) - \mu N$, where $N = N(\mu, V, T)$ is the equilibrium particle number, so it changes when $T$ does (notes p. 30):

<Formula label="Entropy from Ω" tex="\left.\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial T}\right|_{V,\mu} = \left.\frac{\partial A}{\partial T}\right|_{V,N} + \left.\frac{\partial A}{\partial N}\right|_{V,T}\left.\frac{\partial N}{\partial T}\right|_{V,\mu} - \mu\left.\frac{\partial N}{\partial T}\right|_{V,\mu} = \left.\frac{\partial A}{\partial T}\right|_{V,N} = -S" cite="reading1" at="p. 30, point ③" />

The two terms from the change of $N$ cancel, because $\partial A/\partial N = \mu$. This is the same argument as in Chapter I, where the change of the equilibrium energy dropped out of $\partial A/\partial T$: wherever a quantity sits at the minimum of a function, its own change does not count to first order.

**Particle number.** The same argument with respect to $\mu$ leaves only the explicit $-\mu N$ (notes p. 30):

<Formula label="Particle number from Ω" tex="\frac{\partial\Omega}{\partial\mu} = \frac{\partial(A - \mu N)}{\partial N}\,\frac{\partial N}{\partial\mu} - N = -N" cite="reading1" at="p. 30, point ③" />

since $\partial(A - \mu N)/\partial N = 0$ at equilibrium, which confirms $\langle N\rangle = -\partial\Omega/\partial\mu$ found before. Together (our summary, as in 8.044): $d\Omega = -S\,dT - P\,dV - N\,d\mu$.

One consequence is worth drawing out (our addition). Since $A - \mu N = \Omega = -PV$, the Gibbs free energy is $G = A + PV = \mu N$: the chemical potential is the Gibbs free energy per particle. And at fixed $T$, $N = -\partial\Omega/\partial\mu = V\,\partial P/\partial\mu$, so the density is $n = \partial P/\partial\mu$.

## The ideal gas, summed exactly

For the classical ideal gas the sum over $N$ can be done without any approximation (our example; the notes reach the same results on p. 31 by keeping only the largest term, as the [next page](/courses/8-08/equal-chemical-potentials) shows. We use Chapter I's $Q_N = (V/\lambda^3)^N/N!$, with $\lambda = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT}$ the thermal wavelength). The sum is the series of the exponential: $Q_G = \sum_N (zV/\lambda^3)^N/N! = e^{zV/\lambda^3}$, so $\Omega = -k_BT\,zV/\lambda^3$. Then every result above can be checked directly. $\langle N\rangle = -\partial\Omega/\partial\mu = zV/\lambda^3$, so $z = n\lambda^3$ and $\mu = k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3)$. $P = -\partial\Omega/\partial V = k_BT\,z/\lambda^3 = nk_BT$, the ideal gas law, and indeed $\Omega = -\langle N\rangle k_BT = -PV$. The variance is $-k_BT\,\partial^2\Omega/\partial\mu^2 = \langle N\rangle$, and the probability of $N$ particles, $z^NQ_N/Q_G$, is the **Poisson distribution** $\langle N\rangle^N e^{-\langle N\rangle}/N!$.

Treat air as nitrogen ($m = 28$ u $= 4.65 \times 10^{-26}$ kg), with the molecules as structureless points (their rotations are ignored), at 300 K and 101325 Pa. Find the number density, the thermal wavelength and the chemical potential. Then imagine a fixed imaginary box inside the gas, open to it on all sides: find the mean number of molecules and its standard deviation for a box of 1 μm³ and for a box of (10 nm)³ ($k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K, $\hbar = 1.054572 \times 10^{-34}$ J s).
</Problem>
<Step title="Density, thermal wavelength and μ">
$k_BT = 4.142 \times 10^{-21}$ J, so $n = P/k_BT = 2.446 \times 10^{25}$ m⁻³. The thermal wavelength is $\lambda = \sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT} = 1.905 \times 10^{-11}$ m, so $n\lambda^3 = 1.69 \times 10^{-7}$: the gas is very dilute on the scale of $\lambda$, as a classical gas must be. Then $\mu = k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3) = -15.6\,k_BT = -0.40$ eV. The chemical potential of a classical gas is large and negative.
</Step>
<Step title="The open boxes">
The rest of the gas is the particle reservoir. For 1 μm³, $\langle N\rangle = nV = 2.45 \times 10^7$, and the standard deviation is $\sqrt{\langle N\rangle} = 4.9 \times 10^3$, a relative fluctuation of $2.0 \times 10^{-4}$. For $(10\text{ nm})^3 = 10^{-24}$ m³, $\langle N\rangle = 24.5$ and the standard deviation is $4.9$: 20%, the plot above. Particle number is sharp in a micrometre box and visibly random in a box of ten nanometres.
</Step>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Particle numbers that are not fixed">
In the hot early universe, particles are created and destroyed all the time, so their numbers are not fixed and their chemical potentials are set by equilibrium instead. Photons can be made and absorbed singly, so nothing fixes their number. Wen's chapter on the Bose gas therefore treats them with the canonical ensemble, summing freely over the number of photons in each state; that is the same as a grand canonical sum with $\mu = 0$, and it gives the Bose-Einstein distribution behind the black-body radiation that 8.286 studies in Lectures 16 and 17.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Wen's **particle reservoir** holds energy $\mu$ per particle. System plus reservoir in a heat bath is canonical with weight $e^{-\beta(E_{n,N} - \mu N)}$, so the **grand partition function** is $Q_G = \sum_{n,N}e^{-\beta(E_{n,N} - \mu N)} = \sum_N z^NQ_N$, with fugacity $z = e^{\beta\mu}$.
- The **thermodynamic potential** $\Omega = -k_BT\ln Q_G$ equals $A - \mu N$ at the most probable $N$; the system minimises it, and $\partial A/\partial N = \mu$ there.
- $\langle N\rangle = -\partial\Omega/\partial\mu$ and $\langle(N - \bar N)^2\rangle = -k_BT\,\partial^2\Omega/\partial\mu^2$, a variance proportional to the size, so the relative fluctuation falls as $1/\sqrt{\bar N}$.
- $P = -\partial\Omega/\partial V$, $S = -\partial\Omega/\partial T$, and since $\Omega = V\omega(T, \mu)$ is extensive, $\Omega = -PV$.
- For the ideal gas, $Q_G = e^{zV/\lambda^3}$: $\mu = k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3)$, $P = nk_BT$, and $N$ follows a Poisson distribution with variance $\langle N\rangle$.
