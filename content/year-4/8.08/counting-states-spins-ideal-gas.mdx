This supplement is a typed chapter from Wen's own book draft, not part of his handwritten notes. It goes back to the start of statistical physics and builds it from a single principle by counting states, first for spins in a magnetic field and then for the classical ideal gas (pp. 1–9). The 8.044 pages on the [microcanonical ensemble](/courses/8-044/microcanonical-ensemble) and on [entropy, temperature and the second law](/courses/8-044/entropy-temperature-second-law) cover the general argument and the ideal gas in detail, and Chapter I's [ideal gas page](/courses/8-08/information-equipartition-ideal-gas) reaches the same entropy from the canonical ensemble. Here we follow Wen's route: his reading of probability and ergodicity, the spin system worked out in full, his properties of entropy, and the counting for the gas.

## One principle, two readings

Wen opens with a litre of gas, of order $10^{22}$ atoms (Section 1.1, p. 1). A complete description would list where every atom is and how fast it moves, about $6 \times 10^{22}$ numbers, a list no one could ever compile. Fortunately the things we measure, such as pressure and temperature, are averages over all the atoms, insensitive to what any one of them does. Statistical physics computes such averages while admitting that most of the microscopic information is missing, and in Wen's account it needs just one principle:

<Formula label="The principle of statistical physics" tex="\text{all possible states appear with equal probability}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.1, p. 1" />

"Possible" means compatible with what we do know. Knowing a few properties leaves many states that share them, and the principle says we have no reason to prefer any of them.

But at any instant a real system occupies just one state, so in what sense are the others equally probable? Wen offers two answers (Section 1.1, pp. 1–2):

- **Ensemble reading.** Picture a huge collection of replicas, identical in everything we know, each sitting in one of the allowed states. "Equally likely" then says that each allowed state is represented by the same number of replicas. This imagined collection is the **ensemble**; probabilities are defined only with respect to it, so on this reading the theory describes ensembles rather than any one system.
- **Time reading.** If the system's known properties are steady, "equally likely" can instead mean that, watched for long enough, it lingers equally long in each allowed state. Snapshots taken at many different moments then play the part of the replicas.

The two agree only if the system really does wander through all its possible states, many times over, during the time we watch it. That is the **ergodic hypothesis**, and Wen stresses that not every system obeys it. He adopts the ensemble reading as the foundation and applies the results to single systems on the assumption that they are ergodic. (A glass or a magnet frozen into one domain pattern is a system for which the time reading fails over any practical time; that example is ours.)

## Counting the states of spins

A **microcanonical ensemble** is made of isolated systems that all have the same energy, meaning in practice an energy inside a narrow window from $E$ to $E + \Delta E$ (Section 1.2, p. 2).

Wen's first example is $N$ spin-$\frac12$ moments in a magnetic field, each with energy $+\epsilon_0/2$ (spin up) or $-\epsilon_0/2$ (spin down). With $M$ spins up the energy is $E = (M - N/2)\epsilon_0$, so fixing $E$ fixes $M = E/\epsilon_0 + N/2$. Expanding $(1 + 1)^N$ term by term counts all $2^N$ states and sorts them by the number of up spins (Section 1.2.1, p. 2):

<Formula label="Sorting the 2^N states by the number of up spins" tex="2^N = (1 + 1)^N = \sum_{M=0}^{N}\binom{N}{M}, \qquad \binom{N}{M} = \frac{N!}{M!\,(N - M)!}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.1, p. 2, Eqs. (1.2.1)–(1.2.2)" />

The binomial coefficient (written $C_N^M$ in the notes) counts the ways to choose which $M$ of the $N$ spins point up, so it is the number of states with energy $E$. The **entropy** is defined as $k_B$ times its logarithm (Section 1.2.1, p. 2):

<Formula label="Entropy of the spin system" tex="S(E) = k_B\ln(\text{number of states}) = k_B\ln\binom{N}{M}, \qquad M = \frac{E}{\epsilon_0} + \frac N2" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.1, p. 2, Eqs. (1.2.3)–(1.2.4)" />

(The notes write the upper index of $C_N$ in Eq. (1.2.4) as $E/\epsilon_0$; the number of up spins is $E/\epsilon_0 + N/2$.) For large numbers Wen uses Stirling's formula in the form (Section 1.2.1, p. 2)

<Formula label="Stirling's formula" tex="\ln n! = \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\ln(n + 1) - (n + 1) + \tfrac12\ln(2\pi) + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.1, p. 2, Eq. (1.2.5)" />

and keeping only the terms proportional to $N$, with $f = M/N$ the fraction of spins up and $1 - f$ the fraction down,

<Formula label="Entropy for large N" tex="k_B^{-1}S \approx N\ln N - M\ln M - (N - M)\ln(N - M) = N\left[-f\ln f - (1 - f)\ln(1 - f)\right]" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.1, p. 2, Eq. (1.2.6)" />

With $f = \frac12 + E/E_0$, where $E_0 = N\epsilon_0$, this is a function of the energy alone (Section 1.2.1, p. 3):

<Formula label="Entropy as a function of energy" tex="k_B^{-1}S(E) = N\left[-\left(\frac12 + \frac{E}{E_0}\right)\ln\left(\frac12 + \frac{E}{E_0}\right) - \left(\frac12 - \frac{E}{E_0}\right)\ln\left(\frac12 - \frac{E}{E_0}\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.1, p. 3, Eq. (1.2.7)" />

Read backwards, the definition says that the number of states with energy $E$ is $e^{S(E)/k_B}$ (Eq. (1.2.8)). The entropy per spin is zero when all spins point the same way, where there is only one state, and largest at $E = 0$, half up and half down, where it is $k_B\ln 2$.

## Temperature from two systems in contact

Now put two spin systems side by side, $N_1$ spins and $N_2$ spins, and let them trade energy (Section 1.2.2, p. 3). The total energy $E$ is fixed, and the spins wander through every state with that total. By the principle, all of them are equally likely, so the probability that system 1 has energy $E_1$ is proportional to the number of joint states with that split, which is the product of the two systems' counts:

<Formula label="Probability of the energy split" tex="P(E_1) \propto e^{S_1(E_1)/k_B}\,e^{S_2(E - E_1)/k_B} = e^{\left[S_1(E_1) + S_2(E - E_1)\right]/k_B}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.2, p. 3, Eqs. (1.2.9)–(1.2.10)" />

Both entropies are proportional to the number of spins, so for large systems this is an extremely narrow peak, practically a delta function. System 1 is then certain to sit at the energy $\bar E_1$ that maximises the total entropy, where

<Formula label="Condition for equilibrium" tex="S_1'(\bar E_1) = S_2'(E - \bar E_1)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.2, p. 3, Eq. (1.2.11)" />

If system 1 started at some other energy, contact moves it to $\bar E_1$, and the total entropy rises on the way. That is the second law for this example: an isolated system approaching equilibrium gains entropy, with entropy defined by counting states.

The condition (1.2.11) compares one property of each system, so it defines a shared quantity, the **temperature** (Section 1.2.2, pp. 3–4):

<Formula label="Statistical temperature" tex="\frac1T = \beta k_B = \frac{\partial S(E)}{\partial E}, \qquad \text{equilibrium: } T_1 = T_2" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.2, pp. 3–4, Eqs. (1.2.12)–(1.2.13)" />

Differentiating (1.2.7), with $\epsilon = E/N$ the average energy per spin, gives the temperature of the spin system (Section 1.2.2, p. 4):

<Formula label="Temperature of the spin system" tex="\frac1T = \beta k_B = \frac{k_B}{\epsilon_0}\ln\left(\frac{\frac12\epsilon_0 - \epsilon}{\frac12\epsilon_0 + \epsilon}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.2, p. 4, Eq. (1.2.14)" />

(The notes print this fraction upside down, which would make a system with most spins down, $\epsilon < 0$, negative in temperature; the derivative of (1.2.7) gives the form above.) Wen's Figure 1.3 shows $T$ and $\beta$ against $\epsilon$. With most spins down, $\epsilon < 0$, the temperature is positive and grows as $\epsilon$ rises towards zero. At $\epsilon = 0$ the entropy is at its peak, $\beta = 0$, and $T$ is infinite. Beyond it, adding energy lowers the entropy, so $\beta$ and $T$ are **negative**. A negative temperature is hotter than any positive one: $\beta$, which varies smoothly, is the better measure, and energy flows from smaller $\beta$ to larger. Such states are real for spins, whose energy is bounded above, and are made in the laboratory by suddenly reversing the field; the interpretation in this paragraph is ours, the notes give only the figure.

## Curie's law

For spin-$\frac12$ moments in a field $B$ the level splitting is $\epsilon_0 = g\mu_BB$, with $g$ the g-factor and $\mu_B$ the Bohr magneton (Section 1.2.3, p. 4). The energy is $-\mathcal M B$, where $\mathcal M$ is the total magnetic moment along the field, so $\epsilon = -\mathcal M B/N$, and (1.2.14) becomes a relation between the moment, the field and the temperature:

<Formula label="Moment, field and temperature" tex="\frac1T = \frac{k_B}{g\mu_B B}\ln\left(\frac{g\mu_B N + 2\mathcal{M}}{g\mu_B N - 2\mathcal{M}}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.3, p. 4" />

(The notes write the energy as $+\mathcal M B$ and the fraction the other way up; the signs above are the ones that make the moment point along the field, which is also what the notes' final result says.) Solving for $\mathcal M$ (a step the notes skip) gives

<Formula label="The moment solved exactly" tex="\mathcal M = \frac{g\mu_BN}{2}\tanh\frac{g\mu_BB}{2k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.3, p. 4 (solved for the moment here)" />

which saturates at $g\mu_BN/2$, every moment aligned, in a strong field or at low temperature. In a weak field, $B \ll k_BT/g\mu_B$, the moment is far below saturation, $\mathcal M \ll g\mu_BN$, the hyperbolic tangent is close to its argument, and (Section 1.2.3, p. 5)

<Formula label="Curie's law" tex="\mathcal M = \frac{g^2\mu_B^2N}{4k_BT}\,B, \qquad \chi = \frac{g^2\mu_B^2N}{4k_BT} \propto \frac1T" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.3, p. 5" />

The susceptibility $\chi$ falls as $1/T$: **Curie's law**. Wen's Figure 1.4 compares it with measurements on a copper-oxide compound, which follow the $1/T$ curve at high temperature and fall away from it at low temperature, where interactions between the moments, absent from this model, take over. The 8.044 page on [paramagnets](/courses/8-044/paramagnets) reaches the same law from the canonical ensemble.

</Problem>

$g\mu_BN_A/2 = 9.274 \times 10^{-24} \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 5.585$ J/T.
</Step>
<Step title="At 77 K">
The argument is $g\mu_BB/2k_BT = 9.274 \times 10^{-23}/(2 \times 1.063 \times 10^{-21}) = 0.0436$. Its hyperbolic tangent is smaller by only 0.06%, so the exact moment, $\mathcal M = 0.243$ J/T, and Curie's law agree: even 5 T aligns only about 4% of the full moment here.

</Step>

## Properties of entropy

**Extensive** (Section 1.2.4, p. 5). In (1.2.7), written with the energy per spin, $k_B^{-1}S = N[-(\frac12 + \epsilon/\epsilon_0)\ln(\frac12 + \epsilon/\epsilon_0) - (\frac12 - \epsilon/\epsilon_0)\ln(\frac12 - \epsilon/\epsilon_0)]$ (Eq. (1.2.15)), the entropy is $N$ times a function of $\epsilon$. Entropy and total energy grow with the size of the system: they are **extensive**. The energy per spin and the temperature do not: they are **intensive**.

**Independent of the energy window** (Section 1.2.4, p. 5). Strictly, the count depends on how wide a window $\Delta E$ we allow, $S(E, \Delta E) = k_B\ln(\text{number of states between } E \text{ and } E + \Delta E)$. Widening the window by a factor $\alpha$ multiplies the count by about $\alpha$, so

<Formula label="Changing the energy window" tex="S(E, \alpha\Delta E) = S(E, \Delta E) + k_B\ln\alpha" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.4, p. 5, Eqs. (1.2.16)–(1.2.17)" />

The first term is proportional to $N$; the second, even for a window a power of $N$ wider, $\alpha$ of order $N^n$, grows only as $\ln N$. In the limit of a large system the window drops out, and $S$ is a function of $E$ alone.

**Additive** (Section 1.2.4, p. 6). Take two systems of $N$ spins each, with energies $E_1$ and $E_2$ and state counts $\Gamma(E_1)$ and $\Gamma(E_2)$. Put them side by side with a wall that blocks energy exchange (Wen's Figure 1.5a): every state of one goes with every state of the other, so the joint count is $\Gamma(E_1)\Gamma(E_2)$ and

<Formula label="Entropies of separate systems add" tex="S = k_B\ln\left[\Gamma(E_1)\Gamma(E_2)\right] = S_1 + S_2" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.4, p. 6, Eq. (1.2.18)" />

Now let energy flow (Figure 1.5b). The pair settles with each system at the average energy $\bar E = (E_1 + E_2)/2$, and the combined system of $2N$ spins with energy $2\bar E$ has $\bar\Gamma = \sum_{E_1}\Gamma(E_1)\Gamma(2\bar E - E_1)$ states. This sum contains the original product as one of its terms, so $\bar\Gamma > \Gamma(E_1)\Gamma(E_2)$: reaching equilibrium raises the entropy, the second law again.

Finally block the exchange again, leaving each system at $\bar E$ (Figure 1.5c). The count drops to $\Gamma' = \Gamma(\bar E)^2$, the largest term of the sum. Is entropy lost? The sum has fewer than $2N$ terms (Wen's generous count of the possible values of $E_1$), none larger than $\Gamma'$, so $\bar\Gamma/2N < \Gamma' < \bar\Gamma$, that is

<Formula label="Bounds on the entropy after separation" tex="\bar S > S' > \bar S - k_B\ln(2N)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.2.4, p. 6, Eqs. (1.2.19)–(1.2.20)" />

$\bar S$ and $S'$ are of order $N$ and differ by at most of order $\ln N$, so for a large system they are equal: inserting the wall again costs no entropy, and the equilibrium state is the state of maximum entropy.

<WorkedExample title="Entropy bookkeeping for 100 + 100 spins">
<Problem>
Two systems of $N = 100$ spins start with 30 and 70 spins up. Compute $\ln\Gamma$ for the separate start, for the joined system after equilibrium, and for the two separated again with 50 up each, and check Wen's bounds.
</Problem>
<Step title="Before contact">
$\ln\left[\binom{100}{30}\binom{100}{70}\right] = 117.28$.
</Step>
<Step title="After equilibrium">
All states of 200 spins with 100 up: $\ln\binom{200}{100} = 135.75$. The gain, $18.47$ (in units of $k_B$), is the entropy produced by letting the energy spread.
</Step>
<Step title="Separated again">
$\ln\left[\binom{100}{50}^2\right] = 133.57$, below $135.75$ by only $2.19$, and above the lower bound $135.75 - \ln 200 = 130.45$. The difference of 2.19 grows like $\ln N$, while both values grow like $N$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Reversible and irreversible change

Why does entropy grow at all, if each copy of the system follows definite laws of motion? Wen's answer is about information (Section 1.2.4, p. 7). The joined system of Figure 1.5b came from Figure 1.5a, which had only $\Gamma(E_1)\Gamma(E_2)$ possible states, so it can have only that many possible final states: the evolution of each copy is one-to-one. But we cannot say which of the $\bar\Gamma$ states of energy $2\bar E$ those are. All we still know is the total energy, so we must count all $\bar\Gamma$ of them. The entropy grows because we have lost track of where the states went.

Wen's Figure 1.7 draws the levels as lines. In the irreversible case (a), the four occupied levels of the start end up as four among seven possible final levels, and we no longer know which four. In a **reversible**, or adiabatic, change (b), such as slowly changing $\epsilon_0$ by changing the field, each occupied level moves smoothly into one final level, and the number of possible states does not change: the entropy stays the same.

## Counting the states of an ideal gas

For the gas, a degree of freedom is a coordinate $q$ with its momentum $p$, and one particle has three, described by $(x, p_x, y, p_y, z, p_z)$ (Section 1.3, p. 7). Classically the number of states below an energy $E$ is infinite, since positions and momenta vary continuously. Quantum mechanics fixes this: each state of one degree of freedom occupies an area $\Delta q\,\Delta p = h$ of its phase plane. For $N$ particles phase space has $6N$ dimensions, each state takes a volume $h^{3N}$, and the number of states with total energy below $E$, which we call $\Phi(E)$ (the notes write N with a subscript less-than sign), is (Section 1.3, p. 7)

<Formula label="Number of states below E" tex="\Phi(E) = \frac{1}{h^{3N}}\int_{\sum p_i^2/2m < E} d^{3N}q\;d^{3N}p = \frac{V^N S_{3N}\left(\sqrt{2mE}\right)^{3N}}{3N\,h^{3N}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 7, Eq. (1.3.1)" />

The positions give $V^N$; the momenta fill a ball of radius $\sqrt{2mE}$ in $3N$ dimensions, whose volume is $S_nR^n/n$ with $S_n$ the total solid angle in $n$ dimensions. The number in the window from $E$ to $E + \Delta E$ is the derivative times $\Delta E$ (Section 1.3, p. 8):

<Formula label="Number of states in the window" tex="\Gamma(E) = \Phi(E + \Delta E) - \Phi(E) = \frac{m\,V^N S_{3N}\left(\sqrt{2mE}\right)^{3N-2}}{h^{3N}}\,\Delta E" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 8, Eq. (1.3.2)" />

(The notes have a factor $\frac12$ in place of the factor $m$ here; the derivative of (1.3.1) gives $m$. Either way it adds only a term of order one to the entropy.) The solid angle comes from a Gaussian integral done two ways: in Cartesian coordinates it is $\pi^{n/2}$, and in spherical coordinates it is $S_n$ times a radial integral that, with $r^2$ as the variable, is $\frac12\Gamma(n/2)$, with $\Gamma$ here Euler's gamma function (Section 1.3, p. 8):

<Formula label="Solid angle in n dimensions" tex="\int d^nx\,e^{-x^2} = \int S_n\,r^{n-1}dr\,e^{-r^2} = \frac12S_n\int (r^2)^{(n-2)/2}d(r^2)\,e^{-r^2} = \frac12S_n\Gamma\!\left(\frac n2\right) = \pi^{n/2}, \qquad S_n = \frac{2\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma(n/2)}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 8, Eqs. (1.3.3)–(1.3.4)" />

As a check, $S_2 = 2\pi$ (a circle) and $S_3 = 4\pi$ (a sphere). Putting it together, with $v = V/N$ the volume per particle and $\epsilon = E/N$ the average energy per particle, Stirling's formula gives (Section 1.3, p. 8)

<Formula label="Entropy of distinguishable particles" tex="k_B^{-1}S(E) = N\ln N + N\ln\frac{v(2m\epsilon)^{3/2}}{h^3} + N\left(\frac32\ln\frac{2\pi}{3} + \frac32\right) + \ln\Delta E" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 8, Eq. (1.3.5)" />

plus terms of order $\ln N$. The first term is a problem: doubling the gas at fixed $v$ and $\epsilon$ more than doubles $N\ln N$, so this entropy is **not extensive**. The cure comes from quantum physics. The particles are **identical**: exchanging two of them gives the same state, not a new one, so the $N!$ relabellings of a configuration count once (Section 1.3, p. 8):

<Formula label="Counting identical particles" tex="\Phi(E) = \frac{1}{h^{3N}N!}\int_{\sum p_i^2/2m < E} d^{3N}q\;d^{3N}p" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 8, Eq. (1.3.6)" />

With $\ln N! \approx N\ln N - N$ the $N\ln N$ cancels, the constant gains one, and the entropy is extensive:

<Formula label="Entropy of the ideal gas of identical particles" tex="k_B^{-1}S(E) = N\ln\frac{v(2m\epsilon)^{3/2}}{h^3} + N\left(\frac32\ln\frac{2\pi}{3} + \frac52\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 8, Eq. (1.3.7)" />

the **Sackur-Tetrode** entropy. With $\epsilon = \frac32k_BT$ it is the same as the canonical result of [Chapter I](/courses/8-08/information-equipartition-ideal-gas), whose thermal wavelength $h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$ absorbs the term $\frac32\ln(2\pi/3)$.

## Wave packets per particle

Per particle, and dropping the constant, which is about $3.61$, Wen reads the entropy as a count (Section 1.3, p. 8):

<Formula label="Entropy per particle" tex="k_B^{-1}s = k_B^{-1}\frac SN \approx \ln\frac{v(2m\epsilon)^{3/2}}{h^3} = \ln\frac{v}{\lambda^3}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m\epsilon}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 8, Eq. (1.3.8)" />

Here $\sqrt{2m\epsilon}$ is the momentum that goes with the average energy and $\lambda$ the matching de Broglie wavelength, so $v/\lambda^3$ is the number of wave packets of that size that fit into the volume available to one particle. If it is huge, the packets of different particles hardly ever overlap and the gas is **classical**; if it is of order one, they do, and the gas is a **quantum** gas:

<Formula label="Classical and quantum gases" tex="\text{classical gas: } \frac{v}{\lambda^3} \gg 1, \qquad \text{quantum gas: } \frac{v}{\lambda^3} \sim 1" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 8" />

Wen ends with a question for the reader: is air at room temperature a quantum gas or a classical one?

<WorkedExample title="Wen's question: air, and electrons in a metal">
<Problem>
Estimate $v/\lambda^3$, with Wen's $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2m\epsilon}$ and $\epsilon = \frac32k_BT$, for (a) nitrogen ($m = 28.01$ u) at 300 K and 1 atm, and (b) the conduction electrons of copper ($8.47 \times 10^{28}$ per m³), treated as if they were a classical gas at 300 K.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Air">
$\epsilon = 1.5 \times 1.381 \times 10^{-23} \times 300 = 6.21 \times 10^{-21}$ J and $m = 4.65 \times 10^{-26}$ kg, so $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2m\epsilon} = 2.76 \times 10^{-11}$ m. The volume per molecule is $v = k_BT/P = 4.09 \times 10^{-26}$ m³, and $v/\lambda^3 = 1.95 \times 10^6$. Air is a classical gas, by a wide margin.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Electrons in copper">
The electron is about 51,000 times lighter than a nitrogen molecule, so at the same $\epsilon$ its wavelength is $\lambda = 6.23 \times 10^{-9}$ m, while the volume per electron is only $v = 1/(8.47 \times 10^{28}) = 1.18 \times 10^{-29}$ m³. Then $v/\lambda^3 = 4.9 \times 10^{-5}$, far below one: the electrons of a metal are a quantum gas, which is why they need Fermi-Dirac statistics.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The energy as a function of entropy

Inverting the entropy gives the energy as a function of $S$, $V$ and $N$, from which all of thermodynamics follows (Section 1.3, p. 9). Solving (1.3.7) for $\epsilon$ (Wen, starting from the approximate (1.3.8), writes $N\frac{h^2N^{2/3}}{2mV^{2/3}}e^{2S/3Nk_B}$; we keep the constant he drops, which changes neither result below):

<Formula label="Energy of the ideal gas as a function of S, V and N" tex="E(S, V, N) = N\,\frac{3h^2}{4\pi m}\left(\frac NV\right)^{2/3}e^{2S/3Nk_B - 5/3}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 9, Eq. (1.3.9)" />

The temperature is the change of energy with entropy at fixed volume, and the pressure is minus the change of energy with volume at fixed entropy, that is, along a reversible adiabatic change (Section 1.3, p. 9):

<Formula label="Temperature and pressure from E(S, V, N)" tex="T = \left.\frac{\partial E}{\partial S}\right|_V = \frac{2E}{3Nk_B}, \qquad P = -\left.\frac{\partial E}{\partial V}\right|_S = \frac{2E}{3V}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 9, Eqs. (1.3.10)–(1.3.11)" />

The first gives $E = \frac32Nk_BT$, and dividing the second by the first gives the equation of state:

<Formula label="Equation of state" tex="PV = Nk_BT" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 9, Eq. (1.3.12)" />

Everything about the ideal gas, its energy, temperature and pressure, has come out of counting cells of size $h$ in phase space.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Counting states in the early universe">
Wen's reversible change, in which each state moves into exactly one new state and the count stays fixed, is the idea behind a rule 8.286 uses for the early universe: as long as it stays close to thermal equilibrium, its total entropy is conserved as it expands, and with an entropy density proportional to $T^3$ that makes the temperature fall as $1/a$. The test $v/\lambda^3 \gg 1$, on the other hand, fails for the photons of that radiation, whose typical wavelength is comparable to their spacing: they form a quantum gas, described in 8.286 by the black-body formulas rather than by a classical count.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Wen's one principle: all possible states, those compatible with what is known, are equally likely. Probability refers to an ensemble of copies; it describes one system over time only if the system is **ergodic**.
- $N$ spins with energies $\pm\epsilon_0/2$: $S = k_B\ln\binom{N}{M}$, at most $Nk_B\ln 2$; two systems in contact share energy so as to maximise the total entropy, giving $1/T = \partial S/\partial E$ and $T_1 = T_2$.
- For the spins $1/T = (k_B/\epsilon_0)\ln[(\frac12\epsilon_0 - \epsilon)/(\frac12\epsilon_0 + \epsilon)]$, negative when more than half the spins are up; in a weak field the moment follows **Curie's law**, $\chi = g^2\mu_B^2N/4k_BT$.
- Entropy is extensive, independent of the energy window for large systems, additive for separate systems, and maximal in equilibrium. It grows in irreversible change because we lose track of which states the system can be in; a reversible change keeps the count and the entropy.
- Ideal gas: one state per phase-space volume $h^{3N}$, and a factor $1/N!$ for identical particles, give an extensive entropy, $s/k_B \approx \ln(v/\lambda^3)$: classical if $v/\lambda^3 \gg 1$, quantum if it is of order one. From $E(S, V, N)$ follow $E = \frac32Nk_BT$ and $PV = Nk_BT$.
