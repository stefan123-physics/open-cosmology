The [previous page](/courses/8-08/canonical-ensemble-free-energy) derived the canonical ensemble and showed that a system at fixed temperature minimises its free energy $A = E - ST$. Wen's notes now use that idea in three ways: to show that ordering a system costs energy even when no energy is stored in it, which leads to the energy cost of information; to derive the equipartition of energy and see where it fails; and to compute the free energy, entropy and pressure of the classical ideal gas (notes pp. 5–12, points ⑤ to ⑦). The 8.044 pages on [canonical examples](/courses/8-044/canonical-examples) and [polyatomic gases](/courses/8-044/polyatomic-gases) did the last two in more detail; here we follow Wen's route. (Wen's handwritten page numbers skip 8: his p. 9 follows p. 7.)

## Stretching a chain with no internal energy

Wen's model is a **flexible chain** of $N$ links, each pointing either right ($+1$) or left ($-1$) (notes p. 5, point ⑤). Its length is the sum, in units of the link length:

<Formula label="Length of the chain" tex="\text{Length} = (+1) + (+1) + (-1) + (+1) + (+1) + \cdots = N_R - N_L" cite="reading1" at="p. 5, point ⑤" />

with $N = N_R + N_L$. The links do not interact, so every configuration has the same energy: there is **no internal energy**. The chain has $2^N$ configurations in all; the number with length zero, $N_R = N_L = N/2$, is a binomial coefficient, which we call $\Gamma_0$ (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Configurations of zero length" tex="\Gamma_0 = \frac{N!}{\left[(N/2)!\right]^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5, point ⑤" />

(The notes write $N!/(N/2)!$, without the square.) The entropies follow from $S = k_B\ln\Gamma$ (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Entropies of the chain" tex="S_{\text{tot}} = k_B\ln 2^N = Nk_B\ln 2, \qquad S_0 = k_B\ln\Gamma_0 \approx Nk_B\ln 2, \qquad S_1 = k_B\ln 1 = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 5, point ⑤" />

Here $S_{\text{tot}}$ counts all configurations, $S_0$ those of length zero and $S_1$ the single configuration of full length $N$, all links pointing right. The approximation for $S_0$ is Stirling's formula, $\ln N! \approx N\ln N - N$: almost all configurations have length close to zero, so to leading order in $N$ fixing the length at zero costs no entropy at all.

How much energy does it take to stretch the chain from length $0$ to length $N$? Since there is no internal energy, the tempting answer is none. Wen marks that answer wrong (notes p. 5). Put the chain in a heat bath at temperature $T$ (notes p. 6). Pulling it straight lowers its entropy from $Nk_B\ln 2$ to zero, and since the total entropy cannot decrease, the bath's entropy must rise by at least $Nk_B\ln 2$. For a bath, $S_{\text{bath}} = E_{\text{bath}}/T$, so its entropy can only rise if its energy does:

<Formula label="Energy cost of stretching the chain" tex="\Delta E_{\text{bath}} = T\,\Delta S = Nk_BT\ln 2 = A_{\text{final}} - A_{\text{init}} = (-S_{\text{final}}T) - (-S_{\text{init}}T) = \Delta S\,T" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

The puller supplies this energy as work, and it ends up as heat in the bath. With no internal energy, $A = -ST$, so the work is exactly the change in the chain's free energy. This is how a rubber band works: its tension is mostly entropic.

At intermediate lengths the same reasoning gives the free energy as a function of length. (The following is our extension of Wen's two end points.) With $N_R = N(1 + x)/2$ for a relative extension $x = \text{Length}/N$, Stirling's formula gives $A(x)/Nk_BT = -S(x)/Nk_B = \frac{1 + x}{2}\ln\frac{1 + x}{2} + \frac{1 - x}{2}\ln\frac{1 - x}{2}$, which runs from $-\ln 2$ at $x = 0$ up to $0$ at full stretch. Its slope is the force needed to hold the chain at that length; in units of $k_BT$ per link length it is $\tanh^{-1}x$, small and linear (Hooke's law) for small $x$, and growing without limit as the chain approaches full length.

<Problem>
Model a long molecule as a chain of $N = 2000$ segments, each pointing one of two ways as in Wen's model, in water at 310 K. What is the least work needed to pull it fully straight? And how good is the approximation $S_0 \approx Nk_B\ln 2$ for a short chain of $N = 100$?
</Problem>
<Step title="The work">
The work is $Nk_BT\ln 2 = 2000 \times 1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 310 \times 0.6931$ J $= 5.93 \times 10^{-18}$ J, or $2.97 \times 10^{-21}$ J per segment. Small, but compared with the thermal energy $k_BT = 4.28 \times 10^{-21}$ J it is not: each segment costs $0.69\,k_BT$.
</Step>
<Step title="Stirling's formula for N = 100">
Exactly, $\ln\Gamma_0 = \ln[100!/(50!)^2] = 66.78$, against $100\ln 2 = 69.31$. The difference, $2.53$, is close to $\frac12\ln(\pi N/2) = 2.53$, the next term of Stirling's formula. It grows only as $\ln N$, so for a long chain the relative error vanishes.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The energy cost of information

Wen turns the chain into a memory (notes p. 6). A string of bits that is random has entropy $Nk_B\ln 2$, like the chain of length zero; a string set to a definite pattern, say $1111000$, has entropy zero, like the stretched chain. To write a pattern into a random memory is to go from the first to the second, and the same argument applies: the total entropy can only increase, so the surroundings must take up at least $k_B\ln 2$ of entropy per bit. Wen's conclusion, boxed in the notes:

<Formula label="Energy cost of one bit" tex="\text{energy to encode one bit} = k_BT\ln 2" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

This is now called **Landauer's principle**, after Rolf Landauer, who argued in 1961 that erasing a bit must dissipate at least $k_BT\ln 2$; the notes do not name it. Wen puts numbers to it for ideal devices at room temperature (notes p. 7). Taking 300 K:

- An ideal memory chip: programming 1 gigabyte, $8 \times 10^9$ bits, needs at least $k_BT\ln 2 \times 8 \times 10^9 = 2.3 \times 10^{-11}$ J, which is Wen's $2.3 \times 10^{-4}$ erg.
- An ideal bus carrying $10^9$ bits per second needs a power of about $3 \times 10^{-12}$ W ($3 \times 10^{-5}$ erg/s), against the roughly 100 W that a computer actually uses.

Real electronics dissipate many orders of magnitude more than this limit, so it is not yet what limits computers. (Strictly, the bound applies to logically irreversible steps such as erasing or overwriting bits, which is what Wen's random-to-ordered memory does; Charles Bennett showed in the 1970s that a computation done reversibly can in principle avoid it. This caveat is not in the notes.)

## Measuring information

If ordering costs entropy, information can be measured by entropy: the less entropy, the more we know. Wen defines (notes p. 7)

<Formula label="Information and entropy" tex="\text{information} = \text{const} - \text{entropy} \times k_B^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

A single bit makes it concrete. If the bit is $0$ or $1$ with 50% each, we know nothing: the entropy is $k_B\ln 2$ and the information zero. If it is $0$ with certainty, the entropy is zero and the information is $\ln 2$. In general, for a system with $\Gamma$ states and probability $\rho_i$ of state $i$ (notes p. 7, boxed):

<Formula label="Information" tex="\text{information} = \ln\Gamma - \sum_i\rho_i\ln\frac{1}{\rho_i}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

The sum is the entropy in units of $k_B$, the same expression that the [previous page](/courses/8-08/canonical-ensemble-free-energy)'s worked example checked against $(\bar E - A)/T$. The information is zero if all states are equally likely, $\rho_i = 1/\Gamma$, and reaches its largest value, $\ln\Gamma$, if one $\rho_i = 1$. Measured in units of $\ln 2$, it is the number of bits.

## The Maxwell distribution and equipartition

Point ⑥ applies the canonical ensemble to the motion of a gas molecule (notes p. 9). The energy of a molecule of mass $m$ is $\vec p^{\,2}/2m$, so the probability of finding its momentum in a small volume $d^3p$ is proportional to

<Formula label="Distribution of momentum" tex="e^{-\beta\vec p^{\,2}/2m}\,d^3p" cite="reading1" at="p. 9, point ⑥" />

and, since $d^3p$ in a shell of speeds is proportional to $v^2\,dv$, the probability of a speed between $v$ and $v + dv$ is proportional to the **Maxwell distribution**:

<Formula label="Maxwell distribution of speeds" tex="e^{-\beta m v^2/2}\,v^2\,dv" cite="reading1" at="p. 9, point ⑥" />

It rises as $v^2$, peaks and falls off as a Gaussian. (Wen writes normalising constants in front of both; we leave them out.) The mean kinetic energy of motion along $x$ follows with a trick that recurs throughout the course: the average is a derivative of the logarithm of a normalising integral. The integrals over $p_y$ and $p_z$ cancel between top and bottom, and with $\int dx\,e^{-x^2} = \sqrt\pi$ (notes p. 9):

<Formula label="Mean kinetic energy of one component" tex="\left\langle\frac{p_x^2}{2m}\right\rangle = \frac{\int dp_x\,\frac{p_x^2}{2m}\,e^{-\beta p_x^2/2m}}{\int dp_x\,e^{-\beta p_x^2/2m}} = -\frac{d}{d\beta}\ln\int dp_x\,e^{-\beta p_x^2/2m} = -\frac{d}{d\beta}\ln\left(\sqrt\pi\sqrt{\frac{2m}{\beta}}\right) = \frac{1}{2\beta} = \frac{k_BT}{2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 9, point ⑥" />

The mass has dropped out. So every component has the same mean, and in a mixture of molecules of masses $m$ and $m'$ the heavy and the light ones carry the same kinetic energy (notes p. 9):

<Formula label="Equipartition" tex="\left\langle\frac{p_x^2}{2m}\right\rangle = \left\langle\frac{p_y^2}{2m}\right\rangle = \left\langle\frac{p_z^2}{2m}\right\rangle = \frac{k_BT}{2} = \left\langle\frac{p_x'^2}{2m'}\right\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 9, point ⑥" />

This is the **equipartition of energy**: each quadratic term in the energy gets $\frac12k_BT$. For $N$ molecules of a monatomic gas the total energy and the heat capacity at constant volume are (notes p. 9)

<Formula label="Energy and heat capacity of a monatomic gas" tex="E = 3\times\frac{Nk_BT}{2}, \qquad C_V = \frac{dE}{dT} = \frac32Nk_B, \qquad \text{mixed gas: } C_V = \frac32k_B(N_1 + N_2 + \cdots)" cite="reading1" at="p. 9, point ⑥" />

<WorkedExample title="A helium and argon mixture">
<Problem>
A mixture of 0.25 mol of helium (4.003 u) and 0.75 mol of argon (39.95 u) is at 300 K. Find each gas's mean kinetic energy per atom and root-mean-square speed, and the mixture's heat capacity at constant volume.
</Problem>
<Step title="Kinetic energy">
Both: $\frac32k_BT = 1.5 \times 1.380649 \times 10^{-23} \times 300$ J $= 6.21 \times 10^{-21}$ J per atom.
</Step>
<Step title="Speeds">
$v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3k_BT/m}$: $1367$ m/s for helium and $433$ m/s for argon. The light atoms move faster in exactly the proportion that makes their energies equal.
</Step>

$C_V = \frac32k_B(N_1 + N_2) = \frac32R \times 1.00$ mol $= 12.47$ J/K, whatever the proportions: only the number of atoms counts.
</Step>

## When equipartition fails: rotations and vibrations

For hydrogen, $\text{H}_2$, the heat capacity per molecule is not a single constant (notes p. 10). Wen sketches $C_V/Nk_B$ against temperature: $\frac32$ (translation only) at the lowest temperatures, rising to $\frac52$ when the molecule's two rotations join in, and to $\frac72$ when its vibration does. Classical equipartition would give the top value at every temperature.

The reason is that rotation is quantised. Wen takes rotation about one axis, the z direction, whose angular momentum is $L_z = j\hbar$ with $j = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \ldots$ (the notes call this number m). The energy is

<Formula label="Rotation levels about one axis" tex="E_j = \frac{L_z^2}{2I} = E_0\,j^2, \qquad j = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \ldots" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

with $I$ the moment of inertia and $E_0 = \hbar^2/2I$ (the notes leave $E_0$ as a constant; its value is our step), and the mean energy is the Boltzmann average

<Formula label="Mean rotational energy" tex="\langle E\rangle = \frac{\sum_j E_j\,e^{-\beta E_j}}{\sum_j e^{-\beta E_j}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

**High temperature**, $E_0\beta \ll 1$: many levels are occupied, the sums become Gaussian integrals over $j$, and

<Formula label="Mean rotational energy at high temperature" tex="\langle E\rangle = \frac{\int dj\,E_0\,j^2\,e^{-\beta E_0 j^2}}{\int dj\,e^{-\beta E_0 j^2}} = \frac12k_BT" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

equipartition again. (The notes put a factor $\frac12$ in front of $E_0\,j^2$ in the top integral; without it, as here, the integral gives the stated $\frac12k_BT$.)

**Low temperature**, $E_0\beta \gg 1$: only the ground state and the first excited level matter, and

<Formula label="Mean rotational energy at low temperature" tex="\langle E\rangle \approx 2E_0\,e^{-E_0/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

which vanishes exponentially: the rotation is **frozen out**. (The notes write $E_0\,e^{-E_0/k_BT}$; the factor 2 comes from the two states $j = \pm1$, and it does not change the conclusion.) The crossover is at the temperature $T_{\text{rot}} = E_0/k_B$.

Wen's table gives the two crossover temperatures for three gases (notes p. 10):

| Gas | $T_{\text{rot}}$ (K) | $T_{\text{vib}}$ (K) |
|---|---|---|
| H₂ | 85.4 | 6100 |
| N₂ | 2.86 | 3340 |
| O₂ | 2.07 | 2230 |

For air at room temperature, then, translation and both rotations are fully active and the vibrations are frozen, so each molecule contributes $\frac52k_B$ to $C_V$. (The notes write $\frac72k_B$ times the number of O₂ and N₂ molecules here; with the vibrations frozen, $\frac72k_B$ per molecule is the heat capacity at constant pressure, which is larger than $C_V$ by $k_B$ per molecule.) The real rotational levels of a diatomic molecule are those of a rotor in three dimensions, with energies proportional to l(l + 1) and degeneracy 2l + 1 for l = 0, 1, 2, …, as on the 8.044 page on [polyatomic gases](/courses/8-044/polyatomic-gases); Wen's one-axis model shows the same freezing more simply.

## The free energy of the classical ideal gas

Point ⑦ computes everything for the classical ideal gas of $N$ molecules in a volume $V$ (notes p. 11). The partition function is an integral over phase space, divided by $h^{3N}$ (one state per cell of volume $h$ for each coordinate and its momentum) and by $N!$ (the molecules are identical):

<Formula label="Partition function of the classical ideal gas" tex="Q(T, V) = \int\frac{d^{3N}p\,d^{3N}q}{N!\,h^{3N}}\,e^{-\beta(p_1^2 + \cdots + p_N^2)/2m} = \frac{1}{N!}\left(\frac{V}{\lambda^3}\right)^N" cite="reading1" at="p. 11, point ⑦" />

The positions give $V^N$, and each of the $3N$ momentum integrals gives the inverse of a length (notes p. 11):

<Formula label="Thermal wavelength" tex="\int\frac{dp}{h}\,e^{-\beta p^2/2m} = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_BT}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 11, point ⑦" />

the **thermal wavelength**, of the order of the de Broglie wavelength of a molecule with the typical thermal kinetic energy. The free energy, using Stirling's formula for $\ln N!$, is (notes p. 11 and p. 12)

<Formula label="Free energy of the ideal gas" tex="A(V, T) = -k_BT\ln Q = Nk_BT\left[\ln\frac{N\lambda^3}{V} - 1\right]" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–12" />

Since $\lambda^3 \propto T^{-3/2}$, differentiating gives the entropy (notes p. 11):

<Formula label="Entropy of the ideal gas" tex="S = -\left(\frac{\partial A}{\partial T}\right)_V = Nk_B\left(\frac52 + \ln\frac{V}{N\lambda^3}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 11, point ⑦" />

Wen reads $V/N\lambda^3$ as the number of states available per particle: the volume per particle measured in cells of the thermal wavelength. The classical treatment needs it to be large; when it is not, the gas is a quantum gas.

**Pressure.** Differentiate $A = -k_BT\ln Q$ with respect to the volume at fixed temperature (notes p. 11):

<Formula label="Pressure as minus the volume derivative of A" tex="\left.\frac{\partial A}{\partial V}\right|_T = -k_BT\,\frac{\partial Q/\partial V}{Q} = -k_BT\,\frac{\sum_n\left(-\beta\,\frac{\partial E_n}{\partial V}\right)e^{-\beta E_n}}{\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}} = \frac{\sum_n\frac{dE_n}{dV}\,e^{-\beta E_n}}{\sum_n e^{-\beta E_n}} = -\langle P\rangle" cite="reading1" at="p. 11, point ⑦" />

The last step is Wen's reading of $-dE_n/dV$: when an isolated system in its $n$th state is slowly compressed, it stays in that state and its energy rises, and $-dE_n/dV$ is the pressure it exerts. Averaging over states gives the pressure of the canonical ensemble. For the ideal gas (notes p. 12):

<WorkedExample title="Helium at room temperature and pressure">
<Problem>
Helium (4.003 u) is at 300 K and 1 atm (101325 Pa). Find its thermal wavelength, the number of states per particle $V/N\lambda^3$, and its molar entropy.
</Problem>
<Step title="The thermal wavelength">
$\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$, the same as $\sqrt{2\pi\hbar^2/mk_BT}$. With $m = 6.646 \times 10^{-27}$ kg and $k_BT = 4.142 \times 10^{-21}$ J, $\lambda = 5.04 \times 10^{-11}$ m, about half an ångström.
</Step>
<Step title="States per particle">
From the equation of state, $N/V = P/k_BT = 2.45 \times 10^{25}$ m⁻³, so $V/N\lambda^3 = 3.20 \times 10^5$. Each atom has hundreds of thousands of states to itself, and the classical treatment is excellent.
</Step>
<Step title="The entropy">
$S/Nk_B = \frac52 + \ln(3.20 \times 10^5) = 15.18$, so the molar entropy is $15.18\,R = 126.2$ J/(mol K). Tables give about 126.15 J/(mol K) for helium at 298 K and 1 bar, so this formula, with nothing but $h$, $m$ and $k_B$ in it, gets the measured entropy right.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Freezing out in the early universe">
The freezing of rotations and vibrations has a cosmic counterpart. As the universe cooled, each kind of particle contributed to the energy density in proportion to its number of states only while $k_BT$ was well above its rest energy; below that, its share fell by a Boltzmann factor, just as $\langle E\rangle \approx 2E_0e^{-E_0/k_BT}$ for a frozen rotation. 8.286 counts the species still active at each temperature to find how the energy density of the early universe depends on $T$. And the classical condition $V/N\lambda^3 \gg 1$, many states per particle, fails for the photons and neutrinos of the early universe: being (nearly) massless, their typical wavelength is about $\hbar c/k_BT$, comparable to their spacing, which is why they need the quantum gases of later chapters.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A chain of $N$ links with no internal energy still costs energy to stretch at temperature $T$: its entropy falls by $Nk_B\ln 2$, so the bath must gain at least $Nk_BT\ln 2$, the change in $A = -ST$.
- In the same way, writing one bit into a random memory costs at least $k_BT\ln 2$ (Landauer's principle), and information can be measured as $\ln\Gamma - \sum_i\rho_i\ln(1/\rho_i)$, a constant minus the entropy in units of $k_B$.
- In the canonical ensemble the momentum has the Maxwell distribution, and each quadratic term has mean $\frac12k_BT$ whatever the mass (**equipartition**): $C_V = \frac32Nk_B$ for any monatomic gas or mixture.
- Quantised motions drop out below their level spacing: a rotation with levels $E_0j^2$ gives $\frac12k_BT$ for $k_BT \gg E_0$ but only about $2E_0e^{-E_0/k_BT}$ for $k_BT \ll E_0$. Air at room temperature has $C_V \approx \frac52k_B$ per molecule.
- The classical ideal gas: $Q = (V/\lambda^3)^N/N!$, $A = Nk_BT[\ln(N\lambda^3/V) - 1]$, $S = Nk_B[\frac52 + \ln(V/N\lambda^3)]$, and from $-\partial A/\partial V$ the pressure $P = Nk_BT/V$.
