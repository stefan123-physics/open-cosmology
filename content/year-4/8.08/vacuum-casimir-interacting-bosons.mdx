The [previous page](/courses/8-08/bosons-as-vibrating-string) showed that free bosons can be described in two equivalent ways: as particles placed in an empty vacuum, or as the quantized vibrations of a string (a "brane" in more dimensions). This page covers sections 4.6 to 4.8 of Wen's chapter (pp. 37–43): why the two pictures differ physically, the zero-point energy of the vacuum and the Casimir effect, and the field description of non-relativistic and interacting bosons. As before, Wen sets $\hbar = 1$; we restore it in numbers.

## Two pictures, one difference

The two pictures give the same quantum theory, but they say different things about the vacuum (section 4.6, pp. 37–38). In the particle picture the vacuum is an empty stage. In the brane picture it is a **medium**, and bosons are its vibrations. Wen gives two reasons to prefer the medium.

First, it explains identical particles. Vibrations of one brane are always identical bosons; there is no way to make distinguishable particles out of them. In the particle picture, by contrast, identical particles are an extra assumption. Wen turns the argument round: the fact that the particles we see are identical suggests that our vacuum is itself a medium, a "3-brane", and that the theory of elementary particles is the study of one material, the vacuum. (He adds a caution in small print, p. 38: a vibrating brane gives only spinless, scalar bosons. Photons, with their two polarizations, and electrons, which are fermions, need a medium with more internal structure, the subject of his research [Wen 2002, 2003b]. In this chapter photons are treated as massless scalar bosons.)

Second, and more concretely, the medium picture has a **measurable consequence**: the Casimir effect.

## The vacuum energy and the cut-off

In the particle picture, the vacuum is the zero of energy. In the medium picture, every oscillator keeps its zero-point energy $\frac12\epsilon_\kappa$ (see [zero-point energy in 8.044](/courses/8-044/zero-point-energy-fluctuations)), so the vacuum has the energy

<Formula label="Vacuum energy" tex="U_{\text{vac}} = \sum_{\kappa}\frac12\epsilon_{\kappa}" cite="reading1" at="§4.6, p. 38" />

A constant in the total energy cannot be measured. But if the walls of the box move, or the dispersion $\epsilon_\kappa$ changes, $U_{\text{vac}}$ changes, and a **change** of energy is measurable (p. 38).

The sum itself is infinite, since there is no largest wave vector. This infinity, Wen notes, has haunted every field theory and once led people to abandon them. His view (p. 39) is that the culprit is not the brane picture but the idea that space is a continuum. Below the **Planck length** $l_P$, he argues (from an earlier chapter of his book), distances lose their meaning, so wave vectors above about $\Lambda \sim 1/l_P$ do not exist. The string is more like a row of beads (Fig. 4.6). The sum should then stop at the **cut-off** $\Lambda$:

<Formula label="Vacuum energy with a sharp or a soft cut-off" tex="U_{\text{vac}} = \sum_{|\kappa_n| < \Lambda}\frac12\epsilon_{\kappa_n} \qquad\text{or}\qquad U_{\text{vac}} = \sum_{\kappa_n}\frac12\epsilon_{\kappa_n}e^{-|\kappa_n|/\Lambda}" cite="reading1" at="§4.6, p. 39" />

Which one is right? Nobody knows, since we have no theory of quantum gravity. What saves the day is that the effects at low energy and long distance turn out not to depend on how the sum is cut off. We can predict them without knowing the physics at short distances.

## Walls and the Casimir force

Put massless bosons in one dimension (the notes start with bosons of mass $m$ but then use $\epsilon = c|k|$) between two hard walls at $x = 0$ and $x = L$ (pp. 39–40, Fig. 4.7). The string is now pinned, $h(0) = h(L) = 0$, so its modes are $\sin(n\pi x/L)$, with wave vectors $\tilde\kappa_n = n\pi/L$ (only $n = 1, 2, \dots$ are real modes, since $n = 0$ gives zero energy anyway). With $\epsilon = c|\kappa|$ and the soft cut-off, the sum is geometric and can be done exactly:

<Formula label="Vacuum energy between two walls" tex="U^{\text{hw}}_{\text{vac}}(L) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac12\,\frac{c\pi n}{L}\,e^{-n\pi/L\Lambda} = \frac{c\pi}{8L\sinh^2(\pi/2L\Lambda)} = \frac{c\Lambda^2L}{2\pi} - \frac{c\pi}{24L} + O(\Lambda^{-2})" cite="reading1" at="§4.6, p. 40, eqn (4.6.1)" />

The first term grows with the length, like the energy of a material, and depends entirely on the cut-off. The second does not depend on $\Lambda$ at all. (The notes print $c\pi/2L\Lambda$ inside the sinh and $\Lambda^2L/4c\pi$ for the first term. With the cut-off $e^{-|\kappa|/\Lambda}$ that Wen writes, the sum gives the forms above; his forms correspond to a cut-off on the energy, $e^{-\epsilon/\Lambda}$, apart from a factor 2 in the first term. The cut-off-free term $-c\pi/24L$, the only one that matters below, is the same in every version.)

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0.25, 5]} range={[-0.8, 0.1]} xLabel="L Λ" yLabel="U − bulk term (units of ħcΛ)" samples={300}
  curves={[

  caption="The vacuum energy between two walls with the soft cut-off, minus its bulk term cΛ²L/2π, computed from the exact sum cπ/[8L sinh²(π/2LΛ)] (units with ħ = c = 1, energies in units of ħcΛ and lengths in units of 1/Λ). Once the walls are more than a few cut-off lengths apart, what is left is the cut-off-free Casimir term −πħc/24L (dashed); at L = 5/Λ they differ by about 2%, and the difference falls off as 1/L³." />

Now add a third wall at $x = a$ (Fig. 4.8). The modes on each side are separate, so the vacuum energy is the sum for a gap of $a$ and a gap of $L - a$:

<Formula label="Three walls" tex="U^{\text{hw}}_{\text{vac}}(a, L) = \frac{c\Lambda^2L}{2\pi} - \frac{c\pi}{24(L - a)} - \frac{c\pi}{24a} + O(\Lambda^{-2})" cite="reading1" at="§4.6, p. 40" />

The bulk terms add up to a term in the total length $L$ only, which does not change when the middle wall moves. For $L \gg a$ the energy is $-c\pi/24a$ plus a constant, and it falls as the gap closes: the walls attract. With $\hbar$ restored, the force is

<Formula label="Casimir force in one dimension" tex="F = \frac{\pi\hbar c}{24a^2}" cite="reading1" at="§4.6, p. 40" />

independent of the cut-off. This is the **Casimir effect** [Casimir 1948]. For photons in three dimensions, between two plates of area $A$ a distance $a$ apart, Wen quotes the standard result (which counts both polarizations of the photon; a single scalar field would give half of it)

<Formula label="Casimir force between plates" tex="F = \frac{\pi^2\hbar c}{240a^4}\,A" cite="reading1" at="§4.6, p. 40" />

which has been measured, by Sparnaay in 1958 (the notes spell the name "Spamaay") and by Lamoreaux in 1997. Wen reads these measurements as evidence that empty space is a medium with dynamics of its own, so that particle physics becomes, in his words, a kind of materials science of the vacuum.

<WorkedExample title="How strong is the Casimir force?">
<Problem>
(a) Two parallel conducting plates of area 1 cm² are 1 μm apart. Find the Casimir force and the pressure. (b) What are they at 100 nm? (c) For comparison, find the one-dimensional force for massless scalar bosons between walls 1 μm apart.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Plates 1 μm apart">
With $\hbar c = 3.162 \times 10^{-26}$ J m: $F = \pi^2 \times 3.162 \times 10^{-26} \times 10^{-4}/(240 \times 10^{-24})$ N $= 1.30 \times 10^{-7}$ N. The pressure is $F/A = 1.30 \times 10^{-3}$ Pa, about $10^{-8}$ of atmospheric pressure: tiny, which is why the first measurements were so hard.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Plates 100 nm apart">
The force grows as $1/a^4$, so ten times closer means $10^4$ times stronger: $1.30 \times 10^{-3}$ N on a square centimetre, a pressure of $13.0$ Pa. At such gaps the Casimir force matters in micro-machines.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) One dimension">
$F = \pi\hbar c/24a^2 = \pi \times 3.162 \times 10^{-26}/(24 \times 10^{-12})$ N $= 4.14 \times 10^{-15}$ N. In one dimension there is no area, and the force falls only as $1/a^2$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Non-relativistic bosons as a classical field

Everything so far used relativistic bosons. The same steps work for non-relativistic bosons of mass $m$ in $d$ dimensions in a potential $U$ (section 4.7, pp. 40–41), with the dispersion $\epsilon_k = k^2/2m + U$ and the $n$-particle Hamiltonian

<Formula label="Free non-relativistic bosons" tex="\hat H_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(-\frac{1}{2m}\partial_{x_i}^2 + U\right)" cite="reading1" at="§4.7, p. 41, eqn (4.7.1)" />

The corresponding classical field $\phi(x, t)$, whose real and imaginary parts carry the amplitude and the velocity of the vibration as in the previous page, obeys a phase-space equation of motion and has a total energy

<Formula label="Field equation and energy" tex="i\dot\phi(x, t) = \left(-\frac{1}{2m}\partial_x^2 + U\right)\phi(x, t), \qquad E_{\text{tot}} = \int d^dx\,\phi^*\left(-\frac{1}{2m}\partial_x^2 + U\right)\phi" cite="reading1" at="§4.7, p. 41, eqns (4.7.2) and (4.7.3)" />

Both follow from one **phase-space Lagrangian**:

<Formula label="Phase-space Lagrangian" tex="L = \int d^dx\,i\phi^*\dot\phi - E_{\text{tot}} = \int d^dx\left[i\phi^*\dot\phi - \phi^*\left(-\frac{1}{2m}\partial_x^2 + U\right)\phi\right]" cite="reading1" at="§4.7, p. 41, eqn (4.7.4)" />

Treat $\phi$ and $\phi^*$ as independent: setting the variation with respect to $\phi^*$ to zero gives the field equation (Wen's Problem 4.7.1), and the part without a time derivative is the energy. (In a footnote, p. 41, Wen also gives the coordinate-space form, a second-order equation for the real amplitude $h$.)

The potential may depend on position, $U = U(x)$. A **hard wall** is a region of infinite $U$: for the energy to stay finite, $\phi$ must vanish there. This is why a hard wall is the boundary condition $\phi = 0$, or $h = 0$, used for the Casimir effect above (p. 41).

## Interacting bosons, second-quantized

Now let the bosons interact through a pair potential $V(x)$ (section 4.8, pp. 41–43):

<Formula label="Interacting bosons" tex="\hat H_n = \sum_{i}\left(-\frac{1}{2m}\partial_{x_i}^2 + U\right) + \sum_{i<j}V(x_i - x_j)" cite="reading1" at="§4.8, p. 41, eqn (4.8.1)" />

To write it with field operators, note that the pair sum is a double integral over the density $n(x)$, each pair counted once:

<Formula label="Pair energy from the density" tex="\sum_{i<j}V(x_i - x_j) = \int d^dx\,d^dx'\,\frac12\,n(x)V(x - x')n(x')" cite="reading1" at="§4.8, p. 42" />

(strictly, the right side also contains each particle paired with itself, $\frac12 V(0)$ per particle; this is exactly what has to be removed below). With the density operator $\hat n = \hat\phi^\dagger\hat\phi$ from the previous page, in a box of volume $\mathcal{V} = L^d$ with modes $\hat\phi(x) = \sum_kL^{-d/2}e^{ik\cdot x}\hat\phi_k$ (eqn 4.8.2), Wen writes the Hamiltonian as

<Formula label="Hamiltonian with a bare one-boson energy" tex="\hat H = \sum_k\tilde\epsilon_k\left(\hat\phi^\dagger_k\hat\phi_k + \frac12\right) + \frac{1}{2\mathcal{V}}\sum_{q,k,k'}\hat\phi^\dagger_{k+q}\hat\phi_kV_q\hat\phi^\dagger_{k'-q}\hat\phi_{k'}, \qquad V_q = \int d^dx\,e^{-iq\cdot x}V(x)" cite="reading1" at="§4.8, p. 42, eqn (4.8.3)" />

Here $\tilde\epsilon_k$ is a "bare" energy still to be fixed. The naive guess $\tilde\epsilon_k = k^2/2m + U$ is wrong, and the states with few bosons show why (pp. 42–43). The vacuum $|0\rangle$, defined by $\hat\phi_k|0\rangle = 0$ for every $k$ (eqn 4.8.4), has no bosons and energy $E_0 = \sum_k\frac12\tilde\epsilon_k$. Next try one boson, $|k\rangle = \hat\phi^\dagger_k|0\rangle$. Moving every annihilation operator to the right of every creation operator, **normal ordering**, rewrites the Hamiltonian as

<Formula label="Normal-ordered Hamiltonian" tex="\hat H = \sum_k\left(\epsilon_k\hat\phi^\dagger_k\hat\phi_k + \frac12\tilde\epsilon_k\right) + \frac{1}{2\mathcal{V}}\sum_{q,k,k'}V_q\hat\phi^\dagger_{k+q}\hat\phi^\dagger_{k'-q}\hat\phi_k\hat\phi_{k'}, \qquad \epsilon_k = \tilde\epsilon_k + \frac{V(0)}{2}" cite="reading1" at="§4.8, pp. 42–43, eqns (4.8.5) and (4.8.6)" />

The extra piece comes from the single commutator picked up in the reordering; it is $\frac{1}{2\mathcal{V}}\sum_qV_q = \frac12V(0)$ per boson, the self-interaction just mentioned. The interaction term now has two annihilation operators on the right, so it gives zero on a state with one boson, and $|k\rangle$ is an eigenstate with excitation energy $E_k - E_0 = \epsilon_k$. For a lone boson to have its proper energy $k^2/2m + U$, the bare energy must be $\tilde\epsilon_k = k^2/2m + U - V(0)/2$. (The notes describe normal ordering as putting "$a$" to the right of "$a^\dagger$", their usual letters for these operators.)

Many-boson states are built the same way: the state with $n_k$ bosons in each level $k$ is $\prod_k(\hat\phi^\dagger_k)^{n_k}|0\rangle$, up to normalisation. Such a state has a definite number of bosons and a definite momentum $\sum_kkn_k$, but because of the interaction it is **not** an energy eigenstate, and in general the eigenstates are hard to find. The Hamiltonian is no longer quadratic in the operators: interacting bosons are a set of **anharmonic** oscillators. With the proper $\epsilon_k$, the Hamiltonian takes a compact form in position space:

<Formula label="Second-quantized Hamiltonian of interacting bosons" tex="\hat H = \int d^dx\,\hat\phi^\dagger(x)\left(-\frac{\partial_x^2}{2m} + U\right)\hat\phi(x) + \int d^dx\,d^dx'\,\frac12V(x - x')\hat\phi^\dagger(x)\hat\phi^\dagger(x')\hat\phi(x')\hat\phi(x)" cite="reading1" at="§4.8, p. 43, eqn (4.8.7)" />

with $[\hat\phi(x), \hat\phi^\dagger(x')] = \delta(x - x')$. Wen's problems (p. 43) ask the reader to show that it commutes with the number operator $\hat N$ and the momentum, so both are conserved: the Hamiltonian is unchanged by the phase rotation $e^{i\theta\hat N}$, a **U(1) symmetry** that becomes the centre of the next page.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The vacuum energy and gravity">
Wen's argument that only changes of the vacuum energy can be measured holds, we would add, only as long as gravity is left out. Gravity responds to the absolute energy density, and in cosmology the vacuum energy acts as Einstein's cosmological constant, as Guth explains in his [lecture on the cosmological constant](/courses/8-286/cosmological-constant). In his [first lecture](/courses/8-286/eternal-inflation) he does the sum of this page: zero-point energies of all modes, cut off at the Planck length, and the answer is too large by about 120 orders of magnitude. (Both the remark about gravity and the link to Wen's cut-off are ours; Wen does not discuss gravity here.)
</Callout>

## Summary

- The particle and brane pictures of bosons are mathematically equivalent but physically different: the brane picture explains why identical particles exist and makes the vacuum a dynamical medium (only for scalar bosons in this simple form).
- The vacuum energy $U_{\text{vac}} = \sum\frac12\epsilon_\kappa$ is infinite in a continuum; Wen cuts it off near the Planck scale, $\Lambda \sim 1/l_P$. The cut-off part never affects long-distance physics.
- Between hard walls, massless 1D bosons have $U^{\text{hw}}_{\text{vac}}(L) = c\Lambda^2L/2\pi - \pi\hbar c/24L + \dots$; a movable wall feels the attractive, cut-off-free **Casimir force** $\pi\hbar c/24a^2$. For photons between plates, $F = \pi^2\hbar cA/240a^4$, measured in 1958 and 1997.
- Non-relativistic free bosons are the field theory with $i\dot\phi = (-\partial_x^2/2m + U)\phi$, derived from the phase-space Lagrangian $L = \int[i\phi^*\dot\phi - \phi^*(-\partial_x^2/2m + U)\phi]$; a hard wall means $\phi = 0$.
- Interacting bosons: $\hat H = \int\hat\phi^\dagger(-\partial_x^2/2m + U)\hat\phi + \frac12\int\int V\hat\phi^\dagger\hat\phi^\dagger\hat\phi\hat\phi$. Normal ordering shifts the one-boson energy by $V(0)/2$; the Hamiltonian describes anharmonic oscillators and conserves the number of bosons.
