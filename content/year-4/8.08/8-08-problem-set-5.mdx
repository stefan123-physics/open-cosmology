This set practises three chapters of Wen's notes: the [law of mass action](/courses/8-08/equal-chemical-potentials) for a reaction among classical gases (Chapter IV), [random variables and their independence](/courses/8-08/random-variables-binomial-distribution), the binomial distribution and the central limit theorem (Chapter V), and [thermal fluctuations](/courses/8-08/particle-number-fluctuations) found from the free energy (Chapter VI). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 5 (dated March 2, 2005), in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its dates are left out. MIT's Problems 1, 2 and 3 are stated in full on the sheet and are given here as MIT sets them, in our words. Our additions to them are marked where they stand: in Problem 1 the assumption that the carriers are classical gases without spin, and part (c) with its numbers and constants; in Problem 2 the number box of part (a) and part (c); in Problem 3 our reading of $x$ as one horizontal coordinate, the classical treatment of small swings, the values of $g$ and $k_B$, and part (c). MIT's Problem 4 is only a pointer to a problem in a textbook (Huang's *Statistical Mechanics*), named by number. The textbook is not used here: **Problem 4 is our own problem**, on a subject of Chapter V (a random walk, the binomial distribution and the central limit theorem). For the electrons and holes of Problem 1, Chapter VIII of the notes comes back to the same reaction with real semiconductors: see [semiconductors and diodes](/courses/8-08/semiconductors-and-diodes), later in the course, as further reading.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Electrons and holes in a semiconductor" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

At a finite temperature $T$, a semiconductor holds both electrons and holes. An electron and a hole can meet and annihilate, giving off an energy $\Delta$; the reverse process creates a pair:

<Formula label="Annihilation of an electron and a hole" tex="e + h \leftrightarrow \Delta" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

You may give each electron and each hole an internal energy $\Delta/2$, so that a pair carries $\Delta$. Electrons and holes have the same mass $m$. Treat both as ideal classical gases with no internal states besides the energy $\Delta/2$, like the gases of the reactions in Chapter IV of the notes (our assumption: real electrons and holes have two spin states, which the solution comments on).

<Part label="a" points={8}>

In an undoped (pure) semiconductor every electron in the conduction band has left a hole behind, so $n_e = n_h$. Find this density as a function of $m$, $T$ and $\Delta$.

<Hint>

The annihilation is a reaction like those of Chapter IV. Write the equilibrium condition for the chemical potentials, using the coefficients of the reaction.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The energy $\Delta$ that is given off goes into the crystal's vibrations and into light, whose number is not fixed: it has no chemical potential. So the condition is $\mu_e + \mu_h = 0$. For a classical gas with an internal energy, $\mu = k_BT\ln(n\lambda^3) + (\text{internal energy})$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-ni" label="$n_e = n_h =$" expr="(m*kB*T/(2*pi*hbar^2))^(3/2)*exp(-D/(2*kB*T))"
  vars={{ m: [0.5, 2], kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 1.5], D: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ m: "m", kB: "k_B", T: "T", hbar: "\\hbar", D: "\\Delta" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "2*(m*kB*T/(2*pi*hbar^2))^(3/2)*exp(-D/(2*kB*T))", hint: "The factor 2 counts two spin states for each carrier. This problem takes the carriers to have no internal states besides the energy Δ/2 (our assumption), as the solution explains." },
    { expr: "(2*pi*hbar^2/(m*kB*T))^(3/2)*exp(-D/(2*kB*T))", hint: "That is λ³ times the Boltzmann factor, a volume, not a density. From k_BT ln(nλ³) the density is the Boltzmann factor divided by λ³." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** Electrons and holes are made and destroyed in pairs, like the atoms and molecules of a chemical reaction. In equilibrium the chemical potentials, weighted by the coefficients of the reaction, add up to zero; for classical gases this is the law of mass action.

<Step title="The equilibrium condition">

Written as Wen writes reactions, $e + h = \Delta$: one electron and one hole, both with the coefficient $+1$, release the energy $\Delta$. A small step of the reaction changes nothing at the minimum of the free energy, so

<Formula label="Equilibrium of the reaction" tex="\mu_e + \mu_h = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 34, point ④ⓒ (the condition that the chemical potentials weighted by the coefficients add up to zero, applied here by us)" />

The released energy goes into lattice vibrations and light, whose numbers are not conserved, so it adds no chemical potential of its own.

</Step>

<Step title="The chemical potentials">

For a classical gas whose particles carry an internal energy, the internal energy adds to the chemical potential:

<Formula label="Chemical potentials of the carriers" tex="\mu_e = k_BT\ln(n_e\lambda^3) + \frac{\Delta}{2}, \qquad \mu_h = k_BT\ln(n_h\lambda^3) + \frac{\Delta}{2}, \qquad \lambda = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_BT}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 34, point ④ⓒ (the form of μ for hydrogen, applied here by us)" />

Both carriers have the same mass, so the same $\lambda$.

</Step>

<Step title="The law of mass action">

Adding the two and setting the sum to zero:

<Formula label="Mass action for electrons and holes" tex="(n_e\lambda^3)(n_h\lambda^3) = e^{-\Delta/k_BT}, \qquad n_en_h = \lambda^{-6}e^{-\Delta/k_BT}" cite="reading1" at="p. 34, point ④ⓒ (law of mass action, applied here by us)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The pure semiconductor">

With $n_e = n_h$, each is the square root of the product:

<Formula label="Carrier density of a pure semiconductor" tex="n_e = n_h = n_i = \left(\frac{mk_BT}{2\pi\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}e^{-\Delta/2k_BT}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

We call this density $n_i$ (the intrinsic density) for part (b).

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The units are those of $1/\lambda^3$, a number per volume. As $T \to 0$ the density vanishes, as it must when no pair can be made. The density rises like $e^{-\Delta/2k_BT}$, with half the gap in the exponent: each pair costs $\Delta$, but it makes two particles, each of which gains its own share of the entropy. The classical treatment needs $n_i\lambda^3 = e^{-\Delta/2k_BT} \ll 1$, that is $\Delta \gg k_BT$. (Our remark: if each carrier had two spin states, as real electrons and holes do, each chemical potential would contain $\ln(n\lambda^3/2)$ and the density would be twice as large. Chapter VIII of the notes includes this factor.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

In a doped semiconductor, impurity atoms give up (or take) electrons, and the difference of the two densities is fixed by the doping: $n_e - n_h = n_d$, with $n_d$ a given constant. Find $n_e$ and $n_h$. Write each with $n_d$ and $n_i$, the density of part (a).

<Hint>

The doping does not change the reaction, so the product $n_en_h$ is still the one of part (a). How can you write that product with $n_i$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Two equations, $n_en_h = n_i^2$ and $n_e - n_h = n_d$. Put $n_h = n_e - n_d$ into the first: a quadratic equation for $n_e$. Only one root is positive.

</Hint>

    { expr: "nd/2 - sqrt(nd^2/4 + ni^2)", hint: "That is the negative root of the quadratic: a negative density. Take the other root." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-nh" label="$n_h =$" expr="-nd/2 + sqrt(nd^2/4 + ni^2)"

  names={{ nd: "n_d", ni: "n_i" }}

<Solution>

**The idea.** The law of mass action does not care where the electrons came from: the product $n_en_h$ is fixed by $T$ and $\Delta$ alone. The doping fixes the difference. A product and a difference fix both numbers.

<Step title="Two equations">

From part (a) the law of mass action reads $n_en_h = n_i^2$, and the doping gives $n_e - n_h = n_d$:

<Formula label="Conditions in a doped semiconductor" tex="n_en_h = n_i^2, \qquad n_e - n_h = n_d" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Solve">

With $n_h = n_e - n_d$: $n_e^2 - n_dn_e - n_i^2 = 0$. The positive root, and then $n_h = n_e - n_d$:

<Formula label="Electrons and holes in a doped semiconductor" tex="n_e = \frac{n_d}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{n_d^2}{4} + n_i^2}, \qquad n_h = -\frac{n_d}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{n_d^2}{4} + n_i^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Both are positive, and their product is $(n_d^2/4 + n_i^2) - n_d^2/4 = n_i^2$, as required.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the limits">

Without doping, $n_d = 0$, both reduce to $n_i$, the result of part (a). With strong doping, $n_d \gg n_i$, the electrons number about $n_d$ and the holes only about $n_i^2/n_d$: the carriers given by the impurities wipe out most of the holes. (A negative $n_d$, an excess of holes, swaps the roles of the two.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

(Our addition, with our own numbers, chosen near those of silicon.) Take $m = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg (the mass of a free electron), $\Delta = 1.10$ eV and $T = 300$ K, with $k_B = 1.381 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K, $\hbar = 1.055 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and 1 eV $= 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ J. Find the density $n_i$ of the pure crystal, in carriers per cubic metre. Then dope it with $n_d = 1.00 \times 10^{21}$ m⁻³ and find the hole density $n_h$, in m⁻³ (tolerance 3% each).

<Hint>

First the two factors of part (a): $(mk_BT/2\pi\hbar^2)^{3/2}$, which is of order $10^{25}$ m⁻³, and the exponent $\Delta/2k_BT$. The exponent is about 21, so keep four figures in it.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Compare $n_d$ with $n_i$: which limit of part (b) applies?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into part (a), then use the strong-doping limit of part (b).

<Step title="The pure crystal">

$k_BT = 4.143 \times 10^{-21}$ J, so $mk_BT/2\pi\hbar^2 = 5.40 \times 10^{16}$ m⁻² and its $\frac32$ power is $1.25 \times 10^{25}$ m⁻³. The exponent is $\Delta/2k_BT = 1.762 \times 10^{-19}/8.286 \times 10^{-21} = 21.27$, and $e^{-21.27} = 5.80 \times 10^{-10}$. So $n_i = 7.28 \times 10^{15}$ m⁻³: one carrier in a cube about 5 μm on a side.

</Step>

<Step title="The doped crystal">

One dopant in about $5 \times 10^7$ atoms of the crystal (a solid has some $5 \times 10^{28}$ atoms per cubic metre) lowers the number of holes by a factor of more than $10^5$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

$n_i\lambda^3 = e^{-21.27} = 5.80 \times 10^{-10} \ll 1$: the gas of carriers is deep in the classical regime, as assumed. (Real silicon has carriers whose masses differ from the free electron's, and two spin states each, so these numbers show the size of the effect, not the measured densities.)

</Step>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Two dice: which numbers are independent?" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

Roll two fair dice. They give a pair of random numbers $(n_1, n_2)$, each from 1 to 6, and all 36 pairs are equally likely.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Form the sum and the difference of the two numbers,

<Formula label="Sum and difference" tex="k_+ = n_1 + n_2, \qquad k_- = n_1 - n_2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1" />

Are $k_+$ and $k_-$ independent random numbers?

<Hint>

Independent means that the joint probability of every pair of values is the product of the two separate probabilities. One counterexample is enough to show that they are not.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Try an extreme value: if $k_+ = 2$, what can $k_-$ be?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-indep-k" label="$k_+$ and $k_-$ are" choices={["independent: the joint probability of every pair of values is the product of the separate ones", "not independent: some pairs of values cannot occur together, though each value alone can", "independent: their correlation is zero, so knowing one says nothing about the other", "independent: n₁ and n₂ are independent, so any two functions of them are too"]} answer={1} />

(Our addition.) To see how much one number tells about the other, find the probability that $k_- = 0$ when you already know that $k_+ = 4$. Give it as a decimal (tolerance 1%).

<Answer id="p2-cond" label="$P(k_- = 0 \text{ given } k_+ = 4)$:" value="0.333" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-cond" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Look for a pair of values whose joint probability is not the product of the separate ones.

<Step title="A counterexample">

$k_+ = 2$ happens only for the roll $(1, 1)$, with probability $1/36$, and then $k_- = 0$. So $P(k_+ = 2, k_- = 1) = 0$, while separately $P(k_+ = 2) = 1/36$ and $P(k_- = 1) = 5/36$ (the rolls $(2,1)$ to $(6,5)$). The product $5/1296$ is not zero, so the joint probability is not the product of the separate ones, as the boxed test of independence in the notes (p. 38) requires, and $k_+$ and $k_-$ are **not independent**. There is a simple reason: $k_+ - k_- = 2n_2$ is even, so $k_+$ and $k_-$ are always both even or both odd. Knowing $k_+$ halves the possible values of $k_-$.

</Step>

<Step title="The conditional probability">

$k_+ = 4$ comes from three rolls, $(1, 3)$, $(2, 2)$ and $(3, 1)$, equally likely, with $k_- = -2$, $0$ and $2$. So given $k_+ = 4$, $k_- = 0$ has the probability $1/3 = 0.333$. Without that knowledge, $k_- = 0$ comes from the six rolls with $n_1 = n_2$: $P(k_- = 0) = 6/36 = 1/6$. Knowing the sum doubles the odds of a zero difference here.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Sum the joint table over $k_-$: for each $k_+$ the conditional probabilities add up to 1, as for $k_+ = 4$, where $\frac13 + \frac13 + \frac13 = 1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Now form

<Formula label="Two numbers taken modulo 6" tex="m_+ = (n_1 + n_2)\ \text{mod}\ 6, \qquad m_- = n_1\ \text{mod}\ 6" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1" />

where "mod 6" is the remainder after division by 6, a number from 0 to 5. Are $m_+$ and $m_-$ independent random numbers?

<Hint>

$m_-$ is $n_1$ itself, except that 6 is written as 0: knowing $m_-$ is the same as knowing $n_1$. Fix $n_1$, and let $n_2$ run from 1 to 6. Which values does $m_+$ take?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-indep-m" label="$m_+$ and $m_-$ are" choices={["not independent: m₊ is built from n₁, so knowing m₋ changes the odds of m₊", "not independent: some pairs of values of m₊ and m₋ never occur together", "not independent: m₊ = 0 is twice as likely as each other value of m₊", "independent: each of the 36 pairs of values has the same probability 1/36"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a fixed first die, adding the second die and taking the remainder runs once through every remainder.

<Step title="Fix the first die">

Take any $n_1$, so any $m_-$. As $n_2$ runs through 1 to 6, $n_1 + n_2$ runs through six consecutive whole numbers, and six consecutive numbers have six different remainders after division by 6, one each of 0 to 5. So for every value of $m_-$, each value of $m_+$ comes from exactly one of the six equally likely values of $n_2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The joint table">

Each of the $6 \times 6 = 36$ pairs $(m_+, m_-)$ therefore comes from exactly one roll, with probability $1/36$. Separately, each value of $m_+$ and of $m_-$ has probability $1/6$, and $\frac16 \times \frac16 = \frac{1}{36}$: the joint probability is the product for every pair. $m_+$ and $m_-$ **are independent**, although $m_+$ is built from $n_1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The conditional probability of $m_+$ given $m_-$ is $1/6$ for every value, whatever $m_-$ is: knowing the first die tells nothing about the remainder of the sum, because the second die, taken mod 6, is uniform and shifts it at random. Compare part (a): there the sum is not taken mod 6, and the values near 2 and 12 betray the dice.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

(Our addition.) The correlation of two random numbers is $\langle k_+k_-\rangle - \langle k_+\rangle\langle k_-\rangle$, as in the notes. What is it for the sum and the difference of part (a)?

<Hint>

$k_+k_- = n_1^2 - n_2^2$. What is its average for two dice that are alike?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-corr" label="The correlation of $k_+$ and $k_-$ is" choices={["positive, because a large sum tends to go together with a large k₋", "zero, even though part (a) showed that k₊ and k₋ are not independent", "negative, because a large sum tends to go together with a small k₋", "not defined, because k₋ takes negative values as well as positive ones"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write the product with the dice themselves and use that the two dice are alike.

<Step title="The averages">

<Formula label="Correlation of the sum and the difference" tex="\langle k_+k_-\rangle - \langle k_+\rangle\langle k_-\rangle" cite="reading2" at="p. 38 (bottom; written there for two variables s₁ and s₂)" />

Here $\langle k_+k_-\rangle = \langle n_1^2\rangle - \langle n_2^2\rangle = 0$, because the two dice have the same distribution, and $\langle k_-\rangle = \langle n_1\rangle - \langle n_2\rangle = 0$. The correlation is $0 - \langle k_+\rangle \times 0 = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Uncorrelated is not independent">

Part (a) showed that $k_+$ and $k_-$ are not independent, yet their correlation vanishes. Independence makes every average of a product split; a zero correlation tests only one such average. Here, for example, $\langle k_+^2k_-^2\rangle$ does not split: a large $|k_-|$ needs one die high and the other low, so it forces $k_+$ towards the middle. (The lesson notes that for two variables that are each 0 or 1, a zero correlation does prove independence; $k_+$ and $k_-$ take many values.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The thermal fluctuation of a pendulum" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

A pendulum is a mass $M$ on a string of length $L$. It hangs in air at the temperature $T$, and the air molecules that hit it keep it in thermal equilibrium with the air. We take $x$ to be the horizontal displacement of the mass from the point straight below the support, in one direction (our reading: the mass can also move in the perpendicular horizontal direction, independently and with the same fluctuation), and $g$ the acceleration of gravity (our symbol).

<Part label="a" points={8}>

Find the thermal fluctuation of the position of the mass,

<Formula label="Fluctuation of the position" tex="\Delta x = \sqrt{\langle(x - \bar x)^2\rangle}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1" />

where $\bar x$ is the average of $x$. Treat the motion classically, and take the swings to be small (our assumptions).

<Hint>

By symmetry $\bar x = 0$. What does it cost, in energy, to hold the mass at a displacement $x$? Find how high it is lifted.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For a small angle the mass rises by about $x^2/2L$. In thermal equilibrium the probability of a displacement $x$ goes as $e^{-\beta A(x)}$, where $A(x)$ is the free energy at that displacement, here the energy it costs.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-dx" label="$\Delta x =$" expr="sqrt(kB*T*L/(M*g))"
  vars={{ kB: [0.5, 1.5], T: [0.5, 2], L: [0.5, 2], M: [0.5, 2], g: [9, 10] }}
  names={{ kB: "k_B", T: "T", L: "L", M: "M", g: "g" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2*kB*T*L/(M*g))", hint: "That sets the average potential energy equal to k_BT. Equipartition gives k_BT/2 to each quadratic term of the energy, and the potential energy here is one quadratic term in x." },
    { expr: "sqrt(kB*T*L/(2*M*g))", hint: "That takes the potential energy as Mgx²/L. The mass rises by L(1 − cos θ) ≈ x²/2L, so the energy is Mgx²/2L." },
    { expr: "sqrt(kB*T/(M*g*L))", hint: "Check the units: k_BT/(MgL) is a pure number, not a length squared. The spring constant of the pendulum is Mg/L, not MgL." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For small swings the pendulum is a harmonic oscillator with the spring constant $Mg/L$. The probability of a displacement is set by the free energy it costs; for a quadratic cost the distribution is a Gaussian, and its width is the fluctuation.

<Step title="The energy of a displacement">

With $\theta$ the angle of the string, $x = L\sin\theta$ and the mass is lifted by $L(1 - \cos\theta)$. For a small angle, $\theta \approx x/L$ and $1 - \cos\theta \approx \theta^2/2$, so the cost is

<Formula label="Potential energy of the displaced pendulum" tex="U(x) = MgL(1 - \cos\theta) \approx \frac{Mg}{2L}\,x^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The probability of a displacement">

At the temperature $T$, the probability of a state of the system goes as $e^{-\beta A}$:

<Formula label="Thermodynamic functions as probabilities" tex="P \propto e^{-\beta A}" cite="reading3" at="p. 42" />

The kinetic energy of the mass does not depend on $x$, so, up to a constant, $A(x) = U(x)$, and

<Formula label="Distribution of the displacement" tex="P(x) \propto e^{-\beta Mgx^2/2L} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bar x = 0, \qquad \langle x^2\rangle = \frac{k_BT}{Mg/L} = \frac{k_BTL}{Mg}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

since a Gaussian $e^{-x^2/2\langle x^2\rangle}$ has the variance $\langle x^2\rangle$; this is the step the notes take on the same page for the number of particles in a region, whose variance is $k_BT$ divided by the second derivative of the free energy, with $x$ in place of the number.

</Step>

<Step title="The fluctuation">

<Formula label="Thermal fluctuation of a pendulum" tex="\Delta x = \sqrt{\frac{k_BTL}{Mg}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Units: J m/(kg m s⁻²) = m², so $\Delta x$ is a length. Equipartition gives the same: the average of the one quadratic term $\frac12(Mg/L)x^2$ is $\frac12k_BT$. A heavier mass or a stronger gravity holds the pendulum more tightly and shrinks $\Delta x$; a longer string makes the restoring force weaker and lets it wander further.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Evaluate $\Delta x$ for $M = 1$ g, $L = 10$ cm and $T = 300$ K, with $g = 9.81$ m/s² and $k_B = 1.381 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K (our values). Give it in nanometres (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

Work in SI units: $M = 10^{-3}$ kg and $L = 0.1$ m.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-num" label="$\Delta x$ in nm:" value="0.206" tolerance="2%" calc="p3-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into part (a), in SI units.

<Step title="The numbers">

$k_BTL = 4.143 \times 10^{-21}$ J $\times\ 0.1$ m $= 4.143 \times 10^{-22}$ J m and $Mg = 9.81 \times 10^{-3}$ N, so $\langle x^2\rangle = 4.22 \times 10^{-20}$ m² and $\Delta x = 2.06 \times 10^{-10}$ m $= 0.206$ nm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

That is about the size of an atom: the thermal jiggle of an everyday pendulum is far too small to see, which is why a pendulum clock is not disturbed by it. It is not always negligible, though: in the most sensitive balances and pendulums of precision experiments, this thermal noise can be what limits the measurement.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A biased random walk (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 4">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on a subject of Chapter V of the notes: the binomial distribution and the central limit theorem, applied to a random walk.

A particle on a line makes $n$ steps of length $\ell$. Each step goes to the right with the probability $p$ and to the left with the probability $1 - p$, independently of all the others (our own model, stated by us). It starts at $X = 0$. Let $k$ be the number of steps to the right, so that its final position is $X = (2k - n)\ell$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the average final position $\langle X\rangle$.

<Hint>

$k$ is a sum of $n$ independent variables, each 1 (a step to the right) or 0: it has the binomial distribution. What is $\langle k\rangle$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-mean" label="$\langle X\rangle =$" expr="n*l*(2*p - 1)"
  vars={{ n: [10, 100], l: [0.5, 2], p: [0.6, 0.9] }}
  names={{ n: "n", l: "\\ell", p: "p" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "n*l*p", hint: "That counts only the steps to the right. Each of the n(1 − p) steps to the left, on average, takes the particle back by ℓ." },
    { expr: "n*l*(1 - 2*p)", hint: "The sign is reversed. With p > 1/2 most steps go to the right, so the average position is to the right, X > 0." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $X$ is a linear function of $k$, and the average of $k$ is that of the binomial distribution.

<Step title="The average">

The binomial distribution has the average $\langle k\rangle = np$:

<Formula label="Mean and second moment of the binomial distribution" tex="\langle k\rangle = np, \qquad \langle k^2\rangle = n^2p^2 + np(1 - p)" cite="reading2" at="p. 39, point ③" />

so $\langle X\rangle = (2\langle k\rangle - n)\ell$:

<Formula label="Average position of the walk" tex="\langle X\rangle = n\ell\,(2p - 1)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own problem and solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $p = \frac12$ the walk has no drift and $\langle X\rangle = 0$; for $p = 1$ every step goes right and $X = n\ell$. Each step contributes $p\ell - (1 - p)\ell = (2p - 1)\ell$ on average, and there are $n$ of them.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find the variance of the final position, $\langle X^2\rangle - \langle X\rangle^2$.

<Hint>

$X - \langle X\rangle = 2\ell\,(k - \langle k\rangle)$. Use the variance of $k$ from the notes.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-var" label="$\langle X^2\rangle - \langle X\rangle^2 =$" expr="4*n*l^2*p*(1 - p)"
  vars={{ n: [10, 100], l: [0.5, 2], p: [0.1, 0.4] }}
  names={{ n: "n", l: "\\ell", p: "p" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "n*l^2*p*(1 - p)", hint: "That is ℓ² times the variance of k. But X changes by 2ℓ when one step to the left becomes one to the right, so the variance of X is (2ℓ)² times that of k." },
    { expr: "n*l^2", hint: "That is the variance of an unbiased walk, p = 1/2. For p ≠ 1/2 each step is more predictable, and its variance 4ℓ²p(1 − p) is less than ℓ²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The variance of $k$ is that of the binomial distribution, and a linear change of variable multiplies a variance by the square of the factor.

<Step title="The variance">

From the moments above, $\langle k^2\rangle - \langle k\rangle^2 = np(1 - p)$. With $X - \langle X\rangle = 2\ell(k - \langle k\rangle)$:

<Formula label="Variance of the walk" tex="\langle X^2\rangle - \langle X\rangle^2 = 4\ell^2\,np(1 - p)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own problem and solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

For $p = \frac12$ this is $n\ell^2$: each step adds $\pm\ell$ with equal odds, and the variances of $n$ independent steps add. For $p = 0$ or 1 the walk is certain and the variance vanishes. The spread $\Delta X = 2\ell\sqrt{np(1 - p)}$ grows only like $\sqrt n$, while the drift $\langle X\rangle$ grows like $n$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Take $n = 400$ steps and $p = 0.55$. Use the central limit theorem: treat $k$ as a Gaussian with the mean and variance of the binomial distribution. Find the probability that the particle ends to the left of its start, $X < 0$. Since $k$ is a whole number, count "$k$ at most 199" as $k < 199.5$, half a step beyond the last allowed value, as the lesson does. Give it to three significant figures (tolerance 2%).

<Hint>

$X < 0$ means $k < n/2 = 200$, so $k \le 199$. Find the mean $\langle k\rangle$ and the standard deviation $\Delta k$, and how many standard deviations below the mean 199.5 lies.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The probability that a Gaussian variable lies more than $z$ standard deviations below its mean is $\frac12\,\text{erfc}(z/\sqrt2)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-left" label="$P(X < 0)$:" value="0.0197" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-left" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The central limit theorem makes the distribution of $k$, a sum of 400 independent variables, nearly Gaussian; the answer is the area of its tail below 199.5.

<Step title="The Gaussian">

The central limit theorem gives $k$ the distribution

<Formula label="Gaussian distribution of k" tex="P(k) \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi np(1 - p)}}\,e^{-(k - np)^2/2np(1 - p)}" cite="reading2" at="p. 40, point ④ (written there for the mean of the variables; applied to their sum k by us)" />

with the mean $\langle k\rangle = np = 220$ and the variance $np(1 - p) = 99.0$, so $\Delta k = 9.95$.

</Step>

<Step title="The tail">

The point $k = 199.5$ lies $z = (220 - 199.5)/9.95 = 2.06$ standard deviations below the mean. The Gaussian tail beyond it is $\frac12\,\text{erfc}(2.06/\sqrt2) = 0.0197$: about one walk in 51 ends to the left of its start, although its average position is $\langle X\rangle = 400 \times 0.10\,\ell = 40\ell$ to the right.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The exact binomial probability of $k \le 199$, summed by computer, is 0.0199: the Gaussian is within 1%. Without the half step, $z = 2.01$ and the tail is 0.0222, 12% above the exact value: for a whole-number variable the half step matters.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Make the walk four times longer, $n = 1600$, with the same $p = 0.55$. What becomes of the probability of ending to the left of the start?

<Answer id="p4-longer" label="For n = 1600 it is" choices={["about the same, 0.02, since the bias p of each step has not changed", "about 0.005, four times smaller, since the walk is four times longer", "about 3 × 10⁻⁵: the drift grows like n, the spread only like √n", "larger, about 0.08, since a longer walk spreads out further"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Compare how the distance to $X = 0$ and the width of the distribution grow with $n$.

<Step title="The new tail">

Now $\langle k\rangle = 880$ and $\Delta k = \sqrt{1600 \times 0.55 \times 0.45} = 19.9$. The point $k = 799.5$ lies $z = 80.5/19.9 = 4.05$ standard deviations below the mean, and $\frac12\,\text{erfc}(4.05/\sqrt2) = 2.6 \times 10^{-5}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Why it falls so fast">

The distance from the mean to $X = 0$ grows like $n$ and the width like $\sqrt n$, so $z$ grows like $\sqrt n$: four times the steps, twice the $z$. A Gaussian tail falls like $e^{-z^2/2}$, so doubling $z$ cuts the probability by far more than a factor 4. This is the $1/\sqrt n$ of the notes at work: a long enough walk with any bias ends on the side of its drift almost for certain, as a large system almost always shows its average.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
