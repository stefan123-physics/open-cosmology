This set practises the condensates of Chapter VII of Wen's notes: the [ideal Bose gas and its condensate](/courses/8-08/ideal-bose-gas-condensation), the [condensate of interacting bosons as one field obeying the Gross-Pitaevskii equation](/courses/8-08/interacting-bosons-gross-pitaevskii), and [superfluid flow, vortices and the critical velocity](/courses/8-08/superfluidity-vortices). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 8 (dated March 30, 2005), in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its dates are left out. MIT's Problem 1 is stated in full on the sheet and is given here in our words, with three changes, each marked where it stands. In part (a) we define the size of the cloud as its root-mean-square radius. In part (c) MIT asks the reader to read the size of a condensate off a figure of measured density profiles in a textbook (K. Huang, *Statistical Mechanics*) and to use an equation from the same book; neither is used here. We give our own illustrative radius in place of the figure (MIT's maximum density is kept) and state the relation between the interaction strength and the scattering length ourselves. Our extra boxes (the radius formulas and the number of atoms) are marked too. MIT's Problem 2 points to a problem in the same textbook by number only, and the textbook is not used here: **Problem 2 is our own problem**, on a uniform condensate of repelling bosons (Chapter VII, its superfluidity part): its critical velocity, the excitations that make a faster flow unstable and the core of a vortex.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `(3*g*N/(m*w0))^(1/4)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `r0` stands for $r_0$, `w0` for $\omega_0$, `hbar` for $\hbar$, `mu` for $\mu$, `pi` for $\pi$ and `sqrt` is the square root; the other letters (`N`, `g`, `m`) are typed as they are.

MIT's sheet gives no points. The points are ours: 20 for Problem 1 and 15 for Problem 2, 35 points in all; every split between parts is ours too. Parts that ask you to show something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of MIT's sheet and the lecture notes. $\hbar$ is Planck's constant divided by $2\pi$, $k_B$ Boltzmann's constant, $m$ the mass of one atom, $N$ the number of atoms, $\psi$ the shared wavefunction of the condensate, normalised so that $|\psi|^2$ is the density, and $\mu$ the chemical potential. The strength $g$ of the contact repulsion on MIT's sheet is the $v_0$ of the notes (reading 1, p. 67), called $U_0$ on p. 70. In Problem 1, $\omega_0$ is the angular frequency of the trap, $r_0$ its characteristic length, $R$ the radius of the condensate and $a$ the scattering length of two sodium atoms. In Problem 2, $n$ is the density of the uniform condensate, $v_c$ its critical velocity, $v_s$ the speed of a flow and $r_c$ a distance from the axis of a vortex.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Sodium atoms condensed in a harmonic trap" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

A gas of bosonic sodium atoms, each of mass $m$, is held in a harmonic trap, a potential well that grows as the square of the distance $r = |\vec r|$ from its centre:

<Formula label="The trap and its length" tex="U(\vec r) = \frac12 m\omega_0^2|\vec r|^2, \qquad r_0 = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{m\omega_0}} = 5 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{cm}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={5}>

First leave out the interaction between the atoms. Find the size of the condensate at $T = 0$, taken as its root-mean-square radius $\sqrt{\langle r^2\rangle}$ (our definition of the size), with $\langle r^2\rangle = \frac1N\int r^2|\psi|^2\,d^3r$. How does it depend on the number of atoms $N$?

<Hint>

At $T = 0$ a gas of bosons that do not interact puts every atom into the lowest single-particle state. Which state is that for this potential?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The ground state of a three-dimensional harmonic oscillator is $\psi \propto e^{-r^2/2r_0^2}$ (standard quantum mechanics, stated by us). The density $|\psi|^2$ is then a Gaussian in each of $x$, $y$ and $z$ separately.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-rms" label="$\sqrt{\langle r^2\rangle} =$" expr="sqrt(3/2)*r0"
  vars={{ r0: [0.5, 2], N: [1, 100] }}
  names={{ r0: "r_0", N: "N" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(3)*r0", hint: "That averages r² with ψ itself as the weight. The density is |ψ|², whose exponent −r²/r₀² is twice as steep, so each axis gives ⟨x²⟩ = r₀²/2." },
    { expr: "r0/sqrt(2)", hint: "That is the spread along one axis, √⟨x²⟩. In three dimensions r² = x² + y² + z², so three such terms add." },
    { expr: "sqrt(3/2)*r0*N^(1/3)", hint: "That makes the cloud grow like a gas at a fixed density. Without interactions every atom sits in the same oscillator ground state, whose shape does not depend on how many atoms share it." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-ideal-n" label="The size of the ideal condensate" choices={["grows as N^(1/3), as for a gas at a fixed density", "shrinks as N grows, since the trap squeezes more atoms", "does not depend on N, for any number of atoms", "grows as N^(1/2), as each atom adds to the cloud's area"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Without interactions the condensate is $N$ copies of one atom's ground state, so its shape is that of the ground state.

<Step title="The ground state">

With $U = \frac12m\omega_0^2r^2$ the lowest state is $\psi = \sqrt N\,(\pi r_0^2)^{-3/4}e^{-r^2/2r_0^2}$, normalised to $N$ as in the notes (reading 1, p. 62). The density is

<Formula label="Density of the ideal condensate in the trap" tex="|\psi(\vec r)|^2 = \frac{N}{\pi^{3/2}r_0^3}\,e^{-r^2/r_0^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The size">

Along each axis the density is a Gaussian $e^{-x^2/r_0^2}$, with $\langle x^2\rangle = r_0^2/2$. So

<Formula label="Size of the ideal condensate" tex="\langle r^2\rangle = \langle x^2\rangle + \langle y^2\rangle + \langle z^2\rangle = \frac32 r_0^2, \qquad \sqrt{\langle r^2\rangle} = \sqrt{\tfrac32}\,r_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

It does not depend on $N$: adding atoms only raises the density, as $N$, at every point. For MIT's $r_0 = 50$ μm the size is $61.2$ μm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The size is fixed by the only length in the problem, $r_0$, which contains $\hbar$, $m$ and $\omega_0$ but nothing about the number of atoms: without interactions nothing can make $N$ appear in a length. MIT's $r_0$ means a weak trap, $\omega_0 = \hbar/(mr_0^2) = 1.10$ s⁻¹ for sodium.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

Now the atoms repel each other. At $T = 0$ the shape of the condensate is set by the Gross-Pitaevskii equation (MIT's sheet writes the Laplacian $\nabla^2$ with another symbol):

<Formula label="Gross-Pitaevskii equation for the trapped condensate" tex="\left[-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + \left(U(\vec r) - \mu\right) + g|\psi(\vec r)|^2\right]\psi(\vec r) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

the ground-state condition of the notes with a contact repulsion (reading 1, p. 67). In the **Thomas–Fermi approximation** the wavefunction is taken to be smooth, and the term with $\nabla^2$ is dropped:

<Formula label="Thomas–Fermi approximation" tex="\left[\left(U(\vec r) - \mu\right) + g|\psi(\vec r)|^2\right]\psi(\vec r) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1" />

Find the radius $R$ of the condensate in this approximation, in terms of $N$, $g$, $m$ and $\omega_0$ (our box), and say how it depends on the number of atoms.

<Hint>

At each point either $\psi = 0$ or the bracket vanishes. Solve the bracket for $|\psi|^2$; where that would come out negative, there are no atoms.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The edge $R$ is where $U(R) = \mu$. Then fix $\mu$, and so $R$, by the number of atoms: $N = \int|\psi|^2\,d^3r$, an integral over a ball of radius $R$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-radius" label="$R =$" expr="(15*g*N/(4*pi*m*w0^2))^(1/5)"
  vars={{ N: [1, 100], g: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], w0: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ N: "N", g: "g", m: "m", w0: "\\omega_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(3*g*N/(2*pi*m*w0^2))^(1/5)", hint: "That fills the ball of radius R with the peak density μ/g everywhere. In the Thomas–Fermi profile the density falls to zero at the edge, so the same number of atoms needs a larger ball." },
    { expr: "(15*g*N/(8*pi*m*w0^2))^(1/5)", hint: "That uses the trap U = mω₀²r², without the factor ½ of MIT's potential." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-tf-n" label="The radius of the interacting condensate" choices={["grows as N^(1/5), slowly as atoms are added", "grows as N^(1/3), as for a gas at a fixed density", "does not depend on N", "grows as N^(1/2), as the repulsion adds up in pairs"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Without the kinetic term, the density at each point is whatever makes the energy of one more atom, $U + g|\psi|^2$, equal to $\mu$: the cloud fills the trap like a liquid up to the level $\mu$.

<Step title="The density profile">

Where $\psi \neq 0$ the bracket vanishes:

<Formula label="Thomas–Fermi density" tex="|\psi(\vec r)|^2 = \frac{\mu - U(\vec r)}{g} = \frac{\mu}{g}\left(1 - \frac{r^2}{R^2}\right), \qquad \mu = \frac12m\omega_0^2R^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

for $r < R$, and $|\psi|^2 = 0$ outside, where $\mu - U$ is negative. The profile is an inverted parabola with its peak density $n_0 = \mu/g$ at the centre.

</Step>

<Step title="The number of atoms">

<Formula label="Number of atoms in the Thomas–Fermi profile" tex="N = \int_0^R \frac{\mu}{g}\left(1 - \frac{r^2}{R^2}\right)4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{8\pi}{15}\,\frac{\mu}{g}\,R^3 = \frac{4\pi}{15}\,\frac{m\omega_0^2}{g}\,R^5" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The radius">

<Formula label="Thomas–Fermi radius" tex="R = \left(\frac{15\,gN}{4\pi m\omega_0^2}\right)^{1/5} \propto N^{1/5}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The cloud grows with the number of atoms, but slowly: thirty-two times as many atoms make it only twice as wide, because the extra atoms climb the steep walls of the trap.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

Units: $gN$ is an energy times a volume, $m\omega_0^2$ an energy over a length squared, so their ratio is a length to the fifth power. With no repulsion, $g \to 0$, the radius goes to zero: the approximation then fails, since the kinetic term it dropped is what gives the ideal condensate its size $r_0$ of part (a). The approximation holds when $R$ is several times $r_0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={7}>

Now use a density profile with our illustrative numbers in place of the textbook's measured one. (Our numbers, in place of the textbook's figure.) The density of a sodium condensate near $T = 0$ is largest at the centre, $10^{11}$ cm⁻³ (MIT's value), and falls to zero at $R = 0.021$ cm from the centre (our number). Take the mass of a sodium atom as $m = 22.99$ u, with $1$ u $= 1.66054 \times 10^{-27}$ kg, and $r_0$ as in MIT's statement. For slow collisions the contact strength and the scattering length $a$ are related by $g = 4\pi\hbar^2a/m$ (the standard result for low-energy scattering, stated by us; it is not in the notes). Find $a$ in nanometres (tolerance 1%). (Our addition.) Find also the number of atoms in this condensate, in millions (tolerance 1%).

<Hint>

From part (b), the peak density is $n_0 = \mu/g$ and the edge sits where $\mu = \frac12m\omega_0^2R^2$. Together they give $g$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

When you put $g$ into $a = mg/(4\pi\hbar^2)$, write $m^2\omega_0^2 = \hbar^2/r_0^4$: the mass and $\hbar$ then cancel, and only $R$, $n_0$ and $r_0$ remain.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$a$ in nm:" value="2.81" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-a" />

<Answer id="p1-atoms" scale="1e6" label="$N$ in millions:" value="1.55" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-atoms" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The measured edge gives $\mu$, the measured peak gives $\mu/g$, and so the two give $g$ and with it $a$.

<Step title="The contact strength">

From $n_0 = \mu/g$ and $\mu = \frac12m\omega_0^2R^2$, $g = m\omega_0^2R^2/(2n_0)$, and so

<Formula label="Scattering length from the profile" tex="a = \frac{mg}{4\pi\hbar^2} = \frac{m^2\omega_0^2R^2}{8\pi\hbar^2n_0} = \frac{R^2}{8\pi n_0r_0^4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

In SI units, $R = 2.1 \times 10^{-4}$ m, $n_0 = 10^{17}$ m⁻³ and $r_0 = 5 \times 10^{-5}$ m, so $r_0^4 = 6.25 \times 10^{-18}$ m⁴ and $8\pi n_0r_0^4 = 15.71$ m. Then $a = 4.41 \times 10^{-8}/15.71$ m $= 2.81 \times 10^{-9}$ m $= 2.81$ nm. The mass of the atom drops out; it only enters $g = 1.03 \times 10^{-50}$ J m³.

</Step>

<Step title="The number of atoms">

From part (b), $N = \frac{8\pi}{15}n_0R^3 = 1.676 \times 10^{17} \times 9.26 \times 10^{-12} = 1.55 \times 10^6$ atoms, about one and a half million.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

The result is close to the scattering length of sodium quoted in the [lesson on the Gross-Pitaevskii equation](/courses/8-08/interacting-bosons-gross-pitaevskii), about 2.75 nm (we chose our illustrative radius so that it would be). The approximation of part (b) holds: $R/r_0 = 4.2$, so the cloud is four times wider than the ideal condensate, and the same atoms without interactions would reach a peak density of $2.2 \times 10^{12}$ cm⁻³, 22 times the measured one. The chemical potential is $\mu/k_B = 74$ pK, tiny because MIT's trap is so weak.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A uniform condensate: critical velocity, unstable flow and vortex core (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here. The problem below is our own, on the superfluidity part of Chapter VII of the notes.

A condensate of bosons of mass $m$ fills a large container with no outside potential, $U = 0$. The bosons repel with the contact strength $g$, and the condensate is uniform, with density $n$ and chemical potential $\mu = gn$ (reading 1, p. 68). Its small oscillations have the frequencies

<Formula label="Excitations of the uniform condensate" tex="\omega_k^2 = \left(\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} + 2\mu\right)\frac{k^2}{2m}" cite="reading1" at="p. 69 (boxed)" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

A flowing superfluid stays free of friction below the critical velocity $v_c = \min_k \omega_k/|k|$ (reading 2, p. 72, where it is given as a sketch). Find $v_c$ for this condensate.

<Hint>

Write $\omega_k/|k|$ as the square root of a function of $k^2$, and ask where that function is smallest.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-vc" label="$v_c =$" expr="sqrt(mu/m)"
  vars={{ mu: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu: "\\mu", m: "m" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(2*mu/m)", hint: "That is the speed of a free particle with kinetic energy μ, from ½mv² = μ. The ratio ω_k/|k| has its smallest value at k → 0, where only the term 2μ·k²/2m is left under the root." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "That is the critical velocity of free bosons, whose ω_k/|k| = ħ|k|/2m falls to zero at small k. The repulsion adds the term with μ, which keeps ω_k/|k| above a positive value." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The ratio $\omega_k/|k|$ is the slope of the line from the origin to a point of the excitation curve; the critical velocity is the smallest such slope.

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Phase velocity of the excitations" tex="\frac{\omega_k^2}{k^2} = \frac{\mu}{m} + \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{4m^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

grows with $k^2$, so it is smallest at $k \to 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The critical velocity">

$v_c = \sqrt{\mu/m} = \sqrt{gn/m}$: the speed of sound of the notes (reading 1, p. 69). A flow slower than sound cannot create excitations, so it runs without friction.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

As the repulsion goes to zero, $\mu \to 0$ and $v_c \to 0$: free bosons are not a superfluid, as the notes stress (reading 2, p. 72).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Now let the condensate flow with the speed $v_s$ along $x$. On the flowing condensate an excitation with wave number $k$ along the flow ($k < 0$ against it) has the frequency $\omega_k + v_sk$ (reading 2, p. 72, where the notes write it for the sound waves; here it is used with the full $\omega_k$ above). Take $v_s > v_c$. Excitations with a negative frequency then exist, and the flow is unstable. Find the largest size $k_{\text{max}}$ of their wave numbers: those with $-k_{\text{max}} < k < 0$ have a negative frequency. Give $k_{\text{max}}$ in terms of $\hbar$, $m$, $\mu$ and $v_s$.

<Hint>

Only $k < 0$ can give a negative frequency. Write the condition $\omega_k + v_sk < 0$ as $\omega_k/|k| < v_s$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Square both sides and use the ratio $\omega_k^2/k^2$ you found in part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-kmax" label="$k_{\text{max}} =$" expr="2*m*sqrt(vs^2 - mu/m)/hbar"
  vars={{ m: [0.5, 2], vs: [3, 5], mu: [0.5, 2], hbar: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ m: "m", vs: "v_s", mu: "\\mu", hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*m*(vs - sqrt(mu/m))/hbar", hint: "That subtracts the speeds. The condition is v_s² > μ/m + ħ²k²/4m², a statement about squares, so it is the squares that are subtracted under one root." },
    { expr: "m*sqrt(vs^2 - mu/m)/hbar", hint: "That takes the free-particle part of ω_k/|k| as ħ|k|/m. With ε_k = ħ²k²/2m it is ħ|k|/2m, so its square is ħ²k²/4m²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** An excitation moving against a fast enough flow is dragged to negative energy; the slow ones (small $|k|$) are dragged furthest below the line, because $\omega_k/|k|$ is smallest at small $|k|$.

<Step title="The condition">

For $k < 0$, $\omega_k + v_sk = |k|\,(\omega_k/|k| - v_s)$, which is negative when $\omega_k^2/k^2 < v_s^2$. With part (a),

<Formula label="Band of unstable excitations" tex="\frac{\mu}{m} + \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{4m^2} < v_s^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |k| < k_{\text{max}} = \frac{2m}{\hbar}\sqrt{v_s^2 - \frac{\mu}{m}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

At $v_s = v_c = \sqrt{\mu/m}$ the band closes, $k_{\text{max}} = 0$: just below the critical velocity no excitation has a negative frequency, as part (a) says. For a fast flow, $v_s \gg v_c$, $k_{\text{max}} \approx 2mv_s/\hbar$: free bosons with $\hbar|k| < 2mv_s$, the same as for a gas without interactions, where every such state lowers the energy of the flow.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Around a vortex, $\psi = f(r)\,\psi_0e^{i\varphi}$, the fluid circles the core with speed $\hbar/(mr)$ at the distance $r$ (reading 2, p. 71). At what distance $r_c$ would this speed equal the critical velocity of part (a)? Give $r_c$ in terms of $\hbar$, $m$ and $\mu$.

<Answer id="p2-rc" label="$r_c =$" expr="hbar/sqrt(m*mu)"
  vars={{ hbar: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], mu: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar", m: "m", mu: "\\mu" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*pi*hbar/sqrt(m*mu)", hint: "That uses h in place of ħ. The circulation around the vortex is h/m, but the speed at the distance r is that circulation divided by the circumference 2πr." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Close to a vortex the flow is fast; inside some distance it would be faster than the critical velocity.

<Step title="The distance">

Setting $\hbar/(mr_c) = \sqrt{\mu/m}$ gives $r_c = \hbar/\sqrt{m\mu}$.

</Step>

<Step title="What it means">

Inside this radius the flow would be faster than the critical velocity, so by part (b) the condensate there cannot keep its bulk density: it falls to zero at the centre, where the phase is undefined. This estimates the core of a vortex, which the notes sketch but do not size (reading 2, p. 71): a radius of about $r_c$, of the same order as the estimate $\hbar/\sqrt{2m\mu}$ in the [superfluidity lesson](/courses/8-08/superfluidity-vortices).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

(Our values.) Take a sodium condensate, $m = 22.99$ u, with $\mu/k_B = 100$ nK. Give its critical velocity in millimetres per second and the radius $r_c$ of part (c) in micrometres. (Our addition.) For a flow at $v_s = 1.5\,v_c$, give also $k_{\text{max}}$ of part (b) in units of $10^6$ m⁻¹ (tolerance 1% each).

<Answer id="p2-vc-num" label="$v_c$ in mm/s:" value="6.01" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-vc-num" />

<Answer id="p2-rc-num" label="$r_c$ in μm:" value="0.459" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-rc-num" />

<Answer id="p2-kmax-num" scale="1e6" label="$k_{\text{max}}$ in $10^6$ m⁻¹:" value="4.87" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-kmax-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into parts (a), (b) and (c).

<Step title="The numbers">

$m = 22.99 \times 1.66054 \times 10^{-27}$ kg $= 3.818 \times 10^{-26}$ kg and $\mu = 100 \times 10^{-9} \times 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J $= 1.381 \times 10^{-30}$ J. Then $v_c = \sqrt{\mu/m} = \sqrt{3.617 \times 10^{-5}}$ m/s $= 6.01$ mm/s, and $r_c = \hbar/\sqrt{m\mu} = 1.0546 \times 10^{-34}/2.296 \times 10^{-28}$ m $= 0.459$ μm. At $v_s = 1.5\,v_c$, $v_s^2 - \mu/m = 1.25\,\mu/m$, so $k_{\text{max}} = 2\sqrt{1.25}\,\sqrt{m\mu}/\hbar = 4.87 \times 10^6$ m⁻¹, a wavelength $2\pi/k_{\text{max}}$ of $1.29$ μm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check">

A condensate of atoms is a superfluid only for flows slower than a few millimetres per second, and its vortex cores have a radius of about half a micrometre.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
