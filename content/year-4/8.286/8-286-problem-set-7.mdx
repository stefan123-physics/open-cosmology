## What you need from the lessons

Three results from the lessons carry the whole set.

**Counting particles.** Black-body radiation that contains several kinds of particles has the energy density

<Formula label="Energy density of black-body radiation" tex="u = g\,\frac{\pi^2}{30}\,\frac{(kT)^4}{(\hbar c)^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

The number $g$ counts the particles. Photons have $g = 2$, for their two polarisations. A kind of fermion adds

<Formula label="The factor g of a fermion" tex="g = \frac78 \times (\text{number of species}) \times (\text{particle and antiparticle: 1 or 2}) \times (\text{number of spin states})" cite="reading2" at="pp. 8–10 (board)" />

A particle counts only while it acts as radiation, which means while $kT$ is well above its rest energy $mc^2$.

**Entropy.** The entropy density of the same radiation is

<Formula label="Entropy density of black-body radiation" tex="s = g\,\frac{2\pi^2}{45}\,\frac{k^4\,T^3}{(\hbar c)^3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 25 (board)" />

with the same $g$. The early universe stays very close to thermal equilibrium, so its entropy is conserved. This holds even while a kind of particle is leaving the thermal mix as $kT$ falls below its rest energy. (This is called freezing out, and it is exactly when the energy is hard to follow.) A region that expands with the universe has a volume that grows as $a^3$, where $a(t)$ is the scale factor. Its entropy stays the same, so

<Formula label="Conserved entropy" tex="s\,a^3 = \text{constant} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g\,(aT)^3 = \text{constant}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (the second form is our own)" />

for any group of particles that share one temperature $T$ and exchange no heat with the rest. While $g$ stays the same, this is the familiar $T \propto 1/a$. When $g$ drops, $aT$ must rise.

**The redshift.** Light that leaves at time $t_1$ with frequency $\nu_1$ arrives at time $t_2$ with a lower frequency: the wavelength is stretched by the factor $a(t_2)/a(t_1)$, as the lesson on the [cosmological redshift](/courses/8-286/cosmological-redshift) shows. Problem 3 uses this.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Entropy and the temperature of the background neutrinos" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

When $kT$ falls below the rest energy of the electron, $m_ec^2 = 0.511$ MeV, the electron–positron pairs disappear from the thermal mix. By then the weak interactions are so slow that the neutrinos have, to a good approximation, decoupled: they no longer exchange energy with anything. So take it that all the energy and all the entropy of the vanishing pairs go to the photons, and none to the neutrinos.

Before the pairs disappear, the photons, the pairs and the neutrinos all have the same temperature $T$. Afterwards the photons have a temperature $T_\gamma$ and the neutrinos a temperature $T_\nu$.

MIT's Problem 1 is part (b). Parts (a) and (c) are steps of our own: one prepares it, the other puts a number on its result.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

The pairs give their entropy to the photons only. So treat the photons and the electron–positron pairs together as one gas. What is $g$ for this gas before the pairs disappear, while $kT$ is still well above $m_ec^2$? Give a decimal number.

<Hint>

Electrons are fermions with two spin states, and the positron is a different particle from the electron.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Work out $g$ for the pairs with the rule for fermions in "What you need from the lessons", and add the photons' $g$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$g$ of photons and pairs together $=$" value="5.5" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1-g-photons-pairs" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count the photons as bosons and the pairs as fermions, and add.

<Step title="Count">

The electron is one species, it has an antiparticle, and it has two spin states:

<Formula label="Photons and electron–positron pairs" tex="g = 2 + \frac78 \times 1 \times 2 \times 2 = 2 + \frac72 = \frac{11}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson on [counting particle species](/courses/8-286/neutrinos-and-particle-counting) has $g = 10\frac34$ for photons, neutrinos and pairs together. Taking away the neutrinos' $\frac{21}{4}$ leaves $\frac{11}{2}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

Show that while the pairs disappear, $aT_\gamma$ grows by the factor $(11/4)^{1/3}$, and $aT_\nu$ stays the same.

<Hint>

Entropy is conserved for the photons and pairs together, and separately for the neutrinos. Why separately?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Write $g\,(aT)^3$ for the gas of photons and pairs at a time well before the pairs disappear and at a time well after. Only $g$ and $aT$ can differ between the two times. Do the same for the neutrinos.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The neutrinos exchange no heat with anything, so their entropy is conserved by itself. The entropy of everything is conserved too, so the entropy of the rest (photons and pairs) is conserved by itself as well. Each group obeys $g\,(aT)^3 = \text{constant}$ with its own $g$.

<Step title="Photons and pairs">

Well before the pairs disappear, this gas has $g = \frac{11}{2}$ and the common temperature $T$. Well afterwards only the photons are left, with $g = 2$ and the temperature $T_\gamma$. The entropy in a region that expands with the universe is the same at both times:

<Formula label="Entropy of photons and pairs" tex="\frac{11}{2}\,(aT)^3_\text{before} = 2\,(aT_\gamma)^3_\text{after}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

Solve for the value afterwards:

<Formula label="The photons are heated" tex="(aT_\gamma)_\text{after} = \left(\frac{11}{4}\right)^{1/3} (aT)_\text{before}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

Before the pairs disappear, $T_\gamma$ is the common temperature $T$. So $aT_\gamma$ grows by the factor $(11/4)^{1/3} = 1.401$.

</Step>

<Step title="Neutrinos">

The neutrinos have $g = \frac{21}{4}$ before and after: none of them appear or disappear. Their entropy is conserved, so

<Formula label="Entropy of the neutrinos" tex="\frac{21}{4}\,(aT_\nu)^3_\text{after} = \frac{21}{4}\,(aT)^3_\text{before} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (aT_\nu)_\text{after} = (aT)_\text{before}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

The product $aT_\nu$ does not change.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The temperature of the photons does not jump upwards. While the pairs disappear the universe keeps expanding, and $aT_\gamma$ rises by 40% in all. The photons only cool more slowly than $1/a$ for a while. The value $\frac{21}{4}$ of the neutrinos drops out, as it must: a gas whose $g$ does not change cools as $1/a$ whatever its $g$ is.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

It follows that after the pairs are gone, the two temperatures have a fixed ratio $T_\nu/T_\gamma$. As far as is known, nothing changes this ratio afterwards, up to the present day. The photons of the cosmic microwave background now have $T_\gamma = 2.725$ K. Find the temperature of the background neutrinos today, in kelvin, to three significant figures.

<Hint>

Divide the result for the neutrinos in part (b) by the result for the photons.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$T_\nu =$" value="1.95" unit="K" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p1-neutrino-temperature" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both products $aT_\nu$ and $aT_\gamma$ start from the same value, and the scale factor is the same for both. Their ratio after the pairs are gone is the ratio of the temperatures.

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Neutrino temperature" tex="\frac{T_\nu}{T_\gamma} = \frac{(aT_\nu)_\text{after}}{(aT_\gamma)_\text{after}} = \left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{1/3} = 0.714" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

From then on both gases cool as $1/a$, so the ratio stays.

</Step>

<Step title="Today">

$T_\nu = 0.714 \times 2.725$ K $= 1.95$ K. With MIT's rounded 2.7 K you would get 1.93 K.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The neutrinos are colder than the photons, as they should be: the photons received the heat of the pairs and the neutrinos did not. The value agrees with the lesson on [counting particle species](/courses/8-286/neutrinos-and-particle-counting). Nobody has detected this neutrino background directly so far.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Freeze-out of muons" points={25} mit="Problem 2">

The muon seems to be a heavier copy of the electron. Its rest energy is 106 MeV, against 0.511 MeV for the electron. The muon $\mu^-$ has the same charge as the electron, and its antiparticle $\mu^+$ has the charge of the positron. Both have the same spin as the electron. Between the electron and the muon in mass, no particle is known.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

What is $g$ for the muons, counting $\mu^+$ and $\mu^-$ together? Give a decimal number.

<Hint>

For this counting a muon is the same as an electron. Is it a boson or a fermion, and how many spin states does it have?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$g$ of the muons $=$" value="3.5" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-g-muons" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The muon is a fermion with spin $\frac12$, so it has two spin states, and its antiparticle is a different particle.

<Step title="Count">

<Formula label="Muons and antimuons" tex="g = \frac78 \times 1 \times 2 \times 2 = \frac72" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is the same as for the electron–positron pairs, as it must be: the mass does not enter $g$. The mass only decides at which temperature the particle stops counting.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

As the universe cools, there is a time when $kT$ is just above 106 MeV. Which particles, besides the muons, does the thermal radiation contain then? What does each of them add to $g$? Give decimal numbers.

<Hint>

A particle is part of the radiation while $kT$ is above its rest energy. Which particles are lighter than the muon? Do not forget the ones without mass, or with almost none.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Neutrinos come in three species. In the lessons each has one spin state and an antiparticle.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b-which" label="Besides the muons, the radiation contains:"
  choices={[
    "photons only",
    "photons and the three kinds of neutrinos (with their antineutrinos)",
    "photons, the three kinds of neutrinos (with their antineutrinos), electrons and positrons",
    "photons, the three kinds of neutrinos (with their antineutrinos), electrons and positrons, protons and neutrons"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Answer id="p2-b-photons" label="$g$ of the photons $=$" value="2" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-g-photons" />

<Answer id="p2-b-neutrinos" label="$g$ of all the neutrinos $=$" value="5.25" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-g-neutrinos" />

<Answer id="p2-b-electrons" label="$g$ of electrons and positrons $=$" value="3.5" tolerance="0.01" calc="p2-g-electrons" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Every particle with a rest energy below $kT$ is present. With no particle between the electron and the muon, those are the particles of the lessons: photons, neutrinos, and electron–positron pairs.

<Step title="Which particles">

Photons have no mass. The neutrinos have so little that they count as massless. The electron's rest energy, 0.511 MeV, is about 200 times below $kT$. Protons and neutrons exist, but their rest energy is close to 1,000 MeV, far above $kT$: they are not radiation, and they are very few compared with the photons.

</Step>

<Step title="What each adds">

<Formula label="The other particles at kT just above 106 MeV" tex="g_\gamma = 2, \qquad g_\nu = \frac78 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = \frac{21}{4}, \qquad g_{e^\pm} = \frac78 \times 1 \times 2 \times 2 = \frac72" cite="reading2" at="pp. 8–10 (board)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Together they give $2 + 5.25 + 3.5 = 10.75$, the $10\frac34$ of the lesson on [counting particle species](/courses/8-286/neutrinos-and-particle-counting) for the time between the muon and the electron thresholds. With the muons, $g = 14.25$.

(A remark of our own: in the real universe other particles a little heavier than the muon, the pions, are also partly present at this temperature. Like MIT's set, this problem leaves them out.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={10}>

As $kT$ falls below 106 MeV, the muons disappear from the thermal radiation. At these temperatures all the other particles of part (b) still interact fast enough to stay in equilibrium with each other, so they share the heat that the muons give off. By what factor does $aT$ grow when the muons disappear? Give a number with three decimals.

<Hint>

This is Problem 1 again, with one difference: which particles receive the entropy of the vanishing particles this time?

</Hint>

<Hint>

All particles share one temperature before and after. Write $g\,(aT)^3$ with the total $g$ before the muons disappear, and with the total $g$ afterwards.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$aT$ grows by the factor" value="1.099" tolerance="0.5%" calc="p2-at-factor" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Entropy is conserved, and this time every particle that is left takes part. So the total $g$ goes into $g\,(aT)^3 = \text{constant}$.

<Step title="The total g before and after">

Before: $g = 10.75 + 3.5 = 14.25 = \frac{57}{4}$. After: $g = 10.75 = \frac{43}{4}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Conserve the entropy">

<Formula label="Entropy before and after the muons disappear" tex="\frac{57}{4}\,(aT)^3_\text{before} = \frac{43}{4}\,(aT)^3_\text{after}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Growth of aT" tex="\frac{(aT)_\text{after}}{(aT)_\text{before}} = \left(\frac{57}{43}\right)^{1/3} = 1.099" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The factor is above 1: the muons heat what is left. It is much closer to 1 than the 1.401 of Problem 1. There the pairs carried $\frac{7}{11}$ of the entropy of the gas they heated. Here the muons carry only $\frac{14}{57}$, about a quarter, and their heat is spread over photons, neutrinos and pairs. The neutrinos are heated along with the rest this time, so the muons leave no difference between the temperatures of neutrinos and photons.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The redshift of the cosmic microwave background" points={25} mit="Problem 3">

The black-body spectrum has a special property: it keeps its form when every photon is redshifted by the same factor. So as the universe expands, photons that no longer interact with anything still have a black-body spectrum, at a temperature that falls. That is why the cosmic microwave background has a black-body spectrum today, although its photons have hardly interacted with matter since decoupling, a few hundred thousand years after the Big Bang. In this problem you show this property.

For photons at temperature $T$, the energy per unit volume in the frequencies between $\nu$ and $\nu + d\nu$ is $u_\nu(\nu, T)\,d\nu$, with the black-body spectrum

<Formula label="The black-body spectrum" tex="u_\nu(\nu, T) = \frac{16\pi^2\hbar\,\nu^3}{c^3}\;\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/kT} - 1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, Eq. (P3.1), p. 2" />

Here $h = 2\pi\hbar$ is Planck's original constant. (MIT's set writes $\rho_\nu$ for this function. We write $u_\nu$, as the lesson on [the spectrum of the cosmic microwave background](/courses/8-286/cmb-spectrum) does, because it is an energy density.)

Let $\tilde u(\nu, t)$ be the spectrum that the photons in the universe really have at time $t$, thermal or not. Suppose that at a first time $t_1$ it is a black-body spectrum with temperature $T_1$:

<Formula label="The spectrum at the first time" tex="\tilde u(\nu, t_1) = u_\nu(\nu, T_1)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, Eq. (P3.2), p. 3" />

After $t_1$ only two things happen to the photons, to a good approximation: they are redshifted, and they are spread over a growing volume. The aim is to show that at any later time $t_2$ the spectrum is again a black-body spectrum,

<Formula label="The claim" tex="\tilde u(\nu, t_2) = u_\nu\big(\nu, T(t_2)\big), \qquad T(t_2) = \frac{a(t_1)}{a(t_2)}\,T_1" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, Eqs. (P3.3) and (P3.4), p. 3" />

with a temperature that falls as $1/a$, as the lessons found from the entropy.

To follow the photons, pick a region with a fixed volume $V_c$ in comoving coordinates. At time $t_1$ its physical volume is $a^3(t_1)\,V_c$. Tag all the photons in this region whose frequency lies between $\nu_1$ and $\nu_1 + d\nu_1$. Each of them has the energy $h\nu_1$, so their number is the energy in this band divided by the energy of one photon:

<Formula label="Number of tagged photons" tex="dN_1 = \frac{\tilde u(\nu_1, t_1)\,a^3(t_1)\,V_c\,d\nu_1}{h\nu_1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, Eq. (P3.5), p. 3" />

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Follow the tagged photons to the time $t_2$. Each has been redshifted, so their frequencies now lie between $\nu_2$ and $\nu_2 + d\nu_2$. Express $\nu_2$ and $d\nu_2$ in terms of $\nu_1$, $d\nu_1$ and the scale factors $a(t_1)$ and $a(t_2)$.

<Hint>

How does the wavelength of a photon change between $t_1$ and $t_2$? What does that do to its frequency?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The band has two edges, $\nu_1$ and $\nu_1 + d\nu_1$. Redshift each edge. The width of the new band is the difference of the two results.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a-nu" label="$\nu_2 =$" expr="nu1*a1/a2"
  vars={{ nu1: [1, 5], a1: [0.2, 1], a2: [1.5, 4] }}
  names={{ nu1: "\\nu_1", a1: "a(t_1)", a2: "a(t_2)" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "nu1*a2/a1", hint: "This frequency is higher than the one the photon started with. The expansion stretches the wavelength, so the frequency falls." },
    { expr: "nu1", hint: "The photon is redshifted on the way: its wavelength grows with the scale factor." },
    { expr: "nu1*(a1/a2)^3", hint: "The cube belongs to volumes. A wavelength is a length, so it grows with one power of the scale factor." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-a-dnu" label="$d\nu_2 =$" expr="dnu1*a1/a2"
  vars={{ dnu1: [0.01, 0.1], a1: [0.2, 1], a2: [1.5, 4] }}
  names={{ dnu1: "d\\nu_1", a1: "a(t_1)", a2: "a(t_2)" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "dnu1", hint: "Both edges of the band are multiplied by the same factor below 1, so they end up closer together." },
    { expr: "dnu1*a2/a1", hint: "This band is wider than the one you started with. Every frequency is multiplied by a factor below 1, and so is the difference of two frequencies." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The wavelength of every photon grows in step with the scale factor, whatever its frequency. So every frequency is multiplied by the same factor, and so is the difference of two frequencies.

<Step title="One photon">

The wavelength grows by $a(t_2)/a(t_1)$, and the frequency is $c$ divided by the wavelength:

<Formula label="The redshifted frequency" tex="\nu_2 = \frac{a(t_1)}{a(t_2)}\,\nu_1" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The band">

The upper edge $\nu_1 + d\nu_1$ is multiplied by the same factor. Subtract the lower edge:

<Formula label="The width of the redshifted band" tex="d\nu_2 = \frac{a(t_1)}{a(t_2)}\,d\nu_1" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $a(t_2) > a(t_1)$ both are smaller than before: the photons are redder, and the band is narrower. The ratio $d\nu/\nu$ is the same at both times. Part (c) uses that.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

At time $t_2$, tag all the photons with a frequency between $\nu_2$ and $\nu_2 + d\nu_2$ that are in the same comoving region of coordinate volume $V_c$. Explain why their number $dN_2$ is, on average, equal to $dN_1$.

<Hint>

Two things could change the number: photons could be made or destroyed, and photons could cross the boundary of the region. Look at each.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The universe is homogeneous. Compare the photons that leave the region through its boundary with those that come in.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The photons at $t_2$ in this band are the photons that were in the band at $t_1$. None are lost or made, and on average as many enter the region as leave it.

<Step title="The same band">

By part (a), a photon is between $\nu_2$ and $\nu_2 + d\nu_2$ at time $t_2$ exactly when it was between $\nu_1$ and $\nu_1 + d\nu_1$ at time $t_1$. The redshift only depends on the two times, so no photon enters or leaves the band on the way.

</Step>

<Step title="No photons are made or lost">

By assumption the photons do not interact after $t_1$. None is absorbed, scattered to another frequency, or emitted. The total number of tagged photons in the universe stays the same.

</Step>

<Step title="The region">

Photons move, so the tagged photons found in the region at $t_2$ are not the same individual photons as at $t_1$: some have left through the boundary, and others have come in. But the universe is homogeneous and isotropic. Every comoving region of the same size holds, on average, the same number of tagged photons at any one time, and as many cross the boundary inwards as outwards. So the average number in the region stays the same: $dN_2 = dN_1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

"On average" matters. The actual number in a region fluctuates a little, as any count of randomly moving particles does. And the comoving region is the right one to use: a region of fixed physical size would lose photons, because the photons spread out with the expansion.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={13}>

At time $t_2$ the number of photons in the band can be written in the same way as at $t_1$:

<Formula label="Number of photons in the band at the later time" tex="dN_2 = \frac{\tilde u(\nu_2, t_2)\,a^3(t_2)\,V_c\,d\nu_2}{h\nu_2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, Eq. (P3.6), p. 4" />

Use $dN_2 = dN_1$ to show that

<Formula label="The spectrum at the later time" tex="\tilde u(\nu_2, t_2) = \frac{a^3(t_1)}{a^3(t_2)}\,\tilde u(\nu_1, t_1)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, Eq. (P3.7), p. 4" />

Then use this result to show the claim: $\tilde u(\nu, t_2)$ is the black-body spectrum with the temperature $T(t_2) = T_1\,a(t_1)/a(t_2)$.

<Hint>

Set the two expressions for the number of photons equal. What does part (a) say about $d\nu_2/\nu_2$ and $d\nu_1/\nu_1$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the second step, put the black-body formula with $T_1$ in for $\tilde u(\nu_1, t_1)$. The left side is a function of $\nu_2$, so replace $\nu_1$ everywhere by $\nu_2\,a(t_2)/a(t_1)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Look at the two places where $\nu_1$ appears: the factor $\nu_1^3$ in front, and the exponent. The factor in front meets $a^3(t_1)/a^3(t_2)$. In the exponent, move the ratio of the scale factors from the frequency to the temperature.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Equal numbers of photons give the new spectrum in terms of the old one. The factor $a^3$ in front turns $\nu_1^3$ into $\nu_2^3$, and in the exponent only the ratio of frequency to temperature appears, so a redshift of the frequency is the same as a lower temperature.

<Step title="Equal numbers">

Set $dN_2 = dN_1$:

<Formula label="Equal numbers of photons" tex="\frac{\tilde u(\nu_2, t_2)\,a^3(t_2)\,V_c\,d\nu_2}{h\nu_2} = \frac{\tilde u(\nu_1, t_1)\,a^3(t_1)\,V_c\,d\nu_1}{h\nu_1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

By part (a), $d\nu_2/\nu_2 = d\nu_1/\nu_1$. These factors cancel, and so do $V_c$ and $h$. What is left is

<Formula label="The spectrum at the later time, derived" tex="\tilde u(\nu_2, t_2) = \frac{a^3(t_1)}{a^3(t_2)}\,\tilde u(\nu_1, t_1)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, Eq. (P3.7), p. 4" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put in the black-body spectrum">

At $t_1$ the spectrum is thermal, with temperature $T_1$:

<Formula label="With the spectrum at the first time" tex="\tilde u(\nu_2, t_2) = \frac{a^3(t_1)}{a^3(t_2)}\;\frac{16\pi^2\hbar\,\nu_1^3}{c^3}\;\frac{1}{e^{h\nu_1/kT_1} - 1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Write it with the later frequency">

From part (a), $\nu_1 = \nu_2\,a(t_2)/a(t_1)$. In front:

<Formula label="The factor in front" tex="\frac{a^3(t_1)}{a^3(t_2)}\,\nu_1^3 = \nu_2^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

In the exponent:

<Formula label="The exponent" tex="\frac{h\nu_1}{kT_1} = \frac{h\nu_2}{k\,T_1\,a(t_1)/a(t_2)} = \frac{h\nu_2}{k\,T(t_2)}, \qquad T(t_2) = \frac{a(t_1)}{a(t_2)}\,T_1" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

Together:

<Formula label="A black-body spectrum again" tex="\tilde u(\nu_2, t_2) = \frac{16\pi^2\hbar\,\nu_2^3}{c^3}\;\frac{1}{e^{h\nu_2/kT(t_2)} - 1} = u_\nu\big(\nu_2, T(t_2)\big)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

The frequency $\nu_1$ was any frequency, so $\nu_2$ is any frequency too. This is the claim.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Add up all frequencies. For a black body the total energy density is proportional to $T^4$, so the result says that it falls as $1/a^4$: three powers because the photons are spread over a volume that grows as $a^3$, and one more because each photon loses energy as $1/a$. That is how the lesson on [the dilution of radiation](/courses/8-286/mass-energy-and-radiation) found radiation to behave. The number of photons in the region, which is proportional to $T^3 a^3$, stays the same, as part (b) requires.

The argument uses only that the spectrum is $\nu^3$ times a function of $\nu/T$, which the black-body spectrum is. A spectrum without this property would not keep its shape. And the result does not use how $a(t)$ depends on time, so it holds whether radiation, matter or anything else dominates the expansion.

</Step>
