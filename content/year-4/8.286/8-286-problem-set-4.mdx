## What the lessons give you

Every universe in this set holds only matter that moves slowly compared with light, so its mass density $\rho$ falls as the cube of the scale factor $a$. The lessons give four results that you may use without deriving them again.

The Friedmann equation, with the Hubble rate $H = \dot a/a$:

<Formula label="Friedmann equation, matter only" tex="H^2 = \left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2}, \qquad \rho\,a^3 = \text{const}" cite="reading1" at="slide 2" />

The constant $k$ is positive in a closed universe, zero in a flat one and negative in an open one. For a closed universe the lesson writes $\tilde a = a/\sqrt k$, a length, and defines one constant length $\alpha$ that fixes the whole history:

<Formula label="The constant α" tex="\alpha \equiv \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\rho\,\tilde a^3}{c^2}" cite="reading1" at="slide 2" />

The critical density and the density parameter:

<Formula label="Critical density and Ω" tex="\rho_c = \frac{3H^2}{8\pi G}, \qquad \Omega \equiv \frac{\rho}{\rho_c}" cite="reading5" at="pp. 20 and 23" />

The horizon distance at time $t$: the physical distance, at that time, of the farthest matter from which light could have arrived since the Big Bang. (The integration variable is called $t'$, so that it does not share a name with the upper limit.)

<Formula label="Horizon distance (general)" tex="\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t) = a(t)\int_0^{t}\frac{c}{a(t')}\,dt'" cite="reading3" at="p. 18" />

As in the lessons, comoving coordinates are counted in Guth's notches: $a$ is in metres per notch and $k$ in notches to the power $-2$, so $a/\sqrt k$ is in metres. A quantity that can be measured, such as $H$, $\rho$, $\Omega$ or a physical distance, cannot depend on the size of a notch. In this set that means: **only the answer for $a$ itself may contain $\sqrt k$ or $\sqrt\kappa$.**

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Evolution of a closed, matter-dominated universe" points={25} mit="Problem 1">

A closed universe that holds only matter is described by the development angle $\theta$, which runs from 0 at the Big Bang to $2\pi$ at the Big Crunch:

<Formula label="Evolution of a closed universe" tex="c\,t = \alpha\,(\theta - \sin\theta), \qquad \frac{a}{\sqrt k} = \alpha\,(1 - \cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Here $\alpha$ is a constant with the units of length.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the Hubble expansion rate $H$ in terms of $\alpha$, $\theta$ and $c$.

<Hint>

$H$ is the rate of change of $a$ in time, divided by $a$. You know $a$ as a function of $\theta$, not of $t$. What links the two?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the chain rule: $da/dt = (da/d\theta)\,(d\theta/dt)$. The first of the two given equations tells you $dt/d\theta$, and $d\theta/dt$ is one divided by that.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$H =$" expr="c*sin(theta)/(alpha*(1 - cos(theta))^2)"
  vars={{ c: [1, 3], alpha: [0.5, 2], theta: [2, 6] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta", alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "c*sin(theta)/(1 - cos(theta))", hint: "That is the rate of change of a/√k, which is a speed. The Hubble rate is that speed divided by a/√k itself." },
    { expr: "sin(theta)/(1 - cos(theta))", hint: "That is the change of a per unit of θ, divided by a. The Hubble rate is a change per unit of time: multiply by dθ/dt." },
    { expr: "c*sin(theta)/(alpha*(1 - cos(theta)))", hint: "Count the factors of 1 − cos θ below the line: dθ/dt brings one, and dividing by a brings another." },
    { expr: "c*abs(sin(theta))/(alpha*(1 - cos(theta))^2)", hint: "The size is right, but H has a sign: it is negative while the universe contracts, when θ is larger than π." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both $t$ and $a$ are known as functions of $\theta$. The chain rule turns a derivative with respect to $t$ into one with respect to $\theta$.

<Step title="How fast θ runs">

Differentiate the first equation with respect to $\theta$ and turn the result upside down:

<Formula label="Rate of the development angle (closed)" tex="c\,\frac{dt}{d\theta} = \alpha\,(1 - \cos\theta) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{c}{\alpha\,(1 - \cos\theta)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The Hubble rate">

The constant $\sqrt k$ cancels in $\dot a/a$, so work with $\tilde a = a/\sqrt k = \alpha(1 - \cos\theta)$, for which $d\tilde a/d\theta = \alpha\sin\theta$:

<Formula label="Hubble rate of a closed universe" tex="H = \frac{1}{\tilde a}\,\frac{d\tilde a}{d\theta}\,\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{\alpha\sin\theta}{\alpha\,(1 - \cos\theta)}\cdot\frac{c}{\alpha\,(1 - \cos\theta)} = \frac{c\,\sin\theta}{\alpha\,(1 - \cos\theta)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A speed divided by a length is a rate, as $H$ must be. The sign follows $\sin\theta$: positive while the universe expands ($\theta < \pi$), zero at the largest size ($\theta = \pi$) and negative while it contracts.

For small $\theta$ the universe is young and should behave like a flat one. There $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ and $1 - \cos\theta \approx \theta^2/2$, so $H \approx 4c/(\alpha\theta^3)$. The time is $ct \approx \alpha\theta^3/6$, so $H \approx 2/(3t)$: the flat, matter-dominated result of the lessons.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find the mass density $\rho$ in terms of $\alpha$, $\theta$, $c$ and Newton's constant $G$.

<Hint>

The constant $\alpha$ was defined from the density. Look at its definition in "What the lessons give you".

</Hint>

<Hint>

Solve the definition of $\alpha$ for $\rho$, and put in $\tilde a = a/\sqrt k$ as a function of $\theta$. The Friedmann equation with your $H$ of part (a) is a second route, and a good check.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$\rho =$" expr="3*c^2/(4*pi*G*alpha^2*(1 - cos(theta))^3)"
  vars={{ c: [1, 3], G: [0.5, 2], alpha: [0.5, 2], theta: [2, 6] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta", alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*c^2/(8*pi*G*alpha^2*(1 - cos(theta))^3)", hint: "A factor of 2 is missing. The definition of α has 4π/3 in it, not the 8π/3 of the Friedmann equation." },
    { expr: "3*c^2*sin(theta)^2/(8*pi*G*alpha^2*(1 - cos(theta))^4)", hint: "That is the critical density that belongs to your H. The real density of a closed universe is larger: the Friedmann equation also has the term with k." },
    { expr: "3*c^2/(4*pi*G*alpha^2*(1 - cos(theta))^2)", hint: "The density of matter falls as the cube of the scale factor. Check the power of 1 − cos θ." },
    { expr: "3*c^2/(4*pi*G*alpha^3*(1 - cos(theta))^3)", hint: "Check the units, and count the factors of α: one above the line from the definition of α, three below from the cube of a/√k." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The constant $\alpha$ is, apart from fixed factors, the density multiplied by the cube of $\tilde a$. So the density is known as soon as $\tilde a$ is.

<Step title="Solve the definition of α for the density">

<Formula label="Density of a closed universe" tex="\rho = \frac{3\,\alpha\,c^2}{4\pi G\,\tilde a^3} = \frac{3\,c^2}{4\pi G\,\alpha^2\,(1 - \cos\theta)^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check with the Friedmann equation">

The Friedmann equation gives $\tfrac{8\pi}{3}G\rho = H^2 + c^2/\tilde a^2$. With $H$ from part (a):

<Formula label="Check of the density (closed)" tex="H^2 + \frac{c^2}{\tilde a^2} = \frac{c^2}{\alpha^2(1 - \cos\theta)^2}\left[\frac{\sin^2\theta}{(1 - \cos\theta)^2} + 1\right] = \frac{2\,c^2}{\alpha^2\,(1 - \cos\theta)^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

The last step uses $\sin^2\theta = (1 - \cos\theta)(1 + \cos\theta)$, so the square bracket is $2/(1 - \cos\theta)$. Multiplying by $3/(8\pi G)$ gives the same $\rho$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the units and a limit">

The units of $c^2/(G\alpha^2)$ are those of a mass per volume. The density is infinite at the Big Bang and at the Big Crunch and smallest at $\theta = \pi$, when the universe is largest.

For small $\theta$, $\rho \approx 6c^2/(\pi G\alpha^2\theta^6)$, and with $ct \approx \alpha\theta^3/6$ this is $\rho \approx 1/(6\pi G t^2)$. That is the critical density $3H^2/(8\pi G)$ for $H = 2/(3t)$: the young closed universe is nearly flat.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Find $\Omega = \rho/\rho_c$ in terms of $\theta$ by combining your answers to parts (a) and (b). The lesson on the age of a closed universe quotes the result, but here you are asked to reach it from your own $H$ and $\rho$.

<Hint>

The critical density is $3H^2/(8\pi G)$, with the $H$ of part (a).

</Hint>

<Hint>

After dividing, write $\sin^2\theta$ as $(1 - \cos\theta)(1 + \cos\theta)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="$\Omega =$" expr="2/(1 + cos(theta))"
  vars={{ theta: [0.3, 2.8] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(1 + cos(theta))/2", hint: "This is never larger than 1, but a closed universe has Ω above 1. Ω is the density divided by the critical density, not the other way round." },
    { expr: "2/(1 - cos(theta))", hint: "For small θ this grows without limit, but a young universe is nearly flat, with Ω close to 1. Check how you wrote sin²θ." },
    { expr: "1/(1 + cos(theta))", hint: "A factor of 2 is missing: for small θ this gives one half, but Ω must start at 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Divide the density of part (b) by the critical density that belongs to the Hubble rate of part (a).

<Step title="Divide">

<Formula label="Ω of a closed universe" tex="\Omega = \frac{8\pi G\rho}{3H^2} = \frac{2\,c^2}{\alpha^2(1 - \cos\theta)^3}\cdot\frac{\alpha^2(1 - \cos\theta)^4}{c^2\sin^2\theta} = \frac{2\,(1 - \cos\theta)}{\sin^2\theta} = \frac{2}{1 + \cos\theta}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compare with the lecture">

This is the relation that Guth found in Lecture 9 by a different route, through $\tilde a$ and $\alpha$ written in terms of $H$ and $\Omega$:

<Formula label="Ω and the development angle" tex="\Omega = \frac{2}{1 + \cos\theta}" cite="reading4" at="p. 19" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both $\alpha$ and $c$ have cancelled, as they must in a pure number. $\Omega$ starts at 1 for $\theta = 0$, is 2 at $\theta = \pi/2$ and grows without limit as $\theta$ approaches $\pi$, where $H = 0$ and the critical density vanishes. It is never below 1, as befits a closed universe.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={7}>

The horizon distance needs an integral over the whole history of $a(t)$, which looks hard for a closed universe. It becomes easy when you integrate over $\theta$ instead of over $t$. Show that the physical horizon distance of the closed, matter-dominated universe is

<Formula label="Horizon distance of a closed universe" tex="\ell_{p,\text{horizon}} = \alpha\,\theta\,(1 - \cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 1" />

<Hint>

Start from the general formula for the horizon distance in "What the lessons give you". The factor $\sqrt k$ can be taken out of both scale factors.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Replace $c\,dt'$ by its expression in $d\theta$, from the first of the two given equations. What is left under the integral?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the variable $\theta$, the light ray's comoving speed is constant: $c\,dt$ and $\tilde a$ carry the same factor $\alpha(1 - \cos\theta)$.

<Step title="Write the horizon distance with ã">

The scale factor appears once above and once below the line, so $\sqrt k$ cancels:

<Formula label="Horizon distance with the rescaled scale factor" tex="\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t) = \tilde a(t)\int_0^{t}\frac{c\,dt'}{\tilde a(t')}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Change the variable to θ">

From part (a), $c\,dt' = \alpha(1 - \cos\theta')\,d\theta'$, and $\tilde a(t') = \alpha(1 - \cos\theta')$. The Big Bang is $\theta' = 0$ and the time $t$ is $\theta' = \theta$:

<Formula label="The integral over the development angle" tex="\int_0^{t}\frac{c\,dt'}{\tilde a(t')} = \int_0^{\theta}\frac{\alpha\,(1 - \cos\theta')\,d\theta'}{\alpha\,(1 - \cos\theta')} = \int_0^{\theta} d\theta' = \theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Multiply by the scale factor now">

With $\tilde a(t) = \alpha(1 - \cos\theta)$ in front, the horizon distance is $\alpha\,\theta\,(1 - \cos\theta)$, which was to be shown.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is a length and does not contain $\sqrt k$. For small $\theta$ it is about $\alpha\theta^3/2$, and $ct \approx \alpha\theta^3/6$, so $\ell_{p,\text{horizon}} \approx 3ct$: the horizon distance of the flat matter-dominated universe in the lesson.

The integral also has a meaning of its own. Light covers equal amounts of $\sqrt k$ times comoving distance in equal steps of $\theta$, so $\theta$ measures how far light has come since the Big Bang. Problem 3 uses this.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Evolution of an open, matter-dominated universe" points={35} mit="Problem 2">

MIT notes that this problem was first set on a quiz in 1992. An open universe that holds only matter is described by

<Formula label="Evolution of an open universe" tex="c\,t = \alpha\,(\sinh\theta - \theta), \qquad \frac{a}{\sqrt\kappa} = \alpha\,(\cosh\theta - 1)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

Here $\kappa = -k$ is positive, $\alpha$ is again a constant with the units of length, and $\theta$ runs from 0 at the Big Bang upwards without limit. For parts (e) and (f) you may want the hyperbolic functions written out, and the series of the exponential function:

<Formula label="Hyperbolic functions and the exponential series" tex="\sinh\theta = \frac{e^{\theta} - e^{-\theta}}{2}, \qquad \cosh\theta = \frac{e^{\theta} + e^{-\theta}}{2}, \qquad e^{\theta} = 1 + \frac{\theta}{1!} + \frac{\theta^2}{2!} + \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \dots" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the Hubble expansion rate $H$ in terms of $\alpha$, $\theta$ and $c$.

<Hint>

The method of Problem 1(a) works here too. The derivative of $\sinh\theta$ is $\cosh\theta$, and the derivative of $\cosh\theta$ is $\sinh\theta$ (with no minus sign).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$H =$" expr="c*sinh(theta)/(alpha*(cosh(theta) - 1)^2)"
  vars={{ c: [1, 3], alpha: [0.5, 2], theta: [0.3, 4] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta", alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "c*sin(theta)/(alpha*(1 - cos(theta))^2)", hint: "That is the Hubble rate of the closed universe. The open universe has hyperbolic functions in its place." },
    { expr: "c*sinh(theta)/(cosh(theta) - 1)", hint: "That is the rate of change of a/√κ, which is a speed. The Hubble rate is that speed divided by a/√κ itself." },
    { expr: "sinh(theta)/(cosh(theta) - 1)", hint: "That is the change of a per unit of θ, divided by a. The Hubble rate is a change per unit of time: multiply by dθ/dt." },
    { expr: "c*sinh(theta)/(alpha*(cosh(theta) - 1))", hint: "Count the factors of cosh θ − 1 below the line: dθ/dt brings one, and dividing by a brings another." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As in Problem 1(a): the chain rule, with $d\theta/dt$ from the equation for $t$.

<Step title="How fast θ runs">

<Formula label="Rate of the development angle (open)" tex="c\,\frac{dt}{d\theta} = \alpha\,(\cosh\theta - 1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{c}{\alpha\,(\cosh\theta - 1)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The Hubble rate">

With $\tilde a = a/\sqrt\kappa = \alpha(\cosh\theta - 1)$ and $d\tilde a/d\theta = \alpha\sinh\theta$:

<Formula label="Hubble rate of an open universe" tex="H = \frac{1}{\tilde a}\,\frac{d\tilde a}{d\theta}\,\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \frac{c\,\sinh\theta}{\alpha\,(\cosh\theta - 1)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is a speed divided by a length. It is positive for every $\theta > 0$: an open universe never stops expanding. For small $\theta$, $\sinh\theta \approx \theta$ and $\cosh\theta - 1 \approx \theta^2/2$, so $H \approx 4c/(\alpha\theta^3)$, the same as for the young closed universe, and again $H \approx 2/(3t)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the mass density $\rho$ in terms of $\alpha$, $\theta$, $c$ and $G$.

<Hint>

The Friedmann equation gives $\rho$ once $H$ and $a$ are known. Mind the sign of the term with $k$: here $k = -\kappa$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You will need the identity $\cosh^2\theta - \sinh^2\theta = 1$, or $\sinh^2\theta = (\cosh\theta - 1)(\cosh\theta + 1)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$\rho =$" expr="3*c^2/(4*pi*G*alpha^2*(cosh(theta) - 1)^3)"
  vars={{ c: [1, 3], G: [0.5, 2], alpha: [0.5, 2], theta: [0.3, 4] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta", alpha: "\\alpha" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*c^2/(4*pi*G*alpha^2*(1 - cos(theta))^3)", hint: "That is the density of the closed universe. The open universe has cosh θ − 1 in place of 1 − cos θ." },
    { expr: "3*c^2*sinh(theta)^2/(8*pi*G*alpha^2*(cosh(theta) - 1)^4)", hint: "That is the critical density that belongs to your H. The real density of an open universe is smaller: the Friedmann equation also has the term with k." },
    { expr: "3*c^2*cosh(theta)/(4*pi*G*alpha^2*(cosh(theta) - 1)^3)", hint: "Check the sign of the curvature term. In an open universe k = −κ is negative, so the density is less than the critical density, not more." },
    { expr: "3*c^2/(8*pi*G*alpha^2*(cosh(theta) - 1)^3)", hint: "A factor of 2 is missing. Check the last step: sinh²θ − (cosh θ − 1)² equals 2 (cosh θ − 1)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Solve the Friedmann equation for the density, with $k = -\kappa$ and the $H$ of part (a).

<Step title="The Friedmann equation of an open universe">

With $k = -\kappa$ the curvature term changes sign, and $\kappa c^2/a^2 = c^2/\tilde a^2$:

<Formula label="Friedmann equation, open universe" tex="H^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho + \frac{c^2}{\tilde a^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \rho = \frac{3}{8\pi G}\left(H^2 - \frac{c^2}{\tilde a^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put in H and ã">

<Formula label="Density of an open universe" tex="H^2 - \frac{c^2}{\tilde a^2} = \frac{c^2}{\alpha^2(\cosh\theta - 1)^2}\left[\frac{\sinh^2\theta}{(\cosh\theta - 1)^2} - 1\right] = \frac{2\,c^2}{\alpha^2(\cosh\theta - 1)^3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \rho = \frac{3\,c^2}{4\pi G\,\alpha^2\,(\cosh\theta - 1)^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

The square bracket is $(\cosh\theta + 1)/(\cosh\theta - 1) - 1 = 2/(\cosh\theta - 1)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result has the form $\rho = 3\alpha c^2/(4\pi G\tilde a^3)$: the constant $\alpha$ of the open universe is the same combination of $\rho\,\tilde a^3$ as in the closed one, now with $\tilde a = a/\sqrt\kappa$. The density is constant times $\tilde a^{-3}$, as it must be for matter, and it falls towards zero as the universe expands for ever.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Find the density parameter $\Omega$ in terms of $\theta$, by combining your answers to parts (a) and (b).

<Hint>

Divide $\rho$ by $3H^2/(8\pi G)$, as in Problem 1(c).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$\Omega =$" expr="2/(1 + cosh(theta))"
  vars={{ theta: [0.3, 4] }}
  names={{ theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(1 + cosh(theta))/2", hint: "This is never smaller than 1, but an open universe has Ω below 1. Ω is the density divided by the critical density, not the other way round." },
    { expr: "2/(cosh(theta) - 1)", hint: "For small θ this grows without limit, but a young universe is nearly flat, with Ω close to 1. Check how you wrote sinh²θ." },
    { expr: "2/(1 + cos(theta))", hint: "That is Ω of the closed universe. The open universe has the hyperbolic cosine." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same division as in Problem 1(c).

<Step title="Divide">

<Formula label="Ω of an open universe" tex="\Omega = \frac{8\pi G\rho}{3H^2} = \frac{2\,c^2}{\alpha^2(\cosh\theta - 1)^3}\cdot\frac{\alpha^2(\cosh\theta - 1)^4}{c^2\sinh^2\theta} = \frac{2\,(\cosh\theta - 1)}{\sinh^2\theta} = \frac{2}{1 + \cosh\theta}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\Omega$ is a pure number. It starts at 1 for $\theta = 0$ and falls towards zero as $\theta$ grows, because $\cosh\theta$ grows without limit. It is never above 1, as befits an open universe. This agrees with the lesson: in an open universe $\Omega$ falls from 1 towards zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

The powers of $1 - \Omega$ cancel exactly. As $\Omega \to 1$ this goes to $2/3$.

</Step>

<Step title="The terms left out vanish">

The next term of the series is of order $x^5$, which is $(1 - \Omega)^{5/2}$ times a number that stays finite. After the division by $(1 - \Omega)^{3/2}$ it is of order $1 - \Omega$, and every later term has a still higher power. All of them go to zero as $\Omega \to 1$. So the limit is exactly $2/3$, the value on the middle line.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

Keeping the $x^5$ term as well gives $|H|\,t \approx \tfrac23 + \tfrac{2}{15}(1 - \Omega)$ close to $\Omega = 1$. For $\Omega = 0.99$ that is $0.6680$, and the exact first line gives $0.6680$ too. For $\Omega = 0.9$ the exact value is $0.6806$: an open universe is a little older than a flat one with the same $H$, as the graph in the lesson shows.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Isotropy about two points in Euclidean space" points={15} mit="Problem 5" optional>

This problem is MIT's extra credit. It is optional here: it does not count towards the score of the set.

Steven Weinberg's book *The First Three Minutes* argues (Chapter 2, page 24) that a space which is isotropic about two different points must be homogeneous. MIT points out two things. First, the claim can fail in a non-Euclidean space, which Weinberg rules out without saying so. Second, his argument is not complete: it draws two circles and reasons from a point where they cross, but two circles need not cross. So the argument covers some cases and not all.

Devise a proof that holds in all cases. You may use any well-known fact of Euclidean geometry.

To make the claim precise: take a Euclidean space of two or three dimensions, and let $f$ be any property that has a value at each point, such as the density of matter. The space is **isotropic about a point** $A$ if $f$ has the same value at any two points that are equally far from $A$. It is **homogeneous** if $f$ has the same value at all points. Show that isotropy about two different points $A$ and $B$ makes $f$ the same everywhere.

<Hint>

Isotropy about $A$ says that $f$ depends only on the distance from $A$. Isotropy about $B$ says that it depends only on the distance from $B$. Call the distance between $A$ and $B$ $d$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A sphere about $A$ and a sphere about $B$ do not always meet. When do they? Think of a triangle with $A$, $B$ and the meeting point as its corners.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You cannot link every pair of spheres about $A$ in one step. Link spheres whose radii differ by no more than $2d$, and then go from any radius to any other in a chain of such steps.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** On a sphere about $A$ the property $f$ has one value. Each sphere about $A$ meets many spheres about $B$, on each of which $f$ also has one value. So $f$ is passed on from sphere to sphere, and a chain of such steps reaches every point. ("Sphere" means a circle in two dimensions.)

<Step title="Two descriptions of the same property">

Let $d > 0$ be the distance between $A$ and $B$. By isotropy about $A$ there is a function $g$ with $f = g(r)$ at every point at distance $r$ from $A$. By isotropy about $B$ there is a function $h$ with $f = h(s)$ at every point at distance $s$ from $B$.

</Step>

<Step title="When do two spheres meet?">

Take the sphere of radius $r$ about $A$. Its point on the line through $A$ and $B$, on the side towards $B$, is at distance $|r - d|$ from $B$. Its point on the far side is at distance $r + d$ from $B$. Moving over the sphere from one point to the other, the distance from $B$ changes without jumps, so it takes every value in between. Hence the sphere of radius $r$ about $A$ and the sphere of radius $s$ about $B$ share a point whenever

<Formula label="Condition for the two spheres to meet" tex="|r - d| \le s \le r + d" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

This is the triangle inequality: a triangle with sides $r$, $s$ and $d$ exists, or the three points lie on one line. At a shared point $f$ equals both $g(r)$ and $h(s)$. So $g(r) = h(s)$ whenever the condition holds.

</Step>

<Step title="One step: radii that differ by at most 2d">

Let $r \le r' \le r + 2d$, and choose $s = r + d$. The sphere of radius $r$ about $A$ meets the sphere of radius $s$ about $B$ (at its far point), so $g(r) = h(s)$. The sphere of radius $r'$ about $A$ meets it too, because the condition holds for $r'$ as well:

<Formula label="The condition for the second radius" tex="r + d \le r' + d, \qquad r + d \ge r' - d, \qquad r + d \ge d - r'" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

The first holds because $r \le r'$, the second because $r' \le r + 2d$, and the third because radii are not negative. So $g(r') = h(s)$ too, and therefore $g(r) = g(r')$. This includes $r = 0$, where the "sphere" is the point $A$ itself, at distance $d$ from $B$.

</Step>

<Step title="A chain of steps">

Any radius $R$ can be reached from 0 in steps of at most $2d$: $0, 2d, 4d, \dots$, and a last step to $R$. Each step keeps the value of $g$, so $g(R) = g(0)$ for every $R$. Every point of the space lies at some distance $R$ from $A$, so $f$ has the value $g(0)$ everywhere. The space is homogeneous.

</Step>

<Step title="Check: where each assumption was used">

No step needs two chosen circles to cross: the spheres are chosen so that they do. Euclidean geometry entered through the distances on a sphere (a triangle with given sides exists whenever the triangle inequality holds) and through the fact that the space goes on without end in every direction.

The proof needs at least two dimensions. On a line, the "sphere" of radius $r$ about $A$ is just two points, and the distance from $B$ cannot run through the values in between. Indeed the claim is false on a line: a pattern that repeats with period $2d$ and is mirror symmetric about $A$ is also mirror symmetric about $B$, but it is not the same everywhere.

The claim can fail in a non-Euclidean space, as MIT warns. On a globe, with A and B at the two poles, a pattern of bands along the lines of latitude is isotropic about both, yet it is not homogeneous.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>
