With the metric of the universe in hand, the second half of Lecture 13 asks how things **move** in a given metric. The tool is the geodesic: the path of stationary length. (That free particles move on geodesics is taken up in Lecture 14.) The method is general: it works for any metric, including the Schwarzschild metric of stars and black holes, which Guth mentions as a later example. In the lecture he showed the long equations from his lecture notes on screen rather than writing them on the board; the same equations appear on slides 2–5 of Lecture 14, which are cited here.

## Geodesics: stationary paths

Guth starts with the simplest case: a two-dimensional space with coordinates $x$ and $y$ and some metric, the kind of space Gauss, Bolyai and Lobachevsky studied. Everything carries over to spacetime unchanged.

A **geodesic** between two points is a path whose length is **stationary**: change the path slightly, keeping the endpoints fixed, and the length does not change to first order. In an ordinary space a geodesic is usually the shortest path. In spacetime, where the metric is not positive definite, geodesics can also be maxima or saddle points. The derivation only uses "the first-order change vanishes", so it covers all of these at once.

[Guth suggests that in two-dimensional spaces the stationary paths are always minima. Not quite: on a sphere the long way round a great circle between two points is also stationary, but it is not the shortest path: it is a saddle point.]

## A better notation

Two improvements make the metric easier to handle.

**Number the coordinates.** Write $x^1, x^2$ instead of $x, y$. The superscripts are labels, not powers, an unfortunate clash, tolerated because general relativity needs both upper and lower indices.

**Sum with indices.** The metric becomes $\sum_{i=1}^{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2} g_{ij}\,dx^i dx^j$. The $(1,1)$ and $(2,2)$ terms are the old $dx^2$ and $dy^2$ terms; $g_{12}$ and $g_{21}$ are equal and both multiply $dx\,dy$. Then comes **Einstein's summation convention**: an index that appears twice, once up and once down, is summed automatically, so the sum signs can be dropped.

<Formula label="Metric in index notation" tex="ds^2 = g_{ij}(x^k)\,dx^i\,dx^j" cite="reading2" at="slide 3" />

In four dimensions this one line stands for 16 terms (10 different ones, since $g_{ij} = g_{ji}$).

## The length of a path

Describe a path from $A$ to $B$ by two functions $x^i(\lambda)$ of a parameter $\lambda$, which runs from $0$ to $\lambda_f$:

<Formula label="A path from A to B" tex="x^i(\lambda), \qquad x^i(0) = x^i_A, \qquad x^i(\lambda_f) = x^i_B" cite="reading2" at="slide 3" />

Cut the path into small pieces from $\lambda$ to $\lambda + d\lambda$. Over one piece the coordinates change by $dx^i = (dx^i/d\lambda)\,d\lambda$; putting this into the metric gives

<Formula label="One small piece" tex="ds^2 = g_{ij}\big(x^k(\lambda)\big)\,\frac{dx^i}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^j}{d\lambda}\,d\lambda^2" cite="reading2" at="slide 3" />

For a space, $ds^2$ is positive, so the square root is the length of the piece. Adding up all pieces:

<Formula label="Length of the path" tex="S\big[x^i(\lambda)\big] = \int_0^{\lambda_f} \sqrt{g_{ij}\big(x^k(\lambda)\big)\,\frac{dx^i}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^j}{d\lambda}}\;d\lambda" cite="reading2" at="slide 3; transcript pp. 21–22" />

## Varying the path

To test whether a path is a geodesic, compare it with every nearby path between the same endpoints. This is the calculus of variations, which slide 4 dates to 1696. Write a nearby path as

<Formula label="A varied path" tex="\tilde x^i(\lambda) = x^i(\lambda) + \alpha\,w^i(\lambda), \qquad w^i(0) = 0, \quad w^i(\lambda_f) = 0" cite="reading2" at="slide 4" />

Here $w^i(\lambda)$ is any ordinary function (with values like 2 or 5) and $\alpha$ is a small number that controls how far the new path strays. The conditions on $w^i$ keep the endpoints fixed; otherwise you could always find a shorter path by moving them. The path $x^i(\lambda)$ is a geodesic if

<Formula label="Condition for a geodesic" tex="\left.\frac{d S\big[\tilde x^i(\lambda)\big]}{d\alpha}\right|_{\alpha = 0} = 0 \quad \text{for all } w^i(\lambda)" cite="reading2" at="slide 4" />

## Differentiating the length

Name the integrand of the varied path's length:

<Formula label="The integrand A" tex="S\big[\tilde x^i(\lambda)\big] = \int_0^{\lambda_f} \sqrt{A(\lambda, \alpha)}\;d\lambda, \qquad A(\lambda, \alpha) = g_{ij}\big(\tilde x^k(\lambda)\big)\,\frac{d\tilde x^i}{d\lambda}\frac{d\tilde x^j}{d\lambda}" cite="reading2" at="slide 4" />

$\alpha$ appears in three places. The metric does not depend on $\alpha$ directly, but it is evaluated at $\tilde x$, which does; by the chain rule

<Formula label="Chain rule for the metric" tex="\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial\alpha} = \frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^k}\,\frac{\partial\tilde x^k}{\partial\alpha} = \frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^k}\,w^k" cite="reading1" at="pp. 23–24 (on screen from the lecture notes)" />

and each $d\tilde x^i/d\lambda$ gives $dw^i/d\lambda$. The derivative of a square root brings $1/(2\sqrt A)$. After differentiating, everything else is set to $\alpha = 0$, so the tildes disappear. The two terms with $dw/d\lambda$ are equal once the summed ("dummy") indices $i$ and $j$ are renamed, using $g_{ij} = g_{ji}$, which gives a factor 2:

<Formula label="Derivative of the length" tex="\left.\frac{dS}{d\alpha}\right|_{\alpha = 0} = \frac12\int_0^{\lambda_f}\frac{1}{\sqrt{A(\lambda, 0)}}\left[\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^k}\,w^k\,\frac{dx^i}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^j}{d\lambda} + 2\,g_{ij}\,\frac{dw^i}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^j}{d\lambda}\right]d\lambda" cite="reading2" at="slide 5" />

## The key step: integration by parts

The first term is proportional to $w$, the second to its derivative, so it is not yet clear when the sum vanishes for every $w$. Guth calls this the only real difficulty in the derivation; the fix is to integrate the second term by parts, $\int u\,dv = -\int v\,du + [uv]$, with

- $u = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt A}\,g_{ij}\,\dfrac{dx^j}{d\lambda}$ and $dv = \dfrac{dw^i}{d\lambda}\,d\lambda$, so $v = w^i$.

The boundary term $[uv]$ contains $w^i$ at the two endpoints, where it vanishes: no boundary term survives. Now $w$ appears undifferentiated in both terms. Renaming dummy indices so it carries the same index $i$ in each, it can be taken outside:

<Formula label="After integrating by parts" tex="\left.\frac{dS}{d\alpha}\right|_{\alpha = 0} = \int_0^{\lambda_f}\left\{\frac{1}{2\sqrt A}\,\frac{\partial g_{jk}}{\partial x^i}\frac{dx^j}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^k}{d\lambda} - \frac{d}{d\lambda}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt A}\,g_{ij}\,\frac{dx^j}{d\lambda}\right]\right\} w^i(\lambda)\,d\lambda" cite="reading2" at="slide 5" />

## The geodesic equation

This must vanish for **every** $w^i$. One integral can be zero by luck, positive in some places, negative in others. But if the expression in curly brackets were not zero, we could choose $w^i$ equal to it; the integrand would then be a perfect square and the integral could not vanish. So the brackets must vanish identically:

<Formula label="The geodesic equation" tex="\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt A}\,g_{ij}\,\frac{dx^j}{d\lambda}\right] = \frac{1}{2\sqrt A}\,\frac{\partial g_{jk}}{\partial x^i}\,\frac{dx^j}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^k}{d\lambda}" cite="reading2" at="slide 5; transcript p. 27" />

There is one equation for each value of $i$. Both sides depend on the metric and on the path, through $dx/d\lambda$. A continuous path is a geodesic **if and only if** it satisfies this equation. Lecture 14 simplifies it and tries it on examples.

<WorkedExample title="Straight lines in the flat plane">
<Problem>
For the flat plane in Cartesian coordinates, $g_{11} = g_{22} = 1$ and $g_{12} = g_{21} = 0$. What does the geodesic equation say? Then compare the straight path from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ with the parabola $x = \lambda$, $y = \lambda^2$, $0 \le \lambda \le 1$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Right-hand side">
The metric is constant, so $\partial g_{jk}/\partial x^i = 0$ and the right-hand side vanishes.
</Step>
<Step title="Left-hand side">
$g_{ij}\,dx^j/d\lambda = dx^i/d\lambda$, so the equation says $\dfrac{d}{d\lambda}\left[\dfrac{1}{\sqrt A}\dfrac{dx^i}{d\lambda}\right] = 0$. Since $\sqrt A\,d\lambda = ds$, this is $d^2x^i/ds^2 = 0$: the coordinates change at a constant rate with distance. The geodesics are straight lines, as they should be.
</Step>
<Step title="The parabola is not one">
Along the parabola $A = 1 + 4\lambda^2$. For $i = 1$: $\dfrac{d}{d\lambda}\left[(1+4\lambda^2)^{-1/2}\right] = -4\lambda\,(1+4\lambda^2)^{-3/2}$, which is not zero for $\lambda > 0$. So the parabola fails the equation.
</Step>
<Step title="Lengths">
Straight line: $\sqrt 2 \approx 1.414$. Parabola: $\int_0^1\sqrt{1+4\lambda^2}\,d\lambda \approx 1.479$, longer. It is exactly the $\alpha = -1$ path of the family in the graph; its mirror image, $y = 2x - x^2$ at $\alpha = +1$, has the same length.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Where this leads">
In the Robertson–Walker spacetime, free particles follow geodesics. (Light follows *null* geodesics, with $ds^2 = 0$; this form of the equation, which divides by $\sqrt A$, cannot be used for them directly.) Lecture 14 simplifies this equation and tries it on the Schwarzschild metric of a black hole; applying it to the expanding universe, Guth says there, is left to the homework, where it shows how moving particles slow down as the universe expands.
</Callout>

## Summary

- A geodesic is a path of **stationary** length between fixed endpoints: usually a minimum in space, possibly a maximum or saddle in spacetime.
- Index notation: $ds^2 = g_{ij}\,dx^i dx^j$, summed over repeated upper–lower indices (Einstein convention).
- A path $x^i(\lambda)$ has length $S = \int\sqrt{g_{ij}\,\frac{dx^i}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^j}{d\lambda}}\,d\lambda$. Compare it with $x^i + \alpha w^i$, $w^i = 0$ at the ends, and demand $dS/d\alpha = 0$ at $\alpha = 0$ for all $w^i$.
- Integration by parts, with no boundary term, leaves $\int\{\cdots\}\,w^i\,d\lambda = 0$ for all $w^i$, so $\{\cdots\} = 0$: the geodesic equation $\frac{d}{d\lambda}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt A}g_{ij}\frac{dx^j}{d\lambda}\right] = \frac{1}{2\sqrt A}\,\partial_i g_{jk}\,\frac{dx^j}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^k}{d\lambda}$.
