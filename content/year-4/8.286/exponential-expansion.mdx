## Exponential expansion

To see what happens once inflation begins, treat a small region of the patch as a homogeneous Robertson–Walker universe, leave out the curvature term (it quickly becomes negligible, as we'll see) and let the false-vacuum density dominate:

<Formula label="Friedmann equation during inflation" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho_f" cite="reading1" at="p. 11" />

The right side is a constant, so $\dot a/a$ is constant, and

<Formula label="Exponential expansion" tex="a(t) = \text{const}\times e^{\chi t}, \qquad \chi = \sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho_f}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–12" />

With the full equations there would be other solutions for other initial conditions, but this exponential growth always dominates at late times. That is what inflation is: a period of **exponential expansion**.

### The cosmological no-hair conjecture

Why is inflation so robust? Why does it lead to the same result however it starts? One reason is the **cosmological no-hair** statement: if the matter has $p = -\rho c^2$ with $\rho > 0$, essentially **any** starting metric evolves to look, **locally**, like this flat, exponentially expanding spacetime. It can be proven for small initial deviations (perturbatively); beyond that it is believed but unproven, a conjecture. "Locally" matters: a closed universe filled with false vacuum stays closed forever, never literally flat, but as it grows exponentially every piece of it looks flatter and flatter.

### de Sitter space

This flat, exponentially expanding spacetime is **de Sitter space**, after the Dutch astronomer who described it early in the history of general relativity (Guth thinks in 1917, which is right). It has properties de Sitter himself did not notice: by choosing different surfaces of equal time, the very same spacetime can be described as an open or a closed Robertson–Walker universe, homogeneous in each case. For most purposes, though, think of it as flat and exponentially expanding.

## The event horizon

The horizons met earlier in the course are, technically, **particle horizons**: they concern the past: how far light can have travelled since the beginning. de Sitter space also has an **event horizon**, which concerns the future: two events at the same time that are far enough apart can never influence each other's future, because the expansion outruns the light.

Light's coordinate speed is $c/a(t)$, so the coordinate distance it covers between $t_1$ and $t_2$ is

<Formula label="Coordinate distance travelled by light" tex="\Delta r(t_1,t_2) = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} = \frac{c}{\text{const}\cdot\chi}\left(e^{-\chi t_1} - e^{-\chi t_2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 13–14" />

As $t_2 \to \infty$ this approaches a **finite** limit: anything starting farther away in coordinates will never be reached by the light, however long you wait. Multiplying the limit by $a(t_1)$ gives the physical distance at time $t_1$:

<Formula label="Event horizon distance" tex="\ell_\text{event} = a(t_1)\lim_{t_2\to\infty}\Delta r(t_1,t_2) = c\,\chi^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="p. 14" />

This distance is the **Hubble length**. Light emitted now from anything more than one Hubble length away will never reach us.

This matters because nothing travels faster than light. If a de Sitter region is surrounded by "junk" we can't predict, whatever is outside can never penetrate more than about one Hubble length into it. The interior is protected (a rigorous theorem of general relativity, Guth says), so once a sizeable region of de Sitter space exists, it never disappears, whatever happens outside.

## Sample numbers

We don't know at what energy inflation happened, and so far every observable consequence is much the same whatever the scale. Inflation was invented in the context of grand unified theories, still a plausible setting, so Guth gives grand-unified sample numbers.

The **GUT scale** comes from measuring the strengths of the three Standard Model interactions (one for each of SU(3), SU(2) and U(1)) at accessible energies (around 100 GeV) and extrapolating them theoretically to high energies. They meet, to good accuracy, at one point: that is the basis of grand unification, one interaction that looks like three at low energy through spontaneous symmetry breaking. The meeting is decent in the Standard Model as it is, and works much better in its minimal **supersymmetric** extension (where every known particle gets an unseen partner), a piece of evidence for supersymmetry. Either way the lines meet at about $10^{16}$ GeV.

We can't calculate vacuum energies, but dimensional analysis fixes the false-vacuum mass density up to factors: the only way to make a mass density from $E_\text{GUT}$ and the constants of physics is

<Formula label="False-vacuum mass density at the GUT scale" tex="\rho_f \sim \frac{E_\text{GUT}^4}{\hbar^3c^5} \approx 2.3\times10^{81}\ \text{g/cm}^3" cite="reading1" at="p. 17" />

Putting this into $\chi$:

<Formula label="Expansion time and Hubble length" tex="\chi^{-1} \approx 2.8\times10^{-38}\ \text{s}, \qquad c\chi^{-1} \approx 8\times10^{-28}\ \text{cm}" cite="reading1" at="p. 17" />

[Guth first labels the time as $\chi$; a student catches it, and he corrects it to $\chi^{-1}$.] All off scale by human standards, as grand unified theories are.

## The scenario

Start with a patch of roughly that Hubble length, $\sim 10^{-27}$ cm, some 14 or 15 orders of magnitude smaller than a proton ($\sim 10^{-13}$ cm). Inflation must last long enough for the patch to reach roughly **1 to 10 cm** or more by its end. More is no problem: it just means more inflation than the minimum.

How does inflation end? On the plateau, fluctuations destabilise the field; it rolls down the hill and **oscillates** about the bottom. Because it interacts with other fields, it hands its energy to them, ultimately the Standard Model fields, heating them into the hot soup of particles that is the starting point of the conventional Big Bang. This **reheating** produces a temperature comparable to the theory's own scale: about $10^{16}$ GeV for grand unification.

After that the universe coasts, with $aT \approx$ constant, so the scale factor grows by the ratio of temperatures:

<Formula label="Expansion from the end of inflation to today" tex="\frac{a_\text{now}}{a_\text{end}} \approx \frac{10^{16}\ \text{GeV}}{k\,(2.7\ \text{K})}, \qquad 10\ \text{cm} \times \frac{a_\text{now}}{a_\text{end}} \approx 450\times10^{9}\ \text{light-years}" cite="reading1" at="p. 19" />

[Guth says the temperature ratio is $10^{16}$ GeV "times" $k \times 2.7$ K; it is the quotient, as written above.] Guth estimates that we need only about $40 \times 10^9$ light-years to cover the observed universe (a rough figure: its present diameter is about $9 \times 10^{10}$ light-years), so this is ten times too big, which is fine: 1 cm at the end of inflation would do, and 10 cm is generous. In short: a tiny patch inflates to a centimetre or more, reheats, and coasts into something larger than the whole region we observe.

<WorkedExample title="How many e-folds?">
<Problem>
Using the sample numbers, by what factor must the patch grow during inflation (from $c\chi^{-1}$ to 10 cm)? How many e-folds is that, and how long does it take?
</Problem>
<Step title="Growth factor">
$10\ \text{cm}/(8.3 \times 10^{-28}\ \text{cm}) = 1.2 \times 10^{28}$.
</Step>
<Step title="E-folds">
$\ln(1.2 \times 10^{28}) = 64.7$: about 65 e-folds of expansion.
</Step>
<Step title="Duration">
Each e-fold takes $\chi^{-1} = 2.8 \times 10^{-38}$ s, so 65 of them take $1.8 \times 10^{-36}$ s. Inflation can be over by around $10^{-35}$ s, the time Guth quotes for inflation at the end of the lecture.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="Later developments">
The energy scale of inflation is still unknown. If inflation happened near the GUT scale it would produce primordial gravitational waves, potentially detectable as "B-mode" polarisation in the CMB. In 2014 the BICEP2 team announced such a signal, but a joint analysis with Planck in 2015 showed it could be explained by dust in our own galaxy; by 2021 BICEP/Keck and Planck limited the tensor-to-scalar ratio to $r < 0.036$, which, in standard single-field models, caps the energy scale of inflation at about $1.4\times10^{16}$ GeV, still allowing the GUT scale (where $r \approx 0.01$), but not much above it.
</Callout>

## Summary

- With the false vacuum dominating, $(\dot a/a)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho_f$ gives $a \propto e^{\chi t}$, $\chi = \sqrt{8\pi G\rho_f/3}$.
- By the cosmological no-hair conjecture, almost any starting geometry evolves to look locally like this flat exponential expansion: de Sitter space.
- de Sitter space has an event horizon at one Hubble length, $c\chi^{-1}$, which shields an inflating region from whatever lies outside.
- At the GUT scale: $\rho_f \sim E^4/\hbar^3c^5 \approx 2.3\times10^{81}$ g/cm³, $\chi^{-1} \approx 2.8\times10^{-38}$ s, $c\chi^{-1} \approx 8\times10^{-28}$ cm. About 65 e-folds stretch this to ~10 cm; reheating to ~$10^{16}$ GeV and coasting then give a region larger than the observable universe.
