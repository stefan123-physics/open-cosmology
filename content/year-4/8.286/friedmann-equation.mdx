The [expanding-sphere page](/courses/8-286/expanding-sphere) ended with a conserved quantity $E$. In Lecture 7 Guth rewrites it in the form every cosmologist uses: the **Friedmann equation**. It relates how fast the universe expands to what it contains and to a single constant, $k$, which will turn out to describe the shape of space.

## From E to the Friedmann equation

Start from the conserved quantity and the fact that matter density falls as $\rho = \rho_i/a^3$:

<Formula label="The conserved quantity" tex="E = \tfrac12\,\dot a^2 - \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\rho_i}{a}" cite="reading2" at="slide 3" />

Multiply by $2/a^2$ and use $\rho_i/a^3 = \rho$:

<Formula label="Rearranged" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 - \frac{8\pi}{3}\,G\rho = \frac{2E}{a^2}" cite="reading1" />

Nobody in cosmology actually talks about $E$: that was Guth's own stepping stone. The conventional constant is

<Formula label="Curvature constant" tex="k \equiv -\frac{2E}{c^2}" cite="reading1" />

and with it we arrive at the boxed result:

<Formula label="The Friedmann equation" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}\,G\rho - \frac{k c^2}{a^2}" cite="reading1" />

Since $\dot a/a = H$, the left side is the square of the Hubble rate. The equation says: **the expansion rate is set by the density, corrected by the constant $k$.**

The Russian physicist Alexander Friedmann derived this equation from general relativity in 1922, in a paper on universes with positive $k$, followed in 1924 by one on negative $k$. (In the lecture Guth recalls the dates as 1922 and 1923; the second paper actually appeared in 1924.) Guth calls the pair below together "the Friedmann equations":

<Formula label="The two Friedmann equations (pressureless matter)" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2}, \qquad \ddot a = -\frac{4\pi}{3}G\rho\,a" cite="reading1" />

<Callout kind="note" title="What changes with pressure">
The second equation assumes the matter has no pressure. A gas with pressure does work as it expands, loses energy and therefore (relativistically) mass, so $\rho \propto 1/a^3$ fails. Later lectures show that the **first** equation survives unchanged, while the $\ddot a$ equation gets an extra pressure term.
</Callout>

## Why Newton gives the exact answer

It may seem surprising that a Newtonian calculation reproduces general relativity exactly. Guth's argument is homogeneity. Look at a small box, say one metre across. Inside it, the expansion velocities are tiny and gravity is weak, so general relativity must agree with Newton's theory there. But the universe is homogeneous: if you understand one small box, you understand every box, and so the whole universe. (This works for pressureless matter; radiation and dark energy need the relativistic additions mentioned above.)

## Notches, and what k's number means

So far $r$ and $r_i$ were in metres. Guth now makes the comoving coordinate's unit explicit: say a particle's coordinate $r_i$ is 5 **notches** if it was 5 metres from the origin at the initial time $t_i$. Then

<Formula label="Physical radius with notches" tex="r\,[\text{m}] = a(t)\,[\text{m/notch}] \times r_i\,[\text{notch}], \qquad a(t_i) = 1\ \text{m/notch}" cite="reading1" />

The initial time $t_i$ was arbitrary, so its only trace is the size of the notch. Checking the units of $k$:

<Formula label="Units of k" tex="[k] = \left[\frac{\dot a^2}{c^2}\right] = \frac{\text{s}^2}{\text{m}^2}\cdot\frac{\text{m}^2}{\text{notch}^2\,\text{s}^2} = \frac{1}{\text{notch}^2}" cite="reading1" />

So the *number* $k$ depends on how big a notch is. Guth names two conventions that textbooks use (transcript p. 16):

1. **Make $a = 1$ today.** Then a notch is a metre today. Guth notes that Barbara Ryden's textbook, the one the course uses, follows this convention.
2. **Choose the notch so that $k = +1$ or $-1$.** The *sign* of $k$ cannot be changed by rescaling; if $k = 0$, the notch is left arbitrary.

Either way, notches and the bare number $k$ never appear in any physical prediction. Physical results depend only on the sign of $k$ and on notch-free combinations such as $kc^2/a^2$, which later turns out to measure the curvature of space. Guth adds that, among the books he has read, the second convention is the more common, though both are in use (transcript p. 16).

<WorkedExample title="Checking the rearrangement">
<Problem>
Show that $\frac{2}{a^2}E$ gives the left side of the rearranged equation, and confirm that the Friedmann equation has consistent units.
</Problem>
<Step title="Multiply out">
$\dfrac{2}{a^2}\left(\tfrac12\dot a^2 - \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\dfrac{G\rho_i}{a}\right) = \dfrac{\dot a^2}{a^2} - \dfrac{8\pi}{3}\dfrac{G\rho_i}{a^3} = \left(\dfrac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 - \dfrac{8\pi}{3}G\rho$.
</Step>
<Step title="Units of each term">
$(\dot a/a)^2$ has units of 1/s² (notches cancel). $G\rho$: $\text{m}^3\,\text{kg}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2} \times \text{kg}\,\text{m}^{-3} = \text{s}^{-2}$. And $kc^2/a^2$: $\text{notch}^{-2} \times \text{m}^2\text{s}^{-2} \times \text{notch}^2\,\text{m}^{-2} = \text{s}^{-2}$. All three agree.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology">
The Friedmann equation is the backbone of cosmology. Later in 8.286 it gains terms for radiation and for dark energy, but its structure, expansion rate squared equals a density term minus a curvature term, stays the same. The [next page](/courses/8-286/open-closed-flat) uses it to sort universes into open, closed and flat, and to compute the critical density.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Multiplying $E$ by $2/a^2$ and defining $k = -2E/c^2$ gives the Friedmann equation, $(\dot a/a)^2 = \tfrac{8\pi}{3}G\rho - kc^2/a^2$.
- Friedmann derived it from general relativity in 1922; for pressureless matter, the Newtonian derivation is exact because a homogeneous universe is understood from any small region.
- With comoving coordinates in notches, $a$ has units m/notch and $k$ has units 1/notch²; the bare number $k$ is not physical, but its sign and the combination $kc^2/a^2$ are.
- With pressure, the first Friedmann equation survives; the $\ddot a$ equation changes.
