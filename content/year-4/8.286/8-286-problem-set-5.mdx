## What a metric tells you

Every problem of this set uses a metric in the same way, so here is the method once. A metric gives the length $ds$ of a very short step in terms of the changes of the coordinates along that step. To find the length of a curve:

1. see which coordinates change along the curve and which stay fixed, and set the changes of the fixed ones to zero;
2. take the square root of what is left of $ds^2$: that is the length of one short piece;
3. add up the pieces with an integral.

Coordinates themselves are only labels. A difference of coordinates is not a length until the metric has turned it into one.

In Problems 3 and 4 the metric also has a time term, $-c^2\,dt^2$. The lesson on [the spacetime interval](/courses/8-286/spacetime-interval) explains the minus sign, and the lesson after this set, on [the spacetime metric of the universe](/courses/8-286/robertson-walker-spacetime), shows why the metric of an expanding universe has this form. For this set the metric is given, and you need two facts about it. At one moment of cosmic time ($dt = 0$) it measures lengths in space. And along a light ray $ds^2 = 0$: light travels on a lightlike path, which MIT's set calls a null trajectory.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A circle in a non-Euclidean geometry" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

A three-dimensional space has the metric

<Formula label="The metric of Problem 1" tex="ds^2 = R^2\left[\frac{dr^2}{1 - k r^2} + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1" />

Here $R$ is a constant length, and $k$ is a constant that is $+1$, $-1$ or $0$. The angles are the usual ones: $\theta$ runs from $0$ to $\pi$, and $\phi$ from $0$ to $2\pi$, where $\phi = 2\pi$ is the same place as $\phi = 0$. The radial coordinate $r$ is a pure number. It runs from $0$ to $1$ if $k = 1$, and from $0$ without limit otherwise.

This is the [Robertson–Walker metric](/courses/8-286/robertson-walker-closed) of the lessons at one moment of time, with $R$ standing for the scale factor $a(t)$ at that moment and with the units chosen so that $k$ is $\pm 1$ or $0$. You do not need that to solve the problem: it is enough to know what a metric means.

Look at the circle around the origin given by

<Formula label="The circle" tex="r = r_0, \qquad \theta = \frac{\pi}{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 2" />

On the circle $dr = 0$ and $d\theta = 0$, with $r = r_0$ and $\sin\theta = 1$. What is left of the metric is

<Formula label="A short piece of the circle" tex="ds^2 = R^2\,r_0^2\,d\phi^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad ds = R\,r_0\,d\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Formula label="Two integrals" tex="\int \frac{dr}{\sqrt{1 - r^2}} = \sin^{-1} r, \qquad \int \frac{dr}{\sqrt{1 + r^2}} = \sinh^{-1} r" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2" />

**The idea.** Along the radial line only $r$ changes, so the metric gives the length of each short step $dr$. The radius is the sum of the steps.

<Step title="The length of a short step">

With $d\theta = 0$ and $d\phi = 0$:

<Formula label="A short radial step" tex="ds = \frac{R\,dr}{\sqrt{1 - k r^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

<Step title="Closed universe">

For $k = 1$:

<Formula label="Radius of the circle, closed universe" tex="\rho = R\int_0^{r_0} \frac{dr}{\sqrt{1 - r^2}} = R\,\sin^{-1} r_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

<Step title="Open universe">

<Formula label="Radius of the circle, open universe" tex="\rho = R\int_0^{r_0} \frac{dr}{\sqrt{1 + r^2}} = R\,\sinh^{-1} r_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

<Step title="Check the result">

Both results are a length ($R$) multiplied by a pure number. For a small circle, $r_0 \ll 1$, both $\sin^{-1} r_0$ and $\sinh^{-1} r_0$ are close to $r_0$, so $\rho \approx R\,r_0$ as in flat space: a small enough region of a curved space looks Euclidean.

For a larger circle the two cases move apart. With $r_0 = 0.5$ the closed universe gives $\rho = 0.5236\,R$, more than $0.5\,R$, and the open universe gives $\rho = 0.4812\,R$, less than $0.5\,R$. </Step>

</Solution>

Both $S$ and $\rho$ are lengths that can be measured, so this relation does not depend on the coordinates that were used to find it. And because the space is homogeneous and isotropic, it holds for a circle around any point, in any orientation.

**The idea.** The coordinate $r_0$ is only a label. Remove it from the two results, and a relation between two measurable lengths is left.

<Step title="Closed universe">

Part (b) gave $\rho = R\sin^{-1} r_0$, so $r_0 = \sin(\rho/R)$. In $S = 2\pi R\,r_0$:

<Formula label="Circumference and radius, closed universe" tex="S = 2\pi R\,\sin\frac{\rho}{R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

Part (b) gave $\rho = R\sinh^{-1} r_0$, so $r_0 = \sinh(\rho/R)$:

<Formula label="Circumference and radius, open universe" tex="S = 2\pi R\,\sinh\frac{\rho}{R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

<Step title="Larger or smaller than in flat space?">

For any positive number $x$, $\sin x < x < \sinh x$. With $x = \rho/R$ this gives

<Formula label="Comparison with a Euclidean circle" tex="2\pi R\,\sin\frac{\rho}{R} \;<\; 2\pi\rho \;<\; 2\pi R\,\sinh\frac{\rho}{R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

In a closed universe a circle is **smaller** around than a Euclidean circle of the same radius. In an open universe it is **larger**.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

(In the closed universe the coordinate $r$ reaches only as far as the equator, $r = 1$ and $\rho = \pi R/2$. The relation between $S$ and $\rho$ goes on beyond it: a larger $\rho$ gives a circle on the far half of the space, which shrinks again and closes to a point at $\rho = \pi R$.)

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The volume of a closed universe" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

A closed universe has, at one moment, the metric of a 3-sphere of radius $R$:

<Formula label="The metric of a closed universe" tex="ds^2 = R^2\left[d\psi^2 + \sin^2\psi\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

The angle $\psi$ runs from $0$ to $\pi$. Find the total volume $V$ of this space.

MIT's set gives the plan. Cut the space into thin spherical shells, each reaching from $\psi$ to $\psi + d\psi$. For a fixed $\psi$ the rest of the metric is that of [an ordinary spherical surface](/courses/8-286/sphere-metrics),

<Formula label="The metric of a spherical surface" tex="ds^2 = R^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 3" />

but with the radius $R\sin\psi$ in place of $R$. So the shell is a spherical surface of area $4\pi R^2\sin^2\psi$. Read the thickness of the shell from the metric, multiply, and add up all shells.

<Hint>

The thickness of a shell is the length of a step in which only $\psi$ changes. What does the metric give for it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The volume of a shell is its area multiplied by its thickness $R\,d\psi$. To integrate $\sin^2\psi$, write it as $\tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\psi)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-v" label="$V =$" expr="2*pi^2*R^3"
  vars={{ R: [0.5, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(4/3)*pi*R^3", hint: "That is the volume of a ball of radius R in flat space. Here the space is the curved three-dimensional surface itself: add up the shells of the metric." },
    { expr: "pi^2*R^3", hint: "That is half of the space, up to the equator. The angle ψ runs from 0 to π." },
    { expr: "4*pi^2*R^3", hint: "Check the integral of sin²ψ from 0 to π. Its average value is one half." },
    { expr: "8*pi*R^3", hint: "Check the power of sin ψ in the area of a shell: the radius of the shell is R sin ψ, and the area goes as the square of the radius." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A shell is thin, so its volume is its area multiplied by its thickness. Both come from the metric.

<Step title="The thickness of a shell">

Across the shell only $\psi$ changes. With $d\theta = d\phi = 0$ the metric gives $ds = R\,d\psi$. That is the thickness.

</Step>

<Step title="The volume of a shell">

<Formula label="Volume of one shell" tex="dV = 4\pi R^2\sin^2\psi \times R\,d\psi = 4\pi R^3\sin^2\psi\,d\psi" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Add up the shells">

With $\sin^2\psi = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\psi)$, the integral of $\sin^2\psi$ from $0$ to $\pi$ is $\pi/2$:

<Formula label="Volume of a closed universe" tex="V = 4\pi R^3\int_0^{\pi}\sin^2\psi\,d\psi = 4\pi R^3\cdot\frac{\pi}{2} = 2\pi^2 R^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is a length cubed, and it is finite: a closed universe has no edge and still only so much room. In numbers $2\pi^2 = 19.74$, so the volume is $19.74\,R^3$. That is not the $\tfrac43\pi R^3 = 4.19\,R^3$ of a ball of radius $R$, and it need not be: the ball is the inside of an ordinary sphere in flat space, while this space is the three-dimensional "surface" of a sphere in four dimensions.

Near the origin the shells are those of flat space. For small $\psi$, $\sin\psi \approx \psi$, and the volume out to $\psi$ is $\tfrac43\pi (R\psi)^3$: an ordinary ball of radius $R\psi$.

The shells grow up to the equator, $\psi = \pi/2$, and then shrink again. The two halves are equal, each with volume $\pi^2 R^3$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Surface brightness in a closed universe" points={25} mit="Problem 3">

The spacetime of a homogeneous, isotropic, closed universe has the Robertson–Walker metric, here with $k = 1$:

<Formula label="Robertson–Walker metric, closed universe" tex="ds^2 = -c^2\,d\tau^2 = -c^2\,dt^2 + a^2(t)\left[\frac{dr^2}{1 - r^2} + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 3" />

For motion along a radial line another radial coordinate is handier: the angle $\psi$ with $r = \sin\psi$. Then $dr/\sqrt{1 - r^2} = d\psi$, and the metric becomes

<Formula label="The same metric with the angle ψ" tex="ds^2 = -c^2\,d\tau^2 = -c^2\,dt^2 + a^2(t)\left[d\psi^2 + \sin^2\psi\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 3" />

What $a(t)$ is depends on what the universe contains. In this problem it is an unknown function: simplify your answers as far as you can without knowing it.

We are at the origin of the coordinates. We look at a spherical galaxy at $\psi = \psi_G$. The light we receive today, at time $t_0$, left the galaxy at time $t_G$.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

When the light left, the galaxy had the power $P$: the energy it sent out per unit time, as photons, equally in all directions. Find the energy flux $J$ that we receive from the galaxy today, in terms of $P$, $\psi_G$, $a(t_0)$ and $a(t_G)$. The energy flux is the energy that arrives per unit time and per unit area, on a surface that faces the incoming light at a right angle.

<Hint>

Use coordinates of the same kind with the galaxy at the origin. The space is homogeneous, so the metric looks the same, and we are then at $\psi = \psi_G$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The light that left the galaxy at $t_G$ is spread today over a sphere around the galaxy that passes through us. Find the area of that sphere from the metric at the time $t_0$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The redshift lowers the received power twice. Each photon arrives with less energy, and the photons arrive less often than they were sent. The lesson on the cosmological redshift gives the stretching of times between arrivals. The lower energy of each photon follows because a photon's energy is proportional to its frequency, as in Problem 6 of Problem Set 2.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$J =$" expr="P*aG^2/(4*pi*a0^4*sin(psi)^2)"
  vars={{ P: [1, 5], a0: [1, 3], aG: [0.1, 0.9], psi: [0.2, 2.8] }}
  names={{ a0: "a(t_0)", aG: "a(t_G)", psi: "\\psi_G" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "P/(4*pi*a0^2*sin(psi)^2)", hint: "That leaves out the redshift. Each photon arrives with less energy than it had, and the photons arrive less often than they were sent." },
    { expr: "P*aG/(4*pi*a0^3*sin(psi)^2)", hint: "That has only one factor of the redshift. There are two effects: less energy per photon, and fewer photons per second." },
    { expr: "P*aG^2/(4*pi*a0^4*psi^2)", hint: "The area of a sphere at the coordinate ψ is set by sin ψ, not by ψ: look at the angular part of the metric." },
    { expr: "P/(4*pi*aG^2*sin(psi)^2)", hint: "That spreads the light over a sphere measured at the time of emission and leaves out the redshift. The light reaches us today: use the area of the sphere at the time t₀." },
    { expr: "P*a0^2/(4*pi*aG^4*sin(psi)^2)", hint: "The two scale factors have changed places. The sphere is measured today, and the redshift makes the flux smaller, not larger." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow the photons from the galaxy's point of view. Today they are spread evenly over a sphere around the galaxy. Find the area of that sphere, and how much energy reaches it per unit time.

<Step title="Put the galaxy at the origin">

The space is homogeneous: coordinates of the same form can be centred on any point. Centre them on the galaxy. A radial line from us to the galaxy is the same line as the one from the galaxy to us, and along it the metric at a fixed time gives the length $a(t)\,d\psi$ for each step. So the coordinate distance between the galaxy and us is $\psi_G$ either way: in the new coordinates we sit at $\psi = \psi_G$.

</Step>

<Step title="The sphere the light has reached">

The galaxy sends photons equally in all directions, and all of them move radially outward. The photons that left at $t_G$ are, today, on the sphere $\psi = \psi_G$ around the galaxy. On that sphere $d\psi = 0$, and at the fixed time $t_0$ the metric is that of a spherical surface of radius $a(t_0)\sin\psi_G$. Its area is

<Formula label="Area of the sphere through us" tex="A = 4\pi\,a^2(t_0)\,\sin^2\psi_G" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Two factors from the redshift">

The lesson on the cosmological redshift shows that times between signals are stretched on the way, by the factor

<Formula label="Cosmological redshift" tex="1 + z = \frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_G)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

This acts twice. The photons that leave during a short time arrive spread over a time that is $1 + z$ times longer, so fewer arrive per second. And the frequency of each photon is lower by $1 + z$, so each carries less energy by that factor. The energy that crosses the whole sphere per unit time today is therefore $P/(1+z)^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The flux">

A detector of ours faces the galaxy, so it lies in the sphere. Divide the power that crosses the sphere by the area of the sphere:

<Formula label="Energy flux from the galaxy" tex="J = \frac{P}{4\pi\,a^2(t_0)\sin^2\psi_G\,(1+z)^2} = \frac{P\,a^2(t_G)}{4\pi\,a^4(t_0)\,\sin^2\psi_G}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$P$ is a power and $a^2(t_0)\sin^2\psi_G$ an area (the ratio of the scale factors is a pure number), so $J$ is a power per area.

The result is a length, and for a nearby galaxy it becomes $\ell_\text{prop}$ again. Far away the three distances differ. Two of them are tied together by the redshift alone:

<Formula label="Luminosity distance and angular size distance" tex="\ell_\text{lum} = (1 + z_G)^2\,\ell_\text{ang}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

This relation has no trace of the geometry, and it is another way of writing the result of Problem 3(c): the surface brightness goes as $(\ell_\text{ang}/\ell_\text{lum})^2 = (1+z_G)^{-4}$.

A number: for $\psi_G = 1$ and $z_G = 1$, in units of $a(t_0)$, the three distances are $\ell_\text{ang} = 0.588$, $\ell_\text{prop} = 1$ and $\ell_\text{lum} = 2.35$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 5 (optional)

<Exercise id="p5" title="The Klein description of the Gauss–Bolyai–Lobachevsky geometry" points={15} mit="Problem 5" optional>

The lesson on [non-Euclidean geometry](/courses/8-286/non-euclidean-geometry) met the model with which Felix Klein showed that the geometry of Gauss, Bolyai and Lobachevsky is consistent. Its points are pairs $(x, y)$ with $x^2 + y^2 < 1$, and the distance $d(1,2)$ between two points is defined by

<Formula label="Klein's distance function" tex="\cosh\frac{d(1,2)}{a} = \frac{1 - x_1x_2 - y_1y_2}{\sqrt{1 - x_1^2 - y_1^2}\,\sqrt{1 - x_2^2 - y_2^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 7" />

In this problem $a$ is a constant length, not a function of time. Guth states that this is a two-dimensional space of constant negative curvature: a two-dimensional open universe, with the metric

<Formula label="Robertson–Walker metric in two dimensions, k = −1" tex="ds^2 = a^2\left[\frac{dr^2}{1 + r^2} + r^2\,d\theta^2\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 7" />

The problem is to prove that the two are the same space.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Replace $x$ and $y$ by polar coordinates, $x = u\cos\theta$ and $y = u\sin\theta$. Show that the distance function becomes

<Formula label="Klein's distance function in polar coordinates" tex="\cosh\frac{d(1,2)}{a} = \frac{1 - u_1u_2\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)}{\sqrt{1 - u_1^2}\,\sqrt{1 - u_2^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 8" />

<Hint>

Work out $x_1x_2 + y_1y_2$ and $x^2 + y^2$ in the new coordinates. One formula for the cosine of a difference of two angles is all you need.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the polar coordinates in and use two identities of trigonometry.

<Step title="The numerator">

<Formula label="The product term" tex="x_1x_2 + y_1y_2 = u_1u_2\left(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 + \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2\right) = u_1u_2\cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The denominator">

For each point $x^2 + y^2 = u^2(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = u^2$, so the two square roots become $\sqrt{1 - u_1^2}$ and $\sqrt{1 - u_2^2}$. Together with the numerator this is the form to be shown.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For two points that are the same, $u_1 = u_2$ and $\theta_1 = \theta_2$, the right-hand side is $(1 - u^2)/(1 - u^2) = 1$, so $\cosh(d/a) = 1$ and $d = 0$, as it must be. The condition $x^2 + y^2 < 1$ is now simply $u < 1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Now derive the metric from the distance function. Take two points very close together,

<Formula label="Two neighbouring points" tex="u_1 = u, \quad \theta_1 = \theta, \qquad u_2 = u + du, \quad \theta_2 = \theta + d\theta, \qquad d(1,2) = ds" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 8" />

Put these into the distance function of part (a), expand everything to second order in the small quantities, and show that

<Formula label="The metric of Klein's model" tex="ds^2 = a^2\left[\frac{du^2}{\left(1 - u^2\right)^2} + \frac{u^2\,d\theta^2}{1 - u^2}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 8" />

MIT's set warns that this part is rather messy.

<Hint>

On the left, $\cosh x \approx 1 + \tfrac12 x^2$ for small $x$. So you need the right-hand side to second order, and its terms of first order must cancel.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the right, $\cos d\theta \approx 1 - \tfrac12 d\theta^2$. Take the factor $1 - u^2$ out of the square root, and expand what is left with $(1 - \varepsilon)^{-1/2} \approx 1 + \tfrac12\varepsilon + \tfrac38\varepsilon^2$. The term with $\varepsilon^2$ matters.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** For neighbouring points both sides are close to 1. The left side differs from 1 by $ds^2/(2a^2)$, so expand the right side to second order and compare.

<Step title="The left side">

<Formula label="The hyperbolic cosine for a short distance" tex="\cosh\frac{ds}{a} \approx 1 + \frac{ds^2}{2a^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numerator">

Write $A = 1 - u^2$. With $\cos d\theta \approx 1 - \tfrac12 d\theta^2$, and dropping terms of third order:

<Formula label="The numerator to second order" tex="1 - u\,(u + du)\cos d\theta \approx A - u\,du + \tfrac12 u^2\,d\theta^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The denominator">

The second square root contains $1 - (u + du)^2 = A - 2u\,du - du^2$. So the product of the two roots is $A\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon}$, with the small quantity

<Formula label="The small quantity in the denominator" tex="\varepsilon = \frac{2u\,du + du^2}{A}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

To second order, $\varepsilon^2 \approx 4u^2\,du^2/A^2$, and so

<Formula label="One over the denominator, to second order" tex="\frac{1}{A\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon}} \approx \frac{1}{A}\left[1 + \frac{u\,du}{A} + \frac{du^2}{2A} + \frac{3u^2\,du^2}{2A^2}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Multiply">

Multiply the numerator, divided by $A$, with the bracket, and keep terms up to second order:

<Formula label="The right side to second order" tex="\left[1 - \frac{u\,du}{A} + \frac{u^2\,d\theta^2}{2A}\right]\left[1 + \frac{u\,du}{A} + \frac{du^2}{2A} + \frac{3u^2\,du^2}{2A^2}\right] \approx 1 + \frac{du^2}{2A} + \frac{u^2\,du^2}{2A^2} + \frac{u^2\,d\theta^2}{2A}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

The terms of first order, $\pm u\,du/A$, have cancelled. Among the terms with $du^2$, the product of the two first-order terms gave $-u^2du^2/A^2$, which took away part of $3u^2du^2/(2A^2)$. The two terms with $du^2$ that are left combine, because $A + u^2 = 1$:

<Formula label="The du² terms combined" tex="\frac{du^2}{2A} + \frac{u^2\,du^2}{2A^2} = \frac{\left(A + u^2\right)du^2}{2A^2} = \frac{du^2}{2A^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compare the two sides">

<Formula label="The metric from the distance function" tex="1 + \frac{ds^2}{2a^2} = 1 + \frac{du^2}{2\left(1-u^2\right)^2} + \frac{u^2\,d\theta^2}{2\left(1-u^2\right)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad ds^2 = a^2\left[\frac{du^2}{\left(1 - u^2\right)^2} + \frac{u^2\,d\theta^2}{1 - u^2}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Near the centre of the disc, $u \ll 1$, the metric becomes $a^2(du^2 + u^2 d\theta^2)$: the flat plane in polar coordinates, with $a\,u$ as the radius. A number as a test of the expansion: take $u = 0.5$ and a step $du = 0.01$ with $d\theta = 0$. The metric gives $ds/a = 0.01/0.75 = 0.01333$. The exact distance function gives $ds/a = 0.01342$, which differs by less than 1%, as it should for a step that is small but not infinitely small.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Find the relation between $r$ and $u$ that turns the metric of part (b) into the Robertson–Walker metric above, and show that the two metrics are then identical. Start from the coefficients of $d\theta^2$, which must be equal. Can you now see why Klein needed the condition $x^2 + y^2 < 1$?

<Hint>

Setting the coefficients of $d\theta^2$ equal gives $r^2$ in terms of $u$. Then work out $dr$ and $1 + r^2$ in terms of $u$, and check the other term of the metric.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-c" label="$r =$" expr="u/sqrt(1 - u^2)"
  vars={{ u: [0.05, 0.9] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "u", hint: "Then the coefficients of dθ² would be r² = u² in one metric and u²/(1 − u²) in the other. They must be equal." },
    { expr: "u/(1 - u^2)", hint: "The coefficients of dθ² give r², not r. Take the square root." },
    { expr: "u^2/(1 - u^2)", hint: "That is r². Take the square root." },
    { expr: "u/sqrt(1 + u^2)", hint: "That has the form of the inverse relation, u = r/√(1 + r²), with the letters exchanged. Solve r² = u²/(1 − u²) for r." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The angle $\theta$ is the same in both descriptions, so the coefficients of $d\theta^2$ fix the relation between $r$ and $u$. The term with $dr^2$ is then a test with no freedom left.

<Step title="The relation between r and u">

<Formula label="Matching the angular terms" tex="r^2 = \frac{u^2}{1 - u^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{u}{\sqrt{1 - u^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The radial term">

Differentiate, and work out $1 + r^2$:

<Formula label="The pieces of the radial term" tex="dr = \frac{du}{\left(1 - u^2\right)^{3/2}}, \qquad 1 + r^2 = \frac{1}{1 - u^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c) (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="The radial terms agree" tex="\frac{dr^2}{1 + r^2} = \frac{du^2}{\left(1 - u^2\right)^3}\left(1 - u^2\right) = \frac{du^2}{\left(1 - u^2\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c) (our own solution)" />

This is the first term of the metric of part (b). Both terms agree, so Klein's model is the two-dimensional open Robertson–Walker space, drawn in other coordinates.

</Step>

<Step title="Why the disc stops at 1">

As $u$ goes from $0$ towards $1$, $r = u/\sqrt{1 - u^2}$ goes from $0$ to infinity. The inside of the unit disc is therefore the whole infinite open space, each point once. The edge $u = 1$ is not part of the space: it stands for $r \to \infty$, and it is infinitely far from every point. Outside the disc the square roots of the distance function would be imaginary, and there is nothing of the space left to put there.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Turned around, the relation is $u = r/\sqrt{1 + r^2}$, which is below 1 for every $r$. In the coordinate $\psi$ of Problem 4, with $r = \sinh\psi$, it is $u = \tanh\psi$. A point at the proper distance $a\,\psi$ from the centre with $\psi = 3$ already sits at $u = 0.995$: the far regions of the infinite space are crowded against the edge of the disc.

</Step>

</Solution>
