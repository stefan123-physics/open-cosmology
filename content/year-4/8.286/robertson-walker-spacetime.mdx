Lecture 13 picks up where Lecture 12 stopped. The space part of the metric of a homogeneous, isotropic universe is known. Now time is added, so that the metric describes **spacetime**, the basic object of general relativity. On the way, Guth has to answer a question special relativity never raised: once gravity is present, *who* measures the intervals the metric describes?

## Where Lecture 12 left off

The Robertson–Walker metric of space covers all three geometries at once: $k > 0$ is closed, $k < 0$ open, $k = 0$ flat. Isotropy about the origin is clear from the angular part, which is the metric of a sphere of radius $a(t)\,r$. Once homogeneity is shown, isotropy about the origin means isotropy about every point. Homogeneity is harder to see, because the origin looks special.

For the closed case Guth sketches an algebraic proof (slide 3): map any point $(r_0, \theta_0, \phi_0)$ to the origin by going to the four Euclidean coordinates $(x, y, z, w)$ of the embedding space, rotating there, and coming back with the same formulas. Rotations preserve the four-dimensional distances, so the new coordinates have the same metric. The result is a set of algebraic formulas for $(r', \theta', \phi')$ in terms of $(r, \theta, \phi)$. Algebraic identities that hold for positive $k$ also hold for negative $k$, so, Guth argues, the same map proves that the open universe is homogeneous too. He does not write it out, since the algebra gets messy.

Three further facts are recalled (slide 4); only the second was checked by a calculation, in Lecture 12:

- **Every** homogeneous, isotropic three-dimensional space can be given coordinates in which its metric is the Robertson–Walker metric. Other coordinates are possible, but nothing new can appear.
- For $k > 0$ the universe is finite; for $k \le 0$ it is infinite, since the distance from the origin grows without limit as $r$ grows.
- The two-dimensional open universe is exactly the Gauss–Bolyai–Lobachevsky geometry (an optional homework problem maps Klein's coordinates onto Robertson–Walker ones).

## From special relativity to curved spacetime

Lecture 12 ended with the interval of special relativity. Shrunk to two events infinitesimally close together, it becomes the metric of special relativity, the **Minkowski metric**, which Guth writes on the board:

<Formula label="Minkowski metric" tex="ds^2 = dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2 - c^2\,dt^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 8 (board)" />

General relativity keeps Gauss's idea that $ds^2$ is a quadratic expression in the coordinate differentials. But the coordinates themselves change meaning:

- In special relativity, the coordinates of an inertial frame **are** distances and times. You hardly need the metric.
- In general relativity, coordinates are **arbitrary labels** for points of spacetime. No coordinate system on a curved spacetime can measure distances directly, so only the metric turns coordinate differences into physical distances and times.

So there is no privileged coordinate system, and any change of coordinates is allowed. The rule is that the metric is always changed along with the coordinates, so that $ds^2$ between any two nearby events keeps its value: **$ds^2$ is coordinate-invariant** (transcript p. 9).

## Gravity depends on your point of view

In special relativity, $ds^2$ is defined by what inertial observers measure: observers with no forces on them. In general relativity that definition fails, because gravity is not treated as a force like the others. Non-gravitational forces (electric forces, say) are handled much as in special relativity, but gravity becomes part of the geometry of spacetime.

The expanding universe already shows the problem. A comoving observer feels no force at all: as far as she is concerned she is standing still. Yet she sees every other comoving observer accelerating relative to her, as the expansion rate changes (in the matter-dominated models derived so far, it slows down). So she says gravity acts on everyone except herself, and each of them says the same. Whether a gravitational force acts **depends on the frame** (transcript pp. 10–11).

## The equivalence principle

The classic illustration, which also motivated Einstein, is an elevator; Guth points out that Ryden's textbook, the one the course uses, discusses it too (transcript p. 11).

- **Cut the cable.** If the elevator falls freely, everything inside falls with the same acceleration. A passenger can lift his feet and hover; groceries he lets go of float in front of him. Inside, there is no sign of gravity, although from the ground we would say gravity is clearly acting.
- **Rocket in empty space.** Now let the elevator stand on the ground floor: the passenger is pressed to the floor and must hold up his groceries. Then move the same elevator to empty space and tow it with a rocket accelerating at $g$. Inside, he feels exactly the same.

In both pairs, one situation has gravity and the other has none, yet the passenger cannot tell which one he is in. This is the **equivalence principle**: an accelerating frame without gravity is physically equivalent to a gravitational field. Everything gravity does to a physical system can therefore be found by working out what acceleration does to it.

<Callout kind="warning" title="Only in small regions">
Real gravitational fields are never perfectly uniform, so a single accelerating frame cannot cancel gravity everywhere at once. The leftover differences are **tidal effects**. Strictly, the equivalence principle holds in infinitesimal regions (transcript p. 13). (That is enough for the metric, which only describes infinitesimal separations.)
</Callout>

## Free-falling observers and the meaning of ds²

The special class of observers can now be named. It cannot be "no forces at all", since whether gravity acts depends on the frame. Instead it is observers with **no non-gravitational forces** acting on them, like the comoving observers of our universe. They are called **free-falling observers**: free fall in the everyday sense and simply floating in empty space cannot be told apart.

With that, $ds^2$ has the same meaning as in special relativity, with inertial observers replaced by local free-falling ones (transcript pp. 13–14; Lecture 14 slide 1 summarises it):

- $ds^2 > 0$: there are free-falling observers for whom the two events happen at the same time; $ds^2$ is the square of the distance they measure between them.
- $ds^2 < 0$: there are free-falling observers for whom the two events happen at the same place; $ds^2$ is $-c^2$ times the square of the time between them on their clocks.
- $ds^2 = 0$: a light pulse can travel from one event to the other.

## Adding time to the Robertson–Walker metric

Guth writes down the answer first and then justifies it. The spacetime metric of a homogeneous, isotropic universe is the spatial metric with one extra term:

<Formula label="Robertson–Walker spacetime metric" tex="ds^2 = -c^2\,dt^2 + a^2(t)\left[\frac{dr^2}{1 - k r^2} + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 14 (board); also Lecture 14 slide 1" />

Two special cases check the terms that are there.

**Case 1: $dt = 0$.** Only spatial separations remain. These must be described by the Robertson–Walker spatial metric, because that is the most general description of a homogeneous, isotropic space.

**Case 2: $dr = d\theta = d\phi = 0$.** This is a comoving observer, sitting at fixed coordinates while time passes. The metric gives

<Formula label="A comoving observer's clock" tex="ds^2 = -c^2\,dt^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\tau = dt" cite="reading1" at="p. 15 (argued in words; the dτ notation is ours)" />

so the proper time on her wristwatch equals the change in $t$. That is exactly what we want, because **cosmic time was defined** as the time on the wristwatches of comoving observers. A coefficient other than $c^2$ would make the watches and cosmic time tick at different rates.

<WorkedExample title="A traveller's watch in a flat universe">
<Problem>
In a flat universe ($k = 0$), a traveller leaves a comoving observer and moves radially, adjusting her coordinate speed $dr/dt$ so that her **physical** speed $a(t)\,dr/dt$ stays $0.6c$. She travels for 10 years of cosmic time. How much time do the comoving observer's watch and her own watch show?
</Problem>
<Step title="The comoving observer">
Case 2 above: $d\tau = dt$, so the comoving watch shows 10 years.
</Step>
<Step title="The traveller">
Along her path $d\theta = d\phi = 0$ and $dr = \dot r\,dt$, so $ds^2 = -c^2dt^2 + a^2\dot r^2\,dt^2$. Setting $ds^2 = -c^2d\tau^2$ gives $d\tau = dt\sqrt{1 - a^2\dot r^2/c^2} = dt\sqrt{1 - 0.36} = 0.8\,dt$.
</Step>
<Step title="Answer">
Her watch shows 8 years. Locally, the expanding universe gives back the familiar special-relativity time dilation, with $a\,\dot r$ as the speed measured by the comoving observers she passes.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Why there are no dt·dr terms

A difficult roommate could ask: what about terms mixing time and space, $dt\,dr$, $dt\,d\theta$ or $dt\,d\phi$? "It wasn't in the book" is not a good answer. The real reason is that such a term would break **isotropy**: $dr$ changes sign when you reverse direction, so a $dt\,dr$ term treats "outward" and "inward" differently.

Guth makes this concrete with a thought experiment (pp. 16–18). Tweedledee and Tweedledum stand at a point with $r \ne 0$ and synchronise their watches with cosmic time. One sets off towards larger $r$, the other towards smaller $r$, both at the same coordinate speed $v = |dr/dt|$. Each stops when the local cosmic-time clocks read an agreed final time $t_f$, and reads his own watch.

With a $dt\,dr$ term, $dr = +v\,dt$ for one and $-v\,dt$ for the other, so the term adds to $ds^2$ for one and subtracts for the other. Their watches disagree, and the two directions could be told apart by measuring time dilation. In an isotropic universe that must be impossible, so the $dt\,dr$ term is not allowed. The same argument removes $dt\,d\theta$ and $dt\,d\phi$.

That justifies every term. The Robertson–Walker spacetime metric is the complete metric of a homogeneous, isotropic universe, and the rest of the course uses it.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Where this leads">
With the metric fixed, the question becomes how things move in it. The second half of Lecture 13 derives the **geodesic equation**, which gives the paths of free particles and light in any metric. Lecture 14 tries it on the Schwarzschild metric; applying it to this metric is an 8.286 homework problem, which shows how moving particles slow down as the universe expands.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Any homogeneous, isotropic space can be written with the Robertson–Walker metric; for $k > 0$ it is finite, for $k \le 0$ infinite.
- In general relativity coordinates are arbitrary labels; the metric changes with them so that $ds^2$ is coordinate-invariant.
- Whether gravity acts depends on the frame (equivalence principle, valid in small regions). The special observers are **free-falling** ones, with no non-gravitational forces acting.
- $ds^2$ means the same as in special relativity, measured by local free-falling observers.
- The spacetime metric is $ds^2 = -c^2dt^2 + a^2(t)\left[\frac{dr^2}{1-kr^2} + r^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)\right]$. The $-c^2dt^2$ term makes comoving watches read cosmic time; isotropy forbids $dt\,dr$, $dt\,d\theta$ and $dt\,d\phi$ terms.
