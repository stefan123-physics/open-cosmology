[Lecture 9](/courses/8-286/age-closed-universe) ended with the closed universe. Lecture 10 begins with a short review of the closed universe and then finishes the job: the **open** universe, and then the age of any matter-dominated universe, before turning to the geometry of curved space on the [next page](/courses/8-286/non-euclidean-geometry).

## The open universe

For an open universe $k < 0$, so the closed-universe variable $a/\sqrt k$ would be imaginary. Guth avoids that by defining a positive constant:

<Formula label="Open universe: a positive constant" tex="\kappa \equiv -k > 0, \qquad \tilde a \equiv \frac{a}{\sqrt\kappa}" cite="reading2" at="slide 5" />

The rest of the algebra parallels the closed case. Where the closed universe had sines and cosines, the open one gets **hyperbolic** sines and cosines:

<Formula label="Evolution of an open universe" tex="c\,t = \alpha\,(\sinh\theta - \theta), \qquad \frac{a}{\sqrt\kappa} = \alpha\,(\cosh\theta - 1)" cite="reading2" at="slide 5" />

The order of the terms is fixed by positivity: $\sinh\theta > \theta$ and $\cosh\theta > 1$ for $\theta > 0$, just as $\theta > \sin\theta$ and $1 > \cos\theta$ in the closed case. (The slide and the lecture at first say "hypergeometric"; Guth corrects himself to the hyperbolic functions.)

Because $\cosh\theta$ grows without limit, an open universe expands forever. For large $\theta$, $\sinh\theta \approx \cosh\theta$, so $a$ grows in proportion to $t$: the expansion settles into a constant rate.

## Three kinds of universe on one graph

Dividing $a/\sqrt{|k|}$ and $ct$ by $\alpha$ removes every parameter, so all closed universes collapse onto one curve, and all open ones onto another:

The flat curve is a special case: $k = 0$, so its normalisation on these axes is a matter of convention, chosen so that it looks like the limit of the other two. What matters is the shape near the start: **all three curves merge at early times**. Every matter-dominated universe starts out looking flat, with $\Omega$ very close to 1. Guth points out that this is the seed of the **flatness problem**, which he returns to later in the course; it becomes one of the motivations for inflation.

How $\Omega$ changes afterwards depends on the type. In a closed universe it rises from 1 toward infinity at maximum expansion (the density falls, but the critical density falls faster). In an open universe it falls from 1 toward zero.

## The age of any matter-dominated universe

Combining the three cases gives the age in terms of the two quantities astronomers measure, $H$ and $\Omega$:

<Formula label="Age of a matter-dominated universe" tex="|H|\,t = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\Omega}{2(1-\Omega)^{3/2}}\left[\dfrac{2\sqrt{1-\Omega}}{\Omega} - \sinh^{-1}\!\dfrac{2\sqrt{1-\Omega}}{\Omega}\right] & \Omega < 1 \\[2ex] \tfrac23 & \Omega = 1 \\[1ex] \dfrac{\Omega}{2(\Omega-1)^{3/2}}\left[\sin^{-1}\!\left(\pm\dfrac{2\sqrt{\Omega-1}}{\Omega}\right) \mp \dfrac{2\sqrt{\Omega-1}}{\Omega}\right] & \Omega > 1 \end{cases}" cite="reading2" at="slide 6" />

The three formulas look completely different, yet they join into one smooth curve. Guth's explanation: with complex numbers, the open and closed formulas are the *same* function: imaginary arguments turn trigonometric functions into hyperbolic ones.

<WorkedExample title="An open universe of matter alone">
<Problem>
Suppose the universe contained only matter, with $\Omega = 0.3$ (the measured matter density) and $H = 67.3$ km/s/Mpc. How old would it be?
</Problem>
<Step title="The open formula">
With $\Omega = 0.3$: $2\sqrt{1-\Omega}/\Omega = 2\sqrt{0.7}/0.3 = 5.578$ and $\sinh^{-1}(5.578) = 2.420$.
</Step>
<Step title="Put it together">
$Ht = \dfrac{0.3}{2(0.7)^{3/2}}\,(5.578 - 2.420) = 0.256 \times 3.158 = 0.809$.
</Step>
<Step title="In years">
$1/H = 14.5$ billion years, so $t = 0.809 \times 14.5 = 11.8$ billion years, older than the flat case (9.7), but measurements show the universe is very nearly flat, so this is not the answer either. The real universe needs dark energy.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- For an open universe, $\kappa = -k > 0$ and $ct = \alpha(\sinh\theta - \theta)$, $a/\sqrt\kappa = \alpha(\cosh\theta - 1)$: it expands forever, eventually at a constant rate.
- Scaled by $\alpha$, all closed universes follow one curve and all open ones another; all matter universes look flat at early times, the seed of the flatness problem.
- The age $Ht$ is one smooth function of $\Omega$: above $2/3$ for open universes, below for closed ones.
