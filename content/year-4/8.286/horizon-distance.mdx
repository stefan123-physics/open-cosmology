The universe has a finite age and light has a finite speed, so there must be a limit to how far we can see. Like a sailor at sea who sees only as far as the horizon, we can see only objects whose light has had time to reach us. The second half of Lecture 8 turns this into a number.

## Definition

The **horizon distance** is the *present* physical distance of the most distant objects that could, in principle, be seen, limited only by the speed of light, not by telescopes. "Physical" (or **proper**) distance means distance measured now by rulers at rest with respect to the local matter.

Why the *present* distance, when we see these objects as they were long ago? Guth's answer: it is a useful figure of merit for picturing the universe as it is now.

The naive guess, $c$ times the age, would be right only in a static universe. In an expanding one the answer is larger.

## Computing it

The earliest light we could see left its source at $t = 0$. Follow it in comoving coordinates, where it moves at the [coordinate speed of light](/courses/8-286/cosmological-redshift):

<Formula label="Light in comoving coordinates" tex="\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{c}{a(t)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 17" />

By today, $t_0$, it has crossed a coordinate distance $x_\text{max} = \int_0^{t_0} c\,dt/a(t)$. Multiply by today's scale factor to turn notches into metres:

<Formula label="Horizon distance (general)" tex="\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t_0) = a(t_0)\int_0^{t_0}\frac{c}{a(t)}\,dt" cite="reading1" at="p. 18" />

For the flat matter-dominated universe, $a = b\,t^{2/3}$:

<Formula label="Horizon distance, flat matter-dominated" tex="\ell_{p,\text{horizon}} = b\,t_0^{2/3}\int_0^{t_0}\frac{c\,dt}{b\,t^{2/3}} = b\,t_0^{2/3}\cdot\frac{3c\,t_0^{1/3}}{b} = 3c\,t_0 = 2c\,H_0^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 19–20; result summarised on Lecture 9 slide 2" />

The constant $b$, with its notch units, cancels, as it must. The factor 3 arises because light covered much of its path early on, when everything was much closer together, and the source has been receding ever since.

This answers a puzzle a student raised in the lecture: has the source moved three times its own distance? No: when the light left, the source was at essentially zero distance from us. The light first receded, then came back.

<WorkedExample title="How far is the horizon?">
<Problem>
Using the flat matter-dominated model with $H_0 = 67.3$ km/s/Mpc, estimate the horizon distance.
</Problem>
<Step title="Hubble length">
$c/H_0 = 299{,}792\ \text{km/s} \,/\, 67.3\ \text{km/s/Mpc} = 4450$ Mpc.
</Step>
<Step title="Horizon">
$\ell_{p,\text{horizon}} = 2c/H_0 = 8.9$ Gpc, about 29 billion light-years.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare with c times the age">
The model age is $9.7$ billion years, so $ct_0$ is 9.7 billion light-years; the horizon is three times that. (Guth gives the formula, not this number; with dark energy included, the real horizon is larger still.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Did everything start in contact?

At $t = 0$ the scale factor vanishes, so all distances were zero. Could every part of the universe once have exchanged signals? Guth's answer: nobody knows what happened at the singularity itself. But start the calculation at a small time $\varepsilon$ instead of 0, and the horizon distance changes only slightly, shrinking to zero change as $\varepsilon \to 0$. Although separations go to zero at early times, the recession velocities ($H = 2/(3t)$) grow without limit. Light needs exactly the time the integral says, so the horizon stays finite. This finite horizon will become important when we meet the **horizon problem**, one of the motivations for inflation.

## Summary

- The horizon distance is the present physical distance of the most distant objects whose light could have reached us.
- In general $\ell_{p,\text{horizon}} = a(t_0)\int_0^{t_0} c\,dt/a(t)$; for a flat matter-dominated universe it is $3ct_0 = 2c/H_0$.
- It exceeds $ct_0$ because light covered much of its path when the universe was small; the earliest light first moved away from us before turning back.
- The horizon stays finite even though all distances vanish at $t = 0$, because recession speeds then grow without limit.
