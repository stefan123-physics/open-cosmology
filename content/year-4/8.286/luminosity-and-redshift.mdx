The second calculation of Lecture 19 is the one behind the discovery of acceleration itself. In 1998 two teams used **type Ia supernovae** as standard candles to trace the expansion rate back in time. Looking back about 5–6 billion years, they found the expansion was **slower** then than now.

What they actually measured was simply each supernova's **apparent brightness** against its **redshift**: for a standard candle, brightness stands in for distance. So the question for us is: as a function of the cosmological parameters, what intensity should we expect for a source of known power at redshift $z$? Guth stresses that the formula we are about to derive is exactly the one the teams fitted to their data.

Nothing here is really new (the class has calculated intensities before), but now the equations appear in full, with vacuum energy, and allowing for curvature. In the end, Guth says, the curvature of our universe turns out to be negligible as far as anyone can tell: it is not there at the level of one part in 100, though it could still be there at about one part in 1,000, and people keep looking.

## The metric in terms of an angle

Start from the Robertson–Walker metric:

<Formula label="Robertson–Walker metric" tex="ds^2 = -c^2dt^2 + a^2(t)\left\{\frac{dr^2}{1 - kr^2} + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right\}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 13–14 (board)" />

Light from the source to us travels radially, so it pays to simplify the radial part. For a flat universe nothing needs doing; for open and closed universes the best variable differs. Guth takes the **closed** case ($k > 0$) and introduces an angle $\psi$, which, traced back, is the angle from the $w$-axis used when the closed universe was first built as a 3-sphere in four dimensions:

<Formula label="Radial angle" tex="\sin\psi = \sqrt k\,r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\psi = \frac{\sqrt k\,dr}{\cos\psi} = \frac{\sqrt k\,dr}{\sqrt{1 - kr^2}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 14 (board)" />

The radial term of the metric is exactly the square of this, so

<Formula label="Closed metric with angle ψ" tex="ds^2 = -c^2dt^2 + \tilde a^2(t)\left\{d\psi^2 + \sin^2\psi\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right\}, \qquad \tilde a(t) = \frac{a(t)}{\sqrt k}" cite="reading1" at="p. 14 (board)" />

The tilde only distinguishes the new scale factor from the old one; starting from this metric, you could drop it. A student asks whether $a$ and $\tilde a$ have different units. They do. In Guth's convention $r$ is a coordinate distance in notches, so $a$ is in metres per notch; $\psi$ is an angle and naturally dimensionless, so $\tilde a$ is simply a length.

## Spreading the light over a sphere

Put the source at the centre ($\psi = 0$), and imagine a sphere around it, drawn in comoving coordinates so that our detector, of area $A$, lies on its surface at $\psi = \psi_d$. The fraction of the light that hits the detector is the detector's area divided by the sphere's area, and the sphere's area must be computed from the **metric**.

On the sphere only $\theta$ and $\phi$ vary. The angular part of the metric is the usual one for a sphere, multiplied by the square of the sphere's radius, which is therefore $\tilde a\sin\psi$. We evaluate it **today**, $t_0$, because the detector has area $A$ today and the comparison only makes sense if all distances are measured at the same time. So the fraction is

<Formula label="Fraction of the light reaching the detector" tex="\frac{A}{4\pi\,\tilde a^2(t_0)\,\sin^2\psi_d}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 15–16 (board)" />

The redshift costs two more factors. Any clock at the source appears slowed by $1 + z_s$, where $z_s$ is the redshift of the source. One clock is the photon's frequency, so each photon arrives with its energy reduced by $1 + z_s$; the other is the rate at which photons arrive, reduced by the same factor:

<Formula label="Redshift" tex="1 + z_s = \frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_e)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 16 (board)" />

With $P$ the power emitted by the source, the power received and the measured **intensity** $J$ (power per unit area of detector) are

<Formula label="Received power and intensity" tex="P_\text{rec} = \frac{P}{(1+z_s)^2}\,\frac{A}{4\pi\,\tilde a^2(t_0)\sin^2\psi_d}, \qquad J = \frac{P_\text{rec}}{A} = \frac{P}{4\pi(1+z_s)^2\,\tilde a^2(t_0)\,\sin^2\psi_d}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 16–17 (board)" />

## Rewriting it in terms astronomers measure

That is effectively the answer, but $\tilde a(t_0)$ and $\psi_d$ mean nothing to an astronomer. Both can be traded for measured quantities.

**The scale factor.** From the definition of the curvature fraction, $\Omega_{k,0} = -kc^2/(a^2(t_0)H_0^2)$:

<Formula label="ã(t₀) from the curvature fraction" tex="\tilde a(t_0) = \frac{c\,H_0^{-1}}{\sqrt{-\Omega_{k,0}}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 17 (board)" />

For a closed universe $k > 0$, so $\Omega_{k,0}$ is negative and the square root is real. [The transcript reads as if the square root multiplies; from the definition of $\Omega_{k,0}$ it divides.]

**The angle.** Light travels on null geodesics. For a radial ray, $ds^2 = 0$:

<Formula label="Radial light ray" tex="-c^2dt^2 + \tilde a^2(t)\,d\psi^2 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d\psi}{dt} = \frac{c}{\tilde a(t)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 18 (board)" />

Earlier in the course this rule was motivated by intuition; here it follows directly from the metric, even in a curved universe. Integrating from emission to today gives $\psi_d = \int_{t_e}^{t_0} c\,dt/\tilde a(t)$.

To integrate over redshift instead of time, redshift is how astronomers label the past, use $1 + z = 1/x = \tilde a(t_0)/\tilde a(t)$ and differentiate:

<Formula label="Changing variable to redshift" tex="dz = -\frac{\tilde a(t_0)}{\tilde a^2(t)}\,\dot{\tilde a}\,dt = -\tilde a(t_0)\,H(t)\,\frac{dt}{\tilde a(t)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \psi_s = \frac{1}{\tilde a(t_0)}\int_0^{z_s}\frac{c\,dz}{H(z)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 18 (board)" />

The Friedmann equation from the first half of the lecture, $H^2 = H_0^2F(x)/x^4$ with $x = 1/(1+z)$, supplies $H(z)$. Substituting it and the expression for $\tilde a(t_0)$:

<Formula label="Angle to a source at redshift z_s" tex="\psi(z_s) = \sqrt{|\Omega_{k,0}|}\int_0^{z_s}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^3 + \Omega_{\text{rad},0}(1+z)^4 + \Omega_{\text{vac},0} + \Omega_{k,0}(1+z)^2}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 19 (board)" />

Put this $\psi$ and $\tilde a(t_0)$ into the formula for $J$, and the expected intensity depends only on the present Ω's, $H_0$, the redshift of the source and its intrinsic power. That, Guth says, is exactly the formula the supernova teams used in 1998, and to fit their data they needed a very significant vacuum energy, of order 60 or 70 percent.

## Flat and open universes

Guth works out only the closed case. The other two follow from it (this section is the page's own addition):

- **Open** ($k < 0$, $\Omega_{k,0} > 0$): the same steps with $\sinh\psi = \sqrt{|k|}\,r$ give the same formula with $\sin\psi_d$ replaced by $\sinh\psi_d$, and $\sqrt{-\Omega_{k,0}}$ by $\sqrt{\Omega_{k,0}}$.
- **Flat** ($\Omega_{k,0} \to 0$): $\psi_d$ becomes tiny and $\sin\psi_d \approx \psi_d$, so $\tilde a(t_0)\sin\psi_d \to (c/H_0)\int_0^{z_s}dz/E(z)$, where $E(z) = H(z)/H_0$ is the square root in the integral above.

Astronomers package all three cases into a **luminosity distance** $d_L$, defined so that $J = P/(4\pi d_L^2)$ looks like the inverse-square law of a static, flat space. Comparing with the formula above, $d_L = (1 + z_s)\,\tilde a(t_0)\sin\psi_d$.

<WorkedExample title="A supernova at z = 0.5">
<Problem>
A type Ia supernova is seen at $z = 0.5$. Take $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc and compare two flat universes: (A) matter only, (B) $\Omega_m = 0.3$, $\Omega_\text{vac} = 0.7$. Find the luminosity distance in each, and how much fainter the supernova looks in (B). Then check how much a slight closed curvature, $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.31$, $\Omega_{\text{vac},0} = 0.70$ (so $\Omega_{k,0} = -0.01$), changes model (B).
</Problem>
<Step title="(A) Matter only">
$E(z) = (1+z)^{3/2}$, so $\int_0^{0.5}dz/E = 2(1 - 1/\sqrt{1.5}) = 0.3670$. Then $H_0d_L/c = 1.5 \times 0.3670 = 0.5505$. With $c/H_0 = 4283$ Mpc, $d_L = 2358$ Mpc.
</Step>
<Step title="(B) Matter + vacuum energy">
$E(z) = \sqrt{0.3(1+z)^3 + 0.7}$. Numerically, $\int_0^{0.5}dz/E = 0.4410$, so $H_0d_L/c = 0.6615$ and $d_L = 2833$ Mpc.
</Step>
<Step title="How much fainter">
$J \propto 1/d_L^2$, so $J_B/J_A = (0.5505/0.6615)^2 = 0.69$. The supernova delivers only 69% of the light expected in a decelerating matter-only universe.
</Step>
<Step title="A little curvature">
Now $\psi_d = \sqrt{0.01}\int_0^{0.5}dz/E = 0.0440$ radians, a small angle, as the closed universe is huge compared with the distance to the source. $\tilde a(t_0) = (c/H_0)/0.1$, so $H_0d_L/c = 1.5 \times \sin(0.0440)/0.1 = 0.6603$, only 0.2% below the flat value. Curvature at the 1% level hardly changes the brightness at this redshift; the vacuum energy is what matters.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="Later developments">
Larger supernova samples since 2013 (for example Pantheon+ in 2022 and the Dark Energy Survey's five-year sample in 2024), analysed with this same formula, still require a large vacuum-like energy: on their own, roughly two-thirds of the critical density (about 65–70%). Combined with galaxy-survey data from DESI (2024–25), some of these samples also hint that dark energy may change slowly with time; that question is still open.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The 1998 teams measured the intensity of type Ia supernovae against redshift; for standard candles, intensity measures distance.
- In a closed universe, with $\sin\psi = \sqrt k\,r$ and $\tilde a = a/\sqrt k$, the light spreads over a sphere of area $4\pi\tilde a^2(t_0)\sin^2\psi_d$, and redshift costs two factors of $1+z_s$: $J = P/[4\pi(1+z_s)^2\tilde a^2(t_0)\sin^2\psi_d]$.
- $\tilde a(t_0) = cH_0^{-1}/\sqrt{-\Omega_{k,0}}$, and the null-geodesic condition $d\psi/dt = c/\tilde a$ gives $\psi_s = \frac{1}{\tilde a(t_0)}\int_0^{z_s}c\,dz/H(z)$, with $H(z)$ from the Ω's.
- This is the formula fitted to the supernova data; it required a vacuum energy of order 60–70% of the critical density.
