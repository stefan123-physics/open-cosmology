The flat universe gave a simple power law. A [closed universe](/courses/8-286/open-closed-flat), which expands to a maximum size and then recollapses, needs more work. Guth sets up the problem at the end of Lecture 8 and finishes it in Lecture 9, arriving at a beautiful result: the universe's size traces a **cycloid**.

## Setting up the equation

Start from the Friedmann equation with pressureless matter, for which $\rho a^3$ is constant:

<Formula label="Friedmann equation, matter only" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2}, \qquad \rho\,a^3 = \text{const}" cite="reading2" at="slide 2" />

For a closed universe $k > 0$. Strip away the arbitrary notch units by choosing new variables that are both lengths:

<Formula label="Rescaled variables" tex="\tilde a \equiv \frac{a}{\sqrt k}, \qquad \tilde t \equiv c\,t, \qquad \left(\frac{d\tilde a}{d\tilde t}\right)^2 = \frac{2\alpha}{\tilde a} - 1, \qquad \alpha \equiv \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\rho\,\tilde a^3}{c^2}" cite="reading2" at="slide 2" />

Since $\rho a^3$ and $k$ are constant, so is $\alpha$; it has units of length and is the only parameter left.

## Solving it

Separate the variables and integrate from the Big Bang ($\tilde t = 0$, $\tilde a = 0$) to an arbitrary time $\tilde t_f$:

<Formula label="Separated integral" tex="\tilde t_f = \int_0^{\tilde a_f}\frac{\tilde a\,d\tilde a}{\sqrt{2\alpha\tilde a - \tilde a^2}}" cite="reading2" at="slide 3" />

(Near $\tilde a = 0$ the integrand behaves like $\sqrt{\tilde a}$, so the integral converges, Guth's reminder to always check.) Two substitutions crack it:

1. **Complete the square:** $2\alpha\tilde a - \tilde a^2 = \alpha^2 - (\tilde a - \alpha)^2$, and set $x = \tilde a - \alpha$.
2. **Trigonometric substitution:** $x = -\alpha\cos\theta$, so $\sqrt{\alpha^2 - x^2} = \alpha\sin\theta$ and the integrand becomes simply $\alpha(1 - \cos\theta)\,d\theta$.

Integrating, and matching the upper limits ($\tilde a_f - \alpha = -\alpha\cos\theta_f$), gives the **parametric solution**:

<Formula label="Evolution of a closed universe" tex="c\,t = \alpha\,(\theta - \sin\theta), \qquad \frac{a}{\sqrt k} = \alpha\,(1 - \cos\theta), \qquad 0 \le \theta \le 2\pi" cite="reading2" at="slide 4; range of θ from transcript p. 15" />

The angle $\theta$ is a parameter that drives both time and size. You cannot solve $\theta - \sin\theta = ct/\alpha$ for $\theta$ with ordinary functions (a computer can do it numerically), so this pair is "as good as it gets". (In the lecture the integral was first written down wrongly and students caught it, a useful reminder to check every step.)

## A rolling wheel

The pair of equations describes a **cycloid**: the path traced by a point on the rim of a wheel of radius $\alpha$ rolling without slipping along a line.

- **Horizontally:** after the wheel has turned through angle $\theta$, its contact point has moved $\alpha\theta$; the marked point sits $\alpha\sin\theta$ behind the centre, so $x = \alpha(\theta - \sin\theta) = ct$.
- **Vertically:** up $\alpha$ to the centre, then down $\alpha\cos\theta$, so $y = \alpha(1 - \cos\theta) = a/\sqrt k$.

Guth calls $\theta$ the **development angle**. For *every* closed universe it runs from 0 at the Big Bang, through $\pi$ at maximum size, to $2\pi$ at the Big Crunch.

## Lifetime and maximum size

The solution seems to have two parameters, $\alpha$ and $k$, but $k$ can be set to 1 by choosing the notch. Closed matter-only universes form a one-parameter family, labelled by $\alpha$:

<Formula label="Lifetime and maximum size" tex="t_\text{total} = \frac{2\pi\alpha}{c}, \qquad \frac{a_{\max}}{\sqrt k} = 2\alpha \quad (\theta = \pi)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 16–17" />

The geometric meaning of $a/\sqrt k$ (it will turn out to be the radius of curvature of space) comes in later lectures.

<WorkedExample title="Checking the early universe against the flat case">
<Problem>
Show that for small $\theta$ the closed universe grows like $a \propto t^{2/3}$, just like a flat universe.
</Problem>
<Step title="Expand for small θ">
$\sin\theta \approx \theta - \theta^3/6$ and $\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2$. So $ct \approx \alpha\theta^3/6$ and $a/\sqrt k \approx \alpha\theta^2/2$.
</Step>
<Step title="Eliminate θ">
From the first, $\theta = (6ct/\alpha)^{1/3}$. Then $a/\sqrt k \approx \tfrac{\alpha}{2}(6ct/\alpha)^{2/3}$, which is proportional to $t^{2/3}$, the dashed curve in the plot.
</Step>
<Step title="Why">
Early on the density is so high that the curvature term $kc^2/a^2$ in the Friedmann equation is negligible compared with $\tfrac{8\pi}{3}G\rho \propto 1/a^3$. Every matter-dominated universe starts out looking flat.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- With $\tilde a = a/\sqrt k$ and $\tilde t = ct$, the closed-universe Friedmann equation becomes $(d\tilde a/d\tilde t)^2 = 2\alpha/\tilde a - 1$ with one length parameter $\alpha$.
- Completing the square and a trigonometric substitution give $ct = \alpha(\theta - \sin\theta)$, $a/\sqrt k = \alpha(1 - \cos\theta)$: a cycloid.
- The development angle runs from 0 (Big Bang) through $\pi$ (maximum size $2\alpha$) to $2\pi$ (Big Crunch, at $t = 2\pi\alpha/c$).
- At early times the solution matches the flat-universe law $a \propto t^{2/3}$.
