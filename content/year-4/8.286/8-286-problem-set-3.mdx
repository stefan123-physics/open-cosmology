## The model that this set builds on

Three of the four problems change or extend the Newtonian model of the lesson on the expanding sphere. Here is that model in short. At an initial time $t_i$ a sphere of radius $R_{\max,i}$ is filled evenly with matter of mass density $\rho_i$, and every particle moves away from the centre as Hubble's law says:

<Formula label="The initial Hubble flow" tex="\vec v_i = H_i\,\vec r" cite="reading2" at="slide 1" />

A particle that starts at radius $r_i$ is at radius $r(r_i, t)$ at time $t$. It feels only the mass $M(r_i)$ inside its own radius, and that mass never changes:

<Formula label="The motion of one shell of the sphere" tex="\ddot r = -\frac{G\,M(r_i)}{r^2}, \qquad M(r_i) = \frac{4\pi}{3}\,r_i^3\,\rho_i" cite="reading2" at="slide 2" />

The ratio $u(r_i, t) \equiv r(r_i, t)/r_i$ turns out to be the same for every $r_i$. It is the scale factor $a(t)$, with $a(t_i) = 1$ and $\dot a(t_i) = H_i$, and it has a conserved quantity:

<Formula label="The conserved quantity of the sphere" tex="E = \frac12\,\dot a^2 - \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\rho_i}{a}" cite="reading2" at="slide 3" />

A dot over a letter means a derivative with respect to time, and two dots mean the second derivative.

With the constant $k = -2E/c^2$ and the mass density $\rho(t)$, the same statement is the Friedmann equation:

<Formula label="The Friedmann equation" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}\,G\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2}" cite="reading3" at="slide 2" />

Its left-hand side is the square of the Hubble expansion rate, $H = \dot a/a$.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A cylindrical universe" points={25} mit="Problem 1">

Guth remarks that this problem comes from a quiz of 1994. Build the Newtonian model again, but for a universe that expands only in the $x$ and $y$ directions and does not move at all in the $z$ direction. In place of a sphere, take a cylinder that is infinitely long, with the $z$ axis as its axis and with initial radius $R_{\max,i}$.

Use cylindrical coordinates. The distance from the axis is $r$, and the vector $\vec r$ points straight away from the axis:

<Formula label="Distance from the axis of the cylinder" tex="r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}, \qquad \vec r = x\,\hat i + y\,\hat j, \qquad \hat r = \frac{\vec r}{r}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, pp. 1–2" />

Here $\hat i$ and $\hat j$ are the unit vectors along the $x$ and $y$ axes. At the initial time $t_i$ the cylinder has the same mass density $\rho_i$ everywhere, and a particle at $\vec r$ has the velocity $\vec v_i = H_i\,\vec r$. Note that this $\vec r$ has no $z$ part: nothing moves along the axis.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Gauss's law of gravity shows that the gravitational acceleration at any point has the form

<Formula label="Gravitational acceleration of the cylinder" tex="\vec g = -\frac{A\,\mu}{r}\,\hat r" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2" />

where $\mu$ is the mass per unit of length (along $z$) inside the radius $r$, and $A$ is a constant. Find $A$.

<Hint>

Gauss's law needs a closed surface on which $\vec g$ is the same everywhere, or is parallel to the surface. Which surface fits a long cylinder?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Take a cylinder of radius $r$ and length $L$ around the $z$ axis. Nothing passes through its two flat ends, because $\vec g$ points towards the axis. How much mass is inside, written with $\mu$? What is the area of the curved side?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="$A =$" expr="2*G"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "4*pi*G", hint: "That is the whole right-hand side of Gauss's law per unit of mass. You still have to divide by the area of the curved side, which holds a factor 2 pi." },
    { expr: "G", hint: "That is the constant for a point mass, where the surface is a sphere. Here the surface is the curved side of a cylinder: work out the flux again." },
    { expr: "2*pi*G", hint: "Check the area of the curved side of a cylinder of radius r and length L: it is the circumference times L." },
    { expr: "-2*G", hint: "The minus sign is already written in the formula for the acceleration. A itself is positive, because gravity pulls towards the axis." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** By symmetry $\vec g$ points straight at the axis and its size depends only on $r$. Then Gauss's law gives $\vec g$ from the mass inside a well-chosen surface.

<Step title="Gauss's law">

The lesson on Newton's static universe gives the law:

<Formula label="Gauss's law of gravity" tex="\oint \vec g \cdot d\vec a = -4\pi G\,M_\text{enclosed}" cite="reading1" at="slide 1" />

</Step>

<Step title="A cylinder as the surface">

Take a cylinder of radius $r$ and length $L$ around the $z$ axis. On the flat ends $\vec g$ lies in the surface, so they add nothing. On the curved side, with area $2\pi r L$, $\vec g$ points straight inwards and has the same size $g$ everywhere. The mass inside is $\mu L$. So

<Formula label="Gauss's law for the cylinder" tex="-g \cdot 2\pi r L = -4\pi G\,\mu L \quad\Longrightarrow\quad g = \frac{2G\mu}{r} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A = 2G" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The length $L$ has cancelled, as it must for an endless cylinder. Units: $G\mu/r$ is $G$ times a mass divided by a length squared, the same as $GM/r^2$, an acceleration.

The acceleration falls only as $1/r$, more slowly than the $1/r^2$ of a sphere. That difference decides the fate of this universe in part (e).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

As in the lesson, $r(r_i, t)$ is the distance from the axis, at time $t$, of a particle that starts at distance $r_i$ at the time $t_i$. Find $\ddot r(r_i, t)$ in terms of $r$, $r_i$, $\rho_i$ and constants.

<Hint>

Which mass per unit of length pulls on the particle? Does that amount change while the cylinder expands?

</Hint>

<Hint>

As long as shells do not cross, the matter inside the particle's radius stays inside. Work out $\mu$ once, at the initial time, from $\rho_i$ and $r_i$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="$\ddot r =$" expr="-2*pi*G*rhoi*ri^2/r"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 3], rhoi: [0.5, 3], ri: [0.5, 2], r: [0.5, 6] }}
  names={{ rhoi: "\\rho_i", ri: "r_i" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-2*pi*G*rhoi*r", hint: "This takes the initial density inside the present radius r. The mass per length inside the particle's radius keeps its initial value, which is set by r_i." },
    { expr: "-4*pi*G*rhoi*ri^3/(3*r^2)", hint: "That is the result for the sphere. For the cylinder use the acceleration of part (a) and the mass per length." },
    { expr: "2*pi*G*rhoi*ri^2/r", hint: "Check the sign: gravity pulls the particle towards the axis, so the second derivative of r is negative." },
    { expr: "-2*G*rhoi*ri^2/r", hint: "Check the mass per length: it is the density times the area of a circle of radius r_i." },
    { expr: "-4*pi*G*rhoi*ri^2/r", hint: "Check the factors: the constant A is 2G and the area of the circle is pi times r_i squared." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same step as for the sphere: the matter inside a particle's radius stays inside, so the mass per length that pulls on it is fixed.

<Step title="The mass per length inside">

At the initial time, a piece of the cylinder of radius $r_i$ and length $L$ has volume $\pi r_i^2 L$, so the mass per length inside $r_i$ is $\mu = \pi r_i^2 \rho_i$. Every particle moves radially and those further out start faster, so shells do not cross (the lesson gives Guth's argument for the sphere, and it holds here too). So $\mu$ keeps this value.

</Step>

<Step title="The equation of motion">

The acceleration is radial, so $\ddot r = -A\mu/r$ with $A = 2G$:

<Formula label="The motion of one shell of the cylinder" tex="\ddot r = -\frac{2G\,\pi r_i^2\,\rho_i}{r} = -\frac{2\pi G\,\rho_i\,r_i^2}{r}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$G\rho_i$ is 1 divided by a time squared, and $r_i^2/r$ is a length, so the result is an acceleration. At the initial time, $r = r_i$ and $\ddot r = -2\pi G\rho_i\,r_i$: proportional to the distance from the axis, the same pattern as the Hubble flow.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Define

<Formula label="The rescaled radius" tex="u(r_i, t) \equiv \frac{r(r_i, t)}{r_i}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2" />

Show that $u(r_i, t)$ does not depend on $r_i$ at all. The cylinder then expands evenly, as the sphere did, and we may define the scale factor $a(t) \equiv u(r_i, t)$.

<Hint>

A function of time is fixed completely by a second-order differential equation together with its value and its first derivative at one moment. So look at three things: the equation for $u$, and $u$ and $\dot u$ at $t_i$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put $r = u\,r_i$ into the result of part (b) and divide by $r_i$. For the starting values use $r(r_i, t_i) = r_i$ and the Hubble flow.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** If neither the differential equation for $u$ nor its starting values contain $r_i$, then every $r_i$ has the same $u(t)$.

<Step title="The equation for u">

With $r = u\,r_i$ we have $\ddot r = r_i\,\ddot u$. Part (b) becomes

<Formula label="The rescaled equation of the cylinder" tex="r_i\,\ddot u = -\frac{2\pi G\,\rho_i\,r_i^2}{u\,r_i} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot u = -\frac{2\pi G\rho_i}{u}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

The equation holds no $r_i$.

</Step>

<Step title="The starting values">

At $t_i$ the particle is at $r = r_i$ and moves with speed $\dot r = H_i\,r_i$. Dividing by $r_i$:

<Formula label="Starting values of the rescaled radius" tex="u(r_i, t_i) = 1, \qquad \dot u(r_i, t_i) = H_i" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c) (our own solution)" />

These hold no $r_i$ either. The equation and both starting values are the same for every particle, so the solution is the same: $u$ depends on $t$ only, and $r(r_i, t) = a(t)\,r_i$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For the sphere, the $r_i^3$ of the mass cancelled against $r_i^3$ from rescaling a $1/r^2$ force. Here the $r_i^2$ of the mass per length cancels against $r_i^2$ from rescaling a $1/r$ force. In both cases Gauss's law makes the pull at the edge of a uniform body proportional to its size, and that is what keeps a Hubble flow a Hubble flow.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

Write the mass density $\rho(t)$ in terms of $\rho_i$ and $a(t)$. Then use it to write $\ddot a$ in terms of $a$, $\rho$ and constants.

<Hint>

The mass in a piece of the cylinder stays the same. How does the volume of the piece change when every distance from the axis grows by the factor $a$, and lengths along the axis do not change?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $\ddot a$, start from the equation for $u$ in part (c), which holds $\rho_i$, and replace $\rho_i$ by $\rho$ and $a$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d-rho" label="$\rho(t) =$" expr="rhoi/a^2"
  vars={{ rhoi: [0.5, 3], a: [0.5, 4] }}
  names={{ rhoi: "\\rho_i" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "rhoi/a^3", hint: "That is the sphere, which grows in three directions. This universe does not stretch along z." },
    { expr: "rhoi*a^2", hint: "The density must fall when the cylinder grows. The same mass fills a larger volume." },
    { expr: "rhoi/a", hint: "The cross-section of the cylinder is an area: it grows in both the x and the y direction." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-d-addot" label="$\ddot a =$" expr="-2*pi*G*rho*a"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 3], rho: [0.5, 3], a: [0.5, 4] }}
  names={{ rho: "\\rho" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-2*pi*G*rho/a", hint: "That would be right with the initial density in it. The question asks for the density at time t: replace the initial density by the density times a squared." },
    { expr: "-4*pi*G*rho*a/3", hint: "That is the result for the sphere. Start from the cylinder's own equation for u." },
    { expr: "2*pi*G*rho*a", hint: "Check the sign: gravity slows the expansion down." },
    { expr: "-2*pi*G*rho*a^3", hint: "Check the powers of a: the initial density is the density times a squared, and the equation for u has one power of a below the line." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Mass is conserved, and only two of the three directions stretch.

<Step title="The density">

A piece of the cylinder with initial radius $r_i$ and length $L$ holds the mass $\pi r_i^2 L\,\rho_i$. Later its radius is $a\,r_i$ and its length is still $L$, so its volume is $\pi a^2 r_i^2 L$:

<Formula label="Mass density of the cylindrical universe" tex="\rho(t) = \frac{\pi r_i^2 L\,\rho_i}{\pi a^2 r_i^2 L} = \frac{\rho_i}{a^2(t)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The equation for the scale factor">

Part (c) with $u = a$ reads $\ddot a = -2\pi G\rho_i/a$. Put in $\rho_i = \rho\,a^2$:

<Formula label="Deceleration of the cylindrical universe" tex="\ddot a = -\frac{2\pi G\,\rho\,a^2}{a} = -2\pi G\,\rho(t)\,a" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $t_i$, $a = 1$ and $\rho = \rho_i$, as it should be. The result has the same shape as the sphere's $\ddot a = -\tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\rho\,a$, with the number $2\pi$ in place of $4\pi/3$: again $\ddot a/a$ depends only on the density at that moment.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={5}>

Find a conserved quantity of the form

<Formula label="The form of the conserved quantity" tex="E = \frac12\,\dot a^2 + V(a)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), p. 2" />

What is $V(a)$? A constant may always be added to $V$: choose it so that $V = 0$ at $a = 1$, and write $V$ with $\rho_i$. Then decide: does this universe expand for ever, or does it collapse?

<Hint>

The lesson found the conserved quantity of the sphere by multiplying the equation for $\ddot a$ by $\dot a$. Try the same here, with the equation that holds $\rho_i$ (so that $a$ is the only thing that changes with time).

</Hint>

<Hint>

Which function of $a$ has the time derivative $\dot a/a$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the fate of the universe: $\tfrac12\dot a^2$ can never be negative. What does $V(a)$ do when $a$ becomes very large?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e-v" label="$V(a) =$" expr="2*pi*G*rhoi*log(a)"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 3], rhoi: [0.5, 3], a: [0.3, 5] }}
  names={{ rhoi: "\\rho_i" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-2*pi*G*rhoi*ln(a)", hint: "Check the sign. V must rise when a grows, because gravity slows the expansion: the first term of E then falls." },
    { expr: "-2*pi*G*rhoi/a", hint: "A potential that goes as 1 over a belongs to a force that goes as 1 over a squared. Here the right-hand side of the equation goes as 1 over a." },
    { expr: "2*pi*G*rhoi/a", hint: "A potential that goes as 1 over a belongs to a force that goes as 1 over a squared. Here the right-hand side of the equation goes as 1 over a." },
    { expr: "2*pi*G*rhoi*a", hint: "Take the derivative of your V with respect to a: it should give 2 pi G times the initial density divided by a, not a constant." },
    { expr: "4*pi*G*rhoi*ln(a)", hint: "Check the factor: the first term of E is one half of the square of the rate, so nothing is doubled." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-e-fate" label="This universe:" choices={["collapses in the end, whatever the value of $E$", "expands for ever, whatever the value of $E$", "expands for ever if $E > 0$ and collapses if $E < 0$, as the sphere does", "collapses only if it starts with $H_i = 0$"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Multiply the equation of motion by $\dot a$, so that both sides become time derivatives. Then look at how high the "hill" $V(a)$ goes.

<Step title="Integrate once">

Start from $\ddot a = -2\pi G\rho_i/a$ and multiply by $\dot a$:

<Formula label="The first integral of the cylinder's equation" tex="\dot a\,\ddot a + 2\pi G\rho_i\,\frac{\dot a}{a} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac12\,\dot a^2 + 2\pi G\rho_i \ln a\right] = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e) (our own solution)" />

So the conserved quantity and its potential are

<Formula label="Conserved quantity of the cylindrical universe" tex="E = \frac12\,\dot a^2 + V(a), \qquad V(a) = 2\pi G\rho_i \ln a" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e) (our own solution)" />

The logarithm is zero at $a = 1$, so this $V$ has the constant that the question asks for.

</Step>

<Step title="The fate of the universe">

For the sphere, $V = -\tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\rho_i/a$ rises towards zero and never gets above it: with $E > 0$ the sphere expands for ever. The logarithm is different. It rises without limit as $a$ grows. Since $\tfrac12\dot a^2 = E - V(a)$ cannot be negative, the expansion must stop where $V(a) = E$:

<Formula label="Largest size of the cylindrical universe" tex="a_{\max} = e^{E/(2\pi G\rho_i)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e) (our own solution)" />

At that moment $\dot a = 0$ while $\ddot a$ is still negative, so the cylinder turns around and falls back. **This universe always collapses**, however fast it starts.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$G\rho_i$ and $E$ are both 1 divided by a time squared, so the exponent is a pure number.

With $a(t_i) = 1$ the potential is zero at the start, so $E = \tfrac12 H_i^2$ and $a_{\max} = e^{H_i^2/(4\pi G\rho_i)}$. An example: take $H_i^2 = 8\pi G\rho_i$. Then $a_{\max} = e^2 = 7.39$: the cylinder grows to about 7.4 times its first radius and then falls back. A sphere with the same start has three times the speed squared that it needs to escape ($H_i^2 = \tfrac{8\pi}{3}G\rho_i$ is its borderline, the critical density), and expands for ever.

The reason is the slow $1/r$ fall of the cylinder's gravity, found in part (a): the work needed to move away from an endless line of mass grows without limit, so no starting speed is enough.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A flat universe with an unusual time evolution" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

A flat universe is filled with some strange kind of matter, and its scale factor grows as

<Formula label="The scale factor of Problem 2" tex="a(t) = b\,t^{3/4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 3" />

where $b$ is a constant.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the Hubble expansion rate $H(t)$ of this universe.

<Hint>

How does the lesson get the Hubble expansion rate from the scale factor?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$H(t) =$" expr="3/(4*t)"
  vars={{ t: [0.5, 10], b: [0.5, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*b/(4*t^(1/4))", hint: "That is the time derivative of the scale factor. The Hubble rate is that derivative divided by the scale factor, and b drops out." },
    { expr: "4/(3*t)", hint: "Check the power: the scale factor grows as the three-quarters power of the time." },
    { expr: "2/(3*t)", hint: "That is the flat universe filled with ordinary matter, with the two-thirds power. Here the power is three quarters." },
    { expr: "3*t/4", hint: "Check the units: a rate is 1 divided by a time." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The Hubble expansion rate is the rate of change of the scale factor, divided by the scale factor.

<Step title="Differentiate and divide">

<Formula label="Hubble expansion rate of Problem 2" tex="H(t) = \frac{\dot a}{a} = \frac{\tfrac34\,b\,t^{-1/4}}{b\,t^{3/4}} = \frac{3}{4t}" cite="reading3" at="slide 2 (H as the rate of change of a, divided by a)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The constant $b$ has cancelled, as it must: $b$ depends on the size of a notch, and $H$ can be measured. The same steps work for any power of the time: the lesson's flat universe filled with matter, $a \propto t^{2/3}$, has $H = 2/(3t)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

What is the mass density $\rho(t)$ of this universe? Guth adds a warning. The Friedmann equation for $(\dot a/a)^2$ holds for every kind of matter. The equation $\ddot a = -\tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\rho\,a$ changes when the matter has a pressure that matters, so it may not be used here.

<Hint>

Write down the Friedmann equation. What does "flat" say about one of its terms?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$\rho(t) =$" expr="27/(128*pi*G*t^2)"
  vars={{ G: [0.5, 3], t: [0.5, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "9/(64*pi*G*t^2)", hint: "This comes from the equation for the second derivative of a, which holds only for matter without pressure. Use the Friedmann equation." },
    { expr: "1/(6*pi*G*t^2)", hint: "That is the flat universe filled with ordinary matter, where H is 2/(3t). Use the H of part (a)." },
    { expr: "9/(16*t^2)", hint: "That is H squared. The Friedmann equation still has to be solved for the density." },
    { expr: "9/(32*pi*G*t^2)", hint: "Check the square of H: both the 3 and the 4 are squared." },
    { expr: "9/(32*pi*G*t)", hint: "Check the square of H: the 3, the 4 and the time are all squared." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a flat universe the Friedmann equation ties the density to the Hubble expansion rate, with nothing else in it.

<Step title="The Friedmann equation with k = 0">

Take the Friedmann equation from the start of this page. Flat means $k = 0$, so the last term is gone and $H^2 = \tfrac{8\pi}{3}G\rho$. This is the critical density of the lesson on open, closed and flat universes: $\rho = 3H^2/(8\pi G)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Put in the Hubble expansion rate">

<Formula label="Mass density of Problem 2" tex="\rho(t) = \frac{3}{8\pi G}\left(\frac{3}{4t}\right)^2 = \frac{27}{128\pi\,G\,t^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$1/(G t^2)$ is a mass density, since $G\rho$ is 1 divided by a time squared.

What would the forbidden equation have given? Here $\ddot a/a = \tfrac34\cdot\left(-\tfrac14\right)/t^2 = -3/(16t^2)$, and $\ddot a = -\tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\rho\,a$ would then give $\rho = 9/(64\pi G t^2)$. That is only two thirds of the right value. The two equations disagree, which shows that this matter is not the pressureless dust of the lesson: for dust they agree, and give $a \propto t^{2/3}$. A later lecture adds the pressure term that makes them agree again.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Energy and the Friedmann equation" points={30} mit="Problem 3">

The Friedmann equation (see the start of this page) came out of the conserved quantity $E$ of the sphere, with $k = -2E/c^2$. The formula for $E$ looks like a statement that energy is conserved, but $E$ itself is not an energy: with $a(t_i) = 1$ a pure number, as in this set, its unit is 1 divided by a time squared. This problem shows two ways in which $E$ is tied to a real energy. One is the energy of a single test particle that moves with the Hubble flow. The other is the total energy of the whole expanding sphere, whose radius is $R_{\max} = a(t)\,R_{\max,i}$.

For the test particle, of mass $m$ and initial radius $r_i$, define the physical energy

<Formula label="The physical energy of a test particle" tex="E_\text{phys} \equiv m\,r_i^2\,E" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, eq. (3), p. 4" />

The force of gravity on the particle can be written with an "effective" potential energy:

<Formula label="Force on the test particle and its effective potential energy" tex="\vec F = -\frac{G\,m\,M(r_i)}{r^2}\,\hat r = -\vec\nabla V_\text{eff}(r), \qquad V_\text{eff}(r) = -\frac{G\,m\,M(r_i)}{r}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, eqs. (4) and (5), p. 4" />

Here $M(r_i)$ is the mass that was inside the radius $r_i$ at the start, and $r$ is the particle's distance from the centre now.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Show that $E_\text{phys}$ equals the "effective" energy of the test particle,

<Formula label="The effective energy of the test particle" tex="E_\text{eff} = \frac12\,m v^2 + V_\text{eff}(r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, eq. (6), p. 4" />

<Hint>

Write the particle's speed $v$ and its distance $r$ with the scale factor and $r_i$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put in $M(r_i)$ from the model, and take the common factor $m\,r_i^2$ outside.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Everything in $E_\text{eff}$ can be written with $a$, $\dot a$ and $r_i$. Then $m\,r_i^2$ comes out as a common factor.

<Step title="Speed and distance of the particle">

The particle moves with the Hubble flow: $r = a(t)\,r_i$, so $v = \dot r = \dot a\,r_i$. The mass inside is $M(r_i) = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}r_i^3\rho_i$.

</Step>

<Step title="Put them into the effective energy">

<Formula label="The effective energy written with the scale factor" tex="E_\text{eff} = \frac12\,m\,\dot a^2 r_i^2 - \frac{G\,m}{a\,r_i}\cdot\frac{4\pi}{3}\,r_i^3\rho_i = m\,r_i^2\left[\frac12\,\dot a^2 - \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\rho_i}{a}\right] = m\,r_i^2\,E = E_\text{phys}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$E$ is 1 divided by a time squared, so $m\,r_i^2 E$ is a mass times a speed squared: an energy. (In Guth's notch convention, where the scale factor carries metres per notch, $r_i$ is in notches and $E$ in metres squared per notch squared per second squared instead; the notches cancel, and the product is still an energy.)

Why is $E_\text{eff}$ conserved? It is the true energy in a different, simpler problem: a particle that moves in the field of a point of mass $M(r_i)$ fixed at the centre. The force on our particle is exactly the same as in that problem at every moment, so its motion is the same, and the energy of that problem stays constant.

Guth explains why the word is "effective". The true potential energy of the particle is the work needed to bring it from far away to where it is now, through the whole sphere. That function changes with time, because the sphere expands, and so it gives no conserved energy. $V_\text{eff}$ gives the right force only at the place where the particle is, $r = a(t)\,r_i$, but that is all the particle ever feels.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Now the whole sphere. Cut it into thin shells of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$, each of volume

<Formula label="Volume of a thin shell" tex="dV = 4\pi r^2\,dr" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, eq. (7), p. 5" />

Show that the total kinetic energy $K$ of the sphere is

<Formula label="Total kinetic energy of the sphere" tex="K = c_K\,M\,R_{\max,i}^2\left\{\frac12\,\dot a^2(t)\right\}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), eq. (8), p. 5" />

where $M$ is the total mass of the sphere, $R_{\max,i}$ is its initial radius and $c_K$ is a pure number. Find $c_K$ and type it as a decimal number.

<Hint>

All the matter in one shell moves at the same speed. What is the kinetic energy of the shell, in terms of its mass and its speed?

</Hint>

<Hint>

It is easiest to label each shell by its initial radius $r_i$: its mass is $4\pi r_i^2\rho_i\,dr_i$ at all times, and its speed is $\dot a\,r_i$. Add up from $r_i = 0$ to $R_{\max,i}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Your integral holds $\rho_i$. Use the total mass $M$ to get rid of it.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$c_K =$" value="0.6" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-b-ck" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Kinetic energy adds up shell by shell, and every shell moves at one speed.

<Step title="One shell">

The shell that starts between $r_i$ and $r_i + dr_i$ has the mass $dM = 4\pi r_i^2\rho_i\,dr_i$, which never changes, and the speed $v = \dot a\,r_i$. Its kinetic energy is $\tfrac12\,dM\,v^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Add up the shells">

<Formula label="Kinetic energy of the sphere as an integral" tex="K = \int_0^{R_{\max,i}} \frac12\,\dot a^2 r_i^2 \cdot 4\pi r_i^2 \rho_i\,dr_i = \frac12\,\dot a^2 \cdot \frac{4\pi}{5}\,\rho_i\,R_{\max,i}^5" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Bring in the total mass">

The total mass is $M = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\rho_i R_{\max,i}^3$, so $\tfrac{4\pi}{5}\rho_i R_{\max,i}^5 = \tfrac35 M R_{\max,i}^2$:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy of the sphere" tex="K = \frac35\,M\,R_{\max,i}^2\left\{\frac12\,\dot a^2\right\}, \qquad c_K = \frac35 = 0.6" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The same integral over the present radius gives the same answer: the density is $\rho_i/a^3$, the speed at radius $r$ is $(\dot a/a)\,r$, and the sphere reaches to $a\,R_{\max,i}$. The powers of $a$ cancel ($a^{-3}\cdot a^{-2}\cdot a^{5} = 1$).

The number must be less than 1. If all the mass sat at the edge, where the speed is largest, $K$ would be $\tfrac12 M\,(\dot a R_{\max,i})^2$, which is $c_K = 1$. Most of the matter is further in and moves more slowly.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={10}>

Show that the total potential energy of the sphere can be written in the same way,

<Formula label="Total potential energy of the sphere" tex="U = c_U\,M\,R_{\max,i}^2\left\{-\frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\rho_i}{a}\right\}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c), eq. (9), p. 5" />

and that $c_U = c_K$. The total energy of the sphere is then

<Formula label="Total energy of the sphere" tex="E_\text{total} = c_K\,M\,R_{\max,i}^2\,E" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c), eq. (10), p. 5" />

Guth's suggestion: work out the energy needed to build the sphere, by bringing in one shell of matter at a time from far away.

<Hint>

Build the sphere at one fixed moment, with the density $\rho$ it has at that moment. Suppose a ball of radius $r$ is already there. How much energy does it take to bring a shell of mass $dM$ from infinity onto its surface?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The ball already built acts on the new shell as a point mass at the centre, so the energy is $-G\,M(r)\,dM/r$, with $M(r) = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}r^3\rho$ and $dM = 4\pi r^2\rho\,dr$. Add up from $r = 0$ to the present radius of the sphere.

</Hint>

<Hint>

To compare with the form asked for, write the present radius as $a\,R_{\max,i}$ and the present density as $\rho_i/a^3$, and use $M$ to get rid of one factor $\rho_i$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The potential energy of the sphere is the work done in putting it together. Each new shell falls onto a ball that acts as a point mass.

<Step title="One shell">

At one moment the sphere has radius $R = a\,R_{\max,i}$ and density $\rho = \rho_i/a^3$. Build it from the inside out. When the ball has reached radius $r$, its mass is $M(r) = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}r^3\rho$. The next shell, of mass $dM = 4\pi r^2\rho\,dr$, comes in from infinity to the distance $r$ from the centre. Outside the ball its pull is that of a point mass, so the energy of the system changes by

<Formula label="Energy of bringing in one shell" tex="dU = -\frac{G\,M(r)\,dM}{r} = -\frac{16\pi^2}{3}\,G\rho^2\,r^4\,dr" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Add up the shells">

<Formula label="Potential energy of a uniform sphere" tex="U = -\frac{16\pi^2}{3}\,G\rho^2\int_0^{R} r^4\,dr = -\frac{16\pi^2}{15}\,G\rho^2 R^5 = -\frac35\,\frac{G M^2}{R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

The last step uses $M = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\rho R^3$ twice.

</Step>

<Step title="Write it in the form asked for">

In $M\,R_{\max,i}^2\cdot\tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\rho_i/a$, replace $\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\rho_i$ by $M/R_{\max,i}^3$. That gives $G M^2/(a\,R_{\max,i}) = G M^2/R$. So

<Formula label="Potential energy of the sphere with the scale factor" tex="U = \frac35\,M\,R_{\max,i}^2\left\{-\frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\rho_i}{a}\right\}, \qquad c_U = \frac35 = c_K" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The total energy">

<Formula label="Total energy of the sphere, worked out" tex="E_\text{total} = K + U = \frac35\,M\,R_{\max,i}^2\left[\frac12\,\dot a^2 - \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\rho_i}{a}\right] = \frac35\,M\,R_{\max,i}^2\,E" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$-\tfrac35\,GM^2/R$ is the well-known gravitational energy of a ball of even density, which is a good sign. And the two numbers had to be equal: the total energy of the sphere is conserved and so is $E$, so $K + U$ can only be a constant times $E$ if $\dot a^2$ and $1/a$ enter in the same proportion as in $E$.

Compare with part (a). A particle on the edge of the sphere has $m\,R_{\max,i}^2\,E$, with no factor $\tfrac35$. For that particle the effective energy is the true energy, since no matter lies outside it. So the conserved quantity $E$, and with it the constant $k$ of the Friedmann equation, measures the energy of the expansion: positive $E$ (negative $k$) is a sphere with more kinetic energy than gravity can take away.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="A possible change to Newton's law of gravity" points={20} mit="Problem 4">

Guth notes that this problem was set in a quiz of 2011. Go back to the sphere of the lesson: mass density $\rho_i$ at the start, the Hubble flow $\vec v_i = H_i\,\vec r$, and $r(r_i, t)$ for the radius at time $t$ of a particle that starts at $r_i$. With Newton's law of gravity each particle has the acceleration $-G\,M(r_i)/r^2$ along $\hat r$, with $M(r_i) = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}r_i^3\rho_i$.

Now suppose that the law of gravity has one more term. It pushes outwards, it grows as the $n$th power of the distance from the centre, and its strength does not depend on any mass:

<Formula label="Gravity with a repulsive term" tex="\vec g = -\frac{G\,M(r_i)}{r^2(r_i, t)}\,\hat r + \gamma\,r^n(r_i, t)\,\hat r" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 6" />

where $\gamma$ is a constant. The radius then obeys

<Formula label="Equation of motion with the repulsive term" tex="\ddot r = -\frac{G\,M(r_i)}{r^2} + \gamma\,r^n" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 6" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

As in the lesson, define $u(r_i, t) \equiv r(r_i, t)/r_i$. Write the differential equation for $u$. On its right-hand side, $u$ must be the only thing that changes with time: write $r$ and the mass with $u$, $r_i$ and $\rho_i$.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Step title="Put in r = u r_i">

The left-hand side is $\ddot r = r_i\,\ddot u$. On the right, $r^2 = u^2 r_i^2$ and $r^n = u^n r_i^n$:

<Step title="Divide by r_i">

<Formula label="The rescaled equation with the repulsive term" tex="\ddot u = -\frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\rho_i}{u^2} + \gamma\,r_i^{\,n-1}\,u^n" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $\gamma = 0$ this is the rescaled equation of the lesson. In the first term every $r_i$ has cancelled, as before. The new term keeps $r_i^{\,n-1}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

For which value of the power $n$ does the differential equation of part (a) not depend on $r_i$?

<Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The only $r_i$ left in the equation is the factor $r_i^{\,n-1}$.

<Step title="Make the factor disappear">

$r_i^{\,n-1} = 1$ for every $r_i$ only if the power is zero:

<Formula label="The power that keeps the expansion uniform" tex="n - 1 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b) (our own solution)" />

With $n = 1$ the new acceleration is $\gamma\,r$: proportional to the distance, exactly like the velocities of the Hubble flow.
