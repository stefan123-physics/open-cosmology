## The model universe and the galaxy

All six problems are set in one model universe. Its scale factor grows as the two-thirds power of cosmic time:

<Formula label="The scale factor of this set" tex="a(t) = b\,t^{2/3}" cite="mit" at="Introduction to the problem set" />

Here $b$ is a constant. Guth remarks that this is how a flat universe behaves when its mass density is dominated by matter that moves slowly compared with light, which a later lecture derives. In this set it is simply given. Space in this model is flat: the comoving coordinates are an ordinary Euclidean map, and physical distances are $\ell_p(t) = a(t)\,\ell_c$, as in the lessons.

As in the lessons, comoving coordinates are counted in Guth's notches, so $a$ is in metres per notch. The constant $b$ therefore depends on the size of a notch, which anyone may choose freely. **No final answer may contain $b$.** If one of yours does, something has gone wrong.

We call the present time $t_0$, and the time at which a galaxy sent out the light we receive today $t_e$. (The lesson on the redshift calls these two times $t_O$ and $t_S$.) The galaxy and we are both at rest in the comoving coordinates, and we see the galaxy with redshift $z$.

For numbers, MIT's set takes the galaxy UDFy-38135539, which was found in 2009 in infrared images of the Hubble Ultra Deep Field. In 2010 Lehnert and colleagues reported a redshift of $z = 8.55$ from its spectrum, and in 2013 Guth called it the most distant known object with a well-determined redshift. Two remarks for today's reader. An independent search with two other spectrographs (Bunker and colleagues, 2013) did not find the spectral line again, so this redshift is less certain than it seemed. And as a later development, in 2024 the James Webb Space Telescope confirmed a galaxy at $z \approx 14.3$. In this set $z = 8.55$ simply serves as the example, and we call its source "the galaxy".

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The distance to the galaxy" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

Find the physical distance between the galaxy and us today, $\ell_p(t_0)$, in terms of $t_0$, $t_e$ and the speed of light $c$.

<Hint>

The light we see today has travelled from the galaxy to us. In which coordinates is it easy to say how far a light ray gets in a short time $dt$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Add up the comoving distance that the ray covers between $t_e$ and $t_0$. That is the comoving distance $\ell_c$ between the galaxy and us, and it never changes.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The question asks for the physical distance today. Which value of the scale factor turns $\ell_c$ into that distance?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="$\ell_p(t_0) =$" expr="3*c*t0*(1 - (te/t0)^(1/3))"
  vars={{ c: [1, 3], t0: [5, 10], te: [0.1, 2] }}
  names={{ t0: "t_0", te: "t_e" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "c*(t0 - te)", hint: "That is how far light gets in a universe that does not expand. Here the part of the path that the light has already covered keeps stretching afterwards." },
    { expr: "3*c*te^(2/3)*(t0^(1/3) - te^(1/3))", hint: "That is the distance between the galaxy and us at the time of emission. The question asks for the distance today." },
    { expr: "3*c*(t0^(1/3) - te^(1/3))", hint: "Check the units: this is not a length. Did a factor of the scale factor go missing, or a power of the time?" },
    { expr: "3*c*t0*(1 - (te/t0)^(2/3))", hint: "Look again at the integral of the inverse two-thirds power of t: which power of t does it give?" }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In comoving coordinates the galaxy and we stay where we are, and only the light moves. Add up the comoving distance the light covers on its way. Then turn that into a physical distance with today's scale factor.

<Step title="Light in comoving coordinates">

The lesson gives the speed of a light ray in comoving coordinates, in notches per second:

<Formula label="Light in comoving coordinates" tex="\frac{dx}{dt} = \frac{c}{a(t)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 24" />

</Step>

<Step title="The comoving distance to the galaxy">

The ray leaves the galaxy at $t_e$ and reaches us at $t_0$. The comoving distance it covers is the comoving distance between the galaxy and us:

<Formula label="Comoving distance covered by the light" tex="\ell_c = \int_{t_e}^{t_0} \frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} = \frac{c}{b}\int_{t_e}^{t_0} t^{-2/3}\,dt = \frac{3c}{b}\left(t_0^{1/3} - t_e^{1/3}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

This still contains $b$, as a distance in notches must.

</Step>

<Step title="The physical distance today">

The physical distance at any time is the scale factor at that time multiplied by the comoving distance. For the distance today, use $a(t_0) = b\,t_0^{2/3}$:

<Formula label="Physical distance to the galaxy today" tex="\ell_p(t_0) = a(t_0)\,\ell_c = 3c\,t_0^{2/3}\left(t_0^{1/3} - t_e^{1/3}\right) = 3c\,t_0\left[1 - \left(\frac{t_e}{t_0}\right)^{1/3}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The constant $b$ has cancelled, and the result is a speed multiplied by a time: a length.

If the light left only a short while ago, $t_e = t_0 - \Delta t$ with $\Delta t$ small, then $(t_e/t_0)^{1/3} \approx 1 - \Delta t/(3t_0)$ and the distance becomes $c\,\Delta t$, as it must for a nearby source. For example, with $t_e = 0.97\,t_0$ the formula gives $0.0303\,c\,t_0$, close to $c\,(t_0 - t_e) = 0.03\,c\,t_0$.

For earlier emission the distance is larger than $c\,(t_0 - t_e)$, because space behind the light keeps stretching after the light has passed. With $t_e = t_0/8$ the formula gives $1.5\,c\,t_0$, while $c\,(t_0 - t_e)$ is only $0.875\,c\,t_0$. As $t_e$ goes to zero the distance goes to $3c\,t_0$ and no further.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The time of emission" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Write the redshift $z$ of the galaxy in terms of $t_0$ and $t_e$.

<Hint>

The lesson on the cosmological redshift connects $z$ with the scale factor at two moments. Which two?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$z =$" expr="(t0/te)^(2/3) - 1"
  vars={{ t0: [5, 10], te: [0.1, 2] }}
  names={{ t0: "t_0", te: "t_e" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(t0/te)^(2/3)", hint: "That is 1 + z, the factor by which the universe has grown. Take away 1 to get z." },
    { expr: "(te/t0)^(2/3) - 1", hint: "This is negative, a blueshift. Which of the two scale factors belongs on top?" },
    { expr: "(t0/te)^(3/2) - 1", hint: "Check the power: the scale factor grows as the two-thirds power of the time." },
    { expr: "t0/te - 1", hint: "That would hold if the scale factor grew in proportion to the time. Here it grows as the two-thirds power." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The redshift measures how much the universe has grown while the light was on its way.

<Step title="The redshift from the scale factor">

The lesson's result, with $t_0$ for the time of observation and $t_e$ for the time at the source:

<Formula label="Cosmological redshift" tex="1 + z = \frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_e)}" cite="reading2" at="p. 6" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put in the scale factor of this set">

<Formula label="Redshift in the model universe" tex="1 + z = \frac{b\,t_0^{2/3}}{b\,t_e^{2/3}} = \left(\frac{t_0}{t_e}\right)^{2/3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = \left(\frac{t_0}{t_e}\right)^{2/3} - 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The constant $b$ has cancelled and the result is a pure number. Light sent just now ($t_e = t_0$) has $z = 0$. The earlier the light left, the larger $z$, without limit as $t_e$ goes to zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the ratio $t_e/t_0$ for the galaxy with $z = 8.55$.

<Hint>

Turn the result of part (a) around, so that it gives $t_e/t_0$ for a known $z$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$t_e/t_0 =$" value="0.0339" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-b-ratio" />

<Solution>

<Step title="Solve for the ratio of the times">

Raise both sides of $1 + z = (t_0/t_e)^{2/3}$ to the power $-3/2$:

<Formula label="Time of emission from the redshift" tex="\frac{t_e}{t_0} = (1 + z)^{-3/2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put in the number">

With $1 + z = 9.55$: the square root is $\sqrt{9.55} = 3.090$, so $9.55^{3/2} = 9.55 \times 3.090 = 29.51$ and

$t_e/t_0 = 1/29.51 = 0.0339$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The light left when the model universe had about 3.4% of its present age. As a check, $0.0339^{2/3} = 0.1047 = 1/9.55$: at that time every distance between galaxies was 9.55 times smaller than today.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The distance in terms of the redshift" points={10} mit="Problem 3">

Astronomers do not measure $t_0$ and $t_e$ directly. They measure the redshift $z$ and the present value $H_0$ of the Hubble expansion rate.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Write the present physical distance $\ell_p(t_0)$ to the galaxy in terms of $H_0$, $z$ and $c$.

<Hint>

The lesson gives the Hubble expansion rate in terms of the scale factor and its time derivative. Work it out for the scale factor of this set. What does it say about $t_0$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The result of Problem 1 contains $t_0$ and the ratio $t_e/t_0$. Problem 2 gives that ratio in terms of $z$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="$\ell_p(t_0) =$" expr="(2*c/H0)*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))"
  vars={{ c: [1, 3], H0: [0.5, 2], z: [0.1, 10] }}
  names={{ H0: "H_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "c*z/H0", hint: "That is Hubble's law with a speed of c times z. It works only for small z." },
    { expr: "(3*c/H0)*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))", hint: "You seem to have used a present time of 1 over the Hubble rate. Work out the rate from the scale factor of this set." },
    { expr: "(2*c/(3*H0))*(1 - 1/(1 + z)^(3/2))", hint: "That is c multiplied by the travel time of the light. The distance today is larger, because space kept stretching behind the light." },
    { expr: "(2*c/H0)*(1 - 1/(1 + z))", hint: "Check the power of 1 + z: you need the cube root of the ratio of the two times." },
    { expr: "(2*c/H0)*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))/(1 + z)", hint: "That is the distance at the time of emission. The question asks for the distance today." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The Hubble expansion rate today fixes $t_0$, and the redshift fixes $t_e/t_0$. Put both into the result of Problem 1.

<Step title="The Hubble expansion rate of this universe">

The lesson's formula, worked out for $a = b\,t^{2/3}$, whose time derivative is $\dot a = \tfrac{2}{3}\,b\,t^{-1/3}$:

<Formula label="Hubble expansion rate from the scale factor" tex="H(t) = \frac{\dot a(t)}{a(t)} = \frac{\tfrac{2}{3}\,b\,t^{-1/3}}{b\,t^{2/3}} = \frac{2}{3t}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 22–23 (the formula for H; the two-thirds power is this set's)" />

Today this is $H_0 = 2/(3t_0)$, so

<Formula label="The present time from the Hubble expansion rate" tex="t_0 = \frac{2}{3H_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio of the times from the redshift">

From Problem 2, $t_e/t_0 = (1+z)^{-3/2}$. Its cube root is

<Formula label="Cube root of the ratio of the times" tex="\left(\frac{t_e}{t_0}\right)^{1/3} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+z}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put both into the distance">

The result of Problem 1 was $\ell_p(t_0) = 3c\,t_0\left[1 - (t_e/t_0)^{1/3}\right]$. With $3c\,t_0 = 2c/H_0$:

<Formula label="Present distance in terms of the redshift" tex="\ell_p(t_0) = \frac{2c}{H_0}\left[1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+z}}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units are those of $c/H_0$, a length. For small $z$, $1/\sqrt{1+z} \approx 1 - z/2$, and the distance becomes $cz/H_0$. That is Hubble's law $v = H_0\,\ell_p$ for a galaxy with $v = cz$, the Doppler shift at low speed. For very large $z$ the distance approaches $2c/H_0$ and never passes it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Take $H_0 = 67$ km/s/Mpc, the value MIT's set uses, and $z = 8.55$. How far away is the galaxy today? Give the distance in megaparsecs and in light-years.

<Hint>

Work out $c/H_0$ first, with $c$ in km/s. The kilometres per second cancel and megaparsecs are left.

</Hint>

<Hint>

One parsec is about 3.26 light-years, so one megaparsec is about 3.26 million light-years.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b-mpc" label="$\ell_p(t_0) =$" value="6053" unit="Mpc" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-distance-mpc" />

<Answer id="p3-b-ly" label="$\ell_p(t_0) =$" value="19.7" unit="billion light-years" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-distance-gly" />

<Solution>

<Step title="The length c over the Hubble rate">

With $c = 299\,792$ km/s:

$c/H_0 = 299\,792 / 67 = 4475$ Mpc, so $2c/H_0 = 8949$ Mpc.

</Step>

<Step title="The factor that holds the redshift">

$1/\sqrt{9.55} = 1/3.090 = 0.3236$, so $1 - 1/\sqrt{1+z} = 0.6764$.

</Step>

<Step title="The distance">

$\ell_p(t_0) = 8949 \times 0.6764 = 6053$ Mpc, or about 6050 Mpc.

One megaparsec is $3.262 \times 10^{6}$ light-years, so this is $1.97 \times 10^{10}$ light-years: 19.7 billion light-years.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The distance is 68% of the largest possible value $2c/H_0$, as it should be for a redshift that is large but finite.

It is also far more light-years than the model universe has years. With $H_0 = 67$ km/s/Mpc the age of this model universe is $t_0 = 2/(3H_0) = 9.7$ billion years, and the light travelled for $t_0 - t_e = 9.4$ billion years. The galaxy is now 19.7 billion light-years away because space went on stretching behind the light. (The age of 9.7 billion years belongs to this simple model with matter only. Later lessons explain why the real universe is older.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="The speed of recession" points={10} mit="Problem 4">

<Part label="a" points={5}>

At what rate is the physical distance $\ell_p$ between the galaxy and us growing today? Write this speed $v$ in terms of $z$ and $c$.

<Hint>

The galaxy keeps its comoving coordinates. So which of the two factors in $\ell_p(t) = a(t)\,\ell_c$ changes with time?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Take the time derivative at $t_0$. It can be written as the present Hubble expansion rate multiplied by a distance you already know from Problem 3.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$v =$" expr="2*c*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))"
  vars={{ c: [1, 3], z: [0.1, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "c*z", hint: "That is the Doppler shift at low speed. It holds only for small z." },
    { expr: "c*((1 + z)^2 - 1)/((1 + z)^2 + 1)", hint: "That is the speed from the Doppler formula of special relativity. It describes a source moving through one inertial frame, without gravity. Here, take the time derivative of the physical distance." },
    { expr: "2*c*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))/(1 + z)", hint: "You multiplied the Hubble rate by the distance at the time of emission. Today's rate of growth needs today's distance." },
    { expr: "3*c*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))", hint: "Check the present Hubble rate: it is 2 divided by 3 times the present time." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The comoving distance to the galaxy is fixed. The physical distance grows only because the scale factor grows, and that is Hubble's law.

<Step title="The rate of change of the distance">

In $\ell_p(t) = a(t)\,\ell_c$ only $a$ depends on time, as in the lesson:

<Formula label="Hubble's law from the scale factor" tex="\frac{d\ell_p}{dt} = \dot a(t)\,\ell_c = \frac{\dot a(t)}{a(t)}\,\ell_p(t) = H(t)\,\ell_p(t)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 22–23" />

</Step>

<Step title="Today">

At $t_0$ the Hubble expansion rate is $H_0$, and Problem 3 gives the distance:

<Formula label="Speed of recession today" tex="v = H_0\,\ell_p(t_0) = H_0\cdot\frac{2c}{H_0}\left[1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+z}}\right] = 2c\left[1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+z}}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$H_0$ has cancelled: in this model the speed of recession today depends only on the redshift. For small $z$ it becomes $v = cz$, the Doppler shift at low speed. For $z = 3$ it is exactly $c$, and for very large $z$ it approaches $2c$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Work out $v/c$ for the galaxy with $z = 8.55$.

<Hint>

The factor that holds the redshift is the one you already worked out in Problem 3.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$v/c =$" value="1.35" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-speed-ratio" />

<Solution>

<Step title="Put in the number">

$v/c = 2\left[1 - 1/\sqrt{9.55}\right] = 2 \times 0.6764 = 1.35$.

</Step>

<Step title="Is that allowed?">

The distance to the galaxy grows today at 1.35 times the speed of light. Special relativity says that nothing moves faster than light through an inertial frame. But $v$ is not a speed of that kind. It is the rate at which a very long distance grows, added up along a chain of comoving observers who all move apart, and with gravity present no single inertial frame covers the whole chain. This is the lesson's point that the velocity of a distant galaxy is not defined in the sense of special relativity. General relativity, which describes the expanding universe, allows such a rate. Close to any one observer everything still happens as special relativity says: the galaxy is at rest relative to the matter around it, and the light that passes an observer does so at speed $c$.

In this model every galaxy with $z > 3$ recedes today at more than $c$, and we still see it, because its light left long ago, when the galaxy was much closer.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="The apparent angular size" points={20} mit="Problem 5">

Suppose the galaxy is a sphere, and that its physical diameter was $w$ at the time it sent out the light. (The real galaxy shows up as a point of light with no visible size, so its true size and shape are unknown.) Seen from Earth today, it covers a small angle $\theta$ on the sky, measured from one edge to the opposite edge. Assume $\theta \ll 1$, in radians.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Find $\theta$ in terms of $w$, $z$, $H_0$ and $c$.

<Hint>

Draw the comoving map with us at one point. Two light rays reach us today: one from each edge of the galaxy. In comoving coordinates a light ray moves along a straight line, and the angle between two lines on the map is the real angle, because the scale factor stretches all directions equally.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the map, the angle is the comoving diameter of the galaxy divided by its comoving distance from us. The diameter $w$ is a physical length at one particular time. Which scale factor turns it into a comoving length?

</Hint>

<Hint>

You can avoid $b$ by writing the comoving distance as today's physical distance divided by today's scale factor. A ratio of two scale factors is then left, which you know from Problem 2.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-a" label="$\theta =$" expr="w*H0*(1 + z)/(2*c*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z)))"
  vars={{ w: [1, 3], H0: [0.5, 2], z: [0.1, 10], c: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ H0: "H_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "w*H0/(2*c*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z)))", hint: "That is the angle in a static space, at today's distance. The light left when the galaxy was closer, and the galaxy's diameter w was measured at that time." },
    { expr: "w*H0/(2*c*(1 + z)*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z)))", hint: "The factor 1 + z is on the wrong side. Was the galaxy's comoving diameter larger or smaller than w divided by today's scale factor?" },
    { expr: "w*H0*(1 + z)/(c*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z)))", hint: "A factor of 2 has gone astray. The angle is the comoving diameter, not the radius, divided by the comoving distance, and the largest distance is 2c divided by the Hubble rate." },
    { expr: "w*H0*(1 + z)^2/(2*c*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z)))", hint: "One factor of 1 + z too many. Only the diameter needs the scale factor at the time of emission." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow the two light rays from the edges of the galaxy on the comoving map. The map is Euclidean, the rays are straight lines on it, and the galaxy and we stay at fixed points. So the angle is a ratio of two comoving lengths.

<Step title="The rays on the comoving map">

Put us at the origin of the comoving coordinates. Light that reaches us moves along a straight line through the origin: nothing on the map singles out a side for the ray to turn towards. The rays from the two edges of the galaxy are two such lines, and the angle between them at the origin is $\theta$.

The scale factor multiplies all lengths, in all directions, by the same number. So an angle on the map is the same as the physical angle we measure.

</Step>

<Step title="The comoving diameter of the galaxy">

The diameter $w$ is a physical length at the time of emission. The two rays left the edges of the galaxy at that time, so on the map they start a comoving distance $w/a(t_e)$ apart.

</Step>

<Step title="The angle">

The galaxy is at comoving distance $\ell_c$ from us. For a small angle,

<Formula label="Angle on the comoving map" tex="\theta = \frac{w/a(t_e)}{\ell_c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

Write $\ell_c = \ell_p(t_0)/a(t_0)$ and use $a(t_0)/a(t_e) = 1 + z$:

<Formula label="Angular size from the present distance" tex="\theta = \frac{w}{\ell_p(t_0)}\cdot\frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_e)} = \frac{(1+z)\,w}{\ell_p(t_0)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

With the distance from Problem 3:

<Formula label="Apparent angular size of the galaxy" tex="\theta = \frac{H_0\,w}{2c}\cdot\frac{1+z}{1 - 1/\sqrt{1+z}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$H_0\,w/c$ is a length divided by a length, so $\theta$ is a pure number, an angle in radians. For small $z$ the last factor becomes $2/z$ and $\theta = w/(cz/H_0)$: the diameter divided by the distance from Hubble's law, as for a nearby galaxy.

The second formula has a simple reading. $\ell_p(t_0)/(1+z)$ is the physical distance between the galaxy and us at the time of emission, $a(t_e)\,\ell_c$. The galaxy looks as large as an object of diameter $w$ at that smaller distance.

A number of our own: a galaxy with $w = 1$ kpc at $z = 8.55$ would cover $\theta = 9.55 \times 0.001/6053 = 1.58 \times 10^{-6}$ radians, which is 0.33 seconds of arc.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Compare this with a static Euclidean space, where a circle of diameter $w$ at distance $\ell_p(t_0)$ would cover the angle $\theta_s = w/\ell_p(t_0)$. What is the ratio $\theta/\theta_s$, in terms of $z$?

<Hint>

Use the form of $\theta$ that still contains $\ell_p(t_0)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-b" label="$\theta/\theta_s =$" expr="1 + z"
  vars={{ z: [0.1, 10] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/(1 + z)", hint: "This says the galaxy looks smaller than in a static space. Was it closer to us or further away when the light left?" },
    { expr: "z", hint: "Close, but for z going to zero the two angles must become equal, so the ratio must go to 1." },
    { expr: "sqrt(1 + z)", hint: "Check the ratio of the two scale factors: it is 1 + z itself, not its square root." },
    { expr: "(1 + z)^2", hint: "One factor of 1 + z too many. Only the diameter needs the scale factor at the time of emission." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

<Step title="Divide the two angles">

From part (a), $\theta = (1+z)\,w/\ell_p(t_0)$, and the static angle is $\theta_s = w/\ell_p(t_0)$. So

<Formula label="Expanding universe against static space" tex="\frac{\theta}{\theta_s} = 1 + z" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The galaxy looks larger than it would in a static space at today's distance, by the factor by which the universe has grown since the light left. For our galaxy that is 9.55 times: the 0.33 seconds of arc of part (a) would be only 0.034 seconds of arc in the static space. For $z$ going to zero the ratio goes to 1, as it must.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="c" points={6}>

Show that, for a fixed diameter $w$, the angle $\theta$ is smallest at $z = 1.25$ and grows again for larger $z$. Explain in words how a galaxy that is further away can look larger.

<Hint>

The algebra is shorter with the new variable $u = \sqrt{1+z}$. Write $\theta$ in terms of $u$ and look for the value of $u$ at which its derivative is zero.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the explanation, think of the distance between the galaxy and us at the time of emission, not today.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find where the derivative of $\theta$ with respect to $z$ is zero. Then read the result with the distance at the time of emission in mind.

<Step title="A handier variable">

Let $u = \sqrt{1+z}$, which grows with $z$ and is larger than 1. Then $1 + z = u^2$ and $1 - 1/\sqrt{1+z} = (u-1)/u$, so

<Formula label="Angular size in terms of u" tex="\theta = \frac{H_0\,w}{2c}\cdot\frac{u^2}{(u-1)/u} = \frac{H_0\,w}{2c}\cdot\frac{u^3}{u-1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Where the derivative is zero">

<Formula label="Derivative of the angular size" tex="\frac{d}{du}\left(\frac{u^3}{u-1}\right) = \frac{3u^2(u-1) - u^3}{(u-1)^2} = \frac{u^2\,(2u-3)}{(u-1)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

This is negative for $u < 3/2$, zero at $u = 3/2$ and positive for $u > 3/2$. So $\theta$ falls, reaches its smallest value at $u = 3/2$ and rises afterwards. In terms of the redshift:

<Formula label="Redshift of the smallest angular size" tex="1 + z = u^2 = \frac{9}{4} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = 1.25" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

The smallest angle is $\theta = (H_0\,w/2c) \times (27/8)/(1/2) = 3.375\,H_0\,w/c$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

In units of $H_0\,w/c$ the angle is 4.09 at $z = 0.5$, 3.375 at $z = 1.25$, 3.55 at $z = 2$ and 7.06 at $z = 8.55$. The galaxy of this set looks about twice as large as the same galaxy would at $z = 1.25$.

</Step>

<Step title="Why a more distant galaxy can look larger">

What counts for the angle is how far away the galaxy was when the light left, $\ell_p(t_e) = \ell_p(t_0)/(1+z)$. In this model

<Formula label="Distance at the time of emission" tex="\ell_p(t_e) = \frac{2c}{H_0}\cdot\frac{1}{1+z}\left[1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+z}}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution)" />

For small $z$ this grows with $z$: further galaxies look smaller, as in everyday life. But the light of a galaxy with very large $z$ left very early, when the whole universe was small and the galaxy was still close to us. So $\ell_p(t_e)$ has a largest value, at $z = 1.25$, and falls again beyond it. The galaxy with $z = 8.55$ is 6053 Mpc away today, but it was only 634 Mpc away when its light set out. The light needed almost the whole age of the universe to reach us all the same, because the space it had to cross kept stretching.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="The radiation flux we receive" points={20} mit="Problem 6">

At the time of emission the galaxy sent out light with power $P$ (energy per unit time), equally in all directions. The light consists of photons, and the energy of a photon is proportional to its frequency.

The energy flux $J$ is the energy that arrives per unit time and per unit area, on a surface that faces the incoming light at a right angle. Find the flux $J$ of the galaxy's light at Earth today.

A useful picture, which MIT's set suggests: think of the detector as a small piece of a large sphere that has the galaxy at its centre and passes through us today. Follow the photons in comoving coordinates.

<Part label="a" points={12}>

Write $J$ in terms of $P$, $z$ and the present distance $\ell_p(t_0)$. In the answer box, type `lp` for $\ell_p(t_0)$.

<Hint>

Three things decide the flux: the area over which the photons are spread when they reach us, the rate at which they arrive, and the energy each one still has. Take them one at a time.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the area: the sphere has a fixed comoving radius. What is its physical radius today?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the rate: two photons sent a short time apart travel like two crests of a wave. The lesson on the redshift tells you how the time between them changes on the way.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the energy: it is proportional to the frequency. What does the redshift do to the frequency?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-a" label="$J =$" expr="P/(4*pi*lp^2*(1 + z)^2)"
  vars={{ P: [1, 5], lp: [1, 3], z: [0.1, 10] }}
  names={{ lp: "\\ell_p(t_0)" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "P/(4*pi*lp^2)", hint: "That is the flux in a static space. The expansion lowers it in two ways: think of the energy of each photon, and of how often photons arrive." },
    { expr: "P/(4*pi*lp^2*(1 + z))", hint: "You have one of the two effects of the redshift. Each photon has less energy, and the photons also arrive less often." },
    { expr: "P/(4*pi*lp^2*(1 + z)^4)", hint: "Too many factors of 1 + z. The photons are spread over a sphere whose radius is today's distance, and the redshift then enters twice, not four times." },
    { expr: "P*(1 + z)^2/(4*pi*lp^2)", hint: "The redshift makes the light weaker, not stronger. The two factors of 1 + z belong below the line." },
    { expr: "P/(pi*lp^2*(1 + z)^2)", hint: "Check the area of a sphere of radius r: it is 4 pi r squared." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In comoving coordinates the photons move straight out from the galaxy, equally in all directions, and none are lost. When they reach us they are spread evenly over a sphere around the galaxy. The expansion changes two more things on the way: the time between photons and the energy of each photon.

<Step title="The area the photons are spread over">

The photons that reach us today all left at $t_e$ and have all covered the same comoving distance $\ell_c$. They lie on a sphere of comoving radius $\ell_c$ around the galaxy, and we are on that sphere. Its physical radius today is $a(t_0)\,\ell_c = \ell_p(t_0)$. Space in this model is flat, so the area of the sphere today is

<Formula label="Area of the sphere through us today" tex="4\pi\,\ell_p(t_0)^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

The photons move along the radius of the sphere, so they cross it at a right angle, as the definition of $J$ asks.

</Step>

<Step title="The photons arrive less often">

Let the galaxy send out the energy $E_e$ during a short time $\Delta t_e$, so that $P = E_e/\Delta t_e$. The first and the last photon of this batch leave a time $\Delta t_e$ apart. They travel exactly like two crests of a wave in the lesson: both move at the comoving speed $c/a(t)$, so their comoving separation stays fixed and their separation in time stretches with the scale factor. The batch therefore crosses the sphere during the time

<Formula label="The time between photons stretches" tex="\Delta t_0 = (1+z)\,\Delta t_e" cite="reading2" at="pp. 5–6 (the argument for wave crests, used here for photons)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Each photon has less energy">

The energy of a photon is proportional to its frequency. The frequency is 1 divided by the time between crests, which has grown by the factor $1+z$. So every photon arrives with its energy divided by $1+z$, and the same holds for the energy of the whole batch:

<Formula label="The energy of the photons falls" tex="E_0 = \frac{E_e}{1+z}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (the fact that a photon's energy is proportional to its frequency is given in the problem)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The flux">

The energy $E_0$ crosses the whole sphere during the time $\Delta t_0$. The flux is that energy per unit time and per unit area:

<Formula label="Flux in terms of the present distance" tex="J = \frac{E_0/\Delta t_0}{4\pi\,\ell_p(t_0)^2} = \frac{E_e/\Delta t_e}{(1+z)^2\,4\pi\,\ell_p(t_0)^2} = \frac{P}{4\pi\,(1+z)^2\,\ell_p(t_0)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units are those of a power divided by an area. For $z$ going to zero the result is $P/(4\pi\,\ell_p^2)$, the inverse-square law of a static space. For any $z > 0$ the galaxy looks fainter than that law says, by $(1+z)^2$: one factor because each photon is redder, one because the photons arrive less often.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

Now write $J$ in terms of $P$, $z$, $H_0$ and $c$.

<Hint>

Only the distance $\ell_p(t_0)$ is left to replace. Problem 3 gives it in terms of $H_0$, $z$ and $c$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-b" label="$J =$" expr="P*H0^2/(16*pi*c^2*(1 + z)^2*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))^2)"
  vars={{ P: [1, 5], H0: [0.5, 2], c: [1, 3], z: [0.1, 10] }}
  names={{ H0: "H_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "P*H0^2/(16*pi*c^2*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))^2)", hint: "That is the flux in a static space at today's distance. The two factors of 1 + z from part (a) are missing." },
    { expr: "P*H0^2/(16*pi*c^2*(1 + z)*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))^2)", hint: "You have one of the two effects of the redshift. Each photon has less energy, and the photons also arrive less often." },
    { expr: "P*H0^2/(4*pi*c^2*(1 + z)^2*(1 - 1/sqrt(1 + z))^2)", hint: "Check the square of the distance: the distance holds a factor 2, which becomes 4 when squared." },
    { expr: "P*H0^2/(4*pi*c^2*z^2)", hint: "That holds only for small z, where the distance is c times z divided by the Hubble rate." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

<Step title="Put in the distance">

From Problem 3, $\ell_p(t_0) = (2c/H_0)\left[1 - 1/\sqrt{1+z}\right]$. Its square is $(4c^2/H_0^2)\left[1 - 1/\sqrt{1+z}\right]^2$, so

<Formula label="Flux received from the galaxy" tex="J = \frac{P\,H_0^2}{16\pi\,c^2\,(1+z)^2\left[1 - \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+z}}\right]^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6 (our own solution)" />

Because $(1+z)\left[1 - 1/\sqrt{1+z}\right]^2 = \left(\sqrt{1+z} - 1\right)^2$, the same result can be written as $J = P\,H_0^2/\left[16\pi\,c^2\,(1+z)\left(\sqrt{1+z} - 1\right)^2\right]$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$H_0/c$ is 1 divided by a length, so $J$ is a power divided by an area. For small $z$ the square bracket becomes $z/2$ and $J = P/\left[4\pi\,(cz/H_0)^2\right]$: the inverse-square law at the distance from Hubble's law.

Unlike the angular size of Problem 5, the flux falls for every increase of $z$: a more distant galaxy always looks fainter. For the galaxy with $z = 8.55$, the redshift lowers the flux by $(1+z)^2 = 9.55^2 = 91$ compared with a static space at the same present distance.

</Step>

</Solution>
