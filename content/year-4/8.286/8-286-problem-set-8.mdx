## What the lessons give you

The set uses the notation of Lectures 19 and 20. Every kind of mass density is written as a fraction of today's critical density: $\Omega_{m,0}$ for matter that moves slowly compared with light, $\Omega_{\text{rad},0}$ for radiation and $\Omega_{\text{vac},0}$ for the vacuum energy. The subscript 0 means "today". The curvature gets a fraction of its own, which makes all of them add up to 1:

<Formula label="The curvature fraction" tex="\Omega_{k,0} \equiv -\frac{kc^2}{a^2(t_0)\,H_0^2} = 1 - \Omega_{m,0} - \Omega_{\text{rad},0} - \Omega_{\text{vac},0}" cite="reading2" at="slides 1–2" />

A closed universe ($k > 0$) has a negative $\Omega_{k,0}$, an open one ($k < 0$) a positive $\Omega_{k,0}$, and a flat one has $\Omega_{k,0} = 0$. With $x \equiv a(t)/a(t_0)$, the scale factor compared with today's, the first Friedmann equation becomes

<Formula label="Friedmann equation in terms of x" tex="H^2 = \left(\frac{\dot x}{x}\right)^2 = \frac{H_0^2}{x^4}\left[\Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{rad},0} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4 + \Omega_{k,0}\,x^2\right]" cite="reading2" at="slide 2" />

Light that we receive today with redshift $z$ left its source when $x = 1/(1+z)$. Written with $z$, the same equation reads $H(z) = H_0\,E(z)$ with

<Formula label="Hubble expansion rate at redshift z" tex="E(z) \equiv \frac{H(z)}{H_0} = \sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^3 + \Omega_{\text{rad},0}(1+z)^4 + \Omega_{\text{vac},0} + \Omega_{k,0}(1+z)^2}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 18–19 (board); the name E(z) is the lesson's" />

Lecture 19 worked out how bright a source looks in a **closed** universe. A source of power $P$ (energy sent out per second, the same in all directions) is seen at redshift $z_S$. The energy flux $J$ is the energy that arrives per second on each square metre of a detector. Guth found

<Formula label="Flux in a closed universe" tex="J = \frac{P\,H_0^2\,|\Omega_{k,0}|}{4\pi\,(1+z_S)^2\,c^2\,\sin^2\psi_D}, \qquad \psi_D = \sqrt{|\Omega_{k,0}|}\int_0^{z_S}\frac{dz}{E(z)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (Lecture 20 slides 5–6)" />

Here $\psi_D$ is the radial angle between the source and the detector on the three-dimensional sphere that a closed universe is. (The lesson on brightness writes the subscripts of these two symbols as small letters, s and d, as in Lecture 19. This set uses the capitals of MIT's problem set and of the Lecture 20 slides.)

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Brightness against redshift in an open and a flat universe" points={25} mit="Problem 1">

Guth derived the flux formula above for a closed universe only, and left the other two cases as homework. This is that homework.

<Part label="a" points={15}>

Derive the flux $J$ from a source of power $P$ at redshift $z_S$ in an **open** universe ($k < 0$, so $\Omega_{k,0} > 0$). MIT asks for a derivation that is complete in itself: do not write "as in the lecture, except for". Start from the Robertson–Walker metric,

<Formula label="Robertson–Walker metric" tex="ds^2 = -c^2dt^2 + a^2(t)\left\{\frac{dr^2}{1 - kr^2} + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right\}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 13–14 (board)" />

and end with two results: $J$ in terms of $P$, $H_0$, $\Omega_{k,0}$, $z_S$, $c$ and a radial coordinate $\psi_D$ of your choice, and $\psi_D$ as an integral over redshift. Type the first one in the box.

<Hint>

In the closed case Guth replaced $r$ by an angle, with $\sin\psi = \sqrt{k}\,r$, to make the radial part of the metric as simple as possible. In an open universe $k$ is negative. Write $\kappa = -k$, which is positive, and look for a function that does the same job when $1 - kr^2$ becomes $1 + \kappa r^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Put the source at the origin and draw a sphere around it that passes through the detector today. Its area follows from the part of the metric with $d\theta$ and $d\phi$. Then count what the redshift does to the energy of each photon and to the number of photons that arrive per second.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Two quantities are left that no astronomer measures: the scale factor today, and the radial coordinate of the detector. The definition of $\Omega_{k,0}$ gives the first. A light ray has $ds^2 = 0$, which gives the second as an integral over time. Change the variable from $t$ to $z$ with $1 + z = a(t_0)/a(t)$.

</Hint>

  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Follow the light on a comoving map of the open universe. All the light spreads over a sphere around the source, and the metric says how large that sphere is today. The redshift takes away two factors of $1 + z_S$. Then trade the two unmeasurable quantities for $H_0$, the Ω's and the redshift.

<Step title="A better radial coordinate">

For an open universe write $\kappa \equiv -k > 0$, so the radial term of the metric is $dr^2/(1 + \kappa r^2)$. Define a new radial coordinate $\psi$ by

<Formula label="Radial coordinate of the open universe" tex="\sinh\psi = \sqrt{\kappa}\,r \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cosh\psi\,d\psi = \sqrt{\kappa}\,dr \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\psi = \frac{\sqrt{\kappa}\,dr}{\sqrt{1 + \kappa r^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

The last step uses $\cosh^2\psi = 1 + \sinh^2\psi = 1 + \kappa r^2$. So $dr^2/(1 + \kappa r^2) = d\psi^2/\kappa$ and $r^2 = \sinh^2\psi/\kappa$. Both terms in the curly brackets now carry a factor $1/\kappa$, which goes into a new scale factor:

<Formula label="Open metric with the coordinate ψ" tex="ds^2 = -c^2dt^2 + \tilde a^2(t)\left\{d\psi^2 + \sinh^2\psi\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right\}, \qquad \tilde a(t) = \frac{a(t)}{\sqrt{\kappa}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

This is the closed metric of the lesson with $\sin\psi$ replaced by $\sinh\psi$. Unlike the angle of the closed universe, this $\psi$ runs from 0 without limit. It is a pure number, so $\tilde a$ is a length.

</Step>

<Step title="The sphere around the source">

Put the source at $\psi = 0$. Our detector, of area $A$, sits at $\psi = \psi_D$. The source and the detector are at rest in these coordinates, so $\psi_D$ never changes. Think of the sphere $\psi = \psi_D$ around the source, at the present time $t_0$. On it only $\theta$ and $\phi$ vary, and the metric gives distances $ds^2 = \tilde a^2(t_0)\sinh^2\psi_D\,(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)$. That is an ordinary sphere of radius $\tilde a(t_0)\sinh\psi_D$, with area $4\pi\,\tilde a^2(t_0)\sinh^2\psi_D$. The light spreads evenly over it, so the detector catches the fraction

<Formula label="Fraction of the light that reaches the detector" tex="\frac{A}{4\pi\,\tilde a^2(t_0)\,\sinh^2\psi_D}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

The sphere is measured today because the detector has its area $A$ today: the two areas must be compared at one time.

</Step>

<Step title="Two factors from the redshift">

The light left at the time $t_S$ and arrives at $t_0$, with $1 + z_S = a(t_0)/a(t_S)$. Every photon arrives with its frequency, and so its energy, reduced by the factor $1 + z_S$. And the photons that left during one second arrive spread over $1 + z_S$ seconds, since every time interval at the source is stretched in the same way. The power received and the flux are therefore

<Formula label="Flux in terms of the scale factor" tex="P_\text{rec} = \frac{P}{(1+z_S)^2}\,\frac{A}{4\pi\,\tilde a^2(t_0)\,\sinh^2\psi_D}, \qquad J = \frac{P_\text{rec}}{A} = \frac{P}{4\pi\,(1+z_S)^2\,\tilde a^2(t_0)\,\sinh^2\psi_D}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution; the closed case is on reading1 pp. 16–17)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The scale factor today">

In the definition $\Omega_{k,0} = -kc^2/(a^2(t_0)H_0^2)$ put $-k = \kappa$ and $a = \sqrt{\kappa}\,\tilde a$. The $\kappa$ cancels:

<Formula label="Scale factor of the open universe today" tex="\Omega_{k,0} = \frac{c^2}{\tilde a^2(t_0)\,H_0^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tilde a(t_0) = \frac{c\,H_0^{-1}}{\sqrt{\Omega_{k,0}}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

In an open universe $\Omega_{k,0}$ is positive, so the square root is real.

</Step>

<Step title="The radial coordinate of the detector">

A light ray that travels straight from the source to us has $d\theta = d\phi = 0$ and $ds^2 = 0$, so $c^2dt^2 = \tilde a^2(t)\,d\psi^2$ and

<Formula label="Radial light ray" tex="\frac{d\psi}{dt} = \frac{c}{\tilde a(t)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \psi_D = \int_{t_S}^{t_0}\frac{c\,dt}{\tilde a(t)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution; the closed case is on reading1 p. 18)" />

Now change the variable to the redshift $z$ of light sent out at the time $t$, with $1 + z = \tilde a(t_0)/\tilde a(t)$. Differentiating, $dz = -\tilde a(t_0)\,\dot{\tilde a}\,dt/\tilde a^2 = -\tilde a(t_0)\,H\,dt/\tilde a$, so $c\,dt/\tilde a = -c\,dz/(\tilde a(t_0)H)$. At $t_S$ the redshift is $z_S$ and at $t_0$ it is 0. The minus sign turns the limits around:

<Formula label="Radial coordinate of a source at redshift z_S, open universe" tex="\psi_D = \frac{1}{\tilde a(t_0)}\int_0^{z_S}\frac{c\,dz}{H(z)} = \sqrt{\Omega_{k,0}}\int_0^{z_S}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^3 + \Omega_{\text{rad},0}(1+z)^4 + \Omega_{\text{vac},0} + \Omega_{k,0}(1+z)^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

The second form uses $\tilde a(t_0)$ from the step before and $H(z) = H_0\,E(z)$ from the Friedmann equation.

</Step>

<Step title="Put it together">

With $\tilde a^2(t_0) = c^2/(H_0^2\,\Omega_{k,0})$ in the flux:

<Formula label="Flux in an open universe" tex="J = \frac{P\,H_0^2\,\Omega_{k,0}}{4\pi\,(1+z_S)^2\,c^2\,\sinh^2\psi_D}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a) (our own solution)" />

It is the closed formula with $\sin$ replaced by $\sinh$. Since $\Omega_{k,0}$ is positive here, it needs no absolute value signs.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $P\,H_0^2/c^2$ is a power divided by a length squared, and everything else is a pure number.

A nearby source: for small $z_S$ the integrand is close to $1/E(0) = 1$, so $\psi_D \approx \sqrt{\Omega_{k,0}}\,z_S$ and $\sinh\psi_D \approx \psi_D$. Then $J \approx P/[4\pi\,(c\,z_S/H_0)^2]$: the inverse-square law for a source at the Hubble's-law distance $c\,z_S/H_0$, whatever the Ω's are.

A case with a known answer: an open universe with matter only, $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$ and no vacuum energy, so $\Omega_{k,0} = 0.7$. For such a universe the lesson on the supernova evidence quotes a closed form, $H_0\,d_L/c = 2\left[\Omega z + (\Omega - 2)(\sqrt{1 + \Omega z} - 1)\right]/\Omega^2$, where $d_L$ is the luminosity distance of Problem 5, defined by $J = P/(4\pi d_L^2)$, and $\Omega$ stands for $\Omega_{m,0}$. For $z_S = 1$ it gives 1.3712. Our formula says $H_0\,d_L/c = (1 + z_S)\sinh\psi_D/\sqrt{\Omega_{k,0}}$. Integrating numerically, $\psi_D = 0.5461$ and $\sinh\psi_D = 0.5736$, so $H_0\,d_L/c = 2 \times 0.5736/\sqrt{0.7} = 1.3712$ as well.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Now find the flux in a **flat** universe ($k = 0$). You may take the limit $\Omega_{k,0} \to 0$ of the open or the closed result, but take care: the numerator and the denominator of $J$ both go to zero. Or you may start again from the flat metric, without repeating what part (a) already showed.

Write your result with the pure number

<Formula label="The integral over redshift" tex="I(z_S) \equiv \int_0^{z_S}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^3 + \Omega_{\text{rad},0}(1+z)^4 + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (the integral of MIT's formula, with no curvature term; the name I is ours)" />

and type $J$ in terms of $P$, $H_0$, $z_S$, $c$ and $I$.

<Hint>

By the limit: in the open result $\psi_D = \sqrt{\Omega_{k,0}}\,I$ becomes small as $\Omega_{k,0}$ goes to zero. What is $\sinh\psi_D$ for a small $\psi_D$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Directly: with $k = 0$ the metric needs no new radial coordinate. The sphere through the detector has the radius $a(t_0)\,r_D$, and a radial light ray gives $r_D$ as an integral over time. After the change of variable to $z$, the product $a(t_0)\,r_D$ is all you need, so it does not matter that $a(t_0)$ alone has no measurable value.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a flat universe the sphere around the source is an ordinary Euclidean sphere whose radius is the present distance of the source. Find that distance from the light ray, and check the result against the limit of part (a).

<Step title="The flat metric and the sphere">

With $k = 0$ the metric is $ds^2 = -c^2dt^2 + a^2(t)\{dr^2 + r^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)\}$. The sphere $r = r_D$ around the source has, today, the radius $a(t_0)\,r_D$ and the area $4\pi\,a^2(t_0)\,r_D^2$. The two redshift factors are as in part (a), so

<Formula label="Flux in a flat universe, in terms of the coordinate distance" tex="J = \frac{P}{4\pi\,(1+z_S)^2\,a^2(t_0)\,r_D^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The distance of the source today">

A radial light ray has $dr/dt = c/a(t)$. With the same change of variable as in part (a), $c\,dt/a = -c\,dz/(a(t_0)\,H)$:

<Formula label="Present distance of the source" tex="a(t_0)\,r_D = a(t_0)\int_{t_S}^{t_0}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} = \int_0^{z_S}\frac{c\,dz}{H(z)} = \frac{c}{H_0}\,I(z_S)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

In a flat universe the size of a notch is free, so $a(t_0)$ and $r_D$ have no measurable values of their own. Their product, the physical distance of the source today, does.

</Step>

<Step title="The flux">

<Formula label="Flux in a flat universe" tex="J = \frac{P\,H_0^2}{4\pi\,(1+z_S)^2\,c^2\,I^2(z_S)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check: the limit of the open universe">

In part (a), $\psi_D = \sqrt{\Omega_{k,0}}\,I_k$, where $I_k$ is the same integral with the curvature term $\Omega_{k,0}(1+z)^2$ under the root. As $\Omega_{k,0}$ goes to zero, $I_k$ goes to $I$ and $\psi_D$ goes to zero. For a small argument, $\sinh\psi_D = \psi_D + \psi_D^3/6 + \dots$, so

<Formula label="The limit of small curvature" tex="\frac{\Omega_{k,0}}{\sinh^2\psi_D} = \frac{\Omega_{k,0}}{\Omega_{k,0}\,I_k^2\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{6}\Omega_{k,0}\,I_k^2 + \dots\right)^2} \;\to\; \frac{1}{I^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b) (our own solution)" />

The factor $\Omega_{k,0}$ that made the numerator vanish cancels against the same factor from $\sinh^2\psi_D$. The closed universe gives the same limit, since $\sin\psi_D \approx \psi_D$ too.

How fast is the limit reached? Take $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$ and $\Omega_{\text{vac},0} = 0.6$, so $\Omega_{k,0} = 0.1$, and $z_S = 1$. Then $I_k = 0.7495$, $\psi_D = 0.2370$ and $\sinh\psi_D/\psi_D = 1.0094$: the open sphere is 0.94% larger in radius than a flat sphere with the same $I_k$, and the flux is 1.9% smaller.

</Step>

<Step title="Check: matter only">

For a flat universe with matter only, $E(z) = (1+z)^{3/2}$ and the integral can be done: $I = 2\left[1 - 1/\sqrt{1+z_S}\right]$. The flux becomes $P\,H_0^2/\{16\pi\,c^2(1+z_S)^2[1 - 1/\sqrt{1+z_S}]^2\}$, which is the result of Problem 6 of [Problem Set 2](/courses/8-286/8-286-problem-set-2).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The age of a universe with mysterious stuff" points={20} mit="Problem 2">

A universe contains matter that moves slowly compared with light, radiation, vacuum energy and one more ingredient, which MIT's set calls **mysterious stuff**. Its only known property is how it thins out: its mass density is proportional to $1/\sqrt{V}$, where $V$ is the volume it fills. In an expanding universe every volume grows as $a^3(t)$, so the mass density of mysterious stuff falls as $1/a^{3/2}(t)$.

You are given the Hubble expansion rate today, $H_0$, and today's value of $\Omega \equiv \rho/\rho_c$ for each ingredient: $\Omega_{m,0}$ (matter), $\Omega_{\text{rad},0}$ (radiation), $\Omega_{\text{vac},0}$ (vacuum energy) and $\Omega_{\text{ms},0}$ (mysterious stuff). The goal is the age of this universe, $t_0$. (In this problem MIT's set shortens the subscripts for radiation and vacuum energy to the single letters r and v. We keep the lessons' subscripts. MIT also tells its students that they may study the posted solution of an earlier quiz review problem for this one. Our solution is our own.)

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Let $x(t) \equiv a(t)/a(t_0)$. Write the total mass density $\rho(t)$ of this universe in terms of $x$, the given quantities and Newton's constant $G$.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

**The idea.** Each ingredient keeps its own rule for thinning out. Today's Ω's fix the four densities today, and the rules carry them to any other time.

<Step title="How each density changes">

When all lengths are multiplied by $x$, volumes are multiplied by $x^3$. The lesson gives the first three rules below. The fourth follows from $\rho_\text{ms} \propto 1/\sqrt{V}$ with $V \propto x^3$:

<Formula label="Time dependence of the four densities" tex="\rho_m = \frac{\rho_{m,0}}{x^3}, \qquad \rho_\text{rad} = \frac{\rho_{\text{rad},0}}{x^4}, \qquad \rho_\text{vac} = \rho_{\text{vac},0}, \qquad \rho_\text{ms} = \frac{\rho_{\text{ms},0}}{x^{3/2}}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 4–5 (board), for matter, radiation and vacuum energy; the last rule is from Problem 2, p. 2" />

</Step>

<Step title="Today's densities from the Ω's">

By the definition of Ω, each density today is its Ω multiplied by today's critical density:

<Formula label="Critical density today" tex="\rho_c = \frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G} \;\;\text{(today)}, \qquad \rho_{m,0} = \Omega_{m,0}\,\rho_c \quad\text{and so on}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5 (board)" />

<Formula label="Total mass density with mysterious stuff" tex="\rho(t) = \frac{3H_0^2}{8\pi G}\left[\frac{\Omega_{m,0}}{x^3} + \frac{\Omega_{\text{rad},0}}{x^4} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0} + \frac{\Omega_{\text{ms},0}}{x^{3/2}}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

At $x = 1$ the bracket is the sum of the four Ω's, so $\rho$ is that sum times the critical density, as the definitions say. The units are those of $H_0^2/G$, a mass density.

Mysterious stuff thins out more slowly than matter and faster than vacuum energy, so its power of $x$ must lie between 0 and 3. Far in the past radiation is the largest term. Far in the future only the vacuum energy is left.

</Step>

**The idea.** The Friedmann equation gives $dx/dt$ as a function of $x$. Turn it around to get $dt$ for each step $dx$, and add up from the big bang to today, as the lesson does without the mysterious stuff.

<Step title="The Friedmann equation with the new density">

The first Friedmann equation is $(\dot a/a)^2 = \tfrac{8\pi}{3}G\rho - kc^2/a^2$. Since $x$ differs from $a$ only by the constant $a(t_0)$, $\dot a/a = \dot x/x$. With $\rho$ from part (a), the factor $\tfrac{8\pi}{3}G$ times $3H_0^2/(8\pi G)$ is simply $H_0^2$. The curvature term is $-kc^2/a^2 = H_0^2\,\Omega_{k,0}/x^2$, by the definition of $\Omega_{k,0}$. So

<Formula label="Friedmann equation with mysterious stuff" tex="\left(\frac{\dot x}{x}\right)^2 = H_0^2\left[\frac{\Omega_{m,0}}{x^3} + \frac{\Omega_{\text{rad},0}}{x^4} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0} + \frac{\Omega_{\text{ms},0}}{x^{3/2}} + \frac{\Omega_{k,0}}{x^2}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution; without the mysterious stuff it is on reading2 slides 1–2)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The curvature fraction">

Today $x = 1$ and $\dot x/x = H_0$, so the bracket must equal 1:

<Formula label="Curvature fraction with mysterious stuff" tex="\Omega_{k,0} = 1 - \Omega_{m,0} - \Omega_{\text{rad},0} - \Omega_{\text{vac},0} - \Omega_{\text{ms},0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

So $\Omega_{k,0}$ is not a new unknown. It is fixed by the four given fractions.

</Step>

<Step title="Separate the variables">

Take $1/x^4$ out of the bracket and then the square root. The universe is expanding, so the positive root applies:

<Formula label="Separating the variables" tex="\frac{\dot x}{x} = \frac{H_0}{x^2}\sqrt{F(x)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad dt = \frac{1}{H_0}\,\frac{x\,dx}{\sqrt{F(x)}}, \qquad F(x) = \Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{rad},0} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4 + \Omega_{\text{ms},0}\,x^{5/2} + \Omega_{k,0}\,x^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution; the same steps without the mysterious stuff are on reading1 p. 8)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Add up the time">

At the big bang $x = 0$, and today $x = 1$:

<Formula label="Age of a universe with mysterious stuff" tex="t_0 = \frac{1}{H_0}\int_0^1 \frac{x\,dx}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{rad},0} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4 + \Omega_{\text{ms},0}\,x^{5/2} + \left(1 - \Omega_{m,0} - \Omega_{\text{rad},0} - \Omega_{\text{vac},0} - \Omega_{\text{ms},0}\right)x^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Without mysterious stuff this is the age formula of the lesson.

With matter only ($\Omega_{m,0} = 1$, the rest zero) the integrand is $x/\sqrt{x} = \sqrt{x}$ and the integral is $2/3$: the familiar $t_0 = 2/(3H_0)$.

With mysterious stuff only ($\Omega_{\text{ms},0} = 1$) the integrand is $x/x^{5/4} = x^{-1/4}$, and the integral is $4/3$, so $t_0 = 4/(3H_0)$. This agrees with solving the Friedmann equation directly: $\dot a/a \propto a^{-3/4}$ gives $a \propto t^{4/3}$, and then $H = 4/(3t)$. The power $4/3$ is larger than 1, so such a universe speeds up. That fits the lesson on the cosmological constant. The rule for the density of an expanding gas, $\dot\rho = -3(\dot a/a)(\rho + p/c^2)$ (reading4, pp. 20–21), with $\rho \propto a^{-3/2}$ gives the pressure $p = -\tfrac12\rho c^2$. A pressure below $-\tfrac13\rho c^2$ makes the expansion accelerate.

</Step>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A shared causal past" points={20} mit="Problem 3">

In 2013 three of Guth's colleagues, Andrew Friedman, David Kaiser and Jason Gallicchio, published a paper on which events in the universe could ever have influenced each other. One of their results concerns a distant quasar with the following property. Suppose a light signal had left our own place in the universe at the moment of the big bang. It would have had just enough time to reach the quasar and come back, so that we would see its reflection exactly today. The question is at which redshift $z$ we see such a quasar today.

MIT's set says that the authors found $z = 3.65$, and gives as their parameters $\Omega_{\text{vac},0} = 0.685$, $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.315$, $\Omega_{\text{rad},0} = 0$ and $H_0 = 67.3$ km/s/Mpc. Guth's task for his students: do the calculation yourself and find out whether they got it right. Treat the model as exact all the way back to $t = 0$.

Both we and the quasar are at rest in the comoving coordinates.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Which statement is the condition that fixes this quasar?

<Hint>

On a comoving map we and the quasar do not move. Only the signal does: out and back along the same line.

</Hint>

<Hint>

How much comoving distance does light cover between the big bang and today? How much of it does the signal use for each leg of its trip?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="The quasar is the one for which:"
  choices={[
    "the comoving distance from us to the quasar equals the comoving distance that light covers between the big bang and today",
    "the comoving distance from us to the quasar is half the comoving distance that light covers between the big bang and today",
    "the light we see from the quasar today left it when the universe had half its present age",
    "the physical distance from us to the quasar today is half of the speed of light multiplied by the present age"
  ]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In comoving coordinates the two ends of the trip stay put, so the out and return legs have the same comoving length.

<Step title="The trip on the comoving map">

Call the comoving distance between us and the quasar $\ell_c$. In a short time $dt$ light covers the comoving distance $c\,dt/a(t)$. The signal leaves us at $t = 0$, reaches the quasar at some time $t_1$ and is back at $t_0$:

<Formula label="The two legs of the trip" tex="\ell_c = \int_0^{t_1}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} = \int_{t_1}^{t_0}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ell_c = \frac12\int_0^{t_0}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

The quasar sits at half the comoving distance that light covers in the whole history of the universe. (That full distance, turned into a physical distance today, is the horizon distance of the earlier lessons.)

</Step>

<Step title="Why the other statements fail">

A quasar at the full distance (the first statement) could only just be reached by now. Nothing could have come back. The third and fourth statements count in time and in physical distance, but light does not cover equal comoving distances in equal times: early on, when $a$ was small, it crossed the map much faster.

</Step>

<Step title="Why this is called a shared causal past">

Nothing travels faster than light. For a quasar nearer than this one, a signal from our place could have reached it and come back by today. For a quasar farther away there has not been enough time.

There is a second way to read the result. The light we see from this quasar today left it at $t_1$, the moment at which the signal from our place arrived (the next part shows this). So the quasar, as we see it, had only just come into contact with the place where we are now. Take a second quasar at the same distance in the opposite direction. The two, as we see them, could each have been reached from only one common place since the big bang: ours, at the very first moment. Quasars farther away than this, on opposite sides of the sky, share no such place at all. Nothing since the big bang can have influenced both. That is the statement in the paper's abstract, which treats pairs of quasars at any angle and any two redshifts. This problem is its simplest case.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

Calculate the redshift $z$ of this quasar for the parameters that MIT's set gives: a flat universe with $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.315$ and vacuum energy for the rest, and no radiation. The box accepts a value within 1% of ours.

<Hint>

Change the variable in both integrals from $t$ to $x = a/a(t_0)$ or to $z$, as in Problem 1(b). The comoving distance of a source at redshift $z$ is proportional to the integral $I(z)$ of Problem 1(b). The light we see today from the quasar left it at the redshift we are looking for.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The condition becomes $I(z) = \tfrac12 I(\infty)$. First find the number $I(\infty)$. In terms of $x$ it is the integral of $1/\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4}$ from 0 to 1.

</Hint>

<Hint>

That integrand grows without limit at $x = 0$, which upsets simple numerical methods, although the integral is finite. The substitution $x = u^2$ removes the trouble. Then look for the lower limit that makes the integral half as large, by trial and error or with a root finder.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$z =$" value="3.82" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-redshift-no-radiation" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write both comoving distances as integrals over $x$. One number, the total integral, sets the target. The redshift is where the integral from the quasar's $x$ to today reaches half of it.

<Step title="Comoving distance as an integral over x">

From the Friedmann equation, $dt = dx/(xH) = x\,dx/(H_0\sqrt{F(x)})$ with $F(x) = \Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4$ for this flat universe without radiation. Since $a = a(t_0)\,x$,

<Formula label="Comoving distance covered by light" tex="\int\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)} = \frac{c}{a(t_0)\,H_0}\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

The limits on the right are the values of $x$ at the two times on the left. The constant in front is the same for every distance in this problem, so only the pure numbers matter. With $x = 1/(1+z)$ the integral from $x$ to 1 is exactly $I(z)$ of Problem 1(b).

</Step>

<Step title="Which redshift belongs to the quasar">

The light that we see from the quasar today has covered the comoving distance $\ell_c$ and arrived at $t_0$. By part (a), so did the returning signal, which left the quasar at $t_1$. So the light we see today left the quasar at $t_1$ too, when the scale factor was $x_1$: we see the quasar at $z = 1/x_1 - 1$. The condition of part (a) is

<Formula label="The condition for the quasar" tex="\int_{x_1}^{1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4}} = \frac12\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4}}, \qquad 1 + z = \frac{1}{x_1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The total integral">

Near $x = 0$ the integrand behaves as $1/\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}\,x}$, which is infinite at $x = 0$ but has a finite integral. Put $x = u^2$, $dx = 2u\,du$:

<Formula label="A well-behaved form of the integral" tex="\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4}} = \int_0^1\frac{2\,du}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,u^6}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

The new integrand is smooth, between 2 and $2/\sqrt{0.315} = 3.56$. With $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.315$ and $\Omega_{\text{vac},0} = 0.685$, numerical integration gives 3.2402. Half of that is 1.6201.

</Step>

<Step title="Solve for the redshift">

Now find the lower limit for which the integral over $u$ up to 1 is 1.6201. A root finder, or a few tries, gives $u = 0.4553$ for it, so $x_1 = 0.4553^2 = 0.2073$ and

$z = 1/0.2073 - 1 = 3.82$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A case that can be done by hand: with matter only, the integral from $x_1$ to 1 is $2(1 - \sqrt{x_1})$ and the total is 2. The condition $2(1 - \sqrt{x_1}) = 1$ gives $x_1 = 1/4$, so $z = 3$ exactly. Vacuum energy changes the late part of the history only, so an answer somewhat above 3 is what one expects.

The result is not the paper's 3.65. With the numbers as MIT's set gives them, the difference is real: it is more than four times the tolerance of the answer box. Part (c) finds the reason.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

This part is our own addition. The real universe contains radiation. The lesson on the equality of matter and radiation gives, for photons and neutrinos together, $\Omega_{\text{rad},0} \approx 9.2 \times 10^{-5}$. Repeat part (b) with this radiation included. Keep $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.315$ and lower the vacuum energy by the same $9.2 \times 10^{-5}$, so that the universe stays flat. The box accepts a value within 1% of ours.

<Hint>

Only $F(x)$ changes: it gains the constant term $\Omega_{\text{rad},0}$. Small as it is, it is the largest term when $x$ is below about $3 \times 10^{-4}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With radiation the integrand is finite at $x = 0$, but it has a tall, narrow peak there. The substitution $x = u^2$ still helps.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c" label="$z =$" value="3.65" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-redshift-with-radiation" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Radiation makes the early universe expand faster, so light covers less comoving distance in the earliest times. The total shrinks, and the halfway point moves nearer.

<Step title="The integrals with radiation">

Now $F(x) = \Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{rad},0} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4$. With $x = u^2$ the total integral is

<Formula label="Total integral with radiation" tex="\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{rad},0} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4}} = \int_0^1\frac{2u\,du}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}\,u^2 + \Omega_{\text{rad},0} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,u^8}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3 (our own solution)" />

Numerically it is 3.1800, against 3.2402 without radiation: a drop of 1.9%. Half of it is 1.5900.

</Step>

<Step title="The redshift">

The integral from $x_1$ to 1 reaches 1.5900 at $x_1 = 0.2150$, so $z = 3.65$.

</Step>

<Step title="What this means">

With radiation the calculation gives the paper's number. So Friedman, Kaiser and Gallicchio got it right, and their 3.65 belongs to a model with radiation in it. The parameter list in MIT's set, with $\Omega_{\text{rad},0} = 0$, does not lead to 3.65 but to 3.82. [We take the zero to be a slip in the set. We have read only the abstract of the paper, which gives the 3.65 and says that the parameters are the best-fit values of the Planck satellite.]

A second check on the abstract: it also says that our universe has an event horizon at a present redshift of 1.87. That is the redshift of a source whose comoving distance equals the comoving distance light can still cover from today into the endless future, the integral of the same integrand from $x = 1$ to infinity. With the parameters of part (b) that integral is 1.1497, and $I(z)$ reaches it at $z = 1.87$. Radiation plays no part here, since it only matters at early times.

The lesson: a density fraction of less than one part in ten thousand today shifts this answer by 4.5%, because the answer depends on the whole history, and radiation ruled the earliest part of it.

</Step>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Plotting the supernova data" points={20} mit="Problem 5" optional>

The evidence that the expansion is speeding up came from two teams: the High-Z Supernova Search Team (Riess and others, 1998) and the Supernova Cosmology Project (Perlmutter and others, 1999). In 2004 Riess and others published a larger set that reaches beyond $z = 1$, thanks to supernovae found with the Hubble Space Telescope. MIT's set asks you to plot the data of the second or the third paper yourself, and to draw the predictions of three model universes through them:

1. a flat universe with matter only, $\Omega_{m,0} = 1$;
2. an open universe with matter only, $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$ and $\Omega_{\text{vac},0} = 0$;
3. a flat universe with $\Omega_{m,0} = 0.3$ and $\Omega_{\text{vac},0} = 0.7$.

MIT provides both data sets as text files: [the Supernova Cosmology Project table](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-286-the-early-universe-fall-2013/resources/mit8_286f13_pset8_supp/) and [the Riess 2004 table](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/8-286-the-early-universe-fall-2013/resources/mit8_286f13_pset8_supp2/). This problem follows the Riess 2004 table. It lists, for each supernova, the redshift, the **distance modulus** $\mu$ and its uncertainty. The distance modulus is the astronomers' measure of brightness from the lesson on the supernova evidence, which calls it $m - M$:

<Formula label="Distance modulus and luminosity distance" tex="\mu = 5\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d_L}{1\ \text{Mpc}}\right) + 25, \qquad d_L \equiv \sqrt{\frac{P}{4\pi J}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, pp. 4–5" />

The luminosity distance $d_L$ is the distance at which the source would look as bright as it does if space were static and Euclidean. A larger $\mu$ means a dimmer source.

To turn a model into a curve of $\mu$ against $z$ you need a value of $H_0$. The paper fixed its zero point arbitrarily, because its analysis uses only differences between the magnitudes of supernovae. Guth found that the curve in the paper is matched best with $H_0 = 66$ km/s/Mpc, a little below the values of 70 to 72 that were usual at the time. A different $H_0$ moves a whole curve up or down without changing its shape. So does our ignorance of the true power of a type Ia supernova. You are therefore free to slide each model curve up or down for the best fit, by eye or by a method of your own.

(The other table, from the Supernova Cosmology Project, lists an "effective magnitude" $m = -\tfrac52\log_{10}J + \text{constant}$. It is plotted in the same way, with the vertical position of the curves completely free.)

- the difference $\mu_3 - \mu_1$ between models 3 and 1 (within 2%).

<Solution>

**The idea.** Problem 1 already contains the luminosity distance of every model. Write it as $c/H_0$ times a pure number, and take the logarithm.

<Step title="The luminosity distance of the three kinds of universe">

Comparing $J = P/(4\pi d_L^2)$ with the results of Problem 1:

<Formula label="Luminosity distance, flat and open" tex="\frac{H_0\,d_L}{c} = (1+z)\,I(z) \;\;\text{(flat)}, \qquad \frac{H_0\,d_L}{c} = (1+z)\,\frac{\sinh\!\left(\sqrt{\Omega_{k,0}}\,I_k(z)\right)}{\sqrt{\Omega_{k,0}}} \;\;\text{(open)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution, from Problem 1)" />

Here $I_k$ is the integral with the curvature term under the root, as in Problem 1.

</Step>

<Step title="The three models at z = 1">

Model 1: $I(1) = 2(1 - 1/\sqrt2) = 0.5858$, so $H_0 d_L/c = 2 \times 0.5858 = 1.1716$.

Model 2: $\Omega_{k,0} = 0.7$. Numerically $I_k(1) = 0.6527$, so $\psi_D = \sqrt{0.7} \times 0.6527 = 0.5461$ and $H_0 d_L/c = 2\sinh(0.5461)/\sqrt{0.7} = 1.3712$ (the number checked in Problem 1(a)).

Model 3: $I(1) = 0.77143$ from Problem 4, so $H_0 d_L/c = 1.5429$.

</Step>

<Step title="The distance moduli">

With $c = 299\,792$ km/s and $H_0 = 66$ km/s/Mpc, $c/H_0 = 4542.3$ Mpc. For model 1, $d_L = 1.17157 \times 4542.3 = 5321.6$ Mpc and

$\mu_1 = 5\log_{10}(5321.6) + 25 = 43.63$.

The differences need no $H_0$:

$\mu_2 - \mu_1 = 5\log_{10}(1.3712/1.1716) = 0.342$ and $\mu_3 - \mu_1 = 5\log_{10}(1.5429/1.1716) = 0.598$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A difference of 0.598 magnitudes is a factor $(1.5429/1.1716)^2 = 1.73$ in flux: at $z = 1$ a supernova in model 3 delivers only 58% of the light it would in model 1. The lesson on brightness finds the same 58% for these two models.

The open model lies between the other two. The whole question the data must settle is a difference of 0.26 magnitudes between models 2 and 3, about a quarter of a magnitude, at a redshift where each single supernova is uncertain by about as much. It takes many supernovae.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

A systematic way to slide a curve: for each supernova take the measured $\mu$ minus the model's $\mu$. The best shift is the average of these differences, weighted by one over the square of each uncertainty.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The data are points with error bars. Each model is a curve whose shape is fixed and whose height is free. The model whose shape follows the points wins.

<Step title="The curves">

For each $z$ compute $H_0 d_L/c$ as in part (a), then $\mu = 5\log_{10}(H_0 d_L/c) + 5\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{c/H_0}{1\ \text{Mpc}}\right) + 25$. The second term is the only place where $H_0$ enters: it is the free height of the curve. For models 1 and 2 the integral can be avoided, since a universe with matter only has the closed form quoted in the check of Problem 1(a).

</Step>

<Step title="The fit">

Number the supernovae with $n$. Supernova $n$ has the redshift $z_n$, the measured distance modulus $\mu_n$ and the uncertainty $\sigma_n$, and the model predicts $\mu_\text{model}(z_n)$. The best shift $\Delta$ is the weighted average of $\mu_n - \mu_\text{model}(z_n)$ with weights $1/\sigma_n^2$. How well the shifted model fits is measured by

<Formula label="Chi-squared of a model" tex="\chi^2 = \sum_n \frac{\left[\mu_n - \mu_\text{model}(z_n) - \Delta\right]^2}{\sigma_n^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5 (our own solution; the standard measure of a fit, not from MIT's set)" />

A good fit has a $\chi^2$ about equal to the number of data points, because each point then misses the curve by about its own uncertainty.

</Step>

<Step title="The result">

Our own calculation with the 157 gold supernovae of MIT's table, each curve starting from $H_0 = 66$ km/s/Mpc and then shifted:

| Model | Best shift $\Delta$ (magnitudes) | $\chi^2$ for 157 supernovae |
|---|---|---|
| 1: flat, matter only | 0.30 | 325 |
| 2: open, matter only | 0.18 | 211 |
| 3: flat, with vacuum energy | 0.05 | 177 |

Model 3 needs almost no shift and has by far the smallest $\chi^2$, not much above the number of supernovae. Model 1 misses by about twice that. The section below shows the plot.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Solution>

<Step title="Which model fits">

Around $z = 0.5$ the supernovae lie above the curves of the two models without vacuum energy: they are dimmer, so farther away, than a universe that has only slowed down allows. Model 3 passes through them. That is the 1998 discovery, and the 2004 data make it firmer.

</Step>

<Step title="What higher redshifts can tell">

Between $z = 1$ and $z = 2$ the curves of models 2 and 3 come closer together again: their difference is 0.26 magnitudes at $z = 1$ and 0.19 at $z = 2$. In a universe with vacuum energy the expansion was still slowing down at early times, when matter was denser, and only later began to speed up. A cause of dimming that has nothing to do with cosmology, such as dust between the galaxies or supernovae that were fainter when the universe was young, would be expected to grow steadily with distance. Supernovae beyond $z = 1$ can therefore tell the two explanations apart. The abstract of the 2004 paper reports just that: evidence that an era of slowing down came before the present speeding up, and data that do not fit a simple model of dust or evolution.

</Step>
