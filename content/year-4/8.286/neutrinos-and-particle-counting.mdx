The last lecture gave the black-body formulas for photons, with "fudge factors" $g$ and $g^*$. Lecture 17 explains what they are for: other particles also contribute to black-body radiation, each with its own $g$. Adding them up, and following what happens when some disappear, accounts for the radiation density of today's universe.

## Recap, and a question about entropy

Guth's slides (2–5) review Lecture 16: $a \propto \sqrt t$, $H = 1/(2t)$, the $2ct$ horizon and $\rho = 3/(32\pi Gt^2)$ for a flat radiation-dominated universe, and the formulas for $u$, $p$, $n$ and $s$. Photons have $g = 2$ because they are massless spin-1 particles with only the two extreme spin states, $\pm 1$ along the motion, the quantum version of splitting light into left- and right-circular (or $x$ and $y$) polarisation.

Entropy, slide 5 adds, is conserved in the early universe for every process the course discusses, **except inflation**. Guth explains aloud (transcript p. 4) that if inflation is right, nearly all of today's entropy was produced in the phase transition at its end.

Asked why entropy only increases, Guth says that, among physicists, you might get ten different answers. It does not follow from the other known laws, which are essentially time-reversal invariant. The usual story is that the universe began, for unknown reasons, in a state of peculiarly low entropy and has been heading towards equilibrium ever since. Guth mentions that he was writing a paper with a slightly different idea; the arrow of time, he says, remains something of a mystery.

## Neutrinos as massless fermions

Neutrinos carry a significant share of the universe's entropy even today. They come in three flavours ($\nu_e$, $\nu_\mu$, $\nu_\tau$); oscillation between flavours shows that the mass differences are non-zero, so at most one could be massless. For cosmology they can be treated as massless, though that needs justifying, because mass changes the number of spin states.

**Helicity.** In the massless approximation a neutrino has **one** spin state: it is **left-handed**, with its spin opposite to its momentum. Antineutrinos are right-handed. This is a Lorentz-invariant statement only for a massless particle. If a neutrino had mass, it would move slower than light, and a fast enough rocket could overtake it: in the rocket's frame the momentum would reverse while the spin would not, turning a left-handed neutrino into a right-handed one.

A student asks how a spin-½ particle can have only one spin state. For massless particles this is consistent: the photon, with spin 1, lacks the $m = 0$ state. Only for massive particles do Lorentz transformations mix all spin states, so that having one forces having all.

**Fermions and bosons.** Neutrinos have spin ½. By the **spin–statistics theorem** of quantum field theory, half-integer spin means **fermion** (obeying the exclusion principle: no two in the same state), integer spin means **boson** (photons; bosons even prefer sharing states). The black-body formulas come from counting states, each weighted by $e^{-E/kT}$. Fermions allow fewer states, so their factors are smaller:

<Formula label="Fermion factors" tex="g_\text{fermion} = \tfrac78\,\times(\text{boson value}), \qquad g^*_\text{fermion} = \tfrac34\,\times(\text{boson value})" cite="reading1" at="p. 8 (board)" />

The 7/8 is bigger than the 3/4, so fermions carry slightly more energy per particle, as you'd expect, since the exclusion principle pushes particles into higher states.

## Adding up the species

Each species' $g$ is a product of factors: 7/8 for fermions, the number of species, 2 if particle and antiparticle differ, and the number of spin states. For the three neutrinos:

<Formula label="Neutrinos" tex="g_\nu = \tfrac78 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = \tfrac{21}{4}, \qquad g^*_\nu = \tfrac34 \times 3 \times 2 \times 1 = \tfrac92" cite="reading1" at="pp. 8–9 (board)" />

(A photon is its own antiparticle, so photons get no factor 2 for that.) Go further back, to where $kT \gg m_ec^2$, and electron–positron pairs join the black-body radiation too, one species, particle and antiparticle, two spin states:

<Formula label="Electrons and positrons" tex="g_{e^\pm} = \tfrac78 \times 1 \times 2 \times 2 = \tfrac72, \qquad g^*_{e^\pm} = \tfrac34 \times 1 \times 2 \times 2 = 3" cite="reading1" at="p. 10 (board)" />

Between the electron threshold ($m_ec^2 \approx 0.5$ MeV) and the next one, the muon ($m_\mu c^2 \approx 106$ MeV), all three are present at the same temperature:

<Formula label="Total g" tex="g_\text{total} = 2 + \tfrac{21}{4} + \tfrac72 = 10\tfrac34 \qquad (m_ec^2 \ll kT \ll m_\mu c^2)" cite="reading1" at="pp. 10–11 (board)" />

This number governs a long stretch of the universe's history.

## Why the neutrinos are colder

Today $kT$ is far below $m_ec^2$, so the electron–positron pairs are long gone, leaving photons and neutrinos. But we can't simply add their formulas, because they no longer share a temperature. (Working it out is an 8.286 homework problem; Guth gives the outline.)

As $kT$ fell below $m_ec^2$, the pairs left the thermal mix. Following their **energy** is hard, because it depends on the pressure, which is complicated near a threshold. Their **entropy** is easy: it is conserved. By then the photons were still interacting strongly, but the neutrinos had essentially **decoupled**. So the pairs handed nearly all their entropy to the photons and none to the neutrinos, heating the photons relative to the neutrinos. The result:

<Formula label="Neutrino temperature" tex="T_\nu = \left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{1/3} T_\gamma" cite="reading1" at="p. 12 (board)" />

So the universe should be filled with a thermal bath of neutrinos at about 1.95 K, a little colder than the 2.725 K photons. [Guth recalls the value as "about 2.4" K; $(4/11)^{1/3} \times 2.725$ K $= 1.95$ K.] Neutrinos interact so weakly that nobody has come close to detecting this background, a challenge, Guth tells the students, for their careers. If it turned out not to be there, that would be a major shift in cosmology.

With the neutrino temperature in hand, the radiation density of today's universe follows. Energy density goes as $T^4$, so the neutrino term carries $(4/11)^{4/3}$:

<Formula label="Radiation energy density today" tex="u_{r,0} = \left[2 + \frac{21}{4}\left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{4/3}\right]\frac{\pi^2}{30}\,\frac{(kT_\gamma)^4}{(\hbar c)^3} = 7.01 \times 10^{-14}\ \tfrac{\text{J}}{\text{m}^3}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 12–13 (board)" />

This is the number Guth pulled "out of a hat" in Lecture 15 to date matter–radiation equality.

<WorkedExample title="The neutrino sea">
<Problem>
Using $T_\gamma = 2.725$ K, find today's neutrino temperature and the number density of background neutrinos (all three flavours, with antineutrinos), and compare with the 410 photons per cm³.
</Problem>
<Step title="Temperature">
$T_\nu = (4/11)^{1/3} \times 2.725$ K $= 0.714 \times 2.725$ K $= 1.95$ K.
</Step>
<Step title="Number density">
The ratio to photons is $\dfrac{g^*_\nu}{g^*_\gamma}\left(\dfrac{T_\nu}{T_\gamma}\right)^3 = \dfrac{9/2}{2} \times \dfrac{4}{11} = \dfrac{9}{11}$. So $n_\nu = \frac{9}{11} \times 410.5 \approx 336$ per cm³, about 112 per flavour.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy density">
$u_\nu/u_\gamma = \frac{21/4}{2}\left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{4/3} = 0.681$, so neutrinos add 68% to the photons' $4.17 \times 10^{-14}$ J/m³, giving $7.01 \times 10^{-14}$ J/m³ in total.
</Step>
<Step title="Perspective">
About 336 of them are present in every cubic centimetre, including inside you, yet they are undetectable with current technology: they carry extremely little energy and barely interact.
</Step>

## Neutrino masses: Dirac or Majorana?

At least two neutrinos do have mass. Then the counting above seems wrong: a massive particle must have both spin states, so where are the **right-handed neutrinos**, which no one has ever seen? There are two theories (both named after people, so don't read physics into the names), and we don't yet know which is right.

- **Dirac mass**, the same kind of mass the electron has. Right-handed neutrinos would be a **new species**. Because of the peculiar way neutrinos interact, they could interact vastly more weakly than the left-handed ones, so weakly that they are not seen in the laboratory and were essentially never produced even in the early universe.
- **Majorana mass**, possible only for neutral particles, and not known for any other particle. The right-handed partner of the left-handed neutrino would be a particle we already know: the right-handed **antineutrino**. The neutrino would be its own antiparticle, like the photon.

Either way, the counting above stands. For Majorana neutrinos the factor 2 counts two spin states instead of particle and antiparticle, with the same result. What distinguishes the options is **lepton number**: in every interaction seen, electron number (electrons and electron-neutrinos minus their antiparticles), and likewise muon and tau number, is conserved. A Majorana mass requires lepton number not to be exactly conserved. A student points out that oscillations already violate the separate electron, muon and tau numbers; Guth agrees, but total lepton number might still be conserved, which is believed not to be exactly true but not ruled out.

<Callout kind="note" title="Later developments">
As of 2026, whether neutrinos are Dirac or Majorana particles is still unknown. The main test is neutrinoless double-beta decay, possible only if neutrinos are Majorana particles and lepton number is violated; the most sensitive searches, such as KamLAND-Zen, have not seen it. The cosmic neutrino background has not been detected directly, but its effect on the expansion is seen indirectly: Planck 2018 measures an effective number of neutrino species $N_\text{eff} = 2.99 \pm 0.17$, consistent with the three expected.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Where this leads">
With $g$ known for each era, the black-body formulas give the temperature of the early universe as a function of time (the next page), and the entropy argument above explains the photon-to-neutrino ratio we infer today.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Each effectively massless species adds to black-body radiation; fermions count with $\frac78$ in $g$ (energy, pressure, entropy) and $\frac34$ in $g^*$ (number).
- Massless neutrinos are left-handed (antineutrinos right-handed): $g_\nu = 21/4$, $g^*_\nu = 9/2$ for three flavours. Electrons and positrons: $g = 7/2$, $g^* = 3$.
- For $m_ec^2 \ll kT \ll m_\mu c^2$, $g = 10\frac34$.
- When the $e^\pm$ pairs disappeared, their entropy went to the photons, not the already decoupled neutrinos: $T_\nu = (4/11)^{1/3}T_\gamma \approx 1.95$ K, and today $u_r = 7.01 \times 10^{-14}$ J/m³.
- Neutrino masses may be Dirac (new, very weakly interacting right-handed neutrinos) or Majorana (neutrino = antineutrino, lepton number not conserved); the counting is the same either way.
