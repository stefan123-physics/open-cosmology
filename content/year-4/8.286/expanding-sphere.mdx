Gravity cannot leave a uniform universe standing still, as the [previous page](/courses/8-286/newtons-static-universe) showed. So how does it change? In Lecture 6 Guth answers with a model that uses only Newton's law of gravity, and promises that, for ordinary matter, it gives exactly the same answer as general relativity.

## The model

To keep infinity under control, start with something finite and take a limit at the end:

- At some initial time $t_i$, take a **sphere** of radius $R_{\max,i}$, filled uniformly with matter of density $\rho_i$. A sphere is the simplest shape that keeps the model the same in every direction.
- Give it a **Hubble flow**: every particle moves away from the centre with velocity proportional to its distance.

<Formula label="Initial Hubble flow" tex="\vec v_i = H_i\,\vec r" cite="reading2" at="slide 8" />

- Treat the matter as "dust": particles with no pressure, moving slowly compared with light. This describes the real universe from about 50,000 years after the Big Bang (when matter took over from radiation) until a few billion years ago (when dark energy took over).

## Following the shells

Divide the sphere into thin spherical **shells**. Label each by its initial radius $r_i$, and let $r(r_i, t)$ be its radius at time $t$. All velocities and forces point radially, so each shell stays a shell.

By the shell theorem, a shell feels only the mass **inside** it, and as long as shells don't cross, that mass never changes:

<Formula label="Mass inside a shell" tex="M(r_i) = \frac{4\pi}{3}\,r_i^3\,\rho_i" cite="reading1" />

Initially every shell moves outward faster than the ones inside it, so none are about to cross. Guth's argument: the equations, valid up to any first crossing, would have to predict that crossing, and they never do. So there is none.

Newton's second law for the shell is then

<Formula label="Equation of motion of a shell" tex="\ddot r = -\frac{G\,M(r_i)}{r^2} = -\frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\,r_i^3\,\rho_i}{r^2}, \qquad r(r_i, t_i) = r_i,\quad \dot r(r_i, t_i) = H_i\,r_i" cite="reading1" />

## The cancellation

Now the key step. Measure each shell's radius relative to where it started, $u(r_i, t) \equiv r(r_i, t)/r_i$. Dividing the equation by $r_i$ and writing $r = u\,r_i$:

<Formula label="Rescaled equation" tex="\ddot u = -\frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\,r_i^3\,\rho_i}{r_i\,u^2\,r_i^2} = -\frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\rho_i}{u^2}, \qquad u(t_i) = 1,\quad \dot u(t_i) = H_i" cite="reading1" />

The $r_i^3$ from the mass has cancelled against the $r_i^3$ from rescaling the force. **Neither the equation nor its starting values depend on $r_i$ any more**, so every shell follows the same function of time. That function is the scale factor, $u = a(t)$, and

<Formula label="Uniform expansion stays uniform" tex="r(r_i, t) = a(t)\,r_i" cite="reading1" />

Guth emphasises that this works because gravity is *exactly* inverse-square: with a force law of most other forms, the cancellation would fail and the expansion would not stay uniform. (A force growing in proportion to distance would also preserve uniformity: that is how a cosmological constant behaves, a later topic.) The radius $R_{\max,i}$ never appeared, so letting the sphere become infinite changes nothing.

## Density and deceleration

The mass inside a shell is fixed while its volume grows as $a^3$, so the density falls as

<Formula label="Matter density" tex="\rho(t) = \frac{\rho_i}{a^3(t)}" cite="reading1" />

Putting this back into the rescaled equation gives the **equation of motion of the universe** (for matter only):

<Formula label="Deceleration of the scale factor" tex="\ddot a = -\frac{4\pi}{3}\,G\,\rho(t)\,a" cite="reading1" />

The physically meaningful combination is $\ddot a/a$: the deceleration rate depends only on the density at that moment. (In Guth's convention, set up formally in Lecture 7, $a$ carries units of metres per notch, and these units cancel only in $\ddot a/a$.)

<WorkedExample title="How hard is gravity braking the expansion?">
<Problem>
Ignore dark energy and take today's matter density as $\rho = 2.7\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg/m}^3$ and $H = 70$ km/s/Mpc $= 2.27\times10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Compute $\ddot a/a$ and compare it with $H^2 = (\dot a/a)^2$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Deceleration rate">
$\ddot a/a = -\tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\rho = -4.19 \times 6.67\times10^{-11} \times 2.7\times10^{-27} = -7.5\times10^{-37}\ \text{s}^{-2}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Expansion rate squared">
$H^2 = (2.27\times10^{-18})^2 = 5.1\times10^{-36}\ \text{s}^{-2}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
The ratio is about $0.15$: in one Hubble time, gravity from matter alone would slow the recession speed $\dot a$ of any given galaxy by roughly 15%. (The Hubble rate $H = \dot a/a$ falls faster, because $a$ also grows.) In reality dark energy more than cancels this braking today; see [the acceleration page](/courses/8-01/acceleration-1d) and the later lectures on the cosmological constant.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## A conserved quantity

The equation $\ddot a = -\tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\rho_i/a^2$ can be integrated once. Multiply by $\dot a$ and notice that the result is a total time derivative:

<Formula label="Conserved quantity E" tex="E = \tfrac12\,\dot a^2 - \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\rho_i}{a}, \qquad \frac{dE}{dt} = \dot a\left[\ddot a + \frac{4\pi}{3}\frac{G\rho_i}{a^2}\right] = 0" cite="reading1" />

Multiplied by $m\,r_i^2$, $E$ is the kinetic plus potential energy of a test particle of mass $m$ on the outer edge of the sphere. (For a particle inside the sphere this is not its true potential energy, because the outer shells also contribute; Guth notes the interpretation is cleanest at the edge.) So, as Lecture 7 develops, the universe behaves like a ball thrown upward:

- $E > 0$: the expansion slows but never stops.
- $E < 0$: the expansion stops at a maximum size and the universe recollapses.
- $E = 0$: the borderline case, just barely expanding forever.

The next lecture works out these three behaviours in time and names them **closed**, **open** and **flat**; what those names mean for the geometry of space comes later in the course.

## Summary

- A uniformly expanding sphere of dust, followed shell by shell with Newton's gravity, stays uniform: $r = a(t)\,r_i$.
- This works only because gravity is exactly inverse-square; the result does not depend on the sphere's size.
- Matter density falls as $\rho \propto 1/a^3$, and the scale factor obeys $\ddot a = -\tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\rho\,a$.
- $E = \tfrac12\dot a^2 - \tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\rho_i/a$ is conserved; its sign decides between eternal expansion and recollapse.
