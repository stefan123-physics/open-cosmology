The [previous page](/courses/8-286/non-euclidean-geometry) introduced Gauss's idea: a geometry is fixed by its **metric**, the distance between nearby points. Lecture 11 puts that to work on the simplest curved space, the surface of a sphere, and then climbs one dimension to the space of a **closed universe**.

## Why spheres?

A sphere is special because it is **homogeneous** and **isotropic** within its own surface: a two-dimensional creature living on it finds every point and every direction equivalent. That is exactly the property our universe has on large scales. So the curved spaces cosmology needs are spheres (positive curvature), flat space, and their negatively curved relative.

## The 2-sphere

Take the sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = R^2$ in ordinary three-dimensional space, and label points with the usual polar angles: $\theta$ measured down from the $z$-axis and $\phi$ around it. Consider two points that differ by $d\theta$ and $d\phi$, and change one angle at a time:

- **Change $\theta$:** you move along a great circle (a meridian) of radius $R$, so $ds = R\,d\theta$.
- **Change $\phi$:** you move around a circle of latitude, whose radius is $R\sin\theta$ (zero at the poles, largest at the equator), so $ds = R\sin\theta\,d\phi$.

The two displacements are perpendicular, one along a meridian and one along a circle of latitude. Pythagoras then gives the metric:

<Formula label="Metric of a 2-sphere" tex="ds^2 = R^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 7; slides 19–20" />

Now the key move: **forget the three-dimensional space**. A two-dimensional creature needs only the coordinates $\theta, \phi$ and this formula to do all of its geometry. Gauss's "inner properties" are all here.

Guth also shows a brute-force route that needs no pictures. Write $x = R\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y = R\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z = R\cos\theta$, use the chain rule for $dx$, $dy$, $dz$, and add $dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$. After several uses of $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$, the same metric comes out.

<WorkedExample title="Circles on a sphere">
<Problem>
A creature on a sphere of radius $R$ stands at the North Pole and walks a distance $r$ along the surface in every direction, tracing a circle. What is the circle's circumference? Check it for the equator.
</Problem>
<Step title="Set up">
Walking distance $r$ from the pole along a meridian means $\theta = r/R$. The circle is the set of points at that $\theta$, with $\phi$ running from 0 to $2\pi$.
</Step>
<Step title="Use the metric">
Along the circle only $\phi$ changes, so $ds = R\sin\theta\,d\phi$ and the circumference is $2\pi R\sin(r/R)$.
</Step>
<Step title="The equator">
The equator is at $r = \pi R/2$, so its circumference is $2\pi R$, while a flat plane would give $2\pi r = \pi^2 R$. The ratio is $2/\pi \approx 0.64$: circles on a sphere are smaller than their radius suggests. That is how the creature can detect curvature without leaving its world.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## One dimension up: the 3-sphere

A closed universe has three space dimensions, so we need the three-dimensional analogue: the **3-sphere**, the set of points

<Formula label="3-sphere in four dimensions" tex="x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + w^2 = R^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 10" />

in a four-dimensional Euclidean space. Nobody can picture four dimensions, and Guth's advice is: usually don't; the equations are enough. One crutch helps, though. Pretend the fourth coordinate $w$ is time. At each "moment" $w$, the slice through the 3-sphere is an ordinary 2-sphere of radius $\sqrt{R^2 - w^2}$: it appears as a point at $w = -R$, grows to radius $R$ at $w = 0$, and shrinks back to a point at $w = +R$.

  yLabel="radius of the 2-sphere slice (units of R)"

  caption="Slicing a 3-sphere: each slice at fixed w is an ordinary sphere, computed from x² + y² + z² = R² − w². Stacking them gives the 3-sphere."
/>

To give the 3-sphere coordinates, add a new angle $\psi$ measured from the $w$-axis, just as $\theta$ was measured from the $z$-axis:

<Formula label="The new angle ψ" tex="w = R\cos\psi, \qquad \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2} = R\sin\psi" cite="reading2" at="slide 21" />

Then multiply the 2-sphere formulas for $x, y, z$ by $\sin\psi$. The recipe repeats in any number of dimensions: add an axis, add an angle measured from it, and multiply the old coordinates by its sine. The angles range over $0 \le \psi \le \pi$, $0 \le \theta \le \pi$ and $0 \le \phi < 2\pi$.

Now find the metric as before. Changing $\psi$ alone moves along a circle of radius $R$: $ds = R\,d\psi$. Changing $\theta$ and $\phi$ alone moves on a 2-sphere of radius $R\sin\psi$. And the two kinds of displacement are perpendicular. Changing $\theta$ or $\phi$ leaves $w$ unchanged, so the $w$ part of the four-dimensional dot product vanishes. In the remaining $(x, y, z)$ directions, changing $\psi$ rescales all three by a common factor, a radial move, while changing $\theta$ or $\phi$ moves tangentially along a sphere. So once again the squares add:

<Formula label="Metric of a 3-sphere" tex="ds^2 = R^2\left[d\psi^2 + \sin^2\psi\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="pp. 14–15" />

This, Guth says, is precisely the space of a closed universe: homogeneous and isotropic. (Unlike flat space, it is also finite in volume.)

## Coordinates are arbitrary

In curved space there is no single natural coordinate system, and coordinates are not distances. A useful alternative to $\psi$ is

<Formula label="The u coordinate" tex="u \equiv \sin\psi, \qquad ds^2 = R^2\left[\frac{du^2}{1 - u^2} + u^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 18" />

(using $du = \cos\psi\,d\psi = \sqrt{1-u^2}\,d\psi$). Two warnings come with it. First, each value of $u$ below 1 labels **two** points, one on each "hemisphere" in $w$; $u = 1$ is the equator between them. Second, near the origin ($u \ll 1$) the factor $1/(1-u^2)$ is almost 1, and the metric becomes the ordinary flat metric in polar coordinates: the space is locally Euclidean, as Gauss's theorem promises. Isotropy about the origin is obvious in this form; homogeneity is still true but hidden, because moving the origin corresponds to a rotation in the four-dimensional space.

## Summary

- On a 2-sphere, varying $\theta$ gives $ds = R\,d\theta$ and varying $\phi$ gives $ds = R\sin\theta\,d\phi$; they are perpendicular, so $ds^2 = R^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)$.
- The 3-sphere $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 + w^2 = R^2$ gets a third angle $\psi$, and $ds^2 = R^2[d\psi^2 + \sin^2\psi(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)]$: the space of a closed universe.
- With $u = \sin\psi$ the metric becomes $R^2[du^2/(1-u^2) + u^2(\ldots)]$, visibly flat near the origin.
- Once the metric is known, the embedding space can be forgotten: the geometry is fully inside the metric.
