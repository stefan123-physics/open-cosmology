## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Sound when the source and the observer both move" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

A loudspeaker and a listener are on one straight line, in air that is at rest. Sound travels at speed $u$ relative to the air. The loudspeaker moves along the line at speed $v_s$ relative to the air, away from the listener. The listener moves in the opposite direction at speed $v_o$ relative to the air, so the two are moving apart. Both speeds are smaller than $u$.

Find the Doppler shift $z$ that the listener measures, in terms of $u$, $v_s$ and $v_o$. As in the lesson, $z$ is defined by $1 + z = \lambda_O/\lambda_S$, the wavelength at the observer divided by the wavelength at the source. For sound this is the same as the ratio of the times between crests, $\Delta t_O/\Delta t_S$.

<Hint>

You can solve this from the start, but there is a shorter way. The lesson already has two results: one for a moving source and one for a moving observer. In both of them, what is at rest?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Imagine a third person who stands still in the air, somewhere between the loudspeaker and the listener. Compare the loudspeaker with this person first. Then compare this person with the listener.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each comparison gives a ratio of two times between crests. To go from the loudspeaker to the listener, think about what happens to two ratios that share a middle step: do they add, or do they multiply?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-z" label="$z =$" expr="(vs + vo)/(u - vo)"
  vars={{ u: [300, 400], vs: [1, 50], vo: [1, 50] }}
  names={{ vs: "v_s", vo: "v_o" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(u + vs)/(u - vo)", hint: "That is 1 + z, the ratio of the two times. Take away 1 to get z." },
    { expr: "(vs + vo)/u", hint: "This treats the listener like a second moving source. A moving observer changes the time between crests in a different way from a moving source." },
    { expr: "vs/u + vo/(u - vo)", hint: "You added the two shifts. Check how two ratios in a row combine." },
    { expr: "(vs + vo)/(u - vs)", hint: "Look again at which of the two speeds belongs below the line: the one of the sender, or the one of the receiver?" },
    { expr: "(vs + vo)/(u + vs)", hint: "This is the shift in frequency, not in wavelength. Here 1 + z is the observed time between crests divided by the emitted one." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Sound moves at $u$ relative to the air, so the air is the frame in which the lesson's two results hold. Put an imaginary person A at rest in the air, between the loudspeaker and the listener, and call the time between crests that A measures $\Delta t_A$. The motion then splits into two steps that are already solved.

<Step title="From the loudspeaker to A: a moving source">

A is at rest in the air and the loudspeaker moves away from A at $v_s$. This is the lesson's first case, with $v_s$ as the speed of the source:

<Formula label="Source moving, observer at rest in the air" tex="\frac{\Delta t_A}{\Delta t_S} = 1 + \frac{v_s}{u}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 1–2 (the moving-source case)" />

</Step>

<Step title="From A to the listener: a moving observer">

The crests that pass A travel on through the still air to the listener, exactly as if A were a source at rest that sends a crest every $\Delta t_A$. The listener moves away from A at $v_o$. This is the lesson's second case, with $v_o$ as the speed of the observer and with $\Delta t_A$ in the place of the source's time:

<Formula label="Source at rest in the air, observer moving" tex="\frac{\Delta t_O}{\Delta t_A} = \frac{1}{1 - v_o/u}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 3–4 (the moving-observer case)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Combine the two steps">

The middle time cancels when the two ratios are multiplied:

<Formula label="Both moving" tex="1 + z = \frac{\Delta t_O}{\Delta t_S} = \frac{\Delta t_O}{\Delta t_A}\cdot\frac{\Delta t_A}{\Delta t_S} = \frac{1 + v_s/u}{1 - v_o/u} = \frac{u + v_s}{u - v_o}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

Taking away 1 gives the answer:

<Formula label="Doppler shift, source and observer both moving" tex="z = \frac{u + v_s}{u - v_o} - 1 = \frac{v_s + v_o}{u - v_o}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result has no units, as a ratio of speeds should. With the listener at rest ($v_o = 0$) it becomes $z = v_s/u$, the moving-source result. With the loudspeaker at rest ($v_s = 0$) it becomes $z = (v_o/u)/(1 - v_o/u)$, the moving-observer result. As $v_o$ approaches $u$ the shift grows without limit: a listener who moves away as fast as the sound is never reached by the next crest.

The two speeds do not enter in the same way: only the listener's speed is below the line. For example, with $u = 340$ m/s, $v_s = 20$ m/s and $v_o = 30$ m/s the shift is $z = 50/310 = 0.161$. Simply adding the speeds, $(v_s + v_o)/u$, would give $0.147$, which is too small.

The same answer follows without the two cases, by following two crests directly: write down when each one leaves the loudspeaker and when it catches up with the listener, and divide the difference of the arrival times by the difference of the emission times.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The transverse Doppler shift" points={25} mit="Problem 2">

A source of waves moves at constant speed $v$ on a circle of radius $R$. An observer stands still at the centre of the circle and receives the waves. In parts (d) and (e) the circle is replaced by a straight line.

In every part, find the Doppler shift $z$, with $1 + z = \Delta t_O/\Delta t_S$: the time between crests on the observer's clock divided by the time between crests on the source's clock.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

The wave is sound, which travels at speed $u$ relative to the air. The observer is at rest in the air. Both $v$ and $u$ are far below the speed of light $c$, so relativity plays no part. What is $z$?

<Hint>

Follow two crests in a row. How far does each one travel from the source to the observer?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Each crest needs a certain time for its trip. Compare the two trip times, then compare the time between the two departures with the time between the two arrivals.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$z =$" expr="0"
  vars={{ v: [1, 50], u: [300, 400] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "v/u", hint: "That is the shift of a source that moves straight away from the observer. Does the distance between this source and the observer change?" },
    { expr: "v/(u - v)", hint: "That belongs to motion along the line between the two. Here the source moves across that line. Does its distance from the observer change?" }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A Doppler shift of sound comes from a change in the travel time from one crest to the next. Here nothing changes.

<Step title="Every crest has the same trip">

The source is always at distance $R$ from the observer, and the air between them is at rest. So every crest travels the same distance $R$ at the same speed $u$ and needs the same time $R/u$.

</Step>

<Step title="Departures and arrivals">

If two crests leave a time $\Delta t_S$ apart and both take $R/u$ to arrive, they arrive the same time apart:

<Formula label="Sound from a source on a circle around the observer" tex="\Delta t_O = \Delta t_S \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The lesson's result for a source that moves away is $z = v/u$, where $v$ is the speed at which the distance grows. On the circle the distance grows at speed zero, and the formula gives $z = 0$ as well. Neither $v$ nor $R$ appears in the answer: the listener hears the pitch that the source sends.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Now the wave is light, which travels at speed $c$, and $v$ is not small compared with $c$. The source still circles the observer. What is $z$, in terms of $v$ and $c$?

(This is called the transverse Doppler shift: seen from the observer's frame, the light ray leaves at a right angle to the velocity of the source.)

<Hint>

Part (a) still tells you something: in the observer's frame the crests arrive as far apart in time as they leave. What is new when the source moves at a speed close to $c$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The time between crests $\Delta t_S$ is measured on the source's own clock. The observer sees that clock moving. Use the lesson's rule for a moving clock to find the time between two emissions in the observer's frame.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$z =$" expr="1/sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2) - 1"
  vars={{ v: [0.1, 0.9], c: [1, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)", hint: "That is 1 + z, the ratio of the two times. Take away 1 to get z." },
    { expr: "sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2) - 1", hint: "This says the light arrives bluer. Whose clock is the moving one here, and does a moving clock run fast or slow?" },
    { expr: "0", hint: "That is right for sound. At speeds close to c there is one more effect, on the clock of the source." },
    { expr: "sqrt((1 + v/c)/(1 - v/c)) - 1", hint: "That is the shift of a source that moves straight away from the observer. This source moves across the line of sight." },
    { expr: "v/c", hint: "A shift in proportion to v belongs to motion along the line of sight. Here there is none." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Work in the observer's frame. The travel time of the light is the same for every crest, as in part (a). The only effect left is that the source's clock is a moving clock.

<Step title="The trip is still the same for every crest">

In the observer's frame the source is always at distance $R$, and light travels at $c$. Every crest takes the time $R/c$. So the crests arrive as far apart as they leave, when both times are measured in the observer's frame. Call that time $\Delta t$. The observer is at rest in this frame, so $\Delta t_O = \Delta t$.

</Step>

<Step title="The source's clock runs slow">

The source sends one crest every $\Delta t_S$ on its own clock. That clock moves at speed $v$ in the observer's frame, and a moving clock runs slow by the factor $\gamma$:

<Formula label="Time dilation of the source's clock" tex="\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t_S, \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}" cite="reading2" at="slide 11" />

The speed of the source keeps changing direction, but time dilation depends only on the speed, which is constant.

</Step>

<Step title="The shift">

<Formula label="Transverse Doppler shift, source moving" tex="1 + z = \frac{\Delta t_O}{\Delta t_S} = \gamma \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} - 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result has no units. It is positive: the light arrives redder, although the source never moves away. For $v$ far below $c$ it goes to zero, the result for sound in part (a). For $v = 0.6\,c$ it gives $\gamma = 1.25$ and $z = 0.25$. A source moving straight away at the same speed would have $z = 1$, so the transverse shift is the smaller one.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Keep the light and the circle, but let the two people change roles. The person at the centre sends the light, and the person who travels around the circle at speed $v$ receives it. What is $z$ now?

<Hint>

Work in the frame of the person at the centre again. How far apart in time do the crests leave, and how far apart do they reach the circle, in that frame?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The moving clock now belongs to the observer. Between two arrivals, does it count more time or less time than the clocks of the frame in which it moves?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$z =$" expr="sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2) - 1"
  vars={{ v: [0.1, 0.9], c: [1, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)", hint: "That is 1 + z, the ratio of the two times. Take away 1 to get z." },
    { expr: "1/sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2) - 1", hint: "That is the answer to part (b), where the source's clock was the moving one. Which clock moves now?" },
    { expr: "1 - sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)", hint: "The size is right but the sign is not. Does the observer count more or less time between crests than the source does?" },
    { expr: "0", hint: "That is right for sound. At speeds close to c there is one more effect, on the clock of the observer." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Stay in the frame of the person at the centre, who is now the source. The trips are still equal. This time the moving clock is the observer's.

<Step title="Equal trips again">

In the frame of the centre, every crest travels the distance $R$ at speed $c$. The crests leave a time $\Delta t_S$ apart on the source's clock, which is at rest in this frame, and they reach the circle the same time apart: $\Delta t = \Delta t_S$.

</Step>

<Step title="The observer's clock runs slow">

The observer's clock moves at speed $v$ in this frame. Between two arrivals a time $\Delta t$ apart, it counts less:

<Formula label="Time dilation of the observer's clock" tex="\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t_O" cite="reading2" at="slide 11" />

</Step>

<Step title="The shift">

<Formula label="Transverse Doppler shift, observer moving" tex="1 + z = \frac{\Delta t_O}{\Delta t_S} = \frac{1}{\gamma} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2} - 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The shift is negative: a blueshift. The observer's slow clock counts less time between crests, so the light seems to have a higher frequency. For $v = 0.6\,c$ it gives $z = 0.8 - 1 = -0.2$. The ratios of parts (b) and (c) multiply to 1, as they should: sending a signal out and straight back between the same two people must return it unchanged.

Parts (b) and (c) are not the same situation seen from two frames. The person on the circle is accelerating, so the two people are not equivalent, and the answers differ.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

Now the motion is along a straight line. A source of light moves at constant speed $v$, not small compared with $c$, along a line that passes the observer at some distance. The observer is at rest. Consider the light that the source sends at the moment it is closest to the observer. With what $z$ does the observer receive it?

<Hint>

At the moment of closest approach, how fast is the distance between the source and the observer changing?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Take two crests sent just before and just after the closest point, equally long before and after. Compare the lengths of their trips, in the observer's frame. Then the reasoning of one of the earlier parts applies.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-d" label="$z =$" expr="1/sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2) - 1"
  vars={{ v: [0.1, 0.9], c: [1, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1/sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)", hint: "That is 1 + z, the ratio of the two times. Take away 1 to get z." },
    { expr: "sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2) - 1", hint: "This says the light arrives bluer. In the frame where the two trips are equal, whose clock is the moving one?" },
    { expr: "0", hint: "The distance is not changing at that moment, which is half of the answer. What about the clock of the source?" },
    { expr: "sqrt((1 + v/c)/(1 - v/c)) - 1", hint: "That is the shift of a source that moves straight away from the observer. At its closest point this source moves across the line of sight." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At the closest point the source moves neither towards the observer nor away. For a short while the situation is the same as on the circle of part (b).

<Step title="Equal trips, for crests close to the closest point">

Work in the observer's frame. Take one crest sent a short time before the closest point and one sent equally long after it. By symmetry the source is equally far from the observer at both moments, so both crests have the same trip. They arrive as far apart in time as they were sent: $\Delta t_O = \Delta t$, with $\Delta t$ the time between the two emissions in the observer's frame.

(For real light the time between crests is tiny compared with the time the source needs to pass by, so this holds for all the light sent near the closest point.)

</Step>

<Step title="The source's clock runs slow">

As in part (b), the source's clock moves at speed $v$ in this frame, so $\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t_S$.

</Step>

<Step title="The shift">

<Formula label="Light sent at closest approach, source moving" tex="1 + z = \gamma \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} - 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(d) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is the result of part (b): the circle was only a way to keep the distance fixed. The distance of closest approach does not appear in the answer. For $v = 0.6\,c$ the shift is again $z = 0.25$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={5}>

Again the motion is along a straight line, but now the observer moves, at constant speed $v$ not small compared with $c$, along a line that passes a source of light at rest. Consider the light that the observer receives at the moment of being closest to the source. What is $z$?

<Hint>

Choose the frame in which the source is at rest. Which of the earlier parts does the situation resemble, for the short time around the closest point?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In that frame, compare the trips of two crests that arrive just before and just after the closest point. Then ask which clock is the moving one.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-e" label="$z =$" expr="sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2) - 1"
  vars={{ v: [0.1, 0.9], c: [1, 2] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)", hint: "That is 1 + z, the ratio of the two times. Take away 1 to get z." },
    { expr: "1/sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2) - 1", hint: "That is the answer to part (d). There the light was sent at the closest point, seen from the observer's frame. Here it is received at the closest point, seen from the source's frame. Which clock moves in that frame?" },
    { expr: "1 - sqrt(1 - v^2/c^2)", hint: "The size is right but the sign is not. Does the observer count more or less time between crests than the source does?" },
    { expr: "0", hint: "The distance is not changing at that moment, which is half of the answer. What about the clock of the observer?" }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Work in the frame of the source. Around the closest point the observer's distance from the source is not changing, as on the circle of part (c).

<Step title="Equal trips, for crests that arrive close to the closest point">

In the source's frame, take one crest that reaches the observer a short time before the closest point and one that arrives equally long after it. The observer is equally far from the source at both moments, so both crests had the same trip. They were sent as far apart in time as they arrive: $\Delta t = \Delta t_S$, with $\Delta t$ the time between the two arrivals in the source's frame.

</Step>

<Step title="The observer's clock runs slow">

As in part (c), the observer's clock moves at speed $v$ in this frame, so $\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t_O$.

</Step>

<Step title="The shift">

<Formula label="Light received at closest approach, observer moving" tex="1 + z = \frac{1}{\gamma} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = \sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2} - 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(e) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is the blueshift of part (c): for $v = 0.6\,c$, $z = -0.2$.

Parts (d) and (e) can look like one situation described from two frames, since only the relative motion of two unaccelerated people should matter. They are two different light rays. In part (d) the ray leaves at a right angle to the motion as seen in the observer's frame. In part (e) it leaves at a right angle to the motion as seen in the source's frame. A ray sent at the closest point arrives only after the two have passed each other, so it is not the ray that is received at the closest point. Two different rays may have two different shifts.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A high-speed merry-go-round" points={15} mit="Problem 3" optional>

Four cars ride around a central hub. They are evenly spaced on a circle of radius $R$, a quarter of a turn apart, and all move at the same constant speed $v$. Each car sends waves to the other three.

For a given car, compare the Doppler shift $z$ of the waves it receives from (i) the car in front of it, (ii) the car behind it and (iii) the car opposite it.

<Part label="a">

The wave is sound, with speed $u$ relative to the air, and the air is at rest relative to the ground. Both $v$ and $u$ are far below the speed of light.

<Hint>

The answer does not need the path of the sound in detail. Ask what changes, from one crest to the next, about the trip from the sending car to the receiving car.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A moment later, the whole merry-go-round looks the same as before, only turned by some angle. The still air looks the same from every direction. What does that say about the travel time of the next crest?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-a" label="For sound:"
  choices={[
    "$z > 0$ from the car in front, $z < 0$ from the car behind, $z = 0$ from the car opposite",
    "$z < 0$ from the car in front, $z > 0$ from the car behind, $z = 0$ from the car opposite",
    "$z = 0$ from all three cars",
    "The same $z$ from all three cars, but not zero"
  ]}
  answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A shift of sound needs a travel time that changes from crest to crest. The merry-go-round turns steadily, so nothing about a trip changes with time.

<Step title="The travel time is the same for every crest">

Pick a sending car and a receiving car. A crest that leaves the sender travels through the still air and meets the receiver somewhere further along the circle, after some travel time. That time is not simply the straight distance between the cars divided by $u$, because the receiver moves while the sound is on its way.

Now take a crest sent later. By then the whole merry-go-round has turned by some angle, and otherwise everything is as before: the same spacing, the same speeds, and air that is the same in every direction. The second trip is the first trip turned by that angle, so it takes the same time.

</Step>

<Step title="No shift">

Crests that leave $\Delta t_S$ apart and all take the same time arrive $\Delta t_S$ apart. With no relativity, the clocks of all cars agree with the clocks on the ground, so

<Formula label="Sound between cars of the merry-go-round" tex="\Delta t_O = \Delta t_S \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = 0 \quad\text{for (i), (ii) and (iii)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The travel times themselves differ: sound sent to the car in front has to catch up with a car that moves away from where the sound started, and sound sent backwards meets a car that comes towards it. But a shift comes from a change of travel time, and each of the three travel times is constant. Part (a) of Problem 2 is the same argument with the receiver at the centre.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b">

Now the wave is light and $v$ may be close to $c$. What are the three shifts?

<Hint>

The argument about travel times from part (a) still holds in the frame of the ground. Two clocks move in that frame. How do their rates compare with each other?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="For light:"
  choices={[
    "The same redshift ($z > 0$) from all three cars, because every source is moving",
    "$z = 0$ from all three cars, as for sound",
    "The same blueshift ($z < 0$) from all three cars, because every observer is moving",
    "$z = 0$ only from the car opposite; the other two are shifted"
  ]}
  answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the frame of the ground the trips are again equal, and now both clocks are moving clocks, at the same speed.

<Step title="Equal trips in the frame of the ground">

As in part (a), the merry-go-round turns steadily and light travels at $c$ in every direction in the frame of the ground. So every crest between a given pair of cars takes the same time, and the crests arrive as far apart as they leave, measured in that frame. Call that time $\Delta t$.

</Step>

<Step title="Both clocks run slow by the same factor">

The sender's clock moves at speed $v$, so $\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t_S$, as in part (b) of Problem 2. The receiver's clock also moves at speed $v$, so $\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t_O$, as in part (c). The two factors cancel:

<Formula label="Light between cars of the merry-go-round" tex="1 + z = \frac{\Delta t_O}{\Delta t_S} = \frac{\Delta t/\gamma}{\Delta t/\gamma} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z = 0 \quad\text{for (i), (ii) and (iii)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The result is the product of the ratios of parts (b) and (c) of Problem 2: a redshift by $\gamma$ because the source moves, times a blueshift by $1/\gamma$ because the observer moves. It does not depend on which car sends, so it holds for the car in front, the car behind and the car opposite. Riders on a merry-go-round, however fast, see each other's light unshifted.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>
