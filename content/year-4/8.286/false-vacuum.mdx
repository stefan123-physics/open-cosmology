Lecture 23, the last of the course, is Guth's own account of the **inflationary universe**, mostly on the blackboard. This page covers the mechanism; the next two cover the exponential expansion and how it solves the problems of Lectures 21–22.

## Scalar fields

In present-day particle theory (not yet string theory) every particle is a quantum excitation of a field: the photon of the electromagnetic field is the familiar example; for relativistic interacting particles, it is the only consistent description we know. A **scalar** field describes a spinless particle and is just a number at each point of space. The only one seen in nature is the **Higgs field**, and inflation is modelled on it. The field that drives inflation, called the **inflaton**, is probably not the Standard Model Higgs, though in the few years before 2013 several papers proposed that it might be. It is at least a cousin of the Higgs, and like it, its properties are summed up by its **potential energy function**: energy density as a function of the field value $\phi$.

Guth draws two kinds of potential:

- **New inflation:** a broad, flat **plateau** with a peak, usually put at $\phi = 0$.
- **Original inflation** (Guth's first paper, "it does not work", he says, but it is worth seeing why): a **local minimum** at $\phi = 0$ that is not the lowest point, with lower true minima on either side.

In both cases the state of interest is the **false vacuum**: the field sitting at $\phi = 0$. With a local minimum it is classically completely stable (no energy is available to lift it over the barrier), but it can **tunnel** through quantum mechanically. That process, worked out by Sidney Coleman and collaborators, does not happen everywhere at once (the probability of that is zero; distant regions can't coordinate). It happens in a tiny region, which then grows as a bubble at nearly the speed of light. On the plateau the false vacuum is classically **metastable**: the smallest fluctuation starts the field rolling. The interesting case is a hill so gentle (small second derivative at the top) that rolling off takes long compared with the **Hubble time** set by the energy density. So in both cases the field is, for a while, simply stuck.

## Why the false vacuum has negative pressure

"Vacuum" to a particle physicist means the state of lowest energy density. A **false** vacuum is a **temporary** one: for a time long by early-universe standards, its energy density can't decrease. That forces a large **negative** pressure. Guth gives two arguments.

**From the equations.** The energy-conservation equation of the expanding universe is

<Formula label="Energy conservation in an expanding universe" tex="\dot\rho = -3\,\frac{\dot a}{a}\left(\rho + \frac{p}{c^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

As the universe expands the field stays stuck, so the density stays at the false-vacuum value: $\dot\rho = 0$. Then

<Formula label="Pressure of the false vacuum" tex="p = -\rho_f c^2 = -u_f" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

where $u_f = \rho_f c^2$ is the energy density and $\rho_f$ the mass density.

**A thought experiment for your roommate.** Fill a piston chamber with false vacuum of energy density $u_f$, with ordinary vacuum outside. (Our vacuum's energy is not quite zero, but it is incredibly small compared with $u_f$.) Pull the piston out by $\Delta V$, with the false vacuum somehow attached so that it fills the new volume. The energy inside rises by $u_f\,\Delta V$. Energy conservation says this equals the work done on the contents, $-p\,\Delta V$:

<Formula label="The piston argument" tex="\Delta E = \Delta W \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_f\,\Delta V = -p\,\Delta V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p = -u_f" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

With positive pressure the piston would push on you and you would do negative work. For the energy inside to **rise**, you must pull against a **suction**: negative pressure.

A student asks why the energy density doesn't simply drop as the volume grows. For an ordinary gas it would. But here the energy is the field's **potential** energy, and the only way to lower it is for the field to cross the barrier (waiting for a slow quantum process) or to roll off the plateau (which takes time). On the time scales considered it can't, so $p = -u_f$ holds to good accuracy, not exactly on the plateau, where the density can change a little, slowly.

## Negative pressure means repulsion

The second Friedmann equation tells us what gravity does:

<Formula label="Second Friedmann equation" tex="\ddot a = -\frac{4\pi}{3}G\left(\rho + \frac{3p}{c^2}\right)a" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

For the false vacuum the pressure term is negative and three times as large as the density term, so the bracket becomes $-2\rho_f$: instead of slowing the expansion, **gravity accelerates it**. This is exactly how the vacuum energy behaves today. False vacuum means gravitational repulsion, and that is the mechanism of inflation.

Asked whether, on the plateau, it takes a long time to start rolling or to reach the bottom, Guth says both, however you divide it: the field may have an infinitesimal velocity from the start, which grows. The whole process is long compared with the time for exponential expansion to set in.

## Old inflation and the graceful exit problem

Guth's original model used the potential with a local minimum, which seemed generic, created inflation and, as he realised, solved several cosmological problems. But inflation also has to **end**. In that model it ended only by tunnelling, in small regions that grow as **bubbles**, much like water boiling: tiny bubbles form, grow, collide and make a frothy mess. He hoped the bubbles would collide while small and merge into a uniform, hot region of the new phase. They don't [the transcript has him say this "turned out to be the case"; he means the opposite]: bubble formation produces terrible inhomogeneities, with no apparent cure. That was the downfall of the original model, and the difficulty became known as the **graceful exit problem**.

Soon afterwards **Andrei Linde** in the Soviet Union and, independently, **Andreas Albrecht and Paul Steinhardt** in the US proposed **new inflation**, with the flat-plateau potential, a more special assumption. [Guth says "a few years later"; the new-inflation papers appeared in 1982, about a year after his own, published in January 1981.] Inflation then ends not by tunnelling but by small fluctuations pushing the field down the hill. Two features make it work. The fluctuations are spatially correlated, so over some small **coherence region** they are essentially uniform. And once the field starts to roll, the hill is still nearly flat, so a great deal more inflation happens **after** the coherent region forms: a microscopic coherence region is stretched enormously. That was the first solution of the graceful exit problem; later solutions work in very similar ways.

## How does inflation start?

The honest answer is that we don't know: there is still no real theory of initial conditions for any cosmology. But inflation allows a much broader set of initial conditions than the conventional Big Bang, which needs them very precisely specified. Ideas include:

- **Chaotic initial conditions** (Andrei Linde, now at Stanford): the universe began in some random, chaotic state, and inflation started somewhere where the field happened to be right. Reasonable, Guth thinks, but vague.
- **Creation from nothing** (Alex Vilenkin of Tufts and, independently, Linde): the universe begins by quantum tunnelling from absolutely nothing, not empty space, which in general relativity is a dynamical thing that bends and stretches like rubber, but no matter, no space and no time; perhaps a closed universe in the limit of zero size.
- **The wave function of the universe** (Jim Hartle of UC Santa Barbara and Stephen Hawking): since space and time were not defined "before" the universe, it makes no sense to say it was created; it simply has an earliest time. Otherwise it is close to the tunnelling idea: a quantum origin sets the initial state.

None of these is precise: tunnelling while space itself changes needs a quantum theory of gravity we don't have. For inflation, what we need, from whatever source, is a **patch** at least about a **Hubble length** ($c/H$) across, **expanding** fast enough not to collapse, in which the field averages (over any rapid fluctuations) about $\phi \approx 0$. Given that, inflation begins, and once it begins, how it began hardly matters.

<Callout kind="note" title="Later developments">
The proposals Guth mentions include "Higgs inflation" (Fedor Bezrukov and Mikhail Shaposhnikov, 2008), which uses the Standard Model Higgs field as the inflaton with a modified coupling to gravity. Since the lecture, Planck's 2018 data and the BICEP/Keck limits (2021) have favoured plateau-shaped potentials like those of new inflation, and Higgs inflation still fits; it remains one candidate among many.
</Callout>

## Summary

- Inflation is driven by a scalar field (the inflaton), a cousin of the Higgs, stuck at a high-energy **false vacuum**, either a local minimum or the top of a very flat plateau.
- Because its energy density can't drop quickly, $\dot\rho = 0$ gives $p = -\rho_f c^2$; the piston argument gives the same result.
- In $\ddot a = -\frac{4\pi}{3}G(\rho + 3p/c^2)a$ this negative pressure makes gravity repulsive.
- Old inflation ended by bubble nucleation and produced hopeless inhomogeneities (the graceful exit problem); new inflation ends by a slow roll off a flat plateau, stretching a coherent region.
- Inflation needs only an expanding patch at least about a Hubble length across, near the false vacuum, to start.
