This set practises the lessons on [the magnetic monopole problem](/courses/8-286/magnetic-monopoles), on [exponential expansion](/courses/8-286/exponential-expansion) and on [how inflation solves the problems of the Big Bang](/courses/8-286/inflation-solutions). It also returns to [the horizon distance](/courses/8-286/horizon-distance) with the full Friedmann equation of the lesson on [the age of the universe with vacuum energy](/courses/8-286/age-with-vacuum-energy). The four problems follow MIT's Problem Set 10, the last set of the course, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for its problem sets, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

MIT's set has 65 points and 20 points of extra credit. Here Problems 2 and 3 count for the score (40 points). Problem 1 (20 points) is the same problem as Problem 5 of Problem Set 9, where it is scored, so it is not set a second time here. Problem 4 is MIT's extra credit.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `sqrt(k)*c/chi` or `(g/300)^(1/3)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. A number box accepts any value within 1% of ours unless the problem says otherwise, so keep at least three significant figures, and type it in the unit shown next to the box. MIT gives points per problem in this set; the split between parts is ours, and so are the parts of Problems 2 and 4, which MIT sets as one question each. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

One letter does two jobs in this set, as in Guth's lectures. In Problems 2 and 3, $k$ is the curvature constant of the Friedmann equation. In Problem 4, $k$ appears only in the product $kT$ and is the Boltzmann constant.

For the numbers, use $c = 2.998 \times 10^8$ m/s, $G = 6.674 \times 10^{-11}$ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², $\hbar = 1.0546 \times 10^{-34}$ J s, the Boltzmann constant $8.617 \times 10^{-5}$ eV/K, 1 GeV $= 1.602 \times 10^{-10}$ J, 1 Mpc $= 3.0857 \times 10^{22}$ m and 1 light-year $= 9.461 \times 10^{15}$ m.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The magnetic monopole problem" points={20} mit="Problem 1" optional>

MIT's Problem 1 asks when the phase transition of a grand unified theory happened, how many magnetic monopoles it left behind for every photon, and how much those monopoles would add to $\Omega$ today. It is the same problem as Problem 5 of Problem Set 9, with the same four parts, where MIT also set it for 20 points.

On this site the problem is set once, with its answer boxes, hints and solution, as Problem 5 of [Problem Set 9](/courses/8-286/8-286-problem-set-9#p5). Do it there. It is marked optional here only so that its 20 points are not counted twice: in MIT's course it was a required problem of this set.

<Solution>

The full solution is under Problem 5 of [Problem Set 9](/courses/8-286/8-286-problem-set-9#p5). In outline: a flat universe filled with radiation gives the time of the phase transition from its temperature. Kibble's estimate of about one monopole for every horizon volume then gives the number density of monopoles, the black-body formulas give the number density of photons, and the ratio of the two stays the same until today. Multiplied by the mass of a monopole and today's photon density, it gives a mass density far above the critical density, which is the monopole problem. The lesson on [how inflation solves the problems of the Big Bang](/courses/8-286/inflation-solutions) shows the way out: inflation after the monopoles formed thins them out.

</Solution>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Exponential expansion of the inflationary universe" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

The first Friedmann equation describes how a Robertson–Walker universe evolves:

<Formula label="First Friedmann equation" tex="\left(\frac{\dot a}{a}\right)^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

During inflation the mass density is that of the false vacuum, $\rho_f$, which stays constant while space expands. For $k = 0$ the lesson on [exponential expansion](/courses/8-286/exponential-expansion) found the growing solution

<Formula label="Exponential expansion (k = 0)" tex="a(t) = \text{const}\times e^{\chi t}, \qquad \chi = \sqrt{\frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho_f}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–12" />

In the lecture Guth leaves out the curvature term and says that it soon becomes small. Here you put it back, and find the growing solution for any $k$. MIT lists three integrals that may help:

<Formula label="Integrals MIT offers" tex="\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1+x^2}} = \sinh^{-1}x, \qquad \int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} = \sin^{-1}x, \qquad \int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2-1}} = \cosh^{-1}x" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, pp. 2–3" />

MIT sets this problem as one question. The three parts below are our way of marking it.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Take $k > 0$. Find the solution $a(t)$, with the clock set so that $t = 0$ is the moment at which $a$ is smallest. Give it in terms of $k$, $c$, $\chi$ and $t$. (The growing solution is the part with $t > 0$.)

<Hint>

Multiply the Friedmann equation by $a^2$. It then gives $\dot a^2$ in terms of $a$, and you can separate the variables: everything with $a$ on one side, $dt$ on the other.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The combination $\sqrt{k}\,c/\chi$ has the units of $a$. Write $a$ as that combination multiplied by a pure number $x$, and the equation for $x$ will match one of MIT's three integrals.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $k > 0$ the right side of the equation for $\dot a^2$ must not be negative. What is the smallest value $a$ can have, and what is $\dot a$ there?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$a(t) =$" expr="sqrt(k)*c/chi*cosh(chi*t)"
  vars={{ k: [0.5, 3], c: [1, 3], chi: [0.5, 2], t: [0.3, 2] }}
  names={{ chi: "\\chi" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(k)*c/chi*sinh(chi*t)", hint: "Put t = 0 in your answer: a would be zero. For positive k the Friedmann equation does not allow a to fall below a smallest value, because the square of the rate of change of a cannot be negative. The hyperbolic sine belongs to negative k." },
    { expr: "sqrt(k)*c/chi*exp(chi*t)", hint: "Put this in the equation: for a pure exponential the square of the rate of change of a equals chi squared times a squared exactly, which is the equation for k = 0. The curvature term is missing." },
    { expr: "sqrt(k)*c/(2*chi)*exp(chi*t)", hint: "That is what the solution becomes at large times. At early times a second, falling exponential matters as well." },
    { expr: "sqrt(k)*c*cosh(chi*t)", hint: "Check the units. The scale factor is a speed divided by a rate, multiplied by the square root of k: a factor of chi is missing." },
    { expr: "sqrt(k)*c/chi*cos(chi*t)", hint: "An ordinary cosine comes from the integral with the square root of 1 minus x squared. Check the sign under your square root: here it is x squared minus 1." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With a constant density the Friedmann equation is a first-order equation for $a(t)$ in which the variables separate. A change of variable to a pure number turns it into one of the listed integrals.

<Step title="An equation for the rate of change of a">

Put $\rho = \rho_f$, use the definition of $\chi$ and multiply by $a^2$:

<Formula label="Friedmann equation for a false vacuum" tex="\dot a^2 = \chi^2a^2 - kc^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

For $k > 0$ the right side is negative if $a$ is smaller than $\sqrt{k}\,c/\chi$. So $a$ can never be smaller than that, and at that value $\dot a = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="A pure number in place of a">

Write $a = (\sqrt{k}\,c/\chi)\,x$. Then $\dot a^2 = (kc^2/\chi^2)\,\dot x^2$ and the equation becomes $\dot x^2 = \chi^2\,(x^2 - 1)$, with $x \ge 1$. For the growing solution take the positive root and separate the variables:

<Formula label="Separated equation, k > 0" tex="\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2 - 1}} = \chi\,dt \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cosh^{-1}x = \chi\,(t - t_*)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

The constant of integration $t_*$ is the time at which $x = 1$, where $a$ is smallest.

</Step>

<Step title="The solution">

Solving for $a$:

<Formula label="False-vacuum universe with k > 0" tex="a(t) = \frac{\sqrt{k}\,c}{\chi}\,\cosh\!\big(\chi\,(t - t_*)\big)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

With the clock set so that $t_* = 0$, this is the answer of the box. Unlike the case $k = 0$, there is no free constant in front: only the shift $t_*$ in time is free.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate: $\dot a = \sqrt{k}\,c\,\sinh(\chi(t - t_*))$, so $\dot a^2 = kc^2\sinh^2$. And $\chi^2a^2 - kc^2 = kc^2(\cosh^2 - 1) = kc^2\sinh^2$. The two agree.

Units: in Guth's convention $k$ is in 1/notch², so $\sqrt{k}\,c/\chi$ is (1/notch) × (m/s) × s, which is metres per notch, as $a$ must be.

The same formula also holds for $t < t_*$. There the universe contracts, comes to rest at the smallest size and then expands again. The growing solution is the half with $t > t_*$. The lesson on exponential expansion mentions that the spacetime of a false vacuum, de Sitter space, can be described as a closed universe: this is that description.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Now take $k < 0$ and write $\kappa = -k$, so that $\kappa > 0$, as the lesson on [the open universe](/courses/8-286/open-universe) does. Find the growing solution $a(t)$, with the clock set so that $a = 0$ at $t = 0$, in terms of $\kappa$, $c$, $\chi$ and $t$.

<Hint>

The steps are those of part (a), with one sign changed. Write the equation for $\dot a^2$ again, now with $\kappa$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With $a = (\sqrt{\kappa}\,c/\chi)\,x$ the equation for $x$ matches another of MIT's three integrals. Is there still a smallest value of $a$ above zero?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$a(t) =$" expr="sqrt(kappa)*c/chi*sinh(chi*t)"
  vars={{ kappa: [0.5, 3], c: [1, 3], chi: [0.5, 2], t: [0.3, 2] }}
  names={{ kappa: "\\kappa", chi: "\\chi" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(kappa)*c/chi*cosh(chi*t)", hint: "That is the solution for positive k. It is not zero at t = 0, and for negative k the square of the rate of change of a is chi squared times a squared plus kappa c squared, which the hyperbolic cosine does not satisfy." },
    { expr: "sqrt(kappa)*c/chi*exp(chi*t)", hint: "A pure exponential solves the equation for k = 0 only, and it is not zero at t = 0." },
    { expr: "sqrt(kappa)*c/chi*sin(chi*t)", hint: "An ordinary sine comes from the integral with the square root of 1 minus x squared. Here the square root holds 1 plus x squared." },
    { expr: "sqrt(kappa)*c*sinh(chi*t)", hint: "Check the units: a factor of chi is missing." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same separation of variables, with the other sign of the curvature term.

<Step title="The equation">

With $k = -\kappa$:

<Formula label="Friedmann equation for a false vacuum, k < 0" tex="\dot a^2 = \chi^2a^2 + \kappa c^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

Now the right side is positive for every $a$, so nothing stops $a$ from reaching zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Separate and integrate">

Write $a = (\sqrt{\kappa}\,c/\chi)\,x$. Then $\dot x^2 = \chi^2\,(1 + x^2)$, and for the growing solution

<Formula label="Separated equation, k < 0" tex="\frac{dx}{\sqrt{1 + x^2}} = \chi\,dt \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sinh^{-1}x = \chi\,(t - t_*)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The solution">

<Formula label="False-vacuum universe with k < 0" tex="a(t) = \frac{\sqrt{\kappa}\,c}{\chi}\,\sinh\!\big(\chi\,(t - t_*)\big)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

Here $t_*$ is the time at which $a = 0$. With the clock set so that $t_* = 0$, this is the answer of the box.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Differentiate: $\dot a^2 = \kappa c^2\cosh^2(\chi(t - t_*))$, and $\chi^2a^2 + \kappa c^2 = \kappa c^2(\sinh^2 + 1) = \kappa c^2\cosh^2$. The two agree.

For small times the hyperbolic sine is close to its argument, so $a \approx \sqrt{\kappa}\,c\,(t - t_*)$: the universe starts by expanding at a steady rate that the curvature term alone sets, and the false vacuum takes over later.

A remark of our own: the integral with $\sin^{-1}x$ was not needed. It would belong to a negative mass density, which the false vacuum does not have.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Show that for large times $a(t) \propto e^{\chi t}$ for every choice of $k$. Then, for the solution of part (a), give the constant $A$ in $a(t) \to A\,e^{\chi t}$, in terms of $k$, $c$ and $\chi$.

<Hint>

Write the hyperbolic functions as sums of two exponentials. Which of the two is left when $\chi t$ is large?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$A =$" expr="sqrt(k)*c/(2*chi)"
  vars={{ k: [0.5, 3], c: [1, 3], chi: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ chi: "\\chi" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "sqrt(k)*c/chi", hint: "The hyperbolic cosine is half the sum of a growing and a falling exponential. The half stays." },
    { expr: "sqrt(k)*c/2", hint: "Check the units: a factor of chi is missing." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each solution is a sum or difference of $e^{\chi t}$ and $e^{-\chi t}$, and the falling one dies away.

<Step title="Large times">

Use $\cosh u = \tfrac12(e^u + e^{-u})$ and $\sinh u = \tfrac12(e^u - e^{-u})$ with $u = \chi\,(t - t_*)$:

<Formula label="Both solutions at large times" tex="a(t) = \frac{\sqrt{|k|}\,c}{2\chi}\left(e^{\chi(t - t_*)} \pm e^{-\chi(t - t_*)}\right) \;\to\; \frac{\sqrt{|k|}\,c}{2\chi}\,e^{-\chi t_*}\,e^{\chi t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2 (our own solution)" />

The plus sign is for $k > 0$ and the minus sign for $k < 0$. The second exponential is smaller than the first by the factor $e^{-2\chi(t - t_*)}$, so after a few times $1/\chi$ it no longer matters. For $k = 0$ the growing solution is an exponential from the start. So $a \propto e^{\chi t}$ at large times for every $k$. With $t_* = 0$ and $k > 0$ the constant is $A = \sqrt{k}\,c/(2\chi)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers, and what it means">

At $\chi\,(t - t_*) = 3$: $\cosh 3 = 10.068$ and $\tfrac12e^3 = 10.043$. They differ by 0.25%, which is $e^{-6}$. The Hubble rate $\dot a/a = \chi\tanh 3 = 0.9951\,\chi$ is already within half a percent of $\chi$.

In the Friedmann equation the curvature term divided by the density term is $kc^2/(\chi^2a^2) = 1/\cosh^2(\chi(t - t_*))$ for $k > 0$, which falls as $4e^{-2\chi(t - t_*)}$. This is the statement of the lesson on [how inflation solves the flatness problem](/courses/8-286/inflation-solutions): during inflation the curvature term falls as $1/a^2$ while the density term stays fixed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The horizon distance for the present universe" points={25} mit="Problem 3">

The lesson on [the horizon distance](/courses/8-286/horizon-distance) found how far away the most distant matter is from which light can have reached us since $t = 0$:

<Formula label="Horizon distance" tex="\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t) = a(t)\int_0^{t}\frac{c}{a(t')}\,dt'" cite="reading2" at="p. 18" />

(MIT's set writes the same distance with a capital R in place of the ℓ.) The formula was derived with comoving coordinates on a flat map, before the course met curved spaces. MIT states that it holds for every Robertson–Walker universe, open, closed or flat.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Show that the formula holds for a closed universe. MIT's hint is to use the closed-universe metric with the angle $\psi$ from the lesson on [the brightness of a distant supernova](/courses/8-286/luminosity-and-redshift):

<Formula label="Closed metric with angle ψ" tex="ds^2 = -c^2dt^2 + \tilde a^2(t)\left\{d\psi^2 + \sin^2\psi\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right\}, \qquad \tilde a(t) = \frac{a(t)}{\sqrt k}, \qquad \sin\psi = \sqrt k\,r" cite="reading3" at="p. 14 (board)" />

Use the fact that light always passes a local observer at speed $c$, which is the same as $ds^2 = 0$ along every piece of a light ray's path.

<Hint>

Put yourself at $\psi = 0$. By symmetry a light ray that leaves you, or reaches you, keeps $\theta$ and $\phi$ fixed. What does $ds^2 = 0$ then say about $d\psi/dt$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Add up $d\psi$ from $t = 0$ to find the largest $\psi$ that light can have covered. Then find the physical length, at one fixed time $t$, of the radial line from $\psi = 0$ to that value.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** In the coordinate $\psi$ the radial part of the metric has no extra factor, so a radial light ray is as simple as in a flat universe. Find how much $\psi$ a ray covers since $t = 0$, then measure that stretch of $\psi$ at time $t$.

<Step title="A radial light ray">

A closed universe is homogeneous, so we may put the origin $\psi = 0$ where we are. It is isotropic about that point, so a light ray through the origin has no reason to turn to one side: $\theta$ and $\phi$ stay fixed along it. With $d\theta = d\phi = 0$, the condition $ds^2 = 0$ reads $c^2dt^2 = \tilde a^2(t)\,d\psi^2$, so

<Formula label="Radial light ray in the closed universe" tex="\frac{d\psi}{dt} = \pm\frac{c}{\tilde a(t)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="How much ψ light can cover since t = 0">

A ray that reaches us at time $t$ and set out at $t = 0$ has come from

<Formula label="The horizon in the coordinate ψ" tex="\psi_\text{hor}(t) = \int_0^{t}\frac{c}{\tilde a(t')}\,dt'" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

Matter at a larger $\psi$ cannot have sent us any light yet.

</Step>

<Step title="The physical distance at time t">

A physical distance is measured at one time. Along the radial line from us to that matter, $dt = d\theta = d\phi = 0$, so $ds = \tilde a(t)\,d\psi$, and the whole length is $\tilde a(t)\,\psi_\text{hor}(t)$:

<Formula label="Horizon distance in a closed universe" tex="\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t) = \tilde a(t)\int_0^{t}\frac{c}{\tilde a(t')}\,dt' = \frac{a(t)}{\sqrt k}\int_0^{t}\frac{c\,\sqrt k}{a(t')}\,dt' = a(t)\int_0^{t}\frac{c}{a(t')}\,dt'" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a) (our own solution)" />

The two factors of $\sqrt k$ cancel, and the formula of the flat case is back.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\tilde a$ is a length (it is the radius of curvature), so $c\,dt/\tilde a$ is a pure number, as an angle must be, and $\tilde a\,\psi$ is a length.

The argument used only the radial part of the metric, $\tilde a^2d\psi^2$. The factor $\sin^2\psi$, where the curvature shows itself, multiplies only the angular part, which a radial ray never uses. The same steps work with the coordinate $r$: there $c\,dt = a\,dr/\sqrt{1 - kr^2}$ along the ray, and the physical length of the radial line at time $t$ is $a(t)\int dr/\sqrt{1 - kr^2}$, with the same factor.

One remark of our own: $\psi$ runs only from 0 to $\pi$, the point of the closed universe that is farthest from us. The distance above is the distance to the farthest visible matter as long as $\psi_\text{hor}(t) \le \pi$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={15}>

To use the formula you need $a(t)$, which the Friedmann equations give. Take the best fit to the Planck data of 2013, as MIT's set lists it:

<Formula label="Planck 2013 best fit, as given by MIT" tex="H_0 = 67.3\ \text{km/s/Mpc}, \qquad \Omega_{m,0} = 0.315, \qquad \Omega_{\text{vac},0} = 0.685, \qquad \Omega_{\text{rad},0} = 9.2\times10^{-5}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 3" />

The radiation value belongs to a photon temperature today of $T_{\gamma,0} = 2.725$ K. Find the horizon distance today, in billions of light-years and in Mpc.

MIT's hint: first find an integral for the horizon distance that looks like the integral for the age of the universe, then do that integral numerically.

MIT adds a note on what this number means. The model has no inflation in it. With inflation, the integral from $t = 0$ would be vastly larger. What you compute here is an effective horizon distance: the present distance of the farthest matter that we could in principle see with photons that set out after inflation ended. Photons from before the end of inflation have been redshifted so much that they can be counted as unobservable in practice.

<Hint>

In the lesson on the age of the universe with vacuum energy, the variable $x = a(t)/a(t_0)$ turned $dt$ into $x\,dx$ divided by $H_0\sqrt{F(x)}$. Use the same change of variable in the integral of $c\,dt/a$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The integrand is finite at $x = 0$ but changes very quickly there. If your numerical method struggles, the substitution $x = u^2$ gives a smooth integrand.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The integral itself is a pure number of order 1. The unit comes from $c/H_0$: work that out in light-years and in Mpc first.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b-gly" label="$\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t_0) =$" value="46.2" unit="billion light-years" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-horizon-gly" />

<Answer id="p3-b-mpc" label="$\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t_0) =$" value="14170" unit="Mpc" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-horizon-mpc" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Change the variable of integration from the time to the scale factor. The Friedmann equation says how fast the scale factor changes, so the integral becomes one over $x = a/a(t_0)$ from 0 to 1, which a computer can do.

<Step title="From dt to dx">

The lesson on the age of the universe wrote the Friedmann equation with $x = a(t)/a(t_0)$:

<Formula label="Separating the variables" tex="dt = \frac{1}{H_0}\,\frac{x\,dx}{\sqrt{F(x)}}, \qquad F(x) = \Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{rad},0} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4 + \Omega_{k,0}\,x^2" cite="reading3" at="pp. 6–8 (board)" />

In the horizon integral, $a(t_0)/a(t') = 1/x$. So $a(t_0)\,c\,dt/a = c\,dt/x$, and the factor $x$ in $dt$ cancels:

<Formula label="Horizon distance today as an integral over x" tex="\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t_0) = \frac{c}{H_0}\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}\,x + \Omega_{\text{rad},0} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,x^4 + \Omega_{k,0}\,x^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

It differs from the age integral only by the missing $x$ in the numerator.

</Step>

<Step title="The curvature term">

The lesson defines $\Omega_{k,0}$ so that the four $\Omega$'s add up to one. The three given values add up to 1.000092, so $\Omega_{k,0} = -9.2 \times 10^{-5}$, if the numbers are taken as they stand. That is far below the accuracy of the values, and a flat model ($\Omega_{k,0} = 0$) is just as good a reading of MIT's list. We computed both: they give the same result to four figures.

</Step>

<Step title="The length c over the Hubble rate">

With 1 Mpc $= 3.0857 \times 10^{19}$ km, $H_0 = 67.3/(3.0857 \times 10^{19})$ per second $= 2.181 \times 10^{-18}$ s⁻¹. Then $c/H_0 = 1.3745 \times 10^{26}$ m, which is 4455 Mpc or 14.53 billion light-years.

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

Without radiation the integrand would grow without limit as $x \to 0$. With it, the integrand stays finite, but it rises to $1/\sqrt{\Omega_{\text{rad},0}} = 104$ in a very narrow range near $x = 0$. A simple numerical method with equal steps in $x$ handles that badly. With $x = u^2$ and $dx = 2u\,du$ the integral becomes

<Formula label="A smooth form for numerical work" tex="\int_0^1\frac{dx}{\sqrt{F(x)}} = \int_0^1\frac{2u\,du}{\sqrt{\Omega_{m,0}\,u^2 + \Omega_{\text{rad},0} + \Omega_{\text{vac},0}\,u^8 + \Omega_{k,0}\,u^4}} = 3.180" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

whose integrand starts at zero and never exceeds about 3.6. Any standard routine, or Simpson's rule with a few hundred steps in a spreadsheet, gives 3.180.

</Step>

<Step title="The result">

<Formula label="Horizon distance today" tex="\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t_0) = 3.180\,\frac{c}{H_0} = 46.2\ \text{billion light-years} = 14170\ \text{Mpc}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A flat universe with matter only has $F = x$, and the integral is $\int_0^1 x^{-1/2}dx = 2$: the result $2c/H_0$ of the lesson on the horizon distance. The real value is larger, 3.180, because vacuum energy makes $F$ smaller than $x$ would be with $\Omega_{m,0} = 1$ over most of the range.

Leaving out the radiation ($\Omega_{\text{rad},0} = 0$) gives 3.240 for the integral, or 47.1 billion light-years, 2% more. So the radiation matters at the level of the 1% the answer box allows, although its $\Omega$ today is tiny: it dominates $F$ at small $x$, where the integrand is largest.

MIT's fallback for Problem 4, $3ct_0$ with $t_0 \approx 13.8$ billion years, is 41.4 billion light-years, about 10% less. And the horizon distance is far more than $c\,t_0$, because the matter that sent the light has been carried away by the expansion ever since.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 4 (optional)

<Exercise id="p4" title="How much inflation solves the horizon problem?" points={20} mit="Problem 4" optional>

This problem is MIT's extra credit. It is optional here: it does not count towards the score of the set. You calculate how much inflation is needed to explain why the observed universe is so uniform.

**The model.** MIT sets up a simple description of inflation. Inflation starts at a time $t_i$. From then on the universe is treated as homogeneous, isotropic and flat. (This sounds like assuming the answer. The point is that a rough uniformity is assumed at the start, and the very precise uniformity that we observe, one part in $10^5$ in the cosmic background radiation, has to come out of the evolution.) Inflation is driven by a false vacuum with a fixed mass density, written in terms of an energy $E_f$:

<Formula label="False-vacuum mass density" tex="\rho_f \equiv \frac{E_f^4}{\hbar^3c^5}, \qquad E_f \equiv E_{16}\times10^{16}\ \text{GeV}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, Eqs. (P4.1) and (P4.2), p. 4" />

Here $E_{16}$ is a pure number of order 1 for inflation at the energy scale of grand unified theories. The Hubble rate during inflation, $H_i$, follows from the Friedmann equation. It is the $\chi$ of Problem 2:

<Formula label="Hubble rate during inflation" tex="H_i^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho_f" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, Eq. (P4.3), p. 4" />

Uniformity is made on small scales first. Let $r_h(t)$ be the length up to which the universe is uniform at time $t$. At the start of inflation, ordinary thermal processes are assumed to have smoothed the universe up to a fraction $\beta$ of the Hubble length, where $\beta$ is a pure number somewhat below 1:

<Formula label="Smooth patch at the start of inflation" tex="r_h(t_i) \approx \beta\,c\,H_i^{-1}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, Eq. (P4.4), p. 5" />

During inflation the scale factor grows by a factor $Z$. Inflation ends suddenly at a time $t_e$, and reheating is instant: the mass density $\rho_f$ turns into radiation in thermal equilibrium at a temperature $T_\text{RH}$, with the black-body formula of the lesson on [black-body radiation](/courses/8-286/black-body-radiation):

<Formula label="Mass density after reheating" tex="\rho_\text{RH} = g_\text{RH}\,\frac{\pi^2}{30}\,\frac{(kT_\text{RH})^4}{\hbar^3c^5}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, Eq. (P4.5), p. 5" />

The number $g_\text{RH}$ counts the kinds of particles that are effectively massless at that temperature. For a grand unified theory one might take $g_\text{RH} \approx 300$. From its start to today the smooth patch is only stretched with the scale factor:

<Formula label="Smooth patch today" tex="r_h(t_0) = \frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_e)}\,Z\,r_h(t_i)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, Eqs. (P4.6) and (P4.7), p. 5" />

The task is to find the smallest $Z$ for which the smooth patch today is larger than the horizon distance of Problem 3:

<Formula label="Condition for enough inflation" tex="r_h(t_0) > \ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t_0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, Eq. (P4.8), p. 5" />

MIT sets this as one question. The four parts below are our own steps towards it. Use the Planck 2013 values of Problem 3 and your horizon distance from Problem 3(b). (MIT lets students who skipped Problem 3 use $3ct_0$ with $t_0 \approx 13.8$ billion years. The number box of part (c) expects the value of Problem 3.)

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the energy $kT_\text{RH}$ of reheating in terms of $E_f$ and $g_\text{RH}$. Then give its value for $E_{16} = 1$ and $g_\text{RH} = 300$, in units of $10^{15}$ GeV.

<Hint>

Instant reheating means that the mass density just after it equals the mass density just before.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$kT_\text{RH} =$" expr="Ef*(30/(pi^2*g))^(1/4)"
  vars={{ Ef: [0.5, 3], g: [50, 500] }}
  names={{ Ef: "E_f", g: "g_\\text{RH}" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Ef*(pi^2*g/30)^(1/4)", hint: "The fraction is upside down. The same energy shared among more kinds of particles gives a lower temperature, not a higher one." },
    { expr: "Ef*30/(pi^2*g)", hint: "The density goes as the fourth power of the temperature, so a fourth root is needed." },
    { expr: "Ef", hint: "That would hold only if g times pi squared over 30 were 1. Set the black-body density equal to the false-vacuum density and keep the factor." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-a-num" label="$kT_\text{RH} =$" value="3.17" unit="× 10¹⁵ GeV" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-reheat-energy" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Energy is conserved at reheating, so $\rho_\text{RH} = \rho_f$.

<Step title="Equate the densities">

Both densities have the same factor $\hbar^3c^5$ below the line, which drops out:

<Formula label="Reheating energy" tex="g_\text{RH}\,\frac{\pi^2}{30}\,(kT_\text{RH})^4 = E_f^4 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad kT_\text{RH} = \left(\frac{30}{\pi^2g_\text{RH}}\right)^{1/4}E_f" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

For $g_\text{RH} = 300$ the bracket is $30/(9.870 \times 300) = 0.01013$, and its fourth root is 0.3173. So $kT_\text{RH} = 3.17 \times 10^{15}$ GeV for $E_{16} = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The reheating energy is of the order of $E_f$ itself, as the lesson on exponential expansion says: reheating gives a temperature comparable to the energy scale of the theory. Because of the fourth root it depends only weakly on $g_\text{RH}$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

From the end of inflation to today the entropy in a comoving volume is conserved to high accuracy: $a^3s$ is constant, where $s$ is the entropy density. Today's entropy is carried by the photons and by the three kinds of neutrinos, which are colder: $T_\nu = (4/11)^{1/3}\,T_\gamma$. Find $a(t_0)/a(t_e)$ in terms of $g_\text{RH}$, $T_\text{RH}$ and today's photon temperature $T_{\gamma,0}$.

<Hint>

The entropy density of black-body radiation is proportional to $g\,T^3$. Write it just after reheating and today.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Today the photons have $g = 2$ and the neutrinos $g = 21/4$, but at their own, lower temperature. What does the factor $T^3$ make of the temperature ratio?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-b" label="$a(t_0)/a(t_e) =$" expr="(11*g/43)^(1/3)*TRH/T0"
  vars={{ g: [50, 500], TRH: [1, 5], T0: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ g: "g_\\text{RH}", TRH: "T_\\text{RH}", T0: "T_{\\gamma,0}" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "(g/(2 + 21/4))^(1/3)*TRH/T0", hint: "The neutrinos are colder than the photons. Their entropy goes as the cube of their own temperature, which brings in a factor of 4/11." },
    { expr: "(g/(2 + (21/4)*(4/11)^(4/3)))^(1/3)*TRH/T0", hint: "The power 4/3 belongs to the energy density, which goes as the fourth power of the temperature. The entropy density goes as the third power." },
    { expr: "(43/(11*g))^(1/3)*TRH/T0", hint: "The ratio of the two g values is upside down. More kinds of particles at reheating means more entropy to pass on, which keeps the later universe warmer, so it has to expand more to reach today's temperature." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write $a^3s$ just after reheating and today, and set them equal.

<Step title="The two entropy densities">

The lesson on black-body radiation gives

<Formula label="Entropy density of black-body radiation" tex="s = g\,\frac{2\pi^2}{45}\,\frac{k^4\,T^3}{(\hbar c)^3}" cite="reading4" at="p. 25 (board)" />

Just after reheating, $g = g_\text{RH}$ and $T = T_\text{RH}$. Today the photons have $g = 2$. The lesson on [neutrinos](/courses/8-286/neutrinos-and-particle-counting) gives $g_\nu = 21/4$ for the three kinds of neutrinos together, and their temperature:

<Formula label="Neutrino temperature" tex="T_\nu = \left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{1/3} T_\gamma" cite="reading5" at="p. 12 (board)" />

Since $s \propto T^3$, the neutrinos count with $T_\nu^3 = (4/11)\,T_\gamma^3$:

<Formula label="Entropy density today" tex="s_0 = \left[2 + \frac{21}{4}\cdot\frac{4}{11}\right]\frac{2\pi^2}{45}\,\frac{k^4\,T_{\gamma,0}^3}{(\hbar c)^3} = \frac{43}{11}\cdot\frac{2\pi^2}{45}\,\frac{k^4\,T_{\gamma,0}^3}{(\hbar c)^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Conservation of entropy">

Setting $a^3(t_e)\,s_\text{RH} = a^3(t_0)\,s_0$:

<Formula label="Expansion since the end of inflation" tex="\frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_e)} = \left(\frac{s_\text{RH}}{s_0}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\frac{11\,g_\text{RH}}{43}\right)^{1/3}\frac{T_\text{RH}}{T_{\gamma,0}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check with numbers">

Today $kT_{\gamma,0} = 8.617 \times 10^{-5}\ \text{eV/K} \times 2.725\ \text{K} = 2.348 \times 10^{-4}$ eV. With $kT_\text{RH} = 3.173 \times 10^{15}$ GeV from part (a), the ratio of temperatures is $1.351 \times 10^{28}$. The factor in front is $(11 \times 300/43)^{1/3} = 4.25$. So $a(t_0)/a(t_e) = 5.74 \times 10^{28}$ for $E_{16} = 1$ and $g_\text{RH} = 300$.

In his lecture Guth estimates this expansion with $aT$ constant, which is the same formula without the factor 4.25.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={7}>

Find $Z_\text{min}$, the smallest $Z$ that satisfies the condition. Write it as $Z_\text{min} = Z_1 \times f$, where $Z_1$ is its value for $E_{16} = 1$, $g_\text{RH} = 300$ and $\beta = 1$, and $f$ says how it changes with $E_{16}$, $g_\text{RH}$ and $\beta$ (so $f = 1$ for those three values). Give $Z_1$ in units of $10^{26}$ (this box accepts values within 2% of ours), and give $f$.

<Hint>

<Hint>

For $f$, collect the powers of $E_f$ and of $g_\text{RH}$. The Hubble rate $H_i$ contains $E_f^2$. Both the reheating temperature and the factor in front of it in part (b) contain $g_\text{RH}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

<Answer id="p4-c-z1" label="$Z_1 =$" value="9.15" unit="× 10²⁶" tolerance="2%" calc="p4-zmin" />

<Answer id="p4-c-f" label="$f =$" expr="E16*(g/300)^(-1/12)/beta"

<Solution>

**The idea.** The smooth patch today is the starting patch, stretched by $Z$ during inflation and by $a(t_0)/a(t_e)$ afterwards. Setting it equal to the horizon distance gives the smallest $Z$.

<Step title="Solve the condition for Z">

With $r_h(t_i) = \beta c/H_i$ the condition $r_h(t_0) > \ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t_0)$ becomes

<Formula label="Minimum expansion during inflation" tex="Z > Z_\text{min} = \frac{\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t_0)\,H_i}{\beta\,c}\cdot\frac{a(t_e)}{a(t_0)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="In terms of the parameters">

From the model, $H_i = \sqrt{8\pi G/(3\hbar^3c^5)}\;E_f^2$. From parts (a) and (b), $a(t_e)/a(t_0) = (43/(11\,g_\text{RH}))^{1/3}\,(\pi^2g_\text{RH}/30)^{1/4}\,kT_{\gamma,0}/E_f$. Together:

<Formula label="Minimum expansion in terms of the parameters" tex="Z_\text{min} = \left(\frac{43}{11}\right)^{1/3}\left(\frac{\pi^2}{30}\right)^{1/4}\sqrt{\frac{8\pi G}{3\hbar^3c^5}}\;\frac{\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t_0)\,kT_{\gamma,0}}{c}\;\frac{E_f}{\beta\,g_\text{RH}^{1/12}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

One power of $E_f$ is left, and $g_\text{RH}$ enters with the power $\tfrac14 - \tfrac13 = -\tfrac1{12}$. So

<Formula label="How the minimum depends on the parameters" tex="Z_\text{min} = Z_1\,\frac{E_{16}}{\beta}\left(\frac{g_\text{RH}}{300}\right)^{-1/12}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

The horizon distance of Problem 3 is 14170 Mpc $= 4.371 \times 10^{26}$ m, which is $5.25 \times 10^{55}$ Hubble lengths of the inflating universe. Dividing by $a(t_0)/a(t_e) = 5.74 \times 10^{28}$ from part (b):

<Formula label="Minimum expansion, sample values" tex="Z_1 = \frac{5.25\times10^{55}}{5.74\times10^{28}} = 9.15\times10^{26}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Every factor in the formula for $Z_\text{min}$ is a ratio of two lengths or of two energies, so $Z_\text{min}$ is a pure number.

At the end of inflation the smooth patch then measures $Z_1\,c/H_i = 0.76$ cm. That agrees with the lesson on exponential expansion, whose rough estimate from Guth's sample numbers is that about 1 cm at the end of inflation would do.

The value of $g_\text{RH}$ hardly matters, as MIT promises: $g_\text{RH} = 100$ gives a factor 1.10 and $g_\text{RH} = 1000$ a factor 0.90. With MIT's fallback $3ct_0$ for the horizon distance, $Z_1$ would be $8.20 \times 10^{26}$.

Find $N_\text{min} = \ln Z_\text{min}$ for $E_{16} = 1$, $g_\text{RH} = 300$ and $\beta = 1$. (This box accepts values within 0.2 of ours.)

<Hint>

<Answer id="p4-d" label="$N_\text{min} =$" value="62.1" tolerance="0.2" calc="p4-nmin" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A logarithm turns the product of part (c) into a sum.

<Step title="The number of e-foldings">

<Formula label="Minimum number of e-foldings" tex="N_\text{min} = \ln Z_\text{min} = 62.1 + \ln E_{16} - \ln\beta - \frac{1}{12}\ln\frac{g_\text{RH}}{300}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4 (our own solution)" />

The first term is $\ln(9.15 \times 10^{26}) = 2.21 + 26 \times 2.303 = 62.1$.

The lesson on exponential expansion counts about 65 e-foldings for Guth's generous sample numbers, a patch of one Hubble length that grows to 10 cm. The minimum found here, about 62, is a little less, as it should be.

The logarithm makes the answer insensitive to the uncertain inputs. A starting patch ten times smaller ($\beta = 0.1$) costs only $\ln 10 = 2.3$ more e-foldings.
