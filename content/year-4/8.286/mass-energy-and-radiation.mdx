So far the course has followed a universe filled with non-relativistic matter, which describes most of cosmic history. But early on the universe was dominated by **radiation**, and in the recent past by dark energy. Lecture 15 starts on radiation (after a quick review of the Schwarzschild infall, which is on the slides). The key idea is the most famous equation in physics: light has no rest mass, but it has energy, and energy is mass.

## E = mc²: how big?

The numbers are, in Guth's phrase, out of sight. One kilogram of mass is equivalent to

<Formula label="The energy of one kilogram" tex="E = (1\ \text{kg})\,c^2 = 8.9876 \times 10^{16}\ \text{J} = 2.497 \times 10^{10}\ \text{kWh}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

For comparison, the world used about 150 petawatt-hours ($1.5 \times 10^{14}$ kWh) in 2008, the latest year Wikipedia had figures for when Guth looked: an average power of about $1.7 \times 10^{10}$ kW. So converting one kilogram per hour completely into energy would supply about 1.5 times the world's power. Guth's illustration: fill a car's tank with matter, convert it completely, and you could power the world for about two days. In practice nothing comes close: a nuclear reactor converts only a fraction of a percent of its uranium's mass into energy.

## Energy and momentum in special relativity

Guth quotes, without deriving them, the special-relativity results the course needs. Energy and momentum combine into a **four-vector**

<Formula label="Energy–momentum four-vector" tex="p^\mu = \left(p^0, \vec p\,\right), \qquad p^0 \equiv \frac{E}{c}" cite="reading1" at="p. 6 (board)" />

"Four-vector" means it changes from one inertial frame to another exactly as $x^\mu = (ct, \vec x)$ does. Just as $\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + \Delta z^2 - c^2\Delta t^2$ is the same in every frame, so is

<Formula label="The invariant: rest mass" tex="p^2 \equiv |\vec p\,|^2 - \frac{E^2}{c^2} = -\left(m_0 c\right)^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 7 (board)" />

The value follows from the simplest frame, the object's rest frame, where $\vec p = 0$ and $E = m_0c^2$. $m_0$ is the **rest mass**. [On the board an extra factor of $c$ first appeared inside the bracket, $-(m_0c^2)^2$; a student caught it.] In terms of the velocity, with the same $\gamma$ as in time dilation,

<Formula label="Momentum and energy" tex="\vec p = \gamma\,m_0\,\vec v, \qquad E = \sqrt{\left(m_0c^2\right)^2 + |\vec p\,|^2c^2} = \gamma\,m_0c^2, \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 7–8 (board)" />

At rest, $E_0 = m_0c^2$, which is where we started.

Why redefine energy and momentum at all? Einstein noticed that if you keep Newton's definitions but use Lorentz transformations between frames, energy and momentum conserved in one frame are not conserved in another. To keep conservation as a universal law, both had to be redefined: the new versions agree with Newton's at low speed and are conserved in every frame. Since $p^\mu$ transforms linearly, like $x^\mu$, a change $\Delta p^\mu$ is also a four-vector, and a four-vector that vanishes in one frame vanishes in all.

## Binding energy weighs something

A hydrogen atom shows $E = mc^2$ at work. Bringing an electron and a proton together from far apart releases 13.6 eV. That energy leaves the system, so the atom has less energy than the separate particles, and therefore less mass:

<Formula label="Mass of a hydrogen atom" tex="m_\text{H} = m_p + m_e - \frac{\Delta E}{c^2}, \qquad \Delta E = 13.6\ \text{eV}" cite="reading1" at="p. 9 (board)" />

<WorkedExample title="Weighing the binding energy">
<Problem>
By what fraction is a hydrogen atom lighter than a free proton plus a free electron? How much lighter is a kilogram of hydrogen atoms than the same particles kept apart, and how much energy was released in forming it?
</Problem>
<Step title="Fraction">
$m_pc^2 + m_ec^2 = 938.272 + 0.511 = 938.783$ MeV. The deficit is $13.6\ \text{eV}/938.783\ \text{MeV} = 1.45 \times 10^{-8}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Per kilogram">
$1.45 \times 10^{-8} \times 1$ kg $= 1.45 \times 10^{-8}$ kg, about 14.5 micrograms.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy">
$\Delta m\,c^2 = 1.45 \times 10^{-8} \times 8.99 \times 10^{16}$ J $\approx 1.3 \times 10^{9}$ J, a lot of energy for a mass change far too small to weigh directly.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Relativistic mass and gravity

Guth defines the **relativistic mass** of any system as its energy divided by $c^2$; for a single particle it is $\gamma m_0$, growing with speed. Many books avoid the idea: it is just $E/c^2$ (in units with $c = 1$, just the energy), students may confuse it with rest mass, and it does **not** obey $F = ma$. Guth keeps it as a convenient abbreviation because of one property that matters for cosmology: **it is what gravitates**.

The gravitational field of a single fast-moving star is complicated. You can get it by transforming the Schwarzschild metric to a moving frame, and the result is not isotropic, just as the electric field of a moving charge isn't. But for a **gas** of particles moving every which way, like the early universe, seen from outside as a box, the directional effects average out, and the box's mass is the sum of the relativistic masses of its particles. Divided by the volume, that is the mass density that produces gravity. In general relativity's own language: the energy density is the source of gravity, and it equals this mass density times $c^2$.

## Radiation as a gas of photons

For electromagnetic radiation, then, the mass density is

<Formula label="Mass density of radiation" tex="\rho = \frac{u}{c^2}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 11–12 (board)" />

where $u$ is the energy density of the radiation. Photons have **zero rest mass**, meaning there is no lower limit to a photon's energy (rest mass sets a particle's lowest possible energy, when it is at rest, and a photon is never at rest). Setting $m_0 = 0$ in the energy formula:

<Formula label="Energy of a photon" tex="E = c\,|\vec p\,|" cite="reading1" at="p. 12 (board)" />

This is already true for classical electromagnetic waves, so it had better be true for the photons they are made of. Because each photon carries momentum, photons bouncing around a box push on its walls: a photon gas has **pressure**, which the next page calculates.

## Why radiation thins out faster than matter

Put a free photon gas into an expanding universe. Two things happen.

**Dilution.** Photons are not created or destroyed, so their number density falls as the volume grows, exactly as for matter: $n_\gamma \propto 1/a^3(t)$. ($\gamma$ here is just the usual label for photons of any frequency, from the term "gamma ray".)

Asked about absorption and pair creation, Guth notes that photons can be absorbed and two photons can make an electron–positron pair, though with a rather small cross section. But absorption is a very minor effect in the early universe, where there is little to absorb them, and pair creation and annihilation quickly reach **thermal equilibrium**, with as many pairs made as destroyed. The universe stays close to thermal equilibrium as long as it cools slowly, with important exceptions that come later.

**Redshift.** Each photon's frequency falls as the universe expands:

<Formula label="Redshift of frequency" tex="\frac{\nu(t_2)}{\nu(t_1)} = \frac{1}{1 + z} = \frac{a(t_1)}{a(t_2)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 14 (board)" />

and a photon's energy is $E = h\nu$, with Planck's constant $h = 4.136 \times 10^{-15}$ eV·s. So each photon's energy falls as $1/a$. Together:

<Formula label="Energy density of a photon gas" tex="u_\gamma = n_\gamma \times h\nu \propto \frac{1}{a^3}\cdot\frac{1}{a} = \frac{1}{a^4(t)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 15 (board)" />

(Classical Maxwell theory gives the same answer, with more work.) Matter's density falls as $1/a^3$; radiation's falls one power faster, because of the redshift. Run the film backwards and the ratio of radiation to matter **grows** as $1/a$. At some early time the two were equal, and before that radiation dominated. When that was is the subject of the next page.

## Summary

- $E = mc^2$: one kilogram is $9 \times 10^{16}$ J $= 2.5 \times 10^{10}$ kWh, about 1.5 hours of 2008 world power use.
- Energy and momentum form a four-vector $p^\mu = (E/c, \vec p)$ with invariant $|\vec p|^2 - E^2/c^2 = -(m_0c)^2$; $\vec p = \gamma m_0\vec v$, $E = \gamma m_0c^2$.
- Binding energy has mass: hydrogen is lighter than $m_p + m_e$ by $13.6\ \text{eV}/c^2$.
- Relativistic mass $E/c^2$ is what gravitates for a gas; so radiation has mass density $\rho = u/c^2$.
- Photons: $E = c|\vec p|$, $E = h\nu$. Number density $\propto 1/a^3$ and energy $\propto 1/a$ give $u_\gamma \propto 1/a^4$, against $1/a^3$ for matter.
