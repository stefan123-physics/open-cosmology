Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `2*pi^2*c^5/(hbar*G^2)` or `sqrt(1 + r^2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. ## Helium in four steps

Problem 1 is about the making of helium in the first minutes, which the lessons mention but do not treat. Guth set it as a test of the course reading. Here is what you need, taken from the solutions that Guth wrote for his review problems.

**1. Equilibrium.** In the first second, neutrons and protons change into each other all the time, by weak reactions with electrons, positrons and neutrinos. A neutron is slightly heavier than a proton, by $\Delta m = 1.29$ MeV/$c^2$, so in thermal equilibrium there are fewer neutrons:

<Formula label="Neutrons and protons in thermal equilibrium" tex="\frac{n_n}{n_p} = e^{-\Delta m\,c^2/kT}" cite="reading8" at="Problem 12, Eq. (1), p. 20; used in the solution of Problem 5(d), p. 30" />

Here $n_n$ and $n_p$ are the number densities of neutrons and protons. At high temperature the ratio is close to 1. As the temperature falls, the ratio falls.

**2. Freeze-out.** The weak reactions slow down quickly as the universe cools. When they can no longer keep up with the expansion, the ratio stops following the equilibrium formula: it freezes out. In the standard calculation this begins about one second after the Big Bang, when $kT$ is a little below 1 MeV.

**3. Decay.** From then on, only one process still changes neutrons into protons: the decay of the free neutron, with a mean life of about 890 seconds.

**4. Helium.** Nuclei cannot form at once, because the first step, deuterium, is broken apart again by the many photons. Only after about 200 seconds does deuterium survive (the "deuterium bottleneck" breaks). Then almost every neutron that is still there ends up in a nucleus of helium-4, which holds two neutrons and two protons. The fraction $Y$ of all the mass that ends up as helium is therefore twice the fraction of neutrons among all nucleons at that moment:

<Formula label="Helium mass fraction" tex="Y = \frac{2\,n_n}{n_n + n_p}" cite="reading8" at="solution of Problem 6(d), pp. 35–37 (argued in words there; this formula is ours)" />

So every question about helium becomes a question about neutrons: **how many neutrons are left, next to enough protons, when the nuclei form?**

A rough sketch with numbers, to see the sizes. If the ratio froze sharply at one second, when $kT = 0.86$ MeV by the lesson's formula, it would freeze at $e^{-1.29/0.86} = 0.223$, so 18.2% of the nucleons would be neutrons. After 200 seconds of decay a fraction $e^{-200/890} = 0.80$ of them is left: 14.6% of the nucleons, which gives $Y \approx 0.29$. The full calculation follows every reaction rate and gives about a quarter, the value quoted in the lesson on [decoupling](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling). The sketch is good enough to see in which direction a change pushes the answer.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Big Bang nucleosynthesis" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

The predicted amount of helium depends on many measured quantities. In each part one of them is changed. Decide what happens to the predicted helium abundance, and explain your choice to yourself in a sentence or two before you look at the solution.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Suppose there were one more species of neutrino than the three we know. Does the predicted helium abundance go up or down?

<Hint>

An extra species of neutrino is extra radiation. What does more energy density do to the rate of expansion at a given temperature?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Go through the four steps above. Which of them depend on how fast the universe expands?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-a" label="With an extra neutrino species, the helium abundance:" choices={["goes down", "stays the same", "goes up"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** An extra kind of radiation makes the universe expand faster at each temperature. Everything that takes time then has less time.

<Step title="A faster expansion">

In the lesson on [counting particle species](/courses/8-286/neutrinos-and-particle-counting), each species of neutrino adds $\tfrac78 \times 2 = 1\tfrac34$ to $g$. A fourth species raises $g$ from $10\tfrac34$ to $12\tfrac12$. The energy density at a given temperature is proportional to $g$, and the Friedmann equation gives $H \propto \sqrt{\rho}$. So at every temperature the universe expands faster, by the factor $\sqrt{12.5/10.75} = 1.08$.

</Step>

<Step title="Earlier freeze-out">

The weak reaction rates at a given temperature do not change. But they now have to keep up with a faster expansion, and they fail sooner, at a higher temperature. A higher freeze-out temperature means a larger ratio $n_n/n_p = e^{-\Delta m c^2/kT}$: more neutrons.

</Step>

<Step title="Less time to decay">

The temperature at which deuterium survives is set by nuclear physics and by the number of photons per nucleon, so it stays the same. It is reached sooner. The time at a given temperature is

<Formula label="Time at a given temperature" tex="t \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{g}\;T^2}" cite="reading8" at="solution of Problem 6(d), p. 36" />

By then the electron–positron pairs are gone, so the factor is not the 1.08 of freeze-out. The review solution uses $g = 3.36$ for this stage; the fourth species adds $1\tfrac34 \times (4/11)^{4/3} = 0.45$, giving 3.82 and a factor $\sqrt{3.82/3.36} = 1.065$. So the 200 seconds become $200/1.065 = 188$ seconds, and the fraction of neutrons that survive rises from $e^{-200/890} = 0.80$ to $e^{-188/890} = 0.81$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both effects give more neutrons when the nuclei form, so the helium abundance goes **up**. Guth gives the same two reasons for a new light particle in his review problems.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Suppose the weak interactions were stronger than they are, so that neutrons and protons kept their thermal equilibrium ratio until $kT \approx 0.25$ MeV. Is the predicted helium abundance larger or smaller than in the standard calculation?

<Hint>

What does the equilibrium formula give for the ratio of neutrons to protons at this lower temperature?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-b" label="With stronger weak interactions, the helium abundance is:" choices={["smaller", "larger", "the same"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A later freeze-out means a colder freeze-out, and at a lower temperature equilibrium allows fewer neutrons.

<Step title="The ratio at freeze-out">

At $kT = 0.25$ MeV the equilibrium ratio is

<Formula label="Equilibrium ratio at the later freeze-out" tex="\frac{n_n}{n_p} = e^{-1.29/0.25} = 0.0057" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

against 0.22 in the sketch of the standard case. Only about one nucleon in 175 is a neutron.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Even if not a single neutron decayed afterwards, the helium fraction would be at most $Y = 2 \times 0.0057/1.0057 = 0.011$: about 1% instead of a quarter. The helium abundance is much **smaller**.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Suppose the difference between the masses of the neutron and the proton were larger than its real value of 1.29 MeV/$c^2$. Is the predicted helium abundance larger or smaller than in the standard calculation?

<Hint>

The mass difference stands in the exponent of the equilibrium formula.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-c" label="With a larger mass difference, the helium abundance is:" choices={["the same", "larger", "smaller"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A neutron costs more energy, so thermal equilibrium makes fewer of them.

<Step title="The ratio at freeze-out">

At any temperature the ratio $e^{-\Delta m c^2/kT}$ is smaller for a larger $\Delta m$. For example, with $\Delta m = 1.5$ MeV/$c^2$ and the same freeze-out at $kT = 0.86$ MeV, the ratio is $e^{-1.5/0.86} = 0.175$ instead of 0.223.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With the same 200 seconds of decay the sketch gives $Y \approx 0.24$ instead of 0.29. (Our own addition: a larger mass difference would also make the free neutron decay faster, because the decay rate grows steeply with the energy released, which removes still more neutrons.) The helium abundance is **smaller**.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

The standard calculation takes matter to be spread evenly during nucleosynthesis. Since the 1980s people have also studied an uneven version. In it, the baryons clump at about $10^{-6}$ seconds, when the hot soup of quarks turns into protons, neutrons and (at first) pions. The baryons then sit in small dense nuggets, with little matter between them. Before nucleosynthesis starts, the neutrons can wander out of the nuggets and spread almost evenly through space. The protons cannot: they are charged, the plasma around them stops them after a very short distance, and most of them stay in the nuggets.

Does this picture give more or less helium than the standard one?

<Hint>

A helium-4 nucleus needs two neutrons and two protons at the same place.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Look at the two kinds of region one at a time. In each, which ingredient runs out first?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="With neutrons that leave the nuggets, the helium abundance is:" choices={["larger", "smaller", "the same"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Neutrons are the scarce ingredient of helium. The even mixture uses every one of them. Moving neutrons to places with few protons wastes them.

<Step title="The two regions">

Inside the nuggets there are nearly all the protons but only a small share of the neutrons, so few helium nuclei can form there: the neutrons run out. Between the nuggets there are many neutrons but few protons. There the protons run out, and the neutrons that find no partner stay free and decay.

</Step>

<Step title="An example with numbers">

Take 160 nucleons: 20 neutrons and 140 protons. Mixed evenly, all 20 neutrons join 20 protons, and 40 of the 160 nucleons end up in helium: $Y = 0.25$.

Now let 15 of the neutrons wander out, into a region with only 5 protons. Outside, the 5 protons bind 5 neutrons: 10 nucleons in helium, and 10 neutrons left over. In the nuggets, the 5 remaining neutrons bind 5 of the 135 protons: 10 nucleons in helium. Together 20 of the 160 nucleons are in helium: $Y = 0.125$, half of the even value.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In each region the number of nucleons that can go into helium is twice the smaller of the two numbers, neutrons or protons. The sum over the regions can never be more than the value for the even mixture, so in this picture the helium abundance is **smaller**. This is the simple argument that MIT's question asks for. Real calculations of uneven nucleosynthesis also follow how neutrons drift back while the reactions run and how many of the leftover neutrons decay in time, which changes the size of the effect.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The mass density of vacuum fluctuations" points={25} mit="Problem 2">

The lesson on [the vacuum energy](/courses/8-286/vacuum-energy-puzzle) describes the energy of the quantum fluctuations of the electromagnetic field in words. Here you work it out.

To keep everything finite, look at the field inside a cube with sides of length $L$, with $0 \le x \le L$, $0 \le y \le L$ and $0 \le z \le L$. At the end the cube may grow without limit: what counts is the energy **density**.

The field in the cube is a sum of waves (modes). For a very large cube the conditions at the walls do not matter, so choose the simplest: periodic ones, where the field on each wall equals the field on the opposite wall. A wave $\cos(\vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega t)$ with $\omega = c\,|\vec k|$ fits these conditions when

<Formula label="Allowed wave vectors in the periodic cube" tex="k_x L = 2\pi n_x, \qquad k_y L = 2\pi n_y, \qquad k_z L = 2\pi n_z" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, extended hint, p. 4" />

with whole numbers $n_x$, $n_y$ and $n_z$, which may be negative. For each allowed $\vec k$ there are two waves, one for each polarisation of light.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Count only the modes with a wavelength of at least $\lambda_\text{min}$, which means a wave number of at most

<Formula label="Largest wave number" tex="k_\text{max} = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda_\text{min}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

How many such modes are there? Give the number $N$ in terms of $L$ and $\lambda_\text{min}$ (type `lam` for $\lambda_\text{min}$). An exact count is not asked for: your result should become exact when $\lambda_\text{min}$ is much smaller than $L$.

<Hint>

Draw the allowed $\vec k$ as points in a space with axes $k_x$, $k_y$, $k_z$. What pattern do they make, and how much of that space belongs to each point?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The condition $|\vec k| \le k_\text{max}$ cuts out a region of that space. Divide its volume by the volume that belongs to one point. Do not forget the polarisations.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$N =$" expr="8*pi*L^3/(3*lam^3)"
  vars={{ L: [5, 20], lam: [0.2, 1.5] }}
  names={{ lam: "\\lambda_\\text{min}" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "4*pi*L^3/(3*lam^3)", hint: "That counts the allowed wave vectors. Light has two polarisations for each of them." },
    { expr: "pi*L^3/(3*lam^3)", hint: "You seem to have kept only wave vectors with positive components. With periodic conditions the whole numbers may be negative as well: the wave can run either way along each axis." },
    { expr: "16*L^3/lam^3", hint: "That counts a cube in the space of wave vectors. The condition is on the length of the wave vector, which cuts out a sphere." },
    { expr: "L^3/(3*pi^2*lam^3)", hint: "This is the count in terms of the largest wave number, with the wavelength typed in its place. Put in the largest wave number, 2 pi divided by the wavelength." },
    { expr: "2*L/lam", hint: "That is the count for waves along one axis only. The cube has three directions." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The allowed wave vectors form a regular grid. Counting them is the same as measuring a volume in the space of wave vectors.

<Step title="One point for each small cube">

The allowed values of each component are $2\pi/L$ apart. So the allowed $\vec k$ form a cubic grid, and each grid point owns a small cube of volume $(2\pi/L)^3$ in the space of wave vectors.

</Step>

<Step title="The points inside a sphere">

The modes we want have $|\vec k| \le k_\text{max}$: they lie inside a sphere of radius $k_\text{max}$, with volume $\tfrac43\pi k_\text{max}^3$. When the sphere holds very many grid points, their number is its volume divided by the volume for each point. With two polarisations:

<Formula label="Number of modes up to the largest wave number" tex="N = 2 \times \frac{\tfrac43\pi\,k_\text{max}^3}{(2\pi/L)^3} = \frac{L^3\,k_\text{max}^3}{3\pi^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="In terms of the wavelength">

Put in $k_\text{max} = 2\pi/\lambda_\text{min}$:

<Formula label="Number of modes down to the shortest wavelength" tex="N = \frac{L^3}{3\pi^2}\cdot\frac{8\pi^3}{\lambda_\text{min}^3} = \frac{8\pi}{3}\left(\frac{L}{\lambda_\text{min}}\right)^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$N$ is a pure number, and it grows in proportion to the volume $L^3$, as the lesson says it must for the energy density to settle to a fixed value. The count is exact only when $L$ is much larger than $\lambda_\text{min}$, because only then does the sphere's surface cut through a negligible share of the small cubes.

An example: a cube of 1 m filled with light down to a wavelength of 500 nm has $N = (8\pi/3) \times (2 \times 10^{6})^3 = 6.7 \times 10^{19}$ modes.

Reflecting walls give the same count. Their standing waves are half a wavelength apart in each direction, twice as dense along each axis, but only positive wave numbers count: $2^3$ times as many points in one eighth of the sphere.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={10}>

In quantum theory each mode is a harmonic oscillator. A mode of angular frequency $\omega$ has the energies

<Formula label="Energy levels of one mode" tex="E_n = \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 3" />

where $n$ counts the photons in the mode. With no photons at all, the mode still has the energy $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$: the energy of its quantum fluctuations.

Add up this lowest energy over all modes, with the sum cut off at a shortest wavelength $\lambda_\text{min}$ equal to the Planck length $\ell_P = \sqrt{\hbar G/c^3}$ of the lesson. Find the **mass** density $\rho$ of the fluctuations, first as a formula in $\hbar$, $c$ and $G$ (type `hbar` for $\hbar$), then in kg/m³.

<Hint>

The modes do not all have the same frequency, so $N$ times one energy will not do. How many modes have a wave number between $k$ and $k + dk$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Those modes lie in a thin shell of the sphere of part (a). Multiply their number by $\tfrac12\hbar c k$ and integrate from 0 to $k_\text{max}$. Divide by the volume of the cube, and then by $c^2$ to turn an energy density into a mass density.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-b" label="$\rho =$" expr="2*pi^2*c^5/(hbar*G^2)"
  vars={{ c: [1, 3], hbar: [1, 3], G: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ hbar: "\\hbar" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi^2*c^5/(hbar*G^2)", hint: "Count both polarisations of light for each wave vector." },
    { expr: "4*pi^2*c^5/(hbar*G^2)", hint: "The lowest energy of a mode is half of hbar times omega, not the whole of it." },
    { expr: "c^5/(8*pi^2*hbar*G^2)", hint: "You cut the sum off at a wave number of 1 divided by the Planck length. The problem sets the shortest wavelength equal to the Planck length, so the largest wave number is 2 pi divided by it." },
    { expr: "2*pi^2*c^7/(hbar*G^2)", hint: "That is the energy density. The question asks for the mass density: divide by the square of the speed of light." },
    { expr: "8*pi^2*c^5/(3*hbar*G^2)", hint: "You gave every mode the energy of the mode with the highest frequency. Most modes lie lower: integrate over the wave number." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-b-num" label="$\rho =$" value="1.02e98" unit="kg/m³" tolerance="2%" calc="p2-b-density" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Count the modes shell by shell in the space of wave vectors, give each its $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ and integrate.

<Step title="Modes in a thin shell">

The shell between $k$ and $k + dk$ has the volume $4\pi k^2\,dk$. Dividing by the volume $(2\pi/L)^3$ for each wave vector and counting two polarisations gives

<Formula label="Number of modes in a shell" tex="dN = 2 \times \frac{4\pi k^2\,dk}{(2\pi/L)^3} = \frac{L^3\,k^2\,dk}{\pi^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Add up the lowest energies">

Each of these modes has $\omega = ck$ and the lowest energy $\tfrac12\hbar c k$:

<Formula label="Zero-point energy in the cube" tex="E = \int_0^{k_\text{max}} \tfrac12\hbar c k\;\frac{L^3 k^2}{\pi^2}\,dk = \frac{L^3\,\hbar c\,k_\text{max}^4}{8\pi^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

Divide by $L^3$ for the energy density, $u = \hbar c\,k_\text{max}^4/(8\pi^2)$. This is the formula that the lesson's worked example uses. The size of the cube has dropped out, so nothing changes when the cube grows without limit.

</Step>

<Step title="The Planck length as the cut-off">

The mass density is $\rho = u/c^2$. With $k_\text{max} = 2\pi/\ell_P$ and $\ell_P^4 = \hbar^2G^2/c^6$:

<Formula label="Mass density of the vacuum fluctuations" tex="\rho = \frac{\hbar\,k_\text{max}^4}{8\pi^2 c} = \frac{2\pi^2\,\hbar}{c\,\ell_P^4} = \frac{2\pi^2\,c^5}{\hbar\,G^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b) (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put in the numbers">

The Planck length is $\ell_P = 1.616 \times 10^{-35}$ m, so $k_\text{max} = 3.89 \times 10^{35}$ m⁻¹. The combination $c^5/(\hbar G^2)$ is $5.15 \times 10^{96}$ kg/m³, and

$\rho = 2\pi^2 \times 5.15 \times 10^{96} = 1.02 \times 10^{98}$ kg/m³.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $c^5/(\hbar G^2)$ is the only combination of the three constants that is a mass divided by a volume, so the answer had to be a pure number times it. The number $2\pi^2 \approx 20$ comes from the choice of cut-off. The lesson's worked example takes $k_\text{max} = 1/\ell_P$ and gets a density smaller by $(2\pi)^4 \approx 1560$. Guth's problem takes the wavelength, not the wave number, at the Planck scale. Nobody knows the right cut-off, so neither choice is better.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Compare this mass density with the critical density $\rho_c = 3H_0^2/(8\pi G)$ of our universe, for $H_0 = 67.7$ km/s/Mpc (the value the lesson's worked example uses). By how many orders of magnitude is it too large? Give $\log_{10}(\rho/\rho_c)$ to one decimal place. The box accepts answers within 0.1 of ours, so any present-day value of $H_0$ will do.

<Hint>

Work out $\rho_c$ in kg/m³ first. Turn $H_0$ into 1/s: a megaparsec is $3.086 \times 10^{19}$ km.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$\log_{10}(\rho/\rho_c) =$" value="124.07" tolerance="0.1" calc="p2-c-orders" />

<Solution>

<Step title="The critical density">

$H_0 = 67.7/(3.086 \times 10^{19})$ s⁻¹ $= 2.19 \times 10^{-18}$ s⁻¹, so $\rho_c = 3H_0^2/(8\pi G) = 8.61 \times 10^{-27}$ kg/m³, as in the lesson.

</Step>

<Step title="The ratio">

$\rho/\rho_c = 1.02 \times 10^{98}/(8.61 \times 10^{-27}) = 1.2 \times 10^{124}$, and $\log_{10}$ of that is 124.07.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The fluctuations of this one field would weigh 124 orders of magnitude more than everything the universe is observed to contain. The vacuum energy that astronomers measure is about 0.7 of the critical density, so the mismatch with the measured vacuum energy is just as large. ## What the empty universe does and does not show

MIT's set ends with a warning by Guth, which we give here in our own words. Problems 6 and 7 show how the cosmology of general relativity turns into special relativity when gravity does not matter. But the empty model gives a misleading picture of the Big Bang itself.

The mass density of the real universe grows as one looks back in time. However thin the matter is today, an empty model cannot describe the early universe.

In the empty model the Big Bang is one event, the origin of the inertial coordinates, and nothing is singular there. The scale factor is zero at $t = 0$, but Problem 7 shows that spacetime is perfectly smooth: the future light cone of the origin joins on to the rest of flat spacetime, and $t = 0$ is not the beginning of anything. With a mass density that is not zero, the singularity is real. It can be proved that no choice of coordinates removes it, so spacetime cannot be continued smoothly to anything earlier.

The horizon shows the difference too. The horizon distance is

<Formula label="Horizon distance" tex="\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t) = a(t)\int_0^t \frac{c}{a(t')}\,dt'" cite="mit" at="Discussion of the zero mass density universe, p. 10" />

For the scale factor that you found in Problem 6(a), this integral is infinite at every $t$: the empty universe has no horizon, and so no horizon problem. For a universe filled with radiation the horizon distance is the finite $2ct$ of Problem 3.

Last, in the empty model the Big Bang happens at one point of spacetime. With matter it is better to think of it as happening everywhere at once: in Robertson–Walker coordinates the singularity is at $t = 0$ for every $r$, $\theta$ and $\phi$. Because $a = 0$ there, all those points are at zero distance from each other, and the question of whether they are "one point" has no clear meaning: $t = 0$ is too singular to belong to the space at all. At any time after it the answer is clear. The space is homogeneous, and for an open universe it is infinite.
