Lecture 16 begins with a review of Lecture 15, this time on slides. Before moving on to black-body radiation, Guth draws out what the new Friedmann equation says about gravity, and solves it for a universe full of radiation.

## Neutrinos and other "radiation"

Asked why neutrinos, which have mass, count as radiation, Guth points to $|\vec p|^2 - E^2/c^2 = -(m_0c)^2$ (slide 2). When a particle's energy is large compared with its rest energy, the right side is negligible and the particle behaves as if it were massless: $E \approx c|\vec p|$. That is the case for neutrinos in the early universe, and, at early enough times, even for electron–positron pairs. **Any particle acts like radiation when its energy is large compared with its rest energy** (transcript p. 5).

## Pressure gravitates

The review ends with the corrected Friedmann equation from Lecture 15 (slide 8):

<Formula label="Second Friedmann equation" tex="\ddot a = -\frac{4\pi}{3}\,G\left(\rho + \frac{3p}{c^2}\right)a" cite="reading2" at="slide 8" />

As long as the universe is homogeneous and isotropic, Guth says, this is exact as far as we know: it is what general relativity gives. A student asks why the derivation used $dU = -p\,dV$ and not the full first law $dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$. Because the expanding gas stays very close to thermal equilibrium with no heat flowing in or out, it expands **adiabatically**: its entropy does not change, and the $T\,dS$ term is negligible.

The equation contains a startling fact: in general relativity **pressure, not only mass density, produces gravity**. $\ddot a$ measures how fast gravity slows the expansion, and pressure adds to the slowing.

- In the radiation era, $p = \frac13\rho c^2$, so $3p/c^2 = \rho$: the pressure is exactly as important as the mass density in slowing the expansion. Today the pressure of ordinary matter is negligible.
- Energy densities are, for all the laws of physics we know, positive. But **pressures can be negative**. A material with negative pressure would, by the same equation, produce gravitational **repulsion** and make the expansion accelerate.

This is not the mechanical effect of pressure. Mechanical forces come only from pressure **differences**: the air pressure in a room is large, but we don't feel it because it pushes equally in all directions. In a homogeneous universe the mechanical effect is zero. What remains is gravitational: the term carries Newton's $G$.

And it matters: for about the last five billion years, our universe has been accelerating. The only explanation we have, Guth says, is the repulsive gravity of some negative-pressure material, called **dark energy**, very likely vacuum energy, a topic for later in the course.

## Where does the energy go?

In the piston, the gas's lost energy went into pushing the walls. The universe has no walls. For a small region, the answer is that it pushes on the regions around it. But where does the energy **ultimately** go? Guth's answer: into **gravitational potential energy**. Picture a gas cloud held together by its own gravity and made to expand: pulling the particles apart against their mutual attraction raises the gravitational potential energy. So, all in all, energy is still conserved (transcript pp. 12–13).

## A flat universe full of radiation

Now solve the Friedmann equations for a flat ($k = 0$) universe dominated by radiation. With $k = 0$ the first equation is just $H^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho$, and since $\rho \propto 1/a^4$,

<Formula label="Hubble rate during radiation domination" tex="H = \frac{\dot a}{a} \propto \frac{1}{a^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \dot a = \frac{\text{const}}{a} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a\,da = \text{const}\;dt" cite="reading1" at="pp. 13–14 (board)" />

Integrating, $\frac12a^2 = \text{const}\cdot t + \text{const}'$. Choosing $t = 0$ at the moment $a = 0$, the standard way of setting the clocks, makes $\text{const}' = 0$, so

<Formula label="Scale factor, flat radiation-dominated universe" tex="a(t) \propto t^{1/2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 14 (board)" />

replacing $t^{2/3}$ for flat matter domination. For a flat universe the constant of proportionality means nothing: it just fixes the size of a notch. It drops out of every physical quantity, starting with

<Formula label="Hubble rate" tex="H = \frac{\dot a}{a} = \frac{1}{2t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 15 (board)" />

The horizon distance, how far light has travelled since $t = 0$, in physical units, is

<Formula label="Horizon distance" tex="\ell_{p,\text{horizon}}(t) = a(t)\int_0^t \frac{c}{a(t')}\,dt' = 2ct" cite="reading1" at="p. 15 (board)" />

compared with $ct$ in a static universe and $3ct$ in a flat matter-dominated one.

## A density set by the clock

Put $H = 1/(2t)$ back into $H^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho$:

<Formula label="Mass density, flat radiation-dominated universe" tex="\rho = \frac{3}{32\pi\,G\,t^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 15 (board)" />

This is remarkable: **we don't need to know what the radiation is** (photons, neutrinos, whatever), only that it falls as $1/a^4$ and that the universe is flat. The flatness is crucial; without it the density could be anything. (Asked about an almost flat universe, Guth says that a quantitative "almost" would give a correspondingly approximate formula.)

<WorkedExample title="The universe at one second, three minutes and one year">
<Problem>
Treat the early universe as flat and radiation-dominated. What were its mass density and horizon distance at $t = 1$ s, $t = 3$ minutes and $t = 1$ year?
</Problem>
<Step title="One second">
$\rho = 3/(32\pi G t^2) = 3/(32\pi \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 1^2) = 4.47 \times 10^{8}$ kg/m³, about 450,000 times the density of water. The horizon is $2ct = 6.0 \times 10^{8}$ m, about one and a half times the Earth–Moon distance.
</Step>
<Step title="Three minutes">
$\rho \propto 1/t^2$, and $t = 180$ s, so $\rho = 4.47 \times 10^8/180^2 = 1.38 \times 10^4$ kg/m³, still almost 14 times water. The horizon is $1.08 \times 10^{11}$ m, about 0.7 AU.
</Step>
<Step title="One year">
$t = 3.156 \times 10^7$ s gives $\rho = 4.49 \times 10^{-7}$ kg/m³, and a horizon of $1.89 \times 10^{16}$ m, about two light-years.
</Step>
<Step title="What is not used">
None of these numbers needed to know how many kinds of radiation there are, or their temperature. Combined with the black-body formula $u \propto T^4$ of the next page, the same density gives a temperature, which Guth works out in Lecture 17.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Particles act like radiation when their energy is far above their rest energy; early on that includes neutrinos and even electron–positron pairs.
- In $\ddot a = -\frac{4\pi}{3}G(\rho + 3p/c^2)a$, pressure gravitates. In the radiation era it doubles the deceleration compared with pressureless matter of the same density; negative pressure would accelerate the expansion, the dark-energy explanation of today's acceleration.
- Uniform pressure has no mechanical effect; the energy lost by an expanding gas ends up, Guth suggests, as gravitational potential energy.
- Flat and radiation-dominated: $a \propto t^{1/2}$, $H = 1/(2t)$, horizon $2ct$, and $\rho = 3/(32\pi G t^2)$ regardless of what the radiation is.
