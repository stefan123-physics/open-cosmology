Before Hubble, everyone, Newton included, assumed the universe was **static**: the stars as a whole were not going anywhere. But Newton's own law of gravity pulls everything toward everything else. How could a universe full of matter stand still? This page follows Guth's account of Newton's answer and why it fails. It is the starting point for the dynamics of the expanding universe.

## Newton's argument

Newton saw the problem clearly. In [a letter](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/THEM00254) to the theologian Richard Bentley dated 10 December 1692, he argued that a *finite* collection of matter would fall together into one great spherical mass. Matter spread evenly through **infinite** space, he thought, would instead gather into many separate masses scattered through space, which could become the sun and the stars. (He added that he could not explain by natural causes why some matter became shining suns and some opaque planets.)

The modern way to put Newton's intuition, as Guth does, is a symmetry argument: in an infinite, evenly filled universe every particle is pulled equally in every direction, so nothing has a reason to move. Bentley had raised the idea that the infinite pulls on each side of a particle might exactly balance. In his [second letter](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/normalized/THEM00255), 17 January 1693, Newton replied that infinities are neither equal nor unequal, so such a balance cannot be taken for granted. The problem of gravity in an infinite Newtonian universe was taken up seriously again only in the 1890s, by Hugo von Seeliger and Carl Neumann, and later by Einstein.

## The verdict of Gauss's law

The cleanest way to settle the question is not to add up infinitely many pulls, but to use a **local** law. For gravity, the counterpart of Gauss's law in electrostatics says that the flux of the gravitational field $\vec g$ out of any closed surface is fixed by the mass inside:

<Formula label="Gauss's law of gravity" tex="\oint \vec g \cdot d\vec a = -4\pi G\,M_\text{enclosed}" cite="reading2" at="slide 1" />

Now suppose the universe is static and uniform. Then nothing accelerates, so $\vec g = 0$ everywhere, and the left side is zero for every surface. But a uniform density $\rho > 0$ means every surface encloses some mass, so the right side is not zero. **Contradiction: a static, uniformly filled universe is impossible.**

The same result follows from the differential form, **Poisson's equation**:

<Formula label="Poisson's equation" tex="\nabla^2\phi = 4\pi G\rho, \qquad \vec g = -\nabla\phi" cite="reading2" at="slide 2" />

If $\vec g = 0$ everywhere, the potential $\phi$ is constant, so $\nabla^2\phi = 0$, which forces $\rho = 0$.

<Callout kind="key" title="Why local laws win">
Modern physics treats local laws like Poisson's equation as more basic than "action at a distance" force laws. Einstein generalised Poisson's equation, not Newton's force law, when he built general relativity, and every known fundamental law of physics is local.
</Callout>

## Why adding up the pulls goes wrong

If Gauss's law is so clear, why did the symmetry argument seem convincing? Because the infinite sum of pulls is **ambiguous**. A simple integral shows the problem. Take $f(x) = +1$ for $x > 0$ and $-1$ for $x < 0$, and integrate over a window of half-width $L$:

<Formula label="An order-dependent integral" tex="\lim_{L\to\infty}\int_{-L}^{L} f(x)\,dx = 0, \qquad \lim_{L\to\infty}\int_{a-L}^{a+L} f(x)\,dx = 2a" cite="l5t" at="worked on the board" />

Shift the centre of the window and the "limit" changes: you can get any answer you want. An integral like this, which converges only because positive and negative parts cancel, is called **conditionally convergent**, and it is dangerous: rearranging the terms changes the result.

The gravitational sum in an infinite universe is exactly like that. Add up the matter in spherical shells **centred on a point P**: each shell pulls P equally in all directions, so $\vec g = 0$, as Newton argued. Now add up the same matter in shells **centred on a different point Q**, a distance $b$ away. The ball of radius $b$ around Q pulls P toward Q, while every shell outside it surrounds P and exerts no net force:

<Formula label="Shells centred on Q" tex="\vec g = \frac{G M}{b^2}\,\hat e_{QP}, \qquad M = \frac{4\pi}{3}\rho\,b^3 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |\vec g| = \frac{4\pi}{3}G\rho\,b" cite="reading1" at="slide 8" />

Here $\hat e_{QP}$ points from P toward Q. Choose Q anywhere, and the "answer" can have any direction and any size. The matter is the same in both cases; only the bookkeeping changed. Newton's symmetry argument picked one bookkeeping and declared it correct.

<WorkedExample title="How strong is the ambiguity?">
<Problem>
Take a uniform density $\rho = 2.7\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg/m}^3$ (roughly the average density of matter in today's universe). If we add up shells around a point Q that is $b = 100$ Mpc $= 3.1\times10^{24}$ m from P, what acceleration does the "Q bookkeeping" give at P?
</Problem>
<Step title="Use the formula">
$|\vec g| = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\rho\,b = 4.19 \times 6.67\times10^{-11} \times 2.7\times10^{-27} \times 3.1\times10^{24}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Evaluate">
$|\vec g| \approx 2.3\times10^{-12}\ \text{m/s}^2$. Tiny, but it doubles if $b$ doubles, and points wherever Q is. The sum has no single answer.
</Step>
<Step title="What decides physically">
Not the order of adding but the physics: Gauss's law says $\vec g$ cannot be zero everywhere. The universe must be accelerating toward collapse or decelerating in expansion.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## But what about symmetry?

Newton's intuition was not entirely wrong. His mistake was to assume there is one absolute standard of "not accelerating": for him, the fixed stars. In a collapsing (or expanding) universe there are no fixed stars. Accelerations, like velocities, are then only **relative**.

Any observer may regard themselves as unaccelerated and will see every other galaxy accelerating toward them, with acceleration proportional to distance. That looks as if it has a centre, but every observer sees the same pattern, exactly like [Hubble's law](/courses/8-286/homogeneous-expansion) for velocities. The universe can be homogeneous *and* gravitating; it just cannot be static. The [next page](/courses/8-286/expanding-sphere) turns this into an equation for $a(t)$.

## Summary

- Newton argued an infinite uniform universe could be static by symmetry.
- Gauss's law (or Poisson's equation) shows it cannot: $\vec g = 0$ everywhere would require zero density.
- The direct sum of gravitational pulls is conditionally convergent; different groupings (shells around P or Q) give different answers.
- In an evolving universe accelerations are relative, so a homogeneous universe can collapse or expand without a centre.
