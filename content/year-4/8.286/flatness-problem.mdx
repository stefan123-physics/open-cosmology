The second problem of the conventional Big Bang is the **flatness problem**: why is $\Omega_0$, the present ratio of the actual density to the critical density, anywhere near 1? Like the horizon problem, it can be avoided by assuming something about the initial singularity, but not explained within the conventional theory, whose mechanics are completely well defined.

## How close to 1 is Ω today?

Guth quotes the best value from the Planck team (combined, as for all his Planck numbers, with some other data; the satellite alone gives slightly looser limits):

<Formula label="Planck 2013 limit" tex="\Omega_0 - 1 = -0.0010 \pm 0.0065" cite="reading1" at="p. 12 (board)" />

The uncertainty is a little more than half a percent. [Planck published this as a value of $\Omega_k = 1 - \Omega$, so strictly the central value of $\Omega_0 - 1$ has the opposite sign; the size of the bound, which is all that matters here, is the same.]

Guth stresses that the argument does not need this precision. When inflation was invented around 1980, Ω was known far less precisely (only to within roughly an order of magnitude, with a lower end around 0.1), and the problem was nearly as strong. [The transcript gives the 1980 range as "0.1 and 0.2"; since Guth calls it still close to 1 and later says the bound was three orders of magnitude weaker, a range from about 0.1 to about 2 is probably meant.]

## A pencil balanced on its tip

What makes this a problem is the dynamics: **$\Omega = 1$ is an unstable equilibrium**. An exactly flat universe stays exactly flat, so if $\Omega = 1$ exactly, it stays 1 forever. But if Ω in the early universe were a tiny bit **above** 1, it would rise and quickly reach infinity, which is what happens when a closed universe reaches its maximum size, and the universe would recollapse. A tiny bit **below** 1, and Ω would rapidly trail off towards 0. Finding Ω near 1 today is like finding a pencil still almost upright after standing on its tip for a billion years: it must have started incredibly close to vertical. The question is how close.

## Why one second?

Guth evaluates Ω at $t = 1$ second, following **Dicke and Peebles**, whose 1979 discussion he credits with introducing the problem. One second is when the processes of **nucleosynthesis** begin, and the successful predictions of the light-element abundances are real empirical evidence that the conventional model works back to one second after the Big Bang.

## The calculation

Nothing new is needed: only the Friedmann equation and how temperature and density behave in the matter- and radiation-dominated eras. Start from

<Formula label="Friedmann equation, critical density and Ω" tex="H^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2}, \qquad \rho_c = \frac{3H^2}{8\pi G}, \qquad \Omega \equiv \frac{\rho}{\rho_c}" cite="reading1" at="p. 14 (board)" />

together with entropy conservation:

<Formula label="Entropy conservation" tex="aT \approx \text{constant}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 14–15 (board)" />

($aT$ changes by a factor of order one when electron–positron pairs disappear, the $(4/11)^{1/3}$ of the neutrino temperature, which does not matter here.)

Reshuffling the Friedmann equation, with no approximation, gives a form whose time dependence is easy to read off:

<Formula label="The Friedmann equation rearranged" tex="\frac{\Omega - 1}{\Omega} = \frac{A\,T^2}{\rho}, \qquad A \equiv \frac{3kc^2}{8\pi G\,a^2T^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 15 (board)" />

The temperature cancels between $A$ and $T^2$, so this is exactly the Friedmann equation; it is written this way because $A$ is (nearly) constant, so $(\Omega - 1)/\Omega$ follows $T^2/\rho$. (Divide $H^2 = \frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho - kc^2/a^2$ by $\frac{8\pi}{3}G\rho$ to check.) Since Ω is close to 1, the Ω in the denominator hardly matters.

**Today.** The Planck limit is largest on the negative side, $|\Omega_0 - 1| \le 0.0075$, which gives $|\Omega_0 - 1|/\Omega_0 \approx 0.0076$. Guth rounds up:

<Formula label="Present bound" tex="\left|\frac{\Omega_0 - 1}{\Omega_0}\right| < 0.01" cite="reading1" at="p. 16 (board)" />

**Matter era, from about 50,000 years to now.** For a flat matter-dominated universe $a \propto t^{2/3}$, with $T \propto 1/a$ and $\rho \propto 1/a^3$. So $T^2/\rho \propto a \propto t^{2/3}$: $\Omega - 1$ **grows** with time. (Matter–radiation equality was about 50,000 years; the class's own estimate was about 47,000.)

<Formula label="Matter era" tex="\left.\frac{\Omega - 1}{\Omega}\right|_{50{,}000\ \text{yr}} \approx \left(\frac{50{,}000\ \text{yr}}{13.8 \times 10^9\ \text{yr}}\right)^{2/3}\left.\frac{\Omega - 1}{\Omega}\right|_0" cite="reading1" at="p. 17 (board)" />

**Radiation era, from one second to 50,000 years.** Now $a \propto t^{1/2}$ and $\rho \propto 1/a^4$, so $T^2/\rho \propto a^2 \propto t$, growing even faster than in the matter era.

<Formula label="Radiation era" tex="\left.\frac{\Omega - 1}{\Omega}\right|_{1\ \text{s}} \approx \frac{1\ \text{s}}{50{,}000\ \text{yr}}\left.\frac{\Omega - 1}{\Omega}\right|_{50{,}000\ \text{yr}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 18 (board)" />

Putting the pieces together:

<Formula label="The flatness problem" tex="|\Omega - 1|_{t = 1\ \text{s}} \lesssim 10^{-18}" cite="reading1" at="p. 18 (board)" />

To be in the allowed range today, Ω at one second must have equalled 1 to **18 decimal places**, and no known physics forces Ω to be 1. With the 1980 knowledge, the present bound was about three orders of magnitude weaker, giving $10^{-15}$ instead: a thousand times larger, but still an incredibly small number, and the argument sounds exactly the same.

<WorkedExample title="Pushing the numbers">
<Problem>
Use Guth's more accurate figure $|\Omega_0 - 1|/\Omega_0 = 0.0076$ and the class's equality time of 47,000 years. How close to 1 was Ω at one second? And if the radiation-dominated scaling held all the way back to $10^{-35}$ s, how close then?
</Problem>
<Step title="Matter era">
$t_0 = 13.8 \times 10^9$ yr. $(47{,}000/13.8 \times 10^9)^{2/3} = (3.41 \times 10^{-6})^{2/3} = 2.26 \times 10^{-4}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Radiation era">
47,000 yr $= 1.48 \times 10^{12}$ s, so the factor is $6.74 \times 10^{-13}$.
</Step>
<Step title="At one second">
$0.0076 \times 2.26 \times 10^{-4} \times 6.74 \times 10^{-13} = 1.2 \times 10^{-18}$.
</Step>
<Step title="At 10⁻³⁵ s">
In the radiation era the quantity is proportional to $t$, so it shrinks by another factor $10^{35}$: $1.2 \times 10^{-53}$. (This extrapolation assumes nothing but radiation-dominated expansion before one second, exactly what inflation will change.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## No explanation, only a hope

As with the horizon problem, ignorance can be blamed on the singularity: assume the universe started with Ω exactly 1, or 1 to some extraordinary accuracy. But that is a hope for an explanation, not an explanation. Inflation, Guth says, provides a mechanism that drives the early universe towards $\Omega = 1$.

A student suggests that $\Omega = 1$ is natural because a flat universe has zero total energy. Guth disagrees: a closed universe also has zero total energy, so $\Omega = 1$ is not special that way. And $k$ can take any value on the real line, positive (closed) or negative (open), while exactly flat is the single point $k = 0$, hardly what you would expect from a random draw. Ultimately we can't know without understanding the singularity; you are free to speculate, but inflation removes the need to.

<Callout kind="note" title="Later developments">
The Planck 2018 analysis (with baryon acoustic oscillation data) gives $\Omega_k = 0.0007 \pm 0.0019$ (68% confidence): Ω is now known to equal 1 to about 0.2%, a modest tightening of the 2013 bound that leaves the argument unchanged.
</Callout>

## Summary

- $\Omega = 1$ is an unstable equilibrium: any departure grows with time.
- Exactly rewritten, the Friedmann equation reads $(\Omega - 1)/\Omega = AT^2/\rho$ with $A = 3kc^2/(8\pi Ga^2T^2)$ nearly constant, so $(\Omega - 1)/\Omega \propto t^{2/3}$ in the matter era and $\propto t$ in the radiation era.
- With $|\Omega_0 - 1| < 0.01$, at $t = 1$ s (the start of nucleosynthesis) $|\Omega - 1| \lesssim 10^{-18}$; even 1980's rough knowledge gives $10^{-15}$.
- The conventional Big Bang can only assume this; inflation explains it.
