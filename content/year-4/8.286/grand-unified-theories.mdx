The third problem of the conventional Big Bang is the **magnetic monopole problem**. Unlike the horizon and flatness problems, it is not a problem of classical cosmology. It arises only if physics at very high energies is described by **grand unified theories**, which predict magnetic monopoles and let us estimate how many the early universe would have made, far too many, without inflation. If you don't believe in grand unified theories, which you are allowed not to, the problem goes away; but there is some evidence for them.

Guth can't teach grand unified theories in ten minutes, so he collects enough facts to make a consistent picture. He runs out of time before reaching the monopoles themselves; the discussion continues in Lecture 22.

## The Standard Model of particle physics

The Standard Model, developed since the 1970s, has changed little since about 1975. The main later addition is that **neutrinos have masses**, which can be incorporated.

Guth shows the familiar chart of its particles (slide 6, from Wikimedia Commons; a version in words follows):

| | Generation I | Generation II | Generation III |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quarks (charge +2/3) | up | charm | top |
| Quarks (charge −1/3) | down | strange | bottom |
| Neutrinos | electron neutrino | muon neutrino | tau neutrino |
| Charged leptons | electron | muon | tau |

- **Spin-½ particles (fermions)** fall into three generations. There are six **flavours** of quark, and each comes in three **colours**. The colours are perfectly symmetric, absolutely identical, while the flavours all differ, with only approximate symmetries between them. With each family of quarks goes a family of **leptons**, particles without strong interactions: a neutrino and an electron-like particle.
- **Spin-1 particles (gauge bosons)**: the **photon** of electromagnetism; the **gluon**, which plays the photon's role for the strong interactions, which are more complicated; and the **Z⁰** and **W±**, carriers of the **weak interactions**, the only non-gravitational interactions neutrinos feel. A weak process example is neutron decay, $n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu_e$, where the antineutrino balances electron lepton number. Essentially anything involving neutrinos is a weak interaction.
- The chart Guth shows predates 2012 and leaves out the **Higgs boson**, the spin-0 particle of the Higgs field, found at CERN in 2012; it completes the Standard Model, and the Higgs field is the subject of Lecture 22.

With its interactions this is a complete model of how elementary particles behave, and it agrees with **every reliable experiment** since the mid-1970s. That has made particle physics a bit dull, Guth jokes, but it is a magnificent achievement.

### Why nobody thinks it is final

Guth knows nobody who regards the Standard Model as a candidate for the ultimate theory:

1. It does not include **gravity**, and there is no simple way to add it.
2. It has no good candidate for the **dark matter**.
3. It has about **28 free parameters**, numbers that must be measured before it predicts anything. That is only an aesthetic argument, but theorists take it seriously: many parameters mean ugliness, and almost all expect the true theory to have far fewer adjustable knobs, perhaps none.

### Why is the strong force short-ranged?

A student asks: the weak force is short-ranged because the W and Z are very heavy, but the strong force is short-ranged too, and the gluon is effectively massless. The answer is **confinement**. Gluons cannot exist as free particles. In a sense the strong force is long-ranged: pull apart a meson, a quark and an antiquark, and a "string" of gluon force between them pulls with a **constant** force however far you go. What intervenes is that, stretched far enough, it becomes energetically favourable for a new quark–antiquark pair to appear in the middle. The string breaks into two shorter strings, each with a quark at one end and an antiquark at the other, but a string can never simply end in empty space. At very short distances gluons behave like ordinary particles; at larger distances confinement dominates.

## Gauge theories

Grand unified theories grow out of the Standard Model through the idea of a **gauge theory**: a theory in which the fields are not themselves reality. There is a set of transformations that take one field configuration to another describing the **same** physical situation, so many field configurations correspond to one reality.

The example you know is electromagnetism. Writing the fields in terms of potentials,

<Formula label="Fields from potentials" tex="\vec E = -\vec\nabla\phi - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t}, \qquad \vec B = \vec\nabla\times\vec A, \qquad A_\mu = (-\phi, A_i)" cite="reading2" at="slide 7" />

for **any** function $\Lambda(t, \vec x)$ one may replace

<Formula label="Gauge transformation" tex="\phi'(t,\vec x) = \phi - \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial t}, \qquad \vec A'(t,\vec x) = \vec A + \vec\nabla\Lambda, \qquad A'_\mu(x) = A_\mu(x) + \frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial x^\mu}" cite="reading2" at="slide 7" />

and $\vec E$ and $\vec B$, which contain all the physics, are unchanged. [The slide writes $\phi' = \phi - \partial\Lambda/\partial t$; with $\vec E$ defined as above, and $x^0 = ct$ in the covariant form, the factor $1/c$ is needed.] All the important field theories we know are gauge theories.

Two successive gauge transformations must again be a symmetry, so it matters how they combine. In electromagnetism they simply add:

<Formula label="Combining gauge transformations" tex="\Lambda_3 = \Lambda_1 + \Lambda_2" cite="reading2" at="slide 7" />

so the group is **Abelian** (commutative).

The crucial generalisation is to **non-Abelian** gauge theories, introduced by **Yang and Mills** in 1954. Nobody knew what to do with them at first, but they became the Standard Model. Here the gauge parameter is an element of a group, and successive transformations **multiply** according to that group's rules.

## SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1)

The Standard Model uses three gauge groups, itself an example of its ugliness, and a source of extra parameters:

- **SU(3)**: the $3 \times 3$ matrices that are **unitary**, $U^\dagger U = 1$, and **special**, with determinant 1.
- **SU(2)**: the same with 2 in place of 3.
- **U(1)**: the group of phases $e^{i\phi}$, complex numbers of magnitude 1.

The three act independently, so the full gauge group is the product group **SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1)**: an element is just one element of each, written together.

## Grand unification: SU(5)

A **grand unified theory** embeds these three groups in a single **simple** group (a term with a precise meaning in group theory, but "simple" in the everyday sense will do here). The first one invented uses **SU(5)**, the $5 \times 5$ unitary matrices of determinant 1. The embedding is easy:

- put an SU(3) matrix in the upper-left $3 \times 3$ block;
- put an SU(2) matrix in the lower-right $2 \times 2$ block;
- for the U(1), give each block an overall phase: multiply the whole upper $3 \times 3$ block by $e^{2i\phi}$ and the whole lower $2 \times 2$ block by $e^{-3i\phi}$.

The phases are chosen so the determinant stays 1: $(e^{2i\phi})^3(e^{-3i\phi})^2 = e^{6i\phi - 6i\phi} = 1$. [The transcript says "the product of the five phases is 0"; it is the sum of their exponents that vanishes, so the product is 1.] Any SU(3) matrix, SU(2) matrix and angle $\phi$ together give an SU(5) matrix, and the three pieces commute with one another, so SU(5) contains SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1) as a subgroup. That is how the simplest grand unified theory works.

<WorkedExample title="Checking the SU(5) embedding">
<Problem>
Show that the block matrix $M = \text{diag}(e^{2i\phi}U_3,\ e^{-3i\phi}U_2)$, with $U_3 \in$ SU(3) and $U_2 \in$ SU(2), lies in SU(5), and that its three ingredients commute.
</Problem>
<Step title="Unitary">
$M^\dagger M = \text{diag}(U_3^\dagger U_3,\ U_2^\dagger U_2) = \text{diag}(1_3, 1_2) = 1_5$, because the phases have magnitude 1.
</Step>
<Step title="Determinant 1">
$\det M = (e^{2i\phi})^3\det U_3 \cdot (e^{-3i\phi})^2\det U_2 = e^{6i\phi}e^{-6i\phi} \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 1$.
</Step>
<Step title="Commuting">
The SU(3) piece acts only on the first three entries and the SU(2) piece only on the last two, so they commute. The U(1) piece is a multiple of the identity on each block, so it commutes with both. The product of an SU(3), an SU(2) and a U(1) element is therefore well defined in any order.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="note" title="Later developments">
The SU(5) theory Guth describes is the 1974 model of Howard Georgi and Sheldon Glashow. Its simplest version predicted that protons decay faster than experiments allow; the Super-Kamiokande detector shows that the decay $p \to e^+\pi^0$, the mode SU(5) favours, has a partial lifetime above about $10^{34}$ years. Grand unification remains attractive, but no version has been confirmed. Since 2013, the Large Hadron Collider has found no established deviation from the Standard Model.
</Callout>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Where this leads">
In a grand unified theory, the early universe passes through a phase transition in which a Higgs-type field takes on a non-zero value. Lecture 22 shows that this produces magnetic monopoles, and, in the conventional Big Bang, far too many of them. Inflation, in Lecture 23, dilutes them away.
</Callout>

## Summary

- The magnetic monopole problem exists only if grand unified theories are right.
- The Standard Model: three generations of quarks (six flavours, three colours each) and leptons, plus the photon, gluon, W± and Z⁰. It fits every reliable experiment since the mid-1970s, but lacks gravity and a dark matter candidate, and has about 28 free parameters.
- Confinement explains the short range of the strong force despite massless gluons.
- In a gauge theory many field configurations describe the same physics; electromagnetism is Abelian ($\Lambda_3 = \Lambda_1 + \Lambda_2$), while Yang–Mills theories are non-Abelian.
- The Standard Model gauge group SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1) fits inside SU(5), the first grand unified theory.
