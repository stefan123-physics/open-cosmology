The [Friedmann equation](/courses/8-286/friedmann-equation) contains one constant, $k$, or equivalently the conserved quantity $E = -kc^2/2$. In the second half of Lecture 7 Guth shows that its sign decides the whole future of a universe filled with ordinary (pressureless) matter.

## Three kinds of universe

Write the conserved quantity as an equation for the expansion speed:

<Formula label="Expansion speed" tex="\dot a^2 = 2E + \frac{8\pi}{3}\,\frac{G\rho_i}{a}" cite="reading1" />

The second term is always positive and shrinks as the universe grows. Everything depends on $E$:

- **$E > 0$ ($k < 0$), open universe.** $\dot a^2$ never drops below $2E$, so the universe expands forever, never slower than a minimum rate.
- **$E < 0$ ($k > 0$), closed universe.** The system is gravitationally *bound*. $\dot a$ falls to zero when the universe reaches its maximum size, then the expansion reverses and the universe collapses toward $a = 0$, the **Big Crunch**.
- **$E = 0$ ($k = 0$), critical or flat universe.** The borderline: the universe expands forever, but ever more slowly, with $\dot a \to 0$.

<Formula label="Maximum size of a closed universe" tex="a_{\max} = -\frac{4\pi G\rho_i}{3E} \qquad (E < 0)" cite="reading1" />

The names *open*, *closed* and *flat* describe geometry, which comes later in the course. For now they label how the universe evolves in time.

## The critical density

The flat case marks a definite density. Set $k = 0$ in the Friedmann equation, $H^2 = \tfrac{8\pi}{3}G\rho$, and solve for $\rho$:

<Formula label="Critical density" tex="\rho_c = \frac{3H^2}{8\pi G}" cite="reading1" />

A universe denser than this is closed, a less dense one open. It is convenient to measure density in units of $\rho_c$:

<Formula label="Density parameter" tex="\Omega \equiv \frac{\rho}{\rho_c}: \qquad \Omega > 1\ \text{closed}, \quad \Omega = 1\ \text{flat}, \quad \Omega < 1\ \text{open}" cite="reading1" />

Writing today's Hubble constant as $H_0 = 100\,h_0$ km/s/Mpc, the critical density is

<Formula label="Critical density in numbers" tex="\rho_c = 1.88\,h_0^2 \times 10^{-29}\ \text{g/cm}^3" cite="reading1" />

With $h_0 = 0.67$ (the Planck value Guth uses) that is $8.4 \times 10^{-30}$ g/cm³, about **five protons per cubic metre**, some $10^{-29}$ times the density of water, a far better vacuum than anything made in a laboratory.

<WorkedExample title="How big does a closed universe get?">
<Problem>
At some initial time a matter-only universe has $\Omega_i = 2$. Taking $a_i = 1$, how large does it grow before it recollapses? What if $\Omega_i = 1.01$?
</Problem>
<Step title="Express E with Ω">
With $a_i = 1$ and $\dot a_i = H_i$: $E = \tfrac12 H_i^2 - \tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\rho_i$. Since $\tfrac{4\pi}{3}G\rho_c = \tfrac12 H_i^2$, this is $E = \tfrac12 H_i^2(1 - \Omega_i)$, negative for $\Omega_i > 1$.
</Step>
<Step title="Maximum size">
$a_{\max} = -\dfrac{4\pi G\rho_i}{3E} = \dfrac{\tfrac12 H_i^2\,\Omega_i}{\tfrac12 H_i^2(\Omega_i - 1)} = \dfrac{\Omega_i}{\Omega_i - 1}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
$\Omega_i = 2$ gives $a_{\max} = 2$: the universe only doubles in size. $\Omega_i = 1.01$ gives $a_{\max} = 101$. The closer $\Omega$ starts to 1, the longer the universe behaves almost exactly like the flat case.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Our universe

What does the real universe do? In 2013 Guth summarised the measurements as:

- the **total** density is within about half a percent of critical, $\Omega_\text{total} \approx 1$;
- **matter** (ordinary and dark) makes up only about 30% of it, $\Omega_\text{matter} \approx 0.3$;
- the rest, about 70%, is **dark energy**, discovered in 1998.

So today the pressureless equations on this page do not describe our universe well. They apply to the long era of matter domination, from roughly 50,000 years after the Big Bang until a few billion years ago, when dark energy took over. Before 1998, inflation theorists expected $\Omega = 1$ while observers kept finding 0.2–0.3 in matter; both turned out to be partly right.

## The flat universe grows as t^(2/3)

The flat case is simple enough to solve right away. With $E = 0$, $\dot a^2 = \text{const}/a$, so

<Formula label="Separating the variables" tex="\frac{da}{dt} = \frac{\text{const}}{a^{1/2}} \;\Longrightarrow\; a^{1/2}\,da = \text{const}\;dt \;\Longrightarrow\; \tfrac23\,a^{3/2} = \text{const}\cdot t + c'" cite="reading1" />

The zero of cosmic time is ours to choose. Choosing $t = 0$ at the instant when $a = 0$, the Big Bang, makes $c' = 0$, and

<Formula label="Flat, matter-dominated universe" tex="a(t) \propto t^{2/3}" cite="reading1" />

Guth works out the consequence for the age of the universe in Lecture 8:

<WorkedExample title="The age of a flat matter-only universe">
<Problem>
For $a \propto t^{2/3}$, find the Hubble rate as a function of time. If today $H_0 = 67.4$ km/s/Mpc, how old would such a universe be?
</Problem>
<Step title="Hubble rate">
$H = \dot a/a = \dfrac{\tfrac23 t^{-1/3}}{t^{2/3}} = \dfrac{2}{3t}$, so the age is $t_0 = \dfrac{2}{3H_0}$.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
$1/H_0 = (97.8/67.4)\times10^{10}$ yr $= 14.5$ billion years, so $t_0 = \tfrac23 \times 14.5 = 9.7$ billion years.
</Step>
<Step title="A problem">
Some stars appear to be older than that. A flat universe of matter alone is too young, one of the clues that something else, dark energy, is in the universe. Later lectures come back to this tension between the age of the universe and the ages of the oldest stars.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- For pressureless matter, $E > 0$ means an open universe (forever expanding), $E < 0$ a closed one (maximum size, then Big Crunch), $E = 0$ the flat borderline.
- The dividing density is $\rho_c = 3H^2/(8\pi G) \approx 1.88\,h_0^2\times10^{-29}$ g/cm³, about five protons per cubic metre; $\Omega = \rho/\rho_c$.
- Today $\Omega_\text{total} \approx 1$, but only about 30% is matter; about 70% is dark energy.
- A flat matter-dominated universe grows as $a \propto t^{2/3}$ and is $2/(3H_0)$ old.
