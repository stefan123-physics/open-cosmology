The [3-sphere](/courses/8-286/sphere-metrics) describes the space of a closed universe at one moment. But the universe expands. In the last part of Lecture 11 Guth connects the radius of the 3-sphere to the scale factor $a(t)$, the one place in the course where we meet, without deriving it, how matter curves space.

## The radius grows with the scale factor

From the [Newtonian model](/courses/8-286/friedmann-equation), physical distances are $\ell_p(t) = a(t)\,\ell_c$, with comoving objects at fixed coordinates. Every distance in the 3-sphere metric is proportional to its radius $R$, so for the geometry to expand with the universe, $R(t)$ must be proportional to $a(t)$.

The units fix the rest. $R$ is in metres, $a$ in metres per notch and $k$ in 1/notch², so the only possibility is

<Formula label="Radius from units" tex="R^2(t) = (\text{dimensionless constant}) \times \frac{a^2(t)}{k}" cite="reading1" at="pp. 21–22" />

The constant says how strongly a given amount of matter curves space, and finding it needs Einstein's field equations. Guth simply states the answer. The constant is 1:

<Formula label="Radius of curvature of a closed universe" tex="R^2(t) = \frac{a^2(t)}{k}" cite="reading1" at="p. 23" />

This answers a question left open in [Lecture 9](/courses/8-286/closed-universe-cycloid): $a/\sqrt k$ is the **radius of curvature** of space. Guth describes $1/R$ as the curvature; in general relativity courses the (Gaussian) curvature of a sphere is usually defined as $1/R^2$.

## Curvature is a relativistic effect

Recall $k = -2E/c^2$, with $E < 0$ for a closed universe. Then $R^2 = a^2c^2/(2|E|)$: the radius contains $c$. In terms of quantities astronomers measure, using $\rho = \Omega\rho_c$ and $\rho_c = 3H^2/(8\pi G)$:

<Formula label="Radius of curvature from H and Ω" tex="R = \frac{c\,H^{-1}}{\sqrt{\Omega - 1}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 24" />

Two limits are revealing. If $c$ were infinite (at fixed density and expansion rate), $R$ would be infinite: space would be flat. **Curvature is a relativistic effect.** And as $\Omega \to 1$, $R \to \infty$: a universe at exactly the critical density is flat, which is why the critical-density case is called "flat".

<WorkedExample title="How curved could our universe be?">
<Problem>
In [Lecture 7](/courses/8-286/open-closed-flat) Guth quoted measurements putting $\Omega_\text{total}$ within about half a percent of 1. If the universe were closed with $\Omega - 1 = 0.005$ and $H = 67.3$ km/s/Mpc, how large would its radius of curvature be?
</Problem>
<Step title="Hubble length">
$c/H = 299{,}792 / 67.3 = 4450$ Mpc.
</Step>
<Step title="Radius">
$R = 4450/\sqrt{0.005} = 4450 \times 14.1 = 63{,}000$ Mpc, about 63 Gpc.
</Step>
<Step title="Meaning">
That is several times larger than the horizon distance, so over the part of the universe we can see, space would be only very slightly curved. (The relation $R = cH^{-1}/\sqrt{\Omega - 1}$ also holds with dark energy included, as long as $\Omega$ is the total density parameter.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The Robertson–Walker metric

Put $R^2 = a^2/k$ into the 3-sphere metric written with $u = \sin\psi$, and define a new radial coordinate $r \equiv u/\sqrt k$, measured in notches. The metric of space becomes

<Formula label="Robertson–Walker metric (space, closed universe)" tex="ds^2 = a^2(t)\left[\frac{dr^2}{1 - k r^2} + r^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 25" />

This is the **Robertson–Walker metric**, the standard description of a homogeneous, isotropic, expanding universe. The scale factor multiplies everything, so every distance grows with $a(t)$, and the constant $k$ from the Friedmann equation now appears directly in the geometry. Guth derives it here for the closed case; open universes follow in Lecture 12, "Non-Euclidean Spaces: Open Universes and the Spacetime Metric".

## Summary

- Every distance in a closed universe scales with $a(t)$, so its radius of curvature does too: $R^2 = (\text{const})\,a^2/k$, and general relativity fixes the constant to 1.
- $a/\sqrt k$ is the radius of curvature; in observable terms $R = cH^{-1}/\sqrt{\Omega - 1}$.
- Curvature disappears as $c \to \infty$ (it is relativistic) and as $\Omega \to 1$ (the flat universe).
- Substituting into the 3-sphere metric gives the Robertson–Walker metric $ds^2 = a^2(t)[dr^2/(1 - kr^2) + r^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)]$.
