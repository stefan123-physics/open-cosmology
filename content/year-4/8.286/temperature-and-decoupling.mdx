Lecture 17 ends by putting the pieces together. The previous page showed which particles make up the black-body radiation at each era; the radiation-dominated Friedmann equation fixes the density at each time. Together they give the universe's **temperature as a clock**, and follow it down to the moment the universe became transparent.

## Temperature as a function of time

In a flat radiation-dominated universe, $\rho = 3/(32\pi Gt^2)$; for black-body radiation, $\rho c^2 = u = g\frac{\pi^2}{30}\frac{(kT)^4}{(\hbar c)^3}$. Equating the two and solving for $kT$:

<Formula label="Temperature of the early universe" tex="kT = \left(\frac{45\,\hbar^3c^5}{16\pi^3\,g\,G}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{\sqrt t}" cite="reading1" at="p. 17 (board)" />

Here $G$ is Newton's constant and $g$ the effective number of degrees of freedom: spin states and particle–antiparticle pairs of every species present, with fermions counted at 7/8. Strictly $g$ changes as the universe cools through thresholds where particle types drop out, so near a threshold the formula is only approximate. But well inside any one era it is very accurate.

About one second after the Big Bang, photons, neutrinos and electron–positron pairs are all present, still at a common temperature, so $g = 10\frac34$ and

<Formula label="With g = 10¾" tex="kT = \frac{0.860\ \text{MeV}}{\sqrt{t\,[\text{s}]}}, \qquad T = \frac{9.98 \times 10^{9}\ \text{K}}{\sqrt{t\,[\text{s}]}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 17 (board)" />

[Computed exactly, the formula above gives $0.859$ MeV and $9.97 \times 10^{9}$ K; the lecture rounds them to 0.860 MeV and $9.98 \times 10^{9}$ K, the values used on this page.]

Since $0.86 \approx 1$: at one second, $kT$ was about 1 MeV, above the electron's rest energy of half an MeV. The pairs were still largely present then, and began to disappear right afterwards.

## Recombination and decoupling

The universe's ordinary matter is, in Guth's round numbers, about 20% helium and the rest hydrogen. [By mass the helium fraction is closer to 25%.] Until the temperature fell to about **4,000 K**, the hydrogen was ionised. That figure is not a property of hydrogen alone (the ionisation temperature depends on the density), but it is the value for the densities of the early universe. When the hydrogen becomes neutral is called **recombination**, though, as Guth points out, it had never been combined before; the "re" is pure convention.

Recombination is gradual, and is usually dated to the point where about half the hydrogen has recombined. More interesting is **decoupling**: the point after which a typical photon never scatters again. Because half the hydrogen is still ionised at 4,000 K, plenty of free electrons remain to scatter photons, so decoupling comes later, at a lower temperature, about **3,000 K**.

## Cooling as the universe grows

To date decoupling, Guth first adds a useful relation. Entropy is conserved, so as the universe expands the entropy **density** thins out as $1/a^3$, like the mass density of matter: this is always true. If in addition $g$ is fixed, $s \propto T^3$, and then

<Formula label="Temperature and scale factor" tex="s \propto \frac{1}{a^3(t)}, \quad s \propto T^3 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T \propto \frac{1}{a(t)}" cite="reading1" at="p. 18 (board)" />

As long as the number of species contributing is fixed, the temperature simply falls as $1/a$.

To estimate **when**, treat the universe as matter-dominated from decoupling until today. Decoupling comes long after matter–radiation equality, and dark energy (not yet introduced in the course) is ignored. Then $a \propto t^{2/3}$, so $T \propto t^{-2/3}$:

<Formula label="Time of decoupling (estimate)" tex="t_d = \left(\frac{T_0}{T_d}\right)^{3/2} t_0 = \left(\frac{2.7\ \text{K}}{3{,}000\ \text{K}}\right)^{3/2} \times 13.8 \times 10^{9}\ \text{yr} \approx 3.7 \times 10^{5}\ \text{yr}" cite="reading1" at="p. 19 (board)" />

[The transcript has "13.8 trillion years"; the age of the universe is 13.8 billion.] Guth gives the result as about 380,000 years (with $T_0 = 2.725$ K it is 378,000), and notes that this crude estimate lands almost exactly on the number people quote from careful calculations. He then rounds: decoupling was about 400,000 years after the Big Bang.

## What the CMB shows us

From decoupling on, the photons have travelled essentially in straight lines. So when we look at the cosmic microwave background, we see **an image of the universe at decoupling**, in exactly the way you see someone's face by photons travelling straight from it to your eyes. That is why the time of decoupling is such an important number.

<WorkedExample title="Milestones of the hot universe">
<Problem>
Use $kT = 0.860\ \text{MeV}/\sqrt{t\,[\text{s}]}$ (for $g = 10\frac34$) and $T \propto 1/a$ to answer: (a) when did $kT$ drop to the electron rest energy, 0.511 MeV? (b) how hot was the universe at matter–radiation equality, taking $1 + z \approx 3{,}246$ from Guth's method with $h_0 = 0.67$, $\Omega_m = 0.30$ (Guth's rounded ratio gives 3,226; Planck 2018 finds $z_\text{eq} \approx 3{,}400$)? (c) with the later measured redshift of last scattering, $z \approx 1{,}090$ (Planck 2018), what decoupling temperature and time does Guth's method give?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The electron threshold">
$\sqrt t = 0.860/0.511$, so $t \approx 2.8$ s. (Near this threshold $g$ starts to change, so this is only approximate, exactly the kind of borderline case the formula warns about.)
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Equality">
$T = 2.725\ \text{K} \times 3{,}246 \approx 8{,}850$ K, and 8,800–9,300 K for the other values: in every case hotter than 4,000 K, so at equality the hydrogen was still fully ionised.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) Decoupling">
$T_d = 2.725 \times 1{,}091 \approx 2{,}970$ K, close to Guth's 3,000 K. Then $t_d = 1{,}091^{-3/2} \times 13.8 \times 10^9$ yr $\approx 383{,}000$ years, again close to the lecture's 380,000.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Equating $\rho = 3/(32\pi Gt^2)$ with $u = g\frac{\pi^2}{30}\frac{(kT)^4}{(\hbar c)^3}$ gives $kT = (45\hbar^3c^5/16\pi^3gG)^{1/4}/\sqrt t$, accurate well inside any era of fixed $g$.
- With $g = 10\frac34$: $kT = 0.860$ MeV$/\sqrt{t\,[\text{s}]}$, $T = 9.98 \times 10^9$ K$/\sqrt{t\,[\text{s}]}$, about 1 MeV at one second.
- Conserved entropy and fixed $g$ give $T \propto 1/a$.
- Recombination (~4,000 K, half the hydrogen neutral) is followed by decoupling (~3,000 K), when photons stop scattering, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang by a matter-dominated estimate.
- The CMB is an image of the universe at decoupling.
