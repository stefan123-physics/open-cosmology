Almost everything we know about the expansion of the universe comes from one measurement: how much the light of distant galaxies has been shifted toward the red. Before we can read that shift, we need to understand the **Doppler effect**, first for sound and then for light, where special relativity changes the rules.

## Doppler shift for sound

Sound is a wave in air. Its crests travel at a fixed speed $u$ **relative to the air**, whoever made the sound. Let $\Delta t_S$ be the time between crests at the source (one period) and $\Delta t_O$ the time between crests arriving at the observer. The question is how they are related when something is moving.

### Case 1: the source moves away

The observer and the air are at rest; the source moves away at speed $v$. Between emitting one crest and the next, the source moves a distance $\Delta\ell = v\,\Delta t_S$ farther away. The second crest therefore has an extra distance $\Delta\ell$ to travel, which takes an extra time $\Delta\ell/u$:

<Formula label="Sound, source moving" tex="\Delta t_O = \Delta t_S + \frac{v\,\Delta t_S}{u} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\Delta t_O}{\Delta t_S} = 1 + \frac{v}{u}" cite="reading2" at="derived on the board, pp. 1–2; diagrams on slides 1–5" />

Astronomers describe the stretching by the **redshift** $z$, defined so that $z = 0$ means no shift at all:

<Formula label="Definition of redshift" tex="1 + z \equiv \frac{\lambda_O}{\lambda_S} = \frac{\Delta t_O}{\Delta t_S}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 2–3" />

So for a moving source of sound, $z = v/u$.

### Case 2: the observer moves away

Now the source and the air are at rest and the observer moves away at $v$. Between receiving one crest and the next, the *observer* moves $\Delta\ell = v\,\Delta t_O$, and the second crest must chase it. That gives an equation with $\Delta t_O$ on both sides:

<Formula label="Sound, observer moving" tex="\Delta t_O = \Delta t_S + \frac{v\,\Delta t_O}{u} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 1 + z = \frac{1}{1 - v/u}, \qquad z = \frac{v/u}{1 - v/u}" cite="reading2" at="derived on the board, pp. 3–4; diagrams on slides 6–10" />

For slow motion both answers are close to $z \approx v/u$. They differ by $(v/u)^2/(1 - v/u)$: for $v/u = 1/1000$ the difference is about one part in a million. They differ at all because the **air is a real, physical frame**. Moving through the air and having the air move past you are genuinely different situations.

<WorkedExample title="A passing siren">
<Problem>
An ambulance siren sounds at 440 Hz. The speed of sound is 343 m/s. What frequency do you hear (a) if the ambulance drives away from you at 30 m/s, (b) if you drive away from a parked ambulance at 30 m/s?
</Problem>
<Step title="Source moving">
$1 + z = 1 + v/u = 1 + 30/343 = 1.0875$. Frequency is one over the period, so $f_O = 440/1.0875 = 405$ Hz.
</Step>
<Step title="Observer moving">
$1 + z = 1/(1 - 30/343) = 1.0958$, so $f_O = 440 \times (1 - 30/343) = 402$ Hz.
</Step>
<Step title="Compare">
Both drop the pitch by about a semitone and a half, but by slightly different amounts. For sound, it matters who is moving.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Light has no medium

Light is different. In the 1860s Maxwell completed the equations of electromagnetism, and they predict a definite speed of light $c$, but relative to what? For decades physicists assumed an invisible medium, the **ether**, with light moving at $c$ relative to it. Einstein's insight in 1905 was to drop the ether and instead change how measurements in different frames are related, so that light moves at $c$ for every inertial observer. The rules for that change are the **Lorentz transformations** of special relativity.

Without a medium, "the source moves" and "the observer moves" are the same physical situation seen from two frames. So the two calculations **must** agree. Checking that they do is a good test of special relativity.

## Time dilation

The key ingredient is that a moving clock runs slow. If the moving clock itself records a time $\Delta t_\text{clock}$ between two of its ticks, the frame it moves through measures a longer time between those ticks:

<Formula label="Time dilation" tex="\Delta t_\text{frame} = \gamma\,\Delta t_\text{clock}, \qquad \gamma \equiv \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}}, \qquad \beta \equiv \frac{v}{c}" cite="reading1" at="slide 11" />

Guth stresses what "appears" means here. It is not what your eye sees at one instant, which is affected by light travel times. It is what is measured by a whole frame of rulers with synchronised clocks at every point. With that care, a clock moving at speed $v$ ticks once every $\gamma$ of the frame's seconds.

## The relativistic Doppler shift

**Source moving.** Repeat case 1 with $u = c$, but the source's clock now runs slow: in the observer's frame, the time between emissions is $\gamma\,\Delta t_S$, and the source moves $\Delta\ell = \gamma v\,\Delta t_S$ in that time. Then $\Delta t_O = \gamma\,\Delta t_S\,(1 + \beta)$.

**Observer moving.** Repeat case 2 in the source's frame, where the time between receptions is $\Delta t' = \Delta t_S/(1 - \beta)$. The observer's clock runs slow, so it records only $\Delta t_O = \Delta t'/\gamma$.

Using $1 - \beta^2 = (1+\beta)(1-\beta)$, both give exactly the same answer:

<Formula label="Relativistic Doppler shift" tex="1 + z = \frac{\Delta t_O}{\Delta t_S} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \beta}{1 - \beta}}" cite="reading2" at="pp. 15–19; Δℓ = γvΔt_S on slide 14, Δt_O = Δt′/γ on slide 18" />

For small $\beta$ this is $z \approx \beta + \beta^2/2$, between the two sound results. As $\beta \to 1$ the redshift grows without limit.

<WorkedExample title="From a measured line to a speed">
<Problem>
The hydrogen line at 656.3 nm is observed in a galaxy at 662.9 nm. Find $z$ and the recession speed. Then: what speed would give $z = 1$?
</Problem>
<Step title="Redshift">
$1 + z = 662.9/656.3 = 1.0101$, so $z = 0.0101$.
</Step>
<Step title="Speed (slow motion)">
$z$ is small, so $\beta \approx z$ and $v \approx 0.0101\,c \approx 3000$ km/s.
</Step>
<Step title="z = 1 needs the full formula">
$(1+z)^2 = 4 = (1+\beta)/(1-\beta)$ gives $\beta = 3/5 = 0.6$. A naive $v = zc$ would have said the speed of light.
</Step>
<Step title="A warning for cosmology">
For distant galaxies, the next lectures show that redshift is better understood as the stretching of space, $1 + z = a(t_O)/a(t_S)$, not as a velocity in one inertial frame. The Doppler formula is the special case of a universe with no gravity.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Accelerating clocks

Special relativity is sometimes said to fail for accelerated motion. Guth corrects this: acceleration is handled by special relativity plus the relevant forces; general relativity is a theory of **gravity**. What special relativity cannot say is how a real clock responds to being shaken: a wristwatch survives, a clock smashed into a wall stops. Physicists therefore define an **ideal clock**: one whose rate at each moment equals that of an unaccelerated clock moving alongside it, so only its speed matters.

## The three kinematic effects

Everything special relativity says about measuring times and distances comes down to three effects:

1. **Time dilation:** a moving clock runs slow by a factor $\gamma$.
2. **Lorentz–FitzGerald contraction:** a moving rod is shorter by a factor $\gamma$ along its motion; lengths across the motion are unchanged.
3. **Relativity of simultaneity:** two clocks at the ends of a moving rod of rest length $\ell_0$, synchronised in the rod's own frame, are not synchronised in yours: the trailing clock reads later.

<Formula label="Relativity of simultaneity" tex="\text{trailing clock ahead by}\quad \frac{\beta\,\ell_0}{c}" cite="reading1" at="slide 21" />

The third effect resolves an apparent paradox: two observers moving past each other each find the *other's* clocks running slow. There is no contradiction, because they disagree about which distant events are simultaneous.

Cosmological redshift is measured exactly as defined here, $1 + z = \lambda_O/\lambda_S$. What changes in an expanding universe is the *cause*. </Callout>

## Summary

- Redshift is defined by $1 + z = \lambda_O/\lambda_S = \Delta t_O/\Delta t_S$.
- For sound, source motion gives $z = v/u$ and observer motion $z = (v/u)/(1 - v/u)$: the medium makes them different.
- For light there is no medium; with time dilation both cases give $1 + z = \sqrt{(1+\beta)/(1-\beta)}$.
- Special relativity's kinematics is three effects: time dilation, length contraction and the relativity of simultaneity.
