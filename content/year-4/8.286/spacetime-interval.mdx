General relativity is a theory of the geometry of **spacetime**, not just of space. So after the geometry of open, flat and closed universes, the end of Lecture 12 adds time. The starting point is a fact from special relativity, which Guth takes as given (an appendix to 8.286's Lecture Notes 5 derives it).

## Events and the interval

An **event** is a point in spacetime: a place and a time, like the snap of your fingers (ideally shrunk to a single point). For two events $a$ and $b$ define the **interval**:

<Formula label="The spacetime interval" tex="s_{ab}^2 \equiv (x_a - x_b)^2 + (y_a - y_b)^2 + (z_a - z_b)^2 - c^2(t_a - t_b)^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 23" />

Different inertial observers, related by Lorentz transformations, in general disagree about the four differences (a boost along $x$ changes $\Delta x$ and $\Delta t$, for example). Yet they all compute the **same** $s_{ab}^2$: the interval is **Lorentz invariant**. It is to spacetime what distance is to ordinary space.

## Three kinds of separation

The sign of $s_{ab}^2$ is therefore something every observer agrees on, and it sorts pairs of events into three kinds.

**Spacelike, $s^2 > 0$.** There is a frame in which the two events happen at the same time. In that frame $s$ is just the ordinary distance between them, called the **proper distance**.

**Timelike, $s^2 < 0$.** There is a frame in which the two events happen at the same place: think of a strobe light moving at constant velocity, flashing twice. In that frame only time separates them:

<Formula label="Proper time" tex="s_{ab}^2 = -c^2\,\tau_{ab}^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 25" />

$\tau_{ab}$ is the **proper time**: what a wristwatch carried from one event to the other at constant velocity would read.

**Lightlike, $s^2 = 0$.** A light pulse could travel from one event to the other (in one direction only). For such pairs there is no invariant notion of how far apart they are: observers moving along the light ray can make the separation look arbitrarily small or large.

<WorkedExample title="Finding the special frame">
<Problem>
Events $a$ at the origin and $b$ at $x = 5$, $ct = 3$ (light-seconds). Show that they are spacelike, find the proper distance, and find the speed of the frame in which they are simultaneous.
</Problem>
<Step title="Interval">
$s^2 = 5^2 - 3^2 = 16 > 0$: spacelike, with proper distance $s = 4$ light-seconds.
</Step>
<Step title="The frame">
In a frame moving at $\beta$ along $x$, $c\,t' = \gamma(ct - \beta x)$. Setting $t' = 0$ for event $b$: $3 - 5\beta = 0$, so $\beta = 0.6$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check">
In that frame $\gamma = 1.25$ and $x' = \gamma(x - \beta ct) = 1.25(5 - 1.8) = 4$: exactly the proper distance, as promised.
</Step>
<Step title="The timelike twin">
Swap the numbers, $x = 3$, $ct = 5$: $s^2 = 9 - 25 = -16$, timelike, with proper time $\tau = 4$ s, measured in a frame moving at $\beta = 3/5$ in which both events happen at the same place.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The infinitesimal interval

For two events infinitesimally close together, the interval becomes

<Formula label="The interval between nearby events" tex="ds^2 = dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2 - c^2\,dt^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 27" />

This is the spacetime version of Gauss's metric: three space directions add, time subtracts. Mathematicians say it has **three positive and one negative** eigenvalue: it is not positive definite, unlike the metric of a space. (Some books use the opposite overall sign.) Combining this with the Robertson–Walker metric gives the spacetime metric of the expanding universe, which is where the next lecture begins.

<Callout kind="note" title="A common confusion">
A lightlike separation is a statement about two **events**, where and when they happen, not about two objects moving at the speed of light relative to each other.
</Callout>

## Summary

- An event is a point in spacetime; the interval $s^2 = \Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2 + \Delta z^2 - c^2\Delta t^2$ is the same for every inertial observer.
- $s^2 > 0$: spacelike (simultaneous in some frame; $s$ = proper distance). $s^2 < 0$: timelike (same place in some frame; $s^2 = -c^2\tau^2$, $\tau$ = proper time). $s^2 = 0$: lightlike.
- The infinitesimal form $ds^2 = dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2 - c^2dt^2$ has signature (+, +, +, −) and is the starting point for spacetime metrics in general relativity.
