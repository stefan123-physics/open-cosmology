Lecture Notes 15 are Guth's slides for class Lecture 36 (December 10, 2012), the second-last lecture of the course. The [previous notes](/courses/8-07/wave-equation-group-velocity) found electromagnetic waves in empty space. Now Guth asks how changing charges and currents produce fields at all, and answers with potentials. This first page covers slides 1–14: the potentials, the freedom to change them, the two usual gauge choices and the retarded solutions. The [second page](/courses/8-07/lienard-wiechert-point-charge-fields) applies them to a moving point charge. (Our own words and worked example.)

## Maxwell's equations with sources

Guth starts from all four of Maxwell's equations with charges and currents (slide 1):

<Formula label="Maxwell's equations with sources" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec E = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\rho, \qquad \nabla \cdot \vec B = 0, \qquad \nabla \times \vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla \times \vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="slide 1, eq. (1)" />

Guth numbers them (i) to (iv) in this order. The question of the lecture: given the charge density $\rho(\vec r, t)$ and the current density $\vec J(\vec r, t)$ everywhere, can we find $\vec E$ and $\vec B$? If we accept that every integral can be done, at least numerically, the answer is yes, and the rest of the lecture shows how.

## The potentials when fields change

In statics $\nabla \times \vec E = 0$, so $\vec E = -\nabla V$. When $\vec B$ changes in time, (iii) says $\nabla \times \vec E \ne 0$, and that is no longer possible (slide 2). But $\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0$ still holds, so we can still write $\vec B$ as a curl (slide 2):

<Formula label="The magnetic field from the vector potential" tex="\vec B = \nabla \times \vec A" cite="reading1" at="slide 2, eq. (2) (boxed)" />

Put this into Faraday's law (iii). The curl of $\vec E$ equals minus the time derivative of the curl of $\vec A$, so the combination $\vec E + \partial\vec A/\partial t$ has no curl (slide 2):

<Formula label="A combination with no curl" tex="\nabla \times \vec E = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla \times \vec A\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla \times \left(\vec E + \frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t}\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="slide 2, eq. (3)" />

A field with no curl is minus the gradient of something, which we call $V$ (slide 2):

<Formula label="The electric field from the potentials" tex="\vec E + \frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t} = -\nabla V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec E = -\nabla V - \frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="slide 2, eq. (4) (boxed)" />

With these two formulas together (slide 3, eq. (5)), the two source-free equations, (ii) and (iii), hold automatically: the divergence of a curl is zero, and (3) is just (iii) rewritten. All the physics left is in the two equations with sources, (i) and (iv) (slide 3).

## Maxwell's other two equations

Gauss's law (i) with $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial\vec A/\partial t$ becomes (slide 4)

<Formula label="Gauss's law in potentials" tex="\nabla^2 V + \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla \cdot \vec A\right) = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\rho" cite="reading1" at="slide 4, eq. (6)" />

For (iv), put $\vec B = \nabla \times \vec A$ on the left and $\vec E$ on the right (slide 4):

<Formula label="The Ampère–Maxwell law in potentials" tex="\nabla \times \left(\nabla \times \vec A\right) = \mu_0\vec J - \frac{1}{c^2}\nabla\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\right) - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\vec A}{\partial t^2}" cite="reading1" at="slide 4" />

The double curl is a standard vector identity (slide 4):

<Formula label="The curl of a curl" tex="\nabla \times \left(\nabla \times \vec A\right) = \nabla\left(\nabla \cdot \vec A\right) - \nabla^2\vec A" cite="reading1" at="slide 4, eq. (8)" />

and with it, after collecting the gradient terms (slide 4):

<Formula label="The equation for the vector potential" tex="\left(\nabla^2\vec A - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\vec A}{\partial t^2}\right) - \nabla\left(\nabla \cdot \vec A + \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}\right) = -\mu_0\vec J" cite="reading1" at="slide 4, eq. (7)" />

Equations (6) and (7) are four coupled equations for four unknowns, $V$ and the three components of $\vec A$. They look messy. The next step makes them simple.

## Gauge transformations

The potentials are not fixed by the fields. Guth had discussed this for statics; it carries over to electrodynamics unchanged (slide 5). Take any scalar function $\Lambda(\vec r, t)$ and define new potentials by a **gauge transformation** (slide 5):

<Formula label="A gauge transformation" tex="\vec A\,' = \vec A + \nabla\Lambda, \qquad V' = V - \frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="slide 5, eq. (9) (boxed)" />

The magnetic field does not change, because the curl of a gradient is zero (slide 5):

<Formula label="The magnetic field is unchanged" tex="\vec B\,' = \nabla \times \vec A\,' = \nabla \times \vec A + \nabla \times \nabla\Lambda = \nabla \times \vec A = \vec B" cite="reading1" at="slide 5, eq. (10)" />

Nor does the electric field: the extra term from $V'$ and the extra term from $\vec A\,'$ are the same mixed derivative of $\Lambda$ with opposite signs (slide 5):

<Formula label="The electric field is unchanged" tex="\vec E\,' = -\nabla V' - \frac{\partial\vec A\,'}{\partial t} = -\nabla V - \frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t} + \nabla\left(\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial t}\right) - \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\nabla\Lambda = \vec E" cite="reading1" at="slide 5, eq. (11)" />

So the physics is the same in every gauge, and we may pick whichever makes the equations easiest. The same freedom appears in quantum mechanics, where it is tied to the phase of the wave function; see the 8.06 page on [potentials and gauge invariance](/courses/8-06/charged-particles-em-fields-gauge-invariance).

<WorkedExample title="Two sets of potentials for one uniform field">
<Problem>
A uniform, constant electric field $\vec E = E_0\,\hat x$ with $E_0 = 200$ V/m fills a region, and there is no magnetic field. (a) Show that $V = -E_0x$, $\vec A = 0$ and also $V = 0$, $\vec A = -E_0 t\,\hat x$ both describe it. (b) Find the gauge function $\Lambda$ that turns the first set into the second. (c) Evaluate both sets at $x = 3$ m and $t = 5$ s. (d) Which gauge conditions do the two sets satisfy?
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) Both give the same fields">
First set: $-\nabla V = E_0\,\hat x$ and $\partial\vec A/\partial t = 0$, so $\vec E = E_0\,\hat x$; $\nabla \times \vec A = 0$. Second set: $\nabla V = 0$ and $-\partial\vec A/\partial t = E_0\,\hat x$, so again $\vec E = E_0\,\hat x$; and $\vec A$ does not depend on position, so its curl is zero.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The gauge function">
We need $\nabla\Lambda = -E_0 t\,\hat x$, so $\Lambda = -E_0xt$ (plus a constant). Check the scalar potential: $V' = V - \partial\Lambda/\partial t = -E_0x + E_0x = 0$, as required.
</Step>
<Step title="(c) The numbers">
At $x = 3$ m and $t = 5$ s the first set has $V = -200 \times 3 = -600$ V and $\vec A = 0$. The second has $V = 0$ and $A_x = -200 \times 5 = -1000$ V s/m, which is the same as $-1000$ T m. The potentials differ completely; the field, 200 V/m along $x$, is the same.
</Step>
<Step title="(d) The gauge conditions">
Both have $\nabla \cdot \vec A = 0$ and $\partial V/\partial t = 0$, so both satisfy the Coulomb gauge condition and the Lorenz condition of a later section. A gauge condition therefore does not always pick out one set of potentials. Here $\Lambda = -E_0xt$ has $\nabla^2\Lambda = 0$ and $\partial^2\Lambda/\partial t^2 = 0$, and any $\Lambda$ with $\nabla^2\Lambda - (1/c^2)\,\partial^2\Lambda/\partial t^2 = 0$ keeps the Lorenz condition true (a remark of ours, not on the slides).
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Coulomb gauge

Gauge freedom lets us make $\nabla \cdot \vec A$ whatever we want (slide 6): if $\nabla \cdot \vec A$ is some function $f$, a $\Lambda$ with $\nabla^2\Lambda = -f$ makes the new divergence zero (our step). The simplest choice is the **Coulomb gauge** (slide 6):

<Formula label="The Coulomb gauge" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec A = 0" cite="reading1" at="slide 6, eq. (12) (boxed)" />

Then (6) loses its second term and becomes Poisson's equation, exactly as in electrostatics (slide 6):

<Formula label="The scalar potential in Coulomb gauge" tex="\nabla^2 V + \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\nabla \cdot \vec A\right) = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\rho \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \nabla^2 V = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\rho" cite="reading1" at="slide 6, eq. (13)" />

Guth's verdict (slide 6): $V$ is easy to find, but $\vec A$, which still obeys the messy equation (7), is hard. There is something odd here too. Poisson's equation contains no time derivative, so $V$ everywhere responds **instantaneously** to any change in $\rho$, however far away. That does not break relativity, because $V$ by itself is not measurable. Only $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ act on charges, and they receive information only at the speed of light: the instantaneous change of $-\nabla V$ is cancelled by a change in $-\partial\vec A/\partial t$ until a light signal could have arrived (our explanation, not on the slide).

## Lorenz gauge and the d'Alembertian

The other choice makes the second bracket of (7) vanish (slide 6):

<Formula label="The Lorenz gauge" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec A = -\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="slide 6, eq. (14) (boxed)" />

[Guth, like the third edition of Griffiths, calls it the "Lorentz gauge". The condition was published by the Danish physicist Ludvig Lorenz in 1867, so many books now write "Lorenz gauge", as we do; the physics is the same.] Put it into (6) (slide 6):

<Formula label="The scalar potential in Lorenz gauge" tex="\nabla^2 V - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial t^2} = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\rho" cite="reading1" at="slide 6, eq. (15)" />

This is the wave equation of the [previous notes](/courses/8-07/wave-equation-group-velocity) in three dimensions, with a source. The operator on the left gets a name, the **d'Alembertian** (slide 7):

<Formula label="The d'Alembertian" tex="\Box^2 = \nabla^2 - \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}" cite="reading1" at="slide 7, eq. (16)" />

In Lorenz gauge the two equations take the same form (slide 7):

<Formula label="The scalar potential's wave equation" tex="\Box^2 V = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\rho" cite="reading1" at="slide 7, eq. (17) (boxed)" />

<Formula label="The vector potential's wave equation" tex="\Box^2\vec A = -\mu_0\vec J" cite="reading1" at="slide 7, eq. (18) (boxed)" />

since the bracket in (7) that multiplies the gradient is now zero. Four uncoupled equations, one for $V$ and one for each component of $\vec A$, all of the same kind: solve one and you have solved them all. In special relativity they combine into one equation for the 4-potential, as on the 8.033 page on [Maxwell's equations and the 4-potential](/courses/8-033/maxwell-equations-four-potential).

## Guessing the solution

Guth's method for $\Box^2 V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$: guess a solution, then show that it works (slide 8). The guess comes from statics, where the solution of Poisson's equation is (slide 8)

<Formula label="The static solution" tex="\nabla^2 V = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\rho \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r\,', t)}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}" cite="reading1" at="slide 8, eq. (19)" />

In the wave equation, disturbances travel at speed $c$. So try the same integral, but with each piece of charge taken at the moment its influence would have left it to arrive at $\vec r$ at time $t$ (slide 8):

<Formula label="The trial solution" tex="V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}" cite="reading1" at="slide 8, eq. (20) (boxed)" />

where

<Formula label="The retarded time" tex="t_r = t - \frac{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}{c}" cite="reading1" at="slide 8, eq. (21) (boxed)" />

is the **retarded time**. Note that $t_r$ depends on $\vec r\,'$: different parts of the source are seen at different moments.

## Checking the guess

To test it, write $\vec r = x_i\,\hat e_i$ (summed over $i$) and differentiate with respect to the field point's coordinates. Three facts are needed (slide 9). The distance changes as

<Formula label="The gradient of the distance" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}|\vec r - \vec r\,'| = \frac{x_i - x'_i}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}" cite="reading1" at="slide 9, eq. (22)" />

so, through $t_r$, the charge density changes as

<Formula label="The derivative through the retarded time" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\rho(\vec r\,', t_r) = -\frac{1}{c}\,\dot\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)\,\frac{x_i - x'_i}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}" cite="reading1" at="slide 9, eq. (22)" />

where $\dot\rho$ is the derivative of $\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)$ with respect to its time argument, and $\ddot\rho$ the second derivative. The third fact is the delta-function identity of the [8.07 page on the dipole's current](/courses/8-07/ideal-magnetic-dipole-current) and of electrostatics (slide 9):

<Formula label="The divergence of the Coulomb field" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\,\frac{x_i - x'_i}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3} = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r\,')" cite="reading1" at="slide 9, eq. (22)" />

The gradient of the trial $V$ has two terms: one from $\rho$ changing through $t_r$, one from $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ (slide 9):

<Formula label="The gradient of the trial solution" tex="\frac{\partial V}{\partial x_i} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3x'\left[-\frac{1}{c}\,\frac{\dot\rho}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^2}\,(x_i - x'_i) - \frac{\rho}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3}\,(x_i - x'_i)\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 9" />

Differentiate again and sum over $i$. Five terms appear (slide 9): $-4\pi\rho\,\delta^3$ from the identity; $+\frac{1}{c}\dot\rho/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^2$ from differentiating $\rho$ in the second term; $+\frac{1}{c^2}\ddot\rho/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ from differentiating $\dot\rho$ in the first; and, from differentiating the rest of the first term, $+\frac{2}{c}\dot\rho/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^2$ (from $|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^{-2}$) and $-\frac{3}{c}\dot\rho/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^2$ (from $\partial(x_i - x'_i)/\partial x_i = 3$). The three $\dot\rho$ terms cancel, since $1 + 2 - 3 = 0$, and what is left is (slide 10)

<Formula label="The Laplacian of the trial solution" tex="\nabla^2 V = -\frac{\rho(\vec r, t)}{\varepsilon_0} + \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2}\int d^3x'\,\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}\,\frac{\partial^2\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)}{\partial t_r^2}" cite="reading1" at="slide 10" />

The delta function set $\vec r\,' = \vec r$ in the first term, where $t_r = t$. In the second, $\partial^2/\partial t_r^2$ is the same as $\partial^2/\partial t^2$ at fixed $\vec r$ and $\vec r\,'$, and it can come out of the integral, which is then $V$ itself (slide 10):

<Formula label="The guess works" tex="\nabla^2 V = -\frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\rho + \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial t^2}" cite="reading1" at="slide 10" />

That is $\Box^2 V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$: Guth's slide answers "YES!" (slide 10).

## The retarded potentials

Each component of $\vec A$ obeys the same equation with $\mu_0 J_j$ in place of $\rho/\varepsilon_0$, so the same solution works. Together, the **retarded potentials** are (slide 11)

<Formula label="The retarded potentials" tex="V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}, \qquad \vec A(\vec r, t) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\vec J(\vec r\,', t_r)}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}" cite="reading1" at="slide 11, eq. (23) (boxed)" />

<Formula label="The retarded time again" tex="t_r = t - \frac{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}{c}" cite="reading1" at="slide 11, eq. (24) (boxed)" />

[The slide has $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ in front of the integral for $\vec A$ as well; going from $\rho/\varepsilon_0$ to $\mu_0\vec J$ makes it $\mu_0/4\pi$, as written here and as in Guth's own eq. (38) later.] These are the static formulas with the source looked at "late": the potential here and now is built from what each part of the source was doing when light now arriving here left it. One condition we have not checked: the solution must satisfy the Lorenz condition (14) as well as the wave equations. It does, thanks to charge conservation, $\partial\rho/\partial t + \nabla \cdot \vec J = 0$ (a standard textbook exercise; the slides do not discuss it).

## Advanced solutions and the arrow of time

Run the same check with the **advanced time** instead (slide 12):

<Formula label="The advanced potentials" tex="V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r\,', t_a)}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}, \qquad \vec A(\vec r, t) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\vec J(\vec r\,', t_a)}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}" cite="reading1" at="slide 12, eq. (25) (boxed)" />

<Formula label="The advanced time" tex="t_a = t + \frac{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}{c}" cite="reading1" at="slide 12, eq. (26) (boxed)" />

[The slide again has $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ for $\vec A$.] Every step of the check goes through with $+1/c$ in place of $-1/c$: the $\dot\rho$ terms change sign together and still cancel, and the $\ddot\rho$ term, with $1/c^2$, is unchanged. So these potentials, built from what the charges *will* be doing, solve Maxwell's equations just as well. Guth stresses that neither Maxwell's equations nor any law of physics we know tells the future from the past (slide 12).

Yet nature always picks the retarded solution: a radio receives a broadcast after it is sent, never before. Guth calls this the **mystery of the arrow of time** (slide 13). The slide shows still frames from a YouTube clip, which are not under the course's Creative Commons licence and are not shown here. His summary: real events have an arrow of time; the laws of physics are time symmetric.

The most often heard explanation (slide 14): the universe started out in a state of low entropy (highly ordered), so entropy, or disorder, has been increasing ever since. That moves the question rather than answering it: why did the universe start in a low-entropy state? Guth's answer on the slide: "Who knows?" His own preferred explanation (slide 14) is that there may be no upper limit to the entropy of the universe, so any state it may have started in is low-entropy compared with what it can become, and entropy can then always increase.

Both explanations on slide 14 trace the everyday arrow of time, and with it the choice of the retarded solution, to the state in which the universe began. ## Summary

- When $\vec B$ changes in time, $\vec E$ is not a gradient, but $\vec B = \nabla \times \vec A$ and $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial\vec A/\partial t$ always work, and they satisfy $\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0$ and Faraday's law automatically.
- The remaining equations, Gauss's law and the Ampère–Maxwell law, become two coupled equations for $V$ and $\vec A$.
- A **gauge transformation**, $\vec A\,' = \vec A + \nabla\Lambda$ and $V' = V - \partial\Lambda/\partial t$, leaves $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ unchanged. In the worked example $V = -E_0x$ and $\vec A = -E_0t\,\hat x$ describe the same uniform field, joined by $\Lambda = -E_0xt$.
- **Coulomb gauge**, $\nabla \cdot \vec A = 0$: $\nabla^2 V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$, so $V$ changes instantly, but $V$ is not measurable and $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ change only at the speed of light.
- **Lorenz gauge**, $\nabla \cdot \vec A = -(1/c^2)\,\partial V/\partial t$: $\Box^2 V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$ and $\Box^2\vec A = -\mu_0\vec J$, with the d'Alembertian $\Box^2 = \nabla^2 - (1/c^2)\,\partial^2/\partial t^2$.
- The solutions are the **retarded potentials**: the static integrals with each source point taken at $t_r = t - |\vec r - \vec r\,'|/c$. Guth checks this by differentiating twice; three terms cancel and one gives the $\partial^2 V/\partial t^2$ term.
- The **advanced potentials**, with $t_a = t + |\vec r - \vec r\,'|/c$, solve the same equations. That nature uses only the retarded ones is part of the arrow of time, which the usual explanation traces to a low-entropy beginning of the universe.
