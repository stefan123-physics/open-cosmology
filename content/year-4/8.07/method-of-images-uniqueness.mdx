A charge brought near a piece of metal pulls charge of the opposite sign onto the metal's surface. The field is then the sum of the field of the charge and that of the induced surface charge, and the induced charge is exactly what we do not know. Guth's handwritten notes for this lecture show a trick that solves some such problems in a few lines: replace the metal by imaginary **image charges** placed where the metal is (notes p. 58). The tools come from the earlier notes: [the integral form of electrostatics](/courses/8-07/integral-form-electrostatics), [the energy of a set of charges](/courses/8-07/electrostatic-energy-self-energy) and [the field at a conductor's surface](/courses/8-07/conductor-surface-forces-capacitance). (Units are SI, as in the notes. MIT's material for this lecture is a set of handwritten pages numbered 58 to 65, with three more, 63-4 to 63-6, in a second hand; this page covers pp. 58 to 63-4 and the [next page](/courses/8-07/separation-of-variables-cartesian) the rest. Everything here is in our own words, with our own examples.)

## A charge above a conducting plane

Guth discusses two examples, a plane and a sphere, and promises to come back to the question of whether there are others (notes p. 58). The first is a large conducting plane, the $xy$ plane, held at zero potential (grounded), with a point charge $Q$ at height $d$ above it. Above the plane the potential must satisfy Poisson's equation with a single point charge as its source:

<Formula label="Poisson's equation for the problem" tex="\nabla^2 V = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0} = -\frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_d), \qquad \vec r_d = (0, 0, d)" cite="reading1" at="p. 58" />

(The notes write $\vec r_d$ as $d$ times the unit vector along $z$.) This must hold for $z > 0$, with the boundary condition

<Formula label="The boundary condition" tex="V(x, y, 0) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 58" />

The answer is to forget the metal and put a second charge, $-Q$, at the mirror point $z = -d$ (notes p. 58). Every point of the plane is the same distance from the two charges, so their potentials cancel there, and above the plane the only charge is $Q$. Both conditions are met.

## The potential, the induced charge and the force

The potential above the plane is that of the charge and its image (notes p. 59):

<Formula label="Potential above the plane" tex="V(x, y, z) = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z - d)^2}} - \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z + d)^2}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 59" />

It holds only for $z \geq 0$. Below the plane, inside the metal, the real potential is zero and the real field vanishes; the image charge is a device for getting the field right where we want it. The field is $\vec E = -\nabla V$, and the surface charge follows from the field just outside the conductor, $\sigma = \varepsilon_0\vec E \cdot \hat n$, with $\hat n$ the outward normal of the conductor, here pointing up (notes p. 59):

<Formula label="Surface charge from the potential" tex="\sigma = \varepsilon_0 E_z = -\varepsilon_0\left.\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\right|_{z = 0}" cite="reading1" at="p. 59" />

The notes stop at the derivative. Carrying it out, with $s = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ the distance from the point straight below the charge, each term gives the same contribution:

<Formula label="Induced surface charge" tex="\sigma(s) = -\frac{Q\,d}{2\pi\,(s^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 59" />

The induced charge has the opposite sign to $Q$ and is densest right under it, $-Q/(2\pi d^2)$ at $s = 0$, falling off as $1/s^3$ far away.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 4]} range={[-1.1, 0.1]} xLabel="s/d" yLabel="σ · 2πd²/Q" samples={300}
  curves={[
    { expr: "-1/(1 + x^2)^(3/2)" }
  ]}
  caption="The surface charge induced on the grounded plane, in units of Q/(2πd²), against the distance s from the foot of the charge in units of the height d, computed from σ(s) = −Qd/[2π(s² + d²)^(3/2)]. Within s = d lies about 29% of the induced charge, within s = √3 d half of it." />

The total induced charge is (notes p. 59)

<Formula label="Total induced charge" tex="Q_{\text{induced}} = \int\sigma(x, y)\,dx\,dy = 2\pi\int_0^\infty\sigma(s)\,s\,ds = -Q" cite="reading1" at="p. 59" />

The integral is easy with the formula for $\sigma$, but Guth notes that the answer is known in advance by Gauss's law. Take a closed surface made of a huge hemisphere above the plane and a flat lid just under the surface of the metal. Far away, the charge and its image look like a dipole, whose field falls as $1/r^3$, so the flux through the hemisphere goes to zero as it grows; through the lid, inside the metal, there is no flux at all. So the surface encloses no net charge: $Q + Q_{\text{induced}} = 0$.

The force on $Q$ is $\vec F = Q\vec E$, where $\vec E$ must **exclude** the field that $Q$ itself creates (notes p. 59). What is left is the field of the induced charge, which above the plane is the field of the image. So the charge is pulled towards the plane by

<Formula label="Force on the charge" tex="|\vec F| = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q^2}{(2d)^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 59" />

the Coulomb attraction between $Q$ and $-Q$ at distance $2d$. (The notes ask the question and give $\vec F = Q\vec E$; the value is this page's step.)

## The energy, and why it is half

The energy comes from the formula of the earlier notes, $W = \frac12\int\rho V\,d^3x$, where for point charges the **self-potentials** must be left out (notes p. 60). The charge density above the plane is $Q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_d)$, and without its own potential, $V$ at the charge is the image's:

<Formula label="Energy of a charge above a grounded plane" tex="W = \frac12\int Q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_d)\left[\frac{-Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + (z + d)^2}}\right]d^3x = -\frac{Q^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{2d}" cite="reading1" at="p. 60" />

The surface charge $\sigma$ adds nothing, because it sits where $V = 0$ (notes p. 60). This is **half** of the energy of a real pair of charges $Q$ and $-Q$ a distance $2d$ apart, which is $-Q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\cdot 2d)$.

Why half? Guth's answer (notes p. 60): for the real pair, imagine bringing both charges in from infinity symmetrically, each at distance $d$ from the $xy$ plane at every moment. Both charges move, and the field does work on both. In the conductor problem only one charge has to be moved; the induced charge rearranges itself on the plane at zero potential, which costs nothing, and the force on $Q$ at each height is the same as in the pair. So the work is half. A check by direct integration: bringing $Q$ in from infinity against the attraction above takes $W = -\int_d^\infty Q^2\,dz/[4\pi\varepsilon_0(2z)^2] = -Q^2/(16\pi\varepsilon_0 d)$, the same answer.

## Two planes at right angles

The method works for more than one plane (notes p. 61). Take two grounded half-planes meeting at a right angle along the $z$ axis, one along the positive $x$ axis and one along the positive $y$ axis, with $Q$ at $x = h$, $y = d$ in the corner between them. Three images do it: $-Q$ at $(-h, d)$, $+Q$ at $(-h, -d)$ and $-Q$ at $(h, -d)$. The four charges sit at the corners of a rectangle, with signs that alternate around it. Pairs of opposite charges mirror each other across the $y$ axis and across the $x$ axis, so both planes are at zero potential, and in the corner the only real charge is $Q$. Guth's verdict: "Works!" (A similar construction works whenever the angle between the planes is 180° divided by a whole number; that general rule is not in the notes.)

## A grounded sphere

The second of Guth's examples is a grounded conducting sphere of radius $a$, with a charge $Q$ at distance $R > a$ from its centre (notes p. 62). Guess a single image $q$ inside the sphere, on the line from the centre to $Q$, at distance $b$ from the centre (the notes call this distance $r$). Take any point on the sphere, at angle $\theta$ from the direction to $Q$; it is at distance $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\theta}$ from the image and $\sqrt{a^2 + R^2 - 2aR\cos\theta}$ from $Q$. (Guth writes $\cos\theta$ as the dot product of the unit vectors towards the point and towards $Q$.) The total potential must be the same at every such point. Try zero, which is right for a grounded sphere:

<Formula label="Zero potential on the sphere" tex="\frac{q}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\theta}} + \frac{Q}{\sqrt{a^2 + R^2 - 2aR\cos\theta}} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 62" />

(The common factor $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ drops out, and the notes leave it off.) So $q$ must have the opposite sign to $Q$. Rearranging and squaring:

<Formula label="Squared condition" tex="a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos\theta = \left(\frac{q}{Q}\right)^2\left(a^2 + R^2 - 2aR\cos\theta\right)" cite="reading1" at="p. 62" />

This must hold for every $\theta$, so the terms without $\cos\theta$ and the terms with it must match separately:

<Formula label="Two conditions" tex="a^2 + b^2 = \left(\frac{q}{Q}\right)^2\left(a^2 + R^2\right), \qquad b = \left(\frac{q}{Q}\right)^2 R" cite="reading1" at="p. 62" />

Two equations for the two unknowns $q$ and $b$. Putting the second into the first gives $(b - R)(b - a^2/R) = 0$. The root $b = R$, with $q = -Q$, puts the image on top of the charge and cancels everything; it is useless, because the image must lie outside the region where we want the field, that is, inside the sphere. The other root is Guth's boxed solution (notes p. 63):

<Formula label="Image charge for a grounded sphere" tex="q = -\frac{a}{R}\,Q, \qquad b = \frac{a^2}{R}" cite="reading1" at="p. 63" />

The image is smaller than $Q$, and it lies inside the sphere. As $Q$ approaches the surface, $R \to a$, the image moves out to meet it and grows to $-Q$: seen from very close, the sphere looks like a plane. Outside the sphere the field is that of $Q$ and $q$, so by the same Gauss's-law argument as for the plane, the total charge induced on the sphere is $q$, not $-Q$: part of the field lines of $Q$ escape to infinity.

## Other image problems

Are there other problems with an image solution? Yes, as many as one likes (notes p. 63). Take any set of charges, say $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ on one side and $Q_3$ and $Q_4$ on the other, and compute any of their equipotential surfaces, $V = V_0$. Now consider a new problem: $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ together with a conducting surface, of that shape, held at $V_0$. It is solved by the images $Q_3$ and $Q_4$: on their side of the surface, the field of all four charges meets every condition. The plane and the sphere are just the two cases where the surface is simple and the images are easy to find.

## The uniqueness theorem: a licence to guess

Why may we trust a guess? The page that follows in the notes, in a second hand, calls the **uniqueness theorem** "our license to guess" (notes p. 63-4). The theorem says: in a region where the charge density is given, Poisson's equation has only one solution with the given values of $V$ on the boundary (or, for conductors, with the given total charge on each). The notes do not prove it here; the usual argument is short. If $V_1$ and $V_2$ were two solutions, their difference $U = V_1 - V_2$ would satisfy Laplace's equation, $\nabla^2 U = 0$, and vanish on the boundary. Then $\int|\nabla U|^2\,d^3x = \oint U\,\nabla U \cdot d\vec a - \int U\,\nabla^2 U\,d^3x = 0$, so $\nabla U = 0$ everywhere and $U$ is the constant it is on the boundary, zero. If instead each conductor's total charge is given, $U$ is constant on each conductor and the flux of $\nabla U$ through its surface is zero (both solutions put the same charge on it), so each conductor's part of the surface term vanishes again; at infinity $U$ falls off fast enough for that part to vanish too, and again $\nabla U = 0$: the field, and with it the charge on each surface, is unique. This is what the [Lecture 4 notes](/courses/8-07/conductor-surface-forces-capacitance) promised: the charges on a set of conductors can arrange themselves in only one way.

So any trick is allowed, and the notes turn the method of images into a recipe (notes p. 63-4):

1. The charge $q$ at some point is given (or assumed); use the symmetry of the problem.
2. Guess a trial induced (image) charge $q'$ at a point $\vec r\,'$, again guided by symmetry.
3. Compute the resulting field and fix the unknowns by one of these conditions: (a) the total potential is constant on each conductor; or (b) it is constant at least at two convenient points; or (c) the total field is perpendicular to the conductors at their surfaces.

(The notes list (b) as an alternative. This caveat is ours: two points only fix the unknowns, and the guess still has to satisfy (a) everywhere on the conductor, as the check at every angle did for the sphere.) This, the notes conclude, is the method of images.

## Summary

- A charge $Q$ at height $d$ above a grounded plane: above the plane, the potential is that of $Q$ and an **image charge** $-Q$ at depth $d$. Below the plane, in the metal, the real field is zero.
- From the potential: $\sigma = -\varepsilon_0\,\partial V/\partial z = -Qd/[2\pi(s^2 + d^2)^{3/2}]$, with total $-Q$ (known in advance from Gauss's law), and an attraction $Q^2/[4\pi\varepsilon_0(2d)^2]$ towards the plane, computed without the charge's own field.
- The energy, $\frac12\int\rho V\,d^3x$ without self-potentials, is $-Q^2/(16\pi\varepsilon_0 d)$: half that of a real pair, because only one charge has to be moved in.
- Two planes at right angles need three images, at the other corners of a rectangle with alternating signs. A grounded sphere of radius $a$ with $Q$ at distance $R$ needs one: $q = -aQ/R$ at $a^2/R$ from the centre.
- Any equipotential of any set of charges defines an image problem. The **uniqueness theorem** is the licence to guess: a potential that satisfies Poisson's equation and every boundary condition is the only one.
