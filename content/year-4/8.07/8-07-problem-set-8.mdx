This set practises magnetostatics and its first step into matter: the [Biot–Savart law](/courses/8-02/biot-savart-law), the magnetic dipole of [Lecture Notes 10](/courses/8-07/magnetic-multipole-expansion-dipoles) and [Lecture Notes 11](/courses/8-07/ideal-magnetic-dipole-current), the [force on a dipole](/courses/8-07/magnetic-dipole-force-torque-maxwell), and the magnetization $\vec M$, the bound currents and the field $\vec H$, which the last pages of [Lecture Notes 10](/courses/8-07/magnetic-multipole-expansion-dipoles#magnetization-and-bound-currents) introduce. The introductory [8.02 lesson on magnetic materials](/courses/8-02/transformers-and-magnetic-materials) gives the physical picture. The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 8 (the sheet of November 4, 2012), in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `mu0*I*a/(4*pi*z^2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `pi` stands for $\pi$, `mu0` for $\mu_0$, `M0` for $M_0$ and `chi` for $\chi_m$; the other letters (`a`, `b`, `I`, `J`, `L`, `m`, `R`, `s`, `W`, `x`, `z`) are typed as they are.

MIT gives the points per problem: 15 for Problem 2, 10 for Problem 3, 15 for Problem 4, 20 for Problem 5 and 15 each for Problems 6 and 7, 90 points in all (MIT's sheet has 115, with the 25 of its Problem 1). The splits between parts are ours: MIT gives no points for parts. Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes. $\vec B$ is the magnetic field, $\mu_0$ the magnetic constant, $\vec J$ a current density (current per unit area) and $\vec K$ a surface current (current per unit length across the direction of flow). $\vec m$ is a magnetic dipole moment, $\vec M$ the magnetization (dipole moment per unit volume) and $\vec H$ the auxiliary field. In cylindrical coordinates $s$ is the distance from the $z$ axis, $\hat s$ points away from it and $\hat\phi$ around it, counterclockwise as seen from the positive $z$ direction.

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A square current loop on its axis: the Biot–Savart law and the dipole approximation (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Square current loop on axis: Biot–Savart and the magnetic dipole approximation". The problem below is our own, on the same subject.

A square loop of wire with sides of length $a$ lies in the $xy$ plane, centred on the origin, with its sides parallel to the axes. It carries the steady current $I$, counterclockwise as seen from the positive $z$ direction. Each piece $d\vec\ell$ of the wire makes the field

<Formula label="The Biot–Savart law" tex="d\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec\ell \times \hat u}{u^2}" cite="mit802-ch9" at="Eq. (9.1.1), p. 2" />

where $\vec u$ is the vector from the piece of wire to the point where the field is wanted, $u$ its length and $\hat u$ its direction.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find the magnetic field at the point $(0, 0, z)$ on the axis of the loop. Give its $z$ component.

<Hint>

Take one side at a time. The perpendicular from the field point to a side meets it at its middle, at the distance $s = \sqrt{z^2 + a^2/4}$. [Problem 2(a) of our Set 7](/courses/8-07/8-07-problem-set-7#p2) gives the field of a straight piece of length $L$ at the distance $s$ from its middle.

</Hint>

<Hint>

By symmetry the four sides give fields of the same size. Their components across the axis cancel in pairs (opposite sides), so only the $z$ components add. What fraction of each side's field points along $z$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-axis" label="$B_z(z) =$" expr="mu0*I*a^2/(2*pi*(z^2 + a^2/4)*sqrt(z^2 + a^2/2))"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], I: [1, 3], a: [0.5, 2], z: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", I: "I", a: "a", z: "z" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*I*a/(pi*sqrt(z^2 + a^2/4)*sqrt(z^2 + a^2/2))", hint: "That is four times the size of one side's field. The four fields point in four different directions: their parts across the axis cancel, and only the z part of each, a fraction (a/2)/√(z² + a²/4) of it, adds up." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The loop is four straight pieces. Each gives a field of known size; by symmetry only the parts along the axis survive the sum.

<Step title="One side">

Take the side at $x = a/2$, running from $y = -a/2$ to $y = a/2$. The perpendicular from $(0, 0, z)$ meets it at its middle $(a/2, 0, 0)$, at the distance $s = \sqrt{z^2 + a^2/4}$. The field of a straight piece of length $L = a$ seen from the distance $s$ opposite its middle (our Set 7, Problem 2(a)) has the size

<Formula label="The field of one side" tex="B_{\text{side}} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi s}\,\frac{a}{\sqrt{s^2 + a^2/4}} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\frac{a}{\sqrt{z^2 + a^2/4}\,\sqrt{z^2 + a^2/2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

and points at right angles both to the side and to the perpendicular from the side to the field point.

</Step>

<Step title="The axial part">

That direction makes the angle with the $z$ axis whose cosine is $(a/2)/s$: the field is tilted away from the axis exactly as the perpendicular is tilted away from the plane of the loop. The four sides give equal axial parts and cancelling sideways parts, so

<Formula label="The field on the axis of a square loop" tex="B_z(z) = 4\,B_{\text{side}}\,\frac{a/2}{s} = \frac{\mu_0 I a^2}{2\pi\left(z^2 + a^2/4\right)\sqrt{z^2 + a^2/2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

It points along $+z$ for a counterclockwise current, by the right-hand rule.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At the centre, $z = 0$, it gives $\mu_0 I a^2/(2\pi \cdot \tfrac{a^2}{4} \cdot \tfrac{a}{\sqrt 2}) = 2\sqrt2\,\mu_0 I/(\pi a) = 0.900\,\mu_0 I/a$. A circular loop through the corners (radius $a/\sqrt2$) gives $\mu_0 I/(2 \cdot a/\sqrt2) = 0.707\,\mu_0 I/a$, and one touching the middles of the sides (radius $a/2$) gives $\mu_0 I/a$. The square lies between the two circles, and so does its field.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the leading term of $B_z$ far from the loop, $z \gg a$. The magnetic dipole moment of a flat loop is the current times its vector area, so here $\vec m = Ia^2\,\hat z$. Show that the leading term is the field of this dipole on its axis.

<Hint>

For $z \gg a$ drop $a^2/4$ and $a^2/2$ next to $z^2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the axis, $\hat r = \hat z$ and $\vec m \cdot \hat r = m$. Put that into the field of a dipole.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-far" label="For $z \gg a$, $B_z \approx$" expr="mu0*I*a^2/(2*pi*z^3)"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], I: [1, 3], a: [0.5, 2], z: [3, 6] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", I: "I", a: "a", z: "z" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*I*a^2/(4*pi*z^3)", hint: "That is half. On the axis the dipole's 3(m · r̂)r̂ − m is 3m − m = 2m, not m; the size μ₀m/(4πr³) belongs to the equatorial plane." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Far away, the details of the shape are lost; only the dipole moment is left.

<Step title="The limit">

For $z \gg a$, $z^2 + a^2/4 \approx z^2$ and $\sqrt{z^2 + a^2/2} \approx z$, so

<Formula label="The far field on the axis" tex="B_z \approx \frac{\mu_0 I a^2}{2\pi z^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The dipole">

The moment of a loop is $\vec m = I\vec a$, with $\vec a$ its vector area; for the square $\vec m = Ia^2\,\hat z$ (Lecture Notes 11, which uses the same square loop):

<Formula label="The moment of the square loop" tex="\vec m = I\,\vec a = I a^2\,\hat z" cite="reading2" at="slide 9" />

The field of a dipole away from the origin is

<Formula label="The field of a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec B_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec m}{r^3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7 (boxed, without its delta-function term, which is zero away from the origin)" />

On the positive $z$ axis $\hat r = \hat z$, so $3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec m = 2m\,\hat z$ and $B_z = \mu_0 \cdot 2Ia^2/(4\pi z^3) = \mu_0 I a^2/(2\pi z^3)$: the leading term above.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units are those of $\mu_0 I/a$ times $(a/z)^3$, a field. The far field depends on $a$ only through the area $a^2$: any flat loop of the same area and current has the same far field.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

How good is the dipole approximation at $z = 2a$? Give the exact field of part (a) divided by the dipole field of part (b), at $z = 2a$, as a decimal.

<Hint>

Write both with $z = 2a$: the constants cancel, and the ratio depends on no other quantity.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ratio" label="$B_z/B_{\text{dip}}$ at $z = 2a$:" value="0.887" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Divide the two results; all the constants cancel.

<Step title="The ratio">

<Formula label="Exact over dipole" tex="\frac{B_z}{B_{\text{dip}}} = \frac{z^3}{\left(z^2 + a^2/4\right)\sqrt{z^2 + a^2/2}} = \frac{8}{4.25 \times \sqrt{4.5}} = 0.887" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own numbers)" />

At two side lengths the dipole formula is still 13% too large.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For large $z$ the ratio is $\left(1 + \tfrac{a^2}{4z^2}\right)^{-1}\left(1 + \tfrac{a^2}{2z^2}\right)^{-1/2} \approx 1 - \tfrac{a^2}{2z^2}$, which gives $0.875$ at $z = 2a$, close to the exact $0.887$. By this estimate the error falls below 1% only beyond $z = \sqrt{50}\,a \approx 7.1a$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Current loops and Newton's third law (our own problem)" points={10} mit="Problem 3">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Current loops and Newton's third law". The problem below is our own, on the same subject. MIT adds a hint of its own, which we keep (in part (a)): the identity

<Formula label="MIT's hint: the gradient of one over the distance" tex="\frac{\vec r - \vec r\,'}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|^3} = -\vec\nabla_{\vec r}\,\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1" />

where the gradient acts on $\vec r$, with $\vec r\,'$ held fixed.

Two closed loops of wire carry the steady currents $I_1$ and $I_2$. A piece $d\vec\ell_1$ of loop 1 sits at $\vec r_1$ and a piece $d\vec\ell_2$ of loop 2 at $\vec r_2$; let $\vec R = \vec r_2 - \vec r_1$ and $R = |\vec R|$. A piece of wire in a field feels the force

<Formula label="The force on a piece of wire" tex="d\vec F = I\,d\vec\ell \times \vec B" cite="reading2" at="slide 15 (boxed)" />

and the field of loop 1 comes from the Biot–Savart law (as in Problem 2), so the force of loop 1 on loop 2 is

<Formula label="The force of loop 1 on loop 2" tex="\vec F_{2} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,I_1 I_2\oint_2\oint_1\frac{d\vec\ell_2 \times \left(d\vec\ell_1 \times \vec R\right)}{R^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own statement, from the two laws above)" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Expand the double cross product and show that one of the two terms integrates to zero, so that

<Formula label="The force in a symmetric form" tex="\vec F_{2} = -\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,I_1 I_2\oint_2\oint_1\left(d\vec\ell_1 \cdot d\vec\ell_2\right)\frac{\vec R}{R^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own statement)" />

Then answer the question in the box: why does the vanishing term vanish?

<Hint>

Use the rule for a double cross product (often called BAC minus CAB). In the first term, do the integral over loop 2 first, holding $\vec r_1$ fixed.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use MIT's identity with $\vec r = \vec r_2$ and $\vec r\,' = \vec r_1$. For a function of $\vec r_2$, what does $d\vec\ell_2 \cdot \vec\nabla_{\vec r_2}$ applied to it measure?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-closed" label="The integral of $d\vec\ell_2 \cdot \vec\nabla_{\vec r_2}(1/R)$ around loop 2 is zero because" choices={["∇₂(1/R) is perpendicular to dℓ₂ all along loop 2", "Stokes' theorem on loop 1 turns it into a surface integral of zero", "it adds up the changes of 1/R once around a closed path", "1/R takes the same value at every point of loop 2, for each dℓ₁"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One term is a gradient integrated around a closed loop, which gives nothing. The other is already symmetric in the two loops.

<Step title="Expand">

With the rule above, $d\vec\ell_2 \times (d\vec\ell_1 \times \vec R) = d\vec\ell_1\,(d\vec\ell_2 \cdot \vec R) - \vec R\,(d\vec\ell_2 \cdot d\vec\ell_1)$. The second term, divided by $R^3$, is the symmetric form above. The first is

<Formula label="The first term" tex="\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,I_1 I_2\oint_1 d\vec\ell_1\left[\oint_2 d\vec\ell_2 \cdot \frac{\vec R}{R^3}\right] = -\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,I_1 I_2\oint_1 d\vec\ell_1\left[\oint_2 d\vec\ell_2 \cdot \vec\nabla_{\vec r_2}\frac{1}{R}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution, with MIT's identity)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The closed loop">

For fixed $\vec r_1$, $d\vec\ell_2 \cdot \vec\nabla_{\vec r_2}(1/R)$ is the change of the single-valued function $1/R$ along the piece $d\vec\ell_2$. Around a closed loop these changes add up to the value at the end minus the value at the start, which are the same point: zero. (The loops do not touch, so $1/R$ is finite and smooth all along loop 2.) The first term vanishes, and the symmetric form is left.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units: $\mu_0 I_1 I_2$ times length squared over length squared is $\mu_0 I^2$, in newtons, a force.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Show that the force of loop 2 on loop 1 is $\vec F_1 = -\vec F_2$: Newton's third law holds for closed loops.

<Hint>

Write the force on loop 1 by the same steps, with the labels 1 and 2 swapped. What becomes of $\vec R$, and of $d\vec\ell_1 \cdot d\vec\ell_2$?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The symmetric form treats the two loops alike, except for the direction of $\vec R$.

<Step title="Swap the labels">

The force on loop 1 is the symmetric form with 1 and 2 exchanged. The dot product $d\vec\ell_1 \cdot d\vec\ell_2$ is unchanged, the distance $R$ too, but the vector from loop 2 to loop 1 is $\vec r_1 - \vec r_2 = -\vec R$. So the integrand changes sign at every pair of points, and

<Formula label="Newton's third law for two loops" tex="\vec F_{1} = -\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,I_1 I_2\oint_1\oint_2\left(d\vec\ell_1 \cdot d\vec\ell_2\right)\frac{-\vec R}{R^3} = -\vec F_{2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The order of the two integrals does not matter: each is over a fixed closed curve.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Two parallel, coaxial rings with currents in the same direction: $d\vec\ell_1 \cdot d\vec\ell_2 > 0$ for nearby pieces, and the force on loop 2 points along $-\vec R$, towards loop 1. Parallel currents attract, as they should, and loop 1 is pulled towards loop 2 equally.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

(Our addition.) The step that removed the first term needed a closed loop 2. Try two single pieces of wire instead. A short piece $d\vec\ell_1 = d\ell_1\,\hat x$ (current $I_1$) sits at the origin, and a short piece $d\vec\ell_2 = d\ell_2\,\hat y$ (current $I_2$) at the point $(d, 0, 0)$. Use the force law of the problem's statement for each piece, without the integrals. What is the force on piece 2 from piece 1?

<Hint>

For piece 2 the vector from piece 1 is $\vec R = d\,\hat x$. What is $d\vec\ell_1 \times \vec R$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For piece 1 the vector from piece 2 is $-d\,\hat x$; work out $d\vec\ell_2 \times (-\hat x)$ and then the cross product with $d\vec\ell_1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-elem" label="The force on piece 2 from piece 1 is" choices={["equal in size and opposite to the force on piece 1", "zero, while piece 1 feels a force along −y", "along +y, towards the line of piece 1", "along −x, pulling the two pieces together"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For pieces of wire the first term of part (a) does not cancel, and the two forces need not be equal and opposite.

<Step title="The force on piece 2">

The field of piece 1 at piece 2 is proportional to $d\vec\ell_1 \times \hat x = d\ell_1\,(\hat x \times \hat x) = 0$: a piece of wire makes no field along its own line. So piece 2 feels no force.

</Step>

<Step title="The force on piece 1">

The field of piece 2 at the origin is proportional to $d\vec\ell_2 \times (-\hat x) = d\ell_2\,(\hat y \times (-\hat x)) = d\ell_2\,\hat z$, so

<Formula label="The force on piece 1" tex="d\vec F_1 = I_1\,d\ell_1\,\hat x \times \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I_2\,d\ell_2\,\hat z}{d^2} = -\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I_1 I_2\,d\ell_1\,d\ell_2}{d^2}\,\hat y" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two forces are not equal and opposite. There is no contradiction: steady currents in isolated pieces of wire are impossible (charge would pile up at their ends), so the law for pieces only makes sense when they are summed into closed loops, and then part (b) restores the third law.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="An infinite slab of current and the force on a magnetic dipole (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 4">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "An infinite slab of current and the force on a magnetic dipole". The problem below is our own, on the same subject.

A slab of conducting material fills the region $-b \le z \le b$, infinite in $x$ and $y$. It carries a uniform current density $\vec J = J\,\hat x$, with $J > 0$. In magnetostatics the field obeys

<Formula label="Magnetostatics" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0, \qquad \nabla \times \vec B = \mu_0\vec J" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

Lecture Notes 11 derive the force on a magnetic dipole of moment $\vec m$ in a field made by currents elsewhere:

<Formula label="The force on a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec F = \nabla\left(\vec m \cdot \vec B\right)" cite="reading2" at="slide 16 (boxed)" />

with $\vec m$ held fixed while the gradient is taken. Where $\nabla \times \vec B = 0$ this equals $(\vec m \cdot \nabla)\vec B$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

By symmetry the field points along $\pm\hat y$, depends only on $z$, and is odd in $z$: $B_y(-z) = -B_y(z)$. Find $B_y$ inside the slab and above it.

<Hint>

Write out the $x$ component of $\nabla \times \vec B$ for a field $B_y(z)\,\hat y$. It involves only $dB_y/dz$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate from $z = 0$, where the odd field vanishes. Above the slab no current flows, so the field there is constant.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-in" label="Inside, $|z| < b$: $B_y =$" expr="-mu0*J*z"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], J: [0.5, 2], z: [-1, 1] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", J: "J", z: "z" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*J*z", hint: "Check the sign with the right-hand rule: above a sheet of current flowing along +x, the field points along −y (the curl's x component is −dB_y/dz)." },
    { expr: "-mu0*J*z/2", hint: "That is half. With an Ampère loop from height −z to z, both long sides give B ℓ, but the loop encloses the current of the whole layer between −z and z, 2Jzℓ." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p4-out" label="Above, $z > b$: $B_y =$" expr="-mu0*J*b"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], J: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", J: "J", b: "b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-mu0*J*b/2", hint: "That is the field of a sheet μ₀K/2 with K = Jb. The current per unit width of the whole slab is K = 2Jb, since the slab is 2b thick." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With one component depending on one coordinate, the curl equation is an ordinary differential equation.

<Step title="Inside">

For $\vec B = B_y(z)\,\hat y$, $(\nabla \times \vec B)_x = \partial_y B_z - \partial_z B_y = -dB_y/dz$. Inside the slab this equals $\mu_0 J$, and with $B_y(0) = 0$,

<Formula label="The field inside the slab" tex="B_y(z) = -\mu_0 J z \qquad (|z| \le b)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Outside">

Above the slab $dB_y/dz = 0$, and the field is continuous at $z = b$ (no surface current there), so $B_y = -\mu_0 J b$ for $z > b$ and $+\mu_0 J b$ for $z < -b$. The divergence $\partial_y B_y = 0$ everywhere, as it must be.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Seen from outside, the slab is a sheet with the current per unit width $K = 2Jb$, whose field has the size $\mu_0 K/2 = \mu_0 J b$. Above a current along $+x$ it points along $\hat x \times \hat z = -\hat y$, as found.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-f" label="For $\vec m = m\,\hat y$: $F_z =$" expr="-mu0*J*m"

<Answer id="p4-mz" label="For $\vec m = m\,\hat z$ inside the slab," choices={["both are zero, since B has no z component", "both equal −μ₀Jm ŷ", "∇(m · B) = −μ₀Jm ẑ, while (m · ∇)B = 0", "∇(m · B) = 0, while (m · ∇)B = −μ₀Jm ŷ"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The two expressions differ by $\vec m \times (\nabla \times \vec B)$, which is not zero where current flows.

<Step title="Moment along y">

$\vec m \cdot \vec B = m B_y = -\mu_0 J m z$, so $\vec F = \nabla(\vec m \cdot \vec B) = -\mu_0 J m\,\hat z$. For the same moment, $(\vec m \cdot \nabla)\vec B = m\,\partial_y\vec B = 0$, because $\vec B$ does not depend on $y$.

</Step>

<Step title="Moment along z">

Now $\vec m \cdot \vec B = 0$ everywhere, so $\nabla(\vec m \cdot \vec B) = 0$; but $(\vec m \cdot \nabla)\vec B = m\,\partial_z\vec B = -\mu_0 J m\,\hat y$. The two expressions trade places.

</Step>

<Step title="Why they differ">

For a fixed vector $\vec m$, $\nabla(\vec m \cdot \vec B) = (\vec m \cdot \nabla)\vec B + \vec m \times (\nabla \times \vec B)$. Here $\vec m \times (\nabla \times \vec B) = \mu_0\,\vec m \times \vec J$: for $\vec m = m\hat y$ it is $\mu_0 m J\,(\hat y \times \hat x) = -\mu_0 J m\,\hat z$, and for $\vec m = m\hat z$ it is $\mu_0 m J\,(\hat z \times \hat x) = \mu_0 J m\,\hat y$. Both match the differences found above. Outside the slab, where $\vec J = 0$, the two expressions agree (and here both vanish, since the field is uniform).

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Which expression gives the force on a small current loop, the kind of dipole the lecture notes use?

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

<Answer id="p4-loop" label="For a small current loop inside the slab, the force is" choices={["∇(m · B): the derivation for a current loop uses only ∇ · B = 0", "(m · ∇)B: the two forms agree wherever B is a magnetic field", "(m · ∇)B, because the curl of B does not vanish inside the slab", "the average of the two forms, since the loop straddles the current"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The force on a current loop is $\int\vec J \times \vec B$; working it out for a point dipole needs only $\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0$, never $\nabla \times \vec B = 0$.

<Step title="The derivation">

In Lecture Notes 11 the force is $F_i = \int[m_i B_j\,\partial_j\delta^3 - \partial_i\delta^3\,m_j B_j]\,d^3x$. Integrating by parts gives $-m_i\,\partial_j B_j + \partial_i(m_j B_j)$ at the dipole. The first term is zero by $\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0$, which holds inside the slab too. So $\vec F = \nabla(\vec m \cdot \vec B)$ for a current loop, wherever it is.

</Step>

<Step title="A square loop">

Take a square of side $\ell$ in the $xz$ plane, centred at height $z_0$, with $\vec m = I\ell^2\,\hat y$. The field differs between the two sides by $-\mu_0 J\ell$, so the net force is $-\mu_0 J I\ell^2\,\hat z = -\mu_0 J m\,\hat z$: the result of $\nabla(\vec m \cdot \vec B)$ in part (b), and not the zero of $(\vec m \cdot \nabla)\vec B$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$(\vec m \cdot \nabla)\vec B$ is the force on a dipole made of two magnetic charges $\pm q_m$ a small distance apart, if a magnetic charge feels the force $q_m\vec B$. The dipoles of the lecture notes are built from currents, so for them $\nabla(\vec m \cdot \vec B)$ is the right choice.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Step title="The volume current">

$M_\phi = \chi_m I s/(2\pi R^2)$, so $s M_\phi = \chi_m I s^2/(2\pi R^2)$ and

<Formula label="The bound volume current" tex="\vec J_b = \nabla \times \vec M = \frac1s\,\frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\chi_m I s^2}{2\pi R^2}\right)\hat z = \frac{\chi_m I}{\pi R^2}\,\hat z" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

It is uniform and flows with the free current for $\chi_m > 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The surface current">

At $s = R$, $\vec M = \chi_m I/(2\pi R)\,\hat\phi$ and $\hat n = \hat s$:

<Formula label="The bound surface current" tex="\vec K_b = \vec M \times \hat n = \frac{\chi_m I}{2\pi R}\,\left(\hat\phi \times \hat s\right) = -\frac{\chi_m I}{2\pi R}\,\hat z" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The jump of $B_\phi$ across a surface current is $\mu_0$ times its size; the field jumps downwards going out, as for a current along $-z$. Part (a) found the jump $\mu_0\chi_m I/(2\pi R)$, which is $\mu_0|K_b|$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Show that the total bound current through a cross-section of the wire is zero, and explain with it why the field outside does not depend on $\chi_m$.

<Hint>

Add $J_{b,z}$ times the area of the cross-section to $K_{b,z}$ times the circumference.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Bound currents come from little loops in the material; they cannot carry a net current along the wire.

<Step title="The sum">

$J_{b,z}\,\pi R^2 + K_{b,z}\,2\pi R = \chi_m I - \chi_m I = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The field outside">

A circle of radius $s > R$ encloses the free current $I$ plus a bound current of zero, so Ampère's law for $\vec B$, with all currents counted, gives $B_\phi = \mu_0 I/(2\pi s)$ for every $\chi_m$. The material only redistributes current within the wire: more along the axis, a return sheet on the surface.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a diamagnetic material ($\chi_m < 0$) the signs reverse: the volume current opposes the free current and the surface current flows with it. Their sum is still zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="Ring magnets floating on a vertical rod (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 7">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Donut-shaped magnets on a vertical rod". The problem below is our own, on the same subject.

Identical ring magnets slide without friction on a vertical rod of non-magnetic material. Each is magnetized along the rod, and neighbouring magnets are turned so that they repel, so each floats above the one below it. Treat each magnet as a point magnetic dipole of moment $m$ on the axis (a rough model when the gaps are not much larger than the rings), with weight $W$. The energy of a dipole in a field is

<Formula label="The energy of a magnetic dipole" tex="U = -\vec m \cdot \vec B" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

and the field of a dipole is the one used in Problem 2(b).

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Two of the dipoles sit on the axis a distance $x$ apart, with opposite moments $m\,\hat z$ and $-m\,\hat z$. Find the size of the repulsive force between them.

<Hint>

On the axis the field of the lower dipole at the distance $x$ is along the axis. Find it, then the energy of the upper dipole in it, as a function of $x$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The force on the upper dipole is $-dU/dx$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-f" label="$F =$" expr="3*mu0*m^2/(2*pi*x^4)"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], m: [0.5, 2], x: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", m: "m", x: "x" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*mu0*m^2/(4*pi*x^4)", hint: "That is half. On the axis the dipole field is (μ₀/4π) 2m/x³; the size μ₀m/(4πx³) holds only in the equatorial plane." },
    { expr: "mu0*m^2/(2*pi*x^3)", hint: "That is the size of the energy, not the force. Differentiate it with respect to x." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the axis the field and the moment are both along $z$, so the energy is a function of $x$ alone, and the force is its slope.

<Step title="The energy">

The lower dipole $m\,\hat z$ makes the field $B_z = (\mu_0/4\pi)\,2m/x^3$ at the distance $x$ above it on the axis (Problem 2(b)). The upper dipole $-m\,\hat z$ has the energy

<Formula label="The energy of the upper dipole" tex="U(x) = -\left(-m\right)\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{2m}{x^3} = \frac{\mu_0 m^2}{2\pi x^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The force">

<Formula label="The force between two coaxial dipoles" tex="F = -\frac{dU}{dx} = \frac{3\mu_0 m^2}{2\pi x^4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

It is positive: the upper dipole is pushed up, away from the lower one.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The same follows from $\vec F = \nabla(\vec m \cdot \vec B)$: $F_z = -m\,\partial_z B_z = -m\,(\mu_0/4\pi)(-6m/x^4) = 3\mu_0 m^2/(2\pi x^4)$. Units: $\mu_0 m^2/x^4$ is (N/A²)(A² m⁴)/m⁴ = N.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Two magnets: the lower one rests on a table, the upper one floats above it. Find the gap $x$. Then give it for $m = 0.80$ A m² and a mass of 20.0 g ($g = 9.81$ m/s²), in cm.

<Hint>

The upper magnet floats where the repulsion of part (a) equals its weight.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-x" label="$x =$" expr="(3*mu0*m^2/(2*pi*W))^(1/4)"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], m: [0.5, 2], W: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", m: "m", W: "W" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(3*mu0*m^2/(2*pi*W))^(1/3)", hint: "The force falls off as the fourth power of the distance, so solving for x takes a fourth root." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p7-xnum" label="The gap in cm:" value="3.74" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-xnum" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Balance the magnetic force against the weight.

<Step title="The gap">

$3\mu_0 m^2/(2\pi x^4) = W$ gives

<Formula label="The gap above one magnet" tex="x = \left(\frac{3\mu_0 m^2}{2\pi W}\right)^{1/4}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$W = 0.0200 \times 9.81 = 0.196$ N, and $3\mu_0 m^2/(2\pi W) = 3 \times 1.257\times10^{-6} \times 0.64/(2\pi \times 0.196) = 1.96\times10^{-6}$ m⁴. Its fourth root is $x = 0.0374$ m $= 3.74$ cm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The gap grows only as $m^{1/2}$ and $W^{-1/4}$: doubling the weight lowers the magnet by a factor $2^{-1/4} = 0.84$, by 16%. The floating position is stable along the rod: pushed down, the magnet feels more repulsion than its weight, and pulled up, less.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Now three magnets: the bottom one on the table, the middle one a gap $x_2$ above it, the top one a gap $x_1$ above the middle one. Take into account only the forces between neighbours. Find the ratio $x_2/x_1$, as a decimal.

<Hint>

The top magnet carries only its own weight. The middle one is pushed up by the bottom magnet and down by the top magnet; what must the push from below balance?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-ratio" label="$x_2/x_1 =$" value="0.841" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each gap holds up the weight of everything above it.

<Step title="The two balances">

Top magnet: $F(x_1) = W$. Middle magnet: $F(x_2) - F(x_1) = W$, so $F(x_2) = 2W$. With $F \propto 1/x^4$, $(x_1/x_2)^4 = 2$ and

<Formula label="The ratio of the gaps, neighbours only" tex="\frac{x_2}{x_1} = 2^{-1/4} = 0.841" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For the magnets of part (b), $x_1 = 3.74$ cm and $x_2 = 3.15$ cm: the lower gap is the smaller one, because it holds up two magnets.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Now include the force between the top and the bottom magnet too. They point the same way (the middle one points the opposite way), so these two attract. For the magnets of part (b), find the top gap $x_1$ in cm. Solve the two balance equations numerically, or by iteration starting from part (c).

<Hint>

The top and bottom moments are parallel, so the force between them, at the distance $x_1 + x_2$, has the size of part (a) but pulls them together. It acts on the top magnet and the bottom one, not on the middle one.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Top: $F(x_1) - F(x_1 + x_2) = W$. Middle: $F(x_2) - F(x_1) = W$. Start with the gaps of part (c) and correct them in turn.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-x1" label="$x_1$ in cm:" value="3.66" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-x1" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The far pair is a small correction, a few per cent of the force between neighbours.

<Step title="The equations">

Top magnet: up $F(x_1)$ from the middle one, down $F(x_1 + x_2)$ from the bottom one (parallel moments attract) and its weight: $F(x_1) - F(x_1 + x_2) = W$. Middle magnet: $F(x_2) - F(x_1) = W$, unchanged.

</Step>

<Step title="The solution">

With $x_1 = 3.74$ cm and $x_2 = 3.15$ cm from part (c), $F(x_1 + x_2)/F(x_1) = (3.74/6.89)^4 = 0.087$. The top magnet now needs more push from below, so $x_1$ shrinks; the middle balance then needs a larger $F(x_2)$, so $x_2$ shrinks too. Solving the two equations numerically gives $x_1 = 3.66$ cm and $x_2 = 3.11$ cm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Put back: the top balance and the middle balance hold to better than 0.1% with these values. The top gap shrinks by 2% and the lower one by 1%, much less than the 9% of the extra force, because the gaps go as the fourth root of the forces.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
