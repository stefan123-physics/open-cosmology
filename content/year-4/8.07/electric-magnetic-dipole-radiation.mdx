The [Liénard–Wiechert page](/courses/8-07/lienard-wiechert-point-charge-fields) ended with fields that fall off only as one over the distance. Guth's slides now follow those fields out to great distances, where they become **radiation**. This page covers slides 1–13: an oscillating electric dipole, the shape of its radiation, the blue sky, and an oscillating magnetic dipole. The [second page](/courses/8-07/larmor-formula-radiation-reaction) treats a source of any shape, a single accelerated charge and the force that radiation exerts back on it. The total power, slide 11, is moved forward to sit with the intensity, before the blue-sky slides 9 and 10.)

## What radiation is

Guth defines **radiation** as electromagnetic fields that carry energy off to infinity (slide 1). (Our contrast: static fields do not, since the Coulomb field falls as $1/r^2$, so the Poynting vector $\vec S = \vec E \times \vec B/\mu_0$ falls at least as fast as $1/r^4$ and its flux through a large sphere goes to zero.) If at large distances $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ fall off only as $1/r$, the Poynting vector falls as $1/r^2$. Integrated over a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$, the outgoing power then approaches a constant as $r \to \infty$: energy really leaves for good (slide 1).

The previous notes found such $1/r$ fields: the acceleration term of a moving charge's field. Guth recalls the Liénard–Wiechert potentials of a point charge $q$ (slide 2):

<Formula label="The Liénard–Wiechert potentials" tex="V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|\left(1 - \frac{\vec v_p}{c} \cdot \frac{\vec r - \vec r_p}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|}\right)}, \qquad \vec A(\vec r, t) = \frac{\vec v_p}{c^2}\,V(\vec r, t)" cite="reading1" at="slide 2, eqs. (1)–(2)" />

where $\vec r_p$ and $\vec v_p$ are the particle's position and velocity at the retarded time (slide 2):

<Formula label="The retarded time" tex="t_r = t - \frac{|\vec r - \vec r_p|}{c}" cite="reading1" at="slide 2, eqs. (3)–(4)" />

## The simplest dipole

Guth's first source is the simplest oscillating dipole: two tiny metal spheres a distance $d$ apart on the $z$ axis, joined by a wire, with charge sloshing between them (slide 3). The top sphere carries

<Formula label="The charge on the top sphere" tex="q(t) = q_0\cos(\omega t)" cite="reading1" at="slide 3, eq. (5)" />

and the bottom sphere $-q_0\cos(\omega t)$. Put the origin midway between them and let $r_+$ and $r_-$ be the distances from the top and bottom spheres to the field point, which is at distance $r$ from the origin and at angle $\theta$ from the $z$ axis. Each sphere contributes a Coulomb potential evaluated at its own retarded time (slide 3):

<Formula label="The potential of the two spheres" tex="V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left\{\frac{q_0\cos[\omega(t - r_+/c)]}{r_+} - \frac{q_0\cos[\omega(t - r_-/c)]}{r_-}\right\}" cite="reading1" at="slide 3, eq. (6)" />

(The spheres are at rest, so the Liénard–Wiechert factor is 1 here; only the retardation matters.)

## Three approximations

The exact expression is awkward, and Guth simplifies it in three steps, each one valid for radiation.

**Approximation 1: $d \ll r$** (slide 4). By the law of cosines the distances are (slide 4)

<Formula label="The distances to the two spheres" tex="r_\pm = \sqrt{r^2 \mp rd\cos\theta + (d/2)^2}" cite="reading1" at="slide 4" />

and to first order in $d/r$ (slide 4)

<Formula label="First order in d over r" tex="\frac{1}{r_\pm} \approx \frac{1}{r}\left(1 \pm \frac{d}{2r}\cos\theta\right), \qquad \cos[\omega(t - r_\pm/c)] \approx \cos\left[\omega(t - r/c) \pm \frac{\omega d}{2c}\cos\theta\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 4, eq. (7)" />

This approximation always holds for radiation, which is defined by the limit $r \to \infty$ (slide 4).

**Approximation 2: $d \ll c/\omega$** (slide 4). Since the wavelength is $\lambda = 2\pi c/\omega$, this is the same as $d \ll \lambda$, the **ideal dipole approximation**. The two spheres are then seen at almost the same phase of the oscillation. Guth notes that it is really the first term of an expansion in powers of $d/\lambda$, the multipole expansion for radiation, and that the course goes no further than the dipole (slide 4). The small phase shift $\pm(\omega d/2c)\cos\theta$ can now be expanded (slide 4):

<Formula label="Expanding the small phase shift" tex="\cos[\omega(t - r_\pm/c)] \approx \cos[\omega(t - r/c)] \mp \frac{\omega d}{2c}\cos\theta\,\sin[\omega(t - r/c)]" cite="reading1" at="slide 4, eq. (8)" />

Putting both expansions into (6) and keeping first order in $d$, with the dipole moment amplitude $p_0 = q_0d$, gives (slide 5)

<Formula label="The potential of an ideal oscillating dipole" tex="V(r, \theta, t) = \frac{p_0\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r}\left\{\frac{1}{r}\cos[\omega(t - r/c)] - \frac{\omega}{c}\sin[\omega(t - r/c)]\right\}" cite="reading1" at="slide 5, eq. (9)" />

As $\omega \to 0$ the first term is the potential of a static dipole, $p_0\cos\theta/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$.

**Approximation 3: $r \gg \lambda$** (slide 5). The region far beyond a wavelength is the **radiation zone**, and again this always holds when discussing radiation. The second term in the braces is larger than the first by $\omega r/c = 2\pi r/\lambda$, so the first can be dropped (slide 5):

<Formula label="The scalar potential in the radiation zone" tex="V(r, \theta, t) = -\frac{p_0\omega}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c}\left(\frac{\cos\theta}{r}\right)\sin[\omega(t - r/c)]" cite="reading1" at="slide 5, eq. (10) (boxed)" />

In short: $d \ll \lambda \ll r$ (slide 5).

## The vector potential and the fields

The current in the wire also produces a vector potential, given by the retarded integral (slide 6)

<Formula label="The retarded vector potential" tex="\vec A(\vec r, t) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\vec J(\vec r\,', t_r)}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}" cite="reading1" at="slide 6, eq. (11)" />

[The slide has $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ in front, a slip: the factor for the vector potential is $\mu_0/4\pi$, as the slide's own next result uses. In (13) the slide's typesetting also drops or displaces the $\pi$ of $4\pi$.] For a thin wire the current element is (slide 6)

<Formula label="The current element of the wire" tex="d^3x'\,\vec J = I\,d\vec\ell = \frac{dq}{dt}\,dz\,\hat z" cite="reading1" at="slide 6, eq. (12)" />

with $dq/dt = -q_0\omega\sin(\omega t)$. The wire runs from $z = -d/2$ to $z = d/2$, and to lowest order in $d$ the distance and the retarded time can be taken at the origin, so the integral over the wire just multiplies by its length $d$ (slide 6):

<Formula label="The vector potential of the dipole" tex="\vec A(\vec r, t) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{-q_0\omega\sin[\omega(t - r/c)]}{r}\,d\,\hat z = -\frac{\mu_0p_0\omega}{4\pi r}\sin[\omega(t - r/c)]\,\hat z" cite="reading1" at="slide 6, eq. (13) (boxed)" />

Now differentiate, keeping only the terms that fall as $1/r$ (in the radiation zone, differentiating the $1/r$ or the $\cos\theta$ gives terms smaller by $\lambda/r$). The gradient of $V$ is dominated by the derivative of the sine along $\hat r$, and $\hat z = \cos\theta\,\hat r - \sin\theta\,\hat\theta$; the parts along $\hat r$ cancel and leave (slide 7)

<Formula label="The electric field of dipole radiation" tex="\vec E = -\nabla V - \frac{\partial\vec A}{\partial t} = -\frac{\mu_0p_0\omega^2}{4\pi}\left(\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\right)\cos[\omega(t - r/c)]\,\hat\theta" cite="reading1" at="slide 7, eq. (14) (boxed)" />

and for the magnetic field (slide 7)

<Formula label="The magnetic field of dipole radiation" tex="\vec B(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{c}\,\hat r \times \vec E(\vec r, t)" cite="reading1" at="slide 7, eq. (15) (boxed)" />

So $\vec B$ points along $\hat\phi$, at right angles to both $\vec E$ and $\hat r$, and is smaller by $c$: far away, the radiation looks locally like the plane wave of the [Lecture 14 page](/courses/8-07/electromagnetic-plane-waves-reflection), travelling outwards. Both fields fall as $1/r$, as radiation needs.

## Where the energy goes

The Poynting vector is (slide 8)

<Formula label="The Poynting vector of dipole radiation" tex="\vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}(\vec E \times \vec B) = \frac{\mu_0}{c}\left\{\frac{p_0\omega^2}{4\pi}\left(\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\right)\cos[\omega(t - r/c)]\right\}^2\hat r" cite="reading1" at="slide 8, eq. (16) (boxed)" />

Its average over a complete cycle uses $\langle\cos^2\rangle = 1/2$ and gives the **intensity** (slide 8):

<Formula label="The intensity of dipole radiation" tex="\langle\vec S\rangle = \left(\frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2c}\right)\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}\,\hat r" cite="reading1" at="slide 8, eq. (17) (boxed)" />

The factor $\sin^2\theta$ fixes the shape of the radiation: none along the axis of the dipole, the most in the plane at right angles to it. In three dimensions the pattern is a doughnut around the axis. The graph shows a slice through it.

<Plot variable="s" domain={[-1.2, 1.2]} range={[-0.6, 0.6]} xLabel="x" yLabel="z"
  curves={[
    { x: "sin(s)^3", y: "sin(s)^2 * cos(s)", range: [0, 6.2832] }
  ]}
  caption="The radiation pattern of an oscillating electric dipole along the z axis, drawn from eq. (17): in each direction at angle θ from the axis, the distance from the origin to the curve is proportional to sin²θ, the intensity in that direction. Nothing goes along the axis (up and down); the most goes out sideways. Rotating the curve about the z axis gives the full doughnut." />

The total power follows by integrating over a sphere at large $r$ (slide 11):

<Formula label="The total power of dipole radiation" tex="\langle P\rangle = \left(\frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2c}\right)\int\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}\,r^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi = \frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{12\pi c}" cite="reading1" at="slide 11, eq. (18) (boxed)" />

since $\int\sin^3\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi = 2\pi \times \frac43 = \frac{8\pi}{3}$. The power is the same for every sphere: the energy flows out to infinity.

## Polarized blue skies

The power grows as the fourth power of the frequency. Guth uses this to explain the colour of the sky (slide 9). Sunlight passing through air shakes the electrons in the molecules, which then radiate as small oscillating dipoles: they **scatter** the light. Blue light, at the high-frequency end of the visible spectrum, is scattered most: the sky appears blue largely because of the $\omega^4$ (slide 9). Sunsets are red because the blue has been scattered out of the sunlight's path (slide 9). (Two points the slide does not spell out, our additions: the electrons are bound, and visible light is far below the frequencies at which they would resonate, so the induced dipole amplitude hardly depends on the frequency and the scattered power really does go as $\omega^4$; and at sunset the light crosses much more air than at noon.)

How much more? Blue light of wavelength 450 nm has a frequency $700/450$ times that of red light at 700 nm, so it is scattered $(700/450)^4 \approx 5.9$ times more strongly.

The scattered light is also **polarized** (slide 10). The electric field of sunlight is at right angles to the Sun's rays, so it sets the molecular dipoles oscillating in the plane at right angles to the rays. Now look at the sky in a direction at right angles to the rays. Of the possible dipole directions, the one along your line of sight sends you nothing, because a dipole does not radiate along its own axis. Only the component at right angles to both the rays and the line of sight radiates towards you, so the light you receive is polarized along that direction. In the situation Guth considers, that direction is horizontal (slide 10). (Polarizing sunglasses or a camera's polarizing filter make the effect visible: turning them darkens the sky most at about 90° from the Sun.)

## Magnetic dipole radiation

The other simple radiator is a current loop. Guth takes a wire loop of radius $b$ in the $xy$ plane with an alternating current (slide 12)

<Formula label="The current in the loop" tex="I(t) = I_0\cos(\omega t)" cite="reading1" at="slide 12, eq. (19)" />

so that its magnetic dipole moment, the current times the area as on the [magnetic dipole page](/courses/8-07/magnetic-multipole-expansion-dipoles), oscillates along $z$ (slide 12):

<Formula label="The magnetic dipole moment of the loop" tex="\vec m(t) = \pi b^2I(t)\,\hat z = m_0\cos(\omega t)\,\hat z" cite="reading1" at="slide 12, eq. (20)" />

with amplitude (slide 12)

<Formula label="The amplitude of the magnetic moment" tex="m_0 = \pi b^2I_0" cite="reading1" at="slide 12, eq. (21)" />

The loop is neutral, so there is no scalar potential, and the radiation comes from the vector potential alone. Making the same approximations as before, with the loop radius $b$ in place of $d$ as the size of the system ($b \ll \lambda \ll r$), the electric field in the radiation zone is (slide 13)

<Formula label="The electric field of magnetic dipole radiation" tex="\vec E = \frac{\mu_0m_0\omega^2}{4\pi c}\left(\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\right)\cos[\omega(t - r/c)]\,\hat\phi" cite="reading1" at="slide 13, eq. (22)" />

[The slide has a minus sign in front. It does not match the slide's own rule (23) below, which applied to (14) gives the plus sign; differentiating the loop's vector potential directly gives the plus sign too.] Compared with electric dipole radiation, everything follows by the substitutions (slide 13)

<Formula label="From electric to magnetic dipole radiation" tex="p_0 \to \frac{m_0}{c}, \qquad \hat\theta \to -\hat\phi" cite="reading1" at="slide 13, eq. (23)" />

and, as always for radiation (slide 13),

<Formula label="The magnetic field of magnetic dipole radiation" tex="\vec B(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{c}\,\hat r \times \vec E(\vec r, t)" cite="reading1" at="slide 13, eq. (24) (boxed)" />

The electric and magnetic fields have swapped roles: now $\vec E$ circles the axis along $\hat\phi$ and $\vec B$ lies along $\hat\theta$. The pattern, $\sin^2\theta$, is the same doughnut, and applying (23) to (18) gives the total power, $\langle P\rangle = \mu_0m_0^2\omega^4/(12\pi c^3)$ (this step is ours; the slides stop at the fields).

How do the two compare? A loop and a pair of spheres of similar size $b \approx d$ carrying the same current amplitude $I_0 = q_0\omega$ have $m_0/c = \pi b^2q_0\omega/c$ against $p_0 = q_0d$, a ratio of about $\pi b\omega/c = 2\pi^2b/\lambda$. In the dipole approximation, where the size is much smaller than the wavelength, magnetic dipole radiation is therefore much weaker, by the square of that ratio.

<WorkedExample title="A small antenna and a small loop">
<Problem>
Two small spheres 10 cm apart, joined by a wire, carry charges $\pm q_0\cos\omega t$ with $q_0 = 5$ nC, at a frequency of 10 MHz. (a) Check the approximations. (b) Find the radiated power. (c) Find the intensity and the electric field amplitude 1 km away, at right angles to the axis, and the intensity at 45° to it. (d) A loop of radius 5 cm carries the same current amplitude at the same frequency. How does its radiated power compare? Use $\mu_0 = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6}$ T m/A and $c = 2.998 \times 10^8$ m/s.
</Problem>
<Step title="The approximations">
At 10 MHz the wavelength is $\lambda$ = 30.0 m, so $d = 0.1$ m is much smaller than $\lambda$, and 1 km is much larger. The ordering $d \ll \lambda \ll r$ holds.
</Step>
<Step title="The power">
$\omega = 2\pi \times 10^7 = 6.283 \times 10^7$ per second and $p_0 = q_0d = 5 \times 10^{-10}$ C m. Then $\omega^4 = 1.559 \times 10^{31}$ per second to the fourth, and (18) gives $\langle P\rangle = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6} \times (5 \times 10^{-10})^2 \times 1.559 \times 10^{31}/(12\pi \times 2.998 \times 10^8) = 4.33 \times 10^{-4}$ W, about 0.43 mW.
</Step>
<Step title="Far away">
At $r = 1000$ m and $\theta = 90°$, (17) gives $\langle S\rangle = \mu_0p_0^2\omega^4/(32\pi^2cr^2) = 5.17 \times 10^{-11}$ W/m². The field amplitude from (14) is $\mu_0p_0\omega^2/(4\pi r) = 10^{-7} \times 5 \times 10^{-10} \times 3.948 \times 10^{15}/1000 = 1.97 \times 10^{-4}$ V/m, about 0.2 mV/m, a weak but easily received radio signal. At 45° the intensity is smaller by $\sin^2 45° = 1/2$: $2.59 \times 10^{-11}$ W/m².
</Step>
<Step title="The loop">
The current amplitude in the wire is $I_0 = q_0\omega = 0.314$ A. A loop with this current has $m_0 = \pi b^2I_0 = \pi \times 0.0025 \times 0.314 = 2.47 \times 10^{-3}$ A m². Its power relative to the spheres is $(m_0/cp_0)^2 = (2.47 \times 10^{-3}/0.1499)^2 = (0.0165)^2 = 2.71 \times 10^{-4}$, about 3700 times less. Small loops make poor transmitters, which is why broadcast antennas are long straight wires or towers.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Scattered light in the early universe">
The blue sky is light scattered by electrons bound in molecules. In the early universe, before hydrogen atoms formed, light was scattered by free electrons instead. A free electron has no resonance to sit below, so its scattering does not depend on the frequency (Thomson scattering, on the [next page](/courses/8-07/larmor-formula-radiation-reaction)), but it is the same dipole radiation: each shaken electron radiates with the $\sin^2$ pattern about its motion (for unpolarized incoming light, the scattered intensity averages to $(1 + \cos^2\chi)/2$ in the scattering angle $\chi$), and the scattered light is polarized in the same way. Free electrons scattered photons so often that the universe was opaque until they combined into atoms, as 8.286 describes in [temperature and decoupling](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling). The last scatterings also left the cosmic microwave background slightly polarized: the light arriving at each electron was a little uneven around it (a quadrupole pattern, brighter from some directions than from those at right angles), and scattering such light leaves a net polarization; this polarization was first detected in 2002 and is now mapped across the whole sky.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Radiation** is the part of the field that carries energy to infinity: $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ falling as $1/r$, so the flux of the Poynting vector through a large sphere tends to a constant.
- Two small spheres with charges $\pm q_0\cos\omega t$ a distance $d$ apart form an oscillating dipole of amplitude $p_0 = q_0d$. With $d \ll r$, $d \ll \lambda$ (the ideal dipole) and $r \gg \lambda$ (the radiation zone), $V = -\frac{p_0\omega}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c}\frac{\cos\theta}{r}\sin[\omega(t - r/c)]$ and $\vec A = -\frac{\mu_0p_0\omega}{4\pi r}\sin[\omega(t - r/c)]\,\hat z$.
- The fields are $\vec E = -\frac{\mu_0p_0\omega^2}{4\pi}\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\cos[\omega(t - r/c)]\,\hat\theta$ and $\vec B = \frac{1}{c}\hat r \times \vec E$; the intensity is $\frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2c}\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}$ (none along the axis) and the total power $\frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{12\pi c}$.
- The $\omega^4$ makes the sky blue and sunsets red; seen at right angles to the Sun's rays, scattered light is polarized, because a dipole sends nothing along its axis.
- A current loop with moment $m_0\cos\omega t$ gives magnetic dipole radiation: $p_0 \to m_0/c$, $\hat\theta \to -\hat\phi$, much weaker than electric dipole radiation from a source of the same size. In the worked example, $\pm 5$ nC at 10 MHz on spheres 10 cm apart radiate 0.43 mW, and a 5 cm loop with the same current about 3700 times less.
