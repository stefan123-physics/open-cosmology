For a box, [separation of variables in Cartesian coordinates](/courses/8-07/separation-of-variables-cartesian) turns Laplace's equation into three ordinary equations. For a sphere the boundary conditions separate in spherical coordinates instead, and Guth's Lecture 11 (1 October 2012) does the separation there (notes pp. 1–7). The radial part is easy. The angular part is an eigenvalue problem whose answers are the spherical harmonics, and here Guth goes his own way: rather than the special functions of the textbooks, he builds the answers from **traceless symmetric tensors**, using the index notation of [Lecture Notes 2](/courses/8-07/rotations-index-notation). (The notes write the potential as a curly Greek phi; this page keeps $V$, as on the earlier pages, and uses $\phi$ only for the azimuthal angle. Our own words and our own example.)

## Separating the radial and angular parts

In [spherical coordinates](/courses/18-02/spherical-coordinates) the Laplacian of the potential is (notes p. 1)

<Formula label="The Laplacian in spherical coordinates" tex="\nabla^2 V = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial\phi^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Try a product of a function of the radius and a function of the two angles:

<Formula label="A product of radial and angular parts" tex="V(r, \theta, \phi) = R(r)\,F(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Multiply Laplace's equation by $r^2/(RF)$. Every angular derivative carries a factor $1/r^2$, which the $r^2$ cancels, so the equation becomes (notes p. 1)

<Formula label="After multiplying by r squared over RF" tex="0 = \frac{r^2}{RF}\,\nabla^2 V = \frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) + \frac{1}{F}\,\nabla_\theta^2 F" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

where $\nabla_\theta^2$ is the angular part of the Laplacian:

<Formula label="The angular Laplacian" tex="\nabla_\theta^2 F = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial F}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2 F}{\partial\phi^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

(It acts on both angles and could be written with $\theta$ and $\phi$ both underneath, but, the notes remark, that is too hard to write.) As in the Cartesian case, the first term depends only on $r$ and the second only on the angles, and they add up to zero everywhere, so each is a constant: the first is $-C_\theta$ and the second $C_\theta$. The angular part must therefore solve (notes p. 2)

<Formula label="The angular eigenvalue problem" tex="\nabla_\theta^2 F = C_\theta\,F" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

This asks for the **eigenfunctions** and **eigenvalues** of the operator $\nabla_\theta^2$, just as the equation for the factor in $x$ did in the Cartesian case.

## Guth's approach

Guth says how his treatment differs from the books (notes p. 2). It is more general than Griffiths, who keeps only solutions that do not depend on $\phi$, the case of **azimuthal symmetry**, whose answers are the Legendre polynomials. And it differs from Jackson and the other textbooks: he describes the spherical harmonics in terms of traceless symmetric tensors, because he thinks this makes it easier to understand what the spherical harmonics are. (The same functions appear in quantum mechanics as the [spherical harmonics](/courses/8-04/spherical-harmonics-radial-equation) of angular momentum, built there from derivatives of Legendre polynomials.)

## Functions of direction as a power series

The starting point is a claim (notes p. 2): the most general function of the angles $\theta$ and $\phi$ can be written as a power series in $\hat n$, the unit vector pointing in the direction $(\theta, \phi)$:

<Formula label="A function of direction as a power series in n hat" tex="F(\hat n) = C^{(0)} + C^{(1)}_i\,\hat n_i + C^{(2)}_{ij}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Repeated indices are summed from 1 to 3, as for Cartesian coordinates, and the components of $\hat n$ are

<Formula label="The components of n hat" tex="\hat n_1 = \sin\theta\cos\phi, \qquad \hat n_2 = \sin\theta\sin\phi, \qquad \hat n_3 = \cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The $C$'s are constant coefficients, one tensor for each power. As a prelude, the notes announce where this is going (notes p. 3): the term with $\ell$ factors of $\hat n$, $C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}$, will become the spherical harmonics $Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)$, with $\ell$ meaning the same thing in both. For $\ell = 1$, for example, the term is $\vec C \cdot \hat n$, a combination of $\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $\sin\theta\sin\phi$ and $\cos\theta$. (The claim is not proved in these notes, nor in Lecture Notes 9, which adds only that it holds at least for square-integrable, piecewise continuous functions of the angles.)

## Symmetric and traceless coefficients

The series does not fix its coefficients uniquely, and this freedom can be used to impose two restrictions without losing generality (notes p. 3).

**1. $C^{(2)}_{ij}$ can always be chosen symmetric.** The product $\hat n_i\hat n_j$ is unchanged when the two indices are swapped, so an antisymmetric part of $C^{(2)}_{ij}$ would not contribute to $F(\hat n)$: its terms cancel in pairs.

**2. $C^{(2)}_{ij}$ can always be chosen traceless,** $C^{(2)}_{ii} = 0$. Suppose instead that its trace is $C^{(2)}_{ii} = \lambda \neq 0$. Define a new tensor by subtracting a multiple of the [Kronecker delta](/courses/8-07/rotations-index-notation) (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Removing the trace" tex="\tilde C_{ij} = C_{ij} - \frac{1}{3}\lambda\,\delta_{ij}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

Its trace is $\tilde C_{ii} = \lambda - \frac{1}{3}\lambda\,\delta_{ii} = \lambda - \lambda = 0$, since $\delta_{ii} = 3$. And because $\hat n$ is a unit vector, $\delta_{ij}\hat n_i\hat n_j = \hat n_i\hat n_i = 1$, so

<Formula label="The trace becomes a constant" tex="C_{ij}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j = \left[\tilde C_{ij} + \frac{1}{3}\lambda\,\delta_{ij}\right]\hat n_i\hat n_j = \tilde C_{ij}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j + \frac{1}{3}\lambda" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

The constant $\lambda/3$ can be added to $C^{(0)}$. Guth uses $C^{(2)}$ to illustrate, but the same holds for every $\ell$ (notes p. 4): each $C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}$ can be chosen symmetric and traceless, where traceless now means that contracting any two indices gives zero,

<Formula label="Traceless for every degree" tex="C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_{\ell - 2}\,jj} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

and, by the same argument as for $\ell = 2$ (our generalisation; the notes only state the result), a trace that is not zero moves down into the term with two fewer factors of $\hat n$. For $C^{(0)}$ and $C^{(1)}_i$ the conditions are empty, but Guth's vocabulary calls these two traceless and symmetric too.

## Extending the function off the sphere

To evaluate $\nabla_\theta^2 F(\hat n)$, the notes use a trick (notes p. 5): bring back a radial variable $r$, with

<Formula label="Position from radius and direction" tex="r\,\hat n = \vec r = x_i\,\hat e_i = x_1\,\hat e_1 + x_2\,\hat e_2 + x_3\,\hat e_3" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

where the $\hat e_i$ are the Cartesian unit vectors. Given any $F(\hat n)$, define a function of position by writing $x_i$ wherever the series has $\hat n_i$:

<Formula label="The same series with x in place of n hat" tex="F(\vec r) = C^{(0)} + C^{(1)}_i\,x_i + C^{(2)}_{ij}\,x_i x_j + \cdots + C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_\ell} + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

The plan: calculate the ordinary Laplacian $\nabla^2 F(\vec r)$ first, which is easy in Cartesian coordinates, and then infer $\nabla_\theta^2 F(\hat n)$ from it.

## Every term is harmonic

Term by term (notes pp. 5–6): the Laplacian of the constant $C^{(0)}$ is zero, of course, and so is that of $C^{(1)}_i x_i$, whose first derivative is already a constant. The first real case is the quadratic term. With $\partial x_i/\partial x_k = \delta_{ik}$,

<Formula label="The Laplacian of the quadratic term" tex="\nabla^2\left(C^{(2)}_{ij}\,x_i x_j\right) = C^{(2)}_{ij}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}\left[\delta_{ik}\,x_j + \delta_{jk}\,x_i\right] = 2\,C^{(2)}_{ij}\,\delta_{ik}\,\delta_{jk} = 2\,C^{(2)}_{ij}\,\delta_{ij} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

The two terms in the bracket give the same result after the second derivative, $\delta_{ik}\delta_{jk}$, whatever $C^{(2)}$ is, and the last step is the traceless condition, the only property the notes use. The cubic term goes the same way: two derivatives leave three terms like $C^{(3)}_{ijk}\,\delta_{ij}\,x_k$, each the trace of $C^{(3)}$ on one pair of indices times a coordinate, and each zero by tracelessness (notes p. 6). The pattern works in general, the notes say; in our words: two derivatives of $x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_\ell}$ always bring down a Kronecker delta that ties two indices of $C^{(\ell)}$ together, and the trace is zero. So every term, and the whole series, is a solution of Laplace's equation (notes p. 6):

<Formula label="The extended function is harmonic" tex="\nabla^2 F(\vec r) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

## The eigenvalues

Now compare with the spherical form of the Laplacian (notes p. 6):

<Formula label="The Laplacian split into radial and angular parts" tex="\nabla^2 F(\vec r) = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2\frac{\partial F}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\nabla_\theta^2 F(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Look at the term of degree $\ell$ alone. Since $x_i = r\,\hat n_i$, each of its $\ell$ factors brings one power of $r$ (notes p. 7):

<Formula label="The term of degree l, on and off the sphere" tex="F_\ell(\vec r) = C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_\ell} = r^\ell\,C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell} = r^\ell\,F_\ell(\hat n)" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

The radial derivatives act only on $r^\ell$, and the angular ones only on $F_\ell(\hat n)$, so

<Formula label="Laplace's equation for the term of degree l" tex="0 = \nabla^2 F_\ell(\vec r) = r^{\ell - 2}\,\nabla_\theta^2 F_\ell(\hat n) + \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{d r^\ell}{dr}\right)F_\ell(\hat n)" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

The radial part is a short calculation:

<Formula label="The radial part" tex="\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{d r^\ell}{dr}\right) = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\left(\ell\,r^2\,r^{\ell - 1}\right) = \ell(\ell + 1)\,r^{\ell - 2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

Dividing by $r^{\ell - 2}$ gives the boxed result of the notes (notes p. 7):

<Formula label="Eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of the angular Laplacian" tex="\nabla_\theta^2 F_\ell(\hat n) = -\ell(\ell + 1)\,F_\ell(\hat n)" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

Guth's own exclamation mark is deserved: the eigenfunctions are the functions $F_\ell(\hat n) = C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}$ with a symmetric, traceless $C^{(\ell)}$, and the eigenvalues are $-\ell(\ell + 1)$, with $\ell = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$ (Lecture Notes 9, p. 6, adds that these are the only allowed values of the separation constant $C_\theta$, which rests on the claim above that every function of direction is such a series.) No special functions were needed, only the fact that the coefficients have no trace. The same numbers $\ell(\ell + 1)$ are the eigenvalues of the squared angular momentum (times ħ²) in [quantum mechanics](/courses/8-04/angular-momentum-central-potentials).

## The radial solutions

The notes for Lecture 11 stop at the eigenvalues. The radial solutions are quoted in Lecture Notes 7 (eq. (7.2)) and derived in Lecture Notes 9, and the step is short. With $C_\theta = -\ell(\ell + 1)$, the radial equation of the first section becomes $\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) = \ell(\ell + 1)R$. Trying $R = r^p$ gives $p(p + 1) = \ell(\ell + 1)$, a quadratic with the roots $p = \ell$ and $p = -(\ell + 1)$, so (Lecture Notes 9, eq. (9.31))

<Formula label="The radial solutions" tex="R_\ell(r) = A_\ell\,r^\ell + \frac{B_\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}" cite="notes9" at="eq. (9.31), p. 7" />

A product $R_\ell(r)F_\ell(\hat n)$ solves Laplace's equation, and so does any sum of them. Inside a sphere the potential must be finite at the centre, so only $r^\ell$ is allowed; outside, it must vanish far away, so only $1/r^{\ell + 1}$ is. The term with $r^\ell$ is the extended polynomial $F_\ell(\vec r)$ itself.

<WorkedExample title="A sphere held at a potential that varies with latitude">
<Problem>
A thin spherical shell of radius $a = 10$ cm is held, by a suitable surface charge, at the potential $V_0\cos^2\theta$ with $V_0 = 60$ V: 60 V at the poles, zero on the equator. There are no other charges, and the potential vanishes far away. Find the potential inside and outside, and evaluate it 5 cm and 20 cm from the centre, along the axis and in the equatorial plane.
</Problem>
<Step title="Write the boundary value as traceless terms">
On the sphere, $\cos^2\theta = \hat n_3\hat n_3 = C_{ij}\hat n_i\hat n_j$ where the only component of $C_{ij}$ that is not zero is a 1 in the third row and third column. Its trace is $\lambda = 1$, so remove it: $\tilde C_{ij} = C_{ij} - \frac{1}{3}\delta_{ij}$, and $\cos^2\theta = \left(\cos^2\theta - \frac{1}{3}\right) + \frac{1}{3}$. The boundary value is the sum of an $\ell = 0$ term, $V_0/3 = 20$ V, and an $\ell = 2$ term, $V_0(\cos^2\theta - 1/3)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Check the eigenvalue directly">
For $f = \cos^2\theta$ (no $\phi$ dependence), $\sin\theta\,df/d\theta = -2\cos\theta\sin^2\theta$, and the angular Laplacian gives $2\sin^2\theta - 4\cos^2\theta = 2 - 6\cos^2\theta = -6\left(\cos^2\theta - \frac{1}{3}\right)$. So $\nabla_\theta^2$ acting on the traceless part gives $-6 = -2 \times 3$ times it, as the boxed formula says for $\ell = 2$, while $\cos^2\theta$ itself is not an eigenfunction: the trace has to go.
</Step>
<Step title="Inside and outside">
Each term gets its radial factor, matched to the boundary at $r = a$. Inside, $V = V_0/3 + V_0\,(r/a)^2\left(\cos^2\theta - 1/3\right)$. With $r\cos\theta = z$ this is $V_0/3 + V_0\left(2z^2 - x^2 - y^2\right)/(3a^2)$, a polynomial whose Laplacian is plainly a constant times $4 - 2 - 2 = 0$ (four from $2z^2$, minus two from each of $x^2$ and $y^2$). Outside, $V = (V_0/3)(a/r) + V_0\,(a/r)^3\left(\cos^2\theta - 1/3\right)$. Both agree with the boundary value at $r = a$, and by the [uniqueness theorem](/courses/8-07/method-of-images-uniqueness) they are the answers.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
At the centre the potential is 20 V, the average over the sphere. At 5 cm from the centre, $r^2/a^2 = 1/4$: on the axis ($\cos^2\theta = 1$) the potential is $20 + 60 \times \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{2}{3} = 30$ V, and in the equatorial plane it is $20 - 60 \times \frac{1}{4} \times \frac{1}{3} = 15$ V. At 20 cm, $a/r = 1/2$ and $a^3/r^3 = 1/8$: on the axis $10 + 5 = 15$ V, in the equatorial plane $10 - 2.5 = 7.5$ V. Far away only the $\ell = 0$ term survives, the potential of a point charge $Q = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a V_0/3$, about 0.22 nC: the $\ell = 2$ part of the surface charge adds up to zero.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The same expansion on the sky">
The temperature of the cosmic microwave background is a function of direction on the sky, $T(\hat n)$, and cosmologists expand it in exactly the angular eigenfunctions found here, usually written as spherical harmonics $Y_{\ell m}$ (see [the CMB anisotropies page of 8.902](/courses/8-902/cmb-anisotropies)). In Guth's language the $\ell = 0$ term is the average temperature, the $\ell = 1$ term $C^{(1)}_i\hat n_i$ is a dipole pointing along our motion through the radiation, and the $\ell = 2$ term, the quadrupole, has a traceless symmetric $3 \times 3$ tensor as its coefficient. The strength of each degree $\ell$, the angular power spectrum, is how the CMB measures the early universe; large $\ell$ means small patches on the sky.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Separation in spherical coordinates:** with $V = R(r)F(\theta, \phi)$, Laplace's equation times $r^2/(RF)$ splits into a radial term and $\frac{1}{F}\nabla_\theta^2 F$, so the angular part must solve the eigenvalue problem $\nabla_\theta^2 F = C_\theta F$, where $\nabla_\theta^2$ is the angular part of the Laplacian.
- **Guth's approach:** any function of direction is a power series in the unit vector, $F(\hat n) = C^{(0)} + C^{(1)}_i\hat n_i + C^{(2)}_{ij}\hat n_i\hat n_j + \cdots$. Each coefficient tensor can be chosen **symmetric** (an antisymmetric part drops out) and **traceless** (a trace $\lambda$ becomes the constant $\lambda/3$ in a lower term, because $\hat n_i\hat n_i = 1$).
- **The trick:** replace $\hat n$ by $\vec r$. Each term becomes a polynomial $F_\ell(\vec r) = r^\ell F_\ell(\hat n)$, whose Laplacian vanishes because its coefficients are traceless.
- **The result:** the radial part of the Laplacian of $r^\ell$ gives $\ell(\ell + 1)r^{\ell - 2}$, so $\nabla_\theta^2 F_\ell = -\ell(\ell + 1)F_\ell$. The terms of degree $\ell$ are the eigenfunctions (they will become the spherical harmonics $Y_{\ell m}$), and the radial solutions are $r^\ell$ and $1/r^{\ell + 1}$.
- **Example:** a sphere held at $V_0\cos^2\theta$ splits into $V_0/3$ ($\ell = 0$) and $V_0(\cos^2\theta - 1/3)$ ($\ell = 2$), giving $V_0/3 + V_0(r/a)^2(\cos^2\theta - 1/3)$ inside.
