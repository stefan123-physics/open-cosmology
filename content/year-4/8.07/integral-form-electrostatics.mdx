Electrostatics is usually stated as three differential equations, one for each point of space. Guth's Lecture 6 (17 September 2012) turns each of them into an integral statement about paths, surfaces and volumes (notes p. 1). In practice the integral forms are often what one uses to solve a problem. The tools are the [divergence theorem](/courses/18-02/divergence-theorem) and [Stokes' theorem](/courses/18-02/stokes-theorem); the physics is familiar from [Gauss's law](/courses/8-02/gauss-law) and [the electric potential](/courses/8-02/electric-potential) in 8.02. (MIT's material for this lecture is Guth's handwritten notes, ten pages with boxed results and no numbered equations. This page covers their first three pages in its own words, with its own example; the second half, on energy, is the next page.)

## The three equations

The notes start from the differential equations of electrostatics (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="Electrostatics in differential form" tex="\vec E = -\nabla V, \qquad \nabla \cdot \vec E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \nabla \times \vec E = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The first says the field is the downhill slope of a potential, the second (Gauss's law) that charges are the sources of the field, the third that the field has no curl. The goal is the integral form of each, "and some related consequences".

## The potential as a line integral

Take a path $P$ from a point $\vec a$ to a point $\vec b$ and integrate the first equation along it. The integral of a gradient along a path is the change of the function between the ends, so (notes p. 1)

<Formula label="Line integral of the field" tex="\int_{\vec a}^{\vec b}\vec E \cdot d\vec\ell = -\int_{\vec a}^{\vec b}\nabla V \cdot d\vec\ell = V(\vec a) - V(\vec b)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

Read backwards, this gives a second definition of the potential. Pick any reference point $\vec r_0$; then

<Formula label="The potential from a reference point" tex="V(\vec r) = V(\vec r_0) - \int_{\vec r_0}^{\vec r}\vec E(\vec r') \cdot d\vec\ell'" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

The value $V(\vec r_0)$ is anything we like, so $V$ has an arbitrary zero point. Adding a constant to $V$ changes nothing physical, since its gradient, the field, stays the same: **only potential differences are meaningful** (notes pp. 1–2).

The earlier definition of $V$, as an integral of $\rho/(4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec r'|)$ over the charge density, makes a particular choice. Far from a finite charge distribution it goes to zero (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="The usual choice of zero" tex="\lim_{|\vec r| \to \infty} V(\vec r) = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

so it is the line-integral definition with the reference point at infinity and $V = 0$ there.

Why keep both? Because for idealised textbook problems, such as an **infinite line of charge**, the integral over $\rho$ may not converge: there is too much charge far away. The line integral still works, and it defines every potential difference (notes p. 2). The worked example below shows this.

## Gauss's law in integral form

Now integrate $\nabla \cdot \vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ over a region $D$ of space bounded by a closed surface $S$. The divergence theorem turns the left side into the flux through $S$ (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="The divergence theorem for the field" tex="\int_{D}\nabla \cdot \vec E\;d^3x = \oint_S \vec E \cdot d\vec a" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

and the right side is the charge inside, divided by $\varepsilon_0$:

<Formula label="Flux and enclosed charge" tex="\oint_S \vec E \cdot d\vec a = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int_{D}d^3x\,\rho = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\int dq" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

This is Gauss's law (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Gauss's law" tex="\oint_S \vec E \cdot d\vec a = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\,Q_{\text{enc}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

(The page calls the volume $D$, to keep it apart from the potential $V$; the notes use the same letter for both.) Guth adds a note under the box: this is **true even for dynamics**. Gauss's law is one of Maxwell's equations, and it holds when charges move and fields change. The next result is different.

## The field has no curl: loop integrals vanish

Integrate $\nabla \times \vec E = 0$ over any surface $S$ whose boundary is a closed path $P$. Stokes' theorem turns the surface integral of the curl into the loop integral around the edge (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Stokes' theorem for the field" tex="\int_S (\nabla \times \vec E) \cdot d\vec a = \oint_P \vec E \cdot d\vec\ell" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

so

<Formula label="The loop integral of an electrostatic field" tex="\oint_P \vec E \cdot d\vec\ell = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

for every closed path. (Unlike Gauss's law, this one is purely electrostatic: when magnetic fields change, Faraday's law gives $\nabla \times \vec E \neq 0$ and loop integrals of $\vec E$ no longer vanish. That comes later in the course; see [Faraday's law](/courses/8-02/faradays-law) in 8.02.)

## Which path?

This result removes an ambiguity in the formula for $V(\vec r)$. The line integral from $\vec r_0$ to $\vec r$ could be taken along many paths: which one is meant? Guth's answer: **it doesn't matter** (notes p. 3). Take two paths $P_1$ and $P_2$ with the same ends. Going out along $P_1$ and back along $P_2$ is a closed loop, so

<Formula label="Path independence" tex="\int_{P_1}\vec E \cdot d\vec\ell - \int_{P_2}\vec E \cdot d\vec\ell = \oint \vec E \cdot d\vec\ell = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Both paths give the same potential, as 18.02 showed for any [gradient field](/courses/18-02/path-independence).

## Worked example: an infinite line of charge

<WorkedExample title="Potential differences near an infinite line of charge">
<Problem>
A long straight wire, treated as infinite, carries $\lambda = 2.0$ nC per metre. Show that the integral over the charge does not give its potential, then find the field from Gauss's law and the potential difference between $r = 1$ cm and $r = 5$ cm from the wire. Check path independence on a path that is not straight.
</Problem>
<Step title="The integral over the charge diverges">
At distance $r$ from the wire, the charge $\lambda\,dz$ at height $z$ contributes $\lambda\,dz/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\sqrt{r^2 + z^2})$. For large $|z|$ the integrand falls only like $1/|z|$, and the integral of $1/|z|$ out to infinity grows like a logarithm without limit. So this formula gives an infinite potential at every point: the reference "zero at infinity" cannot be used here.
</Step>
<Step title="The field from Gauss's law">
Take a closed cylinder of radius $r$ and length $L$ around the wire. By symmetry the field points straight away from the wire and has the same size all over the curved side; the flat ends contribute no flux. So $E \cdot 2\pi rL = \lambda L/\varepsilon_0$, and $E = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$. With the numbers, $\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0) = 35.95$ V, so $E = 35.95\text{ V}/r$.
</Step>
<Step title="The potential from the line integral">
Choose the reference point at $r_0 = 1$ cm and integrate straight out from the wire: $V(r) = V(r_0) - (\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(r/r_0)$. Between 1 cm and 5 cm, $V(1\text{ cm}) - V(5\text{ cm}) = 35.95\text{ V} \times \ln 5 \approx 57.9$ V. The potential goes on falling, without limit, as $r$ grows, which is why no reference point at infinity exists.
</Step>
<Step title="Another path">
Now take a path in a plane at right angles to the wire. From the point at 1 cm, go first along a quarter circle of radius 1 cm around the wire, then straight out to 5 cm, then back along a quarter circle at 5 cm to the end point, 5 cm out on the original radial line. On the arcs the path runs at right angles to the radial field, so $\vec E \cdot d\vec\ell = 0$ there; the straight part gives the same $57.9$ V as before. Same ends, same answer, as $\oint \vec E \cdot d\vec\ell = 0$ requires.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Integrating $\vec E = -\nabla V$ along a path gives $V(\vec r) = V(\vec r_0) - \int_{\vec r_0}^{\vec r}\vec E \cdot d\vec\ell'$. The reference value is arbitrary, so only potential differences mean anything; the integral over $\rho$ is the special case of a reference at infinity with $V = 0$.
- For idealised sources such as an infinite line of charge the integral over $\rho$ diverges, but the line integral still gives every potential difference: $(\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(b/a)$ for the line.
- The divergence theorem turns $\nabla \cdot \vec E = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ into Gauss's law, $\oint_S \vec E \cdot d\vec a = Q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0$, which holds even when the fields change in time.
- Stokes' theorem turns $\nabla \times \vec E = 0$ into $\oint \vec E \cdot d\vec\ell = 0$ for every closed path, a statement of electrostatics only.
- Because loop integrals vanish, the line integral defining $V$ is the same along every path between the same two points.
