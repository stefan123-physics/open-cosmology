## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `V0*R/(2*z)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `pi` stands for $\pi$, `eps0` for $\varepsilon_0$, `th` for $\theta$, and `V0` for $V_0$; the other letters (`R`, `z`, `q`, `d`, `r`, `p`, `x`) are typed as they are.

MIT gives the points per problem: 20 for Problem 2, 20 for Problem 3, 20 for Problem 4 and 15 for Problem 5, which makes 75 points here (MIT's sheet has 90 with Problem 1), plus 10 points of extra credit for Problem 5(c), which is optional here. The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes. $V$ is the electric potential, $\vec E = -\nabla V$ is the field, and $\varepsilon_0$ is the electric constant. In spherical coordinates $r$ is the distance from the origin, $\theta$ is the angle from the $z$ axis and $\phi$ is the angle around it; $\hat n$ is the unit vector in the direction $(\theta, \phi)$. $P_\ell$ is the Legendre polynomial of degree $\ell$ and $Y_{\ell m}$ the standard spherical harmonic. Curly brackets $\{\ \}$ around a product of vectors mean its traceless symmetric part, and repeated indices are summed from 1 to 3.

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A circular disk at a fixed potential" points={20} mit="Problem 2">

A thin, flat conducting disk of radius $R$ lies in the $xy$ plane with its centre at the origin. It is held at the potential $V_0$, with $V = 0$ far away. Let $s$ be the distance of a point of the disk from its centre, and let $\sigma(s)$ be the charge per unit area of the disk, counting both of its faces together.

<Part label="a" points={14}>

You may take as given that the charge density of a disk held at a fixed potential has the form

<Formula label="The charge density of the disk" tex="\sigma(s) = \frac{A}{\sqrt{R^2 - s^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1" />

with $A$ a constant. Show that outside the sphere of radius $R$ around the centre, for $r > R$, the potential is

<Formula label="The potential outside the disk" tex="V(r, \theta) = \frac{2V_0}{\pi}\,\frac{R}{r}\sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^\ell}{2\ell + 1}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{2\ell}P_{2\ell}(\cos\theta)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1" />

MIT's hint: find the potential (or the field) on the $z$ axis first, and then use what you know about the general solution of Laplace's equation to bring in the dependence on the angle.

The two boxes are our additions. The first asks for $A$ in terms of $V_0$; the second for the potential on the positive $z$ axis, at the height $z > 0$, in closed form.

<Hint>

On the axis every ring of the disk, of radius $s$ and width $ds$, is at the same distance $\sqrt{s^2 + z^2}$ from the point. Add up the potentials of the rings.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the integral over $s$, the substitution $u = s^2$ turns the integrand into $1/\sqrt{(R^2 - u)(u + z^2)}$, up to a constant. The integral of that is an inverse tangent.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The centre of the disk, $z \to 0$, is a point of the conductor, so the potential there is $V_0$. That fixes $A$. For $z > R$, expand the inverse tangent in powers of $R/z$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Outside the sphere $r = R$ there is no charge, $V$ vanishes far away and nothing depends on $\phi$, so $V$ is a Legendre series with only the terms $B_\ell/r^{\ell + 1}$. On the positive axis every $P_\ell(1) = 1$. Compare the two series on the axis term by term.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$A =$" expr="4*eps0*V0/pi"
  vars={{ eps0: [0.5, 2], V0: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", V0: "V_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*eps0*V0/pi", hint: "With this A the potential at the centre of the disk, (A/2ε₀)(π/2), comes out as V₀/2, not V₀." },
    { expr: "eps0*V0", hint: "With this A the potential at the centre of the disk, (A/2ε₀)(π/2), comes out as πV₀/4, not V₀. The integral over the disk gives a factor π/2 that has to be divided out." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-axis" label="$V(z)$ on the positive $z$ axis $=$" expr="2*V0/pi*atan(R/z)"
  vars={{ V0: [1, 3], R: [0.5, 2], z: [0.3, 3] }}
  names={{ V0: "V_0", R: "R", z: "z" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "V0/pi*atan(R/z)", hint: "As z tends to 0 this tends to V₀/2, but the centre of the disk is at V₀." },
    { expr: "2*V0/pi*atan(z/R)", hint: "This grows with z and vanishes at the disk. The potential must be V₀ at the disk and fall to zero far away." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the axis the potential is a single integral over rings, which can be done in closed form. Off the axis, the uniqueness of the Legendre series fixes the angular dependence from the values on the axis.

<Step title="The potential on the axis">

The potential of a charge distribution is the integral of the charge over the distance (Lecture Notes 9):

<Formula label="The potential of a charge distribution" tex="V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}\,d^3x'" cite="reading3" at="eq. (9.47)" />

For the disk the charge is $\sigma(s)$ on rings of area $2\pi s\,ds$, all at the distance $\sqrt{s^2 + z^2}$ from the point at height $z$:

<Formula label="The potential on the axis as an integral" tex="V(z) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^R\frac{A}{\sqrt{R^2 - s^2}}\,\frac{2\pi s\,ds}{\sqrt{s^2 + z^2}} = \frac{A}{2\varepsilon_0}\int_0^R\frac{s\,ds}{\sqrt{(R^2 - s^2)(s^2 + z^2)}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

With $u = s^2$ the integral is $\frac12\int_0^{R^2}du/\sqrt{(R^2 - u)(u + z^2)}$. Since the derivative of $-2\arctan\sqrt{(R^2 - u)/(u + z^2)}$ is exactly $1/\sqrt{(R^2 - u)(u + z^2)}$, the integral is $\frac12 \times 2\arctan(R/z)$, and

<Formula label="The potential on the axis" tex="V(z) = \frac{A}{2\varepsilon_0}\,\arctan\frac{R}{z} \qquad (z > 0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The constant A">

As $z \to 0$ the point reaches the centre of the disk, where the potential is $V_0$, and $\arctan(R/z) \to \pi/2$. So $A\pi/(4\varepsilon_0) = V_0$:

<Formula label="The constant in the charge density" tex="A = \frac{4\varepsilon_0 V_0}{\pi}, \qquad V(z) = \frac{2V_0}{\pi}\,\arctan\frac{R}{z}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The series on the axis">

For $z > R$ the inverse tangent has the convergent series $\arctan(R/z) = (R/z) - (R/z)^3/3 + (R/z)^5/5 - \cdots$:

<Formula label="The potential on the axis as a series" tex="V(z) = \frac{2V_0}{\pi}\,\frac{R}{z}\sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^\ell}{2\ell + 1}\left(\frac{R}{z}\right)^{2\ell} \qquad (z > R)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Off the axis">

All the charge lies inside the sphere $r = R$. Outside it the potential obeys Laplace's equation, does not depend on $\phi$, and vanishes far away, so by Lecture Notes 7 it is a Legendre series with only the decaying terms:

<Formula label="The general solution with azimuthal symmetry" tex="V(r, \theta) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\left[A_\ell\,r^\ell + \frac{B_\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}\right]P_\ell(\cos\theta), \qquad A_\ell = 0 \text{ here}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (7.2) and (7.4), p. 1" />

On the positive axis $\theta = 0$, and every $P_\ell(1) = 1$ (put $x = 1$ in the generating function of Lecture Notes 9, eq. (9.50): $1/(1 - \lambda) = \sum\lambda^\ell$). So on the axis the series is $\sum B_\ell/z^{\ell + 1}$. Two power series in $1/z$ that agree for all $z > R$ have the same coefficients, so comparing with the series above,

<Formula label="The coefficients of the Legendre series" tex="B_{2\ell} = \frac{2V_0}{\pi}\,\frac{(-1)^\ell}{2\ell + 1}\,R^{2\ell + 1}, \qquad B_{2\ell + 1} = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

which is the series to be shown. Only even $\ell$ appear: the disk looks the same from above and from below, and $P_\ell(-\cos\theta) = (-1)^\ell P_\ell(\cos\theta)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Far away only the first term is left, $V \approx (2V_0/\pi)(R/r)$, which falls off as $1/r$ in every direction, as the potential of a net charge should. At $\theta = \pi$ (the negative axis), $P_{2\ell}(-1) = 1$, so the potential is the same at the same distance below the disk. For $R = 1$, $z = 2$ and $V_0 = 1$, the closed form gives $(2/\pi)\arctan 0.5 = 0.2952$, and a direct numerical integral over the rings gives the same.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find the capacitance of the disk, defined by $Q = CV$, where $Q$ is the total charge on the disk and $V$ its potential relative to infinity. The number box is our addition: give $C$ for a disk of radius $R = 10$ cm, in picofarads. Use $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}$ F/m.

<Hint>

Either integrate $\sigma(s)$ over the disk, or read the total charge from the potential far away.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-cap" label="$C =$" expr="8*eps0*R"
  vars={{ eps0: [0.5, 2], R: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", R: "R" }}
  mistakes={[

    { expr: "4*eps0*R", hint: "That is half the right value. Matching the far potential (2V₀/π)(R/r) to Q/(4πε₀r) gives Q = 4πε₀ × (2/π) R V₀." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-capnum" label="$C$ for $R = 10$ cm, in pF:" value="7.08" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-capnum" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The total charge is the integral of the density found in part (a); the far field confirms it.

<Step title="The total charge">

<Formula label="The total charge on the disk" tex="Q = \int_0^R\frac{A}{\sqrt{R^2 - s^2}}\,2\pi s\,ds = 2\pi A\left[-\sqrt{R^2 - s^2}\right]_0^R = 2\pi A R = 8\varepsilon_0 R\,V_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The capacitance">

<Formula label="The capacitance of the disk" tex="C = \frac{Q}{V_0} = 8\varepsilon_0 R" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

For $R = 0.10$ m, $C = 8 \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 0.10 = 7.08 \times 10^{-12}$ F $= 7.08$ pF.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The term $\ell = 0$ of part (a) is the monopole term: $(2V_0/\pi)(R/r) = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$ gives $Q = 8\varepsilon_0RV_0$ again. A sphere of the same radius has $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R = 11.1$ pF; the disk has $2/\pi = 0.64$ of that, which is plausible for a conductor that fits inside the sphere.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Quadrupole and octopole terms of the multipole expansion (our own problem)" points={20} mit="Problem 3">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Quadrupole and octopole terms of the multipole expansion", with two remarks of its own. The problem below is our own, on the same subject, and MIT's remarks apply to it. First, the quadrupole and octopole terms can be found in two ways: from the multipole expansion in Legendre polynomials, or by expanding the integral for the potential in powers of $1/r$ (MIT points to the textbook for both; here they are eqs. (9.52) and (9.47) of Lecture Notes 9). Second, the quadrupole moment $Q_{ij}$ and the octopole moment found here are examples of the traceless symmetric tensors of the lectures.

A charge density $\rho(\vec r\,')$ is confined to a bounded region around the origin. Far away its potential is the multipole expansion of Lecture Notes 9:

<Formula label="The multipole expansion" tex="V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{\ell + 1}}\,C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}, \qquad C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell} = \frac{(2\ell - 1)!!}{\ell\,!}\int\rho(\vec r\,')\,\{x'_{i_1}\cdots x'_{i_\ell}\}\,d^3x'" cite="reading3" at="eqs. (9.68) and (9.69)" />

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Define the quadrupole moment

<Formula label="The quadrupole moment" tex="Q_{ij} = \int\rho(\vec r\,')\left(3x'_i x'_j - r'^2\,\delta_{ij}\right)d^3x'" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

Show that the term $\ell = 2$ of the expansion is

<Formula label="The quadrupole term" tex="V_{\text{quad}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{1}{2r^3}\,Q_{ij}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

and that $Q_{ij}$ is symmetric and traceless. How is the tensor $C^{(2)}_{ij}$ of the lecture notes related to $Q_{ij}$?

<Hint>

$(2\ell - 1)!!/\ell\,!$ for $\ell = 2$ is $3/2$. The traceless symmetric part of $x'_ix'_j$ is $x'_ix'_j - \frac13 r'^2\delta_{ij}$, the rank-2 case of the lecture notes with $\hat z$ replaced by $\vec r\,'$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the trace, set $j = i$ and sum: $\delta_{ii} = 3$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-c2" label="The tensor $C^{(2)}_{ij}$ of the lecture notes equals" choices={["Qᵢⱼ", "Qᵢⱼ/2", "3Qᵢⱼ/2", "Qᵢⱼ/3"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write out the traceless symmetric part of $x'_ix'_j$ and collect the numbers.

<Step title="The tensor of rank 2">

The rank-2 traceless symmetric part (Lecture Notes 9, eq. (9.37), for a unit vector) scales with the length squared:

<Formula label="The traceless symmetric part of rank 2" tex="\{x'_i x'_j\} = x'_i x'_j - \frac13\,r'^2\,\delta_{ij}" cite="reading3" at="eq. (9.37), for r′ in place of ẑ" />

So

<Formula label="The quadrupole tensor of the notes" tex="C^{(2)}_{ij} = \frac32\int\rho(\vec r\,')\left(x'_i x'_j - \frac13\,r'^2\,\delta_{ij}\right)d^3x' = \frac12\int\rho(\vec r\,')\left(3x'_i x'_j - r'^2\,\delta_{ij}\right)d^3x' = \frac12\,Q_{ij}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

and the term $\ell = 2$ is $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{1}{r^3}C^{(2)}_{ij}\hat n_i\hat n_j$, which is the quadrupole term above.

</Step>

<Step title="Symmetric and traceless">

$Q_{ij}$ is symmetric because $x'_ix'_j$ and $\delta_{ij}$ are. Its trace is $Q_{ii} = \int\rho\,(3r'^2 - 3r'^2)\,d^3x' = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The other route">

Expanding $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ to second order in $\vec r\,'$, the term of degree 2 in eq. (9.52) is $(r'^2/r^3)P_2(\cos\theta')$, with $P_2(x) = \frac12(3x^2 - 1)$ and $r'\cos\theta' = \hat n \cdot \vec r\,'$. So it is $\frac{1}{2r^3}\left[3(\hat n \cdot \vec r\,')^2 - r'^2\right] = \frac{1}{2r^3}\left(3x'_ix'_j - r'^2\delta_{ij}\right)\hat n_i\hat n_j$, using $\hat n_i\hat n_i = 1$. Integrating against $\rho$ gives the same term.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A traceless symmetric $3 \times 3$ tensor has $2\ell + 1 = 5$ independent components, the number of spherical harmonics of degree 2. The trace part dropped from $Q_{ij}$ would only add a constant times $\hat n_i\hat n_i = 1$ to the angular factor, which belongs to $\ell = 0$, not to $\ell = 2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

A linear quadrupole: three point charges on the $z$ axis, $+q$ at $z = +d$, $-2q$ at the origin and $+q$ at $z = -d$. Find $Q_{zz}$ and the quadrupole term of the potential at a far point $(r, \theta)$. Then, as a number: for $q = 2.0$ nC and $d = 1.0$ cm, find the quadrupole term on the axis at $r = 20$ cm, in volts. Use $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 8.988 \times 10^9$ N m²/C².

<Hint>

For point charges the integral is a sum over the charges. The charge at the origin has $\vec r\,' = 0$ and adds nothing to $Q_{ij}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The charges sit on the $z$ axis, so $Q_{ij}$ is diagonal with $Q_{xx} = Q_{yy}$, and it is traceless. Then $Q_{ij}\hat n_i\hat n_j$ needs only $\cos\theta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-qzz" label="$Q_{zz} =$" expr="4*q*d^2"

    { expr: "q*d^2*(3*cos(th)^2 - 1)/(4*pi*eps0*r^2)", hint: "The term of degree ℓ falls off as 1/r to the power ℓ + 1: the quadrupole term goes as 1/r³." }

<Answer id="p3-vnum" label="Quadrupole term on the axis at $r = 20$ cm, in V:" value="0.449" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-vnum" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For point charges the moment is a sum, and the symmetry about the axis leaves a single number.

<Step title="The moment">

Only the two outer charges count. Each has $x' = y' = 0$, $z'^2 = d^2$ and $r'^2 = d^2$:

<Formula label="The quadrupole moment of the linear quadrupole" tex="Q_{zz} = 2\,q\left(3d^2 - d^2\right) = 4qd^2, \qquad Q_{xx} = Q_{yy} = 2\,q\left(0 - d^2\right) = -2qd^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

and the off-diagonal components vanish. The trace is $4qd^2 - 2qd^2 - 2qd^2 = 0$, as it must be.

</Step>

<Step title="The potential">

With $\hat n = (\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\theta)$, $Q_{ij}\hat n_i\hat n_j = -2qd^2\sin^2\theta + 4qd^2\cos^2\theta = 2qd^2\left(3\cos^2\theta - 1\right)$, so

<Formula label="The quadrupole term of the linear quadrupole" tex="V_{\text{quad}}(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{qd^2\left(3\cos^2\theta - 1\right)}{r^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

In [Problem Set 4, Problem 6](/courses/8-07/8-07-problem-set-4#p6) the average of the field over a ball of radius $R$ was found: for any charges inside the ball it is $\vec E_{\text{avg}} = -\vec p/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3)$, with $\vec p$ their dipole moment about the centre.

<Step title="The angular average">

<Formula label="The average of n i n j over directions" tex="\int\hat n_i\hat n_j\,d\Omega = \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\delta_{ij}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

## Problem 5(c), extra credit

<Exercise id="p5c" title="The dipole field as a distribution" points={10} mit="Problem 5(c)" optional>

The usual route to the delta-function term, as in part (b), starts from $V_{\text{dip}}$, which cannot be differentiated at $r = 0$, finds a field that breaks a general theorem (the average-field result), and repairs it. MIT's extra-credit part shows how the theory of distributions (generalised functions) makes everything well defined from the start, as Problem Set 2, [Problem 7](/courses/8-07/8-07-problem-set-2#p7), did for the Laplacian of $1/r$.

$V_{\text{dip}}$ is defined as a distribution by what it does to a test function $g(\vec r)$, which is smooth and falls off rapidly at large $|\vec r|$:

<Formula label="The dipole potential as a distribution" tex="F[g] \equiv \int g(\vec r)\,\frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\,d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), eq. (5.1), p. 4" />

The integral is well defined, because $d^3x = r^2\,dr\,\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$ cancels the $1/r^2$. The naive derivative, the integral of $g\,\partial_i(p\cos\theta/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$, is not well defined at the origin (MIT's eq. (5.2)). The derivative $\partial_iV_{\text{dip}} = \partial V_{\text{dip}}/\partial x_i$ in the sense of distributions is defined instead by a formal integration by parts, with no surface term by definition:

<Formula label="The derivative as a distribution" tex="F_i[g] = -\int\partial_i g(\vec r)\,\frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\,d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), eq. (5.3), p. 4" />

This is an ordinary, well-defined integral. Split it at a small radius $\epsilon > 0$, which will tend to zero: $F_i = F^{(1)}_i + F^{(2)}_i$, with $F^{(1)}_i$ the integral over $r > \epsilon$ and $F^{(2)}_i$ the integral over $r < \epsilon$ (MIT's eqs. (5.4) and (5.5)). The inner piece vanishes as $\epsilon \to 0$: with $|\sin\theta| \le 1$ and $|\cos\theta| \le 1$,

<Formula label="The bound on the inner piece" tex="\left|F^{(2)}_i[g]\right| \le \frac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^\pi\!\!\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\epsilon \max_{r < \epsilon}\left|\partial_i g\right|\,dr\,d\phi\,d\theta \le \text{const}\times\epsilon" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), eq. (5.6), p. 4" />

because the smoothness of $g$ keeps $\max|\partial_ig|$ bounded. In the outer piece the lower limit is above zero, so it may be integrated by parts. There is no surface term at infinity, because of how $g$ falls off, but there is one at $r = \epsilon$:

<Formula label="The outer piece after integrating by parts" tex="F^{(1)}_i[g] = -\int_{r > \epsilon}\partial_i\left[g(\vec r)\,\frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\right]d^3x + \int_{r > \epsilon}g(\vec r)\,\partial_i\left(\frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\right)d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), eq. (5.7), p. 5" />

<Part label="c" points={10}>

Evaluate the right-hand side of eq. (5.7) and show that, as $\epsilon \to 0$,

<Formula label="The derivative of the dipole potential as a distribution" tex="F^{(1)}_i[g] \to -\int g(\vec r)\left\{\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{3\left(\vec p \cdot \hat n\right)\hat n_i - p_i}{r^3} - \frac{1}{3\varepsilon_0}\,p_i\,\delta^3(\vec r)\right\}d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), eq. (5.8), p. 5 (with an overall minus sign added: our correction)" />

where the integral of the $1/r^3$ term means its limit over $r > \epsilon$. The braces hold the complete field $E_i$ of part (b), and $\partial_iV_{\text{dip}} = -E_i$. (MIT's eq. (5.8) prints the right-hand side without the overall minus sign, which would make it the distribution $E_i$ rather than $\partial_iV_{\text{dip}}$; the test with a spherically symmetric $g$ in the solution shows which sign is right.) <Answer id="p5c-surface" label="As $\epsilon \to 0$, the first term of eq. (5.7) (the surface term) tends to" choices={["zero, as the sphere shrinks", "+g(0) pᵢ/(3ε₀)", "−g(0) pᵢ/(3ε₀)", "+g(0) pᵢ/(4πε₀)"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The volume integral of a derivative is a surface integral. On the shrinking sphere the potential grows as $1/\epsilon^2$ while the area shrinks as $\epsilon^2$, so a finite piece survives: that is the delta-function term.

<Step title="The surface term">

The gradient theorem turns the first integral into an integral over the boundary of the region $r > \epsilon$. The only boundary that contributes is the sphere $r = \epsilon$, where $d\vec a = -\hat n\,\epsilon^2\,d\Omega$ (pointing out of the region, towards the origin). So

<Formula label="The surface term" tex="-\int_{r > \epsilon}\partial_i\left[g\,V_{\text{dip}}\right]d^3x = +\oint_{r = \epsilon}g\,\frac{\vec p \cdot \hat n}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\epsilon^2}\,\hat n_i\,\epsilon^2\,d\Omega \;\to\; \frac{g(0)\,p_j}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\hat n_j\hat n_i\,d\Omega = \frac{g(0)\,p_i}{3\varepsilon_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

The factors $\epsilon^2$ cancel, and $g(\epsilon\hat n)$ tends to $g(0)$ because $g$ is smooth.

</Step>

<Step title="The volume term">

For $r > 0$ the derivative is the one of part (a) with the sign of $-\vec E$: $\partial_iV_{\text{dip}} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{3(\vec p \cdot \hat n)\hat n_i - p_i}{r^3}$. So the second integral of eq. (5.7) is $-\int_{r > \epsilon}g\,\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{3(\vec p \cdot \hat n)\hat n_i - p_i}{r^3}\,d^3x$. It has a limit as $\epsilon \to 0$: near the origin $g$ differs from $g(0)$ by at most a constant times $r$; the part with $g(0)$ gives zero on every shell (part (b)), and the rest behaves at worst like $r \times r^{-3} \times r^2\,dr$, which is integrable.

</Step>

<Step title="Put together">

<Formula label="The derivative of the dipole potential, assembled" tex="F_i[g] = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0}F^{(1)}_i[g] = \frac{g(0)\,p_i}{3\varepsilon_0} - \lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\int_{r > \epsilon}g\,\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{3\left(\vec p \cdot \hat n\right)\hat n_i - p_i}{r^3}\,d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

and since $g(0) = \int g\,\delta^3(\vec r)\,d^3x$, this is the expression to be shown. As a distribution, $\partial_iV_{\text{dip}} = -E_i$ with the $E_i$ of part (b), delta term included.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $g = e^{-r^2/b^2}$, with $b$ any length, which is spherically symmetric. Then the $1/r^3$ term gives zero, and the result says $F_z = p\,g(0)/(3\varepsilon_0) = p/(3\varepsilon_0)$. Directly from eq. (5.3): $\partial_zg = -(2r/b^2)\cos\theta\,g$, so $F_z = \frac{2p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0b^2}\int_0^\infty r\,e^{-r^2/b^2}\,dr\int\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega = \frac{2p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0b^2} \times \frac{b^2}{2} \times \frac{4\pi}{3} = \frac{p}{3\varepsilon_0}$. The sign is positive, which is why the expression to be shown carries the overall minus sign.

</Step>
