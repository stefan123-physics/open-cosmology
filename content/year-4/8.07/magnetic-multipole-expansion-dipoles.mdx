The last notes expanded the electric potential of a localized charge in [traceless symmetric tensors](/courses/8-07/multipole-expansion-traceless-tensors). Lecture Notes 10, Guth's handwritten notes for Lecture 25 (November 7, 2012), are the first of the posted notes on magnetostatics, and they do the same for the vector potential of a localized current. The tensors are the same; what is new is a constraint that charges do not have: steady currents are conserved, and that removes the monopole term altogether and turns the next term into a single vector, the **magnetic dipole moment**. The notes then cover the dipole's field, force, torque and energy, and the first steps of magnetic fields in matter. (Our own words and our own worked example and figure. The heading on p. 1 speaks of an expansion "for a localized charge distribution", and p. 2 of the "radius of charge distribution"; they mean a current distribution, as everything that follows shows.)

## The expansion of one over the distance

Guth first recalls the result of the last notes (p. 1). For $r' < r$,

<Formula label="One over the distance in traceless tensors" tex="\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\ell - 1)!!}{\ell\,!}\,\frac{r'^\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}\,\{\hat r'_{i_1}\cdots\hat r'_{i_\ell}\}\,\{\hat r_{i_1}\cdots\hat r_{i_\ell}\}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

where $r = |\vec r|$, $\vec r = x_i\,\hat e_i$ and $\hat r_i = x_i/r$ (the last notes called this unit vector $\hat n$), the double factorial is $(2\ell - 1)!! = (2\ell - 1)(2\ell - 3)\cdots 1 = (2\ell)!/(2^\ell\,\ell\,!)$ with $(-1)!! = 1$, and the curly brackets take the traceless symmetric part: $\{1\} = 1$, $\{\hat r_i\} = \hat r_i$, $\{\hat r_i\hat r_j\} = \hat r_i\hat r_j - \frac13\delta_{ij}$, and so on.

## Expanding the vector potential

In magnetostatics each component of the vector potential is given by the same kind of integral as the electric potential, with $\mu_0/4\pi$ in place of $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0$ and a component of the current density in place of the charge density (p. 2; compare the [Biot–Savart law](/courses/8-02/biot-savart-law), which follows from it by taking the curl):

<Formula label="The vector potential of a current distribution" tex="A_j(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{J_j(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}\,d^3x'" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

For $|\vec r|$ larger than the radius of the current distribution, put in the expansion above. Each component of $\vec A$ becomes a multipole series, with one set of moments for each $j$ (p. 2):

<Formula label="The magnetic multipole expansion" tex="A_j(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{\ell + 1}}\,\mathcal M^{(\ell)}_{j;\,i_1\cdots i_\ell}\,\{\hat r_{i_1}\cdots\hat r_{i_\ell}\}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2 (boxed)" />

<Formula label="The magnetic multipole moments" tex="\mathcal M^{(\ell)}_{j;\,i_1\cdots i_\ell} = \frac{(2\ell - 1)!!}{\ell\,!}\int J_j(\vec r)\,\{x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_\ell}\}\,d^3x" cite="reading1" at="p. 2 (boxed)" />

The factor $r'^\ell$ has combined with the unit vectors, $r'^\ell\{\hat r'_{i_1}\cdots\hat r'_{i_\ell}\} = \{x'_{i_1}\cdots x'_{i_\ell}\}$, and Guth drops the primes inside the integral, where $\vec r$ is now just the integration variable. The index before the semicolon says which component of $\vec J$ is integrated; the $\ell$ indices after it are those of the traceless tensor. (The notes write the script letter M; it is not the magnetization of the last sections.)

## What current conservation allows

A steady current is conserved, $\partial J_i/\partial x_i = 0$, and this restricts the moments (pp. 2–3). Multiply by any product of coordinates and integrate over all space:

<Formula label="Integrating the conservation law" tex="\int\frac{\partial J_j}{\partial x_j}\,x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_\ell}\,d^3x = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

Integrate by parts; the surface term vanishes because the current is zero outside a finite region. The derivative then falls on the coordinates, and each $x_{i_k}$ in turn becomes a Kronecker delta:

<Formula label="After integrating by parts" tex="-\int J_j\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_\ell}\right)d^3x = -\int J_j\left(\delta_{i_1 j}\,x_{i_2}\cdots x_{i_\ell} + \delta_{i_2 j}\,x_{i_1}x_{i_3}\cdots x_{i_\ell} + \cdots\right)d^3x = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The deltas turn $J_j$ into $J_{i_1}$, $J_{i_2}$ and so on, so the integrand is a sum of $\ell$ terms, each with one factor of $\vec J$ and $\ell - 1$ coordinates, the index of the $J$ moving through all positions (p. 3):

<Formula label="The sum of the terms" tex="-\int\left(J_{i_1}\,x_{i_2}\cdots x_{i_\ell} + x_{i_1}J_{i_2}\,x_{i_3}\cdots x_{i_\ell} + \cdots\right)d^3x = -\ell\int\text{Sym}_{i_1\cdots i_\ell}\left(x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_{\ell - 1}}J_{i_\ell}\right)d^3x" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

Here Sym means: sum over all $\ell\,!$ permutations of the indices and divide by $\ell\,!$, that is, average over the permutations. The $\ell$ terms of the sum are the distinct positions of the $J$, and each appears equally often among the permutations, so their sum is $\ell$ times the average. Guth's conclusion, one identity for each $\ell \geq 1$ (p. 3, boxed):

<Formula label="The constraint of current conservation" tex="\int\text{Sym}_{i_1\cdots i_\ell}\left(x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_{\ell - 1}}J_{i_\ell}\right)d^3x = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3 (boxed)" />

The first three cases, with their traces (p. 3):

<Formula label="The first three cases" tex="\int J_i\,d^3x = 0, \qquad \int\left(J_i\,x_j + J_j\,x_i\right)d^3x = 0, \qquad \int\left(J_i\,x_j x_k + J_j\,x_i x_k + J_k\,x_i x_j\right)d^3x = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

<Formula label="Their traces" tex="\int\vec r \cdot \vec J\,d^3x = 0, \qquad \int\left[J_i\,r^2 + 2x_i\left(\vec J \cdot \vec r\right)\right]d^3x = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The trace of the second identity is taken with $\delta_{ij}$, that of the third with $\delta_{jk}$. Note how the counting works: the identity with $\ell$ indices has $\ell - 1$ coordinates, so it constrains the moments of degree $\ell - 1$.

## No monopole, and the dipole moment

Now apply this to the moments (p. 4). For $\ell = 0$ the moment is just the integral of the current, which the first identity sets to zero:

<Formula label="No magnetic monopole term" tex="\mathcal M^{(0)}_j \propto \int J_j\,d^3x = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

So there is no $1/r$ term in $\vec A$: a localized steady current has no magnetic "charge". For $\ell = 1$ the coefficient $(2\ell - 1)!!/\ell\,!$ is 1 and $\{x_i\} = x_i$:

<Formula label="The moments of degree one" tex="\mathcal M^{(1)}_{j;\,i} = \int J_j\,x_i\,d^3x" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

The second identity says that the symmetric part of this $3 \times 3$ array vanishes. It must therefore be **antisymmetric**, $\mathcal M^{(1)}_{i;\,j} = -\mathcal M^{(1)}_{j;\,i}$ (p. 4). An antisymmetric tensor has only three independent components and is equivalent to a vector, using the Levi-Civita symbol $\epsilon_{ijk}$ (see [rotations in index notation](/courses/8-07/rotations-index-notation)). This defines the **magnetic dipole moment** (p. 4):

<Formula label="The magnetic dipole moment" tex="m_i = \frac12\,\epsilon_{ijk}\int x_j\,J_k\,d^3x, \qquad \vec m = \frac12\int\vec r \times \vec J\,d^3x" cite="reading1" at="p. 4 (boxed)" />

In terms of the moments, and back again (p. 4):

<Formula label="Between the vector and the tensor" tex="m_i = \frac12\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,\mathcal M^{(1)}_{k;\,j}, \qquad \mathcal M^{(1)}_{j;\,i} = m_k\,\epsilon_{kij}" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

The proof of the second form (p. 5) uses the identity $\epsilon_{kij}\epsilon_{kmn} = \delta_{im}\delta_{jn} - \delta_{in}\delta_{jm}$:

<Formula label="The proof" tex="m_k\,\epsilon_{kij} = \epsilon_{kij}\,\frac12\,\epsilon_{kmn}\,\mathcal M^{(1)}_{n;\,m} = \frac12\left[\delta_{im}\delta_{jn} - \delta_{in}\delta_{jm}\right]\mathcal M^{(1)}_{n;\,m} = \frac12\left(\mathcal M^{(1)}_{j;\,i} - \mathcal M^{(1)}_{i;\,j}\right) = \mathcal M^{(1)}_{j;\,i}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

where the last step uses the antisymmetry. [The notes write the indices of $\mathcal M^{(1)}_{n;\,m}$ in this proof with a comma instead of the semicolon, and go from the bracket straight to $\mathcal M^{(1)}_{j;\,i}$; the middle step, which uses the antisymmetry, is ours.] Putting this into the expansion, the $\ell = 1$ term is $m_k\,\epsilon_{kij}\,\hat r_i/r^2$, which is the $j$ component of $\vec m \times \hat r$ divided by $r^2$ (p. 5):

<Formula label="The vector potential of a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec A(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{\vec m \times \hat r}{r^2} + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="p. 5 (boxed)" />

the leading term of $\vec A$ far from any localized steady current, unless $\vec m$ happens to vanish. (A remark of ours: because $\int\vec J\,d^3x = 0$, moving the origin by a constant vector changes $\int\vec r \times \vec J\,d^3x$ by that vector crossed into zero, so $\vec m$ does not depend on the origin. The next notes take this up.)

## Currents in wires: m = I a

For a current in a thin wire, integrating $\vec J$ over the cross-section of a short piece $d\vec\ell$ of the wire gives the current $I$ times $d\vec\ell$, so (p. 5)

<Formula label="The moment of a wire loop" tex="\vec J\,d^3x \to I\,d\vec\ell, \qquad \vec m = \frac12\,I\oint\vec r \times d\vec\ell" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

Guth then puts this in another form with [Stokes' theorem](/courses/18-02/stokes-theorem) (p. 6). For any vector field $\vec v$, a closed loop and a surface $S$ bounded by it,

<Formula label="Stokes' theorem in index notation" tex="\oint\vec v \cdot d\vec\ell = \int_{S}\left(\nabla \times \vec v\right)\cdot d\vec a, \qquad \oint v_i\,d\ell_i = \int_{S}\epsilon_{ijk}\left(\frac{\partial v_k}{\partial x_j}\right)da_i" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

The $m$ component of the line integral of $\vec r \times d\vec\ell$ is of this form, with $v_i = \epsilon_{imn}x_n$. Since $\partial x_n/\partial x_j = \delta_{jn}$ (p. 6),

<Formula label="The line integral as a surface integral" tex="\oint\left[\vec r \times d\vec\ell\right]_m = \oint\epsilon_{mnp}\,x_n\,d\ell_p = \oint\left(\epsilon_{imn}\,x_n\right)d\ell_i = \int_{S}\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{kmj}\,da_i = \int_{S}\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{mjk}\,da_i = 2\int_{S} da_m" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

using $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{mjk} = 2\delta_{im}$. So half the line integral is the **vector area** $\vec a = \int_{S} d\vec a$ of any surface bounded by the loop, and (p. 6)

<Formula label="The moment of a loop is the current times the vector area" tex="\frac12\oint\vec r \times d\vec\ell = \int_{S} d\vec a = \vec a, \qquad \vec m = I\,\vec a" cite="reading1" at="p. 6 (boxed)" />

For a loop in a plane, $|\vec a|$ is the area and $\vec a$ is perpendicular to the plane, with its sense given by the right-hand rule around the current: the current times the area of [8.02](/courses/8-02/magnetic-dipoles), for a single turn. The result holds for loops that are not flat too, and $\vec a$ is the same for every surface bounded by the loop, since the left-hand side knows only the loop.

<WorkedExample title="A loop that is not flat">
<Problem>
A wire runs along six of the twelve edges of a cube of side $s = 4$ cm, through the corners $(0, 0, 0)$, $(s, 0, 0)$, $(s, s, 0)$, $(s, s, s)$, $(0, s, s)$, $(0, 0, s)$ and back to the origin, carrying $I = 3$ A in that order. Find its magnetic dipole moment, and the dipole field 50 cm from the centre of the cube along the $z$ direction.
</Problem>
<Step title="The line integral, edge by edge">
On a straight edge from corner $\vec P$ to corner $\vec Q$, $\vec r = \vec P + t(\vec Q - \vec P)$ with $t$ from 0 to 1 and $d\vec\ell = (\vec Q - \vec P)\,dt$, so $\vec r \times d\vec\ell = \vec P \times \vec Q\,dt$ and the edge contributes $\vec P \times \vec Q$. The two edges that start or end at the origin give zero. The others give $s^2(0, 0, 1)$, $s^2(1, -1, 0)$, $s^2(0, -1, 1)$ and $s^2(1, 0, 0)$, adding up to $2s^2(1, -1, 1)$. So $\vec m = \frac12 I \cdot 2s^2(1, -1, 1) = I s^2(1, -1, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="The same from the vector area">
The vector area can be read off from the shadows of the loop on the three coordinate planes. Seen from $+z$, the path goes once counterclockwise around a square of side $s$, so $a_z = s^2$; seen from $+x$, it again goes counterclockwise, $a_x = s^2$; seen from $+y$ it goes clockwise, $a_y = -s^2$. The loop encloses no flat area, yet $\vec a = s^2(1, -1, 1)$, of length $\sqrt3\,s^2$.
</Step>
<Step title="The numbers">
$I s^2 = 3 \times (0.04)^2 = 4.8 \times 10^{-3}$ A m², so $\vec m = 4.8 \times 10^{-3}(1, -1, 1)$ A m² and $|\vec m| = 8.31 \times 10^{-3}$ A m². By the remark above the result does not depend on the corner chosen as origin.
</Step>
<Step title="The far field">
50 cm is more than ten times the size of the loop, so the dipole term dominates. With $\hat r = \hat z$ and $m_0 = 4.8 \times 10^{-3}$ A m², the formula of the next section gives $3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec m = m_0\left[3\hat z - (1, -1, 1)\right] = m_0(-1, 1, 2)$, and $\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{m_0}{r^3} = 10^{-7} \times 4.8 \times 10^{-3}/0.125 = 3.84$ nT. So $\vec B = (-3.84, 3.84, 7.68)$ nT, of magnitude $3.84\sqrt6 = 9.41$ nT, tilted away from the $z$ axis because $\vec m$ does not point along it.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The field of a magnetic dipole

The magnetic field of a dipole, worked out in Problem 7 of Problem Set 7 (p. 7), is

<Formula label="The field of a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec B_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec m}{r^3} + \frac{2\mu_0}{3}\,\vec m\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="p. 7 (boxed)" />

Away from the origin the first term is the curl of the dipole vector potential, the same shape as the field of an electric dipole. The second term lives only at the dipole itself. A way to see that it must be there (our remark, not in the notes): for any current distribution inside a sphere, the integral of $\vec B$ over the sphere is $\frac23\mu_0\vec m$, while the first term averages to zero over directions, so for a point dipole the whole integral must sit at the origin.

  ]}

**Why the delta-function term matters.** Guth's example is hydrogen in its ground state (p. 7). The electron's orbit there has $\ell = 0$, zero orbital angular momentum, so its probability density is spherically symmetric. The spins of the proton and the electron interact by the dipole-dipole interaction, and there are two states: spins aligned and antialigned. Their energy difference depends on the average proton field $\vec B$ experienced by the electron. Averaged over directions, $\hat r_i\hat r_j$ gives $\frac13\delta_{ij}$, so the first term averages to $\vec m - \vec m = 0$ on every spherical shell, and the energy difference of the transition comes 100% from the delta-function term (which is therefore weighted by the electron's probability density at the proton, our addition). This transition is the **21 cm line**, which Guth calls astronomically crucial: the Galaxy is mapped by it. The quantum mechanics of it is on the 8.05 page on [hyperfine splitting](/courses/8-05/hyperfine-21cm-adding-angular-momenta).

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A delta function that maps galaxies">
Radio waves at 21 cm pass through the dust that hides much of a galaxy's starlight, and the neutral hydrogen that emits them often extends far beyond the visible disc. The Doppler shifts of the line therefore trace how fast the gas orbits far from the centre. Those rotation curves stay flat where the visible matter has run out, one of the main pieces of evidence for dark matter (see [rotation curves](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/rotation-curves)). In the notes Guth only says that the Galaxy is mapped by the line; the step to dark matter is ours. Every one of those measurements rests on the energy of the delta-function term of this page.
</Callout>

## Force, torque and energy

Guth recalls the results for an electric dipole $\vec p$ in a field $\vec E$ (p. 8): the force is $(\vec p \cdot \nabla)\vec E$, or $\nabla(\vec p \cdot \vec E)$, and

<Formula label="An electric dipole" tex="\vec F = \nabla\left(\vec p \cdot \vec E\right), \qquad \vec\tau = \vec p \times \vec E + \vec r \times \vec F, \qquad U = -\vec p \cdot \vec E" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

The two forms of the force agree in electrostatics, where $\nabla \times \vec E = 0$ (with $\vec p$ held fixed, $\nabla(\vec p \cdot \vec E) = (\vec p \cdot \nabla)\vec E + \vec p \times (\nabla \times \vec E)$). The torque about the origin is the torque about the dipole's own position plus $\vec r \times \vec F$, the torque of the net force applied at $\vec r$. For a magnetic dipole (p. 8):

<Formula label="A magnetic dipole" tex="\vec F = \nabla\left(\vec m \cdot \vec B\right), \qquad \vec\tau = \vec m \times \vec B + \vec r \times \vec F, \qquad U = -\vec m \cdot \vec B" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

Here only the gradient form is given; it equals $(\vec m \cdot \nabla)\vec B$ where no current flows at the dipole, since then $\nabla \times \vec B = 0$. These are the results of the [8.02 page on magnetic dipoles](/courses/8-02/magnetic-dipoles), where the dipole moment is written with the Greek letter mu instead of $\vec m$; the next notes derive them.

## Magnetization and bound currents

Matter contains magnetic dipoles: orbiting electrons and their spins. Averaged over a small volume they are described by the **magnetization** (p. 8),

<Formula label="Magnetization" tex="\vec M = \text{magnetic dipole moment per unit volume}" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

A magnetized object produces the same field as a set of **bound currents** (p. 8): a volume current density and a surface current,

<Formula label="Bound currents" tex="\vec J_b = \nabla \times \vec M, \qquad \vec K_b = \vec M \times \hat n" cite="reading1" at="p. 8" />

where $\hat n$ is the outward normal of the surface and $\vec K$ is a current per unit length across the direction of flow on the surface. The notes state these without proof (the course textbook, Griffiths, derives them from the dipole vector potential in Section 6.2.1). The picture: in a uniformly magnetized slab the little current loops cancel inside, where neighbours flow in opposite directions, so $\nabla \times \vec M = 0$ there, but at the edge nothing cancels them, and a current runs around the surface.

## Maxwell's equations in matter

The magnetostatic equations hold for the total current (p. 9):

<Formula label="Magnetostatics" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0, \qquad \nabla \times \vec B = \mu_0\vec J" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

Now split the current into the free current, which we control, and the bound current (p. 9): $\vec J = \vec J_f + \vec J_b = \vec J_f + \nabla \times \vec M$. Moving the bound part to the left suggests the **auxiliary field**

<Formula label="The field H" tex="\vec H = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\vec B - \vec M" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

which obeys (p. 9)

<Formula label="Magnetostatics in matter" tex="\nabla \times \vec H = \vec J_f, \qquad \nabla \cdot \vec B = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

In **linear materials** the magnetization is proportional to $\vec H$, with the **magnetic susceptibility** $\chi_m$ (p. 9):

<Formula label="Linear materials" tex="\vec M = \chi_m\,\vec H" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

The notes end here; the next lecture continues with magnetic dipoles and fields in matter.

## Summary

- **The expansion:** with $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ in traceless tensors, $A_j = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\sum_\ell \mathcal M^{(\ell)}_{j;\,i_1\cdots i_\ell}\{\hat r_{i_1}\cdots\hat r_{i_\ell}\}/r^{\ell + 1}$, with moments $\frac{(2\ell - 1)!!}{\ell\,!}\int J_j\{x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_\ell}\}\,d^3x$.
- **Current conservation:** integrating $\partial J_i/\partial x_i = 0$ against products of coordinates gives $\int\text{Sym}(x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_{\ell - 1}}J_{i_\ell})\,d^3x = 0$: $\int\vec J\,d^3x = 0$, so there is no monopole term, and the $\ell = 1$ moments are antisymmetric.
- **The dipole:** $\vec m = \frac12\int\vec r \times \vec J\,d^3x$ and $\vec A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\vec m \times \hat r/r^2$; for a wire loop, by Stokes' theorem, $\vec m = I\vec a$, the current times the vector area, flat loop or not. Example: a 3 A loop along six edges of a 4 cm cube has $\vec m = 4.8 \times 10^{-3}(1, -1, 1)$ A m².
- **The field:** $\vec B_{\text{dip}} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}[3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec m]/r^3 + \frac{2\mu_0}{3}\vec m\,\delta^3(\vec r)$; in hydrogen's ground state the first term averages out, so the 21 cm splitting comes entirely from the delta-function term.
- **Force, torque, energy:** $\vec F = \nabla(\vec m \cdot \vec B)$, $\vec\tau = \vec m \times \vec B + \vec r \times \vec F$, $U = -\vec m \cdot \vec B$, as for an electric dipole.
- **Matter:** $\vec M$ is the dipole moment per volume, with bound currents $\vec J_b = \nabla \times \vec M$ and $\vec K_b = \vec M \times \hat n$; $\vec H = \vec B/\mu_0 - \vec M$ obeys $\nabla \times \vec H = \vec J_f$, and $\vec M = \chi_m\vec H$ in linear materials.
