The [first page](/courses/8-07/wave-equation-group-velocity) on these notes treated waves in general. The rest of Guth's slides for Lecture 35 (slides 8–19) show that Maxwell's equations have such waves, work out how their electric and magnetic fields are related and what energy and momentum they carry, and then send them through matter and across a boundary between two materials. Maxwell's equations themselves were derived earlier in the course; the 8.02 page on [Maxwell's equations and light](/courses/8-02/maxwell-equations-and-em-waves) has them, and the 8.03 pages on [electromagnetic waves](/courses/8-03/electromagnetic-waves) and [light in matter](/courses/8-03/em-waves-in-matter-brewster) cover much of this page at an earlier level. (Our own words and worked example.)

## The wave equation from Maxwell's equations

With no charges and no currents, Maxwell's equations read (slide 8)

<Formula label="Maxwell's equations in empty space" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec E = 0, \qquad \nabla \times \vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla \cdot \vec B = 0, \qquad \nabla \times \vec B = \frac{1}{c^2}\,\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="slide 8, eq. (14)" />

where $1/c^2$ stands for $\mu_0\varepsilon_0$ (slide 8). Take the curl of Faraday's law. On the left, the vector identity for a curl of a curl gives $\nabla(\nabla \cdot \vec E) - \nabla^2\vec E$, and the first term vanishes by Gauss's law. On the right, the curl passes through the time derivative and the fourth equation replaces $\nabla \times \vec B$ (slide 8):

<Formula label="The curl of Faraday's law" tex="\nabla \times \left(\nabla \times \vec E\right) = \nabla\left(\nabla \cdot \vec E\right) - \nabla^2\vec E = \nabla \times \left(-\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}\right) = -\frac{1}{c^2}\,\frac{\partial^2\vec E}{\partial t^2}" cite="reading1" at="slide 8, eq. (15)" />

So (slide 9)

<Formula label="The wave equation for the electric field" tex="\nabla^2\vec E - \frac{1}{c^2}\,\frac{\partial^2\vec E}{\partial t^2} = 0" cite="reading1" at="slide 9, eq. (16) (boxed)" />

the wave equation in three dimensions. The curl of the fourth equation gives the same for the magnetic field (slide 9):

<Formula label="The wave equation for the magnetic field" tex="\nabla^2\vec B - \frac{1}{c^2}\,\frac{\partial^2\vec B}{\partial t^2} = 0" cite="reading1" at="slide 9, eq. (17) (boxed)" />

Each component of $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ obeys the wave equation with speed $c$, so electromagnetic waves travel at $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$, which is the speed of light (slide 10). But the wave equation is not the whole story: $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ must still satisfy Maxwell's first-order equations, and these relate them (slide 10).

## Plane waves are transverse

Try a plane wave (slide 10):

<Formula label="A plane wave" tex="\vec E(\vec r, t) = \tilde E_0\,e^{i(\vec k \cdot \vec r - \omega t)}\,\hat n" cite="reading1" at="slide 10, eq. (18) (boxed)" />

where $\tilde E_0$ is a complex amplitude, the unit vector $\hat n$ is the direction of the field (its **polarization**), and $\omega/|\vec k| = v_{\text{phase}} = c$, as the wave equation demands. The wave travels along $\vec k$ and is the same everywhere on each plane perpendicular to it, hence the name. Gauss's law tests it (slide 10):

<Formula label="The divergence of a plane wave" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec E = i\,\hat n \cdot \vec k\,\tilde E_0\,e^{i(\vec k \cdot \vec r - \omega t)}" cite="reading1" at="slide 10, eq. (19)" />

since each derivative $\partial/\partial x_j$ of the exponential brings down $ik_j$. For this to vanish everywhere we need (slide 10)

<Formula label="Transversality" tex="\hat n \cdot \vec k = 0" cite="reading1" at="slide 10, eq. (20) (boxed)" />

The field points across the direction of travel: electromagnetic waves are **transverse** waves. Sound in air, by contrast, is longitudinal.

## The magnetic field of a plane wave

Faraday's law gives the magnetic field. For a plane wave, $\nabla$ acts as $i\vec k$ (slide 11):

<Formula label="Faraday's law for a plane wave" tex="\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t} = -\nabla \times \vec E = -i\vec k \times \vec E = -i\vec k \times \hat n\,\tilde E_0\,e^{i(\vec k \cdot \vec r - \omega t)}" cite="reading1" at="slide 11, eq. (21)" />

Integrating in time divides by $-i\omega$ (a constant field could be added, but it is no part of the wave) (slide 11):

<Formula label="The magnetic field" tex="\vec B = \frac{\vec k}{\omega} \times \hat n\,\tilde E_0\,e^{i(\vec k \cdot \vec r - \omega t)}" cite="reading1" at="slide 11, eq. (22)" />

and since $|\vec k| = \omega/c$ [the slide has $\omega c$, a slip], with $\hat k = \vec k/|\vec k|$ (slide 11):

<Formula label="The magnetic field of a plane wave" tex="\vec B = \frac{1}{c}\,\hat k \times \vec E" cite="reading1" at="slide 11, eq. (23) (boxed)" />

So $\vec B$ is perpendicular to both $\vec E$ and $\vec k$, oscillates in phase with $\vec E$, and is smaller by a factor of $c$. The other two Maxwell equations are then satisfied too (our check): $\nabla \cdot \vec B$ brings down $i\vec k \cdot (\vec k \times \hat n) = 0$, and $\nabla \times \vec B = i\vec k \times (\vec k \times \hat n)\,\tilde E_0 e^{i(\vec k \cdot \vec r - \omega t)}/\omega = -i(k^2/\omega)\vec E$, which is $(1/c^2)\,\partial\vec E/\partial t = -i(\omega/c^2)\vec E$ because $k^2 = \omega^2/c^2$.

## Energy and momentum

The energy density of the fields is (slide 12)

<Formula label="The energy density" tex="u = \frac12\left[\varepsilon_0|\vec E|^2 + \frac{1}{\mu_0}|\vec B|^2\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 12, eq. (24)" />

(for the real fields, the real parts of the complex ones). In a plane wave the electric and magnetic parts are equal, since $|\vec B|^2/\mu_0 = |\vec E|^2/\mu_0c^2 = \varepsilon_0|\vec E|^2$ (slide 12). For a wave travelling along $\vec k = k\,\hat z$, with real amplitude $E_0$ and phase $\delta$, the energy density, the Poynting vector (the energy flux), the momentum density and the intensity are (slide 12)

<Formula label="Energy, flux, momentum and intensity of a plane wave" tex="u = \varepsilon_0E_0^2\cos^2(kz - \omega t + \delta), \qquad \vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\vec E \times \vec B = uc\,\hat z, \qquad \vec{\mathcal P}_{\text{EM}} = \frac{1}{c^2}\,\vec S = \frac{u}{c}\,\hat z, \qquad I = \left\langle|\vec S|\right\rangle = \frac12 c\,\varepsilon_0E_0^2" cite="reading1" at="slide 12, eq. (25)" />

The average of $\cos^2$ over a period is $\frac12$ (slide 12). [The slide writes the intensity as $\frac12\varepsilon_0E_0^2$, which is the average energy density; the energy flux is $c$ times that, as written here.] The energy flows along $\hat z$ at speed $c$, which is why $\vec S = uc\,\hat z$, and the momentum density is the energy flux divided by $c^2$, so light carries momentum $u/c$ per unit volume, in step with a photon, whose momentum is its energy divided by $c$.

## Electromagnetic waves in matter

In a linear, homogeneous material with no free charges or currents, Maxwell's equations in terms of $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$ and $\vec H = \vec B/\mu$ have the same form as in vacuum, with $\mu_0\varepsilon_0$ replaced by $\mu\varepsilon$ (slide 13). Define the **index of refraction** (slide 13)

<Formula label="The index of refraction" tex="n = \sqrt{\frac{\mu\varepsilon}{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}" cite="reading1" at="slide 13, eq. (26) (boxed)" />

Then every wave travels at (slide 13)

<Formula label="The speed of light in matter" tex="v = \frac{c}{n}" cite="reading1" at="slide 13, eq. (27) (boxed)" />

since $1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon} = c/n$. Written in $\vec E$ and $\vec B$, everything above carries over with $\varepsilon_0 \to \varepsilon$, $\mu_0 \to \mu$ and $c \to c/n$ (slide 14):

<Formula label="The plane wave in matter" tex="u = \frac12\left[\varepsilon|\vec E|^2 + \frac{1}{\mu}|\vec B|^2\right], \qquad \vec B = \frac{n}{c}\,\hat k \times \vec E, \qquad \vec S = \frac{1}{\mu}\,\vec E \times \vec B = \frac{uc}{n}\,\hat z" cite="reading1" at="slide 14, eq. (28)" />

The energy still flows at the speed of the wave, now $c/n$.

## Boundary conditions

At a flat interface between two materials, with no free charge or current on it, the fields obey (slide 16)

<Formula label="Boundary conditions at an interface" tex="\varepsilon_1E_{1\perp} = \varepsilon_2E_{2\perp}, \qquad \vec E_{1\parallel} = \vec E_{2\parallel}, \qquad B_{1\perp} = B_{2\perp}, \qquad \frac{1}{\mu_1}\,\vec B_{1\parallel} = \frac{1}{\mu_2}\,\vec B_{2\parallel}" cite="reading1" at="slide 16, eq. (29)" />

where $\perp$ marks the component normal to the interface and $\parallel$ the components along it. They follow from the integral forms of Maxwell's equations applied to a thin pillbox and a thin loop straddling the surface.

The setup (slide 15, described in words, our own): the interface is the plane $z = 0$, material 1 on the left ($z < 0$), material 2 on the right. A wave comes in from the left along $+z$, with $\vec E$ along $\hat x$ and $\vec B$ along $\hat y$; part of it is transmitted into material 2, moving along $+z$, and part is reflected, moving back along $-z$ with $\vec E$ still along $\hat x$.

## Reflection and transmission at normal incidence

The incident wave, for $z < 0$ (slide 17), uses $\vec B = (1/v_1)\,\hat z \times \vec E$:

<Formula label="The incident wave" tex="\vec E_I(z, t) = \tilde E_{0,I}\,e^{i(k_1z - \omega t)}\,\hat x, \qquad \vec B_I(z, t) = \frac{1}{v_1}\,\tilde E_{0,I}\,e^{i(k_1z - \omega t)}\,\hat y" cite="reading1" at="slide 17, eq. (30)" />

The transmitted wave, for $z > 0$ (slide 17):

<Formula label="The transmitted wave" tex="\vec E_T(z, t) = \tilde E_{0,T}\,e^{i(k_2z - \omega t)}\,\hat x, \qquad \vec B_T(z, t) = \frac{1}{v_2}\,\tilde E_{0,T}\,e^{i(k_2z - \omega t)}\,\hat y" cite="reading1" at="slide 17, eq. (31)" />

The boundary conditions must hold at $z = 0$ at every moment, so all three waves share the same $\omega$; the wave numbers differ (slide 17):

<Formula label="The same frequency on both sides" tex="\frac{\omega}{k_1} = v_1 = \frac{c}{n_1}, \qquad \frac{\omega}{k_2} = v_2 = \frac{c}{n_2}" cite="reading1" at="slide 17, eq. (32)" />

The frequency stays, the wavelength changes. The reflected wave, for $z < 0$, moves along $-\hat z$, so $\vec B = (1/v_1)(-\hat z) \times \vec E$ points along $-\hat y$ (slide 18):

<Formula label="The reflected wave" tex="\vec E_R(z, t) = \tilde E_{0,R}\,e^{i(-k_1z - \omega t)}\,\hat x, \qquad \vec B_R(z, t) = -\frac{1}{v_1}\,\tilde E_{0,R}\,e^{i(-k_1z - \omega t)}\,\hat y" cite="reading1" at="slide 18, eq. (33)" />

All fields lie along the interface, so only the parallel conditions matter. At $z = 0$ the exponentials are all $e^{-i\omega t}$ and cancel, leaving (slide 18)

<Formula label="Continuity of the electric field" tex="\vec E_{1\parallel} = \vec E_{2\parallel} \;\Longrightarrow\; \tilde E_{0,I} + \tilde E_{0,R} = \tilde E_{0,T}" cite="reading1" at="slide 18, eq. (34)" />

<Formula label="Continuity of the magnetic field over mu" tex="\frac{1}{\mu_1}\,\vec B_{1\parallel} = \frac{1}{\mu_2}\,\vec B_{2\parallel} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{1}{\mu_1}\left(\frac{1}{v_1}\,\tilde E_{0,I} - \frac{1}{v_1}\,\tilde E_{0,R}\right) = \frac{1}{\mu_2}\,\frac{1}{v_2}\,\tilde E_{0,T}" cite="reading1" at="slide 18, eq. (35)" />

Two equations in two unknowns, $\tilde E_{0,R}$ and $\tilde E_{0,T}$ (slide 19). Call $\beta = \mu_1v_1/\mu_2v_2$, so that (35) reads $\tilde E_{0,I} - \tilde E_{0,R} = \beta\,\tilde E_{0,T}$. Adding (34) gives $\tilde E_{0,T} = 2\tilde E_{0,I}/(1 + \beta)$, and then $\tilde E_{0,R} = (1 - \beta)\,\tilde E_{0,I}/(1 + \beta)$. For most transparent materials $\mu$ is very close to $\mu_0$; with $\mu_1 = \mu_2$, $\beta = v_1/v_2 = n_2/n_1$ and (slide 19)

<Formula label="Reflected and transmitted amplitudes" tex="\tilde E_{0,R} = \left(\frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2}\right)\tilde E_{0,I}, \qquad \tilde E_{0,T} = \left(\frac{2n_1}{n_1 + n_2}\right)\tilde E_{0,I}" cite="reading1" at="slide 19, eq. (36)" />

[The slide puts absolute-value bars around the first fraction, drops the tilde on $\tilde E_{0,T}$ (as eq. (19) drops it on $\tilde E_0$), and leaves the assumption $\mu_1 = \mu_2$ unstated. Without the bars the sign carries real information, so we keep it.] The transmitted wave is always in phase with the incident one. The reflected wave is in phase when $n_2 < n_1$ and inverted, a phase shift of $\pi$, when the second material is optically denser, $n_2 > n_1$: just as a pulse on a string reflects inverted from a heavier string. In the limit $n_2 \to n_1$ nothing is reflected, as it should be.

How does the energy divide? The intensity in a material is $\frac12\varepsilon v E_0^2 = \frac12 n\,\varepsilon_0 c\,E_0^2$ (for $\mu = \mu_0$), so the reflected and transmitted fractions of the power are (our remark)

<Formula label="Reflected and transmitted fractions of the power" tex="R = \left(\frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2}\right)^2, \qquad T = \frac{n_2}{n_1}\left(\frac{2n_1}{n_1 + n_2}\right)^2 = \frac{4n_1n_2}{(n_1 + n_2)^2}, \qquad R + T = 1" cite="reading1" at="slide 19, from eq. (36) and slide 14, eq. (28)" />

<WorkedExample title="A laser beam on a glass window">
<Problem>
A laser beam in air ($n_1 = 1.00$) has an intensity of 1000 W/m², about that of bright sunlight. Find the amplitudes of its electric and magnetic fields and its average energy density, then let it fall at normal incidence on glass with $n_2 = 1.50$ (and $\mu = \mu_0$): find the reflected and transmitted amplitudes and powers.
</Problem>
<Step title="The fields">
From $I = \frac12 c\,\varepsilon_0E_0^2$, $E_0 = \sqrt{2I/c\varepsilon_0} = \sqrt{2000/(2.998 \times 10^8 \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12})} = 868$ V/m. The magnetic amplitude is $B_0 = E_0/c = 868/2.998 \times 10^8 = 2.90 \times 10^{-6}$ T, about a twentieth of a typical 50 μT Earth field.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy and momentum">
The average energy density is $\langle u\rangle = I/c = 1000/2.998 \times 10^8 = 3.34 \times 10^{-6}$ J/m³. The momentum density is $\langle u\rangle/c$, and it arrives at speed $c$, so a surface that absorbs the beam receives a momentum per unit area per second of $\langle u\rangle$: a radiation pressure of $3.34 \times 10^{-6}$ Pa (our application of the momentum density).
</Step>
<Step title="The amplitudes at the glass">
$\tilde E_{0,R}/\tilde E_{0,I} = (1.00 - 1.50)/(1.00 + 1.50) = -0.2$, so the reflected field has amplitude $0.2 \times 868 = 174$ V/m and is inverted. $\tilde E_{0,T}/\tilde E_{0,I} = 2 \times 1.00/2.50 = 0.8$, so the field in the glass has amplitude $0.8 \times 868 = 694$ V/m. Check (34): $1 + (-0.2) = 0.8$.
</Step>
<Step title="The powers">
$R = 0.2^2 = 0.04$: 40 W/m² is reflected. $T = 1.50 \times 0.8^2 = 0.96$: 960 W/m² enters the glass. Together they make the incident 1000 W/m², as energy conservation requires, though the transmitted field is weaker than the incident one: in the glass, the factor $n$ in the intensity makes up the difference. This 4% loss at each surface is why lenses are coated.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The pressure of a gas of light">
A remark of ours, not in the notes. A plane wave carries momentum density $u/c$ along its direction, which crosses a surface facing it as a momentum flux $u$. A wave at angle $\theta$ to the surface's normal carries a normal momentum flux $u\cos^2\theta$, and for radiation moving equally in all directions the average of $\cos^2\theta$ is $\frac13$. So a gas of light, such as the [black-body radiation](/courses/8-286/black-body-radiation) that fills the universe as the cosmic microwave background, has pressure $p = u/3$. In 8.286 Guth reaches the same pressure by another route: radiation's energy density falls as $1/a^4$, and energy conservation with $dU = -p\,dV$ then demands $p = u/3$, the pressure that enters the second Friedmann equation ([matter–radiation equality and the pressure of light](/courses/8-286/matter-radiation-equality)).
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Wave equation:** the curl of Faraday's law with $\nabla \cdot \vec E = 0$ gives $\nabla^2\vec E - (1/c^2)\,\partial^2\vec E/\partial t^2 = 0$, and the same for $\vec B$, with $1/c^2 = \mu_0\varepsilon_0$.
- **Plane waves:** $\vec E = \tilde E_0\,e^{i(\vec k \cdot \vec r - \omega t)}\,\hat n$ with $\omega/|\vec k| = c$; Gauss's law requires $\hat n \cdot \vec k = 0$ (transverse), and Faraday's law gives $\vec B = (1/c)\,\hat k \times \vec E$.
- **Energy and momentum:** $u = \frac12[\varepsilon_0|\vec E|^2 + |\vec B|^2/\mu_0]$ with equal parts; $\vec S = uc\,\hat z$, momentum density $(u/c)\,\hat z$, intensity $I = \frac12 c\,\varepsilon_0E_0^2$.
- **Matter:** $\mu_0\varepsilon_0 \to \mu\varepsilon$, $n = \sqrt{\mu\varepsilon/\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$, $v = c/n$, $\vec B = (n/c)\,\hat k \times \vec E$, $\vec S = (uc/n)\,\hat z$.
- **Normal incidence:** continuity of $\vec E_{\parallel}$ and $\vec B_{\parallel}/\mu$ gives $\tilde E_{0,R} = \frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2}\tilde E_{0,I}$ and $\tilde E_{0,T} = \frac{2n_1}{n_1 + n_2}\tilde E_{0,I}$ for $\mu_1 = \mu_2$; the reflection is inverted when $n_2 > n_1$.
- **Example:** a 1000 W/m² beam has $E_0 = 868$ V/m and $B_0 = 2.90 \times 10^{-6}$ T; on glass with $n = 1.50$ it reflects 4% (inverted) and transmits 96%.
