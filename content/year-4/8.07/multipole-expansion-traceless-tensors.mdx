The [first page on these notes](/courses/8-07/traceless-tensors-legendre-polynomials) found the general solution of Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates, written with traceless symmetric tensors. Section 7 of Lecture Notes 9 (pp. 11–16) applies it to the most common question in electrostatics: what does the potential of a complicated charged object look like from far away? The answer is the **multipole expansion**, and the tensor language gives it in a form where a handful of numbers describe the object completely at large distances. (As before, our own words and our own worked example; the notes have no figures. The notes write the components of $\vec r\,'$ mostly with the letter r (but $x'_i$ in eqs. (9.54) to (9.56)); this page writes $x'_i$ throughout, matching $x_i$ for $\vec r$. In eq. (9.46) the notes call the potential capital phi; this page keeps $V$.)

## The potential outside a charged object

Suppose all the charge of an object lies inside a sphere of radius $R$ centred on the origin (notes p. 11). Then Laplace's equation holds for all $r > R$, and the potential there must have the form of the general solution. The potential of a localized charge tends to a constant far away, which can be taken as zero, so all the coefficients $A_\ell$ of the growing terms $r^\ell$ vanish. The constants $B_\ell$ can be absorbed into the tensors, leaving

<Formula label="The potential outside the charge" tex="V(\vec r) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{\ell + 1}}\,C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.46)" />

Each term has one more power of $1/r$ than the last, so far away the first term, or the first few, dominate (notes p. 12). All the information about the charge is in the tensors $C^{(\ell)}$, and the first few of them describe the field at large distances, however complicated the object. The terms have names: $\ell = 0$ is the **monopole**, $\ell = 1$ the **dipole**, $\ell = 2$ the **quadrupole** and $\ell = 3$ the **octupole**.

To find the $C^{(\ell)}$ from the charge density, start from the potential of an arbitrary distribution (see [the integral form of electrostatics](/courses/8-07/integral-form-electrostatics)),

<Formula label="The potential of a charge distribution" tex="V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho(\vec r\,')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}\,d^3x'" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.47)" />

and expand $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ in powers of $r'$. [The notes print the volume element of eqs. (9.47), (9.52) and (9.74) as $d^3x$, and in eq. (9.74) the component of the source point without its prime; the integration variable is $\vec r\,'$.]

## Griffiths' route: the Legendre generating function

Guth first shows the textbook's way, the simplest but not the most useful form (notes p. 12). With $r$ and $r'$ the lengths of $\vec r$ and $\vec r\,'$ and $\theta'$ the angle between them,

<Formula label="The distance between two points" tex="\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|\vec r|^2 + |\vec r\,'|^2 - 2\,\vec r \cdot \vec r\,'}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2 + r'^2 - 2rr'\cos\theta'}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.48)" />

The Legendre polynomials can be defined by the **generating function**

<Formula label="The generating function" tex="g(x, \lambda) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \lambda^2 - 2\lambda x}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.49)" />

meaning that its power series in $\lambda$ has them as coefficients:

<Formula label="The generating function expanded" tex="g(x, \lambda) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \lambda^2 - 2\lambda x}} = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\lambda^\ell\,P_\ell(x)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.50)" />

(This is sometimes taken as the definition and sometimes derived from another one.) Taking $r$ out of the square root, with $\lambda = r'/r$ and $x = \cos\theta'$ (notes p. 12),

<Formula label="One over the distance in Legendre polynomials" tex="\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} = \frac{1}{r\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{r'}{r}\right)^2 - 2\,\frac{r'}{r}\cos\theta'}} = \frac{1}{r}\sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{r'}{r}\right)^\ell P_\ell(\cos\theta')" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.51)" />

and in the potential (notes p. 13)

<Formula label="The multipole expansion in Legendre polynomials" tex="V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{\ell + 1}}\int r'^\ell\,\rho(\vec r\,')\,P_\ell(\cos\theta')\,d^3x'" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.52)" />

[The notes' text calls the angle $\theta$ and eq. (9.51) ends with $P_\ell(\cos\theta)$; it is the same angle $\theta'$ as in eq. (9.48).] This shows quickly that $V$ is a series in $1/r$, but $\theta'$ is measured from the direction of $\vec r$, so the integral changes with the direction in which we look: it would have to be redone for every direction. Eq. (9.46) is what we want instead, a few numbers $C^{(\ell)}$ calculated (or even measured) once, then the potential in any direction. Griffiths massages eq. (9.52) to extract the monopole and dipole terms, and the students had done the quadrupole and octupole terms in Problem 3 of Problem Set 5. The standard improvement uses spherical harmonics; Guth derives the equivalent with traceless symmetric tensors (notes p. 13).

## A Taylor series in three variables

Think of $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ as a function of the three components $x'_i$ of $\vec r\,'$ (notes p. 13),

<Formula label="The function to expand" tex="f(\vec r\,') = \frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.53)" />

and expand it with the multi-variable [Taylor series](/courses/18-01/taylor-series) about $\vec r\,' = 0$, repeated indices summed:

<Formula label="The Taylor series in three variables" tex="f(\vec r\,') = f(0) + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x'_i}\right)_{\vec r\,' = 0}x'_i + \frac{1}{2!}\left(\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x'_i\,\partial x'_j}\right)_{\vec r\,' = 0}x'_i x'_j + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.54)" />

To separate the angles, write the components as length times unit vector,

<Formula label="Components of r prime" tex="x'_i = r'\,\hat n'_i" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.55)" />

so that the series becomes

<Formula label="The Taylor series with the angles separated" tex="f(\vec r\,') = f(0) + r'\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x'_i}\right)_{\vec r\,' = 0}\hat n'_i + \frac{r'^2}{2!}\left(\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x'_i\,\partial x'_j}\right)_{\vec r\,' = 0}\hat n'_i\hat n'_j + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.56)" />

Since $f$ depends only on $\vec r - \vec r\,'$, a derivative with respect to $x'_i$ is minus a derivative with respect to $x_i$, and setting $\vec r\,' = 0$ afterwards leaves a derivative of $1/r$ (notes p. 13):

<Formula label="Derivatives with respect to the source point" tex="\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x'_i}\right)_{\vec r\,' = 0} = -\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}\right)_{\vec r\,' = 0} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\frac{1}{|\vec r|}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.57)" />

Repeating this $\ell$ times (notes p. 14),

<Formula label="The l-th derivative" tex="\left(\frac{\partial^\ell f}{\partial x'_{i_1}\cdots\partial x'_{i_\ell}}\right)_{\vec r\,' = 0} = (-1)^\ell\,\frac{\partial^\ell}{\partial x_{i_1}\cdots\partial x_{i_\ell}}\frac{1}{|\vec r|}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.58)" />

and the expansion becomes

<Formula label="One over the distance as a Taylor series" tex="\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^\ell\,r'^\ell}{\ell\,!}\left(\frac{\partial^\ell}{\partial x_{i_1}\cdots\partial x_{i_\ell}}\frac{1}{|\vec r|}\right)\hat n'_{i_1}\cdots\hat n'_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.59)" />

## The derivatives are traceless

The tensor in brackets is symmetric, since partial derivatives commute. It is also **traceless** (notes p. 14): setting two indices equal and summing turns two of the derivatives into the Laplacian, and

<Formula label="The trace of the derivative tensor" tex="\frac{\partial^\ell}{\partial x_j\,\partial x_j\,\partial x_{i_3}\cdots\partial x_{i_\ell}}\frac{1}{|\vec r|} = \nabla^2\,\frac{\partial^{\ell - 2}}{\partial x_{i_3}\cdots\partial x_{i_\ell}}\frac{1}{|\vec r|} = \frac{\partial^{\ell - 2}}{\partial x_{i_3}\cdots\partial x_{i_\ell}}\,\nabla^2\frac{1}{|\vec r|} = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.60)" />

because $\nabla^2(1/r) = 0$ everywhere except at $r = 0$. [In the notes the indices of the contracted derivatives are printed as i, i, i + 2 and so on, and the remaining derivative on the right as of order $\ell$ instead of $\ell - 2$; the remaining indices are $i_3$ to $i_\ell$.] So the traceless symmetric tensors appear by themselves, Guth remarks.

## Working out the derivatives

Write $\vec r = r\,\hat n$ (eq. (9.61)). The notes abbreviate the derivative with respect to $x_i$ as a partial sign with the subscript $i$ (eq. (9.62)); this page writes it out in full. The two basic derivatives are (notes p. 14)

<Formula label="Derivatives of r and of n hat" tex="\frac{\partial r}{\partial x_i} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\left(x_j x_j\right)^{1/2} = \frac{x_i}{r} = \hat n_i, \qquad \frac{\partial\hat n_j}{\partial x_i} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\frac{x_j}{r} = \frac{\delta_{ij}}{r} - \frac{x_j}{r^2}\,\frac{\partial r}{\partial x_i} = \frac{1}{r}\left(\delta_{ij} - \hat n_i\hat n_j\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.63)" />

With these it is straightforward to differentiate $1/r$ again and again:

<Formula label="The first derivatives of one over r" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\frac{1}{r} = -\frac{1}{r^2}\,\hat n_i, \qquad \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}\frac{1}{r} = \frac{3}{r^3}\,\{\hat n_i\hat n_j\}, \qquad \frac{\partial^3}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j\,\partial x_k}\frac{1}{r} = -\frac{5 \cdot 3}{r^4}\,\{\hat n_i\hat n_j\hat n_k\}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.64)" />

where the curly brackets are the traceless symmetric parts of the first page. The second one, for example: differentiating $-\hat n_i/r^2$ with respect to $x_j$ gives $-(\delta_{ij} - \hat n_i\hat n_j)/r^3 + 2\hat n_i\hat n_j/r^3 = (3\hat n_i\hat n_j - \delta_{ij})/r^3$, which is $3\{\hat n_i\hat n_j\}/r^3$. The pattern, which can be proved by induction (notes p. 15), is

<Formula label="The l-th derivative of one over r" tex="\frac{\partial^\ell}{\partial x_{i_1}\cdots\partial x_{i_\ell}}\frac{1}{|\vec r|} = \frac{(-1)^\ell\,(2\ell - 1)!!}{r^{\ell + 1}}\,\{\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}\}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.65)" />

with the **double factorial**, the product of every second number,

<Formula label="The double factorial" tex="(2\ell - 1)!! = (2\ell - 1)(2\ell - 3)(2\ell - 5)\cdots 1 = \frac{(2\ell)!}{2^\ell\,\ell\,!}, \qquad (-1)!! = 1" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.66)" />

[In eq. (9.65) the first index of the curly bracket is printed with the subscript i instead of 1.] So $3!! = 3$ and $5!! = 15$, the factors 3 and $5 \cdot 3$ above.

## The expansion of one over the distance

Putting eq. (9.65) into eq. (9.59), the two signs $(-1)^\ell$ cancel (notes p. 15):

<Formula label="One over the distance in traceless tensors" tex="\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\ell - 1)!!}{\ell\,!}\,\frac{r'^\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}\,\{\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}\}\,\hat n'_{i_1}\cdots\hat n'_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.67), first" />

It can be written more symmetrically as

<Formula label="The symmetric form" tex="\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{(2\ell - 1)!!}{\ell\,!}\,\frac{r'^\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}\,\{\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}\}\,\{\hat n'_{i_1}\cdots\hat n'_{i_\ell}\}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.67), second (boxed)" />

because $\{\hat n'_{i_1}\cdots\hat n'_{i_\ell}\}$ differs from $\hat n'_{i_1}\cdots\hat n'_{i_\ell}$ only by terms with Kronecker deltas, which give zero when summed against the traceless tensor $\{\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}\}$. From the symmetric form the brackets can be dropped from either factor, but not from both. [The notes give both equations the number (9.67).] Comparing with eq. (9.51), the term of degree $\ell$ is $(r'^\ell/r^{\ell + 1})P_\ell(\cos\theta')$, which is the [Lecture 8](/courses/8-07/spherical-harmonics-from-traceless-tensors) formula for $P_\ell$ with $\hat z$ replaced by $\hat n'$.

## The multipole moments

Putting the expansion into eq. (9.47) gives Guth's final result (notes p. 15):

<Formula label="The multipole expansion" tex="V(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{\ell + 1}}\,C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.68)" />

<Formula label="The multipole moments" tex="C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell} = \frac{(2\ell - 1)!!}{\ell\,!}\int\rho(\vec r\,')\,\{x'_{i_1}\cdots x'_{i_\ell}\}\,d^3x'" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.69)" />

(Compared with eq. (9.46), the $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ is now written separately.) The factor $r'^\ell$ has combined with the $\hat n'$'s into $x'_{i_1}\cdots x'_{i_\ell}$. The moments depend only on the charge; all dependence on the direction of observation is in the $\hat n$'s. The coefficient can also be written (notes p. 15)

<Formula label="The coefficient of the moments" tex="\frac{(2\ell - 1)!!}{\ell\,!} = \frac{(2\ell)!}{2^\ell\,(\ell\,!)^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.70)" />

the same number that turns the traceless tensors into Legendre polynomials. The first two terms, written out (notes p. 16): the **monopole**

<Formula label="The monopole term" tex="V_{\text{mono}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{Q}{r}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.71)" />

<Formula label="The total charge" tex="Q = C^{(0)} = \int\rho(\vec r\,')\,d^3x'" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.72)" />

and the **dipole** (compare [electric dipoles in 8.02](/courses/8-02/electric-dipoles))

<Formula label="The dipole term" tex="V_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\vec p \cdot \hat n}{r^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.73)" />

<Formula label="The dipole moment" tex="p_i = C^{(1)}_i = \int\rho(\vec r\,')\,x'_i\,d^3x'" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.74)" />

The notes stop here. For orientation (not in the notes): at $\ell = 2$ the moment is $C^{(2)}_{ij} = \frac32\int\rho\left(x'_i x'_j - \frac13 r'^2\delta_{ij}\right)d^3x'$, which is half of the quadrupole moment tensor $\int\rho\,(3x'_i x'_j - r'^2\delta_{ij})\,d^3x'$ used in many textbooks (Jackson, Section 4.1, for example). For point charges the integrals become sums over the charges, each charge times the factors of its position.

<WorkedExample title="Two unequal charges seen from afar">
<Problem>
A charge $+2q$ sits at $z = +d$ and a charge $-q$ at $z = -d$, with $q = 1$ nC and $d = 1$ cm. Find the monopole, dipole and quadrupole terms of the potential about the origin, and compare their sum with the exact potential at $r = 10$ cm, on the $z$-axis above the charges and in the plane $z = 0$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The moments">
Monopole: $Q = 2q - q = q$. Dipole: the integral of eq. (9.74) becomes a sum of charge times position, $\vec p = 2q\,d\,\hat z + (-q)(-d)\,\hat z = 3qd\,\hat z$, which is $3 \times 10^{-11}$ C m. Quadrupole: both charges sit on the axis at distance $d$, so for each $\{x'_i x'_j\} = d^2\{\hat z_i\hat z_j\}$, and $C^{(2)}_{ij} = \frac32(2q - q)\,d^2\{\hat z_i\hat z_j\} = \frac32 q d^2\{\hat z_i\hat z_j\}$. Contracted with $\hat n$, by the first page, $\frac32 q d^2\left(\cos^2\theta - \frac13\right) = q d^2 P_2(\cos\theta)$.
</Step>
<Step title="The series">
$V \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{q}{r} + \frac{3qd\cos\theta}{r^2} + \frac{qd^2 P_2(\cos\theta)}{r^3}\right]$. Here $\theta$ is the angle between $\hat n$ and the $z$-axis. With $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 d) = 898.8$ V and $d/r = 0.1$, the three terms on the axis ($\cos\theta = 1$, $P_2 = 1$) are 89.88 V, 26.96 V and 0.90 V, adding up to 117.74 V. In the plane $z = 0$ ($\cos\theta = 0$, $P_2 = -\frac12$) the dipole term vanishes and the terms are 89.88 V and $-0.45$ V, a total of 89.43 V.
</Step>
<Step title="The exact potential">
On the axis the charges are 9 cm and 11 cm away: $V = 898.8 \times \left(\frac{2}{9} - \frac{1}{11}\right) = 898.8 \times \frac{13}{99} = 118.02$ V. The three terms miss by 0.28 V, 0.24%, which is almost all the octupole: its moment gives $\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,3qd^3 P_3(\cos\theta)/r^4$, 0.27 V here. In the plane $z = 0$ both charges are $\sqrt{101}$ cm away: $V = 898.8 \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{101}} = 89.43$ V, which the series matches to about 0.003 V, since there the octupole term vanishes and the next one is smaller by another factor $(d/r)^2$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="The same expansion for gravity">
Newton's gravity has the same $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$: the gravitational potential of a mass distribution is eq. (9.47) with the charge density replaced by the mass density and $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ by $-G$, so everything on this page carries over. The tree codes of cosmological N-body simulations (see [simulating galaxy formation](/courses/fundamentals-of-dark-matter/simulating-galaxy-formation)) use it: a distant cell of particles is replaced by the first terms of the multipole expansion of its mass. Gravity has one simplification that charge does not (our remark, not in Guth's notes): all masses are positive, so if the expansion is taken about the cell's centre of mass, the dipole moment vanishes by the definition of the centre of mass, and the monopole alone is already accurate up to the quadrupole term, which falls as $1/r^3$.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Outside the charge:** the potential is $\sum_\ell C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}/r^{\ell + 1}$, the decaying half of the general solution; the terms are the monopole, dipole, quadrupole and octupole, and far away the first few dominate.
- **Griffiths' route:** the generating function $1/\sqrt{1 + \lambda^2 - 2\lambda x} = \sum_\ell \lambda^\ell P_\ell(x)$ gives $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'| = \sum_\ell (r'^\ell/r^{\ell + 1})P_\ell(\cos\theta')$, but $\theta'$ depends on the direction of $\vec r$, so the integral must be redone for every direction.
- **Guth's route:** a Taylor series in the components of $\vec r\,'$ turns the coefficients into derivatives of $1/r$, which are traceless because $\nabla^2(1/r) = 0$: $\partial^\ell(1/r) = (-1)^\ell(2\ell - 1)!!\{\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}\}/r^{\ell + 1}$.
- **The moments:** $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_\ell C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}/r^{\ell + 1}$ with $C^{(\ell)} = \frac{(2\ell - 1)!!}{\ell\,!}\int\rho\{x'_{i_1}\cdots x'_{i_\ell}\}d^3x'$, computed once for every direction; $C^{(0)} = Q$ and $C^{(1)}_i = p_i$.
- **Example:** $+2$ nC and $-1$ nC 2 cm apart give 117.74 V from three terms against 118.02 V exactly, 10 cm away on the axis; the missing 0.28 V is mostly the octupole.
