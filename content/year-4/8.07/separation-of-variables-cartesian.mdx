The [method of images](/courses/8-07/method-of-images-uniqueness) solves a few problems with a clever guess. Most boundaries have no such guess, and a general method is needed for solving Laplace's equation, $\nabla^2 V = 0$, in the empty space between conductors. The rest of Guth's handwritten notes for this lecture start on the most useful one, **separation of variables** (notes pp. 64–65), and the two pages in a second hand that close the notes, 63-5 and 63-6, ask when and in which coordinates it works. The method is the one used for the normal modes of a string in [8.03](/courses/8-03/standing-waves-fourier-series), and the coefficients come from [Fourier series](/courses/18-03/fourier-series-introduction). (In the scanned file, pp. 63-5 and 63-6 come after p. 65; this page follows that order. Our own words and our own example.)

## Trying a product

In Cartesian coordinates Laplace's equation reads (notes p. 64)

<Formula label="Laplace's equation in Cartesian coordinates" tex="\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial z^2} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 64" />

Try a solution of the form

<Formula label="A product of three functions" tex="V(x, y, z) = X(x)\,Y(y)\,Z(z)" cite="reading1" at="p. 64" />

Most solutions are not of this form. Guth's point is that this does not matter: even if a single product is not general enough, **sums** of solutions of this form might be, because the equation is linear (notes p. 64). Substituting the product, each second derivative acts on one factor only:

<Formula label="After substituting" tex="Y Z\,\frac{d^2X}{dx^2} + X Z\,\frac{d^2Y}{dy^2} + X Y\,\frac{d^2Z}{dz^2} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 64" />

Now divide by $V = XYZ$:

<Formula label="After dividing by XYZ" tex="\frac{1}{X}\,\frac{d^2X}{dx^2} + \frac{1}{Y}\,\frac{d^2Y}{dy^2} + \frac{1}{Z}\,\frac{d^2Z}{dz^2} = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 64" />

## Each term is a constant

The first term depends only on $x$, the second only on $y$, the third only on $z$ (notes p. 65). Change $x$ while holding $y$ and $z$ fixed: the second and third terms do not change, and the sum must stay zero, so the first term cannot change either. A function of $x$ that does not change when $x$ changes is a constant. The same holds for each term, so

<Formula label="The separation constants" tex="C_1 + C_2 + C_3 = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 65" />

with $C_1$, $C_2$ and $C_3$ the values of the three terms. The partial differential equation has become three ordinary ones, of which the first is

<Formula label="The equation for X" tex="\frac{d^2X}{dx^2} = C_1\,X" cite="reading1" at="p. 65" />

## Three possibilities

The solutions depend on the sign of the constant (notes p. 65):

1. $C_1 = \alpha^2 > 0$. Then $X$ is a combination of $e^{\alpha x}$ and $e^{-\alpha x}$, or equivalently of $\sinh\alpha x$ and $\cosh\alpha x$.
2. $C_1 = -\alpha^2 < 0$. Then $X$ is a combination of $e^{i\alpha x}$ and $e^{-i\alpha x}$, or of $\sin\alpha x$ and $\cos\alpha x$.
3. $C_1 = 0$. Then $X = A + Bx$.

Since the three constants add up to zero, they cannot all be positive or all be negative: if the solution oscillates in some directions, it must grow or decay in another. A potential cannot oscillate in all three directions at once, which fits the fact that a solution of Laplace's equation has no maximum or minimum away from the boundary.

## The example: a rectangular pipe

The notes take their example from the course textbook, Griffiths's Example 3.5, without working it (notes p. 65). Here it is in our own words. A long metal pipe runs along the $x$ axis from $x = 0$ to infinity, with a rectangular cross-section: $0 \leq y \leq a$ and $0 \leq z \leq b$. Its four walls are grounded, $V = 0$, and the end at $x = 0$ is held at a given potential $V_0(y, z)$. Find the potential inside.

The boundary conditions pick the signs. In $y$ the potential vanishes at both $y = 0$ and $y = a$, which an exponential or a straight line cannot do (except by being zero everywhere), but a sine can: $Y = \sin(n\pi y/a)$ with $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$, so $C_2 = -(n\pi/a)^2$. Likewise $Z = \sin(m\pi z/b)$, with $C_3 = -(m\pi/b)^2$. Then $C_1 = -C_2 - C_3$ is positive, and of the two exponentials only the decaying one is allowed, since the potential must stay finite far down the pipe. Each product is a solution, and so is any sum of them:

<Formula label="The general solution in the pipe" tex="V(x, y, z) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty} c_{nm}\,e^{-\pi\sqrt{(n/a)^2 + (m/b)^2}\,x}\,\sin\frac{n\pi y}{a}\,\sin\frac{m\pi z}{b}" cite="griffiths" at="Example 3.5" />

Every term already vanishes on the four walls. The last condition, $V = V_0(y, z)$ at $x = 0$, fixes the coefficients $c_{nm}$: it asks for $V_0$ as a double Fourier sine series. Multiplying by $\sin(n'\pi y/a)\sin(m'\pi z/b)$ and integrating over the cross-section, the orthogonality of the sines picks out one term:

<Formula label="The coefficients" tex="c_{nm} = \frac{4}{ab}\int_0^a\int_0^b V_0(y, z)\,\sin\frac{n\pi y}{a}\,\sin\frac{m\pi z}{b}\,dz\,dy" cite="griffiths" at="Example 3.5" />

For an end at a constant potential $V_0$ each integral is elementary: $c_{nm} = 16V_0/(\pi^2 nm)$ when $n$ and $m$ are both odd, and zero otherwise. By the uniqueness theorem of the previous page, this series is **the** potential in the pipe.

<WorkedExample title="How far does the end's potential reach into the pipe?">
<Problem>
A grounded pipe has a cross-section of $a = 2.0$ cm by $b = 1.0$ cm, and its end is held at 100 V. Find the potential on the pipe's axis (the centre of the cross-section) 1.0 cm from the end, and the distance over which the potential dies away.
</Problem>
<Step title="The terms at the centre">
At $y = a/2$ and $z = b/2$ the sines are $\sin(n\pi/2) = \pm 1$ for odd $n$. The first coefficient is $16 \times 100/\pi^2$ V $\approx 162.1$ V. With lengths in cm, the first term decays at the rate $\pi\sqrt{1/4 + 1} \approx 3.51$ per cm, so at $x = 1.0$ cm it is $162.1\,e^{-3.51}$ V, about 4.84 V.
</Step>
<Step title="The next terms">
The term $n = 3$, $m = 1$ has the coefficient $-162.1/3$ V and decays at $\pi\sqrt{9/4 + 1}$ per cm: it contributes about $-0.19$ V. The term $n = 1$, $m = 3$ decays much faster, at $\pi\sqrt{1/4 + 9}$ per cm, and contributes only about $-0.004$ V. Summing the series gives about 4.65 V.
</Step>
<Step title="How far it reaches">
Far from the end only the slowest term matters, and it falls by a factor e every $1/(\pi\sqrt{1/4 + 1})$ cm, about 0.285 cm. Within a few times the pipe's smaller width, the 100 V at the end has all but vanished: at 2 cm it is down to about 0.14 V. A potential applied at the mouth of a narrow grounded tube reaches only a short way inside.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Two questions

The notes end with two questions, on pages in a second hand (notes p. 63-5): how do we know that separation of variables will work, and which coordinates should we use?

The first answer begins by placing Laplace's equation in its family. A homogeneous linear second-order equation in two variables has the form

<Formula label="A second-order linear equation" tex="A\,\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x^2} + B\,\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial x\,\partial y} + C\,\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial y^2} + \ldots = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 63-5" />

where the dots stand for terms with first derivatives of $V$: the notes write them out with two more coefficients, plus a constant term that is set to zero, since the equation is homogeneous. (A term in $V$ itself could be added without changing the class.) Its character depends on the sign of one number, named after the conic sections that have the same test:

<Formula label="The discriminant" tex="\Delta = B^2 - 4AC" cite="reading1" at="p. 63-5" />

- $\Delta > 0$: **hyperbolic**, for example the wave equation.
- $\Delta = 0$: **parabolic**, for example the equation of heat transport ([diffusion](/courses/18-02/diffusion-equation)).
- $\Delta < 0$: **elliptic**, for example Laplace's equation, which appears in electromagnetism, elasticity, potential theory, heat flow in equilibrium and fluid flow.

Each class has its own characteristic methods and features, and they usually do not mix (notes p. 63-5): the wave equation carries signals along fixed directions at a fixed speed, while a solution of Laplace's equation at any point feels the boundary everywhere at once. The exception the notes give is the sonic boom and its electromagnetic cousin, **Cherenkov radiation**. For a source moving steadily at speed $v$ through a medium in which waves travel at speed $c$ (sound, or light in the medium), the equation for the disturbance, written in the frame of the source, has the factor $1 - v^2/c^2$ in front of the derivative along the motion. Below the wave speed this factor is positive and the equation is elliptic: the disturbance spreads all round the source. At $v = c$ it vanishes and the equation changes type (the notes label this case "sonic boom"; the boom heard on the ground comes from the cone formed above that speed), and above it the equation becomes hyperbolic: the disturbance is confined to a cone behind the source. (This is our way of stating the case; the notes' equation and table for it are hard to read in the scan.) Min Chen's research talk, Lecture Notes 12, returns to Cherenkov radiation.

## Which boundary conditions, which coordinates

The second part of the answer concerns the boundary conditions (notes p. 63-6). They come in three kinds:

<Formula label="Two kinds of boundary condition" tex="V(\vec r) = h(\vec r), \qquad \nabla V \cdot \hat n = k(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="p. 63-6" />

The first gives the potential itself on a boundary, the second its normal derivative (that is, up to sign, the normal component of the field, and so the surface charge on a conductor); $h$ and $k$ are given functions on the boundary. The third kind mixes the two. (The notes write $\vec x$ for the position; the usual names, not used in the notes, are Dirichlet and Neumann conditions.)

Separation of variables then works if (notes p. 63-6):

1. the equation is linear and contains no term that mixes the variables, such as a coefficient $\sin(xy)$ (or, our gloss, a mixed derivative), which would stop the product from splitting into separate ordinary equations; and
2. the boundary conditions are separable in some coordinate system: they are given on surfaces where one coordinate is constant, like the walls $y = 0$, $y = a$ of the pipe.

The second condition decides which coordinates one must use. A box calls for Cartesian coordinates; a sphere, as in the next notes, for spherical ones.

## Summary

- **Separation of variables:** try $V = X(x)Y(y)Z(z)$ in $\nabla^2 V = 0$. After dividing by $XYZ$, each of the three terms depends on one coordinate only, so each is a constant, with $C_1 + C_2 + C_3 = 0$.
- $X'' = C_1X$: for $C_1 = \alpha^2 > 0$, $e^{\pm\alpha x}$ or $\sinh$ and $\cosh$; for $C_1 = -\alpha^2 < 0$, $e^{\pm i\alpha x}$ or $\sin$ and $\cos$; for $C_1 = 0$, $A + Bx$. The constants cannot all have the same sign.
- One product is rarely enough, but sums of products are, because the equation is linear. In a grounded rectangular pipe with its end at $V_0(y, z)$, the potential is a double Fourier sine series whose terms decay as $e^{-\pi\sqrt{(n/a)^2 + (m/b)^2}\,x}$.
- Second-order linear equations are **hyperbolic**, **parabolic** or **elliptic** as $B^2 - 4AC$ is positive, zero or negative (waves, heat, Laplace), each with its own methods; a source moving faster than the waves changes the type (sonic boom, Cherenkov radiation).
- Separation works for an equation without mixing terms and boundary conditions that are separable in some coordinate system; the boundary conditions decide the coordinates.
