The previous notes found the solutions of Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates. Lecture Notes 7 put them to work on a complete problem, one Guth began in the lecture of Friday 5 October 2012 and did not have time to finish, so he wrote it up from the beginning (notes p. 1). The problem is taken from Jackson's *Classical Electrodynamics*, 3rd edition (Problem 3.2). It is a model of how a boundary value problem with a surface charge is solved with **Legendre polynomials**: expand inside and outside, join the two at the surface, and let the charge fix the coefficients. (Our own words and our own worked example; the notes have no figures.)

## The problem

A spherical surface of radius $R$ carries the uniform surface charge density (notes p. 1)

<Formula label="The surface charge density" tex="\sigma_0 = \frac{Q}{4\pi R^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

everywhere except on a cap around the north pole, the region inside the cone $\theta = \alpha$. Find the potential everywhere. So $Q$ is the charge the shell would carry without the hole; the charge actually on it is less. Put the centre of the sphere at the origin and the cap on the positive $z$-axis. Then nothing changes when the whole arrangement is turned about the $z$-axis: the problem has **azimuthal symmetry**, the case that Griffiths treats.

## Azimuthal symmetry and the Legendre polynomials

Guth starts from the general solution of the previous notes, to show how it works (notes p. 1). Outside the charges he writes the most general solution of Laplace's equation as a sum of terms of each degree $\ell$ (that every function of direction can be expanded this way is assumed, not proved, in the earlier notes):

<Formula label="The general solution of Laplace's equation" tex="V(\vec r) = V(r, \theta, \phi) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty} R_\ell(r)\,C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.1)" />

Here $C^{(\ell)}$ is a traceless symmetric tensor, $\hat n$ the unit vector in the direction $(\theta, \phi)$, and the repeated indices are summed from 1 to 3. Laplace's equation fixes the radial functions to

<Formula label="The radial functions" tex="R_\ell(r) = A_\ell\,r^\ell + \frac{B_\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.2)" />

Azimuthal symmetry means that each $C^{(\ell)}$ must be unchanged by rotations about the $z$-axis. The only unit vector with that property is $\hat z$ itself. Guth states, without proving it here, that the most general such tensor is built from it: multiply $\ell$ copies, $\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}$, and take the traceless symmetric part, written with curly brackets. Contracted with the $\hat n$'s this gives the standard Legendre polynomial (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="The symmetric tensor about z gives the Legendre polynomial" tex="C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell} = \text{const}\cdot\left\{\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\right\}\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell} \propto P_\ell(\cos\theta)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.3)" />

(Our own check: for $\ell = 2$ the traceless part of $\hat z_i\hat z_j$ is $\hat z_i\hat z_j - \frac13\delta_{ij}$, and contracted with $\hat n_i\hat n_j$ it gives $\cos^2\theta - \frac13$, which is $\frac23 P_2(\cos\theta)$.) The constants of proportionality go into $A_\ell$ and $B_\ell$, so

<Formula label="The potential with azimuthal symmetry" tex="V(r, \theta, \phi) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty} R_\ell(r)\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.4)" />

The [Legendre polynomials](/courses/8-04/angular-momentum-central-potentials) are the same ones that appear in quantum mechanics: $P_0 = 1$, $P_1 = x$, $P_2 = \frac12(3x^2 - 1)$, with $x = \cos\theta$.

## Inside and outside

At $r = R$ there is a surface charge, at least for some angles, so Laplace's equation fails there (notes p. 2). The potential has the form above inside and outside, but with different radial functions in the two regions:

<Formula label="Separate expansions inside and outside" tex="V_{\text{in}} = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\left[A_{\ell,\text{in}}\,r^\ell + \frac{B_{\ell,\text{in}}}{r^{\ell + 1}}\right]P_\ell(\cos\theta), \qquad V_{\text{out}} = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\left[A_{\ell,\text{out}}\,r^\ell + \frac{B_{\ell,\text{out}}}{r^{\ell + 1}}\right]P_\ell(\cos\theta)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.5)" />

(The notes write "in" and "out" as superscripts.) Half the terms go at once. The $B_{\ell,\text{in}}$ terms would be infinite at the centre, so $B_{\ell,\text{in}} = 0$; the $A_{\ell,\text{out}}$ terms would grow without limit far away, impossible for a finite charge, so $A_{\ell,\text{out}} = 0$.

The two sides must still be joined. The field jumps at the charged surface, but it does not become infinite: the field of a surface charge layer is finite. The potential difference between a point just inside and a point just outside is the line integral of $\vec E$ between them, a finite integrand over a path of zero length, so it is zero (notes p. 2). The potential is **continuous** at $r = R$:

<Formula label="Continuity at the shell" tex="A_{\ell,\text{in}}\,R^\ell = \frac{B_{\ell,\text{out}}}{R^{\ell + 1}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.6)" />

This must hold for each $\ell$ separately. The Legendre polynomials are orthogonal, so a mismatch at one $\ell$ cannot be made up by the others. Thus $B_{\ell,\text{out}} = R^{2\ell + 1}A_{\ell,\text{in}}$, and one coefficient describes both sides. Define it as

<Formula label="One coefficient for both sides" tex="\tilde A_\ell = R^{\ell + 1}A_{\ell,\text{in}} = R^{-\ell}B_{\ell,\text{out}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.7)" />

so that

<Formula label="The potential inside, r less than R" tex="V(r, \theta, \phi) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\tilde A_\ell\,\frac{r^\ell}{R^{\ell + 1}}\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.8)" />

<Formula label="The potential outside, r greater than R" tex="V(r, \theta, \phi) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\tilde A_\ell\,\frac{R^\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.8)" />

With $r_{>}$ the larger and $r_{<}$ the smaller of $r$ and $R$ (notes eq. (7.9)), the two lines become one (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="The potential everywhere" tex="V(r, \theta, \phi) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\tilde A_\ell\,\frac{r_{<}^\ell}{r_{>}^{\ell + 1}}\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.10)" />

## A cleverer start

Guth remarks that a better choice of constants at the start gets here faster (notes p. 3). Write the radial function as

<Formula label="Radial functions with the radius built in" tex="R_\ell(r) = A_\ell\,\frac{r^\ell}{R^{\ell + 1}} + B_\ell\,\frac{R^\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.2′), p. 3" />

The extra powers of $R$ only rename the unknown constants, but now $A_\ell$ and $B_\ell$ have the same units, and continuity says simply $B_{\ell,\text{out}} = A_{\ell,\text{in}}$, with no factors of $R$. This $A_\ell$ is the $\tilde A_\ell$ above.

## The jump in the field

The charge has not been used yet, and it is what determines the $\tilde A_\ell$. [Gauss's law](/courses/8-02/gauss-law), applied to a thin pillbox across the surface, makes the normal component of the field jump by the local surface charge over $\varepsilon_0$ (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="The jump in the radial field" tex="E_r(R^+, \theta) - E_r(R^-, \theta) = \frac{\sigma(\theta)}{\varepsilon_0}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.11)" />

Here $R^+$ and $R^-$ mean just outside and just inside the shell (the notes mark them with a vertical bar), and the charge density is a step in $\theta$:

<Formula label="The charge density with the hole" tex="\sigma(\theta) = \sigma_0 \text{ if } \theta > \alpha, \qquad \sigma(\theta) = 0 \text{ if } \theta < \alpha" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.12)" />

With $E_r = -\partial V/\partial r$ this is a condition on the potential:

<Formula label="The jump in terms of the potential" tex="-\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}(R^+, \theta) + \frac{\partial V}{\partial r}(R^-, \theta) = \frac{\sigma(\theta)}{\varepsilon_0}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.13)" />

Differentiate the two halves of the series at $r = R$. Outside, $R^\ell/r^{\ell + 1}$ gives $-(\ell + 1)R^\ell/R^{\ell + 2}$; inside, $r^\ell/R^{\ell + 1}$ gives $\ell R^{\ell - 1}/R^{\ell + 1}$ (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="The series in the jump condition" tex="\sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\left[(\ell + 1)\tilde A_\ell\,\frac{R^\ell}{R^{\ell + 2}}\,P_\ell(\cos\theta) + \ell\tilde A_\ell\,\frac{R^{\ell - 1}}{R^{\ell + 1}}\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)\right] = \frac{\sigma(\theta)}{\varepsilon_0}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.14)" />

Both terms carry $1/R^2$, and they add up to $2\ell + 1$:

<Formula label="The jump condition simplified" tex="\sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}(2\ell + 1)\,\tilde A_\ell\,P_\ell(\cos\theta) = \frac{R^2\sigma(\theta)}{\varepsilon_0}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.15)" />

## Picking out each coefficient

The left side is a Legendre series of the right side. To pick out one coefficient, use the orthogonality of the Legendre polynomials (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Orthogonality of the Legendre polynomials" tex="\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\ell'}(x)\,P_\ell(x)\,dx = \frac{2}{2\ell + 1}\,\delta_{\ell'\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.16)" />

Multiply the jump condition by $P_{\ell'}(x)$, with $x = \cos\theta$, and integrate from $-1$ to $1$. Only the term with $\ell = \ell'$ survives, and its factor $2\ell + 1$ cancels (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Multiplying by one Legendre polynomial and integrating" tex="\sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}(2\ell + 1)\,\tilde A_\ell\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\ell'}(x)\,P_\ell(x)\,dx = 2\tilde A_{\ell'} = \frac{R^2}{\varepsilon_0}\int_{-1}^{1}P_{\ell'}(x)\,\sigma(x)\,dx" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.17)" />

so

<Formula label="The coefficients as an integral over the charge" tex="\tilde A_\ell = \frac{R^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\int_{-1}^{1}P_\ell(x)\,\sigma(x)\,dx" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.18)" />

This holds for any azimuthally symmetric surface charge on the sphere. In many problems one has to stop here, Guth notes, but this time the integral can be done.

## Doing the integral

The charge is $\sigma_0$ for $\theta > \alpha$, that is for $x < \cos\alpha$, and zero above. With $\sigma_0 = Q/(4\pi R^2)$ (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="The coefficients for the shell with a hole" tex="\tilde A_\ell = \frac{R^2\sigma_0}{2\varepsilon_0}\int_{-1}^{\cos\alpha}P_\ell(x)\,dx = \frac{Q}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{-1}^{\cos\alpha}P_\ell(x)\,dx" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.19)" />

To integrate a Legendre polynomial, write it as a difference of derivatives of its neighbours:

<Formula label="An identity for derivatives of Legendre polynomials" tex="\frac{dP_{\ell + 1}(x)}{dx} - \frac{dP_{\ell - 1}(x)}{dx} - (2\ell + 1)\,P_\ell(x) = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.20)" />

It holds for every $\ell \ge 0$ if one defines (notes p. 4)

<Formula label="A convention for the identity" tex="P_{-1}(x) = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.21)" />

(Our own check, for $\ell = 1$: $dP_2/dx - dP_0/dx = 3x - 0 = 3P_1$.) Then the integral is a difference of values at the limits:

<Formula label="The coefficients after integrating" tex="\tilde A_\ell = \frac{Q}{8\pi\varepsilon_0(2\ell + 1)}\int_{-1}^{\cos\alpha}\left[\frac{dP_{\ell + 1}(x)}{dx} - \frac{dP_{\ell - 1}(x)}{dx}\right]dx = \frac{Q}{8\pi\varepsilon_0(2\ell + 1)}\Big\{\left[P_{\ell + 1}(\cos\alpha) - P_{\ell + 1}(-1)\right] - \left[P_{\ell - 1}(\cos\alpha) - P_{\ell - 1}(-1)\right]\Big\}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.22)" />

The values at $x = -1$ follow from $P_\ell(1) = 1$ and the parity of the polynomials, even for even $\ell$ and odd for odd $\ell$ (or from Rodrigues' formula, which the notes spell "Rodriguez"):

<Formula label="Legendre polynomials at minus one" tex="P_\ell(-1) = (-1)^\ell" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.23)" />

For $\ell > 0$ the indices $\ell + 1$ and $\ell - 1$ differ by 2, so $P_{\ell + 1}(-1)$ and $P_{\ell - 1}(-1)$ are equal and cancel:

<Formula label="The coefficients for l greater than zero" tex="\tilde A_\ell = \frac{Q}{8\pi\varepsilon_0(2\ell + 1)}\left[P_{\ell + 1}(\cos\alpha) - P_{\ell - 1}(\cos\alpha)\right]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.24a)" />

For $\ell = 0$ the convention $P_{-1} = 0$ leaves $P_1(\cos\alpha) - P_1(-1) = \cos\alpha + 1$:

<Formula label="The coefficient for l equal to zero" tex="\tilde A_0 = \frac{Q}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[P_1(\cos\alpha) + 1\right]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.24b)" />

Putting the coefficients into the series gives the final answer (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="The potential of the shell with a hole" tex="V(r, \theta, \phi) = \frac{Q}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2\ell + 1}\left[P_{\ell + 1}(\cos\alpha) - P_{\ell - 1}(\cos\alpha)\right]\frac{r_{<}^\ell}{r_{>}^{\ell + 1}}\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (7.25)" />

One formula covers every $\ell$ only if $P_{-1}(\cos\alpha)$ is read as $-1$ here, which, as Guth points out, conflicts with the convention used for the identity. The $\ell = 0$ term is the one place where it matters.

## Checking the answer

Two checks of our own. Without the hole, $\alpha = 0$ and $\cos\alpha = 1$: every bracket with $\ell > 0$ is $1 - 1 = 0$, and the $\ell = 0$ term is $2Q/(8\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{>}) = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r_{>})$, a constant inside and a point charge outside, the familiar uniform shell. And the charge actually on the shell is $\sigma_0$ times the area below the cap, $2\pi R^2(1 + \cos\alpha)$, which is $Q(1 + \cos\alpha)/2$. Far away only the $\ell = 0$ term survives, $\tilde A_0/r = Q(1 + \cos\alpha)/(8\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$, exactly the potential of that charge.

<WorkedExample title="A 10 cm shell with a 60° hole">
<Problem>
A shell of radius $R = 10$ cm has $\sigma_0 = Q/(4\pi R^2)$ with $Q = 1.0$ nC, except on a cap of half-angle $\alpha = 60°$ around the north pole. Find the charge on the shell, the potential and the field at the centre, the first three coefficients $\tilde A_\ell$ and the dipole moment. Then compare the series with the exact potential on the axis.
</Problem>
<Step title="Charge and potential at the centre">
$\sigma_0 = Q/(4\pi R^2) \approx 7.96 \times 10^{-9}$ C/m². With $\cos\alpha = \frac12$ the charge on the shell is $Q(1 + \cos\alpha)/2 = 0.75$ nC; the missing cap would have held 0.25 nC. At the centre $r_{<} = 0$, so only $\ell = 0$ contributes: $V = \tilde A_0/R = Q(1 + \cos\alpha)/(8\pi\varepsilon_0 R) \approx 67.4$ V, the potential of 0.75 nC all at the distance $R$, as it must be.
</Step>
<Step title="The coefficients">
$Q/(8\pi\varepsilon_0) = 4.49$ V m. With $P_0 = 1$, $P_1 = \frac12$, $P_2 = \frac12\left(3 \cdot \frac14 - 1\right) = -\frac18$ and $P_3 = \frac12\left(5 \cdot \frac18 - 3 \cdot \frac12\right) = -\frac{7}{16}$, all at $\cos\alpha = \frac12$: $\tilde A_0 = 4.49 \times \frac32 \approx 6.74$ V m, $\tilde A_1 = \frac{4.49}{3}\left(-\frac18 - 1\right) \approx -1.69$ V m and $\tilde A_2 = \frac{4.49}{5}\left(-\frac{7}{16} - \frac12\right) \approx -0.843$ V m.
</Step>
<Step title="Field at the centre and dipole moment">
Inside, the $\ell = 1$ term is $\tilde A_1 r\cos\theta/R^2 = \tilde A_1 z/R^2$, so at the centre $E_z = -\tilde A_1/R^2 \approx 169$ V/m: the field points towards the hole, away from the charge on the far side. (A uniform shell would have no field inside at all.) Outside, the same term is $\tilde A_1 R\cos\theta/r^2$, a dipole $p\cos\theta/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$ with $p = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 R\tilde A_1 = -3QR/16 \approx -1.9 \times 10^{-11}$ C m. It is negative, pointing to the south: the charge is lopsided away from the hole. (Directly, adding up $z$ times the charge of each ring of the shell gives $p = -\frac14 QR\sin^2\alpha$, the same.)
</Step>
<Step title="The exact potential on the axis">
On the $z$-axis each ring of charge is at one distance from the point, so the integral can be done exactly. For a point at height $z$ (negative below the centre) it gives $V(z) = \frac{Q(1 + \cos\alpha)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{1}{|R + z| + \sqrt{R^2 + z^2 - 2Rz\cos\alpha}}$. In the centre of the hole, $z = R$, this is 44.9 V; at the south pole, $z = -R$, it is 77.8 V. The series on the axis has $P_\ell(\pm 1) = (\pm 1)^\ell$; its first three terms give $67.4 - 16.9 - 8.4 \approx 42.1$ V at the hole and $67.4 + 16.9 - 8.4 \approx 75.8$ V at the south pole. More terms close the gap, slowly on the shell itself, where $r_{<} = r_{>}$ and the powers of $r_{<}/r_{>}$ no longer shrink.
</Step>

## Summary

- **The problem** (Jackson 3.2): a shell of radius $R$ with $\sigma_0 = Q/(4\pi R^2)$ everywhere except a cap $\theta < \alpha$. Azimuthal symmetry reduces the general solution to $\sum_\ell R_\ell(r)P_\ell(\cos\theta)$, because the traceless symmetric part of $\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}$ gives the Legendre polynomial.
- **Inside and outside:** finiteness keeps $r^\ell$ inside and $1/r^{\ell + 1}$ outside; continuity at the shell (a finite field over a zero-length path) joins them into $V = \sum_\ell \tilde A_\ell\,(r_{<}^\ell/r_{>}^{\ell + 1})\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)$.
- **The charge fixes the coefficients:** the radial field jumps by $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$, giving $\sum_\ell (2\ell + 1)\tilde A_\ell P_\ell = R^2\sigma/\varepsilon_0$; orthogonality gives $\tilde A_\ell = \frac{R^2}{2\varepsilon_0}\int_{-1}^{1}P_\ell\,\sigma\,dx$.
- **The integral:** writing $(2\ell + 1)P_\ell$ as $dP_{\ell + 1}/dx - dP_{\ell - 1}/dx$, together with $P_\ell(-1) = (-1)^\ell$, gives $\tilde A_\ell = Q[P_{\ell + 1}(\cos\alpha) - P_{\ell - 1}(\cos\alpha)]/[8\pi\varepsilon_0(2\ell + 1)]$, with $\tilde A_0 = Q(1 + \cos\alpha)/(8\pi\varepsilon_0)$.
- **Checks:** with no hole the series is the uniform shell; far away it is the charge $Q(1 + \cos\alpha)/2$; in the example (10 cm, 60°) the centre is at 67.4 V with a field of 169 V/m towards the hole, and on the axis the series approaches the exact ring integral.
