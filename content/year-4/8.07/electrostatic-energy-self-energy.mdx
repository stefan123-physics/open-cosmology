How much energy does it take to put a set of charges together, and where is that energy? The second half of Guth's notes for Lecture 6 answers both questions, then follows the answer to two surprises: the energy of a gravitational field is negative, which matters for stars and for the whole universe, and the energy of a point charge is infinite (notes pp. 4–10). The [previous page](/courses/8-07/integral-form-electrostatics) covered the first half, on the potential and Gauss's law in integral form; the stored energy $\frac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ per unit volume was met for a capacitor in [8.02](/courses/8-02/conductors-and-capacitors). (This page follows the handwritten notes in order, in its own words, with its own examples. Units are SI, as in the notes.)

## Work and potential energy

A charge $q$ moves from $\vec a$ to $\vec b$ through an electrostatic field. The field does work on it (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Work done by the field" tex="W_{E} = \int_{\vec a}^{\vec b}\vec F \cdot d\vec\ell = q\int_{\vec a}^{\vec b}\vec E \cdot d\vec\ell = -q\left[V(\vec b) - V(\vec a)\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

which, like the line integral of the last page, does not depend on the path. To move the particle slowly, I must push against the field, so the work I do is the opposite:

<Formula label="Work done against the field" tex="W_{\text{me}} = -W_{E} = q\left[V(\vec b) - V(\vec a)\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

So $qV(\vec r)$ is the **potential energy** of the charge $q$ at $\vec r$ (notes p. 4, boxed). Guth adds a caution: this is the energy for moving **one** charge in a fixed background of other charges. The energy of the whole arrangement is a different question.

## Building up a set of point charges

To find the energy of an arrangement, build it one charge at a time, counting the work done by me (notes pp. 4–5). Start with empty space: no work. Bring in $q_1$ to $\vec r_1$: still no work, since there is no field yet to push against. (We do not count the work it took to make $q_1$ itself; that comes back later as the self-energy.) Bring $q_2$ to $\vec r_2$, in the field of $q_1$:

<Formula label="Two charges" tex="W = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q_1q_2}{|\vec r_2 - \vec r_1|} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q_1q_2}{r_{12}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

A third charge $q_3$ feels both, so it costs $\Delta W = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(q_1q_3/r_{13} + q_2q_3/r_{23}\right)$, and the total is one term for each pair (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Three charges" tex="W_{\text{tot}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{q_1q_2}{r_{12}} + \frac{q_1q_3}{r_{13}} + \frac{q_2q_3}{r_{23}}\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

The pattern continues. For $n$ charges every pair appears once:

<Formula label="n charges, each pair once" tex="W_{\text{tot}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j>i}\frac{q_iq_j}{r_{ij}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

or, summing over all $i$ and $j$ with $i \neq j$, which counts every pair twice, with a factor $\frac12$:

<Formula label="n charges, symmetric form" tex="W_{\text{tot}} = \frac12\,\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i \neq j}\frac{q_iq_j}{r_{ij}}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

The order in which the charges were brought in does not matter, as it should not for an energy.

## A continuous charge density

For a smooth charge density $\rho(\vec r)$, replace each charge by the charge $\rho(\vec r)\,d^3x$ in a small volume and the sums by integrals (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Energy of a continuous charge density" tex="W = \frac{1}{8\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3x\,d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r)\,\rho(\vec r')}{|\vec r - \vec r'|}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

The sum left out $i = j$; the integral includes $\vec r' = \vec r$. If $\rho$ is a smooth function, that single point makes no difference to the integral. But if $\rho$ contains delta functions, that is, point charges, then $W$ includes the **self-energy** of each point charge, and it diverges. For point charges it is easier to use the discrete sum without $i = j$ (notes p. 6).

## The energy as an integral of ρV

Reorder the double integral: do the integral over $\vec r'$ first (notes p. 6). It is exactly the potential at $\vec r$:

<Formula label="The inner integral is the potential" tex="W = \frac12\int d^3x\,\rho(\vec r)\left[\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r')}{|\vec r - \vec r'|}\right], \qquad \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r')}{|\vec r - \vec r'|} = V(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

so

<Formula label="Energy from charge and potential" tex="W = \frac12\int d^3x\,\rho(\vec r)\,V(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Compare $qV$ for a single charge in a fixed background: the $\frac12$ is there because here every charge is also part of the background for all the others.

## The energy in the field

There is another way to write $W$, using only the field (notes pp. 6–7). Replace $\rho$ by $\varepsilon_0\nabla \cdot \vec E$, and use the product rule $\nabla \cdot (V\vec E) = \nabla V \cdot \vec E + V\,\nabla \cdot \vec E$ with $\nabla V = -\vec E$:

<Formula label="Integration by parts" tex="W = \frac12\varepsilon_0\int d^3x\,V\,\nabla \cdot \vec E = \frac12\varepsilon_0\int d^3x\left[\nabla \cdot (V\vec E) - \nabla V \cdot \vec E\right]" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Over a volume $D$ with boundary $S$ (enough to contain all the charge), the divergence theorem turns the first term into a surface integral:

<Formula label="Volume and surface terms" tex="W = \frac12\varepsilon_0\int_{D} d^3x\,|\vec E|^2 + \frac12\varepsilon_0\oint_S V\vec E \cdot d\vec a" cite="reading1" at="p. 6" />

Now let $D$ be a sphere of radius $R$ centred on the origin, and let $R$ grow (notes p. 7). Far from a finite charge distribution $V$ falls like $1/R$ and $E$ like $1/R^2$, while the area of the sphere grows like $R^2$. The surface term therefore falls like $1/R$ and vanishes as $R \to \infty$, at least for any finite charge distribution. What is left is

<Formula label="Energy stored in the field" tex="W = \frac12\varepsilon_0\int_{\text{all space}}|\vec E|^2\,d^3x" cite="reading1" at="p. 7" />

This invites a picture: the energy is **in the field**, with $\frac12\varepsilon_0|\vec E|^2$ per unit volume, everywhere the field is. The two forms agree for the total energy, but they put it in different places: $\frac12\rho V$ is zero wherever there is no charge, $\frac12\varepsilon_0|\vec E|^2$ is not. Unlike the sum over pairs, the field energy is never negative, because it includes the self-energies.

## Worked example: a uniformly charged ball

<WorkedExample title="Energy of a uniformly charged ball, two ways">
<Problem>
A ball of radius $R = 10$ cm carries $Q = 50$ nC spread uniformly through its volume. Find its electrostatic energy from $\frac12\int\rho V\,d^3x$ and from the field energy, and find how the field energy is shared between inside and outside.
</Problem>
<Step title="From ρV">
Gauss's law gives $E = Qr/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3)$ inside and $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2)$ outside. Integrating inwards from infinity, the potential inside is $V = \frac{Q}{8\pi\varepsilon_0R}\left(3 - r^2/R^2\right)$. With $\rho = 3Q/(4\pi R^3)$, $\frac12\int\rho V\,4\pi r^2\,dr$ from 0 to $R$ gives $\frac35\,Q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R)$.
</Step>
<Step title="From the field">
Write $W_0 = Q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R)$. Inside, $\frac12\varepsilon_0E^2\,4\pi r^2\,dr$ integrates to $W_0/10$; outside it integrates to $W_0/2$. The total, $\frac{6}{10}W_0 = \frac35W_0$, agrees. So one sixth of the energy is inside the ball, five sixths outside, and half of it lies beyond $r = \frac53R$.
</Step>
<Step title="The numbers">
$Q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 8.988 \times 10^9 \times (50 \times 10^{-9})^2 = 2.247 \times 10^{-5}$ J m, so $W_0 = 2.247 \times 10^{-4}$ J and $W = \frac35W_0 \approx 1.35 \times 10^{-4}$ J, about 135 microjoules. Of this, $W_0/10 \approx 2.25 \times 10^{-5}$ J, about 22.5 microjoules, is stored inside the ball.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Gravity: a field with negative energy

Guth then turns to an analogy (notes pp. 7–8). Newton's law of gravity looks like Coulomb's law, except for the constants, **and the sign**: like charges repel, but positive masses attract. In Coulomb's law the force between $q_1$ and $q_2$ has the constant $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$; in Newton's law the force between $m_1$ and $m_2$ has $-G$ in its place, the minus sign meaning attraction. The notes draw the conclusion in words only: the energy density of a Newtonian gravitational field is negative. The formula below is this page's own step, not in the notes. Every step above goes through with $\varepsilon_0$ replaced by $-1/(4\pi G)$. With $\vec g$ the gravitational field (the force per unit mass), the energy becomes

<Formula label="Energy of a Newtonian gravitational field" tex="W_{\text{grav}} = -\frac{1}{8\pi G}\int_{\text{all space}}|\vec g|^2\,d^3x" cite="reading1" at="pp. 7–8 (stated in words)" />

**The energy density of a Newtonian gravitational field is negative.** In general relativity gravitational energy is harder to define, but Guth notes that, to the extent it can be defined, it is also negative. He draws two consequences (notes p. 8).

**Stars.** When a star contracts, its gravitational field inside the volume it used to fill becomes stronger, while far away nothing changes (the field there depends only on the mass). A stronger field means more negative field energy, so energy is released, and it goes into the kinetic energy of the star's particles: a collapsing star heats up. For a uniform ball the energy is $-\frac35GM^2/R$, the gravitational twin of the charged ball above. A uniform ball with the Sun's mass and radius would have $-2.28 \times 10^{41}$ J, about a millionth of its rest energy $Mc^2$ ($1.79 \times 10^{47}$ J); halving the radius would release another $2.28 \times 10^{41}$ J. (The real Sun is denser in the middle, so its gravitational energy is larger in size; these round numbers are only to show the scale.)

**Cosmology.** The gravitational field that fills the universe contributes negatively to the total energy.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="A universe with zero total energy">
Guth's notes draw the cosmological conclusion directly: despite the huge energy $Mc^2$ of the matter in the universe, its total energy is consistent with zero, because the gravitational field's energy is negative. This, he writes, makes it possible for inflation "to build an arbitrarily large universe starting from something incredibly small." In 8.286 he counts negative gravitational energy as one of the two "miracles of physics" behind inflation; see [the overview of inflation](/courses/8-286/inflation-overview).
</Callout>

## The self-energy of a point charge

Back to the self-energy left out of the sum over pairs (notes p. 9). A single point charge has the field $\vec E = q\,\hat r/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$. Its field energy, integrated in shells of area $4\pi r^2$, is

<Formula label="Self-energy of a point charge" tex="W = \frac12\varepsilon_0\,\frac{q^2}{(4\pi\varepsilon_0)^2}\int_0^\infty\left(4\pi r^2\,dr\right)\frac{1}{r^4} = \infty" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

The integrand goes like $1/r^2$, and the integral diverges at $r = 0$.

What should be made of this infinite energy? Classically, Guth says, one can build models that work for some questions, but not for all. Picture the charge as a small ball of radius $r_0$. Its field energy counts towards its mass, so the mass we measure is

<Formula label="Measured mass of a charged ball" tex="m_{\text{phys}} = m_0 + \frac{\frac12\varepsilon_0\int|\vec E|^2\,d^3x}{c^2}" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

where $m_0$ is the mass the particle would have without its field. Now take the limit $r_0 \to 0$ while holding $m_{\text{phys}}$ fixed at its measured value. The field energy grows without bound, so $m_0 \to -\infty$ (the notes add an exclamation mark).

<WorkedExample title="How big must a classical electron be?">
<Problem>
Model the electron as a uniformly charged ball, whose field energy is $\frac35\,e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R)$ (from the example above). How large must $R$ be for the field energy alone to equal the electron's rest energy $m_ec^2 = 0.511$ MeV? And how much is it for $R = 10^{-18}$ m?
</Problem>
<Step title="The radius">
Setting $\frac35\,e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R) = m_ec^2$ gives $R = \frac35\,r_e$, where $r_e = e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 m_ec^2) = 2.82$ fm is the classical electron radius. So $R \approx 1.69$ fm, about twice the charge radius of a proton. A smaller ball would have more field energy than the whole mass of the electron.
</Step>
<Step title="A much smaller ball">
For $R = 10^{-18}$ m, $e^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 2.307 \times 10^{-28}$ J m gives $W = \frac35 \times 2.307 \times 10^{-10}$ J $= 1.38 \times 10^{-10}$ J, or about 864 MeV: some 1690 times the electron's rest energy. To keep the measured mass at 0.511 MeV, $m_0c^2$ would have to be about $-863$ MeV. As $R \to 0$ it goes to $-\infty$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Radiation reaction: the Abraham–Lorentz force

According to the notes, this limit, $r_0 \to 0$ with $m_{\text{phys}}$ fixed, applied to an accelerated charged ball, gives the **Abraham–Lorentz formula** for the radiation-reaction force (notes p. 9):

<Formula label="Abraham–Lorentz force" tex="\vec F_{\text{self}} = \frac{\mu_0q^2}{6\pi c}\,\dot{\vec a}" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

where $\dot{\vec a}$ is the rate of change of the acceleration. (The notes label the force with the subscript "rad"; this page calls it the self-force, so that read-aloud does not take the label for the unit radian. The notes state the result without derivation. At the end of the course Guth returns to it: he does not prove it, but shows, using the energy radiated by an accelerated charge, that it is consistent with energy conservation, at least for periodic motion, and calls the derivation from an extended charged object inconclusive, since it depends on the model.) For an electron the force is usually written $m_e\tau\,\dot{\vec a}$ with $\tau = \mu_0e^2/(6\pi m_ec) = 6.27 \times 10^{-24}$ s, a tiny time, so the force matters only for very rapid changes of acceleration.

For an **oscillating** charge, $\vec v$ and $\dot{\vec a}$ point in opposite directions. (For $x = A\cos\omega t$, $\dot a = -\omega^2 v$.) The formula then describes **damping**, and the energy it removes correctly matches the energy the charge radiates away (notes p. 9).

But the same formula misbehaves (notes p. 10). If I push on a charged particle to start it accelerating, $\dot{\vec a}$ points along $\vec v$, so $\vec F_{\text{self}}$ points in the same direction as $\vec v$, and the equation of motion has **runaway solutions**. Guth calls such solutions "clearly an error associated with treating the point charge." (An illustration, this page's own: with no outside force at all, $m_ea = m_e\tau\,\dot a$ is solved by an acceleration that grows as $e^{t/\tau}$, multiplying by $e$ every $6 \times 10^{-24}$ s. Guth solves this equation in the notes for the last lecture, Lecture Notes 16, slide 23, eqs. (38)–(40); see [the Larmor formula and radiation reaction](/courses/8-07/larmor-formula-radiation-reaction).)

In quantum electrodynamics (QED), the problem of point-charge infinities is under better control. Guth promised to say more about this in the next class.

## Summary

- Moving a charge $q$ from $\vec a$ to $\vec b$ against the field takes work $q[V(\vec b) - V(\vec a)]$, so $qV$ is the potential energy of one charge in a fixed background of others.
- Assembling point charges costs $W = \frac12\,\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_{i\neq j}q_iq_j/r_{ij}$, one term per pair. For a continuous density this becomes $W = \frac12\int\rho V\,d^3x$, which includes self-energies and diverges for point charges.
- Using Gauss's law and a surface term that falls like $1/R$, $W = \frac12\varepsilon_0\int|\vec E|^2\,d^3x$ over all space: the energy can be seen as stored in the field. A uniformly charged ball has $\frac35\,Q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R)$, one sixth of it inside.
- Gravity has the same law with the opposite sign, so a Newtonian gravitational field has negative energy, $-|\vec g|^2/(8\pi G)$ per unit volume. Collapsing stars heat up, and, as Guth notes, the total energy of the universe is consistent with zero, which lets inflation build a large universe from something tiny.
- A point charge has infinite field energy. Keeping the measured mass fixed as the radius goes to zero needs a bare mass going to minus infinity, and leads to the Abraham–Lorentz force $\mu_0q^2\dot{\vec a}/(6\pi c)$: it damps an oscillating charge correctly but allows runaway solutions, a sign that classical point charges are not fully consistent.
