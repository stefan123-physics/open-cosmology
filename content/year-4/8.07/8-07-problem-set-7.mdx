This set practises magnetostatics: the magnetic force on a moving charge, the [Biot–Savart law](/courses/8-02/biot-savart-law), the vector potential, and the magnetic dipole of [Lecture Notes 10](/courses/8-07/magnetic-multipole-expansion-dipoles) and [Lecture Notes 11](/courses/8-07/ideal-magnetic-dipole-current), with its [delta-function term](/courses/8-07/magnetic-dipole-force-torque-maxwell). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 7 (the revised sheet of October 30, 2012), in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its due date and its reading assignment (a textbook chapter) are left out. MIT's Problems 1, 2 and 4 are only pointers to problems in a textbook (Griffiths), named by number. The textbook is not used here: **Problems 1, 2 and 4 are our own problems** on the subjects that MIT's titles name (the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron, examples of the Biot–Savart law, and the vector potential of a uniform field), with MIT's points. Problem 5 points to a textbook problem for its parts (a) to (c) and adds a part (d) of its own: **parts (a) to (c) of Problem 5 are our own**, and part (d) is MIT's, rewritten. Problem 6 is again a textbook pointer, but MIT adds two instructions: in one part the average field of a current distribution is to be taken from a given result without proof, and the far vector potential is to be found in the dipole approximation. **The setup and the parts of Problem 6 are ours**, built so that MIT's two instructions apply; the result MIT says to use without proof is stated in the problem. MIT's Problem Set 8 repeats this problem as its Problem 1 ("held over from Problem Set 7"); on this site it stays here, and our Set 8 will link back to it. Problems 3 and 7 are stated in full on MIT's sheet and are given here as MIT sets them. Our additions to them are marked: the worked numbers of Problem 3(a), the number box of Problem 7(d) and the choice box of Problem 7(e).

Three small slips of the sheet are corrected. The title of MIT's Problem 1 spells the physicist's name "Thompson"; he is J. J. Thomson. In Problem 3 the sheet asks for many turns per length compared with $1/a$; the cylinder's radius is $R$, so it means $1/R$. At the very end of Problem 7 the surface term is written with $\varphi(\vec r)$; it is $\varphi(\vec 0)$, as in MIT's own corrected Eq. (7.8). The first page of the sheet has no printed number; "p. 1" below means that page, and "p. 2" to "p. 4" are the printed numbers of the others.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `mu0*I/(2*R)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `pi` stands for $\pi$, `mu0` for $\mu_0$, `w` for $\omega$, `th` for $\theta$, `qe` and `qm` for $q_e$ and $q_m$, and `z1`, `z2` for $z_1$, $z_2$; the other letters (`E`, `B`, `R`, `I`, `L`, `s`, `z`, `n`, `x`, `y`, `q`, `r`, `Q`) are typed as they are.

MIT gives the points per problem: 10 for Problem 1, 15 each for Problems 2 and 3, 10 for Problem 4, 20 for Problem 5, 25 for Problem 6 and 20 for Problem 7, 115 points in all, plus 10 points of extra credit for Problem 7(e), which is optional here. MIT gives the points of the parts of Problems 3, 5 and 7; the splits in Problems 1, 2, 4 and 6 are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes. $\vec B$ is the magnetic field, $\vec A$ the vector potential with $\vec B = \nabla \times \vec A$, $\mu_0$ the magnetic constant and $\varepsilon_0$ the electric constant. $\vec r$ is the position, $r = |\vec r|$, $\hat r = \vec r/r$, and in index notation $x_i$ are the components of $\vec r$, $\hat r_i = x_i/r$ and $\partial_i = \partial/\partial x_i$. In spherical coordinates $\theta$ is the angle from the $z$ axis and $\hat\phi$ points around it. $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta and $\delta^3(\vec r)$ the delta function in three dimensions.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="J. J. Thomson and the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron (our own problem)" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "J. J. Thompson and the charge to mass ratio of the electron". The problem below is our own, on the same subject. It shows the idea of a crossed-field measurement; it is not a description of Thomson's own apparatus.

A beam of electrons, all with the same speed $v$, travels along the $x$ axis through a region with a uniform electric field $\vec E$ along $y$ and a uniform magnetic field $\vec B$ along $z$. An electron has the charge $-e$ and the mass $m_e$; its weight is negligible. The force on it is the Lorentz force,

<Formula label="The force on a moving charge" tex="\vec F = q\left(\vec E + \vec v \times \vec B\right)" cite="reading2" at="slide 15 (the magnetic part, q v × B, boxed)" />

with $q = -e$.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

The two fields are adjusted until the beam goes straight through. What is the speed of the electrons, in terms of the sizes $E$ and $B$ of the fields?

<Hint>

The beam goes straight when the electric and the magnetic force cancel. Write both out for a velocity along $x$; the charge cancels.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-v" label="$v =$" expr="E/B"
  vars={{ E: [1, 3], B: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ E: "E", B: "B" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "B/E", hint: "That is upside down. Check the units: a field in volts per metre divided by a field in tesla is a speed, because one tesla is one volt-second per square metre." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a velocity across both fields the magnetic force is along the electric one, and its size grows with the speed. Only one speed makes the two cancel.

<Step title="Balance the forces">

With $\vec v = v\,\hat x$ and $\vec B = B\,\hat z$, $\vec v \times \vec B = vB\,(\hat x \times \hat z) = -vB\,\hat y$. The total force is $q(E - vB)\,\hat y$, which vanishes for

<Formula label="The speed selected by crossed fields" tex="v = \frac{E}{B}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The charge, its sign and the mass drop out, so the crossed fields select one speed for any particle.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: V/m divided by T = V s/m² gives m/s, a speed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Now the electric field is switched off and the magnetic field left as it was. The electrons move on a circle, and its radius $R$ is measured. Find $e/m_e$ in terms of $E$, $B$ and $R$.

<Hint>

The magnetic force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it does not change the speed. It supplies the centripetal force of a circle: set $e v B$ equal to $m_e v^2/R$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Solve that for $e/m_e$, then put in the speed from part (a).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-em" label="$e/m_e =$" expr="E/(B^2*R)"
  vars={{ E: [1, 3], B: [0.5, 2], R: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ E: "E", B: "B", R: "R" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "E/(B*R)", hint: "That is v/R, the angular frequency of the motion on the circle, in units of 1/s. To reach e/mₑ you still have to divide by B." },
    { expr: "B^2*R/E", hint: "That is the inverse, mₑ/e. A larger charge per mass bends the beam more strongly, so for a given speed and field it gives a smaller radius: R stands in the denominator." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The circle's radius measures the momentum per charge, $m_e v/e = R B$, and part (a) measured the speed. Together they give the charge per mass.

<Step title="The circle">

The magnetic force has the size $evB$ and points to the centre, so

<Formula label="The magnetic force as the centripetal force" tex="e\,v\,B = \frac{m_e v^2}{R}, \qquad \frac{e}{m_e} = \frac{v}{B\,R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put in the speed">

With $v = E/B$ from part (a):

<Formula label="The charge-to-mass ratio from the two measurements" tex="\frac{e}{m_e} = \frac{E}{B^2 R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: (V/m)/(T² m) = (V/m²)/(V² s²/m⁴) = m²/(V s²). Since one volt is one joule per coulomb, that is m² C/(J s²) = C/kg, a charge per mass.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

In a test run the beam goes straight for $E = 1.20\times10^4$ V/m and $B = 0.800$ mT. With the electric field off, the radius of the circle is measured as $R = 10.4$ cm. What value of $e/m_e$ does this give? Answer in units of $10^{11}$ C/kg.

<Hint>

First the speed, $E/B$; then $v/(BR)$. Keep the field in tesla and the radius in metres.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-num" label="$e/m_e$ in units of $10^{11}$ C/kg:" value="1.803" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into the result of part (b).

<Step title="The numbers">

The speed is $v = E/B = 1.20\times10^4/(8.00\times10^{-4}) = 1.50\times10^7$ m/s, about 5% of the speed of light. Then

<Formula label="The measured charge-to-mass ratio" tex="\frac{e}{m_e} = \frac{v}{B\,R} = \frac{1.50\times10^7}{8.00\times10^{-4} \times 0.104}\ \text{C/kg} = 1.803\times10^{11}\ \text{C/kg}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own numbers)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The CODATA 2018 value is $e/m_e = 1.759\times10^{11}$ C/kg, so this run is 2.5% high: the circle measured 10.4 cm, where the true value for these fields would be 10.66 cm. A measured radius a few per cent short is easy to get from a beam that fans out or a field that is not quite uniform.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Examples of the use of the Biot–Savart law (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Examples of the use of the Biot–Savart law". The problem below is our own, on the same subject. A thin wire carries the steady current $I$. Each piece $d\vec\ell$ of it makes the field

<Formula label="The Biot–Savart law" tex="d\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec\ell \times \hat u}{u^2}" cite="mit802-ch9" at="Eq. (9.1.1), p. 2" />

where $\vec u$ is the vector from the piece of wire to the point where the field is wanted, $u$ its length and $\hat u$ its direction.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

A straight piece of wire of length $L$ carries the current $I$ (the rest of the circuit is far away and is left out). Find the size of its field at a point at the distance $s$ from the wire, opposite the middle of the piece.

<Hint>

Put the piece along the $x$ axis from $-L/2$ to $L/2$ and the point at $(0, s, 0)$. For every piece $dx$, the cross product $d\vec\ell \times \hat u$ points the same way, along $z$; only its size changes with $x$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The size of $d\vec\ell \times \hat u$ is $dx$ times the sine of the angle between them, which is $s/u$, with $u^2 = x^2 + s^2$. The integral of $(x^2 + s^2)^{-3/2}$ is $x/(s^2\sqrt{x^2 + s^2})$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-seg" label="$B =$" expr="mu0*I*L/(2*pi*s*sqrt(4*s^2 + L^2))"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], I: [1, 3], L: [0.5, 3], s: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", I: "I", L: "L", s: "s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*I/(2*pi*s)", hint: "That is the field of an infinitely long wire. It is the limit of the right answer for L much longer than s, but this piece is finite." },
    { expr: "mu0*I*L/(4*pi*s*sqrt(4*s^2 + L^2))", hint: "That is half the answer: the field of one half of the piece, from its middle to one end. Both halves add, with the same direction." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** All pieces of a straight wire give fields in the same direction at the point, so the vector integral is a plain integral of sizes.

<Step title="The integral">

With the piece along $x$ and the point at $(0, s, 0)$, each piece gives $dB = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\dfrac{s\,dx}{(x^2 + s^2)^{3/2}}$, all along $\hat z$ for a current in the $+x$ direction. So

<Formula label="The field opposite the middle of a straight piece" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 I s}{4\pi}\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}\frac{dx}{(x^2 + s^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi s}\,\frac{L}{\sqrt{s^2 + L^2/4}} = \frac{\mu_0 I L}{2\pi s\sqrt{4s^2 + L^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

In words: $\mu_0 I/(4\pi s)$ times $2\sin\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle at which each half of the piece is seen from the point, measured from the perpendicular.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $L \gg s$ the square root is $L$ and $B \to \mu_0 I/(2\pi s)$, the field of a long straight wire. For $L \ll s$ it is $\mu_0 I L/(4\pi s^2)$, the Biot–Savart law for one short piece seen from the side.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

A wire is bent into an equilateral triangle of side $L = 10.0$ cm and carries $I = 2.00$ A. How large is the field at the centre of the triangle? Answer in microtesla, with $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ T m/A.

<Hint>

By symmetry the three sides give equal fields, all perpendicular to the triangle and in the same direction. Each side is a straight piece seen from opposite its middle.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The distance from the centre of an equilateral triangle to each side is $L/(2\sqrt3)$. Use part (a) with that $s$, and multiply by three.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-tri" label="$B$ at the centre, in μT:" value="36.0" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-tri" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A loop made of straight pieces gets its field by adding the result of part (a) for each piece.

<Step title="One side">

With $s = L/(2\sqrt3)$, $4s^2 + L^2 = \tfrac43 L^2$ and the square root is $2L/\sqrt3$. Part (a) gives for one side $\sqrt3\,\mu_0 I/(4\pi s) = 3\mu_0 I/(2\pi L)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Three sides">

<Formula label="The field at the centre of a triangle" tex="B = 3 \times \frac{3\mu_0 I}{2\pi L} = \frac{9\mu_0 I}{2\pi L} = \frac{9 \times 4\pi\times10^{-7} \times 2.00}{2\pi \times 0.100}\ \text{T} = 3.60\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

That is 36.0 μT.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The triangle lies between its inscribed circle, of radius $s = 2.887$ cm, and its circumscribed circle, of radius $2s = 5.774$ cm. A ring of current gives $\mu_0 I/(2R)$ at its centre: 43.5 μT for the inner circle and 21.8 μT for the outer one. The triangle's 36.0 μT lies between them, nearer the inner circle, which runs close to the middle of each side, where most of the field comes from.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

A circular ring of radius $R$ carries the current $I$. Find the field at the point on the ring's axis at the height $z$ above its centre: its size, which is also its component along the axis.

<Hint>

Every piece of the ring is at the same distance $\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}$ from the point, and perpendicular to the line to it. So every piece makes a field of the same size.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The pieces' fields point in different directions, on a cone around the axis. The parts across the axis cancel in pairs from opposite sides. Only the part along the axis adds up; it is the size times $R/\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ring" label="$B_z =$" expr="mu0*I*R^2/(2*(R^2 + z^2)^(3/2))"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], I: [1, 3], R: [0.5, 2], z: [0.2, 2] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", I: "I", R: "R", z: "z" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*I*R/(2*(R^2 + z^2))", hint: "That adds the sizes of all the pieces' fields. They point in different directions on a cone: only their components along the axis add, a factor R/√(R² + z²) of each." },
    { expr: "mu0*I/(2*R)", hint: "That is the field at the centre of the ring, z = 0. Higher up the axis the field falls off, as 1/z³ far away." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the axis every piece of the ring is equivalent, so the integral is just the circumference times one piece's axial component.

<Step title="One piece">

A piece $d\ell$ is perpendicular to $\vec u$, so its field has the size $\dfrac{\mu_0 I\,d\ell}{4\pi(R^2 + z^2)}$. Its component along the axis is that times $R/\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}$.

</Step>

<Step title="The whole ring">

The components across the axis cancel between opposite pieces. Adding the axial ones over the length $2\pi R$:

<Formula label="The field on the axis of a ring" tex="B_z = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\frac{2\pi R}{R^2 + z^2}\,\frac{R}{\sqrt{R^2 + z^2}} = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2\left(R^2 + z^2\right)^{3/2}}" cite="mit802-ch9" at="Eq. (9.1.15), p. 8" />

For a current running counterclockwise as seen from above, the field points up the axis.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At the centre, $z = 0$, it gives $\mu_0 I/(2R)$. Far away, $z \gg R$, it is $\mu_0 I R^2/(2z^3) = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\dfrac{2m}{z^3}$ with $m = I\pi R^2$: the field of a magnetic dipole on its axis, $\dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\dfrac{3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec m}{r^3}$ with $\hat r$ along $\vec m$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="The magnetic field on the axis of a tightly wound solenoid" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

A cylinder of radius $R$ stands on the $z$ axis, from $z = z_1$ to $z = z_2$, with $z_2 > z_1$. A wire carrying the current $I$ is wound evenly around it, $n$ turns per unit length, running counterclockwise as seen from the positive $z$ direction. Strictly the wire is a helix, but with many turns per length ($n$ large compared with $1/R$) each turn can be treated as a circle in a plane of constant $z$.

<Part label="a" points={10}>

Find the magnetic field at the origin. Then, as a worked case (our addition), take $R = 2.0$ cm, $z_1 = -5.0$ cm and $z_2 = 10.0$ cm, and give the field at the origin as a fraction of $\mu_0 n I$.

<Hint>

Cut the winding into thin rings. The slice between $z'$ and $z' + dz'$ holds $n\,dz'$ turns, so it is a ring carrying the current $nI\,dz'$. Problem 2(c) gives its field on the axis, at the distance $|z'|$ from its plane.

</Hint>

<Hint>

All the rings' fields at the origin point along $+z$. Add them with an integral over $z'$ from $z_1$ to $z_2$. An antiderivative of $(R^2 + z'^2)^{-3/2}$ is $z'/(R^2\sqrt{R^2 + z'^2})$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-b" label="$B_z$ at the origin $=$" expr="mu0*n*I/2*(z2/sqrt(R^2 + z2^2) - z1/sqrt(R^2 + z1^2))"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], n: [1, 3], I: [1, 3], R: [0.5, 2], z1: [-2, 0.5], z2: [1, 3] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", n: "n", I: "I", R: "R", z1: "z_1", z2: "z_2" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*n*I/2*(z2/sqrt(R^2 + z2^2) + z1/sqrt(R^2 + z1^2))", hint: "The antiderivative is taken at the upper limit minus the lower limit. With z₁ negative, as for a winding around the origin, the minus sign makes the lower end add to the field, as it must: every ring's field points the same way." },
    { expr: "mu0*n*I*(z2/sqrt(R^2 + z2^2) - z1/sqrt(R^2 + z1^2))", hint: "That is twice the answer. Check it on a single ring: a ring at z′ = 0 gives μ₀I/(2R), with the factor 1/2." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-frac" label="For the worked case, $B_z$ at the origin divided by $\mu_0 n I$:" value="0.955" tolerance="1%" calc="p3-frac" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A tightly wound solenoid is a stack of rings, and on the axis the field of each ring is known. The total is one integral.

<Step title="One slice">

The slice at $z'$ carries the current $nI\,dz'$ around a circle of radius $R$, at the distance $|z'|$ from the origin along the axis. By Problem 2(c) its field at the origin is

<Formula label="The field of one slice" tex="dB_z = \frac{\mu_0\,nI\,dz'\,R^2}{2\left(R^2 + z'^2\right)^{3/2}}" cite="mit802-ch9" at="Eq. (9.1.15), p. 8 (with the current n I dz′)" />

pointing along $+z$ for a counterclockwise current.

</Step>

<Step title="Add the slices">

<Formula label="The field at the origin" tex="B_z(0) = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2}\int_{z_1}^{z_2}\frac{R^2\,dz'}{\left(R^2 + z'^2\right)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0 nI}{2}\left[\frac{z_2}{\sqrt{R^2 + z_2^2}} - \frac{z_1}{\sqrt{R^2 + z_1^2}}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The field points along $+z$; the components across the axis are zero, as for each ring. Each fraction is the cosine of an angle: $z_2/\sqrt{R^2 + z_2^2} = \cos\alpha_2$, where $\alpha_2$ is the angle between the axis and the line from the origin to the rim of the top end. So $B_z(0) = \tfrac12\mu_0 nI(\cos\alpha_2 + \cos\alpha_1)$, with $\alpha_1$ measured in the same way towards the bottom end.

</Step>

<Step title="The worked case">

With lengths in cm: $10/\sqrt{4 + 100} = 0.9806$ and $-(-5)/\sqrt{4 + 25} = 0.9285$. Half their sum is $0.955$, so the field at the origin is $0.955\,\mu_0 nI$: within 5% of its value deep inside a very long solenoid, although the bottom end is only 2.5 radii away.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="The vector potential for a uniform field (our own problem)" points={10} mit="Problem 4">

<Part label="a" points={5}>

<Hint>

For a constant vector $\vec c$, $\nabla \times (\vec c \times \vec r) = \vec c\,(\nabla \cdot \vec r) - (\vec c \cdot \nabla)\,\vec r$. What are $\nabla \cdot \vec r$ and $(\vec c \cdot \nabla)\,\vec r$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

<Answer id="p4-a" label="$|\vec A| =$" expr="B*s/2"
  vars={{ B: [1, 3], s: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ B: "B", s: "s" }}
  mistakes={[

<Solution>

**The idea.** The curl of $\vec c \times \vec r$ for a constant $\vec c$ is $2\vec c$, a result of Lecture Notes 11. Half of it is then the field.

<Step title="The curl">

Lecture Notes 11 work out the curl of a basis vector crossed with $\vec r$:

<Formula label="The curl of a constant vector crossed with r" tex="\nabla \times \left(\hat e_i \times \vec r\right) = \hat e_i\left(\nabla \cdot \vec r\right) - \left(\hat e_i \cdot \nabla\right)\vec r = 3\,\hat e_i - \hat e_i = 2\,\hat e_i" cite="reading2" at="slide 3" />

In components $\vec A = \tfrac12 B\,(-y, x, 0)$, so $\nabla \cdot \vec A = \tfrac12 B\left(\partial_x(-y) + \partial_y x\right) = 0$.

</Step>

$|\vec A| = \tfrac12 B\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \tfrac12 Bs$. The vector points around the $z$ axis, along $\hat\phi$, like the current of a ring that would make this field.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Stokes' theorem: the line integral of $\vec A$ around a circle of radius $s$ about the $z$ axis is $2\pi s \cdot \tfrac12 Bs = \pi s^2 B$, the flux of $\vec B$ through the circle.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

The curl of $(0, Bx, 0)$ has only one component that is not obviously zero. For the gauge function, write out the difference $\vec A' - \vec A$ in components.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The difference is $\tfrac12 B\,(y, x, 0)$. Which function has the derivative $\tfrac12 By$ in $x$ and $\tfrac12 Bx$ in $y$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-lam" label="$\lambda =$" expr="B*x*y/2"
  vars={{ B: [1, 3], x: [0.5, 2], y: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ B: "B", x: "x", y: "y" }}

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="A charged particle in the field of a magnetic monopole" points={20} mit="Problem 5">

Magnetic monopoles are not part of Maxwell's equations as they stand, but grand unified theories predict them, and 8.286 asks why the universe holds so few ([magnetic monopoles](/courses/8-286/magnetic-monopoles)). Here we only ask how a charge would move near one. Take a monopole of magnetic charge $q_m$ fixed at the origin, with the field

Does the particle's speed change as it moves?

**The idea.** A magnetic force never does work.

The rate of work is $\vec F \cdot \vec v = q_e(\vec v \times \vec B) \cdot \vec v = 0$, since $\vec v \times \vec B$ is perpendicular to $\vec v$. So $\tfrac12 Mv^2$ is constant, and so is the speed $v$. Nothing here uses the shape of the field: it holds for any magnetic field.

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

<Step title="The derivative">

## Problem 6

<Formula label="The vector potential of a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec A(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{\vec m \times \hat r}{r^2} + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="p. 5 (boxed)" />

With $\vec m = m\,\hat z$, $\hat z \times \hat r = \sin\theta\,\hat\phi$. So

<Formula label="The far vector potential of the spinning ball" tex="\vec A(r, \theta) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{q\,\omega R^2}{5}\,\frac{\sin\theta}{r^2}\,\hat\phi = \frac{\mu_0\,q\,\omega R^2\sin\theta}{20\pi\,r^2}\,\hat\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The potential circles the $z$ axis, the way the charge moves, and vanishes on the axis, where the symmetry allows no direction around it. Its curl gives the familiar dipole field, whose radial component goes as $2\cos\theta/r^3$ and whose component along $\hat\theta$ goes as $\sin\theta/r^3$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={6}>

How good is the dipole approximation of part (c) at points outside the ball, $r > R$, that are not far away? Use the magnetic multipole expansion of Lecture Notes 10, whose moments are integrals of $J_j$ times traceless products of coordinates.

<Hint>

The expansion is exact at any point farther from the origin than all of the current. Each moment of degree $\ell$ is an integral of $J_j$ times $\{x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_\ell}\}$, a traceless symmetric product of $\ell$ coordinates.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$J_j$ is $\rho$ times $(\vec\omega \times \vec r)_j$, a constant combination of the coordinates: it is linear in $\hat r$ at each radius. The integral over directions of one factor $\hat r_k$ times a traceless product of degree $\ell$. What does the orthogonality of spherical harmonics of different degree say about this integral?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-exact" label="For $r > R$ the dipole result of part (c) is" choices={["exact at every point outside the ball, r > R", "accurate only far from the ball, where r ≫ R", "accurate outside, but only to first order in R/r", "exact only in the equatorial plane θ = π/2"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The multipole expansion is exact outside the current, so the question is which moments vanish. The current of a spinning, spherically symmetric charge has the angular shape of degree 1, so every moment but the dipole is zero.

<Step title="The moments">

Lecture Notes 10 expand the vector potential of a localized current as

<Formula label="The magnetic multipole expansion" tex="A_j(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{\ell + 1}}\,\mathcal M^{(\ell)}_{j;\,i_1\cdots i_\ell}\,\{\hat r_{i_1}\cdots\hat r_{i_\ell}\}" cite="reading1" at="p. 2 (boxed)" />

with the moments

<Formula label="The magnetic multipole moments" tex="\mathcal M^{(\ell)}_{j;\,i_1\cdots i_\ell} = \frac{(2\ell - 1)!!}{\ell\,!}\int J_j(\vec r)\,\{x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_\ell}\}\,d^3x" cite="reading1" at="p. 2 (boxed)" />

The series comes from the expansion of $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ in powers of $r'/r$, which converges for every $r$ larger than all the $r'$ of the current: at every point outside the ball.

</Step>

<Step title="Only degree 1 survives">

Here $J_j = \rho\,r\,(\vec\omega \times \hat r)_j$, and $(\vec\omega \times \hat r)_j$ is a fixed combination of the three components of $\hat r$, with coefficients made of the components of $\vec\omega$. So each moment is an integral over $r$ times angular integrals of the form $\int\hat r_k\,\{\hat r_{i_1}\cdots\hat r_{i_\ell}\}\,d\Omega$. A traceless symmetric product of degree $\ell$ is a combination of spherical harmonics of degree $\ell$, and $\hat r_k$ of degree 1, so the angular integral vanishes unless $\ell = 1$ ([traceless tensors and spherical harmonics](/courses/8-07/spherical-harmonics-from-traceless-tensors)). For $\ell = 0$ it is $\int\hat r_k\,d\Omega = 0$ directly. Only the dipole term is left.

</Step>

<Step title="The answer">

So for all $r > R$, not only far away, the vector potential is exactly the dipole result of part (c), and the field outside the ball is exactly a dipole field. The same holds for any spinning, spherically symmetric charge, such as a thin shell.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The average of part (b) fits: the result MIT stated was the same for any currents inside the sphere, and the dipole moment alone fixes it, as it fixes the outside field here.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="A delta-function identity and its consequences" points={20} mit="Problem 7">

Guth notes that essentially every equation of electromagnetism with a delta function in it can be derived from one identity for the derivatives of $\hat r_j/r^2$:

<Formula label="The identity" tex="\partial_i\left(\frac{\hat r_j}{r^2}\right) \equiv \partial_i\left(\frac{x_j}{r^3}\right) = \frac{\delta_{ij} - 3\hat r_i\hat r_j}{r^3} + \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, Eq. (7.1), p. 2" />

Here $\vec r = x_i\hat e_i = x\hat e_x + y\hat e_y + z\hat e_z$, $r = |\vec r|$, $\hat r = \vec r/r$, $\hat r_i = x_i/r$ and $\partial_i = \partial/\partial x_i$. (The sheet adds that Guth had not seen this identity in the literature. Lecture Notes 11 use it, in the form of the second derivatives of $1/r$, on slide 20.) Parts (a) to (c) use it; part (d) checks it.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Show that the well-known identity

<Formula label="The Laplacian of one over r" tex="\nabla^2\left(\frac1r\right) = -4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a), Eq. (7.2), p. 2" />

follows at once from the identity. This part has no box.

<Hint>

MIT's hint: $x_j/r^3 = -\partial_j(1/r)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Set $i = j$ and sum over $i$. What is $\delta_{ii}$, and what is $\hat r_i\hat r_i$?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The Laplacian is the trace of the matrix of second derivatives, and the trace of the identity is easy.

<Step title="Take the trace">

With $x_j/r^3 = -\partial_j(1/r)$, the left side summed over $i = j$ is $-\partial_i\partial_i(1/r) = -\nabla^2(1/r)$. On the right, $\delta_{ii} = 3$ and $\hat r_i\hat r_i = 1$, so the first term is $(3 - 3)/r^3 = 0$ and the second is $\tfrac{4\pi}{3} \cdot 3\,\delta^3(\vec r) = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r)$. So $-\nabla^2(1/r) = 4\pi\delta^3(\vec r)$, which is the identity to be shown.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Integrated over a ball, the left side is the flux of $\nabla(1/r) = -\hat r/r^2$ out of the sphere, $-4\pi$, whatever the radius: Gauss's law for a unit point charge.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Using the identity, show that the potential of an electric dipole,

<Formula label="The potential of an electric dipole" tex="V_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\hat r \cdot \vec p}{r^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(b), Eq. (7.3), p. 2" />

can be differentiated directly to give the field of the dipole, delta-function term included:

<Formula label="The field of an electric dipole" tex="\vec E_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = -\nabla V_{\text{dip}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{3(\vec p \cdot \hat r)\,\hat r - \vec p}{r^3} - \frac{1}{3\varepsilon_0}\,\vec p\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(b), Eq. (7.4), p. 2" />

This part has no box.

<Hint>

Write $\hat r \cdot \vec p/r^2 = p_j\,\hat r_j/r^2$. The dipole moment is constant, so $\partial_i$ acts only on $\hat r_j/r^2$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** In index notation the potential is $p_j$ times the very function the identity differentiates.

<Step title="Differentiate">

$E_i = -\partial_i V_{\text{dip}} = -\dfrac{p_j}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\partial_i\left(\dfrac{\hat r_j}{r^2}\right)$. By the identity,

<Formula label="The field in index notation" tex="E_i = -\frac{p_j}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{\delta_{ij} - 3\hat r_i\hat r_j}{r^3} + \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta^3(\vec r)\right] = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{3(\vec p \cdot \hat r)\,\hat r_i - p_i}{r^3} - \frac{p_i}{3\varepsilon_0}\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

which is the field above, component by component.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The delta-function term agrees with the field of an ideal electric dipole in Lecture Notes 11, $-\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\,\vec p\,\delta^3(\vec r)$ inside the bracket with $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$ in front (slide 23).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Again using the identity, show that the vector potential of a magnetic dipole,

<Formula label="The vector potential of a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec A_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{\vec m \times \hat r}{r^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(c), Eq. (7.5), p. 3" />

leads directly to the field of the magnetic dipole,

<Formula label="The field of a magnetic dipole from its vector potential" tex="\vec B_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \nabla \times \vec A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\,\hat r - \vec m}{r^3} + \frac{2\mu_0}{3}\,\vec m\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(c), Eq. (7.6), p. 3" />

This part has no box.

<Hint>

$\vec A_{\text{dip}} = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\vec m \times \vec F$ with $\vec F = \vec r/r^3$. For a constant $\vec m$, $\nabla \times (\vec m \times \vec F) = \vec m\,(\nabla \cdot \vec F) - (\vec m \cdot \nabla)\,\vec F$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Both terms are pieces of the identity: $\nabla \cdot \vec F = \partial_j(x_j/r^3)$ is its trace, and $[(\vec m \cdot \nabla)\vec F]_i = m_j\,\partial_j(x_i/r^3)$. The identity is symmetric in $i$ and $j$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The curl of a constant vector crossed with $\vec r/r^3$ needs only the first derivatives of $x_i/r^3$, and the identity gives all of them.

<Step title="The two terms">

The trace of the identity, from part (a), is $\nabla \cdot \vec F = \partial_j(x_j/r^3) = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r)$. The other term is

<Formula label="The directional derivative" tex="\left[(\vec m \cdot \nabla)\vec F\right]_i = m_j\,\partial_j\left(\frac{x_i}{r^3}\right) = \frac{m_i - 3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\,\hat r_i}{r^3} + \frac{4\pi}{3}\,m_i\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Put them together">

<Formula label="The curl of the dipole's vector potential" tex="\nabla \times \vec A_{\text{dip}} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\left[4\pi\,\vec m\,\delta^3(\vec r) - \frac{\vec m - 3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\,\hat r}{r^3} - \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\vec m\,\delta^3(\vec r)\right] = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\,\hat r - \vec m}{r^3} + \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{8\pi}{3}\,\vec m\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

and $\tfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi} \cdot \tfrac{8\pi}{3} = \tfrac{2\mu_0}{3}$, which is the field to be shown.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The electric and magnetic delta-function terms differ: $-\tfrac13$ against $+\tfrac23$ of the coefficient of the full delta function, because the magnetic field picks up the trace term $4\pi\delta^3$ as well. Lecture Notes 11 show that it is the $+\tfrac23$ that makes $\nabla \cdot \vec B_{\text{dip}} = 0$ (slides 20–21).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

Now check the identity itself. First show that it holds for $r \ne 0$. Then check the delta-function term by integrating both sides over a small ball of radius $\epsilon$ about the origin. To turn the volume integral

<Formula label="The integral over a small ball" tex="\int_{r < \epsilon}\partial_i\left(\frac{\hat r_j}{r^2}\right)d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(d), Eq. (7.7), p. 3" />

into an integral over the sphere $r = \epsilon$, use the gradient form of the divergence theorem: for any function $T(\vec r)$ on a region $V$ with boundary $S$ and outward unit normal $\hat n$,

<Formula label="The gradient theorem" tex="\int_V\partial_i T\,d^3x = \oint_S T\,\hat n_i\,da" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(d), p. 3 (the identity MIT points to, stated here)" />

It follows from the divergence theorem applied to the vector field $T\,\hat e_i$, and it holds just as well when $T$ is the $j$th component of a vector. As a step on the way (our addition), give the integral of $\cos^2\theta$ over all directions, $\int\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega$, as a decimal.

<Hint>

For $r \ne 0$, differentiate $x_j\,r^{-3}$ with the product rule, using $\partial_i x_j = \delta_{ij}$ and $\partial_i r = x_i/r$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the sphere $r = \epsilon$, $\hat n = \hat r$, $T = \hat r_j/\epsilon^2$ and $da = \epsilon^2\,d\Omega$. The surface integral becomes $\int\hat r_i\hat r_j\,d\Omega$. By symmetry this is a constant times $\delta_{ij}$; its trace tells you the constant.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the right side, the first term integrates to zero over the angles at each radius: the average of $\hat r_i\hat r_j$ over directions is $\delta_{ij}/3$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-cos2" label="$\int\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega =$" value="4.189" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-cos2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Away from the origin the identity is ordinary calculus. At the origin, the integral of the left side over a small ball is a surface integral that does not depend on the radius, and it must be matched by the delta function.

<Step title="Away from the origin">

For $r \ne 0$,

<Formula label="The derivative away from the origin" tex="\partial_i\left(x_j\,r^{-3}\right) = \delta_{ij}\,r^{-3} - 3x_j\,r^{-4}\,\frac{x_i}{r} = \frac{\delta_{ij} - 3\hat r_i\hat r_j}{r^3}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(d), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

which is the identity there, since $\delta^3(\vec r) = 0$ for $r \ne 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The left side over the small ball">

By the gradient theorem, with $\hat n = \hat r$ on the sphere $r = \epsilon$:

<Formula label="The integral as a surface integral" tex="\int_{r < \epsilon}\partial_i\left(\frac{\hat r_j}{r^2}\right)d^3x = \oint_{r = \epsilon}\frac{\hat r_j}{\epsilon^2}\,\hat r_i\,\epsilon^2\,d\Omega = \int\hat r_i\hat r_j\,d\Omega = \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\delta_{ij}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(d), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The last step: the integral is a symmetric tensor that does not change under rotations, so it is $c\,\delta_{ij}$; its trace is $\int 1\,d\Omega = 4\pi = 3c$. For $i = j = z$ it is $\int\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega = 4\pi/3 = 4.189$. The radius $\epsilon$ has dropped out.

</Step>

<Step title="The right side over the small ball">

The first term, $(\delta_{ij} - 3\hat r_i\hat r_j)/r^3$, integrates over the directions at each radius to $(4\pi\delta_{ij} - 3 \cdot \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\delta_{ij})/r^3 = 0$, so it contributes nothing (taking the angular integral first). The delta-function term gives $\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\delta_{ij}$. Both sides agree, for every $\epsilon$: the coefficient $4\pi/3$ is right.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\int\cos^2\theta\,d\Omega = 2\pi\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = 2\pi \cdot \tfrac23 = 4\pi/3$, the same number computed directly.

</Step>

## Problem 7(e)

<Exercise id="p7e" title="The identity in the language of distributions" points={10} mit="Problem 7(e)" optional>

This is the extra-credit part of MIT's Problem 7. Prove the identity of Problem 7 with the definitions of distribution theory. The calculation is close to that of part (d), but the reasoning has to be stated more carefully. A mathematician would object to part (d) that the integral over the small ball was not defined in the first place, because $\hat r_j/r^2$ cannot be differentiated at $r = 0$.

In distribution theory both sides of the identity are given a meaning by multiplying them by an arbitrary **test function** $\varphi(\vec r)$, smooth and falling off fast as $r \to \infty$, and integrating over all space. A distribution is thus a rule that turns each test function into a number. If the two sides give the same number for every test function, they are equal in the sense of distributions. (MIT's footnote adds that this is weaker than ordinary equality: a function that is 1 at $x = 0$ and zero elsewhere is equal, in this sense, to the function that is zero everywhere.) The right-hand side gives

<Formula label="The right-hand side tested" tex="\text{RHS} = \int\varphi(\vec r)\left[\frac{\delta_{ij} - 3\hat r_i\hat r_j}{r^3} + \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta^3(\vec r)\right]d^3x = \int\varphi(\vec r)\,\frac{\delta_{ij} - 3\hat r_i\hat r_j}{r^3}\,d^3x + \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\varphi(\vec 0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(e), Eq. (7.8), p. 3" />

The remaining integral is singular at the origin; it is defined as a limit,

<Formula label="The singular integral as a limit" tex="\int\varphi(\vec r)\,\frac{\delta_{ij} - 3\hat r_i\hat r_j}{r^3}\,d^3x \equiv \lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\int_{r > \epsilon}\varphi(\vec r)\,\frac{\delta_{ij} - 3\hat r_i\hat r_j}{r^3}\,d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(e), Eq. (7.9), p. 4" />

which exists: $d^3x = r^2\,dr\,d\Omega$ cancels two powers of $r$, and the last one is harmless because $\delta_{ij} - 3\hat r_i\hat r_j$ averages to zero over the directions. The left-hand side is **defined** by moving the derivative onto the test function, as in an integration by parts:

<Formula label="The left-hand side tested" tex="\text{LHS} = \int\varphi(\vec r)\,\partial_i\left(\frac{\hat r_j}{r^2}\right)d^3x \equiv -\int\partial_i\varphi(\vec r)\,\frac{\hat r_j}{r^2}\,d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(e), Eqs. (7.10a) and (7.10b), p. 4" />

The last integral is well defined, since $\hat r_j/r^2$ times $r^2\,dr$ is bounded. So nothing changes if the small ball is cut out and the limit taken:

<Formula label="With a small ball cut out" tex="\text{LHS} = -\lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\int_{r > \epsilon}\partial_i\varphi(\vec r)\,\frac{\hat r_j}{r^2}\,d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(e), Eq. (7.11), p. 4" />

and in the region $r > \epsilon$ the product rule can be used:

<Formula label="The product rule outside the small ball" tex="\text{LHS} = -\lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\int_{r > \epsilon}\left\{\partial_i\left[\varphi(\vec r)\,\frac{\hat r_j}{r^2}\right] - \varphi(\vec r)\,\partial_i\left(\frac{\hat r_j}{r^2}\right)\right\}d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(e), Eq. (7.12), p. 4" />

Take over from here. Show that the second term gives the limit that defines the singular integral, and that the first term becomes a surface integral equal to $\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\varphi(\vec 0)$. (MIT's sheet writes $\varphi(\vec r)$ at this last point; the value at the origin is meant, as in its corrected first equation.)

<Part label="e" points={10}>

Carry out the two steps. The box below (our addition) asks about one step on the way.

<Hint>

In the second term, $r > \epsilon$ everywhere, so $\partial_i(\hat r_j/r^2)$ is the ordinary derivative of part (d).

</Hint>

<Hint>

The first term is the integral of a derivative over the region $r > \epsilon$: use the gradient theorem of part (d). The region has two boundaries, a sphere at infinity and the small sphere $r = \epsilon$. Which way does the outward normal of the region point on the small sphere?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-normal" label="On the small sphere $r = \epsilon$, the outward normal of the region $r > \epsilon$ is" choices={["r̂, pointing away from the origin", "−r̂, pointing towards the origin", "θ̂, tangent to the sphere", "either, since the sphere shrinks to a point"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Cutting out the small ball turns the bad derivative into two good pieces: an ordinary integrand away from the origin, and a total derivative whose integral sits on the small sphere, where the test function is close to $\varphi(\vec 0)$.

<Step title="The second term">

For $r > \epsilon$ the derivative is the ordinary one found in part (d), so the second term is

<Formula label="The second term" tex="+\lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\int_{r > \epsilon}\varphi(\vec r)\,\frac{\delta_{ij} - 3\hat r_i\hat r_j}{r^3}\,d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(e), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

which is exactly the limit that defines the singular integral on the right-hand side.

</Step>

<Step title="The first term">

By the gradient theorem the integral of $\partial_i[\varphi\,\hat r_j/r^2]$ over $r > \epsilon$ is a surface integral over the region's boundary. On a sphere of large radius the factor $1/r^2$ of the integrand cancels the $r^2$ of the area, leaving $\varphi\,\hat r_i\hat r_j\,d\Omega$, which tends to zero as the radius grows because $\varphi$ falls off. On the small sphere the outward normal of the region points into the ball, $\hat n = -\hat r$. So

<Formula label="The first term as a surface integral" tex="-\lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\int_{r > \epsilon}\partial_i\left[\varphi\,\frac{\hat r_j}{r^2}\right]d^3x = -\lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\oint_{r = \epsilon}\varphi(\epsilon\hat r)\,\frac{\hat r_j}{\epsilon^2}\left(-\hat r_i\right)\epsilon^2\,d\Omega = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0}\int\varphi(\epsilon\hat r)\,\hat r_i\hat r_j\,d\Omega" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(e), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The limit">

$\varphi$ is smooth, so $\varphi(\epsilon\hat r) = \varphi(\vec 0) + O(\epsilon)$ uniformly in the direction, and the limit is $\varphi(\vec 0)\int\hat r_i\hat r_j\,d\Omega = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\varphi(\vec 0)$, with the angular integral of part (d).

</Step>

<Step title="Both sides">

Adding the two terms, LHS equals the singular integral plus $\tfrac{4\pi}{3}\delta_{ij}\varphi(\vec 0)$, which is RHS, for every test function. The identity holds in the sense of distributions.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take the trace, $i = j$: the singular integral vanishes (its integrand $(3 - 3)/r^3$ is zero) and the rest gives $4\pi\varphi(\vec 0)$, the statement $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3$ of part (a) tested with $\varphi$.

</Step>

</Solution>
