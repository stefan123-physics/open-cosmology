## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The Laplacian as the anti-lumpiness operator" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

Let $f(\vec r)$ be any smooth scalar function of position. This problem compares its value at a point with its average over a sphere centred on that point. The point can be any point, so choose the coordinates so that it is the origin. The relation to prove is

<Formula label="The value at the centre and the average over a sphere" tex="f(\vec 0) - \bar f(R) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{r<R} d^3x\,\left(\frac1r - \frac1R\right)\nabla^2 f" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, eq. (1.1), p. 1" />

The integral runs over the inside of a sphere of radius $R$ centred on the origin, and $\bar f(R)$ is the average of $f$ over the surface of that sphere:

<Formula label="The average over the sphere" tex="\bar f(R) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi} f(R, \theta, \phi)\,\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, eq. (1.2), p. 1" />

Guth's picture: think of $f$ as the density of a pudding. The factor $1/r - 1/R$ is positive inside the sphere. So if $\nabla^2 f = 0$ everywhere inside, the value at the centre equals the average over the sphere, and the pudding has no lump there. If $\nabla^2 f < 0$ inside, the centre is above the average of its surroundings: a lump. The Laplacian measures lumpiness with a minus sign, hence the title.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Write, for short,

<Formula label="The weight function" tex="g(r) \equiv \frac1r - \frac1R" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), eq. (1.3), p. 2" />

Use the divergence theorem to show that

<Formula label="A vanishing integral" tex="\int_{r<R} d^3x\;\nabla\cdot\left(g\,\nabla f\right) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), eq. (1.4), p. 2" />

The box is our addition.

<Hint>

The divergence theorem turns the volume integral into the flux of $g\,\nabla f$ through the boundary. What is $g$ on the sphere $r = R$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The vector field $g\,\nabla f$ is not smooth at the origin, where $g$ grows without limit. To be safe, cut out a small ball of radius $\epsilon$ and see what its surface contributes as $\epsilon \to 0$. Compare how fast $g$ grows with how fast the area of the small sphere shrinks.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-why" label="The flux through the outer sphere $r = R$ vanishes because" choices={["the gradient of f is zero on the sphere", "f is constant on the sphere", "the weight g is zero on the sphere", "the outward and inward parts of the flux cancel"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The integrand is a divergence, so only the boundary matters, and the weight $g$ was built to vanish there.

<Step title="The outer boundary">

By the divergence theorem the integral equals the flux of $g\,\nabla f$ out through the sphere $r = R$. On that sphere $g(R) = 1/R - 1/R = 0$, so the flux is zero, whatever $f$ is.

</Step>

<Step title="The origin">

The divergence theorem needs a smooth vector field, and $g$ is infinite at the origin. So apply it to the region $\epsilon < r < R$, which has a second boundary, the small sphere $r = \epsilon$. On it $|g\,\nabla f|$ is at most $(1/\epsilon)$ times the largest value of $|\nabla f|$ nearby, which is finite because $f$ is smooth. The area is $4\pi\epsilon^2$. So the flux through the small sphere is at most of the order of

<Formula label="The flux through the small sphere" tex="\frac{1}{\epsilon}\cdot 4\pi\epsilon^2\cdot\left|\nabla f\right|_{\max} = 4\pi\epsilon\left|\nabla f\right|_{\max} \;\to\; 0 \quad\text{as } \epsilon \to 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The volume integral over the ball that was cut out vanishes in the same limit: part (b) shows that its integrand grows no faster than $1/r^2$ near the origin, while the volume element is $4\pi r^2\,dr$. So the integral over the whole ball $r < R$ is zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $f = z$, so $\nabla f = \hat z$. Then $\nabla\cdot(g\,\hat z) = \partial g/\partial z = -z/r^3$, which is odd in $z$: its integral over the ball is zero, as it must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Use index notation, in which the gradient has the components $\partial_i$, to show that for any two smooth scalar functions $g(\vec r)$ and $f(\vec r)$

<Formula label="The divergence of a function times a gradient" tex="\nabla\cdot\left(g\,\nabla f\right) = \nabla g\cdot\nabla f + g\,\nabla^2 f" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), eq. (1.5), p. 2" />

<Hint>

Component $i$ of $g\,\nabla f$ is $g\,\partial_i f$. The divergence applies $\partial_i$ and sums over $i$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** With indices this is the product rule and nothing more.

<Step title="The product rule">

<Formula label="The identity with indices" tex="\nabla\cdot\left(g\,\nabla f\right) = \partial_i\left(g\,\partial_i f\right) = \left(\partial_i g\right)\left(\partial_i f\right) + g\,\partial_i\partial_i f" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The first term is the dot product of the two gradients. In the second, $\partial_i\partial_i f$ is the sum of the three second derivatives, which is $\nabla^2 f$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $g = x$ and $f = x^2$. The left side is $\partial_x(x\cdot 2x) = 4x$. The right side is $1\cdot 2x + x\cdot 2 = 4x$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={8}>

Use the identity of part (b) to split the integral of part (a) into two integrals, and work out the one with $\nabla g\cdot\nabla f$ in spherical coordinates. Show that the result of part (a) then becomes the relation at the top of this problem.

The two boxes are our addition. First, apply the relation to a potential $V$ in a region filled with a uniform charge density $\rho$, where $\nabla^2 V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$: give $V(\vec 0) - \bar V(R)$. Second, a potential is $V = 5 + 2x + 3(x^2 - y^2) + 4z^2$, in volts with the coordinates in metres: give its average over the sphere of radius 0.50 m centred on the origin.

<Hint>

The function $g$ depends on $r$ only, so its gradient points along $\hat r$. What is the component of $\nabla f$ along $\hat r$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In spherical coordinates the volume element carries a factor $r^2$. See what it does to the integrand, and do the integral over $r$ first, at fixed angles.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the second box you do not need to integrate over the sphere: find $\nabla^2 V$, which is a constant, and use your first box.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-rho" label="$V(\vec 0) - \bar V(R) =$" expr="rho*R^2/(6*eps0)"
  vars={{ rho: [0.5, 2], R: [0.5, 2], eps0: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ rho: "\\rho", R: "R", eps0: "\\varepsilon_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-rho*R^2/(6*eps0)", hint: "The sign is reversed. Positive charge makes the Laplacian of V negative, so the centre lies above the average of its surroundings: a lump." },
    { expr: "rho*R^2/(2*eps0)", hint: "That keeps only the 1/r part of the weight, which gives R²/2. The −1/R part takes R²/3 away from it." },
    { expr: "rho*R^2/(3*eps0)", hint: "That is the size of the −1/R part alone. The 1/r part gives R²/2 and the 1/R part R²/3; the answer is their difference." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-avg" label="Average of $V$ over the sphere, in volts:" value="5.33" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-avg" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** After the split, the integral with the two gradients can be done exactly, because the $r^2$ of the volume element cancels the $1/r^2$ of $\nabla g$ and leaves a total derivative in $r$.

<Step title="Split the integral">

By parts (a) and (b),

<Formula label="The two pieces" tex="0 = \int_{r<R} d^3x\;\nabla g\cdot\nabla f + \int_{r<R} d^3x\;g\,\nabla^2 f" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The second piece is already the integral in the relation we want.

</Step>

<Step title="The piece with the gradients">

Since $g = 1/r - 1/R$, its gradient is $\nabla g = -\hat r/r^2$, and $\hat r\cdot\nabla f = \partial f/\partial r$. With $d^3x = r^2\,dr\,\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$,

<Formula label="The gradient piece in spherical coordinates" tex="\int_{r<R} d^3x\;\nabla g\cdot\nabla f = -\int\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi\int_0^R \frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\,dr = -\int\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi\,\left[f(R, \theta, \phi) - f(\vec 0)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The integral over the angles of $f(R, \theta, \phi)$ is $4\pi\bar f(R)$, by the definition of the average, and the integral of the constant $f(\vec 0)$ is $4\pi f(\vec 0)$. So this piece equals $4\pi\left[f(\vec 0) - \bar f(R)\right]$.

</Step>

<Step title="Put the pieces together">

<Formula label="The relation, proved" tex="4\pi\left[f(\vec 0) - \bar f(R)\right] = -\int_{r<R} d^3x\;g\,\nabla^2 f \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f(\vec 0) - \bar f(R) = -\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{r<R} d^3x\left(\frac1r - \frac1R\right)\nabla^2 f" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two boxes">

For $\nabla^2 V = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$, a constant, the integral over the ball is

<Formula label="A uniformly charged region" tex="V(\vec 0) - \bar V(R) = \frac{\rho}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_0^R\left(\frac1r - \frac1R\right)4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac{R^2}{2} - \frac{R^2}{3}\right) = \frac{\rho R^2}{6\varepsilon_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own addition)" />

For the second box, $\nabla^2 V = 6 - 6 + 8 = 8$ V/m², which plays the part of $-\rho/\varepsilon_0$. So $\bar V(R) = V(\vec 0) + 8R^2/6 = 5 + 8\times 0.25/6 = 5.33$ V.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The second box can be done directly. On a sphere the averages of $x$ and of $x^2 - y^2$ are zero, and the average of $z^2$ is $R^2/3$. So $\bar V = 5 + 4R^2/3 = 5 + 4\times 0.25/3 = 5.33$ V, the same. The first box reappears in Problem 3: for a uniformly charged ball of radius $R$ the potential at the centre exceeds the potential at the surface by exactly $\rho R^2/(6\varepsilon_0)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Capacitance of a cylindrical capacitor" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

A long conducting cylinder of radius $a$ and length $\ell$ carries a total charge $+q$. Around it, on the same axis, is a thin conducting cylindrical shell of radius $b$ and the same length, with total charge $-q$. (MIT's sketch shows the cross section: a filled circle of radius $a$ inside a thin ring of radius $b$, both centred on the origin of the $xy$ plane.) The cylinders are long enough for the effects of their ends to be neglected. In this problem $r$ is the distance from the axis.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Use Gauss's law to find the electric field between the cylinders, $a < r < b$. The field points straight away from the axis; give its magnitude.

<Hint>

Choose a closed surface on which the field has the same magnitude everywhere it crosses: a cylinder of radius $r$ on the same axis. Which parts of it does the field cross?

</Hint>

<Hint>

How much charge does a piece of length $h$ of the inner cylinder carry, if the charge $q$ is spread evenly over the length $\ell$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-e" label="$E(r) =$" expr="q/(2*pi*eps0*L*r)"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], eps0: [0.5, 2], L: [1.5, 4], r: [0.3, 1.2] }}
  names={{ q: "q", eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", L: "\\ell", r: "r" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "q/(4*pi*eps0*r^2)", hint: "That is the field of a point charge. Here the charge is spread along a line, and the Gauss surface is a cylinder, whose side has an area proportional to r, not to r²." },
    { expr: "q/(2*pi*eps0*r)", hint: "That has the units of a potential, not of a field. Gauss's law needs the charge per unit length of the inner cylinder, q/ℓ." },
    { expr: "q/(pi*eps0*L*r)", hint: "A factor of 2 is missing below: the side of a cylinder of radius r and length h has the area 2πrh." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a long cylinder the field can only point away from the axis and depend on $r$, so a coaxial cylinder is the natural Gauss surface.

<Step title="Gauss's law">

Take a cylinder of radius $r$ and length $h$ on the same axis, with $a < r < b$. The field is parallel to its two flat ends, so only the curved side, of area $2\pi rh$, carries flux. The charge inside is the charge on a length $h$ of the inner cylinder, $qh/\ell$. So

<Formula label="Gauss's law for the coaxial surface" tex="E(r)\cdot 2\pi r h = \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q\,h}{\ell} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec E = \frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,\ell\,r}\,\hat r" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

where $\hat r$ here points straight away from the axis.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The length $h$ has dropped out, as it should. The units are those of a charge over $\varepsilon_0$ times an area, which is a field. Inside the inner cylinder the field is zero (a conductor), and outside the shell the enclosed charge is $q - q = 0$, so the field is zero there as well: it lives in the gap only.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

From the field of part (a), find the potential difference between the outer shell and the inner cylinder. The inner cylinder is at the higher potential: give $V(a) - V(b)$, which is positive for positive $q$. (MIT's sheet does not fix the sign of $\Delta V$; taken as $V(b) - V(a)$, as in the 8.02 notes, it is minus this.)

<Hint>

The potential difference is a line integral of the field. Go straight outwards from $r = a$ to $r = b$, along the field.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-dv" label="$V(a) - V(b) =$" expr="q*log(b/a)/(2*pi*eps0*L)"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], eps0: [0.5, 2], L: [1.5, 4], a: [0.3, 0.8], b: [1.2, 3] }}
  names={{ q: "q", eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", L: "\\ell", a: "a", b: "b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "q*log(a/b)/(2*pi*eps0*L)", hint: "The sign is reversed: ln(a/b) is negative. The field points from the positive inner cylinder to the shell, so the potential falls on the way out and V(a) is the larger." },
    { expr: "q*(1/a - 1/b)/(2*pi*eps0*L)", hint: "That integrates 1/r² instead of 1/r. The field of a line of charge falls off as 1/r, and its integral is a logarithm." },
    { expr: "q*(b - a)/(2*pi*eps0*L*a)", hint: "That treats the field as constant across the gap, with its value at r = a. It holds only for a thin gap, which is part (d)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Integrate the field along a radial line.

<Step title="The line integral">

<Formula label="The potential difference across the gap" tex="V(a) - V(b) = \int_a^b E(r)\,dr = \frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,\ell}\int_a^b\frac{dr}{r} = \frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,\ell}\,\ln\frac{b}{a}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So $\Delta V = V(b) - V(a) = -\dfrac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,\ell}\ln\dfrac{b}{a}$: the shell is at the lower potential.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The logarithm takes a ratio, a pure number, so the units are those of $q/(\varepsilon_0\ell)$: a charge over $\varepsilon_0$ times a length, which is a potential. For $b = a$ the difference is zero.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Find the capacitance of this capacitor in terms of the given quantities, and its capacitance per unit length.

The number box is our addition. A cable has an inner wire of radius 0.50 mm and a shell of radius 1.75 mm, with empty space between: give its capacitance per metre.

<Hint>

The capacitance is the charge on one conductor divided by the size of the potential difference between the two.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-c" label="$C =$" expr="2*pi*eps0*L/log(b/a)"
  vars={{ eps0: [0.5, 2], L: [1.5, 4], a: [0.3, 0.8], b: [1.2, 3] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", L: "\\ell", a: "a", b: "b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*pi*eps0/log(b/a)", hint: "That is the capacitance per unit length. The box asks for the capacitance of the whole capacitor, of length ℓ." },

<Answer id="p2-num" label="Capacitance per metre, in pF/m:" value="44.4" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Divide the charge by the potential difference of part (b); the charge cancels.

<Step title="The capacitance">

<Formula label="The cylindrical capacitor" tex="C = \frac{q}{V(a) - V(b)} = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,\ell}{\ln(b/a)}, \qquad \frac{C}{\ell} = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

It depends on the geometry only, and on the radii only through their ratio.

</Step>

<Step title="The cable">

Here $b/a = 3.5$ and $\ln 3.5 = 1.253$. With $2\pi\varepsilon_0 = 5.563\times10^{-11}$ F/m, the capacitance per metre is $5.563\times10^{-11}/1.253 = 4.44\times10^{-11}$ F/m, or 44.4 pF/m.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The capacitance grows with the length, as it should for more plate area, and it grows without limit as $b \to a$, when the gap closes.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Let the gap $d = b - a$ be small compared with the radii. Show that the capacitance of part (c) then becomes that of a parallel-plate capacitor,

<Formula label="The parallel-plate capacitor" tex="C = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}" cite="mit802-ch5" at="Section 5.2, Eq. (5.2.4), p. 5" />

with $A$ the area of a plate. (MIT's sheet points to this result in a textbook; here it is quoted from the 8.02 course notes, and it is in the lesson [Conductors, capacitors and stored energy](/courses/8-02/conductors-and-capacitors).)

The number box is our addition. For $d = a/10$, by how many per cent is the exact capacitance larger than $\varepsilon_0 A/d$, if $A$ is the area of the inner cylinder?

<Hint>

Write $b/a = 1 + d/a$ and use the first term of the series of $\ln(1 + u)$ for small $u$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-pct" label="Excess of the exact $C$, in per cent:" value="4.92" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-pct" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A thin gap is nearly flat: the logarithm becomes the ratio of the gap to the radius.

<Step title="Expand the logarithm">

For $d \ll a$, $\ln(b/a) = \ln(1 + d/a) \approx d/a$. Then

<Formula label="The thin-gap limit" tex="C \approx \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,\ell\,a}{d} = \frac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}, \qquad A = 2\pi a\,\ell" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

and $A = 2\pi a\ell$ is the area of the curved surface of the inner cylinder (to this accuracy, of the shell as well): the two cylinders are the plates.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

The ratio of the exact capacitance to $\varepsilon_0 A/d$, with $A = 2\pi a\ell$, is $(d/a)/\ln(1 + d/a)$. For $d/a = 0.1$ this is $0.1/0.09531 = 1.0492$: the exact value is 4.92% larger.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

<Exercise id="p4" title="Calculating forces using virtual work" points={10} mit="Problem 4">

Find the force with which the plates attract each other by the method of virtual work of [Lecture Notes 4](/courses/8-07/conductor-surface-forces-capacitance): let one plate move by a very small amount, use conservation of energy to find the work this takes, and read off the force. Do it in two ways.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Keep the charges $\pm Q$ on the plates fixed (the plates are isolated). Give the magnitude of the attractive force.

<Hint>

Write the stored energy in terms of $Q$ and the gap $d$. If the gap grows by a small amount while $Q$ stays the same, by how much does the energy change, and who supplies it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

With the plates isolated, the only energy that enters is the work done by whoever pulls the plate, against the electric force.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-fq" label="$F =$" expr="Q^2/(2*eps0*A)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 2], eps0: [0.5, 2], A: [0.5, 2], d: [0.3, 0.8] }}
  names={{ Q: "Q", eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", A: "A", d: "d" }}

    { expr: "Q^2*d/(2*eps0*A)", hint: "That is the stored energy, not the force. The force is the rate at which this energy changes with the gap." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At fixed charge nothing but the hand that moves the plate exchanges energy with the capacitor, so the work of the hand equals the change of the field energy.

<Step title="The energy at fixed charge">

The field between the plates is $E = Q/(\varepsilon_0A)$, whatever the gap, and it fills the volume $Ad$. So

<Formula label="The stored energy in terms of the charge" tex="W = \frac12\,\varepsilon_0E^2\cdot A\,d = \frac{Q^2\,d}{2\,\varepsilon_0A}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="Virtual work">

Pull the plates apart by a small amount, from $d$ to $d + \delta$. The energy rises by $Q^2\delta/(2\varepsilon_0A)$. The hand supplied it, by pulling with a force equal and opposite to the electric force over the distance $\delta$. So

<Formula label="The force at fixed charge" tex="F\,\delta = \frac{Q^2}{2\,\varepsilon_0A}\,\delta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F = \frac{Q^2}{2\,\varepsilon_0A}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

The energy rises when the gap grows, so the electric force opposes the growth: the plates attract.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The force per unit area is $F/A = \sigma^2/(2\varepsilon_0)$ with $\sigma = Q/A$: the pressure $\frac12\sigma E$ on a conductor's surface that Lecture Notes 4 found for a sphere. The force does not depend on the gap, as expected for a uniform field.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Formula label="The changes at fixed voltage" tex="dW = \frac12\,V^2\,dC, \qquad dQ = V\,dC, \qquad dW_{\text{battery}} = V\,dQ = V^2\,dC" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="The mechanical work at fixed voltage" tex="dW_{\text{mech}} = dW_{\text{battery}} - dW = V^2\,dC - \frac12\,V^2\,dC = \frac12\,V^2\,dC" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Mutual capacitance" points={15} mit="Problem 5">

For $n$ conductors, with the potential zero at infinity, the charges and the potentials are related linearly:

<Formula label="The capacitance matrix" tex="Q_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} C_{ij}\,V_j, \qquad i, j = 1, 2, \ldots, n" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, eq. (5.1), p. 3" />

Here $Q_i$ is the charge on conductor $i$ and $V_i$ its potential. Lecture Notes 4 reach this relation by inverting the one that gives the potentials in terms of the charges,

<Formula label="The potentials in terms of the charges" tex="V_i = \sum_j P_{ij}\,Q_j, \qquad C = P^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (4.15) and (4.17), pp. 4–5" />

where the numbers $P_{ij}$ depend on the shapes and positions of the conductors only.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Prove that $C_{ij} = C_{ji}$.

MIT's hint: start with all conductors uncharged, put a charge $Q_i$ on conductor $i$, and then a charge $Q_j$ on conductor $j$; find the energy this takes. Then do it again in the opposite order, and compare.

The box is our addition. In the first order, give the work needed for the second step alone: adding the charge $Q_j$ to conductor $j$ while conductor $i$ already carries $Q_i$ and all the other conductors are uncharged. Write it with $P_{ji}$ and $P_{jj}$.

<Hint>

It is easier to work with the matrix $P_{ij}$ than with $C_{ij}$, because the steps are described by their charges. A small charge $dq$ brought from infinity to a conductor at potential $V$ takes the work $V\,dq$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

While the charge on conductor $j$ grows from 0 to $Q_j$, its potential has two parts: one from the charge already on conductor $i$, which stays the same, and one from its own charge so far, which grows.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The final state is the same for both orders, and electrostatic forces are conservative. Once you have a statement about $P_{ij}$, ask what it means for the inverse matrix.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-step" label="Work of the second step:" expr="Pji*Qi*Qj + Pjj*Qj^2/2"
  vars={{ Pji: [0.5, 2], Pjj: [0.5, 2], Qi: [0.5, 2], Qj: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ Pji: "P_{ji}", Pjj: "P_{jj}", Qi: "Q_i", Qj: "Q_j" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Pjj*Qj^2/2", hint: "That is the work to charge conductor j when all the others are uncharged. Here conductor i already carries Q_i, which raises the potential of conductor j before any charge arrives there." },
    { expr: "Pji*Qi*Qj + Pjj*Qj^2", hint: "The second term needs a factor ½: the conductor's own part of its potential grows from zero as the charge arrives, so on average it is half its final value." },
    { expr: "Pji*Qi*Qj/2 + Pjj*Qj^2/2", hint: "The first term has no factor ½: the potential that Q_i makes at conductor j is there in full from the start, for every bit of charge that arrives." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The energy of the final state cannot depend on the order in which the charges were brought in. Working out the energy for both orders gives two expressions that differ in one term only.

<Step title="First i, then j">

With only conductor $i$ charged, to an amount $q$, its potential is $P_{ii}\,q$. Bringing in the next $dq$ takes the work $P_{ii}\,q\,dq$, so the first step takes $\frac12P_{ii}Q_i^2$. In the second step conductor $j$ carries $q$, growing from 0 to $Q_j$, and its potential is $V_j = P_{ji}Q_i + P_{jj}\,q$. So

<Formula label="The work of the second step" tex="\int_0^{Q_j}\left(P_{ji}\,Q_i + P_{jj}\,q\right)dq = P_{ji}\,Q_i\,Q_j + \frac12\,P_{jj}\,Q_j^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), pp. 3–4 (our own solution)" />

and the total is

<Formula label="The energy, first order" tex="W = \frac12\,P_{ii}\,Q_i^2 + \frac12\,P_{jj}\,Q_j^2 + P_{ji}\,Q_i\,Q_j" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), pp. 3–4 (our own solution)" />

(No sum over $i$ or $j$ is meant in this part: they are two fixed conductors.)

</Step>

<Step title="First j, then i">

The same steps with the two conductors exchanged give

<Formula label="The energy, opposite order" tex="W' = \frac12\,P_{jj}\,Q_j^2 + \frac12\,P_{ii}\,Q_i^2 + P_{ij}\,Q_j\,Q_i" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), pp. 3–4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Compare">

Both orders end in the same state: the same charges on the same conductors, and so (the charges on conductors arrange themselves in one way only) the same field. The energy of a state is $\frac12\varepsilon_0\int|\vec E|^2\,d^3x$, fixed by the field. So $W = W'$, and since $Q_i$ and $Q_j$ are arbitrary,

<Formula label="The matrix P is symmetric" tex="P_{ij} = P_{ji}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), pp. 3–4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="From P to C">

$C$ is the inverse of $P$, and the inverse of a symmetric matrix is symmetric: taking the transpose of $PC = 1$ gives $C^{T}P^{T} = C^{T}P = 1$, so $C^{T}$ is also the inverse of $P$, and an inverse is unique. Hence $C^{T} = C$, that is $C_{ij} = C_{ji}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The concentric shells of part (c) are an example: there $P_{12} = P_{21} = 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0b)$. The result says something that is not obvious: the potential that a charge on conductor $i$ makes on conductor $j$ equals the potential that the same charge on $j$ would make on $i$, whatever the two shapes.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Formula label="The ordinary capacitance" tex="Q = C\,V, \qquad V = V_1 - V_2" cite="reading1" at="eq. (4.18), p. 5" />

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="The Laplacian of 1/r in the language of distributions" points={20} mit="Problem 7" optional>

This problem is extra credit on MIT's sheet. It comes with a long introduction, because the approach was treated in lecture and not in the course's textbook. Here is that introduction in short.

**The trouble with limits.** In Problem 5 of [Problem Set 1](/courses/8-07/8-07-problem-set-1) the function $1/r$ was smoothed to $1/\sqrt{r^2 + a^2}$, and $g_a(r) \equiv -\nabla^2\left(1/(4\pi\sqrt{r^2 + a^2})\right)$ was shown to have the integral 1 over all space for every $a > 0$, and to go to zero at every $r \neq 0$ as $a \to 0$. That is the intuition behind

<Formula label="The relation to be made precise" tex="-\nabla^2\,\frac{1}{4\pi r} = \delta^3(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, eq. (7.1), p. 4" />

But the order of the limits matters. Integrating first and then letting $a \to 0$ gives 1. Taking the limit first gives a function that is zero everywhere except at one point, and the integral of such a function, as an area under a curve, is zero. So $g_a$ does not tend to a function that acts as a delta function: no ordinary function does. One must integrate first and take the limit afterwards.

**Distributions.** Mathematicians therefore treat the delta function as a **distribution** (or generalized function). What is generalized is integration. An ordinary integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx$ turns a function $f$ into a number, and it does so linearly. A distribution is any linear rule that turns each smooth **test function** $f(x)$ into a number; the test functions are also required to fall off rapidly at large $|x|$. The delta function is the rule that gives the value of the test function at one point:

<Formula label="The delta function as a rule" tex="\int f(x)\,\delta(x - x_0)\,dx \equiv f(x_0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, eq. (7.6), p. 5" />

The integral sign here is not an area under a curve. It is only a way of writing the rule.

**Derivatives.** The derivative of a distribution is defined so that integration by parts keeps working. There are no boundary terms, because the test function vanishes fast enough far away:

<Formula label="The derivative of the delta function" tex="\int f(x)\,\frac{d}{dx}\delta(x - x_0)\,dx \equiv -\int\frac{df}{dx}\,\delta(x - x_0)\,dx = -\frac{df}{dx}(x_0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, eq. (7.7), p. 6" />

**The function 1/r as a distribution.** As a function, $\nabla^2(1/r)$ is not defined at $r = 0$, where $1/r$ cannot be differentiated. But $1/r$ itself is a perfectly good distribution: the rule that sends a test function $f(\vec r)$ to the ordinary integral $\int f(\vec r)\,(1/r)\,d^3x$, which converges because $d^3x/r = r\,dr\,\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$. Its Laplacian is then defined by moving the two derivatives onto the test function, by two integrations by parts:

<Formula label="The Laplacian of 1/r as a distribution" tex="\int f(\vec r)\,\nabla^2\!\left(\frac1r\right)d^3x \;\equiv\; -\int\partial_i f(\vec r)\;\partial_i\!\left(\frac1r\right)d^3x \;\equiv\; \int\nabla^2 f(\vec r)\,\left(\frac1r\right)d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, eqs. (7.10a) to (7.10c), p. 6" />

The last form is an ordinary integral. It defines a number for each test function, which MIT calls $F[f]$; the square brackets say that $F$ is a functional, a function of a function:

<Formula label="The functional F" tex="F[f] \equiv \int\nabla^2 f(\vec r)\,\left(\frac1r\right)d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, eq. (7.11), p. 6" />

<Part label="a" points={12}>

Evaluate $F[f]$ for an arbitrary smooth test function that falls off rapidly at large $r$, and show that

<Formula label="The value of the functional" tex="F[f] = -4\pi\,f(\vec 0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a), eq. (7.12), p. 7" />

Since the three-dimensional delta function is the rule $\int f(\vec r)\,\delta^3(\vec r)\,d^3x = f(\vec 0)$, this is the same as

<Formula label="The Laplacian of 1/r" tex="\nabla^2\!\left(\frac1r\right) = -4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a), eq. (7.14), p. 7" />

in the sense of distributions.

MIT's hint: although the last form above is the definition, nothing stops you from integrating by parts once, back to the middle form with one derivative on each factor. Write that integral in spherical coordinates.

The box is our addition: give $F[f]$ for the test function $f = 3\,e^{-r^2/b^2}$, with $b$ a constant length.

<Hint>

In the middle form you need $\partial_i(1/r)$, the gradient of $1/r$. It points along $\hat r$. What does its dot product with $\nabla f$ pick out?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The integral is the one of Problem 1(c), with the upper limit at infinity. The volume element supplies exactly the factor that the gradient of $1/r$ lacks.

</Hint>

<Hint>

To be careful about the point $r = 0$: integrate by parts over the region $r > \epsilon$ and show that the surface term on the small sphere goes to zero with $\epsilon$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-f" label="$F[f]$ for $f = 3\,e^{-r^2/b^2}$:" value="-37.7" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-f" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One integration by parts gives an integrand that is a total derivative in $r$, just as in Problem 1(c); the only thing that survives is the value of $f$ at the origin.

<Step title="Back to the middle form, with care">

$F[f]$ is an ordinary convergent integral, so it is the limit of the integral over $r > \epsilon$. On that region everything is smooth, and by the identity of Problem 1(b) with $g = 1/r$,

<Formula label="One integration by parts" tex="\int_{r>\epsilon}\frac1r\,\nabla^2 f\,d^3x = \int_{r>\epsilon}\nabla\cdot\left(\frac1r\,\nabla f\right)d^3x - \int_{r>\epsilon}\nabla\!\left(\frac1r\right)\cdot\nabla f\,d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a), p. 7 (our own solution)" />

The first integral on the right is a surface term. At infinity it vanishes because $f$ falls off rapidly. On the small sphere it is at most $(1/\epsilon)\cdot4\pi\epsilon^2\cdot|\nabla f|_{\max}$, which goes to zero with $\epsilon$.

</Step>

<Step title="Spherical coordinates">

With $\nabla(1/r) = -\hat r/r^2$ and $d^3x = r^2\,dr\,\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$,

<Formula label="The remaining integral" tex="-\int_{r>\epsilon}\nabla\!\left(\frac1r\right)\cdot\nabla f\,d^3x = \int\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi\int_\epsilon^\infty\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\,dr = -\int\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi\;f(\epsilon, \theta, \phi)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a), p. 7 (our own solution)" />

because $f$ vanishes at infinity. As $\epsilon \to 0$, $f(\epsilon, \theta, \phi) \to f(\vec 0)$ in every direction, and the integral over the angles gives $4\pi$. So $F[f] = -4\pi f(\vec 0)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The box, and a check">

For $f = 3e^{-r^2/b^2}$, $f(\vec 0) = 3$ and $F = -12\pi = -37.7$, whatever $b$ is. Check it from the definition. For a function of $r$ alone, $\nabla^2f = f'' + 2f'/r$, which here is $3\left(4r^2/b^4 - 6/b^2\right)e^{-r^2/b^2}$. Then

<Formula label="The functional for a Gaussian, directly" tex="F = \int_0^\infty 3\left(\frac{4r^2}{b^4} - \frac{6}{b^2}\right)e^{-r^2/b^2}\,\frac1r\,4\pi r^2\,dr = 12\pi\left(\frac{4}{b^4}\cdot\frac{b^4}{2} - \frac{6}{b^2}\cdot\frac{b^2}{2}\right) = 12\pi\,(2 - 3) = -12\pi" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a), p. 7 (our own addition)" />

using $\int_0^\infty r^3e^{-r^2/b^2}dr = b^4/2$ and $\int_0^\infty r\,e^{-r^2/b^2}dr = b^2/2$. The ordinary integral feels the whole function, yet its value depends on $f(\vec 0)$ alone: that is what it means for $\nabla^2(1/r)$ to be a delta function.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="b" points={8}>

Use the language of distributions to evaluate $\nabla^2\ln r$ in two dimensions, where $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$. (Problem 6(d) of [Problem Set 1](/courses/8-07/8-07-problem-set-1) found the answer by a different route.)

The box is our addition: the result has the form $\nabla^2\ln r = c\,\delta^2(\vec r)$; give the number $c$.

<Hint>

Define the functional in the same way, with the two derivatives on a test function $f(x, y)$ and with $d^2x = r\,dr\,d\phi$ in polar coordinates. Is the integral of $\ln r$ times a smooth function finite near $r = 0$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate by parts once, as in part (a). The gradient of $\ln r$ is $\hat r/r$, and the area element carries a factor $r$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-c" label="$c =$" value="6.28" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-c" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same steps as in part (a), one dimension lower: $\ln r$ takes the place of $1/r$, and a circle the place of the sphere.

<Step title="The definition">

$\ln r$ is a good distribution in two dimensions: $\int f\,\ln r\;r\,dr\,d\phi$ converges at $r = 0$, since $r\ln r \to 0$ there. Its Laplacian is the rule

<Formula label="The Laplacian of ln r as a distribution" tex="\int f\,\nabla^2\ln r\;d^2x \;\equiv\; \int\nabla^2 f\;\ln r\;d^2x" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(b), p. 7 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="One integration by parts">

On the region $r > \epsilon$, $\int\ln r\,\nabla^2 f\,d^2x = \int\nabla\cdot(\ln r\,\nabla f)\,d^2x - \int\nabla\ln r\cdot\nabla f\,d^2x$. The first term is a boundary term. Far away it vanishes; on the small circle it is at most $|\ln\epsilon|\cdot2\pi\epsilon\cdot|\nabla f|_{\max}$, which goes to zero with $\epsilon$. In the second, $\nabla\ln r = \hat r/r$:

<Formula label="The remaining integral in two dimensions" tex="-\int_{r>\epsilon}\frac1r\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\;r\,dr\,d\phi = -\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi\int_\epsilon^\infty\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\,dr = \int_0^{2\pi}f(\epsilon, \phi)\,d\phi \;\to\; 2\pi\,f(\vec 0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(b), p. 7 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The result">

The rule sends every test function to $2\pi$ times its value at the origin, so

<Formula label="The Laplacian of ln r in two dimensions" tex="\nabla^2\ln r = 2\pi\,\delta^2(\vec r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(b), p. 7 (our own solution)" />

and $c = 2\pi = 6.28$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Try $f = e^{-r^2}$, for which $f(\vec 0) = 1$. In two dimensions $\nabla^2f = f'' + f'/r = (4r^2 - 4)e^{-r^2}$, and $\int_0^\infty(4r^2 - 4)e^{-r^2}\ln r\;2\pi r\,dr = 2\pi$, as a numerical integration confirms (it gives 6.2832). The result also agrees with Problem Set 1, and with electrostatics: $-\ln r/(2\pi\varepsilon_0)$ times $\lambda$ is the potential of a line charge $\lambda$ per unit length, and its Laplacian must be $-\lambda\,\delta^2(\vec r)/\varepsilon_0$.

</Step>

</Solution>
