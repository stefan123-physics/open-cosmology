The previous two pages solved Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates with Guth's own tool, **traceless symmetric tensors**, while textbooks such as Jackson's *Classical Electrodynamics* (3rd edition, Sections 3.1, 3.2, 3.5 and 3.6) use the **spherical harmonics** $Y_{\ell m}$. Lecture Notes 8, written the day after the lecture of Wednesday 10 October 2012 as an addendum to it, connect the two (notes p. 1). Guth does not derive the standard formalism; he takes its formulas from the textbooks and shows how each standard function is written as a traceless symmetric tensor. (Our own words and our own worked example; the notes have no figures.)

## Two expansions of a function of direction

In Guth's formalism any function of direction is a series in the unit vector $\hat n$ (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="The traceless symmetric tensor expansion" tex="F(\hat n) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty} C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell} = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty} F_\ell(\hat n) = C^{(0)} + C^{(1)}_i\,\hat n_i + C^{(2)}_{ij}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j + C^{(3)}_{ijk}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j\hat n_k + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.1)" />

where every $C^{(\ell)}$ is a traceless symmetric tensor, the indices are summed from 1 to 3, and

<Formula label="The unit vector in the direction theta, phi" tex="\hat n(\theta, \phi) = \sin\theta\cos\phi\,\hat e_1 + \sin\theta\sin\phi\,\hat e_2 + \cos\theta\,\hat e_3" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.2)" />

with $\hat e_1, \hat e_2, \hat e_3$ also written $\hat e_x, \hat e_y, \hat e_z$. The standard approach expands the same function in spherical harmonics:

<Formula label="The spherical harmonic expansion" tex="F(\hat n) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\,\sum_{m = -\ell}^{\ell} a_{\ell m}\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.3)" />

[The notes start the first sum of eq. (8.1) and the sum in eq. (8.3) at $\ell = 1$; the constant term $\ell = 0$ belongs in both, as the second sum of eq. (8.1) shows.] The [previous notes](/courses/8-07/separation-of-variables-spherical) showed that each term of the tensor series is an eigenfunction of the angular Laplacian (notes p. 1):

<Formula label="The tensor terms are eigenfunctions" tex="\nabla_\theta^2\left[C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}\right] = -\ell(\ell + 1)\,C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.4)" />

<Formula label="The angular Laplacian" tex="\nabla_\theta^2 = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.5)" />

and the textbooks show the same for the spherical harmonics:

<Formula label="The spherical harmonics are eigenfunctions" tex="\nabla_\theta^2\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi) = -\ell(\ell + 1)\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.6)" />

So $\ell$ means the same thing in both languages. There must then be, for each $\ell$ and $m$, a particular traceless symmetric tensor $C^{(\ell, m)}$ that is equivalent to $Y_{\ell m}$ (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="The tensor equivalent to Y l m" tex="C^{(\ell, m)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell} = Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.7)" />

The goal of the notes is to construct it. The count already fits: the lecture showed (written up in Lecture Notes 9, which Guth says logically come before these notes, although they were posted later) that there are $2\ell + 1$ linearly independent traceless symmetric tensors of rank $\ell$, and $m$ runs over the $2\ell + 1$ integers from $-\ell$ to $\ell$. (For $\ell = 2$, a symmetric $3 \times 3$ matrix has 6 independent entries and the zero trace removes one, leaving 5.)

## Azimuthal symmetry and the Legendre polynomials

Start with functions that do not change under rotations about the $z$-axis and so do not depend on $\phi$. The standard treatment expands them in [Legendre polynomials](/courses/8-07/spherical-shell-circular-hole) (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="The Legendre expansion" tex="F(\hat n) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty} a_\ell\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.8)" />

These are the spherical harmonics with $m = 0$, normalized differently:

<Formula label="Y l 0 and the Legendre polynomial" tex="Y_{\ell 0}(\theta, \phi) = \sqrt{\frac{2\ell + 1}{4\pi}}\,P_\ell(\cos\theta)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.9)" />

and they are given explicitly by **Rodrigues' formula**:

<Formula label="Rodrigues' formula" tex="P_\ell(x) = \frac{1}{2^\ell\,\ell\,!}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{\ell}\left[(x^2 - 1)^\ell\right]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.10)" />

In the tensor language, azimuthal symmetry means tensors that are unchanged by rotations about the $z$-axis. For $\ell = 1$ the tensor $C^{(1)}_i$ is a vector, and the only vector unchanged by such rotations points along the axis. With $\hat z$ the unit vector in the $z$-direction (also called $\hat e_z$ and $\hat e_3$),

<Formula label="The l = 1 tensor with azimuthal symmetry" tex="C^{(1)}_i = \text{const}\,\hat z_i, \qquad \hat z_i = \delta_{i3}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.11)" />

and the function it gives is (notes p. 2)

<Formula label="The l = 1 function" tex="F_1(\hat n) = C^{(1)}_i\,\hat n_i = \text{const}\,\hat z_i\,\hat n_i = \text{const}\,\hat z \cdot \hat n = \text{const}\,\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.12)" />

which agrees with $P_1(\cos\theta) = \cos\theta$. For any $\ell$, the product $\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}$ is unchanged by the rotations and plainly symmetric, but it is not traceless (contracting two of its indices gives $\hat z \cdot \hat z = 1$). Guth's remedy is to take its **traceless symmetric part**, written with curly brackets (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="The traceless symmetric part" tex="\{\hat z_{i_1}\hat z_{i_2}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\} = \text{traceless symmetric part of } \hat z_{i_1}\hat z_{i_2}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.13)" />

It is made by subtracting terms that contain one or more Kronecker deltas, with coefficients fixed uniquely by the requirement that every trace vanish. The first five are (notes p. 3)

<Formula label="The first traceless symmetric parts" tex="\begin{aligned} \{1\} &= 1, \qquad \{\hat z_i\} = \hat z_i, \qquad \{\hat z_i\hat z_j\} = \hat z_i\hat z_j - \tfrac13\,\delta_{ij}, \\ \{\hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\} &= \hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k - \tfrac15\left(\hat z_i\,\delta_{jk} + \hat z_j\,\delta_{ik} + \hat z_k\,\delta_{ij}\right), \\ \{\hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\hat z_m\} &= \hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\hat z_m - \tfrac17\left(\hat z_i\hat z_j\,\delta_{km} + \hat z_i\hat z_k\,\delta_{mj} + \hat z_i\hat z_m\,\delta_{jk} + \hat z_j\hat z_k\,\delta_{im} + \hat z_j\hat z_m\,\delta_{ik} + \hat z_k\hat z_m\,\delta_{ij}\right) \\ &\quad + \tfrac{1}{35}\left(\delta_{ij}\,\delta_{km} + \delta_{ik}\,\delta_{jm} + \delta_{im}\,\delta_{jk}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.14)" />

(In the rank-4 line $m$ is just a fourth index, not the $m$ of $Y_{\ell m}$.) A quick check of the rank-3 line (our own, not in the notes): set $j = k$ and sum. The first term gives $\hat z_i(\hat z \cdot \hat z) = \hat z_i$, and the bracket gives $3\hat z_i + \hat z_i + \hat z_i = 5\hat z_i$, so the trace is $\hat z_i - \hat z_i = 0$; the factor $\frac15$ is exactly what that takes.

Guth argues later in the notes that these are the only traceless symmetric tensors unchanged by rotations about $z$. Then, up to a constant, the function

<Formula label="The azimuthally symmetric function of degree l" tex="F_\ell(\hat n) = \{\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.15)" />

is the only one that is azimuthally symmetric and satisfies $\nabla_\theta^2 F_\ell = -\ell(\ell + 1)F_\ell$. The Legendre polynomial $P_\ell(\cos\theta)$ has both properties, so the two must be proportional (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="The Legendre polynomial is proportional to the tensor function" tex="P_\ell(\cos\theta) = \text{const}\,\{\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.16)" />

## Fixing the constant

Both sides of this equation are polynomials in $\cos\theta$ whose highest power is $\cos^\ell\theta$, so it is enough to compare the coefficients of that power (notes pp. 3–4). On the right, every term of $\{\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}$ except the first contains a Kronecker delta, which ties two factors of $\hat n$ together into $\hat n \cdot \hat n = 1$ and leaves fewer factors of $\hat n \cdot \hat z = \cos\theta$. So the leading term on the right is just $\text{const}\,(\hat z \cdot \hat n)^\ell = \text{const}\,\cos^\ell\theta$. On the left, Rodrigues' formula gives the leading term directly: only the highest power $x^{2\ell}$ of $(x^2 - 1)^\ell$ survives into the top power after $\ell$ derivatives, and each derivative lowers it by one (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="The leading term of the Legendre polynomial" tex="\begin{aligned} P_\ell(x) &= \frac{1}{2^\ell\,\ell\,!}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{\ell}\left[x^{2\ell} + (\text{lower powers})\right] = \frac{1}{2^\ell\,\ell\,!}\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^{\ell - 1}\left[(2\ell)\,x^{2\ell - 1} + (\text{lower powers})\right] \\ &= \frac{1}{2^\ell\,\ell\,!}\left[(2\ell)(2\ell - 1)\cdots(\ell + 1)\,x^\ell + (\text{lower powers})\right] = \frac{(2\ell)!}{2^\ell(\ell\,!)^2}\,x^\ell + (\text{lower powers})" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.17)" />

Matching the two leading coefficients fixes the constant (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="The Legendre polynomial as a traceless symmetric tensor" tex="P_\ell(\cos\theta) = \frac{(2\ell)!}{2^\ell(\ell\,!)^2}\,\{\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.18)" />

The lower powers come out right by themselves, which makes a good check (ours, not in the notes). For $\ell = 3$, contracting the rank-3 line of the list with $\hat n_i\hat n_j\hat n_k$ gives $\cos^3\theta - \frac15(3\cos\theta)$, and the constant is $6!/(2^3 \times 36) = \frac52$, so the right side is $\frac12(5\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta)$, which is $P_3$. For $\ell = 4$ the contraction gives $\cos^4\theta - \frac67\cos^2\theta + \frac{3}{35}$, the constant is $\frac{35}{8}$, and the product is $\frac18(35\cos^4\theta - 30\cos^2\theta + 3) = P_4$.

## Rotations about the z-axis and complex basis vectors

Without azimuthal symmetry the expansion needs all the $Y_{\ell m}$, which are chosen with a very simple dependence on $\phi$ (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="The phi dependence of Y l m" tex="Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi) \propto e^{im\phi}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.19)" />

In terms of rotations: turning the coordinate system by an angle $\psi$ about the $z$-axis shifts $\phi$ by $\psi$, so $Y_{\ell m}$ only picks up a phase factor, $e^{im\psi}$ (with a sign that depends on the direction of the rotation; Guth leaves it open, since it is easy to fix at the end). [The notes write the factor as $e^{i\psi}$, the value for $m = 1$.] To match the standard functions, then, we need traceless symmetric tensors that a rotation about $z$ only multiplies by a phase: complex tensors that are **eigenvectors of the rotation**.

Begin with a vector. A rotation about the $z$-axis by $\psi$ changes its components to (notes p. 4)

<Formula label="A rotation about the z-axis" tex="v'_x = v_x\cos\psi - v_y\sin\psi, \qquad v'_y = v_x\sin\psi + v_y\cos\psi" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.20)" />

so we want the eigenvectors of the matrix (notes p. 5)

<Formula label="The rotation matrix" tex="R = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\psi & -\sin\psi \\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.21)" />

As for any matrix (see [eigenvalues and eigenvectors](/courses/18-06/eigenvalues-and-eigenvectors), where a 90° rotation already has the eigenvalues $\pm i$), the eigenvalues $\lambda$ solve the characteristic equation, with $I$ the identity matrix:

<Formula label="The characteristic equation" tex="\det(R - \lambda I) = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.22)" />

<Formula label="The characteristic equation written out" tex="\det\begin{pmatrix} \cos\psi - \lambda & -\sin\psi \\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi - \lambda \end{pmatrix} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda^2 - 2\lambda\cos\psi + 1 = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.23)" />

The roots are a pair of phases:

<Formula label="The eigenvalues of a rotation" tex="\lambda = \cos\psi \pm \sqrt{\cos^2\psi - 1} = \cos\psi \pm i\sin\psi = e^{\pm i\psi}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.24)" />

Putting them back, the eigenvectors satisfy (notes p. 5)

<Formula label="The eigenvector equation" tex="\begin{pmatrix} \cos\psi - e^{\pm i\psi} & -\sin\psi \\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi - e^{\pm i\psi} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix} = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.25)" />

<Formula label="The eigenvector equation simplified" tex="\begin{pmatrix} \mp i\sin\psi & -\sin\psi \\ \sin\psi & \mp i\sin\psi \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \end{pmatrix} = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.26)" />

so $v_y = \mp i\,v_x$. Normalizing, Guth defines the complex unit vectors

<Formula label="The complex basis vectors" tex="\hat u^{(1)} = \hat u^{+} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\hat e_x + i\,\hat e_y\right), \qquad \hat u^{(2)} = \hat u^{-} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(\hat e_x - i\,\hat e_y\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.27)" />

[With the upper sign, the eigenvalue $e^{+i\psi}$ of eq. (8.20) goes with $v_y = -i\,v_x$, that is with $\hat u^{-}$. This is the sign Guth deliberately leaves open: rotating the vector one way is rotating the axes the other way. What fixes the labels is eq. (8.31) below, where $\hat u^{+}$ gives $e^{+i\phi}$.] They are orthonormal in the sense of [complex vectors](/courses/18-06/complex-matrices-fft), with a complex conjugate on the first vector (notes p. 5),

<Formula label="Orthonormality of the complex basis" tex="\hat u^{(i)*} \cdot \hat u^{(j)} = \delta_{ij}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.28)" />

and the third basis vector is the real one along the axis:

<Formula label="The third basis vector" tex="\hat u^{(3)} = \hat z = \hat e_z" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.29)" />

What direction does a vector with imaginary components point in? A definite direction in complex three-dimensional space, which is the same as a real six-dimensional space, Guth answers, but no picture is needed: these vectors are only used to form dot products and so build complex functions of $\theta$ and $\phi$ (notes pp. 5–6).

The conjugate in the orthonormality condition matters. It is what makes the length of a complex vector positive. Without it, $\hat u^{+} \cdot \hat u^{+} = \frac12(1 + i^2) = 0$, and likewise $\hat u^{-} \cdot \hat u^{-} = 0$. That has a useful consequence (notes p. 6): the product

<Formula label="A product of u plus vectors is traceless" tex="\hat u^{+}_{i_1}\cdots\hat u^{+}_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.30)" />

is already both symmetric and traceless, because every trace is a factor $\hat u^{+} \cdot \hat u^{+} = 0$.

## Building the spherical harmonics

The key dot product is (notes p. 6)

<Formula label="u plus dotted into n hat" tex="\hat u^{+} \cdot \hat n = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\,\sin\theta\,e^{i\phi}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.31)" />

(the $x$ and $y$ components of $\hat n$ combine into $\sin\theta(\cos\phi + i\sin\phi)$). So a function proportional to $e^{im\phi}$, for $m > 0$, needs $m$ factors of $\hat u^{+}$, each dotted into $\hat n$. Guth fills the remaining $\ell - m$ places with $\hat z$ and defines

<Formula label="The tensor function for degree l and order m" tex="F_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi) = \{\hat u^{+}_{i_1}\cdots\hat u^{+}_{i_m}\,\hat z_{i_{m+1}}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.32)" />

Why does every $\hat u^{+}$ end up dotted into an $\hat n$? Expand the curly bracket and sum the indices into dot products. Since $\hat u^{+} \cdot \hat z = 0$ and $\hat u^{+} \cdot \hat u^{+} = 0$, every term in which a $\hat u^{+}$ is contracted with another $\hat u^{+}$ or with a $\hat z$ vanishes, so in the surviving terms each $\hat u^{+}$ meets an $\hat n$. [The notes say here that every $\hat u^{+}$ is dotted into $\hat z$; $\hat n$ is meant, as their next sentence shows.] Each gives a factor $e^{i\phi}$, so $F_{\ell m}$ is proportional to $e^{im\phi}$, and by eq. (8.4) it is an eigenfunction of $\nabla_\theta^2$ with eigenvalue $-\ell(\ell + 1)$. Guth argues at the end that such a function is unique up to a constant; assuming that for now,

<Formula label="F l m is proportional to Y l m" tex="F_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi) \propto Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.33)" />

and the constant is found, as for the Legendre polynomials, from the leading term. $F_{\ell m}$ is $(\sin\theta)^m e^{im\phi}$ times a polynomial in $\cos\theta$, so compare the highest power of $\cos\theta$. It comes from the first term of the traceless part (notes p. 6),

<Formula label="The first term of the traceless part" tex="\{\hat u^{+}_{i_1}\cdots\hat u^{+}_{i_m}\,\hat z_{i_{m+1}}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\} = \hat u^{+}_{i_1}\cdots\hat u^{+}_{i_m}\,\hat z_{i_{m+1}}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell} + \text{terms} \propto \delta_{i_p i_q}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.34)" />

[The printed left side of eq. (8.34) stops after the first $\hat z$; it is the same bracket as in eq. (8.32).] Each Kronecker delta in the later terms makes two factors of $\hat n$ dot into each other and leaves fewer to give $\hat n \cdot \hat z = \cos\theta$. Hence

<Formula label="The leading term of F l m" tex="F_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi) = 2^{-m/2}\,(\sin\theta)^m\,e^{im\phi}\left[(\cos\theta)^{\ell - m} + (\text{lower powers of } \cos\theta)\right]" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.35)" />

For the standard side Guth quotes Jackson's eq. (3.53) (notes p. 7):

<Formula label="The standard spherical harmonics" tex="Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi) = \sqrt{\frac{2\ell + 1}{4\pi}\,\frac{(\ell - m)!}{(\ell + m)!}}\;P_\ell^m(\cos\theta)\,e^{im\phi}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.36)" />

where $P_\ell^m$ is the **associated Legendre function**, in Jackson's eq. (3.50)

<Formula label="The associated Legendre function" tex="P_\ell^m(x) = \frac{(-1)^m}{2^\ell\,\ell\,!}\,(1 - x^2)^{m/2}\,\frac{d^{\ell + m}}{dx^{\ell + m}}(x^2 - 1)^\ell" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.37)" />

(the same spherical harmonics as on the [8.04 page](/courses/8-04/spherical-harmonics-radial-equation), where the sign $(-1)^m$ is written in the formula for $Y_{\ell m}$ instead; where the sign goes is a convention, and the product is the same). Here $(1 - x^2)^{m/2} = (\sin\theta)^m$, and the derivatives are done as in eq. (8.17), now $\ell + m$ of them:

<Formula label="The leading term of the derivatives" tex="\frac{d^{\ell + m}}{dx^{\ell + m}}(x^2 - 1)^\ell = (2\ell)\cdots(\ell + 1)\,\ell\,(\ell - 1)\cdots(\ell - m + 1)\,x^{\ell - m} + (\text{lower powers}) = \frac{(2\ell)!}{(\ell - m)!}\,x^{\ell - m} + (\text{lower powers})" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.38)" />

Matching the coefficients of $(\sin\theta)^m e^{im\phi}\cos^{\ell - m}\theta$ on the two sides gives the answer, for $m \ge 0$ (notes p. 7):

<Formula label="Y l m from a traceless symmetric tensor" tex="Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi) = C^{(\ell, m)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.39)" />

<Formula label="The tensor for Y l m" tex="C^{(\ell, m)}_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_\ell} = d_{\ell m}\,\{\hat u^{+}_{i_1}\cdots\hat u^{+}_{i_m}\,\hat z_{i_{m+1}}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.40)" />

<Formula label="The normalizing constant" tex="d_{\ell m} = \frac{(-1)^m\,(2\ell)!}{2^\ell\,\ell\,!}\sqrt{\frac{2^m\,(2\ell + 1)}{4\pi\,(\ell + m)!\,(\ell - m)!}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.41)" />

For $m = 0$ this reproduces eq. (8.18) multiplied by the $\sqrt{(2\ell + 1)/4\pi}$ of eq. (8.9), as it must.

## Negative m

For negative $m$ the construction is the same with $\hat u^{-}$ in place of $\hat u^{+}$, $|m|$ of them, since $\hat u^{-} \cdot \hat n = \sin\theta\,e^{-i\phi}/\sqrt2$ (notes p. 7). Because $\hat u^{-}$ is the complex conjugate of $\hat u^{+}$, the tensor for $m < 0$ is the conjugate of the one for $|m|$, up to a sign:

<Formula label="The tensor for negative m" tex="C^{(\ell, m)}_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_\ell} = d_{\ell m}\,\{\hat u^{-}_{i_1}\cdots\hat u^{-}_{i_{|m|}}\,\hat z_{i_{|m|+1}}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\} = (-1)^m\,C^{(\ell, |m|)*}_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.42), corrected" />

with the constant

<Formula label="The normalizing constant for negative m" tex="d_{\ell m} = \frac{(2\ell)!}{2^\ell\,\ell\,!}\sqrt{\frac{2^{|m|}\,(2\ell + 1)}{4\pi\,(\ell + m)!\,(\ell - m)!}} \qquad (m < 0)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.43), corrected" />

[The notes keep the factor $(-1)^m$ of eq. (8.41) here too and write the last equality without the $(-1)^m$. That is off by a sign for odd $m$: Jackson's own formulas give $Y_{\ell, -m} = (-1)^m Y_{\ell m}^*$, and for $\ell = 1$ the notes' version gives $-Y_{1, -1}$. This page uses the corrected form, which we checked against the standard $Y_{\ell m}$ for every $m$ up to $\ell = 3$. The matching at negative $m$ is easiest with $P_\ell^{-m} = (-1)^m\frac{(\ell - m)!}{(\ell + m)!}P_\ell^m$, because eq. (8.37) with $m < 0$ has a negative power of $\sin\theta$ in front.]

## Where the curly brackets go

The curly brackets can be put on either factor, or on both, in expressions like eq. (8.32) (notes pp. 7–8):

<Formula label="The traceless part can be taken on either factor" tex="\begin{aligned} \{\hat u^{+}_{i_1}\cdots\hat u^{+}_{i_m}\,\hat z_{i_{m+1}}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell} &= \{\hat u^{+}_{i_1}\cdots\hat u^{+}_{i_m}\,\hat z_{i_{m+1}}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}\,\{\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}\} \\ &= \hat u^{+}_{i_1}\cdots\hat u^{+}_{i_m}\,\hat z_{i_{m+1}}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\,\{\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}\}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (8.44a)–(8.44b)" />

The reason is the same in both steps. A product and its traceless part differ only by terms containing Kronecker deltas, and a Kronecker delta summed against a traceless symmetric tensor gives one of its traces, which is zero. So in the first line the extra terms of $\{\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}\}$ contribute nothing against the traceless first factor; and once the second factor is traceless, the brackets on the first are no longer needed.

## Uniqueness

The argument used one unproved claim: that a function proportional to $e^{im\phi}$ with eigenvalue $-\ell(\ell + 1)$ is unique up to a constant. Assuming that the series of eq. (8.1) always exists, the proof is short (notes p. 8). There are $2\ell + 1$ linearly independent traceless symmetric tensors of rank $\ell$, and we have now built $2\ell + 1$ of them, the $C^{(\ell, m)}$ for $m = -\ell, \ldots, \ell$. They are linearly independent, because each is an eigenvector of rotations about $z$ with a different eigenvalue. So they are a **basis**: every traceless symmetric tensor of rank $\ell$ is a sum of them. If we also ask for an eigenvector of rotations about $z$ with a particular eigenvalue, only one member of the basis can appear.

What if one tried to mix $\hat u^{+}$ and $\hat u^{-}$ in one tensor? The tensor $\{\hat u^{+}_{i}\hat u^{-}_{j}\}$ is unchanged by rotations about the $z$-axis (the two phases cancel), so by the argument it must be a multiple of the $m = 0$ tensor. Indeed (notes p. 8),

<Formula label="Mixing u plus and u minus" tex="\{\hat u^{+}_{i}\,\hat u^{-}_{j}\} = -\frac12\,\{\hat z_i\hat z_j\}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (8.45)" />

The notes leave the "little work" to the reader; here it is. Without conjugation $\hat u^{+} \cdot \hat u^{-} = \frac12(1 - i^2) = 1$, so the trace of $\hat u^{+}_{i}\hat u^{-}_{j}$ is 1. Its symmetric part is $\frac12(\hat u^{+}_{i}\hat u^{-}_{j} + \hat u^{-}_{i}\hat u^{+}_{j}) = \frac12(\hat x_i\hat x_j + \hat y_i\hat y_j)$, where the imaginary cross terms cancel, and since $\hat x_i\hat x_j + \hat y_i\hat y_j + \hat z_i\hat z_j = \delta_{ij}$ this is $\frac12(\delta_{ij} - \hat z_i\hat z_j)$. Removing the trace means subtracting $\frac13\delta_{ij}$, which leaves $\frac16\delta_{ij} - \frac12\hat z_i\hat z_j = -\frac12\left(\hat z_i\hat z_j - \frac13\delta_{ij}\right)$.

<WorkedExample title="One function in both languages">
<Problem>
Write the function $F(\hat n) = \hat n_x^2 = \sin^2\theta\cos^2\phi$ (the square of the $x$ component of the direction) as a traceless symmetric tensor series, then as a sum of standard spherical harmonics. Check the result in the direction $\theta = 60°$, $\phi = 30°$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The tensor series">
$\hat n_x^2 = \hat x_i\hat x_j\,\hat n_i\hat n_j$, where $\hat x$ is the unit vector along $x$. The trace of $\hat x_i\hat x_j$ is 1, so as on the Lecture 6 page, $\hat n_x^2 = \frac13 + \{\hat x_i\hat x_j\}\hat n_i\hat n_j$ with $\{\hat x_i\hat x_j\} = \hat x_i\hat x_j - \frac13\delta_{ij}$. There is an $\ell = 0$ part and an $\ell = 2$ part and nothing else.
</Step>
<Step title="Change to the basis of the u vectors">
Inverting eq. (8.27), $\hat x = (\hat u^{+} + \hat u^{-})/\sqrt2$, so $\hat x_i\hat x_j = \frac12\left(\hat u^{+}_{i}\hat u^{+}_{j} + \hat u^{-}_{i}\hat u^{-}_{j} + \hat u^{+}_{i}\hat u^{-}_{j} + \hat u^{-}_{i}\hat u^{+}_{j}\right)$. Take the traceless symmetric part term by term. The first two are already traceless. The last two each give $\{\hat u^{+}_{i}\hat u^{-}_{j}\} = -\frac12\{\hat z_i\hat z_j\}$ by eq. (8.45). So $\{\hat x_i\hat x_j\} = \frac12\hat u^{+}_{i}\hat u^{+}_{j} + \frac12\hat u^{-}_{i}\hat u^{-}_{j} - \frac12\{\hat z_i\hat z_j\}$: an $m = 2$, an $m = -2$ and an $m = 0$ piece, and no $m = \pm1$.
</Step>
<Step title="Divide by the constants">
By eq. (8.40), $\hat u^{+}_{i}\hat u^{+}_{j}\hat n_i\hat n_j = Y_{22}/d_{22}$, and eq. (8.41) gives $d_{22} = \sqrt{15/(8\pi)}$ (with $\hat u^{+} \cdot \hat n$ squared, $Y_{22} = \sqrt{15/(32\pi)}\,\sin^2\theta\,e^{2i\phi}$, the textbook form). The $m = -2$ constant is the same, and $d_{20} = 3\sqrt5/(4\sqrt\pi)$. Finally $\frac13 = \frac13\sqrt{4\pi}\,Y_{00}$. Altogether
$\sin^2\theta\cos^2\phi = \frac{\sqrt{4\pi}}{3}Y_{00} - \frac23\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{5}}\,Y_{20} + \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{15}}\left(Y_{22} + Y_{2,-2}\right)$,
with the coefficients $a_{00} \approx 1.18$, $a_{20} \approx -0.528$ and $a_{2,\pm2} \approx 0.647$. The two $m = \pm2$ coefficients are equal, as the relation for negative $m$ requires of a real function ($a_{2,-2} = (-1)^2 a_{22}^*$).
</Step>
<Step title="Check in one direction">
At $\theta = 60°$, $\phi = 30°$: $\hat n_x = \sin 60°\cos 30° = 0.75$, so $F = 0.5625$. The three terms of the series give $\frac13$ (the constant), $+\frac{1}{24}$ (here $\cos^2\theta = \frac14$, so $P_2 = -\frac18$ and the $m = 0$ term is $-\frac13 P_2$), and $+\frac{3}{16}$ (the $m = \pm2$ pair adds up to $\frac12\sin^2\theta\cos 2\phi = \frac12 \times \frac34 \times \frac12$). The sum is $\frac{16 + 2 + 9}{48} = \frac{9}{16} = 0.5625$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Why a real sky has 2ℓ + 1 numbers per multipole">
Maps of the cosmic microwave background are expanded in exactly these standard, complex $Y_{\ell m}$, with coefficients $a_{\ell m}$ (see [the CMB anisotropies page of 8.902](/courses/8-902/cmb-anisotropies)). The temperature is real, so by the relation for negative $m$ the coefficients obey $a_{\ell, -m} = (-1)^m a_{\ell m}^*$, just as in the worked example: of the $2\ell + 1$ complex numbers, only $2\ell + 1$ real numbers are free, the same count as the components of one real traceless symmetric tensor of rank $\ell$. The power spectrum $C_\ell$ is an average over those numbers, and since we have only one sky (a point not made in these notes), the quadrupole $\ell = 2$ is measured from just five, which limits how precisely it can ever be known.
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Two languages:** a function of direction is either a series of traceless symmetric tensors contracted with $\hat n$ or a sum of spherical harmonics $Y_{\ell m}$; both terms of degree $\ell$ are eigenfunctions of $\nabla_\theta^2$ with eigenvalue $-\ell(\ell + 1)$, and both come in $2\ell + 1$ independent kinds.
- **Azimuthal symmetry:** the only tensors unchanged by rotations about $z$ are the traceless parts $\{\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}$, and matching the highest power of $\cos\theta$ with Rodrigues' formula gives $P_\ell(\cos\theta) = \frac{(2\ell)!}{2^\ell(\ell\,!)^2}\{\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}$.
- **Complex basis:** the eigenvectors of a rotation about $z$ are $\hat u^{+} = (\hat e_x + i\hat e_y)/\sqrt2$ and $\hat u^{-} = (\hat e_x - i\hat e_y)/\sqrt2$, with eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\psi}$ up to the sign convention; $\hat u^{(i)*} \cdot \hat u^{(j)} = \delta_{ij}$ but $\hat u^{+} \cdot \hat u^{+} = 0$, and $\hat u^{+} \cdot \hat n = \sin\theta\,e^{i\phi}/\sqrt2$.
- **The dictionary:** $Y_{\ell m} = d_{\ell m}\{\hat u^{+}_{i_1}\cdots\hat u^{+}_{i_m}\hat z_{i_{m+1}}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}$ for $m \ge 0$, with $d_{\ell m}$ fixed by the leading power; for $m < 0$ use $\hat u^{-}$, and the tensor is $(-1)^m$ times the conjugate of the one for $|m|$ (the notes drop this sign).
- **Uniqueness:** the $2\ell + 1$ tensors $C^{(\ell, m)}$ are independent and so form a basis; mixed products such as $\{\hat u^{+}_{i}\hat u^{-}_{j}\} = -\frac12\{\hat z_i\hat z_j\}$ give nothing new.
- **Example:** $\sin^2\theta\cos^2\phi = \frac13 + \{\hat x_i\hat x_j\}\hat n_i\hat n_j$ becomes $\frac{\sqrt{4\pi}}{3}Y_{00} - \frac23\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{5}}Y_{20} + \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{15}}(Y_{22} + Y_{2,-2})$.
