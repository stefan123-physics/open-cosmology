The [first page](/courses/8-07/electric-magnetic-dipole-radiation) on these notes worked out the radiation of two oscillating charges and of a current loop. The rest of Guth's slides for the last lecture (slides 14–24) generalize in two directions: to a source of any shape, and to a single point charge with any acceleration, which gives the **Larmor formula**. Then comes the question of what radiating does to the charge itself: the **radiation-reaction force**, the course's last topic, where classical electrodynamics reaches its limits. (Our own words, worked example and graph; the diagram on slide 14 is from Griffiths' textbook and is not reproduced.)

## Radiation from a source of any shape

Take any charge distribution $\rho(\vec r, t)$ localized near the origin (slide 14). Its potential is the retarded integral (slide 14)

<Formula label="The retarded potential of any source" tex="V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}" cite="reading1" at="slide 14, eq. (25)" />

with $t_r = t - |\vec r - \vec r\,'|/c$. The plan is to expand both $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$ and $t_r$ in powers of $\vec r\,'$, with the same approximations as for the two spheres (slide 14). There is one minor difference: there is no single $\omega$ now. Previously the condition was $d \ll \lambda$, or $\omega \ll c/d$; here, with $d$ the size of the source, Guth needs $|\ddot\rho/\dot\rho| \ll c/d$, with similar bounds on higher time derivatives (slide 14). In words: the source must change little in the time light needs to cross it.

Here is how the expansion goes (our sketch of the steps the slide leaves out). To first order in $r'/r$, $|\vec r - \vec r\,'| \approx r - \hat r \cdot \vec r\,'$, so the retarded time is $t_r \approx t_0 + \hat r \cdot \vec r\,'/c$, where $t_0 = t - r/c$ is the retarded time at the origin. Then $\rho(\vec r\,', t_r) \approx \rho(\vec r\,', t_0) + \dot\rho(\vec r\,', t_0)\,\hat r \cdot \vec r\,'/c$ and $1/|\vec r - \vec r\,'| \approx (1 + \hat r \cdot \vec r\,'/r)/r$. The integral of $\rho$ is the total charge $Q$, and the integral of $\rho\,\vec r\,'$ is the dipole moment $\vec p$. Keeping first order in $\vec r\,'$ gives (slide 15)

<Formula label="The potential of any source, to dipole order" tex="V(\vec r, t) \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{Q}{r} + \frac{\hat r \cdot \vec p(t_0)}{r^2} + \frac{\hat r \cdot \dot{\vec p}(t_0)}{rc}\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 15, eq. (26)" />

The first term is the static Coulomb potential (the total charge cannot change), the second the dipole potential with the dipole moment at the retarded time, and the third, falling only as $1/r$, produces the radiation. Together with the vector potential, found the same way, the result in the radiation zone is (slide 15)

<Formula label="The radiation fields of any source" tex="\vec E(\vec r, t) \approx \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}\left[(\hat r \cdot \ddot{\vec p})\,\hat r - \ddot{\vec p}\right], \qquad \vec B(\vec r, t) \approx -\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi rc}\left[\hat r \times \ddot{\vec p}\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 15, eq. (27) (boxed)" />

with $\ddot{\vec p}$ evaluated at $t_0$. Although it looks different, Guth points out that this is the same as the simple dipole of the first page, written with vectors and with $-\omega^2p_0$ replaced by $\ddot{\vec p}$ (slide 15). Indeed, for $\vec p = p_0\cos(\omega t)\,\hat z$, $\ddot{\vec p} = -\omega^2p_0\cos(\omega t)\,\hat z$, and $(\hat r \cdot \hat z)\hat r - \hat z = \sin\theta\,\hat\theta$, which brings back the field along $\hat\theta$ proportional to $\sin\theta$.

The electric dipole formula is really the first term of a doubly infinite series (slide 16): there is electric dipole, quadrupole, … radiation, and also magnetic dipole, quadrupole, … radiation. When a source has no changing electric dipole moment, the next terms take over. (Our addition: each further order is weaker by roughly another factor of the source size over the wavelength.)

## Radiation by a point charge

The second generalization starts from the fields of a point charge, found on the [Liénard–Wiechert page](/courses/8-07/lienard-wiechert-point-charge-fields) by differentiating the potentials (slide 17):

<Formula label="The electric field of a moving point charge" tex="\vec E(\vec r, t) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{|\vec r - \vec r_p|}{\left(\vec u \cdot (\vec r - \vec r_p)\right)^3}\left[(c^2 - v_p^2)\,\vec u + (\vec r - \vec r_p) \times (\vec u \times \vec a_p)\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 17, eq. (28) (boxed)" />

with (slide 17)

<Formula label="The vector u" tex="\vec u = c\,\hat n - \vec v_p, \qquad \hat n = \frac{\vec r - \vec r_p}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|}" cite="reading1" at="slide 17, eq. (29) (boxed)" />

and $\vec r_p$, $\vec v_p$, $\vec a_p$ the position, velocity and acceleration of the charge at the retarded time. (Guth writes $\hat n$ and $|\vec r - \vec r_p|$ with Griffiths' script letter.) The first term falls as one over the distance squared; the second, proportional to the acceleration, falls only as one over the distance and is the radiation. If the charge is momentarily at rest, $\vec v_p = 0$ at the retarded time, then $\vec u = c\,\hat n$ and the radiation field is (slide 17)

<Formula label="The radiation field of a charge at rest" tex="\vec E_{\text{rad}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0c^2|\vec r - \vec r_p|}\left[\hat n \times (\hat n \times \vec a_p)\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 17, eq. (30) (boxed)" />

[The slide's denominator has $|\vec r - \vec r\,'|$; it means the distance from the charge, $|\vec r - \vec r_p|$.] The vector $\hat n \times (\hat n \times \vec a_p)$ is minus the part of the acceleration at right angles to the line of sight, so only that part radiates, as the 8.03 page on [radiation from accelerated charges](/courses/8-03/wave-plates-radiation) found with field lines.

## The Larmor formula

With $\vec B_{\text{rad}} = \hat n \times \vec E_{\text{rad}}/c$, the Poynting vector of a charge at rest is (slide 18)

<Formula label="The Poynting vector of an accelerated charge" tex="\vec S_{\text{rad}} = \frac{1}{\mu_0c}|\vec E_{\text{rad}}|^2\,\hat n = \frac{\mu_0q^2a^2}{16\pi^2c}\left(\frac{\sin^2\theta}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|^2}\right)\hat n" cite="reading1" at="slide 18, eq. (31) (boxed)" />

where $a = |\vec a_p|$ and $\theta$ is the angle between $\hat n$ and the acceleration. It is the same doughnut as the oscillating dipole: nothing along the acceleration, the most at right angles to it. Integrating over a sphere around the charge, again with $\int\sin^3\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi = 8\pi/3$, gives the **Larmor formula** for the total radiated power (slide 18):

<Formula label="The Larmor formula" tex="P = \frac{\mu_0q^2a^2}{6\pi c}" cite="reading1" at="slide 18, eq. (32) (boxed)" />

It is strictly for a charge at rest at the retarded time, so in practice for a nonrelativistic one. For any velocity, **Liénard's generalization** is (slide 19)

<Formula label="Liénard's generalization of the Larmor formula" tex="P = \frac{\mu_0q^2\gamma^6}{6\pi c}\left(a^2 - \left|\frac{\vec v \times \vec a}{c}\right|^2\right)" cite="reading1" at="slide 19, eq. (33) (boxed)" />

with $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$. The slide also gives it, "for relativists only", in the form $\mu_0q^2/(6\pi m^2c)$, with $m$ the rest mass, times the square of the rate of change of the four-momentum with respect to proper time (slide 19). (Our remark: written that way, the power is plainly the same in every inertial frame.) Guth defines $P$ here as the rate at which energy that is destined to become radiation leaves the particle (slide 19). [The sentence on the slide lacks its last word, "radiation".]

The factor $\gamma^6$ makes fast particles radiate far more for the same acceleration. With the acceleration along the velocity the cross product vanishes and the factor is $\gamma^6$; at right angles, as in circular motion, $|\vec v \times \vec a| = va$ and the factor becomes $\gamma^6(1 - v^2/c^2) = \gamma^4$.

## Radiation reaction

A charge that radiates must give up the energy it sends away; that much is certain, but the mechanism is not (slide 20). Guth warns that no rigorous treatment combines point charges with the conservation laws (slide 20). A first guess is that the force of radiation on the particle, $\vec F_{\text{self}}$ (Guth labels it "rad"; this page, like the [self-energy page](/courses/8-07/electrostatic-energy-self-energy), calls it the self-force, so that read-aloud does not take the label for the unit radian), does work at the rate of the Larmor power (slide 20):

<Formula label="A first guess for the radiation-reaction force" tex="\vec F_{\text{self}} \cdot \vec v = -\frac{\mu_0q^2a^2}{6\pi c}" cite="reading1" at="slide 20, eq. (34)" />

(The slide puts a question mark over the equals sign.) Guth gives two objections (slide 20). The Larmor power counts only the energy on its way to becoming radiation, while the particle may also trade energy back and forth with its near fields. And the right side contains no $\vec v$ at all, so the two sides cannot be made to agree. (Our illustration of the second point: at an instant when $\vec v = 0$, the left side is zero whatever the force, but the right side is not.)

What can be matched is the total over a cycle of **periodic motion**. If a cycle runs from $t_1$ to $t_2$, an integration by parts gives (slide 21)

<Formula label="Integrating the Larmor power by parts" tex="\int_{t_1}^{t_2}a^2\,dt = \int\frac{d\vec v}{dt} \cdot \frac{d\vec v}{dt}\,dt = \vec v(t_2) \cdot \vec a(t_2) - \vec v(t_1) \cdot \vec a(t_1) - \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\frac{d^2\vec v}{dt^2} \cdot \vec v\,dt" cite="reading1" at="slide 21, eq. (35)" />

(the slide writes the boundary term as $\vec v \cdot d\vec v/dt$ between the limits), and the boundary term vanishes, because the velocity and acceleration at $t_2$ are the same as at $t_1$ (slide 21). So the energy lost over a cycle matches the Larmor formula if (slide 21)

<Formula label="The condition for energy conservation" tex="\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\left(\vec F_{\text{self}} - \frac{\mu_0q^2}{6\pi c}\,\dot{\vec a}\right) \cdot \vec v\,dt = 0" cite="reading1" at="slide 21, eq. (36)" />

which holds if (slide 21)

<Formula label="The Abraham–Lorentz formula" tex="\vec F_{\text{self}} = \frac{\mu_0q^2}{6\pi c}\,\dot{\vec a}" cite="reading1" at="slide 21, eq. (37) (boxed)" />

This is the **Abraham–Lorentz formula** for the radiation-reaction force, the formula stated without proof on the earlier page on [self-energy](/courses/8-07/electrostatic-energy-self-energy), where it is called the self-force. Guth is careful about its status (slide 22). The argument above is no proof; what it shows is that this force keeps the energy books balanced, at least when the motion repeats. One can try a "derivation" by treating the charge as a tiny ball whose pieces push on one another, working out the net self-force, and then shrinking the ball to a point; but what comes out depends on how the ball is modelled, so Guth regards this route as inconclusive (slide 22).

## Runaway solutions

In many situations the formula gives sensible answers, but Guth points to a serious flaw: it admits **runaway solutions** (slide 23). For a particle on which no other force acts, Newton's law reads (slide 23)

<Formula label="A particle with only the radiation-reaction force" tex="\vec F_{\text{self}} = \frac{\mu_0q^2}{6\pi c}\,\dot{\vec a} = m\vec a" cite="reading1" at="slide 23, eq. (38)" />

which has the solution (slide 23)

<Formula label="The runaway solution" tex="a(t) = a_0\,e^{t/\tau}" cite="reading1" at="slide 23, eq. (39)" />

where (slide 23)

<Formula label="The time constant of radiation reaction" tex="\tau = \frac{\mu_0q^2}{6\pi mc}" cite="reading1" at="slide 23, eq. (40)" />

which is about $6 \times 10^{-24}$ s for an electron (slide 23); more precisely $6.27 \times 10^{-24}$ s, the time light takes to cross two thirds of the classical electron radius. Unless the acceleration starts exactly at zero, it grows exponentially with no cause, multiplying by $e$ in each $\tau$: an electron left alone would accelerate itself. The physical solution must be picked out by hand, as on the self-energy page, where the same runaway appears.

The last slide turns to quantum electrodynamics, which Guth presents as the better-behaved successor (slide 24). There the infinities are tamed by **renormalization**, a procedure known to work at every step of a series expansion in the fine-structure constant, about 1/137, even though the series as a whole is thought to diverge. QED has not been shown to be mathematically well-defined, and may not be; yet, as Guth sees it, it describes nature consistently up to energies far above the Planck mass (slide 24).

<WorkedExample title="A slow electron circling in a magnetic field">
<Problem>
An electron moves at $v = 10^7$ m/s at right angles to a uniform magnetic field of 1 T. Find (a) its acceleration and the power it radiates, (b) how fast it loses its kinetic energy, (c) the Abraham–Lorentz force compared with the magnetic force, and check that this force removes exactly the radiated power, and (d) the relativistic correction. Use $q = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C, $m = 9.109 \times 10^{-31}$ kg, $\mu_0 = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6}$ T m/A and $c = 2.998 \times 10^8$ m/s.
</Problem>
<Step title="Acceleration and power">
The magnetic force $qvB$ gives the acceleration $a = qvB/m = 1.602 \times 10^{-19} \times 10^7 \times 1/9.109 \times 10^{-31} = 1.759 \times 10^{18}$ m/s², at right angles to the velocity. The electron circles at the cyclotron angular frequency $\omega_c = qB/m = 1.759 \times 10^{11}$ per second, and $a = \omega_cv$. The Larmor formula gives $P = \mu_0q^2a^2/(6\pi c) = 1.2566 \times 10^{-6} \times (1.602 \times 10^{-19})^2 \times (1.759 \times 10^{18})^2/(6\pi \times 2.998 \times 10^8) = 1.77 \times 10^{-17}$ W.
</Step>
<Step title="The energy loss">
The kinetic energy is $K = \frac12mv^2 = 4.55 \times 10^{-17}$ J, about 284 eV. Since $P \propto a^2 \propto v^2 \propto K$, the energy decays exponentially, with time constant $K/P = 2.58$ s. Slow on the scale of the orbit (one turn takes $2\pi/\omega_c = 36$ ps), but real: this cyclotron radiation is how electrons in strong magnetic fields cool.
</Step>
<Step title="The Abraham–Lorentz force">
In circular motion the acceleration turns with the velocity, and $\dot{\vec a} = -\omega_c^2\vec v$. So $\vec F_{\text{self}} = m\tau\,\dot{\vec a} = -m\tau\omega_c^2\vec v$: a drag, opposite to the velocity. Its size relative to the magnetic force $m\omega_cv$ is $\tau\omega_c = 6.27 \times 10^{-24} \times 1.759 \times 10^{11} = 1.10 \times 10^{-12}$, utterly negligible for the orbit. Its power is $\vec F_{\text{self}} \cdot \vec v = -m\tau\omega_c^2v^2 = -m\tau a^2$, and $m\tau a^2 = \mu_0q^2a^2/(6\pi c)$, exactly the Larmor power. For circular motion the balance holds at every instant, not just over a cycle, because $a$ is constant in size.
</Step>
<Step title="The relativistic correction">
Here $v/c = 0.0334$ and the acceleration is at right angles to the velocity, so Liénard's formula multiplies the Larmor power by $\gamma^4 = 1.0022$: a 0.2% correction, which justifies the nonrelativistic treatment.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Thomson scattering

One more use of the Larmor formula, beyond the slides but close to cosmology. A light wave with electric field $E_0\cos\omega t$ shakes a **free** electron, giving it the acceleration $(qE_0/m)\cos\omega t$. Averaged over a cycle, the Larmor formula gives the scattered power $\mu_0q^4E_0^2/(12\pi m^2c)$, while the wave delivers the intensity $\langle S\rangle = \frac12c\,\varepsilon_0E_0^2$. Their ratio is the area the electron presents to the wave, its **Thomson cross-section** $\sigma_T = \mu_0q^4/(6\pi\varepsilon_0m^2c^2)$. Using $\mu_0\varepsilon_0c^2 = 1$, this is $\sigma_T = \frac{8\pi}{3}\,r_e^2$, where $r_e = q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0mc^2) = 2.818 \times 10^{-15}$ m is the classical electron radius, so $\sigma_T = 6.65 \times 10^{-29}$ m². Unlike the bound electrons of the blue sky on the first page, a free electron scatters every frequency equally, with the same $\sin^2$ pattern and the same polarization.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Thomson scattering and the cosmic background light">
Before about 380,000 years after the Big Bang the universe was hot enough to keep hydrogen ionized, and the free electrons Thomson-scattered photons so often that light could not travel far: the universe was opaque. When the electrons combined with protons into atoms, the scattering nearly stopped, and the photons have travelled freely since. We see them as the cosmic microwave background, as 8.286 explains in [temperature and decoupling](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling) and 8.902 in [the CMB anisotropies](/courses/8-902/cmb-anisotropies). The same cross-section sets the Eddington luminosity of an accreting black hole, on the 8.902 page on [active galactic nuclei](/courses/8-902/g-dwarf-problem-agn).
</Callout>

## Summary

- A source of any shape, small compared with $r$ and slowly varying ($|\ddot\rho/\dot\rho| \ll c/d$), has $V \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{Q}{r} + \frac{\hat r \cdot \vec p}{r^2} + \frac{\hat r \cdot \dot{\vec p}}{rc}\right]$ at the origin's retarded time, and radiates $\vec E \approx \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}\left[(\hat r \cdot \ddot{\vec p})\hat r - \ddot{\vec p}\right]$, $\vec B \approx -\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi rc}\hat r \times \ddot{\vec p}$: the simple dipole with $-\omega^2p_0 \to \ddot{\vec p}$, the first term of a doubly infinite series of electric and magnetic multipoles.
- A point charge momentarily at rest radiates through the acceleration term of its field, with intensity proportional to $\sin^2\theta$ about the acceleration and total power given by the **Larmor formula** $P = \mu_0q^2a^2/(6\pi c)$. Liénard's generalization multiplies by $\gamma^6$ and subtracts $|\vec v \times \vec a/c|^2$.
- The energy radiated must come from the particle, but no force can satisfy $\vec F \cdot \vec v = -P$ at every instant. For periodic motion, the **Abraham–Lorentz force** $\vec F_{\text{self}} = \mu_0q^2\dot{\vec a}/(6\pi c)$ removes exactly the radiated energy per cycle; it is consistent, not proven, and model-dependent derivations are inconclusive.
- It has **runaway solutions** $a = a_0\,e^{t/\tau}$, with $\tau = \mu_0q^2/(6\pi mc) \approx 6 \times 10^{-24}$ s for an electron. Quantum electrodynamics, with renormalization, controls such infinities much better, though it too is not proven to be fully well-defined.
- In the worked example an electron at $10^7$ m/s in a 1 T field radiates $1.77 \times 10^{-17}$ W, losing its energy with a time constant of 2.58 s; the Abraham–Lorentz drag is $10^{-12}$ of the magnetic force and balances the radiated power exactly. With the Larmor formula a free electron scatters light with the Thomson cross-section $\frac{8\pi}{3}r_e^2 = 6.65 \times 10^{-29}$ m².
