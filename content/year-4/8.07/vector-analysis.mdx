8.07 is MIT's second, intermediate course in electromagnetism, after 8.02, taught at the level of Griffiths' *Introduction to Electrodynamics*. Guth's first set of notes, for the opening lecture on 5 September 2012, reviews vector algebra (notes pp. 1–5). Most of it will be familiar from [vectors](/courses/8-01/vectors), [the dot product](/courses/18-02/dot-product) and [the cross product](/courses/18-02/determinants-and-cross-product), so this page goes quickly over the basics. What is new is a question Guth asks along the way: **why** does the dot product contain $\cos\theta$, and the cross product $\sin\theta$? His answer, that two simple properties leave no other choice, is the first taste of an idea that runs through the whole course: what a quantity must look like is fixed by how it behaves under rotations. (MIT's material for this lecture is the typed notes only; there is no video. This page follows them in order, in its own words, with its own example.)

## What a vector is

Guth starts from the elementary definition: a **vector** is a quantity with a magnitude and a direction (notes p. 1). Displacements, velocities, accelerations, forces and momenta are vectors, and so are the electric and magnetic fields that 8.07 is about.

A vector has **no position**. Two arrows that are parallel, point the same way and have the same length are the same vector, wherever they are drawn. (An electric field assigns a vector to each point of space, but each of those vectors is still just a magnitude and a direction.) The next notes show how a vector's components change under a rotation, the starting point of the more careful definition used later in physics. Here, rotating a vector simply means turning its arrow.

## Operations on vectors

The notes list the operations, mostly by small drawings (notes p. 1):

- **Magnitude**: the length of $\vec A$, written $|\vec A|$ or often simply $A$. (The notes write it with double bars and use a three-line equals sign for "is defined to be".)
- **Negation**: $-\vec A$ has the same length as $\vec A$ and the opposite direction.
- **Addition**: place the tail of $\vec B$ at the head of $\vec A$; the arrow from the tail of $\vec A$ to the head of $\vec B$ is $\vec C = \vec A + \vec B$.
- **Subtraction** is defined through the first two:

<Formula label="Subtraction" tex="\vec A - \vec B = \vec A + (-\vec B)" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

- **Multiplication by a scalar** $a$ stretches the length by $|a|$, and reverses the direction if $a$ is negative. It is distributive:

<Formula label="Scalar multiplication is distributive" tex="a(\vec A + \vec B) = a\vec A + a\vec B" cite="reading1" at="p. 1" />

## The dot product, and a question

The dot product of two vectors is a number (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Dot product" tex="\vec A \cdot \vec B = |\vec A|\,|\vec B|\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ when their tails are put together. It has four properties:

- **Rotational invariance**: rotating both vectors together, by the same rotation, does not change the value. Lengths and the angle between the arrows are unchanged by a rotation.
- **Commutative**: $\vec A \cdot \vec B = \vec B \cdot \vec A$.
- **Distributive**: $\vec A \cdot (\vec B + \vec C) = \vec A \cdot \vec B + \vec A \cdot \vec C$.
- **Scalar multiplication**: $(a\vec A) \cdot \vec B = a(\vec A \cdot \vec B)$.

Then Guth asks: why $\cos\theta$? Suppose he defined a rival, the "Guth-dot product", with a sine in place of the cosine:

<Formula label="Guth's joke rival" tex="(\vec A \cdot \vec B)_{G} = |\vec A|\,|\vec B|\sin\theta" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

and hired a very good advertising agency to sell it. (The notes mark it with a vertical bar after the product, with the subscript "Guth"; here a subscript G marks it. They also point out the pun in "product".) Could it compete? Guth's tentative answer is that it would have a serious marketing problem: **it is not distributive**. A quick example shows this. Take $\vec A = \hat e_x$, $\vec B = \hat e_y$ and $\vec C = -\hat e_y$, three unit vectors along the coordinate axes. Each of $\vec B$ and $\vec C$ is at $90^\circ$ to $\vec A$, so each sine product is $1$, and the sum of the two is $2$. But $\vec B + \vec C$ is the zero vector, and its product with $\vec A$ is $0$. The left side of the distributive law gives $0$, the right side $2$.

The notes go further and claim that any product that is rotationally invariant and distributive must be the ordinary one, up to a constant:

<Formula label="Rotational invariance and distributivity fix the dot product" tex="\vec A \cdot \vec B = \text{const}\;|\vec A|\,|\vec B|\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="p. 2" />

The proof needs components, so it comes after them, below.

## The cross product

The cross product of two vectors is a vector (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Cross product" tex="\vec A \times \vec B = |\vec A|\,|\vec B|\sin\theta\;\hat n" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

where $\hat n$ is a unit vector perpendicular to both $\vec A$ and $\vec B$. Two opposite directions are perpendicular to both; the **right-hand rule** picks one. Curl the fingers of the right hand from $\vec A$ towards $\vec B$, through the smaller angle, and the thumb points along $\hat n$. (The notes show this with a picture of a right-handed screw, credited to another website: turning it from $\vec A$ towards $\vec B$ drives it along $\hat n$. This page describes the rule in words.) Its properties mirror those of the dot product:

- **Rotational invariance**, in a slightly different sense: if both vectors are rotated by the same rotation, the cross product is rotated by that rotation too. Since the result is a vector, "unchanged" would be the wrong demand.
- **Anticommutative**: $\vec A \times \vec B = -\vec B \times \vec A$.
- **Distributive**: $\vec A \times (\vec B + \vec C) = \vec A \times \vec B + \vec A \times \vec C$.
- **Scalar multiplication**: $(a\vec A) \times \vec B = a(\vec A \times \vec B)$.

And again: why $\sin\theta$? The answer is the same. Rotational invariance and distributivity together allow only (notes p. 3)

<Formula label="Rotational invariance and distributivity fix the cross product" tex="\vec A \times \vec B = \text{const}\;|\vec A|\,|\vec B|\sin\theta\;\hat n" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The notes state this without proof; the component form below makes it plausible, since the products of the basis vectors decide everything.

## Components

Choose three perpendicular unit vectors $\hat e_x$, $\hat e_y$, $\hat e_z$ along the positive coordinate axes. Any vector is a sum of multiples of them (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="Component form" tex="\vec A = A_x\,\hat e_x + A_y\,\hat e_y + A_z\,\hat e_z" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The notation varies between books: Griffiths writes $\hat x$, $\hat y$, $\hat z$ for the same unit vectors, and many other books, like the 18.02 pages, use $\hat i$, $\hat j$, $\hat k$. Adding vectors means adding their components:

<Formula label="Addition in components" tex="\vec A + \vec B = (A_x + B_x)\,\hat e_x + (A_y + B_y)\,\hat e_y + (A_z + B_z)\,\hat e_z" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

## The dot product in components

Write both vectors in components and multiply out using the distributive law (notes p. 4):

<Formula label="Dot product, expanded" tex="\vec A \cdot \vec B = (A_x\,\hat e_x + A_y\,\hat e_y + A_z\,\hat e_z) \cdot (B_x\,\hat e_x + B_y\,\hat e_y + B_z\,\hat e_z)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

Nine products of basis vectors appear. Each basis vector has length 1, so its product with itself is $1$, and two different basis vectors are perpendicular, so their product is $0$:

<Formula label="Dot products of the basis vectors" tex="\hat e_x \cdot \hat e_x = \hat e_y \cdot \hat e_y = \hat e_z \cdot \hat e_z = 1, \qquad \hat e_x \cdot \hat e_y = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

and the same for every other pair of different basis vectors. Only three of the nine terms survive:

<Formula label="Dot product in components" tex="\vec A \cdot \vec B = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

## Why the cosine: the proof

Now Guth returns to his question (notes p. 4). Suppose all we know about some product $\vec A \cdot \vec B$ is that it is rotationally invariant and distributive (and that numbers come out of it as in the scalar rule; the notes use this without comment). The expansion above used only the distributive law, so it still holds; what we no longer know are the nine products of basis vectors. Two steps fix them.

**The same basis vector twice.** A rotation can turn $\hat e_x$ into $\hat e_y$ or $\hat e_z$. Rotational invariance then says that $\hat e_x \cdot \hat e_x$, $\hat e_y \cdot \hat e_y$ and $\hat e_z \cdot \hat e_z$ are all equal to one constant.

**Two different basis vectors.** Turn the pair $(\hat e_x, \hat e_y)$ through $180^\circ$ about the $x$ axis. The vector $\hat e_x$ stays where it is and $\hat e_y$ becomes $-\hat e_y$, so rotational invariance gives

<Formula label="A half-turn about the x axis" tex="\hat e_x \cdot \hat e_y = \hat e_x \cdot (-\hat e_y)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

<VectorDiagram
  vectors={[{ x: 2, y: 0, label: "\\hat e_x" }, { x: 0, y: 2, label: "\\hat e_y", color: 1 }, { x: 0, y: -2, label: "-\\hat e_y", color: 2 }]}
  extent={[-2, 4, -3, 3]}
  caption="A half-turn about the x axis leaves the x unit vector in place and turns the y unit vector into its opposite, so the pair (x, y) and the pair (x, minus y) are rotated copies of each other. (Arrows drawn twice their unit length for clarity.)"
/>

So the sum $\hat e_x \cdot \hat e_y + \hat e_x \cdot (-\hat e_y)$ is $2\,\hat e_x \cdot \hat e_y$. But the distributive law turns the same sum into a product with the zero vector:

<Formula label="The distributive law finishes it" tex="\hat e_x \cdot \hat e_y + \hat e_x \cdot (-\hat e_y) = \hat e_x \cdot \left(\hat e_y + (-\hat e_y)\right) = \hat e_x \cdot \vec 0 = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

(and a product with the zero vector is zero, since $\vec 0 = 0\,\hat e_x$ and numbers come out of the product). So $\hat e_x \cdot \hat e_y = 0$, and likewise for every pair of different basis vectors. The expansion then leaves

<Formula label="The most general such product" tex="\vec A \cdot \vec B = \text{const}\;(A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z)" cite="reading1" at="p. 4" />

This had better agree with $\text{const}\;|\vec A||\vec B|\cos\theta$, and Guth shows it does by using rotational invariance once more: rotate both vectors so that $\vec A$ points along the positive $x$ axis. Then $A_x = |\vec A|$ and $A_y = A_z = 0$, so the product is $\text{const}\;A_xB_x$. And $B_x = |\vec B|\cos\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec B$ and the $x$ axis, which is now also the angle between $\vec B$ and $\vec A$. The cosine was never a free choice.

## The cross product in components

The same expansion works for the cross product (notes p. 5):

<Formula label="Cross product, expanded" tex="\vec A \times \vec B = (A_x\,\hat e_x + A_y\,\hat e_y + A_z\,\hat e_z) \times (B_x\,\hat e_x + B_y\,\hat e_y + B_z\,\hat e_z)" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

Now the product of a basis vector with itself vanishes (the angle is zero), and two different ones give the third, with a sign set by the right-hand rule:

<Formula label="Cross products of the basis vectors" tex="\hat e_x \times \hat e_x = \hat e_y \times \hat e_y = \hat e_z \times \hat e_z = \vec 0, \qquad \hat e_x \times \hat e_y = \hat e_z" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

together with its **cyclic permutations** ($x \to y \to z \to x$): $\hat e_y \times \hat e_z = \hat e_x$ and $\hat e_z \times \hat e_x = \hat e_y$. Reversing the order reverses the sign, for example $\hat e_y \times \hat e_x = -\hat e_z$. (The typed notes print this last relation as "$\hat e_y \times \hat e_z = -\hat e_z$", a typing slip: the order must be $\hat e_y \times \hat e_x$, by anticommutativity.) Six of the nine terms survive, and they collect into

<Formula label="Cross product in components" tex="\vec A \times \vec B = (A_xB_y - A_yB_x)\,\hat e_z + (A_yB_z - A_zB_y)\,\hat e_x + (A_zB_x - A_xB_z)\,\hat e_y" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

which is the same as a determinant, expanded along its first row:

<Formula label="Cross product as a determinant" tex="\vec A \times \vec B = \begin{vmatrix} \hat e_x & \hat e_y & \hat e_z \\ A_x & A_y & A_z \\ B_x & B_y & B_z \end{vmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="p. 5" />

## Worked example: two vectors, before and after a rotation

<WorkedExample title="Products of two vectors, and a rotation check">
<Problem>
Take $\vec A = (2, -1, 3)$ and $\vec B = (1, 4, -2)$ in components along $\hat e_x$, $\hat e_y$, $\hat e_z$. Find $\vec A \cdot \vec B$, the angle between them, and $\vec A \times \vec B$. Then rotate both vectors by $90^\circ$ about the $z$ axis and check the two kinds of rotational invariance.
</Problem>
<Step title="Dot product and angle">
$\vec A \cdot \vec B = 2 \times 1 + (-1) \times 4 + 3 \times (-2) = 2 - 4 - 6 = -8$. The lengths are $|\vec A| = \sqrt{4 + 1 + 9} = \sqrt{14}$ and $|\vec B| = \sqrt{1 + 16 + 4} = \sqrt{21}$, so $\cos\theta = -8/\sqrt{294} \approx -0.467$ and $\theta \approx 117.8^\circ$. The vectors are more than a right angle apart, as the negative sign says.
</Step>
<Step title="Cross product">
With the component formula: the $x$ component is $A_yB_z - A_zB_y = 2 - 12 = -10$, the $y$ component $A_zB_x - A_xB_z = 3 + 4 = 7$, the $z$ component $A_xB_y - A_yB_x = 8 + 1 = 9$. So $\vec A \times \vec B = (-10, 7, 9)$. Checks: it is perpendicular to both, since $\vec A \cdot (\vec A \times \vec B) = -20 - 7 + 27 = 0$ and $\vec B \cdot (\vec A \times \vec B) = -10 + 28 - 18 = 0$; and its length, $\sqrt{100 + 49 + 81} = \sqrt{230} \approx 15.17$, equals $|\vec A||\vec B|\sin\theta = \sqrt{294 - 64}$, because $\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta$.
</Step>
<Step title="Rotate both by 90° about z">
A quarter-turn about the $z$ axis (counterclockwise seen from above) sends $\hat e_x \to \hat e_y$ and $\hat e_y \to -\hat e_x$, so a vector $(x, y, z)$ becomes $(-y, x, z)$. Then $\vec A' = (1, 2, 3)$ and $\vec B' = (-4, 1, -2)$. Their dot product is $-4 + 2 - 6 = -8$: unchanged. Their cross product is $(2 \times (-2) - 3 \times 1,\; 3 \times (-4) - 1 \times (-2),\; 1 \times 1 - 2 \times (-4)) = (-7, -10, 9)$, which is exactly $(-10, 7, 9)$ turned by the same quarter-turn. The dot product is invariant; the cross product rotates along with its factors.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Where the notes stop

The first set of notes ends with the determinant. The rest of the vector analysis that 8.07 assumes (Griffiths' first chapter) is vector calculus: the [gradient](/courses/18-02/gradient), the [divergence and curl](/courses/18-02/div-curl-and-maxwell), the [divergence theorem](/courses/18-02/divergence-theorem) and [Stokes' theorem](/courses/18-02/stokes-theorem), all covered in 18.02. The next notes take up the idea at the heart of this page, rotations, and describe them in index notation.

## Summary

- A vector is a quantity with a magnitude and a direction, and no position. Vectors can be negated, added head to tail, subtracted and multiplied by scalars.
- The dot product $|\vec A||\vec B|\cos\theta$ is a number, unchanged when both vectors are rotated together; it is commutative and distributive. The cross product $|\vec A||\vec B|\sin\theta\,\hat n$ is a vector perpendicular to both, by the right-hand rule; it rotates with its factors, is anticommutative and distributive.
- Guth's question "why the cosine?" has a sharp answer: rotational invariance and the distributive law allow only a constant times $|\vec A||\vec B|\cos\theta$. A sine version is rotationally invariant but not distributive. The same holds for the sine in the cross product.
- The proof: rotations make the basis vectors' products with themselves equal, a half-turn about the $x$ axis gives $\hat e_x \cdot \hat e_y = \hat e_x \cdot (-\hat e_y)$, and the distributive law makes the sum zero.
- In components, $\vec A \cdot \vec B = A_xB_x + A_yB_y + A_zB_z$, and $\vec A \times \vec B$ is the determinant with rows $\hat e_x, \hat e_y, \hat e_z$; then $A_x, A_y, A_z$; then $B_x, B_y, B_z$.
