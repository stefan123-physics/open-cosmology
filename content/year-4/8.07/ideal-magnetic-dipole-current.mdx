Lecture Notes 11 are the handwritten slides of Guth's Lecture 26 (November 9, 2012). They pick up the [magnetic dipole](/courses/8-07/magnetic-multipole-expansion-dipoles) of the last notes and ask what a dipole of zero size, an **ideal magnetic dipole**, really is: which current density it has, and, on the [next page](/courses/8-07/magnetic-dipole-force-torque-maxwell), what force, energy and torque it feels and whether its field obeys Maxwell's equations. Guth's motto for the lecture is "Don't be afraid of delta functions (but be careful)" (slide 8). (Our own words, worked example and graph. Despite the title, the slides say nothing more about magnetic fields in matter: that material is at the end of the last notes.)

## The dipole moment, recalled

The last notes expanded the vector potential of a localized current distribution. Its leading term is the dipole term (slide 1):

<Formula label="The leading term of the vector potential" tex="\vec A(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{\vec m \times \hat r}{r^2} + O\!\left(\frac{1}{r^3}\right)" cite="reading1" at="slide 1 (boxed)" />

with the magnetic dipole moment, and its form for a current in a wire, where a piece of wire $d\vec\ell$ carries $\vec J\,d^3x = I\,d\vec\ell$ (slide 1):

<Formula label="The magnetic dipole moment" tex="\vec m = \frac12\int d^3x\;\vec r \times \vec J, \qquad \vec m = \frac12\,I\oint\vec r \times d\vec\ell" cite="reading1" at="slide 1 (boxed)" />

## The moment does not depend on the origin

Does $\vec m$ depend on where the origin is? No (slide 2). Measure positions from a shifted origin, $\vec r\,' = \vec r + \vec b$ with a constant $\vec b$. Since $d^3x' = d^3x$,

<Formula label="Shifting the origin" tex="\vec m\,' = \frac12\int d^3x'\;\vec r\,' \times \vec J = \frac12\int d^3x\,(\vec r + \vec b) \times \vec J = \vec m + \frac12\,\vec b \times \int d^3x\;\vec J" cite="reading1" at="slide 2" />

and the last integral is zero by current conservation, as the last notes showed: a steady current confined to a finite region has $\int\vec J\,d^3x = 0$. For a wire the same statement is $\oint d\vec\ell = 0$: going once around a closed loop brings you back to where you started (slide 2). So a magnetic dipole moment is a property of the current alone, unlike, say, the angular momentum of a moving body, which depends on the point it is measured about.

## The vector area, by a vector identity

The last notes turned $\frac12\oint\vec r \times d\vec\ell$ into the vector area with Levi-Civita symbols. The slides do it a second way, with vector identities (slides 3–4). Let $P$ be the loop and $S$ any surface bounded by it. Take the $i$ component with a basis vector $\hat e_i$ and move the dot product round the triple product (the line integrals run once around $P$):

<Formula label="One component of the moment" tex="m_i = \frac12\,I\oint\hat e_i \cdot \left(\vec r \times d\vec\ell\right) = \frac12\,I\oint d\vec\ell \cdot \left(\hat e_i \times \vec r\right) = \frac12\,I\int_{S} d\vec a \cdot \nabla \times \left(\hat e_i \times \vec r\right)" cite="reading1" at="slide 3" />

The last step is [Stokes' theorem](/courses/18-02/stokes-theorem), applied to the vector field $\hat e_i \times \vec r$. Its curl follows from the identity for the curl of a cross product; with $\hat e_i$ constant only two terms survive (slide 3):

<Formula label="The curl of a basis vector crossed with r" tex="\nabla \times \left(\hat e_i \times \vec r\right) = \hat e_i\left(\nabla \cdot \vec r\right) - \left(\hat e_i \cdot \nabla\right)\vec r = 3\,\hat e_i - \hat e_i = 2\,\hat e_i" cite="reading1" at="slide 3" />

since $\nabla \cdot \vec r = 3$, and $(\hat e_i \cdot \nabla)\vec r = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}(x_j\,\hat e_j) = \delta_{ij}\,\hat e_j = \hat e_i$ (slide 3). The 2 cancels the $\frac12$, and $m_i = I\int_{S} d\vec a \cdot \hat e_i$, that is (slide 4),

<Formula label="The moment is the current times the vector area" tex="\vec m = I\,\vec a, \qquad \vec a = \int_{S} d\vec a" cite="reading1" at="slide 4 (boxed)" />

$\vec a$ is the **vector area** of the loop. If the loop lies in a plane, $|\vec a|$ is its area (slide 4), and $\vec a$ is normal to the plane with the right-hand rule around the current, as in [8.02](/courses/8-02/magnetic-dipoles).

## The field and its delta-function term

The slides then recall the field of a dipole, derived in Problem 7 of Problem Set 7 (slide 5):

<Formula label="The field of a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec B_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec m}{r^3} + \frac{2\mu_0}{3}\,\vec m\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="slide 5 (boxed)" />

and the reason the delta-function term matters (slides 5–6): in hydrogen's ground state the electron cloud is spherically symmetric, the first term averages to zero over it (our gloss: the slides state only the conclusion), and the energy difference between aligned and antialigned spins, the astronomer's **21 cm line**, comes 100% from the delta-function term. The previous page treats this [in full](/courses/8-07/magnetic-multipole-expansion-dipoles). The next page checks that this field, delta function included, obeys Maxwell's equations with the current found below.

## The charge density of an ideal electric dipole

As a warm-up, Guth recalls the electric case (slides 7–8). A physical dipole has a charge $+q$ at $z = a/2$ and $-q$ at $z = -a/2$ on the $z$ axis, so $\vec p = aq\,\hat z$. The **ideal dipole** is the limit $a \to 0$ with $\vec p$ fixed. Write the charge density with one-dimensional delta functions and divide and multiply by $a$ (slide 8):

<Formula label="Shrinking an electric dipole" tex="\rho(\vec r) = q\,\delta(x)\,\delta(y)\,\delta\!\left(z - \frac a2\right) - q\,\delta(x)\,\delta(y)\,\delta\!\left(z + \frac a2\right) = -qa\,\delta(x)\,\delta(y)\left[\frac{\delta\!\left(z + \frac a2\right) - \delta\!\left(z - \frac a2\right)}{a}\right] \to -p\,\delta(x)\,\delta(y)\,\frac{d\delta(z)}{dz}" cite="reading1" at="slide 8" />

The bracket is a difference quotient, so as $a \to 0$ it becomes the derivative of $\delta(z)$ (for the [delta function](/courses/18-03/impulse-inputs-delta-function), "derivative" means: under an integral, move it onto the other factor with a minus sign). The minus sign in front is right: $-d\delta/dz$ is a positive spike just above the origin and a negative one just below, where the two charges were. For a dipole pointing in any direction, at any position $\vec r_d$ (slide 8):

<Formula label="The charge density of an ideal electric dipole" tex="\rho_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = -\vec p \cdot \nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_d)" cite="reading1" at="slide 8 (boxed)" />

where $\nabla$ acts on $\vec r$. A quick check of our own: integrating by parts, $\int\vec r\,\rho_{\text{dip}}\,d^3x = \vec p$, and $\int\rho_{\text{dip}}\,d^3x = 0$, as a dipole should have.

## A shrinking square loop

Now the magnetic case, first for the simplest loop (slides 9–11). A square loop of side $a$ lies in the $xy$ plane, centred on the origin, with the current $I$ running counterclockwise seen from $+z$: along $+x$ on the lower edge, $y = -a/2$, and along $-x$ on the upper edge, $y = a/2$. Its moment is (slide 9)

<Formula label="The square loop" tex="\vec m = I\,\vec a = I a^2\,\hat z" cite="reading1" at="slide 9" />

The ideal dipole is the limit $a \to 0$ with $m = I a^2$ fixed (slide 9), so the current must grow as $m/a^2$. For $a \neq 0$, the $x$ component of the current density comes from the two edges along $x$ (slide 10):

<Formula label="The x component of the loop's current" tex="J_x(\vec r) = I\,\delta(z)\left[\delta\!\left(y + \frac a2\right) - \delta\!\left(y - \frac a2\right)\right] \quad \text{for } |x| \leq \frac a2, \qquad J_x = 0 \text{ otherwise}" cite="reading1" at="slide 10" />

Set $I = m/a^2$. One factor of $1/a$ turns the bracket into a difference quotient, which tends to $d\delta(y)/dy$; the other is kept in a box function $g(x)$, equal to $1/a$ for $|x| \leq a/2$ and zero outside, which has unit area and so tends to $\delta(x)$ (slide 10):

<Formula label="The limit of small a" tex="J_x(\vec r) = m\,\delta(z)\,\frac{d\delta(y)}{dy}\,g(x) \to m\,\delta(x)\,\delta(z)\,\frac{d\delta(y)}{dy} = m\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="slides 10–11" />

<Plot variable="y" domain={[-1, 1]} range={[-30, 30]} xLabel="y (mm)" yLabel="f (1/mm²)" samples={400}
  params={{ a: { value: 0.6, min: 0.1, max: 1, step: 0.05, label: "a" } }}
  curves={[
    { expr: "(exp(-(y + a/2)^2/0.02) - exp(-(y - a/2)^2/0.02))/(0.250663*a)", label: "two wires" },
    { expr: "-100*y*exp(-y^2/0.02)/0.250663", label: "limit", dash: true }
  ]}
  caption="How two opposite currents become the derivative of a delta function, computed from the formulas. Across the square loop, Jₓ = m δ(z) g(x) f(y) with f(y) = [δ(y + a/2) − δ(y − a/2)]/a. To draw it, each wire is smeared into a Gaussian 0.1 mm wide (a delta function of unit area); y and the side a are in millimetres. The two edges along $y$ give $J_y$ in the same way, with the opposite sign (slide 11), and the two components combine into a cross product:

<Formula label="The current of the shrunken loop" tex="J_y(\vec r) = -m\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\delta^3(\vec r), \qquad \vec J = -\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="slide 11" />

Check the $x$ component: with $\vec m = m\,\hat z$, $(\hat z \times \nabla)_x = -\partial/\partial y$, so $-(\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3)_x = m\,\partial\delta^3/\partial y$. For a dipole at a position $\vec r_d$ (slide 11):

<Formula label="The current density of an ideal magnetic dipole" tex="\vec J(\vec r) = -\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_d)" cite="reading1" at="slide 11 (boxed)" />

Two remarks of ours. This current is conserved: with $\vec m$ constant, $\nabla \cdot \vec J = -\nabla \cdot (\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3) = \vec m \cdot (\nabla \times \nabla\delta^3) = 0$, since the curl of a gradient vanishes. And it gives back the moment it was built from: integrating by parts, $\frac12\int\vec r \times \vec J\,d^3x = \vec m$.

## Any current distribution

A square loop is a special case, so Guth repeats the limit for an arbitrary localized current $\vec J(\vec r)$ (slides 12–14). Shrink it while scaling up the current so that $\vec m$ stays fixed. With a scale factor $\lambda$ ($\lambda = 1$ is the original size, $\lambda = \frac12$ half size), the shrunken current is $\lambda^{-4}\vec J(\vec r/\lambda)$; substituting $\vec r = \lambda\vec u$ in the integral shows that its moment is unchanged (slide 12):

<Formula label="Shrinking any distribution" tex="\vec m = \frac{1}{2\lambda^4}\int d^3x\;\vec r \times \vec J\!\left(\frac{\vec r}{\lambda}\right)" cite="reading1" at="slide 12" />

The four powers of $\lambda$ are three for the volume, $d^3x = \lambda^3\,d^3u$, and one for the factor $\vec r$. The ideal dipole's current is the limit $\lambda \to 0$. Write the shrunken current as an integral over a delta function, and rescale the delta function, $\delta^3(\vec r\,' - \vec r/\lambda) = \lambda^3\,\delta^3(\vec r - \lambda\vec r\,')$ (slide 13):

<Formula label="The limit as an integral" tex="J_i(\vec r) = \lim_{\lambda \to 0}\frac{1}{\lambda^4}\int d^3x'\;J_i(\vec r\,')\,\delta^3\!\left(\vec r\,' - \frac{\vec r}{\lambda}\right) = \lim_{\lambda \to 0}\frac{1}{\lambda}\int d^3x'\;J_i(\vec r\,')\,\delta^3(\vec r - \lambda\vec r\,')" cite="reading1" at="slide 13" />

[The slides use the same letter $J_i$ for the ideal dipole's current on the left and the original current inside the integral.] Now expand the delta function in powers of $\lambda$, a Taylor series in the shift $\lambda\vec r\,'$ (slide 13):

<Formula label="Expanding in powers of the scale factor" tex="J_i(\vec r) = \lim_{\lambda \to 0}\frac{1}{\lambda}\int d^3x'\;J_i(\vec r\,')\left[\delta^3(\vec r) - \lambda\,\frac{\partial\delta^3(\vec r)}{\partial x_j}\,x'_j + \cdots\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 13" />

The first term would blow up as $1/\lambda$, but it is multiplied by $\int J_i\,d^3x' = 0$, by current conservation again (slide 13). The terms after the second vanish as $\lambda \to 0$. What is left is the moment $\mathcal M^{(1)}_{i;\,j} = \int J_i\,x_j\,d^3x$ of the last notes, which is antisymmetric and equal to $m_k\,\epsilon_{kji}$ (slide 14):

<Formula label="The ideal dipole from any distribution" tex="J_i(\vec r) = -\frac{\partial\delta^3(\vec r)}{\partial x_j}\int d^3x'\;J_i(\vec r\,')\,x'_j = -\frac{\partial\delta^3(\vec r)}{\partial x_j}\,\mathcal M^{(1)}_{i;\,j} = -\epsilon_{ikj}\,m_k\,\frac{\partial\delta^3(\vec r)}{\partial x_j} = -\left[\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3(\vec r)\right]_i" cite="reading1" at="slide 14" />

The same current as for the square loop: every localized current, shrunk to a point with its moment held fixed, becomes the same ideal dipole. This is the magnetic counterpart of $\rho_{\text{dip}} = -\vec p \cdot \nabla\delta^3$, with a cross product where the electric case has a dot product.

<WorkedExample title="Shrinking a loop">
<Problem>
A flat circular loop of radius $R = 1$ cm carries $I = 2$ A in a wire of cross-section 1 mm². Find its dipole moment. It is shrunk by $\lambda = 10^{-3}$, keeping $\vec m$ fixed: what current and current density does it then need? Compare its field on the axis 20 cm away before and after.
</Problem>
<Step title="The moment">
$m = I\pi R^2 = 2 \times \pi \times (0.01)^2 = 6.28 \times 10^{-4}$ A m², along the axis.
</Step>
<Step title="The shrunken loop">
The radius becomes 10 μm and the area falls by $\lambda^2 = 10^{-6}$, so to keep $m = Ia$ the current must rise to $2/10^{-6} = 2 \times 10^{6}$ A. The current density was $2/10^{-6} = 2 \times 10^{6}$ A/m²; the cross-section also falls by $\lambda^2$, so it becomes $2 \times 10^{6}/10^{-12} = 2 \times 10^{18}$ A/m², the factor $\lambda^{-4} = 10^{12}$ of the scaling. No real wire could carry this: the ideal dipole is a limit, useful because real dipoles (an atom, a nucleus, an electron's spin) are tiny compared with the distances at which we look at them.
</Step>
<Step title="The far field">
On the axis the exact field is $B = \mu_0 I R^2/2(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}$ and the dipole term is $\mu_0 m/2\pi z^3$. At $z = 0.2$ m the dipole term is $2 \times 10^{-7} \times 6.28 \times 10^{-4}/0.008 = 1.57 \times 10^{-8}$ T, or 15.7 nT. The exact field is smaller by the factor $(1 + R^2/z^2)^{-3/2}$: for the original loop, $(1.0025)^{-3/2} = 0.9963$, so 0.37% smaller, 15.65 nT. After shrinking, $IR^2$ is unchanged but $R^2/z^2 = 2.5 \times 10^{-9}$, and the difference falls to $4 \times 10^{-9}$ of the field: only the dipole field is left, which is what the ideal dipole describes.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- **The moment:** $\vec m = \frac12\int\vec r \times \vec J\,d^3x = \frac12 I\oint\vec r \times d\vec\ell$; shifting the origin by $\vec b$ adds $\frac12\vec b \times \int\vec J\,d^3x = 0$, so $\vec m$ does not depend on the origin.
- **The vector area:** with $\nabla \times (\hat e_i \times \vec r) = 2\hat e_i$ and Stokes' theorem, $\vec m = I\vec a$, $\vec a = \int_{S} d\vec a$, the area itself for a flat loop.
- **The field:** $\vec B_{\text{dip}} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}[3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec m]/r^3 + \frac{2\mu_0}{3}\vec m\,\delta^3(\vec r)$; the delta-function term gives all of hydrogen's 21 cm splitting.
- **Ideal electric dipole:** $\pm q$ shrunk with $\vec p$ fixed gives $\rho_{\text{dip}} = -\vec p \cdot \nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_d)$.
- **Ideal magnetic dipole:** a square loop with $I = m/a^2$, or any distribution scaled as $\lambda^{-4}\vec J(\vec r/\lambda)$, tends to $\vec J = -\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_d)$; current conservation removes the term that would blow up.
- **Example:** a loop of 1 cm radius carrying 2 A ($m = 6.28 \times 10^{-4}$ A m²) shrunk a thousandfold needs $2 \times 10^{6}$ A, and its field 20 cm away, 15.7 nT, becomes a pure dipole field.
