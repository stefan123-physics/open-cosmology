## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Spheres and image charges (our own problem)" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Spheres and image charges". The problem below is our own, on the same subject.

A conducting sphere of radius $a$ has its centre at the origin. A point charge $Q$ sits at distance $R > a$ from the centre. If the sphere is grounded (held at $V = 0$), the potential outside it is that of $Q$ and one image charge $q$ inside the sphere, on the line from the centre to $Q$, at distance $d$ from the centre. That is the result of the [lesson on image charges](/courses/8-07/method-of-images-uniqueness):

<Formula label="The image charge for a grounded sphere" tex="q = -\frac{a}{R}\,Q, \qquad d = \frac{a^2}{R}" cite="reading3" at="p. 63 (PDF p. 6)" />

(The notes call the distance $r$ and the lesson calls it $b$. It is $d$ here, because $b$ is the radius of the cylinder in Problem 3.) In this problem the sphere is **not** grounded. It is insulated, and it carries no net charge.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Explain why one more image charge, placed at the centre of the sphere, solves the problem of the insulated, uncharged sphere. How large must it be? Then find the potential $V_0$ of the sphere in terms of $Q$, $R$, $a$ and $\varepsilon_0$.

<Hint>

A charge at the centre of a sphere changes the potential by the same amount at every point of the sphere's surface. So the surface stays an equipotential, whatever that charge is.

</Hint>

<Hint>

What fixes the size of the charge at the centre is Gauss's law. The total charge on the conductor equals the sum of the image charges inside it. What must that sum be here?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-v0" label="$V_0 =$" expr="Q/(4*pi*eps0*R)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 2], eps0: [0.5, 2], R: [1.5, 3], a: [0.5, 1] }}
  names={{ Q: "Q", eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", R: "R", a: "a" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q/(4*pi*eps0*a)", hint: "That is the potential of a sphere of radius a that carries the charge Q itself. Here the sphere carries no net charge; the charge that sets its potential is the image at the centre, which is smaller than Q." },
    { expr: "Q/(4*pi*eps0*(R-a))", hint: "That is the potential which Q alone makes at the nearest point of the sphere. The induced charge changes the potential on the surface, and makes it the same everywhere on the sphere." },
    { expr: "Q*a/(4*pi*eps0*R^2)", hint: "The image at the centre is aQ/R, and every point of the surface is at distance a from it, not R." },
    { expr: "0", hint: "Zero is the potential of the grounded sphere, which carries the net charge −aQ/R. An uncharged sphere near a positive charge sits at a positive potential." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Start from the grounded sphere and repair its total charge with a charge that does not disturb the equipotential.

<Step title="What the grounded solution gets wrong">

With $Q$ and the image $q$, the surface of the sphere is at $V = 0$. By Gauss's law, applied to a surface just outside the sphere, the charge on the sphere equals the image charge inside, $q = -aQ/R$. An insulated, uncharged sphere must carry zero net charge instead.

</Step>

<Step title="A second image at the centre">

Add a second image charge at the centre of the sphere. It lies inside the conductor, so Poisson's equation outside still has $Q$ as its only source. It is at the same distance $a$ from every point of the surface, so it raises the potential of the whole surface by the same amount: the surface is still an equipotential. The net charge on the sphere is now the sum of the two images, and this must vanish. So the image at the centre is $-q = +aQ/R$. By the uniqueness theorem (in the form with the total charge of each conductor given), this is the solution.

</Step>

<Step title="The potential of the sphere">

$Q$ and $q$ together give zero on the surface, so the potential of the sphere is that of the central image alone:

<Formula label="The potential of the uncharged sphere" tex="V_0 = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{aQ/R}{a} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

The radius $a$ has dropped out.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$V_0$ is exactly the potential that $Q$ alone makes at the centre of the sphere. That is no accident. The potential at the centre is the sum over all charges of charge divided by distance. The induced charge sits on the surface, all of it at distance $a$ from the centre, and it adds up to zero, so it gives nothing at the centre. What is left is $Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R)$, and the whole conductor is at the potential of its centre.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the magnitude of the force that pulls $Q$ towards the insulated, uncharged sphere. Write it as one fraction in terms of $Q$, $a$, $R$ and $\varepsilon_0$.

<Hint>

The force on $Q$ is the force that the two image charges exert on it, by Coulomb's law. The images are at different distances from $Q$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The image $q$ attracts $Q$ from the distance $R - d$. The image at the centre repels it from the distance $R$. Put the two terms over a common denominator.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-force" label="$|\vec F| =$" expr="Q^2*a^3*(2*R^2 - a^2)/(4*pi*eps0*R^3*(R^2 - a^2)^2)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 2], eps0: [0.5, 2], R: [1.5, 3], a: [0.5, 1] }}
  names={{ Q: "Q", eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", R: "R", a: "a" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q^2*a*R/(4*pi*eps0*(R^2 - a^2)^2)", hint: "That is the force for the grounded sphere, with the image q alone. The image at the centre has the same sign as Q and pushes it away, so the pull on Q is weaker here." },
    { expr: "Q^2*a*R/(4*pi*eps0*(R^2 - a^2)^2) + Q^2*a/(4*pi*eps0*R^3)", hint: "The two image forces were added. The image at the centre has the same sign as Q, so its force is a push: subtract it from the pull of q." },
    { expr: "Q^2*a/(4*pi*eps0*R^3)", hint: "That is the push of the image at the centre alone. The pull of the image q, which is closer to Q, is larger." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Outside the sphere the field is that of $Q$ and the two images, so the force on $Q$ is the Coulomb force of the two images.

<Step title="The two forces">

The image $q = -aQ/R$ is at distance $R - d = R - a^2/R = (R^2 - a^2)/R$ from $Q$ and attracts it. The image $+aQ/R$ at the centre is at distance $R$ and repels it. The net pull towards the sphere is

<Formula label="The pull as a difference of two image forces" tex="|\vec F| = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{aQ^2}{R}\left[\frac{R^2}{(R^2 - a^2)^2} - \frac{1}{R^2}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

</Step>

<Step title="One fraction">

The bracket is $[R^4 - (R^2 - a^2)^2]/[R^2(R^2 - a^2)^2]$, and $R^4 - (R^2 - a^2)^2 = a^2(2R^2 - a^2)$. So

<Formula label="The force on a charge near an uncharged conducting sphere" tex="|\vec F| = \frac{Q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{a^3\left(2R^2 - a^2\right)}{R^3\left(R^2 - a^2\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

The force is a pull for every $R > a$, because the attracting image is closer to $Q$ than the repelling one.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Far away, $R \gg a$, the force tends to $2Q^2a^3/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^5)$. This is what an induced dipole gives. The two images, $-aQ/R$ and $+aQ/R$ a distance $d = a^2/R$ apart, form a dipole of moment $(aQ/R)(a^2/R) = a^3Q/R^2$, which points along the line to $Q$. A dipole pulls a charge $Q$ on its axis with the force $2Q$ times the moment over $4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3$, which is $2Q^2a^3/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^5)$ again. The moment can be written as $4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3E$, where $E = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2)$ is the field of $Q$ at the centre: the sphere is polarised in proportion to the field it sits in and to its own volume.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

A metal sphere of radius 4.0 cm hangs from a silk thread. A point charge of 6.0 nC is held 10 cm from its centre. Take $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 8.988 \times 10^9\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$. Find the force on the charge, in micronewtons, when the sphere is uncharged and when it is grounded by a thin wire instead. Find also the potential of the uncharged sphere, in volts.

<Hint>

The grounded sphere has only the image $q$. For the uncharged sphere use part (b), or simply add the force of the image at the centre.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-fn" label="Force with the sphere uncharged, in µN:" value="5.40" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-fn" />

<Answer id="p1-fg" label="Force with the sphere grounded, in µN:" value="18.3" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-fg" />

<Answer id="p1-vnum" label="Potential of the uncharged sphere, in V:" value="539" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-vnum" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the two images, then use Coulomb's law twice.

<Step title="The images">

The image of the grounded sphere is $q = -(4.0/10) \times 6.0\ \text{nC} = -2.4\ \text{nC}$, at $d = 4.0^2/10\ \text{cm} = 1.6\ \text{cm}$ from the centre, so $8.4\ \text{cm}$ from the charge. For the uncharged sphere there is also $+2.4\ \text{nC}$ at the centre, 10 cm from the charge.

</Step>

<Step title="The forces">

The pull of $q$ is $8.988 \times 10^9 \times 6.0 \times 10^{-9} \times 2.4 \times 10^{-9}/0.084^2\ \text{N} = 18.3\ \text{µN}$. That is the whole force for the grounded sphere. The push of the central image is $8.988 \times 10^9 \times 6.0 \times 10^{-9} \times 2.4 \times 10^{-9}/0.10^2\ \text{N} = 12.9\ \text{µN}$. For the uncharged sphere the net pull is $18.3 - 12.9 = 5.40\ \text{µN}$ (with unrounded values).

</Step>

<Step title="The potential">

$V_0 = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R) = 8.988 \times 10^9 \times 6.0 \times 10^{-9}/0.10\ \text{V} = 539\ \text{V}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The formula of part (b) gives the same force: $a^3(2R^2 - a^2)/[R^3(R^2 - a^2)^2] = 16.69\ \text{m}^{-2}$ for these lengths, and $8.988 \times 10^9 \times (6.0 \times 10^{-9})^2 \times 16.69\ \text{N} = 5.40\ \text{µN}$. Grounding the sphere more than triples the pull, because the wire lets the charge $+2.4\ \text{nC}$ that was pushed to the far side leave the sphere.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Image charges with a plane and a hemispherical bulge" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

A conducting plate fills the $xy$ plane, except that the circular disk $x^2 + y^2 < a^2$ has been cut out of it and replaced by a conducting hemisphere of radius $a$ that bulges upwards:

<Formula label="The hemispherical bulge" tex="x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = a^2, \qquad z > 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, eq. (2.1), p. 1" />

Plate and bulge form one conductor, at $V = 0$. A point charge $q$ is placed on the $z$ axis at $(0, 0, z_0)$, with $z_0 > a$, straight above the top of the bulge.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Find a set of image charges that solves this problem: give the size and the place of each, and say why the set works. The first two boxes ask for the image that lies inside the bulge (at a height between 0 and $a$). The third box is our addition: what is the total charge induced on the conductor, plate and bulge together?

<Hint>

You know the image for a plane alone and the image for a sphere alone. Put in the first. It makes the plane right and the sphere wrong. Put in the second. What does it do to the plane?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Every image that repairs the sphere spoils the plane, unless the whole set of charges is antisymmetric under $z \to -z$: for each charge at height $z$ an opposite charge at $-z$. Check then that the sphere is still at zero potential.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-qimg" label="Charge of the image inside the bulge:" expr="-q*a/z0"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 1], z0: [1.5, 3] }}
  names={{ q: "q", a: "a", z0: "z_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "q*a/z0", hint: "The sign is reversed. The image inside the bulge must cancel the potential of q on the sphere, so it has the opposite sign to q. The image +qa/z₀ lies below the plane." },
    { expr: "-q", hint: "That is the image for the plane, and it sits at depth z₀ below the plane, not inside the bulge. The image for a sphere is smaller than the charge." },
    { expr: "-q*a^2/z0^2", hint: "The factor is the ratio a/z₀ to the first power. It is the image's distance from the centre that goes as a², and it is a²/z₀." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-zimg" label="Its height above the plane:" expr="a^2/z0"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 1], z0: [1.5, 3] }}
  names={{ a: "a", z0: "z_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "a/z0", hint: "This is a pure number, not a length. The ratio a/z₀ is the factor in the image's charge; its distance from the centre is a times that ratio." },
    { expr: "z0 - a", hint: "For z₀ > 2a this height is larger than a: the image would sit outside the conductor, in the region where the only charge is q." }

<Answer id="p2-total" label="Total charge induced on the conductor:" choices={["−q(1 + a/z₀)", "−q(1 − a/z₀)", "−q exactly", "−qa/z₀"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Build the set in steps, using the two image problems of the notes, until both surfaces are at zero potential and no image lies in the region above the conductor.

<Step title="The plane alone">

For a flat plate the image of $q$ is $-q$ at $(0, 0, -z_0)$. Every point of the plane $z = 0$ is equally far from the two, so the plane is at $V = 0$. The sphere $r = a$ is not.

</Step>

<Step title="The sphere alone">

For a sphere of radius $a$ centred at the origin, the image of $q$ at distance $z_0$ is $-qa/z_0$ at distance $a^2/z_0$ from the centre, on the same side: at $(0, 0, a^2/z_0)$. Since $z_0 > a$, this height is less than $a$, so the image is inside the bulge. The pair $q$, $-qa/z_0$ gives $V = 0$ on the whole sphere $r = a$.

</Step>

<Step title="Making both right">

The image $-q$ at $-z_0$ needs a sphere image of its own: $+qa/z_0$ at $(0, 0, -a^2/z_0)$. So there are three images:

- $-q$ at $(0, 0, -z_0)$,
- $-qa/z_0$ at $(0, 0, a^2/z_0)$,
- $+qa/z_0$ at $(0, 0, -a^2/z_0)$.

On the sphere, the pair $q$ and $-qa/z_0$ gives zero, and so does the pair $-q$ and $+qa/z_0$, which is the same pair mirrored with both signs reversed. On the plane, $q$ and $-q$ are mirror charges of opposite sign, and so are $-qa/z_0$ and $+qa/z_0$, so each pair gives zero there. Hence $V = 0$ on the whole plane and on the whole sphere, and in particular on the conductor. All three images lie outside the region above the conductor (two are below the plane, one is inside the bulge), so in that region the only charge is $q$. The potential vanishes far away. By the uniqueness theorem the four charges give the right potential above the conductor.

</Step>

<Step title="The induced charge">

Take a closed surface made of a very large hemisphere above the plate and a flat piece just inside the metal. The field of the four charges together has no monopole part, since the charges add up to zero, so it falls off faster than $1/r^2$ and its flux through the large hemisphere vanishes. Inside the metal the field is zero. So the total charge enclosed is zero: the induced charge is $-q$. It is also the sum of the three images, $-q - qa/z_0 + qa/z_0 = -q$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Let the bulge shrink, $a \to 0$: the two new images become small and meet at the origin, and only the image $-q$ of the flat plate is left. Every field line that starts on $q$ ends on the conductor, which is why the induced charge is $-q$ whatever the size of the bulge.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Show that the charge is attracted towards the plate with the force

<Formula label="The force on the charge above the bulge" tex="|\vec F| = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{q^2}{4z_0^2} + \frac{4q^2a^3z_0^3}{\left(z_0^4 - a^4\right)^2}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, eq. (2.2), p. 1" />

(MIT's sheet writes $z$ for $z_0$ in this formula.) The number box is our addition. For $z_0 = 2a$, by what factor is the force larger than it would be for a flat plate without the bulge?

<Hint>

The force on $q$ is the sum of the Coulomb forces of the three images. All of them lie on the $z$ axis, so only the signs and the distances matter.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The two images that belong to the bulge have opposite signs and nearly cancel. Write their two terms with the denominators $(z_0^2 - a^2)^2$ and $(z_0^2 + a^2)^2$ and combine them.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ratio" label="Force with the bulge divided by force without it, for $z_0 = 2a$:" value="3.28" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-ratio" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Add the forces of the three images on $q$, counting a pull towards the plate as positive.

<Step title="The three terms">

The image $-q$ is at distance $2z_0$ and pulls with $q^2/(2z_0)^2$ (in units of $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0$). The image $-qa/z_0$ is at distance $z_0 - a^2/z_0 = (z_0^2 - a^2)/z_0$ and pulls. The image $+qa/z_0$ is at distance $z_0 + a^2/z_0 = (z_0^2 + a^2)/z_0$ and pushes. So

<Formula label="The sum of the three image forces" tex="|\vec F| = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{4z_0^2} + \frac{a\,z_0}{\left(z_0^2 - a^2\right)^2} - \frac{a\,z_0}{\left(z_0^2 + a^2\right)^2}\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Combining the last two">

The difference of the two fractions is

<Formula label="The two bulge terms combined" tex="a\,z_0\,\frac{\left(z_0^2 + a^2\right)^2 - \left(z_0^2 - a^2\right)^2}{\left(z_0^2 - a^2\right)^2\left(z_0^2 + a^2\right)^2} = \frac{4a^3z_0^3}{\left(z_0^4 - a^4\right)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

because the numerator is $4z_0^2a^2$ and the product of the two denominators is $(z_0^4 - a^4)^2$. This gives MIT's formula. Both terms are positive: the force is a pull, and the bulge makes it stronger.

</Step>

<Step title="The factor for a charge at twice the radius">

Dividing by the flat-plate force $q^2/(16\pi\varepsilon_0z_0^2)$, the factor is $1 + 16a^3z_0^5/(z_0^4 - a^4)^2$. With $z_0 = 2a$ this is $1 + 16 \times 32/15^2 = 1 + 512/225 = 3.28$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Bring the charge close to the top of the bulge, $z_0 = a + h$ with $h \ll a$. Then $z_0^4 - a^4 \approx 4a^3h$ and the second term tends to $4q^2a^6/(16a^6h^2) = q^2/(2h)^2$: the force of a single image $-q$ at distance $2h$. Seen from very close, the top of the bulge is a flat plate, as it should be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

This part is our addition. Far above the plate, $z_0 \gg a$, the first term of the force is the flat-plate result and the second is a small correction due to the bulge. Find the leading form of that correction, and explain its dependence on $a$ and $z_0$ with an induced dipole.

<Hint>

For $z_0 \gg a$, the $a^4$ in the denominator can be dropped.

</Hint>

<Hint>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Images for a conducting cylinder" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

A line charge $\lambda$ alone has the potential $-(\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln s$ plus a constant, where $s$ is the distance from the line (see the [lesson on the integral form of electrostatics](/courses/8-07/integral-form-electrostatics)).

<Part label="a" points={5}>

</Hint>

**The idea.** Find the image that makes the cylinder an equipotential, then use the behaviour far away to decide whether anything else may be added.

<Step title="The two distances">

Put the axis of the cylinder along the $z$ axis and the real line at $x = R$, $y = 0$. Try an image line with $\lambda'$ per unit length at $x = d$, $y = 0$, with $d < b$. A point of the surface at the angle $\phi$ from the $x$ axis is at the distances

<Formula label="Distances from a point of the surface to the two lines" tex="s_1^2 = b^2 + R^2 - 2bR\cos\phi, \qquad s_2^2 = b^2 + d^2 - 2bd\cos\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

from the real line and from the image.

</Step>

<Step title="The image">

The potential on the surface is $-(\lambda\ln s_1 + \lambda'\ln s_2)/(2\pi\varepsilon_0)$ plus a constant. Both $s_1$ and $s_2$ change with $\phi$. With $\lambda' = -\lambda$ the potential depends only on the ratio $s_2/s_1$, and it is constant if that ratio is. Choose $d = b^2/R$. Then

<Formula label="The ratio of the distances is constant on the cylinder" tex="s_2^2 = b^2 + \frac{b^4}{R^2} - \frac{2b^3}{R}\cos\phi = \frac{b^2}{R^2}\left(R^2 + b^2 - 2bR\cos\phi\right) = \frac{b^2}{R^2}\,s_1^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

so $s_2/s_1 = b/R$ at every point of the surface. The image is a line with $-\lambda$ per unit length at distance $d = b^2/R$ from the axis, between the axis and the real line. The place is the same as for a sphere, but the size is not: for a sphere the image charge is reduced by the factor $a/R$, for a cylinder it is the full $-\lambda$. (No other $\lambda'$ works: differentiating $\lambda\ln s_1 + \lambda'\ln s_2$ with respect to $\cos\phi$ and asking for zero at every angle forces $\lambda' = -\lambda$ and $d = b^2/R$, or the useless $d = R$.)

</Step>

<Step title="Nothing on the axis">

A line on the axis would keep the surface an equipotential, just as the charge at the centre of the sphere did in Problem 1. The potential can vanish far away only if the sum is zero. The real line and the image $-\lambda$ already add up to zero, so there is no further image. In other words, the condition that $V \to 0$ far away fixes the charge on the cylinder at $-\lambda$ per unit length, and with it the voltage, found in part (b).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Let the line come close to the surface, $R = b + h$ with $h \ll b$. Then $d = b^2/(b + h) \approx b - h$: the image $-\lambda$ lies as far behind the surface as the real line is in front, which is the image for a flat plate.

</Step>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Capacitance of a single conductor" points={20} mit="Problem 4">

A single conductor carries the charge $Q$ and is at the potential $V_0$, with $V = 0$ far away. Its capacitance $C$ is defined by

<Formula label="Capacitance of a single conductor" tex="Q = C\,V_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 2" />

This is the definition of the [lecture notes](/courses/8-07/conductor-surface-forces-capacitance) (their eq. (4.18)) with the second conductor moved far away. Throughout this problem, $\mathcal V$ is the space outside the conductor and $V(\vec r)$ is the true potential in $\mathcal V$ when the conductor is held at $V_0$: it satisfies Laplace's equation in $\mathcal V$, equals $V_0$ on the conductor and falls to zero far away.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Show that

<Formula label="Capacitance as an integral over the field" tex="C = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{V_0^2}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\left|\nabla V\right|^2 d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), eq. (4.1), p. 2" />

The box is our addition. Use the formula to compute the capacitance of a conducting sphere of radius $R$, for which $V = V_0R/r$ outside.

<Hint>

There are two routes. One uses the energy: write the energy of the charged conductor once with its charge and potential and once as an integral over the field. The other is direct: write $|\nabla V|^2$ as a divergence minus a term that vanishes by Laplace's equation.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the direct route, $\nabla\cdot(V\nabla V) = |\nabla V|^2 + V\,\nabla^2V$. The region $\mathcal V$ has two boundaries: the surface of the conductor and a sphere far away. On the conductor $V$ is a constant and can be taken out of the surface integral.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-sphere" label="$C$ of a sphere of radius $R$:" expr="4*pi*eps0*R"
  vars={{ eps0: [0.5, 2], R: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", R: "R" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "4*pi*eps0*R^2", hint: "The integrand |∇V|² is V₀²R²/r⁴, and the volume element is 4πr² dr. The integral of dr/r² from R outwards is 1/R, which leaves one power of R." },
    { expr: "4*pi*eps0/R", hint: "A larger sphere holds more charge at the same potential, so C must grow with R. Check the factor R² that comes from squaring the gradient of V₀R/r." },
    { expr: "2*pi*eps0*R", hint: "The formula has ε₀, not ε₀/2. The factor ½ of the field energy is cancelled by the ½ in W = ½CV₀²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The energy of the charged conductor can be written with $C$ or as an integral over the field outside. Comparing the two gives the formula.

<Step title="The energy route">

The energy of a charge distribution is $W = \frac12\int\rho V\,d^3x$. All the charge sits on the conductor, where $V = V_0$, so

<Formula label="Energy of the charged conductor" tex="W = \frac12\,Q\,V_0 = \frac12\,C\,V_0^2" cite="reading2" at="eq. (4.5), p. 2 (there for a sphere)" />

The same energy is stored in the field:

<Formula label="Energy stored in the field" tex="W = \frac12\,\varepsilon_0\int_{\text{all space}}|\vec E|^2\,d^3x = \frac12\,\varepsilon_0\int_{\mathcal{V}}\left|\nabla V\right|^2 d^3x" cite="reading1" at="p. 7 (PDF p. 7)" />

The second step uses $\vec E = -\nabla V$ and the fact that the field vanishes inside the conductor. Setting the two expressions equal and dividing by $\frac12V_0^2$ gives the formula.

</Step>

<Step title="The direct route">

Since $\nabla^2V = 0$ in $\mathcal V$, $|\nabla V|^2 = \nabla\cdot(V\nabla V)$. The divergence theorem turns the integral into surface integrals over the boundaries of $\mathcal V$. On a sphere of large radius $r$, $V$ falls as $1/r$ and $\nabla V$ as $1/r^2$ while the area grows as $r^2$, so that part vanishes. On the conductor, the outward normal of $\mathcal V$ points into the metal; with $\hat n$ the normal that points out of the metal,

<Formula label="The integral reduced to the conductor's surface" tex="\int_{\mathcal{V}}\left|\nabla V\right|^2 d^3x = -V_0\oint_S\nabla V\cdot\hat n\,da = V_0\oint_S\vec E\cdot\hat n\,da = \frac{V_0\,Q}{\varepsilon_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

by Gauss's law. Multiplying by $\varepsilon_0/V_0^2$ gives $Q/V_0 = C$.

</Step>

<Step title="The sphere">

With $V = V_0R/r$, $|\nabla V|^2 = V_0^2R^2/r^4$ and

<Formula label="The formula applied to a sphere" tex="C = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{V_0^2}\int_R^\infty\frac{V_0^2R^2}{r^4}\,4\pi r^2\,dr = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2\int_R^\infty\frac{dr}{r^2} = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 2 (our own addition)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Directly: a sphere with the charge $Q$ has the potential $V_0 = Q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R)$, so $Q/V_0 = 4\pi\varepsilon_0R$, the same.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The true potential makes the integral as small as it can be. Any difference from it adds a positive term.

<Step title="Split the trial function">

<Formula label="The integral for the trial function, expanded" tex="\int_{\mathcal{V}}\left|\nabla\Psi\right|^2 d^3x = \int_{\mathcal{V}}\left|\nabla V\right|^2 d^3x + 2\int_{\mathcal{V}}\nabla V\cdot\nabla u\,d^3x + \int_{\mathcal{V}}\left|\nabla u\right|^2 d^3x" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Because $\nabla^2V = 0$, $\nabla V\cdot\nabla u = \nabla\cdot(u\nabla V)$. By the divergence theorem its integral is a surface integral of $u\,\nabla V$ over the boundaries of $\mathcal V$. On the conductor $u = 0$. Far away $u \to 0$ while the flux of $\nabla V$ through a large sphere stays finite (it is $-Q/\varepsilon_0$), so that part vanishes too. Here the true $V$ is needed: for any other function the step $\nabla V\cdot\nabla u = \nabla\cdot(u\nabla V)$ fails.

<Step title="The inequality">

So

<Formula label="The trial value is never below the true capacitance" tex="C[\Psi] = C + \frac{\varepsilon_0}{V_0^2}\int_{\mathcal{V}}\left|\nabla u\right|^2 d^3x \;\ge\; C" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The two are equal only if $\nabla u = 0$ everywhere, which with $u = 0$ on the conductor means $u = 0$: the trial function is then the true potential.

For $\Psi = V_0(R/r)^n$, $|\nabla\Psi|^2 = V_0^2n^2R^{2n}/r^{2n+2}$ and

The true potential of the larger conductor is defined only outside $S$. The trial function for the smaller conductor needs values in the gap between $S'$ and $S$ as well. Which choice there adds nothing to the integral and keeps the function continuous?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-trialc" label="The trial function to use:" choices={["the true potential of the smaller conductor in the gap between S′ and S, and zero outside S", "the constant V₀ everywhere outside S′, in the gap and beyond S alike", "the constant V₀ in the gap between S′ and S, and the true potential of the larger conductor outside S", "the true potential of the larger conductor outside S, multiplied by C′/C, and V₀ in the gap"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Fill the gap between the two surfaces with a constant, where it costs nothing, and use the larger conductor's own potential outside.

<Step title="The trial function">

Let $V$ be the true potential of the larger conductor at $V_0$, defined outside $S$. For the smaller conductor take

<Formula label="A trial function for the smaller conductor" tex="\Psi(\vec r) = \begin{cases} V_0 & \text{between } S' \text{ and } S \\ V(\vec r) & \text{outside } S \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

It equals $V_0$ on $S'$, it vanishes far away, and it is continuous across $S$, where $V = V_0$. So it is an allowed trial function for the smaller conductor. (A jump would not be allowed: the gradient would be infinite there.)

</Step>

<Step title="Its integral">

In the gap $\nabla\Psi = 0$, so only the region outside $S$ contributes, and there the integral is that of part (a) for the larger conductor:

<Formula label="The trial value equals the larger capacitance" tex="C'[\Psi] = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{V_0^2}\int_{\text{outside } S}\left|\nabla V\right|^2 d^3x = C" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Part (b), applied to the smaller conductor, gives $C' \le C'[\Psi] = C$.

</Step>

<Step title="Why the inequality is strict">

Equality would need $\Psi$ to be the true potential of the smaller conductor, a solution of Laplace's equation everywhere outside $S'$. But $\Psi$ is constant in the gap. A solution of Laplace's equation that is constant in some region of space is constant in all the space connected to it (a standard property, quoted here without proof), and a constant $V_0 \neq 0$ cannot fall to zero far away. So, as long as there is a gap somewhere between the two surfaces, $C' < C$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Two concentric spheres: the capacitance of each is $4\pi\varepsilon_0$ times its radius, so the inner one has the smaller capacitance. The result also says that adding metal to a conductor anywhere on its outside can only raise its capacitance.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Use part (c) to find upper and lower limits for the capacitance of a conducting cube of side $a$. Write them as

<Formula label="Limits for the capacitance of a cube" tex="\alpha\left(4\pi\varepsilon_0a\right) < C_{\text{cube}} < \beta\left(4\pi\varepsilon_0a\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(d), p. 2" />

and find the constants $\alpha$ and $\beta$. MIT's sheet quotes a numerical calculation (by Hwang and Mascagni, 2004) that gives $C_{\text{cube}} \approx 0.661\,(4\pi\varepsilon_0a)$. Compare it with your limits.

The last box is our addition. With the numerical value, what is the capacitance of a metal cube of side 10 cm, in picofarads? Use $4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 1.1127 \times 10^{-10}\ \text{F/m}$.

<Hint>

You know the capacitance of one shape exactly. Which is the largest one of that shape that fits inside the cube, and the smallest one that holds the cube?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p4-alpha" label="$\alpha$, as a decimal:" value="0.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-alpha" />

<Answer id="p4-beta" label="$\beta =$" value="0.866" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-beta" />

<Answer id="p4-pf" label="$C$ of a cube of side 10 cm, in pF:" value="7.35" tolerance="1%" calc="p4-pf" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Squeeze the cube between two spheres, whose capacitance is $4\pi\varepsilon_0$ times the radius.

<Step title="The sphere inside">

The largest sphere inside the cube touches the middles of the six faces. Its radius is $a/2$, so $C_{\text{cube}} > 4\pi\varepsilon_0(a/2)$: $\alpha = \frac12$.

</Step>

<Step title="The sphere outside">

The smallest sphere that holds the cube passes through its eight corners. Its radius is half the space diagonal, $\frac12\sqrt3\,a$, so $C_{\text{cube}} < 4\pi\varepsilon_0\left(\frac12\sqrt3\,a\right)$: $\beta = \frac12\sqrt3 = 0.866$.

</Step>

<Step title="The comparison">

The numerical value $0.661$ lies between $0.5$ and $0.866$, as it must, a little below the middle of the interval ($0.683$). The limits pin the capacitance down fairly well without any calculation of the field.

</Step>

<Step title="The cube of 10 cm">

$4\pi\varepsilon_0a = 1.1127 \times 10^{-10} \times 0.10\ \text{F} = 11.13\ \text{pF}$, and $0.661 \times 11.13\ \text{pF} = 7.35\ \text{pF}$. The two limits are $5.56\ \text{pF}$ and $9.64\ \text{pF}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A sphere with the same volume as the cube has the radius $(3/4\pi)^{1/3}a = 0.620\,a$. That is no limit in either direction, but it is a sensible guess, and the true $0.661$ is close to it and above it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Laplace's equation in a box (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 5">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Laplace's equation in a box". The problem below is our own, on the same subject. It uses [separation of variables](/courses/8-07/separation-of-variables-cartesian) in Cartesian coordinates.

A hollow rectangular metal box fills $0 \le x \le a$, $0 \le y \le b$, $0 \le z \le c$. There is no charge inside, so the potential inside satisfies Laplace's equation,

<Formula label="Laplace's equation in Cartesian coordinates" tex="\frac{\partial^2V}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2V}{\partial z^2} = 0" cite="reading3" at="p. 64 (PDF p. 8)" />

The bottom and the four side walls are grounded, $V = 0$. The lid, at $z = c$, is insulated from the walls and held at a potential that is given in each part.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

The lid is held at the potential

<Formula label="The potential on the lid in part (a)" tex="V(x, y, c) = V_0\,\sin\frac{\pi x}{a}\,\sin\frac{\pi y}{b}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

which vanishes where the lid meets the walls. The potential inside has the form $V = V_0\,\sin(\pi x/a)\,\sin(\pi y/b)\,Z(z)$. Find the constant $\gamma$ (positive, with the unit of 1/length) that appears in $Z(z)$, in terms of $a$ and $b$. Then find $Z(z)$ in terms of $\gamma$, $z$ and $c$.

<Hint>

Put the product form into Laplace's equation. The two sines are reproduced by their second derivatives, each with a negative constant. What equation is left for $Z$?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The equation $d^2Z/dz^2 = \gamma^2Z$ has the solutions $e^{\gamma z}$ and $e^{-\gamma z}$, or just as well $\sinh\gamma z$ and $\cosh\gamma z$. Two conditions fix the combination: $Z = 0$ on the bottom and $Z = 1$ on the lid.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-gamma" label="$\gamma =$" expr="pi*sqrt(1/a^2 + 1/b^2)"
  vars={{ a: [0.5, 1], b: [1.2, 2] }}
  names={{ a: "a", b: "b" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "pi*(1/a + 1/b)", hint: "The separation constants add, not their square roots: γ² is the sum of (π/a)² and (π/b)²." },
    { expr: "pi*sqrt(1/a^2 - 1/b^2)", hint: "Both sines give a negative constant, −(π/a)² and −(π/b)², so the constant for Z is the sum of the two squares, not their difference." },
    { expr: "pi^2*(1/a^2 + 1/b^2)", hint: "That is γ², the constant in Z″ = γ²Z. Take the square root." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p5-z" label="$Z(z) =$" expr="sinh(g*z)/sinh(g*c)"
  vars={{ g: [0.5, 2], z: [0.2, 1], c: [1, 2] }}
  names={{ g: "\\gamma", z: "z", c: "c" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "exp(g*(z - c))", hint: "This is 1 on the lid, but it is not zero on the grounded bottom, z = 0. A second exponential is needed to cancel it there." },
    { expr: "cosh(g*z)/cosh(g*c)", hint: "This is 1 on the lid, but on the bottom it is 1/cosh(γc), not zero. The combination that vanishes at z = 0 is the hyperbolic sine." },
    { expr: "sin(g*z)/sin(g*c)", hint: "With sines in both x and y, the constant left for Z is positive, Z″ = +γ²Z. Its solutions grow and decay; they do not oscillate." },
    { expr: "sinh(g*z)", hint: "This vanishes on the bottom, but on the lid it is sinh(γc), not 1. Divide by that constant." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The potential on the lid is a single product of sines that already fits the walls, so a single product solves the problem.

<Step title="Separate">

With $V = V_0\,\sin(\pi x/a)\,\sin(\pi y/b)\,Z(z)$, the $x$ derivative gives the factor $-(\pi/a)^2$ and the $y$ derivative the factor $-(\pi/b)^2$. Laplace's equation becomes

<Formula label="The equation for Z" tex="\frac{d^2Z}{dz^2} = \gamma^2Z, \qquad \gamma = \pi\sqrt{\frac{1}{a^2} + \frac{1}{b^2}}" cite="reading3" at="p. 65 (PDF p. 9), with our constants" />

This is the rule of the notes that the three separation constants add up to zero: two are negative, so the third is positive.

</Step>

<Step title="Fit the bottom and the lid">

The solution that vanishes at $z = 0$ is $\sinh\gamma z$, and dividing by its value on the lid makes $Z(c) = 1$:

<Formula label="The potential in the box for one mode on the lid" tex="V(x, y, z) = V_0\,\sin\frac{\pi x}{a}\,\sin\frac{\pi y}{b}\,\frac{\sinh\gamma z}{\sinh\gamma c}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

The side walls are at zero because of the sines, the bottom because of the hyperbolic sine, and the lid has the given potential. By the uniqueness theorem this is the potential.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Let $b \to \infty$ at fixed $y/b$: the box becomes a long channel, $\gamma \to \pi/a$, and $\sin(\pi x/a)\sinh(\pi z/a)$ satisfies the two-dimensional Laplace equation, since $-(\pi/a)^2 + (\pi/a)^2 = 0$. Also, for a deep box ($\gamma c \gg 1$) the potential near the lid is $e^{-\gamma(c - z)}$ times its value on the lid: it dies out over a depth $1/\gamma$, which is set by the smaller of the two sides.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Let the box of part (a) be a cube, $a = b = c$. Find the potential at the centre of the cube, as a fraction of $V_0$.

<Hint>

At the centre both sines are 1. For the ratio of the two hyperbolic sines, $\sinh 2u = 2\sinh u\cosh u$ saves work.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-centre" label="$V(\text{centre})/V_0 =$" value="0.107" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-centre" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the centre into the result of part (a).

<Step title="The value">

For the cube $\gamma = \pi\sqrt2/a$, so $\gamma c = \pi\sqrt2 = 4.443$ and at the centre $\gamma z = 2.221$. Both sines are 1, and

<Formula label="The potential at the centre of the cube, one mode on the lid" tex="\frac{V(\text{centre})}{V_0} = \frac{\sinh\left(\pi/\sqrt2\right)}{\sinh\left(\pi\sqrt2\right)} = \frac{1}{2\cosh\left(\pi/\sqrt2\right)} = \frac{1}{2\times 4.665} = 0.107" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The value is well below $\frac12$, although the centre is halfway up. The potential does not fall linearly from the lid to the bottom: the grounded side walls pull it down, and it decays roughly exponentially with depth.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Now the lid of the box (sides $a$, $b$, $c$ again) is held at the same potential $V_0$ everywhere. The potential inside is a double sum over modes like that of part (a),

<Formula label="The series for a lid at constant potential" tex="V(x, y, z) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}c_{nm}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,\sin\frac{m\pi y}{b}\,\frac{\sinh\gamma_{nm}z}{\sinh\gamma_{nm}c}, \qquad \gamma_{nm} = \pi\sqrt{\frac{n^2}{a^2} + \frac{m^2}{b^2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

Find the coefficients $c_{nm}$. Show that they vanish unless $n$ and $m$ are both odd, and give $c_{nm}/V_0$ for odd $n$ and odd $m$.

<Hint>

On the lid the last factor is 1, and the series must add up to $V_0$ for all $x$ and $y$ inside the lid. That is a double Fourier sine series for a constant.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Multiply both sides by one of the products of sines, with its two indices fixed, and integrate over the lid. The sines are orthogonal: only the term of the series with the same two indices survives, with the factor $ab/4$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-cnm" label="$c_{nm}/V_0$ for odd $n$ and $m$:" expr="16/(pi^2*n*m)"
  vars={{ n: [1, 7], m: [1, 7] }}
  names={{ n: "n", m: "m" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "4/(pi^2*n*m)", hint: "Each of the two integrals of a sine over its full range gives 2 divided by its wave number for an odd index, not 1. Together with the factor 4/(ab) that makes 16." },
    { expr: "16/(pi^2*n^2*m^2)", hint: "The integral of a sine brings one factor 1/n, not 1/n². Coefficients that fall off as 1/(nm) are typical of a function with a jump, and the lid's potential jumps to zero at the walls." },
    { expr: "4/(pi*n*m)", hint: "There are two integrals, one over x and one over y, and each brings a factor 1/π." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each term of the series is a solution like that of part (a), so only the lid is left to fit, by Fourier's method.

<Step title="The condition on the lid">

At $z = c$ the series must equal $V_0$:

<Formula label="The lid's potential as a double sine series" tex="V_0 = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}c_{nm}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,\sin\frac{m\pi y}{b}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Project out one coefficient">

Multiplying by $\sin(n\pi x/a)\,\sin(m\pi y/b)$ and integrating over the lid, with $\int_0^a\sin^2(n\pi x/a)\,dx = a/2$, gives

<Formula label="The coefficients as integrals" tex="c_{nm} = \frac{4V_0}{ab}\int_0^a\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,dx\int_0^b\sin\frac{m\pi y}{b}\,dy" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The integrals">

$\int_0^a\sin(n\pi x/a)\,dx = (a/n\pi)(1 - \cos n\pi)$, which is $2a/(n\pi)$ for odd $n$ and zero for even $n$. The same holds for $m$. So

<Formula label="The coefficients for a lid at constant potential" tex="c_{nm} = \frac{16\,V_0}{\pi^2\,n\,m} \quad (n \text{ and } m \text{ both odd}), \qquad c_{nm} = 0 \quad \text{otherwise}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The even modes are odd under reflection about the middle of the lid ($x \to a - x$ or $y \to b - y$), and a constant is even, so they cannot appear. A number check at the middle of the lid, where every sine is $\pm1$: the sum over odd $n$ of $(4/\pi n)\sin(n\pi/2)$ is $(4/\pi)(1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \dots) = (4/\pi)(\pi/4) = 1$, and the double sum is the product of two such sums, so the series gives $V_0$ there.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={3}>

Let the box of part (c) be a cube again, with its lid at $V_0$ and the other five faces grounded. Find the potential at the centre of the cube, as a fraction of $V_0$, exactly and without summing the series. Then check your answer against the first term of the series.

<Hint>

Think of six problems, each with a different face of the cube at $V_0$ and the other five grounded. What is the sum of the six potentials, and what does symmetry say about their values at the centre?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-sixth" label="$V(\text{centre})/V_0$, as a decimal:" value="0.1667" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-sixth" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Laplace's equation is linear, so solutions can be added, and the centre of a cube is placed the same way with respect to all six faces.

<Step title="Superposition">

Add the six solutions that each have one face at $V_0$. The sum satisfies Laplace's equation and equals $V_0$ on every face. The constant $V_0$ does that too, so by uniqueness the sum is $V_0$ everywhere inside. At the centre the six solutions have the same value, because a rotation of the cube turns any one of the six problems into any other and leaves the centre where it is. So each gives a sixth:

<Formula label="The potential at the centre of the cube, lid at constant potential" tex="\frac{V(\text{centre})}{V_0} = \frac16 = 0.1667" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The first term of the series, $n = m = 1$, is $(16/\pi^2)$ times the value of part (b): $1.621 \times 0.1072 = 0.1738$. The terms with $(n, m) = (1, 3)$ and $(3, 1)$ are negative, because $\sin(3\pi/2) = -1$, and give $-0.0038$ each: the sum so far is $0.1662$. The term $(3, 3)$ adds $0.0002$, the terms $(1, 5)$ and $(5, 1)$ together add another $0.0002$, and the series settles at $0.1667$. The first term alone is right to about 4%, since the higher modes die out quickly with depth.

</Step>

</Solution>
