## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `mu0*I/(2*pi*r)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `pi` stands for $\pi$, `mu0` for $\mu_0$, `eps0` for $\varepsilon_0$, `th` for $\theta$, `B0` for $B_0$, `I2` for $I_2$, `me` for $m_e$, and `log` is the natural logarithm; the other letters (`I`, `N`, `a`, `b`, `s`, `v`, `z`, `R`, `B`, `e`, `w`, `h`, `r`, `V`, `L`) are typed as they are.

MIT gives the points per problem: 15 each for Problems 1, 2 and 3, 10 for Problem 4, 15 each for Problems 5 and 6 and 20 for Problem 7, 105 points in all, plus 30 points of extra credit: 10 for Problem 3(d) and 20 for Problem 8, which are optional here. MIT gives 15 points to parts (a) to (c) of Problem 3 together and 10 to part (d); every other split between parts is ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes. $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ are the electric and magnetic fields, $\mu_0$ the magnetic constant and $\varepsilon_0$ the electric constant, with $\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1/c^2$. $\Phi$ is a magnetic flux, $\int\vec B \cdot d\vec a$ over a surface, and $\mathcal{E}$ an emf, the line integral of the force per unit charge around a circuit. In cylindrical coordinates $s$ is the distance from the axis and $\hat s$, $\hat\phi$, $\hat z$ are the unit vectors; in spherical coordinates $\theta$ is the angle from the $z$ axis. $\delta(x)$ is the delta function in one dimension and $\theta(x)$, in Problem 8 only, the step function.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A coil moving in the field of a long wire (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Moving coil in the field of a wire". The problem below is our own, on the same subject.

A long straight wire carries a steady current $I$. A flat rectangular coil of $N$ turns lies in a plane through the wire. Its sides of length $a$ are parallel to the wire, its sides of length $b$ point away from it, and its nearer side is at the distance $s$ from the wire. The coil moves straight away from the wire, in its own plane, at the constant speed $v$, so $s$ grows. The field of the wire at the distance $r$ is

<Formula label="The field of a long straight wire" tex="B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}" cite="mit802-ch10" at="Eq. (10.9.2), p. 17" />

directed around the wire, and the emf of a coil whose $N$ turns each enclose the flux $\Phi$ is

<Formula label="Faraday's law for a coil" tex="\mathcal{E} = -N\,\frac{d\Phi}{dt}" cite="mit802-ch10" at="Eq. (10.1.4), p. 2" />

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Find the flux $\Phi$ through one turn of the coil when its nearer side is at the distance $s$.

<Hint>

The field is the same along a line parallel to the wire, but it changes across the coil. Cut the coil into thin strips parallel to the wire.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A strip at the distance $r$, of width $dr$, has the area $a\,dr$ and the field $\mu_0 I/2\pi r$, at right angles to the coil. Add the strips from $r = s$ to $r = s + b$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-flux" label="$\Phi =$" expr="mu0*I*a/(2*pi)*log((s + b)/s)"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], I: [1, 3], a: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2], s: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", I: "I", a: "a", b: "b", s: "s" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*I*a*b/(2*pi*s)", hint: "That takes the field at the nearer side, μ₀I/2πs, as if it filled the whole coil. The field falls off as 1/r across the coil, so integrate over its width." },
    { expr: "mu0*I*a/(2*pi)*log(b/s)", hint: "The coil reaches from r = s to r = s + b, not from s to b: check the upper limit of the integral." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The field is perpendicular to the coil everywhere and depends only on the distance from the wire, so the flux is a one-dimensional integral across the coil.

<Step title="Add the strips">

<Formula label="The flux through one turn" tex="\Phi = \int_s^{s+b}\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\,a\,dr = \frac{\mu_0 I a}{2\pi}\,\ln\frac{s + b}{s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a narrow coil, $b \ll s$, $\ln(1 + b/s) \approx b/s$ and $\Phi \approx (\mu_0 I/2\pi s)\,ab$: the field at the coil times its area, as it should be when the field hardly changes across it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Find the size of the emf induced in the coil while it moves away at the speed $v$, for the position $s$.

<Hint>

The flux depends on time only through $s$, and $ds/dt = v$. Use the chain rule.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Differentiate $\ln(s + b) - \ln s$ with respect to $s$ and bring the two terms over one denominator. Do not forget the $N$ turns.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-emf" label="$|\mathcal{E}| =$" expr="N*mu0*I*a*b*v/(2*pi*s*(s + b))"
  vars={{ N: [1, 100], mu0: [1, 2], I: [1, 3], a: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 2], s: [0.5, 2], v: [0.5, 3] }}
  names={{ N: "N", mu0: "\\mu_0", I: "I", a: "a", b: "b", s: "s", v: "v" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*I*a*b*v/(2*pi*s*(s + b))", hint: "That is the emf of one turn. The coil has N turns in series, each enclosing the same flux, so their emfs add." },
    { expr: "N*mu0*I*a*v/(2*pi*s)", hint: "That counts only the nearer side of the coil, where the motional emf of each turn is vBa. The farther side also moves through the field, a weaker one, and its emf works against the nearer side's." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Moving away, the coil leaves stronger field behind at its nearer side and enters weaker field at its farther side, so the flux falls. Its rate of fall is the emf.

<Step title="Differentiate">

$\dfrac{d\Phi}{ds} = \dfrac{\mu_0 I a}{2\pi}\left(\dfrac{1}{s + b} - \dfrac1s\right) = -\dfrac{\mu_0 I a}{2\pi}\,\dfrac{b}{s(s + b)}$, and $d\Phi/dt = v\,d\Phi/ds$. So

<Formula label="The emf of the moving coil" tex="\mathcal{E} = -N\,\frac{d\Phi}{dt} = \frac{N\mu_0 I a b\,v}{2\pi s(s + b)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The same result from the magnetic force">

Seen as a motional emf, each charge in the coil moves at $v$ away from the wire, and the force $q\,\vec v \times \vec B$ points along the sides of length $a$. On the nearer side it gives $vB(s)\,a$ per turn, on the farther side $vB(s + b)\,a$ the other way round the loop; the sides of length $b$ give nothing, since the force is across them. The net emf per turn is $va\,\dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi}\left(\dfrac1s - \dfrac{1}{s + b}\right)$, the same as above.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\mu_0 I/s$ is a field in tesla, times $ab/(s + b)$ a length, times a speed gives T m²/s = V. Far away, $s \gg b$, the emf falls as $1/s^2$, like the gradient of the wire's field.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Which way does the induced current flow?

<Hint>

The flux through the coil is falling. By Lenz's law the induced current makes a field of its own that opposes the fall.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Parallel currents attract and opposite currents repel. Which of the two would oppose the coil's motion away from the wire?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-dir" label="In the side of the coil nearest the wire, the induced current flows" choices={["against the wire's current, so that the wire pushes the coil away further", "along the wire's current, so the wire pulls the coil back", "nowhere: the field at the coil does not change in time", "both ways in turn, as the coil moves on"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The induced current must keep up the flux that is being lost, and it must brake the motion that causes the loss. Both views give the same direction.

<Step title="Lenz's law">

The flux of the wire through the coil falls as the coil moves away. To oppose the fall, the current in the coil must make a field through the coil in the same direction as the wire's. Inside the coil, the wire's field has the direction that a current along the wire's current, in the nearer side, would also give there. So in the nearer side the induced current flows along the wire's current.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Parallel currents attract: the wire pulls the nearer side back, while it pushes the farther side, carrying the opposite current, away with a smaller force, since the field there is weaker. The net force pulls the coil back toward the wire. Something has to keep pulling the coil to hold its speed, and that work becomes the heat of the induced current, as energy conservation demands.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

A coil of $N = 50$ turns has the sides $a = 20.0$ cm and $b = 10.0$ cm. The wire carries $I = 20.0$ A, and the coil moves away at $v = 2.00$ m/s. What is the emf when the nearer side is $s = 10.0$ cm from the wire, in millivolts? (Our own numbers.)

<Hint>

Put the numbers into the result of part (b), in SI units. $\mu_0/2\pi = 2.00\times10^{-7}$ T m/A.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-num" label="$|\mathcal{E}|$ in mV:" value="0.400" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into the formula of part (b).

<Step title="The number">

$\mathcal{E} = 50 \times 2.00\times10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A} \times 20.0\ \text{A} \times \dfrac{0.200\ \text{m} \times 0.100\ \text{m} \times 2.00\ \text{m/s}}{0.100\ \text{m} \times 0.200\ \text{m}} = 4.00\times10^{-4}$ V, that is 0.400 mV.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The field is $4.00\times10^{-5}$ T at the nearer side and $2.00\times10^{-5}$ T at the farther side. Each turn has the motional emf $va$ times their difference, $2.00 \times 0.200 \times 2.00\times10^{-5} = 8.0\times10^{-6}$ V, and 50 turns give 0.400 mV.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="The mutual inductance of two coaxial loops (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Mutual inductance between two current loops". The problem below is our own, on the same subject.

A circular loop of radius $b$ lies in the plane $z = 0$, centred on the $z$ axis. A much smaller loop of radius $a \ll b$ lies parallel to it, centred on the axis at the height $z$. A current $I_1$ in one loop sends a flux $\Phi_{21}$ through the other, and the mutual inductance is

<Formula label="Mutual inductance" tex="M_{21} = \frac{N_2\,\Phi_{21}}{I_1}, \qquad M_{12} = M_{21} \equiv M" cite="mit802-ch11" at="Eqs. (11.1.3) and (11.1.8), pp. 2–3" />

with $N_2 = 1$ here. The second relation says that the two ways round give the same $M$; this problem checks it for these loops.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Let the current $I$ flow in the large loop. Its field on the axis, at the height $z$, is

<Formula label="The field on the axis of a ring" tex="B_z = \frac{\mu_0 I b^2}{2\left(b^2 + z^2\right)^{3/2}}" cite="mit802-ch9" at="Eq. (9.1.15), p. 8" />

Since $a \ll b$, take this field as uniform over the small loop. Find the mutual inductance $M$.

<Hint>

The flux through the small loop is the field on the axis times the small loop's area.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-m" label="$M =$" expr="mu0*pi*a^2*b^2/(2*(b^2 + z^2)^(3/2))"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], a: [0.1, 0.5], b: [1, 2], z: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", a: "a", b: "b", z: "z" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*pi*a^2/(2*b)", hint: "That is the value at z = 0 only, with both loops in one plane. The field on the axis weakens as the small loop moves away along it." },
    { expr: "mu0*a^2*b^2/(2*(b^2 + z^2)^(3/2))", hint: "The flux is the field times the area of the small loop, and that area is πa², not a²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Over the small loop the large loop's field is almost uniform, so the flux is just field times area.

<Step title="The flux and M">

<Formula label="The mutual inductance from the large loop's field" tex="\Phi_{21} = \pi a^2\,\frac{\mu_0 I b^2}{2\left(b^2 + z^2\right)^{3/2}}, \qquad M = \frac{\Phi_{21}}{I} = \frac{\mu_0\pi a^2 b^2}{2\left(b^2 + z^2\right)^{3/2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\mu_0$ in H/m times a length gives henries. At $z = 0$, $M = \mu_0\pi a^2/2b$, the field $\mu_0 I/2b$ at the centre of a ring times $\pi a^2$, divided by $I$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Now let the current $I_2$ flow in the small loop instead. Seen from the large loop, the small loop is a magnetic dipole of moment $m = I_2\pi a^2$ along $\hat z$ (the moment $\vec m = \tfrac12 I\oint\vec r \times d\vec\ell$ of a loop, Lecture Notes 10, p. 5), with the vector potential

<Formula label="The vector potential of a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec A(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{\vec m \times \hat r}{r^2}" cite="reading2" at="p. 5 (boxed)" />

measured from the centre of the small loop. Find the size $A_\phi$ of the vector potential on the large loop. Then show that the flux of the small loop through the large loop, $\oint\vec A \cdot d\vec\ell$ around it, gives the same $M$ as part (a).

<Hint>

Every point of the large loop is at the same distance $r = \sqrt{b^2 + z^2}$ from the small loop, and the angle $\theta$ between $\hat z$ and the direction to it has $\sin\theta = b/r$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\vec m \times \hat r$ points around the axis, along $\hat\phi$, and its size is $m\sin\theta$. The line integral around the large loop is then $2\pi b\,A_\phi$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-a" label="$A_\phi$ on the large loop $=$" expr="mu0*I2*a^2*b/(4*(b^2 + z^2)^(3/2))"
  vars={{ mu0: [1, 2], I2: [1, 3], a: [0.1, 0.5], b: [1, 2], z: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", I2: "I_2", a: "a", b: "b", z: "z" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*I2*a^2/(4*(b^2 + z^2))", hint: "That is μ₀m/(4πr²) without the angle. The cross product m × r̂ has the size m sin θ, and on the large loop sin θ = b/r." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The flux through a closed loop is the line integral of $\vec A$ around it, because $\vec B = \nabla \times \vec A$ and Stokes' theorem turns the surface integral into a line integral. On the large loop the dipole's $\vec A$ has the same size everywhere and points along the loop.

<Step title="The vector potential on the large loop">

With $\vec m = m\hat z$, $\hat z \times \hat r = \sin\theta\,\hat\phi$, so $A_\phi = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{m\sin\theta}{r^2}$. With $m = I_2\pi a^2$, $r^2 = b^2 + z^2$ and $\sin\theta = b/r$:

<Formula label="The dipole's vector potential on the large loop" tex="A_\phi = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I_2\pi a^2\,b}{\left(b^2 + z^2\right)^{3/2}} = \frac{\mu_0 I_2 a^2 b}{4\left(b^2 + z^2\right)^{3/2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The flux and M">

<Formula label="The mutual inductance from the small loop's field" tex="\Phi_{12} = \oint\vec A \cdot d\vec\ell = 2\pi b\,A_\phi = \frac{\mu_0\pi I_2 a^2 b^2}{2\left(b^2 + z^2\right)^{3/2}}, \qquad \frac{\Phi_{12}}{I_2} = \frac{\mu_0\pi a^2 b^2}{2\left(b^2 + z^2\right)^{3/2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

This is the $M$ of part (a), as the equality $M_{12} = M_{21}$ demands.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The two routes are very different: part (a) used the field of a ring on its axis and the small area of the other loop; this part used the far field of a small loop over a large circle. Their agreement is a check of both. Both use $a \ll b$: part (a) to take the field as uniform over the small loop, this part to treat the small loop as a point dipole, since every point of the large loop is at least the distance $b$ from it.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

The large loop has $b = 30.0$ cm, the small one $a = 2.00$ cm, and their centres are $z = 40.0$ cm apart. What is $M$, in nanohenries? (Our own numbers.)

<Hint>

With these numbers $\sqrt{b^2 + z^2}$ is a round number.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-num" label="$M$ in nH:" value="0.568" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into the result of parts (a) and (b).

<Step title="The number">

$\sqrt{b^2 + z^2} = 0.500$ m, so $M = \dfrac{4\pi\times10^{-7} \times \pi \times (0.0200)^2 \times (0.300)^2}{2 \times (0.500)^3}$ H $= 5.68\times10^{-10}$ H, that is 0.568 nH.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

A current changing at 200 A/s in either loop induces $M \times 200$ A/s $= 0.114$ μV in the other: two loops of this size, this far apart, are only weakly coupled.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Perfect conductors and superconductors (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Perfect conductors and superconductors", with three parts required for 15 points and a fourth part for 10 points of extra credit. The problem below is our own, on the same subject, with the same structure: parts (a) to (c) here, and the extra-credit part as "Problem 3(d)" after it.

A **perfect conductor** has infinite conductivity. Since Ohm's law $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ must give a finite current density as $\sigma \to \infty$, the electric field inside it is zero. Faraday's law, the second of Maxwell's equations

<Formula label="Faraday's law" tex="\nabla \times \vec E = -\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="slide 8, eq. (14)" />

then has something to say about $\vec B$ inside. A **superconductor** is more than a perfect conductor: experiment shows that when it is cooled through its transition in a magnetic field, it expels the field, so that $\vec B = 0$ inside (the Meissner effect, stated here as a fact). At a surface carrying the surface current density $\vec K$ (current per unit width), the field jumps:

<Formula label="The jump of the field at a current sheet" tex="\vec B_{\text{out}} - \vec B_{\text{in}} = \mu_0\,\vec K \times \hat n" cite="mit802-ch9" at="follows from Eq. (9.3.12), p. 19 (the sheet's own field is ∓μ₀K/2 on the two sides); the standard boundary condition, stated here" />

where $\hat n$ is the normal pointing out of the conductor.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Show that the magnetic field inside a perfect conductor cannot change in time. Then answer: a ball of a perfect conductor (not a superconductor) is made perfectly conducting while a uniform field $B_0$ is on, and then the outside field is switched off. What is the field inside the ball afterwards?

<Hint>

Put $\vec E = 0$ into Faraday's law.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The field inside cannot change, whatever happens outside. What must the currents in the ball do when the outside field is switched off?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-frozen" label="The field inside the ball afterwards is" choices={["zero, because a perfect conductor expels the field", "B₀ at first, then dying away as the currents decay", "−B₀, reversed by the induced currents", "still B₀, kept there by induced surface currents"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In a perfect conductor $\vec E = 0$, so Faraday's law freezes $\vec B$: no change of the field can get in or out.

<Step title="The field is frozen">

With $\vec E = 0$ inside, $\nabla \times \vec E = 0$, so $\partial\vec B/\partial t = 0$ at every point inside: $\vec B$ keeps whatever value it had when the material became perfectly conducting.

</Step>

<Step title="The ball">

The field inside was $B_0$ when the ball became perfectly conducting, so it stays $B_0$ after the outside field is switched off. Currents are induced on the surface that make exactly this field inside; with no resistance they never decay. A field inside that is uniform has no curl, so by Ampère's law there is no current in the bulk, only on the surface.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is where a perfect conductor and a superconductor differ. A superconductor cooled in the field would have expelled it already, with $\vec B = 0$ inside both before and after; a perfect conductor keeps the field it had, so its final state depends on its history.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Show that in a superconductor all current flows on the surface, and that just outside it the field has no component along the normal.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A field that vanishes has no curl and no flux, so the bulk carries no current and no field line can enter.

<Step title="No current in the bulk">

Inside, $\vec B = 0$, so $\nabla \times \vec B = 0 = \mu_0\vec J$: the current density is zero in the bulk. Any current is a surface current $\vec K$.

</Step>

<Step title="No normal field outside">

$\vec K \times \hat n$ is perpendicular to $\hat n$, so the jump condition says the normal component of $\vec B$ is continuous. Inside it is zero, so just outside $\vec B \cdot \hat n = 0$: the field lines run along the surface.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The same follows from $\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0$: the flux out of a thin box across the surface must vanish, and no flux comes from the inside face.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

<Hint>

<Answer id="p4-r" label="To first order in $B$, the radius of the orbit" choices={["grows, because the electron has sped up", "shrinks, because the magnetic force points inward", "stays the same: the two first-order changes match", "grows or shrinks, depending on the direction of B"]} answer={2} />

<Step title="The directions">

<Step title="The balance">

<Step title="Check the result">

</Step>

## Problem 8

<Exercise id="p8" title="More fun with δ-functions" points={20} mit="Problem 8" optional>

This problem is MIT's extra credit, optional here. It practises what may and may not be done with delta functions. Treat them, and the functions in this problem, as **distributions**: objects defined only by what they give when multiplied by a test function $\varphi(x)$ and integrated over all $x$. A test function is smooth and falls off fast as $|x| \to \infty$. (Mathematicians often take the Schwartz functions: infinitely differentiable, with the function and all its derivatives falling off faster than any power. No rigour is expected here: trust the usual rules of calculus.) Two objects are equal as distributions if they give the same integral for every test function. The derivative of a distribution $\Psi(x)$ is defined by moving the derivative onto the test function, as in an integration by parts:

<Formula label="The derivative of a distribution" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(x)\,\frac{d\Psi(x)}{dx}\,dx \equiv -\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\varphi(x)}{dx}\,\Psi(x)\,dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(a), Eq. (8.3), p. 2" />

<Part label="a" points={3}>

The step function is

<Formula label="The step function" tex="\theta(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x > 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } x < 0 \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(a), Eq. (8.1), p. 2" />

with $\theta(0)$ often set to $\tfrac12$; as a distribution its value at the single point $x = 0$ does not matter. Prove, as distributions,

<Formula label="The derivative of the step function" tex="\frac{d\theta(x)}{dx} = \delta(x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(a), Eq. (8.2), p. 2 (boxed)" />

that is, show that for every test function

<Formula label="The same, tested" tex="\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(x)\,\frac{d\theta(x)}{dx}\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(x)\,\delta(x)\,dx" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(a), Eq. (8.4), p. 2" />

This part has no box.

<Hint>

MIT's hint: once you see what is really being asked, it takes one or two lines.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Use the definition of the derivative. The step function then only cuts the integral down to $x > 0$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** By definition the left side is an ordinary integral of $\varphi'$, which the fundamental theorem of calculus does at once.

<Step title="Two lines">

$\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi\,\frac{d\theta}{dx}\,dx \equiv -\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\varphi}{dx}\,\theta\,dx = -\int_0^{\infty}\frac{d\varphi}{dx}\,dx = -\left[\varphi(\infty) - \varphi(0)\right] = \varphi(0)$, since $\varphi$ vanishes at infinity. And $\int\varphi\,\delta\,dx = \varphi(0)$ by the definition of the delta function. The two sides agree for every test function.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The value $\theta(0)$ never entered: a single point does not change an integral, which is why it is irrelevant for a distribution.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

<Formula label="Powers of the step function" tex="\theta^n(x) = \theta(x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 8(b), Eq. (8.5), p. 3 (boxed)" />

for every positive integer $n$: multiplied by any test function and integrated from $-\infty$ to $\infty$, the two sides give the same number. This part has no box.

<Hint>

</Hint>

**The idea.** $1^n = 1$ and $0^n = 0$; the two functions can differ only at $x = 0$.

<Step title="Compare the integrals">

For $x \ne 0$, $\theta^n(x) = \theta(x)$, so both integrals equal $\int_0^{\infty}\varphi(x)\,dx$. At $x = 0$ they may differ (with $\theta(0) = \tfrac12$, $\theta^n(0) = 2^{-n}$), but one point does not change an integral.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

As ordinary functions the two sides are not equal at $x = 0$, which is exactly the kind of difference that equality as distributions ignores.

</Step>

</Solution>

With $\varphi(x) = e^{-(x - 1/2)^2}$ and $a = 1$: $G = \tfrac12\left[e^{-1/4} + e^{-9/4}\right] = \tfrac12(0.7788 + 0.1054) = 0.442$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Replacing the delta function by a narrow Gaussian of width $10^{-2}$ and integrating numerically gives 0.442 too. Units: $\delta(x^2 - a^2)$ has the units of $1/a^2$, and $G$ those of $\varphi/a$, as the result shows.

</Step>
