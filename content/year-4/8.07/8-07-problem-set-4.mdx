This set practises the two general methods for finding a potential when the charges are not known in advance: [image charges](/courses/8-07/method-of-images-uniqueness), and separation of variables in [Cartesian](/courses/8-07/separation-of-variables-cartesian) and in [spherical coordinates](/courses/8-07/separation-of-variables-spherical). The spherical problems use the Legendre series of [Lecture Notes 7](/courses/8-07/spherical-shell-circular-hole) and the spherical harmonics of [Lecture Notes 8](/courses/8-07/spherical-harmonics-from-traceless-tensors). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 4 (the revised sheet of October 2, 2012), in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its due date and its reading assignment (a textbook chapter) are left out. MIT's Problems 1, 4, 5 and 6 are only pointers to problems in a textbook (Griffiths), named by number. The textbook is not used here: **Problems 1, 4, 5 and 6 are our own problems** on the subjects that MIT's titles name (Laplace's equation in a box, a charged metal sphere in a uniform field, a sphere with oppositely charged hemispheres, and the average field inside a sphere), with MIT's points. Problems 2 and 3 are stated in full on MIT's sheet and are given here as MIT sets them. In Problem 2 the image charges are numbered $q_1$ to $q_4$ here (MIT marks them with one to four primes). In Problem 3 the degree is written $\ell$, as in the lecture notes (MIT's sheet writes $l$). In Problems 2 and 3 the boxes check what MIT asks for, with two exceptions that are our additions and are marked as such: the number box of Problem 2(d), and Problem 3(b), a worked case with two boxes. The first page of the sheet has no printed number; "p. 1" below means that page, and "p. 2" is the printed number of the second.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `Q/(4*pi*eps0*R^2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `pi` stands for $\pi$, `eps0` for $\varepsilon_0$, `th` for $\theta$, `s0` for $\sigma_0$, and `V0`, `q0`, `z0`, `E0` for $V_0$, $q_0$, $z_0$, $E_0$; the other letters (`L`, `h`, `n`, `m`, `R`, `Q`, `r`, `z`, `q`, `b`) are typed as they are.

MIT gives the points per problem: 15 for Problem 1, 25 for Problem 2, 15 each for Problems 3, 4 and 5, and 20 for Problem 6, 105 points in all. The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes. $V$ is the electric potential, $\vec E = -\nabla V$ is the field, and $\varepsilon_0$ is the electric constant. In spherical coordinates $r$ is the distance from the origin, $\theta$ is the angle from the $z$ axis and $\phi$ is the angle around it. $P_\ell$ is the Legendre polynomial of degree $\ell$ and $Y_{\ell m}$ is the standard spherical harmonic. A surface that is "grounded" is held at $V = 0$.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Laplace's equation in a box (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Laplace's equation in a box". The problem below is our own, on the same subject.

A metal box has a square base of side $L$ and the height $h$. It fills the region $0 \le x \le L$, $0 \le y \le L$, $0 \le z \le h$. The base and the four side walls are welded together and grounded. The lid, at $z = h$, is insulated from the rest by a thin gap, and its potential can be set from outside. There is no charge inside the box, so the potential there obeys Laplace's equation.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

The lid is made so that its potential follows the pattern

<Formula label="The potential on the lid in part (a)" tex="V(x, y, h) = V_0\,\sin\frac{\pi x}{L}\,\sin\frac{\pi y}{L}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own problem)" />

Find the potential everywhere inside the box. For the first box give its value at the centre of the box, the point $x = y = L/2$, $z = h/2$, in terms of $V_0$, $h$ and $L$. For the second box take a cube, $h = L$, and give the potential at the centre as a fraction of $V_0$.

<Hint>

Try a product $X(x)\,Y(y)\,Z(z)$. Which functions of $x$ and of $y$ vanish on both side walls and also match the pattern on the lid?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The three separation constants add up to zero. The sines in $x$ and $y$ each give a negative constant, so the constant for $Z$ is positive and $Z$ is built from exponentials. Which combination of them vanishes on the base, $z = 0$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-centre" label="$V$ at the centre $=$" expr="V0*sinh(pi*h/(sqrt(2)*L))/sinh(sqrt(2)*pi*h/L)"
  vars={{ V0: [1, 3], h: [0.5, 2], L: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ V0: "V_0", h: "h", L: "L" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "V0/2", hint: "That would be right if the potential rose in a straight line from the base to the lid. It does not: the grounded side walls pull it down, and the hyperbolic sine sags below the straight line." },
    { expr: "V0*sinh(pi*h/(2*L))/sinh(pi*h/L)", hint: "That uses the decay constant π/L, which belongs to a pattern that varies along x only. Here the pattern curves along x and along y, and both curvatures add: the square of the constant is (π/L)² + (π/L)²." },
    { expr: "V0*sinh(pi*h/L)/sinh(2*pi*h/L)", hint: "That uses the decay constant 2π/L, the sum of the constants for x and y. It is their squares that add: the square of the decay constant is (π/L)² + (π/L)²." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1-cube" label="For the cube, $V$ at the centre divided by $V_0$:" value="0.107" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-cube" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** One product of three functions already fits every wall of the box, because the pattern on the lid is itself a product of two sines that vanish on the side walls.

<Step title="Separate the variables">

Put $V = X(x)\,Y(y)\,Z(z)$ into Laplace's equation and divide by $XYZ$. Each of the three terms depends on one coordinate only, so each is a constant, and

<Formula label="The separation constants add up to zero" tex="\frac{1}{X}\frac{d^2X}{dx^2} = C_1, \qquad \frac{1}{Y}\frac{d^2Y}{dy^2} = C_2, \qquad \frac{1}{Z}\frac{d^2Z}{dz^2} = C_3, \qquad C_1 + C_2 + C_3 = 0" cite="reading1" at="pp. 64–65" />

</Step>

<Step title="Fit the side walls and the base">

$X = \sin(\pi x/L)$ vanishes at $x = 0$ and $x = L$ and has $C_1 = -(\pi/L)^2$. The same holds for $Y = \sin(\pi y/L)$. Then $C_3 = 2\pi^2/L^2$ is positive: $Z'' = \gamma^2 Z$ with $\gamma = \sqrt2\,\pi/L$. The solutions are $e^{\gamma z}$ and $e^{-\gamma z}$, and the combination that vanishes on the base is $\sinh(\gamma z)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Fit the lid">

The constant in front is fixed by $Z(h) = V_0$:

<Formula label="The potential in the box for the single pattern" tex="V(x, y, z) = V_0\,\frac{\sinh\left(\sqrt2\,\pi z/L\right)}{\sinh\left(\sqrt2\,\pi h/L\right)}\,\sin\frac{\pi x}{L}\,\sin\frac{\pi y}{L}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

It obeys Laplace's equation and has the right value on all six faces, so by the uniqueness theorem it is the potential in the box. At the centre both sines are 1 and $z = h/2$:

<Formula label="The potential at the centre of the box" tex="V_{\text{centre}} = V_0\,\frac{\sinh\left(\pi h/(\sqrt2\,L)\right)}{\sinh\left(\sqrt2\,\pi h/L\right)} = \frac{V_0}{2\cosh\left(\pi h/(\sqrt2\,L)\right)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The last step uses $\sinh 2u = 2\sinh u\cosh u$. For the cube, $\pi/\sqrt2 = 2.221$ and $\cosh 2.221 = 4.665$, so $V_{\text{centre}} = 0.107\,V_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a very flat box, $h \ll L$, the hyperbolic cosine tends to 1 and the centre is at $V_0/2$: lid and base are then like the plates of a capacitor, far from the side walls, and the potential rises in a straight line between them. For a tall box the value falls off exponentially with $h$, because the grounded walls screen the lid.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Now the lid is cut along the line $x = L/2$ into two equal strips, insulated from each other. The strip with $x < L/2$ is held at $+V_0$ and the strip with $x > L/2$ at $-V_0$. The potential in the box is then a double series,

<Formula label="The potential in the box as a double sine series" tex="V(x, y, z) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{\infty} c_{nm}\,\frac{\sinh(\gamma_{nm}z)}{\sinh(\gamma_{nm}h)}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\sin\frac{m\pi y}{L}, \qquad \gamma_{nm} = \frac{\pi}{L}\sqrt{n^2 + m^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own problem)" />

Explain why the series has this form, and find the coefficients $c_{nm}$. Which of them are not zero, and what is their value?

<Hint>

Every term is a product solution of the kind found in part (a), with $n$ and $m$ half-waves between the side walls. On the lid the ratio of hyperbolic sines is 1, so there the series is a double Fourier sine series for the potential of the lid.

</Hint>

<Hint>

To pick out one coefficient, multiply the lid's potential by $\sin(n\pi x/L)\,\sin(m\pi y/L)$ and integrate over the lid. The sines are orthogonal: the integral of $\sin^2$ over one side is $L/2$. The double integral splits into an integral over $x$ times an integral over $y$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In the integral over $x$ the two strips enter with opposite signs. Work it out for $n = 1, 2, 3, 4$ and look for the pattern; a sketch of each sine against the two strips shows why most of them give zero.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-which" label="The coefficients that are not zero have" choices={["n odd and m odd", "n even and m odd", "n = 2, 6, 10, … and m odd", "n = 2, 6, 10, … and any m"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p1-coef" label="For those $n$ and $m$, $c_{nm} =$" expr="32*V0/(n*m*pi^2)"
  vars={{ V0: [1, 3], n: [2, 6], m: [1, 5] }}
  names={{ V0: "V_0", n: "n", m: "m" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "16*V0/(n*m*pi^2)", hint: "That is the coefficient for a lid at V₀ all over (for odd n and m). With the two strips, the integral over x is 4L/(nπ) for the n that survive, twice the 2L/(nπ) of a uniform lid." },
    { expr: "8*V0/(n*m*pi^2)", hint: "Check the factor in front of the double integral. Each direction gives 2/L, from the integral of sin² over a side, so the factor is 4/L², not 1/L²." },
    { expr: "32*V0/(n^2*m^2*pi^2)", hint: "Each integral of a sine gives one factor 1/n or 1/m, not its square: the integrand is a sine times a constant." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Any sum of product solutions that vanish on the five grounded faces is again a solution. On the lid the sum must reproduce the two strips, which is a Fourier problem.

<Step title="Why the series has this form">

As in part (a), $\sin(n\pi x/L)$ and $\sin(m\pi y/L)$ vanish on the side walls for every positive whole number $n$ and $m$. Their constants are $-(n\pi/L)^2$ and $-(m\pi/L)^2$, so $Z'' = \gamma_{nm}^2 Z$ with $\gamma_{nm}^2 = (\pi/L)^2(n^2 + m^2)$, and $\sinh(\gamma_{nm}z)$ vanishes on the base. Dividing by $\sinh(\gamma_{nm}h)$ makes the $z$ factor equal to 1 on the lid. Laplace's equation is linear, so the sum with any constants $c_{nm}$ is a solution that is zero on the five grounded faces.

</Step>

<Step title="The coefficients as integrals">

On the lid the series must equal the lid's potential $V_{\text{lid}}(x, y)$. Multiply by $\sin(n'\pi x/L)\sin(m'\pi y/L)$ and integrate over the lid. Only the term with $n = n'$ and $m = m'$ survives, with the factor $(L/2)^2$, so

<Formula label="The coefficients of the double sine series" tex="c_{nm} = \frac{4}{L^2}\int_0^L\!\!\int_0^L V_{\text{lid}}(x, y)\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\sin\frac{m\pi y}{L}\,dx\,dy" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The two integrals">

The lid's potential is $V_0$ times a function of $x$ alone, $+1$ on the first half and $-1$ on the second. The integral over $y$ is

<Formula label="The integral over y" tex="\int_0^L \sin\frac{m\pi y}{L}\,dy = \frac{L}{m\pi}\left(1 - \cos m\pi\right) = \frac{2L}{m\pi} \text{ for odd } m, \quad 0 \text{ for even } m" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The integral over $x$ is

<Formula label="The integral over x" tex="\int_0^{L/2}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx - \int_{L/2}^{L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx = \frac{L}{n\pi}\left(1 - 2\cos\frac{n\pi}{2} + \cos n\pi\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The bracket is $0$ for odd $n$ (then $\cos(n\pi/2) = 0$ and $\cos n\pi = -1$), it is $1 - 2 + 1 = 0$ when $n$ is a multiple of 4, and it is $1 + 2 + 1 = 4$ for $n = 2, 6, 10, \ldots$ So

<Formula label="The coefficients for the lid in two strips" tex="c_{nm} = \frac{4}{L^2}\,V_0\,\frac{4L}{n\pi}\,\frac{2L}{m\pi} = \frac{32\,V_0}{\pi^2\,n\,m} \qquad (n = 2, 6, 10, \ldots;\ m = 1, 3, 5, \ldots)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

and all the others are zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The pattern on the lid changes sign under a reflection in the plane $x = L/2$ and keeps its sign under a reflection in $y = L/2$. A sine with odd $n$ is even about $x = L/2$, so it cannot appear. A sine with $n = 4, 8, \ldots$ is odd about $x = L/2$, but within each strip it has as much positive as negative area. In $y$ only the sines that are even about the middle appear: odd $m$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Take the cube, $h = L$, with the lid in two strips as in part (b). Find the potential at the point $x = L/4$, $y = L/2$, $z = L/2$ (the middle height, under the centre of the positive strip), as a fraction of $V_0$. Keep enough terms of the series for the 2% tolerance of the box.

<Hint>

At this point both sines of the first term, $n = 2$ and $m = 1$, are 1. For the height use the same simplification as in part (a): $\sinh u/\sinh 2u = 1/(2\cosh u)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The next term in size has $n = 2$ and $m = 3$. Mind the sign of $\sin(3\pi/2)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-point" label="$V(L/4, L/2, L/2)$ divided by $V_0$:" value="0.0465" tolerance="2%" calc="p1-point" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** At the middle height each term carries the factor $1/(2\cosh(\gamma_{nm}L/2))$, which falls off very fast with $n$ and $m$. Two terms are enough.

<Step title="The first term">

For $n = 2$, $m = 1$: $c_{21} = 32V_0/(2\pi^2) = 1.621\,V_0$, the sines are $\sin(\pi/2)\sin(\pi/2) = 1$, and $\gamma_{21}L/2 = \sqrt5\,\pi/2 = 3.512$ with $\cosh 3.512 = 16.78$. The term is $1.621\,V_0/(2 \times 16.78) = 0.0483\,V_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The second term">

For $n = 2$, $m = 3$: $c_{23} = 32V_0/(6\pi^2) = 0.540\,V_0$, the sines are $\sin(\pi/2)\sin(3\pi/2) = -1$, and $\gamma_{23}L/2 = \sqrt{13}\,\pi/2 = 5.664$ with $\cosh 5.664 = 144.1$. The term is $-0.540\,V_0/(2 \times 144.1) = -0.0019\,V_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The sum">

The next term, $n = 6$ and $m = 1$, is $-0.00004\,V_0$, and the rest are smaller still. So

<Formula label="The potential at the chosen point of the cube" tex="V\left(\tfrac{L}{4}, \tfrac{L}{2}, \tfrac{L}{2}\right) = \left(0.0483 - 0.0019 - 0.00004 + \cdots\right)V_0 = 0.0465\,V_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The value is positive, as it should be under the positive strip, and it is small: the point is as near to the grounded base and walls as to the lid, and the negative strip pulls it down further. The first term alone is 4% too large, which the box does not accept.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="A spherical conductor and a conducting plane" points={25} mit="Problem 2">

A solid conducting sphere of radius $R$ has its centre on the positive $z$ axis, at $z = z_0$ with $z_0 > R$. The $xy$ plane is a conductor too and is held at $V = 0$. The sphere is held at the potential $V_0$.

As a first approximation, think of the field as that of a point charge $q_0$ at the centre of the sphere, where $q_0$ is tied to $V_0$ by

<Formula label="The first charge and the potential of the sphere" tex="V_0 = \frac{q_0}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 R}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, eq. (2.1), p. 1" />

This charge alone gives the potential $V_0$ all over the surface of the sphere, as wanted. But the potential on the $xy$ plane is then not zero.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

The potential on the plane can be brought back to zero by one image charge $q_1$ below the plane (at negative $z$). What is $q_1$, and where does it go?

<Hint>

This is the first image problem of Lecture Notes 5: a point charge in front of a grounded plane.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-first" label="The image charge $q_1$ is" choices={["+q₀ at z = −z₀", "−q₀ at z = −z₀", "−q₀ R/z₀ at z = −R²/z₀", "−q₀ at z = −(z₀ − R)"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Every point of the plane must be equally far from $q_0$ and from an opposite charge.

<Step title="The mirror charge">

Put $q_1 = -q_0$ at $z_1 = -z_0$, the mirror image of the centre of the sphere. A point of the $xy$ plane is at the same distance from $z = z_0$ and from $z = -z_0$, so the two potentials cancel there (Lecture Notes 5, pp. 58–59).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The image lies below the plane, outside the region where the potential is wanted, so it adds no charge to that region. That is what an image charge has to do.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={9}>

With $q_1$ in place the surface of the sphere is no longer at one potential. It can be made so again by another image charge $q_2$. Then the plane is restored to zero by an image $q_3$, and the sphere to a constant by an image $q_4$. The series goes on for ever, but it converges fairly quickly. Find the charges $q_2$, $q_3$, $q_4$ and their heights $z_2$, $z_3$, $z_4$ on the $z$ axis.

You will need the image of a point charge in a sphere. A charge $Q$ at the distance $d$ from the centre of a sphere of radius $R$, with $d > R$, together with a charge $q$ on the line from the centre to $Q$, at the distance $s$ from the centre, makes the potential zero all over the sphere when

<Formula label="The image of a point charge in a sphere" tex="q = -\frac{R}{d}\,Q, \qquad s = \frac{R^2}{d}" cite="reading1" at="p. 63 (the notes write a for the radius, R for the distance of the charge and r for that of the image)" />

The boxes ask for $q_2$, $z_2$, $q_4$ and $z_4$, in terms of $q_0$, $R$ and $z_0$. The charge $q_3$ and its height have no box: compare with the solution.

<Hint>

The charge $q_0$ already gives the sphere the potential $V_0$. So $q_2$ only has to cancel the potential of $q_1$ on the sphere. How far is $q_1$ from the centre of the sphere?

</Hint>

<Hint>

$q_3$ is the mirror image of $q_2$ in the plane, as in part (a). $q_4$ cancels the potential of $q_3$ on the sphere; the distance of $q_3$ from the centre of the sphere is $z_0 - z_3$, and $z_3$ is negative.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The images inside the sphere lie below its centre, on the side of the plane. Their heights above the plane are $z_0$ minus their distance from the centre.

</Hint>

<Exercise id="p3" title="Electrostatics inside a spherical cavity" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

MIT notes that this problem is patterned after a problem in Jackson's textbook; it is written here from MIT's sheet alone.

A hollow sphere has the inner radius $a$. On its inner surface the potential is given as a function of direction, $V = V_0(\theta, \phi)$. There is no charge in the cavity.

<Part label="a" points={9}>

Show that the potential inside the sphere can be written as

<Formula label="The potential inside the cavity" tex="V(\vec r) = \sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\,\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell} A_{\ell m}\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)^{\ell}\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, eq. (3.1), p. 2" />

You may use that the spherical harmonics are orthonormal. With $d\Omega = \sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$ and the integral taken over all directions,

<Formula label="Orthonormality of the spherical harmonics" tex="\int Y_{\ell' m'}^*(\theta, \phi)\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)\,d\Omega = \delta_{\ell\ell'}\,\delta_{mm'}" cite="qm" at="eq. (3.33), p. 10" />

Here the star is the complex conjugate and $\delta$ is the Kronecker delta, 1 for equal indices and 0 otherwise.

<Hint>

Start from the general solution of Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates: for each degree $\ell$ a radial function with two terms, times a function of direction of degree $\ell$. Which of the two radial terms cannot appear in a cavity that contains the origin?

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-coef" label="$A_{\ell m}$ is" choices={["the integral of Y*ₗₘ V₀ over all directions (solid angle)", "the integral of Yₗₘ V₀ over all directions, with no complex conjugate", "the integral of Y*ₗₘ V₀ over all directions, divided by 4π", "the integral of Y*ₗₘ V₀ over all directions, times (2l + 1)/(4π)"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The general solution of Laplace's equation, cut down to the terms that stay finite at the centre, already has the form asked for. The boundary value then fixes the coefficients through orthonormality.

<Step title="The general solution">

Lecture Notes 7 write the general solution as a sum over the degree $\ell$ of a radial function times a function of direction of degree $\ell$, with

<Formula label="The radial functions" tex="R_\ell(r) = A_\ell\,r^\ell + \frac{B_\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}" cite="reading2" at="eq. (7.2), p. 1" />

The cavity contains the origin and holds no charge, so the potential is finite there: every $B_\ell$ is zero. (A term $B_\ell/r^{\ell+1}$ would be the potential of a point charge or point multipole at the centre.)

</Step>

<Step title="The functions of direction">

Any function of direction can be expanded in spherical harmonics, and the part of degree $\ell$ is a combination of the $2\ell + 1$ functions $Y_{\ell m}$ with $m = -\ell, \ldots, \ell$:

<Formula label="The spherical harmonic expansion" tex="F(\theta, \phi) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\,\sum_{m = -\ell}^{\ell} a_{\ell m}\,Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading3" at="eq. (8.3), p. 1" />

So the potential in the cavity is a sum of terms $r^\ell\,Y_{\ell m}$ with constant coefficients. Writing each constant as $A_{\ell m}/a^\ell$ gives the form of MIT's eq. (3.1). The factor $a^{-\ell}$ only makes the radial factor equal to 1 on the surface.

</Step>

<Step title="The coefficients">

At $r = a$ the series must equal the given potential: $V_0(\theta, \phi) = \sum A_{\ell m}Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)$. Multiply both sides by $Y_{\ell' m'}^*$ and integrate over all directions. By orthonormality only one term of the sum survives, and

<Formula label="The coefficients of the potential in the cavity" tex="A_{\ell m} = \int Y_{\ell m}^*(\theta, \phi)\,V_0(\theta, \phi)\,d\Omega = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^{\pi} Y_{\ell m}^*(\theta, \phi)\,V_0(\theta, \phi)\,\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The series with these coefficients obeys Laplace's equation in the cavity and takes the given values on its wall. By the uniqueness theorem it is the potential.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take a constant surface potential, $V_0(\theta, \phi) = V_c$. Since $Y_{00} = 1/\sqrt{4\pi}$ and every other $Y_{\ell m}$ is orthogonal to a constant, only $A_{00} = \sqrt{4\pi}\,V_c$ is left, and $V = A_{00}Y_{00} = V_c$ throughout the cavity, as it must be. More generally the potential at the centre is the $\ell = 0$ term alone, $A_{00}/\sqrt{4\pi}$, which is the average of $V_0$ over the sphere.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Solution>

**The idea.** Seen from outside, the fictitious ball of part (a) acts as a point charge $-q$ at the origin.

<Step title="The field of the fictitious ball at the charge">

At $\vec b$ with $b > R$ the ball of charge $-q$ makes the field of a point charge $-q$ at the origin:

<Formula label="The average field of a charge outside the ball" tex="\vec E_{\text{avg}} = -\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\vec b}{b^3} \qquad (b > R)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Its component along $\vec b$ is $-q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0b^2)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The field of q at the centre">

The field that $q$, sitting at $\vec b$, makes at the origin is $\dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\dfrac{\vec 0 - \vec b}{b^3}$, which is the same vector. So for a charge outside, the average of its field over the ball equals its field at the centre, whatever the radius of the ball.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $b = R$ the results of parts (b) and (c) agree: both give $-q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^2)$. The result of this part is the field version of a better known fact: the average of a potential over a sphere that contains no charge is its value at the centre.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

The field of several charges is the sum of their fields, and so is its average. Take a ball of radius $R = 10$ cm and three charges on the $z$ axis: $+4.0$ nC at $z = +3.0$ cm, $-4.0$ nC at $z = -3.0$ cm, and $+9.0$ nC at $z = +30$ cm. Find the $z$ component of the average field over the ball, in volts per metre. Use $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 8.988 \times 10^9$ N m²/C².

Then say what the average field of a set of charges inside the ball depends on.

<Hint>

Two of the charges are inside the ball and one is outside. Use part (b) for each of the first two and part (c) for the third, and mind the signs of $q$ and of $z$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-num" label="$z$ component of $\vec E_{\text{avg}}$, in V/m:" value="-3056" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-num" />

<Answer id="p6-dep" label="The average field of the charges inside the ball depends on them only through" choices={["their total charge", "the charge that is nearest to the surface", "their total charge and their distances from the centre, whatever the directions", "their total dipole moment about the centre"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Superposition: add the results of parts (b) and (c), one charge at a time.

<Step title="The charges inside">

Each inside charge gives $-q_i\vec b_i/(4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3)$, so together they give

<Formula label="The average field of the charges inside a ball" tex="\vec E_{\text{avg}} = -\frac{\vec p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R^3}, \qquad \vec p = \sum_i q_i\,\vec b_i" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

where $\vec p$ is the total dipole moment of the inside charges about the centre. Here $p_z = 4.0 \times 10^{-9} \times 0.030 + (-4.0 \times 10^{-9}) \times (-0.030) = 2.4 \times 10^{-10}$ C m, and the $z$ component of the average is $-8.988 \times 10^9 \times 2.4 \times 10^{-10}/(0.10)^3 = -2157$ V/m.

</Step>

<Step title="The charge outside">

Its average field is its field at the centre: $-8.988 \times 10^9 \times 9.0 \times 10^{-9}/(0.30)^2 = -899$ V/m along $z$ (the charge is positive and above the ball, so its field in the ball points down).

</Step>

<Step title="The sum">

The $z$ component of the average field is $-2157 - 899 = -3056$ V/m.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The pair of inside charges is a dipole pointing up, and the field in the middle of a dipole points from the positive charge to the negative one: down, as found. Nothing else about the inside charges matters. If the two were moved to $z = \pm 6.0$ cm and halved, the dipole moment and the average would stay the same, although the field itself would be very different from point to point.

</Step>

</Solution>
