The [first page](/courses/8-07/potentials-gauges-retarded-solutions) on these notes found the retarded potentials: the static integrals with each piece of the source taken at the retarded time $t_r = t - |\vec r - \vec r\,'|/c$. The rest of Guth's slides (slides 15–20) apply them to the simplest source of all, a single point charge moving on any path. The answer, the **Liénard–Wiechert potentials**, holds a surprise, and differentiating it gives the fields of a moving charge, from which the next notes get radiation. (Our own words and worked example.)

## A point charge in the retarded potential

Start from the retarded scalar potential (slide 15):

<Formula label="The retarded scalar potential" tex="V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\rho(\vec r\,', t_r)}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}" cite="reading1" at="slide 15 (eq. (23) of slide 11)" />

A point charge $q$ moving on a trajectory $\vec r_p(t)$ has the charge density (slide 15)

<Formula label="The charge density of a point charge" tex="\rho(\vec r, t) = q\,\delta^3\left(\vec r - \vec r_p(t)\right)" cite="reading1" at="slide 15, eq. (27)" />

Put it in. The delta function makes the integrand vanish except where $\vec r\,' = \vec r_p(t_r)$, so the distance can be taken out of the integral, evaluated there (slide 15):

<Formula label="The potential of a point charge, first step" tex="V(\vec r, t) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int d^3x'\,\frac{\delta^3\left(\vec r\,' - \vec r_p(t_r)\right)}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec r - \vec r_p(t_r)|}\int d^3x'\,\delta^3\left(\vec r\,' - \vec r_p(t_r)\right)" cite="reading1" at="slide 15, eq. (28)" />

It is tempting to say the last integral is 1 and stop: the potential would be the Coulomb potential of the charge at its retarded position. But, perhaps surprisingly, as Guth says, it is not (slide 16):

<Formula label="The integral Z" tex="Z = \int d^3x'\,\delta^3\left(\vec r\,' - \vec r_p(t_r)\right) \ne 1" cite="reading1" at="slide 16, eq. (29)" />

The reason is that $t_r$ depends on $\vec r\,'$. The argument of the delta function is not $\vec r\,'$ minus a fixed point, but a more complicated function of $\vec r\,'$.

## Evaluating Z

A delta function of a function needs the rule (slide 16)

<Formula label="A delta function of a function" tex="\delta\left(g(x)\right) = \sum_i\frac{\delta(x - x_i)}{|g'(x_i)|}, \qquad g(x_i) = 0" cite="reading1" at="slide 16, eq. (30)" />

which follows by changing the variable of integration from $x$ to $g$ near each zero. To keep things simple, Guth turns the axes so that the particle's velocity at $t_r$ points along $x$ (slide 16). The three-dimensional delta function is a product of three, and the $y'$ and $z'$ integrals are simple because the particle's $y$ and $z$ velocity components vanish at $t_r$: a small change of $t_r$ does not move $y_p$ or $z_p$ (slide 16):

<Formula label="Reducing Z to one dimension" tex="Z = \int d^3x'\,\delta\left(x' - x_p(t_r)\right)\delta\left(y' - y_p(t_r)\right)\delta\left(z' - z_p(t_r)\right) = \int dx'\,\delta\left(x' - x_p(t_r)\right)" cite="reading1" at="slide 16, eq. (31)" />

[The slide writes the vanishing velocity components as $y'_p(t_r)$ and $z'_p(t_r)$, with primes for time derivatives; the primes on $x'$, $y'$, $z'$ mark the integration variables.] So (slide 17)

<Formula label="Z as a one-dimensional integral" tex="Z = \int dx'\,\delta\left(x' - x_p(t_r)\right), \qquad t_r = t - \frac{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}{c}" cite="reading1" at="slide 17, eq. (32)" />

In the rule (30), the function is (slide 17)

<Formula label="The function inside the delta function" tex="g(x') = x' - x_p\left(t - \frac{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}{c}\right)" cite="reading1" at="slide 17, eq. (33)" />

and its derivative, by the chain rule through $t_r$ (slide 17):

<Formula label="Its derivative" tex="g'(x') = 1 - \frac{dx_p}{dt}\,\frac{dt_r}{dx'} = 1 + \frac{1}{c}\,\frac{dx_p}{dt}\,\frac{d}{dx'}|\vec r - \vec r\,'| = 1 + \frac{1}{c}\,\frac{dx_p}{dt}\,\frac{x' - x}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}" cite="reading1" at="slide 17, eq. (34)" />

For $v < c$ there is exactly one zero, because the retarded time is unique (our remark), and $Z = 1/g'$ there (slide 17). The combination $\frac{dx_p}{dt}(x' - x)$ is minus the velocity dotted into $\vec r - \vec r\,'$, so for a velocity in any direction (slide 17)

<Formula label="The value of Z" tex="Z = \left(1 - \frac{\vec v}{c} \cdot \frac{\vec r - \vec r\,'}{|\vec r - \vec r\,'|}\right)^{-1}" cite="reading1" at="slide 17, eq. (35)" />

with $\vec r\,'$ at the charge's retarded position. A picture of why (ours, not on the slides): the retarded integral samples each part of the source at a different time, later for the parts nearer the field point. A charge moving towards the field point is caught over a stretch of its path, and it counts more than once; one moving away counts less. $Z$ lies between $1/(1 + v/c)$ and $1/(1 - v/c)$.

## The Liénard–Wiechert potentials

Putting $Z$ into (28) gives the potential of a point charge on any path (slide 18):

<Formula label="The Liénard–Wiechert scalar potential" tex="V(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|\left(1 - \frac{\vec v_p}{c} \cdot \frac{\vec r - \vec r_p}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|}\right)}" cite="reading1" at="slide 18, eq. (36) (boxed)" />

where $\vec r_p$ and $\vec v_p$ are the position and velocity of the particle at the retarded time $t_r$ (slide 18), found from $|\vec r - \vec r_p(t_r)| = c(t - t_r)$. For the vector potential, the current density of a point particle is (slide 18)

<Formula label="The current density of a point charge" tex="\vec J(\vec r, t) = q\,\vec v\,\delta^3\left(\vec r - \vec r_p(t)\right)" cite="reading1" at="slide 18, eq. (37)" />

and the same steps, with $\mu_0/4\pi$ in front, give (slide 18)

<Formula label="The Liénard–Wiechert vector potential" tex="\vec A(\vec r, t) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{q\,\vec v_p}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|\left(1 - \frac{\vec v_p}{c} \cdot \frac{\vec r - \vec r_p}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|}\right)} = \frac{\vec v_p}{c^2}\,V(\vec r, t)" cite="reading1" at="slide 18, eq. (38) (boxed)" />

using $\mu_0 = 1/(\varepsilon_0c^2)$ for the last step. These potentials were found around 1900 by Alfred-Marie Liénard and Emil Wiechert, independently, before special relativity.

## The fields of a point charge

The fields follow from $\vec E = -\nabla V - \partial\vec A/\partial t$ and $\vec B = \nabla \times \vec A$. The differentiation is long, because $\vec r_p$, $\vec v_p$ and $t_r$ all depend on $\vec r$ and $t$; in Guth's words, it takes several pages (slide 19). The result is (slide 19)

<Formula label="The electric field of a moving point charge" tex="\vec E(\vec r, t) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{|\vec r - \vec r_p|}{\left(\vec u \cdot (\vec r - \vec r_p)\right)^3}\left[(c^2 - v_p^2)\,\vec u + (\vec r - \vec r_p) \times (\vec u \times \vec a_p)\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 19, eq. (39) (boxed)" />

with

<Formula label="The vector u" tex="\vec u = c\,\frac{\vec r - \vec r_p}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|} - \vec v_p" cite="reading1" at="slide 19, eq. (40) (boxed)" />

and the magnetic field is fixed by the electric one (slide 19):

<Formula label="The magnetic field of a moving point charge" tex="\vec B(\vec r, t) = \frac{1}{c}\,\frac{\vec r - \vec r_p}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|} \times \vec E(\vec r, t)" cite="reading1" at="slide 19, eq. (41) (boxed)" />

Here $\vec r_p$, $\vec v_p$ and $\vec a_p$ are the position, velocity and acceleration of the particle at the retarded time (slide 20). The field has two parts. The first, with $c^2 - v_p^2$, depends only on the velocity and falls off as one over the distance squared, like the Coulomb field. The second contains the acceleration and falls off only as one over the distance: far away it dominates, and it is the **radiation** that the next notes study.

## Constant velocity

If the particle moves at constant velocity, the acceleration term of (39) is absent and $\vec E$ points along $\vec u$ (slide 20). Since $|\vec r - \vec r_p| = c(t - t_r)$, $\vec u$ can be rewritten (slide 20):

<Formula label="The vector u rewritten" tex="\vec u = \frac{c}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|}\left[\vec r - \left(\vec r_p + \vec v_p(t - t_r)\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 20, eq. (42)" />

For constant velocity, $\vec r_p + \vec v_p(t - t_r)$ is where the particle is *now*, at time $t$. So the electric field of a uniformly moving charge points straight away from its present position, although the field at $\vec r$ was determined by the charge at the earlier time $t_r$ (slide 20). The same result, found by a Lorentz transformation of the Coulomb field, is on the 8.033 page on [the 4-potential and the field of a moving charge](/courses/8-033/maxwell-equations-four-potential).

<WorkedExample title="A charge passing at 0.6c">
<Problem>
A charge $q = 1$ nC moves along the $x$ axis at constant speed $v = 0.6c$ and passes the origin at $t = 0$. Find, at $t = 0$, the potentials and fields at the point $\vec r = (0, 4, 0)$ m. Use $1/4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 8.988 \times 10^9$ m/F and $c = 2.998 \times 10^8$ m/s.
</Problem>
<Step title="The retarded time and position">
The charge is at $\vec r_p(t_r) = (vt_r, 0, 0)$, and $t_r$ must satisfy $|\vec r - \vec r_p(t_r)| = c(0 - t_r)$, that is $(0.6\,ct_r)^2 + 4^2 = (ct_r)^2$. So $0.64\,(ct_r)^2 = 16$ and $ct_r = -5$ m (the negative root: the past). The retarded position is $x_p = 0.6 \times (-5) = -3$ m, and the distance is $|\vec r - \vec r_p| = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$ m, which is indeed $c(t - t_r)$: light needs about 16.7 ns for it.
</Step>
<Step title="The potentials">
The unit vector from the retarded position is $(0.6, 0.8, 0)$, so $\frac{\vec v_p}{c} \cdot \frac{\vec r - \vec r_p}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|} = 0.6 \times 0.6 = 0.36$ and $|\vec r - \vec r_p|\,(1 - 0.36) = 5 \times 0.64 = 3.2$ m. Then $V = 8.988 \times 10^9 \times 10^{-9}/3.2 = 2.809$ V. A charge at rest 4 m away would give $8.988/4 = 2.247$ V, so the moving charge's potential is $4/3.2 = 1.25$ times larger, which is $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.6^2}$. The vector potential is along $x$, with $A_x = 0.6\,V/c = 0.6 \times 2.809/2.998 \times 10^8 = 5.62 \times 10^{-9}$ T m.
</Step>
<Step title="The vector u">
$\vec u = c\,(0.6, 0.8, 0) - (0.6c, 0, 0) = (0, 0.8c, 0)$. It points along $y$, straight from the origin, where the charge is now, to the field point, as (42) says.
</Step>
<Step title="The electric field">
With no acceleration, (39) gives $\vec E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{|\vec r - \vec r_p|\,(c^2 - v^2)}{\left(\vec u \cdot (\vec r - \vec r_p)\right)^3}\,\vec u$. Here $\vec u \cdot (\vec r - \vec r_p) = 0.8c \times 4 = 3.2c$ m, so the magnitude is $\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \times \frac{5 \times 0.64 \times 0.8}{3.2^3} = 8.988 \times 0.078125 = 0.702$ V/m, along $y$. The Coulomb field of a charge at rest 4 m away is $8.988/16 = 0.562$ V/m: again a factor $\gamma = 1.25$, the known result for the field straight to the side of a moving charge.
</Step>
<Step title="The magnetic field">
From (41), $\vec B = \frac{1}{c}(0.6, 0.8, 0) \times (0, E, 0) = \frac{0.6\,E}{c}\,\hat z$, so $B = 0.6 \times 0.702/2.998 \times 10^8 = 1.40 \times 10^{-9}$ T, about 1.4 nT along $z$. This is $\vec v \times \vec E/c^2$, as for a slowly moving charge in the [Biot–Savart law](/courses/8-02/biot-savart-law), but now exact.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Shaken electrons and the opaque early universe">
The acceleration term of (39) means that any charge that is shaken radiates. A free electron in a passing light wave is shaken by its electric field and re-radiates in all directions: it scatters the light. In the early universe, while hydrogen was ionized, free electrons scattered photons so often that the universe was opaque; once the electrons combined into atoms, the light went free, and we see it today as the cosmic microwave background. 8.286 follows this in [temperature and decoupling](/courses/8-286/temperature-and-decoupling); the next 8.07 notes find how much power an accelerated charge radiates (the Larmor formula), and use dipole radiation to explain the blue sky.
</Callout>

## Summary

- For a point charge, $\rho = q\,\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r_p(t))$. In the retarded potential the delta function's argument depends on the integration point through $t_r$, so $Z = \int d^3x'\,\delta^3(\vec r\,' - \vec r_p(t_r))$ is not 1.
- With $\delta(g(x)) = \sum_i\delta(x - x_i)/|g'(x_i)|$, $Z = \left(1 - \vec v \cdot (\vec r - \vec r\,')/(c|\vec r - \vec r\,'|)\right)^{-1}$, between $1/(1 + v/c)$ and $1/(1 - v/c)$.
- The **Liénard–Wiechert potentials**: $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|(1 - \vec v_p \cdot (\vec r - \vec r_p)/(c|\vec r - \vec r_p|))}$ and $\vec A = (\vec v_p/c^2)V$, with $\vec r_p$ and $\vec v_p$ at the retarded time.
- The fields: $\vec E$ has a velocity term along $\vec u = c(\vec r - \vec r_p)/|\vec r - \vec r_p| - \vec v_p$, falling as one over the distance squared, and an acceleration term falling as one over the distance; $\vec B = \frac{1}{c}\frac{\vec r - \vec r_p}{|\vec r - \vec r_p|} \times \vec E$.
- For constant velocity $\vec E$ points away from the charge's present position. In the worked example, a 1 nC charge at 0.6c passing 4 m away gives $V = 2.809$ V, $E = 0.702$ V/m and $B = 1.40$ nT, with $V$ and $E$ both $\gamma = 1.25$ times their static values.
