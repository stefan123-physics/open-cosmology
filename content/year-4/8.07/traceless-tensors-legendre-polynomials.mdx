Lecture Notes 9, dated Wednesday 17 October 2012, are Guth's complete write-up of the method that the lectures had been using: solving Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates with **traceless symmetric tensors** in place of the usual special functions. Guth explains why he wrote them (notes p. 1): he knows of no reference for this method. For the standard method with azimuthal symmetry he points to the course textbook, Griffiths, and for the spherical harmonics to Jackson's *Classical Electrodynamics*, 3rd edition, Sections 3.1, 3.2, 3.5 and 3.6. The [previous notes](/courses/8-07/spherical-harmonics-from-traceless-tensors) translate the tensors into the standard functions; logically, Guth says, they come after these, although they were posted first. He also thinks the tensor method is the simplest way into the subject, and that it extends easily: to analogues of spherical harmonics in more dimensions, and to the vector spherical harmonics used in the most general treatments of radiation (not part of this course).

The first half of the notes repeats, in more detail, what the [Lecture 6 page](/courses/8-07/separation-of-variables-spherical) derived, so it is summarized briefly here. This page takes the notes up to the Legendre polynomials (Sections 1 to 6); the [next page](/courses/8-07/multipole-expansion-traceless-tensors) covers the multipole expansion (Section 7). (The notes write the potential as a curly Greek phi; as on the earlier pages, this page uses $V$ and keeps $\phi$ for the azimuthal angle. Our own words and our own worked example; the notes have no figures.)

## Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates

In spherical coordinates Laplace's equation reads (notes p. 1)

<Formula label="Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates" tex="\nabla^2 V(r, \theta, \phi) = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\nabla_\theta^2 V = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.1)" />

with the angular part

<Formula label="The angular Laplacian" tex="\nabla_\theta^2 V = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2 V}{\partial\phi^2}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.2)" />

(Guth remarks that $\nabla^2_{\theta,\phi}$ would be the more logical name, but tiresome to write.) The radial term can also be written in a form that is sometimes handy (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Another form of the radial term" tex="\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2\frac{\partial V}{\partial r}\right) = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2}\left(rV\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.3)" />

Both sides equal $\partial^2 V/\partial r^2 + (2/r)\,\partial V/\partial r$, as differentiating out shows. Separation of variables tries a product (notes p. 2),

<Formula label="A product of radial and angular parts" tex="V(r, \theta, \phi) = R(r)\,F(\theta, \phi)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.4)" />

and multiplying Laplace's equation by $r^2/(RF)$ gives

<Formula label="The separated equation" tex="0 = \frac{r^2}{RF}\,\nabla^2 V = \frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) + \frac{1}{F}\,\nabla_\theta^2 F" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.5)" />

The first term depends only on $r$ and the second only on the angles, so each is a constant:

<Formula label="The angular equation" tex="\frac{1}{F}\,\nabla_\theta^2 F = C_\theta" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.6)" />

<Formula label="The radial equation" tex="\frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) = -C_\theta" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.7)" />

## Functions of direction as tensor series

The angular equation, rewritten as $\nabla_\theta^2 F = C_\theta F$, asks for the **eigenfunctions** of $\nabla_\theta^2$ and their **eigenvalues** $C_\theta$ (notes p. 2, eq. (9.8)). A function of the two angles is the same thing as a function of the unit vector pointing in that direction,

<Formula label="The unit vector in the direction theta, phi" tex="\hat n = \sin\theta\cos\phi\,\hat e_1 + \sin\theta\sin\phi\,\hat e_2 + \cos\theta\,\hat e_3" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.9)" />

where $\hat e_1, \hat e_2, \hat e_3$ are also called $\hat e_x, \hat e_y, \hat e_z$ (numbers when indices are summed, letters otherwise). Guth then claims, without proof, that the most general function of direction is a power series in the components of $\hat n$; it is true at least for square-integrable, piecewise continuous functions (notes pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="The power series in n hat" tex="F(\hat n) = C^{(0)} + C^{(1)}_i\,\hat n_i + C^{(2)}_{ij}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j + \cdots + C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\hat n_{i_2}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell} + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.10)" />

Repeated indices are summed from 1 to 3. The coefficients are **tensors**, and the number of indices is the **rank**: $C^{(0)}$ is a scalar, $C^{(1)}$ a vector, $C^{(2)}$ a matrix. Two restrictions can be imposed on them without restricting the functions the series can represent (notes p. 3). The tensors can be taken **symmetric**, unchanged by any reordering of the indices,

<Formula label="Symmetric" tex="C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_\ell} = C^{(\ell)}_{j_1 j_2 \cdots j_\ell}, \qquad \{j_1, \ldots, j_\ell\} \text{ any permutation of } \{i_1, \ldots, i_\ell\}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.11)" />

because an antisymmetric part would cancel against the symmetric product of the $\hat n$'s. And they can be taken **traceless**: setting any two indices equal and summing gives zero, and by the symmetry it is enough to say so for the last two,

<Formula label="Traceless" tex="C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_{\ell - 2}\,jj} = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.12)" />

The argument, given in the notes for $\ell = 2$ (notes pp. 3–4), is the one on the Lecture 6 page. If $C^{(2)}_{ii} = \lambda \neq 0$, then $\tilde C^{(2)}_{ij} = C^{(2)}_{ij} - \frac13\lambda\,\delta_{ij}$ has zero trace because $\delta_{ii} = 3$, and since $\delta_{ij}\hat n_i\hat n_j = 1$ for a unit vector,

<Formula label="The trace moves into the constant term" tex="C^{(0)} + C^{(1)}_i\,\hat n_i + C^{(2)}_{ij}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j = \tilde C^{(0)} + C^{(1)}_i\,\hat n_i + \tilde C^{(2)}_{ij}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j, \qquad \tilde C^{(0)} = C^{(0)} + \frac13\lambda" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (9.13)–(9.18)" />

Guth calls the term of rank $\ell$ (notes p. 4)

<Formula label="The term of degree l" tex="F_\ell(\hat n) = C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\hat n_{i_2}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.19)" />

## Each term is an eigenfunction

The trick (notes pp. 4–5) is to bring back a radius, $\vec r = r\,\hat n = x_i\,\hat e_i$ (eqs. (9.20)–(9.21), with Griffiths' names $x, y, z$ or $x_i$ for the coordinates), and to define a function in space by putting $x_i$ wherever $F_\ell$ has $\hat n_i$:

<Formula label="The term extended off the sphere" tex="\tilde F_\ell(\vec r) = C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_\ell}\,x_{i_1}x_{i_2}\cdots x_{i_\ell} = r^\ell\,F_\ell(\hat n)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.22)" />

Its full Laplacian is easy in Cartesian coordinates. For $\ell = 0$ and $\ell = 1$ it vanishes trivially (eqs. (9.23)–(9.24)). For $\ell = 2$ (notes p. 5),

<Formula label="The Laplacian of the term of degree 2" tex="\nabla^2\tilde F_2 = C^{(2)}_{ij}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_m}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_m}\left(x_i x_j\right) = C^{(2)}_{ij}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_m}\left[\delta_{im}\,x_j + \delta_{jm}\,x_i\right] = 2\,C^{(2)}_{ij}\,\delta_{im}\,\delta_{jm} = 2\,C^{(2)}_{ii} = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.25)" />

and for $\ell = 3$ the same steps leave terms like $C^{(3)}_{iik}\,x_k$, plus the ones that symmetrize it in $ijk$, each zero because the tensor is traceless (eq. (9.26)). In general, the two derivatives of the Laplacian use up two factors of $x$, so $\ell - 2$ factors of $x$ remain while $C^{(\ell)}$ still has $\ell$ indices, all summed; two of them must be contracted with each other, and the trace kills the result (notes p. 6). Hence

<Formula label="Every term is harmonic" tex="\nabla^2\tilde F_\ell(\vec r) = 0 \quad \text{for all } \ell" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.27)" />

Putting $\tilde F_\ell = r^\ell F_\ell(\hat n)$ into eq. (9.1), the radial part gives $r^{\ell - 2}\,\ell(\ell + 1)F_\ell(\hat n)$ and the angular part $r^{\ell - 2}\,\nabla_\theta^2 F_\ell(\hat n)$, so (notes p. 6)

<Formula label="The eigenvalue equation" tex="\nabla_\theta^2 F_\ell(\hat n) = -\ell(\ell + 1)\,F_\ell(\hat n)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.28)" />

"This is a very useful result!", Guth writes: the eigenfunctions are the $F_\ell$, and the eigenvalues are $-\ell(\ell + 1)$.

## The general solution

Since every function of direction is a sum of the $F_\ell$, the separation constant can only be $C_\theta = -\ell(\ell + 1)$ with $\ell$ a whole number, and the radial equation becomes (notes p. 6)

<Formula label="The radial equation for degree l" tex="\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) = \ell(\ell + 1)\,R" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.29)" />

Trying $R = r^p$ gives

<Formula label="The condition on the power" tex="p(p + 1) = \ell(\ell + 1)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.30)" />

with the two roots $p = \ell$ and $p = -(\ell + 1)$. Two independent solutions of a second-order linear equation give all of them (notes p. 7):

<Formula label="The radial solutions" tex="R_\ell(r) = A_\ell\,r^\ell + \frac{B_\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.31)" />

and the most general solution of Laplace's equation in spherical coordinates is

<Formula label="The general solution of Laplace's equation" tex="V(\vec r) = \sum_{\ell = 0}^{\infty}\left(A_\ell\,r^\ell + \frac{B_\ell}{r^{\ell + 1}}\right)C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 i_2 \cdots i_\ell}\,\hat n_{i_1}\hat n_{i_2}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.32)" />

with arbitrary constants $A_\ell$, $B_\ell$ and arbitrary traceless symmetric tensors $C^{(\ell)}$. Compared with the standard expansion, the term of degree $\ell$ is the sum of all the terms with that $\ell$ and every $m$ from $-\ell$ to $\ell$: $2\ell + 1$ spherical harmonics $Y_{\ell m}$ (notes p. 7).

## Counting the traceless symmetric tensors

For this to fit, a traceless symmetric tensor of rank $\ell$ must have exactly $2\ell + 1$ independent components (notes pp. 7–8). Count the symmetric ones first, calling them $S$ to keep $C$ for traceless ones. $S^{(0)}$ is one number and $S^{(1)}_i$ three. $S^{(2)}_{ij}$ has nine entries, but $S^{(2)}_{ij} = S^{(2)}_{ji}$ leaves six, those with the index pairs 11, 12, 13, 22, 23 and 33.

Since the order of the indices does not matter, write them in ascending order. Then an independent component is fixed by saying how many indices are 1, how many are 2 and how many are 3. Guth's picture: $\ell$ identical balls (the indices) put into three hats labelled 1, 2 and 3. Nine balls with three in the first hat, two in the second and four in the third, for example, stand for the component of the rank-9 tensor $S^{(9)}$ with the indices 111223333.

To count the ways, label the balls for a moment, and add two dividers (one fewer than the hats), also labelled. The $\ell + 2$ distinct objects can be lined up in $(\ell + 2)!$ orders, and each order puts the balls left of the first divider in hat 1, those between the dividers in hat 2 and the rest in hat 3. Reordering the balls among themselves ($\ell\,!$ ways) or swapping the two dividers ($2!$ ways) gives the same filling, so the number of independent symmetric tensors of rank $\ell$ is (notes p. 8; the notes label it "sym", this page $S$)

<Formula label="The number of symmetric tensors of rank l" tex="N_{S}(\ell) = \frac{(\ell + 2)!}{\ell\,!\,2!} = \frac12(\ell + 1)(\ell + 2)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.33)" />

which gives 1, 3 and 6 for $\ell = 0, 1, 2$, as found above. Tracelessness adds the conditions

<Formula label="The trace conditions" tex="S^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_{\ell - 2}\,jj} = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.34)" />

whose left side is itself a symmetric tensor of rank $\ell - 2$, with free indices $i_1 \ldots i_{\ell - 2}$: that is $N_{S}(\ell - 2)$ conditions. So the number of traceless symmetric ones, $N_{C}$ (labelled "traceless-sym" in the notes), is

<Formula label="The number of traceless symmetric tensors of rank l" tex="N_{C}(\ell) = N_{S}(\ell) - N_{S}(\ell - 2) = \frac12(\ell + 1)(\ell + 2) - \frac12(\ell - 1)\ell = 2\ell + 1" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.35)" />

(For $\ell = 0$ and $1$ there are no trace conditions, and the formula $\frac12(\ell - 1)\ell$ duly gives zero.) The count agrees with the $2\ell + 1$ values of $m$, as it must: the spherical harmonic expansion is eq. (9.32) written in one particular basis of the traceless symmetric tensors of each rank (notes p. 8).

## Azimuthal symmetry and the traceless part

With **azimuthal symmetry**, nothing depends on $\phi$: the problem is unchanged by rotations about the $z$-axis (notes p. 9). The tensors must then be unchanged by those rotations too, and the natural way to build them is from $\hat z$, the unit vector along the axis, whose components are

<Formula label="The components of z hat" tex="\hat z_i = \delta_{i3}" cite="reading1" at="p. 9" />

(Guth writes $\hat z$ rather than $\hat e_z$ here on purpose: the components $(\hat e_z)_i$ would be a pain to write, and $\hat z$ recalls the basis $\hat u^{+}$, $\hat u^{-}$, $\hat z$ of Lecture Notes 8.) The product $\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}$ is symmetric; its **traceless symmetric part**, written with curly brackets, subtracts terms with one or more Kronecker deltas so that every trace vanishes. Scalars and vectors are traceless already, and for rank 2 the trace $\hat z \cdot \hat z = 1$ is removed with a third of $\delta_{ij}$, since $\delta_{ii} = 3$ (notes p. 9):

<Formula label="Ranks 0, 1 and 2" tex="\{1\} = 1, \qquad \{\hat z_i\} = \hat z_i, \qquad \{\hat z_i\hat z_j\} = \hat z_i\hat z_j - \frac13\,\delta_{ij}" cite="reading1" at="eqs. (9.36)–(9.38)" />

At rank 3 the trace of $\hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k$ over $i$ and $j$ is $\hat z_k$, and the subtraction must be symmetrized (notes p. 10):

<Formula label="Rank 3" tex="\{\hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\} = \hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k - \frac15\left(\hat z_i\,\delta_{jk} + \hat z_j\,\delta_{ik} + \hat z_k\,\delta_{ij}\right)" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.39)" />

To check the $\frac15$, sum over $i = j$:

<Formula label="The trace of the rank-3 part" tex="\delta_{ij}\{\hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\} = \hat z_k - \frac15\left(\hat z_k + \hat z_k + 3\hat z_k\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.40)" />

At rank 4 there are terms with one delta and terms with two, and both are needed. Guth leaves their coefficients open (notes p. 10):

<Formula label="Rank 4 with unknown coefficients" tex="\begin{aligned} \{\hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\hat z_m\} = \hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\hat z_m &+ c_1\left(\hat z_i\hat z_j\,\delta_{km} + \hat z_i\hat z_k\,\delta_{mj} + \hat z_i\hat z_m\,\delta_{jk} + \hat z_j\hat z_k\,\delta_{im} + \hat z_j\hat z_m\,\delta_{ik} + \hat z_k\hat z_m\,\delta_{ij}\right) \\ &+ c_2\left(\delta_{ij}\,\delta_{km} + \delta_{ik}\,\delta_{jm} + \delta_{im}\,\delta_{jk}\right) \end{aligned}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.41)" />

(Here $m$ is simply a fourth index.) Summing over $i = j$: the first term gives $\hat z_k\hat z_m$; in the $c_1$ bracket the first term gives $\delta_{km}$, the next four $\hat z_k\hat z_m$ each and the last $3\hat z_k\hat z_m$; in the $c_2$ bracket the terms give $3\delta_{km}$, $\delta_{km}$ and $\delta_{km}$. Altogether

<Formula label="The trace of the rank-4 part" tex="\delta_{ij}\{\hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\hat z_m\} = \left(1 + 7c_1\right)\hat z_k\hat z_m + \left(c_1 + 5c_2\right)\delta_{km}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.42)" />

For this to vanish for every $k$ and $m$, both brackets must be zero, so $c_1 = -\frac17$ and $c_2 = \frac{1}{35}$ (notes p. 10):

<Formula label="Rank 4" tex="\begin{aligned} \{\hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\hat z_m\} &= \hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\hat z_m - \frac17\left(\hat z_i\hat z_j\,\delta_{km} + \hat z_i\hat z_k\,\delta_{mj} + \hat z_i\hat z_m\,\delta_{jk} + \hat z_j\hat z_k\,\delta_{im} + \hat z_j\hat z_m\,\delta_{ik} + \hat z_k\hat z_m\,\delta_{ij}\right) \\ &\quad + \frac{1}{35}\left(\delta_{ij}\,\delta_{km} + \delta_{ik}\,\delta_{jm} + \delta_{im}\,\delta_{jk}\right) \end{aligned}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.43)" />

## The Legendre polynomials

Are these the only traceless symmetric tensors that rotations about $z$ leave alone? Yes, up to a constant, and the reason comes from Lecture Notes 8 (notes pp. 10–11). There a basis of $2\ell + 1$ traceless symmetric tensors of rank $\ell$ is built whose contractions with $\hat n$ depend on the azimuthal angle as $e^{im\phi}$, for $m = -\ell, \ldots, \ell$. These are orthogonal over a full turn,

<Formula label="Orthogonality of the azimuthal factors" tex="\int_0^{2\pi}e^{-im'\phi}\,e^{im\phi}\,d\phi = 2\pi\,\delta_{m'm}" cite="reading1" at="p. 11" />

so a combination that does not depend on $\phi$ can contain only the $m = 0$ basis tensor, which is the traceless part of the product of $\hat z$'s. A function of $\theta$ alone, an azimuthally symmetric function of $\hat n$, can therefore be expanded as (notes p. 11)

<Formula label="The expansion of an azimuthally symmetric function" tex="F(\theta) = c_0 + c_1\,\{\hat z_i\}\,\hat n_i + c_2\,\{\hat z_i\hat z_j\}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j + \cdots + c_\ell\,\{\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}\,\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell} + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.44)" />

with constants $c_\ell$ (unrelated to the $c_1$, $c_2$ of rank 4). This is the standard **expansion in Legendre polynomials**, and with $\hat z \cdot \hat n = \cos\theta$ the functions can be read off directly: each Kronecker delta joins two factors of $\hat n$ into $\hat n \cdot \hat n = 1$. Contracting the tensors above with $\hat n$ gives (notes p. 11)

<Formula label="The traceless tensors contracted with n hat" tex="\begin{aligned} \{1\} &= 1, \qquad \{\hat z_i\}\,\hat n_i = \cos\theta, \qquad \{\hat z_i\hat z_j\}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j = \cos^2\theta - \frac13, \\ \{\hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j\hat n_k &= \cos^3\theta - \frac35\cos\theta, \qquad \{\hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\hat z_m\}\,\hat n_i\hat n_j\hat n_k\hat n_m = \cos^4\theta - \frac67\cos^2\theta + \frac{3}{35} \end{aligned}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (9.45)" />

Up to their normalization (fixed on the [Lecture 8 page](/courses/8-07/spherical-harmonics-from-traceless-tensors), which multiplies each by $(2\ell)!/(2^\ell(\ell\,!)^2)$), these are the Legendre polynomials $P_\ell(\cos\theta)$: for example $\frac32\left(\cos^2\theta - \frac13\right) = P_2(\cos\theta)$. The coefficients come out of the trace condition alone, with no differential equation to solve.

<WorkedExample title="A sphere held at a potential proportional to cos³θ">
<Problem>
A spherical shell of radius $a = 5$ cm, with no charge inside or outside it, is held at the potential $V_0\cos^3\theta$ with $V_0 = 100$ V (by a suitable surface charge), and the potential vanishes far away. Find the potential everywhere, and evaluate it on the axis ($\theta = 0$) at 2.5 cm and 10 cm from the centre, and at 2.5 cm in the direction $\theta = 60°$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Split the boundary value into traceless parts">
$\cos^3\theta = \hat z_i\hat z_j\hat z_k\,\hat n_i\hat n_j\hat n_k$. By eq. (9.45) the traceless part gives $\cos^3\theta - \frac35\cos\theta$, so $\cos^3\theta = \left(\cos^3\theta - \frac35\cos\theta\right) + \frac35\cos\theta$: an $\ell = 3$ term and an $\ell = 1$ term, with no $\ell = 0$ or $\ell = 2$ part. (Removing the trace of a rank-3 tensor moves something into rank 1, two steps down, never into rank 2.)
</Step>
<Step title="Inside and outside">
Each term gets the radial factor of eq. (9.31) that stays finite, matched to the boundary at $r = a$. Inside, $V = V_0\left[\frac35\,\frac{r}{a}\cos\theta + \frac{r^3}{a^3}\left(\cos^3\theta - \frac35\cos\theta\right)\right]$. Outside, $V = V_0\left[\frac35\,\frac{a^2}{r^2}\cos\theta + \frac{a^4}{r^4}\left(\cos^3\theta - \frac35\cos\theta\right)\right]$. Both equal $V_0\cos^3\theta$ at $r = a$. As a check on the inside solution, with $z = r\cos\theta$ it is $V_0\left[\frac35\,z/a + \left(z^3 - \frac35 z\,r^2\right)/a^3\right]$, and the Laplacian of $z^3 - \frac35 z(x^2 + y^2 + z^2)$ is $6z - \frac35(2z + 2z + 6z) = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
At the centre $V = 0$: the average of $\cos^3\theta$ over the sphere is zero. On the axis at 2.5 cm, $r/a = \frac12$: $V = 100 \times \left(\frac35 \times \frac12 + \frac18 \times \frac25\right) = 30 + 5 = 35$ V. On the axis at 10 cm, $a/r = \frac12$: $V = 100 \times \left(\frac35 \times \frac14 + \frac{1}{16} \times \frac25\right) = 15 + 2.5 = 17.5$ V. At 2.5 cm and $\theta = 60°$, $\cos\theta = \frac12$: $V = 100 \times \left(\frac35 \times \frac12 \times \frac12 + \frac18\left(\frac18 - \frac{3}{10}\right)\right) = 15 - 2.19 \approx 12.8$ V. Far away the $\ell = 1$ term, falling as $1/r^2$, outlasts the $\ell = 3$ term, falling as $1/r^4$: the shell looks like a dipole, the subject of the next page.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- **Separation:** with $V = R(r)F(\theta, \phi)$, Laplace's equation splits into $\nabla_\theta^2 F = C_\theta F$ and a radial equation; any function of direction is a series $\sum_\ell C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}$ whose tensors can be taken symmetric and traceless.
- **Eigenfunctions:** $C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}x_{i_1}\cdots x_{i_\ell} = r^\ell F_\ell(\hat n)$ is harmonic because $C^{(\ell)}$ is traceless, so $\nabla_\theta^2 F_\ell = -\ell(\ell + 1)F_\ell$, and the general solution is $\sum_\ell (A_\ell r^\ell + B_\ell/r^{\ell + 1})C^{(\ell)}_{i_1 \cdots i_\ell}\hat n_{i_1}\cdots\hat n_{i_\ell}$.
- **Counting:** balls in three hats give $N_{S}(\ell) = \frac12(\ell + 1)(\ell + 2)$ symmetric tensors of rank $\ell$; the trace conditions are a symmetric tensor of rank $\ell - 2$, leaving $2\ell + 1$, one for each $Y_{\ell m}$.
- **Azimuthal symmetry:** only the traceless parts $\{\hat z_{i_1}\cdots\hat z_{i_\ell}\}$ are allowed, made by subtracting Kronecker deltas whose coefficients the trace fixes ($-\frac13$ at rank 2, $-\frac15$ at rank 3, $-\frac17$ and $+\frac{1}{35}$ at rank 4).
- **Legendre polynomials:** contracted with $\hat n$ they give $1$, $\cos\theta$, $\cos^2\theta - \frac13$, $\cos^3\theta - \frac35\cos\theta$, $\cos^4\theta - \frac67\cos^2\theta + \frac{3}{35}$, the $P_\ell(\cos\theta)$ up to normalization.
- **Example:** a sphere at $V_0\cos^3\theta$ has only $\ell = 1$ and $\ell = 3$ parts; with $V_0 = 100$ V and $a = 5$ cm the potential is 35 V on the axis at 2.5 cm and 17.5 V at 10 cm.
