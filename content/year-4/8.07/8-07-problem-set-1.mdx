The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes. An index $i$, $j$, $k$, $n$ or $m$ runs over 1, 2, 3, which stand for $x$, $y$, $z$, so $A_i$ is one of the components of $\vec A$ and $x_i$ is one of the coordinates. An index that appears twice in one term is summed from 1 to 3 (the summation convention); MIT's sheet uses this convention from Problem 1 on, and so does this page. The Kronecker delta $\delta_{ij}$ is 1 when $i = j$ and 0 otherwise. The symbol $\partial_i$ is short for $\partial/\partial x_i$, so the gradient of a function $f$ has the components $\partial_i f$ and the divergence of $\vec A$ is $\partial_i A_i$. The unit vectors along the axes are $\hat e_x$, $\hat e_y$, $\hat e_z$, and $r$ is the distance from the origin.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Vector identities involving cross products" points={20} mit="Problem 1">

The Levi-Civita symbol $\epsilon_{ijk}$ makes cross products easy to handle. It is defined by

<Formula label="The Levi-Civita symbol" tex="\epsilon_{ijk} = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{if } ijk \text{ is } 123,\ 231 \text{ or } 312 \\ -1 & \text{if } ijk \text{ is } 213,\ 321 \text{ or } 132 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, eq. (1.1.1), p. 1" />

So $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is zero unless its three indices are all different. It is $+1$ when they are 123 in cyclic order and $-1$ in the opposite order. It is totally antisymmetric: swapping any two indices changes its sign, and moving the indices round in a cycle changes nothing.

<Formula label="Antisymmetry of the Levi-Civita symbol" tex="\epsilon_{ijk} = -\epsilon_{ikj} = \epsilon_{kij}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, eq. (1.1.2), p. 1" />

With it, component $i$ of a cross product is a double sum, written with the summation convention as

<Formula label="The cross product in index notation" tex="\left(\vec A\times\vec B\right)_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\,A_j\,B_k" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, eq. (1.1.3), p. 1" />

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Starting from the definition of $\epsilon_{ijk}$, show that

<Formula label="The product of two Levi-Civita symbols" tex="\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{inm} = \delta_{jn}\,\delta_{km} - \delta_{jm}\,\delta_{kn}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), eq. (1.1.4), p. 1" />

Here $i$ is summed over, and $j$, $k$, $n$ and $m$ are free indices: the equation holds for each of their $3^4 = 81$ sets of values.

The two boxes are our addition. Use the identity to find the number $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{ijk}$ (all three indices summed) and the quantity $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{ijm}$ (summed over $i$ and $j$).

<Hint>

You do not need to check 81 cases one by one. Sort them: what happens to both sides when $j = k$? And when $j \neq k$, how many values of $i$ give a term that is not zero?

</Hint>

<Hint>

For $j \neq k$ only the one value of $i$ that differs from both contributes. Then $\epsilon_{inm}$ is not zero only when $n$ and $m$ are the same pair as $j$ and $k$, in the same order or swapped.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the boxes: set $n = j$ in the identity and sum over $j$. Remember that $\delta_{jj}$ is a sum of three terms.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-full" label="$\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{ijk} =$" value="6" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-full" />

<Answer id="p1-two" label="$\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{ijm} =$" choices={["δₖₘ", "3 δₖₘ", "2 δₖₘ", "6 δₖₘ"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both sides are small tables of numbers. Sorting the cases by the pair $(j, k)$ leaves only a few to compare.

<Step title="The case j = k">

If $j = k$, every $\epsilon_{ijk}$ on the left has two equal indices, so the left side is zero. The right side is $\delta_{jn}\delta_{jm} - \delta_{jm}\delta_{jn} = 0$ as well.

</Step>

<Step title="The case j ≠ k">

Now $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is zero unless $i$ is the one value that differs from both $j$ and $k$. So the sum over $i$ has a single term, with $\epsilon_{ijk} = \pm 1$. The other factor, $\epsilon_{inm}$ with that same $i$, is not zero only if $n$ and $m$ are the two values other than $i$, which are $j$ and $k$. There are three possibilities:

- $n = j$ and $m = k$. The left side is $(\epsilon_{ijk})^2 = 1$. The right side is $1\cdot 1 - \delta_{jk}\delta_{kj} = 1$, because $j \neq k$.
- $n = k$ and $m = j$. The left side is $\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{ikj} = -(\epsilon_{ijk})^2 = -1$. The right side is $\delta_{jk}\delta_{kj} - 1\cdot 1 = -1$.
- Any other $n$, $m$. The left side is zero. On the right, $\delta_{jn}\delta_{km}$ needs $n = j$ and $m = k$, and $\delta_{jm}\delta_{kn}$ needs $m = j$ and $n = k$; neither holds, so the right side is zero too.

The two sides agree in every case, which proves the identity.

</Step>

<Step title="The two contractions">

Set $n = j$ and sum over $j$. Since $\delta_{jj} = 3$ and $\delta_{jm}\delta_{kj} = \delta_{km}$,

<Formula label="Two indices summed" tex="\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{ijm} = \delta_{jj}\,\delta_{km} - \delta_{jm}\,\delta_{kj} = 3\,\delta_{km} - \delta_{km} = 2\,\delta_{km}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 1 (our own addition)" />

Setting $m = k$ and summing once more gives $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{ijk} = 2\,\delta_{kk} = 6$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The number 6 can be counted directly: $\epsilon_{ijk}\epsilon_{ijk}$ is the sum of the squares of all 27 entries, and exactly six of them (the six orders of 1, 2, 3) are $\pm 1$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Use the index form of the cross product and the identity of part (a) to show that, for any three vectors,

<Formula label="The triple cross product" tex="\vec A\times\left(\vec B\times\vec C\right) = \vec B\left(\vec A\cdot\vec C\right) - \vec C\left(\vec A\cdot\vec B\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), eq. (1.1.5), p. 1" />

The number box is our addition. Take $\vec A = (1, 2, -1)$, $\vec B = (3, 0, 2)$ and $\vec C = (-2, 1, 4)$ and give the $x$ component of $\vec A\times(\vec B\times\vec C)$.

<Hint>

Write component $i$ of the left side with two Levi-Civita symbols. Give the inner cross product its own index names, so that no index appears more than twice.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The identity of part (a) needs the summed index in the first place of both symbols. A cyclic shift, $\epsilon_{ijk} = \epsilon_{kij}$, puts it there.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-bx" label="$x$ component of $\vec A\times(\vec B\times\vec C)$:" value="-10" tolerance="1%" calc="p1-bx" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two cross products give two Levi-Civita symbols that share one summed index. Part (a) turns them into Kronecker deltas, and each delta just renames an index.

<Step title="Write it with indices">

The inner product is $(\vec B\times\vec C)_k = \epsilon_{knm}B_nC_m$, so

<Formula label="The triple product with indices" tex="\left[\vec A\times\left(\vec B\times\vec C\right)\right]_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\,A_j\,\epsilon_{knm}\,B_n\,C_m = \epsilon_{kij}\,\epsilon_{knm}\,A_j\,B_n\,C_m" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Use the identity">

With $k$ as the summed index, part (a) gives $\epsilon_{kij}\epsilon_{knm} = \delta_{in}\delta_{jm} - \delta_{im}\delta_{jn}$. So

<Formula label="The deltas rename the indices" tex="\left[\vec A\times\left(\vec B\times\vec C\right)\right]_i = \left(\delta_{in}\,\delta_{jm} - \delta_{im}\,\delta_{jn}\right)A_j\,B_n\,C_m = B_i\,A_j\,C_j - C_i\,A_j\,B_j" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

Since $A_jC_j = \vec A\cdot\vec C$ and $A_jB_j = \vec A\cdot\vec B$, this is component $i$ of $\vec B(\vec A\cdot\vec C) - \vec C(\vec A\cdot\vec B)$.

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

Here $\vec A\cdot\vec C = -2 + 2 - 4 = -4$ and $\vec A\cdot\vec B = 3 + 0 - 2 = 1$, so

<Formula label="The triple product for the three given vectors" tex="\vec A\times\left(\vec B\times\vec C\right) = -4\,(3, 0, 2) - (-2, 1, 4) = (-10, -1, -12)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 1 (our own numbers)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Directly: $\vec B\times\vec C = (-2, -16, 3)$, and the $x$ component of $\vec A\times(\vec B\times\vec C)$ is $A_y\cdot 3 - A_z\cdot(-16) = 6 - 16 = -10$. Also, the result must be perpendicular to $\vec A$: $(1, 2, -1)\cdot(-10, -1, -12) = -10 - 2 + 12 = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

In the same way, show that for any two vector fields

<Formula label="The divergence of a cross product" tex="\nabla\cdot\left(\vec A\times\vec B\right) = \vec B\cdot\left(\nabla\times\vec A\right) - \vec A\cdot\left(\nabla\times\vec B\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), eq. (1.1.6), p. 2" />

<Hint>

The divergence is $\partial_i$ acting on component $i$. The derivative acts on a product $A_jB_k$: use the product rule. The numbers $\epsilon_{ijk}$ are constants.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In index notation a curl is $(\nabla\times\vec A)_k = \epsilon_{kij}\,\partial_iA_j$. Shift or swap the indices of each $\epsilon$ until you recognise one, and keep track of the sign.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The product rule gives two terms. In each, the $\epsilon$ and the derivative together form a curl.

<Step title="Product rule">

<Formula label="The divergence of a cross product with indices" tex="\nabla\cdot\left(\vec A\times\vec B\right) = \partial_i\left(\epsilon_{ijk}\,A_j\,B_k\right) = \epsilon_{ijk}\left(\partial_iA_j\right)B_k + \epsilon_{ijk}\,A_j\left(\partial_iB_k\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Recognise the curls">

In the first term $\epsilon_{ijk} = \epsilon_{kij}$, and $\epsilon_{kij}\,\partial_iA_j = (\nabla\times\vec A)_k$. In the second term swap the first two indices, $\epsilon_{ijk} = -\epsilon_{jik}$, and $\epsilon_{jik}\,\partial_iB_k = (\nabla\times\vec B)_j$. So

<Formula label="The two curls" tex="\nabla\cdot\left(\vec A\times\vec B\right) = B_k\left(\nabla\times\vec A\right)_k - A_j\left(\nabla\times\vec B\right)_j = \vec B\cdot\left(\nabla\times\vec A\right) - \vec A\cdot\left(\nabla\times\vec B\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Swapping $\vec A$ and $\vec B$ changes the sign of the left side, because the cross product changes sign. The right side changes sign too. No identity of part (a) was needed: one $\epsilon$ is enough for one cross product.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={4}>

Show that for any vector field $\vec A$, with $A^2 = \vec A\cdot\vec A$,

<Formula label="A field crossed with its own curl" tex="\vec A\times\left(\nabla\times\vec A\right) = \frac12\,\nabla\left(A^2\right) - \left(\vec A\cdot\nabla\right)\vec A" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), eq. (1.1.7), p. 2" />

The box is our addition. For the field $\vec A = (-ky,\ kx,\ 0)$, with a constant $k$, give the $x$ component of $\vec A\times(\nabla\times\vec A)$.

<Hint>

Proceed as in part (b), with $\vec B$ replaced by $\nabla$. The one new rule: a derivative acts on what stands to its right, so do not move $A_j$ past $\partial_n$ unless the derivative is not meant to act on it.

</Hint>

<Hint>

One of the two terms is $A_j\,\partial_iA_j$. Compare it with $\partial_i(A_jA_j)$.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same steps as in part (b), with the order of the factors kept, so that the derivative still acts on the last $\vec A$ only.

<Step title="With indices">

<Formula label="A field crossed with its curl, with indices" tex="\left[\vec A\times\left(\nabla\times\vec A\right)\right]_i = \epsilon_{kij}\,\epsilon_{knm}\,A_j\,\partial_nA_m = \left(\delta_{in}\,\delta_{jm} - \delta_{im}\,\delta_{jn}\right)A_j\,\partial_nA_m = A_j\,\partial_iA_j - A_j\,\partial_jA_i" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Name the two terms">

By the product rule $\partial_i(A_jA_j) = 2A_j\,\partial_iA_j$, so the first term is $\frac12\partial_i(A^2)$, component $i$ of $\frac12\nabla(A^2)$. The second term is the operator $A_j\partial_j = \vec A\cdot\nabla$ acting on $A_i$. That is the identity.

</Step>

<Step title="The example">

For $\vec A = (-ky, kx, 0)$ the curl is $(0, 0, 2k)$, and

<Formula label="The example, directly" tex="\vec A\times\left(\nabla\times\vec A\right) = \left(A_y\cdot 2k,\ -A_x\cdot 2k,\ 0\right) = \left(2k^2x,\ 2k^2y,\ 0\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(d), p. 2 (our own example)" />

<Step title="Check the result">

The identity gives the same. Here $A^2 = k^2(x^2 + y^2)$, so $\frac12\nabla(A^2) = (k^2x, k^2y, 0)$. And $\vec A\cdot\nabla = -ky\,\partial_x + kx\,\partial_y$ acting on $(-ky, kx, 0)$ gives $(-k^2x, -k^2y, 0)$. The difference is $(2k^2x, 2k^2y, 0)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

Show that for any two vector fields

<Formula label="The curl of a cross product" tex="\nabla\times\left(\vec A\times\vec B\right) = \left(\vec B\cdot\nabla\right)\vec A - \left(\vec A\cdot\nabla\right)\vec B + \vec A\left(\nabla\cdot\vec B\right) - \vec B\left(\nabla\cdot\vec A\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(e), eq. ## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Triple cross products (our own problem)" points={10} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet gives only the numbers of two textbook problems here, under the title "Triple cross products". The problem below is our own, on the same subject. It uses the result of Problem 1(b).

<Part label="a" points={4}>

In a triple cross product the brackets matter. Take the three vectors of Problem 1(b), $\vec A = (1, 2, -1)$, $\vec B = (3, 0, 2)$ and $\vec C = (-2, 1, 4)$, for which $\vec A\times(\vec B\times\vec C) = (-10, -1, -12)$. Now put the brackets the other way and work out $(\vec A\times\vec B)\times\vec C$. Give its $x$ component.

<Hint>

You can compute two cross products in a row. Or use Problem 1(b): since $\vec P\times\vec C = -\vec C\times\vec P$, the rule for $\vec C\times(\vec A\times\vec B)$ gives the answer with two dot products.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-ax" label="$x$ component of $(\vec A\times\vec B)\times\vec C$:" value="-14" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-ax" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Turn the product round so that Problem 1(b) applies.

<Step title="The rule for the other bracketing">

<Formula label="The other bracketing" tex="\left(\vec A\times\vec B\right)\times\vec C = -\vec C\times\left(\vec A\times\vec B\right) = -\vec A\left(\vec C\cdot\vec B\right) + \vec B\left(\vec C\cdot\vec A\right) = \vec B\left(\vec A\cdot\vec C\right) - \vec A\left(\vec B\cdot\vec C\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

With $\vec A\cdot\vec C = -4$ and $\vec B\cdot\vec C = -6 + 0 + 8 = 2$,

<Formula label="The other bracketing for the three given vectors" tex="\left(\vec A\times\vec B\right)\times\vec C = -4\,(3, 0, 2) - 2\,(1, 2, -1) = (-14, -4, -6)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

This is not $(-10, -1, -12)$: the cross product is not associative.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Directly: $\vec A\times\vec B = (4, -5, -6)$, and the $x$ component of $(4, -5, -6)\times(-2, 1, 4)$ is $(-5)(4) - (-6)(1) = -14$. The result is perpendicular to $\vec C$: $(-14)(-2) + (-4)(1) + (-6)(4) = 0$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Show that for any three vectors the two bracketings differ by a third triple product:

<Formula label="The difference of the two bracketings" tex="\vec A\times\left(\vec B\times\vec C\right) - \left(\vec A\times\vec B\right)\times\vec C = \vec B\times\left(\vec A\times\vec C\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

Then say for which vectors (none of them zero) the two bracketings give the same result.

<Hint>

Write all three triple products with the rule of Problem 1(b). The term $\vec B(\vec A\cdot\vec C)$ appears in both bracketings.

</Hint>

<Hint>

A cross product of two vectors that are not zero vanishes only if they are parallel. Apply this twice to $\vec B\times(\vec A\times\vec C)$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-when" label="The two bracketings agree exactly when" choices={["never, unless one of the three vectors is zero", "the three vectors lie in one plane", "A is parallel to B, or C is perpendicular to both A and B", "A is parallel to C, or B is perpendicular to both A and C"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Each triple product is a combination of two of the three vectors. Subtracting, one vector drops out.

<Step title="Subtract">

From Problem 1(b) and part (a),

<Formula label="Subtracting the two bracketings" tex="\vec A\times\left(\vec B\times\vec C\right) - \left(\vec A\times\vec B\right)\times\vec C = \left[\vec B\left(\vec A\cdot\vec C\right) - \vec C\left(\vec A\cdot\vec B\right)\right] - \left[\vec B\left(\vec A\cdot\vec C\right) - \vec A\left(\vec B\cdot\vec C\right)\right] = \vec A\left(\vec B\cdot\vec C\right) - \vec C\left(\vec A\cdot\vec B\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2 (our own problem)" />

By Problem 1(b) with the letters renamed, $\vec B\times(\vec A\times\vec C) = \vec A(\vec B\cdot\vec C) - \vec C(\vec B\cdot\vec A)$, which is the same vector.

</Step>

<Step title="When it vanishes">

The bracketings agree when $\vec B\times(\vec A\times\vec C) = 0$. Either $\vec A\times\vec C = 0$, which means $\vec A$ is parallel (or antiparallel) to $\vec C$; or $\vec B$ is parallel to $\vec A\times\vec C$, which means $\vec B$ is perpendicular to both $\vec A$ and $\vec C$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

<Exercise id="p3" title="Properties of the rotation matrix R" points={15} mit="Problem 3">

Under a rotation the components of a vector change as in [Lecture Notes 2](/courses/8-07/rotations-index-notation):

<Formula label="How a rotation changes the components" tex="A'_i = R_{ij}\,A_j" cite="reading2" at="eq. (2.8), p. 2" />

<Part label="a" points={5}>

<Formula label="The orthogonality condition" tex="R_{ij}\,R_{ik} = \delta_{jk}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), eq. (1.2.2), p. 2" />

Matrices with this property are called orthogonal.

The box is our addition: what does the condition say in the language of matrices?

<Hint>

Write $A'_iA'_i$ with two copies of the rotation rule. The two summed indices need different names.

</Hint>

<Hint>

You get a condition of the form $M_{jk}A_jA_k = 0$ for every vector $\vec A$, with a symmetric $M_{jk}$. Choose simple vectors to read off the entries: first a basis vector, then the sum of two basis vectors.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-mat" label="In matrix language, $R_{ij}R_{ik} = \delta_{jk}$ says" choices={["R R = 1: the matrix R is its own inverse", "RᵀR = 1: the transpose of R is its inverse", "Rᵀ = R: the matrix R is symmetric", "the determinant of R is 1, and nothing more"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The squared length after the rotation is a quadratic expression in the components of $\vec A$. It can equal $A_jA_j$ for every $\vec A$ only if the coefficients agree.

<Step title="The length after the rotation">

<Formula label="The squared length of the rotated vector" tex="A'_i\,A'_i = R_{ij}\,A_j\,R_{ik}\,A_k = \left(R_{ij}\,R_{ik}\right)A_j\,A_k" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The old squared length is $A_jA_j = \delta_{jk}A_jA_k$. The two are equal for all $\vec A$ if and only if

<Formula label="The condition on the coefficients" tex="M_{jk}\,A_j\,A_k = 0 \ \text{ for all } \vec A, \qquad M_{jk} \equiv R_{ij}\,R_{ik} - \delta_{jk}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Every entry of M vanishes">

$M_{jk}$ is symmetric, because swapping $j$ and $k$ only swaps the two factors $R_{ij}$ and $R_{ik}$. Take $\vec A = \hat e_x$: the sum reduces to $M_{11} = 0$. In the same way $M_{22} = M_{33} = 0$. Take $\vec A = \hat e_x + \hat e_y$: the sum is $M_{11} + M_{22} + M_{12} + M_{21} = 2M_{12} = 0$. The same works for every pair, so all entries of $M$ vanish, which is the orthogonality condition. (The symmetry matters: an antisymmetric $M_{jk}$ would give $M_{jk}A_jA_k = 0$ for every vector without being zero.)

</Step>

<Step title="As matrices">

$R_{ij}R_{ik} = (R^T)_{ji}R_{ik}$ is the entry $jk$ of the matrix product $R^TR$, and $\delta_{jk}$ is the entry of the identity matrix. So the condition is $R^TR = 1$: the transpose is the inverse. It then follows that $(\det R)^2 = 1$, but that is a consequence and not the whole condition.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For the rotation about the $z$ axis of Lecture Notes 2, the first two columns are $(\cos\phi, \sin\phi, 0)$ and $(-\sin\phi, \cos\phi, 0)$. Their dot product is zero and each has length 1, as $R_{ij}R_{ik} = \delta_{jk}$ demands of the columns $j$ and $k$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Hint>

<Answer id="p4-origin" label="The origin is" choices={["neither the warmest nor the coldest point nearby", "the warmest point of the whole block", "the coldest point of the whole block", "a point where the temperature itself is zero"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For a small step $d\vec l$ the temperature changes by $dT = \nabla T\cdot d\vec l$. Everything asked follows from this one statement.

<Step title="The gradient at P">

<Formula label="The gradient of the temperature" tex="\nabla T = \left(2y,\ 2x,\ -2z\right), \qquad \nabla T = (4, 2, -2) \ \text{ at } P" cite="mit" at="Problem 4, p. 3 (our own problem)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Fastest rise">

For a step of fixed length, $\nabla T\cdot d\vec l$ is largest when the step is along $\nabla T$. The rate is then the length of the gradient: $\sqrt{16 + 4 + 4} = \sqrt{24} = 4.90$ °C per metre.

<Step title="Along the given direction">

</Step>

<Step title="The origin">

Along the line $y = x$, $z = 0$ the field is $20 + 2x^2$, which has a minimum at the origin. Along $y = -x$, $z = 0$ it is $20 - 2x^2$, which has a maximum there. So the origin is a saddle point: neither the warmest nor the coldest point. A vanishing gradient says only that the field is flat to first order.

</Step>

Look for an antiderivative of the form $r^3$ times a power of $r^2 + a^2$, or substitute $r = a\tan u$. A third way uses no integral of $g_a$ at all: the divergence theorem with $\nabla f_a$ on a large sphere.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-frac" label="Fraction of the integral inside $r < a$:" value="0.354" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-frac" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The integrand in shells is an exact derivative.

<Step title="An antiderivative">

<Formula label="The integrand is a derivative" tex="\frac{d}{dr}\,\frac{r^3}{\left(r^2 + a^2\right)^{3/2}} = \frac{3r^2}{\left(r^2 + a^2\right)^{3/2}} - \frac{3r^4}{\left(r^2 + a^2\right)^{5/2}} = \frac{3a^2r^2}{\left(r^2 + a^2\right)^{5/2}} = 4\pi r^2\,g_a(r)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

<Formula label="The integral inside a sphere of radius r" tex="\int_{r' < r}g_a\,d^3x = \int_0^r4\pi r'^2\,g_a(r')\,dr' = \frac{r^3}{\left(r^2 + a^2\right)^{3/2}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

For $r \to \infty$ this tends to 1, whatever the value of $a$. For $r = a$ it is $2^{-3/2} = 0.354$: about a third of the integral lies inside $r = a$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

By the divergence theorem the integral of $g_a = \nabla\cdot\nabla f_a$ over a ball of radius $r$ is the flux of $\nabla f_a$ through its surface. Part (a) gave $\nabla f_a = \frac{1}{4\pi}\,r\,(r^2 + a^2)^{-3/2}\,\hat r$, and the surface is $4\pi r^2$: the flux is $r^3/(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}$, the same expression. Far away $\nabla f_a$ is the inverse-square field $\hat r/(4\pi r^2)$, whose flux is 1 through any sphere.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Show that

<Formula label="The limit away from the origin" tex="\lim_{a\to 0}g_a(r) = 0 \quad \text{if } r \neq 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), eq. (1.5.5), p. 3" />

So in the limit $a \to 0$ the smooth function $g_a$ has the properties expected of a three-dimensional delta function.

The two boxes are our addition. How does $g_a(r)$ at a fixed $r \neq 0$ depend on $a$ once $a$ is much smaller than $r$? And what is $r^3g_a(r)$ when $a = r/10$?

<Hint>

For $a \ll r$ the $a^2$ in the denominator no longer matters. The one in the numerator does.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5-scale" label="For fixed $r \neq 0$ and $a \ll r$, $g_a(r)$ is proportional to" choices={["1/a³", "a²", "a", "1/a"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p5-small" label="$r^3g_a(r)$ for $a = r/10$:" value="0.00233" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-small" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Away from the origin the denominator stays finite as $a \to 0$, and the numerator goes to zero.

<Step title="The limit">

For $r \neq 0$, $(r^2 + a^2)^{5/2} \geq r^5$, so

<Formula label="A bound away from the origin" tex="0 < g_a(r) \leq \frac{3a^2}{4\pi r^5} \to 0 \quad \text{as } a \to 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

For $a \ll r$ the bound is also the value, so $g_a$ falls as $a^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="The number">

With $a = r/10$: $r^3g_a = \frac{3\times 0.01}{4\pi\,(1.01)^{5/2}} = \frac{0.03}{12.566\times 1.0252} = 0.00233$. Compare the origin, where $g_a(0) = 0.239/a^3$ grows without limit.

</Step>

<Step title="Why this is a delta function">

As $a \to 0$, $g_a$ goes to zero at every point but the origin (this part), the value at the origin grows as $1/a^3$ (part a), and the integral over all space stays 1 (part b). By part (b) the integral inside any fixed radius $r$, $r^3/(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}$, tends to 1 as well. Those are the defining properties of $\delta^3(\vec r)$. Since $f_a \to -\frac{1}{4\pi r}$, this is the identity at the top of the problem: $-\nabla^2\frac{1}{4\pi r} = \delta^3(\vec r)$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Exercises with delta functions" points={10} mit="Problem 6">

Spherical coordinates are $(r, \theta, \phi)$, with the volume element $r^2\sin\theta\,dr\,d\theta\,d\phi$. Cylindrical coordinates are $(s, \phi, z)$, with $s$ the distance from the $z$ axis and the volume element $s\,ds\,d\phi\,dz$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

A charge $Q$ is spread evenly over a spherical shell of radius $R$ centred on the origin. Write its volume charge density with a delta function in spherical coordinates, as $\rho = C_1\,\delta(r - R)$, and give $C_1$. Then do the same for a thin ring of radius $R$ and charge $Q$ in the $xy$ plane, centred on the origin, as $\rho = C_2\,\delta(r - R)\,\delta(\theta - \frac{\pi}{2})$ in spherical coordinates, and give $C_2$.

<Hint>

The delta functions put the charge in the right place. The constant in front follows from one condition: the integral of $\rho$ over all space is $Q$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate with the full volume element. Each delta function removes one integral and sets its coordinate; the factors $r^2\sin\theta$ stay.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-shell" label="$C_1 =$" expr="Q/(4*pi*R^2)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 3], R: [0.5, 2.5] }}
  names={{ Q: "Q", R: "R" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q/(4/3*pi*R^3)", hint: "That is the density of a ball filled evenly with the charge Q. Here the constant multiplies δ(r − R), which itself has the units of one over length." },
    { expr: "Q", hint: "Integrate Q δ(r − R) over all space: the factor r² and the angles give 4πR² times Q, not Q." },
    { expr: "Q/(4*pi*R)", hint: "Check the units: δ(r − R) is one over a length, so the constant must be a charge per area." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p6-ring" label="$C_2 =$" expr="Q/(2*pi*R^2)"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 3], R: [0.5, 2.5] }}
  names={{ Q: "Q", R: "R" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q/(2*pi*R)", hint: "That is the charge per length of the ring, the right constant in cylindrical coordinates with δ(s − R) δ(z). The function δ(θ − π/2) has no units, and the volume element supplies a factor R² here." },
    { expr: "Q/(4*pi*R^2)", hint: "That is the shell. For the ring the integral over θ is removed by its delta function, so the angles give 2π and not 4π." },
    { expr: "Q/(2*pi)", hint: "The volume element has a factor r², which the delta function sets to R². It has to be divided out." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A delta function in a coordinate confines the charge to a surface or a line. The constant is fixed by the total charge.

<Step title="The shell">

<Formula label="Total charge of the shell" tex="\int\rho\,d^3x = C_1\int_0^\infty\delta(r - R)\,r^2\,dr\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,d\theta\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi = 4\pi R^2\,C_1 = Q" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

So $\rho = \frac{Q}{4\pi R^2}\,\delta(r - R)$. The constant is the charge per area of the shell, as it should be.

</Step>

<Step title="The ring">

<Formula label="Total charge of the ring" tex="\int\rho\,d^3x = C_2\int_0^\infty\delta(r - R)\,r^2\,dr\int_0^\pi\delta\left(\theta - \frac{\pi}{2}\right)\sin\theta\,d\theta\int_0^{2\pi}d\phi = 2\pi R^2\,C_2 = Q" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

So $\rho = \frac{Q}{2\pi R^2}\,\delta(r - R)\,\delta(\theta - \frac{\pi}{2})$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Near the ring a small change $d\theta$ is a distance $R\,d\theta$, so $\delta(\theta - \frac{\pi}{2}) = R\,\delta(z)$ there, and the density becomes $\frac{Q}{2\pi R}\,\delta(s - R)\,\delta(z)$: the charge per length $Q/(2\pi R)$ times delta functions in the two directions across the ring. That is the same ring in cylindrical coordinates.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

In Cartesian coordinates $\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r') = \delta(x - x')\,\delta(y - y')\,\delta(z - z')$. How is $\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r')$ written in cylindrical coordinates $(s, \phi, z)$? (Take a point $\vec r'$ that is not on the $z$ axis.)

<Hint>

The defining property is that the integral over a volume containing $\vec r'$ is 1. Integrate your candidate with the volume element $s\,ds\,d\phi\,dz$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-cyl" label="$\delta^3(\vec r - \vec r') =$" choices={["δ(s − s′) δ(φ − φ′) δ(z − z′)", "s δ(s − s′) δ(φ − φ′) δ(z − z′)", "(1/s) δ(s − s′) δ(φ − φ′) δ(z − z′)", "(1/(2πs)) δ(s − s′) δ(z − z′)"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Three delta functions fix the three coordinates. A factor in front must cancel the factor $s$ of the volume element.

<Step title="Integrate">

<Formula label="The delta function in cylindrical coordinates" tex="\int\frac{1}{s}\,\delta(s - s')\,\delta(\phi - \phi')\,\delta(z - z')\;s\,ds\,d\phi\,dz = 1, \qquad \delta^3(\vec r - \vec r') = \frac{1}{s}\,\delta(s - s')\,\delta(\phi - \phi')\,\delta(z - z')" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

The factor may be written $1/s'$ just as well, because the delta function sets $s = s'$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The units fit: $\delta(s - s')$ and $\delta(z - z')$ are each one over a length, $\delta(\phi - \phi')$ has none, and $1/s$ supplies the third. The last choice also has the right units and the integral 1, but it spreads the charge over a whole ring around the axis instead of putting it at one point. On the axis itself ($s' = 0$) the angle $\phi'$ has no meaning and the formula cannot be used as it stands.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

A charge $\lambda$ per unit length is spread evenly over the surface of a long cylinder of radius $b$ around the $z$ axis. Write the volume charge density as $\rho = C_3\,\delta(s - b)$ in cylindrical coordinates and give $C_3$.

<Hint>

The charge in a slab of the cylinder of height $h$ is $\lambda h$. Integrate $\rho$ over that slab.

</Hint>

<Step title="Integrate over a slab">

<Part label="d" points={2}>

What is $\nabla^2\ln r$ in two dimensions, where $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$?

<Hint>

Follow Problem 5: first away from the origin, with indices that now run over 1 and 2 only, so that $\partial_ix_i = 2$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Then the origin: the two-dimensional divergence theorem turns the integral of $\nabla\cdot\nabla\ln r$ over a disk into the flux of $\nabla\ln r$ through its edge, a circle.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-ln" label="$\nabla^2\ln r =$" choices={["0 everywhere, the origin included", "δ²(r), with the factor 1", "−4π δ²(r)", "2π δ²(r)"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In two dimensions $\ln r$ plays the part that $-1/r$ plays in three: its gradient is the field whose flux through every circle around the origin is the same.

<Step title="Away from the origin">

<Formula label="The Laplacian of ln r away from the origin" tex="\partial_i\ln r = \frac{x_i}{r^2}, \qquad \partial_i\,\frac{x_i}{r^2} = \frac{2}{r^2} - \frac{2\,x_i\,x_i}{r^4} = 0 \quad (r \neq 0)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="The origin">

The gradient is $\hat r/r$. Its flux through a circle of radius $r$ is $\frac{1}{r}\times 2\pi r = 2\pi$, for every radius. So the integral of $\nabla^2\ln r$ over any disk around the origin is $2\pi$, while the function vanishes away from the origin:

<Formula label="The Laplacian of ln r in two dimensions" tex="\nabla^2\ln r = 2\pi\,\delta^2(\vec r) = 2\pi\,\delta(x)\,\delta(y)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(d), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Compare three dimensions, where the flux of $\hat r/r^2$ through a sphere is $4\pi$ and $\nabla^2(-1/r) = 4\pi\,\delta^3(\vec r)$, the identity of Problem 5. The number in front is the size of the unit circle here and of the unit sphere there. The same smoothing works too: $\nabla^2\frac12\ln(r^2 + a^2) = 2a^2/(r^2 + a^2)^2$ in two dimensions, which integrates to $2\pi$ for every $a$.

</Step>

</Solution>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="Corollaries of the fundamental integral theorems" points={15} mit="Problem 7">

The two theorems are the divergence theorem, $\int_V\nabla\cdot\vec v\,d^3x = \oint_S\vec v\cdot d\vec a$ for a volume $V$ with the closed surface $S$ (see [the divergence theorem](/courses/18-02/divergence-theorem)), and Stokes' theorem, $\int_S(\nabla\times\vec v)\cdot d\vec a = \oint_\Gamma\vec v\cdot d\vec l$ for a surface $S$ with the edge $\Gamma$ (see [Stokes' theorem](/courses/18-02/stokes-theorem)). Here $d\vec a$ is a small piece of surface as a vector along its normal (outward for a closed surface), and the direction round $\Gamma$ and the normal of $S$ are related by the right-hand rule. Below, $f$ is any smooth scalar function and $\vec A$ any smooth vector field.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Show that

<Formula label="The gradient theorem for a volume" tex="\int_V\nabla f\;d^3x = \oint_S f\;d\vec a" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a), p. 4" />

where $S$ is the surface that bounds $V$. Show that it follows that $\oint_Sd\vec a = 0$ for every closed surface.

The number box is our addition. An open hemisphere (the curved surface only, without its flat base) has the radius $2\ \text{m}$ and sits on the $xy$ plane with its pole on the positive $z$ axis. Give the $z$ component of $\int d\vec a$ over it, in m², with the normal pointing away from the centre.

<Hint>

A vector equation holds if its dot product with every constant vector $\vec c$ holds. Apply the divergence theorem to the field $\vec v = \vec c\,f$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the box: the hemisphere and its flat base together make a closed surface.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-hemi" label="$z$ component of $\int d\vec a$, in m²:" value="12.57" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-hemi" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The divergence theorem is one equation between numbers. Feeding it $\vec c\,f$ with an arbitrary constant vector $\vec c$ turns it into three.

<Step title="Apply the divergence theorem">

Since $\vec c$ is constant, $\nabla\cdot(\vec c\,f) = \vec c\cdot\nabla f$. The theorem gives

<Formula label="The divergence theorem for a constant vector times a scalar" tex="\vec c\cdot\int_V\nabla f\;d^3x = \oint_S f\,\vec c\cdot d\vec a = \vec c\cdot\oint_S f\;d\vec a" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(a), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

This holds for every $\vec c$. Taking $\vec c = \hat e_x$, $\hat e_y$, $\hat e_z$ in turn shows that the two vectors agree component by component.

</Step>

<Step title="A closed surface has no net area vector">

Take $f = 1$. Its gradient vanishes, so $\oint_Sd\vec a = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The hemisphere">

The curved surface and the flat base (a disk of radius 2 m) form a closed surface, so their area vectors add up to zero. The outward normal of the base is $-\hat e_z$, and its area is $\pi\times 2^2 = 12.57\ \text{m}^2$. So for the curved part $\int d\vec a = +12.57\ \text{m}^2\,\hat e_z$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Directly: on the hemisphere $d\vec a = \hat r\,R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$ and the $z$ component of $\hat r$ is $\cos\theta$, so the integral is $2\pi R^2\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = \pi R^2$. It is the area of the shadow of the surface, not its true area $2\pi R^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Show that

<Formula label="The curl theorem for a volume" tex="\int_V\nabla\times\vec A\;d^3x = -\oint_S\vec A\times d\vec a" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(b), p. 4" />

where $S$ is the surface that bounds $V$.

<Hint>

Apply the divergence theorem to $\vec A\times\vec c$ with a constant $\vec c$. Problem 1(c) gives its divergence.

</Hint>

<Hint>

In a triple product $\vec a\cdot(\vec b\times\vec c)$ the three vectors may be moved round in a cycle. Use this to bring $\vec c$ to the front on the surface side.

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same trick as in part (a), with a cross product in place of the product with a scalar.

<Step title="The divergence">

By Problem 1(c), $\nabla\cdot(\vec A\times\vec c) = \vec c\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A) - \vec A\cdot(\nabla\times\vec c) = \vec c\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A)$, because $\vec c$ is constant.

</Step>

<Step title="The surface side">

$(\vec A\times\vec c)\cdot d\vec a = \vec c\cdot(d\vec a\times\vec A) = -\vec c\cdot(\vec A\times d\vec a)$, by a cyclic shift and then a swap.

</Step>

<Step title="Put together">

<Formula label="The divergence theorem for a field crossed with a constant vector" tex="\vec c\cdot\int_V\nabla\times\vec A\;d^3x = -\vec c\cdot\oint_S\vec A\times d\vec a" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(b), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

for every constant $\vec c$, which gives the statement.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Take $\vec A = (-ky, kx, 0)$ of Problem 1(d) and a ball of radius $R$ around the origin. The curl is $2k\,\hat e_z$, so the left side is $2k\cdot\frac43\pi R^3\,\hat e_z$. On the right, $d\vec a = \hat r\,da$ and $-\vec A\times\hat r = \hat r\times\vec A$ has the $z$ component $(x\,kx + y\,ky)/R = k(x^2 + y^2)/R$. The average of $x^2 + y^2$ over the sphere is $\frac23R^2$, so the integral is $\frac{k}{R}\cdot\frac23R^2\cdot 4\pi R^2 = \frac83\pi kR^3$. The two sides agree.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Show that

<Formula label="The gradient theorem for a surface" tex="\int_S\nabla f\times d\vec a = -\oint_\Gamma f\;d\vec l" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(c), p. 4" />

where $\Gamma$ is the edge of the surface $S$.

The number box is our addition. Take $f = x$ and for $S$ the flat disk of radius $1.5\ \text{m}$ in the $xy$ plane, centred on the origin, with its normal along $+\hat e_z$. Both sides are then a vector along $\hat e_y$. Give its $y$ component, in m².

<Hint>

Now it is Stokes' theorem that gets the field $\vec c\,f$. You need the curl of a constant vector times a scalar.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$\nabla\times(\vec c\,f) = \nabla f\times\vec c$. Then move $\vec c$ to the front of the triple product, as in part (b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-disk" label="$y$ component of both sides, in m²:" value="-7.07" tolerance="1%" calc="p7-disk" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Part (a) again, one dimension lower: Stokes' theorem applied to $\vec c\,f$.

<Step title="The curl">

Component $i$ of $\nabla\times(\vec c\,f)$ is $\epsilon_{ijk}\,\partial_j(c_k f) = \epsilon_{ijk}(\partial_j f)c_k$, which is component $i$ of $\nabla f\times\vec c$.

</Step>

<Step title="Apply Stokes' theorem">

<Formula label="Stokes' theorem for a constant vector times a scalar" tex="\int_S\left(\nabla f\times\vec c\right)\cdot d\vec a = \oint_\Gamma f\,\vec c\cdot d\vec l" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(c), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

On the left, $(\nabla f\times\vec c)\cdot d\vec a = \vec c\cdot(d\vec a\times\nabla f) = -\vec c\cdot(\nabla f\times d\vec a)$. So $-\vec c\cdot\int_S\nabla f\times d\vec a = \vec c\cdot\oint_\Gamma f\,d\vec l$ for every constant $\vec c$, which is the statement.

</Step>

<Step title="The disk">

With $f = x$ the gradient is $\hat e_x$, and $d\vec a = \hat e_z\,da$. Since $\hat e_x\times\hat e_z = -\hat e_y$, the left side is $-\hat e_y$ times the area of the disk: its $y$ component is $-\pi\times 1.5^2 = -7.07\ \text{m}^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The right side, directly: on the circle $x = R\cos\phi$ and $d\vec l = R\,(-\sin\phi, \cos\phi, 0)\,d\phi$, counterclockwise seen from above, as the right-hand rule demands for a normal along $+\hat e_z$. Then $\oint x\,d\vec l = R^2\left(-\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\phi\sin\phi\,d\phi,\ \int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\phi\,d\phi,\ 0\right) = (0, \pi R^2, 0)$, and with the minus sign the $y$ component is $-\pi R^2 = -7.07\ \text{m}^2$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Show that for a closed surface $S$

<Formula label="The flux of a curl through a closed surface" tex="\oint_S\left(\nabla\times\vec A\right)\cdot d\vec a = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 7(d), p. 4" />

<Hint>

There are two ways, one with each theorem. With Stokes' theorem: what is the edge of a closed surface? With the divergence theorem: what is the divergence of a curl?

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A closed surface has no edge, and a curl has no divergence.

<Step title="With the divergence theorem">

The flux is the integral of $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A)$ over the volume inside. With indices, $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A) = \epsilon_{ijk}\,\partial_i\partial_jA_k$. Swapping the names $i$ and $j$ changes the sign of $\epsilon_{ijk}$ but not of $\partial_i\partial_j$, so the sum equals minus itself and vanishes.

</Step>

<Step title="With Stokes' theorem">

Cut the closed surface into two pieces along a closed curve $\Gamma$. Each piece has $\Gamma$ as its edge, but with the outward normals the right-hand rule sends the two round $\Gamma$ in opposite directions. So the two fluxes are $+\oint_\Gamma\vec A\cdot d\vec l$ and $-\oint_\Gamma\vec A\cdot d\vec l$, and they add up to zero.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For the field of part (b)'s check, the curl is the constant vector $2k\,\hat e_z$, and its flux through a closed surface is $2k\,\hat e_z\cdot\oint d\vec a$, which vanishes by part (a).

</Step>

</Solution>
