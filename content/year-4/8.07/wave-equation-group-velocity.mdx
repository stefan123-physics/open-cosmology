## The wave equation in one dimension

A travelling wave is a shape that moves without changing (slide 1). If $f(z, 0)$ is the shape at time zero and it moves to the right at speed $v$, then at time $t$ the value at $z$ is the value that was a distance $vt$ further back:

<Formula label="A travelling wave" tex="f(z, t) = f(z - vt, 0) = g(z - vt)" cite="reading1" at="slide 1, eq. (1)" />

<Plot variable="z" domain={[0, 12]} range={[-0.2, 1.4]} xLabel="z" yLabel="f"
  curves={[
    { expr: "exp(-((z - 2)^2) / 0.3) + 0.6 * exp(-((z - 3.2)^2) / 0.3)", label: "t = 0", dash: true },
    { expr: "exp(-((z - 2 - v*t)^2) / 0.3) + 0.6 * exp(-((z - 3.2 - v*t)^2) / 0.3)" }
  ]}
  params={{ t: { value: 3, min: 0, max: 6, step: 0.1, label: "t" }, v: { value: 1, min: 0.5, max: 1, step: 0.05, label: "v" } }}
  caption="A pulse g(z − vt) with two bumps, computed from its formula. The dashed curve is the shape at t = 0; the solid curve is the same shape at time t (slider), shifted to the right by vt without any change of form." />

Waves can just as well move to the left, as $g(z + vt)$, and the sum of two solutions is again a solution (slide 2):

<Formula label="Waves in both directions" tex="f(z, t) = g_1(z - vt) + g_2(z + vt)" cite="reading1" at="slide 2, eq. (2)" />

What equation does every such $f$ obey? Differentiate with the chain rule, writing primes for derivatives of $g_1$ and $g_2$ with respect to their argument (slide 2):

<Formula label="The derivatives" tex="\frac{\partial f}{\partial z} = g_1'(z - vt) + g_2'(z + vt), \qquad \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2} = g_1''(z - vt) + g_2''(z + vt), \qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = -v\,g_1'(z - vt) + v\,g_2'(z + vt), \qquad \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial t^2} = v^2 g_1''(z - vt) + v^2 g_2''(z + vt)" cite="reading1" at="slide 2, eq. (3)" />

[The slide's last line has single primes; two time derivatives give second derivatives, $g_1''$ and $g_2''$, as written here.] Each time derivative brings a factor $\mp v$, so the second time derivative is $v^2$ times the second space derivative. That is the one-dimensional **wave equation** (slide 2):

<Formula label="The wave equation" tex="\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2} - \frac{1}{v^2}\,\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial t^2} = 0" cite="reading1" at="slide 2, eq. (4) (boxed)" />

The converse holds too (a remark of ours): in the variables $u = z - vt$ and $w = z + vt$ the equation becomes $\partial^2 f/\partial u\,\partial w = 0$, whose solutions are exactly a function of $u$ plus a function of $w$. So (2) is the general solution.

## Sinusoidal waves

The most important travelling waves are sinusoidal (slide 3):

<Formula label="A sinusoidal wave" tex="f(z, t) = A\cos\left[k(z - vt) + \delta\right] = A\cos\left[kz - \omega t + \delta\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 3, eq. (5)" />

with these names (slide 3):

<Formula label="The parameters of a sinusoidal wave" tex="v = \frac{\omega}{k}, \qquad \omega = 2\pi\nu, \qquad \lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}" cite="reading1" at="slide 3, eq. (6)" />

Here $v$ is the **phase velocity**, $\omega$ the angular frequency, $\nu$ the frequency, $\delta$ the phase (or phase constant), $k$ the wave number, $\lambda$ the wavelength, $T$ the period and $A$ the amplitude. At a fixed time the pattern repeats when $z$ grows by $\lambda$; at a fixed place, when $t$ grows by $T$. In one period a crest moves one wavelength, so $v = \lambda/T = \lambda\nu$.

## Complex notation and Fourier's theorem

Any wave can be built by adding up sinusoidal waves: this is **Fourier's theorem**, which Guth notes is also called Dirichlet's theorem (slide 4). Sinusoids are easiest to handle as complex exponentials. With Euler's formula (slide 4)

<Formula label="Euler's formula" tex="e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta" cite="reading1" at="slide 4, eq. (8)" />

and a complex amplitude $\tilde A = Ae^{i\delta}$, which holds both the amplitude and the phase, the wave (5) is a real part (slide 4):

<Formula label="The wave in complex notation" tex="f(z, t) = \text{Re}\left[\tilde A\,e^{i(kz - \omega t)}\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 4, eq. (7)" />

The usual conventions drop the "Re" and the tilde (slide 4): one calculates with the complex wave and takes the real part at the end, which works as long as every step is linear.

<Formula label="The complex wave" tex="f(z, t) = Ae^{i(kz - \omega t)}" cite="reading1" at="slide 4, eq. (9)" />

Adding such waves with every wave number gives the general solution (slide 4):

<Formula label="The general solution as a Fourier integral" tex="f(z, t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} A(k)\,e^{i(kz - \omega t)}\,dk" cite="reading1" at="slide 4, eq. (10)" />

with $\omega/k = v$, the wave speed or phase velocity. (With $\omega = vk$ every term is a function of $z - vt$, so (10) is the general wave moving to the right, the $g_1$ of (2); a wave moving left needs $\omega = -vk$ as well. That is our remark.)

## Group velocity and phase velocity

This part is not in Griffiths; Guth points to Jackson's *Classical Electrodynamics*, pp. 324–325 (slide 5). So far $\omega/k$ was the same for every wave number. In many media it is not: $\omega$ depends on $k$ in a more complicated way, and the medium is **dispersive** (slide 5). Take a wave packet whose amplitude $A(k)$ is peaked around a wave number $k_0$, and expand $\omega(k)$ around it (slide 5):

<Formula label="Expanding the frequency near the centre of the packet" tex="\omega(k) = \omega(k_0) + \frac{d\omega}{dk}(k_0)\,(k - k_0) + \cdots = \omega(k_0) - k_0\,\frac{d\omega}{dk} + k\,\frac{d\omega}{dk} + \cdots" cite="reading1" at="slide 5, eq. (11)" />

Put this into (10). The first two terms do not depend on $k$ and come out of the integral as a phase factor; the third combines with $kz$ (slide 6):

<Formula label="The wave packet" tex="f(z, t) = e^{-i\left[\omega(k_0) - k_0\frac{d\omega}{dk}\right]t}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} dk\,A(k)\,e^{ik\left(z - \frac{d\omega}{dk}t\right)}" cite="reading1" at="slide 6, eq. (12)" />

[The slide's prefactor has $+i$ in the exponent; with $e^{-i\omega t}$ in (10) it is $-i$, as written here. Only the phase factor's sign changes, not the conclusion.] The integral is a function of $z - (d\omega/dk)\,t$ alone: a fixed shape moving at the **group velocity** (slide 6),

<Formula label="The group velocity" tex="v_{\text{group}} = \frac{d\omega}{dk}(k_0)" cite="reading1" at="slide 6, eq. (13) (boxed)" />

The integral itself still oscillates quickly, at the central wave number $k_0$. To separate the two motions (our rewriting), take out a factor $e^{ik_0(z - v_{\text{group}}t)}$: what remains, $\mathcal{E}(z - v_{\text{group}}t) = \int dk\,A(k)\,e^{i(k - k_0)(z - v_{\text{group}}t)}$, varies slowly because $A(k)$ is narrow, and it is the packet's **envelope**. Putting the prefactor back, the exponents combine to

<Formula label="Carrier times envelope" tex="f(z, t) = e^{i(k_0z - \omega(k_0)t)}\,\mathcal{E}(z - v_{\text{group}}t)" cite="reading1" at="slide 6, eq. (12), rewritten" />

The envelope moves at $v_{\text{group}}$ (slide 7). The prefactor is the central wave, the carrier, and it is what sets the crests moving at the **phase velocity** $v_{\text{phase}} = \omega(k_0)/k_0$ (slide 7 writes $\omega(k)/k$, the same thing for the central wave number). When the two differ the crests slide through the envelope: if $v_{\text{phase}} > v_{\text{group}}$, crests appear at the back (left) of the envelope, move forward through it and disappear at the front (slide 7).

<WorkedExample title="A radio pulse in a plasma">
<Problem>
In a plasma such as the ionosphere, radio waves obey the dispersion relation $\omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + c^2k^2$, where $\omega_p$ is the plasma frequency (a result we take as given here). Take a plasma frequency of $\nu_p = 9$ MHz and a pulse whose carrier is at $\nu = 15$ MHz. Find the phase and group velocities, the wavelength inside the plasma, and how long the pulse takes to cross 300 km of such plasma.
</Problem>
<Step title="The wave number">
From the dispersion relation, $ck = \sqrt{\omega^2 - \omega_p^2} = 2\pi\sqrt{15^2 - 9^2}$ MHz $= 2\pi \times 12$ MHz. So the wavelength is $\lambda = 2\pi/k = c/(12\ \text{MHz}) = 2.998 \times 10^8/1.2 \times 10^7 \approx 25$ m, longer than the 20 m the same frequency has in vacuum.
</Step>
<Step title="The phase velocity">
$v_{\text{phase}} = \omega/k = c \times 15/12 = 1.25c$. The crests move faster than light.
</Step>
<Step title="The group velocity">
Differentiating the dispersion relation, $2\omega\,d\omega = 2c^2k\,dk$, so $v_{\text{group}} = d\omega/dk = c^2k/\omega = c \times 12/15 = 0.8c$. Notice $v_{\text{phase}}\,v_{\text{group}} = c^2$. The envelope, which carries the pulse and any signal on it, travels slower than light, as it must. Since $v_{\text{phase}} > v_{\text{group}}$, the crests are born at the back of the pulse and die at its front.
</Step>
<Step title="The crossing time">
$t = 3 \times 10^5/(0.8 \times 2.998 \times 10^8) = 1.25 \times 10^{-3}$ s, a quarter longer than the 1.00 ms light needs in vacuum. Lower frequencies, closer to $\nu_p$, are slowed more; that spread of arrival times is how such a plasma reveals itself.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- **Travelling waves:** $g(z - vt)$ moves right and $g(z + vt)$ left at speed $v$; every sum $g_1(z - vt) + g_2(z + vt)$ solves the wave equation $\partial^2 f/\partial z^2 - (1/v^2)\partial^2 f/\partial t^2 = 0$, and every solution has this form.
- **Sinusoidal waves:** $A\cos(kz - \omega t + \delta)$, with $v = \omega/k$, $\omega = 2\pi\nu$, $\lambda = 2\pi/k$, $T = 2\pi/\omega$.
- **Complex notation:** $f = \text{Re}[\tilde A e^{i(kz - \omega t)}]$ with $\tilde A = Ae^{i\delta}$, usually written $Ae^{i(kz - \omega t)}$; by Fourier's theorem every wave is an integral $\int A(k)e^{i(kz - \omega t)}dk$.
- **Dispersion:** when $\omega(k)$ is not proportional to $k$, a packet around $k_0$ keeps its envelope moving at $v_{\text{group}} = d\omega/dk(k_0)$, while crests move at $v_{\text{phase}} = \omega/k$; with $v_{\text{phase}} > v_{\text{group}}$ they run forward through the envelope.
- **Example:** a 15 MHz pulse in a plasma with $\nu_p = 9$ MHz has $v_{\text{phase}} = 1.25c$, $v_{\text{group}} = 0.8c$, a wavelength of about 25 m, and takes 1.25 ms to cross 300 km.
