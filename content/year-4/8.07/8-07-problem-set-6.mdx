This set is about electric fields in matter: insulators whose atoms and molecules are stretched by a field into small dipoles. **This site has no lesson on dielectrics yet** (OCW has no 8.07 lecture notes for the lectures on dielectrics), so each problem states the few definitions it needs, and the introductory [8.02 lesson on dielectrics](/courses/8-02/capacitors-and-dielectrics) gives the physical picture. The dipoles themselves come from the [multipole expansion](/courses/8-07/multipole-expansion-traceless-tensors), and the force and torque on a dipole from the [notes on magnetic dipoles](/courses/8-07/magnetic-multipole-expansion-dipoles), where Guth recalls the electric case too (those notes are dated two weeks after this set was due). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 6 (the revised sheet of October 24, 2012), in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its due date and its reading assignment (a textbook chapter) are left out, but we keep the restriction the reading assignment states: only linear, isotropic materials. MIT's Problems 1, 2, 3 and 5 are only pointers to problems in two textbooks (Griffiths, and Jackson's *Classical Electrodynamics*), named by number. The textbooks are not used here: **Problems 1, 2, 3 and 5 are our own problems** on the subjects that MIT's titles name (a simple atomic model of polarizability, cavities in a dielectric, a point charge at the centre of a dielectric ball, and a concentric spherical capacitor half filled with dielectric), with MIT's points. For Problem 1 MIT adds one instruction of its own, which we keep: the electron cloud is rigid and shifts as a whole. Problems 4 and 6 are stated in full on MIT's sheet and are given here as MIT sets them, except that in Problem 6 the split into parts, the small-gap approximation and parts (c) and (d) are ours; MIT's diagram for Problem 6 is described in words. The revised sheet notes two corrections to the first version, both followed here (in Problem 6 the square's side is larger than $2R$). The first page of the sheet has no printed number; "p. 1" below means that page, and "p. 2" and "p. 3" are the printed numbers of the others.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `q/(4*pi*eps0*r^2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `pi` stands for $\pi$, `eps0` for $\varepsilon_0$, `er` for $\varepsilon_r$, and `E0`, `D0`, `V0`, `x0`, `p1`, `p2` for $E_0$, $D_0$, $V_0$, $x_0$, $p_1$, $p_2$; the other letters (`a`, `b`, `d`, `q`, `Q`, `r`, `R`) are typed as they are.

MIT gives the points per problem: 10 for Problem 1, 15 for Problem 2, 10 for Problem 3 and 15 each for Problems 4, 5 and 6, 80 points in all. The splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation: $\vec E$ is the electric field, $V$ the potential, and $\varepsilon_0$ the electric constant. In spherical coordinates $r$ is the distance from the origin and $\theta$ the angle from the $z$ axis. The unit vectors along the axes are $\hat e_x$, $\hat e_y$, $\hat e_z$, as on MIT's sheet, and $\hat r$ points away from the origin.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="A simple atomic model of polarizability (our own problem)" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "A simple atomic model of polarizability", and adds one instruction: treat the electron cloud as rigid, so that in an applied field it shifts as a whole relative to the point-like nucleus until the two are in equilibrium. The problem below is our own, on the same subject, with that instruction.

A field pulls the nucleus of an atom one way and its electrons the other, so the atom gets a small dipole moment $\vec p$. For weak fields $\vec p$ is proportional to the field, and the constant is the **polarizability** $\alpha$ of the atom (the standard definition):

<Formula label="The polarizability of an atom" tex="\vec p = \alpha\,\vec E" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (standard definition, our own statement)" />

Model an atom as a point nucleus of charge $+q$ inside a cloud of charge $-q$ spread evenly through a ball of radius $a$. With no field the nucleus sits at the centre of the ball. Now apply a uniform field $\vec E$.

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Show that the field of the cloud at a distance $s < a$ from its centre is $q\,s/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^3)$, pointing toward the centre. Then find how far the nucleus sits from the centre of the cloud once the two are in equilibrium, and find the polarizability $\alpha$ of the model atom in terms of $\varepsilon_0$ and $a$.

<Hint>

Use Gauss's law on a sphere of radius $s$ centred on the cloud. What fraction of the cloud's charge is inside it?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In equilibrium the force of the applied field on the nucleus, $q\vec E$, is balanced by the pull of the cloud on the nucleus. (The forces on the cloud then balance too, by Newton's third law.) The dipole moment is the charge $q$ times the separation of the two centres.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-alpha" label="$\alpha =$" expr="4*pi*eps0*a^3"
  vars={{ eps0: [0.5, 2], a: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", a: "a" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "4*pi*eps0*a^3/3", hint: "That still has the factor 1/3 of a ball's volume in it. It cancels: the charge density of the cloud is 3q/(4πa³), and Gauss's law gives the field ρs/(3ε₀), so the factors of 3 cancel and the field is qs/(4πε₀a³)." },
    { expr: "eps0*a^3", hint: "The factor 4π is missing. The field of the cloud is qs/(4πε₀a³), so setting it equal to E gives s = 4πε₀a³E/q." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Inside a uniformly charged ball the field grows in proportion to the distance from the centre, like the force of a spring. A shifted nucleus is pulled back by a force proportional to its shift, so the shift, and the dipole moment, are proportional to the applied field.

<Step title="The field inside the cloud">

The cloud has the charge density $\rho = -3q/(4\pi a^3)$. A sphere of radius $s < a$ about its centre encloses the charge $-q\,s^3/a^3$, so Gauss's law gives

<Formula label="The field inside the uniform cloud" tex="E_{\text{cloud}}(s)\cdot 4\pi s^2 = -\frac{q\,s^3}{\varepsilon_0\,a^3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec E_{\text{cloud}} = -\frac{q\,s}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,a^3}\,\hat s" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

where $\hat s$ points away from the centre of the cloud: the field points inward, as the problem says.

</Step>

<Step title="Equilibrium">

Let the nucleus sit at the displacement $\vec d$ from the centre of the cloud. The total force on it is zero:

<Formula label="The force balance on the nucleus" tex="q\,\vec E - \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,a^3}\,\vec d = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec d = \frac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,a^3}{q}\,\vec E" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The nucleus moves along the field and the cloud against it, as they should.

</Step>

<Step title="The polarizability">

The atom is a charge $+q$ at $\vec d$ and a charge $-q$ whose centre is at the origin, so its dipole moment is $\vec p = q\,\vec d$:

<Formula label="The polarizability of the model atom" tex="\vec p = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,a^3\,\vec E, \qquad \alpha = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,a^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\varepsilon_0$ is in C/(V m) and $a^3$ in m³, so $\alpha$ is in C m²/V, and $\alpha E$ is in C m, a dipole moment. The answer does not depend on $q$: a larger charge is pulled harder by the field, but held back more strongly by the cloud, in the same proportion. The formula holds only while $d < a$; part (b) shows that this is not a worry for laboratory fields.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Take a model atom of radius $a = 0.53 \times 10^{-10}$ m with $q = e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}$ C, in a field of $1.0 \times 10^{6}$ V/m. How far is the nucleus shifted from the centre of the cloud? Give the answer in femtometres (1 fm $= 10^{-15}$ m).

<Hint>

Use the shift $d = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^3 E/q$ from part (a), with $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854 \times 10^{-12}$ F/m.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-d" label="Shift of the nucleus, in femtometres:" value="0.103" tolerance="1.5%" calc="p1-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into the shift of part (a).

<Step title="The numbers">

$4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 1.113 \times 10^{-10}$ F/m and $a^3 = 1.489 \times 10^{-31}$ m³, so $\alpha = 1.656 \times 10^{-41}$ C m²/V. Then

$d = \alpha E/q = 1.656 \times 10^{-41} \times 1.0 \times 10^{6}/(1.602 \times 10^{-19})$ m $= 1.03 \times 10^{-16}$ m $= 0.103$ fm.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The shift is about $2 \times 10^{-6}$ of the atom's radius, so $d < a$ by a huge margin and the linear model holds. A field of a million volts per metre hardly deforms an atom: the field of the cloud at its own edge, $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 a^2) = 5.1 \times 10^{11}$ V/m at its edge, is half a million times stronger.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Cavities in dielectric media (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Cavities in dielectric media". The problem below is our own, on the same subject.

First the definitions used in Problems 2, 3, 5 and 6 (the standard ones). In a field the molecules of an insulator become small dipoles. The dipole moment per unit volume is the **polarization** $\vec P$. In a **linear, isotropic** material, the only kind MIT's sheet asks for, it is proportional to the field, with the electric susceptibility $\chi_e$ as constant (MIT's sheet, p. 1):

<Formula label="A linear, isotropic dielectric" tex="\vec P = \varepsilon_0\,\chi_e\,\vec E" cite="mit" at="reading assignment, p. 1" />

The **displacement** $\vec D$ is defined so that its sources are the free charges only (the charges put there from outside, not the ones that belong to the polarized molecules):

<Formula label="The displacement and its sources" tex="\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E + \vec P = \varepsilon\,\vec E, \qquad \varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\,(1 + \chi_e) = \varepsilon_0\,\varepsilon_r, \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_{\text{free}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (standard definitions, our own statement)" />

$\varepsilon$ is the permittivity of the material and $\varepsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e$ its relative permittivity (the dielectric constant), which is 1 for vacuum. Since $\nabla\times\vec E = 0$ and, where no free charge sits, $\nabla\cdot\vec D = 0$, two things hold at the boundary between two materials that carries no free charge: the component of $\vec E$ along the boundary is the same on both sides, and so is the component of $\vec D$ across it.

A large block of a linear dielectric with relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r$ has a uniform field $\vec E_0$ inside it, so $\vec D_0 = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0\vec E_0$. Small empty cavities (vacuum) are cut into it, far from each other and from the block's faces, so that each one disturbs the field only near itself. Find the fields at the centre of each cavity.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

The first cavity is a long thin needle, lined up with $\vec E_0$. Find $\vec E$ at its centre, and $\vec D$ at its centre in terms of $D_0$ and $\varepsilon_r$.

<Hint>

Most of the needle's wall runs along the field. Which component of which field is the same on both sides of such a wall? (The two small ends are far from the centre of a long needle.)

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-needle" label="$D$ at the centre of the needle $=$" expr="D0/er"
  vars={{ D0: [1, 3], er: [1.5, 6] }}
  names={{ D0: "D_0", er: "\\varepsilon_r" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "D0", hint: "D is continuous across a wall that the field crosses. The long wall of the needle runs along the field, so there it is E, the component along the wall, that is the same inside and outside." },
    { expr: "er*D0", hint: "That multiplies by εᵣ where it should divide. In the empty needle D is ε₀ times the field, and the field there equals the field in the material around it, so D is smaller than D₀, not larger." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the long side wall the field runs along the wall, and the component of $\vec E$ along a boundary is continuous.

<Step title="The field">

Near the middle of a long needle the field is along the wall on both sides, so $\vec E = \vec E_0$ inside. The ends, where the field crosses the wall, are far away and change little at the centre.

</Step>

<Step title="The displacement">

The needle is empty, so $\vec D = \varepsilon_0\vec E = \varepsilon_0\vec E_0 = \vec D_0/\varepsilon_r$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Seen from inside the needle, the polarization of the material does not reach in: the bound charges sit only on the small ends, far away, so the field is just $E_0$. For $\varepsilon_r = 1$ the "material" is vacuum too and $D = D_0$, as it must be.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

The second cavity is a thin flat wafer, perpendicular to $\vec E_0$. Find $\vec D$ at its centre, and $\vec E$ at its centre in terms of $E_0$ and $\varepsilon_r$.

<Hint>

Now most of the wall is crossed by the field at right angles. Which component is continuous across such a wall?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-wafer" label="$E$ at the centre of the wafer $=$" expr="er*E0"
  vars={{ E0: [1, 3], er: [1.5, 6] }}
  names={{ E0: "E_0", er: "\\varepsilon_r" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "E0", hint: "That would hold if E were continuous across the wall. The wafer's faces are crossed by the field at right angles, and across such a face it is D, not E, that is the same on both sides." },
    { expr: "E0/er", hint: "That has the ratio upside down. Across the face D is the same, D₀ = εᵣε₀E₀ outside and ε₀E inside, so E = εᵣE₀, larger than E₀." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the large faces of the wafer the field crosses the wall, and the component of $\vec D$ across a boundary with no free charge is continuous.

<Step title="The displacement and the field">

$\vec D = \vec D_0$ inside the wafer, and since it is empty, $\vec E = \vec D_0/\varepsilon_0 = \varepsilon_r\vec E_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The bound charges $\pm P$ per unit area on the two faces of the wafer make a field $P/\varepsilon_0$ in the gap, like a capacitor, which adds to $E_0$: $E_0 + \varepsilon_0\chi_e E_0/\varepsilon_0 = (1 + \chi_e)E_0 = \varepsilon_r E_0$. Same answer.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

The third cavity is a sphere of radius $a$. Find the field inside it. (Take $\vec E_0$ along $z$ and solve for the potential inside and outside the sphere.)

<Hint>

Far from the cavity the potential is $-E_0 r\cos\theta$. Try the terms of the Legendre series with $\ell = 1$ only: $-E_c r\cos\theta$ inside (finite at the centre), and $-E_0 r\cos\theta + B\cos\theta/r^2$ outside.

</Hint>

<Hint>

There is no free charge on the wall of the cavity. So at $r = a$ the potential is continuous (that makes the component of $\vec E$ along the wall continuous), and $\varepsilon\,\partial V/\partial r$ is the same on both sides, with $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0$ outside and $\varepsilon_0$ inside.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-sphere" label="$E$ inside the sphere $=$" expr="3*er*E0/(2*er + 1)"
  vars={{ E0: [1, 3], er: [1.5, 6] }}
  names={{ E0: "E_0", er: "\\varepsilon_r" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "3*E0/(er + 2)", hint: "That is the field inside a ball of dielectric placed in vacuum: the roles of the two materials are swapped. For εᵣ > 1 it is smaller than E₀, but an empty hole in a dielectric has a stronger field than the material around it." },
    { expr: "(er + 2)*E0/3", hint: "That is E₀ + P/(3ε₀): the field in the hole if the polarization of the material around it stayed uniform and unchanged. In a linear dielectric it changes near the hole, because the field there changes, so the boundary conditions must be solved." },
    { expr: "er*E0", hint: "That is the wafer of part (b). A sphere is in between the needle and the wafer: its wall is crossed by the field near the poles and runs along it near the equator." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** As for a metal sphere in a uniform field, only the $\ell = 1$ terms are needed: the boundary conditions have the angular factor $\cos\theta$ on both sides, so they reduce to two equations for two constants.

<Step title="The potentials">

Inside, $V_{\text{in}} = -E_c r\cos\theta$: a uniform field $E_c$ along $z$. Outside, $V_{\text{out}} = -E_0 r\cos\theta + B\cos\theta/r^2$: the applied field plus a dipole that the cavity adds.

</Step>

<Step title="The two conditions at the wall">

$V$ continuous at $r = a$: $-E_c a = -E_0 a + B/a^2$. The radial component of $\vec D$ continuous: $\varepsilon_0 E_c = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0\left(E_0 + 2B/a^3\right)$. With $\beta = B/a^3$ these are

<Formula label="The conditions on the wall of the spherical cavity" tex="E_c = E_0 - \beta, \qquad E_c = \varepsilon_r\left(E_0 + 2\beta\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

so $\beta\,(2\varepsilon_r + 1) = E_0(1 - \varepsilon_r)$ and

<Formula label="The field in a spherical cavity" tex="E_c = E_0 - \beta = \frac{3\,\varepsilon_r}{2\varepsilon_r + 1}\,E_0" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\varepsilon_r = 1$ the cavity is not there and $E_c = E_0$. For $\varepsilon_r > 1$, $\beta < 0$. The bound charge on the wall, $\vec P\cdot\hat n$ with $\hat n$ pointing out of the dielectric into the hole, is negative near the top pole and positive near the bottom, and its field in the hole points up, which strengthens the field there. For very large $\varepsilon_r$, $E_c \to \frac32 E_0$. The field is uniform inside, so the centre is not special for a sphere.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

For $\varepsilon_r = 4$, what is the field at the centre of the spherical cavity divided by $E_0$? Give it as a decimal. Put the three cavities in order of their field.

<Answer id="p2-num" label="$E_c/E_0$ for $\varepsilon_r = 4$:" value="1.333" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put $\varepsilon_r = 4$ into part (c).

<Step title="The number">

$E_c/E_0 = 3 \times 4/(2 \times 4 + 1) = 12/9 = 1.333$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The needle has $E/E_0 = 1$ and the wafer $E/E_0 = 4$, so the sphere lies between them, as its shape does.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="A point charge at the centre of a spherical dielectric (our own problem)" points={10} mit="Problem 3">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "A point charge at the center of a spherical dielectric". The problem below is our own, on the same subject. It uses the definitions at the start of Problem 2 and two more (also standard): a polarization $\vec P$ acts like a volume density of bound charge $\rho_b$ inside the material and a surface density $\sigma_b$ on its surface, where $\hat n$ is the outward normal of the surface,

<Formula label="The bound charges" tex="\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\vec P, \qquad \sigma_b = \vec P\cdot\hat n" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (standard definitions, our own statement)" />

A point charge $q$ sits at the centre of a ball of radius $R$ made of a linear dielectric with relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r$. Outside the ball is vacuum.

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Find the displacement $\vec D$ and the field $\vec E$ inside the ball, at a distance $r$ from the centre.

<Hint>

The only free charge is $q$. Use the symmetry and Gauss's law for $\vec D$ on a sphere of radius $r$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-ein" label="$E$ inside the ball $=$" expr="q/(4*pi*er*eps0*r^2)"
  vars={{ q: [1, 3], er: [1.5, 6], eps0: [0.5, 2], r: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ q: "q", er: "\\varepsilon_r", eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", r: "r" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "q/(4*pi*eps0*r^2)", hint: "That is D/ε₀, the field in vacuum. Inside the material E = D/ε with ε = εᵣε₀: the polarized molecules screen part of the charge." },
    { expr: "er*q/(4*pi*eps0*r^2)", hint: "The dielectric weakens the field, it does not strengthen it: E = D/(εᵣε₀), so εᵣ goes in the denominator." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** $\vec D$ has the free charges as its only sources, and the free charge is the point charge alone.

<Step title="The displacement">

By symmetry $\vec D = D(r)\,\hat r$, and Gauss's law for $\vec D$ on a sphere of radius $r$ gives $4\pi r^2 D = q$:

<Formula label="The displacement around the charge" tex="\vec D = \frac{q}{4\pi r^2}\,\hat r \qquad \text{(inside and outside the ball)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The field">

Inside, $\vec E = \vec D/(\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0) = q\,\hat r/(4\pi\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0 r^2)$. Outside, $\vec E = q\,\hat r/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $r = R$, the radial component of $\vec D$ is continuous, as it must be with no free charge on the surface, and $E$ jumps up by the factor $\varepsilon_r$ on the way out. A symmetric solution that satisfies $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho_{\text{free}}$ and $\nabla\times\vec E = 0$ (a radial field that depends on $r$ only has no curl) is the solution.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the polarization $\vec P$ inside the ball and the bound surface charge density $\sigma_b$ on its surface. What is the bound charge density $\rho_b$ inside the ball, away from the centre? The total bound charge of a neutral piece of material is zero: where is the bound charge that balances the charge on the surface?

<Hint>

$\vec P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e\vec E$ with $\chi_e = \varepsilon_r - 1$. For $\rho_b$ remember that $\hat r/r^2$ has zero divergence everywhere except at the origin.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Compare the field inside with the field of $q$ alone in vacuum. What does Gauss's law say about the total charge inside a small sphere around $q$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-sigma" label="$\sigma_b$ on the surface $=$" expr="(er - 1)*q/(4*pi*er*R^2)"
  vars={{ q: [1, 3], er: [1.5, 6], R: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ q: "q", er: "\\varepsilon_r", R: "R" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(er - 1)*q/(4*pi*R^2)", hint: "That uses the field of the bare charge, q/(4πε₀R²), in P = ε₀χₑE. Inside the material the field is smaller by the factor εᵣ." },
    { expr: "-(er - 1)*q/(4*pi*er*R^2)", hint: "The sign is off. P points away from the positive charge, along the outward normal, so σ_b = P · n̂ is positive for q > 0." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-where" label="The bound charge that balances the surface charge sits" choices={["spread evenly through the volume of the ball", "on the outer surface, next to the positive bound charge", "nowhere: the bound charges of this ball add up to more than zero", "at the centre, right next to the point charge q"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The polarization follows from the field of part (a). Its divergence vanishes everywhere except at the centre, where the molecules next to the charge leave a point-like bound charge.

<Step title="The polarization">

<Formula label="The polarization in the ball" tex="\vec P = \varepsilon_0\,\chi_e\,\vec E = \frac{\varepsilon_r - 1}{\varepsilon_r}\,\frac{q}{4\pi r^2}\,\hat r" cite="mit" at="Problem 3, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The bound charges">

</Step>

<Step title="The total charge at r = a">

Below, the free charge is $\varepsilon_r Q/(2\pi(1 + \varepsilon_r)a^2)$, and adding $\sigma_b$ leaves $Q/(2\pi(1 + \varepsilon_r)a^2)$. Above, there is no dielectric, and the free charge is $\varepsilon_0 E(a) = Q/(2\pi(1 + \varepsilon_r)a^2)$. The two totals agree.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is why the field is the same in both halves: the field in vacuum is made by all the charges, free and bound, and they are spread evenly over the inner sphere. (At the outer sphere the same happens with the signs reversed.)

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="e" points={2}>

Take $a = 1.0$ cm, $b = 2.0$ cm and $\varepsilon_r = 3.0$. Find the capacitance in picofarads.

<Answer id="p5-num" label="$C$, in pF:" value="4.45" tolerance="1%" calc="p5-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into part (b).

<Step title="The number">

$C = 2\pi \times 8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 4.0 \times (0.010 \times 0.020/0.010)$ F $= 4.45 \times 10^{-12}$ F $= 4.45$ pF.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Empty, the capacitor would have $4\pi\varepsilon_0 \times 0.020$ m $= 2.23$ pF; half filled with $\varepsilon_r = 3$ it has $(1 + 3)/2 = 2$ times that.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Force on a dielectric slab part way inside a circular capacitor" points={15} mit="Problem 6">

A capacitor is made of two thin circular metal disks of radius $R$, parallel to the $x$-$y$ plane and centred on the $z$ axis, one at $z = +\frac12 d$ and the other at $z = -\frac12 d$. They are held at the potential difference $V_0$. A square sheet of linear dielectric, with relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r$, a thickness just under $d$ and a side larger than $2R$, is pushed part way in between the disks. Its left-hand edge is the line $x = x_0$. Seen from above (MIT's diagram), the disks are a circle of radius $R$ about the origin, and the square covers all of the circle to the right of the line $x = x_0$; its other three edges lie outside the circle. Find the force on the sheet as a function of $x_0$. Take $d$ much smaller than $R$, so that the field between the disks is uniform and the fringing field at their rim does not change the capacitance noticeably (our assumption).

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the capacitance $C(x_0)$ for $-R \le x_0 \le R$.

<Hint>

The part of the disk covered by the sheet and the part that is empty act like two capacitors in parallel, with capacitance $\varepsilon\times$(area)$/d$ each.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The covered part is the piece of the circle to the right of a chord at $x = x_0$. Its area is the integral of the chord length $2\sqrt{R^2 - x^2}$ from $x_0$ to $R$, which is $R^2\arccos(x_0/R) - x_0\sqrt{R^2 - x_0^2}$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-c" label="$C(x_0) =$" expr="eps0*(pi*R^2 + (er - 1)*(R^2*acos(x0/R) - x0*sqrt(R^2 - x0^2)))/d"
  vars={{ eps0: [0.5, 2], er: [1.5, 6], R: [2, 3], x0: [-1.5, 1.5], d: [0.1, 0.5] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", er: "\\varepsilon_r", R: "R", x0: "x_0", d: "d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "eps0*(pi*R^2 + (er - 1)*(pi*R^2 - R^2*acos(x0/R) + x0*sqrt(R^2 - x0^2)))/d", hint: "That puts the dielectric in the part of the disk to the left of the edge. The sheet lies to the right of x = x₀: as x₀ falls toward −R it covers more of the disk." },
    { expr: "eps0*er*(R^2*acos(x0/R) - x0*sqrt(R^2 - x0^2))/d", hint: "That leaves out the empty part of the disk, which still holds charge: the two parts are in parallel and their capacitances add." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $d \ll R$ the field between the disks is $V_0/d$ everywhere, in the sheet as well as in the gap (the field runs along the sheet's edge, so it is continuous there). Each piece of area is a small parallel-plate capacitor.

<Step title="The two areas">

The covered area is

<Formula label="The area of the disk covered by the sheet" tex="A(x_0) = \int_{x_0}^{R} 2\sqrt{R^2 - x^2}\,dx = R^2\arccos\frac{x_0}{R} - x_0\sqrt{R^2 - x_0^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

and the empty area is $\pi R^2 - A$.

</Step>

<Step title="The capacitance">

<Formula label="The capacitance with the sheet part way in" tex="C(x_0) = \frac{\varepsilon_0\left(\pi R^2 - A\right) + \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0\,A}{d} = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{d}\left[\pi R^2 + (\varepsilon_r - 1)\,A(x_0)\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $x_0 = R$ the area is $A = 0$ and $C = \varepsilon_0\pi R^2/d$, the empty capacitor. At $x_0 = -R$, $\arccos(-1) = \pi$, so $A = \pi R^2$ and $C = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0\pi R^2/d$, the full one. At $x_0 = 0$ half is covered.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find the force on the sheet, $F_x$, as a function of $x_0$ for $-R < x_0 < R$. (Positive $F_x$ pushes the sheet to the right, out of the capacitor.)

<Hint>

Move the sheet a small step $\delta x_0$ while the battery holds $V_0$ fixed. The battery does work $V_0\,\delta Q$, the stored energy $\frac12 CV_0^2$ changes, and the rest is the mechanical work $F_x\,\delta x_0$ done by the field on the sheet.

</Hint>

<Hint>

With $\delta Q = V_0\,\delta C$, the battery supplies twice the change of the stored energy. The derivative of $A(x_0)$ is minus the chord length at $x_0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-f" label="$F_x =$" expr="-eps0*(er - 1)*V0^2*sqrt(R^2 - x0^2)/d"
  vars={{ eps0: [0.5, 2], er: [1.5, 6], V0: [1, 3], R: [2, 3], x0: [-1.5, 1.5], d: [0.1, 0.5] }}
  names={{ eps0: "\\varepsilon_0", er: "\\varepsilon_r", V0: "V_0", R: "R", x0: "x_0", d: "d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "eps0*(er - 1)*V0^2*sqrt(R^2 - x0^2)/d", hint: "The sign is off: that is −d(½CV₀²)/dx₀ with V₀ held fixed, which leaves out the work done by the battery. At fixed V₀ the battery supplies twice the change of stored energy, so F_x = +½V₀² dC/dx₀ (the same as −d(Q²/2C)/dx₀ at fixed charge), and C falls as x₀ grows." },
    { expr: "-2*eps0*(er - 1)*V0^2*sqrt(R^2 - x0^2)/d", hint: "That is V₀² dC/dx₀, twice the force: it counts the battery's work but leaves out the half of it that goes into the stored energy ½CV₀²." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Energy conservation for a small move at fixed voltage: the battery's work equals the change of the stored energy plus the mechanical work.

<Step title="The energy balance">

<Formula label="The energy balance at fixed voltage" tex="V_0\,\delta Q = \delta\!\left(\tfrac12 C V_0^2\right) + F_x\,\delta x_0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_x = V_0^2\,\frac{dC}{dx_0} - \frac12 V_0^2\,\frac{dC}{dx_0} = \frac12 V_0^2\,\frac{dC}{dx_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The derivative">

$dA/dx_0 = -2\sqrt{R^2 - x_0^2}$, minus the chord length, so $dC/dx_0 = -2\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)\sqrt{R^2 - x_0^2}/d$ and

<Formula label="The force on the sheet" tex="F_x = -\frac{\varepsilon_0\,(\varepsilon_r - 1)\,V_0^2}{d}\,\sqrt{R^2 - x_0^2} \qquad (-R < x_0 < R)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 3 (our own solution)" />

It points toward $-x$: the sheet is pulled into the capacitor. For $x_0 > R$ (the sheet outside) and $x_0 < -R$ (the disk fully covered) $C$ does not change with $x_0$ and there is no force (in our approximation, which leaves out the fringing field).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The same at fixed charge: $F_x = -\frac{d}{dx_0}\left(\frac{Q^2}{2C}\right) = \frac{Q^2}{2C^2}\frac{dC}{dx_0} = \frac12 V_0^2\frac{dC}{dx_0}$, the same force at the same position, as it must be: the force depends on where the charges are, not on what holds them there. Written as $\frac{\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)}{2}\left(\frac{V_0}{d}\right)^2 \times d \times 2\sqrt{R^2 - x_0^2}$, it is a pressure $\frac12\varepsilon_0(\varepsilon_r - 1)E^2$ times the area $d\times(\text{chord})$ of the sheet's cross-section at its edge, the same form as for a rectangular capacitor.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Where between $x_0 = -R$ and $x_0 = R$ is the pull strongest?

<Answer id="p6-where" label="The pull is strongest" choices={["at x₀ = −R, when the sheet nearly fills the capacitor", "at x₀ = +R, just as the edge of the sheet enters", "at x₀ = 0, when the edge of the sheet crosses the centre", "nowhere in particular: it is the same for every x₀ in between"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The force is proportional to the chord length at $x_0$.

<Step title="The answer">

$\sqrt{R^2 - x_0^2}$ is largest at $x_0 = 0$, where the chord is a diameter, and falls to zero at $x_0 = \pm R$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For a rectangular capacitor the edge always has the same length and the force is the same for every position: here only the shape of the plates makes it vary.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="d" points={2}>

Take $R = 5.0$ cm, $d = 1.0$ mm, $V_0 = 1.0$ kV, $\varepsilon_r = 3.0$ and $x_0 = 3.0$ cm. Find the size of the force in millinewtons.

<Answer id="p6-num" label="Size of the force, in mN:" value="0.708" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Put the numbers into part (b).

<Step title="The number">

The half chord is $\sqrt{5.0^2 - 3.0^2}$ cm $= 4.0$ cm, so the size of the force is $8.854 \times 10^{-12} \times 2.0 \times (1.0 \times 10^{3})^2 \times 0.040/(1.0 \times 10^{-3})$ N $= 7.08 \times 10^{-4}$ N $= 0.708$ mN.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $x_0 = 0$ the half chord is 5.0 cm and the force $0.885$ mN, the largest value, as part (c) says. Both are small: the weight of a gram is about 10 mN.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
