This set practises the conservation laws of electrodynamics: the energy, momentum and angular momentum that the fields themselves carry, the Maxwell stress tensor that describes the flow of momentum, and the [plane waves](/courses/8-07/electromagnetic-plane-waves-reflection) of Lecture Notes 14, with their energy, momentum and reflection. The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 10, in our own words. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own. OCW has no 8.07 lecture notes on the conservation laws (the class lectures on them fall between Lecture Notes 11 and 14), so the definitions this set needs are stated in the problems.

Several things differ from MIT's sheet. Its due date and its reading assignment (textbook chapters) are left out. MIT's Problems 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6 are only pointers to problems in a textbook (Griffiths), named by number. The textbook is not used here: **Problems 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6 are our own problems** on the subjects that MIT's titles name, with MIT's points. Two of these pointers come with a remark of MIT's, which is kept: in Problem 4, that the energy flux of sunlight is large although its pressure is tiny, and in Problem 5, that every calculation is to be done with time-averaged quantities. Problems 3 and 7 are stated in full on MIT's sheet and are given here as MIT sets them, rewritten. Our additions to them are marked where they stand: the box for the dipole moment in Problem 3(a), the assumption of slow change in Problem 3(b) and the three choice boxes of Problem 7.

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `mu0*q*w/(8*pi*R)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer with the letters of that box. Write the symbols exactly as the box names them: `pi` stands for $\pi$, `eps0` for $\varepsilon_0$, `mu0` for $\mu_0$, `w` for $\omega$, `wd` for $\dot\omega$, `th` for $\theta$, `b` for $\beta$ and `E0` for $E_0$; the other letters (`q`, `Q`, `s`, `d`, `B`, `I`, `R`, `A`) are typed as they are.

MIT gives the points per problem: 15 each for Problems 1 and 2, 25 for Problem 3, 10 for Problem 4 and 15 each for Problems 5 and 6, 95 points in all, plus 15 points of extra credit for Problem 7, which is optional here. MIT does not split any problem into points per part; all the splits between parts are ours. Parts that ask you to show something have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The notation is that of the lecture notes, in SI units. $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ are the electric and magnetic fields, $\varepsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ the electric and magnetic constants, with $\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1/c^2$. $u$ is the energy density of the fields and $\vec S$ the Poynting vector. In index notation $E_i$ are the components of $\vec E$ and $\delta_{ij}$ is the Kronecker delta. In spherical coordinates $\theta$ is the angle from the $z$ axis and $\hat\phi$ points around it. Angle brackets $\langle\ \rangle$ are averages over one period of a wave.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="The force between two point charges from the Maxwell stress tensor (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 1">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Calculating the force between two point charges using the Maxwell stress tensor". The problem below is our own, on the same subject.

The **Maxwell stress tensor** is built from the fields at a point: $T_{ij} = \varepsilon_0\left(E_iE_j - \frac12\,\delta_{ij}\,E^2\right) + \frac{1}{\mu_0}\left(B_iB_j - \frac12\,\delta_{ij}\,B^2\right)$. For static fields, the total electromagnetic force on all the charges inside a closed surface is the surface integral of the tensor, $F_i = \oint T_{ij}\,n_j\,da$, with $\hat n$ the outward normal of the region. (These are standard definitions, stated by us: MIT's sheet only names the tensor, and the OCW 8.07 notes do not print it.)

So the force can be found from the fields on any surface that separates the charges, without knowing how the fields look anywhere else.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Two equal point charges $q$ sit on the $z$ axis at $z = +d$ and $z = -d$. Find the size of the electric field on the plane $z = 0$, at the distance $s$ from the axis, and its direction.

<Hint>

Add the two fields as vectors. By symmetry, their $z$ components cancel on the plane.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-e" label="$E(s) =$" expr="q*s/(2*pi*eps0*(s^2+d^2)^(3/2))"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], s: [0.2, 3], d: [0.5, 2], eps0: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ q: "q", s: "s", d: "d", eps0: "\\varepsilon_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "q*s/(4*pi*eps0*(s^2+d^2)^(3/2))", hint: "That is the part along the plane of the field of one charge. The other charge gives the same part in the same direction, so the two add." },
    { expr: "q/(2*pi*eps0*(s^2+d^2))", hint: "That adds the sizes of the two fields. Their components along z cancel; only the components along the plane add, and each is a fraction s/√(s² + d²) of its field." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** On the mid-plane each charge is at the distance $\sqrt{s^2 + d^2}$; the parts of the two fields along $z$ are opposite, and the parts along the plane are equal.

<Step title="Add the two fields">

The charge at $z = +d$ gives at the point $(s, 0, 0)$ the field $\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{(s, 0, -d)}{(s^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}$, the one at $z = -d$ the field $\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{(s, 0, +d)}{(s^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}$. The sum is

<Formula label="The field on the mid-plane, equal charges" tex="\vec E = \frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{s}{\left(s^2 + d^2\right)^{3/2}}\,\hat s" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

pointing away from the axis, along $\hat s$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $s = 0$ the field vanishes, as it must halfway between two equal charges. Far away, $s \gg d$, it tends to $2q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0 s^2)$, the field of the total charge $2q$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Take as the closed surface the plane $z = 0$ together with a hemisphere of very large radius above it, so that it encloses the upper charge. Use the stress tensor to find the $z$ component of the force on the upper charge.

<Hint>

On the hemisphere the fields fall off like $1/r^2$, so $T_{ij}$ falls off like $1/r^4$ while the area grows only like $r^2$: that part of the surface gives nothing in the limit.

</Hint>

<Hint>

On the plane, the outward normal of the upper region is $-\hat z$, so you need $T_{zz}$ only. The field there has no $z$ component, which makes $T_{zz}$ simple. Then integrate over rings of radius $s$ and width $ds$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-f" label="$F_z =$" expr="q^2/(16*pi*eps0*d^2)"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], d: [0.5, 2], eps0: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ q: "q", d: "d", eps0: "\\varepsilon_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-q^2/(16*pi*eps0*d^2)", hint: "Check the sign of the normal. The region above the plane has its outward normal along −z on the plane, and T_zz is negative there, so the force points along +z: the charges repel." },
    { expr: "q^2/(8*pi*eps0*d^2)", hint: "That is twice the right answer. T_zz on the plane is −ε₀E²/2, not −ε₀E²: with E along the plane, E_z E_z is zero and only the −½δ_zz E² term is left." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The momentum flows through the plane between the charges. With $E_z = 0$ there, $T_{zz}$ is just minus half of $\varepsilon_0 E^2$.

<Step title="The stress on the plane">

There is no magnetic field. On the plane $E_z = 0$, so

<Formula label="The zz component on the mid-plane" tex="T_{zz} = \varepsilon_0\left(E_z^2 - \frac12\,E^2\right) = -\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\,E^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

With $\hat n = -\hat z$, $F_z = \int T_{zj}n_j\,da = -\int T_{zz}\,da = +\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,da$.

</Step>

<Step title="The integral">

With $da = 2\pi s\,ds$ and the field of part (a),

<Formula label="The force on the upper charge" tex="F_z = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\left(\frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\right)^2 2\pi\int_0^\infty \frac{s^3\,ds}{\left(s^2 + d^2\right)^3} = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{1}{4d^2} = \frac{q^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0 d^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The integral is $1/(4d^2)$: put $x = s^2 + d^2$, so $s^3\,ds = \frac12(x - d^2)\,dx$, and $\frac12\int_{d^2}^\infty (x - d^2)x^{-3}\,dx = \frac12\left(\frac{1}{d^2} - \frac{1}{2d^2}\right)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

This is Coulomb's law, $q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0(2d)^2)$, for two charges $2d$ apart, and it points along $+z$: they repel. The stress tensor never looked at the charges themselves, only at the field on a plane between them.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Now make the lower charge $-q$, keeping $+q$ at $z = +d$. Find the $z$ component of the force on the upper charge, again from the stress tensor on the plane $z = 0$.

<Hint>

Add the two fields again. This time the components along the plane cancel, and the field on the plane is along $z$. What is $T_{zz}$ then?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1-fopp" label="$F_z =$" expr="-q^2/(16*pi*eps0*d^2)"
  vars={{ q: [0.5, 2], d: [0.5, 2], eps0: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ q: "q", d: "d", eps0: "\\varepsilon_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "q^2/(16*pi*eps0*d^2)", hint: "With the field along z on the plane, T_zz = ε₀(E_z² − ½E_z²) = +ε₀E²/2 is positive: a tension, not a pressure. With the normal −z the force on the upper charge points down, towards the other charge." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** For opposite charges the field crosses the plane at right angles. The field lines then pull the two sides together, like stretched strings.

<Step title="The field on the plane">

The charge $+q$ at $z = d$ gives $\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(s, 0, -d)}{(s^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}$, and $-q$ at $z = -d$ gives $-\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(s, 0, d)}{(s^2 + d^2)^{3/2}}$. The sum is

<Formula label="The field on the mid-plane, opposite charges" tex="\vec E = -\frac{q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{d}{\left(s^2 + d^2\right)^{3/2}}\,\hat z" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The force">

Now $T_{zz} = \varepsilon_0(E_z^2 - \frac12 E_z^2) = +\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2$, and $F_z = -\int T_{zz}\,da$:

<Formula label="The force on the upper charge, opposite charges" tex="F_z = -\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\left(\frac{q\,d}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\right)^2 2\pi\int_0^\infty \frac{s\,ds}{\left(s^2 + d^2\right)^3} = -\frac{q^2 d^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{1}{4d^4} = -\frac{q^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0 d^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Again Coulomb's law for the distance $2d$, now attractive. Along a field line the stress tensor is a tension $\frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2$; across field lines it is a pressure of the same size. Part (b) used the pressure, part (c) the tension.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Momentum stored in a parallel-plate capacitor in a uniform magnetic field (our own problem)" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

MIT's sheet gives only the number of a textbook problem here, under the title "Momentum storage in a parallel-plate capacitor in a uniform magnetic field". The problem below is our own, on the same subject.

Fields carry momentum. Its density is the Poynting vector divided by $c^2$:

<Formula label="The momentum density of the fields" tex="\vec{\mathcal P}_{\text{EM}} = \frac{1}{c^2}\,\vec S = \varepsilon_0\,\vec E \times \vec B" cite="reading1" at="slide 12, eq. (25)" />

(the second form uses $\vec S = \vec E \times \vec B/\mu_0$ and $\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1/c^2$). A parallel-plate capacitor has plates of area $A$, a distance $d$ apart, with $A$ so large compared with $d^2$ that the edges can be ignored. The lower plate, at $z = 0$, carries the charge $+Q$ and the upper plate, at $z = d$, the charge $-Q$. The capacitor sits in a uniform magnetic field $\vec B = B\,\hat x$, parallel to the plates, made by a magnet far away.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the total momentum in the fields between the plates: its direction, and its $y$ component in terms of $Q$, $B$ and $d$. Then put in numbers: plates of area $A = 0.0100$ m² a distance $d = 1.00$ mm apart, charged to $V = 1.00$ kV, in a field $B = 2.00$ T. Give the momentum in units of $10^{-10}$ kg m/s.

<Hint>

Between the plates $\vec E = (\sigma/\varepsilon_0)\,\hat z$ with $\sigma = Q/A$. Work out $\hat z \times \hat x$, and multiply the density by the volume $A\,d$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-p" label="$\mathcal P_y =$" expr="Q*B*d"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 2], B: [0.5, 2], d: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ Q: "Q", B: "B", d: "d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-Q*B*d", hint: "Check the direction: E points from the positive lower plate to the negative upper one, along +z, and ẑ × x̂ = +ŷ." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2-num" scale="1e-10" label="$\mathcal P_y$ in units of $10^{-10}$ kg m/s:" value="1.771" tolerance="1%" calc="p2-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The fields are uniform between the plates and zero outside, so the momentum is the density times the volume.

<Step title="The momentum">

$\vec E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0 A}\,\hat z$, so $\varepsilon_0\,\vec E \times \vec B = \frac{Q B}{A}\,(\hat z \times \hat x) = \frac{QB}{A}\,\hat y$. Times the volume $A\,d$:

<Formula label="The momentum stored in the capacitor" tex="\vec{\mathcal P} = Q\,B\,d\;\hat y" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

The area has dropped out.

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

$Q = CV = \varepsilon_0 A V/d = 8.854\times10^{-12} \times 0.0100 \times 1000/0.00100 = 8.854\times10^{-8}$ C, and

<Formula label="The stored momentum in numbers" tex="\mathcal P_y = Q\,B\,d = 8.854\times10^{-8} \times 2.00 \times 1.00\times10^{-3}\ \text{kg m/s} = 1.771\times10^{-10}\ \text{kg m/s}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own numbers)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: C T m = C (kg/(C s)) m = kg m/s, a momentum. The same number comes from $\varepsilon_0 E B$ times the volume: $8.854\times10^{-12} \times 10^6 \times 2.00 \times 10^{-5} = 1.771\times10^{-10}$ kg m/s.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Now the plates are joined by a thin resistive wire that runs straight along $z$ from one plate to the other, inside the capacitor. While the current $I$ flows, what is the $y$ component of the magnetic force on the wire? Show that, by the time the capacitor is empty, the total impulse on the wire equals the momentum that was stored in the fields.

<Hint>

The force on a straight wire is $I\,\vec L \times \vec B$. Which way does the current run, from which plate to which?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-f" label="$F_y =$" expr="I*B*d"
  vars={{ I: [0.5, 2], B: [0.5, 2], d: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ I: "I", B: "B", d: "d" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-I*B*d", hint: "The current flows from the positive plate at z = 0 to the negative one at z = d, along +z, and ẑ × x̂ = +ŷ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The discharge current crosses the magnetic field, so the wire is pushed sideways. Summed over the discharge, the push carries off exactly the momentum the fields lose.

<Step title="The force">

The current runs from $+Q$ to $-Q$, along $+\hat z$, over the length $d$:

<Formula label="The force on the wire" tex="\vec F = I\,d\,\hat z \times B\,\hat x = I\,B\,d\;\hat y" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The impulse">

With $I = -dQ/dt$, the impulse is $\int F_y\,dt = B\,d\int I\,dt = Q\,B\,d$, the whole charge having flowed through the wire. It points along $+\hat y$, as the stored momentum of part (a) did. As the charge drains away, $\vec E$ and with it the field momentum go to zero, and the capacitor with its wire picks up the same momentum.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

How fast the capacitor is emptied does not matter: a large current for a short time and a small one for a long time give the same $\int I\,dt = Q$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Go back to the charged capacitor, with no wire. This time the magnet is switched off: $B$ falls slowly from its value to zero. The changing field induces an electric field, which pushes on the charges of the plates. What total impulse does the capacitor receive?

<Hint>

Faraday's law with $\vec B = B(t)\,\hat x$: try an induced field $E_y(z)$ that depends on $z$ only. Its curl has an $x$ component $-\partial E_y/\partial z$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Only the difference $E_y(0) - E_y(d)$ matters for the net force on $+Q$ at $z = 0$ and $-Q$ at $z = d$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2-off" label="The impulse on the capacitor is" choices={["zero: no current flows, so there is no force on the capacitor", "Q d B along +ŷ, the momentum that was stored in the field", "Q d B along −ŷ, opposite to the stored momentum", "half the stored momentum; the rest stays in the field"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** However the field momentum disappears, the same momentum must turn up in the matter. Here it is delivered by the induced electric field.

<Step title="The induced field">

$\nabla \times \vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t$ has the $x$ component $-\partial E_y/\partial z = -\dot B$, so $E_y = \dot B\,z + \text{const}$. (Other induced fields are possible, but for any field that does not change along $y$, Faraday's law on a rectangle in the $yz$ plane with sides at $z = 0$ and $z = d$ fixes the difference $E_y(0) - E_y(d) = -\dot B\,d$, and only this difference enters the force on the plates.)

</Step>

<Step title="The impulse">

The net force on the plates is

<Formula label="The force while the field changes" tex="F_y = Q\,E_y(0) - Q\,E_y(d) = -Q\,\dot B\,d" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 1 (our own solution)" />

and over the switch-off $\int F_y\,dt = -Q\,d\,\Delta B = Q\,B\,d$, since $\Delta B = -B$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Again $Q\,B\,d$ along $+\hat y$, the momentum of part (a). In parts (b) and (c) the fields disappear in different ways, through $\vec E$ and through $\vec B$, and both times the matter receives what the fields held.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 3

<Exercise id="p3" title="Angular momentum and a rotating shell of charge" points={25} mit="Problem 3">

A charge $Q$ is spread evenly over a spherical surface of radius $R$, which rotates about the $z$ axis with angular velocity $\omega$.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

Write down the magnetic field inside and outside the sphere, and the electrostatic field inside and outside. (MIT says that these may be taken from a book, the notes or earlier homework.) In the boxes: the magnetic dipole moment $m$ of the spinning shell (this box is our addition, a step towards the field outside), and the size of the magnetic field inside.

<Hint>

The surface current is $\vec K = \sigma\,\vec v$ with $\sigma = Q/4\pi R^2$ and $\vec v = \omega R\sin\theta\,\hat\phi$. Its dipole moment is $\frac12\int\vec r \times \vec K\,da$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Try a pure dipole field outside and a uniform field inside. The normal component of $\vec B$ must be the same on both sides of the shell.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p3-m" label="$m =$" expr="Q*w*R^2/3"
  vars={{ Q: [0.5, 2], w: [0.5, 2], R: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ Q: "Q", w: "\\omega", R: "R" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Q*w*R^2/5", hint: "That is the moment of a uniformly charged solid ball. On a shell all the charge sits at the radius R, so it contributes more." },
    { expr: "Q*w*R^2/2", hint: "That would be the moment if all the charge went round the equator. On the sphere much of it is nearer the axis, at R sin θ." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p3-bin" label="$B_{\text{in}} =$" expr="mu0*Q*w/(6*pi*R)"
  vars={{ mu0: [0.5, 2], Q: [0.5, 2], w: [0.5, 2], R: [0.5, 2] }}
  names={{ mu0: "\\mu_0", Q: "Q", w: "\\omega", R: "R" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "mu0*Q*w/(4*pi*R)", hint: "That is the field at the centre of a ring of radius R carrying all the charge round the equator, with the current Qω/2π. The shell's charge is spread towards the poles." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A spinning shell is a set of current loops whose field outside is exactly that of a dipole and inside is uniform. The two are tied together by the boundary conditions at the shell.

<Step title="The dipole moment">

With $\vec K = \sigma\omega R\sin\theta\,\hat\phi$ and $\vec r \times \hat\phi = -R\,\hat\theta$ on the shell, the $z$ component of $\vec r \times \vec K$ is $\sigma\omega R^2\sin^2\theta$. So

<Formula label="The moment of the spinning shell" tex="m = \frac12\,\sigma\omega R^4\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega = \frac12\,\sigma\omega R^4\cdot\frac{8\pi}{3} = \frac{Q\,\omega R^2}{3}" cite="reading2" at="p. 4 (boxed), m = ½ ∫ r × J d³x, applied by us to the shell" />

along $\hat z$.

</Step>

<Step title="The magnetic field">

Outside, the field is that of the dipole,

<Formula label="The field outside the shell" tex="\vec B_{\text{out}} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec m}{r^3} = \frac{\mu_0 m}{4\pi r^3}\left(2\cos\theta\,\hat r + \sin\theta\,\hat\theta\right)" cite="reading2" at="p. 7 (boxed; the spherical components are ours)" />

and inside it is uniform, $\vec B_{\text{in}} = B_{\text{in}}\,\hat z$. The radial component must be continuous at $r = R$: $\mu_0 m\cos\theta/(2\pi R^3) = B_{\text{in}}\cos\theta$, so

</Step>

</Solution>

Both are linear and lossless, with permittivities $\varepsilon_1, \varepsilon_2$, permeabilities $\mu_1, \mu_2$ and wave speeds $v_1 = 1/\sqrt{\mu_1\varepsilon_1}$, $v_2 = 1/\sqrt{\mu_2\varepsilon_2}$. Lecture Notes 14 gives the two boundary conditions for the amplitudes (slide 18):

<Formula label="The boundary conditions for the amplitudes" tex="\tilde E_{0,I} + \tilde E_{0,R} = \tilde E_{0,T}, \qquad \frac{1}{\mu_1}\left(\frac{1}{v_1}\,\tilde E_{0,I} - \frac{1}{v_1}\,\tilde E_{0,R}\right) = \frac{1}{\mu_2}\,\frac{1}{v_2}\,\tilde E_{0,T}" cite="reading1" at="slide 18, eqs. (34) and (35)" />

Write $\beta = \mu_1 v_1/\mu_2 v_2$, as the [lesson on these notes](/courses/8-07/electromagnetic-plane-waves-reflection) does. The intensity of a wave of amplitude $E_0$ in a material is $\frac12\varepsilon v E_0^2$, from (slide 14)

<Formula label="Energy density and Poynting vector in matter" tex="u = \frac12\left[\varepsilon|\vec E|^2 + \frac{1}{\mu}|\vec B|^2\right], \qquad \vec S = \frac{1}{\mu}\,\vec E \times \vec B = \frac{uc}{n}\,\hat z" cite="reading1" at="slide 14, eq. (28)" />

with $c/n = v$. The lesson works out the fractions of the power for $\mu_1 = \mu_2$ only; here $\mu$ may differ.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Find the reflection coefficient $R$, the reflected intensity divided by the incident one, in terms of $\beta$.

<Hint>

Solve the two conditions for $\tilde E_{0,R}/\tilde E_{0,I}$. Both waves are in material 1, so the factor $\frac12\varepsilon_1 v_1$ cancels in the ratio of intensities.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-r" label="$R =$" expr="((1-b)/(1+b))^2"
  vars={{ b: [1.5, 4] }}
  names={{ b: "\\beta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(1-b)/(1+b)", hint: "That is the ratio of the amplitudes. The intensity goes as the square of the amplitude." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Two linear equations give the two unknown amplitudes. The reflected and incident waves share one material, so their intensities compare as the squares of their amplitudes.

<Step title="The amplitudes">

The second condition reads $\tilde E_{0,I} - \tilde E_{0,R} = \beta\,\tilde E_{0,T}$. Adding the first gives $2\tilde E_{0,I} = (1 + \beta)\tilde E_{0,T}$, and then

<Formula label="The amplitudes for general mu" tex="\tilde E_{0,T} = \frac{2}{1 + \beta}\,\tilde E_{0,I}, \qquad \tilde E_{0,R} = \frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}\,\tilde E_{0,I}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The reflection coefficient">

<Formula label="The reflection coefficient" tex="R = \frac{\frac12\varepsilon_1 v_1\left|\tilde E_{0,R}\right|^2}{\frac12\varepsilon_1 v_1\left|\tilde E_{0,I}\right|^2} = \left(\frac{1 - \beta}{1 + \beta}\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\mu_1 = \mu_2$, $\beta = v_1/v_2 = n_2/n_1$ and $R = \left(\frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2}\right)^2$, the lesson's result. For $\beta = 1$ nothing is reflected.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={6}>

Find the transmission coefficient $T$, the transmitted intensity divided by the incident one, in terms of $\beta$. Show that $R + T = 1$.

<Hint>

The transmitted wave is in material 2: its intensity has the factor $\frac12\varepsilon_2 v_2$. Since $\varepsilon v = 1/\mu v$ in each material, $\varepsilon_2 v_2/\varepsilon_1 v_1$ can be written with $\beta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-t" label="$T =$" expr="4*b/(1+b)^2"
  vars={{ b: [1.5, 4] }}
  names={{ b: "\\beta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(2/(1+b))^2", hint: "That is the ratio of the squared amplitudes. The intensity in material 2 carries ε₂v₂ instead of ε₁v₁, and the ratio of the two is β." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The transmitted intensity has the other material's $\varepsilon v$ in front, and that factor is exactly what makes the energy add up.

<Step title="The factor">

From $v^2 = 1/\mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon v = 1/\mu v$, so $\varepsilon_2 v_2/\varepsilon_1 v_1 = \mu_1 v_1/\mu_2 v_2 = \beta$.

</Step>

<Step title="The transmission coefficient">

<Formula label="The transmission coefficient" tex="T = \frac{\varepsilon_2 v_2}{\varepsilon_1 v_1}\left|\frac{\tilde E_{0,T}}{\tilde E_{0,I}}\right|^2 = \beta\left(\frac{2}{1 + \beta}\right)^2 = \frac{4\beta}{(1 + \beta)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Energy conservation">

<Formula label="Reflected plus transmitted" tex="R + T = \frac{(1 - \beta)^2 + 4\beta}{(1 + \beta)^2} = \frac{(1 + \beta)^2}{(1 + \beta)^2} = 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $\mu_1 = \mu_2$ this is the lesson's $T = 4n_1n_2/(n_1 + n_2)^2$. And $T$ is unchanged by $\beta \to 1/\beta$: light passing the interface the other way is transmitted just as well.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

A wave in air ($\varepsilon_1 = \varepsilon_0$, $\mu_1 = \mu_0$) meets an idealized lossless material with $\varepsilon_2 = 9.00\,\varepsilon_0$ and $\mu_2 = 4.00\,\mu_0$. What fraction of the power is reflected? Give it as a decimal.

<Hint>

First $v_2 = 1/\sqrt{\mu_2\varepsilon_2}$ as a fraction of $c$, then $\beta$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6-num" label="$R =$" value="0.0400" tolerance="1%" calc="p6-num" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** What counts is $\beta$, not the index of refraction alone.

<Step title="The numbers">

$v_2 = c/\sqrt{4.00 \times 9.00} = c/6.00$, so the index is $n_2 = 6.00$. Then $\beta = \mu_0 c/(4.00\,\mu_0 \times c/6.00) = 1.50$ and

<Formula label="The reflected fraction for the magnetic material" tex="R = \left(\frac{1 - 1.50}{1 + 1.50}\right)^2 = 0.0400, \qquad T = \frac{4 \times 1.50}{2.50^2} = 0.960" cite="mit" at="Problem 6, p. 2 (our own numbers)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$R + T = 1$. Glass with $n = 1.50$ and $\mu = \mu_0$ reflects the same 4%, although this material has four times its index. For $\mu_2 = \mu_0$ and $\varepsilon_2 = 9.00\,\varepsilon_0$, with index 3.00, $\beta$ would be 3.00 and $R = 0.25$: the magnetic response lowers the reflection. With $\varepsilon_2/\varepsilon_0 = \mu_2/\mu_0$, $\beta = 1$ and nothing would be reflected at all.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 7

<Exercise id="p7" title="Energy conservation in the presence of magnetic monopoles" points={15} mit="Problem 7" optional>

This is MIT's extra-credit problem. If magnetic charges existed, with density $\rho_m$ and current density $\vec J_m$, Maxwell's equations would take the more symmetric form that MIT's sheet quotes (the electric charge and current densities are now written $\rho_e$ and $\vec J_e$; in these units the magnetic charge $q_m$ is measured in ampere-metres):

<Formula label="Maxwell's equations with magnetic monopoles" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec E = \frac{\rho_e}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \nabla \cdot \vec B = \mu_0\rho_m, \qquad \nabla \times \vec E = -\mu_0\vec J_m - \frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}, \qquad \nabla \times \vec B = \mu_0\vec J_e + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2" />

and a monopole of magnetic charge $q_m$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ would feel the force (also on the sheet)

<Formula label="The force on a magnetic monopole" tex="\vec F = q_m\left(\vec B - \frac{1}{c^2}\,\vec v \times \vec E\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2" />

MIT notes that the factor $1/c^2$, or something with the same units, is needed for the second term to have the same units as the first. The task is to generalize Poynting's theorem to this case. The energy density and the Poynting vector need no change (slide 12 of Lecture Notes 14):

<Formula label="Energy density and Poynting vector" tex="u = \frac12\left[\varepsilon_0|\vec E|^2 + \frac{1}{\mu_0}|\vec B|^2\right], \qquad \vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\vec E \times \vec B" cite="reading1" at="slide 12, eqs. (24) and (25)" />

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Use the curl equations above to work out $\partial u/\partial t + \nabla \cdot \vec S$. Without monopoles it is $-\vec J_e \cdot \vec E$, minus the work done per unit volume and time on the electric charges. Which term do the monopoles add? (The box is our addition.)

<Hint>

Use $\nabla \cdot (\vec E \times \vec B) = \vec B \cdot (\nabla \times \vec E) - \vec E \cdot (\nabla \times \vec B)$, and put in the two curl equations.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-term" label="With monopoles, $\partial u/\partial t + \nabla \cdot \vec S = -\vec J_e \cdot \vec E\ +$" choices={["+Jₘ · B: the monopole currents feed energy into the field", "−Jₘ · E, the same form as for electric charges", "−Jₘ · B, the rate at which the field does work on the monopoles", "nothing: the two monopole terms of Maxwell's equations cancel each other"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Repeat the derivation of Poynting's theorem. The new term in Faraday's law brings a new term into the balance.

<Step title="The divergence of the Poynting vector">

<Formula label="The divergence of S" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\left[\vec B \cdot \left(-\mu_0\vec J_m - \frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}\right) - \vec E \cdot \left(\mu_0\vec J_e + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\frac{\partial\vec E}{\partial t}\right)\right] = -\vec J_m \cdot \vec B - \vec J_e \cdot \vec E - \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

since $\partial u/\partial t = \varepsilon_0\vec E \cdot \partial\vec E/\partial t + \vec B \cdot \partial\vec B/\partial t/\mu_0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Poynting's theorem with monopoles">

<Formula label="Poynting's theorem with magnetic monopoles" tex="\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \vec S = -\vec J_e \cdot \vec E - \vec J_m \cdot \vec B" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

With $\rho_m = 0$ and $\vec J_m = 0$ the ordinary theorem comes back. The units agree: $\vec J_m = \rho_m\vec v$ is in A m⁻¹ s⁻¹, and times tesla, N/(A m), that is N/(m² s) = W/m³, the units of $\vec J_e \cdot \vec E$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={5}>

Find the power that the force above delivers to one monopole, and from it the work done per unit volume and time on a distribution of monopoles with current density $\vec J_m = \rho_m\vec v$. Show that energy is conserved: the energy of fields and particles together changes only by what the Poynting vector carries through the surface. (The box is our addition.)

<Hint>

The power is $\vec F \cdot \vec v$. What is $\vec v \cdot (\vec v \times \vec E)$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p7-power" label="The power delivered to one monopole is" choices={["qₘ v · B, since the v × E term is perpendicular to v", "qₘ (v · B − v · (v × E)/c²), with both terms doing work", "qₘ v · E/c²", "zero, as for an electric charge in a magnetic field"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A monopole is pushed along $\vec B$, just as an electric charge is pushed along $\vec E$, and that push does the work. The velocity-dependent part is sideways and does none.

<Step title="The power">

$\vec v \cdot (\vec v \times \vec E) = 0$, so $\vec F \cdot \vec v = q_m\,\vec v \cdot \vec B$. Summed over the monopoles in a unit volume, the work per unit time is $\rho_m\vec v \cdot \vec B = \vec J_m \cdot \vec B$; for the electric charges it is $\vec J_e \cdot \vec E$ as before.

</Step>

<Step title="The balance">

With $u_{\text{mech}}$ the energy density of the particles, $\partial u_{\text{mech}}/\partial t = \vec J_e \cdot \vec E + \vec J_m \cdot \vec B$. Adding part (a):

<Formula label="Energy conservation with monopoles" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(u + u_{\text{mech}}\right) + \nabla \cdot \vec S = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 7, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

Integrated over a volume, the total energy inside changes only by the flux of $\vec S$ through its surface.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The work done on the monopoles is exactly the term that part (a) found missing from the field energy: what the field loses, the monopoles gain.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Would energy still be conserved if the force on a monopole were $q_m(\vec B + \vec v \times \vec E/c^2)$, with a plus sign (MIT marks this form as wrong)? (The box is our addition.)

<Answer id="p7-wrong" label="With the plus sign, energy conservation" choices={["fails: with the plus sign the power qₘ v · B would pick up an extra term that the field equations cannot balance", "still works: the v × E term does no work whatever its sign, so the energy balance is unchanged", "still works, but only for monopoles at rest", "fails, unless c² were negative"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Energy conservation tests only the work, and the $\vec v \times \vec E$ term does none.

<Step title="The answer">

With either sign, $\vec v \cdot (\vec v \times \vec E) = 0$, the power is $q_m\vec v \cdot \vec B$, and the proof of part (b) goes through unchanged. So energy conservation cannot decide the sign.

</Step>

<Step title="What does fix the sign">

A remark of ours. For a monopole moving slowly through a field, the magnetic field in its own rest frame is, to first order in $v/c$, $\vec B - \vec v \times \vec E/c^2$, and in that frame the force is $q_m$ times the field there. That gives the minus sign. Momentum conservation, which involves the full force and not just its work, also requires it.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The same is true of electric charges: the magnetic part $q\,\vec v \times \vec B$ of the Lorentz force does no work, and Poynting's theorem would hold whatever its sign.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
