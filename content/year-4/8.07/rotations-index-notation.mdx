How do you describe the rotation of a vector in index notation? Guth answered this quickly at the end of a lecture, and his Lecture Notes 2 work it out properly (notes p. 1). The answer is one short formula, $A'_i = R_{ij}A_j$, but getting there carefully shows how basis vectors, components and matrices fit together. (MIT's material for this lecture is Guth's three typed pages of notes; this page follows them in its own words, with its own worked examples.)

## Index notation in brief

This section is this page's addition: the notes take the notation as known (the Kronecker delta and the Levi-Civita symbol appear only in the aside at the end), so here are the three pieces it needs. The components of a vector are labelled by an index $i$, $j$ or $k$ that runs over 1, 2, 3, standing for $x$, $y$, $z$. So the basis vector $\hat e_i$ is one of the unit vectors $\hat e_x$, $\hat e_y$, $\hat e_z$ of the last lecture, and $A_i$ is one of the components $A_x$, $A_y$, $A_z$.

- **Summation convention.** An index that appears twice in one term is summed from 1 to 3, with no summation sign written. So $A_j\hat e_j$ means $A_x\hat e_x + A_y\hat e_y + A_z\hat e_z$. A repeated index is a dummy: renaming it changes nothing. An index that appears once is free, and an equation with a free index $i$ holds for each value of $i$.
- **Kronecker delta.** $\delta_{ij}$ is 1 when $i = j$ and 0 otherwise, the entries of the identity matrix. In this notation the dot products of the basis vectors are $\hat e_i\cdot\hat e_j = \delta_{ij}$.
- **Levi-Civita symbol.** $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is $+1$ when $(i, j, k)$ is $(1, 2, 3)$ or a cyclic shift of it, $(2, 3, 1)$ or $(3, 1, 2)$; it is $-1$ for the other orders, $(2, 1, 3)$, $(1, 3, 2)$ and $(3, 2, 1)$; and it is 0 when two indices are equal. It is the building block of the cross product in index notation.

## Active and passive rotations

A rotation can be described in two ways (notes p. 1). In an **active** rotation the object turns and the coordinate axes stay where they are. In a **passive** rotation the coordinate system turns and the object stays. Guth finds the active picture easier to visualise (picturing a turning object comes more naturally than picturing your own head going round), and the advantage grows when one rotation about one axis is followed by another about a different axis. So the notes, and this page, describe rotations actively.

He admits that the passive picture is easier to define rigorously (notes p. 1). Rotating the axes says nothing about the object. Rotating the object assumes that it can be turned without being changed in any other way, which is fine for something rigid but much less clear for something that flows, such as running water. So if someone asks what is really meant, the safe answer is a rotation of the coordinate system; the active language is a convenient way to think about it. The two are related simply: turning the object counterclockwise by an angle $\phi$ about the $z$ axis changes its components exactly as turning the coordinate system clockwise by the same $\phi$ would, since only the position of the object relative to the axes matters.

## Rotating a vector by rotating its basis

Take the example of a counterclockwise rotation by $\phi$ about the $z$ axis (notes p. 1). Any vector can be expanded in the basis vectors:

<Formula label="A vector in components" tex="\vec A = A_j\,\hat e_j" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.1), p. 1" />

Call the rotated vector $\vec A'$. The trick is to notice that rotating $\vec A$ is the same as keeping its expansion coefficients $A_j$ and rotating the basis vectors under them (notes p. 1). If $\hat e'_j$ is the basis vector $\hat e_j$ turned counterclockwise by $\phi$ about $z$, then

<Formula label="The rotated vector, same coefficients on rotated basis vectors" tex="\vec A' = A_j\,\hat e'_j" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.2), p. 1" />

This works because a rotation is linear: rotating a sum of arrows gives the sum of the rotated arrows, and rotating an arrow three times as long gives an arrow three times as long.

## The rotated basis vectors

Seen from above, with the $z$ axis out of the page, $\hat e_x$ turns by $\phi$ towards $\hat e_y$, and $\hat e_y$ turns by $\phi$ towards $-\hat e_x$; $\hat e_z$ lies on the axis and does not move.

Reading off the components of the rotated vectors (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="The rotated basis vectors" tex="\hat e'_x = \hat e_x\cos\phi + \hat e_y\sin\phi, \qquad \hat e'_y = -\hat e_x\sin\phi + \hat e_y\cos\phi, \qquad \hat e'_z = \hat e_z" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.3), p. 2" />

All three equations have the same form: each rotated basis vector is a combination of the old ones. Collecting the coefficients in a matrix $R_{ij}$ (notes p. 2):

<Formula label="Rotated basis vectors through the rotation matrix" tex="\hat e'_j = \hat e_i\,R_{ij}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.4), p. 2" />

with $i$ summed. For this rotation the matrix is called $R_z(\phi)$:

<Formula label="Rotation by φ about the z axis" tex="R_z(\phi)_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi & -\sin\phi & 0 \\ \sin\phi & \cos\phi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.5), p. 2" />

As usual for a matrix, the first index ($i$) labels the rows and the second ($j$) the columns (notes p. 2). Comparing with Eq. (2.3), the column $j$ of $R$ holds the components of the rotated basis vector $\hat e'_j$: the first column, $(\cos\phi, \sin\phi, 0)$, is $\hat e'_x$.

## How the components change

Now put Eq. (2.4) into Eq. (2.2) (notes p. 2). This gives $\vec A' = A_j\,\hat e_i\,R_{ij}$, and since the factors are just numbers and vectors multiplied together, they can be reordered:

<Formula label="The rotated vector in the old basis" tex="\vec A' = \left(R_{ij}\,A_j\right)\hat e_i" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.6), p. 2" />

The components $A'_i$ of the rotated vector are, by definition, its coefficients in the original basis:

<Formula label="Components of the rotated vector" tex="\vec A' = A'_i\,\hat e_i" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.7), p. 2" />

Comparing the two expressions term by term gives the result the notes box (notes pp. 2–3):

<Formula label="How a rotation changes the components" tex="A'_i = R_{ij}\,A_j" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.8), p. 2" />

Here $j$ is summed from 1 to 3, since it is repeated, and the equation holds for each value of the free index $i$.

## Index notation and matrix multiplication

When the repeated indices sit next to each other, as the two $j$'s do in Eq. (2.8), and no factor carries more than two indices, index notation is simply another way to write matrix multiplication (notes p. 3). Eq. (2.8) is the same statement as

<Formula label="The same rule as a matrix equation" tex="\begin{pmatrix} A'_1 \\ A'_2 \\ A'_3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} R_{11} & R_{12} & R_{13} \\ R_{21} & R_{22} & R_{23} \\ R_{31} & R_{32} & R_{33} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} A_1 \\ A_2 \\ A_3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.9), p. 3" />

where the row-times-column rule of matrix multiplication is exactly the sum over $j$. Index notation goes further than matrices, though: it handles objects with three or more indices, such as $\epsilon_{ijk}$, and sums over indices that are not adjacent, where no matrix picture exists.

<WorkedExample title="Turning a vector by 30° about the z axis">
<Problem>
The vector $\vec A$ has components $(3, 1, 2)$. Rotate it counterclockwise by $\phi = 30^\circ$ about the $z$ axis. Find the components of $\vec A'$, and check that its length has not changed.
</Problem>
<Step title="Write down the matrix">
With $\cos 30^\circ \approx 0.8660$ and $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$, Eq. (2.5) gives the rows $(0.8660, -0.5, 0)$, $(0.5, 0.8660, 0)$ and $(0, 0, 1)$.
</Step>
<Step title="Apply A′ᵢ = Rᵢⱼ Aⱼ">
Each component is a row of $R$ times the column $(3, 1, 2)$:
$A'_1 = 0.8660 \times 3 - 0.5 \times 1 \approx 2.098$,
$A'_2 = 0.5 \times 3 + 0.8660 \times 1 \approx 2.366$,
$A'_3 = 2$.
So $\vec A'$ is about $(2.098, 2.366, 2)$: the part in the $xy$-plane has swung towards the $y$ axis, and the $z$ component is untouched.
</Step>
<Step title="Check the length">
Before: $3^2 + 1^2 + 2^2 = 14$. After: $2.098^2 + 2.366^2 + 2^2 \approx 4.402 + 5.598 + 4 = 14.000$. A rotation keeps lengths, as it must; in matrix language, the columns of $R$ are perpendicular unit vectors, so $R$ is an orthogonal matrix.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Rotation about any axis (an aside)

Guth adds, as an aside that will not be needed later in the course and will not be on exams, that Eq. (2.5) is easy to generalise to a rotation by $\phi$ about the direction of any unit vector $\hat n$ (notes p. 3). Eq. (2.5) is the case $\hat n = \hat e_z$, the $z$ axis, so the idea is to rewrite it using only $\hat n$, $\delta_{ij}$ and $\epsilon_{ijk}$, building blocks that do not single out any axis.

The diagonal elements come first. On the diagonal, $R_z(\phi)$ has $\cos\phi$, $\cos\phi$ and 1, and the last of these equals $\cos\phi + (1 - \cos\phi)$. The extra $(1 - \cos\phi)$ appears only in row 3, column 3, which for $\hat n = \hat e_z$ is the one place where $\hat n_i\hat n_j$ is not zero (notes p. 3):

<Formula label="The diagonal part, built from the axis" tex="R_z(\phi)_{ij} = \delta_{ij}\cos\phi + \hat n_i\,\hat n_j\,(1 - \cos\phi) + \text{off-diagonal terms}" cite="reading1" at="p. 3" />

The off-diagonal elements are less obvious. They must give $R_{21} = -R_{12} = \sin\phi$ and nothing anywhere else. An antisymmetric pair like this is what the Levi-Civita symbol provides, and with a little thought the whole rotation can be written as (notes p. 3)

<Formula label="Rotation by φ about the unit vector n̂" tex="R(\hat n, \phi)_{ij} = \delta_{ij}\cos\phi + \hat n_i\,\hat n_j\,(1 - \cos\phi) - \epsilon_{ijk}\,\hat n_k\sin\phi" cite="reading1" at="eq. (2.10), p. 3" />

For $\hat n = \hat e_z$ the last term is $-\epsilon_{ij3}\sin\phi$, which gives $R_{12} = -\epsilon_{123}\sin\phi = -\sin\phi$ and $R_{21} = -\epsilon_{213}\sin\phi = \sin\phi$, and nothing else, since $\epsilon_{ij3}$ vanishes when $i$ or $j$ is 3 or when $i = j$. So Eq. (2.10) reproduces Eq. (2.5) for rotations about $z$ (notes p. 3).

Why should it then hold for any axis? Any other rotation is just this one seen in a rotated coordinate system. Guth's argument needs a little more about rotational invariance than the course covers (notes p. 3): both sides of Eq. (2.10) change in the same way under rotations, that is, they behave as **tensors**. An equation between tensors that is true in one coordinate system, here the one whose $z$ axis is the rotation axis, is then true in every rotated system, where $\hat n$ points in an arbitrary direction.

<WorkedExample title="A third of a turn about the diagonal">
<Problem>
Use Eq. (2.10) to find the matrix of a rotation by $\phi = 120^\circ$ about the diagonal axis $\hat n = (1, 1, 1)/\sqrt{3}$, and apply it to $\vec A = (3, 1, 2)$.
</Problem>
<Step title="The three ingredients">
$\cos 120^\circ = -1/2$ and $\sin 120^\circ = \sqrt 3/2$. Every $\hat n_i\hat n_j$ is $1/3$, so the middle term is $\tfrac13 \times \tfrac32 = \tfrac12$ for every $i$ and $j$. In the last term, $\hat n_k\sin\phi = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 3}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt 3}{2} = \tfrac12$ for each $k$, so it is $-\tfrac12\epsilon_{ijk}$ summed over $k$.
</Step>
<Step title="Assemble the matrix">
Diagonal: $-\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 0$. Off the diagonal only one $k$ contributes: $R_{12} = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\epsilon_{123} = 0$ and $R_{21} = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\epsilon_{213} = 1$; in the same way $R_{23} = R_{31} = 0$ and $R_{32} = R_{13} = 1$. So $R$ has rows $(0, 0, 1)$, $(1, 0, 0)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$.
</Step>
<Step title="What it does">
By Eq. (2.4) the columns are the rotated basis vectors: $\hat e'_x = \hat e_y$, $\hat e'_y = \hat e_z$, $\hat e'_z = \hat e_x$. The rotation carries the $x$ axis to $y$, $y$ to $z$ and $z$ to $x$, as a third of a turn about the diagonal should. Each row sums to 1, so $R\hat n = \hat n$: the axis stays put. For $\vec A = (3, 1, 2)$, $A'_i = R_{ij}A_j$ gives $(A_3, A_1, A_2) = (2, 3, 1)$, again of length $\sqrt{14}$.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A rotation can be **active** (the object turns) or **passive** (the axes turn). Guth pictures it actively; the passive version is easier to define. A counterclockwise active rotation by $\phi$ matches a clockwise rotation of the axes by $\phi$.
- Rotating $\vec A = A_j\hat e_j$ keeps the coefficients and rotates the basis: $\vec A' = A_j\hat e'_j$ with $\hat e'_j = \hat e_i R_{ij}$, so the new components are $A'_i = R_{ij}A_j$.
- For a counterclockwise rotation about $z$, $R_z(\phi)$ has rows $(\cos\phi, -\sin\phi, 0)$, $(\sin\phi, \cos\phi, 0)$, $(0, 0, 1)$; column $j$ holds the components of $\hat e'_j$.
- With adjacent repeated indices and at most two indices per factor, index notation is matrix multiplication.
- As an aside, the rotation by $\phi$ about any unit vector $\hat n$ is $R_{ij} = \delta_{ij}\cos\phi + \hat n_i\hat n_j(1 - \cos\phi) - \epsilon_{ijk}\hat n_k\sin\phi$, true for every axis by an argument the notes only sketch: both sides behave as tensors.
