The [first page](/courses/8-07/ideal-magnetic-dipole-current) on these notes found the current density of an ideal magnetic dipole at the origin, $\vec J = -\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3(\vec r)$. The second half of Guth's slides (slides 15–23) puts it to work. First, it gives the force, energy and torque on a dipole in an external field, which the [last notes](/courses/8-07/magnetic-multipole-expansion-dipoles) only stated. Then Guth checks, "more fun with delta functions" (slide 19), that the dipole's field, delta function included, satisfies Maxwell's equations with exactly this current. (Our own words and worked example.)

## The force on a current

For an electric dipole the results are familiar (slide 15): the force is $(\vec p \cdot \nabla)\vec E$, or $\nabla(\vec p \cdot \vec E)$, the torque is $\vec p \times \vec E + \vec r \times \vec F$, and the potential energy is $U = -\vec p \cdot \vec E$. For the magnetic case Guth starts from the force on a moving charge and on a piece of current (slide 15):

<Formula label="The magnetic force" tex="\vec F = q\,\vec v \times \vec B, \qquad d\vec F = I\,d\vec\ell \times \vec B = \vec J \times \vec B\,d^3x" cite="reading1" at="slide 15 (boxed)" />

using $I\,d\vec\ell = \vec J\,d^3x$ for a wire. The total force on a current distribution in an external field $\vec B$, and for the ideal dipole at the origin (slide 15):

<Formula label="The force on a dipole as an integral" tex="\vec F = \int\vec J \times \vec B\,d^3x = -\int\vec B \times \vec J\,d^3x = \int\vec B \times \left(\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3(\vec r)\right)d^3x" cite="reading1" at="slide 15" />

## The force on a magnetic dipole

Expand the triple product, $\vec B \times (\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3) = \vec m\,(\vec B \cdot \nabla\delta^3) - \nabla\delta^3\,(\vec m \cdot \vec B)$. In components (slide 16):

<Formula label="The force in components" tex="F_i = \int d^3x\left[m_i\,B_j\,\frac{\partial\delta^3(\vec r)}{\partial x_j} - \frac{\partial\delta^3(\vec r)}{\partial x_i}\,m_j B_j\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 16" />

A derivative of a delta function is evaluated by integrating by parts (slide 16): the derivative moves onto the other factor with a minus sign, and there is no surface term since $\delta^3$ vanishes away from the origin.

<Formula label="After integrating by parts" tex="F_i = -\int d^3x\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\left(m_i B_j\right)\delta^3(\vec r) - \delta^3(\vec r)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\left(m_j B_j\right)\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 16" />

The first term is $m_i\,\partial B_j/\partial x_j = m_i\,\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0$ (slide 16). The second, integrated against the delta function, is the derivative of $\vec m \cdot \vec B$ at the dipole:

<Formula label="The force on a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec F = \nabla\left(\vec m \cdot \vec B\right)" cite="reading1" at="slide 16 (boxed)" />

with $\vec m$ held constant and the gradient taken at the dipole's position. Where no current flows at the dipole, $\nabla \times \vec B = 0$ there, and this equals $(\vec m \cdot \nabla)\vec B$, the form that parallels the electric case.

## The energy

Because the force is a gradient, it has a potential energy. Keeping $\vec m$ fixed and taking $U = 0$ at infinity, where the external field vanishes (slide 17):

<Formula label="The potential energy of a magnetic dipole" tex="U(\vec r) = -\int_{\infty}^{\vec r}\vec F \cdot d\vec\ell = -\int_{\infty}^{\vec r}\nabla\left(\vec m \cdot \vec B\right)\cdot d\vec\ell = -\vec m \cdot \vec B(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="slide 17 (boxed)" />

The energy is lowest when the dipole lines up with the field. A dipole with $\vec m \cdot \vec B > 0$ is pulled toward stronger field; one pointing against the field is pushed toward weaker field. (This is the energy of moving a dipole of fixed moment, as for a permanent magnet. The energy needed to keep the current of a loop constant while it moves is a separate matter, which the notes do not take up.)

## The torque

The torque about the dipole's position, the origin, adds up $\vec r \times d\vec F$ (slide 17):

<Formula label="The torque as an integral" tex="\vec\tau = \int\vec r \times d\vec F = -\int\vec r \times \left(\vec B \times \vec J\right)d^3x = \int\vec r \times \left[\vec B \times \left(\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3(\vec r)\right)\right]d^3x" cite="reading1" at="slide 17" />

Three cross products make three [Levi-Civita symbols](/courses/8-07/rotations-index-notation). Integrate by parts once more (slide 18):

<Formula label="The torque in components" tex="\tau_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{klm}\,\epsilon_{mnp}\int d^3x\;x_j\,B_l\,m_n\,\frac{\partial\delta^3(\vec r)}{\partial x_p} = -\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{klm}\,\epsilon_{mnp}\int d^3x\;\frac{\partial}{\partial x_p}\left(x_j\,B_l\,m_n\right)\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="slide 18" />

The derivative gives two terms, $\delta_{pj}\,B_l\,m_n + x_j\,(\partial B_l/\partial x_p)\,m_n$; the second contains $x_j$, which is zero where the delta function sits (slide 18). Setting $p = j$ and using $\epsilon_{mkl}\,\epsilon_{mnj} = \delta_{kn}\,\delta_{lj} - \delta_{kj}\,\delta_{ln}$ (slide 18):

<Formula label="Reducing the Levi-Civita symbols" tex="\tau_i = -\epsilon_{ijk}\,\epsilon_{mkl}\,\epsilon_{mnj}\,B_l(\vec 0)\,m_n = -\epsilon_{ijk}\left(\delta_{kn}\,\delta_{lj} - \delta_{kj}\,\delta_{ln}\right)B_l(\vec 0)\,m_n = -\epsilon_{ijk}\,B_j(\vec 0)\,m_k = \left(\vec m \times \vec B\right)_i" cite="reading1" at="slide 18" />

The second product of deltas drops out because $\epsilon_{ijk}\,\delta_{kj}$, summed over $j$ and $k$, is zero: $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is antisymmetric in $j$ and $k$ (the slide simply crosses it out). The result (slide 19):

<Formula label="The torque on a magnetic dipole" tex="\vec\tau = \vec m \times \vec B" cite="reading1" at="slide 19 (boxed)" />

About any other point, add $\vec r \times \vec F$ for the force applied at the dipole, as in the electric formula. All three results are those of the [8.02 page on magnetic dipoles](/courses/8-02/magnetic-dipoles), found there for a rectangular loop; here they hold for any ideal dipole, and the delta functions did the work.

<WorkedExample title="A small magnet next to a wire">
<Problem>
A long straight wire along the $z$ axis carries $I = 10$ A in the $+z$ direction. A small magnet with $m = 0.2$ A m², treated as an ideal dipole, sits at $(s, 0, 0)$ with $s = 5$ cm. Find its energy, the force and the torque on it (a) when $\vec m$ points along $+\hat y$, and (b) when it points along $+\hat x$, away from the wire.
</Problem>
<Step title="The field">
The wire's field circles it: $\vec B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi}\,\frac{(-y, x, 0)}{x^2 + y^2}$. At the magnet, $\vec B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi s}\,\hat y$, with $\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi} = 2 \times 10^{-7} \times 10 = 2 \times 10^{-6}$ T m, so $B = 2 \times 10^{-6}/0.05 = 4.0 \times 10^{-5}$ T.
</Step>
<Step title="(a) Moment along the field">
$U = -mB = -0.2 \times 4.0 \times 10^{-5} = -8.0 \times 10^{-6}$ J and $\vec\tau = 0$. For the force, $\vec m \cdot \vec B = m B_y = \frac{m\mu_0 I}{2\pi}\,\frac{x}{x^2 + y^2}$, whose gradient at $(s, 0, 0)$ is $-\frac{m\mu_0 I}{2\pi s^2}\,\hat x$. So $F = 0.2 \times 2 \times 10^{-6}/0.0025 = 1.6 \times 10^{-4}$ N toward the wire, the direction in which $U = -m\mu_0 I/2\pi s$ falls.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) Moment away from the wire">
Now $\vec m \cdot \vec B = 0$ at the magnet, so $U = 0$, and the torque is $\vec m \times \vec B = 0.2 \times 4.0 \times 10^{-5}\,\hat x \times \hat y = 8.0 \times 10^{-6}\,\hat z$ N m, turning $\vec m$ toward the field. The force is not zero: $\vec m \cdot \vec B = m B_x = -\frac{m\mu_0 I}{2\pi}\,\frac{y}{x^2 + y^2}$ changes along $y$, and its gradient at the magnet is $-1.6 \times 10^{-4}\,\hat y$ N, a sideways push of the same size, against the field direction.
</Step>
<Step title="A check">
No current flows at the magnet, so $(\vec m \cdot \nabla)\vec B$ must agree. In (b) it is $m\,\partial\vec B/\partial x$ at $(s, 0, 0)$, where $\partial B_y/\partial x = -\mu_0 I/2\pi s^2$ and $\partial B_x/\partial x = 0$: again $-1.6 \times 10^{-4}\,\hat y$ N.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Maxwell's equations for the dipole field

The field of an ideal dipole, with $2\mu_0/3 = (\mu_0/4\pi)(8\pi/3)$ (slide 19):

<Formula label="The dipole field" tex="\vec B_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\left[\frac{3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec m}{r^3} + \frac{8\pi}{3}\,\vec m\,\delta^3(\vec r)\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 19" />

should satisfy Maxwell's equations of magnetostatics with the dipole's own current (slide 19):

<Formula label="What the field should satisfy" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec B_{\text{dip}} = 0, \qquad \nabla \times \vec B_{\text{dip}} = \mu_0\,\vec J_{\text{dip}} = -\mu_0\,\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="slide 19" />

Can we check? Away from the origin both are easy, and both sides vanish. The test is at the origin, where every term is a delta function or its derivative. Guth recalls an identity for the second derivatives of $1/r$ (slide 20):

<Formula label="Second derivatives of one over r" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\left(\frac{x_j}{r^3}\right) = -\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}\left(\frac1r\right) = \frac{\delta_{ij} - 3\hat r_i\hat r_j}{r^3} + \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\delta_{ij}\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="slide 20" />

(Why $\frac{4\pi}{3}$, a remark of ours: the trace of the left side is $-\nabla^2(1/r) = 4\pi\delta^3$, the first term on the right has zero trace, and by symmetry the delta-function term must be proportional to $\delta_{ij}$, so it is a third of $4\pi$ on each diagonal entry.)

## The divergence of the dipole field

Call the first part of the field $\vec G$. By the identity (slide 20),

<Formula label="The first part of the field" tex="\vec G = \frac{3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec m}{r^3}, \qquad G_i = -\frac{\delta_{ij} - 3\hat r_i\hat r_j}{r^3}\,m_j = m_j\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i\,\partial x_j}\left(\frac1r\right) + \frac{4\pi}{3}\,m_i\,\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="slide 20" />

so $\vec G$ itself hides a delta function. Take the divergence, and use $\nabla^2(1/r) = -4\pi\delta^3(\vec r)$ (slide 20):

<Formula label="The divergence of G" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec G = m_j\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x_j}\nabla^2\!\left(\frac1r\right) + \frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\vec m \cdot \nabla\right)\delta^3(\vec r) = -4\pi\left(\vec m \cdot \nabla\right)\delta^3(\vec r) + \frac{4\pi}{3}\left(\vec m \cdot \nabla\right)\delta^3(\vec r) = -\frac{8\pi}{3}\left(\vec m \cdot \nabla\right)\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="slide 20 (boxed)" />

The other term of the field has divergence $\frac{8\pi}{3}(\vec m \cdot \nabla)\delta^3$, which cancels it exactly (slide 21):

<Formula label="No divergence" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec B_{\text{dip}} = 0" cite="reading1" at="slide 21 (boxed)" />

## The curl of the dipole field

For the curl, the first part of $G_k$ is a derivative of $m_l\,\partial(1/r)/\partial x_l$, so its curl contains $\epsilon_{ijk}$ times two derivatives, $\partial^2/\partial x_j\,\partial x_k$, which is symmetric in $j$ and $k$ and gives zero (slide 21). Only the hidden delta function survives:

<Formula label="The curl of G" tex="\epsilon_{ijk}\,\frac{\partial G_k}{\partial x_j} = \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\epsilon_{ijk}\,\frac{\partial\delta^3(\vec r)}{\partial x_j}\,m_k, \qquad \nabla \times \vec G = -\frac{4\pi}{3}\,\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="slide 21 (boxed)" />

The curl of the explicit delta-function term is (slide 22)

<Formula label="The curl of the delta-function term" tex="\nabla \times \left(\frac{8\pi}{3}\,\vec m\,\delta^3(\vec r)\right) = -\frac{8\pi}{3}\,\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="slide 22" />

and the two add up to $-4\pi\,\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3$. Times $\mu_0/4\pi$ (slide 22):

<Formula label="Ampère's law for the dipole" tex="\nabla \times \vec B_{\text{dip}} = \mu_0\,\vec J_{\text{dip}}, \qquad \vec J_{\text{dip}} = -\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3(\vec r)" cite="reading1" at="slide 22 (boxed)" />

the current density of the first page. The coefficient $\frac{8\pi}{3}$ is the only one that makes both equations work (a remark of ours): any other would leave $\nabla \cdot \vec B \neq 0$ at the origin and give the wrong current in the curl.

## The electric dipole

The same check works for the electric dipole, whose delta-function term has the opposite sign and half the size (slide 23):

<Formula label="The field of an ideal electric dipole" tex="\vec E_{\text{dip}}(\vec r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{3(\vec p \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec p}{r^3} - \frac{4\pi}{3}\,\vec p\,\delta^3(\vec r)\right]" cite="reading1" at="slide 23" />

Now the delta term cancels the curl of the first part instead of its divergence, and the divergences add, $-\frac{8\pi}{3} - \frac{4\pi}{3} = -4\pi$, so (slide 23)

<Formula label="Maxwell's equations for the electric dipole" tex="\nabla \cdot \vec E_{\text{dip}} = \frac{\rho_{\text{dip}}}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \rho_{\text{dip}} = -\vec p \cdot \nabla\delta^3(\vec r), \qquad \nabla \times \vec E_{\text{dip}} = 0" cite="reading1" at="slide 23 (boxed)" />

with the charge density of the shrunken electric dipole from the first page. The two dipoles have the same field away from the origin and different fields at it, because one is made of charges and the other of currents.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Loops of current, not pairs of monopoles">
A remark of ours, not in the notes. If magnetic dipoles were pairs of opposite magnetic charges close together, their field would be built like the electric dipole's, with $-\frac{4\pi}{3}$ instead of $+\frac{8\pi}{3}$, and $\nabla \cdot \vec B$ would not vanish at the dipole. The hyperfine splitting of hydrogen, the 21 cm line, depends on that coefficient, and the measured value fits the current-loop form: the proton's moment behaves as circulating current, not as separated magnetic charge. No magnetic charge has ever been found, yet grand unified theories predict heavy magnetic monopoles, and the early universe should have made many of them. That puzzle, and how inflation solves it, is on the 8.286 page on [the magnetic monopole problem](/courses/8-286/magnetic-monopoles).
</Callout>

## Summary

- **Force:** with $\vec J = -\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3$, $\vec F = \int\vec J \times \vec B\,d^3x$; integrating by parts and using $\nabla \cdot \vec B = 0$ gives $\vec F = \nabla(\vec m \cdot \vec B)$.
- **Energy:** for fixed $\vec m$, $U = -\int_\infty\vec F \cdot d\vec\ell = -\vec m \cdot \vec B$.
- **Torque:** $\vec\tau = \int\vec r \times (\vec J \times \vec B)\,d^3x$ reduces, with three Levi-Civita symbols, to $\vec m \times \vec B$.
- **Example:** a 0.2 A m² magnet 5 cm from a 10 A wire ($B = 40$ μT) has $U = -8.0$ μJ and feels $1.6 \times 10^{-4}$ N toward the wire when aligned; pointing away from the wire, it feels a torque of $8.0 \times 10^{-6}$ N m and a sideways force of $1.6 \times 10^{-4}$ N.
- **Maxwell's equations:** $\vec G = [3(\vec m \cdot \hat r)\hat r - \vec m]/r^3$ has $\nabla \cdot \vec G = -\frac{8\pi}{3}(\vec m \cdot \nabla)\delta^3$ and $\nabla \times \vec G = -\frac{4\pi}{3}\vec m \times \nabla\delta^3$; the term $\frac{8\pi}{3}\vec m\,\delta^3$ makes $\nabla \cdot \vec B_{\text{dip}} = 0$ and $\nabla \times \vec B_{\text{dip}} = \mu_0\vec J_{\text{dip}}$.
- **Electric dipole:** the term $-\frac{4\pi}{3}\vec p\,\delta^3$ gives $\nabla \cdot \vec E_{\text{dip}} = \rho_{\text{dip}}/\varepsilon_0$ with $\rho_{\text{dip}} = -\vec p \cdot \nabla\delta^3$, and $\nabla \times \vec E_{\text{dip}} = 0$.
