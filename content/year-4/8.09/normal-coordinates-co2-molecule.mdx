[Section 3.1](/courses/8-09/small-oscillations-simultaneous-diagonalization) showed that small oscillations about a minimum are governed by two constant symmetric matrices, the kinetic matrix $\hat T$ and the potential matrix $\hat V$, and found $n$ normal modes $\vec a^{(\alpha)}e^{-i\omega_\alpha t}$. Section 3.2 of Stewart's notes (pp. 55–59) asks the natural next question: is there a better choice of generalised coordinates, one in which the modes are simply the coordinates themselves? There is, and the notes then use it on a real molecule, carbon dioxide. (The source is the course's typed lecture notes, without videos; this page follows them in order, in its own words, and draws no figures from them.)

## The mode matrix

Place the normalised mode vectors side by side as the columns of an $n \times n$ matrix, and collect the eigenvalues in a diagonal matrix $\hat\lambda = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$ (p. 56):

<Formula label="Mode matrix" tex="A = \big[\, \vec a^{(1)} \ \ \vec a^{(2)} \ \ \ldots \ \ \vec a^{(n)} \,\big]" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.26), p. 56" />

The modes were allowed to be complex, but $A$ can be chosen real. The notes' footnote gives the reason (p. 56): the equation $\hat V\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)} = \lambda_\alpha\hat T\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)}$ does not fix the overall size of $\vec a^{(\alpha)}$, so one component may be chosen real; the equations then have real coefficients (the eigenvalue is real), and so every ratio $a_j^{(\alpha)}/a_i^{(\alpha)}$ is real. A mode vector can therefore differ from a real one only by an overall phase, and that phase can be moved into the constant $C_\alpha$ of the general solution.

Element $(\beta, \alpha)$ of $A^T\hat TA$ is $\vec a^{(\beta)}\cdot\hat T\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)}$, which the normalisation of Section 3.1 makes $\delta_{\alpha\beta}$. Element $(\beta, \alpha)$ of $A^T\hat VA$ is $\vec a^{(\beta)}\cdot\hat V\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)} = \lambda_\alpha\,\vec a^{(\beta)}\cdot\hat T\cdot\vec a^{(\alpha)} = \lambda_\alpha\delta_{\alpha\beta}$. So the one matrix $A$ makes both matrices diagonal at once (p. 56):

<Formula label="Simultaneous diagonalisation" tex="A^T \hat T A = \hat 1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad A^T \hat V A = \hat\lambda" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.27), p. 56" />

This is the "simultaneous diagonalisation of $\hat T$ and $\hat V$" that gives Section 3.1 its title. It is not the usual diagonalisation $S^{-1}MS$ of [18.06](/courses/18-06/diagonalization-and-powers): $A$ is not orthogonal, and what appears is the transpose $A^T$, not the inverse. That is the right operation for a quadratic form, such as an energy, which changes as $\vec\eta\cdot\hat M\cdot\vec\eta \to \vec\xi\cdot(A^T\hat MA)\cdot\vec\xi$ when $\vec\eta = A\vec\xi$.

## Normal coordinates

Define new coordinates $\vec\xi$, the **normal coordinates**, by (p. 56)

<Formula label="Normal coordinates" tex="\vec\eta = A\,\vec\xi \qquad\text{and}\qquad \dot{\vec\eta} = A\,\dot{\vec\xi}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.28), p. 56" />

$A$ is constant, so the velocities transform the same way. In the new coordinates the Lagrangian of Section 3.1 becomes (p. 56)

<Formula label="Lagrangian in normal coordinates" tex="L = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vec\eta}\cdot\hat T\cdot\dot{\vec\eta} - \tfrac12\,\vec\eta\cdot\hat V\cdot\vec\eta = \tfrac12\,\dot{\vec\xi}\cdot(A^T\hat TA)\cdot\dot{\vec\xi} - \tfrac12\,\vec\xi\cdot(A^T\hat VA)\cdot\vec\xi = \tfrac12\sum_\alpha\big(\dot\xi_\alpha^2 - \omega_\alpha^2\,\xi_\alpha^2\big)" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.29), p. 56" />

There are no cross terms left. The Lagrangian is a sum of $n$ separate harmonic oscillators, each of unit "mass", and the Euler-Lagrange equations are (p. 56)

<Formula label="Equations of motion of the normal coordinates" tex="\ddot\xi_\alpha + \omega_\alpha^2\, \xi_\alpha = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.30), p. 56" />

Each normal coordinate oscillates on its own, at its own normal mode frequency. Exciting only $\xi_\alpha$ makes $\vec\eta = \vec a^{(\alpha)}\xi_\alpha$: the system moves in the pure mode $\alpha$. To find the normal coordinates of a given motion, invert: from $A^T\hat TA = \hat 1$, $A^{-1} = A^T\hat T$, so $\vec\xi = A^T\hat T\,\vec\eta$, and $\xi_\alpha = \vec a^{(\alpha)}\cdot\hat T\cdot\vec\eta$.

## A model of carbon dioxide

The notes' example is the linear molecule CO₂ (pp. 56–59): a carbon atom of mass $M$ between two oxygen atoms of mass $m$. Each oxygen is tied to the carbon by a spring of constant $k$ and natural length $b$. Springs alone cost no energy when the molecule bends at fixed bond lengths, so the model adds a second term that favours the straight shape.

Three atoms have 9 coordinates, the components of $\vec r_1$, $\vec r_2$, $\vec r_3$ (oxygen, carbon, oxygen). Six of them describe moving and turning the molecule as a rigid body, which costs no energy, so only 3 describe internal motion. Before computing anything, the notes guess the three normal modes from symmetry:

- the two oxygens move along the axis in the same direction, and the carbon moves the opposite way (a longitudinal mode, the antisymmetric stretch);
- the two oxygens move along the axis in opposite directions, and the carbon stays put (also longitudinal, the symmetric stretch);
- the two oxygens move sideways in the same direction, and the carbon the other way (the transverse, or bending, mode).

To remove the rigid-body motion the notes choose body axes (p. 57). All three atoms lie in the $xy$-plane, so $z_1 = z_2 = z_3 = 0$ (three conditions). The centre of mass is at the origin, $m(x_1 + x_3) + Mx_2 = 0$ and $m(y_1 + y_3) + My_2 = 0$ (two more). And the axes are turned so that the two oxygens are at the same height, $y_1 = y_3$ (one more). With the mass ratio $\rho = m/M$, the carbon's coordinates are then $x_2 = -\rho(x_1 + x_3)$ and $y_2 = -2\rho y_1$, and three coordinates remain: $(x_1, x_3, y_1)$.

## Potential and kinetic energy

The potential has the two spring terms plus a bending term (p. 57). The notes call the bending stiffness $\lambda$; this page calls it $\kappa$, to keep $\lambda$ for the eigenvalues:

<Formula label="Potential of the CO₂ model" tex="V = \frac{k}{2}(s_1 - b)^2 + \frac{k}{2}(s_2 - b)^2 + \frac{\kappa b^2}{2}\big(\alpha_1^2 + \alpha_2^2\big)" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.31), p. 57 (bending stiffness λ in the notes)" />

Here $s_1$ and $s_2$ are the lengths of the two bonds and $\alpha_1$, $\alpha_2$ the angles they make with the $x$ axis. In the three remaining coordinates (p. 58):

<Formula label="Bond lengths" tex="s_1 = \sqrt{[x_1 + \rho(x_1 + x_3)]^2 + (1 + 2\rho)^2 y_1^2}, \qquad s_2 = \sqrt{[x_3 + \rho(x_1 + x_3)]^2 + (1 + 2\rho)^2 y_1^2}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.32), p. 58" />

<Formula label="Bond angles" tex="\tan\alpha_1 = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{(1 + 2\rho)\, y_1}{(1 + \rho)x_1 + \rho x_3}, \qquad \tan\alpha_2 = \frac{y_3 - y_2}{x_3 - x_2} = \frac{(1 + 2\rho)\, y_1}{(1 + \rho)x_3 + \rho x_1}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.33), p. 58 (denominators corrected)" />

(The notes write the denominators as $(1 + \rho)x_1 + x_3$ and $(1 + \rho)x_3 + x_1$, attached to the other angle; working out $x_2 - x_1$ and $x_3 - x_2$ with $x_2 = -\rho(x_1 + x_3)$ gives the forms above. Near the straight shape the correct denominators are $\pm b$; the printed ones would be $\mp\rho b$ there and would give a bending term $1/\rho^2$ times too large. The notes' matrix, eq. (3.36), has the correct value, so their results below are unaffected.)

The kinetic energy of the three atoms is (p. 59)

<Formula label="Kinetic energy, all atoms" tex="T = \frac{m}{2}\big(\dot x_1^2 + \dot x_3^2\big) + \frac{M}{2}\dot x_2^2 + \frac{m}{2}\big(\dot y_1^2 + \dot y_3^2\big) + \frac{M}{2}\dot y_2^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.34), p. 59" />

and, with the carbon's velocities $\dot x_2 = -\rho(\dot x_1 + \dot x_3)$, $\dot y_2 = -2\rho\dot y_1$, $\dot y_3 = \dot y_1$, and $M\rho^2 = m\rho$, it becomes (p. 59)

<Formula label="Kinetic energy, three coordinates" tex="T = \frac{m}{2}(1 + \rho)\big(\dot x_1^2 + \dot x_3^2\big) + m\rho\,\dot x_1 \dot x_3 + m(1 + 2\rho)\,\dot y_1^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.35), p. 59" />

## The two matrices

The minimum is the straight molecule at rest: $y_1 = 0$ and $x_3 = -x_1 = b$, where both angles vanish, both springs have their natural length and $V = 0$. The displacements are $\eta_1 = x_1 + b$, $\eta_2 = y_1$ and $\eta_3 = x_3 - b$. Along the axis, $s_1 - b = -(1 + \rho)\eta_1 - \rho\eta_3$ and $s_2 - b = (1 + \rho)\eta_3 + \rho\eta_1$. Sideways, a bond length changes only at second order in $y_1$, so the spring energy has no $y_1^2$ term, while each angle is about $\pm(1 + 2\rho)y_1/b$, so the bending term is $\kappa(1 + 2\rho)^2y_1^2$. Taking second derivatives (p. 59):

<Formula label="Potential matrix of CO₂" tex="\hat V = \begin{pmatrix} k(1 + 2\rho + 2\rho^2) & 0 & 2k\rho(1 + \rho) \\ 0 & 2\kappa(1 + 2\rho)^2 & 0 \\ 2k\rho(1 + \rho) & 0 & k(1 + 2\rho + 2\rho^2) \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.36), p. 59" />

<Formula label="Kinetic matrix of CO₂" tex="\hat T = \begin{pmatrix} m(1 + \rho) & 0 & m\rho \\ 0 & 2m(1 + 2\rho) & 0 \\ m\rho & 0 & m(1 + \rho) \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.37), p. 59" />

The middle row and column of both matrices are empty apart from the diagonal, so the transverse coordinate does not talk to the other two. Its equation of motion is that of a single oscillator (p. 59):

<Formula label="Bending mode" tex="\ddot y_1 + \frac{2\kappa(1 + 2\rho)^2}{2m(1 + 2\rho)}\, y_1 = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.38), p. 59" />

so the bending frequency is $\omega^2 = \kappa(1 + 2\rho)/m$. The reason for the decoupling is a symmetry: reflecting the molecule in its own axis turns $y_1$ into $-y_1$ and leaves $x_1$, $x_3$ alone, so the energy cannot contain a term with one power of $y_1$ times $x_1$ or $x_3$.

## The two stretching modes

For the longitudinal pair $\vec\eta = (\eta_1, \eta_3)$, the condition $\det(\hat V - \lambda\hat T) = 0$ reads (p. 59)

<Formula label="Longitudinal frequencies" tex="\det\begin{pmatrix} k(1 + 2\rho + 2\rho^2) - \lambda m(1 + \rho) & 2k\rho(1 + \rho) - \lambda m\rho \\ 2k\rho(1 + \rho) - \lambda m\rho & k(1 + 2\rho + 2\rho^2) - \lambda m(1 + \rho) \end{pmatrix} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.39), p. 59" />

A $2 \times 2$ matrix with equal diagonal elements $d$ and equal off-diagonal elements $c$ has determinant $(d - c)(d + c)$, so the roots come from $d = c$ and $d = -c$. The first gives $k - \lambda m = 0$, the second $k(1 + 2\rho)^2 - \lambda m(1 + 2\rho) = 0$ (p. 59):

<Formula label="Stretching frequencies" tex="\lambda_1 = \omega_1^2 = \frac{k}{m}, \qquad \lambda_2 = \omega_2^2 = \frac{k}{m}\,(1 + 2\rho)" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.40), p. 59" />

with mode vectors, normalised so that $\vec a^{(\alpha)}\cdot\hat T\cdot\vec a^{(\beta)} = \delta_{\alpha\beta}$ (p. 59):

<Formula label="Stretching modes" tex="\vec a^{(1)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2m}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \vec a^{(2)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2m(1 + 2\rho)}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="Section 3.2, eq. (3.41), p. 59" />

The first mode has $\eta_3 = -\eta_1$: the oxygens move apart and together, and the carbon, by $x_2 = -\rho(x_1 + x_3)$, does not move. That is the symmetric stretch, and its frequency $\sqrt{k/m}$ does not involve the carbon at all. The second has $\eta_3 = \eta_1$: both oxygens move the same way, and the carbon moves $2\rho$ times as far in the opposite direction. That is the antisymmetric stretch, and it is faster, by $\sqrt{1 + 2\rho}$. The normal coordinates are $\xi_1 \propto x_1 - x_3$ and $\xi_2 \propto x_1 + x_3$ (up to constants), exactly the combinations one would have guessed.

## A worked example

<WorkedExample title="Putting numbers into the CO₂ model">
<Problem>
Take the mass numbers 16 for oxygen and 12 for carbon. Find $\rho$, the ratio $\omega_2/\omega_1$ of the stretching frequencies, and how far the carbon moves in the antisymmetric stretch compared with each oxygen. Then check that $\vec a^{(2)}$ is correctly normalised, and find the normal coordinate $\xi_2$ of the displacement $\eta_1 = \eta_3 = \epsilon$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Mass ratio and frequencies">
$\rho = m/M = 16/12 = 4/3$, so $1 + 2\rho = 11/3$ and $\omega_2/\omega_1 = \sqrt{11/3} \approx 1.915$: in this model the antisymmetric stretch vibrates almost twice as fast as the symmetric one. (The model is simple: real molecules have other effects, so this ratio tests the model rather than predicting a measured value exactly.)
</Step>
<Step title="The carbon's motion">
In the antisymmetric stretch $\eta_1 = \eta_3$, so $x_1 + x_3 = 2\eta_1$ and $x_2 = -2\rho\,\eta_1 = -(8/3)\eta_1$. The carbon moves about 2.67 times as far as each oxygen, in the opposite direction; the centre of mass stays put because $12 \times 8/3 = 32 = 2 \times 16$.
</Step>
<Step title="Normalisation">
With $\hat T$ restricted to $(\eta_1, \eta_3)$, $(1, 1)\cdot\hat T\cdot(1, 1) = m(1 + \rho) + 2m\rho + m(1 + \rho) = 2m(1 + 2\rho)$, so dividing $(1, 1)$ by $\sqrt{2m(1 + 2\rho)}$ gives a weighted length of exactly 1. In the same way $(1, -1)\cdot\hat T\cdot(1, -1) = 2m(1 + \rho) - 2m\rho = 2m$, and the two vectors are orthogonal with $\hat T$ as weight: $(1, 1)\cdot\hat T\cdot(1, -1) = m(1 + \rho) - m\rho + m\rho - m(1 + \rho) = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="A normal coordinate">
From $\xi_\alpha = \vec a^{(\alpha)}\cdot\hat T\cdot\vec\eta$ with $\vec\eta = (\epsilon, \epsilon)$: $\hat T\cdot\vec\eta = m(1 + 2\rho)\,\epsilon\,(1, 1)$, so $\xi_2 = 2m(1 + 2\rho)\epsilon/\sqrt{2m(1 + 2\rho)} = \sqrt{2m(1 + 2\rho)}\,\epsilon$, and $\xi_1 = 0$. The displacement is a pure antisymmetric stretch, as it should be; released from rest it oscillates at $\omega_2$ alone.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The normalised mode vectors, as the columns of a matrix $A$, diagonalise both energy matrices at once: $A^T\hat TA = \hat 1$ and $A^T\hat VA = \hat\lambda$. $A$ can be chosen real, since each mode vector has at most an overall phase.
- In the normal coordinates $\vec\xi$, defined by $\vec\eta = A\vec\xi$, the Lagrangian is $\tfrac12\sum_\alpha(\dot\xi_\alpha^2 - \omega_\alpha^2\xi_\alpha^2)$: $n$ independent oscillators $\ddot\xi_\alpha + \omega_\alpha^2\xi_\alpha = 0$. Inversely, $\xi_\alpha = \vec a^{(\alpha)}\cdot\hat T\cdot\vec\eta$.
- CO₂ model: oxygens of mass $m$, carbon of mass $M$, springs $k$ and a bending stiffness. Fixing the centre of mass and the orientation leaves three coordinates $(x_1, x_3, y_1)$.
- By reflection symmetry the bending mode decouples, with $\omega^2 = \kappa(1 + 2\rho)/m$. The stretching modes are $\omega_1^2 = k/m$ (oxygens opposite, carbon still) and $\omega_2^2 = (k/m)(1 + 2\rho)$ (oxygens together, carbon against them), $\rho = m/M$; for $\rho = 4/3$ the ratio is $\sqrt{11/3} \approx 1.915$.
- The notes' expressions for the two bond angles have a slip in the denominators; the notes' potential matrix uses the correct values, so the frequencies are unaffected.
