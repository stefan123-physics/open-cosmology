Section 4.1 found canonical transformations by building their generating functions, which means integrating. This page follows Section 4.2 of the notes (pp. 66–69), which gives a test that needs only differentiation, and along the way introduces a compact matrix notation in which coordinates and momenta stand on an equal footing.

## The Poisson bracket

The Poisson bracket of two functions $u(q, p)$ and $v(q, p)$ on phase space, met in the previous course, Classical Mechanics II, is (notes p. 66)

<Formula label="Poisson bracket" tex="\{u, v\}_{q,p} = \sum_i\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial v}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial v}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial u}{\partial p_i}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.43), p. 66" />

The subscript records which variables the derivatives are taken in; part of the point of this section is that it does not matter, as long as they are canonical. Applied to the coordinates and momenta themselves, the definition gives the **fundamental Poisson brackets**:

<Formula label="Fundamental Poisson brackets" tex="\{q_i, q_j\}_{q,p} = \{p_i, p_j\}_{q,p} = 0, \qquad \{q_i, p_j\}_{q,p} = \delta_{ij}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.44), p. 66" />

The bracket has a short list of algebraic properties (eqs. (4.45)–(4.49)): a function has zero bracket with itself; swapping the two entries changes the sign; the bracket is linear in each entry, $\{au + bv, w\} = a\{u, w\} + b\{v, w\}$ for constants $a$ and $b$; there is a product rule, and the three cyclic double brackets add up to zero:

<Formula label="Product rule" tex="\{uv, w\} = u\{v, w\} + \{u, w\}v" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.48), p. 66" />

<Formula label="Jacobi identity" tex="\{u, \{v, w\}\} + \{v, \{w, u\}\} + \{w, \{u, v\}\} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.49), p. 66" />

All of these follow from the definition by the rules of partial differentiation. The Jacobi identity takes the most bookkeeping: each term has a second derivative of one function, and those terms cancel in pairs.

## The link to quantum mechanics

The same list of properties belongs to the commutator of operators in quantum mechanics, such as $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$ (eq. (4.50)). Dirac was the first to study turning a classical theory into a quantum one by replacing every Poisson bracket with a commutator (notes p. 66):

<Formula label="Canonical quantization" tex="[\hat u, \hat v] = i\hbar\,\{u, v\}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.51), p. 66" />

The fundamental bracket $\{x, p\} = 1$ then becomes $[\hat x, \hat p] = i\hbar$. This rule is where the name **canonical quantization** comes from. The order of the factors in the product rule above was chosen to match the quantum formula, where operators need not commute; for ordinary functions the order does not matter. (The notes write the commutator without hats; the operators are meant.) The 8.05 page on [the Heisenberg picture](/courses/8-05/time-evolution-heisenberg-picture) shows the quantum side of this correspondence.

## The test for canonical transformations

The result of the section is this theorem (notes p. 66): a transformation $Q_j = Q_j(q, p, t)$, $P_j = P_j(q, p, t)$ is canonical **if and only if** the new variables have the fundamental brackets, computed in the old ones:

<Formula label="Poisson-bracket test for a canonical transformation" tex="\{Q_i, Q_j\}_{q,p} = \{P_i, P_j\}_{q,p} = 0, \qquad \{Q_i, P_j\}_{q,p} = \delta_{ij}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.52), p. 66" />

Only derivatives appear. Before the proof, here is the test at work.

<WorkedExample title="Testing two transformations with brackets">
<Problem>
(a) Check the transformation $Q = q^2$, $P = p/(2q)$ (with $q > 0$) from the worked example of Section 4.1. (b) Check the oscillator transformation of Section 4.1, written in the old variables as $Q = \arctan(m\omega q/p)$ and $P = (p^2 + m^2\omega^2q^2)/(2m\omega)$, at the phase-space point $q = 1$ m, $p = 2$ kg m/s, with $m = 1$ kg and $\omega = 2$ per second.
</Problem>
<Step title="(a) The squared coordinate">
$\{Q, P\} = \dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial q}\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial p} - \dfrac{\partial Q}{\partial p}\dfrac{\partial P}{\partial q} = 2q \cdot \dfrac{1}{2q} - 0 = 1$. With one degree of freedom $\{Q, Q\}$ and $\{P, P\}$ vanish automatically, so the transformation is canonical, as the generating function showed. No integration was needed.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The oscillator: derivatives">
Write $s = m\omega q/p$, so $Q = \arctan s$ and $dQ/ds = 1/(1 + s^2)$. Then $\partial Q/\partial q = m\omega p/(p^2 + m^2\omega^2q^2)$ and $\partial Q/\partial p = -m\omega q/(p^2 + m^2\omega^2q^2)$, while $\partial P/\partial p = p/(m\omega)$ and $\partial P/\partial q = m\omega q$.
</Step>
<Step title="(b) The bracket">
$\{Q, P\} = \dfrac{m\omega p}{p^2 + m^2\omega^2q^2}\cdot\dfrac{p}{m\omega} + \dfrac{m\omega q}{p^2 + m^2\omega^2q^2}\cdot m\omega q = \dfrac{p^2 + m^2\omega^2q^2}{p^2 + m^2\omega^2q^2} = 1$ at every point. At the given point $m\omega q = 2$ kg m/s equals $p$, so $Q = \arctan 1 = \pi/4$ and $P = (4 + 4)/4 = 2$ J s; the two terms of the bracket are $\tfrac{1}{2}$ each, adding up to 1.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Coordinates and momenta in one vector

For the proof, the notes stop treating $q$ and $p$ separately and stack all $2n$ of them into one column, with a fixed $2n \times 2n$ matrix $J$ (notes p. 66):

<Formula label="Phase-space vector and the matrix J" tex="\vec\eta = \begin{pmatrix} q_1 \\ \vdots \\ q_n \\ p_1 \\ \vdots \\ p_n \end{pmatrix}, \qquad J = \begin{pmatrix} 0_n & I_n \\ -I_n & 0_n \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.53), p. 66" />

Here $0_n$ is the $n \times n$ zero matrix and $I_n$ the $n \times n$ identity matrix. The matrix $J$ is antisymmetric, squares to minus the identity, and is orthogonal (notes p. 67):

<Formula label="Properties of J" tex="J^T = -J, \qquad J^2 = -I_{2n}, \qquad J^T J = JJ^T = I_{2n}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.54), p. 67" />

and $\det J = 1$. Both of Hamilton's equations now fit into one line: the upper half of $J$ sends $\partial H/\partial p_i$ to $\dot q_i$, the lower half sends $-\partial H/\partial q_i$ to $\dot p_i$:

<Formula label="Hamilton's equations in matrix form" tex="\dot{\vec\eta} = J\,\frac{\partial H}{\partial\vec\eta}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.55), p. 67" />

Here $\partial H/\partial\vec\eta$ is the column of all $2n$ partial derivatives, the gradient of $H$ in phase space.

## The symplectic condition

The notes prove the theorem for a transformation with no explicit time dependence, so that $K = H$; Section 4.3 indicates how the argument extends to time-dependent transformations, referring to Goldstein for the details. Stack the new variables in the same way, $\vec\xi = (Q_1, \ldots, Q_n, P_1, \ldots, P_n)$, a function $\vec\xi(\vec\eta)$ of the old ones (eqs. (4.56)–(4.57)). By the chain rule its time derivative involves the **Jacobian matrix** $M$ of the transformation:

<Formula label="The Jacobian of the transformation" tex="\dot\xi_i = \frac{\partial\xi_i}{\partial\eta_j}\,\dot\eta_j \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \dot{\vec\xi} = M\dot{\vec\eta}, \qquad M_{ij} = \frac{\partial\xi_i}{\partial\eta_j}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.58), p. 67" />

The transformation is canonical when the new variables obey $\dot{\vec\xi} = J\,\partial H/\partial\vec\xi$ (eq. (4.60)). Insert Hamilton's equations for $\dot{\vec\eta}$, and use the chain rule once more to turn $\partial H/\partial\eta_k$ into derivatives with respect to the $\xi_l$:

<Formula label="Hamilton's equations carried to the new variables" tex="\dot\xi_i = \frac{\partial\xi_i}{\partial\eta_j}J_{jk}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\eta_k} = \frac{\partial\xi_i}{\partial\eta_j}J_{jk}\frac{\partial\xi_l}{\partial\eta_k}\frac{\partial H}{\partial\xi_l} \quad\Leftrightarrow\quad \dot{\vec\xi} = MJM^T\frac{\partial H}{\partial\vec\xi}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.61), p. 68" />

This must equal $J\,\partial H/\partial\vec\xi$ for every Hamiltonian, so the transformation is canonical exactly when

<Formula label="The symplectic condition" tex="MJM^T = J" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.62), p. 68" />

This is the **symplectic condition**. It can be turned around: rewrite it as $MJ = J(M^T)^{-1}$, multiply by $J$ on both sides and use $J^2 = -I$ to get $JM = (M^T)^{-1}J$ (eqs. (4.63)–(4.64)), which rearranges to the equivalent form

<Formula label="The symplectic condition, second form" tex="M^T J M = J" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.65), p. 68" />

## From the symplectic condition to the brackets

The Poisson bracket also has a compact form in this notation: a row of derivatives of $u$, the matrix $J$, and a column of derivatives of $v$ (notes p. 68):

<Formula label="Poisson bracket in matrix form" tex="\{u, v\}_{q,p} = \{u, v\}_{\vec\eta} = \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\vec\eta}\right)^{\!T} J\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial\vec\eta}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.66), p. 68" />

and the fundamental brackets collapse into one statement, $\{\eta_i, \eta_j\}_{\vec\eta} = J_{ij}$ (eq. (4.67)). Now put the new variables into the bracket. The column $\partial\xi_j/\partial\vec\eta$ is the $j$-th column of $M^T$, so

<Formula label="Brackets of the new variables" tex="\{\xi_i, \xi_j\}_{\vec\eta} = \left(\frac{\partial\xi_i}{\partial\vec\eta}\right)^{\!T} J\,\frac{\partial\xi_j}{\partial\vec\eta} = (MJM^T)_{ij}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.68), p. 68" />

The new variables have the fundamental brackets, $\{\xi_i, \xi_j\}_{\vec\eta} = J_{ij}$ (eq. (4.69)), exactly when $MJM^T = J$, which is the symplectic condition, which holds exactly when the transformation is canonical. That proves the theorem. (The notes print eq. (4.68) with $M^T JM$ and then compare it with eq. (4.65); the chain of brackets actually gives $MJM^T$, eq. (4.62), and eq. (4.70) repeats the slipped form. Since the two forms of the condition are equivalent, the proof is unaffected.)

## Two consequences

**Poisson brackets do not care which canonical variables are used** (notes p. 69). The chain rule gives $\partial u/\partial\vec\eta = M^T\,\partial u/\partial\vec\xi$, and then the symplectic condition removes $M$:

<Formula label="Poisson brackets are canonical invariants" tex="\{u, v\}_{\vec\eta} = \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\vec\xi}\right)^{\!T} MJM^T\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial\vec\xi} = \left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial\vec\xi}\right)^{\!T} J\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial\vec\xi} = \{u, v\}_{\vec\xi}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eqs. (4.71)–(4.73), p. 69" />

so from now on the subscript can be dropped: $\{u, v\}$ has one value. This is what makes brackets useful for physics, in the next section.

**Canonical transformations preserve phase-space volume**, as discussed in Classical Mechanics II. A small region of phase space has its volume multiplied by the Jacobian determinant when the variables change (eq. (4.74)), $V_{\vec\xi} = |\det M|\,V_{\vec\eta}$. Taking the determinant of the symplectic condition settles its value:

<Formula label="Phase-space volume is preserved" tex="\det(M^T JM) = \det J = (\det M)^2\det J \quad\Rightarrow\quad |\det M| = 1" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.2, eq. (4.75), p. 69" />

For one degree of freedom the symplectic condition says no more than this: with $M = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d\end{pmatrix}$, $M^T JM = (ad - bc)J$, so a transformation of the $(q, p)$ plane is canonical exactly when it preserves areas (and orientation). The graph shows a linear example, $Q = 2q + p$, $P = q + p$, with $\det M = 2 - 1 = 1$: a circle of phase-space points is sheared into a tilted ellipse of the same area.

Liouville's theorem, that a cloud of phase-space points keeps its volume as it moves, is the special case in which the transformation is the motion itself; Section 4.3 shows that the motion is indeed a canonical transformation. The 8.044 page on [the microcanonical ensemble](/courses/8-044/microcanonical-ensemble) uses the theorem.

## Summary

- The Poisson bracket $\{u, v\}$ of eq. (4.43) is antisymmetric, linear, has a product rule and obeys the Jacobi identity; $\{q_i, p_j\} = \delta_{ij}$. Canonical quantization replaces it by $[\hat u, \hat v]/(i\hbar)$.
- **Test:** $(q, p) \to (Q, P)$ is canonical if and only if $\{Q_i, Q_j\} = \{P_i, P_j\} = 0$ and $\{Q_i, P_j\} = \delta_{ij}$. Only derivatives are needed.
- With $\vec\eta = (q, p)$ and the matrix $J$, Hamilton's equations read $\dot{\vec\eta} = J\,\partial H/\partial\vec\eta$, and a transformation with Jacobian $M$ is canonical exactly when $MJM^T = J$, equivalently $M^T JM = J$ (the symplectic condition).
- Consequences: Poisson brackets have the same value in all canonical variables, and phase-space volume is preserved, $|\det M| = 1$.
