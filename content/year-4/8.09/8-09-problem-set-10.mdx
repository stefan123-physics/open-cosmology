## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `a*omega0^2/g`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. The points per problem are MIT's, and so is their split between the parts of Problems 2 to 5. Problem 1 has no parts on the sheet; its split into three parts is ours. Several parts ask you to show or sketch something. Those parts have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

All five problems use the language of Section 7.3. A flow $\dot{\vec x} = \vec f(\vec x)$ has fixed points where $\vec f = 0$. Near one, the small displacement $\vec u$ obeys a linear system whose matrix holds the derivatives of $\vec f$ there:

<Formula label="Linearization about a fixed point" tex="\dot{\vec u} = M\vec u, \qquad M_{ij} = \left.\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}\right|_{\vec x = \vec x_*}" cite="reading1" at="Section 7.3.1, eq. (7.47), p. 170" />

In two dimensions the eigenvalues of $M$ follow from its trace $\tau$ and determinant $\Delta$:

<Formula label="Eigenvalues of a 2 × 2 system" tex="\lambda_\pm = \frac{\tau \pm \sqrt{\tau^2 - 4\Delta}}{2}" cite="reading1" at="Section 7.3.1, eqs. (7.42) and (7.43), p. 166" />

$\Delta < 0$ gives a saddle. For $\Delta > 0$: a node if $\tau^2 > 4\Delta$, a spiral if $\tau^2 < 4\Delta$, stable when $\tau < 0$ and unstable when $\tau > 0$, and a centre when $\tau = 0$ (pp. 166–168). An overdot is $d/dt$.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Classifying fixed points" points={8} mit="Problem 1">

A flow in the $(x, y)$ plane is

<Formula label="The flow of Problem 1" tex="\dot x = x\,(4 + y - x^2), \qquad \dot y = y\,(x - 1)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 2" />

Find all its fixed points, their stability and type, and the eigenvectors where they help; then sketch the trajectories. The sheet gives this as one task; we split it into three parts (our choice).

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Find all the fixed points. Give their number in the first box. Exactly one of them lies off both axes: give its $y$ coordinate in the second box.

<Hint>

$\dot y = 0$ needs $y = 0$ or $x = 1$. Take each case into $\dot x = 0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1a-n" label="Number of fixed points:" value="4" tolerance="0.1" calc="p1a-n" />

<Answer id="p1a-y" label="$y$ of the fixed point off the axes:" value="-3" tolerance="0.01" calc="p1a-y" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Both right-hand sides are products, so each vanishes when one of its factors does.

<Step title="The cases">

If $y = 0$, then $\dot x = x(4 - x^2) = 0$ gives $x = 0$ or $x = \pm 2$. If $x = 1$, then $\dot x = 1\cdot(4 + y - 1) = 0$ gives $y = -3$. So

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the fixed points" tex="(0, 0), \qquad (2, 0), \qquad (-2, 0), \qquad (1, -3)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

four fixed points, and $(1, -3)$ is the one off the axes.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $(1, -3)$: $\dot x = 1\cdot(4 - 3 - 1) = 0$ and $\dot y = -3\cdot 0 = 0$. The case $x = 0$ needs nothing new: then $\dot y = -y = 0$ gives the origin again.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Linearize about each fixed point and give its stability and type, one box for each. Then (our addition) give the angular frequency, the imaginary part of the eigenvalues, at the one fixed point whose eigenvalues are complex, as a decimal (in units of 1/time).

<Hint>

The matrix is $M = \begin{pmatrix} 4 + y - 3x^2 & x \\ y & x - 1\end{pmatrix}$. Put in each fixed point, then use $\tau$ and $\Delta$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

At three of the points $M$ is triangular, so its eigenvalues are the numbers on its diagonal.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1b-00" label="$(0, 0)$ is:" choices={["a saddle", "a stable node", "an unstable node", "a stable spiral", "a centre"]} answer={0} />

<Answer id="p1b-20" label="$(2, 0)$ is:" choices={["a stable node", "a stable spiral", "a saddle", "an unstable node", "a centre"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p1b-m20" label="$(-2, 0)$ is:" choices={["a saddle", "a stable node", "a stable spiral", "an unstable spiral", "a centre"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p1b-13" label="$(1, -3)$ is:" choices={["a stable node", "a saddle", "an unstable spiral", "a centre", "a stable spiral"]} answer={4} />

<Answer id="p1b-freq" label="Angular frequency of the spiral:" value="1.414" unit="1/time" tolerance="1%" calc="p1b-freq" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Evaluate the matrix of derivatives at each fixed point and read off its eigenvalues, or its trace and determinant.

<Step title="The matrix">

With $f_x = 4x + xy - x^3$ and $f_y = xy - y$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the matrix of the linearized flow" tex="M = \begin{pmatrix} 4 + y - 3x^2 & x \\ y & x - 1 \end{pmatrix}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The four points">

- $(0, 0)$: $M = \mathrm{diag}(4, -1)$. Eigenvalues $4$ and $-1$, of opposite sign: a **saddle** (unstable).
- $(2, 0)$: $M = \begin{pmatrix} -8 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$. Eigenvalues $-8$ and $1$: a **saddle**.
- $(-2, 0)$: $M = \begin{pmatrix} -8 & -2 \\ 0 & -3\end{pmatrix}$. Eigenvalues $-8$ and $-3$, both negative: a **stable node**.
- $(1, -3)$: $M = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & 1 \\ -3 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, with $\tau = -2$, $\Delta = 3$. Since $\tau^2 - 4\Delta = -8 < 0$ the eigenvalues are complex, $\lambda = -1 \pm i\sqrt2$, with negative real part: a **stable spiral**.

The spiral decays at the rate 1 and turns at the angular frequency $\sqrt2 = 1.414$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For each point the eigenvalues add up to $\tau$ and multiply to $\Delta$: for example $(-8)(1) = -8 = \Delta$ at $(2, 0)$, and $(-1 + i\sqrt2)(-1 - i\sqrt2) = 3$ at $(1, -3)$. None of the four points is a borderline case ($\Delta = 0$, $\tau = 0$ or $\tau^2 = 4\Delta$), so the linear classification holds for the full nonlinear flow (p. 170).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

Find the eigenvectors that help the sketch, and sketch the trajectories in the $(x, y)$ plane. As checks (our additions), give the slope $dy/dx$ of the unstable direction at the saddle $(2, 0)$, and the slope of the slow direction (the eigenvalue $-3$) at the node $(-2, 0)$, both as decimals.

<Hint>

The $x$ axis and the $y$ axis are both made of whole trajectories: on $y = 0$ the flow has $\dot y = 0$, and on $x = 0$ it has $\dot x = 0$. So one eigenvector of each point on an axis lies along that axis.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For the other eigenvector $(1, s)$ of $\begin{pmatrix} p & q \\ 0 & d\end{pmatrix}$ with eigenvalue $d$, the first row gives $p + qs = d$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1c-s1" label="Slope of the unstable direction at $(2, 0)$:" value="4.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p1c-s1" />

<Answer id="p1c-s2" label="Slope of the slow direction at $(-2, 0)$:" value="-2.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p1c-s2" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The axes carry trajectories of their own, which fixes one eigenvector at each point on an axis; the other comes from the first row of $M$.

<Step title="The eigenvectors">

- $(0, 0)$: the unstable direction is the $x$ axis (eigenvalue 4), the stable one the $y$ axis (eigenvalue $-1$).
- $(2, 0)$: eigenvalue $-8$ along the $x$ axis; for the eigenvalue 1, $-8 + 2s = 1$ gives $s = 9/2$, the direction $(2, 9)$, slope $4.5$.
- $(-2, 0)$: eigenvalue $-8$ along the $x$ axis; for $-3$, $-8 - 2s = -3$ gives $s = -5/2$, the direction $(2, -5)$, slope $-2.5$. Since $|-3| < |-8|$, trajectories approach this node along $(2, -5)$, tangent to that line, after the fast part along $x$ has died.
- $(1, -3)$: complex eigenvectors, so no special straight directions: trajectories spiral in.

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the real eigenvectors" tex="(2, 0):\ \lambda = 1 \text{ along } (2, 9), \qquad (-2, 0):\ \lambda = -3 \text{ along } (2, -5)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1, p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The sketch">

On the $x$ axis the flow runs away from the origin, towards $x = -2$ from both sides, and away from $x = 2$ on both sides. On the $y$ axis it runs into the origin. In the upper half plane ($y > 0$) points with $x > 1$ move up and those with $x < 1$ move down, and $\dot x = 0$ on the parabola $y = x^2 - 4$. Trajectories leave the saddle $(2, 0)$ along $(2, 9)$: one branch rises up and to the right without bound, the other goes down into the lower half plane and spirals into $(1, -3)$. Since both axes are trajectories, no trajectory crosses them, and each quadrant has its own fate. Points to the left of the $y$ axis end at the node $(-2, 0)$. Points with $x > 0$, $y < 0$ end in the spiral, and points with $x > 0$, $y > 0$ run off to infinity (in our numerical check every start we tried in these quadrants did so).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$M\,(2, 9) = (-16 + 18,\ 9) = (2, 9)$ at $(2, 0)$, and $M\,(2, -5) = (-16 + 10,\ 15) = (-6, 15) = -3\,(2, -5)$ at $(-2, 0)$. Both are eigenvectors with the stated eigenvalues.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

</Exercise>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Bead on a rotating hoop" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

A bead of mass $m$ slides on a hoop of radius $a$, with friction coefficient $\beta > 0$. The hoop stands vertically and turns about its vertical diameter (the $z$ axis) at the constant angular velocity $\omega_0$. With $\theta$ the angle of the bead from the bottom of the hoop, its equation of motion is

<Formula label="The bead on a rotating hoop" tex="m a\,\ddot\theta = -\beta\,\dot\theta + m a\,\omega_0^2\sin\theta\left(\cos\theta - \frac{g}{a\omega_0^2}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

The notes study the same equation (eq. (7.35), p. 162) in the overdamped limit, where $\ddot\theta$ is dropped. Here you keep it, so the motion lives in the two-dimensional phase space $(\theta, w)$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Show that new variables bring the equation to the dimensionless form

<Formula label="The dimensionless bead" tex="\dot\theta = w, \qquad \dot w = \sin\theta\left(\cos\theta - \frac{1}{\gamma}\right) - b\,w" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2" />

with $\gamma > 0$ and $b > 0$, where the overdot now means the derivative with respect to the dimensionless time $s = \omega_0 t$. Type $\gamma$ with `a`, `omega0` and `g`, and $b$ with `beta`, `m`, `a` and `omega0`. Then choose, in the third box, a symmetry of the system that involves both variables.

<Hint>

Divide the equation by $ma\omega_0^2$. Which time unit then makes the coefficient of $\sin\theta\cos\theta$ equal to 1?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2a-sym" label="The system is unchanged under:" choices={["θ → −θ with w kept", "θ → θ + π with w kept", "θ → −θ, w → −w", "w → −w with θ kept"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Divide by $ma\omega_0^2$ and measure time in units of $1/\omega_0$.

<Step title="The change of variables">

Dividing by $ma\omega_0^2$:

$\ddot\theta/\omega_0^2 = -\frac{\beta}{ma\omega_0^2}\,\dot\theta + \sin\theta\left(\cos\theta - \frac{g}{a\omega_0^2}\right)$.

With $s = \omega_0 t$, $d\theta/ds = \dot\theta/\omega_0$ and $d^2\theta/ds^2 = \ddot\theta/\omega_0^2$, so with $w = d\theta/ds$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the dimensionless numbers" tex="\gamma = \frac{a\omega_0^2}{g}, \qquad b = \frac{\beta}{ma\omega_0}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The symmetry">

Replace $\theta \to -\theta$ and $w \to -w$. The first equation becomes $-\dot\theta = -w$, the same. In the second, $\sin\theta$ changes sign and $\cos\theta$ does not, so the right-hand side changes sign, as does the left. It is the mirror image of the bead in the axis of rotation: a bead on the left side moving left behaves as one on the right moving right.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $a\omega_0^2/g$ is an acceleration over an acceleration, and $\beta$ has the units of $ma\ddot\theta/\dot\theta$, kg·m/s, so $\beta/(ma\omega_0)$ is a pure number too. The other choices fail: $\theta \to -\theta$ alone flips the sign of $\dot w$ but not of $w$ in $\dot\theta = w$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

$\Delta = -F'(\theta_*) = \cos\theta_*/\gamma - \cos 2\theta_*$:

(At $\gamma = 1$ the bottom has $\Delta = 0$, a borderline case where linear analysis says nothing: this is the pitchfork of the notes.)

</Step>

For $\gamma = 2$: $\theta_* = \arccos(1/2) = \pi/3$, $\Delta = 3/4$, and the change from spiral to node is at $b = 2\sqrt{3/4} = \sqrt3 = 1.73$. So for $b = 1$ in part (d) the side points are stable spirals. The centres for $b = 0$ are a linear verdict; part (c) confirms them with a conserved quantity.

</Step>

**The idea.** With $b = 0$ the equation is $\dot w = F(\theta)$, a particle in a potential; the potential includes the centrifugal term of the rotating hoop.

</Step>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Chaos in maps" points={15} mit="Problem 5">

Chaos in a flow of first-order differential equations needs three variables and nonlinearity. A map, $x_{n+1} = f(x_n)$, which gives a discrete sequence of points, can be chaotic with a single variable. (The Poincaré section of the driven pendulum is a hint of this: it, too, is a sequence of points.) The simplest example is the logistic map,

<Formula label="The logistic map" tex="x_{n+1} = r\,x_n(1 - x_n)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5, p. 4" />

with a fixed control parameter $r$. The sheet shows its bifurcation plot, made by discarding the first 300 values of $x_n$ and plotting the next 301 for many values of $r$: after the transients have died the points lie on an attractor that does not depend on $x_0$, and as $r$ grows the attractor goes through a sequence of period doublings into chaos, as the driven damped pendulum does. The sheet suggests MIT's Mathematica notebook for the map; any tool will do. The start of the plot, where it has simple formulas, is drawn here:

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Test the sensitivity to initial conditions: compute $\{x_n\}$ and $\{x'_n\}$ from two nearby starts $x_0$ and $x'_0$, once for a value of $r$ where the map is periodic and once where it is chaotic, and plot $x_n - x'_n$ to large enough $n$. Take $r = 3.2$ and $r = 4$, with $x_0 = 0.3$ and $x'_0 = x_0 + \delta$ (our choices). For $r = 4$, how small must $\delta$ be to make sure that $|x_n - x'_n| < 10^{-6}$ at $n = 15$? Give the largest such $\delta$ (for small $\delta$ the difference grows in proportion to $\delta$), typed for example as `2.5e-9`.

<Hint>

For a small $\delta$, $x_n - x'_n \approx -\frac{dx_n}{dx_0}\,\delta$, and by the chain rule $\frac{dx_n}{dx_0}$ is the product of the factors $f'(x_j)$ along the orbit, for $j = 0$ to $n - 1$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

$f'(x) = r(1 - 2x)$. Multiply the fifteen factors on a computer, or iterate both starts and shrink $\delta$ until the condition holds.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5a-d" label="Largest $\delta$:" value="3.66e-11" tolerance="2%" calc="p5a-d" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In chaotic motion a small difference is multiplied at every step by the size of $f'$, on average by $e^\lambda$ with $\lambda$ the Lyapunov exponent; in periodic motion it shrinks.

<Step title="The periodic case">

At $r = 3.2$ the attractor is the 2-cycle ($3 < r < 3.449$). With $\delta = 10^{-6}$ our difference is $4.0\times10^{-6}$ at $n = 10$ (the orbit first passes near the unstable fixed point), $5.4\times10^{-8}$ at $n = 20$ and $5.8\times10^{-12}$ at $n = 30$: every two steps it is multiplied by the multiplier of the 2-cycle, $f'(p)f'(q) = 4 + 2r - r^2 = 0.16$ (the product of eq. (7.77), p. 185), so it shrinks by a factor of about 6 every two steps: no sensitivity.

</Step>

<Step title="The chaotic case">

At $r = 4$, the product of the fifteen factors $4(1 - 2x_n)$ along the orbit from $x_0 = 0.3$ is

<Formula label="Problem 5(a): the stretching after fifteen steps" tex="\frac{dx_n}{dx_0} = \prod_{j=0}^{n-1} f'(x_j) = -2.729\times10^{4} \ \ (n = 15), \qquad \delta < \frac{10^{-6}}{2.729\times10^{4}} = 3.66\times10^{-11}" cite="reading1" at="Section 7.5, eq. (7.79), p. 186" />

Iterating both starts directly agrees: $\delta = 3\times10^{-11}$ gives $|x_n - x'_n| = 8.2\times10^{-7}$ at $n = 15$, and $\delta = 4\times10^{-11}$ gives $1.09\times10^{-6}$. To control 15 steps you need about eleven digits.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $r = 4$ the substitution $x = \sin^2(\pi u)$ turns the map into the doubling $u \to 2u$, so the Lyapunov exponent is $\ln 2$ and a difference grows on average by $2^{15} = 32768$ in 15 steps; it gives $\delta \approx 3.1\times10^{-11}$, close to but not the same as the exact value for this start, which the box checks. The same substitution gives the derivative exactly, $dx_n/dx_0 = 2^n\sin(2^{n+1}\pi u_0)/\sin(2\pi u_0)$ with $\sin^2(\pi u_0) = x_0$, and for $n = 15$ it has the same value.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

By zooming into the bifurcation plot, find the first four values of $r$ at which period doublings occur (the sheet calls them a₁ to a₄; we write $r_1$ to $r_4$, as the notes do). Two are $r_1 = 3$ and $r_2 = 1 + \sqrt6 = 3.4495$. Give $r_3$ and $r_4$ to four decimals, and the two ratios $(r_2 - r_1)/(r_3 - r_2)$ and $(r_3 - r_2)/(r_4 - r_3)$, which should agree to about two significant figures.

<Hint>

The branches split slowly near a doubling, so a bifurcation plot gives the doubling points only to about three decimals. For better values, find where the multiplier of the $2^k$-cycle, the product of $f'$ over the cycle, reaches $-1$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5b-r3" label="$r_3 =$" value="3.5441" tolerance="0.0005" calc="p5b-r3" />

<Answer id="p5b-r4" label="$r_4 =$" value="3.5644" tolerance="0.0005" calc="p5b-r4" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** A $2^k$-cycle turns unstable when its multiplier passes $-1$; the spacing between successive doublings shrinks by a nearly constant factor.

<Step title="The doubling points">

Solving $f^{(2^k)}(x) = x$ together with $\prod f'(x_j) = -1$ over the cycle (Newton's method in mpmath), we find

<Formula label="Problem 5(b): the first four period doublings" tex="r_1 = 3, \qquad r_2 = 3.449490, \qquad r_3 = 3.544090, \qquad r_4 = 3.564407" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 4 (our own computation)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The ratios">

$(r_2 - r_1)/(r_3 - r_2) = 0.449490/0.094600 = 4.751$ and $(r_3 - r_2)/(r_4 - r_3) = 0.094600/0.020317 = 4.656$. They agree to two significant figures, 4.7, and approach the Feigenbaum number

<Formula label="The Feigenbaum number" tex="\delta_F = \lim_{\alpha\to\infty}\frac{r_\alpha - r_{\alpha-1}}{r_{\alpha+1} - r_\alpha} \approx 4.669201" cite="reading1" at="Section 7.5, eq. (7.81), p. 187" />

(the next ratio, with the fifth doubling at $r = 3.568759$, is 4.668).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The first two values are exact: the fixed point $1 - 1/r$ has multiplier $2 - r$ (notes, eq. (7.74), p. 184), which is $-1$ at $r = 3$, and the 2-cycle's multiplier $4 + 2r - r^2$ is $-1$ at $r = 1 + \sqrt6$. With the spacings shrinking by $\delta_F$, the doublings accumulate near $3 + 0.4495/(1 - 1/4.669) = 3.572$ (eq. (7.83)), close to the true onset of chaos, 3.5699.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="c" points={3}>

Show that between the first and the second bifurcation, the two values of $x$ on the attractor satisfy

<Formula label="The cubic for the 2-cycle" tex="r^3x^2(2 - x) - r^2(r + 1)\,x + (r^2 - 1) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 5" />

The cubic has a third root. As checks (our additions), type the larger of the two attractor values and the third root, with `r`.

<Hint>

The two values are each a fixed point of $f(f(x))$, but not of $f$. Write out $f(f(x)) = x$ and remove the root you already know.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5c-p" label="Larger attractor value:" expr="(r + 1 + sqrt((r - 3)*(r + 1)))/(2*r)"
  vars={{ r: [3.05, 3.44] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(r + 1 - sqrt((r - 3)*(r + 1)))/(2*r)", hint: "This is the smaller of the two values: the square root enters with a minus sign." }

<Answer id="p5c-third" label="Third root:" expr="1 - 1/r"
  vars={{ r: [3.05, 3.44] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "0", hint: "x = 0 is a fixed point of the map, but it is not a root of the cubic: the cubic has the constant term r² − 1, which is not zero. It was removed when dividing by x." }

<Solution>

**The idea.** A point of the 2-cycle returns after two steps: $f(f(x)) = x$. That equation also holds for the fixed points, $0$ and $1 - 1/r$; removing the root $x = 0$ leaves the cubic.

<Step title="The cubic">

$f(f(x)) = r\,f(x)\,(1 - f(x))$ with $f(x) = rx(1 - x)$. For $x \neq 0$, divide $f(f(x)) = x$ by $x$:

<Formula label="Problem 5(c): the cubic from f(f(x)) = x" tex="r^2(1 - x)\bigl[1 - rx(1 - x)\bigr] - 1 = (r^2 - 1) - r^2(r + 1)\,x + r^3x^2(2 - x) = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

which is the sheet's cubic. Expanding: $r^2 - r^2x - r^3x(1 - x)^2 - 1$, and $-r^3x(1 - 2x + x^2) - r^2x = -r^2(r + 1)x + 2r^3x^2 - r^3x^3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Its three roots">

The fixed point $1 - 1/r$ also satisfies $f(f(x)) = x$, so $rx - (r - 1)$ is a factor:

$r^3x^2(2 - x) - r^2(r + 1)x + (r^2 - 1) = -\big(rx - r + 1\big)\big(r^2x^2 - r(r + 1)x + (r + 1)\big)$.

The quadratic gives the two points of the cycle,

<Formula label="Problem 5(c): the 2-cycle" tex="x = \frac{r + 1 \pm \sqrt{(r - 3)(r + 1)}}{2r}" cite="reading1" at="Section 7.5, p. 185" />

real for $r > 3$. Between $r = 3$ and $1 + \sqrt6$ they form the attractor, and the third root, $1 - 1/r$, is the unstable fixed point.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $r = 3$ the square root vanishes and both points equal $2/3 = 1 - 1/3$: the cycle is born out of the fixed point, as a period doubling should. At $r = 3.2$: $x = 0.7995$ and $0.5130$, and $3.2 \times 0.7995 \times 0.2005 = 0.5130$.

</Step>

</Solution>

<Part label="d" points={5}>

Make bifurcation plots for three more maps:

<Formula label="Three more maps" tex="\text{(i)}\ x_{n+1} = r\cos x_n, \qquad \text{(ii)}\ x_{n+1} = r x_n - x_n^3, \qquad \text{(iii)}\ x_{n+1} = e^{-r x_n}" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 5" />

All three show period doubling; two of them are chaotic, and for those choose a range of $r$ that shows both chaotic and regular regions. Choose the map that is not chaotic in the first box. Then (our additions) give the value of $r$ of the first period doubling of each map, to three decimals.

<Hint>

A fixed point of a map period-doubles where its multiplier $f'(x_*)$ passes $-1$. For (i) that means $x_* = r\cos x_*$ and $r\sin x_* = 1$ together.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Map (iii) is decreasing in $x$. What does that imply for $f(f(x))$, and for the cycles it can have?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5d-not" label="The map that is not chaotic:" choices={["(i) r cos x", "(iii) exp(−r x)", "(ii) r x − x³"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p5d-i" label="(i), first doubling at $r =$" value="1.319" tolerance="1%" calc="p5d-i" />

<Answer id="p5d-ii" label="(ii), first doubling at $r =$" value="2" tolerance="1%" calc="p5d-ii" />

<Answer id="p5d-iii" label="(iii), first doubling at $r =$" value="2.718" tolerance="1%" calc="p5d-iii" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the fixed point whose multiplier reaches $-1$ first; then run the map to see what follows.

<Step title="(i) r cos x">

$x_* = r\cos x_*$ and $f'(x_*) = -r\sin x_* = -1$ give $x_*\tan x_* = 1$, so $x_* = 0.8603$ and $r = \sqrt{x_*^2 + 1} = 1.319$. Our runs (start $x_0 = 0.5$, 1000 steps discarded, Lyapunov exponent over 4000 steps): a 2-cycle at $r = 1.4$ to 1.8, a 4-cycle at 1.9, chaos from about $r = 2.0$ (exponent 0.16 at $r = 2.0$, 0.36 at 2.2), with a periodic window at 2.6 and a stable fixed point again near $x = -r$ from $r \approx 3$. A range such as $1 < r < 3$ shows it all.

</Step>

<Step title="(ii) r x − x³">

The fixed points are $0$ (multiplier $r$) and $x_* = \pm\sqrt{r - 1}$ for $r > 1$, with multiplier $r - 3x_*^2 = 3 - 2r$, which is $-1$ at $r = 2$. Our runs: a 2-cycle at 2.05 to 2.2, a 4-cycle at 2.25, chaos from about $r = 2.35$, a window near 2.7, and chaos up to $r = 3$, beyond which orbits escape to infinity. The map is odd, $f(-x) = -f(x)$, so every attractor below about $r = 2.6$ has a mirror twin at negative $x$; the two bands merge into one near $r = 2.6$. A range $1.5 < r < 3$ shows it.

</Step>

<Step title="(iii) exp(−r x)">

The fixed point $x_* = e^{-rx_*}$ has multiplier $-rx_*$, which is $-1$ when $rx_* = 1$: then $x_* = e^{-1}$ and $r = e = 2.718$. Beyond that, a 2-cycle, and nothing more: our runs up to $r = 20$ show the 2-cycle with negative Lyapunov exponents. The reason is that $f$ is decreasing, so $f(f(x))$ is increasing, and an increasing map of a line moves every point monotonically towards a fixed point of it. So the orbit of $f$ settles on a fixed point or a 2-cycle, and never on anything longer or chaotic.

<Formula label="Problem 5(d): the first period doublings" tex="\text{(i)}\ r = 1.319, \qquad \text{(ii)}\ r = 2, \qquad \text{(iii)}\ r = e = 2.718" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(d), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At each doubling the multiplier is exactly $-1$: for (i), $-1.319\sin(0.8603) = -1.000$; for (ii), $3 - 2(2) = -1$; for (iii), $-e\cdot e^{-1} = -1$. Map (iii) does show one period doubling, as the sheet says, which is why "all three show period doubling" and "two are chaotic" fit together.

</Step>
