Section 4.2 introduced the Poisson bracket as a test for canonical transformations. This page follows Section 4.3 of the notes (pp. 69–72), where the bracket turns into a tool for physics: it gives the time evolution of any quantity, recognises conserved quantities, and, through transformations that differ only slightly from the identity, ties each conservation law to a symmetry. Section 1.3 of the notes reached that link in Lagrangian language (Noether's theorem); here it comes back in Hamiltonian form.

## The time derivative of any quantity

Take any function on phase space that may also depend on time, $u = u(q, p, t)$ (eq. (4.76)). Along the motion its rate of change follows from the chain rule, and Hamilton's equations replace $\dot q_i$ and $\dot p_i$ (notes p. 69):

<Formula label="Chain rule along the motion" tex="\dot u = \frac{\partial u}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i + \frac{\partial u}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i + \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial u}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial u}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eq. (4.77), p. 69" />

The first two terms are exactly a Poisson bracket:

<Formula label="Equation of motion of any quantity" tex="\dot u = \{u, H\} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eq. (4.78), p. 69" />

The derivation used only Hamilton's equations, so the result holds in any canonical variables, with the Hamiltonian that belongs to them. Canonical quantization (Section 4.2) turns it into the **Heisenberg equation** of quantum mechanics, $d\hat u/dt = (i/\hbar)[\hat H, \hat u] + \partial\hat u/\partial t$, met on the 8.05 page about [the Heisenberg picture](/courses/8-05/time-evolution-heisenberg-picture).

Three special cases check the formula (notes pp. 69–70). With $u = q_i$, $\{q_i, H\} = \partial H/\partial p_i$, and with $u = p_i$, $\{p_i, H\} = -\partial H/\partial q_i$: these are Hamilton's equations (eqs. (4.79)–(4.80)). With $u = H$ the bracket $\{H, H\}$ vanishes, so

<Formula label="Rate of change of the Hamiltonian" tex="\dot H = \{H, H\} + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eq. (4.81), p. 70" />

the familiar statement that the Hamiltonian is conserved unless it depends on time explicitly.

## Conserved quantities

A conserved quantity has $\dot u = 0$, so by eq. (4.78), $\{u, H\} + \partial u/\partial t = 0$ (eq. (4.82)). The common case is a quantity with no explicit time dependence, $\partial u/\partial t = 0$ (eq. (4.83)); then the test is a single bracket (notes p. 70):

<Formula label="Test for a conserved quantity" tex="\{u, H\} = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad u \text{ is conserved}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eq. (4.84), p. 70" />

The quantum version: an observable is conserved if it commutes with the Hamiltonian.

**Poisson's theorem** (the usual name; the notes simply call it another fact). If $u$ and $v$ are both conserved, so is their bracket $\{u, v\}$. Sometimes this hands us a new constant of the motion for free. The notes prove it in two steps. If neither depends on time explicitly (eq. (4.85)), then $\{v, H\} = \{H, u\} = 0$ and the Jacobi identity finishes the job:

<Formula label="Poisson's theorem, no explicit time dependence" tex="\{H, \{u, v\}\} = -\{u, \{v, H\}\} - \{v, \{H, u\}\} = -\{u, 0\} - \{v, 0\} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eq. (4.86), p. 70" />

In general, conservation means $\{v, H\} = -\partial v/\partial t$ and $\{H, u\} = \partial u/\partial t$. Put these into the Jacobi identity:

<Formula label="Poisson's theorem in general" tex="\{\{u, v\}, H\} = \{u, \{v, H\}\} + \{v, \{H, u\}\} = -\left\{u, \frac{\partial v}{\partial t}\right\} + \left\{v, \frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\right\} = -\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\{u, v\}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eq. (4.87), p. 70" />

The last step is the product rule for $\partial/\partial t$ acting on a bracket (the time derivative passes through the $q$ and $p$ derivatives). So $d\{u, v\}/dt = \{\{u, v\}, H\} + \partial\{u, v\}/\partial t = 0$.

<WorkedExample title="Two conserved components of angular momentum force the third">
<Problem>
For one particle, $L_x = yp_z - zp_y$, $L_y = zp_x - xp_z$ and $L_z = xp_y - yp_x$. Show that $\{L_x, L_y\} = L_z$, and use Poisson's theorem to conclude that if $L_x$ and $L_y$ are conserved, then so is $L_z$. Then evaluate the bracket at the point $\vec r = (1, 2, 0)$ m, $\vec p = (0, 1, 3)$ kg m/s.
</Problem>
<Step title="Only some derivatives survive">
$L_x$ contains $y, z, p_y, p_z$ and $L_y$ contains $z, x, p_z, p_x$. In the bracket $\{L_x, L_y\}$ of eq. (4.43), summed over $x$, $y$ and $z$, a term survives only when one function depends on a coordinate and the other on the matching momentum. The pairs are $(z, p_z)$ in both orders.
</Step>
<Step title="The bracket">
From $q_i = z$: $\partial L_x/\partial z \cdot \partial L_y/\partial p_z - \partial L_y/\partial z \cdot \partial L_x/\partial p_z = (-p_y)(-x) - (p_x)(y) = xp_y - yp_x = L_z$. The other coordinates give zero, because whenever $\partial L_x/\partial q_i \neq 0$, $\partial L_y/\partial p_i = 0$ and vice versa, except for $z$.
</Step>
<Step title="Poisson's theorem">
If $L_x$ and $L_y$ are conserved, their bracket $L_z$ is conserved too. (The quantum version, $[\hat L_x, \hat L_y] = i\hbar\hat L_z$, is the starting point of [the angular momentum algebra](/courses/8-05/angular-momentum-algebra) in 8.05.)
</Step>
<Step title="Numbers">
At the given point $L_x = 2 \times 3 - 0 \times 1 = 6$, $L_y = 0 \times 0 - 1 \times 3 = -3$, and $L_z = 1 \times 1 - 2 \times 0 = 1$, all in kg m²/s. The bracket of the first two equals the third, $1$ kg m²/s.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Infinitesimal canonical transformations

Section 4.1 found that $F_2 = q_iP_i$ generates the identity. Add a small piece to it (notes p. 70):

<Formula label="A transformation close to the identity" tex="F_2(q, P, t) = q_iP_i + \epsilon\,G(q, P, t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eq. (4.88), p. 70" />

with $|\epsilon| \ll 1$. The function $G$ is the **generator** of an infinitesimal canonical transformation. The type-2 rules of Section 4.1 give the change in each momentum and coordinate:

<Formula label="Change in the momentum" tex="p_j = \frac{\partial F_2}{\partial q_j} = P_j + \epsilon\frac{\partial G}{\partial q_j} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \delta p_j = P_j - p_j = -\epsilon\frac{\partial G}{\partial q_j}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eq. (4.89), p. 71" />

and $Q_j = \partial F_2/\partial P_j = q_j + \epsilon\,\partial G/\partial P_j$ (eq. (4.90)). Since $P_j$ and $p_j$ differ only at order $\epsilon$, so do $\partial G/\partial P_j$ and $\partial G/\partial p_j$, and to first order

<Formula label="Change in the coordinate" tex="Q_j = q_j + \epsilon\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_j} + O(\epsilon^2) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \delta q_j = Q_j - q_j = \epsilon\frac{\partial G}{\partial p_j}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eq. (4.91), p. 71" />

so $G$ may be treated as a function $G(q, p, t)$ of the old variables. In the stacked notation of Section 4.2, both changes are one bracket:

<Formula label="The generator moves phase-space points" tex="\delta\vec\eta = \epsilon\,\{\vec\eta, G\}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eq. (4.92), p. 71" />

Three generators show what this means (notes pp. 71–72).

- **Momentum generates translations.** With $G = p_i$, $\delta p_j = 0$ and $\delta q_j = \epsilon\,\delta_{ij}$: only $q_i$ moves, by $\epsilon$.
- **Angular momentum generates rotations.** With $G = L_z = \sum_i(x_ip_{iy} - y_ip_{ix})$, summed over particles, and $\epsilon = \delta\theta$ (eq. (4.93)):

<Formula label="Rotation generated by L_z" tex="\delta x_i = -y_i\,\delta\theta, \quad \delta y_i = x_i\,\delta\theta, \quad \delta z_i = 0; \qquad \delta p_{ix} = -p_{iy}\,\delta\theta, \quad \delta p_{iy} = p_{ix}\,\delta\theta, \quad \delta p_{iz} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eqs. (4.94)–(4.95), p. 71" />

  Positions and momenta both turn by the small angle $\delta\theta$ about the $z$ axis. For the particle of the worked example and $\delta\theta = 0.01$: $\delta x = -0.02$ m, $\delta y = 0.01$ m, $\delta p_x = -0.01$ kg m/s, $\delta p_y = 0$.

- **The Hamiltonian generates the motion.** With $G = H$ and $\epsilon = dt$,

<Formula label="The Hamiltonian generates time evolution" tex="\epsilon\,\{\vec\eta, G\} = \{\vec\eta, H\}\,dt = \dot{\vec\eta}\,dt = d\vec\eta" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, p. 71 (unnumbered)" />

  The left side is the change produced by the transformation; the right side is how far the system actually moves in time $dt$. They are equal.

## The motion as a canonical transformation

So far a canonical transformation was a **passive** relabelling, $(q, p) \to (Q, P)$, of the same point. The last example invites an **active** reading: the transformation generated by $H$ moves the point within one fixed set of phase-space coordinates. Use time as the parameter, start from $\vec\eta_0(t_0) = \vec\eta_0$ (eq. (4.96)), and apply the step with $\epsilon = dt$ again and again (eq. (4.97)): $\vec\eta_0 \to \vec\eta_1 \to \cdots \to \vec\eta_n$, up to the final time $t_n = t$. Strung together, infinitely many infinitesimal steps make a finite transformation (notes p. 72):

<Formula label="The motion as a finite canonical transformation" tex="\vec\eta = \vec\eta(\vec\eta_0, t) \quad\text{or}\quad \vec\eta_0 = \vec\eta_0(\vec\eta, t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eq. (4.98), p. 72" />

Each step is canonical, so the whole map from initial to present values is canonical, and it **is** the solution of the motion. (Our remark, not the notes': this is why Liouville's theorem follows from Section 4.2: the motion preserves phase-space volume.) The graph shows the idea for an oscillator: the points are snapshots of one phase-space point, each obtained from the last by the transformation generated by $H$.

Could the transformation be found directly, without adding up infinitesimal steps? Look for a canonical transformation whose new coordinates and momenta are all **constants**, an equation of the form $\vec\eta_0 = \vec\eta_0(\vec\eta, t)$ (eq. (4.99)); inverting it gives the motion. That is the programme of the Hamilton-Jacobi equation in Section 4.4. The notes also mention, citing Goldstein, that this active view completes the proof of Section 4.2's theorem for transformations that depend on time.

## Symmetries and conservation laws

Finally, how does the Hamiltonian change under the active transformation generated by $G$? Using $K = H + \epsilon\,\partial G/\partial t$, Goldstein shows (the notes quote the result, p. 72)

<Formula label="Change of the Hamiltonian under a generator" tex="\Delta H = -\epsilon\,\{G, H\} - \epsilon\frac{\partial G}{\partial t} = -\epsilon\,\dot G" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.3, eq. (4.100), p. 72" />

Since $\epsilon$ is not zero, $\Delta H$ vanishes exactly when $\dot G$ does: **$G$ is a constant of the motion exactly when the transformation it generates leaves $H$ unchanged**. Every conservation law therefore comes paired with a symmetry of the Hamiltonian, and every such symmetry with a conserved quantity. Momentum is conserved when $H$ does not change under translations, angular momentum when it does not change under rotations. For example, for one particle in a central potential, $H = |\vec p|^2/2m + V(|\vec r|)$; a rotation changes neither the length of $\vec r$ nor that of $\vec p$, so $\{L_z, H\} = 0$ and $L_z$ is conserved.

## Summary

- Any $u(q, p, t)$ evolves as $\dot u = \{u, H\} + \partial u/\partial t$; this contains Hamilton's equations and $\dot H = \partial H/\partial t$, and quantizes to the Heisenberg equation.
- A quantity without explicit time dependence is conserved exactly when $\{u, H\} = 0$. By the Jacobi identity, the bracket of two conserved quantities is conserved (Poisson's theorem); for example $\{L_x, L_y\} = L_z$.
- $F_2 = q_iP_i + \epsilon G$ generates $\delta\vec\eta = \epsilon\{\vec\eta, G\}$: momentum generates translations, angular momentum rotations, and $H$ with $\epsilon = dt$ the motion.
- The motion is a canonical transformation from initial to present values; finding it directly, as a transformation to constant variables, is the Hamilton-Jacobi programme.
- $\Delta H = -\epsilon\dot G$: a generator is conserved exactly when it is a symmetry of $H$.
