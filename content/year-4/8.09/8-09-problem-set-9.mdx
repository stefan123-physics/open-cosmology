## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `g*sin(theta)/nu`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. It treats its letters as independent, so each question says which letters to type: for example `theta` for $\theta$, `nu` for $\nu$, `rho` for $\rho$, `psi` for $\psi$, `sigma` for $\sigma$, `qc` for $q_c$, `phi` for $\varphi$.

The points per problem are MIT's. MIT splits them between the parts of Problems 1 to 4 and gives each case of Problem 5 four points; the split of those four points between the boxes of a case is ours. Several parts ask you to show or sketch something. Those parts have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The chaos problems use the language of Section 7.1. A system of first-order equations $\dot{\vec x} = \vec f(\vec x)$ is a flow in phase space. A volume of starting points changes at a rate set by the divergence of $\vec f$:

<Formula label="The rate of change of a phase-space volume" tex="\dot V = \int dV\,\nabla\cdot\vec f" cite="reading2" at="Section 7.1.2, eq. (7.11), p. 145" />

A fixed point $\vec x^\star$ has $\vec f(\vec x^\star) = 0$. Near it, $\vec\eta = \vec x - \vec x^\star$ obeys a linear system, whose solutions grow or decay as $e^{\lambda t}$ with $\lambda$ an eigenvalue of its matrix. In two dimensions the eigenvalues follow from the trace $\tau$ and the determinant $\Delta$ of the matrix:

<Formula label="Eigenvalues of a 2 × 2 system" tex="\lambda_\pm = \frac{\tau \pm \sqrt{\tau^2 - 4\Delta}}{2}" cite="reading2" at="Section 7.3.1, eqs. (7.42) and (7.43), p. 166" />

An overdot is $d/dt$.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Viscous flow on an inclined plane" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A layer of water of constant depth $h$ runs down a very large flat plane that is tilted at an angle $\theta$ to the horizontal. The water has density $\rho$ and kinematic viscosity $\nu$. The flow is steady, incompressible and laminar, and gravity drives it. Above the water is air at pressure $p_\text{atm}$. Take $x$ along the plane, pointing down the slope, $y$ perpendicular to the plane, with $y = 0$ on the plane and $y = h$ at the open surface, and $z$ across the slope (our choice of axes; the sheet's figure has $x$ down the slope). Gravity $\vec g$ then has the components $g\sin\theta$ along $x$ and $-g\cos\theta$ along $y$.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Which form of the Navier-Stokes equation fits this problem? Choose it in the box.

<Hint>

Start from the general equation of the notes and strike out what the problem lets you drop: is anything changing in time, is the flow compressed, is there a force on every bit of water?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1a-ns" label="The equation for this flow is:" choices={["∂v/∂t + (v·∇)v = −∇p/ρ + ν∇²v", "(v·∇)v = −∇p/ρ + ν∇²v + g", "(v·∇)v = −∇p/ρ + g", "(v·∇)v = −∇p/ρ − ν∇²v + g"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Take the Navier-Stokes equation with an external force and keep only what this flow needs.

<Step title="The general equation">

With a force density $\vec f$ the notes write

<Formula label="The Navier-Stokes equation" tex="\frac{\partial\vec v}{\partial t} + (\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v + \frac{\nabla p}{\rho} - \frac{\eta}{\rho}\nabla^2\vec v - \frac{1}{\rho}\Big(\zeta + \frac{\eta}{3}\Big)\nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec v) = \frac{\vec f}{\rho}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.112), p. 128" />

with $\eta = \rho\nu$ the (shear) viscosity and $\zeta$ the bulk viscosity.

</Step>

<Step title="What this flow allows">

The flow is steady, so $\partial\vec v/\partial t = 0$. It is incompressible, so $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$ and the bulk-viscosity term drops out, as in the notes' eq. (6.113) (p. 129). The force is gravity, $\vec f = \rho\vec g$. What is left is

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the equation for the film" tex="(\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v = -\frac{\nabla p}{\rho} + \nu\nabla^2\vec v + \vec g, \qquad \nabla\cdot\vec v = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Every term has units of acceleration: $\nu\nabla^2\vec v$ is (m²/s)(m/s)/m² = m/s². Without viscosity it falls back to the Euler equation with gravity; without gravity it is the equation the notes use for pipes, eq. (6.120) (p. 130).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

What does symmetry tell you about the velocity $\vec v$ and the pressure $p$? Choose it in the first box. Then choose, in the second box, the boundary conditions on $x̂\cdot\vec v = v_x$ at the plane and at the open surface.

<Hint>

The plane is unbounded and the depth is the same everywhere. Which coordinates can the flow then depend on? Then use $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$ for the component $v_y$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

At the plane the water sticks to it. At the open surface the force on the water must match what the air can exert; air at rest exerts its pressure and nothing along the surface (our choice).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1b-sym" label="Symmetry gives:" choices={["v = vₓ(x, y) x̂ and p = p(x, y)", "v = vₓ(y) x̂ + v_y(y) ŷ and p = p(y)", "v = vₓ(y) x̂ and p = p(y)", "v = vₓ(y) x̂, p constant"]} answer={2} />

<Answer id="p1b-bc" label="The boundary conditions are:" choices={["vₓ = 0 at y = 0 and ∂vₓ/∂y = 0 at y = h", "vₓ = 0 at y = 0 and at y = h", "∂vₓ/∂y = 0 at y = 0 and vₓ = 0 at y = h", "vₓ = 0 at y = 0 and no condition at all at y = h"]} answer={0} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Nothing changes along the slope or across it, so the flow can depend only on the distance from the plane. Incompressibility and the plane then fix the direction of the flow.

<Step title="The velocity">

Moving the whole picture along $x$ or $z$ changes nothing, so $\vec v$ and $p$ depend on $y$ only. Then $\nabla\cdot\vec v = dv_y/dy = 0$, so $v_y$ is the same at every height; it vanishes at the plane, which water cannot pass through, so $v_y = 0$ everywhere. Nothing pushes the water across the slope, and the mirror symmetry $z \to -z$ forbids a $v_z$. So $\vec v = v_x(y)\,\hat x$. The convective term then vanishes, $(\vec v\cdot\nabla)\vec v = v_x\,\partial\vec v/\partial x = 0$, as for the plates in the notes (eq. (6.121), p. 131).

</Step>

<Step title="The pressure">

The same symmetry makes $p = p(y)$. (A pressure that changed along $x$ would also break the condition $p = p_\text{atm}$, which holds all along the open surface.)

</Step>

<Step title="Boundary conditions">

A viscous fluid sticks to a wall: $\vec v = \vec v_\text{wall}$ (notes, Section 6.6, p. 129). The plane is at rest, so $v_x = 0$ at $y = 0$. The force per unit area on a surface with normal $\hat n$ is

<Formula label="Force per unit area on a surface" tex="F_i = -p\,n_i + \sigma_{ij}n_j" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.6, eq. (6.117), p. 129" />

At the open surface $\hat n = \hat y$, and its tangential part is $\sigma_{xy} = \eta\,(\partial v_x/\partial y + \partial v_y/\partial x) = \eta\,dv_x/dy$. The air exerts no tangential force (our choice), so

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the boundary conditions" tex="v_x = 0 \text{ at } y = 0, \qquad \frac{dv_x}{dy} = 0 \text{ and } p = p_\text{atm} \text{ at } y = h" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Three conditions for one second-order equation (for $v_x$) and one first-order equation (for $p$): exactly as many as the solution in (c) needs. Setting $v_x = 0$ at $y = h$ instead would treat the air like a second wall, which is the flow between two plates of eq. (6.125).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Solve the equation to find $\vec v$ and $p$ in the water. Type $v_x(y)$ with `g`, `theta`, `nu`, `h` and `y`, and $p(y)$ with `patm`, `rho`, `g`, `theta`, `h` and `y`. Then (our addition) take a film $h = 0.50$ mm deep on a plane tilted at $\theta = 10°$, with $\nu = 1.0\times10^{-6}$ m²/s (water near room temperature) and $g = 9.81$ m/s² (our numbers), and give the speed of the water at its surface in m/s.

<Hint>

Split the equation of (a) into its $x$ and $y$ components. One gives $p(y)$ alone; the other gives a second derivative of $v_x$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Integrate $d^2v_x/dy^2 = \text{constant}$ twice and fix the two constants with the two conditions on $v_x$ from (b).

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1c-us" label="Speed at the surface (m/s):" value="0.213" unit="m/s" tolerance="1%" calc="p1c-us" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With $\vec v = v_x(y)\hat x$ and $p = p(y)$ the equation becomes two ordinary differential equations, one for each component.

<Step title="The two components">

The $y$ component has no velocity term left: $0 = -\frac{1}{\rho}\frac{dp}{dy} - g\cos\theta$. The $x$ component has no pressure term, since $p$ does not depend on $x$: $0 = \nu\frac{d^2v_x}{dy^2} + g\sin\theta$. Gravity along the slope is balanced by viscous friction, as the pressure drop is in a pipe (eq. (6.123), p. 131).

</Step>

<Step title="The pressure">

Integrating from the surface down, with $p(h) = p_\text{atm}$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the pressure" tex="p(y) = p_\text{atm} + \rho g\cos\theta\,(h - y)" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The velocity">

Integrating twice, $v_x = -\frac{g\sin\theta}{2\nu}y^2 + Cy + D$. The plane gives $D = 0$; the open surface gives $-\frac{g\sin\theta}{\nu}h + C = 0$. So

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the velocity" tex="\vec v = \frac{g\sin\theta}{2\nu}\,y(2h - y)\,\hat x" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

a half parabola, fastest at the open surface.

</Step>

<Step title="The numbers">

At $y = h$ the speed is $g\sin\theta\,h^2/(2\nu) = 9.81 \times 0.1736 \times (0.50\times10^{-3})^2/(2.0\times10^{-6}) = 0.213$ m/s.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $g h^2/\nu$ is (m/s²)(m²)/(m²/s) = m/s. The profile is half of the two-plate profile of eq. (6.125) for plates $2h$ apart, with the open surface on the middle line, where the two-plate flow has zero shear too. The flow stops when the plane is level ($\theta = 0$), and the pressure at the plane, $p_\text{atm} + \rho gh\cos\theta$, is the weight per unit area of the water column pressing on the plane.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Damped nonlinear oscillator" points={15} mit="Problem 2">

A damped pendulum, in dimensionless units, obeys

<Formula label="The damped nonlinear oscillator" tex="\dot\theta = w, \qquad \dot w = -\frac{1}{q}\,w - \sin\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 2, p. 2" />

where the quality factor $q$ is positive. Its fixed points have $w = 0$ and $\theta = n\pi$, with $n$ any integer. Near a fixed point write $\theta = n\pi + \psi$ (our letter), with $\psi$ small.

<Part label="a" points={2}>

Expand the nonlinear term about a fixed point to get linear equations for $\psi$ and $w$. Type $\dot w$ near a fixed point with $n$ even, and near one with $n$ odd, using `w`, `psi` and `q`.

<Hint>

$\sin(n\pi + \psi) = \sin(n\pi)\cos\psi + \cos(n\pi)\sin\psi$. What is $\cos(n\pi)$?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2a-even" label="$n$ even: $\dot w =$" expr="-w/q - psi"
  vars={{ w: [-1, 1], psi: [0.1, 1], q: [0.5, 5] }} names={{ psi: "\\psi" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-w/q + psi", hint: "This is the equation near the top (n odd). At the bottom, cos(nπ) = +1 and the force pulls ψ back." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2a-odd" label="$n$ odd: $\dot w =$" expr="-w/q + psi"
  vars={{ w: [-1, 1], psi: [0.1, 1], q: [0.5, 5] }} names={{ psi: "\\psi" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-w/q - psi", hint: "This is the equation near the bottom (n even). For n odd, cos(nπ) = −1, so the sign of the ψ term flips." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Only $\sin\theta$ is nonlinear; replace it by its first-order expansion about $n\pi$.

<Step title="The expansion">

$\sin(n\pi + \psi) = (-1)^n\sin\psi \approx (-1)^n\psi$. The equation $\dot\theta = w$ is already linear and becomes $\dot\psi = w$. So

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the linear equations" tex="\dot\psi = w, \qquad \dot w = -\frac{1}{q}\,w - (-1)^n\,\psi" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The matrix of the system is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -(-1)^n & -1/q\end{pmatrix}$, with trace $\tau = -1/q$ and determinant $\Delta = (-1)^n$. For $n$ even $\Delta > 0$ and $\tau < 0$, the signs of a stable point; for $n$ odd $\Delta < 0$, the sign of a saddle (notes, Section 7.3.1, pp. 166–168). This is the pendulum hanging down and standing up.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Take $q = \infty$ (no damping). Near the fixed points with $n$ even the motion is an oscillation, an ellipse in phase space; near those with $n$ odd the fixed point is a saddle. Solve your equations from (a) in both cases. As a sample (our addition), type $w(t)$ near an even fixed point for the start $\psi(0) = \psi_0$, $w(0) = 0$, using `psi0` and `t`. For the saddle, find the directions in the $(w, \theta)$ phase space along which a solution purely grows or purely decays, and choose them in the second box.

<Hint>

For $n$ even the two equations combine to $\ddot\psi = -\psi$. For $n$ odd they combine to $\ddot\psi = +\psi$: try $e^{t}$ and $e^{-t}$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

For a pure exponential $\psi = Ce^{\lambda t}$, the first equation gives $w = \lambda\psi$. That is a straight line through the fixed point.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2b-w" label="$w(t) =$" expr="-psi0*sin(t)"
  vars={{ psi0: [0.05, 0.5], t: [0.1, 3] }} names={{ psi0: "\\psi_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "psi0*sin(t)", hint: "Check the sign: starting at rest on the positive side, the pendulum first swings back towards ψ = 0, so w is negative at first." }

<Answer id="p2b-dir" label="Near the saddle:" choices={["it grows along w = −ψ, decays along w = ψ", "it grows along the θ axis and decays along the w axis", "it grows along w = ψ and decays along w = −ψ", "it grows along w = 2ψ and decays along w = −ψ/2"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Without damping both cases are second-order equations with constant coefficients; their solutions are sines and cosines or real exponentials.

<Step title="Near the bottom: oscillations">

For $n$ even, $\ddot\psi = \dot w = -\psi$, so $\psi = A\cos(t + \delta)$ and $w = \dot\psi = -A\sin(t + \delta)$, with $A$ and $\delta$ set by the start. Then $\psi^2 + w^2 = A^2$: a closed curve round the fixed point, a circle in these units and an ellipse once the two axes are drawn to different scales. For $\psi(0) = \psi_0$, $w(0) = 0$: $A = \psi_0$, $\delta = 0$, so $w = -\psi_0\sin t$.

</Step>

<Step title="Near the top: a saddle">

For $n$ odd, $\ddot\psi = +\psi$, so

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): near the saddle" tex="\psi = C_+e^{t} + C_-e^{-t}, \qquad w = \dot\psi = C_+e^{t} - C_-e^{-t}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The purely growing solution ($C_- = 0$) has $w = \psi$, the line at 45° between the $w$ and $\theta$ axes. The purely decaying one ($C_+ = 0$) has $w = -\psi$, the line at 45° the other way.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Both are what energy conservation predicts: with $q = \infty$ the energy $\frac12 w^2 - \cos\theta$ is conserved, and near the top it is $\frac12(w^2 - \psi^2) + 1$, whose level curves are hyperbolas with the asymptotes $w = \pm\psi$. The decaying direction $w = -\psi$ is the pendulum swinging up towards the top, just fast enough to stop there.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

**The idea.** Near an attractor the linear equation is the damped harmonic oscillator, $\ddot\psi + \dot\psi/q + \psi = 0$, whose solutions are exponentials $e^{\lambda t}$.

<Step title="The rates">

<Formula label="Case (a)" tex="\dot x = x\,(r - e^{x})" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 5" />

Give $r_c$, the type, and (our addition) the slope $f'(x^\star)$ at the fixed point $x^\star \ne 0$, typed with `r` (for $r > 0$).

<Hint>

Find the fixed points: one is there for every $r$, the other only for $r > 0$. Where do they meet?

</Hint>

<Hint>

The sign of $f'(x)$ at a fixed point tells whether it is stable. $f'(x) = r - e^x - xe^x$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5a-rc" label="$r_c =$" value="1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p5a-rc" />

<Answer id="p5a-type" label="Type:" choices={["saddle-node: two fixed points meet and vanish", "transcritical: two fixed points cross and swap stability", "supercritical pitchfork: one stable point splits into two stable points and an unstable one", "subcritical pitchfork: two unstable points close in on a stable one", "Hopf: a limit cycle is born"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p5a-slope" label="$f'(x^\star) =$" expr="-r*log(r)"
  vars={{ r: [1.2, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "-log(r)", hint: "This drops a factor: at x = ln r the last term of f′(x) = r − eˣ − x eˣ is x eˣ = r ln r." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Find the fixed points, then their stability from the sign of $f'$, on both sides of the place where they meet.

<Step title="Fixed points">

$\dot x = 0$ for $x = 0$ (every $r$) and for $e^x = r$, that is $x^\star = \ln r$ (only for $r > 0$). The two coincide when $\ln r = 0$: $r_c = 1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Stability">

$f'(0) = r - 1$: the origin is stable for $r < 1$ and unstable for $r > 1$. At $x^\star = \ln r$: $f'(x^\star) = r - r - r\ln r = -r\ln r$, positive (unstable) for $0 < r < 1$ and negative (stable) for $r > 1$. So the two branches cross at $r = 1$ and swap their stability:

<Formula label="Problem 5(a): fixed points and their slopes" tex="x^\star = 0:\ f' = r - 1, \qquad x^\star = \ln r:\ f' = -r\ln r, \qquad r_c = 1" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

a transcritical bifurcation.

</Step>

<Step title="The pictures">

Different pictures come for $r \le 0$ (only $x = 0$, stable; $\dot x$ is positive for $x < 0$ and negative for $x > 0$), $0 < r < 1$ ($\ln r < 0$ unstable, $0$ stable), $r = 1$ (one point where the curve touches zero from below) and $r > 1$ ($0$ unstable, $\ln r > 0$ stable). The bifurcation diagram has the line $x = 0$ (solid for $r < 1$, dashed for $r > 1$) crossed at $r = 1$ by the curve $x = \ln r$ (dashed before, solid after).

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Near the crossing, write $x$ small and $r = 1 + \epsilon$: $\dot x = x(1 + \epsilon - 1 - x - \ldots) \approx x(\epsilon - x)$, the normal form of the transcritical bifurcation, eq. (7.26).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Case (b), for $x > -1$:

<Formula label="Case (b)" tex="\dot x = r + x - \ln(1 + x)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 5" />

Give $r_c$, the type, and (our addition) the slope $f'(x)$ as a function of $x$, typed with `x`.

<Hint>

Write the condition for a fixed point as $r = \ln(1 + x) - x$, and sketch the right-hand side. What is its largest value?

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5b-rc" label="$r_c =$" value="0" tolerance="0.01" calc="p5b-rc" />

<Answer id="p5b-type" label="Type:" choices={["transcritical: two fixed points cross and swap stability", "saddle-node: two fixed points meet and vanish", "supercritical pitchfork: one stable point splits into two stable points and an unstable one", "Hopf: a limit cycle is born", "subcritical pitchfork: two unstable points close in on a stable one"]} answer={1} />

<Answer id="p5b-slope" label="$f'(x) =$" expr="x/(1 + x)"
  vars={{ x: [0.1, 3] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "1 + 1/(1 + x)", hint: "Check the sign: the derivative of −ln(1 + x) is −1/(1 + x)." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Fixed points exist where the curve $\ln(1 + x) - x$ reaches the level $r$; they are born where $r$ equals its largest value.

<Step title="Fixed points">

The function $g(x) = \ln(1 + x) - x$ has $g'(x) = 1/(1 + x) - 1 = -x/(1 + x)$, so it rises for $-1 < x < 0$, has its largest value $g(0) = 0$, and falls for $x > 0$. So: two fixed points for $r < 0$, one (at $x = 0$) for $r = 0$, none for $r > 0$. That gives $r_c = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Stability">

$f'(x) = 1 - 1/(1 + x) = x/(1 + x)$. The fixed point with $x < 0$ is stable and the one with $x > 0$ unstable; they meet at $x = 0$ when $r = 0$ and vanish: a saddle-node bifurcation.

<Formula label="Problem 5(b): the bifurcation" tex="f'(x) = \frac{x}{1 + x}, \qquad r_c = 0 \text{ at } x = 0" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(b), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The pictures">

The curve $\dot x(x)$ is $r$ plus the U-shaped $x - \ln(1 + x)$, which is never negative and touches zero at $x = 0$. For $r < 0$ the U crosses zero twice, for $r = 0$ it touches it, for $r > 0$ it stays above it and every $x$ flows to the right. The bifurcation diagram is a parabola-like curve opening to the left in the $(r, x)$ plane, solid below $x = 0$, dashed above, with its nose at $(0, 0)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Near $x = 0$, $\ln(1 + x) = x - x^2/2 + \ldots$, so $\dot x \approx r + x^2/2$: the normal form $r + x^2$ of the saddle-node bifurcation, eq. (7.20), with the scale of $x$ changed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={4}>

Case (c):

<Formula label="Case (c)" tex="\dot x = x + \tanh(rx)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 5" />

Give $r_c$, the type, and (our addition) the coefficient $c$ of $x^3$ in the expansion $\dot x = (1 + r)x + cx^3 + \ldots$ about $x = 0$, typed with `r`.

<Hint>

$x = 0$ is always a fixed point. When does it change stability? Then expand $\tanh u = u - u^3/3 + \ldots$ to see what happens next to it.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Near the critical value the sign of $c$ decides between the two kinds of pitchfork: compare with $rx - x^3$ and $rx + x^3$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5c-rc" label="$r_c =$" value="-1" tolerance="0.01" calc="p5c-rc" />

<Answer id="p5c-type" label="Type:" choices={["saddle-node: two fixed points meet and vanish", "Hopf: a limit cycle is born", "transcritical: two fixed points cross and swap stability", "subcritical pitchfork: two unstable points close in on a stable one", "supercritical pitchfork: one stable point splits into two stable points and an unstable one"]} answer={3} />

<Answer id="p5c-c" label="$c =$" expr="-r^3/3"
  vars={{ r: [-2, -0.5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "r^3/3", hint: "Check the sign: tanh u = u − u³/3 + …, and with u = rx the cubic term is −r³x³/3." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** The flow is odd in $x$ ($x \to -x$ changes the sign of $\dot x$), so fixed points other than $0$ come in pairs: a pitchfork. Expand to third order to tell which kind.

<Step title="The origin">

$f'(0) = 1 + r$: $x = 0$ is stable for $r < -1$ and unstable for $r > -1$, so $r_c = -1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Next to the origin">

With $\tanh(rx) = rx - r^3x^3/3 + \ldots$:

<Formula label="Problem 5(c): the expansion" tex="\dot x = (1 + r)\,x - \frac{r^3}{3}\,x^3 + \ldots" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(c), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

Near $r = -1$, $c = -r^3/3 \approx +1/3 > 0$: the cubic term pushes away from the origin, as in $rx + x^3$. The other fixed points, $x^2 \approx 3(1 + r)/r^3$, exist for $r < -1$ (where $1 + r < 0$ and $r^3 < 0$), on the side where the origin is stable, and they are unstable: a subcritical pitchfork.

</Step>

<Step title="The pictures">

For $r < -1$ the curve $\dot x(x)$ crosses zero three times: unstable, stable at $0$, unstable (for $r = -2$, at $x = \pm 0.96$). For $r \ge -1$ it crosses only at $0$, now unstable, and every other $x$ runs away to $\pm\infty$. The bifurcation diagram has the line $x = 0$ solid for $r < -1$ and dashed for $r > -1$, and two dashed branches that open to the left from $(-1, 0)$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For large $|x|$ the term $x$ wins over $\tanh$, which stays between $-1$ and $1$, so trajectories run off to infinity when the origin is unstable: the jump typical of a subcritical pitchfork. For $r = -1.2$ a numerical root gives $x^\star = 0.659$, where $f' = 1 + r\,\text{sech}^2(rx^\star) = 0.32 > 0$, unstable as claimed.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
