[Section 5.1](/courses/8-09/perturbation-theory-hamilton-jacobi) set up perturbation theory with the Hamilton-Jacobi equation and tried it on a problem whose answer was known anyway. Section 5.2 of Stewart's notes (pp. 95–98) turns to one whose answer is not elementary: a pendulum swinging through a finite angle. It also brings out the most useful idea of the chapter, the difference between **periodic** and **secular** effects of a perturbation. The unperturbed variables are the action-angle variables of the harmonic oscillator, from Chapter 4 of the notes (Section 4.6 there). (The source is the course's typed lecture notes, without videos; this page follows them in order, in its own words, with its own worked example and its own graph; the notes' two figures are not reproduced.)

## The pendulum as a perturbed oscillator

A mass $m$ on a rod of length $a$ has the Hamiltonian (p. 95)

<Formula label="Pendulum Hamiltonian" tex="H = \frac{p^2}{2ma^2} - mga\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.2, eq. (5.19), p. 95" />

where $p$ is the momentum conjugate to the angle $\theta$. For small swings, $\theta \ll 1$, expand the cosine (p. 95):

<Formula label="Expanded pendulum Hamiltonian" tex="H = -mga + \frac{p^2}{2ma^2} + \frac{mga}{2}\,\theta^2 + \frac{mga}{2}\,\theta^2\Big(-\frac{\theta^2}{12} + \frac{\theta^4}{360} + \ldots\Big)" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.2, eq. (5.20), p. 95" />

The constant $-mga$ does not affect the motion. What is left splits naturally: the first two terms are a harmonic oscillator, and the rest is the correction. To first order only its leading term matters, so (p. 96)

<Formula label="Unperturbed part and perturbation" tex="H_0 = \frac{p^2}{2I} + \frac{mga}{2}\,\theta^2, \qquad \Delta H = -\frac{mga}{24}\,\theta^4, \qquad I = ma^2, \quad \Omega^2 = \frac{g}{a}" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.2, p. 96" />

$H_0$ is an oscillator with moment of inertia $I$ and angular frequency $\Omega$. (The notes write $\Omega$ rather than $\omega$ here, because $\omega$ will be the angle variable.) The terms of order $\theta^6$ would only matter at second order.

## Action-angle variables of the oscillator

For $H_0$ the action-angle variables are the action $J$ and the angle variable $w$ (written $\omega$ in the notes), with (p. 96)

<Formula label="Action-angle variables of the oscillator" tex="\alpha = H_0 = \frac{\Omega}{2\pi}\,J, \qquad w = \nu t + \beta, \qquad \nu = \frac{\Omega}{2\pi}" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.2, eq. (5.21), p. 96" />

so $\nu$ is the frequency in cycles per unit time and $w$ counts cycles. In these variables the oscillator's motion is (p. 96)

<Formula label="Angle and momentum in action-angle form" tex="\theta = \sqrt{\frac{J}{\pi I \Omega}}\,\sin\big[2\pi(\nu t + \beta)\big], \qquad p = \sqrt{\frac{I J \Omega}{\pi}}\,\cos\big[2\pi(\nu t + \beta)\big]" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.2, eq. (5.22), p. 96" />

(The notes first write these as $\sqrt{2\alpha/I\omega^2}\,\sin(\omega t + \delta)$ and $\sqrt{2I\alpha}\cos(\omega t + \delta)$, where $\omega$ and $\delta$ stand for the oscillator frequency $\Omega$ and the phase $2\pi\beta$; the forms above are the ones used afterwards.) Since $w$ and $\beta$ differ only by $\nu t$, the pair $(J, \beta)$ serves as the new canonical variables. Notice that $J$ sets the size of the swing and $\beta$ its timing.

## The perturbation in the new variables

Substituting $\theta$ into $\Delta H$ and using $mga = I\Omega^2$ gives (p. 96)

<Formula label="Perturbation in action-angle variables" tex="\Delta H = -\frac{mga}{24}\,\theta^4 = -\frac{J^2}{24\pi^2 I}\,\sin^4\!\big(2\pi(\nu t + \beta)\big)" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.2, eq. (5.23), p. 96" />

With starting values $J^{(0)} = J_0$ and $\beta^{(0)} = \beta_0$, the first-order equations of Section 5.1 are (p. 96)

<Formula label="First-order rates" tex="\dot\beta^{(1)} = \Big(\frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial J}\Big)_{0} = -\frac{J_0}{12\pi^2 I}\,\sin^4\!\big(2\pi(\nu t + \beta_0)\big), \qquad \dot J^{(1)} = -\Big(\frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial \beta}\Big)_{0} = \frac{J_0^2}{3\pi I}\,\sin^3\!\big(2\pi(\nu t + \beta_0)\big)\cos\!\big(2\pi(\nu t + \beta_0)\big)" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.2, eq. (5.24), p. 96" />

These can be integrated in closed form. But before doing so, the notes step back to a question that can often be answered without the full solution.

## Periodic and secular changes

When $H_0$ has periodic orbits with frequency $\nu$, a natural question is what the perturbation does over one period $T = 1/\nu$, or over many (pp. 96–97). There are two possibilities:

- the change of a variable can itself be **periodic**: after each period it comes back to where it started, and the perturbed orbit stays close to the unperturbed one;
- or each period can leave a net increment, a **secular** change. These add up, and after many periods the variable is far from its unperturbed value.

To tell which, average the first-order rate over one period (p. 97):

<Formula label="Average rate over one period" tex="\overline{\dot J^{(1)}} = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \dot J^{(1)}(t)\, dt = \frac{J^{(1)}(T) - J^{(1)}(0)}{T}" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.2, eq. (5.25), p. 97" />

which is the net change per period divided by the period. A zero average means periodic, a nonzero one secular.

## The action wobbles

For the pendulum, $\dot J^{(1)}$ is proportional to $\sin^3x\cos x$, the derivative of $\tfrac14\sin^4x$, so its integral over a full period vanishes and $\overline{\dot J^{(1)}} = 0$: the change of $J$ is periodic. Indeed the integral of eq. (5.24) is (p. 97)

<Formula label="Action to first order" tex="J^{(1)}(t) = J_0 + \frac{J_0^2}{24\pi^2 I\nu}\,\sin^4\!\big(2\pi(\nu t + \beta_0)\big)" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.2, eq. (5.26), p. 97" />

(As printed this equals $J_0$ at $t = 0$ only when $\sin(2\pi\beta_0) = 0$; for a general starting phase subtract the constant $\sin^4(2\pi\beta_0)$ inside the bracket. The notes take $\beta_0 = 0$ for their figure.) Since $J$ fixes the size of the swing, the orbit in the $(\theta, p)$ plane is slightly reshaped, but it closes after each period. In the notes' phase-space picture the perturbed orbit bulges out beyond the unperturbed ellipse along the $\theta$ axis. Using $J_0 = \pi I\Omega\theta_0^2$ for a swing of amplitude $\theta_0$, the size of the wobble is $J_0/(24\pi^2I\nu) = \theta_0^2/12$ of $J_0$.

## The phase drifts: a lower frequency

$\dot\beta^{(1)}$ is proportional to $-\sin^4x$, which is never positive, so it cannot average to zero. With the average of $\sin^4$ over a period equal to $3/8$ (p. 97):

<Formula label="Average drift of the phase" tex="\overline{\dot\beta^{(1)}} = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \dot\beta^{(1)}(t)\, dt = \frac{\beta^{(1)}(T) - \beta^{(1)}(0)}{T} = -\frac{J_0}{32\pi^2 I}" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.2, eq. (5.27), p. 97" />

This is a secular change. After many periods, $\beta^{(1)}(t) \approx \overline{\dot\beta^{(1)}}\,t + \beta_0$, plus terms that repeat every period (p. 98). Since the motion depends on time only through $\nu t + \beta$, a $\beta$ that falls behind at a steady rate is the same as a slower clock: on average the first-order effect is a new frequency $\nu' = \nu + \overline{\dot\beta^{(1)}}$.

The graph shows both effects for a pendulum released through the bottom at $t = 0$ ($\beta_0 = 0$), from the integrated forms of eq. (5.24):

    { expr: "-x/16", label: "average drift", dash: true }

  caption="First-order changes of the pendulum's action-angle variables, divided by θ₀², computed from the integrated rates of eq. (5.24) with β₀ = 0. The relative change of the action J returns to zero every period; the phase β falls behind by θ₀²/16 cycles per period on average (dashed line), which is a lower frequency." />

In Chapter 4 the notes computed the pendulum's exact frequency $\nu_{\text{full}}(E)$ numerically as a function of the energy (their Fig. 5.2 repeats it). The first-order result is the start of that curve: with $J_0 = H_0/\nu = (E + mga)/\nu$, where $E + mga$ is the energy above the bottom of the well (p. 98),

<Formula label="Frequency shift against energy" tex="\nu' - \nu = \overline{\dot\beta^{(1)}} = -\frac{E + mga}{32\pi^2 m a^2 \nu}" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.2, eq. (5.28), p. 98" />

This is the straight-line drop of $\nu_{\text{full}}$ just above $E = -mga$: a pendulum slows down as it swings wider.

## A worked example

<WorkedExample title="A pendulum swinging to 23 degrees">
<Problem>
A pendulum of length $a = 0.80$ m swings with amplitude $\theta_0 = 0.40$ rad (about 23°), with $g = 9.81$ m/s². Find the small-swing frequency and period, the first-order frequency shift, and the corrected period. Compare with the exact period, $T_{\text{exact}} = (4/\Omega)\,K(k)$ with $k = \sin(\theta_0/2)$ and $K(k) = \int_0^{\pi/2} d\phi/\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2\phi}$. After how many full periods (swings there and back) has the real pendulum fallen half a period behind the small-swing one?
</Problem>
<Step title="Small swings">
$\Omega = \sqrt{g/a} = \sqrt{12.26}$ s⁻¹ $\approx 3.502$ rad/s, so $\nu = \Omega/2\pi \approx 0.5573$ Hz and $T = 1/\nu \approx 1.794$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="First-order shift">
At the turning point all the energy of $H_0$ is potential, so to first order $E + mga = \tfrac12 mga\theta_0^2$. Putting this into eq. (5.28) and using $g/a = 4\pi^2\nu^2$ gives $\nu' - \nu = -\nu\theta_0^2/16$. With $\theta_0^2 = 0.16$ the frequency is 1.0% lower, $\nu' \approx 0.5518$ Hz, and the period is about 1.0% longer, $T' \approx 1.812$ s.
</Step>
<Step title="Exact period">
$k = \sin 0.20 \approx 0.1987$, and the integral gives $K(k) \approx 1.5866$, so $T_{\text{exact}}/T = 2K/\pi \approx 1.0101$: the period is 1.01% longer. First-order perturbation theory, $1/(1 - 0.01) \approx 1.0101$, agrees to about one part in $10^5$; the difference is a second-order effect.
</Step>
<Step title="The secular effect">
Each period the real pendulum falls behind by $\theta_0^2/16 = 0.01$ of a cycle. Half a cycle behind takes about $0.5/0.01 = 50$ full periods, roughly 90 s. However small the perturbation, a secular effect eventually becomes large; the periodic wobble of $J$, about $\theta_0^2/12$, about 1.3%, never grows.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- The pendulum $H = p^2/2ma^2 - mga\cos\theta$ is, near the bottom, a harmonic oscillator $H_0 = p^2/2I + \tfrac12 mga\theta^2$ ($I = ma^2$, $\Omega^2 = g/a$) plus $\Delta H = -mga\theta^4/24$.
- In the oscillator's action-angle variables, $\theta = \sqrt{J/\pi I\Omega}\sin[2\pi(\nu t + \beta)]$ and $\Delta H = -(J^2/24\pi^2I)\sin^4[2\pi(\nu t + \beta)]$; first-order theory gives $\dot J^{(1)} \propto \sin^3\cos$ and $\dot\beta^{(1)} \propto -\sin^4$.
- A change is periodic if its rate averages to zero over a period of $H_0$ and secular if it does not. The action $J$ only wobbles (by up to $\theta_0^2/12$); the phase $\beta$ drifts at the average rate $-J_0/(32\pi^2I)$.
- A steadily drifting phase is a shifted frequency: $\nu' - \nu = -(E + mga)/(32\pi^2ma^2\nu)$, or $-\nu\theta_0^2/16$ for amplitude $\theta_0$, the initial slope of the exact $\nu_{\text{full}}(E)$.
- For $\theta_0 = 0.40$ rad the period grows by 1.0%, in agreement with the exact elliptic integral; after about 50 full periods the pendulum is half a period behind a small-swing one.
