In two dimensions a trajectory has one more option besides settling on a fixed point or running away: it can close on itself. The [previous section](/courses/8-09/fixed-points-two-dimensions) classified fixed points; this one studies isolated closed orbits, proves when they must exist, and shows how they appear and disappear as a parameter changes. The idea of a limit cycle and Bendixson's test for ruling them out are on the 18.03 page on [limit cycles](/courses/18-03/limit-cycles). (Citations give the printed page numbers 173 to 182 of the Chapter 7 notes.)

## Limit cycles

A **limit cycle** is an isolated closed trajectory, a new kind of attractor in two dimensions (p. 173). If nearby trajectories converge onto it, it is **stable**; if they move away, **unstable**; and if they approach from one side but leave on the other, **semi-stable** (pp. 173–174). It differs from the orbits around a centre: those come in a continuous family, each surrounded by others, while a limit cycle has no closed neighbours (p. 174).

**A first example** (p. 174). In polar coordinates $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$,

<Formula label="A system with a limit cycle" tex="\dot r = r(1 - r^2), \qquad \dot\theta = 1, \qquad r \ge 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 174, eq. (7.57)" />

The angle turns uniformly, so only the radial equation matters for stability, and it is a one-dimensional flow: $\dot r > 0$ for $0 < r < 1$ and $\dot r < 0$ for $r > 1$. The circle $r = 1$ is a stable limit cycle (p. 174). The radial equation can be solved exactly (our step): $r(t) = \bigl[1 + (r_0^{-2} - 1)e^{-2t}\bigr]^{-1/2}$.

**The van der Pol oscillator** (p. 175). The notes' second example, first studied for electric circuits in the 1920s (and found to give irregular "noise" when driven), is

<Formula label="Van der Pol oscillator" tex="\ddot x + \mu(x^2 - 1)\dot x + x = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dot x = \omega, \quad \dot\omega = \mu(1 - x^2)\omega - x" cite="reading1" at="p. 175, eq. (7.58)" />

with $\mu > 0$. The damping coefficient $\mu(x^2 - 1)$ depends on the amplitude: positive for $|x| > 1$, so large oscillations are damped, and negative for $|x| < 1$, so small ones grow. In between, the motion settles on a limit cycle, nearly a circle for small $\mu$ (the notes show $\mu = 0.1$) and a strongly distorted loop, slow phases alternating with fast jumps, for larger $\mu$ (the notes show $\mu = 2$) (p. 175).

## The Poincaré–Bendixson theorem

There are several tests for ruling limit cycles out; the notes concentrate on a way to prove that one exists (p. 175).

**Theorem** (p. 175). Let $\dot x = f_x(x, y)$, $\dot y = f_y(x, y)$ with $\vec f$ continuous and differentiable, and let $\mathbb D$ be a closed, bounded region. If a trajectory $C$ stays inside $\mathbb D$ for all $t \ge 0$, then as $t \to \infty$ it approaches either a fixed point or a limit cycle.

The proof needs topology and is not given. The use is this: if a closed, bounded region contains no fixed points and on its boundary the flow points inward, trapping trajectories inside, then it must contain a limit cycle (p. 175). The intuition is that a trajectory that can neither escape nor cross its own path has nowhere new left to go. The striking consequence is that **there is no chaos in two dimensions**; with three or more variables a trajectory can avoid itself indefinitely, and chaos becomes possible (pp. 175–176). This is the reason, promised in Section 7.1, for the condition $n \ge 3$.

**Example** (p. 176). Does this system have a limit cycle?

<Formula label="A system with a trapping annulus" tex="\dot x = x - y - x(x^2 + 5y^2), \qquad \dot y = x + y - y(x^2 + y^2)" cite="reading1" at="p. 176, eq. (7.59)" />

In polar coordinates, using $r\dot r = x\dot x + y\dot y$ and $4x^2y^2 = r^4\sin^2 2\theta$ (p. 176):

<Formula label="Radial equation of the example" tex="r\dot r = x\dot x + y\dot y \quad\Rightarrow\quad r\dot r = r^2\bigl(1 - r^2 - r^2\sin^2(2\theta)\bigr)" cite="reading1" at="p. 176, eq. (7.60)" />

Since $0 \le \sin^2 2\theta \le 1$, the bracket is positive for every $\theta$ when $r < 1/\sqrt2$ and negative when $r > 1$. Trajectories cross the circle $r = 1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.71$ outward and the circle $r = 1$ inward, so the annulus between them traps them; it contains no fixed points (the only one is the origin), so it contains a limit cycle (p. 176).

## Bifurcations of fixed points in two dimensions

Adding a parameter to a two-dimensional system, fixed points can again appear, vanish or change stability, and now limit cycles can also come and go (p. 176). The saddle-node, transcritical and pitchfork bifurcations of [Section 7.2](/courses/8-09/one-dimensional-bifurcations) still occur. The simplest example is two uncoupled equations (p. 176)

<Formula label="Saddle-node in two dimensions" tex="\dot x = \mu - x^2, \qquad \dot y = -y" cite="reading1" at="p. 176, eq. (7.61)" />

with a stable node at $(\sqrt\mu, 0)$ and a saddle at $(-\sqrt\mu, 0)$ for $\mu > 0$, which merge at $\mu = 0$ and are gone for $\mu < 0$ (pp. 176–177).

Why the one-dimensional catalogue carries over (p. 177): fixed points are the crossings of the two curves $f_x(x, y) = 0$ and $f_y(x, y) = 0$. As a parameter varies, the curves deform and their crossings slide along them into one another, a motion along a line, which is one-dimensional. So saddle-nodes, transcritical bifurcations and both kinds of pitchfork appear in higher dimensions unchanged (p. 177).

**A pitchfork in two dimensions** (p. 177). Consider

<Formula label="A symmetric 2D system" tex="\dot x = \mu x + y + \sin x, \qquad \dot y = x - y" cite="reading1" at="p. 177, eq. (7.62)" />

which is unchanged by $(x, y) \to (-x, -y)$, so the origin is always a fixed point. Its linearization $\begin{pmatrix} \mu + 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ has $\tau = \mu$ and $\Delta = -(\mu + 2)$: a stable node for $\mu < -2$ and a saddle for $\mu > -2$ (p. 177). The symmetry suggests a pitchfork near $\mu = -2$. Other fixed points need $y = x$; then, expanding $\sin x$ (p. 177),

<Formula label="Fixed points near the pitchfork" tex="\dot x = (\mu + 1)x + x - \frac{x^3}{6} + \ldots = 0" cite="reading1" at="p. 177, eq. (7.63)" />

Near $\mu = -2$ the linear coefficient $\mu + 2$ is small, so the cubic term matters while higher ones do not, and $x_* = y_* = \pm\sqrt{6(\mu + 2)}$ for $\mu > -2$: a supercritical pitchfork (p. 177). In terms of eigenvalues, the bifurcation is where $\Delta = \lambda_+\lambda_- = 0$, that is where one real eigenvalue, $\lambda_+$, crosses zero (p. 177).

## Hopf bifurcations

A **Hopf bifurcation** happens when a spiral fixed point changes stability as a parameter varies, and a limit cycle is created or destroyed with it (pp. 177–178). It is the analogue of a pitchfork with a cycle in place of the two prongs, and it comes in supercritical and subcritical versions. The eigenvalues behave differently from a pitchfork: a complex pair $\alpha \pm i\omega$ moves across the imaginary axis, so the real parts of both eigenvalues change sign together (p. 178).

**Supercritical Hopf** (p. 178). In polar coordinates,

<Formula label="Supercritical Hopf normal form" tex="\dot r = \mu r - r^3, \qquad \dot\theta = \omega + br^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 178, eq. (7.64)" />

For $\mu < 0$ the origin is a stable spiral and there is no cycle. For $\mu > 0$ the origin is an unstable spiral and the circle $r = \sqrt\mu$ is a stable limit cycle: $\mu = 0$ is a supercritical Hopf bifurcation (p. 178). Near the origin, $\dot x \approx \mu x - \omega y$ and $\dot y \approx \omega x + \mu y$, with eigenvalues $\lambda_\pm = \mu \pm i\omega$, which cross the imaginary axis at $\mu = 0$ (p. 178). The cycle grows from zero radius as $\sqrt\mu$, just as the prongs of a supercritical pitchfork.

**Subcritical Hopf** (p. 179). With a destabilizing cubic and a stabilizing fifth power,

<Formula label="Subcritical Hopf normal form" tex="\dot r = \mu r + r^3 - r^5, \qquad \dot\theta = \omega + br^2" cite="reading1" at="p. 179, eq. (7.65)" />

has a subcritical Hopf bifurcation at $\mu = 0$ (p. 179). The radial equation is the hysteresis example of Section 7.2, so its cycles are at $r^2 = \frac12(1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 4\mu})$ (our solution). Slightly below $\mu = 0$ the origin is a stable spiral surrounded by an unstable cycle, which in turn lies inside a large stable cycle: starting points inside the unstable cycle spiral into the origin, those outside go to the large cycle (p. 179). Above $\mu = 0$ the unstable cycle has shrunk onto the origin and gone, and every trajectory goes to the large cycle (p. 179). (Going back down, the large cycle survives until $\mu = -\frac14$, where it meets the unstable one and both vanish; this is our addition.)

<WorkedExample title="Cycles of the subcritical Hopf system">
<Problem>
For $\dot r = \mu r + r^3 - r^5$ at $\mu = -0.1$, find the limit cycles and their stability.
</Problem>
<Step title="The radii">
Cycles are zeros of $\mu + r^2 - r^4$: $r^2 = \frac12\bigl(1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 0.4}\bigr) = \frac12(1 \pm 0.775)$, so $r = 0.336$ and $r = 0.942$.
</Step>
<Step title="Stability">
$\dot r/r = -0.1 + r^2 - r^4$ is $-0.1$ at small $r$, positive between the two radii (for example $+0.13$ at $r = 0.6$) and negative beyond. So the origin is stable, the inner cycle at $0.336$ is unstable (it separates the two basins), and the outer cycle at $0.942$ is stable.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## The pendulum with constant torque

A physical system with a limit cycle is the damped pendulum driven by a constant torque, now with inertia kept (pp. 179–180):

<Formula label="Damped pendulum with constant torque" tex="\ddot\theta + \frac{1}{q}\dot\theta + \sin\theta = \tau \quad\Rightarrow\quad \dot\theta = \omega, \quad \dot\omega = -\frac{1}{q}\omega - \sin\theta + \tau" cite="reading1" at="p. 180, eq. (7.66)" />

**Small torque.** For $\tau \le 1$ the fixed points are $\omega_* = 0$ with $\sin\theta_* = \tau$, two solutions in each turn (where the horizontal line $\tau$ meets the curve $\sin\theta$); one is stable and the other a saddle (p. 180). The overdamped version was the bottleneck example of Section 7.2 (the notes' footnote 2 relates the constants; it points to eq. 7.35, but the torque example there is eq. 7.36).

<WorkedExample title="Fixed points at τ = 0.6">
<Problem>
Find and classify the fixed points of (7.66) for $\tau = 0.6$ and $q = 0.5$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Positions">
$\sin\theta_* = 0.6$: $\theta_* = 0.644$ and $\pi - 0.644 = 2.498$.
</Step>
<Step title="Types">
The Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -\cos\theta_* & -1/q \end{pmatrix}$, so $\Delta = \cos\theta_*$ and the trace is $-1/q = -2$. At $\theta_* = 0.644$, $\cos\theta_* = 0.8$: $\Delta = 0.8 > 0$ and $\tau_M^2 - 4\Delta = 4 - 3.2 = 0.8 > 0$ (writing $\tau_M$ for the trace to keep $\tau$ for the torque), a stable node. At $\theta_* = 2.498$, $\cos\theta_* = -0.8$: $\Delta < 0$, a saddle.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

**Large torque** (pp. 180–181). For $\tau > 1$ there are no fixed points, and there is a unique stable limit cycle. Write the second equation as (p. 180)

<Formula label="Angular acceleration of the torqued pendulum" tex="\dot\omega = -\frac{1}{q}\Bigl[\omega - q(\tau - \sin\theta)\Bigr]" cite="reading1" at="p. 180, eq. (7.67)" />

For very negative $\omega$, $\dot\omega > 0$; for very positive $\omega$, $\dot\omega < 0$. So a band of $\omega$ values traps the motion, with no fixed points inside, and the notes invoke Poincaré–Bendixson to conclude there is a limit cycle (p. 180). (Since $\theta$ is an angle, the phase space is a cylinder and the cycle winds around it rather than enclosing a region of the plane; the conclusion holds, but the strict planar theorem needs this adaptation.) The cycle is the pendulum rotating steadily over the top (p. 181). From the same starting point, a trajectory with $\tau < 1$ comes to rest at the stable fixed point, while one with $\tau > 1$ keeps going round (p. 181).

**Both at once** (pp. 181–182). For weak enough damping the rotating cycle also exists for a range $\tau_c < \tau < 1$, where the fixed points exist too, so the final state depends on the initial conditions; $\tau_c$ depends on $q$ and approaches 1 as the damping grows. The notes' sketch puts the threshold at $q = 1$ and says that below it the cycle exists only for $\tau \ge 1$ (pp. 181–182); this is approximate, since a numerical solution shows the two already coexisting slightly below $q = 1$ (for instance at $q = 0.95$, $\tau = 0.99$), so the meeting point of the three boundaries lies somewhat below $q = 1$. The boundaries of these regions in the $q$–$\tau$ plane are three kinds of bifurcation (p. 182):

- along $\tau = 1$ for small $q$, the saddle and the node are born on the cycle itself, which disappears: an **infinite-period bifurcation** (the period of the rotation diverges, as for the bottleneck);
- along $\tau = 1$ for large $q$, a saddle-node bifurcation creates the two fixed points while the cycle survives (the notes write that "the saddle persists", evidently meaning the cycle);
- along the curve $\tau = \tau_c(q)$, the saddle collides with the cycle and destroys it: a **homoclinic bifurcation**.

The notes do not attempt a full catalogue of bifurcations involving cycles; this example shows several kinds at once (p. 182).

## Summary

- A **limit cycle** is an isolated closed trajectory (stable, unstable or semi-stable), unlike the family of closed orbits around a centre. Examples: $\dot r = r(1 - r^2)$, $\dot\theta = 1$, and the van der Pol oscillator.
- **Poincaré–Bendixson**: in two dimensions a trajectory confined to a closed, bounded region approaches a fixed point or a limit cycle. A trapping region with no fixed points contains a limit cycle, as for the annulus $1/\sqrt2 < r < 1$ of eq. (7.59). There is no chaos in two dimensions.
- The one-dimensional bifurcations of fixed points carry over to higher dimensions, because the fixed points slide into one another along a line; e.g. the symmetric system (7.62) has a supercritical pitchfork at $\mu = -2$.
- In a **Hopf bifurcation** a complex pair of eigenvalues crosses the imaginary axis: a spiral changes stability and a limit cycle is born (supercritical, $\dot r = \mu r - r^3$, radius $\sqrt\mu$) or an unstable cycle shrinks onto the fixed point (subcritical, $\dot r = \mu r + r^3 - r^5$), with a jump to a large cycle.
- The damped pendulum with constant torque has a stable fixed point for $\tau < 1$ and a rotating limit cycle for $\tau > 1$; for weak damping both coexist, bounded by infinite-period, saddle-node and homoclinic bifurcations.
