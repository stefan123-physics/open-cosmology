Section 1.3 of Stewart's notes (pp. 12–16) collects the conservation laws that come from symmetries. It is short on new formalism and long on examples, and this page follows them in order, in its own words, adding its own worked example and graph.

## Cyclic coordinates

A coordinate that does not appear in the Lagrangian is called **cyclic** (notes p. 12). Since $H = \dot q_i p_i - L$, a coordinate missing from $L$ is also missing from $H$. Its absence is a **symmetry**: shifting that coordinate leaves the dynamics unchanged.

In the form used in the notes, **Noether's theorem** says that a symmetry gives a cyclic coordinate, and a cyclic coordinate gives a conservation law: the canonical momentum $p_j$ that goes with it stays constant. The proof is one line. If $\partial L/\partial q_j = 0$, the Euler–Lagrange equation reads

<Formula label="A cyclic coordinate conserves its momentum" tex="\dot p_j = \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 12" />

or, even more directly, $\partial H/\partial q_j = 0$ is the same as $\dot p_j = 0$ by Hamilton's equations.

## Translations: total momentum

Take $N$ particles with total mass $M = \sum_i m_i$ and centre of mass

<Formula label="Centre of mass" tex="\vec R = \frac{1}{M}\sum_i m_i\vec r_i" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 12" />

and suppose no force, external or internal, acts on $\vec R$ (notes p. 12):

<Formula label="No force on the centre of mass" tex="\vec F_R = -\nabla_R V = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, eq. (1.48), p. 12" />

so $V$ does not depend on $\vec R$. To see that $T$ does not either, measure positions from the centre of mass, $\vec r_i' \equiv \vec r_i - \vec R$. Then

<Formula label="Kinetic energy split" tex="T = \frac12\sum_i m_i\dot{\vec r}_i^{\,2} = \frac12 M\dot{\vec R}^2 + \frac12\sum_i m_i\dot{\vec r}_i'^{\,2}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, eq. (1.49), p. 12" />

The cross term, $\dot{\vec R}\cdot\frac{d}{dt}\sum_i m_i\vec r_i'$, vanishes because $\sum_i m_i\vec r_i' = 0$ by the definition of $\vec R$. The kinetic energy splits into the motion of the centre of mass and the motion relative to it. Neither part contains $\vec R$ itself, so $\vec R$ is cyclic and the total momentum $\vec P = M\dot{\vec R}$ is conserved. For a continuous body with mass density $\rho(\vec r)$, used later for rigid bodies, the sums become integrals: $M = \int d^3r\,\rho(\vec r)$ and $\vec R = \frac1M\int d^3r\,\rho(\vec r)\,\vec r$.

In Newtonian terms the condition $\dot{\vec P} = 0$ needs two things (notes p. 13): no net external force, $\vec F^{\text{ext}} = \sum_i \vec F_i^{\text{ext}} = 0$, and internal forces obeying Newton's third law, $\vec F_{i\to j} = -\vec F_{j\to i}$ (the notes write the same with arrows; on this page $\vec F_{i\to j}$ is the force of $i$ on $j$), so that the internal ones cancel in pairs:

<Formula label="Newton's version" tex="M\ddot{\vec R} = \sum_i \vec F_i^{\text{ext}} + \sum_{i,j}\vec F_{i\to j} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, eq. (1.50), p. 13" />

## Rotations: angular momentum

Now let the system be unchanged by rotations through an angle $\phi$ about some axis $\hat n$ (notes p. 13). Choose $\phi$ as one of the generalized coordinates; $L$ does not contain it, so $p_\phi = \partial L/\partial\dot\phi$ is constant. If $V$ does not depend on $\dot\phi$, only $T$ contributes, and using $\partial\dot{\vec r}_i/\partial\dot\phi = \partial\vec r_i/\partial\phi$ (the "cancellation of dots" from the [Lagrangian page](/courses/8-09/lagrangian-mechanics-review)),

<Formula label="Momentum conjugate to a rotation angle" tex="p_\phi = \frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot\phi} = \sum_i m_i\dot{\vec r}_i\cdot\frac{\partial\dot{\vec r}_i}{\partial\dot\phi} = \sum_i m_i\vec v_i\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_i}{\partial\phi}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, eq. (1.51), p. 13" />

Turning the system by a small angle $d\phi$ moves each position by $d\phi\,\hat n\times\vec r_i$, so $\partial\vec r_i/\partial\phi = \hat n\times\vec r_i$. (The notes refer here to "Fig. 2.2"; the figure meant is their Fig. 1.5, on the same page.) Rearranging the triple product,

<Formula label="It is the angular momentum about the axis" tex="p_\phi = \sum_i m_i\vec v_i\cdot(\hat n\times\vec r_i) = \hat n\cdot\sum_i\vec r_i\times m_i\vec v_i = \hat n\cdot\vec L_{\text{total}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, eq. (1.52), p. 13" />

In Newtonian terms, the angular momentum about the centre of mass is conserved under two conditions: no external torque, $\vec\tau^{\text{ext}} = \sum_i\vec r_i\times\vec F_i^{\text{ext}} = 0$, and **central** internal forces. If the potential between two particles depends only on their distance, $V_{ij} = V_{ij}(r_{ij})$ with $\vec r_{ij} \equiv \vec r_i - \vec r_j$, then the force $\vec F_{j\to i} = -\nabla_i V_{ij}$ (no sum) points along $\vec r_{ij}$. Then

<Formula label="Rate of change of the angular momentum" tex="\frac{d\vec L}{dt} = \sum_i\vec r_i\times\dot{\vec p}_i = \sum_i\vec r_i\times\vec F_i^{\text{ext}} + \sum_{i,j}\vec r_i\times\vec F_{j\to i}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, eq. (1.53), p. 13" />

The first sum is the external torque, zero. In the second, pairing the terms $(i, j)$ and $(j, i)$ gives $\vec r_{ij}\times\vec F_{j\to i}$, a cross product of parallel vectors:

<Formula label="Central forces exert no net torque" tex="\frac{d\vec L}{dt} = \sum_{i<j}\vec r_{ij}\times\vec F_{j\to i} = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, eq. (1.54), p. 14" />

## Scaling: mechanical similarity

A less obvious symmetry is a change of scale (notes p. 14). Suppose the potential is **homogeneous of degree $k$**: stretching every position, $\vec r_i \to \lambda\vec r_i$, multiplies it by $\lambda^k$. The kinetic energy is quadratic in the velocities. If we also stretch time,

<Formula label="Scaling of time and velocity" tex="t \to \lambda^{1 - k/2}\,t, \qquad \dot{\vec r}_i \to \lambda^{k/2}\,\dot{\vec r}_i" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 14" />

then $T \to \lambda^k T$ as well, and the whole Lagrangian is multiplied by $\lambda^k$. A constant factor drops out of the Euler–Lagrange equations, so every motion, stretched in space by $\lambda$ and in time by $\lambda^{1-k/2}$, is again a motion. Only the scale changes, not the relation between positions and times. Three cases:

- **Harmonic oscillator, $k = 2$.** The time factor is $\lambda^0 = 1$: an oscillation twice as large takes exactly as long. The frequency does not depend on the amplitude.
- **Coulomb or Newtonian gravity, $k = -1$.** Times scale as $\lambda^{3/2}$, so for similar orbits the period obeys $T^2 \propto a^3$, with $a$ the semi-major axis: Kepler's third law.
- **Uniform gravity, $k = 1$.** Times scale as $\lambda^{1/2}$: the time to fall a height $h$ grows as $\sqrt h$.

## A charge in fields

For a charge in electromagnetic fields (notes p. 14),

<Formula label="Lagrangian of a charge" tex="L = \frac12 m\dot{\vec r}^2 - e\phi + e\vec A\cdot\dot{\vec r}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, p. 14" />

and the canonical momentum $\vec p = \partial L/\partial\dot{\vec r} = m\dot{\vec r} + e\vec A$ differs from the kinetic momentum $m\dot{\vec r}$. If $\phi$ and $\vec A$ do not depend on the Cartesian coordinate $x$, then $x$ is cyclic and $p_x = m\dot x + eA_x$ is what is conserved.

## Energy and the Hamiltonian

Finally, the time derivative of $H$ along a motion (notes p. 14) is $\dot H = \frac{\partial H}{\partial q}\dot q + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}\dot p + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}$. Hamilton's equations make the first two terms cancel, leaving

<Formula label="How H changes in time" tex="\dot H = \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, eq. (1.55), p. 14" />

Two separate statements follow, and they should not be confused:

- If $H$ (equivalently $L$) has no **explicit** time dependence, $H = \dot q_i p_i - L$ is conserved.
- The **energy** $E = T + V$ is conserved if $\dot E = 0$.

When $H = E$ the two coincide. Otherwise either can be true while the other fails. The notes show both failures with one system, a mass $m$ joined by a spring of constant $k$ to a cart that is driven along at constant speed $v_0$ (notes pp. 15–16). Let $x$ be the mass's position measured from a fixed wall, with the spring relaxed when $x = v_0 t$. Then

<Formula label="Lagrangian in the wall's coordinate" tex="L(x, \dot x) = \frac m2\dot x^2 - \frac k2\left(x - v_0 t\right)^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, eq. (1.56), p. 15" />

$T$ is quadratic in $\dot x$ and $V$ has no velocity in it, so the argument of the previous page goes through (an explicit $t$ in $V$ does not affect it): $\dot x p = 2T$ and

<Formula label="H equals E, but changes" tex="H = E = T + V = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac k2\left(x - v_0 t\right)^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, eq. (1.57), p. 15" />

But $\partial H/\partial t \neq 0$, so this energy is not conserved. Nothing paradoxical: whatever keeps the cart moving at constant speed does work on the system, and we have left it out.

Now use the displacement from the cart instead, $x' = x - v_0 t$. Then $\dot x = \dot x' + v_0$ and

<Formula label="Lagrangian in the cart's coordinate" tex="L'(x', \dot x') = \frac m2\dot x'^2 + mv_0\dot x' + \frac m2 v_0^2 - \frac k2 x'^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, eq. (1.58), p. 15" />

(The notes print the middle term as $mv_0x'$, without the dot; it comes from expanding $\frac m2(\dot x' + v_0)^2$.) The momentum is unchanged, $p' = m\dot x' + mv_0 = m\dot x = p$. This Lagrangian has the general form of the previous page with $a = mv_0$ and $L_0 = mv_0^2/2 - kx'^2/2$, so

<Formula label="H differs from E, but is conserved" tex="H'(x', p') = \dot x'p' - L' = \frac{1}{2m}\left(p' - mv_0\right)^2 + \frac k2 x'^2 - \frac m2 v_0^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.3, eq. (1.59), p. 15" />

The last term is a constant. The first two are the energy that a passenger on the cart would assign to the mass, not the energy above, so $H' \neq E$. But $H'$ has no explicit time dependence, so it is conserved.

<WorkedExample title="A bead on a spinning wire">
<Problem>
A bead of mass $50$ g slides without friction on a straight horizontal wire that a motor turns at a steady $\omega = 2.0$ rad/s about a vertical axis through one end. The bead starts at rest relative to the wire, $10$ cm from the axis. Follow $H$ and the kinetic energy $T$ for one second.
</Problem>
<Step title="Lagrangian and Hamiltonian">
With $r$ the distance along the wire, the bead's velocity has components $\dot r$ along the wire and $\omega r$ across it, so $L = T = \frac m2(\dot r^2 + \omega^2r^2)$. Then $p_r = m\dot r$ and $H = \dot r p_r - L = \frac m2(\dot r^2 - \omega^2r^2)$. There is no explicit $t$, so $H$ is conserved; but it is not $T$.
</Step>
<Step title="The motion">
The Euler–Lagrange equation is $\ddot r = \omega^2 r$, so with $\dot r(0) = 0$, $r = r_0\cosh\omega t$ and $\dot r = r_0\omega\sinh\omega t$. At $t = 1.0$ s, $\omega t = 2$: $r = 0.10 \times 3.762 = 0.376$ m and $\dot r = 0.10 \times 2.0 \times 3.627 = 0.725$ m/s.
</Step>
<Step title="Energy and H">
At the start $T = \frac m2\omega^2r_0^2 = 0.025 \times 4.0 \times 0.010 = 0.0010$ J and $H = -0.0010$ J. After one second $T = 0.025\,(0.526 + 0.566) = 0.0273$ J, more than 27 times larger, while $H = 0.025\,(0.526 - 0.566) = -0.0010$ J, unchanged. The motor supplies the extra energy through the sideways push of the wire, which is why $T$ is not conserved even though $H$ is.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- A cyclic coordinate (absent from $L$ and $H$) is a symmetry, and its canonical momentum is conserved.
- Translation invariance conserves the total momentum $\vec P = M\dot{\vec R}$; the kinetic energy splits into centre-of-mass and relative parts.
- Rotation invariance about $\hat n$ conserves $p_\phi = \hat n\cdot\vec L_{\text{total}}$; in Newtonian terms this needs no external torque and central internal forces.
- For $V$ homogeneous of degree $k$, scaling lengths by $\lambda$ and times by $\lambda^{1-k/2}$ maps motions to motions: amplitude-independent oscillator periods, $T^2 \propto a^3$, fall times $\propto\sqrt h$.
- A charge in $x$-independent fields conserves $p_x = m\dot x + eA_x$.
- $\dot H = \partial H/\partial t = -\partial L/\partial t$: $H$ is conserved without explicit time dependence, which is different from $E = T + V$ being conserved, as the spring-and-cart example shows in both directions.
