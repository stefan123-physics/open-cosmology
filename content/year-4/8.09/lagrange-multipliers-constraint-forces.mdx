The [previous page](/courses/8-09/calculus-of-variations-review) reviewed the calculus of variations. The rest of Section 1.5 of Stewart's notes (pp. 23–28) introduces **Lagrange multipliers**, the same idea as in [constrained optimisation](/courses/18-02/lagrange-multipliers) in 18.02, now applied to equations of motion, and ends Chapter 1 with three examples. This page follows the notes in order, in its own words, with its own worked example.

## Why multipliers

Choosing independent coordinates removes holonomic constraints, but it also hides the forces that maintain them. Multipliers help in two situations (notes p. 23), assuming for now that all forces come from a potential, $R_j = 0$:

1. when we want the forces of holonomic constraints, for instance to know when a constraint stops holding;
2. when the constraints are semi-holonomic, so no choice of coordinates removes them.

## The method

Take $n$ coordinates and $k$ constraints, labelled by $\alpha = 1, \dots, k$, each linear in the velocities (notes p. 24):

<Formula label="Constraints, holonomic or semi-holonomic" tex="g_\alpha(q, \dot q, t) = a_{j\alpha}(q, t)\,\dot q_j + a_{t\alpha}(q, t) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.85), p. 24" />

with repeated indices summed. Multiplying by $dt$, $a_{j\alpha}dq_j + a_{t\alpha}dt = 0$, and a virtual displacement has $dt = 0$, so

<Formula label="Constraints on virtual displacements" tex="\sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{j\alpha}\,\delta q_j = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.86), p. 24" />

These are $k$ conditions on the $\delta q_j$. Multiply each by a function $\lambda_\alpha(t)$, a **Lagrange multiplier**, and add them up: the sum is still zero, and adding zero to d'Alembert's principle changes nothing:

<Formula label="D'Alembert's principle with multipliers" tex="\left[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} - \lambda_\alpha a_{j\alpha}\right]\delta q_j = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.87), p. 24" />

summed over $j$ and $\alpha$. Summed over $j$ first, the multiplier term is zero for any $\lambda_\alpha$. Now look instead at each $j$ separately. Only $n - k$ of the $\delta q_j$ can be chosen freely, so for those the bracket must vanish. For the other $k$ values of $j$, choose the $k$ multipliers so that their brackets vanish too. Then every bracket is zero:

<Formula label="Euler–Lagrange equations with constraint forces" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} = \lambda_\alpha a_{j\alpha}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.88), p. 24" />

These are $n$ equations, one per $j$, each with a sum over $\alpha$ on the right. The right side is the **generalized force of constraint** $Q_j$. Together with the $k$ constraints $g_\alpha = 0$ there are $n + k$ equations for the $n + k$ unknowns $q_j$ and $\lambda_\alpha$.

**Holonomic constraints**, $f_\alpha(q, t) = 0$. Then $g_\alpha = \dot f_\alpha = \frac{\partial f_\alpha}{\partial q_j}\dot q_j + \frac{\partial f_\alpha}{\partial t}$, so $a_{j\alpha} = \partial f_\alpha/\partial q_j$ and

<Formula label="Multipliers for holonomic constraints" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\lambda_\alpha\frac{\partial f_\alpha}{\partial q_j}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.89), p. 24" />

The same follows from Hamilton's principle with an enlarged Lagrangian, varying both the $q_j$ and the $\lambda_\alpha$:

<Formula label="Generalized Hamilton's principle" tex="J[q_j, \lambda_\alpha] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\left(L + \lambda_\alpha f_\alpha\right)dt" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.90), p. 24" />

(varying $\lambda_\alpha$ gives back $f_\alpha = 0$). One can read $-\lambda_\alpha f_\alpha$ as an extra potential energy, which costs work to leave the surface $f_\alpha = 0$. Its force, $-\nabla_q(-\lambda_\alpha f_\alpha) = \lambda_\alpha\nabla_q f_\alpha$, points along the gradient of $f_\alpha$, normal to the constraint surface, as a constraint force should (notes p. 25).

**Semi-holonomic constraints**, $g_\alpha = a_{j\alpha}\dot q_j + a_{t\alpha} = 0$ with $a_{j\alpha} = \partial g_\alpha/\partial\dot q_j$:

<Formula label="Multipliers for semi-holonomic constraints" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} = \sum_{\alpha=1}^{k}\lambda_\alpha\frac{\partial g_\alpha}{\partial \dot q_j}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.91), p. 25" />

In general this does **not** follow from Hamilton's principle; it needs d'Alembert's principle, which is why the notes spent time on it. Static friction enforcing rolling is such a constraint, and the force term $\lambda\,\partial g/\partial\dot q$ has the same form as the friction force $-\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial\dot q$ from the dissipation function of the [previous section](/courses/8-09/constraints-friction-dalembert).

## A mass sliding off a sphere

A particle of mass $m$ sits at the top of a frictionless sphere of radius $a$, is nudged to a small angle $\theta_0$, and slides down (notes pp. 25–26). Where does it leave the surface? Use spherical coordinates with $\theta$ measured from the top, and drop the azimuth by symmetry. The true constraint $r \ge a$ is an inequality, but while the particle touches the sphere $f = r - a = 0$ holds, and the particle leaves where the force of this constraint drops to zero. With $T = \frac m2(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2)$ and $V = mgz = mgr\cos\theta$, the $r$ and $\theta$ equations are

<Formula label="Radial equation" tex="m\ddot r - mr\dot\theta^2 + mg\cos\theta = \lambda" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.92), p. 25" />

<Formula label="Angular equation" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(mr^2\dot\theta\right) - mgr\sin\theta = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.93), p. 25" />

($\partial f/\partial\theta = 0$, so no multiplier in the second). With $r = a$ these are three equations for $r$, $\theta$ and $\lambda$. On the sphere $\dot r = \ddot r = 0$, leaving $ma^2\ddot\theta = mga\sin\theta$ and $-ma\dot\theta^2 + mg\cos\theta = \lambda$. Multiplying the first by $\dot\theta$ and integrating from rest at the top gives $\dot\theta^2 = \frac{2g}{a}(1 - \cos\theta)$, and then

<Formula label="Force of the sphere on the mass" tex="\lambda = mg\left(3\cos\theta - 2\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.94), p. 25" />

the radial constraint force. It vanishes, and the mass leaves the sphere, at $\cos\theta = 2/3$, $\theta \approx 48^\circ$.

<Plot variable="x" domain={[0, 90]} range={[-2.2, 1.3]} xLabel="angle from the top, degrees" yLabel="λ / mg" samples={200}
  curves={[{ expr: "3*cos(x*pi/180) - 2", label: "normal force while in contact" }]}
  points={[{ x: 48.19, y: 0, label: "leaves at 48.2°" }]}
  caption="The radial force of the sphere on the sliding mass, in units of its weight, computed from λ = mg(3 cos θ − 2). It starts at mg at the top and falls to zero at cos θ = 2/3; past that point the sphere would have to pull, which it cannot, so the mass flies off. The negative part of the curve is never reached." />

The multiplier depends on how the constraint is written. With $f' = r^2 - a^2 = 0$ and multiplier $\lambda'$, the force is $\lambda'\,\partial f'/\partial r = 2a\lambda'$ at $r = a$, so $2a\lambda' = \lambda$: a multiplier with another meaning and other units, but the same constraint force (notes p. 26).

**After leaving.** For $\theta > \arccos(2/3)$ the constraint is gone and the equations lose their $\lambda$. Leaving happens at $r_1 = a$, $\theta_1 = \arccos(2/3)$, $\dot r_1 = 0$ and $\dot\theta_1^2 = 2g/(3a)$. Cartesian coordinates $x = r\sin\theta$, $z = r\cos\theta$ are now simpler:

<Formula label="Free flight" tex="L = \frac m2\left(\dot x^2 + \dot z^2\right) - mgz" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.95), p. 26" />

so $\ddot x = 0$ and $\ddot z = -g$, starting from $z_1 = 2a/3$ and $x_1 = \sqrt5\,a/3$ with velocities $\dot z_1 = -a\sin\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1$ and $\dot x_1 = a\cos\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1$:

<Formula label="Projectile after leaving" tex="x(t) = \dot x_1(t - t_1) + x_1, \qquad z(t) = -\frac g2(t - t_1)^2 + \dot z_1(t - t_1) + z_1" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eqs. (1.96)–(1.97), p. 26" />

The time $t_1$ of leaving follows from the speed on the sphere,

<Formula label="Angular speed on the sphere" tex="\dot\theta^2 = \frac{2g}{a}\left(1 - \cos\theta\right) = \frac{4g}{a}\sin^2\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.98), p. 26" />

so $t_1 = \sqrt{a/4g}\int_{\theta_0}^{\arccos(2/3)}d\theta/\sin(\theta/2)$. The integral grows without limit as $\theta_0 \to 0$: a particle starting exactly at the top, at rest, would stay there.

## A hoop rolling down a ramp

A hoop of mass $m$ and radius $a$ rolls without slipping down a ramp inclined at $\phi$ (notes pp. 26–27). Let $x$ be the distance travelled down the slope, with the bottom of the ramp at $x = \ell$ so that $V = mg(\ell - x)\sin\phi$ vanishes there, and $\theta$ the angle the hoop has turned. Rolling means $a\dot\theta = \dot x$; this could be integrated to a holonomic constraint, but the notes treat it as semi-holonomic, $h = a\dot\theta - \dot x = 0$. (The notes print "$= a$" at the end of this; it should be $= 0$.) With $I_{\text{hoop}} = ma^2$,

<Formula label="Lagrangian of the hoop" tex="L = T - V = \frac m2\dot x^2 + \frac{ma^2}{2}\dot\theta^2 + mg(x - \ell)\sin\phi" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.99), p. 26" />

With $\partial h/\partial\dot x = -1$ and $\partial h/\partial\dot\theta = a$, eq. (1.91) gives

<Formula label="Equations of the hoop" tex="m\ddot x - mg\sin\phi = -\lambda, \qquad ma^2\ddot\theta = \lambda a" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.100), p. 27" />

(The notes write $+\lambda$ in the first equation; the sign must be minus, since $\partial h/\partial\dot x = -1$, and only then do the results below follow.) Differentiating the constraint, $\ddot x = a\ddot\theta$, so the second equation says $m\ddot x = \lambda$. The first then gives $2m\ddot x = mg\sin\phi$:

<Formula label="Friction on the rolling hoop" tex="\ddot x = \frac g2\sin\phi, \qquad \lambda = \frac12 mg\sin\phi" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.101), p. 27" />

The hoop accelerates at half the rate of a frictionless sliding block, $\lambda$ is the static friction force pointing up the slope (along $-\hat x$), and $\ddot\theta = \frac{g}{2a}\sin\phi$.

## A block on a wedge on ice

Last, a wedge of mass $m_2$ and angle $\alpha$ slides without friction on ice, and a block of mass $m_1$ slides without friction down its sloping face (notes pp. 27–28). Two constraints are of interest (the one keeping the wedge from rotating is ignored): the wedge stays on the ice, $f_2 = y_2 = 0$, and the block stays on the face, $f_1 = (y_1 - y_2) - (x_1 - x_2)\tan\alpha = 0$. With $T = \frac{m_1}{2}(\dot x_1^2 + \dot y_1^2) + \frac{m_2}{2}(\dot x_2^2 + \dot y_2^2)$ and $V = m_1gy_1 + m_2g(y_2 + y_0)$, where $y_0$ is the height of the wedge's centre of mass above its base, eq. (1.89) gives

<Formula label="Equations for block and wedge" tex="m_1\ddot x_1 = -\lambda_1\tan\alpha, \quad m_1\ddot y_1 + m_1 g = \lambda_1, \quad m_2\ddot x_2 = \lambda_1\tan\alpha, \quad m_2\ddot y_2 + m_2 g = -\lambda_1 + \lambda_2" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.102), p. 28" />

Number these (1) to (4), and the two constraints (5) and (6). From (6), $\ddot y_2 = 0$, so (4) says $\lambda_2 - \lambda_1 = m_2 g$: $\lambda_2$ is the push of the ice on the wedge and $\lambda_1$ the vertical part of the push of the wedge on the block. Adding (1) and (3) gives $m_1\ddot x_1 + m_2\ddot x_2 = 0$: no horizontal force acts on the pair. Differentiating (5) twice, $\ddot y_1 = (\ddot x_1 - \ddot x_2)\tan\alpha$, and using (1) to (3),

<Formula label="The multiplier" tex="\lambda_1 = \frac{m_1 m_2 g\cos^2\alpha}{m_2 + m_1\sin^2\alpha}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.103), p. 28" />

(the notes write it as $g$ divided by $1/(m_1\cos^2\alpha) + \tan^2\alpha/m_2$; this is the same over a common denominator). It is a constant, so all the accelerations are constant:

<Formula label="Accelerations" tex="\ddot x_2 = \frac{\tan\alpha}{m_2}\lambda_1, \qquad \ddot x_1 = -\frac{\tan\alpha}{m_1}\lambda_1, \qquad \ddot y_1 = \frac{\lambda_1}{m_1} - g" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.104), p. 28" />

Two checks. A very heavy wedge, $m_2 \to \infty$, does not move, $\ddot x_2 = 0$, and then $\lambda_1 = m_1 g\cos^2\alpha$, $\ddot x_1 = -g\sin\alpha\cos\alpha$ and $\ddot y_1 = -g\sin^2\alpha$: a block on a fixed incline. And as $\alpha \to 0$ the wedge flattens onto the ice and both accelerations vanish.

<WorkedExample title="The pull of a pendulum rod">
<Problem>
A bob of mass $m = 0.20$ kg on a light rigid rod of length $\ell$ is released from rest with the rod horizontal. Use a Lagrange multiplier to find the force of the rod on the bob as a function of the angle $\theta$ from the downward vertical, and its value at the lowest point.
</Problem>
<Step title="Set up">
Keep $r$ as a coordinate, with the constraint $f = r - \ell = 0$. Measuring $\theta$ from the downward vertical, $L = \frac m2(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2) + mgr\cos\theta$.
</Step>
<Step title="The radial equation">
The $r$ equation is $m\ddot r - mr\dot\theta^2 - mg\cos\theta = \lambda\,\partial f/\partial r = \lambda$. On the constraint $\ddot r = 0$, so $\lambda = -m\ell\dot\theta^2 - mg\cos\theta$. It is negative: the rod pulls the bob inwards, a tension of size $|\lambda|$.
</Step>
<Step title="The speed">
The $\theta$ equation, or energy conservation, gives $\frac12 m\ell^2\dot\theta^2 = mg\ell\cos\theta$ when starting from rest at $\theta = 90^\circ$. So $m\ell\dot\theta^2 = 2mg\cos\theta$ and $|\lambda| = 3mg\cos\theta$, whatever the length of the rod.
</Step>
<Step title="At the bottom">
At $\theta = 0$, $|\lambda| = 3mg = 3 \times 0.20 \times 9.81 = 5.89$ N, three times the bob's weight of $1.96$ N.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Summary

- Constraints $g_\alpha = a_{j\alpha}\dot q_j + a_{t\alpha} = 0$, multiplied by $\lambda_\alpha$ and added to d'Alembert's principle, give $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_j} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} = \lambda_\alpha a_{j\alpha}$; with the constraints, $n + k$ equations for $q_j$ and $\lambda_\alpha$.
- The right side is the generalized force of constraint: $\lambda_\alpha\,\partial f_\alpha/\partial q_j$ for holonomic constraints (also from $\delta\int(L + \lambda_\alpha f_\alpha)dt = 0$), $\lambda_\alpha\,\partial g_\alpha/\partial\dot q_j$ for semi-holonomic ones (from d'Alembert only).
- A mass sliding from the top of a sphere feels $\lambda = mg(3\cos\theta - 2)$ and leaves at $\cos\theta = 2/3$; rewriting the constraint changes the multiplier, not the force.
- A hoop rolling down a ramp accelerates at $\frac12 g\sin\phi$ with friction $\frac12 mg\sin\phi$.
- A block on a wedge on ice has constant accelerations set by the constant multiplier $\lambda_1$, and reduces to a block on a fixed incline as $m_2 \to \infty$.
