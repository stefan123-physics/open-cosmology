[The previous page](/courses/8-09/action-angle-variables) built action-angle variables for one degree of freedom. This page follows the rest of Section 4.6 of the notes (pp. 87–91): several degrees of freedom, a two-dimensional oscillator, and an extended example, the Kepler problem of [Section 4.5](/courses/8-09/kepler-problem-hamilton-jacobi), where the action variables explain why bound orbits close and supply the constants that perturbation theory will set in motion.

## Several degrees of freedom

Action-angle variables extend to $(q_1, \ldots, q_n, p_1, \ldots, p_n)$ provided each pair $(q_i, p_i)$ by itself librates or rotates, and the H-J equation separates completely (notes p. 87):

<Formula label="A completely separated characteristic function" tex="W = \sum_j W_j(q_j, \alpha)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.192), p. 87" />

Then each momentum depends on its own coordinate only, and each pair has its own action, integrated over that pair's own cycle (no sum over repeated indices in this formula):

<Formula label="One action per degree of freedom" tex="p_i = \frac{\partial W_i}{\partial q_i} = p_i(q_i, \alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n) \;\Rightarrow\; J_i = \oint p_i\,dq_i = J_i(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.193), p. 87" />

Inverting gives $\alpha_j = \alpha_j(J_1, \ldots, J_n)$, and so $H = \alpha_1 = H(J_1, \ldots, J_n)$. The angle variables are

<Formula label="Angle variables" tex="\omega_i = \frac{\partial W}{\partial J_i} = \sum_j\frac{\partial W_j}{\partial J_i} = \omega_i(q_1, \ldots, q_n, J_1, \ldots, J_n)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.194), p. 87" />

and, as in one dimension, each advances at a constant rate (notes p. 88):

<Formula label="The frequencies" tex="\dot\omega_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial J_i} = \nu_i(J_1, \ldots, J_n)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.195), p. 88" />

These $n$ frequencies describe a **multiply periodic** motion. Each coordinate repeats on its own schedule, yet the state as a whole, a point in the $2n$-dimensional phase space, may never come back to where it was.

**Example: a two-dimensional oscillator.** With $x = A\cos(2\pi\nu_1t)$, $y = B\cos(2\pi\nu_2t)$, $p_x = m\dot x$ and $p_y = m\dot y$ (eqs. (4.196)–(4.197)), the point $(x, y)$ returns to where it started only if $\nu_1/\nu_2$ is a rational number. Otherwise the path never closes and, given time, fills a whole rectangle. The graph shows the path for a frequency ratio you can choose.

## The Kepler problem: the separated W

Return to the Kepler potential $V = -k/r$ (eq. (4.198)) in spherical coordinates, where Section 4.5 found $W = W_r(r, \alpha) + W_\theta(\theta, \alpha) + W_\varphi(\varphi, \alpha)$ (eq. (4.199)). For $E < 0$, $r$ and $\theta$ librate between turning points while $\varphi$ rotates. From the separated equations of Section 4.5 (notes p. 88):

<Formula label="The three parts of W" tex="W_\varphi = \alpha_\varphi\varphi, \qquad W_\theta = \pm\int\sqrt{\alpha_\theta^2 - \frac{\alpha_\varphi^2}{\sin^2\theta}}\,d\theta, \qquad W_r = \pm\int\sqrt{2m(\alpha_1 - V(r)) - \frac{\alpha_\theta^2}{r^2}}\,dr" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, p. 88 (unnumbered)" />

(The notes print $W_r$ without its integral sign; the integral is meant, as for $W_\theta$.)

## The angular actions

The azimuthal angle is cyclic and its momentum constant. Its "period" is taken as $2\pi$, one full turn back to the same point in space (the notes remark that any period would do):

<Formula label="Azimuthal action" tex="J_\varphi = \oint p_\varphi\,d\varphi = \oint\frac{\partial W}{\partial\varphi}\,d\varphi = \oint\alpha_\varphi\,d\varphi = 2\pi\alpha_\varphi" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eqs. (4.200)–(4.201), p. 88" />

with $\alpha_\varphi$ the angular momentum about $\hat z$. For the polar angle,

<Formula label="Polar action" tex="J_\theta = \oint p_\theta\,d\theta = \oint\pm\sqrt{\alpha_\theta^2 - \frac{\alpha_\varphi^2}{\sin^2\theta}}\,d\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.202), p. 88" />

Define $\cos\gamma \equiv \alpha_\varphi/\alpha_\theta$, the fraction of the angular momentum that lies along $\hat z$ (eq. (4.203)); then $J_\theta = \alpha_\theta\oint\pm\sqrt{1 - \cos^2\gamma/\sin^2\theta}\,d\theta$ (eq. (4.204)). The momentum $p_\theta$ vanishes where $\sin\theta_0 = \cos\gamma$, so $\theta$ swings between $\theta_0$ and $\pi - \theta_0$. The integrand is symmetric about $\theta = \pi/2$, and the cycle consists of four equal quarters (notes p. 89):

<Formula label="Polar action, four quarters" tex="J_\theta = 4\alpha_\theta\int_{\theta_0}^{\pi/2}\sqrt{1 - \frac{\cos^2\gamma}{\sin^2\theta}}\,d\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.205), p. 89" />

(The notes write the limits the other way round, from $\pi/2$ to $\theta_0$, which with $\theta_0 < \pi/2$ would give a negative result; the action is positive.) Two substitutions, $\cos\theta = \sin\gamma\sin\psi$ and then $u = \tan\psi$ (eq. (4.206)), reduce the integral to elementary ones:

<Formula label="Polar action evaluated" tex="J_\theta = 4\alpha_\theta\int_0^\infty\left(\frac{1}{1 + u^2} - \frac{\cos^2\gamma}{1 + u^2\cos^2\gamma}\right)du = 2\pi\alpha_\theta(1 - \cos\gamma) = 2\pi(\alpha_\theta - \alpha_\varphi)" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.207), p. 89" />

Each term integrates to an arctangent: the first gives $\pi/2$, the second $\cos\gamma\,\pi/2$. Adding the azimuthal action,

<Formula label="Total angular momentum as an action" tex="J_\theta + J_\varphi = 2\pi\alpha_\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.208), p. 89" />

which is $2\pi\ell$, since Section 4.5 identified $\alpha_\theta$ with the total angular momentum $\ell$.

## The radial action, and a degeneracy

Finally (notes p. 89), with $\alpha_\theta = (J_\theta + J_\varphi)/2\pi$:

<Formula label="Radial action" tex="J_r = \oint\sqrt{2mE - 2mV(r) - \frac{(J_\theta + J_\varphi)^2}{4\pi^2r^2}}\,dr" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.209), p. 89" />

Look at what this says before evaluating it. For **any** central potential, $J_r$ is a function of only two quantities, $E$ and the sum $J_\theta + J_\varphi$. Inverting, $E = E(J_r, J_\theta + J_\varphi)$, so the derivatives with respect to $J_\theta$ and $J_\varphi$ are equal:

<Formula label="Degenerate angular frequencies" tex="\frac{\partial E}{\partial J_\theta} = \frac{\partial E}{\partial J_\varphi} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \nu_\theta = \nu_\varphi" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.210), p. 89" />

The two angular frequencies are **degenerate** for every central potential. This is the action-angle form of a fact from Section 4.5: the orbit stays in a fixed plane, so the motion in $\theta$ and in $\varphi$ are two views of a single revolution.

## The inverse-square potential: all frequencies equal

For $V = -k/r$ and $E < 0$, the radial integral can be done (for example, by contour integration), and gives (notes p. 89)

<Formula label="Kepler radial action" tex="J_r = -(J_\theta + J_\varphi) + \pi k\sqrt{\frac{2m}{-E}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad J_r + J_\theta + J_\varphi = \pi k\sqrt{\frac{2m}{-E}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.211), p. 89" />

Now the energy depends on the three actions only through their sum (notes p. 90):

<Formula label="Kepler energy in action variables" tex="E = -\frac{2\pi^2k^2m}{(J_r + J_\theta + J_\varphi)^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \nu_\theta = \nu_\varphi = \nu_r" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.212), p. 90" />

All three frequencies coincide. In particular the radial oscillation, from perihelion to aphelion and back, takes exactly as long as one revolution: that is why a bound Kepler orbit closes into an ellipse. The common frequency is

<Formula label="Kepler frequency" tex="\nu_r = \frac{\partial E}{\partial J_r} = \frac{4\pi^2k^2m}{(J_r + J_\theta + J_\varphi)^3} = \frac{1}{\pi k}\sqrt{\frac{-2E^3}{m}}" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.213), p. 90" />

the correct orbital frequency of a bound Kepler orbit. (The notes leave the factor $m$ out of the middle expression; the end result, which they give correctly, requires it.) With $E = -k/2a$ this is Kepler's third law, $\nu^2 \propto a^{-3}$.

## The angle variables and the five constants

Writing $\alpha_1 = E$, $\alpha_\theta$ and $\alpha_\varphi$ in terms of the actions turns $W$ into a function $W(r, \theta, \varphi, J_r, J_\theta, J_\varphi)$ (written out on p. 90 of the notes), and the three angle variables follow by differentiation (eq. (4.214)): $\omega_r = \partial W/\partial J_r$ depends on $r$, $\omega_\theta = \partial W/\partial J_\theta$ on $r$ and $\theta$, and $\omega_\varphi = \partial W/\partial J_\varphi$ on all three coordinates. Each grows at its frequency, and here all three frequencies are equal. So the differences of angle variables are constant. Together with the three actions, the notes collect **five constants** of the Kepler motion (notes p. 90):

<Formula label="Five constants of the Kepler problem" tex="J_1 = J_\varphi, \quad J_2 = J_\theta + J_\varphi, \quad J_3 = J_r + J_\theta + J_\varphi, \quad \omega_1 = \omega_\varphi - \omega_\theta, \quad \omega_2 = \omega_\theta - \omega_r" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, eq. (4.215), p. 90" />

(The same five could be found from the angular momentum $\vec L$, the energy and the Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector.) Astronomers describe an orbit by five **orbital elements**: two fix the plane of the orbit in space, the **inclination** $i$ and the **longitude of the ascending node** $\Omega$; three fix the ellipse within that plane, the **semi-major axis** $a$ (size), the **eccentricity** $\varepsilon$ (shape) and the **argument of the pericentre** (orientation in the plane). The notes draw them in Figure 4.9 and state, without derivation, how they relate to the action-angle constants (notes p. 91):

<Formula label="Orbital elements from action-angle constants" tex="\cos i = \frac{J_1}{J_2}, \qquad a = -\frac{k}{2E} = \frac{J_3^2}{4\pi^2mk}, \qquad \varepsilon = \sqrt{1 - \left(\frac{J_2}{J_3}\right)^2}, \qquad \Omega = 2\pi\omega_1, \qquad \omega = 2\pi\omega_2" cite="reading1" at="Section 4.6, p. 91 (unnumbered)" />

In the last relation $\omega$ on the left is the astronomers' argument of the pericentre, not an angle variable; the notes use the letter for both. Recall too that $J_2 = 2\pi\alpha_\theta = 2\pi\ell$, so $\cos i = J_1/J_2$ is the $z$ component of the angular momentum over its length, and the eccentricity formula is Section 4.5's $\varepsilon = \sqrt{1 + 2E\ell^2/(mk^2)}$ rewritten.

<WorkedExample title="The satellite of Section 4.5 in action-angle variables">
<Problem>
Take the satellite of Section 4.5 ($m = 1000$ kg, $k = 3.986 \times 10^{17}$ J m, $E = -1.898 \times 10^{10}$ J, $\ell = 6.099 \times 10^{13}$ kg m²/s) and suppose its orbit is inclined at $i = 30°$ to the equator, taken as the $xy$ plane. Find $J_1$, $J_2$, $J_3$ and $J_r$, and from them the semi-major axis, the eccentricity and the period.
</Problem>
<Step title="The actions">
$J_3 = \pi k\sqrt{2m/(-E)} = \pi \times 3.986 \times 10^{17} \times \sqrt{2000/1.898 \times 10^{10}} = 4.065 \times 10^{14}$ J s. $J_2 = 2\pi\ell = 3.832 \times 10^{14}$ J s, and $J_1 = J_2\cos 30° = 3.319 \times 10^{14}$ J s. The radial action is the difference $J_r = J_3 - J_2 = 2.32 \times 10^{13}$ J s, small because the orbit is not very eccentric (it would vanish for a circle).
</Step>
<Step title="Size and shape">
$a = J_3^2/(4\pi^2mk) = 1.050 \times 10^{7}$ m, the 10500 km semi-major axis (half of perigee plus apogee, $7000 + 14000$ km). $J_2/J_3 = 0.94281$, so $\varepsilon = \sqrt{1 - 0.94281^2} = 0.3333$, the $1/3$ found before.
</Step>
<Step title="The period">
$\nu = 4\pi^2k^2m/J_3^3 = 9.34 \times 10^{-5}$ Hz, so the period is $1/\nu = 1.071 \times 10^{4}$ s, about 2.97 hours. As a check, Kepler's third law $2\pi\sqrt{a^3/GM}$ with $a = 1.05 \times 10^{7}$ m and $GM = 3.986 \times 10^{14}$ m³/s² gives the same.
</Step>

## Why this matters: perturbed orbits

When some extra force disturbs an orbit (the pull of a third body, say, or a relativistic correction to gravity), these action-angle constants are the natural language for the change: they are no longer constant but drift slowly in time. For example, a slow change of the argument of the pericentre, $2\pi\omega_2$, is the precession of the perihelion. Chapter 5 of the notes develops the perturbation theory that computes such drifts.

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="From closed ellipses to precessing orbits">
The exact equality $\nu_r = \nu_\theta = \nu_\varphi$ is special to the $1/r$ potential. Any change to it, such as the extra term that general relativity adds to the effective potential, splits the radial frequency from the angular one, and the ellipse slowly turns. That is the perihelion precession of Mercury, worked out on the 8.033 page on [orbits around a black hole](/courses/8-033/schwarzschild-orbits-perihelion), one of the first tests of the theory on which modern cosmology is built. Section 5.3 of the notes computes the same precession with this page's action-angle variables.
</Callout>

## Summary

- With a fully separated $W$ and each pair librating or rotating, every degree of freedom has an action $J_i = \oint p_i\,dq_i$ and an angle $\omega_i$ advancing at $\nu_i = \partial H/\partial J_i$; the motion is periodic only for rational frequency ratios.
- Kepler: $J_\varphi = 2\pi\alpha_\varphi$, $J_\theta = 2\pi(\alpha_\theta - \alpha_\varphi)$, $J_\theta + J_\varphi = 2\pi\ell$. For any central potential $E$ depends on $J_\theta + J_\varphi$ only, so $\nu_\theta = \nu_\varphi$.
- For $V = -k/r$: $E = -2\pi^2k^2m/(J_r + J_\theta + J_\varphi)^2$, all three frequencies are equal (bound orbits close), and $\nu = (1/\pi k)\sqrt{-2E^3/m}$.
- The constants $J_1, J_2, J_3, \omega_1, \omega_2$ correspond to the inclination, node, size, eccentricity and orientation of the orbit; perturbations make them drift, which Chapter 5 computes.
