In 8.01, a body turning about a fixed axis had one moment of inertia, $I = \int r_\perp^2\,dm$, and its angular momentum along the axis was $I\omega$ (see [moment of inertia](/courses/8-01/moment-of-inertia) and [angular momentum of a rigid body](/courses/8-01/angular-momentum-rigid-body)). That page already noticed that an unsymmetric body can have angular momentum pointing off the axis. Section 2.3 of the notes (pp. 37–44) handles rotation about any axis at once. The single number becomes a $3 \times 3$ symmetric matrix, the **moment of inertia tensor**, which gives both the kinetic energy and the angular momentum. The section then shows how the tensor changes when the origin moves or the axes turn, and how to find the special axes in which it is diagonal. (The notes write the tensor with a hat, $\hat I$; this page writes $I$.)

## Kinetic energy of a rotating body

Take a body of $N$ particles with masses $m_i$. Let $\vec R$ be the centre of mass and, for now, let $\vec r_i$ be the position of particle $i$ **relative to the centre of mass**. Summing over repeated particle labels, the kinetic energy splits into the motion of the centre of mass and the motion about it (the cross term vanishes because $\sum_i m_i\vec r_i = 0$):

<Formula label="Kinetic energy split at the centre of mass" tex="T = \frac{1}{2}M\dot{\vec R}^2 + \frac{1}{2}m_i\dot{\vec r}_i^{\,2}" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.34), p. 37" />

In a rigid body the particles cannot move relative to one another, only turn together, so $\dot{\vec r}_i = \vec\omega \times \vec r_i$. Using $(\vec\omega \times \vec r)^2 = \omega^2 r^2 - (\vec\omega \cdot \vec r)^2$, the rotational part is

<Formula label="Rotational kinetic energy" tex="T_R = \frac{1}{2}m_i\dot{\vec r}_i^{\,2} = \frac{1}{2}m_i(\vec\omega \times \vec r_i)^2 = \frac{1}{2}m_i\left[\omega^2 r_i^2 - (\vec\omega \cdot \vec r_i)^2\right]" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.35), p. 37" />

Now write the components out. Use $a, b$ for the Cartesian directions, keeping $i, j$ for particles, and the Kronecker delta $\delta_{ab}$ (1 if $a = b$, 0 otherwise), so that $\omega^2 = \delta_{ab}\,\omega_a\omega_b$:

<Formula label="Rotational kinetic energy in components" tex="T_R = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,a,b} m_i\left(\delta_{ab}\,r_i^2 - r_{ia}r_{ib}\right)\omega_a\omega_b" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.36), p. 38" />

## The inertia tensor

The bracket depends only on how the mass is arranged, not on how fast the body turns. Pulling it out defines the **moment of inertia tensor**:

<Formula label="Moment of inertia tensor (particles)" tex="I_{ab} \equiv \sum_i m_i\left(\delta_{ab}\,r_i^2 - r_{ia}r_{ib}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.37), p. 38" />

<Formula label="Moment of inertia tensor (continuous body)" tex="I_{ab} \equiv \int_V \left(r^2\delta_{ab} - r_ar_b\right)\rho(\vec r)\,dV" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.38), p. 38" />

The rotational kinetic energy is then a quadratic form in $\vec\omega$, which can be written as a row vector times a matrix times a column vector:

<Formula label="Kinetic energy from the tensor" tex="T_R = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{a,b} I_{ab}\,\omega_a\omega_b = \frac{1}{2}\,\vec\omega \cdot I \cdot \vec\omega" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.39), p. 38" />

With $\vec r_i = (x_i, y_i, z_i)$, the matrix in full is

<Formula label="The inertia tensor as a matrix" tex="I = \sum_i m_i\begin{pmatrix} y_i^2 + z_i^2 & -x_iy_i & -x_iz_i \\ -x_iy_i & x_i^2 + z_i^2 & -y_iz_i \\ -x_iz_i & -y_iz_i & x_i^2 + y_i^2 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.40), p. 38" />

The diagonal entries are the **moments of inertia** about the three axes: $I_{zz}$, for example, adds up mass times squared distance from the $z$ axis, $x^2 + y^2$. The off-diagonal entries are the **products of inertia**. The matrix is symmetric, $I_{ab} = I_{ba}$, in any set of axes. If the body turns about the $z$ axis only, $\vec\omega = (0, 0, \omega)$, then $T_R = \tfrac{1}{2}I_{zz}\omega^2$, the familiar one-axis formula.

## Angular momentum

Now let the $\vec r_i$ be measured from any point of the body that stays at rest, not necessarily the centre of mass. Each particle moves with $\vec v_i = \vec\omega \times \vec r_i$, and the angular momentum about that point is, using the rule for a double cross product,

<Formula label="Angular momentum of a rotating body" tex="\vec L = m_i\,\vec r_i \times \vec v_i = m_i\,\vec r_i \times (\vec\omega \times \vec r_i) = m_i\left[r_i^2\,\vec\omega - (\vec\omega \cdot \vec r_i)\,\vec r_i\right]" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.41), p. 38" />

In components the same tensor appears:

<Formula label="Angular momentum in components" tex="L_a = \sum_i m_i\left(r_i^2\,\omega_a - (\vec\omega \cdot \vec r_i)\,r_{ia}\right) = \sum_{i,b} m_i\left(\delta_{ab}r_i^2 - r_{ia}r_{ib}\right)\omega_b = \sum_b I_{ab}\,\omega_b" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.42), p. 38" />

<Formula label="Angular momentum from the tensor" tex="\vec L = I \cdot \vec\omega" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.43), p. 39" />

<Formula label="Kinetic energy from L" tex="T_R = \frac{1}{2}\,\vec\omega \cdot \vec L" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.44), p. 39" />

The same definition of $I$ serves whether the $\vec r_i$ are measured from the centre of mass or from another fixed point. The important lesson of (2.43) is that **$\vec L$ is in general not parallel to $\vec\omega$**: a matrix times a vector usually points somewhere else.

**A tilted dumbbell.** (The notes give this example after the parallel axis theorem; it is placed here, next to the result it illustrates.) The notes show this with two equal point masses $m$ on a light rigid rod through the origin, at distances $r_1$ and $r_2$ on either side, spinning with $\vec\omega = \omega\hat z$ while the rod makes a fixed angle $\alpha$ with the $z$ axis (notes pp. 40–41). Choose axes so that the masses lie in the $yz$ plane at that instant:

<Formula label="Positions of the two masses" tex="\vec r_1 = (0,\ r_1\sin\alpha,\ r_1\cos\alpha) \quad\text{and}\quad \vec r_2 = (0,\ -r_2\sin\alpha,\ -r_2\cos\alpha)" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.51), p. 40" />

Each entry of (2.40) is a two-term sum:

<Formula label="The dumbbell's tensor" tex="\begin{aligned} I_{zz} &= m(r_1^2 + r_2^2)\sin^2\alpha, & I_{xx} &= m(r_1^2 + r_2^2), & I_{yy} &= m(r_1^2 + r_2^2)\cos^2\alpha, \\ I_{xy} &= 0, & I_{xz} &= 0, & I_{yz} &= -m(r_1^2 + r_2^2)\sin\alpha\cos\alpha \end{aligned}" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.52), p. 41" />

With only $\omega_z$ non-zero, $\vec L$ is the third column times $\omega$:

<Formula label="The dumbbell's angular momentum" tex="\vec L = (0,\ I_{yz}\,\omega,\ I_{zz}\,\omega)" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.53), p. 41" />

Since $I_{yz} \ne 0$ (unless $\alpha$ is $0$ or $90^\circ$), $\vec L$ leans away from the $z$ axis. In fact $\vec L \propto (0, -\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)$ is perpendicular to the rod. As the dumbbell spins, $\vec L$ is carried round the $z$ axis with it, so $\vec L$ changes direction, and a torque from the bearings is needed to keep the rod spinning about $z$. This is why an unbalanced wheel shakes its axle.

## Moving the origin: the parallel axis theorem

The tensor depends on where the origin is (the $\vec r_i$ are measured from it) and on how the axes are oriented (the components $a, b$ refer to them). Both dependences can be worked out from one calculation.

First, keep the orientation and move the origin from the centre of mass to another point $Q$. Let $\vec r\,'_i$ be the position of particle $i$ seen from $Q$, $\vec r_i$ as seen from the centre of mass, and $\vec R$ the position of the centre of mass seen from $Q$:

<Formula label="Positions from Q and from the centre of mass" tex="\vec r\,'_i = \vec R + \vec r_i" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.45), p. 39" />

<Formula label="Definition of the centre of mass" tex="\sum_i m_i\vec r_i = 0 \quad\text{and}\quad M = \sum_i m_i" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.46), p. 39" />

Insert (2.45) into the definition (2.37) about $Q$:

<Formula label="The tensor about Q, expanded" tex="\begin{aligned} I^Q_{ab} &= m_i\left(\delta_{ab}\,r'^{\,2}_i - r'_{ia}r'_{ib}\right) \\ &= m_i\left(\delta_{ab}(r_i^2 + 2\vec r_i \cdot \vec R + R^2) - r_{ia}r_{ib} - r_{ia}R_b - R_ar_{ib} - R_aR_b\right) \end{aligned}" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eqs. (2.47)–(2.48), p. 40" />

Every term with a single $\vec r_i$ adds up to zero by (2.46). The terms with two factors of $\vec r_i$ are the tensor about the centre of mass, and the terms with none give the tensor of a single point mass $M$ at $\vec R$:

<Formula label="Parallel axis theorem" tex="I^Q_{ab} = M\left(\delta_{ab}R^2 - R_aR_b\right) + I^{\text{CM}}_{ab}" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.49), p. 40" />

To go between two points $P$ and $Q$, neither of them the centre of mass, use it twice, via the centre of mass. Projected onto one axis $\hat n$ (a unit vector) through $Q$, with $\theta$ the angle between $\hat n$ and $\vec R$, it becomes the version known from 8.01, with $R\sin\theta$ the distance between the two parallel axes:

<Formula label="Parallel axis theorem for one axis" tex="\hat n \cdot I^Q \cdot \hat n = M\left(R^2 - (\hat n \cdot \vec R)^2\right) + \hat n \cdot I^{\text{CM}} \cdot \hat n = MR^2\sin^2\theta + \hat n \cdot I^{\text{CM}} \cdot \hat n" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.50), p. 40" />

## Turning the axes

Now keep the origin and rotate the axes by an orthogonal matrix $U$, $U^TU = UU^T = 1$. The components of every vector change the same way:

<Formula label="Vectors in rotated axes" tex="\vec L' = U\vec L, \qquad \vec\omega' = U\vec\omega \quad\text{and therefore}\quad \vec\omega = U^T\vec\omega'" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.54), p. 41" />

Combining with $\vec L = I \cdot \vec\omega$:

<Formula label="The inertia tensor in rotated axes" tex="\vec L' = U I \cdot \vec\omega = (U I U^T) \cdot \vec\omega' \quad\Rightarrow\quad I' = U I U^T" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.55), p. 41" />

This rule, with one factor of $U$ for each index, is what **makes** $I$ a tensor (a vector has one index and one factor of $U$).

## Principal axes

Because $I$ is real and symmetric, there is always a rotation $U$ that makes it diagonal:

<Formula label="Diagonal form of the inertia tensor" tex="I_D = \begin{pmatrix} I_1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & I_2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & I_3 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.56), p. 41" />

This is the real version of a result usually proved in quantum mechanics: a Hermitian matrix can be diagonalised by a unitary one and has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors (see also [symmetric matrices](/courses/18-06/symmetric-matrices-positive-definite)). The entries $I_\alpha$ are real, and they cannot be negative: with no off-diagonal terms, each diagonal entry of (2.40) is a sum of masses times squares. (The notes call them positive; an idealised body lying entirely on one line has a zero moment about that line.)

The axes that make $I$ diagonal are the **principal axes**, and the $I_\alpha$ the **principal moments of inertia**. They are found as an eigenvalue problem:

<Formula label="Principal axes as an eigenvalue problem" tex="I \cdot \vec\xi = \lambda\,\vec\xi" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.57), p. 42" />

The three roots of $\det(I - \lambda 1) = 0$ are the principal moments, and the three real, orthogonal eigenvectors $\vec\xi^{(\alpha)}$ are the principal axes; placed as columns they form the rotation, $U^T = (\vec\xi^{(1)}\ \vec\xi^{(2)}\ \vec\xi^{(3)})$ for normalised eigenvectors, so the eigenvectors are the rows of $U$. In principal axes nothing couples (no sum over $\alpha$):

<Formula label="Angular momentum and energy in principal axes" tex="L_\alpha = I_\alpha\,\omega_\alpha \quad\text{and}\quad T = \frac{1}{2}\sum_\alpha I_\alpha\,\omega_\alpha^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.58), p. 42" />

where $L_\alpha$ and $\omega_\alpha$ are the components along the principal axes. So for any origin there are principal axes; if the kinetic energy is also to separate into a translational and a rotational part, the origin must be the centre of mass.

**Symmetry often shows the axes** (notes p. 42). For a thin rectangular plate in the $yz$ plane with one edge along the $z$ axis and the origin at the middle of that edge, $I_{yz} = 0$ because the plate is unchanged by $z \to -z$, and $I_{xz} = I_{xy} = 0$ because every point has $x = 0$: the coordinate axes are already principal. Symmetry can also leave a choice. For a sphere about its centre, **any** three perpendicular axes are principal; for a cylinder about a point on its axis, the axis is principal and **any** pair of perpendicular axes across it completes the set.

## Finding principal axes by solving: a cube about a corner

When symmetry is less obvious, the notes solve the eigenvalue problem for a uniform cube of mass $m$ and side $a$, with the origin at one corner and the axes along the three edges that meet there (notes pp. 43–44). Each moment and each product of inertia is the same by symmetry:

<Formula label="The cube's moments and products of inertia" tex="I_{xx} = I_{yy} = I_{zz} = \frac{m}{a^3}\int_0^a\!\!\int_0^a\!\!\int_0^a (x^2 + y^2)\,dx\,dy\,dz = \frac{2}{3}ma^2, \qquad I_{xy} = I_{yz} = I_{xz} = \frac{m}{a^3}\int_0^a\!\!\int_0^a\!\!\int_0^a (-xz)\,dx\,dy\,dz = -\frac{1}{4}ma^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.59), p. 43" />

<Formula label="The cube's tensor about a corner" tex="I = ma^2\begin{pmatrix} \tfrac{2}{3} & -\tfrac{1}{4} & -\tfrac{1}{4} \\ -\tfrac{1}{4} & \tfrac{2}{3} & -\tfrac{1}{4} \\ -\tfrac{1}{4} & -\tfrac{1}{4} & \tfrac{2}{3} \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.60), p. 43" />

<Formula label="The cube's characteristic equation" tex="\det(I - \lambda 1) = \left(\frac{11}{12}ma^2 - \lambda\right)^2\left(\frac{1}{6}ma^2 - \lambda\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.61), p. 43" />

The single root is $I_1 = \lambda_1 = \tfrac{1}{6}ma^2$; solving $(I - \lambda_1 1)\vec\xi^{(1)} = 0$ gives $\vec\xi^{(1)} = (1, 1, 1)$, the body diagonal through the corner (each row of $I - \tfrac{1}{6}ma^2\,1$ adds up to zero). The double root is

<Formula label="The cube's degenerate principal moments (corrected)" tex="I_2 = I_3 = \lambda_2 = \lambda_3 = \frac{11}{12}ma^2" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.63), p. 43 (corrected)" />

(The notes print $\tfrac{1}{12}ma^2$ here and carry it into (2.65); the characteristic equation (2.61) they write just above gives $\tfrac{11}{12}ma^2$, and only that value is consistent with the parallel axis theorem below.) Because the root is double, any two directions perpendicular to the diagonal, and to each other, will serve, for example

<Formula label="One choice of the other principal axes" tex="\vec\xi^{(2)} = (1, -1, 0) \quad\text{and}\quad \vec\xi^{(3)} = (1, 1, -2)" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.64), p. 43" />

so that in these principal axes, with the origin still at the corner,

<Formula label="The cube's tensor in principal axes (corrected)" tex="I_D = \frac{ma^2}{12}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 11 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 11 \end{pmatrix}" cite="reading1" at="Section 2.3, eq. (2.65), p. 44 (corrected)" />

About the cube's **centre**, the same directions are principal, but by symmetry so is every direction: $I^{\text{CM}} = \tfrac{1}{6}ma^2\,1$. The parallel axis theorem connects the two. Along the diagonal, $\vec R$ is parallel to the axis and nothing changes: $\tfrac{1}{6}ma^2$. Across it, $\vec R$ (of length $\sqrt{3}\,a/2$) is perpendicular to the axis and adds $M R^2 = \tfrac{3}{4}ma^2$, giving $\tfrac{1}{6} + \tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{11}{12}$ in units of $ma^2$.

<Problem>
A thin uniform square plate of mass $m$ and side $a$ lies in the $xy$ plane, with one corner at the origin and two edges along the $x$ and $y$ axes. Find its inertia tensor about the corner, its principal axes and moments, and check the result against the parallel axis theorem.
</Problem>
<Step title="The tensor">
The plate has $z = 0$ and surface density $m/a^2$. Then $I_{xx} = \tfrac{m}{a^2}\int_0^a\!\int_0^a y^2\,dx\,dy = \tfrac{1}{3}ma^2$, and likewise $I_{yy} = \tfrac{1}{3}ma^2$; $I_{zz} = \tfrac{m}{a^2}\int\!\int (x^2 + y^2) = \tfrac{2}{3}ma^2$. The only non-zero product is $I_{xy} = -\tfrac{m}{a^2}\int_0^a\!\int_0^a xy\,dx\,dy = -\tfrac{1}{4}ma^2$, since $I_{xz}$ and $I_{yz}$ contain $z = 0$.
</Step>
<Step title="Principal axes">
The $z$ axis is already principal, with $I_3 = \tfrac{2}{3}ma^2$. In the plane, the $2 \times 2$ block $ma^2\begin{pmatrix} 1/3 & -1/4 \\ -1/4 & 1/3 \end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvectors $(1, 1)$ and $(1, -1)$, with eigenvalues $ma^2(\tfrac{1}{3} - \tfrac{1}{4}) = \tfrac{1}{12}ma^2$ along the diagonal and $ma^2(\tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{4}) = \tfrac{7}{12}ma^2$ across it. The two in-plane moments add up to $I_3$, as they must for a flat plate.
</Step>
<Step title="Check with the parallel axis theorem">
Seen from the corner, the centre is at $\vec R = (a/2, a/2, 0)$, with $R^2 = a^2/2$. The correction $M(\delta_{ab}R^2 - R_aR_b)$ has $xx$ entry $m(a^2/2 - a^2/4) = \tfrac{1}{4}ma^2$ and $xy$ entry $-\tfrac{1}{4}ma^2$. Subtracting from the corner tensor: $I^{\text{CM}}_{xx} = \tfrac{1}{3} - \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{12}$ and $I^{\text{CM}}_{xy} = -\tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{4} = 0$, in units of $ma^2$. That is the familiar $\tfrac{1}{12}ma^2$ of a square plate about a line through its centre parallel to an edge, and the zero product says that, about the centre, the edge directions are principal too.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

The moment of inertia of the plate about a line in its plane through the corner, at angle $\beta$ to the $x$ axis, is $\hat n \cdot I \cdot \hat n$ with $\hat n = (\cos\beta, \sin\beta, 0)$, which works out to $ma^2(\tfrac{1}{3} - \tfrac{1}{4}\sin 2\beta)$. ## Summary

- For a rigid body, $T = \tfrac{1}{2}M\dot{\vec R}^2 + T_R$ about the centre of mass, with $T_R = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\vec\omega \cdot I \cdot \vec\omega$ and the inertia tensor $I_{ab} = \sum_i m_i(\delta_{ab}r_i^2 - r_{ia}r_{ib})$ (an integral for a continuous body). Diagonal entries are moments, off-diagonal ones products of inertia; the tensor is symmetric.
- About a fixed point, $\vec L = I \cdot \vec\omega$ and $T_R = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\vec\omega \cdot \vec L$. $\vec L$ is in general **not** parallel to $\vec\omega$: for a tilted dumbbell it is perpendicular to the rod.
- **Parallel axis theorem**: $I^Q_{ab} = M(\delta_{ab}R^2 - R_aR_b) + I^{\text{CM}}_{ab}$, or $MR^2\sin^2\theta$ added for one axis. **Rotated axes**: $I' = UIU^T$, the defining property of a tensor.
- A real symmetric tensor has three real, orthogonal eigenvectors, the **principal axes**, and eigenvalues $I_\alpha \ge 0$, the **principal moments**; in those axes $L_\alpha = I_\alpha\omega_\alpha$ and $T = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum I_\alpha\omega_\alpha^2$. Symmetry often reveals them; otherwise solve $\det(I - \lambda 1) = 0$.
- A uniform cube about a corner has principal moments $\tfrac{1}{6}ma^2$ along the diagonal and $\tfrac{11}{12}ma^2$ (twice) across it (the notes misprint the latter as $\tfrac{1}{12}$); about its centre, $\tfrac{1}{6}ma^2$ in every direction.
