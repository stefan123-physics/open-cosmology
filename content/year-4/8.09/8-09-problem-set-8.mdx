This set practises the second half of the chapter on fluids. Problems 1 and 2 use [potential flow](/courses/8-09/potential-flow) (Section 6.4), Problem 3 the [sound waves](/courses/8-09/fluid-sound-waves) of Section 6.5, Problem 4 the [Navier-Stokes equation and the force of a viscous fluid on a wall](/courses/8-09/viscous-fluid-equations) (Section 6.6) and the [steady flows in pipes](/courses/8-09/viscous-pipe-flow-reynolds) of Section 6.7. Problems 5 and 6 use the Reynolds number, similar flows and the counting of dimensionless ratios from Section 6.7, on the vortices and boundary layers described at the end of [Section 6.8](/courses/8-09/stokes-flow-sphere). The problems follow MIT's Problem Set 8, in our own words and in MIT's order. MIT publishes no solutions for the problem sets of this course, so the hints and the solutions here are our own.

A few things to know about the source. The sheet has printed page numbers; the problems are on pp. 2 and 3, cited below as "p. 2" and "p. 3". The notes are cited by their printed page and equation number. The sheet's reading list (sections of three textbooks) is left out; the course's own notes are cited instead. The sheet has no figures. In Problem 3 the sheet writes the pressure wave as $p$ and the speed of sound as $c_s$; here they are $P'$ and $c_S$, as in the notes, and the radial function in the sheet's formula for the vector Laplacian is $\chi(r)$, since $\phi$ is the velocity potential on this page. The notes write the Reynolds number as $R$; here $R$ is the radius of the cylinder in Problem 2, so the Reynolds number is written out as $VD/\nu$ or $\rho VD/\eta$.

Some choices are ours, each marked where it appears:

## How to answer

Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `u*(1 - R^2/r^2)`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. It treats its letters as independent, so each question says which letters to type: for example `theta` for $\theta$, `rho` for $\rho$, `eta` for $\eta$, `pi` for $\pi$, `R1` for $R_1$, `Pinf` for $P_\infty$. A choice box asks you to pick one answer.

Give a number answer to 3 significant figures and keep 4 in intermediate steps; an answer with only 2 significant figures may or may not be accepted. Where a box needs more digits, its label says so.

The points per problem are MIT's, and so is their split between the parts, except in Problem 4, which MIT does not split: there the split is ours. Some parts ask you to show something. Those parts have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The conventions are those of the notes and the lessons. A flow that is irrotational and incompressible has a velocity potential $\phi$:

<Formula label="Potential flow" tex="\vec v = \nabla\phi, \qquad \nabla^2\phi = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eqs. (6.70) and (6.71), p. 121" />

and for a steady potential flow of constant density, without gravity, the pressure follows from Bernoulli's equation in the form

<Formula label="Bernoulli's equation for potential flow" tex="\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t} + \frac{v^2}{2} + \Phi + \frac{P}{\rho} = b(t)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.4, eq. (6.74), p. 121" />

with $\partial\phi/\partial t = 0$, $\Phi = 0$ and a constant $b$. In polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ in a plane, $v_r = \partial\phi/\partial r$ and $v_\theta = (1/r)\,\partial\phi/\partial\theta$. A **stagnation point** is a point where the fluid is at rest.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Flow geometries" points={12} mit="Problem 1">

An ideal fluid flows in planes $z = $ constant, with a velocity potential $\phi(r, \theta)$ in cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$. A fixed solid surface can sit wherever the flow does not cross it. For each of the three potentials below, find a geometry of fixed walls that fits the flow, find any stagnation points at finite $r$, and sketch a streamline near the walls. In every part $C$ is a constant, and we take $C > 0$ (our choice).

The walls of this problem are half-planes that end on the $z$ axis, at fixed $\theta$. One wall lies along $\theta = 0$ (our choice). In each part, give the angle $\theta$ of the second wall: the first half-plane counterclockwise from $\theta = 0$ that the flow does not cross. The fluid fills the wedge between the two walls.

<Part label="a" points={4}>

For $\phi = Cr^2\cos(2\theta)$: give the angle of the second wall in degrees, and choose where the stagnation points at finite $r$ are. Then (our addition) give the speed $|\vec v|$ of the fluid at distance $r$ from the axis, typed with `C` and `r`.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** A wall must be a surface the flow never crosses. For walls at fixed $\theta$ that means $v_\theta = 0$ there, for every $r$.

<Step title="The velocity">

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the velocity" tex="v_r = \frac{\partial\phi}{\partial r} = 2Cr\cos 2\theta, \qquad v_\theta = \frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\theta} = -2Cr\sin 2\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

So $|\vec v| = 2Cr$, the same at every angle.

</Step>

<Step title="The walls">

$v_\theta = 0$ at $\sin 2\theta = 0$: at $\theta = 0$ and next at $\theta = 90°$. The fluid fills a right-angled corner between two perpendicular walls. In Cartesian coordinates $\phi = C(x^2 - y^2)$, so $v_x = 2Cx$ and $v_y = -2Cy$: with $C > 0$ the fluid comes down towards the wall $y = 0$ and turns away along it, outwards along $x$.

</Step>

<Step title="Stagnation point and streamlines">

The speed $2Cr$ is zero only at $r = 0$: the corner is a stagnation point. A streamline is everywhere along $\vec v$, so $dy/dx = v_y/v_x = -y/x$, whose solutions are the hyperbolas $xy = $ constant. Near the walls (small constant) a streamline runs close to the wall $x = 0$ on its way down, turns near the corner and leaves close to the wall $y = 0$.

The closer a streamline runs to the walls, the sharper it turns near the corner." />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\nabla^2\phi = 2C - 2C = 0$ in Cartesian coordinates: the potential is a valid flow. Doubling the picture across $x = 0$ gives the same flow hitting a flat wall $y = 0$ head on, with the stagnation point where the middle streamline meets the wall.

</Step>

For $\phi = Cr^4\cos(4\theta)$: give the angle of the second wall in degrees, and choose where the stagnation points at finite $r$ are.

<Solution>

**The idea.** The same as in (a), with $n = 4$ in place of $n = 2$.

<Step title="Velocity and walls">

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the velocity" tex="v_r = 4Cr^3\cos 4\theta, \qquad v_\theta = -4Cr^3\sin 4\theta, \qquad |\vec v| = 4Cr^3" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

$v_\theta = 0$ at $\theta = 0$ and next at $\theta = 45°$: the fluid flows in a corner of $45°$. The speed is zero only at the corner, $r = 0$, which is the stagnation point. With $C > 0$ the fluid comes in along the wall at $45°$, where $v_r = -4Cr^3$, and leaves along $\theta = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="Streamlines">

The flow does not cross the curves on which $Cr^4\sin 4\theta$ is constant (the stream function; its level curves are along $\vec v$, since $\vec v\cdot\nabla(r^4\sin 4\theta) = 0$). Solved for $r$, a streamline is $r = (k/\sin 4\theta)^{1/4}$ with a constant $k$. In the narrow corner the streamlines turn more sharply than in (a), and the fluid near the corner moves even more slowly, as $r^3$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\phi$ is the real part of $C(x + iy)^4$, so it solves Laplace's equation. The pattern of (a) and (b): $\phi = Cr^n\cos n\theta$ fits a wedge of angle $180°/n$.

<Solution>

**The idea.** Now the exponent is less than 1, so the wedge is wider than a half-plane and the speed grows towards the corner.

<Step title="Velocity and walls">

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the velocity" tex="v_r = \frac{2C}{3r^{1/3}}\cos\frac{2\theta}{3}, \qquad v_\theta = -\frac{2C}{3r^{1/3}}\sin\frac{2\theta}{3}, \qquad |\vec v| = \frac{2C}{3r^{1/3}}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

$v_\theta = 0$ at $\theta = 0$ and next at $2\theta/3 = 180°$, so $\theta = 270°$. The fluid fills three quarters of the plane, and the solid fills the quarter $270° < \theta < 360°$ ($x > 0$, $y < 0$): this is flow round the outside of a right-angled edge, such as the corner of a building. With $C > 0$ the fluid comes up along the wall $\theta = 270°$ (where $v_r$ is negative, so it moves towards the edge), turns round the edge and leaves along $\theta = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="No stagnation point">

The speed $2C/(3r^{1/3})$ is never zero at finite $r$; at the edge it grows without bound. There is no stagnation point. (A real fluid cannot turn round a sharp edge at infinite speed: viscosity matters there, and the flow separates from the wall behind the edge.)

$\phi$ is the real part of $Cz^{2/3}$ with $z = x + iy$, so it solves Laplace's equation. The wedge angle is $180°/n$ with $n = 2/3$, the rule found in (b). For $n < 1$ the speed diverges at the corner, for $n > 1$ it vanishes there.

## Problem 2

<Exercise id="p2" title="Ideal fluid flow round a cylinder" points={16} mit="Problem 2">

A long solid cylinder of radius $R$ lies along the $z$ axis, at rest in an ideal fluid of density $\rho$ that is incompressible and irrotational. Far upstream, at $x \to -\infty$, the fluid moves with the uniform velocity $\vec u = u\hat x$. Use polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ in the $xy$ plane, with $\theta$ measured from the $+x$ axis, the downstream direction (our choice). Neglect gravity (our choice).

<Part label="a" points={6}>

Solve Laplace's equation for the steady potential flow round the cylinder, and find the velocity field $\vec v$. Make sure the boundary conditions hold. Take the flow without circulation round the cylinder, so that $\phi$ has no term proportional to $\theta$, and fix the constant in $\phi$ so that $\phi = 0$ on the plane $x = 0$ (both our choice). Give $\phi$, $v_r$ and $v_\theta$, typed with `u`, `R`, `r` and `theta`.

<Hint>

There are two boundary conditions: $\vec v \to u\hat x$ far away, and no flow through the surface, $v_r = 0$ at $r = R$. Far away $\phi \approx ux = ur\cos\theta$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Try $\phi = \chi(r)\cos\theta$. Laplace's equation in polar coordinates then allows $\chi = Ar + B/r$. In two dimensions the term $\cos\theta/r$ plays the part that $\cos\theta/r^2$ played for the sphere in the notes.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2a-phi" label="$\phi =$" expr="u*(r + R^2/r)*cos(theta)"
  vars={{ u: [0.5, 2], R: [0.5, 1], r: [1.2, 3], theta: [0.2, 1.3] }} names={{ theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[

<Answer id="p2a-vr" label="$v_r =$" expr="u*(1 - R^2/r^2)*cos(theta)"
  vars={{ u: [0.5, 2], R: [0.5, 1], r: [1.2, 3], theta: [0.2, 1.3] }} names={{ theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "u*(1 + R^2/r^2)*cos(theta)", hint: "The derivative of R²/r with respect to r is −R²/r². With your sign the fluid would cross the surface." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2a-vt" label="$v_\theta =$" expr="-u*(1 + R^2/r^2)*sin(theta)"
  vars={{ u: [0.5, 2], R: [0.5, 1], r: [1.2, 3], theta: [0.2, 1.3] }} names={{ theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "u*(1 + R^2/r^2)*sin(theta)", hint: "Check the sign: the derivative of cos θ is −sin θ. Above the cylinder (θ near 90°) the fluid moves along +x, which is the −θ direction there." },
    { expr: "-u*(1 - R^2/r^2)*sin(theta)", hint: "In v_θ = (1/r)∂φ/∂θ the factor (r + R²/r)/r is 1 + R²/r²; the minus sign belongs to v_r." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Add to the uniform flow the simplest solution of Laplace's equation that decays far away and has the same angular dependence, and fix its strength by the condition on the surface.

<Step title="The general solution">

For $\phi = \chi(r)\cos\theta$, Laplace's equation in polar coordinates, $\frac1r\partial_r(r\,\partial_r\phi) + \frac1{r^2}\partial_\theta^2\phi = 0$, becomes $r^2\chi'' + r\chi' - \chi = 0$, with solutions $\chi = r$ and $\chi = 1/r$. Far away $\phi \to ur\cos\theta$, so $\phi = (ur + B/r)\cos\theta$. (A term $\Gamma\theta/2\pi$ would also be allowed: a circulation round the cylinder, left out here by choice. A constant is fixed by $\phi = 0$ at $x = 0$.)

</Step>

<Step title="The boundary condition">

$v_r = (u - B/r^2)\cos\theta$ must vanish at $r = R$ for every $\theta$, so $B = uR^2$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the potential" tex="\phi = u\left(r + \frac{R^2}{r}\right)\cos\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Problem 2(a): the velocity" tex="v_r = u\left(1 - \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)\cos\theta, \qquad v_\theta = -u\left(1 + \frac{R^2}{r^2}\right)\sin\theta" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

At $r = R$, $v_r = 0$ and $v_\theta = -2u\sin\theta$: the fluid slides along the surface, fastest at the top and bottom ($\theta = \pm 90°$), at twice the speed of the stream. Far away both components tend to those of $u\hat x$: $v_r \to u\cos\theta$, $v_\theta \to -u\sin\theta$. In Cartesian form $\phi = ux\left[1 + R^2/(x^2 + y^2)\right]$, which is $ux$ plus a dipole: $uR^2$ times $\partial_x\ln\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, the $x$ derivative of a source in the plane.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Derive the pressure on the surface of the cylinder. Call the pressure far away $P_\infty$ (our choice), and type the pressure with `Pinf`, `rho`, `u` and `theta`. Then (our addition) take water ($\rho = 1000$ kg/m³) flowing at $u = 1.5$ m/s: what is the lowest pressure on the surface minus $P_\infty$, in pascals? Finally (our addition), choose the net force of the fluid on the cylinder.

<Hint>

The flow is steady and irrotational, so Bernoulli's equation holds between any two points, for example between a point far away and a point on the surface.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p2b-p" label="Pressure on the surface:" expr="Pinf + rho*u^2*(1 - 4*sin(theta)^2)/2"
  vars={{ Pinf: [90000, 110000], rho: [900, 1100], u: [0.5, 2], theta: [0.6, 1.3] }} names={{ Pinf: "P_\\infty", rho: "\\rho", theta: "\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "Pinf + rho*u^2*(1 - sin(theta)^2)/2", hint: "This takes the speed on the surface as u sin θ. Part (a) gives |v_θ| = 2u sin θ at r = R." },
    { expr: "Pinf - rho*u^2*(1 - 4*sin(theta)^2)/2", hint: "Check the sign: where the fluid is faster than u the pressure is lower than P∞." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p2b-min" label="Lowest pressure minus $P_\infty$ (Pa):" value="-3375" unit="Pa" tolerance="1%" calc="p2b-min" />

<Answer id="p2b-force" label="Net force of the fluid on the cylinder:" choices={["along +x, of size about ρu²R per unit length", "none: the pushes cancel", "along −x, of size about ρu²R per unit length", "along +y, a lift"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Bernoulli between infinity and the surface, with the surface speed from part (a).

<Step title="The pressure">

On the surface $v^2 = v_\theta^2 = 4u^2\sin^2\theta$. Bernoulli's equation with $\partial\phi/\partial t = 0$ and no gravity gives $P + \frac12\rho v^2 = P_\infty + \frac12\rho u^2$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(b): the pressure on the cylinder" tex="P(\theta) = P_\infty + \tfrac12\rho u^2\left(1 - 4\sin^2\theta\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Highest and lowest">

At the front and back ($\theta = 180°$ and $0$) the fluid is at rest: these are stagnation points, with $P = P_\infty + \frac12\rho u^2$. The lowest pressure is at the top and bottom, $P = P_\infty - \frac32\rho u^2$. For water at $1.5$ m/s that is $-\frac32 \times 1000 \times 1.5^2 = -3375$ Pa below $P_\infty$.

</Step>

<Step title="The net force">

$P(\theta)$ depends on $\sin^2\theta$ only, which is the same at $\theta$ and $180° - \theta$ (front and back) and at $\theta$ and $-\theta$ (top and bottom). The pushes on mirror points cancel, so the net force is zero: d'Alembert's paradox again, in two dimensions, as the notes found for the sphere. A real fluid exerts a drag because of viscosity.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $\rho u^2$ is a pressure. The average of $1 - 4\sin^2\theta$ over the surface is $-1$, and $\int_0^{2\pi}(1 - 4\sin^2\theta)\cos\theta\,d\theta = 0$: the $x$ component of the force, $-\oint P\cos\theta\,R\,d\theta$, vanishes.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

**The idea.** The slope of a streamline is the ratio of the velocity components; the curves of the family are the level curves of the stream function.

<Step title="The equation">

From $\phi = ux\left[1 + R^2/(x^2 + y^2)\right]$:

<Formula label="Problem 2(c): the velocity in Cartesian form" tex="v_x = u\left[1 - \frac{R^2(x^2 - y^2)}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}\right], \qquad v_y = -\frac{2uR^2xy}{(x^2 + y^2)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="Problem 2(c): the streamlines" tex="\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{v_y}{v_x} = -\frac{2R^2xy}{(x^2 + y^2)^2 - R^2(x^2 - y^2)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 2(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

<Step title="The solution">

Write the given curves as $\psi \equiv y\left(1 - \frac{R^2}{x^2 + y^2}\right) = K$. Along a curve with $\psi$ constant, $dy/dx = -\partial_x\psi/\partial_y\psi$. </Step>

Over the top, at $x = 0$: $y^2(1 - K/y) = R^2$, or $y^2 - Ky - R^2 = 0$, so $y = \frac12\left(K + \sqrt{K^2 + 4R^2}\right)$. For $K = R/2$: $y = \frac12(0.5 + \sqrt{4.25})R = 1.28R$.

</Step>

</Step>

## Problem 5

<Exercise id="p5" title="Vortex shedding by dimensional analysis" points={6} mit="Problem 5">

The notes count the independent dimensionless ratios of a problem as the number of variables minus the number of independent dimensions among them (Section 6.7, p. 134).

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Use dimensional analysis to write a general relation for $f$ in terms of the other quantities. First (our addition) give the number of independent dimensionless ratios, counting the one that contains $f$; then choose the general relation ($F$ and $G$ are unknown functions).

<Hint>

List the dimensions: $[f] = $ 1/s, $[D] = [L] = $ m, $[V] = $ m/s, $[\nu] = $ m²/s. How many independent dimensions appear?

</Hint>

<Hint>

Make $f$ dimensionless with $D$ and $V$, then make $\nu$ and $L$ dimensionless with the same two.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5a-n" label="Number of dimensionless ratios:" value="3" tolerance="1%" calc="p5a-n" />

<Answer id="p5a-rel" label="The general relation:" choices={["fD/V = F(VD/ν)", "fD/V = F(L/D)", "fD/V = F(VD/ν) + G(L/D), a sum of two functions", "fD/V = F(VD/ν, L/D)"]} answer={3} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Five quantities built from two dimensions, metres and seconds, make three independent dimensionless ratios; one of them contains $f$, and it can only be a function of the other two.

<Step title="The ratios">

With $D$ and $V$ as the scales of length and time: $fD/V$ (the Strouhal number), $VD/\nu$ (the Reynolds number) and $L/D$. Five quantities minus two dimensions leaves three:

<Formula label="Problem 5(a): the general relation" tex="\frac{fD}{V} = F\!\left(\frac{VD}{\nu}, \frac{L}{D}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 5(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

with $F$ an unknown function of two variables.

</Step>

<Step title="Why the other choices fail">

Leaving out $L/D$ or $VD/\nu$ assumes that the length or the viscosity does not matter, which dimensional analysis cannot tell. A sum $F + G$ is one special form of a function of two variables, again an assumption. Other sets of ratios, such as $fL/V$ with $VL/\nu$ and $D/L$, are equally right: each is made from the set above.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Each ratio is dimensionless: $[fD/V] = (1/\text{s})\,\text{m}/(\text{m/s}) = 1$, $[VD/\nu] = (\text{m/s})\,\text{m}/(\text{m}^2/\text{s}) = 1$. The notes' rule (count the variables, subtract the dimensions) gives $5 - 2 = 3$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={1}>

On another day a second chimney has half the diameter and half the length of the first. Take the same air on both days, with the same $\nu$ (our choice). How fast must the wind blow at the second chimney, as a multiple $V_2/V_1$ of the wind speed at the first, for a similarity analysis to apply? Give it as a decimal.

<Hint>

Two flows are similar when every dimensionless ratio on the right of (a) is the same for both.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5b-v" label="$V_2/V_1 =$" value="2" tolerance="1%" calc="p5b-v" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Similarity needs the same $L/D$ and the same $VD/\nu$.

<Step title="The wind speed">

$L/D$ is the same, since both lengths are halved. The Reynolds number is the same when $V_2D_2 = V_1D_1$, so $V_2 = V_1D_1/D_2 = 2V_1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The smaller chimney needs a faster wind, so that the air has the same ratio of inertia to viscous friction. With $V$ halved instead, the Reynolds number would be four times too small.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={2}>

With the wind speed of (b), what is the shedding frequency of the second chimney as a multiple $f_2/f_1$ of that of the first? Give it as a decimal.

<Hint>

In similar flows the left-hand side of (a) is the same too.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p5c-f" label="$f_2/f_1 =$" value="4" tolerance="1%" calc="p5c-f" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Equal arguments give an equal value of $F$, so $fD/V$ is the same for both chimneys.

<Step title="The frequency">

$f_2D_2/V_2 = f_1D_1/V_1$, so $f_2/f_1 = (V_2/V_1)(D_1/D_2) = 2 \times 2 = 4$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The time for the wind to pass one diameter, $D/V$, is four times shorter at the second chimney, and in similar flows every time scale scales the same way, the shedding period included.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 6

<Exercise id="p6" title="Golf ball drag by dimensional analysis" points={6} mit="Problem 6">

The drag force $F_D$ on a golf ball depends on its speed $V$, its diameter $D$, its spin rate $\omega$ (in radians per second), the density $\rho$ and viscosity $\eta$ of the air, and the speed of sound $c_S$ (the sheet writes $c_s$).

<Part label="a" points={3}>

Use dimensional analysis to find a general relation for $F_D$ in terms of the other quantities. First (our addition) give the number of independent dimensionless ratios, counting the one that contains $F_D$; then choose the general relation, where "drag" stands for $F_D$ and $F$, $G$, $H$ are unknown functions.

<Hint>

Now three dimensions appear: kilograms, metres and seconds. Use $\rho$, $V$ and $D$ as the scales of mass, length and time.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6a-n" label="Number of dimensionless ratios:" value="4" tolerance="1%" calc="p6a-n" />

<Answer id="p6a-rel" label="The general relation:" choices={["drag/(ρV²D²) = F(ρVD/η, ωD/V)", "drag/(ρV²D²) = F(ρVD/η, ωD/V, V/c_S)", "drag/(ρV²D²) = F(ρVD/η, V/c_S)", "drag/(ρV²D²) = F(ρVD/η) × G(ωD/V) × H(V/c_S), a product"]} answer={1} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Seven quantities and three dimensions make four ratios; the one with the drag is a function of the other three.

<Step title="The ratios">

$\rho V^2D^2$ is a force, so $F_D/(\rho V^2D^2)$ is dimensionless. The others: the Reynolds number $\rho VD/\eta$, the spin ratio $\omega D/V$ (the speed of the surface compared with the speed of the ball, up to a factor 2) and the Mach number $V/c_S$. Seven quantities minus three dimensions leaves four:

<Formula label="Problem 6(a): the general relation" tex="\frac{F_D}{\rho V^2D^2} = F\!\left(\frac{\rho VD}{\eta}, \frac{\omega D}{V}, \frac{V}{c_S}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(a), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$[\eta] = $ kg/(m·s), so $[\rho VD/\eta] = (\text{kg/m}^3)(\text{m/s})\,\text{m}/(\text{kg/(m·s)}) = 1$; $\omega$ in radians per second has the unit 1/s, so $\omega D/V$ is dimensionless. Dropping a ratio (the first and third choices) or fixing the form of the function (the last) assumes more than dimensional analysis gives.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={3}>

Suppose the speed of sound does not matter. How does the relation of (a) change? With this assumption, an experiment is done in a wind tunnel, with the same air (our choice), at speed $2V$. What diameter and spin rate does the ball in the tunnel need for its flow to be similar to the flow round the real ball at speed $V$? Give them as multiples $D'/D$ and $\omega'/\omega$, as decimals. Then (our addition) give the ratio $F_D'/F_D$ of the drag in the tunnel to the drag on the real ball.

<Hint>

Without $c_S$ there are six quantities and three dimensions. Which ratios must then be equal in the two flows?

</Hint>

<Hint>

In similar flows $F_D/(\rho V^2D^2)$ is the same too.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p6b-d" label="$D'/D =$" value="0.5" tolerance="1%" calc="p6b-d" />

<Answer id="p6b-w" label="$\omega'/\omega =$" value="4" tolerance="1%" calc="p6b-w" />

<Answer id="p6b-f" label="$F_D'/F_D =$" value="1" tolerance="1%" calc="p6b-f" />

<Solution>

**The idea.** With the Mach number gone, two ratios fix the flow; matching both fixes the diameter and the spin.

<Step title="The relation">

<Formula label="Problem 6(b): without the speed of sound" tex="\frac{F_D}{\rho V^2D^2} = G\!\left(\frac{\rho VD}{\eta}, \frac{\omega D}{V}\right)" cite="mit" at="Problem 6(b), p. 3 (our own solution)" />

with $G$ an unknown function of two variables: $6 - 3 = 3$ ratios.

</Step>

<Step title="Diameter and spin">

The same air has the same $\rho$ and $\eta$, so an equal Reynolds number needs $V'D' = VD$: $D' = D/2$. An equal spin ratio needs $\omega'D'/V' = \omega D/V$: $\omega' = \omega(V'/V)(D/D') = 4\omega$.

</Step>

<Step title="The drag">

With equal arguments $G$ is the same, so $F_D'/F_D = (V'/V)^2(D'/D)^2 = 4 \times \frac14 = 1$: the half-size ball at twice the speed feels the same drag.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

$\rho VD/\eta$ is unchanged because $V$ doubles while $D$ halves, and $\omega D/V$ because $\omega$ grows by the same factor 4 as $V/D$. The assumption matters: doubling $V$ doubles the Mach number. A golf ball at about 70 m/s has $V/c_S \approx 0.2$ (with $c_S \approx 343$ m/s), so the tunnel at 140 m/s would be at about 0.4, where compressibility starts to show and the assumption needs checking.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>
