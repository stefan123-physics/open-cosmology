So far the constraints in Stewart's notes have been simple relations between coordinates, removed by choosing fewer coordinates. Section 1.4 (notes pp. 16–21) sorts constraints into kinds, introduces virtual displacements and d'Alembert's principle, and uses them to bring forces that do not come from a potential, above all friction, into the Lagrangian method. This page follows the notes in order, in its own words, with its own worked example.

## Holonomic and non-holonomic constraints

A constraint that is a relation among the coordinates, possibly changing with time,

<Formula label="Holonomic constraint" tex="f(q_1, \dots, q_N, t) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.60), p. 16" />

is **holonomic**. The particle in a cone had one, $z - r\cot\alpha = 0$. Every other kind is **non-holonomic** (notes p. 16). Two examples:

- A mass sliding on the outside of a sphere of radius $a$ is held by an **inequality**, $r^2 - a^2 \ge 0$: it may leave the surface.
- A body **rolling without slipping**. The point of contact is momentarily at rest, so the constraint is a condition on **velocities**.

Sometimes a velocity condition can be integrated. For a disk of radius $a$ rolling straight down an incline, the distance travelled and the angle turned obey $a\dot\theta = \dot x$, which integrates to $a\theta = x + \text{const}$, a holonomic constraint. A harder case is a disk standing upright and rolling on a horizontal plane, steering freely (notes pp. 16–17). Its coordinates are $(x, y, \theta, \phi)$: the point of contact $(x, y)$, the heading $\theta$ of the disk in the plane, and the angle $\phi$ it has turned about its own axle. (The disk is assumed never to tip over.) Rolling without slipping means the centre moves at $v = a\dot\phi$ in the direction the disk faces, so

<Formula label="Rolling conditions for the vertical disk" tex="dx - a\sin\theta\,d\phi = 0, \qquad dy + a\cos\theta\,d\phi = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, p. 17" />

which come from $\dot x = a\sin\theta\,\dot\phi$ and $\dot y = -a\cos\theta\,\dot\phi$.

## Semi-holonomic constraints

Constraints of the general linear form

<Formula label="Semi-holonomic constraint" tex="\sum_j a_j(q)\,dq_j + a_t(q)\,dt = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.61), p. 17" />

are not holonomic in general; following Goldstein, the notes call them **semi-holonomic**. A holonomic constraint can always be written this way, by taking its differential:

<Formula label="A holonomic constraint in differential form" tex="df = \sum_j\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_j}\,dq_j + \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}\,dt = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.62), p. 17" />

so then $a_j = \partial f/\partial q_j$ and $a_t = \partial f/\partial t$. Mixed partial derivatives do not depend on their order, so such coefficients must satisfy

<Formula label="Conditions for an exact differential" tex="\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial q_i} = \frac{\partial a_i}{\partial q_j}, \qquad \frac{\partial a_t}{\partial q_i} = \frac{\partial a_i}{\partial t}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.63), p. 18" />

If a semi-holonomic constraint passes these tests, it is secretly holonomic. In mathematical language, a holonomic constraint is an **exact** differential form $df$, and the form in eq. (1.61) need not be exact.

Passing the test after multiplying by some function $h$ (an integrating factor) would do too. The vertical disk shows that this can fail (notes p. 18). Take its first condition times $h(x, y, \theta, \phi)$: the coefficients are $a_x = h$, $a_\phi = -a\sin\theta\,h$, $a_\theta = a_y = 0$. The test $\partial a_\phi/\partial\theta = \partial a_\theta/\partial\phi$ requires $a\cos\theta\,h + a\sin\theta\,\partial h/\partial\theta = 0$, so $h = k/\sin\theta$ for some $k$ independent of $\theta$. (The notes drop the factor $h$ from the first term of this condition.) But the test $\partial a_x/\partial\theta = \partial a_\theta/\partial x$ requires $\partial h/\partial\theta = 0$, which this $h$ violates unless $k = 0$. No integrating factor exists, and the constraint is truly non-holonomic. Physically: by steering, the disk can reach any position with any heading and any $\phi$, so no relation among the four coordinates holds.

If instead the disk is made to roll along a fixed line, say along $\hat x$ with $\theta = \pi/2$, the conditions become $\dot x = a\dot\phi$ and $\dot y = 0$. These integrate to $x = a\phi + x_0$, $y = y_0$, $\theta = \pi/2$: a set of holonomic constraints.

## Virtual displacements and d'Alembert's principle

A **virtual displacement** $\delta\vec r_i$ of particle $i$ (notes p. 18) is

- infinitesimal,
- consistent with the constraints, and
- made at a fixed time, so that a constraint that changes with time is frozen while it is made.

For example, take a bead on a straight wire along the $x$ axis that is itself carried along $y$ at speed $v_0$, $y = v_0 t$. A virtual displacement of the bead is always along $\hat x$, since the wire does not move during it. A real displacement $d\vec r$ in time $dt$ also has a part $v_0\,dt$ along $\hat y$.

For many constraints the constraint force $\vec Z_i$ is perpendicular to every allowed $\delta\vec r_i$, so its **virtual work** $\vec Z_i\cdot\delta\vec r_i$ vanishes, by analogy with the work $\int\vec F\cdot d\vec r$. More generally the constraints do no net virtual work, $\sum_i\vec Z_i\cdot\delta\vec r_i = 0$ (notes p. 19); this holds for surfaces, for rolling and for similar cases. Writing Newton's law as $\dot{\vec p}_i = \vec F_i + \vec Z_i$, with $\vec F_i$ the other forces, the constraint forces drop out:

<Formula label="D'Alembert's principle" tex="\sum_i\left(\dot{\vec p}_i - \vec F_i\right)\cdot\delta\vec r_i = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.64), p. 19" />

This is **d'Alembert's principle**. It could serve instead of Hamilton's principle as the starting point of mechanics.

## Into generalized coordinates

To use generalized coordinates, write $\vec r_i = \vec r_i(q, t)$. At fixed time, $\delta\vec r_i = \frac{\partial\vec r_i}{\partial q_j}\delta q_j$ (summed over $j$). The force term becomes

<Formula label="Virtual work of the applied forces" tex="\vec F_i\cdot\delta\vec r_i = \vec F_i\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_i}{\partial q_j}\,\delta q_j \equiv Q_j\,\delta q_j" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.65), p. 19" />

which defines the **generalized forces**

<Formula label="Generalized force" tex="Q_j \equiv \vec F_i\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_i}{\partial q_j}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.66), p. 19" />

(summed over $i$). A generalized force need not be a force: for an angle it is a torque. The momentum term takes more work. Two facts help: the cancellation of dots, $\partial\vec v_i/\partial\dot q_j = \partial\vec r_i/\partial q_j$, and the fact that a time derivative and a $q_j$ derivative of $\vec r_i(q, t)$ can be taken in either order, $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial\vec r_i}{\partial q_j} = \frac{\partial\vec v_i}{\partial q_j}$. Moving the time derivative off $m_i\ddot{\vec r}_i$ by the product rule and then using both facts,

<Formula label="The momentum term" tex="\sum_i\dot{\vec p}_i\cdot\delta\vec r_i = \sum_{i,j}\left[\frac{d}{dt}\left(m_i\vec v_i\cdot\frac{\partial\vec v_i}{\partial\dot q_j}\right) - m_i\vec v_i\cdot\frac{\partial\vec v_i}{\partial q_j}\right]\delta q_j = \sum_j\left[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_j}\right) - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_j}\right]\delta q_j" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.67), p. 19" />

with $T = \frac12\sum_i m_i v_i^2$. D'Alembert's principle becomes

<Formula label="D'Alembert's principle in generalized coordinates" tex="\sum_j\left[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_j}\right) - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_j} - Q_j\right]\delta q_j = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.68), p. 19" />

which, as we will see, contains the Euler–Lagrange equations as a special case.

Suppose for now that all constraints are holonomic (other kinds wait for Section 1.5). Then we can choose $N - k$ coordinates that satisfy the $k$ constraints automatically, their virtual displacements $\delta q_j$ are independent, and each bracket must vanish on its own:

<Formula label="Lagrange's equations with generalized forces" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_j}\right) - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_j} - Q_j = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.69), p. 20" />

## Three special cases

1. **Conservative forces**, $\vec F_i = -\nabla_i V$ with $V = V(q, t)$. Then

<Formula label="Generalized force from a potential" tex="Q_j = -\left(\nabla_i V\right)\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_i}{\partial q_j} = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial q_j}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.70), p. 20" />

and with $L = T - V$, eq. (1.69) is exactly the Euler–Lagrange equation. (In eq. (1.70) the notes drop the $\partial$ in the numerator of $\partial\vec r_i/\partial q_j$.)

2. **Velocity-dependent potentials.** If $Q_j = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial q_j} + \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial V}{\partial\dot q_j}$ for some $V(q, \dot q, t)$, as for the magnetic part of the Lorentz force, the Euler–Lagrange equations with $L = T - V$ come out again.

3. **Everything else.** If, besides forces of either kind above, there are generalized forces $R_j$ that come from no potential, they stay on the right:

<Formula label="Euler–Lagrange with non-conservative forces" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_j}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} = R_j" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.71), p. 20" />

## Friction and the dissipation function

Friction is the main example of such forces (notes p. 20). The usual models:

- **Static friction**: $F_s \le F_s^{\max} = \mu_s F_N$, with $F_N$ the normal force.
- **Sliding friction**: $\vec F = -\mu F_N\,\vec v/v$, a force of fixed size opposite to the motion (and zero without motion).
- **Rolling friction**: $\vec F = -\mu_R F_N\,\vec v/v$, of the same form with its own coefficient.
- **Fluid friction at low speed**: $\vec F = -bv\,\vec v/v = -b\vec v$.

All the moving ones have the form $\vec F_i = -h_i(v_i)\,\vec v_i/v_i$ (no sum on $i$). Their generalized forces (notes p. 21) are

<Formula label="Generalized friction force" tex="R_j = -\sum_i h_i\,\frac{\vec v_i}{v_i}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec r_i}{\partial q_j} = -\sum_i h_i\,\frac{\vec v_i}{v_i}\cdot\frac{\partial\vec v_i}{\partial\dot q_j}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.72), p. 21" />

(the notes write $\partial\vec r_j/\partial q_j$ in the middle, a typo for $\partial\vec r_i/\partial q_j$). Since $\vec v_i\cdot\partial\vec v_i/\partial\dot q_j = \frac12\partial(v_i^2)/\partial\dot q_j = v_i\,\partial v_i/\partial\dot q_j$, each term is $h_i(v_i)\,\partial v_i/\partial\dot q_j$, which is the $\dot q_j$ derivative of $\int_0^{v_i}h_i(v')\,dv'$. Hence

<Formula label="Friction from the dissipation function" tex="R_j = -\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\dot q_j}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.73), p. 21" />

<Formula label="Dissipation function" tex="\mathcal{F} = \sum_i\int_0^{v_i}dv'\,h_i(v')" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.74), p. 21" />

$\mathcal{F}$, the **dissipation function**, is a single scalar like $L$, which makes it easy to carry into any coordinates.

**A sphere sinking through a viscous fluid** (notes p. 21). Let a sphere of radius $a$ fall vertically with height $y$. The notes take $T = \frac12 m'\dot y^2$ and $V = m'gy$, where $m' < m$ is the mass minus the mass of the displaced fluid, which accounts for buoyancy by Archimedes' principle. (Strictly, buoyancy reduces only the weight, while the sphere's inertia is its full mass plus some fluid dragged along; the simplification changes how quickly the motion settles, not the final speed.) The drag of a viscous fluid of viscosity $\eta$ is proportional to the speed, $h = 6\pi\eta a\,v$, so $\mathcal{F} = 3\pi\eta a\,\dot y^2$, and $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot y} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\dot y}$ gives

<Formula label="Sphere falling with Stokes drag" tex="m'\ddot y + m'g = -6\pi\eta a\,\dot y" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, p. 21" />

The drag $6\pi\eta a\dot y$ is **Stokes' law**, derived from the fluid equations much later in the course. The equation is linear: add a particular solution, the constant velocity $\dot y_p = -m'g/(6\pi\eta a)$, to the solution of the homogeneous equation $m'\ddot y_H + 6\pi\eta a\,\dot y_H = 0$, which is $\dot y_H = A\,e^{-6\pi\eta a t/m'}$, with $A$ fixed by the starting velocity. One more integration gives $y(t)$.

**Sliding friction on two masses** (notes p. 21). For two masses on a table joined by a spring (a Problem Set 1 system), sliding friction has $h_i = \mu_f m_i g$, so

<Formula label="Dissipation function for sliding friction" tex="\mathcal{F} = \mu_f g\left(m_1 v_1 + m_2 v_2\right) = \mu_f g\left(m_1\sqrt{\dot x_1^2 + \dot y_1^2} + m_2\sqrt{\dot x_2^2 + \dot y_2^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.4, eq. (1.75), p. 21" />

Rewriting it in coordinates that suit the frictionless problem (such as the centre of mass and the separation) and taking $R_j = -\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial\dot q_j$ adds the friction to those equations of motion. (The notes leave the details to the lecture.)

<WorkedExample title="A steel ball in syrup">
<Problem>
A steel ball of radius $a = 2.0$ mm and density $7.8\ \text{g/cm}^3$ is released from rest in a syrup of density $1.3\ \text{g/cm}^3$ and viscosity $\eta = 1.5$ Pa s. Using the equation above, find its terminal speed and how quickly it gets there.
</Problem>
<Step title="Masses">
The volume is $\tfrac{4}{3}\pi a^3 = 3.35\times10^{-8}\ \text{m}^3$, so $m = 2.61\times10^{-4}$ kg, the displaced syrup has mass $4.36\times10^{-5}$ kg, and $m' = 2.18\times10^{-4}$ kg.
</Step>
<Step title="Terminal speed">
The drag coefficient is $6\pi\eta a = 6\pi \times 1.5 \times 0.0020 = 0.0565$ kg/s. Setting $\ddot y = 0$, the speed is $m'g/(6\pi\eta a) = 2.14\times10^{-3}/0.0565 = 0.0378$ m/s, about 3.8 cm/s downwards.
</Step>
<Step title="Time scale">
The homogeneous solution decays with the time constant $\tau = m'/(6\pi\eta a) = 3.9$ ms, so after a few hundredths of a second the ball falls at its terminal speed. The slow start leaves it behind a ball that had moved at terminal speed from the beginning by only $v\tau = 0.15$ mm.
</Step>
<Step title="Is Stokes drag right here?">
The linear drag law needs slow flow. The Reynolds number, the syrup's density times the speed times the diameter divided by the viscosity, is $1300 \times 0.0378 \times 0.0040/1.5 = 0.13$, well below 1, so it is. (The Reynolds number is introduced in Section 6.7 of the notes.)
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

<Callout kind="cosmology" title="Hubble drag">
A friction force proportional to the velocity turns up in cosmology too. In 8.902 Lecture 12 the inflaton field obeys $\ddot\phi + 3H\dot\phi = -dV/d\phi$: a particle rolling in the potential $V$, slowed by a drag term $3H\dot\phi$ that the expansion of the universe supplies. ## Summary

- Holonomic constraints are relations $f(q, t) = 0$; inequalities and velocity conditions such as rolling are non-holonomic.
- Semi-holonomic constraints $\sum_j a_j\,dq_j + a_t\,dt = 0$ are holonomic only if they are exact (or can be made exact by a factor); the steerable rolling disk is not, rolling along a line is.
- Virtual displacements are taken at fixed time; constraint forces do no net virtual work, which gives d'Alembert's principle $\sum_i(\dot{\vec p}_i - \vec F_i)\cdot\delta\vec r_i = 0$.
- In generalized coordinates: $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot q_j} - \frac{\partial T}{\partial q_j} = Q_j$, with $Q_j = \vec F_i\cdot\partial\vec r_i/\partial q_j$; forces from a potential, even velocity-dependent, give the Euler–Lagrange equations, and the rest appear as $R_j$.
- Friction $-h(v)\,\vec v/v$ gives $R_j = -\partial\mathcal{F}/\partial\dot q_j$ with the dissipation function $\mathcal{F} = \sum_i\int_0^{v_i}h_i\,dv'$; Stokes drag on a falling sphere is the standard example.
