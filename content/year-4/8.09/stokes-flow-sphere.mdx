[Section 6.4](/courses/8-09/potential-flow) found that an ideal fluid exerts no drag on a sphere. This section solves the same problem for a viscous fluid and resolves that puzzle (pp. 135–141). Take a steady, incompressible, viscous flow past a sphere of radius $a$, with velocity $\vec u$ far away and a small [Reynolds number](/courses/8-09/viscous-pipe-flow-reynolds); to make the problem solvable, the notes work in the limit $R \ll 1$ (p. 135). The notes' figures are not reproduced; the worked example and the plot are our own.

## Slow viscous flow

The steady Navier-Stokes equation is (p. 135)

<Formula label="Steady Navier-Stokes equation" tex="\vec v\cdot\nabla\vec v = -\frac{\nabla P}{\rho} + \frac{\eta}{\rho}\,\nabla^2\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.135), p. 135" />

Compare its two velocity terms, measuring lengths by $a$ and speeds by $u$ (p. 135):

<Formula label="Sizes of the two velocity terms" tex="\vec v\cdot\nabla\vec v = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{u^2}{a}\right), \qquad \frac{\eta}{\rho}\,\nabla^2\vec v = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\eta u}{\rho a^2}\right) = \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{u^2}{aR}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.136), p. 135" />

The friction term is larger by a factor $1/R$ and dominates. Dropping the advective term (p. 135):

<Formula label="Stokes' equation" tex="\nabla P = \eta\nabla^2\vec v" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.137), p. 135" />

Written with $\eta$, the density has dropped out, so it plays no part in the solution (p. 135). The divergence of (6.137) gives $\nabla^2P = \eta\nabla^2(\nabla\cdot\vec v) = 0$: the pressure obeys **Laplace's equation**.

## Pressure and vorticity

Dimensional analysis fixes the form of the pressure (p. 135). It may contain only one power of $\vec u$ and must be a scalar, so a factor $\vec u\cdot\vec r$ appears; $R$ is not an argument, since we expand in $R \ll 1$:

<Formula label="Form of the pressure" tex="P = \frac{\eta\,\vec u\cdot\vec r}{a^2}\,g\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.138), p. 135" />

A solution of Laplace's equation proportional to $\vec u\cdot\vec r$ that dies away far from the sphere is the **dipole** already met for the potential in the ideal-fluid problem (p. 135):

<Formula label="Dipole pressure" tex="P = P_0 + k\,\frac{\eta a\,\vec u\cdot\vec r}{r^3}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.139), p. 135" />

with $P_0$ the pressure far away and $k$ a dimensionless constant still to be found. (The $g$ here is not the $g$ of the velocity below; the notes reuse the letter.)

The vorticity follows from the identity $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec v) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec v) - \nabla^2\vec v$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec v = 0$ and (6.137) (p. 135):

<Formula label="Curl of the vorticity" tex="\nabla\times\vec\Omega = \nabla\times\left(\nabla\times\vec v\right) = \nabla\left(\nabla\cdot\vec v\right) - \nabla^2\vec v = -\nabla^2\vec v = -\frac{1}{\eta}\,\nabla P = -\frac{ka}{r^3}\left(\vec u - \frac{3(\vec u\cdot\vec r)\,\vec r}{r^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.140), p. 135" />

Requiring $\vec\Omega \to 0$ far away, the solution is (p. 136)

<Formula label="Vorticity of Stokes flow" tex="\vec\Omega = ka\,\frac{\vec u\times\vec r}{r^3}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.141), p. 136" />

Unlike the ideal fluid, the viscous flow carries **vorticity** (p. 136).

## The velocity

The velocity can depend only on $\vec r$, $\vec u$ and $a$ (again not on $R$), and by dimensional analysis it is linear in $\vec u$. The most general form is (p. 136)

<Formula label="Form of the velocity" tex="\vec v = \vec u\,f\left(\frac{r}{a}\right) + \frac{\vec r\,(\vec u\cdot\vec r)}{a^2}\,g\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.142), p. 136" />

with dimensionless functions $f$ and $g$ (primes below are derivatives with respect to $r/a$). Since $\nabla f(r/a) = (\vec r/ar)f'(r/a)$, incompressibility reads (p. 136)

<Formula label="Incompressibility" tex="0 = \nabla\cdot\vec v = \frac{\vec u\cdot\vec r}{ar}\,f' + \frac{(\vec u\cdot\vec r)\,r}{a^3}\,g' + \frac{4(\vec u\cdot\vec r)}{a^2}\,g" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.143), p. 136" />

so (p. 136)

<Formula label="Relation between f and g" tex="f'\left(\frac{r}{a}\right) = -\frac{r^2}{a^2}\,g'\left(\frac{r}{a}\right) - \frac{4r}{a}\,g\left(\frac{r}{a}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.144), p. 136" />

Next, set $\nabla^2\vec v$ equal to $\nabla P/\eta$ (p. 136). Both sides are combinations of the two vectors $\vec u$ and $(\vec u\cdot\vec r)\vec r$:

<Formula label="Laplacian of the velocity and the pressure gradient" tex="\nabla^2\vec v = \left(\nabla^2f + \frac{2g}{a^2}\right)\vec u + \left(\nabla^2g + \frac{4g'}{ar}\right)\frac{(\vec u\cdot\vec r)\,\vec r}{a^2} = \frac{1}{\eta}\,\nabla P = \frac{ka}{r^3}\,\vec u + \left(-\frac{3ka^3}{r^5}\right)\frac{(\vec u\cdot\vec r)\,\vec r}{a^2}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.145), p. 136" />

where $\nabla^2g(r/a) = (1/r^2)(d/dr)\,r^2(d/dr)\,g(r/a)$. Matching the coefficients of each vector (p. 136):

<Formula label="Equations for f and g" tex="\nabla^2g + \frac{4g'}{ar} = \frac{g''}{a^2} + \frac{6g'}{ar} = -\frac{3ka^3}{r^5}, \qquad \frac{f''}{a^2} + \frac{2f'}{ar} + \frac{2g}{a^2} = \frac{ka}{r^3}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.146), p. 136" />

For $g$ try powers, $g(x) = C_nx^n$ with $x = r/a$ (p. 136):

<Formula label="Powers in the equation for g" tex="C_n\left[n(n - 1) + 6n\right]x^{n-2} = -3kx^{-5}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.147), p. 136" />

$n = -3$ is a particular solution, with $C_{-3} = k/2$; $n = 0$ and $n = -5$ make the bracket vanish and solve the equation without the right side, with coefficients set by boundary conditions (pp. 136–137). Looking back at (6.142), the boundary conditions are (p. 137)

<Formula label="Boundary conditions" tex="\vec v = \vec u\ \text{at}\ r = \infty: \quad r^2g(r/a) \to 0, \quad f(r/a) \to 1; \qquad \vec v = 0\ \text{at}\ r = a: \quad g(1) = 0, \quad f(1) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.148), p. 137" />

They give $C_0 = 0$ and $C_{-5} = -k/2$, so (p. 137)

<Formula label="The function g" tex="g\left(\frac{r}{a}\right) = \frac{ka^3}{2r^3}\left(1 - \frac{a^2}{r^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.149), p. 137" />

Putting $g$ into (6.144) and integrating once gives (p. 137)

<Formula label="The function f" tex="f\left(\frac{r}{a}\right) = 1 + \frac{ka}{2r} + \frac{ka^3}{6r^3}" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.150), p. 137" />

[The notes set the integration constant to zero and then impose "$f(1) = 1$". The condition $f \to 1$ at infinity needs the constant to be 1, and no slip on the sphere is $f(1) = 0$; that gives $1 + k/2 + k/6 = 0$, the notes' value $k = -3/2$. With their version, $k$ would come out as $+3/2$.] So $k = -3/2$, which also fixes the pressure and vorticity above (p. 137). The full solution is (p. 137)

<Formula label="Stokes' solution" tex="\vec v = \vec u\left(1 - \frac{3a}{4r} - \frac{a^3}{4r^3}\right) - \frac{3a^3}{4r^3}\,\frac{\vec r\,(\vec u\cdot\vec r)}{a^2}\left(1 - \frac{a^2}{r^2}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.151), p. 137" />

The stream lines curve round the sphere symmetrically, front and back (p. 137). But the disturbance reaches very far: the velocity returns to $\vec u$ only as $1/r$, much more slowly than the $1/r^3$ of the ideal dipole flow (our comparison).

## Stokes' drag

The force of the fluid on the body in direction $j$ is the surface integral of the force per unit area (6.117) (p. 137):

<Formula label="Drag force" tex="F_{Dj} = \int_{\partial V} dS_i\left(P\delta_{ij} - \sigma_{ij}\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.152), p. 137" />

(here the surface element of the fluid's boundary points into the sphere, so the sign matches (6.117)). In practice one uses the sphere's own outward normal $\hat r$ and writes the integrand directly as the force per area (6.117), $-P\hat r + \sigma_{rr}\hat r + \sigma_{r\theta}\hat\theta$, as below. Let the flow far away be $u\hat x$, so $\hat x\cdot\vec F_D$ is the drag along the flow. In spherical coordinates with polar angle $\theta$ from $\hat x$, the sphere's surface element is $d\vec S = \hat r\,a^2\,d\cos\theta\,d\phi$, with $\hat r\cdot\hat x = \cos\theta$ and $\hat\theta\cdot\hat x = -\sin\theta$ (p. 137):

<Formula label="Drag along the flow" tex="\hat x\cdot\vec F_D = a^2\int d\cos\theta\,d\phi\left(-P\cos\theta + \sigma_{rr}\cos\theta - \sigma_{r\theta}\sin\theta\right)" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.153), p. 137" />

On the sphere $r = a$, the needed pieces are (p. 138)

<Formula label="Stresses and pressure on the sphere" tex="\sigma_{rr} = 2\eta\,\frac{\partial v_r}{\partial r}\ \text{at}\ r = a, \ = 0, \qquad \sigma_{r\theta} = \eta\left(\frac{1}{r}\,\frac{\partial v_r}{\partial\theta} + \frac{\partial v_\theta}{\partial r} - \frac{v_\theta}{r}\right)\ \text{at}\ r = a, \ = -\left(\frac{3\eta}{2a}\right)u\sin\theta, \qquad P = P_0 - \left(\frac{3\eta}{2a}\right)u\cos\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.154), p. 138" />

The constant $P_0$ integrates to zero, and both remaining terms are $(3\eta u/2a)$ times $\cos^2\theta$ or $\sin^2\theta$, which add to 1. So the drag is (p. 138)

<Formula label="Stokes' formula" tex="\hat x\cdot\vec F_D = \left(\frac{3\eta u}{2a}\right)\int d\cos\theta\,d\phi\,a^2 = \left(6\pi\eta a\right)u" cite="reading1" at="Section 6.8, eq. (6.155), p. 138" />

**Stokes' formula** for the viscous drag on a sphere. The combination $\eta au$ could have been found by dimensional analysis alone (p. 138); the $6\pi$ needs the solution. (Of the $6\pi$, the pressure gives $2\pi$ and the friction $4\pi$; our split.) Besides drag along the flow, bodies can feel **lift**, a force across the flow, which matters for wing shapes and aircraft (p. 138). [The notes say lift is "tangential" to the flow direction; lift acts perpendicular to the oncoming flow.]

<WorkedExample title="How fast does a cloud droplet fall?">
<Problem>
A water droplet of radius $a$ = 10 μm (density 1000 kg/m³) falls through still air with $\eta = 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$ Pa s and density 1.2 kg/m³. Find its terminal speed using Stokes' drag, and check that $R \ll 1$.
</Problem>
<Step title="Terminal speed">
At terminal speed the weight balances the drag (the air's buoyancy is about 0.1% of the weight and is neglected): $\tfrac43\pi a^3\rho g = 6\pi\eta av$, so $v = 2a^2\rho g/9\eta = 2 \times 10^{-10} \times 1000 \times 9.81/(9 \times 1.8 \times 10^{-5}) \approx 0.0121$ m/s, about 1.2 cm/s. Cloud droplets drift down slowly, which is why clouds float.
</Step>
<Step title="Reynolds number">
$R = \rho_{\text{air}}va/\eta = 1.2 \times 0.0121 \times 10^{-5}/(1.8 \times 10^{-5}) \approx 0.008$, far below 1, so Stokes' law is a good approximation. A droplet 100 times larger would fall much faster and leave the Stokes regime.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Eddies, wakes and turbulence

What happens to the flow round the fixed sphere as $R$ grows (pp. 138–140)? The dropped term $\vec v\cdot\nabla\vec v$ eventually matters, and so does $\partial\vec v/\partial t$: the flow starts to change with time. The notes describe the stages:

- $R \ll 1$: the flow is symmetric and, perhaps surprisingly, its stream lines look qualitatively like those of the $\eta \to 0$ case. This is **Stokes flow**, a **laminar** flow (p. 138).
- $R \approx 1$: still like Stokes flow, but less symmetric, with a clearer **wake** behind the sphere (p. 138).
- $10 \lesssim R \lesssim 100$: detached vortices, **eddies**, sit behind the sphere, though the flow is still steady. Between them, right behind the sphere, the fluid flows backwards, against $\vec u$. Towards the top of the range the flow stops being steady and the vortices begin to oscillate (p. 139).
- $150 \lesssim R \lesssim 1000$: vortices are shed one after another and drift downstream in a wake, each pair seeming to push the next along (p. 139).
- $10^3 \lesssim R \lesssim 2 \times 10^5$: the wake becomes highly irregular, **turbulent**, with unsteady interacting vortices on all length scales (p. 139).
- $R \gtrsim 2 \times 10^5$: the turbulent wake narrows, and the thin **boundary layer** round the sphere itself becomes turbulent (p. 140).

**Turbulence** is flow that (p. 140): is highly irregular, technically chaotic, in space and/or time; has non-zero vorticity; dissipates energy much faster than laminar flow; and has eddies on many scales, with energy (in three dimensions) passed from the largest eddies down to the smallest, where viscosity turns it into heat. Turbulent mixing transports energy, momentum and heat very effectively. Everyday examples abound: the circulation of the atmosphere, a tap that turns white when opened further, rapids, dust raised by wind, the water beside a moving ship, clear-air turbulence that costs an aircraft lift (p. 140).

The turbulent boundary layer at the largest $R$ brings an abrupt **drop in drag**, which is useful (p. 140). Surface roughness makes the boundary layer turbulent at smaller $R$, that is at lower speeds: this is why golf balls have dimples and baseballs and tennis balls have raised seams (p. 140).

Pipes become turbulent too at large $R$ (pp. 140–141). The parabolic profiles of [Section 6.7](/courses/8-09/viscous-pipe-flow-reynolds) are laminar flows at smaller $R$; a turbulent flow is irregular on small scales but, averaged, flows more uniformly across the pipe. Because it is chaotic and irregular, turbulence is hard to treat analytically, and a complete description of it remains an unsolved problem (p. 141).

## Summary

- For $R \ll 1$ the viscous term beats the advective one by $1/R$, leaving **Stokes' equation** $\nabla P = \eta\nabla^2\vec v$; the density drops out and $\nabla^2P = 0$.
- Dimensional analysis and linearity give a dipole pressure $P_0 + k\eta a\,\vec u\cdot\vec r/r^3$, a vorticity $ka\,\vec u\times\vec r/r^3$ and $\vec v = \vec u f + \vec r(\vec u\cdot\vec r)g/a^2$; no slip fixes $k = -3/2$ and the Stokes solution.
- **Stokes' drag:** $F = 6\pi\eta au$, the drag the ideal fluid missed.
- As $R$ grows: wake, steady eddies, shed vortices, turbulent wake, turbulent boundary layer (with lower drag, hence dimpled golf balls). **Turbulence** is chaotic, vortical, strongly dissipative and multi-scale, and still not fully understood.
