The last section of Chapter 1 of Stewart's notes (pp. 22–28) has two parts: a review of the calculus of variations, which turns Hamilton's principle into equations of motion, and the method of Lagrange multipliers. This page covers the first part (notes pp. 22–23), and the [next page](/courses/8-09/lagrange-multipliers-constraint-forces) the second. The same mathematics appears in 8.033 as [geodesics and the calculus of variations](/courses/8-033/geodesics-calculus-of-variations); here it is set up for any number of functions, in the notes' order and in this page's own words, with its own worked example.

## Making an integral stationary

The calculus of variations looks for functions $y_i(s)$, $i = 1, \dots, n$, between two values $s_1$ and $s_2$ of a parameter, that make a **functional** (a number that depends on whole functions) stationary:

<Formula label="A functional" tex="J[y_i] = \int_{s_1}^{s_2} ds\, f\big(y_1(s), \dots, y_n(s), \dot y_1(s), \dots, \dot y_n(s), s\big)" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.76), p. 22" />

In this general discussion only, the dot means $d/ds$, not $d/dt$. Compare the path $y_i(s)$ with a neighbouring one, $y_i'(s) = y_i(s) + \eta_i(s)$, where the change $\delta y_i = \eta_i$ vanishes at both ends, $\eta_i(s_1) = \eta_i(s_2) = 0$: the end points stay fixed and only the path between them moves. To first order the change in $J$ is

<Formula label="First-order variation" tex="0 = \delta J = \int_{s_1}^{s_2} ds \sum_i\left[\delta y_i\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial y_i} + \delta\dot y_i\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial \dot y_i}\right]" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.77), p. 22" />

Since $\delta\dot y_i$ is the derivative of $\delta y_i$, integrating the second term by parts moves the derivative onto $\partial f/\partial\dot y_i$. The boundary term contains $\delta y_i$ at $s_1$ and $s_2$, which is zero. What is left is $\int ds\,\sum_i\left[\frac{\partial f}{\partial y_i} - \frac{d}{ds}\frac{\partial f}{\partial\dot y_i}\right]\delta y_i(s)$, and if the $\delta y_i$ are independent (for instance once holonomic constraints have been used to remove the dependent coordinates) and arbitrary, this vanishes only if every bracket does:

<Formula label="Euler–Lagrange equation for a functional" tex="\frac{\partial f}{\partial y_i} - \frac{d}{ds}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \dot y_i}\right) = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.78), p. 22" />

The result holds for any integrand $f$, not only in mechanics.

**Hamilton's principle** is the case $s = t$, $y_i = q_i$, $f = L$ and $J = S$: requiring $\delta S = 0$ gives the Euler–Lagrange equations of motion.

## Great circles

A geometric example (notes pp. 22–23): the shortest path between two points on a sphere of radius $a$ is an arc of a great circle. The length of a path is $J = \int_{s_1}^{s_2} ds$, and on the sphere ($dr = 0$, spherical angles $\theta$ and $\phi$)

<Formula label="Arc length on a sphere" tex="ds = \sqrt{(dx)^2 + (dy)^2 + (dz)^2} = \sqrt{a^2(d\theta)^2 + a^2\sin^2\theta\,(d\phi)^2}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.79), p. 22" />

Using $\theta$ as the parameter and $\phi(\theta)$ as the unknown function,

<Formula label="Arc length with θ as parameter" tex="ds = a\sqrt{1 + \sin^2\theta\left(\frac{d\phi}{d\theta}\right)^2}\;d\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.80), p. 22" />

so $f = \sqrt{1 + \sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2}$ (dropping the constant $a$, with $\dot\phi = d\phi/d\theta$). $f$ does not contain $\phi$, so the Euler–Lagrange equation says $\partial f/\partial\dot\phi$ is constant along the path. Some algebra then gives the solutions

<Formula label="Great circles" tex="\sin(\phi - \alpha) = \beta\cot\theta" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, p. 23" />

with constants $\alpha$ and $\beta$. (Multiplying by $a\sin\theta$ turns this into a linear relation among $x$, $y$ and $z$, the equation of a plane through the centre of the sphere, which cuts it in a great circle.)

<WorkedExample title="Flying between two cities at 60° N">
<Problem>
Two cities lie on the parallel of latitude 60° N, 90° apart in longitude. Compare the length of the route along the parallel with the great-circle route, on a sphere of radius $a = 6371$ km, and find how far north the great circle goes.
</Problem>
<Step title="Along the parallel">
Latitude 60° is colatitude $\theta = 30^\circ$. Along the parallel $d\theta = 0$, so eq. (1.79) gives $ds = a\sin\theta\,d\phi$ and the length is $a\sin 30^\circ \times \pi/2 = 0.785a = 5004$ km. This path does not satisfy the great-circle equation: a parallel other than the equator is not a great circle.
</Step>
<Step title="The great circle">
Put the two cities at $\phi = 0$ and $\phi = 90^\circ$, both at $\theta = 30^\circ$, into $\sin(\phi - \alpha) = \beta\cot\theta$: $-\sin\alpha = \beta\sqrt3$ and $\cos\alpha = \beta\sqrt3$. So $\alpha = -45^\circ$ and $\beta = \cos 45^\circ/\sqrt3 = 0.408$. The angle $c$ between the two cities seen from the centre follows from the cosine rule of spherical trigonometry (or from the dot product of the two position vectors): $\cos c = \cos^2 30^\circ + \sin^2 30^\circ\cos 90^\circ = 0.75$, so $c = 0.723$ rad and the route is $0.723a = 4605$ km, about 400 km shorter.
</Step>
<Step title="How far north">
The path is closest to the pole where $\sin(\phi - \alpha) = 1$, at $\phi = 45^\circ$. There $\cot\theta = 1/\beta$, so $\theta = \arctan 0.408 = 22.2^\circ$: latitude 67.8° N, nearly 8° north of the two cities.
</Step>
</WorkedExample>

## Hamilton's equations from a variational principle

Hamilton's principle can also be set up in phase space (notes p. 23). Treat the $2N$ functions $q_1, \dots, q_N, p_1, \dots, p_N$ as independent and require

<Formula label="Hamilton's principle in phase space" tex="\delta J[q, p] = \delta\int_{t_1}^{t_2} dt\left[p_i\dot q_i - H(q, p, t)\right] = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.81), p. 23" />

with all variations zero at the ends: $\delta q_i(t_1) = \delta q_i(t_2) = 0$ and $\delta p_i(t_1) = \delta p_i(t_2) = 0$. With $f = p_i\dot q_i - H$, the Euler–Lagrange equations for the two kinds of variable are

<Formula label="Hamilton's equations from the variation" tex="\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \dot q_i}\right) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \dot p_i}\right) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \dot q_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.82), p. 23" />

which are Hamilton's equations. Because $f$ does not contain $\dot p_i$, the boundary term from the $p$ variations is zero anyway, so the conditions $\delta p_i = 0$ at the ends seem unnecessary. The notes insist that they are needed all the same: they put $q_i$ and $p_i$ on the same footing, which is exploited later in the course for canonical transformations.

## Differential and integral forms

Finally the notes compare the two principles (notes p. 23). D'Alembert's principle,

<Formula label="D'Alembert's principle" tex="\left[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j}\right) - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} - R_j\right]\delta q_j = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.83), p. 23" />

is a **differential** statement, holding at each instant, while Hamilton's principle,

<Formula label="Hamilton's principle, written out" tex="\delta J = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} dt\left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j} - \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q_j}\right)\right]\delta q_j = 0" cite="reading1" at="Section 1.5, eq. (1.84), p. 23" />

is an **integrated** statement about the whole path, and needs $R_j = 0$: all forces must come from a potential.

## Summary

- A functional $J = \int f(y_i, \dot y_i, s)\,ds$ is stationary under all variations with fixed end points exactly when $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y_i} - \frac{d}{ds}\frac{\partial f}{\partial \dot y_i} = 0$.
- With $s = t$ and $f = L$ this is Hamilton's principle and gives the Euler–Lagrange equations.
- Minimising arc length on a sphere gives great circles, $\sin(\phi - \alpha) = \beta\cot\theta$; a parallel of latitude is longer.
- Varying $q$ and $p$ independently in $\int(p_i\dot q_i - H)\,dt$ gives Hamilton's equations; fixing $\delta p$ at the ends puts $q$ and $p$ on an equal footing.
- D'Alembert's principle is the differential form of the dynamics; Hamilton's principle is the integrated form, valid when $R_j = 0$.
