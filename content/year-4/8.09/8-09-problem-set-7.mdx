Work each problem on paper first. Then type your result in the answer box and press Check. A number box accepts any value within the tolerance written next to it, in the unit named in the question. A formula box takes plain typing, for example `k*(a - b)^2`, and accepts every correct way of writing the answer. It treats its letters as independent, so each question says which letters to type: for example `theta` for $\theta$, `omega` for $\omega$, `rho` for $\rho$, `pi` for $\pi$, `H1` for $H_1$, `AT` for $A_T$.

The points per problem are MIT's, and so is their split between the parts. Several parts ask you to show something. Those parts have no box, or only a small one: open the solution and mark yourself. If you are stuck, open the hints one at a time. Each part ends with its full solution. Try before you open it: an answer given after its solution was opened earns no points. Your score is kept in this browser only.

The conventions are those of the notes and the lessons. Perturbation theory takes the constants $(\alpha, \beta)$ of the unperturbed motion as new canonical variables, with the perturbation $\Delta H$ as their Hamiltonian, and finds them order by order by putting the previous order on the right-hand side:

<Formula label="Order n of perturbation theory" tex="\dot\alpha_i^{(n)} = -\Big(\frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial \beta_i}\Big)_{n-1}, \qquad \dot\beta_i^{(n)} = \Big(\frac{\partial \Delta H}{\partial \alpha_i}\Big)_{n-1}" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.1, eq. (5.13), p. 94" />

For a harmonic oscillator $H_0 = p^2/(2I) + I\Omega^2\theta^2/2$ the new pair is the action $J$ and the phase $\beta$, with $H_0 = \nu J$, $\nu = \Omega/2\pi$, and

<Formula label="An oscillator in action-angle variables" tex="\theta = \sqrt{\frac{J}{\pi I \Omega}}\,\sin\big[2\pi(\nu t + \beta)\big], \qquad p = \sqrt{\frac{I J \Omega}{\pi}}\,\cos\big[2\pi(\nu t + \beta)\big]" cite="reading1" at="Section 5.2, eq. (5.22), p. 96" />

(The middle form of the notes' eq. (5.22) writes $\omega$ for the frequency $\Omega$; the form quoted here is the one on the right of that equation.) A change that builds up period after period is **secular**; one that returns to its starting value after each period is **periodic**. The secular part is found from the average over one period, as in eq. (5.25) of the notes (p. 97). An overdot is $d/dt$.

## Problem 1

<Exercise id="p1" title="Perturbation theory for two springs" points={10} mit="Problem 1">

A mass $m$ can only move along a straight line. Two ideal springs hold it, each with force constant $k$ and unstretched length $b$. The fixed end of one spring is a distance $a$ on one side of the line, the fixed end of the other a distance $a$ on the other side, both on the same perpendicular to the line, and $a > b$. When the mass is away from that perpendicular, each spring makes the same angle $\theta$ with it; $\theta = 0$ is the middle. The line is horizontal, so gravity does no work along it (our reading of MIT's figure).

<Part label="a" points={3}>

What is the Hamiltonian $H$ of this system in terms of the canonical coordinates $\theta$ and $p_\theta$? Type it with `theta`, `p` (for $p_\theta$), `m`, `a`, `k` and `b`.

<Hint>

Write the position of the mass along the line, and the length of each spring, as functions of $\theta$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The kinetic energy is $\frac12 m\dot x^2$ with $x(\theta)$. Find $p_\theta = \partial L/\partial\dot\theta$ and solve it for $\dot\theta$ before you write $H = p_\theta\dot\theta - L$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1a-h" label="$H(\theta, p_\theta) =$" expr="p^2*cos(theta)^4/(2*m*a^2) + k*(a/cos(theta) - b)^2"
  vars={{ theta: [0.1, 0.6], p: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], a: [2, 3], k: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 1.5] }} names={{ theta: "\\theta", p: "p_\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "p^2*cos(theta)^4/(2*m*a^2) + k*(a/cos(theta) - b)^2/2", hint: "This counts the energy of one spring only. There are two springs, each stretched to the same length." },
    { expr: "p^2/(2*m*a^2) + k*(a/cos(theta) - b)^2", hint: "This takes the speed along the line as aθ̇. The mass moves along the line, not on a circle: its speed has an extra factor that depends on θ." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Write the position and the spring length in $\theta$, form the Lagrangian, and Legendre-transform it.

<Step title="Position and spring length">

The mass sits at $x = a\tan\theta$ along the line, measured from the perpendicular. Each spring then has length $a/\cos\theta$, so each is stretched by $a/\cos\theta - b$. The speed is $\dot x = a\dot\theta/\cos^2\theta$.

</Step>

<Step title="Lagrangian and momentum">

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the Lagrangian" tex="L = \frac{ma^2\dot\theta^2}{2\cos^4\theta} - 2\cdot\frac{k}{2}\left(\frac{a}{\cos\theta} - b\right)^2, \qquad p_\theta = \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta} = \frac{ma^2\dot\theta}{\cos^4\theta}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The Lagrangian has no explicit time and its kinetic energy is quadratic in $\dot\theta$, so $H$ is the energy, written with $p_\theta$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(a): the Hamiltonian" tex="H = \frac{p_\theta^2\cos^4\theta}{2ma^2} + k\left(\frac{a}{\cos\theta} - b\right)^2" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(a), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

Units: $p_\theta$ is an angular momentum, so $p_\theta^2/(ma^2)$ is an energy, as is $k$ times a length squared. Hamilton's equation $\dot\theta = \partial H/\partial p_\theta = p_\theta\cos^4\theta/(ma^2)$ gives back the momentum of the Lagrangian. At $\theta = 0$ each spring has length $a$ and is stretched by $a - b > 0$: even the middle is under tension.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="b" points={2}>

Expand your result from (a) about $\theta = 0$ and split it as $H = H_0 + \Delta H$, where $H_0$ is a harmonic-oscillator Hamiltonian (with the constant term in it, our choice). For $\Delta H$ keep only the first correction, counting $\theta$ and $p_\theta$ as being of the same size. Type $\Delta H$ with `theta`, `p`, `m`, `a`, `k` and `b`. Also (our addition) give the square of the angular frequency $\omega_0$ of $H_0$, typed with `m`, `a`, `k` and `b`.

<Hint>

You need $1/\cos\theta$ to order $\theta^4$ and $\cos^4\theta$ to order $\theta^2$. With $\theta \sim p_\theta$ small, $H_0$ holds the terms of second order, and the first correction is everything of fourth order.

</Hint>

<Hint>

Compare $H_0$ with $p_\theta^2/(2I) + I\omega_0^2\theta^2/2$ to read off $I$ and $\omega_0$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1b-dh" label="$\Delta H =$" expr="-theta^2*p^2/(m*a^2) + k*a*(8*a - 5*b)*theta^4/12"
  vars={{ theta: [0.1, 0.6], p: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], a: [2, 3], k: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 1.5] }} names={{ theta: "\\theta", p: "p_\\theta" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "k*a*(8*a - 5*b)*theta^4/12", hint: "This keeps only the potential. The kinetic term has a fourth-order part too, from the expansion of cos⁴θ, and it counts at the same order." },
    { expr: "-theta^2*p^2/(m*a^2) + k*a^2*theta^4/4", hint: "This takes 1/cos θ to order θ² only. Its θ⁴ term, multiplied by the stretch a − b in the square, adds to the θ⁴ term of the potential." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1b-w0" label="$\omega_0^2 =$" expr="2*k*(a - b)/(m*a)"
  vars={{ m: [0.5, 2], a: [2, 3], k: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 1.5] }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "2*k/m", hint: "This is the value for springs of zero unstretched length (b = 0). With b > 0 the sideways restoring force is weaker." },
    { expr: "k*(a - b)/(m*a)", hint: "This is half the right value: both springs add a θ² term to the potential." }
  ]} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Expand to fourth order in the small quantities. The second-order terms make the oscillator; the fourth-order terms make $\Delta H$.

<Step title="The expansions">

With $1/\cos\theta = 1 + \theta^2/2 + 5\theta^4/24 + \ldots$ the stretch of each spring is $(a - b) + a\theta^2/2 + 5a\theta^4/24$, and its square is $(a - b)^2 + a(a - b)\theta^2 + \left[a^2/4 + 5a(a - b)/12\right]\theta^4$. With $\cos^4\theta = 1 - 2\theta^2 + \ldots$ the kinetic term is $p_\theta^2/(2ma^2) - \theta^2p_\theta^2/(ma^2)$. Since $a^2/4 + 5a(a - b)/12 = a(8a - 5b)/12$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the split" tex="H_0 = k(a - b)^2 + \frac{p_\theta^2}{2ma^2} + ka(a - b)\,\theta^2, \qquad \Delta H = -\frac{\theta^2p_\theta^2}{ma^2} + \frac{ka(8a - 5b)}{12}\,\theta^4" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The oscillator">

$H_0$ is a constant plus $p_\theta^2/(2I) + I\omega_0^2\theta^2/2$ with $I = ma^2$ and $I\omega_0^2/2 = ka(a - b)$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(b): the frequency of H₀" tex="I = ma^2, \qquad \omega_0^2 = \frac{2k(a - b)}{ma}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(b), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

In terms of $x = a\tan\theta$ the potential is $k(\sqrt{a^2 + x^2} - b)^2$, whose curvature at $x = 0$ is $2k(1 - b/a)$; divided by $m$ that is $\omega_0^2$ again. For $b = 0$ the potential is $k(a^2 + x^2)$, exactly harmonic in $x$, with $\omega_0^2 = 2k/m$.

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

<Part label="c" points={5}>

Use first-order perturbation theory with the action-angle variables $(J, \beta)$ of $H_0$ to find the secular change of the frequency caused by $\Delta H$, and show that the amplitude has no secular change. Give the frequency shift $\nu' - \nu$ (in cycles per unit time, our choice) in terms of the initial action $J_0$, typed with `J` (for $J_0$), `m`, `a`, `b` and `pi`. Then (our addition) give the relative shift $(\nu' - \nu)/\nu$ in terms of the energy $E = \nu J_0$ of the oscillation above the bottom of the well, typed with `E`, `k`, `a` and `b`.

<Hint>

Write $\Delta H$ with $\theta$ and $p_\theta$ from the action-angle form in the conventions above, with $\Omega \to \omega_0$ and $I = ma^2$. You will meet $\sin^2\cos^2$ and $\sin^4$ of the angle $2\pi(\nu t + \beta)$.

</Hint>

<Hint>

The secular parts are the averages over one period of $\partial\Delta H/\partial J$ and $-\partial\Delta H/\partial\beta$. The averages of $\sin^2\cos^2$ and $\sin^4$ over a period are $1/8$ and $3/8$.

</Hint>

<Answer id="p1c-dnu" label="$\nu' - \nu =$" expr="3*b*J/(32*pi^2*m*a^2*(a - b))"
  vars={{ J: [0.5, 2], m: [0.5, 2], a: [2, 3], b: [0.5, 1.5] }} names={{ J: "J_0" }}
  mistakes={[
    { expr: "(8*a - 5*b)*J/(32*pi^2*m*a^2*(a - b))", hint: "This leaves out the kinetic part of ΔH, the term in θ²p_θ². Its average is negative and cancels most of the potential's shift." },
    { expr: "3*b*J/(16*pi*m*a^2*(a - b))", hint: "This is 2π times too large: it is the shift of the angular frequency ω, not of ν = ω/2π." }
  ]} />

<Answer id="p1c-rel" label="$(\nu' - \nu)/\nu =$" expr="3*b*E/(16*k*a*(a - b)^2)"
  vars={{ E: [0.5, 2], a: [2, 3], k: [0.5, 2], b: [0.5, 1.5] }} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** In action-angle variables the perturbation is a function of $J$ and of the phase. Its average over a period gives the drift of the phase, which is the frequency shift; the derivative with respect to the phase averages to zero.

<Step title="The perturbation in action-angle variables">

Write $\phi = 2\pi(\nu t + \beta)$. With $\theta^2 = J\sin^2\phi/(\pi I\omega_0)$, $p_\theta^2 = IJ\omega_0\cos^2\phi/\pi$ and $I\omega_0^2 = 2ka(a - b)$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): ΔH in action-angle variables" tex="\Delta H = \frac{J^2}{\pi^2 I}\left[-\sin^2\phi\cos^2\phi + \frac{8a - 5b}{24(a - b)}\,\sin^4\phi\right]" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

</Step>

<Step title="The frequency">

To first order, $\dot\beta^{(1)} = (\partial\Delta H/\partial J)_0$. Its average over a period uses $\overline{\sin^2\phi\cos^2\phi} = 1/8$ and $\overline{\sin^4\phi} = 3/8$: the bracket averages to $-\frac18 + \frac{3(8a - 5b)}{192(a - b)} = \frac{3b}{64(a - b)}$. So

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the frequency shift" tex="\nu' - \nu = \overline{\dot\beta^{(1)}} = \frac{2J_0}{\pi^2 I}\cdot\frac{3b}{64(a - b)} = \frac{3b\,J_0}{32\pi^2 ma^2(a - b)}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

The phase drifts at a steady rate on top of $\nu t$, so the mass oscillates at $\nu' = \nu + \overline{\dot\beta^{(1)}}$, as in the notes' pendulum example. Dividing by $\nu$ and using $J_0 = E/\nu$, $I\nu^2 = I\omega_0^2/4\pi^2 = ka(a - b)/2\pi^2$:

<Formula label="Problem 1(c): the relative shift" tex="\frac{\nu' - \nu}{\nu} = \frac{3bE}{16ka(a - b)^2}" cite="mit" at="Problem 1(c), p. 2 (our own solution)" />

For $a > b > 0$ the frequency rises with the energy: the springs stiffen as the mass moves out.

</Step>

<Step title="The amplitude">

$\dot J^{(1)} = -(\partial\Delta H/\partial\beta)_0 = -2\pi\,\partial\Delta H/\partial\phi$. $\Delta H$ is a periodic function of $\phi$, and the derivative of a periodic function has zero average over its period. So $J$, and with it the amplitude $\sqrt{J/(\pi I\omega_0)}$, only oscillates about $J_0$: no secular change.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

For $b = 0$ the shift vanishes, as it must: then the potential $k(a^2 + x^2)$ is exactly harmonic in $x$ and the frequency cannot depend on the amplitude, even though $\Delta H$ itself is not zero in the coordinate $\theta$. An independent route in $x$: $\sqrt{a^2 + x^2} = a + x^2/2a - x^4/8a^3$ gives $V = \text{const} + \frac12 m\omega_0^2x^2 + \frac{kb}{4a^3}x^4$, and the standard first-order shift of an oscillator with an $x^4$ term, $\Delta\omega/\omega_0 = 3\lambda A^2/(2m\omega_0^2)$ for $\lambda x^4$ and amplitude $A$, gives the same relative shift once $E = \frac12 m\omega_0^2A^2$ is put in. A numerical period of the full potential at small energy gives the same relative shift (calcs file).

</Step>

</Solution>

</Part>

## Problem 4

<Exercise id="p4" title="Fluid angular momentum and a vortex without vorticity" points={14} mit="Problem 4">

This problem uses the momentum conservation law of an ideal fluid with an external force density $\vec f$,

<Formula label="Momentum conservation" tex="\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\rho v_i\right) + \frac{\partial T_{ij}}{\partial x_j} = f_i, \qquad T_{ij} = P\delta_{ij} + \rho v_iv_j" cite="reading2" at="Section 6.2.3, eqs. (6.41) and (6.42), pp. 113–114" />

with a sum over the repeated index $j$, and the Levi-Civita symbol $\epsilon_{ijk}$, with which a cross product reads $(\vec r\times\vec f)_i = \epsilon_{ijk}x_jf_k$.

<Part label="a" points={5}>

Take the angular momentum density $\vec\ell = \vec r\times(\rho\vec v)$, where $\vec r$ runs from a fixed origin to a fixed point $(x, y, z)$ in the fluid. Derive a conservation law for $\vec\ell$ from the result above, with gravity as the external force, in the same form as for the momentum:

<Formula label="Problem 4(a): the form of the law" tex="\frac{\partial\vec\ell}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\hat J = \vec Q" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 4" />

Find the angular momentum flux tensor $J_{ij}$ and the source term $\vec Q$. Take $(\nabla\cdot\hat J)_i = \partial J_{ij}/\partial x_j$ (our choice of index order, as for $T_{ij}$ above) and gravity along $-\hat z$. As a sample (our addition), give the component $J_{xy}$, typed with `x`, `y`, `z`, `P`, `rho`, `vx`, `vy` and `vz`, and (our addition) choose $\vec Q$.

<Hint>

</Hint>

    { expr: "z*P + rho*(y*vz - z*vy)*vy", hint: "Check the sign of the pressure term: it is P times the x component of r × ŷ, and that component is −z." }

<Answer id="p4a-q" label="The source term $\vec Q$ is:" choices={["ρg (r × ẑ)", "zero, as for the momentum", "ρg (ẑ × r)", "−ρg z ẑ"]} answer={2} />

<Solution>

**The idea.** Take the cross product of $\vec r$ with the momentum law, then move $\vec r$ inside the divergence; the symmetry of $T_{ij}$ removes the leftover term.

<Step title="The time derivative">

$\frac{\partial\ell_i}{\partial t} = \epsilon_{ijk}x_j\frac{\partial(\rho v_k)}{\partial t} = \epsilon_{ijk}x_jf_k - \epsilon_{ijk}x_j\frac{\partial T_{km}}{\partial x_m}$.

</Step>

<Step title="Into a divergence">

$\epsilon_{ijk}x_j\,\partial_mT_{km} = \partial_m\left(\epsilon_{ijk}x_jT_{km}\right) - \epsilon_{ijk}\delta_{jm}T_{km}$, and the last term is $\epsilon_{ijk}T_{kj} = 0$, since $T$ is symmetric and $\epsilon$ is antisymmetric in $j, k$. So the flux is $J_{im} = \epsilon_{ijk}x_jT_{km}$. With $T_{km} = P\delta_{km} + \rho v_kv_m$, and renaming the indices:

<Formula label="Problem 4(a): the angular momentum law" tex="\frac{\partial\ell_i}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial J_{ij}}{\partial x_j} = Q_i, \qquad J_{ij} = \epsilon_{ikj}\,x_kP + \ell_i\,v_j, \qquad \vec Q = \vec r\times\vec f = \rho g\,\hat z\times\vec r" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(a), p. 4 (our own solution)" />

using $\vec f = -\rho g\hat z$, so $\vec r\times\vec f = -\rho g\,\vec r\times\hat z = \rho g\,\hat z\times\vec r$.

</Step>

<Step title="The sample component">

$J_{xy} = \epsilon_{xky}x_kP + \ell_xv_y = -zP + \rho(yv_z - zv_y)v_y$, since only $k = z$ contributes and $\epsilon_{xzy} = -1$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The flux has the two parts one expects: angular momentum carried along with the flow, $\ell_iv_j$, and the moment of the pressure force, $\epsilon_{ikj}x_kP$, the counterpart of $P\delta_{ij}$ for momentum. The source is the torque density of gravity, $\vec r\times\vec f$. Integrated over a volume, the law says that the angular momentum inside changes by the flow through the surface, the torque of the pressure on the surface and the torque of gravity, as for a system of particles.

</Step>

</Solution>

Now take a horizontal tank with a small hole in the middle of its floor. An incompressible ideal fluid goes rapidly round the hole on almost circular paths, creeping slowly inward in a tight spiral. Away from the hole the flow can be treated as steady; near the hole viscosity matters, so that region is cut out of the problem. Take the steady fluid to have zero vorticity, $\nabla\times\vec v = 0$. Let $r$ be the distance from the vertical axis through the hole and $\theta$ the angle round it.

<Part label="b" points={4}>

Using Stokes' theorem with a suitable closed curve $C$, show that the angular velocity is $\dot\theta \propto 1/r^2$. The region inside $C$ must contain only the steady flow: a circle centred on the hole would enclose the hole, where there is vorticity. Then (our addition) if the fluid at radius $r_1$ goes round with angular velocity $\dot\theta_1$, what is $\dot\theta$ at radius $r$? Type it with `w1` (for $\dot\theta_1$), `r1` and `r`.

<Hint>

</Hint>

<Solution>

**The idea.** With no vorticity inside $C$, the circulation round $C$ is zero; a curve of two arcs and two radial segments then compares the circulation at two radii.

<Step title="Stokes' theorem">

<Formula label="Stokes' theorem for the velocity" tex="\int_S\left(\nabla\times\vec v\right)\cdot\hat n\,dS = \oint_{\partial S}\vec v\cdot d\vec l" cite="reading2" at="Section 6.2.1, eq. (6.29), p. 111" />

Inside $C$ the vorticity is zero, so $\oint_C\vec v\cdot d\vec l = 0$.

</Step>

<Step title="The curve">

Take $C$ as the edge of the region $r_1 < r < r_2$, $\theta_a < \theta < \theta_b$: it encloses no part of the hole. Along the outer arc $\vec v\cdot d\vec l = v_\theta(r_2)\,r_2\,d\theta$; along the inner arc, traversed the other way, $-v_\theta(r_1)\,r_1\,d\theta$. The two radial segments carry the same integral of $v_r\,dr$, since the flow is the same at every angle, and are traversed in opposite directions: they cancel. So

<Formula label="Problem 4(b): zero circulation" tex="\left[r_2\,v_\theta(r_2) - r_1\,v_\theta(r_1)\right](\theta_b - \theta_a) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad r\,v_\theta = r^2\dot\theta = \text{constant}" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(b), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

So $\dot\theta \propto 1/r^2$, and $\dot\theta(r) = \dot\theta_1 r_1^2/r^2$.

</Step>

<Step title="Check the result">

The flow $v_\theta = C/r$ has zero vorticity at every $r > 0$: in cylindrical coordinates $(\nabla\times\vec v)_z = \frac1r\frac{d}{dr}(rv_\theta) = 0$. A circle round the hole, by contrast, has circulation $2\pi C$, which Stokes' theorem puts in the hole: all the vorticity sits in the cut-out region.

</Step>

**The idea.** For a steady incompressible flow the conservation law becomes a law for one fluid element, and the torques about the axis vanish by symmetry.

<Step title="The divergence">

<Formula label="Problem 4(c): following the fluid" tex="\frac{d\vec\ell}{dt} = \frac{\partial\vec\ell}{\partial t} + \left(\vec v\cdot\nabla\right)\vec\ell = -\vec r\times\nabla P + \vec r\times\vec f" cite="mit" at="Problem 4(c), p. 5 (our own solution)" />

<Formula label="The total (convective) time derivative" tex="\frac{d}{dt} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \vec v\cdot\nabla" cite="reading2" at="Section 6.1, eq. (6.19), p. 109" />
